Abstract

When ϕ ⁡ ( s ) = s \phi(s)=s this equation includes a generalized prototype of a classical model going back to Moore and Nehari [ 35 ] , 1959. This is the first paper where the general case when λ ∈ ℝ \lambda\in\mathbb{R} has been addressed when a ⪈ 0 a\gneq 0 . In the past decades a great deal of research has been devoted
to the so-called nonlinear problems with sign

This analysis is motivated by some recent researches in semilinear boundary
value problems with nonnegative weights that are “degenerate” in some

[

More recently, the existence of nodal solutions for the symmetric variant of the Moore–Nehari problem

The plan of this paper is the following. In Section 2 we analyze the semilinear problem (

In some sense, these highly oscillatory solutions are lost when

(\lambda,u)
and
(\lambda,-u)
, which facilitates the construction of the set
of solutions of the problem. Another pivotal feature of (
2.1
) is that, for every solution
(\lambda,u)
with
u\neq 0
and any

such that
u(z)=0
, necessarily
u^{\prime}(z)\neq 0
. Thus, the zeroes of any non trivial solution are simple and isolated. Therefore, any non-trivial solution of (
2.1
) has, at most, finitely many zeroes in
(0,L)
.

2.1
The special case when
a\equiv\mu>0
in
(0,L)

In such case, the planar system associated to the differential equation of (
2.1
) becomes

(\mathcal{N}_{\lambda})\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{\prime}&=y,\\
y^{\prime}&=-\lambda x-\mu|x|^{p-1}x.\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

When
p\in(0,1)
,
|x|^{p-1}x
admits a continuous extension up to
x=0
and the uniqueness of a solution for the associated Cauchy problem follows from Rebelo
[
43
, Th. 1]
; the existence being a direct consequence of the Peano’s theory. When
p>1
the nonlinearity is Lipschitz continuous and these results are a direct consequence from the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem.

Our construction of the set of solutions of (
2.3
) is based on the several phase portraits of the model and hence, it relies on the period function associated to the periodic orbits of (
2.3
) surrounding the origin. By the oddity of
y
and
|x|^{p-1}x
, the necessary time to cross each quadrant in the phase-plane is the same. Thus, it is the quarter of the period. Let denote by
\mathcal{H}(x,y)
the associated Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}+\frac{\mu}{p+1}|x|^{p+1}.

Then, for the appropriate range of
x_{+}>0
, the periodic orbit of (
2.3
),
(x(t),y(t))
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, through the point
(x_{+},0)
satisfies

\mathcal{H}(x(t),y(t))=\frac{1}{2}y^{2}(t)+\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}(t)+\frac{\mu}{p+1}|x(t)|^{p+1}=\frac{\lambda}{2}x_{+}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{p+1}x_{+}^{p+1}

(2.4)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, after some straightforward manipulations, it is apparent that the period of the orbit trough
(x_{+},0)
can be expressed in the form

\mathcal{T}(x_{+})\equiv\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(x_{+}):=4\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{\lambda(1-s^{2})+\frac{2\mu}{p+1}x_{+}^{p-1}(1-s^{p+1})}}.

(2.5)

The precise range of
x_{+}
’s for which
\mathcal{T}(x_{+})
is well defined, as well as the precise behavior of the map
\mathcal{T}
depends on
\lambda
and the energy level

\mathcal{H}=c
of the Hamiltonian. Indeed,
when
\lambda\geq 0
, the origin, which is the unique equilibrium of (
2.3
), is a global nonlinear center
and (
2.5
) makes sense for all
x_{+}>0
. However, when
\lambda<0
, then the situation is a bit more subtle, as (
2.3
) possesses two additional equilibria at the points

\omega_{\pm}:=\pm\left(\frac{-\lambda}{\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}},

(2.6)

and the global structure of the phase portrait depends on the size of
p>0
. Suppose
p>1
. Then,
the origin is a saddle point whose stable and unstable manifolds consist of two homoclinic connections
surrounding
\omega_{\pm}
, with zero energy level, passing through
(\pm x^{*},0)
, where we are setting

x^{*}:=\left(\frac{-\lambda(p+1)}{2\mu}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}.

(2.7)

In this case, the integral curves
{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)=c
with

are periodic orbits surrounding
the origin with period given by (
2.5
) for all
x_{+}>x^{*}
, while,
for every

(\omega_{\pm},0)
in the phase portrait.
Instead, when

(\omega_{\pm},0)

(2.8)

Thus, for every
x_{+}\in(0,\omega_{+})

\mathcal{H}(x_{+},0)<c^{*}
and the solution through
(x_{+},0)
is a periodic orbit surrounding
(0,0)

Proof.

Note that a solution

\left(0,\tfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)=\mathcal{T}((0,+\infty))\;\,\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\geq 0,\qquad\left(0,+\infty\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{T}((x^{*},+\infty))&\;\,\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda<0,\;p>1,\\
\mathcal{T}((0,\omega_{+}))&\;\,\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda<0,\;p\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

,

\lambda<\sigma_{n}

is monotone with respect to

. The distribution of the zeroes is a direct consequence of the symmetries of the problem, because the solution with

.

The continuity of the map

Suppose

, which concludes the proof of Part (a).

Suppose

\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(x_{+})

x_{+}
for each fixed
\lambda
.
Moreover, for every (fixed)

\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(x_{+})
is decreasing with respect to
\lambda

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\sigma_{n}

2.2
A generalized Moore–Nehari model

In this section we consider a piecewise continuous weight function
a(t)\gneq 0
defined in
[0,L]
,
which is divided into finitely many disjoint intervals where, alternately, either
a
is positive (not necessarily constant) or
a\equiv 0
. Precisely, we assume that
[0,L]
splits out into

I^{+}_{i}=[t_{i},s_{i}]\quad\text{and }\;I^{0}_{j}=(s_{j},t_{j+1}),\quad i\in\{0,....,m\},\;\;j\in\{0,...,m-1\},

where

0=t_{0}<s_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{i}<s_{i}<\dots<s_{m-1}<t_{m}<s_{m}=L,

(2.11)

in such a way that
a\in\mathcal{C}[t_{i},s_{i}]
satisfies
a(t)>0
for all
t\in[t_{i},s_{i}]

and
i\in\{0,...,m\}
, while
a\equiv 0
in
I^{0}_{j}
for all
j\in\{0,...,m\}
.
Thus, there are
m+1
intervals where the weight function
a(t)
is positive,
separated away by
m
intervals where
a\equiv 0
. Our class of weight functions extends considerably the one introduced by Moore and Nehari
[
35
]
, later considered by Kajikiya
[
13
,
14
]
, where
\lambda=0
and, for some
\mu\in(0,\frac{L}{2})
,

a=1-\chi_{(\frac{L}{2}-\mu,\frac{L}{2}+\mu)},

where
\chi_{J}
stands for the characteristic function of the interval
J\subset[0,L]
, i.e.,

a(t)=0
if
|t-\frac{L}{2}|<\mu
, while
a(t)=1
if
|t-\frac{L}{2}|\geq\mu
.

Essentially, the results of this section establish that the global bifurcation diagrams already found
for the autonomous problem when
a\equiv\mu>0
, collected in Figure
3
, are “preserved”  for the general class of weight functions considered in this section, though for some values of

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and some integer
n\geq 1
, the problem might have an arbitrarily large number of solutions with
n-1
interior nodes (see Cubillos et al.
[
8
]
for some examples in this direction when

a\lneq 0
). The main technical tool to get these results is bifurcation theory
with the a priori bounds of Amann and López-Gómez
[
1
]
.
As it will become apparent from the proofs in this section, the general bifurcation approach
combined with
[
1
]
, applies to a broader class of weight functions than the ones
in which the intervals of positivity and degeneracy follow the split of (
2.11
). Indeed, the
location of the intervals of positivity is not important; our only assumption here is that

\min a(t)>0
in each interval where
a(t)>0.
In any case,
it is convenient to adopt (
2.11
)
in order to have a comparison with the results in the other sections as well as with respect to
other cited articles.

Subsequently, we adapt the analysis of Fencl and López-Gómez
[
10
]
to our setting here. The next result is Lemma 2.1 of
[
10
]
. As usual,
W^{k,p}_{0}(0,L)
denotes the closed subspace of the Sobolev space
W^{k,p}(0,L)
consisting of the functions
u\in W^{k,p}(0,L)
such that
u(0)=0=u(L)
. Similarly,
C^{k}_{0}[0,L]
stands for the set of functions of class
\mathcal{C}^{k}[0,L]
vanishing at
0
and
L
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
f\in L^{2}(0,L)
, the function

u(t)\equiv Kf(t):=\int_{0}^{t}(s-t)f(s)\,ds-\frac{t}{L}\int_{0}^{L}(s-L)f(s)\,ds

provides us with the unique solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=f\quad\hbox{in}\;\,(0,L),\\
u(0)=u(L)=0,\end{array}\right.

in
W^{2,2}(0,L)
. Moreover, the solution operator
K:L^{2}(0,L)\rightarrow W^{2,2}_{0}(0,L)
is linear and continuous. Thus, when considering the compact embedding
\iota:W^{2,2}_{0}(0,L)\hookrightarrow C^{1}_{0}[0,L]
, the composition

\mathcal{K}:=\iota\circ K|_{C^{1}_{0}[0,L]}:C^{1}_{0}[0,L]\rightarrow C^{1}_{0}[0,L]

is a linear, continuous and compact operator.

Thanks to Lemma
2.1
, the problem (
2.1
) can be expressed as a fixed point equation for a compact operator. Indeed, a function
u
piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{2}
in
[0,L]
solves (
2.1
) if, and only if,

u=\mathcal{K}(\lambda u+a(t)|u|^{p-1}u).

Thus, the solutions of (
2.1
) are the zeroes of the nonlinear operator

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathfrak{F}:\mathbb{R}\times C^{1}_{0}[0,L]&\longrightarrow&C^{1}_{0}[0,L]\\[5.0pt]
(\lambda,u)&\longmapsto&\mathfrak{F}(\lambda,u):=u-\mathcal{K}(\lambda u+a(t)|u|^{p-1}u),\end{array}

(2.12)

which can be decomposed in the form

\mathfrak{F}(\lambda,u)=\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)u+\mathfrak{N}(\lambda,u),

where

\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)u:=u-\lambda\mathcal{K}u\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathfrak{N}(\lambda,u):=-\mathcal{K}(a(t)|u|^{p-1}u).

(2.13)

By (
2.13
),
\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)
is a compact perturbation of the identity
I
in
C^{1}_{0}[0,L]
and, hence, a Fredholm operator of index
0
.
Moreover,

\mathfrak{N}(\lambda,u)=o(\|u\|)\quad\hbox{as}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\|u\|\rightarrow 0&\quad\hbox{if}\;p>1,\\
\|u\|\rightarrow+\infty&\quad\hbox{if}\;0<p<1.\end{array}\right.

Thus, we can apply the abstract theory of
[
16
, Ch. 6]
for
p>1
, going back to
Rabinowitz
[
40
]
, as well as Theorem 1.6 of Rabinowitz
[
41
]
for
0<p<1
.

The generalized spectrum,
\Sigma(\mathfrak{L})
, of the Fredholm curve
\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)
is the set of
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
u=\lambda\mathcal{K}u
for some
u\neq 0
,
u\in C^{1}_{0}[0,L]
. It is folklore that

\Sigma(\mathfrak{L})=\left\{\sigma_{n}=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\,:\,n\geq 1\right\}.

As in the general case when
p>1
, the nonlinearity is not of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, unless
p>2
,
the local theorem of Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
7
]
cannot be applied to show the existence of a
smooth curve of nodal solutions with
n-1
interior nodes bifurcating from
u=0
at
\lambda=\sigma_{n}
. Consequently, to establish the existence of a component of nodal solutions bifurcating from
u=0
at
\lambda=\sigma_{n}
, we must find out the Leray–Schauder degree
\mathrm{Deg\,}(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda),B_{R})
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\Sigma(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda))
, where
B_{R}
denotes the ball of radius
R>0
centered at the origin of
C^{1}_{0}[0,L]
. The degree can be computed through the Schauder’s formula

{\rm Deg}(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda),B_{R})=(-1)^{m(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda))},

where
m(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda))
is the sum of the algebraic multiplicities of the negative eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)
. According to the Schauder formula, the next result holds.

Lemma 2.2
.

Setting
\sigma_{0}\equiv-\infty
, it turns out that, for every integer
n\geq 0
,

\mathrm{Deg}(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda),B_{R})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,\lambda\in(\sigma_{2n},\sigma_{2n+1}),\\
-1&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,\lambda\in(\sigma_{2n+1},\sigma_{2n+2}).\end{array}\right.

(2.14)

Proof.

To determine
m(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda))
, we should construct the set of
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for some
u\in C^{1}_{0}[0,L]\setminus\{0\}
,

\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)u=u-\lambda\mathcal{K}u=\mu u.

(2.15)

Setting
\sigma_{0}:=-\infty
, for every
n\geq 1
,
\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)
is invertible for
\lambda\in(\sigma_{n-1},\sigma_{n})
. Thus, by the homotopy invariance of the degree,

d_{n}:=\mathrm{Deg}(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda),B_{R})\;\;\hbox{is constant for all}\;\;\lambda\in(\sigma_{n-1},\sigma_{n}).

In particular,
\mathrm{Deg}(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda),B_{R})
is constant in
(-\infty,\sigma_{1})
. Thus, to determine
d_{1}
we can assume, without lost of generality, that
\lambda>0
. Then, by (
2.15
),
\mu\neq 1
and hence, the equation (
2.15
) can be equivalently written as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\lambda}{1-\mu}u&\quad\hbox{in}\;\;[0,L],\\[2.0pt]
u(0)=u(L)=0.&\end{array}\right.

Thus, the set of classical eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)
is

\sigma(\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)):=\left\{\mu_{n}:=1-\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{n}}\;:\;n\geq 1\right\}.

The
\mu_{n}
’s are algebraic simple eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)
because, for every
n\geq 1
, setting
\psi_{n}(t):=\sin(\frac{n\pi}{L}t)
,
t\in[0,L]
, we have that

N[I-\lambda\mathcal{K}-\mu_{n}I]=N[\mathfrak{L}(\sigma_{n})]={\rm span}[\psi_{n}],\qquad\psi_{n}\notin R[I-\lambda\mathcal{K}-\mu_{n}I].

Indeed, if there exists
u\in C^{1}_{0}[0,L]
such that

\psi_{n}=(1-\mu_{n})u-\lambda\mathcal{K}u\Longleftrightarrow(1-\mu_{n})u=\lambda\mathcal{K}u+\psi_{n}\in C^{2}_{0}[0,L],

differentiating twice with respect to
t
, it follows from the definition of
\mu_{n}
that

\displaystyle-(1-\mu_{n})u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u-\psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u+\sigma_{n}\psi_{n}\Longleftrightarrow-u^{\prime\prime}-\sigma_{n}u=\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{\lambda}\psi_{n}.

Thus, multiplying by
\psi_{n}
and integrating in
(0,L)
yields

0<\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{L}\psi_{n}^{2}=\int_{0}^{L}(-u^{\prime\prime}-\sigma_{n}u)\psi_{n}=0,

which is impossible. Therefore,
m(\lambda)
equals the number of negative eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{L}(\lambda)
. Consequently, since for every
n\geq 1
and any given
\sigma_{n}<\lambda<\sigma_{n+1}
we have that

\mu_{1}=1-\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{1}}<\cdots<\mu_{n}=1-\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{n}}<0<\mu_{n+1}=1-\frac{\lambda}{\sigma_{n+1}}<\cdots,

the identity (
2.14
) holds.

\square

Remark 2.1
.

In the proof of Lemma

Next, we will ascertain the global behavior of the components

for

. Essentially, we will show that

behaves much like the curve bifurcating from

in Figure

, though in the general case covered in this section the uniqueness of the nodal solution with

might fail.
Our main result will follow after a series of technical lemmas. The next one shows that (

) cannot admit a solution

with

interior zeroes if

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
p>1
, or
p\in(0,1)
, and (
2.1
) has a solution with
n-1
interior zeroes,
(\lambda,u)
. Then,
\lambda<\sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,u)
be a solution with
n-1
zeroes in
(0,L)
. Then,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\left(-D^{2}-a(t)|u|^{p-1}\right)u=\lambda u\quad\hbox{in}\;\;[0,L],\\
u(0)=u(L)=0,\end{array}\right.

and hence,
u
is an eigenfunction with
n-1
zeroes associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
of the
differential operator
-D^{2}-a(t)|u|^{p-1}
under homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Consequently, by the uniqueness of these eigenvalues,

\lambda=\sigma_{n}\left[-D^{2}-a(t)|u|^{p-1}\right]

and, thanks to the monotonicity with respect to the potential (see Butazzo et al.
[

\lambda=\sigma_{n}\left[-D^{2}-a(t)|u|^{p-1}\right]<\sigma_{n}[-D^{2}]=\sigma_{n}.

This ends the proof.

\square

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}_{\kappa}
be the component of nodal solutions of (
2.1
) with
\kappa-1
interior nodes
whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem

. Then,

(i)

Whenever

),
\{(\lambda_{n},u_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
, such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, one has that

\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})=0

(2.18)

if, and only if,
\lambda_{*}=\sigma_{\kappa}

(ii)

Similarly, when

\{(\lambda_{n},u_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}

\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})=+\infty

if, and only if,
\lambda_{*}=\sigma_{\kappa}
for some
\kappa\geq 1

Proof.

Let
\{(\lambda_{n},u_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of solutions in
\mathfrak{C}_{k}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,p>1,\\[5.0pt]

(2.20)

Then, dividing by

the differential equation satisfied by

and inverting

, we find that, for every

\frac{u_{n}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})}=\mathcal{K}\left(\lambda_{n}\frac{u_{n}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})}+a(t)\frac{|u_{n}|^{p-1}u_{n}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})}\right).

(2.21)

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, by (
2.20
), the sequence

\lambda_{n}\frac{u_{n}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})}+a(t)\frac{|u_{n}|^{p-1}u_{n}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})},\qquad n\geq 1,

is bounded. Thus, since

is compact, there exists a convergent subsequence

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{u_{n_{m}}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n_{m}},u_{n_{m}})}=\psi

to some function
\psi
with
\kappa-1
interior zeroes such that
\|\psi\|_{\infty}=1
. Moreover,
letting
m\to\infty
in

\frac{u_{n_{m}}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n_{m}},u_{n_{m}})}=\mathcal{K}\left(\lambda_{n_{m}}\frac{u_{n_{m}}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n_{m}},u_{n_{m}})}+a(t)\frac{|u_{n_{m}}|^{p-1}u_{n_{m}}}{M_{+}(\lambda_{n_{m}},u_{n_{m}})}\right)

it becomes apparent that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}&-\psi^{\prime\prime}=\lambda_{*}\psi,\\
&\psi(0)=0=\psi(L).\end{array}\right.

Therefore,
\lambda_{*}=\sigma_{k}=\left(\frac{k\pi}{L}\right)^{2}
. The converses follow straight ahead from
Theorem

\square

The next lemma provides us with a priori bounds in both cases,

and

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}_{\kappa}
be the component of nodal solutions of (
2.1
) with
\kappa-1
interior nodes whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem
2.1
. Then, for every compact subinterval

(i)

For every
(\lambda,u)\in\mathfrak{C}_{\kappa}
with
\lambda\in J

M(\lambda,u)\leq C
if
p>1
.

(ii)

For every
(\lambda,u)\in\mathfrak{C}_{\kappa}
with
\lambda\in J
,

M(\lambda,u)\geq\omega
if
p\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Assume that there exists a sequence
\{(\lambda_{n},u_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathfrak{C}_{\kappa}^{+}
such that in some interval
(\alpha,\beta)
where
a(t)>0

\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset(\alpha,\beta)
such that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}x_{n}=x_{\infty}\in(\alpha,\beta),\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{\infty}\in(-\infty,\sigma_{\kappa}),

and

M(\lambda_{n},u_{n})=u_{n}(x_{n}):=\max_{t\in[\alpha,\beta]}u_{n}(t)\overset{n\uparrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}+\infty&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,p>1,\\
0&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,0<p<1.\end{array}\right.

Since
u_{n}(x_{n})>0
, there exists
r_{n}>0
such that

J_{n}:=(x_{n}-r_{n},x_{n}+r_{n})\subset(\alpha,\beta)

and
u_{n}(t)>0
for all

. Then, setting
\varrho_{n}:=M_{n}^{-\frac{p-1}{2}}
,
n\geq 1
, it is apparent that, for every
p>0
,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\varrho_{n}=0.

(2.22)

Now, for every

, by performing the change of variables

y:=\frac{t-x_{n}}{\varrho_{n}},\qquad\quad v_{n}(y):=\varrho_{n}^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{n}(t),\qquad n\geq 1,

(2.23)

it becomes apparent that, for every
n\geq 1
,

-\ddot{v}_{n}(y)=\lambda_{n}\varrho_{n}^{2}v_{n}(y)+a(x_{n}+\varrho_{n}y)|v_{n}(y)|^{p-1}v_{n}(y).

(2.24)

By (

) and the definitions of
M_{n}
and
\varrho_{n}
, it is easily seen that, for every

,

|v_{n}(y)|\leq 1\quad\hbox{and}\quad|v_{n}(0)|=1.

Thus,
v_{n}\not\equiv 0
is bounded for all
n\geq 1
. Consequently, by (
2.22
) and arguing as in
[
1
, Le. 4.2]
, along some subsequence, relabeled by
n\geq 1
, we have that

converges weakly to some positive function

such that

-\ddot{w}(y)=a(x_{\infty})|w(y)|^{p-1}w(y),\qquad w(0)=1.

(2.25)

Since

, the origin of (
2.25

infinitely many zeroes in
\mathbb{R}
, contradicting the fact that

is a non-trivial positive solution defined in
\mathbb{R}
. Adapting this argument, one can also get a contradiction when

. In such case,
(
2.25

\mathbb{R}
but only in

, or

is continuous

will be treated in a forthcoming paper.

This concludes the proof of the theorem if the maximum of the

’s is attained in some of the finite intervals of

, which is always the case if
\lambda_{n}<0
. Indeed, if
u_{n}
reaches its maximum at
x_{n}\in(0,L)
, then

0\leq-u^{\prime\prime}_{n}(x_{n})=\lambda_{n}u_{n}(x_{n})+a(x_{n})|u_{n}(x_{n})|^{p-1}u_{n}(x_{n}).

(2.26)

Thus,
a(x_{n})>0
if
\lambda_{n}<0
.

To complete the proof, suppose that
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
and that the maximum of
u_{n}
is attained in some
subinterval of
a^{-1}(0)

and
\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{+}(\lambda_{n},u_{n})=0

\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{t\in[t_{i},s_{i}]}|u_{n}(t)|=0

, for any given solution

, we are plotting

M_{+}(\lambda,u)
versus
\lambda

u^{\prime}(0)>0
, while

is plotted versus
M_{-}(\lambda,u)
if

.
Naturally, the “fictitious”  intersections between the components

3
Some preliminaries for the quasilinear prototype

Throughout the rest of this paper we will focus attention into the quasilinear equation

-(\phi(u^{\prime}))^{\prime}=\lambda u+a(t)g(u),\quad\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}},

(3.1)

already introduced in Section 1, where
a(t)
is a piece-wise constant
function in the class of (
1.2
). Thus,

a(t)\equiv\mu_{i}>0,\;\;\forall\,t\in[t_{i},s_{i}],\quad i\in\{0,...,m\}.

(3.2)

So,
a(t)
is a stepwise function with constant values
\mu_{i}>0
on the
I^{+}_{i}
-intervals.
In the special case when
\phi(s)=s
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, (
3.1
)
is a semilinear equation of the form (
2.1
). Thus, (
3.1
) can be regarded as a quasilinear generalized prototype of the generalized Moore–Nehari equation (
2.1
).

Our study of (
3.1
) in this paper will be performed by analyzing the dynamical system associated with the associated first order system

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}=h(y),\\
y^{\prime}=-\lambda x-a(t)g(x),\end{cases}

(3.3)

where
h(y):=\phi^{-1}(y)
, whose general properties were fixed in Section 1.
Under assumption (
3.2
), the system (
3.3
) can be seen
as a
switched system
, i.e., a system made of a superposition of
a fully nonlinear system,

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}=h(y),\\
y^{\prime}=-f_{i,\lambda}(x),\end{cases}

[formula]

where, for every
i\in\{0,...,m\}
,

f_{i,\lambda}(x):=\lambda x+\mu_{i}g(x),\quad\lambda,x\in\mathbb{R},

(3.4)

together with a linear system in the
x
-variable

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}=h(y),\\
y^{\prime}=-\lambda x.\end{cases}

[formula]

Since both
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
and
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})

are autonomous systems, we can equivalently assume that

({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
acts on a time-interval
[0,\tau_{i}]

with
\tau_{i}=s_{i}-t_{i}
and its effect is followed by that of the
system
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
acting on a time-interval
[0,\varsigma_{i}]

with
\varsigma_{i}=t_{i+1}-s_{i}
. In this framework, if we denote by
\Phi_{i}

the Poincaré map associated with the system
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})

on the interval
[0,\tau_{i}]
and by
\Psi_{i}
that one associated with system
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})

on the interval
[0,\varsigma_{i}]
, we have that the Poincaré map of (
3.3
) in
[0,L]

splits as

{\mathscr{P}}_{L}:=\Phi_{m}\circ\Psi_{m-1}\circ\Phi_{m-1}\circ\dots\circ\Psi_{0}\circ\Phi_{0}.

To make sure that the Poincaré map is well defined, we impose the uniqueness
of the solutions for the initial value problems associated with
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})

and
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
. Since we are dealing with planar Hamiltonian systems, the
uniqueness holds under very general conditions of
h
and
g
. If some extra assumptions would be required, we will highlight them when needed. Actually, we can directly apply a uniqueness result from Rebelo
[
43
]
if
\lambda>0
, and the same result applies when
\lambda=0
for the system
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
,
while for the Cauchy problem associated to
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
the uniqueness holds from an elementary direct argument. The situation might be more subtle when
\lambda<0
, where we need to prevent the
possibility that nontrivial solutions reach the origin in finite time. For instance, this could
happen for the system
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
for
\lambda=-1
and
h(y)=|y|^{q}{\rm sgn}(y)

with
0<q<1
. In the case
\lambda<0
the uniqueness can be inferred via the
strong maximum principle for
\phi
-Laplacian operators (see Pucci and Serrin
[
38
, Th. 1.1.1]
).

In this section, we are going to describe some general properties of the systems

({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
and
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
. Throughout the rest of this paper,
we will consider the primitives

H(y):=\int_{0}^{y}h(s)\,ds,\qquad F_{i,\lambda}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}f_{i,\lambda}(s)\,ds=\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}+\mu_{i}G(x),

where

G(x):=\int_{0}^{x}g(s)\,ds,

and we define

H(\varrho_{\pm})_{=}\lim_{y\to\varrho_{\pm}}H(y),

where
(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})
is the interval of definition of
h
.
Note that
H(\varrho_{\pm})=+\infty
if
\varrho_{\pm}=\pm\infty
.

3.1
A first glance at the system
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})

Observe that
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
is an Hamiltonian system with

{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)={\mathcal{H}}_{i,\lambda}(x,y):=H(y)+F_{i,\lambda}(x),\quad\text{for }\;(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+}).

Hamiltonian functions “with separable variables”  of this form
have been already studied in Schaaf
[
44
,
45
]
, Cima et al.
[
6
]
, and, more recently, in Villadelprat and Zhang
[
47
]
, where the reader is sent for some recent studies in this area.
Given
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

0<c<H^{*}:=\min\{H(\varrho_{-}),H(\varrho_{+})\},

(3.5)

assume that there are constants
x_{\pm}(c)
, with
x_{-}(c)<0<x_{+}(c)
, such that

\begin{split}&F_{i,\lambda}(x_{-}(c))=F_{i,\lambda}(x_{+}(c))=c,\\
&F_{i,\lambda}(x)<c\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;x\in(x_{-}(c),x_{+}(c)),\\
&F^{\prime}_{i,\lambda}(x_{-}(c))<0<F^{\prime}_{i,\lambda}(x_{+}(c))\end{split}

(3.6)

Thus, the level line
{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)=c
is a simple closed curve around the origin
intersecting the
x
-axis at the points
(x_{\pm}(c),0)
and the
y
-axis at the points
(0,y_{\pm}(c))
,
where
y_{-}(c)<0<y_{+}(c)
and
H(y_{\pm}(c))=c
. Since
\mathcal{H}^{-1}(c)
does not contain
equilibrium points of the system
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
, it is a periodic orbit running clockwise.
The next result shows that the existence of a
c\in(0,H^{*})
for which (
3.6
) holds is ensured by a more general condition.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
F_{i,\lambda}(x^{\pm})>0
for some
x^{-}<0<x^{+}
. Then, there exists an interval
[c_{1},c_{2}]\subset(0,H^{*})
such that, for every
c\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
, there are
x_{\pm}(c)
satisfying (
3.6
).

Proof.

Since
F_{i,\lambda}(0)=0
and
F_{i,\lambda}
is continuous, for every
c\in(0,\min\{H^{*},F_{i,\lambda}(x^{\pm})\})
, there exist
x_{\pm}(c)
satisfying the first two relations of (
3.6
). Moreover, since
F_{i,\lambda}
is of class
C^{1}
, by applying Sard’s theorem, the existence of a regular value
\tilde{c}
of
F_{i,\lambda}
in
(0,\min\{H^{*},F_{i,\lambda}(x^{\pm})\})
holds. Thus, there exist
x_{\pm}(\tilde{c})
satisfying (
3.6
). Finally, since
F_{i,\lambda}
is
C^{1}
, there exists a closed neighborhood
[c_{1},c_{2}]
of
\tilde{c}
where (
3.6
) holds.

\square

To determine the period of the orbit
\mathcal{H}^{-1}(c)
, we can sum up the
times needed to cross each quadrant. First, from the relation

H(y)+F_{i,\lambda}(x)=c
, since
x^{\prime}=h(y)
, we find that

x^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})(c-F_{i,\lambda}(x))&\text{if}\;x>0,\\[3.0pt]
(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})(c-F_{i,\lambda}(x))&\text{if}\;x<0,\end{array}\right.

where
H_{+}^{-1}
and
H_{-}^{-1}
are the inverse functions of
H

restricted to
[0,\varrho_{+})
and
(\varrho_{-},0]
, respectively. From this, we
obtain that the necessary time to move from
(0,y_{+}(c))
to
(x_{+}(c),0)
in the first quadrant
is given by

{\mathcal{T}}_{i,I}(c):=\int_{0}^{x_{+}(c)}\frac{dx}{(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})(c-F_{i,\lambda}(x))}.

Similarly, the times to cross the fourth, third and second quadrants, can be expressed as

{\mathcal{T}}_{i,IV}(c):=\int_{0}^{x_{+}(c)}\frac{dx}{-(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})(c-F_{i,\lambda}(x))},

{\mathcal{T}}_{i,III}(c):=\int_{x_{-}(c)}^{0}\frac{dx}{-(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})(c-F_{i,\lambda}(x))}

and

{\mathcal{T}}_{i,II}(c):=\int_{x_{-}(c)}^{0}\frac{dx}{(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})(c-F_{i,\lambda}(x))},

respectively. Obviously, the period of the closed orbit
corresponding to the level line
{\mathcal{H}}=c
is given by

{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(c)={\mathcal{T}}_{i,I}(c)+{\mathcal{T}}_{i,IV}(c)+{\mathcal{T}}_{i,III}(c)+{\mathcal{T}}_{i,II}(c).

Observe that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{i,I}(c)={\mathcal{T}}_{i,IV}(c)\;\;\text{and }\;\;{\mathcal{T}}_{i,II}(c)={\mathcal{T}}_{i,III}(c)\quad\text{if $h$ is odd},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{i,I}(c)={\mathcal{T}}_{i,II}(c)\;\;\text{and }\;\;{\mathcal{T}}_{i,III}(c)={\mathcal{T}}_{i,IV}(c)\quad\text{if $g$ is odd},

so that all the quarter-laps times coincide with
{\mathcal{T}}_{i,I}(c)

if both
h
and
g
are odd.
The formulas for the times
{\mathcal{T}}_{i,j}(c)
have been already given in

[
6
, Pr. 2.4]
.

Observe that in the case
\lambda\geq 0
there is always an interval
[c_{1},c_{2}]\subset(0,H^{*})
for which
(
3.6
) holds on
x_{\pm}(c)
for every
c\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
. The case
\lambda<0
is more delicate. First, as the existence of such an interval is not a priori ensured, the existence of
x^{\pm}
in the statement of Lemma
3.1
should be imposed when working in the interval
[t_{i},s_{i}]
with the problem
(\mathcal{N}_{i,\lambda})
. Second, the geometric structure of the orbits can change completely depending on the sublinear/superlinear behavior of
g(x)
near zero and/or infinity, as one can see, by analyzing the function

f_{i,\lambda}(x)=\lambda x+\mu_{i}|x|^{p-1}x

for
p>1
or
0<p<1
.

3.2
A first glance at the system
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})

In this section, we evaluate the necessary time to move along the trajectories
of the
x
-linear system
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
.
The trajectories lie on the level lines of the Hamiltonian
function

{\mathcal{E}}(x,y)={\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}(x,y):=H(y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2},\quad\text{for }\;(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+}).

The dynamics changes substantially according to the sign of the parameter
\lambda
. Thus,
we will consider three different cases.

•

The case
\lambda<0
.

Then, the origin is a saddle point whose stable and unstable
manifolds lie on the level line of zero-energy, given by
2H(y)=-\lambda x^{2}
. The level lines
with positive energy correspond to trajectories in the upper half-plane
moving from the second towards the first quadrant, and to trajectories in the lower half-plane
moving from the fourth towards the third quadrant. Let us consider now the energy level lines passing through the points
(0,y_{+})
and
(0,y_{-})
with
\varrho_{-}<y_{-}<0<y_{+}<\varrho_{+}
,
namely

H(y)=H(y_{\pm})-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}.

Then, either

x^{\prime}=(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})\left(H(y_{+})-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}\right),\quad\hbox{or}\quad x^{\prime}=(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})\left(H(y_{-})-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}\right).

Thus, for any given
\ell<r
, the transition times from the straight line

x=\ell
to the straight line
x=r
, with
y>0
, and from the straight line

x=r
to the straight line
x=\ell
, with
y<0
, are given by

{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}(\ell,r;(0,y_{+})):=\int_{\ell}^{r}\frac{dx}{(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})\left(H(y_{+})-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}\right)}

(3.7)

and

{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}(r,\ell;(0,y_{-})):=\int_{\ell}^{r}\frac{dx}{-(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})\left(H(y_{-})-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}\right)},

(3.8)

respectively. In order to have the arc of trajectory well defined between these two straight lines,
it is required that

\max\left\{H(y_{\pm})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\ell^{2},H(y_{\pm})-\frac{\lambda}{2}r^{2}\right\}<H(\varrho_{\pm}).

The level lines with negative energy correspond to trajectories on the right half-plane
moving from the fourth towards the first quadrant, and to trajectories on the left half-plane
moving from the second towards the third quadrant.
Figure
5
shows the deformation of some regions under the flow associated
with
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
for
\lambda<0
.

Figure 5
:
The action of the Poicaré map.

Precisely, Figure
5
shows the deformation of two trapezoidal regions
under the action of the Poincaré map associated with
({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda})
.
For this particular example we have chosen
h(y)=y
and
\lambda=-1/2
, for
a time interval of length
\varsigma=1.5
. The two trapezoids are originally supported on each of the
two components of the stable manifold through the origin, which is the straight
line
y=-\sqrt{|\lambda|}x
. As an effect of the Poincaré map, the segments on the components of
the stable manifold are shifted towards the origin, while the remaining points of the trapezoids
move towards the unstable manifold
y=\sqrt{|\lambda|}x
.

Next, we will consider the energy level lines passing through the points

(x_{\pm},0)
, with
x_{-}<0<x_{+}
, namely

H(y)=\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{\pm}^{2}-x^{2}).

Then, either

x^{\prime}=(h\circ H_{\pm}^{-1})\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{+}^{2}-x^{2})\right),\quad\hbox{or}\quad x^{\prime}=(h\circ H_{\pm}^{-1})\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{-}^{2}-x^{2})\right),

and hence, for any given
r_{1},r_{2}>x_{+}
and
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}<x_{-}
, the transition time between the straight lines
x=r_{1}
, with
y<0
, and
x=r_{2}
, with
y>0
, along the respective energy level lines, is given by

{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}(r_{1},r_{2};(x_{+},0)):=\!\!\int_{x_{+}}^{r_{2}}\frac{dx}{(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{+}^{2}-x^{2})\right)}\!+\!\int_{x_{+}}^{r_{1}}\frac{dx}{-(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{+}^{2}-x^{2})\right)}.

(3.9)

Similarly, the transition time between the straight line
x=\ell_{1}
, with
y>0
, and
x=\ell_{2}
, with
y>0
, is given through

{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2};(x_{-},0)):=\!\!\int_{\ell_{1}}^{x_{-}}\frac{dx}{(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{-}^{2}-x^{2})\right)}\!+\!\int_{\ell_{2}}^{x_{-}}\frac{dx}{-(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{-}^{2}-x^{2})\right)}.

(3.10)

The next compatibility condition

\max\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{+}^{2}-r_{j}^{2}),\frac{\lambda}{2}(x_{-}^{2}-\ell_{j}^{2})\right\}<H^{*},\quad\text{for }\;j=1,2,

makes sure that the arc of trajectory between each of these two lines is contained in the strip

{\mathbb{R}}\times(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})
.

•

The case
\lambda=0
.

In this case, the system
({\mathcal{L}}_{0})
reduces to
x^{\prime}=h(y)
,
y^{\prime}=0
. Thus,
the
x
-axis consists of equilibria. Moreover,
the motion has a constant speed,
h(y)
, on parallel lines to the
x
-axis
from left to the right, or the right to the left, according to whether
y>0
, or
y<0
.
Figure
6
shows the deformation of some rectangles by the action of the flow
induced by
({\mathcal{L}}_{0})
.

:

({\mathcal{L}}_{0})
.

Figure
6
shows the deformation of two rectangles
under the action of the Poincaré map associated with
({\mathcal{L}}_{0})
.
For this particular example we have chosen
h(y)=y/\sqrt{1+y^{2}}
, which comes
from inverting the relativistic operator

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-(x^{\prime})^{2}}}\bigr)
, for
a time interval of length
\varsigma=6
. The rectangles are
[-2,-1]\times[0,4]
and
[2,3]\times[-5,0]
.

Let us consider the orbits passing through the points

(0,y_{+})
and
(0,y_{-})
with
\varrho_{-}<y_{-}<0<y_{+}<\varrho_{+}
. From
x^{\prime}=h(y_{\pm})
we find that, for any given
\ell<r
, the transition times from the straight line

x=\ell
to the straight line
x=r
with
y>0
, and from
x=r
to
x=\ell
with

, are given by

respectively.

•

The case
\lambda>0
.

This situation is reminiscent of the case already analyzed for the system

({\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda})
. Indeed, the origin is a center because, for every
c\in(0,H^{*})
, the integral curve
{\mathcal{E}}_{\lambda}=c
is a periodic orbit surrounding the origin that is symmetric with respect to the
y
-axis. This curve intersects the
x
-axis at the points
(x_{\pm}(c),0)
with

x_{\pm}(c)=\pm(2c/\lambda)^{1/2}
, and the
y
-axis at
(0,H^{-1}_{+}(c))
and
(0,H^{-1}_{-}(c))
.
Thus, by adapting the formulas for the times
{\mathcal{T}}_{i,j}
,
j\in\{I,II,III,IV\}
, needed to cross each quadrant, after a simple change of variable, we find that

{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}_{I}(c):=(2c/\lambda)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{(h\circ H_{+}^{-1})(c(1-x^{2}))}

is the crossing time of the first quadrant, and

{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}_{IV}(c):=(2c/\lambda)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{-(h\circ H_{-}^{-1})(c(1-x^{2}))}

is the crossing time of the fourth quadrant. By the symmetry with respect to the
y
-axis,
the crossing times of the third and second quadrants are given by

{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}_{II}(c)={\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}_{I}(c)\quad\text{ and }\;\;{\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}_{III}(c)={\mathcal{S}}^{\lambda}_{IV}(c),

respectively.

4
Transformation of arcs in
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})

This section analyzes the effect of the transformation of certain arcs through the Poincaré map

\Phi_{i}
,
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
. To describe the movement of those arcs, we need to introduce some regions and a few notations. As these regions are always defined in the domain

{\mathbb{R}}\times(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})
, throughout this section (
3.5
) is required to be satisfied.

Let
0<c_{1,i}<c_{2,i}
be such that, for every
c\in[c_{1,i},c_{2,i}]
, the condition
(
3.6
) is satisfied. Then, the annulus

{\mathcal{A}}_{i}:=\{(x,y):c_{1,i}\leq{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)\leq c_{2,i}\}

consists of the union of the periodic orbits
{\mathcal{H}}(x,y)=c
of the system
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
for
c\in[c_{1,i},c_{2,i}]
. As these orbits surround the origin, also
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
is “centered ” at the origin. Next, we split
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
into four rectangular
regions,
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}
, with
j\in\{I,II,III,IV\}
, where the index
j
corresponds to the
intersection of
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
with the closed
j
-quadrant. For each of these
regions, we select an orientation, by fixing two opposite sides,
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}^{-}
, that we denote as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\rm energy}_{i,j}:=({\mathcal{A}}_{i,j},{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}^{-}),\quad\hbox{where}\;\;{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}^{-}:={\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}\cap\left(\{{\mathcal{H}}=c_{1,i}\}\cup\{{\mathcal{H}}=c_{2,i}\}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,j}:=({\mathcal{A}}_{i,j},{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}^{-}),\quad\hbox{where}\;\;{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}^{-}:={\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}\cap\bigl(\{xy=0\}\bigr).

For each of the
four regions represented on each picture of Figure
7
we have
drawn also an arc connecting the two opposite sides of

{\mathcal{A}}_{i,j}^{-}
.

Figure 7
:
The oriented regions

\mathcal{A}^{\rm energy}_{i,j}
(left) and

\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,j}
(right).

To generate Figure
7
, we we have taken
h(y)=\log(\varrho_{+}/(\varrho_{+}-y))
, with
\varrho_{+}=4
,
which is an increasing homeomorphism
(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, for

\varrho_{-}=-\infty
, with
h(0)=0
,
g(x)=-1+e^{x}
, and
f_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda x+\mu g(x)
with
\lambda=-1/20
and
\mu=4/5
. For this particular choice of
h
,
g
and
\lambda
, the
integral curves of
{\mathcal{H}}
are closed trajectories around the origin contained
in the domain
{\mathbb{R}}\times(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})
only for sufficiently small
c>0
.

Throughout the rest of this paper, given two oriented rectangular
regions,
({\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{R}}^{-})
and
({\mathcal{Q}},{\mathcal{Q}}^{-})
, and a homeomorphism
\eta
between these two
rectangles
, by setting

\eta:({\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{R}}^{-})\overset{\mathfrak{m}}{\Bumpeq{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\longrightarrow}}}({\mathcal{Q}},{\mathcal{Q}}^{-})\quad(\mathfrak{m}\geq 1)

we mean that there are
\mathfrak{m}
compact disjoint sets
K_{1},\ldots,K_{\mathfrak{m}}

contained in
{\mathcal{R}}
such that any arc
\gamma
contained in

{\mathcal{R}}
and linking the two opposite sides of
{\mathcal{R}}^{-}

contains
\mathfrak{m}
sub-arcs
\gamma_{j}\subset K_{j}
,
j\in\{1,...,\mathfrak{m}\}
,
such that, for every
j\in\{1,...,\mathfrak{m}\}
,
\eta(\gamma_{j})
is an arc contained in
{\mathcal{Q}}

connecting the two opposite sides of
{\mathcal{Q}}^{-}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{m}

can be regarded as a sort of
crossing number
, as discussed by Kennedy and Yorke
[
15
]
. When
\mathfrak{m}=1
, we omit it and simply set
\Bumpeq{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\longrightarrow}}
.

Once guaranteed that the annulus
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
is properly defined as a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}\times(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})
, the Poincaré map
\Phi_{i}
of
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
induces an homeomorphism of the annulus onto itself. Now, we will impose a suitable twist condition on the
boundaries of
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
in order to prove that an arc connecting
the inner with the outer boundaries of the annulus stretches into a spiral like curve
winding a certain number of times across the annulus itself, in order to prove
a property of the form

\mathcal{A}^{\rm energy}_{i,j^{\prime}}\overset{\mathfrak{m}}{\Bumpeq{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\longrightarrow}}}\,\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,j^{\prime\prime}}

for a suitable choice of

\mathfrak{m}
,
j^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime\prime}
. This is the main result of this section. Precisely, the following result holds. Recall that
the Poincaré map
\Phi_{i}
acts on a time-interval of length
\tau_{i}=s_{i}-t_{i}
for system

({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Given the cyclic clockwise order
IV\prec III\prec II\prec I\prec IV
and
d,e\in\{c_{1,i},c_{2,i}\}
with
d\not=e
, assume that, for every
\kappa\in\{IV,III,II,I\}
, and, for some non-negative integers

0\leq\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}
, the following twist condition is satisfied

\begin{split}&{\mathcal{T}}_{i,\kappa+1}(d)+{\mathcal{T}}_{i,\kappa+2}(d)+\alpha_{i}{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(d)>\tau_{i},\\
&{\mathcal{T}}_{i,\kappa}(e)+{\mathcal{T}}_{i,\kappa+1}(e)+\beta_{i}{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(e)<\tau_{i}.\end{split}

(4.1)

Then,

\Phi_{i}:\mathcal{A}^{\rm energy}_{i,\kappa}\overset{\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}}{\Bumpeq{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\longrightarrow}}}\,\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,\kappa-1}\cup\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,\kappa+1}.

(4.2)

Furthermore, fixing
\kappa=I\;\hbox{or}\;III
, for every
j=\{1,2,\ldots,2(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})\}
, there is at least one solution of
(\mathcal{N}_{i,\lambda})
starting at
\mathcal{A}_{i,\kappa}
and having exactly

(i)

2\alpha_{i}+j
zeroes in the
x
-component in the interval
(0,\tau_{i})
, and

(ii)

2\alpha_{i}+j+1
zeroes in the
y
-component in
[0,\tau_{i}]
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
4.1
, which is divided into two parts. The first part shows (
4.2
). The second one holds from a technical lemma where
the nodal behavior of the solutions starting at
\mathcal{A}_{i,\kappa}
for
\kappa\in\{I,III\}
is described. This provides us with the multiplicity result of the zeroes in the
x
and
y
components
stated in the proposition. A similar result holds for solutions starting at
\mathcal{A}_{i,\kappa}
with
\kappa\in\{II,IV\}
. However, as it will become apparent in later sections, we are just interested in the nodal behavior of the solutions starting at
\mathcal{A}_{i,I}
or
\mathcal{A}_{i,III}
.

Proof.

Suppose (
4.1
). We will detail the proof of (
4.2
) in the case
\kappa=I
, as in the
remaining cases follows easily by symmetry. Just to fix ideas, suppose that

d=c_{1,i}
and
e=c_{2,i}
(the other case is analogous).

Using a system of polar coordinates with the angle
counted in the clockwise sense starting from the positive
y
-axis, we can associate to the solutions of

(\mathcal{N}_{i,\lambda})
an angular component
\theta(t;z)
, with
t\in[0,\tau_{i}]
and
z\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}
representing the angular coordinate of the solution at time
t
, starting
at the point
z
at time
0
. The map
\theta
is continuous, and
0\leq\theta(0,z)\leq\pi/2
, for all
z\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}
.

Let
\gamma
be a continuous arc of curve contained in
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}
linking the two sides of

{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}^{-}
which are the part of the level lines
{\mathcal{H}}=c_{1,i}
and

{\mathcal{H}}=c_{2,i}
in the first quadrant. The arc
\gamma

is the homeomorphic image of the compact interval
[0,1]
through a homeomorphism,
still denoted by
\gamma:[0,1]\to{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}
, such that
\gamma(0)=z_{0}\in\{{\mathcal{H}}=c_{1,i}\}
and
\gamma(1)=z_{1}\in\{{\mathcal{H}}=c_{2,i}\}
. Without loss of generality,
we can assume that
c_{1,i}<{\mathcal{H}}(\gamma(s))<c_{2,i}
for all
s\in(0,1)
.
Now, consider the image of
\gamma=\gamma([0,1])
through the Poincaré map
\Phi_{i}
. Then,

\vartheta(t,s):=\theta(t;\gamma(s)),\quad t\in[0,\tau_{i}],\;\;s\in[0,1],

represents the angular coordinate of the solution of
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})
at time
t
, starting
at
\gamma(s)
(at time
0
). The map
\vartheta
is continuous and satisfies

0\leq\vartheta(0,s)\leq\pi/2
for all
s\in[0,1]
. Moreover, for each nonnegative integer
k
, as soon as
c=\mathcal{H}(\gamma(s))
, we have that

{\mathcal{T}}_{i,IV}(c)+{\mathcal{T}}_{i,III}(c)+k{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(c)>t\;\Longrightarrow\;\vartheta(t,s)<\frac{3}{2}\pi+2k\pi,

(4.3)

{\mathcal{T}}_{i,I}(c)+{\mathcal{T}}_{i,IV}(c)+k{\mathcal{T}}_{i}(c)<t\;\Longrightarrow\;\vartheta(t,s)>\pi+2k\pi.

(4.4)

Thus, from (
4.3
) and the first condition of (
4.1
), we find that

\vartheta(\tau_{i},0)<\frac{3}{2}\pi+2\alpha_{i}\pi.

On the other hand, from (
4.4
) and the second condition of (
4.1
), it becomes apparent
that

\vartheta(\tau_{i},1)>\pi+2\beta_{i}\pi.

Thus, the continuous map

[0,1]\ni s\mapsto\vartheta(\tau_{i},s)
covers the whole compact interval

\left[\tfrac{3}{2}\pi+2\alpha_{i}\pi,\pi+2\beta_{i}\pi\right].

Hence, there are
2(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})
pairwise disjoint
compact subintervals of
[0,1]
such that this map covers, for every
k\in\{\alpha_{i},\dots,\beta_{i}-1\}
, the intervals

\left[\tfrac{3}{2}\pi+2k\pi,2(k+1)\pi\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\pi+2(k+1)\pi,\pi+2(k+1)\pi\right].

Now, we introduce the sets

\begin{split}&K_{j,II}:=\left\{z\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}:\,\theta(\tau_{i};z)\in\left[\tfrac{3}{2}\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j-1)\pi,2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi\right]\right\},\\[3.0pt]
&K_{j,IV}:=\left\{z\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}:\,\theta(\tau_{i};z)\in\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi,\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi\right]\right\},\end{split}

(4.5)

for
j=1,\dots,\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i},
which are compact, nonempty and pairwise
disjoint. By a simple continuity argument, given an arbitrary arc
\gamma

in
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,I}
connecting the two opposite sides of
\mathcal{A}^{\rm energy}_{i,I}
,
it is easily seen that, for every
\kappa\in\{II,IV\}
, there are
\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}
sub-arcs
\gamma_{j,\kappa}\subset K_{j,\kappa}
such that
\Phi_{i}(\gamma_{j,\kappa})

is an arc contained in
{\mathcal{A}}_{i,\kappa}
linking the two sides
y=0
and
x=0
of

\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,\kappa}
. This proves that

\Phi_{i}:\mathcal{A}^{\rm energy}_{i,I}\overset{\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}}{\Bumpeq{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\longrightarrow}}}\,\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,II}\cup\mathcal{A}^{\rm axis}_{i,IV}

according to our definition. The multiplicity results of the zeroes
of the solutions stated in the second part of the proposition holds as a direct consequence of the next technical lemma, which completes the proof of the proposition.

\square

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
\kappa\in\{I,III\}
, and any arc
\gamma\subset\mathcal{A}_{i,\kappa}^{\rm energy}
joining the opposite sides of
\mathcal{A}_{i,\kappa}^{-}
, the solutions of
({\mathcal{N}}_{i,\lambda})

with initial value
on any sub-arc
\gamma_{j,\tilde{\kappa}}\subset K_{j,\tilde{\kappa}}
of
\gamma
have exactly, for every
j=1,2,\ldots,\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}
,

\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}2(\alpha_{i}+j)-1&\;\hbox{zeroes in the x-component in}\;\;(0,\tau_{i})\\
2(\alpha_{i}+j)&\;\hbox{zeroes in the y-component in}\;\;[0,\tau_{i}]\end{array}\right\}\;\hbox{if}\;\;\tilde{\kappa}=\kappa-1

and

\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}2(\alpha_{i}+j)&\;\hbox{zeroes in the x-component in}\;\;(0,\tau_{i})\\
2(\alpha_{i}+j)+1&\;\hbox{zeroes in the y-component in}\;\;[0,\tau_{i}]\end{array}\right\}\;\hbox{if}\;\;\tilde{\kappa}=\kappa+1.

Proof.

We focus our attention on case
\kappa=I
, since the proof for
\kappa=III
is completely analogous. By the definition of
\vartheta(t,s)
, we have that
0\leq\vartheta(0,s)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
for all
s\in[0,1]
.
Moreover, if the arc
\gamma_{j,II}\subset K_{j,II}
is parameterized on a subinterval

[s^{0}_{j,II},s^{1}_{j,II}]
of
[0,1]
, then

\tfrac{3}{2}\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j-1)\pi\leq\vartheta(\tau_{i},s)\leq 2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi,

with

\vartheta(\tau_{i},s^{0}_{j,II})=\tfrac{3}{2}\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j-1)\pi,\qquad\vartheta(\tau_{i},s^{1}_{j,II})=2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi.

Thus, for every
z\in\gamma_{j}
, the solution
(x(t;z),y(t;z))
satisfies:

•

x(t;z)
has exactly
2(\alpha_{i}+j)-1
zeroes in the interval
(0,\tau_{i})
,

•

y(t,z)
, as well as
x^{\prime}(t;z)
, has exactly
2(\alpha_{i}+j)
zeroes in the interval
[0,\tau_{i}]
.

Similarly, if the arc
\gamma_{j,IV}\subset K_{j,IV}
is parameterized on a subinterval

[s^{0}_{j,IV},s^{1}_{j,IV}]
of
[0,1]
,

\tfrac{1}{2}\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi\leq\vartheta(\tau_{i},s)\leq\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi,

with

\vartheta(\tau_{i},s^{0}_{j,IV})=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi,\qquad\vartheta(\tau_{i},s^{1}_{j,IV})=\pi+2(\alpha_{i}+j)\pi.

Therefore, for every
z\in\gamma_{j}
, the solution
(x(t;z),y(t;z))
satisfies:

•

x(t,z)
has exactly
2(\alpha_{i}+j)
zeroes in the interval
(0,\tau_{i})
,

•

y(t,z)
, as well as
x^{\prime}
, has exactly
2(\alpha_{i}+j)+1
zeroes in the interval
[0,\tau_{i}]
.

This end the proof.

\square

6
Linking dynamics in general Sturm–Liouville BVP’s

In this section we show some applications of the preceding results to the existence
of multiple solutions to (

) satisfying prescribed boundary conditions. In our analysis, we will restrict ourselves to consider regular Sturm–Liouville boundary value problems.

From a geometrical point of view, the homogeneous boundary conditions of Sturm–Liouville type can be described by, e.g., assigning two lines
r_{0}
and

passing through the origin and looking for solutions of (

and

{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{m}

and

two general curves crossing

and
{\mathcal{A}}_{m}

See Papini and Zanolin
[
37

and the references therein for further
details on generalized boundary conditions of Sturm–Liouville type.

More precisely, we will study the boundary value problem

is an arc contained in
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
and

is an arc contained in

,

inner with the outer boundary of the corresponding annulus

{\mathbb{R}}\times(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})
and, when
\lambda>0

with respect to (
