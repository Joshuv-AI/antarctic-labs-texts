Abstract
We introduce continuous R R -valuations on directed-complete posets (dcpos, for short), as a generalization of continuous valuations in domain theory, by extending values of continuous valuations from reals to so-called Abelian d-rags R R . Like the valuation monad 𝐕 \mathbf{V} introduced by Jones and Plotkin, we show that the construction of continuous R R -valuations extends to a strong monad 𝐕 R \mathbf{V}^{R} on the category of dcpos and Scott-continuous maps. Additionally, and as in recent work by the two authors and C. Théron, and by the second author, B. Lindenhovius, M. Mislove and V. Zamdzhiev, we show that we can extract a commutative monad 𝐕 m R \mathbf{V}^{R}_{m} out of it, whose elements we call minimal R R -valuations. We also show that continuous R R -valuations have close connections to measures when R R is taken to be 𝐈 ​ ℝ + ⋆ \mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star} , the interval domain of the extended nonnegative reals: (1) On every coherent topological space, every non-zero, bounded τ \tau -smooth measure μ \mu (defined on the Borel σ \sigma -algebra), canonically determines a continuous 𝐈 ​ ℝ + ⋆ \mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star} -valuation; and (2) such a continuous 𝐈 ​ ℝ + ⋆ \mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star} -valuation is the most precise (in a certain sense) continuous 𝐈 ​ ℝ + ⋆ \mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star} -valuation that approximates μ \mu , when the support of μ \mu is a compact Hausdorff subspace of a second-countable stably compact topological space. This in particular applies to Lebesgue measure on the unit interval. As a result, the Lebesgue measure can be identified as a continuous 𝐈 ​ ℝ + ⋆ \mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star} -valuation. Additionally, we show that the latter is minimal.

1
Introduction

The probability of an event is most often than not understood as a
real number between
0
and
1
, and measures, as well as continuous
valuations, take their values in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, the set of non-negative
real numbers extended with
+\infty
. What is there that is so
special with real numbers, and can we replace
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
by some
elements in some other structure? The question was once asked by
Vincent Danos to the first author, and came back to the authors in an
attempt to formulate an alternative to measures and continuous
valuations with values taken as
exact reals
, in the sense of
Real PCF
[
10
,
9
,
8
,
24
]
for example.
Exact reals are modeled there as
intervals
that enclose the
true value that is intended, and computation proceeds by refining
these intervals further and further. Indeed, one of
the points of this paper is that we can extend continuous valuations
to an interval-valued form of continuous valuations, with an
interval-valued integration theory. In addition, this also leads us to commutative
valuations monads with intervals as values on the category of dcpos
and Scott-continuous maps.

We should warn the reader that such an endeavor is probably useless
for computation purposes. In the setting of type 2 theory of
effectivity, Weihrauch has shown that, under reasonable assumptions,
the map that sends a representable measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
to

\mu[0,1/2)
cannot be continuous if the target space
[0,1]
is
given the Scott topology of the reverse ordering
\geq

[
27
, Theorem 2.7]
. (It is continuous with respect to
the usual ordering
\leq
. In passing, type 2 theory of effectivity
on the reals does not differ much from ordinary domain-based notions
of computability, as Schulz has shown
[
25
]
.)
This roughly means that if we insist on representing
\mu[0,1/2)
as

precise
intervals, namely as intervals
[a,a]
with the same
left and right bounds, then the rightmost
a
will evolve in a
discontinuous manner.

Despite this, we will show in Section
7
that
quite a lot of measures (in the ordinary sense) have representations
as interval-valued “measures” with
precise
intervals; see
Remark
7.13
, in particular. Before that, we will have to
define what we mean by “measure” with values in a domain of
intervals. We will start (after recapitulating some preliminary
notions and results in Section
2
) by giving a
pretty general possible answer to V. Danos’ question in
Section
3
: we may safely replace
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
by any
structure of a kind that we call an
Abelian d-rag
, which is a
weaker form of Abelian semiring, with a compatible ordering that turns
it into a dcpo. This will allow us to define a notion of

continuous
R
-valuation
on a space
X
, for any Abelian d-rag

R
, in Section
4
. The obvious definition would
be as a function from the open subsets of
X
satisfying certain
requirements, but those requirements have proved elusive, especially
in Abelian d-rags where the additive zero
0
differs from the bottom
element
\bot
, as with the domain of intervals. For example, would
you define the measure of the empty set as
0
or as
\bot
? One is
needed for algebraic reasoning, so that adding the measure of the
empty set does nothing; the other is needed for approximation
purposes, e.g., in order to define the integral of a function
f
as
the supremum of simpler sums. We sidestep the issue by defining our
continuous R-valuations as being directly the functionals that one
would usually obtain by defining an integral out of a measure. In
Section
5
, we show that continuous
R-valuations, much like continuous valuations
[
18
,
17
]
,
can be organized to form a strong monad on the category
{\mathbf{Dcpo}}
of
dcpos and Scott-continuous maps. Furthermore, as in

[
14
]
and
[
16
]
, one can
carve out a
commutative
monad of so-called
minimal

R
-valuations from the latter. We start to examine the relationship
between measures and continuous
R
-valuations when
R
is either

\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
or the interval domain
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
in
Section
6
. That section is devoted to a few
simple facts, and notably to the fact that every continuous

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation induces an ordinary continuous
(
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
-)valuation, which we call it view from the left. In
Section
7
, we will see that every non-zero,
bounded
\tau
-smooth measure
\mu
on a coherent topological space
gives rise to a continuous
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation
\widetilde{\mu}
in a natural
way, and that
\widetilde{\mu}
is
precise
in the sense alluded to
above. In Section
8
, under slightly different
assumptions, we study the continuous
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuations that
approximate a given, not necessarily bounded, measure, and we show
that there is a

one; it so happens that this is

\widetilde{\mu}
, once again. In all those cases, there is no reason why

\widetilde{\mu}
should be minimal. In
Section

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation

. We note that

\widetilde{\lambda}

Preliminaries

We refer to
[
3
]
for basics of measure theory, and to

[
1
,

,

]
for basics of domain theory and

Measure theory.

A
\sigma
-algebra
on a set
X
is a collection of subsets
closed under countable unions and complements. A
measurable
space

X
is a set with a
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma_{X}
. The
elements of
\Sigma_{X}
are usually called the
measurable
subsets
of
X
.

A
measure

\mu
on
X
is a
\sigma
-additive map from

\Sigma_{X}
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
, where
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is the set of extended
non-negative real numbers . The property of

\sigma
-additivity
means that, for every countable family of
pairwise disjoint sets
E_{n}
,

\mu(\bigcup_{n}E_{n})=\sum_{n}\mu(E_{n})
. (Here
n
ranges over any
subset of
{\rm Nature}
, possibly empty.)

A
measurable map

f\colon X\to Y
between measurable spaces
is a map such that
f^{-1}(E)\in\Sigma_{X}
for every

E\in\Sigma_{Y}
. The
image measure

f[\mu]
of a measure

\mu
on
X
is defined by
f[\mu](E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mu(f^{-1}(E))
.

The
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma(A)

generated by
a family
A
of
subsets of
X
is the the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing
A
.
The
Borel
\sigma
-algebra
on a topological space is the

\sigma
-algebra generated by its topology. The
standard
topology
on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is generated by the intervals
[0,b[
,

]a,b[
and
]a,+\infty
, with
0<a<b<+\infty
. Its Borel

\sigma
-algebra is also generated by the intervals
]a,+\infty]

along (the Scott-open subsets, see below). Hence a measurable map

h\colon X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a map such that

h^{-1}(]t,+\infty])\in\Sigma
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Its

Lebesgue integral
can be defined elegantly through

Choquet’s formula
:

\int_{X}hd\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mu(h^{-1}(]t,+\infty]))dt
, where the right-hand integral is an ordinary Riemann integral.

This formula makes the following
change-of-variables formula
an
easy observation: for every measurable map
f\colon X\to Y
, for
every measurable map
h\colon Y\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

\int_{Y}hdf[\mu]=\int_{X}(h\circ f)d\mu
.

The
monotone convergence theorem
states that, given any measure

\mu
on a measurable space
X
, given any sequence

{(h_{n})}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
of measurable maps from
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
that is
pointwise monotonic, their pointwise supremum
h
is measurable, and

\int_{X}hd\mu=\sup_{n\in{\rm Nature}}\int_{X}h_{n}d\mu
. If

{(h_{n})}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
is antitonic instead, then a similar theorem
holds provided that
\int_{X}h_{n}d\mu<+\infty
for some
n\in{\rm Nature}

(but not in general): the pointwise infimum
h
is measurable, and

\int_{X}hd\mu=\inf_{n\in{\rm Nature}}\int_{X}h_{n}d\mu
.

A practical way of building measures is
Carathéodory’s measure
existence theorem
, which is the following. A
semi-ring

\mathcal{R}
on
X
is a collection of subsets of
X
that is closed
under finite intersections, and such that the complement of every
element of
\mathcal{R}
can be written as a finite disjoint union of
elements of
\mathcal{R}
. A map
\mu\colon\mathcal{R}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is called

\sigma
-additive
, extending the definition given above, if and
only if for every countable (possibly empty) collection of pairwise
distinct elements
E_{n}
of
\mathcal{R}
whose union
E
is also in

\mathcal{R}
,
\mu(E)=\sum_{n}\mu(E_{n})
. Then
\mu
extends to a
measure on some
\sigma
-algebra containing
\mathcal{R}
. A first use
of this theorem is to establish the existence of
Lebesgue measure

\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}
, defined so that
\lambda(]a,b[)=b-a
for
every open bounded interval
]a,b[
.

A measure
\mu
on
X
is
bounded
if and only if

\mu(X)<+\infty
. A measure
\mu
is
\sigma
-finite
if
there is a sequence

E_{0}\subseteq E_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq E_{n}\subseteq\cdots
of
measurable subsets of
X
whose union is
X
and such that

\mu(E_{n})<+\infty
for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
. A
\pi
-system

\Pi
on a set
X
is a family of sets closed under finite
intersections. If
X
is a measurable space such that

\Sigma_{X}=\Sigma(\Pi)
, any two
\sigma
-finite measures that agree
on
\Pi
also agree on
\Sigma_{X}
. In particular, Lebesgue measure on

\mathbb{R}
is uniquely defined by the specification

\lambda(]a,b[)=b-a
.

Domain theory and topology.

A
dcpo
is a poset in which every directed family
D
has a
supremum
\sup D
. A prime example is
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
, the poset of closed
intervals
[a,b]
with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
and

a\leq b
, ordered by reverse inclusion. Every directed family

{([a_{i},b_{i}])}_{i\in I}
has a supremum

\bigcap_{i\in I}[a_{i},b_{i}]=[\sup_{i\in I}a_{i},\inf_{i\in I}b_{i}]
.
Among them, we find the
total

numbers
a\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
, which are equated
with the maximal elements
[a,a]
in
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
.

Another example is
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, with the usual ordering. We will also
consider
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
, the subdcpo of
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
consisting of its elements
of the form
[a,b]
with
a\geq 0
.

We will also write
\leq
for the ordering on any poset. In the
example of
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
,
\leq
is
\supseteq
. The
upward closure

\mathop{\uparrow}A
of a subset
A
of a poset
X
is

\{y\in X\mid\exists x\in A,x\leq y\}
. The
downward
closure

\mathop{\downarrow}A
is defined similarly. A set
A
is
upwards
closed
if and only if
A=\mathop{\uparrow}A
, and
downwards closed
if
and only if
A=\mathop{\downarrow}A
. A subset
U
of a dcpo
X
is

Scott-open
if and only if it is upwards closed and, for every
directed family
D
such that
\sup D\in U
, some element of
D
is
in
U
already. The Scott-open subsets of a dcpo
X
form its

Scott topology
.

The
way-below
relation
\ll
on a poset
X
is defined by

x\ll y
if and only if, for every directed family
D
with a
supremum
z
, if
y\leq z
, then
x
is less than or equal to some
element of
D
already. We write
\hbox to0.0pt{$\uparrow$\hss}\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\uparrow$}x
for

\{y\in X\mid x\ll y\}
, and
\hbox to0.0pt{$\downarrow$\hss}\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\downarrow$}y
for

\{x\in X\mid x\ll y\}
. A poset
X
is
continuous
if and
only if
\hbox to0.0pt{$\downarrow$\hss}\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\downarrow$}x
is directed and has
x
as supremum for every

x\in X
. A
basis

B
of a poset
X
is a subset of
X
such
that
\hbox to0.0pt{$\downarrow$\hss}\raise 2.15277pt\hbox{$\downarrow$}x\cap B
is directed and has
x
as supremum for every

x\in X
. A poset
X
is continuous if and only if it has a basis
(namely,
X
itself). A poset is
\omega
-continuous
if and
only if it has a countable basis. Examples include
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, with any
countable dense subset (with respect to its standard topology), such
as the rational numbers in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, or the
dyadic numbers

k/2^{n}
(
k,n\in{\rm Nature}
); or
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
, with the basis of
intervals
[a,b]
where
a
and
b
are both dyadic or rational.

We write
{\mathcal{O}}X
for the lattice of open subsets of a topological
space
X
. This applies to dcpos
X
as well, which will always be
considered with their Scott topology. The continuous maps

f\colon X\to Y
between two dcpos coincide with the

Scott-continuous
maps, namely the monotonic (order-preserving)
maps that preserve all directed suprema. We write
\mathcal{L}X
for the
space of continuous maps from a topological space
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, the
latter with its Scott topology, as usual. Such maps are usually
called
lower semicontinuous
, or
lsc
, in the mathematical
literature. Note that
\mathcal{L}X
, with the pointwise ordering, is a
dcpo.

There are several ways in which one can model probabilistic choice.
The most classical one is through measures. A popular alternative
used in domain theory is given by
continuous valuations

[
18
,
17
]
. A continuous valuation is a Scott-continuous
map
\nu\colon{\mathcal{O}}X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
\nu(\emptyset)=0

(
strictness
) and, for all
U,V\in{\mathcal{O}}X
,

\nu(U\cup V)+\nu(U\cap V)=\nu(U)+\nu(V)

(
modularity
). There is a notion of integral

\int_{x\in X}h(x)d\nu
, or briefly
\int hd\nu
, for every

h\in\mathcal{L}X
, which can again be defined by a Choquet formula.
The map
h\in\mathcal{L}X\mapsto\int hd\nu
is Scott-continuous and

linear
. By definition, a linear map

G\colon\mathcal{L}X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
satisfies
G(h+h^{\prime})=G(h)+G(h^{\prime})
and

G(\alpha.h)=\alpha.G(h)
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}X
. Conversely, any Scott-continuous linear map

G\colon\mathcal{L}X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is of the form
h\mapsto\int hd\nu

for a unique continuous valuation
\nu
, given by

\nu(U)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}G(\chi_{U})
, where
\chi_{U}
is the characteristic map
of
U
(
\chi_{U}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}1
if
x\in U
,
0
otherwise).

Rags, d-rags and continuous d-rags

Definition 3.1
.

A
rag
is a tuple
(R,0,+,1,\times)
(or simply
R
) where

(R,0,+)
is an Abelian monoid,
(R,1,\times)
is a monoid, and

\times
distributes over
+
. An
Abelian rag
is a rag whose
multiplication
\times
is commutative.

A
semi-ring
, or
rig
, is a rag which satisfies the extra
law
0\times r=r\times 0=0
.
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, for example, is an
Abelian rig, where
+
and
\times
are as usual, modulo the
convention that
0\times(+\infty)=0
. We will see that
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}

is a rag, but not a rig.

We also need some topological structure.

Definition 3.2
.

A
d-rag
is a rag
R
together with an ordering that makes it
a dcpo, in such a way that
+
and
\times
are Scott-continuous.

\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a d-rag. In order to turn
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
into a d-rag, we
define its
0
element as
[0,0]
; addition by

[a,b]+[c,d]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}[a+c,b+d]
; its
1
element as
[1,1]
; and
product by

[a,b]\times[c,d]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}[a\cdot_{\ell}c,b\cdot_{r}d]
. The
operations
\cdot_{\ell}
and
\cdot_{r}
are product operations (for the

\ell
eft and
r
ight part, respectively), and are defined so that

x\cdot_{\ell}y
and
x\cdot_{r}y
are equal to the usual product
xy

unless one of
x
,
y
is equal to
0
and the other is equal to

+\infty
. We need two distinct, left and right, product operations
in order to ensure Scott-continuity, as we now explain. We must
define
0\cdot_{\ell}(+\infty)
(
=(+\infty)\cdot_{\ell}0
) as
0
,
since
0\cdot_{\ell}(+\infty)
must be equal to

\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}0\cdot_{\ell}r=0
. Symmetrically, we must
define
0\cdot_{r}(+\infty)
(
=(+\infty)\cdot_{r}0
) as
+\infty
,
because
0\cdot_{r}(+\infty)
must be equal to

\inf_{r>0}r\cdot_{r}(+\infty)=+\infty
. With those choices, we
have the following easily checked fact.

Lemma 3.3
.

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
is an Abelian d-rag.

4
Continuous
R
-Valuations

Let
R
be a fixed Abelian d-rag. One might be tempted to define
continuous
R
-valuations on a space
X
as Scott-continuous maps from

{\mathcal{O}}X
to
R
satisfying some appropriate forms of strictness and
modularity, but, as we have argued in the introduction, this is
fraught with difficulties when the additive unit is not the least
element of
R
.

Since continuous valuations on
X
correspond bijectively to linear
Scott-continuous maps from
\mathcal{L}X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, another route is to
define continuous
R
-valuations as certain maps from a variant of

\mathcal{L}X
to
R
instead of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. As we will see, this leads to
a streamlined theory.

Given any space
X
, let
\mathcal{L}^{R}X
be the dcpo of all continuous
maps from
X
to
R
, with the pointwise ordering. With pointwise
addition and multiplication,
\mathcal{L}^{R}X
is also an Abelian d-rag.

Definition 4.1

(Continuous
R
-valuation)
.

A
continuous
R
-valuation
on a space
X
is a
Scott-continuous map
\nu\colon\mathcal{L}^{R}X\to R
that is

linear
in the sense that

\nu(\mathbf{a}\times h)=\mathbf{a}\times\nu(h)

(
homogeneity
) and
\nu(h+h^{\prime})=\nu(h)+\nu(h^{\prime})

(
additivity
) for all
\mathbf{a}\in R
,

h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}^{R}X
. We write
\mathbf{V}^{R}X
for the dcpo of all
continuous
R
-valuations on
X
, with the pointwise ordering.

Remark 4.2
.

When
R=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
\mathcal{L}^{R}X=\mathcal{L}X
, so that
\mathbf{V}^{R}X
can
be equated with the dcpo
\mathbf{V}X
of ordinary continuous valuations.

In order to help understand the definition, it is profitable to use
the integral notation
\int hd\nu
to mean
\nu(h)
. Hence
Definition
4.1
requires that

\int(\mathbf{a}\times h)d\nu=\mathbf{a}\times\int hd\nu
and

\int(h+h^{\prime})d\nu=\int hd\nu+\int h^{\prime}d\nu
.

Beware that the constant
0
map from
\mathcal{L}^{R}X
to
R
is

not
a continuous
R
-valuation, unless
R
is a rig:
homogeneity would imply
0=\mathbf{a}\times 0
, which fails in

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
, for example.
Also, we do not require
\nu(\mathbf{0})=0
in
Definition
4.1
, where
\mathbf{0}
is the constant
0
map.
This would be a consequence of homogeneity if
R
were a rig.

Addition and multiplication by scalars in
R
are defined pointwise on

\mathbf{V}^{R}X
. This allows us to make sense of the following definition.

Definition 4.3
.

The
R
-Dirac mass at
x\in X
is the continuous

R
-valuation
\delta_{x}\colon h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}X\mapsto h(x)\in R
.

An
elementary
R
-valuation
on
X
is a continuous

R
-valuation of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\times\delta_{x_{i}}
,
where
n\geq 1
, each
r_{i}
is in
R
, and mapping each

h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}X
to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\times h(x_{i})
.

We write
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
for the poset of elementary
R
-valuations on

X
, and
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
for the inductive closure of
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
in

\mathbf{V}^{R}X
. The elements of
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
are called the

minimal
R
-valuations
on
X
.

Our definition of the
R
-Dirac mass reads
\int hd\delta_{x}=h(x)

in integral notation.

Remark 4.4
.

A
simple valuation
on
X
is one of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
, where
n\in{\rm Nature}
, each point

x_{i}
is in
X
, and each coefficient
r_{i}
is in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. While
continuous valuations can be equated with continuous
R
-valuations
with
R=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
(Remark
4.2
), simple valuations and
elementary
R
-valuations are closely related but different
concepts, even when
R=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. First,
r\delta_{x}
is an
elementary
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
-valuation even when
r=+\infty
, but is a
simple valuation only if
r<+\infty
. Second, we require

n\geq 1
in the definition of elementary
R
-valuations, but
n

can be equal to
0
in the definition of a simple valuation. The
reason why we require
n\geq 1
is that the constant
0
map is not
a continuous
R
-valuation in general, as noticed above.

The
inductive closure
of a subset
A
of a dcpo
Z
is the
smallest subset of
Z
that contains
A
and is closed under directed
suprema. It is obtained by taking all directed suprema of elements of

A
, all directed suprema of elements obtained in this fashion, and
proceeding this way transfinitely.

A
pointed
dcpo is one with a least element
\bot
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
R
be an Abelian
d
-rag with a least element
\bot
that is
absorbing for multiplication, viz.,
\bot\times\mathbf{a}=\bot

for every
\mathbf{a}\in R
. For every non-empty space
X
, the
constant map
\tilde{\bot}\colon h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}X\mapsto\bot
is
the least element of
\mathbf{V}^{R}X
, and also of
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
.
Thus,
\mathbf{V}^{R}X
and
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
are pointed dcpos.

Proof 4.6
.

Since
\bot
is absorbing,
\tilde{\bot}
is equal to

\bot\times\delta_{x}
, for any fixed
x\in X
, hence is in

\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
. It is clearly least in
\mathbf{V}^{R}X
and in

\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
. The last claim is obvious.

Proposition
4.5
applies to the case
R=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
where the bottom element is
0
, and
0\times r=r
for every
r

(including
+\infty
). It also applies to the case
R=\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
, where
the bottom element is
[0,+\infty]
, and again
[0,+\infty]\times[a,b]=[0\cdot_{\ell}a,(+\infty)\cdot_{r}b]=[0,+\infty]
.

Remark 4.7
.

There is a very similar notion of integration of interval-valued
functions, yielding interval values, due to Edalat

[
7
]
, which he uses to define interval-valued integrals
of measurable functions. The purpose is to set up a computable
framework for Lebesgue measure and integration theory. The two
integrals considered in
[
7
]
and in the present paper
are similar, but different in a subtle way. There are small
differences, such as the fact that Edalat allows one to integrate
functions with values in
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}
, whereas we only integrate with
values in
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
, but the main difference is best illustrated
by the following example. Let
\lambda
be Lebesgue measure on

[0,1]
, and
h_{n}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
map every

x\in[0,1/2^{n}]
to
[0,\infty]
and every
x\in]1/2^{n},1]

to
[0,0]
. The maps
h_{n}
form a chain whose supremum is the
function
h
that maps every element of
]0,1]
to
[0,0]
and

0
to
[0,\infty]
. Using Edalat’s integral, we have

\int h_{n}d\lambda=[0,\infty]
for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
, but

\int hd\lambda=[0,0]
. This shows that Edalat’s integral is

not
a continuous
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation in general. We will
propose a way to fix this issue in Section
7
.

5
Monads of continuous
R
-valuations

We fix an Abelian d-rag
R
. We will see that
\mathbf{V}^{R}
and
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}

define strong monads on the category
{\mathbf{Dcpo}}
of dcpos and
Scott-continuous maps. This is essential in describing probabilistic
effects, following Moggi’s seminal work
[
22
,
23
]
. We
use Manes’ presentation of monads
[
21
]
: a monad

(T,\eta,\_^{\dagger})
on a category
{\mathbf{C}}
is a function
T

mapping objects of
{\mathbf{C}}
to objects of
{\mathbf{C}}
, a collection of
morphisms
\eta_{X}\colon X\to TX
, one for each object
X
of

{\mathbf{C}}
, and called the
unit
, and for every morphism

f\colon X\to TY
, a morphism
f^{\dagger}\colon TX\to TY
called
the
extension
of
f
; those are required to satisfy the axioms:

(1)

f^{\dagger}\circ\eta_{X}=f
;

(2)

\eta_{X}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{id}_{TX}
;

(3)

(g^{\dagger}\circ f)^{\dagger}=g^{\dagger}\circ f^{\dagger}
.

Then
T
extends to an endofunctor, acting on morphisms through

Tf=(\eta_{Y}\circ f)^{\dagger}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The triple
(\mathbf{V}^{R},\eta,\_^{\dagger})
is a monad on the category
of dcpos and Scott-continuous maps, where

\eta_{X}\colon X\to\mathbf{V}^{R}X
maps
x
to
\delta_{x}
, and for every

f\colon X\to\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
,
f^{\dagger}
is defined by

f^{\dagger}(\nu)(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\nu(\lambda x\in X.f(x)(k))
for
every
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X
, and for every
k\in\mathcal{L}^{R}Y
.

Proof 5.2
.

Verifying that
\eta
is Scott-continuous is routine.

Let us look at
f^{\dagger}
. For every
k\in\mathcal{L}^{R}Y
, it is
easy to see that
\lambda x\in X.f(x)(k)
is Scott-continuous,
because
f
is Scott-continuous and directed suprema are computed
pointwise in
\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
. Hence
\nu(\lambda x\in X.f(x)(k))

makes sense. The map

f^{\dagger}(\nu)\colon k\mapsto\nu(\lambda x\in X.f(x)(k))

is also Scott-continuous, since
f(x)
is Scott-continuous for
every
x\in X
and since
\nu
is itself Scott-continuous. It is
easy to see that
f^{\dagger}(\nu)
is linear, too, because
f(x)

is linear for every
x\in X
, and because
\nu
is linear. Hence

f^{\dagger}(\nu)
is an element of
\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
for every

\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X
. Finally,
f^{\dagger}
itself is
Scott-continuous, as one easily checks.

The monad equations (i), (ii) and (iii) are immediate.

Fact 1
.

The
\mathbf{V}^{R}
functor acts on morphisms by

\mathbf{V}^{R}(f)(\nu)(k)=\nu(k\circ f)
.

In integral notation, this means
\nu^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathbf{V}^{R}(f)(\nu)

satisfies
\int kd\nu^{\prime}=\int(k\circ f)d\nu
. This is a formula
that is typical of the image measure of
\nu
by
f
, where
\nu
is a
measure. We may think of
\mathbf{V}^{R}(f)(\nu)
as the image of the
continuous
R
-valuation
\nu
by
f
.

We will now show that
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}
defines a submonad of
\mathbf{V}^{R}
. To
this end, we need to know more about inductive closures. A

d-closed subset
of a dcpo
Z
is a subset
C
such that the
supremum of every directed family of elements of
C
, taken in
Z
, is
in
C
. The d-closed subsets form the closed subsets of a topology
called the
d-topology

[
19
, Section 5]
, and the
inductive closure of a subset
A
coincides with its
d-closure

cl_{d}(A)
, namely its closure in the d-topology.

We note that every Scott-continuous map is continuous with respect to
the underlying d-topologies. This is easily checked, or see

[
19
, Lemma 5.3]
. In particular:

Fact 2
.

For every Scott-continuous map
f\colon\mathbf{V}^{R}X\to\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
, for
every
A\subseteq\mathbf{V}^{R}X
,
f(cl_{d}(A))\subseteq cl_{d}(f(A))
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every space
X
,
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
is closed under addition and
multiplication by elements of
R
, as computed in the larger space

\mathbf{V}^{R}X
.

Proof 5.4
.

Let us deal with addition. Multiplication is similar.

For every elementary
R
-valuation
\mu
, the map

f_{\mu}\colon\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X\mapsto\mu+\nu
is
Scott-continuous, and maps elementary
R
-valuations to elementary

R
-valuations. By Fact
2
with

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
,
f_{\mu}
maps all elements of

cl_{d}(A)=\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
to

cl_{d}(f_{\mu}(A))\subseteq cl_{d}(\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X)=\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
.

It follows that for every minimal
R
-valuation
\nu
, the map

g\colon\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X\mapsto\mu+\nu=f_{\mu}(\nu)
maps
elementary
R
-valuations to minimal
R
-valuations. We observe that

g
is also Scott-continuous. By Fact
2
with the same

A
as above,
g
maps all elements of
cl_{d}(A)=\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
to

cl_{d}(g(A))\subseteq cl_{d}(\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X)=\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
. Hence, for
every
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
, for every
\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
,
\mu+\nu
is in
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any Scott-continuous map
f\colon X\to\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
,

f^{\dagger}
is a Scott-continuous map from
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
to
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
.

Proof 5.6
.

The only challenge is to show that, for every
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
,

f^{\dagger}(\nu)
is in
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
. Scott-continuity follows
from the fact that
f^{\dagger}
is Scott-continuous from
\mathbf{V}^{R}X

to
\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
.

For every
\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\times\delta_{x_{i}}
in

\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
(
n\geq 1
),
f^{\dagger}(\nu)
is the continuous

R
-valuation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\times f(x_{i})
: for every

k\in\mathcal{L}^{R}Y
,

f^{\dagger}(\nu)(k)=\nu(\lambda x\in X.f(x)(k))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\times\allowbreak f(x_{i})(k)=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\times f(x_{i}))(k)
.
By Lemma
5.3
, and since
f(x_{i})
is in
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
for each
i
,
f^{\dagger}(\nu)
is in
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
as well.

Hence
f^{\dagger}
maps
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
to
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
. Using
Fact
2
with
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
,

f^{\dagger}(cl_{d}(A))=f^{\dagger}(\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X)
is included in

cl_{d}(f^{\dagger}(A))\subseteq cl_{d}(\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y)=\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
.

We observe that
\eta_{X}(x)=\delta_{x}
is in

\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X\subseteq\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
for every dcpo
X
, and every

x\in X
, whence the following.

Proposition 5.7
.

The triple
(\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m},\eta,\_^{\dagger})
is a monad on the category
of dcpos and Scott-continuous maps.

A tensorial strength for a monad
(T,\eta,\_^{\dagger})
is a
collection
t
of morphisms

t_{X,Y}\colon X\times TY\to T(X\times Y)
, natural in
X
and

Y
, satisfying certain coherence conditions (which we omit, see

[
23
]
.) We then say that
(T,\eta,\_^{\dagger},t)
is a

strong
monad. We will satisfy ourselves with the following
result. By
[
23
, Proposition 3.4]
, in a category with finite
products and enough points, if one can find morphisms
t_{X,Y}
for
all objects
X
and
Y
such that

t_{X,Y}\circ\langle x,\nu\rangle=T(\langle x\circ!,\mathrm{id}_{Y}\rangle)\circ\nu
, where
!\colon Y\to 1
is the unique morphism from
Y
to the terminal object,
then the collection of those morphisms is the
unique tensorial strength. The category of dcpos has finite products,
and has enough points, and specializing this to the
\mathbf{V}^{R}
monad, we
obtain the following.

Lemma 5.8
.

The maps
t_{X,Y}\colon X\times\mathbf{V}^{R}Y\to\mathbf{V}^{R}(X\times Y)

defined by

t_{X,Y}(x,\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\lambda h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y).\nu(\lambda y\in Y.h(x,y))
define the unique tensorial strength
for the monad
(\mathbf{V}^{R},\eta,\_^{\dagger})
.

Proof 5.9
.

The previous observation shows that we must define
t_{X,Y}
by

t_{X,Y}(x,\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathbf{V}^{R}(\lambda y\in Y.(x,y))(\nu)
.
By Fact
1
, the latter is equal to

\lambda h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y).\nu(h\circ\lambda y\in Y.(x,y))=\lambda h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y).\nu(\lambda y\in Y.h(x,y))
.

It is enough to check that
t_{X,Y}
is Scott-continuous. This
follows from the fact that application (of
\nu
to
\lambda y\in Y.h(x,y))
) is Scott-continuous.

In integral notation,
t_{X,Y}(x,\nu)
is the continuous

R
-valuation
\nu^{\prime}
such that
\int hd\nu^{\prime}=\int h(x,\_)d\nu
for
every
h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y)
.

For every
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
, for every
x\in X
,
t_{X,Y}(x,\nu)
is equal to
\mathbf{V}^{R}(\lambda y\in Y.(x,y))(\nu)=(\eta\circ\lambda y\in Y.(x,y))^{\dagger}(\nu)
. By
Lemma
5.5
, this is an element of
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}(X\times Y)
. It follows:

Proposition 5.10
.

(\mathbf{V}^{R},\eta,\_^{\dagger},t)
and

(\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m},\eta,\_^{\dagger},t)
are strong monads on
{\mathbf{Dcpo}}
.

We now show that
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}
is a
commutative
monad. The
corresponding result is unknown for
\mathbf{V}^{R}
, even when
R=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Given a tensorial strength
t
, there is a dual tensorial strength

t^{\prime}
, where
t^{\prime}_{X,Y}\colon TX\times Y\to T(X\times Y)
. Here

t^{\prime}_{X,Y}(\mu,y)=\lambda h\in\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y).\mu(\lambda x\in X.h(x,y))
. We can then define two morphisms from

TX\times TY
to
T(X\times Y)
, namely

{t^{\prime}}^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t_{TX,Y}
and

t^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t^{\prime}_{X,TY}
. The monad
T
is

commutative
when they coincide.

Lemma 5.11
.

Two morphisms
f,g\colon X\to Y
in
{\mathbf{Dcpo}}
that coincide on

A\subseteq X
also coincide on
cl_{d}(A)
.

Proof 5.12
.

Let
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\{x\in X\mid f(x)=g(x)\}
. Since
f
and
g

preserve directed suprema,
B
is d-closed. By assumption,
A
is
included in
B
, so
B
also contains
cl_{d}(A)
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
X
,
Y
be two dcpos. The maps

t^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t^{\prime}_{X,TY}
and

{t^{\prime}}^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t_{TX,Y}
coincide on those pairs

(\mu,\nu)\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X\times\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
such that

\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
or
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
.

Proof 5.14
.

We prove the claim when
\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
. The case where
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
is symmetric.

In the sequel,
h
ranges over
\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times\mathbf{V}^{R}Y)
,
k

over
\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y)
,
x
over
X
,
y
over
Y
, and
\nu

over
\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
. For all
\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X
and
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
,
we verify that:

\displaystyle(t^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t^{\prime}_{X,TY})(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\lambda k.\mu(\lambda x.\nu(\lambda y.k(x,y)))

\displaystyle({t^{\prime}}^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t_{TX,Y})(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\lambda k.\nu(\lambda y.\mu(\lambda x.k(x,y))).

Those two quantities are equal when
\mu
is an elementary

R
-valuation
\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\times\delta_{x_{i}}
(
m\geq 1
),
since the first one is equal to

\lambda k.\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\times\nu(\lambda y.k(x_{i},y))
,
the second one is equal to

\lambda k.\nu(\lambda y.\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\times k(x_{i},y))
,
and since
\nu
is linear.

For fixed
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}Y
, the maps

f\colon\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X\mapsto(t^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t^{\prime}_{X,TY})(\mu,\nu)
and

g\colon\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}X\mapsto({t^{\prime}}^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t_{TX,Y})(\mu,\nu)
therefore coincide on
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
. They
are both Scott-continuous, since
t^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t^{\prime}_{X,TY}

and
{t^{\prime}}^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t_{TX,Y}
are. By
Lemma
5.11
, they must coincide on the d-closure of

\mathbf{V}^{R}_{\mathrm{f}}X
, which is
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
by definition.

Corollary 5.15
.

(\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m},\eta,\_^{\dagger},t)
is a commutative monad.

The equality of Proposition
5.13
is that, for every

\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
and for every
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
, for every

k\in\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y)
,

\mu(\lambda x\in X.\nu(\lambda y\in Y.k(x,y)))=\nu(\lambda y\in Y.\mu(\lambda x\in X.k(x,y)))
. In integral
notation,

\int_{x\in X}\left(\int_{y\in Y}k(x,y)d\nu\right)d\mu=\int_{y\in Y}\left(\int_{x\in X}k(x,y)d\mu\right)d\nu,

which we recognize as the integral permutation property, obtained
in the classical measure-theoretic case as a consequence of Fubini’s
theorem.

Fubini’s theorem is more general, and states the existence of a
product measure. A similar fact follows from the above results, as
noticed by Kock
[
20
]
. We write
\otimes
for the
morphism
{t^{\prime}}^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t_{TX,Y}
and

t^{\dagger}_{X,Y}\circ t^{\prime}_{X,TY}
from
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X\times\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y

to
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}(X\times Y)
, as with any commutative monad

[
20
, Section 5]
. Then, for all
\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X

and
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
,
\otimes(\mu,\nu)
, which we prefer to
write as
\mu\otimes\nu
, is in
\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}(X\times Y)
, and by
definition

(\mu\otimes\nu)(k)=\mu(\lambda x\in X.\nu(\lambda y\in Y.k(x,y)))=\nu(\lambda y\in Y.\mu(\lambda x\in X.k(x,y)))
. In integral notation, we obtain the following form of Fubini’s
theorem:

\int_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}k(x,y)d(\mu\otimes\nu)=\int_{x\in X}\left(\int_{y\in Y}k(x,y)d\nu\right)d\mu=\int_{y\in Y}\left(\int_{x\in X}k(x,y)d\mu\right)d\nu,

for all
\mu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}X
,
\nu\in\mathbf{V}^{R}_{m}Y
, and

k\in\mathcal{L}^{R}(X\times Y)
. As an additional benefit, we obtain (for
free!) that the map

\otimes\colon(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\mu\otimes\nu
is
Scott-continuous.

Remark 5.16
.

A similar Fubini-like theorem was already obtained by Jones

[
17
]
for arbitrary (subprobability) continuous valuations,
but in the setting of continuous dcpos only. Whether the
Fubini-like formula above holds for every pair of continuous
valuations
\mu
and
\nu
on arbitrary dcpos is unknown. We note
that the problem would be easily solved if all continuous valuations
were minimal, but that is not the case, as is shown in the paper
[
13
]
.

6
Continuous
R
-valuations and measures I: A brief viewpoint

We look at the special cases of continuous
R
-valuations when
R
is

\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
or
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
, and we investigate their relations to measures.

When
R=\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, this is simple: as noticed in Remark
4.2
,
we can equate continuous
R
-valuations with continuous valuations.
Next, continuous valuations and measures are pretty much the same
thing on
\omega
-continuous dcpos, namely on continuous dcpos with a
countable basis. This holds more generally on de Brecht’s
quasi-Polish spaces
[
5
]
, a class of spaces that
contains not only the
\omega
-continuous dcpos from domain theory but
also the Polish spaces from topological measure theory. One can see
this as follows. In one direction, every measure
\mu
on a
hereditarily Lindelöf space
X
is
\tau
-smooth

[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
, meaning that its restriction to
the lattice of open subsets of
X
is a continuous valuation. A
hereditarily Lindelöf space is a space whose subspaces are all
Lindelöf, or equivalently a space in which every family of open sets
contains a countable subfamily with the same union. Every
second-countable space is hereditarily Lindelöf, and that includes
all quasi-Polish spaces. In the other direction, every continuous
valuation on an LCS-complete space extends to a Borel measure

[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
. An
LCS-complete
space is a
space that is homeomorphic to a
G_{\delta}
subset of a locally compact
sober space. Every quasi-Polish space is LCS-complete; in fact, the
quasi-Polish spaces are exactly the second-countable LCS-complete
spaces
[
6
, Theorem 9.5]
.

Remark 6.1
.

A continuous valuation
\mu
on an LCS-complete space
X
may extend
to more than one Borel measure. However, the extension is unique
if
\mu
is
\sigma
-finite
, namely if there is a monotone
sequence

U_{0}\subseteq U_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq U_{n}\subseteq\cdots

of open subsets of
X
whose union is the whole of
X
, and such
that
\mu(U_{n})<+\infty
for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
. Indeed, any
extension of
\mu
will be
\sigma
-finite in the usual sense. We
conclude since any two
\sigma
-finite measures that agree on all
open sets (which form a
\pi
-system) must agree on the Borel

\sigma
-algebra.

We now look in detail at the more complex case
R=\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
. We use
the following notation. Given any element
\mathbf{x}
of
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
or
of
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
, we write

and

for its endpoints, viz.,

\mathbf{x}=[x^{-},x^{+}]
. Every map

defines
two maps

by

h^{-}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}h(x)^{-}
and
h^{+}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}h(x)^{+}
. Given two maps

such that

f\leq g
, we write
[f,g]
for the function that maps
x
to

[f(x),g(x)]
. Note that, given any map
f
in
\mathcal{L}X
, the map

[f,+\infty.\mathbf{1}]\colon x\mapsto[f(x),+\infty]
is in

\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}}X
. (We will write
r.\mathbf{1}
for the constant
function with value

, in order to distinguish it from the scalar
value
r

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-continuous valuation

on a space

, we also define
F^{-}(h)
as

and
F^{+}(h)
as

,
for every

.

Lemma 6.2

(The view from the left I)
.

Let
X
be any topological space. For every continuous

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation
F
on
X
, for every

h\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}}X
,
F^{-}(h)
only depends on
h^{-}
, not on

h^{+}
. Moreover, there is a unique continuous valuation
\nu_{F}
on

X
such that, for every
h\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}}X
,

\displaystyle F^{-}(h)=\int_{X}h^{-}d\nu_{F}.

Proof 6.3
.

For the first part, it suffices to show that

F^{-}(h)=F^{-}([h^{-},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])
. We note that the bottom
element
[0,+\infty]
of
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
is multiplicatively absorbing:
for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
,

[0,+\infty]\times\mathbf{x}=[0,+\infty]
. It follows that

\displaystyle F([0.\mathbf{1},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])

\displaystyle=F([0,+\infty]\times[0.\mathbf{1},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])

\displaystyle=[0,+\infty]\times F([0.\mathbf{1},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])=[0,+\infty].

Next,
[0,+\infty]
satisfies the following partial absorption law
for addition:
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
,

[0,+\infty]+\mathbf{x}=[x^{-},+\infty]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle F([h^{-},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])

\displaystyle=F(h+[0.\mathbf{1},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])

\displaystyle=F(h)+F([0.\mathbf{1},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])

\displaystyle=F(h)+[0,+\infty]

by our previous result

\displaystyle=[F^{-}(h),+\infty].

It follows that
F^{-}(h)=F^{-}([h^{-},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])
, and the
right-hand side does not depend on
h^{+}
.

In order to show the second part of the lemma, it suffices to
observe that the map
f\mapsto F^{-}([f,+\infty.\mathbf{1}])
is
linear and Scott-continuous, and is therefore the integral
functional of a unique continuous valuation
\nu_{F}
.

It follows that, for every
f\in\mathcal{L}X
,

\int_{X}fd\nu_{F}=F^{-}([f,+\infty.\mathbf{1}])
. In particular, for
every open subset
U
of
X
,

\nu_{F}(U)=F^{-}([\chi_{U},+\infty.\mathbf{1}])
.

There are many ways in which we can reconstruct a continuous

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation from a continuous valuation, and here is the
simplest of all.

Lemma 6.4

(The view from the left II)
.

Let
X
be any topological space. For every continuous valuation

\nu
on
X
, there is a smallest continuous
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation
F

such that
\nu_{F}=\nu
. For every
h\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}}X
,

\displaystyle F(h)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\left[\int_{X}h^{-}d\nu,+\infty\right].

For the continuous
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation just given, the view from the
right, namely
F^{+}(h)
, is the constant
+\infty
, for every
integrand
h
, including for the constant zero map. This cannot be
the integral of
h
with respect to any measure, since the integral of the
zero map is always zero, with respect to any continuous valuation or
measure.

One possible view of continuous
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuations

is that of
the specification of some unknown measure.
F^{-}
gives a continuous
valuation that is a lower bound on that measure, while
F^{+}
measures
how precise that specification is. In this setting, the continuous

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation

built in Lemma
6.4
is the
least precise specification for
\nu
.

On more special topological spaces, we will see that every measure
has a much more precise specification, and that it is minimal.

7
Continuous
R
-valuations and measures II: Measures as continuous

-valuations

We will see that every non-zero, bounded
\tau
-smooth measure
\mu

on a coherent topological space
X
gives rise to a continuous

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuation in a natural way. (A measure
\mu
on
X
is

bounded
if
\mu(X)<\infty
, and we recall that it is

\tau
-smooth if and only if it restricts to a continuous valuation on

{\mathcal{O}}X
.) As a first step, we need to define integrals of functions
with values in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, not just
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, as is done classically.

More precisely, given a measure
\mu
on a topological space
X
(with
its Borel
\sigma
-algebra), we can define the Lebesgue integral

\int_{x\in X}f(x)d\mu
of any measurable map

f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We extend this definition to measurable maps

f
from
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Just as with multiplication in rags, this
comes in two flavors.

Perhaps the most natural extension is:

\displaystyle\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{x\in X}\min(f(x),r)d\mu.

(1)

It is known that the Lebesgue integral, as used on the right of
(
1
) can be defined through the following, so-called
Choquet formula
[
4
, Chapter VII, Section 48.1, p. 265]
:

\displaystyle\int_{x\in X}f(x)d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(f^{-1}(]t,\infty]))dt

(2)

where the integral on the right is now an indefinite Riemann integral.
As a consequence, and since
(\min(f(\_),r)^{-1}(]t,\infty])
is
empty for every
t\geq r
, and equal to
f^{-1}(]t,\infty])
for
every
t<r
, we can rewrite (
1
) as:

\displaystyle\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{0}^{r}\mu(f^{-1}(]t,\infty]))dt.

(3)

We observe that this lower integral is linear and
\omega
-continuous
(by the monotone convergence theorem). It also commutes with the
product structure of the d-rag
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. We also note the following
change of variable formula, for future reference:

\displaystyle\int_{y\in Y}^{-}f(y)dj[\mu]

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(j(x))d\mu,

(4)

for every measurable map
j\colon X\to Y
, for every measurable
map
f\colon Y\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, for every measure
\mu
on
X
, and where

j[\mu]
is the
image measure
, defined by
j[\mu](E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mu(j^{-1}(E))
. This is an obvious consequence of (
3

A function

is lower semicontinuous if and only
if it is continuous from
X
to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with the Scott topology;
equivalently, for every
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
,

is open in

. Every lower semicontinuous
function is measurable.

Lemma 7.1
.

The lower integral (

) is:

(1)

additive: for all measurable maps
f
,
g
from

to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

\int_{x\in X}^{-}(f(x)+g(x))d\mu=\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu+\int_{x\in X}^{-}g(x)d\mu

(2)

\cdot_{\ell}
-homogeneous: for every measurable map

a\cdot_{\ell}\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu=\int_{x\in X}^{-}(a\cdot_{\ell}f(x))d\mu

(3)

\omega

{(f_{n})}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
of measurable maps from

to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

\int_{x\in X}^{-}\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}f_{n}(x)d\mu=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}\int_{x\in X}^{-}f_{n}(x)d\mu

(4)

Scott-continuous on lower semicontinuous maps, provided that

\mu
is
\tau
-smooth: for every directed family

\int_{x\in X}^{-}\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{i\in I}f_{i}(x)d\mu=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{i\in I}\int_{x\in X}^{-}f_{i}(x)d\mu

Proof 7.2
.

1. This follows from the additivity of Lebesgue integral of

\mathbb{R}_{+}
-valued functions and the inequalities

\min(f(x)+g(x),r)\leq\min(f(x),r)+\min(g(x),r)\leq\min(f(x)+g(x),2r)
.

2. For every
a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle a\cdot_{\ell}\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{x\in X}a\min(f(x),r)d\mu

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{x\in X}\min(a\cdot_{\ell}f(x),r^{\prime})d\mu

\displaystyle\text{by letting }r^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}ar

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in X}^{-}(a\cdot_{\ell}f(x))d\mu.

When

,

is equal to
0
by definition, and
\int_{x\in X}^{-}(a\cdot_{\ell}f(x))d\mu=\int_{x\in X}0d\mu=0
.

The new, key case is when
a=\infty
. We split this into two
subcases. If
\mu(f^{-1}(]0,\infty]))=0
, namely if

is

\mu
-a.e. zero, then
\min(f(\_),r)
and

\min(\infty\cdot_{\ell}f(\_),r)
are also
\mu
-a.e. zero, so

\infty\cdot_{\ell}\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu
and

\int_{x\in X}^{-}(\infty\cdot_{\ell}f(x))d\mu
are both equal to

. If
\mu(f^{-1}(]0,\infty]))>0
, then

\mu(f^{-1}(]r,\infty]))>0
for some

,
since

\mu(f^{-1}(]0,\infty]))=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{q\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}\setminus\{0\}}\mu(f^{-1}(]q,\infty]))
. It follows that

\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu\geq r\mu(f^{-1}(]r,\infty]))>0
,
so
\infty\cdot_{\ell}\int_{x\in X}^{-}f(x)d\mu=\infty
, while

\int_{x\in X}^{-}\infty\cdot_{\ell}f(x)d\mu\geq\int_{x\in X}\min(\infty\cdot_{\ell}f(x),r)d\mu\geq r\mu(f^{-1}(]0,\infty]))
for every

3. We use the monotone convergence theorem, and the fact that

\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}\min(f_{n}(x),r)=\min(\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\uparrow$}\;}}{\sup}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}f_{n}(x),r)
for all

and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

4. Since Riemann integration of non-increasing maps from
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is Scott-continuous (see for example Lemma 4.2 in

[
26
]
), it follows from (

lower integral
\int_{x\in X}f(x)d\mu
is Scott-continuous in
the lower semicontinuous map

, provided that
\mu
is

\tau
-smooth.

We also consider the following, upper integral. This will really only
make sense when the integrated function

is upper semicontinuous,
namely when for every

,

is

\mu
is non-zero
(

), and
\tau

A

of a measure
\mu
on
X
is any set

such that,
for all measurable subsets

and
B
of
X
such that

\mu(A)=\mu(B)
. When

is itself measurable, this is
equivalent to requiring
\mu(E)=\mu(X)
, and when
\mu
is
additionally bounded (i.e.,
\mu(X)<\infty
), this is equivalent to

\mu(X\setminus E)=0
. We sometimes say that
\mu
is
supported
on

to mean that

is a support of
\mu
.

For every
\tau
-smooth measure
\mu
on
X
, the intersection of all
closed supports of
\mu
is again a closed support of
\mu
: this
smallest closed support will be denote as
\mathrm{supp}\;\mu
. But beware
that there might be smaller (non-closed) supports. For example,

\mathrm{supp}\;(\delta_{x})
is equal to the closure
\mathop{\downarrow}x
of the point

,
but
\{x\}
is a smaller (non-closed) support. Note that
\{x\}
is
the intersection of the compact saturated set
\mathop{\uparrow}x
, which happens
to be a support of
\mu=\delta_{x}
, with
\mathrm{supp}\;(\mu)
.

In general, not all compact saturated sets
Q
are measurable, so we
will restrict to
measurable
compact saturated subsets in the
sequel. The intersection of two supports
E
and
E^{\prime}
may also fail
to be a support, but if one of them (say
E^{\prime}
) is measurable, then

is also a support. (Indeed, let
A
,
B
be measurable
such that
A\cap(E\cap E^{\prime})=B\cap(E\cap E^{\prime})
. Then

(A\cap E^{\prime})\cap E=(B\cap E^{\prime})\cap E
, and since
E
is a support
of
\mu
,
\mu(A\cap E^{\prime})=\mu(B\cap E^{\prime})
. Since
E^{\prime}
is a
support of
\mu
, and since
A
and
A\cap E^{\prime}
have the same

,
\mu(A\cap E^{\prime})=\mu(A)
, and similarly

\mu(B\cap E^{\prime})=\mu(B)

\tau
-smooth measure

Lemma 7.3
.

For every non-zero
\tau
-smooth measure
\mu

,

Proof 7.4
.

Otherwise,
\mathrm{supp}\;\mu
and the empty set have the same intersection
with
Q
, and since

is a support of
\mu
, we would have

\mu(\mathrm{supp}\;\mu)=\mu(\emptyset)=0
. Since

, and

Lemma 7.5
.

For every non-zero
\tau
-smooth measure
\mu
on a topological

X
, and for every upper semicontinuous map

Proof 7.6

Every upper semicontinuous

-valued function

reaches its
maximum on any non-empty compact set

. Here is a quick proof:
let

, and assume that

for

. The open sets

. We extract a finite

We now apply this to

Proof 7.9

For every non-zero, bounded

Proof 8.2

We consider any measurable map

Our objective is now to show that

-valuation that approximates

The value of restricting to second-countable spaces is the following.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let

be a base of its
topology that is closed under finite unions.

(1)

(2)

.

(i) We write

that are

. This forms an open cover of

, from which we can
extract a finite subcover. Since

(ii) Let

When

is measurable.

A space

is
stably compact
if and only if it is sober,
locally compact, compact, and coherent. We let

denote

with its

, which is the smallest topology that
contains the original open subsets of

and the complements of
compact saturated subsets of

. When

is stably compact, and

\leq
is its specialization ordering,

is a

compact pospace
, meaning that

is compact Hausdorff,
and that the graph of
\leq
is closed in

.
We say that a subset of

is

if it is open in

. Similarly, we use the terms

,

patch-compact
. If

is stably compact, then patch-closed and
patch-compact are synonymous. We should add that the original open
subsets of

can be recovered as those patch-open subsets that are
upwards-closed with respect to

.

Example 8.5
.

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
is stably compact in its Scott topology.
Indeed, it is a continuous dcpo in which any pair of elements

and
[c,d]
with an upper bound (namely, such that

[

Reasoning similarly, the larger dcpo

of all closed intervals
in

is also a bc-domain, hence is also stably compact. (To make it

not only contains the usual intervals

Both bc-domains are second-countable as well. Indeed, as continuous

\mu

We claim that

in

\mathrm{supp}\;\mu

, and this follows
from the fact that

j[\lambda](K)=\lambda(j^{-1}(K))=\lambda([0,1])=1
.

We claim that every compact saturated support
Q
of
j[\lambda]

must contain
K
. We argue as follows. By
Lemma
8.9
, and since
Q
is patch-closed in

X^{\mathsf{patch}}
(where
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
),
Q\cap K
is closed in
K
.
If
Q
does not contain
K
, then
Q\cap K
is a proper subset of

K
. Let
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}K\setminus Q
: this is a non-empty open subset of

K
, and therefore
j^{-1}(U)
is a non-empty subset of
[0,1]
.
Hence
\lambda(j^{-1}(U))>0
, so
j[\lambda](U)>0
. It
follows that
j[\lambda](Q\cap K)=1-j[\lambda](U)<1
.
Since
K
is a support of
j[\lambda]
, we obtain that

j[\lambda](Q)=j[\lambda](Q\cap K)<1
, and that contradicts
that
Q
is a support of
j[\lambda]
.

It follows that
\mathop{\uparrow}K
(
=K
) is the smallest compact saturated
support of
j[\lambda]
. In that case, and with

\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}j[\lambda]
, the definition of
\mu
-boundedness
simplifies:
h^{+}
is
\mu
-bounded if and only if
h^{+}
is bounded
on
\mathop{\uparrow}K\cap\mathrm{supp}\;\mu=\mathop{\uparrow}K\cap\mathop{\downarrow}K=K
.

It is even easier to show that
h^{+}\circ j
is
\lambda
-bounded if
and only if it is bounded on
[0,1]
. Indeed,

\mathrm{supp}\;\lambda=[0,1]
, and therefore the only compact (hence
closed) support
Q
of
\lambda
is
[0,1]
, so that there is only
one possible set
Q\cap\mathrm{supp}\;\lambda
to be considered, namely

[0,1]
.

If
h^{+}
is
\mu
-unbounded, then

\int_{y\in\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}}h^{+}(y)dj[\lambda]=\infty
by definition. By
Corollary
7.7
with
Q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathop{\uparrow}K=K
, there is
a point
[x,x]\in Q\cap\mathrm{supp}\;\mu=K
such that

h^{+}([x,x])=\infty
. For every
n\in{\rm Nature}
, there is a natural
number
i
such that
x\in[\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}]
,

1\leq i\leq 2^{n}
. Then

[\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}]\leq[x,x]
, and since every
upper semicontinuous map is antitonic,

h^{+}([\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}])\geq h^{+}([x,x])=\infty
. It follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}h^{+}([\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}])=\infty
. Since that holds for every
n\in{\rm Nature}
,

\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\inf}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}h^{+}([\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}])
, which is the right endpoint of the final
interval of (
9
), is equal to
\infty
, hence to

\int_{y\in\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}}h^{+}(y)dj[\lambda]
.

It remains to deal with the case where
h^{+}
is
\mu
-bounded, and
we have seen that this means that
h^{+}
is bounded on
K
.
Let
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
be such that for every
y\in K
,
h^{+}(y)<r
.
Hence
K
is included in the open set
{h^{+}}^{-1}([0,r[)
.

For every
n\in{\rm Nature}
, let

Q_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{{\scriptsize def}}}}{{=}}\mathop{\uparrow}\{[\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}]\mid i\in\{1,2,\cdots,2^{n}\}\}
, a compact saturated set that contains
K
.
(The upward closure of any finite set is compact saturated.) It is
also easy to see that any point of
\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\bigcap}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}Q_{n}
is of
the form
[x,x]
with
x\in[0,1]
, so

\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\bigcap}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}Q_{n}=K
. Since
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
is sober, it is
well-filtered, and therefore
K\subseteq{h^{+}}^{-1}([0,r[)

implies the existence of an
n\in{\rm Nature}
such that

Q_{n}\subseteq{h^{+}}^{-1}([0,r[)
. (Here is an alternate argument
that avoids well-filteredness.
Q_{n}
is compact saturated hence
closed in
{\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}}^{\mathsf{patch}}
. The complements of the sets
Q_{n}
then
form an open cover of the complement of
{h^{+}}^{-1}([0,r[)
in

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}
. That complement is closed hence compact in

{\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}}^{\mathsf{patch}}
. We can then extract a finite subcover, and since
the sets
Q_{n}
form a chain, there is a single
n\in{\rm Nature}
such
that the complement of
Q_{n}
contains the complement of

{h^{+}}^{-1}([0,r[)
.)

Let
n_{0}
be the natural number
n
that we have just found. Then
we perform the following computation:

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}}^{+}h^{+}(y)dj[\lambda]

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}}^{-}h^{+}(y)dj[\lambda]

since
h^{+}
is
j[\lambda]
-bounded

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in[0,1]}^{-}h^{+}(j(x))d\lambda

by the change of variable formula (
4
)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in[0,1]}^{+}h^{+}(j(x))d\lambda.

The latter is justified by the fact that
h^{+}\circ j
is bounded
(by
r
) on
[0,1]
, hence is
\lambda
-bounded, as we have seen
above.

Let us proceed. The first step below is justified by the fact that

h^{+}
is upper semicontinuous, hence Scott-continuous from
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}

to
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with the opposite ordering; in particular,
h^{+}
maps
directed suprema to filtered infima:

\displaystyle\int_{x\in[0,1]}^{+}h^{+}(j(x))d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in[0,1]}^{+}\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\inf}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}h^{+}(j_{n}(x))d\lambda

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\inf}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}\int_{x\in[0,1]}^{+}h^{+}(j_{n}(x))d\lambda

by Lemma
7.8
, item 3

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\inf}_{n\in{\rm Nature},n>n_{0}}\int_{x\in[0,1]}^{+}h^{+}(j_{n}(x))d\lambda,

where we have restricted the infimum to the indices above
n_{0}
in
the last line. (The infimum of a chain coincides with the infimum
of any coinitial chain.)

For every
n>n_{0}
, for every
x\in[0,1]
,
j_{n}(x)
is an
interval of the form
[\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}]
(if
x
is
in the interval
]\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}[
, or if
x=0
,
or if
x=1
), or of the form

[\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i+1}{2^{n}}]
(if
x
is exactly

\frac{i}{2^{n}}
,
1\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1
). Since
n>n_{0}
, whichever
the case is,
j_{n}(x)
is in
Q_{n_{0}}
, hence in

{h^{+}}^{-1}([0,r[)
. This means that
h^{+}\circ j_{n}
is a bounded
function, and therefore that

\int_{x\in[0,1]}^{+}h^{+}(j_{n}(x))d\lambda
is the ordinary
Lebesgue integral
\int_{x\in[0,1]}h^{+}(j_{n}(x))d\lambda
.
Since
h^{+}\circ j_{n}
is piecewise constant (as with

h^{-}\circ j_{n}
, earlier on), that Lebesgue integral is equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}h^{+}([\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}])
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}^{\star}}^{+}h^{+}(y)dj[\lambda]

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\inf}_{n\in{\rm Nature},n>n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}h^{+}\left(\left[\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\downarrow$}\;}}{\inf}_{n\in{\rm Nature}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}h^{+}\left(\left[\frac{i-1}{2^{n}},\frac{i}{2^{n}}\right]\right),

and we recognize the right endpoint of the final interval of
(
9
).

10
Conclusion

We have proposed an extension of the notion of continuous valuation,
or measure, with values in suitable domains beyond
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. We have
argued that continuous
R
-valuations, where
R
is a so-called
Abelian d-rag, provide such an extension. Beyond
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, a
particularly interesting Abelian d-rag is the domain of intervals

\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
, and we have shown that there is an ample supply of
continuous
\mathbf{I}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\star}
-valuations stemming from measures.

There are many pending questions. For example, is
\mathbf{V}^{R}(X)
a

continuous
dcpo, provided that
X
is a continuous dcpo and
R

is a continuous Abelian d-rag? Is there a form of the Fubini theorem
for continuous
R
-valuations, beyond the one we have obtained for
minimal
R
-valuations? None of the usual proof arguments, in realms
of measures or of continuous valuations, seems to apply.
