Models of modified-inertia formulation of MOND
Mordehai Milgrom

Abstract
Models of “modified-inertia” formulation of MOND are described and applied to nonrelativistic many-body systems. Whereas the interbody forces are Newtonian, the expression for their inertia is modified from the Newtonian m ​ 𝐚 m{\bf a} to comply with the basic tenets of MOND. This results in time-nonlocal equations of motion. Momentum, angular momentum, and energy are (nonlocally) defined for bodies, and the total values are conserved for isolated many-body systems. The models make all the salient MOND predictions. Yet, they differ in important ways from existing “modified-gravity” formulations in their second-tier predictions. Indeed, the heuristic value of the model is in limelighting such possible differences. The models describe correctly the motion of a composite body in a low-acceleration field even when the internal accelerations of its constituents are high (e.g., a star in a galaxy). They exhibit a MOND external field effect (EFE) that shows some important differences from what we have come to expect from modified-gravity versions: In one, simple example of the models, what determines the EFE, in the case of a dominant external field, is μ ⁡ ( θ ⁡ ⟨ a e ​ x ⟩ / a 0 ) \mu(\theta\langle a_{\scriptscriptstyle ex}\rangle/a_{0}) , where μ ⁡ ( x ) \mu(x) is the MOND “interpolating function” that describes rotation curves, compared with μ ⁡ ( a e ​ x / a 0 ) \mu(a_{\scriptscriptstyle ex}/a_{0}) for presently known modified-gravity formulations. The two main differences are that while a e ​ x a_{\scriptscriptstyle ex} is the momentary value of the external acceleration, ⟨ a e ​ x ⟩ \langle a_{\scriptscriptstyle ex}\rangle is a certain time average of it, and that θ > 1 \theta>1 is an extra factor that depends on the frequency ratio of the external- and internal-field variations. Only ratios of frequencies enter, and a 0 a_{0} remains the only new dimensioned constant. For example, for a system on a circular orbit in a galaxy (such as the vertical dynamics in a disk galaxy), the first difference disappears, since ⟨ a e ​ x ⟩ = a e ​ x \langle a_{\scriptscriptstyle ex}\rangle=a_{\scriptscriptstyle ex} . But the θ \theta factor can appreciably enhance the EFE in quenching MOND effects, over what is deduced in modified gravity. This θ \theta enhancement is important in most applications of the EFE. Some exact solutions are also described, such as for rotation curves, for an harmonic force, and the general, two-body problem, which in the deep-MOND regime reduces to a single-body problem.

Introduction

From the basic tenets of MOND
Milgrom 1983
follow a number of major predictions concerning aspects of the mass discrepancies in galactic systems, which MOND accounts for without invoking “dark matter”
milgrom14a
. Reviews of MOND can be found in Refs.
Famaey and McGaugh 2012
;
Milgrom 2014
;
Milgrom 2020
;
mcgaugh20
;
merritt20
;
bz22
. However, different formulations that embody these tenets may still differ to varying degrees in making important second-tier predictions. Among the first-tier prediction, one may mention those that pertain to the rotation curves of isolated disk galaxies: asymptotic flatness, the relation between asymptotic speed and total baryonic mass,
M
(also known as “the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation”),
V_{\infty}^{4}=MGa_{0}
, and the full prediction of rotation curves. Other first-tier predictions are: the mass-velocity-dispersion relation,
MGa_{0}=\eta\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle 4}
, with
\eta\sim 1
, in deep-MOND, pressure-supported systems; the presence of effects of an external field on the internal dynamics of a gravitating system – the external-field effect (hereafter, EFE), etc. Among second-tier predictions, one may count some fine details of the rotation curves (e.g., Refs.
brada95
;
milgrom12
;
brown18
;
pl20
;
chae22
), the exact value of
\eta
in the
M~-~\sigma
relation, and its dependence on dimensionless attributes of the system; the exact dependence of the effective two-body force on the masses; the exact nature and strength of the EFE, etc.

There are some full-fledged, self-consistent, working formulations of MOND. Among them we have nonrelativistic formulations, such as AQUAL
Bekenstein and Milgrom 1984
and QUMOND
Milgrom 2010
; and we have several relativistic formulations, such as the more recent ones described in Refs.
Skordis and Zlosnik 2021
;
milgrom22

1

1

The definition is not always clear cut. There are theories that can be described equivalently as either a modification of the Einstein-Hilbert action for the metric, with test particles moving on geodesics of the modified metric, or, leaving the gravitational action intact, they modify the particle equation of motion away from the geodesics.

The presently known nonrelativistic, MG theories (AQUAL and QUMOND) do differ somewhat on second-tier predictions, or else they would not be different theories (e.g., Res.
Milgrom 2010
;
zf10
).
For example, they exhibit small differences in the EFE (e.g., Refs.
Milgrom 2010
;
bz15
;
chaemil22
), small differences in the stability criteria of disks
bmz18
, and small differences in the predicted rotation curves of disk galaxies (e.g. Ref.
chae22
).
However, they do tend to differ only little from each other even on such prediction (less than would be necessary to distinguish between them observationally, at present). For example, Ref.
milgrom14b
derived a
general
, deep-MOND, virial theorem for nonrelativistic MG theories, showing that all such theories predict the same value of
\eta
in the
M-\sigma
relation, independent of system parameters, the same dependence of the deep-MOND, two-body force on the two masses, and the same “
Q
parameter” (as defined in Ref.
milgrom12
) for all deep-MOND, disk galaxies.

Concentrating on MG formulations might, however, be too restrictive. For one, because this may lead us to accept the second-tier predictions of such theories as absolute predictions of MOND.

Also, MOND, as we know it now, and as described by any of its presently-known formulations, is, arguably, an effective theory, an approximation, that must have roots in a more fundamental theory – a “Fundamond”.
Indeed, beyond their usefulness as practical tools for making predictions and affording self-consistent calculations in MOND, such effective versions of MOND are expected to point the way to a Fundamond.

It is thus quite valuable to explore a wide variety of models that embody the basic tenets of MOND – and thus make all its salient prediction – even if these models are not satisfactory in all regards. Such models can give us some idea of the variety of predictions that MOND can make regarding the second-tier predictions. They can also, potentially, point to more promising paths to the Fundamond.

One possible approach to constructing MOND effective theories is to modify not the gravitational part, but the ‘‘free,’’ or ‘‘inertial’’ part of the equations of dynamics (gravitational or otherwise).
2
2

2

Such theories may modify directly the equations of motion, and are not necessarily governed by an action.

This idea has been discussed at length in the context of MOND
Milgrom 1994
;
milgrom99
;
Milgrom 2011
.

In a way of support for the idea, one can recall that physics is replete with examples of acquired, or modified, inertia: inertia of bodies that emerges fully from, or is modified by, the interaction of the body with some omnipresent background medium: the acquisition of particle masses (inertia) by their interaction with the ambient Higgs field in the standard model of particle physics; the renormalization of masses (e.g., in quantum electrodynamics) by interaction with the vacuum; effective modification of the inertia of electrons and holes in solids; effective inertia of a body moving in a perfect fluid
milgrom06
; etc. etc.

In Ref.
milgrom99
, I suggested that inertia results from such interaction with the quantum vacuum. The vacuum can, in the least, define an absolute inertial frame, in that bodies can “know” that they are accelerated with respect to it, for example via the Unruh effect.

It has to be noted though, that the Einstein-Hilbert action may be considered the free action of the gravitational field. So, an eventual relativistic Fundamond will probably involve modification of all parts of the action.

3

This will also be necessary, it appears, if we want to reproduce the observation that gravitational waves follow the same world lines as photons.
Remember, for example, that general relativity modifies all parts of the Newtonian action: On top of the modified choice of gravitational degrees of freedom (a metric instead of a potential), it modifies the Poisson action to the Einstein-Hilbert action, and the Newtonian kinetic Lagrangian,
mv^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}/2
, first to the special relativistic Lagrangian (even in the absence of gravity),
-mc^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\gamma(v/c)
,

4

4

Special relativity can be viewed as modifying Newtonian inertia.
and then to the general relativistic one. However, here we consider only the nonrelativistic limit, where the gravitational field may be considered static. I thus restrict myself to modifications of only the kinetic part of the equations of motion.

Following a seed idea in Ref.
Milgrom 2011
, I construct here a class of effective, nonrelativistic models of “modified inertia” that embody the basic axioms of MOND. The equations of motion describe a system of many (pointlike) bodies interacting through some (not necessarily gravitational) force. These equations are time nonlocal. (See the discussion in Ref.
Milgrom 1994
as to why such nonlocality arises naturally in modified-inertia theories.)

The models are described in Sec.
II
, where I also show that the equations of motion obey conservation laws of momentum, energy, and angular momentum, with certain modified definitions of these quantities. Here, I also consider the two-body problem, and scaling laws in the deep-MOND limit. Further details are worked out in Sec.
III
. In Sec.
IV
, I discuss some consequences of the models: the prediction of rotation curves of disk galaxies, exact solutions for harmonic forces, the equation of motion of composite bodies, and the MOND external-field effect.
Section
V

II
Models

Consider a system of (pointlike) bodies of masses
m_{\scriptscriptstyle p}
, with the Newtonian force (not necessarily gravitational) of body
q
on body
p
being
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}({\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p},{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle q})
, and
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle qp}=-{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}
.
5
5

5

We usually deal with forces, whereby
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle qp}
is along
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}={\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}-{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle q}
, and depends on
|{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}|
.
We want the trajectories of the bodies,
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(t)
, to be dictated by MOND.
6
6

6

I use “trajectory” for the path
{\bf r}(t)
, including its time history, and “orbit” for the collection of points covered by the trajectory.

Along its trajectory, body
p
is subject to the total, time-dependent force

{\bf F}_{p}(t)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle q\not=p}{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}[{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(t),{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle q}(t)].

(1)

Newtonian dynamics tell us that the acceleration
{\bf a}(t)
to which the body is subject at time
t
depends only on the force at time
t
(for now I omit the subscript
p
) according to
m{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(t)={\bf F}(t)
, which in Fourier space reads
m\hat{\bf a}_{N}(\omega)=\hat{\bf F}(\omega)
. Hatted quantities are Fourier components:

\hat{\bf r}(\omega)=\int_{\scriptscriptstyle-\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}{\bf r}(t)e^{\scriptscriptstyle-i\omega t}dt,~~~~{\bf r}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle-\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\hat{\bf r}(\omega)e^{\scriptscriptstyle i\omega t}d\omega,

(2)

with the velocity and acceleration Fourier components
\hat{\bf v}(\omega)=i\omega\hat{\bf r}(\omega),~\hat{\bf a}(\omega)=-\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\hat{\bf r}(\omega)
.

We want to modify the Newtonian relation, in accordance with the basic axioms of MOND, by modifying the left-hand, inertia term. We can then write, quite generally,

m\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]=\hat{\bf F}(\omega),~~~~~{\rm or}~~~~~\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]=\hat{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(\omega),

(3)

where the inertia functional,
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]
, is a dimensionless functional
of the whole trajectory
– designated
\{\hat{\bf r}\}
to distinguish it from the number
\hat{\bf r}(\omega)
.
\mathcal{I}
can also be an explicit function of
\omega
, and it is constructed using
a_{0}
as the only dimensioned constant (in a minimalist view of MOND, we want
a_{0}
to be the only new, dimensioned constant appearing).

To avoid possible confusion, note that the right-hand side of Eq. (
3
) is not calculable directly from Newtonian dynamics. It is not the Newtonian acceleration on Newtonian orbits; it is rather the Newtonian acceleration on the MOND orbits, which are not known
a priori
. Thus, these equations have to be solved self-consistently.

As has been discussed in detail, e.g., in Refs.
milgrom99
;
Milgrom 2019a
, it is natural in MOND to posit the existence of an absolute inertial frame with respect to which the absolute accelerations we call
{\bf a}(t)
here, are measured. For example, as argued in Ref.
milgrom99
, the quantum vacuum may define such a frame.

To comply with the MOND basic tenets,
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]
has to approach unity in the formal limit
a_{0}\rightarrow 0
, so as to restore the Newtonian relation when all quantities with dimensions of acceleration are much larger than
a_{0}
. In the gravitational context, at least, MOND is posited to become space-time scale invariance in the opposite limit
Milgrom 2009
, defined by
a_{0}\rightarrow\infty
and
G\rightarrow 0
, such that
Ga_{0}
remains fixed; i.e., invariant under
(t,{\bf r})\rightarrow\lambda(t,{\bf r})
. The Newtonian gravitational force scales as
\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle-2}
, so
\hat{\bf F}(\omega)
whose dimensions are of force times time, scales as
\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}
, while
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)
(whose dimensions are of velocity) is invariant to scaling.
This means that in this deep-MOND limit,
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]
has to scale as
\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}
. But, under scaling,
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]\rightarrow\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,\lambda a_{0}]
,
7
7

7

To see this, first apply to
\mathcal{I}
scaling of the degrees of freedom by
\lambda
. Then, make a change of time and length unit – under which
\mathcal{I}
, being dimensionless, does not change – multiplying them by a factor
\lambda
. All the degrees of freedom (so
\{\hat{\bf r}\}
and
\omega
) go back to their original numerical values, but the value of
a_{0}
in the new units is multiplied by
\lambda
. In other words, under scaling,
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]\rightarrow\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,\lambda a_{0}]
.
which means that in this limit
\mathcal{I}
must be of the form

\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]\rightarrow\mathcal{A}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega]/a_{0},

(4)

where
\mathcal{A}
is a functional of the orbit, and a function of the frequency, with the dimensions of acceleration, and with no dimensioned constant appearing in its construction.

Note that since for gravity
\hat{\bf F}(\omega)\propto m
, the theory obeys the universality of free fall (the weak equivalence principle).

If instead of applying Eq. (
3
) to a self-interacting closed system, or to a body moving in some given force field, we dictate a time-dependent force on a body,
{\bf F}(t)
, one may ask whether we should not make sure that the law of motion is causal, in the sense that

{\bf a}(t)
should depend only on
{\bf F}(t^{\prime})
for
t^{\prime}\leq t
. In linear-response systems, where the cause (input) can be clearly separated from the effect (output), such causality is guaranteed by certain analyticity properties in the complex frequency plane, of the analog of
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]
here. Perhaps the models here can be modified to incorporate such a requirement. But to avoid complications, I assume that we are dealing with a closed system, allowed to evolve on its own; so the forces are determined as part of the solution, and are not dictated at will.

In some applications below, I shall specify
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]
to be of the form

\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]=\mu[\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}(\omega)/a_{0},\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}(\omega)/a_{0},...],

(5)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\scriptscriptstyle a}(\omega)
– which may be called “nonlocal acceleration parameters
of the trajectory” – are of the type
\mathcal{A}
described above. In fact, for simplicity of presentation, and also to make more direct comparison with presently known MG theories, I proceed with just one argument in
\mu
.

Then, the interpolating function,
\mu(x)
, has to approach unity for high arguments
\mu(x\rightarrow\infty)\rightarrow 1
, and become linear in its argument,
\mathcal{A}(\omega)/a_{0}
, in the opposite limit.

II.1
Uniqueness, the “initial conditions” problem, and solution by iteration

The inertia functional,
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]
, has to obey some condition, to ensure a certain desired uniqueness requirement of the solutions.

In the MG theory AQUAL, the MOND gravitational potential is determined from the equation

\vec{\nabla}\cdot[\mu(|\vec{\nabla}\phi|/a_{0})\vec{\nabla}\phi]=4\pi G\rho({\bf r})
, where
\rho
is the baryonic density. In one-dimensional configurations such as spherically symmetric systems, this gives an algebraic relation between the Newtonian acceleration,
g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
, and the MOND acceleration,
g
, at any position:
g\mu(g/a_{0})=g_{\scriptscriptstyle N}
. The Newtonian acceleration field is unique, given
\rho
and spatial boundary conditions. For the MOND field to be unique, it is necessary that
x\mu(x)
be monotonic. Reference
milgrom86
shows that this monotonicity is also a sufficient condition for the AQUAL equation to have a unique solution under analogs of the Neumann or Dirichlet spatial boundary conditions, for an arbitrary density distribution.

There is an analogous requirement of our models here. Consider a physical trajectory,
{\bf r}(t)
, of a body of mass
m
, in a Newtonian force field
{\bf F}({\bf r})
.
We do not want another trajectory,
{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(t)\equiv{\bf r}(\lambda t)
, to also be a solution of the equations of motion. Such a solution would have the same orbit as the former, but with the velocities
{\bf v}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}({\bf r})=\lambda{\bf v}({\bf r})
and accelerations
{\bf a}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}({\bf r})=\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}{\bf a}({\bf r})
. We would have, for example, in the case of a circular orbit in an axisymmetric potential, more than one orbital velocity at the same radius. This, in turn, will not give a unique prediction for the rotation curve in a disk galaxy.

For the undesired trajectory,
{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(t)
, we can write the relevant quantities appearing in the equations of motion in terms of those of
{\bf r}(t)
: For the frequency
\omega
in the spectrum of
{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(t)
we have
\hat{\bf F}^{\lambda}(\omega)=\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\hat{\bf F}(\omega^{\prime})
and
\hat{\bf a}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(\omega)=\lambda\hat{\bf a}(\omega^{\prime})
, where
\omega^{\prime}\equiv\omega/\lambda
is the matching frequency in the spectrum of
{\bf r}(t)
. Also, on dimensional grounds it can be shown that
\mathcal{I}^{\lambda}[\{{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}\},\omega,a_{0}]=\mathcal{I}[\{{\bf r}\},\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle-2}a_{0}]
.
Thus, if
{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}(t)
satisfies Eq. (
3
), we have

m\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\hat{\bf a}(\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega^{\prime},\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle-2}a_{0}]=\hat{\bf F}(\omega^{\prime}).

(6)

But, because
{\bf r}(t)
satisfies the equations of motion, Eq. (
6
)
is also satisfied for all
\omega^{\prime}
with
\lambda=1
.
To avoid the above unwanted nonuniqueness it is necessary that, at least for single-frequency orbits,

U(a_{0},\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega^{\prime})\equiv a_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega^{\prime},a_{0}]

(7)

be monotonic as a function of
a_{0}
, so the left-hand side of Eq. (
6
) cannot take the same value for different values of
\lambda
.
8
8

8

In the multifrequency case, nonuniqueness can be avoided even if
U
is not monotonic, because there may not be a single
\lambda
for all frequencies.

In the special case where
\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}]=\mu[\mathcal{A}(\omega)/a_{0}]
, this necessary uniqueness requirement is tantamount to
x\mu(x)
being monotonic, as in the case of AQUAL.

It is useful to require that
U(a_{0},\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega)
is monotonic in
a_{0}
for all physical trajectories and all frequencies, not only for single-frequency trajectories (see, e.g., an application in Sec.
II.3
).
From the basic tenets of MOND,
U\propto a_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}
for
a_{0}\rightarrow 0
, and
U\propto a_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle-2}
for
a_{0}\rightarrow\infty
. So
U
is decreasing with
a_{0}
, and takes up all values between
0
and
\infty
for all trajectories and for all frequencies.

Another matter of principle concerns the question of “initial conditions”:
in Newtonian dynamics, which is local, the theory leads to second-order differential equations, which are solved to propagate the system from some values of the positions and velocities of all constituents at some given time. This is not the situation with time-nonlocal theories. These may be thought of as a sieve – some conditions on the full history – that pinpoint the physical system histories out of the many imagined histories. Technically, this is much more difficult to apply, especially given that MOND is also nonlinear even in the nonrelativistic regime. But this does not argue against a theory.

It would be interesting to establish whether in the present models it is still the case that a unique history is defined by dictating the positions and velocities of all particles at some time
t
.
I will show below that this is indeed the case for a particle in a harmonic force field. It is also the case for a body in a constant force field, in which the acceleration is also constant.
9
9

9

In this case
{\bf F}(\omega)\propto\delta(\omega)
, and so
{\bf a}(\omega)\propto\delta(\omega)
.

But is it the case in general?

We can, in principle, add this as a requirement on
\mathcal{I}
, but we cannot be sure that there is a choice of
\mathcal{I}
that satisfies it in addition to the other requirements.

We may come closer to an answer by considering a gedanken process of solving the equations of motion by iteration.
For all bodies in the system, take some values of
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}
and
{\bf v}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}
at some time
t_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, which I shall refer to as the “initial values”, even though in the present framework there is no initiation of the system.
Start with some initial-guess trajectories
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(t)
and
{\bf v}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(t)
that take the initial values at
t_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
. Substitute these trajectories in the right hand side to calculate the initial guess for
\hat{\bf F}_{p}(\omega)
. Then, in one option, try to calculate the corresponding
\hat{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(\omega)
from the equations.
In another option, substitute the initial guess also in
\mathcal{I}_{p}
on the left-hand side, then obtain
\hat{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(\omega)
directly. From
\hat{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(\omega)
calculate
{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(t)
; then, use it to integrate the trajectories forward and backward in time from the initial conditions. These will not generally coincide with our initial guess, but will be our second-iteration trajectories, used for the next iteration.
There are many possible iteration schemes. If there is one for which the process converges to a unique set of trajectories, we have established our goal, since all the trajectories on the iteration path satisfy the initial conditions exactly.

One promising iteration scheme involves also changing the value of
a_{0}
“adiabatically” in the iteration process, as follows: start with the (unique) Newtonian trajectory that satisfies the initial conditions; calculate the nonlocal acceleration for all frequencies; and start with a value of
a_{0}
that is (much) smaller than all these. The field equations are then satisfied to a desired accuracy by the Newtonian solution. Use this as the initial guess, then increase
a_{0}
a little and calculate the new trajectories as described above, possibly iterating until convergence for this new value of
a_{0}
is achieved (which is what I mean by changing
a_{0}
adiabatically). (In the iteration process we also need to let the frequencies change, since the frequencies of the sought-after solution are, generally, not those of the initial guess.) Then, increase
a_{0}
more, and repeat, until the actual value of
a_{0}
is obtained. In this way, we can be sure that the guess is as close as we want to the convergence value.
If this process does converge uniquely, it also establishes an interesting path between the Newtonian and MOND solutions.

We cannot, at present, be sure that any iteration scheme actually converges, that there are no bifurcation points on the iteration path that could lead to multiple convergent solutions, nor that all MOND trajectories can be reached by starting from Newtonian trajectories. But at least this can provide a means to probe the question numerically, and it can also afford solving the equations of motion, at least for simple enough systems, such as few-body problems.

If it can be established that in some version of the model the standard initial conditions determine the solution uniquely, we can say that the theory determines the future of a system from only the initial conditions at some finite time, even though, unlike the standard case, the (putative?) past of the system also enters the dynamics and must be part of the procedure for determining the future.

II.2
Conservation laws

We can define a quantity that stands for the momentum,
{\bf P}(t)
, of a body, in such a way that
\frac{d{\bf P}}{dt}={\bf F}(t)
, or in terms of the Fourier components
i\omega\hat{\bf P}(\omega)=\hat{\bf F}(\omega)
. Thus, from Eq. (
3
)

\hat{\bf P}(\omega)=m\hat{\bf v}(\omega)\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega,a_{0}].

(8)

While
{\bf P}
depends on time, it is not defined locally by the instantaneous velocity but, rather, depends also on the whole trajectory via
\mathcal{I}
that appears in its definition.

Because, for an isolated system,
\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle p}{\bf F}_{p}=0
, the total momentum in the system is conserved:
d(\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle p}{\bf P}_{p})/dt=0
.

From this follows that the radius
{\bf R}(t)
, whose Fourier transform is

\hat{\bf R}(\omega)=(\sum_{p}m_{\scriptscriptstyle p})^{-1}\sum_{p}m_{\scriptscriptstyle p}\hat{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}(\omega)\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}\},\omega,a_{0}],

(9)

moves at a constant speed
d^{2}{\bf R}/dt^{2}=0
.
{\bf R}
can be viewed as a (nonlocal) representation of the “center of mass”.

We can also define a (nonlocal) kinetic energy,
E_{k}(t)
, by its Fourier transform. It is defined such that

dE_{k}/dt={\bf v}(t)\cdot{\bf F}(t)
. Using the convolution theorem for the Fourier transform of a product

[\alpha(t)\beta(t)]_{\scriptscriptstyle\omega}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\alpha(\omega^{\prime})\beta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime},

(10)

we have from Eq. (
3
)

\hat{E}_{k}(\omega)=\frac{m}{2\pi}\int\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}\hat{\bf v}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\bf v}(\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{I}[\{\hat{\bf r}\},\omega^{\prime},a_{0}]d\omega^{\prime}.

(11)

If the interbody forces are derivable from potentials,
\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}({\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq})
, with
{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}=-{\bf f}_{\scriptscriptstyle qp}=-d\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}({\bf r}_{pq})/d{\bf r}_{pq}
(
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}={\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}-{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle q}
), we have
\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle p}{\bf F}_{p}\cdot{\bf v}_{\scriptscriptstyle p}=-d\phi/dt
, where

\phi(t)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle p<q}\phi_{\scriptscriptstyle pq}[{\bf r}_{pq}(t)]
, and the total energy

II.4
Deep-MOND limit for self-gravitating systems

There are general scaling laws obeyed by self-gravitating systems in the deep-MOND limit, which follow from its space-time scale invariance, and dimensional arguments.

Take a many-body system, of masses
m_{\scriptscriptstyle p}

III
Construction of the nonlocal acceleration
\mathcal{A}

I now concentrate on the special case where
\mathcal{I}
is described by Eq. (
5
), and consider the construction of the nonlocal acceleration parameters, collectively designated
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
.
We already stated that
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
has to be of the dimensions of acceleration, and has to be constructed from the orbit (and
\omega
) without using dimensioned constants. What other constrains can we cast on its choice?

III.1
Symmetry requirements

To ensure translation and Galilei invariance of this model, we construct
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
from
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)
, because it is invariant to these [depending only on
{\bf a}(t)
]. Formally, under translations by
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, and a Galilei boost by
{\bf v}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, under which
{\bf r}(t)\rightarrow{\bf r}(t)+{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}+{\bf v}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}t
, we have
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\rightarrow\hat{\bf a}(\omega)-2\pi(\omega^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}-i\omega{\bf v}_{\scriptscriptstyle 0})\delta(\omega)
, which equals
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)
if not applied (as a distribution) to functions that diverge as
1/\omega
or faster, at
\omega=0
.

Rotational invariance dictates that only scalars, such as
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\cdot\hat{\bf a}(\omega)
, or
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\hat{\cdot}{\bf a}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}(\omega)
appear.

We also require time-translation invariance of the inertia term, so that trajectories
in a time-independent
force field are not affected by a time shift; namely, if
{\bf r}(t)
is a trajectory, so is
{\bf r}(t+t_{\scriptscriptstyle 0})
.
Under time translations,
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\rightarrow e^{i\omega t_{0}}\hat{\bf a}(\omega)
. To this, constructs such as
\hat{\bf a}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1})\hat{\bf a}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 2})...
are invariant, provided
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}+\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+...=0
.
For example,
|\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\cdot\hat{\bf a}(\omega)|
and
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\hat{\bf a}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}(\omega)
are invariant. For the sake of concreteness, I shall construct
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
from
|\hat{\bf a}(\omega)|
, where the absolute value in
|\hat{\bf a}|=(\hat{\bf a}\cdot\hat{\bf a}^{\scriptscriptstyle*})^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}
is both in the complex and the vectorial sense, and from
|\hat{\bf a}\cdot\hat{\bf a}|^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}
(here, the absolute value is in the complex sense), which both satisfy all the above requirements, in addition to invariance to time and space reflections.

III.2
Explicit dependence on
\omega
?

If
\mathcal{A}
does not depend explicitly on
\omega
; i.e., if, for a given trajectory, all frequencies are affected by the same factor
\mu(\mathcal{A}/a_{0})
– for example, if
\mathcal{A}\propto\int|\hat{\bf a}(\omega)|d\omega
, the theory can be simply solved for trajectories in a static external field.
Take some Newtonian trajectory
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(t)
.
Then, the trajectory
{\bf r}(t)={\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(\beta t)
is a solution of the field equations for some unique value of
\beta>0
. For the above MOND trajectory,
{\bf F}(t)={\bf F}_{N}(\beta t)
, so
\hat{\bf F}(\omega)=\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\hat{\bf F}_{N}(\omega/\beta)
. Also,
\hat{\bf r}(\omega)=\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\hat{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(\omega/\beta)
,

\hat{\bf a}(\omega)=\beta\hat{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(\omega/\beta)
, and since
\mathcal{A}
does not depend on
\omega
, we have on dimensional grounds

\mathcal{A}=\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{N}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{N}
is the value of
\mathcal{A}
for the Newtonian trajectory.
12
12

12

If
\mathcal{A}
does depend explicitly on
\omega
we have
\mathcal{A}(\omega)=\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{N}(\omega/\beta)
, and our argument does not go through.
Thus, given that
{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(t)
satisfies the field equations with
\mu\equiv 1
,
{\bf r}(t)
satisfies them, provided

\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\mu(\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{N}/a_{0})=1.

(19)

This equation has a unique solution for
\beta>1
. This is because
x\mu(x)
is required to be increasing, and it takes up all values from 0 to
\infty
, as discussed in Sec.
II.1
.

This MOND trajectory has the same orbit as the Newtonian one, but with velocities scaled up by a factor
\beta
and accelerations scaled up by a factor
\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}=1/\mu(\mathcal{A}/a_{0})
.

The correspondence is one to one: if
{\bf r}(t)
solves the equations of motion and
\mathcal{A}
is its frequency-independent, nonlocal acceleration, define
\beta
such that
\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\mu(\mathcal{A}/a_{0})=1

{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(t)={\bf r}(t/\beta)
solves the Newtonian equations.

Such a theory would satisfy the basic tenets of MOND, and imply its salient predictions.
However, it is too restricted, and, importantly, it does not produce the correct center-of-mass motion of
bodies with high intrinsic accelerations (e.g., stars) in a low-acceleration field (e.g., of a galaxy).

We will thus require some appropriate, explicit dependence of
\mathcal{A}
on
\omega
, as discussed below.

III.3
Decoupling of the frequencies?

If
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
is
a function
of
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)
(and
\omega
) – i.e., depends only on
\hat{\bf a}(\omega)
at the same frequency, the theory decouples different frequencies; i.e., it modifies the acceleration frequency by frequency. Such a theory would satisfy the basic MOND axioms, and predict the salient MOND predictions; but it would practically exhibit no MOND EFE (see Sec.
IV.4
below for more details). The EFE would act only if the frequency of the external force were the same as that of the internal one, which is hardly ever the case. This demonstrates that an EFE can, in practice, be avoided in a theory satisfying the basic tenets of MOND. But there are strong indications that an EFE is required in MOND (e.g., to account for the fact that no MOND effects have shown up in terrestrial experiments). In the framework of the present model, this requires that different frequencies (e.g., of the external and internal accelerations) are coupled in
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
.

III.4
Heuristic example of a choice of
\mathcal{A}(\omega)

A way to define
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
under all the above requirements, using only

for concreteness (see below on the effects of using

as well) is

\mathcal{A}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2^{\scriptscriptstyle 3/2}\pi}\int_{\scriptscriptstyle-\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\theta\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}\right)|\hat{\bf a}(\omega^{\prime})|d\omega^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}\right)|\hat{\bf a}(\omega^{\prime})|d\omega^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}\right)|\hat{\bf r}(\omega^{\prime})|{\omega^{\prime}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}d\omega^{\prime},

(20)

where
\theta(y)
is a dimensionless function, which I take to be symmetric,
\theta(-x)=\theta(x)
. In the second equality in Eq. (
20
), I use the fact that
|\hat{\bf a}(\omega)|
is symmetric, since
{\bf a}(t)
is real, and hence,
\hat{\bf a}^{*}(\omega)=\hat{\bf a}(-\omega)
.
13
13

13

|\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\cdot\hat{\bf a}(\omega)|^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}
, which can also be used in the construction of
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
, is also symmetric under
\omega\rightarrow-\omega
.

The normalization of
\theta(y)
is degenerate with that of

. We pick the normalization such that
\theta(1)=1
because then, as we shall see below, the standard value of

and the form of

IV
Examples

As a general comment, we note that it is impractical, in general, to solve a many-body system in full, but as is usually realized, there are problems where we can apply a mean-field approximation, where we consider the motion of a single body (e.g., a star in a galaxy) in the combined mean field of all the others assumed time independent.
14
14

14

If the (Newtonian) force field in which the body is moving is derivable from a potential,
{\bf F}(t)=-\vec{\nabla}\phi[{\bf r}(t)]
, with the above definition of the kinetic energy, we have conservation of the energy
d(E_{k}+\phi)/dt=0
along a trajectory.

It may go without saying, but I emphasize that such a mean field does not define an effective, MOND force field – or an acceleration field in the case of gravity – as is the case of MG. This is because the accelerations of different bodies, at the same position, may differ from each other, depending on their kinematics, and, in the present, time-nonlocal case, on details of their trajectories. What is common to all bodies at the same position,
{\bf r}
, is their
d{\bf P}/dt={\bf F}({\bf r})
in a given force field, or
m^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}d{\bf P}/dt={\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}({\bf r})
, in a Newtonian, acceleration field
{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}({\bf r})
.
15
15

15

In special relativity, where the equation of motion is
d{\bf p}/dt=md(\gamma{\bf v})/dt=m\gamma[{\bf a}+\gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}c^{\scriptscriptstyle-2}({\bf v}\cdot{\bf a}){\bf v}]={\bf F}
, it is also the case that an electric field does not define an acceleration field for an electron, say. What is common to all electrons at the same position is
d{\bf P}/dt
.

In some important applications, the motion of the constituents is described, to a good approximation, as the combination of several trajectories, with distinct frequencies, or of bunches of frequencies (which we lump into one for simplicity),

{\bf r}(t)=\sum_{k}{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}(t),~~~~~~~~{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}e^{\scriptscriptstyle i\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}t}+\bar{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}e^{\scriptscriptstyle-i\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}t}),

(21)

where
\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}
are complex amplitudes, normalized such that for a circular orbit, the orbital radius is
|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|=(\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\cdot\bar{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}_{\scriptscriptstyle k})^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}
. Furthermore, in such instances, the (Newtonian) force on a constituent separates, to a good approximation, into forces that depend on the separate component trajectories:

{\bf F}[{\bf r}(t)]\approx\sum_{k}{\bf F}_{k}[{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}(t)].

(22)

Consider, e.g., a small many-body system, such as a binary star, or a star cluster, moving on a “large” orbit, of scales much larger than its size, in the field of a large body, such as a galaxy. Then, one component of the motion of a constituent corresponds to the large motion, and the force associated with it is that applied by the large body, while the other components describe the motion of the constituent within the body, and is dictated mainly by the inter body force. So, there is a natural separation of trajectories and the associated forces.

Another example is the vertical dynamics in a thin galactic disk. Each constituent – such as a star, or a gas element, may be viewed as performing a combination of two motions: one in the mean field of the galaxy characterized by an orbital size close to the galactic radius,
R
, at the position of the body and the other, a vertical motion, governed by the local density distribution vertical to the disk. Provided that we are probing heights much smaller than
R
, and that the characteristic frequencies are distinct, the separation of forces, as above, is a good approximation. A separation may also be justified if we are considering separately motions along different axes, i.e., if the vectorial components of Eq. (
3
) are described by different frequencies (e.g., in a triaxial harmonic field).

We can then write separate equations for the different components that are coupled though
\mathcal{A}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k})
, which depends on the motions in all the components.
The equations of motion (
3
) then read

m\hat{\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k})\mu[\mathcal{A}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k})/a_{0}]=\hat{\bf F}_{k}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}).

(23)

Each frequency component describes an elliptical orbit, with

{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}(t)=\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\cdot\bar{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}+{\rm Re}(\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\cdot\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}e^{\scriptscriptstyle 2i\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}t})=\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\cdot\bar{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}+|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\cdot\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|\cos{(2\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}t+\varphi_{\scriptscriptstyle k})}.

(24)

So,
|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|
is the root-mean-square radius, and
|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\cdot\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}
measures the ellipticity: it vanishes for a circular orbit, and equals
|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|
for a radial orbit.
Then,

\hat{\bf a}(\omega)=-\sqrt{2}\pi\sum_{k}\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}[\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k})+\bar{\bf r}^{\scriptscriptstyle*}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\delta(\omega+\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k})].

(25)

For
\omega>0
, needed in Eq. (
20
), we have (remembering that the product of delta functions with different frequencies vanishes)

|\hat{\bf a}(\omega)|=\sqrt{2}\pi\sum_{k}\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|\delta(\omega-\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}),~~~~~~~|\hat{\bf a}(\omega)\cdot\hat{\bf a}(\omega)|^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}=\sqrt{2}\pi\sum_{k}\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}\cdot\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}).

(26)

The two are equal for radial trajectories, and the second vanishes for circular ones.
Proceeding, using only the first for concreteness sake, we have

\mathcal{A}(\omega)=\sum_{k}\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|\theta\left(\frac{\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}}{\omega}\right).

(27)

To determine the motion, we need to know
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
at the frequencies underlying each component. For the
n
th component,

\mathcal{A}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n})=\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle n}|+\sum_{k\not=n}\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle k}|\theta\left(\frac{\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}}{\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n}}\right).

(28)

Thus, importantly, the acceleration measure
\mathcal{A}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n})
, which enters that MOND “magnification factor” for frequency
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
, namely,
1/\mu[\mathcal{A}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n})/a_{0}]
, does not equal the acceleration at
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
itself, which is
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle n}|
, but it picks up contributions from all other frequencies, weighted by
\theta(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle k}/\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle n})
.

IV.1
Circular orbits – rotation curves

For the important example of circular orbits in an axisymmetric field, underlying rotation curves of disk galaxies, there is only one frequency,
and the basic equation (
3
)
gives directly the MOND algebraic relation between the Newtonian and MOND accelerations, radius by radius,

a\mu(a/a_{0})=a_{\scriptscriptstyle N},

(29)

where

r

Here it follows simply from the general equations of motion (

\omega

\mathcal{I}

When the models are applied to the case where the forces are gravitational, this describes correctly the rotation curves of galaxies, with

r

IV.2

Another example that is easy to solve is that of the motion of a particle in a harmonic force field, for example, a star moving in the gravitational field of a spherical mass of constant density.

IV.3
Motion of a composite system in an external field

Consider now the so-called “center-of-mass-motion problem”. It concerns the fact that objects – such as stars and gas clouds – move in a galaxy according to MOND, while their constituents (e.g., ions) are subject to very high accelerations. The problem was solved early on for modified-gravity MOND
Bekenstein and Milgrom 1984
. In the context of the models discussed here, what matters is how the high-acceleration, high-frequency components of the constituent motions affect the low-acceleration, low-frequency components of motion in the galaxy. Take
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}
to be that of the dominant acceleration in the galaxy and
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
to represent the frequencies of the internal motions.

We need the MOND acceleration at
\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}

{\bf a}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1})\mu[\mathcal{A}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1})/a_{0}]={\bf a}_{\scriptscriptstyle N}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1})
,

\mathcal{A}(\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1})=\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}|+\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\bar{\bf r}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}|\theta\left(\frac{\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}{\omega_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}\right).

(33)

While for the problem at hand ,

IV.4
External-field effect

The MOND EFE pertains to a subsystem that is falling in the field of some larger mother system. Examples are a dwarf satellite, a globular cluster, or a binary star in the field of a galaxy; a galaxy in a field of a galaxy cluster; or the dynamics perpendicular to a galactic disk, while the region is being accelerated on its circular orbit in the field of the galaxy. MOND predicts, quite generically,

17

that the internal dynamics of the subsystem, with a characteristic acceleration

This is unlike Newtonian dynamics (and general relativity) where, as long as the external field may be considered constant across the subsystem (so that there are no tidal effects), the internal dynamics is oblivious to the external field in which the subsystem falls freely. This, in general relativity, is an expression of the strong equivalence principle, which is not obeyed in MOND.

We already saw in Sec.

The EFE was, in fact, found to be relevant, in varying degrees, in many astrophysical circumstances.
