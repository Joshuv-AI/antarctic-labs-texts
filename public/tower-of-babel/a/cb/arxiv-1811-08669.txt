Abstract
We present a preliminary study of the leading hadronic contribution to the running of the Weinberg angle \thetaW \thetaW \thetaW . The running is extracted from the correlation function of the electromagnetic current with the vector part of the weak neutral current using both the standard time-momentum representation method and the Lorentz-covariant coordinate-space method recently introduced by Meyer. Both connected and disconnected contributions have been computed on N f = 2 + 1 subscript 𝑁 f 2 1 N_{\mathrm{f}}=2+1 non-perturbatively ( a ) order 𝑎 \order{a} -improved Wilson fermions configurations from the Coordinated Lattice Simulations initiative. Similar covariant coordinate-space methods can be used to compute the leading hadronic contribution to the anomalous magnetic moment of the muon ( g − 2 ) μ subscript 𝑔 2 𝜇 (g-2)_{\mu} and to the running of the QED coupling α 𝛼 \alpha . Introduction

The Weinberg angle or weak mixing angle
\thetaW
is the parameter of the Standard Model of particle physics that parametrizes the mixing between electromagnetic and weak interactions

\sinW=\frac{g^{\prime 2}}{g^{2}+g^{\prime 2}},\qquad e=g\sin\thetaW=g^{\prime}\cos\thetaW

(1)

where
g
and
g^{\prime}
are the
\mathrm{SU}(2)_{L}
and
\mathrm{U}(1)_{Y}
couplings, respectively.
As a consequence of the running with energy of
g
and
g^{\prime}
, the Weinberg angle is a function of the energy scale
Q^{2}

\sinW(Q^{2})=\sinW\left[1+\Delta\sinW(Q^{2})\right],

(2)

where
\sinW=\sinW(Q^{2}=0)=$0.238\,71(9)$

[
1
]
is the value in low-energy limit.In particular, the leading hadronic contribution to the running is given by
[
2
,
3
]

\Delta_{\text{had}}\sin^{2}\thetaW(Q^{2})=-\frac{e^{2}}{\sinW}\left[\Pi^{\gamma Z}(Q^{2})-\Pi^{\gamma Z}(0)\right],

(3)

proportional to the subtracted
hadronic vacuum polarization
(
HVP
)

(Q_{\mu}Q_{\nu}-\delta_{\mu\nu}Q^{2})\Pi^{\gamma Z}(Q^{2})=\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\gamma Z}(Q^{2})=\int\differential[4]{x}\e{\I Q\cdot x}\expectationvalue{j_{\mu}^{Z}(x)j_{\nu}^{\gamma}(0)}

(4)

of the electromagnetic current
j_{\mu}^{\gamma}
and the vector part of the neutral weak current
j_{\mu}^{Z}

\displaystyle j_{\mu}^{\gamma}=\frac{2}{3}\bar{u}\gamma_{\mu}u-\frac{1}{3}\bar{d}\gamma_{\mu}d-\frac{1}{3}\bar{s}\gamma_{\mu}s+\frac{2}{3}\bar{c}\gamma_{\mu}c,

(5a)

\displaystyle j_{\mu}^{Z}=j_{\mu}^{3}-\sinW j_{\mu}^{\gamma},\qquad j_{\mu}^{3}=\frac{1}{4}\bar{u}\gamma_{\mu}u-\frac{1}{4}\bar{d}\gamma_{\mu}d-\frac{1}{4}\bar{s}\gamma_{\mu}s+\frac{1}{4}\bar{c}\gamma_{\mu}c.

(5b)

The vacuum polarization
\Pi^{\gamma Z}
is directly accessible to lattice computations
[
4
,
5
]
.
The computation is similar to that of the leading
HVP
contribution to the anomalous magnetic moment of the muon and, as in that case, different methods are available, such as the four-momentum approach or the
time-momentum representation
(
TMR
) method
[
6
]
.Using the latter,
\Pi^{\gamma Z}
is given by

\Pi^{\gamma Z}(Q^{2})-\Pi^{\gamma Z}(0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{x_{0}}G^{\gamma Z}(x_{0})K(x_{0},Q^{2}),\qquad K(x_{0},Q^{2})=x_{0}^{2}-\frac{4}{Q^{2}}\sin[2](\frac{Qx_{0}}{2}),

(6)

where
G^{\gamma Z}(x_{0})
is the zero-momentum projection of the correlator

G^{\gamma Z}(x_{0})=-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1,2,3}\int\differential[3]{x}G^{\gamma Z}_{kk}(x),\qquad G^{\gamma Z}_{\mu\nu}(x)=\expectationvalue{j_{\mu}^{Z}(x)j_{\nu}^{\gamma}(0)}.

(7)

Writing explicitly Wick’s contractions results in both connected and disconnected contributions

G^{\gamma Z}_{\mu\nu}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{5}{9}\sinW\right)C^{\ell,\ell}_{\mu\nu}(x)+\left(\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{9}\sinW\right)C^{s,s}_{\mu\nu}(x)\\
+\left(\frac{1}{6}-\frac{4}{9}\sinW\right)C^{c,c}_{\mu\nu}(x)-\frac{1}{9}\sinW D^{\ell+As,\ell-s}_{\mu\nu}(x),

(8)

where
A=3/(4\sinW)-1
, the disconnected charm contribution has been neglected, and

\displaystyle C^{f_{1},f_{2}}_{\mu\nu}(x)=-\expectationvalue{\Tr{D_{f_{1}}^{-1}(x,0)\gamma_{\mu}D_{f_{2}}^{-1}(0,x)\gamma_{\nu}}},

(9a)

\displaystyle D^{f_{1},f_{2}}_{\mu\nu}(x)=\expectationvalue{\Tr{D_{f_{1}}^{-1}(x,x)\gamma_{\mu}}\Tr{D_{f_{2}}^{-1}(0,0)\gamma_{\nu}}}.

(9b)

2
The
CCS
method

As an alternative to the
TMR
method, in this work we also implement the recently proposed
Lorentz-covariant coordinate space
(
CCS
) method
[
7
]
.
We rewrite the subtracted vacuum polarization as a covariant integral in four-dimensional coordinate space

\displaystyle\Pi^{\gamma Z}(Q^{2})-\Pi^{\gamma Z}(0)=\int\differential[4]{x}G^{\gamma Z}_{\mu\nu}(x)H_{\mu\nu}(x)=\smashoperator[l]{\sum_{i=1,2}^{}}\int\differential[4]{x}g_{i}(x)\mathcal{H}_{i}(\absolutevalue{x}),

(10)

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)=-\delta_{\mu\nu}G^{\gamma Z}_{\mu\nu}(x),\qquad g_{2}(x)=\frac{x_{\mu}x_{\nu}}{x^{2}}G^{\gamma Z}_{\mu\nu}(x),

(11)

where we used the Lorentz structure of the
CCS
kernel

H_{\mu\nu}(x)=-\delta_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\absolutevalue{x})+\frac{x_{\mu}x_{\nu}}{x^{2}}\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x}),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{i}(\absolutevalue{x})=x^{2}\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{i}(\absolutevalue{Q}\absolutevalue{x}),

(12)

that can be expressed in term of generalized hypergeometric functions
\hypgeo{2}{3}

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{4608}\left[24\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,3;-z^{2}/4\right)-20\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,4;-z^{2}/4\right)\right.\\
\left.+3\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,5;-z^{2}/4\right)\right],\end{multlined}\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{4608}\left[24\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,3;-z^{2}/4\right)-20\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,4;-z^{2}/4\right)\right.\\
\left.+3\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,5;-z^{2}/4\right)\right],

(13c)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{2}(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{1152}\left[6\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,3;-z^{2}/4\right)-8\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,4;-z^{2}/4\right)\right.\\
\left.+4\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,4,4;-z^{2}/4\right)-\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,4,5;-z^{2}/4\right)\right].\end{multlined}\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{2}(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{1152}\left[6\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,3;-z^{2}/4\right)-8\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,3,4;-z^{2}/4\right)\right.\\
\left.+4\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,4,4;-z^{2}/4\right)-\hypgeo{2}{3}\left(1,1;2,4,5;-z^{2}/4\right)\right].

(13f)

2.1
Non-transverse kernel

The kernel defined in Eq. (
12
) is transverse, i.e. it satisfies the condition
\partial_{\mu}H_{\mu\nu}(x)=0
, or equivalently
\absolutevalue{x}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}(\absolutevalue{x})=\absolutevalue{x}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x})+3\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x})
.
We can modify it by adding a non-transverse component

\partial_{\mu}[x_{\nu}\mathcal{F}(\absolutevalue{x})]=\delta_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{F}(\absolutevalue{x})+\frac{x_{\mu}x_{\nu}}{x^{2}}\absolutevalue{x}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{x}).

(14)

Using the fact that
\partial_{\mu}G_{\mu\nu}(x)=0
, this modification entails only a surface term
\int\differential[4]{x}\partial_{\mu}[G_{\mu\nu}x_{\nu}\mathcal{F}]
, that vanishes in infinite volume.
We parametrize the non-transverse component with
\mathcal{F}(\absolutevalue{x})=(\alpha+\gamma)\mathcal{H}_{1}(\absolutevalue{x})-\gamma\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x})
, so that the following choices of the
\alpha
and
\gamma
parameters

H_{\mu\nu}(x)=\begin{cases}-\delta_{\mu\nu}\left[4\mathcal{H}_{1}(\absolutevalue{x})-\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x})\right]/3,&\alpha=0,\,\gamma=-1/3,\\
-\delta_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x})+4\frac{x_{\mu}x_{\nu}}{x^{2}}\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x}),&\alpha=0,\,\gamma=1,\\
\frac{x_{\mu}x_{\nu}}{x^{2}}\left[\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x})+\absolutevalue{x}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}(\absolutevalue{x})\right],&\alpha=1,\,\gamma=0,\end{cases}

(15)

correspond respectively to kernels that have a
“monopole”

\delta_{\mu\nu}
structure, a traceless
“quadrupole”
one, and a
(x_{\mu}x_{\nu})/x^{2}
structure.
Depending on the value of
\alpha
and
\gamma
, the kernel samples differently the correlator at short or long distances.
In calculating
\Pi^{\prime}(0)
, one easily shows that, using a kernel containing no
\delta_{\mu\nu}
tensor structure, the integrand falls off at long distances with one power of
\absolutevalue{x}
faster than with the transverse kernel, if one assumes the correlator to be dominated by a single vector meson.
In our case, both the
\alpha=1
and
\gamma=1
options result in shorter-range kernels, which in turn leads to smaller statistical errors from the integration tail, but might be affected by larger short-distance discretization artefacts.

2.2
Lattice discretization and
\order{a}
improvement

The lattice discretization of Eq. (
10
) is straightforward and amounts to just employing the lattice-determined
G^{\gamma Z}_{\mu\nu}(x)
.
Here we show only the
\order{a}
-improved local vector current

(V_{\mathrm{I}})_{\mu}(x)=V_{\mu}(x)+ac_{V}\partial_{\alpha}T_{\mu\alpha}(x),\qquad V_{\mu}(x)=\bar{\psi}(x)\gamma_{\mu}\psi(x),\quad T_{\mu\alpha}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\psi}(x)\commutator{\gamma_{\mu}}{\gamma_{\alpha}}\psi(x),

(16)

with non-perturbatively determined improvement coefficient
c_{V}
and renormalization
[
8
,
9
]
, but the conserved definition has also been studied.
Improving the integral in Eq. (
10
) results in

\int\differential[4]{x}\left\{\expectationvalue{V_{\mu}(x)V_{\nu}(0)}H_{\mu\nu}(x)-ac_{V}\left[\expectationvalue{T_{\mu\alpha}(x)V_{\mu}(0)}-\expectationvalue{V_{\mu}(x)T_{\mu\alpha}(0)}\right]\partial_{\mu}H_{\mu\nu}(x)\right\}\\
=\smashoperator[l]{\sum_{i=1,2,3}^{}}\int\differential[4]{x}g_{i}(x)\mathcal{H}_{i}(\absolutevalue{x}),

(17)

where we integrated by part and used translation invariance, and the
\order{a}
-improvement contribution is encoded in the
g_{3}
correlator and the
\mathcal{H}_{3}
kernel

g_{3}(x)=\frac{ax_{\alpha}}{x^{2}}c_{V}\left[\expectationvalue{T_{\mu\alpha}(x)V_{\mu}(0)}-\expectationvalue{V_{\mu}(x)T_{\mu\alpha}(0)}\right],\qquad\mathcal{H}_{3}(\absolutevalue{x})=\absolutevalue{x}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}(\absolutevalue{x})+\mathcal{H}_{2}(\absolutevalue{x}).

(18)

3.1
Integration strategy

In order to implement the four-dimensional integral in Eq. (
10
) on the lattice, we define contributions summed over lattice points in a four-dimensional sphere of radius
r

\Delta_{\text{had}}\sin^{2}\thetaW(Q^{2},r)=-\frac{e^{2}}{\sinW}\int_{S_{r,L}}\differential[4]{x}s(r,L)g_{i}(x)\mathcal{H}_{i}(\absolutevalue{x}),\quad S_{r,L}=\{x:\absolutevalue{x}<r,\,x_{i}<L/2-\delta\}.

(20)

Having to deal with lattices of finite size
L
in space directions, we modify the sum including only points that satisfy
x_{i}<L/2-\delta
for
i=1,2,3
, with
\delta=4a
, and correcting for the missing points with an exactly calculable geometric factor
s(r,L)
.

The different contributions are plotted in Figure
1
against the value of
r
.
The left plot shows that the light, strange and valence-charm contribution are precisely determined, with statistical errors that are smaller than the line thickness.
At large
\absolutevalue{x}
, the exponential suppression of both the signal and the statistical error of
g_{i}(x)
dominates over the polynomial growth of the kernel, and the connected contributions can be extracted for
r\to\infty
.

3.2
Long-distance systematics of the disconnected contribution

In contrast with the connected-contribution case, the statistical error of the disconnected integrand grows at large
\absolutevalue{x}
.
As a consequence, the integral in the right plot in Figure
1
looses its signal at distances
\gtrsim 50a
.
To estimate the disconnected contribution, we cut the integration of the noisy correlator tail.
In order to constrain the induced systematic effect, we observe that the long-distance behaviour of
G^{\gamma Z}(x)
is dominated by the isospin-triplet component

G^{\gamma Z}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sinW\right)G^{I=1}(x),\qquad G^{I=1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}C^{\ell,\ell}(x).

(21)

This component is easily estimated since it does not include disconnected contributions. In turns, the ratio between the disconnected contribution
G^{\gamma Z}_{\text{disc}}(x)
and
G^{I=1}(x)
tends asymptotically to a constant

\frac{G^{\gamma Z}_{\text{disc}}(x)}{G^{I=1}(x)}=\frac{G^{\gamma Z}(x)-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\sinW\right)G^{I=1}(x)}{G^{I=1}(x)}+\frac{1}{9}\sinW\\
-\left(\frac{1}{6}-\frac{2}{9}\sinW\right)\frac{C^{s,s}(x)}{C^{\ell,\ell}(x)}-\left(\frac{1}{3}-\frac{8}{9}\sinW\right)\frac{C^{c,c}(x)}{C^{\ell,\ell}(x)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{9}\sinW.

(22)

Thus, we compare the statistical error of
\Delta_{\text{had}}^{\text{disc}}\sin^{2}\thetaW(Q^{2},r)
with the value of
\Delta_{\text{had}}^{I=1}\sin^{2}\thetaW(Q^{2},r\to\infty)-\Delta_{\text{had}}^{I=1}\sin^{2}\thetaW(Q^{2},r)
.
The latter represents an upper bound on the neglected contribution, and
r
is chosen such that this upper bound is less than half of the statistical error of
\Delta_{\text{had}}^{\text{disc}}\sin^{2}\thetaW(Q^{2},r)
.

Preliminary results in Table
2
and Figure
2
indicate that we are able to estimate the disconnected contribution with a statistical error that is only
0.1
-
0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\char 37\relax}
of
\Delta_{\text{had}}\sinW(Q^{2})
at
Q^{2}=$4\text{\,}{\mathrm{GeV}}^{2}$
, and the systematic errors from the truncation of the spacetime summation under control.
The statistical error is dominated by the light connected contribution, that has been computed with a low statistics and can easily be improved.
A direct comparison between the
TMR
and
CCS
methods has been performed:
In the connected-contribution case, the two methods result in the same statistical precision, and the difference in the central values can be attributed to different discretization effects.
In the disconnected-contribution case, the statistical precision of the
CCS
method shows a different volume dependence than the
TMR
method one.
In particular, disconnected contributions estimated with the
CCS
method are comparatively slightly less precise on the two
($3.1\text{\,}\mathrm{fm}$)^{3}
boxes, but they are more precise on the larger-volume ensemble, in particular when a non-transverse kernel is employed.
This suggests to further investigate
CCS
methods on lattices with a large physical volume, applying them also to the computation of the leading
HVP
contribution to
(g-2)_{\mu}
and to the running of the QED coupling
\alpha
.
Varying the energy scale up to
Q^{2}=$10\text{\,}{\mathrm{GeV}}^{2}$
does not affect these conclusions.

The results presented in Table
2
are preliminary and a full assessment of systematic errors is lacking.
The main systematics to be assessed are finite-volume effects and scale-setting errors.
Moreover, ensembles with different lattice spacings are needed to confirm that different methods result in compatible values in the continuum limit.
Nevertheless, these results shows that it is possible to achieve a sub-percent determination of
\Delta_{\text{had}}\sinW(Q^{2})
, including the disconnected contribution with full control of the integration tail systematics.
In general, lattice methods compare very favourably to the phenomenological determination
[
3
]
, which is affected by the systematics from flavour separation.
