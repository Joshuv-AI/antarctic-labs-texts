Abstract
. We provide the detailed asymptotic behavior for first-order aggregation models of heterogeneous oscillators. Due to the dissimilarity of natural frequencies, one could expect that all relative distances converge to definite positive value and furthermore that each oscillator converges to a possibly different stationary point. In order to establish the desired results, we introduce a novel method, called dimension reduction method that can be applied to a specific situation when the degree of freedom of the natural frequency is one. In this way, we would say that although a small perturbation is allowed, convergence toward an equilibrium of the gradient flow is still guaranteed. Several first-order aggregation models are provided as concrete examples by using the dimension reduction method to study the structure of the equilibrium, and numerical simulations are conducted to support theoretical results.

1.
Introduction

Collective behaviors of synchronous many-body systems have been widely studied after the work of two pioneers Kuramoto
[
9
,
10
]
and Winfree
[
15
,
16
]
. Depending on the dissimilarity of particles (or agents), a synchronous system is classified into two types: homogeneous and heterogeneous, and such dissimilarity is modeled as natural frequencies of particles. If natural frequencies are all the same, then particles are called identical and the corresponding system is said to be homogeneous. Otherwise, particles are called nonidentical and the system becomes heterogeneous.

In literature, there have been lots of developments for homogeneous systems compared to heterogeneous ones.
As will be shown later, we use the Kuramoto model
[
9
]
, swarm sphere models on real and complex spheres
[
5
,
11
,
13
]
and the Lohe matrix model
[
12
]
as concrete examples. All these models with homogeneous structures can be represented as gradient flows with total relative distances as (analytic) potentials. Then, since underlying manifolds are compact, it follows from dynamical system theories that all solutions converge to stationary points. However, when a heterogeneous system is considered, natural frequencies are incorporating into the system and this leads to the breakdown of a gradient flow. This causes numerous mathematical difficulties in analyzing asymptotic behaviors of the systems. That’s why studying a heterogeneous system is much harder than a homogeneous system. Precisely, when the gradient flow is slightly perturbed in the sense that

\dot{\mathcal{X}}=-\kappa\nabla_{\mathcal{X}}V(\mathcal{X})+\varepsilon f(\mathcal{X}),\quad\mathcal{X}(t)\in\mathcal{M},\quad t\geq 0,

(1.1)

where perturbation term
f(\mathcal{X})
is given to maintain the positive invariance of the underlying manifold (see
[
2
]
for another perturbation of a gradient flow). Note that equation (
1.1
) cannot be written as a gradient flow. Thus, convergence towards equilibrium would not be guaranteed. In addition, structure or existence of an equilibrium is not even known. In fact, if
\kappa
is sufficiently small, then equilibrium might not exist (see Proposition
2.3
). However, if we adopt our strategy, then we can show that a solution to (
1.1
) with some initial frameworks converges to an equilibrium, and a structure of the equilibrium is revealed.

The purpose of this paper is to analyzing several heterogeneous systems with our newly developed technique, so-called
dimension reduction method
which consists of the following two steps:

\bullet
(Step 1): reduce a given heterogeneous system into two subsystems: one is the heterogeneous Kuramoto system and the other is a homogeneous-like system.

\bullet
(Step 2): use the results for the heterogeneous Kuramoto system, such as the emergence of phase-locked states to investigate the asymptotic behavior of the original heterogeneous system.

In this work, we apply this technique to various first-order heterogeneous aggregation models. As a simple example and motivation for later argument, we here briefly introduce our method with a complex swarm sphere model in
[
8
]
as a simple example:

\dot{z}_{j}={\mathrm{i}}H_{j}z_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(z_{k}-\langle z_{j},z_{k}\rangle z_{j}),\quad t>0,\quad j\in[N]:=\{1,2,\cdots,N\},

where
z_{j}\in\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
H_{j}
is a
d\times d
Hermitian matrix. First, we impose the conditions on natural frequencies so that their degree of freedom is one; that is, natural frequencies are only determined by one single parameter: for
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
being a scalar,
H_{j}=a_{j}I_{d}
. Of course, since degree of freedom for a Hermitian matrix over
\mathbb{R}
is
\frac{d(d+1)}{2}
, there arises loss in this sense. As compensate, we are able to associate the Kuramoto model whose natural frequencies are given as
\{a_{i}\}
:

\dot{\theta}_{j}=a_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j}),\quad t>0,\quad j\in[N].

(1.2)

One of the most notable feature of (
1.2
) is that convergence toward an equilibrium can be achieved under a large coupling strength regime (see Proposition
2.1
). In other words, asymptotic behavior of a solution to (
1.2
) is somehow fully known. Then, we define an auxiliary intermediate variable
\xi_{j}
which is defined as

\xi_{j}(t):=z_{j}(t)e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}(t)}.

After deriving a temporal evolution of
\xi_{j}
, we will find a sufficient condition such that all
\xi_{j}
converges to a common stationary point, say,
\xi^{\infty}
. By virtue of good convergence property for (
1.2
), we combine two convergences to show that
z_{j}
converges to
\xi^{\infty}e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}^{\infty}}
whose modulus is turn out to be one.

Our main result of this paper is developing a novel method, namely, dimension reduction method in the above to study the asymptotic behavior of heterogeneous (or nonidentical) aggregation models. For detailed analysis, we consider four different aggregation models: the real swarm sphere model, the Schrödinger–Lohe model, the Lohe Hermitian sphere model and the Lohe matrix model. The first result is concerned with the swarm sphere model on the real unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
:

\dot{x}_{i}=\Omega_{i}x_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(x_{k}-\langle x_{i},x_{k}\rangle x_{i}),

(1.3)

subject to the initial data
x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Here,
\Omega_{i}
is an
d\times d
skew-symmetric matrix which plays a role of natural frequency of the
i
-th agent. Then, one can verify that
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is positively invariant under (
1.3
). We reformulate it to the model defined on the complex unit sphere
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
by means of the complexification (see Lemma
3.1
). It is worthwhile mentioning that it would not be possible to analyze (
1.3
), since a skew-symmetric matrix is more harder to handle compared to a skew-Hermitian matrix. Thus, we find an equivalence reformulation of (
1.3
) in
\mathbb{C}
. Then, after associating the Kuramoto model and using aggregation estimates, one can deduce that a solution converges to equilibrium (see Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
). It should be noted that to the best of our knowledge, this is the first mathematically rigorous result for (
1.3
) with different
\{\Omega_{i}\}
where convergence to equilibrium is established.

Second, we deal with two aggregation models on
\mathbb{C}
: the Schrödinger–Lohe model and the Lohe Hermitian sphere model. Similar to the previous circumstance, we use the dimension reduction method to show that solutions to both models converge to the equilibrium. Previously, there are few available results (e.g.,
[
4
]
) for the convergence of relative distances, not the point itself. However in this approach, existence of equilibrium for each configuration can be shown.

Third, the models on real and complex unit spheres are lastly extended to the model on the unitary group, so-called the Lohe matrix model:

{\mathrm{i}}\dot{U}_{i}U_{i}^{\dagger}=H_{i}+\frac{{\mathrm{i}}\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(U_{k}U_{i}^{\dagger}-U_{i}U_{k}^{\dagger}),\quad t>0,\quad i\in[N],

where
H_{i}
is a
d\times d
Hermitian matrix. We assume that there exist a common Hermitian matrix
H
and scalars
\{a_{i}\}
such that
H_{i}=H-a_{i}I_{d}
.
We again apply the dimension reduction method to show that each element tends to a constant unitary matrix not necessarily the same under a strong coupling regime. Similar to the aforementioned argument, there is still a loss of degree of freedom; as a reward, we instead have fully analyzed the asymptotic behavior.
It should be mentioned that the authors in
[
7
]
have intensively studied the Lohe matrix model by using orbital stability estimate. In this way, they can show that existence of limit for relative composite matrices
\{U_{i}U_{j}^{\dagger}\}
. However, that for each
\{U_{i}\}
is still unknown. We extend this previous results by associating additional Kuramoto model with the help of the dimension reduction method.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review previous results in relevant literature that will be used in later sections, and provide gradient flow formulation of the aggregation models. In Section
3
, the swarm sphere model on the real unit sphere is considered. In order to prepare the basic setting, we reformulate the swarm sphere model on
\mathbb{R}
to the equivalent model but defined on
\mathbb{C}
. Then, we introduce our dimension reduction method to show that convergence toward equilibrium has been established. The Schrödinger–Lohe model and the Lohe Hermitian sphere model on
\mathbb{C}
in Section
4
and the Lohe matrix model on the unitary group in Section
5
are discussed. By applying the same method, we obtain the same results in the previous section. Finally, Section
6
is devoted to a brief summary of the main results and some future direction.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we review the state-of-art results of first-order aggregation models on the real and complex spheres and the unitary group. For the purpose of this article, we restrict ourselves to heterogeneous oscillators.

2.1.
Kuramoto model

First, we recall basic conservation law. If we sum the relation (
1.2
) with respect to
j\in[N]
, then one can easily find

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\dot{\theta}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}.

Hence, if the total sums of initial data and natural frequencies are zero, i.e.,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}=0,

(2.1)

then the zero sum is conserved along the flow.

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)=0,\quad t>0.

In what follows, when the Kuramoto model is considered, we always assume (
2.1
). Although the Kuramoto model has been intensively and extensively investigated in literature, however for the concise presentation of the paper, we only introduce the results without proofs that will be used for our later argument.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\{\theta_{j}\}
be a solution to the following Cauchy problem:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{j}=a_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j}),\quad t>0,\\
\theta_{j}(0)=\theta_{j}^{0}\in\mathbb{R},\quad j\in[N],\end{cases}

with initial data and natural frequencies satisfying (
2.1
), and
\mathcal{D}(a)
be the maximal diameter for natural frequencies:

\mathcal{D}(a):=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq N}|a_{i}-a_{j}|.

Then, the following assertions hold.

(i) (
[
3
]
)
If
\mathcal{D}(\Theta^{0})<\pi
, then the maximal diameter satisfies

\frac{{\textup{d}}}{{\textup{d}t}}\mathcal{D}(\Theta)\leq\mathcal{D}(a)-\kappa\mathcal{D}(\Theta),\quad t>0.

Thus, if initial diameter satisfy
\mathcal{D}(\Theta^{0})<\frac{\mathcal{D}(a)}{\kappa}<\pi
, then

\mathcal{D}(\Theta(t))<\frac{\mathcal{D}(a)}{\kappa},\quad t>0.

(ii) (
[
6
]
)
If the coupling strength and initial data satisfy

R_{0}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\cos(\theta_{i}^{0}-\theta_{j}^{0})}>0,\quad\kappa>1.6\frac{\mathcal{D}(a)}{R_{0}},

then there exists
\{\theta_{j}^{\infty}\}
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\theta_{j}(t)=\theta_{j}^{\infty},\quad j\in[N].

Real swarm sphere model

In
[

]
, the author has proposed the consensus protocol on the unit sphere, and later, the author in

\displaystyle x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\quad i\in[N].\end{cases}

(2.2)

Here,

1
]
)
Suppose that the coupling strength and initial data satisfy

\displaystyle\rho\in(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+}),\quad\alpha_{\pm}:=\frac{\kappa\pm\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-2\kappa(d+1)N\mathcal{D}(\Omega)}}{2\kappa},

Proposition 2.3

[

Suppose that system parameters and initial data satisfy

[

3.
Convergence toward equilibrium on the real unit sphere

In this section, we study the asymptotic behavior of the real swarm sphere model

\begin{cases}\vskip 2.84544pt\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=\Omega_{i}x_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(x_{k}-\langle x_{i},x_{k}\rangle x_{i}),\quad t>0,\\
\displaystyle x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\quad i\in[N].\end{cases}

(3.1)

3.1.
Complexification

In what follows, we find an equivalent formulation of (
3.1
) by means of ‘complexification.’ We first consider the case of an even dimension. For a given
\Omega_{i}\in\textup{Skew}_{2d}(\mathbb{R})
, we decompose
\Omega_{i}
into

\Omega_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{i}&B_{i}\\
-B_{i}^{\top}&C_{i}\end{pmatrix},\quad A_{i},C_{i}\in\textup{Skew}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),\quad B_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R}).

In addition for
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we associate a
d
-dimensional complex vector
\omega_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
defined as

x_{i}=:(y_{i},z_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\omega_{i}:=y_{i}+{\mathrm{i}}z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Then, one can easily check

|\omega_{i}|^{2}=|y_{i}|^{2}+|z_{i}|^{2}=|x_{i}|^{2}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(x_{i},\Omega_{i})
be a solution to (
3.1
) that can be decomposed into

x_{i}=(y_{i},z_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\Omega_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{i}&B_{i}\\
-B_{i}^{\top}&C_{i}\end{pmatrix}\in\textup{Skew}_{2d}(\mathbb{R}),\quad i\in[N].

(3.2)

Suppose that the composition matrices
A_{i},B_{i}
and
C_{i}
satisfy

A_{i}=C_{i}\in\textup{Skew}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),\quad B_{i}\in\textup{Sym}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),\quad i\in[N].

(3.3)

Then, the swarm sphere model (
3.1
) is equivalent to the following model on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
: for
\omega_{i}=y_{i}+{\mathrm{i}}z_{i}
,

\dot{\omega}_{i}=\Xi_{i}\omega_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\omega_{k}-\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle]\omega_{i}),\quad\Xi_{i}:=A_{i}-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i}.

(3.4)

Moreover, the unit modulus is conserved along flow (
3.4
). Hence, (
3.4
) is defined on
\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Proof.

We divide (
1.3
) as two subsystems: a free flow and an interaction flow.

(i) For a free flow part, since
x_{i}
and
\Omega_{i}
satisfy (
3.2
), we rewrite the free flow as

\dot{x}_{i}=\Omega_{i}x_{i}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{pmatrix}\dot{y}_{i}\\
\dot{z}_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{i}y_{i}+B_{i}z_{i}\\
-B_{i}^{\top}y_{i}+C_{i}z_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

Then,
\dot{\omega}_{i}
satisfies

\dot{\omega}_{i}=\dot{y}_{i}+{\mathrm{i}}\dot{z}_{i}=(A_{i}y_{i}+B_{i}z_{i})+{\mathrm{i}}(-B_{i}^{\top}y_{i}+C_{i}z_{i})=(A_{i}-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i}^{\top})y_{i}+(C_{i}-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i})({\mathrm{i}}z_{i}).

Since
A_{i},B_{i}
and
C_{i}
satisfy (
3.3
), a free flow
\dot{x}_{i}=\Omega_{i}x_{i}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is equivalent to
\dot{\omega}_{i}=\Xi_{i}\omega_{i}
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with a skew-Hermitian matrix
\Xi_{i}=A_{i}-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i}
.

(ii) On the other hand for an interaction flow, we observe

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\langle y_{i}+{\mathrm{i}}z_{i},y_{k}+{\mathrm{i}}z_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\langle y_{i},y_{k}\rangle+\langle z_{i},z_{k}\rangle+{\mathrm{i}}(\langle z_{i},y_{k}\rangle-\langle y_{i},z_{k}\rangle).

Since the relation
\langle x_{i},x_{k}\rangle=\langle y_{i},y_{k}\rangle+\langle z_{i},z_{k}\rangle
holds, we find the relation between the complex and real inner products:

\langle x_{i},x_{k}\rangle=\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}].

Finally for the last assertion, the unit modulus property direct follows from the equivalence relation between (
3.1
) and (
3.4
):

\displaystyle\frac{{\textup{d}}}{{\textup{d}t}}|\omega_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\dot{\omega}_{i},\omega_{i}\rangle+\langle\omega_{i},\dot{\omega}_{i}\rangle=\langle(A_{i}-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i})\omega_{i},\omega_{i}\rangle+\langle\omega_{i},(A_{i}-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i})\omega_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big(\langle\omega_{k},\omega_{i}\rangle-\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle]|\omega_{i}|^{2}+\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle-\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle]|\omega_{i}|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\langle A_{i}\omega_{i},\omega_{i}\rangle+\langle\omega_{i},A_{i}\omega_{i}\rangle+\langle-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i}\omega_{i},\omega_{i}\rangle+\langle\omega_{i},-{\mathrm{i}}B_{i}\omega_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{2\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle](1-|\omega_{i}|^{2})

\displaystyle=\langle(A_{i}+A_{i}^{\top})\omega_{i},\omega_{i}\rangle+\langle{\mathrm{i}}(B_{i}^{\top}-B_{i})\omega_{i},\omega_{i}\rangle+\frac{2\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle](1-|\omega_{i}|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle](1-|\omega_{i}|^{2}).

Hence, we have

1-|\omega_{i}(t)|^{2}=(1-|\omega_{i}^{0}|^{2})\exp\left(-\frac{2\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\textup{Re}[\langle\omega_{i},\omega_{k}\rangle]\right),

which gives the desired result.
∎

On the other hand for the odd dimension, we will basically extend
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2d+2}
and apply the same argument of an even dimensional case. Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
and
\Omega_{i}\in\textup{Skew}_{2d+1}(\mathbb{R})

3.2.
Dimension reduction method

In this subsection, we introduce a novel method to investigate the asymptotic behavior for heterogeneous aggregation models. First, we assume that the natural frequency matrices satisfy the following zeroth-order approximation condition: precisely, there exists
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\Xi_{j}=\Xi+{\mathrm{i}}a_{j}I_{d},\quad j\in[N].

Due to the hermitian symmetry, one would set

\Xi=O_{d}.

Then, our system reads as

\dot{w}_{j}=\mathrm{i}a_{j}w_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(w_{k}-\mathrm{Re}\left(\langle w_{k},w_{j}\rangle\right)w_{j}\right),\quad t>0,\quad j\in[N].

(3.5)

For
a_{i}
that appears in (
3.5
), we associate the Kuramoto model whose natural frequencies are exactly same as
\{a_{i}\}
. To be more specific, let
\{\theta_{i}\}
be a solution to

\dot{\theta}_{j}=a_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j}),\quad t>0,\quad j\in[N],

(3.6)

together with initial data and natural frequencies satisfying zero sum conditions:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(0)=0\quad\textup{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}=0.

Thus, we would say that two models (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) are weakly coupled through natural frequencies. We define a vector

z_{j}(t):=w_{j}(t)e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}(t)}\in\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d},\quad t>0.

Below, we find the governing equation for
z_{i}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
w_{j}
and

) and (

(3.7)

Proof.

By straightforward calculation, one finds the desired dynamics for
z_{i}

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}a_{j}z_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})}z_{k}-\mathrm{Re}(\langle e^{\mathrm{i}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})}z_{k},z_{j}\rangle)z_{j}\right)-\mathrm{i}\left(a_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})\right)z_{j}

∎

.

4.
Convergence toward equilibrium on the complex unit sphere

In this section, we investigate the detailed asymptotic behavior of the Schrödinger–Lohe model and the Lohe Hermitian model whose states are represented in general by complex numbers. We continue to apply our dimension reduction method.

4.1.
Schrödinger–Lohe model

In this subsection, we consider the heterogeneous Schrödinger–Lohe model with a specific structure:

V_{j}(x)=V(x)+a_{j},\quad a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\quad j\in[N].

In other words, external potentials are given as a (small) perturbation
\{a_{j}\}
of a common potential
V(x)
. Furthermore, as in the previous section, we assume that the perturbation
a_{j}
has zero average. Then, our model reads as

{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{t}\psi_{j}=\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V\right)\psi_{j}+a_{j}\psi_{j}+\frac{{\mathrm{i}}\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\psi_{k}-\langle\psi_{j},\psi_{k}\rangle\psi_{j}).

Since
e^{-{\mathrm{i}}(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V)}
is a unitary operator, it follows from the solution splitting property that we may assume
\psi_{j}=\psi_{j}(t)
satisfying

\dot{\psi}_{j}={\mathrm{i}}a_{j}\psi_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\psi_{k}-\langle\psi_{j},\psi_{k}\rangle\psi_{j}).

\psi_{j}(t)\in\mathbb{C}

\mathbb{C}^{d}
without any effort. In this case, the inner product is given as the standard one in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. As discussed in the previous section, we associate the Kuramoto model whose natural frequencies are determined as
\{a_{j}\}
with zero-average natural frequency:

\dot{\theta}_{j}=a_{j}+\frac{2\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j}).

(4.2)

It is worthwhile mentioning that the coupling strength in (
4.2

4.1
). We define

|\langle z_{i}^{\infty},z_{j}^{\infty}\rangle|=1,\quad i,j\in[N].

Proof.

In order to apply Proposition
4.2
, we recall (
4.9
):

|1-\langle\xi_{i}^{0},\xi_{j}^{0}\rangle|=|1-\langle\psi_{i}^{0},\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle+\langle\psi_{i}^{0},\psi_{j}^{0}\rangle(1-e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{j}^{0}-\theta_{i}^{0})})|\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{Z}^{0})+2\sin\frac{\mathcal{D}(\Theta^{0})}{2}.

Since initial data
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{Z}^{0})
satisfy (
4.16
), initial conditions for
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}^{0})
and
\mathcal{D}(\Theta^{0})
in (
4.15
) hold. Hence, it follows from Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
2.1
that there exist
\xi^{\infty}\in\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}^{\infty}}
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}z_{j}(t)=\xi^{\infty}e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}^{\infty}}=:z_{j}^{\infty},

which directly yields that
\langle z_{i}^{\infty},z_{j}^{\infty}\rangle
has unit modulus:

\langle z_{i}^{\infty},z_{j}^{\infty}\rangle=\langle\xi^{\infty}e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{i}^{\infty}},\xi^{\infty}e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}^{\infty}}\rangle=e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{i}^{\infty}-\theta_{j}^{\infty})}.

∎

Remark 4.3

As in Remark
4.1
, the Kuramoto model (
4.11
) with the coupling strength
2(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})
can be formally derived from (
4.10
) by using the ansatz:

z_{j}(t)=e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}(t)}.

5.
Convergence toward equilibrium on the unitary group

In this section, we are interested in the detailed asymptotic behavior of first-order matrix aggregation model, namely the Lohe matrix model:

\dot{U}_{j}=-{\mathrm{i}}H_{j}U_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(U_{k}-U_{j}U_{k}^{\dagger}U_{j}),\quad t>0,\quad j\in[N].

In particular, we restrict ourselves to specific natural frequency matrices satisfying

H_{j}=-a_{j}I_{d},\quad j\in[N].

(5.1)

Then, our model reads as

\dot{U}_{j}={\mathrm{i}}a_{j}U_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(U_{k}-U_{j}U_{k}^{\dagger}U_{j}),

(5.2)

linked with the Kuramoto model by natural frequencies
\{a_{j}\}
with zero average:

\dot{\theta}_{j}=a_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j}).

(5.3)

In order to proceed our dimension reduction method, we introduce

V_{j}(t):=e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}(t)}U_{j}(t).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
U_{j}
and
\theta_{j}
be solutions to (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), respectively. Then,
V_{j}=e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}}U_{j}
satisfies

\dot{V}_{j}=\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big[e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})}(V_{k}-V_{j})-e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})}V_{j}(V_{k}^{\dagger}-V_{j}^{\dagger})V_{j}\Big].

(5.4)

In addition, temporal evolution for
V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}
is determined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})^{\prime}&=\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big[e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{i})}(V_{k}V_{j}^{\dagger}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})-e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{k})}V_{i}(V_{k}^{\dagger}-V_{i}^{\dagger})V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt+e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})}(V_{i}V_{k}^{\dagger}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})+e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})}V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}(V_{k}-V_{i})V_{j}^{\dagger}\Big].\end{aligned}

(5.5)

Proof.

Direct calculation gives the desired result.
∎

We denote the maximal diameter

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V}(t)):=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\|V_{i}(t)-V_{j}(t)\|_{\textup{F}}=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\|I_{d}-V_{i}(t)V^{\dagger}_{j}(t)\|_{\textup{F}},\quad t>0.

Below, we derive a differential inequality for
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\{V_{j}\}
be a solution to (
5.4
). Then, the maximal diameter
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
satisfies

\frac{{\textup{d}}}{{\textup{d}t}}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})\leq-\kappa\left(1-2\sin\frac{\mathcal{D}(\Theta)}{2}\right)\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})+\kappa\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})^{2},\quad t>0.

(5.6)

Proof.

We use (
5.5
) to rewrite

\displaystyle\frac{{\textup{d}}}{{\textup{d}t}}(I_{d}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-2(I_{d}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})+(I_{d}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})(I_{d}-V_{k}V_{j}^{\dagger})+(I_{d}-V_{i}V_{k}^{\dagger})(I_{d}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}))

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big[(e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{i})}-1)(V_{k}V_{j}^{\dagger}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})-(e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{k})}-1)V_{i}(V_{k}^{\dagger}-V_{i}^{\dagger})V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+(e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})}-1)(V_{i}V_{k}^{\dagger}-V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger})+(e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})}-1)V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}(V_{k}-V_{i})V_{j}^{\dagger}\Big].

Hence, the desired inequality is derived.

∎

Next, we provide a sufficient condition leading to the zero convergence of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that coupling strength and natural frequencies satisfy

\kappa>\mathcal{D}(a),\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}=0,

and that there exists a positive constant
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\mathcal{D}(\Theta^{0})<\delta<\frac{\mathcal{D}(\Omega)}{\kappa}<1,\quad\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V}^{0})<1-\delta

(5.7)

Let
\{V_{j}\}
and
\{\theta_{j}\}
be solutions to (
5.4
) and (
5.3
), respectively. Then, the maximal diameter converges to zero exponentially:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V}(t))=0.

Moreover, there exists a unitary matrix
V^{\infty}\in\mathbf{U}(d)
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}V_{j}(t)=V^{\infty},\quad j\in[N].

(5.8)

Proof.

Since initial data
\Theta^{0}
satisfy
\eqref{E-8}_{1}
, we use Proposition
2.1
to find

\mathcal{D}(\Theta(t))<\delta,\quad t>0.

Then, (
5.6
) becomes

\frac{{\textup{d}}}{{\textup{d}t}}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})\leq-\kappa(1-\delta)\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})+\kappa\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})^{2},

which yields the desired zero convergence of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
, since initial data
\mathcal{V}^{0}
satisfy condition
\eqref{E-8}_{2}
.

On the other hand for the second assertion, we use (
5.4
) to attain

\|\dot{V}_{j}\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\kappa\sqrt{2d}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V}),

which gives
\|\dot{V}_{j}\|_{\textup{F}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Thus, the limit of
V_{j}(t)
exists:

\lim_{t\to\infty}V_{j}(t)=V_{j}^{0}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\dot{V}_{j}(s){\textup{d}}s=:V_{j}^{\infty}.

Moreover, since
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V})
tends to zero, all
\{V_{j}^{\infty}\}
should be same. Hence,
V^{\infty}
in (
5.8
) exists.
∎

In Proposition
5.1
, we have show that all
V_{j}
converge to constant unitary matrix
V^{\infty}\in\mathbf{U}(d)
under some initial conditions. We are now concerned with the asymptotic behavior of (
5.2
) as our main interest in this section. For this, we write

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{U}(t)):=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\|I_{d}-U_{i}(t)U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}\|_{\textup{F}},\quad t>0.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that the coupling strength and natural frequencies satisfy

\kappa>\mathcal{D}(a),\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}=0,

and that there exists
\delta\in(0,\beta)
such that

0<\delta<\frac{\mathcal{D}(a)}{\kappa}<\beta,\quad\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{U}^{0})<1-\delta-2d\sin\frac{\delta}{2},

(5.9)

where
\beta=\beta(d)
is a unique positive root for
1-s=2d\sin\frac{s}{2}
. Let
\{U_{j}\}
be a solution to (
5.2
). Then, there exists
U_{j}^{\infty}\in\mathbf{U}(d)
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}U_{j}(t)=U_{j}^{\infty},\quad j\in[N].

Moreover, the following relation holds:

U_{i}^{\infty}U_{j}^{\infty,\dagger}=e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{i}^{\infty}-\theta_{j}^{\infty})}I_{d},\quad i,j\in[N].

(5.10)

Proof.

In order to apply Proposition
5.1
, we write

I_{d}-V_{i}^{0}V_{j}^{0,\dagger}=I_{d}-U_{i}^{0}U_{j}^{0,\dagger}+U_{i}^{0}U_{j}^{0,\dagger}(1-e^{{\mathrm{i}}(\theta_{j}^{0}-\theta_{i}^{0})}),

and this gives

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V}^{0})\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{U}^{0})+2d\sin\frac{\mathcal{D}(\Theta^{0})}{2}.

Since (
5.9
) holds, initial conditions for
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{V}^{0})
and
\mathcal{D}(\Theta^{0})
in (
5.7
) are fulfilled. Hence, Proposition
5.1
together with Proposition
2.1
gives
V^{\infty}\in\mathbf{U}(d)
and
e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}^{\infty}}
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}U_{j}(t)=e^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{j}^{\infty}}V^{\infty}=:U_{j}^{\infty}\in\mathbf{U}(d).

Thus, (
5.10
) directly follows.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

In
[
7
]
, the authors studied the Lohe matrix model:

\dot{U}_{j}={\mathrm{i}}H_{j}U_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(U_{k}-U_{j}U_{k}^{\dagger}U_{j}),

with a general Hermitian matrix
H_{j}
. As mentioned in Section
2.4
, the authors showed that for a large coupling strength, asymptotic phase-locking emerges. In other words,

\lim_{t\to\infty}U_{i}(t)U_{j}(t)^{\dagger}\quad\textup{exists.}

Although limits of
\{U_{i}U_{j}^{\dagger}\}
exist, existence of limit for each
U_{j}
would not be guaranteed. Thus, if we let

X_{i}^{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}(U_{i}U_{1}^{\dagger})(t),\quad i\in[N],

then
\{X_{i}^{\infty}\}
should satisfy

X_{i}^{\infty}\Lambda X_{i}^{\infty,\dagger}=H_{i}-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}\kappa}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big(X_{i}^{\infty}X_{k}^{\infty,\dagger}-X_{k}^{\infty}X_{i}^{\infty,\dagger}\Big),

where
\Lambda
is an index-independent Hermitian matrix. Our result stated in Theorem
5.1
says that if
H_{j}
has a specific structure (
5.1
), then the convergence of limit for
U_{j}
can be exactly identified.

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the detailed asymptotic dynamics of first-order heterogeneous aggregation models, for instance, real and complex swarm sphere models, the Schrödinger-Lohe model, the Lohe Hermitian sphere model, and the Lohe matrix model. It should be noted that the aforementioned models can be regarded as generalized and high-dimensional Kuramoto models and thus can be reduced to the Kuramoto model by a simple ansatz. Since the existence and structure of equilibria for the Kuramoto model are well-known, the key idea, called dimension reduction method, is to decompose the given high-dimensional system into two subsystems: a heterogeneous Kuramoto model and a homogeneous modified model. In this manner, we can establish the existence of a solution to the high-dimensional model. Several numerical examples are provided in order to support theoretical results. There are still remaining issues. In fact, we have assumed that the natural frequency has the degree of freedom
1
. One of the future work is dedicated to generalizing this issue.
