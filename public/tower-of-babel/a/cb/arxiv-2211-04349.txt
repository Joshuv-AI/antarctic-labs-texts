A Deep solver for BSDEs with jumps
Kristoffer Andersson, Alessandro Gnoatto, Marco Patacca, Athena Picarelli

Abstract
. The aim of this work is to propose an extension of the deep solver by Han, Jentzen, E (2018) to the case of forward backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs) with jumps. As in the aforementioned solver, starting from a discretized version of the FBSDE and parametrizing the (high dimensional) control processes by means of a family of artificial neural networks (ANNs), the FBSDE is viewed as a model-based reinforcement learning problem and the ANN parameters are fitted so as to minimize a prescribed loss function. We take into account both finite and infinite jump activity by introducing, in the latter case, an approximation with finitely many jumps of the forward process. We successfully apply our algorithm to option pricing problems in low and high dimension and discuss the applicability in the context of counterparty credit risk.

1.
Introduction

Forward backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs) have become a popular tool in several application domains. One reason is that, due to the Feynman-Kac theorem, they essentially represent the stochastic counterpart of partial differential equations (PDEs): such equations naturally arise when modelling phenomena in diverse fields such as physics, engineering and finance. In finance, for example, PDEs/FBSDEs emerge in the context of the problem of pricing a contingent claim, when the underlying security, i.e. the state variable, is modelled by means of a diffusion process. Similar remarks apply when we consider state variables following a jump diffusion process: in this case a further integral/non local term appears in the Cauchy problem, which is then referred to as a partial integro-differential equation (PIDE). BSDEs in this case are in turn generalized by the introduction of a jump term driven by a Poisson random measure.

When a closed-form solution to the PIDE is not available and the dimension of the vector of state variables is low, several approaches for the numerical solution are availble, the most famous being finite difference and finite element methods. For further details, we refer the reader to
Cont and Tankov, 2003
,
Hilber et al., 2013
. However, the application of such standard numerical techniques becomes increasingly difficult as the dimension of the state space increases: the tightness of error bounds may be negatively affected or, more simply, the required computational time might increase significantly. Such phenomena are often referred to as the
curse of dimension
.

The above mentioned problems in a high dimensional setting provide the motivation for the recent surge in interest in machine-learning based methods to solve PDEs/BSDEs, where artificial neural networks (ANNs) are employed in order to parametrize e.g. the function satisfying the PDE and/or its gradient. From a mathematical perspective, ANNs are multiple nested compositions of
relatively simple multivariate functions.
ANNs can be graphically represented by logical maps with a structure that loosely resembles the one of the
human brain, where each neuron (corresponding to the application of a simple function on a multi-dimensional vector) is linked to a multitude of
neighboring neurons grouped in sequential layers.
The term deep neural networks refers to ANNs with several interconnected layers.
One remarkable property of ANNs is given in the ‘Universal Approximation Theorem’, which essentially states that any continuous function in any dimension can be represented to arbitrary accuracy by means of an ANN, and has been proven in different versions,
starting from the remarkable insight of Kolmogorov’s Representation Theorem in
Kolmogorov, 1956
and the seminal works of
Cybenko, 1989
and
Hornik, 1991
.
In a nutshell, this result states that any continuous function in any dimension can be represented to arbitrary accuracy by means of an ANN. Several authors have shown, under different assumption and in different settings, that ANNs can overcome the curse of dimension when approximating the solutions of PDEs, see e.g.
Jentzen et al., 2021
,
Reisinger and Zhang, 2020
,
Hutzenthaler et al., 2020
.

Recently, several authors have proposed different numerical schemes for PDEs/BSDEs based on the Feynman-Kac theorem in absence of jumps. Seminal works in this sense are
E et al., 2017
and
Han et al., 2018
, together with the convergence study in
Han and Long, 2020
. In these works, the BSDE is discretized forward in time via a standard Euler scheme. The initial condition and the controls of the BSDE, at each point in time, are then parametrized by a family of ANNs. The parameters of the ANNs are optimized by minimizing the expected square distance between the known terminal condition and the terminal value of the discretized BSDE. An alternative route has been followed in
Huré et al., 2020
, involving a backward recursion based on the dynamic programming principle. Another alternative is the so-called
deep splitting method
, first proposed in
Beck et al., 2021
: in this case the differential operator of a parabolic PDE is split into a linear and a non-linear part. The non-unique split is chosen is such a way that the non-linear part becomes small. The PDE is then solved iteratively over small time intervals. Such numerical solution involves the recursive computation of conditional expectations which are approximated by means of ANNs. We also mention, among others, the
deep Galerkin method
proposed in
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2018
: this method is not based on the link between FBSDE and PDEs instead, in their approach, the ANN is trained in order to satisfy the differential operator, the initial condition, and the boundary conditions. The above mentioned papers have been generalized in a multitude of directions and we refer to
Beck et al., 2021
for an extensive literature review.

Concerning cases involving jumps, we mention the work by
Barigou and Delong, 2022
, focusing on actuarial applications and the recent preprint
Georgoulis et al., 2024
.

The aforementioned contribution
Huré et al., 2020
has been extended to the jump diffusion case in
Castro, 2022
, which also proposes a convergence analysis. More recently, the backward dynamic programming approach has been considered also in
Bayraktar et al., 2023
, where reflected BSDEs are also considered. A generalization of
Beck et al., 2021
is presented in
Frey and Köck, 2022
with a convergence analysis provided in
Frey and Köck, 2025
. The deep Galerkin method instead is extended in
Al-Aradi et al., 2022
. We point out that in these works only finite jump activity is taken into account.

In this paper, we generalize the algorithm of
E et al., 2017
and
Han et al., 2018
to the jump diffusion setting taking into account the possible infinite jump activity. Following their reasoning, we discretize the FBSDE with jumps in a forward loop with respect to the time dimension. Next, we introduce deep learning approximations, in the form of two families of ANNs with different tasks: the first family approximates the value process of the BSDE, i.e. the solution of the PIDE, whereas the second is introduced in order to approximate the possibly high dimensional integral with respect to the Lévy measure that appears in the discretized BSDE. Our algorithm is first formulated for processes where the jump term exhibits finite activity. The first family of networks is trained by minimizing the expected square distance between the terminal value of the parametrized BSDE and the known terminal condition, in line with
E et al., 2017
and
Han et al., 2018
. To train the second family of ANNs we exploit the martingale property of compensated Poisson integrals and the
L^{2}
-minimality of conditional expectations, which results in a second penalty term to be minimized. We also propose an extension for the infinite activity/infinite variation of jumps case: we rely on the literature on the approximation of paths of infinite activity Lévy processes by means of compound Poisson processes, (see e.g.
Asmussen and Rosiński, 2001
among others) and we also introduce a perturbation of the diffusion coefficient to approximate jumps smaller than a pre-specified threshold. We provide a full error analysis of the approximation of infinite activity FBSDEs by means of finite activity FBSDEs. The full study of the convergence of the algorithm beyond the scope of the present paper and is the subject of future work. We point out that, during the review process of this paper, the preprint
Alasseur et al., 2024
appeared, introducing new algorithms and presenting an insightful comparison between local and global solvers for (possibly coupled) FBSDEs with jumps.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present our main assumptions and notations, whereas Section
3
is devoted to the presentation of our algorithm both in the finite and the infinite activity case. Section
4
presents some a priori error estimates that study the distance between the solution of the true FBSDE with jumps of infinite activity and the one of the approximating FBSDE driven by a compound Poisson process. Finally Section
5
presents some numerical experiments both in the finite and infinite activity setting with a focus on financial modeling: among others, we show that our methodology can be used to treat a PIDE in dimension
100
related to the pricing of a basket option. We also highlight the applicability of our methodology in the context of counterparty credit risk along the lines of
Gnoatto et al., 2023

Our objective is to numerically solve FBSDEs with jumps, possibly in a high dimensional setting, by means of deep learning techniques. It is well known that FBSDEs with jumps are strongly related to PIDEs, so our solver offers in turn a numerical approach to the solution of high dimentional PIDEs. The main theoretical tool that underpins this link is the Feynman-Kac theorem, that links PIDEs and FBSDEs in a Markovian setting. For the reader’s convenience, let us recall the version of the Feynman-Kac theorem that is relevant for our purposes. We are interested in finding a function
u\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
that satisfies the PIDE

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &-u_{t}(t,x)-\mathscr{L}u(t,x)\\
&\quad-f\left(t,x,u(t,x),D_{x}u(t,x)\sigma(x),\mathscr{J}u(t,x)\right)=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
&u(T,x)=g(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{aligned}

where

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathscr{L}u(t,x)=&\left\langle\mu(x),D_{x}u(t,x)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\sigma(x)D^{2}_{x}u(t,x),\sigma(x)\right\rangle\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(u(t,x+\Gamma(x,z))-u(t,x)-\left\langle\Gamma(x,z),D_{x}u(t,x)\right\rangle\right)\nu(dz),\\
\mathscr{J}u(t,x)=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(t,x+\Gamma(x,z))-u(t,x))\nu(dz).\end{aligned}

2.1.
The Deep BSDE solver by E-Han-Jentzen

We provide an overview of the algorithm proposed in
E et al., 2017
. The main idea of their approach is to consider, in a setting without jumps, a discretized version of the stochastic control problem in (
2
) and then approximate, at each time step, the control process by using an artificial neural network (ANN). First of all, let us observe that in absence of jumps the dynamics constraints in (
2
) becomes

(2.13)

\begin{cases}X^{t,x}_{s}=x+\int_{t}^{s}b(X^{t,x}_{r-})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{t}^{s}\sigma(X^{t,x}_{r-})^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{r},\\
Y^{y,Z}_{s}=y-\int_{t}^{s}f(r,X^{t,x}_{r-},Y^{y,Z}_{r-},Z_{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{t}^{s}(Z_{r})^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{r},\hskip 28.45274pt\hfill s\in[t,T],\end{cases}

and the control problem (
2
) reduces to an optimization with respect to
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
Z\in\mathbb{H}_{T}^{2}
.
Given
M\in\mathbb{N}
, a time discretization
0\leq t=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{M}=T
is introduced. For simplicity let us assume that the mesh grid is uniform with step
\Delta t>0
. Let
\Delta W_{n}=W_{t_{n+1}}-W_{t_{n}}
denote the increments of the Brownian motion. An Euler-Maruyama discretization of the FBSDE is considered, i.e. for
n=0,\ldots,M-1
we define the following discrete time version of the dynamics (
2.13
),

(2.14)

\begin{cases}{\widetilde{X}}_{{n+1}}&={\widetilde{X}}_{n}+b({\widetilde{X}}_{n})\Delta t+\sigma({\widetilde{X}}_{n})\Delta W_{n},\hfill\widetilde{X}_{0}=x,\\
{\widetilde{Y}}_{{n+1}}&={\widetilde{Y}}_{n}-f(t_{n},{\widetilde{X}}_{n},{\widetilde{Y}}_{n},\widetilde{Z}_{n})\Delta t+\widetilde{Z}_{n}^{\top}\Delta W_{n},\hfill\hskip 28.45274pt{\widetilde{Y}}_{0}=y.\end{cases}

The next step is to represent, for each
n
, the control process
\widetilde{Z}_{n}
in (
2.14
) by using an artificial neural network (ANN). In particular, feedforward ANN with
\mathcal{L}+1\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1,2\}
layers are employed. Each layer consists of
\upsilon_{\ell}

nodes
(also called
neurons
), for
\ell=0,\ldots,\mathcal{L}
. The
0
-th layer represents the
input layer
, while the
\mathcal{L}
-th
layer is called the
output layer
. The remaining
\mathcal{L}-1
layers are
hidden layers
. For simplicity, we set
\upsilon_{\ell}=\upsilon
,
\ell=1,\ldots,\mathcal{L}-1
. In our setting, the dimension of the input layer is set equal to
d
, i.e. the dimension of the forward process
X
.

A feedforward neural network is a function defined via the composition

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni x\longmapsto\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{L}}\circ\varrho\circ\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{L}-1}\circ\ldots\circ\varrho\circ\mathcal{A}_{1}(x),

where all
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,\mathcal{L}
, are affine transformations of the form

\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(x):=\mathcal{W}_{\ell}x+\beta_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,\mathcal{L}
,
where
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}
and
\beta_{\ell}
are matrices and vectors of suitable size called, respectively, weights and biases. The function
\varrho
, called
activation function
, is a univariate function
\varrho\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that is applied component-wise to vectors. With an abuse of notation, we denote

\varrho(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\upsilon})=\left(\varrho(x_{1}),\ldots,\varrho(x_{\upsilon})\right).

The elements of
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}
and
\beta_{\ell}
are the parameters of the neural network. One can regroup all parameters in a vector of size
R=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\mathcal{L}}\upsilon_{\ell}(1+\upsilon_{\ell})
.

Let, for each
n=0,\ldots,M-1
,
\mathcal{Z}^{{\rho_{n}}}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an ANN with parameters
{\rho_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
. Replacing
Z_{n}
with
\mathcal{Z}^{{\rho_{n}}}_{n}({\widetilde{X}}_{n})
in (
2.14
) one obtains the following dynamics for the process
\widetilde{Y}
parametrized by
\rho{=(\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{M-1})\in(\mathbb{R}^{R})^{M}}

(2.15)

\widetilde{Y}^{y,\rho}_{n+1}={\widetilde{Y}^{y,\rho}}_{n}-f(t_{n},{\widetilde{X}}_{n},\widetilde{Y}^{y,\rho}_{n},\mathcal{Z}^{{\rho_{n}}}_{n}({\widetilde{X}}_{{n}}))\Delta t+(\mathcal{Z}^{{\rho_{n}}}_{n}({\widetilde{X}}_{{n}}))^{\top}\Delta W_{n},\hfill\hskip 28.45274pt{\widetilde{Y}^{y,\rho}}_{0}=y.

Observe that the solver involves the introduction of
M
distinct neural networks, one network for each time step.

The family of neural network is then trained simultaneously over a set of simulated Monte Carlo samples of the dynamics via a standard training algorithm such as stochastic gradient descent or its extensions (one notable example in this sense being given by the ADAM algorithm, see
Kingma and Ba, 2015
) in order to minimize with respect to
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{R({\mathcal{Z}})}
the loss function

(2.16)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g(\widetilde{X}_{M})-\widetilde{Y}^{y,\rho}_{M}\right)^{2}\right].

3.
Deep Solver with jumps

The aim of this section is to present our proposed extension of the Deep BSDE solver of
E et al., 2017
to cover the case of the FBSDE with jumps (
2.3
)-(
2.4
).

The main challenge in view of the extension is represented by the case where the forward (and consequently the backward) process exhibits infinitely many jumps, be it of finite or infinite variation. Clearly, such jumps cannot be simulated exactly on a pre-specified grid of time instants. This problem has been first encountered in the literature on the discretization and simulation of Lévy-driven stochastic differential equations, see
Fournier, 2011
among others. For the reader’s convenience, we split the presentation of our solver in two subsections. In the first subsection we only consider the case with finitely many jumps since it can be simulated exactly via an Euler type discretization. In the second subsection we present the additional steps that we propose in order to cover the infinite activity case.

3.1.
Finite activity case

In this subsection we assume that the Lévy measure satisfies the condition
\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty
, meaning that the jumps are those of a compound Poisson process. We fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and introduce a uniform time discretization
0\leq t=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{M}=T
with time step
\Delta t=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
. As a starting point, we consider the forward SDE (
2.3
) between two subsequent time steps
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}
, namely,

\displaystyle X_{t_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=X_{t_{n}}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}b\left(X_{r-}\right)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\sigma\left(X_{r-}\right)^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\int^{t_{n+1}}_{t_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma\left(X_{r-},z\right)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z),

with
X_{t_{0}}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We freeze the coefficients between two consecutive time steps introducing the discrete time process
\widetilde{X}=(\widetilde{X}_{n})_{n=0,\ldots,M}
, defined recursively by

\widetilde{X}_{n+1}=\widetilde{X}_{{n}}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}b(\widetilde{X}_{n})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{n})^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\int^{t_{n+1}}_{t_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma(\widetilde{X}_{n},z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z),\qquad n=0,\ldots,M-1,

with
\widetilde{X}_{0}=x.

We write
N([0,t_{n}],\mathbb{R}^{d})
to denote the number of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued jumps occurring over the time interval
[0,t_{n}]
. The previous equation can then be rewritten as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \widetilde{X}_{n+1}&=\widetilde{X}_{n}+b(\widetilde{X}_{n})\Delta t+\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{n})^{\top}\Delta W_{n}\\
&\quad+\sum_{i=N([0,t_{n}],\mathbb{R}^{d})+1}^{N([0,t_{n+1}],\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Gamma(\widetilde{X}_{n},z_{i})-\Delta t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma(\widetilde{X}_{n},z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z),\qquad n=0,\ldots,M-1.\end{aligned}

We proceed similarly for the backward dynamics (
2.4
). We consider again two consecutive time instants
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}

\displaystyle Y_{t_{n+1}}=

\displaystyle Y_{t_{n}}-\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}f\Big(r,X_{r-},Y_{r-},Z_{r},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U_{r}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}Z_{r}^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U_{r}(z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z).

We now make use of the Feynman-Kac Theorem
2.5
, meaning that above dynamics can be read as follows

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Y_{t_{n+1}}=&Y_{t_{n}}-\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}f\Big(r,X_{r-},Y_{r-},\sigma({X}_{r-})^{\top}D_{x}u(r,{X}_{r-}),\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(r,X_{r-}+\Gamma(X_{r_{-}},z))-u(r,X_{r-})\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)\mathrm{d}r\\
&+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(D_{x}u(r,{X}_{r-}))^{\top}\sigma({X}_{r-})\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(r,X_{r-}+\Gamma(X_{r_{-}},z))-u(r,X_{r-})\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z).\end{split}

Next, we freeze again the coefficients and consider the following discrete time approximation

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{Y}_{{n+1}}=&\widetilde{Y}_{{n}}-f\Big(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{{n}},\widetilde{Y}_{{n}},\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{n})^{\top}D_{x}u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n}),\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n}+\Gamma(\widetilde{X}_{n},z))-u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n})\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)\Delta t\\
&+(D_{x}u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n}))^{\top}\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{n})\Delta W_{n}+\Delta u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n},\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n})-C(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n})\Delta t.\\
\end{split}

where we have defined the jumps of the forward SDE, the jumps of the BSDE and compensator of the BSDE, respectively as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=N([0,t_{n}],\mathbb{R}^{d})+1}^{N([0,t_{n+1}],\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Gamma(\widetilde{X}_{n},z_{i}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Delta u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n},\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n})

\displaystyle=u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n}+\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n})-u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n}),

(3.6)

\displaystyle C(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n}+\Gamma(\widetilde{X}_{n},z))-u(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n})\nu(\mathrm{d}z).

At this point, we introduce the neural networks used to approximate the solution of the FBSDE (
2.3
)-(
2.4
). Similar to the algorithm proposed in
Han et al., 2018
, we introduce two single trainable parameters to approximate the deterministic initial value
Y_{0}
, and the deterministic gradient
D_{x}u(0,x)
, respectively. Moreover, we use a sequence of neural networks to approximate
D_{x}u
at the discrete time points
\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{M-1}\}
. Since we consider BSDEs with jumps we have to approximate additional terms in the BSDE (the last two terms in (
3.3
)). Below we introduce all approximations and explain the algorithm.

We employ first three sequences of neural networks to parametrize the functions
D_{x}u
,
\Delta u
and
C
. Thus, for
n=1,2,\ldots,M-1
, we introduce the mappings
\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\rho_{n}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\varphi_{n}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\vartheta_{n}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
parametrized by
\rho_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{\rho}}
,
\varphi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{\varphi}}
and
\vartheta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{\vartheta}}
, respectively. Here
R_{\rho}\in\mathbb{N}
,
R_{\varphi}\in\mathbb{N}
and
R_{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{N}
represents the number of trainable parameters in each neural network. Note that we have not yet introduced approximations for
D_{x}u
,
\Delta u
and
C
at the initial time point
t_{0}
. The reason for this is that we consider a fixed, deterministic initial state
\widetilde{X}_{0}=x
and hence
D_{x}u
and
C
are fixed, deterministic and
C
depends only on potential jumps in
(t_{0},t_{1}]
. Moreover, as stated above, in order to apply a forward Euler–Maruyama scheme on the BSDE we need an approximation of
Y_{0}
. Thus, we introduce the single trainable parameters
\widetilde{Y}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{U}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{C}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and the neural network
\Delta\mathcal{U}_{0}^{\varphi_{0}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, parameterized by
\varphi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{\varphi}^{0}}
, where
R_{\varphi}^{0}\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of trainable parameters in
\Delta\mathcal{U}_{0}^{\varphi_{0}}
. To summarize, we have the trainable parameters
\widetilde{Y}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\rho=\text{concat}(\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{U}_{0},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{M-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+R_{\rho}\times(M-1)}
,
\varphi=\text{concat}(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{M-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{\varphi}^{0}+R_{\varphi}\times(M-1)}
and
\vartheta=\text{concat}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{M-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+R_{\vartheta}\times(M-1)}
, where
\text{concat}(\cdot)
is a concatenation of a list of tensors into a vector. Finally, we define the collection of all trainable parameters as
\Theta=\text{concat}(\widetilde{Y}_{0},\rho,\varphi,\vartheta)\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
, where
R
is the total number of trainable parameters in the full model. In Table

, we summarize all functions and constants that are approximated.

The aim is then to construct a loss function such that when minimized with respect to
\Theta
, our model accurately approximates the solution of the BSDE. Our starting point is the mean squared error of the terminal condition, which is the entire loss function used in
Han et al., 2018
for regular BSDEs. In the presence of jumps, we must also ensure that both the jumps in the BSDE and the compensator are accurately captured by our neural network approximations. By comparing (
3.3
) with (
3.2
) it is clear that we want to approximate

\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(r,X_{r-}+\Gamma(X_{r_{-}},z))-u(r,X_{r-})\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)

with

\Delta\mathcal{U}_{0}^{\varphi_{0}}(\Delta\widetilde{X}_{0})-\mathcal{C}_{0}\Delta t+\sum_{n=1}^{M-1}\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\varphi_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n},\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n})-\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\vartheta_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n})\Delta t.

Recall that the stochastic integral with respect to the compensated Poisson random measure is a square integrable martingale under our assumptions, which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\left(r,X_{r-}+\Gamma(X_{r_{-}},z)\right)-u\left(r,X_{r-}\right)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)\right]=0.

To enforce this structure on our approximations we introduce an additional term in the loss function based on

\mathbbm{E}\Big[\Delta\mathcal{U}_{0}^{\varphi_{0}}(\Delta\widetilde{X}_{0})-\mathcal{C}_{0}\Delta t\Big]^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{M-1}\mathbbm{E}\Big[\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\varphi_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n},\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n})-\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\vartheta_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n})\Delta t\Big]^{2}.

This concludes the description of the algorithm for the finite activity case. To summarize, our approach considers the discretized stochastic optimal control problem below. For some constant
K>0

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \underset{\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}{\text{minimize}}\quad&\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g(\widetilde{X}_{M})-\widetilde{Y}_{M}^{\Theta}\right)^{2}\right]+K\times\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\Theta}-\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\Theta}\Delta t\big]^{2}\end{aligned}

subject to:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\widetilde{X}_{0}=&x,\quad\widetilde{Y}_{0}^{\Theta}=\widetilde{Y}_{0},\quad\mathcal{Z}_{0}^{\Theta}=\sigma(x)^{\top}\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{U}_{0},\quad\Delta\mathcal{U}_{0}^{\Theta}=\Delta\mathcal{U}_{0}(\Delta\widetilde{X}_{0}),\quad\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\Theta}=\mathcal{C}_{0}\\
\widetilde{X}_{n+1}=&\widetilde{X}_{n}+b(\widetilde{X}_{n})\Delta t+\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{n})^{\top}\Delta W_{n}+\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n}-\Delta t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma(\widetilde{X}_{n},z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\\
\widetilde{Y}^{\Theta}_{n+1}=&\widetilde{Y}^{\Theta}_{n}-f\left(t_{n},\widetilde{X}_{n},\widetilde{Y}^{\Theta}_{n},\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\Theta},\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\Theta}\right)\Delta t+\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\Theta}\Delta W_{n}+\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\Theta}-\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\Theta}\Delta t\\
\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\Theta}=&\sigma(\widetilde{X}_{n})^{\top}\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\rho_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}),\quad\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\Theta}=\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\varphi_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n},\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n}),\quad\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\Theta}=\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\vartheta_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}).\end{cases}

To avoid introducing overly cumbersome notation, we have allowed a slight abuse of notation in the above equation, as the last line is valid only for
n\geq 1
.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that
\Delta u
, which represents changes in
u
due to jumps in
X
, is identically zero when no jumps occur between adjacent time points. Thus, it is reasonable to structure
\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\varphi_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n},\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n})
to enforce this property. One way to achieve this is by defining
\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\Theta}=\Delta\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\varphi_{n}}(\widetilde{X}_{n},\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n})\Delta\widetilde{X}_{n}
. This approach is used for all numerical experiments in this paper, however, alternative methods could be considered, particularly for high-dimensional settings with complex terminal conditions. For example, a more general alternative might involve an indicator function that returns one if a jump occurs in any of the
d
dimensions of
X
and zero otherwise.

3.2.
Infinite activity case

To cover the case where the forward process
X
exhibits infinitely many jumps, we proceed by introducing an approximating jump diffusion
X^{\epsilon}
with finitely many jumps, meaning that the jump component corresponds to a compound Poisson process. The small jumps might be truncated or, as we do, they could be approximated via the diffusion term by increasing the volatility. This constitutes the first source of numerical error of our algorithm. This error is empirically analyzed in the numerical results section. Concerning this first approximation step for the forward SDE for
X
we follow, among others
Asmussen and Rosiński, 2001
,
Cohen and Rosiński, 2007
and
Jum, 2015

Moving from the process
X
to the process
X^{\epsilon}
has an implication on the FBSDE as well. To proceed, we need to introduce some additional assumptions, in particular regarding the structure of the Poisson random measure: let
\epsilon\in(0,1]
. We define

\displaystyle\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{|z|>\epsilon\}}\nu(\mathrm{d}z),\quad\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{|z|\leq\epsilon\}}\nu(\mathrm{d}z).

It then holds that
\nu=\nu_{\epsilon}+\nu^{\epsilon}
.
From the definition of the Lévy measure
\nu
we immediately have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(dz)<\infty\;
and
\;\nu^{\epsilon}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)<\infty\;
.
We are factorizing the jump term in two components, the first one corresponding to the
small
jumps, and the second one to the
big
jumps, i.e. a compound Poisson process. The factorization of the Lévy measure means that we are assuming that we can write the Poisson Random Measure
N
as

(3.8)

\displaystyle N=N_{\epsilon}+N^{\epsilon}\;,

with
N_{\epsilon}
and
N^{\epsilon}
having the compensator
\nu_{\epsilon}
and
\nu^{\epsilon}
, respectively.

In line with
Cohen and Rosiński, 2007
we introduce the following quantity

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\epsilon}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}zz^{\top}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)=\int_{|z|<\epsilon}zz^{\top}\nu(\mathrm{d}z),

taking values in the cone of positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrices
S^{d}_{+}
. We also introduce a further assumption on the jump term of the forward SDE:

(A5)

the function
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is of the form

\Gamma(x,z)\coloneq\gamma(x)z

for some Lipschitz continuous function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

The remaining assumptions on the vector fields
b,\sigma,\Gamma
are unchanged with respect to (
2.3
).

Given
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we approximate the solution
X^{t,x}_{\cdot}
of the forward SDE (
2.3
) by means of the process
X^{\epsilon,t,x}_{\cdot}
, whose dynamics are

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} X^{\epsilon,t,x}_{s}=&x+\int_{t}^{s}b(X^{\epsilon,t,x}_{r-})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{t}^{s}\Big(\sigma(X^{\epsilon,t,x}_{r-})^{\top}+\gamma(X^{\epsilon,t,x}_{r-})\sqrt{\Sigma_{\epsilon}}\Big)\mathrm{d}W_{r}+\int^{s}_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma(X^{\epsilon,t,x}_{r-})z\tilde{N}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z),\end{aligned}

where
\sqrt{\Sigma_{\epsilon}}
denotes the matrix square root of
\Sigma_{\epsilon}\in S^{d}_{+}
and we set
\tilde{N}^{\epsilon}\coloneq N^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)-\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}r
. Again, whenever possible, we will simply use the notation
X^{\epsilon}
, thus neglecting the dependence on the starting point
(t,x)
.
A priori error estimates of the error we introduce by approximating
X
via
X^{\epsilon}
can be derived following, for instance,
Cohen and Rosiński, 2007
and
Jum, 2015
and will be discussed in the next section.

At this point we apply the solver as described in Section
3.1
to the following BSDE

\displaystyle Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}=

\displaystyle g(X^{\epsilon}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{r-},Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{r-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{r},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon\epsilon}(r,z)\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z))\mathrm{d}r-\int_{t}^{T}{Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{r}}^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{r}

(3.10)

\displaystyle-\int^{T}_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{r}(z)\tilde{N}^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z).

whose solution
(Y^{\epsilon\epsilon},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon},U^{\epsilon\epsilon})
is intended to approximate
(Y,Z,U)
solving (
2.4
). By the above approximation, we are left with a finite activity FBSDE, which can be approximated with the algorithm outlined in Section
3.1
.

Remark 3.2
.

The use of a double superscript
{\ }{}^{\epsilon\epsilon}
in (
3.10
) is motivated by the introduction, later in Section
4.2
, of an intermediate approximation step denoted with a single superscript
{\ }{}^{\epsilon}
.

4.
Error estimates

In this section we present a priori error estimates for the error introduced by the approximation of small jumps in the infinite activity case on the solution of the FBSDE.

4.1.
A priori error estimates for the forward approximation

We start by providing the
L^{2}
estimate of the error that we introduce by approximating
X_{\cdot}
via
X^{\epsilon}_{\cdot}
.
We proceed along the lines of
Cohen and Rosiński, 2007
and
Jum, 2015
. We report the main step of the proof for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem 4.4,
Jum, 2015
)
.

Under the assumptions (A1)-(A2), (A5), there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\left|X_{s}-X^{\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z),\qquad\forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

In the following we do not keep track of constants.
Applying Theorem 2.11 in
Kunita, 2004
with
p=2
, we get the following estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\left|X_{s}-X^{\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg\{\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left|b\left(X_{r-}\right)-b\left(X^{\epsilon}_{r-}\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left|\sigma\left(X_{r-}\right)^{\top}-\sigma\left(X^{\epsilon}_{r-}\right)^{\top}-\gamma\left(X^{\epsilon}_{r-}\right)\sqrt{\Sigma_{\epsilon}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\gamma\left(X_{r-}\right)-\gamma\left(X^{\epsilon}_{r-}\right)\right|^{2}\left|z\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}r\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\gamma\left(X^{\epsilon}_{r-}\right)\right|^{2}\left|z\right|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}r\right]\bigg\}.

Exploiting the Lipschitz property of the coefficient functions
b,\sigma,\gamma
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\left|X_{s}-X^{\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle{\leq C\left\{\left(1+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu(dz)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left|X_{r}-X^{\epsilon}_{r}\right|\mathrm{d}r\right]+\left(\left|\sqrt{\Sigma_{\epsilon}}\right|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(dz)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}1+\left|X^{\epsilon}_{r}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right]\right\}}

so that, observing that
\left|\sqrt{\Sigma_{\epsilon}}\right|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(dz)
(see
(
Jum, 2015
, Proposition 4.1.2)
) and making use of the classical moment estimate

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\left|X^{\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C\left(1+|x|^{2}\right),\qquad\forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\text{for some $C>0$,}

we can show the existence of a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\left|X_{s}-X^{\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\left|X_{r}-X^{\epsilon}_{r}\right|\mathrm{d}r\right]+(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(dz)\right\}.

The result follows by Gronwall’s inequality.
∎

4.2.
A priori error estimates for the backward approximation

In this section we estimate the error induced by the approximation of small jumps on the solution
(Y,Z,U)\in S^{2}_{T}\times\mathbb{H}_{T}\times\mathbb{H}_{T,N}
to (
2.4
).
Let
(Y^{\epsilon\epsilon},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon},U^{\epsilon\epsilon})\in S^{2}_{T}\times\mathbb{H}_{T}\times\mathbb{H}_{T,N^{\epsilon}}
be the solution to (
3.10
).
The error between
(Y,Z,U)
and
(Y^{\epsilon\epsilon},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon},U^{\epsilon\epsilon})
can be seen as the result of two contributions: the error coming from the forward approximation (i.e. the replacement of
X
with
X^{\epsilon}
) and the one related to the replacement of the infinite activity driving jumps of the backward dynamics (
2.4
) with the finite activity one appearing in (
3.10
). To estimate the first contribution to the error one can combine the results of Section
4.1
with classical continuous dependence results for FBSDEs as those in
Delong, 2017
. The main part of the proof is devoted to estimate the second error term. We point out that similar estimates for the first component of the BSDE (
Y
) have previously obtained in
Dumitrescu et al., 2021
by means of probabilistic techniques.

We have the following result

Theorem 4.2
.

Under assumptions (A1)-(A5), there exists a constant
C>0
such that the following estimates hold

\left\|Y-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|Z-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|U-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N^{\epsilon}}}^{2}\leq C(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

for some positive constant
C
.

Proof.

Let us consider, in addition to (
2.4
) and (
3.10
), a third BSDE where we only substitute the forward process with the jump diffusion approximation and keep the original Poisson measure
N
for the jump component:

(4.3)

\displaystyle Y^{\epsilon}_{t}=

\displaystyle g(X^{\epsilon}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}f\left(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\right)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}{Z_{s}^{\epsilon}}^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{s}

\displaystyle-\int^{T}_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z).

We clearly have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|Y-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}\coloneq&\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|Y_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|Y_{t}-Y^{\epsilon}_{t}+Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq 2\left(\left\|Y-Y^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|Y^{\epsilon}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}\right)\end{aligned}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|Z-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\coloneq&\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\left|Z_{t}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\left|Z_{t}-Z^{\epsilon}_{t}+Z^{\epsilon}_{t}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
&\leq 2\left(\left\|Z-Z^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|Z^{\epsilon}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}\right)\end{aligned}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|U-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N^{\epsilon}}}^{2}\coloneq&\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U_{t}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U_{t}(z)-U^{\epsilon}_{t}(z)+U^{\epsilon}_{t}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
&\leq 2\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U_{t}(z)-U^{\epsilon}_{t}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{t}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t\right]\right)\\
&\leq 2\left(\left\|U-U^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N}}^{2}+\left\|U^{\epsilon}-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N^{\epsilon}}}^{2}\right)\end{aligned}

In what follows we will not keep track of the constants and we will denote them by a generic
C
.
We start by the first term in the right hand side of (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and (
4.6
).
For any
(\omega,s,y,z,u)\in\Omega\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times L^{2}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let us define

F(\omega,s,y,z,u):=f(s,X_{s-},y,z,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))

and

F^{\epsilon}(\omega,s,y,z,u):=f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},y,z,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)).

Applying the estimates (3.5) and (3.7) in
Delong, 2017
one has that for any
\rho>0
there exists some constant
C
such that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}|Y_{t}-Y^{\epsilon}_{t}|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}e^{\rho t}|Z_{t}-Z^{\epsilon}_{t}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho t}|U_{t}(z)-U^{\epsilon}_{t}(z)|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
&\leq C\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\rho T}\left|g(X_{T})-g(X^{\epsilon}_{T})\right|^{2}\right]\right.\\
&\quad\left.+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho t}\left|f(t,X_{t-},Y_{t-},Z_{t},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U_{t}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(t,X^{\epsilon}_{t-},Y_{t-},Z_{t},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U_{t}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\right).\end{aligned}

Thanks to the Lipschitz continuity of
g
and
f
, see (A1) and (A2), and Theorem
4.1
we then obtain

\displaystyle\left\|Y-Y^{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}+\|Z-Z^{\epsilon}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}+\|U-U^{\epsilon}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}|Y_{t}-Y^{\epsilon}_{t}|^{2}\right|+\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}e^{\rho t}|Z_{t}-Z^{\epsilon}_{t}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho t}|U_{t}(z)-U^{\epsilon}_{t}(z)|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X_{t}-X^{\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z).

Let us now move to the estimates for the terms
\left\|Y^{\epsilon}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}
,
\left\|Z^{\epsilon}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}^{2}
and
\left\|U^{\epsilon}-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N^{\epsilon}}}^{2}
in (
4.4
), (
4.5
), (
4.6
), respectively. First of all, let us observe that defining

\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z):=U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\mathbbm{1}_{|z|\geq\epsilon}

we can write
Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}
as

\displaystyle Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}=

\displaystyle g(X^{\epsilon}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}f\left(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\right)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}{Z_{s}^{\epsilon\epsilon}}^{\top}\mathrm{d}W_{s}

(4.8)

\displaystyle-\int^{T}_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z).

We proceed in line with
(
Delong, 2017
, Lemma 3.1.1)
.
We apply the Itô’s formula to
e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}
and we derive, for any
\tau\in[t,T]
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}+\rho\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\\
&+\int_{t}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\\
=&e^{\rho\tau}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{\tau}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{\tau}\right|^{2}\\
&-2\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{\rho s}\left(Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right)\\
&\cdot\left(-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))+f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right)\mathrm{d}s\\
&-2\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{\rho s}\left(Y^{\epsilon}_{s-}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-}\right)\left(Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right)\mathrm{d}W_{s}\\
&-2\int_{t}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left(Y^{\epsilon}_{s-}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-}\right)\left(U^{\epsilon}(s,z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}(s,z)\right)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)\\
&-\int_{t}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),\quad 0\leq t\leq\tau\leq T.\end{aligned}

The stochastic integrals appearing above are local martingales. Even more, they are uniformly integrable martingales. Taking the expectation of the inequality above and choosing
\tau=T
we find

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &e^{\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]+\rho\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
=&2\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left(Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right)\\
&\cdot\left(f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right)\mathrm{d}s\bigg],\quad 0\leq t\leq T.\end{aligned}

We can use the following inequality: for any
\alpha>0
.

(4.10)

\displaystyle 2|uv|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}|u|^{2}+\alpha|v|^{2}.

We apply this to the term involving the difference between the drivers and obtain

\displaystyle 2\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|^{2},

so that we get

(4.11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &e^{\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]+\rho\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\leq\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg].\end{aligned}

Using the Lipschitz continuity of
f
one has

\displaystyle\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s-}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-}\right|^{2}+\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\right|^{2}\right\}.

Therefore, from (
4.11
) one gets

(4.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &e^{\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]+\rho\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\leq\left(\alpha+\frac{C}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\frac{C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\\
&\quad+\frac{C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z))\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\\
&\leq\left(\alpha+\frac{C}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\frac{C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\\
&\quad+\frac{2C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]+\frac{2C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\\
\end{aligned}

Observing that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]

one obtains

(4.13)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &e^{\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]+\rho\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\leq\left(\alpha+\frac{C}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\frac{C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\\
&\quad+\frac{C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|(U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z))\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]+\frac{C}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\end{aligned}

for a generic constant
C
that majorates all constants appearing in (
4.12
).
Assuming
\rho
sufficiently big we can take
\alpha
such that
\rho=\alpha+\frac{C}{\alpha}
and
C<\alpha
in order to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\rho s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg].

To estimate the right end side of the last inequality we can use the representation of
U^{\epsilon}
given by Theorem
2.5
. Indeed, for the starting FBSDE (
2.4
) where we replace the forward component
X
with
X^{\epsilon}
, we have

U^{\epsilon}(s,z)=u\left(s,{X}^{\epsilon}_{s-}+\Gamma\left({X}^{\epsilon}_{s-},z\right)\right)-u\left(s,{X}^{\epsilon}_{s-}\right),\quad t\leq s\leq T,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

so that by the Lipschitz continuity of
u
(see
(
Delong, 2017
, Lemma 4.1.1)
) and the growth condition on
\Gamma
the following holds

(4.14)

\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}=\left|u\left(s,{X}^{\epsilon}_{s-}+\Gamma\left({X}^{\epsilon}_{s-},z\right)\right)-u\left(s,{X}^{\epsilon}_{s-}\right)\right|^{2}\leq C(1+\left|{X}^{\epsilon}_{s-}\right|^{2})|z|^{2}.

In conclusion, thanks to the moment estimate (
4.2
), we obtain

(4.15)

\displaystyle\|Z^{\epsilon}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}+\|U^{\epsilon}-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N^{\epsilon}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{\rho T}(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)

Again from (
4.9
), taking
\tau=T
, we have the following estimates

(4.16)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sup_{t\in[0,T]}&e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\\
\leq&\;2\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\\
&\cdot\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|\mathrm{d}s\\
&+4\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{\rho s}\left(Y^{\epsilon}_{s-}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-}\right)\left(Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right)\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right|\\
&+4\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left(Y^{\epsilon}_{s-}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-}\right)\left(U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)\right|.\end{aligned}

Taking the expectation and using the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality together with other classical estimates we get

(4.17)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}&\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\right.\\
&\cdot\left.\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}(s,z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}(s,z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+C\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{2\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s-}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-}\right|^{2}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\\
&+C\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{2\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s-}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-}\right|^{2}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\\
&\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\right.\\
&\cdot\left.\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+C\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\frac{\rho}{2}t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\\
&+C\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\frac{\rho}{2}t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\end{aligned}

We use again (
4.10
) to majorate the two final terms.

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}&\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\right.\\
&\cdot\left.\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+\frac{C}{\tilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&+C\tilde{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+C\tilde{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)\right]\end{aligned}

In the last expectation we can substitute the random measure with its compensator hence

(4.19)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}&\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\right.\\
&\cdot\left.\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+\frac{C}{\tilde{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&+C\tilde{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+C\tilde{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\end{aligned}

We choose
\tilde{\alpha}>C
, then we have

(4.20)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}&\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&\quad\leq 2C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\right.\\
&\cdot\left.\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&+C\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\right)\end{aligned}

Applying again (
4.10
), for some
\hat{\alpha}>0
,

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|\right.

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left.\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq\hat{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\hat{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-},Y^{\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))-f(s,X^{\epsilon}_{s-}Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s-},Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s},\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z))\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\hat{\alpha}+\frac{C}{\hat{\alpha}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{C}{\hat{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\frac{C}{\hat{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}&\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{T}_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\end{aligned}

For controlling the last to terms we use (
4.14
)-(
4.15
) getting

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-\hat{U}^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Z^{\epsilon}_{s}-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\rho s}\left|U^{\epsilon}_{s}(z)\right|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\right]\\
&\leq Ce^{\rho T}(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z).\end{aligned}

Then, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{\rho s}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{s}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+C(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)

and applying Gronwall’s inequality we obtain

\|Y^{\epsilon}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{\rho t}\left|Y^{\epsilon}_{t}-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(1+|x|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)

that together with (
4.15
) gives the desired result.
∎

4.3.
On error estimates for the Deep BSDEs solver with jumps

In order to obtain complete estimates of the error between the numerical solution provided by our solver and the exact solution
(Y,Z,U)
of the BSDE (
2.4
) one should take into account, on top of the error introduced by the approximation of small jumps and estimated in sections
4.1
and
4.2
, also the error associated to the ANN approximation and to the optimization procedure.

Indeed, if we denote by
(\hat{Y},\hat{\mathcal{Z}},\hat{\mathcal{C}})
the output provided by the solver,
i.e. given
\hat{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
the optimal set of parameters resulting from the solution of the optimization of (
3.7
) where
\sigma
and
\nu
are replaced by
\sigma_{\epsilon}:=\sigma+\sqrt{\Sigma_{\epsilon}}^{\top}\gamma
and
\nu^{\epsilon}
, respectively, we take
for
n=0,\ldots,M
,
\hat{Y}_{n}=\tilde{Y}^{\hat{\Theta}}_{n}
,
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}=\mathcal{Z}^{\hat{\Theta}}_{n}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{n}={\mathcal{C}}^{\hat{\Theta}}_{n}
,
we clearly have

\displaystyle\underset{n=0,\ldots,M-1}{\max}\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}{\sup}|Y_{t}-\hat{Y}_{n}|^{2}\right]+\sum^{M-1}_{n=0}\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{t_{n+1}}_{t_{n}}|Z_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum^{M-1}_{n=0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int^{t_{n+1}}_{t_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U_{t}(z)\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t-\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\Delta t\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|Y-Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}+\|Z-Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}+\|U-U^{\epsilon\epsilon}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{N^{\epsilon}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2\bigg(\underset{n=0,\ldots,M-1}{\max}\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]}{\sup}|Y^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}-\hat{Y}_{n}|^{2}\right]+\sum^{M-1}_{n=0}\mathbb{E}\left[\int^{t_{n+1}}_{t_{n}}|Z^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}-\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum^{M-1}_{n=0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int^{t_{n+1}}_{t_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U^{\epsilon\epsilon}_{t}(z)\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t-\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\Delta t\right|^{2}\right]\bigg)

The terms in the first brackets of the right hand side are bounded by Theorem
4.2
. We are currently working to establish some bounds for the terms in the second brackets. We conjecture that results in this direction can be obtained by extending the techniques in
Han and Long, 2020
to the case with jumps.

Numerical results

To evaluate the performance of our algorithm, we present four examples where we compare the results either from a theoretical perspective or through Monte Carlo simulations. All Monte Carlo simulations, used to generate reference solutions are based on
2^{16}
sample paths. Moreover, the weight in (
3.7
) is set to
K=\frac{T}{M}\times 0.1
, for all experiments. Here we recall that
M+1
is the number of temporal grid points. The code for our experiments is available at
https://github.com/AlessandroGnoatto/DeepBsdeSolverWithJumps
. In the context of applying our methodology to option pricing, we impose additional assumptions on the Lévy measure to ensure the integrability of the underlying asset. Specifically, we require the driving Lévy process to have finite exponential moments.

(A6)

There exists a constant
\mathcal{M}>1
such that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{u^{\top}z}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty,

for all
u\in[-\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}]^{d}

5.1.
Pure jump expectation

Consider the following pure jump process

(5.2)

\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{X_{t-}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t)\;,

where
\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=\lambda\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z
is the Lévy measure with
\lambda>0
and
\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{J}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{J}}{\sigma_{J}}\right)^{2}}
.

The solution to (
5.2
) satisfies

(5.3)

X_{T}=X_{t}\exp\left\{-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)(T-t)+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}z\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}s)\right\}\;.

The characteristic function of
\ln X_{T}
is

(5.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{iq\ln X_{T}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\exp\left\{iq\ln X_{t}-iq(T-t)\psi(-i)+(T-t)\psi(q)\right\}\;,

where
\psi(q)\coloneqq\lambda\left(e^{iu\mu_{J}-\frac{1}{2}q^{2}\sigma_{J}^{2}}-1\right)
is the characteristic exponent.

Thanks to the Markov property we can write

(5.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{iq\ln X_{T}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=u(t,X_{t})\;,

where
u
solves the following PIDE:

(5.6)

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t,x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,xe^{z})-u(t,x)-x(e^{z}-1)u_{x}(t,x)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=0\\
u(T,x)=e^{iq\ln x}\;.\end{cases}

Therefore, the related BSDE is

(5.7)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{d}Y_{s}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{s}(z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}s)\\
Y_{T}=e^{iq\ln X_{T}}\;,\end{cases}

where, by the Feynman-Kac representation formula,
Y_{t}=u(t,X_{t})
and
U_{t}(z)=u(t,X_{t^{-}}e^{z})-u(t,X_{t^{-}})
.

Assuming that
q=-i
in Equation (
5.4
), then

(5.8)

Y_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{iq\ln X_{T}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=X_{t}\;,\;\forall t\in[0,T]\;.

In this first example we set
X_{0}=1
,
\lambda=0.3
,
\mu_{J}=0.5
,
\sigma_{J}=0.25
. The ANNs have
\mathcal{L}-1=2
hidden layers, each with
\upsilon=10
nodes. We consider
M=40
time steps and perform
10,000
batch iterations with batch size
2^{10}=1024
,
i.e.,
each batch consists of
2^{10}
samples and the algorithm runs through
10,000
batches in total. The high number of paths is used to ensure that the algorithm has converged, but we can empirically observe that we reach a high level of accuracy with significantly fewer training samples. The results are reported in Table
2
and in Figure

Call option example

Let

described by the following dynamics

\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{X_{t-}}=r\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q}}_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t)\;,\quad X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

where
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R^{+}}
and
\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=\lambda\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z
is the Lévy measure with
\lambda>0
and
\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{J}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{J}}{\sigma_{J}}\right)^{2}}
.

For
t,s\in[0,T]
with
t\geq s
, the solution to (
5.9
) is given by:

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=X_{s}\exp\left\{\left(r-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)(t-s)+\sigma(W_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}}-W_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}})-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)(t-s)+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}z\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)\right\}\;.

Let
Y
be a European call option on
X
with value at time

(x)^{+}=\max(x,0)

X

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{d}Y_{s}=-rY_{s}\mathrm{d}s-Z_{s}\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}U(s,z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}s)\\
Y_{T}=(X_{T}-k)^{+}\;,\end{cases}

Z_{t}=u_{x}(t,X_{t^{-}})\sigma X_{t^{-}}
and
U_{t}(z)=u(t,X_{t^{-}}e^{z})-u(t,X_{t^{-}})
.

Remark 5.1
.

Clearly,
Y_{0}
could be accurately approximated via a Monte Carlo method in significantly less time (even in high-dimensional problems) than our algorithms. However, the comparison is not fair since our method approximates the solution of the PIDE along the stochastic paths

and not only at

. Pathwise approximate solutions could be obtained via the Monte Carlo method by resorting to nested simulations, which are notoriously expensive.

5.3.
A Basket call option example

The examples we presented so far are mainly meant to provide a validation of the methodology in the one dimensional case. The example we proceed to discuss now instead provides evidence that the methodology is viable in a high dimensional setting. We consider the case of several underlying assets
(X^{1};\cdots;X^{d})
:

(5.10)

\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{i}}{X_{t-}^{i}}=r^{i}\mathrm{d}t+\sigma^{i}\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{Q},i}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1)\tilde{N}^{i}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}t)\;,\quad X_{0}^{i}=x_{0}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad i=1,\cdots,d\,,

where
r^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma^{i}\in\mathbb{R^{+}},i=1,\cdots,d
,
W^{\mathbb{Q}}=(W^{\mathbb{Q},1},\cdots,W^{\mathbb{Q},d})
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\nu^{i}(\mathrm{d}z)=\lambda\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z
,
i=1,\cdots,d
, is the Lévy measure with
\lambda>0
and
\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{J}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{J}}{\sigma_{J}}\right)^{2}}
.

The solution to (
5.10
) satisfies, for any
i=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} X_{T}^{i}=X_{t}^{i}\exp&\left\{\left(r^{i}-\frac{\sigma^{i,2}}{2}\right)(T-t)+\sigma^{i}(W_{T}^{\mathbb{Q},i}-W_{t}^{\mathbb{Q},i})\right.\\
&\left.-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu^{i}(\mathrm{d}z)(T-t)+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}z\tilde{N}^{i}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}s)\right\}\;.\end{aligned}

Let
Y
be a basket of European call option on
(X^{1};\cdots;X^{d})
with value at time
t\in[0,T]
given by

(5.11)

Y_{t}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{-r(T-t)}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}X_{T}^{i}-k\right)^{+}\biggr|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\;.

where
k\in\mathbb{R^{+}}
is the strike price. From the Feynman-Kac representation formula we have
Y_{t}=u(t,X_{t}^{1},\cdots,X_{t}^{d})=u(t,X_{t})
where
u
solves the following PIDE:

(5.12)

\begin{cases}\!\begin{aligned} u_{t}(t,x)+\nabla_{x}u(t,x)b_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left[\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}(t,x)D^{2}_{x}u(t,x)\right]&-ru(t,x)\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t,x_{.}e^{z})-u(t,x)&-x_{.}(e^{z}-\mathbf{1})\nabla_{x}u(t,x)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=0\end{aligned}\\
u(T,x)=\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}-k\right)^{+},\end{cases}

where we use
.
to denote the component-wise product between vectors,
e^{z}
is the vector where each entry is of the form
e^{z_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots d
,
\mathbf{1}
is the vector with all elements equal to one and
\nu
is the product of the
d
Lévy measures of the individual driving processes.
Therefore, the related BSDE is

(5.13)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{d}Y_{s}=-rY_{s}\mathrm{d}s-Z_{s}\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{\mathbb{Q}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U(s,z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}s)\\
Y_{T}=\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}X_{T}^{i}-k\right)^{+},\end{cases}

where
U_{t}(z)=u(t,X_{t^{-}}{}_{.}e^{z})-u(t,X_{t^{-}})
.

For the basket option, we approximate
Y_{0}
using the deep solver. Our approximation is compared with a reference solution computed with a Monte Carlo approximation. To compute reference solutions of
(Y_{t})_{t\in(0,T]}
we resort to a nested Monte Carlo approximation. We set
X_{0}=\mathbf{1}
,
{k=0.9},r=0.05,\sigma^{i}=\sigma=0.25
for
i=1,\ldots,d
,

5.4.
Infinite activity example

The final example we propose provides evidence for the feasibility of the proposed algorithm for the infinite activity case. We consider the CGMY process, which is a pure jump Lévy process
L
with characteristic triplet
(0,0,\nu(\mathrm{d}z))
, where the Lévy measure is given by

\displaystyle\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=C\frac{e^{-G|z|}}{|z|^{1+Y}}\mathtt{1}_{\{z<0\}}\mathrm{d}z+C\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathtt{1}_{\{z>0\}}\mathrm{d}z.

The Lévy measure corresponds to that of a difference of two tempered stable subordinators, meaning that we can write
L_{t}=L^{+}_{t}-L^{-}_{t}
where

•

L^{+}
has triplet
(0,0,\nu^{+}(\mathrm{d}z))
, where
\nu^{+}(\mathrm{d}z):=C\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathtt{1}_{\{z>0\}}\mathrm{d}z
;

•

L^{-}
has triplet
(0,0,\nu^{-}(\mathrm{d}z))
, where
\nu^{-}(\mathrm{d}z):=C\frac{e^{-Gz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathtt{1}_{\{z>0\}}\mathrm{d}z
.

In the following we outline the procedure to approximate a tempered stable subordinator by following
Cont and Tankov, 2003
and references therein among others. Since the procedure is the same for
L^{+}
and
L^{-}
, we choose to concentrate on
L^{+}
. We fix
\epsilon>0
, which represents a threshold for jump sizes where all jumps smaller than
\epsilon
are approximated by a diffusion process, whereas all jumps larger than
\epsilon
is approximated by a suitably constructed compensated compound Poisson process. We first look at the large jumps. The jump compensator of the approximating compound Poisson process is given by

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}zC\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=C\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}z^{-Y}e^{-Mz}\mathrm{d}z=C\int_{M\epsilon}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\ell}{M}\right)^{-Y}e^{-\ell}\mathrm{d}\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{M^{a}}\int_{M\epsilon}^{\infty}\ell^{a-1}e^{-\ell}\mathrm{d}\ell\;,

where we set
a:=1-Y
. Let us now recall the lower incomplete Gamma function as implemented in
scipy
or
Matlab

\Gamma(a,M\epsilon):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{M\epsilon}\ell^{a-1}e^{-\ell}\mathrm{d}\ell
, where
\Gamma(a)
is the Gamma function computed in
a
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}zC\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle={C}\Gamma(a)\left(1-\Gamma(a,M\epsilon)\right)\eqcolon-b_{\epsilon^{+}}\;.

The intensity of the approximating compound Poisson process is given by

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}_{\epsilon}:=\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}C\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathrm{d}z\;.

To find a convenient expression for the intensity, we integrate by parts and obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}zC\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathrm{d}z=\frac{C}{M}e^{-M\epsilon}\epsilon^{-Y}-\frac{YC}{M}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}e^{-Mz}z^{-1-Y}\mathrm{d}z\;.

By rearranging terms, we can write

\displaystyle\lambda^{+}_{\epsilon}=Ce^{-M\epsilon}\frac{\epsilon^{-Y}}{Y}+\frac{M}{Y}b_{\epsilon^{+}}\;.

Next, we need to determine the jump size distribution. We denote by
f_{\epsilon^{+}}
such density. Recalling the general form of the Lévy measure of a compound Poisson process we can write

\displaystyle f_{\epsilon^{+}}(z)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{+}_{\epsilon}}C\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathtt{1}_{\{z>\epsilon\}}\;.

It is possible to sample from such distribution by means of the acceptance rejection method as outlined in
Cont and Tankov, 2003
. Finally, we also introduce a diffusion approximation of small jumps. The variance for the small jumps is given by

\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}z^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)-\Big(\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}z\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)^{2}\approx\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}z^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z),

since the second term vanishes for small
\epsilon
, and hence, the diffusion coefficient is set to

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}z^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\;.

Again, due to the particular structure of the Lévy measure, we can split the computation between the positive and negative jumps. For the positive jumps we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\epsilon}z^{2}C\frac{e^{-Mz}}{z^{1+Y}}\mathrm{d}z=\frac{C}{M^{2-Y}}\Gamma(a+1)\Gamma(a+1,M\epsilon)\;,

and similarly for the negative jumps. In summary, to simulate the CGMY process
L
, we introduce the discrete time approximation
L^{\epsilon}
given by

\displaystyle L^{\epsilon}_{t_{n+1}}=L^{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}+\sigma_{\epsilon}\Delta W_{n+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n+1}^{+}}\Delta L_{n+1,j}^{+}-b_{\epsilon^{+}}{\Delta t}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n+1}^{-}}\Delta L_{n+1,j}^{-}+b_{\epsilon^{-}}{\Delta t}\;,

where

N_{n+1}^{+}\sim\mathcal{P}\left(\lambda_{\epsilon}^{+},\Delta t\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\right)
,
N_{n+1}^{-}\sim\mathcal{P}\left(\lambda_{\epsilon}^{-},{\Delta t}\right)
,
\Delta L_{n+1,j}^{+},\sim f_{\epsilon^{+}}
, and

\Delta L_{n+1,j}^{-}\sim f_{\epsilon^{-}}
.
The approximating asset price process is then given by

(5.14)

X^{\epsilon}_{t_{n+1}}=X^{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}\exp\left\{\Big({r-\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2}}-\int_{|z|\geq\epsilon}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big){\Delta t}+L^{\epsilon}_{t_{n+1}}-L^{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}\right\}.\\

In the above
\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2}
and
\int_{|z|\geq\epsilon}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)
are the exponential compensators for the small jumps (modeled by a Brownian motion) and the large jumps (modeled by the Poisson random measure), respectively. On the other hand, for small
\epsilon
,
\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}{2}+\int_{|z|\geq\epsilon}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)
can be approximated by the full convexity correction for the CGMY model
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)
, for which we have the following expression

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=-C\Gamma(-Y)\left((M-1)^{Y}-M^{Y}+(G+1)^{Y}-G^{Y}\right).

Therefore, we set

(5.15)

X^{\epsilon}_{t_{n+1}}=X^{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}\exp\left\{\Big({r}-\int_{\mathbbm{R}}(e^{z}-1-z)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big){\Delta t}+L^{\epsilon}_{t_{n+1}}-L^{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}\right\}.\\

We note that while equation (
5.14
) is a martingale, equation (
5.15
) is generally not. However, for the purposes of numerical implementation, this discrepancy has no significant impact as long as
\epsilon
is small enough. A brief sensitivity analysis of
\epsilon
is conducted in this section.

5.5.
Application to counterparty credit risk

In the examples above, we considered the pricing of some financial payoffs. It would be natural to argue that, in pricing applications, a simple use of a Monte Carlo method allows us to approximate the price at the initial time. This is indeed the way we use to benchmark our results when closed formulas are not available. However, the knowledge of the initial price of a contract is in most cases insufficient in view of managing the risks affecting the claim: typically one needs the possibility to simulate future scenarios of the price process. The Deep BSDE solver with jumps we propose allows us to perform this task: once the training of the family of ANNs has been performed, we can simulate the BSDE’s trajectories, thus producing future simulated scenarios for the price process.
To stress the importance of this feature of the algorithm, let us now focus on the issue of counterparty credit risk. In the aftermath of the financial crisis, the market practice for the valuation of derivative contracts has changed significantly. It is now standard to adjust the standard risk neutral price in order to account for a series of frictions. For the sake of brevity, we concentrate here on the possibility that the bank’s conterparty defaults and refer to
Biagini et al., 2021
for a complete treatment.

On a filtered probability space as before, let
Y=(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denote the value of a contingent claim with a
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable payoff
H
depending on some jump-diffusion process of the form (
2.3
). Let
r=(r_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be the discount rate, then the standard risk-neutral valuation approach leads to the following expression for the price of the claim in the absence of counterparty risk

Y_{t}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{-\int^{T}_{t}r_{s}\mathrm{d}s}H\big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

which we refer to as the clean value. The counterparty, however, could default at some stopping time
\tau_{C}
with hazard rate
\lambda^{C}=(\lambda^{C}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. To account for this potential loss the Credit Value Adjustment (CVA) is introduced. The value of the contract is reduced by the expected loss represented by the CVA, so that the adjusted value of the contract
V=(V_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is given by

V_{t}=Y_{t}-\text{CVA}_{t},

where the CVA is given by the following conditional expectation

\text{CVA}_{t}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\int^{T}_{t}\lambda^{C}_{u}e^{-\int^{u}_{t}(r_{s}+\lambda^{C}_{s})\mathrm{d}s}\left(Y_{u}\right)^{+}\mathrm{d}u\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right].

Inside the conditional expectation one can notice the presence of the positive part of
Y_{u}
,
u\in[t,T]
, that represents the potential future liability of the counterparty towards the bank. It is then clear from the CVA formula above that to compute the adjusted price of the claim we need the ability to simulate future scenarios of the clean value
Y
of the contract. As
Y
satisfies a BSDE, our algorithm can be employed to solve such problem. For illustrative purposes we plot in Figure
7
paths of the underlying risk factor using the model from Section
5.2
together with the corresponding sample paths of the call option.

An in depth analysis of the present algorithm for the computation of the whole set of valuation adjustments (xVA) can be obtained along the lines of
Gnoatto et al., 2023
.

Conclusions

In this paper, we presented an extension of the deep BSDE solver of
E et al., 2017
and
Han et al., 2018
to the setting of FBSDEs with jumps. As in the original formulation of the solver, we discretize the FBSDE and parametrize it by means of ANNs. A delicate part of the proposed extension is given by the treatment of the integral with respect to the compensator inside the jump term, which involves an integration over the whole support of the Lévy measure. The approximation of such integral terms is increasingly difficult as the dimension of the space increases. With the aim of formulating an algorithm which is feasible in a possibly high-dimensional setting, we parametrize both the integral with respect to the Poisson random measure and the integral with respect to the jump compensator via two further families of ANNs. The parameters of such additional ANNs are then optimized by introducing a second penalty term in the loss that enforces the martingale property of the compensated jump integrals at every point in time.

We test the proposed methodology first in a one-dimensional setting, which allows us to validate the algorithm against known alternative approaches for the computation of the solution. After that, we introduce a high dimensional example, i.e. the PIDE associated to a basket option on
100
underlyings as a proof for the fact that the algorithm is feasible in high dimension. Finally we provide an example that shows how one can treat the case where the jumps are of infinite activity.

There are several directions for future research. First of all, even though a full theory for the approximation quality of ANNs is still missing, one could still provide a convergence analysis of the algorithm in the spirit of
Han and Long, 2020
. A further line of research could look at applications in finance: in
Gnoatto et al., 2023
the authors employ the deep BSDE solver to evaluate valuation adjustments in the context of counterparty credit risk in a pure diffusive setting. Since jump processes have not enjoyed a large popularity in the context of counterparty credit risk mainly due to the increased numerical complexity, our approach could allow the use of more complex and realistic asset price models in such context. Another stream of application could be given by quadratic hedging problems, where the deep solver can also be employed as in
Gnoatto et al., 2024
.
