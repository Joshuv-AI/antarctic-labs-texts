Introduction

The
somewhere-to-below shuffles

t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
(and their
linear combinations, called the
one-sided cycle shuffles
) are certain
elements in the group algebra of a symmetric group
S_{n}
. They have been
introduced in
[
GriLaf22
]
by Lafrenière and the present author, and are a
novel generalization of the top-to-random shuffle (also known as the

Tsetlin library
). They are defined by the formula

t_{\ell}:=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell}+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1}+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ell+2}+\cdots+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,n}\in\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right],

where
\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,k}
denotes the cycle
that sends
\ell\mapsto\ell+1\mapsto\ell+2\mapsto\cdots\mapsto k\mapsto\ell

(and leaves all remaining elements of
\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
unchanged).

One of the main results of
[
GriLaf22
]
was the construction of a basis

\left(a_{w}\right)_{w\in S_{n}}
of the group algebra in which
multiplication by these shuffles acts as an upper-triangular matrix (i.e., for
which
a_{w}t_{\ell}
equals a linear combination of
a_{u}
’s with
u\leq w

for a certain total order on
S_{n}
). Consequences of this fact (or, more
precisely, of a certain filtration that entails this fact) include an explicit
description of the eigenvalues of each one-sided cycle shuffle, as well as
analogous properties of some related shuffles.

Another consequence of the joint triangularizability of
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
is the fact that the commutators
\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]:=t_{i}t_{j}-t_{j}t_{i}
are nilpotent (since the commutator of two
upper-triangular matrices is strictly upper-triangular and thus nilpotent).
Explicitly, this means that
\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]^{n!}=0
, since the

t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
act on a free module of rank
n!
. However,
experiments have suggested that the minimal
m\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]^{m}=0
is far smaller than
n!
, and in fact is
bounded from above by
n
.

In the present paper, we shall prove this. Concretely, we will prove the
following results (the notation
\left[m\right]
means the set
\left\{1,2,\ldots,m\right\}

2
Notations and notions

2.1
Basic notations

Let
\mathbf{k}
be any commutative ring. (The reader can safely take

\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{Z}
.)

Let
\mathbb{N}:=\left\{0,1,2,\ldots\right\}
be the set of all
nonnegative integers.

For any integers
a
and
b
, we set

\left[a,b\right]:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}\ \mid\ a\leq k\leq b\right\}=\left\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\right\}.

This is an empty set if
a>b
. In general,
\left[a,b\right]
is called an

integer interval
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\left[n\right]:=\left[1,n\right]=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
.

Fix an integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
S_{n}
be the
n
-th symmetric group,
i.e., the group of all permutations of
\left[n\right]
. We multiply
permutations in the “continental” way: that
is,
\left(\pi\sigma\right)\left(i\right)=\pi\left(\sigma\left(i\right)\right)
for all
\pi,\sigma\in S_{n}
and
i\in\left[n\right]
.

For any
k
distinct elements
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}
of
\left[n\right]
, we let
\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}
be the
permutation in
S_{n}
that sends
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k-1},i_{k}
to

i_{2},i_{3},\ldots,i_{k},i_{1}
, respectively while leaving all remaining
elements of
\left[n\right]
unchanged. This permutation is known as a

cycle
. Note that
\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{i}=\operatorname*{id}

for any single
i\in\left[n\right]
.

For any
i\in\left[n-1\right]
, we let
s_{i}:=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{i,i+1}\in S_{n}
. This permutation
s_{i}
is called a
simple
transposition
, as it swaps
i
with
i+1
while leaving all other elements of

\left[n\right]
unchanged. It clearly satisfies

s_{i}^{2}=\operatorname*{id}.

(2)

Furthermore, two simple transpositions
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
commute whenever

\left|i-j\right|>1
. This latter fact is known as
reflection
locality
.

Consider the group algebra
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
. In this
algebra, define
n
elements
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
by setting
2

2

We
view
S_{n}
as a subset of
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
in the obvious
way.

t_{\ell}:=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell}+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1}+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ell+2}+\cdots+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,n}\in\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]

(3)

for each
\ell\in\left[n\right]
. Thus, in particular,
t_{n}=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{n}=\operatorname*{id}=1
(where
1
means the
unity of
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
). We shall refer to the
n

.
These shuffles were studied in

GriLaf22
]
(where, in particular, their

2.3
Commutators

If
a
and
b
are two elements of some ring, then
\left[a,b\right]

shall denote their commutator
ab-ba
. This notation clashes with our
above-defined notation
\left[a,b\right]
for the interval
\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}\ \mid\ a\leq k\leq b\right\}
(when
a
and
b
are two
integers), but we don’t expect any confusion to arise in practice, since we
will only use the notation
\left[a,b\right]
for

a
and
b

are visibly elements of the ring
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
(as

3
Elementary computations in
S_{n}

In this section, we will perform some simple computations in the symmetric
group
S_{n}
. The results of these computations will later become ingredients
in some of our proofs.

3.1
The cycles
\left(v\Longrightarrow w\right)

Definition 3.1
.

Let
v,w\in\left[n\right]
satisfy
v\leq w
. Then,
\left(v\Longrightarrow w\right)
shall denote the permutation
\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{v,v+1,\ldots,w}
.

The symbol “
\Longrightarrow
” in this
notation
\left(v\Longrightarrow w\right)
has nothing to do with logical
implication; instead, it is meant to summon an image of a “current” flowing from
v
to
w
. The symbol
“
\Longrightarrow
” is understood to bind
less strongly than addition or subtraction; thus, for example, the expression
“
\left(v+1\Longrightarrow w\right)
” means
\left(\left(v+1\right)\Longrightarrow w\right)
.

Every
v\in\left[n\right]
satisfies

\left(v\Longrightarrow v\right)=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{v}=\operatorname*{id}=1.

(4)

The following is just a little bit less obvious:

Let

satisfy

. Then,

\left(v\Longrightarrow w\right)=s_{v}s_{v+1}\cdots s_{w-1}
.

Proof 3.3
.

Easy verification.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
v,w\in\left[n\right]

v<w
. Then:

(a)

We have
\left(v\Longrightarrow w\right)=s_{v}\left(v+1\Longrightarrow w\right)

.

Proof 3.5

Easy verification (easiest using Proposition

Next we recall how conjugation in

Let

i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}
be

\sigma\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}\sigma^{-1}=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\sigma\left(i_{1}\right),\sigma\left(i_{2}\right),\ldots,\sigma\left(i_{k}\right)}.

Proof 3.7

Well-known.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
i,j,v,w\in\left[n\right]
be such that
w\geq v>j\geq i
. Then,

\left(j+1\Longrightarrow v\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)=\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(j\Longrightarrow v-1\right).

Proof 3.9
.

Let
\sigma:=\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)
. We have
i\leq j
(since

j\geq i
) and
v-1\leq w-1
(since
w\geq v
). Thus, the numbers

j,j+1,\ldots,v-1
all belong to the interval
\left[i,w-1\right]
. Hence,
the permutation
\sigma=\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{i,i+1,\ldots,w}
sends these numbers to

j+1,j+2,\ldots,v
, respectively. In other words,

\left(\sigma\left(j\right),\sigma\left(j+1\right),\ldots,\sigma\left(v-1\right)\right)=\left(j+1,j+2,\ldots,v\right).

However, from
\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)=\sigma
and
\left(j\Longrightarrow v-1\right)=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{j,j+1,\ldots,v-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(j\Longrightarrow v-1\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sigma\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{j,j+1,\ldots,v-1}\sigma^{-1}=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\sigma\left(j\right),\sigma\left(j+1\right),\ldots,\sigma\left(v-1\right)}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{by (\ref{eq.prop.cyc-conj.eq})}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{j+1,j+2,\ldots,v}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }\left(\sigma\left(j\right),\sigma\left(j+1\right),\ldots,\sigma\left(v-1\right)\right)=\left(j+1,j+2,\ldots,v\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(j+1\Longrightarrow v\right).

In other words,
\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(j\Longrightarrow v-1\right)=\left(j+1\Longrightarrow v\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)
. Thus, Lemma
3.8
is proved.

Let

4
Basic properties of somewhere-to-below shuffles

We now return to the group algebra
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
. We
begin by rewriting the definition of the somewhere-to-below shuffle
t_{\ell}
:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\ell\in\left[n\right]
. Then,

t_{\ell}=\sum\limits_{w=\ell}^{n}\left(\ell\Longrightarrow w\right).

Proof 4.2
.

From (
3
), we have

\displaystyle t_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell}+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1}+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ell+2}+\cdots+\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{w=\ell}^{n}\underbrace{\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,w}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(\ell\Longrightarrow w\right)\\
\text{(by the definition of }\left(\ell\Longrightarrow w\right)\text{)}\end{subarray}}=\sum\limits_{w=\ell}^{n}\left(\ell\Longrightarrow w\right).

Lemma 4.5

\sigma
commutes with

. This proves Lemma

In other words,

(15)

(16)

\displaystyle t_{i+1}t_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{v=i+1}^{n}\left(i+1\Longrightarrow v\right)\cdot\sum\limits_{w=i}^{n}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)

\text{(by

\text{(since }w\leq v-1<v\text{ and thus }v>w\geq i\text{))}\end{subarray}}+\sum\limits_{v=i+1}^{n}\ \ \sum\limits_{w=v}^{n}\underbrace{\left(i+1\Longrightarrow v\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow v-1\right)\\

\text{(since

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{v=i+1}^{n}\ \ \underbrace{\sum\limits_{w=i}^{v-1}\left(i\Longrightarrow w+1\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow v\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\sum\limits_{w=i+1}^{v}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow v\right)\\

}v-1\text{)}\end{subarray}}

Comparing this with

\displaystyle\left(t_{i}-1\right)t_{i}

\text{(since }i\leq v\leq n\text{)}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{v=i}^{n}\left(\sum\limits_{w=i+1}^{v}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow v\right)+\sum\limits_{w=v+1}^{n}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow v\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{v=i}^{n}\ \ \sum\limits_{w=i+1}^{v}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow v\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\sum\limits_{w=i+1}^{i}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow i\right)+\sum\limits_{v=i+1}^{n}\ \ \sum\limits_{w=i+1}^{v}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)\left(i\Longrightarrow v\right)\\

we obtain

.

.

The definition of a commutator yields

\displaystyle\left[a-1,\ b-1\right]

\displaystyle=\left(a-1\right)\left(b-1\right)-\left(b-1\right)\left(a-1\right)

\displaystyle=\left(ab-a-b+1\right)-\left(ba-b-a+1\right)

\displaystyle=ab-ba=\left[a,b\right].

From

. Hence, Corollary

(applied to

. Thus,

s_{i+1}\underbrace{\left(b-1\right)}_{=s_{i+1}t_{i+2}}=\underbrace{s_{i+1}s_{i+1}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=s_{i+1}^{2}=1\end{subarray}}t_{i+2}=t_{i+2}.

(26)

However, Theorem
6.1

t_{i+2}\left(t_{i}-1\right)=\left(t_{i}-1\right)\left(t_{i+1}-1\right).

In view of
a=t_{i}
and

Hence,

\left(a-1\right)\left(b-1\right)=\underbrace{t_{i+2}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=s_{i+1}\left(b-1\right)\\
\text{(by
(\ref{pf.lem.1+si+1titi+1.=ti+2}))}\end{subarray}}\left(a-1\right)=s_{i+1}\left(b-1\right)\left(a-1\right).

(27)

Now, (

\displaystyle\left[a,b\right]

\displaystyle=\left[a-1,\ b-1\right]=\underbrace{\left(a-1\right)\left(b-1\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=s_{i+1}\left(b-1\right)\left(a-1\right)\\
\text{(by (\ref{pf.lem.1+si+1titi+1.3}))}\end{subarray}}-\left(b-1\right)\left(a-1\right)

\displaystyle=s_{i+1}\left(b-1\right)\left(a-1\right)-\left(b-1\right)\left(a-1\right)

7.2

The following lemma is useful for reducing questions about

Lemma 7.5
.

Let

We have

Proof 7.6
.

A well-known identity for commutators says that if

is a ring, then any

\left[ab,c\right]=\left[a,c\right]b+a\left[b,c\right].

(28)

Hence, if
R

\displaystyle\left[ab,b\right]

\displaystyle=\left[a,b\right]b+a\underbrace{\left[b,b\right]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=bb-bb\\
=0\end{subarray}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{by
(\ref{pf.lem.ti-to-tj-1.b.0}), applied to }c=b\right)

\displaystyle=\left[a,b\right]b+a0=\left[a,b\right]b.

(29)

(a)
Proposition
4.1

\displaystyle t_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{w=i}^{n}\left(i\Longrightarrow w\right)=\sum\limits_{k=i}^{n}\left(i\Longrightarrow k\right)

\text{since }i\leq j\leq k\text{)}\end{subarray}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }i<j\leq n\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=i}^{j-1}\left(i\Longrightarrow k\right)+\left(i\Longrightarrow j\right)\underbrace{\sum\limits_{k=j}^{n}\left(j\Longrightarrow k\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\sum\limits_{w=j}^{n}\left(j\Longrightarrow w\right)=t_{j}\\
\text{(since Proposition \ref{prop.tl-as-sum}}\\

\displaystyle=\left[\sum\limits_{k=i}^{j-1}\left(i\Longrightarrow k\right)+\left(i\Longrightarrow j\right)t_{j},\ t_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=i}^{j-1}\underbrace{\left[\left(i\Longrightarrow k\right),\ t_{j}\right]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=0\\

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since the commutator
}\left[a,b\right]\text{ is bilinear in }a\text{ and }b\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{k=i}^{j-1}0}_{=0}+\left[\left(i\Longrightarrow j\right)t_{j},\ t_{j}\right]=\left[\left(i\Longrightarrow j\right)t_{j},\ t_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\left(i\Longrightarrow j\right),t_{j}\right]t_{j}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{by (\ref{pf.lem.ti-to-tj-1.b.0abb}), applied
to }a=\left(i\Longrightarrow j\right)\text{ and }b=t_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-1},t_{j}\right]t_{j}

(since Proposition
3.2
yields
\left(i\Longrightarrow j\right)=s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-1}
). This proves Lemma

7.5

(a)
.

(b)
Set

However,

(since

On the other hand, applying Lemma

.

7.3
The identity
\left(1+s_{j}\right)\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]=0
for all
i\leq j

We are now ready to prove the following surprising result:

Theorem 7.9
.

Let
i,j\in\left[n-1\right]
satisfy
i\leq j
. Then,

\left(1+s_{j}\right)\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]=0.

Proof 7.10
.

If
i=j
, then this is obvious (since
i=j
entails
\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]=\left[t_{j},t_{j}\right]=0
). Hence, we WLOG assume that

i\neq j
. Thus,
i<j
(since
i\leq j
).

The transpositions
s_{i},s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{j-2}
all commute with
s_{j}
(by
reflection locality, since the numbers
i,i+1,\ldots,j-2
differ by more than

1
from
j
). Thus, their product
s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}
commutes with

s_{j}
as well. In other words,

s_{j}\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)=\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)s_{j}.

Thus, in
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
, we have

\displaystyle\left(1+s_{j}\right)\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)

\displaystyle=s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}+\underbrace{s_{j}\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)}_{=\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)s_{j}}

\displaystyle=s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}+\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)s_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)\left(1+s_{j}\right).

(33)

However, Lemma
7.5

(b)
yields
\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]=\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)\left[t_{j-1},t_{j}\right]
(since
i<j
). Hence,

\displaystyle\left(1+s_{j}\right)\underbrace{\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]}_{=\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)\left[t_{j-1},t_{j}\right]}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(1+s_{j}\right)\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)\left(1+s_{j}\right)\\
\text{(by (\ref{pf.thm.1+sjtitj.2}))}\end{subarray}}\left[t_{j-1},t_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}\right)\underbrace{\left(1+s_{j}\right)\left[t_{j-1},t_{j}\right]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=0\\
\text{(by Lemma
\ref{lem.1+sjtj-1tj})}\end{subarray}}=0.

This proves Theorem
7.9
.

.

Let
n\geq 2
and

. Then,

.

.

This is true for

. Therefore,

. Also,

n-1\in\left[n-1\right]
(since

).

The definition of

yields

8.1
The elements
s_{k}^{+}
and the left ideals
H_{k,j}

We now introduce two crucial notions for the proof of our first main theorem:

Definition 8.1
.

We set
\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
.
Furthermore, for any
i\in\left[n-1\right]
, we set

s_{i}^{+}:=s_{i}+1\in\mathbf{A}.

We also set
s_{i}^{+}:=1\in\mathbf{A}
for all integers
i\notin\left[n-1\right]
. Thus,
s_{i}^{+}
is defined for all integers
i
.

Definition 8.2
.

Let
k
and
j
be two integers. Then, we define

H_{k,j}:=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\left[j,k\right];\\
u\equiv k\operatorname{mod}2\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}s_{u}^{+}.

This is a left ideal of
\mathbf{A}
. Note that

H_{k,j}=0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{whenever }k<j.

(34)

Example 8.3
.

We have

\displaystyle H_{7,3}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\left[3,7\right];\\
u\equiv 7\operatorname{mod}2\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}s_{u}^{+}=\mathbf{A}s_{3}^{+}+\mathbf{A}s_{5}^{+}+\mathbf{A}s_{7}^{+}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle H_{7,2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\left[2,7\right];\\
u\equiv 7\operatorname{mod}2\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}s_{u}^{+}=\mathbf{A}s_{3}^{+}+\mathbf{A}s_{5}^{+}+\mathbf{A}s_{7}^{+},

so that
H_{7,2}=H_{7,3}
. Similarly,
H_{7,4}=H_{7,5}=\mathbf{A}s_{5}^{+}+\mathbf{A}s_{7}^{+}
and
H_{7,6}=H_{7,7}=\mathbf{A}s_{7}^{+}
.

Let us prove some basic properties of the left ideals
H_{k,j}
:

Remark 8.4
.

Let
k
be an integer such that
k\notin\left[n-1\right]
.
Let
j\in\left[n\right]
satisfy
j\leq k
. Then,
H_{k,j}=\mathbf{A}
.

Proof 8.5
.

Since
k\notin\left[n-1\right]
, we have
s_{k}^{+}=1
(by the definition
of
s_{k}^{+}
). Also,
k\in\left[j,k\right]
(since
j\leq k
).

Recall that
H_{k,j}
is defined as the sum
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\left[j,k\right];\\
u\equiv k\operatorname{mod}2\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}s_{u}^{+}
. But this
sum contains the addend
\mathbf{A}s_{k}^{+}
(since
k\in\left[j,k\right]
and
k\equiv k\operatorname{mod}2
). Hence,
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\left[j,k\right];\\
u\equiv k\operatorname{mod}2\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}s_{u}^{+}\supseteq\mathbf{A}\underbrace{s_{k}^{+}}_{=1}=\mathbf{A}1=\mathbf{A}
. Now,

H_{k,j}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\left[j,k\right];\\
u\equiv k\operatorname{mod}2\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}s_{u}^{+}\supseteq\mathbf{A},

so that
H_{k,j}=\mathbf{A}
. This proves Remark
8.4
.

Lemma 8.6

and
j

Lemma 8.10

Further directions

10.1
More identities?

A few other properties of somewhere-to-below shuffles can be shown. For
example, the proofs of the following two propositions are left to the reader:

Proposition 10.1
.

We have
t_{i}=\sum\limits_{k=i}^{j-1}s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{k-1}+s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-1}t_{j}
for any

.

Proposition 10.2
.

Let

be such that
i\leq j
. Then,
\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]=\left[s_{i}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-1},\ s_{j}\right]t_{j+1}t_{j}
.

Proposition 10.3
.

Set
B_{i}:=\prod\limits_{k=0}^{i-1}\left(t_{1}-k\right)
for each
i\in\left[0,n\right]
. Then,
B_{i}=t_{i}B_{i-1}
for each
i\in\left[n\right]
.

We wonder to what extent the identities that hold for
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
can be described. For instance, we can ask:

Question 10.4
.

(a)

What are generators and relations for the
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
\mathbb{Q}\left[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}\right]
for a given

?

(b)

Fix

. What identities hold for

for
all

n
? Is there a single algebra
that “governs” the relations between

t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\ldots
that hold independently of
n
?

(c)

If a relation between
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
holds for
all sufficiently high
n\geq k
, must it then hold for all
n\geq k
?

We suspect that these questions are hard to answer, as we saw in Remark

6.3
that even the quadratic relations between
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
exhibit some rather finicky behavior. The dimension of

-vector
space does not seem to follow a simple rule either (see (
1
) for
the first few values), although there appear to be some patterns in how this

9

9

Namely, for all

.

10.2
Optimal exponents?

Corollary
8.32
and Corollary
9.18
give two
different answers to the question “what powers of

are

In other words:

Question 10.6
.

Given

, what is the smallest

such

\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]^{m}=0

.

10.3
Generalizing to the Hecke algebra

The
type-A Hecke algebra
(also known as the
type-A Iwahori-Hecke
algebra
) is a deformation of the group algebra
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
involving a new parameter
q\in\mathbf{k}
. It is commonly
denoted by
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{q}\left(S_{n}\right)
; it has a basis

\left(T_{w}\right)_{w\in S_{n}}
indexed by the permutations
w\in S_{n}
, but its multiplication is more complicated than composing the indexing
permutations. We refer to
[
Mathas99
]
for the definition and a deep study
of this algebra. We can define the
q
-deformed somewhere-to-below shuffles

t_{1}^{\mathcal{H}},t_{2}^{\mathcal{H}},\ldots,t_{n}^{\mathcal{H}}
by

t_{\ell}^{\mathcal{H}}:=T_{\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell}}+T_{\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1}}+T_{\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ell+2}}+\cdots+T_{\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{\ell,\ell+1,\ldots,n}}\in\mathcal{H}.

Surprisingly, it seems that many of the properties of the original
somewhere-to-below
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
still hold for these
deformations. In particular:

Conjecture 10.7
.

Corollary
9.18
and Corollary
8.32
both seem
to hold in
\mathcal{H}
when the
t_{\ell}
are replaced by the
t_{\ell}^{\mathcal{H}}
.

This generalization is not automatic. Our above proofs do not directly apply
to
\mathcal{H}
, as (for example) Lemma
3.10
does not generalize
to
\mathcal{H}
. The
\mathcal{H}
-generalization of Theorem
5.1

appears to be

qt_{i+1}^{\mathcal{H}}t_{i}^{\mathcal{H}}=\left(t_{i}^{\mathcal{H}}-1\right)t_{i}^{\mathcal{H}}=t_{i}^{\mathcal{H}}\left(t_{i}^{\mathcal{H}}-1\right)

(79)

(verified using SageMath for all
n\leq 11
). (The
q
on the left hand side is
necessary; the product

is not a

\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of

and

when

does not seem to adapt to (

10.4
One-sided cycle shuffles

We return to
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
.

The
\mathbf{k}
-linear combinations
\lambda_{1}t_{1}+\lambda_{2}t_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}t_{n}
(with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbf{k}
) of the somewhere-to-below shuffles are called the

one-sided cycle shuffles
. They have been studied in
[
GriLaf22
]
. Again,
the main result of
[
GriLaf22
]
entails that their commutators are nilpotent,
but we can ask “how nilpotent?”.

This question remains wide open, not least due to its computational complexity
(even the
n=6
case brings SageMath to its limits). All that I can say with
surety is that the commutators of one-sided cycle shuffles don’t vanish as
quickly (under taking powers) as the
\left[t_{i},t_{j}\right]
’s.

Example 10.8
.

For instance, let us set
n=6
and choose arbitrary
a,b,c,d,e,a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime},d^{\prime},e^{\prime}\in\mathbf{k}
, and then
introduce the elements

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle:=at_{1}+bt_{2}+ct_{3}+dt_{4}+et_{5}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=a^{\prime}t_{1}+b^{\prime}t_{2}+c^{\prime}t_{3}+d^{\prime}t_{4}+e^{\prime}t_{5}

(two completely generic one-sided shuffles, except that omit
t_{6}
terms
since
t_{6}=1
does not influence the commutator). Then, 10 minutes of
torturing SageMath reveals that
\left[u,u^{\prime}\right]^{6}=0
, but

\left[u,u^{\prime}\right]^{5}
is generally nonzero.

Even this example is misleadingly well-behaved. For
n=7
, it is not hard to
find two one-sided cycle shuffles
u,u^{\prime}
such that
\left[u,u^{\prime}\right]^{n}\neq 0
.

Question 10.9
.

For each given
n
, what is the smallest (or at least a reasonably small)

m\in\mathbb{N}
such that every two one-sided cycle shuffles

satisfy
\left[u,u^{\prime}\right]^{m}=0
?
