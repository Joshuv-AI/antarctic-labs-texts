Full linear Phan-Thien–Tanner fluid model: Exact analytical solutions for steady, startup, and cessation regimes of shear and extensional flows
D. Shogin

Abstract
Exact, fully explicit, purely real analytical expressions for the material functions describing steady, startup, and cessation regimes of shear flows and of planar, uniaxial, and biaxial extensional flows of full linear Phan-Thien–Tanner fluids are obtained. These expressions, which have no analogs in the literature, are formulated in compact, beautiful forms, partially due to the unique scaling procedure reducing the number of the model parameters from four to one. The properties of the material functions are investigated in detail. For steady extensional flows, the possible shapes of the extensional viscosity curves are described and the conditions for these shapes to occur are determined. For startup flows, it is found when exactly the stress dynamics is oscillatory, and, in this case, a detailed characterization of oscillations is given, which includes expressions for the position and magnitude of stress overshoots and undershoots.

Introduction

The linear

and exponential
Phan-Thien 1978
Phan-Thien–Tanner (PTT) models, derived in the early 1970s from the Lodge–Yamamoto network theory, are among the most used physical non-Newtonian fluid models. Nonlinear in stresses but compact and relatively simple, differential constitutive PTT equations capture a wide range of non-Newtonian phenomena in steady and transient flows. The explicit formulas for the material functions of the linear PTT (LPTT) fluid in steady shear flows have been obtained by Alves
et al.

Alves, Pinho, and Oliveira 2001

et al.

have proposed an implicit expression for steady planar extensional viscosity, where an unknown function is defined as a solution of a certain cubic equation. Such formulation is not completely satisfactory because of its possible ambiguity: Three real solutions for the extensional viscosities of the PTT models, of which only one is physically reasonable, can exist.
Keunings and Crochet 1984
This problem has been partially resolved by Shogin,
Shogin 2020
who has obtained unambiguous analytical expressions for the material functions in steady uniaxial, biaxial, and planar extensional flows. Still, those expressions are not general: They apply exclusively to the so-called “simplified” version of the LPTT model and only at values of the model parameters in a certain range. Furthermore, the formulation of Shogin
Shogin 2020
involves inverses of monotonic functions and, therefore, is not fully explicit.

Even fewer analytical results have been obtained for transient flows of PTT fluids, which is not surprising: In this case, systems of coupled nonlinear differential equations are typically involved. The only exact analytical solutions for startup and cessation flows are those for the simplified LPTT model, obtained recently by Shogin.
Shogin 2020
Earlier analytical works on transient flows were focused on the qualitative analysis of the stress growth. In particular, Missaghi and Petrie
Missaghi and Petrie 1982
have demonstrated that, somewhat unexpectedly, it is possible for PTT fluids to have a stress overshoot in uniaxial stress growth, and Stephenson
Stephenson 1986
has pointed out that the full PTT fluids may behave unphysically in startup of shear flows, providing that the step strain rate is considerably large.

Thus, despite the huge popularity of the PTT fluid models, some important aspects of their rheological properties remain unknown. The purpose of this work is to address this issue and provide a complete, detailed, and fully explicit analytical description of the LPTT fluid rheology in the most common steady and transient flows for all physically reasonable values of the model parameters.

II.2
The constitutive equation

The stress and rate-of-strain tensors of a single-mode LPTT fluid are related through a nonlinear differential constitutive equation,

\left(1-\dfrac{\epsilon\lambda}{\eta_{0}}\mathrm{tr}\bm{\tau}\right)\bm{\tau}+\lambda\overset{\circ}{\bm{\tau}}=-\eta_{0}\bm{\dot{\gamma}}.

(4)

In addition to
\xi
in the Gordon–Schowalter derivative [see Eq. (
1
)], the three other model parameters encountered in Eq. (
4

(\eta_{0})
, the time constant
(\lambda)

(\epsilon)

The dimensionless extensional parameter,
\epsilon
, also called the PTT factor,
Nasseri et al. 2004

Phan-Thien and Tanner 1977

\epsilon\sim 10^{-2}
). Later, however, it was shown that larger values (

Dietz 2015
;
Hatzikiriakos et al. 1997
;
Christodoulou, Hatzikiriakos, and Vlassopoulos 2000
;
Nasseri et al. 2004
;
Campo-Deaño et al. 2013

For the affinity parameter, we shall restrict our consideration to
0\leq\xi<1
, with
\xi\neq 0
corresponding to the “full” (non-affine) PTT model and
\xi=0
yielding the “simplified” (affine) version of the model. The case when
\xi=1
is not of actual rheological interest: It is easy to show that a LPTT fluid with
\xi=1
behaves as a corotational Maxwell fluid in shear flows and as a standard Maxwell fluid in extensional flows.

Finally, we should note that the material functions of multimode PTT fluids are simply the sums of the corresponding material functions of separate modes.
Shogin 2020
Therefore, a single-mode version of the LPTT fluid model shall be considered in this work without loss of generality.

For startup of steady shear flow, the result of nondimensionalization can be written as

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+})\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}+2\Lambda\hat{\mathbb{S}}^{+},

(8)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{S}}^{+}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+})\hat{\mathbb{S}}^{+}-\chi\Lambda\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}+\Lambda,

(9)

with initial conditions
\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}(0)=\hat{\mathbb{S}}^{+}(0)=0
. The dimensionless normal stress differences,
\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}
and
\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{2}^{+}
, are proportional to
\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}
, with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+},

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{2}^{+}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\xi}{2}\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}

(11)

at any
\bar{t}
, and therefore do not appear in the system as independent variables. From Eq. (
11
), it immediately follows that

\Psi_{2}^{+}=-\dfrac{\xi}{2}\Psi_{1}^{+}.

(12)

III.3
Startup of steady planar extensional flow

For startup of steady planar extensional flow, the same procedure leads to

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+})\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}+2\Lambda\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+},

(13)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+})\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}+2\Lambda\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}+4\alpha\Lambda,

(14)

with initial conditions
\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}(0)=\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}(0)=0
. The dimensionless second normal stress difference is simply a halfsum of
\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}
and
\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}
,

\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{2}^{+}=\dfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}+\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+})

(15)

at any
\bar{t}
.

III.4
Startup of steady uniaxial and biaxial extensional flows

For startup of steady uniaxial and biaxial extensional flows [with upper and lower sign chosen in Eq. (
17
), respectively], one arrives at

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+})\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}+2\Lambda\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+},

(16)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{t}}

\displaystyle=-(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+})\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}+\Lambda(\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}\pm\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+})+3\alpha\Lambda,

(17)

with
\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{+}(0)=\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{+}(0)=0
.

III.5
Cessation of steady shear and extensional flows

For cessation of steady shear and extensional flows, nondimensionalization leads to a single independent differential equation,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{-}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{t}}=-(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{-})\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{-},

(18)

with
\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{-}(0)=\hat{\mathbb{T}}
. The other dimensionless stresses, when relevant (note that for affine LPTT fluids in cessation of steady shear flow,
\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{2}^{-}=0
identically), are proportional to
\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{-}
,

\left(\dfrac{\hat{\mathbb{S}}^{-}}{\hat{\mathbb{S}}}=\right)\dfrac{\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{-}}{\hat{\mathbb{T}}}=\dfrac{\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}^{-}}{\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{1}}\left(=\dfrac{\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{2}^{-}}{\hat{\mathbb{N}}_{2}}\right)

(19)

at any
\bar{t}
.

Equations (
18
) and (
19
) contain neither
\chi
nor
\alpha
and thus are identical to those for affine LPTT fluids. The corresponding solving procedure can be found in our recent work
Shogin 2020
and shall not be reproduced here. The final result, however, possesses some distinct features and shall be discussed in Sec.
V.4

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have obtained exact analytical expressions description for the material functions of non-affine and affine LPTT fluids in startup, cessation, and steady regimes of shear flows and of planar, uniaxial, and biaxial extension. Our formulas are simple, fully explicit, purely real, and as general as possible, since no additional restrictions on the model parameters were imposed. To the best of our knowledge, our results have no analogs in the literature.

Containing a detailed investigation of the properties of the aforementioned material functions, this work provides a complete analytical description of rheological properties of LPTT fluids in the most important simple rheometric flows. Given the classical status of the PTT models, we hope that this paper will be useful both to researchers and students in rheology and non-Newtonian fluid mechanics.

Despite the relative simplicity of the mathematical methods implemented in this work, we believe that multiple technical aspects (e.g., reducing the number of the model parameters to one using a nonstandard scaling procedure, extensive application of cubic equations, and solving a system of coupled nonlinear differential equations by analytical means) deserve the attention of mathematical physicists and, in general, of anyone interested in applied mathematics.

Appendix 0.A
General solutions of cubic equations

As seen from Sec.
IV
, exact analytical solutions for steady flows rely heavily on finding positive real solutions of cubic equations of general form

x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{1}x+a_{0}=0,

(113)

where the coefficients
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
a_{0}\neq 0
. The character of the roots of Eq. (
113
) is determined by the sign of the discriminant,

D=18a_{2}a_{1}a_{0}-4a_{2}^{3}a_{0}+a_{2}^{2}a_{1}^{2}-4a_{1}^{3}-27a_{0}^{2}.

(114)

At
D\geq 0
, all three roots of Eq. (
113
) are real (at
D\neq 0
, they are distinct), while at
D<0
, Eq. (
113
) has only one real root, the other two being a couple of (non-real) complex conjugates. In either case, the roots of Eq. (
113
) can be written in compact form using two auxiliary quantities

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{9}(3a_{1}-a_{2}^{2}),

(115)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{54}(27a_{0}-9a_{1}a_{2}+2a_{2}^{3}),

(116)

with the sign of
q^{2}+p^{3}
always being opposite to that of the discriminant.

In the case when Eq. (
113
) has only one real root (
D<0
), this root can be expressed by Cardano’s formula,

x=-\dfrac{a_{2}}{3}+\sqrt[3]{-q+\sqrt{q^{2}+p^{3}}}+\sqrt[3]{-q-\sqrt{q^{2}+p^{3}}}.

(117)

More generally, the three roots of Eq. (
113
) can be written in trigonometric form,

x_{j}=-\dfrac{a_{2}}{3}+2\sqrt{-p}\cos\left[\dfrac{1}{3}\arccos\dfrac{q}{p\sqrt{-p}}-\dfrac{2\pi(j-1)}{3}\right],\quad j\in\{1,2,3\}.

(118)

If
D\geq 0
(three real roots), expression (
118
) is purely real. Furthermore, a straightforward evaluation of the cosine shows that
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq x_{3}
. If
D<0
, Eq. (
118
) is still valid but involves complex functions; in calculations, the principal values of these functions should be used.

From Vieta’s formulas, it can be seen that the three roots of Eq. (
113
) satisfy

\displaystyle-a_{0}

\displaystyle=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3},

(119)

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}x_{3}+x_{3}x_{1},

(120)

\displaystyle-a_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}.

(121)

These relations are helpful when the signs of the (real) roots are to be determined. In particular, it follows immediately from Eq. (
119
) that in the case of one real root, the sign of this root is identical to that of
(-a_{0})
.

Appendix 0.C
Monotonic increase of
\hat{\mathbb{T}}(\Lambda)
in steady flows

The purpose of this section is to prove that
\hat{\mathbb{T}}(\Lambda)
is a strictly increasing function for all steady flows considered in this work. The proof shall be conducted as follows: Instead of investigating the explicit formulas [Eqs. (
29
), (
45
), and (
61
)] directly, we shall demonstrate that the inverse functions,
\Lambda(\hat{\mathbb{T}})
, are well-defined and monotonically increasing with
\hat{\mathbb{T}}
.

Equations (
28
), (
44
), and (
60
) are all quadratic with respect to the dimensionless strain rate and can be solved for
\Lambda
. For each of these equations, the solutions satisfying
\Lambda>0
at
\hat{\mathbb{T}}>0
must be chosen.

For shear flow [Eq. (
28
)], one gets

\Lambda(\hat{\mathbb{T}})=(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}})\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{\mathbb{T}}}{2(1-\chi\hat{\mathbb{T}})}}.

(133)

One immediately observes that
\hat{\mathbb{T}}(\Lambda)
is bounded at
\chi\neq 0
[since the right-hand side of Eq. (
133
) implies
0<\chi\hat{\mathbb{T}}<1
] and unbounded at
\chi=0
. Differentiating Eq. (
133
) with respect to
\hat{\mathbb{T}}
, one obtains

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}}=\dfrac{1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}(3-2\chi\hat{\mathbb{T}})}{2\sqrt{2\hat{\mathbb{T}}(1-\chi\hat{\mathbb{T}})^{3}}},

(134)

which is positive at
0\leq\chi\hat{\mathbb{T}}<1
.

For planar extensional flow [Eq. (
44
)], one gets

\Lambda(\hat{\mathbb{T}})=\dfrac{1+\hat{\mathbb{T}}}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{\mathbb{T}}}{2\alpha+\hat{\mathbb{T}}}},

(135)

the derivative of which,

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}}=\dfrac{\alpha+3\alpha\hat{\mathbb{T}}+\hat{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}{2\sqrt{\hat{\mathbb{T}}(2\alpha+\hat{\mathbb{T}})^{3}}},

(136)

is clearly positive.

Finally, for uniaxial and biaxial extensional flows [Eq. (
60
), with upper and lower signs chosen, respectively],

\Lambda(\hat{\mathbb{T}})=\dfrac{2\hat{\mathbb{T}}(1+\hat{\mathbb{T}})}{Y\pm\hat{\mathbb{T}}},

(137)

where

Y=\sqrt{3\hat{\mathbb{T}}(8\alpha+3\hat{\mathbb{T}})}>0.

(138)

Differentiating Eq. (
137
) with respect to
\hat{\mathbb{T}}
, one arrives after some algebraic transformations at

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}}=\dfrac{2\hat{\mathbb{T}}\left[12\alpha(1+3\hat{\mathbb{T}})+\hat{\mathbb{T}}(9\hat{\mathbb{T}}\pm Y)\right]}{Y(Y\pm\hat{\mathbb{T}})^{2}},

(139)

For uniaxial extensional flow [upper signs in Eq. (
139
)], the positivity of
\mathrm{d}\Lambda/\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}
is obvious. For biaxial extension [lower signs in Eq. (
139

\mathrm{d}\Lambda/\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}
is identical to that of the expression in square brackets [see the numerator on the right-hand side of Eq. (

which is positive. Therefore,
\mathrm{d}\Lambda/\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{T}}>0
also for biaxial extension, which completes the proof of the original statement.

Appendix 0.D
Monotonic properties of stresses and material functions in steady flows

As demonstrated in Appendix

\hat{\mathbb{T}}
