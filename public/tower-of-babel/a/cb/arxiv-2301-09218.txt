An Exponential Separation Between Quantum Query Complexity and the Polynomial Degree
Andris Ambainis, Aleksandrs Belovs

Abstract
While it is known that there is at most a polynomial separation between quantum query complexity and the polynomial degree for total functions, the precise relationship between the two is not clear for partial functions. In this paper, we demonstrate an exponential separation between exact polynomial degree and approximate quantum query complexity for a partial Boolean function. For an unbounded alphabet size, we have a constant versus polynomial separation.

2.1
Polynomials

For a (partial) Boolean function

The motivation behind this definition is that, as shown in

The notion of representing polynomial can be generalised for functions with larger input alphabets as well.
Let

be a (partial) function with alphabet size

Then, we can define its representing polynomial (see, e.g.,

]
)

Namely, it is a real multivariate polynomial

•

•

0\leq P(1_{x_{1}=1},\dots,1_{x_{n}=q})\leq 1
for all

The motivation is similar to the Boolean case.

It may seem that the two definitions do not match for

The

Similarly, one can define an approximating polynomial and the approximate degree, but we will not need these notions in the paper.

Assume for simplicity that

In this case, we can convert a function

into a function

We have

1_{x_{i}=a}=1_{x_{i,1}=a_{1}}1_{x_{i,2}=a_{2}}\cdots 1_{x_{i,\ell}=a_{\ell}}.

Therefore, every representing polynomial for

The function for which we give the separation is defined in the following way.

3n

Quantum Lower Bound

We prove a slightly stronger result, as we prove a lower bound for an easier function.
For

\mu_{s,t}=\bigl\{(j,\;n+1+(j+s\bmod n),\;2n+1+(j+t\bmod n))\mathbin{\bigl|}j\in[n]\bigr\}.

Each

is a tripartite matching between

,

and

.
We call it a shifted tripartite matching.
We use

to denote the set of all shifted tripartite matchings (for a fixed value of

Definition 7
.

In the
tripartite shift problem
, given
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{3n}
, the task is to distinguish the following two cases:

•

there is no equation satisfied in the tripartite system (

) (negative case); and

Since each shifted tripartite matching specifies

the tripartite shift problem is a restriction of the threshold satisfiability problem.
Therefore, any lower bound for the former is a lower bound for the latter.

A closely related problem was studied in

It was like in Definition
7

•

Input-Related Sets.

We begin with defining some input-related sets.
Let

\widetilde{N}=[q]^{3n}

be the set of

N

be the set of

P

be the set of

For

\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}=\bigl\{x\in[q]^{\{a,b,c\}}\mid x_{a}+x_{b}+x_{c}=r_{a,b,c}\bigr\},

be the solution set of the corresponding tripartite equation.
For

\widetilde{P}^{\mu}=\prod_{(a,b,c)\in\mu}\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}.

(5)

In other words,

if and only if all the equations of the tripartite system (

Finally,

We use the disjoint union here because an input

We can define

We consider

as a subset of

As one can guess from the notation, we use

We first prove the claim for

The probability

By the union bound, the probability

We can write

The most important of them is

\chi_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The Fourier basis of
\mathcal{H}^{T}
is given by tensor products
\chi_{s}=\bigotimes_{j\in T}\chi_{s_{j}}
, where each
s_{j}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}
.
The
support
of
\chi_{s}
is
\{j\in T\mid s_{j}\neq 0\}
.

Define two orthogonal projectors in
\mathcal{H}
:

\Pi_{0}=\chi_{0}\chi_{0}^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{1}=I-\Pi_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\chi_{i}\chi_{i}^{*}.

An important relation is

\Pi_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\longmapsto}}\Pi_{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\longmapsto}}-\Pi_{0},

(10)

where
\Delta
is as in (
1
) and acts on the sole variable, and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\longmapsto}}
is consequently defined as at the end of Section
2.2
.
For
S\subseteq T
, define the projector
\Pi^{T}_{S}
in the space
\mathcal{H}^{T}
by

\Pi^{T}_{S}=\bigotimes\nolimits_{j\in T}\Pi_{1_{j\in S}}.

(11)

Let
\mathcal{H}^{T}_{S}
be its image.
It is equal to the span of all the Fourier basis elements of
\mathcal{H}^{T}
with support equal to
S
.
For a fixed
T
, the set of all
\mathcal{H}^{T}_{S}
gives an orthogonal decomposition of
\mathcal{H}^{T}
.

We have the following properties of
\Pi^{T}_{S}
.
First, from the definition, we get the union property

\Pi^{T}_{S}\otimes\Pi^{T^{\prime}}_{S^{\prime}}=\Pi^{T\cup T^{\prime}}_{S\cup S^{\prime}}

(12)

whenever
T
and
T^{\prime}
are disjoint.
Next, by (
10
), we get the reduction property

\Pi^{T}_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta_{j}}}{{\longmapsto}}\begin{cases}\Pi^{T}_{S},&\text{if $j\notin S$;}\\
-\Pi^{T}_{S\setminus\{j\}},&\text{if $j\in S$.}\end{cases}

(13)

The Building Blocks.

Now let us describe the building blocks our matrices are comprised of.
Assume that
(a,b,c)\in A\times B\times C
, and
S\subset\{a,b,c\}
is of size
|S|\leq 1
.
We define

\Psi^{a,b,c}_{S}=\sqrt{q}\,\Pi^{a,b,c}_{S}[\![\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c},{[q]^{\{a,b,c\}}}]\!].

(14)

These are the matrices from (
11
) with
T=\{a,b,c\}
whose rows have been restricted to the solution set
\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}
of the corresponding tripartite equation.
The factor
\sqrt{q}
is due to normalisation purposes.

Claim 10
.

The operator
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{S}
is an isometry from
\mathcal{H}^{a,b,c}_{S}
into
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}}
.
Moreover, the operators
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\emptyset}
,
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\{a\}}
,
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\{b\}}
, and
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\{c\}}
have pairwise orthogonal ranges.

Proof.

From the definition, it is clear that the coimage of
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{S}
is contained in the coimage of
\Pi^{a,b,c}_{S}
, which is
\mathcal{H}^{a,b,c}_{S}
.
The operators

\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\emptyset}
,
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\{a\}}
,
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\{b\}}
, and
\Psi^{a,b,c}_{\{c\}}

map the corresponding Fourier basis elements

\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0},\quad\chi_{s_{a}}\otimes\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0},\quad\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{s_{b}}\otimes\chi_{0},\quad\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{s_{c}},

where
s_{a},s_{b},s_{c}
are non-zero, into the vectors

\displaystyle\sqrt{q}(\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0})[\![\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}]\!],

\displaystyle\sqrt{q}(\chi_{s_{a}}\otimes\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0})[\![\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}]\!],

(15)

\displaystyle\sqrt{q}(\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{s_{b}}\otimes\chi_{0})[\![\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}]\!],

\displaystyle\sqrt{q}(\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{s_{c}})[\![\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}]\!],

respectively.
It remains to prove that all these vectors together form an orthonormal system in
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}}
.

We can identify
x\in\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}
with
x\in[q]^{\{a,b\}}
as the third element
x_{c}
is uniquely determined by
x_{c}=r_{a,b,c}-x_{a}-x_{b}
.
Therefore, we may treat the vectors from (
15
) as belonging to
\mathcal{H}^{a,b}
.
Under this assumption, the first three vectors in (
15
) become

\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{0},\qquad\chi_{s_{a}}\otimes\chi_{0},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\chi_{0}\otimes\chi_{s_{b}}.

(16)

(Here we used the
\sqrt{q}
prefactor to compensate for one missing
\chi_{0}
.)
Considering the last vector, its entry corresponding to
x\in\widetilde{P}^{a,b,c}
is

\tfrac{1}{q}\omega_{q}^{s_{c}x_{c}}=\tfrac{1}{q}\omega_{q}^{s_{c}(r_{a,b,c}-x_{a}-x_{b})}=\tfrac{\omega_{q}^{s_{c}r_{a,b,c}}}{q}\omega_{q}^{-s_{c}x_{a}-s_{c}x_{b}}.

Hence, the last vector of (
15
) becomes

\omega_{q}^{s_{c}r_{a,b,c}}\;\chi_{-s_{c}}\otimes\chi_{-s_{c}}.

(17)

Clearly, the vectors in (
16
) and (
17
) form an orthonormal system.
∎

Now, let
\mu\in M
, and
S\subseteq[3n]
be good for
\mu
, i.e.,
|S\cap\{a,b,c\}|\leq 1
for every
(a,b,c)\in\mu
.
We define the operator

\Psi^{\mu}_{S}=q^{n/2}\Pi^{[3n]}_{S}[\![\widetilde{P}^{\mu},\widetilde{N}]\!]=\bigotimes_{(a,b,c)\in\mu}\Psi^{a,b,c}_{S\cap\{a,b,c\}},

(18)

where the equality follows from the union property (
12
) and the definition (
5
) of
\widetilde{P}^{\mu}
.

Claim 11

(Orthogonal Isometry Claim)
.

The operator
\Psi^{\mu}_{S}
is an isometry from
\mathcal{H}^{[3n]}_{S}
into
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{P}^{\mu}}
.
Moreover, for a fixed
\mu
, the ranges of
\Psi^{\mu}_{S}
are pairwise orthogonal.

Proof.

This follows from Claim
10
and the second definition of
\Psi^{\mu}_{S}
in (
18
).
∎

Also, from (
13
) and the first definition of
\Psi^{\mu}_{S}
in (
18
), we get the reduction property

\Psi^{\mu}_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta_{j}}}{{\longmapsto}}\begin{cases}\Psi^{\mu}_{S},&\text{if $j\notin S$;}\\
-\Psi^{\mu}_{S\setminus\{j\}},&\text{if $j\in S$.}\end{cases}

(19)

The Matrix
G(\alpha)
.

Now we are able to define the matrix
G(\alpha)
for
\alpha
from (
6
).
It is a
\widetilde{P}\times\widetilde{N}
-matrix defined as the vertical stack of matrices

G(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}G^{\mu_{1,1}}(\alpha)\\
\vdots\\
G^{\mu_{n,n}}(\alpha)\end{pmatrix},

(20)

where we have one block

G^{\mu}(\alpha)=\sum_{S\subseteq[3n]}\alpha(\mu,S)\Psi^{\mu}_{S}

for each shifted tripartite matching
\mu\in M
.
Recall that we implicitly assume that
\alpha(\mu,S)=0
if
S
is not good for
\mu
, therefore, the
\Psi^{\mu}_{S}
that appear in the latter sum are well-defined (satisfy the conditions above (
18
)).

For two
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
as in (
6
), we can define
\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}
element-wise, and
G(\alpha)
is linear in
\alpha
:
G(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime})=G(\alpha)+G(\alpha^{\prime})
.

Claim 12
.

Thus defined matrix
G(\alpha)
satisfies the claims in (
8
):
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert G(\alpha)}}\right\rVert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\alpha}}\right\rVert

and

G(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta_{j}}}{{\longmapsto}}G(\partial_{j}\alpha)
for all
j\in[3n]
.

Proof.

Let us start with the first claim.
We can write
G(\alpha)=\sum_{S}G(\alpha|_{S})
, where

\alpha|_{S}(\mu,S^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\alpha(\mu,S^{\prime}),&\text{if $S^{\prime}=S$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(21)

We have that
G^{\mu}(\alpha|_{S})=\alpha(\mu,S)\Psi^{\mu}_{S}
.
Since by the Orthogonal Isometry Claim
11
, each
\Psi^{\mu}_{S}
is an isometry from
\mathcal{H}^{[3n]}_{S}
, we get

\bigl\lVert{G(\alpha|_{S})}\bigr\rVert=\sqrt{\sum\nolimits_{\mu\in M}\alpha(\mu,S)^{2}}.

By the same Claim
11
, the ranges and coimages of all
G(\alpha|_{S})
are pairwise orthogonal.
Hence,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert G(\alpha)}}\right\rVert=\max_{S}\bigl\lVert{G(\alpha|_{S})}\bigr\rVert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\alpha}}\right\rVert
as defined in (
7
).

By the reduction property (
19
), the second claim holds for each block of
G(\alpha)
, that is:

G^{\mu}(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta_{j}}}{{\longmapsto}}G^{\mu}(\partial_{j}\alpha).

Therefore, it holds for the whole matrix
G(\alpha)
.
∎

Construction of
\alpha
.

We define

\alpha(\mu,S)=\frac{1}{n}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{n^{1/3}-|S|,0}}\right\}

(22)

if
S
is good for
\mu
. Otherwise, we assume
\alpha(\mu,S)=0
.

Claim 13
.

The
\alpha
as defined in (
22
) satisfies the conditions in (
9
):

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\alpha}}\right\rVert=n^{1/3}

and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\partial_{j}\alpha}}\right\rVert=O(1)
for all
j\in[3n]
.

Proof.

The value
\|\alpha\|=n^{1/3}
is attained at
S=\emptyset
.

Now let us prove the second property.
It suffices to check that, for all
S
and
j\in[3n]\setminus S
,

\sum\nolimits_{\mu\in M}\bigl(\alpha(\mu,S)-\alpha(\mu,S\cup\{j\})\bigr)^{2}=O(1).

(23)

Fix
S
and
j
. If
|S|\geq n^{1/3}
, then (
23
) is zero, so we may assume
|S|\leq n^{1/3}
.
There are
n^{2}
choices of
\mu\in M
. They fall into three categories:

•

S
is not good for
\mu
. Then,
S\cup\{j\}
is also not good for
\mu
and

\alpha(\mu,S)-\alpha(\mu,S\cup\{j\})=0.

•

S\cup\{j\}
is good for
\mu
. Then,
S
is also good for
\mu
, and

\bigl\lvert\alpha(\mu,S)-\alpha(\mu,S\cup\{j\})\bigr\rvert\leq\frac{1}{n}.

(The
\leq
case may hold for
|S|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor n^{1/3}}}\right\rfloor
. Otherwise, we have equality.)

•

S
is good for
\mu
, but
S\cup\{j\}
is not good for
\mu
.
In this case, we have an upper bound of

\bigl\lvert\alpha(\mu,S)-\alpha(\mu,S\cup\{j\})\bigr\rvert=\bigl\lvert\alpha(\mu,S)\bigr\rvert\leq n^{-2/3}.

Let us estimate for how many
\mu
the third option above holds.
This happens only if there is
(a,b,c)\in\mu
such that
j\in\{a,b,c\}
and
\bigl\lvert S\cap\{a,b,c\}\bigr\rvert=1
.
Assume for concreteness
j=a\in A=\{1,\dots,n\}
, the other two cases being similar.
Let
\mu=\mu_{s,t}
as defined in (
4
).
We get that the third option holds only if

either
n+1+(j+s\bmod n)
or
2n+1+(j+t\bmod n)
belongs to
S
.

There are at most
|S|n\leq n^{4/3}
choices of
s
and
t
that satisfy the above condition.
Therefore,

\sum_{\mu\in M}\bigl(\alpha(\mu,S)-\alpha(\mu,S\cup\{j\})\bigr)^{2}\leq n^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}+n^{4/3}\cdot n^{-4/3}=O(1).\qed

Finishing the Proof

As mentioned previously, we define the adversary matrix as

\Gamma=G(\alpha)[\![P,N]\!]

with the choice of
\alpha
from (
22
).
Then,
\Gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta_{j}}}{{\longmapsto}}G(\partial_{j}\alpha)[\![P,N]\!]
and for the latter matrix by (
8
) and (
9
), we have

\bigl\lVert G(\partial_{j}\alpha)[\![P,N]\!]\bigr\rVert\leq\bigl\lVert G(\partial_{j}\alpha)\bigr\rVert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\partial_{j}\alpha}}\right\rVert=O(1).

It remains to lower bound
\|\Gamma\|
.
We can write
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}+\alpha^{\prime\prime}
where
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha|_{\emptyset}
as in (
21
) and
\alpha^{\prime\prime}=\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}
.
Let
u_{P}
,
u_{\widetilde{P}}
,
u_{N}
, and
u_{\widetilde{N}}
denote the uniform unit vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{P}
,
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{P}}
,
\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{N}}
, respectively.
That is,
u_{P}[\![x]\!]=1/\sqrt{|P|}
for all
x\in P
, and similarly for other vectors.
We have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\Gamma}}\right\rVert\geq u_{P}^{*}\Gamma u_{N}=u_{P}^{*}\bigl(G(\alpha^{\prime})[\![P,N]\!]\bigr)u_{N}+u_{P}^{*}\bigl(G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})[\![P,N]\!]\bigr)u_{N}.

(24)

We bound both terms separately.

By construction,
G(\alpha^{\prime})
is an
\widetilde{P}\times\widetilde{N}
matrix which is a vertical stack of matrices (
20
), where each block is
n^{-2/3}\Psi^{\mu}_{\emptyset}
.
Each
\Psi^{\mu}_{\emptyset}
has all its entries equal (to
q^{-5n/2}
).
Thus,

u_{\widetilde{P}}^{*}G(\alpha^{\prime})u_{\widetilde{N}}=\|G(\alpha^{\prime})\|=\|\alpha^{\prime}\|=n^{1/3}.

Using Claim
9
, we get

u_{P}^{*}\bigl(G(\alpha^{\prime})[\![P,N]\!]\bigr)u_{N}=\sqrt{\frac{|P|\cdot|N|}{|\widetilde{P}|\cdot|\widetilde{N}|}}\;u_{\widetilde{P}}^{*}G(\alpha^{\prime})u_{\widetilde{N}}\geq\frac{3n^{1/3}}{4}.

(25)

Now consider the second term.
First, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})}}\right\rVert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\alpha^{\prime\prime}}}\right\rVert<n^{1/3}.

Next, by Claim
11
, the vector
u_{\widetilde{P}}
is orthogonal to the range of
G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})
.
Therefore,

u_{\widetilde{P}}^{*}G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})[\![\widetilde{P},N]\!]=0.

We will use the vector
\widetilde{u}=\sqrt{|\widetilde{P}|/|P|}u_{\widetilde{P}}
.
It has the property
\widetilde{u}[\![P]\!]=u_{P}
.
Also, by Claim
9
,
\bigl\lVert\widetilde{u}[\![\widetilde{P}\setminus P]\!]\bigr\rVert\leq 1/\sqrt{3}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle u_{P}^{*}\bigl(G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})[\![P,N]\!]\bigr)u_{N}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{u}^{*}\bigl(G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})[\![\widetilde{P},N]\!]\bigr)u_{N}-\widetilde{u}[\![\widetilde{P}\setminus P]\!]^{*}\bigl(G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})[\![\widetilde{P}\setminus P,N]\!]\bigr)u_{N}

(26)

\displaystyle\geq-\bigl\lVert\widetilde{u}[\![\widetilde{P}\setminus P]\!]\bigr\rVert\cdot\bigl\lVert G(\alpha^{\prime\prime})[\![\widetilde{P}\setminus P,N]\!]\bigr\rVert\geq-n^{1/3}/\sqrt{3}.

Combining Eq. (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
), we get

\|\Gamma\|\geq\Bigl(\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\Bigr)n^{1/3}=\Omega(n^{1/3}).

5
Discussion

The choice of the problem (
3
) has been chiefly motivated by the availability of a relatively simple lower bound in
[
11
]
.
In principle, it is possible to analyse other problems.
For instance, consider a problem of the form

x_{a}+x_{b}=r_{a,b},\qquad\text{for $a\neq b$ in $[2n]$,}

(27)

and take
M
as consisting of all perfect matchings in
[2n]
.

If
+
is the bit-wise xor and all
r_{a,b}
are zeroes, we get the collision problem.
The lower bound on its quantum query complexity is
\Omega(n^{1/3})
and it was obtained by Aaronson and Shi
[
5
]
using the polynomial method.
We see no reason to expect the non-homogeneous case (with
r_{a,b}
non-zero) to be any simpler, but the argument of Section
3
shows that it completely breaks down Aaronson’s and Shi’s lower bound.
For the adversary method, which we used in Section
4
, there was essentially no difference between the homogeneous case of
[
11
]
and the non-homogeneous case of the current paper.
The right-hand sides
r_{a,b,c}
of (
3
) manifested themselves as the phases in (
17
), which are irrelevant to the proof.
It would be interesting to establish lower bounds for the problem in (
27
). This would improve the constants in our lower bounds.

Concerning Corollary
2
, it is not clear whether
O(\log n)
can be improved to something better, thus resulting in a superexponential separation.
