The Perturbative Resolvent Method: spectral densities of random matrix ensembles via perturbation theory
Wenping Cui, Jason W. Rocks, Pankaj Mehta

Abstract
We present a simple, perturbative approach for calculating spectral densities for random matrix ensembles in the thermodynamic limit we call the Perturbative Resolvent Method (PRM). The PRM is based on constructing a linear system of equations and calculating how the solutions to these equation change in response to a small perturbation using the zero-temperature cavity method. We illustrate the power of the method by providing simple analytic derivations of the Wigner Semi-circle Law for symmetric matrices, the Marchenko-Pastur Law for Wishart matrices, the spectral density for a product Wishart matrix composed of two square matrices, and the Circle and elliptic laws for real random matrices.



Random matrices are central to variety of problems ranging from statistical physics
Dyson 1962
;
Auffinger et al. 2013
to quantum chaos
Kriecherbauer et al. 2001
, ecology
May 1972
;
Allesina and Tang 2015
;
Biroli et al. 2018
, and wireless communication
Couillet and Debbah 2011
.
An important problem in Random Matrix Theory (RMT) is to calculate the spectral density of an ensemble of random matrices
Livan et al. 2018
. The spectral density measures the density of eigenvalues in the complex plane and plays a central role in many RMT-based approaches. There exist numerous methods for calculating
spectral densities, including many prominent methods that draw heavily from the physics of disordered systems such as the replica method
Kühn 2008
, large-N diagrammatic expansions
Sengupta and Mitra 1999
, and the finite temperature cavity method
Rogers et al. 2008
;
Rogers and Castillo 2009
.

Here, we introduce a new simple, flexible, perturbative approach for calculating spectral densities in the thermodynamic limit (i.e. in the limit where the size of the matrices become infinitely large). Our method is inspired by recent zero temperature cavity calculations in the context of ecology
Advani et al. 2018
;
Mehta et al. 2018
;
Cui et al. 2020
;
Cui et al. 2019
. The central observation underlying the method is the observation that the spectral density can be calculated by constructing an appropriately chosen system of random linear equations and then asking how the solutions to these equations change in response to small constant perturbations. In particular, we show that the trace of the susceptibility matrix which measures responses to perturbations is precisely the resolvent or Green’s function and can be calculated easily using the zero-temperature cavity method. For this reason, we refer to this approach as the Perturbative Resolvent Method (PRM). Since at its core the PRM is just simply a perturbative way of computing Green’s functions, we can make use of many powerful results in RMT and statistical physics relating Green’s functions to spectral densities. These include generalizations of the Green’s function method to non-Hermitian matrices through hermitian reduction
Feinberg and Zee 1997a
;
Feinberg and Zee 1997b
, which associates with each
N
-dimensional non-hermitean ensemble an auxiliary ensemble of
2N
-dimensional Hermitian matrices. In the PRM, this “doubling” of the degrees of freedom simply corresponds to “doubling” the the number of random equations, allowing us to also easily calculate spectral densities of many non-Hermitian ensembles.

The PRM assumes a Replica Symmetric Ansatz and exploits the zero-temperature cavity method to calculate Green’s functions
Brézin and Zee 1995
. An important technical consideration
that makes the method particularly simple to implement is that, for many random matrix ensembles, there is no need to explicitly solve the resulting self-consistent mean-field cavity equations. Instead, the problem often reduces to simply solving a polynomial equation for the susceptibility. We illustrate this procedure below by giving simple derivations of spectral densities for a number of random matrix ensembles

The paper is organized as follows. We begin by giving some background on the resolvent/Green’s function method for Hermitian and non-Hermitian matrices. We then introduce the Perturbative Resolvent Method (PRM) in the context of real symmetric random matrices and show how to derive the Wigner’s semi-circle law
Wigner 1993
. We then show how this construction can be generalized to calculate the Marchenko-Pastur Law for Wishart matrices
Marchenko and Pastur 1967
and to calculate the spectral density for a product Wishart matrix composed of two square matrices. Finally, we show how the PRM can be generalized to real non-symmetric matrices and provide simple derivations of Girko’s circle law
Girko 1985
and elliptic laws
Girko 1986
for real random matrices .

I
Resolvent Methods for Hermitian and Non-Hermitian Matrices

We begin by briefly summarizing the mathematical results we make use of in the paper. For a full discussion, of the Resolvent/Green’s function method in RMT we urge the reader to consult one of the many excellent reviews or textbooks in the field
Rogers 2010
;
Livan et al. 2018
;
Tao 2012
;
Couillet and Debbah 2011
;

;

I.1
Hermitian Matrices

Let
A
be a
N\times N
symmetric matrix. The resolvent or Green’s function of
A
is given by the expression

G_{A}(z)={1\over zI-A}.

(1)

where
z
is a scalar constant and
I
is the
N\times N
identity matrix.
We define the spectral density of
A
as

\rho_{A}(x)={1\over N}\sum_{i}\delta(x-\lambda_{i}),

(2)

where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the matrix
A
. It what follows we will be concerned almost exclusively with the thermodynamic limit
where
N\rightarrow\infty
and the spectrum becomes continuous. The spectral density
\rho_{A}
of the eigenvalues of
A
can be
extracted from the Green’s function using the standard relationship

\rho_{A}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}{1\over\pi}\mathrm{Im}\quantity[{1 \over N}\mathrm{Tr} \, G_A(x-i \epsilon)],

(3)

where
\mathrm{Tr}
is the trace.

I.2
Non-Hermitian Matrices

Zee and Feinberg
Feinberg and Zee 1997a
generalized this to non-Hermitian matrices (which we also denote by
A
) by carefully considering differentials in the complex plane.
Generically, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
of a non-hermitian matrix are complex and have non-zero real and imaginary parts. The spectral density of
A

over the complex plane
z=x+iy
is given by

\rho_{A}(x,y)={1\over N}\sum_{i}\delta(x-\mathrm{Re}[\lambda_{i}])\delta(y-\mathrm{Im}[\lambda_{i}]).

(4)

Again, we will focus on the thermodynamic limit where
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Zee and Feinberg exploited the fact that over the complex plane on a single Riemann cut

\partial_{z}\frac{1}{z^{*}}=\pi\delta(x)\delta(y)

(5)

to generalize the resolvent formalism above to calculate such spectral functions.
In particular, they considered a “symmetrization” of the matrix
A
by defining a new
2N\times 2N
Hermitian matrix

H(z)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
0 & A-z \\
A^*-z^* & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)

(6)

and a corresponding Green’s function in the
2N
dimensional space

\mathcal{G}_{A}(\eta)={1\over\eta I-H}

(7)

where
\eta
is a scalar constant.
This Green’s function can be rewritten in block diagonal form (with each block matrix is of size
N\times N
) as

\mathcal{G}_{A}(\eta)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\chi& \nu\\
\nu^* & \chi\endlx@physics@matrix),

(8)

where in writing this in terms of
\chi
and
\nu
we have exploited the symmetries and block diagonal structure of
H(z)
.
In terms of block matrices, these relationships can be written as

\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
\eta& zI-A \\
z^*I-A^* & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\chi& \nu\\
\nu^* & \chi\endlx@physics@matrix)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
I & 0 \\
0 & I\endlx@physics@matrix).

(9)

Focusing on the upper left block, one has
\eta\chi+(zI-A)\nu^{*}=I
, which for the special case
\eta=0
implies that the resolvent (Eq.
13
) is

G_{A}(z)={1\over zI-A}=\nu^{*}

(10)

and

\rho_{A}(x,y)={1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}\evaluated{\qty[ {1 \over N}\Tr\, \nu^*(z, z^*)]}_{\eta=0},

(11)

where in going to the second equation we make use of Eq.
5
.

Here, we slightly modify this prescription. The reason is that a careful analysis actually shows that this formalism, while essentially correct, results
in choosing the wrong complement of the full Riemann sphere for the domain where the spectral density is non-zero (though the boundary between regions
is correctly identified). We note that this mistake is already present in the explicit expressions for the Circle law derived in
Feinberg and Zee 1997a
. To correct this mistake, we modify a sign in the original construction and consider the
2N
-dimensional non-symmetric matrix

H(z)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
0 & A-z \\
-A^*+z^* & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)

(12)

and once again calculate the corresponding Green’s function in the
2N
dimensional space

\mathcal{G}_{A}(\eta)={1\over\eta I-H}.

(13)

This Green’s function can be rewritten in block diagonal form (with each block matrix is of size
N\times N
) as

\mathcal{G}_{A}(\eta)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\chi& \nu\\
-\nu^* & \chi\endlx@physics@matrix),

(14)

and an almost identical calculation yields that

\rho_{A}(x,y)=-{1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}\evaluated{\qty[ {1 \over N}\Tr\, \nu^*(z, z^*)]}_{\eta=0}

(15)

This sign change ensures that the domain where the spectral density is non-zero density occurs on the correct complement of the full Riemann sphere.

II
The Wigner Semi-circle Law

We start by using the Perturbative Resolvent Method (PRM) to derive the spectrum for the ensemble of real, symmetric random matrices
A
.
We assume that the entries of
A
, denoted
A_{ij}
(
i,j=1,\ldots,N
), are independent (up to symmetry) with mean and variance of the form

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{A_{ij}}=0,\quad\expectationvalue{A_{ij}A_{kl}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}).

(16)

Wigner showed that the spectral density of this ensemble is described by the semi-circle law shown in Fig.
1

Wigner 1993
. More explicitly, in thermodynamic
limit the spectral density is just

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{4\sigma^{2}-x^{2}}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\absolutevalue{x}\leq 2\sigma\\
0&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\absolutevalue{x}>2\sigma.\end{array}\right.

. The spectrum of a
N\times N
symmetric random matrix
A
whose entries are
i.i.d.
(up to symmetry)
with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}/N
for
\sigma^{2}=1
and
N=1000
with 100 independent realizations [see Eq. (
16
)]. The solid black line shows analytic predictions of the
Wigner semi-circle law which is exact in the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow\infty
.

II.1
Resolvent Equations

To calculate this spectral density using the PRM,
we first construct a system of equations that that will allow us to solve for the Green’s function
G_{A}(z)
of Eq. (
1
).
We propose the following system of
N
equations for
N
unknown variables
u_{i}
:

\displaystyle zu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}u_{j}+a_{i}.

(19)

We have also introduced the constant
z
along with
N
constant auxiliary variables
a_{i}
.

To see how this system of equation encodes
G_{A}(z)
,
we ask how the solutions change if we add a small, independent perturbation
\delta a_{i}
to each of the
a_{i}
.
In the PRM, the response to
these perturbations is characterized by the
N\times N
“susceptibility matrix”

\displaystyle\nu_{ij}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{u_i}{a_j}.

(20)

It is straightforward to show that this susceptibility matrix is equivalent to
G_{A}(z)
.

To see this, we take the derivative of Eq. (
19
) with respect to
a_{k}
to get

\displaystyle z\nu_{ik}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\nu_{jk}+\delta_{ik}

(21)

where
\delta_{ik}
is just the Kronecker-Delta function.
In matrix form, this set of equations can be written as

\displaystyle(zI-A)\nu(z)

\displaystyle=I

(22)

where
I
is the
N\times N
identity matrix.
Equivalently, we can write

\displaystyle\nu(z)=\frac{1}{zI-A}

(23)

which we see is identical to Eq. (
1
),
showing that the Green’s function
G_{A}(z)
is exactly the susceptibility of the linear equations above.
In particular, we are interested in the trace of the susceptibility

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nu_{jj}(z)

(24)

which allows us to calculate the spectral density using Eq. (
3
).

II.2
Cavity Expansion

To calculate the susceptibility, we assume replica symmetry and make use of the zero-temperature cavity method.
In the cavity method, one relates a system of
N
equations of the
N
variables
u_{i}
to a system with
N+1
equations and
N+1
variables.
By convention, we denote the additional variable by
u_{0}
and the additional rows and columns of the matrix
A
by
A_{0i}
and
A_{0j}
, respectively.
In the presence of these new variables, Eq. (
19
) becomes

\displaystyle zu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}A_{ij}u_{j}+a_{i}+A_{i0}u_{0}

(25)

(Note: indices are implied to range from
1
to
N
and terms with
0
-valued indices always be specified explicitly).

From Eq. (
16
), we know that the extra terms
A_{i0}u_{0}
scale as
1/N
.
Therefore, we treat these extra terms as small perturbations to the original equaitons via the auxiliary variables
a_{i}
,

\displaystyle\delta a_{i}=A_{i0}u_{0},

(26)

and treat them using perturbation theory.
We also know that the solutions
u_{i}
to Eq. (
25
) must be related to the solutions
u_{i\setminus 0}
of Eq. (
19
) (i.e., without the new variable
u_{0}
) by the perturbative relation

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{i}&\approx u_{i\setminus 0}+\sum_{j}\nu_{ij}\delta a_{j}\\
&=u_{i\setminus 0}+u_{0}\sum_{j}\nu_{ij}A_{j0}\end{split}

(27)

where we have made use of the definition of the susceptibility [Eq. (
20
)].

Now let us turn to additional equation corresponding to the new row in the matrix:

\displaystyle zu_{0}=\sum_{j}A_{0j}u_{j}+a_{0}+A_{00}u_{0}

(28)

Substituting the perturbative expansion above, we get

\displaystyle zu_{0}=\sum_{j}A_{0j}u_{j\setminus 0}+u_{0}\sum_{jk}\nu_{ij}A_{j0}A_{k0}+a_{0}+A_{00}u_{0}

(29)

II.3
Approximation via Central Limit Theorem

In the next step in the PRM, we approximate the sum in Eq. (
29
) that includes the susceptibility matrix.
In this sum, the elements of the susceptibility matrix are statistically independent of the the new elements of
A
(those with at least one
0
-valued index).
In addition, this sum includes a very large (order
N
) number of statistically independent terms.
Due to these two properties, it is straightforward to show using the central limit theorem that this sum will be dominated by its mean with respect to the new elements of
A
.
Performing this average, we find that the sum takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{jk}\nu_{ij}A_{j0}A_{k0}&\approx\sum_{jk}\nu_{ij}\expectationvalue{A_{j0}A_{k0}}\\
&=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\sum_{jk}\nu_{ij}\delta_{ij}\\
&=\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}\end{split}

(30)

where
\bar{\nu}
is the trace of the susceptibliity defined in Eq. (
24
).

II.4
Self-Consistency Equation for Susceptibility

Applying the approximation from the previous section we rewrite Eq. (
29
) as

\displaystyle zu_{0}\approx\sum_{j}A_{0j}u_{j\setminus 0}+u_{0}\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}+a_{0}

(31)

where we have also dropped the term
A_{00}u_{0}
because it is small (order
1/N
).
Rearranging we find the following expression for
u_{0}
:

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j}A_{0j}u_{j\setminus 0}+a_{0}}{1-\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}}.

(32)

Thus,
u_{0}
is a Gaussian random variable. By definition, we know that

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\nu_{00}}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\pdv{u_0}{a_0}}=\frac{1}{1-\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}}.

(33)

However, since there is nothing special about
u_{0}
(i.e., the system self-averages) ,
it is evident that

\displaystyle\bar{v}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\nu_{jj}\approx\expectationvalue{\nu_{00}}.

(34)

This gives us a self-consistency equation for
\bar{v}
of the form

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}}

(35)

or equivalently, the quadratic equation

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}-z\bar{\nu}+1

\displaystyle=0.

(36)

II.5
Spectral Density via Resolvent

Using the quadratic formula, we get

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{z\pm\sqrt{z^{2}-4\sigma^{2}}}{2\sigma^{2}}.

(37)

To extract the spectrum, we make use of Eq. (
3
) to relate the spectral density to the imaginary part of the Green’s function.
After substituting
z=x+i0^{+}
, it is easy to convince oneself that the only way to get imaginary numbers here is to have
z=x
with
\absolutevalue{x}\leq 2\sigma
. This yields the expression

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{4\sigma^{2}-x^{2}}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\absolutevalue{x}\leq 2\sigma\\
0&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\absolutevalue{x}>2\sigma\end{array}\right.

which is simply Wigner’s semi-circle law.

III
Marchenko-Pastur Distribution

We now use the PRM to derive the Marchenko-Pastur distribution. As before, we define
a linear system of equations whose susceptibilities are related to the relevant Green’s function. We will be interested in the spectral density of the ensemble of
the
M\times M
Wishart matrices
A=CC^{T}
where
C
is an
M\times N
matrix with entries
C_{i\alpha}
(
i=1,\ldots,M
and
\alpha=1,\ldots,N
) which are drawn from a normal distribution with
mean and variance given by

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{C_{i\alpha}}=0,\quad\expectationvalue{C_{i\alpha}C_{j\beta}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\delta_{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta}

(40)

Furthermore, we define the ratio

\gamma={M\over N}.

(41)

We will be interested in the limit
M,N\rightarrow\infty
with
\gamma
fixed. A well known result in RMT is that the spectrum of such Wishart matrices is given by the Marchenko-Pastur distribution
Marchenko and Pastur 1967
(see Fig.
2
).
We re-derive this result using the PRM.

III.1
Resolvent Equations

To apply the PRM, we start with the same system of equations used in the in the previous section to compute the Wigner semi-circle law, Eq. (
19
).
Inserting the definition of the Wishart matrix
A=CC^{T}
,
we get

\displaystyle zu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\alpha}C_{i\alpha}C_{j\alpha}u_{j}+a_{i}

(42)

where
u_{i}
are a set of
N
unknown variables,
z
is a constant, and
a_{i}
are a set of
N
of constant auxiliary variables (Note: indices represented by Roman letters are implied to range from
1
to
N
, while indices represented by Greek letters range from
1
to
M
).
Following the derivation in Sec.
II.1
,
it is clear that the susceptibility matrix

\displaystyle\nu_{ij}^{(u)}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{u_i}{a_j}

(43)

is equivalent to the Green’s function
G_{A}(z)
.

However, to apply the PRM, we require a system of equations that that is linear in the elements of the constituent matrix
C
.
Such a system of equations is found by rewriting Eq. (
42
) in terms of an extra set of
M
unknown variables
v_{\alpha}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{i}&=\sum_{\alpha}C_{i\alpha}v_{\alpha}+a_{i}\\
v_{\alpha}&=\sum_{j}C_{j\alpha}u_{j}+b_{\alpha}\end{split}

(44)

where we have introduced an extra set of
M
constant auxiliary variables
b_{\alpha}
to complement those in the first equation.
We note that this system of equations is unique as the right hand side of the first equation must depend on the index
i
and thus the sum must range over the index
\alpha
.
We note that as long as a matrix can be written as a product of matrices such a system of equations can always be uniquely defined (see Sec.
IV
for an example for a product of four matrices).

As before, we will be interested in small perturbations around the solutions to these equations.
Therefore, we must consider an expanded set of susceptibilities,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu_{ij}^{(u)}&=\partialderivative{u_i}{a_j},\quad\nu_{\alpha j}^{(v)}=\partialderivative{v_\alpha}{a_j},\\
\chi_{i\beta}^{(u)}&=\partialderivative{u_i}{b_\beta},\quad\chi_{\alpha\beta}^{(v)}=\partialderivative{v_\alpha}{b_\beta},\end{split}

(45)

that measure the extent to which
u_{i}
and
v_{\alpha}
change in response to a small perturbations in the constants
a_{i}
and
b_{\alpha}
.

Just as
\nu_{ij}^{(u)}
is the Green’s function, the other susceptibility matrices can also be expressed in terms of
z
and
C
.
Differentiating this system of equations with respect to the auxiliary variables
a_{j}
and
b_{\alpha}
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}z\nu^{(u)}_{ik}&=\sum_{\alpha}C_{i\alpha}\nu^{(n)}_{\alpha k}+\delta_{ik}\\
z\chi^{(u)}_{i\beta}&=\sum_{\alpha}C_{i\alpha}\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\beta}\\
\nu^{(v)}_{\alpha k}&=\sum_{j}C_{j\alpha}\nu^{(u)}_{jk}\\
\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\beta}&=\sum_{j}C_{j\alpha}\chi^{(u)}_{j\beta}+\delta_{\alpha\beta}\end{split}

(46)

which we can rewrite in matrix form as

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix zI_N & -C\\
-C^T & I_M\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\nu^{(u)} & \chi^{(u)}\\
\nu^{(v)} & \chi^{(v)}\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix I_N & 0 \\0 & I_M\endlx@physics@matrix)

(47)

where
I_{N}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix and
I_{M}
is the
M\times M
identity matrix.
Inverting this equation yields

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\nu^{(u)} & \chi^{(u)}\\
\nu^{(v)} & \chi^{(v)}\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix zI_N & -C\\
-C^T & I_M\endlx@physics@matrix)^{-1}

(48)

We then use standard formulas for inverting block matrices
Lu and Shiou 2002
to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu^{(u)}&=(zI_{N}-CC^{T})^{-1}=\frac{1}{z-A}\\
\chi^{(u)}&=(zI_{N}-CC^{T})^{-1}C\\
\nu^{(v)}&=C^{T}(zI_{N}-CC^{T})^{-1}\\
\chi^{(v)}&=I_{M}+C^{T}(zI_{N}-CC^{T})^{-1}C\end{split}

(49)

Comparing with Eq. (
1
) we see that
\nu^{(u)}
is exactly the resolvent of the random matrix
A
as expected.

III.2
Cavity Expansion

We once again calculate susceptibilities using the zero temperature cavity method. To do so, we introduce two new variables

u_{0}
and
v_{0}
, and a new row and column to the matrix
C
denoted by
C_{0\alpha}
and
C_{i0}
, respectively. In the presence of these new variables, the original
M+N
equations are modified to

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{i}&=\sum_{\alpha}C_{i\alpha}v_{\alpha}+a_{i}+C_{i0}v_{0}\\
v_{\alpha}&=\sum_{j}C_{j\alpha}u_{j}+b_{\alpha}+C_{0\alpha}u_{0}\end{split}

(50)

Next, we interpret the terms
C_{i0}v_{0}
and
C_{0\alpha}u_{0}
as small perturbations to the constants
a_{i}
and
b_{\alpha}
since the matrix elements of
C
scale
as
1/N
,

\displaystyle\delta a_{i}=C_{i0}v_{0},\quad\delta v_{\alpha}=C_{0\alpha}u_{0}.

(51)

We then perturbatively relate the solution to Eq. (
50
) in the presence of the new variables,
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
, to the solutions of
Eq. (
44
) without the new variables, denoted
u_{i\setminus 0}
and
v_{\alpha\setminus 0}
, using the definitions of the susceptibilities [Eq. (
45
)],

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{i}&\approx u_{i\setminus 0}+\sum_{j}\nu^{(u)}_{ij}C_{j0}v_{0}+\sum_{\beta}\chi^{(u)}_{i\beta}C_{0\beta}u_{0}\\
v_{\alpha}&\approx v_{\alpha\setminus 0}+\sum_{j}\nu^{(v)}_{\alpha j}C_{j0}v_{0}+\sum_{\beta}\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\beta}C_{0\beta}u_{0}.\end{split}

(52)

Now we consider the new equations for the two new variables,

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{0}&=\sum_{\alpha}C_{0\alpha}v_{\alpha}+a_{0}+C_{00}v_{0}\\
v_{0}&=\sum_{j}C_{j0}u_{j}+b_{0}+C_{00}u_{0}.\end{split}

(53)

Substituting the expansions into Eq.
53
gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{0}&=\sum_{\alpha}C_{0\alpha}v_{\alpha\setminus 0}+v_{0}\sum_{\alpha j}\nu^{(v)}_{\alpha j}C_{0\alpha}C_{j0}+u_{0}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\beta}C_{0\alpha}C_{0\beta}+a_{0}+C_{00}v_{0}\\
v_{0}&=\sum_{j}C_{j0}u_{j\setminus 0}+v_{0}\sum_{jk}\nu^{(u)}_{jk}C_{j0}C_{k0}+u_{0}\sum_{j\beta}\chi^{(u)}_{j\beta}C_{j0}C_{0\beta}+b_{0}+C_{00}u_{0}.\end{split}

(54)

III.3
Approximation via Central Limit Theorem

Similar to the approximation made in the derivation of the Wigner semi-circle law in Sec.
II.3
, we approximate each of the large sums involving the susceptibilities matrices using the Central Limit Theorem.
In particular, it is straightforward to show that the variance of each of these sums is small in the thermodynamic limit
N,M\rightarrow\infty
,
allowing us to approximate each with just its mean with respect to the new row an column of
C
(those elements with at least one
0
-valued index).
First, we approximate the the sums involving the square susceptibility matrices
\nu^{(u)}_{jk}
and
\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\beta}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{jk}\nu^{(u)}_{jk}C_{j0}C_{k0}&\approx\sum_{jk}\nu^{(u)}_{jk}\expectationvalue{C_{j0}C_{k0}}=\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}\\
\sum_{\alpha\beta}\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\beta}C_{0\alpha}C_{0\beta}&\approx\sum_{\alpha\beta}\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\beta}\expectationvalue{C_{0\alpha}C_{0\beta}}=\sigma^{2}\gamma\bar{\chi}\end{split}

(55)

where we have defined the traces to the susceptibilities as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\nu}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\nu^{(u)}_{jj}\\
\bar{\chi}&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\alpha}\chi^{(v)}_{\alpha\alpha}.\end{split}

(56)

Each term in the other two sums contains a pair of independent elements of
C
, resulting in zero mean,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\alpha j}\nu^{(v)}_{\alpha j}C_{0\alpha}C_{j0}&\approx\sum_{\alpha j}\nu^{(v)}_{\alpha j}\expectationvalue{C_{0\alpha}C_{j0}}=0\\
\sum_{j\beta}\chi^{(u)}_{j\beta}C_{j0}C_{0\beta}&\approx\sum_{j\beta}\chi^{(u)}_{j\beta}\expectationvalue{C_{j0}C_{0\beta}}=0.\end{split}

(57)

As a result only the two square susceptibility matrices are required for this calculation.

III.4
Self-Consistency Equations for Susceptibilities

Applying the approximations from the previous sections to Eq. (
54
) (and dropping the terms
C_{00}v_{0}
and
C_{00}u_{0}
since there scale as
1/N
) we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{0}&=\sum_{\alpha}C_{0\alpha}v_{\alpha\setminus 0}+u_{0}\sigma^{2}\gamma\bar{\chi}+a_{0}\\
v_{0}&=\sum_{j}C_{j0}u_{j\setminus 0}+v_{0}\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}+b_{0}\end{split}

(58)

which can be rearranged to yield

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{0}&=\frac{\sum_{\alpha}C_{0\alpha}v_{\alpha\setminus 0}+a_{0}}{z-\sigma^{2}\gamma\bar{\chi}}\\
v_{0}&=\frac{\sum_{j}C_{j0}u_{j\setminus 0}+b_{0}}{1-\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}}.\end{split}

(59)

From the cavity construction, we know that the traces of the susceptibility self-averaging, allowing us to write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\nu}&\approx\expectationvalue{\nu^{(u)}_{00}}=\expectationvalue{\pdv{u_0}{a_0}}=\frac{1}{z-\sigma^{2}\gamma\bar{\chi}}\\
\bar{\chi}&\approx\expectationvalue{\chi^{(v)}_{00}}=\expectationvalue{\pdv{v_0}{b_0}}=\frac{1}{1-\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}}.\end{split}

(60)

We combine these equations to get

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-\sigma^{2}\frac{1}{1-\gamma\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}}}

(61)

which gives us a quadratic equation for
\bar{\nu}
,

\displaystyle z\gamma\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}-\quantity[z + \sigma^2( \gamma-1)]\bar{\nu}+1=0.

(62)

III.5
Spectral Density via Resolvent

Solving the quadratic equation, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}(z)=\frac{z+\sigma^{2}(\gamma-1)}{2z\gamma\sigma^{2}}\pm\frac{\sqrt{\quantity[z + \sigma^2( \gamma-1)]^{2}-4z\gamma\sigma^{2}}}{2z\gamma\sigma^{2}}.

(63)

To extract the spectral density, we make use of Eq. (
3
). Notice that the imaginary part of the first term in the sum above for
z=x+i0^{+}

is simply

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}{1\over 2\pi}(1-\gamma^{-1}){\epsilon\over x^{2}+\epsilon^{2}},

(64)

which we recognize as the definition of delta function at zero. For this reason, this term accounts for the spectral weight of the zero eigenvalues and hence is non-zero only
if the Wishart matrix is not full rank (i.e.
\gamma>1
).

Now consider the second term in Eq. (
63
). In order to have a non-zero imaginary part of
\bar{\nu}
when
z=x+i0^{+}
, the discriminant must be negative.
This allows us to calculate the upper and lower bounds for where
\rho(x)
is non-zero by setting the discriminant in Eq. (
63
) to zero with
z=x
,

\displaystyle\quantity[x + \sigma^2( \gamma-1)]^{2}-4x\gamma\sigma^{2}=0,

(65)

yielding,

\displaystyle\begin{split}x_{\min}&=(\gamma+1)\sigma^{2}-2\sqrt{\gamma}\sigma^{2}\\
x_{\max}&=(\gamma+1)\sigma^{2}+2\sqrt{\gamma}\sigma^{2}.\end{split}

(66)

Combining this with Eq.(
63
), the contribution of this term to the spectral density is non-zero only on a finite region
[x_{\min},x_{\max}]
. Together,
these observations yield the following expression for the spectral density

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(1-\gamma^{-1})\delta(x)+\frac{1}{2\pi x\gamma\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{(x-x_{\min})(x_{\max}-x)},&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\gamma>1\\
\frac{1}{2\pi x\gamma\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{(x-x_{\min})(x_{\max}-x)},&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\gamma\leq 1.\end{array}\right.

This is exactly the Marchenko-Pastur distribution.

IV
Wishart Product Matrices

Next, we use the PRM to derive the spectrum for a special case of Wishart product matrices
Burda et al. 2010
;
Dupic and Castillo 2014
.
In the general case, these matrices take the form
A=CC^{T}
where
C=B_{1}B_{2}\times\cdots\times B_{n}
where each matrix
B_{i}
can be a rectangular matrix of a different size (such that the matrix multiplications are valid).
In this section, we consider the the spectral density of the ensemble of Wishart product matrices
A=CBB^{T}C^{T}
where
C
and
B
are both
N\times N
matrices whose entries,
B_{ij}
and
C_{ij}
, respectively, are independently drawn from normal distributions with mean and variances given by

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\expectationvalue{B_{ij}}=0,\quad\expectationvalue{B_{ij}B_{kl}}=\frac{\sigma_{B}^{2}}{N}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\\
\expectationvalue{C_{ij}}=0,\quad\expectationvalue{C_{ij}C_{kl}}=\frac{\sigma_{C}^{2}}{N}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\\
\expectationvalue{B_{ij}C_{kl}}=0.\end{gathered}

IV.1
Resolvent Equations

Following Sec.
II.1
, we begin by inserting the definition for
A
into Eq. (
19
),

\displaystyle zu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{jklm}C_{ij}B_{jk}B_{lk}C_{ml}u_{m}+a_{i}

(72)

where
u_{i}

(i=1,\cdots,N)
are a set of unknown variables,
z
is a constant, and
a_{i}
are a set of constant auxiliary variables.
Just as the previous section, it is clear that the Green’s function
G_{A}(z)
is given by the susceptibility matrix

\displaystyle\nu^{(u)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{u_i}{a_j}.

(73)

Next, we follow the setup described for product matrices in Sec.
III.1
, defining a new set of unknown variables,
v_{i}
,
w_{i}
, and
x_{i}

(i=1,\cdots,N)
, allowing us to decompose Eq. (
72
) such that each equation is linear in the elements of the random matrices
C
and
B
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{i}&=\sum_{j}C_{ij}v_{j}+a_{i}\\
v_{i}&=\sum_{j}B_{ij}w_{j}+b_{i}\\
w_{i}&=\sum_{j}B_{ji}x_{j}+c_{i}\\
x_{i}&=\sum_{j}C_{il}u_{i}+d_{i}.\end{split}

(74)

In each equation of the additional three equations, we have also introduced a new set of constant auxiliary variables,
b_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and
d_{i}
.

In order to explore small perturbations about the solutions to these equations, we define all possible susceptibility matrices with respect to the auxiliary variables,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu^{(u)}_{ij}&=\partialderivative{u_i}{a_j},\nu^{(v)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{v_i}{a_j},\nu^{(w)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{w_i}{a_j},\nu^{(x)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{x_i}{a_j}\\
\chi^{(u)}_{ij}&=\partialderivative{u_i}{b_j},\chi^{(v)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{v_i}{b_j},\chi^{(w)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{w_i}{b_j},\chi^{(x)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{x_i}{b_j}\\
\phi^{(u)}_{ij}&=\partialderivative{u_i}{c_j},\phi^{(v)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{v_i}{c_j},\phi^{(w)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{w_i}{c_j},\phi^{(x)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{x_i}{c_j}\\
\omega^{(u)}_{ij}&=\partialderivative{u_i}{d_j},\omega^{(v)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{v_i}{d_j},\omega^{(w)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{w_i}{d_j},\omega^{(x)}_{ij}=\partialderivative{x_i}{d_j}.\end{split}

(75)

As in the previous section, only a small subset of these susceptibilities will be relevant.

IV.2
Cavity Expansion

Next, we begin the zero-cavity method by introducing a four variables,
u_{0}
,
v_{0}
,
w_{0}
, and
x_{0}
, and a new row and column to each matrices denoted by
C_{i0}
,
C_{0j}
,
B_{i0}
, and
B_{0j}
. In the presence of these new variables, the original
4N
equations [Eq. (
74
)] become

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{i}&=\sum_{j}C_{ij}v_{j}+a_{i}+C_{i0}v_{0}\\
v_{i}&=\sum_{j}B_{ij}w_{j}+b_{i}+B_{i0}w_{0}\\
w_{i}&=\sum_{j}B_{ji}x_{j}+c_{i}+B_{0i}x_{0}\\
x_{i}&=\sum_{j}C_{ji}u_{j}+d_{i}+C_{0i}u_{0}.\end{split}

(76)

From here, we interpret the extra terms as small (order
1/N
) perturbations to the auxiliary variables,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta a_{i}=C_{i0}v_{0},\quad\delta b_{i}=B_{i0}w_{0}\\
\delta c_{i}=B_{0i}x_{0},\quad\delta d_{i}=C_{0i}u_{0},\end{split}

(77)

allowing us to perturbatively relate the solutions of these equations with the new variables to those of Eq. (
74
), denoted
u_{i\setminus 0}
,
v_{i\setminus 0}
,
w_{i\setminus 0}
,
x_{i\setminus 0}
. This gives us

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{i}&\approx u_{i\setminus 0}+\sum_{j}\nu^{(u)}_{ij}C_{i0}v_{0}+\sum_{j}\chi^{(u)}_{ij}B_{j0}w_{0}+\sum_{j}\phi^{(u)}_{ij}B_{0j}x_{0}+\sum_{j}\omega^{(u)}_{ij}C_{0j}u_{0}\\
v_{i}&\approx v_{i\setminus 0}+\sum_{j}\nu^{(v)}_{ij}C_{i0}v_{0}+\sum_{j}\chi^{(v)}_{ij}B_{j0}w_{0}+\sum_{j}\phi^{(v)}_{ij}B_{0j}x_{0}+\sum_{j}\omega^{(v)}_{ij}C_{0j}u_{0}\\
w_{i}&\approx w_{i\setminus 0}+\sum_{j}\nu^{(w)}_{ij}C_{i0}v_{0}+\sum_{j}\chi^{(w)}_{ij}B_{j0}w_{0}+\sum_{j}\phi^{(w)}_{ij}B_{0j}x_{0}+\sum_{j}\omega^{(w)}_{ij}C_{0j}u_{0}\\
x_{i}&\approx x_{i\setminus 0}+\sum_{j}\nu^{(x)}_{ij}C_{i0}v_{0}+\sum_{j}\chi^{(x)}_{ij}B_{j0}w_{0}+\sum_{j}\phi^{(x)}_{ij}B_{0j}x_{0}+\sum_{j}\omega^{(x)}_{ij}C_{0j}u_{0}.\end{split}

(78)

Next, we will consider the additional equations for the the new variables, given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{0}&=\sum_{j}C_{0j}v_{j}+a_{0}+C_{00}v_{0}\\
v_{0}&=\sum_{j}B_{0j}w_{j}+b_{0}+B_{00}w_{0}\\
w_{0}&=\sum_{j}B_{j0}x_{j}+c_{0}+B_{00}x_{0}\\
x_{0}&=\sum_{j}C_{j0}u_{j}+d_{0}+C_{00}u_{0}.\end{split}

(79)

IV.3
Approximation via Central Limit Theorem

Following the procedure described in the previous sections,
we substitute the expansions in Eq. (
78
) into the equations for the new variables in Eq. (
79
).
The resulting set of equations contains many sums over large numbers of random variables.
In particular, for each sum containing one of the susceptibility matrices, we make use of the fact that the susceptibilities are statistically independent of the new rows and columns of
C
and
B
.
Using the central limit theorem, each sum can be shown to be dominated by its mean.
Four of these sums can be shown to be nonzero, following the same form as those in Eq. (
55
),

\displaystyle\sum_{jk}\nu^{(u)}_{jk}C_{j0}C_{k0}

\displaystyle\approx\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}

(80)

\displaystyle\sum_{jk}\omega^{(v)}C_{0j}C_{0k}

\displaystyle\approx\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\omega}

(81)

\displaystyle\sum_{jk}\phi^{(w)}B_{0j}B_{0k}

\displaystyle\approx\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\phi}

(82)

\displaystyle\sum_{jk}\chi^{(x)}_{ij}B_{j0}B_{k0}

\displaystyle\approx\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\chi}

(83)

where we have defined the traces of the susceptibilities matrices as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\nu}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\nu^{(u)}_{jj},\quad\bar{\chi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\chi^{(x)}_{jj}\\
\bar{\phi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\phi^{(w)}_{jj},\quad\bar{\omega}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\omega^{(v)}_{jj}.\end{split}

(84)

It can easily be shown that the remaining twelve sums containing one of the susceptibilities are all similar in form to those in Eq. (
57
) and are therefore approximately zero in the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow\infty
.

IV.4
Self-Consistency Equations for Susceptibilities

Applying the approximations from the previous section and neglecting the additional terms of order
1/N
, Eq. (
79
) becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}zu_{0}&\approx\sum_{j}C_{0j}v_{j\setminus 0}+u_{0}\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\omega}+a_{0}\\
v_{0}&\approx\sum_{j}B_{0j}w_{j\setminus 0}+x_{0}\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\phi}+b_{0}\\
w_{0}&\approx\sum_{j}B_{j0}x_{j\setminus 0}+w_{0}\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\chi}+c_{0}\\
x_{0}&\approx\sum_{j}C_{j0}u_{j\setminus 0}+v_{0}\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}+d_{0}.\end{split}

(85)

Solving these equations for the new variables, we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{0}&=\frac{\sum_{j}C_{0j}v_{j\setminus 0}+a_{0}}{z-\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\omega}}\\
v_{0}&=\frac{\sum_{j}B_{0j}w_{j\setminus 0}+b_{0}+\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\phi}\quantity(\sum_jC_{j0}u_{j\setminus 0} + d_0)}{1-\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}\bar{\phi}}\\
w_{0}&=\frac{\sum_{j}B_{j0}x_{j\setminus 0}+c_{0}}{1-\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\chi}}\\
x_{0}&=\frac{\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}\quantity(\sum_jB_{0j}w_{j\setminus 0} + b_0)+\sum_{j}C_{j0}u_{j\setminus 0}+d_{0}}{1-\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}\bar{\phi}}.\end{split}

(86)

Next, we approximate the traces of the susceptibility matrices as the average over a single element, giving us a set of self-consistent equations

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\bar{\nu}}&\approx\expectationvalue{\nu^{(u)}_{00}}=\expectationvalue{\pdv{u_0}{a_0}}=\frac{1}{z-\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\omega}}\\
\bar{\chi}&\approx\expectationvalue{\chi^{(x)}_{00}}=\expectationvalue{\pdv{x_0}{b_0}}=\frac{\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}}{1-\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}\bar{\phi}}\\
\bar{\phi}&\approx\expectationvalue{\phi^{(w)}_{00}}=\expectationvalue{\pdv{w_0}{c_0}}=\frac{1}{1-\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\chi}}\\
\bar{\omega}&\approx\expectationvalue{\bar{\omega}^{(v)}_{00}}=\expectationvalue{\pdv{v_0}{d_0}}=\frac{\sigma_{B}^{2}\bar{\phi}}{1-\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}\bar{\nu}\bar{\phi}}.\end{split}

(87)

Combining these equations, we arrive at a cubic equation for
\bar{\nu}
of the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}z^{2}\bar{\nu}^{3}-z\bar{\nu}+1

\displaystyle=0.

(88)

IV.5
Spectral Density via Resolvent

To solve this cubic equation, we first rewrite it in terms of
z\bar{\nu}
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(z\bar{\nu})^{3}-\frac{z}{\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}}(z\bar{\nu})+\frac{z}{\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}},

(89)

allowing to express the three solutions in general form as

\displaystyle\begin{split}z\bar{\nu}^{(1)}&=S+T\\
z\bar{\nu}^{(2)}&=-\frac{1}{2}(S+T)+\frac{1}{2}i\sqrt{3}(S-T)\\
z\bar{\nu}^{(2)}&=-\frac{1}{2}(S+T)-\frac{1}{2}i\sqrt{3}(S-T)\end{split}

(90)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}S&=\sqrt[3]{R+\sqrt{D}}\\
T&=\sqrt[3]{R-\sqrt{D}}\\
D&=R^{2}-Q^{3}\end{split}

(91)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q&=\frac{1}{3}\frac{z}{\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}}\\
R&=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{z}{\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}}.\end{split}

(92)

In writing these expressions, we have made us of standard mathematical identities for the roots of a cubic equation.

We then make use of Eq. (
3
) to extract the spectral density.
After substituting
z=x+i0^{+}
, it is possible to convince oneself that spectral density is given by the imaginary part of solution
\bar{\nu}^{(2)}
when the polynomial discriminant
D
is positive.
To solve for the the bounds of the distribution, we solve for the roots of
D
after setting
z=x
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}D&=\frac{1}{4}\quantity(\frac{x}{\sigma_B^2\sigma_C^2})^{2}-\frac{1}{27}\quantity(\frac{x}{\sigma_B^2\sigma_C^2})^{3}=0.\end{split}

(93)

The limiting eigenvalues are then

\displaystyle\begin{split}x_{\min}&=0\\
x_{\max}&=\frac{27}{4}\sigma_{B}^{2}\sigma_{C}^{2}.\end{split}

(94)

Finally, the spectral density is given by

\displaystyle\rho(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi x}\quantity[S_+\qty(\frac{x}{\sigma_B^2\sigma_C^2}) - S_-\qty(\frac{x}{\sigma_B^2\sigma_C^2})]

(95)

where

\displaystyle S_{\pm}(a)

\displaystyle=\sqrt[3]{\frac{1}{2}a\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{27}a^{2}\quantity(\frac{27}{4}-a)}}.

(96)

Numerical checks of these expression is show in Figure
3
.

\zeta=0

and more generally,

(see Fig.

V.1
Resolvent Equations

We now derive both these spectral densities using the PRM. Inspired by Eq. (
12
), we consider the
2N\times 2N
dimensional matrix

\displaystyle H(z)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0 & A-zI \\
z^*I-A^* & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)

(98)

where
z
is a constant and
I
is the
N\times N
identity matrix.
In analogy to Eq. (
19
), we construct a system of
2N
real equations for the variables
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots N
) of the form

\displaystyle\eta\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\vec{x} \\
\vec{y}\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle=H(z)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\vec{x} \\
\vec{y}\endlx@physics@matrix)+\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\vec{a} \\
\vec{b}\endlx@physics@matrix)

(99)

where
\eta
is a constant and
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are
2N
real-valued auxiliary variables.
We rewrite these equations in component form as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta x_{i}&=\sum_{j}A_{ij}y_{j}-zy_{i}+a_{i}\\
\eta y_{i}&=-\sum_{j}A^{*}_{ij}x_{j}+z^{*}x_{i}+b_{i}.\end{split}

(100)

As before, we define a set of susceptibilities,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi_{ij}^{(x)}&=\partialderivative{x_i}{a_j},\quad\nu_{ij}^{(y)}=\partialderivative{y_i}{a_j},\\
\nu_{ij}^{(x)}&=\partialderivative{x_i}{b_j},\quad\chi_{ij}^{(y)}=\partialderivative{y_i}{b_j},\end{split}

(101)

that measure how
x_{i}
and
y_{j}
change in response to a small changes in the constants
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
.
Taking the derivative of Eq. (
100
) with respect to
b_{k}
and
a_{k}
gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta\chi_{ik}^{(x)}&=\sum_{j}A_{ij}\nu_{jk}^{(y)}-z\nu_{ik}^{(y)}+\delta_{ik}\\
\eta\nu_{ik}^{(x)}&=\sum_{j}A_{ij}\chi_{jk}^{(y)}-z\chi_{ik}^{(y)}\\
\eta\nu_{ik}^{(y)}&=-\sum_{j}A^{*}_{ij}\chi_{jk}^{(x)}+z^{*}\chi_{ik}^{(x)}\\
\eta\chi_{ik}^{(y)}&=-\sum_{j}A^{*}_{ij}\nu_{jk}^{(x)}+z^{*}\nu_{ik}^{(x)}+\delta_{ik}.\end{split}

(102)

We then rewrite this in matrix form as

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\eta I & zI-A\\
-z^*I+A^* & \eta I \endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\chi^{(x)} & \nu^{(x)}\\
\nu^{(y)} & \chi^{(y)}\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix I & 0 \\ 0 & I\endlx@physics@matrix)

(103)

Notice that the equations for the susceptibilities do not depend on the value of
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
. This implies that they are independent of the exact values of
a_{i}
and
b_{i}

and should be valid even for special case where all the constants are chosen to be identical,
a_{i}=b_{i}=c
for all
i
.
In this case, there
is a hidden symmetry in our system of linear equations, Eq. (
100
), implying that the four susceptibilities are not independent, but instead
related by complex conjugation. In light of these observations, it is easy to convince oneself that we must have

\chi^{(x)}=\chi^{(y)}=\chi
,
\nu^{(x)}=\nu
and
\nu^{(y)}=-\nu^{*}
where
\nu^{*}
denotes the Hermitian conjugate of
\nu
.
This implies that we can rewrite our equations in the simpler form

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\eta I & z I-A\\
-z^* I +A^* & \eta I \endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\chi& \nu\\
-\nu^* & \chi\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix I & 0 \\ 0 & I\endlx@physics@matrix).

(104)

Inverting this equation we see that the susceptibility matrices correspond exactly to the complex Green’s function of Eq. (
13
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{A}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\chi& \nu\\
-\nu^* & \chi\endlx@physics@matrix).

(105)

We can use these expressions to calculate the spectral function of
A
using Eq. (
107
):

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-{1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}\evaluated{\qty[ {1 \over N}\Tr\, \nu^*(z, z^*)]}_{\eta=0}

(106)

\displaystyle={1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}\evaluated{\qty[ {1 \over N}\Tr\, \nu^{(y)}(z, z^*)]}_{\eta=0}

(107)

V.2
Susceptibilities via Cavity Method

Once again we calculate the susceptibilities using the cavity method.
To do so, we introduce two additional variables
x_{0}
and

y_{0}
and an additional row
A_{0j}
and column
A_{i0}
to the matrix
A
.
With the addition of these new variables, Eq.
100
for
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
is modified to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta x_{i}&=\sum_{j}A_{ij}y_{j}-zy_{i}+a_{i}+A_{i0}y_{0}\\
\eta y_{i}&=-\sum_{j}A^{*}_{ij}x_{j}+z^{*}x_{i}+b_{i}-A_{i0}^{*}x_{0}.\end{split}

(108)

As before, we treat the effect of these two extra variables as small perturbations since the additional terms in the
equation above scale as
1/N
.
In particular, we relate the solutions to Eq. (
108
) (denoted by
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
) to the solutions to Eq. (
108
) without the new variables
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
(denoted by
x_{i\setminus 0}
and
y_{i\setminus 0}
)
using the definition of the susceptibilities [Eq. (
101
)],

\displaystyle\begin{split}x_{j}&=x_{j\setminus 0}-\sum_{k}\nu_{jk}^{(x)}A_{k0}^{*}x_{0}+\sum_{k}\chi_{jk}^{(x)}yA_{k0}y_{0}\\
y_{j}&=y_{j\setminus 0}-\sum_{k}\chi_{jk}^{(y)}A_{k0}^{*}x_{0}+\sum_{k}\nu_{jk}^{(y)}A_{k0}y_{0},\end{split}

(109)

where in writing this we have made use of the relationships between the various susceptibilities noted above.

In the cavity method, there are also two additional equations for the new variables
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta x_{0}&=\sum_{j}A_{0j}y_{j}-zy_{0}+a_{0}+A_{00}y_{0}\\
\eta y_{0}&=-\sum_{j}A^{*}_{0j}x_{j}+z^{*}x_{0}+b_{0}-A_{00}^{*}x_{0}.\end{split}

(110)

Substituting in the expansions above gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta x_{0}&=\sum_{j}A_{0j}y_{j\setminus 0}-\sum_{jk}\chi_{jk}^{(y)}A_{0j}A_{k0}^{*}x_{0}+\sum_{jk}\nu^{(y)}_{jk}A_{0j}A_{k0}y_{0}-zy_{0}+a_{0}+A_{00}y_{0}\\
\eta y_{0}&=-\sum_{j}A^{*}_{0j}x_{j\setminus 0}+\sum_{jk}\nu_{jk}^{(x)}A^{*}_{0j}A_{k0}^{*}x_{0}-\sum_{jk}\chi_{jk}^{(x)}A^{*}_{0j}A_{k0}y_{0}+z^{*}x_{0}+b_{0}-A_{00}^{*}x_{0}.\end{split}

(111)

Next, we approximate each sum containing a susceptibility matrix with its mean (with respect to the new row and column of
A
) using the central limit theorem in the large
N
limit (see previous sections).
Using Eq. (
97
) and noting that
A^{*}_{ij}=A_{ji}
for real matrices and relationships between susceptibilities, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta x_{0}&=\sum_{j}A_{0j}y_{j\setminus 0}-x_{0}\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi}^{(y)}+y_{0}\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(y)}-zy_{0}+a_{0}\\
\eta y_{0}&=-\sum_{j}A_{j0}x_{j\setminus 0}+x_{0}\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(x)}-y_{0}\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi}^{(x)}+z^{*}x_{0}+b_{0},\end{split}

(112)

where the bar on the susceptibility denotes the trace. We are now in a position to solve for
\bar{\nu}^{(y)}
and calculate
the spectral density.

V.3
Circle Law

Let us first focus on the special case where the entries are completely decorrelated with
\zeta=0
. In this case, we know that the spectrum is described by the Girko circle law.
To derive this, we use Eq. (
112
) with
\zeta=0
(noting that
\bar{\chi}^{(x)}=\bar{\chi}^{(y)}=\bar{\chi}
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta x_{0}&=\sum_{j}A_{0j}y_{j\setminus 0}-x_{0}\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi}-zy_{0}+a_{0}\\
\eta y_{0}&=-\sum_{j}A_{j0}x_{j\setminus 0}-y_{0}\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi}+z^{*}x_{0}+b_{0}.\end{split}

(113)

We solve this equation for
x_{0}
to get

\displaystyle y_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\quantity(\eta+ \sigma^2\bar{\chi})\quantity(\sum_jA_{j0}x_{j \setminus 0} + b_0)+z^{*}\quantity(\sum_jA_{0j}y_{j \setminus 0} + a_0)}{|z|^{2}+(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})^{2}}

(114)

By definition of the susceptibilities and self-consistency of the mean field cavity equations, we know that

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\pdv{y_0}{b_0}}=\frac{(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})}{|z|^{2}+(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})^{2}},

(115)

and

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}^{(y)}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\pdv{y_0}{a_0}}=\frac{z^{*}}{|z|^{2}+(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})^{2}}.

(116)

Next, we examine the special case where
\eta=0
.
We further simplify this expression by exploiting the fact that Eq. (
115
) gives a cubic self-consistency equation for
\chi
.
When
\eta=0
, we get

\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{3}+\bar{\chi}(|z|^{2}-\sigma^{2})=0.

(117)

which has two solutions:

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}=0\mbox{\quad or\quad}\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}+|z|^{2}=\sigma^{2}.

(118)

Substituting the first solution
\bar{\chi}=0
into the formula for the spectral density gives

\rho_{A}(u,v)={1\over\pi}\evaluated{\partial_{z^*}\bar{\nu}^{(y)}}_{\eta=0}={1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}{z^{*}\over|z|^{2}+\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}}\\
={1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}{z^{*}\over|z|^{2}}={1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}{1\over z}=0

(119)

while the second solution yields

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(u,v)={1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}{z^{*}\over|z|^{2}+\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}}={1\over\pi}\partial_{z^{*}}{z^{*}\over\sigma^{2}}=\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2}}.

(120)

All together, we find

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{1\over\pi\sigma^{2}}&\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}+|z|^{2}=\sigma^{2}\\
0&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\bar{\chi}=0.\end{array}\right.

We would like translate this into a condition on
z
not
\bar{\chi}
. To do so, we note that

\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}+|z|^{2}=\sigma^{2}
implies that
|z|^{2}\leq|\sigma|^{2}
. Since the two solutions must match at
\chi=0
, this
implies that the density takes the form

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{1\over\pi\sigma^{2}}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}|z|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\\
0&\mbox{\quad if\quad}|z|^{2}\geq\sigma^{2},\end{array}\right.

which is precisely the circle law.

V.4
Elliptic Law

We now generalize this basic calculation to derive the Ellispe law for correlated matrices where
\zeta\neq 0
. We start once again
with Eq.
112
and solve for
y_{0}
to get

\displaystyle y_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{(z^{*}+\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu})\quantity(\sum_jA_{0j}y_{j \setminus 0} + a_0)+\quantity(\eta+ \sigma^2\bar{\chi})\quantity(\sum_jA_{j0}x_{j \setminus 0} + b_0)}{|(z+\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{*})|^{2}+(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})^{2}}.

(125)

We then use the usual cavity arguments to write

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\pdv{y_0}{b_0}}=\frac{(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})}{|(z+\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{*})|^{2}+(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})^{2}}

(126)

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}^{(y)}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\pdv{y_0}{a_0}}=\frac{z^{*}+\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}}{|(z+\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{*})|^{2}+(\eta+\sigma^{2}\bar{\chi})^{2}}.

(127)

To calculate the density, once again we will make use of Eq. (
107
). For
\eta=0
, notice the equation for
\bar{\chi}
has two
solutions

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}=0\mbox{\quad or\quad}|(z-\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{*})|^{2}+\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}=\sigma^{2}.

(128)

Plugging this into the expressions above yields (using the identity
\bar{\nu}^{*}=-\bar{\nu}^{(y)}
)

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}^{(y)}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{1\over(z-\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(y)})}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}\chi=0\\
\frac{z^{*}-\zeta\sigma^{2}(\bar{\nu}^{(y)})^{*}}{\sigma^{2}}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}|z-\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(y)}|^{2}+\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}=\sigma^{2},\end{array}\right.

When
\chi=0
, the top expression in the equation above implies that
\bar{\nu}^{(y)}
is an analytic function of
z
and does
not depend on
z^{*}
. Hence, from Eq. (
107
) we conclude
that when
\chi=0
that
\rho_{A}(u,v)=0
. When
|z-\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(y)}|^{2}+\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
,
we can easily solve for
\bar{\nu}^{(y)}
by combining the bottom equation with its complex conjugate to get

\bar{\nu}^{(y)}=\frac{z^{*}-\zeta z}{\sigma^{2}(1-\zeta^{2})}

(131)

Using Eq. (
107
), we conclude that for this solution the density is then given by

\rho_{A}(x,y)={1\over\pi\sigma^{2}(1-\zeta^{2})}
. Summarizing, we find that

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{1\over\sigma^{2}\pi(1-\zeta^{2})}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}|z-\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(y)}|^{2}+\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}=\sigma^{2}\\
\ 0&\mbox{\quad otherwise\quad}\end{array}\right.

Notice that
|z-\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(y)}|^{2}+\sigma^{4}\bar{\chi}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
implies that
|z-\zeta\sigma^{2}\bar{\nu}^{(y)}|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
.
Plugging in the explicit expression for
\bar{\nu}^{(y)}
into this inequality and writing
z=u+iv
allows us to rewrite this inequality in terms of
u
and

v
as

{u^{2}\over(1+\zeta)^{2}}+{v^{2}\over(1-\zeta)^{2}}\leq\sigma^{2}

(134)

This is the precisely the elliptic law first derived by Ginbre and Girko
Girko 1986
.
To summarize, for a general
\zeta
, the spectral
density is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{1\over\sigma^{2}\pi(1-\zeta^{2})}&\mbox{\quad if\quad}{u^{2}\over(1+\zeta)^{2}}+{v^{2}\over(1-\zeta)^{2}}\leq\sigma^{2}\\
\ 0&\mbox{\quad otherwise\quad}\end{array}\right.

When
\zeta=0
, these reduces to the circle law derived above.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper we present a simple, perturbative approach for calculating spectral densities for a variety of random matrix ensembles
in the thermodynamic limit we call the Perturbative Resolvent Method (PRM). The key idea of the PRM is to construct a system of random
linear equations and ask how the solution to these equations change in response to slight perturbations. The key mathematical
quantity that we make use of are appropriately defined susceptibilities which we show are directly related to Green’s functions that can be
used to calculate the spectral densities.

We have illustrated the generality and power of the method by providing simple derivations of the
Wigner Semi-circle Law for symmetric matrices, the Marchenko-Pastur Law for Wishart matrices, the spectral density of a simple product Wishart matrix,
and the Circle and elliptic laws
for real random matrices. In general, we expect the PRM will be useful for relating approaches based on the zero-temperature cavity method to
results in RMT. For example, we have found the PRM can offer a new perspective for understanding problems in
ecology
Advani et al. 2018
;
Cui et al. 2020
;
Cui et al. 2019
, machine learning

Rocks and Mehta 2020
, and statistical inference problems such as compressed sensing
Ramezanali et al. 2015
where zero-temperature
cavity calculations have been used to identify phase transitions. More generally, we hope that the PRM can serve as a useful method for analyzing
disordered systems and random matrices.
