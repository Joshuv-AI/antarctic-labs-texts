Relativity for games
Daiju Nakayama and Kin-ya Oda

Abstract
We present how to implement special relativity in computer games. The resultant relativistic world shows the time dilation and Lorentz contraction exactly, not only for the player but also for all the nonplayer characters, who obey the correct relativistic equation of motion according to their own accelerations. Causality is explicitly maintained in our formulation by use of the covariant velocities, proper times, worldlines, and light cones. Faraway relativistic scenes can be accurately projected onto the skydome. We show how to approximate a rigid body consisting of polygons, which is ubiquitous in computer games but itself is not a relativistically invariant object. We also give a simple idea to mimic the Doppler effect within the RGB color scheme.

3.4
Short summary

At each moment, we draw all the objects as if each of them is placed at the spatial position

4
Time evolution

We show how to take into account the relativistic time evolution of the system, with an application to a first-person shooter (FPS) in mind.
As reviewed above, a game in Newtonian mechanics draws the world at each
equal-time slice
. One problem is that such a time slice is not Lorentz invariant and is not compatible with relativity. Instead we employ the foliation of spacetime by the PLCs of the player, which is Lorentz invariant, as reviewed above. A schematic diagram is shown in Fig.
4
.

4.1
Player’s time evolution

Schematic diagram in

What should the time evolution be?
At each time step, the future-most surface of the world that is perceivable by the player is
\PLC\!\left(\overrightarrow{x_{\text{P}}}\right)
, on which the particle’s latest position
\overrightarrow{x}

\Delta\tau_{\text{P}}

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{P}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\tau_{\text{P}}+\Delta\tau_{\text{P}},

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{x_{\text{P}}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\overrightarrow{x_{\text{P}}}+\overrightarrow{u_{\text{P}}}\,\Delta\tau_{\text{P}},

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{u_{\text{P}}}

We update the values of the player’s proper time, position, and velocity as in Eq. (

Now the player’s new position

5.3
Approximately rigid body

In the relativistic world, it is impossible to have an exactly rigid body, as it violates the locality.
However, a thing is usually introduced as a rigid body, described by a set of polygons, in computer games.
We show how to treat such a thing approximately.

Suppose that a body

N

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a}

\displaystyle=\left(\xi_{a}^{1},\dots,\xi_{a}^{d}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{a}^{1}\\

\overrightarrow{x_{\texttt{t}}}

\Set{\overrightarrow{x_{1}},\overrightarrow{x_{2}},\dots}

35

35

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{X}_{\texttt{t}}+R_{\texttt{t}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{a},

\displaystyle X_{a,\texttt{t}}^{0}

\displaystyle=X_{\text{P}}^{0}-\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{a,\texttt{t}}-\boldsymbol{X}_{\text{P}}\right|,

5.4
Doppler effect

Light that comes into our eyes has a spectrum; namely, its intensity
I
is a function of the angular frequency
\omega
:

\displaystyle I\!\left(\omega\right),

(139)

where
\omega
is related to the frequency

\lambda

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=2\pi f={2\pi c\over\lambda}.

(140)

Here and hereafter, we recover

from the natural units in the expressions involving
\omega

When light ray points in a direction
\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}
, where
\left|\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}\right|=1

vector
36
36

36

In an nonrelativistic context, the
d

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{k}

\displaystyle:=\omega\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{\hat{n}}\end{bmatrix}

D
momentum and that
\overrightarrow{k}
transforms as a
D

Suppose that a source
S
and an observer

have their velocities
\overrightarrow{u_{S}}
and
\overrightarrow{u_{\text{O}}}
, respectively, in the reference frame.
Let us consider light of an angular frequency
\Omega
pointing towards
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
with
\left|\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\right|=1

S

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{K}

\displaystyle=\Omega\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\end{bmatrix}.

(142)

\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
can be obtained as follows: When
O
at
\overrightarrow{x_{\text{O}}}

\overrightarrow{x_{S}}

\overrightarrow{x_{S}}

\displaystyle\left[\overrightarrow{x_{\text{O}}}-\overrightarrow{x_{S}}\right]^{2}=0

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{X_{OS}}:=L\!\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{S}\right)\left(\overrightarrow{x_{\text{O}}}-\overrightarrow{x_{S}}\right).

Then we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{X}_{OS}\over X_{OS}^{0}}.

(145)

The light (

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{K^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=L\!\left(\boldsymbol{u_{\text{O}}}\right)L\!\left(-\boldsymbol{u}_{S}\right)\overrightarrow{K}.

\displaystyle I_{\text{O}}\!\left(\Omega\right)

\displaystyle=I\!\left(\Omega/C\right).

(148)

This is all needed to determine the Doppler effect on light.

Most fundamentally, one should consult the above procedure.
In practice, it does not work. Why?
In the RGB color model used in computers, a color is specified by three numbers
\left(R,G,B\right)
that roughly represent the perception of three types of cone cells in our eyes.
For a given spectrum
I\!\left(\omega\right)
, the values of

,
G
,
B
are obtained, up to an overall normalization of
\left(R,G,B\right)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\text{min}}}^{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\text{d}\lambda\,\overline{r}\!\left(\lambda\right)I\!\left(2\pi c\over\lambda\right),

(149)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\text{min}}}^{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\text{d}\lambda\,\overline{g}\!\left(\lambda\right)I\!\left(2\pi c\over\lambda\right),

(150)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\text{min}}}^{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\text{d}\lambda\,\overline{b}\!\left(\lambda\right)I\!\left(2\pi c\over\lambda\right),

(151)

where the CIE standard observer color matching functions
\overline{r}
,
\overline{g}
, and
\overline{b}
are given in Ref.
[
20
]
and

and
\lambda_{\text{max}}=780\,\text{nm}
in the CIE 1931 color space.
If
I\!\left(\omega\right)
were given to specify a color, instead of
\left(R,G,B\right)

\left(R^{\prime},G^{\prime},B^{\prime}\right)
for
O

\displaystyle R^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\text{min}}}^{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\text{d}\lambda\,\overline{r}\!\left(\lambda\right)I\!\left(2\pi c\over\lambda C\right),

(152)

\displaystyle G^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\text{min}}}^{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\text{d}\lambda\,\overline{g}\!\left(\lambda\right)I\!\left(2\pi c\over\lambda C\right),

(153)

\displaystyle B^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\text{min}}}^{\lambda_{\text{max}}}\text{d}\lambda\,\overline{b}\!\left(\lambda\right)I\!\left(2\pi c\over\lambda C\right).

(154)

The problem is that one needs the full spectrum
I\!\left(\omega\right)
for the exact relativistic computation, whereas one cannot know the functional form of
I\!\left(\omega\right)
from merely the three numbers
R
,
G
, and
B

Here we present a naive procedure to mimic the Doppler effect.
For blackbody radiation,
\left(R,G,B\right)
is known as a function of the temperature
T
. That is, we know the functions

\displaystyle R\!\left(T\right),\quad G\!\left(T\right),\quad B\!\left(T\right)

(155)

for the blackbody radiation; see, e.g., Ref.
[
21
]
for possible approximate fit functions.
37
37

37

The explicit form in Ref.
[
21
]
is

\displaystyle R\!\left(T\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}255&T<6688\,\text{K},\\
329.70\left(T-6000\,\text{K}\over 100\,\text{K}\right)^{-0.133205}&T>6688\,\text{K},\end{cases}

\displaystyle G\!\left(T\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&T<505\,\text{K},\\
99.47\ln{T\over 100\,\text{K}}-161.12&505\,\text{K}<T<6503\,\text{K},\\
287.12\left(T-6000\,\text{K}\over 100\,\text{K}\right)^{-0.0755148}&T>6503\,\text{K},\end{cases}

\displaystyle B\!\left(T\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&T<1904\,\text{K},\\
138.52\ln{T-1000\,\text{K}\over 100\,\text{K}}-305.04&1904\,\text{K}<T<6700\,\text{K},\\
255&T>6700\,\text{K}.\end{cases}

For a given set of
\left(R,G,B\right)

\left(T_{R},T_{G},T_{B}\right)
by inverting Eq. (
155

38
38

38

A color function, say,

may not be a single-valued function of

37

Even then, one may in practice take the lower (higher) value of

when

\left(R,G,B\right)
.

Physically, a temperature roughly behaves as an energy, which is the 0th component of the energy-momentum
D
vector, proportional to the wave vector (
163

O
would be estimated by
T/C
for each color. Then one may get the naive Doppler-shifted colors as

\displaystyle R\!\left(T_{R}/C\right),\quad G\!\left(T_{G}/C\right),\quad B\!\left(T_{B}/C\right).

(156)

We have shown how to mimic the Doppler effect within the RGB color scheme.
As said, the full treatment requires the inclusion of the spectrum of the light for each pixel, instead of the current approximation using the three RGB numbers. That will allow visualization of the currently invisible ultraviolet and infrared lights when one is boosted significantly by a Lorentz transformation.

6
Summary

We have shown how to implement special relativity in computer games. The past light cone is used for the foliation of the world without violating causality. This is the first realization of interacting nonplayer characters that perceive the world and react to others, both relativistically without violating causality, under fully relativistic time evolution. In the formulation, the notion of the Lorentz-covariant velocity is extensively used, instead of the more widely used noncovariant velocity. We have shown several ideas to approximate the relativistic world such as the relativistic generalization of the skydome, rigid body, and Doppler effect.

You may find an implementation of the ideas presented in this paper in Ref.
[
15
]
.

39

39

Appendix B
Lorentz contraction and dilation

We show that a measuring rod moving towards the observer looks

For simplicity, we work in the

We consider a measuring rod that has a length

Suppose it stays at rest forever in its rest frame. Then its “worldsheet” can be written in the rest frame as

\displaystyle W_{\text{rod}}

\displaystyle=\Set{\overrightarrow{X}=\left(X^{0},X^{1}\right)}{-\infty<X^{0}<\infty,\quad 0\leq X^{1}\leq\ell}.

a\mathcal{X}_{0}^{2}/2c^{2}\sim 10\,\text{m}
. This is a tiny effect for the distance and acceleration of everyday life. In computer games, one may consider a very large distance and/or acceleration to see such a relativistic effect.

The final proper time of collision is

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{P}}^{f}

\displaystyle={c\over a}\arccosh\!\left(1+{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}\right)=\begin{cases}{c\over a}\ln\!\left(2a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)^{-1}\right)&\text{for $a\mathcal{X}_{0}\gg c^{2}$,}\\
\sqrt{2\mathcal{X}_{0}\over a}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)^{3/2}\right)&\text{for $a\mathcal{X}_{0}\ll c^{2}$.}\end{cases}

(213)

That is, the proper-time difference between the turnover and collision is

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{P}}^{f}-\tau_{\text{P}}^{c}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{c\over a}\ln 2+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)^{-2}\right)&\text{for $a\mathcal{X}_{0}\gg c^{2}$,}\\
\sqrt{\mathcal{X}_{0}\over a}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)&\text{for $a\mathcal{X}_{0}\ll c^{2}$.}\end{cases}

(214)

The corresponding apparent speed is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}^{c}\right)\over\tau_{\text{P}}^{f}-\tau_{\text{P}}^{c}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{c\over 2\ln 2}\left(a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)&\text{for $a\mathcal{X}_{0}\gg c^{2}$,}\\
c\sqrt{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over 2c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}\right)&\text{for $a\mathcal{X}_{0}\ll c^{2}$,}\end{cases}

(215)

which exceeds the speed of light when
a\mathcal{X}_{0}\gtrsim c^{2}
.
Note that this does not lead to any contradiction: The distance for a given proper-time interval can be arbitrarily large, as is the case for the covariant velocity discussed in Sec.
2.4
.

Recovering the speed of light
c
, we may rewrite Eqs. (
208
)–(
210
) in nonnatural units:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{a\tau_{\text{P}}/c}\left(\mathcal{X}_{0}+{c^{2}\over a}\left[1-\cosh\!\left(a\tau_{\text{P}}\over c\right)\right]\right),

(216)

\displaystyle{\text{d}\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)\over\text{d}\tau_{\text{P}}}

\displaystyle=c\,e^{a\tau_{\text{P}}/c}\left(1+{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}-e^{a\tau_{\text{P}}/c}\right),

(217)

\displaystyle{\text{d}^{2}\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)\over{\text{d}\tau_{\text{P}}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=a\,e^{a\tau_{\text{P}}/c}\left(1+{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}-2e^{a\tau_{\text{P}}/c}\right).

(218)

In a formal expansion of Eqs. (
216
)–(
218
), assuming that
c=1
were large, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{X}_{0}-{a\over 2}\tau_{\text{P}}^{2}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(c^{-1}\right),

(219)

\displaystyle{\text{d}\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)\over\text{d}\tau_{\text{P}}}

\displaystyle=-a\tau_{\text{P}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(c^{-1}\right),

(220)

\displaystyle{\text{d}^{2}\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)\over{\text{d}\tau_{\text{P}}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-a+\mathcal{O}\!\left(c^{-1}\right),

(221)

which coincides with the result for the constant acceleration in ordinary Newtonian mechanics.
On the other hand, the correct limit for
c\gg a\tau_{\text{P}}
reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{X}_{0}+c\tau_{\text{P}}{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}-{a\over 2}\tau_{\text{P}}^{2}\left(1-{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(a\tau_{\text{P}}\over c\right)^{3}\right),

(222)

\displaystyle{\text{d}\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)\over\text{d}\tau_{\text{P}}}

\displaystyle=c{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}-a\tau_{\text{P}}\left(1-{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(a\tau_{\text{P}}\over c\right)^{2}\right),

(223)

\displaystyle{\text{d}^{2}\mathcal{X}\!\left(\tau_{\text{P}}\right)\over{\text{d}\tau_{\text{P}}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-a\left(1-{a\mathcal{X}_{0}\over c^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(a\tau_{\text{P}}\over c\right).

(224)

Again we see that the relativistic correction is significant for
\mathcal{X}_{0}\gtrsim c^{2}/a
, as well as for
\mathcal{X}_{0}\gtrsim c\tau_{\text{P}}
.

Appendix D
Rocket propulsion

As a concrete example of the relativistic acceleration, we show how the rocket propulsion is formulated.

Suppose the following:
A rocket has a mass
m\!\left(\tau\right)
, is located at
\overrightarrow{x}\!\left(\tau\right)
, and has a velocity
\overrightarrow{u}\!\left(\tau\right)
in a reference frame
\Set{\overrightarrow{x}}
at its proper time
\tau
.
Note that a mass is a Lorentz-invariant notion and does not depend on frames.
The rocket is in an inertial frame, i.e., under no outer force.

Let us first switch to the rest frame of the rocket at
\tau

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{X}

\displaystyle=L\!\left(\boldsymbol{u}\!\left(\tau\right)\right)\overrightarrow{x},

(225)

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{U}\!\left(\tau\right)

\displaystyle=L\!\left(\boldsymbol{u}\!\left(\tau\right)\right)\overrightarrow{u}\!\left(\tau\right)=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(226)

Suppose that the rocket’s mass, including its fuel, has changed by an amount

\displaystyle\Delta m<0

(227)

during an infinitesimal proper time
\Delta\tau
.
That is, its fuel is consumed by an amount
\left|\Delta m\right|
.
At the proper time
\tau+\Delta\tau

the rocket’s mass becomes
m+\Delta m
, with
\Delta m<0
, and
its velocity

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{U}\!\left(\tau+\Delta\tau\right)=\overrightarrow{U}\!\left(\tau\right)+\overrightarrow{\Delta U}.

(228)

Note that Eq. (
43
) implies that
\left[\overrightarrow{U}+\overrightarrow{\Delta U}\right]^{2}=-1
, which leads to
45
45

45

We may instead directly take the derivative of

54
).

\displaystyle\left[\overrightarrow{U}\!\left(\tau\right),\,\overrightarrow{\Delta U}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(229)

This relation applies in any frame.
In the rest frame (
226
), we further obtain

\displaystyle\Delta U^{0}

\displaystyle=0.

(230)

Suppose that the fuel
\left|\Delta m\right|
is converted into a jet of mass
\Delta m_{\text{jet}}>0

\overrightarrow{W}

The energy and

-momentum conservation reads

\displaystyle mU^{0}

\displaystyle=\left(m+\Delta m\right)\left(U^{0}+\Delta U^{0}\right)+\Delta m_{\text{jet}}W^{0}+\Delta E_{\text{dis}},

(231)

\displaystyle m\boldsymbol{U}

\displaystyle=\left(m+\Delta m\right)\left(\boldsymbol{U}+\Delta\boldsymbol{U}\right)+\Delta m_{\text{jet}}\boldsymbol{W},

(232)

is a possible energy dissipation.
46
46

46

\Delta U^{0}=0

236

In terms of the acceleration in Eq. (

(237)

\displaystyle={\left|\boldsymbol{W}\right|\over\sqrt{1+\boldsymbol{W}^{2}}}=\sqrt{1-\left(C_{\text{jet}}\over 1-C_{\text{dis}}\right)^{2}}.

Handling rotation with quaternions

We review how to implement a spatial rotation in three dimensions using the quaternion, which avoids the gimbal lock. This can be used to store the information on the direction of each 3D object introduced in Sec.
5.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{q}
be a three-vector:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}=\left(q^{1},q^{2},q^{3}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}q^{1}\\
q^{2}\\
q^{3}\end{bmatrix}.

(244)

We parametrize a quaternion

,

,
q^{2}
and
q^{3}

\displaystyle=\left(q;\,q^{1},\,q^{2},\,q^{3}\right)=q+q^{1}\mathbbm{i}_{1}+q^{2}\mathbbm{i}_{2}+q^{3}\mathbbm{i}_{3}=:q+\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{i}},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{i}_{1}^{2}=\mathbbm{i}_{2}^{2}=\mathbbm{i}_{3}^{2}=\mathbbm{i}_{1}\mathbbm{i}_{2}\mathbbm{i}_{3}=-1,

\mathbbm{i}_{i}^{2}:=\left(\mathbbm{i}_{i}\right)^{2}=\mathbbm{i}_{i}\mathbbm{i}_{i}
.
That is,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{i}_{i}\mathbbm{i}_{j}

\displaystyle=-\delta_{ij}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{ijk}\mathbbm{i}_{k},

(247)

where

is the totally antisymmetric tensor

48

48

To be explicit, the nonzero components of the totally antisymmetric tensor are
\epsilon_{123}=\epsilon_{231}=\epsilon_{312}=1
and
\epsilon_{132}=\epsilon_{321}=\epsilon_{213}=-1
. All the other components are zero.

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{for $\left(i,j,k\right)$ being even permutation of $\left(1,2,3\right)$,}\\
-1&\text{for $\left(i,j,k\right)$ being odd permutation of $\left(1,2,3\right)$,}\\

(248)

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{i}}\right)^{2}

(250)

\displaystyle=\left(q;-q^{1},-q^{2},-q^{3}\right)=q-\sum_{i}q^{i}\mathbbm{i}_{i}=q-\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{i}}.

(251)

Note that

\displaystyle\overline{PQ}

We express a three-vector, say, the Cartesian coordinates
