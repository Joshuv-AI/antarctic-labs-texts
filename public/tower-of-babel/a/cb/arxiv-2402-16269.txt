From Large Language Models and Optimization to Decision Optimization CoPilot: A Research Manifesto
Segev Wasserkrug, Leonard Boussioux, Dick den Hertog, Farzaneh Mirzazadeh, Ilker Birbil, Jannis Kurtz, Donato Maragno

Abstract
Significantly simplifying the creation of optimization models for real-world business problems has long been a major goal in applying mathematical optimization more widely to important business and societal decisions. The recent capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) present a timely opportunity to achieve this goal. Therefore, we propose research at the intersection of LLMs and optimization to create a Decision Optimization CoPilot ( DOCP ) - an AI tool designed to assist any decision maker, interacting in natural language to grasp the business problem, subsequently formulating and solving the corresponding optimization model. This paper outlines our DOCP vision and identifies several fundamental requirements for its implementation. We describe the state of the art through both a literature survey and experiments using ChatGPT. We show that a) LLMs already provide substantial novel capabilities relevant to a DOCP, and b) major research challenges remain to be addressed. We also propose possible research directions to overcome these gaps. We also see this work as a call to action to bring together the LLM and optimization communities to pursue our vision, thereby enabling much more widespread improved decision-making.



1
Introduction

Many important real-world decision-making problems can be modeled and solved using mathematical optimization - a formal technique in which the problem is modeled by a set of decision variables subject to constraints, with the goal of optimizing some objective function. Mathematical optimization has provided huge financial and societal benefits in many cases, including more efficient food delivery by the United Nations
(
Peters et al. 2022
)
, millions of dollars in savings for e-commerce sites
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
, and better radiotherapy treatment for cancer patients
(
Zarepisheh et al. 2022
)
. However, most decision-makers currently lack the necessary mathematical skills and/or access to the experts required to apply mathematical optimization. Consequently, it has been a long-standing goal to make optimization accessible to a broad range of decision-makers.

Large Language Models
(
LLMs
) such as ChatGPT
(
OpenAI 2023a
)
have democratized access to AI, making advanced AI skills accessible to almost anyone through natural language and significantly simplifying many tasks such as document and code generation. This technology has also resulted in the creation of a variety of intelligent
copilots
that help users be much more effective and productive in their professional and personal lives (for example, the GitHub Copilot
(
GitHub Inc. 2021
)
helps developers significantly accelerate software development through natural language interaction).

Given the AI advancements enabled by LLMs, we believe the time is right to realize the vision of widely accessible optimization by bringing together LLMs with mathematical optimization. We propose the creation of a
Decision Optimization CoPilot
(
DOCP
): an AI at the disposal of every decision-maker that interacts with the user in natural language and utilizes the human’s knowledge and feedback to create and solve a problem-specific optimization model, thereby leading to better decision making.

This article is organized as follows: after establishing the background in Section

.

2.2
Mathematical Optimization

Mathematical optimization requires formally modeling a decision-making problems as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{minimize\hskip 11.38109pt }&f(x)\\
\mbox{subject to\hskip 11.38109pt}&g(x)\leq 0,\\
&h(x)=0,\end{array}

(1)

where
x\in\Re^{n}
is the decision variables vector (representing the possible decisions),
f(\cdot)
is the objective function or goal to optimize, and the vector-valued functions
g(\cdot)
and
h(\cdot)
are used to define constraints on the acceptable solutions of the problem.

Once modeled as an optimization problem, there are many possible algorithms for finding a solution to the decision problem. The algorithms that can be used depend on the exact type of optimization model created. Efficient algorithms exist for
Linear optimization
(
LO
), which is appropriate when all functions in (
1
) are linear.
Nonlinear optimization
extends this concept to situations where the objective or the constraint functions can be nonlinear functions.
Mixed integer linear optimization
(
MILO
) deals with problems of the form (
1
) where the objective and constraint functions are linear, but some or all decision variables are constrained to be integer. MILO is crucial in practical applications and is widely used in logistics, manufacturing, and supply chain management. However, unlike LO, most real-world MILO problems are NP-hard, making it computationally difficult to find exact solutions for large-scale real-world instances. Therefore, as there could be different optimization formulations for the same decision problem, an important modeling skill is finding the most efficient model. In addition, several advanced techniques have been developed to handle MILO problems efficiently. These strategies include, among others, decomposition or branch-and-bound methods, which break down complex problems into simpler subproblems, and cut generation techniques, which iteratively add constraints to simplify the solution search procedure
(
Wolsey & Nemhauser 1988
)
. As implementing these techniques is often problem-dependent, this also requires significant skill.

Finally, due to the complexity of solving real-world problems using MILO, the use of
optimization engines
such as Gurobi
(
Gurobi Optimization, LLC 2024
)
and CPLEX
(
IBM 2024
)
has become common among practitioners. These engines receive a mixed integer linear optimization problem and solve the problem by employing the latest algorithms. In addition, they have many parameters that can provide additional efficiencies when fine-tuned for specific models.

3
Decision Optimization CoPilot

We envision a
Decision Optimization CoPilot (DOCP)
: A friendly AI, incorporating an LLM, accessible to any business decision-maker who needs to make a decision that can benefit from optimization modeling. The DOCP will guide the decision-maker through deciding by interacting with the user in natural language and carrying out, behind the scenes, the process depicted in Figure
1
, ultimately resulting in an optimization model that provides good solutions to the specific business work.

As a concrete example of such a decision problem, consider Example
3.1
(based on a real-world use case [Anonymous 2023]).

Example 3.1
.

A company wants to have a methodology to identify optimal locations for their bike rental hubs. A hub is a parking facility where users (commuters and tourists) can park their cars, and bicycles are available for rental. The methodology should optimize the maximum coverage of the users’ needs in the cities targeted to maximize the number of users and minimize the number of kilometers driven in the city.

The user would begin by providing the DOCP with the natural language description of the decision problem as in the example. The DOCP, through conversational interaction with the decision-maker, should first understand the precise objectives and constraints of the problem and the available data. Subsequently, the DOCP should validate that optimization is the right approach and which type of optimization model is required (e.g., LO model, MILO model, etc.) based on the problem characteristics (if optimization is not the right approach, the DOCP should notify the user and stop). The DOCP would then create the optimization model (possible models for the problem in Example
3.1
appear in Appendix
C
) and run an appropriate algorithm (typically through an optimization engine) to solve it and provide actionable recommendations to the user.

The DOCP would also need to be able to receive appropriate feedback from the user at any stage and course-correct or update the models and pipelines based on this feedback. Finally, the DOCP would need to communicate in a language understandable to the business decision-maker.

3.1
Core Capabilities Required by a DOCP

Requirement #1 - Translating a Business Level problem definition to an Optimization Model:
A significant part of the optimization modeling process (Figure
1
) is the creation of a
mental model
of the decision-making problem at a sufficient level of detail and precision so that a formal optimization model can be created. Therefore, a DOCP would need to be able to guide the decision-maker through the modeling process that starts from (often a high-level) business problem definition and ends with the level of detail required for optimization model creation. Moreover, the DOCP would need to understand what data is available and decide what type of optimization model is required: Can it be modeled using an LO model, or does it require discrete decision variables, which would both require a MILO model (which would be much harder to solve)? Should uncertainty be considered, in which cases techniques such as Robust or Stochastic optimization
(
Vladimirou & Zenios 1997
)
would be required?

Requirement #2 - Enabling the Decision Maker to Verify Optimization Model Correctness:
As LLMs can produce erroneous output, the optimization model generated by the DOCP could contain errors. While some generated model errors could manifest as runtime errors, there are often many much more subtle errors that would therefore be harder to detect. Such errors could include the generated objective not corresponding to the real-world decision objective or a missing or erroneous constraint. Using such erroneous formulations could result in the model being infeasible or generate solutions that, while optimal for the generated optimization problem, are not good solutions for the actual decision-making problem. When optimization experts are using LLMs, the onus of validating the generated models can be placed on these experts. However, decision-makers who do not have optimization expertise cannot be expected to validate the model. Therefore, a DOCP must both a) take whatever steps are possible to reduce the number of errors in the model and b) provide sufficient tools appropriate for the business decision-maker not only to validate the model but also provide feedback in a way that will enable correction of the optimization model.

Requirement #3 - Creating Efficient Optimization Models:
Creating an optimization model that captures the real problem’s constraints and objectives is insufficient. As described in Section
2.2
many times the resulting optimization problems are NP-hard. In such cases, many correct optimization models can still not provide useful solutions within a reasonable time frame.
Therefore, advanced modeling techniques such as finding equivalent, more efficient models, problem decomposition, column generation, and approximations must often be introduced. Moreover, when using off-the-shelf engines such as CPLEX (see section
2.2
) to solve such models, optimization engine parameters such as cuts, branching variable selection, and primal/dual algorithms must be selected. As the most effective advanced modeling techniques or engine parameter values are often problem-specific, a DOCP must be able to apply these suitably to the specific problem.

4
State-of-the-Art with regards to DOCP Requirements

To understand the state-of-the-art capabilities relevant to a DOCP, we carried out a survey of related work and experimented with ChatGPT
(
OpenAI 2023a
)
, a state-of-the-art LLM, as described below.

4.1
Related work

An increasing amount of work has leveraged LLMs
to convert natural language descriptions into mathematical models and formalizations
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Meadows & Freitas 2022
;
Wu et al. 2022
)
. Of these,
many works have used LLMs to generate optimization models with the goal of making optimization more widely accessible. The work of
Amarasinghe et al. 2023
states as a goal the ability to “leverage LLMs to support non-expert users to carry out business optimizations without having to consult experts”. Focusing on production scheduling, this work constructed an open-source dataset consisting of relatively precise natural language problem descriptions and corresponding model formulations and used it to fine-tune a code-generating LLM to generate optimization models. This approach achieved good performance as measured by running the generated models, ensuring that they run correctly, and then comparing both execution time and obtained solution to the “ground truth” models. However, it does not address model validation by non-experts, nor explicitly how to generate efficient models.

OptiMUS
(
AhmadiTeshnizi et al. 2023
)
uses an LLM to generate and code a mathematical model. It then uses an optimization engine to solve the model and run some feasibility tests to evaluate the correctness of the generated solutions. These unit tests can be automatically generated or suggested by optimization modeling experts. Despite showcasing the capability of integrating LLMs and optimization engines for automated optimization problem-solving, OptiMUS is contingent on a structured input representation (SNOP) and expert-created unit testing, presenting difficulties for users lacking proficiency in mathematical optimization.

Li et al. 2023b
propose a framework for automatic formulation from unstructured text descriptions of MILO problems. It utilizes a constraint classification scheme and constraint templates to guide the LLMs in creating MILO models from natural language descriptions. The authors assess the quality of the LLM by the quality of the generated models. In this work, the evaluation is entirely conducted by OR experts who must understand the correctness of the model.

There are additional works that have used LLMs to generate optimization models
(
Jang 2022
;
Almonacid 2023
;
Tsouros et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
. In 2022, the NL4Opt NeurIPS competition
(
Ramamonjison et al. 2023
)
garnered significant attention in the realm of extracting linear optimization (LO) models from natural language. The competition was inspired by the pilot study of
Ramamonjison et al. 2022
where the authors introduced an automated formulation method named OptGen, designed to handle LO problem descriptions with diverse constraints. OptGen employs a two-step mapping strategy where, in the first step, all problem entities of the optimization problem (e.g., objective function, decision variables, etc.) are detected. In the second step, the problem description, together with the labeled semantic problem entities, is used to generate the final mathematical representation of the problem. For the entity detection problem,
Ning et al. 2023
achieved the highest score, while
Gangwar & Kani 2023
achieved the highest accuracy in generating optimization models. This work utilized BART
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
, a large encoder-decoder model and enhanced the input by encapsulating entities with XML-like tagging. In all of these works, the quality of the solution was judged by how close the model was to the “ground truth” model.

Inspired by the NL4OPT challenge,
Tsouros et al. 2023
propose a modular framework for problem modeling via LLMs. The authors break down the modeling task into four subtasks, each implemented using prompting
(
Khot et al. 2022
)
. Beginning with a natural language description as input, the system sequentially extracts problem entities, establishes relationships, formalizes the optimization problem, and translates it into a constraint modeling language like CPMpy. Following the modeling phase, the code is compiled, executed, and automatically debugged if necessary. The system then engages in an interactive refinement process with the user, offering a comprehensive and efficient approach to problem modeling. In their survey paper,
Fan et al. 2024
assessed LLMs’ performance on textbook problems and real-world scenarios using the NL4OPT dataset and evaluation metrics. LLMs demonstrated impressive proficiency in modeling textbook-level problems; however, in the context of real-world problems, the generated models exhibited errors such as incorrect constraints, extra constraints, and missing constraints. Nonetheless, pointing out errors and prompting corrections proved effective, making LLM-generated formulations more accurate.

To summarize, a lot of work has focused on generating models from natural language descriptions. While good progress has been made, there are still many errors in the generated models. In addition, all works require quite a precise natural language description of the problem and not a high-level description, such as in Example
3.1
, and only a few have gone beyond model generation to actually solve the generated models and analyze the results. Moreover, only
AhmadiTeshnizi et al. 2023
addresses the need to generate efficient models, and this also in a limited, implicit fashion (by having execution time as one of the measurement metrics). Finally, none of the above works address the need of a business user to verify the correctness of the model or the need to update the model as a result of business user feedback.

Unrelated to optimization, some works have attempted to use LLMs directly for complex reasoning tasks such as mathematical problem solving
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
;
Wei et al. 2022b
;
Lightman et al. 2023
;
Trinh et al. 2024
)
. Following this approach,
Yang et al. 2023
employed LLMs to generate solutions for optimization problems, attempting to bypass the need for problem modeling and optimization engines. They applied this method to various Traveling Salesmen Problem (TSP) instances. In these instances, the LLM received node locations as input and produced a sequence as output. If the generated solution was not optimal, the authors provided the optimality gap as input and asked the model to refine its output iteratively. The approach adopted by the authors relies on the availability of an “oracle” that can generate the optimality gap and feasibility for a given solution.

However, previous works trying to use LLMs directly for reasoning (including
(
Bubeck et al. 2023
)
) have shown that while the reasoning capabilities of LLMs are impressive, they still seem severely lacking. For example, in the aforementioned study of optimizing TSP problems, the performance of the LLM significantly deteriorates when dealing with large-sized problems. As the problem size increases beyond 10 nodes, the optimality gaps escalate rapidly, and optimization heuristics begin to surpass all LLMs. Furthermore, when a problem involves multiple constraints and nonlinear functions, the LLM is susceptible to generating solutions that are either infeasible or significantly distant from the optimum. In general, the work of
Gao et al. 2023
showed for that for multiple reasoning tasks, an LLM that first generates Python code and then runs that code to carry out the actual reasoning does much better than an LLM that directly tries to reason. This reinforces our proposed DOCP workflow: first generating an optimization model and then solving it externally to the LLM to find good solutions.

There are also other works at the intersection of LLMs and optimization whose learning may be incorporated into a validation capability for the business user.
Li et al. 2023a
uses an LLM to provide a natural language interface to an existing optimization model. This interface can be used to query the model and its results and also provides some customization capabilities.
Chen et al. 2023
develop a chatbot to assist an optimization expert in identifying and resolving infeasible optimization problems by taking Pyomo code as input.

4.2.2
Requirement #2: Verifying Optimization Model Correctness

In these experiments, we tested ChatGPT’s ability to generate correct models for optimization problems when given a very precise description of the problem and not high-level ones as in Example

(the full details of the experiments appear in Appendix

However, for both problems, one of the five experiments generated an incorrect objective function expression (both correct and incorrect objective functions for both experiments appear in Figure

4.2.3
Requirement #3: Creating Efficient Optimization models

In this last set of experiments, our goal was to test whether ChatGPT can create models that are more efficiently solvable by optimization solvers.

We began by assessing ChatGPT’s proficiency in transforming a correct formulation of the bike hub optimization model into a simpler one by prompting it to restructure the problem into an equivalent form with fewer variables. In this test, ChatGPT successfully produced an accurate version in only one out of five attempts.

In the four instances where the formulations were incorrect, ChatGPT exhibited a variety of errors. These included, among others, introducing nonlinear constraints or objective functions and incorporating constraints that only include parameters without variables (for the full experiment and list of errors, see Appendix
D.1
). Furthermore, ChatGPT could not produce satisfactory reformulations without explicit instructions to maintain an equivalent form while minimizing variables. In scenarios with more open-ended instructions, ChatGPT struggled to deliver adequate solutions. It tended to oversimplify the problem, either by omitting constraints or by incorrectly redefining sets.

In another experiment, we offered ChatGPT a constraint with absolute values and asked ChatGPT if this constraint could be represented by linear constraint, which is much more efficient. For the case of a convex
\leq
constraint, the answer was always correct. However, for the case of a concave
\geq
constraint, ChatGPT did not use binary variables, and the answer was incorrect. We observed similar results for the case of ‘the sum of max of linear functions.’ Such components often occur in, e.g., production-inventory problems, and it is well known that linear constraints can reformulate such a nonlinear constraint. ChatGPT also finds this reformulation but again forgets to introduce binary variables for the
\geq
constraint.

Overall, this set of experiments shows that there is a significant gap in ChatGPT’s ability to formulate efficient optimization models.

4.3
Summary of State of the Art

As can be seen, LLMs already provide important capabilities in realizing our DOCP vision. However, most of the progress of previous works has been in using precise and detailed natural language descriptions of a problem to generate correct optimization models (Requirement #2). Little or no work has been done to generate optimization models from a business-level problem description or to ensure the creation of models that can be efficiently solved by solvers (Requirement #1 and Requirement #3), and our experiments show significant gaps in these areas. Even for requirement #2, the current state of the art is such that often incorrect models are generated, including subtle errors that are very hard to detect. Therefore, much work remains for the DOCP vision to be realized.

5
Research Directions for Creating a DOCP

In creating a DOCP, LLM adaptation can play a significant part. As there are many ways in which an LLM can be adapted to a specific task (see Section
2.1
), an important research direction is how best to combine the different adaptation techniques - prompting, fine-tuning and RLHF. Specific examples include: should LLMs be fine-tuned with specific decision optimization knowledge? What can prompting techniques (such as
Chain of thoughts prompting

(
Wei et al. 2022b
)
- a prompting technique found effective in reasoning tasks) accomplish? What are the best prompting techniques for each of the requirements? How should LLMs be adapted to generate correct and efficient optimization models? How best could LLMs be adapted to reduce the number of variables, reformulate nonlinear models into linear, and add problem-specific decomposition and column generation?

Another important research direction is which parts of the DOCP should be driven by the LLM, and which parts by external workflows such as Tree of Thoughts
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
or Graph of Thoughts
(
Besta et al. 2023
)
? For a concrete example, it may be possible to utilize existing methodological frameworks, such as
CRISP-DM
(CRoss Industry Process for Data Mining
(
Wirth & Hipp 2000
)
) that guide an analytics practitioner through the process described by Figure
1
). Would a graph-of-thought DOCP implementation incorporating such methodologies yield good results?

Regarding Requirement #2, as there is always a chance that an incorrect model will be generated, tools are required to enable the business user to validate any solution provided by a DOCP. Even today, business users often struggle to validate and understand solutions provided by expert-created optimization models, so tools such as what-if analysis are used. Specifically, business users can change a solution or provide an alternate one and see which model constraints are violated and what the resulting objective function value is. These existing techniques could be augmented by LLM capabilities such as those in
Li et al. 2023a
, where the user can ask questions about the model in natural language. It may also be worthwhile to explore creating LLM-based chatbots to whom the user can direct questions and have a conversation to understand better and validate the models.

Another important research direction for Requirement #2 is how a specific generated model and the DOCP’s general capabilities can be updated based on user feedback. For example, during the validation of a model, the user may realize that for specific solutions, the wrong objective has been calculated, or a business constraint has been wrongly marked as being violated. Similarly, the user may be able to provide solutions that satisfy the business constraints, possibly along with their objective function value. It would be important to both correct the specific model and continuously improve the DOCP based on this feedback.

Regarding requirement #3, when an optimization engine is used to solve an optimization model, the parameter settings, such as which heuristics to use, can have a significant effect. A large body of existing works known as
Learning to Optimize

(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chi et al. 2022
;
Gupta et al. 2022
;
Khalil et al. 2022
)
uses machine learning techniques to find good heuristic settings for specific problem types to reduce the solution time. Currently, such techniques are used by optimization experts, tailoring them to specific problem instances. An interesting avenue of research would be to see if LLMs could be adapted to select and customize the appropriate learning to optimize techniques in a problem-specific manner. As a final point, could the huge body of knowledge on the internet about the theory and application of optimization, including problem modeling and best practices (see, e.g.,
Williams 2013
), be better used to fine-tune an LLM to generate more efficient optimization models and optimization engine parameters’ settings?

5.1
Datasets and Benchmarks

Research towards a DOCP will require appropriate datasets and well-defined quality metrics. Clearly, any such dataset should consist of a set of optimization problems, each containing a business-level description and a correct and efficient “ground truth” optimization model. Creating these parts of the datasets could utilize journals (e.g.,
IJA 2024
) and books
(
Williams 2013
)
describing real-world case studies and their associated models. It may also be possible to extend datasets created by existing works bringing together LLMs and optimization such as
Amarasinghe et al. 2023
. However, in addition to the natural language problem descriptions and “ground truth” models, as the DOCP is required to have a conversation with the business user to understand the problem and create an appropriate model, a component that can carry out the business user side of the conversation is also required. An interesting avenue of research is to see whether an LLM independent of the DOCP can be used to emulate the business user’s side of the conversation, to enable DOCP development and testing.

Regarding evaluation,
measures used in most existing works using LLMs to generate optimization models are based on the syntactical accuracy of the generated model as compared to the “ground truth” model (see Section
4.1
). However, the goal of a DOCP is to generate good
solutions
to the business problem, and two very syntactically different models could model the same identical set of constraint-satisfying optimal solutions. Therefore, we posit that the metrics should directly relate to solution quality. While we leave a precise definition of these metrics to future work, we propose that these metrics should be based on measures such as whether the solutions generated by the DOCP satisfy the constraint of the ground truth model, how good they are in terms of ground truth objective value, and their execution time running time (possibly building on and extending the metrics defined by
Amarasinghe et al. 2023
).

6
Summary and call to action

We outlined our vision to combine LLMs and optimization modeling to create a DOCP to widely democratize the use of optimization for decision-making. We outlined three core requirements, discussed the current state of the art, and demonstrated the significant gaps to overcome. Due to these gaps and the huge positive implications of realizing this vision, we hope this work serves as a call for action to mobilize the LLM and optimization research communities around this goal.

We would also like to highlight that even intermediary techniques and tools created on the path to a DOCP will already significantly democratize the use of optimization by reducing the level of expertise required and enhancing accessibility. For instance, the improved automatic generation of efficient optimization models will empower novice modelers to achieve greater effectiveness. An optimization modeler could utilize a chatbot to facilitate discussions with business users (per requirement #1), bridging the communication gap between business decision-makers and optimization experts. Introducing a natural language interface tailored for business use will increase the acceptance and application of these models in real-world scenarios. Such incremental advancements could already result in much more widespread data and model-driven decision-making.

7
Statement of impact

The vision of DOCP, if realized, has the potential to make decision-makers much more effective in their jobs and provide immense benefits by enabling optimization to be used across a vastly larger number of real-world decision-making problems, thereby leading to much more widespread better decision-making. As can be seen in the use cases referenced in the article, such optimization-driven decisions result in significant monetary and societal benefits such as better food utilization and healthcare.

Ethical implications

In any optimization solution, there are dangers if the recommendations are followed blindly and poorly understood by the
decision-maker
whose responsibility is to make the decision. Introducing a DOCP does not and should not lessen the decision-maker’s responsibility. When collaborating with an optimization modeler, there’s the added benefit of a knowledgeable professional who can provide insights. This expert not only grasps the intricacies of the model but can also elucidate the underlying assumptions and its potential limitations for the business decision-maker. Therefore, using a DOCP underscores the need for features that allow the business decision-maker to understand the proposed solution and its limitations deeply. In high-stakes cases, the business decision-maker should still consult with an optimization modeler to review the optimization model created by the DOCP. In very high-risk cases, it may be better for the business decision-maker to forgo the direct use of a DOCP and instead engage with an optimization modeler, who may then leverage the DOCP to accelerate the creation of the optimization solution, ensuring a more informed and cautious approach to decision-making.

The need for extra care and validation is an additional key advantage of our proposed approach: having the LLM generate an optimization model rather than directly produce a solution. This approach is beneficial because it is considerably easier for an optimization modeler to review and understand the assumptions and limitations of a mathematical optimization model than a solution directly generated by an LLM. This step allows for a more transparent and reviewable decision-making process, ensuring that the decision-maker is fully informed of all aspects of the solution.

Appendix A
A note about optimization notation

In this work, we use the terms ‘Linear Optimization (LO)’ and ‘Mixed Integer Linear Optimization’ instead of, respectively, ‘Linear Programming’ and ‘Mixed Integer Linear Programming’, which are terms the optimization community now attempts to avoid.
