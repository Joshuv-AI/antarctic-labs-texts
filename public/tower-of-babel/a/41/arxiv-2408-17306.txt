𝕃 \mathbb{L} -valued integration
Xingni Jiang, Jan Harm van der Walt, Marten Wortel

Abstract
. We develop integration theory for integrating functions taking values into a Dedekind complete unital f f -algebra 𝕃 \mathbb{L} with respect to 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -valued measures. We then discuss and prove completeness results of 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -valued L p L^{p} -spaces.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a Dedekind complete unital
f
-algebra. In
[
9
]
,
\mathbb{L}
-normed spaces were investigated. These are
\mathbb{L}
-modules equipped with an
\mathbb{L}^{+}
-valued norm satisfying the usual properties. Convergence in an
\mathbb{L}
-normed space
X
is determined by order convergence in
\mathbb{L}
, in the sense that a net
(x_{\alpha})
in
X
is defined to converge to
x\in X
whenever
\left\|x_{\alpha}-x\right\|
order converges to
0
in
\mathbb{L}
. One can then define Cauchy nets and completeness, and
\mathbb{L}
-valued ‘sequence’ spaces
\ell^{p}(S,\mathbb{L})
(generalized to
S
being any nonempty set rather than
\mathbb{N}
) were investigated in detail: completeness and the usual duality results were shown.

This paper is an attempt at developing the corresponding theory of
\mathbb{L}
-valued
L^{p}
-spaces, for which it is clearly necessary to first developed a theory of integration in this setting. We have to consider
\mathbb{L}
-valued functions, since real-valued functions have no natural
\mathbb{L}
-module structure, and
\mathbb{L}
-valued functions clearly do. Furthermore, in order to generalize for example the duality
c_{0}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{L})^{*}\cong\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{L})
(see
[
9
, Theorem 4.11]
), it will be necessary to consider
\mathbb{L}
-valued measures as well. So both our functions and measures will be vector-valued, which deviates from many approaches in the literature where either the functions or the measures are real-valued.

A big inspiration for this paper is
[
4
]
, in which a comprehensive theory of integration of real-valued functions with respect to measures taking values in some nice partially ordered vector space is developed. This paper largely follows the approach of
[
4
]
. Indeed, many of our results closely parallel the techniques developed in
[
4
]
. However, there is one important exception:
[
4
, Lemma 6.2]
, which is crucial for defining the integral of measurable functions, has no obvious extension to
\mathbb{L}
-valued functions. Indeed, in certain natural cases the analogue of
[
4
, Lemma 6.2]
fails, see
Example

4.2
. We solve this by introducing proper measures in an approach similar to the approach in
[
11
]
.

This paper is organized as follows.

Section

2
discusses properties of the Dedekind complete unital
f
-algebra
\mathbb{L}
as well as other preliminaries on local convergence in measure and
\mathbb{L}
-valued measures.

In
Section

3
, we discuss measurable functions and (bounded) measurable step functions. We show that measurable functions can be approximated by bounded measurable step functions.

We use the above result in
Section

4
to show that there is a relatively large class of measures which are proper. Proper measures are defined as those measures for which the analogue of
[
4
, Lemma 4.2]
holds.

The theory is then developed smoothly for proper measures in
Section

5
, closely following the corresponding theory in
[
4
]
. This section finishes by obtaining some properties of the space of integrable functions.

The important convergence theorems, like the Dominated Convergence Theorem, the Monotone Convergence Theorem, and Fatou’s Lemma, are then proven in
Section

6
. Again this section closely follows the theory developed in
[
4
]
.

These results are then used to define
\mathbb{L}
-valued
L^{p}
-spaces in
Section

7
and study their properties. The usual norm (in)equalities are shown, and the section ends by showing that such
L^{p}
-spaces have the property that every absolutely convergent series converges.

In general it is not clear whether for an
\mathbb{L}
-module
X
, the property that every absolutely convergent series converges implies that
X
is sequentially complete, like in the classical case. However, in
Section

8
, we show that under some not too restictive conditions on
\mathbb{L}
this does hold, and so we conclude that our
L^{p}
-spaces are sequentially complete under these conditions.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Dedekind complete unital
f
-algebras

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a Dedekind complete unital
f
-algebra, here “unital” means
\mathbb{L}
contains a multiplicative unit which we denote by
1
. By
[
5
, Theorem 10.7]
,
1
is automatically a weak order unit of
\mathbb{L}
. We denote by
\mathbb{L}_{1}
the order ideal by
\mathbb{L}
generated by 1. Combining
[
2
, Theorem 2.64]
and
[
1
, Theorem 7.29]
shows that there exists a unique Stonean space
K
such that
\mathbb{L}
is an order dense sublattice and subalgebra of
{\mathrm{C}}_{\infty}(K)
, and
1
corresponds to the constant one function
\textbf{1}_{K}
in
{{\mathrm{C}}}(K)
. Moreover
\mathbb{L}
is an order ideal in
{\mathrm{C}}_{\infty}(K)
, see
[
1
, Theorem 1.40]
. Hence we have that (as in
[
9
, Remark 2.1]
)

(2.1)

{{\mathrm{C}}}(K)=\mathbb{L}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{L}\subseteq{\mathrm{C}}_{\infty}(K).

This representation allows us to define
\lambda^{p}
for
0\leq p<\infty
pointwise in
C_{\infty}(K)
and therefore also in
\mathbb{L}
(cf.
[
9
, page 5]
). By
[
9
, Lemma 4.2
(ii)
]
,
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{p}
is order continuous as a map from
\mathbb{L}
to
\mathbb{L}
.

As in
[
4
]
, we extend the partial ordering on
\mathbb{L}
by adding a new element
\infty
, and declaring that
\lambda\leq\infty
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
. We denote
\overline{\mathbb{L}}=\mathbb{L}\cup\{\infty\}
,
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}={\mathbb{L}^{+}}\cup\{\infty\}
, and the elements of
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
that are in
\mathbb{L}

will be called
finite
. Clearly every nonempty subset of
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
has a supremum.

Remark 2.1
.

There are more sophisticated ways of adding infinite elements to
\mathbb{L}
: one can consider the sup-completion
\mathbb{L}^{s}
of
\mathbb{L}
, which was first introduced by Donner in
[
6
]
. This is used in
[
8
]
to construct a vector-valued integration theory using the Daniell integral. However, for our purposes it suffices to consider
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
.

On
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
, we define
\infty+\lambda=\lambda+\infty=\infty
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
. For the multiplication, we define
\infty\cdot\lambda=\lambda\cdot\infty=\infty
for any
\lambda\in\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}\setminus\{0\}
and
0\cdot\infty=\infty\cdot 0=0
. It is easy to verify that for any
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
with
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
and
\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}
, we have
0\leq\lambda_{1}\alpha_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\alpha_{2}
. Note that
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
does not satisfies the
f
-algebra property, for example, take
\lambda
and
\alpha
in
{\mathbb{L}^{+}}\setminus\{0\}
with
\lambda\wedge\alpha=0
, then
[\lambda\cdot\infty]\wedge\alpha=\infty\wedge\alpha=\alpha\neq 0
. (This will not cause any trouble for us, since the measure is finite, and the integrable functions are almost everywhere finite, see
Lemma

5.3
. So in the end, we still work in
\mathbb{L}
.)

2.2.
Local convergence in measure

The next remark summarizes
[
9
, Remark 2.4]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In (
2.1
), the order continuous real-valued functionals on
C(K)
separate the points of
C(K)
if and only if
K
is
hyper-Stonean
(i.e. the union of the supports of the normal measures on
K
is dense in
K
), if and only if
C(K)_{\mathbb{C}}
is a von Neumann algebra, if and only if
C(K)
can be represented as a space
L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},m)=L^{\infty}(m)
of essentially bounded measurable functions with respect to a decomposable measure
m
on a locally compact Hausdorff space. In this case
C_{\infty}(K)
can be identified with the space of all measurable functions
L^{0}(m)
, so that

L^{\infty}(m)=\mathbb{L}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{L}\subseteq L^{0}(m).

In the rest of this subsection, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},m)
be a measure space. We recall the well-known concept of local convergence in measure.

Definition 2.3
.

A sequence
(f_{n})
in
L^{0}(m)
converges to
f\in L^{0}
locally in measure if for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\mu(F)<\infty
and for every
\epsilon>0
,

\lim_{n}\mu(\left|f-f_{n}\right|^{-1}[[\epsilon,\infty]]\cap F)=0.

The next result is a combination of
[
7
, 245D, 245E & 245Y (d)]
. Although Fremlin does not state that the topology of local convergence in measure is locally solid, this is obvious. In
[
1
, page 210]
this fact is stated for the case of a
\sigma
-finite measure.

Theorem 2.4
.

Local convergence in measure is induced by an order continuous locally solid topology
\tau
on
L^{0}(m)
.

(i)

\tau
is Hausdorff if and only if
m
is semi-finite.

(ii)

\tau
is metrizable if and only if
m
is
\sigma
-finite.

(iii)

\tau
is Hausdorff and
(L^{0}(m),\tau)
is complete if and only if
m
is localisable.

It is well-known (see for example
[
10
]
) that
L^{0}(m)
has the countable sup property for a
\sigma
-finite measure
m
.

2.3.
Summability in
\mathbb{L}

A net
(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}
in
\mathbb{L}
is called summable if the net of finite partial sums

\left(\sum_{\alpha\in F}\lambda_{\alpha}\right)_{F\subseteq I\text{ finite}}

is (order) convergent in
\mathbb{L}
. In this case, we denote the (order) limit of this net by
\displaystyle{\sum}\lambda_{\alpha}
. Observe that if
\lambda_{\alpha}\geq 0
for every
\alpha\in I
then

\sum\lambda_{\alpha}=\sup\left\{\sum_{\alpha\in F}\lambda_{\alpha}~:~F\subseteq I\text{ is finite}\right\}.

2.4.
\mathbb{L}
-valued measures

Let
(S,\Sigma)
be a measurable space, i.e., a set
S
equipped with a
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma
. A finite
\mathbb{L}
-valued measure is a map
\mu\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{L}^{+}
satisfying
\mu(\emptyset)=0
and if
(A_{n})
is a disjoint sequence in
\Sigma
, then

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n}).

This definition is a special case of
[
4
, Definition 4.1]
. In this paper
(S,\Sigma,\mu,\mathbb{L})
always denotes a measurable space
(S,\Sigma)
equipped with a finite
\mathbb{L}
-valued measure
\mu
.

2.5.
Sequences of functions

Let
S
be a set, let
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of functions from
S
to a partially ordered set
E
, and let
f\colon S\to E
. If for every
s\in S
,
(f_{n}(s))_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an increasing sequence in
E
with
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}(s)=f(s)
in
E
, then we say
f_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise. The notation
f_{n}\downarrow f
pointwise is defined similarly.

3.
Measurable functions and measurable step functions

A function
\varphi\colon S\to\mathbb{L}
is called a
measurable step function
if it can be written as a finite sum
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}
for some
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{L}
and a partition
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
of
S
in
\Sigma
. We define the integral of
\varphi
with respect to
\mu
by

\int_{S}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mu(A_{i}).

It is easy to verify that the integral is a positive
\mathbb{L}
-linear map from the
\mathbb{L}
-module of measurable step functions into
\mathbb{L}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A function
f\colon S\to\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
is called
measurable
if there exists a sequence of positive measurable step functions
({\varphi}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
\varphi_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise. The set
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
denotes the set of measurable functions
f\colon S\to\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
.

Let
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
denote the set of positive extended valued measurable functions, and let
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
be its subset contains only finite valued positive measurable functions. As in the real valued function case,
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
and
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
contain the pointwise sum, supremum, and infimum of two of their elements.
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
and
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
are invariant under the pointwise multiplication of elements of
{\mathbb{L}^{+}}
. Later on, the reader will find in the proof of the monotone convergence theorem (
Theorem

6.1
) that
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
also contains the supremum of any monotone increasing sequence in it.

We need to be able to approximate elements of
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
by bounded step functions, which we now define.

Definition 3.2
.

A measurable step function
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}
is a
bounded measurable step function
if
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{L}_{1}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. We denote by
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
the set of all bounded measurable step functions.

Note that
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
is a vector lattice algebra with the pointwise algebraic and lattice operations in
\mathbb{L}
of two of its elements, and its positive cone is

{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}})}=\{\varphi\in{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}\colon\varphi(s)\in{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}},\forall s\in S\}.

We recall the following result from
[
3
, Lemma 36]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
P
be a lattice. For ever
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(p_{nm})
be a sequence in
P
so that
p_{nm}\uparrow p_{n}
. Assume further that
p_{n}\uparrow p
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

q_{n}\coloneqq\bigvee\{p_{1n},p_{2n},\ldots,p_{nn}\}.

Then
q_{n}\uparrow p
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
. Then there exists an increasing sequence
(\varphi_{n})
of bounded measurable step functions so that
\varphi_{n}(s)\uparrow f(s)
for every
s\in S
.

Proof.

By definition of
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
there exists a sequence
(\psi_{m})
of positive measurable step functions so that
\psi_{m}(s)\uparrow f(s)
for every
s\in S
. For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
let

\psi_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{m}}\lambda_{i}^{(m)}\chi_{A_{i}^{(m)}}.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
. By Freudenthal’s Spectral Theorem, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k_{m}\}
there exists a sequence
(\lambda_{i}^{(nm)})
in
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
so that
\lambda_{i}^{(nm)}\uparrow\lambda_{i}^{(m)}
. Define

\varphi_{nm}=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{m}}\lambda_{i}^{(nm)}\chi_{A_{i}^{(m)}},~n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then
(\varphi_{nm})
is a sequence of
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
-valued measurable step functions, and
\varphi_{nm}(s)\uparrow\psi_{m}(s)
for every
s\in S
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

\varphi_{n}\coloneqq\bigvee\{\varphi_{1n},\varphi_{2n},\ldots,\varphi_{nn}\}.

Then
(\varphi_{n})
is a sequence of positive,
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
-valued measurable step functions. By Proposition
3.3
,
\varphi_{n}(s)\uparrow f(s)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Combining
Theorem

3.4
and
3.1
yields that a function
f\colon S\to\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
is in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
if and only if it is the pointwise supremum of a sequence in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}})}
.

4.
Proper measures

The validity of our integration theory depends on the fact that if a sequence of bounded measurable step functions decrease pointwise to 0, then so do the integrals.

Definition 4.1
.

An
\mathbb{L}
-valued measure
\mu
on
(S,\Sigma)
is
proper
if for every sequence
(\varphi_{n})
in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}})}
, if
\varphi_{n}\downarrow\textbf{0}
pointwise, then

\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu\downarrow 0.

The next example, which is slightly modified from
[
11
, Example 5.4]
to ensure that the sequence of functions is decreasing, shows that fairly natural
\mathbb{L}
-valued measures need not be proper.

Example 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be such that
L^{\infty}[0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{L}\subseteq L^{0}[0,1)
and let
S=[0,1)
equipped with the
\mathbb{L}
-valued measure
\mu
defined by
\mu(A)=\mathbf{1}_{A}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1
, denote by
I_{n,k}
the interval
[k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n})
, so that for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
[0,1)
is the disjoint union
\cup_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}I_{n,k}
. In particular, for each
s\in S
, there is a unique
I_{n,k}
such that
s\in I_{n,k}
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\varphi_{n}\colon S\to\mathbb{L}
by
\varphi_{n}(s)=\mathbf{1}_{I_{n,k}}
whenever
s\in I_{n,k}
. In other words,

\varphi_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbf{1}_{I_{n,k}}\chi_{I_{n,k}},

so
\varphi_{n}
is a step function. Now for each
s\in S
,
\varphi_{n}(s)
is the indicator function of an interval of size
2^{-n}
which decreases to
0
in
\mathbb{L}
(in order), so
\varphi_{n}
decreases to
0
pointwise. But

\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,d\mu=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbf{1}_{I_{n,k}}\mu(I_{n,k})=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbf{1}_{I_{n,k}}\mathbf{1}_{I_{n,k}}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,1)},

showing that
\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,d\mu
does not decrease to
0
.

Our next efforts are aimed at showing that there is a large class of measures which are proper. The terminology of the next definition is taken from
[
11
, Definition 3.4]
.

Definition 4.3
.

An
\mathbb{L}
-valued measure
\mu
on
(S,\Sigma)
is
\sigma
-scalar
if
\mu\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{L}_{1}
, and there exists a collection
\{m_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of real-valued measures on
(S,\Sigma)
and a collection
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of bounded elements in
\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}
so that, for every
A\in\Sigma
,

\mu(A)=\sum m_{j}(A)\lambda_{j}\text{ in }\mathbb{L}_{1}.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mu
be a
\sigma
-scalar measure. Assume that the order continuous functional on
\mathbb{L}_{1}
separate the points of
\mathbb{L}_{1}
. Then
\mu
is proper.

Note that the above assumption on
\mathbb{L}_{1}
is equivalent to requiring that
K
in (
2.1
) is hyper-Stonean.

Proof.

Let
\Psi
be a positive order continuous linear functional on
\mathbb{L}_{1}
. Suppose first that
\mu=am
with
0\leq a\in\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
m
and real-valued measure on
\Sigma
. Let
\varphi
be a positive, measurable
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-valued step function on
S
so that

\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}.

Define
g^{(\varphi)}:S\to\mathbb{R}
as

g^{(\varphi)}(s)\coloneqq\Psi(\varphi(s)a),~s\in S.

Then
g^{(\varphi)}
is a real-valued measurable step function on
S
. In particular,

g^{(\varphi)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi(\lambda_{i}a)\chi_{A_{i}}.

Observe that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{S}g^{(\varphi)}\ dm&=&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi(\lambda_{i}a)m(A_{i})\\
&=&\displaystyle\Psi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}am(A_{i})\right)\\
&=&\displaystyle\Psi\left(\int_{S}\varphi\ d\mu\right).\\
\end{array}

Let
(\varphi_{n})
be a sequence of
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-valued, measurable step functions. Assume that
\varphi_{n}\downarrow 0
pointwise on
S
. Then
g^{(\varphi_{n})}\downarrow 0
pointwise on
S
. By (
4
) and the classical Lebesgue Monotone Convergence Theorem,

\Psi\left(\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu\right)\downarrow 0.

Since this is true for every positive order continuous functional on
\mathbb{L}_{1}
, and these functionals separate the points of
\mathbb{L}_{1}
it follows that

\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu\downarrow 0.

Now consider the general case:
\mu=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}\mu_{j}
. For each
j\in J
let
\mu_{j}=a_{j}m_{j}
. Let
(\varphi_{n})
be a sequence of
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-valued, measurable step functions so that
\varphi_{n}\downarrow 0
pointwise on
S
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
let

\varphi_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\lambda_{i}^{(n)}\chi_{A_{i}^{(n)}}.

Then

\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\lambda_{i}^{(n)}\mu(A_{i}^{(n)})=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\lambda_{i}^{(n)}\sum_{j\in J}\mu_{j}(A_{i}^{(n)})a_{j}.

By the order continuity of multiplication in
\mathbb{L}
,

\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\sum_{j\in J}\mu_{j}(A_{i}^{(n)})a_{j}\lambda_{i}^{(n)}.

It now follows from
[
10
, Theorem 15.8 (iii)]
that

\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\mu_{j}(A_{i}^{(n)})a_{j}\lambda_{i}^{(n)}=\sum_{j\in J}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu_{j}.

By the linearity and order continuity of
\Psi
,

\Psi\left(\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu\right)=\sum_{j\in J}\Psi\left(\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu_{j}\right).

We have already shown that
\displaystyle\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu_{j}\downarrow 0
, so once again by the order continuity of
\Psi
,
\displaystyle\Psi\left(\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu_{j}\right)\downarrow 0
for every
j\in J
. Therefore
1
1

1

This follows by applying the classical Lebesgue Monotone Convergence Theorem to the counting measure on
J
.

\bigwedge_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Psi\left(\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu\right)=\bigwedge_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{j\in J}\Psi\left(\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu_{j}\right)=0.

Since this holds for all positive, order continuous functionals on
\mathbb{L}_{1}
, and these functionals separate the points of
\mathbb{L}_{1}
, it follows that
\displaystyle\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\ d\mu\downarrow 0
.
∎

5.
Integration

In the rest of the paper, we fix a measure space
(S,\Sigma,\mu,\mathbb{L})
such that
\mu
is proper. The next lemma is crucial for proving well-definedness of the integral of measurable functions.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\varphi
and
({\varphi}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}})}
such that
\varphi_{n}
is increasing and
\varphi\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\varphi_{n}
pointwise in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
. Then

\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

The inequality clearly holds when
\displaystyle{\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\infty}
, so we may assume that
\displaystyle{\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\in{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
. For each
s\in S
, the assumptions imply that
(\varphi(s)-\varphi_{n}(s))\vee 0
decreases to
0
. Hence
([\varphi-\varphi_{n}]\vee\mathbf{0})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}})}
decreasing pointwise to
\mathbf{0}
. Since
\mu
is proper, it follows that

\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}(\varphi-\varphi_{n})\vee\mathbf{0}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0.

The integral is a positive operator, so

\int_{S}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}(\varphi-\varphi_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}(\varphi-\varphi_{n})\vee\mathbf{0}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0,

which shows the lemma.
∎

We now extend the integral to
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
in the obvious way: for any
f\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
, we define the integral of
f
with respect to
\mu
as

(5.1)

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\coloneqq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

where
({\varphi}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}})}
such that
\varphi_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise. If the supremum exists in
\mathbb{L}
, then we say
f
is
integrable
. In order to show that this definition is independent of the choice for the sequence
({\varphi}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, let
({\psi}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}_{1}^{+}})}
be a second sequence such that
\psi_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise. Since
\psi_{k}\leq f=\sup_{n\geq 1}\varphi_{n}
, by
Lemma

5.1
, we have
\displaystyle{\int_{S}\psi_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
. And since this is true for all
k
, we obtain
\displaystyle{\sup_{k\geq 1}\int_{S}\psi_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
. The reverse inequality holds by the same argument, and we conclude that
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
is well defined as an element of
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f_{1},\,f_{2}\colon S\to\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
be measurable functions, and let
\lambda_{1},\,\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{L}^{+}}
, then,

(1)

\int_{S}\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\lambda_{2}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\lambda_{1}\int_{S}f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\lambda_{2}\int_{S}f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
;

(2)

If
f_{1}\leq f_{2}
pointwise in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
, then
\int_{S}f_{1}\leq\int_{S}f_{2}
in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
.

Proof.

By the
\mathbb{L}
-linearity of the integral on
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
and
\mathbb{L}
-linearity of order convergence.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
. If
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\in{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
, then
f
is almost everywhere finite.

Proof.

Set
A=\{s\in S\colon f(s)=\infty\}
. Then
0\leq n\chi_{A}\leq f
pointwise for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and integrating yields
\displaystyle{0\leq n\mu(A)\leq\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\in\mathbb{L}^{+}}
. Since
\mathbb{L}
is Archimedean,
\mu(A)=0
.
∎

For any function
f\colon S\to\mathbb{L}
, its positive and negative parts are defined in the usual way, i.e.
{f^{+}}(s)=f(s)\vee 0
,
{f^{-}}(s)=[-f(s)]\vee 0
, for every
s\in S
. An
\mathbb{L}
-valued function
f
on
S
is called
measurable
if its positive and negative parts are both in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
. We denote by
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
as the collection of all
\mathbb{L}
-valued measurable functions. An
\mathbb{L}
-valued measurable function
f
is called
integrable
with respect to
\mu
, if
\int_{S}\left|f\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\in\mathbb{L}
. We denote the set of all positive integrable function as
{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
, and the set of all integrable functions as
{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
. By
Lemma

5.2
,
f
is integrable if and only if its positive and negative parts are both integrable. For any
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, we define its integral with respect to
\mu
as

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\coloneqq\int_{S}{f^{+}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_{S}{f^{-}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Invoking
Lemma

5.2
, the usual argument show that this is well defined, and it defines a positive linear map from
{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
into
\mathbb{L}
.

Remark 5.4
.

We do not consider integrable functions taking values in
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
because such a function can always be adjusted to take values in
\mathbb{L}
by
Lemma

5.3
.

The next lemma follows from approximating by step functions.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
, if
f(s)=0
for almost all
s\in S
, then
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0}
.

Remark 5.6
.

In the real valued functions case, a positive measurable function
f
is almost everywhere zero if and only if
\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
, see
[
4
, Lemma 6.5]
. For
\mathbb{L}
-valued functions,
Lemma

5.5
states that if
f\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
is almost everywhere zero, then
\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
. But the converse need not hold. Indeed, consider a step function
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}
, then
\int_{S}\left|\varphi\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
implies
\left|\lambda_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})=0
for each
i
, which holds if and only if
\lambda_{i}
and
\mu(A_{i})
are disjoint in
\mathbb{L}
for all
i
.

Proposition 5.7
.

{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
is a
\sigma
-Dedekind complete vector lattice with positive cone
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
.

Proof.

{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
is a lattice cone so
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}={{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}-{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
is a vector lattice. To show that
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
is
\sigma
-Dedekind complete, we only need to show that it is
\sigma
-monotone complete. Suppose
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is monotone increasing in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
and bounded from above by
g\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
. We define
f
as the pointwise limit of
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, i.e.
f(s)=\sup_{n}f_{n}(s)
for all
s\in S
. Then
f(s)
is in
\mathbb{L}
since
f_{n}\leq g
for all
n
.

For each
f_{n}
, we take a sequence
(\varphi_{n,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
such that
\varphi_{n,k}\uparrow f_{n}
pointwise in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in S
, we set

\psi_{n}(s)=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i,n}(s).

Then each
\psi_{n}
is in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
and
\psi_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
. So
f
is also measurable, hence it is the least upper bound of
\{f_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
is an order ideal of
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
, so it inherits the structure of a
\sigma
-Dedekind complete vector lattice from
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
Lemma

5.2
.

6.
properties of the integral

Theorem 6.1
.

[Monotone convergence theorem] Suppose
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a monotone increasing sequence in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
Let
f
be the pointwise supremum of
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, then
f\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
and

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Therefore, if
\displaystyle{\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\in\mathbb{L}}
, then
f
is integrable, so it is almost everywhere finite.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of the monotone convergence theorem in
[
4
, Theorem 6.9]
.

For each
f_{n}
, we take a sequence
(\varphi_{n,k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
such that
\varphi_{n,k}\uparrow f_{n}
pointwise in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
. We set
\psi_{n}=\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i,n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then each
\psi_{n}
is in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
and
\psi_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
. So,
f
is in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
. Hence
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sup_{n}\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
by definition, see (
5.1
).

Since
\varphi_{n}\leq f_{n}
, it follows that
\int_{S}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
, and so

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

The positivity of the integral implies that
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
. We conclude that

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

∎

The same straightforward argument as the proof of
[
4
, Corollary 6.10]
now shows the corresponding result about decreasing functions.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
and
f\in{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}})}
be such that
f_{n}(s)\downarrow f(s)
in
\overline{{\mathbb{L}^{+}}}
for almost all
s\in S
. If
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\in\mathbb{L}}
, then

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Lemma 6.3
.

[Fatou’s lemma]
Let
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be sequence in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;{\mathbb{L}^{+}})}
, then

\int_{S}\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

The proof for this lemma is similar to
[
4
, Theorem 6.12]
.

Proof.

For any
n\geq 1
, the set
\{f_{k}\}_{k=n}^{\infty}

is bounded from below by
0
. By the
\sigma
-Dedekind completeness of
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
,
\displaystyle\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k}
is measurable. Since
0\leq\displaystyle\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k}\leq f_{n}

for any
k\geq n
, by part (
2
) of
Lemma

5.2
,

\int_{S}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\int_{S}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

for every
k\geq n
. Therefore

(6.1)

\int_{S}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Applying
Theorem

6.1
to the monotone increasing sequence
\displaystyle(\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, we obtain

(6.2)

\int_{S}\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Combining (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

[Dominated convergence theorem] Let
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
f
be
\mathbb{L}
-valued measurable functions on
S
and suppose that
f_{n}(s)
order converges to
f(s)
for all
s\in S
. If there exists
g\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
such that
\left|f_{n}\right|\leq g
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

(1)

({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
f
are all integrable functions;

(2)

\displaystyle{\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}\left|f_{k}-f\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0}
;

(3)

\displaystyle{\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
.

Proof.

The proof for this theorem is similar to
[
4
, Theorem 6.13]
.

Since
f_{n}\xrightarrow{o}f
pointwise and
\left|f_{n}\right|\leq g
, it follows that
\left|f\right|\leq g
.
Now the positivity of the integral implies
(1)
.

Since
2g\geq 2g-\left|f_{k}-f\right|\geq 2g-\left|f_{k}\right|-\left|f\right|\geq 0
, it follows that
\{2g-\left|f_{k}-f\right|\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence in
{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
bounded from below by
0
. So for any
n
,
\displaystyle{h_{n}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}(2g-\left|f_{k}-f\right|)}
exists in
{{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}^{+}}
, see
Proposition

5.8
.
Clearly
({h}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a monotone increasing sequence in
{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
and

h_{n}\uparrow 2g
pointwise. So by
Theorem

6.1
and
Corollary

6.2
,

\displaystyle\int_{S}2g\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}h_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}(2g-\left|f_{k}-f\right|)\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}2g-\left|f_{k}-f\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{S}2g\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}\left|f_{k}-f\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

By adding
\displaystyle{\int_{S}2g\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
on both side of the above inequatity, we get

\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}\left|f_{k}-f\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 0.

Since the reverse inequality is obvious, the proof of part
(2)
is complete.

Since
g+f_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 1
, Fatou’s lemma shows that

\displaystyle\int_{S}g+f\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}g+f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}g+f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{S}g\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

from which we see that

(6.3)

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Since
g-f_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 1
, Fatou’s lemma shows that

\displaystyle\int_{S}g-f\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}g-f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}g-f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{S}g\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

from which we see that

(6.4)

\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Combining (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) we have

\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\int_{S}f\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigwedge_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigwedge_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigvee_{k=n}^{\infty}\int_{S}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

which completes the proof of
(3)
.
∎

7.
The
\mathbb{L}
-module
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}

An
\mathbb{L}
-valued measurable function is called
essentially bounded
if there exists
\lambda\in{\mathbb{L}^{+}}
such that
\left|f\right|\leq\lambda

\mu
-almost everywhere, i.e. the complement of
\{s\colon\left|f(s)\right|\leq\lambda\}
is contained in a zero measure subset of
\Sigma
. Let
{{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
denote as the collection of all essentially bounded functions. For any essentially bounded function we define
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}=\inf\{\lambda\colon\left|f\right|\leq\lambda\ \mu\textup{-almost everywhere}\}
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. An
\mathbb{L}
-valued measurable function
f
is called
p
-integrable
if
\left|f\right|^{p}\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
. And we denote
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}=\{f:f\ \textup{is}\ p\textup{-integrable}\}
. Since
(|f|\vee|g|)^{p}=|f|^{p}\vee|g|^{p}
, similarly to
Proposition

5.8
,
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
is also an order ideal of
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
and hence is a Dedekind complete vector lattice. As in the classical case, we will introduce an
\mathbb{L}
-seminorm on
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
by
\displaystyle{\left\|f\right\|_{p}\coloneqq\left(\int_{S}\left|f\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}
, for any
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
. The following result shows that each
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{p}
is indeed an
\mathbb{L}
-seminorm.

Lemma 7.1
.

For any
p\in[1,\infty]
,
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}\subseteq{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
. Furthermore, for any
f
and
g
in
{{\mathcal{S}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L}_{1})}
, and for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
,

(1)

\left\|f+g\right\|_{p}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{p}+\left\|g\right\|_{p}
;

(2)

\left\|\lambda f\right\|_{p}=\left|\lambda\right|\left\|f\right\|_{p}
;

(3)

\left\|fg\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{p}\left\|g\right\|_{q}
, where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. ( we define
\frac{1}{\infty}=0
.)

Proof.

Since measurable step functions are bounded, hence essentially bounded, (
1
) and (
2
) for
p=\infty
are direct consequences from the properties of absolute value in vector lattices.

If
p\in[1,\infty)
, then for any measurable step function
f
,
\int_{S}\left|f\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
is a finite sum in
\mathbb{L}
, hence
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
.

Take any measurable step functions
f
and
g
, then we can write

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\chi_{A_{i}},\quad g=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}

for some
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
in
\mathbb{L}
.
Then, using
[
9
, Theorem 4.4]
in the fourth step, we get

\displaystyle\left\|f+g\right\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{p}\times p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left|\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left|\alpha_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}+\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left|\beta_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}\right|^{p}\mu(A_{i})\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}+\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}\right|^{p}\mu(A_{i})\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\left\|f\right\|_{p}+\left\|g\right\|_{p}.

We also obtain that

\left\|\lambda f\right\|_{p}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\lambda\alpha_{i}\right|^{p}\mu(A_{i})\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left|\lambda\right|\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}\right|^{p}\mu(A_{i})\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left|\lambda\right|\left\|f\right\|_{p}.

Using
[
9
, Theorem 4.3]
in the third step, we get

\displaystyle\left\|fg\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{p}}\times\left|\beta_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{p}}\right|^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\times\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}\mu(A_{i})^{\frac{1}{q}}\right|^{q}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{i}\right|^{p}\mu(A_{i})\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\times\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}\right|^{q}\mu(A_{i})\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle=\left\|f\right\|_{p}\left\|g\right\|_{q}\qed

Lemma 7.2
.

For any positive functions
({f}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, if
f_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise, then
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f_{n}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\uparrow\int_{S}f^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
, so that
\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{p}\uparrow\left\|f\right\|_{p}
.

Proof.

If
f_{n}\uparrow f
pointwise, then by
[
9
, Lemma 4.1 (ii)]

f_{n}^{p}\uparrow f^{p}
pointwise. The monotone convergence theorem,
Theorem

6.1
, implies that
\displaystyle{\int_{S}f_{n}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\uparrow\int_{S}f^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu}
.
Again by
[
9
, Lemma 4.1 (ii)]
, we have
\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{p}\uparrow\left\|f\right\|_{p}
.
∎

The results of
Lemma

7.1
can now be extended to
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
by the order continuity of the
f
-algebra operations and
Lemma

7.2
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Suppose
f
and
g
are
\mathbb{L}
-valued measurable functions, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
, then for any
p\geq 1
,

(1)

if
f,g\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, then
f+g\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, and

\left\|f+g\right\|_{p}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{p}+\left\|g\right\|_{p};

(2)

if
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, then
\lambda f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, and

\left\|\lambda f\right\|_{p}=\left|\lambda\right|\left\|f\right\|_{p};

(3)

if
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
,
g\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{q}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
, then
fg\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
and

\left\|fg\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{p}\left\|g\right\|_{q}.

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
Proposition

7.3
shows that
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{p}
is an
\mathbb{L}
-valued seminorm on
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
. We let
{{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
be the kernel of
\left\|\ \right\|_{p}
, i.e.
{{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}=\{f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}\colon\left\|f\right\|_{p}=0\}
. Obviously,
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}=\{f\colon\left|f\right|\ \textup{ is zero}\ \mu\textup{-almost everywhere}\}
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
1\leq p<q\leq\infty
. Then

(1)

{{\mathcal{L}}^{q}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}\subseteq{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
;

(2)

{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}\subseteq{{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}={{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
.

Proof.

Take any real numbers
p,q
satisfying
1\leq p<q\leq\infty
and
\frac{p}{q}+\frac{q-p}{q}=1
.
Suppose
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
so that
\left|f\right|^{p}\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, then by using Hölder inequality ((
3
) in
Proposition

7.3
) on
\left|f\right|^{p}
and the constant
1
function, we get

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{p}^{p}=\left\|\left|f\right|^{p}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\ \left|f\right|^{p}\cdot\chi_{S}\ \right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left|f\right|^{p}\right\|_{\frac{q}{p}}\cdot\left\|\chi_{S}\right\|_{\frac{q}{q-p}}

\displaystyle=\left\|f\right\|_{q}^{p}\mu(S)^{\frac{q-p}{q}}.

We obtain

\left\|f\right\|_{p}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{q}\cdot\mu(S)^{\frac{q-p}{qp}}.

Therefore

(7.1)

{{\mathcal{L}}^{q}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}\subseteq{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}

and

(7.2)

{{\mathcal{N}}_{q}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}\subseteq{{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}.

Next, we take
h\in{{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
and
1\leq p<\infty
.
If
h
is a measurable step function
h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}
, then
\displaystyle{\int_{S}\left|h\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0}
implies that
\left|\lambda_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})=0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
. Hence
\left|\lambda_{i}\right|^{p}\mu(A_{i})=\left|\lambda_{i}\right|^{p-1}\cdot\left|\lambda_{i}\right|\mu(A_{i})=0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
. Therefore,
\left\|h\right\|_{q}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\lambda_{i}\right|^{q}\mu(A_{i})=0
.

For the general case we approximate
|h|
by measurable step functions and then
Lemma

7.2
yields
\left\|h\right\|_{p}=0
. Hence

(7.3)

{{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}\subseteq{{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}.

Combining (
7.2
) and (
7.3
) yields

{{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}={{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}.

The final statement
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}\subseteq{{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
is a direct consequence of
Lemma

5.5
.
∎

By
Theorem

7.4

{{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}={{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
for any
1\leq p<\infty
and we will these sets by
\mathcal{N}
. It is immediate that
\mathcal{N}
is an order ideal in
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
and that
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
is an order ideal in
{{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
. Therefore the quotient spaces
{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}={{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}/\mathcal{N}
and
{{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}={{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}/{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
are vector lattices. We shall write
[f]
for the image of
f\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
(
1\leq p\leq\infty
) under the quotient map. As in the classical case, the map defined by
\left\|[f]\right\|_{p}=\left\|f\right\|_{p}
for any
[f]\in{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
is an
\mathbb{L}
-norm.

The proof that
{{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
is sequentially complete is as in the classical case; modulo almost everywhere equivalence it is
[
9
, Theorem 3.24]
. The situation for
{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
is more complicated. We will first show that every absolutely converging series in
{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
converges.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Then any absolutely converging series in
{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
converges.

Proof.

Let
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, suppose
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{p}=\bigvee_{N=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{p}
exists in
{\mathbb{L}^{+}}
, and we denote it limit by
\lambda
.
Let
G_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|f_{k}\right|
, then
({G}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a monotone increasing sequence in
{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
. We set
G
as the pointwise limit of
({G}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, then
G_{n}^{p}\uparrow G^{p}
pointwise. By the monotone convergence theorem (
Theorem

6.1
),
G
and
G^{p}
are measurable and

\int_{S}G^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}G_{n}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Using (
1
) of
Proposition

7.3
we obtain

\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}G_{n}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{S}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|f_{k}\right|\right)^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\bigvee_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{S}\left|f_{k}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\lambda^{p}.

Hence
G^{p}\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, i.e.
G\in{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
.

By
Lemma

5.3

G(s)\in\mathbb{L}
almost everywhere, so for
A=\{s\colon G(s)\in\mathbb{L}\}
we have that
\mu(A^{c})=0
. We define, for all
n\geq 1
,

F_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}\chi_{A}+0\chi_{A^{c}}

then each
F_{n}
is in
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
and
\left|F_{n}\right|\leq G
.
Since

\left|F_{m}(s)-F_{n}(s)\right|\leq\begin{cases}\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}\left|f_{k}(s)\right|,&s\in A,\\
0,&s\in A^{c},\end{cases}

\{F_{n}(s)\}_{n}
order converges in
\mathbb{L}
for all
s\in S
. We let
F
to be the pointwise limit of
({F}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
, i.e.

F(s)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(s)\chi_{A}(s),\ &s\in A,\\
0,&s\in A^{c}.\end{cases}

Since
{{\mathcal{M}}(S,\Sigma;\mathbb{L})}
is
\sigma
-Dedekind complete (see
Proposition

5.7
),
F
is measurable and
\left|F\right|\leq G
.

By
[
9
, Lemma 4.2
(ii)
]

\left|F-F_{n}\right|^{p}\rightarrow 0
pointwise, and
\left|F-F_{n}\right|^{p}\leq(2G)^{p}
, so by the dominated convergence theorem (
Theorem

6.4
)
\int_{S}\left|F-F_{n}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\rightarrow 0
.

We will now verify that
F
is the
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{p}
-limit of
\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}
.
Note that

\displaystyle\left\|F-\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}\right\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\left|F-\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\left|F\chi_{A}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}\chi_{A}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{S}\left|\mathbf{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}\chi_{A^{c}}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

By the above we have that

\int_{S}\left|F\chi_{A}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}\chi_{A}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{S}\left|F-F_{n}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\rightarrow 0,

and furthermore,

0\leq\int_{S}\left|0-\sum_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}\chi_{A^{c}}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\int_{S}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|f_{k}\right|^{p}\chi_{A^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{p}^{p}\mu(A^{c})=0.

Therefore
\left\|F-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}\right\|_{p}^{p}\rightarrow 0
and so
\left\|F-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}\right\|_{p}\rightarrow 0
by
[
9
, Lemma 4.2
(ii)
]
.
∎

To conclude sequential completeness of
{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(S,\Sigma,\mu;\mathbb{L})}
, we will now investigate when an
X
-normed space in which every absolutely converging sequence converges is sequentially complete.

8.
Sequential completeness

We will need the following theorem about locally solid spaces, see
[
1
, Theorem 2.21]
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
E
be a locally solid Riesz space,
(x_{\alpha})
an increasing net in
E
and
x\in E
. If
x_{\alpha}\to x
then
x_{\alpha}\uparrow x
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a Dedekind complete vector unital
f
-algebra satisfying the countable sup property which admits a
\sigma
-order continuous completely metrizable locally solid topology. Let
X
be an
\mathbb{L}
-normed space such that every absolutely converging series in
X
converges. Then
X
is sequentially complete.

Proof.

Let
d
be a
\sigma
-order continuous complete metric on
\mathbb{L}
. Let
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a Cauchy sequence in
X
(see
[
9
, Definition 3.3]
). Since
\mathbb{L}
has the countable sup property, there exists a sequence
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathbb{L}^{+}
with
\inf_{k}\varepsilon_{k}=0
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\left\|x_{n}-x_{m}\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
for all
n,m\geq n_{k}
. By passing to finite infima we may assume that
\varepsilon_{k}
decreases to
0
. Since
d
is
\sigma
-order continuous,
d(0,\varepsilon_{k})\to 0
, and by passing to a subsequence we may assume that
d(0,\varepsilon_{k})<2^{-k}
so that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}
converges by completeness of
d
. By
Theorem

8.1
,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}
converges in order.

We may assume that
(n_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
is strictly increasing and so
(x_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a subsequence of
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. For
k\geq 1
, define
y_{k}=x_{n_{k}}-x_{n_{k-1}}
(where
x_{0}=0
). Since

\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\left\|y_{k}\right\|=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\left\|x_{n_{k}}-x_{n_{k-1}}\right\|\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k-1}

which converges in order,
\sum_{k}y_{k}
is an absolutely converging series hence it converges by assumption. The partial sums of
\sum_{k}y_{k}
equals
x_{n_{k}}
and so
(x_{n_{k}})_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges. Therefore the Cauchy sequence
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
has a converging subsequence, and the proof that such a sequence converges is exactly as in the classical case.
∎

Corollary 8.3

Let
