Abstract
. Recently, Bochi and Laskawiec constructed an example of a set of matrices { A , B } \{A,B\} having two different (up to cyclic permutations of factors) spectrum maximizing products, A ​ A ​ B ​ A ​ B ​ B AABABB and B ​ B ​ A ​ B ​ A ​ A BBABAA . In this paper, we identify a class of matrix sets for which the existence of at least one spectrum maximizing product with an odd number of factors automatically entails the existence of another spectrum maximizing product. Moreover, in addition to the Bochi–Laskawiec example, the number of factors of the same name (factors of the form A A or B B ) in these matrix products turns out to be different. The efficiency of the proposed approach is confirmed by constructing an example of a set of 2 × 2 2{\times}2 matrices { A , B } \{A,B\} that has spectrum maximizing products of the form B ​ A ​ A BAA and B ​ B ​ A BBA .

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathscr{A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\}
be a set of
m
real
d{\times}d
matrices and
{\|\cdot\|}
be a norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. With every finite ordered tuple of symbols

\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n})\in{\{1,\ldots,m\}}^{n},\qquad n\geq 1,

we associate the matrix

A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=A_{\nu_{n}}\cdots A_{\nu_{2}}A_{\nu_{1}}

(1.1)

and define two numerical values:

\rho({\mathscr{A}})=\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}({\mathscr{A}}),\qquad\bar{\rho}({\mathscr{A}})=\varlimsup_{n\to\infty}\bar{\rho}_{n}({\mathscr{A}}),

(1.2)

where

\rho_{n}({\mathscr{A}})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in{\{1,\ldots,m\}}^{n}}\|A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\|^{1/n},\qquad\bar{\rho}_{n}({\mathscr{A}})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in{\{1,\ldots,m\}}^{n}}{\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}^{1/n},

and
\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
denotes the spectral radius of the matrix
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
.

The first limit in (
1.2
), which actually does not depend on the choice of the norm
\|\cdot\|
, was introduced by Rota and Strang
[
21
]
, and the second somewhat later by Daubechies and Lagarias
[
8
]
. Both quantities in (
1.2
) are analogues of the well-known Gelfand formula
[
10
]
for the spectral radius of the matrix, and therefore the first of them was called
joint
, and the second
generalized spectral radius
of a set of matrices
\mathscr{A}
. For bounded sets of matrices
\mathscr{A}
the quantities
\rho({\mathscr{A}})
and
\bar{\rho}({\mathscr{A}})
coincide with each other
[
3
,
7
]
and in this case

\bar{\rho}_{n}({\mathscr{A}})\leq\bar{\rho}({\mathscr{A}})=\rho({\mathscr{A}})\leq\rho_{n}({\mathscr{A}})\qquad\forall~n.

(1.3)

Traditionally, the possibility of explicitly calculating the generalized spectral radius is associated with the fulfillment of the so-called
finiteness hypothesis
, which assumes that the second limit (
1.2
) is always achieved at some finite value of
n
, i.e., the leftmost inequality in (
1.3
) turns into an equality for some
n
. This hypothesis was put forward in
[
17
]
, but subsequently refuted
[
6
]
. Later, alternative counterexamples appeared
[
4
,
15
]
. The first “explicit” counterexample to the finiteness conjecture was constructed in
[
13
]
, and general methods for constructing counterexamples of this kind were later developed in
[
19
,
14
]
.

Although the finiteness conjecture has been refuted in the general case, the question of whether it holds for some specific sets of matrices remains relevant. Recently, a number of significant results have been obtained in this direction
[
5
,
18
,
23
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\ldots,\nu_{n})
, be a product of the form (
1.1
). Following
[
12
]
, we say that
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is a
spectrum maximizing product
for the set of matrices
\mathscr{A}
if

\bar{\rho}({\mathscr{A}})=\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{\frac{1}{n}}.

When defining spectrum maximizing products, one often adds the requirement that the matrix
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is not a power of any other (shorter) spectrum maximizing product. In this work we do not require this.

Proving that some matrix product
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
of the form (
1.1
) is spectrum maximizing is, as a rule, quite difficult. The following reasoning can help here: suppose we managed to find a vector norm
\|\cdot\|
such that for each matrix
A_{i}\in\mathscr{A}
the inequality

\|A_{i}x\|\leq\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{\frac{1}{n}}\|x\|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.4)

holds. Then, by the definition of the joint spectral radius, the inequality
\rho({\mathscr{A}})\leq\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{\frac{1}{n}}
will hold, whence, by (
1.3
) it follows that

\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{\frac{1}{n}}\leq\bar{\rho}({\mathscr{A}})=\rho({\mathscr{A}})\leq\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{\frac{1}{n}}.

Thus,
\rho(A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{\frac{1}{n}}=\bar{\rho}({\mathscr{A}})
, which implies that
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=A_{\nu_{n}}\cdots A_{\nu_{2}}A_{\nu_{1}}
is the spectrum maximizing product. In this case, inequality (
1.4
) will take the following form:

\|A_{i}x\|\leq\bar{\rho}({\mathscr{A}})\|x\|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.5)

A norm in which inequality (
1.5
) holds is called
extremal
for the set of matrices
\mathscr{A}
. This term apparently first appeared in
[
2
]
. Later, in
[
1
]
extremal norms were used as a basic tool for analyzing the growth rate of matrix products. Subsequently, this approach was widely used in numerous works, among which we highlight
[
24
]
. More detailed information about the growth rate of the norms of matrix products can be obtained in the case when the norm
\|\cdot\|
for some
\rho
satisfies the equality

\max\left\{\|A_{0}x\|,\|A_{1}x\|,\ldots,\|A_{m-1}x\|\right\}=\rho\|x\|

(1.6)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A norm satisfying condition (
1.6
) is usually called the
Barabanov norm
corresponding to the set of matrices
\mathscr{A}
. In
[
1
, Thm. 2]
it is shown that such a norm exists for
“almost all” sets of matrices
\mathscr{A}
, but unfortunately it is defined by some computationally nonconstructive limit procedure. Moreover, the equality (
1.6
) can be satisfied if and only if
\rho=\rho(\mathscr{A})
, and therefore in view of (
1.3
) any Barabanov norm is extremal.

The impetus for this work was the article
[
5
]
, in which an example of a set of matrices
\{A,B\}
was constructed that has two different (up to cyclic permutations of factors) spectrum maximizing products of matrices,
AABABB
and
BBABAA
. Below we propose another way to construct matrix sets, for which the number of spectrum maximizing products with an odd number of factors, if any, automatically turns out to be at least two. Moreover, in addition to the example from
[
5
]
, the number of factors of the same name (factors
A
or
B
) in these matrix products is different.

In our approach, we develop the idea from
[
20
]
(proposed and used there, but not explicitly formulated by the authors) that the very structure of the set of matrices can significantly contribute to the answer to this question. Recall that in
[
20
, Prop. 18]
the so-called “symmetric” sets of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{m}\}
were considered, which have the property that, together with each matrix
A_{i}
, this set also contains the transposed matrix
A_{i}^{\mathtt{t}}
. This (symmetry) turned out to be sufficient for the Euclidean norm to be an extremal norm for a given family of matrices, and the generalized spectral radius was achieved on one of the matrix products of the form
{A_{i}^{\mathtt{t}}A_{i}}
.

In Section
2
(Theorem
2.5
and Corollary
2.6
) we will present the details of the implementation of the corresponding idea only to the extent that will be sufficient for construction in Section
3
of an example of a set of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
for which the spectrum maximizing products have the forms
BAA
and
BBA
(up to cyclic permutations of factors). The proof that in this example the matrix products
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=BAA
or
A_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=BBA
are spectrum maximizing will be done by constructing an appropriate norm
\|\cdot\|
, in which these matrix products satisfy inequality (
1.4
).

2.
Main result

Let’s start by defining the key concept in this section. We present this concept, as well as the following Lemma
2.2
, in accordance with the recommendations of an anonymous reviewer, who drew our attention to the fact that the use of Friedland’s theorem
[
9
, Thm. 2.2]
can significantly simplify the study of the problem of simultaneous similarity of sets of matrices.

Definition 2.1
.

A set of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
,
A\neq B
, will be called
\tau
-permutable
if there exists a mapping
\tau(X):=S^{-1}XS
that performs a simultaneous similarity transformation between the sets of matrices
\{A,B\}
and
\{B,A\}
, i.e., such that

\tau(A)=B,\quad\tau(B)=A.

In this definition, the matrix
S
is naturally assumed to be nonsingular. Obviously, a set of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
can be
\tau
-permutable only in the case when the matrices
A
and
B
are
isospectral
, i.e., their spectra coincide:

\sigma(A)=\sigma(B).

If in Definition
2.1
we take the mapping
\tau(X):=X^{\mathtt{t}}
as
\tau
, then the set of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
will be so-called “symmetric”. Sets of matrices of this kind, as we have already mentioned, were introduced and studied in
[
20
, Prop. 18]
.

In what follows, we will focus on irreducible matrix sets consisting of a pair of real matrices
A
and
B
of dimension
2{\times}2
. Recall that a set of matrices is called
irreducible
if its matrices do not have common invariant subspaces, with the exception of the zero space and the entire space. For such sets of matrices, the
\tau
-permutability condition becomes very simple.

Lemma 2.2
.

An irreducible set of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
is permutable under some similarity transformation
\tau
if and only if

\tr(A)=\tr(B),\quad\det(A)=\det(B).

Proof.

We use the theorem of
Friedland
[
9
, Thm. 2.2]
, according to which irreducible sets
\{A,B\}
and
\{\tilde{A},\tilde{B}\}
of
2{\times}2
matrices are simultaneously similar if and only if the
5
-tuples

\bigl(\tr(A),~\tr(A^{2}),~\tr(B),~\tr(B^{2}),~\tr(AB)\bigr)

and

\bigl(\tr(\tilde{A}),~\tr(\tilde{A}^{2}),~\tr(\tilde{B}),~\tr(\tilde{B}^{2}),~\tr(\tilde{A}\tilde{B})\bigr)

coincide. Then the sets of matrices
\{A,B\}
and
\{B,A\}
are simultaneously similar if and only if

\bigl(\tr(A),\tr(A^{2}),\tr(B),\tr(B^{2}),\tr(AB)\bigr)=\bigl(\tr(B),\tr(B^{2}),\tr(A),\tr(A^{2}),\tr(BA)\bigr).

Now let us note that
\tr(AB)=\tr(BA)
, and for
2{\times}2
matrices the following equalities hold:

\tr(A^{2})=\tr(A)^{2}-2\det(A),\quad\tr(B^{2})=\tr(B)^{2}-2\det(B).

This implies that the last two
5
-tuples coincide if and only if
\tr(A)=\tr(B)
and
\det(A)=\det(B)
. The lemma is proved.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let the set of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
be
\tau
-permutable under some mapping

M=M_{k}M_{k-1}\cdots M_{1},\qquad M_{i}\in\mathscr{A}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,2,\ldots,k,

(2.5)

the spectrum of the matrix
\tau(M)
coincides with the spectrum of the matrix

If the number of factors

in (

have different numbers of factors of the form
A
(as well as of the form

We will put the proof of the theorem in Section

. We also formulate a consequence of this theorem that does not require special proof and relates to spectrum maximizing products of matrices.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let, under the conditions of Theorem
2.5
, the product of matrices (
2.5

\tau(M)
is also a spectrum maximizing product other than
M
(up to cyclic permutations), and the matrices
M
and
\tau(M)
have different numbers of factors of the same name, i.e., factors
A
(and also
B
).

.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
be a

3.
Basic example

In this section, we consider the matrix set
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
from Example
2.4
, which is
\tau
-permutable with respect to a mapping
\tau
of the form (
2.4
). Note that the mapping
\tau(X):=S^{-1}XS
is
multiplicative
, i.e.,

\tau(X_{k}X_{k-1}\cdots X_{1})=\tau(X_{k})\tau(X_{k-1})\cdots\tau(X_{1})

for any set of matrices
X_{1},X_{2}\ldots,X_{k}
. Therefore

\tau(BAA)=\tau(B)\tau(A)\tau(A)=ABB.

Then, by Theorem
2.5
, the matrices
BAA
and
ABB
are isospectral:
\sigma(BAA)=\sigma(ABB)
. Since the matrix
BAA
is isospectral to the products of its cyclic permutations
AAB
and
ABA
, and the matrix
ABB
is isospectral to the products of its cyclic permutations
BBA
and
BAB
, then all triple products of the matrices
A
and
B
, other than the products
AAA
and
BBB
, are also isospectral:

\sigma(BAA)=\sigma(AAB)=\sigma(ABA)=\sigma(ABB)=\sigma(BBA)=\sigma(BAB).

Hence, similar equalities also hold for the spectral radii of the indicated matrices:

\lambda:=\rho(BAA)=\rho(AAB)=\rho(ABA)=\rho(ABB)=\rho(BBA)=\rho(BAB).

Further we will assume that in the equalities defining the matrices
A
and
B
in Example
2.4
, the value of
\varphi
is determined by the equality
\varphi=\frac{2\pi}{3}
. In this case, the matrices
A
and
B
take the form (
2.6
) and, as is easy to calculate,

BAA=\begin{pmatrix}[c]\varkappa^{2}&0\\[6.0pt]
\varkappa-\frac{1}{\varkappa}&\frac{1}{\varkappa^{2}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad BBA=\begin{pmatrix}[c]\varkappa^{2}&\frac{1}{\varkappa}-\varkappa\\[6.0pt]
0&\frac{1}{\varkappa^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.1)

Both of these matrices have unit determinant and the same trace:

\tr(BAA)=\tr(BBA)=\varkappa^{2}+\frac{1}{\varkappa^{2}}
. Therefore

\lambda=\varkappa^{2}

(3.2)

is their common maximum eigenvalue.

Our goal is to show in the remainder of this section that in this case the generalized spectral radius
\bar{\rho}(\mathscr{A})
of a set of matrices (
2.6
) is given by the equality

\bar{\rho}(\mathscr{A})=\lambda^{1/3},

and the products of matrices
BAA
and
BBA
with matrices
A
and
B
of the form (
2.6
) are spectrum maximizing. To do this, following the scheme outlined in the introduction, we will show that for the set of matrices (
2.6
), a norm
\|\cdot\|
can be found in which the inequalities

\|Ax\|,\|Bx\|\leq\lambda^{1/3}\|x\|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(3.3)

are satisfied. Technically, it will be easier to do this not for the set of matrices
\mathscr{A}=\{A,B\}
, but for the “normalized” set of matrices
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}=\{\tilde{A},\tilde{B}\}
, where

\tilde{A}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{1/3}}A,\qquad\tilde{B}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{1/3}}B.

(3.4)

In terms of the matrices
\tilde{A}
and
\tilde{B}

3.3

\|\tilde{A}x\|,\|\tilde{B}x\|\leq\|x\|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(3.5)

In addition, since the matrices

and the remaining triple products of the matrices

\tilde{B}

\rho(\tilde{B}\tilde{A}\tilde{A})=\rho(\tilde{A}\tilde{A}\tilde{B})=\rho(\tilde{A}\tilde{B}\tilde{A})=\rho(\tilde{A}\tilde{B}\tilde{B})=\rho(\tilde{B}\tilde{B}\tilde{A})=\rho(\tilde{B}\tilde{A}\tilde{B})=1.

(4.3)

Consequently, all vertices
v_{2},v_{3},\ldots,v_{6}
belong to the open half-plane

\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}(v_{1})
:

v_{2},v_{3},\ldots,v_{6}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}(v_{1}).

It also follows from (
4.3
) that for
i=2,3,\ldots,5
each vector
v_{i+1}
is obtained from
v_{i}
by a clockwise rotation by some angle
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
and subsequent appropriate compression or expansion. This means that, when going clockwise around the origin, the vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{6}
of the polygon
S
appear in the order of their indexing. Considering that the vertices
v_{7},v_{8},\ldots,v_{12}
are obtained from
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{6}
by mirroring relative to the origin, we can repeat the same conclusion regarding their order that was made for the vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{6}
. And then a similar conclusion can be made for the entire set of vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{12}
, and this conclusion does not depend on the choice of parameters
\varkappa>1
and
\mu>0
.

4.3.
Convexity of the polygon
S

As shown in the previous section, the vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{6}
of the polygon
S
appear in the order of their indexing when going clockwise around the origin. Therefore, to prove the convexity of the polygon
S
, it suffices to show that each of the vertices
v_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,12
, does not belong to the interior of the triangle
\triangle v_{i-1}v_{i+1}0
, where it is assumed that
v_{0}=v_{12}
and
v_{13}=v_{1}
. By Lemma
3.6
the corresponding “nonbelonging” conditions are equivalent to the inequalities

h(v_{i-1},v_{i+1},v_{i})\geq 1,\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,6.

Let us write down the corresponding conditions, as well as their reformulations in terms of the parameter
\mu
:

\displaystyle h(v_{12},v_{2},v_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varkappa^{4/3}+1)\mu}{\varkappa^{2}+1}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{1}(\varkappa):=\frac{\varkappa^{2}+1}{\varkappa^{4/3}+1},

\displaystyle h(v_{1},v_{3},v_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varkappa^{2}+1)(\varkappa^{2}+\varkappa^{2/3})}{(\varkappa^{4}+\varkappa^{2}+1)\mu}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{2}(\varkappa):=\frac{(\varkappa^{2}+1)(\varkappa^{2}+\varkappa^{2/3})}{\varkappa^{4}+\varkappa^{2}+1},

\displaystyle h(v_{2},v_{4},v_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varkappa^{8/3}+1)\mu}{\varkappa^{2/3}(\varkappa^{2}+1)}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{3}(\varkappa):=\frac{\varkappa^{2/3}(\varkappa^{2}+1)}{\varkappa^{8/3}+1},

\displaystyle h(v_{3},v_{5},v_{4})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varkappa^{2}+1)(\varkappa^{2}+\varkappa^{2/3})}{(\varkappa^{4}+\varkappa^{2}+1)\mu}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{4}(\varkappa):=\frac{(\varkappa^{2}+1)(\varkappa^{2}+\varkappa^{2/3})}{\varkappa^{4}+\varkappa^{2}+1},

\displaystyle h(v_{4},v_{6},v_{5})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varkappa^{4/3}+1)\mu}{\varkappa^{2}+1}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{5}(\varkappa):=\frac{\varkappa^{2}+1}{\varkappa^{4/3}+1},

\displaystyle h(v_{5},v_{7},v_{6})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\varkappa^{2}+1)(\varkappa^{8/3}+1)}{(\varkappa^{4}+\varkappa^{2}+1)\mu}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{6}(\varkappa):=\frac{(\varkappa^{2}+1)(\varkappa^{8/3}+1)}{\varkappa^{4}+\varkappa^{2}+1}.

It is easy to check that for all
\varkappa>1
and
\mu>0
the following inequalities hold:

\omega_{3}(\varkappa)\leq\omega_{1}(\varkappa)=\omega_{5}(\varkappa)\leq\mu_{1}(\varkappa),\quad\mu_{2}(\varkappa)\leq\omega_{2}(\varkappa)=\omega_{4}(\varkappa)\leq\omega_{6}(\varkappa).

Then, for values of the parameters
\varkappa
and
\mu
satisfying (
3.19
) and (
3.20
), the inequalities

\omega_{3}(\varkappa)\leq\omega_{1}(\varkappa)=\omega_{5}(\varkappa)\leq\mu_{1}(\varkappa)\leq\mu\leq\mu_{2}(\varkappa)\leq\omega_{2}(\varkappa)=\omega_{4}(\varkappa)\leq\omega_{6}(\varkappa)

are also satisfied and, therefore, for these values of the parameters
\varkappa
and
\mu
, the polygon
S
is convex.

6.
Conclusion

The motivation for this work was the example constructed in
[
5
]
of a set of matrices
\{A,B\}
that has two different (up to cyclic permutations of factors) spectrum maximizing products,
AABABB
and
BBABAA
. The specific feature of this example (and other similar examples in
[
5
]
) was that the number of factors
A
(or
B
) in both products turned out to be the same. In this regard, the question arose: are there pairs of spectrum maximizing products with different numbers of factors
A
(or
B
) in these products? This paper gives a positive answer to this question. For this purpose, a method for constructing matrix sets
\{A,B\}
is proposed, different from the method used in
[
5
]
, in which the number of spectrum maximizing products with an odd number of factors automatically turns out to be at least two, and the number of factors of the form
A
(or
B
) in such products is different. Using this approach, we constructed an example of a set of
2{\times}2
matrices
\{A,B\}
, for which the spectrum maximizing products have the form
BAA
and
BBA
. Unlike
[
5
]
, where the construction of the corresponding examples essentially used the numerical implementation of the “polytope algorithm”
[
11
,
22
]
, in this work, analytical methods were used to construct the examples.
