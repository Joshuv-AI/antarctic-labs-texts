Fast Estimations of Lower Bounds on Hitting Time of Elitist Evolutionary Algorithms
Jun He, Siang Yew Chong, Xin Yao

Abstract
The fitness level method is an easy-to-use tool for estimating the hitting time of elitist evolutionary algorithms. Recently, linear lower and upper bounds by fitness levels have been constructed. But these bounds require recursive computation, which makes them difficult to use in practice. We address this shortcoming with a new directed graph (digraph) method that does not require recursive computation and significantly simplifies the calculation of coefficients in the lower bound. In the method, we select a sub-digraph and divide it into fitness levels, then construct an explicit formula for computing the linear lower bound coefficients using transition probabilities restricted to the subdigraph. A major advantage of the new method is the derivation of tight lower bounds on fitness functions with shortcuts, which are difficult to achieve using previous fitness methods. We use three examples (FullyDeceptive, TwoMax1 and Deceptive) to demonstrate that each new lower bound is tight, but previous lower bounds are not. Our work significantly extends the fitness level method from addressing simple fitness functions without shortcuts to more complex functions with shortcuts.

The number of generations that an evolutionary algorithm (EA) takes to find an optimal solution for the first time is called hitting time

,

,

.

This method works by splitting the search space into several ranks

based on fitness values from high to low, where the highest rank
S_{0}
represents the set of optimal solutions. Each rank is called a fitness level. Then, the hitting time when an EA starts from the fitness level
S_{k}
is estimated by a bound

. Compared with other tools, the biggest advantage of the fitness level method is that it provides explicit formulas to quickly estimate hitting time bounds from transition probabilities
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. The formulas can be expressed in linear form as follows
[
15
]
:

\mbox{lower bound }d_{k}=\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\frac{c_{k,\ell}}{\displaystyle\max_{X_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}}p(X_{\ell},S_{0}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell-1})},

(1)

\mbox{upper bound }d_{k}=\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\frac{c_{k,\ell}}{\displaystyle\min_{X_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}}p(X_{\ell},S_{0}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell-1})},

(2)

where
c_{k,\ell}\in[0,1]
is a coefficient,

is a state in level

, and

is the transition probability from
X_{\ell}
to levels
S_{0}\cup\cdots\cup S_{\ell-1}
. For deterministic initialization, the coefficient
c_{k,k}=1
. There are three ways to assign values to other coefficients

, namely
c_{k,\ell}=c
(constant),
c_{\ell}
(depending on

but not on
k
) or
c_{k,\ell}
(depending both on
k
and
\ell
).
Linear bounds with coefficients
c
,
c_{\ell}
and

are called Type-
c
,
c_{\ell}
and

bounds, respectively
[
15
]
.

In the fitness level method, the main research is to construct an explicit formula to quickly calculate
c_{k,\ell}
.
Wegener
[
5
]
applied a constant coefficient
c_{k,\ell}=1
to the linear upper bound (
2
). In many cases this trivial coefficient is sufficient to obtain a tight upper bound. Wegener
[
5
]
also considered trivial constant coefficients

and

in the lower bound. Unfortunately, the constant
0
is not useful for finding a tight lower bound. Several attempts have been made to tighten the lower bound afterwards. Sudholt
[
6
]
assigned a constant coefficient

(called viscosity). Using a constant

, tight lower time bounds are obtained for the (1+1) EA that maximizes various unimodal functions such as LeadingOnes, OneMax, and long
k

[
7
]
considered random initialization and assigned coefficients

(called visit probability). Using the coefficient

, tight lower bounds of the (1+1) EA on LeadingOnes, OneMax, long
k
-paths and jump function are obtained.

The main disadvantage of Type-
c
and
c_{\ell}
lower bounds is that they become loose on fitness landscapes with shortcuts. This is shown in
[
15
]
and also later in Subsections
IV-C
and
IV-D
. Since most fitness functions have shortcuts, this means that traditional fitness level methods have significant limitations.

To address this problem, drift analysis combined with fitness levels is recently proposed in
[
15
]
. Linear lower and upper bounds are constructed from fitness levels. However, an issue with these bounds is that coefficients are calculated recursively. Such recursive calculation is inconvenient in practice.

In this paper, we aim to calculate lower bound coefficients non-recursively. Our idea is based on directed graphs (digraphs)
[
16
]
, which helps better understand fitness landscapes with shortcuts. The behavior of an elitist EA on the search space with fitness levels can be described as a Markov chain on a digraph
[
17
,
18
]
. In the digraph, a vertex represents a fitness level, and an arc (directed edge) represents a transition between two fitness levels.

To obtain a lower bound on hitting time, it is sufficient to consider a sub-digraph with a subset of non-optimal states. It is obvious the time an EA spends on a subset of non-optimal states is less than that on the set of all non-optimal states. We propose a new method to non-recursively calculate lower bound coefficients using the transition probabilities in the sub-digraph. Taking a path through all vertices in the sub-digraph, we obtain an explicit expression for the coefficients
c_{k,\ell}
in terms of the conditional transition probabilities along the path. This non-recursive calculation of the coefficients
c_{k,\ell}
is faster than the previous recursive calculation
[
15
]
.

On first thought, using sub-digraphs to estimate lower bounds might be worse than using the entire digraph. But interestingly, there are some fitness landscape with specific structures where the use of sub-digraphs lets us obtain tighter lower bounds compared to using the entire digraph. These structures are represented as shortcuts in digraphs (which are defined in Subsection
IV
). Such digraphs describe elitist EAs skipping all the fitness levels associated with the shortcut with conditional probability
o(1)
. These shortcuts constitute small coefficients that can be computationally ignored for linear lower bound estimation.

We compare our new digraph method with previous fitness level methods
[
6
,
7
]
through three examples. We show that our new method can be used to derive tight bounds for (1+1) EAs on three typical fitness landscapes with shortcuts (TwoMax1, FullyDeceptive and Deceptive), but the previous fitness level method
[
6
,
7
]
cannot.

concludes the paper.

II-B

Fitness Levels and Transition Probabilities

The fitness level method is a convenient tool used for estimating the hitting time of EAs based on a fitness level partition
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. The definition and notation below are slightly different from that in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
because the highest rank
S_{0}
represents the optimal set. We follow the notation used in
[
15
]

Definition 2

In a
fitness level partition
, the state space
S
is split into
K+1
ranks
(S_{0},\cdots,S_{K})
such that (i) the highest rank
S_{0}=S_{\mathrm{opt}}
, (ii) the rank of
S_{k}
is higher than that of

, that is, for any
X_{k}\in S_{k}
and
X_{k+1}\in S_{k+1}

.
Each rank

is called a

.

In the fitness level method, we estimate time bounds by transition probabilities between fitness levels rather than transition probabilities between states. The number of levels
K
is chosen to be a small number. By default, we set
K\leq n
, where
n
is the dimension of the search space.

First, we use the transition probability
p(X_{k},S_{\ell})
from
X_{k}\in S_{k}
to the level
S_{\ell}
. Let
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
and
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
denote its maximum and minimum, respectively.

\displaystyle p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})=\max_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}p(X_{k},S_{\ell}).

\displaystyle p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})=\min_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}p(X_{k},S_{\ell}).

Secondly, we use the transition probability
p(X_{k},S_{[i,j]})
from
X_{k}\in S_{k}
to a union of levels
S_{[i,j]}
. Here
[i,j]
denotes the index set
\{i,i+1,\cdots,j-1,j\}
and
S_{[i,j]}
denotes the union
S_{i}\cup\cdots\cup S_{j}
. Let

p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{[i,j]})
and
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{[i,j]})
denote its maximum and minimum, respectively.

\displaystyle p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{[i,j]})

\displaystyle=\max_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}p(X_{k},S_{[i,j]}).

\displaystyle p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{[i,j]})

\displaystyle=\min_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}p(X_{k},S_{[i,j]}).

Thirdly, we use the conditional transition probability
r(X_{k},S_{\ell})
from
X^{[t]}=X_{k}
to
X^{[t+1]}\in S_{\ell}
condition on
X^{[t+1]}\in S_{[0,k-1]}
.

\displaystyle r(X_{k},S_{\ell})=\frac{p(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{p(X_{k},S_{[0,k-1]})}.

Let
r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
and
r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})
denote its maximum and minimum, respectively.

\displaystyle r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\max_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}r(X_{k},S_{\ell}).

\displaystyle r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\min_{X_{k}\in S_{k}}r(X_{k},S_{\ell}).

The hitting time of an EA is the number of generations for the EA to hit the optimal set for the first time
[
1
,
2
]
.

Definition 3

The

\displaystyle\min\{t\geq 0,X^{[t]}\in S_{\mathrm{opt}}\mid X^{[0]}=X\}.

(3)

\displaystyle=\sum^{K}_{k=1}\Pr(X^{[0]}\in S_{k})\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\frac{c_{k,\ell}}{\displaystyle\max_{X_{\ell}\in S_{\ell}}p(X_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}

Let

IV
Fitness Landscapes with Shortcuts

We categorize fitness landscapes into two types based on the existence of shortcuts: (1) those without shortcuts and (2) those with shortcuts. This section illustrates these concepts through examples.

IV-A

Digraphs, Paths and Shortcuts

The behavior of elitist EA is represented by a Markov chain on a digraph
G=(V,A)

[
18
,
17
]
in which

•

V
denotes the set of vertices where a vertex
k
represents a fitness level
S_{k},\ k=0,\ldots,K
.

•

A
denotes the set of arcs where an arc
(k,\ell)
represents the transition from
S_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
with
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})>0
. If
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S_{\ell})=0
, the arc
(k,\ell)
does not exist.

Here, a vertex is a fitness level. This is different from
[
18
,
17
]
.
For an elitist EA, the transition only happens from a lower fitness level to a higher or equal level. Therefore, the graph
G
is a digraph.

A
path
from
S_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
is an acyclic sequence of vertices (levels)

S_{v_{0}}\to S_{v_{1}}\to\cdots\to S_{v_{m-1}}\to S_{v_{m}}
such that
v_{0}=k
,
v_{m}=\ell
, and the
transition probability
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{v_{j}},S_{v_{j+1}})>0
for any arc
(v_{j},v_{j+1})
on the path. A
full path
from
S_{k}
to
S_{\ell}
includes all vertices between
S_{k}
and
S_{\ell}
, that is,
S_{k}\to S_{k-1}\to\cdots\to S_{\ell+1}\to S_{\ell}
.

There are different paths from a lower fitness level to a higher one, where some paths are shorter than others. Such paths are shortcuts. He and Zhou
[
15
]
rigorously defined the (weak) shortcut as follows.

Definition 5

Given an elitist EA for maximizing
f(x)
and a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
, there is a (
weak
)
shortcut
from
X_{k}\in S_{k}
to
S_{[0,\ell-1]}
skipping
S_{\ell}
(where
k\ >\ell
) if the conditional probability

\displaystyle\frac{p(X_{k},S_{\ell})}{p(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]})}=o(1).

(13)

Condition (
13
) means that the conditional probability of the EA accessing
S_{\ell}
is
o(1)
, but accessing
S_{[0,\ell-1]}
is
1-o(1)
. Therefore, level
S_{\ell}
may be skipped.

In this paper, we define strong shortcuts as follows.

Definition 6

Given an elitist EA for maximizing
f(x)
and a fitness level partition
(S_{0},\ldots,S_{K})
, there is a
strong

shortcut
from
X_{k}\in S_{k}
to
S_{[0,\ell-1]}
skipping
S_{[\ell,k-1]}
(where
k\ >\ell
) if the conditional probability

\displaystyle r(X_{k},S_{[\ell,k-1]})=\frac{p(X_{k},S_{[\ell,k-1]})}{p(X_{k},S_{[0,k-1]})}=o(1).

(14)

Condition (
14
) requires skipping all levels from
S_{\ell}
to
S_{k-1}
, but (
13
) only requires skipping the level
S_{\ell}
. A strong shortcut is a weak shortcut, which can be proven by the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{p(X_{k},S_{[\ell,k-1]})}{p(X_{k},S_{[0,k-1]})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(X_{k},S_{\ell})+p(X_{k},S_{[\ell+1,k-1]})}{p(X_{k},S_{[0,\ell]})+p(X_{k},S_{[\ell+1,k-1]})}

For Type-

V
The Digraph Method for Lower Bounds

We propose a new method for estimating lower bounds based on sub-digraphs. The idea is to select a sub-digraph consisting of some non-optimal states and use the transition probabilities in this sub-digraph to calculate the linear coefficients. Intuitively, a Markov chain takes less time in a sub-digraph than in the entire non-optimal set. Therefore, the digraph method can be used to derive a lower bound on hitting time. The surprising outcome is that this method can be used to quickly derive tight lower bounds on the fitness landscape with shortcuts, which are difficult for existing fitness level methods on the entire digraph.

V-A

The digraph method

Select a subset of non-optimal states and denote it by
S^{\prime}
. A new absorbing Markov chain is constructed whose transition probabilities (denoted by
p(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
) satisfy

p(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})&\mbox{if }X^{\prime}\in S^{\prime},\\
0&\mbox{if }X^{\prime}\in S\setminus S^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}.\end{array}\right.

(32)

Denote
S^{\prime}_{0}=S\setminus S^{\prime}
. Obviously
S^{\prime}_{0}\supset S_{\mathrm{opt}}
.
For the new chain, its hitting time is modified to be the number of generations for the EA to hit
S^{\prime}_{0}
for the first time.

Definition 7

The
(random) hitting time
for an EA starting from
X^{[0]}=X
to hit
S^{\prime}_{0}
is

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}(X)=\min\{t\geq 0,X^{[t]}\in S^{\prime}_{0}\mid X^{[0]}=X\}.

(33)

The
mean hitting time

m^{\prime}(X)=\mathrm{E}[\tau^{\prime}(X)].

Definition 8

A subset
S^{\prime}
of non-optimal states is split into
K
ranks according to the fitness value from high to low, denoted by
(S^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{K})
. Let
S^{\prime}_{0}=S\setminus S^{\prime}
, then
(S^{\prime}_{0},S^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{K})
forms a
level partition
.

The level partition is different from the fitness level partition because
S^{\prime}_{0}
might include some non-optimal states.

The behavior of an
elitist EA on a sub-digraph
can be represented by a Markov chain on a sub-digraph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},A^{\prime})
.

V^{\prime}
denotes the set of vertices where a vertex
k
represents
S^{\prime}_{k},\ k=0,\ldots,K
.
A^{\prime}
denotes the set of arcs where an arc
(k,\ell)
represents the transition from
S^{\prime}_{k}
to
S^{\prime}_{\ell}
(where
k>\ell
) with
p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X_{k},S^{\prime}_{\ell})>0
.

Sub-digraphs can be used to estimate lower bounds on the hitting time
m(X)
.
Let
m^{\prime}(X)
be the hitting time of an EA to hit
S^{\prime}_{0}
on a sub-digraph.
Since
S_{\mathrm{opt}}\subset S^{\prime}_{0}
, the mean time to hit
S^{\prime}_{0}
is not more than the mean time to hit
S_{\mathrm{opt}}
.

The following corollary shows that lower bounds can be derived from the fitness level method on sub-digraphs. It is the sub-digraph version of Theorem
1
, with the same proof argument.

Corollary 2

Given a level partition
(S^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{K})
, the bound
d^{\prime}_{k}
(where
k=1,\cdots,K
) is constructed as follows,

d^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{1}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{[0,k-1]})}+\sum^{k-1}_{\ell=1}\frac{c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X^{\prime}_{\ell},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})},

(34)

where the coefficient
c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}\in[0,1]
satisfies

c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}\leq r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{\ell})+\sum^{k-1}_{j=\ell+1}{r}_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{j})c^{\prime}_{j,\ell}.

(35)

Then

m^{\prime}(X)\geq d^{\prime}_{k}.

V-B

Calculation of Coefficients in the Lower Bound

We propose a new explicit formula to calculate coefficients based on a sub-digraph.
To calculate coefficient
c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}
, we consider the conditional probability
r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})
for all vertices
\ell<i\leq k
.

Theorem 2

Given a level partition
(S^{\prime}_{0},S^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{K})
, the bound
d^{\prime}_{k}
(where
k=1,\cdots,K
) is constructed as follows,

d^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{1}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{[0,k-1]})}+\sum^{k-1}_{\ell=1}\frac{c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X^{\prime}_{\ell},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})},

(36)

where the coefficient
c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}\in[0,1]
satisfies

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{k,l}=

\displaystyle\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]}).

(37)

Then
m^{\prime}(X^{\prime}_{k})\geq d^{\prime}_{k}
.

Note: The product of conditional probabilities (
37
) is equivalent to (
38
) and (
39
).

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{k,l}=

\displaystyle\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\min_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in S^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,i-1]})}

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\min_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in S^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{1}{1+\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}}.

(39)

Proof:

Since for
0<\ell<j\leq k
, the sequence
\{c^{\prime}_{j,\ell},j=\ell+1,\cdots,k\}
decreases monotonically on
j
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{\ell})+\sum^{k-1}_{j=\ell+1}r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{j})c^{\prime}_{j,\ell}\\
&\geq r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{\ell})+\sum^{k-1}_{j=\ell+1}r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{j})c^{\prime}_{k-1,\ell}\\
&\geq\left(r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{\ell})+\sum^{k-1}_{j=\ell+1}r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{j})\right)c^{\prime}_{k-1,\ell}\\
&=r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,k-1]})\times c^{\prime}_{k-1,\ell}=c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}.\end{split}

Thus,
c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}
satisfies Inequality (
35
).
According to Corollary
2
,
m^{\prime}(X^{\prime}_{k})\geq d^{\prime}_{k}
.
∎

In Theorem
2
,
r_{{\scriptscriptstyle\min}}(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})
is the conditional transition probability from
i
to
[\ell,i-1]
. The coefficient
c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}
is the product of these probabilities.

The general usage of Theorem
2
is summarized as follows: (1) Using the entire digraph is suitable for fitness functions without shortcuts or with weak shortcuts, e.g., OneMax and FullyDeceptive. (2) Using an appropriate sub-digraph is helpful to handle fitness functions with strong shortcuts, e.g., TwoMax1 and Deceptive.

V-C

Determination of Large Coefficients

Theorem
2
gives explicit expressions for lower bound coefficients but the product term seems complex. Unless the goal is to obtain an explicit expression of each coefficient, there is no need to calculate the product. To obtain large coefficients, it is sufficient to estimate each conditional probability in the product but without having to calculate the whole product.

Three criteria for identifying large coefficients are provided in the theorem below. According to Theorem
2
, in order to ensure that a large coefficient
\Omega(1)
, most conditional transition probabilities
r(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})
(where
\ell<i\leq k
) in the product must be close to 1. This requirement is satisfied on an appropriate sub-digraph, but often does not hold on the entire digraph if shortcuts exist.

Theorem 3

Given a level partition
(S^{\prime}_{0},S^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{K})
(where
1\leq K\leq n
), the bound
d^{\prime}_{k}
(where
k=1,\cdots,K
) is constructed as follows,

d^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{1}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X^{\prime}_{k},S^{\prime}_{[0,k-1]})}+\sum^{k-1}_{\ell=1}\frac{c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X^{\prime}_{\ell},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})},

where coefficients
c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}\in[0,1]
satisfies

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{k,\ell}=\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\min_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in S^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{1}{1+\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}}.

If one of the following conditions is met,

1.

there is a constant
C>0
such that for
i=\ell+1,\ldots,k
, the ratio

\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}\leq\frac{C}{n-1},

(40)

2.

or there is a constant
C>0
such that for
i=\ell+1,\ldots,k
, the ratio

\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}\leq\frac{C}{(i-\ell+1)!},

(41)

3.

or there is a constant
C>0
such that for
i=\ell+1,\ldots,k
, the ratio

\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}\leq\frac{C}{(n-i+2)!},

(42)

then for any
\ell<j\leq k
, the coefficient

c^{\prime}_{j,\ell}=\Omega(1)
.

Proof:

1) The coefficient

\displaystyle\begin{split}c^{\prime}_{j,\ell}\geq\prod^{n}_{i=2}\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{(n-1)}}\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{C}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}=e^{-C}.\end{split}

2) The coefficient

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\prod^{j}_{i=\ell+1}\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{(i-\ell+1)!}}\geq\prod^{n}_{i=2}\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{i!}}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\exp\left({-\sum^{n}_{i=1}\ln\left(1+\frac{C}{i!}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\exp\left({-\sum^{n}_{i=1}\frac{C}{i!}}\right)=e^{-C(e-1)}.

3) This case is equivalent to the above case. Since
1\leq\ell<i\leq k\leq n
, we have
2\leq n-i+2<n
.
The coefficient

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\prod^{j}_{i=\ell+1}\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{(n-i+2)!}}\geq\prod^{n}_{i=2}\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{i!}}\geq e^{-C(e-1)}.

The constants
e^{-C}
and
e^{-C(e-1)}
confirm the bound
\Omega(1)
.
∎

Note: The above theorem also applies to combinations of 1) to 3), for example,

\displaystyle\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{n-1}+\frac{C}{(i-\ell+1)!}.

If two products (where
a_{i},b_{i}>0
)

\displaystyle\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\frac{1}{1+a_{i}}=\Omega(1),

\displaystyle\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\frac{1}{1+b_{i}}=\Omega(1),

then the product

\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\frac{1}{1+a_{i}+b_{i}}\geq\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\frac{1}{1+a_{i}}\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\frac{1}{1+b_{i}}=\Omega(1).

The above theorem makes it easy to identify large coefficients based on the ratio between the transition probabilities
{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}
and
{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})}
, but does not take into account the product.

Summary

To conclude this section, the procedure of the digraph method is summarized in Procedure
2
.

Procedure 2

The digraph method for lower bound

1:

An elitist EA, a fitness function and an initial population
X
.

Step 2.

Select an appropriate non-optimal subset
S^{\prime}
where
X\in S^{\prime}
. Construct a level partition
(S^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{K})
where
S^{\prime}_{0}=S\setminus S^{\prime}
. Assume
X\in S^{\prime}_{k}
.

Step 3.

Calculate transition probabilities

\frac{1}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X^{\prime}_{\ell},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})},\quad\ell=1,\ldots,k.

Step 4.

Calculate

\max_{X^{\prime}_{i}\in S_{i}}\frac{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{[\ell,i-1]})},\quad i=\ell+1,\ldots,k.

Verify ratios are bounded by

O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),O\left(\frac{1}{(i-\ell+1)!}\right)\mbox{ or }O\left(\frac{1}{(n-i+2)!}\right).

Step 5.

Obtain a lower bound

VII
Conclusions

We propose a new fitness level method to quickly derive lower bounds on the hitting time of elitist EAs. The method is straightforward. According to Theorem
2
, a sub-digraph is selected and divided into fitness levels
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{k})
. Then, a lower bound is calculated by an explicit formula:

d_{k}=\frac{1}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{k},S_{[0,k-1]})}+\sum^{k-1}_{\ell=1}\frac{c_{k,\ell}}{p_{{\scriptscriptstyle\max}}(X_{\ell},S_{[0,\ell-1]})},

where coefficients are given by

c_{k,\ell}=\prod^{k}_{i=\ell+1}\min_{X_{i}\in S_{i}}\frac{1}{1+\frac{p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X_{i},S_{[\ell,i-1]})}}.

It is sufficient to consider large coefficients but discard small coefficients. According to Theorem
3
, to get large coefficients, there is no need to calculate the product term, just to verify that the ratio meets the following requirement

\displaystyle\frac{p(X_{i},S_{[0,\ell-1]})}{p(X_{i},S_{[\ell,i-1]})}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),O\left(\frac{1}{(i-\ell+1)!}\right)\mbox{ or }O\left(\frac{1}{(n-i+2)!}\right).

The new method holds a major advantage over previous fitness level methods
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
