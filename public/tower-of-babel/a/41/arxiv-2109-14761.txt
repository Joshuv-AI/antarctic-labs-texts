Asymptotic formation and orbital stability of phase-locked states in Kuramoto–Lohe type synchronization models on Lie groups
Seung-Yeon Ryoo

Abstract
Some mathematical models of synchronization, such as the Kuramoto model (1975) and its generalizations pioneered by Lohe (2009), are formulated as ordinary differential equations describing populations of particles on Lie groups with locally attractive interactions. We suggest a model of synchronization on Lie groups and present a framework to understand the formation of phase-locked states and their orbital stability. This is a sequel to a previous joint work with Ha and Ko (2017).

Kuramoto model; Lohe model; Lie group; Gronwall inequality; functional calculus

1.1
Motivation and significance of the generalized Kuramoto–Lohe model and Theorem
1.4
.

The
Kuramoto model

[
11
,
12
]
is the Cauchy problem for the real variables
\{\theta_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
given by the nonlinear system of ordinary differential equations

\begin{cases}\dot{\theta}_{i}(t)=\omega_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{j}(t)-\theta_{i}(t))\\
\theta_{i}(0)=\theta_{i}^{0}\end{cases},\quad i=1,\cdots,N,

(1.4)

where
\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
are the intrinsic frequencies,
\kappa>0
is the coupling strength, and
\theta_{1}^{0},\cdots,\theta_{N}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}
are the initial data.

We may consider the Kuramoto model to be a special case of the generalized Kuramoto–Lohe model, with Lie group
G=\mathbb{S}^{1}
and interaction function
\phi(\theta)=\sin\theta
(we are considering the dynamics on the universal cover
\mathbb{R}
of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
). Since the interactions are pairwise attractive, we may expect that if the coupling strength
\kappa
is large enough compared to the differences
\omega_{i}-\omega_{j}
in the intrinsic frequencies, then the population should become more and more ordered as time goes by.

Theorem
1.4
was proven for the Kuramoto model by Ha, Ha, and Kim
[
6
]
and Choi, Ha, Jung, and Kim
[
1
]
, with
c_{1}=\frac{\pi}{4}
. Namely, if the particles all lie on the same quarter-circle, then they stay within a quarter-circle for all time, their relative phases
\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
converge to a unique value,
\theta_{i}-\theta_{i}^{\infty}-\nu t
converges to zero for some speed
\nu
and some phase-locked state
\{\theta_{i}^{\infty}\}
unique up to an additive constant, and
\dot{\theta}_{i}-\dot{\theta}_{j}
converges exponentially to zero (one can extend the quarter-circle to a half-circle with some complications).

Lohe
[
13
,
14
]
suggested a nonabelian generalization of the Kuramoto model to the unitary groups
U(d)
as follows: the
Lohe model
describes the dynamics of
\{U_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset U(d)
as

\dot{U}_{i}U_{i}^{\dagger}=iH_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(U_{j}U_{i}^{\dagger}-U_{i}U_{j}^{\dagger}\right),

(1.5)

where
\kappa>0
is again the coupling strength, the Hermitian matrix
H_{i}
is the Hamiltonian of
U_{i}
, and
\dagger
denotes the Hermitian conjugate. It can be checked that the Kuramoto model is the
U(1)
case of the Lohe model (
1.5
), and the Lohe model (
1.5
) is in turn the case
G=U(d)
,
\phi(U)=\frac{1}{2}(U-U^{\dagger})
of the generalized Kuramoto–Lohe model. Lohe
[
13
]
numerically observed Theorem
1.4
for the
U(2)
model, and Ha and the author
[
9
]
gave a rigorous proof of Theorem
1.4
for all unitary groups
U(d)
.

It was suggested by Lohe
[
13
]
that the model (
1.5
) can be extended to other matrix groups. Here is a direct quotation from
[
13
, p.5]
:

…There also exist non-Abelian Kuramoto models for the symplectic groups, with defining equations similar to [(
1.5
)], and hence for all classical compact Lie groups. By choosing an indefinite metric one may extend these models to the noncompact case, for which trajectories are unbounded, however, we do not discuss synchronization or other properties of these particular models here…

In
[
8
]
, Ha, Ko, and the author interpreted this as a model on
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as follows:

\dot{X}_{i}X_{i}^{-1}=H_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(X_{j}X_{i}^{-1}-X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}\right),

(1.6)

and characterized various matrix Lie groups, such as
U(d)
,
O(m,n)
,
SO(m,n)
,
SP(d)
,
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, on which this model defines a closed trajectory. We were also able to establish Theorem
1.4
for the model (
1.6
) in
[
8
]
.

We remark that the following variant model on
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})

\dot{X}_{i}X_{i}^{-1}=H_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma_{ij}\left(f(X_{j}X_{i}^{-1})-f(X_{i}X_{j}^{-1})\right),

(1.7)

was considered by DeVille
[
4
]
.

The author was thus led to propose the model (
1.1
) in the general setting of Lie groups
G
with locally attractive interaction
\phi
, and to consider whether Theorem
1.4
holds true in this setting. Part (2) of Theorem
1.4
is unsurprising: as the particles are pairwise attractive, if they are initially close together, then they should stay close together for all times. Thus, part (4) of Theorem
1.4
, the orbital stability statement, is the crux of Theorem
1.4
: it only requires (
H
), namely that
\phi
is
C^{1}
and that it is locally attractive in the sense that
(d\phi)_{e}
has only eigenvalues with positive real parts. Note that higher order considerations, such as
C^{2}
regularity of
\phi
or curvature properties of
G
, are irrelevant.

Thus, as long as we define any model in the form of (
1.1
), by choosing a Lie group
G
and an interaction function
\phi:G\to\mathfrak{g}
to satisfy (
H
), then this model will automatically have the stability properties of Theorem
1.4
. This grants us significant liberty in defining locally well-behaved synchronization models: we only need to choose a Lie group
G
and an interaction function
\phi:G\to\mathfrak{g}
satisfying (
H
).

For example, it was suggested by an anonymous referee for the previous paper
[
9
]
that we consider the model on
G=SU(d),SL_{d}(\mathbb{C}),SL_{d}(\mathbb{R})
given by

\dot{X}_{i}X_{i}^{-1}=H_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(X_{j}X_{i}^{-1}-X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}-\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}[X_{j}X_{i}^{-1}]I_{d}+\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}[X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}]I_{d}\right).

(1.8)

One can check that (
H
) holds for this interaction function, so that Theorem
1.4
holds for (
1.8
).

Here is an intuitive heuristic as to why Theorem
1.4
should be true. If the particles are concentrated locally, say around the identity
e\in G
, then we may linearize the system by setting

X_{i}=\exp(Y_{i})\quad i=1,\cdots,N,

and the governing equation (
1.1
) linearizes to

\dot{Y}_{i}=H_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(d\phi)_{e}(Y_{k}-Y_{i}),\quad i=1,\cdots,N.

(1.9)

This is an exactly solvable linear differential equation. In particular, the dynamics for the differences
Y_{i}-Y_{j}
is simple:

\dot{Y}_{i}-\dot{Y}_{j}=H_{i}-H_{j}-\kappa(d\phi)_{e}(Y_{i}-Y_{j}),\quad i,j=1,\cdots,N.

As
(d\phi)_{e}
has only eigenvalues with positive real parts, if we make
\kappa
large enough compared to
|H_{i}-H_{j}|
, then
Y_{i}-Y_{j}
should converge exponentially to a fixed value.

Thus, the proof of Theorem
1.4
for the nonlinear model (
1.1
) will be a “nonlinearization” of the above heuristic argument, i.e., we will check that the nonlinear effects are controllable compared to the dominant linear term. One caveat of this nonlinearization is that we will need to require that
\kappa
is large compared to the
|H_{i}|
’s as in (
1.3
), instead of requiring
\kappa
to be large compared to the
|H_{i}-H_{j}|
’s. There will also be the complication that the
X_{i}
’s may not stay in the neighborhood of a single point for all time, so we will work with the logarithms of the
ratios

X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}
, which will stay close to the identity
e\in G
.

1.2
Directions for further research.

One reason for considering generalizations of the Kuramoto model (
1.4
) should come from a search for new phenomena not observed in the Kuramoto model. Thus, since Theorem
1.4
is true not only for the Kuramoto model but also for its Lie group generalizations in the form of (
1.1
), the merit of studying such models in the form of (
1.1
) will have to come from the behavior of the model when the particles are not extremely concentrated. For example, DeVille
[
4
]
presents several special solutions to (
1.7
) and shows that it has multiple attractors under rich network topologies, while Ritchie, Lohe, and Williams
[
16
]
present a detailed study of the global solutions of the special case
G=SO(1,1)
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.4
, one proves the statements in the order of (2), (3), (1), (4), and (5), with possibly smaller
c_{1}
and larger
c
(and consequently larger
\kappa
) along the way. Thus there might be a sequence of phase transitions

X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}\mbox{ bounded }\Rightarrow X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}\mbox{ converges }\Rightarrow(X_{i}\to X_{j}^{\infty}\exp(\Lambda t)),

as we increase the coupling strength
\kappa
. It would be interesting to see whether the first phase-transition actually occurs. That is, for small
\kappa
, the solutions might stay bounded from each other with their ratios not converging, possibly exhibiting chaotic behavior. Observing such ‘synchronous chaos’ in a prototypical model such as (
1.5
) would be interesting.

possess a singularity at the origin. Are there certain types of singularities of

Another drawback of Theorem

Question 1.8

For incarnations of the model (

It would also be interesting to study infinite limits such as the mean-field limit, as performed for the Lohe model (

2
Notation and preliminaries on Lie groups and Lie algebras

We establish some notation about our Lie group
(G,\mathfrak{g})
and recall some standard facts from the literature.

As
(d\phi)_{e}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
has only eigenvalues with positive real parts, we may assume that the right-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
, which is given as an inner product
\langle,\rangle
on
\mathfrak{g}
with corresponding norm
|\cdot|=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{1/2}
, is so that for some
\lambda>0
,

\langle v,(d\phi)_{e}(v)\rangle\geq\lambda|v|^{2},\quad\forall v\in\mathfrak{g}.

(2.1)

(This change of Riemannian metric only distorts the geodesic metric of
G
by a constant factor, and thus does not affect the validity of Theorem
1.4
.)
We define, for
r>0
, the ball of radius
r

B_{r}=\{v\in\mathfrak{g}:|v|<r\}.

The Lie group exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
is a local diffeomorphism at
0\in\mathfrak{g}
, with
(d\exp)_{0}=\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}
. We may choose a radius
r>0
depending on
G
so that
\exp:B_{r}\to\exp(B_{r})
is a diffeomorphism, and consequently the logarithm
\log:\exp(B(0,r))\to B_{r}
is well defined and smooth; we will use this as a coordinate chart for
G
in this paper.

The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula describes the group operation on this coordinate chart. Given
v,w\in\mathfrak{g}
, with
|v|,|w|
sufficiently small, if we define

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u\coloneqq&\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m}\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}r_{1}+s_{1}>0\\
\vdots\\
r_{m}+s_{m}>0\end{smallmatrix}}\frac{[v^{r_{1}}w^{s_{1}}v^{r_{2}}w^{s_{2}}\dotsm v^{r_{m}}w^{s_{m}}]}{(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(r_{j}+s_{j}))\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}!s_{i}!}\\
=&v+w+a(v,w),\end{aligned}

(2.2)

where we have used the notation

[g^{r_{1}}h^{s_{1}}\dotsm g^{r_{m}}h^{s_{m}}]=[\underbrace{g,[g,\dotsm[g}_{r_{1}},[\underbrace{h,[h,\dotsm[h}_{s_{1}},\,\dotsm\,[\underbrace{g,[g,\dotsm[g}_{r_{m}},[\underbrace{h,[h,\dotsm h}_{s_{m}}]]\dotsm]],

and
a(v,w)
denotes the sum of the terms of second or higher degree, then the power series defining
u
is absolutely convergent within some positive radius of convergence, and satisfies

\exp(u)=\exp(v)\exp(w).

We may assume that the radius
r>0
is sufficiently smaller than this radius of convergence so that for
u,v\in B_{r}
, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula for
u
and
v
converges absolutely, with the map
a:B_{r}\times B_{r}\to\mathfrak{g}
being smooth with bounded derivatives:

\displaystyle a(u,v)

\displaystyle=O_{G}(|u||v|),

\displaystyle a(u,v)-a(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})

\displaystyle=O_{G}((|u|+|u^{\prime}|)|v-v^{\prime}|+(|v|+|v^{\prime}|)|u-u^{\prime}|),~\forall u,v,u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in B_{r}.

(2.3)

We denote the adjoint map
\operatorname{Ad}:G\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
, which is defined to be
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}=(dL_{g})_{g^{-1}}\circ(dR_{g^{-1}})_{e}=(dR_{g^{-1}})_{g}\circ(dL_{g})_{e}
for
g\in G
. The derivative of the adjoint map,
\operatorname{ad}:\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
, is given by
\operatorname{ad}_{v}=[v,\cdot]
, for all
v\in\mathfrak{g}
. It is well-known that

\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(v)}=e^{\operatorname{ad}_{v}},\quad\forall v\in\mathfrak{g},

(2.4)

where the right-hand side denotes the element of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
given by

e^{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}(\operatorname{ad}_{v})^{m}.

We will need to take derivatives under the exponential map. If
Y:I\to\mathfrak{g}
is a differentiable curve in
\mathfrak{g}
defined on some interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, then it is well-known that

\frac{d}{dt}\exp(Y)=(dR_{\exp(Y)})_{e}\frac{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y}}-1}{\operatorname{ad}_{Y}}\left(\frac{dY}{dt}\right),

(2.5)

where, on the right-hand side, the operator
\frac{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y}}-1}{\operatorname{ad}_{Y}}
is defined using the power series expansion for
\frac{e^{z}-1}{z}
. By functional calculus, if
r>0
is sufficiently small and
v\in B_{r}
, then the operator
\frac{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}-1}{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}
is invertible with the well-defined inverse
\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}-1}
. Thus, if
Y(t)\in B_{r}
for all
t\in I
, then
denoting
X=\exp(Y)
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\log(X)=\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y}}-1}(dR_{X^{-1}})_{X}\left(\frac{dX}{dt}\right).

(2.6)

Again by functional calculus, if
r>0
is sufficiently small, we have

\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}-1},\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{v}}}=\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}+O(|v|),\quad v\in B_{r},

(2.7)

and

\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}-1}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{w}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{w}}-1},\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{v}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{v}}}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{w}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{w}}}=O(|v-w|),\quad v,w\in B_{r},

(2.8)

in the sense of operator norms. We will later use (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) to control (
2.6
).

3.2
Reducing from a model on the Lie group to a model on the Lie algebra.

Our goal now is to prove Theorem
3.2
. Theorem
3.2
roughly says that if the particles
X_{i}
are initially close together, they will stay together for all time and will exhibit good stability behavior. However, we have a priori little control on where the whole aggregate will head toward; it is possible that the entire population heads away from its initial position exponentially fast.

Thus, if we restrict our attention to the ratios
X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}
of the particles, we should be able to analyze them in a small neighborhood of the identity.

From (
3.3
), we may formulate the Cauchy problem for the relative phases
X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(X_{i}X_{j}^{-1})=(dR_{X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}})_{e}\Bigg(H_{i}-\operatorname{Ad}_{X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}}H_{j}\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}[\phi(X_{k}X_{i}^{-1})-\operatorname{Ad}_{X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}}\phi(X_{k}X_{j}^{-1})]\Bigg),\\
X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}(0)=X_{i}^{0}(X_{j}^{0})^{-1},\quad i,j=1,\cdots,N.\end{cases}

(3.5)

Again, the standard Cauchy–Lipschitz theory guarantees the existence of a local
C^{1}
-solution to (
3.5
). Observe that a global solution to (
1.1
) exists if and only if a global solution to (
3.5
) exists. Indeed, the only if direction is trivial, and conversely if the
X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}
’s are globally defined, we may consider the right-hand side of (
1.1
) to be a time-dependent bounded
C^{1}
-forcing term, and so by a standard argument (using the fact that
G
as a manifold is complete) the solutions
X_{i}(t)
must exist for all time.

Assuming
X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}\in\exp(B_{r})
for all
i,j=1,\cdots,N
, define
Y_{ij}=\log(X_{i}X_{j}^{-1})
. We will analyze the
Y_{ij}
’s on
\mathfrak{g}
instead of the
X_{i}X_{j}^{-1}
’s on
G
; we are using
B_{r}
as a coordinate chart for
\exp(B_{r})
. Now, by (
2.4
) and (
2.6
), the
Y_{ij}
’s are the solution to the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{ij}=\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1}H_{i}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}H_{j}\\
\qquad\quad+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1}\phi\circ\exp(Y_{ki})-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}\phi\circ\exp(Y_{kj})\right],\\
Y_{ij}(0)=\log(X_{i}^{0}(X_{j}^{0})^{-1}),\quad i,j=1,\cdots,N.\end{cases}

(3.6)

Once again, the standard Cauchy–Lipschitz theory guarantees the existence of a local
C^{1}
-solution to (
3.6
). This time, we can only observe that the existence of a global solution to (
3.6
) guarantees the existence of a global solution to (
3.5
), but not the other way around.

Note that, by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, the
Y_{ij}
’s satisfy the compatibility equations

Y_{ji}=-Y_{ij},\quad Y_{ik}=Y_{ij}+Y_{jk}+a(Y_{ij},Y_{jk}),\quad i,j,k=1,\cdots,N

(3.7)

for all times of their existence.

We now translate Theorem
3.2
, a statement about the
X_{i}
’s, into a statement about the
Y_{ij}
’s.

Theorem 3.4
.

There exist a universal constant
C>0
, a sufficiently large constant
c>0
and a sufficiently small constant
c_{1}>0
depending on
G
,
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\phi
such that if

\kappa>c\|H\|_{\infty}

(3.8)

then the following holds.

(1)

(Local stability) If

|Y_{ij}(0)|<c_{1},\quad\forall i,j=1,\cdots,N,

then there exists a unique global solution
Y(t)=\{Y_{ij}(t)\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
to (
3.6
) satisfying

|Y_{ij}(t)|<c_{1},~t\geq 0,\quad\forall i,j=1,\cdots,N,

and

\limsup_{t\to\infty}|Y_{ij}(t)|\leq\frac{C\|H\|_{\infty}}{\kappa\lambda},\quad\forall i,j=1,\cdots,N.

(2)

(Convergence of two flows) Let
\tilde{Y}(t)=\{\tilde{Y}_{ij}(t)\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
be a solution to (
3.6
) with initial data
\{\tilde{Y}_{ij}^{0}\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
satisfying the compatibility equations (
3.7
) and

|\tilde{Y}_{ij}^{0}|<c_{1},\quad\forall i,j=1,\cdots,N.

Then

|Y_{ij}(t)-\tilde{Y}_{ij}(t)|\leq|Y_{ij}^{0}-\tilde{Y}_{ij}^{0}|\cdot e^{-\kappa\lambda t/3},\quad t\geq 0.

It is clear that Theorem
3.4
implies Theorem
3.2
, so our goal is now to prove Theorem
3.4
.

3.3
Two Gronwall inequalities.

The proof of Theorem
3.4
depends on two Gronwall inequalities for two Lyapunov functionals for
Y
, each respectively corresponding to statements (1) and (2) of Theorem
3.4
.

First, we consider the Lyapunov functional

\|Y\|_{\infty}=\max_{i,j=1,\cdots,N}|Y_{ij}|.

(3.9)

Lemma 3.5

(First Gronwall inequality)
.

Let
Y(t)=\{Y_{ij}(t)\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
,
t\in[0,T)
, be a solution to (
3.6
), with
Y_{ij}(t)\in B_{r}
for all
i,j=1,\cdots,N
and
t\in[0,T)
. Then

\dot{Y}_{ij}=-\kappa\left[d\phi_{e}(Y_{ij})+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y\|_{\infty})\right]+O(\|H\|_{\infty}),\quad i,j=1,\cdots,N,

(3.10)

and thus

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|^{+}\|Y\|_{\infty}\leq-\kappa\left[\lambda\|Y\|_{\infty}+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y\|_{\infty})\right]+O(\|H\|_{\infty}),

(3.11)

where
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|^{+}
denotes the upper right Dini derivative, and the little
o
notation is with respect to
\|Y\|_{\infty}\to 0
and whose constants depend on
G
,
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\phi
.

Remark 3.6
.

Intuitively, since the original model (
1.1
) is locally attractive, it is expected and unsurprising that a measure such as
\|Y\|_{\infty}
for the diameter of a concentrated population should stay small; the ‘first-order force’
-\kappa d\phi_{e}(Y_{ij})
in (
3.10
) represents this local attraction.

Proof 3.7
.

(Proof of Lemma
3.5
.)

By functional calculus (
2.7
),

\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1},\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}=\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}+O(|Y_{ij}|),

and

\phi\circ\exp(Y_{ij})=d\phi_{e}(Y_{ij})+o_{G,\phi}(|Y_{ij}|),

so by (
3.6
)

\dot{Y}_{ij}=O(\|H\|_{\infty})+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[d\phi_{e}(Y_{ki})-d\phi_{e}(Y_{kj})+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y\|_{\infty})\right].

Applying the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula (
3.7
),

Y_{ki}-Y_{kj}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{compatibility}}}{{=}}-Y_{ij}-a(Y_{jk},Y_{ki})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{a-bound}}}{{=}}-Y_{ij}+O_{G}(\|Y\|_{\infty}^{2}),

so

\dot{Y}_{ij}=O(\|H\|_{\infty})-\kappa\left[\phi_{e}(Y_{ij})+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y\|_{\infty})\right]

which is (
3.10
). Now (
3.11
) follows from (
3.10
) by (
2.1
).

This Gronwall inequality allows us to prove the first part of Theorem
3.4
.

Proof 3.8
.

(Proof of Theorem
3.4
(1).)

Let
c_{1}
be small enough so that
|Y_{ij}|<c_{1}
,
i,j=1,\cdots,N
, guarantees that
Y_{ij}\in B_{r}
and that the
o(\|Y\|_{\infty})
in (
3.11
) is smaller than
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|Y\|_{\infty}
. Then (
3.11
) becomes

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|^{+}\|Y(t)\|_{\infty}\leq-\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2}\|Y(t)\|_{\infty}+C\|H\|_{\infty}

for some absolute constant
C
, for all times
t\geq 0
such that
\|Y(t)\|<c_{1}
. If
\kappa
is large enough so that

\frac{2C\|H\|_{\infty}}{\kappa\lambda}<c_{1},

then by a standard exit-time argument,
Y(t)<c_{1}
for all
t\geq 0
and
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|Y(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2C\|H\|_{\infty}}{\kappa\lambda}
.

Our second Lyapunov functional measures the maximal mismatch between the two flows:

\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i,j=1,\cdots,N}|Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij}|.

(3.12)

Lemma 3.9

(Second Gronwall inequality)
.

Let
Y(t)=\{Y_{ij}(t)\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
and
\tilde{Y}(t)=\{\tilde{Y}_{ij}(t)\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
,
t\in[0,T)
be solutions to (
3.6
) with initial data
\{Y_{ij}^{0}\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
and
\{\tilde{Y}_{ij}^{0}\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
, respectively, with
Y_{ij}(t),\tilde{Y}_{ij}(t)\in B_{r}
for all
i,j=1,\cdots,N
and
t\in[0,T)
. Then

\dot{Y}_{ij}-\dot{\tilde{Y}}_{ij}=-\kappa\left[d\phi_{e}(Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij})+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})\right]+O(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|H\|_{\infty}),

(3.13)

and thus

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|^{+}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\leq-\kappa\left[\lambda\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})\right]+O(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|H\|_{\infty}),

(3.14)

where the little o notation is with respect to
\|Y\|,\|\tilde{Y}\|\to 0
.

Remark 3.10
.

Observe that (
3.13
) is not the difference of (
3.10
) for
Y
and
\tilde{Y}
. One can accurately guess the leading term
-\kappa d\phi_{e}(Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij})
in this manner, but cannot get the correct error terms. It is crucial that the error terms are controlled in terms of
\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|
, as opposed to simply
\|Y\|
and
\|\tilde{Y}\|
, in order to use (
3.14
) to fruition; this fact is the core mathematical content of the proof of Theorem
3.2
(2).

Proof 3.11
.

(Proof of Lemma
3.14
.)

We expand

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{ij}-\dot{\tilde{Y}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1}H_{i}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}H_{j}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1}\phi\circ\exp(Y_{ki})-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}\phi\circ\exp(Y_{kj})\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}-1}H_{i}+\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}}H_{j}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}-1}\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{ki})-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}}\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{kj})\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}-1}\right)\left(H_{i}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{ki})\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}}\right)\left(H_{j}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{kj})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Bigg[\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1}\Big(\phi\circ\exp(Y_{ki})-\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{ki})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}\Big(\phi\circ\exp(Y_{kj})-\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{kj})\Big)\Bigg].

By functional calculus (
2.8
),

\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}-1}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{e^{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}-1},\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{Y_{ij}}}}-\frac{\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}{1-e^{-\operatorname{ad}_{\tilde{Y}_{ij}}}}=O(|Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij}|),

and

\displaystyle\phi\circ\exp(Y_{ij})-\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{ij})=

\displaystyle(d\phi_{e}\circ d\exp_{0})(Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij})+o_{G,\phi}(|Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\phi_{e}(Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij})+o_{G,\phi}(|Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij}|).

So we may simplify

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{ij}-\dot{\tilde{Y}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=O(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}(\|H\|_{\infty}+\kappa\|Y\|_{\infty}+\kappa\|\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\Big(\phi\circ\exp(Y_{ki})-\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{ki})\Big)-\Big(\phi\circ\exp(Y_{kj})-\phi\circ\exp(\tilde{Y}_{kj})\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=O(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\|H\|_{\infty})+\kappa\cdot o_{G}(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\kappa}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[d\phi_{e}(Y_{ki}-\tilde{Y}_{ki}-Y_{kj}+\tilde{Y}_{kj})+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})\right].

Again, by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula (
3.7
),

\displaystyle Y_{ki}-Y_{kj}-\tilde{Y}_{ki}+\tilde{Y}_{kj}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{compatibility}}}{{=}}-Y_{ij}+\tilde{Y}_{ij}-a(Y_{jk},Y_{ki})+a(\tilde{Y}_{jk},\tilde{Y}_{ki})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{a-bound}}}{{=}}-Y_{ij}+\tilde{Y}_{ij}+O_{G}((\|Y\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}),

and this last big O term is again
o_{G}(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})
. Thus

\dot{Y}_{ij}-\dot{\tilde{Y}}_{ij}=-\kappa\left[d\phi_{e}(Y_{ij}-\tilde{Y}_{ij})+o_{G,\phi}(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})\right]+O(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|H\|_{\infty}).

Proof 3.12
.

(Proof of Theorem
3.4
(2).)
If
c_{1}
is small enough so that
\|Y\|,\|\tilde{Y}\|<c_{1}
implies that
Y,\tilde{Y}\in B_{r}
and that the
o(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty})
term in (
3.14
) is at most
\frac{\lambda\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}}{3}
, (
3.14
) becomes

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|^{+}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\leq-\frac{2\kappa\lambda}{3}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}+C\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|H\|_{\infty}

for some absolute constant
C>0
. By choosing
\kappa>\frac{3C}{\lambda}\|H\|_{\infty}
we have

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|^{+}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}\leq-\frac{\kappa\lambda}{3}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty}

for times
t
at which
\|Y(t)\|,\|\tilde{Y}(t)\|<c_{1}
. This hypothesis is satisfied by Theorem
3.4
(1) if
\|Y(0)\|,\|\tilde{Y}(0)\|<c_{1}
, in which case we have

\|Y(t)-\tilde{Y}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|Y(0)-\tilde{Y}(0)\|_{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa\lambda}{3}t\right).

(3.15)

Remark 3.13
.

Question
1.5
may be reformulated as follows. Is it possible that in a semi-local regime with intermediate coupling strength
\kappa
:

c_{1}^{\prime}<\max_{i,j=1,\cdots,N}d(X_{i}^{0},X_{j}^{0})<c_{1},\quad c\|H\|_{\infty}<\kappa<c^{\prime}\|H\|_{\infty},

with different constants
c_{1}^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}
, (
3.10
) holds yet (
3.13
