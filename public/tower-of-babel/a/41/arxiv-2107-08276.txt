Fractal uncertainty principle for discrete Cantor sets with random alphabets
Suresh Eswarathasan, Xiaolong Han

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the fractal uncertainty principle (FUP) for discrete Cantor sets, which are determined by an alphabet from a base of digits. Consider the base of M M digits and the alphabets of cardinality A A such that all the corresponding Cantor sets have a fixed dimension log ⁡ A / log ⁡ M ∈ ( 0 , 2 / 3 ) \log A/\log M\in(0,2/3) . We prove that the FUP with an improved exponent over Dyatlov-Jin [ DJ1 ] holds for almost all alphabets, asymptotically as M → ∞ M\to\infty . Our result provides the best possible exponent when the Cantor sets enjoy either the strongest Fourier decay assumption or strongest additive energy assumption. The proof is based on a concentration of measure phenomenon in the space of alphabets.

Introduction

The fractal uncertainty principle (FUP) was recently introduced by Dyatlov-Zahl
[
DyZa
]
and has quickly become an emerging topic in Fourier analysis. It concerns the phenomenon that no function can be localized in both position and frequency close to a fractal set. More precisely, the FUP is formulated in the context of estimating the norm

(1.1)

\|\mathds{1}_{X}\mathcal{F}_{h}\mathds{1}_{Y}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

in which
0<h<1
is the semiclassical parameter, the
h
-dependent sets
X=X(h),Y=Y(h)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are equipped with certain fractal-type structures, and
\mathcal{F}_{h}
is the semiclassical Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}_{h}f(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\frac{ix\cdot\xi}{h}}f(x)\,dx.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{h}
is unitary in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we always have that
\eqref{eq:FUP}\leq 1
. Following Dyatlov
[
Dy
, Definition 2.1]
, we say that
X,Y
satisfy the FUP with exponent
\beta\geq 0
if
\eqref{eq:FUP}=O(h^{\beta})
as
h\to 0
. It can be understood as one quantitative version of the uncertainty principle that no function can be localized both near
Y
in the position space and near
X
in the frequency space. The main objective about the FUP is to prove the existence of exponent
\beta>0
and to find the sharp one for which the FUP holds. Here, “the sharp exponent”, denoted by
\beta^{s}(X,Y)
, means the largest exponent such that
\eqref{eq:FUP}=O(h^{\beta})
holds for all
0<h<h_{0}
with some
h_{0}>0
. It usually depends on the regularity and dimensions of
X
and
Y
.

In this paper, via the discrete Fourier transform, we establish a new connection: the FUP and the concentration of measure phenomenon in asymptotic geometric analysis and probability
[
MS
,
L
]
. We follow Dyatlov-Jin
[
DJ1
]
and briefly recall the setup of the FUP for discrete Cantor sets. Each discrete Cantor set

and

(1.2)

\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A})=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}M^{j}:a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1}\in\mathcal{A}\right\}.

It is evident that
\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A}))=A^{k}
, where
A=\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{A})
, the cardinality of
\mathcal{A}
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and

be the Hilbert space of functions

with norm

\|u\|_{l^{2}_{N}}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|u(j)|^{2}.

Define the (unitary) discrete Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}:l^{2}_{N}\to l^{2}_{N}

(1.3)

\mathcal{F}_{N}u(j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}e^{-\frac{2\pi ijl}{N}}u(l).

For the discrete Cantor sets
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A})
and
N=M^{k}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the FUP (
1.1
) in terms of the operator norm

(1.4)

r_{k}:=\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\|_{l^{2}_{N}\to l^{2}_{N}}.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is unitary,
\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\|_{l^{2}_{N}\to l^{2}_{N}}\leq 1
. Throughout the paper, we denote

(1.5)

\delta=\frac{\log A}{\log M}.

Note that
\delta
is the dimension of the Cantor set

(1.6)

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}=\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}\bigcup_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\left[\frac{j}{M^{k}},\frac{j+1}{M^{k}}\right].

The FUP for discrete Cantor sets (
1.4
) in two extreme cases can be trivially derived:

(i).

If
\delta=0
(i.e.,
A=1
), then
\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A}))=1
so
r_{k}=N^{-1/2}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(ii).

If
\delta=1
(i.e.,
A=M
), then
\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A})=\{0,...,N-1\}
and
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}=\mathcal{F}_{N}
so
r_{k}=1
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

We therefore consider the cases when
0<\delta<1
(i.e.,
1<A<M
) in the following discussion. In these cases, the mapping
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}:l^{2}_{N}\to l^{2}_{N}
is given by a symmetric

matrix, which contains exactly

non-zero rows and columns (of the form
e^{-2\pi ijl/N}/\sqrt{N}
). Hence,

r_{k}=\left\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\right\|_{l^{2}_{N}\to l^{2}_{N}}\leq\left\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\right\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{C}_{k})^{2}}{N}}=M^{-k\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta\right)}=N^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\delta\right)},

in which
N=M^{k}\to\infty
as

and
1/N\to 0
plays the role of the semiclassical parameter

From (

\beta=1/2-\delta>0

\beta^{s}(M,\mathcal{A}):=\sup\left\{\beta\geq 0:r_{k}\leq N^{-\beta}\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

We now derive an immediate consequence of the FUP in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
for open quantum maps (also called the “quantized open baker’s map”), a popular model for the study of scattering theory in open chaotic systems. Again we follow Dyatlov-Jin’s program and refer to their paper
[

for a detailed presentation of the background and related literature.

For
M,A\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq 3
and
1<A<M
, an open quantum map
B_{N}
is defined as

B_{N}=B_{N,\chi}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{N/M}\mathcal{F}_{N/M}\chi_{N/M}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\chi_{N/M}\mathcal{F}_{N/M}\chi_{N/M}\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
\mathcal{F}_{N}
and
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}
is the discrete Fourier transform (
1.3
) and its inverse,
N=M^{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, and
\chi
is a cutoff function. For intuition, we consider the following elementary example. Let
M=3
and
\mathcal{A}=\{0,2\}
. Then

B_{N}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{N/3}\mathcal{F}_{N/3}\chi_{N/3}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&\chi_{N/3}\mathcal{F}_{N/3}\chi_{N/3}\end{pmatrix}.

The operator
B_{N}
is a quantization of the (classical) open baker’s map
\varkappa_{M,\mathcal{A}}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
defined as

\varkappa_{M,\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)=\left(Mx-a,\frac{\xi+1}{M}\right)\quad\text{if }(x,\xi)\in\left(\frac{a}{M},\frac{a+1}{M}\right)\times(0,1)\text{ for }a\in\mathcal{A}.

Formally,
\varkappa_{M,\mathcal{A}}
sends the points which are not in
(a,a+1)/M\times(0,1)
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
, to infinity, and therefore is “open”. The iterations
\varkappa_{M,\mathcal{A}}^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, induce a discrete dynamical system in the phase space
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. This dynamical system is chaotic with trapped set exactly as the Cantor set
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
(
1.6
), i.e., the points in this set stay in the finite region under the map
\varkappa_{M,\mathcal{A}}^{k}
as
k\to\infty
and
k\to-\infty
.

The scattering resonances of the open quantum system correspond to the eigenvalues of the matrix
B_{N}
. The spectrum of
B_{N}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Sp}(B_{N})
, is affected by the fractal structure of the trapped set,
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
, of the classical dynamics of
\varkappa_{M,\mathcal{A}}
. In particular, the matrix
B_{N}
has norm bounded by
1
and the spectral gap of
B_{N}
, i.e., the distance between
\mathrm{Sp}(B_{N})
and
1
, is directly provided by the operator norm in (
1.4
). (See Dyatlov-Jin
[

, Section 5]
for the independence of

on the cutoff function

[

Let

Outline of the proof

We briefly outline the proof of the FUP in Theorem
1.2
and the organization of the paper. For discrete Cantor sets
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, the FUP concerns the operator norm
r_{k}=\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\|_{l^{2}_{N}\to l^{2}_{N}}
as
k\to\infty
in (
1.1
). Such estimate is greatly simplified due to the algebraic structure of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. That is, Dyatlov-Jin
[
DJ1
, Section 3.1]
proved a “submultiplicativity property” that
r_{k_{1}+k_{2}}\leq r_{k_{1}}r_{k_{2}}
for all
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
(see Proposition
3.1
). Therefore, one reduces the FUP to the estimate of
r_{1}=\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{F}_{M}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}\|_{l^{2}_{M}\to l^{2}_{M}}
, which is the singular value of the corresponding matrix of
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{F}_{M}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
.

The matrix
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
has exactly
A=\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{A})
non-zero rows and columns, and non-zero entries of the form
e^{-2\pi ijl/M}/\sqrt{M}
for
j,l\in\mathcal{A}
, in the view of (
1.3
). We estimate
r_{1}
via the analysis of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})^{\star}
, which has non-zero entries of the form

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi(k-j)l}{M}}\quad\text{for }k,j\in\mathcal{A}.

By Schur’s lemma (see Dyatlov-Jin
[
DJ1
, Lemma 3.8]
and Dyatlov
[
Dy
, Equation (4.11)]
), we have that

r_{1}^{2}\leq\sup_{j\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi(k-j)l}{M}}\right).

We are then led to study the exponential sum
\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{2\pi(k-j)l/M}
for
k,j\in\mathcal{A}
with
k\neq j
and its dependence on the random alphabets
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)
.

To this end, we build a concentration of measure theory in the space
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
, see Section
2
. In short, the theory implies that a Lipschitz function on
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
has values exponentially concentrated around the expectation. Then in Section
3
we apply such theory to the above exponential sum as a function on
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
. Indeed, we show that this exponential sum (when
k\neq j
) always obeys a “square-root cancellation” so it is bounded by
M^{2\varepsilon}\sqrt{A}
for any
\varepsilon>0
(except a set of alphabets of exponentially small probability). Putting this estimate back to the one for
r_{1}
, we then have that
r_{1}^{2}\leq_{\varepsilon}M^{2\varepsilon}\sqrt{A}\cdot A/M
so

r_{1}\leq_{\varepsilon}M^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{4}\delta+\varepsilon\right)},

noticing that
A=M^{\delta}
by (
1.5
). Hence, the FUP in Theorem
1.2
follows.

2.
Concentration of measure in the space of alphabets

The concentration of measure theory in part is concerned with the phenomenon that in some metric spaces of large dimension and equipped with certain probability measure, any Lipschitz function has its values exponentially concentrated around the expectation. See the monographs Milman-Schechtman
[
MS
]
and Ledoux
[
L
]
.

In this section, we establish such a theory in the space of alphabets
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
. Equip
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
with the uniform counting probability measure
\mu
(
1.9
). Set the metric in
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
by

(2.1)

d(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})=\mathrm{Card}\left(\mathcal{A}_{1}\triangle\mathcal{A}_{2}\right)=\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{A}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{2})+\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{A}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{1})\quad\text{for }\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A).

Here,
\mathcal{A}_{1}\triangle\mathcal{A}_{2}
denotes the symmetric difference. Now, for any function
F:\mathbb{A}(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
, its Lipschitz norm is

\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=\max_{\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A),\mathcal{A}_{1}\neq\mathcal{A}_{2}}\frac{|F(\mathcal{A}_{1})-F(\mathcal{A}_{2})|}{d(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})}.

Under this setup, we have that

Theorem 2.1

(Concentration of measure in the space of alphabets)
.

Let
M,A\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq 3
and
1<A<M
. Then for any function
F:\mathbb{A}(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
and
t>0
,

\mu\Big(\left\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A):|F(\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{E}(F)|\geq t\right\}\Big)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{16A\|F\|^{2}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}\right),

in which
\mathbb{E}(F)
is the expectation of
F
with respect to
\mu
.

Theorem
2.1
is largely inspired by Maurey
[
Ma
]
and Schechtman
[
Sche
]
, in which they studied the concentration of measure theory in the space of permutations. See also McDiarmid
[
Mc
]
and Talagrand
[
Tal
]
for a more general theory on symmetric groups.

Remark
.

A similar result to Theorem
2.1
was obtained by Greenhill-Isaev-Kwan-McKay
[
GIKM
, Section 2.2]
using a different argument. We thank Jie Ma for this reference.

In Subsection
2.1
, we quote a relevant concentration of measure theorem for finite metric spaces as presented in Milman-Schechtman
[
MS
, Section 7]
and Ledoux
[
L
, Section 4.1]
. In Subsection
2.2
, we apply such a theorem to the space of permutations and then in Subsection
2.3
, we deduce our own based on the one for the permutations.

2.1.
Finite metric spaces

We first need a concept of “length” of finite metric spaces, as described in Milman-Schechtman
[
MS
, Section 7.7]
.

Definition 2.2

(Lengths of finite metric spaces)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space. We say that
(X,d)
is of length at most
l
if there exist positive numbers
a_{1},...,a_{n}
with

(2.2)

l=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{k}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and a sequence
\{\mathcal{X}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
,
\mathcal{X}^{k}=\{\Omega^{k}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m_{k}}
, of partitions of
X
with the following properties.

(i).

m_{0}=1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}^{0}=\{X\}
,

(ii).

m_{n}=\mathrm{Card}(X)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}^{n}=\{\{x\}:x\in X\}
,

(iii).

\mathcal{X}^{k}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{X}^{k-1}
for all
k=1,...,n
,

(iv).

for all
k=1,...,n
,
r=1,...,m_{k-1}
and
p,q
such that
\Omega^{k}_{p},\Omega^{k}_{q}\subset\Omega^{k-1}_{r}
, there exists a bijection
\phi:\Omega^{k}_{p}\to\Omega^{k}_{q}
with
d(x,\phi(x))\leq a_{k}
for all
x\in\Omega^{k}_{p}
.

Remark
.

Taking the trivial sequence of partitions
\mathcal{X}^{0}=\{X\}
and
\mathcal{X}^{1}=\{\{x\}:x\in X\}
, we see that the above conditions hold with

l=\mathrm{diam}(X):=\max_{x,y\in X}d(x,y).

It then follows that the length of a finite metric space is at most
\mathrm{diam}(X)
.

Let
F:X\to\mathbb{C}
. We remind the reader that the Lipschitz norm of
F
is

\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=\max_{x_{1},x_{2}\in X,x_{1}\neq x_{2}}\frac{|F(x_{1})-F(x_{2})|}{d(x_{1},x_{2})}.

Under these notations, as taken from Milman-Schechtman
[
MS
, Section 7.8]
, the concentration of measure phenomenon states that

Theorem 2.3

(Concentration of measure in finite metric spaces )
.

Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space of length at most
l
. Suppose that
\mu
is the uniform counting probability measure on
X
. Then for any function
F:X\to\mathbb{C}
and
t>0
,

\mu\Big(\{x\in X:|F(x)-\mathbb{E}(F)|\geq t\}\Big)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{4l^{2}\|F\|^{2}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}\right),

in which
\mathbb{E}(F)
is the expectation of
F
with respect to
\mu
.

2.2.
From metric spaces to spaces of permutations

Let
\Pi(M,A)
be the space of permutations of
A
elements from
\{0,...,M-1\}
, that is, each
\pi\in\Pi(M,A)
is an injective mapping
\pi:\{0,...,A-1\}\to\{0,...,M-1\}
. Equip
\Pi(M,A)
with the metric

d^{p}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})=\mathrm{Card}\{j=0,...,A-1:\pi_{1}(j)\neq\pi_{2}(j)\}\quad\text{for }\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi(M,A).

Here and thereafter, we use the superscript
p
to indicate the objects associated with permutations. Equip
\Pi(M,A)
with the uniform counting probability measure
\mu^{p}
, i.e.,

\mu^{p}(\Omega)=\frac{\mathrm{Card}(\Omega)}{\mathrm{Card}(\Pi(M,A))}=\frac{(M-A)!}{M!}\cdot\mathrm{Card}(\Omega)\quad\text{for any }\Omega\subset\Pi(M,A).

Then we have that

Theorem 2.4

(Concentration of measure in the space of permutations)
.

Suppose that
M,A\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq 3
and
1<A<M
. Then for any function
F:\Pi(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
and
t>0
,

\mu^{p}\Big(\{\pi\in\Pi(M,A):|F(\pi)-\mathbb{E}^{p}(F)|\geq t\}\Big)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{16A\|F\|^{2}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}\right),

in which
\mathbb{E}^{p}(F)
is the expectation of
F
with respect to
\mu^{p}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

To apply the concentration of measure theory in finite metric spaces, Theorem
2.3
, we need to estimate the length of
\Pi(M,A)
. To this end, we construct a sequence
\{\mathcal{X}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{A}
of partitions as follows. For
k=0
, assign
\mathcal{X}^{0}=\Pi(M,A)
. For
k=1,...,A
, write

\mathcal{X}^{k}=\left\{\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}}:j_{0},...,j_{k-1}\text{ are distinct in }\{0,...,M-1\}\right\},

in which

\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}}=\{\pi\in\Pi(M,A):\pi(0)=j_{0},...,\pi(k-1)=j_{k-1}\}.

That is,
\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}}
is the collection of permutations such that the first
k
elements are mapped to
j_{0},...,j_{k-1}
.

In Definition
2.2
, Conditions (i), (ii), and (iii) are clearly valid. For Condition (iv), choose

\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}r},\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}s}\subset\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}}
. Let
\tau
be the transposition that switches
r
with
s
. Then
\pi(\{0,...,A-1\})
and
\tau\circ\pi(\{0,...,A-1\})
differ by exactly two elements. Define
\phi=\tau\circ\pi:\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}r}\to\Omega_{j_{0}\cdots j_{k-1}s}
. It follows that

d^{p}(\pi,\phi(\pi))\leq 2.

Thus, we can take
a_{k}=2
for all
k=1,...,A
. The length of
\Pi(M,A)
is then computed by (
2.2
) and is at most

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{A}|a_{k}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=2A^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The proof is complete after applying Theorem
2.3
.
∎

2.3.
From permutations to alphabets

In this section, we establish our relevant concentration of measure theorem in the space of alphabets
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
(Theorem
2.1
) through a slight detour. That is, we build upon the corresponding theorem in the space of permutations
\Pi(M,A)
, which in turn depends on the length estimate of
\Pi(M,A)
(Definition
2.2
).

We adapt the concentration of measure result for permutations given in Theorem
2.4
to that for alphabets in Theorem
2.4
.

Recall that any permutation
\pi\in\Pi(M,A)
is an injective mapping from
\{0,...,A-1\}
to
\{0,...,M-1\}
. Define
P:\Pi(M,A)\to\mathbb{A}(M,A)
by
P(\pi)=\mathrm{Im}(\pi)
, the image of
\pi
, for
\pi\in\Pi(M,A)
. (That is, the mapping
P
removes the order in the permutation.) Then
P
is surjective and
\mathrm{Card}(P^{-1}(\mathcal{A}))=A!
for any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{A}(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
. Then
F
naturally induces a function
F^{p}:\Pi(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
by
F^{p}=F\circ P
. For the concentration of measure theory, we need to compare the expectation (with respect to different probability measures
\mu
and
\mu^{p}
) and Lipschitz norms (with respect to different metrics
d
and
d^{p}
) of
F
and
F^{p}
:

\bullet
Expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{p}(F^{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Card}(\Pi(M,A))}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(M,A)}F^{p}(\pi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(M-A)!}{M!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(M,A)}F\circ P(\pi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(M-A)!}{M!}\sum_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)}\sum_{P(\pi)=\mathcal{A}}F(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(M-A)!}{M!}\sum_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)}A!\cdot F(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Card}(\mathbb{A}(M,A))}\sum_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)}F(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(F).

\bullet
Lipschitz norm: Notice that if
P(\pi_{1})=P(\pi_{2})
, then

F^{p}(\pi_{1})=F(P(\pi_{1}))=F(P(\pi_{2}))=F^{p}(\pi_{2}).

Hence, it suffices to consider the case when
P(\pi_{1})\neq P(\pi_{2})
. In this case,

d^{p}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})\geq d(P(\pi_{1}),P(\pi_{2})).

It is based on a simple observation that if the combinations
P(\pi_{1})
and
P(\pi_{2})
satisfy that

d(P(\pi_{1}),P(\pi_{2}))=\mathrm{Card}(P(\pi_{1})\triangle P(\pi_{2}))=k,

then

d^{p}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})=\mathrm{Card}\{j=0,...,A-1:\pi_{1}(j)\neq\pi_{2}(j)\}\}\geq k.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|F^{p}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi(M,A),P(\pi_{1})\neq P(\pi_{2})}\frac{|F^{p}(\pi_{1})-F^{p}(\pi_{2})|}{d^{p}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi(M,A),P(\pi_{1})\neq P(\pi_{2})}\frac{|F(P(\pi_{1}))-F(P(\pi_{2}))|}{d(P(\pi_{1}),P(\pi_{2}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A),\mathcal{A}_{1}\neq\mathcal{A}_{2}}\frac{|F(\mathcal{A}_{1})-F(\mathcal{A}_{2})|}{d(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}.

With these facts, we now prove Theorem
2.1
:

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
F:\mathbb{A}(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
and
t>0
. With
F^{p}=F\circ P:\Pi(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
, we have that
\mathbb{E}^{p}(F^{p})=\mathbb{E}(F)
as shown above. Thus,

\left|F^{p}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}^{p}(F^{p})\right|\geq t

if and only if
P(\pi)=\mathcal{A}
for some
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)
such that

\left|F(\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{E}(F)\right|\geq t.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mu^{p}\Big(\{\pi\in\Pi(M,A):|F^{p}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}^{p}(F^{p})|\geq t\}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Card}(\Pi(M,A))}\cdot\mathrm{Card}\left(\{\pi\in\Pi(M,A):|F^{p}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}^{p}(F^{p})|\geq t\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(M-A)!}{M!}\cdot\mathrm{Card}\left(\{\pi\in\Pi(M,A):|F(P(\pi))-\mathbb{E}(F)|\geq t\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(M-A)!}{M!}\cdot A!\cdot\mathrm{Card}\left(\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A):|F(\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{E}(F)|\geq t\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Card}(\mathbb{A}(M,A))}\cdot\mathrm{Card}\left(\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A):|F(\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{E}(F)|\geq t\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A):|F(\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{E}(F)|\geq t\}\Big).

By Theorem
2.4
and that
\|F^{p}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}
as shown above,

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A):|F(\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{E}(F)|\geq t\}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu^{p}\Big(\{\pi\in\Pi(M,A):|F^{p}(\pi)-\mathbb{E}^{p}(F^{p})|\geq t\}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{16A\|F^{p}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{16A\|F\|^{2}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}\right),

which is Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Remark
.

It would be interesting to derive an estimate of the length of
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
, which is expected to be at most
O(\sqrt{A})
, as indicated in Theorem
2.3
. Such estimate leads to a direct proof of Theorem
2.1
without using permutations.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we prove the FUP for discrete Cantor sets with random alphabets in Theorem
1.2
. Recall the notations: Let
M,A\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq 3
and
1<A<M
so
\delta=\log A/\log M\in(0,1)
. Equip the space of alphabets
\mathbb{A}(M,A)=\{\mathcal{A}\subset\{0,...,M-1\}:\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{A})=A\}
with the uniform counting probability measure
\mu
as in (
1.9
).

By the FUP for discrete Cantor sets
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A})
as in (
1.2
) with random alphabets, we estimate

r_{k}=\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{F}_{N}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\|_{l^{2}_{N}\to l^{2}_{N}},

in which the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is chosen randomly from
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
with respect to
\mu
. The starting point of the above estimate is the crucial submultiplicativity property proved by Dyatlov-Jin
[
DJ1
, Section 3.1]
:

Proposition 3.1
.

For all
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.1)

r_{k_{1}+k_{2}}\leq r_{k_{1}}r_{k_{2}}.

Hence,
r_{k}\leq r_{1}^{k}
and it suffices to establish the bound of
r_{1}=r_{1}(\mathcal{A})
. In this case,
\mathcal{C}_{1}(M,\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}
and the mapping
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{F}_{M}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}:l^{2}_{M}\to l^{2}_{M}
is given by a symmetric
M\times M
matrix. Denote this matrix by
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
and its rows by
R_{j}(\mathcal{A})
,
j=0,...,M-1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{\star}
be the complex conjugate of
\mathcal{M}
. We estimate the largest singular value
r_{1}(\mathcal{A})
of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
via the analysis of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})^{\star}
, which has entries

\displaystyle F_{jk}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle R_{j}(\mathcal{A})\overline{R_{k}(\mathcal{A})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}(j)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{A}}(k)}{M}\sum_{l\in A}e^{\frac{2\pi i(k-j)l}{M}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{A}{M},&\text{if }j=k\in\mathcal{A},\\
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi i(k-j)l}{M}},&\text{if }j,k\in\mathcal{A}\text{ and }j\neq k,\\
0,&\text{if }j\not\in\mathcal{A}\text{ or }k\not\in\mathcal{A},\end{cases}

in the view of the discrete Fourier transform (
1.3
). For the exponential sum appeared above, we have that

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that
L>0
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Then there exists
\mathbb{G}_{L,m}\subset\mathbb{A}(M,A)
with

\mu\big(\mathbb{A}(M,A)\setminus\mathbb{G}_{L,m}\big)\leq 2e^{-\frac{L^{2}}{16}}

such that for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{G}_{L,m}
,

\left|\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi iml}{M}}\right|\leq L\sqrt{A}.

That is, outside a set of alphabets comprising exponentially small measure, the exponential sums exhibits “square-root cancellation”.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Define the function
F:\mathbb{A}(M,A)\to\mathbb{C}
by

F(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi imj}{M}}\quad\text{for }\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A).

The expectation
\mathbb{E}(F)
is straightforward:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Card}(\mathbb{A}(M,A))}\sum_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi imj}{M}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{M\choose A}^{-1}{M-1\choose A-1}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}e^{\frac{2\pi imj}{M}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Here, we first interchange the sums and use the fact that for any fixed
j=0,...,M-1
, the number of combinations
\mathcal{A}
which contains
j
is
{M-1\choose A-1}
. Observe that for
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A)
,

\left|F(\mathcal{A}_{1})-F(\mathcal{A}_{2})\right|=\left|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{2}}e^{\frac{2\pi imj}{M}}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{1}}e^{\frac{2\pi imj}{M}}\right|\leq\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{A}_{1}\triangle\mathcal{A}_{2}),

in the view of the metric (
2.1
). Hence,
\|F\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 2
and Theorem
2.1
implies that

\mu\Big(\left\{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{A}(M,A):|F(\mathcal{A})|\geq t\right\}\Big)\leq 2e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{16A}},

for any
t>0
. Taking
t=L\sqrt{A}
, we have the corollary.
∎

With these preparation, we prove Theorem
1.2
:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
L>2
be chosen later. By Proposition
3.2
, for each
m\in\{-M+1,...,-1,1,...,M-1\}
, there exists
\mathbb{G}_{L,m}\subset\mathbb{A}(M,A)
with

\mu\big(\mathbb{A}(M,A)\setminus\mathbb{G}_{L,m}\big)\leq 2e^{-\frac{L^{2}}{64}}

such that for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{G}_{L,m}
,

\left|\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi iml}{M}}\right|\leq L\sqrt{A}.

Write

\mathbb{G}_{L,M}=\bigcap_{m=-M+1,...,-1,1,...,M-1}\mathbb{G}_{L,m}.

Then

(3.2)

\mu\big(\mathbb{A}(M,A)\setminus\mathbb{G}_{L}\big)\leq\sum_{m=-M+1,...,-1,1,...,M-1}\mu\big(\mathbb{A}(M,A)\setminus\mathbb{G}_{L,m}\big)\leq 4Me^{-\frac{L^{2}}{64}},

moreover, for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{G}_{L}
and all
m=-M+1,...,-1,1,...,M-1
,

\left|\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi iml}{M}}\right|\leq L\sqrt{A}.

Pick any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{G}_{L}
and let
j\in\mathcal{A}
. Notice that
\{k-j:k=0,...,M-1\text{ and }k\neq j\}\subset\{-M+1,...,-1,1,...,M-1\}
and contains
A-1
numbers. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A},k\neq j}\left|F_{jk}(\mathcal{A})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A},k\neq j}\left|\sum_{l\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\frac{2\pi i(k-j)l}{M}}\right|\leq\frac{(A-1)L\sqrt{A}}{M}\leq\frac{LA^{\frac{3}{2}}}{M}.

Since the diagonal terms
|F_{jj}|=A/M
, Schur’s lemma (
[
DJ1
, Lemma 3.8]
and also
[
Dy
, Equation (4.11)]
) implies an upper bound of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})^{\star}
:

\max_{j\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\left|F_{jj}\right|+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A},k\neq j}\left|F_{jk}(\mathcal{A})\right|\right\}\leq\frac{A}{M}+\frac{LA^{\frac{3}{2}}}{M}\leq\frac{2LA^{\frac{3}{2}}}{M}.

Since
A=M^{\delta}
, the largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
satisfies that

r_{1}(\mathcal{A})\leq\sqrt{\frac{2LA^{\frac{3}{2}}}{M}}=\sqrt{2L}\cdot M^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{4}\delta\right)},

if
\delta<2/3
. For
\varepsilon>0
, set
\sqrt{2L}=M^{\varepsilon}
. Then
L=M^{2\varepsilon}/2
and

\displaystyle\beta^{s}(M,\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\log r_{1}(\mathcal{A})}{\log M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\log\left[M^{\varepsilon}\cdot M^{-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{4}\delta\right)}\right]}{\log M}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{4}\delta-\varepsilon,

for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{G}_{L}
(depending on
M
and
\varepsilon
) with

\mu\big(\mathbb{A}(M,A)\setminus\mathbb{G}_{L}\big)\leq 4Me^{-\frac{M^{4\varepsilon}}{64}},

in the view of (
3.2
).
∎

4.
Future investigations

For
M,A\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq 3
and
1<A<M
, still use
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
as the space of alphabets with cardinality
A
. In this paper, we consider the discrete Cantor sets with
random alphabets
, i.e.,

(i).

\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A})=\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}M^{j}:a_{0},...,a_{k-1}\in\mathcal{A}\right\}.

In this randomization process, one chooses an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
randomly from
\mathbb{A}(M,A)
(with respect to the uniform counting probability measure
\mu
); then the Cantor sets are constructed in each step
j\in\mathbb{N}
using the same alphabet
\mathcal{A}
of digits. The resulting Cantor sets
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
(
1.6
) always have dimension
\delta=\log A/\log M
.

The concentration of measure phenomenon in Theorem
2.1
emerges as
M\to\infty
(and
A=M^{\delta}\to\infty
as well when
\delta>0
). It is then responsible for the estimates of the exponent in the FUP for
\mathcal{C}_{k}(M,\mathcal{A})
with random alphabets (Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
).

The randomization process in (i) is designed to rigorously verify the observation in Dyatlov-Jin
[
DJ1
]
that the FUP with better exponent holds on average than the worst case, with respect to different alphabets of same cardinality. It is different from and should be compared with the
random Cantor sets
, which have been extensively studied in the literature, see Falconer
[
F
, Section 15.1]
