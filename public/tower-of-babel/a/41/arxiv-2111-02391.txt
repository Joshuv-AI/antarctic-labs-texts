Topologically driven no-superposing theorem with a tight error bound
Zuzana Gavorová

Abstract
To better understand quantum computation we can search for its limits or no-gos, especially if analogous limits do not appear in classical computation. Classical computation easily implements and extensively employs the addition of two bit strings, so here we study ‘quantum addition’: the superposition of two quantum states. We prove the impossibility of superposing two unknown states, no matter how many samples of each state are available. The proof uses topology; a quantum algorithm of any sample complexity corresponds to a continuous function, but the function required by the superposition task cannot be continuous by topological arguments. Our result for the first time quantifies the approximation error and the sample complexity N N of the superposition task, and it is tight. We present a trivial algorithm with a large approximation error and N = 1 N=1 , and the matching impossibility of any smaller approximation error for any N N . Consequently, our results exclude the superposition as an elementary gate for quantum computation, and limit state tomography as a useful subroutine for the superposition. State tomography is useful only in a model that tolerates randomness in the superposed state. The optimal protocol in this random model remains open.

State tomography for superposition

Here we prove Results

\hat{\mathcal{H}}

\mathcal{D}_{\text{pure}}(\mathcal{H})

Vector tomography should satisfy a precision guarantee similar to that of state tomography. State tomography protocols

Discussion

Our superposition impossibility is significant from a practical as well as a foundational point of view. Practically, quantum computation loses a potential elementary building block. From the foundational perspective, our impossibility sets quantum theory apart from classical computation, whether deterministic or probabilistic. Comparing to deterministic classical computation seems natural, because the quantum state tree
[
12
]
strongly resembles the arithmetic formula tree
[
25
]
. The resulting contrast is that the quantum
+
is impossible while the arithmetic
+
is easy. However, a more insightful comparison is to probabilistic classical computation, i.e., comparing the weighted superposition of states to a weighted addition of probabilities. We get another sharp contrast. Adding quantum states is impossible by a quantum postselection protocol, while adding probability vectors is done by the classical probabilistic protocol in
Figure

1a
. From this perspective, the impossibility is even more distinctly quantum than the no-cloning, because cloning classical probabilities is as impossible as cloning quantum states
[
26
]
.

Further consequences are the separations between computational models. The random model is strictly stronger than the trace-preserving or postselection model, when asked to implement certain tasks (functions). In terms of sample complexity, these separations are infinite! The separating task has restricted inputs (a ‘partial function’) or unrestricted inputs (a ‘total function’). Consider the superposition task with inputs restricted to orthogonal pairs. Replace all occurrences of

With unrestricted inputs (primarily considered in this work), we have shown superposition and vector tomography no-go results for any sample complexity in the postselection (or trace-preserving) model. As the second use of the topological method, our superposition no-go elucidates the method’s applicability. When no algorithm can implement a certain function (task), the topological method might be able to prove so, if both of its two steps are applicable: 1) the existence of an algorithm imposes continuity on the function, 2) topology shows that the required function cannot be continuous. We can reverse the direction, first asking which functions cannot be continuous to then find the tasks for which the topological method is relevant.
The topologically forbidden functions in this work are of the same type as those in

While the latter facilitate quantitative no-gos, the former, sections, provide intuition for the past tasks and a way to recognize future use cases. Specifically, the superposition and the controlled unitary

both seem to involve choosing an unphysical representative (vector or operator) for the physical input (density matrix or superoperator). No section means no representative can be chosen continuously. The method may work for other tasks that involve such choices. More generally, the tasks must be quantum. Consistent with the fact that boolean functions are always computable by a complex enough algorithm, the topological method is restricted to tasks (functions) with topologically nontrivial output spaces, e.g. density states or superoperators. In this sense, and as opposed to the polynomial method, the topological method is purely quantum. Quantum outputs are inherent in state complexity

\left|s_{ij}\scriptstyle(x,y)\right\rangle=\left|s_{ii}\scriptstyle(x,y)\right\rangle=\alpha(\operatorname{v}_{i}(x)+\operatorname{v}_{i}(y))
and

\displaystyle\left|\left|\left|s_{ii}\scriptstyle(x,y)\right\rangle\right|\right|_{2}=\alpha\left|\left|\operatorname{v}_{i}(x)+\operatorname{v}_{i}(y)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left(2-\left|\left|\operatorname{v}_{i}(x)-\operatorname{v}_{i}(y)\right|\right|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left(2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\langle i|x|i\rangle}}\left|\left|x\left|i\right\rangle-y\left|i\right\rangle\right|\right|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left(2-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\langle i|x|i\rangle}}\left|\left|x-y\right|\right|_{\operatorname{tr}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left(2-\sqrt{d}\frac{2}{\sqrt{2d}}\right)=\alpha(2-\sqrt{2})\text{,}

(42)

where we applied the triangle inequality in the first line, inequality (
38
) in the second line, the contractivity of the trace norm in the third line, and the
r
and
r^{\prime}
rules in the fourth line.
The resulting lower bound is our
\sqrt{K}
for this case.

Now suppose
\alpha=\beta
and

are such that
\left|\left|x-y\right|\right|_{\operatorname{tr}}\geq\frac{1}{2d}
. We have

\left|\left|\left|s_{ij}\scriptstyle(x,y)\right\rangle\right|\right|_{2}=\alpha\left|\left|\operatorname{v}_{i}(x)+\operatorname{v}_{j}(y))\right|\right|_{2}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\left|\left|x-y\right|\right|_{\operatorname{tr}}\geq\frac{\alpha}{4d}\text{,}

(43)

which is an application of (
39
). This lower bound is our
\sqrt{K}
for this final case. Taking the minimum
K

) is upper bounded by
\epsilon/2
. This completes the proof.
