Topological dynamics of kaleidoscopic groups
Gianluca Basso, Todor Tsankov

Abstract
. Kaleidoscopic groups are a class of permutation groups recently introduced by Duchesne, Monod, and Wesolek. Starting with a permutation group Γ \Gamma , the kaleidoscopic construction produces another permutation group 𝒦 ⁡ ( Γ ) \mathcal{K}(\Gamma) which acts on a Ważewski dendrite (a densely branching tree-like compact space). In this paper, we study how the topological dynamics of 𝒦 ⁡ ( Γ ) \mathcal{K}(\Gamma) can be expressed in terms of the one of Γ \Gamma , when the group Γ \Gamma is transitive. By proving a Ramsey theorem for decorated rooted trees, we show that the universal minimal flow (UMF) of 𝒦 ⁡ ( Γ ) \mathcal{K}(\Gamma) is metrizable iff Γ \Gamma is oligomorphic and the UMF of Γ \Gamma is metrizable. More generally, we give concrete calculations, in an appropriate model-theoretic framework, of the UMF of 𝒦 ⁡ ( Γ ) \mathcal{K}(\Gamma) when the UMF of a point stabilizer Γ c \Gamma_{c} has a comeager orbit. Our results also give a large class of examples of non-metrizable UMFs with a comeager orbit. These results extend previous work of Kwiatkowska and Duchesne about the full homeomorphism groups.

1.
Introduction

Dendrites are one-dimensional, compact topological spaces that appear naturally in dynamics (for example, as Julia sets of quadratic polynomials).
They are tree-like, but can have dense branching. This is the case for the universal spaces in this class, the
Ważewski dendrites

W_{n}
(where
n=3,4,\ldots,\infty
is the order of the branch points), whose topology is also captured by Fraïssé limits of finite trees and whose rich homeomorphism groups have been studied in topology, in dynamics, and in model theory
[
9
,
21
,
14
,
15
]
. Even though the spaces
W_{n}
are connected, their homeomorphism groups are
non-archimedean
(i.e., admit a basis at the identity consisting of open subgroups) and are best studied as permutation groups of the countable set of branch points of the dendrite.

Kaleidoscopic groups
are subgroups of
\Homeo(W_{n})
that were recently introduced by Duchesne, Monod, and Wesolek in
[
12
]
. For us a
permutation group
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is simply a
closed
subgroup of the full symmetric group
\Sym(M)
, endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence. Starting with a permutation group
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
, where
M
is a countable set with
n\geq 3
elements, the kaleidoscopic construction produces another group
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\leq\Homeo(W_{n})
whose
local action
is prescribed by
\Gamma
, that is, for any branch point
x\in W_{n}
, the action of the stabilizer
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{x}
on the connected components of
W_{n}\setminus\{x\}
is isomorphic to the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
. The groups
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
are Polish, non-archimedean, algebraically simple, and they have many other interesting properties, as discussed in
[
12
]
. They can also be represented as permutation groups acting on the branch points of the dendrite.
The construction was inspired by a classical paper of Burger and Mozes
[
8
]
on groups acting on trees but in contrast with
[
8
]
, kaleidoscopic groups are never locally compact because of the density of branch points.

In this paper, we are interested in the topological dynamics of kaleidoscopic groups and, more precisely, in their continuous actions on compact spaces (or
flows
). In particular, we study which dynamical properties are preserved when passing from
\Gamma
to
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
.
We recall that a flow is called
minimal
if every orbit is dense, and that, for any topological group
G
, there exists a
universal minimal flow (UMF)
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}(G)
, characterized by the property that it equivariantly maps onto any other minimal
G
-flow.

\mathcal{M}(G)
captures a fair amount of the dynamical information about
G
: the action on
\mathcal{M}(G)
is free whenever
G
admits a free flow, and
\mathcal{M}(G)
is a singleton iff every
G
-flow has a fixed point, for instance.
For locally compact, non-compact groups
G
, universal minimal flows are non-metrizable objects that are difficult to understand but, somewhat surprisingly, for large Polish groups, the UMFs often admit a concrete description and carry interesting combinatorial information. The landmark paper of Kechris, Pestov, and Todorčević
[
19
]
made explicit the connection between Ramsey theory and UMFs of non-archimedean Polish groups and motivated a lot of the recent work in structural Ramsey theory and in abstract topological dynamics. At the heart of the theory is the correspondence between the Ramsey property for the finite substructures of a given ultrahomogeneous structure
{\mathbf{N}}
and the fact that the automorphism group
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
is
extremely amenable
, i.e., such that its UMF is a singleton.

In the recent investigations of minimal flows of Polish groups, two dividing lines have emerged. The better established one is metrizability: if
\mathcal{M}(G)
is metrizable, it is always the completion of a homogeneous space of the form
G/H
for some closed, extremely amenable
H\leq G
that is unique up to conjugation
[
4
,
22
]
. In this case, one has a good understanding of the topological dynamics of
G
: for example, the minimal flows are all metrizable, they are concretely classifiable
[
22
]
, and for any
G
-flow
X
, the collection of almost periodic points in
X
is closed
[
28
]
.
Following
[
3
]
, we say that a Polish group is
CAP
if its UMF is metrizable.
Notice that if
G
is compact, then
\mathcal{M}(G)\cong G
, so in particular it is metrizable.
Another important property is that if
G
is CAP, then
\mathcal{M}(G)
, and therefore all minimal
G
-flows, have a comeager orbit.

In fact, the existence of a comeager orbit in
\mathcal{M}(G)
provides the second interesting dividing line.
In
[
1
]
, Polish groups with this property are said to have the
generic point property
.
It is a tameness condition, which still implies a strong structure on the category of minimal
G
-flows, but is not completely understood at the moment.
Indeed, it was only recently that Kwiatkowska
[
21
]
found the first examples of groups
G
such that
\mathcal{M}(G)
has a comeager orbit but is not metrizable, namely the homeomorphism groups of certain
generalized Ważewski dendrites
.
It is a consequence of our results about UMFs of kaleidoscopic groups that many of them have comeager orbits without being metrizable, suggesting that this phenomenon is more prevalent than previously thought.
Generalizing the metrizable case, Zucker
[
29
]
showed that if
\mathcal{M}(G)
has a comeager orbit, then it is of the form
\Sam(G/H)
, where
H\leq G
is closed and extremely amenable, and
\Sam
denotes the
Samuel compactification
. We refer to Section
2
for all relevant definitions and more details.

The main results of this paper connect properties of the original permutation group
\Gamma
and its UMF with properties of the UMF of the kaleidoscopic group
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
. Prior work about the full homeomorphism group
G=\Homeo(W_{n})
had been done by Kwiatkowska
[
21
]
(for all
n
) and Duchesne
[
15
]
(for
n=\infty
), who proved that
\mathcal{M}(G)
is metrizable and identified the appropriate subgroup
H\leq G
such that
\mathcal{M}(G)\cong\widehat{G/H}
. Duchesne also proposed (cf.
[
15
]
*Theorem 1.12) a concrete description of
\mathcal{M}(\Homeo(W_{\infty}))
as a space of linear orders on the branch points of the dendrite, which is unfortunately not correct. We discuss this in detail in Section
9
. In another paper
[
16
]
, Duchesne proved that the group
\mathcal{K}(C_{3})
, where
C_{3}
is the cyclic group of order
3
acting on itself by translations, also has a metrizable UMF. All of these previous results can be deduced from our general theorem below.

Our first result is a characterization of when
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))
is metrizable. Recall that a permutation group
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is called
oligomorphic
if the diagonal action
\Gamma\curvearrowright M^{k}
has finitely many orbits for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Below and everywhere in the paper, when we consider a permutation group
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
, the set
M
is tacitly assumed to be countable with
n\geq 3
elements.
This is so that the corresponding Ważewski dendrite and kaleidoscopic group
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
are well-defined.

Theorem 1.1

(cf.
Theorem

8.6
)
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be a transitive permutation group. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
is metrizable and the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is oligomorphic;

(ii)

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))
is metrizable.

Of course, item
(i)
above is trivially satisfied when
M
is
finite, so we obtain the following.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be a transitive permutation group with
M
finite. Then
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))
is metrizable.

As the action
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright W_{n}
is minimal, it is clear that if we want to search for an extremely amenable subgroup of
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
, we have to look at subgroups of the stabilizers of the points in
W_{n}
. As the action
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright W_{n}
has a comeager orbit, namely that of the
endpoints
(for the definition of the various types of points in
W_{n}
and their properties, see Section
4
), it is natural to study the stabilizer
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi}
for some fixed endpoint
\xi\in W_{n}
.

To that end, if
\Delta\curvearrowright M
is a permutation group that stabilizes some distinguished point
c\in M
, we construct a
root-kaleidoscopic group

{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
that is a subgroup of the stabilizer
\Homeo(W_{n})_{\xi}
. The root-kaleidoscopic construction
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
has a similar behavior to
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
: for any branch point
x\in W_{n}
, the local action of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)_{x}
on the connected components of
W_{n}\setminus\{x\}
is isomorphic to
\Delta\curvearrowright M

and the isomorphism is such that the component containing the root
\xi
is always labeled by
c
.

This construction has the crucial property that if
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is transitive, then
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})=\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi}
.
An important insight of our work is that, therefore, in order to describe
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))
, for transitive
\Gamma
, one needs to understand the dynamics of the point stabilizers of the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
rather than that of
\Gamma
itself.

Even though all results above are stated in the language of permutation groups, our combinatorial work is done in the model-theoretic setting of ultrahomogeneous structures (which we recall in Section
3
).
We associate ultrahomogeneous structures
{\mathbf{N}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
to
\Delta
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
, respectively.
The finite substructures of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
are rooted trees decorated by substructures of
{\mathbf{N}}
.

We then prove a transfer Ramsey theorem (
Theorem

6.8
), which states that rooted trees decorated with Ramsey structures have the Ramsey property.
This generalizes a result of Sokić
[
25
]
, in which the decorations are linear orders.
The proof goes by induction on the height of such trees, and uses a group-theoretic argument about permutational wreath products for the induction step (see
6.2
).

Theorem

6.8
translates to the following statement about root-kaleidoscopic groups.

Theorem 1.3

(cf. Corollary
6.9
)
.

Let
\Delta\curvearrowright M
be a permutation group, let
c\in M
, and suppose that
\Delta
stabilizes
c
. If
\Delta
is extremely amenable, then so is
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
.

Using this theorem, together with the techniques from
[
19
]
and
[
29
]
, we obtain the following, from which Theorem
1.1
can be deduced as a consequence.

Theorem 1.4

(cf. Theorem
8.4
)
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be a transitive permutation group and let
c\in M
. If
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{c})
has a comeager orbit, then so does
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))
. More precisely, if
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{c})=\Sam(\Gamma_{c}/\Delta)
for some closed
\Delta\leq\Gamma_{c}
, then

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))=\Sam(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)/{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)).

In order to obtain a more concrete description of the Samuel compactification, we develop model-theoretic framework to represent it as an appropriate type space.
It encompasses the relational expansions, which are used in
[
19
]
to describe metrizable UMFs of automorphism groups.

As opposed to Theorem
1.1
, which is a characterization, we do not have a converse of Theorem
1.4
. This is because it is not known whether the property of the UMF having a comeager orbit is closed under taking open subgroups (cf. Question
2.9
).

Theorem
1.4
is already interesting when
\Gamma_{c}
is the trivial group (so that one can take
\Delta=\Gamma_{c}
above). Recall that a permutation action
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is called
simply transitive
if it is transitive and free (so that it is isomorphic to the left translation action of the group on itself).

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be a simply transitive action. Then

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))=\Sam(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)/\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi})

and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))
is metrizable iff
M
is finite. In particular, if
M
is infinite,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma))
is a non-metrizable UMF with a comeager orbit.

As a consequence of the results of
[
29
]
, another way to view the space
\Sam(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)/\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi})
that appears in the above corollary is as the
universal highly proximal extension
of the action
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright W_{n}
. In Section
7
, we give an explicit description of this flow as a type space in an appropriate model-theoretic structure.

Corollary
1.5
gives a large supply of non-metrizable UMFs with a comeager orbit and a fairly concrete description of them, given by Theorem
7.1
. Continuum many such flows had been exhibited by Kwiatkowska
[
21
]
, as mentioned before. Her examples are parametrized by elements of the Cantor set, while Corollary
1.5
gives examples parametrized by countable groups, which, from descriptive-set theoretic point of view, are much more complicated.

In some cases, we can also calculate the
Furstenberg boundary
(or the
universal strongly proximal flow
) of
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
. This result generalizes one of Duchesne
[
15
]
about the full homeomorphism group.

Theorem 1.6

(cf. Theorem
8.11
)
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be a transitive permutation group and let
c\in M
.
If
\Gamma_{c}
is amenable and
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{c})
is metrizable, then the Furstenberg boundary of
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
is isomorphic to
\Sam(\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)/\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi})
.

Finally, we say a few words about our methods. It is well-known that model theory provides useful tools for studying oligomorphic permutation groups, because of Ryll-Nardzewski’s theorem.
To aid with the study of a non-oligomorphic permutation group
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
, we develop the notion of a
full structure
for
\Gamma
which associates to it an
\aleph_{0}
-categorical structure in a possibly uncountable language (cf. Theorem
3.3
) and which coincides with the classical construction in the oligomorphic case. While for model-theorists,
\aleph_{0}
-categorical structures in an uncountable language are probably just a curiosity, this viewpoint seems useful in dynamics: in particular, we obtain an interesting model-theoretic description of the universal highly proximal extension of the action
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright W_{n}
even when it is non-metrizable (cf. Theorem
7.1
).

After some preliminary results in dynamics and model theory, most of the technical work in the paper is about constructing the root-kaleidoscopic structures, establishing the connection between them and point-stabilizers in kaleidoscopic groups (in Section
5
), and proving the appropriate Ramsey theorem about them (Section
6
). The results about UMFs are collected and proved in Section
8
.

In Section
9
, we apply the techniques we have developed to the full homeomorphism group
\Homeo(W_{n})
and study the relation of its UMF to the flow of
convex, converging linear orders (CCLO)
on the branch points introduced by Duchesne, which he proposed as the UMF of
\Homeo(W_{\infty})
. More precisely, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.7

(cf.
Theorem

9.3
)
.

Let
n=3,4,\ldots,\infty
, let
G=\Homeo(W_{n})
, and let
X
denote the set of branch points of
W_{n}
. Then the following hold:

(i)

If
n
is finite, then the flow
G\curvearrowright\mathrm{CCLO}(X)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}(G)
;

(ii)

If
n=\infty
, then the flow
G\curvearrowright\mathrm{CCLO}(X)
is a proper factor of
\mathcal{M}(G)
. In particular,
\mathrm{CCLO}(X)
is not isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}(G)
.

2.
Preliminaries on topological dynamics

Let
G
be a topological group. A
G
-flow
is a continuous action
G\curvearrowright X
on a compact Hausdorff space
X
.
A
G
-flow is called
minimal
if every orbit is dense. A
factor map
between flows is a continuous, surjective,
G
-equivariant map. It is a classical fact, due to Ellis, that among all minimal
G
-flows there is a universal one that admits a factor map to any minimal
G
-flow and this property characterizes it uniquely up to isomorphism. It is called the
universal minimal flow
(
UMF
for short) of
G
and we will denote it by
\mathcal{M}(G)
. A group
G
is
extremely amenable
if
\mathcal{M}(G)
is a singleton.
Following
[
3
]
, we say that a Polish group
G
is
CAP
if
\mathcal{M}(G)
is metrizable. (The name comes from a characterization of CAP groups: a group
G
is CAP iff in every
G
-flow, the set of almost periodic points is closed. It turns out that this a more robust property that also makes sense for general topological groups.)

If
Y
is a uniform space, we will denote by
\Sam(Y)
the
Samuel compactification
of
Y
, i.e., the Gelfand space of the algebra
\UCB(Y)
of all uniformly continuous, bounded functions on
Y
. It is well-known and easy to see that for a metrizable uniform space
Y
,
\Sam(Y)
is metrizable iff
Y
is precompact and in that case,
\Sam(Y)
is simply the completion of
Y
.
The Samuel compactification has the following universal property: any uniformly continuous function
Y\to X
, with
X
a compact Hausdorff space, extends (uniquely) to a continuous function
\Sam(Y)\to X
.

Let
H\leq G
be a closed subgroup of
G
. The homogeneous space
G/H
carries a natural uniformity inherited from the right uniformity of
G
: a basis of entourages is given by sets of the form

\{(gH,vgH):v\in V,g\in G\},

where
V
varies over (a basis of) symmetric open neighborhoods of
1_{G}
.
In the case where
G
is non-archimedean, it is convenient to restrict to open subgroups
V\leq G
because then the sets above are equivalence relations whose classes are the
(V,H)
-double cosets. We will say that
H
is
co-precompact
in
G
if the space
G/H
is precompact in this uniformity or, equivalently, if for every open
V\ni 1_{G}
, there exists a finite set
F\subseteq G
such that
VFH=G
; if
G
is non-archimedean, this just means that the set
\{VgH:g\in G\}
of double
(V,H)
-cosets is finite for every open
V\leq G
. In particular, a permutation group
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is oligomorphic if and only if it is co-precompact in
\Sym(M)
.
We say that
H
is
presyndetic
if for every open
V\ni 1_{G}
, there exists a finite set
F\subseteq G
such that
FVH=G
.

The Samuel compactification
\Sam(G/H)
is always a
G
-flow.
When
G
is Polish,
\Sam(G/H)
is metrizable iff
H
is co-precompact and it is minimal iff
H
is presyndetic (
[
29
]
*Proposition 6.6). Flows of the type
\Sam(G/H)
appear naturally when one studies metrizable UMFs and UMFs with a comeager orbit. In the following fact, we collect the results that we will need.

Fact 2.1
.

Let
G
be a Polish group. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

For any closed
H\leq G
,
\mathcal{M}(G)=\Sam(G/H)
iff
H
is extremely amenable and presyndetic.

(ii)

\mathcal{M}(G)
has a comeager orbit iff there exists
H\leq G
extremely amenable and presyndetic. In that case,
\mathcal{M}(G)=\Sam(G/H)
.

(iii)

G
is CAP iff there exists
H\leq G
which is extremely amenable, co-precompact (and presyndetic).

Proof.

(i)
,
(ii)
This follows from
[
29
]
*Theorem 7.5.

(iii)
(
\Leftarrow
) is due to Pestov
[
24
]
but the argument is simple enough to include here. The flow
\Sam(G/H)
has the universal property that it maps to any
G
-flow with an
H
-fixed point. As
H
is extremely amenable, every
G
-flow has an
H
-fixed point, so any minimal subflow of
\Sam(G/H)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}(G)
. As
H
is co-precompact in
G
,
\Sam(G/H)
is metrizable and so is any subflow thereof.

(
\Rightarrow
) is Theorem 1.1 in
[
4
]
.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a topological group and let
K\leq H\leq G
be closed subgroups. If
H
is presyndetic in
G
and
K
is presyndetic in
H
, then
K
is presyndetic in
G
.
Similarly, if we substitute co-precompact for presyndetic.

Proof.

We prove the statement for presyndeticity; the proof for co-precompactness follows the same lines.

Let a neighborhood
V\ni 1_{G}
be given and let
U\ni 1_{G}
be open such that
U^{2}\subseteq V
. Let
F_{1}\subseteq H
be finite such that
F_{1}(U\cap H)K=H
. Let
U^{\prime}=\bigcap_{f\in F_{1}}fUf^{-1}
and let
F_{2}\subseteq G
be finite such that
F_{2}U^{\prime}H=G
. We claim that
(F_{2}F_{1})VK=G
. Indeed, let
g\in G
. There exist
f_{2}\in F_{2},u_{2}\in U^{\prime}
, and
h\in H
such that
g=f_{2}u_{2}h
. There are also
f_{1}\in F_{1},u_{1}\in U
, and
k\in K
such that
h=f_{1}u_{1}k
. Finally, we have that

g=f_{2}u_{2}h=f_{2}u_{2}f_{1}u_{1}k=f_{2}f_{1}(f_{1}^{-1}u_{2}f_{1})u_{1}k\in F_{2}F_{1}UUK\subseteq(F_{2}F_{1})VK,

as desired.
∎

It is easy to see that if
K\leq H\leq G
are closed subgroups and
K
is co-precompact in
G
, then
K
is co-precompact in
H
.
In particular, an oligomorphic subgroup of any permutation group is always co-precompact.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G
be a Polish group. Then the following hold:

(i)

If
H\leq G
is a closed, presyndetic subgroup and
\mathcal{M}(H)=\Sam(H/K)
for some closed
K\leq H
, then
\mathcal{M}(G)=\Sam(G/K)
.

(ii)

If
G
has a co-precompact CAP subgroup, then
G
is CAP.

Spaces of the form
\Sam(G/H)
, where
G
is non-archimedean, are not difficult to describe. We have the following.

Fact 2.4
.

Let
G
be a non-archimedean topological group and let
H\leq G
be a closed subgroup. Then

\Sam(G/H)=\varprojlim\beta(V\mkern 2.5mu\reflectbox{/}\mkern 1.0muG/H),

where the inverse limit is taken over the collection of open subgroups of
G
ordered by reverse inclusion and
\beta
denotes the Stone–Čech compactification.

Proof.

This follows from Gelfand duality and the fact that one can view the algebra
\ell^{\infty}(V\mkern 2.5mu\reflectbox{/}\mkern 1.0muG/H)
as a subalgebra of
\UCB(G/H)
by identifying it with the
V
-invariant bounded functions and the union of all of these is dense in
\UCB(G/H)
.
∎

Effros’s classical theorem from
[
17
]
about non-meager orbits of Polish group actions is usually stated about actions on Polish spaces but it holds in considerably greater generality. We are grateful to Andy Zucker for explaining this to us. It is also used in the proof of
[
29
]
*Theorem 5.5.

Fact 2.5

(Effros)
.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff space. Let
G
be a Polish group, let
G\curvearrowright X
be a continuous action, and let
x_{0}\in X
be a point with a dense orbit. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The orbit
G\cdot x_{0}
is non-meager;

(ii)

The map
G/G_{x_{0}}\to G\cdot x_{0},gG_{x_{0}}\mapsto g\cdot x_{0}
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

This is Lemma 2.5 in

is

*29.14 and that in a Hausdorff space, analytic sets can be obtained via the Souslin operation from closed sets (see

*6.6.8).

(iii)

(i).

The hypothesis implies that

is Polish. Suppose that it is meager. Then there exist closed, nowhere dense subsets

∎

If

and

.
We say that

is comeager.
If

If

, there is a (highly proximal) map

such that the appropriate diagram commutes.
An important feature of highly proximal extensions is that they preserve many dynamical properties.

Fact 2.6

(
[

Let

A flow

If

Proposition 2.7

Let

(iii)

Since

(iii)

3.
The model theory of permutation groups

Even though the Ważewski dendrites are connected spaces, their homeomorphism groups (and subgroups thereof) are non-archimedean and are best studied as permutation groups. An important tool for understanding infinite permutation groups is Fraïssé theory, where the group is viewed as the automorphism group of an
ultrahomogeneous structure
and one can study it via the combinatorial properties of the
age
of the structure. This is particularly helpful in topological dynamics, where one can use the Kechris–Pestov–Todorčević correspondence to study extreme amenability and describe the universal minimal flows of automorphism groups.

3.1.
Fraïssé limits and full structures

We recall all relevant definitions and establish some terminology and notation. Most of them are standard except perhaps for our treatment of expansions. A
signature

\mathcal{L}
is a collection of relation symbols
\{R_{i}\}_{i}
and function symbols
\{F_{j}\}_{j}
to each of which is associated a natural number called its
arity
. An
\mathcal{L}
-structure
{\mathbf{M}}
consists of a non-empty set
M
, called
the universe of
{\mathbf{M}}
, together with interpretations of the relation and function symbols. Each relation symbol of arity
k
is interpreted as a subset of
M^{k}
and each function symbol of arity
k
is interpreted as a function
M^{k}\to M
. A function symbol of arity
0
is called a
constant symbol
. A signature without function symbols is called
relational
. An
automorphism
of
{\mathbf{M}}
is an element of
\Sym(M)
which respects all relation and function symbols. A
substructure

{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathbf{M}}
is given by a subset
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
which is closed under the functions in the signature (in particular, it contains all constants). The interpretations of the symbols in
{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}
are inherited from
{\mathbf{M}}
. An
embedding

{\mathbf{M}}\to{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}
is an isomorphism from
{\mathbf{M}}
onto a substructure of
{\mathbf{M}}^{\prime}
. A structure
{\mathbf{M}}
is called
ultrahomogeneous
if every isomorphism between finitely generated substructures of
{\mathbf{M}}
extends to an automorphism of
{\mathbf{M}}
. In this paper, we will only be interested in countable structures (but in possibly uncountable signatures).

The
age
of a countable structure
{\mathbf{M}}
is given by the isomorphism classes of all finitely generated substructures of
{\mathbf{M}}
. Every age is countable,
hereditary
(closed under taking substructures) and
directed
(every two structures in the age embed into a third). An age is simply a collection of isomorphism classes of finitely generated structures with these three properties. It is easy to see that every age is the age of some countable structure. If
\mathcal{F}
is an age, we will say that
{\mathbf{M}}
is an
\mathcal{F}
-structure
if
\Age({\mathbf{M}})\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.

Fraïssé isolated an important additional property, called
amalgamation
, which characterizes the ages of ultrahomogeneous structures, and proved that for every age with amalgamation, there exists a unique countable, ultrahomogeneous structure with this age, up to isomorphism (often called its
Fraïssé limit
).

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a signature. An
atomic formula
in the variables
\bar{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
is of the form
R(t_{1}(\bar{v}),\ldots,t_{k}(\bar{v}))
, where
R
is a relation symbol and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
are
terms
, i.e., compositions of functions symbols. A
quantifier-free formula
is a Boolean combination of atomic formulas. A
first-order formula
(or just a
formula
) is one where we also allow quantifiers. Note that all quantifier-free formulas are preserved by embeddings but first-order formulas in general are not. For the purposes of Fraïssé theory, we may and will simply consider quantifier-free formulas as part of the signature. If
\theta(\bar{v})
is a formula and
{\mathbf{M}}
is a structure, we will denote by
\theta({\mathbf{M}})
the set
\{\bar{a}\in M^{\bar{v}}:{\mathbf{M}}\models\theta(\bar{a})\}
. Sets of this form are called
definable
(or
0
-definable
when we want to emphasize the absence of parameters).
For a fixed
B\subseteq{\mathbf{M}}
, we can consider the subsets of
M^{k}
which are
definable with parameters from
B
, or
B
-definable
, i.e., of the form
\{\bar{a}\in M^{\bar{v}}:{\mathbf{M}}\models\phi(\bar{a},\bar{b})\}
for some formula
\phi(\bar{v},\bar{w})
and
\bar{b}\in B^{\bar{w}}
. A structure
eliminates quantifiers
if every
0
-definable set is definable by a quantifier-free formula.

The
theory of
{\mathbf{M}}
, denoted by
\Th({\mathbf{M}})
, is the collection of all sentences true in
{\mathbf{M}}
.
Recall that the space of
k
-types
(for
k\in\mathbb{N}
) of
\Th({\mathbf{M}})
, denoted by
\tS_{k}(\Th({\mathbf{M}}))
, is the Stone space of the Boolean algebra of
0
-definable subsets of
M^{k}
. For
A\subseteq M
, we can also similarly consider the type space with parameters from
A
, denoted by
\tS_{k}(A)
. We also let
\tS_{\omega}=\varprojlim\tS_{k}
. These are all
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbf{M}})
-flows (if the set of parameters is
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
-invariant). They are metrizable if the signature is countable.
For a tuple
\bar{a}\in M^{k}
, the
type of
\bar{a}
, denoted by
\tp\bar{a}
is the element
p\in\tS_{k}(\Th({\mathbf{M}}))
given by

p(\bar{v})\models\phi(\bar{v})\iff M\models\phi(\bar{a}),\quad\text{ for every formula }\phi.

We say that such types are
realized
in
{\mathbf{M}}
. Any type
p\in\tS_{k}(\Th({\mathbf{M}}))
is realized in some model of
\Th({\mathbf{M}})
.

We will at some point use quantifiers, so we will also need the following weaker notion of homogeneity: a structure
{\mathbf{M}}
is
homogeneous
if for all tuples
\bar{a}
,
\bar{b}
from
M
such that
\tp\bar{a}=\tp\bar{b}
, there exists
g\in\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
such that
g\cdot\bar{a}=\bar{b}
. Of course, if a model is ultrahomogeneous, then it is homogeneous. A type is
isolated
if it is an isolated point in the corresponding type space. A model that only realizes isolated types is called
atomic
. It is a standard fact that a countable, atomic model is always homogeneous.

Let now
\mathcal{F}
be an age in the signature
\mathcal{L}
. An
imaginary sort

\mathcal{S}
for
\mathcal{F}
is given by a pair of quantifier-free formulas
\theta(\bar{v})
,
\epsilon(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{u}_{2})
such that
|\bar{v}|=|\bar{u}_{1}|=|\bar{u}_{2}|
and for every
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{F}
,
\epsilon({\mathbf{A}})\cap(\theta({\mathbf{A}})\times\theta({\mathbf{A}}))
is an equivalence relation on
\theta({\mathbf{A}})
. We define
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{A}})
to be the set of classes of this equivalence relation.

The following lemma records some basic facts about imaginary sorts and we omit the routine proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an age and let
\mathcal{S}
be an imaginary sort for
\mathcal{F}
given by
(\theta,\epsilon)
. Then the following hold:

(i)

For any
\mathcal{F}
-structure
{\mathbf{M}}
,
\epsilon({\mathbf{M}})\cap(\theta({\mathbf{M}})\times\theta({\mathbf{M}}))
is an equivalence relation on
\theta({\mathbf{M}})
and, in particular, we can define

\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{M}})=\{[\bar{a}]_{\epsilon({\mathbf{M}})}:\bar{a}\in\theta({\mathbf{M}})\}.

(ii)

If
f\colon{\mathbf{M}}\to{\mathbf{N}}
is an embedding between
\mathcal{F}
-structures, then
f
naturally defines an injective map
\mathcal{S}(f)\colon\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{M}})\to\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{N}})
by

\mathcal{S}(f)([\bar{a}]_{\epsilon({\mathbf{M}})})=[f(\bar{a})]_{\epsilon({\mathbf{N}})}\quad\text{ for }\bar{a}\in\theta({\mathbf{M}}).

Moreover, this correspondence is functorial.

(iii)

In particular, for any
\mathcal{F}
-structure
{\mathbf{M}}
, the group
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
acts on
\mathcal{S}({\mathbf{M}})
.

We will also allow to have symbols from the signature defined on imaginary sorts. This will be particularly useful for defining expansions.

If one is given a set
M
and a permutation group
\Gamma\leq\Sym(M)
, one can define a relational, ultrahomogeneous structure
{\mathbf{M}}
with universe
M
such that
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})=\Gamma
. There are many such structures and all of them give rise to equivalent Fraïssé classes, however they do differ from the point of view of model theory. First, we always make the assumption that
{\mathbf{M}}
is atomic: equivalently, for every
k
and every
\Gamma
-orbit
O\subseteq M^{k}
, there exists a quantifier-free formula
\theta(\bar{u})
such that
\theta(M)=O
. We will say that the formula
\theta

isolates
the orbit
O
. With this assumption, there are two canonical choices corresponding to the minimal and the maximal possible Boolean algebras of definable sets, respectively, under the constraint that the structure is ultrahomogeneous and
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})=\Gamma
. The minimal one is obtained by introducing a relation symbol for every
\Gamma
-orbit on
M^{k}
for every
k
and it has the advantage that the resulting signature is always countable; however, for us, it is the other one which will be more relevant.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
M
be a countable set and let
\Gamma\leq\Sym(M)
be a closed subgroup. We will say that a structure
{\mathbf{M}}
with universe
M
is
full for
\Gamma
if
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})=\Gamma
and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, every
\Gamma
-invariant subset of
M^{k}
is definable in
{\mathbf{M}}
. We will say it is
qf-full
if every such set is quantifier-free definable. A structure is
full
if it is full for its automorphism group.

Note if
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is a permutation group, then all full structures for
\Gamma
are interdefinable. If
\Gamma
is oligomorphic, then every structure
{\mathbf{M}}
with automorphism group
\Gamma
is full; if moreover
{\mathbf{M}}
is ultrahomogeneous, then it is qf-full. If
\Gamma
is not oligomorphic, then every full structure necessarily has a signature of cardinality continuum. As we will see shortly, a full structure is always
\aleph_{0}
-categorical
(i.e, the unique countable model of its first-order theory), which makes it a canonical choice for studying permutation groups model-theoretically.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
M
be a countable set and let
\Gamma\leq\Sym(M)
be a closed subgroup. Let
{\mathbf{M}}
be a full structure for
\Gamma
with universe
M
and let
T=\Th({\mathbf{M}})
. Then the following hold:

(i)

\tS_{k}(T)=\beta(M^{k}/\Gamma)
;

(ii)

{\mathbf{M}}
is an atomic model of
T
;

(iii)

The structure
{\mathbf{M}}
is
\aleph_{0}
-categorical;

(iv)

If
{\mathbf{M}}
is qf-full, then
{\mathbf{M}}
is ultrahomogeneous and eliminates quantifiers.

Proof.

(i)
A subset of
M^{k}
is definable iff it is
\Gamma
-invariant and the Boolean algebra of
\Gamma
-invariant subsets of
M^{k}
is isomorphic to the power set of
M^{k}/\Gamma
.

(ii)
The types realized in
{\mathbf{M}}
correspond to the principal ultrafilters in
\beta(M^{k}/\Gamma)
. As the principal ultrafilters are isolated, this implies that
{\mathbf{M}}
is atomic.

(iii)
We will show that any countable model of
T
is atomic. Then the claim will follow from the uniqueness of the countable atomic model. The proof is inspired by a construction of Blass
[
5
]
.

Suppose that
q\in\tS_{k}(T)
is a non-isolated type; our goal is to show that any model that realizes
q
is uncountable. Let
p_{0},p_{1},\ldots
enumerate the isolated types in
\tS_{k}(T)
and let
\phi_{i}
be a formula that isolates
p_{i}
. Let
\{B_{\alpha}:\alpha\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}\}
be a collection of infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
such that for all
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
,
B_{\alpha}\cap B_{\alpha^{\prime}}
is finite. Let
\psi_{\alpha}(x)
be the relation defined in
{\mathbf{M}}
by
\bigvee_{i\in B_{\alpha}}\phi_{i}(x)
and let
<
be the relation defined in
{\mathbf{M}}
by

x<y\iff\phi_{i}(x)\land\phi_{j}(y)\land i<j.

Let now
c
be a realization of
q
in some model
{\mathbf{N}}
and consider the collection of formulas

\{\exists x\ \psi_{\alpha}(x)\land x>c:\alpha\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}\}.

We claim that each one of them holds and their solutions are disjoint, showing that
{\mathbf{N}}
is uncountable. The first claim follows from the fact that the sentence
\forall y\exists x\ \psi_{\alpha}(x)\land x>y
holds in
{\mathbf{M}}
(because
B_{\alpha}
is infinite). For the second, note that for
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
, the sentence

\forall x\ \psi_{\alpha}(x)\land\psi_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x)\implies\bigvee_{i\in B_{\alpha}\cap B_{\alpha^{\prime}}}\phi_{i}(x)

is true in
{\mathbf{M}}
and the fact that
q
is non-principal implies that
c>y
for every
y
for which there is
i
such that
\phi_{i}(y)
.

(iv)
This follows from the fact that all definable sets in
{\mathbf{M}}
are
\Gamma
-invariant, so quantifier-free definable.
∎

3.2.
Expansions as types

Starting from
[
19
]
, metrizable universal minimal flows of automorphism groups of a countable structure
{\mathbf{M}}
are often represented as spaces of expansions of
{\mathbf{M}}
in a suitable relational signature. In principle, this can always be done and it works well when the expansion is simple (say, by a linear ordering) but in more complicated situations, a more expressive formalism may be convenient. For example, in order to describe the UMF of kaleidoscopic groups, we are naturally led to consider expansions by a constant not belonging to the original structure, which, while possible to deal with in the traditional KPT setting (see
[
21
]
), require a lot of coding and are less transparent.

Our definition of expansions is more general than the usual one on two counts: first, we allow expansions by function symbols (in particular, constants) that may add new elements to the structure, and second, we also allow new symbols on the imaginary sorts. Formally, let
{\mathbf{M}}
be an
\mathcal{L}
-structure and let
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathcal{L}
. An
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
-expansion
of
{\mathbf{M}}
is an
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
-structure
{\mathbf{N}}
together with a map
\iota\colon M\to N
such that:

•

\iota
is an
\mathcal{L}
-embedding;

•

N=\dcl^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\iota[M]
;

•

\iota[M]
is
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
-invariant.

Moreover, we allow some of the new symbols in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
to apply to the imaginary sorts of
{\mathbf{N}}
. As usual,
\dcl
denotes the
definable closure
, i.e.,
\dcl A
is the collection of elements
b\in N
such that the singleton
\{b\}
is
A
-definable.
The main property that our definition ensures is that an expansion gives rise to a closed subgroup of the automorphism group, which, as the proof of
3.5
shows, is all that matters.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}
be an
\mathcal{L}
-structure and
{\mathbf{N}}
be a
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
-expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
with embedding
\iota\colon M\to N
.
Then the restriction map
f\colon\Aut({\mathbf{N}})\to\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
defined by
f(g)=g|_{\iota[M]}
is a topological group embedding.

Proof.

We may assume that
\iota
is the identity, so that
{\mathbf{M}}
is a substructure of the
\mathcal{L}
-reduct of
{\mathbf{N}}
.
Since
M
is
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
-invariant,
g|_{M}\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbf{M}})
.

Suppose that
f(g)=\id_{M}
.
Then
g(b)=b
for any
b\in\dcl(M)=N
, so
g=\id_{N}
.

To show that
f
is an embedding, let
(g_{n})
be a sequence of elements of
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
such that
g_{n}|_{M}\to\id_{M}
.
Fix
b\in N
in order to prove that eventually
g_{n}(b)=b
.
Let
\phi(v,\bar{a})
define
\{b\}
; then for any
h\in\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
, which fixes
\bar{a}
, we have
h(b)=b
.
But
g_{n}\to\id_{M}
, so eventually
g_{n}\cdot\bar{a}=\bar{a}
and we are done.
∎

We say that an expansion is
relational
if
N=M
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{L}
is relational.

Next we explain how to represent any flow of the form
\Sam(G/H)
, where
G=\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
and
H\leq G
is an arbitrary closed subgroup, as a space of types for an appropriate expansion.
The following is just a restatement of Fact
2.4
in a model-theoretic setting but being able to use arbitrary first order formulas is convenient in applications and also makes the action of the group
G
more explicit.

If
{\mathbf{N}}
is an expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
, we denote

\Exp({\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{N}})=\{p\in\tS_{\omega}(\Th({\mathbf{N}})):p|_{\mathcal{L}}=\tp_{\mathcal{L}}\bar{a}\},

where
\bar{a}
is some fixed enumeration of
{\mathbf{M}}
. It will also often be convenient to identify the variables of the type with the elements of
M
via this fixed enumeration. Note that
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
acts naturally on
\Exp({\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{N}})
by permuting the variables.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}
be a countable structure in a signature
\mathcal{L}
, which is an atomic model of its first-order theory. Suppose that
{\mathbf{N}}
is an expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
in a signature
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, and that
{\mathbf{N}}
is full. Denote
G=\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
and
H=\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
. Then

\Sam(G/H)\cong\Exp({\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{N}}).

Proof.

From Fact
2.4
, we have that

\Sam(G/H)=\varprojlim\beta(V\mkern 2.5mu\reflectbox{/}\mkern 1.0muG/H)=\varprojlim\beta(G_{\bar{b}}\mkern 2.5mu\reflectbox{/}\mkern 1.0muG/H),

where the second limit is taken over all finite tuples
\bar{b}
in
M
. The double coset space
G_{\bar{b}}\mkern 2.5mu\reflectbox{/}\mkern 1.0muG/H
can be identified with the set of orbits of the action
H\curvearrowright G\cdot\bar{b}
. Now, since
{\mathbf{M}}
is atomic, it is homogeneous, so

G\cdot\bar{b}=\{\bar{c}\in M^{|\bar{b}|}:\tp_{\mathcal{L}}\bar{c}=\tp_{\mathcal{L}}\bar{b}\}

is an
\mathcal{L}
-definable set and by the fullness of
{\mathbf{N}}
, every
H
-invariant subset of
G\cdot\bar{b}
is
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
-definable, so we obtain a natural identification between the power set of
G_{\bar{b}}\mkern 2.5mu\reflectbox{/}\mkern 1.0muG/H
and the algebra of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
-definable subsets of
G\cdot\bar{b}
. Passing to the respective Stone spaces, we obtain the identification

\beta(G_{\bar{b}}\mkern 2.5mu\reflectbox{/}\mkern 1.0muG/H)=\{p\in\tS_{|\bar{b}|}(\Th({\mathbf{N}})):p|_{\mathcal{L}}=\tp_{\mathcal{L}}\bar{b}\}.

Finally, taking the inverse limit over all finite tuples
\bar{b}
in
{\mathbf{M}}
completes the proof.
∎

As a simple illustration, we show how to represent model-theoretically the flow
\Sam(G/V)
for an open subgroup
V\leq G
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\Sym(M)
be a transitive permutation group and let
{\mathbf{M}}
be a full structure for
\Gamma
. Let
c\in M
. Then the following hold:

(i)

\Sam(\Gamma/\Gamma_{c})\cong\tS_{1}(M)
as
\Gamma
-flows;

(ii)

\Sam(\Gamma/\Gamma_{c})
is metrizable iff
\Gamma
is oligomorphic.

Proof.

(i)
Apply Proposition
3.5
to the expansion by a single constant symbol
\mathbf{c}
which is interpreted as an element
c\in M
(it does not matter which one because of transitivity).
This expansion is a full structure for
\Gamma_{c}
since any
\Gamma_{c}
-invariant set
D\subseteq M^{k}
is defined by
\phi(\mathbf{c},\bar{v})
, where
\phi(u,\bar{v})
is a formula defining
\Gamma\cdot(\{c\}\times D)
in
{\mathbf{M}}
.

(ii)
(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose that
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is not oligomorphic. Then there exists a tuple
\bar{a}
in
M
such that
\Gamma_{\bar{a}}
has infinitely many orbits on
M
. Reasoning as above, we see that the expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
by constants for
\bar{a}
remains full. Therefore, by Theorem
3.3
,
\tS_{1}(\bar{a})\cong\beta(M/\Gamma_{\bar{a}})
, which is not metrizable. So
\tS_{1}(M)
, which surjects onto
\tS_{1}(\bar{a})
, is not metrizable either.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
\Gamma
is oligomorphic, then
\Gamma_{c}
is also oligomorphic and in particular,
\Gamma_{c}
is co-precompact in
\Gamma
. Therefore
\Sam(\Gamma/\Gamma_{c})
is metrizable.
∎

We recall a combinatorial criterion for presyndeticity. If
{\mathbf{M}}
is an ultrahomogeneous structure and
{\mathbf{N}}
is an ultrahomogeneous, relational expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
, we will say that
{\mathbf{N}}
is a
minimal expansion
of
{\mathbf{M}}
if for every
{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}\in\Age({\mathbf{N}})
, there exists
{\mathbf{A}}\in\Age({\mathbf{M}})
such that for every
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}\in\Age({\mathbf{N}})
which is an expansion of
{\mathbf{A}}
, there exists an embedding of
{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}
into
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
.
Zucker considered this condition in a slightly different context in
[
28
]
.
A similar property had already been studied in the literature under the name of the
ordering property
(see, e.g.,
[
19
]
), when the expansion is by a linear order, or the
expansion property
for general relational expansions (see
[
23
]
).
The definition above is equivalent to the expansion property for a precompact expansion but not in general. The following is well-known and implied in
[
28
]
but we could not find a direct reference, so we include the proof of the direction we need.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}
be a countable, ultrahomogeneous structure and let
{\mathbf{N}}
be an ultrahomogeneous, relational expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathbf{N}}
is a minimal expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
;

(ii)

\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
is presyndetic in
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

Write
G=\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
and
H=\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
. Let
\bar{b}
be a finite tuple in
M
and denote by
{\mathbf{B}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}
the substructure generated by
\bar{b}
in
{\mathbf{M}}
and in
{\mathbf{N}}
, respectively. (Note that because the expansion is relational,
{\mathbf{B}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}
are structures with the same underlying set
B
.) Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in\Age({\mathbf{M}})
be a witness of minimality for
{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}
and realize
{\mathbf{A}}
as a substructure of
{\mathbf{M}}
. Let
F
be a finite subset of
G
such that
\binom{{\mathbf{A}}}{{\mathbf{B}}}=\{f|_{B}:f\in F\}
. Our goal is to show that
G=HG_{B}F^{-1}
. To that end, let
g\in G
be arbitrary. We want to find
h\in H
and
f\in F
such that
hgf
fixes
B
. Let
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
be the substructure of
{\mathbf{N}}
with underlying set

. By minimality of the expansion and the choice of

, there exists
f\in F
such that
gf\cdot{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}\cong{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}
. Now we conclude by ultrahomogeneity of
{\mathbf{N}}
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

The proof is similar and we omit it.
∎

4.
Kaleidoscopic and root-kaleidoscopic groups

4.1.
Ważewski dendrites and kaleidoscopic groups

An important property of topological trees is that they are
uniquely arcwise connected
, i.e., between every two points there is a unique arc connecting them. (An
arc
is just a homeomorphic copy of the unit interval
[0,1]
.) Dendrites are topological spaces, generalizing trees, where branch points are allowed to be dense. More formally, a
dendrite
is a compact, metrizable, locally connected, uniquely arcwise connected, topological space.

Let
D
be a dendrite and for a point
x\in D
, denote by
\hat{x}
the set of connected components of
D\setminus\{x\}
. The points of
D
are classified by the cardinality of
\hat{x}
:
x
is called an
endpoint
if
\lvert\hat{x}\rvert=1
; it is called a
regular point
if
\lvert\hat{x}\rvert=2
; and it is called a
branch point
if
\lvert\hat{x}\rvert\geq 3
. If
x
is a branch point, the cardinal
\lvert\hat{x}\rvert
is called the
order
of
x
. The fact that
D
is second countable and locally connected implies that the order of every point is countable. We will denote by
\mathrm{End}(D)
,
\mathrm{Reg}(D)
, and
\Br(D)
the sets of endpoints, regular points, and branch points of
D
, respectively. The set
\Br(D)
is always countable and
\mathrm{End}(D)
is a
G_{\delta}
subset of
D
.

We will denote

(4.1)

\widehat{\Br(D)}=\bigsqcup_{x\in\Br(D)}\hat{x}.

When
a\in\hat{x}
will say that
a
is a component
around

x
. For a set
Z
, denote by
\Delta_{Z}
the diagonal
\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in Z^{2}:z_{1}=z_{2}\}
. Define the map

\Phi\colon(\Br(D)\times D)\setminus\Delta_{\Br(D)}\to\widehat{\Br(D)}

by

(4.2)

\Phi(x,y)=a\iff a\in\hat{x}\text{ and }y\in a.

In words,
\Phi(x,y)
is the component around
x
that contains
y
.

If
x,y\in D
with
x\neq y
, we will denote by
[x,y]
the unique arc connecting
x
and
y
in
D
and we let
(x,y)=[x,y]\setminus\{x,y\}
. We define the function
K\colon D^{3}\to D
by

(4.3)

\{K(x,y,z)\}=[x,y]\cap[y,z]\cap[x,z]

and we say that
K(x,y,z)
is the
center of
x
,
y
, and
z
. Note that if
x
,
y
, and
z
are branch points or distinct endpoints, then
K(x,y,z)
is always a branch point.

An arc
[x,y]
with
x,y\in\Br(D)
is called
free
if its interior
(x,y)
is an open set in
D
; equivalently, if
(x,y)
does not contain any branch points. The following theorem of Ważewski
[
26
]
(see also Charatonik
[
9
]
) is the basis for much of what follows.

Theorem 4.1

(Ważewski)
.

For every
n\in\{3,4,\ldots,\infty\}
, there exists a unique, up to homeomorphism, dendrite
W_{n}
such that:

•

every branch point of
W_{n}
has order
n
;

•

W_{n}
has no free arcs.

The second condition of the theorem is equivalent to the statement that
\Br(W_{n})
is dense in
W_{n}
.
It is not hard to see that
\mathrm{Reg}(W_{n})
and
\mathrm{End}(W_{n})
are also dense in
W_{n}
. The dendrite
W_{n}
is called the
Ważewski dendrite of order
n
.

Throughout the paper, we will usually suppress the number
n\in\{3,4,\ldots,\infty\}
from the notation and we will write
W
instead of
W_{n}
to avoid clutter.

The homeomorphism group
\Homeo(W)
acts transitively on each of the sets
\Br(W)
,
\mathrm{Reg}(W)
, and
\mathrm{End}(W)
and, by density, a homeomorphism
g
is determined uniquely by its action on each of these sets. The action
\Homeo(W)\curvearrowright\Br(W)
defines an injective continuous homomorphism
\Homeo(W)\to\Sym(\Br(W))
, where
\Sym(\Br(W))
is equipped with the pointwise convergence topology with
\Br(W)
considered as a discrete set. It was shown in
[
14
]
that this homomorphism is a topological group embedding and, consequently, the image is closed and
\Homeo(W)
is a non-archimedean Polish group.

Next we explain the construction of kaleidoscopic permutation groups carried out in
[
12
]
. To lighten notation, we will denote by
X
the set
\Br(W)
and as in (
4.1
), we write:

\widehat{X}=\bigsqcup_{x\in X}\hat{x}.

For the rest of the section and throughout the paper, fix a set
M
of cardinality
n
.
A function
\kappa\colon\widehat{X}\to M
is called a
coloring
if for each
x\in X
, the restriction
\kappa_{x}\coloneqq\kappa|_{\hat{x}}
is a bijection.

Definition 4.2

(
[
12
]
)
.

A coloring
\kappa
is
kaleidoscopic
if for all
x\neq y\in X
and all
a\neq b\in M
, there exists
z\in X\cap(x,y)
such that
\kappa(\Phi(z,x))=a
and
\kappa(\Phi(z,y))=b
.

It is shown in
[
12
]
that a kaleidoscopic coloring exists and is unique up to homeomorphism of
W
. Any coloring
\kappa
also defines a cocycle
\alpha_{\kappa}\colon\Homeo(W)\times X\to\Sym(M)
by

(4.4)

\alpha_{\kappa}(g,x)=\kappa_{g\cdot x}\circ g|_{\hat{x}}\circ\kappa_{x}^{-1},

which satisfies the usual cocycle identity:

(4.5)

\alpha_{\kappa}(gg^{\prime},x)=\alpha_{\kappa}(g,g^{\prime}\cdot x)\alpha_{\kappa}(g^{\prime},x).

We call
\alpha_{\kappa}(g,x)
the
local action of
g
at
x
.

Now let
\Gamma
be a closed subgroup of
\Sym(M)
and fix a kaleidoscopic coloring
\kappa
of
\widehat{X}
. Define the
kaleidoscopic group

\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
by

(4.6)

\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)=\{g\in\Homeo(W):\alpha_{\kappa}(g,x)\in\Gamma\text{ for all }x\in X\}.

It follows from the uniqueness of the kaleidoscopic coloring that
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
does not depend on
\kappa
, up to conjugation by an element of
\Homeo(W)
.

Below we collect several facts from
[
12
]
that we will use.

Fact 4.3
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\Sym(M)
be a closed subgroup and let
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
be defined as above. Then the following hold:

(i)

\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
is a closed subgroup of
\Homeo(W)
(and therefore also of
\Sym(X)
);

(ii)

The action
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright X
is oligomorphic iff the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is oligomorphic;

(iii)

The actions of
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
on
\mathrm{End}(W),\mathrm{Reg}(W),X
are transitive. In particular, the action
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright W
is minimal.

(iv)

For each
x\in X
, the map
\alpha_{\kappa}(\cdot,x)\colon\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{x}\to\Gamma
is a surjective homomorphism.

(v)

The action
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright\mathrm{End}(W)
is primitive, so the stabilizer
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi}
is a maximal subgroup of
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

We point to the statements in
[
12
]
.
Items
(i)
and
(ii)
are Theorem 1.9 and Theorem 1.4, respectively.
Item
(iii)
is Proposition 5.5,

(iv)
is Proposition 5.1, and

(v)
is Corollary 5.11.
∎

The groups
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
are never locally compact. This can be seen in many ways; perhaps the fastest way to deduce it from what we have said so far is to apply
(iii)
from the fact above: the set
\mathrm{End}(W)
is dense
G_{\delta}
and its complement is dense, so it is not
F_{\sigma}
and therefore cannot be a continuous image of a locally compact Polish group.

4.2.
Rooted kaleidoscopic groups

An important role in the theory of kaleidoscopic groups is played by the stabilizer
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi}
of an endpoint
\xi\in\mathrm{End}(W)
. By Fact
4.3
, this stabilizer does not depend on
\xi
, up to conjugation.
In this subsection we consider a rooted variant of the kaleidoscopic construction and we describe the relation between this construction and the endpoint stabilizer for a transitive
\Gamma
.

Let
\xi\in\mathrm{End}(W)
be a fixed endpoint which we call the
root
.
The root allows us to define two other functions which will be useful:
\rho\colon X\to\widehat{X}
by

\rho(x)=a\iff a\in\hat{x}\text{ and }\xi\in a

and the
meet
,
{\mathbin{\wedge}}\colon X^{2}\to X
by

x\mathbin{\wedge}y=K(x,y,\xi).

Since the meet is associative, we can write
\bigwedge\bar{x}
as a shorthand for
x_{1}\mathbin{\wedge}\cdots\mathbin{\wedge}x_{k}
.
The meet also defines a partial order
\preceq
on
X
by

(4.7)

x\preceq y\iff x\mathbin{\wedge}y=x,

which makes
X
a meet semi-lattice.

We define root-kaleidoscopic colorings, the appropriate analogue of kaleidoscopic colorings (cf. Definition
4.2
) for rooted groups. They have similar genericity properties but satisfy the additional condition that around each branch point the color of the component containing the root is always the same element
c
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
c\in M
be a distinguished element. A
rooted coloring
of
W
is a function
\kappa^{*}\colon\widehat{X}\to M
such that for each
x
,
\kappa^{*}_{x}\coloneqq\kappa^{*}|_{\hat{x}}
is a bijection and
\kappa^{*}(\rho(x))=c
. A rooted coloring
\kappa^{*}
is called
root-kaleidoscopic
if for all
x\in X
,
y\in\left(\xi,x\right)
and
a\neq c
in
M
, there is
z\in(y,x)
with
\kappa^{*}(\Phi(z,x))=a
.

Concerning the existence of root-kaleidoscopic colorings, it is possible to follow the approach of
[
12
]
and show that the generic rooted coloring is root-kaleidoscopic. However, we prefer to give a construction based on a given kaleidoscopic coloring, which will be useful later.

Let
\kappa
be a kaleidoscopic coloring (as in Definition
4.2
). Let
\sigma\colon M\to\Sym(M)
be a function such that
\sigma(c)=1_{\Sym(M)}
and
\sigma(a)\cdot a=c
for all
a\in M
. Define

(4.8)

\kappa^{*}(a)=\sigma(\kappa(\rho(x)))\cdot\kappa(a),\quad\text{where }a\in\hat{x}.

Lemma 4.5
.

The function
\kappa^{*}
defined by (
4.8
) is a root-kaleidoscopic coloring of
W
.

Proof.

That
\kappa^{*}
is a rooted coloring is clear from the definition. To check that it is root-kaleidoscopic, let
a\in M
and
x,y
with
y\in(\xi,x)
be given. By kaleidoscopicity of
\kappa
, there is
z\in(y,x)
such that
\kappa(\Phi(z,x))=a
and
\kappa(\Phi(z,y))=c
. But
\rho(z)=\Phi(z,y)
, so
\sigma(\kappa(\rho(z)))=\sigma(c)
is the identity.
Therefore
\kappa^{*}(\Phi(z,x))=\kappa(\Phi(z,x))=a
and
\kappa^{*}(\Phi(z,y))=\kappa(\Phi(z,y))=c
.
∎

Root-kaleidoscopic colorings are also unique up to translation by an element of
\Homeo(W)_{\xi}
(cf. Corollary
5.9
).
As in the non-rooted case,
\kappa^{*}
defines a cocycle
\alpha_{\kappa^{*}}\colon\Homeo(W)_{\xi}\times X\to\Sym(n)_{c}
using the same formula (
4.4
).

Now let
\Delta\curvearrowright M
be a permutation group which fixes some
c\in M
.
Fix a root-kaleidoscopic coloring
\kappa^{*}
.
We define the
root-kaleidoscopic group

{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
by

(4.9)

{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)=\{g\in\Homeo(W)_{\xi}:\alpha_{\kappa^{*}}(g,x)\in\Delta\text{ for all }x\in X\}.

It follows from the uniqueness of the root-kaleidoscopic coloring that
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
does not depend on
\kappa^{*}
, up to conjugation by an element of
\Homeo(W)_{\xi}
. When
\Gamma
is transitive, the root-kaleidoscopic group of a point stabilizer is equal to the stabilizer of
\xi
in
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
, as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be a transitive permutation group, let
c\in M
, let
\kappa
be a kaleidoscopic coloring of
\widehat{X}
, and let
\kappa^{*}
be the root-kaleidoscopic coloring given by (
4.8
). Then

{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})=\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi},

where the groups
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})
and
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
are constructed using the colorings
\kappa^{*}
and
\kappa
, respectively.

Proof.

By transitivity of
\Gamma
, there is
\sigma\colon M\to\Gamma
such that
\sigma(c)=1_{\Gamma}
and
\sigma(a)\cdot a=c
for all
a\in M
. Suppose that
\kappa
is a kaleidoscopic coloring and
\kappa^{*}
is defined by (
4.8
) from
\kappa
and
\sigma
.
It follows from the uniqueness of root-kaleidoscopic colorings that
\kappa^{*}
gives rise to
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})
by (
4.9
).
From the definition (
4.8
) it is clear that for all
g\in\Homeo(W)
and
x\in X
,

\alpha_{\kappa^{*}}(g,x)\in\Gamma_{c}\text{ if and only if }\alpha_{\kappa}(g,x)\in\Gamma.

Now, looking at the definitions (
4.6
) and (
4.9
), we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

5.
Structures for kaleidoscopic groups and endpoint stabilizers

In this section and the next lies the combinatorial core of this paper.
The goal is to prove the following statements, from which we will derive our description of the UMFs of kaleidoscopic groups.
If
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is a transitive permutation group and
\Delta\leq\Gamma_{c}
is a closed subgroup, for some
c\in M
, then:

•

if
\Delta
is presyndetic in
\Gamma_{c}
, then
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
is presyndetic in
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})=\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi}
(
5.13
).

•

if
\Delta
is oligomorphic then
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
is oligomorphic (
5.10
).

•

if
\Delta
is extremely amenable, so is
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
(
6.9
).

The first step is to reduce them to combinatorial problems, by representing
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
,
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})
, and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
as automorphism groups of appropriate ultrahomogeneous structures.

5.1.
Structures for the homeomorphism groups and endpoint stabilizers

First we explain how to view the full homeomorphism group of the Ważewski dendrite
W_{n}
as the automorphism group of an appropriate ultrahomogeneous structure. A similar approach was already used by Kwiatkowska
[
21
]
; our structure is somewhat different as we use a signature with function symbols rather than a relational one, and it is better adapted to our treatment of kaleidoscopic groups.

For the rest of this section, we fix
n\in\{3,4,\ldots,\infty\}
and as before, we will write
W
instead of
W_{n}
. We consider a signature
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{X}}
with one ternary function symbol
K
and we define a structure
{\mathbf{X}}
with universe
X\coloneqq\Br(W)
by interpreting
K
as the (restriction of) the center function given by (
4.3
).
It is a particular case of
5.4
that
{\mathbf{X}}
is ultrahomogeneous.

If
A\subseteq X
is a finite set, the substructure of
{\mathbf{X}}
generated by
A
is the smallest center-closed subset of
X
containing
A
; it can be visualized as a combinatorial tree whose geometric realization embeds as a subdendrite of
W
. A finitely generated substructure of
{\mathbf{X}}
is always finite. We denote
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}=\Age({\mathbf{X}})
.

The function
K
allows us to define (via a quantifier-free formula) the
betweenness relation

B
by:

B(x,y,z)\iff K(x,y,z)=y.

Conversely,
K
can be defined from
B
by

K(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=y\iff B(x_{1},y,x_{2})\text{ and }B(x_{1},y,x_{3})\text{ and }B(x_{2},y,x_{3}).

Thus for checking whether a certain map between
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}
-structures is an embedding, it suffices to see that it preserves
B
. This observation allows us to see that our structure is enough to recover the dendrite, at least as far as automorphisms are concerned.

Proposition 5.1
.

\Aut({\mathbf{X}})=\Homeo(W)
.

Proof.

By
[
14
]
*Proposition 2.4 and
[
12
]
*Proposition 2.4, the group of homeomorphisms of
W
is equal to the group of permutations of
X
which preserve
B
.
∎

If
{\mathbf{T}}\in\mathcal{F}_{{\mathbf{X}}}
and
x\in T
, we can define, as before,
\hat{x}
as the set of connected components of
T\setminus\{x\}
(where we view
T
as a geometric tree) and
\widehat{T}=\bigsqcup_{x\in T}\hat{x}
. This construction can be captured as an imaginary sort for
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}
. Indeed, consider the formulas

\theta(x,y)\equiv\big(x\neq y\big)\text{ and }\epsilon(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})=\big(x_{1}=x_{2}\land\neg B(y_{1},x_{1},y_{2})\big).

It is clear that
\epsilon
is precisely the equivalence relation “
x_{1}=x_{2}
and
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are in the same component of
T\setminus\{x_{1}\}
”. For a general
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}
-structure
{\mathbf{Y}}
, we will denote by
\widehat{Y}
the elements of this sort.

Now we describe a structure whose automorphism group is the stabilizer
\Homeo(W)_{\xi}
for an endpoint
\xi\in\mathrm{End}(W)
, which we call the root, as in
Section

4.2
.
Let
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mathbf{X}}
be the signature
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{X}}\sqcup\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a constant symbol. Let
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
be the
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mathbf{X}}
-expansion of
{\mathbf{X}}
obtained by taking
X^{*}=X\sqcup\{\xi\}
, interpreting
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as
\xi
, and interpreting the function
K
as usual by (
4.3
).
Notice that this respects our definition of expansion since
\xi\in\dcl(\emptyset)
and
X
is
\Aut({\mathbf{X}}^{*})
-invariant.
Recall that the root allows us to define
\rho\colon X\to\widehat{X}
, the meet
{\mathbin{\wedge}}\colon(X^{*})^{2}\to X^{*}
and the partial order
\prec
on
X^{*}
.
All of their definitions are by quantifier-free formulas, so they are also valid for the age of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
.

Similarly as before, we have the following.

Proposition 5.2
.

\Aut({\mathbf{X}}^{*})=\Homeo(W)_{\xi}
.

Proof.

The
\supseteq
inclusion follows from Proposition
5.1
and the definition of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
. For the other, suppose that
g\in\Aut({\mathbf{X}}^{*})
, viewed as a homeomorphism of
W
via the identification
\Aut({\mathbf{X}}^{*})\subseteq\Aut({\mathbf{X}})=\Homeo(W)
, moves
\xi
to another endpoint
\xi^{\prime}
. Let
x\neq y\in X
be such that
\xi,x,y,\xi^{\prime}
lie on an arc, in this order. Then, on the one hand, we have that
B(x,y,\xi^{\prime})
and as
g
is a homeomorphism, we obtain that
B(g^{-1}\cdot x,g^{-1}\cdot y,\xi)
. On the other,
g
is an automorphism of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
, so it preserves the binary relation
B(\cdot,\cdot,\xi)
and the fact that
B(y,x,\xi)
implies that
B(g^{-1}\cdot y,g^{-1}\cdot x,\xi)
, contradiction.
∎

5.2.
Structures for kaleidoscopic groups

Next we turn to kaleidoscopic groups. Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}
be a relational signature, let
{\mathbf{M}}
be an ultrahomogeneous
\mathcal{L}_{0}
-structure with
|M|=n
and let
\Gamma=\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
. (As we explained earlier, this entails no loss of generality: any closed subgroup
\Gamma\leq\Sym(M)
can be represented in this fashion.)

Fix
\kappa\colon\widehat{X}\to M
, a kaleidoscopic coloring of
W
.
We use
\kappa
to define an expansion
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
of
{\mathbf{X}}
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
be the relational expansion of
{\mathbf{X}}
in the signature
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{X}}\sqcup\mathcal{L}_{0}
, where the relations in
\mathcal{L}_{0}
are interpreted on the sort
\widehat{X}
, as follows.
For a
k
-ary relation
R\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\bar{a}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{k-1})\in\widehat{X}^{k}
, we define

R^{\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})}(\bar{a})\iff\exists x\in X\ a_{0},\ldots,a_{k-1}\in\hat{x}\text{ and }R^{\mathbf{M}}(\kappa_{x}(\bar{a})).

In other words, for all
x\in X
,
\kappa_{x}\colon\hat{x}\to M
is an
\mathcal{L}_{0}
-isomorphism.

It follows easily from the results of
[
12
]
that this structure captures the kaleidoscopic construction, as we see in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.4
.

The groups
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
and
\Aut(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}))
coincide. Moreover, the structure
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
is ultrahomogeneous and does not depend on the choice of kaleidoscopic coloring, up to isomorphism.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the definitions.

Next we prove ultrahomogeneity.
Let
{\mathbf{S}}
and
{\mathbf{T}}
be two finite substructures of
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
and let
f\colon{\mathbf{S}}\to{\mathbf{T}}
be an isomorphism.
For every
s\in S
,

\kappa_{f(s)}\circ f\circ\kappa_{s}^{-1}\colon\kappa_{s}[\hat{s}]\to\kappa_{f(s)}[\widehat{f(s)}]

is an isomorphism of finite substructures of
{\mathbf{M}}
. As
{\mathbf{M}}
is ultrahomogeneous, this isomorphism extends to some
\gamma_{s}\in\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
. Now it follows from
[
12
]
*Lemma 5.8 that there is
g\in\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)=\Aut(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}))
which extends
f
.

Finally, let
\kappa^{\prime}\colon\widehat{X}\to M
be some other kaleidoscopic coloring.
By
[
12
]
*Theorem 3.4, there is
h\in\Homeo(W)
such that
\kappa=\kappa^{\prime}\circ h
and it is easy to see that
h
defines an isomorphism between
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}({\mathbf{M}})
and
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa^{\prime}}({\mathbf{M}})
.
∎

5.3.
Structures for rooted kaleidoscopic groups

Let now
\mathcal{L}_{0}
be a relational signature as before and let
{\mathbf{N}}
be an ultrahomogeneous structure in the signature
\mathcal{L}_{0}\sqcup\{\mathbf{c}\}
, where
\mathbf{c}
is a constant symbol. As usual, let
N
denote the universe of
{\mathbf{N}}
and suppose that
|N|=n
. We denote by
c\in N
the interpretation of the constant symbol
\mathbf{c}
.

We also fix some root-kaleidoscopic coloring
\kappa^{*}
of
W
, with
\kappa^{*}(\rho(x))=c
for all
x\in X
.
We use it to define the expansion
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
of the structure
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
be the relational expansion of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
to the signature
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mathbf{X}}\sqcup\mathcal{L}_{0}
defined as follows:
for each
R\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
of arity
k
, we interpret
R
as a relation on
\widehat{X}
, which for
\bar{a}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{k-1})\in\widehat{X}^{k}
, is given by

R^{{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})}(\bar{a})\iff\exists x\in X\ a_{0},\ldots,a_{k-1}\in\hat{x}\text{ and }R^{\mathbf{N}}(\kappa^{*}(\bar{a})).

It will be convenient to establish some combinatorial notation and terminology. For us, a
combinatorial tree
is just an element of the age of
{\mathbf{X}}
. Two vertices of a tree are
adjacent
if there is no other vertex between them.
Similarly, we call a
rooted tree
an element of the age of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
. Note that every combinatorial tree as well as every rooted tree is finite. The
root
of a rooted tree is the interpretation of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. A
leaf
of a rooted tree is a
\prec
-maximal element. The
height
of an element
t
of a rooted tree
{\mathbf{T}}
is the number
\lvert\{s\in T:s\prec t\text{ and }s\neq t\}\rvert
. The
height
of the tree is the maximum of the heights of its elements. If
t\in T
, we denote by
\Succ(t)
the set of
\prec
-immediate successors of
t
. Notice that the immediate successors are adjacent to
t
.
If
A\subseteq T
, we denote by
\bigwedge A
the
\prec
-greatest lower bound of
A
. Every rooted tree
{\mathbf{T}}
with more than one vertex also has another distinguished vertex apart from the root, namely
\bigwedge(T\setminus\boldsymbol{\xi})
, the unique immediate successor of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. As we will use this vertex frequently, we denote it by
r_{{\mathbf{T}}}
. Note, however, that
r_{{\mathbf{T}}}
is not preserved by embeddings.
For
t\in T
, let

{\mathbf{T}}(t)\coloneqq\{s\in T:t\preceq s\}\cup\{\xi\}.

So
t=r_{{\mathbf{T}}(t)}
.

Our next step is to describe a cofinal family in the age of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
that is easier to work with.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite structure in the signature
\mathcal{L}_{0}\cup\{\mathbf{c}\}
and let
h
be a natural number. We define
{\mathbf{A}}[h]
to be the finite
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mathbf{X}}\sqcup\mathcal{L}_{0}
-structure such that:

•

the reduct of
{\mathbf{A}}[h]
to
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mathbf{X}}
is a rooted tree whose every leaf has height
h+1
and such that every non-leaf different from
\boldsymbol{\xi}
has exactly
|A|-1
immediate successors;

•

for any
s\in A[h]
which is not a leaf and not equal to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, the
\mathcal{L}_{0}\sqcup\{\mathbf{c}\}
-structure on
\hat{s}
, given by interpreting
\mathbf{c}
as
\rho(s)
, is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{A}}
. We denote by
\lambda_{s}
this isomorphism.

\includeinkscape

Figure 1.
A graphical representation of
A[h]
.

Proposition 5.7
.

The family of structures

\{{\mathbf{A}}[h]:{\mathbf{A}}\in\Age({\mathbf{N}}),h=0,1,\ldots\}

is cofinal in
\Age({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}}))
.

Proof.

First we show that for any
{\mathbf{A}}\in\Age({\mathbf{N}})
and
h\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a substructure of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
isomorphic to
{\mathbf{A}}[h]
.
We do so by fixing
{\mathbf{A}}\subseteq{\mathbf{N}}
and arguing by induction on
h
.
For
h=0
, choose any
x\in X
. Then the generated substructure
\langle x\rangle=\{x,\xi\}
of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{A}}[0]
.
Now suppose we have found
{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
which is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{A}}[h-1]
, for some
h>0
.
For each leaf
x\in S
, and each
a\in A\setminus\{c\}
, pick a point
x_{a}\in(\kappa^{*}_{x})^{-1}(a)
.
Let
{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}
be the substructure of
\mathcal{K}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
consisting of
{\mathbf{S}}
and all of these new points
x_{a}
. Notice that this set is closed under
K
. It is also easy to see that
{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{A}}[h]
.

Conversely, let
{\mathbf{S}}
be a finite substructure of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
. Let
A=\{\kappa^{*}(a):a\in\widehat{S}\}
and let
{\mathbf{A}}
be the substructure of
{\mathbf{N}}
with universe
A
. We also let
h
be the height of
{\mathbf{S}}
and we define an embedding
f
of
{\mathbf{S}}
into
{\mathbf{A}}[h]
by induction as follows. First we let
f(\xi)=\xi
and
f(r_{\mathbf{S}})=r_{{\mathbf{A}}[h]}
. Then, supposing that for some non-leaf
s\in S
,
f(s)
has been defined, we let

f|_{\Succ(s)}=\lambda_{f(s)}^{-1}\circ\kappa_{s}^{*}.

It is easy to check that
f
is an embedding.
∎

Now let
\Delta\curvearrowright N
be a permutation group which fixes some
c\in N
.
Let
{\mathbf{N}}
be an ultrahomogeneous structure in the signature
\mathcal{L}_{0}\cup\{\mathbf{c}\}
, where
\mathbf{c}
is a constant symbol, interpreted as
c
, such that
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})=\Delta
.

Theorem 5.8
.

The groups
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
and
\Aut({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}}))
coincide.
Moreover,
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
is ultrahomogeneous and does not depend on the choice of
\kappa^{*}
, up to isomorphism.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the definitions.

To verify ultrahomogeneity, we apply the standard back-and-forth argument. Given an isomorphism
f\colon{\mathbf{S}}\to{\mathbf{T}}
between finite substructures of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
and a point
x\in{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})\setminus S
, it suffices to extend
f
to an embedding
f^{\prime}\colon\langle S,x\rangle\to{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
.

Denote
{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}=\langle S,x\rangle
and let
y
be the immediate
\prec
-predecessor of
x
in
{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}
.
There are three cases depending on the position of
y
.

Case 1.

y\in S
. Let
a=\Phi(y,x)
.
The isomorphism
f
induces an isomorphism
\tilde{f}\colon\kappa^{*}_{y}[\widehat{S}]\to\kappa^{*}_{f(y)}[\widehat{T}]
between finite substructures of
{\mathbf{N}}
. Since
{\mathbf{N}}
is ultrahomogeneous, there is
\tilde{g}\in\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
that extends
\tilde{f}
.
Let
b=(\kappa^{*}_{f(y)})^{-1}(\tilde{g}(\kappa^{*}(a)))
. Finally, choose any
x^{\prime}\in b\setminus T
and let
f^{\prime}=f\cup\{(x,x^{\prime})\}
.

Case 2.

y\in(\xi,r_{\mathbf{S}})
. Let
a=\Phi(y,r_{\mathbf{S}})
and notice that
\kappa^{*}(a)\neq c
. By root-kaleidoscopicity of
\kappa^{*}
, there is
y^{\prime}\in(\xi,f(r_{\mathbf{S}}))
such that
\kappa^{*}(\Phi(y^{\prime},f(r_{\mathbf{S}})))=\kappa^{*}(a)
. We let
f^{\prime}=f\cup\{(y,y^{\prime})\}
. If
x=y
, we are done; if not, we have reduced to Case 1.

Case 3.
There are
z_{1},z_{2}\in S
adjacent in
{\mathbf{S}}
, with
z_{1}\in(\xi,z_{2})
and such that
y\in(z_{1},z_{2})
. Let
a=\Phi(y,z_{2})
and note that
\kappa^{*}(a)\neq c
. By root-kaleidoscopicity of
\kappa^{*}
, there is
y^{\prime}\in(f(z_{1}),f(z_{2}))
such that
\kappa^{*}(\Phi(y^{\prime},f(z_{2})))=\kappa^{*}(a)
. We let
f^{\prime}=f\cup\{(y,y^{\prime})\}
. If
x=y
, we are done; otherwise, we have again reduced to Case 1. This concludes the proof of ultrahomogeneity.

If
{\kappa^{*}}^{\prime}
is another root-kaleidoscopic coloring, then
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\kappa^{*}}({\mathbf{N}})
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{{\kappa^{*}}^{\prime}}({\mathbf{N}})
are two ultrahomogeneous structures with the same age (by Proposition
5.7
), so they are isomorphic by Fraïssé’s theorem.
∎

By considering a structure
{\mathbf{N}}
with trivial automorphism group, we obtain the following.

Corollary 5.9
.

If
\kappa^{*}_{1}
and
\kappa^{*}_{2}
are two root-kaleidoscopic colorings, then there exists
g\in\Homeo(W)_{\xi}
such that
\kappa^{*}_{2}=\kappa^{*}_{1}\circ g
.

Proof.

If
g
is an isomorphism between
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\kappa^{*}_{1}}({\mathbf{N}})
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\kappa^{*}_{2}}({\mathbf{N}})
, then
g
preserves betweenness on
X
and
\xi
, so
g\in\Homeo(W)_{\xi}
. Since
{\mathbf{N}}
has trivial automorphism group, it follows that
\kappa^{*}_{2}=\kappa^{*}_{1}\circ g
.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
\Delta\curvearrowright N
be a permutation group which fixes some
c\in N
.
Then
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)\curvearrowright X
is oligomorphic if and only if
\Delta\curvearrowright N
is.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)

Let
{\mathbf{N}}
be an ultrahomogeneous structure such that
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})=\Delta
, as above. The hypothesis means that we can choose a signature for
{\mathbf{N}}
that contains, apart from
\mathbf{c}
, finitely many relation symbols in each arity. Theorem
5.8
implies that the conclusion is equivalent to: for every
k
, there are only finitely many
k
-generated structures in
\Age({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}}))
, up to isomorphism. This follows from the fact that a
k
-generated substructure of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
has at most
2k
elements and that a finite substructure
{\mathbf{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
is determined by its reduct to the signature of
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
and the isomorphism types of
\hat{t}
for
t\in T
, as elements of
\Age({\mathbf{N}})
.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose there is
k>0
such that there are infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},{\mathbf{A}}_{2},\ldots
elements of
\Age({\mathbf{N}})
with
k+1
elements (including
\mathbf{c}
). Then
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}[1],{\mathbf{A}}_{2}[1],\ldots
are
k
-generated non-isomorphic elements of
\Age({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}}))
.
∎

Now fix
x\in X
and a component
a\in\hat{x}
which does not contain the root
\xi
.
Then
a\cup\{x\}
is a dendrite homeomorphic to
W
(by Theorem
4.1
) and
\kappa^{*}
restricted to it is again a root-kaleidoscopic coloring, this time with root
x
.
By Corollary
5.9
, there exists a homeomorphism
h_{a}\colon W\to a\cup\{x\}
mapping
\xi
to
x
and such that
\kappa^{*}=\kappa^{*}\circ h_{a}
. Using these homeomorphisms, one can run the argument in
[
12
]
*Proposition 5.1 to obtain the following.

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
\Delta\curvearrowright n
be a permutation group which fixes some
c\in n
and let
x\in X
.
Then the homomorphism
\alpha_{\kappa^{*}}(\cdot,x)\colon{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)_{x}\to\Delta
is
split-surjective
, i.e., there exists a continuous homomorphism
\phi\colon\Delta\to{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)_{x}
with a closed image such that
c_{\kappa}^{*}(\cdot,x)\circ\phi=\id_{\Delta}
.

5.4.
The endpoint stabilizer and root-kaleidoscopic groups

Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}
be a relational signature, let
{\mathbf{M}}
be an ultrahomogeneous
\mathcal{L}_{0}
-structure with universe
M
and let
\Gamma=\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
. Assume that the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
is transitive. Let
c\in M
and let
\Delta
be a closed subgroup of
\Gamma_{c}
. In this subsection, we explain how to represent
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)
as the automorphism group of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
for an appropriate expansion
{\mathbf{N}}
of the structure
{\mathbf{M}}
satisfying
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})=\Delta
.

Let
\kappa
be some fixed kaleidoscopic coloring of
W
. We have seen in Proposition
5.4
that
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)=\Aut(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}))
. Let
{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}
be the expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
to the signature
\mathcal{L}_{0}\sqcup\{\mathbf{c}\}
obtained by interpreting the symbol
\mathbf{c}
as
c
. Then
\Aut({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})=\Gamma_{c}
. Let
{\mathbf{N}}
be an expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}
to a signature
\mathcal{L}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{L}_{1}\sqcup\{\mathbf{c}\}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is relational,
{\mathbf{N}}
is ultrahomogeneous, and
\Aut({\mathbf{N}})=\Delta
.

We have that
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})=\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi}
by
4.6
, and therefore that
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)\leq{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})\leq\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
. We see how this is reflected at the level of structures and expansions.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be transitive and let
{\mathbf{M}}
,
{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}
and
{\mathbf{N}}
be defined as above. Then the following hold:

(i)

{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
is a relational expansion of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
is an expansion of
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
;

(ii)

{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Delta)=\Aut({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}}))
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}(\Gamma_{c})=\Aut({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}))=\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)_{\xi}
.

Proof.

(i)
It is clear that
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}})
is a relational expansion of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
. The only symbol of the signature of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
which is not in the signature of
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
is the constant symbol
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and the respective universes of
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
are

and

. Thus to check the claim, it suffices to prove that the identity on
X
is an embedding, that is, that the interpretations of the other symbols agree on
X
and
\widehat{X}
. We have already checked that
{\mathbf{X}}^{*}
is an expansion of
{\mathbf{X}}
. For a relation symbol
R
in
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\bar{a}\in\hat{x}^{k}
, we have

R^{{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})}(\bar{a})\iff R^{{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}}(\kappa^{*}(\bar{a}))\iff R^{\mathbf{M}}(\kappa(\bar{a}))\iff R^{\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})}(\bar{a}).

The middle equivalence follows from the definition (
4.8
) of
\kappa^{*}
and the fact that we can use
\sigma
which take values in
\Gamma=\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
.

(ii)
This is
4.6
and
Theorem

5.8
applied to

5.5.
Rendering structures full

In our representation theorems of flows as type spaces, it will be important that we deal with full structures. It is unfortunately not true in general that
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
is full, even if we start with a full structure
{\mathbf{M}}
, unless
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
is oligomorphic (or, equivalently,
{\mathbf{M}}
is
\aleph_{0}
-categorical in a countable language).
So our remedy is to render them qf-full by expanding the language to include relations for all
\Aut(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}))
-invariant sets of tuples. We denote this full expansion by
\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})
and its signature by
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
and note that by Theorem
3.3
, it eliminates quantifiers (and, of course, its automorphism group is equal to
\Aut(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}))
).
If
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})
is transitive and
c\in M
, we define
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
as the relational expansion of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
to the signature
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\coloneqq\overline{\mathcal{L}}\sqcup\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\}
. Note that this expansion does not change the automorphism group of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
and that for any quantifier-free
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\phi(\bar{v})
and
\bar{x}\in X^{\bar{v}}
, we have that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})\models\phi(\bar{x})\iff\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})\models\phi(\bar{x})
.

We will call a formula
\phi(\bar{v})

isolating
if it isolates a type, i.e.,
\phi(\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}}))
is a single
\Aut(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}))
-orbit. Then every
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
-formula is equivalent in
\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})
to a (possibly infinite) disjunction of isolating formulas.

Lemma 5.14
.

Suppose that
\Aut({\mathbf{M}})\curvearrowright M
is transitive,
{\mathbf{M}}
is ultrahomogeneous, and
c\in M
, and define
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
as before. Then the structure
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
is full and, moreover, every formula is equivalent in
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
to an existential one.

Proof.

Let
G=\Aut(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}))
and recall that then
G_{\xi}=\Aut({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}))
.
Let
D\subseteq(X\cup\{\xi\})^{k}
be a
G_{\xi}
-invariant set which we want to prove is definable.
Up to taking a conjunction with a formula stating which of the
k
variables are equal to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, we can assume that tuples in
D
do not contain
\xi
, i.e.,
D\subseteq X^{k}
.
Let us write
D=\bigsqcup_{\alpha}D_{\alpha}
, where each
D_{\alpha}
is a
G_{\xi}
-orbit.

By ultrahomogeneity, for each
\alpha
, the tuples in
D_{\alpha}
are exactly those of a given type, so there is some
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
-formula
\theta_{\alpha}(\bar{v})
which isolates it.
We may assume that
\Phi(\xi,v_{i})
does not occur in
\theta_{\alpha}
, for any

, since no atomic formulas mentioning the unique component around
\xi
hold in
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
.

Let
\phi_{\alpha}(u,\bar{v})
be the
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
-formula obtained by substituting each occurrence of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in
\theta_{\alpha}
by
u
.

Claim
.

For any
\bar{x}\in X^{k}
,
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})\models\theta_{\alpha}(\bar{x})
if and only if for some (equivalently, for all)
z\in\rho(\bigwedge\bar{x})
, it holds that
\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}})\models\phi_{\alpha}(z,\bar{x})
.

Proof of Claim.

Since
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},v_{i})
does not occur in
\theta_{\alpha}
, neither does
\Phi(u,v_{i})
in
\phi_{\alpha}
.
Therefore it is only a matter of noting that for any
z\in\rho(\bigwedge\bar{x})
and
1\leq i,j\leq k
, we have
K(z,x_{i},x_{j})=K(\xi,x_{i},x_{j})
and
\Phi(x_{i},z)=\Phi(x_{i},\xi)
.
∎

Using the qf-fullness of
\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})
, let
\phi(u,\bar{v})
be the quantifier free
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
-formula equivalent to the possibly infinite disjunction
\bigvee_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(u,\bar{v})
in
\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})
.
Then we claim that the formula

(5.1)

\psi(\bar{v})\coloneqq\exists u\in\rho(\bigwedge\bar{v})\ \phi(u,\bar{v})

defines
D
.
If
\bar{x}\in D
, then there is
\alpha
such that
\bar{x}\in D_{\alpha}
, so
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})\models\theta_{\alpha}(\bar{x})
.
The Claim tells us that for any
z\in\rho(\bigwedge\bar{v})
,
\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})\models\phi_{\alpha}(z,\bar{x})
.
As
\phi_{\alpha}
is quantifier-free, this implies that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})\models\phi_{\alpha}(z,\bar{x})
, so
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})\models\psi(\bar{x})
.

Conversely, suppose that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})\models\psi(\bar{x})
.
It follows that there are
z\in\rho(\bigwedge\bar{x})
and
\alpha
such that
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})\models\phi_{\alpha}(z,\bar{x})
.
Since
\phi_{\alpha}
doesn’t mention
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,
\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})\models\phi_{\alpha}(z,\bar{x})
, which, by the Claim, shows that

.
∎

6.
A Ramsey interlude

The goal of this section is to prove a transfer Ramsey theorem that will be our main combinatorial tool for calculating universal minimal flows of kaleidoscopic groups. First we recall the definition of the Ramsey property.

If
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are two structures in the same signature, we denote by
\binom{{\mathbf{B}}}{{\mathbf{A}}}
the set of embeddings of
{\mathbf{A}}
into
{\mathbf{B}}
. For three structures
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
{\mathbf{C}}\to({\mathbf{B}})_{\mathbf{A}}^{k}
the statement that
\binom{{\mathbf{C}}}{{\mathbf{A}}}\neq\emptyset
and for any coloring
\gamma\colon\binom{{\mathbf{C}}}{{\mathbf{A}}}\to k
, there exists
f\in\binom{{\mathbf{C}}}{{\mathbf{B}}}
such that
\gamma
is constant on the set
f\circ\binom{{\mathbf{B}}}{{\mathbf{A}}}
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an age. We will say that
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{F}
is a
Ramsey structure
for
\mathcal{F}
if for every
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists
{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
{\mathbf{C}}\to({\mathbf{B}})_{\mathbf{A}}^{k}
. The age
\mathcal{F}
has the
Ramsey property
if all structures in
\mathcal{F}
are Ramsey for
\mathcal{F}
. We will say that an ultrahomogeneous structure
{\mathbf{N}}
has the Ramsey property if its age does.

The reason the Ramsey property is important in topological dynamics is the following fundamental result of Kechris, Pestov, and Todorčević.

Theorem 6.1

(Kechris–Pestov–Todorčević
[
19
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{N}}
be an ultrahomogeneous structure. Then the following are equivalent:

•

{\mathbf{N}}
has the Ramsey property;

•

\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
is extremely amenable.

There is also a local version of the KPT theorem, which we will use. If
G
is any topological group,
V\leq G
is an open subgroup and
Z
is a compact space, then
G
acts naturally on
Z^{G/V}
by

(g\cdot z)(hV)=z(g^{-1}hV).

We will say that
V\leq G
is a
Ramsey subgroup of
G
if the only minimal subflows of
2^{G/V}
(equivalently, of
Z^{G/V}
for any zero-dimensional compact
Z
) are the fixed points, that is, the constant functions
G/V\to 2
. Note that if
V\leq G
is Ramsey and
\phi\in\Aut(G)
, then
\phi[V]
is also Ramsey.

Let now
{\mathbf{N}}
be ultrahomogeneous and let
G=\Aut({\mathbf{N}})
. If
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finitely generated substructure of
{\mathbf{N}}
, we will denote by
G_{\mathbf{A}}
the pointwise stabilizer of
{\mathbf{A}}
in
G
. This is an open subgroup of
G
and it is well-known and easy to see that
G_{{\mathbf{A}}}
is a Ramsey subgroup of
G
iff
{\mathbf{A}}
is a Ramsey substructure of
{\mathbf{N}}
.
One equivalent way to state Theorem
6.1
is that a non-archimedean
G
is extremely amenable iff every open subgroup of
G
is Ramsey.

It is well-known that extreme amenability is stable under taking group extensions. Below we prove a local version of this fact.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
G
be a topological group,
H\lhd G
be a closed, normal subgroup and let
K=G/H
. Denote by
\pi\colon G\to K
the quotient map. Let
V\leq G
be an open subgroup of
G
. If
V\cap H
is Ramsey in
H
and
\pi(V)
is Ramsey in
K
, then
V
is Ramsey in
G
.

Proof.

Let
z_{0}\in 2^{G/V}
. Our goal is to show that
\overline{G\cdot z_{0}}
contains a fixed point. Let
T\subseteq G
be a set of representatives for the double
(H,V)
-cosets in
G
, i.e., such that
G=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}HtV
. For
t\in G
, we denote by
V^{t^{-1}}
the conjugate
tVt^{-1}
.
There is a natural
H
-equivariant bijection
\theta\colon G/V\to\bigsqcup_{t\in T}H/(V^{t^{-1}}\cap H)
given by

\theta(gV)=h(V^{t^{-1}}\cap H),\quad\text{ where }h\in H,t\in T,gV=htV,

which allows to identify
2^{G/V}
and
\prod_{t\in T}2^{H/(V^{t^{-1}}\cap H)}
, with the diagonal action, as
H
-spaces. Our assumption that
V\cap H
is Ramsey in
H
tells us that the set
Z
of
H
-fixed points in
\overline{G\cdot z_{0}}
is non-empty.
Indeed, the only minimal
H
-subflows of each factor
2^{H/(V^{t^{-1}}\cap H)}
are the fixed points, so we can take an element of the product all of whose coordinates are fixed points.
We can define an injective map
\phi\colon Z\to 2^{K/\pi(V)}
by

\phi(z)(\pi(g)\pi(V))=z(gV).

To conclude, note that any
K
-fixed point in
\phi(Z)
gives us a
G
-fixed point in
Z
.
∎

Note also that
G
is always a Ramsey subgroup of itself.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\{G_{i}:i\in I\}
be a family of topological groups and let
G=\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}

V_{i}\leq G_{i}
be an open Ramsey subgroup for each

Proof.

The case where

from Proposition
6.2
. For an arbitrary

and apply Proposition

1\to\prod_{i\in F}G_{i}\to G\to\prod_{i\in I\setminus F}G_{i}\to 1.\qed

As elements of the age of

Proof of
Theorem

By Proposition

7.
The universal highly proximal extension of the action on
W

As a first application of the tools we have developed, we give a description of the universal highly proximal extension of the flow
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\curvearrowright W
and characterize when it is metrizable.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\Gamma\curvearrowright M
be transitive and let
{\mathbf{M}}
be an ultrahomogeneous, relational structure for
\Gamma
with universe

. Then

(7.1)

\MHP_{\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)}(W)\cong\{p\in\tS_{1}(\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})):\forall x,y\in X\ p(v)\models\neg B(x,v,y)\},

on the right-hand side given by permuting the parameters. The highly proximal factor map

(7.2)

\pi(p)=\zeta\iff\forall x\in X\setminus\{\zeta\}\ p(v)\models\Phi(x,v)=\Phi(x,\zeta).

Proof.

Write
G=\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
and let
\xi\in W
be an endpoint. By Fact
4.3
, the orbit
G\cdot\xi=\mathrm{End}(W)
is comeager in
W
, so from Proposition
2.7
, we have that
\MHP_{G}(W)\cong\Sam(G/G_{\xi})
. Let
\mathbf{c}
be a new constant symbol, fix some
c\in M
and let
{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}
be the expansion of
{\mathbf{M}}
where one interprets
\mathbf{c}
as
c
, as in Subsection
5.4
. Proposition
5.12
gives us a representation of

as the automorphism group
\Aut({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}))
. Denote by
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
the qf-full signature of
\overline{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbf{M}})
and let
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\coloneqq\overline{\mathcal{L}}\sqcup\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\}
. By Lemma
5.14
,
\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
, the expansion of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
to
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, is full.
Now we can apply Proposition
3.5
to obtain that

(7.3)

\MHP_{G}(W)\cong\Exp(\mathcal{K}({\mathbf{M}}),\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})).

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
denote the right-hand sides of (

) and (

), respectively. Next we explain how to identify
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.
We define the map

by

\Theta(p)(v)\models\phi(v,\bar{x})\iff p\models\exists z\in\rho(\bigwedge\bar{x})\ \phi(z,\bar{x})

for every quantifier-free

-formula

.

That

eliminates quantifiers in
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
. It is obvious that

is continuous and
G
-equivariant. To see that it is injective, note that if

and (

because the sentence

\forall x_{1},x_{2}\ \forall z\in\rho(x_{1}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{2})\ \neg B(x_{1},z,x_{2})

holds in

.
Next we see that if

is an isolating, quantifier-free

-formula and

Now, it suffices to choose as

Indeed, for any such

, so we can take

.
This shows

It remains to check the final assertion of the theorem. First, we see that (

∎

.

(ii)

\Gamma

(iii)

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow

(iii)

are on a line. So
\Phi(x\mathbin{\wedge}x^{\prime},x)=\Phi(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1},x_{0})\prec\Phi(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1},x_{1})=\Phi(x\mathbin{\wedge}x^{\prime},x^{\prime})
and thus
x<_{\pi(p)}x^{\prime}
.
Otherwise,
x\mathbin{\wedge}x^{\prime}\in T
, so by definition
\Phi(x\mathbin{\wedge}x^{\prime},x)\prec\Phi(x\mathbin{\wedge}x^{\prime},x^{\prime})
and again
x<_{\pi(p)}x^{\prime}
.

(i)
Now we suppose that
n
is finite and show that
\pi
is injective. Suppose that
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
in order to show that
\pi(p_{1})\neq\pi(p_{2})
. We denote by
\theta\colon\Xi\to\MHP_{G}(W)
the natural factor map defined by the restriction of the type to the language of
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{c}})
and taking
\tp(\boldsymbol{\xi}/X)
(here we use the description of
\MHP_{G}(W)
given by Theorem
7.1
). First suppose that
\theta(p_{1})\neq\theta(p_{2})
. Then by the description of
\MHP_{G}(W)
given in Example
7.3
, there exist
x,y_{1},y_{2}\in X
such that
x\in(y_{1},y_{2})
and

p_{1}\models\Phi(x,y_{1})=\rho(x)\text{ and }p_{2}\models\Phi(x,y_{2})=\rho(x).

(This is where we use that
\hat{x}
is finite. In the infinite-branching case, there exist types
p
such that
p\models\Phi(x,y)\neq\rho(x)
for all
y\in X
; see the proof of
(ii)
below.) But then

p_{1}\models x\mathbin{\wedge}y_{2}=x\ \&\ y_{1}\mathbin{\wedge}x=y_{1}\mathbin{\wedge}y_{2},

which implies that
x<_{\pi(p_{1})}y_{1}
and
x,y_{2}
are on the same side of
y_{1}
in
<_{\pi(p_{1})}
. Similarly, when we replace
p_{1}
by
p_{2}
and exchange
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. These conditions imply that the orders
\pi(p_{1})
and
\pi(p_{2})
cannot agree on
x,y_{1},y_{2}
.

Finally, suppose that
\theta(p_{1})=\theta(p_{2})
. Realize
\theta(p_{1})
in some model
{\mathbf{Y}}^{*}
of
\Th({\mathbf{X}}^{*})
. In that way, we can identify
X
with a subset of this model. We can consider
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
as the types of the same tuple (as usual, indexed by
X
) in two expansions of
{\mathbf{Y}}^{*}
to different models of
\Th({\mathcal{K}}^{*}({\mathbf{N}}))
. In particular, these two models have the same universe and only differ in the interpretation of
\prec
.
Then there must exist tuples
\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z}
from
X
such that, denoting
x=\bigwedge\bar{x},y=\bigwedge\bar{y},z=\bigwedge\bar{z}
, we have that
x\in(y,z)
and

p_{1}\models\Phi(x,y)\prec\Phi(x,z)\text{ and }p_{2}\models\Phi(x,z)\prec\Phi(x,y).

(Note, however, that
x,y,z
need not belong to
X
. They are elements of the model of
\Th({\mathbf{X}}^{*})
, where we realized
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
.)
We consider the position of
y\mathbin{\wedge}z
. If
y\mathbin{\wedge}z\in[y,x)
, then
\rho(x)=\Phi(x,y)
must be the
\prec
-least element of
\hat{x}
in both
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, which is not possible. Similarly, it is not possible that
y\mathbin{\wedge}z\in[z,x)
, so the only possibility is that
y\mathbin{\wedge}z=x
. But then, for each
i,j
, we have that
y_{i}\mathbin{\wedge}z_{j}=x
,
\Phi(x,y_{i})=\Phi(x,y),\Phi(x,z_{j})=\Phi(x,z)
, so by the definition of
\pi
,
y_{i}<_{\pi(p_{1})}z_{j}
and
z_{j}<_{\pi(p_{2})}y_{i}
, implying that
\pi(p_{1})\neq\pi(p_{2})
.

(ii)
We consider the case
n=\infty
and construct two types
p_{1}\neq p_{2}\in\Xi
with
\pi(p_{1})=\pi(p_{2})
. Fix two distinct vertices
x_{0},x_{1}\in X
and let

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\{p\in\Xi:p\models\rho(x_{0})=\Phi(x_{0},x_{1})\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\{p\in\Xi:p\models\rho(x_{0})\neq\Phi(x_{0},x_{1})\}.

Note that
A
and
B
are closed and disjoint. By compactness, it suffices to construct for every finite subtree
T\subseteq X
containing
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, two types
q_{1}\in A
and
q_{2}\in B
such that the orders
\pi(q_{1})
and
\pi(q_{2})
agree on
T
. For
q_{1}
, we choose an endpoint
\xi_{1}
as the root in a way that
x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1}\in(x_{0},x_{1})
and no point in
T
lies between
x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1}
and
x_{0}
.
For
q_{2}
, using that the dendrite is infinitely branching, we choose a root
\xi_{2}
that does not belong to any component in
\hat{x}_{0}\cap\widehat{T}
(so that
q_{2}\models\rho(x_{0})\neq\Phi(x_{0},y)
for all
y\in T\setminus\{x_{0}\}
).
Let
{\mathbf{S}}_{1},{\mathbf{S}}_{2}
be the
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{{\mathbf{X}}}
-structures generated from
T
and the choices of
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, respectively. In particular,
S_{1}=T\cup\{\xi_{1},x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1}\}
,
S_{2}=T\cup\{\xi_{2}\}
.
In particular, each
x\in T\setminus\{x_{0}\}
has the same set of components
\hat{x}
in
{\mathbf{S}}_{1}
and in
{\mathbf{S}}_{2}
.
We can choose the same arbitrary ordering
\prec
on
\hat{x}
(for
x\in T\setminus\{x_{0}\}
) for both
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, subject to the condition that
\rho(x)
is always the least element.

For clarity, denote by
A_{1},A_{2}
the respective sets of components around
x_{0}
in
{\mathbf{S}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{2}
, and by
\rho_{1}(x_{0}),\rho_{2}(x_{0})
the components containing the root.
Notice that
\rho_{1}(x_{0})=\Phi(x_{0},x_{1})
and that
\rho_{2}(x_{0})
does not appear in
A_{1}
.
For
q_{1}
, let
\prec_{1}
be an arbitrary order on
A_{1}
with minimum
\rho_{1}(x_{0})
.
For
q_{2}
, let
\prec_{2}
be the same order except that
\Phi(x_{0},x_{1})=\rho_{1}(x_{0})
is the maximum, and extend it to
A_{2}
as usual, by letting
\rho_{2}(x_{0})
be its minimum.
Finally, for the order around the point
x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1}
in
q_{1}
, we let
q_{1}\models\rho(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1})\prec\Phi(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1},x_{0})\prec\Phi(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1},x_{1})
.

Extend
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
arbitrarily to full types in
\Xi
. We claim that
\pi(q_{1})
and
\pi(q_{2})
agree on
T
.
The only non-trivial relation to check is that the orders between
x_{1}
and any
y
such that
B(y,x_{0},x_{1})
coincide.
We have that
q_{1}\models y\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1}=x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1}
, and that
q_{1}\models\Phi(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1},y)=\Phi(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1},x_{0})\prec\Phi(x_{0}\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1},x_{1})
, so
y<_{\pi(q_{1})}x_{1}
.
On the other hand,
q_{2}\models(y\mathbin{\wedge}x_{1})=x_{0}
.
If
y=x_{0}
, by the definition of
\pi
,
y<_{\pi(q_{2})}x_{1}
;
if
y\neq x_{0}
, then by the definition of
\prec_{2}
,
q_{2}\models\Phi(x_{0},y)\prec\Phi(x_{0},x_{1})
, so again
y<_{\pi(q_{2})}x_{1}
.

The final claim in the statement follows from the fact that every endomorphism of an UMF is an automorphism.
∎
