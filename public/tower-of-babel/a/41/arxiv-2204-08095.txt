Mixed Isogeometric Discretizations for Planar Linear Elasticity
Jeremias Arf and Bernd Simeon

Abstract
In this article we suggest two discretization methods based on isogeometric analysis (IGA) for planar linear elasticity. On the one hand, we apply the well-known ansatz of weakly imposed symmetry for the stress tensor and obtain a well-posed mixed formulation. Such modified mixed problems have been already studied by different authors. But we concentrate on the exploitation of IGA results to handle also curved boundary geometries. On the other hand, we consider the more complicated situation of strong symmetry, i.e. we discretize the mixed weak form determined by the so-called Hellinger-Reissner variational principle. We show the existence of suitable approximate fields leading to an inf-sup stable saddle-point problem. For both discretization approaches we prove convergence statements and in case of weak symmetry we illustrate the approximation behavior by means of several numerical experiments.

Introduction

In this article we identify a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with some elastic body that is loaded according to some body force
\boldsymbol{f}
. The deformation of the body can be expressed through the displacement field
\boldsymbol{u}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
that measures the difference between current configuration
\Omega+\boldsymbol{u}
and initial configuration
\Omega
. Governing equations guaranteeing a physically reasonable description of the deformation process are given by the theory of linear elasticity and read:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\hskip 5.69046pt\textup{and}

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}\hskip 2.84544pt\textup{in}\ \ \Omega,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{T})
denotes the symmetric gradient that corresponds to the mechanical strain within the infinitesimal strain theory. Further,
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\colon\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{S},\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
is the compliance tensor which comprises the material properties and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{S}
is the symmetric stress tensor, where
\mathbb{S}
stands for the space of symmetric matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
. We note that the linear relation between strain and stress as given in equation (
1
) is the key assumption in the linearized framework. A classical conservation of momentum argument relates the stress tensor and the force
\boldsymbol{f}
and as a result one obtains equation (
2
). An important special case are homogeneous and isotropic materials. Then the compliance tensor simplifies to

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\Big(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\frac{\lambda}{2\lambda+2\mu}\textup{tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\boldsymbol{I}\Big),

and the material properties are condensed in form of so-called Lamé parameters
\lambda\geq 0,\ \mu>0
. In all sections below we assume
\boldsymbol{A}
to be symmetric, positive definite and bounded w.r.t. the
L^{2}
-norm. Actually, we have to specify some boundary conditions to complete the model, where we first consider the easy case of pure displacement conditions, i.e. we have
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{D}
on
\Gamma\coloneqq\partial\Omega
for some given
\boldsymbol{u}_{D}
. Supposing the invertibility of
\boldsymbol{A}
one can obtain a formulation which depends only on the displacement variable. A combination of (
1
) and (
2
) yields

\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))=\boldsymbol{f},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{C}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{{A}}^{-1},

(3)

with
\boldsymbol{C}
as the elasticity tensor.
The standard weak form of the mentioned equation in case of homogeneous boundary conditions is the primal formulation:

\textup{Find}\ \ \boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\ \ \textup{s.t.}\ \ \langle\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle,\ \ \forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(4)

with
\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
denoting the Sobolev space with square-integrable weak derivatives, square-

integrable component functions and zero boundary values. Further
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product. Unfortunately, the primal formulation becomes unstable for incompressible problems, i.e. if
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Another point is the non-locality of stress-strain relations in more general material situations. Consequently, the primal weak formulation is not optimal also in view of generalizability. This is why we find in literature mixed formulations that seek for stress and displacement simultaneously. A standard mixed formulation reads:

Find
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{{H}}}(\Omega,\textup{div},\mathbb{S}),

\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
s.t.

\displaystyle\langle\textbf{A}\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}\rangle\ +\

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{u},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}\rangle\ \color[rgb]{0,0,0}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\textbf{n};\boldsymbol{u}_{D}\rangle_{\Gamma},\ \hskip 11.38092pt

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{{H}}}(\Omega,\textup{div},\mathbb{S}),

(5)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle,\ \hskip 11.38092pt

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Above
\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\textbf{n};\boldsymbol{u}_{D}\rangle_{\Gamma}
denotes the duality pairing determined by the normal trace which corresponds to the
L^{2}(\Gamma)
inner product on the boundary if
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\tau}}
is regular enough. Furthermore, we wrote
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{{H}}}(\Omega,\textup{div},\mathbb{S})
for the space of square-integrable symmetric matrix fields which have a square-integrable divergence and
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
denotes the space of square-integrable vector fields.
As explained in
[
6
]
, background of this saddle-point problem is the
Hellinger-Reissner
functional where
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is given as the corresponding unique critical point. Regarding the discretization of the mixed weak system there are two main ways which are frequently used. On the one hand, one can try to define discrete spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{h}\subset\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div},\Omega,\mathbb{S}),\ \boldsymbol{V}_{h}\subset\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)
to approximate the stress tensor and the displacement. However, the choice of suitable subspaces is not trivial. To obtain a well-posed numerical method the discretized saddle-point problem has to satisfy
Brezzi
’s stability conditions (see
[
9
]
). It becomes apparent that the symmetry condition and divergence compatibility for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{h}
is hard to achieve simultaneously, especially in case of more complex computational domains
\Omega
. Methods that follow the strong symmetry ansatz can be found e.g. in
[
4
]
and
[
7
]
. However, the mentioned discretizations are quite complicated. For example in
[
7
]
one needs at least
24
degrees of freedom per triangle element.
Therefore, on the other hand authors in several articles (e.g.
[
6
,
23
,
5
,
15
]
) study a modified mixed problem which guarantees symmetry only in a variational sense. Such formulations with weakly imposed symmetry are related to the de Rham complex and the elegant theory of Finite Element Exterior Calculus, see
[
6
]
and
[
15
]
, but require an additional Lagrange multiplier.

In this article we shall consider both ways of discretization, namely strong and weak symmetry conservation. Our aim is to show that there are discrete spaces satisfying the Brezzi conditions and thus lead to well-posedness. In contrast to classical Finite Elements (FEM) based on simplicial decompositions or quadrilateral elements we use B-splines and results from Isogeometric Analysis (IGA) to define test and trial fields. Advantages of IGA are the possibility to consider geometries with curved boundaries and the fact that the global regularity of the B-splines can be increased efficiently. Such special features are useful if the method should be applicable in more complex scenarios. Although our ansatz for strongly imposed symmetry is only valid in the so-called single-patch case, it is capable to handle domains with curved boundaries. Nevertheless, for the weak symmetry approach the generalization to multi-patch situations is possible.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we introduce briefly some mathematical notation and basic notions in the context of IGA. Next, in Section
3
we explain the mixed formulation of linear elasticity with weakly imposed symmetry and consider spline spaces that are suitable for discretization in planar domains. In particular, we establish a corresponding error estimate. Afterwards, in Section
4
we face the
2D
case with strong symmetry, where we define likewise proper discrete spaces. Section
5
is dedicated to numerical examples to validate the theoretical statements, where we restrict ourselves to the weak symmetry case. A detailed numerical study of the strong symmetry ansatz should be the issue of a further article. We close with a short conclusion in Section
6
.

•

If for all internal knots the multiplicity satisfies

The B-splines

For continuous univariate splines

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\Pi_{h,2}^{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi_{h,3}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}-\Pi_{h}^{TH}\boldsymbol{p}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{2}}\Big)

The second estimate of the assertion can be obtained by a standard approach. Due to the second lines of (

\hskip 28.45274pt\left\lVert\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\leq\left\lVert\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\Pi_{h,3}^{2}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\leq Ch^{k}\left\lVert\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{H}^{k}}.

3.3
Remarks concerning generalizations

3.3.1
Higher regularity splines

A major advantage of the B-spline spaces is the possibility to increase the global smoothness without worsening the approximation capabilities, where the polynomial degrees bound the regularity parameter. To be more precise, one can choose for example
r\leq p-1
in the definition of the isogeometric de Rham spaces. Therefore, a generalization of the discretization method for
r>0
seems natural. In fact, for most of the steps above we do not need to set
r=0
and a lot of properties like the approximation estimates in Lemma
2
and (
15
) are still valid if the regularity parameter is increased considering the underlying polynomial degrees. Only the inf-sup stability result from Lemma
5
uses
r=0
. But in the IGA community there is numerical evidence indicating the uniform inf-sup stability of the Taylor-Hood pair also for
r>0
; see
[
10
]
. Hence, if we make the reasonable assumption of inf-sup stability of the Taylor-Hood space pair for increased regularity parameter we obtain error estimates analogous to the ones in Theorem
3
also for
1\leq r+1<p
. The numerical experiments we carried out in Section
5
utilizing splines with higher regularity support the assumption of stability.

3.3.3

\displaystyle=\hat{\partial}_{1}\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p+1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p-1}^{c,2}\big)\hat{\tau}_{11}+\hat{\partial}_{2}\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\big)\hat{\tau}_{12}

\displaystyle=\big(\hat{\partial}_{1}\widehat{\Pi}_{p+1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p-1}^{c,2}\big)\hat{\tau}_{11}+\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\otimes\hat{\partial}_{2}\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\big)\hat{\tau}_{12}

\displaystyle=\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\hat{\partial}_{1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p-1}^{c,2}\big)\hat{\tau}_{11}+\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p-1}^{c,2}\hat{\partial}_{2}\big)\hat{\tau}_{12}

\displaystyle=\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p-1}^{c,2}\big)\hat{\partial}_{1}\hat{\tau}_{11}+\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p-1}^{c,2}\big)\hat{\partial}_{2}\hat{\tau}_{12}=\big(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{c,1}\otimes\widehat{\Pi}_{p-1}^{c,2}\big)(\widehat{\nabla}\cdot[\hat{\tau}_{11},\hat{\tau}_{12}]^{T}).

A similar calculation can be applied for the second entry of
(\widehat{\nabla}\cdot)\circ\widehat{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
. Consequently, the commutativity is clear.

The estimates for the projection are based on the boundedness and spline-preserving properties of the univariate projections in Lemma
22
. The proof is analogous to the proof of Lemma 5.1 in
[
11
]
and we state briefly the underlying idea. Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}(\widehat{\Omega},\widehat{\textup{div}},\mathbb{S})\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}(\widehat{\Omega}),\ p\geq k\geq 1,\ p>r\geq 1
. One uses Lemma 3.1 of
[
8
]
which gives us the existence of a spline mapping
\boldsymbol{s}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{h,2}^{s}
such that

\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}\leq Ch^{k-1}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}},\ \ \ \left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{2}}\leq Ch^{k}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}},

for a suitable constant
C
. Since the univariate projections are spline preserving and the tensor-product structure of the multivariate projections fits to the product structure of the spline spaces we get directly the spline preserving property of
\widehat{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}
and
\widehat{\Pi}_{h,3}^{s}
. Thus, an application of the triangle inequality and a standard inverse estimate for polynomials yields

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\widehat{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}+\left\lVert\widehat{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}\big(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\big)\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}}+\frac{C}{h}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}}.

Above we used the boundedness of the projection w.r.t. to the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{2}
-norm following from the boundedness properties in Lemma
22
.
∎

Lemma 24
.

If
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}({\Omega},{\textup{div}},\mathbb{S})\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{k}(\Omega),\ k\geq 0
, then it holds

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{Y}_{3}^{s}(\boldsymbol{{v}})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{H}^{k}},

assuming a regular enough
\boldsymbol{F}
; e.g.
\boldsymbol{F}\in C^{k+2}(\overline{\widehat{\Omega}})
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}\in C^{k+2}({\overline{\Omega}})
. The constant
C
depends on the parametrization.

Proof.

Again we concentrate on the first estimate, since the second one is proven with a similar approach. Further, we first assume
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in C^{k+1}(\overline{\Omega})\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{S})
and start with the case
k=1
. Note that the assertion is clear for
k=0
; see proof of Lemma
19
. With the definition (
39
) of
\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s}
, problems may arise due to the term
\mathcal{Y}_{2,A}^{s}
because of the evaluation on the boundary part
\hat{\Gamma}_{1}
. Applying derivatives
\hat{\partial}_{1},\hat{\partial}_{2}
to
\mathcal{Y}_{2,A}^{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
we obtain expressions involving values and first derivatives
\partial_{m}\boldsymbol{\tau}\circ\boldsymbol{F}(0,\cdot)
on the boundary edge
\hat{\Gamma}_{1}
which are multiplied by geometry dependent terms. By the trace theorem (see
[
26
, Chapter 2]
) we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\tau_{ij}}\circ\boldsymbol{F}\right\rVert_{{L}^{2}(\hat{\Gamma}_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert{\tau_{ij}}\circ\boldsymbol{F}\right\rVert_{{H}^{1}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert{\tau_{ij}}\right\rVert_{{H}^{1}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{m}{\tau_{ij}}\circ\boldsymbol{F}\right\rVert_{{L}^{2}(\hat{\Gamma}_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\partial_{m}{\tau_{ij}}\circ\boldsymbol{F}\right\rVert_{{H}^{1}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert{\tau_{ij}}\right\rVert_{{H}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Consequently, if the geometry function
\boldsymbol{F}
is regular enough, one can see that

\left\lVert\mathcal{Y}_{2,A}^{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{2}({\Omega})}

and clearly
\left\lVert\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\widehat{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{2}({\Omega})}
.
Since the smooth mappings are dense in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{2}(\Omega)
the inequality is still valid for a general element in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{2}(\Omega)
.
A similar reasoning can be used for the cases
k\geq 2
.

∎

Lemma 25

(Projections on the physical domain)
.

Utilizing the mappings
\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s},\ \mathcal{Y}_{3}^{s}
we define the projections onto the actual test function spaces

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}

\displaystyle\colon\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\Omega,\textup{div},\mathbb{S})\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}_{h,2}^{s}\ ,\ \boldsymbol{\tau}\mapsto\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}^{s}\circ\widehat{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{h,3}^{s}

\displaystyle\colon\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{V}_{h,3}^{s}\ ,\ \boldsymbol{v}\mapsto(\mathcal{Y}_{3}^{s})^{-1}\circ\widehat{\Pi}_{h,3}^{s}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{3}^{s}(\boldsymbol{v}).

Let
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}({\Omega},\textup{div},\mathbb{S})\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}(\Omega),\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{k}(\Omega)
, where
1\leq k\leq p,\ 1\leq r<p
. Then the next inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{{v}}-{\Pi}_{h,3}^{s}{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{H}^{k}}.

Proof.

We show the inequality for
\boldsymbol{\tau}
. Set
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\coloneqq\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. Combining Lemma
18
, Lemma
19
and Lemma
24
one can write

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}^{s}\circ\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s}({\boldsymbol{\tau}})-\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}^{s}\circ\widehat{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\widehat{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}\left\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}}.

The second estimate follows by similar steps.
∎

Theorem 6

(Convergence for the strong symmetry case)
.

It holds the quasi-optimal error estimate

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\leq C\underset{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}_{h,2}^{s},\ \boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in{\boldsymbol{V}}_{h,3}^{s}}{\inf}\hskip 2.84544pt\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}

between the exact
(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u})
and discrete
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
solution of the mixed formulation with strong symmetry and boundary condition
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{D}
on
\partial\Omega
. In case of sufficiently smooth exact solution, meaning
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}({\Omega},{\textup{div}},\mathbb{S})\cap\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}(\Omega),\ \boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{k}({\Omega}),\ 1\leq k\leq p,\ 1\leq r<p
, we can specify the convergence behavior via the estimate

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L^{2}}}\leq Ch^{k-1}\big(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{H}^{k}}\big).

(57)

Again
C<\infty
is independent of the mesh size
h
.

Proof.

In view of classical results for mixed problems (see e.g.
[
9
]
) the quasi-optimal error estimate is a consequence of the inf-sup stability of the discrete method, see Theorem
5
, and the well-posedness of the continuous problem; see Theorem
4
. Therefore it is enough to show the convergence rates w.r.t.
h
. But this is clear since we can apply the result of the previous Lemma
25
to the quasi-optimal error estimate, namely

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}-{\Pi}_{h,2}^{s}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-{\Pi}_{h,3}^{s}\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k-1}\big(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k+1}}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{H}^{k}}\big).

∎

Remark 5
.

The error estimate (
57
) of the last theorem shows an order reduction and further we have to require a relatively smooth exact solution. Hence, the approximation estimate is certainly not entirely satisfactory. We hope that this first estimate is too pessimistic and it is advisable to study numerical examples in order to check the actual approximation performance. Problems within our estimate derivation are caused by the boundedness result for the mapping
\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{s}
(see Lemma
19
) where the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{1}
-norm appears instead of the wanted
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})
-norm. At least in the case
\hat{\partial}_{1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}=\boldsymbol{0}
one can prove boundedness w.r.t. the
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})
-norm. The latter implies an improved error estimate of the form

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{L^{2}}}\leq Ch^{k}\big(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{k}(\textup{div})}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{\boldsymbol{H}^{k}}\big).

(58)

We obtain such an improved estimate also in case of the mixed boundary conditions from Assumption
5
. In other words, one can verify that in situations where the additional term
\mathcal{Y}^{s}_{2,A}
in (
39
) vanishes, estimates of the form (
58
) are valid.

5.4
Local stress oscillations

One point we observed in numerical experiments and we want to mention here is the appearance of local overshoots and oscillations of the stress components in case of mixed boundary conditions. For example, if we come back to the multi-patch disk case from above (Section

In the general, this behavior can be observed in case of mixed-boundary conditions independent of the parametrization regularity, single- or multi-patch situation and independent of the global spline regularity. To demonstrate this, we look at the simplest single-patch square geometry, meaning
\boldsymbol{F}=\textup{id}

\boldsymbol{t}_{n}=(-1,0)^{T}

p=3

(0,1)
and
(1,1)

\Gamma_{t}

One reason for the stress issue might be changed role of boundary conditions for the mixed weak form compared to the classical primal weak elasticity formulation. In our experiments the traction BCs are explicitly enforced by prescribing the respective stress components, but the displacement boundary conditions are only implemented in a weak sense. Thus, at points where Dirchlet and traction sides meet we specify the traction force while at the same time the displacement is enforced weakly on
\Gamma_{D}
according to
\boldsymbol{u}_{D}
. As a result it seems that a non-smooth behavior of the solution is triggered near such points. Nevertheless, even if we obtain such local stress wiggles within numerical computations, we still get adequate solutions for the displacement; compare Fig.
14
(c) -(d).
However, we do not have a strict proof or explanation to clarify this issue with the stresses at hand. Hence, a more detailed study of the stress solutions in the situation of mixed boundary conditions is advisable.

6
Conclusion

In this article we defined discrete spaces utilizing B-splines for the approximation of the mixed weak form of planar linear elasticity with weakly imposed symmetry as well as for the mixed formulation implied by the Hellinger-Reissner functional. The latter requires symmetric stress fields and the derivation of suitable finite-dimensional test spaces needed more effort. For both approaches our choice of discretization spaces satisfies the Brezzi inf-sup condition and we obtain well-posed saddle-point problems together with a quasi-optimality estimate. Moreover, we showed the convergence of the methods at least if the underlying geometry and the exact solution are regular enough. And if we use B-spline parametrizations
\boldsymbol{F}\in(S_{p,p}^{r,r})^{2}
, then the corresponding inf-sup constant for the weak symmetry case can be chosen independent of
\lambda
which indicates stability for the quasi-incompressible regime. Several numerical examples within the scope of weakly imposed symmetry confirmed the theoretical error estimate. However, for the ansatz with strong symmetry a numerical study is missing and might be a starting point of a further paper. Unfortunately, the space definitions in Section
4
are quite involved and thus symmetry preserving computations might be expensive. Another point that deserves a clarification is the issue of local stress oscillations in case of mixed boundary conditions; compare Section
5.4
. The main advantage of the proposed methods is the exploitation of results from IGA. This is why we can consider computational domains with curved boundaries which is not directly possible if classical FEM is used.
