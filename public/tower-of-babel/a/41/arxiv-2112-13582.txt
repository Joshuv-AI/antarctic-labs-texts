On k k -Shifted Antimagic Spider Forests
Fei-Huang Chang, Wei-Tian Li, Daphne Der-Fen Liu, Zhishi Pan

Abstract
Let G ⁡ ( V , E ) G(V,E) be a simple graph with m m edges. For a given integer k k , a k k -shifted antimagic labeling is a bijection f : E ⁡ ( G ) → { k + 1 , k + 2 , … , k + m } f:E(G)\to\{k+1,k+2,\ldots,k+m\} such that all vertices have different vertex-sums, where the vertex-sum of a vertex v v is the total of the labels assigned to the edges incident to v v . A graph G G is k k -shifted antimagic if it admits a k k -shifted antimagic labeling. For the special case when k = 0 k=0 , a 0 0 -shifted antimagic labeling is known as antimagic labeling ; and G G is antimagic if it admits an antimagic labeling. A spider is a tree with exactly one vertex of degree greater than two. A spider forest is a graph where each component is a spider. In this article, we prove that certain spider forests are k k -shifted antimagic for all k ⩾ 0 k\geqslant 0 . In addition, we show that for a spider forest G G with m m edges, there exists a positive integer k 0 < m k_{0}<m such that G G is k k -shifted antimagic for all k ⩾ k 0 k\geqslant k_{0} and k ⩽ − ( m + k 0 + 1 ) k\leqslant-(m+k_{0}+1) .

Introduction

All graphs considered in this paper are finite, simple, and undirected. For any two integers
a\leqslant b
, denote
[a,b]=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:a\leqslant x\leqslant b\}
. In 1990, Hartsfield and Ringel
[
11
]
introduced the following labeling problem on graphs:

Definition 1
.

Let
G(V,E)
be a graph with
m
edges. For a bijection
f:E\to[1,m]
, define the
vertex-sum
of
v\in V(G)

, as the total of the labels of edges incident to
v
. That is,
\phi_{f}(v)=\sum_{e\cap v\neq\emptyset}f(e)
. The
antimagic condition
is defined as

\phi_{f}(v)\neq\phi_{f}(u),\ \ \ \mbox{if}\ u\neq v.

If the antimagic condition is satisfied, then
f
is called an
antimagic labeling
of
G
. Further,
G
is

if
G
admits an antimagic labeling. When the bijective mapping
f
is clear in the context, we simply denote
\phi_{f}(v)
by
\phi(v)
.

Clearly, the 2-path
P_{2}
(one-edge path) is not antimagic. For connected graphs with at least three vertices, Hartsfield and Ringel
[
11
]
proved that some families of graphs, such as paths, stars, cycles, and complete graphs are antimagic, and proposed the following two conjectures:

Conjecture 1
.

(
[
11
]
)

Every connected graph on at least three vertices is antimagic.

Conjecture 2
.

(
[
11
]
)

Every tree on at least three vertices is antimagic.

1

Many classes of graphs have been proved to be antimagic
[
1
,
4
,
6
]
. For a comprehensive list of known results, we refer the readers to the survey by Gallian
[
10
]
.

For trees, Kaplan, Lev, and Roditty
[
13

proved that every tree with at most one vertex of degree two is
antimagic
1
1

1

Their original proof contains a minor error within a special case which was corrected by Liang, Wong, and Zhu
[
14
]
.

In
[
14
]
, Liang, Wong, and Zhu
[
14
]
extended the results to trees with more vertices of degree 2. For instance it was proved that for a tree

induces a path, or both

A

is a tree with exactly one vertex of degree greater than two. It was proved by Shang
[

]
and Huang
[

]
independently that every spider is antimagic. A

[

proved that every double spider is antimagic. A

[

and Lozano, Mora, and Seara
[

Recently, Lozano, Mora, Seara, and Tey completely settled the problem that all caterpillars are antimagic
[
16
]
.

Although
1
is widely open, there exist disconnected graphs that are not anitmagic. It is easy to see that the disjoint union of two 3-paths
P_{3}
is not antimagic. A
forest
is a graph where each component is a tree. When every component is a path, then it is called a

.
In
[
19

, Shang proved that every linear forest without

or

as components is antimagic. A

is a graph where each component is a star. Chen, Huang, Lin, Shang, and Lee
[

gave conditions of the components of a star forest to guarantee its antimagicness.
Moreover, Dhananjaya

Definition 2

Let
G(V,E)
be a graph with
m

The aim of this article is to investigate

In this article, we prove the following three results:

Theorem 1

3
Discussion and Open Problems

A basic idea behind our labeling schemes is to classify the labels as small, middle, and large. Each pendant edge is labeled with a small label, while the edges incident to a degree-2 vertex receive a small and a middle labels, or a middle and a large labels, and the edges incident to the center of a spider are labeled with middle or large labels.

When applying the above idea to label the stars in a spider forest difficulties arose as every leg of a star is both a pendant edge and an edge incident to the center. Our original intent might lead to a small vertex-sum of the center of a star, increasing the possibility for it to be the same as the vertex-sum of a degree-2 vertex. One way to resolve this problem is to label the stars with the middle or large labels, giving larger vertex-sums of the centers of the stars. However, this might create the problem that the vertex-sums of the leaves of the stars might coincide with the vertex-sums of some degree-2 vertices of other non-star spiders.

To resolve both issues, we use larger labels so that the vertex-sum of any center (with degree at least 3) will always be large enough. For example, if we use labels in
[2m+1,3m]
, then the vertex-sum of a center is always greater than that of a degree-2 or degree-1 vertex. This motivates us to shift the labels from
[1,m]
to
[k+1,k+m]
, and then minimize the values of
k
.
Theorem

2
for general spider forests came out from this approach.

In addition to the conditions in
Theorem

1
and
Theorem

3
, we could
impose other conditions on the numbers of odd legs and even legs of the spiders, and show those spider forests are antimagic. However, the conditions become relatively complicated and tedious, and the arguments are analogously lengthy.
Nevertheless,
We believe that every spider forest is antimagic and propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 3
.

Every spider forest is
k
-shifted antimagic for all
k\geqslant 0
.

Recall that a star forest is also a spider forest. Recently, Dhananjaya and Li
[
9
]
proved that if a star forest is not one of the following cases: a star, or the union of two
S_{3}
’s, or the union of three
S_{3}
’s, or the union of two
S_{4}
’s, and every component is not an
S_{2}
, then it is
k
-shifted antimagic for every integer
k
.
For spiders other than stars, an example in
[
2
]
shows that a spider with one length-1 leg and two length-2 legs is
k
-shifted antimagic if and only if
k\neq-3
.
A more challenging problem than Conjecture
3

might be to determine all the integers
k
for which a given spider forest is
k
-shifted antimagic.
