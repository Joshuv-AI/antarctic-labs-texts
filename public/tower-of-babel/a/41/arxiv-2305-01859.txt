Regularities and multiplicities of Veronese type algebras Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification . Primary 05E40 13F55 13F65 Secondary 14M25 13H15 13D02 Thanks: Keyword: Algebra of Veronese type, Regularity, Cohen–Macaulay, Multiplicity
Kuei-Nuan Lin and Yi-Huang Shen

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the algebra of Veronese type. We show that the presentation ideal of this algebra has an initial ideal whose Alexander dual has linear quotients. As an application, we explicitly obtain the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of the Veronese type algebra. Furthermore, we give an effective upper bound on the multiplicity of this algebra.



1.
Introduction

Let
S={\mathbb{K}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over a field
{\mathbb{K}}
with
n\geq 2
. Suppose that
I
is an ideal minimally generated by some monomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{u}
in
S
. The
semigroup ring
associated to
I
, denoted by
{\mathbb{K}}[I]
,
is the subalgebra of
S
generated by
f_{1},\dots,f_{u}
. This ring is
also known as the
toric ring
associated with
I
.
In this work, we focus on investigating several algebraic invariants of
{\mathbb{K}}[I]
associated with an ideal of Veronese type.

We fix a degree
d
and a sequence
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
of integers with
1\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{n}\leq d
and
d<\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
. Let
{\mathbb{N}}=\Set{0,1,2,\dots}
be the set of non-negative integers and

I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}\coloneqq\Braket{{\bm{x}}^{\bm{c}}:{\bm{c}}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n},\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=d\text{ and }c_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}\text{ for each $i$ }}

be
an ideal of Veronese type
. Related, let
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}={\mathbb{K}}[I]\subset S
and call it an
algebra of Veronese type
. If
\alpha_{1}=\cdots=\alpha_{n}
, then
I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is a special strongly symmetric shifted ideal. Moreover, if
\alpha_{i}=d
for all
i
, then
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is the
d
-th Veronese subring of
S
. Algebras of Veronese type have been studied from the viewpoint of toric varieties, see
[
23
]
. De Negri and Hibi also classified the Gorensteinness of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
in
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
. In addition, Costantini and Seceleanu studied algebras associated with strongly symmetric shifted ideals in
[
5
]
. In this paper, we continue their work and compute the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity and the multiplicity of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
.

Recall that for a finitely generated
graded module
M
over the polynomial ring

S
,
the
Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity
of
M
, denoted by
\operatorname{reg}(M)
, is
\max\Set{j-i:\beta_{i,j}\neq 0}
, where the
\beta_{i,j}
’s are the graded Betti numbers of
M
. This regularity can be used to bound the degree of the syzygy generators of the module
M
. Another important invariant that we study here is the
multiplicity

\mathtt{e}(M)
of
M=\bigoplus_{k}M_{k}
with respect to the graded maximal ideal,
where
M_{k}
is the degree
k
component of the graded module
M
.
Recall that the Hilbert function
H(M,k):=\operatorname{dim}_{{\mathbb{K}}}(M_{k})
is eventually a polynomial in
k
of degree
\operatorname{dim}(M)-1
. The leading coefficient of this polynomial is of form
\mathtt{e}(M)/(\operatorname{dim}(M)-1)!
.
If
M
is the homogeneous coordinate ring of a projective variety
X
, then the multiplicity is just the
degree
of
X
.

One way to study the semigroup ring
{\mathbb{K}}[I]
algebraically is to investigate its presentation ideal.
Let
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]={\mathbb{K}}[T_{f_{1}},\ldots,T_{f_{u}}]
be a polynomial ring in
u
variables over
{\mathbb{K}}
.
Then the
presentation ideal

J
is the kernel of the canonical
{\mathbb{K}}
-algebra homomorphism
\psi:{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]\rightarrow{\mathbb{K}}[I]
with
\psi(T_{f_{i}})=f_{i}
. Obviously,
J
is a prime ideal and we have
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]/J\cong{\mathbb{K}}[I]
.

The study of algebra
{\mathbb{K}}[I]
becomes more feasible if the Gröbner basis of the presentation ideal
J
exists; see
[
2
]
for the case of chordal bipartite graphs,
[
21
]
for the
d
-th Veronese subring, and
[
19
]
for the case of three-dimensional Ferrers diagrams.
This is because the invariants of the initial ideal and the original ideal are closely related, c.f.
[
15
, Section 3.3]
. According to the work of Conca and Varbaro in
[
3
]
, these relations become tight when the initial ideal is squarefree.
On the other hand, without knowing the Gröbner basis of
J
, finding the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity or the multiplicity of
{\mathbb{K}}[I]
, for example, becomes a difficult task; cf.
[
16
]
.

It is worth noting that if
J
has a quadratic Gröbner basis, then the algebra
{\mathbb{K}}[I]
is Koszul (that is,
{\mathbb{K}}
has a linear resolution over
{\mathbb{K}}[I]
) by
[
10
]
.
The authors of the current paper proved in
[
18
]
that if
I
is associated with a three-dimensional Ferrers diagram satisfying a mild property, then the presentation ideal
J
has a quadratic Gröbner basis. This is the foundation for the calculation of the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity and the multiplicity in
[
19
]
. However, the way we obtained the quadratic Gröbner basis seems to be model-dependent.
A more useful tool is the idea of
sorting monomials
introduced by Sturmfels. With this technique,
Sturmfels
[
23
]
proved that the presentation ideal of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
has a quadratic Gröbner basis.
More recently, Herzog et al.
[
14
]
generalized the notion of sortability to the non-equigenerated case and proved that the presentation ideal of the algebra associated with a sortable monomial ideal also has a quadratic Gröbner basis.

With the quadratic Gröbner basis of
J
described earlier by Sturmfels, we can study the Veronese type algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
in more detail. As a main result, we obtain the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
explicitly in
5.6
. This generalizes the result
[
21
, Theorem 4.2]
of Nitsche in the case of the
d
-th Veronese subring. We then offer an effective bound on the multiplicity of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
in
5.14
.

This work is organized as follows. In Section
2
,
we recall some essential definitions and terminology that we will need later.
In Section
3
, we use the combinatorial structure of the
sorting operation to study the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
. From the initial ideal of the presentation ideal
J
of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
, we introduce a structural graph
\mathcal{G}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
and study its maximal cliques. As a result, we derive the generators of the Alexander dual of
\operatorname{in}(J)
in
3.10
. In Section
4
, we propose a carefully constructed order in
4.13
. With it, we show in
4.21
that this Alexander dual ideal has linear quotients. Combinatorial details of the quotients are also presented in this section. Finally, in Section
5
, we gather all the tools and results and present the two main theorems mentioned above.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we fix a positive integer
n\geq 2
. Following the convention,
[n]
stands for the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Let
S={\mathbb{K}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over a field
{\mathbb{K}}
, and
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the symmetric group of
[n]
.

Notation 2.1
.

(1)

Let
{\bm{a}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
be a tuple written in boldface. Unless otherwise stated, we usually write
a_{i}
correspondingly with subscript, not in boldface, for its
i
-th coordinate. Namely, we expect that
{\bm{a}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. Following this convention, if we encounter several tuples
{\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{t}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, we will have
{\bm{a}}^{i}=(a_{1}^{i},\dots,a_{n}^{i})
for each
i\in[t]
.

(2)

Suppose that
{\bm{a}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
. We will write
{\bm{x}}^{\bm{a}}
for the monomial
x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
in
S

and define
|{\bm{a}}|\coloneqq a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}
.

(3)

Let
I
be a monomial ideal of
S
. We write
G(I)
for the minimal monomial generating set
of
I
, and
\mu(I)=\#G(I)
for the minimal number of generators.

Recall that Sturmfels introduced in
[
23
, Chapter 14]
a sorting operator for monomials of the same degree.

Definition 2.2
.

(1)

Let
\operatorname{Mon}_{d}
be the set of monomials of degree
d
in
S={\mathbb{K}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. Then, we have the
sorting operator
for
p\geq 2
:

\operatorname{sort}:\underbrace{\operatorname{Mon}_{d}\times\cdots\times\operatorname{Mon}_{d}}_{\text{$p$ times}}\to\underbrace{\operatorname{Mon}_{d}\times\cdots\times\operatorname{Mon}_{d}}_{\text{$p$ times}},\qquad(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})\mapsto(v_{1},\dots,v_{p}),

which is defined as follows. Suppose that
u_{1}\cdots u_{p}=x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{pd}}
with
i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{pd}
. Then,
v_{k}\coloneqq x_{i_{k}}x_{i_{p+k}}\cdots x_{i_{(d-1)p+k}}
for
k\in[p]
. The sequence
(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})
of monomials in
\operatorname{Mon}_{d}
is called
sorted
if
\operatorname{sort}(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})=(u_{1},\dots,u_{p})
. A subset
U
of
V_{n,d}
is
sortable
if
\operatorname{sort}(U\times U)\subseteq U\times U
.

(2)

Let
V_{n,d}\coloneqq\{{\bm{a}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}:|{\bm{a}}|=d\}
. Since we have a natural correspondence between
{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{a}}}\in\operatorname{Mon}_{d}
and
{\bm{a}}\in V_{n,d}
, by abuse of notation, we also derive a sorting operator

\operatorname{sort}:V_{n,d}\times\cdots\times V_{n,d}\to V_{n,d}\times\cdots\times V_{n,d}.

Similarly, we can also talk about whether a subset of
V_{n,d}
is sortable.

In this paper, we always assume the following.

Setting 2.3
.

Suppose that
n\geq 3
.
We fix a degree
d
and a sequence
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
of integers with
1\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{n}\leq d
and
d<|{\bm{\alpha}}|
. Let
V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\coloneqq\Set{{\bm{c}}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in V_{n,d}:c_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}\text{ for all $i$}}
. Furthermore, let
I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}\coloneqq\braket{{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{c}}}:{\bm{c}}\in V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}}
be the Veronese type ideal in
S
, and
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}=\mathbb{K}[I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}]
be the Veronese type algebra.

Remark 2.4
.

(1)

When
n=2
, it is not difficult to see that the Veronese type algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is isomorphic to a Veronese ring, up to a shift.

(2)

If
d=|{\bm{\alpha}}|
, then the Veronese type algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is generated principally by the monomial
{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
be the Veronese type algebra in

2.3
.
Since
V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is sortable by

[
9
, Proposition 6.11]
, the set

\mathscr{J}\coloneqq\Set{\underline{T_{{\bm{a}}^{1}}T_{{\bm{a}}^{2}}}-T_{{\bm{b}}^{1}}T_{{\bm{b}}^{2}}:{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2}\in V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}},\text{ $({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2})$ unsorted and
$\operatorname{sort}({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2})=({\bm{b}}^{1},{\bm{b}}^{2})$}}

is a Gröbner basis of the presentation ideal
J
of

\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
with respect to a term order such that the marked
(underlined) monomials are the leading terms by

[
9
, Theorem 6.16]
. In particular,
J
has a quadratic
squarefree initial ideal. Consequently,
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is a Cohen–Macaulay
Koszul normal domain, by
[
9
, Theorems 5.16, 5.17, and 6.7]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
be the Veronese type algebra in
2.3
. Then,
\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})=n
.

Proof.

By assumption, we have
d<|{\bm{\alpha}}|
. Since
I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is equigenerated, the dimension of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
equals the rank of the matrix
{\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
whose rows are the exponent vectors of the monomial generators of
I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
(see
[
17
, Exercise 8.21]
). Based on this, we consider two cases.

(1)

Suppose that
d<n
. Then, let
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}=(1,1,\dots,1)
. Since the corresponding matrix
{\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}
is a submatrix of
{\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
, it remains to show that
\operatorname{rank}({\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}})=n
, or equivalently,
\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}})=n
. Notice that
I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}
is a strongly symmetric shifted ideal. That
\operatorname{dim}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}})=n
follows from
[
5
, Proposition 3.29]
.

(2)

Suppose that
d\geq n
. Then, we can find suitable
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
such that
1\leq\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\leq\alpha_{i}
for all
i
and
d=|{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}|-1
. Since the corresponding matrix
{\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}
is a submatrix of
{\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
, it remains to show that
\operatorname{rank}({\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}})=n
. Let
{\bm{e}}^{i}
be the
i
-th standard basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. It is not difficult to see that
{\bm{M}}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}
is an
n\times n
matrix, whose rows take the form
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}-{\bm{e}}^{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. It is then an elementary task to show that
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}-{\bm{e}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}-{\bm{e}}^{n}
are linearly independent. Say,
\sum_{i}k_{i}({\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}-{\bm{e}}^{i})={\bm{0}}
for suitable
k_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus,
\sum_{i}k_{i}{\bm{e}}^{i}=(\sum_{i}k_{i}){\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
and consequently
\sum_{i}k_{i}=(\sum_{i}k_{i})|{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}|
. If
\sum_{i}k_{i}=0
, then
\sum_{i}k_{i}{\bm{e}}^{i}=(\sum_{i}k_{i}){\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}={\bm{0}}
. As a result, each
k_{i}=0
. If instead that
\sum_{i}k_{i}\neq 0
, then
|{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}|=1
, which contradicts the assumption that
|{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}|-1=d\geq n\geq 3
. ∎

3.
Maximal cliques

Let
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
be the Veronese type algebra in
2.3
. In one of the main results of this paper, we determine the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
.
Recall that if
\beta_{i,j}
is the graded Betti number of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
considered
as a
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]
-module,
then the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is defined to be

\operatorname{reg}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})\coloneqq\max_{i,j}\Set{j-i:\beta_{i,j}\neq 0}.

Our exploration of the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity depends on the
following key observations.

Observation 3.1
.

Let
J
be the presentation ideal of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
generated by the set
\mathscr{J}
in
2.5
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is Cohen–Macaulay and
\operatorname{in}(J)
is squarefree by
2.5
, it follows from
[
3
, Corollary 2.7]
that the quotient ring
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]/\operatorname{in}(J)
is Cohen–Macaulay.
Let
(\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee}
be the Alexander dual of the squarefree ideal
\operatorname{in}(J)
. By
[
3
, Corollary 2.7]

and
[
15
, Proposition 8.1.10]
,
\operatorname{reg}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})=\operatorname{reg}({\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]/J)=\operatorname{reg}({\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]/\operatorname{in}(J))=\operatorname{pd}((\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee})
.

Inspired by the above observation, we turn to find the projective dimension of
(\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee}
.
To do this, we proceed as follows.
First, since
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]/\operatorname{in}(J)
is Cohen–Macaulay,
(\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee}
is an equigenerated ideal.
We will describe the minimal monomial generators of this ideal as the maximal cliques of length
n
of some graph in
3.5
.
Then, in Section
4
, we give a total order on the minimal monomial generating set
G((\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee})
and show that
(\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee}
has linear quotients with respect to this order. Details of these quotients are important by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
15
, Corollary 8.2.2]
)
.

Let
I
be an equigenerated ideal in the polynomial ring
S
. Suppose that the minimal generating set
G(I)
consists of
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
such that
Q_{i}\coloneqq\braket{f_{1},\dots,f_{i-1}}:_{S}f_{i}
is linear for each
i
. Let
p=\max_{i}\mu(Q_{i})
be the maximum of the minimal numbers of generators of the successive linear quotient ideals. Then, the top nonzero total Betti number of
I
is
\beta_{p}(I)
, which has the value
\#\Set{i:\mu(Q_{i})=p}
. In particular, the projective dimension of
I
is given by
p
.

Definition 3.3
.

(1)

Recall that
V_{n,d}
is the set
\{{\bm{a}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}:|{\bm{a}}|=d\}
. For any distinct
{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\in V_{n,d}
, we write
{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}
if the leftmost nonzero entry of
{\bm{a}}-{\bm{b}}
is positive.

(2)

Let
{\bm{\eta}}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})
be the smallest tuple in
V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
with respect to
>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}
, namely, if
{\bm{a}}\in V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
, then
{\bm{a}}\geq_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{\eta}}
. It is clear that
\eta_{n}=\alpha_{n}
and
\eta_{i}=\min\{\alpha_{i},d-\eta_{n}-\eta_{n-1}-\cdots-\eta_{i+1}\}
for
i=n-1,n-2,\dots,1
.

(3)

Let
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(d,{\bm{\alpha}})
be a simple graph on the set
V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
, such that
\{{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\}
with
{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}
is an edge if and only if
({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})
is sorted. A
clique
of
\mathcal{G}
is a collection of vertices, in which, every two different vertices are adjacent. A
maximal clique
is a clique that is maximal with respect to inclusion. Let
\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G})=\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G}(d,{\bm{\alpha}}))
be the set of maximal cliques of
\mathcal{G}
.

Remark 3.4
.

(1)

If
({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})
is a sorted pair in
V_{n,d}
, then
{\bm{a}}\geq_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}
.

(2)

The fact (ii) before
[
23
, Theorem 14.2]
can be restated as follows. Suppose that
\{{\bm{a}}^{1}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{a}}^{2}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}\cdots>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{a}}^{s}\}
is a subset of
V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
such that
\operatorname{sort}({\bm{a}}^{i},{\bm{a}}^{j})=({\bm{a}}^{i},{\bm{a}}^{j})
for all
1\leq i<j\leq s
. Then,
\operatorname{sort}({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{s})=({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{s})
. In other words, sorted subsets of
V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
are precisely cliques in
\mathcal{G}
.

(3)

It follows from the description in
2.5
and the fact just stated in
2
that the squarefree monomial ideal
\operatorname{in}(J)
is the edge ideal of the complement graph
\mathcal{G}^{\complement}
. Suppose that
T_{{\bm{a}}^{1}}T_{{\bm{a}}^{2}}\cdots T_{{\bm{a}}^{m}}\in G(\operatorname{in}(J)^{\vee})
is a minimal monomial generator of the Alexander dual ideal. This is equivalent to saying that
\{{\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{m}\}
is a minimal vertex cover of the complement graph
\mathcal{G}^{\complement}
, by
[
15
, Corollary 9.1.5]
. In other words, the set complement
\{{\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{m}\}^{\complement}
is a maximal clique of
\mathcal{G}
.

Consequently, in this section, we turn to the study of the maximal cliques.

Proposition 3.5
.

Every maximal clique of
\mathcal{G}
has cardinality
n
.

Proof.

The Veronese type algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is Cohen–Macaulay by
2.5
. In other words, the presentation ideal
J
is Cohen–Macaulay. Since
\operatorname{in}(J)
is squarefree, this implies that
\operatorname{in}(J)
is Cohen–Macaulay by
[
3
, Corollary 2.7]
. In particular,
\operatorname{in}(J)
is height unmixed. Notice that
\operatorname{ht}(\operatorname{in}(J))=\operatorname{ht}(J)=\#{\bm{T}}-n
by
2.6
, where
\#{\bm{T}}
is the number of variables in
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]
, i.e., the dimension of this polynomial ring.
Consequently, every minimal monomial generator of
\operatorname{in}(J)^{\vee}
has degree
\#{\bm{T}}-n
, and every maximal clique has size
\#{\bm{T}}-(\#{\bm{T}}-n)=n
by
3.4

3
.
∎

We need more tools to describe these maximal cliques in detail.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
be two tuples. Suppose that the following
condition is satisfied.

(
S
):

There exist
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{2k-1}<i_{2k}\leq n
such that

b_{i_{2j-1}}=a_{i_{2j-1}}-1
while
b_{i_{2j}}=a_{i_{2j}}+1
for

1\leq j\leq k
. Furthermore, for
t\in[n]\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{2k}\}
, one has
b_{t}=a_{t}
.

In this case, the
sorting signature set

\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})
of this
pair is defined to be

\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})\coloneqq\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}[i_{2j-1},i_{2j}),

which is considered as the union of intervals on the real line.
Consequently, the
length
of this set is

\sum_{j=1}^{k}(i_{2j}-i_{2j-1})
, denoted by
\operatorname{length}(\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}))
.

From the definition, we can directly verify the following simple fact.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}
be two tuples in
V_{n,d}
. Then,

\operatorname{sort}({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})=({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})
if and only if the condition

(
S
)
is satisfied.

The following are further important observations when using the sorting operator.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
\{{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{c}}\}
is a sorted subset in
V_{n,d}
,
then
\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{c}})=\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})\sqcup\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{c}})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})\cap\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{c}})=\emptyset
. Suppose for contradiction that
\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})\cap\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{c}})
is not empty. Then, we can find suitable intersecting
[i_{2j-1},i_{2j})
and
[i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime},i_{2j^{\prime}}^{\prime})
in the definition of
\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})
and
\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{c}})
respectively. Let
j
and
j^{\prime}
be the smallest such that this happens.

(1)

Suppose that
i_{2j-1}=i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}
. Whence
b_{i_{2j-1}}=a_{i_{2j-1}}-1
and
c_{i_{2j-1}}=b_{i_{2j-1}}-1
. This forces
c_{i_{2j-1}}=a_{i_{2j-1}}-2
. Since condition (
S
) is not satisfied here, we have a contradiction to the assumption that
({\bm{a}},{\bm{c}})
is a sorted pair by
3.7
.

(2)

Suppose that
i_{2j-1}<i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}<i_{2j}
. Now, we derive
i_{2j^{\prime}-2}^{\prime}\leq i_{2j-1}
from the minimality assumption. From the interval positions, it is clear that
a_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}=b_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}=c_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}+1
and consequently
a_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}+b_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}+c_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. Meanwhile, it is not difficult to check that
-1+\sum_{k\leq i_{2j-1}}a_{k}=\sum_{k\leq i_{2j-1}}b_{k}=\sum_{k\leq i_{2j-1}}c_{k}
. Furthermore, we have
a_{t}=b_{t}=c_{t}
for
i_{2j-1}<t<i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}
. Whence, the sorting operator will produce
a_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}+1=b_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}=c_{i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}}
, which is a contradiction.

(3)

Suppose that
i_{2j^{\prime}-1}^{\prime}<i_{2j-1}<i_{2j^{\prime}}^{\prime}
. This case is similar to
2
above. ∎

Lemma 3.9
.

(1)

If
\{{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{d}}\}
and
\{{\bm{b}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{c}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{d}}\}
are two cliques in
\mathcal{G}
, then
\{{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{c}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{d}}\}
is also a clique in
\mathcal{G}
.

(2)

Symmetrically, if
\{{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{c}}\}
and
\{{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{c}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{d}}\}
are two cliques in
\mathcal{G}
, then
\{{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{c}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{d}}\}
is also a clique in
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we will only consider the first case. Since
\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})\cap\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{d}})=\emptyset
while
\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{c}})\subseteq\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{d}})
by
3.8
, we have
\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})\cap\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{c}})=\emptyset
. Then, we can apply
3.7
and
3.8
to show that
\operatorname{sort}({\bm{a}},{\bm{c}})=({\bm{a}},{\bm{c}})
with
\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{c}})=\Delta({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}})\sqcup\Delta({\bm{b}},{\bm{c}})
. Whence,
\{{\bm{a}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{b}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{c}}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{d}}\}
is a clique in
\mathcal{G}
.
∎

We know from

that every maximal clique has cardinality
n
. Additional information can be drawn here.

Corollary 3.10
.

For each maximal clique
\{{\bm{a}}^{1}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}\cdots>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{a}}^{n}\}
in

\mathcal{G}
, we have
\operatorname{length}(\Delta({\bm{a}}^{i},{\bm{a}}^{j}))=j-i

a_{n}^{n}=a_{n}^{1}+1

for

Proof.

Since
\Delta({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{n})\subseteq[1,n)

\operatorname{length}(\Delta({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{n}))\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{length}(\Delta({\bm{a}}^{i},{\bm{a}}^{i+1}))\geq n-1

, the first two statements are clear.
The last statement follows from the definition of the sorting signature
set.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\{{\bm{a}}^{1}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{a}}^{2}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}\cdots>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{a}}^{n}>_{{\operatorname{lex}}}{\bm{a}}^{n+1}\}
be a set of vertices in
\mathcal{G}
. Suppose that

\mathcal{G}

We have

4.
Linear Quotients

In this section, we continue to assume that
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is the Veronese type algebra of
2.3
and
{\bm{\eta}}
is the tuple given in
3.3
. As explained at the beginning of Section
3
, we intend to show that the Alexander dual ideal

in
Section

To prove the linear quotient property, we need to impose a total order
\prec
on the minimal monomial generating set
G(\operatorname{in}(J)^{\vee})
, such that with respect to this order, the ideal
\operatorname{in}(J)^{\vee}
has linear quotients. Let
\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G})
be the set of maximal cliques of the graph
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(d,{\bm{\alpha}})
. As observed in
3.4
3
,

G(\operatorname{in}(J)^{\vee})=\Set{{\bm{T}}_{A^{\complement}}\coloneqq\prod_{v\in V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\setminus A}T_{v}:A\in\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G})}.

Thus, we will consider the corresponding total order, still denoted by

\prec
, on
\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G})
. By definition, we want to show that the quotient
ideal
\braket{{\bm{T}}_{B^{\complement}}:B\prec A}:{\bm{T}}_{A^{\complement}}
is
generated by some ring variables of
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]
for each
A\in\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G})
.
Notice that

(1)

\braket{{\bm{T}}_{B^{\complement}}}:{\bm{T}}_{A^{\complement}}=\prod_{v\in A\setminus B}T_{v}.

Therefore, for any given maximal clique
B
with
B\prec A
, it suffices to
find a suitable maximal clique
D
with

such that
A\setminus D
is
a singleton set with
A\setminus D\subseteq A\setminus B
.

Unfortunately, the total order
\prec
introduced here is really involved. We have to postpone its debut to

Definition 4.1
.

(1)

A priori, a maximal clique of the graph

(3)

(5)

Suppose that

(1)

(1)

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

(2)

(3)

Firstly, we show that

\Rightarrow

{\bm{a}}^{1}={\bm{b}}^{1}

1
.

Lemma 4.9

For each

, by definition, we apply an
s_{i}
-jump to
{\bm{a}}^{i}
in order to get
{\bm{a}}^{i+1}
.

•

If
a_{s_{i}}^{1}=0
and
s_{i}>1
, then we must have applied an
(s_{i}-1)
-jump somewhere before the
s_{i}
-jump. Meanwhile, we require that
(s_{i}-1)\,\triangleleft\,s_{i}
in
4.8
.

•

Likewise, if
a_{s_{i}+1}^{1}=\alpha_{s_{i}+1}
and
s_{i}+1<n
, then we must have applied an
(s_{i}+1)
-jump somewhere before the
s_{i}
-jump. Meanwhile, we require that
(s_{i}+1)\,\triangleleft\,s_{i}
in
4.8
.

Note that we won’t have
s_{i}\,\triangleleft\,(s_{i}+1)
and
(s_{i}+1)\,\triangleleft\,s_{i}
at the same time: the first requires the
s_{i}
-jump to be applied before the
(s_{i}+1)
-jump in
A
, while the second requires the opposite. This shows that the poset is well-defined. It is also clear that the condition (
PO
) holds.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Conversely, let
{\bm{s}}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1})
be a permutation of in
\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}
. Suppose that the condition
(
PO
)
is satisfied by
{\bm{s}}
. Then,
{\bm{s}}
is a legitimate signature with respect to
\mathcal{E}
, namely, there exists some
A\in\mathcal{E}
such that
\operatorname{sgn}(A)={\bm{s}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the maximal cliques in
\mathcal{E}
all start with
{\bm{a}}^{1}
and end with
{\bm{a}}^{n}
. It suffices to construct
{\bm{a}}^{2},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{n-1}\in V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
, such that
\Delta({\bm{a}}^{i-1},{\bm{a}}^{i})=[s_{i-1},s_{i-1}+1)
when
i\geq 2
. We do this by induction on
i
.
The degenerate base case when
i=1
is trivial.
Next, assume that
{\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{i-1}
have been constructed and
2\leq i\leq n-1
.

(1)

If
a_{s_{i-1}}^{i-1}>0
and
a_{s_{i-1}+1}^{i-1}<\alpha_{s_{i-1}+1}
, it is clear that we can apply the
s_{i-1}
-jump to
{\bm{a}}^{i-1}
to get a tuple
{\bm{a}}^{i}\in V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
. Certainly
\Delta({\bm{a}}^{i-1},{\bm{a}}^{i})=[s_{i-1},s_{i-1}+1)
.

(2)

Suppose that
a_{s_{i-1}}^{i-1}=0
. As
a_{s_{i-1}}^{1}\geq 0
, we deduce that
a_{s_{i-1}}^{i-1}\neq a_{s_{i-1}}^{1}+1
. Since
{\bm{s}}
is a permutation, we cannot apply the
s_{i-1}
-jump before
{\bm{a}}^{i-1}
. Thus, it follows from
4.7
that we cannot use the
(s_{i-1}-1)
-jump before
{\bm{a}}^{i-1}
.

(a)

Suppose that
s_{i-1}=1
. Whence,
s_{k}\neq 1
for
k<i-1
. Consequently, we deduce that
a_{1}^{1}=\cdots=a_{1}^{i-1}=0
. But this is impossible since it is necessary that
a_{1}^{1}\geq 1
.

(b)

Suppose that
s_{i-1}>1
. If in addition
a_{s_{i-1}}^{1}=0
, we have
(s_{i-1}-1)\,\triangleleft\,s_{i-1}
from
4.8
. Then, the condition (
PO
) implies that the
(s_{i-1}-1)
-jump is applied before the
s_{i-1}
-jump, i.e., the
(s_{i-1}-1)
-jump is applied before
{\bm{a}}^{i-1}
, a contradiction. If instead
a_{s_{i-1}}^{1}>0
, then
a_{s_{i-1}}^{i-1}=a_{s_{i-1}}^{1}-1
. It follows from
4.7
that the
s_{i-1}
-jump is applied before
{\bm{a}}^{i-1}
, again a contradiction.

(3)

Suppose that
a_{s_{i-1}+1}^{i-1}=\alpha_{s_{i-1}+1}
. We can argue as in the previous case to see that this is impossible.
∎

The final step is to provide each equivalence class with additional structures.

Remark 4.11
.

Suppose that
i\,\triangleleft\,j
in the poset of obstructions with respect to
\mathcal{E}
. If
i<j
, then we have
i\,\triangleleft\,(i+1)\,\triangleleft\,\cdots\,\triangleleft\,(j-1)\,\triangleleft\,j
. If instead

, then we have
i\,\triangleleft\,(i-1)\,\triangleleft\,\cdots\,\triangleleft\,(j+1)\,\triangleleft\,j
.

Definition 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an equivalence class of maximal cliques.

(1)

In
\mathcal{E}
, we will take a maximal clique

as
follows. If

, then let
\kappa_{1}
be the largest integer

\kappa_{1}\leq n-1
and
a_{2}^{1}=\cdots=a_{\kappa_{1}}^{1}=0
.

. Symmetrically,

be the smallest integer

for all

\kappa_{2}\leq j\leq n-1
. When

simply take

. It is clear that
\kappa_{1}<\kappa_{2}
if

4.8
, we have the

and
(n-1)\,\triangleleft\,(n-2)\,\triangleleft\,\cdots\,\triangleleft\,(\kappa_{2}-1)

Note that these relations are the only nontrivial ones that
include the integers

in the

. On the other

(2)

We will call

(3)

Suppose that

.

Recall that
\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G})
is the set of maximal cliques of

.

Setting 4.13

(
Rules of order
)
.

Let

be a total order on
\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G})
satisfying the following conditions.

(1)

If

.

(2)

also induces a total order on the set of equivalence classes.

(3)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an equivalence class and

be the special maximal clique we chose in

.

In the rest of this paper, we will simply fix one that works.

Lemma 4.15
.

Suppose that

\operatorname{sgn}(B)=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n-1})

\operatorname{sgn}(C)=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k},s_{k+1},t_{k+1},p_{k+3},\dots,p_{\ell-1},s_{\ell},\dots,s_{n-1})

{\bm{a}}^{k+1}=(a_{1}^{k+1},\dots,a_{n}^{k+1})

{\bm{a}}^{1}={\bm{b}}^{1}
and

, we have

{\bm{b}}^{k+1}={\bm{a}}^{k+1}

{\bm{b}}^{k+2}=(a_{1}^{k+1},\dots,a_{t_{k+1}-1}^{k+1},a_{t_{k+1}}^{k+1}-1,a_{t_{k+1}+1}^{k+1}+1,a_{t_{k+1}+2}^{k+1},\dots,a_{n}^{k+1}).

s_{k+1}<t_{k+1}

(1)

Suppose that

. Let
{\bm{c}}^{k+3}
be the tuple such that

The following proposition guarantees the linear quotients within an equivalence class.

Proposition 4.18

Suppose that
A
and
B
are two maximal cliques in an equivalence class

\mathcal{E}
such that

D\in\mathcal{E}
such that
D\prec A
, and the set difference
A\setminus D
is a
singleton set with
A\setminus D\subseteq A\setminus B

Suppose that in the marked equivalence class
(\mathcal{E},L)
, one has
\operatorname{sgn}(L)={\bm{\tau}}
. In addition, assume that
A=({\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{n})
and
B=({\bm{b}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{b}}^{n})\in\mathcal{E}
. Recall that we previously defined
\operatorname{diff}(A,B)\coloneqq\Set{i:{\bm{a}}^{i}\neq{\bm{b}}^{i}}
.

We may further assume that
A\in\mathcal{E}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1},j_{k}}
and
B\in\mathcal{E}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1},j_{k}^{\prime}}
with
j_{k}^{\prime}<j_{k}
. Then
k+1=\min\operatorname{diff}(A,B)
and it is clear that
k\leq n-2
.
Let us consider a set of maximal cliques

\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\Set{F\in\mathcal{E}:\operatorname{diff}(A,F)\subseteq\operatorname{diff}(A,B)\setminus\{k+1\}}.

Since
A\in\mathcal{D}
, the set
\mathcal{D}
is not empty. Let
A^{\prime}
be the first maximal clique in
\mathcal{D}
with respect to
\prec
and suppose that
\operatorname{sgn}(A^{\prime})=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1})
. Obviously,
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}
and
A^{\prime}\preceq A
. Furthermore, we have both
\operatorname{diff}(A^{\prime},B)\subseteq\operatorname{diff}(A,B)
and
\operatorname{diff}(A,A^{\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{diff}(A,B)
. Let
C=({\bm{c}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{c}}^{n})
be the tuple of elements in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, such that
{\bm{c}}^{1}={\bm{a}}^{1}
and
\operatorname{sgn}(C)=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-1},s_{k+1},s_{k},s_{k+2},\dots,s_{n-1})
. By
4.5
,
\operatorname{diff}(C,A^{\prime})=\{k+1\}
. In addition, we
claim
that
C
is a legitimate maximal clique in
\mathcal{E}
and
C\prec A^{\prime}
. With this, if
A=A^{\prime}
, then we take
D=C
since
\operatorname{diff}(A,B)\supseteq\operatorname{diff}(A,C)=\{k+1\}
. If instead
A\neq A^{\prime}
, then we will replace
B
by
A^{\prime}
. Our proof will be done by induction on the cardinality
\#\operatorname{diff}(A,B)
, since
\operatorname{diff}(A,A^{\prime})\subsetneq\operatorname{diff}(A,B)
and
A^{\prime}\prec A
.

It remains to prove the above claim about
C
. Since
A\in\mathcal{E}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1},j_{k}}
, we may assume that the signatures of the maximal cliques in
\mathcal{E}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1},\ell}
have the form
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-1},s_{k,\ell},p_{k+1},\dots,p_{n-1})
for each
\ell<j_{k}
with suitable
p_{i}
’s. From
4.17
we derive that either
s_{k}
precedes
s_{k+1}
with respect to
{\bm{\tau}}
, or
s_{k+1}\in\{s_{k,1},\dots,s_{k,j_{k}-1}\}
.

(1)

Suppose that
s_{k}
precedes
s_{k+1}
with respect to
{\bm{\tau}}
. Since
B\in\mathcal{E}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1},j_{k}^{\prime}}
with
j_{k}^{\prime}<j_{k}
, we can write
\operatorname{sgn}(B)=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-1},s_{k,j_{k}^{\prime}},p_{k+1},\dots,p_{n-1})
with suitable
p_{i}
’s. Since
j_{k}^{\prime}<j_{k}
,
s_{k,j_{k}^{\prime}}
precedes
s_{k}
with respect to
{\bm{\tau}}
. As a result of our assumption here,
s_{k,j_{k}^{\prime}}
also precedes
s_{k+1}
with respect to
{\bm{\tau}}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\operatorname{diff}(A^{\prime},B)=\{k+1,k+2,\dots,r\}
is a “continuous” segment. Applying
4.15
to
A^{\prime}
and
B
, we can find some maximal clique
A^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathcal{E}
such that
\operatorname{diff}(A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{diff}(A^{\prime},B)
and
\operatorname{sgn}(A^{\prime\prime})=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k},s_{k,j_{k}^{\prime}},r_{k+2},\dots,r_{n-1})
for suitable
r_{i}
’s. In particular,
k+1\notin\operatorname{diff}(A,A^{\prime\prime})
. Meanwhile, we observe that

\operatorname{diff}(A,A^{\prime\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{diff}(A,A^{\prime})\cup\operatorname{diff}(A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{diff}(A,B)\cup\operatorname{diff}(A^{\prime},B)=\operatorname{diff}(A,B).

Thus, the maximal clique
A^{\prime\prime}
belongs to the previously defined set
\mathcal{D}
. But since
s_{k,j_{k}^{\prime}}
precedes
s_{k+1}
with respect to
{\bm{\tau}}
while
\operatorname{sgn}(A^{\prime})=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1})
, this contradicts our choice of
A^{\prime}
.

(2)

Suppose instead that
s_{k+1}=s_{k,\ell}
for some
\ell<j_{k}
. Then,
{\bm{c}}^{k+1}
is a legitimate tuple by the existence of
\mathcal{E}_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1},\ell}
. Meanwhile, notice that
A^{\prime}
is a legitimate maximal clique such that
\operatorname{diff}(C,A^{\prime})=\{k+1\}
. Consequently,
C
is also a legitimate maximal clique. Since
\ell<j_{k}
, the index
s_{k+1}
precedes
s_{k}
with respect to
{\bm{\tau}}
. Thus,
C\prec A^{\prime}
, fully confirming our claim about
C
.
∎

Next, we consider the linear quotients across equivalence classes.

Lemma 4.19
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}
is an equivalence class such that
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{E})>0
. Let
\kappa_{1}
,
\kappa_{2}
, and
L
be as introduced in
4.12
. Then, we have
\kappa_{1}+1<\kappa_{2}-1
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
\kappa_{1}+1\geq\kappa_{2}-1
. Since
\kappa_{1}<\kappa_{2}
, we obtain either
\kappa_{1}+1=\kappa_{2}
or
\kappa_{1}+1=\kappa_{2}-1
. Since
n\geq 3
,
\kappa_{1}\leq n-1
, and
\kappa_{2}\geq 2
, we encounter the following four cases.

(1)

Suppose that
\kappa_{1}=n-1
. Then
{\bm{a}}^{1}=(1,0,\dots,0,a_{n}^{1})
with
0\leq a_{n}^{1}\leq\alpha_{n}-1
. Consequently,
{\bm{a}}^{n}=(0,\dots,0,a_{n}^{1}+1)
, which has to be
{\bm{\eta}}
.

(2)

Suppose that
\kappa_{2}=2
. Then
{\bm{a}}^{1}=(a_{1}^{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}-1)
with
a_{1}^{1}>1
. Consequently,
{\bm{a}}^{n}=(a_{1}^{1}-1,\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
, which has to be
{\bm{\eta}}
.

(3)

Suppose that
\kappa_{1}+1=\kappa_{2}
and
2\leq\kappa_{1}\leq n-2
. Then
{\bm{a}}^{1}=(1,0,\dots,0,\alpha_{\kappa_{1}+1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}-1)
. Consequently,
{\bm{a}}^{n}=(0,\dots,0,\alpha_{\kappa_{1}+1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
, which has to be
{\bm{\eta}}
.

(4)

Suppose that
\kappa_{1}+1=\kappa_{2}-1
and
1\leq\kappa_{1}\leq n-2
. Then

{\bm{a}}^{1}=(1,0,\dots,0,a_{\kappa_{1}+1}^{1},\alpha_{\kappa_{1}+2},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}-1)

with
0<a_{\kappa_{1}+1}^{1}<\alpha_{\kappa_{1}+1}
. Consequently,

{\bm{a}}^{n}=(0,\dots,0,a_{\kappa_{1}+1}^{1},\alpha_{\kappa_{1}+2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
, which has to be
{\bm{\eta}}
.

In each case, we always have
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{E})=0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
A=({\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{n})
be a maximal clique in the equivalence class

\mathcal{E}
such that
rA
does not exist. Suppose that
B\prec A
is a
maximal clique such that
B\notin\mathcal{E}
. Then, there exists another
maximal clique
D\in\mathcal{E}
and some
j\in[n-1]
such that
D\prec A

and
A\setminus D=\{{\bm{a}}^{j+1}\}\subseteq A\setminus B
.

Proof.

We will prove this by using the notation and arguments of
4.12
.
As
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{E})>0
by the existence of
B
, we showed in
4.19
that
\kappa_{1}+1<\kappa_{2}-1
, and the first maximal clique
L
in
\mathcal{E}
satisfies

\operatorname{sgn}(L)=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1}-2},\underbrace{n-1,n-2,\dots,\kappa_{2}-1},\underbrace{1,2,\dots,\kappa_{1}}),

for appropriate
\tau_{i}
’s.

Suppose that
\operatorname{sgn}(A)=(s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1})
and let
W
be the set

[n-1]\setminus(\{n-1,n-2,\dots,\kappa_{2}-1\}\sqcup\{1,2,\dots,\kappa_{1}\})=\{\kappa_{1}+1,\dots,\kappa_{2}-2\}.

Since
\kappa_{1}+1<\kappa_{2}-1
,
W
is not empty. Meanwhile, since
rA
does not exist,
s_{1}=1
or
n-1
by
4.4
, and
s_{1}\notin W
.
In the following, let
j\geq 1
be the smallest such that
s_{j+1}\in W
. We can construct a maximal clique
D
in
\mathcal{E}
such that
\operatorname{sgn}(D)=(s_{1},\dots,s_{j-1},s_{j+1},s_{j},s_{j+2},\dots,s_{n-1})
.

To confirm the legitimacy of
D
, notice that
s_{j}\notin W
while
s_{j+1}\in W
.
Since
s_{j+1}
precedes
s_{j}
in
\operatorname{sgn}(L)
while
s_{j}
precedes
s_{j+1}
in
\operatorname{sgn}(A)
, these two indices
are not comparable in the poset of obstructions, i.e., they can exchange positions in any legitimate signature. As
\operatorname{sgn}(A)
is a legitimate signature, so is
\operatorname{sgn}(D)
, namely,
D
is a maximal clique in
\mathcal{E}
.

Next, since
s_{j+1}
precedes
s_{j}
in
\operatorname{sgn}(L)
, we deduce that
D\prec A
. From
4.5
we also have
A\setminus D=\{{\bm{a}}^{j+1}\}
.
It remains to show that this
{\bm{a}}^{j+1}\notin B
. Suppose for
contradiction that this is not true. Then, we can write
B=({\bm{b}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{b}}^{n})
and
{\bm{b}}^{j^{\prime}}={\bm{a}}^{j+1}
for some
j^{\prime}
. Since
B\prec A
, we
must have
j^{\prime}\leq j+1
, by rank reason. At the same time, given

4.9
, we can find
i_{1}\leq\kappa_{1}
and
i_{2}\leq n-\kappa_{2}
such
that
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{j}\}=\{1,\dots,i_{1}\}\sqcup\{n-i_{2},\dots,n-1\}
,
by the choice of
j
. Whence, we can write
{\bm{a}}^{j+1}=(\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{i_{1}},a^{j+1}_{i_{1}+1},\dots,a^{j+1}_{n-i_{2}},\underbrace{\alpha_{n-i_{2}+1},\dots,\alpha_{n}}_{i_{2}})
. Since
{\bm{b}}^{j^{\prime}}={\bm{a}}^{j+1}
, we obtain that
1,2,\dots,i_{1},n-i_{2},\dots,n-1\notin\Delta({\bm{b}}^{j^{\prime}},{\bm{b}}^{n})
. Thus, if
\operatorname{sgn}(B)=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n-1})
, then

\{1,\dots,i_{1},n-i_{2},\dots,n-1\}\subseteq\{q_{1},\dots,q_{j^{\prime}-1}\}
,
forcing
j\leq j^{\prime}-1
. Therefore,
j^{\prime}=j+1
. It is now clear that
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{j}\}=\{1,\dots,i_{1}\}\sqcup\{n-i_{2},\dots,n-1\}
, and

\Delta({\bm{b}}^{1},{\bm{b}}^{j+1})=\Delta({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{j+1})
. As

{\bm{b}}^{j+1}={\bm{a}}^{j+1}
, it follows that
{\bm{b}}^{1}={\bm{a}}^{1}
and
B\in\mathcal{E}

as well. This contradicts the assumption that
B\notin\mathcal{E}
.
∎

Finally, we are ready to show the announced linear quotient result.

Theorem 4.21
.

The total order
\prec
of
4.13
induces a linear quotient order of the monomial generating set
G(\operatorname{in}(J)^{\vee})
.

Proof.

Take arbitrary maximal cliques
A
and
B
such that
B\prec A
. Given
Equation

1
, it suffices to find suitable maximal clique
D
with
D\prec A
such that
A\setminus D
is a singleton set with
A\setminus D\subseteq A\setminus B
. Suppose that
A=({\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{n})
and
A\in\mathcal{E}
. We have two cases.

(1)

Assume that
B\in\mathcal{E}
. We apply
4.18

for the existence of such

.

(2)

Assume that
B\notin\mathcal{E}
.

(a)

If
rA
exists, then

. Note that
{\bm{a}}^{1}\notin B

(b)

5.

This section is devoted to two applications of the linear quotient structure that we established earlier. Here, we continue to assume that
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is the Veronese type algebra of
2.3
and
{\bm{\eta}}
is the tuple given in

5.1.
Regularity of the algebra

First of all, we determine the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
.

For each maximal clique
A
in the graph
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(d,{\bm{\alpha}})

\braket{{\bm{T}}_{B^{\complement}}:B\prec A}:_{{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]}{\bm{T}}_{A^{\complement}}
by
\omega_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(A)

\omega(A)
for short.
By the proof of
4.21
, we have

(2)

\omega(A)=\#\Set{B\prec A:\operatorname{diff}(A,B)\text{ is a singleton set}}.

Furthermore, by
3.2
, we have the following formula:

(3)

\operatorname{pd}((\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee})=\max\limits_{A}\omega(A).

Since
\operatorname{pd}((\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee})=\operatorname{reg}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})
by

, the task is now clear:
find the largest
\omega(A)
.
We start with a quick estimate.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have
\omega(A)\leq n-1
for each maximal clique
A=({\bm{a}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{n})
.

Proof.

Since
\braket{{\bm{T}}_{B^{\complement}}:B\prec A}:_{{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]}{\bm{T}}_{A^{\complement}}
is linear, it follows from
Equation

1
that

\omega(A)=\#\Set{{\bm{a}}^{i}:\text{there exists some $B\prec A$ with
$\{{\bm{a}}^{i}\}=A\setminus B$}}.

Therefore, it suffices to show that there is no
B
with
B\prec A
such that
\{{\bm{a}}^{n}\}=A\setminus B
. Suppose for contradiction that this is not true. By
3.10
, we have either
B=({\bm{b}},{\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{n-1})
or
B=({\bm{a}}^{1},{\bm{a}}^{2},\dots,{\bm{a}}^{n-1},{\bm{b}})
for some suitable tuple
{\bm{b}}
. In the first case,
\operatorname{rank}(B)=\operatorname{rank}(A)+1
, which contradicts the assumption that
B\prec A
. In the second case, we have
B=A
by
3.10
, which is also a contradiction.
∎

It is natural to ask: when do we have
\omega(A)=n-1
? Let us start with a simple observation. In any equivalence class
\mathcal{E}
, every maximal clique
A
uniquely corresponds to the signature
\operatorname{sgn}(A)
, which is a permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}
. Thus, it is clear that
\mathcal{E}
contains at most
(n-1)!
elements. In the following, we classify when an equivalent class
\mathcal{E}
contains exactly
(n-1)!
elements.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an equivalence class such that every maximal clique in it begins with
{\bm{a}}^{1}=(a_{1}^{1},\ldots,a_{n}^{1})
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)

the cardinality
\#\mathcal{E}=(n-1)!
;

(2)

the poset of obstructions in
4.8
is trivial for
\mathcal{E}
;

(3)

one has
1\leq a_{1}^{1}\leq\alpha_{1}
,
1\leq a_{j}^{1}\leq\alpha_{j}-1
for all
2\leq j\leq n-1
, and
0\leq a_{n}^{1}\leq\alpha_{n}-1
.

Proof.

The cardinality
\#\mathcal{E}=(n-1)!
if and only if every permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}
is
the signature of some maximal clique in
\mathcal{E}
. But the latter is equivalent to saying that the poset of obstructions in
4.8
is trivial for
\mathcal{E}
, namely,
1\leq a_{j}^{1}\leq\alpha_{j}-1
for all
2\leq j\leq n-1
. That
1\leq a_{1}^{1}\leq\alpha_{1}
and
0\leq a_{n}^{1}\leq\alpha_{n}-1
is automatic for all such
{\bm{a}}^{1}
in view of
3.10
.
∎

In the following, we characterize when
\operatorname{pd}((\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee})
is exactly
n-1
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

the projective dimension
\operatorname{pd}((\operatorname{in}(J))^{\vee})=n-1

(2)

there exists a maximal clique

(3)

and

.

5.2.
Multiplicity bound of the algebra

We conclude this work with a reasonable upper bound on the multiplicity of the
Veronese type algebra
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
. To begin with, we count the number
of generators of the ideal
I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}
, the number of different equivalent
classes of maximal cliques, and the number of equivalent classes that have
precisely
(n-1)!
maximal cliques.

Lemma 5.9
.

The dimension of the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}]\coloneqq{\mathbb{K}}[T_{\bm{a}}:{\bm{a}}\in V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}]
is given by

\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{K}[{\bm{T}}])=\sum_{i\geq 0}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\subseteq[n],\\
\#P=i\end{subarray}}\binom{d-\sum_{p\in P}(\alpha_{p}+1)+n-1}{n-1}.

Moreover, there are

\displaystyle G\coloneqq\sum_{i,j\geq 0}(-1)^{i+j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\subseteq\{2,3,\dots,n-1\},\\
\#P=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq\{1,n\},\\
\#Q=j\end{subarray}}\binom{d-\sum_{p\in P}(\alpha_{p}+1)-\sum_{q\in Q}\alpha_{q}+n-2}{n-1}

different equivalent classes of maximal cliques in the graph
\mathcal{G}(d,{\bm{\alpha}})
, and there are

\displaystyle H\coloneqq\sum_{i,j\geq 0}(-1)^{i+j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\subseteq\{2,3,\dots,n-1\},\\
\#P=i\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subseteq\{1,n\},\\
\#Q=j\end{subarray}}\binom{d-\sum_{p\in P}(\alpha_{p}-1)-\sum_{q\in Q}\alpha_{q}}{n-1}

equivalent classes which have precisely
(n-1)!
maximal cliques. In particular, there exists at most
H\cdot(n-1)!+(G-H)\cdot(n-1)!/2
different maximal cliques.

Proof.

It is clear that
\operatorname{dim}({\mathbb{K}}[{\bm{T}}])=\#V_{n,d}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
is equal to the number of ways to write
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=d
such that
0\leq a_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}
.
Furthermore, by
3.10
and
4.3
, the number
G
of equivalent classes of maximal cliques is the number of ways to have
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=d
, under the conditions that
0\leq a_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}
for
i=2,\ldots,n-1
,
1\leq a_{1}\leq\alpha_{1}
, and
0\leq a_{n}\leq\alpha_{n}-1
. It is not hard to see that
G=\#V_{n,d-1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}
, where
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}=(\alpha_{1}-1,\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}-1)
.
Similarly, by
5.2
, finding the number
H
is counting how many possible ways one can write
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=d
, subject to the conditions
1\leq a_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}-1
for
i=2,\ldots,n-1
,
1\leq a_{1}\leq\alpha_{1}
, and
0\leq a_{n}\leq\alpha_{n}-1
. It is not difficult to see that
H=\#V_{n,d-(n-1)}^{{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime\prime}}
, where
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime\prime}=(\alpha_{1}-1,\alpha_{2}-2,\alpha_{3}-2,\dots,\alpha_{n-1}-2,\alpha_{n}-1)
.
The three formulas given above then follow from the classical
stars and bars method
and the
inclusion-exclusion principle
in combinatorics.

Notice that if an equivalence class
\mathcal{E}
does not have
(n-1)!
maximal cliques, then its poset of obstructions is not trivial by
5.2
. Say, we have
p\,\triangleleft\,q
in this poset. Then, for any
A\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
p
preceding
q
in
\operatorname{sgn}(A)
. Thus,

\#\mathcal{E}=\#\Set{\operatorname{sgn}(A):A\in\mathcal{E}}\leq(n-1)!/2,

namely,
\mathcal{E}
contains at most
(n-1)!/2
maximal cliques. The “in particular” part then follows.
∎

For a homogeneous
\mathbb{K}
-algebra
R
, let
\mathtt{e}(R)
denote its
multiplicity

with respect to its graded maximal ideal.
This number is clear in the squarefree case, by the work of Terai
[

]
.

Lemma 5.10

(
[

)

Let

be a

\mathtt{e}(A/\mathfrak{a})=\beta_{1,h_{1}}(A/\mathfrak{a}^{\vee})
, where

\mathfrak{a}^{\vee}
.

Corollary 5.11
.

The multiplicity of
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}

\#\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G}(d,{\bm{\alpha}}))

Proof.

Note that
\mathtt{e}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})

\mathtt{e}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})
, we only need to compute the minimal

.

In addition, Terai gave the following upper bound on multiplicity.

Lemma 5.12

(

)
.

Let

If

We are ready to state our final result regarding the multiplicity of

.

Theorem 5.14
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}={\mathbb{K}}[I_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}]

whose presentation ideal

Then, we have

\displaystyle r+t-n\leq\mathtt{e}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})

\displaystyle\leq\min\Bigg\{H\cdot(n-1)!+\frac{(G-H)\cdot(n-1)!}{2},\;d_{1}^{n-1},\;d_{2}^{n-1},\;

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\binom{r+t-n}{t-n}-\binom{r-2+t-n}{t-n}\Bigg\}.

The codimension of

2.6

{\mathbb{K}}

5.13
.
As for the second inequality, we apply first Lemmas

5.9
and
5.11
.
In addition, notice that the multiplicity in the Veronese case is
well-known:
\mathtt{e}(\mathcal{A}_{d_{1},(d_{1},\ldots,d_{1})})=d_{1}^{n}
. Since

\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G}(d,{\bm{\alpha}}))\subseteq\operatorname{MC}(\mathcal{G}(d_{1},(d_{1},\dots,d_{1})))

\mathtt{e}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})\leq d_{1}^{n-1}

\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}}\cong\mathcal{A}_{d_{2},{\bm{\alpha}}}

\mathtt{e}(\mathcal{A}_{d,{\bm{\alpha}}})\leq d_{2}^{n-1}
.
As for the remaining piece, we observe that the presentation ideal

5.12
