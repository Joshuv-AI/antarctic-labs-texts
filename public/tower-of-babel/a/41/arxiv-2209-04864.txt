Activity-induced droplet propulsion and multifractality Preprint: APS/123-QED
Nadia Bihari Padhan, Rahul Pandit

Abstract
We develop a minimal hydrodynamic model, without an orientational order parameter, for assemblies of contractile swimmers encapsulated in a droplet of a binary-fluid emulsion. Our model uses two coupled scalar order parameters, ϕ \phi and ψ \psi , which capture, respectively, the droplet interface and the activity of the contractile swimmers inside this droplet. These order parameters are also coupled to the velocity field 𝒖 \bm{u} . At low activity, our model yields a self-propelling droplet whose center of mass ( C ​ M ) (CM) displays rectilinear motion, powered by the spatiotemporal evolution of the field ψ \psi , which leads to a time-dependent vortex dipole at one end of the droplet. As we increase the activity, this C ​ M CM shows chaotic super-diffusive motion, which we characterize by its mean-square displacement; and the droplet interface exhibits multifractal fluctuations, whose spectrum of exponents we calculate. We explore the implications of our results for experiments on active droplets of contractile swimmers.



I
Introduction

Active matter comprises systems that are far from equilibrium and in which the constituents extract energy from their surroundings, do mechanical work, and dissipate energy to the same environment
Ramaswamy 2017
;
Bowick et al. 2022
;
Marchetti et al. 2013
;
Mahault 2018
. The self-organisation of the constituents of such systems can lead to large-scale pattern formation, observed in, e.g., crowds
Castellano et al. 2009
;
Bottinelli et al. 2016
, fish schools
Becco et al. 2006
,
bird flocks
Bialek et al. 2012
;
Cavagna et al. 2010
, and bacterial colonies
Chen et al. 2017
. They exhibit a variety of fascinating emergent phenomena, e.g., Motility-Induced-Phase-Separation (MIPS), in which an initially uniform state of active swimmers separates spontaneously into dense and dilute phases, driven by persistent motion and repulsion
Wittkowski et al. 2014
;
Cates and Tailleur 2015
;
Gonnella et al. 2015
.
Most experiments, with motile bacteria or synthetic micro-swimmers, use confinement – solid immovable or soft, e.g., by
a droplet boundary – that plays a crucial role in the spatiotemporal patterns in assemblies of active micro-swimmers
Wioland et al. 2013
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Ramos et al. 2020
.
Certain bacterial systems, when confined to a droplet, can propel and deform the droplet
Kokot et al. 2022
;
Sokolov et al. 2018
;
Ramos et al. 2020
; self-propelling, or
active
droplets
have been considered in nematically ordered, active-polar, chemically driven, and phase-field systems
Gao and Li 2017
;
Copar et al. 2019
;
Tjhung et al. 2012
;
De Magistris et al. 2014
;
A Whitfield et al. 2014
;
Yoshinaga 2019
;
Ruske and Yeomans 2021
;
Fadda et al. 2017
;
Singh et al. 2020
.

We develop a
minimal phase-field model
for assemblies of
contractile swimmers
encapsulated in a droplet of a binary-fluid emulsion, to obtain self-propelling droplets, which are powered by the rich spatio-temporal dynamics of the contractile-swimmer field; this propulsion does not require any orientational ordering. Our results are of direct relevance to active droplets of contractile swimmers such as
Chlamydomonas reinhardtii

Yeomans et al. 2014
;
Fragkopoulos et al. 2021
(
C. reinhardtii
).
Our phase-field theory has two conserved scalar order parameters
\phi
and
\psi
. The former distinguishes between two, coexisting liquid phases, separated by an interface at the droplet boundary; the latter is related to the bacterial concentration;
\phi
and
\psi
are coupled to each other and to the flow velocity
\bm{u}
, as in the Cahn-Hilliard-Navier-Stokes (CHNS) system or model H
Pal et al. 2016
;
Perlekar et al. 2017
. In the absence of the direct coupling between the two order parameters, our model decouples into (a) the CHNS model, for
\phi
and
\bm{u}
, that has been used to study mutifractal droplet dynamics
Pal et al. 2016
in a turbulent flow and (b) the
active model
H, for
\psi
and
\bm{u}
, that has been used to study MIPS
Wittkowski et al. 2014
;
Tiribocchi et al. 2015
;
Shaebani et al. 2020
.

We carry out pseudospectral direct numerical simulations (DNSs) of our model to uncover the dependence of the spatiotemporal evolution of an emergent active droplet on the
activity
parameter
A
(defined below). For low values of
A
, the center-of-mass
(CM)
of the droplet shows rectilinear motion, associated with a time-dependent vortex dipole at one of its ends; as
A
increases, the droplet
fluctuates and its
CM
exhibits a crossover from rectilinear to super-diffusive motion, reminiscent of Lévy walks. Furthermore, at large values of
A
, the bacterial field generates low-Reynolds-number, but turbulent, flows and
multifractal deformation
of the active-droplet boundary.

V
Conclusion

We have developed a minimal model for assemblies of contractile swimmers, without alignment interactions, encapsulated in a droplet of a binary-fluid emulsion. Our hydrodynamic model, with the scalar order parameter
\phi
and the active scalar
\psi
coupled to each other and the velocity field
\bm{u}
, not only captures the droplet
interface (via the
\phi=0
contour) and its fluctuations, but also leads to droplet self-propulsion, which is rectilinear at low
A(\simeq 0.15)
and chaotic for large values of
A
, at which the
CM
of the droplet shows super-diffusive motion and
the droplet interface exhibits multifractal fluctuations. Our study is distinct from earlier theoretical studies of active droplets that consider cell-level models in nematically ordered, active polar, chemically driven, or phase-field systems
Gao and Li 2017
;
Copar et al. 2019
;
Tjhung et al. 2012
;
De Magistris et al. 2014
;
A Whitfield et al. 2014
;
Ruske and Yeomans 2021
;
Fadda et al. 2017
;
Singh et al. 2020
. By contrast, the activity-induced droplet propulsion in our model arises from the interplay of

Appendix A
Cahn-Hilliard formalism

It is convenient to write the Cahn-Hilliard free-energy functional for a binary-fluid mixture in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\phi)=\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{3}{16}\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}(\phi^{2}-1)^{2}+\frac{3}{4}\sigma\epsilon|\nabla\phi|^{2}\right]\,;

(11)

this depends on two important physical parameters:
\sigma
, the surface tension coefficient, and
\epsilon
, the interface width. The first term is a double-well potential with two minima at
\phi=\pm 1
. The equilibrium interfacial
profile
\phi_{0}(x)
can computed be computed by solving the following boundary value problem (if
\phi

is assumed to vary along one spatial direction, say
x
):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mu(x)=-\frac{3}{2}\sigma\epsilon\frac{d^{2}\phi_{0}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{3}{4}\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}(\phi_{0}^{3}-\phi_{0})=0\,;\\
\displaystyle{\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}}\phi_{0}=\pm 1\,.\end{cases}

(12)

Here,
\mu(x)\equiv\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}
is the chemical potential. The solution is

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(x)=\tanh{\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\epsilon}\right)}\,,

(13)

where
x_{0}
is the midpoint of the interface. The interfacial free-energy is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}(\phi_{0})dx=\sigma\,.

(14)

The advantage of writing the free-energy functional in the above form is that
\sigma
and
\epsilon
can be varied independently of each other. In our direct numerical simulations, we tune the value of
\epsilon
, depending upon the computational mesh size, without changing the surface tension
\sigma
.

The stress terms:
In the passive CHNS or model-H equations, the stress in the Navier-Stokes equations is

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathfrak{S}}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\phi\nabla\mu=\mu\nabla\phi-\nabla(\phi\mu)

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}\sigma\epsilon\nabla^{2}\phi\nabla\phi-\nabla\left(\phi\mu-\frac{3}{4}\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}(\phi^{4}/4-\phi^{2}/2)\right)\,.

The second term on the right-hand side of the above equation vanishes when we take the curl to obtain the vorticity equation, so we use the stress
\mathfrak{S}^{\phi}=-(3/2)\sigma\epsilon\nabla^{2}\phi\nabla\phi
.
There is an alternative way of writing this stress, in terms of a stress tensor, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{\bm{S}^{\phi}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\Sigma^{\phi}=-(3/2)\sigma\epsilon\nabla^{2}\phi\nabla\phi\,;

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ij}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(3/2)\sigma\epsilon\left[(\partial_{i}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi)-|\nabla\phi|^{2}\delta_{ij}/2\right]\,.

(16)

Similarly, for the stress terms in our active CHNS model, in the main paper, we use:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(3/2)\sigma_{1}\epsilon_{1}\nabla^{2}\phi\nabla\phi\,;

(17)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(3/2)\tilde{\sigma}_{2}\epsilon_{2}\nabla^{2}\psi\nabla\psi\,.

(18)

Appendix C
Multifractal anlysis

We use the wavelet-leader technique
Jaffard et al. 2006
;
Wendt and Abry 2007
to obtain the multifractal spectrum
of the Hurst exponents for the time series
\Gamma(t)
; we employ
MATLAB

MAT 2020
to perform this
multifractal analysis. The wavelet leader
T_{\psi}[f](t,a)
is obtained from the convolution operation for a time series

f(t)
as follows:

\displaystyle T_{\chi}[f](t,a)=\frac{1}{a}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)f(t)dt\,.

(24)

Here,
\chi(t)
is the form for a single wavelet, with
a
and
b
the scale and translation parameters, respectively. The structure function of order
q
, based on this wavelet leader, is

\displaystyle S_{T}(q,a)=\frac{1}{N_{w}}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{w}}|T_{\chi}[f](t,a)|^{q}\sim a^{\tau(q)}\,,

(25)

with
N_{w}=n/a
the number of wavelets for a particular scale
a
and
n
the length of the entire time series. The Legendre transform of the generalized exponents
\tau(q)
gives an upper bound for the generalized dimensions
D(h)
:

\displaystyle D(h)\leq\displaystyle\min_{q\neq 0}[1+qh(q)-\tau(q)]\,,

(26)

where
h(q)\equiv d\tau(q)/dq
is the Hölder exponent of order
q
. For a monofractal time series,
\tau(q)
is a linear function of
q
, so
h
is independent of
q
and
D(h)
is nonzero for only this value of
h
. By contrast, for a multifractal time series,
\tau(q)
is a nonlinear function of
q
[Fig.
4
(b)] and
D(h)
is a nontrivial function of
h

(see the main paper).

Appendix D
Calculation of the droplet’s center-of-mass
(CM)

The Fourier transform of the field
\phi(x,y)
is

\hat{\phi}(k_{x},k_{y})=\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)}\phi(x,y)\exp^{\imath(k_{x}x+k_{y}y)}\,.

(27)

The two components of the position of the droplet’s
CM
can now be calculated as follows
Bai and Breen 2008
:

\displaystyle X_{CM}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arctan\left(\Im(\hat{\phi}(1,0)),\Re(\hat{\phi}(1,0))\right)\,;

\displaystyle Y_{CM}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arctan\left(\Im(\hat{\phi}(0,1)),\Re(\hat{\phi}(0,1))\right)\,.

(28)
