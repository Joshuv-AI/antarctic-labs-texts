Belyi maps from zeroes of hypergeometric polynomials
Raimundas Vidunas

Abstract
Evaluation of low degree hypergeometric polynomials to zero defines an algebraic hypersurface in the affine space of the free parameters and the argument. This article investigates the algebraic surfaces F 1 2 ​ ( − N , b , c , z ) = 0 {}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}(-N,b;c;z)=0 for N = 3 N=3 and N = 4 N=4 . As a captivating application, these surfaces parametrize certain families of genus 0 Belyi maps.

Introduction

Systematic cataloging of Belyi maps is an active research undertaking
[
MSSV19
,
AS22
,
vHV15
]
.
Contributing to this venture, this article considers comprehensively Belyi maps
\varphi:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of the form

\varphi(x)=(1-x)^{p}(1-\lambda x)^{q}\,G_{m}(x)^{r}

(1.1)

or

\varphi(x)=(1+\alpha x+\beta x^{2})^{p}\,G_{m}(x)^{r},

(1.2)

where
G_{m}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
m
without multiple roots, and

\varphi(x)=1+O(x^{m+2}).

(1.3)

This implies
G_{m}(0)=1
.
The powers
p,q,r
are allowed to be positive or negative integers.
If the powers are positive integers, then the considered Belyi maps
are called
Shabat polynomials

[
LZ04
, §2.2]
or
generalized Chebyshev polynomials

[
Adr09
]
.
The powers
p,q,r
are assumed to be different.

Assuming that the point
x=\infty
is above
\varphi\in\{0,\infty\}
,
the distinct points in the three canonical fibers
\varphi\in\{0,1,\infty\}
are:

•

two roots of
(1-x)(1-\lambda x)
or

, of branching order
|p|
or
|q|
,
above
\varphi=0
or
\varphi=\infty

;

•

the roots of
G_{m}(x)
, of branching order
|r|
, above
\varphi\in\{0,\infty\}
;

•

the point
x=\infty
, of branching order
|p+q+mr|\neq 0
or
|2p+mr|\neq 0
respectively for the two forms,
above
\varphi\in\{0,\infty\}
;

•

the point
x=0
, of branching order
m+2
, above
\varphi=1
;

•

d-(m+2)
non-branching points above
\varphi=1
,
where

\varphi

In total we count

2+m+1+1+(d-m-2)=d+2

(1.4)

distinct points in the 3 fibers, which is the minimal number of points in 3 fibers
for a covering

by the Riemann-Hurwitz formula
[

, Theorem 5.9]
, and the number of points that Belyi maps of genus 0 must have in the 3 fibers.
This will be recapped in Lemma

.

As it turns out, the considered families of Belyi maps are parametrized
by the zero sets of hypergeometric polynomial equations,
namely

{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-m-1,\,\frac{q}{r}\atop-m-\frac{p}{r}}\right|\,\lambda\right)=0

(1.5)

for the Belyi maps of the form (
1.1

{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-\frac{m+1}{2},-\frac{m}{2}\atop-m-\frac{p}{r}}\right|\frac{4\beta}{\alpha^{2}}\right)=0

(1.6)

These hypergeometric equations were derived earlier by Adrianov

The equations give the generic number of distinct Belyi maps (up to Möbius transformations)
of the considered branching patterns, namely
m+1
for fixed
p,r,q,m
in the form (
1.1
),
or
\lceil m/2\rceil
for fixed
p,q,m
in the form (
1.2
). As we show in Lemmas

,
this number is smaller exactly when one of the quotients

,

is a smaller positive integer.
It appears that the hypergeometric polynomials in (

The considered Belyi maps constitute a borderline easy case

]
.
The new examples and methodology will not be radically novel for active readers of

The investigation of this article starts with recalling the relevant properties of hypergeometric functions. Sections

2
Hypergeometric details

The Gauss hypergeometric function
[
AAR99
, Ch. 2]
is defined by the series

\displaystyle{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{a,\,b\,\atop c}\right|\,z\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{k}\,(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}\,k!}\,z^{k}.

(2.1)

Here
(\alpha)_{k}=\alpha\,(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)\ldots(\alpha+k-1)

denotes the
Pochhammer symbol
, or the “raising” variant of the factorial.
Using the
\Gamma
-function
[
AAR99
, Ch. 1]
we can write

\displaystyle(\alpha)_{k}=\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+k)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

(2.2)

The standard analytic continuation of the
{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}
-function is onto
\mathbb{C}\setminus[1,\infty)
.
This function is undefined when
c
equals zero or a negative integer.

2.1
Hypergeometric polynomials

If well-defined, the hypergeometric series (
2.1
) is a polynomial in
z
when
a
or
b
are non-positive integers. In addition to (
1.8
), we have these symmetries of hypergeometric polynomials:

\displaystyle(c)_{N}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-N,\,b\atop-c+1-N}\right|z\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N}(b+c)_{N}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-N,\,b\,\atop b+c}\right|1-z\right)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=(b+c)_{N}\,(-z)^{N}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-N,\,c\,\atop b+c}\right|1-\frac{1}{z}\,\right)

(2.4)

\displaystyle=(c)_{N}\,(1-z)^{N}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-N,\,e\atop-c+1-N}\right|\frac{z}{z-1}\right)

(2.5)

\displaystyle=(b)_{N}\,(z-1)^{N}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-N,\,e\atop-b+1-N}\right|\frac{1}{1-z}\right)\!.

(2.6)

where
e=-b-c+1-N
.
The following expression defines a polynomial of degree
\lfloor N/2\rfloor

for any integer
N\geqslant 0
:

{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-\frac{N}{2},-\frac{N-1}{2}\atop c}\right|z\right)

(2.7)

If
c
is a non-negative integer, but
a
or
b
is a non-negative integer
\geqslant c
,
the hypergeometric summation (
2.1
)
can be considered as a polynomial, of degree
-a
or
-b
.
This interpretation is often used in theory of orthogonal polynomials
[
KLS10
]
.
We adopt this interpretation of hypergeometric functions like

{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-n,\,b\atop-n-m}\right|z\right)

(2.8)

with integer
n,m\geqslant 0
.
The well-known identities

\displaystyle{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{a,\,b\,\atop c}\right|\,z\right)=

\displaystyle\,(1-z)^{-a}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{a,\,c-b\,\atop c}\right|\,\frac{z}{z-1}\right)

(2.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(1-z)^{c-a-b}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{c-a,\,c-b\,\atop c}\right|\,z\right)

(2.10)

of Pfaff and Euler
[
AAR99
, Th. 2.2.5]
should not be automatically applied then; see
[
Vid07
, Lemma 3.1]
.
Yet these identities are valid when all three numbers
a,b,c
are non-negative integers,
and
-c\geqslant\max(-a,-b)
; see
[
Vid07
, §9]
.

2.2
Distinctive roots of hypergeometric polynomials

Distinctiveness of roots of hypergeometric polynomials is easily proved by
using
contiguous relations

[
AAR99
, §2.5, §3.7]
of
{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}
-functions.
In particular, we use the observations in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider the sequence of hypergeometric polynomials

P(k)={}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-k,\,b\atop 1-k-c}\right|z\right),

(2.11)

with some
b,c\in\mathbb{C}
.
Suppose that for a positive integer
k
we have
(b)_{k}\neq 0
,
(c)_{k}\neq 0
, and
(b+c)_{k}\neq 0
.
Then:

(
i
)

The polynomials
P(k)
and
P(k-1)
do not have common roots.

(
ii
)

The polynomial
P(k)
has
k
distinct roots.

(
iii
)

z=1
is not a root of
P(k)
.

Proof.

The sequence
P(k)
satisfies the recurrence relation

\frac{k(k-1+b+c)z}{k-1+c}\,P(k-1)=(kz+k+bz+c)\,P(k)-(k+c)\,P(k+1).\

(2.12)

This follows from the contiguous relations
[
Vid03
]
; or by applying Zeilberger’s algorithm
[
AAR99
, §3.11]
; or by checking the series expansion.
We apply the recurrence with decreasing
k
, and use the assumption that
(b+c)_{k}\neq 0
.
The recurrence implies that a common root of
P(k)
and
P(k-1)
is either
z=0
,
which conflicts with the hypergeometric values
1
at
z=0
,
or it would be a common root of
P(k-2),\ldots
, and of
P(0)=1
contradictorily.

The second statement follows from the degree of
k
of
P(k)
thanks to
(b)_{k}\neq 0
,

and the fact that another contiguous relation

\displaystyle\frac{1-z}{k}\,\frac{dP(k)}{dz}+P(k)=\frac{k-1+b+c}{k-1+c}\,P(k-1)

(2.13)

implies that a multiple root of
P(k)
would be also a root of
P(k-1)
, leading to the established first statement.

The last statement follows from the Chu-Vandermonde identity
[
AAR99
, p. 67]

{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-k,B\atop C}\right|1\right)=\frac{(C-B)_{k}}{(C)_{k}},

(2.14)

giving the value
(b+c)_{k}/(c)_{k}
for
P(k)
evaluated at
z=1
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider the sequence of hypergeometric polynomials

P(k)={}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-\frac{k}{2},\,-\frac{k-1}{2}\atop 1-k-c}\right|z\right),

(2.15)

with some
c\in\mathbb{C}
.
Suppose that for a positive integer
k
we have
(c)_{k}\neq 0
and
(2c)_{k}\neq 0
.
Then:

(
i
)

The polynomials
P(k)
and
P(k-1)
do not have common roots.

(
ii
)

The polynomial
P(k)
has
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
distinct roots.

(
iii
)

z=1
is not a root of
P(k)
.

Proof.

The sequence
P(k)
satisfies the recurrence relation

P(k)-P(k+1)=\frac{kz\,(2c+k-1)}{4(c+k)(c+k-1)}\,P(k-1).

(2.16)

Under the assumption that
(b+c)_{k}\neq 0
, the recurrence shows that
a common root of
P(k)
and
P(k+1)
is either the rejectable
z=0
, or it would be a common root of
P(k-1)
,
P(k-2),\ldots
, and of
P(0)=1
contradictorily.

The second statement follows from the degree of
k
of
c_{k}
, thanks to
(a)_{k}\neq 0
,

and the fact that another contiguous relation

\displaystyle\frac{2(1-z)}{k}\frac{dP(k)}{dz}+P(k)=\frac{2c+k-1}{2(k+c-1)}\,P(k-1)

(2.17)

implies that a multiple root of
P(k)
would be also a root of
P(k+1)
,
leading to the established first statement.

The last statement follows from applying the Chu-Vandermonde identity (
2.14
).
The evaluation at
z=1
is

\left.\left({\textstyle c+\frac{1}{2}}\,\right)_{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\,\right/\left(c+\left\lceil{\textstyle\frac{k}{2}}\right\rceil\right)_{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}

(2.18)

for both even and odd
k
.
∎

Belyi maps of the form (
1.2
)

The form (
1.2
) of Belyi maps is the compacted case
q=p
of the form (
1.1
), where
(1-x)^{p}(1-\lambda x)^{p}
is replaced by the aggregate power
(1+\alpha x+\beta x^{2})^{p}
.
This grouping of points with the same branching order is routinely used to simplify maximally
the
field of definition
of Belyi maps
[
LZ04
]
.
Similarly, here we are not interested in the case
r=p
.
These maps correspond to the cases
(1-x^{m+2})^{p}
of Remark
5.5
,
with a quadratic factor of
1-x^{m+2}
distinguished.

Let us denote
H_{2}(x)=1+\alpha x+\beta x^{2}
.
The condition (
1.3
) translates into the power series relation

G_{m}(x)=H_{2}(x)^{-p/r}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{mod }x^{m+2}.

(7.1)

The power series term with
x^{m+1}
has to equal
0
.
The polynomial
G_{m}
is determined uniquely by the power series

H_{2}(x)^{-p/r}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}x^{k}

(7.2)

truncated at the
(m+1)
-the term. The power series of can be computed by expanding
(-\alpha-\beta x)^{j}
in

\displaystyle H_{2}(x)^{-p/r}=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\,\frac{1}{j!}\,\Big(\,\frac{p}{r}\,\Big)_{\!j}\,(-\alpha-\beta x)^{j}x^{j}.

(7.3)

Explicitly,

g_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{k-j}(p/r)_{k-j}\,\alpha^{k-2j}\beta^{j}}{(k-2j)!\,j!}.

(7.4)

If
(p/r)_{k}\neq 0
and
\alpha\neq 0
, we have the hypergeometric expression

g_{k}=\frac{(-\alpha)^{k}}{k!}\,\Big(\,\frac{p}{r}\,\Big)_{\!k}\;{}_{2}\mbox{\rm F}_{\!1}\!\left(\left.{-\frac{k}{2},-\frac{k-1}{2}\atop 1-k-\frac{p}{r}}\right|\frac{4\beta}{\alpha^{2}}\right).

(7.5)

Distinct Belyi maps correspond to the roots of
g_{m+1}(\alpha\!:\!\beta)
,
identified up to the
(1\!:\!2)
-weighted homogeneous action of scaling
x
.
There is a Belyi map with
\alpha=0
when
m
is even.
It is obtained from Example
5.3
after the substitution
x\mapsto x^{2}
,
q=r

(with
q,m
over there equal to the current
r,m/2
).

The following degeneratated cases are encountered:

•

There are no Belyi maps when
p=-\ell r
for a positive integer
\ell\leqslant\lfloor m/2\rfloor
,

because then

, and possibly of lesser degree than
m
.

•

If
p=-\ell r
for a positive integer satisfying
\lfloor m/2\rfloor<\ell\leqslant m
,

then the first
m+1-\ell
terms for the sum (
7.4
) for
g_{m+1}
are zero,
and

has the degenerate root
\beta=0
of that multiplicity.
After shifting the summation index by
k-\ell

(7.6)

There are

proper Belyi maps then, including the

case for even

.

•

If

for a positive odd integer

(7.7)

by Euler’s formula (

). The root

The transformed hypergeometric sum can be identified as

Belyi maps, including the

.
In particular, there are no Belyi maps when the odd

and there is only the map with

when the odd

Otherwise, that is when
