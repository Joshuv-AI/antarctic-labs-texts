Abstract
Markov decision processes (MDPs) provide a fundamental model for sequential decision making under process uncertainty. A classical synthesis task is to compute for a given MDP a winning policy that achieves a desired specification. However, at design time, one typically needs to consider a family of MDPs modelling various system variations. For a given family, we study synthesising (1) the subset of MDPs where a winning policy exists and (2) a preferably small number of winning policies that together cover this subset. We introduce policy trees that concisely capture the synthesis result. The key ingredient for synthesising policy trees is a recursive application of a game-based abstraction. We combine this abstraction with an efficient refinement procedure and a post-processing step. An extensive empirical evaluation demonstrates superior scalability of our approach compared to naive baselines. For one of the benchmarks, we find 246 winning policies covering 94 million MDPs. Our algorithm requires less than 30 minutes, whereas the naive baseline only covers 3.7% of MDPs in 24 hours.

Introduction

Markov decision processes (MDPs) are the ubiquitous model to describe sequential decision making uncertainty: the outcomes of nondeterministic actions are determined by a probability distribution over the successor states. MDP policies resolve the non-determinism and describe for each state which action to take. A classical synthesis task in MDPs is to compute policies that achieve reachability specifications: Given a set of target states and a threshold
\lambda

winning policy

\lambda

In this paper, we generalise the synthesis task: We consider not one, but a set of MDPs. The MDPs model variations of the same system, this is a natural approach to capture alternative environment conditions for an agent

[

[

. In such scenarios, even huge sets of MDPs can be concisely represented.
The goal is to compute a (potentially different) winning policy for every MDP, if possible. Naively, this problem is straightforward to solve using either of two baselines: Either we

A game-based approach.

To find a (concise) expression capturing all MDPs that allow for a winning policy,
we propose an adaptation of a game-based abstraction approach
[
27
,
36
]
that has been successful in the verification of sets of Markov chains
[
3
]
and for parametric MDPs
[
33
]
. We discuss differences in Section
6
.
In a nutshell, the game-based approach operates as follows: from the concise representation of the set of MDPs, a stochastic game (SG) is constructed that mimics all MDPs at once. One player selects actions in the MDP, and the other player selects in which MDP to evaluate the action. The constructed game is an abstraction, as the player choosing the MDPs can switch between MDPs based on the current state of the system.
If the SG satisfies a reachability property, then there is a winning policy for every MDP. In fact, there must be a robust winning policy, and this robust policy is easy to obtain.
The reverse does not hold. Thus, we either find a robust policy or obtain an inconclusive result. In the latter case, we divide-and-conquer: We split the set of MDPs and recursively invoke our algorithm on the smaller sets. The abstraction is effective when the number of necessary splits remains limited. Importantly, the size of the resulting tree corresponds to the number of splits.

Practical efficacy.

An abstraction-refinement approach must be paired with efficient heuristics for refinement. We carefully study several variations that are guided by the model checking results. Furthermore, once we have found robust policies for various subfamilies, they are a good basis for further pruning of the tree, thereby yielding smaller trees with negligible overhead.

Alternatives.

We studied a rich set of alternatives to the main approach we present in the paper.
Most prominently, to ensure that our game-based abstraction is not overly conservative, we compute policies that work well against a (uniform) distribution and then only verify whether they are robust. Our experiments show that the game-based abstraction dominates such an alternative.

Contributions and structure.

In summary, we contribute a scalable approach to policy synthesis for sets of MDPs. The key technique is a game-based abstraction (Sec.
3
), embedded in a recursive approach with context-specific ways to refine the abstraction (Sec.
4
). The result is an algorithm that finds policies for millions of (similar) MDPs at once and represents the necessary policies compactly. Our extensive evaluation demonstrates (Sec.
5
) the superior scalability and discusses strengths and weaknesses, along with an ablation study to understand the origins of the performance. We extensively discuss further related work in Sec.
6
.

We aim to compute a policy map for a family of MDPs, assuming compact input and output representations, which we formalise below.

\textstyle\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[G\models\Diamond T\right]\coloneqq\sup_{\sigma_{1}\in\Sigma^{M}_{1}}\inf_{\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma^{M}_{2}}\mathbb{P}\left[G^{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\models\Diamond T\right].

SGs with reachability objectives are determined and thus:

\textstyle\sup_{\sigma_{1}\in\Sigma^{M}_{1}}\inf_{\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma^{M}_{2}}\mathbb{P}\left[G^{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\models\Diamond T\right]=\inf_{\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma^{M}_{2}}\sup_{\sigma_{1}\in\Sigma^{M}_{1}}\mathbb{P}\left[G^{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\models\Diamond T\right].

It has also been shown that for these objectives, optimal policies for both players exist, and memoryless deterministic
policies suffice. In particular, the probability
\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[G\models\Diamond T\right]
is achieved by policy
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
where
\sigma_{1}
is the policy of Player 1 and
\sigma_{2}
is the policy of Player 2. No polynomial-time algorithm is known for reachability objectives. However, the worst-case exponential-time algorithms based on value/policy iteration work reasonably well on many practical problems
[
39
]
and are available in tools like Prism-Games
[
40
]
and Storm
[
29
]
.

3.2
Quotient Game Abstraction

We now define the SG abstraction of
\mathcal{M}
, allowing us to effectively reason about the winning policies in
\mathcal{M}
. Without loss of generality, assume a threshold
\geq\lambda
. Intuitively, the game abstraction of
\mathcal{M}
is a two-player turn-based stochastic game where Player 1 in state
s
chooses one of the actions
\alpha\in Act(s)
and the Player 2 subsequently chooses one of the MDPs
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
in which
\alpha
will be executed.

Definition 8
(Game abstraction)

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a family of MDPs. The
game abstraction
for
\mathcal{M}
is a stochastic game
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}=(M,S_{1},S_{2})
with
S_{1}=S_{\mathcal{M}}
,
S_{2}=S_{\mathcal{M}}{\times}Act_{\mathcal{M}}
and an underlying MDP

where

Act_{\mathcal{G}}=Act_{\mathcal{M}}\cup 2^{\mathcal{I}}
.
For each
s\in S_{1}
,
Act_{\mathcal{G}}(s)=Act_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
and
\forall\alpha\in Act(s)\colon P_{\mathcal{G}}(s,\alpha,(s,\alpha))=1
;
For each
(s,\alpha)\in S_{2}
,
Act_{\mathcal{G}}(s,\alpha)=\mathcal{I}/\!\,\begin{subarray}{c}s,\alpha\\
\mathrel{\scalebox{1.5}[1]{$\sim$}}\end{subarray}\,
and
\forall I\in\mathcal{I}/\!\,\begin{subarray}{c}s,\alpha\\
\mathrel{\scalebox{1.5}[1]{$\sim$}}\end{subarray}\,:P_{\mathcal{G}}((s,\alpha),I)=P_{i}(s,\alpha)
where
i\in I
.

We omit the subscript from
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}
whenever family
\mathcal{M}
is clear from the context.
We say that Player 2 policy
\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma^{M}_{2}
is

i

iff
\exists i\in\mathcal{I}\,\forall s\in S_{2}\colon i\in\sigma_{2}(s)

M_{i}
.
We say that the game policy is consistent iff its Player 2 policy is consistent.

\text{$\sigma_{2}$ is consistent in $i$}\quad\text{implies}\quad\text{$\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[M_{i}\models\Diamond T\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[M_{i}^{\sigma_{1}}\models\Diamond T\right]$}.

Lemma

Theorem 3.1
(Policy from winning game)

Consider a family
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}

The game abstraction is a proper abstraction

and thus
\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[\mathcal{G}\models\Diamond T\right]<\lambda
does not imply that an unsatisfiable MDP exists in the family, nor does it imply that the robust policy does not exist for
\mathcal{M}
. The reason is that Player 2 is too powerful as they are not bound to play consistently and can adapt their selection of an MDP based on the state-action selection of Player 1. In our illustrative example, this means that the chair can be placed based on the selected route of the agent.

However, if
\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[\mathcal{G}\models\Diamond T\right]<\lambda
under the optimal policies

\sigma_{2}

\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[M_{i}\models\Diamond T\right]<\lambda

Theorem

Remark 1

Consider a relaxed version of our problem statement that aims to synthesise a Boolean map
\mathcal{B}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\{\top,\varnothing\}

Remark 2

We designed the game abstraction such that Player 1, choosing actions, chooses before Player 2, choosing identifiers, which leads to the use of auxiliary Player 2 states consisting of state-action pairs.
This is the action-identifier (AI) ordering. There naturally also exits the identifier-action (IA) ordering where Player 2 chooses identifiers

(s,I)

In general, the maximisation in the IA ordering can lead to a higher (i.e. better) probability than the AI ordering – the maximiser (Player 1) can have a better reaction after the Player 2 action selection. However, the IA ordering requires, for each state
s\in S

|\mathcal{M}|
auxiliary states, which is typically significantly more compared to the AI ordering that requires only up to
Act(s)
states. Thus, we use the AI ordering

Game-based abstraction for MDPs
[
27

36
]
prefer the IA ordering as it is tighter.

4
Recursive Abstraction-Refinement for Policy Trees

We first propose a divide-and-conquer algorithm for the policy tree synthesis that builds on the game abstraction and briefly discuss alternative sub-routines for the synthesis algorithm. Further, we propose several splitting strategies that drive the abstraction refinement. Finally, we introduce an essential post-processing routine that reduces the size of the police tree.

4.2
Alternative Strategies for Finding Robust Policies

As already discussed in Sec.
3.2
, in our game abstraction, Player 2 is very powerful since they can adapt their selection of an MDP during the game. In the experimental section, we show that in some models this hinders the performance of the synthesis algorithm. We also consider a different abstraction where Player 2 is replaced by the randomised selection of identifiers
I
, i.e. by the policy
\sigma_{\textrm{rand}}
that in every state
(s,\alpha)\in S_{2}
uniformly selects one of the identifier classes
I\in\mathcal{I}/\!\,\begin{subarray}{c}s,\alpha\\
\mathrel{\scalebox{1.5}[1]{$\sim$}}\end{subarray}\,
. In the game
\mathcal{G}
,
\sigma_{\textrm{rand}}
thus induces an MDP
\mathcal{G}^{\sigma_{\textrm{rand}}}
. The maximising policy
\sigma_{\max}
for
\mathcal{G}^{\sigma_{rnd}}
indeed does not provide any guarantees on the existence or non-existence of the robust policy. Thus we need also a
\textsc{testSat}(\mathcal{M},\sigma_{\max}
) sub-routine that checks whether
\sigma_{\max}
is robust. On the other hand, model checking
\mathcal{G}^{\sigma_{rnd}}
is computationally less demanding than solving the game
\mathcal{G}
, and the randomisation ensures that
\sigma_{\max}
is aware of all possible MDPs
M_{i}
.

Implementing
testSat
using an enumeration of all
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
is not tractable. We instead use a more sophisticated approach leveraging the recent work on the inductive synthesis of MCs
[
1
]
. The candidate policy
\sigma_{\max}
induces in the family
\mathcal{M}
a family of MCs. The inductive synthesis methods based on abstraction refinement and counter-example generalisation enable an efficient analysis of this family of MCs. In particular, we can find MCs satisfying the property that corresponds to the MDPs where
\sigma_{\max}
is winning.
Our preliminary experiments suggested that running MC synthesis loop within each iteration is too expensive.
Instead, we run MC synthesis in a lightweight but incomplete fashion:
\sigma_{\max}
is accepted only if
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{Q}^{\sigma_{\max}}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\lambda
.

4.3
Splitting strategies

We design two splitting strategies.
Pessimistic
splitting is an adoption of an approach for families of MCs
[
16
]
and is particularly suitable for splitting families containing both satisfiable and unsatisfiable MDPs.
Optimistic
splitting is geared towards increasing the likelihood that we find a robust policy.
To present both approaches, we will need the following definitions.
Given MDP
M
and a policy
\sigma\in\Sigma^{M}
, the
reachable fragment (under
\sigma
)
is a set
\mathsf{Reach}(M,\sigma)
of states reachable in
M^{\sigma}
from the initial state
s_{0}
. Given quotient MDP
\left(\mathcal{Q},\Gamma\right)
, a policy
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}
is
consistent in identifier
i
on the reachable fragment
iff
\forall s\in\mathsf{Reach}(\mathcal{Q},\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}})\colon i\in\Gamma(\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}(s))
. Let
\mathsf{ConsIDs}(\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}})\subseteq\mathcal{I}
be the set of all identifiers in which
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
is consistent on the reachable fragment.
To
split
the input index set
\mathcal{I}
is to partition it into
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}\coloneqq\mathcal{I}{\setminus}\mathcal{I}_{1}
; let
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}
denote the corresponding partition of the family
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
of MDPs.

Pessimistic splitting.

The goal of the pessimistic splitting is to partition the family
\mathcal{M}
into
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}
s.t. at least one of the corresponding quotient MDPs
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}
.
To achieve this, we inspect the policy
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
, computed in

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}
, and compute the set
\mathsf{ConsIDs}(\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}})
.
If
\mathsf{ConsIDs}(\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}){\neq}\emptyset

\mathcal{I}_{1}{=}\mathsf{ConsIDs}(\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}})
. This will guarantee that policy
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
is
robust
for
\mathcal{M}_{1}

11

11

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}}
is the same MC as

\overline{\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}}(s)=\alpha

Otherwise, if

The name

When we use the

Optimistic splitting.

Optimistic splitting executes the same reasoning as above, but, instead of MDP policy

4.4
Post-Processing the Policy Tree

We propose a post-processing step that reduces the size of the policy tree based on similarities between policies generated for particular leaf nodes. We say that policies
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}
associated with families
\mathcal{M}_{i},\mathcal{M}_{j}
are
compatible
if they agree on their choices in states that are in both reachable fragments:
\forall{s\in S}\colon

\left(s\in\mathsf{Reach}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}},\sigma_{i})\right)

\land\left(s\in\mathsf{Reach}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}},\sigma_{j})\right)\Rightarrow\sigma_{i}(s){=}\sigma_{j}(s)
.
We also introduce the following policy merging operation:
\sigma_{i+j}(s)=\sigma_{i}(s)
if
s\in\mathsf{Reach}(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}},\sigma_{i})
, and
\sigma_{i+j}(s)=\sigma_{j}(s)
otherwise.
Note that if policies
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{j}
are compatible, then
\sigma_{i+j}
and
\sigma_{j+i}
differ only in states that are unreachable in both
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}}^{\sigma_{i}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}^{\sigma_{j}}
.
The proposed post-processing algorithm executes the following three steps.

Step 1.

For each pair of sibling nodes associated with families
\mathcal{M}_{l},\mathcal{M}_{r}
and robust policies
\sigma_{l},\sigma_{r}
, we attempt to verify whether
\sigma_{l}
is a suitable policy for
\mathcal{M}_{r}
by constructing
\sigma_{l+r}
and checking whether
\mathbb{P}_{\min}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}_{r}}^{\sigma_{l+r}}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\lambda
, i.e. if
\sigma_{l+r}
is robust for
\mathcal{M}_{r}
(see Lemma
1
).
If this policy is robust for
\mathcal{M}_{r}
, we label both nodes with policy
\sigma_{l+r}
. Otherwise, we try to verify whether
\sigma_{r+l}
works for
\mathcal{M}_{l}
.

Step 2.

We inspect each pair
n_{i},n_{j}
of leaf nodes. If they are labelled with compatible policies
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}
, then
the policy
\sigma_{i+j}
must be winning for both
\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
\mathcal{M}_{j}
and we label both nodes with
\sigma_{i+j}
.

Step 3.

If for any inner node
n
it holds that
L(l(n))=L(r(n))
, that is, both its children are labelled as unsatisfiable or both labelled with the same policy, we remove nodes
l(n),r(n)
and set
L(n)=L(l(n))
.

Remark 3

A final step would be to convert the resulting policy tree to a directed acyclic graph by merging
all
nodes labelled with the same policy and
all
nodes associated with
\varnothing
. In our experimental evaluation, we avoid this step because the number of leaves in the resulting policy tree gives us a useful measure of how the size of the tree relates to the size of the family of MDPs.

Q1 Efficacy and scalability

Tab.
1
reports the experimental results. We first report the model information discussed above. For our approach, including post-processing, we report four columns, where the first two concern the computed trees and the latter two the algorithm performance. In particular, they display:
(1) The ratio L/
\mathcal{M}
between the number of the tree leaves and the family size
|\mathcal{M}|
.
(2) The ratio P/SAT between the number of leaves containing a policy and the number of satisfiable MDPs.
(3) The ratio I/
\mathcal{M}
between the total number of game & MDP model checking calls required during the tree construction (from here on: the number of
iterations
)
and
|\mathcal{M}|
.
(4) The overall runtime.
Finally, we provide the speedup obtained with respect to the baseline algorithms.

A small number of iterations.

On the majority of benchmarks, our approach requires only a fraction (
{<}
2%) of iterations compared to the total number of MDPs, even for families containing millions of members.
The use of the game-based abstraction supplemented by the smart splitting heuristics allows our abstraction refinement approach to use (relatively) few splittings to obtain structurally similar sub-families.
First, this allows our approach to identify sub-families of unsatisfiable MDPs that are resolved by
testUnsat
.
Second, given a family of satisfiable MDPs, the splitting strategy also helps the game abstraction to find a few robust policies that together cover this family.

A challenging benchmark.

The
rocks-6-4
model is the only benchmark where the ratio of iterations exceeds 100%. In this benchmark, the robot must deliver coffee to four desks in a room without obstacles. The location of the desks is parameterised. While there exists a single policy that is winning for all MDPs – a policy that visits each cell of the room – the proposed game abstraction cannot find it:
as long as the (sub-)family
\mathcal{M}
contains at least two MDPs, in the corresponding game abstraction, Player 2 may put the desk out of the way of the agent. This model clearly demonstrates the principal limitation of the game abstraction. A similar limitation can also be observed in the
virus
model.

Comparison with the baselines.

Comparing the proposed method to the one-by-one enumeration, we observe speedups of orders of magnitude for many benchmarks. Typically, the acceleration increases when increasing the family size, see differences in the variants of
dodge
,
dpm
and
obstacles
benchmarks. This is given by the fact that the ability of the game abstraction to reason about larger families is naturally more beneficial in the larger variants of these models. The largest speedup is observed on
obs-10-6
model: the proposed method constructs a solution in under 5 minutes, whereas one-by-one enumeration would take at least 3 days. On the other hand, on the models where the abstraction is too coarse and splitting does not help (see above), the one-by-one enumeration outperforms our approach. In some models, the symbolic representation in the all-in-one baseline can capture similarities across the individual MDPs effectively; For such models, the speedup achieved by our approach is reduced compared to the one-by-one enumeration. However, for a large set of models, the symbolic method runs out of available memory when constructing the symbolic representation itself.

Q2: Size of the policy trees

The size of the tree is directly related to the number of iterations the algorithm performs. Thus, we observe that
for the majority of benchmarks, the constructed tree contains fewer leaves than 1% of the total number of MDPs. This means that, on average, each leaf of the policy tree describes hundreds of MDPs for which either a robust policy was found, or no winning policy exists.
This ratio drops below 0.001% for e.g.
dodge-3
benchmark, where we find 246 policies that satisfy 94 million MDPs.

Post-processing.

The proposed post-processing routine plays a prominent role in providing compact policy trees.
Tab.
2
presents an empirical evaluation of the proposed policy tree post-processing algorithm on all of the considered benchmarks. It reports the number of nodes, leaves and policies before and after the post-processing step as well as the total time post-processing takes in seconds and the portion of time relative to the total synthesis time. The post-processing indeed does not guarantee that the minimal number of policies is found (e.g. despite the threefold reduction for the
rocks-6-4
model, we are still very far from the single policy that covers the entire family). On the other hand, for the majority of the models, it significantly reduces the size of the tree as well as the number of policies while introducing only a reasonable overhead – typically only a few percent of the total synthesis time. In the
rover-100
model, it achieves the largest reduction in the number of policies: from 38k to 69 with a 7% overhead.

Related Work

Synthesis of robust policies as well as the verification of sets of Markov models are broad topics. This paper brings them together using a game-based approach. Below, we discuss these (overlapping) areas as well as closely related aspects such as permissive policies and compact policy representation.

Game-based abstraction.

Game-based abstraction was primarily explored towards the verification of very large or infinite MDPs
[
27
,
36
]
. In that setting, Player 1 vertices correspond to abstract states that partition the state space of the original MDP: Player 1 thus chooses a concrete state and Player 2 chooses an available action from this state. The literature focuses on extracting predicates
allowing for a good partitioning. Recall that we use a different game construction, allowing us to reason about robust policies for a set of MDPs.
Game-based abstraction on POMDPs
[
58
]
is conceptually close to our game. However, for POMDPs, the abstraction cannot use the specific structure of our problem, and for refinement towards a robust policy, it can (and must) add memory to the policies, whereas we aim to find a concise policy tree via divide-and-conquer.

Families of MCs.

Feature-based models are popular to describe software product lines
[
20
,
42
]
and are conceptually similar to our quotient MDPs.
Closest is work on abstraction-refinement on the quotient MDP to partition a family of MCs based on a specification
[
16
,
1
]
and alternative approaches that prune these families based on counterexamples obtained on a single model
[
53
]
. Neither supports nondeterminism, thus no policies can be obtained.
The closest all-in-one approach is presented in
[
19
]
and also discusses how to represent the optimal value for every MDP. Symbolic all-in-one approaches profit from variable reordering
[
23
]
.

Parametric MDPs.

Parametric Markov models use constant but unknown transition probabilities. Families can be seen as a special case, a precise relation is discussed in
[
32
, Ch. 6]
. Algorithms for parametric MDPs, however, require that the parameter choices do not change the underlying graph. Under that assumption, both formalisms are orthogonal.
For parametric MDPs, a game-based approach is known as
parameter lifting
technique
[
33
]
. It creates an SG abstraction, but the policies themselves are not extracted there, and no refinement techniques are studied. There is limited work on robust memoryless policies in pMDPs
[
57
]
: They are complexity-wise harder to obtain than in our setting, unless so-called rectangularity is assumed, which leads to variations of
interval MDPs

[
26
,
44
,
30
,
45
]
. In that context, a game-based view on robustness is popular
[
30
,
56
]
.
The existence of small policy sets over pMDPs has been studied (theoretically) in
[
57
]
and empirically as a by-product of a verification routine
[
28
]
.

Robust policies in hidden-model MDPs.

Finding one policy for a set of MDPs has been studied in the context of sets of MDPs (under different names and with different assumptions).
Algorithmically, a robust policy can then be found by mapping this to POMDP synthesis. For MDPs with parametric transition probabilities, this has been studied in
[
4
]
(supporting up to 10 MDPs or 300 states).
The work in
[
18
]
considers specializations of POMDP planners (both offline and online) suitable for discounted expected rewards. The experiments cover benchmarks with tens of MDPs. The work in
[
17
]
considers a specialization of point-based solvers with a particular interest in obtaining compactly represented robust policies.
Qualitative properties are studied in
[
47
,
54
]
(by considering policies with memory). Alternatively, assuming a prior on the set of MDPs, one can optimize the expected performance
[
13
,
51
]
. Finally, as an alternative, PAC-robustness over a distribution over sets of MDPs has been studied
[
8
]
.

Data-driven robust policies.

In the setting where the transition and reward functions are unknown, reinforcement learning (RL) is applicable. Robustness in RL is studied in its relation to
generalisation
, see
[
7
,
37
]
. The field of curriculum learning
[
43
]
considers learning policies for different but related tasks, motivated to ease the learning of complex tasks.

Permissive policies and shielding.

An alternative approach towards finding robust memoryless policies is to compute all (memoryless) winning policies for every MDP and to construct their intersection. The sets of policies can be captured via permissive policies that allow for a nondeterministic action choice
[
22
]
and are relevant in the context of shielding for MDPs
[
38
,
34
]
. However, preliminary results in this direction confirmed that the computational overhead of computing permissive policies on individual MDPs is prohibitive.

Compact policies.

To compactly represent individual policies, decision trees are a popular target, either via a direct search
[
55
]
or as post-processing
[
5
]
. Concise representations for policies with memory can be synthesised with techniques akin to this paper
[
2
]
or as a post-processing step
[
11
]
.
Finally, such procedures can also be realised by first learning a (recurrent) neural network and then extracting an automaton from this net
[
15
]
.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented a divide-and-conquer algorithm enabling the construction of policy trees that compactly represent winning policies in large families of MDPs. The algorithm leverages a novel form of a game abstraction supplemented by smart abstraction-refinement strategies. Our extensive experimental evaluation demonstrates superior scalability compared to existing baselines as well
as novel alternatives. For future work, we are interested in i) further investigating strategies to search for robust policies, potentially incorporating richer feedback from model checking subroutines similar to inductive synthesis approaches
[
1
]
and ii) combining a compact representation of policies (e.g. using decision diagrams) with the compact representation of the policy trees.

Our approach assumes that there exists a compact representation of the family
\mathcal{M}
.
In our implementation and the experimental evaluation, we consider families represented by MDP
sketches
. A sketch
[
50
]
is a syntactic template defining a high-level structure of an underlying operational model. It allows for a concise representation of a priori knowledge about the system under development. Sketches for finite-state probabilistic programs (i.e. finite-state MCs) written in the Prism guarded-command language
[
41
]
have been proposed in
[
53
]
. The sketch contains a finite number of
holes
representing the program’s open, i.e., undefined parts. A hole may be used in the guard or updates of a command similar to a constant and
may occur multiple times within one or multiple commands. Each hole is associated with a finite domain representing the possible options in which the hole can be filled. In this work, we extend the MC sketches to MDP sketches. Since the extension is straightforward, we omit the formal definition and rather illustrate MDP sketching by presenting the sketch for our running example in Fig.

Appendix 0.B
Proofs

This section provides proof of the Theorems and Lemmas presented in our paper.

Proof of Lemma
1

Assume
\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}\models\Diamond T\right]<\lambda
and that there exists MDP
M_{i}
s.t.
\exists\sigma\in\Sigma^{M}\ \colon\mathbb{P}\left[M_{i}^{\sigma}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\lambda
.
Construct policy
\overline{\sigma}
as follows:
\overline{\sigma}(s)=(\sigma(s),I)
where
I\in\mathcal{I}/\!\,\begin{subarray}{c}s,\alpha\\
\mathrel{\scalebox{1.5}[1]{$\sim$}}\end{subarray}\,
s.t.
i\in I
and
\alpha=\sigma(s)
.
Note that
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\overline{\sigma}}
is the same MC as
M_{i}^{\sigma}
: in each state
s\in S_{\mathcal{M}}
of the quotient, policy
\overline{\sigma}
executes action
\sigma(s)
from MDP
M_{i}
.
Thus,
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\overline{\sigma}}\models\Diamond T\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[M_{i}^{\sigma}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\lambda
, i.e. policy
\overline{\sigma}
achieves in MDP
\mathcal{Q}
value larger than
\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}\models\Diamond T\right]
, which is a contradiction.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

If
\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\lambda
under the optimal policy
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
, then from the definition of
\mathbb{P}_{\max}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}\models\Diamond T\right]
we know that:

\forall\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{M}_{2}:\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\lambda.

This means that no matter what strategy Player 2 chooses, the value of the game abstraction will stay
\geq\lambda
. Assume arbitrary
i\in\mathcal{I}
and let
\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{M}_{2}
be a policy that is consistent in identifier
i
. From Lemma
2
it follows that:

\mathbb{P}\left[M_{i}^{\sigma_{1}}\models\Diamond T\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}^{\prime}}\models\Diamond T\right]\geq\lambda

and therefore
\sigma_{1}
is a robust policy for family
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1

First, we remark that if the subfamily is a singleton, there is either a winning policy for this MDP (which is then a robust policy for that subfamily), or the MDP is not winning, and thus all MDPs in the subfamily are unsatisfiable. The recursion must be finite, and the algorithm must terminate since we consider finite families only and
createSplit
returns non-trivial subsets. In fact, because of this, the recursion is linearly bounded by the number of MDPs in the family. The output is a valid policy tree following Theorem
3.1
(covering correctness of
findRobust
) and Lemma
1
(covering correctness of
testUnsat
).
