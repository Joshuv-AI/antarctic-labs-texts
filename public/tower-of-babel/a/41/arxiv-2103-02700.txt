Decoding Supercodes of Gabidulin Codes and Applications to Cryptanalysis
Maxime Bombar ( ✉ ), Alain Couvreur

Abstract
This article discusses the decoding of Gabidulin codes and shows how to extend the usual decoder to any supercode of a Gabidulin code at the cost of a significant decrease of the decoding radius. Using this decoder, we provide polynomial time attacks on the rank metric encryption schemes Ramesses and Liga .



Introduction

It is well–known that error correcting codes lie among the possible
candidates for post quantum cryptographic primitives. For codes
endowed with the Hamming metric the story begins in 1978 with
McEliece’s proposal
[
14
]
. The security of this scheme relies on
two difficult problems: the hardness of distinguishing classical Goppa
codes from arbitrary codes and the hardness of the syndrome decoding
problem. To instantiate McEliece scheme, the only requirement is to
have a family of codes whose structure can be hidden and benefiting
from an efficient decoder. In particular, this paradigm does not
require the use of codes endowed with the Hamming metric.
Hence other metrics may be considered such as the rank metric,
as proposed by Gabidulin, Paramonov and Tretjakov in
[
8
]
.

Besides McEliece’s paradigm, another approach to perform encryption
with error correcting codes consists in using codes whose structure is
no longer hidden but where encryption is performed so that decryption
without the knowledge of the secret key would require to decode the
public code far beyond the decoding radius. This principle has been
first instantiated in Hamming metric by Augot and Finiasz in

[
3
]
using Reed–Solomon codes. Later, a rank metric
counterpart is designed by Faure and Loidreau in
[
7
]
. Both
proposals have been subject to attacks, by Coron
[
6
]
for the
Hamming metric proposal and by Gaborit, Otmani and Talé–Kalachi

[
9
]
for the rank metric one. More recently, two independent
and different repairs of Faure–Loidreau scheme resisting to the
attack of Gaborit, Otmani and Talé–Kalachi appeared. The first one,
due to Renner, Puchinger and Wachter–Zeh and is called
Liga

[
19
,
16
]
. The second one, due to Lavauzelle, Loidreau and
Pham is called
Ramesses

[
12
]
.

Our contribution

In the present article, we show how to
extend the decoding of a Gabidulin code to a supercode at the cost of
a significant decrease of the decoding radius. With this decoder in hand
we perform a polynomial time message
recovery attack on
Ramesses
and
Liga
.

Notation and Prerequisites

In this article, we work over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and we
frequently consider two nested extensions denoted
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}
.
Rank metric codes will be subspaces
\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
,
the code length and dimension are respectively denoted as

n
and
k
.

Vectors are represented by lower case bold face letters such as

\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{e}
and matrices by upper case letters

\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{M}
. The space of
m\times n
matrices with entries in
a field
\mathbb{K}
is denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{K})
. When

m=n
we denote it by
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{K})
and the group of
non-singular matrices is denoted by
\textrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})
. Given a
matrix
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
its rank is denoted by

\mathbf{rank}_{q}(\boldsymbol{M})
. Similarly, given a vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
, the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–rank
or
rank
of
\boldsymbol{c}
is defined as the dimension of
the subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
spanned by the entries of
\boldsymbol{c}
. Namely,

\mathbf{rank}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\mathbf{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\}\right).

We will consider two notions of
support
in the rank
metric. Inspired by the Hamming metric, the most natural one which we
will denote by the
column support
of a vector
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}

is the linear subspace spanned by its coordinates:

\textrm{Supp}(\boldsymbol{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\}.

But we can define another notion, namely the
row
support
. Let
\mathscr{B}
be a basis of the extension field

\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then, we define the extension of
\boldsymbol{c}
with respect to

\mathscr{B}
as the matrix
\textrm{Ext}_{\mathscr{B}}(\boldsymbol{c})\in\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
whose
columns are the entries of
\boldsymbol{c}
represented in the basis
\mathscr{B}
. The
row space of
\textrm{Ext}_{\mathscr{B}}(\boldsymbol{c})
with respect to
\mathscr{B}
will be called the

row support
of
\boldsymbol{c}
,
i.e.

\textrm{RowSupp}(\boldsymbol{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\boldsymbol{x}\textrm{Ext}_{\mathscr{B}}(\boldsymbol{c})\mid\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}.

Notice that the above definition does not depend on the choice
of the basis
\mathscr{B}
.

Given
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
, and
j\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
,
we denote

{\boldsymbol{c}}^{[j]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}(c_{1}^{q^{j}},\dots,c_{n}^{q^{j}}).

Similarly, for a code
\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
, we denote

{\mathscr{C}}^{[j]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{c}}^{[j]}~\big|~\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C}\right\}.

Let
n\leqslant m
,
k\leqslant n
and
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
with
\mathbf{rank}_{q}(\boldsymbol{g})=n
,
the
Gabidulin code of dimension
k
supported by
\boldsymbol{g}
is defined as

\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{g}}^{[i]}~\big|~0\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\right\}.

A
q
–polynomial
is a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}[X]
whose monomials
are only
q
–th powers of
X
,
i.e.
a polynomial of the form

P(X)=p_{0}X+p_{1}X^{q}+\cdots+p_{r}X^{q^{r}}.

Assuming that
p_{r}\neq 0
then the integer
r
is called the
q
–degree
of
P
. Such a polynomial induces an

\mathbb{F}_{q}
–linear map
P:\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
and we call the
rank
of the
q
–polynomial, the rank of the induced
map.
A well–known fact on
q
–polynomials is that the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–dimension
of the kernel of the induced endomorphism is bounded from
above by their
q
-degree.
Conversely, any
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–linear endomorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is uniquely
represented by a
q
–polynomial of degree
<m
. Denote by

\mathcal{L}
the space of
q
–polynomials, this space equipped with the
composition law is a non commutative ring which is left and right
Euclidean
[
11
, § 1.6]
and the two–sided ideal

(X^{q^{m}}-X)
is the kernel of the canonical map

\mathcal{L}\longrightarrow\textrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})

inducing an isomorphism :

\mathcal{L}/(X^{q^{m}}-X)\simeq\textrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}},\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}).

Finally, given a positive integer
k<m
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{<k}

(resp.
\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant k}
) the space of
q
–polynomials of
q
–degree
less than (resp. less than or equal to)
k
.
The Gabidulin code
\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})
is canonically isomorphic to

\mathcal{L}_{<k}
under the isomorphism:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{L}_{<k}&\longrightarrow&\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})\\
P&\longmapsto&(P(g_{1}),\dots,P(g_{n})).\end{array}\right.

The above map is actually an isometry: it is rank preserving. In this
article, we will extensively use this isometry and Gabidulin codes
will be represented either as an evaluation code or as a space of

q
–polynomials of bounded degree
\mathcal{L}_{<k}
, when one
representation is more suitable than the other.

In particular, given two
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
–linear subspaces
\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}

of
\mathcal{L}/(X^{q^{m}}-X)
we define their
composition
as

\mathscr{A}\circ\mathscr{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\{P\circ Q~|~P\in\mathscr{A},\ Q\in\mathscr{B}\}.

This definition may be regarded as a rank metric analogue of
the so–called Schur product of codes in Hamming metric.

Another notion which is very useful in the sequel is the notion of
adjoint
of a class of
q
–polynomial
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}p_{i}X^{q^{i}}

in
\mathcal{L}/(X^{q^{m}}-X)
,
which is defined as

{P}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}X^{q^{m-i}}p_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}p_{i}^{q^{m-i}}X^{q^{m-i}}.

(1)

Regarding
P
as an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–linear endomorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
, the notion
of adjoint is nothing but the usual notion of
adjoint
or
transposed
endomorphism with respect to the inner product

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\times\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{F}_{q}\\
(x,y)&\longmapsto&{\rm Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(xy)\end{array}\right..

Details are given in a more general context in

[
2
, Section 4.2]
, we give them here in the context of

q
–polynomials for the sake of self–containedness.

With respect to this bilinear form, the multiplication map by
a scalar
x\mapsto ax
for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\times}
is
symmetric

(or
self-adjoint
) since,

\langle x,ay\rangle={\rm Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(xay)={\rm Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(axy)=\langle ax,y\rangle.

Next, the Frobenius endomorphism is
orthogonal
, that is to say
its adjoint
{(X^{q})}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}
is its inverse
X^{q^{m-1}}
because:

\langle x,y^{q}\rangle={\rm Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(xy^{q})={\rm Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left({(xy^{q})}^{q^{m-1}}\right)={\rm Tr}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(x^{q^{m-1}}y)=\langle x^{q^{m-1}},y\rangle.

Finally, using that adjunction is anticommutative,
i.e.
for any

\mathbb{F}_{q}
–endomorphisms
f,g
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
we have

{(fg)}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}={g}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}{f}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}
we can prove that
Definition
1
coincides with that of adjoint
endomorphism.
In particular, for any

P\in\mathcal{L}/(X^{q^{m}}-X)
, we have
\mathbf{rank}_{q}{(P)}=\mathbf{rank}_{q}{({P}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}})}
.

In a nutshell, our approach can be explained as follows. Starting from
the decoding problem (
6

adjunction operator we have to solve the problem

{Y}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}={C}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}+{E}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}},

\mathbf{rank}_{q}{{E}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}}=\mathbf{rank}_{q}{E}\leqslant\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\rfloor

and
{C}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}
is contained in a code which is equivalent to a Gabidulin
code. Hence,
{C}^{\raise 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vee$}}
can be recovered by applying the decoding
algorithm of
[
13
]

In what follows, we give a detailed and self–contained presentation
of how to apply and implement this algorithm practically. We believe that this algorithm might be folklore, but
we weren’t able to find it in the literature.

Starting from the decoding problem
Y=C+E
, the decoding problem can be
thought as finding the
q
–polynomial

analogy with Reed–Solomon codes, Loidreau introduced in

to the resolution of a linear system that can be efficiently solved

suffices to compute a left Euclidean division to recover

and
therefore the codeword
\boldsymbol{c}
.

The core of the algorithm to follow consists in searching a

4
Decoding Supercodes of Gabidulin Codes

A common feature of the cryptanalyses to follow can be understood
as the decoding of a supercode of a Gabidulin code.
Consider a code (represented as a subspace of
\mathcal{L}_{<m}
)

\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}_{<k}\oplus\mathscr{T},

where
\mathscr{T}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{<m}
, the code
\mathscr{C}
benefits from a
decoding algorithm in a similar manner to that of
[
13
]
.
Indeed, given a received word

Y=C+E

where
C\in\mathscr{C}
and
E\in\mathcal{L}_{<m}
with
\mathbf{rank}_{q}{E}=t
, one can
look for the left annihilator of
E
. Let
\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}
be the left annihilator of
E
. We have to solve

\Lambda\circ Y\equiv\Lambda\circ C\mod(X^{q^{m}}-X),

where the unknowns are
\Lambda,C
. Then, similarly to the decoding
of Gabidulin codes, one may linearise the system. For this sake, recall
that
C=C_{0}+T
for
C_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{<k}
and
T\in\mathscr{T}
.
Therefore, we are looking for the solutions of a system

\Lambda\circ Y\equiv N\mod(X^{q^{m}}-X)

(12)

where
N\in(\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathcal{L}_{<k})+\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T}=\mathcal{L}_{<k+t}+\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T}
.

Lemma 1

Under the assumption that

(\mathcal{L}_{<k+t}+\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T})\cap(\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ E)=\{0\}
, any nonzero solution
(\Lambda,N)
of the
system (
12
) satisfies

\Lambda\circ E=0
.

Proof

Let
(\Lambda,N)
be such a nonzero solution.
Then,

\Lambda\circ Y-\Lambda\circ C\equiv\Lambda\circ E\mod(X^{q^{m}}-X).

Since
\Lambda\circ Y\equiv N\mod(X^{q^{m}}-X)
, the left–hand
side is contained in
(\mathcal{L}_{<k+t}+\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T})

while the right–hand one is contained in
\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ E
.
Therefore, by assumption, both sides are zero. This yields the
result.

Under the hypotheses of Lemma
1
,
decoding can be performed as follows.

1.

Solve System (
12
).

2.

Take a nonzero solution
(\Lambda,N)
of the system. Compute
the right kernel of
\Lambda
. This kernel contains the image of
E

and hence the support of the error.

3.

Knowing the support of
E
, one can recover it by solving a linear
system. See for instance
[
10
, §3]
or
[
1
, §1.4]
.

Remark 3

Note that for the decoding of Gabidulin codes,
once a solution
(\Lambda,N)
is computed, one can recover

C
by left Euclidean division of
N
by
\Lambda
. In the present
situation, this calculation is no longer efficient. Indeed,
the proof of Lemma
1
permits
only to assert that
N\equiv\Lambda\circ C\mod(X^{q^{m}}-X)
.
In the Gabidulin case, the fact that
\deg_{q}C<k
permits
to assert that
\deg_{q}\Lambda\circ C<m
and hence that

N=\Lambda\circ C
. This is no longer true in our setting
since, there is
a priori
no upper bound on the

q
–degree of
C
. For this reason, we need to use the knowledge
of the support of the error to decode.

For the decoding to succeed, the condition

(\mathcal{L}_{<k+t}+\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T})\cap(\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ E)=\{0\}
needs to be satisfied. In the case of Gabidulin codes
(
i.e.

\mathscr{T}=\{0\}
), this is
guaranteed by a minimum distance argument entailing that

\mathcal{L}_{<k+t}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ E
is zero as soon as

t\leqslant\frac{n-k}{2}
. In our situation, estimating the minimum
distance of
\mathcal{L}_{<k+t}+\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T}
is
difficult. However, one can expect that in the typical case, the
intersection

(\mathcal{L}_{<k+t}+\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T})\cap(\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ E)
is
0
when the sums of the dimensions of the codes is
less than that of the ambient space. Therefore, one can reasonably
expect to correct almost any error of rank
t
as soon as

k+2t+\dim(\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t}\circ\mathscr{T})\leqslant n.

(13)

In the case
\mathscr{T}=\{0\}
, we find back the decoding radius of
Gabidulin codes.

Remark 4

Note that the previous approach applies
mutatis mutandis

to the decoding of supercodes of Reed–Solomon codes.

The right–hand side version.

In the spirit of
Section
3
, a similar approach using
right–hand side decoding shows that decoding is also possible when

k+2t+\dim(\mathscr{T}\circ\mathcal{L}_{\leqslant t})\leqslant n.

(14)

We will use this decoding algorithm to attack
Liga

and
Ramesses
. For
Liga
, the code
\mathscr{T}
is a random code of
low dimension, while for
Ramesses
,
\mathscr{T}
is a Gabidulin code.

5.2
A Message Recovery Attack Against
Liga
Cryptosystem

In this section, we show that it is possible to recover the plaintext from a ciphertext. Notice that
Liga
cryptosystem has been proven IND-CCA2 in
[
16
]
, under some computational assumption, namely the Restricted Gabidulin Code Decision Problem (
[
16
]
, Problem 4). We are not disproving this claim here, however our attack can be precisely used as a distinguisher, and hence this problem is not as hard as supposed.

Recall that
\boldsymbol{G}
is a generator matrix of a public Gabidulin code

\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})
, the public key is a noisy vector

\boldsymbol{k}_{pub}=\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{z}
and the encryption of a message

\boldsymbol{m}
is
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\alpha\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})+\boldsymbol{e}
for some
uniformly random element
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}
and a uniformly random error

\boldsymbol{e}
of small rank weight

t_{pub}=\lfloor\frac{n-k-w}{2}\rfloor
both chosen by Alice.
See Section
2.1
for further details.

The attack works in two parts. First, we introduce a supercode of the public Gabidulin code, in which we are able to decode the ciphertext and get rid of the small error. Then, we recover the plaintext.

Step 1: Get rid of the small error.

Let
\zeta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{rank}_{q^{m}}(\boldsymbol{z})
, so that

\boldsymbol{z}=\sum_{i=1}^{\zeta}\mu_{i}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
where the
\mu_{i}

\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}
and
the
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}

\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}
are both linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. The
ciphertext can now be written as

\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}+\sum_{i=1}^{\zeta}{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\alpha\mu_{i})\boldsymbol{z}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}

(16)

Let

\mathscr{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})+\mathbf{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\{\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{\zeta}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}.

(17)

The ciphertext can be seen as a
codeword of
\mathscr{C}
corrupted by a small rank weight error

\boldsymbol{e}
. Moreover,
\mathscr{C}
can be computed from public data as
suggested by the following statement.

Theorem 5.1

Let
\mathscr{C}
be the code defined in (

\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})

The proof of Theorem

Lemma 2

Let
F
be a linear subspace of dimension
m
in a linear space
E

of dimension

. Then,

\#\{G\mid F\oplus G=E\}=q^{m(n-m)}

Proof

Let
{\rm Stab}(F)
denote the stabiliser of
F
under the action of

\textrm{GL}(E)
. It is isomorphic to the group of the matrices of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}&\boldsymbol{B}\\
\large\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{C}\end{pmatrix}
with
\boldsymbol{A}\in\textrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
\boldsymbol{C}\in\textrm{GL}_{n-m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathcal{M}_{m,n-m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
i.e.

{\rm Stab}(F)\cong(\textrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\textrm{GL}_{n-m}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\ltimes\mathcal{M}_{m,n-m}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

This group acts transitively on the complement spaces of
F
. Indeed, let
G
and
G^{\prime}
be such that
F\oplus G=F\oplus G^{\prime}=E
. Let
(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
be a basis of
F
and
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n-m})
(respectively
(g_{1}^{\prime},\dots,g_{n-m}^{\prime})
) be a basis of
G
(resp.
G^{\prime}
). Then the linear map that stabilises
F
and maps
g_{i}
onto

is an element of
{\rm Stab}(F)
that maps
G
onto
G^{\prime}
. The stabiliser of a complement

under this action is simply
\textrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\textrm{GL}_{n-m}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).
Hence,

\#\{G\mid F\oplus G=E\}=\dfrac{(\#\textrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\times(\#\textrm{GL}_{n-m}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\times q^{m(n-m)}}{(\#\textrm{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))\times(\#\textrm{GL}_{n-m}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))}=q^{m(n-m)}.

Proof

We wish to estimate the probability that
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{C}_{\rm pub}
.
Note first that inclusion
\supseteq
is always satisfied.

Indeed, let

\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C}_{pub}
. Then, there exist
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k}

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\zeta}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{c}&=\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{G}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\zeta}\lambda_{i}{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})&\\

Therefore, the following equality of events holds:

(\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{C}_{\rm pub})=(\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathscr{C}_{\rm pub}),

and we are reduced to study the probability that

Let
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathscr{C}

exists
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{k}

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\zeta}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}

\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{G}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\zeta}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}.

If we can find

\mathbf{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\zeta})\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{\zeta}

\boldsymbol{c}-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\zeta}\alpha_{i}{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})\in\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})

\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{c}-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\zeta}\alpha_{i}{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})=&\\

\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma_{\zeta}\mu_{1})&\cdots&{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma_{\zeta}\mu_{\zeta})\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\boldsymbol{M}
denote this last matrix. The previous remark implies
\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathscr{C}_{pub})\geqslant\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{M}\text{ is non singular })

\boldsymbol{M}
is non singular with overwhelming probability over the choice of
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{\zeta}

Let

\Gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{Span}(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{\zeta})\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathscr{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{Span}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{\zeta}).

Then,
\boldsymbol{M}
is singular if and only if
\Gamma\cap\mathscr{M}^{\perp}\neq\{0\}

Since
\Gamma
and
\mathscr{M}
have the same dimension
\zeta
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}

\Gamma\cap\mathscr{M}^{\perp}=\{0\}
if and only if
\Gamma\oplus\mathscr{M}^{\perp}=\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{M}\text{ is non singular })=\dfrac{\#\{\Gamma\mid\mathscr{M}^{\perp}\oplus\Gamma=\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}\}}{\#\{\Gamma\mid\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}(\Gamma)=\zeta\}}\cdot

Recall the Gaussian binomial coefficient

\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\zeta\end{bmatrix}_{q^{m}}
denotes the number of

\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
–linear subspaces of dimension
\zeta
in an
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
–vector

2

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{M}\text{ is non singular})=\dfrac{q^{m\zeta(u-\zeta)}}{\begin{bmatrix}u\\

where the inequality on the right–hand side can be found

5.1
.

Set

\mathscr{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\zeta}\mathbf{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\left\{{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma_{i}\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})\right\}.

By interpolation, it can be regarded as a subspace of

Step 1 is summed up in Proposition

Proposition 4

If
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\alpha\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})+\boldsymbol{e}
is the encryption of a plaintext
\boldsymbol{m}
, then we can recover the support of the error
\boldsymbol{e}
and the corrupted codeword
\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\alpha\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})
in polynomial time using only the knowledge of the public key.

Step 2: Remove the
\boldsymbol{z}
dependency.

From now on, since we got rid of the small error term
\boldsymbol{e}
,
we can do as if the ciphertext was

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\alpha\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})

(19)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{m}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\alpha\boldsymbol{x}))\cdot\boldsymbol{G}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\alpha\boldsymbol{z}).

This is a codeword of a Gabidulin code
\mathscr{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})
, corrupted by an error of rank
w>\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\rfloor
. Hence, we cannot decode in
\mathscr{G}
to recover the plaintext. However, thanks to the knowledge of the public key, one can easily recover the affine space

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}\mid\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}-{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\beta\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})\in\mathscr{G}\}

using linear algebra.

Lemma 3

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}
. Then
\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}-{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\beta\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})\in\mathscr{G}
if and only if

{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\boldsymbol{z})~=~0
.

Proof

Note that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}
,

\mathbf{rank}_{q}({\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\lambda\boldsymbol{z}))\leqslant\mathbf{rank}_{q}(\boldsymbol{z})=w<n-k.

Indeed, let
\mathscr{B}
be a basis of the extension field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then, if
\lambda\neq 0
, the extension of
\lambda\boldsymbol{z}
in
\mathscr{B}
is the extension of
\boldsymbol{z}
in the basis
\lambda\mathscr{B}
. Therefore,

\textrm{RowSupp}(\lambda\boldsymbol{z})=\textrm{RowSupp}(\boldsymbol{z})

and the trace cannot increase the rank.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}
. Then

\boldsymbol{c}^{\prime}-{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\beta\boldsymbol{k}_{pub})=(\boldsymbol{m}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\boldsymbol{x}))\boldsymbol{G}+{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\boldsymbol{z}).

Therefore,
\beta\in A
if and only if
{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\boldsymbol{z})\in\mathscr{G}
. Since it has rank weight less than the minimum distance of
\mathscr{G}
, it follows that
{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\boldsymbol{z})=0
.

Lemma 4

Let
\mathscr{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{\zeta}\langle\mu_{i}\rangle^{\perp}
. Then
A
is the affine space
\alpha+\mathscr{E}
.

Proof

\beta\in A
if and only if

{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\boldsymbol{z})=\sum_{i=1}^{\zeta}{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\mu_{i})\boldsymbol{z}_{i}=0.

By the linear independence of the
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
’s, it follows that
{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}((\alpha-\beta)\mu_{i})=0
for all
i
,
i.e.

A=\alpha+\bigcap_{i=1}^{\zeta}\langle\mu_{i}\rangle^{\perp}.

We are now able to remove the
\boldsymbol{z}
dependency in the ciphertext. Indeed, let
\mathscr{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\{{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma\boldsymbol{x})\mid\gamma\in\mathscr{E}\}.
The knowledge of
A
gives finally access to the affine space
\boldsymbol{m}+\mathscr{F}
.

Step 3: Recover the plaintext.

Denote by
f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}\mathscr{F}
. Since
\mathscr{F}
is the image of
\mathscr{E}

by a surjective map, we have
f\leqslant\dim\mathscr{E}=u-\zeta\leqslant u-1
.
Let
\boldsymbol{s}
be some random element of
\boldsymbol{m}+\mathscr{F}
. Notice that from a description of the affine space
\boldsymbol{m}+\mathscr{F}
it is possible to recover a basis
(\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{f})
of
\mathscr{F}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{s}
can be decomposed as

\boldsymbol{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{m}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{f}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}

for some unknown coefficients
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. Furthermore, recall that the last
u
positions of
\boldsymbol{m}
are
0
. Then,
\boldsymbol{m}
is a solution of the following linear system of
k+f
unknowns and
u+k
equations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{m}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{f}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}=\boldsymbol{s}\\
\boldsymbol{m}_{k-u+1}=\dots=\boldsymbol{m}_{k}=0\end{array}\right.

(20)

Finally, the following lemma shows that
\boldsymbol{m}
can be recovered from
any
solution of (
20
).

Lemma 5

Let
(\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\mathbf{\lambda^{\prime}})
be another solution of (
20
). Then
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{m}
.

Proof

Since

\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{f}(\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-\lambda_{i})\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
, it is of the form
{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma\boldsymbol{x})
for some

\gamma\in\mathscr{E}
. Moreover, its last
u
positions are
0
. Recall
that
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-u+1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
is a basis of

\mathbb{F}_{q^{mu}}/\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
. Then, the last
u
positions of
{\rm Tr}_{q^{mu}/q^{m}}(\gamma\boldsymbol{x})
are
the coefficients of
\gamma
in the dual basis

\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast}_{k-u+1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\ast}\}
. Hence,
\gamma=0

and
\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}
.

•

\mathscr{F}

•

20

\boldsymbol{m}

Appendix 0.A
Further Details About RAMESSES’
Specifications

As explained in Section
2.2
, our presentation of

Ramesses
may seem to differ from the original proposal

[
12
]
. Indeed in Section
2.2
, we present the
scheme using only
q
–polynomials, while the original publication
prefers using matrices and vectors.
The purpose of the present appendix is to prove that our way to
present
Ramesses
is equivalent to that of
[
12
]
.

Caution.
In the present article we use
q
to denote
the cardinality of the ground field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. In
[
12
]
the ground
field is always supposed to be
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
q
refers to some power of

2
,
i.e.

q=2^{n}
for some positive
n
. Moreover, the exponent of

q
is denoted
n
while it is denoted
m
in the present article.
This might be confusing while reading both papers in parallel.

The other notations
w,k,\ell,t
are the same in the two articles.
Finally, in
[
12
]
a public
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–basis
\boldsymbol{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})

of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
is fixed once for all. Our presentation does not require
such a setting.

0.A.1
Key Generation.

Recall that
[
12
]
fixes a vector
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{m}
of rank
m

(
i.e.
an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
). This data together
with a parity–check matrix
\boldsymbol{H}
of the Gabidulin code

\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})
are public.

Original presentation

The key generation consists in picking a uniformly random

\boldsymbol{k}_{{\rm priv}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{m}
of weight
w
and the public key is its syndrome

\boldsymbol{k}_{pub}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{k}_{{\rm priv}}^{\top}
with respect to the public Gabidulin
code.

Our presentation

Since the code
\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})
is public,
any of its elements may be associated to an element of

\mathcal{L}_{<k}
. The transition from codewords to
q
–polynomials is
nothing but interpolation.
Similarly, the choice of a vector
\boldsymbol{k}_{{\rm priv}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{m}
of rank
w
is
(again by interpolation) equivalent to that of a
q
–polynomial
K

of rank
w
. Finally, publishing its syndrome
\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{k}_{{\rm priv}}^{\top}
is
equivalent to publish the coset
\boldsymbol{k}_{{\rm priv}}+\mathscr{G}_{k}(\boldsymbol{g})
, which in our
setting is nothing but publishing the affine space

K_{\textrm{sec}}+\mathcal{L}_{<k}
.

Encryption

Original presentation

The plain text is encoded into a matrix
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times m}

t
.

•

Compute
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{m}

\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{k}_{pub}

•

Pick a uniformly random

0.A.3

Original presentation

Start by computing
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}^{n}

\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}

\boldsymbol{k}_{{\rm priv}}

\boldsymbol{z}

\mathscr{G}_{k+\ell+w}(\boldsymbol{g})

\textrm{Ext}_{\boldsymbol{g}}(\boldsymbol{a})

\boldsymbol{P}

Our presentation

Similarly, the approach is based on applying

and
performing Gabidulin codes decoding. Indeed, starting from ciphertext
(
4

V=V_{\boldsymbol{k}_{{\rm priv}}}
