The von Neumann extension theory for abstract Friedrichs operators
M. Erceg, S. K. Soni

Abstract
. The theory of abstract Friedrichs operators was introduced some fifteen years ago with the aim of providing a more comprehensive framework for the study of positive symmetric systems of first-order partial differential equations, nowadays better known as (classical) Friedrichs systems. Since then, the theory has not only been frequently applied in numerical and analytical research of Friedrichs systems, but has continued to evolve as well. In this paper we provide an explicit characterisation and a classification of abstract Friedrichs operators. More precisely, we show that every abstract Friedrichs operators can be written as the sum of a skew-selfadjoint operator and a bounded self-adjoint strictly-positive operator. Furthermore, we develop a classification of realisations of abstract Friedrichs operators in the spirit of the von Neumann extension theory, which, when applied to the symmetric case, extends the classical theory.

1.
Introduction

Abstract Friedrichs operators
were introduced by Ern, Guermond and Caplain
[
25
]
some fifteen years ago with the aim of providing a more comprehensive framework for the study of
positive symmetric systems
.
These systems originate from the work of Friedrichs
[
27
]
(following his research on symmetric hyperbolic systems
[
26
]
), and today are customarily referred to as
(classical) Friedrichs systems
.
The reason why these systems are still attractive to the community lies in the fact that a wide variety of (semi)linear equations of mathematical physics (regardless of their order), including classical elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic equations, can be adapted, or rewritten, in the form of Friedrichs systems.
Moreover, the same applies to equations that change their type (the so-called mixed-type equations), such as the Tricomi equation, the study of which was actually the main motivation of Friedrichs to introduce this concept.
A nice historical exposition of the classical Friedrichs’ theory (which was very active until 1970’s) can be found in
[
32
]
.

The renewed interest in Friedrichs systems arose from numerical analysis (see e.g.
[
31
,
32
]
) based on the need to apply (discontinuous) Galerkin finite element methods to partial differential equations of various types.
The well-posedness results for Friedrichs systems obtained within the classical theory were not satisfactory; there were only results on the existence of weak solutions, and the uniqueness of strong ones, leaving the general question open on the joint existence and uniqueness of either a weak or a strong solution.
This brings us to abstract Friedrichs operators since in
[
25
]
the authors obtained a proper well-posedness result (Theorem
2.2
(xii) below) within the operator-theoretic framework introduced for this purpose (see also
[
2
]
).
This novel approach initiated a number of new investigations in various directions. For example, studies of different representations of boundary conditions and the relation with classical theory
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
7
,
9
,
21
]
, applications to diverse (initial-)boundary value problems of elliptic, hyperbolic, and parabolic type
[
6
,
12
,
14
,
20
,
22
,
34
]
, and the development of different numerical schemes
[
11
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
23
,
24
]
.

While we postpone the introduction of the precise definition of abstract Friedrichs operators to the subsequent section (Definition
2.1
), here we discuss the main ideas.
Assume we are given two densely defined linear operators
T_{0}
,
\widetilde{T}_{0}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that
T_{0}\subseteq\widetilde{T}_{0}^{*}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}\subseteq T_{0}^{*}
(
T_{0}^{*}
denotes the adjoint operator of
T_{0}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}\subseteq T_{0}^{*}
is understood in the standard way:
\operatorname{dom}\widetilde{T}_{0}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}T_{0}^{*}
and
T_{0}^{*}|_{\operatorname{dom}\widetilde{T}_{0}}=\widetilde{T}_{0}
). Now we seek for realisations (or extensions)
T
of
T_{0}
, i.e.
T_{0}\subseteq T\subseteq\widetilde{T}_{0}^{*}
, such that
T:\operatorname{dom}T\to\mathcal{H}
is bijective. Namely, for any right-hand side
f\in\mathcal{H}
the inverse linear problem induced by
T
:

Tu=f\,,

has a unique solution
u\in\operatorname{dom}T
. Hence, if we think of
T
as a differential operator (e.g. a classical Friedrichs operator), then the associated problem is well-posed and the choice of realisation
T
corresponds to the prescribed (initial-)boundary conditions.
Then it is natural to develop a mechanism for recognition, i.e. classification, of all bijective realisations.
Furthermore, we distinguish bijective realisations with signed boundary map (see Theorem
2.2
(xii) below; cf.
[
2
,
25
]
), and a particular representative of such a class is given by
\operatorname{dom}T=\operatorname{dom}T^{*}
.
The latter case is particularly desirable in the analysis, as it is evident in
[
25
, Section 4]
, where such a situation ensures existence of certain projectors which were needed to relate different abstract notions of imposing boundary conditions (see also
[
2
]
). On the other hand, in
[
7
, Theorem 18]
the same is required to have a complete characterisation of all bijective realisations with signed boundary map.

Concerning the classification of all bijective realisations, in
[
7
]
Grubb’s universal operator extension theory for the non-symmetric setting
[
28
]
(see also
[
29
, Chapter 13]
) was applied.
The first main result of this contribution is the development of an alternative approach based on the adaptation (or generalisation) of the von Neumann extension theory for symmetric operators (cf.
[
37
, Section 13.2]
), which in a way has already started in
[
21
, Section 3]
by deriving the decomposition of the graph space (see Theorem
2.2
(ix) below).
This new approach proved to be better when restricting only to bijective realisations with signed boundary map (see Theorem
4.7
below). Moreover, it is recognised that bijective realisations
T
with the property that
\operatorname{dom}T=\operatorname{dom}T^{*}
, mentioned above, exist if and only if the kernels
of maximal operators
\widetilde{T}_{0}^{*}
and
T_{0}^{*}
are isomorphic.

In the second main result we show that every abstract Friedrichs operator can be written as a sum of a skew-selfadjoint operator and a bounded self-adjoint strictly-positive operator (see Theorem
3.1
below).
This result enables a convenient connection between theories of abstract Friedrichs and (skew-)symmetric operators, many aspects of which are addressed throughout the manuscript. In particular in the last section, where a generalisation of the von Neumann extension theory for symmetric operators is provided.
Therefore, we hope that this article will bring the theory of abstract Friedrichs systems closer to a wider group of people working (or interested) in operator theory.

The paper is organised as follows.
In Section
2
we recall the definition of abstract Friedrichs systems and concisely present the main properties. Two main results of the paper are the subject of the following two sections.
A characterisation of abstract Friedrichs operators is provided in Section
3
, while the classification in the spirit of the von Neumann extension theory is developd in Section
4
. Finally, the paper is closed with some corollaries for the symmetric setting.

2.
Abstract Friedrichs operators

2.1.
Definition and main properties

The abstract Hilbert space formalism for Friedrichs systems which we
study in this paper was introduced
and developed in
[
25
,
2
]
for real vector spaces, while
the required differences for complex vector spaces have been
supplemented more recently in
[
4
]
.
Here we present the definition in the form given in

[
7
, Definition 1]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A (densely defined) linear operator
T_{0}
on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

(a scalar product is denoted by

\langle\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,\rangle
, which we take to be anti-linear in the second entry)
is called an
abstract Friedrichs operator
if there exists a
(densely defined) linear operator
\widetilde{T}_{0}
on
\mathcal{H}
with the following properties:

(T1)

T_{0}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}
have a common domain
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.
\operatorname{dom}T_{0}=\operatorname{dom}\widetilde{T}_{0}=\mathcal{D}
,
which is dense in
\mathcal{H}
, satisfying

\langle\,T_{0}\phi\mid\psi\,\rangle\;=\;\langle\,\phi\mid\widetilde{T}_{0}\psi\,\rangle\;,\qquad\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{D}\,;

(T2)

there is a constant
\lambda>0
for which

\|(T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0})\phi\|\;\leqslant\;2\lambda\|\phi\|\;,\qquad\phi\in\mathcal{D}\,;

(T3)

there exists a constant
\mu>0
such that

\langle\,(T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0})\phi\mid\phi\,\rangle\;\geqslant\;2\mu\|\phi\|^{2}\;,\qquad\phi\in\mathcal{D}\,.

The pair
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
is referred to as a
joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators

(the definition is indeed symmetric in
T_{0}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}
).

Before moving to the main topic of the paper,
let us briefly recall the essential properties
of (joint pairs of) abstract Friedrichs operators,
which we summarise in the form of a theorem.
At the same time, we introduce the notation that is used throughout the paper.
The presentation consists of two steps: first we deal with the consequences
of conditions (T1)–(T2), and then we highlight the additional structure implied by condition (T3).
A similar approach can be found in
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
.
This result enables a convenient connection between theories of abstract Friedrichs and symmetric operators, many aspects of which are addressed throughout the manuscript.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let a pair of linear operators
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
on
\mathcal{H}
satisfy
(T1)
and
(T2)
. Then the following holds.

i)

T_{0}\subseteq\widetilde{T}_{0}^{*}=:T_{1}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}\subseteq T_{0}^{*}=:\widetilde{T}_{1}
, where

\widetilde{T}_{0}^{*}
and
T_{0}^{*}
are adjoints of
\widetilde{T}_{0}
and
T_{0}
, respectively.

ii)

The pair of closures
(\overline{T}_{0},\overline{\widetilde{T}}_{0})
satisfies
(T1)–(T2)
with the same constant
\lambda
.

iii)

\operatorname{dom}\overline{T}_{0}=\operatorname{dom}\overline{\widetilde{T}}_{0}=:\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
\operatorname{dom}T_{1}=\operatorname{dom}\widetilde{T}_{1}=:\mathcal{W}
.

iv)

The graph norms
\|\cdot\|_{T_{1}}:=\|\cdot\|+\|T_{1}\cdot\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{\widetilde{T}_{1}}:=\|\cdot\|+\|\widetilde{T}_{1}\cdot\|
are equivalent,
(\mathcal{W},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{T_{1}})
is a Hilbert space (the
graph space
)
and
\mathcal{W}_{0}
is a closed subspace containing
\mathcal{D}
.

v)

The linear operator
\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}}
is everywhere defined, bounded and self-adjoint on

\mathcal{H}
such that on
\mathcal{W}
it coincides with
T_{1}+\widetilde{T}_{1}
.

vi)

The sesquilinear map

[\,u\mid v\,]\;:=\;\langle\,T_{1}u\mid v\,\rangle-\langle\,u\mid\widetilde{T}_{1}v\,\rangle\;,\quad u,v\in\mathcal{W}\,,

(2.1)

is an indefinite inner product on
\mathcal{W}
(cf.
[
10
]
) and we have
\mathcal{W}^{[\perp]}=\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
\mathcal{W}_{0}^{[\perp]}=\mathcal{W}
, where
the
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]
-orthogonal complement of a set
X\subseteq\mathcal{W}

is defined by

X^{[\perp]}:=\bigl\{u\in\mathcal{W}:(\forall v\in X)\quad[\,u\mid v\,]=0\bigr\}

and it is closed in
\mathcal{W}
. Moreover,
X^{[\perp][\perp]}=X
if and only if

X
is closed in
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{W}_{0}\subseteq X
.

For future reference, let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{u\in\mathcal{W}:[\,u\mid u\,]\geq 0\bigr\}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{u\in\mathcal{W}:[\,u\mid u\,]\leq 0\bigr\}\,.

Note that
X\subseteq X^{[\perp]}
implies
X\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\mathcal{W}^{-}
.

Assume, in addition,
(T3)
, i.e.
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
is
a joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators. Then

vii)

(\overline{T}_{0},\overline{\widetilde{T}}_{0})
satisfies
(T3)
with the same constant
\mu
.

viii)

A lower bound of
\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}}
is
2\mu>0
.

ix)

We have

\mathcal{W}\;=\;\mathcal{W}_{0}\dotplus\operatorname{ker}T_{1}\dotplus\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}\;,

(2.3)

where the sums are direct and all spaces on the right-hand side
are pairwise
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]
-orthogonal.
Moreover, the linear projections

p_{\mathrm{k}}:\mathcal{W}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{1}\quad\hbox{and}\quad p_{\mathrm{\tilde{k}}}:\mathcal{W}\to\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}

(2.4)

are continuous as maps
(\mathcal{W},\|\cdot\|_{T_{1}})\to(\mathcal{H},\|\cdot\|)
, i.e.
p_{\mathrm{k}},p_{\mathrm{\tilde{k}}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{H})
.

x)

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a subspace of the graph space
\mathcal{W}
such that

\mathcal{W}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
(see (
2.2
)). Then

(\forall u\in\mathcal{V})\qquad\|T_{1}u\|\geq\mu\|u\|\,.

In particular,
\overline{\operatorname{ran}(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}})}=\operatorname{ran}\overline{T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}}
.

Analogously, if
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{W}
such that

\mathcal{W}_{0}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
, then

\|\widetilde{T}_{1}v\|\geq\mu\|v\|
,
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
,
and
\overline{\operatorname{ran}(\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}})}=\operatorname{ran}\overline{\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}}}
.

xi)

Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
be a closed subspace (in
\mathcal{W}
) containing
\mathcal{W}_{0}
.
Then operators
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}}
are
mutually adjoint, i.e.
(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}})^{*}=\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}}

and
(\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}})^{*}=T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
, if and only if

\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}=\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
.

xii)

Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
be a closed subspace containing
\mathcal{W}_{0}

such that
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
and
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
.
Then
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{H}
and
\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{{\mathcal{V}}^{[\perp]}}:\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\to\mathcal{H}
are bijective,
i.e. isomorphisms when we equip their domains with the
graph topology, and for every
u\in\mathcal{V}
the following estimate holds:

\|u\|_{T_{1}}\leq\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\mu}\Bigr)\|T_{1}u\|\,.

(2.5)

The same estimate holds for
\widetilde{T}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{[\perp]}
replacing
T_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}
, respectively.

These bijective realisations of
T_{0}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}
we call

bijective realisations with signed boundary map
.

xiii)

Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
be a closed subspace containing
\mathcal{W}_{0}
.
Then
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{H}
is bijective if and only if
\mathcal{V}\dotplus\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\mathcal{W}
.

The statements i)–iv), vii) and viii) follow easily from the corresponding
assumptions (cf.
[
7
,
25
]
).
The claims v), x) and xii) are already
argued in the first paper on abstract Friedrichs operators
[
25
]
for real vector spaces
(see sections 2 and 3 there), while in
[
4
]

the arguments are repeated in the complex setting.
The same applies for vi) with a remark that for a further structure
of indefinite inner product space
(\mathcal{W},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
we refer to

[
2
]
.
The decomposition given in ix) is derived in
[
21
, Theorem 3.1]
,
while for additional claims on projectors we refer to the proof of Lemma 3.5 in the
aforementioned reference. In the same reference one can find the proof
of part xiii) (Lemma 3.10 there).
Finally, a characterisation of mutually self-adjointness xi) is obtained
in
[
7
, Theorem 9]
.

2.2.
Additional remarks

Let us close this section with a few remarks concerning the statements of
the previous theorem.

Remark 2.3
.

Not all bijective realisations, characterised in part xiii), are bijective realisation with signed
boundary map (described in part xii)),
i.e.
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
and
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
is only a sufficient condition.
However, we have the following equivalence:
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
is bijective with
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
if and only if
\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}}

is bijective with
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
. Thus, there is no need in considering pairs of bijective
realisations with signed boundary map, but we can denote (in this case) each of
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}}

as a bijective realisation with signed boundary map of
T_{1}
and
\widetilde{T}_{1}
, respectively.
Let us comment on this.

Since
(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}})^{*}=\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}}
(see part xi)) and each of spaces
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
and
\mathcal{H}

is complete, we have equivalence on the level of bijectivity.
Let us assume that
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
and let us denote by
\mathcal{V}_{1}
a maximal subspace of
\mathcal{W}
such that

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
(it exists by Zorn’s lemma; cf.
[
10
, Section I.6]
).
Then
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
(cf.
[
10
, Lemma 6.3]
or
[
2
, Theorem 2(b)]

for a perspective in the context of abstract Friedrichs operators), hence
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{1}}
is bijective as well.
Since
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{1}
, it must be
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{1}
. Hence,
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}=\mathcal{V}_{1}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
.
The opposite implication can be proved analogously.

The previous argument actually shows that a subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
with the property of

T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
being bijective is maximal nonnegative subspace, i.e. if for a subspace
\mathcal{V}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
we have
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
, then it must be
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{1}
. Then it is known that such
\mathcal{V}

defines a pair of bijective realisations with signed boundary map (see
[
2
, Theorem 2]
).

Let us close this remark by recalling that for any joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators there
exist bijective realisations with signed boundary map
(see
[
7
, Theorem 13(i)]
and
[
21
, Corollary 3.2]
).

Remark 2.4
.

In part vi) it is commented that for any subset
X
of
\mathcal{W}
we have
that
X\subseteq X^{[\perp]}
implies
X\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\mathcal{W}^{-}
.
On the other hand, if
X
is a subpsace, then the converse holds as well
by the means of the polarisation formula (cf.
[
10
, (2.3)]
).

Remark 2.5
.

If
\mathcal{V}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{W}
satisfying
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
, then
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\mathcal{W}^{-}
(see part vi)).
Thus,
\mathcal{V}
fulfills all assumptions of part xii) implying that
the corresponding realisations are bijective realisations with signed boundary map.
Since such realisations are of particular interest (see Introduction),
we will pay attention to examining when such realisations exist
(see corollaries
3.12
and
4.10
).

Remark 2.6
.

Note that in part ix) of the previous theorem we can also consider the graph norm
in the codomain of projections, since the graph norm and the (standard)
norm are equivalent on the kernels
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.
Moreover, another equivalent norm on
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
is
\sqrt{-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]}

(for
\nu,\mu\in\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
we have
-[\,\nu\mid\mu\,]=\langle\,\nu\mid(\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}})\mu\,\rangle
),
while on
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
we can take
\sqrt{[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]}
.
In particular,
(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
and
(\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
are Hilbert spaces.

A more detailed point of view can be found in
[
2
]
where the authors recognised that the
quotient space
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}:=\mathcal{W}/\mathcal{W}_{0}
is a Kreĭn space (cf.
[
10
]
), while the pair of subspaces

(\widehat{\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}},\widehat{\operatorname{ker}T_{1}})
represents a fundamental decomposition
of
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}
(cf.
[
21
, remarks 2.13(iii) and 3.3(ii)]
).

Remark 2.7
.

It holds
\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\mathcal{W}^{-}\cap\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}=\{0\}
.
Indeed, let
\nu\in\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
. Then
-[\,\nu\mid\nu\,]\leq 0
,
but since
(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
is a Hilbert space,
we get
-[\,\nu\mid\nu\,]=0
, implying
\nu=0
. The second identity is proved in
the same way.

Remark 2.8
.

A pair of operators satisfying (T1), and thus any pair of abstract Friedrichs operators, is a special case of
dual
or
symmetric
pairs of operators, where in general it is not required that the operators have the same domains (cf.
[
28
,
33
]
). However, the assumption on common domains is not overly restrictive from the perspective of applications to partial differential equations. Indeed, in this setting a standard choice for the ambient Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is the Lebesgue space of square integrable functions, while for the domain
\mathcal{D}
of minimal operators the space of smooth functions with compact support (cf.
[
2
,
25
]
).

3.
Characterisation of abstract Friedrichs operators

3.1.
Characterisation

Abstract Friedrichs operators were introduced in
[
25
]
, while its reformulation completely
in the spirit of Hilbert spaces was provided in
[
7
]
.
Now we make a step forward by deriving the following simple and explicit characterisation that will allow us to connect the theory of abstract Friedichs operators with the well-established theory for (skew-)symmetric operators.

Theorem 3.1
.

A pair of densely defined operators
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
on
\mathcal{H}

satisfies
(T1)
and
(T2)
if and only if there exist a densely defined skew-symmetric operator
L_{0}
and a bounded self-adjoint operator
S
,
both on
\mathcal{H}
, such that

T_{0}=L_{0}+S\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\widetilde{T}_{0}=-L_{0}+S\,.

(3.1)

For a given pair, the decomposition (
3.1
)
is unique.

If in the above we include condition
(T3)
, then the same holds with
S
being strictly positive, i.e.

\langle\,Su\mid u\,\rangle\geq\mu\|u\|^{2}\;,\quad u\in\mathcal{H}\,,

where
\mu>0
is the constant appearing in
(T3)
.

Proof.

Let
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
satisfies (T1) and (T2).
First, we will comment on the uniqueness.
Let
L_{0},L_{0}^{\prime}
and
S,S^{\prime}
be two densely defined skew-symmetric operator and two bounded self-adjoint operators, respectively, such that

T_{0}=L_{0}+S=L_{0}^{\prime}+S^{\prime}
.
Since
\operatorname{dom}T_{0}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}L_{0}\cap\operatorname{dom}L_{0}^{\prime}
, the operator
L_{0}-L_{0}^{\prime}
is densely defined and both skew-symmetric and symmetric (since coincides with
S^{\prime}-S
). Thus, it is necessarily equal to the zero operator, implying
L_{0}=L_{0}^{\prime}
and
S=S^{\prime}
(note that the boundedness of
S
did not play any role).

Let us now proceed with the existence of such operators
L_{0}
and
S
. We define

S:=\frac{1}{2}(\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}})
, which is a bounded and self-adjoint operator by Theorem
2.2
(v).
Therefore, by

T_{0}=\frac{T_{0}-\widetilde{T}_{0}}{2}+\frac{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}}{2}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\widetilde{T}_{0}=-\frac{T_{0}-\widetilde{T}_{0}}{2}+\frac{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}}{2}

it is left to prove that
L_{0}:=\frac{T_{0}-\widetilde{T}_{0}}{2}
is
skew-symmetric.
Since
\operatorname{dom}L_{0}=\operatorname{dom}T_{0}\cap\operatorname{dom}\widetilde{T}_{0}=\mathcal{D}
,

L_{0}
is densely defined. Furthermore, using
L_{0}=S-\widetilde{T}_{0}

and the boundedness of
S
we get

L_{0}^{*}=S^{*}-\widetilde{T}_{0}^{*}=S-T_{1}=S|_{\mathcal{W}}-T_{1}=\frac{T_{1}+\widetilde{T}_{1}}{2}-T_{1}=-\frac{T_{1}-\widetilde{T}_{1}}{2}\supseteq-L_{0}\,,

where in addition we used the second part of Theorem
2.2
(v).
Finally, if condition (T3) is satisfied
as well,
S
has a positive lower bound by Theorem
2.2
(viii).

The converse follows easily by direct inspection.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

From the proof of the previous theorem it is easy to see that for general
mutually adjoint closed realisations
(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}},\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}})

(see Theorem
2.2
(xi))
we have

\bigl((T_{1}-\widetilde{T}_{1})|_{\mathcal{V}}\bigr)^{*}=-(T_{1}-\widetilde{T}_{1})|_{\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}}\,.

Note that for
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{W}_{0}
we have the identity
obtained in the proof of the previous theorem.

Remark 3.3
.

Even for a pair of densely defined operators
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying solely (T1),
we can get decomposition (
3.1
), but with
S
being only densely defined and symmetric.
Of course, the decomposition is provided for
L_{0}=(T_{0}-\widetilde{T}_{0})/2
and
S=(T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0})/2

and it is unique. Observe that the approach for proving existence of the previous theorem is not appropriate here (since
S
is not bounded), but instead one just needs to notice that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
we have

\langle\,L_{0}\varphi\mid\varphi\,\rangle=-\langle\,\varphi\mid L_{0}\varphi\,\rangle\quad\hbox{and}\quad\langle\,S\varphi\mid\varphi\,\rangle=\langle\,\varphi\mid S\varphi\,\rangle\,.

By Theorem
3.1
, the study of (pairs of) abstract
Friedrichs operators is reduced to the study of operators of the form
(
3.1
), which, in our opinion, makes the situation much more explicit (cf.
[
16
, Remark 4.3]
).
Let us illustrate some straightforward conclusions.
For a pair of abstract Friedrichs operators
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
,
let
L_{0}
and
S
be operators given in
Theorem
3.1
.
If we denote
L_{1}:=-L_{0}^{*}\supseteq L_{0}
, then we have

\begin{split}T_{0}&=L_{0}+S\;,\\
T_{1}&=L_{1}+S\;,\qquad\end{split}\begin{split}\widetilde{T}_{0}&=-L_{0}+S\;,\\
\widetilde{T}_{1}&=-L_{1}+S\;.\end{split}

(3.2)

In particular,
\mathcal{W}_{0}=\operatorname{dom}\overline{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{W}=\operatorname{dom}L_{1}
,
i.e. spaces
\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
\mathcal{W}
are independent of
S
.
This is also clear by noting that the graph norms

\|\cdot\|_{T_{1}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L_{1}}
are equivalent,
due to the boundedness of
S
.
The same holds for the sesquilinear map (
2.1
)
since

[\,u\mid v\,]=\langle\,L_{1}u\mid v\,\rangle+\langle\,u\mid L_{1}v\,\rangle\;,\quad u,v\in\mathcal{W}\,.

(3.3)

Thus, all conditions on subspaces
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
given in
Theorem
2.2
(xii) depend only on
L_{1}
(i.e.
L_{0}
).
In particular, we can formulate the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
be a joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators
on
\mathcal{H}
and let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
be a closed subspace containing
\mathcal{W}_{0}

such that
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
and
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
(with respect to
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
).
For any joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators
(A_{0},\widetilde{A}_{0})

on
\mathcal{H}
such that

(A_{0}-\widetilde{A}_{0})^{*}=(T_{0}-\widetilde{T}_{0})^{*}

we have that
\bigl((\widetilde{A}_{0})^{*}|_{\mathcal{V}},(A_{0})^{*}|_{\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}}\bigr)

is a pair of bijective realisations with signed boundary map.

Remark 3.5
.

Another perspective to the previous corollary can be made in terms of (linear)
m-accretive
operators
[
37
, Section 3.3]
.
To start, note that by the definition of accretive operators on Hilbert spaces for
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
we have that
L:=L_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
is accretive (see (
2.2
) and (
3.3
)), where we use the notation given in (
3.2
).
If in addition
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
(i.e.
\mathcal{V}
is a maximal nonnegative subspace; see Remark
2.3
), then
L
is m-accretive. Indeed, it is sufficient to apply Theorem
2.2
(xii) on a pair of abstract Friedrichs operators
(L_{0}+\mathbbm{1},-L_{0}+\mathbbm{1})
for the identity operator
\mathbbm{1}
(see Theorem
3.1
).
Furthermore, since
S
is bounded and positive, a standard perturbative argument (cf.
[
35
, Ch. 3, Corollary 3.3]
) implies that
T:=T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
is m-accretive as well.
Therefore, the statement of the previous corollary can be seen in the following way: if
L=L_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
is m-accretive, then for any
S
bounded and strictly positive,
L+S
is also m-accretive.

On the other hand, if a realisation
T=L+S
is m-accretive, one can show that then
T
is a bijective realisation with signed boundary map
[
39
, Theorem 5.2.2]
, and then, by the discussion above,
L
is also m-accretive.
Thus, we have that the study of bijective realisations with a signed boundary map of abstract Friedrichs operators is tantamount to the study of m-accretive extensions of skew-symmetric operators. This problem of studying m-accretive extensions of skew-symmetric operators has been intensively investigated over the past few decades. For instance, recent studies include

[
8
,
36
,
40
,
42
]
, where various approaches have been applied, as well as different levels of generality (e.g. linear and/or nonlinear relations). In the following section, we shall make a more precise comparison to our results.

Not all domains of bijective realisations have the feature described in
the previous corollary, i.e. there are subspaces
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
such that
realisations
T=L_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}+S
are bijective for some admissible
S
’es,
but not all.
Furthermore, if a subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
defines bijective
realisations for any admissible
S
, that does not imply that

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
and
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{-}
.
All this can be illustrated by the following simple example.

Example 3.6
.

Let
a<b
,
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}((a,b);\mathbb{R})

(for simplicity we consider only real functions) and
\mathcal{D}=C^{\infty}_{c}(a,b)
.
For
\mu>0
and
\beta\in L^{\infty}(a,b)
such that
\beta\geq\mu
a.e. on
(a,b)
,
we consider operators
T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{H}
given by

T_{0}u=u^{\prime}+\beta u\;,\quad\widetilde{T}_{0}u=-u^{\prime}+\beta u\,.

Then it is easy to see that
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
is a joint pair of
abstract Friedrichs operators, while
\mathcal{W}=H^{1}(a,b)
(which is embedded
into
C([a,b])
) and
\mathcal{W}_{0}=H^{1}_{0}(a,b)
.
Of course, in the notation of Theorem
3.1
, here we have

L_{0}u=u^{\prime}
and
Su=\beta u
. Then
L_{1}u:=-L_{0}^{*}u=u^{\prime}
(here the derivative is in the weak sense),
T_{1}=L_{1}+S
,
\widetilde{T}_{1}=-L_{1}+S
, and

[\,u\mid v\,]=u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a)\;,\quad u,v\in\mathcal{W}\,.

Let us comment on all bijective realisations of operators
T_{0}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}
,
i.e. all bijective restrictions of
T_{1}
and
\widetilde{T}_{1}
.

We define closed subspaces
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
(here we identify
-\infty
and
+\infty
), by

\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}:=\bigl\{u\in\mathcal{W}:u(b)=\alpha u(a)\bigr\}\;,\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\,,

and
\mathcal{V}_{\infty}:=\bigl\{u\in\mathcal{W}:u(a)=0\bigr\}
.
Since
(L_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}})^{*}=-L_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}

(this can be verified by direct calculations),
we have
(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}})^{*}=\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}
,
where we use the convention:
\frac{1}{\infty}=0
and
\frac{1}{0}=\infty
.
Thus, we want to see for which values of
\alpha
,

\bigl(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}},\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\bigr)

(3.4)

is a pair of mutually adjoint bijective realisations (with signed boundary map).

By
[
21
, Remark 5.1]
we have that
all
mutually adjoint bijective realisations are given for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\setminus\{\alpha_{\beta}\}
,
where
\alpha_{\beta}:=e^{-\int_{a}^{b}\beta(y)dy}
.
Indeed, since

\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{span}\bigl\{e^{-\int_{a}^{x}\beta(y)dy}\bigr\}

we have
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\alpha_{\beta}}
.
Note that for
\alpha\in\{-1,1\}
we have
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{V}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
, hence

L_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}
is skew-selfadjoint.

By direct inspection we get that only for
\alpha\in(-1,1)

bijective realisations are not
with signed boundary map (one can also consider
\beta\equiv\mu
by
Corollary
3.4
and read the result from
[
1
, Example 1]
).
This is in the correspondence with the above result since
\alpha_{\beta}\in(-1,1)
.
More precisely, we have
\alpha_{\beta}\in(0,1)
and by varying
\mu
and
\beta

one can get any number in that interval for
\alpha_{\beta}
.

Therefore, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\setminus(-1,1)
the corresponding
domains, i.e. boundary conditions, give rise to bijective realisations
independent of the choice of admissible
\beta
(see Corollary
3.4
).
The same holds for
\alpha\in(-1,0]
although these bijective realisations
are not with signed boundary map. Here the reason lies in the fact that

\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\{0\}
(cf. Theorem
2.2
(xiii))
for any
\alpha\in(-1,0]
and any choice of
admissible
\beta
.
Finally, there is no
\alpha
in
(0,1)
with this property. However, for
fixed
\beta
, all
\alpha\in(0,1)
but one (
\alpha=\alpha_{\beta}
)
correspond to mutually adjoint bijective realisations (
3.4
).

We will return to this example to consider general symmetric parts.

Remark 3.7
.

If we consider
T_{0}=L_{0}+C
where
L_{0}
is skew-symmetric and
C
bounded (i.e.
C
is not necessarily self-adjoint), then
it is easy to see that the discussion preceding Corollary
3.4
still holds.
More precisely, spaces
\mathcal{W}_{0}
,
\mathcal{W}
and indefinite inner product
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]

are independent of
C
((
3.3
) holds precisely as it is; recall that
L_{1}=-L_{0}^{*}
) and
the graph norm is equivalent with
\|\cdot\|_{L_{1}}
(cf.
[
13
, Subsection 2.2]
).
Thus, these objects depend solely on the unbounded component of the skew-symmetric part of
T_{0}
.

3.2.
Deficiency indices

The previous example illustrates that in order to get all bijective realisations
(not only with signed boundary map) it is not enough to consider
L_{0}
alone,
we must also bring the symmetric part
S
into play.
In particular, information on kernels
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
is
essential.
By Theorem
2.2
(ix) we have

\mathcal{W}\;=\;\mathcal{W}_{0}\dotplus\operatorname{ker}T_{1}\dotplus\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}\;.

Hence, the sum of dimensions of the kernels is constant and equals the
codimension of
\mathcal{W}_{0}
in
\mathcal{W}
. However, from here we cannot conclude that
(cardinal) numbers
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
are independent of

S
, where
T_{1}=L_{1}+S
.
If so, this would be beneficial in the analysis (see Example
3.11
below).
Let us motivate why one should expect such a result.
Since
L_{0}
is skew-symmetric, we have that
-iL_{0}
is symmetric.
Thus, for any positive constant
\beta>0
we have

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}+\beta\mathbbm{1})=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(iL_{0}^{*}-i\beta\mathbbm{1})=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}((-iL_{0})^{*}-i\beta\mathbbm{1})=d_{+}(-iL_{0})\,,

where
\mathbbm{1}
denotes the identity operator and on the right we have the deficiency index (or the defect number)
of
-iL_{0}
, which is known to be independent of
\beta>0

(see
[
37
, Section 3.1]
).
Analogously,
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}-\beta\mathbbm{1})=d_{-}(-iL_{0})
.
Therefore, all that we need is to show that instead of
\beta\mathbbm{1}

we can put an arbitrary bounded self-adjoint strictly positive
operator.
Below is a slightly more general statement.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
L_{0}
be a densely defined skew-symmetric operator and let
us denote
L_{1}:=-L_{0}^{*}
. For a bounded linear operator
C
with strictly positive
symmetric part
\frac{1}{2}(C+C^{*})
, we define

d^{C}_{+}(L_{0}):=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}+C)\quad\hbox{and}\quad d^{C}_{-}(L_{0}):=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}-C)\,.

Then
d^{C}_{+}(L_{0})
and
d^{C}_{-}(L_{0})
are independent of
C
,
i.e.
d_{\pm}^{C}(L_{0})=d_{\pm}(-iL_{0})
.

Proof.

Since
L_{0}
is closable and
d_{\pm}^{C}(L_{0})=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}\pm C)=d_{\pm}^{C}(\overline{L_{0}})
,
we can assume that
L_{0}
is closed.
We shall prove the claim for
d_{+}^{C}(L_{0})
, while the same argument
applies on
d_{-}^{C}(L_{0})
.

Let us take arbitrary bounded operators

C
and
C^{\prime}
with strictly positive symmetric parts,
and let us denote by
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
the greatest
lower bounds of their symmetric parts, respectively.
We shall first argue in a specific situation when
\|C-C^{\prime}\|<\min\{\mu,\mu^{\prime}\}
,
where here
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm.
Before we start, let us note that according to Theorem
3.1
,
both operators
-L_{0}+C^{*}
and
-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*}
define a (pair of) abstract Friedrichs operators (the skew-symmetric part is equal to the sum of
L_{0}
(unbounded part) and the skew-symmetric component of
C
or
C^{\prime}
(bounded part) with the negative sign).
Hence, all results of Theorem
2.2
are applicable.
In particular, since
L_{0}
is assumed to be closed,
part (x) of the aforementioned theorem implies that
the ranges of the operators
-L_{0}+C^{*}
and
-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*}
are closed.

If
d_{+}^{C}(L_{0})>d_{+}^{C^{\prime}}(L_{0})
, then there exists

0\neq\nu\in\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}+C)\cap\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}+C^{\prime})^{\perp}

(cf.
[
37
, Lemma 2.3]
).
Since
\operatorname{ran}(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})
is closed, we have
\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}+C^{\prime})^{\perp}=\operatorname{ran}(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})
.
Thus, we also have
\nu\in\operatorname{ran}(-L_{0}+C^{*})^{\perp}\cap\operatorname{ran}(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})
.
Let
0\neq u\in\operatorname{dom}L_{0}
be such that
\nu=(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u
.
Then it holds

\langle\,(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u\mid(-L_{0}+C^{*})u\,\rangle=0\,.

(3.5)

Applying Theorem
2.2
(x) and the identity above, we have

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}\|u\|\|(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\,(-L_{0}+C^{*})u+(C^{\prime}-C)^{*}u\mid(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u\,\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\,u\mid(C^{\prime}-C)(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u\,\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|\|C^{\prime}-C\|\|(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u\|\,.

Since
u\neq 0
and
\nu=(-L_{0}+(C^{\prime})^{*})u\neq 0
, this implies
\|C^{\prime}-C\|\geq\mu^{\prime}
,
which contradicts the starting assumption
\|C^{\prime}-C\|<\mu^{\prime}
.
Hence, it should be
d_{+}^{C}(L_{0})\leq d_{+}^{C^{\prime}}(L_{0})
.

Since the identity (
3.5
) is symmetric with respect to
C
and
C^{\prime}
,
the same holds even if we start with the assumption
d_{+}^{C}(L_{0})<d_{+}^{C^{\prime}}(L_{0})
.
However, then we have
(-L_{0}+C^{*})u\neq 0
. Repeating the last calculations
with
C^{\prime}
and
C
swapping places, we come to the analogous conclusion:

\mu\leq\|C-C^{\prime}\|<\mu
. Therefore, it must be
d_{+}^{C}(L_{0})=d_{+}^{C^{\prime}}(L_{0})
.

It is left to prove the statement without the additional assumption

\|C-C^{\prime}\|<\min\{\mu,\mu^{\prime}\}
. This easily follows by noting that the set of all bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
with strictly positive symmetric part is convex. More precisely,
for each
\lambda\in[0,1]
we have that

C_{\lambda}:=\lambda C+(1-\lambda)C^{\prime}
is bounded and
the greatest lower bound of its symmetric part is
\lambda\mu+(1-\lambda)\mu^{\prime}\geq\min\{\mu,\mu^{\prime}\}
.
Moreover,
\|C_{\lambda_{1}}-C_{\lambda_{2}}\|=|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}|\|C-C^{\prime}\|
.
Thus, we can pick finitely many values
0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda<\dots<\lambda_{m}=1
such that

\|C_{\lambda_{j}}-C_{\lambda_{j+1}}\|<\min\{\mu,\mu^{\prime}\}
,
j=1,2,\dots,,m-1
.
Therefore, by applying the previously obtained result, we get

d_{+}^{C^{\prime}}(L_{0})=d_{+}^{C_{\lambda_{1}}}(L_{0})=d_{+}^{C_{\lambda_{2}}}(L_{0})=\dots=d_{+}^{C_{\lambda_{m}}}(L_{0})=d_{+}^{C}(L_{0})\,,

concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

The stability of the indices
d_{\pm}^{C}(L_{0})
with respect to
C
is, in fact, a consequence of the homotopy argument. Indeed, for a fixed
C
, let us consider
H_{\pm}(C^{\prime},\lambda)=d_{\pm}^{(1-\lambda)C+\lambda C^{\prime}}(L_{0})
, where
C
runs through all bounded linear operators with a strictly positive symmetric part and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Then one just needs to show that
H_{\pm}
behave well (i.e. in a continuous manner) with respect to
\lambda
, which is precisely what we studied in the proof.

Remark 3.10
.

For a densely defined skew-symmetric operator
L_{0}
we will refer to the cardinal numbers
d_{\pm}(-iL_{0})
as the deficiency indices (or the defect numbers) of
L_{0}
,
and we introduce the notation
d_{\pm}(L_{0}):=d_{\pm}(-iL_{0})
.
The definition is not ambiguous because depending on whether the operator is
symmetric or skew-symmetric the corresponding definition applies.

Let us return to the analysis of Example
3.6
.

Example 3.11
.

In Example
3.6
we studied specific (multiplicative) symmetric parts.
Let us now consider a general situation where

T_{0}u=u^{\prime}+Cu\;,\quad\widetilde{T}_{0}u=-u^{\prime}+Cu\,,

for an arbitrary bounded linear operator
C
with strictly positive
symmetric part
\frac{1}{2}(C+C^{*})
.

First note that by Example
3.6
(cf.
[
7
, Subsection 6.1]
)
and Lemma
3.8
we have

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{dim}\widetilde{T}_{1}=1
(for any admissible
C
).

The conclusion of Example
3.6
for the range
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\setminus(-1,1)
remains the same (cf. Corollary
3.4
), i.e. for
these values of
\alpha
we get for any admissible
C
bijective realisations
(even with signed boundary map).

Let us take
\alpha\in(-1,0)
. Since the codimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
in
\mathcal{W}

equals 1, and
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=1
,
by Theorem
2.2
(xiii) it is sufficient to prove that

\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
and
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{span}\{\varphi_{C}\}
have a trivial intersection
to get that the corresponding realisations are bijective.
Let us assume on the contrary that
\varphi_{C}\in\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
.
Since
\alpha<0
, we have
\varphi_{C}(a)\varphi_{C}(b)<0
. Thus, recalling
that
\mathcal{W}\hookrightarrow C([a,b])
, there exists
c\in(a,b)
such that

\varphi_{C}(c)=0
. Moreover,
\varphi_{C}\in\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
implies that

\varphi_{C}^{\prime}+C\varphi_{C}=0
in
(c,b)
as well. This together with
\varphi_{C}(c)=0

implies that
\varphi_{C}\equiv 0
in
(c,d)
. Indeed, just recall that
\mathcal{V}_{\infty}

defines a bijective realisation. In particular, we have
\varphi_{C}(b)=0
, implying

\alpha=0
, which is a contradiction.
Therefore, for any
\alpha\in(-1,0)
we get bijective realisations independently
of the choice of
C
.
Note that here we were not able to capture the value
\alpha=0
.

The argument given in Example
3.6
is sufficient to conclude that
in general there is no
\alpha
in
(0,1)
with the property that the pair
of domains
(\mathcal{V}_{\alpha},\mathcal{V}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}})
gives rise to (mutually adjoint) bijective realisations (
3.4
) for any choice of admissible
C
.
On the other hand, for fixed
C
, since
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=1
, there exists precisely one
\alpha=\alpha_{C}\in[0,1)
for which the corresponding realisations are not bijective.
Even though we have bijective realisations for all other values of
\alpha
, i.e.
\alpha\in[0,1)\setminus\{\alpha_{C}\}
, this case still depends on
C
.

A particularly interesting case of bijective realisations with signed boundary map
is when
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
(see Introduction). By Remark
3.2
this occurs if and only
if the associated realisation of the skew-symmetric part
L_{0}
is skew-selfadjoint.
Thus, such subspace
\mathcal{V}
exists if and only if
d_{+}(L_{0})=d_{-}(L_{0})
(see

[
37
, Theorem 13.10]
).
Applying Lemma
3.8
we can formulate the following corollary.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
be a joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
There exists a closed subspace
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{W}
with
\mathcal{W}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and such that
(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}},\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}})
is a pair of mutually adjoint bijective realisations related to
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
if and only if

\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
are isomorphic.

Remark 3.13
.

The notion of isomorphism of Hilbert spaces used in the previous corollary is the standard (and natural) one
(cf.
[
19
, I.5.1. Definition]
): two Hilbert spaces are
isomorphic
if there exists a linear
surjective isometry (
isomorphism
or
unitary transformation
) between them.

One can find several characterisations, e.g. two Hilbert spaces are isomorphic if and only if

i)

they have the same dimension.

ii)

there exists a linear bounded bijection between them.

For the first claim we refer to
[
19
, I.5.4. Theorem]
, while in the latter one needs to discuss only
the converse. This can be done in a straigtforward constructive way. Indeed, if we denote by
A

a linear bounded bijection between two given Hilbert spaces, then
U:=A(A^{*}A)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is
an isomorphism (in the above sense).

Remark 3.14
.

Since
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
is a Hilbert space when both equipped with
\langle\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,\rangle
(the standard inner product of the
ambient space
\mathcal{H}
) and
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]
(the indefinite inner product on
\mathcal{W}
), and the identity map

i:(\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1},\langle\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,\rangle)\to(\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
is continuous
(due to the boundedness of
T_{1}+\widetilde{T}_{1}
on
\mathcal{H}
),
it is irrelevant which Hilbert space structure we consider on
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
in Corollary
3.12
.
The same applies on
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
as well, with the only difference that
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]

should be replaced by
-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]
.

4.
Classification of the von Neumann type

4.1.
Preliminaries

Applying von Neumann’s extension theory of symmetric operators (cf.
[
37
, Theorem 13.9]
) on
-iL_{0}
, we can classify all skew-selfadjoint
(even closed skew-symmetric) realisations of
L_{0}
in terms of
unitary transformations between (closed subspaces of)
\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}+\mathbbm{1})

and
\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}-\mathbbm{1})
.
Of course, by Lemma
3.8
(see also Remark
3.13
)
this can also be done when
\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}+\mathbbm{1})
and
\operatorname{ker}(L_{1}-\mathbbm{1})

are replaced by
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.
We are about to focus on this situation since, as it was demonstrated in examples
3.6
and
3.11
,
often it is desirable to keep abstract Friedrichs operators
T_{0}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}
as a whole (e.g. not all bijective realisations of
T_{0}=L_{0}+S
correspond to skew-symmetric realisations of
L_{0}
). Also, in terms of partial differential operators (especially with variable coefficients), it is sometimes easier to work with the operator
L_{0}+C
, for some
C
, than with skew-symmetric operator
L_{0}
itself.
However, we will not make use of
[
37
, Theorem 13.9]
, but develop an
independent constructive proof. This will allow for an explicit classification and
at the same time provide an alternative proof in the symmetric setting (for even more general situations).

Let us start with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
be a joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators
on
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
be a closed subspace containing
\mathcal{W}_{0}

and let us define
\mathcal{G}:=p_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathcal{V})
and

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}:=p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})
, where
p_{\mathrm{k}}
and
p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}
are given by
(
2.4
).
Then, we have the following.

i)

T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
is a bijective realisation of
T_{0}
if and only if
\mathcal{V}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\{0\}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}=\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.

ii)

Let
\mathcal{V}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\{0\}
. Then
U:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathcal{G}

defined by

U(p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u))=p_{\mathrm{k}}(u),\quad u\in\mathcal{V}\,,

(4.1)

is a well-defined closed linear map.
Moreover,
U
is bounded if and only if
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
is closed in
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
(cf. Remark
3.14
).

iii)

If
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
is closed and
U:(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])\to(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
is non-expansive, i.e. the norm of
U
with respect to the indicated norms is less than or equal to
1
.

iv)

If
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\mathcal{W}^{-}
, then both
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{G}
are closed and

U:(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])\to(\mathcal{G},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])

is a unitary transformation (cf. Remark

3.13
).

v)

Let
\mathcal{V}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\{0\}
. Then
\mathcal{V}
coincides with
\mathcal{V}_{U}
given
by

\mathcal{V}_{U}:=\bigl\{u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu}:u_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0},\,\tilde{\nu}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\bigr\}\,,

(4.2)

where
U
is defined by (
4.1
),
and
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}(u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu})=\overline{T}_{0}u_{0}+(\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}})\tilde{\nu}
.

Moreover, such
U
is unique, i.e. if for a subspace
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
and a
closed operator
U:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
we have
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{U}
,
where
\mathcal{V}_{U}
is defined by the formula above, then
U
is given by (
4.1
).

Remark 4.2
.

Notice that the assumptions in statements ii)-iv) are gradually strengthened
(see Remark
2.7
).
Furthermore, by Remark
2.4
the assumption of part iv) is equivalent to

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
.

Remark 4.3
.

Of course, in part iv) it is implicitly required that
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{G}
are isomorphic.

A trivial situation when the assumption
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\mathcal{W}^{-}
is satisfied occurs for

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{W}_{0}
. Then
\mathcal{G}=\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}=\{0\}
, hence they are obviously isomorphic.

Remark 4.4
.

By Remark
3.13
, in the regime
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\mathcal{W}^{-}
of part iv) of the previous lemma
from the mapping
U
, given by (
4.1
),
we can construct an isomorphism between spaces
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{G}
when both are equipped with the standard
inner product
\langle\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,\rangle
.
Indeed, if we denote by
\tilde{\iota}:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}
and

{\iota}:\mathcal{G}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}
canonical embeddings (note that then
\tilde{\iota}^{*}
and
\iota^{*}

are orthogonal projections in
\mathcal{H}
onto
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{G}
, respectively),
then a unitary transformation is given by

U\Bigl(\tilde{\iota}^{*}\,\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}}\,U^{*}\iota^{*}(\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}})^{-1}U\Bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

A trivial situation is when
\overline{T_{0}+\widetilde{T}_{0}}=\alpha\mathbbm{1}
, for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, since then
the expression above equals
U
.

Remark 4.5
.

It is clear that for any given
U:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
,
\mathcal{V}_{U}
defined by (
4.2
)
is a subspace of
\mathcal{W}
which contains
\mathcal{W}_{0}
.
Moreover, if
U
is closed, then
\mathcal{V}_{U}
is closed as well. Indeed, from

u_{0}^{n}+U\tilde{\nu}_{n}+\tilde{\nu}_{n}\to u_{0}+\nu+\tilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{W}
it follows (see the proof of
[
21
, Lemma 3.5]
)
that
U\tilde{\nu}_{n}\to\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}_{n}\to\tilde{\nu}
. Hence, for closed
U
we have
\tilde{\nu}\in\operatorname{dom}U

and
\nu=U\tilde{\nu}
, implying that
u_{0}+\nu+\tilde{\nu}=u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

i)

Let us assume that
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}
is a bijective realisation.
Injectivity implies that
\mathcal{V}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\{0\}
, while

p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}

is trivial. Let us prove the opposite inclusion.
By Theorem
2.2
(xiii), we have

\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{V}\dot{+}\operatorname{ker}{T}_{1}
.
Thus, for any
\tilde{\nu}\in\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
(recall that
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
) there exist unique

u\in\mathcal{V}
and
\nu\in\operatorname{ker}{T}_{1}
, such that
\tilde{\nu}=u+\nu
.
This implies
u=-\nu+\tilde{\nu}
, so
\tilde{\nu}=p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\in p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})
.

For the converse, we shall make use of Theorem
2.2
(xiii)
again. Let us take an arbitrary
w\in\mathcal{W}
. By Theorem
2.2
(ix) there exist unique
w_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
,
\nu\in\operatorname{ker}T_{1}

and
\tilde{\nu}\in\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
such that

w=w_{0}+\nu+\tilde{\nu}
.
The assumption ensures existence of
u\in\mathcal{V}
such that

u=u_{0}+\mu+\tilde{\nu}
, for some
u_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
\mu\in\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
.
By subtracting
\tilde{\nu}
from the second equation and inserting it into
the first, we get

w=(w_{0}-u_{0}+u)+(\nu-\mu)\,.

Since the first term on the right hand side belongs to
\mathcal{V}
(note that
\mathcal{W}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
) and the second
one to
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
, Theorem
2.2
(xiii) is applicable

ii)

We start by showing that
U
is a well-defined function. Let
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
be such that
p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)=p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(v)
. By the decomposition given in Theorem
2.2
(ix), there exist
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
, such that

\displaystyle u=u_{0}+p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)+p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\,,\quad v=v_{0}+p_{\mathrm{k}}(v)+p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(v)\;.

Thus,

\displaystyle u-v=(u_{0}-v_{0})+\bigl(p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)-p_{\mathrm{k}}(v)\bigr)\;.

Since
(u-v)-(u_{0}-v_{0})\in\mathcal{V}
, we get

p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)-p_{\mathrm{k}}(v)\in\mathcal{V}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\{0\}
.
Hence,
U
is well-defined.

Linearity follows from the linearity of projections
p_{\mathrm{k}}
and
p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}
.
Let us show that
U
is closed. Take
(u_{n})
in
\mathcal{V}
such that

p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u_{n})\to\tilde{\nu}
in
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
and

Up_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u_{n})=p_{\mathrm{k}}(u_{n})\to\nu
in
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}

(as
n
tends to infinity). This implies that
\bigl(p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u_{n})+p_{\mathrm{k}}(u_{n})\bigr)

converges to
\tilde{\nu}+\nu
in
\mathcal{W}
. Since
\mathcal{W}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

we have
p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u_{n})+p_{\mathrm{k}}(u_{n})\in\mathcal{V}
.
Thus, by the closedness of
\mathcal{V}
, we obtain
u:=\tilde{\nu}+\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, implying

\tilde{\nu}=p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\in p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})
and

U\tilde{\nu}=Up_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)=p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)=\nu
.

Similarly as with the closedness of
U
, in the last part the goal is to exploit the fact that

\mathcal{V}
is closed.
Indeed, let us assume that
U
is bounded, i.e. there exists
c>0
such that
\|p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)\|\leq c\|p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\|
,
u\in\mathcal{V}
.
Let us take
(u_{n})
in
\mathcal{V}
such that

p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u_{n})\to\tilde{\nu}
in
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
, as
n
tends to infinity.
Using the boundedness of
U
we get that the sequence
(p_{\mathrm{k}}(u_{n}))
is a Cauchy sequence
in
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
, hence convergent. Now we get
\tilde{\nu}\in p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})

following the previous reasoning. On the other hand, if
p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})
is closed,
then
U
is a closed linear map between two Hilbert spaces
p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})
and

\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
. Thus,
U
is bounded by the closed graph theorem.

iii)

Let
u\in\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
.
Using
u=u_{0}+p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)+p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)
,

\mathcal{W}^{[\perp]}=\mathcal{W}_{0}
(see Theorem
2.2
(vi)) and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
, we get

0\leq[\,u\mid u\,]=[\,p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)\mid p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)\,]+[\,p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\mid p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\,]\;,

(4.3)

which in terms of the operator
U
reads

-[\,Up_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\mid Up_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\,]\leq[\,p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\mid p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)\,]\;.

Thus,
U:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
is bounded (hence
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
is closed by part ii))
and
\|U\|\leq 1
(with respect to the norms

\sqrt{[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]}
and
\sqrt{-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]}
, respectively).

iv)

When
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}\cap\mathcal{W}^{-}
, then in
(
4.3
) we have equality.
This allows us to follow the last part of the proof of part ii) to conclude that

\mathcal{G}
is closed as well.
Furthermore,
U
is obviously a unitary transformation between Hilbert spaces

(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
and
(\mathcal{G},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
.

v)

Since for any
u\in\mathcal{V}
there exists
u_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
such that

u=u_{0}+p_{\mathrm{k}}(u)+p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)=u_{0}+Up_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)+p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(u)
, it is clear that
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

For arbitrary
u_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
\tilde{\nu}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
we have

T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}}(u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu})=T_{1}(u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu})=T_{1}u_{0}+T_{1}\tilde{\nu}=\overline{T}_{0}u_{0}+(T_{1}+\widetilde{T}_{1})\tilde{\nu}\,,

where we have used
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
,
T_{1}(U\tilde{\nu})=0
,
T_{1}|_{\mathcal{W}_{0}}=\overline{T}_{0}

and
\widetilde{T}_{1}\tilde{\nu}=0
.

Let us take two subspaces
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{i}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, and two
closed operators
U_{i}:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{i}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, such that

\mathcal{V}_{U_{1}}=\mathcal{V}_{U_{2}}
. This means that for an arbitrary
\tilde{\nu}_{1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{1}

there exist
u_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{2}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
such that

U_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{1}+\tilde{\nu}_{1}=u_{0}+U_{2}\tilde{\nu}_{2}+\tilde{\nu}_{2}\;.

Applying Theorem
2.2
(ix) we get
u_{0}=0
,
\tilde{\nu}_{1}=\tilde{\nu}_{2}

and
U_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{1}=U_{2}\tilde{\nu}_{2}
. Hence,
U_{1}\subseteq U_{2}
. By the symmetry we can conclude
that in fact we have
U_{1}=U_{2}
.
This proves that such
U
is unique, and by the first part we have that
U
is necessarily
given by (
4.1
).

∎

Remark 4.6
.

For
\mathcal{V}_{U}
given by (
4.2
) we can explicitly write
\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}
(i.e. the domain of

(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{U}})^{*}
; see Theorem
2.2
(xi)) in terms of the adjoint
operator
U^{*}
. For simplicity, let us elaborate on this only in the case of bounded

U:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
, i.e.
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}=p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})
is closed
(see Lemma
4.1
(ii)).
Then
U^{*}:\operatorname{ker}T_{1}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
is also bounded and

p_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]})=\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
. Moreover,

U^{*}p_{\mathrm{k}}(v)=U^{*}P_{\bar{\mathcal{G}}}p_{\mathrm{k}}(v)=\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(v)\,,\quad v\in\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\,,

where
P_{\bar{\mathcal{G}}}
and
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}
denote the orthogonal projections on

\bar{\mathcal{G}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
within spaces
(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
and
(\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
, respectively
(
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
is the closure of
\mathcal{G}=p_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathcal{V})
in
\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
).
Furthermore,
\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}
is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}:=\bigl\{v_{0}+\mu_{1}+U^{*}\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\tilde{\mu}_{2}:v_{0}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{W}_{0},\,\mu_{1}\in\bar{\mathcal{G}},

\displaystyle\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{G}^{[\perp]}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1},\,\tilde{\mu}_{2}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{[\perp]}\cap\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}\bigr\}\,.

Note that
\mathcal{G}^{[\perp]}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
is just the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{G}
within the space

(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
, and analogously for
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{[\perp]}\cap\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.

As an example, let us consider
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{W}_{0}+\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
, for which obviously we have

p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V})=\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
.
It is also easy to check that
\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}=\mathcal{W}_{0}+\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
(see
[
21
, Corollary 3.2]
).
For this
\mathcal{V}
we get
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}=\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}

and
\mathcal{G}=\{0\}
, hence both
U
and
U^{*}
are zero operators. Since
\mathcal{G}^{[\perp]}\cap\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{ker}T_{1}

and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{[\perp]}\cap\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}=\{0\}
, it is easy to read
that the above expression for
\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}
gives the right space
\mathcal{W}_{0}+\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
.

4.2.
Classification

Now we are ready to formulate and prove the main result concerning a classification of realisations (of interest) of abstract Friedrichs operators.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
be a joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators
on
\mathcal{H}
and let
T
be a closed realisation of
T_{0}
, i.e.
T_{0}\subseteq T\subseteq T_{1}
. In what follows we use
\mathcal{V}_{U}
to denote the space (
4.2
)
for a given
U
.

i)

T
is bijective if and only if there exists a bounded operator

U:\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}\to\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
such that
\operatorname{dom}T=\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

ii)

\operatorname{dom}T\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}
if and only if there exist a closed subspace

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
and a non-expansive linear operator
U:(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])\to(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
, i.e. the norm of
U
with respect to the indicated norms is less than or equal to
1
, such that
\operatorname{dom}T=\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

iii)

T
is a bijective realisation with signed boundary map
if and only if there exists a non-expansive linear operator
U:(\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])\to(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
such that
\operatorname{dom}T=\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

iv)

\operatorname{dom}T\subseteq\operatorname{dom}T^{*}
if and only if there exist closed subspaces

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and a unitary transformation

U:(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])\to(\mathcal{G},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])

(cf. Remark
3.13
) such that
\operatorname{dom}T=\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

v)

\operatorname{dom}T=\operatorname{dom}T^{*}
if and only if there exists a unitary transformation

U:(\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1},[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])\to(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
such that
\operatorname{dom}T=\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

vi)

By the mapping
U\mapsto T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{U}}
, a one-to-one correspondence between realisations
T
, i.e.
\operatorname{dom}T
, and classifying operators
U
is established in each of the above cases.

Proof.

Existence of such
U
in all parts is a direct consequence of Lemma
4.1
.
Thus, it remains to comment only the converse of each claim.

Since in all parts
U
is bounded, by Remark
4.5
we have that
\mathcal{V}_{U}
is
a closed subspace of
\mathcal{W}
containing
\mathcal{W}_{0}
. This means that

T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{U}}
is indeed a closed realisation of
T_{0}
.

In part i) it is evident that
p_{\tilde{\mathrm{k}}}(\mathcal{V}_{U})=\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
, hence we just
apply Lemma
4.1
(i) to conclude.

For parts ii) and iii) we need to show that
\mathcal{V}_{U}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{+}

(bijectivity in part iii) is again consequence of Lemma
4.1
(i); see also Remark
2.3
). For an arbitrary

u=u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{V}_{U}
we have

[\,u\mid u\,]=[\,U\tilde{\nu}\mid U\tilde{\nu}\,]+[\,\tilde{\nu}\mid\tilde{\nu}\,]\geq-[\,\tilde{\nu}\mid\tilde{\nu}\,]+[\,\tilde{\nu}\mid\tilde{\nu}\,]=0\,,

where we have used that the norm of
U
is less than or equal to 1
(see also Theorem
2.2
(ix)).

Let us recall that by Theorem
2.2
(xi) we have

\operatorname{dom}(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}_{U}})^{*}=\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}
. Thus, for parts iv) and v) we
need to show that
\mathcal{V}_{U}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}
and (only for
part v))
\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{U}
.
This can be done using Remark
4.6
, but let us present here
a direct proof.
For arbitrary
u=u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu}
and
v=v_{0}+U\tilde{\mu}+\tilde{\mu}

from
\mathcal{V}_{U}
, similarly as in the previous calculations, we have

[\,u\mid v\,]=[\,U\tilde{\nu}\mid U\tilde{\mu}\,]+[\,\tilde{\nu}\mid\tilde{\mu}\,]=-[\,\tilde{\nu}\mid\tilde{\mu}\,]+[\,\tilde{\nu}\mid\tilde{\mu}\,]=0\,,

where we have used that
U
is an isometry.
Thus,
\mathcal{V}_{U}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}
.

Let us prove now the opposite inclusion for
U
given in
part v). Let
v\in\mathcal{V}_{U}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
.
By Theorem
2.2
(ix), there exist

v_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
,
\mu\in\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
,
\tilde{\mu}\in\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
,
such that
v=v_{0}+\mu+\tilde{\mu}
. For any
u=u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu}\in\mathcal{V}_{U}
, we have

\displaystyle 0=[\,u\mid v\,]

\displaystyle=[\,U\tilde{\nu}\mid\mu\,]+[\,\tilde{\nu}\mid\tilde{\mu}\,]

\displaystyle=[\,U\tilde{\nu}\mid\mu\,]-[\,U\tilde{\nu}\mid U\tilde{\mu}\,]=[\,U\tilde{\nu}\mid\mu-U\tilde{\mu}\,]\,,

where we have used that
U
is a unitary transformation.
The identity above holds for any
\tilde{\nu}\in\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.
Since
U
is surjective and
(\operatorname{ker}T_{1},-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,])
is a Hilbert space, we get
\mu=U\tilde{\mu}
. Thus,
v\in\mathcal{V}_{U}
and hence,
V_{U}^{[\perp]}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{U}
.

Surjectivity of the map
U\mapsto T
follows from parts i)-v), while
injectivity holds by Lemma
4.1
(v).
∎

Remark 4.8
.

In
[
7
, Section 4]
Grubb’s classification was applied on
abstract Friedrichs operators, which differs significantly from the
method of the previous theorem. For instance, in the result just developed
a realisation is bijective if and only if the classifying operator is
defined on the whole kernel (Theorem
4.7
(i)),
while in the theory of
[
29
, Chapter 13]

(see also
[
7
, Theorem 17]
) the same holds for bijective
classifying operators. Another difference is that Grubb’s classification
is developed around the reference operator, while such distinguished
operator is not needed here. One can notice the same also in the
symmetric case (see e.g.
[
17
]
) when comparing von Neumann’s (
absolute
) theory
(cf.
[
37
, Section 13.2]
) and the (
relative
) theory developed
by Kreĭn, Višik and Birman (cf.
[
29
, Section 13.2]
).

If we focus on bijective realisations with signed boundary map, then
the result of part iii) of the previous theorem (see also part vi))
offers a full and explicit
characterisation contrast to
[
7
, Theorem 18]
, where
the result is optimal only when kernels are isomorphic (cf. Corollary

3.12
).

Remark 4.9
.

It is evident that parts iv) and v) of Theorem
4.7
pertain to closed skew-symmetric and skew-selfadjoint realisations of
L_{0}
(see (
3.2
)), respectively.
Furthermore, in light of Remark
3.5
, we observe that parts ii) and iii) are associated to accretive and m-accretive realisations, respectively.
Such results can readily be found in the literature for the case
S=\mathbbm{1}
, which are in complete agreement with ours (cf. Remark
4.4
). However, the theory extends for even more general nonlinear relations, following both the von Neumann approach
[
36
]

and the more recent approach based on boundary systems (or boundary quadruples)

[
8
,
40
,
42
]
. It is worth mentioning that boundary systems were introduced in
[
38
]
as a generalisation of boundary triplets, with the advantage over the latter being the applicability of the theory irrespective of the values of the deficiency indices
[
41
]
, which is the feature we also have with our results.

In conclusion, the main novelty of Theorem
4.7
lies in connecting the theory with abstract Friedrichs operators and considering general positive symmetric parts
S
, while still preserving the geometrical structure.

By Theorem
4.7
we know that the number of certain type of
realisations agrees with the number of corresponding classifying
operators
U
.
For instance, it is easy to deduce the number of isomorphisms between
Hilbert spaces. Hence, having this point of view at our hands,
we can formulate the following
straightforward quantitative generalisation of Corollary
3.12
.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
be a joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators
on
\mathcal{H}
and let us denote by
m
the cardinality of the set of all subspaces
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{W}
such that
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
,
i.e. such that
(T_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}},\widetilde{T}_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}})
is a pair of mutually adjoint bijective realisations related to
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
.

i)

If
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}\neq\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
, then
m=0
.

ii)

If
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}=0
, then
m=1
.

iii)

If
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}=1
, then
m=2
in the real case, and
m=\infty
in the complex case.

iv)

If
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}\geq 2
, then
m=\infty
.

Let us conclude the section by illustrating the obtained results with several examples.

Example 4.11
.

a)

In Example
3.11
we have commented that
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}=1
. Thus, since the problem was addressed in the real setting, by Corollary
4.10
there are two closed subspaces with the property that
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
. They are precisely
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\{-1,1\}
(see Example
3.6
).

b)

Let us consider operators from examples
3.6
and
3.11
on
(0,\infty)
, instead of the bounded interval
(a,b)
, i.e.
L_{0}u=u^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}((0,\infty);\mathbb{R})
. Then (see
[
37
, Example 3.2]
) we have
d_{+}(L_{0})=d_{+}(-iL_{0})=1
and
d_{-}(L_{0})=d_{-}(-iL_{0})=0
.
Thus, there is no closed subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
such that
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
, or in other words, there is no skew-selfadjoint realisation of the operator
-iL_{0}
.

c)

The previous example is very specific since there is also only one bijective realisation. This can be justified by Theorem
4.7
(i), but we also refer to
[
7
, Theorem 13]
.

Let us now present an example where still there is no closed subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
such that
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}
, but for which there are infinitely many bijective realisations. More precisely, we need
\min\bigl\{\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1},\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}\bigr\}\geq 1
and
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}\neq\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{C}^{2})

(all conclusions are also valid for the real case) and
\mathcal{D}=C^{\infty}_{c}((0,1);\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
For
{\sf u}\in\mathcal{D}
and

{\mathbb{A}}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1-x\end{bmatrix}

we define
T_{0}{\sf u}:=({\mathbb{A}}{\sf u})^{\prime}+{\sf u}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}{\sf u}:=-({\mathbb{A}}{\sf u})^{\prime}+{\mathbb{A}}^{\prime}{\sf u}+{\sf u}
.
It is easy to see that
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
is a joint pair of abstract Friedrichs operators (just apply Theorem
3.1
or notice that
T_{0}
is a classical Friedrichs operator
[
25
, Section 5]
).
As usual, we put
T_{1}:=\widetilde{T}_{0}^{*}
and
\widetilde{T}_{1}:=T_{0}^{*}
.
Since both
T_{1}
and
\widetilde{T}_{1}
are of a block structure, calculations of the kernels can be done by studying each component separately. More precisely,
{\sf u}=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
if and only if

\displaystyle u_{1}^{\prime}+u_{1}=0\quad\hbox{and}\quad(a_{2}u_{2})^{\prime}+u_{2}=0\,,

where
a_{2}(x):=1-x
. Thus, we can apply the available results for scalar ordinary differential equations (see e.g. the second example of
[
21
, Section 6]
).

Informally speaking, the equation above for the first component
u_{1}
contributes with 1 for both
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.
On the other hand, the second equation contributes with 1 for
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}
and 0 for
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}
.
The overall result then reads

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}T_{1}=2\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{T}_{1}=1\;,

which corresponds to what we wanted to get.

Remark 4.12
.

Another possibility to connect joint pairs of abstract Friedrichs operators
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
with symmetric operators is to study the operator matrix

J_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&T_{0}\\
\widetilde{T}_{0}&0\end{bmatrix}

on
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
, with
\operatorname{dom}J_{0}=\mathcal{D}\oplus\mathcal{D}
.
Indeed,
J_{0}
is symmetric (cf.
[
33
, Theorem 2.17]
) and

J_{1}:=J_{0}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}0&T_{1}\\
\widetilde{T}_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\,,\quad\operatorname{dom}J_{1}=\mathcal{W}\oplus\mathcal{W}\,.

Moreover,
d_{+}(J_{0})=d_{-}(J_{0})
(
[
33
, Theorem 2.20]
) and it is easy to see that all self-adjoint realisations of
J_{0}
are given by
J_{1}|_{\mathcal{V}^{[\perp]}\oplus\mathcal{V}}
(see Theorem
2.2
(xi)), where
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
is a closed subspace containing
\mathcal{W}_{0}
(or equivalently
\mathcal{D}
).

5.
Symmetric case

In this last part of the paper we focus on symmetric operators
and present several results that can be directly extracted from the
theory just developed.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
A
be a densely defined symmetric operator on
\mathcal{H}

and let
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
be bounded self-adjoint linear operators such that
S_{2}
is in
addition strictly positive. Define an indefinite inner product on
\operatorname{dom}A^{*}
by

[\,u\mid v\,]_{A}:=i\Bigl(\langle\,A^{*}u\mid v\,\rangle-\langle\,u\mid A^{*}v\,\rangle\Bigr)\;,\quad u,v\in\operatorname{dom}A^{*}\;.

(5.1)

Then we have the following.

i)

It holds

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}-iS_{2})=d_{+}(A)\quad\hbox{and}\quad\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}+iS_{2})=d_{-}(A)\,,

where
d_{\pm}(A)
denote deficiency indices of
A
(cf.
[
37
, Section 3.1]
).

ii)

\operatorname{dom}A^{*}=\operatorname{dom}\overline{A}\dotplus\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}-iS_{2})\dotplus\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}+iS_{2})
,
where the sums are direct and all spaces on the right-hand side
are pairwise
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A}
-orthogonal.

iii)

There is one-to-one correspondence between all closed symmetric realisations
of
A
and all unitary transformations
U
between any closed isomorphic subspaces
of
(\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}+iS_{2}),[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A})
and
(\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}-iS_{2}),-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A})
,
respectively.

iv)

There is one-to-one correspondence between all self-adjoint realisations
of
A
and all unitary transformations
U:(\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}+iS_{2}),[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A})\to(\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-S_{1}-iS_{2}),-[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A})
.

v)

Correspondences of parts iii) and iv) can be expressed by
U\mapsto A_{U}=A^{*}|_{\operatorname{dom}A_{U}}
, where

\operatorname{dom}A_{U}:=\bigl\{u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu}:u_{0}\in\operatorname{dom}\overline{A},\,\tilde{\nu}\in\operatorname{dom}U\bigr\}\,,

and
A_{U}(u_{0}+U\tilde{\nu}+\tilde{\nu})=\overline{A}u_{0}+(S_{1}+iS_{2})\tilde{\nu}+(S_{1}-iS_{2})U\tilde{\nu}
.

Proof.

If we define
T_{0}:=iA-iS_{1}+S_{2}
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}:=-iA+iS_{1}+S_{2}
,
then the pair
(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})

is a joint pair of abstract Friedirchs operators by Theorem
3.1
.
Moreover, corresponding indefinite inner product (
2.1
)
agrees with
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A}
(see Remark
3.7
).

Therefore, the statements of the corollary follow from
Lemma
3.8
, Theorem
2.2
(ix) and
Theorem
4.7
(note that
\operatorname{dom}A_{U}
agrees with (
4.2
) for the above choice of

(T_{0},\widetilde{T}_{0})
).
∎

If
S_{i}=\alpha_{i}\mathbbm{1}
,
i=1,2
, where
\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha_{2}>0
,
then the statement of the previous theorem is well-known and can be found
in many textbooks on unbounded linear operators. For instance, in
[
37
]

part i) is present in Section 3.1, part ii) in Proposition 3.7 (von Neumann’s formula)
and parts iii)-v) are studied in Section 13.2 as part of the von Neumann extension theory
(see also
[
19
, Chapter X]
).
Moreover, the correspondence given in part v) completely agrees with the one of

[
37
, Theorem 13.9]
since for this choice of bounded operators
S_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
the same
U
represents a unitary transformation when the standard inner product of the
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is considered (see Remark
4.4
).

Let us just remark that the geometrical point of view provided in part ii), i.e. orthogonality
with respect to
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A}
, is something that is not commonly present,
although
[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A}
is (up to a multiplicative constant).
More precisely, in
[
37
, Definition 3.4]
(see also Lemma 3.5 there)
the indefinite inner product
-i[\,\cdot\mid\cdot\,]_{A}

is referred to as the
boundary form
and it is an important part of
the extension theory of boundary triplets (
[
37
, Chapter 14]
and
[
29
, Section 13.4]
; see also
[
38
]
for more general boundary systems).
A more advanced study of boundary forms for Hilbert complexes can be found in a recent work
[
30
]
.

Of course, in the standard theory of symmetric operators it is usually satisfactory to observe only the case
S_{1}=0
and
S_{1}=\mathbbm{1}
. Thus, the preceding corollary may seem like an excessive technical complication.
Here we want to stress one more time that our primary focus was in developing a classification result for abstract Friedrichs operators where such approach can be justified, e.g. by perceiving that not all bijective realisations of
T_{0}=L_{0}+S
correspond to skew-symmetric realisations of
L_{0}
(see Section
4
).
Therefore, our intention is to see the last corollary principally as a way to connect two theories, while an additional abstraction can sometimes offer a better sense of the underlying structure.
