Entanglement in quantum field theory via wavelet representations
Daniel J. George, Yuval R. Sanders, Mohsen Bagherimehrab, Barry C. Sanders, Gavin K. Brennen

Abstract
Quantum field theory (QFT) describes nature using continuous fields, but physical properties of QFT are usually revealed in terms of measurements of observables at a finite resolution. We describe a multiscale representation of free scalar bosonic and Ising model fermionic QFTs using wavelets. Making use of the orthogonality and self-similarity of the wavelet basis functions, we demonstrate some well-known relations such as scale-dependent subsystem entanglement entropy and renormalization of correlations in the ground state. We also find some new applications of the wavelet transform as a compressed representation of ground states of QFTs which can be used to illustrate quantum phase transitions via fidelity overlap and holographic entanglement of purification.



I
Introduction

Quantum information has provided new perspectives into quantum field
theories (QFT), such as using entanglement as a way to characterize
quantum phases
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
, and quantum algorithms for simulating
scattering cross sections in QFTs that are exponentially faster than
classical algorithms
Jordan et al. 2012
.
Other insights include the study of coarse-graining and renormalization
from a quantum information perspective
Vidal 2008
, and the harvesting
of entanglement from vacuum states of QFTs
Reznik 2003
;
Henderson and Menicucci 2020
.
Recently, it was shown that quantum field theories can be
represented in a way that organizes properties at multiple scales
using a wavelet functional basis, referred to as a
multiscale representation
Bulut and Polyzou 2013
;
Brennen et al. 2015
.
Wavelet-based multiscale representations of QFT have proved
particularly well suited for studying the holographic principle

Qi 2013
;
Lee and Qi 2016
;
Lee 2017
;
Singh and Brennen 2016
and renormalization physics
Evenbly and White 2016
;
Altaisky 2018
.

Several recent works have demonstrated a connection between wavelets
and tensor-network- or quantum-circuit-based representations of
quantum states.
For example,
Evenbly and White 2016
used a Daubechies wavelet basis to
analytically construct the tensors in a multiscale entanglement
renormalization ansatz (MERA) description of a ground state of
massless (critical) fermions on a 1D lattice.

Haegeman et al. 2018
showed how to rigorously construct quantum circuits
that approximate metallic states of massless fermions on 1D and 2D
lattices based on a discrete wavelet transform using an approximate
Hilbert pair.
For quadratic bosonic systems on a lattice,
Witteveen and Walter 2021
developed
a scale-invariant entanglement renormalization procedure based on
biorthogonal wavelets that disentangles the wavelet output
at each step.
Finally,
Witteveen et al. 2022
found a procedure for constructing MERA-based
quantum circuits that rigorously approximate the continuum correlation
functions for the massless Dirac conformal field theory.

In this paper we derive the wavelet-based multiscale representations of
two types of QFT: the one-dimensional Ising fermionic QFT and free
scalar bosonic QFT, both introduced in
Section

II
.
Wavelet-based multiscale representations can be understood as a
more nuanced form of discretization, in which the continuum Hamiltonian
is expressed as an infinite number of terms corresponding to ever-finer
length scales.
A minimum length scale is then enforced by the truncation of terms at
finer length scales.
We demonstrate that a number of established results remain valid
when using these representations, and suggest
some advantages of such representations for identifying phase transitions.
We present a brief introduction to the relevant aspects of the
wavelet formalism in
Section

III
.

The main results of our paper are contained in
Section

IV
.
We show that wavelet-based multiscale representations provide
natural access to entanglement renormalization physics.
In
Section

IV.1
we show numerically that the two-point
correlators of a coarse-grained QFT decay algebraically in a
scale-invariant manner at the critical point and with an exponential
decay with correlation length given by the inverse renormalized mass in
the massive phase.
In
Section

IV.2
we reproduce using a
wavelet-based discretization the results of
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
for
subsystem entanglement in noninteracting bosonic and fermionic QFTs.

Calabrese and Cardy 2004
model the QFT with a lattice spin system that is treated
as a discrete approximation to the true continuum theory, justifiable
by computing a continuum limit (see, e.g., Sec. II in
Boyanovsky 1989
).
We demonstrate that the discretization of these field theories using
wavelet scale modes reproduces the correct scaling of entanglement
in both gapped and gapless phases of the theories and we connect the
phenomenological cutoff length to the scale of our scale modes.

In
Section

IV.4
we consider a multiscale wavelet
representation of the ground state for a fermionic Ising QFT, and
show that selection of a subsystem consisting of a small number of
coarse-grained modes amounts to a form of lossy compression, capturing the
physics of the global pure state up to some error.
We demonstrate the utility of this approach for approximating the
fidelity overlap between ground states adjacent in some parameter,
and therefore as a witness for quantum phase transitions,
where the direct calculation or measurement of fidelity over the global
state may be computationally or experimentally infeasible.
In
Section

IV.5
we show that
the entanglement of purification for a reduced quantum state, the
calculation of which quickly becomes unwieldy for large numbers of
modes, can be well approximated by a coarse-grained state.
This is significant in the context of the work by
Umemoto and Takayanagi 2018
,
in which the authors conjecture that the entanglement of purification in
conformal field theories (CFTs) is equal to the minimal-area cross section
of the entanglement wedge.
Finally, in
Sections

V
and
VI
we
summarize our results and conclude with an outlook for further
applications of our methodology.

II
Background

We focus here on noninteracting one-dimensional fermionic and
bosonic quantum field theories, due to their mathematical simplicity
and frequent use as a starting point for perturbative models,
especially in quantum algorithms
Jordan et al. 2012
;
Brennen et al. 2015
.
They are exactly solvable and therefore allow for direct comparison
of wavelet-based results to known continuum physics.

II.1
Ising fermionic continuum QFT

The Hamiltonian density for the free Ising model fermionic quantum field
theory in one dimension is (see Eq. (11a) in
Boyanovsky 1989
)

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{f}}(x,t):=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(-\mathrm{i}\hat{\bm{b}}^{T}(x,t)\bm{Z}\partial_{x}\hat{\bm{b}}(x,t)+m_{0}\hat{\bm{b}}^{T}(x,t)\bm{Y}\hat{\bm{b}}(x,t)\bigr),

(1)

where
\hat{\bm{b}}(x,t)\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\hat{b}_{0}(x,t)\\
\hat{b}_{1}(x,t)\end{bmatrix}

is the spinor of Majorana mode operators at location
x
,
m_{0}
is
the bare mass, and
\bm{Z}=\smallmatrixquantity[\lx@physics@smallmatrix\pmat{3}\endlx@physics@smallmatrix]
and

\bm{Y}=0&-\smallmatrixquantity[\lx@physics@smallmatrix\pmat{2}\endlx@physics@smallmatrix]\\
i&0
are the usual Pauli matrices.
Note that the spinor components satisfy the equal-time Majorana
anticommutation relation

\anticommutator{\hat{b}_\sigma(x)}{\hat{b}_{\sigma'} (x')}=2\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\delta(x-x^{\prime})

(2)

for
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
.
The Majorana mode operators have units of inverse square root of length.
In the massless phase, the theory is described by the Ising model CFT
with central charge
c=1/2
.

II.2
Free scalar bosonic continuum QFT

The Hamiltonian density for the free scalar bosonic quantum
field theory in
d
spatial dimensions is (see Eq. (11) in
Brennen et al. 2015
)

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{b}}(\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\Pi}^{2}(\bm{x},t)+\left(\nabla\hat{\Phi}(\bm{x},t)\right)^{2}+m_{0}^{2}\hat{\Phi}^{2}(\bm{x},t)\right),

(3)

where the field operator
\hat{\Phi}(\bm{x},t)
and its conjugate
momentum
\hat{\Pi}(\bm{x},t):=\partial_{t}\hat{\Phi}(\bm{x},t)

satisfy the canonical equal-time commutation relations

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\Phi}(\bm{x},t),\hat{\Pi}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t)\right]=\mathrm{i}\updelta^{(d)}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\mathds{1},\text{ and}

(4)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\Phi}(\bm{x},t),\hat{\Phi}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t)\right]=\left[\hat{\Pi}(\bm{x},t),\hat{\Pi}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t)\right]=0.

(5)

Most of the results below are for the
d=1
case where the field
operator is dimensionless.
In the massless phase, the theory is described by the free bosonic
CFT with central charge
c=1
.

II.3
Entanglement entropy scaling

An important physical characterization of QFT is given by
subsystem entanglement of ground states.
The entanglement entropy of a bipartite pure state

\ket{\psi}_{AB}
is given by the von Neumann
entropy:
S_{A}=-\Tr(\rho_A \log\rho_A)
, where the subsystem
state with support on region
A
is

\rho_{A}=\Tr_{B}[\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}_{AB}]
.
For most of the work here, the QFT is assumed to be in one spatial
dimension over the compact interval
[0,X)

with specified boundary conditions.
A subsystem
A
consists of a single subinterval
[0,X_{A})

of the compact interval
[0,X)
.
The relevant results are given in
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
and
Holzhey et al. 1994
:

\displaystyle S_{A}^{\text{critical, periodic}}(x)=\frac{c}{3}\log\left(\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi\epsilon}\right)+\text{constant}

(6)

\displaystyle S_{A}^{\text{critical, open}}(x)=\frac{c}{6}\log\left(\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi\epsilon}\right)+\text{constant}

(7)

\displaystyle S_{A}^{\text{noncritical}}(x)=\text{(\# boundary points)}\times\frac{c}{6}\log(m_0 \epsilon)

(8)

which correspond to entropy scaling in the massless (critical) case, for
periodic (see Eq. (1) in
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
) and open boundary
conditions (see Eq. (2) in
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
), and in the massive (noncritical)
case (see Eq. (1) in
Calabrese and Cardy 2004
).
Here
\epsilon
is the ultraviolet cutoff length,

c
the central charge of the relevant CFT, and
m_{0}
the mass.
Note also that
Eq.

8
is valid only
for the subsystem length
X_{A}\gg 1/m_{0}
.

III
Wavelet-based discretization of quantum field theory

III.1
Definition

Here we use the Daubechies family of wavelets, a family indexed
by a positive integer
\mathcal{K}
(with
\mathcal{K}=1
corresponding
to the well-known Haar wavelet), which has additional beneficial
properties such as compactness, allowing study of spatially separated
operators and zero-valued moments.
The Daubechies family of compactly supported wavelets can be defined
as follows.
For an alternative introduction, see Sec. 2 in
Beylkin 1992
, or for
a more thorough treatment, see Chap. 7 in
Mallat 2009
or Chap. 5
in
Daubechies 1992
.

The wavelet basis is defined in terms of a pair of functions,
the scale and wavelet functions, here denoted by
s

and
w
respectively.
Elsewhere these are sometimes referred to as the father and mother
wavelets and denoted
\phi
and
\psi
, respectively.

A function
s\in\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
is called a scale function if it
satisfies the orthonormality condition

\forall\ell\in\mathds{Z}:\quad\int_{\mathds{R}}s(x)\,s(x-\ell)\differential{x}=\delta_{0,\ell}

(9)

and if, for any other function
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
,

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{a.e.}}}{{=}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\sum_{\ell\in\mathds{Z}}\sqrt{2^{r}}\,c_{\ell}^{(r)}\,s\!\left(2^{r}x-\ell\right),\text{ with}

(10)

\displaystyle c_{\ell}^{(r)}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2^{r}}\int_{\mathds{R}}f(x)\,s\!\left(2^{r}x-\ell\right)\differential{x},

(11)

where the symbol
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{a.e.}}}{{=}}
means “equal almost
everywhere,” which is to say that
f(x)
is equal to the right-hand
side for all
x
except for a measure-zero set.
For notational convenience, we denote the scale function at
scale (or resolution)
r
and position
\ell
by

\forall r,\ell\in\mathds{Z}:\;s_{\ell}(x):=s(x-\ell),\;s_{\ell}^{(r)}(x):=\sqrt{2^{r}}\,s_{\ell}\!\left(2^{r}x\right).

(12)

The scale and wavelet functions at scale
r
are defined recursively
as a linear combination of scale functions at scale
r+1
, with
weights given by the set of scale filter coefficients
\{h_{\ell}\}
,

\ell\in\mathds{Z}
:

\displaystyle s_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}}h_{\ell^{\prime}}s_{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}^{(r+1)}(x),

(13)

\displaystyle w_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}}(-1)^{\ell^{\prime}}h_{\Lambda-\ell^{\prime}-1}s_{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}^{(r+1)}(x)

(14)

where
\Lambda
is the number of nonzero filter coefficients such
that
h_{\ell}=0
if
\ell<0
or
\ell\geq\Lambda
,
and a similar notational convention has been adopted for the
wavelet functions as in
Eq.

12
.
Specification of these coefficients uniquely determines the
wavelet basis.

For the Daubechies-
\mathcal{K}
(db
\mathcal{K}
) wavelet, the scale filter
coefficients
\{h_{\ell}\}
are uniquely determined for
\mathcal{K}=1
,
and up to reflection for any integer
\mathcal{K}>1
, by requiring
simultaneously that the first
\mathcal{K}
moments vanish:

\int\differential{x}w_{0}^{(r)}(x)\,x^{p}=0,\quad p=0,1,\ldots,\mathcal{K}-1,

(15)

and that the number of nonzero coefficients,
\Lambda
, is minimized.
It turns out that this occurs for
\Lambda=2\mathcal{K}
filter
coefficients.
Additionally, it can be shown that the scale and wavelet functions at
scale
0
are supported on the interval
[0,2\mathcal{K}-1]
, and that
the first differentiable scale function is the scale function of the
db
3
wavelet (see page 239 in
Daubechies 1992
), hence its predominant use in this
paper.

In addition to
Eq.

9
, the scale and
wavelet functions further obey the orthonormality properties

\displaystyle\forall\ell,\ell^{\prime},r,r^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}\text{ s.t. }r^{\prime}\geq r:\;\int_{\mathds{R}}s_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)\,w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(r^{\prime})}(x)\differential{x}=0,

(16)

\displaystyle\forall\ell,\ell^{\prime},r,r^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}:\int_{\mathds{R}}w_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)\,w_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(r^{\prime})}(x)\differential{x}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{r,r^{\prime}}.

(17)

The fixed-resolution subspace
\mathcal{S}_{r}
at
resolution
r\in\mathds{Z}
, along with the associated wavelet
subspace
\mathcal{W}_{r}
, can be defined in terms of scale and wavelet
functions at resolution
r
:

\mathcal{S}_{r}:=\spn\left\{s_{\ell}^{(r)}\,\middle|\,\ell\in\mathds{Z}\right\},\quad\mathcal{W}_{r}:=\spn\left\{w_{\ell}^{(r)}\,\middle|\,\ell\in\mathds{Z}\right\}

(18)

where
\mathcal{S}_{r},\mathcal{W}_{r}\subset\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
.

Equation

14
implies that

\mathcal{S}_{r},\mathcal{W}_{r}\subset\mathcal{S}_{r+1}
, and
from this and the orthogonality conditions in

Eq.

17
, it follows that the
space
\mathcal{W}_{r}
is precisely the orthogonal complement
of
\mathcal{S}_{r}
in
\mathcal{S}_{r+1}
, and therefore

\mathcal{S}_{r}=\mathcal{S}_{r-1}\oplus\mathcal{W}_{r-1}.

(19)

The wavelet transform in one dimension at scale
r
is defined as
the isomorphism

\bm{W}^{(r)}:\mathcal{S}_{r}\to\mathcal{S}_{r-1}\oplus\mathcal{W}_{r-1}

(20)

which functions as the basis transform

\left\{s_{\ell}^{(r)}\right\}\to\left\{s_{\ell}^{(r-1)}\right\}\cup\left\{w_{\ell}^{(r-1)}\right\},\quad\ell\in\mathds{Z}.

(21)

The
d
-level wavelet transform for
d>1
is defined by the
recursive application of
\bm{W}^{(r)}
resulting in

\bm{W}^{(r)}_{d}:\mathcal{S}_{r}\to\mathcal{S}_{r-d}\oplus\mathcal{W}_{r-d}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{W}_{r-1}

(22)

which functions as the basis transform

\begin{split}\left\{s_{\ell}^{(r)}\right\}&\to\left\{s_{\ell}^{(r-d)}\right\}\cup\left\{w_{\ell}^{(r-d)}\right\}\cup\left\{w_{\ell}^{(r-d+1)}\right\}\\
&\qquad\cup\cdots\cup\left\{w_{\ell}^{(r-1)}\right\},\ell\in\mathds{Z}.\end{split}

(23)

In this paper we identify
r=0
with the coarsest scale modes and
consider scales
0\leq r<n
, and therefore make use of
the
n
-level wavelet transform acting at scale
n
:

\bm{W}^{(n)}_{n}:\mathcal{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{W}_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{W}_{n-1}.

(24)

The numeric construction of the single-level and multilevel discrete
wavelet transform from wavelet coefficients is discussed in
detail in
Bagherimehrab et al. 2022
(see Appendix A in
Bagherimehrab et al. 2022
).

III.2
Fixed-resolution and multiresolution representations

It is useful to define a fixed-scale representation in terms of the
action of an idempotent projection operator.
Define for each scale
r
the projection operator mapping from the
vector space
\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
to the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{r}

defined in
Eq.

18
:

\operatorname{proj}_{r}:\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)\to\mathcal{S}_{r}:f\mapsto\sum_{\ell\in\mathds{Z}}\innerproduct{s_\ell^{(r)}}{f}s_{\ell}^{(r)}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathds{Z}}c_{\ell}^{(r)}s_{\ell}^{(r)}

(25)

where
\ket{s_\ell^{(r)}}
are the scale functions
in
Eq.

14

with
\ell\in\mathds{Z}
and the inner product corresponds to
the coefficients
c_{\ell}^{(r)}
defined
in
Eq.

11
.
This projection operator is discussed in more depth by
Daubechies 1992
.

Both fermionic and bosonic Hamiltonians include the action of a
derivative operator, the projection of which requires some care
since the derivative is not strictly in
\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
.
Specifically, the
\alpha
-order derivative

\derivative[\alpha]{x}
acts only upon a proper vector (but not
Hilbert) subspace of
\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
corresponding to the set of
functions whose derivatives up to
\alpha
order also belong to

\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
.
The projected derivative operator is

\evaluated{\dv[\alpha]{}{x}}_{r}:=\operatorname{proj}_{r}\circ\derivative[\alpha]{x}\circ\operatorname{proj}_{r},

(26)

subject to the requirement that
f
is a square-integrable function
with
\alpha
continuous and square-integrable derivatives.
Wavelet analysis of these Hamiltonians is therefore restricted to
scale functions with the requisite properties.
The
\alpha
-order derivative of an arbitrary
\alpha
-order
differentiable function
f
is then

\evaluated{\dv[\alpha]{f}{x}}_{r}=\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\innerproduct{s_\ell^{(r)}}{f}\matrixelement{s_{\ell'}^{(r)}}{\dv[\alpha]{}{x}}{s_\ell^{(r)}}s_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(r)}

(27)

and the action of
\evaluated{\dv[\alpha]{}{x}}_{r}
is entirely
determined by the coefficients

\displaystyle\matrixelement{s_{\ell'}^{(r)}}{\dv[\alpha]{}{x}}{s_\ell^{(r)}}

\displaystyle=\int\differential{x}s_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(r)}(x)\derivative[\alpha]{x}s_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)

(28)

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha r}\int\differential{x}s_{\ell^{\prime}-\ell}(x)\derivative[\alpha]{x}s(x)

(29)

\displaystyle=:2^{\alpha r}\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{\ell^{\prime}-\ell}

(30)

henceforth referred to as the derivative overlap coefficients.

Beylkin
Beylkin 1992
showed how to compute the derivative overlap
coefficients for any scale functions specified by filter
coefficients
h_{\ell},\;\ell\in\{0,\ldots,2\mathcal{K}-1\}
(all
other coefficients are set to zero) by first defining the
autocorrelation coefficients (see Eq. (3.19) in
Beylkin 1992
)

a_{\ell}:=2\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}h_{\ell}h_{\ell^{\prime}}.

(31)

Subject to a condition on the number of vanishing moments of the
resulting wavelet function, Beylkin proved (see Eqs. (4.3)-(4.4)
in
Beylkin 1992
) that the derivative overlap coefficients

\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{\ell}
constitute the unique solution to the system
of equations

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{\ell}&=2^{\alpha}\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{2\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}a_{2k-1}\\
&\qquad\times\left(\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{2\ell-2k+1}+\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{2\ell+2k-1}\right);\end{split}

(32)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=-2\mathcal{K}+2}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\ell^{\alpha}\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\alpha}\alpha!

(33)

and for a Daubechies wavelet it can be shown
that (see Eqs. (3.51)-(3.52) in
Beylkin 1992
)

\begin{split}a_{2n-1}&=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{(\mathcal{K}-n)!(\mathcal{K}+n-1)!(2n-1)}\\
&\qquad\times\left(\frac{(2\mathcal{K}-1)!}{4^{\mathcal{K}-1}}(\mathcal{K}-1)!\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(34)

Note that these autocorrelation coefficients are rational, from which
it follows that the derivative overlap
coefficients
\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{\ell}
are also rational.

III.3
One-dimensional Ising fermionic QFT

A wavelet-based multiscale representation of a continuum Hamiltonian
over a length interval
[0,X)
consists of a (countably) infinite sum
of terms.
Initial terms correspond to the scale modes at the coarsest length scale
(scale
r=0
, length of order
2^{-r}=1
), and progress to wavelet modes
at finer and finer length scales (down to scale
r=\infty
).
A minimum scale
n
(length of order
2^{-n}
) is applied by truncating
terms corresponding to scales
r>n
, and is equivalent to projecting the
Hamiltonian onto the scale subspace
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
The number of modes
V
in the system is then

V:=2^{n}X.

(35)

Equivalently, the Hamiltonian of a system can be directly expressed
in terms of
V
scale modes at scale
n
and the multiscale representation
from scales
0
to
n
is then recovered via the application of the

n
-level wavelet transform.
This latter process is superficially similar to discretization, and it
is often easier to use the language of discretization
(e.g. “wavelet-discretized modes”); however it should be remembered
that the underlying concepts are distinct.

The scale Majorana modes spanning the scale subspace
\mathcal{S}_{r}

are defined in terms of the continuum modes like so:

\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}^{(r;\text{s})}:=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{b}_{\ell,0}^{(r;\text{s})}\\
\hat{b}_{\ell,1}^{(r;\text{s})}\end{bmatrix},\quad\hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma}^{(r;\text{s})}:=\int_{0}^{X}\differential{x}s_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)\hat{b}_{\sigma}(x).

(36)

In the fermionic case we consider antiperiodic boundary conditions,
which correspond to the even parity sector.
The discretized Majorana modes
\hat{b}^{(r;\text{s})}_{\ell,\sigma}
satisfy
anticommutation relations analogous to those
of
Eq.

2

but with the Dirac delta replaced with a Kronecker delta.
Also note that our restriction to a finite-sized subspace of

\mathcal{L}^{2}\left(\mathds{R}\right)
ensures that only finitely many of these discretized
Majorana operators are nonzero.

The wavelet Majorana modes are defined analogously by

\hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma}^{(r;\text{w})}:=\int_{0}^{X}\differential{x}w_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)\hat{b}_{\sigma}(x),

(37)

and complement the scale-discretized Majorana
modes
\hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma}^{(r;\text{s})}
.

To project the Hamiltonian from
Eq.

1

to the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{n}
defined in

Eq.

18
corresponding to the
coarse-graining scale
n
, conjugate
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{f}}

with the canonical projection operator
\operatorname{proj}(n)

from
Eq.

25
.
This results in the discrete Hamiltonian

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{(n)}_{\text{f}}&=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}}2^{n}\Delta^{(1)}_{\ell^{\prime}}\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}^{(n;s)}{}^{\intercal}\bm{Z}\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}^{(n;s)}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}m_{0}\sum_{\ell\in\mathds{Z}}\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}^{(n;s)}{}^{\intercal}\bm{Y}\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}^{(n;s)},\end{split}

(38)

where the symbol
\Delta^{(\alpha)}_{\ell}
refers to
the
\ell
th derivative overlap coefficient of order
\alpha

as defined in
Eq.

30
,
and
m_{0}
is the bare (unrenormalized) mass at scale
n
and should
be chosen accordingly.

The quadratic structure of the Hamiltonian can be made explicit by
reexpressing
\hat{H}^{(n)}_{\text{f}}
as

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(n)}_{\text{f}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}\\
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}Q_{\ell,\sigma;\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}^{(n)}\hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma}^{(n;s)}\hat{b}_{\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}^{(n;s)},

(39)

\displaystyle Q_{\ell,\sigma;\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{\sigma}2^{n}\Delta^{(1)}_{\ell^{\prime}-\ell}\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}+m_{0}\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma).

(40)

Note that

Q_{\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime};\ell,\sigma}^{(n)}=-Q_{\ell,\sigma;\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}^{(n)}

because
\Delta^{(1)}_{-\ell}=-\Delta^{(1)}_{\ell}
.

The coefficients
Q_{\ell,\sigma;\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}^{(n)}
are
effectively the entries of a
2V\times 2V
matrix
\bm{Q}^{(n)}

acting on a vector space with basis vectors indexed by
(\ell,\sigma)
.
The matrix
\bm{Q}^{(n)}
is real and antisymmetric and hence has
pure imaginary
eigenvalues
\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}^{(n)},k\in\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\ldots,V-\frac{1}{2}\}

where
\omega_{k}^{(n)}\in\mathds{R}
.
To find these eigenvalues, observe that due to the applied
antiperiodic boundary conditions, the submatrix consisting of
entries
Q_{\ell,0;\ell^{\prime},0}^{(n)}
is the antiperiodic analog
of a circulant matrix, with a negative sign applied to the entries
below the main diagonal.
Hence it can be diagonalized using a half-integer-indexed discrete
Fourier transform, resulting in submatrix eigenvalues

\lambda_{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}2^{n}\Delta_{\ell}^{(1)}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\pi k\ell/V}=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}2^{n+1}\Delta_{\ell}^{(1)}\sin(\frac{2 \pi k \ell}{V})

(41)

for
k\in\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\ldots,V-\frac{1}{2}\}
,
where the second equality is obtained by using the antisymmetry and
periodicity of the derivative overlap coefficients
\Delta_{\ell}^{(1)}

and noting that
\Delta_{0}^{(1)}=0
.
Similarly the eigenvalues of the submatrix
Q_{\ell,1;\ell^{\prime},1}

are
-\lambda_{k}
.
Therefore
\bm{Q}^{(k)}
is unitarily equivalent to the direct
sum of
V

2\!\times\!2
matrices:

\bm{Q}^{(n)}\sim\bigoplus_{k=\frac{1}{2}}^{V-\frac{1}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{k}&-m_{0}\\
m_{0}&-\lambda_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(42)

The eigenvalues of each
2\!\times\!2
matrix are then

\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}:=\pm\sqrt{-m_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}

and so

\omega_{k}^{(n)}=\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}2^{n+1}\Delta_{\ell}^{(1)}\sin(\frac{2\pi k \ell}{V})\right)^{2}}.

(43)

In the continuum limit as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\to\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\left(\frac{4\pi k}{X}\sum_{\ell}\ell\Delta_{\ell}^{(1)}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\left(\frac{2\pi k}{X}\right)^{2}},

(44)

where the first simplification uses the small-angle approximation
for sine and the second the antisymmetry of the derivative
overlap coefficients
\Delta_{\ell}^{(1)}
together
with
Eq.

33
.
This recovers the well-known dispersion relation for the continuum
Ising model.

The projected Hamiltonian
in
Eq.

39
is quadratic
in the fermionic operators.
Therefore, its ground state is a Gaussian state.
Any Gaussian state is fully characterized by its covariance matrix.
For a fermionic system, this matrix is obtained from the two-point
correlations of the system’s fermionic operators
Eisert et al. 2010
.

The entries of the covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}
for
the ground state of the projected Hamiltonian are defined so that

\Gamma^{(n)}_{\ell,\sigma;\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}:=\mathrm{i}\expectationvalue{\left[\hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma}^{(n;s)}\,,
\hat{b}_{\ell',\sigma'}^{(n;s)}\right]}

(45)

where
\expectationvalue{\cdot}:=\Tr(\cdot\rho)
.
\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}
can be
calculated by first observing that entries of the covariance
matrix
\bar{\bm{\Gamma}}
for the uncoupled Majorana operators

\hat{\bar{\bm{b}}}
are

\bar{\Gamma}_{\ell,\sigma;\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathrm{i}\expectationvalue{\left[\hat{\bar{b}}_{\ell,\sigma}\,,
\hat{\bar{b}}_{\ell',\sigma'}\right]}=\updelta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}).

(46)

which follows from the definition of the ground state and from the
anticommutation relations for Majorana fermions.

Let
\bm{O}^{(n)}
be the orthogonal transformation matrix that
uncouples Majorana operators at scale
n
:

\hat{\bar{\bm{b}}}:=\bm{O}^{(n)}\hat{\bm{b}}^{(n;s)}
.
Then

\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}=\bm{O}^{(n)\intercal}\bm{\bar{\Gamma}}\bm{O}^{(n)}.

(47)

Calculation of
\bm{O}
is via symplectic diagonalization

1
1

1

In the case that the eigenvalues of
\bm{Q}^{(n)}
are all
nonzero, numeric construction of
\bm{O}^{(n)}
is simple: of the
eigenvectors of
\bm{Q}^{(n)}
having eigenvalues
\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}
,
let
\{\bm{w}_{k}\}
be all the eigenvectors corresponding to
eigenvalues of either
+\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}
or
-\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}
.
The rows of
\bm{O}^{(n)}
are then the normalized real and imaginary
components of
\bm{w}_{k}
, that is,

\real\bm{w}_{k}/\lVert\real\bm{w}_{k}\rVert
followed by

\imaginary\bm{w}_{k}/\lVert\imaginary\bm{w}_{k}\lVert
.

of the coupling matrix
\bm{Q}^{(n)}
which takes the analogous form:

\bm{Q}^{(n)}=\bm{O}^{(n)\intercal}\begin{bmatrix}0&\bm{\Omega}\\
-\bm{\Omega}&0\end{bmatrix}\bm{O}^{(n)},

(48)

where
\bm{\Omega}
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal
entries
\omega_{k}
.

Our next step is to approximate the fine-scale correlator

\expectationvalue{ \hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma}^{(n; s)}
\hat{b}_{\ell',\sigma'}^{(n; s)}}
.
As this is an expectation value of anticommuting operators, its value is
zero whenever
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
.
Translational symmetry requires that

\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{\ell, 0}^{(n; s)} \hat{b}_{\ell', 1}^{(n; s)}}=\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0,0}^{(n; s)} \hat{b}_{\ell' - \ell,1}^{(n; s)}}
,
and together with anticommutation it further follows that

\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(n; s)} \hat{b}_{-\ell, 1}^{(n; s)}}=-\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(n; s)} \hat{b}_{\ell, 1}^{(n; s)}}
.
We derive expressions for the correlators in

.

III.4
One-dimensional bosonic QFT

To obtain a multiscale representation of the bosonic continuum field
theory, we again first define the projection of the bosonic field and
conjugate momentum operators onto the scale subspace
\mathcal{S}_{r}
.
Let

\hat{\Phi}^{(r)}_{\ell}(t):=\int_{0}^{X}\differential{x}s_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)\hat{\Phi}(x,t)

(49)

and

\hat{\Pi}^{(r)}_{\ell}(t):=\int_{0}^{X}\differential{x}s_{\ell}^{(r)}(x)\hat{\Pi}(x,t).

(50)

be the canonical position and momentum field operators projected onto
the scale subspace
\mathcal{S}_{r}
.
The scale field and conjugate momentum operators satisfy commutation
relations analogous to those of
Eq.

4

but with the Dirac delta replaced with the Kronecker delta.
The wavelet field and conjugate momentum operators are defined
analogously to

Eqs.

36
and
37
.

Projecting the bosonic Hamiltonian
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{b}}(x,t)

from
Eq.

3
to a scale subspace

\mathcal{S}_{n}
with the projection operator
from
Eq.

25
results in

\hat{H}^{(n)}_{\text{b}}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in\mathds{Z}}\hat{\Pi}_{\ell}^{(n;\text{s})}\hat{\Pi}_{\ell}^{(n;\text{s})}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathds{Z}}\hat{\Phi}_{\ell}^{(n;\text{s})}K^{(n)}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\hat{\Phi}_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n;\text{s})},

(51)

where, for notational simplicity, dependence on time has been dropped,
and

K^{(n)}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:=m_{0}^{2}\,\updelta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}-4^{n}\left(\Delta^{(2)}_{\ell^{\prime}-\ell}+\Delta^{(2)}_{\ell^{\prime}-(\ell+N)}\right)

(52)

corresponding to periodic boundary conditions.
The spectrum of the projected periodic Hamiltonian is then

\omega_{k}^{(n)}=\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}-\sum_{\ell=-2\mathcal{K}+2}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}4^{n}\Delta^{(2)}_{\ell}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k\ell}{V}\right)}.

(53)

In the continuum limit as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\to\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}-\sum_{\ell}4^{n}\Delta^{(2)}_{\ell}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi k\ell}{V}\right)^{2}\right)}

(54)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\left(\frac{2\pi k}{X}\right)^{2}}

(55)

using the cosine small-angle
approximation,
Eq.

33

and
\sum_{\ell}\Delta^{(2)}_{\ell}=0
(see Eq. (3.35) in
Beylkin 1992
).
In the thermodynamic limit
X\to\infty
,
2\pi k/X

becomes a continuum parameter that is the momentum of the
continuum theory, recovering the well-known dispersion
relation for the continuum bosonic field theory.

The covariance matrix for a bosonic state is defined
as
Eisert et al. 2010
;
Ferraro et al. 2005

\Gamma^{(n)}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}:=\expectationvalue{\left\{\hat{r}^{(n;\text{s})}_\ell,
\hat{r}^{(n;\text{s})}_{\ell'}\right\}},

(56)

where
\hat{\bm{r}}^{(n;\text{s})}:=(\hat{\Phi}^{(n;\text{s})}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\Phi}^{(n;\text{s})}_{N-1},\hat{\Pi}^{(n;\text{s})}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\Pi}^{(n;\text{s})}_{N-1})

is the vector of canonical scale operators at scale
n
.
For the ground state of the Hamiltonian
in
Eq.

51
,
the covariance matrix is
simply
\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}=\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\Phi}\oplus\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\Pi}

where
\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\Pi}:=\sqrt{\bm{K}^{(n)}}

and
\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\Phi}:=(\bm{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{\Pi})^{-1}

Eisert et al. 2010
.

IV.1
Renormalization in multiscale correlators

As described in
Eq.

24
,
the Hilbert space spanned by scale modes at scale
n
is
equivalent to the Hilbert space spanned by coarser scale modes at
scale
0
completed by wavelet modes from scales
0\leq r<n
.
This allows us to express correlations between wavelet modes at
some scales
r<n
in terms of a linear combination of finer scale
modes at scale
n
.
This constitutes the so-called bulk/boundary correspondence where
the wavelet modes (and coarse scale modes) comprise the bulk with
two dimensions indexed by position and scale, while the finer
scale modes comprise the one-dimensional boundary with one
position index.
The general expression for the correlators of wavelet mode operators

\hat{A}^{(\text{w})}
and
\hat{B}^{(\text{w})}
at scales
r

and
r^{\prime}
and positions
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
in terms of equivalent
scale mode operators at scale
n\gg r
is
Singh and Brennen 2016

\begin{split}&\expectationvalue{\hat{A}^{(r;\text{w})}_\ell\hat{B}^{(r';\text{w})}_{\ell'}}=2^{-n}2^{(r+r^{\prime})/2}\sum_{j=2^{n-r}\ell}^{2^{n-r}(\ell+2\mathcal{K}-1)}\sum_{j^{\prime}=2^{n-r^{\prime}}\ell^{\prime}}^{2^{n-r^{\prime}}(\ell^{\prime}+2\mathcal{K}-1)}\\
&\qquad\times w_{0}^{(0)}(2^{r-n}(j-2^{n-r}\ell+1))\\
&\qquad\times w_{0}^{(0)}(2^{r^{\prime}-n}(j^{\prime}-2^{n-r^{\prime}}\ell^{\prime}+1))\expectationvalue{\hat{A}^{(n;\text{s})}_j \hat{B}^{(n;\text{s})}_{j'}}.\end{split}

(57)

IV.1.1
Fermionic case

For a theory with bare mass
m_{0}
defined at the scale
n
, the
fine-scale correlators are (see

Appendix

A
for derivation):

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(n; s)} \hat{b}_{\ell, 1}^{(n; s)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{V}\sum_{k\in S}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\theta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi\ell k/V},

(58)

\displaystyle\text{where }\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\arctan{\frac{-q_k}{m_0 + \sqrt{m_0^2 + q_k^2}}},

(59)

\displaystyle\text{and }q_{k}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\Delta^{(1)}_{j}\sin{\frac{2 \pi j k}{V}}.

(60)

In the massless case this simplifies to

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(n; s)} \hat{b}_{\ell, 1}^{(n; s)}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{-2}{V\sin{\left( \pi\ell/ V \right)}}&\text{$\ell$ odd},\\
0&\text{$\ell$ even}.\end{cases}

(61)

As per
Singh and Brennen 2016
the correlator between wavelet modes at
different scales
r,r^{\prime}<n
is

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(r; \text{w})}
\hat{b}_{\ell, 1}^{(r'; \text{w})}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=0}^{V-1}\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(n; s)} \hat{b}_{j - j', 1}^{(n; s)}}f_{j,0}^{n,r}f_{j^{\prime},\ell}^{n,r^{\prime}},

(62)

\displaystyle f_{j,\ell}^{n,r}

\displaystyle=\int\differential{x}s_{j}^{(n)}(x)w_{\ell}^{(r)}(x),

(63)

with other expressions given by translational symmetry and anticommutation.
Correlations at the same scale
r=r^{\prime}
can be approximated in the continuum
limit for
r
sufficiently far from the boundary.
Assuming
r\ll n
, let
2^{r-n}\ell\to x
, treating
x
as
a continuous variable so that
\delta\ell=2^{n-r}\differential{x}
, and
replacing sums by integrals

\sum_{m=0}^{2^{n-r}(2\mathcal{K}-1)}\to 2^{n-r}\int_{0}^{2\mathcal{K}-1}\differential{x}
,
results in:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(r; \text{w})}
\hat{b}_{\ell, 1}^{(r; \text{w})}}&=\mathrm{i}2^{-r}\int_{0}^{2\mathcal{K}-1}\differential{x}\int_{\ell}^{2\mathcal{K}-1+\ell}\differential{x'}w_{0}^{(0)}(x)\\
&\qquad\times w_{0}^{(0)}(x^{\prime}-\ell)\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_0(x)\hat{b}_1(x')}.\end{split}

(64)

In the massless phase, for large
n
, the continuous correlator can
be replaced by the discrete correlator
from
Eq.

61
.
Assuming
r\gg 0
leads to

\sin(\pi 2^{-r}(x-x')/V)\approx\pi 2^{-r}(x-x^{\prime})/V
.
Restrict attention to correlations longer range than the size
of the wavelet modes, i.e.
\ell>2\mathcal{K}-1
, so that the
integrals satisfy

\int_{0}^{2\mathcal{K}-1}\differential{x}w_{0}^{0}(x)w_{0}^{0}(x-\ell)=0
.
Define the new variable
x^{\prime\prime}=x^{\prime}-\ell
, then

\begin{split}&\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(r; \text{w})}
\hat{b}_{\ell, 1}^{(r; \text{w})}}=\mathrm{i}\int\differential{x}\int\differential{x''}\frac{w_{0}^{(0)}(x)w_{0}^{(0)}(x^{\prime\prime})}{\pi((x-x^{\prime\prime})-\ell)}\\
&\qquad=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi\ell}\int\differential{x}\int\differential{x''}w_{0}^{(0)}(x)w_{0}^{(0)}(x^{\prime\prime})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x-x^{\prime\prime})^{k}}{\ell^{k}}\\
&\qquad=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell^{k+1}}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\binom{k}{t}(-1)^{t}\expectationvalue{x^t}_{w}\expectationvalue{x^{k-t}}_{w}.\end{split}

functions must be computed numerically.
However the wavelet moments

be computed recursively in closed form (see e.g.
Bulut and Polyzou 2013

The dominant term in the correlation is determined by the lowest
nontrivial wavelet moment:

\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{0, 0}^{(k; \text{w})}
\hat{b}_{\ell, 1}^{(k; \text{w})}}\approx\frac{\mathrm{i}\,(-1)^{\mathcal{K}}}{\pi\ell^{2\mathcal{K}+1}}\times\binom{2\mathcal{K}}{\mathcal{K}}\expectationvalue{ x^\dbK}_{w}^{2}.

IV.2.1
Subsystem specification

In the wavelet scale basis, as in the canonical basis, the covariance
matrix
\bm{\Gamma}^{A}
of a subsystem
A
is obtained by selecting a
subset of the rows and columns of the system’s covariance
matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
.
Suppose that the subsystem
A
of a system containing
V
scale modes
at scale
n
over the interval
[0,X)
is an interval of the form

[x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}):=[\ell_{\text{min}}/2^{n},\ell_{\text{max}}/2^{n})
for integers

0\leq\ell_{\text{min}}<\ell_{\text{max}}\leq V
.
The subset of modes
s_{\ell}^{(n)}
retained belong to the
interval
[x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}})
and correspond
to
\ell\in\{\ell_{\text{min}},\ldots,\ell_{\text{max}}-1\}
.
Setting
V_{A}:=\ell_{\text{max}}-\ell_{\text{min}}
,
the resulting covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}^{A}
is of size

V_{A}\times V_{A}
.
The total length of the interval
[x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}})
,
or length of the subsystem
A
, is
X_{A}=V_{A}/2^{n}
.

In these calculations a subsystem
A
with
V_{A}\leq V/2
modes
is selected to be an interval of the form
[0,X_{A})
.
The subsystem’s modes are taken to be less than
V/2
as the entropy
of two subsystems with
V/2-V_{A}
and
V/2+V_{A}
modes are the same.

4

IV.2.2
Fermionic case

The covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
for a fermionic state is real
and antisymmetric, and
satisfies
\bm{\Gamma}^{2}\geq-\mathds{1}
(
Meichanetzidis et al. 2016
, page 2).
Therefore its eigenvalues are all purely imaginary and come
in positive and negative pairs; i.e., the set of eigenvalues
is
\{\pm\mathrm{i}\sigma_{\ell}\}
.
Moreover,
\absolutevalue{\sigma_\ell}\leq 1
.
Note that
\{\pm\sigma_{\ell}\}
is the set of singular values
of
\bm{\Gamma}
which is equal to the set of
eigenvalues of
\mathrm{i}\bm{\Gamma}
.
The entanglement entropy of an
N
-mode fermionic Gaussian
state
\rho^{A}
can then be expressed in terms of the singular
values
\{\sigma^{A}_{\ell}\}
of its covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}^{A}

as (
Meichanetzidis et al. 2016
, page 2)

\displaystyle S\left(\rho^{A}\right)=\sum_{\sigma^{A}_{\ell}\in[0,1]}H\left(\frac{1+\sigma^{A}_{\ell}}{2}\right),

(72)

where
H(x):=-x\log_{2}(x)-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)
is the binary
entropy function.

For the massive fermionic theory, we observe that the entanglement
entropy of a subsystem is constant as a function of the subsystem’s
length in different scales, and this constant increases with the scale
r
.
This is expected from the entanglement area law.
For the massless theory, we observe that the functional form of the
Calabrese-Cardy relations are correct as given in

Section

II.3
, and the central charge is
correct by means of a line with slope equal to
c/3
and
c/6
,
for periodic and open boundary conditions respectively.

IV.2.3
Bosonic case

The covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
for a bosonic state is a real
and positive-definite symmetric matrix, and
satisfies
\bm{\Gamma}+\mathrm{i}\bm{\Omega}/2\geq 0
(
Ferraro et al. 2005
, page 2)
where

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\mathds{1}_{N}\\
-\mathds{1}_{N}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(73)

Williamson’s theorem states that any symmetric and positive-definite
matrix, such as the covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
, can be decomposed
as
\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{S}^{T}(\bm{\Lambda}\oplus\bm{\Lambda})\bm{S}
(
Ferraro et al. 2005
, page 18),
where
\bm{S}
is a symplectic matrix and
\bm{\Lambda}
is a
diagonal matrix whose spectrum
\{\lambda_{\ell}\}
is equal to
the set of positive eigenvalues
of
\mathrm{i}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\Gamma}
(
Eisert et al. 2010
, page 281).
The eigenvalues of
\bm{\Lambda}
are called the
symplectic eigenvalues of the covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
.

\bm{\Gamma}+\mathrm{i}\bm{\Omega}/2\geq 0
further implies
that
\lambda_{\ell}\geq 1/2
.

The entanglement entropy of an
N
-mode bosonic Gaussian
state
\rho^{A}
can then be expressed in terms of the symplectic
eigenvalues of its covariance matrix
\bm{\Gamma}^{A}

as
Demarie 2018

\displaystyle S\left(\rho^{A}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{2N}f\left(\lambda^{A}_{\ell}\right),

(74)

where

\displaystyle f(\lambda):=(\lambda+\frac{1}{2})\log_{2}(\lambda+\frac{1}{2})-(\lambda-\frac{1}{2})\log_{2}(\lambda-\frac{1}{2}).

(75)

Similar to the fermionic case, we again observe that the functional
form of the Calabrese-Cardy relations are correct as given in

Section

II.3
.

IV.2.4
Cutoff scaling behavior

For a massless bosonic field theory in one spatial dimension
with periodic boundary conditions the
subsystem entanglement is related to the ultraviolet cutoff
parameter
\epsilon
as per
Eq.

6

with
c=1
.
We computed the half-chain entropy in a wavelet basis (
\mathcal{K}=3
)
for resolutions
V=2^{n}X
with
X=32

and for
n=0,\ldots,7
(mass was technically
m_{0}=10^{-4}
to avoid
a singularity in the zero-mass limit).
The numerical results demonstrate linear scaling in

n
with a least-squares fit of
S=6.23+0.333n
, in agreement
with the expected scaling law

\epsilon\propto\frac{1}{V}=\frac{1}{2^{n}X}
.

IV.4
Discriminating quantum phases via fidelity overlap

One witness of a quantum phase transition (QPT) is a sudden drop
in the overlap fidelity between ground states of Hamiltonians
straddling a critical point
Zanardi and Paunković 2006
.
Specifically, for a Hamiltonian which experiences a QPT as a
function of one parameter
g
, the witness is

F=\absolutevalue{\braket{\Psi(g_+)}{\Psi(g_-)}},

(77)

where
g_{\pm}=g\pm\delta/2
, and
\ket{\Psi(g)}
is the ground
state of the Hamiltonian with parameter
g
. Here
\delta
is
some increment small enough to resolve the change in
F
.

As in
Appendix

A
, the ground state
of the fermionic Ising model, where the relevant parameter is
g=m_{0}
,
is specified by the condition

\hat{\eta}_{k}\ket{\Psi}=0,

(78)

where the normal fermionic modes are defined in terms of the
momenta annihilation and creation
operators
\hat{p}_{k},\hat{p}_{k}^{\dagger}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\cos\theta_{k}\hat{p}_{k}+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta_{k}\hat{p}_{-k}^{\dagger},

(79)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\cos^{-1}\left[\frac{-(m_{0}+\omega_{k})}{\sqrt{(m_{0}+\omega_{k})^{2}+q_{k}^{2}}}\right],

(80)

\displaystyle q_{k}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\ell=1}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\Delta^{(1)}_{\ell}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k\ell}{V}\right).

(81)

Negative momenta modes are defined by positive indexing via
-k\equiv V-k
.
For antiperiodic boundary conditions the set of allowable positive
momenta
k
is

k\in S_{L}=\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\ldots,\frac{V-1}{2}\}
.
Using the inverse Jordan-Wigner transformation and ordering qubits
in pairs
\{(k,-k)\}_{k\in S_{L}}
results in expressions

\hat{p}_{k}=[\prod_{j<k}\bm{Z}_{j}\bm{Z}_{-j}]\sigma^{+}_{k}
and

\hat{p}_{-k}^{\dagger}=[\prod_{j<k}\bm{Z}_{j}\bm{Z}_{-j}]\bm{Z}_{k}\sigma^{-}_{-k}
,
where
\sigma^{\pm}_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\bm{X}_{k}\pm\mathrm{i}\bm{Y}_{k})
are
the usual raising and lowering operators acting on mode
k
.
The ground state can then be written as a tensor product of
entangled qubit pairs

\ket{\Psi}=\bigotimes_{k\in S_{L}}(\cos\theta_{k}\ket{1}_{k}\ket{1}_{-k}+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta_{k}\ket{0}_{k}\ket{0}_{-k}).

(82)

The fidelity is then

F=\prod_{k\in S_{L}}\cos(\theta_k(m_+) - \theta_k(m_-)),

(83)

where
\theta_{k}(m_{\pm})
is the value
\theta_{k}
with
mass
m_{0}\pm\delta/2
.
Most of the terms in the product formula for the fidelity are equal
to one up to numerical precision for any mass, but near
m_{0}=0
there
are deviations which are most prominent at the longest wavelength,
namely
|k|=1/2
.
Approximating the fidelity at criticality by the overlap on this single
pair of modes and assuming
1/\delta,V\gg 1
results in

F\approx 1-\frac{\delta^{2}V^{2}}{8\pi^{2}},\quad m_{0}=0,

(84)

where the derivative coefficients have been summed as
in
Eq.

33
.
By contrast, away from criticality, letting
\delta=1/V
,

F\approx 1-\frac{\pi^{2}\delta^{2}}{8m_{0}^{4}V^{2}},\quad\absolutevalue{m_0}\gg 1/V,

(85)

which quickly approaches
1
.

Rather than computing the fidelity overlap of the global ground states,
an approximation can be obtained in a multiresolution wavelet basis
by computing the fidelity overlap between reduced states of a
few coarse modes.
This reduced state effectively acts as a compressed representation
of the global state.
Specifically, the fidelity between reduced states is given by:

F(\rho_{s}(g_{+}),\rho_{s}(g_{-}))=\Tr[\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_{s}(g_{+})}\rho_{s}(g_{-})\sqrt{\rho_{s}(g_{+})}}],

(86)

where
\rho_{s}(g)
is the reduced state on subsystem
s
of the global
pure state
\ket{\Psi(g)}
.
For the fermionic Ising model field theory, the fidelity between two
mixed fermionic Gaussian states with covariance matrices

\Gamma_{s}(g_{+}),\Gamma_{s}(g_{-})
(expressed as covariances in the
Majorana representation) is given by
Banchi et al. 2014

F=\frac{\det[\mathds{1}+\sqrt{e^{\tanh^{-1}{\Gamma_{s}(g_{+})}}e^{2\tanh^{-1}{\Gamma_{s}(g_{-})}}e^{\tanh^{-1}{\Gamma_{s}(g_{+})}}}]^{1/2}}{\det[\mathds{1}+e^{2\tanh^{-1}{\Gamma_{s}(g_{+})}}]^{1/4}\det[\mathds{1}+e^{2\tanh^{-1}{\Gamma_{s}(g_{-})}}]^{1/4}}.

(87)

In
Fig.

6
the signature
of a QPT is evident in the fidelity overlap calculated between
two-mode subsystems of coarse modes.
As expected, the minimum occurs at
m_{0}=0
and is considerably
more significant for subsystems of coarser modes (corresponding to
higher wavelet transform levels) compared to finer modes.

We note also a dependence on the Daubechies wavelet index
\mathcal{K}
,
such that higher wavelet indices provide a better approximation
to the whole-state/analytic behavior with only a few coarse modes
(i.e. a lower fidelity minimum).
However in the case of calculating the fidelity overlap the effect
is weak, of order
10^{-5}
, and becomes weaker for higher
\mathcal{K}
.

.

Recently there has been progress in connecting entanglement in a
boundary quantum field theory to geometric quantities in the bulk
dual
Bhattacharyya et al. 2018
;
Umemoto and Takayanagi 2018
;
Lee 2017
.
A particularly compelling idea inspired by holographic duality
is the conjectured equality
Umemoto and Takayanagi 2018

E_{p}(\rho_{AB})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}E_{W}(\rho_{AB}),

(88)

where
E_{p}(\rho_{AB})
is the entanglement of purification of a
subsystem
\rho_{AB}
of a boundary CFT, and
E_{W}(\rho_{AB})
is
the entanglement wedge cross section, a geometric quantity
defined in the bulk.
The entanglement of purification is defined as

E_{p}(\rho_{AB})={\rm min}_{\ket{\psi}_{A\bar{A}B\bar{B}};\Tr_{\bar{A}\bar{B}}[\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}]=\rho_{AB}}S(\rho_{A\bar{A}}),

(89)

where
\bar{A},\bar{B}
are auxiliary systems to
A,B
and
the minimum is taken over all purifications
\ket{\psi}
of the
state
\rho_{AB}
.
The entanglement wedge cross section is

E_{W}(\rho_{AB})=\frac{\absolutevalue{\Sigma^{\ast}_{AB}}}{4G_{N}},

(90)

where
|\Sigma^{\ast}_{AB}|
is the area of the minimal cross section
of the entanglement wedge in the bulk dual that connects the boundary
subsystem
A
with
B
.
We use units where
4G_{N}=1
.
In the case of a
(1+1)
D boundary CFT,
\Sigma^{\ast}_{AB}
is a
one-dimensional surface.

An appealing feature of the entanglement wedge cross section is that it is
blind to cutoff-dependent features of the entanglement entropy due
to cancellation of such terms.
The motivation for the conjectured equivalence in

Eq.

88
is that—assuming that the global state
is pure—the entanglement wedge satisfies several inequalities
shared by the entanglement of purification (EoP) including

1.

I(A:B)/2\leq E_{W}(\rho_{AB})\leq{\rm min}(S(\rho_{\text{A}}),S(\rho_{\text{B}}))

2.

E_{W}(\rho_{AB})\leq E_{W}(\rho_{A(BC)})\leq E_{W}(\rho_{AB})+E_{W}(\rho_{BC})

3.

E_{W}(\rho_{(AA^{\prime})(BB^{\prime})})\geq E_{W}(\rho_{AB})+E_{W}(\rho_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}})

The first statement refers to the mutual information

I(A:B)=S(\rho_{\text{A}})+S(\rho_{\text{B}})-S(\rho_{AB}).

(91)

The left-hand side of the second statement follows from the
extensiveness of the entanglement of mutual information and
the right-hand side is a polygamy inequality.
Both these inequalities are shared by EoP.
The third inequality is a statement of strong superadditivity
Umemoto and Takayanagi 2018
.
EoP in fact satisfies subadditivity:

E_{P}(\rho\otimes\sigma)\leq E_{P}(\rho)+E_{P}(\sigma)
with equality
only if the optimal purification of the joint state
\rho\otimes\sigma

is the product of optimal purifications of
\rho
and
\sigma

separately, which is expected
in holographic CFTs
Umemoto and Takayanagi 2018
, in which case EoP becomes additive
like
E_{W}
.

In order to better understand holographic entanglement of purification
from a wavelet perspective, consider the case of a ground state

(1+1)
D CFT of overall length
l
and with periodic boundaries.
The entanglement wedge cross section has an analytic formula,
whereas the entanglement of purification does not, rather it
involves a complex minimization.
To see the former, consider two regions
A
and
B
, ordered left to
right, separated by a distance
d
and with equal lengths

\absolutevalue{A}=\absolutevalue{B}=\ell\ll L
, and with boundary points

\{\partial A_{L},\partial A_{R},\partial B_{L},\partial B_{R}\}
.
The entropy (up to an additive constant) of the joint region

A\cup B
is given by the minimal length curve in AdS space that
separates it from its complement.
This will either be the sum of the geodesic connecting boundary
points
\{\partial A_{L},\partial A_{R}\}
and that connecting

\{\partial B_{L},\partial B_{R}\}
(see
Fig.

7
, top),
i.e.
\frac{2c}{3}\log(\ell)
, or the sum of the geodesic
which connects
\{\partial A_{L},\partial B_{R}\}
and that
connecting
\{\partial A_{R},\partial B_{L}\}
(see

Fig.

7
, bottom).
i.e,
\frac{c}{3}\log(d(d+2\ell))
.
These two lengths become degenerate at
d/\ell=\sqrt{2}-1
.
The mutual information is then

I(A:B)=\begin{cases}\hfil 0&d/\ell\geq\sqrt{2}-1\\
-\frac{c}{3}\log((d/\ell)^2+2d/\ell)&d/\ell<\sqrt{2}-1\end{cases}.

(92)

A,B

W

from its complement

Fig.

7

\absolutevalue{\Sigma^*_{AB}}

\Sigma_{AB}
in AdS space that divides

A

(for the example above)

E_{W}(\rho_{AB})=\frac{c}{6}\log(1+2\ell/d).

(93)

V
Discussion

We show in result
IV.1
that the same-scale
correlators in the multiscale representation of the ground states
for the bosonic and fermionic theories demonstrate the expected
power-law decay in the massless case, with an exponent that depends
on the Daubechies index
\mathcal{K}
, and exponential decay in the massive
cases.
Mass renormalization is naturally emergent as a function
of scale.
We also consider in result
IV.2
the
entanglement features of the ground states of the
two QFTs in a scale field representation and verified that the
Calabrese-Cardy relations are obeyed.
For the massive bosonic theory in 1D and 2D, we observe that the
entanglement entropy of a subsystem is constant as a function of
the subsystem’s length in different scales.
The constant value increases as we increase the scale
parameter
k
as is to be expected from the entanglement area law.
For the massless bosonic and fermionic CFTs, we obtain the correct
central charges and also the cutoff dependence of the entropy as a
function of scale.

Results
IV.4
and

IV.5
demonstrate two potential
applications of using a wavelet-based multiscale representation as
a form of compression, where some function on a system with
2^{n}X

modes can be approximated by applying that function to a reduced state
of
X
coarse scale modes obtained from an
n
-level wavelet transform.

In the case of result
IV.4
, we show that in a
fermionic QFT this wavelet compression technique can be used to identify
a phase transition, evident in a decrease in fidelity overlap
between ground states adjacent in some parameter space (here, mass).
Given that the fast wavelet transform (FWT) has an efficient classical
implementation that scales with
\mathcal{O}(N\log(N))
,
where
N
is the dimension of the vector space, this technique
holds promise for approximating the value of a many-body observable
that might otherwise be prohibitively difficult to observe directly
due to experimental, computational, or other constraints.

Finally, result
IV.5
demonstrates
that, for a bosonic QFT, wavelet compression qualitatively captures
the physics of the mutual information between isolated subsystems,
including identification of a phase transition.

The use of higher-order wavelet basis functions results in more desirable
mathematical properties, such as increased accuracy of approximations
using a small number of modes, and well-defined higher-order
derivatives, at the cost of moderately increased computational complexity.
As noted in
Beylkin and Keiser 1997
(page 179), the error incurred by the
wavelet discretization method on a second-order differential
equation described by a Laplacian is
\order{(\Delta x)^{2\dbK}}

where
\Delta x=2^{-k}
and
k
is the number of scales in the
multiresolution analysis.
We can extrapolate this point to the bosonic field theory for an

\order{2^{-2k\dbK}}
error scaling, and to the fermionic field theory
(arises from a first-order equation) to find an error scaling of

\order{2^{-k\dbK}}
.
This suggests that we have a strategy for reducing error in the
discretization that is not simply increasing the number of
scales
k
, i.e. reducing the size of the length cutoff.
We may also reduce error by increasing the value of
\mathcal{K}
.
Note however that increasing
\mathcal{K}
results in a corresponding
computational cost since the number of nonzero bands in the
associated wavelet transform scales as
2\mathcal{K}
.

In this paper we have mostly used a uniform wavelet basis with
either periodic or antiperiodic boundary conditions.
This conforms to the usual application of the discrete wavelet
transform with periodic/antiperiodic signal extension modes in
numerical signal processing.
In the case of
Section

IV.2
, the application
of open boundaries corresponds to the absence of a signal extension,
which is sufficient for the calculation of bulk entanglement since
this property is sensitive primarily to the underlying topology of
the space.
However, when studying open or nonperiodic systems with essential
physics at the boundaries, for example, symmetry-protected
topological phases
Chen et al. 2013
, then a careful consideration of
the wavelet representation of that theory at the boundaries is
necessary.
For an overview of boundary wavelet construction,
see
Mallat 2009
(pages 322-328).

VI
Conclusion

We have demonstrated the utility of wavelet analysis
when describing quantities such as entanglement in fermionic and
bosonic QFTs.
Specifically, the scale dependence of various quantities such as
subsystem entropy and correlations emerge simply by fixing
a wavelet basis, unlike, for example, tensor network
representations, where generally the elements of the tensors
must be obtained by numerical optimization.
We have additionally shown that wavelets provide a way to compress
quantum states in a way that enables the calculation of quantum
informational quantities on a very few number of modes.
Such a result could be useful in experimental probes of quantum
simulations of QFTs where measuring observables over an extensive
number of modes is costly or error prone.

Wavelet analyses have potential in more general QFT
simulation algorithms, and are already showing promise in algorithms
for ground state generation with spatial inhomogeneities
Bagherimehrab et al. 2022
.
By showing that cutoff-dependent results like those of
Calabrese and Cardy 2004

appear directly as a function of an input scale parameter in
wavelet-based representations of QFT, we bolster the case for
wavelet-based representations as a key tool of analysis for the
physics of quantum fields.

Appendix A
Expressions for fermionic covariance matrix

A.1
General solution in wavelet scale basis

The Hamiltonian for the Ising model field theory in the continuous case is

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}\int\!\differential{x}\;\left[\,\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}(x)\,\mathrm{i}\,Y\,\partial_{x}\,\hat{\bm{c}}(x)+m_{0}\,\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}(x)\,Z\,\hat{\bm{c}}(x)\,\right],

(98)

where

\mathrm{i}\,Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}
,

Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}

and the fermionic field operators are

\hat{\bm{c}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}(x)\\
\hat{c}^{\dagger}(x)\\
\end{pmatrix}
,

\hat{\bm{c}}^{\dagger}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}^{\dagger}(x)&\hat{c}(x)\\
\end{pmatrix}
, with the
anticommutation relation

\left\{\hat{c}(x),\hat{c}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime})\right\}=\delta(x-x^{\prime})
.
Discretizing the continuous Hamiltonian into
2^{n}X=V
scale modes

\hat{\bm{r}}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{r}_{\ell}\\
\hat{r}^{\dagger}_{\ell}\\
\end{pmatrix}

where

\hat{r}_{\ell}=\hat{r}_{\ell}^{(n;s)}=\int\!\differential{x}\;s^{(n)}_{\ell}(x)\,\hat{c}(x)
and

\left\{\hat{r}_{\ell},\hat{r}_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\right\}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
gives the discrete Hamiltonian in the
scale-n basis

\hat{H}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}\left[\sum_{j=-2\mathcal{K}+2}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\hat{\bm{r}}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\,\mathrm{i}\,Y\,\Delta_{j}^{(1)}\,\hat{\bm{r}}_{\ell+j}+m_{0}\,\hat{\bm{r}}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\,Z\,\hat{\bm{r}}_{\ell}\right],

(99)

where
\mathcal{K}
is the Daubechies wavelet index and

\Delta_{j}^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{\ell,\ell+j}^{(1)}
is the first
derivative operator in the base scale (scale-0 as
per
Eq.

12
) and is nonzero only when

-2\mathcal{K}+2\leq j\leq 2\mathcal{K}-2
.

Note that
\hat{H}
commutes with the total fermionic parity operator

\hat{J}=\prod_{\ell}\left(1-2\hat{r}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\hat{r}_{\ell}\right)
,
and in order to have translational invariance on the even parity
sector, application of antiperiodic boundary conditions requires

\hat{r}_{V+\ell}\equiv-\hat{r}_{\ell}
.
In practice this means that the upper-right and lower-left corner
terms in the matrix
\Delta^{(1)}
will be the negative of those
along the corresponding main diagonals.

This Hamiltonian can be expressed in terms of uncoupled
modes
\hat{\eta}_{k}
in the usual diagonal form as follows:

\hat{H}=\sum_{k\in S}\omega_{k}\left(\hat{\eta}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{\eta}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

(100)

where

S=\left\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\cdots,V-\frac{1}{2}\right\}

due to the antiperiodic boundary conditions.
The ground state
\ket{G}
is defined by

\hat{\eta}_{k}\ket{G}=0
, from which follow the uncoupled correlations

\expectationvalue{\hat{\eta}_k \hat{\eta}_{k'}}=\expectationvalue{\hat{\eta}_k^\dag\hat{\eta}_{k'}^\dag}=\expectationvalue{\hat{\eta}_k^\dag\hat{\eta}_{k'}}=0
,
and
\expectationvalue{\hat{\eta}_k \hat{\eta}_{k'}^\dag}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
.

The uncoupled modes
\hat{\eta}_{k}
are related to the original
fermionic modes
\hat{r}_{\ell}
via a pair of transforms.
The original modes are related to the momenta modes by the usual
Fourier transform

\hat{p}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}\hat{r}_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi k\ell/V}
.
The momenta modes are related to the uncoupled modes by way of the
Bogoliubov transform

\hat{\eta}_{k}=u_{k}\hat{p}_{k}+\mathrm{i}v_{k}\hat{p}_{-k}^{\dagger}
, where

u_{k}=-(m_{0}+\omega_{k})/\sqrt{(m_{0}+\omega_{k})^{2}+q_{k}^{2}}
,

v_{k}=q_{k}/\sqrt{(m_{0}+\omega_{k})^{2}+q_{k}^{2}}
,

\omega_{k}=\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+q_{k}^{2}}
(
\omega_{k}
are the eigenvalues of
the Hamiltonian) and
q_{k}=2\sum_{j=1}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\Delta^{(1)}_{j}\sin{\frac{2 \pi j k}{V}}
.
The combined transform can be expressed in the form:

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{k}}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}\hat{r}_{\ell}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi k\ell/V}+\frac{\mathrm{i}v_{k}}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}\hat{r}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi k\ell/V}.

(101)

Introduce the Majorana scale modes
\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{b}_{\ell,0}\\
\hat{b}_{\ell,1}\\
\end{pmatrix}

with
\hat{b}_{\ell,0}=\hat{b}_{\ell,0}^{(n;s)}=\hat{r}_{\ell}+\hat{r}_{\ell}^{\dagger}
,

\hat{b}_{\ell,1}=\hat{b}_{\ell,1}^{(n;s)}=-\mathrm{i}\left(\hat{r}_{\ell}-\hat{r}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\right)
, such that

\left\{\hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma},\hat{b}_{\ell^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\right\}=2\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
.
The Hamiltonian transforms to

\hat{H}^{(n)}=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}\left[\sum_{j=-2\mathcal{K}+2}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\,\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}^{T}\,\mathrm{i}\,X\,\Delta_{j}^{(1)}\,\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell+j}+m_{0}\,\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}^{T}\,Y\,\hat{\bm{b}}_{\ell}\right].

(102)

That
u_{k}=u_{-k}
and
v_{k}=-v_{-k}
follows from the properties
of the Bogoliubov transform.
Furthermore introduce
\theta_{k}
such that

u_{k}=\cos{\theta_k}
,
v_{k}=\sin{\theta_k}
, and so

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\eta}_{k}+\hat{\eta}_{-k}^{\dagger}&=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{k}}}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}\hat{b}_{\ell,0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi k\ell/V}\\
\hat{\eta}_{k}-\hat{\eta}_{-k}^{\dagger}&=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{k}}}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{V-1}\hat{b}_{\ell,1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi k\ell/V}\end{split}

(103)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{b}_{\ell,0}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{k\in S}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{k}}(\hat{\eta}_{k}+\hat{\eta}_{-k}^{\dagger})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi k\ell/V}\\
\hat{b}_{\ell,1}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{k\in S}-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{k}}(\hat{\eta}_{k}-\hat{\eta}_{-k}^{\dagger})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi k\ell/V}\end{split}

(104)

Noting that
\theta_{-k}=\arctan{(v_{-k}/u_{-k})}=\arctan{(-v_k/u_k)}=-\theta_{k}
, the correlations can now be
computed directly:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{\ell,0} \hat{b}_{\ell',0}}&=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in S}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\theta_{k}+\theta_{k^{\prime}})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(k\ell+k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime})/V}\\
&\qquad\times\underbrace{\expectationvalue{(\hat{\eta}_k + \hat{\eta}^\dag_{-k})
(\hat{\eta}_{k'} + \hat{\eta}^\dag_{-k'})}}_{=\delta_{k,-k^{\prime}}}\\
&=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\end{split}

(105)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{\ell,0} \hat{b}_{\ell',1}}&=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{V}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in S}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\theta_{k}-\theta_{k^{\prime}})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(k\ell+k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime})/V}\\
&\qquad\times\underbrace{\expectationvalue{(\hat{\eta}_k + \hat{\eta}^\dag_{-k})
(\hat{\eta}_{k'} - \hat{\eta}^\dag_{-k'})}}_{=-\delta_{k,-k^{\prime}}}\\
&=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{V}\sum_{k\in S}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\theta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k/V}\end{split}

(106)

Similarly

\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{\ell,1} \hat{b}_{\ell',1}}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}

and
\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{\ell,1} \hat{b}_{\ell',0}}=-\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{\ell,0} \hat{b}_{\ell',1}}
.
The covariance matrix
\Gamma
defined as

\expectationvalue{\hat{b}_{\ell,\sigma} \hat{b}_{\ell',\sigma'}}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\sigma,\ell;\sigma^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}
in the basis

\ket{\sigma}\ket{\ell},\sigma\in\{0,1\}
is therefore

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\sigma,\ell;\sigma^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\Gamma^{\text{01}}\\
-(\Gamma^{\text{01}})^{T}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(107)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}^{\text{01}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k\in S}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\theta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k/V}

(108)

with
\theta_{k}=\arctan{\frac{-q_k}{m_0 + \sqrt{m_0^2 + q_k^2}}}
and

q_{k}=2\sum_{j=1}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\Delta^{(1)}_{j}\sin{\frac{2 \pi j k}{V}}
.

A.2
Zero-mass limit

Consider the zero-mass limit
m_{0}\to 0
:

\displaystyle\lim_{m_{0}\to 0}\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m_{0}\to 0}\arctan{\frac{-q_k}{m_0
+ \sqrt{m_0^2 + q_k^2}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{4}\sgn{q_{k}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi}{4}&k\in S_{L}\\
-\frac{\pi}{4}&k\in S_{U}\\
\end{cases}

(109)

where
S_{L}
is the lower half of momenta
modes
S_{L}:\left\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\dots,\frac{V-1}{2}\right\}
and
S_{U}
is the upper
half
S_{U}:\left\{\frac{V+1}{2},\frac{V+3}{2},\dots,\frac{2V-1}{2}\right\}
.
The covariance matrix is then

\begin{split}\lim_{m_{0}\to 0}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{V}\Bigl[&\sum_{k\in S_{L}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k/V}\\
-&\sum_{k\in S_{U}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k/V}\Bigr].\end{split}

(110)

Let
\zeta=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})/V}
, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{m_{0}\to 0}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}&=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{V}\bigl[\left(\zeta+\zeta^{3}+\cdots+\zeta^{V-1}\right)\\
&\quad-\left(\zeta^{V+1}+\zeta^{V+3}+\cdots+\zeta^{2V-1}\right)\bigr]\end{split}

(111)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{m_{0}\to 0}\bigl(\zeta^{2}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}-&\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\bigr)\\
&=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{V}\bigl[-\zeta+\zeta^{V+1}+\zeta^{V+1}-\zeta^{2V+1}\bigr]\end{split}

(112)

\displaystyle\lim_{m_{0}\to 0}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{V}\frac{1}{\zeta-\zeta^{-1}}\left(2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})}-2\right)

(113)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{-2}{V\sin{ \left( \pi(\ell- \ell') / V \right) }}&\ell-\ell^{\prime}\text{ odd}\\
0&\ell-\ell^{\prime}\text{ even}\\
\end{cases}

(114)

A.3
Finite mass

For finite mass, define
s_{k}=q_{k}/m_{0}
.
Then for
m_{0}\gg\absolutevalue{q_k}
,

\theta_{k}=\arctan{\left( -s_k / \left( 1 + \sqrt{1 + s_k^2} \right) \right)}\approx-s_{k}/2+O(s_{k}^{3})
, and so

\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k\in S}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k/V}.

(115)

In the special case of Haar wavelets (
\mathcal{K}=1
),

\Delta^{(1)}_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}
,
\Delta^{(1)}_{\ell>1}=0
, and so

s_{k}=-\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sin{ \left( 2 \pi k / V \right)}
:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k\in S}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{m_{0}}\sin{ \left( 2 \pi k / V \right)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k/V}

(116)

\displaystyle\lim_{V\to\infty}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(-\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sin{k}-(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k)}\,\differential{k}

(117)

\displaystyle=J_{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}\left(-\frac{1}{m_{0}}\right),

(118)

where
J
is the Bessel function of the first kind.
Note also that

J_{-\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{m_{0}}\right)=(-1)^{\alpha}J_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{m_{0}}\right)
.

For
\frac{1}{m_{0}}<<\sqrt{\absolutevalue{\ell- \ell'}+1}
,

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{m_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\left(\frac{1}{2m_{0}}\right)^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha!}\left(\frac{1}{2m_{0}}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(1-\ln{2 m_0\alpha})}

(119)

And so,

\lim_{V\to\infty}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\ell-\ell^{\prime})}}\mathrm{e}^{(\ell-\ell^{\prime})\left(1-\ln{2 m_0 (\ell- \ell')}\right)}

(120)

Alternatively, in the limit as the number of modes
V\to\infty
,

Eq.

108
becomes

\displaystyle\lim_{V\to\infty}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(2\theta_{k}+(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k)}\,\differential{k}

(121)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(2\theta_{k}+(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k)}\,\differential{k}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(2\theta_{k}+(\ell-\ell^{\prime})k)}\,\differential{k}\Bigr)\end{split}

(122)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos{(2\theta_k + (\ell- \ell') k)}\,\differential{k},

(123)

where, as before,

\theta_{k}=\arctan{(-q_k/(m_0 + \sqrt{m_0^2 + q_k^2}))}

and
q_{k}
has been redefined to

q_{k}=2\sum_{j=1}^{2\mathcal{K}-2}\Delta^{(1)}_{j}\sin{(j k)}
.
Letting
\theta_{k}=\arctan{(A_k)}
,

A_{k}=-q_{k}/(m_{0}+\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+q_{k}^{2}})
,
this can be further simplified to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{V\to\infty}\Gamma^{\text{01}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}&=-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{A_{k}}{1+A_{k}^{2}}\bigl(A_{k}\cos{((\ell- \ell') k)}\\
&\qquad+\sin{((\ell- \ell') k)}\bigr)\,\differential{k}\end{split}

(124)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}B_{k}\sin{((\ell- \ell') k)}\,\differential{k},&\ell-\ell^{\prime}\text{ odd}\\
-\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}C_{k}\cos{((\ell- \ell') k)}\,\differential{k},&\ell-\ell^{\prime}\text{ even}\end{cases},

\displaystyle B_{k}=\frac{A_{k}}{1+A_{k}^{2}},\quad C_{k}=\frac{A_{k}^{2}}{1+A_{k}^{2}}

(125)

In the special case of Haar wavelets (
\mathcal{K}=1
),

\Delta^{(1)}_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}
,

\Delta^{(1)}_{\ell>1}=0
, and so

q_{k}=-\sin{ \left( 2 \pi k / V \right)}
.
The above then simplifies to

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin{k}(m_{0}+\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\sin^{2}{k}})}{\sin^{2}{k}+(m_{0}+\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\sin^{2}{k}})^{2}},

(126)

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin^{2}{k}}{\sin^{2}{k}+(m_{0}+\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}+\sin^{2}{k}})^{2}}.

(127)
