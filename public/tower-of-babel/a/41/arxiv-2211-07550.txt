Tree-layout based graph classes: proper chordal graphs Research supported by the project DEMOGRAPH (ANR-16-CE40-0028) and the French-German Collaboration ANR/DFG Project UTMA (ANR-20-CE92-0027). An extended abstract of this paper appeared in: International Symposium on Theoretical Aspects of Computer Science (STACS). Leibniz International Proceedings in Informatics (LIPIcs), Volume 289, pp. 55:1-55:18, 2024.
Christophe Paul, Evangelos Protopapas

Abstract
Many standard graph classes are known to be characterized by means of layouts (a vertex ordering) excluding some patterns. Important such graph classes are among others: proper interval graphs, interval graphs, chordal graphs, permutation graphs, (co-)comparability graphs. For example, a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is a proper interval graph if and only if G G has a layout 𝐋 G \mathbf{L}_{G} such that for every triple of vertices such that x ≺ 𝐋 G y ≺ 𝐋 G z x\prec_{\mathbf{L}_{G}}y\prec_{\mathbf{L}_{G}}z , if x ​ z ∈ E xz\in E , then x ​ y ∈ E xy\in E and y ​ z ∈ E yz\in E . Such a triple x x , y y , z z is called an indifference triple and layouts excluding indifference triples are known as indifference layouts . In this paper, we investigate the concept of tree-layouts . A tree-layout 𝐓 G = ( T , r , ρ G ) \mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G}) of a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is a tree T T rooted at some node r r and equipped with a one-to-one mapping ρ G \rho_{G} between V V and the nodes of T T such that for every edge x ​ y ∈ E xy\in E , either x x is an ancestor of y y , denoted x ≺ 𝐓 G y x\prec_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}y , or y y is an ancestor of x x . Clearly, layouts are tree-layouts. Excluding a pattern in a tree-layout is defined similarly as excluding a pattern in a layout, but now using the ancestor relation. Unexplored graph classes can be defined by means of tree-layouts excluding some patterns. As a proof of concept, we show that excluding non-indifference triples in tree-layouts yields a natural notion of proper chordal graphs . We characterize proper chordal graphs and position them in the hierarchy of known subclasses of chordal graphs. We also provide a canonical representation of proper chordal graphs that encodes all the indifference tree-layouts rooted at some vertex. Based on this result, we first design a polynomial time recognition algorithm for proper chordal graphs. We then show that the problem of testing isomorphism between two proper chordal graphs is in 𝖯 \mathsf{P} , whereas this problem is known to be 𝖦𝖨 \mathsf{GI} -complete on chordal graphs.

The first questions are probably to understand the graph classes we obtain by excluding patterns of size at most

Our contributions.

As a first study of tree-layout based graph classes, we consider the patterns characterizing interval graphs and proper interval graphs. We first show that if we consider the interval graphs patterns
\mathcal{P}_{\sf int}
, the same phenomena as for
\big\{\langle\overline{12}\rangle\big\}

As already discussed, proper interval graphs are obtained by restricting interval graphs to the intersection of a set of
proper
intervals (no interval is a subinterval of another). This restriction leads to a distinct graph class as the
K_{1,3}
is an interval graph but not a proper one. Following this line, in his seminal paper
[
19

proper
subtrees of a tree (no subtree is contained in an another). Using an easy reduction, Gavril proved that this again yields a characterization of chordal graphs. So this left open the question of proposing a natural definition for proper chordal graphs, a class of graphs that should be sandwiched between proper interval graphs and chordal graphs but incomparable to interval graphs. Our main contribution is to propose such a natural definition of
proper chordal graphs
by means of forbidden patterns on tree-layouts: a graph is proper chordal if it belongs to
\mathfrak{T}(\mathcal{P}_{\sf proper})
, the class of graphs admitting a

-free tree-layout. Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{\sf proper}
are the patterns characterizing proper interval graphs on layouts.

2.3
Proper interval graphs and proper chordal graphs

Proper interval graphs.

A graph

is a

if it is an interval graph admitting an interval model

such that for every pair of intervals none is a subinterval of another
[
42
,
43
]
. Proper interval graphs are also characterized as

-free interval graphs or as

, that are interval graphs admitting an interval model
\mathbf{M}^{\sf I}_{G}
in which every interval has the same length.
Let us consider the following set of patterns:

\mathcal{P}_{\sf indifference}=\big\{\langle\overline{12},{13},{23}\rangle,\langle\overline{12},{13},\overline{23}\rangle,\langle{12},{13},\overline{23}\rangle\big\}.

A
\mathcal{P}_{\sf indifference}
-free layout is called an

. Indifference layouts have several characterizations:

Theorem 4
.

[
36
,
42
]

Let
\mathbf{L}_{G}
be a layout of a graph
G
. The following properties are equivalent.

1.

\mathbf{L}_{G}
is an indifference layout;

2.

for every vertex
v
,

is consecutive in
\mathbf{L}_{G}
;

3.

every maximal clique is consecutive in
\mathbf{L}_{G}
;

4.

for every pair of vertices
x
and
y
with

,

and
N(x)\cap D_{\mathbf{L}_{G}}(y)\subseteq N(y)\cap D_{\mathbf{L}_{G}}(y)
.

Moreover

has an indifference layout if and only if it is a proper interval graph.

Proper chordal graphs.

From the discussion of
Subsection 2.2
, we would like to understand what are the
”tree-like”
proper interval graphs. In
[

, Gavril already considered this question, but didn’t provide a conclusive answer. He observed that any family

of subtrees of a tree

is the same as the one of

Definition 2

A graph

is a proper chordal graph if

.

-trees and

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of patterns. In general, a graph

admits several

-free layouts. A basic example is the complete graph

on

is an indifference layout (i.e. a
\mathcal{P}_{\sf proper}
-free layout), but also a
\mathcal{P}_{\sf int}
-free layout and a
\mathcal{P}_{\sf chordal}
-free layout. Let us discuss in more details the case of proper interval graphs and interval graphs.

Proper interval graphs.

Two vertices
x
and
y
of a graph
G
are
true-twins
if
N[x]=N[y]
. It is easy to see that the true-twin relation is an equivalence relation. If
G
contains some true-twins, then, by
Theorem 4
, the vertices of any equivalence class occurs consecutively (and in arbitrary order) in an indifference layout. It follows that for proper interval graphs, the set of indifference layouts depends on the true-twin equivalence classes. Indeed, a proper interval graph
G
without any pair of true-twins has a unique (up to reversal) indifference layout.

Interval graphs.

In the case of interval graphs, the set of intersection models, and hence of
\mathcal{P}_{\sf int}
-free layouts, is structured by means of
modules

[
18
]
, in a similar way to the true-twin equivalence classes for
\mathcal{P}_{\sf proper}
-free layouts. A subset
M
of vertices of a graph
G
is a
module
if for every
x\notin M
, either
M\subseteq N(x)
or
M\subseteq\overline{N}(x)
. Observe that a true-twin equivalence class is a module. A graph may have exponentially many modules. For example, every subset of vertices of the complete graph is a module. Hsu
[
30
]
proved that interval graphs having a unique intersection model are those without any trivial module.

The set of modules of a graph forms a so-called
partitive family

[
9
]
and can thereby be represented through a linear size tree, called the
modular decomposition tree
(see
[
26
]
for a survey on modular decomposition). To recognize interval graphs in linear time, Booth and Lueker
[
7
]
introduced the concept of
PQ
-trees which is closely related to the modular decomposition tree or more generally to the theory of (weakly-)partitive families
[
9
,
11
]
. Basically, a
PQ
-tree on a set

is a labelled ordered tree having
X
as its leaf set. Since every ordered tree defines a permutation of its leaf set, by defining an equivalence relation based on the labels of the node, every
PQ
-tree can be associated to a set of permutations of
X
. In the context of interval graphs,
X
is the set of maximal cliques and a
PQ
-tree represents the set of so-called
consecutive orderings of the maximal cliques
characterizing interval graphs.

that for a fixed vertex
x
of a proper chordal graph, drives the structure and the combinatorics of the set of indifference tree-layouts rooted at
x
.

4.1
Blocks and indifference tree-layouts

Let
S
be a non-empty vertex subset of a connected graph
G=(V,E)
and let
C
be a connected component of
G-S
. We say that
x\in C
is
S
-maximal
if for every vertex
y\in C
,
N(y)\cap S\subseteq N(x)\cap S
. Observe that if
C
contains two distinct
S
-maximal vertices
x
and
y
, then
N(x)\cap S=N(y)\cap S
.

Definition 3
.

Let
S
be a subset of vertices of a graph
G=(V,E)
and let
C
be a connected component of
G-S
. A maximal subset of vertices
X\subseteq C
is an
S
-block
, if every vertex of
X
is
S
-maximal and
(N(S)\cap C)
-universal.

We let the reader observe that a connected component
C
of
G-S
may not contain an
S
-block. However, if
C
contains an
S
-block, then it is uniquely defined.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
be a tree-layout of a graph
G=(V,E)
. Then
\mathbf{T}_{G}
is an indifference tree-layout if and only if for every vertex
x
distinct from
\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)
,
x
is
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-maximal and
(N(A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x))\cap D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}[x])
-universal.

Proof.

Suppose that every vertex
x
distinct from
\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)
, is
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-maximal and
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-universal.
Let
y
,
z
be two distinct vertices of
G
such that
y\prec_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}x\prec_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}z
and
yz\in E
. Observe that
y\in A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
and

z\in N(A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x))
. As
x
is
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-maximal and
(N(A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x))\cap D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}[x])
-universal,
yz\in E
implies that
xy\in E
and
xz\in E
. So
\mathbf{T}_{G}
is an indifference tree-layout.

To prove the reverse, consider
x\in V
distinct from
\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)
and
C
the connected component of
G-A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
containing
x
. First observe that as
\mathbf{T}_{G}
is a tree-layout of
G
, every vertex
z\in C
is a descendant of
x
is
\mathbf{T}_{G}
. Suppose that either
x
is not
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-maximal or not
(N(A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x))\cap D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}[x])
-universal.
In the former case, there exist two vertices
y\in A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
and
z\in C
such that
yz\in E
but
xy\notin E
. In the latter case, there exists
y\in A_{\mathbf{T}}(x)
and
z\in C
such that
yz\in E
but
xy\notin E
.
By the above observation, we have that
z\prec_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}x\prec_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}y
and so in both cases
\mathbf{T}_{G}
is not an indifference tree-layout.
∎

So
Lemma 6
and the discussion above shows that if
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
is an indifference tree-layout of a proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
, then every vertex
x
distinct from
\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)
can be associated with a non-empty
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-block which contains
x
. Hereafter, we let
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)

denote the
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-block containing
x
. If
x=\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)
, then we set
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)=\{x\}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
be an indifference tree-layout of a connected proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
. For every vertex
x\in V
, the vertices of the block
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
appear consecutively on a path rooted at
x
and induces a clique in
G
.

Proof.

The property trivially holds for
x=\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)
. Let us consider
x\neq\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)
and let
C
be the connected component of
G-A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
containing
x
. By the definition of blocks, every vertex of
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
is
(N(A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x))\cap D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}[x])
-universal. This implies that
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
induces a clique. Observe that in every tree-layout, the vertices of a clique appear on a path from the root to a leaf.
By definition of
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
, every vertex of
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
distinct from
x
is a descendant of
x
in
\mathbf{T}_{G}
. Let
z
be the lowest vertex of
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
in
\mathbf{T}_{G}
and let
y\in V
be a vertex such that
x\prec_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}y\prec_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}z
. Then observe that by the indifference property of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
,
y
is
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
-maximal and
y
is adjacent to every vertex of
N(A_{T_{G}}(x))
that is a descendant of
x
. It follows that
y\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
be an indifference tree-layout of a connected proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
. If
y\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
, then
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)\subset B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists
z\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)\setminus B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
. Let us observe that
x
,
y
and
z
are pairwise adjacent:
xy\in E
because
y\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
;
yz\in E
because
z\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
; and finally,
xz\in E
because
x\in A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
and
N(z)\cap A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)=N(y)\cap A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
. As
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)\subset A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
, we also have that
N(x)\cap A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)=N(y)\cap A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)=N(z)\cap A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
. So the fact that
z\notin B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
, implies that there exists
v\in N(A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x))\cap(D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)\cup\{x\})
such that
vz\notin E
. As
xz\in E
, we know that
x\neq v
. As
y\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
, we also have
yv\in E
. It follows that
v\in N(A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y))\cap(D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)\cup\{y\})
. Finally, since
z\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
, we also have
vz\in E
: contradiction.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
be an indifference tree-layout of a connected proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
. For every vertex
x
,
G[D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)]
is connected.

Proof.

Let
x
and
y
be two vertices such that
x
is the parent of
y
in
\mathbf{T}_{G}
. We prove that
xy\in E
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
xy\notin E
. Observe that
N(x)\cap D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)=\emptyset
, as otherwise
\mathbf{T}
would not be an indifference tree-layout. For the same reason, we have that for every
z\in D_{\mathbf{T}}(y)
,
N(z)\cap A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)=\emptyset
. This implies that
D_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
and
A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
are not in the same connected component of
G
: contradiction.
∎

4.2
The block tree of an indifference tree-layout

Given an indifference tree-layout
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho)
of a connected proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
, we define the set
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})
as the set containing the inclusion-maximal blocks of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
. Observe that
\{\rho^{-1}(r)\}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})
.
Corollary 1
is a direct consequence of
Lemma 8
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
be an indifference tree-layout of a connected proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
. Then,
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})
is a partition of
V
.

From
Lemma 7
and
Corollary 1
, we can define the
block tree
of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
, which will be denoted
\mathbf{T}_{G}\!\!\mid_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{T}}(G)}
, obtained by contracting every block of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})
into a single node.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
and
\mathbf{T}^{\prime}_{G}=(T^{\prime},r^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}_{G})
be two indifference tree-layouts of a connected proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
both having the same root
x=\rho_{G}^{-1}(r)=\rho_{G}^{\prime-1}(r^{\prime})
. Then
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})=\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}^{\prime}_{G})
and
T_{G}\!\!\mid_{\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})}
and
T^{\prime}_{G}\!\!\mid_{\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}^{\prime}_{G})}
are isomorphic trees.

Proof.

We prove the property by induction on the inclusion-maximal blocks of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
and
\mathbf{T}_{G}^{\prime}
. We have that
\{x\}
belongs to both
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}^{\prime}_{G})
. Let us assume that the property holds for a subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})\cap\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}^{\prime}_{G})
of maximal blocks inducing a connected subtree of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
and
\mathbf{T}_{G}^{\prime}
containing the root block
\{x\}
. Let
S
be the set of vertices belonging to blocks in
\mathcal{B}
. Let
C
be a connected component of
G-S
. By
Lemma 9
,
C
induces a subtree of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
(respectively of
\mathbf{T}_{G}^{\prime}
) such that the root
y
(respectively
y^{\prime}
) of that subtree is the child of some vertex
z
of some block in
\mathcal{B}
(respectively
z^{\prime}
). Now observe that
as
\mathbf{T}_{G}
and
\mathbf{T}_{G}^{\prime}
are two indifference tree-layouts of
G
, both
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
and
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})
contain the vertices of
C
that are
S
-maximal vertices and that are
N(S)\cap C
-universal. It follows that
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)=B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})
. Set
B=B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
. Moreover by the indifference property, observe that
N(B)\cap S
is consecutive in
[r,y]_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}
and contains
z
. Similarly,
N(B)\cap S
is consecutive in
[r,y^{\prime}]_{\mathbf{T}_{G}^{\prime}}
and contains
z^{\prime}
. As the induction hypothesis holds on
\mathcal{B}
,
y^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}
belong to the same block of
\mathcal{B}
. It follows that the property is satisfied on
\mathcal{B}\cup\{B\}
as well, implying that
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})=\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}^{\prime}_{G})
and
T_{G}\!\!\mid_{\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})}
and
T^{\prime}_{G}\!\!\mid_{\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}^{\prime}_{G})}
are isomorphic trees.
∎

It follows that the block tree only depends on the root vertex and not on a given indifference tree-layout rooted at that vertex. Thereby, from now on, we let
\mathcal{B}_{G}(x)
and
\mathsf{B}^{\sf tree}_{G}(x)
respectively denote the set of maximal blocks and the block tree of any indifference tree-layout of
G
rooted at vertex
x
.
However, we observe that the block tree does not fully describe the possible set of indifference tree-layouts. The block tree does not reflect how each block is precisely attached to its parent block.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
be an indifference tree layout of a graph
G=(V,E)
. Let
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{T}_{G})
with
x
distinct from the root of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
. We denote by
C_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
the connected component of
G-A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
containing
x
. Let
C_{1},\dots C_{k}
be the connected components of
G[C_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)\setminus B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)]
. We define
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)=\langle\mathcal{N}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}_{k}\rangle
a collection of sets of
2^{B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)}
: for every
1\leq i\leq k
and every vertex
y\in C_{i}
such that
N(y)\cap B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)\neq\emptyset
, then we add
N(y)\cap B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
to
\mathcal{N}_{i}
. We define
\mathcal{S}_{x}=\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathcal{N}_{i}
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
be an indifference tree-layout of a connected proper chordal graph
G
. Let
x
be a vertex of
G
distinct from the root
\rho_{G}(r)
such that
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(G)
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)=\langle\mathcal{N}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}_{k}\rangle
is a collection of nested sets such that
\textsf{Nested-Convex}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x),\mathcal{S}_{x})\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We first argue that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
is a collection of nested sets. Consider two vertices
y
and
z
of some connected component
C_{i}
of
G[C_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)\setminus B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)]
that both have a neighbor in
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
. Observe that
\mathsf{lca}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y,z)
is a node mapped to a vertex of
C_{i}
. By the indifference property of
\mathbf{T}_{G}
, it follows that
N(y)\cap B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
and
N(z)\cap B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
form in
\mathbf{T}_{G}
two subpaths sharing the same least boundary, implying that
\mathcal{N}_{i}
is a nested set. Finally, observe the permutation
\sigma
of
B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
such that for every two vertices
y,z\in B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x)
,
y\prec_{\sigma}z
if and only if
y\in A_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(z)
is a permutation of
\textsf{Nested-Convex}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(x),\mathcal{S}_{x})
.
∎

.

If
\mathbf{T}_{G}\in\mathfrak{T}_{\textsf{FPQ}}(\mathsf{H}_{G}(x))

.

\mathbf{T}_{G}

and

and

and

. ∎

5
Algorithmic aspects

This section is dedicated to the design of polynomial time algorithms for proper chordal graphs. We first tackle the recognition problem of proper chordal graphs. Then we show that using the
FPQ
-hierarchies, we can resolve the graph isomorphism problem between two proper chordal graphs in polynomial time. In a way, this generalizes the
PQ
-tree based graph isomorphism algorithm for interval graphs
[
37
]
. This latter result is of interest as it shows that proper chordal graphs extends the known limit of tractability for the graph isomorphism problem. Indeed, it is known that graph isomorphism is
GI
-complete on strongly chordal graphs
[
47
]
.

5.1
Recognition

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, the recognition algorithm test for every vertex
x\in V
, if
G
has an indifference tree-layout rooted at
x
. So from now on, we assume that we are given as input the graph
G
and a fixed vertex
x
. The algorithm is then two-step. First, the first step of the algorithm aims at computing the block tree
\mathsf{B}^{\sf tree}_{G}(x)
of
G
rooted at
x
that would correspond to the skeleton tree of the
FPQ
-hierarchy
\mathsf{H}_{G}(x)
if
G
has an indifference tree-layout rooted at
x
. Then, in the second step, instead of computing
\mathsf{H}_{G}(x)
, we verify that
\mathsf{B}^{\sf tree}_{G}(x)
can indeed be turned into an indifference tree-layout of
G
. If eventually we can construct an indifference tree-layout, then
G
is proper chordal. If
G
is proper chordal and has an indifference tree-layout rooted at
x
, then the algorithm will succeed.

Computing the blocks and the block tree.

Let us assume that the input graph

is proper chordal and let us consider a vertex

that can be obtained from any indifference tree-layout rooted at

). To compute

and connects it to the current block tree, or (if

does not contain an

-block) stops and declares that there is no block tree rooted at

.

Proper chordal graph recognition

Theorem 8
.

We can decide in polynomial time whether a graph
G=(V,E)
is proper chordal. Moreover, if
G
is proper chordal, an indifference tree-layout of
G
can be constructed in polynomial time.

Proof.

We prove that Algorithm
2
recognizes proper chordal graphs. Suppose first that
G
is proper chordal. Then for some vertex
x\in V
there is an indifference tree-layout
\mathbf{T}_{G}=(T,r,\rho_{G})
rooted at
x=\rho^{-1}(r)
. By
Lemma 12
, Algorithm
1
computes
\mathcal{B}_{G}(x)
and
\mathsf{B}^{\sf tree}_{G}(x)
.
Let
B\in{\cal B}_{G}(x)
. Then, we can additionally observe that for some vertex
y\in B
,
B=B_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)\in{\cal B}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(G)
and thereby

{\cal C}_{B}={\cal C}_{\mathbf{T}_{G}}(y)
. Thus Lemma
11
implies that
\textsf{Nested-Convex}(\mathcal{C}_{B},\mathcal{S}_{B})\neq\emptyset
. It follows that Algorithm
2
correctly concludes that
G
is a proper chordal graph.

Now suppose that Algorithm
2
concludes that
G
is a proper chordal graph. This implies that for some vertex
x\in V
: Algorithm
1
returns
\mathsf{B}^{\sf tree}_{G}(x)
; and for every block
B\in\mathcal{B}(x)
,
\mathcal{C}_{B}
is a collection of nested sets such that
\textsf{Nested-Convex}(\mathcal{C}_{B},\mathcal{S}_{B})\neq\emptyset
. Now, using the proof of
Theorem 7
and the construction therein, it is possible to compute (in polynomial time) from
\mathsf{B}^{\sf tree}_{G}(x)
and
G
, an indifference tree-layout of
G
rooted at
x
, certifying that
G
is proper chordal.

Finally observe that every step of Algorithm
2
(including Algorithm
1
, see
Lemma 12
) requires polynomial time.
∎

So suppose that the block of
B_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}(G_{1})
containing
z_{1}
does not contain
y_{1}
. Let
B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{T}_{2}}(G_{2})
be the corresponding block that contains
z_{2}
but not
y_{2}
. Let
e_{1}
, respectively
e_{2}
, be the skeleton edge of
\mathsf{H}_{1}
, respectively
\mathsf{H}_{2}
incident to the root of the
FPQ
-tree of
B_{1}
, respectively of
B_{2}
. As
\mathsf{H}^{*}_{1}(x_{1})\approx^{*}_{\textsf{FPQ}}\mathsf{H}^{*}_{2}(x_{2})
, we have
\hat{A}(e_{1})=\hat{A}(e_{2})
. We observe that
y_{1}z_{1}\in E_{1}
if and only if the distance along the path from
x_{1}
to
z_{1}
in
\mathbf{T}_{1}
, between
y_{1}
and
z_{1}
is at most
\hat{A}(e_{1})+|A_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}(z_{1})\cap B_{1}|
. Likewise,
y_{2}z_{2}\in E_{2}
if and only if the distance along the path from
x_{2}
to
z_{2}
in
\mathbf{T}_{2}
, between
y_{2}
and
z_{2}
is at most
\hat{A}(e_{2})+|A_{\mathbf{T}_{2}}(z_{2})\cap B_{2}|
. Since
\mathbf{T}_{1}
and
\mathbf{T}_{2}
are isomorphic, and since
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}(G_{1})
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{T}_{2}}(G_{2})
are isomorphic, this implies that
|A_{\mathbf{T}_{1}}(z_{1})\cap B_{1}|=|A_{\mathbf{T}_{2}}(z_{2})\cap B_{2}|
. It follows that
y_{1}z_{1}\in E_{1}
if and only if
y_{2}z_{2}\in E_{2}
. We conclude that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic graphs.
∎

From
Lemma 13
, testing graph isomorphism on proper chordal graphs reduces to testing the equivalence between two indifference
FPQ
-hierarchies. To that aim, we use a similar approach to the one developed for testing interval graph isomorphism
[
37
]
. That is, we adapt the standard unordered tree isomorphism algorithm that assigns to every unordered tree a canonical
isomorphism code

[
48
,
1
]
. Testing isomorphism then amounts to testing equality between two isomorphism codes.

Let
\mathsf{H}^{*}
be an indifference
FPQ
-hierarchy of a proper chordal graph
G=(V,E)
. Intuitively, the isomorphism code of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
is a string obtained by concatenating information about the root node of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
and the isomorphism codes of the sub-hierarchies rooted at its children. To guarantee the canonicity of the isomorphism code of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
, some of the codes of these sub-hierarchies need to be sorted lexicographically. To that aim, we use the following convention:

\mathsf{L}<_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{F}<_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{P}<_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Q}<_{\mathsf{lex}}0<_{\mathsf{lex}}1\dots<_{\mathsf{lex}}n<_{\mathsf{lex}}\dots,

Moreover the separating symbols (such as brackets, commas…) used in the isomorphism code for the sake of readability are irrelevant for the sort.

Before formally describing the isomorphism code of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
, let us remind that, in an indifference
FPQ
-hierarchy, we can classify the children of any node
t
in two categories: we call a node
t^{\prime}
a
skeleton child
of
t
if the tree edge
e=tt^{\prime}
is a skeleton edge of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
, otherwise we call it a
block child
of
t
. We observe that the block children of a node
t
belong with
t
to the
FPQ
-tree of some block of
\mathcal{B}_{G}(x)
. It follows from the definition of an
FPQ
-tree, that the block children of a given node
t
are ordered and depending on the type of
t
, these nodes can be reordered. On the contrary, the skeleton children of a node
t
are not ordered.

For every node
t
of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
, we define a code, denoted
\mathsf{code}(t)
. We will define the isomorphism code of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
as
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}^{*})=\mathsf{code}(r)
, where
r
is the root node of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
. We let
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
denote the block children of node
t
(if any, and ordered from
1
to
k
) and
s_{1},\dots,s_{\ell}
denote the skeleton children of
t
(if any). For a node
t
, the set of
eligible permutations
of the indices
[1,k]
of its chidlren depends on
\mathsf{type}(t)
:

•

if
\mathsf{type}(t)=\mathsf{F}
, then the identity permutation is the unique eligible permutation;

•

if
\mathsf{type}(t)=\mathsf{P}
, then every permutation is eligible;

•

if
\mathsf{type}(t)=\mathsf{Q}
, then the identity or its reverse permutation are the two eligible permutations.

The code of
t
, denoted
\mathsf{code}(t)
, is obtained by minimizing with respect to
<_{\mathsf{lex}}
over all eligible permutations
\beta
of
t
:

\mathsf{code}(t,\beta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{size}(t)\circ\mathsf{type}(t)\circ\\
~~~~~~~~\mathsf{code}(b_{\beta(1)})\circ\dots\circ\mathsf{code}(b_{\beta(k)})\circ\\
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\mathsf{label}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(1)},\beta)\circ\mathsf{code}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(1)})\circ\dots\circ\mathsf{label}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(\ell)},\beta)\circ\mathsf{code}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(\ell)})\end{array}\right.

where:

•

\mathsf{type}(t)\in\{\mathsf{L},\mathsf{F},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Q}\}
, indicates whether
t
a leaf (
\mathsf{L}
), a
\mathsf{F}
-node, a
\mathsf{P}
-node, or a
\mathsf{Q}
-node.

•

\mathsf{size}(t)\in\mathbb{N}
, stores the number of nodes in the sub-hierarchy rooted at
t
(including
t
).

•

\mathsf{label}(s,\beta)
, with
s
being a skeleton child
s
of
t
and
\beta
being a permutation of
[1,k]
. Let
e
be the skeleton edge of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
between
s
and
t
. If
I(e)=[a,b]
, we set
I^{c}(e)=[k+1-b,k+1-a]
. Then, we set
\mathsf{label}(s)=\langle I^{\beta}(e),A(e)\rangle
, where

I^{\beta}(e)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}I(e),&\mbox{$\beta$ is the identity permutation}\\
I^{c}(e),&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

•

\pi_{\beta}
is, for some permutation
\beta
of
[1,k]
, a permutation of
[1,\ell]
that minimizes, with respect to
<_{\mathsf{lex}}
:

\mathsf{label}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(1)},\beta)\circ\mathsf{code}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(1)})\circ\dots\circ\mathsf{label}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(\ell)},\beta)\circ\mathsf{code}(s_{\pi_{\beta}(\ell)}).

Remark.
Observe that for a
\mathsf{P}
-node
t
, the permutation
\pi_{\beta}
is independant of the choice of
\beta
. Indeed, thanks to
Lemma 3
,
I(e)=[1,k]
and thereby
I^{\beta}(e)=I(e)
. So, if
t
is an
\mathsf{F}
-node or a
\mathsf{P}
-node, then
\mathsf{code}(t)
can be greedily computed in polynomial time. If
t
is a
\mathsf{Q}
-node,
\mathsf{code}(t)
can still be computed in polynomial time by comparing
\mathsf{code}(t,\beta)
and
\mathsf{code}(t,\overline{\beta})
where
\beta
is the identity permutation and
\overline{\beta}
its reverse.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*}
be indifference
FPQ
-hierarchies of the graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
respectively.
Then
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}\approx^{*}_{\textsf{FPQ}}\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*}
if and only if
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*})=\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{H}^{*}
be an indifference
FPQ
-hierarchy. We claim that given
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}^{*})
, one can deterministically construct an
FPQ
-hierarchy
\mathsf{H}^{*(rep)}
such that
\mathsf{H}^{*}\approx_{\textsf{FPQ}}^{*}\mathsf{H}^{*(rep)}
. To that aim, we need to parse
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}^{*})
in order to identify the children of each node. This can be done using the
\mathsf{size}(\cdot)
information. The smallest possible indifference
FPQ
-hierarchy
\mathsf{H}^{*}
contains a
\mathsf{Q}
-node as root and one leaf. It can clearly be reconstructed from
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}^{*})=2\mathsf{Q}1\mathsf{L}
. So suppose that the property is true for every indifference
FPQ
-hierarchy containing
i
nodes with
2\leq i\leq n
and assume that
\mathsf{H}^{*}
contains
n+1
nodes. Let
r
be the root of
\mathsf{H}^{*}
. Then
\mathsf{size}(r)\circ\mathsf{type}(r)\circ\mathsf{size}(c_{1})
, where
c
is the first child of
r
is a prefix of
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}^{*})
. Then the second child
c_{2}
of
r
can be identified after reading
\mathsf{size}(c_{1})
times a
\mathsf{type}(\cdot)
tag in
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}^{*})
. So every child of
r
can be identified. Moreover, it is possible to distinguish whether a child
c_{j}
of
r
is a block child or a skeleton child by the presence or not of the string
\mathsf{label}(c_{j})
delimited with the special characters
\langle\cdot\rangle
and to assign each of them the correct
\mathsf{label}(\cdot)
information. Finally, observe that by construction of
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}^{*})
the block children of
r
are permuted in a way compatible with its type (
\mathsf{F}
,
\mathsf{P}
or
\mathsf{Q}
). As every child of
r
is the root of a sub-hierarchy containing at most
n
nodes, by the recursive hypothesis, we can conclude that the returned
FPQ
-hierarchy
\mathsf{H}^{*(rep)}
verifies
\mathsf{H}^{*}\approx_{\textsf{FPQ}}^{*}\mathsf{H}^{*(rep)}
.

From the above discussion, if
\mathsf{code}({\mathsf{H}}_{1}^{*})=\mathsf{code}({\mathsf{H}}_{2}^{*})
, then
{\mathsf{H}}_{1}^{*(rep)}
and
\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*(rep)}
are isomorphic
FPQ
-hierarchies (in terms of labeled ordered trees). As
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}\approx^{*}_{\textsf{FPQ}}\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*(rep)}
and
\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*}\approx^{*}_{\textsf{FPQ}}\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*(rep)}
, we obtain that
{\mathsf{H}}_{1}^{*}\approx^{*}_{\textsf{FPQ}}{\mathsf{H}}_{2}^{*}
.

Suppose now that
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}\approx^{*}_{\textsf{FPQ}}\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*}
. Consider
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*(rep)}
and
\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*(rep)}
respectively computed from
\mathsf{code}({\mathsf{H}}_{1}^{*})
and
\mathsf{code}({\mathsf{H}}_{2}^{*})
. It follows that
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*(rep)}\approx^{*}_{\textsf{FPQ}}\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*(rep)}
. Now observe that by construction
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*})=\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*(rep)})
and henceforth among the set of
FPQ
-hierarchies equivalent to
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}
,
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*(rep)})
is the smallest lexicographic one. The same holds for
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*(rep)}
. Consequently, if
\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*})\neq\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*})
, then one is smaller lexicographically than the other: a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 9
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be two proper chordal graphs. One can test in polynomial time if
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
are isomorphic graphs.

Proof.

The algorithm is working as follows. First, compute a tree-layout
\mathbf{T}_{1}
of
G_{1}
and the indifference
FPQ
-hierarchy
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}
such that
\mathbf{T}_{1}\in\mathfrak{T}_{\textsf{FPQ}}(H_{1})
. This can be done in polynomial time by
Theorem 8
. Then for every vertex
x_{2}\in V_{2}
, we test if there exists an indifference tree-layout
\mathbf{T}_{2}
rooted at
x_{2}
; compute the corresponding indifference
FPQ
-hierarchy
\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*}
and test whether
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}\approx_{\textsf{FPQ}}^{*}\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*}
. By
Lemma 14
, testing equivalence between
FPQ
-hierarchies can be done by computing and comparing the isomorphism codes of
\mathsf{H}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathsf{H}_{2}^{*}
. Moreover, this latter task can be achieved in polynomial time. By
Lemma 13
, if one of these tests is positive, then we can conclude that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic graphs.
∎

6
Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that proper chordal graphs form a rich class of graphs. First, its relative position with respect to important graph subclasses of chordal graphs and the fact that the isomorphism problem belongs to
P
for proper chordal graphs shows that they form a non-trivial potential island of tractability for many other algorithmic problems. In this line, we let open the status of Hamiltonian cycle, which is polynomial time solvable in proper interval graphs
[
4
,
31
]
and interval graphs
[
32
,
5
]
, but
NP
-complete on strongly chordal graphs
[
39
]
. We were only able to resolve the special case of split proper chordal graphs. An intriguing algorithmic question is whether proper chordal graphs can be recognized in linear time. Second, the canonical representation we obtained of the set of indifference tree-layouts rooted at some vertex witnesses the rich combinatorial structure of proper chordal graphs. We believe that this structure has to be further explored and could be important for the efficient resolution of more computational problems. For example, as proper chordal graphs form a hereditary class of graphs, one could wonder if the standard graph modification problems (vertex deletion, edge completion or deletion, etc.), which are
NP
-complete by
[
34
]
, can be resolved in
FPT
time. The structure of proper chordal graphs is not yet fully understood. The first natural question on this aspect is to provide a forbidden induced subgraph characterization. This will involve infinite families of forbidden subgraphs. Furthermore, understanding what makes a vertex the root of an indifference tree-layout is certainly a key ingredient for a fast recognition algorithm. We would like to stress that a promising line of research is to consider further tree-layout based graph classes. For this, following the work of Damaschke
[
12
]
, Hell et al.
[
29
]
and Feuilloley and Habib
[
17
]
on layouts, we need to investigate in a more systematic way various patterns to exclude, including rooted tree patterns.
