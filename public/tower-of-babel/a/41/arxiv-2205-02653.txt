Analysis of the survival time of the SIRS process via expansion
Tobias Friedrich ∗, Andreas Göbel ∗, Nicolas Klodt ∗, Martin S. Krejca †, Marcus Pappik ∗

Abstract
We study the SIRS process, a continuous-time Markov chain modeling the spread of infections on graphs. In this process, vertices are either susceptible, infected, or recovered. Each infected vertex becomes recovered at rate 1 and infects each of its susceptible neighbors independently at rate λ \lambda , and each recovered vertex becomes susceptible at a rate ϱ \varrho , which we assume to be independent of the graph size. A central quantity of the SIRS process is the time until no vertex is infected, known as the survival time . The survival time of the SIRS process is studied extensively in a variety of contexts. Surprisingly though, to the best of our knowledge, no rigorous theoretical results exist so far. This is even more surprising given that for the related SIS process, mathematical analysis began in the 70s and continues to this day. We address this imbalance by conducting the first theoretical analyses of the SIRS process on various graph classes via their expansion properties. Our analyses assume that the graphs start with at least one infected vertex and no recovered vertices. Our first result considers stars, which have poor expansion. We prove that the expected survival time of the SIRS process on stars is at most polynomial in the graph size for any value of λ \lambda . This behavior is fundamentally different from the SIS process, where the expected survival time is exponential already for small infection rates. Due to this property, for the SIS process, stars constitute an important sub-structure for proving an expected exponential survival time of more complicated graphs. For the SIRS process, this argument is not sufficient. Our main result is an exponential lower bound of the expected survival time of the SIRS process on expander graphs. Specifically, we show that on expander graphs G G with n n vertices, degree close to d d , and sufficiently small spectral expansion, the SIRS process has expected survival time at least exponential in n n when λ ≥ c / d \lambda\geq c/d for a constant c > 1 c>1 . This result is complemented by established results for the SIS process, which imply that the expected survival time of the SIRS process is at most logarithmic in n n when λ ≤ c / d \lambda\leq c/d for a constant c < 1 c<1 . Combined, our result shows an almost-tight threshold behavior of the expected survival time of the SIRS process on expander graphs. Additionally, our result holds even if G G is a subgraph. This allows, for the SIRS process, the use of expanders as sub-structures for lower bounds, similar to stars in the SIS process. Notably, our result implies an almost-tight threshold for Erdős–Rényi graphs and a regime of exponential survival time for hyperbolic random graphs, one of the most popular graph models, as it incorporates many properties found in real-world networks. The proof of our main result draws inspiration from Lyapunov functions used in mean-field theory to devise a two-dimensional potential function and applying a negative-drift theorem to show that the expected survival time is exponential.

Our contribution.

We conduct the first rigorous, theoretical study of the expected survival time of the SIRS process on a large variety of graph classes, most prominently expanders.
In all of our results, we assume that the deimmunization rate is independent of the graph size and that the process starts with at least one infected vertex and no recovered vertices.
Our results showcase the similarities and the differences between the SIS and the SIRS process, highlighting the impact of the state

1.1
Expected survival time on stars

For stars, we prove the following upper bound on the expected survival time of the SIRS process.

{restatable*}

theoremStarSurvival

Let

\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]\leq\big(\!\ln(n)+2\big)(4n^{\varrho}+1)\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{\varrho}\ln(n)}}}\right)
.

Note that this bound is independent of
\lambda

[
FSP16
]

Note that these experimental results consider the infection rate

Our results also show a behavior similar to the deterministic variant of the process considered by
[
Sai19
]

The analysis mainly relies on the method of investigating independent phases in which the center is not infected, bounding the probability of the infection process dying out during that time, as is common

BBC+05
]

A phase lasts at most until all leaves triggered their recovery at least once, which occurs in expectation after a time of about

Thus, if the center just recovered, it needs to become susceptible more quickly than that bound, as otherwise all leaves are recovered.
Since deimmunization triggers at rate
\varrho

\mathrm{e}^{-\varrho\ln n}

that the infection dies out. Since these phases are independent, the infection process survives, in expectation, about
n^{\varrho}
of these trials, each lasting about
\ln(n)
time in expectation.

Note that the deimmunization rate and the state

are important for this argument to hold.
Without this additional state, that is, in the SIS process, it is quite likely that the center becomes quickly reinfected before all leaves are not infected, which leads to an exponential expected survival time once
\lambda\geq n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
in this setting
[
GMT05
]
, for all positive constants
\varepsilon

Expected survival time on expanders

Before we state our main result, we formally introduce the notion of expansion we use for our results.
To this end, let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with

-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{deg}(v_{i})\mathrm{deg}(v_{j})}}&\text{if there is an edge between $v_{i}$ and $v_{j}$},\\

The

As noted above, in contrast to stars, expanders feature many edges between arbitrary subsets of vertices. The key property we require for our results from

and

Our results hold for any expander

More formally, we define the

The proof of

1.2
consists of two main parts. First, we prove that a linear number of vertices in

becomes infected. Then, we show that the number of infected vertices stays linear for an expected exponential amount of time. For both parts, we make use of potential functions, which map the configuration of the process to a single real number that allows us to quantify how likely the process is to die out. In order to get the result on the projection of the process, we use that the influence of
G\setminus G^{\prime}
only increases the rate at which vertices in
G^{\prime}
get infected. In the considered configurations, this rate increase only helps the process to get into the desired region of the potential.

In more detail, the first part shows that the process reaches a configuration with at least
\varepsilon n

We show that

In the second part, we define a more advanced potential function

The negative-drift theorem results in the lower exponential bound of the expected survival time.

Our definition of

[

The mean-field theory assumes a fully mixed graph, which roughly corresponds to a clique for our process. In order to show global stability, the authors show a negative drift towards an equilibrium point. However, this drift is

We adjust their function appropriately to create a region in the potential that has a sufficiently large negative drift. We also alter the analysis of the function to work in the stochastic process and on expander graphs.

Expected survival time on special graph classes

The generality of

Hyperbolic random graphs

Many properties of complex real-world networks, such as the internet and social networks, are captured by hyperbolic random graphs

[

The key parameter

Using these two properties, we identify the following parameter regime for the exponential expected survival time of the SIRS process on hyperbolic random graphs.

1.4
Outlook

Although our results cover already a great range of interesting graph classes, this article is just the first step to understanding the SIRS process more thoroughly.
Our analyses pose exciting new challenges for different scenarios, which we briefly delineate in the following.

Our upper bound of the expected survival time on stars (
Section

1.1
) is off from empirical results
[
FSP16
]
by a logarithmic factor.
This shows that there is potential for improvement in the analysis.
Ideally, proving a matching lower bound would answer the question for the exact expected survival time.

Combined, our results for stars (
Section

1.1
) and expanders (
Section

1.2
) show that adding edges to a graph leads, eventually, from a polynomial expected survival time to an exponential.
However, it is not clear so far when this transition happens.
An interesting next step is to look into connected stars instead of single stars.
Connected stars appear as subgraphs in important real-world network models, such as the Chung–Lu
[
CL03
]
or the preferential-attachment model
[
BA99
]
, motivating this research question.

With respect to expanders with vertex degrees concentrated around
d
, our result (
Section

1.2
) implies that
1/d
is the threshold for the infection rate
\lambda
at which the expected survival time transitions from logarithmic to exponential.
However, our bounds require
\lambda
to be bounded away from
1/d
by a constant.
It is not clear, given a value
\varepsilon\in\mathrm{o}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1}}}\right)
, what happens if
\lambda=\frac{1\pm\varepsilon}{d}
.
A more detailed analysis could provide insights into how rapidly the transition at the threshold occurs.

A different extension of our results is to consider deimmunization rates that are dependent on the graph size.
Comparing the behavior of the SIS and the SIRS process on stars suggests that an increased deimmunization rate leads to far longer expected survival times.
Thus, an interesting question is whether the survival time exhibits a threshold behavior with respect to the deimmunization rate.

Multi-dimensional potentials, as the one we use for the SIRS process on expanders, are rare in the analysis of stopping times of stochastic processes. Our approach draws inspiration from Lyapunov stability to devise a potential function for the stochastic process under study and then applies drift theory to convert this into a rigorous proof. Lyapunov functions are used in mean-field theory to show stable points of dynamical systems
[
Lya92
]
, and epidemic processes constitute only a glimpse of their applicability. We believe that our approach might inspire further rigorous results of determining stopping times of other stochastic processes, not limited to epidemic models.

Preliminaries

We study the SIRS process, which is a continuous-time Markov chain on graphs in which the vertices change between different states, following events triggered by Poisson processes. We analyze how this process behaves asymptotically in the number of vertices
n
of the graph. Especially, when we use big-O notation or refer to variables as constants, this is with respect to
n
.
When we use big-O notation inside of a term in a relation, this means that there exists a function from the big-O expression such that the relation holds, for example, the equation
a=2^{\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)}
means that there exists a function
f\in\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
such that
a=2^{f(n)}
holds.
If not stated otherwise, all variables we consider may depend on
n
. Whenever we talk about Poisson processes, we refer to one-dimensional Poisson point processes that output a random subset of the non-negative real numbers.

We first define our infection models and some related terms that we use throughout the paper. We then state the probabilistic tools we use in the proofs.

R_{\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}=R_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}
. We say that
v

gets infected
at time point
\gamma_{i}
by

.

•

If for some
v\in V
we have
\gamma_{i}\in N_{v}
and
v\in I_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}
then
S_{\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}=S_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}
,
I_{\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}=I_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}\setminus\{v\}
and
R_{\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}=R_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}\cup\{v\}
. We say that
v

recovers
at time point
\gamma_{i}
.

•

If for some
v\in V
we have
\gamma_{i}\in O_{v}
and
v\in R_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}

S_{\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}=S_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}\cup\{v\}
,
I_{\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}=I_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}

R_{\gamma_{i}}^{\prime}=R_{\gamma_{i-1}}^{\prime}\setminus\{v\}

v

gets susceptible
at time point
\gamma_{i}
.

If none of the above three cases occurs, the configuration of
C
at
\gamma_{i}
is the same as the configuration of
C
at
\gamma_{i-1}
. Note that at all times between
\gamma_{i-1}

\gamma_{i}

\gamma_{i-1}
.

In our proofs, we only consider the time points in
P
at which the configuration changes. To this end, let
P^{\prime}=\{\gamma_{0}\}\cup\{\gamma_{i}\mid i\in\mathds{N}_{>0}\land C_{\gamma_{i}}\neq C_{\gamma_{i-1}}\}
. We index the times in
P^{\prime}
by the increasing sequence
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i\in\mathds{N}}
. For all
i\in\mathds{N}
, we call
\tau_{i}
the
i
-th
step
of the process.

If at any point in time no vertex is infected, then from that point onward, no vertex is infected.
We say that the infection
dies out
or
goes extinct
at the first (random) time
T
with
I_{T}^{\prime}=\emptyset
. We call

the

of the SIRS process.

We only keep track of the number of vertices in each of the sets. To this end, we define for all
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
the random variables
S_{t}=|S_{t}^{\prime}|
,
I_{t}=|I_{t}^{\prime}|
, and
R_{t}=|R_{t}^{\prime}|
. These random variables change depending on the clocks in
P
. We say that an event

r\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
if and only if the set of clocks that cause this event when they trigger has a sum of rates equal to
r
.

We define the
projection

C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
as the process on
G^{\prime}
such that, at each point in time, each vertex of
G^{\prime}
in

is in the same state as it is in
C
. When considering such a projection, we use

,
I_{t}
, and

to only count the vertices of
C^{\prime}
in the corresponding state. Also
\tau_{i}
only contains times at which the state of a vertex in
C^{\prime}
changes.
The survival time of a projected process is the first point in time that the projected process has no infected vertices.
Note that the survival time
T^{\prime}
of

is a lower bound for the survival time

of
C

are also infected in

.

We use stochastic domination to transfer results from one random variable to another. We say that a random variable
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}}

dominates
another random variable
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}}
if and only if there exists a coupling
(X^{\prime}_{t},Y^{\prime}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}}
in a way such that for all

2.3
Expander graphs

There are many notions of how to define expander graphs. We use algebraic expanders in which all but one of the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian of the graph are very close to
1
. These graphs have some nice properties that let us bound the number of edges between infected and susceptible vertices.
Formally, let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
n
vertices
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and let
L
be its normalized Laplacian, which is defined as

L_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ i=j,\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{deg}(v_{i})\mathrm{deg}(v_{j})}}&\text{if there is an edge between $v_{i}$ and $v_{j}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let
L
have eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq...\leq\lambda_{n}
.
The
spectral expansion
of
L
is defined as
\delta=\max_{i\geq 2}|1-\lambda_{i}|
.
We call
G
an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander if and only if it has
n
vertices, a spectral expansion of
\delta
and only vertices with degree between
(1-\varepsilon_{d})d
and
(1+\varepsilon_{d})d
.

For two vertex sets
X,Y\subseteq V
, let
E(X,Y)
denote the number of edges between
X
and
Y
. Using this notation, we have the following theorem

Theorem 2.5
(
[
Chu97
, Theorem
5.2
]
).

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
X,Y\subseteq V
. Then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left||E(X,Y)|-\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(Y)}{\text{vol}(V)}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\delta\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\text{vol}(X)\text{vol}(\overline{X})\text{vol}(Y)\text{vol}(\overline{Y})}}{\text{vol}(V)}.

Applying
Theorem

2.5
to expanders, we get the following two corollaries..

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander, and let
X\subseteq V
. Then

\displaystyle|E(X,\overline{X})|

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)(1-3\varepsilon_{d})d\frac{|X|\cdot|\overline{X}|}{n}.

Proof.

Because the vertex degrees of all vertices in
G
are bounded, we know that for each
S\subseteq V
holds
(1-\varepsilon_{d})d|S|\leq\text{vol}(S)\leq(1-\varepsilon_{d})d|S|
. Plugging that into the result of
Theorem

2.5
gives us

\displaystyle|E(X,\overline{X})|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(\overline{X})}{\text{vol}(V)}-\delta\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\text{vol}(X)\text{vol}(\overline{X})\text{vol}(\overline{X})\text{vol}(\overline{\overline{X}})}}{\text{vol}(V)}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(\overline{X})}{\text{vol}(V)}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)\frac{(1-\varepsilon_{d})d|X|\cdot(1-\varepsilon_{d})d|\overline{X}|}{(1+\varepsilon_{d})dn}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)(1-3\varepsilon_{d})d\frac{|X|\cdot|\overline{X}|}{n}.\qed

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander, and let
X,Y\subseteq V
.
If
\varepsilon_{d}\leq 1/5
, then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left||E(X,Y)|-d\frac{|X|\cdot|Y|}{n}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 4\varepsilon_{d}d\frac{|X|\cdot|Y|}{n}+2\delta d\sqrt{|X|\cdot|Y|}.

Proof.

Because the vertex degrees of all vertices in
G
are bounded, we know that for each
S\subseteq V
holds
(1-\varepsilon_{d})d|S|\leq\text{vol}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon_{d})d|S|
.
Theorem

2.5
gives us both an upper and a lower bound for
|E(X,Y)|-\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(Y)}{\text{vol}(V)}
. We solve them for
|E(X,Y)|
and bound them separately using that
\varepsilon_{d}\leq 1/5
.

\displaystyle|E(X,Y)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(Y)}{\text{vol}(V)}-\delta\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\text{vol}(X)\text{vol}(\overline{X})\text{vol}(Y)\text{vol}(\overline{Y})}}{\text{vol}(V)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(Y)}{\text{vol}(V)}-\delta\cdot\sqrt{\text{vol}(X)\text{vol}(Y)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon_{d})d|X|\cdot(1-\varepsilon_{d})d|Y|}{(1+\varepsilon_{d})dn}-\delta(1+\varepsilon_{d})d\sqrt{|X|\cdot|Y|}

\displaystyle\geq(1-4\varepsilon_{d})d\frac{|X|\cdot|Y|}{n}-2\delta d\sqrt{|X|\cdot|Y|}\textrm{ as well as}

\displaystyle|E(X,Y)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(Y)}{\text{vol}(V)}+\delta\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\text{vol}(X)\text{vol}(\overline{X})\text{vol}(Y)\text{vol}(\overline{Y})}}{\text{vol}(V)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\text{vol}(X)\cdot\text{vol}(Y)}{\text{vol}(V)}+\delta\cdot\sqrt{\text{vol}(X)\text{vol}(Y)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon_{d})d|X|\cdot(1+\varepsilon_{d})d|Y|}{(1-\varepsilon_{d})dn}+\delta(1+\varepsilon_{d})d\sqrt{|X|\cdot|Y|}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{3\varepsilon_{d}+\varepsilon_{d}^{2}}{1-\varepsilon_{d}}}}\right)d\frac{|X|\cdot|Y|}{n}+\delta(1+\varepsilon_{d})d\sqrt{|X|\cdot|Y|}

\displaystyle\leq(1+4\varepsilon_{d})d\frac{|X|\cdot|Y|}{n}+2\delta d\sqrt{|X|\cdot|Y|}.

Subtracting
d\frac{|X|\cdot|Y|}{n}
from both inequalities and combining them proves the corollary.
∎

3
SIRS on Stars

We show that the expected survival time of the SIRS process on stars is bounded from above by a polynomial in the number of vertices that is independent of the infection rate (
Section

1.1
). To this end, we bound the number of times that the center gets infected and the time between two infections of the center. We use that while the center is not infected, no leaf gets infected. Hence, if all of the leaves recover before the center gets susceptible after it recovered, the infection dies out.

We first bound the expected time that it takes for all of the leaves to recover.
We refer to each clock at a vertex whose rate is the recovery (of
1
) rate as
recovery clock
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a star with
n\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
leaves, and let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Let
T
be the time that it takes for all recovery clocks of the leaves to trigger at least once. Then
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]\leq\ln(n)+1
.

Proof.

The star has
n
leaves, which all have a clock that recovers them at a rate of
1
. For each of the clocks, the time until the first trigger happens is exponentially distributed with parameter
1
. Hence,
T
is calculated as the maximum of the
n
exponential distributions of the independent clocks. By
Theorem

2.3
,
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]\leq\ln(n)+1
.
∎

We now use
Lemma

3.1
to bound the time it takes from one infection of the center until it gets infected again or until the infection dies out.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a star with
n\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
leaves, and let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Let
t_{0}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
be a time at which the infection has not died out yet. Further, let
T\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
be the first time after
t_{0}
at which either the center gets infected after being susceptible or the infection dies out. Then
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T-t_{0}}}}\right]\leq\ln(n)+2
.

Proof.

If the center starts infected, in order for either the center to get infected again after being susceptible or the infection to die out, the center has to recover first. Let
T^{\prime}\in\mathds{R}
be the first time after
t_{0}
at which the center recovers. As all vertices recover at a rate of
1
, the random variable
T^{\prime}-t_{0}
is exponentially distributed with a parameter of
1
.

Between
T^{\prime}
and
T
, no leaf gets infected, as the center is not infected and all edges are incident to the center. Hence, when all recovery clocks of the leaves trigger in this time interval at least once, the infection dies out. Therefore, the last point in time after
T^{\prime}
at which any of these recovery clocks trigger is an upper bound for
T
. By
Lemma

3.1
, the expected time for this last trigger to happen is at most
\ln(n)+1
. That gives us

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T-t_{0}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T-T^{\prime}+T^{\prime}-t_{0}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T-T^{\prime}}}}\right]+\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T^{\prime}-t_{0}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ln(n)+2.\qed

Next, we bound the probability from below that when starting with an infected center, the infection dies out before the center gets infected again. We use this later to get an upper bound on the number of times that the center gets infected in total.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a star with
n\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
leaves, and let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Let
t_{0}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
be a time at which the center is infected. Further, let
E_{0}
be the event that the infection dies out after
t_{0}
before the center gets infected again (after being recovered in between). Then for sufficiently large
n
, it holds that
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{0}}}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{4}n^{-\varrho}
.

Proof.

In order for either the center to get infected again after being susceptible or for the infection to die out, the center has to recover first. Let
t_{1}\in\mathds{R}
be the first time after
t_{0}
at which the center recovers. As long as the center is in the recovered state, no vertex gets infected, as all edges of the graph are incident to the center. If all leaves recover before the center gets susceptible, the infection dies out. In order to bound the probability of this event, we consider the first time
T\in\mathds{R}
after
t_{1}
at which the center gets susceptible, and we also consider the first time
T^{\prime}\in\mathds{R}
after
t_{1}
at which all of the recovery clocks of the leaves trigger at least once in the interval
(t_{1},T^{\prime}]
. In particular, we use that all leaves recover before the center gets susceptible if
T^{\prime}-t_{1}<\ln(n)
and
T-t_{1}\geq\ln(n)
.

Each vertex recovers after a time that is exponentially distributed with parameter
1
. As
T^{\prime}
is the first time after
t_{1}
at which all of the recovery clocks of the leaves trigger at least once in the interval
(t_{1},T^{\prime}]
, it is the maximum of
n
exponentially distributed random variables. In order for
T^{\prime}-t_{1}<\ln(n)
, all of those random variables have to be smaller than
\ln(n)
. As all of them are independent, we get that, for sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T^{\prime}-t_{1}<\ln(n)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\text{Exp}(1)<\ln(n)}}}\right]^{n}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-1\ln(n)}}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}.

All vertices lose their immunity at a rate of
\varrho
. Hence,
T-t_{1}
is exponentially distributed with parameter
\varrho
. Using the exponential probability distribution, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T-t_{1}\geq\ln(n)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\varrho\ln(n)}

\displaystyle=n^{-\varrho}.

Now using the fact that the infection dies out when all leaves recover before the center gets susceptible and that
T-t_{1}
and
T^{\prime}-t_{1}
are independent, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{0}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T^{\prime}-t_{1}<T-t_{1}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T^{\prime}-t_{1}<\ln(n)\land T-t_{1}\geq\ln(n)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T^{\prime}-t_{1}<\ln(n)}}}\right]\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T-t_{1}\geq\ln(n)}}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}n^{-\varrho}.\qed

Using the previous bounds, we now derive an upper bound on the expected survival time of a SIRS process on a star.

\StarSurvival

Proof.

Let
S
be the random variable that counts the number of times that the center gets infected before the infection dies out. For all
i\in\mathds{N}_{\leq S+1}
, let
X_{i}
be the
i
-th time at which either the center gets infected or the infection dies out (we define
X_{0}=0
). It then holds that
T=X_{S+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{S}{X_{i+1}-X_{i}}
. We aim to bound the expectation of this value using the generalized Wald’s equation (
Theorem

2.4
).

Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}
be the natural filtration of
C
. By
Lemma

3.2
, it holds for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{\leq S}
that
0\leq\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X_{i+1}-X_{i}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{X_{i+1}-X_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{X_{i}}}}\right.}}}\right]\leq\ln(n)+2
. Hence, the expectations of all of the summed random variables are bounded. By
Lemma

3.3
, for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
, the
i
-th infection of the center has a probability of at least
\frac{1}{4}n^{-\varrho}
to be the last one if there is an
i
-th infection of the center. Therefore,
S
is dominated by a geometrically distributed random variable
A\sim\text{Geom}(\frac{1}{4}n^{-\varrho})
. Hence,
\sum_{i=0}^{S}{X_{i+1}-X_{i}}
is integrable. By
Theorem

2.4
, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T}\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{X_{i+1}-X_{i}}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{X_{i+1}-X_{i}}}\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X_{i+1}-X_{i}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{X_{i+1}-X_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{X_{i}}}}\right.}}}\right]}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X_{i+1}-X_{i}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{X_{i+1}-X_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{X_{i}}}}\right.}}}\right]}}\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{\ln(n)+2}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{\ln(n)+2}}\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle=(\ln(n)+2)\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\sum_{i=0}^{S}{1}}\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(\ln(n)+2)(4n^{\varrho}+1).\qed

4
SIRS on Expanders

We consider the SIRS process on graphs that have expanders as subgraphs. In particular, we show an exponential expected survival time for the projection of the SIRS process onto the expander when the deimmunization rate is constant and the infection rate is sufficiently high (
Section

1.2
). Note that the exponential expected survival time and the required infection rate depend only on the size and vertex degrees of the expander. In
Section

4.1
, we begin by analyzing basic properties of the process, such as the transition rates between all of the states.

In
Section

4.2
, we show that the expected survival time of the considered SIRS processes is exponential if
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
. We first prove that the process reaches a configuration with at least
\varepsilon n
infected vertices with sufficiently high probability. We then provide a lower bound for the expected survival time starting at such a configuration. To this end, we define a potential over the configuration space that has in a specific region a constant negative drift away from the configuration with no infected vertices. We then translate this region into bounds for the potential, allowing us to apply the negative-drift theorem (
Theorem

2.2
) to get an exponential expected survival time.

4.1
The SIRS Process

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be a subgraph of
G
that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
C
be a SIRS process with infection rate
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
and deimmunization rate
\varrho
on
G
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
. We define for all
t\in\mathds{N}
the random variable
P_{\tau_{t}}=S_{\tau_{t}}+\frac{\varrho}{c}n
. We use
P_{\tau_{t}}
to define the potential later. Roughly, using
P_{\tau_{t}}
instead of
S_{\tau_{t}}
has the effect that changes of
S_{\tau_{t}}
have a lower impact on the potential. Note that, at all times
t
, it holds that
S_{\tau_{t}}+I_{\tau_{t}}+R_{\tau_{t}}=n
, since every vertex of
G
is always in exactly one of these three sets. Additionally,
P_{\tau_{t}}+I_{\tau_{t}}+R_{\tau_{t}}=n+\frac{\varrho}{c}n=n^{\prime}
.

For all
t\in\mathds{N}
, one of the following four events occurs at step
t+1
(i.e.,
\tau_{t+1}
): either a susceptible vertex is infected through an edge outside of
G^{\prime}
, which we call
E_{o,t}
;
or a susceptible vertex is infected through an edge inside of
G^{\prime}
, which we call
E_{si,t}
; or an infected vertex recovers in the event
E_{ir,t}
; or a recovered vertex loses its immunity, which we call
E_{rs,t}
.

For each time point
\tau_{t}
, let
E_{\tau_{t}}(I,S)
be the number of edges between the infected and the susceptible vertices in
G^{\prime}
. At the time point
\tau_{t}
, vertices get infected by other vertices via edges inside
G^{\prime}
at a rate of
r_{si,t}=\lambda E_{\tau_{t}}(I,S)
, because every infected vertex infects each susceptible vertices at a rate of
\lambda
. Vertices recover from an infection at a rate of
r_{ir,t}=I_{\tau_{t}}
and get susceptible at a rate of
r_{rs,t}=\varrho R_{\tau_{t}}
. As we only consider the states of the vertices in
G^{\prime}
, we cannot calculate the rate
r_{o,t}
at which susceptible vertices get infected through edges outside of
G^{\prime}
, we only know that it is non-negative. Now let
r_{t}=r_{o,t}+r_{si,t}+r_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t}
. We get

\displaystyle p_{o,t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{o,t}}}}\right]=\frac{r_{o,t}}{r_{t}}\geq 0,

\displaystyle p_{si,t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{si,t}}}}\right]=\frac{r_{si,t}}{r_{t}}=\frac{\lambda E_{\tau_{t}}(I,S)}{r_{t}},

\displaystyle p_{ir,t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{ir,t}}}}\right]=\frac{r_{ir,t}}{r_{t}}=\frac{I_{\tau_{t}}}{r_{t}},\textrm{ and }

\displaystyle p_{rs,t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{rs,t}}}}\right]=\frac{r_{rs,t}}{r_{t}}=\frac{\varrho R_{\tau_{t}}}{r_{t}}.

Note that we only consider these probabilities in configurations in which at least one vertex is infected, hence
r_{t}\neq 0
and the above probabilities are well-defined. We now define

\displaystyle I^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varrho(c-1)}{(1+\varrho)c}n.

This value is the number of infected vertices in an equilibrium configuration of a SIRS process on a clique with
n
vertices and an infection rate of
\frac{c}{n}
. We use this value as a clique and the expanders we consider behave very similarly, thus,
I^{*}
is a good estimate for the number of infected vertices that
C
tends to have on
G^{\prime}
.

4.2
Exponential survival time

We now show that the infection becomes epidemic if
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
.
We start by proving that, when starting with one infected vertex inside of the expander, the infection reaches a configuration with at least
\varepsilon n
infected vertices with sufficiently large probability.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
G
that is a
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
d\rightarrow\infty
and
\delta,\varepsilon_{d}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Also let
C
start with at least one infected vertex in
G^{\prime}
and no recovered vertices in
G^{\prime}
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
.
If
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
, then there exists an
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that for sufficiently large
n
, the probability that there exists a
t\in\mathds{N}
with
I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon n
is at least
\frac{1}{n+2}
.

Proof.

Let
c^{\prime}=c-1
. Note that
c^{\prime}
is positive because
c>1
. Let
\varepsilon_{H},\varepsilon_{S}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constants that we specify later. We define for all
t\in\mathds{N}
the potential
H_{t}=H(I_{\tau_{t}},R_{\tau_{t}})=I_{\tau_{t}}-\varepsilon_{H}R_{\tau_{t}}
. Additionally, we define the stopping time
T=\inf\{t\in\mathds{N}\mid H_{t}\leq 0\lor S_{\tau_{t}}<(1-\varepsilon_{S})n\}
and the natural filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}
of
C
. We aim to show that
(H_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
is a sub-martingale until
T
. This allows us to apply the optional-stopping theorem (
Theorem

2.1
) to bound
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}}}}\right]
from below. The law of total expectation then yields a lower bound of
\frac{1}{n+2}
for
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}>0}}}\right]
. We conclude the proof by showing that if
H_{T}>0
, then
I_{\tau_{T}}\geq\varepsilon n
.

We first bound
r_{si,t}
using
Corollary

2.6
for all times
t<T
. We get

\displaystyle r_{si,t}

\displaystyle=\lambda E(I,S)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E(I+R,S)-(1+\varepsilon_{d})dR_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1-\delta)(1-3\varepsilon_{d})\frac{d(I_{\tau_{t}}+R_{\tau_{t}})S_{\tau_{t}}}{n}-(1+\varepsilon_{d})dR_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1-\delta)(1-3\varepsilon_{d})(1-\varepsilon_{S})d(I_{\tau_{t}}+R_{\tau_{t}})-(1+\varepsilon_{d})dR_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1-\delta-3\varepsilon_{d}-\varepsilon_{S})d(I_{\tau_{t}}+R_{\tau_{t}})-(1+\varepsilon_{d})dR_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq cI_{\tau_{t}}-(\delta+4\varepsilon_{d}+\varepsilon_{s})c(I_{\tau_{t}}+R_{\tau_{t}}).

We now bound for all
t\in\mathds{N}
the drift
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(H_{t+1}-H_{t})\cdot\mathds{1}_{t<T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{(H_{t+1}-H_{t})\cdot\mathds{1}_{t<T}}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{t}}}}\right.}}}\right]
. To improve readability, we omit the multiplicative
\mathds{1}_{t<T}
in all of the terms.

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{t+1}-H_{t}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{t+1}-H_{t}}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{t}}}}\right.}}}\right]

\displaystyle=(p_{si,t}+p_{o,t})\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H(I_{\tau_{t}}+1,R_{\tau_{t}})-H_{t}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+p_{ir,t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H(I_{\tau_{t}}-1,R_{\tau_{t}}+1)-H_{t}}}\right)+p_{rs,t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H(I_{\tau_{t}},R_{\tau_{t}}-1)-H_{t}}}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{si,t}+p_{o,t}-p_{ir,t}(1+\varepsilon_{H})+p_{rs,t}\varepsilon_{H}

\displaystyle\geq p_{si,t}-p_{ir,t}(1+\varepsilon_{H})+p_{rs,t}\varepsilon_{H}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(cI_{\tau_{t}}-(\delta+4\varepsilon_{d}+\varepsilon_{s})c(I_{\tau_{t}}+R_{\tau_{t}})-I_{\tau_{t}}(1+\varepsilon_{H})+\varrho R_{\tau_{t}}\varepsilon_{H}}}\right)/r_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{H}-(\delta+4\varepsilon_{d}+\varepsilon_{S})c}}\right)I_{\tau_{t}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varrho\varepsilon_{H}-(\delta+4\varepsilon_{d}+\varepsilon_{S})c}}\right)R_{\tau_{t}}}{r_{t}}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

The last inequality holds by first choosing
\varepsilon_{H}<c^{\prime}
and then choosing
\varepsilon_{S}
small enough. Then for sufficiently small
\delta
and
\varepsilon_{d}
, both of the summands are positive.

Note that
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]<\infty
because in each step
t\in\mathds{N}_{<T}
, there is a non-zero probability (independent of
t
) to recover a vertex, hence there is always a non-zero probability to recover all vertices within the next
n
steps, which stops the process. Therefore, by applying the optional-stopping theorem (
Theorem

2.1
), we get
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}}}}\right]\geq\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{0}}}}\right]
.

By the law of total expectation, we get that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{T}}H_{T}\leq 0}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\leq 0}}}\right]+\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{T}}H_{T}>0}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}>0}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{T}}H_{T}\leq 0}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot(1-\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}>0}}}\right])+\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{T}}H_{T}>0}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}>0}}}\right].

Because of the definition of
T
and the fact that
H
changes by at most
1+\varepsilon_{H}\leq 2
in one step, we get that
H_{T}\geq-2
. We also know that
H_{T}\leq n
as
I_{\tau_{T}}\leq n
. By definition of
C
, it holds that
H_{0}\geq 1
. By substituting
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}}}}\right]
in
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}}}}\right]\geq\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{0}}}}\right]
and solving for
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}>0}}}\right]
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}>0}}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{T}}H_{T}\leq 0}}\right.}}}\right]}{\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{T}}H_{T}>0}}\right.}}}\right]-\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{H_{T}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{H_{T}}H_{T}\leq 0}}\right.}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n+2}.

Now assume
H_{T}>0
. By the definition of
T
, it then holds that
S_{\tau_{T}}<(1-\varepsilon_{S})n
. Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{\tau_{T}}+R_{\tau_{T}}=n-S_{\tau_{T}}>\varepsilon_{S}n.

With
H_{T}>0
, we then get
I_{\tau_{T}}>\varepsilon_{H}R_{\tau_{T}}
, which implies

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\varepsilon_{H}^{-1}}}\right)I_{\tau_{T}}

\displaystyle>\varepsilon_{S}n.

Choosing
\varepsilon
accordingly concludes the proof.
∎

To show that the infection survives long from that point onward, we define a potential function that assigns a real number to each configuration of the process, and we analyze its drift. The potential function is an adjusted version of the Lyapunov function of
[
KW02
]
. We first define a helper function
f
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
f\colon(\mathds{R}_{>0})^{2}\to\mathds{R}
be such that, for all
x,x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, we have

\displaystyle f(x^{*},x)

\displaystyle=x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x}{x^{*}}-1}}\right).

Note that the derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}f(x^{*},x)}{\mathrm{d}x}=1-\frac{x^{*}}{x}
. Hence, for a given
x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, the value
x=x^{*}
is the only local optimum of
f(x^{*},x)
, and it is a global minimum. We now define the potential function that we use in the following lemmas.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
G
that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
and with deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
. Let
n^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\varrho}{c}}}\right)n
.
For all
t\in\mathds{N}
, we define
F_{t}
as

\displaystyle F_{t}=F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})=f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}})+f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}}).

Further, let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}
be the natural filtration of
C
. We define for all
t\in\mathds{N}
the
drift

D_{t}
as

\displaystyle D_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{F_{t+1}-F_{t}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{F_{t+1}-F_{t}}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{t}}}}\right.}}}\right].

The potential
F
becomes very large when the infection is close to dying out. We aim to show that the process tends to drift away from that high-potential region when we ignore the impact of the vertices outside of the considered subgraph and that there is a region in which the extra vertices only enlarge that drift.
To calculate the differences of the
F
values in the drift, we first have a look at
f
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and
x\in\mathds{R}_{>2}
. Then

\displaystyle f(x^{*},x+1)-f(x^{*},x)\leq 1-\frac{x^{*}}{x}+\frac{x^{*}}{x(x+1)}\textrm{ and}

\displaystyle f(x^{*},x-1)-f(x^{*},x)\leq-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{x^{*}}{x}-\frac{x^{*}}{x(x-1)}}}\right).

Proof.

We use that for all
y\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{y+1}<\ln(y+1)-\ln(y)<\frac{1}{y}.

Together with the definition of
f
, we have

\displaystyle f(x^{*},x+1)-f(x^{*},x)

\displaystyle=x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+1}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x+1}{x^{*}}-1}}\right)-x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x}{x^{*}}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-x^{*}\big(\!\ln(x+1)-\ln x\big)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{x^{*}}{x+1}.

For the second part, we get

\displaystyle f(x^{*},x-1)-f(x^{*},x)

\displaystyle=x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x-1}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x-1}{x^{*}}-1}}\right)-x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x}{x^{*}}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=-1+x^{*}\big(\!\ln x-\ln(x-1)\big)

\displaystyle\leq-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{x^{*}}{x-1}}}\right).

Noting that
\frac{x^{*}}{x+1}=\frac{x^{*}}{x}-\frac{x^{*}}{x(x+1)}
and
\frac{x^{*}}{x-1}=\frac{x^{*}}{x}+\frac{x^{*}}{x(x-1)}
concludes the proof.
∎

To bound the drift, we first show that there is an
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that if there are less than
\varepsilon n
infected vertices, the drift is maximized when
r_{o,t}
is 0.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
G
that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
. Let
E(I,S)
be the amount of edges between the infected and the susceptible vertices at time
t
, and let
r^{\prime}_{t}=\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)+r_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t}
.
If
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
, then there exists a constant
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that, for all
t\in\mathds{N}
and sufficiently large
n
, if
2\leq I_{\tau_{t}}\leq\varepsilon n
, then

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{t}\cdot D_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}}-1,I_{\tau_{t}}+1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+r_{ir,t}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}}-1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+r_{rs,t}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}}+1,I_{\tau_{t}})-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right).

Proof.

Let
t\in\mathds{N}
. For easier notation, we first define

\displaystyle F_{si,t}

\displaystyle=F(P_{\tau_{t}}-1,I_{\tau_{t}}+1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}}),

\displaystyle F_{ir,t}

\displaystyle=F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}}-1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})

\displaystyle\text{and }F_{rs,t}

\displaystyle=F(P_{\tau_{t}}+1,I_{\tau_{t}})-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}}).

We know that
r_{si,t}=\lambda E(I,S)=\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)+r_{c,t}
for some
r_{c,t}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
.
By the definition of
D_{t}
and the fact that
r_{t}=r^{\prime}_{t}+r_{o,t}+r_{c,t}
, we get that

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{t}\cdot D_{t}

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}_{t}\cdot\frac{r_{o,t}F_{si,t}+r_{si,t}F_{si,t}+r_{ir,t}F_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t}F_{rs,t}}{r_{t}}

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}_{t}\cdot\frac{r_{o,t}F_{si,t}+r_{c,t}F_{si,t}+\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)F_{si,t}+r_{ir,t}F_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t}F_{rs,t}}{r_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)F_{si,t}+r_{ir,t}F_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t}F_{rs,t}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{r_{o,t}+r_{c,t}}{r_{t}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{\prime}_{t}F_{si,t}-\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)F_{si,t}-r_{ir,t}F_{ir,t}-r_{rs,t}F_{rs,t}}}\right)

As
\frac{r_{o,t}+r_{c,t}}{r_{t}}
is non-negative, to prove the lemma it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{t}F_{si,t}-\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)F_{si,t}-r_{ir,t}F_{ir,t}-r_{rs,t}F_{rs,t}\leq 0.

By
Lemma

4.4
, we know that for all
x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and
x\in\mathds{R}_{>2}
holds

\displaystyle 1-\frac{x^{*}}{x}\leq f(x^{*},x+1)-f(x^{*},x)\leq 1-\frac{x^{*}}{x+1}.

Using these bounds, we get that

\displaystyle-F_{ir,t}

\displaystyle=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}}-1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}}-1)-f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}\textrm{, that}

\displaystyle-F_{rs,t}

\displaystyle=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}}+1,I_{\tau_{t}})-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}}+1)-f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}-1\textrm{, and that}

\displaystyle F_{si,t}

\displaystyle=F(P_{\tau_{t}}-1,I_{\tau_{t}}+1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})

\displaystyle=f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}}-1)-f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}})+f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}}+1)-f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}})

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}+1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}-1}-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}+1}.

Note that
n^{\prime}
is in
\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
and
P_{\tau_{t}}
is bounded from below by
\frac{\varrho}{c}n
, therefore
\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}-1}
is bounded from above by a constant
a
. Assume that
I_{\tau_{t}}+1\leq\varepsilon n
. Let
b=\frac{\varrho(1-c)}{(1+\varrho)c}
. Note that
b>0
is constant and
I^{*}=bn
. Using the bounds from above we get

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{t}F_{si,t}-\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)F_{si,t}-r_{ir,t}F_{ir,t}-r_{rs,t}F_{rs,t}

\displaystyle=(r_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t})F_{si,t}-r_{ir,t}F_{ir,t}-r_{rs,t}F_{rs,t}

\displaystyle\leq(r_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a-\frac{bn}{\varepsilon n}}}\right)+r_{ir,t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{bn}{\varepsilon n}}}\right)+r_{rs,t}(a-1)

\displaystyle\leq(r_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2a-\frac{b}{\varepsilon}}}\right).

We know that
(r_{ir,t}+r_{rs,t})
is non-negative, therefore we can choose
\varepsilon
small enough such that the right-hand side of the previous equation is always at most 0, which concludes the proof.
∎

We now show that the drift
D_{t}
is bounded from above by a negative constant for configurations in which the number of infected vertices is very small but still linear in
n
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
G
that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
d\rightarrow\infty
and
\delta,\varepsilon_{d}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda

\varrho
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C

G^{\prime}

\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon\in(0,1)

.

4.4

-mn

\mathrm{o}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)

r^{\prime}_{t}
and dividing the obtained bound for

by it.

Using
Lemma

4.5

Lemma

, we get

\displaystyle r^{\prime}_{t}\cdot D_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}}-1,I_{\tau_{t}}+1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+r_{ir,t}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}}-1)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+r_{rs,t}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F(P_{\tau_{t}}+1,I_{\tau_{t}})-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}+\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}(I_{\tau_{t}}+1)}}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}(P_{\tau_{t}}-1)}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+r_{ir,t}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}(I_{\tau_{t}}-1)}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+r_{rs,t}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}+\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}(P_{\tau_{t}}+1)}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)-r_{ir,t}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r_{rs,t}-\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}(I_{\tau_{t}}+1)}+\frac{\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}(P_{\tau_{t}}-1)}+\frac{r_{ir,t}I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}(I_{\tau_{t}}-1)}+\frac{r_{rs,t}n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}(P_{\tau_{t}}+1)}.

Note that with the given conditions, all values of
P_{\tau_{t}}
,
I_{\tau_{t}}
,
n^{\prime}
, and
I^{*}
are in
\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
. All of
\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)

r_{ir,t}
, and

are in
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
. Therefore, the terms in the second row of the last sum are in
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1}}}\right)
, thus we only need to bound the first part.

We know the exact values of
r_{rs,t}
and
r_{ir,t}
. However, the value of
\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)
depends on which vertices are infected. We use the expander properties of
G
and
Corollary

2.7
to bound this number. Note that both
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)
are negative and lower bounded by some constant. We get for sufficiently large
n
that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)-r_{ir,t}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r_{rs,t}-\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}-r_{ir,t}-4\varepsilon_{d}\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}-2c\delta\sqrt{I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r_{rs,t}-\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}-4\varepsilon_{d}\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}-2c\delta\sqrt{I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}-r_{ir,t}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r_{rs,t}-\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}+\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2c\delta\sqrt{I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}}+4\varepsilon_{d}\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}}}\right).

Note that
\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}+\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}
is in
\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1}}}\right)
, hence the last part of the last sum is in
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({(\delta+\varepsilon_{d})n}}}\right)
. As
\delta+\varepsilon_{d}
goes towards
0
, this is asymptotically smaller then the rest of the drift, which we show now.

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}-r_{ir,t}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r_{rs,t}-\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}S_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}P_{\tau_{t}}-\varrho I_{\tau_{t}}-I_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varrho R_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}P_{\tau_{t}}+\varrho I_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}P_{\tau_{t}}-(1+\varrho)I_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varrho n^{\prime}-\varrho P_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}P_{\tau_{t}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}P_{\tau_{t}}-(1+\varrho)I_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{c}{n}I^{*}P_{\tau_{t}}+(1+\varrho)I^{*}+\varrho n^{\prime}-\varrho P_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}P_{\tau_{t}}-\varrho\frac{n^{\prime}{}^{2}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}+\varrho n^{\prime}+\frac{c}{n}I_{\tau_{t}}n^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(c-1)I_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{\varrho(c-1)}{1+\varrho}P_{\tau_{t}}+\frac{\varrho(c-1)}{c}n+2\varrho n^{\prime}-\varrho P_{\tau_{t}}-\varrho\frac{n^{\prime}{}^{2}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}

\displaystyle=\varrho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(c-1)I_{\tau_{t}}}{\varrho}+\frac{c-1}{c+\varrho}n^{\prime}+2n^{\prime}-\frac{c+\varrho}{1+\varrho}P_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{n^{\prime}{}^{2}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right).

We aim to bound this term from above. To this end, we bound
-\frac{(c+\varrho)}{1+\varrho}P_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{n^{\prime}{}^{2}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}
from above. This term has exactly one maximum for positive
P_{\tau_{t}}
which is at
P_{\tau_{t}}=n^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{1+\varrho}{c+\varrho}}
. We also bound
I_{\tau_{t}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{0}c}{c+\varrho}n^{\prime}
from above. We get

\displaystyle\varrho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(c-1)I_{\tau_{t}}}{\varrho}+\frac{c-1}{c+\varrho}n^{\prime}+2n^{\prime}-\frac{c+\varrho}{1+\varrho}P_{\tau_{t}}-\frac{n^{\prime}{}^{2}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varrho n^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(c-1)c}{\varrho(c+\varrho)}\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{c-1}{c+\varrho}+2-2\sqrt{\frac{c+\varrho}{1+\varrho}}}}\right).

The expression in the brackets is a constant, and we aim to show that it is negative for sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{0}
. We achieve this by showing that the part without the
\varepsilon_{0}
is negative and then choosing
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough. As both
\varrho
and
c-1
are positive, we get

\displaystyle\frac{c-1}{c+\varrho}+2-2\sqrt{\frac{c+\varrho}{1+\varrho}}<0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{c-1}{c+\varrho}+2<2\sqrt{\frac{c+\varrho}{1+\varrho}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{(c-1)^{2}}{(c+\varrho)^{2}}+4\frac{c-1}{c+\varrho}+4<4\frac{c+\varrho}{1+\varrho}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{(c-1)^{2}}{(c+\varrho)^{2}}+4\frac{c-1}{c+\varrho}<4\frac{c-1}{1+\varrho}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\frac{(c-1)^{2}}{(c+\varrho)^{2}}<4\frac{(c-1)^{2}}{(1+\varrho)(c+\varrho)}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle 1+\varrho<4(c+\varrho).

The last line holds because
c>1
. Taking everything together, we get that
r^{\prime}_{t}\cdot D_{t}
is bounded from above by the sum of a constant, by a term that is in
\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({(\delta+\varepsilon_{d})n}}}\right)
, by and
-b\varrho n^{\prime}
, where
b
is a positive constant for sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{0}
.

We know that
r^{\prime}_{t}=\frac{c}{d}E(I,S)+I_{\tau_{t}}+\varrho R_{\tau_{t}}\leq cn(1+\varepsilon_{d})+n+\varrho n=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c(1+\varepsilon_{d})+1+\varrho}}\right)n
. As also
r^{\prime}_{t}\geq I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon n>0
, by dividing the inequality for
r^{\prime}_{t}\cdot D_{t}
by
r^{\prime}_{t}
, we get that there exists a constant
a\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that

, concluding the proof.
∎

We aim to apply the negative-drift theorem (
Theorem

2.2
) to bound the expected survival time of the infection. In
Lemma

4.6
, we showed a constant negative drift of the potential in a region of the configuration space. To apply the drift theorem, we first transform the configuration space restrictions into restrictions on the value of the potential. The first lemma shows that if there is at least a constant amount of infected vertices, the potential does not get too large.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
G
that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
d\rightarrow\infty
and
\delta,\varepsilon_{d}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
. Let
t\in\mathds{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be constants such that
I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon n
.
If
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
, then
F_{t}\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
.

Proof.

We aim to bound
F_{t}
from above by writing it as a sum and bounding the individual summands. To this end, we first bound the terms that appear in the summands. By the definition of our random variables and the fact that there are only
n
vertices, we get

\displaystyle\max(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}},I^{*})

\displaystyle\leq n^{\prime},

\displaystyle\min(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})

\displaystyle\geq\min(\varepsilon,\varrho/c)n.

Applying these bounds to
F_{t}
results in

\displaystyle F_{t}

\displaystyle=f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}})+f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}})

\displaystyle=n^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{P_{\tau_{t}}}{n^{\prime}}-\ln\frac{P_{\tau_{t}}}{n^{\prime}}-1}}\right)+I^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{\tau_{t}}}{I^{*}}-\ln\frac{I_{\tau_{t}}}{I^{*}}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P_{\tau_{t}}+n^{\prime}\ln\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}}+I_{\tau_{t}}+I^{*}\ln\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\prime}+n^{\prime}\ln\frac{n^{\prime}}{\min(\varepsilon,\varrho/c)n}}}\right).

As
n^{\prime}=(1+\varrho/c)n
, the calculated upper bound for
F_{t}
is linear in
n
. Thus,
F_{t}\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
.
∎

The next lemma shows that when the number of vertices becomes small, the potential gets rather large. Together with the previous lemma, this shows that having few infected vertices and having a high drift is more or less the same.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
G
that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
d\rightarrow\infty
and
\delta,\varepsilon_{d}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
. Let
t\in\mathds{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,I^{*}/n)
be constants such that
1\leq I_{\tau_{t}}\leq\varepsilon n
.
If
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
, then

\displaystyle F_{t}

\displaystyle\geq I^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{I^{*}}{n}-1}}\right).

Proof.

We aim to bound
F_{t}
from below by bounding the
f
values in its definition. Recall that for a given
x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, the function
f(x^{*},x)
is minimized for
x=x^{*}
, which is the only local extreme value for
x\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
. Therefore, we get for all
x,x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}

\displaystyle f(x^{*},x)\geq f(x^{*},x^{*})=0.

Using
1\leq I_{\tau_{t}}\leq\varepsilon n
and that for all
x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, the function
f(x^{*},x)
decreases monotonically in
x
while
x<x^{*}
, we conclude

\displaystyle F_{t}

\displaystyle=f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}})+f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}})

\displaystyle\geq 0+f(I^{*},\varepsilon n)

\displaystyle\geq I^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{I^{*}}-\ln\frac{\varepsilon n}{I^{*}}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq I^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{I^{*}}{n}-1}}\right).\qed

The next lemma shows that while the process has at least a constant fraction of vertices in the infected state, each potential next step only changes the potential by at most a constant.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
G
that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let
d\rightarrow\infty
and
\delta,\varepsilon_{d}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let
C
be a SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
. Let
t\in\mathds{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\varrho/c)
be constants. Assume that
I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon n
. Further, let
\Delta P,\Delta I\in\{-1,0,1\}
.
If
\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
, then for sufficiently large
n
, it holds that

\displaystyle|F(P_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta P,I_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta I)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})|

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+2(1+\varrho/c)\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right).

Proof.

We aim to use the triangle inequality to bound the absolute change in the
F
-values from above by the sum of the absolute changes in the
f
-values. We use that for all
x\in\mathds{R}_{>1}
holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x+1}<\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+1}{x}}}\right)<\frac{1}{x}.

Further, for all
x,x^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>2}
and
\Delta x\in\{-1,0,1\}
holds that

\displaystyle|f(x^{*},x+\Delta x)-f(x^{*},x)|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+\Delta x}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x+\Delta x}{x^{*}}-1}}\right)-x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x}{x^{*}}-1}}\right)}}\right|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\Delta x-x^{*}\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+\Delta x}{x}}}\right)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\Delta x|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x^{*}\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x+\Delta x}{x}}}\right)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{x^{*}}{x-1}.

We apply this inequality to bound the absolute change in potential from above. Note that for sufficiently large
n
, it holds that
\min(P_{\tau_{t}}-1,I_{\tau_{t}}-1)\geq\varepsilon n/2
. We conclude

\displaystyle|F(P_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta P,I_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta I)-F(P_{\tau_{t}},I_{\tau_{t}})|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta P)+f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta I)-f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}})-f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta P)-f(n^{\prime},P_{\tau_{t}})}}\right|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}}+\Delta I)-f(I^{*},I_{\tau_{t}})}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{n^{\prime}}{P_{\tau_{t}}-1}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{I^{*}}{I_{\tau_{t}}-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+\frac{n^{\prime}}{\varepsilon n/2})

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+2(1+\varrho/c)\varepsilon^{-1}).\qed

We now have the tools to apply the negative-drift theorem (
Theorem

2.2
) to bound the survival time of the infection.

Lemma 4.10

G
be a graph, and let
G^{\prime}
be a subgraph of

that is an
(n,(1\pm\varepsilon_{d})d,\delta)
-expander. Let

and
\delta,\varepsilon_{d}\rightarrow 0

. Let

G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Consider the projection
C^{\prime}
of
C
onto
G^{\prime}
. Let
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
be a constant and let

be the event that there exists a

such that

. Let

be the first time after
\tau_{t}
with

.

\lambda\geq\frac{c}{d}
for a constant

, then
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T}E_{\varepsilon_{0}}}}\right.}}}\right]=2^{\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)}

We assume that

occurs. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}
be the natural filtration of
C
, and let
t\in\mathds{N}
be such that

t
instead of

As
I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon_{0}n

4.7

a_{0}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that

\varepsilon_{c}
be the minimum of the constants from

4.5
and

We first show that for all

is large enough such that

4.9

are positive constants. Then by the contraposition of

4.8

. For all
i\in\mathds{N}
with

. Hence, by

, for all

|F_{i+1}-F_{i}|\cdot\mathds{1}_{i<T_{1}}\leq s\cdot\mathds{1}_{i<T_{1}}
. Thus, for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{\geq t}
and

holds that
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{|F_{i+1}-F_{i}|\geq j\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{|F_{i+1}-F_{i}|\geq j}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathds{1}_{i<T_{1}}\leq\frac{2^{s}}{2^{j}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{i<T_{1}}

is a constant.

We now show that condition 1 is satisfied as well. We already showed that for all

with

. Hence, the conditions for

such that for all

holds that
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{F_{i+1}-F_{i}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{F_{i+1}-F_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathds{1}_{an<F_{i}<2an}\leq-r\cdot\mathds{1}_{an<F_{i}<2an}
.

Now all of the conditions of
Theorem

2.2
are satisfied, and we get that there exists a constant
c^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T_{1}-t\leq 2^{c^{*}an/2^{s}}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T_{1}-t\leq 2^{c^{*}an/2^{s}}}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{t}}}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathds{1}_{F_{t}\leq an}=2^{-\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({an/2^{s}}}}\right)}\cdot\mathds{1}_{F_{t}\leq an}.

Note that this probability goes towards
0
as
n
goes towards infinity. Hence,
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T_{1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{t}}}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathds{1}_{F_{t}\leq an}=2^{\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)}\cdot\mathds{1}_{F_{t}\leq an}
. Remember that
I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon_{0}n
implies
F_{t}\leq an
. We therefore get
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T_{1}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{t}}}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathds{1}_{I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon_{0}n}=2^{\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)}\cdot\mathds{1}_{I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon_{0}n}
.

We showed that for all
i\in\mathds{N}
with
t\leq i<T_{1}
holds that
I_{\tau_{i}}\geq\varepsilon_{1}n>0
. Thus,
T
dominates
\tau_{T_{1}}
. Note that clocks in
C
trigger at an arbitrarily high rate, as we do not have an upper bound on
r_{t}
. However, the amounts of recovery triggers, infection triggers, and deimmunization triggers that occur until
\tau_{T_{1}}
differ by at most
n
, pairwise by type, and each of them also has an exponential expectation. As we only consider
n
recovery clocks, they trigger at a rate of at most
n
, and the expected time between each trigger is at least
\frac{1}{n}
. By Wald’s equation (
Theorem

2.4
), we get that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T}\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathds{1}_{E_{\varepsilon_{0}}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\tau_{T_{1}}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\tau_{T_{1}}}\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\right.}}}\right]\cdot\mathds{1}_{E_{\varepsilon_{0}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}2^{\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)}\cdot\mathds{1}_{E_{\varepsilon_{0}}}.\qed

We now prove our main result.

\SIRSClique

Proof.

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, let
E_{\varepsilon}
be the event that there exists a
t\in\mathds{N}
such that
I_{\tau_{t}}\geq\varepsilon n
. By
Lemma

4.1
, there exists an
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that for sufficiently large
n
holds that
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{\varepsilon}}}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{n+2}
. By
Lemma

4.10
, it holds that
\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T}E_{\varepsilon}}}\right.}}}\right]=2^{\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)}
. Using the law of total expectation, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{\varepsilon}}}}\right]\mathds{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{T}E_{\varepsilon}}}\right.}}}\right]+\mathrm{Pr}\big[{\overline{E_{\varepsilon}}}\big]\mathds{E}\big[{T\ \big|\ \vphantom{T}\overline{E_{\varepsilon}}}\big]

Hyperbolic random graphs

For the formal definition of a hyperbolic random graph, we refer the reader to the article by

The two key properties we require for our main result to be applicable on hyperbolic random graphs are the following.

Theorem 5.2
(
[
FK18
, Theorem
1
]
).

Let
G
be a hyperbolic random graph with
n
vertices that follows a power-law degree distribution with exponent
\gamma\in(2,3)
. Then the diameter of the giant component of
G
is
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({(\log n)^{2/(3-\gamma)}}}}\right)
with probability
1-\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-3/2}}}}\right)
.

Theorem 5.3
(
[
FK15
]
).

Let
G
be a hyperbolic random graph with
n
vertices that follows a power-law degree distribution with exponent
\gamma\in(2,3)
. Then the size of the largest clique of
G
is in
\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{(3-\gamma)/2}}}}\right)
with high probability.

We first use the poly-logarithmic diameter to show that the infection reaches the largest clique with a sufficient probability when the process starts with at least one infected vertex.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
G
be a hyperbolic random graph with
n
vertices that follows a power-law degree distribution with exponent
\gamma\in(2,3)
, and let
C
be an SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho
. Further, let
C
start with at least one infected vertex in the giant component and no recovered vertices in the giant component.
If
\lambda\geq cn^{(\gamma-3)/2}
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, then the probability that the infection reaches a state in which a vertex in the largest clique is infected is at least
\exp\!\big(\!-\!(\ln n)^{3/(3-\gamma)}\big)
for sufficiently large
n
.

Proof.

Let
v
be a vertex that starts infected, and let
d
be the shortest distance from
v
to any vertex of the largest clique. Note that
d
is bounded from above by the diameter of the giant component. Therefore, by
Theorem

5.2
, there exists a constant
a\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that for sufficiently large
n
with a probability of at least
\frac{1}{2}
, it holds that
d\leq a(\ln n)^{2/(3-\gamma)}
.

For all
i\in\mathds{N}
, let
E_{i}
be the event that
C
reaches a state with an infected vertex that has a distance of
i
to the largest clique. Consider for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{<d}
the probability
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{i}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{E_{i}}E_{i+1}}}\right.}}}\right]
. Each vertex with a distance of
i+1
to the largest clique has a neighbor that has a distance of
i
to the clique. With a probability of
\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}
, an infected vertex infects a specific neighbor before recovering. Therefore,
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{i}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{E_{i}}E_{i+1}}}\right.}}}\right]\geq\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\geq\frac{c}{2}n^{(\gamma-3)/(2)}
for sufficiently large
n
.

With a probability of at least
\frac{1}{2}
, it holds that
d\leq a(\ln n)^{2/(3-\gamma)}
. This yields for sufficiently large
n
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{0}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}{\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{E_{i}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{E_{i}}E_{i+1}}}\right.}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}{\frac{c}{2}n^{\frac{\gamma-3}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{2}n^{\frac{\gamma-3}{2}}}}\right)^{d}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{2}n^{\frac{\gamma-3}{2}}}}\right)^{a(\ln n)^{\frac{2}{3-\gamma}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\gamma-3}{2}a(\ln n)^{\frac{5-\gamma}{3-\gamma}}+\ln(c/2)}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-(\ln n)^{\frac{3}{3-\gamma}}}.\qed

1.2
yields an exponential expected survival time for a sufficiently large infection rate.

k
be the size of the largest clique of

5.3
, there exists a constant
a\in\mathds{R}_{>0}

k\geq an^{(3-\gamma)/2}
