Control-enhanced multiparameter quantum estimation
Jing Liu, Haidong Yuan

Abstract
Most studies in multiparameter estimation assume the dynamics is fixed and focus on identifying the optimal probe state and the optimal measurements. In practice, however, controls are usually available to alter the dynamics, which provides another degree of freedom. In this paper we employ optimal control methods, particularly the gradient ascent pulse engineering (GRAPE), to design optimal controls for the improvement of the precision limit in multiparameter estimation. We show that the controlled schemes not only capable to provide a higher precision limit, but also have a higher stability to the inaccuracy of the time point performing the measurements. This high time stability will benefit the practical metrology where it is hard to perform the measurement at a very accurate time point due to the response time of the measurement apparatus.

II
multiparameter estimation

Typically to estimate multiple parameters encoded in some quantum state
\rho_{\vec{x}}
, where

\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{d})
, one needs to perform a set of positive-operator valued measurements (POVM)

\{E(y)\}
(
\sum_{y}E(y)=\openone
with
\openone
the identity) on the state, which will give the result
y
with
probability
p_{y|\vec{x}}=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{\vec{x}}E(y))
. From the measurement result one can then construct an estimator

\vec{\hat{x}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},...,\hat{x}_{d})
. According to the Cramér-Rao bound, the covariance of any unbiased
estimator is bounded below by the (classical) Fisher information matrix as
Helstrom
;
Holevo

C\geq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}.

(1)

Here
C
denotes the covariance matrix with the entries
C_{\alpha\beta}:=\sum_{y}(\hat{x}_{\alpha}-x_{\alpha})(\hat{x}_{\beta}-x_{\beta})p_{y|\vec{x}}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\{1,2,...,d\}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}

denotes the classical Fisher information matrix (CFIM) with the
\alpha\beta
th entry given by

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\beta}:=\sum_{y}\frac{(\partial_{x_{\alpha}}p_{y|\vec{x}})(\partial_{x_{\beta}}p_{y|\vec{x}})}{p_{y|\vec{x}}}.

(2)

The covariance matrix can be further bounded by the quantum Fisher information matrix

C\geq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}\geq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{q}}^{-1},

(3)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{q}}
is the quantum Fisher information matrix (QFIM) with the

\alpha\beta
th entry given by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{q},\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\{L_{\alpha},L_{\beta}\})
.

L_{\alpha}
is the symmetric logarithmic derivative for
x_{\alpha}
which is the solution to the equation

\partial_{x_{\alpha}}\rho=(\rho L_{\alpha}+L_{\alpha}\rho)/2
.
In multiparameter quantum estimation the bound given by the QFIM
is usually not saturable
Dattareview
;
Matsumoto2002
;
Ragy2016
;
Pezze2017
,
therefore we will focus on the classical Cramér-Rao bound. In this paper we will take

|\delta\vec{\hat{x}}|^{2}:=\sum^{d}_{i=1}\delta^{2}\hat{x}_{i}

as the figure of merit, from the Cramér-Rao bound we have

|\delta\vec{\hat{x}}|^{2}\geq\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}.

(4)

Thus the controls should be designed to minimize
\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}
.

For the case with only two parameters, Eq. (
4
) reduces to

|\delta\vec{\hat{x}}|^{2}\geq F^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl,e}}

Liu2016
, where

F_{\mathrm{cl,e}}=\det\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}/\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}
, here
\det(\cdot)
denotes the determinant.
For the cases with more than two parameters, it is in general difficult to obtain the
analytical expression for
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}
directly, which means
this function is not very friendly to a gradient-based algorithm in general as in each iteration the
gradient needs to be evaluated numerically which is very demanding computationally.
Usually the bound will be further relaxed to ease the computation. Since

[\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}]_{\alpha\alpha}\geq 1/\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}
, we then have

\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}\geq\sum_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}}.

(5)

We will then also take the following quantity

f_{0}(T)=\left(\sum_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}(T)}\right)^{-1}

(6)

as an objective function to be maximized. However, the obtained controls will be eventually evaluated by their effects on the
figure of merit
\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}
, which characterizes the precision limit of
\delta\vec{\hat{x}}
.
In many cases this provides efficient algorithms lead to near optimal controls.

Algorithm

We consider the systems whose dynamics can be described by the master equation

\partial_{t}{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)],

(7)

where
\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\Gamma(\rho)
, here

\Gamma(\rho)
describes the effect of the noises and
H
denotes the Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian can be decomposed as

Khaneja05
;
Ernst1987

H=H_{0}(\vec{x})+\sum_{k=1}^{p}V_{k}(t)H_{k},

(8)

where
H_{0}(\vec{x})
is the Hamiltonian for the free evolution which contains the unknown parameters
\vec{x}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{p}V_{k}(t)H_{k}
is the control Hamiltonian with
V_{k}(t)
denotes the amplitude
of
k
th control field.

The solution of Eq. (
7
) at the target time
T
can be approximated as

\rho(T)=\Pi_{i=1}^{m}\exp(\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{i})\rho(0)
, with
\mathcal{L}_{i}
the super-operator
for the
i
th time step and
m=T/\Delta t
sufficiently large, here the multiplication
in
\rho(T)
is taken from right to left in the increasing order of
i
.

We then employ gradient ascent pulse engineering (GRAPE)
Khaneja05
to obtain optimal controls
that can maximize
f_{0}(T)
(
F_{\mathrm{cl,e}}
for two-parameter estimation). The flow of the algorithm,
shown in Fig.
1
, is as following:

1.

guess a set of initial values for
V_{k}(j)
(
V_{k}(j)
is the
k
th control at the
j
th time step);

2.

evolve the dynamics with the controls;

3.

calculate the objective function
f_{0}(T)
(
F_{\mathrm{cl,e}}
for two-parameter estimation);

4.

calculate the gradient
\frac{\delta f_{0}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

(
\frac{\delta F_{\mathrm{cl,e}}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}
for two-parameter estimation
)
;

5.

update
V_{k}(j)
to
V_{k}(j)+\epsilon\frac{\delta f_{0}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}
;

6.

go to step 2 until the objective function converges.

The detailed calculation of the gradient for
f_{0}(T)
, which is based on the gradient
of the entries of CFIM and an essential step for GRAPE, can be found in appendix
A
.

GRAPE can be further improved via the quasi-Newton optimization

,
which utilize the gradient history to construct an approximation to the Hessian matrix. Davidon-Fletcher-Powell
formula

and Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno algorithm

are two well applied methods
for this construction. This quasi-Newton optimization can also be utilized here in quantum parameter estimation
for the improvement of GRAPE performance.

Apart from GRAPE, other optimization methods, such as Krotov’s
method

;
Machnes2011
;
Reich2012
;
Goerz2015
;
Kochreview
, hybrid update method

and hybrid optimization
method

for quantum parameter estimation will be studied in future works.

IV
Application

We demonstrate the effect of controls with several examples in Hamiltonian parameter estimation.

IV.1
Noiseless scenario

Example 1
: Consider a two-qubit system where one qubit is in a magnetic field and the other
qubit acts as an ancillary qubit. The Hamiltonian of this system is
H=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)
where

H_{0}=\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{(1)},

(9)

\vec{\sigma}^{(1)}=(\sigma^{(1)}_{1},\sigma^{(1)}_{2},\sigma^{(1)}_{3})
with

\sigma^{(1)}_{i}=\sigma_{i}\otimes\openone_{2}
,
here
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
are Pauli matrices,
\openone_{2}
denotes the 2 by 2 identity matrix.
Similarly we will use
\vec{\sigma}^{(2)}=(\sigma^{(2)}_{1},\sigma^{(2)}_{2},\sigma^{(2)}_{3})

to denote
\sigma^{(2)}_{i}=\openone_{2}\otimes\sigma_{i}
. With the absence of control, the QFIM can be
attained via Bell measurement
Yuan2016
. For controlled scheme, the local controls on the qubits

H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\sum_{i=1,2}\vec{V}_{i}(t)\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{(i)}

(10)

are employed. This problem has been studied previously and analytical solutions for optimal controls have
been obtained

. It has been shown that the optimal controls is to reverse the free evolution
and the optimal initial state is any maximally entangled state, such as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle\right)
,
and the optimal measurement is the projective measurement on the Bell basis:

|\Phi^{\pm}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle\right)

|\Psi^{\pm}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|01\rangle\pm|10\rangle\right)

The precision limit for the three parameters

\vec{B}=(B\sin\theta\cos\phi,B\sin\theta\sin\phi,B\cos\theta)

optimal control is

\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{3}{4T^{2}}.

(11)

As a confirmation of validity of GRAPE, we compare the performance of the controls obtained from GRAPE
(the objective function is
f_{0}
). It can be seen from Fig.
2
(a) that the
performance of the controls (red dots) obtained from GRAPE coincides with the analytical solutions, which

Example 2
: Next we consider the Hamiltonian estimation for a two-qubit system

IV.2
Noisy scenario

We now consider the dynamics with dephasing noises in all examples discussed above.

Example 1
: Again we consider a two-qubit system where the magnetic field only acts on the first
qubit and the second qubit acts as an ancillary system. For example, in Nitrogen vacancy center, the electron spin can act
as the sensing qubit while a neighboring nuclear spin can act as an ancillary system. Recall that the Hamiltonian is given
by
H=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)
where
H_{0}=\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{(1)}
and

H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\sum_{i=1,2}\vec{V}_{i}(t)\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{(i)}
.

Assume that the sensing qubit (first qubit) suffers from the dephasing noise, the dynamics is then described by the master equation

\partial_{t}\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(\sigma^{(1)}_{3}\rho\sigma^{(1)}_{3}-\rho\right).

(16)

Figure
2
(a) (cyan stars) shows the advantages of controlled scheme in improving the precision limit.
From the figure it can also be seen that the controls also suppress the oscillations, thus improve the time stability.

Example 2
: Consider the Hamiltonian with ZZ coupling
H=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)

H_{0}=\omega_{1}\sigma^{(1)}_{3}+\omega_{2}\sigma^{(2)}_{3}+g\sigma^{(1)}_{3}\sigma^{(2)}_{3}
and

H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\sum_{i=1,2}\vec{V}_{i}(t)\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{(i)}
.
With the presence of dephasing, the dynamics is described by

\partial_{t}\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{i=1,2}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{2}\left(\sigma_{3}^{(i)}\rho\sigma_{3}^{(i)}-\rho\right),

(17)

where
\gamma_{i}
is the dephasing rate for
i
th qubit. Fig.
2
(b) shows that the
controlled scheme improves the precision limit under the local measurement
E_{\pm}
. It can also be seen
that the controls suppressed the oscillations in the precision limit, thus improved the time stability.

Example 3
: Consider the Hamiltonian estimation with XXZ coupling where

H_{0}=-x_{1}(\sigma^{(1)}_{1}\sigma^{(2)}_{1}+\sigma^{(1)}_{2}\sigma^{(2)}_{2})-x_{2}\sigma^{(1)}_{3}\sigma^{(2)}_{3}
and the control Hamiltonian is

H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\sum_{i=1,2}\vec{V}_{i}(t)\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{(i)}
,
both qubits have the dephasing. The master equation for this system is in the form of Eq. (
17
),
and we will assume the dephasing rates are the same for both qubits, i.e.,
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\gamma
.
The CFIM for non-controlled scheme under the practical local measurement

E_{\pm}
is given by (see detailed derivation in appendix
D
)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}=2T^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\delta_{+}+\delta_{-}&\delta_{+}-\delta_{-}\\
\delta_{+}-\delta_{-}&\delta_{+}+\delta_{-}\end{array}\right),

(18)

where

\delta_{\pm}:=\frac{\cos^{2}\left[2T(x_{1}\pm x_{2})\right]}{e^{2\gamma T}-\sin^{2}\left[2T(x_{1}\pm x_{2})\right]}.

(19)

The precision limit for non-controlled scheme is then

\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{4T^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{+}}+\frac{1}{\delta_{-}}\right).

(20)

It can be seen that when

2
(c).

F_{\mathrm{cl,e}}
, the precision
limit (cyan stars in Fig.
2
(c)) is improved compared the uncontrolled case
(dashed blue line in Fig.
2
(c)). Also, the oscillations is suppressed, indicating
that the controlled scheme provides a higher time stability.

V
Summary

In this paper, we employed the optimal control method, GRAPE in particular, in designing optimal controls for
the improvement of the precision limit in multiparameter quantum estimation. Advantages on the precision limit
of controlled schemes are demonstrated through three examples, including the estimation of a
magnetic field, Hamiltonian estimation with ZZ and XXZ couplings. The controls not only improve the precision limit,
but also the time stability, especially for noisy systems.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[D_{j+1}^{m}\frac{\delta\rho_{j}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}E(y)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(1)}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x_{\alpha}}p_{y|\vec{x}}\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left[E(y)\partial_{x_{\alpha}}\!\!\left(D_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}\right)\right]

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{E(y)\Big[\left(\partial_{x_{\alpha}}D_{j+1}^{m}\right)H_{k}^{\times}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle+D_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\partial_{x}\rho_{j}\Big]\Big\}.

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\left(\partial_{x_{\alpha}}p_{y|\vec{x}}\right)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j,\alpha}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{m}H_{k}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{j}\dot{H}_{0}^{\times}(\rho_{i}),

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j,\alpha}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\delta_{jm})\!\!\!\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\!\!\mathcal{D}_{i+1}^{m}\dot{H}_{0}^{\times}\mathcal{D}_{j+1}^{i}H_{k}^{\times}(\rho_{j}).

Utilizing above expressions, the gradient for
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\beta}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\beta}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{L}_{2,\alpha\beta}\mathcal{M}_{j}^{(1)}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}t\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tilde{L}_{1,\beta}\left(\!\mathcal{M}_{j,\alpha}^{(2)}\!+\!\mathcal{M}_{j,\alpha}^{(3)}\!\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}t\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tilde{L}_{1,\alpha}\left(\!\mathcal{M}_{j,\beta}^{(2)}\!+\!\mathcal{M}_{j,\beta}^{(3)}\!\right)\right],

where
\tilde{L}_{1,\alpha(\beta)}
and
\tilde{L}_{2,\alpha\beta}
are

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{1,\alpha(\beta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\left(\partial_{x_{\alpha(\beta)}}\ln p_{y|\vec{x}}\right)E(y),

(29)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{2,\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}\left(\partial_{x_{\alpha}}\ln p_{y|\vec{x}}\right)\left(\partial_{x_{\beta}}\ln p_{y|\vec{x}}\right)E(y).

(30)

For two parameter estimation, the objective function is

F_{\mathrm{cl,e}}=\frac{\det\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}}=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\beta\beta}-\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\beta}}{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}+\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\beta\beta}}.

(31)

The corresponding gradient is

\frac{\delta F_{\mathrm{cl,e}}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=\!\!\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}\!\!\frac{\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mathrm{cl},\beta\beta}+\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\beta}}{\mathrm{Tr}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}-\frac{2\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\beta}}{\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl}}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\beta}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}

(32)

For parameter estimation with more than two parameters, recall the objective function is

f_{0}(T)=\left(\sum_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}(T)}\right)^{-1}.

(33)

The gradient of
f_{0}(T)
then reads

\frac{\delta f_{0}(T)}{\delta V_{k}(j)}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{f^{2}_{0}(T)}{\mathcal{F}^{2}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\alpha\alpha}}{\delta V_{k}(j)}.

(34)

Appendix B
Calculation of noisy CFIM and QFIM in the estimation of magnetic field

In this appendix we show the detailed calculation in the estimation of magnetic field.
Recall that the scenario is a two-qubit system where the magnetic field only acts on the first qubit and the
second qubit acts as an ancillary system, the Hamiltonian is given by
H=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{c}}(t)
where

H_{0}=\vec{B}\cdot\vec{\sigma}^{(1)},

(35)

\vec{\sigma}^{(1)}=(\sigma^{(1)}_{1},\sigma^{(1)}_{2},\sigma^{(1)}_{3})
with

\sigma^{(1)}_{i}=\sigma_{i}\otimes\openone_{2}
,
where
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
are Pauli matrices,
\openone_{2}
denotes the 2 by 2 identity matrix.
Similarly we denote
\vec{\sigma}^{(2)}=(\sigma^{(2)}_{1},\sigma^{(2)}_{2},\sigma^{(2)}_{3})

with
\sigma^{(2)}_{i}=\openone_{2}\otimes\sigma_{i}
.

With the initial state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)
, the evolved state without noise can be
straightforwardly obtained in the basis
\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}
as below

|\psi(T)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos(BT)-i\sin(BT)\cos\theta\\
-i\sin(BT)\sin\theta e^{-i\phi}\\
-i\sin(BT)\sin\theta e^{i\phi}\\
\cos(BT)+i\sin(BT)\cos\theta\\
\end{array}\right).

(36)

For the further calculation, we rewrite the corresponding density matrix in block form

|\psi(T)\rangle\langle\psi(T)|=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X&Y\\
Y^{\dagger}&Q\end{array}\right),

where
X
,
Y
and
Q
are 2 by 2 matrices. Recall the master equation involving the noise is

\partial_{t}\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(\sigma^{(1)}_{3}\rho\sigma^{(1)}_{3}-\rho\right).

(37)

The solution for above equation can be expressed in the following form

\rho(T)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X&e^{-\gamma T}Y\\
e^{-\gamma T}Y^{\dagger}&Q\end{array}\right).

(38)

Performing the Bell measurements, the probability distribution is

\displaystyle p_{\Phi^{+}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[(1+e^{-\gamma T})\!\cos^{2}(BT)\!+\!(1-e^{-\gamma T})\!\sin^{2}(BT)\!\cos^{2}\theta],

\displaystyle p_{\Phi^{-}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[(1-e^{-\gamma T})\!\cos^{2}(BT)\!+\!(1+e^{-\gamma T})\!\sin^{2}(BT)\!\cos^{2}\theta],

\displaystyle p_{\Psi^{+}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}(BT)\sin^{2}\theta[1+e^{-\gamma T}\cos(2\phi)],

\displaystyle p_{\Psi^{-}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}(BT)\sin^{2}\theta[1-e^{-\gamma T}\cos(2\phi)].

According to the probabilities, the entries of CFIM can then be calculated as below

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\phi\phi}=\frac{4e^{-2\gamma T}\sin^{2}(BT)\sin^{2}\theta\sin^{2}(2\phi)}{1-e^{-2\gamma T}\cos^{2}(2\phi)},

(39)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\theta\phi}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},B\phi}=0
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\sin^{2}(BT)\left\{\cos^{2}\theta+\frac{\sin^{2}(BT)\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\theta[(1+3e^{-2\gamma T})\cos^{2}(BT)+(1-e^{-2\gamma T})\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]}{[\cos^{2}(BT)+\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]^{2}-e^{-2\gamma T}[\cos^{2}(BT)-\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]^{2}}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},BB}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4T^{2}\cos^{2}(BT)\Big\{\sin^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}(BT)\times

\displaystyle\frac{[\sin^{4}\theta+e^{-2\gamma T}(1+\cos^{2}\theta)^{2}][1-\sin^{2}(BT)\sin^{2}\theta]+2e^{-2\gamma T}\sin^{2}\theta(1+\cos^{2}\theta)[-\cos^{2}(BT)+\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]}{[\cos^{2}(BT)+\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]^{2}-e^{-2\gamma T}[\cos^{2}(BT)-\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]^{2}}\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cl},B\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\sin(2BT)\sin(2\theta)\Big\{1+\sin^{2}(BT)\times

\displaystyle\frac{(1+e^{-2\gamma T})\cos^{2}(BT)\sin^{2}\theta+(1-e^{-2\gamma T})\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta-2e^{-2\gamma T}\cos^{2}(BT)(1+\cos^{2}\theta)}{[\cos^{2}(BT)+\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]^{2}-e^{-2\gamma T}[\cos^{2}(BT)-\sin^{2}(BT)\cos^{2}\theta]^{2}}\Big\}.

Although the CFIM has analytical solution, it is still difficult to obtain the general solution of

\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}
analytically.
Here we consider the case that
\theta
is small. The expression of
\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}
approximates to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathrm{cl}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2\gamma T}-\cos^{2}(2\phi)}{2[1-\cos(2BT)]\sin^{2}(2\phi)}\theta^{-2}-\frac{1+\cos(2BT)}{1-\cos(4BT)}

(40)

\displaystyle-\frac{1-2e^{2\gamma T}+\cos(4BT)}{4T^{2}[1-\cos(4BT)]}.

The non-zero eigenvalues of the noisy density matrix are
\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm e^{-\gamma T})
, which
are independent of the magnetic field. The eigenstates for
\lambda_{\pm}
are
|\lambda_{+}\rangle=|\psi(T)\rangle
and

|\lambda_{-}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos(BT)-i\sin(BT)\cos\theta\\
-i\sin(BT)\sin\theta e^{-i\phi}\\
i\sin(BT)\sin\theta e^{i\phi}\\
\cos(BT)+i\sin(BT)\cos\theta\end{array}\right).

(41)

It is known that the QFIM can be calculated via the density matrix’s non-zero eigenvalues and
corresponding eigenstates
Liu2014
;
Liu2014a
. Utilizing above eigenstates, The entries of
QFIM can be calculated as below

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{q},BB}=4T^{2}\left(\cos^{2}\theta e^{-2\gamma T}+\sin^{2}\theta\right),

(42)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{q},\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\sin^{2}(BT)\Big\{\cos^{2}\theta

(43)

\displaystyle+\sin^{2}\theta\left[e^{-2\gamma T}\cos^{2}(BT)+\sin^{2}(BT)\right]\!\!\Big\}\!,

and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{q},\phi\phi}\!=\!4\sin^{2}\!\theta\sin^{2}(BT)\!\left[1\!-\!(1-e^{-2\gamma T})\sin^{2}\!\theta\sin^{2}(BT)\right]\!.

(44)

The off-diagonal entries are

(45)

it can be seen that the Bell measurement fails to be optimal when noise exists.
