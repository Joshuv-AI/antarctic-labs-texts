Radial Profiles of Surface Density in Debris Discs 2020 Radial Profiles of Surface Density in Debris Discs – D
Roman R. Rafikov

Abstract
Resolved observations of debris discs can be used to derive radial profiles of Azimuthally-averaged Surface Density (ASD), which carries important information about the disc structure even in presence of non-axisymmetric features and has improved signal-to-noise characteristics. We develop a (semi-)analytical formalism allowing one to relate ASD to the underlying semi-major axis and eccentricity distributions of the debris particles in a straightforward manner. This approach does not involve the distribution of particle apsidal angles, thus simplifying calculations. It is a much faster, more flexible and effective way of calculating ASD than the Monte Carlo sampling of orbital parameters of debris particles. We present explicit analytical results based on this technique for a number of particle eccentricity distributions, including two cases of particular practical importance: a prescribed radial profile of eccentricity, and the Rayleigh distribution of eccentricities. We then show how our framework can be applied to observations of debris discs and rings for retrieving either the semi-major axis distribution or (in some cases) the eccentricity distribution of debris, thus providing direct information about the architecture and dynamical processes operating in debris discs. Our approach also provides a fast and efficient way of forward modeling observations. Applications of this technique to other astrophysical systems, e.g. the nuclear stellar disc in M31 or tenuous planetary rings, are also discussed.



1
Introduction

High-resolution observations of debris discs allow us to determine the spatial distribution of brightness contributed by debris particles in projection onto the sky plane. Brightness distribution can be converted to a spatial distribution of the surface density of emitting particles
\Sigma(r,\phi)
in polar
(r,\phi)
coordinates in the plane of the disc, using straightforward assumptions, e.g. the knowledge of particle emissivity as a function of
r
(and
\phi
in the case of a disc directly imaged in the near-IR or optical bands), information on disc inclination (and the possibility of it being warped), and so on
(
Wyatt 2008
)
. Such a conversion provides us with very important information about the instantaneous spatial distribution of debris, which can be linked to processes that shaped the underlying planetary system in the first place.

Inference of
\Sigma(r,\phi)
from observations can also inform us about the eccentricities and inclinations of the emitting particles (as non-circular motion of the debris particles is another factor affecting
\Sigma(r,\phi)
), shedding light on the dynamical processes shaping the disc. This may allow one to distinguish dynamical shaping of the disc by the embedded planets via secular forcing
(
Lee &
Chiang 2016
;
Pearce &
Wyatt 2014
;
Sefilian
et al. 2021
)
or through self-stirring
(
Kennedy &
Wyatt 2010
;
Marino 2021
)
.

The goal of this work is to present a mathematical formalism allowing one to easily connect the semi-major axis distribution of debris particles and their dynamical state (i.e. their eccentricity distribution) to a certain characteristic of
\Sigma(r,\phi)
that can be inferred from observation. More specifically, focusing on the case of a flat, co-planar disc we define
Axisymmetric (or Azimuthally-averaged) Surface Density

\bar{\Sigma}
(hereafter ASD) as the azimuthal average (zeroth-order azimuthal moment) of its
\Sigma(r,\phi)
:

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Sigma(r,\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi.

(1)

Clearly,
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
can be easily determined once
\Sigma(r,\phi)
is inferred from observations as described earlier. ASD has a higher signal-to-noise ratio than
\Sigma(r,\phi)
, making
\bar{\Sigma}
a metric of choice for detailed analysis in many studies of debris discs
(
Marino 2021
)
.

In this work we provide a way to link
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
to the semi-major axis distribution of Keplerian particles comprising the disc, given the knowledge of their kinematic properties. We also explore the possibility of using the observationally inferred ASD to solve the inverse problem of determining the radial mass distribution in the disc, or the dynamical characteristics (i.e. the eccentricity distribution) of the debris particles.

This work is organised as follows. After outlining our basic setup (§
2
), we proceed to develop our formalism for discs with a prescribed profile of eccentricity (§
3
), with the master equation (
16
) being the key result. We then extend our approach to the case of a continuous distribution of particle eccentricities (§
4
), quoting some explicit analytical results for the case of the Rayleigh distribution in §
4.1
; a number of other eccentricity distributions are explored in Appendices
C
&
D
. We explore the use of our results for retrieving the semi-major axis distribution (§
5
) or the eccentricity distribution (§
6
) of debris particles from observations. We discuss our findings in §
7
and summarise in §
8
. A number of additional technical but useful results for certain types of debris discs and rings can be found in Appendices.

2
Basic Setup

We consider a disc of particles orbiting a central mass
M_{\star}
. We restrict ourselves to the planar geometry and employ polar
(r,\phi)
coordinates. The orbits of objects comprising the disc are characterized by their semi-major axis
a
, eccentricity
e
and apsidal angle
\varpi
(relative to a fixed reference direction), and are given by

\displaystyle r=\frac{a(1-e^{2})}{1+e\cos(\phi-\varpi)}.

(2)

Keplerian motion of a particle with a non-zero eccentricity
e
allows it to contribute to surface density at radii different from its semi-major axis, namely at
r
satisfying

\displaystyle r_{p}\leq r\leq r_{a},

(3)

where
r_{p}=a(1-e)
and
r_{a}=a(1+e)
are the periastron and apoastron distances. Spatial overlap of a large number of disc particles moving along their orbits with different
a
,
e
and
\varpi
and random mean anomalies — a natural assumption adopted in this work (as well as many others) — determines the spatial distribution of the surface density
\Sigma(r,\phi)
, which is potentially observable.

Let
\mu(a)
be the semi-major axis distribution of mass in the disc, such that
\mathrm{d}m(a)=\mu(a)\mathrm{d}a
is the amount of mass in disc particles having their semi-major axes in the interval
(a,a+\mathrm{d}a)

(
Statler 2001
)
. We find it useful to define
\Sigma_{a}(a)
such that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{a}(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi a}\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}a}=\frac{\mu(a)}{2\pi a}.

(4)

Note that
\Sigma_{a}(a)
does not have a clear physical interpretation, but for a disc composed of objects on circular orbits one naturally has
a=r
and
\Sigma_{a}(a)=\Sigma(r)
, with no
\phi
-dependence.

Knowledge of
\Sigma_{a}(a)
or
\mu(a)
is important, since in some cases it might be linked to the initial distribution of mass in the disc. For example, if the disc evolves secularly under the action of massive external perturber(s), then the semi-major axes of its constituent particles would be conserved
(
Murray &
Dermott 1999
)
. As a result, both
\mu(a)
and
\Sigma_{a}
remain invariant in the course of secular evolution, and their knowledge at present time can inform us about the mass distribution in a debris disc at its formation.

We will first consider in §
3
a simple disc model in which
e
is a
unique
function of
a
, i.e. a disc in which all particles with a semi-major axis
a
have a single value of eccentricity
e_{a}(a)
. However, in a debris disc, particles having the same semi-major axis
a
may also have different values of eccentricity and orbit crossings are allowed (unlike fluid discs for which
e
is always a unique function of
a
). As a result, kinematic properties of a debris disc are best characterized by a
distribution
of eccentricities
\psi_{e}(e,a)
such that
\psi_{e}(e,a)\mathrm{d}e
is the fraction of particles having
e
in the interval
(e,e+\mathrm{d}e)
at a given
a
. We will explore this more general model in §
4
.

3
discs with
e=e(a)

Let us assume that eccentricity of the debris disc particles is a unique function of their semi-major axis,
e=e_{a}(a)
. This is equivalent to a disc having eccentricity distribution in the form

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a)=\delta(e-e_{a}(a)),

(5)

where
\delta
is the Dirac
\delta
-function.

We introduce an auxiliary function
\eta(r|a)
such that
\eta(r|a)\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}a
is the amount of mass per radial interval
(r,r+\mathrm{d}r)
contributed by objects with eccentricity
e
and semi-major axes in the interval
(a,a+\mathrm{d}a)
. It is clear that
\eta(r|a)
is non-zero only as long as the condition (
3
) is fulfilled, with
r_{p}=r_{p}(a)
and
r_{a}=r_{a}(a)
, where

\displaystyle r_{p}(a)=a(1-e_{a}(a)),~~~r_{a}(a)=a(1+e_{a}(a)).

(6)

Moreover, from the definition of
\eta(r|a)
and mass conservation it immediately follows that the following normalization condition must be obeyed, see equation (
4
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}a\int_{r_{p}(a)}^{r_{a}(a)}\eta(r|a)\mathrm{d}r=\mathrm{d}m(a)=2\pi a\Sigma_{a}(a)\mathrm{d}a.

(7)

It is clear on physical grounds that the amount of mass
\eta(r|a)\mathrm{d}r
that an orbiting particle contributes to the radial interval
(r,r+\mathrm{d}r)
, in the orbit-averaged sense, should be proportional to the amount of time
\mathrm{d}t
that it spends in this radial range, i.e.
\eta(r|a)\mathrm{d}r\propto\mathrm{d}t
. Thus,
\eta(r|a)\propto\mathrm{d}t/\mathrm{d}r\propto v_{r}^{-1}
, where
(
Murray &
Dermott 1999
)

\displaystyle v_{r}=\sqrt{\frac{GM_{\star}}{a}}\frac{\sqrt{(r-r_{p})(r_{a}-r)}}{r}

(8)

is the radial velocity of a particle with eccentricity
e
and semi-major axis
a
in a Keplerian potential. This implies that

\displaystyle\eta(r|a)=\frac{C(a)r}{\sqrt{(r-r_{p})(r_{a}-r)}},

(9)

where
C(a)
is a normalization constant, which still depends on
a
.

To fix
C(a)
we plug the expression (
9
) into the condition (
7
), finding as a result
C(a)=2\pi I^{-1}a\Sigma_{a}(a)
, where

\displaystyle I=\int_{r_{p}}^{r_{a}}\frac{r\mathrm{d}r}{\sqrt{(r-r_{p})(r_{a}-r)}}=\pi a

(10)

is independent of
e
, because of the definitions of
r_{p}
and
r_{a}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\eta(r|a)=\frac{2r\Sigma_{a}(a)}{\sqrt{(r-r_{p})(r_{a}-r)}}.

(11)

The behavior of
\eta(r|a)
as a function of
r
(for a fixed
a
) is illustrated in Figure
1
for several values of
e_{a}
.

Let us call
\eta(r)
the amount of mass in debris particles per unit physical radius
\mathrm{d}r
(and not
\mathrm{d}a
, distinguishing
\eta(r)
from
\mu(a)
). Since the mass at a given radius
r
is contributed by all particles with semi-major axes satisfying the condition (
3
),
\eta(r)
can be obtained by summing over all contributions
\eta(r|a)
satisfying this condition. In other words,

\displaystyle\eta(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta(r|a)\mathrm{d}a=2r\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma_{a}(a)\theta(r,a)\,\mathrm{d}a}{\sqrt{(r-r_{p}(a))(r_{a}(a)-r)}}.

(12)

with

\displaystyle\theta(r,a)=\Theta((r-r_{p}(a))(r_{a}(a)-r)),

(13)

where
\Theta(z)
is a Heaviside step-function;
\theta=1
for all
a
such that
r_{p}(a)\leq r\leq r_{a}(a)
, while
\theta=0
if this condition is violated. Introduction of the indicator function
\theta(r,a)
forces us to count in the integral (
12
) the contribution to the local surface density only from particles with orbits crossing the radius
r
. It also constrains the expression inside the radical to be non-negative.

Schematic illustration showing the intervals of semi-major axis

(grey shaded) which contribute to particle density at radius

. Blue curve shows
\kappa(r,a)
, see equation (

), while orange curve shows some
e_{a}(a)
profile. Only the particles with

can reach

.

On the other hand, by its definition,
\eta(r)
must coincide with
r\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Sigma(r,\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi=2\pi r\bar{\Sigma}(r)
, see equation (
1
). Expressing
\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\eta(r)/(2\pi r)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\pi^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma_{a}(a)\theta(r,a)}{\sqrt{(r-r_{p}(a))(r_{a}(a)-r)}}\mathrm{d}a,

(14)

which is the desired relation between
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
and
\Sigma_{a}
(a). One can easily show that
\bar{\Sigma}(r)\to\Sigma_{a}(r)
for
e_{a}(a)\to 0
, as expected.

3.1
Master equation

One can rewrite the result (
14
) in a different form, making more explicit the role of the constraint (
3
). This constraint can be written as a
lower limit
on the eccentricity
e_{a}(a)
of a particle with the semi-major axis
a
capable of reaching radius
r
:
e_{a}(a)>1-(r/a)
for
r<a
, and
e_{a}(a)>(r/a)-1
for
r>a
, see Figure
2
for an illustration. Alternatively, this can be written as

\displaystyle e_{a}(a)>\kappa(r,a),~~~\mbox{where}~~~\kappa(r,a)=\left|1-\frac{r}{a}\right|,

(15)

a constraint valid for both
r<a
and
r>a
. Since
e_{a}(a)<1
, equation (
15
) necessarily implies that
a>r/2
. Indeed, particles with
a<r/2
have their apoastra
r_{a}(a)<r
(i.e. do not reach
r
) even for
e_{a}(a)\to 1
. For that reason, we can set the lower limit of integration in equation (
14
) to
r/2
. On the contrary,
a
can be arbitrarily large and still allow particle periastron to be below any given
r
(for
e_{a}(a)\to 1
), contributing to
\bar{\Sigma}
at this radius.

After some manipulation, these considerations allow us to rewrite equation (
16
) as

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\pi^{-1}\int_{r/2}^{\infty}a^{-1}\Sigma_{a}(a)\Phi_{e}(r,a)\mathrm{d}a,

(16)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(r,a)=\frac{\theta(r,a)}{\sqrt{e_{a}^{2}(a)-\kappa^{2}(r,a)}}

(17)

is the dimensionless weighting function (a kernel), and

\displaystyle\theta(r,a)=\Theta\left(e_{a}(a)-\kappa(r,a)\right)

(18)

is equivalent to the definition (
13
). In Appendix
A
we provide an alternative derivation of the relations (
16
)-(
18
) based on the results of
Statler 2001
.

Equation (
16
) is the master equation relating
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
. Function
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
is the key ingredient of this relation. For a given eccentricity profile
e_{a}(a)
, the kernel
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
describes the coupling between the different semi-major axes and the radius
r
, i.e. it determines (through equation (
16
)) how the mass at different
a
contributes of
\bar{\Sigma}
at
r
. One can easily check that
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
satisfies the following integral conditions

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{e}(r,a)dr=\pi a,~~~~\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{e}(r,a)rdr=\pi a^{2},

(19)

with the second one effectively ensuring mass conservation in the disc when going from
\Sigma_{a}(a)
to
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
. Note that even though
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
formally diverges as
r\to a(1\pm e_{a}(a))
, these singularities are integrable, resulting in a finite
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
in equation (
16
).

Very importantly, in deriving the relations (
14
) and (
16
)-(
18
) we did not use any information about the behavior of apsidal angles of disc particles
\varpi
— they can follow any distribution (uniform or non-uniform) without affecting our results. This makes sense since the time that a particle with a given
a
and
e
spends within some radial interval is independent of the orientation of its orbital ellipse (which is set by
\varpi
). The insensitivity to apsidal orientation of individual objects comprising the disc allows us to obtain some rather general results, as we show in the rest of the paper.

4
disc with a distribution of eccentricity

Next we consider a more general situation, in which particles at a given semi-major axis
a
might have a
distribution
of eccentricities
\psi_{e}(e,a)
, with properties (e.g. mean, dispersion, etc.) that also depend on
a
. We will assume that
\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{e}(e,a)\mathrm{d}e=1
at every
a
.

Generalizing the results of §
3
, we introduce a function
\eta(r|a,e)
such that
\eta(r|a,e)\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}e
is the amount of mass per radial interval
(r,r+\mathrm{d}r)
contributed by objects with eccentricity in the interval
(e,e+\mathrm{d}e)
and semi-major axes in the interval
(a,a+\mathrm{d}a)
. Clearly,
\eta(r|a,e)
is non-zero only if the condition (
3
) is fulfilled, in which now
r_{p}=r_{p}(a,e)
,
r_{a}=r_{a}(a,e)
. The normalization condition (
7
) generalizes to

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}e\int_{r_{p}(a)}^{r_{a}(a)}\eta(r|a,e)\mathrm{d}r=\psi_{e}(e,a)\,\mathrm{d}m(a)\,\mathrm{d}e.

(25)

Repeating the development in §
3
, and extending equation (
12
) by adding integration over eccentricity, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\pi^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}a\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}e\frac{\Sigma_{a}(a)\psi_{e}(e,a)\theta(r,e,a)}{\sqrt{(r-a(1-e))(a(1+e)-r)}},

(26)

which is a natural generalization of the equation (
14
). The generalized indicator function

\displaystyle\theta(r,a,e)=\Theta\left([r-a(1-e)][a(1+e)-r]\right),

(27)

ensures that the constraint (
3
) is obeyed when integration over
e
and

is carried out. That constraint can also be written as
e>\kappa(r,a)
, analogous to the equation (
15
).

Finally, by manipulating equation (
26
) one can again put it in the form (
16
) but with a new weighting function
\Phi_{e}
given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(r,a)=\int_{\kappa}^{1}\frac{\psi_{e}(e,a)}{\sqrt{e^{2}-\kappa^{2}}}\mathrm{d}e

(28)

with
\kappa=\kappa(r,a)
. It is easy to see that the integral in (
28

\Phi_{e}
is finite.

Equation (
28
) represents a natural generalization of the equation (
17
) in §
3

). For
\psi_{e}(e,a)
in the form (

naturally reduces to the equation (

\Phi_{e}
in the form (

\Phi_{e}(r,a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(a-z,a)=\Phi_{e}(a+z,a)

The weighting function
\Phi_{e}

Equations (
16

\bar{\Sigma}
to the mass distribution in the disc (i.e.
\Sigma_{a}(a)

\psi_{e}(e,a)
). They allow efficient computation of
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
once
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and
\psi_{e}(e,a)
are specified.

4.1
Rayleigh distribution of eccentricity

We now illustrate our results for a disc with the Rayleigh distribution of eccentricity, which is expected to be established as a result of two-body relaxation (self-stirring) in the disc
(
Ida &
Makino 1992
;
Kennedy &
Wyatt 2010
)
. Namely, we will consider
\psi_{e}(e,a)
in the form

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a)=\frac{e}{\sigma^{2}(1-e^{-1/2\sigma^{2}})}e^{-e^{2}/2\sigma^{2}},

(30)

where
\sigma
is the dispersion of eccentricity, which can be a function of
a
in general. Note that the factor multiplying the exponential in (
30
) accounts for the fact that
e<1
and the normalization
\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{e}(e,a)\mathrm{d}e=1
.

Plugging (
30
) in the equation (
28
) and integrating we find

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(r,a)=\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{1/2}\frac{e^{-\kappa^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{\sigma(1-e^{-1/2\sigma^{2}})}{\rm erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right),

(31)

where
\sigma=\sigma(a)
,
\kappa=\kappa(r,a)
as per equation (
15
), and
{\rm erf}(z)=2\pi^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}\mathrm{d}t
is the Gauss error function. Relation (
16
) between
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
can then be written as

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{r/2}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma_{a}(a)e^{-\kappa^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{a\sigma(1-e^{-1/2\sigma^{2}})}{\rm erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)\mathrm{d}a,

(32)

which is a convolution of
\Sigma_{a}(a)
with a known kernel. These results are valid for arbitrary eccentricity dispersion, including
\sigma\sim 1
.

4.3
Small eccentricity limit,
\sigma(a)\ll 1
, for the Rayleigh distribution

Of large practical significance is the situation when the eccentricities of debris disc particles are small,
e\ll 1
, meaning that
\sigma(a)\ll 1
. In this limit
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
is non-negligible only for

because of the exponential term in (
31
). Physically this means that particles with semi-major axes
|r-a|\lesssim\sigma r
(i.e. a typical radial excursion of disc particles for
\psi_{e}
in the form (
30

r
. For
\kappa\lesssim\sigma\ll 1

{\rm erf}
is of order
\sigma^{-1}\gg 1
, so that
{\rm erf}\to 1
. Also,
e^{-1/2\sigma^{2}}\to 0
. As a result, for
\sigma\ll 1
we can reasonably approximate

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(r,a)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{1/2}\sigma^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

(34)

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{r/2}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma_{a}(a)}{a\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}a.

(35)

In Figure
5
we illustrate how well the approximations (
34
), (
35
) work compared to the exact equations (
31
), (
32
) for different values of (spatially constant)
\sigma
using a power law disc model (see §
3.3
)
{\cal M}_{\Sigma}=(0.5,1,1,5)
. One can see that even for
\sigma
as high as

\bar{\Sigma}(r)
is below 3

for

; the two methods are essentially indistinguishable for
\sigma=0.2
. Only for
\sigma=0.6
one finds deviations at the level of

. Thus, the approximations (
34
), (
35
) perform very well, even for quite substantial values of
\sigma
.

5
Reconstruction of mass distribution from observations

Observations of debris discs allow us to directly obtain their ASD
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
. A natural question to ask then is whether this information can be used to infer the distribution of particle semi-major axes
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and/or the eccentricity distribution
\psi_{e}(e,a)
. In other words, one would like to use observations to obtain the underlying disc properties by somehow inverting the equation (
16
).

It is clear that the knowledge of
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
cannot help us uniquely determine both
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and
\psi_{e}(e,a)
at the same time: multiple pairs of
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and
\Phi_{e}(e,a)
can result in the same
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
via the equation (
16
). Thus, some additional information about one of these functions has to be provided based on some additional considerations. In this section we will assume that we somehow possess independent knowledge of
\psi_{e}(r,a)
.

For example, as already alluded to in §
4.1
, particle discs dynamically excited by self-stirring are expected to follow Rayleigh distribution of eccentricities
\psi_{e}

(
Ida &
Makino 1992
)
. Moreover, the dispersion of particle inclinations in such discs is typically found to be about a half of the local eccentricity dispersion
(
Ida &
Makino 1992
;
Ida
et al. 1993
)
. If a self-stirred debris disc is observed edge-on (relative to our line of sight), the measurement of the radial variation of its vertical thickness would provide us with the radial profile of the particle inclinations
i(a)
, or the dispersion of inclinations, see
Han
et al. 2022
. Consequently, the eccentricity dispersion as a function of
a
can be obtained as
\sigma(a)\approx 2i(a)
, giving us the full information about
\psi_{e}(e,a)
.

Another situation in which we could have a priori knowledge of particle dynamics arises when particle eccentricities are known to be secularly excited by a massive planet
1
1

1

Close to being co-planar with the disc to not violate our assumption of a two-dimensional disc.
. In this case an assumption of a unique
e=e_{a}(a)
(as in §
3
) would be natural. Moreover, if the age of the system, as well as the planetary mass and orbital properties (i.e. its semi-major axis and eccentricity) are known, then one could use the classical Laplace-Lagrange secular theory
(
Murray &
Dermott 1999
)
to predict the profile of
e_{a}(a)
in the disc.

In both situations we would know the behavior of
\psi_{e}(e,a)
, allowing us to compute
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
via equations (
17
) or (
28
), as appropriate. Then, by making a change of variable
r\to 2x
we can formally convert the relation (
16
) to a linear Volterra integral equation of the first kind

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(2x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}K(x,a)\Sigma_{a}(a)\mathrm{d}a,

(36)

with the kernel

\displaystyle K(x,a)=\frac{\Phi_{e}(2x,a)}{\pi a}.

(37)

This equation can be discretized on a grid, converting it to a linear (upper triangular) matrix equation, which can be easily solved for
\Sigma_{a}(a)
using standard inversion methods (e.g. forward substitution,
Press et al. 2002
), since
\bar{\Sigma}(2x)
is available from observations and
K(x,a)
is known (as assumed).

Thus, knowledge of the dynamical characteristics of a debris disc allows one to use its observed
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
to infer the semi-major axis distribution
\Sigma_{a}(a)
of particles in the disc in a straightforward manner.

6
Reconstruction of eccentricity distribution from observations

We will now look into the question of whether the knowledge of ASD
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
can be used to retrieve the distribution of particle eccentricities
\psi_{e}(e,a)
.

We start by noting that equation (
28
) can be viewed as a version of the Abel integral equation for the unknown function
\psi_{e}(e,a)

(
Binney &
Tremaine 2008
)
, with
a
being a parameter. Also, we can consider
\Phi_{e}
to be a function of
\kappa
and
a
(instead of
r
and
a
) as
\Phi_{e}(a(1\pm\kappa),a)
, see equation (
15
). The sign ambiguity of
\kappa
is not important because of the property (
29
) which
\Phi_{e}
must obey in the first place. Then equation (
28
) can be easily inverted as
2
2

2

This solution can be checked by substituting the definition (
28
) for
\Phi_{e}
, differentiating with respect to
\kappa
, and changing the order of integration in the resultant double integral. To show that
\Phi_{e}
in the form (
17
) leads to
\psi_{e}
in the form (
5
), one should first re-write it as
\Phi_{e}=\int_{\kappa}^{1}\delta(e-e_{a}(a))(e^{2}-\kappa^{2})^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}e
and then carry out the above procedure.

(
Binney &
Tremaine 2008
)

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a)=-\frac{2e}{\pi}\int_{e}^{1}\frac{d\kappa}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-e^{2}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\Phi_{e}(a(1\pm\kappa),a).

(38)

Thus, if the function
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
defined by the equation (
28
) is somehow known to us as a function of
r
and
a
, then the behavior of
\psi_{e}(e,a)
can be determined uniquely through the equation (
38
). But the really tricky part of this exercise is obtaining the knowledge of
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
behavior in the first place.

Indeed, let us assume that we know exactly the behavior of both
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
and
\Sigma_{a}(a)
in the whole disc. Even then we would still not be able to infer
\Phi_{e}(r,a)
from the equation (
16
): mathematically, this is an ill-posed problem equivalent to inferring a kernel of an integral equation and has no unique solution
3
3

3

Discretization of the equation (
16
) on
N\times N
grid in
(r,a)
space yields
N
equations for
N^{2}
unknown components of the discretized kernel
\Phi_{e}
.
. This is unlike the situation considered in §
5
, where we have shown that the knowledge of the kinematic properties of the disc (i.e.
\psi_{e}(e,a)
) allows us to infer
\Sigma_{a}(a)
.

Progress can still be made if the functional form of the eccentricity distribution is known, and we are just looking to determine the dependence of one of its characteristics as a function of
a
. For example, if for whatever reason we expect particle eccentricity to be a unique function of
a
, as in §
3
(i.e.
\psi_{e}
to have the form (
5
)), then equations (
16
), (
17
) can be combined to provide an integral equation for
e_{a}(a)
only (once
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
and
\Sigma_{a}(a)
are specified). As a complicating factor,
e_{a}(a)
enters the equation (
17
) in a
nonlinear
fashion, unlike
\Sigma_{a}
in equation (
36
). However, the original integral equation (
16
), (
17
) can still be reduced to a (nonlinear) Volterra equation of the first kind by the same method as used in §
5
for equation (
36
). The numerical solution of such an equation, even if nonlinear, is still reasonably straightforward, see
Press et al. 2002
.

A similar situation arises for self-stirred discs, in which we expect
\psi_{e}(e,a)
to be given by the Rayleigh distribution (
30
). In this case we would like to determine the run of
\sigma(a)
. As we have shown in §
4.1
, for such discs equation (
32
) provides an integral relation between the known
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
,
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and unknown
\sigma(a)
. Again,
\sigma(a)
enters this equation in a highly non-linear fashion, but the same method based on a reduction of (
32
) to a nonlinear Volterra equation would still apply for the determination of
\sigma(a)
.

Also, in some special cases one can determine
\psi_{e}(e,a)
in a non-parametric form (i.e. not assuming a particular functional dependence on
e
). This possibility arises for certain special forms of
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and is discussed next.

6.1
Eccentricity distribution from observations of narrow rings

Let us assume that it is known a priori that the debris particles are clustered in semi-major axis around

Our derivation of the master equation (
16
) in §
3
relies on the assumption that debris particles move on eccentric orbits in a Newtonian potential (with randomized mean anomalies). One may wonder whether some additional physical effects may affect this assumption. Collisions between particles are certainly important for the long-term evolution of debris discs. However, as long as the characteristic time between collisions is longer than the particle orbital period, our assumption of eccentric orbits in the form (
2
) would remain valid.

Radiative effects are also important for debris disc evolution
(
Burns
et al. 1979
)
. Poynting-Robertson drag is a slow process which causes only minuscule departures of particle orbits from Keplerian ellipses (especially for bigger grains) and can be neglected. Radiation pressure is unimportant for large grains such as the ones responsible for thermal emission visible in sub-mm (e.g. by
ALMA
), but it can play an important role in the dynamics of
\mu
m and sub-
\mu
m grains that dominate the scattered light observations. However, even for these small grains the only effect of radiation pressure is the effective reduction of the central object’s mass
(
Burns
et al. 1979
)
, while the orbits still remain closed ellipses with all other features of Keplerian dynamics fully preserved. Since all our results, including the master equation (
16
) are completely independent of the central mass
M_{\star}
, they should remain valid even for small dust particles strongly affected by radiation pressure.

Gas has been detected in a number of debris discs
(
Hughes et al. 2018
)
and may affect the motion of debris particles. However, as long as the gas density is low enough and/or the emitting particle size is large enough for gas drag to not cause significant departures of their orbits from purely Keplerian, presence of gas should not affect our conclusions, similar to the Poynting-Robertson drag.

7.1
Application to debris disc observations

Application of the inversion techniques described in §§
5
,
6
to observations of debris discs can shed light on their architecture at formation, as well as on the dynamical processes operating in them. In particular, knowledge of
\Sigma_{a}(a)
for discs undergoing long-term secular evolution translates into the knowledge of initial mass distribution in the disc, as particle semi-major axes do not change in the course of secular evolution. Thus, if the disc started off dynamically cold, with
e\to 0
everywhere, and then attained its current (non-zero) eccentricity distribution as a result of secular evolution (e.g. due to a planetary or stellar perturber), then the initial
\Sigma(r,\phi)|_{t=0}=\bar{\Sigma}(r)|_{t=0}
of the disc and its current
\Sigma_{a}(a)
must be related as
\Sigma(r,\phi)|_{t=0}=\Sigma_{a}(r)
.

Constraints on the shape of
\psi_{e}(e,a)
, even if obtained only locally, like in §
6
, can shed light on the processes that govern eccentricities of the debris disc particles. For example, a truly smooth and extended (in
e
)
\psi_{e}
close in form to the Rayleigh distribution (§
4.1
) would support the idea that particle eccentricities are excited by disc self-stirring
(
Kennedy &
Wyatt 2010
)
.

On the other hand, if
\psi_{e}(e,a)
is found to be clustered around a particular value of
e
, i.e. being close to
e=e_{a}(a)
anzatz (§
3
), this might indicate the prevalence of secular processes in determining eccentricity dynamics. In the case of a spatially extended disc this would also suggest that the disc is "secularly young", i.e. its age is not very large compared to the local apsidal precession period, as otherwise
e_{a}(a)
would rapidly oscillate in
a
(as we alluded to in §
6.2.3
), potentially masquerading as an extended and continuous (in
e
)
\psi_{e}
.

It should be noted that some of the inversion recipes proposed in §§
5
,
6
may have somewhat limited practical utility when applied to real observational data, simply because the latter is intrinsically noisy. For example, re-construction of
\Phi_{e}(r,a_{0})
near a sharp edge of a disc at
a=a_{0}
via the equation (
47
) involves a differentiation of (noisy)
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
derived from observations, which is going to amplify the noise. Subsequent retrieval of
\psi_{e}(a_{r},e)
from this
\Phi_{e}(r,a_{0})
using equation (
38
) involves yet another differentiation, exacerbating the noise issue even further. Thus, one should be cautious when directly applying equations (
36
)-(
38
), (
47
) to real data, which may need to be smoothed prior to using this technique.

In many cases a forward modeling approach, i.e. varying the parameters of a model for
\Sigma_{a}(a)
or
\psi_{e}(a,e)
until
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
predicted by the equation (
16
) best matches the data, would be more appropriate. Our analytical framework provides a very efficient tool for such forward modeling, see the earlier discussion of Figure
9
.

To obtain a reliable
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
from observations one needs to have an accurate mapping of the surface density in
(r,\phi)
coordinates in the plane of the disc in the first place. This requires de-projecting the observed brightness distribution in the plane of the sky, accounting for the overall disc inclination, and converting brightness distribution to
\Sigma(r,\phi)
. The latter should be relatively straightforward for systems imaged in sub-mm with e.g.
ALMA
, when one expects particle brightness to decay as
r^{-1/2}
in the optically thin, Rayleigh-Jeans regime. Things are more complicated for systems imaged in the optical and near-IR through scattered light
6
6

6

It should be remembered that thermal sub-mm emission and scattered light observations are probing very different dust populations, which may have rather different kinematic properties.
, as in this case one also need to account for the scattering phase function.

Disc warping might complicate de-projection, especially for discs which are close to edge-on. If a disc has a substantial spread of particle inclinations around its midplane, this would be another complication (especially for close to edge-on discs, see
Han
et al. 2022
), although less severe for almost face-on systems for which inclination enters the determination of
\Sigma(r,\phi)
only as a second order effect compared to eccentricity
(
Marino 2021
)
. And having gone through these procedures, one would obtain
\Sigma(r,\phi)
for the luminous dust particles only — the actual mass surface density distributed in objects of all sizes may be quite different from this
\Sigma(r,\phi)
.

Despite these complications, ASD profiles have been derived and analyzed for a number of debris discs (e.g.
Marino 2021
), enabling application of our technique to real observations.

7.2
Application to other astrophysical discs/rings

Throughout this work we focused on modeling debris discs. However, our framework can be applied to other types of discs/rings as well. For example, particles in planetary rings with low optical depth may experience collisions rarely enough for the assumptions of their Keplerian motion and randomized mean longitudes to be valid. In such tenuous rings our results would apply for a properly measured ASD.

Large dust grains in the outer parts of protoplanetary discs are weakly affected by gas drag (if their Stokes number is much larger than unity) and move on essentially Keplerian orbits. Thus, our methods would apply for such discs, even though particle motion in them is unlikely to strongly deviate from the gas motion.

Another example of relevant nearly-Keplerian systems is provided by the nuclear stellar discs orbiting supermassive black holes in centers of galaxies, with stars replacing debris particles. In particular, the eccentric stellar disc in the center of M31
(
Tremaine 1995
)
has been a subject of numerous observations
(
Lauer
et al. 1993
;
Lauer et al. 1998
)
and extensive modeling
(
Salow &
Statler 2001
;
Salow &
Statler 2004
;
Peiris &
Tremaine 2003
;
Brown &
Magorrian 2013
)
. Our framework can be directly applied to modeling this system. This disc has a particular benefit of also having a resolved kinematic information available to us
(
Bacon et al. 2001
;
Bender
et al. 2005
)
, providing an additional constraint on the semi-major axis and eccentricity distributions
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and
\psi_{e}(a,e)
.

Finally, we note that the residence time argument can be applied even to non-Keplerian discs, as long as their potential can be approximated as axisymmetric in the disc plane. For example, it can be employed to provide a relation similar to the equation (
16
) between the stellar energy and angular momentum distributions in disc galaxies and their (azimuthally-averaged) surface density. It should be remembered, however, that the form of the underlying potential is not known a priori in this case (but kinematic data might help in constraining it).

8
Summary

In this work we developed a (semi-)analytical formalism allowing one to relate the (arbitrary) semi-major axis and eccentricity distributions of particles in debris discs to their azimuthally-averaged surface density, which can be inferred from the brightness distribution of the disc. Our method can be used for fast and efficient forward-modeling of debris disc observations, allowing one to compute multiple surface density profiles for a given eccentricity distribution. Alternatively, in certain situations our framework can be used for retrieving orbital parameter distributions of the disc particles directly from observations (§§
5
,
6
). Explicit formulae have been derived for a number of eccentricity distributions
\psi_{e}
, including a prescribed eccentricity profile
e_{a}(a)
and the Rayleigh distribution. This framework does not require the knowledge of the distribution of apsidal angles of disc particles, simplifying the inference of orbital parameters. It can also be applied to understanding the architecture and kinematic properties of other disc-like systems, e.g. nuclear stellar discs in centers of galaxies harboring supermassive black holes. Future work will aim to use the full
\Sigma(r,\phi)
information to further explore the underlying disc kinematics, beyond the level presented in this study.

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\frac{\Sigma_{a}(r)}{\pi}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{x^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{e_{a}^{2}(r)x^{-2\beta}-(1-x^{-1})^{2}}},

(54)

where
x_{1,2}
are the two solutions of the equation

\displaystyle x^{\beta-1}|x-1|=e_{a}(r),

(55)

provided that they exist.

There are several cases in which we can obtain a more explicit relation between
\Sigma_{a}(a)
and
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
for power law discs. This relation has a general form

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\Sigma_{a}(r)\varphi(r)F(r,\alpha),

(56)

with

\displaystyle F(r,\zeta)=\pi^{-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[1+\lambda(r)\cos t\right]^{-\zeta}dt,

(57)

where
\varphi(r)
and
\lambda(r)
are certain functions determined by the value of
\beta
.

In particular, for
\beta=0

\displaystyle\varphi(r)=\left(1-e_{a}^{2}\right)^{\alpha-1/2},~~~\lambda(r)=e_{a};

(58)

are independent of
r
since
e_{a}
is a constant; this implies that
F(r,\alpha)
is a constant as well. As a result,
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)
is independent of
r
and is a function of
e_{a}
and
\alpha
only. In Figure
10
we show this ratio
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)=\varphi(r)F(r,\alpha)
for
\beta=0
and several values of
e_{a}
, as a function of
\alpha
. For small enough
e_{a}\lesssim 0.1
this ratio does not deviate from unity by more than several per cent.

For
\beta=1/2
one finds

\displaystyle\varphi(r)=\left[1+\frac{e_{a}^{2}(r)}{2}\right]^{-\alpha},~~~\lambda(r)=e_{a}(r)\frac{\sqrt{1+e_{a}^{2}(r)/4}}{1+e_{a}^{2}(r)/2}.

(59)

And for
\beta=1
it is easy to show that
\varphi(r)=1
,
\lambda(r)=e_{a}(r)
. It may be possible to obtain analytical results for
\bar{\Sigma}/\Sigma_{a}
for other values of
\beta
, but we will not pursue this here.

We illustrate these results by showing several examples of
\Sigma_{a}
,
\bar{\Sigma}
and their ratios in four power law discs in Figure
11
. One can see that in general the ratio
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)
in the power law region of the disc (unshaded) is a function of
r
, and can be both larger (e.g. panels (e)-(h)) and smaller (panels (c)-(d)) than unity. Also, for some combinations of
\alpha
,
\beta
the ratio
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)
is constant. This is certainly true for discs with
\beta=0
, i.e. having constant eccentricity, for arbitrary
\alpha
, see Equation (
58
). Figure
11
e,f shows an example of such a disc with
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)\approx 1.018
, in agreement with Figure
10
. Also, using the fact that
F(r,0)=1
,
F(r,1)=\left[1-\lambda^{2}(r)\right]^{-1/2}
,

F(r,2)=\left[1-\lambda^{2}(r)\right]^{-3/2}

(
Gradshteyn &
Ryzhik 2014
)
, one can easily show that
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)=1
for
(\alpha,\beta)=(0,1)
,
(1,1/2)
(model shown in Figure (
11
)a,b),
(1,0)
,
(2,0)
.

Note that the model of a disc with the sharp outer edge and constant
e
everywhere shown in Figure
7
is a power law model with
{\cal M}_{\Sigma}=(0,1,0,1)
and
{\cal M}_{e}=(0,e_{a},1)
. According to equations (
56
)-(
58
), in such a disc
F=1
and
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)=(1-e_{a}^{2})^{-1/2}>1
is a constant. In particular,
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)\approx 1.005
for
e_{a}=0.1
and
\bar{\Sigma}(r)/\Sigma_{a}(r)\approx 1.00125
for
e_{a}=0.05
. This is in perfect agreement with the vertical offsets from unity of the difference curves for
r<r_{p}=1-e_{a}
in Figure
7
b.

Appendix C
Reconstruction of
\psi_{e}
for debris rings

Figure 12:
Illustration of the
\psi_{e}
-reconstruction using
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
profiles of narrow rings. Four columns correspond to the different ASD models: (a)-(c) top-hat, §
C.1
, (d)-(f) triangular, §
C.2
, (g)-(i) power law, §
C.3
, (j)-(l) truncated Gaussian, §
C.4
. Top, middle and bottom rows show the corresponding
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
,
\Phi_{e}(\kappa)
, and
\psi_{e}(e)
, correspondingly, for several values of the relevant parameters indicated in the middle row panels. See text for details.

Here we illustrate the process of reconstruction of the explicit form of
\psi_{e}(e,a)=\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})
using equation (
38
) for several profiles of
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
that might be featured by narrow debris rings. These profiles must necessarily obey the constraint (
42
), i.e. be symmetric with respect to the ring semi-major axis
a_{r}
. We will assume the rings to be confined within the radial range of (half-)width
\lambda a_{r}
, such that

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=0~~~~\mbox{for}~~~~|r-a_{r}|>\lambda a_{r}.

(60)

Also,
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
should be normalized such that the total ring mass is
m_{r}
.

Given such
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
we will express
\Phi_{e}(\kappa)
through the equation (
41
), keeping in mind that
\kappa=|r-a_{r}|/a_{r}
in the ring case. After that we will use equation (
38
) to obtain
\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})
. Examples of application of this technique follow, providing us with a set of useful
\psi_{e}-\Phi_{e}
pairs.

C.1
Ring with a top-hat
\bar{\Sigma}(r)

We start with a top-hat ring of mass
m_{r}
, for which

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\frac{m_{r}}{4\pi a_{r}^{2}\lambda},~~~~~~|r-a_{r}|<\lambda a_{r},

(61)

(see Figure
12
a). Equation (
41
) gives for this ASD

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(\kappa)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\pi/2)\lambda^{-1},&\kappa\leq\lambda,\\
0,&\kappa>\lambda.\end{array}\right.

(see Figure
12
b). Since
\Phi_{e}(\kappa)
is discontinuous at
\kappa=\lambda
, we explicitly take this into account by writing
\partial\Phi_{e}/\partial\kappa=-(\pi/2\lambda)\delta(\kappa-\lambda)
. Plugging this into the equation (
38
) we deduce

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})=\lambda^{-1}\frac{e}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-e^{2}}},~~~e<\lambda,

(65)

shown in Figure
12
c. Note the divergence of
\psi_{e}
near
e=\lambda
.

One can easily verify this form of
\psi_{e}
by substituting (
65
) into the definition (
28
) and recovering
\Phi_{e}
given by the equation (
C.1
). Also, it is easy to check that the distribution function (
65
) is normalized to unity, as it should.

C.2
Ring with a triangular
\bar{\Sigma}(r)

A ring with a triangular ASD has (see Figure
12
d)

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\frac{m_{r}}{2\pi a_{r}^{3}\lambda^{2}}\left(\lambda a_{r}-|r-a_{r}|\right),~~~~~~|r-a_{r}|<\lambda a_{r},

(66)

for which (see Figure
12
e)

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(\kappa)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\pi\lambda^{-2}(\lambda-\kappa),&\kappa\leq\lambda,\\
0,&\kappa>\lambda.\end{array}\right.

This
\Phi_{e}(\kappa)
is continuous everywhere so the Dirac
\delta
-function no longer appears in
\partial\Phi_{e}/\partial\kappa
. Equation (
38
) gives in this case

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})=\frac{2e}{\lambda^{2}}\ln\frac{\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-e^{2}}}{e},~~~e<\lambda,

(70)

see Figure
12
f.

C.3
Ring with a power law
\bar{\Sigma}(r)

Next we look at an ASD in the "power law" form

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{r}}{2\pi^{3/2}a_{r}^{2(1+\zeta)}\lambda^{2\zeta+1}}\frac{\Gamma(\zeta+3/2)}{\Gamma(\zeta+1)}

\displaystyle\times\left[(\lambda a_{r})^{2}-(r-a_{r})^{2}\right]^{\zeta},~~~~~~|r-a_{r}|<\lambda a_{r},

(71)

with
\zeta>0
being a free parameter; normalization of this expression again ensures that the ring mass is
m_{r}
. Variation of
\zeta
allows us to explore a whole class of ASD profiles, continuous at
r=a_{r}(1\pm\lambda)
, but with rather different characteristics, see Figure
12
g. For example, for
\zeta>1
not only
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
vanishes at
r=a_{r}(1\pm\lambda)
, but also its derivative, while for
\zeta<1
the derivative diverges at the ring edges.

This ASD has a corresponding

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(\kappa)=\pi^{1/2}\lambda^{-(2\zeta+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\zeta+3/2)}{\Gamma(\zeta+1)}\left(\lambda^{2}-\kappa^{2}\right)^{\zeta},

(72)

(see Figure
12
h) for
\kappa\leq\lambda
(and zero otherwise), which gives, using equation (
38
),

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})=(2\zeta+1)\lambda^{-(2\zeta+1)}e\left(\lambda^{2}-e^{2}\right)^{\zeta-1/2},~~~e<\lambda.

(73)

In particular, as
\zeta\to 0
, ASD (
71
) evolves into a top-hat profile and equations (
71
)-(
73
) reduce to the equations (
61
)-(
65
), as expected. For
\zeta=1/2
(a "semi-circular" ASD profile) one finds

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})=2\lambda^{-2}e,~~~e<\lambda,

(74)

while for
\zeta=2
one obtains

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})=5\lambda^{-5}e\left(\lambda^{2}-e^{2}\right)^{3/2},~~~e<\lambda,

(75)

see Figure
12
i.

C.4
Ring with a truncated Gaussian
\bar{\Sigma}(r)

We also consider a debris ring with the ASD profile in the form of a truncated Gaussian (see Figure
12
j) with a (relative, dimensionless) dispersion
\sigma_{\kappa}
:

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\frac{m_{r}C}{2\pi^{2}a_{r}^{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r-a_{r}}{\sigma_{\kappa}a_{r}}\right)^{2}\right],

(76)

for
|r-a_{r}|<\lambda a_{r}
, where

\displaystyle C=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sigma_{\kappa}^{-1}\left[\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2^{1/2}\sigma_{\kappa}}\right)\right]^{-1}

(77)

guarantees that the ring mass is
m_{r}
. The corresponding
\Phi_{e}
is

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(\kappa)=C\exp\left(-\kappa^{2}/2\sigma_{\kappa}^{2}\right),~~~~\kappa\leq\lambda,

(78)

and zero otherwise, thus, discontinuous at
\kappa=\lambda
, see Figure
12
k. Similar to the case considered in §
C.1
, this means that
\partial\Phi_{e}/\partial\kappa
has a contribution proportional to
\delta(\kappa-\lambda)
. With this in mind, equation (
38
) yields

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e,a_{r})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}Ce\Bigg[

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-e^{2}/2\sigma_{\kappa}^{2}}}{\sigma_{\kappa}}\mathrm{erf}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{2}-e^{2}}{2\sigma_{\kappa}^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{e^{-\lambda^{2}/2\sigma_{\kappa}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-e^{2}}}\Bigg],

(79)

which is shown in Figure
12
l. In the low-
e
(
\sigma_{\kappa}\ll 1
), untruncated (
\sigma_{\kappa}\ll\lambda
) limit, equations (
79
), (
78
) agree with the equations (
30
), (
34
).

Note the divergent tail of
\psi_{e}
as
e\to\lambda
, caused by the sharp truncation of the ring at
r=a_{r}(1\pm\lambda)
, similar to that in Figure
12
c. As indicated by the blue curves corresponding to
\zeta=0.1
in panels (g)-(i), such divergence of
\psi_{e}
is not endemic to discontinuously truncated
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
profiles. It also arises in rings with continuous
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
, as long as they decay to zero sufficiently rapidly (i.e. for
\zeta<1/2
for ASD (
71
)).

It is straightforward to check that all
\psi_{e}
derived in this Appendix are normalized to unity and that plugging them back in equation (
28
) recovers the corresponding
\Phi_{e}
. Given the ring setup, with only a single value of
a=a_{r}
possible, neither
\psi_{e}
nor
\Phi_{e}
depend on
a
. But the relations between
\Phi_{e}
and
\psi_{e}
that we found here are completely general and independent of the semi-major axis distribution
\Sigma_{a}(a)
of the debris particles; the ring setup was used just for an illustration (see also §
D
). For a general
\Sigma_{a}(a)
one would find
\Phi_{e}
depending on
a
at least through the
\kappa(r,a)
dependence.

Moreover, for general
\Sigma_{a}(a)
one can easily introduce some additional
a
-dependence in
\Phi_{e}
. For example, in all cases considered here
\lambda
can be made a function of
a
. In equations (
71
) and (
76
) one can also make
\zeta
and
\sigma_{\kappa}
to depend on
a
. Introducing such dependencies would not change the
mutual
relations between
\psi_{e}
and
\Phi_{e}
found here, as equations (
28
) and (
38
) leave them unaffected.

Appendix D
Reconstruction of
\psi_{e}
near a sharp edge

We now show an example of
\psi_{e}
reconstruction from
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
near a sharp edge of
\Sigma_{a}(a)
distribution, see §
6.2.3
. For that procedure to work, ASD should, in the first place, obey the constraint (
48
), i.e.
\mathrm{d}\bar{\Sigma}/\mathrm{d}\ln r
should be symmetric with respect to the disc edge at
a_{0}
. For illustration, let us assume that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bar{\Sigma}/\mathrm{d}\ln r=K\left[(\lambda a_{0})^{2}-(r-a_{0})^{2}\right],~~~|r-a_{0}|\leq\lambda a_{0},

(80)

and zero for
|r-a_{0}|>\lambda a_{0}
, which satisfies this constraint;
K<0
is a constant which will turn out being not a free parameter, see below. This assumption implies that
\bar{\Sigma}
is constant for
|r-a_{0}|>\lambda a_{0}
. We set
\bar{\Sigma}(r)=0
for
r>a_{0}(1+\lambda)
(i.e. an assumption of an outer edge) and denote
\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\Sigma_{m}
for
r<a_{0}(1-\lambda)
, i.e. in the bulk of the disc, unaffected by the edge effects. In general,
\Sigma_{m}
is different from
\Sigma_{0}
— the height of plateau of
\Sigma_{a}
(equation (
43
)) — see Appendix
B
and equation (
85
).

Solving equation (
80
) with the boundary condition
\bar{\Sigma}(a_{0}(1+\lambda))=0
we find

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=Ka_{0}^{2}\Bigg[

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}-1)\ln\frac{a_{0}(1+\lambda)}{r}-2\left(\frac{r}{a_{0}}-1-\lambda\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{(r/a_{0})^{2}-(1+\lambda)^{2}}{2}\Bigg].

(81)

Thus, starting with this profile we need to determine the corresponding
\psi_{e}
following the recipe outlined in §
6.2.3
and then using equation (
38
).

We start by noting that equation (
80
) also defines
\Phi_{e}
through the equation (
47
):

\displaystyle\Phi_{e}(\kappa)=-\frac{\pi Ka_{0}^{2}}{\Sigma_{0}}\left(\lambda^{2}-\kappa^{2}\right).

(82)

This
\Phi_{e}
is of the form (
72
) with
\zeta=1
(green curve in Figure
12
h). Using the results of (
C.3
), two things follow immediately. First, setting
\zeta=1
in equation (
73
) we conclude that
\psi_{e}
is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{e}(e)=3\lambda^{-3}e\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-e^{2}},~~~e<\lambda,

(83)

see the green curve in Figure
12
i. Second, from the fact that
\psi_{e}
in the form (
83
) is normalized to unity, it immediately follows that
K
must be such that the equation (
82
) reduces to the equation (
72
) with
\zeta=1
, i.e.
K=-(3/4)\lambda^{-3}\Sigma_{0}/a_{0}^{2}
. This implies that our starting ASD profile (
81
) must look like

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(r)=\frac{3\Sigma_{0}}{4\lambda^{3}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}-1)\ln\frac{1+\lambda}{x}-2\left(x-1-\lambda\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{x^{2}-(1+\lambda)^{2}}{2}\Bigg],~~~~~~~x=\frac{r}{a_{0}}.

(84)

This immediately gives us
\Sigma_{m}
as
\bar{\Sigma}(a_{0}(1-\lambda))
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}=\frac{3\Sigma_{0}}{4\lambda^{3}}\left[2\lambda-(1-\lambda^{2})\ln\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right],

(85)

which indeed differs from
\Sigma_{0}
. Figure
13
illustrates this profile for two values of
\lambda=0.2,0.3
, showing in particular that
\bar{\Sigma}(r)
smoothly connects at
r=a_{0}(1\pm\lambda)
to the flat segments outside the transition region.
