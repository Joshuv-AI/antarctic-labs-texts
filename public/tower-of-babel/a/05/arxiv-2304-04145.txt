Abstract
. Our work proves rigidity theorems for initial data sets associated with compact smooth spin manifolds with boundary and with compact convex polytopes, subject to the dominant energy condition. For manifolds with smooth boundary, this is based on the solution of a boundary value problem for Dirac operators. For convex polytopes we use approximations by manifolds with smooth boundary.

Introduction

An
initial data set
is a triple
(M,g,q)
, where
M
is a manifold,
g
is a Riemannian metric on
M
, and
q
is a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor on
M
.
Denote the scalar curvature of
g
by
R
.
We define

\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}\,R+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}(q)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,|q|^{2}

and

J=\text{\rm div}(q)-\nabla\mathrm{tr}(q).

We say that
(M,g,q)
satisfies the
dominant energy condition
if
\mu\geq|J|
at each point in
M
.

Example 1.1
.

Let
M
be a spacelike hypersurface in the Minkowski spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
.
Let
g
and
q
denote the induced metric and second fundamental form on
M
, respectively.
Then the initial data set
(M,g,q)
satisfies
\mu=0
and
J=0
by the Gauss and Codazzi-Mainardi equations, respectively.
In particular,
(M,g,q)
satisfies the dominant energy condition.

In this paper we will formulate conditions which insure that a given initial data set is of the form described in this example.
The study of rigidity for initial data sets under the dominant energy condition can be traced back to Schoen and Yau’s work
[
24
]
, in which they proved the spacetime positive energy theorem using minimal surface techniques in dimension 3.
Eichmair, Huang, Lee, and Schoen
[
11
]
later extended this work by proving a more general spacetime positive mass theorem up to dimension 7.
Huang and Lee
[
17
]
subsequently affirmed the rigidity statement of this theorem studied in
[
11
]
.
In a different approach, Witten
[
27
]
used spinors to prove the spacetime positive mass theorem, a method that generalizes to all higher dimensions for spin manifolds. Parker and Taubes
[
22
]
gave a mathematically rigorous formulation of Witten’s argument.
Chruściel and Maerten
[
9
]
later provided a rigorous proof of the desired rigidity statement in all dimensions for spin manifolds, building on Beig and Chruściel’s proof
[
6
]
in dimension 3. Lee’s book
[
18
]
offers a comprehensive exposition on the positive mass theorem.

We consider the following class of manifolds with boundary, specializing Example
1.1
.

Example 1.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact convex domain with smooth boundary
\partial K
and exterior normal
N\colon\partial K\to S^{n-1}
.
Note that
N
has degree
+1
.
Let
f\colon K\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth map satisfying

(a)

|df|<1
on
K
,

(b)

f=0
along
\partial K
,

(c)

At each point on
\partial K
, we have
df(N)=0
or
dN=0
.

Let

M:=\{(x,f(x))\colon x\in K\} \subset\mathbb{R}^{n,1}

be the graph of
f
. Then
M
is a compact smooth spacelike hypersurface with boundary
\Sigma=\partial K\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
, see Figures
1
and
2
.

Let
I\!I
denote the vector-valued second fundamental form of
\Sigma
, viewed as a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
of codimension
2
. Then

I\!I(X,Y)=-\langle dN(X),Y\rangle\,N

for all tangent vectors
X,Y\in T\Sigma
.

We denote by
T
the future-oriented normal field to
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
, where
T
is normalized so that
\langle T,T\rangle=-1
. Let
g
denote the induced Riemannian metric and let
q
denote the second fundamental form of
M
with respect to
T
. In other words,
q(X,Y)=\langle\bar{D}_{X}T,Y\rangle
for all
X,Y\in TM
, where
\bar{D}
denotes the standard flat connection on
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
. The initial data set
(M,g,q)
satisfies the dominant energy condition, cf. Example
1.1
.
At each point on
\Sigma
, we denote by
\nu
the outward-pointing unit normal vector to
\Sigma
in
M
. Moreover, we denote by
h
the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
, viewed as a hypersurface in
M
, with respect to
\nu
.
In other words,
h(X,Y)=\langle D_{X}\nu,Y\rangle
for
X,Y\in T\Sigma
, where
D
denotes the Levi-Civita connection on
(M,g)
.
We will use these sign conventions for
q
and
h
throughout our paper.

With this understood,

I\!I(X,Y)=-h(X,Y)\,\nu+q(X,Y)\,T

for all tangent vectors
X,Y\in T\Sigma
.
Putting these facts together, we conclude that

h(X,Y)\,\nu-q(X,Y)\,T=\langle dN(X),Y\rangle\,N

for all tangent vectors
X,Y\in T\Sigma
. In particular,

h(X,Y)=\langle dN(X),Y\rangle\,\langle N,\nu\rangle

and

q(X,Y)=\langle dN(X),Y\rangle\,\langle N,T\rangle

for all tangent vectors
X,Y\in T\Sigma
.
We now use Assumption (
c
).
Hence, at each point on
\Sigma
, we either have
dN=0
, or else
\langle N,\nu\rangle=1
and
\langle N,T\rangle=0
.
Consequently,
h(X,Y)=\langle dN(X),Y\rangle
and
q(X,Y)=0
for all tangent vectors
X,Y\in T\Sigma
.
In particular,
H=\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
and
\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)=0
at each point on
\Sigma
.
Here,
H
denotes the unnormalized mean curvature, i.e. the sum of the principal curvatures, of
\Sigma
in
M
.
In other words,
H
is equal to the trace of
h
, considered as an endomorphism field.
Moreover,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
denotes the trace norm of a linear map, i.e., the sum of all singular values.

Our first result identifies this class of examples as an extremal case of initial data sets.

Theorem I
.

Let
(M,g,q)
be an initial data set.
Assume that
M
is a compact, connected spin manifold, has dimension
n\geq 2
and nonempty boundary
\partial M=\Sigma
.
Let
N\colon\Sigma\to S^{n-1}
be a smooth map.
We assume that

•

(M,g,q)
satisfies the dominant energy condition,

•

H\geq|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
holds along
\Sigma
,

•

N
has positive degree.

Then
(M,g)
is isometric to a manifold appearing in Example
1.2
such that
q
is the second fundamental form of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
. Moreover, the map
N
agrees, up to an element of
O(n)
, with the exterior normal
\partial K\to S^{n-1}
.

Remark 1.3
.

We do not assume that
\Sigma
is connected.
If
\Sigma
is not connected, the degree of
N\colon\Sigma\to S^{n-1}
equals the sum of the mapping degrees of
N
restricted to the components of
\Sigma
with their orientations induced from that of
M
.

Remark 1.4
.

The quantity
H-|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|
is related to the notion of marginally trapped surfaces in general relativity.
Recall that a marginally outer trapped surface (MOTS) is a hypersurface in an initial data set
(M,g,q)
satisfying
H+(\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu))=0
; see
[
11
]
*p. 89.

Remark 1.5
.

There is also a variant of Theorem
I
for
n=1
.
In this case,
M=[a,b]
is a compact interval,
g=dt\otimes dt
is the standard metric, and
q=\kappa g
where
\kappa\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is a given smooth function.
We can choose
N\colon\partial M=\{a,b\}\to S^{0}=\{-1,1\}
to be bijective.
Then all quantities
R
,
\mu
,
H
,
J
,
dN
, and
\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)
vanish.
Thus the assumptions of Theorem
I
are trivially satisfied.

The conclusion is now that there is a spacelike curve
[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
, parametrized by arc-length and with curvature
\kappa
.
Indeed, this can be shown directly by ODE methods.
The analogue for the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
is known as the fundamental theorem of planar curve theory.

For
q=0
, Theorem
I
gives the following statement:

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact connected Riemannian spin manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
and nonempty boundary
\partial M=\Sigma
.
Let
N\colon\Sigma\to S^{n-1}
be a smooth map.
We assume that

•

R\geq 0
holds on
M
,

•

H\geq\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
holds along
\Sigma
,

•

N
has positive degree.

Then
M
can be isometrically embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a compact convex domain with smooth boundary and exterior normal
N
.

In the second part of this paper, we consider initial data sets of the form
(\Omega,g,q)
, where
\Omega
is a compact, convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with non-empty interior. When
q=0
, the dominant energy condition simplifies to the non-negativity of the scalar curvature
R\geq 0
, and the boundary condition reduces to mean convexity
H\geq 0
, allowing us to compare
(\Omega,g)
with Euclidean polytopes. Gromov
[
14
]
initiated the study of scalar curvature comparison for polytopes with nonnegative scalar curvature to explore the notion of scalar curvature lower bounds in low-regularity spaces, and this problem has been extensively investigated; see
[
14
,
15
,
13
,
19
,
21
,
26
]
. For
q=g
, the dominant energy condition translates to
R\geq-n(n-1)
. Li
[
20
]
established a polyhedral comparison result for metrics with scalar curvature at least
-n(n-1)
.

To explore the notion of energy density lower bounds in spacetimes with low regularity, we extend Gromov’s initial work
[
14
]
to initial data sets under the dominant energy condition. In the case where
\Omega
is a cube, Tsang
[
25
]
addressed this problem in dimension 3 using the spacetime harmonic function approach.

We will employ the strategy developed by the second named author in
[
7
]
, adapting it to initial data sets. To achieve this, we utilize a boundary value problem for Dirac operators on smooth domains, which have been extensively studied thanks to the work of Hörmander
[
16
]
, and the first named author and Ballmann
[
2
,
3
]
. We write the given polytope in the form
\Omega=\bigcap_{i\in I}\{u_{i}\leq 0\}
, where
I
is a finite set and
u_{i}
are linear functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For each
i\in I
, let
N_{i}\in S^{n-1}
and
\nu_{i}
denote the outward pointing unit normal vectors to the half-space
\{u_{i}\leq 0\}
with respect to the Euclidean metric and the metric
g
, respectively.
We make the following assumption:

Matching Angle Hypothesis
.

If
x\in\partial\Omega
is a point on the boundary and
i_{1},i_{2}\in I
satisfy
u_{i_{1}}(x)=u_{i_{2}}(x)=0
, then
\langle\nu_{i_{1}}(x),\nu_{i_{2}}(x)\rangle_{g}=\langle N_{i_{1}},N_{i_{2}}\rangle
.
Here the inner product
\langle\nu_{i_{1}},\nu_{i_{2}}\rangle_{g}
is computed with respect to the metric
g
and
\langle N_{i_{1}},N_{i_{2}}\rangle
is the standard Euclidean inner product.

Theorem II
.

Assume that
n\geq 2
is an integer.
Let
\Omega
be a compact, convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with nonempty interior. Let
g
be a Riemannian metric which is defined on an open set containing
\Omega
.
Let
q
be a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor, which is defined on an open set containing
\Omega
.
We assume that

•

(\Omega,g,q)
satisfies the dominant energy condition,

•

H\geq|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|
on the boundary faces of
\Omega
,

•

the Matching Angle Hypothesis is satisfied.

Then
(\Omega,g,q)
can be isometrically immersed as a spacelike hypersurface in the Minkowski spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
such that
q
is the second fundamental form. Moreover, each boundary face of
\Omega
is totally geodesic as a codimension 2 submanifold in the Minkowski spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
.

Conjecture 1.7
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
II
, there exists a smooth map
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

•

|df|<1
on
\Omega
,

•

f=0
along
\partial\Omega
,

and an isometry of
(\Omega,g)
to

\textrm{graph}(f)=\{(x,f(x))\colon x\in\Omega\} \subset\mathbb{R}^{n,1}

with the induced Riemannian metric.
Furthermore, under this isometry,
q
is the second fundamental form of
\textrm{graph}(f)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
.

2.
A boundary value problem for the Dirac operator in odd dimensions

Throughout this section, we assume that
M
is a compact, connected spin manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
with boundary
\partial M=:\Sigma
.
Let
g
be a Riemannian metric on
M
.
Suppose that
\nu
is the unit outward normal field along
\partial M
and that
N\colon\partial M\to S^{n-1}
is a smooth map.

Let
m=2^{[\frac{n}{2}]}
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the spinor bundle over
M
.
Note that
\mathcal{S}\to M
is a complex vector bundle of rank
m
equipped with a Hermitian inner product and compatible connection
\nabla^{\mathcal{S}}
.

Let
S_{0}
denote the spinor module of the complex Clifford algebra
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{l}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Now both
S_{0}
and its dual space
S_{0}^{*}
are
m
-dimensional Hermitian vector spaces.

We define a complex vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
over
M
by
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{S}\otimes S_{0}^{*}
.
The bundle
\mathcal{E}
has rank
m^{2}
and inherits a natural Hermitian inner product and compatible connection
\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The fiber of
\mathcal{E}\to M
over
x\in M
is given by

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}|_{x}=\mathcal{S}|_{x}\otimes S_{0}^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}(S_{0},\mathcal{S}|_{x}).

Thus, a section
s
of
\mathcal{E}
can be considered as a field of homomorphisms.
Given an element
\sigma\in S_{0}
we can apply
s
at each point
x\in M
to it and obtain a section
x\mapsto s(x)\sigma
of
\mathcal{S}
.

Denote by
\gamma\colon TM\to\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{S})
the Clifford multiplication of
(M,g)
and by
\gamma_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathrm{End}(S_{0})
the one of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This gives an induced Clifford multiplication
\gamma_{0}^{*}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathrm{End}(S_{0}^{*})
, where
\gamma_{0}^{*}(v)
is defined as the adjoint of
\gamma_{0}(v)
. We define

(2.2)

\chi\colon\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}\to\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}\quad\mbox{ by }\quad\chi_{x}:=-\gamma(\nu(x))\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N(x)).

Since
\gamma(\nu)^{2}=\gamma_{0}^{*}(N)^{2}=-1
we have

\chi^{2}=1.

Since both
\gamma(\nu)
and
\gamma_{0}^{*}(N)
are skew-adjoint we get

\chi^{*}=\chi.

For
\xi\in T\partial M
the linear map
\gamma(\xi)\otimes 1
anticommutes with
\chi
and is an isomorphism if
\xi\neq 0
.
Therefore the
\pm 1
-eigenspaces of
\chi
have the same dimension.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the spinorial Dirac operator on
M
, acting on sections of
\mathcal{E}
.
This Dirac operator is formally selfadjoint. By Corollary 7.23 in
[
2
]
, the conditions
\chi s=s
and
\chi s=-s
both form elliptic boundary conditions for
\mathcal{D}
.
Since
\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1
commutes with
\chi
, the boundary conditions
\chi s=s
and
\chi s=-s
are adjoint to each other, see
[
2
]
*Section 7.2.

We denote by
L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
the Hilbert space of square integrable sections of
\mathcal{E}
and by
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
the Sobolev space of
L^{2}
-sections whose distributional derivatives are also square integrable.
If
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}
is a subbundle of
\mathcal{E}
along the boundary, we write
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}):=\{u\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})\mid u|_{\Sigma}\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathcal{F})\}
.
Note that the restriction map extends uniquely to a bounded linear map
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})\to L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathcal{E})
by the trace theorem.

We decompose the bundle

(2.3)

\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{F}^{+}\oplus\mathcal{F}^{-}

where
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}
are the eigensubbundles of
\chi
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\pm 1
.
We need the following index computation, which generalizes the results in
[
7
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
M
have odd dimension
n\geq 3
.
Then the operator

\mathcal{D}\colon H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}^{+})\to L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})

is a Fredholm operator.
Its Fredholm index equals the degree of
N\colon\Sigma\to S^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Since
n
is odd, the restriction
\mathcal{S}|_{\Sigma}
can be canonically identified with the spinor bundle of
\Sigma
. The field of involutions
i\gamma(\nu)
can be used to decompose

\mathcal{S}|_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{+}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{-}

where
\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{\pm}
are the eigensubbundles corresponding to the eigenvalues
\pm 1
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}
denote the trivial bundle over
S^{n-1}
with fiber
S_{0}
, and let
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
denote the dual bundle over
S^{n-1}
with fiber
S_{0}^{*}
. With this understood, we may write
\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{S}|_{\Sigma}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
. We may decompose the bundle
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
as

(2.4)

\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-},

where, for each point
v\in S^{n-1}
,
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,\pm}
denote the eigensubbundles of
i\gamma(v)
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\pm 1
. This gives a decomposition

(2.5)

N^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}=N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+}\oplus N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-},

where
N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,\pm}
are the eigensubbundles of
i\gamma_{0}^{*}(N)
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\pm 1
. We then have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{+}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{+}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})\oplus(\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{-}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{+}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-})\oplus(\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{-}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+}).

Note that even though
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
is a trivial bundle, the two subbundles
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+}
and
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-}
are not because the decomposition depends on the base point. The splitting (
2.4
) is not parallel with respect to the canonical flat connection on
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
, but the Levi-Civita connection of
S^{n-1}
induces a connection on the dual of its spinor bundle for which it is.
We equip
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,\pm}
with these latter connections and
N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,\pm}
with the corresponding pull-back connections.

We next consider the twisted Dirac operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{+}

\displaystyle\colon H^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{+}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})\to L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{-}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{-}

\displaystyle\colon H^{1}(\Sigma,\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{-}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-})\to L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathcal{S}_{\Sigma}^{+}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-}).

Then the operator

(2.6)

\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{-}\\
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{+}&0\end{pmatrix}

maps sections of
\mathcal{F}^{+}
to sections of
\mathcal{F}^{-}
.

In the following,
\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)
denotes the
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
-form of the tangent bundle of
\Sigma
and
\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,\pm})
the Chern character form of the bundle
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,\pm}
.
The lower index
k
in
\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)_{k}
indicates the homogeneous part of degree
k
and similarly for the Chern character.
By the Atiyah-Singer index theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{ind}(\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{+})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)\wedge N^{*}\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)_{0}\wedge N^{*}\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})_{n-1}+\int_{\Sigma}\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)_{n-1}\wedge N^{*}\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}N^{*}\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})_{n-1}+\mathsf{rank}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})\int_{\Sigma}\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{deg}(N)\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})_{n-1}+\frac{m}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)_{n-1}

and similarly

\displaystyle-\mathsf{ind}(\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{-})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{deg}(N)\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-})_{n-1}+\frac{m}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\hat{\mathsf{A}}(\Sigma)_{n-1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{ind}(\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{ind}(\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{+})+\mathsf{ind}(\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{-})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{deg}(N)\int_{S^{n-1}}(\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,+})-\mathsf{ch}(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*,-}))_{n-1}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathsf{deg}(N).

Now, we observe that
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}
are formally selfadjoint,
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}
is an adapted boundary operator for
\mathcal{D}
, the involution
i\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1
preserves the splitting (
2.3
) and anticommutes with
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}
.
Therefore, the holographic index theorem from Appendix B in
[
4
]
applies and tells us that the operator

\mathcal{D}\colon H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}^{+})\to L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})

is Fredholm and

\mathsf{ind}(\mathcal{D}\colon H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}^{+})\to L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E}))=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathsf{ind}(\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma})=\mathsf{deg}(N).\qed

3.
Spin geometry for initial data sets

Let
M
be a compact spin manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
with boundary
\partial M=\Sigma
.
Let
g
be a Riemannian metric on
M
, let
q
be a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor field on
M
and let
N\colon\Sigma\to S^{n-1}
be a smooth map.
Let
\chi
be defined as in (
2.2
).
We will denote the selfadjoint endomorphism field corresponding to
q
also by
q\colon TM\to TM
, i.e.

q(X,Y)=g(q(X),Y).

Furthermore, we put

(3.1)

\mu:=\tfrac{1}{2}\,R+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}(q)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,|q|^{2}

and

(3.2)

J:={\mathrm{div}}(q)-\nabla\mathrm{tr}(q)

where
R
is the scalar curvature of
g
.
We use
q
to modify the connections on
\mathcal{S}
and on
\mathcal{E}
.

Definition 3.1
.

We define a connection
\nabla^{\mathcal{S},q}
on
\mathcal{S}
by

(3.3)

\nabla^{\mathcal{S},q}:=\nabla^{\mathcal{S}}+\frac{i}{2}\gamma\circ q

on
\mathcal{S}
. We next introduce the homomorphism field
Q\colon\mathcal{E}\to T^{*}M\otimes\mathcal{E}
which maps spinors to spinor-valued
1
-forms by setting

(3.4)

(Qs)(X):=(\gamma(q(X))\otimes 1)s

for every section
s
of
\mathcal{E}
and every tangent vector
X
.
With this understood, we define a connection
\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}
on
\mathcal{E}
by

(3.5)

\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}:=\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}+\tfrac{i}{2}Q.

Note that the connection
\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}
on
\mathcal{E}
is the tensor product connection of
\nabla^{\mathcal{S},q}
with the flat connection on the trivial bundle over
M
with fiber
S_{0}
.

In order to derive a modified Weitzenböck formula involving
\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}
we need some preparation.

Lemma 3.2
.

Denote the (pointwise) adjoint of
Q
by
Q^{*}\colon T^{*}M\otimes\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}
.
Then we have

(a)

Q^{*}Q=|q|^{2}
,

(b)

Q^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}-(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}Q=\gamma(\mathrm{div}(q))\otimes 1
.

Proof.

Let
p\in M
and let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{p}M
.
Denote the dual basis of
T^{*}_{p}M
by
e^{1},\dots,e^{n}
.
Then we have for
s\in\mathcal{E}|_{p}
and
\psi\in T^{*}_{p}M\otimes\mathcal{E}|_{p}
:

\displaystyle\langle Q^{*}\psi,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi,Qs\rangle=\Big\langle\psi,\sum_{j}e^{j}\otimes(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)s\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\langle\psi(e_{j}),(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)s\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}\langle(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)\psi(e_{j}),s\rangle

and hence

Q^{*}\psi=-\sum_{j}(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)\psi(e_{j}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle Q^{*}Qs

\displaystyle=Q^{*}\Big(\sum_{j}e^{j}\otimes(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)s\Big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)s

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}|q(e_{j})|^{2}s=|q|^{2}s.

This proves (
a
).
To show (
b
), extend the orthonormal basis to a neighborhood of
p
such that it is synchronous at
p
.
Let
s
be a smooth section of
\mathcal{E}
, defined near
p
.
Then we compute at
p
:

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}Qs

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}\Big(\sum_{j}e^{j}\otimes(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)s\Big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j}(\gamma((D_{e_{j}}q)(e_{j}))\otimes 1)s-\sum_{j}(\gamma(q(e_{j}))\otimes 1)\nabla_{e_{j}}^{\mathcal{E}}s

\displaystyle=-(\gamma(\mathrm{div}(q))\otimes 1)s+Q^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}s.\qed

The well-known Weitzenböck formula gives

\mathcal{D}^{2}=(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}+\tfrac{1}{4}R.

We find a modification, taking
q
into account.

Proposition 3.3

(Modified Weitzenböck formula)
.

We have the following operator identity:

(\mathcal{D}+\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))=(\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q})^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu+\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(J)\otimes 1.

Proof.

We compute, using Lemma
3.2
:

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q})^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}

\displaystyle=((\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}-\tfrac{i}{2}Q^{*})(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}+\tfrac{i}{2}Q)

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}-\tfrac{i}{2}(Q^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}-(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}Q)+\tfrac{1}{4}Q^{*}Q

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\mathcal{E}})^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}-\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(\mathrm{div}(q))\otimes 1+\tfrac{1}{4}|q|^{2}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}+\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{2}-\tfrac{i}{2}[\mathcal{D},\mathrm{tr}(q)]+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}(q)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{2}-\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(\nabla\mathrm{tr}(q))\otimes 1+\tfrac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}(q)^{2}.

These two equations together with the standard Weitzenböck formula yield

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}+\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))(\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))-(\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q})^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}R+\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(\mathrm{div}(q)-\nabla\mathrm{tr}(q))\otimes 1+\tfrac{1}{4}(\mathrm{tr}(q)^{2}-|q|^{2})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mu+\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(J)\otimes 1.\qed

Again we denote
\partial M=\Sigma
and the unit outward normal along
\Sigma
by
\nu
.
Let
H
denote the (unnormalized) mean curvature.
The sign convention is such that the boundary of a Euclidean ball has positive mean curvature.

We define an operator
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}
acting on sections of
\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{S}|_{\Sigma}\otimes S_{0}^{*}
by

\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes 1)\,\nabla_{e_{j}}^{\mathcal{E}}-\tfrac{1}{2}H,

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
denotes a local orthonormal frame on
\Sigma
and
\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}
denotes the connection on
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{S}\otimes S_{0}^{*}
defined above. By Proposition 2.2 in
[
1
]
,
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}
can be identified with a twisted Dirac operator acting on sections of
\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{S}|_{\Sigma}\otimes N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
, where the twist bundle
N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
carries the flat connection. Note that
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}
differs from the operator
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}
defined in (
2.6
). In the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}
, we used a nonflat connection on the twist bundle
N^{*}\mathcal{S}_{0}^{*}
that preserves the splittting (
2.5
). The operators
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}
both anticommute with
\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1
.

Corollary 3.4
.

For all
s\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
we have

\displaystyle-\int_{M}|(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))s|^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}\langle(\mu+i\gamma(J)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\langle(-\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}-\tfrac{1}{2}H+\tfrac{i}{2}(\gamma(q(\nu)-\mathrm{tr}(q)\nu)\otimes 1))s,s\rangle.

Proof.

Note that

(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)\mathcal{D}s=-\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s-\nabla_{\nu}^{\mathcal{E}}s-\tfrac{1}{2}Hs

along
\Sigma
. Using this and integrating by parts we find

\displaystyle\int_{M}

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{D}+\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))s|^{2}+\int_{\Sigma}\langle(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))s|^{2}+\int_{\Sigma}\big\langle\big(-\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}-\nabla_{\nu}^{\mathcal{E}}-\tfrac{1}{2}H-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q)(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)\big)s,s\big\rangle.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\langle(\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q})^{*}\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}-\int_{\Sigma}\langle\nabla_{\nu}^{\mathcal{E},q}s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}-\int_{\Sigma}\langle(\nabla_{\nu}^{\mathcal{E}}+\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(q(\nu))\otimes 1)s,s\rangle.

Inserting these two equations into the modified Weitzenböck formula concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Along
\Sigma
we have

\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}\chi+\chi\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(dN(e_{j}))

where
e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
is a local orthonormal tangent frame to
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}
anticommutes with
\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1
. Hence, we compute for a smooth section
s
of
\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}\chi s

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N))s

\displaystyle=(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N))s

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes 1)\,\nabla_{e_{j}}^{\mathcal{E}}((1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N))s)-\tfrac{1}{2}H\,(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N))s

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N))\,\nabla_{e_{j}}^{\mathcal{E}}s-\tfrac{1}{2}H\,(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N))s

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(dN(e_{j})))s

\displaystyle=(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(N))\,\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(dN(e_{j})))s

\displaystyle=-\chi\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes 1)(1\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(dN(e_{j})))s.\qed

Lemma 3.6
.

We have

\Big|\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(dN(e_{j})))s,s\Big\rangle\Big|\leq\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,|s|^{2}

at each point on
\Sigma
. Here,
e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
denotes a local orthonormal frame on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let us fix a point
p\in\Sigma
. We choose orthonormal bases
e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
of
T_{p}\Sigma
and
e^{0}_{1},\dots,e^{0}_{n-1}
of
T_{N(p)}S^{n-1}
such that
dN(e_{j})=\lambda_{j}e^{0}_{j}
, where
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
denote the singular values of
dN
.
Then

\displaystyle\Big|\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(dN(e_{j})))s,s\Big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\,\langle(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(e^{0}_{j}))s,s\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\,|(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(e^{0}_{j}))s|\,|s|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}\,|s|^{2}.\qed

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose that
s\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
satisfies the boundary condition
\chi s=\varepsilon s
along
\Sigma
, where
\varepsilon\colon\Sigma\to\{-1,1\}
is locally constant.
Then we have the estimate

\displaystyle-\int_{M}|(\mathcal{D}-\tfrac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q))s|^{2}+\int_{M}

\displaystyle|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}\langle(\mu+i\gamma(J)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}-H+|q(\nu,\nu)-\mathrm{tr}(q)|)|s|^{2}.

Proof.

We first observe that

2\,\langle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s,s\rangle=\varepsilon\,\langle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}\chi s,s\rangle+\varepsilon\,\langle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s,\chi s\rangle=\varepsilon\,\langle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}\chi s+\chi\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s,s\rangle

at each point on
\Sigma
. Using Lemma
3.5
, we obtain

2\,|\langle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s,s\rangle|=\Big|\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\gamma(e_{j})\otimes\gamma_{0}^{*}(dN(e_{j})))s,s\Big\rangle\Big|

at each point on
\Sigma
.
Lemma
3.6
implies

(3.6)

2\,|\langle\mathcal{D}^{\Sigma}_{0}s,s\rangle|\leq\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,|s|^{2}

at each point on
\Sigma
. Moreover, for each
X\in T\Sigma
we have

\displaystyle\langle(\gamma(X)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\langle(\gamma(X)\otimes 1)\chi s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\langle\chi(\gamma(X)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\langle(\gamma(X)\otimes 1)s,\chi s\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle(\gamma(X)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle,

hence

\langle(\gamma(X)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle=0.

Putting
X=q(\nu)^{\text{\rm tan}}
, the part of
q(\nu)
tangential to
\Sigma
, gives

\langle(\gamma(q(\nu))\otimes 1)s,s\rangle=q(\nu,\nu)\,\langle(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle.

We conclude

\displaystyle\big|\langle(\gamma(q(\nu)-\mathrm{tr}(q)\nu)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle\big|

\displaystyle=\big|(q(\nu,\nu)-\mathrm{tr}(q))\,\langle(\gamma(\nu)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle\big|

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq|q(\nu,\nu)-\mathrm{tr}(q)|\,|s|^{2}.

Inserting (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) into Corollary
3.4
concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Suppose that
M
has odd dimension
n\geq 3
and that the map
N\colon\Sigma\to S^{n-1}
has positive degree. Then there exists a non-trivial section
s\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}(-\mu+|J|)|s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}-H)\,|s|^{2}.

Moreover,
\chi s=s
holds at each point on the boundary
\Sigma
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
, the operator
\mathcal{D}
subject to the boundary conditions
\chi s=s
has positive index.
By deformation invariance of the Fredholm index, the same holds for the operator
\mathcal{D}-\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{tr}(q)
.
Hence we can find a non-trivial section
s
of
\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{D}s-\frac{i}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}(q)\,s=0
on
M
and
\chi s=s
along
\Sigma
.
By elliptic regularity,
s
is smooth.
Using Proposition
3.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}\langle(\mu+i\gamma(J)\otimes 1)s,s\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}-H)\,|s|^{2}.

From this, the assertion follows easily.
∎

In the next step, we establish an analogue of Proposition

Lemma 3.9
.

We can find a smooth map
\varphi\colon S^{n-1}\times S^{1}\to S^{n}

•

The map
\varphi

.

•

\varphi^{*}g_{S^{n}}\leq g_{S^{n-1}}+4g_{S^{1}}

\int_{M}|s|^{2}>0

and

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}(-\mu+|J|)|s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}-H)\,|s|^{2}+r^{-1}\int_{\Sigma}|s|^{2}.

Moreover, since
\tilde{\chi}\tilde{s}=\tilde{s}
along the boundary
\tilde{\Sigma}
, we conclude that
\gamma(\nu)\circ s=s\circ\gamma_{0}(\hat{N})
at each point on
\Sigma
, where the map
\hat{N}:\Sigma\to S^{n}
is defined by
\hat{N}(x)=\tilde{N}(x,\hat{t})=\varphi(N(x),\hat{t})
for all
x\in\Sigma
.
∎

Corollary 3.12
.

Suppose that
M
has even dimension
n\geq 2
and that the map
N\colon\Sigma\to S^{n-1}
has positive degree. We can find an element
\hat{t}\in S^{1}
and a non-trivial section
s\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}(-\mu+|J|)|s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}-H)\,|s|^{2}.

Moreover, we have
\gamma(\nu)\circ s=s\circ\gamma_{0}(\hat{N})
along
\Sigma
. Here, the map
\hat{N}:\Sigma\to S^{n}
is defined by
\hat{N}(x)=\tilde{N}(x,\hat{t})=\varphi(N(x),\hat{t})
for all
x\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary sequence
r_{l}\to\infty
. For each
l
, Proposition
3.11
implies the existence of a section
s^{(l)}\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that

\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2}=1

and

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s^{(l)}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}(-\mu+|J|)|s^{(l)}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}-H)\,|s^{(l)}|^{2}+r_{l}^{-1}\int_{\Sigma}|s^{(l)}|^{2}.

Moreover, we can find an element
\hat{t}_{l}\in S^{1}
such that
\gamma(\nu)\circ s^{(l)}=s^{(l)}\circ\gamma_{0}(\hat{t}_{l})
along
\Sigma
, where the map
\hat{N}_{l}:\Sigma\to S^{n}
is defined by
\hat{N}_{l}(x)=\tilde{N}(x,\hat{t}_{l})=\varphi(N(x),\hat{t}_{l})
for all
x\in\Sigma
. This implies

\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}s^{(l)}|^{2}\leq C\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2}+C\int_{\Sigma}|s^{(l)}|^{2},

where
C
is a constant that does not depend on
l
. Using a standard interpolation inequality, we can bound

\int_{\Sigma}|s^{(l)}|^{2}\leq C\,\bigg(\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\bigg(\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}s^{(l)}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}+C\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2},

where
C
is a constant that does not depend on
l
. Putting these facts together, we obtain

\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}s^{(l)}|^{2}\leq C\,\bigg(\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\bigg(\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}s^{(l)}|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}+C\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2},

where
C
is a constant that does not depend on
l
. Using Young’s inequality, we conclude that

\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E}}s^{(l)}|^{2}\leq C\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2},

where
C
is a constant that does not depend on
l
. Since
\int_{M}|s^{(l)}|^{2}=1
for each
l
, it follows that the sequence
s^{(l)}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
.

After passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
\hat{t}_{l}\to\hat{t}
for some
\hat{t}\in S^{1}
. Moreover, we may assume that the sequence
s^{(l)}
converges weakly in
H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
to some limit
s\in H^{1}(M,\mathcal{E})
. Then
s_{l}\to s
strongly in
L^{2}(M,\mathcal{E})
. Moreover,
s_{l}|_{\Sigma}\to s|_{\Sigma}
strongly in
L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathcal{E}|_{\Sigma})
. Thus, we conclude that

\int_{M}|s|^{2}=1

and

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}s|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}(-\mu+|J|)|s|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}-H)\,|s|^{2}.

Finally, on the boundary
\Sigma
, we have
\gamma(\nu)\circ s^{(l)}=s^{(l)}\circ\gamma_{0}(\hat{N}_{l})
for each
l
. Passing to the limit as
l\to\infty
, we conclude that
\gamma(\nu)\circ s=s\circ\gamma_{0}(\hat{N})
, where the map
\hat{N}:\Sigma\to S^{n}
is defined by
\hat{N}(x)=\tilde{N}(x,\hat{t})=\varphi(N(x),\hat{t})
for all
x\in\Sigma
.
∎

Let

We denote the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
in

Proposition 4.1
.

The following assertions hold.

-parallel smooth section

satisfying

along

We prove Proposition

4.1.
Proof of Proposition
4.1
in odd dimensions

Suppose that
n\geq 3
is an odd integer.
Since by assumption
\mu\geq|J|
on
M
and
H\geq|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|+\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
along
\Sigma
, Proposition
3.8
yields non-trivial
\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}
-parallel section
s\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathcal{E})
such that
\chi s=s
holds along
\Sigma
.

Lemma 4.2
.

At each point
p\in\Sigma
, the second fundamental form
h
and the tensor field
q
satisfy the relation

\gamma(h(X))\circ s+i\,\gamma(q(X)^{\text{\rm tan}})\circ\gamma(\nu)\circ s=s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(X))

for all
X\in T_{p}\Sigma
.

Proof.

Since
s
is
\nabla^{\mathcal{E},q}
-parallel,

(4.1)

\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{E}}s=-\tfrac{i}{2}Qs(X)=-\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(q(X))\circ s

for every vector field
X
on
M
. Using the boundary condition
\chi s=s
, we obtain

(4.2)

\gamma(\nu)\circ s=s\circ\gamma_{0}(N)

on
\Sigma
. In the next step, we differentiate (
4.2
) along a vector field
X\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma,T\Sigma)
. Using (
4.1
) we find for the left hand side

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{E}}(\gamma(\nu)\circ s)

\displaystyle=\gamma(D_{X}\nu)\circ s+\gamma(\nu)\circ\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{E}}s

\displaystyle=\gamma(h(X))\circ s-\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(\nu)\circ\gamma(q(X))\circ s

and for the right hand side

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{E}}(s\circ\gamma_{0}(N))

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{S}}s)\circ\gamma_{0}(N)+s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(X))

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{i}{2}\gamma(q(X))\circ s\circ\gamma_{0}(N)+s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(X)).

Thus (
4.2
) implies

\displaystyle\gamma(h(X))

\displaystyle\circ s-s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(X))

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{i}{2}\big(\gamma(q(X))\circ s\circ\gamma_{0}(N)-\gamma(\nu)\circ\gamma(q(X))\circ s\big)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{i}{2}\big(\gamma(q(X))\circ\gamma(\nu)\circ s-\gamma(\nu)\circ\gamma(q(X))\circ s\big)

\displaystyle=-i\,\gamma(q(X)^{\text{\rm tan}})\circ\gamma(\nu)\circ s.

For the last equality, we have used the fact that the normal component of
q(X)
commutes with
\gamma(\nu)
while the tangential component of
q(X)
anticommutes with
\gamma(\nu)
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

-\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\circ s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(e_{j})),s\Big\rangle=\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,|s|^{2}

at each point on
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let us fix a point
p\in\Sigma
. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{p}\Sigma
.
Using Lemma
4.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\Big\langle

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\circ\gamma(h(e_{j}))\circ s,s\Big\rangle-i\,\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\circ\gamma(q(e_{j})^{\tan})\circ\gamma(\nu)\circ s,s\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\circ s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(e_{j})),s\Big\rangle.

This implies

\displaystyle H\,|s|^{2}+i\,(\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu))\,\langle\gamma(\nu)\circ s,s\rangle=-\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\circ s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(e_{j})),s\Big\rangle.

By assumption,
H-|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|\geq\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
at each point on
\Sigma
. Consequently,

\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,|s|^{2}\leq H\,|s|^{2}-|\mathrm{tr}(q)-q(\nu,\nu)|\,|s|^{2}\leq-\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\circ s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(e_{j})),s\Big\rangle.

On the other hand, using Lemma
3.6

-\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\gamma(e_{j})\circ s\circ\gamma_{0}(dN(e_{j})),s\Big\rangle\leq\|dN\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,|s|^{2}.

Putting these facts together, the assertion follows.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{0}

is a
\nabla^{\mathcal{S},q}
-parallel section of

∎

We define a linear subspace

The subspace

\gamma_{0}(N(p))
for each point

Proof.

Let us fix a point

Lemma 4.6

.
Moreover, the section

Proof.

It follows from Lemma

4.3.
Existence of a local isometric immersion into

Definition 4.15

\langle(Y,\varphi),(Z,\psi)\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}=g(Y,Z)-\varphi\psi

Lemma 4.16

\nabla^{\mathcal{T},q}

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}

Proof.

Let
X

(Y,\varphi)
,
(Z,\psi)

\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{T},q}(Y,\varphi),(Z,\psi)\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}+\langle(Y,\varphi),\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{T},q}(Z,\psi)\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}

Lemma 4.17
.

Suppose that
u,v
are
\nabla^{\mathcal{S},q}
-parallel sections of
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

By assumption,

\displaystyle g(D_{X}Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\text{\rm Re}(i\,\langle\gamma(Z)\,\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{S}}u,v\rangle)+\text{\rm Re}(i\,\langle\gamma(Z)\,u,\nabla_{X}^{\mathcal{S}}v\rangle)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{\rm Re}(\langle\gamma(Z)\,\gamma(q(X))\,u,v\rangle)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{\rm Re}(\langle\gamma(Z)\,u,\gamma(q(X))\,v\rangle)

\displaystyle=\text{\rm Re}(i\,\langle\gamma(q(X))\,u,v\rangle)

This completes the proof of Lemma

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}h(e_{i},e_{j})\,\langle T\wedge e_{j},\nu\wedge e_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n,1}}

Proposition 4.30

with respect to the Euclidean metric.
A homotopy is induced by deforming

Proposition 5.1

Appendix A
A metric characterization of the ball

Theorem A.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact connected Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
with boundary such that the following conditions are satisfied:

•

g
is flat.

•

The second fundamental form of
\partial M
is positive semi-definite.

•

There exists a point in
\partial M
, where the second fundamental form is positive definite.

Then
(M,g)
is isometric to a compact convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary.
In particular,
M
is diffeomorphic to a ball.

Proof.

For small enough
\varepsilon>0
, we consider the smooth manifold

M^{\prime}:=M\cup_{\sim}(\partial M\times(-\varepsilon,\infty)),

where
\sim
identifies
\partial M\times(-\varepsilon,0]\subset\partial M\times(-\varepsilon,\infty)
with an open neighborhood of
\partial M\subset M
by the normal exponential map along
\partial M
with respect to
g
.
Denote the metric on
\partial M
induced by
g
by
g_{0}
and the second fundamental form of
\partial M
(considered as a
(1,1)
-tensor field) by
h
.
The Gauss lemma, the Riccati equation for the second fundamental form and the vanishing of the curvature of
M
imply that on
\partial M\times(-\varepsilon,0]
the metric
g
takes the form
g=dt^{2}+g_{t}
where
g_{t}
is the metric on
\partial M
given by
g_{t}(X,Y)=g_{0}((\textup{id}+th)^{2}X,Y)
.
Here
t\in(-\varepsilon,\infty)
is the standard parameter.
Since
h
is positive semi-definite, we can use this formula to extend
g
to
\partial M\times[0,\infty)
and hence to a smooth metric
g^{\prime}
on all of
M^{\prime}
.
By Theorem 7.2 in
[
5
]
, the manifold
(M^{\prime},g^{\prime})
is flat.
Since the projection
(\partial M\times[0,\infty),dt^{2}+g_{t})\to(\partial M,g_{0})
is
1
-Lipschitz, it is easy to see that
(M^{\prime},g^{\prime})
is complete.

Let
\tilde{M}^{\prime}
be the universal covering of
M^{\prime}
equipped with the complete flat Riemannian metric
\tilde{g}^{\prime}
induced by
g^{\prime}
.
Since
(\tilde{M}^{\prime},\tilde{g}^{\prime})
is complete, flat and simply connected, it is isometric to Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Using this isometry, we regard the preimage
\tilde{M}\subset\tilde{M}^{\prime}
of
M\subset M^{\prime}
as a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary
\partial\tilde{M}
.
Since the embedding
M\hookrightarrow M^{\prime}
is a homotopy equivalence,
\tilde{M}
is connected and
\tilde{M}
is the universal covering of
M
.

We first assume that
n\geq 3
.
By assumption, the second fundamental form of
\partial\tilde{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is positive semi-definite and there exists a connected component
\Sigma\subset\partial\tilde{M}
with the property that the second fundamental form is positive definite at some point on
\Sigma
.
This implies that the sectional curvature of the induced Riemannian metric on
\Sigma\subset\tilde{M}
is non-negative and positive at some point.
This Riemannian metric of
\Sigma
is complete because it is pulled back from a compact manifold.

By the main theorem in
[
10
]
(see also
[
23
]
),
\Sigma=\partial K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex closed subset in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary.
Furthermore,
\Sigma
is diffeomorphic to
S^{n-1}
or to
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, hence it is the universal covering of a component of
\partial M
with the induced metric.
In the case
\Sigma\approx\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, the Cheeger-Gromoll splitting theorem (Theorem 3 in
[
8
]
) implies that the induced metric on
\Sigma
is flat, which is a contradiction.

Hence
\Sigma\approx S^{n-1}
and
K
is a compact convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary.
As
\tilde{M}
is connected and complete, we have
\tilde{M}\subset K
as a closed, hence compact subset.
Assume
K\setminus\tilde{M}\neq\emptyset
.
Then there exists a connected component
\Sigma^{\prime}\neq\Sigma
of
\partial\tilde{M}
and, again by
[
10
]
,
\Sigma^{\prime}=\partial K^{\prime}
for a convex closed subset
K^{\prime}\subset{\rm int}(K)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since
\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\mathrm{int}(K)
, we have that
K^{\prime}\subset\mathrm{int}(K)
.
In particular,
K^{\prime}\subset K
and hence
K^{\prime}
is compact.
Arguing as before, we obtain
\tilde{M}\subset K^{\prime}
.
Thus
\tilde{M}\subset\mathrm{int}(K)
, which is a contradiction.

Hence
\tilde{M}=K
and
\tilde{M}
is diffeomorphic to a ball.
By the Brouwer fixed point theorem, we have
M=\tilde{M}
.
This implies the assertion.

Now assume
n=2
.
Then the Gauss-Bonnet formula shows that the Euler characteristic of
M
is positive, which implies that
M
is diffeomorphic to a
2
-ball and, in particular, simply connected.
Since the inclusion
M\hookrightarrow M^{\prime}
is a homotopy equivalence, we get
\tilde{M}^{\prime}=M^{\prime}
.
In particular,
(M^{\prime},g^{\prime})
is isometric to Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
(M,g)\subset(M^{\prime},g^{\prime})
is isometric to a compact domain
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with smooth, mean-convex boundary.
This implies the assertion.
∎

Suppose the assertion is false. Then we can find a sequence of nonnegative functions
F_{j}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\int_{\Omega}F_{j}^{2}=1
,
\int_{U}F_{j}^{2}\to 0
, and

\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla F_{j}|-KF_{j})_{+}^{2}\to 0.

This implies

\int_{\Omega}(-F_{j})_{+}^{2}\to 0.

Moreover, using the triangle inequality, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla F_{j}|^{2}\leq C.

After passing to a subsequence, the sequence
F_{j}
converges to a function
F
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The function
F
satisfies
\int_{\Omega}F^{2}=1
. Moreover,
F=0
almost everywhere in
U
, and
F\geq 0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
.

Let
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
denote a smooth function which is supported in the unit ball and satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta=1
. For each
\varepsilon>0
, we define a smooth function
\eta_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
by

\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{n}}\eta\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The function
\eta_{\varepsilon}
is supported in a ball of radius
\varepsilon
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta_{\varepsilon}=1
.

Let us fix an arbitrary point
p\in U
. Let
\varepsilon
be a real number such that
0<\varepsilon<d(p,\partial U)
. Then
d(p,\partial\Omega)>\varepsilon
. We denote by
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
the connected component of the set
\{x\in\Omega:d(x,\partial\Omega)>\varepsilon\}
containing
p
. Let
F_{j}^{\varepsilon}\colon\Omega_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the convolution of
F_{j}
with
\eta_{\varepsilon}
, and let
F^{\varepsilon}\colon\Omega_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the convolution of
F
with
\eta_{\varepsilon}
. Then
|\nabla F_{j}^{\varepsilon}|\leq\eta_{\varepsilon}*|\nabla F_{j}|
at each point in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. This implies

|\nabla F_{j}^{\varepsilon}|-KF_{j}^{\varepsilon}\leq(\eta_{\varepsilon}*|\nabla F_{j}|)-K\,(\eta_{\varepsilon}*F_{j})\leq\eta_{\varepsilon}*(|\nabla F_{j}|-KF_{j})_{+}

at each point in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. We now pass to the limit as
j\to\infty
, keeping
\varepsilon
fixed. Since
F_{j}\to F
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we know that
F_{j}^{\varepsilon}\to F^{\varepsilon}
and
|\nabla F_{j}^{\varepsilon}|\to|\nabla F^{\varepsilon}|
at each point in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, since
(|\nabla F_{j}|-KF_{j}\big)_{+}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, it follows that
\eta_{\varepsilon}*(|\nabla F_{j}|-KF_{j})_{+}\to 0
at each point in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. Putting these facts together, we conclude that

|\nabla F^{\varepsilon}|-KF^{\varepsilon}\leq 0

at each point in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. On the other hand, we know that
F=0
almost everywhere in
U
. Since
d(p,\partial U)>\varepsilon
, it follows that
F^{\varepsilon}
vanishes at the point
p
. Since
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is connected, standard ODE arguments imply that
F^{\varepsilon}=0
at each point in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Finally, we send
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
\Omega
is connected, we conclude that
F=0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
. This contradicts the fact that
\int_{\Omega}F^{2}=1
.
