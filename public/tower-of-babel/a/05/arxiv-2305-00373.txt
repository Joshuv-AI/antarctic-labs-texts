Chemical equilibrium under vibrational strong coupling
Kaihong Sun, Raphael F. Ribeiro

Abstract
We introduce a theory of chemical equilibrium in optical microcavities, which allows us to relate equilibrium reaction quotients in different electromagnetic environments. Our theory shows that in planar microcavities under strong coupling with polyatomic molecules, hybrid modes formed between all dipole-active vibrations and cavity resonances contribute to polariton-assisted chemical equilibrium shifts. To illustrate key aspects of our formalism, we explore a model S N {}_{\text{N}} 2 reaction within a single-mode infrared resonator. Our findings reveal that chemical equilibria can be shifted in either direction of a chemical reaction, depending on the oscillator strength and frequencies of reactant and product normal-modes. Polariton-induced zero-point energy changes provide the dominant contributions, though the effects in single-mode cavities tend to diminish quickly as the temperature and number of molecules increase. Our approach is valid in generic electromagnetic environments and paves the way for understanding and controlling chemical equilibria with microcavities.

Introduction

Light-matter interactions are almost always irrelevant in equilibrium thermodynamics
McQuarrie 2000
;
Pathria 2016
. However, recent experiments have suggested otherwise, that the chemical equilibrium of aromatic-halogen charge-transfer complexes may be significantly changed via strong light-matter coupling

The signature of strong light-matter interactions is the formation of hybrid states referred to as polaritons, consisting of a superposition of electromagnetic (EM) and matter excitations

;
Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Hertzog et al. 2019

\mu
m)]

;
Vahala 2003
;
Bellessa et al. 2004
;
Hakala et al. 2009
;
Chikkaraddy et al. 2016

). A simple paradigmatic model of this phenomenon includes an isolated cavity mode under strong interaction with the collective polarization of a molecular system containing
N
identical molecules. This system has two hybrid light-matter modes denoted lower and upper polaritons (LP and UP, respectively), and
N-1
molecular reservoir modes with zero photonic content.

In this work, we present a quantum theoretical investigation of chemical equilibrium under vibrational strong coupling (VSC). We provide a general theory of nonperturbative light-matter interaction effects on chemical equilibrium and obtain an exact relationship between equilibrium reactive mixtures inside and outside microcavities in Sec.
II

III

We examine the temperature, normal mode frequency, oscillator strength, and size dependence of the polariton effect on the reactive mixture composition at equilibrium. We summarize our main results and explain how the provided formalism informs future work in Sec.

II
Theory

In this section, we present a general formalism for the investigation of nonperturbative light-matter interaction effects on the composition of generic reactive mixtures. Let A, B, C, and E denote reactive chemical species in equilibrium according to

\displaystyle\nu_{\text{A}}\text{A}+\nu_{\text{B}}\text{B}\rightleftharpoons\nu_{\text{C}}\text{C}+\nu_{\text{E}}\text{E}.

(1)

We write the total molecular quantum electrodynamic Hamiltonian
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1998
for this system as

\displaystyle H=H_{\text{M}}+H_{\text{L}}+H_{\text{LM}},

(2)

where
H_{\text{L}}
is the transverse EM field Hamiltonian,
H_{\text{M}}
is the pure matter part of the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\text{M}}=h_{\text{M}}+V_{\text{M}},

(3)

including the electrostatic intermolecular interactions in
V_{\text{M}}
, and
h_{\text{M}}
is the noninteracting (ideal-gas) Hamiltonian for the multicomponent mixture

\displaystyle h_{\text{M}}=h_{\text{A}}+h_{\text{B}}+h_{\text{C}}+h_{\text{E}},

(4)

where
h_{\text{F}}
corresponds to the Hamiltonian describing a pure ensemble of noninteracting molecules of type
F\in\{\text{A, B, C, E}\}
.

We will assume a Born-Oppenheimer description of the molecular system where each molecule is in the electronic ground-state and only vibrational excitations contribute to the light-matter interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\text{LM}}
representing the photon-matter coupling.

The bare EM field Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{\text{L}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\lambda}^{k<k_{\text{M}}}\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\left(a_{\mathbf{k}\lambda}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+G_{\text{L}},

(5)

where
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
,
k=|\mathbf{k}|
,
\lambda=1,2
denotes the field polarization, and
k_{\text{M}}
is a wave vector cutoff (upper bound) for photon modes that will be treated in a nonperturbative fashion below. Only photon modes with
k<k_{\text{M}}
are assumed to form polaritons. In a practical application, the value of
k_{\text{M}}
would depend on the particular molecular system considered and the strength of the collective light-matter interactions. The contribution of the modes with
k>k_{\text{M}}
to
H_{\text{L}}
is given by the term
G_{\text{L}}
which we single out since it is not going to provide any contributions to strong light-matter induced changes to chemical equilibria discussed below.

Vibrational, rotational and translational motions of each molecule are assumed to be separable, and the dipole-active molecular modes undergo small oscillations around equilibrium. For the sake of simplicity, the molecular infrared polarization operator is assumed to be linear with respect to displacements from the equilibrium geometry of each (isolated) chemical species. This is a reasonable assumption under moderate temperatures, especially when strong light-matter coupling happens between IR cavity modes and molecular vibrations with high vibrational temperature
h\nu/k_{\text{B}}
, where
k_{\text{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant. These approximations lead to a quadratic light-matter Hamiltonian
h_{M}+H_{\text{L}}+V_{\text{M}}
with three types of eigenmodes: (i) polaritons with frequency
\omega_{\alpha}
, (ii) highly off-resonant photon modes, and (iii) molecular translations, rotations, dipole-inactive vibrations described by the Hamiltonian
H_{\text{D}}
which also includes the ground-state electronic energy contribution of each chemical species. The total light-matter Hamiltonian is given in the new basis by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{\text{Pol}}+H_{\text{D}}+G_{\text{L}}+V_{\text{M}},

(6)

where
H_{\text{Pol}}
represents the vibrational polariton Hamiltonian in the normal-mode representation

\displaystyle H_{\text{Pol}}=\sum_{\alpha}\hbar\omega_{\alpha}\left(c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(7)

and
c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
is the bosonic creation operator associated with polariton mode
\alpha
. Note that, in principle,
V_{\text{M}}
includes all direct intermolecular interactions involving molecular contributions from both
H_{\text{D}}
and
H_{\text{Pol}}
. As usual, we will assume the interactions modeled by
V_{\text{M}}
are weak enough that they can be ignored for the purpose of computing the thermodynamic equilibrium properties of the molecular system. Therefore, we will employ

\displaystyle h=H_{\text{Pol}}+H_{\text{D}}+G_{\text{L}}

(8)

to compute the partition function of the light-matter system.

The canonical partition function for the system at fixed volume
V
, temperature
T
, including
N_{\text{A}},N_{\text{B}},N_{\text{C}},N_{\text{E}}
molecules is given by

\displaystyle Q(N,V,T)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}~e^{-\beta h}

\displaystyle=Q_{\text{Pol}}(N,V,T)Q_{\text{D}}(N,V,T)Q_{G_{\text{L}}}(V,T),

(9)

where
N=(N_{\text{A}},N_{\text{B}},N_{\text{C}},N_{\text{E}})
and we assumed the electromagnetic field satisfies only fixed volume and temperature constraints (with zero chemical potential since their particle number is not conserved
McQuarrie 2000
;
Pathria 2016
). The partition function for the electronic-translational-rotational and non-dipole active vibrational degrees of freedom of the molecular system is given by:

\displaystyle Q_{\text{D}}(N,V,T)=\prod_{\text{F}}\frac{\left[q_{\text{el}}^{\text{F}}(T)q_{\text{trans}}^{\text{F}}(V,T)q_{\text{rot}}^{\text{F}}(T)\tilde{q}_{\text{vib}}^{\text{F}}(T)\right]^{N_{\text{F}}}}{N_{\text{F}}!},

(10)

where
\tilde{q}_{\text{vib}}^{\text{F}}(T)
is the partition function associated with the dipole-inactive normal modes of the F species (single-molecule). It follows that the Helmholtz free energy of the system is

\displaystyle A(N,V,T)

\displaystyle=-\text{k}_{\text{B}}T~\text{ln}[Q(N,V,T)],

\displaystyle=A_{\text{Pol}}(N,V,T)+A_{\text{D}}(N,V,T)+A_{\text{G}}(V,T),

(11)

where
A_{\text{Pol}}(N,V,T)=-\text{k}_{\text{B}}T~\text{ln}[Q_{\text{Pol}}(N,V,T)]
with
Q_{\text{Pol}}(N,V,T)=\text{Tr}\left[\text{exp}\left(-\beta H_{\text{Pol}}\right)\right]
and corresponding expressions apply for
A_{\text{D}}(N,V,T)
and
A_{\text{G}}(V,T)
.

The chemical equilibrium condition at fixed
V
and
T
is
McQuarrie 2000
;
Pathria 2016

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{F}}\nu_{\text{F}}\frac{\partial{A}}{\partial N_{\text{F}}}

\displaystyle=-\nu_{\text{A}}\mu_{\text{A}}-\nu_{\text{B}}\mu_{\text{B}}+\nu_{\text{C}}\mu_{\text{C}}+\nu_{\text{E}}\mu_{\text{E}}

\displaystyle=0,

(12)

where the chemical potential of each species is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{F}}(N,V,T)=\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}(N,V,T)+\mu_{\text{F,D}}(N_{F},V,T),

(13)

with
\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}(N,V,T)=\partial A_{\text{Pol}}/\partial N_{\text{F}}
and
\mu_{\text{F,D}}(N_{\text{F}},V,T)=\partial A_{\text{D}}/\partial N_{\text{F}}
. We can rewrite Eq.
13
in terms of a bare contribution and a polariton-induced change by adding and subtracting the contribution to the chemical potential from the bright vibrational part of
h_{\text{F}}
which we write as
\mu_{\text{F,vib, bright}}(T)
. Noting that
\mu_{\text{F,D}}(N_{\text{F}},V,T)+\mu_{\text{F,vib,bright}}(T)
is the bare chemical potential for a system of noninteracting
N_{\text{F}}
molecules of type
F
, we define the bare (for our purposes, the standard-state) chemical potential of species
F
by

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{F}}^{(0)}(N_{\text{F}},V,T)=\mu_{\text{F,D}}(N_{\text{F}},V,T)+\mu_{\text{F,vib,bright}}(T).

(14)

It follows that the chemical potential of species F under the influence of the light-matter interaction is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{F}}(N,V,T)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\text{F}}^{(0)}(N_{F},V,T)+\Delta\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}(N,V,T),

(15)

where we introduced

\displaystyle\Delta\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}=\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}-\mu_{\text{F,vib, bright}}.

(16)

The bare
F
chemical potential can be obtained directly from Eqs.
14
and
10
as

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{F}}^{(0)}(N_{F},V,T)

\displaystyle=-\text{k}_{\text{B}}T\frac{\partial}{\partial N_{\text{F}}}\text{ln}\left[\frac{q_{\text{F}}^{N_{\text{F}}}(V,T)}{N_{\text{F}}!}\right]

\displaystyle=-\text{k}_{\text{B}}T~\text{ln}\left[\frac{q_{\text{F}}(V,T)}{N_{\text{F}}}\right].

(17)

where
q_{\text{F}}(V,T)
is the single-molecule partition function for bare isolated species
F
. Inserting Eq.
17
into Eq.
15
and using the result in Eq.
12
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\text{k}_{\text{B}}T\sum_{\text{F}}\nu_{\text{F}}~\text{ln}\left[\frac{q_{\text{F}}}{N_{\text{F}}}\right]+\sum_{\text{F}}\nu_{\text{F}}\Delta\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}=0.

(18)

A simple rearrangement leads to our expression for the equilibrium reaction (Eq.
19
) quotient under the influence of nonperturbative light-matter interactions

\displaystyle\frac{N_{\text{E}}^{\nu_{\text{E}}}N_{\text{C}}^{\nu_{\text{C}}}}{N_{\text{A}}^{\nu_{\text{A}}}N_{\text{B}}^{\nu_{\text{B}}}}=\frac{q_{\text{E}}^{\nu_{\text{E}}}q_{\text{C}}^{\nu_{\text{C}}}}{q_{\text{A}}^{\nu_{\text{A}}}q_{\text{B}}^{\nu_{\text{B}}}}\text{exp}\left[-\beta\sum_{\text{F}}\nu_{\text{F}}\Delta\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}(N,V,T)\right].

(19)

By solving Eq.
19
for the number of molecules of each species, we obtain the polariton effect on the equilibrium composition of the reactive mixture.

The relation expressed by Eq.
19
indicates several important features of polariton effects on chemical equilibria. For instance, Eq.
19
highlights that a multimode description of the electromagnetic field is essential for modeling the nonperturbative effects of planar microcavities on chemical equilibria, for the polariton contributions to the chemical potential of each component depends on all cavity modes forming polaritons with the considered species. In particular, polaritons originating from all bands of a microcavity in resonance or sufficiently close to resonance with dipole-active molecular vibrations will contribute to Eq.
19
. Clearly, no a priori special role is played by modes corresponding to incidence angles near zero.

Equation
19
also demonstrates that in a polyatomic system with multiple bright vibrations, the chemical equilibrium shift induced by an IR microcavity depends on the density of EM modes at the various bright IR resonances of
both
reactants and products and their corresponding oscillator strengths.

In the next section, we will apply our equilibrium condition (Eq.
19
) to a reactive mixture where a single molecular species strongly interacts with a microcavity represented by a single mode. For this purpose, let us consider here the following example. Suppose only the reactant species
A
strongly interacts with the single-mode cavity EM field, and the mean equilibrium number of molecules of type A (obtained from solving Eq.
19
) is
N_{\text{A}}
. It follows the nonperturbative light-matter Hamiltonian contains
N_{\text{A}}+1
eigenmodes corresponding to the
N_{\text{A}}-1
purely molecular modes that have the same spectrum as the bright vibrations of
A
and the hybrid LP and UP. The contribution of the
N_{\text{A}}-1
reservoir normal modes to
\mu_{\text{F,Pol}}
cancels out the term
\mu_{\text{A,vib,bright}}(T)
in
\Delta\mu_{\text{A,Pol}}(N,V,T)
(Eq.
16
). As expected, the effect of nonperturbative light-matter interactions on the composition of the reactive mixture at equilibrium is entirely due to the LP and UP modes. We determine the polariton effect on the chemical equilibrium
F_{\text{Pol}}(V,T)
by computing the ratio of the equilibrium reaction quotient inside the microcavity
R(V,T)=N_{\text{E}}^{\nu_{\text{E}}}N_{\text{C}}^{\nu_{\text{C}}}/N_{\text{A}}^{\nu_{\text{A}}}N_{\text{B}}^{\nu_{\text{B}}}
to the standard-state reaction quotient (equilibrium constant)
K^{(0)}(T)=(q_{\text{E}}^{\nu_{\text{E}}}q_{\text{C}}^{\nu_{\text{C}}})/(q_{\text{A}}^{\nu_{\text{A}}}q_{\text{B}}^{\nu_{\text{B}}})
. It follows from Eq.
19
that

\displaystyle F_{\text{Pol}}(V,T)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(V,T)}{K^{(0)}(T)}

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\nu_{\text{A}}\left[\mu_{\text{A}}^{\text{LP}}(N_{A},V,T)+\mu_{\text{A}}^{\text{UP}}(N_{A},V,T)\right]},

(20)

where the LP and UP contributions to the chemical potential of the strongly coupled chemical species
A
is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{A}}^{\text{LP}}(N_{\text{A}},V,T)=\frac{\partial A_{\text{LP}}(N_{\text{A}},V,T)}{\partial N_{\text{A}}},

(21)

and
A_{\text{LP}}=-\text{k}_{\text{B}}T~\text{ln}~q_{\text{LP}}(N_{\text{A}},V,T)
, and identical definitions exist for UP.

III
Bimolecular nucleophilic substitution reaction in a single-mode cavity

III.1
Model

To illustrate the theory described above, we consider a lossless cavity interacting with a gas-phase reactive mixture where equilibrium is established via the S
{}_{\text{N}}
2 reaction

\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{Br}+\text{Cl}^{-}\rightleftharpoons\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{Cl}+\text{Br}^{-}.

(22)

This reaction has been thoroughly studied in the gas phase

;
Hase 1994

We construct its chemical equilibrium constant in free space from the gas-phase partition function of each chemical species assuming separability between the internal degrees of freedom and ideal gas conditions. We ignore the electronic contribution to the reaction free energy, for in this case, the equilibrium constant is close to 1 at room temperature and strong light-matter coupling becomes more likely to significantly affect the composition of the reactive mixture at equilibrium. Vibrational contributions to the partition function were constructed using the quantum harmonic oscillator model, whereas classical partition functions were employed in the treatment of rotational and translational degrees of freedom of each species. Vibrational frequencies and geometric parameters were extracted from data available in the Chemistry WebBook
Caldwell et al. 1984
.

In order to probe polariton effects on the chemical equilibrium associated with Eq.
22
, we suppose the system is embedded in an optical cavity with a single high-quality mode in resonance with a single vibrational mode of reactants or products. To examine the distinct effects of reactant and product strong light-matter coupling, we chose two strongly absorbing IR modes of reactants and products in the gas phase

When a single normal mode (of

III.2
Results and Discussion

We have investigated the effect of single-mode strong light-matter coupling on the equilibrium composition of the reactive molecular mixture described by Eq.
22
at various temperatures, system sizes, and light-matter interaction strengths assuming that strong coupling occurs between the cavity and a single set of normal vibrational modes of reactant or product.

The bare cavity frequency
\omega_{\text{C}}
is set to be on resonance with the strongly coupled vibrational mode. Renormalization (Eq.
24
) of the cavity frequency in the presence of the molecular system leads to a nonzero detuning that is insignificant relative to the light-matter interaction strength under the conditions analyzed in this work.

The temperature dependence of the ratio between the reaction quotient of the selected S
{}_{\text{N}}
2 reaction inside and outside a microcavity is provided in Fig.
2
. This figure shows four notable features: a. polariton effects are strongest at low temperatures and vanish at the high-temperature limit, b. the equilibrium is shifted towards the products when reactants are strongly coupled to light and vice-versa, c. the observed effects are extremely small considering the large single-molecule light-matter coupling strength employed (for the purposes of illustrating our theory), and d. polaritons formed between molecular modes with lower frequency have a stronger impact on the chemical equilibrium constant. Below, we discuss each of these trends.

Low- and high-temperature behavior.
Figure
2

To understand this, note that at low temperatures, the polaritons and bare modes are essentially in their ground-state, and therefore any polariton-induced change in free energy responsible for modifying chemical equilibrium is generated by the difference between polariton and bare molecule zero-point energies. IV
Conclusions

We provided a general theory of chemical equilibrium under nonperturbative light-matter interactions and applied it to an S
{}_{\text{N}}
2 reaction in a single-mode cavity. We found that polaritons can shift chemical equilibrium constants towards either direction of a reaction depending on the species (reactant or product) strongly coupled to the EM field, light-matter interaction effects are strongest at lower temperatures, and the change induced by VSC on the internal energy of the light-matter system provide the dominant contribution to the VSC effect on chemical equilibria. We also showed that strong light-matter coupling is more effective at shifting chemical equilibria when polaritons are formed between IR cavity modes and molecular vibrations with larger oscillator strength and lower frequency.

These trends were obtained in an idealized scenario where strong light-matter coupling occurs between a single EM mode of an IR resonator and the normal-modes of a particular component of the reactive mixture (reactant or product ensemble) but are based on fundamental features of our theory that are expected to hold more generally. Future work, based on Eq.
19
including a quantitative analysis of equilibrium effects in realistic systems with simultaneous strong coupling of multiple IR modes of reactants and products with multimode Fabry-Perot cavities will allow direct comparison with experiments
Pang et al. 2020
.
