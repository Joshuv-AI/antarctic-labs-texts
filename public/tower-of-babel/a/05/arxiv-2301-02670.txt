Brane wrapping, AKSZ sigma models, and QP manifolds
Alex S. Arvanitakis, and David Tennyson

Abstract
We introduce a technique to realise brane wrapping and double dimensional reduction in the context of AKSZ topological sigma models and also in their target spaces, which are symplectic L n L_{n} -algebroids (i.e. QP-manifolds). Our procedure involves a novel coisotropic reduction combined with an AKSZ transgression that realises degree-shifting; the reduced QP-manifold depends on topological data of the ‘wrapped’ cycle. We check our procedure against the known rules for fluxes under wrapping in the context of M-theory/type IIA duality, and we also find a new relation between Courant algebroids and Poisson manifolds.

Wrapping QP manifolds

We will describe a process of creating new QP manifolds from old, which effectively generalises the notion of dimensional reduction, that we describe as ‘wrapping’ QP manifolds. The nomenclature arises due to the consistency of this process with the AKSZ construction
Alexandrov:1995kv

We will describe the relation to AKSZ sigma models in a later section and will describe the wrapping procedure here.

The coisotrope

We need to perform a coisotropic reduction to obtain a finite dimensional NQP manifold. This is a generalisation of symplectic reduction for Poisson manifolds which requires a coisotropic ideal
\mathcal{I}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}})

[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}]\subseteq\mathcal{I}

(2.11)

The description of the quotient manifold is given in two equivalent ways. In one description, we take the submanifold

\mathcal{I}

\mathcal{I}

\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{C}/[\mathcal{I},\cdot]\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad C^{\infty}(\mathcal{W})=N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}

To remove these, we include harmonic generators of the maps
z^{A}_{k}
for maps such that
\deg z^{A}-k\leq 0
. The exception to this is the maps
x_{0}
for which we do not include the harmonic (i.e. constant map) representatives. We therefore have

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\begin{cases}P_{\mathrm{co}}z^{A}_{k},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}z^{A}_{k^{\prime}}\,|\,\forall k^{\prime}\geq\deg z^{A}\quad&\text{if}\quad\deg z^{A}>0\\
P_{\mathrm{co}}z^{A}_{k}\,,P_{\mathcal{H}}z^{A}_{k^{\prime}}\,|\,\forall k^{\prime}>0\quad&\text{if}\quad\deg z^{A}=0\end{cases}}}\right>

(2.16)

To see that this is coisotropic, we use (
2.6
) and the surrounding discussion to note that the co-exact generators are dual to exact generators. Hence, these terms are coisotropic with respect to all of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
. The harmonic generators could be dual to some other harmonic generator in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
. However, since we only include harmonic
z^{A}_{k}
for
0\geq\deg z^{A}-k
, the dual coordinate
z^{B}_{k^{\prime}}
on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
has

\begin{split}\deg z^{B}-k^{\prime}&=n-\deg z^{A}-(d-k)\\
&=(n-d)-(\deg z^{A}-k)\\
&>0\end{split}

(2.17)

so is not included in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
. The total ideal
\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}}}\right>
is coisotropic, as required.

Metric and coordinate independence

The construction of the ideal
\mathcal{I}
appears to rely on a choice of metric on
X
but we claim that the resulting reduced manifold depends on only the topological data of
X
. This can be best seen from the vanishing locus
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. This is given by maps which are either
\mathrm{d}
-closed (for
\deg z^{A}=0
and some values of
k,k^{\prime}
) or ones which are also
\mathrm{d}
-exact (in all other cases), as specified in (
2.16
). The metric only appears in the specification of the vanishing ideal that represents
\mathcal{C}
but
\mathcal{C}
does not in itself depend on metric data. (This apparent metric dependence thus may perhaps be seen as due to ‘gauge-fixing’.)

Our construction also appears to depend on a choice of coordinates on
\mathcal{N}
. To see that this is well defined, we will show that the submanifold
\mathcal{C}
is invariant under a change of coordinates
\tilde{z}^{A}=\tilde{z}^{A}(z)
. Using formula (
2.4
), we can write the
k
-form component of the transgressed
\tilde{\bm{z}}
as

\tilde{z}^{A}_{k}\sim\sum_{j}C^{A}{}_{A_{1}...A_{j}}(z_{0})z^{A_{1}}_{k_{1}}...z^{A_{j}}_{k_{j}}

(2.18)

such that
k_{1}+...+k_{j}=k
and
\deg z^{A_{1}}+...+\deg z^{A_{j}}\leq\deg\tilde{z}^{A}_{k}
; here we emphasise that the last inequality holds true because
\mathcal{M}
— and thus
\mathcal{N}
—
was assumed to be an N-manifold
(its structure sheaf is non-negatively graded). Restricting to
\mathcal{C}
, the coordinates
z^{A_{i}}_{k_{i}}
are all closed under
\mathrm{d}
and hence so is
\tilde{z}^{A}_{k}
. Further, if
\deg\tilde{z}^{A}-k\leq 0
, then at least one of
\deg z^{A_{i}}-k_{i}\leq 0
. This means that
z^{A_{i}}_{k_{i}}
is exact. A product of closed and exact forms is exact and hence so is
\tilde{z}^{A}_{k}
as required.

Closure under

The final condition to check is that the ideal
\mathcal{I}
is closed under the Q-structure
D=[\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}},\cdot]
on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. We have already checked that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is
D
-closed and so we need only check how

acts on the generating coordinates of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
. We can use formula (
2.6
) with
P_{\mathrm{co}}\epsilon=0
. This choice of epsilon selects out the harmonic and co-exact generators

, respectively. We have

\begin{split}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}},\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{z}^{A}\epsilon}}\right]&=(-1)^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\Theta}_{\mathcal{M}},\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{z}^{A}\epsilon}}\right]+(-1)^{d+n+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\imath_{\mathrm{d}}\bm{\vartheta}_{\mathcal{M}},\bm{z}^{A}\epsilon}}\right]\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},z^{A})_{\mathcal{M}}}\,\epsilon+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}^{A}\epsilon\end{split}

(2.19)

The second term vanishes when
\epsilon
is closed. We therefore need to only consider the first term. We can see whether this term is contained within
\mathcal{I}
by transgressing the function
(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},z^{A})_{\mathcal{M}}
to the mapping space and evaluating it over
\mathcal{C}
. If the integral vanishes when integrated against all closed

then the ideal is closed under

. Using the form of the Hamiltonian function we find

(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},z^{A})_{\mathcal{M}}\propto(\omega_{\mathcal{M}})^{AB}\sum_{k}\frac{\partial\theta_{k}(z,y)}{\partial z^{B}}\xi^{k}

(2.20)

Transgressing this to the mapping space and restricting to
\mathcal{C}
, we replace
y\to\mathfrak{w}
,
\xi\to\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{w}
,
z\to\bm{z}
for
\bm{z}
closed (or exact). Integrating this against
\epsilon
we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}},\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{z}^{A}\epsilon}}\right]}}\right|_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\omega_{\mathcal{M}})^{AB}\mathfrak{w}^{*}\sum_{k}\frac{\partial\theta_{k}}{\partial z^{B}}(\bm{z})}}\right)\epsilon

(2.21)

We require this to vanish for the above
\epsilon
. When
\epsilon
is exact, this indeed vanishes if we impose
\mathrm{d}_{Y}\theta_{k}=0
. If
\epsilon
is harmonic, however, we find constraints on the coefficients

that we address case-by-case, in general.

The reduction, metric independence, and homotopy invariance

Given the coisotropic reduction
\mathcal{I}
, we consider the reduction given in (
2.12
). We will consider the structure sheaf construction of the reduced manifold. The normaliser
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{I})
of the ideal is generated by

N(\mathcal{I})\sim\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{P_{\mathcal{H}}x_{0},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}z^{A}_{k},\,\mathcal{I}\,|\,0<\deg z^{A}-k\leq n-d}}\right\}

(2.22)

We can expand the
P_{\mathcal{H}}z^{A}_{k}=z^{A,a}_{k}e_{a}
in some basis
\{e_{a}\}
of
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, so the
z^{A,a}_{k}
are constant parameters of degree
\deg z^{A}-k
. In the case that the respective cohomology group is 1 dimensional (e.g. for
\mathcal{H}^{0},\mathcal{H}^{d}
) we will omit the
a
index and simply identify e.g.
z^{A}_{d}=z^{A}_{d}\mathrm{vol}_{X}
. We see that the structure sheaf
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{W})=N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}
is given therefore generated by

C^{\infty}(\mathcal{W})=N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}\sim\{x_{0},\,z^{A,a}_{k}\,|\,0<\deg z^{A}-k\leq n-d\}

(2.23)

That is, the structure sheaf is given by all smooth functions in the
z^{A,a}_{k}
(and
x_{0}
).

The Hamiltonian function on
\mathcal{W}
is given by the projection
\Pi:N(\mathcal{I})\rightarrow N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}
of
\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}}
. We will confirm in the examples that the final result is given by

\begin{split}\Theta_{\mathcal{W}}&=\Pi(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\mathfrak{w}^{*}\theta_{k}(z)\end{split}

(2.24)

where the
z
are now the harmonic representatives of the cohomology groups on
X
. Expanding the harmonic
z^{A}_{k}
in terms of the constant coordinates
z^{A,a}_{k}
, we can perform the integral over
X
with the convention that the volume form is on the right of the integrand, so we pull out constants from the left. Once this is done the final result will no longer be an integral but will be a function in the
z^{A,a}_{k}
which will involve, in general, a sum over cohomology groups, which will be discrete in all cases (we only consider wrapping over compact cycles).

Formula (
2.24
) seems to depend on some metric to choose the harmonic representatives for the
z
. However, under a change of metric, the harmonic representatives change by a
\mathrm{d}
-exact term, and since we have assumed that the forms
\theta_{k}
are closed, this shift will not change the integral. Furthermore, since the forms
\theta_{k}
are closed, the evaluation of the integral only depends on the homotopy class of
\mathfrak{w}:X\to Y
. Therefore the construction is metric-independent and homotopy invariant.

Now that we have defined the reduction in complete generality, we will see many examples of how this works in practice. There are three interesting cases to consider.

1.

\dim X=0
– The process effectively shrinks
Y
to a point.

2.

\dim Y=0
– We produce a QP manifold with the same underlying commutative manifold but with a different degree.

3.

X=Y
– We produce a QP manifold which corresponds to a brane wrapping the internal manifold.

3
Example –
\dim X=0

We consider first a simple example to show that in the simple case that
\dim X=0
, our procedure effectively reduces to dimensional reduction on
Y
. Consider the ingredients

\mathcal{M}=T^{*}[n]T[1](N\times Y)\qquad X=\mathrm{pt}

(3.1)

Taking
\mathcal{N}=T^{*}[n]T[1]N
,
\mathcal{Y}=T^{*}[n]T[1]Y
and
\mathcal{X}=T[1]X=\mathrm{pt}
, we introduce the Darboux coordinates

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{N}&&\mathcal{Y}\\
\begin{array}[]{r|cccc}\text{coord}&x^{\mu}&\psi^{\mu}&\chi_{\mu}&p_{\mu}\\
\hline\cr\text{deg}&0&1&n-1&n\end{array}&&\begin{array}[]{r|cccc}\text{coord}&y^{m}&\xi^{m}&\phi_{m}&q_{m}\\
\hline\cr\text{deg}&0&1&n-1&n\end{array}\end{array}

(3.2)

We will take the QP structure to be given by the symplectic form and Hamiltonian function

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}y-\mathrm{d}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\chi-\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle\Theta_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=-\psi p-\xi q+\tfrac{1}{n!}F_{n}\psi^{n}+\tfrac{1}{(n-1)!}F_{n-1}\psi^{n-1}\xi+...+\tfrac{1}{d!(n-d)!}F_{n-d}\psi^{n-d}\xi^{d}

We have suppressed all indices but they should be read as being contracted in the natural way. The coefficients

can be thought of as elements of
\Omega^{k}(N)\times\Omega^{n-k}(Y)
. These should be closed under the differential
\mathrm{d}_{Y}
on

. So for example,

N

is the connection, however it won’t change our final result so we omit it for simplicity.

The first step in the reduction process is to transgress the QP structure to
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. But since
\mathcal{X}
is 0 dimensional, we have
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}\simeq\mathcal{M}
. Next, we need to choose a wrapping map
\mathfrak{w}:X=\text{pt}\to Y
, or equivalently, a (degree preserving) embedding

. This is equivalent to choosing some point
\hat{y}\in Y
and defining the embedding
N\to(N,\hat{y})\subset M
. This is described by the ideal

\mathcal{I}_{0}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<y^{m}-\hat{y}^{m}}}\right>

(3.5)

We then want to form the closure of this ideal with respect to differential
Q
on
\mathcal{M}
. We get

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathcal{I}_{0},Q\mathcal{I}_{0}}}\right>=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<y^{m}-\hat{y}^{m},\,\xi^{m}}}\right>\end{split}

(3.6)

It is easy to check from (
3.3
) that this is indeed coisotropic with respect to the Poisson bracket on
\mathcal{M}
. In principal, we also need to restrict the maps into
\mathcal{N}
to those that are closed/exact with respect to
\mathrm{d}
on
X
. However, since
\dim X=0
, this is a trivial constraint and so we just have
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.

To perform the coisotropic reduction, we need to go to first find the normaliser
N(\mathcal{I})
of
\mathcal{I}
, which can easily be verified to be generated by the coordinates

N(\mathcal{I})\sim\{x^{\mu},\psi^{\mu},\chi_{\mu},p_{\mu},y^{m}-\hat{y}^{m},\xi^{m}\}

(3.7)

The structure sheaf of the new QP manifold
\mathcal{W}
is then defined to be the quotient of this by the ideal
\mathcal{I}
. That is
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{W})=N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}
, which is generated by

N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}\sim\{x^{\mu},\psi^{\mu},\chi_{\mu},p_{\mu}\}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{W}=T^{*}[n]T[1]N

(3.8)

Note that, by construction
\Theta_{\mathcal{M}}\in N(\mathcal{I})
and so we can find the new Hamiltonian function through the natural projection
\Pi:N(\mathcal{I})\rightarrow N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}
, which gives

\Theta_{\mathcal{W}}=\Pi(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}})=-\psi p+\tfrac{1}{n!}F_{n}(x,\hat{y})\psi^{n}

(3.9)

and the final symplectic form is

\omega_{\mathcal{W}}=\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}x-\mathrm{d}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\chi

(3.10)

We see that this procedure has produced a new QP manifold with the same degree but with underlying commutative manifold
N
. We see that we have effectively collapsed
Y
to the point
\hat{y}
. In the case where

is a Lie group, we find the same result as in symplectic reduction modulo
T[1]Y

Arvanitakis:2021lwo
. If we were to choose a different wrapping map
\mathfrak{w}^{\prime}:X\mapsto\hat{y}^{\prime}
that is homotopic to

, then we end up with the same graded manifold where the Q-structure is evaluated for
F_{n}(x,\hat{y}^{\prime})
. However, the condition that the
F_{n}
is closed on

says that it is constant, and hence the Q-structures are the same. This demonstrates the homotopy invariance of our construction.

A quick calculation shows that
(\Theta_{\mathcal{W}},\Theta_{\mathcal{W}})=0
if and only if
\pi
is Poisson.

Alternatively, we can consider the Lie algebroid structure on
T^{*}M
whose anchor map is given by the bivector
\pi:T^{*}M\rightarrow TM
and whose bracket is given by

[\alpha,\beta]=\mathcal{L}_{\pi(\alpha)}\beta-\imath_{\pi(\beta)}\mathrm{d}\alpha

(4.19)

This Lie algebroid can be lifted to a Dirac structure
L=(1+\pi)T^{*}M
within the Courant algebroid
TM\oplus T^{*}M
. Such a structure can be described by a QP manifold via

\mathcal{M}=T^{*}[2]T[1]M\qquad\begin{array}[]{r|cccc}\text{coord}&x&\psi&\chi&p\\
\hline\cr\text{deg}&0&1&1&2\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega_{\mathcal{M}}&=&\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}x-\mathrm{d}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\chi\\
\Theta_{\mathcal{M}}&=&-\pi p\chi+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial\pi\psi\chi^{2}\end{array}

(4.20)

Once again
(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},\Theta_{\mathcal{M}})=0
if and only if
\pi
is Poisson. We have suppressed indices for convenience. We want to see how, if at all, these constructions are related.

Let us perform a circle reduction of
\mathcal{M}
as above. We transgress the structure to
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
where
\mathcal{X}=T[1]S^{1}
. As before, we define
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
by first including all co-exact generators

\mathcal{I}\supset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<P_{\mathrm{co}}x_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\psi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\chi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}p_{0}}}\right>

(4.21)

Then we include harmonic representatives to remove coordinates of 0 or negative degree. We will slightly relax the construction set out in section
2
by allowing some new coordinates of degree 0.
7
7

7

The construction, as set out previously, would still work in this case but we would end up with a trivial Q-structure. To result in a QP manifold with non-trivial Q-structure, we will need to perform an intermediate step before removing the additional degree 0 coordinates.
We will define

\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<P_{\mathrm{co}}x_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\psi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\chi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}p_{0},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}x_{1},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}\chi_{1}}}\right>

(4.22)

As before, this ideal is coisotropic.

We will check the closure of this ideal with respect to the Q-structure
D
on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. The transgressed Hamiltonian function is

\begin{split}\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}}&=-\int_{T[1]S^{1}}\bm{\Theta}_{\mathcal{M}}+\int_{T[1]S^{1}}\imath_{\mathrm{d}}\bm{\vartheta}_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-\int_{T[1]S^{1}}-\pi(\bm{x})\bm{p\chi}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial\pi(\bm{x})\bm{\psi\chi}^{2}+\int_{T[1]S^{1}}\bm{p}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\psi}\mathrm{d}\bm{\chi}+\bm{\chi}\mathrm{d}\bm{\psi})\end{split}

(4.23)

We then act with this on
\int\mathbf{Z}^{A}\epsilon
for some test function
\epsilon
that must be harmonic, or exact. As in (
4.12
), the only non-trivial constraint to check is for the harmonic representatives. We need to check if the following vanishes

\int_{T[1]S^{1}}\bm{(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},Z^{A})_{\mathcal{M}}}\,\epsilon

(4.24)

whenever the function
(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},Z^{A})
is transgressed and evaluated on
\mathcal{C}
, and if
\epsilon
is harmonic. Since the only harmonic generators of
\mathcal{I}
are
x_{1},\chi_{1}
, we calculate

(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},x)=\pi(x)\chi\,,\qquad\qquad(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},\chi_{\mu})=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial\pi(x)\chi^{2}

(4.25)

We transgress these functions to the mapping space and evaluate on the vanishing locus
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{I}
. Noting that these are functions of
x,\chi
alone, evaluating them on
\mathcal{C}
means that the zero-form component must be constant functions on
X
, while the 1-form component must be an exact form. Integrating these against a constant function
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
selects the 1-form component, which is exact and hence the integral vanishes as required.

The next step is to perform the coisotropic reduction with respect to this ideal. The normaliser is generated by all coordinates not dual to those in
\mathcal{I}
.

N(\mathcal{I})\sim\{P_{\mathcal{H}}x_{0},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}\chi_{0},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}\psi_{1},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}p_{1},\,\mathcal{I}\}

(4.26)

and so we obtain the structure sheaf
C^{\infty}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})=N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}
which is generated by

N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}\sim\{x_{0},\,\psi_{1},\,\chi_{0},\,p_{1}\}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=T^{*}[1]TM

(4.27)

This time, we restrict to harmonic functions for
x,\chi
so they retain their degree, while we take harmonic 1-forms for
\psi,p
and hence their degree is shifted down by 1. The resulting Hamiltonian function is
\Pi(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}})
and the symplectic form is derived from the Poisson brackets (
2.6
) with harmonic representatives for
\epsilon,\eta
.

\displaystyle\Theta_{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}

\displaystyle=-\pi p_{1}\chi_{0}-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial\pi\psi_{1}\chi_{0}^{2}

(4.28)

\displaystyle\omega_{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{d}p_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{0}+\mathrm{d}\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}\chi_{0}

(4.29)

We performed the change of coordinates
p_{1}\rightarrow-p_{1}
,
\chi_{0}\rightarrow-\chi_{0}
to remove minus signs.

We have arrived at a ‘halfway house’ QP manifold
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
. Interestingly, this is the cotangent lift of the complete lift of the Poisson structure
\pi
on
M
to the tangent bundle
(TM,\pi^{c})

mitric2003poisson
. That is, given any Poisson structure
(M,\pi)
we define a Poisson structure
(TM,\pi^{c})
by

\pi^{c}=\pi^{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}^{\mu}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi_{1}^{\nu}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\psi_{1}^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}\pi^{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi_{1}^{\mu}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi_{1}^{\nu}}

(4.30)

where
x_{0}
are coordinates on
M
and
\psi_{1}
are coordinates along the vector bundle fibres. We can reduce the QP manifold
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
further by following
mitric2003poisson
. Given any (torsionless) connection on
M
, we can define a global vector field on
TM
given by the geodesic spray

s=\psi_{1}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}^{\mu}}-\psi^{\mu}_{1}\psi_{1}^{\nu}\Gamma_{\mu\nu}^{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi_{1}^{\rho}}\,.

(4.31)

This has a cotangent lift to
T^{*}[1]TM
whose hamiltonian is

S=\psi_{1}p_{1}-\Gamma\psi_{1}^{2}\chi_{0}

(4.32)

From this, we define a new Hamiltonian function

\begin{split}\Theta^{\prime}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}&=-\tfrac{1}{2}(S,\Theta_{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}})=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi p_{1}^{2}+\psi_{1}f(x_{0},\psi_{1},\chi_{0},p_{1})\end{split}

(4.33)

where
f
is some function of the coordinates whose precise form is not important. All we will need is that besides the first term,
\tfrac{1}{2}\pi p_{1}^{2}
, each term is at least linear in the coordinate
\psi_{1}
.

Consider the ideal generated by the single coordinate
\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\psi_{1}}}\right>
. This ideal is automatically closed under the Q-structure since

\begin{split}(\Theta^{\prime}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}},\psi_{1})&=(\tfrac{1}{2}\pi p_{1}^{2}+\psi_{1}f(x_{0},\psi_{1},\chi_{0},p_{1}),\psi_{1})\\
&=(\psi_{1}f(x_{0},\psi_{1},\chi_{0},p_{1}),\psi_{1})\\
&\propto\psi_{1}(f,\psi_{1})\\
&\in\mathcal{I}\end{split}

(4.34)

Performing the coisotropic reduction with respect to this ideal we obtain the structure sheaf

N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}\sim\{x_{0},p_{1}\}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{W}=T^{*}[1]M

(4.35)

That is, we reproduce the non-commutative manifold
\mathcal{W}
. Further, the symplectic form and Hamiltonian function are easily shown to be the following:

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}p_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}\,,

(4.36)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi p_{1}^{2}\,,

(4.37)

We see then that we precisely reproduce the QP manifold associated to the cotangent lift of the Poisson bivector that we described at the beginning of this section. This construction provides new relations between the Courant sigma model and the Poisson sigma model that is different from the WZW-Poisson model
Klimcik:2001vg
. There they view the Poisson model arising at the boundary of a topological WZW-like theory. Instead, our construction is closer to dimensional reduction and can be viewed as the geometric counterpart of the Courant sigma model reduction found in
Cabrera2022DimensionalRO
.

5
Examples –
X=Y

We will now generalise the previous two sections to allow for cases where the source manifold
X
wraps the target space fibre
Y
. In particular, we will be interested in the case where
X=Y
. We will see that the reduction procedure requires us to choose some self-wrapping map
\mathfrak{w}:X\rightarrow X
. The examples we choose are physically motivated and fill our understanding of how brane dualities in M-theory/IIA arise in the QP setting. In particular, when
X=S^{1}
, we will see that our procedure produces the known relations from M-theory/type IIA duality. We will also see that this procedure reproduces other interesting relations between the M5 brane and the heterotic string
Cherkis:1997bx
;
Park:2009me
.

5.1
M2 on
S^{1}

Our first example will be wrapping the M2 brane on an

. This will be very similar to the
n
-brane example in section

, except in this case the wrapping will allow for more interesting Hamiltonian functions to be produced.

We start with the QP manifold
\mathcal{M}
associated to the M2 brane and a source manifold
X
:

\mathcal{M}=T^{*}[3]T[1](N\times S^{1})\qquad X=S^{1}

(5.1)

Writing
\mathcal{N}=T^{*}[3]T[1]N
,
\mathcal{Y}=T^{*}[3]T[1]S^{1}
, and
\mathcal{X}=T[1]S^{1}
, we will introduce the coordinates

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{N}&&\mathcal{Y}\\
\begin{array}[]{r|cccc}\text{coord}&x^{\mu}&\psi^{\mu}&\chi_{\mu}&p_{\mu}\\
\hline\cr\text{deg}&0&1&2&3\end{array}&&\begin{array}[]{r|cccc}\text{coord}&y&\xi&\phi&q\\
\hline\cr\text{deg}&0&1&2&3\end{array}\end{array}

(5.2)

and use coordinates
(\sigma,\mathrm{d}\sigma)
on
\mathcal{X}
. The Hamiltonian function and symplectic form are

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}y-\mathrm{d}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\chi-\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\phi

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=-\psi p-\xi q+\tfrac{1}{4!}F_{4}\psi^{4}+\tfrac{1}{3!}H_{3}\psi^{3}\xi

(5.4)

We require
F_{4}
to be
\mathrm{d}_{S^{1}}
-closed; we will also use the fact that
H_{3}\xi
is
\mathrm{d}_{S^{1}}
-closed (which is automatic).

We transgress this to the mapping space
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
and choose an ideal whose vanishing locus describes, in degree 0, some embedding
\imath:N\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. As explained in section
2
, this depends on the choice of some wrapping map
\mathfrak{w}:S^{1}\rightarrow S^{1}
. In fact, as stated, the final result only depends on the homotopy class of
w
and hence we can take, for some

,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathfrak{w}:S^{1}&\longrightarrow&S^{1}\\
\sigma&\longmapsto&w\sigma\end{array}

To restrict to this wrapping sector of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
, we define a coisotropic ideal
\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}}}\right>
with

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\bm{y}-w\sigma,\bm{\xi}+w\,\mathrm{d}\sigma}}\right>

(5.6)

As explained in section
2
, this is coisotropic and closed under the Q-structure
D=[\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}},\cdot]
. The ideal
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
restricts all maps into
\mathcal{N}
to closed maps. That is, we take

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}\supset\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<P_{\mathrm{co}}x_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\psi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\chi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}p_{0}}}\right>

(5.7)

For any coordinate
z^{A}_{k}
with
\deg z^{A}-k\leq 0
, we need to further restrict to exact maps by including the harmonic representative in the ideal (except for
x_{0}
). Hence, we have

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<P_{\mathrm{co}}x_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\psi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}\chi_{0},\,P_{\mathrm{co}}p_{0},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}x_{1},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}\psi_{1}}}\right>

(5.8)

The ideal
\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}}}\right>
is clearly coisotropic.

We need to check that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
is closed under

. The transgressed Hamiltonian is

\begin{split}\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}}&=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\Theta}_{\mathcal{M}}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\imath_{\mathrm{d}}\bm{\vartheta}_{\mathcal{M}}\\
&=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}-\bm{\psi p}-\bm{\xi q}+\tfrac{1}{4!}F_{4}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\bm{\psi}^{4}+\tfrac{1}{3!}H_{3}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\bm{\psi}^{3}\bm{\xi}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{p}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\bm{q}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}-\tfrac{1}{3}(\bm{\psi}\mathrm{d}\bm{\chi}+2\bm{\chi}\mathrm{d}\bm{\psi}+\bm{\xi}\mathrm{d}\bm{\phi}+2\bm{\phi}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi})\end{split}

(5.9)

As in the previous cases, the only non-trivial constraint comes from the Poisson bracket between the first term and the harmonic generators of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
. We calculate

(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},x)=\psi\,,\qquad\qquad(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},\psi)=0

(5.10)

and hence we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}},\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\psi}\epsilon}}\right]

\displaystyle\propto\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},\psi)_{\mathcal{M}}}\,\epsilon=0

(5.11)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}},\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{x}\epsilon}}\right]

\displaystyle\propto\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}},x)_{\mathcal{M}}}\,\epsilon=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\psi}\epsilon

(5.12)

Evaluating this on
\mathcal{C}
, we take
\psi_{1}
to be exact and so the integral vanishes when integrated over a constant
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}

vanish when evaluated on

To perform the coisotropic reduction we find the normaliser is generated by

N(\mathcal{I})\sim\{P_{\mathcal{H}}x_{0},P_{\mathcal{H}}\psi_{0},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}\chi_{1},\,P_{\mathcal{H}}p_{1},\,\mathcal{I}\}

(5.13)

and hence the structure sheaf is generated by by

C^{\infty}(\mathcal{W})=N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}\sim\{x_{0},\,\psi_{0},\,\chi_{1},\,p_{1}\}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{W}=T^{*}[2]T[1]N

(5.14)

where the coordinates represent harmonic maps. The symplectic form can be derived from the Poisson brackets on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}

, and we find

\omega_{\mathcal{W}}=-\mathrm{d}p_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}-\mathrm{d}\psi_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\chi_{1}

(5.15)

The Hamiltonian function is given by

\begin{split}\Theta_{\mathcal{W}}&=\Pi(\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}})=\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\Theta}_{\mathcal{M}}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\imath_{\mathrm{d}}\bm{\vartheta}_{\mathcal{M}}}}\right)\end{split}

(5.16)

The second term vanishes when evaluated on harmonic maps where
\mathrm{d}
annihilates the maps, except for the term
\bm{q}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}
. We also get a piece

from the first term. We find

\begin{split}\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\xi q}-\bm{q}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}}}\right)&=\int_{\mathcal{X}}-\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\,\bm{q}-\bm{q}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}=0\end{split}

(5.17)

where we pick up a minus sign from commuting
\mathrm{d}\bm{y}
(degree 1) through
\bm{q}
(degree 3). This verifies the statement made in section
2
about this cancellation. We then have

\begin{split}\Theta_{\mathcal{W}}&=\Pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{T[1]S^{1}}\bm{\psi p}-\tfrac{1}{4!}F_{4}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\bm{\psi}^{4}-\tfrac{1}{3!}H_{3}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\bm{\psi}^{3}\bm{\xi}}}\right)\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{0}p_{1}+-\tfrac{1}{4!}F_{4}(x_{0},w\sigma)\psi_{0}^{4}+\tfrac{1}{3!}H_{3}(x_{0},w\sigma)\psi_{0}^{3}w\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi_{0}p_{1}+\tfrac{w}{3!}H_{3}(x_{0},w\sigma)\psi_{0}^{3}}}\right)\mathrm{d}\sigma\\
&=\psi_{0}p_{1}+\tfrac{w}{3!}\tilde{H}_{3}\psi_{0}^{3}\end{split}

(5.18)

where
\tilde{H}_{3}
is the average of
H_{3}
over the fibre.

Under the change of coordinates
p_{1}\to-p_{1}
, we see that we recover the QP manifold associated to the F1 string with
w
units of
\tilde{H}_{3}
flux, as we would expect from our intuition of M-theory/IIA duality. Note that in the case that
w=0
, the physical interpretation seems to break down - we find a string which doesn’t couple to the NS 3-form. However, as is noted in

From the IIA perspective, the resulting string is tensionless and thus the M2 brane must somehow be tensionless. We should discard that case on account of such objects appear not to exist on physical grounds; nevertheless, the QP procedure is well defined.

The next example will be to wrap the M5 brane over a 4-manifold

Writing

6
AKSZ sigma models and brane wrapping

In previous sections we obtained an NQP manifold
\mathcal{W}
from a coisotropic reduction of the mapping space
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to a coisotropic submanifold
\mathcal{C}
that is invariant with respect to the Q-structure
D=Q_{\mathcal{M}}+\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. (In the expression for
D
we have the lifts of vector fields on the target and source to the mapping space.) We will now point out that these data give rise — essentially trivially! — to a reduction of AKSZ sigma models from an AKSZ model with target
\mathcal{M}
to an AKSZ model with target
\mathcal{W}
.

We start with the BV manifold of an AKSZ sigma model with target
\mathcal{M}
where the source takes the form
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}
. The N-manifold
\mathcal{S}
is taken to be
T[1]S
where the (bosonic) manifold
S
has dimension
\dim S=n+1-\dim X
(
n
being the degree of the target P-structure). Then the BV master action is the hamiltonian corresponding to the Q-structure on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
given by

Q_{\mathrm{BV}}\equiv Q_{\mathcal{M}}+\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}

(6.1)

where again
Q_{\mathcal{M}}
denotes the lift to
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
of the target space
\mathcal{M}
Q-structure of the same name, and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
is the lift of the source
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}
de Rham differential again to
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
. Since the source is a product we can write
\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}=\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{X}}+\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

The key point that leads to reduction is that we can write

\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}=(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}})^{\mathcal{S}}

(6.2)

which is known as the product-exponential adjunction. Explicitly, this corresponds to interpreting a function
f\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
, which is a function
f(x,s)
of two arguments, as a function
s\to f(\bullet,s)
where
f(\bullet,s)
is a function of
x\in\mathcal{X}
for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
10
10

10

The definition of mapping spaces for graded manifolds is such that this property is true; see e.g.
Roytenberg:2006qz
.
Since
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
is a QP-manifold and
\mathcal{S}
is an NQ-manifold with an integral measure we can
consider the BV structure on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
as arising from an AKSZ construction with source
\mathcal{S}
and target
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. If
\mathcal{C}
is coisotropic in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
, then the mapping space
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S}}
will be a coisotropic submanifold in
(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}})^{\mathcal{S}}\cong\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
.

The reduced AKSZ sigma model will be given by the coisotropic reduction of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
with respect to
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S}}
. We need to confirm that
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S}}
is invariant with respect to
Q_{\mathrm{BV}}
, so that the BV master action reduces. We rewrite
Q_{\mathrm{BV}}
as

Q_{\mathrm{BV}}=(Q_{\mathcal{M}}+\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{X}})+\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{S}}=\hat{D}+\bar{\mathrm{d}}_{\mathcal{S}}

(6.3)

where in the last formula
\hat{D}
is the lift from
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
to
(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}})^{\mathcal{S}}
of the vector field
D
on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
, while
\bar{\mathrm{d}}_{\mathcal{S}}
is the lift of
\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{S}}
from
\mathcal{S}
to
(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}})^{\mathcal{S}}
. We denote these lifts explicitly now because it is the properties of these lifts that guarantee the reduction: if
V_{\mathcal{S}}
is any vector field on
\mathcal{S}
, then the lift
\bar{V}_{\mathcal{S}}
always leaves
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S}}
invariant (for any submanifold
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
);
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S}}
is invariant for
\hat{D}
if
\mathcal{C}
is invariant for
D
. Therefore
Q_{\mathrm{BV}}
gives rise to a homological (and hamiltonian) vector field on the coisotropic reduction of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}}
, which is simply
\mathcal{W}^{\mathcal{S}}
. Using the results of appendix
C
we find that the new BV master action is given by evaluating the original action on
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{S}}
. In all examples we have investigated the result is another topological field theory of AKSZ type.

In summary, the brane wrapping of QP manifolds that we already discussed always leads to a brane wrapping procedure that takes the BV master action associated to an AKSZ topological field theory and produces the BV master action of another topological field theory.

AKSZ 3-brane to membrane example

7
Conclusions

We defined a reduction procedure of NQP manifolds
\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{W}
which encompasses the properties of wrapped branes. This is consistent with the AKSZ procedure in the sense that the reduction naturally lifts to a reduction of the AKSZ theory with target
\mathcal{M}
to the AKSZ theory with target
\mathcal{W}
. We applied this to many examples, including many physically motivated examples of wrapped branes and we saw that it reproduced the known M-theory/IIA dualities. We also were able to find a novel relation between the Courant algebroid and the Poisson algebroid through this reduction. We expect that our work will have many interesting applications to other topological AKSZ theories.

One can ask how general our procedure is, or whether it is possible to relax some of the assumptions made in section
2
. For example, can we relax the trivial bundle condition
M=N\times Y
, perhaps by introducing some flat connection similar to
Bonechi:2010tbl
? We can also ask whether we can extend our construction to manifolds
X
with boundary. We can also relax the constraint on
\mathcal{X}=T[1]X
, and instead just take
\mathcal{X}
to be some DG manifold with some invariant measure of degree
n+1
. For example, we can try to extend the reduction procedure to
\mathcal{X}=T^{1,0}[1]X
for some complex manifold
X
with
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X=n+1
. We could then apply the reduction to, say, the work of
Qiu:2009zv
.

In section
4.2
, we found an interesting relation between the Courant algebroid and the Poisson algebroid QP structures. This was based on the embedding of the Poisson differential
\mathrm{d}_{\pi}
into
T\oplus T^{*}
. There are other interesting differentials that can appear in these Courant algebroids
Ashmore:2021pdm
that are associated to topological theories on
\mathrm{G}_{2}
and
\mathrm{Spin}(7)
manifolds. One can try to embed these differentials in the language of QP structures and perform the reduction to get new topological models associated to these special holonomy manifolds. There are also similar structures that appear in higher algebroids. That is, one can define the notion of a Dirac structure for these higher algebroids and define the associated differential
severa2001some
;
Tennyson:2021qwl
;
Ashmore:2019rkx
;
Ashmore:2019qii
;
10.1093/imrn/rnx163
;
2021JGP…16104055C
;
Arvanitakis:2021wkt
. These can be embedded into the Q-structure of the QP manifolds associated to these higher algebroids. Their reductions may provide further insight into supersymmetric geometries of string/M-theory.

Graded manifolds

A graded manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a supermanifold whose coordinates come equipped with a
\mathbb{Z}
grading.

11

From
JOZWIKOWSKI2016212
, the consistency of the
\mathbb{Z}
grading of coordinates comes from the existence of a global degree counting vector field
\varepsilon
and transition functions which preserve degree.
One can always find homogeneous coordinates
Z^{A}
of definite degree, where
\deg Z^{A}\,\mathrm{mod}\,2
is the Grassman parity of the coordinate. We will denote by
A
the degree of
Z^{A}
and so we have

Z^{A}Z^{B}=(-1)^{AB}Z^{B}Z^{A}

(B.1)

The sheaf of functions on
\mathcal{M}
splits into subsheafs
C^{\infty}_{n}(\mathcal{M})
of functions of definite degree. The degree of a homogeneous function
f
is measured by the degree counting vector field
\varepsilon
(The ‘Euler vector field’) via

\varepsilon(f)=\deg(f)f

(B.2)

In local homogeneous coordinates
Z^{A}
, we have

\varepsilon=\sum_{A}\deg(Z^{A})Z^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{A}}

(B.3)

Unless otherwise stated, all derivations are left derivations. Hence, the de Rham
\mathrm{d}
is

\mathrm{d}f=\mathrm{d}Z^{A}\partial_{A}f

(B.4)

and any homogeneous (in degree) vector field
X
acts as

X(fg)=X(f)g+(-1)^{Xf}fX(g)

(B.5)

where we have used the shorthand
X,f
for the degree of the respective components. In local coordinates we can write
X=X(Z)^{A}\partial_{A}
, and so
\deg X=\deg X^{A}-\deg Z^{A}
. We also define

\deg(\mathrm{d}f)=\deg f+1

(B.6)

For this to be consistent with
\imath_{A}\mathrm{d}Z^{B}=\delta^{B}{}_{A}
, where
\imath_{A}
denotes contraction with the vector field
\partial_{A}
, we require that the interior product has degree

\deg\imath=-1

(B.7)

Poisson and symplectic structures

A graded Poisson structure of degree
-n
is defined to satisfy

(f,g)=(-1)^{1+(f+n)(g+n)}(g,f)

(B.8)

and the graded Jacobi identity

(f,(g,h))=((f,g),h)+(-1)^{(f+n)(g+n)}(g,(f,h))

(B.9)

for all homogeneous functions
f,g,h
. It also acts as a left derivation on the right hand arguments, but a right derivation on the left hand arguments. That is

\begin{split}(f,gh)&=(f,g)h+(-1)^{(f+n)g}g(f,h)\\
(fg,h)&=f(g,h)+(-1)^{(h+n)g}(f,h)g\end{split}

(B.10)

If the Poisson structure is induced from a symplectic structure
\omega
, we have that

\imath_{X_{f}}=(-1)^{f}\mathrm{d}f\qquad X_{f}:=(f,\cdot)

(B.11)

In local homogeneous coordinates we can write

\omega=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}Z^{A}\omega_{AB}\mathrm{d}Z^{B}

(B.12)

which implies the symmetry

\omega_{AB}=(-1)^{1+AB+n(A+B)}\omega_{BA}

(B.13)

If we define
\omega^{AB}
via
\omega^{AB}\omega_{BC}=\delta^{A}{}_{C}
, then (
B.11
) implies

(f,g)=(-1)^{f}\partial^{R}_{A}f\,\omega^{AB}\,\partial_{B}g

(B.14)

where
\partial^{R}_{A}
is defined by
\mathrm{d}f=\mathrm{d}Z^{A}\partial_{A}f=\partial^{R}_{A}\,\mathrm{d}Z^{A}
. Note that it is not a right derivation by itself, but the combination
(-1)^{f}\partial^{R}_{A}f
is a right derivation. This is consistent with (
B.10
). Note that this implies

(Z^{A},Z^{B})=(-1)^{A}\omega^{AB}

(B.15)

The symplectic potential is defined such that
\mathrm{d}\vartheta=\omega
, and can be defined canonically through the Euler vector field
\varepsilon

\mathcal{L}_{X}=\imath_{X}\mathrm{d}+(-1)^{X}\mathrm{d}\imath_{X}

Transgressed QP structure on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}

Let
(\mathcal{X}=T[1]X,\mathrm{d})
be a DG manifold with homogeneous coordinates
\sigma,\mathrm{d}\sigma
. A point
f\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
can be defined by how it pulls back the coordinates on
\mathcal{M}
. We have

f^{*}Z^{A}=\bm{Z}^{A}(\sigma,\mathrm{d}\sigma)=Z^{A}_{0}(\sigma)+Z^{A}_{1\,\alpha}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\alpha}+...+\tfrac{1}{d!}Z^{A}_{d\,\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{d}}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\alpha_{1}}...\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\alpha_{d}}

(B.18)

We use the shorthand
Z^{A}_{k}=\tfrac{1}{k!}Z^{A}_{k\,\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{k}}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\alpha_{1}}...\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\alpha_{k}}
, where
Z^{A}_{k\,\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{k}}(\sigma)
is a function of degree
\deg Z^{A}-k
. These act as coordinates on
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. Our conventions are always that the form components come to the right of the function. So, e.g.

Z^{A}_{1}=Z^{A}_{1\,\alpha}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\alpha}=(-1)^{A-1}\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\alpha}Z^{A}_{1\,\alpha}(\sigma)

(B.19)

We can always define an evaluation map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{ev}:\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{X}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{M}\\
(f,\sigma,\mathrm{d}\sigma)&\longmapsto&f(\sigma,\mathrm{d}\sigma)\end{array}

(B.20)

We also have the chain map defined by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu_{*}:\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{X})&\longrightarrow&\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}})\\
\alpha&\longmapsto&\int_{\mathcal{X}}\alpha\end{array}

(B.21)

The combination
\mu_{*}\mathrm{ev}^{*}:\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathcal{M})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}})
is called the transgression map. The QP structure on the mapping space is defined by

\omega_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu_{*}\mathrm{ev}^{*}\omega_{\mathcal{M}}\qquad\Theta_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}}=(-1)^{d}\mu_{*}\mathrm{ev}^{*}\Theta_{\mathcal{M}}+(-1)^{n+d+1}\imath_{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{*}\mathrm{ev}^{*}\vartheta

where we use the same symbol
\mathrm{d}
for the lift of the vector field on
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}

This can be given more explicitly in the coordinates (
B.18
). We will use the bold face notation to denote a function, differential form, or coordinate on
\mathcal{M}
that is pulled back to
\mathcal{X}
via some function
f\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
. That is, we effectively take
\bm{f}=\mathrm{ev}^{*}f

\omega_{\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{X}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta\bm{Z}^{A}(\omega_{\mathcal{M}})_{AB}\,\delta\bm{Z}^{B}

Our conventions for integrals is that constants are pulled out from the left. The symplectic form above gives rise to a Poisson bracket which takes the following form on homogeneous functionals
F,G

[F,G]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(-1)^{F}\frac{\delta^{R}F}{\delta\bm{Z}^{A}}(\omega_{\mathcal{M}})^{AB}\frac{\delta G}{\delta\bm{Z}^{B}}

(B.24)

where

\delta F=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta\bm{Z}^{A}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\bm{Z}^{A}}=\int_{X}\frac{\delta^{R}F}{\delta\bm{Z}^{A}}\delta\bm{Z}^{A}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{f}\epsilon,\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{g}\eta}}\right]=(-1)^{(f+n)\epsilon+d}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bm{(f,g)_{\mathcal{M}}}\epsilon\eta

Appendix C
Coisotropic reduction of graded Poisson algebras

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a graded algebra with a graded Poisson bracket
[\bullet,\bullet]
of degree
-P
along with a left derivation
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathcal{P}
, possibly
hamiltonian
(i.e. given by Poisson brackets, so
\mathcal{V}=[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}},\bullet]
for
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathcal{P}
). We will explain how all of these objects behave under
coisotropic reduction
. The derivation is as in the ungraded case considered originally by Weinstein and Śniatycki
sniatycki1983reduction
.

If
\mathcal{I}
is a (multiplicative, degree-homogeneous) ideal of
\mathcal{P}
, it is a
coisotrope
if it is a Poisson subalgebra, i.e.
[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}]\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. Then the
coisotropic reduction
of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\mathcal{I}
is the quotient

\overline{\mathcal{P}}\equiv N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}

(C.1)

where
N(\mathcal{I})\equiv\{f\in\mathcal{P}|[f,\mathcal{I}]\subseteq\mathcal{I}\}
is the Poisson normaliser of
\mathcal{I}
. Then the bracket on
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
is defined in terms of the bracket
[\bullet,\bullet]
via

[\Pi f,\Pi g]_{\bar{\mathcal{P}}}\equiv\Pi{[f,g]}\,.

(C.2)

where
\Pi f
is the equivalence class
f+\mathcal{I}
. For any
\mathcal{P}
derivation
\mathcal{V}
we define its reduction
\overline{\mathcal{V}}
via

\overline{\mathcal{V}}(\Pi{f})=\Pi{\mathcal{V}(f)}\qquad f\in\mathcal{P}\,.

(C.3)

Theorem 1
.

Given any coisotrope
\mathcal{I}
, the bracket
[\bullet,\bullet]_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
is well-defined. It is moreover a Poisson bracket of degree
-P
, and so
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
is a graded Poisson algebra.

If the derivation
\mathcal{V}
on
\mathcal{P}
preserves the Poisson structure (
\mathcal{V}[f,g]=[\mathcal{V}f,g]\pm[f,\mathcal{V}g]
) and the coisotrope (
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathcal{I}
) then the reduced derivation
\overline{\mathcal{V}}
is well-defined.

Finally if
\mathcal{V}
is furthermore hamiltonian with hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathcal{P}
(so
\mathcal{V}=[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}},\bullet]
) then
\overline{\mathcal{V}}
is hamiltonian with hamiltonian
\Pi(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}})
. (In this latter case
\mathcal{V}
automatically preserves the Poisson structure, but the condition
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathcal{I}
implies
[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{I}]\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.)

If all derivations we are interested in are in fact hamiltonian (which is the case in the main text) then we just need to check that the ideal
\mathcal{I}
is a coisotrope and that
[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{I}]\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

The bracket
[\bullet,\bullet]_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
is well-defined because

[\Pi f,\Pi g]_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}=\Pi{[f+\mathcal{I},g+\mathcal{I}]}=\Pi([f,g]+[f,\mathcal{I}]+[\mathcal{I},g]+[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}])=\Pi{[f,g]}

(C.4)

where the last three terms in the second equality vanish because
f,g\in N(\mathcal{I})
and
[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}]\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. This new bracket inherits the antisymmetry and Jacobi identity properties from
[\bullet,\bullet]
. Since furthermore
\mathcal{I}
is homogeneous in degree,
\Pi{f}
will have a well-defined degree, and so the new bracket defines a graded Poisson algebra structure.

Similarly since
\mathcal{V}(f+\mathcal{I})=\mathcal{V}(f)+\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I})
we have that
\overline{\mathcal{V}}
is well-defined on
\mathcal{P}/\mathcal{I}
when
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. We then need to show that it preserves the subspace
N(\mathcal{I})/\mathcal{I}=\overline{\mathcal{P}}
. Since
\mathcal{V}
preserves the Poisson bracket we have

[\mathcal{V}f,\mathcal{I}]=\mathcal{V}[f,\mathcal{I}]\pm[f,\mathcal{V}\mathcal{I}]

(C.5)

If
f\in N(\mathcal{I})
this becomes
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I})\pm[f,\mathcal{V}\mathcal{I}]
which lies in the coisotrope when
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{I})\subset\mathcal{I}
. Therefore
\mathcal{V}(f)
lies in
N(\mathcal{I})
.

Finally if
\mathcal{V}=[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}},\bullet]
then
\Pi{\mathcal{V}}(\Pi{f})=\Pi{\mathcal{V}(f)}=\Pi{[\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}},f]}=[\Pi{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{V}}},\Pi{f}]_{\Pi{\mathcal{P}}}
,
which completes the proof.
∎
