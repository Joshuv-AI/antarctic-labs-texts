Group separation strikes back
Thomas Place, Marc Zeitoun

Abstract
Group languages are regular languages recognized by finite groups, or equivalently by finite automata in which each letter induces a permutation on the set of states. We investigate the separation problem for this class of languages: given two arbitrary regular languages as input, we show how to decide if there exists a group language containing the first one while being disjoint from the second. We prove that covering, a problem generalizing separation, is decidable. A simple covering algorithm was already known: it can be obtained indirectly as a corollary of an algebraic theorem by Ash. Unfortunately, while deducing the algorithm from this algebraic result is straightforward, all proofs of Ash’s result itself require a strong background on algebraic concepts, and a wealth of technical machinery outside of automata theory. Our proof is independent of previous ones. It relies exclusively on standard notions from automata theory: we directly deal with separation and work with input languages represented by nondeterministic finite automata. We also investigate two strict subclasses. First, the alphabet modulo testable languages are those defined by counting the occurrences of each letter modulo some fixed integer (equivalently, they are the languages recognized by a commutative group). Secondly, the modulo languages are those defined by counting the length of words modulo some fixed integer. We prove that covering is decidable for both classes, with algorithms that rely on the construction made for group languages. Our proofs lead to tight complexity bounds for separation for all three classes, as well as for covering for both alphabet modulo testable languages and for modulo testable languages.



I
Introduction

Context.
A prominent question in automata theory is to understand natural classes of languages defined by restricting the common definitions of regular languages (such as regular expressions, automata, monadic second-order logic or finite monoids). Of course, “understanding a class” is an informal goal. The standard approach is to show that the class under investigation is recursive by looking for
membership algorithms
: given a regular language as input, decide whether it belongs to the class. Rather than the procedure itself, the motivation is that formulating such an algorithm often requires a deep understanding of the class. This approach was initiated in the 60s by Schützenberger
[
37
]
, who provided a membership algorithm for the class of
star-free languages
(those defined by a regular expression without Kleene star but with complement instead). This theorem started a fruitful line of research, which is now supported by a wealth of results. In fact, some of the most famous open problems in automata theory are membership questions (see
[
40
,
23
,
22
]
for surveys).

In this paper, we look at two problems, which both generalize membership. The first one is
separation
: given
two
regular languages
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
as input, decide whether there exists a third language that belongs to the investigated class, includes
L_{1}
and is disjoint from
L_{2}
. The second one is
covering
. It generalizes separation to an arbitrary number of input languages. These problems have been getting a lot of attention recently, and one could even argue that they have replaced membership as the central question. The motivation is twofold. First, it has recently been shown
[
28
]
that separation and covering are key ingredients for solving some of the most difficult membership questions (see
[
27
]
for a survey). Yet, the main motivation is tied to our original goal: “understanding classes”. In this respect, separation and covering are more rewarding than membership (albeit more difficult). Intuitively, a membership algorithm for a class
\mathscr{C}
can only detect the languages in
\mathscr{C}
, while a covering algorithm provides information on how
arbitrary regular languages
interact with
\mathscr{C}
.

Group languages.
In the paper, we look at three specific classes. The main one is the class of
group languages

. While natural, this class is rather unique since its only known definition is based on machines: group languages are those recognized by a finite group, or equivalently, by a
permutation automaton

[

(a deterministic finite automaton in which each letter induces a permutation on the set of states). On the other hand, no “descriptive” definition of

is known (

AMT
, consists of all languages recognized by
Abelian
(
i.e.
, commutative) groups. From a language theoretic point of view, these are the languages that can be defined by counting the occurrences of each letter modulo some fixed integer. The second is a subclass of

named

. A language is in

Motivations.
The class

More generally, given an arbitrary class

Connection with other fields.
Separation by group languages is related to another area of independent interest: the decidability of separation for

Ash’s result spawned other lines of research in algebra. For instance, it motivated the computation of closures of regular languages in profinite topologies. Indeed, deciding whether such profinite closures intersect corresponds to deciding a property of subsets of finite monoids, which in turn is equivalent to deciding covering

However, this line of research is disconnected from our motivation: to obtain a direct and purely automata theoretic proof of the decidability of

Unfortunately, the existing proofs of Ash’s result do not meet our motivation. Indeed, they do not involve covering. Their use therefore requires a detour: abstract the problem as a purely algebraic/topological question, do the proof in this framework and then come back to covering.

From our perspective, this detour has several drawbacks. First, it relies on “black box” results: to get a complete proof requires to gather and understand a lot of material. Secondly, the proofs demand a solid background on algebraic concepts and a wealth of technical machinery outside automata theory: for instance, for Ash’s theorem, some proofs
[
7
,
8
]
are based on the theory of inverse semigroups while others rely on topological arguments

,

,

. For these reasons, beyond their intrinsic difficulty, these proofs do not bring any intuition
at the level of languages
. This means that these results and their proofs are not satisfactory with respect to our primary objective: “understanding classes of languages”.

It has been shown that this detour can be avoided for almost all natural classes
[
25
,
32
,
31
,
30
]
: one can work directly with languages and use only basic algebraic notions (typically, the definition of regular languages by morphisms into finite monoids and standard combinatorics on monoids). This direct approach is much more rewarding with respect to our original goal. In particular, the proofs of separation algorithms provide an explicit description of generic separators (when they exist).

Group languages are among the few classes for which it is not known whether a fully language-theoretic approach is possible. This is the question we address in this paper.

Contributions.
We present self-contained proofs that covering and separation are decidable for
GR
,
AMT
and
MOD
. They avoid the detour through algebra and are based on new ideas that are independent of any pre-existing indirect proofs in this area. Of course, our proofs remain involved: these are hard questions. However, they rely exclusively on basic notions of automata theory, which makes them accessible to computer scientists. We work with nondeterministic finite automata (NFA). Paradoxically, we use very few algebraic notions beyond the standard definition of a group. Roughly speaking, proofs are based on word combinatorics for
GR
, on arithmetic for
AMT
, while
MOD
reduces to the other two for unary alphabets.

All separation and covering algorithms are neat and simple. They work directly with input languages represented by NFAs. However, it is worth mentioning that the main value of the paper lies not in the algorithms themselves, but in their proofs. Indeed, is actually easy to derive these algorithms from the aforementioned independent algebraic results. In particular, the covering algorithm we present for
GR
is essentially a reformulation on automata and a simple corollary of the original algorithm obtained from Ash’s theorem
[
7
]
, which uses inputs represented by monoid morphisms rather than automata. Actually, we show how to deduce our algorithm from Ash’s one. Furthermore, an algorithm similar to ours is given in
[
24
]
. It relies on a conjecture proved later in
[
35
]
, and on an algorithm to compute closures of certain regular languages in an appropriate topology
[
10
,
18
]
, itself based on Stallings foldings
[
38
]
. In contrast, our new proof is direct, matching our original objective: to remain in the framework of automata throughout the whole argument. In fact, NFAs are a key ingredient of this proof: we use nondeterminism in a crucial way.

Let us illustrate the simplicity of the algorithms using
GR
. We present a simple construction that inputs an NFA
\mathscr{A}
and outputs a new one
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
. Then, we show that the languages recognized by two NFAs
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
can be separated by a group language if and only if the languages recognized by
\langle\mathscr{A}_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
and
\langle\mathscr{A}_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
do
not
intersect. Since
\langle\mathscr{A}_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
and
\langle\mathscr{A}_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
can be computed in polynomial time, this shows that
GR
-separation is in
P
(this goes up to
PSPACE
for covering as this boils down to deciding intersection between an arbitrary number of NFAs). The approach for
AMT
is similar with one key difference: we look at
Parikh images
. More precisely, we show that whether the languages recognized by two NFAs
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
can be separated by
AMT
boils down to some specific condition on the Parikh images of
\langle\mathscr{A}_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
and
\langle\mathscr{A}_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
. The standard result that states that existential Presburger arithmetic is in
NP

[
36
]
implies then that
AMT
-separation is in co-
NP
. Actually, we show that both
AMT
-separation and
AMT
-covering are co-
NP
-complete. Finally, we show that in the much simpler case of
MOD
, separation is NL-complete and covering co-
NP
-complete.

We fix an arbitrary finite alphabet

L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle)\subseteq\tilde{A}^{*}
. Observe that for all
u\in\tilde{A}^{*}
and all strongly connected
q,r\in Q
, we have
u\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,r)
if and only if
u^{-1}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,r,q)
. Moreover, we can compute
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
from
\mathscr{A}
in polynomial time: this boils down to computing the pairs of strongly connected states,
i.e.
, to directed graph reachability. The following lemma is used to “simulate” the runs in
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
into the original NFA
\mathscr{A}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=(Q,I,F,\delta)
be an NFA and
\alpha:A^{*}\to G
be a morphism into a finite group. For every
q,r\in Q
and
w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,r)\subseteq\tilde{A}^{*}
, there exists a word
w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
w^{\prime}\in L(\mathscr{A},q,r)
and
\alpha(w)=\alpha(w^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We have to show that for every
(s,a^{-1},t)\in\langle\delta\rangle
where
a\in A
, there exists
x\in L(\mathscr{A},s,t)
such that
\alpha(x)=(\alpha(a))^{-1}
. By definition of
\langle\delta\rangle
, we have
(t,a,s)\in\delta
and
s,t
are strongly connected. Hence, we get
y\in L(\mathscr{A},s,t)
. Since
G
is a
finite
group, it is standard that there exists
p\geq 1
such that
g^{p}=1_{G}
for all
g\in G
. Thus,
\alpha((ay)^{p})=1_{G}
. Let
x=y(ay)^{p-1}
. By hypothesis on
a
and
y
, we know that
x\in L(\mathscr{A},s,t)
. Since
\alpha(ax)=\alpha((ay)^{p})=1_{G}
, we obtain
\alpha(x)=(\alpha(a))^{-1}
, as desired.
∎

III
Covering for group languages

We prove that separation and covering are decidable for
GR
. Historically, this was first obtained as a corollary of a difficult independent algebraic theorem by Ash
[
7
]
(see
[
15
]
for details and
[
3
]
for the link with separation). Our algorithm is essentially the one obtained from this theorem. Yet, we choose a different presentation: our inputs are represented by NFAs whereas the original algorithm considers a single monoid morphism recognizing all inputs. In itself, the NFA-based procedure is merely a natural reformulation of the one based on monoids (see Section
III-D
for details). On the other hand, we work exclusively with NFAs, which is a new idea, and nondeterminism is a key ingredient in our proof.

This proof is our main contribution. The known proofs of Ash’s theorem (
e.g.
,
[
7
,
8
,
35
,
16
]
) are arduous. Typically, they rely on specialized notions from independent fields such as algebra, topology or model theory. Moreover, they use black box results. In contrast, our proof is direct. While still difficult, it is fully self-contained and relies only on elementary notions from automata theory and combinatorics on words.

III-A

Statement

The procedure is based on a theorem characterizing the finite sets of regular languages that are
GR
-coverable. We first extend the core construction
\mathscr{A}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
introduced in the previous section (this extension is specific to
GR
-covering).

Given an arbitrary NFA
\mathscr{A}
, we further modify the NFA
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
and construct a new NFA
with
\varepsilon
-transitions

\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
(these are the only NFAs with
\varepsilon
-transitions that we consider). The definition is based on a language
L_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\tilde{A}^{*}
that we define first. We introduce a standard rewriting rule that one may apply to words in
\tilde{A}^{*}
. If
w\in\tilde{A}^{*}
contains an infix of the form
aa^{-1}
or
a^{-1}a
for some
a\in A
, one may delete it. More precisely, given
w,w^{\prime}\in\tilde{A}^{*}
, we write
w\rightarrow w^{\prime}
if there exist
x,y\in\tilde{A}^{*}
and
a\in A
such that either
w=xaa^{-1}y
or
w=xa^{-1}ay
, and
w^{\prime}=xy
. We write “
\xrightarrow{*}
” for the reflexive transitive closure of “
\rightarrow
”. That is, given
w,w^{\prime}\in\tilde{A}^{*}
, we have
w\xrightarrow{*}w^{\prime}
if
w=w^{\prime}
or there exist words
w_{0},\dots,w_{n}\in\tilde{A}^{*}
with
n\geq 1
such that
w=w_{0}\rightarrow w_{1}\rightarrow w_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow w_{n}=w^{\prime}
. We let
L_{\varepsilon}=\{w\in\tilde{A}^{*}\mid w\xrightarrow{*}\varepsilon\}
. This is a variant of the well-known
Dyck language
which is
not
regular (it is only context-free).

Consider an NFA
\mathscr{A}=(Q,I,F,\delta)
and the associated NFA
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle=(Q,I,F,\langle\delta\rangle)
. We extend
\langle\delta\rangle
with
\varepsilon
-transitions. We define
\langle\delta\rangle_{\varepsilon}\subseteq Q\times(\tilde{A}\cup\{\varepsilon\})\cup Q
as follows:

\langle\delta\rangle_{\varepsilon}=\langle\delta\rangle\cup\bigl\{(q,\varepsilon,r)\mid q,r\in Q\text{ and }L_{\varepsilon}\cap L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,r)\neq\emptyset\bigr\}.

Moreover, we let
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon}=(Q,I,F,\langle\delta\rangle_{\varepsilon})
.

.

Let

and

. We complete the definition with two simple but useful properties.

Fact 6

Let
\mathscr{A}=(Q,I,F,\delta)
be an NFA,
q,r\in Q
and
w\in\tilde{A}^{*}
such that

, then

. Also, if

are strongly connected, then
w^{-1}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},r,q)
.

Proof.

Since
w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q,r)
, the definition of
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
yields
u_{0},\dots,u_{n}\in\tilde{A}^{*}
,
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L_{\varepsilon}
and
x\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,r)
such that
w=u_{0}\cdots u_{n}
and
x=u_{0}v_{1}u_{1}\cdots v_{n}u_{n}
. Assume first that
w\in L_{\varepsilon}
. Since
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L_{\varepsilon}
, we have
v_{i}\xrightarrow{*}\varepsilon
for every
i\leq n
. Hence,
x\xrightarrow{*}w
and since

, we get
x\xrightarrow{*}\varepsilon
. Since

, we get

by definition. Assume now that
q,r
are strongly connected. Since

, we get

by definition of
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
. Moreover,
x^{-1}=u_{n}^{-1}v_{n}^{-1}\cdots u_{1}^{-1}v_{1}^{-1}u_{0}^{-1}
and since
v_{i}\xrightarrow{*}\varepsilon
for all

, we have
v_{i}^{-1}\xrightarrow{*}\varepsilon
for all

. Thus,

implies that
u_{n}^{-1}\cdots u_{0}^{-1}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},r,q)
. Since
u_{n}^{-1}\cdots u_{0}^{-1}=w^{-1}
, this completes the proof.
∎

Let us now extend Lemma
4
to this new automaton
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=(Q,I,F,\delta)
be an NFA and
\alpha:A^{*}\to G
be a morphism into a finite group. For every
q,r\in Q
and
w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q,r)\subseteq\tilde{A}^{*}
, there exists a word
w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
w^{\prime}\in L(\mathscr{A},q,r)
and
\alpha(w)=\alpha(w^{\prime})
.

Proof.

As
w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q,r)
, there are
u_{0},\dots,u_{n}\in\tilde{A}^{*}
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in L_{\varepsilon}
such that
x=u_{0}v_{1}u_{1}\cdots v_{n}u_{n}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,r)
and
w=u_{0}\cdots u_{n}
. Lemma
4
yields a word
w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
w^{\prime}\in L(\mathscr{A},q,r)
and
\alpha(x)=\alpha(w^{\prime})
. Moreover, since

, we have

for all

. Hence,
\alpha(v_{i})=1_{G}
. We get
\alpha(w)=\alpha(x)=\alpha(w^{\prime})
, as desired.
∎

We now state the main theorem. It characterizes
GR
-coverability using the construction
\mathscr{A}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
k\geq 1
and let
\mathscr{A}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{A}_{k}
be
NFAs
. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

The set
\big\{L(\mathscr{A}_{1}),\dots,L(\mathscr{A}_{k})\big\}
is
GR
-coverable.

2.

We have
\bigcap_{i\leq k}L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle_{\varepsilon})=\emptyset
.

Clearly, the second condition in Theorem
8
can be decided. Indeed, for every
i\leq k
, we can compute
\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
from
\mathscr{A}_{i}
in polynomial time. Moreover, it one can decide whether an arbitrary number of NFAs intersect (in polynomial space). Hence, we obtain as desired that
GR
-covering is decidable and in
PSPACE
(it is unknown whether this is tight). Additionally, when the number
k
of inputs is fixed, intersection can be decided in polynomial
time
. In particular,
GR
-separation (the case
k=2
) is in
P
. We prove at the end of the section that the problem is
P
-complete.

Example 9
.

Recall the languages from Example
5
. Observe that
a^{-1}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon})\cap L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon})
. We deduce from Theorem

that no group language can separate
L_{1}
from
L_{2}
.

III-B

Proof argument

We fix a number
k\geq 1
and for every
j\leq k
, we consider an NFA
\mathscr{A}_{j}=(Q_{j},I_{j},F_{j},\delta_{j})
. The two implications in the theorem are handled independently. Let us start with
1)\Rightarrow 2)
.

Implication

.
We prove the contrapositive. Assume that there exists
w\in\bigcap_{j\leq k}L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon})
. We prove that
\{L(\mathscr{A}_{1}),\dots,L(\mathscr{A}_{k})\}
is
not

GR
-coverable. Hence, we fix an arbitrary
GR
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of

and exhibit

.

For all

, let
\alpha_{i}:A^{*}\to G_{i}
be a morphism into a finite group recognizing

. Clearly,

is a finite group for the componentwise multiplication and the morphism

Implication

.
Let

.

There exists a synchronizer for
\mathscr{A}
.

We first use this result to prove

\bigcap_{j\leq k}L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon})\!=\!\emptyset

10
, there exists a synchronizer
\alpha\!:\!A^{*}\!\to\!G

\mathscr{A}
. Let

. We show that for every

10

(q,a,r)\in\delta

. Note that

if and only if

Let

Construction of

Base case:

and

\varepsilon\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q,r)

\varepsilon\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},r,s)

\varepsilon\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q,s)

We now prove that for all

\varepsilon\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q,r)

Consider

We use induction on the length of

III-D

Connection with Ash’s historical result

We compare Theorem
8
with the historical
GR
-covering algorithm that can be deduced from Ash’s results. We prove that the former is essentially a reformulation of the latter.

Preliminaries.
Let
\mathscr{C}
be a Boolean algebra and
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism into a finite monoid. We define
\mathscr{I}_{\mathscr{C}}[\alpha]\subseteq 2^{M}
as the set of all subsets
S\subseteq M
such that
\{\alpha^{-1}(s)\mid s\in S\}
is
not

\mathscr{C}
-coverable. It carries enough information to decide
\mathscr{C}
-covering for every input set consisting only of languages recognized by
\alpha
. More precisely, for
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}\subseteq M
, one may verify that
\{\alpha^{-1}(F_{i})\mid i\leq n\}
is not
\mathscr{C}
-coverable if and only if there is
S\in\mathscr{I}_{\mathscr{C}}[\alpha]
such that
S\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
i\leq n
.

Thus, a procedure computing
\mathscr{I}_{\mathscr{C}}[\alpha]\subseteq 2^{M}
from an input morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
yields an algorithm for
\mathscr{C}
-covering. Given a finite set of languages
\mathbf{H}
, one first computes a single morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
recognizing all
H\in\mathbf{H}
(this is straightforward). Then, one computes
\mathscr{I}_{\mathscr{C}}[\alpha]\subseteq 2^{M}
. It carries enough information to decide whether
\mathbf{H}
is
\mathscr{C}
-coverable.

Historical algorithm.
Ash’s results
[
7
]
yield a characterization of

. We present this characterization (we use a formulation taken from
[
15
]
) and prove that Theorem
8
is a natural reformulation on automata and a simple corollary.

We need weak inverses (they are the counterpart of automata construction
\mathscr{A}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
of Section
II
). Let
\alpha:A^{*}\to M
be a morphism into a finite monoid. For
s\in M
, a
weak inverse
of
s
in an element
t\in M
such that
tst=t
. We use this definition to associate a second morphism

over the extended alphabet
\tilde{A}
. For

, we let,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\gamma_{\alpha}(a)&=&\{\alpha(a)\}\in 2^{M},\\
\gamma_{\alpha}(a^{-1})&=&\{s\in\alpha(A^{*})\mid\text{$s$ is a weak inverse of $\alpha(a)$}\}.\end{array}

We now present the characterization. Recall that we write
L_{\varepsilon}=\{w\in\tilde{A}^{*}\mid w\xrightarrow{*}\varepsilon\}
(see Section

Theorem 22

(

q_{0},\dots,q_{n}\in Q
such that
q_{0}=q
,
q_{n}=r
,
(q_{i-1},q_{i})\in T_{i}
and
T_{i}\in\gamma_{\alpha}(b_{i})
for
i\leq n
. We show that
b_{i}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q_{i-1},q_{i})
for all
i\leq n
. This yields
w=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle_{\varepsilon},q,r)
,
i.e.

w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon})
as desired since
(q,r)\in I_{j}\times F_{j}
. Let
i\leq n
. If
b\in A
, then
\gamma_{\alpha}(b_{i})=\{\alpha(b_{i})\}
. Hence,

and since

by definition of

. Otherwise, if

, the fact that

IV
Covering for alphabet modulo testable languages

We consider the alphabet modulo testable languages. We first prove formally that these are the languages that can be recognized by a commutative group (this will be useful later). We then prove that
AMT
-covering is decidable in Theorem
27
below. Finally, we prove that
AMT
-separation and
AMT
-covering are co-
NP
-complete.

IV-A

Algebraic characterization of
AMT

For every number
d\geq 1
, we associate an equivalence
\sim_{d}
over
A^{*}
and use it to characterize the languages in
AMT
. Let
d\geq 1
and
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
, we write
w\sim_{d}w^{\prime}
if and only if
|w|_{a}\equiv|w^{\prime}|_{a}\bmod d
for every
a\in A
. It is immediate from the definition that
\sim_{d}
is an equivalent of finite index.

Lemma 25
.

Let
L\subseteq A^{*}
. We have
L\in\textup{AMT}
if and only if there exists
d\geq 1
such that
L
is a union of
\sim_{d}
-classes.

Proof.

Assume first that
L\in\textup{AMT}
:
L
is built from finitely many languages
L^{a}_{q,r}
(for
a\in A
and
q,r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r<q
) using only unions and intersections. Let
d
be the least common multiplier of all numbers
q\geq 1
used in these languages. We show that
L
is a union of
\sim_{d}
-classes. Let
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
w\sim_{d}w^{\prime}
. We prove that
w\in L\Leftrightarrow w^{\prime}\in L
. Clearly, it suffices to show that each language
L^{a}_{q,r}
used to define
L
satisfies
w\in L^{a}_{q,r}\Leftrightarrow w^{\prime}\in L^{a}_{q,r}
. Since
d
is a multiple of
q
, the hypothesis that
w\sim_{d}w^{\prime}
yields
|w|_{a}\equiv|w^{\prime}|_{a}\bmod q
. Hence, since
L^{a}_{q,r}=\{w\in A^{*}\mid|w|_{a}\equiv r\bmod q\}
by definition, we have
w\in L^{a}_{q,r}\Leftrightarrow w^{\prime}\in L^{a}_{q,r}
as desired.

Assume now that
L
is a union of
\sim_{d}
-classes for
d\geq 1
. We show that
L\in\textup{AMT}
. Since
\sim_{d}
has finite index, it suffices to show that all
\sim_{d}
-classes belongs to
AMT
. Let
w\in A^{*}
and consider its
\sim_{d}
-class. For every
a\in A
, let
r_{a}<d
by the remainder of the Euclidean division of
|w|_{a}
by
d
. By definition, for every
w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
, we have
w^{\prime}\sim_{d}w
if and only if
|w^{\prime}|_{a}\equiv r_{a}\bmod d
for every
a\in A
. It follows that the
\sim_{d}
-class of
w
is
\bigcap_{a\in A}L^{a}_{d,r_{a}}
which belongs to
AMT
by definition.
∎

We now prove the algebraic characterization of
AMT
.

Lemma 26
.

The class
AMT
consists of all languages that are recognized by a morphism into a finite commutative group.

Proof.

First consider
L\in\textup{AMT}
. We show that
L
is recognized by a morphism into a finite commutative group. By definition of
AMT
, it suffices to prove that this property is true for all basic languages
L^{a}_{q,r}
and that it is preserved by union and intersection. by definition
L^{a}_{q,r}=\{w\in A^{*}\mid|w|_{a}\equiv r\bmod q\}
for
a\in A
and
q,r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r<q
. It is recognized by the morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to{\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}}
(where
{\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}}=\{0,\dots,q-1\}
is the standard cyclic group) defined by
\alpha(a)=1
and
\alpha(b)=0
for
b\in A\setminus\{a\}
. We have
L^{a}_{q,r}=\alpha^{-1}(r)
. Finally, if
L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq A^{*}
are such that
L_{i}
is recognized by a morphism
\alpha_{i}:A^{*}\to G_{i}
into a finite commutative group for
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
L_{1}\cup L_{2}
and
L_{1}\cap L_{2}
are recognized by the natural morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to G_{1}\times G_{2}
(where
G_{1}\times G_{2}
is equipped with the componentwise multiplication).

Assume now that
L
is recognized by a morphism
\alpha\!:\!A^{*}\!\to\!G
into a finite commutative group
G
. We show that
L\in\textup{AMT}
. Since
G
is a finite group, it is standard that there exists a number
d\geq 1
such that
g^{d}=1_{G}
for every
g\in G
. We show that for every
u,v\in A^{*}
, it
u\sim_{d}v
, then
\alpha(u)=\alpha(v)
. It will follows that every language recognized by
\alpha
is a union of
\sim_{d}
-classes and therefore belongs to
AMT
by Lemma
25
. Recall that
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
. As
G
is commutative, reorganizing the letters in
u,v
does not change their image under
\alpha
. Thus,

\alpha(u)=\alpha(a_{1}^{|u|_{a_{1}}}\cdots a_{n}^{|u|_{a_{n}}})\text{ and }\alpha(v)=\alpha(a_{1}^{|v|_{a_{1}}}\cdots a_{n}^{|v|_{a_{n}}}).

If
u\sim_{d}v
, then
|u|_{a_{i}}\equiv|v|_{a_{i}}\bmod d
for every
i\leq n
. We get
r_{i}<d
and
h_{i},k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|u|_{a_{i}}=r_{i}+h_{i}\times d
and
|v|_{a_{i}}=r_{i}+k_{i}\times d
. Therefore, since
g^{d}=1_{G}
for all
g\in G
, we obtain that
\alpha(a_{i}^{\smash{|u|_{a_{i}}}})=\alpha(a_{i}^{\smash{|v|_{a_{i}}}})=\alpha(a_{i}^{\smash{r_{i}}})
. Altogether, we get
\alpha(u)=\alpha(v)=\alpha(a_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots a_{n}^{r_{n}})
, concluding the proof.
∎

IV-B

Covering for
AMT

We prove that covering is decidable for
AMT
as well. Let us point out that this can be obtained from an algebraic theorem of Delgado
[
12
]
. Yet, this approach is indirect: Delgado’s results are purely algebraic and do not mention separation. Formulating them would require a lot of groundwork. We use a direct approach based on standard arithmetical and automata theoretic arguments. As for
GR
, we present a theorem characterizing the finite sets of regular languages which are
AMT
-coverable. We reuse the construction
\mathscr{A}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
of Section
II
. We start with terminology that we need to formulate the result.

Let
n=|A|
. Consider an arbitrary linear order
A
and let
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
. We define a map
\zeta:\tilde{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(where
\mathbb{Z}
is the set of integers). Given,
w\in\tilde{A}^{*}
, we define,

\zeta(w)=(|w|_{\smash{a_{1}}}-|w|_{\smash{a_{1}^{-1}}},\dots,|w|_{\smash{a_{n}}}-|w|_{\smash{a_{n}^{-1}}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

For a language
L\subseteq\tilde{A}^{*}
over
\tilde{A}
, we shall consider the direct image
\zeta(L)=\{\zeta(w)\mid w\in L\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We may now present the characterization theorem.

Theorem 27
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
k

NFAs

\mathscr{A}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{A}_{k}
. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

The set
\{L(\mathscr{A}_{1}),\dots,L(\mathscr{A}_{k})\}
is
AMT
-coverable.

2.

We have
\bigcap_{i\leq k}\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle))=\emptyset
.

We first explain why Theorem
27
implies the decidability of
AMT
-covering. This follows from standard results and the decidability of Presburger arithmetic. Let us present a sketch.

The definition of the map
\zeta:\tilde{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a variation on a standard notion. Given a word
w\in\tilde{A}^{*}
, its
Parikh image
(also called commutative image) is defined as the following vector,

\pi(w)=(|w|_{\smash{a_{1}}},\dots,|w|_{\smash{a_{n}}},|w|_{\smash{a_{1}^{-1}}},\dots,|w|_{\smash{a_{n}^{-1}}})\in\mathbb{N}^{2n}.

Clearly,
\pi(w)
determines
\zeta(w)
and for every
L\subseteq\tilde{A}^{*}
,
\pi(L)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2n}
determines
\zeta(L)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Consider
k
NFAs
\mathscr{A}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{A}_{k}
. We know that
\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle
can be computed from
\mathscr{A}_{i}
in polynomial time for every
i\leq k
. Moreover, it is known
[
14
]
that an
existential
Presburger formula
\varphi_{i}
describing the set
\pi(L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle))\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2n}
can be computed from
\tilde{\mathscr{A}_{i}}
in polynomial time. It is then straightforward to combine the formulas
\varphi_{i}
into a single
existential
Presburger sentence which is equivalent to
\bigcap_{i\leq k}\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle))\neq\emptyset
. Finally, it is known
[
36
]
that the existential fragment of Presburger arithmetic can be decided in
NP
. Hence, deciding whether
\bigcap_{i\leq k}\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle))\neq\emptyset
can be achieved in
NP
. It then follows from Theorem
27
that
AMT
-covering (and therefore
AMT
-separation as well) can be decided in co-
NP
. It turns out that this complexity upper bound is optimal:
AMT
-covering and
AMT
-separation are both co-
NP
-complete (we present a simple proof for the lower bound using a reduction from 3-SAT).

Proof of Theorem
27
.

We fix a number
k\geq 1
and for every
j\leq k
, we consider an NFA
\mathscr{A}_{j}=(Q_{j},I_{j},F_{j},\delta_{j})
. The two implications in the theorem are handled independently.

Implication
1)\Rightarrow 2)
.
We prove the contrapositive. Consider
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\bigcap_{i\leq k}\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle))
. We show that
\{L(\mathscr{A}_{1}),\dots,L(\mathscr{A}_{k})\}
is
not

AMT
-coverable. Thus, we fix an
AMT
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
A^{*}
and show that there exists
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
K\cap L(\mathscr{A}_{j})\neq\emptyset
for every
j\leq k
. Let
\{K_{1},\dots,K_{\ell}\}=\mathbf{K}
. For each
i\leq\ell
, since
K_{i}\in\textup{AMT}
, Lemma
26
yields a morphism
\alpha_{i}:A^{*}\to G_{i}
into a finite
commutative
group recognizing

. Clearly,
G=G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{\ell}
is a commutative group for the componentwise multiplication and each

is recognized by the morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to G
defined by
\alpha(w)=(\alpha_{1}(w),\dots,\alpha_{n}(w))
.

Since
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\bigcap_{i\leq k}\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle))
, we get
w_{i}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{i}\rangle)
such that
\zeta(w_{i})=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
for all
i\leq\ell
. Also, Lemma
4
yields

such that
\alpha(u_{i})=\alpha(w_{i})
. As
G
is
commutative
, the image under
\alpha
of a word

depends only on
\zeta(w)
. Hence,
\alpha(w_{1})=\cdots=\alpha(w_{k})
. We get
\alpha(u_{1})=\cdots=\alpha(u_{k})
. As
\mathbf{K}
is a cover of
A^{*}
, we get
K\in\mathbf{K}
such that
u_{1}\in K
. Since
K
is recognized by
\alpha
and
\alpha(u_{1})=\cdots=\alpha(u_{k})
, this yields
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\in K
. Thus,
K\cap L(\mathscr{A}_{i})\neq\emptyset
for all
i\leq\ell
as desired.

Implication
2)\Rightarrow 1)
.
We use standard arithmetical tools. Consider the componentwise addition on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We abuse notation and write “
0
” for the identity element (
i.e.
, the vector whose entries are all equal to zero). For a single vector
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and a
finite
set of vectors
V=\{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\dots,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{\ell}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we write,

\mathscr{L}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,V)=\{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+k_{1}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\cdots+k_{\ell}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{\ell}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mid k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

Following
[
11
]
, we call these sets the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear subsets
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Likewise,
\mathbb{Z}
-semilinear
subsets are finite unions of
\mathbb{Z}
-linear sets (including
\emptyset
, which is the empty union). We need two results about these sets. The first one is a variation on Parikh’s theorem (which implies that the Parikh images of regular languages are semilinear subsets of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
). It is specific to the automata built with
\mathscr{A}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
.

Lemma 28
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be an NFA. Then,
\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle))
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-semilinear subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Proof.

The proof is based on standard ideas which are typically used to prove the automata variant of Parikh’s theorem. However, let us point out that we do require a specific property of the automaton
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
at some point (the lemma is not true for an arbitrary NFA over the extended alphabet
\tilde{A}
). For all
q\in Q
, we associate a finite set
V_{q}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. We define,

V_{q}=\{\zeta(w)\mid w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,q)\text{ and }|w|\leq|Q|\}.

Observe that if
w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,q)
for some
w\in\tilde{A}^{*}
, the states encountered on this run are strongly connected. Hence, in that case, we also have
w^{-1}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,q)
by definition of
\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
. Moreover, we have
\zeta(w^{-1})=-\zeta(w)
by definition of
\zeta
. Consequently, for every
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in V_{q}
, the opposite vector also belongs to
V_{q}
: we have
-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in V_{q}
. This property is where we need the hypothesis that are considering an automata built with the construction
\mathscr{A}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
(it fails for an arbitrary NFA). For every
P\subseteq Q
, we write
V_{P}=\bigcup_{q\in P}V_{q}
.

Finally, we associate a second finite set

to every
P\subseteq Q
. Let
w\in\tilde{A}^{*}
. We say that
w
is a
P
-witness if there exist
q\in I
and

such that there is a run from
q
to
r
labeled by
w
such that

is exactly the set of all states encountered in that run (in particular, we have

). We define,

X_{P}=\{\zeta(w)\mid\text{$w$ is a $P$-witness and $|w|\leq|Q|^{2}$}\}.

We now prove the following,

\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle))=\bigcup_{P\subseteq Q}\bigcup_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in X_{P}}\mathscr{L}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,V_{P}).

This equality concludes the proof:
\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle))
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-semilinear subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, as desired. We start with the right to left inclusion. Let
P\subseteq Q
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in X_{P}
. We show that
\mathscr{L}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,V_{P})\subseteq\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle))
.

Let
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\mathscr{L}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,V_{P})
. By definition, we have
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\dots,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{\ell}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in V_{P}
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+k_{1}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\cdots+k_{\ell}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{\ell}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. Moreover, recall that by construction, for every
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, the opposite vector
-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
belongs to
V_{P}
as well. Therefore, we may assume without loss of generality that
k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
: they are
positive integers
. By definition of
V_{P}
, we know that for every
i\leq\ell
, we have
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in V_{q_{i}}
for some
q_{i}\in P
. Hence, there exists
x_{i}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q_{i},q_{i})
such that
\zeta(x_{i})=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. Let
y_{i}=(x_{i})^{k_{i}}
(this is well-defined since
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
). Clearly,
\zeta(y_{i})=k_{i}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
y_{i}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q_{i},q_{i})
. Moreover,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in X_{P}
which yields a

-witness

such that
\zeta(w)=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. Since
q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}\in P
and
w
is a
P
-witness, we have
q\in I
and
r\in F
such that there exists a run from
q
to
r
labeled by
w
which encounters all states

. Therefore, we have a permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
w_{0},\dots,w_{\ell}\in\tilde{A}^{*}
such that

,
w_{0}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q,q_{\sigma(1)})
,
w_{i}\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle,q_{\sigma(i)},q_{\sigma(i+1)})

and

. Consider the word
w^{\prime}=w_{0}y_{\sigma(1)}w_{1}\cdots y_{\sigma(\ell)}w_{\ell}
. It is clear from the definitions that

which yields

and
\zeta(w^{\prime})\in\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle))
. Moreover, it is immediate that
\zeta(w^{\prime})=\zeta(w)+\zeta(y_{1})+\cdots+\zeta(y_{\ell})
which yields
\zeta(w^{\prime})=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+k_{1}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\cdots+k_{\ell}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv_{\ell}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. We get
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in\zeta(L(\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle))
as desired.

We turn to the converse inclusion which is based on pumping arguments. Given a word

, we need to prove that
\zeta(w)\in\bigcup_{P\subseteq Q}\bigcup_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in X_{P}}\mathscr{L}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,V_{P})
. Since

, there exists

and

such that

. We let

is a

-witness. We use induction on the length of

to show that there exists

Remark 32

or the letter

We let
H_{i}=\{x_{i}^{p}\mid 1\leq p\leq k\}\cup\{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mux_{i}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{p}\mid 1\leq p\leq k\}

Clearly

is finite and all the words in

are encodings. We turn to the definition of

. For every

, we associate a language

to the

where
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\in\{x_{1},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mux_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\ldots,x_{n},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mux_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\}

Finally, we define,

L_{2}=T_{1}\cdots T_{k}(\{\varepsilon\}\cup H_{1})\cdots(\{\varepsilon\}\cup H_{n}).

Clearly,
L_{2}
is finite as well. Observe that the words in

need not be encodings. On the other hand, all encodings within

(if any) correspond to an assignment of truth values which satisfies

.

It remains to show that

are not

-separable if and only if the

\varphi
. By definition of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}

and

and
w_{2}
so that
|w_{1}|_{a}=|w_{2}|_{a}
for every

. This implies that
\alpha(w_{1})=\alpha(w_{2})
for every morphism
\alpha:A^{*}\to G
into an commutative group

. Hence, in view of Lemma
26
, every language

which contains
w_{1}
must contain
w_{2}
as well. Since
w_{1}\in L_{1}
and
w_{2}\in L_{2}
, it follows that
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are not
AMT
-separable.

Conversely, assume that
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are not
AMT
-separable. By definition,
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are finite. Thus, there exists
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|w|<d
for every

. We consider the equivalence
\sim_{d}
over

. By Lemma

, every union of
\sim_{d}

AMT
. Hence, since
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are not

\sim_{d}

and
L_{2}

and
w_{2}\in L_{2}
such that

|w_{1}|_{a}\equiv|w^{\prime}_{2}|_{a}\bmod d

and

by definition of

for every

encodes an assignment of truth values. Moreover, since

encodes the same assignment of truth values. Finally, since

V
Covering for modulo languages

In this section, we reduce
MOD
-covering to
GR
-covering and
AMT
-covering for unary alphabets. Then, we show that
MOD
-covering is co-
NP
-complete, while
MOD
-separation is NL-complete.

V-A

MOD
-covering algorithm

Getting a “naive” direct algorithm for
MOD
-covering is straightforward. Here, we prove that
MOD
-covering reduces to both
GR
-covering and
AMT
-covering. This approach provides much better complexity upper bounds than the naive one.

The reduction is based on a simple construction. It takes a language
L\subseteq A^{*}
as input and builds a new one over a
unary alphabet
(
i.e.
, containing a unique letter). We let
U\!=\!\{\$\}
and
\mu:A^{*}\to U^{*}
be the morphism defined by
\mu(a)=\$
for every
a\in A
. It is standard that if
L\!\subseteq\!A^{*}
is recognized by an input NFA
\mathscr{A}
, one may compute an NFA recognizing
\mu(L)
in logarithmic space (all transitions must be relabeled by “
\$
”).

Theorem 33
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\subseteq A^{*}
. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

The set
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}
is
MOD
-coverable.

2.

The set
\{\mu(L_{1}),\dots,\mu(L_{k})\}
is
AMT
-coverable.

3.

The set
\{\mu(L_{1}),\dots,\mu(L_{k})\}
is
GR
-coverable.

Proof.

We prove that
1)\Rightarrow 2)\Rightarrow 3)\Rightarrow 1)
. Let us start with
1)\Rightarrow 2)
. Assume that
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}
is
MOD
-coverable. We get a
MOD
-cover
\mathbf{K}
of
A^{*}
such that for each
K\in\mathbf{K}
, there is
i\leq k
satisfying
K\cap L_{i}=\emptyset
. Let
\mathbf{H}=\{\mu(K)\mid K\in\mathbf{K}\}
. Since
\mathbf{K}
is a cover of
A^{*}
and
\mu
is surjective,
\mathbf{H}
must be a cover of
U^{*}
. One may verify that all
H\in\mathbf{H}
belongs to
MOD
since this is the case for all
K\in\mathbf{K}
. Hence, since
\textup{MOD}\subseteq\textup{AMT}
, we obtain that
\mathbf{H}
is an
AMT
-cover of
U^{*}
. It remains to verify for each
H\in\mathbf{H}
, there exists
i\leq k
such that
H\cap\mu(L_{i})=\emptyset
. By definition,
H=\mu(K)
for some
K\in\mathbf{K}
. By hypothesis on
\mathbf{K}
, we get
i\leq k
such that
K\cap L_{i}=\emptyset
. We show that
H\cap\mu(L_{i})=\emptyset
. By contradiction, assume that there exists
u\in H\cap\mu(L_{i})
. As
H=\mu(K)
, we get
w\in K
and
w^{\prime}\in L_{i}
such that
\mu(w)=\mu(w^{\prime})=u
. By definition of
\mu
, we have
|w|=|w^{\prime}|=|u|
. Since
w\in K
and
K\in\textup{MOD}
, this yields
w^{\prime}\in K
. Thus,
w^{\prime}\in K\cap L_{i}
, a contradiction.

The implication
2)\Rightarrow 3)
is trivial as
\textup{AMT}\subseteq\textup{GR}
. It remains to prove
3)\Rightarrow 1)
. Assume that
\{\mu(L_{1}),\dots,\mu(L_{k})\}
is
GR
-coverable. This yields a
GR
-cover
\mathbf{H}
of
U^{*}
such that for each
H\in\mathbf{H}
, there exists
i\leq k
satisfying
H\cap\mu(L_{i})=\emptyset
. We let
\mathbf{K}=\{\mu^{-1}(H)\mid H\in\mathbf{H}\}
. By definition of
\mathbf{H}
, one may verify that
\mathbf{K}
is a cover of
A^{*}
and that for all
K\in\mathbf{K}
, there is
i\leq k
such that
K\cap L_{i}=\emptyset
. It remains to show that
\mathbf{K}
is a
MOD
-cover (which implies that
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}
is
MOD
-coverable, as desired). Let
H\in\mathbf{H}
. We prove that
\mu^{-1}(H)\in\textup{MOD}
. By definition, we have to exhibit
q\geq 1
such that for
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
, if
|w|\equiv|w^{\prime}|\bmod q
, then
w\!\in\!\mu^{-1}(H)\Leftrightarrow w^{\prime}\!\in\!\mu^{-1}(H)
(
i.e.
,
\mu(w)\in H\Leftrightarrow\mu(w^{\prime})\in H
). Since
H\in\textup{GR}
, we get a morphism
\alpha:U^{*}\to G
into a finite group

recognizing
H
. It is standard that there is
q\geq 1
such that
g^{q}=1_{G}
for all
g\in G
. We now fix
w,w^{\prime}\in A^{*}
such that
|w|\equiv|w^{\prime}|\bmod q
. This yields
r\geq 0
and
k,k^{\prime}\geq 1
such that
|w|=r+kq
and
|w^{\prime}|=r+k^{\prime}q
. Hence, we have
\mu(w)=\$^{r+qk}
and
\mu(w^{\prime})=\$^{r+qk^{\prime}}
. By definition of
q
, this yields
\alpha(\mu(w))=\alpha(\mu(w^{\prime}))=\alpha(\$^{r})
. As
\alpha
recognizes
H
, we get
\mu(w)\in H\Leftrightarrow\mu(w^{\prime})\in H
, as desired.
∎

Theorem
33
provides log-space reductions from
MOD
-covering to
AMT
-covering and from
MOD
-separation to
GR
-separation. Hence by Section
IV
,
MOD
-covering is in co-

and by Section

,

-separation is in

. In the next two subsections, we show that the co-

upper bound for covering is tight, while
MOD
-separation is in fact NL-complete.

V-B

Complexity of
MOD
-covering

As we explained above,
MOD
-covering is in co-
NP
: Theorem
33
provides a logarithmic space reduction to
AMT
-covering which is itself in co-
NP
. Here, we prove that this upper bound is tight. We actually show that
non

MOD
-coverability is
NP
-hard. More precisely, we present a logarithmic space reduction from 3-satisfiability (3-SAT) to this problem. Given a 3-SAT formula
\varphi
, we explain how to construct a finite set of regular languages and show that it is not
MOD
-coverable if and only if
\varphi
is satisfiable. We only describe the construction: that NFAs for the regular languages in the set can be computed from
\varphi
in logarithmic space is straightforward and left to the reader.

Remark 34
.

Note that Theorem
33
also provides a logarithmic space reduction from
MOD
-covering to
GR
-covering. Hence, the lower bound for
MOD
-covering that we prove in this section transfers to
GR
-covering, which is therefore co-
NP
-hard (recall that the upper bound for this problem is
PSPACE
, since it amounts to checking nonemptiness of an intersection of automata).

Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
be the
3
-clauses such that
\varphi=\bigwedge_{i\leq k}C_{i}
and let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be the propositional variables in
\varphi
. Consider the unary alphabet
U=\{\$\}
. We construct a finite set of regular languages over
U
. We encode the assignment of truth values for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
by single words in
U^{*}
. Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
be the first
n
prime numbers. For each
w\in U^{*}
, we associate an assignment
val(w)\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that encodes a mapping
x_{i}\mapsto b_{i}
giving truth values for the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. We define
val(w)=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
such that for each
i\leq n
, we let
b_{i}=1
if
|w|
is a multiple of
p_{i}
and
b_{i}=0
otherwise. Note that since
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are primes, each assignment of truth values for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is encoded by some word in
U^{*}
. We now specify the regular languages associated to
\varphi
. For every
i\leq n
, we let,

\begin{array}[]{lllll}P_{i}&=&\{w\in U^{*}\mid|w|\equiv p_{i}\bmod 0\},&&\\
N_{i}&=&\{w\in U^{*}\mid|w|\not\equiv p_{i}\bmod 0\}&=&U^{*}\setminus P_{i}.\end{array}

Finally, with every
j\leq k
, we associate a language
L_{j}
to the
3
-clause
C_{j}
. By definition, we have
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\leq n
such that
C_{j}=\ell_{i_{1}}\wedge\ell_{i_{2}}\wedge\ell_{i_{3}}
where
\ell_{i_{k}}\in\{x_{i_{k}},\neg x_{i_{k}}\}
. For
k\in\{1,2,3\}
, we let
H_{k}=P_{k}
if
\ell_{i_{k}}=x_{i_{k}}
and
H_{k}=N_{k}
if
\ell_{i_{k}}=\neg x_{i_{k}}
. We then define
L_{j}=H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup H_{3}
. One may verify from the definition that
L_{j}\in\textup{MOD}
and that an NFA recognizing
L_{j}
can be computed from
\varphi
in logarithmic space. Moreover, the following lemma may also be verified.

Lemma 35
.

The language
\bigcap_{j\leq k}L_{j}
consists of every word
w\in U^{*}
such that the assignment
val(w)
satisfies
\varphi
.

Since each assignment of truth values is encoded by some word in
U^{*}
, Lemma
35
implies that
\varphi
is satisfiable if and only if
\bigcap_{j\leq k}L_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Finally, since
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\in\textup{MOD}
, one may verify that
\bigcap_{j\leq k}L_{j}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}
is
not

MOD
-coverable. Altogether, we obtain that
\varphi
is satisfiable if and only if
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}
is
not

MOD
-coverable: this is indeed a logarithmic space reduction from 3-SAT to non-coverability for
MOD
.

V-C

Complexity of
MOD
-separation

We now prove that
MOD
-separation is in NL, by an analysis the
GR
-separation procedure for
unary alphabets
. This implies that
MOD
-separation is NL-complete, as NL is a generic lower bound for separation. Indeed, there exists a straightforward reduction from NFA emptiness (which is NL-complete) to
\mathscr{C}
-separation for an arbitrary Boolean algebra
\mathscr{C}
: given an NFA
\mathscr{A}
,
L(\mathscr{A})=\emptyset
if and only if
L(\mathscr{A})
is
\mathscr{C}
-separable from
A^{*}
.

Theorem
33
presents a log-space reduction from
MOD
-separation to
GR
-separation for languages over
unary alphabets
. Hence, it suffices to prove that the latter problem is in NL. Fix a single letter alphabet
A=\{a\}
. We prove that given as input two NFAs
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
over
A
, one may decide in NL whether
L(\mathscr{A}_{1})
is
not

GR
-separable from
L(\mathscr{A}_{2})
. Since
\textup{NL}=\textup{co{-}NL}
by the Immerman-Szelepcsényi theorem, this implies as desired that
GR
-separation is in NL for languages over unary alphabets. By Theorem
8
, the two following conditions are equivalent:

1.

L(\mathscr{A}_{1})
is not
GR
-separable from
L(\mathscr{A}_{2})
.

2.

L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon})\cap L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon})\neq\emptyset
.

Therefore, we have to prove that the second condition can be decided in NL. For
j\in\{1,2\}
, we write
\mathscr{A}_{j}=(Q_{j},I_{j},F_{j},\delta_{j})
. By definition,
\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
is built from
\mathscr{A}_{j}
by adding new transitions labeled by
a^{-1}
(this is the construction of
\mathscr{A}_{j}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle
) and
\varepsilon
-transitions (this is the construction of
\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
). It is standard that if we have
\langle\mathscr{A}_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
and
\langle\mathscr{A}_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
in hand, deciding whether
L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon})\cap L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon})\neq\emptyset
can be achieved in NL since this boils down to graph reachability (in the product of
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
whose set of states is
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
). Therefore, we have to prove that one may decide in NL whether a given transition belongs to
\langle\delta_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
or
\langle\delta_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
.

This is immediate for the transitions labeled by
a\in A
as they already belong to
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
. Let us now consider the transitions labeled by
a^{-1}\in A^{-1}
which belong to
\langle\delta_{1}\rangle
and
\langle\delta_{2}\rangle
. By definition, for
j=1,2
, and
q,r\in Q_{j}
, we have
(r,a^{-1},q)\in\langle\delta_{j}\rangle
if and only if
(q,a,r)\in\delta_{j}
and
q,r
are strongly connected. Clearly, this can be checked in NL since testing whether
q,r
are strongly connected boils down to graph reachability (which is in NL). It remains to consider the
\varepsilon
-transitions in
\langle\delta_{1}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
and
\langle\delta_{2}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
. We do so in the following lemma (this is where we use the hypothesis that the alphabet is unary).

Lemma 36
.

Let
j\in\{1,2\}
and
q,r\in Q_{j}
, one may decide in
NL
whether
(q,\varepsilon,r)\in\langle\delta_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

By definition, we have
(q,\varepsilon,r)\in\langle\delta_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
if and only if there exists
w\in L_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\tilde{A}^{*}
such that
w\in L(\langle\mathscr{A}_{j}\rangle,q,r)
. Observe that since we have
A=\{a\}
, it follows that
L_{\varepsilon}=\{w\in\tilde{A}^{*}\mid|w|_{a}\ =|w|_{a^{-1}}\}
. We use this property to prove that deciding whether
(q,\varepsilon,r)\in\langle\delta_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
boils down to graph reachability, which again can be decided in NL.

We let
U=Q_{j}\times\mathbb{Z}
be a set of vertices and consider the following set of edges:

\begin{array}[]{llll}E&=&&\{((q,k),(q^{\prime},k+1))\mid(q,a,q^{\prime})\in\langle\delta_{j}\rangle\}\\
&&\cup&\{((q,k),(q^{\prime},k-1))\mid(q,a^{-1},q^{\prime})\in\langle\delta_{j}\rangle\}.\end{array}

Consider the graph
G=(U,E)
. One may verify that
(q,\varepsilon,r)\in\langle\delta_{j}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
if and only if there exists a path from
(q,0)
to
(r,0)
in
G
. We prove that the latter condition can be checked in NL. Let
V=\{(q,k)\in U\mid|k|\leq|Q_{j}|^{2}\}
. We show that there exists a path
(q,0)
to
(r,0)
in
G
if and only if there exists a path from
(q,0)
to
(r,0)
in
G
using only states in
V
. It is then straightforward that this last property can be tested in NL, since this is again a graph reachability problem over a graph with
|V|=|Q_{j}|\times(2|Q_{j}|+1)
vertices, whose edges can be computed from
\mathscr{A}_{j}
in NL.

The right to left implication is immediate. For the converse one, we consider a path from
(q,0)
to
(r,0)
in
G
. We prove that if this path contains a vertex in
U\setminus V
, then there exists a strictly shorter path from
(q,0)
to
(r,0)
. One may then iterate the result to build a path that only contains states in
V
, completing the proof. Let
(s_{0},k_{0}),\dots,(s_{n},k_{n})\in U
be the vertices along our path:
(s_{0},k_{0})=(q,0)
,
(s_{n},k_{n})=(r,0)
, and for every
i\leq n
, we have
((s_{i},k_{i}),(s_{i+1},k_{i+1}))\in E
. Moreover, we know that there exits some index
h\leq n
such that
(s_{h},k_{h})\not\in V
,
i.e.
, such that
|k_{h}|>|Q_{j}|^{2}
. By symmetry, we assume that
k_{h}>|Q_{j}|^{2}
and leave the case
k_{h}<-|Q_{j}|^{2}
to the reader. We write
m=k_{h}
for the proof. By definition of
E
and since
k_{0}=k_{n}=0
, there exist,

0<i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m-1}<h<i^{\prime}_{m-1}<\cdots<i^{\prime}_{1}<n,

such that
k_{i_{1}}=k_{i^{\prime}_{1}}=1,\dots,k_{i_{m-1}}=k_{i^{\prime}_{m-1}}=m-1
. We also write
i_{0}=0
and
i^{\prime}_{0}=n
. By hypothesis,
k_{i_{0}}=k_{i^{\prime}_{0}}=0
. Since
m>|Q_{j}|^{2}
, it now follows from the pigeonhole principle that there exist
0\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}\leq m-1
such that
s_{i_{\ell_{1}}}=s_{i_{\ell_{2}}}
and
s_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}}=s_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{2}}}
. Let
\ell=\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}
. One may verify from the definition of
E
that the following paths exist in
G
:

\begin{array}[]{l}(s_{0},k_{0})\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow(s_{i_{\ell_{1}}},k_{i_{\ell_{1}}})\rightarrow(s_{i_{\ell_{2}+1}},k_{i_{\ell_{2}+1}}-\ell)\\
(s_{i_{\ell_{2}+1}},k_{i_{\ell_{2}+1}}-\ell)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow(s_{h},k_{h}-\ell)\\
(s_{h},k_{h}-\ell)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow(s_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{2}-1}},k_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{2}-1}}-\ell)\rightarrow(s_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}},k_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}})\\
(s_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}},k_{i^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}})\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow(s_{n},k_{n}).\end{array}

Altogether, we get a strictly shorter path from
(q,0)
to
(r,0)
, which completes the proof.
∎

VI
Conclusion

We proved simple separation and covering algorithms for the classes
GR
,
AMT
and
MOD
using only standard notions from automata theory. For
GR
and
AMT
, the proofs are based on the automata-theoretic construction “
\mathscr{A}\mapsto\langle\mathscr{A}\rangle
”. Since the statements behind the two algorithms (
i.e.
, Theorem
8
and Theorem
27
) are similar, a natural question is whether their proofs can be unified (as of now, they are independent).
We also obtained tight complexity bounds: separation is NL-complete for
MOD
, co-
NP
-complete for
AMT
and
P
-complete for
GR
. Covering is co-
NP
-complete for both
MOD
and
AMT
, and between co-
NP
and
PSPACE
for
GR
. This raises the question of the exact complexity of
GR
-covering.
