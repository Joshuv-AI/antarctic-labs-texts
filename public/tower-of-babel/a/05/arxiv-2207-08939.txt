Learning Sparsity-Promoting Regularizers using Bilevel Optimization
Avrajit Ghosh, Michael T. McCann, Madeline Mitchell, Saiprasad Ravishankar

Abstract
We present a gradient-based heuristic method for supervised learning of sparsity-promoting regularizers for denoising signals and images. Sparsity-promoting regularization is a key ingredient in solving modern signal reconstruction problems; however, the operators underlying these regularizers are usually either designed by hand or learned from data in an unsupervised way. The recent success of supervised learning (e.g., with convolutional neural networks) in solving image reconstruction problems suggests that it could be a fruitful approach to designing regularizers. Towards this end, we propose to denoise signals using a variational formulation with a parametric, sparsity-promoting regularizer, where the parameters of the regularizer are learned to minimize the mean squared error of reconstructions on a training set of ground truth image and measurement pairs. Training involves solving a challenging bilevel optimization problem; we derive an expression for the gradient of the training loss using the closed-form solution of the denoising problem and provide an accompanying gradient descent algorithm to minimize it. Our experiments with structured 1D signals and natural images indicate that the proposed method can learn an operator that outperforms well-known regularizers (total variation, DCT-sparsity, and unsupervised dictionary learning) and collaborative filtering for denoising.

Image reconstruction has been studied extensively under the lens of bilevel optimization

2.3
Unrolling and related approaches

The idea of unrolling approaches is to replace the lower level optimization problem by a sequence of iterations. Iterative algorithms have been used extensively to solve convex optimization problems. A very common optimization problem prevalent in image reconstruction is the unconstrained
\ell_{1}
minimization problem which consists of an
\ell_{1}
penalty and a data-fidelity term. ISTA (Iterative Shrinkage Thresholding Algorithm) algorithm and ADMM (Alternating direction method of multipliers) are well known iterative algorithms to solve this type of problem. Say if the algorithm used to solve the lower level problem is unrolled for
T
iterations, then for each iterative update, let
\Psi
denote the update as a function, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}^{(t)}=\Psi({\bm{x}}^{(t-1)},\bm{\beta})\;\;\textrm{for t = 1,2,...,T}.

(5)

Assuming the algorithm runs for
T
iterations, we will use
{\bm{x}}^{(T)}
as the approximate reconstruction in the upper level loss. Let
\mathbf{H_{t}}=\nabla_{x}\Psi({\bm{x}}^{(t-1)},\bm{\beta})
and
\mathbf{J_{t}}=\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\Psi({\bm{x}}^{(t-1)},\bm{\beta})
, then using chain rule yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}{\bm{x}}^{*}(\bm{\beta})\approx\mathbf{J_{T}}+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}(\mathbf{H}_{T}\mathbf{H}_{T-1}..\mathbf{H}_{t+1})\mathbf{J_{t}}.

(6)

In the unrolling based approaches, a chain of sub-gradients are obtained for the unrolled iterations. For example,
[
39
]
differentiates the iterations of a non-linear primal primal-dual algorithm. Depending on the sequence order of calculating the sum in the implicit gradient (
6
), unrolling methods can be classified into forward and reverse mode. For the more common reverse mode
[
14
]
, the gradient computation starts from the
T
th step to calculate the product of
\mathbf{H}_{t}
’s. In each iteration,
\mathbf{H}_{t+1}
and
\mathbf{J}_{t}
are calculated. In doing so, for each iteration, the corresponding
{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
is stored in memory and the matrices
\mathbf{H}_{t+1}
and
\mathbf{J}_{t}
are calculated and stored in memory.

Hence, the memory cost for the unrolling methods using the reverse mode becomes
\mathcal{O}(Tn+n^{3})
. Also, unrolling for a large number of iterations may suffer from the problem of vanishing gradients.

As the reconstruction may not be accurate if the algorithm solving the lower-level problem is unrolled only for finite iterations, researchers have recently proposed Deep Equilibrium models (DEM)
[
22
]
. These models correspond to potentially infinite iterations of unrolling in the lower-level and hence may achieve better accuracy. DEM assumes that equation (
5
) has a fixed point
{\bm{x}}^{\infty}
such that a deep network or mapping
f
with parameters
\theta
can represent the fixed point as
{\bm{x}}^{\infty}=f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\infty})
. The network parameters are learned to minimize the loss
L=\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}^{\infty}\|_{2}^{2}
by an implicit differentiation of the fixed point equation
{\bm{x}}^{\infty}=f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\infty})
. This helps convert the memory intensive task of backpropagating through many iterations of
f_{\theta}(.)
to calculating an inverse Jacobian product, which can be further calculated by finding another approximate fixed point (solved by Neumann series). Hence, the task of finding the gradient of the loss
L
, i.e.,
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}
just boils down to three major steps each iteration: a) calculating the residual, b) solving a fixed point equation by Neumann series (assuming it converges), and c) multiplying gradient of network output
\frac{\partial f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\infty})}{\partial\theta}
with the fixed point solution obtained in (b). Similar to DEM, our approach BLORC can be thought of as
unrolling the lower-level optimization
to infinite iterations. This is because in BLORC, the lower-level is replaced by an implicit closed-form expression, just like the fixed point expression
{\bm{x}}^{\infty}=f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}^{\infty})
in DEM is implicit. The difference is that we solve the lower-level problem in its exact form for learning the transform
{\bm{W}}
rather than relying on the mapping
f_{\theta}
to
represent generic fixed points.

Differentiating through a transformed cone problem (Diffcp)
[
1
]

Every convex optimization problem including LASSO type problems (
2b
) can be converted to a cone program which deals with minimizing a linear function over the intersection of a subspace and convex cone. A solution map is defined to be a function mapping numerical data defining the problem to the primal or dual solution of the problem. For example, the lower-level problem can be written as an implicit solution map
\mathbf{G}
of the form
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})=\mathbf{G}({\bm{y}},\bm{\beta},{\bm{W}})
. The objective of differentiating through a cone problem is to calculate how the perturbations in the variable (
{\bm{W}}
) affect the solution
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
, i.e., calculating
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}}+\delta{\bm{W}})=\mathbf{G}({\bm{y}},\bm{\beta},{\bm{W}}+\delta{\bm{W}})
. This is done by decomposing the function
\mathbf{G}
into a composition of differentiable atomic functions and then representing the derivative of each atomic function as an abstract linear map
[
1
]
by a method called Automatic Differentiation (AD). For a LASSO type problem, there exist points in the numerical data domain (
{\bm{W}}
) on which the problem is non-differentiable. On these points, AD libraries compute heuristic quantities instead of derivatives
[
1
]
.
The drawbacks of autodifferentiation methods over BLORC are:

1.

In AD approaches, the task is divided into a sequence of differentiable operations as computational graphs on which backpropagation is performed through chain rule
[
36
]
. This division into a sequence of operations and calculating gradients for each node of the graph can utilize significant memory.
3
3

3

For AD approaches, the memory requirement is dynamic, depending on the implementation of the expression graphs. Memory optimizing strategies
[
36
]
like retaping, checkpoints, and region-based memory aim to increase memory efficiency.
. This can be avoided if there is an analytical explicit expression for gradients.

2.

Hand-coded analytical method of calculating gradient is known to be the most accurate method
[
7
]
as it calculates exact derivatives. Hand-coded gradient methods are also known to have less “Gradient to Function Compute Time Ratio" than AD methods (see Figure 1 in
[

]
). To further validate this, we perform an experiment in Section

to compare BLORC with the Diffcp implementation in terms of accuracy and time complextiy.

3
Methods

To tackle the bilevel optimization problem, we proceed with the following steps:

1.

An implicit closed-form expression of the lower-level problem is derived using KKT stationarity conditions. We will show both theoretically (in some special cases) and experimentally that in local neighborhoods of the transform, we get an explicit closed-form expression.

2.

We take the differential of the locally explicit closed-form expression, and using matrix algebra
[
37
]
, explicit gradient expressions of the upper-level loss w.r.t. the rows of the transform are obtained. This helps us to avoid explicitly calculating and storing the implicit gradient
\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
. Hence for BLORC, the memory cost is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
(
1
) which involves just storing the gradient of upper-level loss (
\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}Q
) each iteration.

3.

Using the analytical gradient expression, mini-batch gradient descent is performed.

It is important to note two things in the bilevel problem (
2
):

a

No constraints are imposed on the matrix
{\bm{W}}
while learning. This lets us learn more general operators.

b

We let the algorithm learn the scaling of the regularization penalty and hence we do not require a separate scalar regularization strength to be learned. (
\beta||{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}||_{1}=||(\beta{\bm{W}}){\bm{x}}||_{1})
). Thus, the lower level problem does not contain a scalar regularization parameter.

3.1
Closed-form solution obtained by duality

Consider the lower-level functional

{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{W}},{\bm{y}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}\|_{1},

(7)

with
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and

{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
.
It is strictly convex in
{\bm{x}}

(because the
\ell_{2}
norm term is strictly convex
and the
\ell_{1}
norm term is convex)
and therefore has a unique global minimizer.
Thus we are justified in writing
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})=\argmin_{\bm{x}}{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{W}},{\bm{y}})

without the possibility of
the minimizer not existing or being a nonsingleton. Note that although
{\bm{x}}^{*}
depends on
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{W}}
,
the
{\bm{y}}
-dependence is not relevant for this derivation
and
we will not notate it explicitly.
Our key observation in deriving the closed-form expression of (
7
) is that we need to know the sign pattern of
{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
. So, the closed-form expression is an implicit equation where the reconstruction
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
is itself dependent on
\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*})
(where
[\sign({\mathbf{z}})]_{i}

is defined to be -1 when
[{\mathbf{z}}]_{i}<0
;
0 when
[{\mathbf{z}}]_{i}=0
;
and 1 when
[{\mathbf{z}}]_{i}>0
).
Let
k_{=0}
denote the set {
i

\in
(1,2,3,..,k)  :
({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*})_{i}=0
} and
k_{\neq 0}
denotes {
i

\in
(1,2,3,..,k)  :
({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*})_{i}\neq 0
}.
Also, let us define
\bm{W}_{0}
as the matrix containing the rows of
{\bm{W}}
that are indexed by the set
k_{=0}
and
\bm{W}_{\pm}
contains rows of
{\bm{W}}
that are indexed by the set
k_{\neq 0}
. Then we have the following theorem. A similar closed-form expression has been obtained in equation (33) in
[
52
]
using a different method.

Theorem 1
(Closed-form expression for
\argmin_{\bm{x}}{\mathcal{J}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{W}})
).

Let the nonzero pattern
{\bm{s}}
denote
\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*})_{{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*}\neq 0}
and let
\bm{W}_{0}
,
\bm{W}_{\pm}
contain the rows of
{\bm{W}}
, whose indices are given by the sets
k_{=0}
and
k_{\neq 0}
, respectively.
Then the closed-form expression of the optimization problem
{\bm{x}}^{*}=\arg\min_{{\bm{x}}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}\|_{1}
is obtained from Lagrangian dual analysis as

{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})={\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{N}(\bm{W}_{0})}({\bm{y}}-\bm{W}_{\pm}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\bm{s}}),

(8)

where
{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{N}(\bm{W}_{0})}
is the projector matrix onto the nullspace of
\bm{W}_{0}
and is given by
{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{N}(\bm{W}_{0})}=(\bm{I}-\bm{W}_{0}^{+}\bm{W}_{0})
.

To obtain the closed-form expression for a particular point on the domain of
{\bm{W}}
, we solve the lower-level problem (
2b
) to obtain the reconstruction
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
. This is because
\bm{W}_{\pm},\bm{W}_{0},{\bm{s}}
in (
8
) depend on
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
. One may ask the question of why should we use an implicit closed-form equation if we can find the reconstruction by methods like ADMM or PGD? But we re-emphasize that our objective in this step is not to find
{\bm{x}}^{*}
but to obtain a closed-form expression that allows us to take gradients with respect to
{\bm{W}}
.

3.2
Local analysis of the closed-form expression

Generalized LASSO-type problems do not have an explicit closed-form solution
[
52
]
. In fact, the closed-form expression we derived in (
8
) is an implicit equation. Note that
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
is a function of the sign pattern
c({\bm{W}})=\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}}))
because the row-splits
\bm{W}_{0}
and
\bm{W}_{\pm}
are based on
c({\bm{W}})
. But again the sign pattern
c({\bm{W}})
itself depends on
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
. The equation is implicit because
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
appears on both sides; however when
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
is restricted to lie in a certain region, the dependency on
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
in the right hand side may be dropped, making the equation explicit. Thus, if the sign-pattern
c({\bm{W}})
is constant in a neighborhood of
{\bm{W}}
, the equation (right-hand side) is explicit in the neighborhood. So, to take gradients of the expression in (
8
)
w.r.t.

{\bm{W}}
, it is important to establish the fact there exists an
\epsilon
-neighborhood

[\triangle{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}|\max(|\triangle{\bm{W}}|_{ij})\leq\epsilon]
such that
c({\bm{W}}+\triangle{\bm{W}})=c({\bm{W}})
. Only then, we can treat the equation (
8
) as an explicit equation w.r.t.
{\bm{W}}
in an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
{\bm{W}}
. For clarification, we do not assume this pattern is constant on the whole domain, but only in a neighbourhood and this allows us to derive gradient expressions.
Since there exists no explicit closed-form for
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
, it is difficult to prove that the sign-pattern remains constant in an
\epsilon
-neighborhood in general. So, we analyze two cases when
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
has an explicit closed-form expression.

3.2.1
The scalar denoising problem

Consider the scalar denoising problem

x^{*}(w)=\argmin_{x}\frac{1}{2}(x-y)^{2}+|wx|
and
c(w)=\sign(wx^{*}(w))
. The measurement is assumed to be generated as
y=x_{t}+\epsilon
, where
x_{t}
is the ground-truth.
Assuming that
y\geq 0
,
one can show that
x^{*}(w)=y-|w|
when
y-|w|\geq 0
and
0
otherwise.
As a result,
c((0,y))=1
,
c((-y,0))=-1
, and
c((-\infty,-y]\cup 0\cup[y,\infty))=0
is piecewise constant. A similar result holds when
y\leq 0
.
Thus
{\mathcal{Q}}(w)=(x^{*}(w)-x_{t})^{2}
is smooth except at
w=0,-y,y
. Note that
{\mathcal{Q}}(w)
is differentiable in the intervals near the
minimizers of
{\mathcal{Q}}
and the non-differentiable points form a set of zero measure.

Figure 1
:
The sign pattern
c(w)=\sign(wx^{*}(w))
is piecewise constant for the scalar denoising problem. Also, the upper-level cost
Q(w)
is piecewise smooth with respect to
w
. Note that its non-differentiable points are also not local/global minimizers.

3.2.2
Denoising problem under orthogonal constraint

Consider the denoising problem
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{*}({\bm{W}})=\underset{x}{\arg\min}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda||{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}||_{1}
but under an orthogonal constraint on (square)

{\bm{W}}
(lying on the Stiefel manifold), i.e.,
{\bm{W}}{\bm{W}}^{T}=\mathbf{I}
. It is easy to show that the solution to this optimization problem is given by
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{*}({\bm{W}})={\bm{W}}^{T}\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}({\bm{W}}{\bm{y}}_{t})
where
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(.)
denotes the soft-thresholding function with parameter
\lambda
. Then the sign-vector
c_{t}({\bm{W}})=\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{W}}^{T}\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}({\bm{W}}{\bm{y}}_{t}))=\sign(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}({\bm{W}}{\bm{y}}_{t}))
. Considering each of the indices,
[c_{t}({\bm{W}})]_{i}
is piecewise constant except where
|({\bm{W}}{\bm{y}}_{t})_{i}|=\lambda
. Hence,
c_{t}({\bm{W}})
is piecewise constant in each of the coordinates except at points where
|({\bm{W}}{\bm{y}}_{t})_{i}|=\lambda
. We show in Section
7.3
that these points form a set of Lebesgue measure zero over the Stiefel manifold. Thus, the upper level cost function
Q({\bm{W}})
can be seen to be a piecewise smooth function over orthogonal
{\bm{W}}
matrices except on a set of measure zero.

3.2.3
Experimental validation for constant sign pattern

In the generalized setting for
{\bm{W}}
, the denoising problem does not have a closed-form expression. So, to experimentally check our claim, we perform an experiment consisting of the following steps:

1.

Setting
{\bm{W}}=\mathbf{I}
, the lower-level denoising problem is solved using a noisy signal
{\bm{y}}
to obtain
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
and hence using that to obtain
c({\bm{W}})=\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}}))
.

2.

Perturbing
{\bm{W}}
in an
\ell_{\infty}
radius ball, we obtain the sign-pattern in a
neighborhood of
{\bm{W}}
as
c({\bm{W}}+\triangle{\bm{W}})=\sign(({\bm{W}}+\triangle{\bm{W}}){\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}}+\triangle{\bm{W}}))
such that
\max(|\triangle{\bm{W}}|_{ij})\leq\eta
for small
\eta>0
.

3.

We then checked if
c({\bm{W}})=c({\bm{W}}+\triangle{\bm{W}})
. Our experiments showed that there always exists a
\triangle{\bm{W}}
with any
\eta=10^{-8}
(machine precision is
10^{-16}
) for which
c({\bm{W}})=c({\bm{W}}+\triangle{\bm{W}})
. For a particular point on the domain of
{\bm{W}}
, we performed thousands of trials of generating
\triangle{\bm{W}}
with
\eta=10^{-8}
, and everytime
c({\bm{W}})=c({\bm{W}}+\triangle{\bm{W}})
was satisfied. We also performed the experiment with
{\bm{W}}
being a 1D-finite difference matrix, a zero matrix, and also a random matrix with its entries drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and also with different measurements (
{\bm{y}}
) and observed similar results.

In the experiments above, we chose the measurement
{\bm{y}}
to have length
64
and the matrix dimensions were
64\times 64
.

3.3
Gradient calculations

Next, we compute the
gradient of the scalar
Q({\bm{W}})
with respect to
{\bm{W}}

using locally explicit closed-form expressions for the lower level problems.
Second, for the gradients to exist for a specific training signal’s loss, the sign pattern vector
\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*})
has to remain constant in an open set containing
{\bm{W}}
. Only then the closed-form expression for
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})

is valid in each region where
\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*})
is constant.

We refer the readers to

[
37
]
, which summarizes how to generate derivatives (Jacobian) of scalars with respect to matrices using differentials. We briefly summarize how we obtain gradients of the upper level scalar loss
Q({\bm{W}})
with respect to sub-rows (
\bm{W}_{0},\bm{W}_{\pm}
) of the transform
{\bm{W}}
using differentials.

We denote the Jacobian of the scalar
Q
w.r.t.
{\bm{W}}
as
\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}Q
, which has the same dimensions as
{\bm{W}}
as it is just
[\frac{\partial Q}{\partial w_{ij}}]
where
w_{ij}
is the element on the
i
th row and
j
th column of matrix
{\bm{W}}
. The differential of the scalar
\partial Q({\bm{W}})
is defined as
\partial Q({\bm{W}})=Q({\bm{W}}+\partial{\bm{W}})-Q({\bm{W}})
, where
\partial{\bm{W}}
is the differential of the matrix
{\bm{W}}
, i.e.,
\partial{\bm{W}}=[\partial w_{ij}]_{i,j}
, and has the same dimension as
{\bm{W}}
.

From
[
37
]
, we see that if we can write
Q({\bm{W}}+\partial{\bm{W}})
as
Q({\bm{W}})+\Tr({\mathbf{E}}^{T}\partial{\bm{W}})
, then the Jacobian

\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}Q={\mathbf{E}}
. Note that the canonical form
\Tr({\mathbf{E}}^{T}\partial{\bm{W}})
is important to take the derivative of a scalar with respect to a matrix. So, a general rule of thumb to calculate such derivative of scalar w.r.t. matrix is

1.

Compute the differential relating the scalar (
\partial Q
) and the matrix
\partial{\bm{W}}
.

2.

Rewrite the result into the canonical form
\partial Q({\bm{W}})=\Tr({\mathbf{E}}^{T}\partial{\bm{W}})
to derive the Jacobian
\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}Q={\mathbf{E}}
.
4
4

4

When we use the term differential
\partial Q
,
\partial{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
or
\partial{\bm{W}}
(same dimensions as
Q
,
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
, and
{\bm{W}}
, respectively), we just mean the incremental changes in the scalar, vector, or matrix.
A relationship such as
\partial Q=\Tr({\mathbf{E}}^{T}\partial{\bm{W}})
indicates how the scalar
Q
changes for incremental changes in
{\bm{W}}
.
And when we use
\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}Q
, we mean the derivative of the scalar
Q
w.r.t.
{\bm{W}}
.
And the derivative can be obtained from the differential by writing it in the canonical form. That is if we have a differential relation
\partial Q({\bm{W}})=\Tr({\mathbf{E}}^{T}\partial{\bm{W}})
, then the derivative
\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}Q={\mathbf{E}}
.

For our problem, we don’t have a direct relationship between
Q
and
{\bm{W}}
to get a differential relation like
\partial Q({\bm{W}})=\Tr({\mathbf{E}}^{T}\partial{\bm{W}})
. But rather the relation between
\partial Q
and
\partial{\bm{W}}
is connected by an intermediate differential variable
\partial{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
.
\partial{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
is the differential of the reconstruction in the lower-level problem which is a function of the transform
{\bm{W}}
.
Since we have already defined a locally explicit expression for
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
in (
8
), we can relate

\partial{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
and
\partial{\bm{W}}
using (
8
) as discussed next. The proof for the following theorem is discussed in Section
7
.

To answer this question, we first perform several experiments to learn the transform matrix
{\bm{W}}
with piecewise constant training signal pairs
({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}

{\bm{x}}_{t}

{\bm{x}}_{t}
’s values are from
0
to

) and performed the BLORC experiment to obtain the learned transform
{\bm{W}}
for each of the noise levels.
The learned transforms for the different noise levels are shown in Figure
7
. For all the 8 experiments, the transform was initialized to be
{\bm{W}}=\mathbf{I}
, and we see in Figure
7
that except for
\sigma=0.5\ \text{and}\ 0.7
,
{\bm{W}}
converged to something very close to the finite-differences matrix (lets call it
{\bm{W}}^{*}
).

Now consider the noiseless case (
\sigma=0
), for which we have
{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{y}}_{t}
. From Figure
7
, we observe that the finite-difference matrix (
{\bm{W}}^{*}
) is a minimizer for the upper-level cost

It is also easy to observe that
{\bm{W}}=\mathbf{0}

{\bm{W}}
, the upper level loss (

) reaches its minimum value, i.e., zero.

The problem gets interesting when the noise level (standard deviation) is increased beyond

. Note that for
{\bm{W}}=\mathbf{0}
, the upper level loss is roughly
\sigma^{2}
, i.e., linear in the noise variance. This is obvious since the lower level reconstruction is just

Thus, with higher noise levels, the zero matrix would not be expected to remain a local minimizer anymore as the upper level loss increases drastically with increase in

However, we could expect
{\bm{W}}^{*}

It is noteworthy that beyond noise level

{\bm{W}}

\sigma=0.5

Another interesting observation in Figure

With increasing noise-level, reconstructing from the noisy signal becomes increasingly difficult, hence the regularization term is learned to take more weight than the data-fidelity term to achieve a more stable reconstruction in the lower level problem. Even at significant noise levels like
\sigma=0.2
(which is
20\%
of the peak intensity level), BLORC manages to learn a transform
{\bm{W}}
close to the finite-differences matrix
{\bm{W}}^{*}
.

in Figure

{\bm{W}}

7.1
Obtaining the closed-form: Proof for Theorem
1

We propose to solve signal reconstruction problems using a sparsifying analysis operator that is learned from training data in a supervised way.
Our learning problem is

\argmin_{\bm{W}}{\mathcal{Q}}({\bm{W}}),\text{\quad where \quad}{\mathcal{Q}}({\bm{W}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}_{t}^{*}({\bm{W}},{\bm{y}}_{t})-{\bm{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2},

(14a)

where

{\bm{x}}_{t}
and
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the
t
th training signal and its corresponding noise-corrupted version,
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a sparsifying operator we intend to learn with the help of the corresponding lower level problem,

\quad{\bm{x}}_{t}^{*}({\bm{W}},{\bm{y}}_{t})=\argmin_{\bm{x}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\|{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}\|_{1}.

(14b)

Let
\bm{c}({\bm{W}})
denote the sign pattern
associated with a given
{\bm{W}}
,
i.e.,

\bm{c}({\bm{W}})=\sign({\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}}))
,
where
[\sign({\mathbf{z}})]_{i}

is defined to be -1 when
[{\mathbf{z}}]_{i}<0
;
0 when
[{\mathbf{z}}]_{i}=0
;
and 1 when
[{\mathbf{z}}]_{i}>0
. Note that we omit the
t
-dependence from
{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
as it is not relevant for the derivation.
Considering a fixed sign-pattern
\bm{c}_{0}=\bm{c}({\bm{W}})
,
we define matrices that pull out the rows
of
{\bm{W}}
that give rise to zero, negative, and positive values in
{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
.
Let
k_{=0}
,
k_{\neq 0}
,
k_{<0}
, and
k_{>0}
denote the number of
zero, nonzero, negative, and positive elements of
\bm{c}
, respectively.
Similarly, let
[\bm{\pi}_{0}]_{m}
,
[\bm{\pi}_{<0}]_{m}
, and
[\bm{\pi}_{>0}]_{m}

denote the indices of the
m
th zero, negative, and positive element of
\bm{c}_{0}
, respectively.
Let
\bm{S}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{=0}\times k}
and
\bm{S}_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\neq 0}\times k}

be defined as

[\bm{S}_{0}]_{m,n}=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if}\quad[\pi_{0}]_{m}=n;\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise};\end{cases}\text{\quad and \quad}[\bm{S}_{\pm}]_{m,n}=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if}\quad[\pi_{>0}]_{m}=n;\\
-1&\quad\text{if}\quad[\pi_{<0}]_{m-k_{>0}}=n;\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(15)

The nonzero sign pattern is
{\bm{s}}=\bm{S}_{\pm}\bm{c}_{0}
. Let
\bm{W}_{0}=\bm{S}_{0}{\bm{W}}
and
\bm{W}_{\pm}=\bm{S}_{\pm}{\bm{W}}
contain the rows of
{\bm{W}}
, whose indices are given by the sets
k_{=0}
and
k_{\neq 0}
, respectively.
With these notations in place, we can write

{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})=\argmin_{\bm{x}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\|{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{1},\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{W}_{0}{\bm{x}}=\bm{0}

(16)

for all
{\bm{W}}
such that
\bm{c}({\bm{W}})=\bm{c}_{0}
.
This is true because
whenever
\bm{c}({\bm{W}})=\bm{c}_{0}
,
the minimizer of (
7
)
is feasible for (
16
).
Similarly,
we use
\bm{W}_{\pm}
to simplify the

norm term,

{\bm{x}}^{*}({\bm{W}})=\argmin_{\bm{x}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta{\bm{s}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{W}_{\pm}\bm{x},\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{W}_{0}{\bm{x}}=\bm{0},

(17)

which holds again for all
{\bm{W}}
such that
\bm{c}({\bm{W}})=\bm{c}_{0}
.

Now that we have transformed the problem into an equality-constrained quadratic minimization,

This section analyses the closed-form expression for the denoising problem obtained on the Stiefel manifold (under the orthogonal constraint)

It is easy to see that in

8
Conclusions

This paper presented an approach for supervised learning of sparsity-promoting nonsmooth (
\ell_{1}
) regularizers for denoising problems.
The underlying training problem is a challenging bilevel optimization problem, where the upper level loss is a task-based one (e.g., mean squared error for denoising) and the lower level problem is a variational problem for denoising, whose solution is used in the upper level loss.
Our approach, BLORC, allows learning the sparsifying operator in the lower level problem by exploiting local closed-form expressions for the solution of the variational problem.
The closed-form expressions enable computing gradients directly and efficiently with respect to the operator parameters.
Experimental results show the ability of BLORC to learn underlying sparsifying operators robustly in the presence of noise for both 1D signals and 2D images.
For images, the proposed approach outperforms recent denoising schemes, including unsupervised analysis dictionary learning.
In future work, we plan to extend the approach to handle non-unique lower-level minimizers and extend to
other inverse problems with complex-valued images such as in magnetic resonance imaging.
