Polynomial kernels for edge modification problems towards block and strictly chordal graphs Thanks: A preliminary version of this work appeared in IPEC’21 ( 21 ) . All results concerning Block Graph Editing are new to this submission.
Maël Dumas, Anthony Perez, Mathis Rocton, Ioan Todinca

Abstract
We consider edge modification problems towards block and strictly chordal graphs, where one is given an undirected graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) and an integer k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} and seeks to edit (add or delete) at most k k edges from G G to obtain a block graph or a strictly chordal graph. The completion and deletion variants of these problems are defined similarly by only allowing edge additions for the former and only edge deletions for the latter. Block graphs are a well-studied class of graphs and admit several characterizations, e.g. they are diamond-free chordal graphs. Strictly chordal graphs, also referred to as block duplicate graphs , are a natural generalization of block graphs where one can add true twins of cut-vertices. Strictly chordal graphs are exactly dart and gem-free chordal graphs. We prove the NP-completeness for most variants of these problems and provide: • O ⁡ ( k 2 ) O(k^{2}) vertex-kernels for Block Graph Editing and Block Graph Deletion • O ⁡ ( k 3 ) O(k^{3}) vertex-kernels for Strictly Chordal Completion and Strictly Chordal Deletion • an O ⁡ ( k 4 ) O(k^{4}) vertex-kernel for Strictly Chordal Editing

Introduction

Parameterized algorithms are among the most natural approaches to tackle NP-hard optimization problems
(
14
)
.
In particular, they have been very successful in dealing with so-called edge modification problems on graphs: given as input an arbitrary graph
G=(V,E)
and a parameter
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the goal is to transform
G
into a graph with some specific properties (i.e., belonging to a specific graph class
\mathcal{G}
) by adding and/or deleting at most
k
edges. Parameterized algorithms (also called FPT for
fixed parameter tractable
) aim at a time complexity of type
f(k)\cdot n^{O(1)}
, where
f
is some computable function, hence the combinatorial explosion is restricted to parameter
k
.

When the target class
\mathcal{G}
is characterized by a finite family of forbidden induced subgraphs, modification problems are FPT by a result of Cai
(
9
)
. Indeed, as long as the graph contains one of the forbidden subgraphs, one can try each possibility to correct this obstruction and branch by recursive calls. On each branch, the budget
k
is strictly diminished, therefore the whole algorithm has a number of calls bounded by some function
f(k)
. The situation is more complicated when the target class
\mathcal{G}
is characterized by an infinite family of forbidden induced subgraphs. Nonetheless, a large literature is devoted to edge modification problems towards chordal graphs (where we forbid all induced cycles with at least four vertices) as well as sub-classes of chordal graphs, typically obtained by requiring some fixed set of obstructions, besides the long cycles. Observe that, in this case, the situation remains relatively simple if we restrict ourselves to
edge completion
problems, where we are only allowed to
add
edges to the input graphs. Indeed, in this case, if a graph has an induced cycle of length longer than
k+3
, it cannot be made chordal by adding at most
k
edges. Therefore we can use again the approach of Cai to deal with cycles of length at most
k+3
and other obstructions, and either the algorithm finds a solution in
f(k)
recursive calls, or we can conclude that we have a no-instance. The cases of
edge deletion
problems (where we are only allowed to remove edges) and
edge editing
problems (where we are allowed to both remove edges or add missing edges) are more complicated, since even long cycles can be eliminated by a single edge removal. Therefore more efforts and more sophisticated techniques were necessary in these situations, but several such problems turned out to be FPT
(
11
,
16
,
17
)
.
The interested reader can refer to
(
12
)
for a broad and comprehensive survey on parameterized algorithms for edge modification problems.

We focus here on a sub-family of parameterized algorithms, namely on
kernelization
. The goal of kernelization is to provide a polynomial algorithm transforming any instance
(I,k)
of the problem into an equivalent instance
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
where
k^{\prime}
is upper bounded by some function of
k
(in our case we will simply have
k^{\prime}\leqslant k
), and the size of the new instance
I^{\prime}
is upper bounded by some function
g(k)
. Hence the size of the reduced instance does not depend on the size of the original instance. Kernels are obtained through a set of
reduction rules
.
While kernelization is possible for all FPT problems (the two notions are actually equivalent),
the interesting question is whether a given FPT problem admits
polynomial kernels
, where the size of the reduced instance is bounded by some polynomial in
k
. Note that, under some complexity assumptions, not all FPT problems admit polynomial kernels
(
5
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
25
,
33
)
.

In this paper we focus on

and all three
variants of modification problems towards strictly chordal graphs.

They are moreover the graphs in which every biconnected component induces a clique. Notice that this observation allows for a simple polynomial-time algorithm for

Strictly chordal
graphs are another subclass of chordal graphs, also known as

(

31

. They can be obtained from block graphs by repeatedly choosing some cut-vertex

and all neighbors of

(

and next section) and are thus ptolemaic (
i.e.
chordal distance-hereditary
(
8
)
).
Strictly chordal graphs are also known to be a subclass of 4-leaf power graphs
(

, and a super-class of 3-leaf power graphs
(

Very informally, the target class

admits a tree-like decomposition, in the sense that the vertices of any graph

can be partitioned into clique modules (having the same neighborhood in
H

G

2k

G
in a very regular way, through one or two nodes of the decomposition tree. The kernelization algorithms need to analyze these chunks and provide reduction rules, typically by ensuring a small number of nodes in the decomposition tree, plus the fact that each node corresponds to a module of small size. However, finding a general framework that captures this particular decomposition does not seem to be an easy task as each class exhibits particular structural properties that may need to be dealt with independently.

As mentioned earlier, when restricted to completion problems any input graph cannot have large induced cycles and this property may be exploited to design kernelization algorithms. This observation lead to the following conjecture by Bessy and Perez

Several other questions remain open, for example it is not known whether
chordal deletion
or

.

Our contribution

We first prove that most variants of the considered problems are NP-Complete, the
only exception being the
Block Graph Completion
problem that admits a polynomial-time algorithm.
Secondly, we give kernelization algorithms for all these problems, with different
bounds.
First of all, we begin by illustrating the techniques we will use on the
Block Graph Editing
problem and obtain the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Block Graph Editing
and
Block Graph Deletion
admit a kernel with
O(k^{2})
vertices.

Next, we present kernelization algorithms for the
Strictly Chordal Editing
problem and obtain better vertex-kernels for both
Strictly Chordal Completion
and
Strictly Chordal Deletion
.

Theorem 2
.

Strictly Chordal Editing
admits a kernel with
O(k^{4})
vertices.

Theorem 3
.

Strictly Chordal Completion
and
Strictly Chordal Deletion
admit kernels with
O(k^{3})
vertices.

Above all, our purpose is to exhibit general techniques that might, we hope, be extended to kernelizations for edge modification problems towards other graph classes. In particular, unlike the general strategy we previously described, both the classes of block graphs and of strictly chordal graphs do not have exactly a tree-like decomposition. Still, they can be decomposed into structures than can be seen as a generalization of a tree. Our algorithms exploit these informal observations and provide the necessary reduction rules together with the combinatorial analysis for the kernel size.
Finally, we note that while both kernels use structures and rules that are similar, the ones for
Block Graph Editing
are significantly simpler, hence the kernel for this problem is a nice warm-up for the one for
Strictly Chordal Editing
. Moreover, even though reduction rules for
Strictly Chordal Editing
may be directly adapted for
Block Graph Editing
, it would lead to a significantly larger kernel.

Lemma 2

Let

, it is clear that it does not intersect any minimal separator of
G_{i}
. If
S_{i}
is a critical clique, we claim that either
S_{i}
is a minimal separator of
G_{i}
or does not intersect any minimal separator of
G_{i}
. Indeed, if there is a minimal (clique) separator
S
of
G_{i}
such that
S_{i}\subset S
, then since
S_{i}
is a critical clique, there exist
u\in S_{i},v\in S\setminus S_{i}
and
w\in V(G_{i})\setminus S
such that
w
is adjacent to exactly one of
u
and
v
. Suppose w.l.o.g. that
w
is adjacent to
u
and not to
v
, then
u
is in a minimal
vw
-separator that intersects
S
and is not equal to it. This is a contradiction since
G_{i}
is strictly chordal and by
Theorem

4
its minimal separators are pairwise disjoint. It follows that the minimal separators of
G
are pairwise disjoint and by
Theorem

4

G
is strictly chordal.
∎

Spanning block subgraph

The following result is crucial to bound the size of the kernels for all variants of the studied modification problems. For a graph
G
, let
d(G)
be the set of vertices of degree at least
3
in
G
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected block graph,
A
be a subset of
V(G)
and
T_{A}
be a minimal connected induced subgraph of
G
that spans all vertices of
A
. Denote by
d(T_{A})
the set of vertices of degree at least
3
in
T_{A}
. The following holds:

(i)

The subgraph
T_{A}
is unique,

(ii)

|d(T_{A})|\leqslant 3\cdot|A|
,

(iii)

The graph
T_{A}\setminus(A\cup d(T_{A}))
contains at most
2\cdot|A|
connected components.

Proof.

We first prove the uniqueness of
T_{A}
. Observe that the internal vertices of a shortest path
P
in
G
between two vertices
u
and
v
are cut-vertices of
G
, since it is a block graph. Moreover, for any such internal vertex
w
of
P
, vertices
u
and
v
are in different components of
G-w
.
Therefore,
P
is unique, and any path between
u
and
v
must contain every vertex of
P
. Hence, the vertices of
T_{A}
are either in
A
or separating vertices of
A
, and the uniqueness of
T_{A}
follows, proving
Item

i
.

To prove
Item

ii
we will proceed by induction on the size of
A
.
For
|A|\leqslant 2
we have that
|d(T_{A})|=0
. Suppose now that
|A|\geqslant 3
.
Let
a\in A
be a non-cut-vertex of
T_{A}
. Such a vertex exists, e.g., it is sufficient to choose a longest path of
T_{A}
, and let
a
be one of the endpoints. Then
a
is not a cut vertex of
T_{A}
(as the end of a longest path), and
a\in A
by minimality of
T_{A}
. Notice moreover that
a
is in only one maximal clique of
T_{A}
.
Let
A^{\prime}=A\setminus\{a\}
and let
T_{A^{\prime}}
be a minimal connected induced subgraph of
G
that spans all vertices of
A^{\prime}
.
Observe that
T_{A^{\prime}}
is an induced subgraph of
T_{A}
.
We show that
|d(T_{A})|\leqslant|d(T_{A^{\prime}})|+3
.
Since
T_{A}
is connected, there exists a path in
T_{A}
from
a
to a vertex
u\in V(T_{A})\setminus V(T_{A^{\prime}})
adjacent to some vertices of
T_{A^{\prime}}
(
a=u
is possible), moreover
u
is unique.
Any vertex on the path except for
u
is of degree at most
2
in
T_{A}
.
Let
N=N_{G}(u)\cap V(T_{A^{\prime}})
and note that since
T_{A^{\prime}}
is a connected block graph,
N
is a clique.
By construction, the vertices of
T_{A^{\prime}}
have the same degree in
T_{A}
and
T_{A^{\prime}}
, except for the ones in
N
.
Since
u
might be of degree
3
or more, if
|N|\leqslant 2
then
|d(T_{A})|\leqslant|d(T_{A^{\prime}})|+3
.
If
|N|\geqslant 4
, then all vertices in
N
already have degree
3
in
T_{A^{\prime}}
, hence
|d(T_{A})|=|d(T_{A^{\prime}})|+1
.
If
|N|=3
and
N
contains a vertex of degree
3
in
T_{A^{\prime}}
then
|d(T_{A})|\leqslant|d(T_{A^{\prime}})|+3
.
Otherwise, we claim that
V(T_{A^{\prime}})=N
. Indeed, if all vertices in
N
have degree
2
in
T_{A^{\prime}}
,
the only possibility is that
N
is exactly the vertex set of
T_{A^{\prime}}
, because
T_{A^{\prime}}
is connected,
N
induces a clique of size
3
in this graph and no vertex of
N
has other neighbors than the ones in
N
.
In this case we have
|A|=4
and
|d(T_{A})|=4
, which satisfies
Item

ii
.
Therefore, except for the latter case, we have
|d(T_{A})|\leqslant|d(T_{A^{\prime}})|+3
, and using the induction hypothesis we have
|d(T_{A})|\leqslant|d(T_{A^{\prime}})|+3\leqslant 3\cdot|A^{\prime}|+3=3\cdot|A|
.

We are left with
Item

iii
, which can be proven in a similar way as the previous one. Using the same notation,
the only vertices of
T_{A^{\prime}}
whose degree may increase in
T_{A}
are in
N
. Therefore, every connected component of
T_{A^{\prime}}\setminus(A^{\prime}\cup d(T_{A^{\prime}}))
that does not contain vertices of
N
is also a connected component of
T_{A}\setminus(A\cup d(T_{A}))
. Moreover, a connected component of
T_{A^{\prime}}\setminus(A^{\prime}\cup d(T_{A^{\prime}}))
may contain vertices of
N
only if these vertices have degree at most
2
in
T_{A^{\prime}}
and are not in
A
. In particular
|N|\leqslant 2
and both vertices lie in the same connected component of
T_{A^{\prime}}\setminus(A^{\prime}\cup d(T_{A^{\prime}}))
(if any).
Therefore, this connected component may be split in two disjoint ones in
T_{A}\setminus(A\cup d(T_{A}))
if the vertices of
N
are removed. Moreover, an additional connected component corresponding to the path between
a
and
u
may exist in
T_{A}
. Hence, we can only increase the number of connected components of
T_{A}\setminus(A\cup d(T_{A}))
by compared to
T_{A^{\prime}}\setminus(A^{\prime}\cup d(T_{A^{\prime}}))
. Since the property is verified for
|A|=1
, it follows by induction that there are
2\cdot|A|
connected components in
T_{A}\setminus(A\cup d(T_{A}))
.
∎

2.1
Hardness results

We first show the NP-completeness of
Block Graph Deletion
and
Block Graph Editing
by giving a reduction from
Cluster Deletion
and
Cluster Editing
, known to be NP-complete
(
34
,
39
,
36
)
. Notice that
Block Graph Completion
admits
a polynomial-time algorithm due to the very nature of block graphs:
any biconnected component of the input graph (computable in linear time
(
28
)
) must be turned into a clique.

Lemma 6
.

Block Graph Deletion
and
Block Graph Editing
are NP-Complete.

Proof.

We first prove that
Block Graph Deletion
is NP-Complete by providing a reduction from
Cluster Deletion
. A graph is a cluster graph if it does not contain any induced path on three vertices (so-called
P_{3}
). Given an instance
(G=(V,E),k)
of
Cluster Deletion
, we construct an instance of
Block Graph Deletion
by adding a universal vertex
u
adjacent to all vertices of
V
. Let
(G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),k)
be the produced instance.

We show that the graph
G
admits a
k
-deletion into a cluster graph if and only if
G^{\prime}
admits a
k
-deletion into a block graph. Suppose first that there is a
k
-deletion
F
of
G
into a cluster graph. The graph
G-F
is a graph without any
P_{3}
as induced subgraph. Now consider the graph
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}-F
. By construction
H^{\prime}[V]
contains no
P_{3}
, so
H^{\prime}
is chordal and contains no diamond, thus
H^{\prime}
is a block graph.

Now suppose that there exists a
k
-deletion
F^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
into a block graph.
We claim that either
F^{\prime}
does not affect the universal vertex
u
or we can construct a
k
-deletion
F^{*}
from
F^{\prime}
that does not affect
u
.
To support this claim, assume that
F^{\prime}
contains at least one edge incident to
u
.

First, suppose that
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}-F^{\prime}
is not connected. Let
C
be the connected component that contains
u
. For any other connected component
C^{\prime}
of
H^{\prime}
, take a vertex
v\in V(C^{\prime})
and construct the deletion set
F^{\prime\prime}=F^{\prime}\setminus\{(u,v)\}
. By
Lemma

3
,
(C,\{u\})\otimes(C^{\prime},\{v\})
is a block graph, and the other connected components of
H^{\prime}
are unchanged. Hence
G^{\prime}-F^{\prime\prime}
is a block graph and since
|F^{\prime\prime}|<|F^{\prime}|
,
F^{\prime\prime}
is a
k
-deletion. We iterate this construction until we get an intermediate solution
F^{*}_{1}
such that
H^{*}_{1}=G^{\prime}-F^{*}_{1}
is connected.

If
F^{*}_{1}
does not affect
u
we set
F^{*}=F^{*}_{1}
and we are done. Otherwise there is at least one vertex
v
not adjacent to
u
in
H^{*}_{1}
. Since
H^{*}_{1}
is connected we can choose
v
at distance exactly
2
from
u
. Since
H^{*}_{1}
is a block graph we have
N(u)\cap N(v)=\{w\}
. Consider the biconnected component
C
containing
v
and
w
,
and let
C^{\prime}=C\setminus\{w\}
. Since
H^{*}_{1}
is a block graph,
C
(and thus
C^{\prime}
) is a clique. Let
K_{u}
and
K_{v}
be the connected components of
H^{*}_{1}-(\{w\}\times C^{\prime})
that contain the vertices
u
and
v
respectively, they are block graphs by heredity. By
Lemma

3
the graph
H^{*}_{2}=(K_{u},\{u\})\otimes(K_{v},\{C^{\prime}\})
is a block graph. Observe that
(\{u\}\times C^{\prime})\subseteq F^{*}_{1}
since
u
is an universal vertex and let
F^{*}_{2}
be the deletion set such that
H^{*}_{2}=G^{\prime}-F^{*}_{2}
. By construction we can observe that
|F^{*}_{2}|=|F^{*}_{1}|
, hence
F^{*}_{2}
is a
k
-deletion of
G^{\prime}
into a block graph. We iterate this construction until we get a
k
-deletion
F^{*}
of
G
that does not affect
u
.

Finally,
H=G-F^{*}
does not contains any
P_{3}
or else there would be a diamond in
G^{\prime}-F^{*}
with the universal vertex
u
, thus
H
is a cluster graph.

The same reduction can be done for
Block Graph Editing
, one needs only to observe that there are no added edges incident to
u
, and we can use the same argument as above on the deleted edges: if there is no deleted edge incident to
u
we are done, otherwise we construct another set of deleted edges of equal size, but containing fewer edges incident to
u
.
If in the construction we have to delete an edge that was added by the edition, it is clear that removing this edge from the edition set result in a strictly smaller edition set.
∎

The NP-completeness of
Strictly Chordal Completion
follows directly from the proof of NP-completeness of
3-Leaf Power Completion
from
(
16
, Theorem 3)
.

Lemma 7
.

Strictly Chordal Completion
is NP-complete.

We show the NP-completeness of
Strictly Chordal Editing
and
Strictly Chordal Deletion
by giving a reduction from
Cluster Editing
and
Cluster Deletion
, respectively.

Lemma 8
.

Strictly Chordal Editing
and
Strictly Chordal Deletion
are NP-complete.

Proof.

Given an instance
(G=(V,E),k)
of
Cluster Editing
, we construct an instance of
Strictly Chordal Editing
by adding a clique
U=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k+1}\}
of size
k+1
adjacent to all vertices of
V
, and for each vertex
x
in
V
,
k+1
vertices
\{v^{x}_{1},\dots,v^{x}_{k+1}\}
adjacent only to
x
. Let
(G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),k)
be the produced instance.
We show that the graph
G
admits a
k
-edition into a cluster graph if and only if
G^{\prime}
admits a
k
-edition into a strictly chordal graph. Suppose first that there is a
k
-edition
F
of
G
into a cluster graph. The graph
G\triangle F
is a graph without any
P_{3}
as induced subgraph. Consider the graph
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\triangle F
. By construction
H^{\prime}[V]
contains no
P_{3}
, so
H^{\prime}
is chordal and contains neither gems nor darts since these obstructions contain an induced
P_{3}
and a vertex adjacent to every vertex of this
P_{3}
. By
Theorem

4
, it follows that
H^{\prime}
is strictly chordal. Now suppose that there exists a
k
-edition
F^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
into a strictly chordal graph
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\triangle F^{\prime}
.
We claim that
H=G\triangle F^{\prime}
is a cluster graph. By contradiction, suppose that
H=H^{\prime}[V]
contains a
P_{3}

\{x,y,z\}
where
x,z
are the ends of the path. Then, there exist
i,j\in\{1,\dots,k+1\}
such that
\{x,y,z,u_{i},v^{y}_{j}\}
forms a dart in
H^{\prime}
, contradicting that
H^{\prime}
is strictly chordal, and thus
H
is a cluster graph.

The same reduction can be done from
Cluster Deletion
to
Strictly Chordal Deletion
. This concludes the proof.
∎

3
Kernelization algorithm for
Block Graph Editing

We begin this section by providing a high-level description of our kernelization algorithm. A similar technique will be applied for modification problems towards
strictly chordal graphs but on the critical clique graph rather than the original one. Let us consider a positive instance
(G=(V,E),k)
of
Block Graph Editing
,
F
a suitable solution and
H=G\triangle F
. Since
|F|\leqslant k
, we know that at most
2k
vertices of
H
may be affected vertices.
Let
A
be the set of such vertices,
T
the minimum induced subgraph of
H
that spans all vertices of
A
and
A^{\prime}
the set of vertices of degree at least
3
in
T
.
From
Lemma

5
we have
|A^{\prime}\setminus A|\leqslant 3\cdot|A|
. We will define the notion of BG-branch, corresponding to subgraphs of
G
that induce block graphs. We will focus our interest on two types of BG-branches: the ones that are connected to the rest of the graph by only one (cut) vertex, called
1
-BG-branches
, and the ones that are connected to the rest of the graph by exactly two non-adjacent vertices, called
2
-BG-branches
. We will prove that we can keep only the cut-vertices of
1
-BG-branches and reduce the
2
-BG-branches containing more than
k+3
vertices.
We will see that there are two kinds of connected components remaining in the graph
H\setminus(A\cup A^{\prime})
, the ones adjacent to maximal cliques of vertices of
A\cup A^{\prime}
, corresponding to critical cliques (which can be bounded to a linear number of vertices) and the ones adjacent to two non-adjacent vertices of
A\cup A^{\prime}
(which correspond to
2
-BG-branches in
H
). Since
H
is a block graph, we know that there is at most
|A\cup A^{\prime}|=O(k)
maximal cliques of vertices of
A\cup A^{\prime}
. From
Lemma

5
we have that there are at most
4k
connected components in
T\setminus(A\cup A^{\prime})
, and each one corresponds to a
2
-BG-branch in the graph
H
. It remains that
H\setminus(A\cup A^{\prime})
contains a linear number of connected components, each one containing
O(k)
vertices. Altogether, the graph
H
contains
O(k^{2})
vertices.

3.1
Classical reduction rules

We first give classical reduction rules when dealing with modification problems. Notice that
Lemma

1
cannot be
directly applied to
Block Graph Editing
and
Block Graph Deletion
since block graphs are not closed under true twin addition. Hence one cannot directly reduce the size of critical cliques to
k+1
as mentioned
Section

2
. To circumvent this issue, we provide a slightly weaker reduction rule.

Rule 3.1
.

Let
C
be a block connected component of
G
.
Remove
V(C)
from
G
.

Rule 3.2
.

Let
K\subseteq V
be a set of true twins of
G
such that
|K|>2k+2
. Remove
|K|-(2k+2)
arbitrary vertices from
K
in
G
.

Lemma 9
.

3.1
and
3.2
are safe and can be computed in linear time.

Proof.

3.1
is safe since block graphs are hereditary and closed under disjoint union.

We now turn our attention to
3.2
. Let
G^{\prime}
denote the
graph obtained from the removal of
|K|-(2k+2)
arbitrary vertices from
K

in
G
and
K^{\prime}=V(G)\setminus V(G^{\prime})
.
Assume first that
G
admits a
k
-edition
F
. Observe that

F
is a
k
-edition of
G^{\prime}
by heredity. Conversely, assume that
G^{\prime}
admits
a
k
-edition
F
and let
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\triangle F
.
Notice that since
F
affects at most
2k
vertices and since

|K\setminus K^{\prime}|=2k+2
, there
exist two unaffected vertices
u
and
v
in
K\setminus K^{\prime}
.
We claim that
H=G\triangle F^{\prime}
is a block graph. Assume for a contradiction that
H
contains an obstruction
W
.
By construction,
W
must intersect
K^{\prime}
since otherwise
W
would exist in
H^{\prime}
. Moreover, notice that

|W\cap K^{\prime}|\leqslant 2
, otherwise
W
would contain a clique module
with
3
or
4
vertices, which is impossible in a cycle or
a diamond. Hence assume first that
|W\cap K^{\prime}|=\{a\}
: in this case, since
u
is unaffected we have
N_{H}[a]\setminus K^{\prime}=N_{H^{\prime}}[u]
and thus the
set
(W\setminus\{a\})\cup\{u\}
induces an obstruction in
H^{\prime}
, a contradiction. Similarly, if
|W\cap K^{\prime}|=\{a,b\}
the set

(W\setminus\{a,b\})\cup\{u,v\}
induces an obstruction in
H^{\prime}
, leading
once again to a contradiction.
Finally, since the biconnected components can be computed in
O(n+m)
time
(
28
)
, since block graphs can be recognized in
O(n+m)
time and since true twins can be detected in
O(n+m)
time (using modular decomposition
(
38
)
), both rules can be computed in linear time.
∎

3.2
Reducing Block Graph branches

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, a
BG-branch
is a connected induced subgraph
B
of
G
such that
B
is a block graph. A vertex
v\in V(B)
is an
attachment point
of
B
if
N_{G}(v)\setminus V(B)\neq\emptyset
. A BG-branch is a
p
-BG-branch if it has exactly
p
attachment points.
We denote
B^{R}
the induced subgraph of
B
in which all attachment points have been removed.
We first define a simple rule that allows to remove all the vertices of a
1
-BG-branch except for its attachment point.

Rule 3.3
.

Let
B
be a
1
-BG-branch of
G
such that
B^{R}
is connected and not empty. Remove
V(B^{R})
from
G
.

Lemma 10
.

3.3
is safe.

Proof.

Let
B
be such a
1
-BG-branch and
G^{\prime}=G\setminus V(B^{R})
.
Let
F
be a
k
-edition of
G
into a block graph.
Since the class of block graphs is hereditary,
(G\triangle F)\setminus V(B^{R})
is a block graph. It follows that
G^{\prime}\triangle F
is a block graph and
F
is a
k
-edition of
G^{\prime}
into a block graph.

Conversely, let
F^{\prime}
be a
k
-edition of
G^{\prime}
into a block graph. Let
u
be the attachment point of
B
in
G
. Observe that since
B^{R}
is connected and not empty,
u
is not a cut-vertex of the block graph
B
and its neighborhood in
B
is either a maximal clique or a single vertex. Since
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}\triangle F^{\prime}
and
B
are block graphs and
N_{B}(u)
is a single vertex or a maximal clique of
B
, Lemma
3
ensures that
H=(H^{\prime},\{u\})\otimes(B^{R},N_{B}(u))
is a block graph. We can observe that
H=G\triangle F^{\prime}
, hence
F^{\prime}
is a
k
-edition of
G
into a block graph. This concludes the proof. ∎

We now deal with the
2
-BG-branches, which require more technical skills.
A
2
-BG-branch
B
is

if
B^{R}
is connected and if the two attachment points are not adjacent. For a clean
2
-BG-branch
B
, a
min-cut
of
B
is a set of edges
M\subseteq E(B)
of minimum size such that the attachment points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are not in the same connected component of
B-M
and
mc(B)
is the size of a min-cut of
B
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
(G,k)
be a yes-instance of
Block Graph Editing
reduced by Rule
3.3
, and

a clean

-BG-branch of

. If
|V(B)|\geqslant k+3
then there exists a
k
-edition
F
of
G
into a block graph such that the set of pairs of
F
containing a vertex of
V(B^{R})
is either empty or exactly a min-cut of
B
.

Proof.

Let
B
be a clean
2
-BG-branch in
G
with attachment points
p_{1},p_{2}
such that
|V(B)|\geqslant k+3
. We can observe that
N_{B}(p_{1})
and
N_{B}(p_{2})
are maximal cliques or single vertices since
B^{R}
is connected. Since
B
is reduced by
3.3
, we can observe that the cut-vertices of
B
are contained in any path
\pi

4

As in the previous section, we begin this section by providing a high-level description of our kernelization algorithm. We use the critical clique graph of strictly chordal graphs, which is a block graph by definition, to bound the number of vertices of a reduced instance. While the technique used to bound the size of the kernels for block graphs and strictly chordal graphs is similar, the structure and rules for the latter are more involved. Conclusion

We presented polynomial kernels for the edition and deletion variants of block graph edge modification problems and the three variants of strictly chordal edge modification problems.
Our conviction is that the approach based on decompositions of the target class, combined with the ability of reducing the size of the bags of the decomposition and of limiting the number of affected bags to
O(k)
is a promising starting point for edge modification problems, especially into subclasses of chordal graphs. The technique has been employed especially for classes that admit a tree-like decompositions with disjoint bags (e.g., 3-leaf power
(
3
)
, trivially perfect graphs
(
22
,
20
)
and
\mathcal{M}
-free graphs
(
32
)
), also for other types of tree-like decompositions with non-disjoint bags (e.g., ptolemaic graphs
(
13
)
). We generalize it here to strictly chordal graphs, that have a decomposition into disjoint bags as nodes of a block graph.

The difficulty is that, at this stage, each class requires ad-hoc arguments and reduction rules, based on its specific decomposition. An ambitious goal would be to obtain a generic algorithm for edge modification problems into any class of chordal graphs, plus a finite set of obstructions, as conjectured by Bessy and Perez
(
4
)
for completion problems. As an intermediate step, we ask whether
4
-leaf power completion, deletion and editing problems admit a polynomial kernel.

On another level, it would be really interesting to obtain polynomial kernelization lower bounds for edge-modification problems. To the best of our knowledge, such bounds are only known for vertex-deletion problems; more precisely, any such problem where the target class is closed under subgraphs does not admit a kernel with
O(k^{2-\epsilon})
edges unless
coNP\subseteq NP/poly
, see
(
15
)
.
