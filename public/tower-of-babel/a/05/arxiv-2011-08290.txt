Estimating the correlation in network disturbance models
A. D. Barbour, Gesine Reinert

Abstract
The Network Disturbance Model of [ 3 ] expresses the dependency between observations taken at the vertices of a network by modelling the correlation between neighbouring vertices, using a single correlation parameter ρ \rho . It has been observed that estimation of ρ \rho in dense graphs, using the method of Maximum Likelihood, leads to results that can be both biased and very unstable. In this paper, we sketch why this is the case, showing that the variability cannot be avoided, no matter how large the network. We also propose a more intuitive estimator of ρ \rho , which shows little bias. The related Network Effects Model is briefly discussed.



Keywords:
Network disturbance, network autocorrelation, maximum likelihood.

MRC subject classification:
91D30; 91Cxx, 62P25, 62J05

1
Introduction

The classical Gaussian linear regression model can be written as

y\ =\ X\beta+\varepsilon.

(1.1)

Here, the
n
-dimensional vector
y
of data is described as the sum of a structural element

X\beta
, where
X
is a known
n\times m
matrix of full rank
m
,

\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})^{\top}
is an
m
-vector of unknown parameters, to be estimated,
and the elements
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
of the
n
-vector
\varepsilon
are realizations of independent
normally distributed random variables
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
,
each with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Here and throughout,
we conventionally use bold face to denote random elements, and italic for their realizations.

If the indices
i=1,2,\ldots,n
represent the vertices of a simple undirected graph, whose
(symmetric) adjacency matrix is denoted by
A
, it may be reasonable to suppose
that the errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}
at neighbouring vertices
i
and
j
are correlated, instead of being independent.
A similar idea lies at the basis of time series analysis, where the error at a given time point
is assumed to depend on the errors at the closest previous time points. In analogy with the
moving average models of time series analysis, the
Network Disturbance Model

of
[
3
]
(see also
[
12
, (4.6)]
) supposes that

\mathbf{y}\ =\ X\beta+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, where the errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
are now implicitly given by the equation

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}\ =\ \rho\sum_{{j=1}}^{n}W_{ij}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

(1.2)

or more succinctly, by

K_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\ =\ \boldsymbol{\nu},\qquad\mbox{where}\quad K_{\rho}\ :=\ I-\rho W.

(1.3)

Here, the weight matrix
W
is known, the parameter
\rho
is unknown, and the random variables
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{n}

are independent normal random variables with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
The weight
W_{ij}\geq 0
can be thought of
as a (relative) measure of
the influence of vertex
j
on vertex
i
, and is assumed to take the value zero if
i
and
j
are
not neighbours, i.e. if
A_{ij}=0
; we also assume that
W_{ii}=0
for each
i
.
The parameter
\rho
can be varied, and
|\rho|
mirrors the overall strength of the
dependence between the observations at neighbouring vertices.

The network disturbance model can be estimated by using the method of maximum likelihood (MLE); details of the
procedure are given in Section
2
.
In classical settings, including repeated independent observations from a common underlying distribution,
the maximum likelihood estimator is asymptotically unbiased, as the number of observations tends to infinity.
Here, the observations are dependent, and the standard asymptotic conclusions need no longer be valid.
Indeed, it has been widely observed in simulation studies that, as the underlying network becomes denser,
and the corresponding weight matrix
W
becomes less sparse,
the maximum likelihood estimator
{\hat{\rho}}
of
\rho
becomes more variable and more negatively biased. This has
been extensively documented, largely in the context of the related network effects models
of
[
3
]
, defined in (
1.4
) below,
for instance in
[
10
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
4
]
,

[
11
]
and
[
8
]
. Methods for correcting the bias of the MLE, using asymptotics
appropriate in settings where consistent estimation is possible, have since been proposed in

[
1
]
,
[
19
]
and
[
18
]
. Other methods of estimation have been developed by

[
7
]
, who introduce a generalized method of moments estimator, by
[
2
]
, with
a Bayesian approach, and by
[
17
]
, whose techniques are based on Hodges–Lehmann estimators.
In
[
16
]
, theoretical investigation
shows that, at least for networks very close to the complete graph, the MLE may lead to misleading results:
see the final example in Section
4.4
for a fuller explanation, that makes clear why
no estimator of
\rho
can work well in such circumstances.

In this paper, we examine the problem in greater depth, showing why the MLE can be expected to be biased and
lacking in precision, whenever the underlying network is dense.
In Section
3
, we use Cramér–Rao theory to show that, if the underlying network is
sufficiently dense, then
any
(more or less unbiased)
estimator of
\rho
must necessarily be unreliable. The bound is expressed as a lower bound (
3.7
) on the variance of any (unbiased) estimator,
and is given explicitly as a function
of the matrix
W
of weights and of the true value of
\rho
. The structure of the network enters by way of
W
.
For instance, the choice
W_{ij}:=A_{ij}/\deg(i)
makes explicit the dependence of the weights on the adjacency
matrix
A
of the network.
We then, in Section
4
, examine the behaviour of the maximum likelihood estimator in enough
detail to explain the empirical observations. In particular, we are able to show that the MLE of
\rho
is biased
when the network is sufficiently dense. In ordinary least squares estimation, the estimator of
\beta
has
small variance if the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
X^{\top}X
is large. Here, it is possible, for rather special
graphs, that the MLE of
\beta
may still have substantial variance, even when this condition is satisfied; see Examples
1 and 2 in Section
4.4
.

The exact formulae for the bias and variance of the MLE are
in general indigestible, but we discuss some special cases in which it is possible to estimate their
magnitudes.
In Section
5
, we consider a
simpler and more intuitive estimator of
\rho
, that has better properties as regards bias, but is still
lacking in precision in dense graphs, as it must be, in view of the lower bound
given in Section
3
. Its properties are illustrated by some simulations.
An R function for computing the estimator, as well an empirical measure of its precision,
is available on request from the authors.

There is a related model, the
Network Effects Model
of
[
3
]

(see also
[
12
, (4.1)]
, which takes the form

K_{\rho}y\ =\ X\beta+\nu,

(1.4)

where the components of
\nu
are realizations of independent normal random variables with mean zero and
variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
K_{\rho}
is as defined in (
1.3
). This differs from the network
disturbance model because, rewriting it as

y\ =\ K_{\rho}^{-1}X\beta+\varepsilon,

(1.5)

the errors in
y
have the same dependence as before, but the
model structure
explaining
y
also
changes with
\rho
. The limitations as regards estimating
\rho
in dense networks are analogous, and for
similar reasons. We discuss the model briefly in Section
6
.

The main conclusions of the paper are sketched in Section
7
, and additional calculations
can be found in the Appendix.

2
Details of the model

The maximum likelihood estimates of the parameters in the simple linear regression model

\mathbf{y}\ =\ X\beta+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
of (
1.1
), with independent and identically distributed errors,
are given by

\hat{\beta}\ =\ \hat{\beta}(y)\ =\ (X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y;\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{2}\ =\ \hat{\sigma}^{2}(y)\ =\ n^{-1}y^{\top}(I-H(X))y,

(2.1)

where

H(X)\ :=\ X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}

(2.2)

is symmetric and idempotent, and represents the orthogonal projection
from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto the linear span of the columns of
X
.
Writing

M_{0}\colon\ \mathbf{y}\ =\ X\beta+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}

(2.3)

to denote the probabilistic model from which
the data are sampled, the random quantities
\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{y})
and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbf{y})
have distributions given by

\hat{\beta}(\mathbf{y})\ \sim\ {\mathrm{MVN}\,}_{m}(\beta,\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1});\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{2}(\mathbf{y})\ \sim\ n^{-1}\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}_{n-m},

(2.4)

where
{\mathrm{MVN}\,}_{m}(\mu,\Sigma)
denotes the
m
-dimensional multivariate normal distribution with mean
\mu

and covariance marix
\Sigma
, and
\chi^{2}_{l}
denotes the chi-squared distribution with
l
degrees of
freedom. The two estimators are also independent of one another (see
[
13
, Section 4.b]
).

In the network disturbance model
\mathbf{y}\ =\ X\beta+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, the errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
are given by (
1.3
),
with dependence deriving from the weight matrix
W
.
It is often convenient to assume that the row sums of
W
are all equal to
1
, in which case the quantity

\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{j}
represents an average of the errors at the neighbours of vertex
i
; then
\rho

would typically be smaller than
1
in modulus, so as to ensure that the average influence of the neighbours is not
larger than the size of a typical error. A simple choice for
W_{ij}
is
A_{ij}/\deg(i)
, where
\deg(i)
denotes
the degree of the vertex
i
. If there are isolated vertices
i
, the corresponding row sums cannot be made
equal to
1
, since then
W_{ij}=0
for all
j\neq i
.

The implicit representation (
1.3
) of the errors can be written as a direct formula for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}

in terms of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, as

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\ =\ K_{\rho}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu},

(2.5)

provided that the matrix
K_{\rho}
can be inverted. The expansion

K_{\rho}^{-1}\ =\ (I-\rho W)^{-1}\ =\ I+\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}W^{l}

(2.6)

makes sense provided that the sum
\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}W^{l}x
converges absolutely for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;
that is, if
{|\rho|}
is less than
1/r(W)
, where
r(W)
is the spectral radius of
W
.
In particular, if
W
has all of its row sums equal to
1
, the vector
{\bf{1}}
with all components equal to
1

is a right eigenvector with eigenvalue
1
, implying that
r(W)=1

(see
[
6
, Corollary 8.1.30, p.493]
). Then

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\ =\ K_{\rho}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}\ =\ \boldsymbol{\nu}+\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}W^{l}\boldsymbol{\nu},\qquad|\rho|<1,

represents
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}
as a weighted sum of the contributions of the different elements
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}
,
averaged along paths in the network from
j
to
i
, and with overall weight
\rho^{l}
for paths of length
l

(possibly negative, if
\rho<0
). If
|\rho|>1/r(W)
, this interpretation no longer holds, since the
expansion (
2.6
) is no longer well defined. The resulting model is then not
so easy to understand, since, at least locally, the cumulative effect on the observation at
i
from those at
distance
d
from
i
increases geometrically with
d
. In time series analysis, the analogue is that of processes
that are ‘explosive’, and the analogue of the condition
|\rho|<1/r(W)
is used to ensure that a time series is
stationary. For this reason,
we shall assume in what follows that
|\rho|<1/r(W)
, so
that the inverse of
K_{\rho}
exists, and is explicitly given by (
2.6
).

The model
\mathbf{y}\ =\ X\beta+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
with errors given by (
1.2
) is simple to analyze if the value of
\rho

is known, since it can be written in the form

M_{\rho}\colon\quad K_{\rho}\mathbf{y}\ =\ K_{\rho}X\beta+\boldsymbol{\nu},

(2.7)

with the components of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
being independent normal
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2})
random variables, and can thus be fitted as
a standard linear regression based on data
y^{\prime}:=K_{\rho}y
. Equations (
2.1
) then give the estimates

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{\rho}(y)\ =\ ((K_{\rho}X)^{\top}K_{\rho}X)^{-1}(K_{\rho}X)^{\top}K_{\rho}y;

(2.8)

\displaystyle{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(y)\ =\ {n^{-1}\{K_{\rho}(y-X\hat{\beta}_{\rho})\}^{\top}\{K_{\rho}(y-X\hat{\beta}_{\rho})\}}\ =\ n^{-1}(K_{\rho}y)^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}y,

where, with
H
as in (
2.2
),

H_{\rho}\ :=\ H(K_{\rho}X)

(2.9)

and so
K_{\rho}X\hat{\beta}_{\rho}=H_{\rho}K_{\rho}y
. The residuals after fitting
M_{\rho}
are thus

{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(y)\ :=\ y-X{\hat{\beta}}_{\rho}(y)\ =\ K_{\rho}^{-1}(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}y.

(2.10)

The distributions of
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})
and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})
under the model
M_{\rho}
are given by

{\hat{\beta}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})\ \sim\ {\mathrm{MVN}\,}_{m}(\beta,\sigma^{2}((K_{\rho}X)^{\top}K_{\rho}X)^{-1});\quad{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})\ \sim\ n^{-1}\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}_{n-m},

(2.11)

and the two estimators are independent of one another. In particular, both
{\hat{\beta}}
and
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}

have precision much as in simple linear regression, and (under
reasonable assumptions about
X
) can be expected to become more accurate as
n
increases.
If the true model is
M_{\rho^{\prime}}
, for
\rho^{\prime}\neq\rho
, the expectation of
{\hat{\beta}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})
is still equal to
\beta
,
and, although the variances of its components may be larger, they are typically comparable to those
on the true model, again under reasonable conditions; see Appendix
A.4
for more detail.

If
\rho
is not known, the estimates (
2.8
) still maximize
the log-likelihood

\ell(\theta;y)\ :=\ -{\textstyle{\frac{n}{2}}}\{\log(\sigma^{2})+\log(2\pi)\}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\{K_{\rho}(y-X\beta)\}^{\top}\{K_{\rho}(y-X\beta)\}+\log\det K_{\rho}

(2.12)

of the model
M_{\rho}
of (
2.7
), for each given value of
\rho
,
where
\theta:=(\beta,\sigma^{2},\rho)
denotes the full set of parameters; see
[
3
, Equation (1.3)]
.
The resulting maximum of the log-likelihood, for fixed
\rho
, is given by

-{\textstyle{\frac{n}{2}}}\{\log({\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(y))+\log(2\pi)+1\}+\log\det K_{\rho};

(2.13)

this can be deduced by observing that, with
\beta
replaced by
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
, the middle term of (
2.12
)
reduces to
-n/2
, in view of (
2.8
).
Indeed, the first term in (
2.13
) is the maximum of the likelihood for ordinary least squares applied
to the data
y^{\prime}:=K_{\rho}y
,
and the determinant enters because the probability densities
f_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}
of
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
and
f_{\mathbf{y}}
of
\mathbf{y}
are related by

f_{\mathbf{y}}(v)=\det K_{\rho}\,f_{\mathbf{y}^{\prime}}(K_{\rho}v)
. Then
\rho
can be chosen to (numerically) maximize the expression
in (
2.13
), or, equivalently, to minimize

F(\rho;y)\ :=\ \log(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\rho}(y))-{\textstyle{\frac{2}{n}}}\log\det K_{\rho},\qquad|\rho|<1/r(W).

(2.14)

3
A general limit to precision

The
\rho
-component of the score function is obtained by differentiating the log-likelihood (
2.12
) with respect to
\rho
.
Noting that
\frac{d}{d\rho}K_{\rho}=-W
, and that,
if the eigenvalues of
W
are denoted by
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
, we have
\det K_{\rho}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\rho\lambda_{i})
.
This gives the
\rho
-component of the score function as

\displaystyle s^{(\rho)}(\theta;y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\ell(\theta;y)\ =\ \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(y-X\beta)^{\top}K_{\rho}^{\top}W(y-X\beta)-\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{l}}{1-\rho\lambda_{l}}

(3.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigl(\{K_{\rho}(y-X\beta)\}^{\top}Z_{\rho}\{K_{\rho}(y-X\beta)\}\bigr)-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}\},

where we define the matrix

Z_{\rho}\ :=\ WK_{\rho}^{-1}.

(3.2)

Now, if
C
is any
n\times n
matrix, and
{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
denotes an
n
-vector of independent

standard
normal random variables, then the quadratic form
{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{\top}C{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
has mean

{\mathbb{E}}\{{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{\top}C{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\}\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{C\}.

(3.3)

Furthermore, for
symmetric

C
,
since
{\mbox{Cov}}\{{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}_{i}{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}_{j},{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}_{k}{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}_{l}\}=0
unless either
i\neq j
and
\{i,j\}=\{k,l\}
or
i=j=k=l
,
the variance of
{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{\top}C{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\{{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{\top}C{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}C_{ij}(C_{ij}+C_{ji})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{ii}^{2}({\mathbb{E}}\{{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}_{1}^{4}\}-1)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{Tr}}(C^{2}),

(3.5)

because
{\mathbb{E}}\{{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}_{1}^{4}\}=3
when
{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}_{1}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
; see
[
14
, Chapter 2, Theorem 4 (i) & (ii)]
.
If
C
is not symmetric, the
expression (
3.5
) can be used with
C
replaced by the symmetric matrix
C^{\prime}:={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(C+C^{\top})
,
since
{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{\top}C^{\prime}{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}={\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{\top}C{\bf\tilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
.
Hence, taking expectations on the model with parameters
\theta_{0}:=(\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0},\rho_{0})
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{0}}\,s^{(\rho)}(\theta_{0};\mathbf{y})\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}\}-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}\}\ \ =\ 0,

as it must, and

{\rm Var}_{\theta_{0}}\,s^{(\rho)}(\theta_{0};\mathbf{y})\ =\ 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\bigl\{\bigl({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})\bigr)^{2}\bigr\}\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\}.

(3.6)

This, together with
[
5
, Theorem 6.2]
and
[
9
, Theorem 6.6 and (6.25)]
,
implies the following limit on the precision of estimators of
\rho
.

Cramér–Rao lower bound.
The variance of any unbiased estimator of
\rho
is no smaller than

\gamma_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\ :=\ \frac{1}{{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\}}\,,

(3.7)

where
Z_{\rho}:=WK_{\rho}^{-1}
and
K_{\rho}
is as in (
2.5
).

In practice, the bound implies a precision that cannot be significantly improved upon for any estimator of
\rho
,
without introducing substantial bias;
even the best ‘sensible’ estimator of
\rho
has to have random variability on the scale of
\gamma_{\rho_{0}}
.

Note that, for any
n\times n
matrix
M
,

|{\mathrm{Tr}}\{M^{2}\}|\ =\ \Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}M_{ji}\Bigr|\ \leq\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}^{2}\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{MM^{\top}\},

(3.8)

by Cauchy–Schwarz, so that the two traces appearing in
\gamma_{\rho_{0}}^{2}
are not in general the same,
and that

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{M^{2}+MM^{\top}\}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\{M_{ij}M_{ji}+M_{ij}^{2}\}\ =\ {\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(M_{ij}+M_{ji})^{2}\ \geq\ 0.

(3.9)

Consequences of (
3.7
): the case
\rho_{0}=0
.

As a simple example of what is to be expected, suppose that
\rho_{0}=0
, and that the underlying graph
has no isolated vertices, so that
\deg(i)\geq 1
for all
i
. Let
W:=D^{-1}A
,
where
D
is the diagonal matrix with elements
D_{ii}:=\deg(i)
,
1\leq i\leq n
. Then
Z_{\rho_{0}}=Z_{0}=W
,
and we can be specific about the traces in (
3.7
):

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{2}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{A_{ij}}{\deg(i)\deg(j)}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WW^{\top}\}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{A_{ij}}{\deg(i)^{2}}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\deg(i)}\ \leq\ n.

It follows from the equality in (
3.10
)
that
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WW^{\top}\}\geq n/{\overline{d}}
, where
{\overline{d}}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\deg(i)
denotes the average degree of
a vertex, and hence
that
\gamma_{0}^{2}\leq{\overline{d}}/n
. However, if the vertex degrees are reasonably homogeneous, in the sense all the vertices have
degrees at least
c{\overline{d}}
, for some
0<c<1
, then it follows from (
3.10
) that
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WW^{\top}\}\leq n/c{\overline{d}}
,
and thus, from (
3.7
), that
\gamma_{0}^{2}\geq c{\overline{d}}/n
. Hence, in these simple circumstances, the variability of any
sensible estimator of
\rho
is on a scale of at least
({\overline{d}}/n)^{1/2}
.

These calculations have particular implications for dense networks. If
W=D^{-1}A
, and if
all vertices have degrees exceeding
cn
, for some fixed
c>0
, it follows that
\gamma_{0}\geq\sqrt{c}
, and it
is not possible to obtain an accurate estimate of
\rho
by any method, no matter how large
the size
n
of the network, if in fact
\rho_{0}=0
. For more general choices of
W
,

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WW^{\top}\}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}W_{ij}^{2}
, and
if all the elements of
W
are smaller than
C/n
, for some
C<\infty
, it follows that
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WW^{\top}\}\leq C^{2}
,
and hence, from (
3.7
), that
\gamma_{0}^{2}>1/C^{2}
. Hence, here too, any estimator of
\rho
has
significant variability on the scale of
1/C
, whatever the size
n
of
the network, if in fact
\rho_{0}=0
.

Consequences of (
3.7
): general
\rho_{0}
.

If
\rho_{0}\neq 0
, analogous results are true.
Supposing that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}=1
for each
i
, it follows that, for all
l\geq 1
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(W^{l})_{ij}=1
for all
i
also.
Hence
(W^{l})_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(W^{l-1})_{ik}W_{kj}
is an average of the values
W_{1j},\ldots,W_{nj}
,
Thus, if all the elements of
W
are smaller than
C/n
, it follows that

(W^{l})_{ij}\leq C/n
also. Hence it is immediate that, for
l,l^{\prime}\geq 1
,

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{l}\}\ \leq\ C\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{l}(W^{\top})^{l^{\prime}}\}\ \leq\ C.

(3.11)

Thus, for
\rho\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}^{2}\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}Z_{\rho}^{\top}\}\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{W(I-\rho W)^{-1}(I-\rho W^{\top})^{-1}W^{\top}\}

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{l\geq 0}(l+1)\rho^{l}\ =\ \frac{C}{(1-\rho)^{2}}\,,

and so

\gamma_{\rho_{0}}\ \geq\ C^{-1/2}(1-\rho).

Once again, there is substantial variability in the estimate of
\rho
unless
C:=n\max_{i,j}W_{ij}
is large.
If
\rho<0
, the bounds in (
3.11
) can be used to justify similar conclusions, by bounding
the difference
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}Z_{\rho}^{\top}\}-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WW^{\top}\}
, provided that
|\rho|
is not too large.

In particular, in uniformly dense networks, if
W=D^{-1}A
, there is substantial variability in any estimator
of
\rho
if all vertex degrees exceed
n/C
, and
C
is not large.

4
Maximum likelihood estimation

The discussion in the previous section shows that there is an upper limit on the precision of any
(unbiased) estimator of
\rho
, and that, in uniformly dense networks, any such estimator must have
substantial variability. In this section, we go into more detail about the properties of the MLE.

4.1
General theory

An equation for the MLE

To minimize the quantity
F(\rho;y)
of (
2.14
), it is usual to look for values of
\rho

such that

U(\rho;y)\ :=\ \frac{d}{d\rho}F(\rho;y)\ =\ 0.

(4.1)

Since
F(\rho;y)
is explicitly given as a function of
\rho
, its derivative can be calculated, giving

\displaystyle U(\rho;y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(y)}\,\frac{d{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(y)}{d\rho}-\frac{2}{n}\,\frac{d}{d\rho}\,\log\det K_{\rho}

(4.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(y)}\,(K_{\rho}y)^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})(Z_{\rho}^{\top}+Z_{\rho})(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}y+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{l+1}\};\phantom{XX}

the details are given in Appendix
A.1
.
The maximum likelihood estimate
{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ML}}
of
\rho
is now typically obtained by solving
U(\rho;y)=0
;
Example 3 in Section
4.4
shows that this may not always work.

Note that the data enter through a ratio of quadratic forms
in
y
. Under
M_{\rho_{0}}
, as observed in (
2.11
),
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathbf{y})\ \sim\ n^{-1}\sigma^{2}\chi^{2}_{n-m}
,
having mean
\sigma^{2}(1-m/n)
and variance less than
2n^{-1}\sigma^{4}
, so that, to a reasonable approximation,

{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho_{0}}(y)
can be replaced by
\sigma^{2}
in (
4.2
) if
M_{\rho_{0}}
is true, provided that
n
is large.
As a result,
U(\rho;y)
depends on the data
y
largely through a single quadratic form in
y
.

Approximating the distribution of
U(\rho;\mathbf{y})
under
M_{\rho_{0}}

In order to gauge the typical values of
U(\rho;y)
to be obtained under the true model
M_{\rho_{0}}
,
we express
U(\rho;\mathbf{y})
in terms of the (not directly observable) errors
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, rather than in terms of
\mathbf{y}
.
Under
M_{\rho_{0}}
,
\mathbf{y}=X\beta+K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}
, and it thus follows from (
2.14
)
and (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle U(\rho;\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})}\,\frac{d{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})}{d\rho}-\frac{2}{n}\,\frac{d}{d\rho}\,\log\det K_{\rho}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu})}\,\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}Q(\rho;\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nu}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{l+1}\},

where

Q(\rho;\rho_{0})\ :=\ (K_{\rho}K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1})^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})(Z_{\rho}^{\top}+Z_{\rho})(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}.

(4.4)

Note that
\beta
does not appear in (
4.3
) because
(I-H_{\rho})^{T}=I-H_{\rho}
and
(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}X=0
,
implying in particular, using (
2.8
), that
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y}-X\beta)={\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})
.
Hence, and because
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu})
is close to
\sigma^{2}
for large
n
,
the random part of
U(\rho;\mathbf{y})
is given, to a close approximation, by
-\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}Q(\rho;\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nu}/(n\sigma^{2})
.

In the remaining argument, we make frequent use of elementary properties of the trace, that
{\mathrm{Tr}}(AB)={\mathrm{Tr}}(BA)

and that
{\mathrm{Tr}}(A^{\top})={\mathrm{Tr}}(A)
, as well as noting that
z^{\top}Mz=z^{\top}M^{\top}z

for any compatible
z
and
M
, and that
H_{\rho}
and
I-H_{\rho}

are idempotent.
The expectation of
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}Q(\rho_{0},\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nu}
, using (
3.3
), is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}Q(\rho_{0},\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nu}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Q(\rho_{0},\rho_{0})\}\ =\ {2\sigma^{2}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(I-H_{\rho_{0}})Z_{\rho_{0}}(I-H_{\rho_{0}})\}}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sigma^{2}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(I-H_{\rho_{0}})Z_{\rho_{0}}\}\ =\ 2\sigma^{2}\bigl({\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}\}-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}\}\bigr),

because
K_{\rho_{0}}K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}=I
, and using
{\mathrm{Tr}}(AB)={\mathrm{Tr}}(BA)
and
(I-H_{\rho_{0}})^{2}=I-H_{\rho_{0}}
.
Then, using
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W\}=0
,

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}\}\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{W(I-\rho_{0}W)^{-1}\}\ =\ \sum_{l\geq 1}\rho_{0}^{l}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{l+1}\}.

(4.6)

Thus we have

-{\mathbb{E}}\bigl\{\sigma^{-2}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}Q(\rho_{0};\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nu}\bigr\}+2\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{l+1}\}\ =\ 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}\}.

Hence, from (
4.3
) and (
4.5
), neglecting the small variability in
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})
,
the quantity
nU(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})
under the model
M_{\rho_{0}}
satisfies

{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{0}}\{nU(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})\}\ \approx\ \mu_{0}\ :=\ \mu(\rho_{0})\ :=\ 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}\}.

(4.7)

The principal source of variability in
nU(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})
arises from

\sigma^{-2}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}Q(\rho_{0};\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nu}
, whose variance under
M_{\rho_{0}}
, from (
3.5
), is given
by
2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Q(\rho_{0};\rho_{0})^{2}\}
. This yields

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{\rho_{0}}(nU(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y}))

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Q(\rho_{0};\rho_{0})^{2}\}

(4.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})\}}

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{2}\ :=\ \tau_{0}^{2}(\rho_{0}).

Combining this with (
4.7
), we reach the following

Conclusion 1:
the distribution of
nU(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})
can be written in the form

nU(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})\ =\ \mu_{0}+\tau_{0}L(\mathbf{y}),

(4.9)

where
L(\mathbf{y})
has mean approximately
0
and variance approximately
1
.

Consequences for the distribution of the MLE

Using the information above, we can now make approximate deductions about the MLE
{\hat{\rho}}:={\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ML}}
, obtained by setting

U(\rho;y)
equal to zero. We begin by noting that
U({\hat{\rho}},y)=0
, whereas, as in (
4.9
),

nU(\rho_{0};y)=\mu_{0}+\tau_{0}L(y)
, where
L(\mathbf{y})
approximately has mean zero and variance
1
.
Writing
U^{\prime}(\rho;y):=\frac{d}{d\rho}U(\rho;y)
, this implies that

-n^{-1}(\mu_{0}+\tau_{0}L(y))\ =\ \{U({\hat{\rho}};y)-U(\rho_{0};y)\}\ =\ ({\hat{\rho}}-\rho_{0})U^{\prime}(\rho_{0};y)+o(|{\hat{\rho}}-\rho_{0}|),

where the final error term is relatively small
if
(|\mu_{0}|+\tau_{0})/n|U^{\prime}(\rho_{0};y)|
is small enough, leading to the approximate expression
for the error
{\hat{\rho}}-\rho_{0}
in estimating
\rho_{0}
:

{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ML}}-\rho_{0}\ \approx\ -\frac{\mu_{0}+\tau_{0}L(y)}{nU^{\prime}(\rho_{0};y)}\,.

(4.10)

This is essentially a one–step Newton–Raphson approximation to the value of
\rho
that
solves
U(\rho;y)=0
, starting from the guess
\rho_{0}
.
Thus it is necessary also to examine the magnitude of
nU^{\prime}(\rho_{0};y)
. Since
U(\rho;y)
is explicitly given,
there are exact formulae for its derivative, given in Appendix
A.2
; however, they are not easy to understand.
Instead, it is possible to approximate the
expectation
of
nU^{\prime}(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})
on the model
M_{\rho_{0}}
, giving

n{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{0}}U^{\prime}(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})\ \approx\ \Delta_{0},

(4.11)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\tau_{0}^{2}+2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{-Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})+H_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\};

(4.12)

the details are again to be found in Appendix
A.2
.
Thus, from (
4.10
), we have the following

Conclusion 2:
the difference between
{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ML}}
and
\rho_{0}
can be expected to be of
magnitude roughly

\frac{|\mu_{0}|{\pm}\tau_{0}}{\Delta_{0}}\,,

(4.13)

with the term
|\mu_{0}|/\Delta_{0}
corresponding to systematic bias, and
\psi_{0}:=\tau_{0}/\Delta_{0}
reflecting the scale
of the randomness in the estimator.

Note that the measure
\psi_{0}
should be treated only as a rough
guide to the actual variability of
{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ML}}
under
M_{\rho_{0}}
, since (
4.10
) is only a linear
approximation to the solution of a non-linear equation, and because there may be added variability
arising from the variation of
nU^{\prime}(\rho_{0};\mathbf{y})
about its mean.

4.2
Estimating
\rho
when there is no structural element

To get an idea of the relative magnitudes of the terms
\mu_{0}
,
\tau_{0}
and
\Delta_{0}

in (
4.13
), it is helpful to look
at some special cases. For the first, suppose that there is no structural element at all — not even a mean to
be fitted — which corresponds to taking
X=0
; in this case, the formulae (
4.7
), (
4.8
)
and (
4.12
) should be interpreted as having
H_{\rho}=0
, the projection onto the space
\{0\}
.
Then
\mu_{0}=0
, so that any bias is small,

\tau_{0}^{2}\ =\ 4{\mathrm{Tr}}\bigl\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\bigr\},\quad\mbox{where}\quad Z_{\rho}\ =\ W(I-\rho W)^{-1},

and

\Delta_{0}\ =\ \tau_{0}^{2}/2,

giving

\psi_{0}\ =\ \frac{\tau_{0}}{\Delta_{0}}\ =\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\}}}.

(4.14)

This is the same as
\gamma_{\rho_{0}}
in (
3.7
).
Thus, from (
4.13
), the typical magnitude of
|{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ML}}-\rho_{0}|
is of order
\gamma_{\rho_{0}}
.
This is the best order of error that could theoretically have been hoped for. Nonetheless, as observed above, it
may not lead to accurate estimation of
\rho
, for instance if
W=D^{-1}A
and the underlying network
is sufficiently dense, even though the
bias
is small.

4.3
Estimating
\rho
when structure is present

To accommodate the effect of having structure in the model, suppose first that only an overall mean is to be fitted,
so that
X
is the
n
-vector
{\bf{1}}
, having all elements equal to
1
. Suppose also that there are no isolated vertices,
and that
W
is chosen to have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}=1
for all
j
, so that
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
and the spectral radius
r(W)=1
.
Then it is immediately verified that
H_{\rho}=H_{*}:=n^{-1}{\bf{1}}{\bf{1}}^{\top}
for all
\rho
,
because, for instance,
K_{\rho}X=(I-\rho W){\bf{1}}=(1-\rho){\bf{1}}
. Hence
WH_{\rho}=H_{*}
and
Z_{\rho}H_{\rho}=(1-\rho)^{-1}H_{*}
.
As a result, by arguments that are made precise in Appendix
A.3
, (
4.8
) and (
4.12
) yield

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2})(I-H_{*})\}{+2\{(1-\rho_{0})^{-2}-n^{-1}|Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}|^{2}\}}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{4{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2})(I-H_{*})\}};

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\tau_{0}^{2}+2(1-\rho_{0})^{-2};

here, and in what follows,
|x|
for a vector
x
denotes the Euclidean norm.
Hence, if
\rho_{0}
is fixed and less than
1
,
\psi_{0}
is of order
1/(\tau_{0}+1)
,
and this, from (
4.13
), represents the scale of the
random
variation in the estimate of
\rho_{0}
.
Now
1/(\tau_{0}+1)
is small if
\tau_{0}^{2}
is large compared to
1
,
but can be significantly larger than the lower bound
\gamma_{\rho_{0}}
in (
3.7
), if
\tau_{0}^{2}
is much smaller than

1/\gamma_{\rho_{0}}^{2}={\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\}
.
As in the case where there is no structure, if
W=D^{-1}A
and the graph is sufficiently dense, then

\gamma_{\rho_{0}}
need not be small, in which case the MLE has substantial variability.

In contrast to the situation without structure, the principal element
\mu_{0}
of the bias in the estimation of
\rho

need not be zero. If
X={\bf{1}}
,
it follows from (
4.7
) that

\mu_{0}\ =\ 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{0}\}\ =\ 2(1-\rho_{0})^{-1}\ >\ 0.

In view of Conclusion 2,
the bias becomes important when
\Delta_{0}
is not large, which, from (
4.15
), is when
\tau_{0}^{2}
is not large;
as discussed above, this occurs for example if
W=D^{-1}A
and the graph is uniformly dense.
Note also that, from (
4.10
) and (
4.13
), the value of the difference
{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ML}}-\rho_{0}
resulting
from
\mu_{0}
is roughly
-2/\{\Delta_{0}(1-\rho_{0})\}
, which typically implies a negative bias, as demonstrated
in the results of the simulations referred to in the introduction.

If there is more structure in the regression, but still assuming that the first column of
X
is
{\bf{1}}

and that
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
,
the detailed formulae become more complicated, but the basic message remains the same; the details are given in
Appendix
A.3
. The matrix
H_{\rho}
can be represented in the form

H_{\rho}\ =\ H_{*}+\sum_{l=2}^{m}x_{\rho}^{(l)}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top},

(4.16)

where
H_{*}:=n^{-1}{\bf{1}}{\bf{1}}^{\top}
is as before, and
x_{\rho}^{(2)},\ldots,x_{\rho}^{(m)}
are
orthonormal
vectors
orthogonal to
{\bf{1}}
that, with
{\bf{1}}
, span the space generated by the columns of
K_{\rho}X
.
The expression for
\tau_{0}^{2}
derived in (
A.13
), in addition to the quantity

v(\rho_{0})\ :=\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})\},

(4.17)

also involves the traces of the matrices
Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}H_{\rho_{0}}
,

Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}}
,
(Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}})^{2}
and
Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}H_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}}
.
Similarly, from (
4.12
), the quantity
\Delta_{0}
differs from
{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\tau_{0}^{2}
by a quantity that
is also expressed in terms of these traces.
It is shown in Appendix
A.3
that if,
for instance, it is assumed that each of the vectors
x_{\rho}^{(l)}
,

2\leq l\leq m
, has components bounded in modulus by
cn^{-1/2}
, for some fixed
c\geq 1
—
as is the case for
x^{(1)}=n^{-1/2}{\bf{1}}
— then

each of the traces listed above is bounded in modulus by
m^{2}c^{4}/(1-|\rho|)^{2}
.
This gives rise to the following

Conclusion 3:

If, in the setting of this section,
m
and
c
are also fixed for all
n
, and
|\rho_{0}|
is bounded
away from
1
, then the formulae for
\tau_{0}^{2}
and
\Delta_{0}
given in (
4.8
) and (
4.12
)
differ from
4v(\rho_{0})
and
2v(\rho_{0})
by quantities that are uniformly bounded in
n
as
n\to\infty
,
where
v(\rho_{0})
is as defined in (
4.17
). The variability
in the MLE of
\rho
is then on the scale
1/\max\{1,\sqrt{v(\rho_{0})}\}
.

Again, this scale of variation may be much larger than
\gamma_{0}

if
v(\rho_{0})
is much smaller than
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\}
.

However, the principal difference is in the quantity
\mu_{0}
that is responsible for
the bias in estimating
\rho
, which, from (
4.7
), (
4.10
), (
4.11
) and (
A.16
),
and because

(I-\rho W)^{-1}{\bf{1}}=(1-\rho)^{-1}{\bf{1}}
, can be expressed in the following

Bias formula:
assuming that
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
, and that
x_{\rho}^{(2)},\ldots,x_{\rho}^{(m)}
are the remaining
vectors in the expression (
4.16
) for
H_{\rho}
, the bias in the MLE is approximately given by
-\mu_{0}/\Delta_{0}
,
where

\mu_{0}{\ =\ 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{0}\}}\ =\ 2(1-\rho_{0})^{-1}+\sum_{l=2}^{m}(x_{\rho_{0}}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}x_{\rho_{0}}^{(l)}.

In the simulations in
[
10
]
, the remaining columns of
X
were realized from independent
standard normally
distributed random variables, and therefore had no particular relationship with
A
or
W
, or with each other.

In such circumstances, they make no appreciable contribution to the bias. To see this, consider, for
simplicity, what happens if
\rho_{0}=0
.

Then, for
2\leq l\leq m
, the
orthonormal
vectors
x_{0}^{(l)}
can be thought of roughly as being columns of
X
,
divided by
|x^{(l)}|\approx\sqrt{n}
so as to have norm
1
;
‘roughly’ involves neglecting the small adjustments that are needed, so as to be made orthogonal to
{\bf{1}}
and
to each other.
As a result, using (
3.3
) and (
3.5
) (with
C:={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})
),
it follows that typical values of
(x_{0}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{0}x_{0}^{(l)}=(x_{0}^{(l)})^{\top}Wx_{0}^{(l)}
are roughly centred
around
n^{-1}{\mathrm{Tr}}\,W=0
, with standard deviation
n^{-1}\bigl({\mathrm{Tr}}\{{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(W+W^{\top})^{2}\}\bigr)^{1/2}
.

Hence they are individually negligible when compared to
\tau_{0}\asymp\{{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WW^{\top}\}\}^{1/2}
, and
thus have no appreciable effect on the bias. The same is true for their sum, if
m
is not large.
A similar, but more complicated, argument leads to the same conclusion for
\rho_{0}\neq 0
. This phenomenon is
confirmed in the simulations of
[
10
]
. However, if the underlying graph encodes some community
structure, with more edges between members of the same community than outside, and if the regression is designed
to investigate community related effects, then the resulting contributions to
\mu_{0}
can be as important as that from
fitting an overall mean, and can have a corresponding effect on the bias.
Model
M2
of Section
5.2
is of this form.

4.4
Ill conditioned graphs

If the weight matrix associated with the underlying network is ill conditioned, there are consequences for
estimation, both of
\rho
and of
\beta
. Taking
\beta
first,
the distribution of
\hat{\beta}_{\rho_{0}}
from (
2.8
) on the model
M_{\rho_{0}}
has covariance matrix

\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}S_{\rho_{0}}X)^{-1}
,
as in (
2.11
), where
S_{\rho}:=K_{\rho}^{\top}K_{\rho}
. It is shown in Appendix
A.4
that
the variance of the linear combination
a^{\top}\beta
, for any given
m
-dimensional unit vector
a
, is bounded
above by

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}S_{\rho_{0}}X)}\ \leq\ \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}X)\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(S_{\rho_{0}})},

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(M)
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(M)
denote the smallest and largest eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix
M
.
Thus, for fixed
\sigma^{2}
, provided that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(S_{\rho_{0}})
is not close to zero,
linear combinations of
\beta
can be accurately estimated if
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}X)
is large, which is the usual
condition for ordinary least squares. And, even if
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(S_{\rho_{0}})
is small, it may still be the
case that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}S_{\rho_{0}}X)
is comparable to
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}X)
, if the space spanned by the
columns of
X
does not contain vectors that are substantially shrunk by
S_{\rho_{0}}^{1/2}
.

If
\rho_{0}
is not accurately estimated, it will typically be the case that
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
is used to estimate
\beta
,
for some value
\rho\neq\rho_{0}
. In such cases, it is
shown in Appendix
A.4
that linear combinations of
\beta
can be estimated using
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}

to an accuracy comparable to that obtained using
\hat{\beta}_{\rho_{0}}
,
provided that
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(S(\rho,\rho_{0}))
is not large, where

S(\rho,\rho_{0}):=S_{\rho}^{1/2}S_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}S_{\rho}^{1/2}
. However, if
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(S(\rho,\rho^{\prime}))
can be large
for values of
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
that are plausible, estimation of
\beta
may be rather less accurate. In particular,
it is shown in Appendix
A.4
that a large value of
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(W^{\top}W)

indicates that problems with the estimation of
\beta
may also arise; see Example 1 in Section
4.4
.

For bias and variability in the estimation of
\rho
, it has already been shown that problems can
easily arise in dense networks, without
W
having any unusual structure. However, these problems can
be accentuated by particular networks and design matrices. Again assuming that
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
,
this is because the trace
{v(\rho_{0})=}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})\}
in (
4.17
)
can be very much smaller than

Example 2.

Example 3.

A further, more extreme example, is the one considered

Hence, in particular,

{{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}Z_{\rho}^{\top}+Z_{\rho}^{2}\}=2\Bigl\{\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^{2}}+\frac{n-1}{(n-1+\rho)^{2}}\Bigr\},}

Returning to the estimation of

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(I-H_{0})WW^{\top}\}\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{(I-H_{*})W^{2}\}\ =\ \frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{I-H_{*}\}\ =\ \frac{1}{n-1}.

First, allowing a more general design matrix

so that the

As a result, it follows that

so that, from (

), (

) and (
4.21

\tau_{0}^{2}\ =\ 8{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}(I-H_{0})Z_{\rho_{0}}(I-H_{0})\}\ =\ \frac{8}{(n-1+\rho_{0})^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{I-H_{0}\}\ =\ \frac{8(n-m)}{(n-1+\rho_{0})^{2}}\ \approx\ \frac{8}{n}\,.

Similar calculations show that, using (
4.20
) and (
4.21
) in (
4.12
), there is great simplification,
yielding

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\tau_{0}^{2}+2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{-Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{0}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{0})+H_{0}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\tau_{0}^{2}+2\Bigl\{\frac{1}{(1-\rho_{0})^{2}}+\frac{m-1}{(n-1+\rho_{0})^{2}}\Bigr\}{\ =\ \frac{2}{(1-\rho_{0})^{2}}+O(n^{-1})},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{0}Z_{\rho_{0}}\}\ =\ \frac{2}{1-\rho_{0}}{-\frac{m-1}{n-1+\rho}}.

(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}y\ =\ (I-H_{0})(I-\rho W)y\ =\ \Bigl\{1+\frac{\rho}{n-1}\Bigr\}(I-H_{0})y,

For the complete graph, the likelihood gives no information

For this choice of

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{}-{\textstyle{\frac{2}{n}}}\,\bigl\{\log(1-\rho)+(n-1)\log\{1+\rho/(n-1)\}\bigr\}

5
Estimation using a quadratic form

The conclusions of Section
4
indicate that, in dense graphs, the MLE of the
correlation parameter
\rho

in the network disturbance model may quite generally exhibit significant bias and variability.
The ill–conditioned networks of Section
4.4
indicate that these effects can be
dramatic. Because of the Cramér–Rao bound given in (
3.7
), it is not possible to avoid the
variability, whatever estimator of
\rho
is used. However, estimators with smaller bias can be envisaged;
see
[
1
]
,
[
19
]
and
[
18
]
for progress in the context of correcting the bias in
the MLE for network effects models, when the variability is small.
In this section, we move away from the MLE, and instead propose a simple and intuitive estimator of
\rho
,
which suffers much less from bias, even in circumstances
in which all estimators have substantial variability.

5.1
A simple estimator of
\rho

An important element in
n\frac{dF(\rho;y)}{d\rho}
, the quantity that is set equal to zero for the MLE,
is the quadratic form

(K_{\rho}y)^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})(Z_{\rho}+Z_{\rho}^{\top})(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}y

in
y
, as can be seen in (
4.2
). Here, we replace this quadratic form with
a simpler one. Since, under the model
M_{\rho_{0}}
from (
2.7
), the
innovations
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}=(K_{\rho_{0}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, are independent,
even at neighbouring vertices,

the expectation of
(K_{\rho_{0}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})_{i}(K_{\rho_{0}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})_{j}
is zero for all
i\neq j
.
On the other hand,
for
\rho\neq\rho_{0}
, the expectation of
(K_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})_{i}(K_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})_{j}
under
M_{\rho_{0}}
is typically not zero,
if
i
and
j
are neighbours. For example, if
\rho_{0}=0
, then, for
i\neq j
,

{\sigma^{-2}}{\mathbb{E}}_{0}\{(K_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})_{i}(K_{\rho}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})_{j}\}\ =\ \{(I-\rho W)(I-\rho W)^{\top}\}_{ij}\ =\ -\rho(W_{ij}+W_{ji})+\rho^{2}(WW^{\top})_{ij},

(5.1)

which is not zero whenever
\rho\neq 0
is small enough, if one of
W_{ij}
and
W_{ji}
is non-zero.
This suggests building sums

T^{C}_{\rho}(y)\ :=\ (K_{\rho}{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(y))^{\top}CK_{\rho}{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(y)

(5.2)

of products of the form

(K_{\rho}{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(y))_{i}(K_{\rho}{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(y))_{j}
, where
{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(y):=K_{\rho}^{-1}(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}y
is the vector of
residuals after fitting
M_{\rho}
, as derived in (
2.10
); thus

T^{C}_{\rho}(y)\ =\ y^{\top}K_{\rho}^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})C(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}y.

(5.3)

The matrices
C
, having non-negative coefficients that do not depend on the data or on
\rho
,
and with all diagonal elements equal to zero, are to be suitably chosen.
Any such sum
T^{C}_{\rho}(y)
, for fixed
C
and
y
, should take values close to zero
when
\rho
is close to
\rho_{0}
, and this can potentially be exploited to gain information about the value of
\rho
.
As indicated in (
5.1
), the elements of
C
should be chosen so as to give larger weight to
pairs
(i,j)
where
W_{ij}+W_{ji}
is large, and a natural choice is thus to take
C={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(W+W^{\top})
,
or, equivalently, to take
C=W
. Another intuitive choice, reflecting the structure of the underlying graph,
rather than the model weights, is to take
C=A
, roughly analogous to the lag-one autocovariance in time series
analysis.

Recalling that, on
M_{\rho_{0}}
,
\mathbf{y}=X\beta+K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}
, it follows that, on
M_{\rho_{0}}
,

{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ K_{\rho}^{-1}(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu},

since
(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}X=0
, and hence that, on
M_{\rho_{0}}
,

T^{C}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ \boldsymbol{\nu}^{\top}Q_{C}(\rho,\rho_{0})\boldsymbol{\nu},

(5.4)

where, much as in (
4.4
),

Q_{C}(\rho,\rho_{0})\ :=\ (K_{\rho}K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1})^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})C(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}.

(5.5)

From (
5.4
) and (
5.5
), we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{0}}T^{C}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ \sigma^{2}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(I-H_{\rho_{0}})C\}\ =\ -\sigma^{2}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho_{0}}C\}\ =:\ \sigma^{2}{\hat{\mu}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}},

(5.6)

since
C
has zero diagonal, and so
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{C\}=0
.
Equation (
5.6
) suggests the following ‘method of moments’ procedure for estimating
\rho
.

Elementary estimate of

\rho
: find
\rho={\hat{\rho}}_{C}
so that

T^{C}_{\rho}(y)+{\hat{\sigma}}_{\rho}^{2}(y){\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho}C\}\ =\ 0.

(5.7)

Equation (
5.7
) is used to
correct the bias that would result, in view of (
5.6
), if, in estimating
\rho
,
T^{C}_{\rho}(y)
were itself
set equal to zero.

Procedure
:

Input:

a network with weight matrix
W
, a linear model with design matrix
X
and observed data
y
,
and a non-negative coefficient matrix
C
with zero diagonal.

Function:

for any value of
\rho
:

1.

Calculate the trace
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho}C\}
, using (
2.9
) and (
2.2
);

2.

Calculate the parameter estimates
{\hat{\beta}}_{\rho}(y)
and
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(y)
using (
2.8
);

3.

Calculate
{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{\rho}(y)
, using
{\hat{\beta}}_{\rho}(y)
and (
2.10
);

4.

Calculate the value of
T^{C}_{\rho}(y)
from (
5.2
).

Solution:

use a root finding algorithm to find the zero of
T^{C}_{\rho}(y)+{\hat{\sigma}}_{\rho}^{2}(y){\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho}C\}
,
which is the estimate
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
of the correlation
\rho
using
T^{C}
.

In this paper, we do not attempt to give detailed conditions under which the procedure
using (
5.7
) gives good estimates
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
of
\rho
. It is clear from the example of
the complete graph in Section
4.4
that this
cannot always be so; indeed, for the complete graph, (
5.7
) has no solution. Instead,
we carried out some simulations, illustrating that
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
can be effective,
within the limitations of what is possible. These are discussed in Section
5.2
below.
In the remainder of this section, we give a rough guide to its precision from a theoretical standpoint,
as well as proposing a permutation procedure for assessing its precision based on the data to hand.

The scale of variation of
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}

To determine the scale of variation about
\rho_{0}
of the resulting estimator
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
, write

U^{C}_{\rho}(y)\ :=\ T^{C}_{\rho}(y)+{\hat{\sigma}}_{\rho}^{2}(y){\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho}C\}.

(5.8)

As in the discussion of the MLE around (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) in Section
4
,
a plausible measure is given by

{\hat{\psi}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}:={\hat{\tau}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}/|{\widehat{\Delta}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}|
, to be estimated by
{\hat{\tau}}^{C}_{{\hat{\rho}}_{C}}/|{\widehat{\Delta}}^{C}_{{\hat{\rho}}_{C}}|
, where

({\hat{\tau}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}})^{2}\ :=\ \sigma^{-4}{\rm Var}_{\rho_{0}}(U^{C}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathbf{y}))\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\widehat{\Delta}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}\ :=\ {-}\sigma^{-2}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho_{0}}\Bigl\{\frac{dU^{C}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})}{d\rho}\Bigr|_{\rho=\rho_{0}}\Bigr\}.

The quantity
({\hat{\tau}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}})^{2}
is dominated by the trace of a matrix,

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(I-H_{\rho_{0}})(C+C^{\top})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})(C+C^{\top})\},

(5.9)

which is reminiscient of the definition of
\tau^{2}_{0}
in (
4.8
),
and the quantity
{\widehat{\Delta}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}
has leading term

{\mathrm{Tr}}\bigl\{(I-H_{\rho_{0}}){[Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}(I-H_{\rho_{0}})-Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}}]}(C+C^{\top})\bigr\}.

(5.10)

The derivations, and more detailed expressions, are given in Section
A.5
.
As before, the scale of variation

{\hat{\psi}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}\ :=\ {\hat{\tau}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}/|{\widehat{\Delta}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}|,

(5.11)

determined from (
5.9
) and (
5.10
),
need not correspond precisely to the standard deviation of
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
on
M_{\rho_{0}}
,
because
U^{C}_{\rho}(y)
is not linear as a function of
\rho
, and because the derivative
of
U^{C}_{\rho}(y)
may fluctuate appreciably about its expectation.

To get an idea of the magnitude of
{\hat{\psi}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}
, let
C=W
, and
suppose that
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
and that
X={\bf{1}}
, so that only the overall mean is being fitted. Then,
as in Section
4.3
,

K_{\rho}X=(1-\rho){\bf{1}}
, implying that, for all
\rho
,
H_{\rho}=H_{*}:=n^{-1}{\bf{1}}{\bf{1}}^{\top}=WH_{\rho}
and that

Z_{\rho}H_{\rho}=(1-\rho)^{-1}H_{*}
. As a result, we easily deduce that

({\hat{\tau}}^{W}_{\rho_{0}})^{2}\ =\ {2}({\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{2}+WW^{\top}-H_{*}WW^{\top}\}-1)+O(n^{-1}),

and that

{\widehat{\Delta}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}\ =\ -{\mathrm{Tr}}\{WZ_{\rho_{0}}+WZ_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}-H_{*}WZ_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\}+(1-\rho)^{-1}+n^{-1}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{*})\},

where we have used the full expressions in (
A.25
) and (
A.30
) instead of
(
5.9
) and (
5.10
) to obtain the smaller order terms.
In particular, if
\rho_{0}=0
, so that
Z_{\rho_{0}}=W
,
{{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}}({\hat{\tau}}^{W}_{0})^{2}=-{\widehat{\Delta}}^{C}_{0}+O(n^{-1})
,
and the magnitude of
{\hat{\psi}}^{C}_{0}
is roughly
2/{\hat{\tau}}^{W}_{0}
; this remains the case for
\rho_{0}

not too far from zero. As in the discussion in Section
3
, this implies that if, for instance,

W_{ij}\leq c/n
for all
i,j
, then the scale of variation is at least
1/\sqrt{c}
, and is appreciable
if
c
is not large. For
W:=D^{-1}A
, this corresponds to having a dense underlying graph, as before.

The justification for using (
5.7
) to estimate
\rho
relies only on the first two joint moments
of the vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
of errors, and does not assume that the components are independent or normally distributed, though they
should be uncorrelated. However, the formula (
A.25
), used in the theoretical discussion of the scale of
random variation
{\hat{\psi}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}
of the estimator
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
, is already based on stronger assumptions. For instance,
if the errors
\boldsymbol{\nu}
are independent
and have zero mean, but if
{\mathbb{E}}\{\sigma^{-4}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}^{4}\}\neq 3
, the expression (
3.4
) for the variance of
a quadratic form needs to be used in place of (
3.5
).

A permutation procedure for assessing the accuracy of
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}

Instead of assessing the precision of
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
using
{\hat{\psi}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}}
, some form of simulation procedure can be used.
One possibility is the following permutation algorithm.
Start by computing the esimated
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-residuals

{\hat{\nu}}(y)\ :=\ K_{{\hat{\rho}}_{C}}{\hat{\varepsilon}}_{{\hat{\rho}}_{C}}(y)\ =\ (I-H_{{\hat{\rho}}_{C}})K_{{\hat{\rho}}_{C}}y.

(5.12)

Since the components of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
are independent and identically
distributed, this suggests that synthetic data could be derived by replacing the
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-residuals
({\hat{\nu}}_{i}(y),\,1\leq i\leq n)

by permuted residuals

{\hat{\nu}}^{\pi}_{i}(y)\ :=\ {\hat{\nu}}_{\pi(i)}(y),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n,

(5.13)

where
\pi
is a randomly chosen permutation of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. These can be used to determine residuals

{\hat{\varepsilon}}^{\pi}(y)\ :=\ K_{{\hat{\rho}}_{C}}^{-1}{\hat{\nu}}^{\pi}

(5.14)

for the original model, and hence to generate synthetic data
y^{\pi}:=X{\hat{\beta}}+{\hat{\varepsilon}}^{\pi}(y)
. These can in turn be
analyzed in the same way as the original data, to provide an estimate
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}^{\pi}
of
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}(y)
.
Repeating this procedure for a large number
M
of independently chosen random permutations
\pi_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq M
,
yields a selection of estimates
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}^{\pi_{j}}
of
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}(y)
, whose empirical variability
should be much like that of the variability of
{\hat{\rho}}_{C}
about the model value
\rho_{0}
, when
\rho_{0}={\hat{\rho}}_{C}(y)
.

6
Network effects models

The network effects model (
1.4
) can be written in the form

y\ =\ K_{\rho}^{-1}X\beta+\varepsilon,

(6.1)

where the errors
\varepsilon
in
y
have the same dependence as in the network disturbance model, but the

model structure
explaining
y
also changes with
\rho
.

The corresponding log-likelihood, as in
[
3
, Equation (1.7)]
, is given by

{l(\theta;y)\ :=\ }-{\textstyle{\frac{n}{2}}}\{\log(\sigma^{2})+\log(2\pi)\}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(K_{\rho}y-X\beta)^{\top}(K_{\rho}y-X\beta)+\log\det K_{\rho},

(6.2)

where
\theta:=(\beta,\sigma^{2},\rho)
. Since the structural element
also contains information about
\rho
, this is reflected in the estimation procedures. In particular,
the estimate of
\beta
using maximum likelihood, for a given value of
\rho
, is

{\hat{\beta}}_{\rho}(y)\ :=\ (X^{\top}X)^{-1}XK_{\rho}y,

as can be deduced from ordinary least squares (
2.1
) applied to the data
y^{\prime}:=K_{\rho}y
. As a result, if the
true model is
M_{\rho^{\prime}}
, for
\rho^{\prime}\neq\rho
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho^{\prime}}{\hat{\beta}}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ (X^{\top}X)^{-1}XK_{\rho}K_{\rho^{\prime}}^{-1}X\beta.

(6.3)

This expectation is in general different from
\beta
, in which case the MLE of
\beta
is likely to be biased if
\rho
is
incorrectly estimated.
Hence, in order to get good estimates of the structural component
\beta
, it is important to have
an accurate estimate of
\rho
.

Now, differentiating (
6.2
) with respect to
\rho
, the
\rho
-component of the score function analogous
to (
3.1
) takes the form

\displaystyle{\tilde{s}}^{(\rho)}(\theta;y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2}(K_{\rho}y-X\beta)^{\top}Z_{\rho}K_{\rho}y-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}\},

(6.4)

where
Z_{\rho}:=WK_{\rho}^{-1}
. Its variance at
\theta_{0}
under
the model with
\theta=\theta_{0}
is now given by

V_{1}\ :=\ {\rm Var}_{\theta_{0}}\,{\tilde{s}}^{(\rho)}(\theta_{0};\mathbf{y})\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\}+\sigma_{0}^{-2}|Z_{\rho_{0}}X\beta_{0}|^{2},

(6.5)

containing an extra term, as compared to (
3.6
), that involves the parameter
\beta_{0}/\sigma_{0}
.
This implies that
the lower bound
1/V_{1}
for the variance of an unbiased estimator is smaller than in the analogous network
disturbance model.
This does not, of itself, mean that estimation of
\rho
can be made more precise, since there is no guarantee
that the lower bound can be attained; instead, a closer analysis is needed.

As an example, suppose that
X
consists solely of the column
{\bf{1}}
, with parameter
\beta
, and that
the weight matrix
W
satisfies

W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
. Then the additional term is just
n(\beta_{0}/(1-\rho_{0})\sigma_{0})^{2}
, a quantity that grows with
n
.
On the other hand, the network effects model (
6.1
) translates into

y\ =\ (1-\rho)^{-1}\beta{\bf{1}}+\varepsilon\ =\ {\tilde{\beta}}{\bf{1}}+\varepsilon,

which can also be viewed as a network disturbance model, with a new structural parameter
{\tilde{\beta}}=\beta/(1-\rho)
in place of
\beta
.
Thus
{\tilde{\beta}}
can be accurately estimated, if the network is large enough, as can be deduced from (
2.11
),
but, as before, any estimator of
\rho
exhibits
substantial variability if the network is dense. This explains the simulation results observed
in
[
10
]
, in which
the maximum likelihood estimators of both
\rho
and the coefficient of the constant exhibited substantial
variability, and were strongly negatively correlated. In the same experiments, the remaining
m-1
columns
X^{\prime}
of
X

had independent,
normally distributed entries with mean zero and variance
1
. In this case, with
\beta_{1}/(1-\rho)
replaced by
a parameter
{\tilde{\beta}}
and with
\beta^{\prime}:=(\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{m})^{T}
, the extra term in the variance of the score function reduces to

\sigma^{-2}{\mathbb{E}}\{|Z_{\rho}X^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}|^{2}\}\ =\ (|\beta^{\prime}|/\sigma)^{2}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}Z_{\rho}^{\top}\}

(where the expectation is with respect to the random choice of
X^{\prime}
), and, as in (
3.12
), this does not
become large with
n
for uniformly dense graphs. Hence there are still substantial limitations on the precision of estimation of
\rho
,
for Cramér–Rao reasons, and, in consequence, on the estimation of
\beta_{1}={\tilde{\beta}}(1-\rho)
also.

The same phenomenon makes itself felt if the model structure
X
is more complicated, but the columns of
WX

all belong to the linear span of the columns of
X
;
WX=X\Gamma
, for an
m\times m
matrix
\Gamma
.
In this case, the model structure
K_{\rho}^{-1}X\beta
can be expressed as

\displaystyle K_{\rho}^{-1}X\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I-\rho W)^{-1}X\beta\ =\ X\beta+\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}W^{l}X\beta

(6.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\beta+\sum_{l\geq 1}\rho^{l}X\Gamma^{l}\beta\ =\ X(I-\rho\Gamma)^{-1}\beta\ =:\ X\gamma,

where the new parameter
\gamma:=(I-\rho\Gamma)^{-1}\beta
. Once again, this yields a network disturbance
model, in which the parameter
\gamma
can be accurately estimated if
n
is large, whereas, in dense networks,
the estimator of
\rho
may exhibit substantial variability. For the original parameter
\beta
, this would
express itself in variable, but strongly dependent, estimators of the components of
\beta=(I-\rho\Gamma)\gamma
, which are
linked through the common value of the estimator of
\rho
.

In general,
WX
can be written in the form

WX\ =\ X\Gamma+E,

(6.7)

where
E
is an
n\times m
matrix, whose columns are orthogonal to those of
X
,
which measures how far
WX
lies from the linear span of the columns of
X
.
In practice, except for the model in which only the overall mean is to be estimated,
and
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
by construction, it is usual to expect that
E
is not precisely
the zero matrix.
It is shown in Appendix
A.6
that, if the quantity

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\{E^{\top}E\}+{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{2}+WW^{\top}\}\ =\ \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\{(WX)^{\top}(I-H)WX\}+{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{2}+WW^{\top}\}

is not large, then neither
\rho
nor
\beta
can be accurately estimated.

Conclusion 6:
In the network effects model,
if the columns of
WX
(almost) belong to the space generated by the columns of
X
, then estimating
\rho

and the parameters
\beta
in uniformly dense networks may both be problematic.

7
Conclusion

Maximum likelihood estimation of the correlation parameter
\rho
in the network disturbance and network effects
models suffers from substantial bias and variability when the underlying graph is dense, as has been
documented, for instance, in
[
10
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
4
]
,

[
11
]
and
[
8
]
. In the context of the network disturbance model (
2.7
),
we have shown
that, in dense networks, there is a lower bound to the variability of
any
reasonable
estimator of
\rho
, as a consequence of Cramér–Rao theory, and that increasing the size
of the network does not reduce the variability, if the density remains constant. We also demonstrate that,
under similar circumstances, the maximum likelihood estimator is significantly biased. For certain ill conditioned
networks, these effects are particularly marked. In Section
5.1
, we
propose a simple estimator of
\rho
that has little bias, though its variability in dense networks is
still substantial, because all estimators are subject to the limitations imposed by the Cramér–Rao
lower bound. We also suggest a permutation algorithm for assessing the variability of the estimator,
on the basis of data consisting of a single network. The effectiveness of the new estimator is illustrated
by simulation
in a few simple settings in Section
5.2
. There is a discussion of network effects
models in Section
6
.

Here, in circumstances in which
\rho
cannot be accurately
estimated, the estimates of the structural parameters
\beta
are also affected. An explicit quantity is given that
indicates whether such problems are likely to occur. It is expressed in terms
of the design matrix
X
and the weight matrix
W
.

Appendix

A.1
Rewriting the likelihood equation for
\rho

To minimize the quantity
F(\rho;y)
of (
2.14
), it is usual to look for values of
\rho

such that
\frac{d}{d\rho}F(\rho;y)=0
. First, as for (
3.1
),

\frac{d}{d\rho}\,\log\det K_{\rho}\ =\ -{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}\}.

(A.1)

Then the derivative of
{\hat{\sigma}}_{\rho}^{2}
in (
2.8
) is given by

n\,\frac{d{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}_{\rho}(y)}{d\rho}\ =\ y^{\top}\Bigl\{-W^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})K_{\rho}-K_{\rho}^{\top}\Bigl(\frac{dH_{\rho}}{d\rho}\Bigr)K_{\rho}-K_{\rho}^{\top}(I-H_{\rho})W\Bigr\}y.

(A.2)

To compute
\frac{d}{d\rho}H_{\rho}
, note that, for any family of invertible matrices
(Y_{x},\,x\in\mathbb{R})
with
differentiable components,
0=\frac{d}{dx}\{Y_{x}^{-1}Y_{x}\}
, implying that

\frac{d}{dx}\{Y_{x}^{-1}\}\ =\ -Y_{x}^{-1}\frac{d}{dx}\{Y_{x}\}Y_{x}^{-1}.

(A.3)

Hence, writing
Y_{\rho}:=((K_{\rho}X)^{\top}K_{\rho}X)
, and using
\frac{dK_{\rho}}{d\rho}=-W
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{dY_{\rho}^{-1}}{d\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y_{\rho}^{-1}\{X^{\top}W^{\top}K_{\rho}X+X^{\top}K_{\rho}^{\top}WX\}Y_{\rho}^{-1}.

Thus, noting for the last line that
W=Z_{\rho}K_{\rho}
and that
K_{\rho}XY_{\rho}^{-1}(K_{\rho}X)^{\top}=H_{\rho}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{\rho}}{d\rho}

\displaystyle=

A.3
Details of the MLE when structure is present

Suppose that
X
consists solely of the
n
-vector
{\bf{1}}
, having all elements equal to
1
, and that
there are no isolated vertices.
Suppose also that

is chosen to have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}=1
for all

, so that
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
.
Then
H_{\rho}=H_{*}:=n^{-1}{\bf{1}}{\bf{1}}^{\top}
for all
\rho
,
because
K_{\rho}X=(I-\rho W){\bf{1}}=(1-\rho){\bf{1}}
, and hence

and
Z_{\rho}H_{\rho}=(1-\rho)^{-1}H_{*}

The matrix product in (
4.8
) can be multiplied out to give

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})\}

(A.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{2(Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})-2H_{\rho_{0}}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})^{2}+[H_{\rho_{0}}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})]^{2}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{\rho_{0}})\}-2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{\rho_{0}}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})^{2}-[H_{\rho_{0}}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})]^{2}\}.

With the particular choices of
X
and
W
, and repeatedly using the properties of the trace, this gives

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4{\mathrm{Tr}}\{(Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{*})\}+{2}\Bigl\{\frac{1}{(1-\rho_{0})^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}|Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}|^{2}\Bigr\},

where, for instance, the final term is just
-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{*}Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\}

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{*}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}\}\ =\ \frac{1}{1-\rho_{0}}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{*}Z_{\rho_{0}}\}\ =\ \frac{1}{1-\rho_{0}}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{*}\}\ =\ \frac{1}{(1-\rho_{0})^{2}}\,.

In much the same way, and in particular because

H_{*}(I-H_{*})=0
, we obtain

2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{-Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{*}(Z_{\rho_{0}}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top})(I-H_{*})+H_{*}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\}\ =\ 2{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}H_{*}\}\ =\ \frac{2}{(1-\rho_{0})^{2}}\,,

giving the formula for

in (

Note that

\frac{1}{n}|Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}|^{2}\ \geq\ \Bigl\{\frac{1}{n}{\bf{1}}^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}\Bigr\}^{2}\ =\ \frac{1}{(1-\rho_{0})^{2}}\,,

by Cauchy–Schwarz, giving the upper bound for
\tau_{0}^{2}
in (
4.15
).
Note also that, again using
Z_{\rho}H_{\rho}=(1-\rho)^{-1}H_{*}
, and because
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{*}\}=1
and
H_{*}^{2}=H_{*}
,
it follows that

\displaystyle{\textstyle{\frac{1}{4}}}\tau_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\}-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{H_{*}Z_{\rho_{0}}(H_{*}Z_{\rho_{0}})^{\top}\}-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}H_{*}\}

(A.14)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\}-\frac{1}{(1-\rho_{0})^{2}}\,,

so that

is indeed smaller than
4{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}+Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\}
.

Suppose now that there is more structure in the regression, so that
X
consists of columns in addition to the
first column
{\bf{1}}
. If we still assume that
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
, the basic message remains the same.
Because the matrix

is idempotent, its spectral decomposition can be written as

H_{\rho}\ =\ H_{*}+\sum_{l=2}^{m}x_{\rho}^{(l)}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top},

(A.15)

where
H_{*}:=n^{-1}{\bf{1}}{\bf{1}}^{\top}
is as before, and

are

orthogonal to
{\bf{1}}
that, with
{\bf{1}}
, span the space generated by the columns of

.
If, for instance, it is assumed that each of the vectors

,

, has components bounded in modulus by
cn^{-1/2}
, for some fixed
c\geq 1
—
as is the case for

— then the traces of the matrices
Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}H_{\rho_{0}}
,

Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}}
,
(Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}})^{2}
and

can all be shown to be bounded by
m^{2}c^{4}/(1-|\rho|)^{2}
, which is not large if
m
and

are moderate
and
|\rho|
is not too close to
1
. The argument runs as follows.

In view of (
4.16
), and writing

, the trace
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{MH_{\rho}\}
,
for any
n\times n
matrix

, can be written in the form

{\mathrm{Tr}}\{MH_{\rho}\}\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{m}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Mx_{\rho}^{(l)}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}\}\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{m}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Mx_{\rho}^{(l)}.

(A.16)

Thus, using (
2.6
), it follows that, for any
1\leq l,l^{\prime}\leq m
,

\displaystyle|Z_{\rho}x_{\rho}^{(l)}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{r\geq 0}W^{r+1}\rho^{r}x_{\rho}^{(l)}\Bigr|

(A.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r\geq 0}|\rho|^{r}cn^{-1/2}|W^{r+1}{\bf{1}}|\ =\ \frac{c}{n^{1/2}(1-|\rho|)}|{\bf{1}}|\ =\ \frac{c}{1-|\rho|}\,;

that

\displaystyle|(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho}x_{\rho}^{(l^{\prime})}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{r\geq 0}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}W^{r+1}\rho^{r}x_{\rho}^{(l^{\prime})}\Bigr|

(A.18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\Bigl|\sum_{r\geq 0}|\rho|^{r}(cn^{-1/2}{\bf{1}}^{\top})W^{r+1}(cn^{-1/2}{\bf{1}})\Bigr|}\ =\ c^{2}n^{-1}{{\bf{1}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}}\sum_{r\geq 0}|\rho|^{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}}{1-|\rho|}\,;

and that

\displaystyle|(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho}^{2}x_{\rho}^{(l^{\prime})}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}W^{2}(1-\rho W)^{-2}x_{\rho}^{(l^{\prime})}|\ =\ \Bigl|\sum_{r\geq 0}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}(r+1)W^{r+2}\rho^{r}x_{\rho}^{(l^{\prime})}\Bigr|

(A.19)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c^{2}n^{-1}{\bf{1}}^{\top}{\bf{1}}\sum_{r\geq 0}(r+1)|\rho|^{r}\ =\ \frac{c^{2}}{(1-|\rho|)^{2}}\,.

Then, for instance, from (
A.16
) and (
A.19
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}H_{\rho_{0}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}x_{\rho}^{(l)}\ \leq\ \frac{mc^{2}}{(1-|\rho|)^{2}}\,;

from (
A.16
) and (
A.17
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}x_{\rho}^{(l)}\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{m}|Z_{\rho_{0}}x_{\rho}^{(l)}|^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{mc^{2}}{(1-|\rho|)^{2}}\,;

and, from (
A.16
) and (
A.18

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}H_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}H_{\rho_{0}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}H_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}x_{\rho}^{(l)}\ =\ \sum_{l=1}^{m}{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}x_{\rho}^{(l)}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}H_{\rho_{0}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{m}(x_{\rho}^{(l^{\prime})})^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}x_{\rho}^{(l)}(x_{\rho}^{(l)})^{\top}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}x_{\rho}^{(l^{\prime})}\ \leq\ \frac{m^{2}c^{4}}{(1-|\rho|)^{2}}\,.

By similar calculations, each of the traces listed above is bounded in modulus by the quantity
m^{2}c^{4}/(1-|\rho|)^{2}
.

A.4
Estimation of
\beta

From (
2.8
) and (
2.11
),
the distribution of
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
on the model
M_{\rho}
has covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}
,
where
S_{\rho}:=K_{\rho}^{\top}K_{\rho}
. The variance of a linear combination
a^{\top}\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
,
for a given
m
-dimensional unit vector
a
, is thus

\sigma^{2}a^{\top}(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}a\ \leq\ \sigma^{2}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\{(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}\}\ =\ \sigma^{2}/\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X),

(A.20)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(M)\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(M)
denote the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
M
, both positive if
M

is positive definite symmetric.
Letting
{\tilde{u}}_{X}
denote the unit
n
-vector
Xu/|Xu|
, and using Rayleigh–Ritz twice, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\colon|u|=1}u^{\top}X^{\top}S_{\rho}Xu\ =\ \min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\colon|u|=1}|Xu|^{2}{\tilde{u}}_{X}^{\top}S_{\rho}{\tilde{u}}_{X}

(A.21)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(S_{\rho})\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\colon|u|=1}|Xu|^{2}\ =\ \lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}X)\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(S_{\rho}).

Hence, for fixed
\sigma^{2}
, provided that
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(S_{\rho})
is not close to zero, (
A.20
)
and (
A.21
) imply that accurate estimation of
all linear combinations of
\beta
depends on having a large value of
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}X)
, which is the usual
condition for ordinary least squares. In particular, if the network size
n
becomes large, but

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(S_{\rho})
remains uniformly bounded away from zero, the estimate of
\beta
is consistent if

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X^{\top}X)\to\infty
. Example 1 in Section
4.4
shows that the latter condition
alone is not enough to guarantee consistent estimation of
\beta
.

If the true value of
\rho
is
\rho_{0}
, the distribution of
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}(\mathbf{y})
, as determined by substituting

\mathbf{y}=X\beta+K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}
for
y
into (
2.8
), is multivariate normal, with mean
\beta
and covariance
matrix
\sigma^{2}\Sigma(\rho,\rho_{0})
, where

\displaystyle\Sigma(\rho,\rho_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}(K_{\rho}X)^{\top}K_{\rho}K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1}(K_{\rho_{0}}^{-1})^{\top}K_{\rho}^{\top}K_{\rho}X(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}

(A.22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}X^{\top}S_{\rho}(S_{\rho_{0}})^{-1}S_{\rho}X(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}.

Writing
\widetilde{X}_{\rho}:=S_{\rho}^{1/2}X(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}
, where
M^{1/2}
denotes the non-negative square root of
a non-negative definite
matrix
M
, and setting
S(\rho,\rho_{0}):=S_{\rho}^{1/2}(S_{\rho_{0}})^{-1}S_{\rho}^{1/2}
, (
A.22
) gives

\Sigma(\rho,\rho_{0})\ =\ \widetilde{X}_{\rho}^{\top}S(\rho,\rho_{0})\widetilde{X}_{\rho}.

(A.23)

Using Rayleigh–Ritz, for any unit
m
-vector
a
, we thus have

\displaystyle a^{\top}\Sigma(\rho,\rho_{0})a\ =\ (\widetilde{X}_{\rho}a)^{\top}S(\rho,\rho_{0})\widetilde{X}_{\rho}a

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\widetilde{X}_{\rho}a|^{2}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(S(\rho,\rho_{0}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{a^{\top}(X^{\top}S_{\rho}X)^{-1}a\}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(S(\rho,\rho_{0})).

Thus, if the true model is
M_{\rho_{0}}
, estimating a linear combination
a^{\top}\beta
using
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
instead
of
\hat{\beta}_{\rho_{0}}
gives an estimator
a^{\top}\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
whose variance is at most
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(S(\rho,\rho_{0}))

times the value, given in (
A.20
), that it would have had, were
M_{\rho}
the true model.
Hence, if estimation on the model
M_{\rho}
,
using
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
, is accurate for any
\rho
, then estimating using
\hat{\beta}_{\rho}
on the model
M_{\rho_{0}}
is also
accurate, provided that
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(S(\rho,\rho_{0}))
is not large.

Note that, if
n(W):=\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(W^{\top}W)}
denotes the operator norm of the matrix
W
, then,
by Rayleigh–Ritz, because
|Wy|\leq n(W)|y|
for all
y
, the largest eigenvalue
of
S(\rho,0)=I-\rho(W+W^{\top})+\rho^{2}W^{\top}W
is at least
(|\rho|n(W)-1)^{2}
, and this
is large, for moderate non-zero values of
\rho
, if
n(W)
is large. Hence, if
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(W^{\top}W)
is large, this
indicates that there may possibly be problems with estimating
\beta
.

\displaystyle({\hat{\tau}}^{C}_{\rho_{0}})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{-4}{\rm Var}_{\rho_{0}}(U^{C}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathbf{y}))\ =\ {\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathrm{Tr}}\bigl\{\{{\widehat{Q}}(C+C^{\top},\rho_{0})\}^{2}\bigr\}.

(A.25)

In view of the factor
n^{-1}

{\widehat{Q}}(C,\rho_{0})

{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}{\mathrm{Tr}}\bigl\{\{Q_{C}(\rho_{0},\rho_{0})\}^{2}\bigr\}\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{(I-H_{\rho_{0}})C(I-H_{\rho_{0}})(C+C^{\top})\},

, now with

To find

A.6
Details of network effects estimation

In the network effects model, except when only the overall mean is to be estimated,
and
W{\bf{1}}={\bf{1}}
by construction, it is usual to expect that

WX\ =\ HWX+(I-H)WX\ =\ X\Gamma+E,

(A.31)

for a non-zero
n\times m
matrix
E
. Here,
H:=X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}
is the projection onto the space spanned by
the columns of
X
;
HWX
is the corresponding projection of the columns of
WX
, and can thus be written in the
form
X\Gamma
; and
E:=(I-H)WX
has columns orthogonal to the columns of
X
. Then, as in the calculation leading
to (
6.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle K_{\rho}^{-1}X\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X(I-\rho\Gamma)^{-1}\beta+\rho K_{\rho}^{-1}E\beta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\gamma+\rho K_{\rho}^{-1}E(I-\rho\Gamma)\gamma\ =:\ X_{\rho}\gamma,

say, parametrized in terms of
\gamma
. The log-likelihood, in terms of
\gamma
, becomes

\ell(\gamma,\sigma^{2},\rho;y)\ =\ -{\textstyle{\frac{n}{2}}}\{\log(\sigma^{2})+\log(2\pi)\}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\{K_{\rho}(y-X_{\rho}\gamma)\}^{\top}\{K_{\rho}(y-X_{\rho}\gamma)\}+\log\det K_{\rho},

and differentiating with respect to
\rho
gives the analogue of the score function in (
6.4
) to be

\displaystyle{\tilde{s}}^{(\rho)}(\gamma,\sigma^{2},\rho;y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigl(\{K_{\rho}(y-X_{\rho}\gamma)\}^{\top}Z_{\rho}\{K_{\rho}(y-X_{\rho}\gamma)\}\bigr)

(A.32)

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\{K_{\rho}(y-X_{\rho}\gamma)\}^{\top}G_{\rho}\gamma-{\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho}\},

where

\displaystyle G_{\rho}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle K_{\rho}\frac{d}{d\rho}\{\rho K_{\rho}^{-1}E(I-\rho\Gamma)\}

(A.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(I-\rho\Gamma)-\rho K_{\rho}^{-1}WE(I-\rho\Gamma)-\rho E\Gamma.

The variance of
{\tilde{s}}^{(\rho)}(\gamma_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2},\rho_{0};y)
on the model with parameters
(\gamma_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2},\rho_{0})
is now

V_{2}\ :=\ {\mathrm{Tr}}\{Z_{\rho_{0}}^{2}+Z_{\rho_{0}}Z_{\rho_{0}}^{\top}\}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}|G_{\rho_{0}}\gamma|^{2},

(A.34)

differing from (
6.5
) in the second term. The corresponding Cramér–Rao lower bound for
the variance of any unbiased estimator of
\rho
is thus at least as big as
1/V_{2}
.

Just as for the network
disturbance model, the first term in (
A.34
) may not be large in dense networks.
To get an idea of the magnitude of the second term,
consider the case where
\rho_{0}=0
, so that
G_{0}=E
and
|G_{0}\gamma|^{2}=\gamma^{\top}E^{\top}E\gamma
.
Then

\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}|G_{0}\gamma|^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{|\gamma|^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(E^{\top}E),

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(E^{\top}E)
denotes the largest eigenvalue of the positive definite symmetric
m\times m
matrix
E^{\top}E
.
For instance, if the elements of
E
, that represent to what extent the columns of
WX
do not belong
to the linear span of the columns of
X
, are uniformly of magnitude at most
c/\sqrt{n}
,
then the elements of
E^{\top}E
are at most of magnitude
c^{2}
, and thus
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(E^{\top}E)
is not
large if
c
is not large. In such circumstances, any reasonable
estimator of
\rho
has to have substantial variability. In practice, the model decomposition (
A.31
)
can be applied to yield the value of

\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\{E^{\top}E\}\ =\ \lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\{(WX)^{\top}(I-H)WX\},

and, if neither this nor
{\mathrm{Tr}}\{W^{2}+WW^{\top}\}
is large, then neither
\rho
nor
\beta
can be accurately estimated.
