Clean Single-Valued Polylogarithms This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Algebraic Structures in Perturbative Quantum Field Theory in honor of Dirk Kreimer for his 60th birthday. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Kreimer.html

Abstract
We define a variant of real-analytic polylogarithms that are single-valued and that satisfy “clean” functional relations that do not involve any products of lower weight functions. We discuss the basic properties of these functions and, for depths one and two, we present some explicit formulas and results. We also give explicit formulas for the single-valued and clean single-valued version attached to the Nielsen polylogarithms S n , 2 ​ ( x ) S_{n,2}(x) , and we show how the clean single-valued functions give new evaluations of multiple polylogarithms at certain algebraic points.

Introduction

1.1
Background and first definitions

The logarithm function and generalisations of it have originally been studied, having first been mentioned (in 1696) in correspondence between (Johann) Bernoulli and Leibniz
[
45
, p. 351]
, by many mathematicians, notably by Abel
[
1
]
and Kummer
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
with regard to their functional properties, and by Lobachevsky
[
47
]
and later by Schläfli in connection with volume functions in hyperbolic space (for a far more comprehensive list of the early bibliography see Lewin’s book
[
46
, pp. 349–353]
).
Over the last 40–50 years, seminal works on the dilogarithm, pioneered by Bloch
[
5
]
in algebraic geometry and algebraic K-theory and by ’t Hooft and Veltman
[
57
]
in connection with quantum field theory, have led to a renaissance of interest in those functions and have triggered many new and often unexpected and surprisingly parallel developments, resulting in “cross-fertilisation” from which both mathematics (keyword “mixed motives (over a field)”) and physics (keyword “Feynman integrals”) communities have benefited.

The logarithm is a complex multi-valued function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and it can be defined on its principal branch
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
by the integral

\displaystyle\log x:=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t},\qquad x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0].

The most prominent generalisations of the logarithm function are the so-called
classical polylogarithms
, defined for integers
n>0
by

\displaystyle\li_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{k^{n}}.

The integer
n
is called the
weight
.
The series converges for
|x|<1
. It can be analytically continued to a multi-valued function on the whole complex plane via the integral representation

\displaystyle\li_{n}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\li_{n-1}(t)\frac{{\rm d}t}{t},\qquad n>1,

and the recursion starts with
\li_{1}(x)=-\log(1-x)
.
Classical polylogarithms are not rich enough to cover all the generalisations of the logarithm that appear in mathematics and physics. A broader class of generalisations of the logarithm function are
multiple polylogarithms
(MPL’s) (also known as hyperlogarithms), which were first introduced in the works of Poincaré, Kummer and Lappo-Danilevsky
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
and have recently reappeared in both mathematics
[
16
,
34
,
33
]
and physics
[
3
,
29
,
54
]
. Multiple polylogarithms can be defined by the iterated integral

\displaystyle I(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}):=\int_{x_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}<x_{n+1}}\frac{{\rm d}t_{1}}{t_{1}-x_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{{\rm d}t_{n}}{t_{n}-x_{n}},

(1.1)

where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. The integer
n
is again called the weight, and the number of non-zero elements of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is called the depth. The notation

\displaystyle I_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}):=I\big(0;x_{1},\{0\}^{m_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{k},\{0\}^{m_{k}-1};1\big)

is often employed to write a depth
k
integral, where
\{a\}^{n}
denotes
a
repeated
n
times. The integral implicitly depends on the choice of a path going from
x_{0}
to
x_{n+1}
, where the integration variables
x_{i}
are considered to be ordered on the path. Depending on the values of the
x_{i}
, the integral in (
1.1
) may diverge and requires regularisation. This can be done by introducing suitable tangential base points, cf.
[
19
, Chapter 15]
.
The class of functions defined by (
1.1
) contains the logarithm and classical polylogarithm functions as special cases, e.g., for generic values of
x_{0}
,
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,

\displaystyle I(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})=\log\bigg(\frac{x_{1}-x_{2}}{x_{1}-x_{0}}\bigg),

\displaystyle I\big(0;1,\{0\}^{n};x_{0}\big)=-\li_{n+1}(x_{0}).

The definition of MPL’s in (
1.1
) implies that they satisfy the following basic relations common to all iterated integrals (cf., e.g.,
[
16
]
):

1.

Path reversal
(here we assume the same path for both, except that it is being traversed in opposite directions):

\displaystyle I(x_{n+1};x_{n},\ldots,x_{1};x_{0})=(-1)^{n}I(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

2.

Path composition
(for any
x\in\mathbb{C}
and any path from
x_{0}
to
x_{n+1}
avoiding any
x_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq n
)):

\displaystyle I(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\sum_{p=0}^{n}I(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{p};x)I(x;x_{p+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

3.

Shuffle product
(in this equality we assume the same path for each iterated integral):

\displaystyle I(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{m};x)I(x_{0};x_{m+1},\ldots,x_{m+n};x)=\!\!\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(m,n)}\!I(x_{0};x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(m+n)};x),

where
\Sigma(m,n)=\big\{\sigma\in S_{m+n}\colon\sigma^{-1}(1)<\!\cdots\!<\sigma^{-1}(m)\text{ and }\sigma^{-1}(m+1)<\!\cdots\!<\sigma^{-1}(m+n)\big\}
is the set of shuffles of
m
and
n
elements, and
S_{m+n}
is the group of permutations on
m+n
elements.

1.2
Identities among polylogarithms

The identities at the end of the previous section are special, in the sense that they relate many different MPL’s evaluated at the same arguments, albeit in a different order. More interesting are identities involving a single type of function evaluated at different arguments. The most famous identity involving dilogarithms is arguably the five-term relation due to Abel (cf., e.g.,
[
46
, Chapter 1.5]
), a version of which where the order of the five arguments for
\li_{2}(z)
defines a 5-cycle (
1-z_{i}=z_{i+2}z_{i-2}
, indices mod 5) being given by

\displaystyle\li_{2}(x)+\li_{2}(y)+\li_{2}\bigg(\frac{1-x}{1-xy}\bigg)+\li_{2}(1-xy)+\li_{2}\bigg(\frac{1-y}{1-xy}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\zeta_{2}-\log x\log(1-x)-\log y\log(1-y)+\log\bigg(\frac{1-x}{1-xy}\bigg)\log\bigg(\frac{1-y}{1-xy}\bigg),

(1.2)

with
\zeta_{n}:=\li_{n}(1)
.
Since the logarithm and dilogarithm are multi-valued functions, it is important to specify the branches of the functions and the ranges for
x
and
y
for which this identity holds. It is straightforward to check that on the principal branches of the logarithm (branch cut from
\-\infty
to
0
) and dilogarithm (branch cut from 1 to
\infty
) the identity in (
1.2
) holds whenever
|x|+|y|<1
. It has often been claimed in the literature, without explicit proof, as a kind of “folklore” statement, that every functional equation for
\li_{2}
with arguments being rational functions in finitely many variables is a linear combination of this five-term relation. Wojtkowiak proved it for the 1-variable case
[
61
]
, and for a recent proof of the general statement we refer to a recent preprint by de Jeu
[
17
]
.

Seminal non-trivial identities involving logarithms and classical polylogarithms of higher weight have been found, e.g., by Kummer
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
(in two variables, up to weight 5), Goncharov (in three variables, weight 3)
[
32
]
, Wojtkowiak (in many variables, weight 3)
[
60
]
and by Gangl (in two variables, up to weight 7
[
24
,
26
]
; in four variables, weight 4
[
27
]
), as well as many others, and particularly interesting recent findings are given by Golden, Goncharov, Spradlin, Vergu and Volovich
[
31
]
, Charlton
[
13
]
, Radchenko
[
52
]
and Goncharov–Rudenko
[
37
]
. No such results beyond weight 7 are currently known.
There are also families of (sometimes called “trivial”) identities in one variable known for all weights relating a specific classical polylogarithm at different arguments, and possibly products of logarithms, in particular the
distribution relations
, valid as power series in the unit disk

\displaystyle\li_{n}(x^{m})=m^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\li_{n}\hskip-3.0pt\big(x\xi_{m}^{k}\big),\qquad|x|<1,\quad\xi_{m}^{m}=1,

(1.3)

and the
inversion relations
,

\displaystyle\li_{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{x}\bigg)=(-1)^{n+1}\li_{n}(x)-\frac{(-2\pi{\rm i})^{n}}{n!}B_{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\log(-x)}{2\pi{\rm i}}\bigg),

(1.4)

where
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
and
B_{n}(\alpha)
are the Bernoulli polynomials, defined by the generating series

\displaystyle\frac{t{\rm e}^{\alpha t}}{{\rm e}^{t}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}(\alpha)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

(1.5)

Much less is known about identities satisfied by MPL’s of depth greater than one, although the basic shuffle and stuffle relations were established by Goncharov in
[
34
]
, along with a generalisation of the distribution relations to any fixed MPL of depth greater than one, and an “inversion-reversion” relation
[
34
, Section 2.6, formulas (33) and (34)]
valid on the unit
m
-torus.
The study of MPL identities of depth greater than one has recently obtained new impetus from physics, where MPL’s and their identities play an important role in the computation of scattering amplitudes in quantum field theory, cf., e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
20
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
. Goncharov
[

]
introduced the arguably most important invariant for multiple polylogarithms, its “symbol”. Based on techniques to compute it, developed originally for quantum field theory calculations
[
56
]
, new functional identities for polylogarithms of different depth have been found,
for example: a 40-term trilogarithm identity whose arguments arise from a single cluster algebra is obtained in
[

]
; a new family of functional equations for
\li_{4}
are given in
[

]
, based on a depth reduction in weight 4; various relations between weight 4 MPL’s of any depths are given in
[

, including a reduction of a certain 5-term combination of

to depth 1, from which a highly symmetric 4-variable

1.3
Clean single-valued polylogarithms and their identities

As already mentioned, the multi-valuedness of MPL’s implies that identities among them are to be understood as holding on appropriate branches. In order to circumvent this cumbersome issue, it is useful to replace any MPL’s by a version of it that, while only real-analytic, has the virtue that it is single-valued. For example, the original single-valued version of the dilogarithm was given by Bloch and by Wigner
1
1

1

This is the mathematician David Wigner, as opposed to the arguably better known physicist Eugene (incidentally his father).

[
5
]
and generalised to polylogarithms (implicitly) by Ramakrishnan
[
53
]
and (explicitly) by Wojtkowiak
[
59
]
and Zagier
[
62
]
. The latter author proposed in fact several versions, the most standard one being defined as

\displaystyle P_{n}(x):=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\Bigg\{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{2^{k}B_{k}}{k!}\log^{k}|x|\li_{n-k}(x)\Bigg\},

(1.6)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{n}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Re},&\text{if}\ n\text{ odd},\\
\operatorname{Im},&\text{if}\ n\text{ even},\end{cases}

(1.7)

and Re and Im denote the real and imaginary parts respectively.
Moreover,
B_{k}:=B_{k}(0)
, are the Bernoulli numbers, defined as the constant terms of the Bernoulli polynomials defined above.
A rather different single-valued version was given by Brown

[
7
,
8
]
. We will relate the two explicitly in Section
3.2
.
The functions in equation (
1.6
) satisfy a “product-free” variant of the five-term relation in (
1.2
)

\displaystyle P_{2}(x)+P_{2}(y)+P_{2}\bigg(\frac{1-x}{1-xy}\bigg)+P_{2}(1-xy)+P_{2}\bigg(\frac{1-y}{1-xy}\bigg)=0,\vskip-4.30554pt

(1.8)

and of the distribution and inversion relations in (
1.3
) and (
1.4
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P_{n}\big(x^{m}\big)=m^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}P_{n}\big(x\xi_{m}^{k}\big),\\
&P_{n}\big(x^{-1}\big)=(-1)^{n+1}P_{n}(x),\qquad n>1.\end{split}

(1.9)

Since the functions
P_{n}(z)
are single-valued, the identities in (
1.9
) are valid for all complex numbers
x\not=0
, while the five-term relation in (
1.8
) holds for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus L
, where
L
is the union of curves defined by
x=0
,
x=1
,
y=0
,
y=1
and
xy=1
.

The identities in (
1.8
) and (
1.9
) have an additional feature compared to their analogues in (
1.2
), (
1.3
) and (
1.4
): they do not involve product terms of
(poly-)
logarithms of lower weights! We refer to an identity with this property, in line with standard terminology, e.g.,
[
62
, Section 6]
, as a
clean
identity. More generally, roughly stated for every identity involving classical polylogarithms of weight
\leq n

we can obtain a clean identity by replacing
\li_{n}
by
P_{n}
and dropping all product terms. For the precise statement we refer to
[
62
, Propositions 2 and 3]
.
For MPL’s of higher depths, however, in general no real-analytic analogues are known that satisfy clean versions of identities between the iterated integrals in (
1.1
).
In the classical case, the product-freeness of relations permits one to mimic the functional behaviour via rather simple general (linear and multi-linear) algebraic tools, more precisely of quotients of free abelian groups like the so-called higher Bloch groups. For the latter groups the relations arise from taking only the non-product terms in a functional equation for
\li_{n}
, i.e., non-linear contributions are simply being ignored. In a similar way, one might hope that the clean functions give rise to “simpler” higher depth analogues of said Bloch groups, without the need to consider products of lower weight terms. One of the main results of this paper is to define such functions for all weights and depths. In the remainder of this section we summarise our main result.

It is possible to lift the iterated integrals
I(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})
, for
x_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, to motivic versions
I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})
, which live in a ring of motivic periods
\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
(see, e.g.,
[
9
,
10
,
12
,
35
]
). The ring
\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
is graded by the weight of the MPL’s, and we denote the subspace of weight
n
by
\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},n}
, and
\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0}:=\bigoplus_{n>0}\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},n}
. The iterated integrals in (
1.1
) can be retrieved from their motivic avatars through the period homomorphism
\per\colon\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}\to\mathbb{C}
, which is conjectured to be injective
[
38
]
.
Therefore, it is expected that all relations among MPL’s arise from relations among their motivic avatars. Within the motivic setting, we prove the following result in Section
4
:

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})
there is a real-analytic single-valued function
I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})
such that for every linear combination of motivic MPL’s that can be reduced to products,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{k,0};x_{k,1},\ldots,x_{k,n};x_{k,n+1})\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0}\cdot\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0},\qquad c_{k}\in\mathbb{Q},

there is a clean identity where products are mapped to zero in going from
I^{\mathfrak{m}}
to
I^{\mathrm{csv}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{k,0};x_{k,1},\ldots,x_{k,n};x_{k,n+1})=0.

The paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
we review some basic facts about graded and connected Hopf algebras and the Dynkin operator. In Section
3
we review the Hopf algebra on (de Rham) multiple polylogarithms, and we introduce the single-valued projection, which assigns to every multiple polylogarithm a real-analytic single-valued analogue. In Section
4
we define the clean version of single-valued multiple polylogarithms, and we discuss their basic properties. In particular, we show that they satisfy Theorem
1.1
. In Section
5
we present some examples of clean single-valued MPL’s in depths 1 and 2, and in Section
6
we explicitly compute the single-valued and clean single-valued versions of the Nielsen polylogarithm
S_{n,2}
. Finally in Section
7
we derive some explicit numerical evaluations of depth 2 MPL’s using this machinery and some known functional equations.

2
Graded connected Hopf algebras and the Dynkin operator

This section reviews material from
[
40
,
50
,
51
,
55
]
.
Let
H
be a graded connected commutative Hopf algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
. The counit is simply the augmentation map
\epsilon\colon H\to H_{0}\simeq\mathbb{Q}
, and we have a splitting

\displaystyle H=H_{0}\oplus H_{>0},\qquad\text{with}\quad H_{>0}:=\ker\epsilon.

The multiplication in
H
is denoted by
m
and the coproduct by
\Delta
. For
x\in H_{>0}
it takes the form

\displaystyle\Delta(x)=1\otimes x+x\otimes 1+\Delta^{\prime}(x),\qquad\Delta^{\prime}(x)\in H_{>0}\otimes H_{>0}.

The antipode for
x\in H_{>0}
is uniquely determined in a recursive way by

\displaystyle 0=m(\id\otimes S)\Delta(x)=S(x)+x+m(\id\otimes S)\Delta^{\prime}(x).

2.1
The convolution product

Let
R
be a unital
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra, with multiplication
m_{R}
and unit
u_{R}\colon\mathbb{Q}\to R
. Let
\varphi,\psi\colon H\to R
be
\mathbb{Q}
-linear maps. Their convolution is the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map

\displaystyle\varphi\star\psi:=m_{R}(\varphi\otimes\psi)\Delta.

The co-associativity of
\Delta
implies associativity of the convolution product. The set of all
\mathbb{Q}
-linear maps from
H
to
R
equipped with the convolution product forms a unital
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra, whose unit is
u_{R}\epsilon\colon H\to R
. Moreover, if
\varphi\colon H\to R
is an algebra morphism, then it is invertible for the convolution product, and the inverse is simply composition with the antipode,
\varphi^{\star-1}:=\varphi S
. In particular, the antipode is the inverse of the identity for the convolution product,
\id^{\star-1}=S
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a linear map
\varphi\colon H\to H
is:

(1)

a
derivation
, if it satisfies
\varphi m=m(\varphi\otimes\id+\id\otimes\varphi)
,

(2)

a
co-derivation
, if it satisfies
\Delta\varphi=(\varphi\otimes\id+\id\otimes\varphi)\Delta
,

(3)

an
infinitesimal character
, if it satisfies
\varphi m=m(\varphi\otimes\epsilon+\epsilon\otimes\varphi)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
H
be a graded, connected, commutative Hopf algebra and let
\varphi\colon H\to H
be a derivation. Then
S\star\varphi
is an infinitesimal character.

Proof.

We denote by
\tau\colon H\otimes H\to H\otimes H
the operator that swaps the factors in a tensor product,
\tau(a\otimes b)=b\otimes a
. We have

\displaystyle(S\star\varphi)m

\displaystyle=m(S\otimes\varphi)\Delta m=m((Sm)\otimes(\varphi m))(\id\otimes\tau\otimes\id)(\Delta\otimes\Delta)

\displaystyle=m(m\otimes m)(S\otimes S\otimes\varphi\otimes\id+S\otimes S\otimes\id\otimes\varphi)(\id\otimes\tau\otimes\id)(\Delta\otimes\Delta)

\displaystyle=m(m\otimes m)(\id\otimes\tau\otimes\id)(S\otimes\varphi\otimes S\otimes\id+S\otimes\id\otimes S\otimes\varphi)(\Delta\otimes\Delta)

\displaystyle=m((S\star\varphi)\otimes\epsilon+\epsilon\otimes(S\star\varphi)).

∎

2.2
The grading operator and the Dynkin operator

On every graded connected Hopf algebra there is a natural
grading operator

Y\colon H\to H
which acts on homogeneous elements by multiplication by the weight. It is both a derivation and a co-derivation:

\displaystyle Ym=m(Y\otimes\id+\id\otimes Y),

\displaystyle\Delta Y=(Y\otimes\id+\id\otimes Y)\Delta.

We now introduce the
Dynkin operator

D
on a graded connected Hopf algebra. For the origin of the name, see
[
51
]
and references therein.

Definition 2.3
.

The Dynkin operator on
H
is defined by

\displaystyle D:=S\star Y.

Since
\id\star S=\epsilon
, we can write the previous equation in the equivalent form

\displaystyle\id\star D=Y.

(2.1)

Since
Y
is a derivation,
D
is an infinitesimal character by Lemma
2.2
.
It is convenient to define the operator
\Pi
which is the identity on
H_{0}
and
\Pi=Y^{-1}D
on
H_{>0}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

1.

The kernel of
\Pi
is generated by all non-trivial products,
\ker\Pi=H_{>0}\cdot H_{>0}
.

2.

\Pi
is a projector,
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
.

Proof.

(1) Since
D
is an infinitesimal character, we have for all
x,y\in H_{>0}=\ker\epsilon
,

\displaystyle D(x\cdot y)=D(x)\cdot\epsilon(y)+\epsilon(x)\cdot D(y)=0,

and so
\Pi(x\cdot y)=0
. Hence
H_{>0}\cdot H_{>0}\subset\ker\Pi
.
Conversely, let
x\in\ker\Pi
. We can assume without loss of generality that
x\in H_{n}
,
n>1
. Again writing the
coproduct as
\Delta(x)=1\otimes x+x\otimes 1+\Delta^{\prime}(x)
, we find

\displaystyle 0=\Pi(x)=x+\frac{1}{n}m(S\otimes Y)\Delta^{\prime}(x),

and so

\displaystyle x=-\frac{1}{n}m(S\otimes Y)\Delta^{\prime}(x)\in H_{>0}\cdot H_{>0}.

(2) If

,
n>0

\displaystyle\Pi^{2}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\Pi\left[nx+m(S\otimes Y)\Delta^{\prime}(x)\right]=\Pi(x).

∎

3.2
Single-valued projection

Unlike motivic MPL’s, to which the period map assigns a (complex) number, de Rham MPL’s do not allow for an analogous construction. Instead, they can be equipped with a ring homomorphism
\sv\colon\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\text{MPL}}\to\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
, called the
single-valued projection
(cf.
[
12
, Section 8.3]
, and also
[
7
,
8
]
). The single-valued projection can be given explicitly in a combinatorial way on
\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\text{MPL}}

[
11
,
12
]
(see also
[
18
, Section 3.4]
),

\displaystyle\sv:=m(F_{\infty}\Sigma\otimes\id)\widetilde{\Delta},

where
m
is the multiplication in
\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
,
F_{\infty}\colon\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}\to\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
is the real Frobenius, which can be thought of as complex conjugation (i.e.,
\per F_{\infty}=\overline{\per}
, where
\overline{\per}(x)
denotes the complex conjugate of
\per(x)
) and
\Sigma\colon\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}\to\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\Sigma\big(I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\big):=(-1)^{n}\widetilde{S}\big(I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\big).

Here
\widetilde{\Delta}\colon\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\text{MPL}}\to\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}\otimes\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
and
\widetilde{S}\colon\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}\to\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}
are given by the same formulas as the coproduct
\Delta
and the antipode
S
on
\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\text{MPL}}
, with the replacement
I^{\mathfrak{dr}}\to I^{\mathfrak{m}}
everywhere.

The single-valued projection associates to every
I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})
a (family of) motivic periods, whose image under the period map defines a single-valued function of the
x_{i}
. We can compose the single-valued projection with the period map and the projection
\pi^{\mathfrak{dr}}
to associate to every motivic MPL its single-valued version:

\displaystyle\svmot:=\per\circ\sv\circ\pi^{\mathfrak{dr}}\colon\ \mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\text{MPL}}\to\mathbb{C}.

Example 3.1

(single-valued version of the motivic logarithm)
.

We can apply the previous construction to the motivic logarithm

\displaystyle\log^{\mathfrak{m}}x:=I^{\mathfrak{m}}(0;0;x),\qquad x\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0\}.

We have, with
\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x=\pi^{\mathfrak{dr}}(\log^{\mathfrak{m}}x)
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Delta\big(\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x\big)=\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x\otimes 1+1\otimes\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x,\\
&S\big(\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x\big)=-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x.\end{split}

The single-valued version attached to
\log^{\mathfrak{m}}x
is therefore

\displaystyle\svmot(\log^{\mathfrak{m}}x)=\log\overline{x}+\log x=\log|x|^{2}.

In particular, letting
x=-1
, we see that the single-valued version attached to
(\pi{\rm i})^{\mathfrak{m}}
is zero, i.e.,

\displaystyle\svmot\big((\pi{\rm i})^{\mathfrak{m}}\big)=0.

Example 3.2

(single-valued version of the classical motivic polylogarithm)
.

The motivic lift of the classical polylogarithm of weight
n
is

\displaystyle\li^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n}(x):=-I^{\mathfrak{m}}\big(0;1,\{0\}^{n-1};x\big),\qquad x\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}.

The coproduct and the antipode of
\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x):=\pi^{\mathfrak{dr}}\big(\li^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n}(x)\big)
are

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Delta\big(\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)\big)=\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)\otimes 1+1\otimes\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n-k}(x)\otimes\frac{\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)^{k}}{k!},\\
&S\big(\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)\big)=-\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{k}}{k!}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n-k}(x).\end{split}

(3.2)

The single-valued version attached to the classical motivic polylogarithm of weight
n
is then

\displaystyle\svmot\big(\li^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n}(x)\big)=\li_{n}(x)-(-1)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-\log|x|^{2})^{k}}{k!}\li_{n-k}(\overline{x}).

(3.3)

Letting
x=1
in (
3.3
), we obtain the single-valued version associated to the motivic zeta values,
\zeta_{n}^{\mathfrak{m}}:=\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{m}}(1)
,
n>1

[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\svmot(\zeta_{n}^{\mathfrak{m}})=\svmot\big(\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{m}}(1)\big)=\begin{cases}2\zeta_{2m+1},&n\ \text{odd},\\
0,&n\ \text{even}.\end{cases}

(3.4)

These functions are closely related, but not identical, to Zagier’s single-valued version of the classical polylogarithms from (
1.6
). The relationship is most conveniently expressed in terms of the function

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{B_{k}}{k!}\log^{k}|x|^{2}\big(\li_{n-k}(x)-(-1)^{n}\li_{n-k}(\overline{x})\big)=\begin{cases}2P_{n}(x)&\text{if $n$ odd},\\
2{\rm i}P_{n}(x)&\text{if $n$ even}.\end{cases}

Proposition 3.3
.

For
n>0
and
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\displaystyle\svmot\big(\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{m}}(x)\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\log^{k}|x|^{2}}{(k+1)!}\mathcal{P}_{n-k}(x).

Proof.

We directly compute the right hand side, and show that it gives the expression for
\svmot(\li^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n}(x))
from (
3.3
). We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\log^{k}|x|^{2}}{(k+1)!}\mathcal{P}_{n-k}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-k-1}\frac{B_{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{\log^{k+\ell}{|x|^{2}}}{(k+1)!}\big(\li_{n-k-\ell}(x)-(-1)^{n-k}\li_{n-k-\ell}(\overline{x})\big).

By reindexing the sum with
\alpha=k+\ell
, we find it is equal to

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\alpha}\frac{B_{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{\log^{\alpha}{|x|^{2}}}{(\alpha+1-l)!}\big(\li_{n-\alpha}(x)-(-1)^{n+\alpha-l}\li_{n-\alpha}(\overline{x})\big)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{}=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n-1}\Bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\alpha}\frac{B_{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{1}{(\alpha+1-\ell)!}\Bigg)\li_{n-\alpha}(x)\log^{\alpha}{|x|^{2}}\\
&\hphantom{=}{}-(-1)^{n}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n-1}\Bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\alpha}(-1)^{\ell}\frac{B_{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{1}{(\alpha+1-\ell)!}\Bigg)(-\log{|x|^{2}})^{\alpha}\li_{n-\alpha}(\overline{x}).\end{split}

(3.5)

We notice

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\alpha}\frac{B_{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{1}{(\alpha+1-\ell)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)!}(B_{\alpha+1}(1)-B_{\alpha+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)!}((-1)^{\alpha+1}B_{\alpha+1}-B_{\alpha+1})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\alpha=0,\\
0,&\alpha\neq 0,\end{cases}

where we have used the symmetry
B_{\alpha+1}(1-x)=(-1)^{\alpha+1}B_{\alpha+1}(x)
, to find that
B_{\alpha+1}(1)=(-1)^{\alpha+1}B_{\alpha+1}
. The second case above follows by combining the odd
\alpha>0
case where the terms cancel, and the even
\alpha>0
case where the terms are identically zero. Likewise

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\alpha}\frac{B_{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{(\alpha+1-\ell)!}=\frac{(-1)^{\alpha+1}}{(\alpha+1)!}(B_{\alpha+1}(-1)-B_{\alpha+1}).

Now using the symmetry and multiplication theorems for Bernoulli polynomials, we have

\displaystyle B_{\alpha+1}(-x)=(-1)^{\alpha+1}B_{\alpha+1}(x)+(\alpha+1)(-1)^{\alpha+1}x^{\alpha+1-1},

so

\displaystyle B_{\alpha+1}(-1)=(-1)^{\alpha+1}B_{\alpha+1}(1)+(\alpha+1)(-1)^{\alpha+1}=B_{\alpha+1}+(\alpha+1)(-1)^{\alpha+1}.

So this sum is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\alpha+1}}{(\alpha+1)!}\big(B_{\alpha+1}+(\alpha+1)(-1)^{\alpha+1}-B_{\alpha+1}\big)=\frac{(\alpha+1)}{(\alpha+1)!}=\frac{1}{\alpha!}.

Inserting these evaluations into (
3.5
) shows that it is equal to

\displaystyle=\li_{n}(x)-(-1)^{n}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-\log{|x|^{2}})^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\li_{n-\alpha}(\overline{x})=\svmot(\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{m}}(x)),

as claimed.
∎

4.1
Definition

Definition 4.1

The

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}):=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\big[\cR(I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}))\big],

\displaystyle 0=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\left[\cR(A)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{k,0};x_{k,1},\ldots,x_{k,n};x_{k,n+1}),

Remark 4.2
.

It is possible to use Theorem
1.1
to obtain identities among (non-clean) single-valued polylogarithms. Indeed, it is often easier to find identities modulo product terms, e.g., by starting from identities that hold modulo shuffle products at the symbol level (cf.
[
13
,
15
]
). The combinatorics involved in
\cR
will restore all the product terms necessary to obtain a numerical identity between single-valued polylogarithms, up to a single constant of integration. In some cases this may even give hints for valid identities among the non-single-valued analogues, e.g., by dropping all terms depending on the complex-conjugated variables, and accounting for factors of 2 introduced by the single-valued map on real constants (e.g., (
3.4

can be applied directly to the symbol Hopf algebra to restore the (functional) product terms in a modulo products identity between functions (of holomorphic variables) at the symbol level; one can then study product terms involving constants iteratively via slices of the coaction.

Remark 4.3
.

The restriction to the real (resp. imaginary) part for odd (resp. even) weights in Definition
4.1
can be motivated by the fact that the other parity can be expressed entirely in terms of products of lower weights functions. To see this, we start from the following property of the single-valued projection on de Rham MPL’s (cf., e.g.,
[
18
]
):

Proposition 4.4
.

For every
x\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\mathrm{MPL},n}
, we have

\displaystyle F_{\infty}\sv(x)=(-1)^{n}\sv S(x).

Proof.

The following two properties are well known and hold in any commutative, graded and connected Hopf algebra (see, e.g.,
[
48
, Proposition I.7.1]
and references therein):

\displaystyle(S\otimes S)\tau\Delta=\Delta S,

\displaystyle S^{2}=\id.

Since
\widetilde{\Delta}
and
\widetilde{S}=(-1)^{Y}\Sigma
are defined by the same combinatorial formulas as
\Delta
and
S
, but with
I^{\mathfrak{dr}}
replaced by
I^{\mathfrak{m}}
, it is easy to see that the following identities hold:

\displaystyle(\Sigma\otimes\Sigma)\tau\widetilde{\Delta}=\widetilde{\Delta}S(-1)^{Y},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}=\id.

This gives, with
F_{\infty}^{2}=\id
,

\displaystyle F_{\infty}\sv

\displaystyle=F_{\infty}m(F_{\infty}\Sigma\otimes\id)\widetilde{\Delta}=m(\Sigma\otimes F_{\infty})\widetilde{\Delta}

\displaystyle=m(\id\otimes F_{\infty}\Sigma)\tau\widetilde{\Delta}S(-1)^{Y}=m\tau(F_{\infty}\Sigma\otimes\id)\widetilde{\Delta}S(-1)^{Y}

\displaystyle=\sv S(-1)^{Y}.

∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0}
. Then

\displaystyle\sv(x)+(-1)^{Y}F_{\infty}\sv(x)\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0}\cdot\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0}.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\mathrm{MPL},n}
,
n>0
.
Proposition
4.4
implies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sv(x)+(-1)^{n}F_{\infty}\sv(x)&=\sv\left(x+S(x)\right)\\
&=-\sv m(S\otimes\id)\Delta^{\prime}(x)\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0}\cdot\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathrm{MPL},>0},\end{split}

where the last equality follows from

.
∎

4.2
Elementary properties of clean single-valued polylogarithms

4.2.1
Shuffle products, path composition and reversal

The clean single-valued polylogarithms inherit the basic properties of iterated integrals (see Section
1.1
). Using Theorem
1.1
, we see that they take the form:

1.

Path reversal:

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{n+1};x_{n},\ldots,x_{1};x_{0})=(-1)^{n}I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

2.

Path composition:

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x)+I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x;x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

3.

Shuffle product:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(m,n)}\!\!\!I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(m+n)};x)=0.\end{split}

4.2.2
Reversal of arguments

Proposition 4.6
.

For
n>0
, we have

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{n},\ldots,x_{1};x_{n+1})=(-1)^{n+1}I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

Proof.

Consider the shuffle algebra generated by the letters
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. It is a Hopf algebra whose coproduct is deconcatenation and the antipode is the reversal of words, up to a sign:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{sh}}(w)=\sum_{uv=w}u\otimes v,

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{sh}}(w)=(-1)^{|w|}\widetilde{w},

where
\widetilde{w}
is the word
w
in reverse order, and
|w|
its length. If
m_{\mathrm{sh}}
denotes the shuffle multiplication, we have

\displaystyle 0=m_{\mathrm{sh}}(S_{\mathrm{sh}}\otimes\id)\Delta_{\mathrm{sh}}(w)=w+(-1)^{|w|}\widetilde{w}+m_{\mathrm{sh}}(S_{\mathrm{sh}}\otimes\id)\Delta^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sh}}(w).

If we take
w=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
, we see that
x_{1}\cdots x_{n}+(-1)^{n}x_{n}\cdots x_{1}
must vanish modulo non-trivial products. This relations must hold in every shuffle algebra, and so in particular the combination
I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{0};x_{n},\ldots,x_{1};x_{n+1})+(-1)^{n}I^{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})
must vanish modulo non-trivial products, from which we deduce Proposition
4.6
via Theorem
1.1
.
∎

4.2.3
Unshuffling of leading zeros

Proposition 4.7
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the following holds

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}\big(0;\{0\}^{k},x_{1},\{0\}^{n_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{r},\{0\}^{n_{r}-1};x_{r+1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{r}=k}\binom{n_{1}+i_{1}-1}{i_{1}}\cdots\binom{n_{r}+i_{r}-1}{i_{r}}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}\times I^{\mathrm{csv}}\big(0;x_{1},\{0\}^{n_{1}-1+i_{1}},\ldots,x_{r},\{0\}^{n_{r}-1+i_{r}};x_{r+1}\big).

Proof.

This is proven by induction. The case
k=0
holds trivially wherein both sides are identical, so we may suppose this formula holds for all
n\leq k
. Now observe

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}\big(0;\{0\}^{k+1},x_{1},\{0\}^{n_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{r},\{0\}^{n_{r}-1};x_{r+1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}n_{j}I^{\mathrm{csv}}\big(0;\{0\}^{k},x_{1},\{0\}^{n_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{j},\{0\}^{(n_{j}-1)+1},\ldots,x_{r},\{0\}^{n_{r}-1};x_{r+1}\big),

using the shuffle product property (
3
) above. Substituting the induction assumption into the second line shows that each term in the result is indexed by a composition
i_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+i_{r}^{\prime}=k+1
, added to the exponents
n_{1}-1,\ldots,n_{r}-1
of the original integral. Therefore we need only to compute the coefficient and check that it matches the one claimed in the formula.

This coefficient is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}n_{j}(-1)^{k}\binom{n_{1}+i_{1}^{\prime}-1}{i_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\binom{(n_{j}+1)+(i_{j}^{\prime}-1)-1}{(i_{j}^{\prime}-1)}\cdots\binom{n_{r}+i_{r}^{\prime}-1}{i_{r}^{\prime}},

where
i_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+i_{r}^{\prime}=k+1
. Observe that

\displaystyle n_{j}\binom{(n_{j}+1)+(i_{j}^{\prime}-1)-1}{(i_{j}^{\prime}-1)}=i_{j}^{\prime}\binom{n_{j}+i_{j}^{\prime}-1}{i_{j}^{\prime}},

so the coefficient is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}i_{j}^{\prime}\binom{n_{1}+i_{1}^{\prime}-1}{i_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\binom{n_{j}+i_{j}^{\prime}-1}{i_{j}^{\prime}}\cdots\binom{n_{r}+i_{r}^{\prime}-1}{i_{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}\underbrace{\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{r}i_{j}^{\prime}\Bigg)}_{k+1}\binom{n_{1}+i_{1}^{\prime}-1}{i_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\binom{n_{r}+i_{r}^{\prime}-1}{i_{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(-1)^{k+1}\binom{n_{1}+i_{1}^{\prime}-1}{i_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\binom{n_{r}+i_{r}^{\prime}-1}{i_{r}^{\prime}},

as claimed.
∎

4.3
Recursion and the total holomorphic differential of
\boldsymbol{I^{\rm csv}}

Proposition 4.8
.

Write the following shorthand

\displaystyle C(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}):=\cR\big[I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\big],

(4.1)

then
C
satisfies the following recursive formula

\displaystyle\begin{split}&C(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\\
&{}-\frac{1}{n}\Bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
(i,j)\neq(0,n)\end{subarray}}(j-i)I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})\Bigg].\end{split}

(4.2)

Proof.

Since
Y=\id\star D
, the Dynkin operator satisfies the following recursion, valid in every graded commutative Hopf algebra
H
:

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle=nx-m(\id\otimes D)\Delta^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=nx-m(\id\otimes(Y\cdot\Pi))\Delta^{\prime}(x),\qquad x\in H_{n},\quad n>0.

(4.3)

This gives

\displaystyle(\sv\circ\Pi)(I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}))={}

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}m(\sv\otimes(Y\cdot(\sv\circ\Pi)))\Delta^{\prime}(I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})).

Because of the projector
\Pi
in the second entry of the tensor product, we only need to consider terms in the reduced coproduct that have no product in the second entry, cf. (
3.1
)). This constraint is described via the infinitesimal coproduct (cf.
[
10
]
), and one obtains the recursive formula directly therefrom, wherein we must exclude the case
(i,j)=(0,n)
because we have taken the reduced coproduct.
∎

Since
I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\big[C(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\big]
, (
4.2
) can be interpreted as a recursion for the clean single-valued MPL’s.

We recall now that the total differential of an MPL is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}I(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}I\big(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1}).

The function
C
satisfies a similar formula for the total holomorphic differential
\partial
. There is no correspondingly simple formula for the total antiholomorphic differential, though, since the single-valued map only preserves the holomorphic differential.

Proposition 4.9
.

The total holomorphic differential of the function
C
is given in weight
1
by

\displaystyle\partial C(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})=\mathrm{d}I(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2}),

and in weight
n>1
by

\displaystyle\partial C(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\frac{n-1}{n}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{n}C(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial C(x_0; x_1,\ldots,x_n; x_{n+1}) = \frac{n-1}{n} \Bigg[}{}-C(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1};x_{n})\mathrm{d}I(x_{0};x_{n};x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial C(x_0; x_1,\ldots,x_n; x_{n+1}) = \frac{n-1}{n} \Bigg[}{}-C(x_{1};x_{2},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\mathrm{d}I(x_{0};x_{1},x_{n+1})\Bigg].

Proof.

We prove this via the recursion in Proposition
4.8
; one can check directly the case
n=1
. Namely, we aim to compute

\displaystyle\partial C(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})=\partial I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2}),

where we have computed
C(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})=I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})
either via the recursion in Proposition
4.8
or directly from the definition. The holomorphic derivative is reserved by the single-valued map, so we immediately obtain

\displaystyle\partial C(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})=\sv\mathrm{d}I(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2})=\mathrm{d}I(x_{0};x_{1};x_{2}),

since the total derivative of the weight 1 function is rational, and hence single-valued already.

Note that the total holomorphic differential of
I^{\mathrm{csv}}
is given by the same formula as for the total differential of
I
, with
I\mapsto I^{\mathrm{sv}}
but with
\mathrm{d}I
unchanged, namely

\displaystyle\partial I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1}).

Now for weight
n
the recursion implies

\displaystyle\partial C(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\partial I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
(i,j)\neq(0,n)\end{subarray}}\bigg[\frac{j-i}{n}\cdot\partial I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
(i,j)\neq(0,n)\end{subarray}}\bigg[}{}+\frac{j\!-\!i}{n}\cdot I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\partial C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})\bigg].

Then by taking care of terms which cross the jump
x_{i}
,
x_{j+1}
, we can write

\displaystyle\partial I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}+\sum_{k=j+2}^{n}I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}+I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\mathrm{d}I(x_{i-1};x_{i};x_{j+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}+I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+2},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\mathrm{d}I(x_{i};x_{j+1};x_{j+2}).

Correspondingly, by the induction hypothesis, for
j-i>1
we get

\displaystyle\partial C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{j-i-1}{j-i}\Bigg[\sum_{k=i+1}^{j}C\big(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1}\big)\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=\frac{j-i-1}{j-i}\Bigg[}{}-C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j-1};x_{j})\mathrm{d}I(x_{i};x_{j};x_{j+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=\frac{j-i-1}{j-i}\Bigg[}{}-C(x_{i+1};x_{i+2},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})\mathrm{d}I(x_{i};x_{i+1};x_{j})\Bigg],

and for
j-i=1
we find
\partial C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})=\mathrm{d}I(x_{i};x_{i+1};x_{i+2})
.

Now we note that the
\mathrm{d}I
terms in
\partial C
occur only with certain fixed patterns, namely
\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1})
wherein all arguments are consecutive, and either
\mathrm{d}I(x_{i};x_{i+1};x_{j})
and/or
\mathrm{d}I(x_{i};x_{j};x_{j+1})
wherein the first two, respectively last two, are consecutive arguments.

So first we ask what the coefficient of
\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1})
, for fixed
k
, is. It is seen to be the following, where the first line arises from differentiating the
I^{\mathrm{sv}}
appearing outside the sum, the second and third line arise from differentiating the
I^{\mathrm{sv}}
inside the sum, the fourth line from differentiating
C
inside the sum, and the last line deals with the edge case where one has differentiated
C(x_{i};x_{i+1};x_{i+1})
when
j=i+1
, for
j=k
in order to obtain
\mathrm{d}I(x_{k-1};x_{k};x_{k+1})
. (Note that the corresponding term in line 4 gives 0 in this case, so no extra restriction is necessary there.)

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
(i,j)\neq(0,n)\\
k<i\end{subarray}}\frac{j-i}{n}I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
(i,j)\neq(0,n)\\
j+1<k\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\frac{j-i}{n}I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)C(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq n\\
(i,j)\neq(0,n)\\
i<k<j+1\end{subarray}}\frac{j-i}{n}\,\frac{j-i-1}{j-i}I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,,x_{i},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{-}{}\times C\big(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1}\big)-\frac{1}{n}I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

This is nothing but the recursion for
C
applied to

\displaystyle\frac{n-1}{n}C\big(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{k}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big).

Now we ask about the coefficient of
\mathrm{d}I(x_{k},x_{k+1};x_{\ell+1})
, where
k+2<\ell+1
for non-consecutive arguments. This term arises from either differentiating the
I^{\mathrm{sv}}
term in

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},x_{k+1},x_{\ell+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})C(x_{k+1};x_{k+2},\ldots,x_{\ell};x_{\ell+1}),

where
(i,j)=(k+1,\ell)
, or by differentiating the
C
term in

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k},x_{\ell+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})C(x_{k};x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{\ell};x_{\ell+1}),

where
(i,j)=(k,\ell)
. We note that for each choice of
(k,\ell)\neq(0,n)
such that
0\leq k<\ell\leq n
with
k+1<\ell
, both terms contribute to the coefficient. However when
(k,\ell)=(0,n)
only the
I^{\mathrm{sv}}
derivative contributes as
(i,j)=(k,\ell)=(0,n)
is excluded from the summation, and the
C
derivative therewith.

When
(k,\ell)\neq(0,n)
we find the coefficient of
\mathrm{d}I(x_{k};x_{k+1};x_{\ell})
to be

\displaystyle\frac{\ell-(k+1)}{n}I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\widehat{x_{k+1}},x_{\ell+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}\big)C(x_{k+1};x_{k+2},\ldots,x_{\ell};x_{\ell+1})

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\frac{\ell\!-k}{n}\frac{\ell-k\!-1}{\ell-k}I^{\mathrm{sv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k},x_{\ell+1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}\times C\big(\widehat{x_{k}};x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{\ell};x_{\ell+1}\big)\!=0.

However when
(k,\ell)=(0,n)
we find the coefficient of
\mathrm{d}I(x_{0};x_{1};x_{n+1})
to be

\displaystyle\frac{\ell-(k+1)}{n}I^{\mathrm{sv}}\big(x_{0};\widehat{x_{1}};x_{n}\big)C(x_{1};x_{2},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\frac{n-1}{n}C(x_{1};x_{2},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}).

Exactly the same consideration applies to the coefficient of
\mathrm{d}I(x_{k};x_{\ell};x_{\ell+1})
, wherein the terms pairwise cancel, except for the case
(k,\ell)=(0,n)
, where only one term occurs which cannot cancel. This completes the proof of the formula for the total holomorphic derivative of
C
.
∎

We note that this differential formula is closely related to a recursion for the mod-products symbol
\Pi_{\bullet}\mathcal{S}
of an iterated integral, as given in
[
15
, equation (4)]

\displaystyle\Pi\mathcal{S}\big(I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};\ldots;x_{n+1})\big)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Pi\mathcal{S}\big(I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{j}},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\big)\otimes I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{j-1};x_{j};x_{j+1})

\displaystyle-\Pi\mathcal{S}\big(I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{1};x_{2},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})\big)\otimes I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{1};x_{n+1})

\displaystyle-\Pi\mathcal{S}\big(I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1};x_{n})\big)\otimes I^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x_{0};x_{n};x_{n+1}).

5
Examples in small depths

In this section we present results for clean single-valued polylogarithms in small depths.
The path composition formula, together with Proposition
4.7
and

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}(k\cdot x_{0};k\cdot x_{1},\ldots,k\cdot x_{n};k\cdot x_{n+1})=I^{\mathrm{csv}}(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1}),

with
x_{1}\neq x_{0}
,
x_{n}\neq x_{n+1}
and
k\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}

(this identity follows immediately from the corresponding identity for MPL’s, where it is a direct consequence of the integral representation (
1.1
)), imply that it is sufficient to consider the functions

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}):=I^{\mathrm{csv}}\big(0;x_{1},\{0\}^{m_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{k},\{0\}^{m_{k}-1};1\big).

We will also use the objects

\displaystyle I^{\bullet}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}):=I^{\bullet}\big(0;x_{1},\{0\}^{m_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{k},\{0\}^{m_{k}-1};1\big),\qquad\bullet\in\{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{dr}\},

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}):=\svmot\big(I^{\mathfrak{m}}(0;x_{1},\{0\}^{m_{1}-1},\ldots,x_{k},\{0\}^{m_{k}-1};1)\big).

5.1
Results in depth 1

Proposition 5.1
.

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(x)=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\bigg[I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n}(x)+\frac{1}{n}\log|x|^{2}I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n-1}(x)\bigg],

where we interpret
I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n}(x)=0
when
n\leq 0
.

Proof.

It follows from (
2.1
) that if
x\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{\text{MPL}}
has weight
n
, we obtain the recursion

\displaystyle D(x)=nx-m(\id\otimes D)\Delta^{\prime}(x).

Proposition
3.2
then implies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pi\big(I^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)\big)&=-\Pi\bigg(\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{x}\bigg)\bigg)\\
&=-\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{x}\bigg)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k!}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n-k}\bigg(\frac{1}{x}\bigg)D\bigg(\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}\bigg(\frac{1}{x}\bigg)^{k}\bigg)\\
&=I^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)+\frac{1}{n}\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}xI^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n-1}(x).\end{split}

(5.1)

The claim follows upon acting with
\per\circ\sv
, and taking the real or imaginary part.
∎

The functions
I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(x)
are real-analytic and single-valued, and they reduce to (single-valued) zeta values for
x=1
(cf. (
3.4
)),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{2m}(1)=0,\\
&I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{2m+1}(1)=-2\zeta_{2m+1}.\end{split}

(5.2)

This is a special case of the following more general result:

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\xi_{N}={\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}/N}
, and let
n>1
and
a
be integers.
Then

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}\big(\xi_{N}^{a}\big)=2(-1)^{n}\Cl_{n}\bigg(\frac{2\pi a}{N}\bigg),

where
\Cl_{n}\!(\alpha):=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\big(\li_{n}\!({\rm e}^{i\alpha})\big)
denotes the Clausen function. The same formula also holds for
n=1
and
a\neq 0\mod N
.

Proof.

From Propositions
5.1
and
3.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}\big(\xi_{N}^{a}\big)

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{R}_{n}\big[\svmot\big(\li_{n}\big(\xi_{N}^{-a}\big)\big)\big]

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{R}_{n}\big[\li_{n}\big(\xi_{N}^{-a}\big)-(-1)^{n}\li_{n}\big(\xi_{N}^{a}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{n}\mathfrak{R}_{n}\big[\li_{n}\big(\xi_{N}^{a}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{n}\Cl_{n}\!\bigg(\frac{2\pi a}{N}\bigg).

∎

I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{2}(x)
satisfies a clean version of the five-term relation, and
I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(x)
satisfies for all
n>1
the inversion relation

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{x}\bigg)=(-1)^{n+1}I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(x).

We see that the functions
I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(x)
have the same properties and satisfy the same identities as the Zagier’s single-valued versions of the classical polylogarithms
P_{n}(x)
defined in (
1.6
). However, the two families of functions are not identical, but we have the relation:

Corollary 5.3
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
and any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>1}
, we have

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(x)=2(-1)^{n}\Bigg[P_{n}(x)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}(n-2k-1)\frac{\log^{2k}|x|^{2}}{(2k+1)!}P_{n-2k}(x)\Bigg].

Moreover,
I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{1}(x)=-2P_{1}(x)-\log|x|^{2}
.

Proof.

It suffices to inject the result of Proposition
3.3
into the expression for
I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n}(x)=-\svmot(\li^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n}(1/x))
from Proposition
5.1
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Corollary
5.3
shows that, for classical polylogarithms (i.e., in depth 1), it is possible to define at least two distinct real-analytic single-valued analogues that satisfy clean functional relations. It would be an interesting question to investigate whether alternative definitions are also possible in higher depth. As a starting point it could be interesting to clarify the relationship between our construction of clean single-valued MPL’s and the Lie-period map defined in
[
36
, Section 2.5]
. We are grateful to Clément Dupont for this remark.

5.2
Results in depth 2

Proposition 5.5
.

For
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{(x,y)\colon x\neq 0,1,y\neq 0,1,x\}
,
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and
n=m_{1}+m_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\big[I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})\big]-\frac{1}{n}\mathfrak{R}_{n}\Bigg\{m_{1}I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1}}\bigg(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}}\bigg)I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{2}}(x_{2})\\
&\quad\hphantom{=}{}-\log|x_{2}|^{2}I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1},m_{2}-1}(x_{1},x_{2})+\log\bigg|\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\bigg|^{2}\bigg[I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1}-1,m_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})-I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1}-1}\bigg(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}}\bigg)I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{2}}(x_{2})\bigg]\\
&\quad\hphantom{=}{}+\sum_{r=1}^{m_{2}}(-1)^{m_{1}}\binom{n-r-1}{m_{1}-1}I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{r}(x_{1})\bigg[(n-r)I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n-r}\bigg(\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\bigg)+\log\bigg|\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\bigg|^{2}I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n-r-1}\bigg(\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\bigg)\bigg]\\
&\quad\hphantom{=}{}+\sum_{r=1}^{m_{1}}(-1)^{m_{1}-r}\binom{n-r-1}{m_{2}-1}I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{r}(x_{1})\big[(n-r)I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n-r}({x_{2}})+\log|{x_{2}}|^{2}I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{n-r-1}({x_{2}})\big]\Bigg\},\end{split}

(5.3)

where we interpret
I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1}}(x_{1})=0
and
I^{\mathrm{sv}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
whenever
m_{1}
or
m_{2}
are negative.

Proof.

The recursion (
4.2
) holds for arbitrary values of the
x_{i}
. We can now specialise to the case of depth two with

\displaystyle(x_{0};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x_{n+1})=\big(0;y_{1},\{0\}^{m_{1}-1},y_{2},\{0\}^{m_{2}-1};1\big).

It is easy to check that in that case (
4.2
) substantially simplifies, and only those terms contribute where
(x_{i};x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{j};x_{j+1})
takes one of the following values:

\displaystyle\big(0;y_{1},\{0\}^{m_{1}-1};y_{2}\big),\qquad(y_{1};0;0),\qquad(0;0;y_{2}),\qquad(y_{2};0;0),

\displaystyle\big(y_{1};\{0\}^{m_{1}-1},y_{2},\{0\}^{\alpha};0\big),\qquad 0\leq\alpha<m_{2}-1,

\displaystyle\big(0;\{0\}^{\beta},y_{2},\{0\}^{m_{1}-1};1\big),\qquad 0\leq\beta<m_{1}-1,

The functions

are real-analytic single-valued functions. For

, they reduce to zeta values:

Proposition 5.6
.

For
m_{1},m_{2}>0
, we have

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(1,1)=\begin{cases}c_{m_{1}m_{2}}\zeta_{m_{1}+m_{2}},&m_{1}+m_{2}\ \text{odd},\\
0,&m_{1}+m_{2}\ \text{even},\end{cases}

with

\displaystyle c_{m_{1}m_{2}}=(-1)^{m_{2}}\binom{m_{1}+m_{2}}{m_{1}}-1.

Proof.

For

even,

vanishes, because it is the imaginary part of a real number.
If

is odd, we need to distinguish two cases depending on the parity of

and
m_{2}
.
If

is odd (and thus

is even), we have

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(1,1)=\operatorname{Re}_{n}\big[\cR\big(I^{\mathfrak{m}}(0;1,\{0\}^{m_{1}-1},1,\{0\}^{m_{2}-1};1)\big)\big]=\cR\big(\zeta^{\mathfrak{m}}_{m_{2},m_{1}}\big),

where

is the motivic lift of the double zeta value

For

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(1,1)=I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(1)-I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{m_{2},m_{1}}(1,1)=-2\zeta_{n}-\left[\binom{n}{m_{2}}-1\right]\zeta_{n}=c_{m_{2},m_{1}}\zeta_{n}.

With

\displaystyle+(-1)^{m_{1}}\binom{n-1}{m_{2}}I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}}\right)-(-1)^{n}I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n}(x_{1}).

5.3
Results in depth 3

We recall that the explicit version of the parity theorem given by Panzer
[
49
]
in depth 3 is as follows. Write

\displaystyle\li_{m_{1},m_{2}}(x,y)=I_{m_{1},m_{2}}\big((xy)^{-1},z^{-1}\big),

\displaystyle\li_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(x,y,z)=I_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\big((xyz)^{-1},(yz)^{-1},z^{-1}\big)

in terms of the iterated integral notation.
Let
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, then on the simply-connected domain

therein, the following identity holds,

\displaystyle\li_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})+(-1)^{m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}}\li_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\big(z_{1}^{-1},z_{2}^{-1},z_{3}^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\li_{m_{1}}(z_{1})\big(\li_{m_{2},m_{3}}(z_{2},z_{3})-(-1)^{m_{2}+m_{3}}\li_{m_{2},m_{3}}\big(z_{2}^{-1},z_{3}^{-1}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}-\li_{m_{1}+m_{2},m_{3}}(z_{1}z_{2},z_{3})+\li_{m_{2},m_{1}}(z_{2},z_{1})\mathcal{B}_{m_{3}}(z_{3})+\li_{m_{2}+m_{3},m_{1}}(z_{2}z_{3},z_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}-\sum_{\mathclap{\mu+\nu+s=m_{2}}}\mathcal{B}_{s}(z_{1}z_{2}z_{3})\binom{-m_{3}}{\mu}\binom{-m_{1}}{\nu}\li_{m_{3}+\mu}(z_{3}^{-1})\li_{m_{1}+\nu}(z_{1})(-1)^{m_{3}+\mu}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}-\sum_{\mathclap{\mu+\nu+s=m_{3}}}\mathcal{B}_{s}(z_{1}z_{2}z_{3})\binom{-m_{2}}{\mu}\binom{-m_{1}}{\nu}\li_{m_{2}+\mu,m_{1}+\nu}(z_{2},z_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}-\sum_{\mathclap{\mu+\nu+s=m_{1}}}\mathcal{B}_{s}(z_{1}z_{2}z_{3})\binom{-m_{2}}{\mu}\binom{-m_{3}}{\nu}\li_{m_{2}+\mu,m_{3}+\nu}\big(z_{2}^{-1},z_{3}^{-1}\big)(-1)^{m_{2}+\mu+m_{3}+\nu},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n}(z)\mathop{:=}\frac{(2\pi{\rm i})^{n}}{n!}B_{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\log(-z)}{2\pi{\rm i}}\bigg)

and

We observe that if

. Moreover, the first sum is in fact always a product, as are the first and third terms. After passage to the clean single-valued functions, we obtain the following version of the parity theorem in depth 3, for the clean single-valued functions.

Proposition 5.9

Let

6.2
Antipode of
\boldsymbol{S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)}

Next we compute the antipode; the general recursion says

\displaystyle S\big(S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)\big)=-S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)-m(\operatorname{id}\otimes S)\Delta^{\prime}S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x).

The only terms appearing on the right hand side of the coproduct, whose antipode we recursively need, are
S(\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)=-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x
,
S(\zeta_{k}^{\mathfrak{dr}})=-\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{k}
for
k
odd, as odd Riemann zeta values are primitives for
\Delta
, and the previously computed

\displaystyle S\big(\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)\big)=-\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n}(x)-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{k}}{k!}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n-k}(x)=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{k}(x)\frac{(-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{n-k}}{(n-k)!},

as given in (
3.2
). Since we have these we can directly evaluate the antipode
S(S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x))
to see

\displaystyle S\big(S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)\big)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{n}S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{j,2}(x)\frac{(-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}-\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{j}(x)\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}(1-x)\frac{(-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{n+1-j}}{(n+1-j)!}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{j}(x)\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{k+2}\frac{(-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{n-j-k}}{(n-j-k)!}.

6.3
Single-valued version
\boldsymbol{\sv^{\mathfrak{m}}S^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n,2}(x)}

From this we are in a position to write the single-valued version via

\displaystyle\sv^{\mathfrak{m}}S^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n,2}(x)=\per\circ m(F_{\infty}\Sigma\otimes\id)\widetilde{\Delta}S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x).

We shall treat each term of the above coproduct in turn.

Firstly

\displaystyle\per\circ m(F_{\infty}\Sigma\otimes\id)\big(1\otimes S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)\!-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}(1-x)\otimes\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n+1}(x)\big)=S_{n,2}(x)\!-\log(1\!-\bar{x})\li_{n+1}(x).

More complicated is the term

\displaystyle\per\circ m(F_{\infty}\Sigma\otimes\id)\Bigg({-}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\li^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{j}(x)\otimes\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{k+2}\frac{(\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{n-k-j}}{(n-k-j)!}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\Bigg((-1)^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\li_{\ell}(\bar{x})\frac{(-\log\bar{x})^{j-\ell}}{(j-\ell)!}\Bigg)\zeta_{k+2}\frac{\log^{n-k-j}x}{(n-k-j)!}.

Now switch the order of summation between
j
and
\ell
, then set
j^{\prime}=j-\ell
in the inner-most sum. We obtain

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{\ell}\li_{\ell}(\bar{x})\zeta_{k+2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-\ell}\frac{(\log\bar{x})^{j}}{j!}\frac{(\log x)^{n-k-\ell-j}}{(n-k-j-\ell)!}

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{\ell}\li_{\ell}(\bar{x})\zeta_{k+2}\frac{(\log|x|^{2})^{n-k-\ell}}{(n-k-\ell)!}.

Here we have written
\log\bar{x}+\log x=\log|x|^{2}
, and applied the binomial theorem to evaluate the inner-most sum.

Finally, we can compute (note we take
j=n
in the sum, accounting also for the
S_{n,2}(x)\otimes 1
term)

\displaystyle\per\circ m(F_{\infty}\Sigma\otimes\id)\Bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{j,2}(x)\otimes\frac{(\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x)^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\Bigg\{\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}S_{\ell,2}(\bar{x})\frac{(-\log\bar{x})^{j-\ell}}{(j-\ell)!}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}\li_{\ell}(\bar{x})\log(1-\bar{x})\frac{(-\log\bar{x})^{j+1-\ell}}{(j+1-\ell)!}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=-\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\Bigg\{}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-k}\li_{\ell}(\bar{x})\zeta_{k+2}\frac{(-\log\bar{x})^{j-\ell-k}}{(j-\ell-k)!}\Bigg\}\frac{(\log x)^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}.

Like previously, we interchange the
j
and
\ell
sums, or more accurately move the
j
sum inside the
\ell
sum for the third term. In each case, the resulting sum of
j
can be evaluated as a power of
\log x+\log\bar{x}=\log|x|^{2}
via the binomial theorem. We obtain

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}(-1)^{\ell}S_{\ell,2}(\bar{x})\frac{\log^{n-\ell}|x|}{(n-\ell)!}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{\ell}\li_{\ell}(\bar{x})\log(1-\bar{x})\frac{(\log|x|^{2})^{n+1-\ell}}{(n+1-\ell)!}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{\ell}\li_{\ell}(\bar{x})\zeta_{k+2}\frac{(\log|x|^{2})^{n-k-\ell}}{(n-k-\ell)!}.

We note the simplification
-(-1)^{\ell+k}=(-1)^{\ell}
since
k
is odd has been used to obtain the last term. Moreover, the resulting term is exactly the same as already obtained above.

Summing the above 3 contributions, and rewriting slightly, gives the following formula for
\sv^{\mathfrak{m}}S^{\mathfrak{m}}_{n,2}(x)
, as stated in
[
15
, Section 4.3]
. Namely

\displaystyle\sv^{\mathfrak{m}}S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{m}}(x)={}

\displaystyle\big(S_{n,2}(x)+(-1)^{n+1}S_{n,2}(\bar{x})\big)-\log(1-\bar{x})\big(\li_{n+1}(x)+(-1)^{n}\li_{n+1}(\bar{x})\big)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(n-j)!}\log^{n-j}|x|^{2}\big(S_{j,2}(\bar{x})+\log(1-\bar{x})\li_{j+1}(\bar{x})\big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\frac{2(-1)^{j}\zeta_{k+2}}{(n-j-k)!}\li_{j}(\bar{x})\log^{n-j-k}|x|^{2}.

6.4
Clean version of
\boldsymbol{S_{n,2}(x)}

We also briefly repeat the calculation of
\Pi S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
, which was completed in detail in
[
15
]
. Here we proceed directly via the already calculated coproduct of
S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
, whereas the calculation in
[
15
]
was reduced to an analysis of which terms contribute in the infinitesimal coproduct. From (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle D\big(S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)\big)=(n+2)S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)-m(\id\otimes(Y\cdot\Pi))\Delta^{\prime}S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}.

Again, because of the projector in the second tensor factor, we are free to ignore all products in the coproduct while evaluating this. From the calculation above of
\Delta S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)\equiv{}

\displaystyle-\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}(1-x)\otimes\li_{n+1}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\li_{n-k}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)\otimes\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{k+2}

\displaystyle+S_{n-1,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)\otimes\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x\quad\pmod{\text{right-$\otimes$-factor products}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Pi S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)={}

\displaystyle S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)-\frac{1}{n+2}m(\id\otimes(Y\cdot\Pi))\big(\Delta^{\prime}S^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{n,2}(x)\big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)-S_{n-1,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)\frac{\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x}{n+2}+\frac{n+1}{n+2}\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}(1-x)\li_{n+1}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n+2}\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}(1-x)\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\frac{k+2}{n+2}\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{k+2}\li_{n-k}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x).

Here we have applied the result that
\Pi\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)=\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\log^{\mathfrak{dr}}x\li_{n-1}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
, which can be obtained from (
5.1
). Likewise, we have also used that
\Pi\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{2k+1}=\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{2k+1}
, since
\zeta_{2k+1}^{\mathfrak{dr}}
is a primitive for the coproduct.

6.5
Clean single-valued
\boldsymbol{S_{n,2}(x)}

Application of the period and the single-valued map to the expression
\Pi S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
, using the previous computations of the single-valued versions of
\li_{n}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
and
\zeta^{\mathfrak{dr}}_{2k+1}
, and the computation of
S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
directly above gives us an expression for
\mathcal{R}S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
. We can then define the clean single-valued version of
S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}
.

Definition 6.2
.

The
clean single-valued Nielsen polylogarithm
S^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n,2}(x)
is defined by
S^{\mathrm{csv}}_{n,2}(x):=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\mathcal{R}S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)
, where
\mathcal{R}S_{n,2}(x)
is as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}S_{n,2}^{\mathfrak{dr}}(x)={}

\displaystyle\big(S_{n,2}(x)-(-1)^{n+2}S_{n,2}(\bar{x})\big)-\frac{\log|x|^{2}}{n+2}\big(S_{n-1,2}(x)+\log(1-x)\li_{n}(x)\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n+2}\big((n+1)\log(1-x)-\log(1-\bar{x})\big)\big(\li_{n+1}(x)-(-1)^{n+1}\li_{n+1}(\bar{x})\big)

\displaystyle+\!\sum_{j=1}^{n}\!\frac{(-1)^{j}}{n\!+\!2}\Big\{\!(j+1)S_{j-1,2}(\bar{x})\!-\!\big(j\log(1\!-\!x)\!-\!\log(1\!-\!\bar{x})\big)\li_{j}(\bar{x})\!\Big\}\frac{\log^{n-j+1}|x|^{2}}{(n-j+1)!}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
\text{$k$ odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\frac{2\zeta_{k+2}}{n+2}\Bigg\{(k+2)\li_{n-k}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}j(-1)^{j}\frac{\log^{n-j-k}|x|^{2}}{(n-j-k)!}\li_{j}(\bar{x})\Bigg\}.

These clean single-valued functions (or more precisely the version without
{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}
, which still satisfies Theorem
1.1
) are used in
[
15
]
to obtain numerically verifiable identities and reductions for Nielsen polylogarithms, including numerical results on their special values.

7
Numerical evaluations

In this last section, we derive a few numerical evaluations of depth 2 MPL’s through use of the clean single-valued functions. These evaluations would perhaps otherwise not be obtainable in such a straightforward manner. For the sake of simplicity, we restrict to one identity in weight 4 and one in weight 5 obtained from the 2-term symmetry of
I_{3,1}
relating
I_{3,1}(x,y)
and
I_{3,1}(1-x,y)
and a similar identity for
I_{4,1}(x,y)
. Further treatment of special values of Nielsen polylogarithms
(
in particular
S_{3,2}
at elements of the weight 2 Bloch group, such as
S_{3,2}(\phi)
for
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
)
which were obtained from this clean single-valued procedure, can be found in
[
15
]
.

7.1
Weight 4

We recall first an identity that “reduces a depth 2 combination to depth 1”, which was predicted by Goncharov, with
\li_{4}
-terms first made explicit in
[
27
]
.
For the following, we note that
\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}(x)=I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}(x)
, because of the symbol level identity
\li_{4}(x)=I_{4}(x)
modulo products.

Proposition 7.1
.

The following identity of clean single-valued functions holds

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(1-x,y)+I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad{}=\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1-x}{1-y}\Big)-\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1-y}{x}\Big)-3\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{y}{1-x}\Big)-3\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{y}{x}\Big)+\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{y}{y-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\hphantom{=}{}-\frac{1}{2}\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{(1-x)y}{x(1-y)}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{xy}{(1-x)(1-y)}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{(1-y)y}{(1-x)x}\Big).

Proof.

As indicated above, a (slight variant of the) corresponding identity was given in
[
27
]
on the level of the symbol modulo products, and with
I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}
and
\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}
replaced by
I_{3,1}
and
\li_{4}
.
Hence the difference of the two sides in the equation stated above is a constant by Theorem
1.1
. This constant must be 0 since each side vanishes on the real line, as we take the imaginary part in the definition of the functions.
∎

Taking
x=\frac{1}{2}
,
y={\rm i}
(any non-real
y
will also give a reduction) in the above identity leads to

\displaystyle 2I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}\Big(\frac{1}{2},{\rm i}\Big)=

\displaystyle-\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{-1+{\rm i}}{2}\Big)-6\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}(2{\rm i})+\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1+{\rm i}}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle+\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1-{\rm i}}{2}\Big)-\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}(2-2{\rm i})+\frac{1}{2}\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}(4+4{\rm i}).

One can apply the inversion relation
\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}(x)=-\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\big(x^{-1}\big)
, and the inversion relation (with
x=\frac{1}{2}
,
y={\rm i}
) from Proposition
5.7
, namely

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(2,-{\rm i})-I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}\Big(\frac{1}{2},{\rm i}\Big)=\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}({\rm i})-3\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big({-}\frac{\rm i}{2}\Big)-\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big),

(
wherein
\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\big(\frac{1}{2}\big)=0
as we take the imaginary part
)
to obtain

\displaystyle 2I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(2,-{\rm i})={}

\displaystyle-\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{-1+{\rm i}}{2}\Big)+2\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}({\rm i})-\frac{1}{2}\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1-{\rm i}}{8}\Big)

\displaystyle+2\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1+{\rm i}}{4}\Big)+\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}\Big(\frac{1-{\rm i}}{2}\Big).

It is possible to compute explicitly how
I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(x,y)
is expressed using the classical polylogarithms and iterated integrals (an implementation is available in the
PolyLogTools
package
[
22
]
). One then obtains that

\displaystyle 2I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(2,-{\rm i})=\operatorname{Im}\bigg[4\li_{3,1}\Big({-}\frac{\rm i}{2},{\rm i}\Big)+4\log 2\li_{2,1}\Big({-}\frac{\rm i}{2},{\rm i}\Big)+2\zeta_{2}\li_{2}({\rm i})

\displaystyle\hphantom{2I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(2, -{\rm i}) =}\qquad{}-\Big(2\li_{2}\Big(\frac{2+{\rm i}}{5}\Big)-2\li_{2}\Big(\frac{4+2{\rm i}}{5}\Big)+\log\Big(\frac{4+3{\rm i}}{5}\Big)\log 2\Big)\log^{2}2\bigg]

\displaystyle\hphantom{2I^{\mathrm{csv}}_{3,1}(2, -{\rm i}) =}{}-\frac{\pi}{8}\Big(\li_{3}\Big({-}\frac{1}{4}\Big)+2\li_{2}\Big({-}\frac{1}{4}\Big)\log 2+4\log^{3}2-2\log 5\log^{2}2\Big),

with

\displaystyle\li_{m_{1},m_{2}}(x,y)=I_{m_{1},m_{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{xy},\frac{1}{y}\Big)=\sum_{0<n_{1}<n_{2}}\frac{x^{n_{1}}y^{n_{2}}}{n_{1}^{m_{1}}n_{2}^{m_{2}}}.

Likewise the
\li^{\mathrm{csv}}_{4}
