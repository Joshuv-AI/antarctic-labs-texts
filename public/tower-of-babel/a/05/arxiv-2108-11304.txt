Jonas Frey and Nima Rasekh

Abstract
. We prove that every locally Cartesian closed ∞ \infty -category with a subobject classifier has a strict initial object and disjoint and universal binary coproducts .

Introduction

Elementary Toposes and Finite Colimits

Categorical logic
uses results and constructions from category theory to
study
type theory
,
set theory
and other concepts in mathematical
logic. One key concept in categorical logic is that of an
elementary
topos
. Elementary toposes admit a natural interpretation of
higher-order
logic

[
Joh02b
, Chapter D4]
, and also give rise to models of set
theories
[
MLM94
,
JM95
]
.

Elementary
toposes were defined by Lawvere and Tierney as a generalization of

Grothendieck toposes
. The latter always admit small limits and colimits
since they are defined as categories of sheaves and are therefore locally
presentable
[
AGV72
]
. Hence, the first definitions of elementary topos
assumed the existence of both finite limits and finite colimits

[
Law70
,
Tie72
]
. However, it was soon realized
that the existence of finite colimits could in fact be deduced from the other
axioms and concretely that we have the following result: every finitely
complete Cartesian closed category with a subobject classifier has finite
colimits
[
Mik72
,
Par74
,
Mik76
]
.

The recent decades have witnessed significant advances in the study of
homotopy invariant
mathematics. In particular, there is now a well developed
theory of homotopy invariant categories, known as
(\infty,1)
-categories

or simply
\infty
-categories

[
Ber10
]
, which have been
used extensively in many areas relevant to homotopy theory, such as
homotopy coherent algebraic structures
or
derived geometry

[
Lur17
]
.

The theory of Grothendieck toposes has successfully been generalized to the
higher categorical setting – both in the context of model categories

[
Rez10
]
and
\infty
-categories
[
Lur09
]
– giving rise to the
notion of (Grothendieck-)
\infty
-topos
.

At the same time, categorical logicians have devised a homotopy invariant
interpretation of
Martin-Löf type theory

[
MLS84
]
,
known as
homotopy type theory

[
Uni13
]
. This interpretation was
quickly conjectured to generalize from homotopy types to arbitrary

\infty
-toposes, and a complete proof of this fact has recently been
given
[
Shu19
]
.

Just as the interpretation of higher order logic in
1
-toposes, the
interpretation of type theory in
\infty
-toposes does not rely on the
(co)completeness of the topos, which suggested to formulate a notion of
‘finitary’ or ‘elementary’
\infty
-topos as natural target for the
interpretation of type theory, analogous to Lawvere and Tierney’s elementary

1
-toposes. Concrete proposals for a definition of elementary
\infty
-topos
were given in
[
Shu17
,
Ras18
]
, and similarly to the
first definitions of elementary
1
-topos, these definitions explicitly
postulate the existence of finite colimits.

This leaves us with the question whether we can recover finite colimits from the
remaining axioms just as in the
1
-dimensional case. In the present paper we
give a partial answer, by proving the following main result.

Theorem
5.7
.

Let
\{A_{k}\}_{k\in I}
be a finite family of objects in a locally Cartesian
closed
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
with subobject classifier. Then the coproduct
\coprod_{k\in I}A_{k}
exists, and pullback along the inclusion maps
i_{k}:A_{k}\to\coprod_{k\in I}A_{k}
give rise to an equivalence of
\infty
-categories

(i_{k}^{*})_{k\in I}:\mathcal{C}_{/\coprod_{k\in I}A_{k}}\to\prod_{k\in I}\mathcal{C}_{/A_{k}}.

This result can be reformulated as saying that
\mathcal{C}
admits a
strict

initial object and
disjoint and universal
binary coproducts. Of these
properties, universality
[
Lur09
, Definition 6.1.1.2]
and
strictness say that the respective colimits are preserved by pullback functors,
which is a direct consequence of local Cartesian closure.
Disjointness
of
binary coproducts says that the commutative squares

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A+B}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A+B}

are pullbacks for all objects
A
,
B
, and the combination of universality and
disjointness is the special case of Rezk’s
descent condition

[
Rez10
, 6.5]
for binary coproducts. In the context of
1
-categories,
descent for coproducts is also known as
extensivity

[
CLW93
]
.

What about pushouts?

Having settled the issue of coproducts, the remaining question is that of
pushouts and coequalizers.

However, it turns out that unlike the
1
-categorical situation, assuming the
existence of a subobject classifier in fact does not suffice to prove the
existence of pushouts in locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-categories as we
illustrate via the following example.

Example
6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\sf tr}
be the full subcategory of the
\infty
-category
\mathcal{S}
of spaces
spanned by truncated spaces. Then
\mathcal{S}^{\sf tr}
is locally Cartesian closed and the
discrete space
1\!+\!1
is a subobject classifier. However, the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}

does not have a pushout.

We can in fact give a more conceptual argument why it is possible to recover
coproducts from the subobject classifier but not pushouts: the universal
property of coproducts in
\infty
-categories only depends on the homotopy types of the
mapping spaces, since the diagram used for coproducts is
discrete
and so
cannot involve any higher homotopies. On the other side the diagram used to
construct pushouts
(\bullet\leftarrow\bullet\rightarrow\bullet)
is not
discrete which means that the universal property of pushouts necessarily
involves the notion of
homotopy coherent diagram

[
Lur09
, Section 1.2.6]
.

Hence, it remains to determine what precise conditions we need to add to a
locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category with a subobject classifier to be
able to construct all finite colimits. The current hope is that we can obtain
this result by additionally assuming the existence of universes.

2.
Some Facts about Locally Cartesian Closed

\infty
-Categories

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
\infty
-category with finite limits. Then for every morphism

. If

3.1.
Subobject lattices

Let

\infty
-category with pullbacks.
The

2

For

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*}}

In other words, for each
f:B\to A
in
\operatorname{\mathsf{Ho}}(\mathcal{C})
, the monotone map

f^{*}:\mathsf{Sub}(A)\to\mathsf{Sub}(B)
has a right adjoint which we denote

\forall_{f}:\mathsf{Sub}(B)\to\mathsf{Sub}(A)
.

By uniqueness of adjoints, this ‘universal quantification’ operation gives
rise to a
covariant
functor of type
\operatorname{\mathsf{Ho}}(\mathcal{C})\to\operatorname{\mathsf{SLat}}
with the same
object part as (
3.1
).

3.2.
Subobject classifiers

Let
\mathcal{C}
be again an
\infty
-category with pullbacks.
We define
\operatorname{\mathsf{cMono}}(\mathcal{C})
to be the non-full subcategory of the arrow category
\operatorname{\mathsf{Fun}}(\Delta^{1},\mathcal{C})

with monomorphisms as objects and pullback squares as morphisms.
Then the codomain projection
p:\operatorname{\mathsf{cMono}}(\mathcal{C})\to\mathcal{C}
is a right fibration
[
Lur09
, 6.1.3.4]
.
Observe that for
A
in
\mathcal{C}
, the fiber of
p
over
A
is a Kan complex which is equivalent
to the underlying set of
\mathsf{Sub}(A)
.
We recall the following definition from
[
Lur09
, 6.1.6.1]
.

Definition 3.2
.

A
subobject classifier
in
\mathcal{C}
is a terminal object in
\operatorname{\mathsf{cMono}}(\mathcal{C})
.

Thus, a subobject classifier is a monomorphism from which any other monomorphism can be obtained as a pullback in an essentially unique way.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}:U\rightarrowtail\Omega
be a subobject classifier in an
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
with
pullbacks. Then
U
is terminal and
\Omega
is
0
-truncated.

Proof.

The object
\Omega
is
0
-truncated because for every object
A
, the space

\operatorname{\mathsf{Map}}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,\Omega)
is equivalent to the fiber of
p
over
A
and therefore
to the underlying set of
\mathsf{Sub}(A)
, i.e.
\operatorname{\mathsf{Map}}(-,A)
classifies the
0
-presheaf
of subobjects. The object
U
is terminal since it classifies
maximal
subobjects.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
m:A\to B
,
e:B\to A
be maps in a locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
such that
e\circ m=\operatorname{\mathsf{id}}_{A}
in
\operatorname{\mathsf{Ho}}(\mathcal{C})
. Then given

U\in\mathsf{Sub}(B)
, we have
\forall_{e}\,U\leq m^{*}\,U
in
\mathsf{Sub}(A)
.

Proof.

By adjunction we have
U\leq\forall_{m}\,m^{*}\,U
, and therefore we can argue

\displaystyle\forall_{e}\,U\leq\forall_{e}\,\forall_{m}\,m^{*}\,U\leq\forall_{e\circ m}\,m^{*}\,U\leq m^{*}\,U

by functoriality of
\forall
on
\operatorname{\mathsf{Ho}}(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category with subobject classifier
\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}:U\rightarrowtail\Omega
. Then for every
object
A\in\mathcal{C}
the poset
\mathsf{Sub}(A)
has finite joins.

Proof.

Given
A\in\mathcal{C}
we claim that a least element of
\mathsf{Sub}(A)
is given by

\bot=\forall_{\pi_{1}}\,\pi_{2}^{*}\,\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}},

where
A\xleftarrow{\pi_{1}}A\times\Omega\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}}\Omega
is a product
span. Let
U\in\mathsf{Sub}(A)
, and let
f:A\to\Omega
with
f^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}=U
. Then we have

\displaystyle\bot=\forall_{\pi_{1}}\,\pi_{2}^{*}\,\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{id}}_{A},f\rangle^{*}\,\pi_{2}^{*}\,\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}

by
Lemma

3.4

\displaystyle\leq f^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}

by functoriality of
(-)^{*}

\displaystyle=U\,.

Given
U,V\in\mathsf{Sub}(A)
we claim that a binary join is given by

U\vee V=\forall_{\pi_{1}}\bigl((\pi_{1}^{*}U\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}V\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}\bigr).

The derivation

\displaystyle\quad\pi_{1}^{*}U\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}U\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\quad\leq\quad\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\quad\pi_{1}^{*}U\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}U\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}V\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\quad\leq\quad\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad\pi_{1}^{*}U\quad\leq\quad(\pi_{1}^{*}U\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}V\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad U\quad\leq\quad\forall_{\pi_{1}}\bigl((\pi_{1}^{*}U\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}V\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}\bigr)

shows that
U
is indeed smaller than
U\vee V
, and similarly for
V
. To show
that
U\vee V
is a least upper bound let
W\in\mathsf{Sub}(A)
with
U\leq W
and

V\leq W
, and let
g:A\to\Omega
with
g^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}=W
. Then we have

\displaystyle U\vee V

\displaystyle=\forall_{\pi_{1}}\bigl((\pi_{1}^{*}U\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}V\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{id}}_{A},g\rangle^{*}\bigl((\pi_{1}^{*}U\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\wedge(\pi_{1}^{*}V\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}})\Rightarrow\pi_{2}^{*}\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}\bigr)

by
Lemma

3.4

\displaystyle=(U\Rightarrow W)\wedge(V\Rightarrow W)\Rightarrow W

since
(-)^{*}
preserves
\wedge,\Rightarrow

\displaystyle=W

∎

Remark 3.6
.

The argument in the previous proof is well known from second order logic, and in
its categorical incarnation from
tripos theory

[
HJP80
,
Pit81
]
and
elementary topos theory
[
BJ81
]
. It
works in general whenever we have a presheaf
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\operatorname{\mathsf{HSLat}}
of Heyting
semilattices on a
1
-category with finite products, such that

(1)

reindexing maps along product projections have right adjoints, and

(2)

\mathcal{H}
has a
generic predicate
, i.e. the category of elements of
the underlying presheaf of sets of
\mathcal{H}
has a weakly terminal object.

(Note that we do
not
require a Beck-Chevalley condition.)

From the point of view of locally Cartesian closed categories we point out that the construction applies exponentiation and pushforward functors
f_{*}
only to
subobjects
rather than general morphisms.

4.
Initial Objects

In this section we prove that every locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category
with subobject classifier has a strict initial object.

Definition 4.1
.

An
initial
object in an
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
is an object
0
such that

\operatorname{\mathsf{Map}}_{\mathcal{C}}(0,A)
is contractible for all
A\in\mathcal{C}
. The initial object is called

strict
, if
\mathcal{C}_{/0}
is equivalent to the terminal
\infty
-category.

The following theorem gives a characterization of initial objects.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category and
I
an object of
\mathcal{C}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

I
is initial in
\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

\mathcal{C}_{/I}
is equivalent to the terminal
\infty
-category.

(3)

\mathsf{Sub}(I)
is equivalent to the terminal preorder.

Proof.

Evidently (1) implies (3) since every subobject of an initial object has to be
trivial.

Conversely, if
\mathsf{Sub}(I)\simeq 1
then for any
X\to I
the subobject
\operatorname{\mathsf{isContr}}_{I}(X)\rightarrowtail I
is maximal, meaning that
X\to I
is an
equivalence. This shows that (3) implies (2).

Finally, to show that
I
is initial we have to show that the mapping space

\operatorname{\mathsf{Map}}_{\mathcal{C}}(I,X)
is terminal for all
X\in\mathcal{C}
. Since
\operatorname{\mathsf{Map}}_{\mathcal{C}}(I,X)\simeq\operatorname{\mathsf{Map}}_{\mathcal{C}}(1,X^{I})
and

\operatorname{\mathsf{Map}}_{\mathcal{C}}(1,-)
preserves finite limits, it is enough to show that
X^{I}
is
terminal in
\mathcal{C}
. Since
X^{I}=\Pi_{I}I^{*}X
and
\Pi_{I}:\mathcal{C}_{/I}\to\mathcal{C}

preserves limits, it is enough to show that
I^{*}X
is terminal in
\mathcal{C}_{/I}
.
This follows from (2).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Implication (1) to (2) of the theorem tells us in particular that
initial objects in locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-categories are always
strict
(
Definition

4.1
).

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category with subobject classifier. Then
\mathcal{C}
has a strict
initial object.

Proof.

By
Theorem

3.5
, the terminal object of
\mathcal{C}
has a least subobject

0\rightarrowtail 1
. Since any subobject of a least subobject is trivial we
have
\mathsf{Sub}(0)\simeq 1
, and
Theorem

4.2
together with

Remark

4.3
imply that
0
is
a strict initial object.
∎

5.
Binary Coproducts

In this section we prove that every locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category with subobject classifier
\Omega
has finite coproducts by using the fact that the subobject lattices have finite joins (
Theorem

3.5
). To motivate our proof, we
start by discussing the
1
-categorical case.

According to Johnstone
[
Joh02a
, A2.2]
, the first proofs of the existence
of finite colimits in elementary toposes were given by
Mikkelsen and
Paré
[
Mik72
,
Par74
,
Mik76
]
.
Mikkelsen’s proof does not seem to have been published. Paré proved – using
Beck’s theorem – that in any elementary
1
-topos
\mathcal{E}
the power object functor

\Omega^{(-)}:\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{E}
is monadic, which implies that
\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{op}}
has finite
limits as a category of Eilenberg-Moore algebras over a finite-limit category.

Although there is an
\infty
-categorical analogue of Beck’s theorem

[
Lur17
, Theorem 4.7.3.5]
, this proof cannot be generalized as the
corresponding functor of
\infty
-categories
\Omega^{(-)}:\mathcal{C}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{C}
is not
monadic and in fact not even conservative for the most simple examples:
if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{S}
then
\Omega=\{0,1\}
, the two element set, and the functor
\Omega^{(-)}:\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{op}}\to\mathcal{S}

takes every connected space to
\Omega
, and every map between connected spaces
to an equivalence.

Our proof of the existence of binary coproducts is based on an ‘internal-language proof’ in
1
-toposes that avoids the monadicity theorem and was given as an
Exercise in
[
LS86
, Exercise II.5.]
. The idea is to
‘carve out’ the coproduct
A+B
as subobject of
\Omega^{A}\times\Omega^{B}
. In
trying to adapt this proof to
\infty
-categories, we are met with two
obstacles:

(1)

While in a
1
-topos every object
A
embeds into its power object

\Omega^{A}
, this cannot work in higher toposes as, by
Theorem

3.3
,

\Omega
– and therefore
\Omega^{A}
and all its subobjects – are

0
-truncated.

(2)

To verify the universal property, the internal-language proof exhibits the unique arrow by first defining a (monic) binary relation,
and then showing that it is single-valued and total. This kind of argument
cannot work in the higher setting since it relies on the fact that the
graph
\langle 1,f\rangle:A\to A\times B
of a map
f:A\to B
is always monic, which is
not the case e.g. in
\mathcal{S}
.

To overcome the first hurdle, we replace the
\Omega^{A}

in the construction with an object
\overline{A}
known as

partial map classifier
or
partial map representer

[
Joh02a
, pg. 101]

in
1
-topos theory (
Lemma

5.1
). To address the second point, we replace the classical internal-logic proof by an argument which is inspired by homotopy type theory (
Lemma

5.3
),
and which crucially relies on the technique of the object of contractibility, which we reviewed in
Section

2.2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A
be an object in a locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category
\mathcal{C}
with
subobject classifier
\operatorname{\mathsf{t\!t}}:1\to\Omega
. Then there exists an object
\overline{A}

admitting disjoint monomorphisms of

and
1

Proof.

Let

be the terminal projection, and define

. Then by
