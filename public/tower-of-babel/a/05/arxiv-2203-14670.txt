Introduction

Assume that
X
is a complex separable infinite dimensional Banach space and
\mathcal{B}(X)
denotes the Banach algebra of all bounded linear operators from
X
to itself.
We say that a subspace
Y\subset X
is an invariant subspace of an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
if
TY\subset Y
;

T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is called intransitive if it leaves invariant spaces other than the trivial space
0

The following famous invariant subspace problem was raised a century ago.

Problem 1.1

(The invariant subspace problem)
.

Does every
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
have a nontrivial invariant subspace?

This problem has been intensively studied, especially for Hilbert spaces, and positive results have been obtained for many classes of operators. (See, for instance
[
40
,
35
]

The celebrated counterexample of Enflo
[
12
]
gave a negative answer to the problem. It was done by showing that there is an operator

which does not admit a nontrivial invariant subspace of
\ell_{1}
.
The paper has existed in manuscript form for about 12 years prior to its publication, the delay being due to its enormous complexity.
In the meantime, constructions of operators without invariant subspaces have been published by
C. Read
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
and B. Beauzamy
[
6

[
42
,
43
]
showed that every infinite dimensional separable Banach space

admits a bounded linear operator without nontrivial invariant subspaces whenever

contains
\ell_{1}
, or,
c_{0}
as its complemented subspaces. On the other hand, Beauzamy’s example possesses a cyclicity property stronger than the nonexistence of invariant subspaces. The construction of an operator without a nontrivial invariant subspace on a nuclear Fréchet space (a rather different problem) has been achieved by A. Atzmon
[
4
]
. In 2011,
S.A. Argyros and R.G. Haydon
[
2

constructed a remarkable Banach space

that solves the scalar-plus-compact problem.
The space

is the first example of a Banach space for which it is known that every bounded linear operator on the space has the form
\lambda+K

where

is a real scalar and

is a compact operator, it is also the first example of an infinite-dimensional space on which
every bounded linear operator has a nontrivial invariant subspace.

However, there are many important questions left unknown.
For example, we do not know that whether every bounded linear operator on a separable Hilbert space admits a nontrivial invariant subspace; we do not even know if there exists a bounded linear operator on a reflexive Banach space that does not have a non-trivial invariant subspace. Therefore, mathematicians have not stopped the pace of further research on these and related questions.

A problem closely related to the invariant subspace problem is the third problem of Halmos
[
22
]

Problem 1.2

(The third problem of Halmos)
.

If an intransitive operator has an inverse, is its inverse also intransitive?

This problem has been around for over fifty years. Not only is the answer to this problem still unknown, but little research has progressed. 16 years ago, R.G. Douglas, C. Foias and C. Pearcy hoped their joint paper
[
13
]
will stimulate
interest in this problem of Halmos – the only problem of the ten set forth in
[

In 1970, P.R. Halmos
[
22
]
raised ten open problems in a separable Hilbert space. The first problem asks whether the set of cyclic operators has a nonempty interior and has been subsequently answered in negative by P.A. Fillmore, J.G. Stampfli and J.P. Williams

[

Whether the inverse of an operator that is known to have an invariant subspace must also have one is the third one (Problem 1.2). In 1975, D. Hadwin
[
21

showed that for every separable complex Banach space there is an unbounded linear bijection
S
on it with a nontrivial invariant subspace so that its algebraic inverse

fails to have a nontrivial invariant subspace. The fourth problem asks whether every normal operator on a separable space is the sum of a diagonal operator and a compact one. The first proof of this is due to I.D. Berg (unpublished), while an elegant one is due to Halmos himself. The fifth problem asks if every subnormal Toeplitz operator is either analytic or normal, which was resolved independently by C.C. Cowen
[
10
]
, J. Long and S. Sun
[
50
]
,
[

]
.
The sixth problem asks if every polynomially bounded operator is similar to a contraction? This problem was answered in negative by G. Pisier
[
38
]
in 1997. Problem seven asks if every quasinilpotent operator is the norm limit of nilpotent ones. It was solved by C. Apostol and D. Voiculescu
[
1
]
in 1974. Problem eight asks if every operator is the norm limit of reducible ones. It was solved by D. Voiculescu
[
53

in 1976. Problem nine asks whether every complete Boolean is algebra reflexive. Problem ten asks whether every non-trivial strongly closed transitive atomic lattice is either medial or self-conjugate.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper, all notations and symbols are standard. Unless stated explicitly otherwise, we will assume that
X
is a complex separable infinite dimensional Banach space and
X^{*}
its dual. We denote by
\mathcal{B}(X)
the Banach algebra of all bounded linear self-mappings defined on
X
, and by
{\mathcal{K}}(X)
be the closed ideal of all compact operators in
\mathcal{B}(X)
, i.e., all operators in
\mathcal{B}(X)
which maps every bounded subset of
X
into a relatively compact subset. Therefore, the quotient space
\mathcal{B}(X)/{\mathcal{K}}(X)
is the Calkin algebra, that is, the natural quotient mapping
\pi:\mathcal{B}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(X)/{\mathcal{K}}(X)
is a homomorphism projection.

For an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
,
{\rm ker}T
(resp.,
{\rm ran}T
) denotes the kernel (resp., the range) of
T
. If
M\subset X
is an invariant subspace of
T
, then
T|_{M}
denotes the restriction of
T
to
M
;
\sigma(T)
denotes the spectrum of
T
. i.e.,

\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:T-\lambda\;{\rm is\;not\;invertible}\},

and its complement
\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(T)
is denoted by
\rho(T)
;
\sigma_{p}(T)
stands for the point spectrum of
T
, that is,
\sigma_{p}(T)=\{\lambda:\mbox{dim}\ker(T-\lambda)\geq 1\}
;

\sigma_{e}(T)
stands for the essential spectrum of
T
, that is, the spectrum of
\pi(T)
in
\mathcal{B}(X)/{\mathcal{K}}(X)
.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is said to be Fredholm (resp., semi-Fredholm) provided
\mbox{ran}(T)
(the range of
T
) is closed, and both of the dimension of its kernel ker
T
and the codimension of ran
T
are finite dimensional, i.e.,
dim(X/ranT)<\infty
(resp., either ker
T
, or codimension of ran
T
is finite dimensional).
\rho_{F}(T)\equiv\mathbb{C}\backslash\sigma_{e}(T)
is said to be the Fredholm domain of
T
. The well-known Atkinson theorem states that
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is Fredholm if and only if
\pi(T)
is invertible in
\mathcal{B}(X)/{\mathcal{K}}(X)
.

{\rm Lat}T
represents the invariant subspace lattice of
T
.

For a subset
A\subset X
,
[A]
is the closure of the linear hull
{\rm span}(A)
of
A
. If
A=\{x_{n}\}_{n=k}^{\infty}\subset X
is a sequence,
then we denote the closure of the linear hull
{\rm span}(A)
by
[x_{n}]_{n\geq k};
if no confusion arises, we simply write it as
[x_{n}]
.

A^{\bot}\equiv\{x^{*}\in X^{*}:\langle x^{*},x\rangle=0,\;\forall\;x\in A\}
is called the annihilator of
A
.
If
A\subset X^{*}
, then we also use
[A]^{w^{*}}
to denote the weak-star closure of the linear hull
{\rm span}(A)
in
X^{*}
.

Recall that an operator
P\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is said to be an idempotent if
P^{2}=P
; and an nontrivial idempotent
P
means the idempotent
P
is neither
0
, nor the identity
I
. For
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
, its commutant
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(T)
is defined by

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(T)=\{S\in\mathcal{B}(X):ST=TS\}.

The following definition of strongly irreducible operators was introduced independently by Gilfeather
[
15
]
and Jiang Zejian
[
30
]
in 1970s.

Definition 2.1
.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is called strongly irreducible provided the commutant of
T
does not admits any nontrivial idempotent operator. Otherwise, it is called strongly reducible.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
.
i) A (nonzero) element
x\in X
is said to be a cyclic vector of
T
provided
[T^{k}x,\,k\geq 0]
(the closure of the linear hull span
\{T^{k}x,\,k\geq 0\}
) is the whole space
X
. We denote by
\mathcal{C}(T)
the set of all cyclic vectors of
T
.

ii) We say that
T
is transitive provided
\mathcal{C}(T)=X\backslash\{0\}
.

Clearly, if an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is strongly reducible, then
T
is intransitive.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
be an invertible operator. If
T
is strongly reducible, then

i)
T^{-1}
is also strongly reducible;

ii)
T
and
T^{-1}
have a common nontrivial invariant subspace.

Proof.

Suppose that
P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(T)
is a nontrivial idempotent operator. Then it follows from definition of the commutant
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(T)
and invertibility of
T
that
P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(T^{-1})
. Therefore, both
{\rm ran}P
and
{\rm ran}(I-P)
are common nontrivial invariant subspaces of
T
and
T^{-1}
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
be an invertible operator. If its spectrum
\sigma(T)
is not connected, then
T
and
T^{-1}
have a common nontrivial invariant subspace.

Proof.

Suppose that
\sigma(T)
is not connected.
Then by Riesz’ functional calculus, there is a nontrivial idempotent
P
such that
P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(T)\bigcap\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(T^{-1})
.
Therefore,
{\rm ran}P\in{\rm Lat}T\bigcap{\rm Lat}T^{-1}
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
be invertible. If
\sigma_{p}(T)\cup\sigma_{p}(T^{*})\neq\emptyset
, then
T
and
T^{-1}
have a common nontrivial invariant subspace.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
T
is not a scalar operator.
Suppose that
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(T)\bigcup\sigma_{p}(T^{*})
. The two cases below may occur.

(1) If
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(T)
, then there is

such that
(T-\lambda)x=0
, that is,

T^{-1}((T-\lambda)x)=(I-\lambda T^{-1})x=0
. Therefore,

, and
\ker(T-\lambda)\in{\rm Lat}(T^{-1})\bigcap{\rm Lat}(T)
.

(2) If
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(T^{*})
, then for
x\in\ker(T^{*}-\lambda)
, we obtain

. Thus,

\ker(T^{*}-\lambda)^{\bot}\in{\rm Lat}(T^{-1})\bigcap{\rm Lat}(T).

∎

Recall that
\sigma(T)^{\land}
denotes the polynomial convex hull of
\sigma(T)
. Note that
\sigma(T)^{\land}
is the union of
\sigma(T)
and all of the bounded connected components of

(Proposition

Proposition 2.6
.

[

Let

be an invertible operator. If

3.
Existence of right inverses

In this section, we show a characterization related to existence of the right inverse of a bounded linear operator.

Theorem 3.1
.

T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
admits a right inverse, i.e., there is
S\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
so that
TS=I
if and only if
T
is surjective and
{\rm ker}T
is complemented in
X
.
Consequently,
TS=I
implies that
X={\rm ker}T\oplus{\rm ran}S
.

Proof.

Sufficiency. Since
TX=X
,
X/{\rm ker}T
is isomorphic to
X
. Let
Y\subset X
be a closed subspace so that
X=Y\oplus{\rm ker}T
. Then
Y\approx\,\text{(isomorphic)}\,X
. Let
P:X\rightarrow Y
be the projection from
X
along
{\rm ker}T
to
Y
. Then the restriction
U\equiv TP|_{Y}=T|_{Y}
is an isomorphism from
Y
to
X
. Therefore,

U^{-1}:X\rightarrow Y
is again an isomorphism. Consequently,
TU^{-1}:X\rightarrow X
is a self-isomorphism of
X
. We finish the proof of the sufficiency by letting
S=U^{-1}(TU^{-1})^{-1}
.

Necessity. Suppose that there is
S\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
so that
TS=I
. Clearly,
T
is surjective and
S^{*}T^{*}=(TS)^{*}=Id_{X^{*}}
. Thus,
S^{*}:X^{*}\rightarrow X^{*}
is surjective, which further entails that
S:X\rightarrow X
is injective. We write

Y={\rm ran}S,\;{\rm and\;}\;Z={\rm ker}T.

Note that
TS=I
implies that
T|_{Y}:Y\rightarrow X
is an isomorphism. Therefore,
Y
is a closed subspace of
X
. This and injectivity of
S
entail that
S:X\rightarrow Y
is again an isomorphism.
Next, we define two mappings as follows.

T_{X/Z}:X/Z\rightarrow X\;\;{\rm by\;\;}T_{X/Z}(\bar{x})=Tx,\;\bar{x}=x+Z\in X/Z,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;

and

T_{Y/Z}:Y/Z\rightarrow X\;\;{\rm by\;\;}T_{Y/Z}(\bar{Sx})=(TS)x=x,\;\bar{Sx}=Sx+Z\in Y/Z.

Clearly, both
T_{X/Z}
and
T_{Y/Z}
are isomorphisms. Since
Y/Z\subset X/Z
and since the restriction
(T_{X/Z})|_{Y/Z}
of
T_{X/Z}
to
Y/Z
is just
T_{Y/Z}
, we obtain that

Y/Z=X/Z.

Therefore, to show
Z
is complemented in
X
, it suffices to prove

Y+Z=Y\oplus Z
, i.e.,
Y
and
Z
are complemented each other in
Y+Z
. Suppose, to the contrary, that there are two sequences
\{y_{n}\}\subset Y
,
\{z_{n}\}\subset Z
,
\|y_{n}\|=\|z_{n}\|=1
, such that
\|y_{n}-z_{n}\|\rightarrow 0
. Then

0=\lim_{n}\|T\|\cdot\|y_{n}-z_{n}\|\geq\lim_{n}\|T(y_{n}-z_{n})\|=\lim_{n}\|T(y_{n})\|=\lim_{n}\|T|_{Y}({y}_{n})\|.

This contradicts to that
T|_{Y}:Y\rightarrow X
is an isomorphism.
∎

T\in B_{n}(\Omega)
has a right inverse.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a nonempty bounded open connected subset of

\mathbb{C}
,
T\in B_{1}(\Omega)
and that
\lambda_{0}\in\Omega
. Then

(4.1)

[{\rm ker}(T-\lambda_{0})^{k},\,k\geq 1]=X=[{\rm ker}(T-\lambda),\,\lambda\in\Omega].

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\lambda_{0}=0
; Otherwise, we substitute
T-\lambda_{0}
for
T
.
Since
T
is surjective with
{\rm dimker}T=1
, by Corollary
4.1
, there exists an operator
S\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
such that
TS=I
.
Given
x_{0}\in{\rm ker}T
, choose
0<\delta<\|S\|
and let
O_{\delta}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:|\lambda|<\delta\}
. Then

S_{\lambda}\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}S^{n}\in\mathfrak{B}(X),

and
S_{(\cdot)}
is a holomorphic map from
O_{\delta}
into
\mathfrak{B}(X)
. Note that

TS_{\lambda}(x_{0})=\lambda S_{\lambda}(x_{0}),{\rm for\;all\;}\lambda\in O_{\delta}.

Then

S_{\lambda}(x_{0})\in{\rm ker}(T-\lambda),\;{\rm for\;all\;}\lambda\in O_{\delta}.

We first show the second equality in (
4.1
), i.e.,
[{\rm ker}(T-\lambda),\,\lambda\in\Omega]=X
, which is equivalent to

(4.2)

[S_{\lambda}(x_{0}):\,\lambda\in O_{\delta}]=X.

Notice that
S_{\lambda}(x_{0})
is holomorphic on
O_{\delta}
and that
T\in B_{1}(\Omega)
. There
exists a holomorphic frame
e(\cdot):\,\Omega\rightarrow X
such that
e(\lambda)=S_{\lambda}(x_{0})
and

Te(\lambda)=\lambda e(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in O_{\delta}
.
To prove
4.2
, it suffices to show that for any
x^{*}\in X^{*}
,

(4.3)

\langle x^{*},S_{\lambda}(x_{0})\rangle=0\;{\rm for\;all\;}\lambda\in O_{\delta}\;\;{\rm implies\;}x^{*}=0.

Assume that
\langle x^{*},S_{\lambda}(x_{0})\rangle=0\;{\rm for\;all\;}\lambda\in O_{\delta}
. Then
\langle x^{*},e(\lambda)\rangle=0\;{\rm for\;all\;}\lambda\in O_{\delta}
.
Note that
\langle x^{*},e(\cdot)\rangle
is a holomorphic function on
\Omega
. We obtain that
\langle x^{*},e(\lambda)\rangle=0,\;{\rm for\;all\;}\lambda\in\Omega,
and which entails

x^{*}=0
. Thus, (
4.2
) holds. The second equality in (
4.1
) has been shown.

To show the first equality in (
4.1
), note that

\langle x^{*},S_{\lambda}(x_{0})\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\langle x^{*},S^{n}(x_{0})\rangle

is a holomorphic function of
\lambda\in O_{\delta}
for every
x^{*}\in X^{*}
. Then a sufficient and necessary condition for
\langle x^{*},S_{(\cdot)}(x_{0})\rangle=0
on
O_{\delta}
is that
\langle x^{*},S^{n}(x_{0})\rangle=0
for all
n\geq 0
. Therefore, (
4.2
) entails
[S^{n}(x_{0}):\,n\geq 0]=X
. Since

{\rm ker}T^{k}=[x_{0},S(x_{0}),\cdots,S^{k-1}(x_{0})]

for all

, we obtain

[{\rm ker}T^{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}]=X
. This is just the first equality in (
4.1
).
∎

5.
A bi-orthogonal system

To begin with this section, we introduce some new notations. Recall that for a subset
A\subset X
,
[A]
denotes the closure of span
\{A\}
.
Assume that
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is invertible. For each fixed
x_{0}\in X
and each integer
k\geq 0
, we write

\mathfrak{N}_{T,-k}(x_{0})=[T^{-n}x:\,n\geq k],\;\mathfrak{N}_{T,-\infty}(x_{0})=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{N}_{T,-k}(x_{0}).

If there is no confusion, we denote them as
\mathfrak{N}_{-k}
and
\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}
for all integers
k\geq 0
. In particular, we simply write

(5.1)

\mathfrak{N}\;(=\mathfrak{N}_{-1}(x_{0}))\;=[T^{-n}x_{0}:\,n\geq 1],\;\;\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{N}_{-k}.

Keeping the notations above in mind, we state and prove the following result.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
be invertible. Then for each fixed
x_{0}\in X
,

(5.2)

\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}\in{\rm Lat}T.

Proof.

Note that
\mathfrak{N}_{-k}\supset\mathfrak{N}_{-(k+1)}
for all
\;k\in\mathbb{N}
. Continuity and invertibility of
T
imply

T(\mathfrak{N}_{-k})=\mathfrak{N}_{-k+1},\;{\rm for\;all}\;k\in\mathbb{N}.

Consequently,

T\Big(\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}\Big)\subset\mathfrak{N}_{-k},\;{\rm for\;all}\;k\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, (
5.2
) follows.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is an invertible transitive operator and
0\neq x_{0}\in X
. If
\mathfrak{N}\neq X
, then
\{\mathfrak{N}_{-k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a strictly decreasing sequence of closed subspaces with
\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Since
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is transitive,
\mathfrak{C}(T)=X\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore,

{\rm Lat}T=\{0,X\}.

By Lemma
5.1
, for every
x_{0}\in X
,
\cap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{N}_{-k}\in{\rm Lat}T=\{0,X\}
.
\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}(x)=\{0\}
follows from
\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}\subset\mathfrak{N}\neq X
.

Suppose that
\{\mathfrak{N}_{-k}\}
is not strictly decreasing. Then
\mathfrak{N}_{-k}=\mathfrak{N}_{-k+1}
for some
k\geq 1
. This entails that

T\big(\mathfrak{N}_{-k}\big)=\mathfrak{N}_{-k+1}=\mathfrak{N}_{-k}.

Since
x_{0}\neq 0
, it follows that
\{0\}\neq\mathfrak{N}_{-k}\;(\subset\mathfrak{N}\neq X)
is a nontrivial invariant subspace of
T
.
∎

Recall that a sequence
(x_{n},x^{*}_{n})\subset X\times X^{*}
is said to be a bi-orthogonal system provided
\langle x_{i},x^{*}_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is an invertible transitive operator and
0\neq x_{0}\in X
. If
\mathfrak{N}\neq X
, then there is a sequence
\{x^{*}_{n}\}\subset X^{*}
so that

\{(x_{n},x^{*}_{n})\}\subset X\times X^{*}

is a bi-orthogonal system, where
x_{n}=T^{-n}x_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Since
T
is invertible transitive, it is easy to observe that
\{x_{n}\}
is a linearly independent set of
X
. Note that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathfrak{N}_{-n}=[x_{k}]_{k\geq n}
, and that

[x_{k}]_{1\leq k\leq n}
is a finite dimensional subspace of
X
. Then

(5.3)

\mathfrak{N}=[x_{k}]_{k\geq 1}=[x_{k}]_{1\leq k\leq n}+[x_{k}]_{k\geq n+1}=[x_{k}]_{1\leq k\leq n}+\mathfrak{N}_{-n-1}(x).

Since
T
is invertible transitive with
\mathfrak{N}\neq X
, Lemma
5.2
entails that
\big\{[x_{k}]_{k\geq n}\big\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a strict decreasing sequence of closed subspaces with

(5.4)

\mathfrak{N}_{-\infty}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}[x_{k}]_{k\geq n}=\{0\}.

Strict decreasing monotonicity of
\big\{[x_{k}]_{k\geq n}\big\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and linear independence of
\{x_{n}\}
imply that
\big\{[x_{k}]_{1\leq k\leq n}\big\}
is a strict increasing sequence and turn (
5.3
) into

(5.5)

\mathfrak{N}=[x_{k}]_{1\leq k\leq n}\oplus[x_{k}]_{k\geq n+1},\;{\rm for\;all\;}n\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore,

x_{n}\notin[x_{k}]_{1\leq k\leq n-1}\oplus[x_{k}]_{k\geq n+1}\equiv X_{n},\;{\rm for\;all\;}n\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
X_{n}
is a closed subspace of
X
. Then by separation theorem of convex sets there is
x^{*}_{n}\in X^{*}
so that

\langle x^{*}_{n},x_{n}\rangle=1,\;\langle x^{*}_{n},x\rangle=0,\;{\rm for\;all\;}x\in X_{n}.

∎

6.
Four operators

For an invertible transitive operator
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
, If
0\neq x_{0}\in X
, so that
\mathfrak{N}\equiv[T^{-k}x_{0}]_{k\geq 1}\neq X
, then by Lemma
5.3
, there is a bi-orthogonal system
\{(x_{n},x^{*}_{n})\}\subset X\times X^{*}
with
x_{n}=T^{-n}x_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Please keep this in mind. The assumption and conclusion will play an important role in the proofs of Theorems
1.2
–
1.5
.

With the same assumption on the operator
T
, the vector
x_{0}
and the space
\mathfrak{N}
as above, we know that the following two operators
U,V\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N})
are well-defined, where
\mathfrak{N}=[x_{n}]=[T^{-n}x_{0}]_{n\geq 1}
.

(6.1)

U\equiv T^{-1}|_{\mathfrak{N}},\;\;V=(T-x_{0}\otimes x_{1}^{*})|_{\mathfrak{N}}.

Next, we define a closed subspace
\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}
as follows.

(6.2)

\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}=[x_{n}^{*}]_{n\geq 1},\;\mbox{the norm closure of span}\{x^{*}_{n}\}\;\mbox{in}\;\mathfrak{N}^{*}.

Note
\mathfrak{N}^{*}=[x^{*}_{n}]^{w^{*}}

\mathfrak{N}^{*}
of
\mathfrak{N}
is just the
w^{*}
-closure of
\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}

U^{\dagger},V^{\dagger}:\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}\rightarrow\mathfrak{N}^{*}
below.

(6.3)

U^{\dagger}=U^{*}|_{\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}},\;\;V^{\dagger}=V^{*}|_{\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}}.

Then we have the following result.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose that
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is an invertible transitive operator,
0\neq x_{0}\in X
, and
\mathfrak{N}\neq X
. Let

and
V^{\dagger}
be defined by (
6.1
) and (
6.3
). Then

i)
\mbox{ker}U=\{0\},\;\mbox{ran}U=[x_{k}]_{k\geq 2}
; consequently,
\mbox{ind}U=-1
;

ii)
\mbox{ker}V=[x_{1}],\;\mbox{ran}V=\mathfrak{N}
; consequently,
\mbox{ind}V=1
;

iii)
VU=I_{\mathfrak{N}}
,
U^{*}V^{*}=I_{\mathfrak{N}^{*}}
;

iv)
\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}
is a
w^{*}
-dense subspace of
\mathfrak{N}^{*}
;

v)
U^{\dagger},V^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger})

satisfy

\mbox{ker}U^{\dagger}=[x_{1}^{*}],\;\mbox{ran}U^{\dagger}=\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger},\;\mbox{consequently,\;ind}U^{*}=indU^{\dagger}=1;

\mbox{ker}V^{\dagger}=\{0\},\;\mbox{ran}V^{\dagger}=[x_{n}^{*}]_{n\geq 2},\;\mbox{therefore,\;ind}V^{*}=indV^{\dagger}=-1;

in particular,

I_{{\mathfrak{N}}^{\dagger}}=(U^{*}V^{*})|_{{\mathfrak{N}}^{\dagger}}=U^{*}|_{{\mathfrak{N}}^{\dagger}}V^{*}|_{{\mathfrak{N}}^{\dagger}};

vi)
x_{1}=T^{-1}x_{0}
is a cyclic vector of
U
;
x^{*}_{1}
is a cyclic vector of
V^{\dagger}
;

vii)
V\in B_{1}(\Omega_{1})
and
U^{\dagger}\in B_{1}(\Omega_{2})
, where
\Omega_{1}
is the connected component of
\rho_{\text{s-F}}(V)
containing the origin and
\Omega_{2}
is the connected component of
\rho_{s-F}(U^{\dagger})
containing the origin. Furthermore
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
are maximal, and
\partial\Omega_{1}\subseteq\sigma_{e}(V)
,
\partial\Omega_{2}\in\sigma_{e}(U^{\dagger})
.

Proof.

i)– iii)  follow directly from (
6.1
) the definition of
U
and
V
.

iv)  Since
\{(x_{n},x^{*}_{n})\}\subset X^{*}
is a total and complete bi-orthogonal system, i.e.,
[x_{n}]=\mathfrak{N}
and
\{x^{*}_{n}\}
separates points of
\mathfrak{N}
, by separation theorem of convex sets in locally convex spaces, it follows.

v) & vi)
\mbox{ker}U=\{0\}
and
\mbox{ran}U=[x_{k}]_{k\geq 2}
in i) entail that
U^{*}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}^{*})
is surjective with

\mbox{ker}U^{*}=(\mbox{ran}U)^{\bot}=[x_{k}]_{k\geq 2}^{\bot}=[x^{*}_{1}].

\langle U^{*}x^{*}_{i},x_{j}\rangle=\langle x^{*}_{i},Ux_{j}\rangle=\langle x^{*}_{i},x_{j+1}\rangle=\delta_{i,j+1},\;i,j\in\mathbb{N}

deduce that

Therefore,
U^{\dagger}=U^{*}|_{\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger})
with
\mbox{ind}U^{*}=1

\mbox{ker}V=[x_{1}]
and
\mbox{ran}V=\mathfrak{N}
in ii) imply that
V^{*}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}^{*})
is injective with

\mbox{ran}V^{*}=(\mbox{ker}V)^{\bot}=[x_{1}]^{\bot}=[x^{*}_{k}]^{w^{*}}_{k\geq 2}.

While

for all

deduce that

with

and further, that
x_{1}^{*}
is a cyclic vector of
V^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger})

x_{1}
is a cyclic vector of

vii) follows from ii), iii), v), vi), Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.9
.

7.
A lifting property

In this section, we begin with the following simple property.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
. Then

i)
T
is invertible if and only if
T^{*}
is invertible;

ii)
\sigma(T^{*})=\sigma(T).

iii) If
X
is a complex Hilbert space, then the equality ii) should be replaced by

\sigma(T^{*})=\overline{\sigma(T)}\equiv\{\bar{\lambda}:\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}
.

Proof.

i) It suffices to note
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is invertible if and only if
T
is bijective, and which is equivalent to
T^{*}:X^{*}\rightarrow X^{*}
is bijective.

ii) follows from i) and the definition of the conjugate operator
T^{*}
of
T
.
∎

We need also the following “lefting property” related to
w^{*}
-to-
w^{*}
continuous operators defined a subspace of the dual
X^{*}
of
X
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Suppose that
Z^{\dagger}\subset Z^{*}
is a separable Banach space which is
w^{*}
- dense subspace of
Z^{*}
, and
A^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{\dagger})
is
w^{*}
-continuous. Then

i)
A^{\dagger}
has a
w^{*}
-continuous extension
A^{*}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{*})
;

ii)   If
A^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{\dagger})
is invertible, then
A^{*}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{*})
is also invertible. Furthermore, if
A^{\dagger}\in B_{1}(\Omega)
, then
A^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{\dagger})
is invertible if and only if
A^{*}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{*})
is invertible. Consequently,

iii)

\sigma(A^{\dagger})\supseteq\sigma(A^{*}),\;{\rm and}\;\;\rho(A^{\dagger})\subseteq\rho(A^{*});

Furthermore, if
A^{\dagger}\in B_{1}(\Omega)
, then

\sigma(A^{\dagger})=\sigma(A^{*}),{\rm and}\;\;\rho(A^{\dagger})=\rho(A^{*}).

Proof.

It suffices to show ii) and iii).

ii) Since
A^{\dagger}\equiv A^{*}|_{Z^{\dagger}}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{\dagger})
is invertible,
(A^{\dagger})^{-1}:Z^{\dagger}\rightarrow Z^{\dagger}
is an isomorphism.
We first claim that
(A^{\dagger})^{-1}
is also a b-
w^{*}
-isomorphism of
Z^{\dagger}
, i.e., it is continuous with respect to the bounded weak-star topology of
Z^{*}
. Since
Z^{\dagger}
is separable,
the b-
w^{*}
topology is metrizable on each bounded subset of
Z^{\dagger}
. Consequently, the
w^{*}
-convergence on bounded sets is equivalent to
w^{*}
-sequential convergence.
Let
\{x^{*}_{n}\}\subset Z^{\dagger}
be a (bounded) sequence
w^{*}
-converging to
x^{*}\in Z^{\dagger}
. Then invertibility of
A^{\dagger}
on
Z^{\dagger}
implies that
\{y^{*}_{n}\}
is also
bounded in
Z^{\dagger}
, where
y^{*}_{n}=A^{\dagger}x^{*}_{n}
. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem says that there is a subsequence of
\{y^{*}_{n}\}
(again denoted by
\{y^{*}_{n}\}
)

w^{*}
-converging to some
y^{*}\in Z^{*}
. We want to show that
y^{*}=A^{\dagger}x^{*}
. Since
A^{\dagger}
is
w^{*}
-continuous, we obtain
x^{*}_{n}=A^{\dagger}y^{*}_{n}

w^{*}
-converges to
A^{\dagger}y^{*}
.
Therefore,
A^{\dagger}y^{*}=x^{*}
. Consequently,
(A^{\dagger})^{-1}x^{*}=y^{*}\in Z^{\dagger}
.

We will show that
(A^{\dagger})^{-1}
can be b-
w^{*}
-continuously extended to
Z^{*}
. It is equivalent to that for each
z^{*}\in Z^{*}
, there exists
x^{*}\in Z^{*}
such that for every bounded sequence
\{z^{*}_{n}\}
in
Z^{\dagger}
which is
w^{*}
-converging to
z^{*}\in Z^{*}
, we have
w^{*}
-
\lim_{n}(A^{\dagger})^{-1}z^{*}_{n}=x^{*}
. Indeed, suppose that
\{u^{*}_{n}\}
and
\{v^{*}_{n}\}
are two bounded sequences in
Z^{\dagger}
so that both of them are
w^{*}
-converging to
z^{*}\in Z^{*}
. Then
\{w_{n}^{*}\}
(
w^{*}_{n}=u^{*}_{n}-v^{*}_{n}
) is
w^{*}
-converging to
0
. Therefore,
w^{*}
-continuity of
(A^{\dagger})^{-1}
on
Z^{\dagger}
entails that
(A^{\dagger})^{-1}w^{*}_{n}
is again
w^{*}
-converging to
0
. This says that the two sequences
\{(A^{\dagger})^{-1}u_{n}^{*}\}
and
\{(A^{\dagger})^{-1}v_{n}^{*}\}
have same
w^{*}
-cluster points. Relative
w^{*}
-compactness of them deduces that
w^{*}
-
\lim_{n}(A^{\dagger})^{-1}u^{*}_{n}=w^{*}\text{-}\lim_{n}(A^{\dagger})^{-1}v^{*}_{n}
. Therefore, we have shown that
(A^{\dagger})^{-1}
can be b-
w^{*}
-continuously extended to the b-
w^{*}
completion of
Z^{\dagger}
. By the Krein-S̆mulian theorem, the b-
w^{*}
completion of
Z^{\dagger}
is just
Z^{*}
and both the b-
w^{*}
continuity and the
w^{*}
-continuity coincide on
Z^{*}
for all linear operators in
\mathfrak{B}(Z^{*})
. We denote the
w^{*}
-continuous extension of
A^{\dagger}
by
B^{*}
. For completion of the proof of v), it remains to show that
A^{*}B^{*}=I_{Z^{*}}
.
Given
z^{*}\in Z^{*}
, let
\{z_{n}^{*}\}\subset Z^{\dagger}
be a sequence
w^{*}
-converging to
z^{*}
. Then
w^{*}
-continuity of both
A^{*}
and
B^{*}
entails that

(A^{*}B^{*})z^{*}=w^{*}\text{-}\lim_{n}(A^{*}B^{*})z^{*}_{n}=w^{*}\text{-}\lim_{n}(A^{*}B^{*})|_{Z^{\dagger}}z^{*}_{n}=w^{*}\text{-}\lim_{n}z^{*}_{n}=z^{*}.

Therefore,
A^{*}B^{*}=I_{Z^{*}}
and (1) has been shown.

Suppose that
A^{\dagger}\in B_{1}(\Omega)
and that
A^{*}\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{*})
is invertible. Then there is
K>0
such that
\|A^{*}x\|\geq K\|x\|
for all
x\in Z^{*}
. In particular,
\|A^{\dagger}x\|\geq K\|x\|
for all
x\in Z^{\dagger}
. Thus,
{\rm ran}A^{\dagger}
is closed. By Lemma
4.4
,
{\rm ran}A^{\dagger}=Z^{\dagger}
and
A^{\dagger}
is invertible.

iii) By ii) we have just proven, if
A^{\dagger}-\lambda\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{\dagger})
is invertible on
Z^{\dagger}
, then its natural extension
A^{*}-\lambda\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{*})
is invertible. Equivalently, if

\lambda\in\rho(A^{\dagger})
, then
\lambda\in\rho(A^{*})
. Consequently,
\rho(A^{\dagger})\subseteq\rho(A^{*})
. Suppose
A^{\dagger}\in B_{1}(\Omega)
. By ii),
A^{\dagger}-\lambda\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{\dagger})
is invertible on
Z^{\dagger}
if and only if its natural extension
A^{*}-\lambda\in\mathfrak{B}(Z^{*})
is invertible. Equivalently, if

\lambda\in\rho(A^{\dagger})
, then
\lambda\in\rho(A^{*})
. Consequently,
\rho(A^{\dagger})=\rho(A^{*})
.

∎

8.
Spectrum properties

Lemma 8.1
.

Suppose that
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is invertible transitive. Let the spaces
\mathfrak{N}
,
\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}
, and the operators
U,V\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N})
and
U^{\dagger},V^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger})
be defined in Lemma
6.1
. Then

(1)

\sigma(U^{\dagger})=\sigma(U^{*})\;\;\;{\rm and}\;\rho(U^{\dagger})=\rho(U^{*})
;

(2)

\sigma(V^{\dagger})\supseteq\sigma(V^{*})\;\;\;{\rm and}\;\rho(V^{\dagger})\subseteq\rho(V^{*})
;

(3)

\sigma_{e}(U^{\dagger})=\sigma_{e}(U^{*}).

Proof.

(1) and (2) follow directly from Lemma
6.1
and Theorem
7.2
. By Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
6.1
,
U^{\dagger}\in B_{1}(\Omega_{2})
,
\Omega_{2}
is maximal and

\partial\Omega_{2}\subseteq\sigma_{e}(U^{\dagger}).

By Lemma
6.1
,
x_{1}=T^{-1}x_{0}
is a cyclic vector of
U
. By Proposition
4.7
,
{\rm dimker}(U^{*}-\lambda)\leq 1
for all
\lambda
and
{\rm dimker}(U^{\dagger}-\lambda)\leq 1
for all
\lambda
.

By Lemma
4.2
, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
U^{\dagger}-\lambda
has dense range. To prove (3), it suffices to show that surjectivity of
U^{\dagger}-\lambda
on
Z^{\dagger}
implies surjectivity of
U^{*}-\lambda
on
Z^{*}
. Let
z^{*}\in Z^{*}
, and
\{z_{n}^{*}\}\subset Z^{\dagger}
be a sequence such that

z^{*}=w^{*}\text{-}lim_{n}z_{n}^{*}.

Let
Y^{\dagger}=\ker(U^{\dagger}-\lambda)\subset Z^{\dagger}
. Define
Q:\,Z^{\dagger}/Y^{\dagger}\rightarrow Z^{\dagger}
by

Q(y^{*}+Y^{\dagger})=(U^{\dagger}-\lambda)y^{*},\;\;y^{*}\in Z^{*}.

Then
Q
is an isomorphism from
Z^{\dagger}/Y^{\dagger}
onto
Z^{\dagger}
. Therefore, for each
z_{n}^{*}
, there exists a
y_{n}^{*}\in Z^{\dagger}
such that

Q(y_{n}^{*}+Y^{\dagger})=(U^{\dagger}-\lambda)y_{n}^{*}=z_{n}^{*}.

Since,
\{y_{n}^{*}+Y^{\dagger}\}
is a bounded sequence in
Z^{\dagger}/Y^{\dagger}
, we may assume that
\{y^{*}_{n}\}
is bounded in
Z^{*}
. By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there is a subsequence of
\{y^{*}_{n}\}
(again denoted by
\{y^{*}_{n}\}
)
w^{*}
-convergent to some point
y^{*}\in Z^{*}
.
Then

z^{*}=w^{*}\text{-}\lim_{n}z_{n}^{*}=w^{*}\text{-}\lim_{n}(U^{\dagger}-\lambda)y_{n}^{*}=w^{*}\text{-}\lim_{n}(U^{*}-\lambda)y_{n}^{*}=(U^{*}-\lambda)y^{*}.

Therefore,
U^{*}-\lambda
is surjective.

∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Suppose that
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is invertible transitive,
0\neq x_{0}\in X
, and that the spaces
\mathfrak{N}
,
\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger}
, and the operators
U,V\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N})
and
U^{\dagger},V^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}^{\dagger})
be defined in Lemma
6.1
.
Assume that
\Omega_{0}
is the connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(T^{-1})
containing the origin.
Then

i)  For every
\lambda\in\Omega_{0}
,
U-\lambda
is a Fredholm operator with

\text{dimker}(U-\lambda)=0,\;\;{\rm and\;}\;\text{co-dim}(U-\lambda)=1.

Consequently,

\text{ind}(U-\lambda)\equiv\text{dimker}(U-\lambda)-\text{co-dim}(U-\lambda)=-1.

ii)
V\in B_{1}(\Omega_{V})
, where
\Omega_{V}=\left\{\lambda:\,\frac{1}{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(U)^{\land}\bigcup\{\infty\}\right\}
and
\partial\Omega_{V}\subseteq\sigma_{e}(V)
.

Proof.

i) By Lemma
6.1
,
\mbox{dim ker}U=0
,
\mbox{co-dim ran}U=1
, and ind
U=-1
.
Since
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is invertible transitive, for every
\lambda\in\Omega_{0}
,
T^{-1}-\lambda
is again invertible.
Therefore,
U-\lambda=(T^{-1}-\lambda)|_{\mathfrak{N}}
is injective, and consequently,
\mbox{dim ker}(U-\lambda)=0
. Since
\Omega_{0}
is the connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(T^{-1})
containing the origin, and since ind
U=-1
, continuity of the index entails ind
(U-\lambda)=-1
and
\mbox{co-dim ran}(U-\lambda)=1
.

ii)
By Lemma
6.1
,
\mbox{dimker}V=1
,
\mbox{co-dimran}U=0
, ind
V=1
and
VU=I
.
Write
e_{0}=T^{-1}x_{0}
and
\delta=\frac{1}{\|U\|}
. Set

S_{\lambda}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}U^{n}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{N}),\;{\rm for\;all\;}|\lambda|<\delta.

Then
S_{\lambda}
is holomorphic on
\{\lambda:\,|\lambda|<\delta\}
. Since
V
is surjective and since

VS_{\lambda}e_{0}=\lambda S_{\lambda}e_{0}
for all
|\lambda|<\delta
, we obtain
V\in B_{1}(\Omega_{V})
. It follows from uniqueness of the extension of the holomorphic map
S_{\lambda}
. Note that
\Omega_{V}
is maximal. Then by Lemma
4.5
,
\partial\Omega_{V}\subseteq\sigma_{e}(V)
.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

Suppose
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is transitive invertible and
U,V
be defined by Lemma
6.1
. Then

\rho_{\text{s-F}}(U)=\rho_{\text{F}}(U).

Furthermore,

i)
\sigma_{e}(U)\subseteq\sigma_{e}(T^{-1})
;

ii)
\sigma_{e}(V)=\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda}:\,\lambda\in\sigma_{e}(U)\right\}
;

\sigma_{e}(V^{\dagger})=\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda}:\,\lambda\in\sigma_{e}(U^{\dagger})\right\}
.

Since
T
is transitive,
\sigma_{p}(T^{-1})=\emptyset
. Note that
U=T^{-1}|_{\mathfrak{N}}
. Then
\sigma_{p}(U)=\emptyset
. By Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
6.1
,
x_{1}=T^{-1}x_{0}
is a cyclic vector of

4.7

Note that

∎

10.
More spectrum properties

Recall (
1.3
) that for
A\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
, we can define a quotient operator
A^{Y}\in\mathfrak{B}(X/Y)
by

(10.1)

A^{Y}(x+Y)=Ax+Y,\;\;{\rm for\;all\;}x\in X.

if
Y\subset X
is a closed invariant subspace of
A
.

Lemma 10.1
.

Suppose that
A\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
and
Y\subset{\rm Lat}A
. Then
A\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
admits the three-operator property, i.e., invertibility of any two of the three operators
A,A^{Y}
and
A|_{Y}
implies invertibility of the third one. Consequently,

(1)

\sigma(A)\subseteq\sigma(A|_{Y})\cup\sigma(A^{Y})
;

(2)

\sigma(A|_{Y})\subseteq\sigma(A)\cup\sigma(A^{Y})
;

(3)

\sigma(A^{Y})\subseteq\sigma(A)\cup\sigma(A|_{Y})
.

Proof.

Here we only show that if both
A_{Y}
and
A^{Y}
are invertible, then
A
is invertible. Suppose, to the contrary, that
A
is either not injective, or, not surjective.
The former implies that there is
0\neq z\in X
so that
Az=0
. If
z\in Y
, then
A_{Y}
is not invertible. If
z\in X\setminus Y
, then
z+Y\neq 0
,
A(z+Y)=0
, which means that
A^{Y}
is not injective. The latter entails that there is
z\in X\setminus\mbox{ran}A
. Consequently, if
z\notin Y
, then
z+Y\notin\mbox{ran}(A^{Y})
; if
z\in Y
, then
A|_{Y}y\neq z
for all
y\in Y
. Thus, either
A^{Y}
, or,
A|_{Y}
is not surjective.
∎

Lemma 10.2
.

Suppose that
T\in\mathfrak{B}(X)
is an invertible transitive operator and
0\neq x_{0}\in X
so that
\mathfrak{N}\equiv[x_{n}]_{n\geq 1}\subset X
, and that

\{(x_{n},x^{*}_{n})\}\subset X\times X^{*}
is the bi-orthogonal system with
x_{n}=T^{-n}x_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
defined in Lemma
5.3
.
Let
T_{1}=T-x_{0}\otimes x^{*}_{1}
.
Then

(1)

\mathfrak{N}\in\mbox{Lat}T_{1}\bigcap\mbox{Lat}T^{-1}
;

(2)

(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}T_{1}^{\mathfrak{N}}=I_{X/\mathfrak{N}}
;

(3)

\mbox{ind}(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}=1
. Consequently, there is a maximal connected open set
\Omega_{\rm max}\subset\mathbb{C}
containing the origin such that
(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}\in B_{1}(\Omega_{\rm max})
.

Proof.

(1) It suffices to note that
T_{1}x_{1}=0
and
T_{1}x_{k}=x_{k-1}
for all
k\geq 2
.

(2) It follows from (1) that we can define the quotient mappings as following:

(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}(x+\mathfrak{N})=T^{-1}x+\mathfrak{N},

T_{1}^{\mathfrak{N}}(x+\mathfrak{N})=T_{1}x+\mathfrak{N}.

Therefore, for every
x+\mathfrak{N}\in X/\mathfrak{N}
,

(10.2)

(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}T_{1}^{\mathfrak{N}}(x+\mathfrak{N})=T^{-1}T_{1}x+\mathfrak{N}.

This shows that
(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}T_{1}^{\mathfrak{N}}=I_{X/\mathfrak{N}}
.

(3) Since
(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}(x_{0}+\mathfrak{N})=x_{1}+\mathfrak{N}=\mathfrak{N}
,
0\neq x_{0}+\mathfrak{N}\in\mbox{ker}(T^{-1})^{\mathfrak{N}}
. Note that

T_{1}^{\mathfrak{N}}(x_{0}+{\mathfrak{N}})=Tx_{0}+\alpha x_{0}+{\mathfrak{N}}.

Then
[x_{0}+{\mathfrak{N}},T_{1}^{\mathfrak{N}}(x_{0}+{\mathfrak{N}})]=[x_{0}+{\mathfrak{N}},Tx_{0}+{\mathfrak{N}}]
. By induction, we know that for all
