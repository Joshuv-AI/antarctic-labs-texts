A pressure-robust HHO method for the solution of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations on general meshes
Daniel Castanon Quiroz, Daniele A. Di Pietro

Abstract
In a recent work [ 11 ] , we have introduced a pressure-robust Hybrid High-Order method for the numerical solution of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations on matching simplicial meshes. Pressure-robust methods are characterized by error estimates for the velocity that are fully independent of the pressure. A crucial question was left open in that work, namely whether the proposed construction could be extended to general polytopal meshes. In this paper we provide a positive answer to this question. Specifically, we introduce a novel divergence-preserving velocity reconstruction that hinges on the solution inside each element of a mixed problem on a subtriangulation, then use it to design discretizations of the body force and convective terms that lead to pressure robustness. † † margin: R#1 and R#2 remark: The proposed method now works for arbitrary k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 and general meshes An in-depth theoretical study of the properties of this velocity reconstruction, and their reverberation on the scheme, is carried out for arbitrary polynomial degrees k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 and meshes composed of general polytopes . The theoretical convergence estimates and the pressure robustness of the method are confirmed by an extensive panel of numerical examples. Key words: Hybrid High-Order methods, incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, general meshes, pressure robustness MSC 2010: 65N08, 65N30, 65N12, 35Q30, 76D05

Introduction

This paper focuses on numerical approximations of the Navier–Stokes equations robust with respect to large irrotational body forces.
Specifically, we address a nontrivial question left open in the previous work
[
11
]
, namely whether robustness can be achieved on general polyhedral meshes such as the ones supported by the Hybrid High-Order (HHO) method
[
22
,
16
]
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote an open, bounded, simply connected polyhedral domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Let
\nu>0
be the kinematic viscosity of the fluid and
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
a given vector field representing a body force.
Setting
\boldsymbol{U}\coloneq H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{3}
and
P\coloneq{L}^{2}_{0}({\Omega})=\left\{q\in{L}^{2}({\Omega}):\int_{\Omega}q=0\right\}
, we consider the Navier–Stokes problem:
Find
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\boldsymbol{U}\times P
such that

\displaystyle\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}((\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u})\times\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v})p

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{U},

(1a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u})q

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall q\in{L}^{2}({\Omega}).

(1b)

Above,
\nabla\cdot
and
\nabla\times
denote, respectively, the divergence and curl operators, while
\times
is the cross product of two vectors.
The convective term in (
1a
) is expressed in rotational form, so
p
is here the Bernoulli pressure, which is related to the kinematic pressure
p_{\text{kin}}
by the equation
p=p_{\text{kin}}+\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}
.

The domain
\Omega
being simply connected, we have the following Hodge decomposition of the body force (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Section 4.3]
):

\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{g}+\lambda\nabla\psi,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{g}
is the curl of a function in
\boldsymbol{H}({\bf curl};\Omega)
the tangent trace of which vanishes on
\partial\Omega
,

\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is such that
\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}=1
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
It is well-known that, at the continuous level, the velocity field is entirely determined by the first component in the decomposition (
2
).
This property, however, does not carry out automatically to the discrete level.
The development of numerical methods that possess this property, and which are sometimes referred to in the literature as
pressure-robust
, has been an active field of research over the last few years;
see, e.g.,
[
26
,
42
,
41
,
33
,
1
]
concerning finite element methods on standard meshes.

Recently, the mathematical community have become interested in the development of arbitrary-order approximation methods that support more general meshes than standard finite elements and which can include, e.g., polyhedral elements and non-matching interfaces.
A representative but by far non exhaustive list of references concerning incompressible flow problems includes
[
18
,
19
,
23
,
29
,
8
,
5
,
4
,
52
]
; see also the recent works
[
10
,
12
]
concerning non-Newtonian fluids.
Pressure-robust variations of the HHO method on matching simplicial meshes for the Stokes and Navier–Stokes problem have been proposed, respectively, in
[
20
,
11
]
.

The development of pressure-robust methods on polyhedral meshes is, however, a challenging task.
Some of the first genuinely pressure-robust polyhedral methods for the Stokes equations have been proposed in
[
43
,
51
,
53
]
.
These methods handle the lowest order case using a velocity reconstruction in
\boldsymbol{H}({\rm div};\Omega)
introduced in
[
13
]
and relying on Wachspress (generalized barycentric) coordinates.
This approach has two shortcomings:
first, the faces of each (convex) polyhedral element must be either triangles or parallelograms;
second, error estimates for the approximated velocity would require gradient bounds for the Wachspress coordinates on an arbitrary convex polyhedron, the derivation of which remains, to the best of our knowledge, an open problem.
Regarding arbitrary-order methods on general meshes, a pressure-robust Virtual Element method has been recently proposed in
[
27
]
for the Stokes equations.
The extension of this method to the Navier–Stokes equations remains, to the best of our knowledge, an open problem.
A pressure-robust discretization scheme for the full Navier–Stokes equations has been proposed in
[
35
]
based on the staggered Discontinuous Garlekin method.
This method solves for three unknowns (the pressure, the velocity, and its gradient), thus leading to larger algebraic systems.
Recently, a novel HHO method for which pressure-robustness has been numerically demonstrated has been proposed in
[
9
]
.
This method uses a larger pressure space than the one considered in the present work, and the derivation of rigorous pressure-robust error estimates is still to be done.
An entirely different approach to pressure-robustness on polyhedral meshes has also been recently pursued in
[
49
]
, hinging on the compatibility features of Discrete de Rham
[
17
,
15
]
and Virtual Element methods.
While this approach leads to a fully pressure-robust, arbitrary-order method, it is based on a curl-curl formulation of the viscous term, which does not lend itself naturally to the treatment of certain standard boundary conditions.

In the present work, we propose a novel fully pressure-robust HHO method for the Navier–Stokes problem (
1
) that works in space dimension two and three and supports general meshes composed of polytopal elements.
The cornerstone of the method is a local divergence-preserving reconstruction of the velocity built inside each mesh element
T
by solving a mixed problem inspired by
[
38
,
39
,
40
]

The assumptions made in Section

2.2
Local and broken spaces and projectors

Let

denote a mesh element or face and, for an integer

, denote by

\mathcal{P}^{l}(X)

of polynomials in the space variables of total degree

The

\int_{X}(\zeta-\pi_{X}^{l}\zeta)w=0\qquad\forall w\in\mathcal{P}^{l}(X).

(4)

2.5
Gradient reconstruction on a submesh

Let an element
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
be fixed.
For every face
\sigma\in\mathfrak{F}_{T}^{\rm i}
, we introduce an arbitrary but fixed ordering of the elements
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
such that

\tau_{i}
(see Figure
1

With this convention, for every scalar-valued function

admitting a possibly two-valued trace on
\sigma

\zeta
across

\llbracket\zeta\rrbracket_{\sigma}\coloneq\zeta_{|\tau_{1}}-\zeta_{|\tau_{2}}.

When applied to vector- or tensor-valued functions, the jump operator acts component-wise.

For any polyomial degree

, we then define the local gradient reconstruction

\displaystyle=-\int_{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\cdot(\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}})+\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{F}_{T}^{\rm i}}\int_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\cdot\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\rrbracket_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n}_{\sigma}+\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T}}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{v}_{F}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{n}_{TF},

(46b)

where we have used an integration by parts to pass to the second line.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
3

Proof.

For this proof we take inspiration mainly from
[
37
, Section 3]
.

†
†
margin:

R#1 major point: checking and citing related work

First of all, let us introduce a few new definitions.
We denote by
\hat{\tau}
the reference tetrahedron. From the assumptions of Section
2.1
, there exists
\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
which is a common vertex
for all simplices in
\mathfrak{T}_{T}
.
Then, for each
\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T}
, it is possible to construct a one-to-one affine map
\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}:\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\tau
such that

\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{x}_{T},

(79)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}
is an invertible real matrix of size
3\times 3
.
Now,
given
\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}},\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{P}^{l}(\hat{\tau})^{3}
where
l\geq 0
we introduce, respectively, the contravariant and covariant Piola’s transformations (see
[
24
]
) as follows,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{d,\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}})\coloneq|\det\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}|^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\circ\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}^{-1})

and

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{c,\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}})\coloneq\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}^{-\text{T}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\circ\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}^{-1}),

(80)

which crucially satisfy

\int_{\tau}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{d,\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{c,\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{w}})=\int_{\hat{\tau}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}.

(81)

We now introduce the space
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\hat{\tau})\coloneq\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\mathcal{P}^{k-2}(\hat{\tau})^{3}
, and the operator

\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\hat{\tau}}:L^{2}(\hat{\tau})^{3}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\hat{\tau})

such that, for all
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in L^{2}(\hat{\tau})^{3}
and all
\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\hat{\tau})
,

\int_{\hat{\tau}}\hat{\lambda}^{2}{\widehat{\nabla}\times}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\hat{\tau}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\widehat{\nabla}\times}\hat{\boldsymbol{g}}=\int_{\hat{\tau}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{g}},

(82)

where
\hat{\lambda}
is defined as the product of all the barycentric coordinates of
{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
in
\hat{\tau}
, i.e.,
\hat{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{4}\hat{\lambda}_{i}
.
The fact that (
82
) defines
\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\hat{\tau}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}})
uniquely follows from the Riesz representation theorem after observing that
\widehat{\nabla}\times:\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\hat{\tau})\to{\mathcal{P}^{k-2}(\hat{\tau})^{3}}
is an isomorphism.
We then define the operator

{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\tau}:L^{2}(\tau)^{3}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^{k+5}(\tau)^{3}\cap H^{1}_{0}(\tau)^{3}
as follows
(see
[
37
, Eq. (45)]
)

{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{w})\coloneq{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{d,\tau}\left({\widehat{\nabla}\times}\left[\hat{\lambda}^{2}\widehat{\nabla}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\hat{\tau}}({\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{-1}_{d,\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}))\right]\right).

(83)

Using standard properties of the contravariant transformation
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{d,\tau}(\cdot)
, we infer
\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{w})=0
; moreover,
it is proved in
[
37
, Proof of Proposition 17]
that
\|\boldsymbol{E}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{{{L}^{2}({\tau})^{3}}}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{{{L}^{2}({\tau})^{3}}}
.
Then, for given a function
\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(T)^{3}
,
we define
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}
as the interpolate of
\boldsymbol{E}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v})
onto the space

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{RT}}^{k}(\tau)
, thus we have
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}=0
. Additionally, since
\boldsymbol{E}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v})\in H^{1}_{0}(\tau)^{3}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}

has zero normal trace at the boundary of
\tau
and,
using standard interpolation estimates for
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}
(see, e.g.,
[
6
, Proposition 2.5.1]
),
the bound
\|\boldsymbol{E}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{{{L}^{2}({\tau})^{3}}}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{{{L}^{2}({\tau})^{3}}}
,
and a discrete inverse inequality (this is valid since
\boldsymbol{E}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v})
is a polynomial function), it is inferred that

\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}\|_{{{L}^{2}({\tau})^{3}}}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{{{L}^{2}({\tau})^{3}}}
.
We now define
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{RT}}^{k}_{0}(\mathfrak{T}_{T})
as

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})_{|\tau}\coloneq\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}\qquad\forall\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T},

(84)

and observe that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})
clearly satisfies the properties (
18b
–
18d
) from the above discussion.

To prove (
18a
), we introduce the space

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\mathfrak{T}_{T})\coloneq(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{T})\times\mathcal{P}^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}_{T})^{3},

(85)

and denote the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projector onto this space by
{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{{{\rm c},k-1}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\mathfrak{T}_{T}}
.
Observe that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(T)\subset\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\mathfrak{T}_{T})
,
thus

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{{\rm c},k-1}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},T}({\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{{\rm c},k-1}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\mathfrak{T}_{T}}\boldsymbol{w})={\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{{\rm c},k-1}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},T}\boldsymbol{w}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{w}\in L^{2}(T),

then (
18a
) holds a fortiori if we prove that

{\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{{\rm c},k-1}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\mathfrak{T}_{T}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})={\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{{\rm c},k-1}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}},\mathfrak{T}_{T}}\boldsymbol{v}.

(86)

To prove it, let
\boldsymbol{g}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\mathfrak{T}_{T})
and

\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T}
.
Then, using the definition (
84
) of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})
, we obtain, for all
\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T}
,

\int_{\tau}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{g}=\int_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}=\int_{\tau}\boldsymbol{E}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{g}=\int_{\hat{\tau}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{-1}_{d,\tau}(\boldsymbol{E}^{k-1}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{v}))\cdot{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{-1}_{c,\tau}(\boldsymbol{g})\eqcolon\mathfrak{T},

(87)

where in the second step we have used the interpolation properties of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{0,\tau}
and the fact that
\boldsymbol{g}_{|\tau}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(\tau)^{3}
along with the definition of the Raviart–Thomas interpolator, and in the last step the definitions (
80
) of the Piola transformations and the identity (
81
).
By definition (
85
),
we have that
\boldsymbol{g}_{|\tau}=(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{T})\times\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{g}}
where
\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{g}}\in\mathcal{P}^{k-2}(\tau)^{3}
.
With this mind, and using (
79
), we get that

{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{-1}_{c,\tau}(\boldsymbol{g})(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})=(\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}^{-1})^{-\text{T}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\boldsymbol{q}_{g}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}))\right)=\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\det(\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau})\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{q}_{g}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})),

(88)

where in the last step we have used the matrix-cross-product identity
(see
[
24
, Ex.9.5]
)
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{A}}^{-\text{T}}(\boldsymbol{y}\times\boldsymbol{z})=\det(\boldsymbol{\mathbb{A}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbb{A}}\boldsymbol{y}\times\boldsymbol{\mathbb{A}}\boldsymbol{z}
valid for any
\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and any invertible real matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{A}}
of size
3\times 3
.
Now, define

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{g}\coloneq\det(\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau})\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}_{\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{q}_{g}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}))\in\mathcal{P}^{k-2}(\hat{\tau})^{3}
.
Then, using the definition (
83
), we compute
\mathfrak{T}
in (
87
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\tau}}{\widehat{\nabla}\times}\left(\hat{\lambda}^{2}{\widehat{\nabla}\times}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\hat{\tau}}({\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{-1}_{d,\tau}(\boldsymbol{v}))\right)\cdot(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{g})

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\tau}}\hat{\lambda}^{2}{\widehat{\nabla}\times}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}_{\hat{\tau}}({\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{-1}_{d,\tau}(\boldsymbol{v}))\cdot{\widehat{\nabla}\times}{}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{g})

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\tau}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{-1}_{d,\tau}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{q}}_{g})=\int_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}^{-1}_{\tau}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathbb{J}}^{\text{T}}_{\tau}((\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{T})\times{\boldsymbol{q}}_{g})=\int_{\tau}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{g},

where, in the second line, we have used integration by parts,
along with the fact that
\hat{\lambda}^{2}{\widehat{\nabla}\times}\hat{\boldsymbol{E}}^{k-1}(\cdot)
vanishes at the boundary of
\hat{\tau}
,
in the third line first the definition (
82
),
then a change of coordinates using (
79
) along with the definitions (
80
)
and the same matrix-cross-product identity as before, and finally some standard properties of the transpose.
Thus, using the last equation above and (
87
), we have that

\int_{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{g}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T}}\int_{\tau}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}_{T}^{k}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot\boldsymbol{g}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T}}\int_{\tau}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}=\int_{T}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}.

(89)

Since
\boldsymbol{g}
is an arbitrary element of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\mathfrak{T}_{T})
, it implies (
86
), and we conclude.
∎

Remark 16

(The common vertex assumption)
.

In the proof of Lemma
3
, the fact that
\boldsymbol{x}_{T}
is a common vertex for all simpex
\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_{T}
allows to express the affine transformation
\boldsymbol{F}_{\tau}:\hat{\tau}\rightarrow\tau
as (
79
) implying the key property that the covariant transformation
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{c,\tau}:\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}^{{\rm c},k-1}(\hat{\tau})\rightarrow({\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{T})\times\mathcal{P}^{k-2}({\tau})^{3}
, defined in (
80
), is an isomorphism. This is required for (
88
) and (
89
), and then making possible to prove (
18a
) and (
18d
).
