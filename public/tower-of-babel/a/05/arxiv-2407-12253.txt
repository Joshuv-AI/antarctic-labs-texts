Abstract
. In 1942 Mann solved a famous problem, the α + β \alpha+\beta conjecture, about the lower bound of the Shnirel’man density of sums of sets of positive integers. In 1945, Dyson generalized Mann’s theorem and obtained a lower bound for the Shnirel’man density of rank r r sumsets. His proof introduced the Dyson transform, an important tool in additive number theory. This paper explains the background of Dyson’s work, gives Dyson’s proof of his theorem, and includes several applications of the Dyson transform, such as Kneser’s inequality for sums of finite subsets of an arbitrary additive abelian group.

1.
A theorem on the densities of sets of integers

In 1945 Freeman Dyson
[
5
]
published a paper in additive number
theory that gave a lower bound for the Shnirel’man density of
r
-rank
sums of sets of nonnegative integers.
In a commentary on this paper in his

Dyson wrote an autobiographical history of this result.

This paper was inspired by …the little book,
“Über einige neuere Fortschritte der additiven Zahlentheorie,”
by Edmund Landau, [Cambridge University Press, 1937].
As soon as I arrived in Cambridge in 1941, I scoured the book-shops for bargains
and bought Landau’s book
for six shillings and sixpence….
His Chapter 4 describes the “alpha-beta conjecture”, which was in 1937
a famous unsolved problem.
The conjecture concerns the densities of infinite sets of positive integers….
I laid the problem in my heart and worked hard to find a proof….

While I was at Cambridge, I learned that Henry Mann …had proved
the alpha-beta conjecture, [Annals of Mathematics,
43
, 523–527 (1942)]….
I was sorry that Mann had beaten me in the race to find a proof,
but I was glad to see that his strengthening of the conjecture still left me something to do….
The generalization of Mann’s theorem to the sum of more than two sets
remained to be proved….
I took the problem with me when I left Cambridge in 1943 to work in the
Operational Research Section of the Royal Air Force Bomber Command
at High Wycombe. I solved it in the early months of 1944 during a particularly grim
period in the history of the Bomber Command….
The Mann Theorem helped to keep me sane amid the insanities of the bombing campaign.

|A_{h}^{\prime}\cap\{1,\ldots,g\}|<|A_{h}\cap\{1,\ldots,g\}|

for some positive integer
g
.
Through the work of Martin Kneser, a variant of this transformation,
variously called the

,

-transform
, and the
Dyson
e
-transform
,
has become a fundamental tool in additive number theory.
This paper explains the mathematical context of Dyson’s paper,
presents a proof of Dyson’s addition theorem, and describes some
applications of the Dyson transform.
For a history of the

conjecture, see Erdős and Niven

2.
Shnirel’man density and sums of primes

Associated to every set
A
of integers is the number
\sigma(A)
, called the

Shnirel’man density
of
A
.
The
Dyson transform
was introduced to obtain
a lower bound for Shnirel’man density. To appreciate Dyson’s work, we
need to understand the importance of Shnirel’man density in additive number theory.

Let
(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{h})
be an
h
-tuple of sets of positive integers.
The
sumset

\sum_{i=1}^{h}A_{i}=A_{1}+A_{2}+\cdots+A_{h}

is the set of all integers
that can be written in the form
a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots+a_{h}
, where
a_{i}\in A_{i}\cup\{0\}

for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,h\}
. The
h
-fold sumset
of a set
A
is the set

hA=\underbrace{A+A+\cdots+A}_{\text{$h$ summands}}.

Let
\mathbf{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
be the set of nonnegative integers.
The set
A
of positive integers is a
basis of order
h
if
every nonnegative integer is a sum of
h
elements of
A\cup\{0\}
,
or, equivalently, if
hA=\mathbf{N}_{0}
.
The set
A
is a
basis
if
A
is a basis of order
h
for
some positive integer
h
.
For example, by Lagrange’s theorem, the set of squares is a basis of order 4.
Waring’s problem states that, for every integer
k\geq 2
, there is a positive
integer
g(k)
such that the set of positive
k
th powers is a basis of order
g(k)
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=\{2,3,5,7,\ldots\}
be the set of prime numbers and let

\widehat{\mathbf{P}}=\{1\}\cup\mathbf{P}
.
The Goldbach conjecture that every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes
implies that the set
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}
is a basis of order 3.
In 1930, in an extraordinarily original and powerful work, the Soviet mathematician
Lev Genrikhovich Shnirel’man
[
31
,
33
,
18
]

proved that there exists an integer
h
such that every integer greater than 1
is the sum of at most
h
prime numbers.
Equivalently, the set
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}
is a basis.
To obtain this result, Shnirel’man
introduced a new density, which we call
Shnirel’man density
,
on sets of integers and proved that every set of positive integers
with positive Shnirel’man density is a basis.
He used the Brun sieve to prove that the sumset
h^{\prime}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}

has positive Shnirel’man density for some positive integer
h^{\prime}
, and,

mirabile dictu
, obtained his result.

Recent results on the Goldbach conjecture include Ramaré’s proof
[
29
]
that
every even integer
n\geq 2
is the sum of at most 6 primes, Tao’s proof
[
35
]

that every odd integer
n\geq 3
is the sum of at most 5 primes,
and Helfgott’s proof
[
11
]
that every odd integer
n\geq 7
is the sum of 3 primes.

To define Shnirel’man density, we use the

counting function

A(x)
of a set
A
of integers.
For all real numbers
x
, this function counts the number of positive integers
in
A
that do not exceed
x
, that is,

A(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
1\leq a\leq x\end{subarray}}1.

Note that
A(x)=0
if
x<1
.
The
Shnirel’man density

of the set
A
is

\sigma(A)=\inf_{n\in\mathbf{N}}\frac{A(n)}{n}.

Shnirel’man density has some usual and some unusual properties.
Let
A
be set of positive integers.
We have
0\leq\sigma(A)\leq 1
.
The set of odd integers has Shnirel’man density
1/2
.
However,
\sigma(A)=0
if
1\notin A
(because
A(1)=0
)
and so the set of even integers has Shnirel’man density
0
.
A fundamental fact about Shnirel’man
density is that
\sigma(A)=1
if and only if
A
contains all positive integers.
Thus, the set
A
is basis of order
h
for some positive integer
h

if and only if
\sigma(hA)=1
.
An important result is Shnirel’man’s theorem that a set
A
with
\sigma(A)>0

is a basis of order
h
for some
h
.
The proof begins with a simple counting argument.
If
\sigma(A)=\alpha\geq 1/2
, then, for every positive integer
n
,
we have

A(n)\geq\alpha n\geq\frac{n}{2}.

Let

1\leq a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}\leq n

be the
k=A(n)
positive integers in
A
that do not exceed
n
.
If
n\in A
, then
n=0+n\in 2A
. If
n\notin A
, then
a_{k}\leq n-1
and so

1\leq a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}\leq n-1

and

1\leq n-a_{k}<\cdots<n-a_{1}\leq n-1.

Thus, the set
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
contains the subsets
A_{1}=\{a_{i}:i=1,\ldots,k\}
and

A_{2}=\{n-a_{j}:j=1,\ldots,k\}
.
Both sets have cardinality
k=A(n)\geq n/2
, and so they cannot be disjoint.
Thus, there exist
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that

a_{i}=n-a_{j}\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}

and
n=a_{i}+a_{j}\in 2A
.
Thus,
\sigma(A)\geq 1/2
implies
\sigma(2A)=1
and so
A
is a basis of order 2.

The crux of Shnirel’man’s proof is a lower bound for the density of an
h
-fold sumset.
Let
A
and
B
be sets of positive integers.
If
\sigma(A)=\alpha
and
\sigma(B)=\beta
, then

(1)

\sigma(A+B)\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

(For a proof of this inequality, see Nathanson
[
26
, Section 7.4]

Equivalently,

1-\sigma(A+B)\leq(1-\alpha)(1-\beta).

It follows by induction that if
A_{1},\ldots,A_{h}
are sets of integers
with
\sigma(A_{i})=\alpha_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,h
, then

1-\sigma\left(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{h}\right)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{h}(1-\alpha_{i}).

Let
A
be a set of integers with
\sigma(A)=\alpha
.
Applying (
1
) with
A_{i}=A

and
\alpha_{i}=\alpha
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
,
we obtain

1-\sigma\left(hA\right)\leq(1-\alpha)^{h}.

Equivalently,

\sigma\left(hA\right)\geq 1-(1-\alpha)^{h}.

If
\alpha>0
, then
\lim_{h\rightarrow\infty}(1-\alpha)^{h}=0

and so
\sigma(hA)\geq 1/2
for all
h\geq h_{0}
.
It follows that
\sigma(2hA)=1
for all
h\geq 2h_{0}
. Thus, to prove that a set
A

is a basis, it suffices to prove that the sumset
hA
has positive
Shnirel’man density for some positive integer
h
.

A
and
B

\sigma(A)=\alpha

\sigma(B)=\beta

3.
The Dyson transform

Let
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{h})
be an
h
-tuple of sets of positive integers and
let
n
be a positive integer with
A_{h}(n)\geq 1
.
A
Dyson triple
is a triple
(a,\ell,c)

such that

(i)

c\in A_{h}

with

c\leq n
,

(ii)

a\in A_{\ell}\cup\{0\}

for some

\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
or
a>n
,

(iii)

if
c+a\leq n
, then
c+a\notin A_{\ell}
.

Let
(a_{0},\ell_{0},c_{0})
be a Dyson triple. We define the set

(4)

T=T(a_{0},\ell_{0})=\{c\in A_{h}:\text{$c\leq n$ and $(a_{0},\ell_{0},c)$ is a Dyson triple}\}.

Note that
c_{0}\in T
and so
T
is a nonempty subset of
A_{h}\cap\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
If
c\in T
and
c\leq n-a_{0}
, then

c+a_{0}\leq n
and so
c+a_{0}\notin A_{\ell_{0}}
.

The
Dyson transform

of the
h
-tuple
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{h})
with respect to
the Dyson triple
(a_{0},\ell_{0},c_{0})

is the
h
-tuple
(A^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,A^{\prime}_{h})
defined as follows:

(5)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{h}

\displaystyle=A_{h}\setminus T

(6)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}

\displaystyle=A_{\ell_{0}}\cup(T+a_{0})

(7)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=A_{\ell}\qquad\text{for all $\ell\in\{1,\ldots,h-1\}\setminus\{\ell_{0}\}$.}

We have

A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}(m)=A_{\ell_{0}}(m)+T(m-a_{0})

for all
m\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
T(n-a_{0})\geq 1
, then
A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}(n)>A_{\ell_{0}}(n)
.
For mathematical induction, the important inequality is

(8)

A^{\prime}_{h}(n)=A_{h}(n)-T(n)<A_{h}(n).

Lemma 1
.

Let
(A^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,A^{\prime}_{h})
be the Dyson transform of
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{h})
with respect to
the Dyson triple
(a_{0},\ell_{0},c_{0})
.
Let
m\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For every set
S
of positive integers,
if
p\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
p\in S+A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}+A^{\prime}_{h}
, then
p\in S+A_{\ell_{0}}+A_{h}
and

(9)

(S+A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}+A^{\prime}_{h})(m)\leq(S+A_{\ell_{0}}+A_{h})(m).

Proof.

Let
p\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
We first prove that if
p\in A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}+A^{\prime}_{h}
,
then
p\in A_{\ell_{0}}+A_{h}
.

If
p\in A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}+A^{\prime}_{h}
, then

p=x+y

for some

x\in A^{\prime}_{\ell_{0}}\cup\{0\}=A_{\ell_{0}}\cup\{0\}\cup(T+a_{0})

and

If
x\in A_{\ell_{0}}\cup\{0\}
, then

p=x+y\in A_{\ell_{0}}+A^{\prime}_{h}\subseteq A_{\ell_{0}}+A_{h}.

If
x\in T+a_{0}
, then
x=c+a_{0}
for some
c\in T\subseteq A_{h}
.
The inequality
c>0
implies
a_{0}<x
.
If
y=0
, then

p=x=c+a_{0}\in A_{\ell_{0}}+A_{h}.

If
y\in A^{\prime}_{h}=A_{h}\setminus T
, then

1\leq y\leq a_{0}+y<x+y=p\leq n.

Because
a_{0}\in A_{\ell_{0}}
and

Thus, if
p\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and

.

Let
S
be a set of positive integers and let

If

, then
m=s+p

It follows that

6.
Asymptotic density and Kneser’s theorem

Let
A
and
B
be sets of positive integers.
By Mann’s
\alpha+\beta
theorem, Shnirel’man density is superadditive
in the following sense:

\sigma(A+B)\geq\min(1,\sigma(A)+\sigma(B)).

The
lower asymptotic density

of the set
A
is

d_{L}(A)=\liminf_{n=1,2,\ldots}\frac{A(n)}{n}.

Asymptotic density is not superadditive. For example, let
k
,
\ell
, and
m
be positive integers
such that

k+\ell\leq m.

Consider the sets

A=\left\{n\in\mathbf{N}:n\equiv r\pmod{m}\text{ for some }r\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\right\}

and

B=\left\{n\in\mathbf{N}:n\equiv s\pmod{m}\text{ for some }s\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}\right\}

with lower asymptotic densities

d_{L}(A)=\frac{k}{m}\qqand d_{L}(B)=\frac{\ell}{m}.

The sumset

A+B=\left\{n\in\mathbf{N}:n\equiv t\pmod{m}\text{ for some }t\in\{0,1,\ldots,k+\ell-2\}\right\}.

has lower asymptotic density

\displaystyle d_{L}(A+B)

\displaystyle=\frac{k+\ell-1}{m}

\displaystyle=d_{L}(A)+d_{L}(B)-\frac{1}{m}

\displaystyle<d_{L}(A)+d_{L}(B).

In 1953, Martin Kneser
[
15
]
proved the deep and beautiful theorem that
the only counterexamples to superadditivity for lower asymptotic density of sumsets
are pairs of sets
A
and
B
that are essentially unions of congruence classes modulo
m

for some positive integer
m
. The long and difficult proof is a
tour de force
based
on the masterful application of iterated Dyson transforms.
For proofs of Kneser’s theorem, see Kneser
[
15
]
and also
Halberstam-Roth
[
9
]
, Mann
[
23
]
, and Nathanson
[
28
]
.

7.
The
e
-transform in an abelian group

Analogous to his theorem on the lower asymptotic density
of sums of sets of integers, Kneser also proved a theorem
on sums of finite subsets of an additive abelian group.
The proof uses a simple form of the Dyson transform.
Let
G
be an abelian group, written additively, with identity element
0
.
For
X\subseteq G
and
e\in G
, we define the
translates

X+e=\{x+e:x\in X\}

and

X-e=\{x-e:x\in X\}.

Let
(A,B)
be a pair of nonempty subsets of
G
.
For all
e\in A
, let

A(e)=A\cup(B+e)

and

B(e)=B\cap(A-e)

The pair of sets
(A(e),B(e))
is the
e
-transform

of the pair
(A,B)
.
Note that

A\subseteq A(e)

and

B(e)\subseteq B.

Theorem 7
.

Let
A
and
B
be nonempty subsets of the abelian group
G
and let
e\in G
. Then

(29)

A(e)+B(e)\subseteq A+B

and

(30)

A(e)\setminus A=e+\left(B\setminus B(e)\right).

If
A
and
B
are finite sets, then

(31)

|A|+|B|=|A(e)|+|B(e)|.

If
e\in A
and
0\in B
, then
e\in A(e)
and
0\in B(e)
.

Proof.

Let
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\in A(e)+B(e)
, where
a^{\prime}\in A(e)
and
b^{\prime}\in B(e)
.
Then
a^{\prime}\in A
or
a^{\prime}\in B+e
.
If
a^{\prime}\in A
, then
b^{\prime}\in B(e)\subseteq B
implies
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\in A+B
.
If
a^{\prime}\in B+e
, then
a^{\prime}=b^{\prime\prime}+e
for some
b^{\prime\prime}\in B
.
We have
b^{\prime}\in B(e)
and so
b^{\prime}=a^{\prime\prime}-e
for some
a^{\prime\prime}\in A
.
It follows that

a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}=(b^{\prime\prime}+e)+(a^{\prime\prime}-e)=a^{\prime\prime}+b^{\prime\prime}\in A+B.

This proves (
29
).

We have

b\in B\setminus B(e)

if and only if

b\in B\qqand b\notin A-e

if and only if

b+e\in B+e\qqand b+e\notin A

if and only if

b+e\in A(e)\setminus A.

This proves (
30
).

If
A
and
B
are finite sets, then (
30
) implies

\left|A(e)\right|-\left|A\right|=\left|A(e)\setminus A\right|=\left|B\setminus B(e)\right|=\left|B\right|-\left|B(e)\right|.

This proves (
31
).

If
e\in A
and
0\in B
, then
e\in A(e)
and
0\in B\cap(A-e)=B(e)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

As an example of the use the
e
-transform in additive number theory,
we prove I. Chowla’s generalization of the Cauchy-Davenport theorem.
We begin with a simple counting argument.

Lemma 7
.

Let
G
be a finite group, written multiplicatively and not necessarily abelian.
Let
A
and
B
be subsets of
G
and let

AB=\{ab:a\in A\text{ and }b\in B\}.

If

|A|+|B|>|G|

then

AB=G.

Proof.

Let
x\in G
and let

xB^{-1}=\{xb^{-1}:b\in B\}.

Then

\left|xB^{-1}\right|=|B|

and

|A|+\left|xB^{-1}\right|=|A|+|B|>|G|.

By the pigeonhole principle, the sets
A
and
xB^{-1}
are not disjoint,
and so there exist
a\in A
and
b\in B
such that
a=xb^{-1}
.
Thus,
x=ab\in AB
and
AB=G
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 8

(I. Chowla)
.

Let
m\geq 2
and let
A
and
B
be nonempty subsets of
the additive abelian group
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
of congruence classes modulo
m
.
If
0\in B
and
\gcd(b,m)=1
for all
b\in B\setminus\{0\}
, then

(32)

|A+B|\geq\min(m,|A|+|B|-1).

Proof.

By Lemma
7
, if
|A|+|B|>m
, then
A+B=\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}

and
|A+B|=m
. Thus, we can assume that

|A|+|B|\leq m.

Inequality (
32
) also holds if
|A|=1
or
|B|=1
, and so we can assume
that

|A|\geq 2\qqand|B|\geq 2.

If the theorem is false, then there is a counterexample, that is, a pair of sets
(A,B)
such that

|A|\geq 2
and
|B|\geq 2
and

|A+B|<|A|+|B|-1.

Choose the counterexample
(A,B)
so that the cardinality of
B
is minimal.
Because
|B|\geq 2
, there exists
b^{*}\in B
with
b^{*}\neq 0
and
\gcd(b^{*},m)=1
.

If
a+b^{*}\in A
for all
a\in A
, then
a+jb^{*}\in A
for all
j=0,1,2,3,\ldots
.
Because the set
A
is finite, there exist integers
j_{1}<j_{2}
with
j_{0}=j_{2}-j_{1}

minimal such that

a+j_{1}b^{*}=a+j_{2}b^{*}
. It follows that

j_{0}b^{*}=\left(a+j_{1}b^{*}\right)-\left(a+j_{2}b^{*}\right)=0.

The divisibility condition
\gcd(b^{*},m)=1
implies
j_{0}=m
and so
A=G
,
which is absurd. Therefore, there exists
e\in A
with
e+b^{*}\notin A
.

We apply this element
e
to the pair
(A,B)

to obtain the
e
-transform
(A(e),B(e))
.
By Theorem
7
,

\displaystyle|A(e)+B(e)|

\displaystyle\leq|A+B|

\displaystyle<|A|+|B|-1

\displaystyle=|A(e)|+|B(e)|-1

and also
0\in B(e)
.
Moreover,
B(e)\subseteq B
implies
\gcd(b,m)=1
for all
b\in B(e)\setminus\{0\}
.
Thus, the pair
(A(e),B(e))
is also a counterexample.

Because
e+b^{*}\notin A
, we have
b^{*}\notin A-e
and so
b^{*}\notin B(e)
.
Thus,
B(e)
is a proper subset of
B
, which contradicts the minimality of
|B|
.
This completes the proof.
∎

From I. Chowla’s theorem we immediately obtain the Cauchy-Davenport theorem.

Theorem 9

(Cauchy-Davenport)
.

Let
p
be a prime number and let
A
and
B
be nonempty subsets of
\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}
.
Then

(33)

|A+B|\geq\min(p,|A|+|B|-1).

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be nonempty subsets of
\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}
. Let
b\in B
and let
B^{\prime}=B-b
.
Then
|B^{\prime}|=|B|
and
|A+B^{\prime}|=|A+B-b|=|A+B|
. Also,
0\in B^{\prime}
.

Because
p
is prime, we have
\gcd(b^{\prime},p)=1
for all
b^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
with
b^{\prime}\neq 0
.
Applying Theorem
8
with
m=p
to the sets
A
and
B^{\prime}
,
we obtain

|A+B|=|A+B^{\prime}|\geq\min(p,|A|+|B^{\prime}|-1)=\min(p,|A|+|B|-1).

This completes the proof.
∎

Martin Kneser used the
e
-transform to prove the following important group theoretic inequality.

Theorem 10

(Kneser)
.

Let
G
be an abelian group, written additively, and let
A
and
B

be nonempty finite subsets of
G
.
If
|A|+|B|\leq|G|
, then there exists a proper subgroup
H
of
G
such that

|A+B|\geq|A|+|B|-|H|.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
|B|
. If
|B|=1
, then

|A+B|=|A|\geq|A|+1-|H|

for every subgroup
H
of
G
.

Let
k\geq 2
and assume that Kneser’s inequality is valid for all pairs
(A,B)
of
nonempty finite subsets of
G
with
|B|<k
.

Let
(A,B)
be a pair of nonempty finite subsets of
G
with
|B|=k
.
There are two cases.

In the first case, we have

a+b-b^{\prime}\in A

for all
a\in A
and
b,b^{\prime}\in B
and so

A+b-b^{\prime}\subseteq A

for all
b,b^{\prime}\in B
. Because
A
is finite and
|A+b-b^{\prime}|=|A|
, it follows that

A+b-b^{\prime}=A

for all
b,b^{\prime}\in B
.
Let
H
be the subgroup of
G
generated by the difference set
B-B=\{b-b^{\prime}:b,b^{\prime}\in B\}
.
Note that
|B|\leq|B-B|\leq|H|
and that
A\neq G
because
|A|+|B|\leq|G|
.
We have

A+H=A\neq G

and so
H\neq G
. Thus,
H
is a proper subgroup of
G
such that

|A+B|\geq|A|\geq|A|+|B|-|H|.

In the second case, there exist
a\in A
and
b,b^{\prime}\in B
such that

a+b-b^{\prime}\not\in A.

Let

e=a-b^{\prime}.

We apply the
e
-transform to the pair
(A,B)
to obtain the pair
(A(e),B(e))
, where

A(e)=A\cup(B+e)

and

B(e)=B\cap(A-e).

We have
b+e\in B+e
and
b+e=a+b-b^{\prime}\notin A
and so
b\notin A-e
.
Thus,
|A(e)|>|A|
and
|B(e)|<|B|
.
Also,
b^{\prime}=a-(a-b^{\prime})=a-e\in A-e
and so
b^{\prime}\in B\cap(A-e)=B(e)\neq\emptyset
.
Therefore,
B(e)
is a nonempty finite subset of
G
with
|B(e)|<|B|
.
Applying the induction hypothesis to the pair
(A(e),B(e))
and applying also
Theorem
7
,
we obtain a proper subgroup
H
of
G
such that

|A+B|\geq|A(e)+B(e)|\geq|A(e)|+|B(e)|-|H|=|A|+|B|-|H|.

This completes the proof.
∎

If
p
is a prime number and
G=\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}

8.
Sums of measurable sets

A. M. Macbeath proved the first density addition theorem for sums of Lebesgue measurable sets.
Sierpinski
[
34
]
had previously shown that the sum of Lebesgue measurable sets
is not necessarily Lebesgue measurable. Introducing inner Lebesgue measure,
Macbeath obtained a continuous analogue of Mann’s theorem
for the sum of two measurable sets of positive real numbers. Let
\mu
denote Lebesgue
measure and let
\mu_{*}
denote inner Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 12

(Macbeath
[
21
]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be Lebesgue measurable sets of positive real numbers such that
\inf A=\inf B=0

and let
A+B=C
. Let
x
and
\gamma
be real numbers such that

\mu(A\cap[0,t])+\mu(B\cap[0,t])\geq\gamma t

for all
t\leq x
.
Let
\delta=\min(1,\gamma)
. Then

\mu_{*}(C\cap[0,x])\geq\delta x.

Extending this result to
r
-fold sums of
h
sets of integers, Nathanson obtained a continuous
analogue of Dyson’s theorem.

Theorem 13

(Nathanson
[
24
]
)
.

Let
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{h})
be an
h
-tuple of Lebesgue measurable sets of positive real numbers
such that
\inf A_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
For all
r\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, let

\Phi_{r}(t)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(r,h)}\mu_{*}(S_{I}\cap[0,t])

Let
x
and
\gamma
be real numbers such that

\Phi_{1}(t)=\mu\left(A_{1}\cap[0,t]\right)+\cdots+\mu\left(A_{h}\cap[0,t]\right)\geq\gamma t

for all
t\leq x
. Let
\delta=\min(1,\gamma)
. Then

\Phi_{r}(x)\geq\binom{h-1}{r-1}\delta x.

abelian groups. Less understood is the density and size of sums of sets of
nonnegative lattice points in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
