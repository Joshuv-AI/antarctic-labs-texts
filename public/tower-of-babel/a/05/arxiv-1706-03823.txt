Inverse Boundary Value Problem of Determining Up to a Second Order Tensor Appear in the Lower Order Perturbation of a Polyharmonic Operator
Sombuddha Bhattacharyya ∗ and Tuhin Ghosh ∗∗

Abstract
. We consider the following perturbed polyharmonic operator ℒ ⁡ ( x , D ) \mathcal{L}(x,D) of order 2 ​ m 2m defined in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ n , n ≥ 3 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 3 with smooth boundary, as ℒ ⁡ ( x , D ) ≡ ( − Δ ) m + ∑ j , k = 1 n A j ​ k ​ D j ​ D k + ∑ j = 1 n B j ​ D j + q ⁡ ( x ) , \mathcal{L}(x,D)\equiv(-\Delta)^{m}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}A_{jk}D_{j}D_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{j}D_{j}+q(x), where A A is a symmetric 2 2 -tensor field, B B and q q are vector field and scalar potential respectively. We show that the coefficients A = [ A j ​ k ] A=[A_{jk}] , B = ( B j ) B=(B_{j}) and q q can be recovered from the associated Dirichlet-to-Neumann data on the boundary. Note that, this result shows an example of determining higher order ( 2 2 nd order) symmetric tensor field in the class of inverse boundary value problem.

2.1.
Interior Carleman estimates

We start by recalling the definition of a limiting Carleman weight for the semiclassical Laplacian

Let

Let

Consider the conjugated operator

Definition 2.1

(
[
18
]
)
.

We say that
\varphi
is a limiting Carleman weight for

in

if
\nabla\varphi\neq 0

\widetilde{\Omega}
and the Poisson
bracket of

\Big\{\mathrm{Re}(p_{0,\varphi}),\mathrm{Im}(p_{0,\varphi})\Big\}(x,\xi)=0\quad\mbox{ whenever }p_{0,\varphi}(x,\xi)=0\mbox{ for }(x,\xi)\in(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}).

An example of such

, where

with
|\alpha|=1

Throughout this article we consider the limiting Carleman weight to be

As the principal symbol of the semiclassical conjugated

One always has the Poisson bracket of

is zero when

In order to get the Carleman estimate for the polyharmonic operator we iterate the Carleman estimate for the semiclassical Laplacian.

We use the semiclassical Sobolev spaces

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\lVert{\langle hD\rangle}^{s}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

With these notations we now prove the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let for each
\alpha
with
\lvert\alpha\rvert=1,2
,
A_{\alpha}\in W^{1+\lvert\alpha\rvert,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n^{|\alpha|}})
,
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})

and

be a limiting Carleman weight for the semiclassical Laplacian on
\widetilde{\Omega}
. Then for
0<h\ll 1
and
-2m\leq s\leq 0
, we have

h^{m}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq C\lVert h^{2m}e^{\frac{\varphi}{h}}\mathcal{L}(x,D)e^{-\frac{\varphi}{h}}u\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}},\mbox{ for all }u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.2)

the constant
C=C_{s,\Omega,A_{\alpha},q}
is independent of
h
.

Proof.

Let us consider the convexified Carleman weight as in
[
18
]
defined as

\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\varphi+\frac{h}{2\varepsilon}\varphi^{2}\mbox{ on }\widetilde{\Omega}.

We begin with the Carleman estimate for the semiclassical Laplacian with a gain of two derivatives proved in
[
18
]
:

\frac{h}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq C\left\lVert e^{\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}(-h^{2}\Delta)e^{-\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}},\mbox{ for all }u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.3)

Let
-2m\leq s\leq 0
, then iterating the above estimate (
2.3
)
m
times, we get the following estimate:

\left(\frac{h}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)^{m}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq C\left\lVert e^{\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}(-h^{2}\Delta)^{m}e^{-\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}},\mbox{ for all }u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

Let us consider
s\in[-2m,0]
and we get

\left(\frac{h}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)^{m}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq C\left\lVert e^{\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}(-h^{2}\Delta)^{m}e^{-\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}},\mbox{ for all }u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.4)

Next to get the required Carleman estimate for
\mathcal{L}(x,D)
, we add the lower order perturbations (given in (
2.1
)) to the above estimate. Similar literature on convexified Carleman estimates can be found in
[
11
]
in the context of a magnetic Schrödinger operator.

We first add the zeroth order term
(h^{2m}q)
, where
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
in the above estimate. We get

\lVert h^{2m}qu\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq h^{2m}\lVert q\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq h^{2m}\lVert q\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}.

Next we consider the first order term
h^{2m}A_{\alpha}D^{\alpha}
, with
\lvert\alpha\rvert=1
(here
A_{\alpha}\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}^{n})
).
We observe

h^{2m-1}e^{\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}\sum\limits_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=1}A_{\alpha}(hD)^{\alpha}e^{-\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{h}}u=h^{2m-1}\sum\limits_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=1}A_{\alpha}(-D^{\alpha}\varphi_{\varepsilon}+hD^{\alpha})u.

(2.5)

The first term in the right hand side of the above expression can be estimated as

\lVert(A_{\alpha}D^{\alpha}\varphi_{\epsilon})u\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq\lVert A_{\alpha}D^{\alpha}\varphi_{\epsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}

Now as
\varphi_{\epsilon}=\varphi+\frac{h}{\epsilon}\varphi^{2}
and
0<h\ll\epsilon\ll 1
, that is,
0<\frac{h}{\epsilon}<1

\lVert D^{\alpha}\varphi_{\epsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
for any
\alpha

\lVert(A_{\alpha}D^{\alpha}\varphi_{\epsilon})u\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}.

For the second term in the right hand side of (
2.5
) we observe that

\displaystyle\lVert A_{\alpha}(hD)^{\alpha}u\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert hD^{\alpha}(A_{\alpha}u)\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}}+h\lVert(D^{\alpha}A_{\alpha})u\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\lVert A_{\alpha}u\rVert_{H^{s+1}_{\mathrm{scl}}}+\mathcal{O}(h)\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}.

The last inequality follows from the fact that the operator given as multiplication by
A_{\alpha}
is continuous from

to

where

,

.
Therefore for

\lVert h^{2m-1}e^{\frac{\varphi_{\epsilon}}{h}}(A_{\alpha}hD^{\alpha})e^{-\frac{\varphi_{\epsilon}}{h}}u\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{scl}}}\leq\mathcal{O}(h^{2m-1})\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s+2m}_{\mathrm{scl}}}.

(2.6)

Now consider the term
h^{2m}A_{\alpha}D^{\alpha}e^{\varphi_{\epsilon}/h}u
, for

\displaystyle+\sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=2}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})D^{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{a_{0}}+h\widetilde{a_{1}}+\widetilde{r}\right)\overline{\left({a}_{0}+ha_{1}+{r}\right)}\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\sum_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=1}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})\frac{(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha}}{h}\left(\widetilde{a_{0}}+h\widetilde{a_{1}}+\widetilde{r}\right)\overline{\left({a}_{0}+ha_{1}+{r}\right)}\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\sum_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=1}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})D^{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{a_{0}}+h\widetilde{a_{1}}+\widetilde{r}\right)\overline{\left({a}_{0}+ha_{1}+{r}\right)}\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(q-\widetilde{q})\left(\widetilde{a_{0}}+h\widetilde{a_{1}}+\widetilde{r}\right)\overline{\left({a}_{0}+ha_{1}+{r}\right)}\ \mathrm{d}x.

Now we consider following two cases for the different forms of the second order perturbation.

Case(1):

Let us assume that
m>2
and
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})
for
|\alpha|=2
is not an isotropic matrix.
Then multiplying (
3.10
) by
h^{2}
and letting

we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\\
\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert=\lvert\alpha_{2}\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{1}}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{2}}\,\widetilde{a_{0}}\,\overline{{a}_{0}}\ \mathrm{d}x=0.

(3.11)

This follows from the fact that
A_{\alpha},{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}}\in L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
,
\widetilde{a_{0}},\,a_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\widetilde{\Omega}})
and
\widetilde{a_{1}},\,a_{1}\in W^{2,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.
Note that here we have crucially used the fact that
\|\widetilde{r}\|_{H^{2}_{\textrm{scl}}},\,\|r\|_{H^{2}_{\textrm{scl}}}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
to obtain
\|\widetilde{r}\|_{L^{2}},\,\|r\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
,
\|D^{\beta}\widetilde{r}\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(h)
, for
|\beta|=1
and
\|D^{\alpha}\widetilde{r}\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, for

.

Case(2):

Let us assume that
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})
for

is isotropic.
Observe that the first term in (
3.10
) is always zero.
Hence, multiplying (
3.10
) by
h

, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\\
\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert=\lvert\alpha_{2}\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{1}}D^{\alpha_{2}}\widetilde{a_{0}}\ \overline{{a}_{0}}\ \mathrm{d}x+\sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=1}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha}\widetilde{a_{0}}\,\overline{{a}_{0}}\ \mathrm{d}x=0,

(3.12)

It follows from the fact that
A_{\alpha},{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}}\in L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
,
\widetilde{a_{0}},\,a_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\widetilde{a_{1}},\,a_{1}\in W^{2,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.
Note that here we use
\|\widetilde{r}\|_{L^{2}},\,\|r\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
\|hD^{\beta}\widetilde{r}\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(h)
with
|\beta|=1
, and
\|h^{j}D^{\alpha}\widetilde{r}\|_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(h^{j})
with

,

, specially in the third term of (

).

3.2.
Determination of the coefficients

In this section we will establish the equality of
A_{\alpha}=\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}
for
|\alpha|=1,2
and
q=\widetilde{q}
in
\Omega
from the integral identities (
3.11
) and (
3.12
).

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us begin with the integral identity (
3.11
) in the
Case (1)
mentioned above.

In
[
25
]
, it is proved that a symmetric
2
-form
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})\in W^{3,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})
can be uniquely decomposed in an
1
-form
V\in W^{4,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{n})
with
V=0
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
and a symmetric
2
-form
F\in W^{3,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}})
so that

(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})=F+d_{s}V,\quad\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega}.

(3.13)

Here
F
is divergence free, i.e.

(\delta F)_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial F_{jk}}{\partial x_{k}}=0,\quad\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega},\quad j=1,2,..,n

and

d_{s}
is the symmetrized differentiation defined as

(d_{s}V)_{jk}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial V_{j}}{\partial x_{k}}+\frac{\partial V_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\right),\quad\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega}.

The above expression (
3.13
) can be realized in the following manner. Let us take any vector field
B\in W^{k,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
,
k\geq 1
, and consider the following Laplace equation
-\Delta\varphi=div\,B
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
. We can get a unique solution
\varphi\in W^{k+1,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
subject to suitable Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition.
For example, we get a unique
\varphi\in W^{3,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
by solving

(-\Delta)\varphi=div\,B\mbox{ in }\widetilde{\Omega},\quad\mbox{with }\varphi=0\mbox{ on }\partial\widetilde{\Omega}.

Then we can write
B=\widetilde{B}+\nabla\varphi
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
, where
\widetilde{B}=(B-\nabla\varphi)
with
div\,\widetilde{B}=0
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
, and
\varphi=0
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Applying the above technique for each row (or column) vector of the matrix
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})
and using symmetrization one can obtain the decomposition in (
3.13
).

Using the decomposition (
3.13
) in (
3.11
) we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\\
\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert=\lvert\alpha_{2}\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(F+d_{s}V)_{\alpha}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{1}}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{2}}\,\widetilde{a_{0}}\overline{a_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(3.14)

We choose
{a}_{0}=1,\widetilde{a_{0}}=e^{-ix\cdot\xi}
, where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is perpendicular to
\omega
and
\widetilde{\omega}
. Then
a_{0},\widetilde{a_{0}}
are indeed solutions of the transport equations (
3.8
)
and we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\\
\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert=\lvert\alpha_{2}\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(F+d_{s}V)_{\alpha}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{1}}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{2}}\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Since,
V|_{\partial\widetilde{\Omega}}=0
and
\xi\cdot\omega=\xi\cdot\widetilde{\omega}=0
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(d_{s}V)_{\alpha}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{1}}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{2}}\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=\left((\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})\cdot\xi\right)\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(V\cdot(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})\right)\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\\
\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert=\lvert\alpha_{2}\rvert=1\end{subarray}}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}F_{\alpha}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{1}}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha_{2}}\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(3.15)

Now extending
F
by
0
outside
\widetilde{\Omega}
and denoting the extended function by the same notation
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\widehat{F}_{jk}(\xi)(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{j}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{k}=0.

(3.16)

Let us now fix
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
Consider an orthonormal basis
\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
\mu_{n}=\frac{\xi}{\lvert\xi\rvert}
.
Choose
\omega=\mu_{p}
and
\widetilde{\omega}=\mu_{q}
for
p\neq q
and
1\leq p,q\leq(n-1)
.
Observe that the construction of the solutions
\widetilde{u}
and
v
allows us to make these choices of
\omega
and
\widetilde{\omega}
.
In fact, without loss of generality, the relation in (
3.16
) is true whenever
\xi,\omega,\widetilde{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\{\xi,\omega,\widetilde{\omega}\}
are mutually perpendicular and
\lvert\omega\rvert=\lvert\widetilde{\omega}\rvert=1
.

Now, equation (
3.16
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\widehat{F}_{jk}(\xi)\left((\mu_{p})_{j}((\mu_{1})_{k}-(\mu_{q})_{j}(\mu_{2})_{k})+2i((\mu_{p})_{j}(\mu_{q})_{k})\right)=0.

(3.17)

Choosing
-\mu_{q}
in place of
\mu_{q}
we get an another
orthonormal
basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and consequently we find

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\widehat{F}_{jk}(\xi)\left(((\mu_{p})_{j}(\mu_{p})_{k}-(\mu_{q})_{j}(\mu_{q})_{k})-2i((\mu_{p})_{j}(\mu_{q})_{k})\right)=0.

(3.18)

Adding and subtracting equations (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) for
1\leq p,q\leq n-1
,
p\neq q
we obtain

\langle\widehat{F}(\xi)\mu_{p},\mu_{p}\rangle=\langle\widehat{F}(\xi)\mu_{q},\mu_{q}\rangle\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\langle\widehat{F}(\xi)\mu_{p},\mu_{q}\rangle=0=\langle\widehat{F}(\xi)\mu_{q},\mu_{p}\rangle.

(3.19)

Since
F
is divergence free, we also have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{F}_{jk}(\xi)\xi_{k}=0\quad\mbox{for each }j=1,\ldots,n.

(3.20)

Using the symmetry of
\widehat{F}(\xi)
we obtain
\langle\widehat{F}(\xi){\mu_{p}},{\xi}\rangle=0
for any
p=1,\ldots,n-1
.
This leads to have the following representation of the element
\widehat{F}(\xi)\mu_{p}
with respect to orthonormal basis
\{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n-1},\frac{\xi}{\lvert\xi\rvert}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\widehat{F}(\xi){\mu_{p}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}d^{(p)}_{j}(\xi){\mu_{j}},\quad p=1,\ldots,n-1,

for some
d^{(p)}_{j}(\xi)\in\mathbb{C}
,
j=1,\ldots,n-1
.
Using the second relation in (
3.19
) we see
d^{(p)}_{j}(\xi)=0
whenever
j\neq p
. Hence,

\widehat{F}(\xi){\mu_{p}}=d^{(p)}_{p}(\xi){\mu_{p}},\quad p=1,\ldots,n-1.

Now, the first relation in (
3.19
) implies

\displaystyle d^{(p)}_{p}(\xi)=\langle\widehat{F}(\xi){\mu_{p}},{\mu_{p}}\rangle=\langle\widehat{F}(\xi){\mu_{q}},{\mu_{q}}\rangle=d^{(q)}_{q}(\xi),\quad\forall p,q=1,\ldots,(n-1).

Hence, we have
\widehat{F}(\xi){\mu_{p}}=d(\xi){\mu_{p}}
, where

d(\xi)=\langle\widehat{F}(\xi)\mu_{p},\mu_{p}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle F(x)\mu_{p},\mu_{p}\rangle e^{-ix.\xi}\,dx,\quad\mbox{for all }1\leq p\leq n-1.

(3.21)

Therefore,
\widehat{F}(\xi)
has eigenvalue
d(\xi)
of multiplicity
(n-1)
corresponding to the eigenvectors
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{n-1}
and eigenvalue
0
corresponding to the eigenvector
\xi
.
Let us now define the orthonormal matrix

P^{t}=\left(\mu_{1}\quad\mu_{2}\quad\dots\quad\mu_{n-1}\quad\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)

and observe that
\widehat{F}=P^{t}DP
, where
P^{t}
is the transpose of the matrix
P
and
D(\xi)
is the diagonal matrix given as
D(\xi)=diag\,(\underbrace{d(\xi),\ldots,d(\xi)}_{n-1},0)
.

This leads to provide a formal expression of
\widehat{F}(\xi)
for any
\xi\neq 0
as

\widehat{F_{jk}}(\xi)=\sum_{l,m=1}^{n}P_{lj}(\xi)D_{lm}(\xi)P_{mk}(\xi)=\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}d(\xi)P_{lj}P_{lk}=d(\xi)(\delta_{jk}-P_{nj}P_{nk})=d(\xi)\left(\delta_{jk}-\frac{\xi_{j}\xi_{k}}{\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}}\right).

(3.22)

In other words

\widehat{F}(\xi)=d(\xi)\left(I-\frac{\xi\otimes\xi}{|\xi|^{2}}\right),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

and
\widehat{F}(0)
can be taken as
\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}F(x)\,dx
.

Consequently, in the physical space
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

F_{jk}(x)=d_{\#}(x)\delta_{jk}+R_{j}R_{k}(d_{\#}(x)),

(3.23)

where
d_{\#}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\widehat{d_{\#}}(\xi)=d(\xi)
and
R_{j}
is the classical Riesz transformation defined as
\widehat{R_{j}f}(\xi)=\frac{1}{i}\frac{\xi_{j}}{\lvert\xi\rvert}\widehat{f}(\xi)
, for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Since
F_{jk}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
supported inside
\widetilde{\Omega}
, therefore
\mathrm{trace}[F(x)]=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
.
As
\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}^{2}=-I
, from (
3.23
) we get
\mathrm{trace}[F(x)]=(n-1)d_{\#}(x)
, therefore we derive
d_{\#}(x)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
.

Having
d_{\#}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with its compact support, let us consider
\widetilde{d}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solving uniquely

-\Delta\widetilde{d}=d_{\#}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then by using a standard property of the Riesz transform given as
R_{j}R_{k}(-\Delta)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}}
, from (
3.23
) we get

F_{jk}(x)=d_{\#}(x)\delta_{jk}+\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{d}}{\partial x_{k}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{d}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\right]\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.24)

The above expression shows that
F_{jk}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be written as a sum of two
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
functions.

As a next step, we show
\widetilde{d}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Since we got
d_{\#}(x)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Then from (
3.24
) we get
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}}\widetilde{d}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
for all
j,k=1,..,n
.
This implies
\widetilde{d}
is some linear function in
x
in the domain
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
. However, as
\widetilde{d}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
i.e. having
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega})
-integrability of
\widetilde{d}
in the unbounded (in all direction) domain
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
that linear function in
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
could only be zero function, i.e.
\widetilde{d}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Therefore,
\widetilde{d}\in H^{2}_{0}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.

So our decomposition in (
3.13
) is now modified into

\left(A-\widetilde{A}\right)_{jk}=(F+d_{s}V)_{jk}=d_{\#}(x)\delta_{jk}+\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{d}}{\partial x_{k}}+V_{k}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{d}}{\partial x_{j}}+V_{j}\right)\right]\quad\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega}.

(3.25)

We denote
\widetilde{V}=\left(\nabla\widetilde{d}+V\right)\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
and observe that
\widetilde{V}|_{\partial\widetilde{\Omega}}=0
, since
\widetilde{d}\in H^{2}_{0}(\widetilde{\Omega})
and
V|_{\partial\widetilde{\Omega}}=0
.

Next we show that
\widetilde{V}=0
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
. For that we consider
(
3.14
) with the form of
(F+d_{s}V)
given in (
3.25
) and obtain

\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{k}}+\frac{\partial\widetilde{V}_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{j}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{k}\,\widetilde{a_{0}}\,\overline{{a_{0}}}\ \mathrm{d}x=0.

(3.26)

We consider
\widetilde{a_{0}}=e^{-ix\cdot\xi}
and
a_{0}=(\omega\cdot x)
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
, with
\omega\cdot\omega=1
and
\omega\cdot\widetilde{\omega}=\omega\cdot\xi=0=\widetilde{\omega}\cdot\xi
, which are indeed the solutions of the transport equations (
3.8
).
By integration by parts with using
\widetilde{V}=0
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, from (
3.26
) we obtain

\sum_{l=1}^{n}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\widetilde{V}_{l}(x)\,(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{l}\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(3.27)

Then following
[
26
,
20
]
, from (
3.27
) one can directly conclude that

\partial_{j}\widetilde{V}_{k}-\partial_{k}\widetilde{V}_{j}=0\quad\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega},\ \forall j,k=1,\ldots,n.

Since
\widetilde{\Omega}
is simply connected, thus

\widetilde{V}=\nabla p
for some scalar function
p\in H^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.

Since
\widetilde{V}=0
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, we infer
\nabla_{tan}\,p|_{\partial\widetilde{\Omega}}=\left(\nabla p-(\partial_{\nu}p)\nu\right)|_{\partial\Omega}=0
and consequently get that
p
is constant (
p|_{\partial\widetilde{\Omega}}=c\in\mathbb{C}
) on the boundary.
Here
\nabla_{tan}
denotes the tangential component of
\nabla
along the boundary
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Hence, by considering
(p-c)
in place of
p
, we assume that
p=0
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Therefore, from (
3.26
) we get
p=\partial_{\nu}p=0
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
and consequently we see

0=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{j}(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{k}\,\widetilde{a_{0}}\,\overline{{a_{0}}}\ \mathrm{d}x=\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}p(x)\left((\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}\left(\widetilde{a_{0}}\overline{{a_{0}}}\right)\ \mathrm{d}x.

(3.28)

Consider
\widetilde{a_{0}}=e^{-ix\cdot\xi}
and
a_{0}=(\omega\cdot x)^{2}
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
with
\omega\cdot\omega=1
and
\omega\cdot\widetilde{\omega}=\omega\cdot\xi=0
, which are indeed solutions of the transport equations (
3.8
) for
m>2
.
Therefore we get

\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}p(x)\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Hence,
p=0
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
and so,
\widetilde{V}=0
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
.

This shows that
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})
(see (
3.25
)) is an isotropic matrix given as

(A-\widetilde{A})_{\alpha}=d_{\#}I\quad\mbox{in }\widetilde{\Omega}\mbox{ for }|\alpha|=2.

Hence we fall into the regime of
Case (2)
.

Next, we turn back to the
Case (2)
when
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})
is isotropic for
|\alpha|=2
. In that case we have the integral identity (

).
First we show that
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})=0
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
for
|\alpha|=1
and then we move into proving
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})=0
in

for
|\alpha|=2
.

Let us consider
\widetilde{a_{0}}=1
and

in
\widetilde{\Omega}
, which are indeed the solutions of the transport equations (
3.8
). Plugging in the functions

and

\sum_{\lvert\alpha\rvert=1}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})_{\alpha}\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\ \mathrm{d}x=0.

(3.29)

This implies
(A_{\alpha}-{\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}})=\nabla\widetilde{p}
,

, for some scalar function
\widetilde{p}\in W^{3,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
.
Moreover, as
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})=0

, hence we conclude that

is a constant (
\widetilde{p}|_{\partial\widetilde{\Omega}}=\widetilde{c}\in\mathbb{C})

\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\nabla\widetilde{p}\cdot(\omega+i\widetilde{\omega})\,\widetilde{a}\,\overline{a_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(3.30)

Now in (
3.30
) we consider
\widetilde{a_{0}}=1
and

in

, which are the solution of the transport equations (

Using integration by parts with the fact that

, we obtain

\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\widetilde{p}(x)\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

, for

Next in order to show the same for

and

.
Taking

\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}m(x)\,e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,

where

.
Thus

in
\widetilde{\Omega}
, consequently
(A_{\alpha}-\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})=0
for
|\alpha|=2
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
. So in the

we have shown
A_{\alpha}=\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=1,2
in
\Omega
.

Now we go back to
Case (1)
again and by the above analysis we get
A_{\alpha}=\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}
for
|\alpha|=2
in
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
Now we will show that for
Case (1)
,
A_{\alpha}=\widetilde{A}_{\alpha}
for
|\alpha|=1
in
