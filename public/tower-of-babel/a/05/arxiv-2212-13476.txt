On bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space Thanks: Corresponding author.
Igor A.R. Almeida

Abstract
In this paper, we study a problem related to geometry of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic geometry. We develop some of the basic theory of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space H ℚ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}} . In particular, we show that quaternionic bisectors enjoy various decompositions by totally geodesic submanifolds of H ℚ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}} . In contrast to complex hyperbolic geometry, where bisectors admit only two types of decomposition (described by Mostow and Goldman), we show that in the quaternionic case geometry of bisectors is more rich. The main purpose of the paper is to describe an infinite family of different decompositions of bisectors in H ℚ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}} by totally geodesic submanifolds of H ℚ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}} isometric to complex hyperbolic space H ℂ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}} which we call the fan decompositions. Also, we derive a formula for the orthogonal projection onto totally geodesic submanifolds in H ℚ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}} isometric to H ℂ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}} . Using this, we introduce a new class of hypersurfaces in H ℚ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}} , which we call complex hyperbolic packs in H ℚ n {\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}} . We hope that the complex hyperbolic packs will be useful for constructing fundamental polyhedra for discrete groups of isometries of quaternionic hyperbolic space.

Introduction

It is well known that the rank one symmetric spaces of non-compact
type are real, complex and quaternionic hyperbolic spaces, or a Cayley hyperbolic plane. In order to construct discrete groups of isometries in these geometries, one needs an appropriate
notion of polyhedra, which becomes non-trivial in the absence totally geodesic hypersurfaces from which to form a polyhedron’s faces
in spaces with non-constant sectional curvature. This happens in complex and quaternionic hyperbolic geometries, and in geometry of the Cayley hyperbolic plane. Since the faces of
Dirichlet fundamental polyhedra are in bisectors, it is natural to use bisectors as the building blocks for polyhedra in these geometries. This idea goes back to Giraud
[
10
]
and was developed further by Mostow
[
15
]
and Goldman
[
11
]
, (see also
[
3
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
8
]
for other examples of fundamental domains bounded by bisectors).

Therefore, it is necessary to understand the geometric structure of such hypersurfaces. In complex hyperbolic geometry, it was done by Mostow
[
15
]
and Goldman
[
11
]
. They showed that bisectors in complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
admit a decomposition into complex hyperplanes and into totally real totally geodesic submanifolds (meridian decomposition). Goldman also proved that these decompositions are unique.

In this paper, we will show that some basic results about bisectors from complex hyperbolic
geometry carry over to the quaternionic case. But, it will be shown that the geometry of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space is more rich.
First, we prove an analogue
of the Mostow decomposition of bisectors in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
and its generalization. Then we show that any bisector in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is a union of totally geodesic submanifolds of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
intersecting in a common point. In some sense, this is an analogue of the Goldman meridian decomposition. We call such decompositions the fan decompositions. We will show that such decompositions are not unique. The existence of fan decompositions implies, in particular, that any bisector in quaternionic hyperbolic space is star-like with respect to any point in its real spine. Also, we derive a formula for the orthogonal projection onto totally geodesic submanifolds in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. Using this, we introduce a new class of hypersurfaces in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, which we call complex hyperbolic packs in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. The construction of complex hyperbolic packs in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
in somewhat similar to the construction of packs introduced by Parker-Platis, see
[
16
]
, in complex hyperbolic geometry. We hope that the complex hyperbolic packs will be useful for constructing fundamental polyhedra for discrete groups of isometries of quaternionic hyperbolic space.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 1, we summarize some basic results about geometry of quaternionic hyperbolic space. In Section 2, we study geometry of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space. In Section 3, we derive a formula for the orthogonal projection onto totally geodesic submanifolds of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
and introduce a new class of hypersurfaces in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, which we call complex hyperbolic packs in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

1
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some basic results related to quarternions and geometry of projective and hyperbolic spaces.

1.1
Quaternions

First, we recall some basic facts about the quaternions we need. The quaternions
\mathbb{Q}
are the
\mathbb{R}
-algebra generated by the symbols
i,j,k
with the relations

l^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=-1,\ \ \ ij=-ji=k,\ \ \ jk=-kj=1,\ \ \ ki=-ik=j.

So,
\mathbb{Q}
is a skew field and a 4-dimensional division algebra over the reals.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Q}
. We write
a=a_{0}+a_{1}i+a_{2}j+a_{3}k
,
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
then by definition

\bar{a}=a_{0}-a_{1}i-a_{2}j-a_{3}k,\ \ \ {\rm Re}\ a=a_{0},\ \ \ {\rm Im}\ a=a_{1}i+a_{2}j+a_{3}k.

Note that, in contrast with the complex numbers,
{\rm Im}\ a
is not a real number (if
a_{i}\neq 0
for some
i=1,2,3
), and that conjugation obeys the rule

\overline{ab}=\bar{b}\bar{a}.

Also, we define
|a|=\sqrt{a\bar{a}}
. We have that if
a\neq 0
then
a^{-1}=\bar{a}/|a|^{2}.

In what follows, we will identify the reals numbers
\mathbb{R}
with
{\mathbb{R}}1
and the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
with the subfield of
\mathbb{Q}
generated over
\mathbb{R}
by
1
and
i
.

Two quaternions
a
and
b
are called
similar
if there exists
\lambda\neq 0
such that
a=\lambda b\lambda^{-1}.

By replacing
\lambda
by
\tau=\lambda/|\lambda|
, we may always assume
\lambda
to be unitary.

The following proposition was proved in
[
6
]
.

Proposition 1.1

Two quaternions
a
and
b
are similar if and only if
{\rm Re}\ a={\rm Re}\ b
and

|a|=|b|
. Moreover, every similarity class contains a complex number, unique up to conjugation.

Corollary 1.1

Any quaternion
a
is similar to a unique complex number
b=b_{0}+b_{1}i
such that
b_{1}\geq 0.

Also, this proposition implies that every quaternion is similar to its conjugate.

Example:

jzj^{-1}=\bar{z}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

We say that
a\in\mathbb{Q}
is
purely imaginary
if
{\rm Re}(a)=0
. Let us suppose that
a
is purely imaginary and
|a|=1
. Then
a^{2}=-1
. This implies that the real span of
1
and
a
is a subfield of
\mathbb{Q}
isomorphic
to the field of complex numbers. We denote this subfield by
\mathbb{C}(a)
. It is easy to prove that any subfield of
\mathbb{Q}
containing real numbers and isomorphic to the field of complex numbers is of the form
\mathbb{C}(a)

for some
a
purely imaginary with
|a|=1
.

The following was proved in
[
7
]
.

Proposition 1.2

Let
a
be as above. Then the centralizer of
a
is
\mathbb{C}(a)
.

More generally, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
, let
\mathbb{C}(\lambda)
also denote the real span of
1
and
\lambda
.

Proposition 1.3

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. Then the centralizer of
\lambda
is
\mathbb{C}(\lambda)
.

1.2
Hyperbolic spaces

In this section, we discuss two models for the hyperbolic spaces, its isometry group and totally geodesics submanifolds.

1.2.1
Projective Model

We denote by
\mathbb{F}
one of the real division algebras
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, or
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let us write
\mathbb{F}^{n+1}
for a right
\mathbb{F}
- vector space of dimension
n+1
.
The
\mathbb{F}
- projective space
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{F}^{n}
is the manifold of right
\mathbb{F}
-lines in

\mathbb{F}^{n+1}
. Let
\pi
denote a natural projection
from
\mathbb{F}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
to the projective space
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{F}^{n}
.

Let
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
denote a
(n+1)
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}
-vector space
equipped with a Hermitian form
\Psi=\langle-,-\rangle
of signature
(n,1)
.
Then there exists a (right) basis in
\mathbb{F}^{n+1}
such that the Hermitian
product is given by

\langle v,w\rangle=v^{*}J_{n+1}w,
where
v^{*}
is the Hermitian transpose of

v
and
J_{n+1}=(a_{ij})
is the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
-matrix with

a_{ij}=0
for all
i\neq j
,
a_{ii}=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,
and
a_{ii}=-1
when
i=n+1
.

That is,

\langle v,w\rangle=\bar{v}_{1}w_{1}+\ldots+\bar{v}_{n}w_{n}-\bar{v}_{n+1}w_{n+1},

where
v_{i}
and
w_{i}
are coordinates of
v
and
w
in this basis. We call such a basis in
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
an
orthogonal basis
defined by
a Hermitian form
\Psi=\langle-,-\rangle
.

Let
V_{-},V_{0},V_{+}
be the subsets of
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}\setminus\{0\}

consisting of vectors where
\langle v,v\rangle
is negative, zero, or
positive respectively. Vectors in
V_{0}
are called
null
or

isotropic
, vectors in
V_{-}
are called
negative
, an
vectors in
V_{+}
are called
positive
. Their projections to
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{F}^{n}

are called
isotropic
,
negative
, and
positive
points respectively.

The projective model of
hyperbolic space

{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is
the set of negative points in
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{F}^{n}
, that is,

{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}=\pi(V_{-}).

We will consider
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
equipped with the Bergman metric
[
7
]
:

d(p,q)=\cosh^{-1}\{|\Psi(v,w)|[\Psi(v,v)\Psi(w,w)]^{-1/2}\},

where
p,q\in{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
, and
\pi(v)=p,\pi(w)=q
.

The boundary
\partial{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}=\pi(V_{0})
of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is the sphere formed by
all isotropic points.

Let
{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
be the unitary group
corresponding to this Hermitian form
\Phi
. If
g\in{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
, then

g(V_{-})=V_{-}
and
g(v\lambda)=(g(v))\lambda
, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
. Therefore,
{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
acts on
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{F}^{n}
leaving
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}

invariant.

The group
{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
does not act effectively on
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
. The kernel of this
action is the center
{\rm Z}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
. Thus, the projective group
{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{F})={\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{F})/{\rm Z}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
acts
effectively. The center
\mathbb{Z}(n,1,\mathbb{F})
in
{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
is
\{\pm{\rm E}\}
if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Q}
, and is the circle group
\{\lambda{\rm E}:|\lambda|=1\}

if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. Here
{\rm E}
is the identity transformation of
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
.

It is well-known, see for instance
[
7
]
, that
{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{F})

acts transitively on
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
and doubly transitively on

\partial{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
.

We remark that

•

if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
then
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is a real hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
,

•

if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
then
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is a complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
,

•

if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
then
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is a quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

It is easy to show
[
7
]
that
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is isometric to
{\rm H^{4}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

1.2.2
The ball model

In this section, we consider the space
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
equipped by an orthogonal basis

e=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n},e_{n+1}\}.

For any
v\in\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
, we write
v=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},z_{n+1})
, where
z_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n+1
are coordinates
of
v
in this basis.

If
v=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},z_{n+1})\in V_{-}
, the condition
\langle v,v\rangle<0
implies that
z_{n+1}\neq 0.
Therefore, we may define a set of coordinates
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
by
w_{i}(\pi(z))=z_{i}z_{n+1}^{-1}.

In this way
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
becomes identified with the ball

\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{F})=\{w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{n}:\Sigma_{i=1}^{n}|w_{i}|^{2}<1\}.

With this identification the map
\pi:V_{-}\rightarrow{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
has the coordinate representation
\pi(z)=w
, where

w_{i}=z_{i}z_{n+1}^{-1}
.

Let
\mathbb{F}^{n}
denote a
n
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}
-vector space
equipped with the standard positive definite Hermitian form.
Then there exists a (right) basis in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that the Hermitian
product
{\langle\!\langle z,w\rangle\!\rangle}
of two vectors
z,w
in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
is given by

{\langle\!\langle z,w\rangle\!\rangle}=\overline{z}_{1}w_{1}+\ldots,+\overline{z}_{n}w_{n},

where
z_{i}
,
w_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
are coordinates of
z
and
w
in this basis.

Using this, we have that

\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{F})={w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{n}:{\langle\!\langle w,w\rangle\!\rangle}<1}.

1.2.3
Totally geodesic submanifolds

We will need the following result, see
[
7
]
, which describes all totally
geodesic submanifolds of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
.

Let
F
be a subfield of
\mathbb{F}
. An
F
-
unitary subspace
of
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
is an
F
-subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{n+1}

in which the Hermitian form
\Phi
is
F
-valued. An
F
-
hyperbolic subspace
of
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
is an
F
-unitary subspace in which
the Hermitian form
\Phi
is non-degenerate and indefinite.

Proposition 1.4

Let
M
be a totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
. Then either

(a)
M
is the intersection of the projectivization of an
F
-hyperbolic subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
with
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
for some
subfield
F
of
\mathbb{F}
, or

(b)
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
, and
M
is a 3-dimensional totally geodesic submanifold of a totally geodesic quaternionic line
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

From the last proposition follows that

•

in the real hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
any totally geodesic submanifold is isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}},\ k=1,\ldots,n,

•

in the complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
any totally geodesic submanifod is isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{C}}},
or to

{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}},

\ k=1,\ldots,n,

•

in the quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
any totally geodesic submanifold is isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}},

or to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{C}}},
or to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}},

k=1,\ldots,n,
or to a 3-dimensional totally geodesic submanifold of a totally geodesic quaternionic line
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

In what follows we will use the following terminology:

•

A totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is called a
quaternionic geodesic
.

•

A totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}}
is called a
complex geodesic
.

•

A totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}
is called a
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-plane.

•

A totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}
is called a
real plane
.

It is clear that two distinct points in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\cup\partial{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
span a unique quaternionic geodesic. We also remark that any
2
-dimensional totally geodesic submanifold of a totally geodesic
quaternionic line
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is isometric to
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}}
.

Proposition 1.5

Let
V
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
. Then each linear isometry of
V
into
\mathbb{F}^{n,1}
can be extended to an element of
{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{F})
.

This is a particular case of the Witt theorem, see
[
17
]
.

Corollary 1.2

Let
S\subset{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
be a totally geodesic submanifold. Then each linear isometry of
S
into
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
can be extended to an
element of the isometry group of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
.

An interesting class of totally geodesic submanifolds of the quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
are submanifods which we call
totally geodesic submanifolds of complex type, or simply, submanifolds of complex type. Their construction is the following.
Let
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}(a)\subset\ \mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
be the subset of vectors in
\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
with coordinates in
\mathbb{C}(a)
, where
a
is a purely imaginary quaternion,

|a|=1
. Then
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}(a)
is a vector space over the field
\mathbb{C}(a)
. The projectivization of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}(a)
, denoted by
{\rm M}^{n}(\mathbb{C}(a))
, is a
projective submanifold of
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
of real dimension
2n
. We call this submanifold
{\rm M}^{n}(\mathbb{C}(a))
a
projective submanifold of complex type of maximal dimension
.
It is clear that the space
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}(a)
is indefinite. The intersection

{\rm M}^{n}(\mathbb{C}(a))
with
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, called a
totally geodesic submanifold of complex type of maximal dimension
. It was proven in
[
7
]
that all these submanifolds are isometric, and, moreover, they are globally equivalent
with respect to the isometry group of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, that is, for any two such submanifolds
{\rm M}
and
{\rm N}
there exists an element

g\in{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{Q})
such that
{\rm M=g(N)}
. In particular, all of them are globally equivalent with respect to
{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{Q})
to the

canonical totally geodesic complex submanifold

{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
defined by
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
. This corresponds to the canonical
subfield of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{C}(i)\subset\mathbb{Q}
in the above.

If
V^{k+1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n+1}(a)
is a subspace of complex dimension
k+1
, then its projectivization
W
is called a
projective submanifold of complex type of complex dimension k
. When
V^{k+1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, then its projectivization
W
is called a
canonical projective submanifold of complex type of complex dimension
k
. In this case, we will denote
W
as
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{C}^{k}
.

If
V^{k+1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n+1}(a)
is indefinite, then the intersection
of its projectivization with
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. We call this submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
a
totally geodesic submanifold of complex type of complex dimension
k
. When
V^{k+1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, we call this totally geodesic submanifold a
canonical totally geodesic submanifold of complex type of complex dimension
k
, or a
canonical complex hyperbolic submanifold of dimension
k
of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. In this case, we will denote this submanifold as
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{C}}}
.

Stabilizators of totally geodesic submanifolds

Let

denote the subgroup of

If

is a subfield of

{\mathcal{N}}^{+}(F,\mathbb{F})=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}^{+};\lambda F\lambda^{-1}=F\}
, where

The following propositions can be found in

]
, p.74.

1.2.5
A little more about the isometry group of the quaternionic hyperbolic space

Let us consider the complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. It has a natural complex structure related to its isometry group, and the isometry group of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

is generated by the holomorphic isometry group, which is the projective group
{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{C})
, and the anti-holomorphic isometry
\sigma
induced by complex
conjugation in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. This anti-holomorphic isometry corresponds to the unique non-trivial automorphism of the field of complex numbers. Below we consider a similar isometry
of quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

We recall that if
f:\mathbb{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{Q}
, then
f
is an inner automorphism of
\mathbb{Q}
, that is,

f(q)=aqa^{-1}
for some
a\in\mathbb{Q},a\neq 0
.

It follows from the fundamental theorem of projective geometry, see
[
4
]
, that each projective map
L:\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
is induced by a semilinear or linear map
\tilde{L}:\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
.

It is easy to see that if a projective map

L:\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}

is induced by a semilinear map

\tilde{L}:\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{n+1},\tilde{L}(v)=ava^{-1},v\in\mathbb{Q}^{n+1},a\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\mathbb{R},

then it is also induced by a linear map
v\mapsto av
.

Therefore, the projective group of

\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
is the projectivization of the linear group of
\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
.

This implies that if

L:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

is an isometry, then
L
is induced by a linear isometry

\tilde{L}:\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}.

This explains why the group of all isometries of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

is the projectivization of the linear group
{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{Q})
, that is,
{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{Q}).

Next we consider a curious map, which is an isometry of the quaternionic hyperbolic space, that has no analogue in geometries over
commutative fields. Let
\tilde{L}_{a}:v\mapsto av
,
v\in\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
,
a\in\mathbb{Q}
,
a
is not real. The projectivization of this linear map
defines a non-trivial map
L_{a}:\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. We remark that in projective spaces over commutative fields this map
L_{a}
is identity.
It easy to see that
\tilde{L}_{a}\in{\rm U}(n,1;\mathbb{Q})
if and only if
|a|=1
, so
L_{a}
is in
{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{Q})
if and only if
|a|=1
.

Proposition 1.8

Let
a\in\mathbb{Q}
be such that
|a|=1
. Then the fixed point set
S_{a}
of
L_{a}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of complex type of maximal dimension in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}.

This submanifold is globally equivalent to the canonical complex hyperbolic submanifold
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

Proof
: The proof follows from Proposition
1.3
.

It is also easy to see that if
a
is purely imaginary and
|a|=1
, then
L_{a}
is an involution. We call this isometry
L_{a}
a
geodesic reflection
with respect to
S_{a}
, or a geodesic reflection in
S_{a}
.

2
Bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space

In
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
, as it follows from Proposition
1.4
, totally geodesic (real) hypersurfaces exist only when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
.
Therefore, when
\mathbb{F}\neq\mathbb{R}
, a reasonable substitute are the bisectors which are close as possible to being totally geodesic.
The reader can find a comprehensive study of bisectors in complex hyperbolic space in the Goldman book
[
11
]
.

Giraud
[
10
]
and Mostow
[
15
]
described the structure of bisectors in complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
in terms of a foliation by
totally geodesic complex submanifolds of maximal dimension. Goldman
[
11
]
proved that bisectors enjoy another decomposition
into totally real, totally geodesic submanifolds, which is called the
meridian decomposition
. It is important that
these decompositions are unique.

In this section, we will describe various decompositions of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space.
We show that geometry of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space is more rich than in complex hyperbolic geometry. First, we prove an analogue
of the Mostow decomposition of bisectors in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Finally, we will show that any bisector in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is the union of totally geodesic submanifolds of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
intersecting in a common point. In some sense, this is an analogue of the Goldman meridian decomposition. We call such decompositions
the
fan decompositions
. The existence of fan decompositions implies, in particular,
that any bisector in quaternionic hyperbolic space is star-like with respect to any point in its real spine.

The proof of these results is based on the classification of reflective submanifolds in quaternionic hyperbolic space
[
14
]
.

2.1
Bisectors in hyperbolic spaces

Let
p_{1},p_{2}
be two distinct points in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
. The
bisector equidistant from
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
,
or the
bisector of
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
is defined as

B(p_{1},p_{2})=\{p\in{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}:d(p_{1},p)=d(p_{2},p)\}.

An equidistant hypersurface or
bisector
in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is a subset
B=B(p_{1},p_{2})
for some pair of points
p_{1},p_{2}
.

In this case, we will say that
B

is equidistant from
p_{1}
(or equidistant from
p_{2}
).

Next we consider the following two cases: (1)
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, and (2)
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
.

2.1.1
Bisectors in complex hyperbolic space

In this section, we recall some basic results on bisectors in complex hyperbolic space.

Let
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
be as above. Then there exists the unique complex geodesic
\Sigma\subset{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
spanned by
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. Following Mostow
[
15
]
,
we call
\Sigma
the
complex spine
with respect to the pair
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
. The
spine
of
B
(with respect to
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
)
equals

\sigma\{p_{1},p_{2}\}=B(p_{1},p_{2})\cap\Sigma=\{p\in\Sigma:d(p_{1},p)=d(p_{2},p)\}

that is, the orthogonal bisector of the geodesic segment joining
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in
\Sigma
.

The following result is due to Giraud
[
10
]
and Mostow
[
15
]
, see also
[
11
]
.

Theorem 2.1

Let
B,\Sigma
and
\sigma
be as above. Let
\Pi_{\Sigma}:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}\rightarrow\Sigma
be the orthogonal projection onto
\Sigma
. Then

B=\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\sigma)=\bigcup_{s\in\sigma}\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(s)

.

The complex hyperplanes
\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(s)
, for
s\in\sigma
, are called the
slices
of
B
(with respect to
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
), and the decomposition
of a bisector into slices is called the
slice decomposition
, or the
Mostow decomposition
.

Since orthogonal projection
\Pi_{\Sigma}:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}\rightarrow\Sigma
is a real analytic fibration, we obtain that a bisector is a real
analytic hypersurface in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}
.

It was shown by Goldman
[
11
]
that the slices, the spine and complex spine of a bisector
B
depend intrinsically on the hypersurface
B
, and not on the defining pair
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
.

Theorem 2.2

Suppose that
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
and
\{p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2}\}
are two pairs of distinct points in complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
such that
B=B(p_{1},p_{2})
and
B^{\prime}=B(p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2})

are equal. Then the slices (respectively spine, complex spine) of
B
with respect to
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
equal the slices (respectively spine, complex spine) of
B^{\prime}

with respect to
\{p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2}\}
.

Goldman’s proof of this theorem is based on the following: the complex hyperbolic space is a complex analytic manifold; a bisector is Levi-flat and its maximal holomorphic
submanifolds are its slices.

The endpoints of the spine of a bisector
B
are called the
vertices
of
B
. Since a geodesic
\sigma\in{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
is completely determined by the unordered
pair
\partial\sigma\subset\partial{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
consisting of its endpoints, bisectors are completely parametrized by unordered pairs of distinct points in
\partial{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
.
Therefore, there is a duality between bisectors and geodesics. We associate to every bisector a geodesic, its spine
\sigma
. Conversely, if
\sigma\subset{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
is
a geodesic, then there exists a unique bisector
B=B_{\sigma}
with the spine
\sigma
. Given a geodesic
\sigma
, there exists a unique complex geodesic
\Sigma\supset\sigma
.
Let
R_{\sigma}:\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma
be the unique reflection whose fixed-point set is
\sigma
. Take an arbitrary point
p_{1}\in\Sigma\smallsetminus\sigma

and let
p_{2}=R_{\sigma}(p_{1})
. Then

B=B(p_{1},p_{2})=\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\sigma)

is a bisector having
\sigma
as its spine. Therefore, we have

Theorem 2.3

There is a natural bijective correspondence between bisectors in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
and geodesics in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
.

Next, we recall so called the
meridianal decomposition
of bisectors in complex hyperbolic space invented by Goldman
[
11
]
.

The following result shows that bisectors decompose into totally real geodesic submanifolds as well as into complex totally geodesic submanifolds of complex dimension
n-1
.

Theorem 2.4

Let
\sigma\in{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
be a real geodesic. For each
k\geq 2
, the bisector
B
having spine
\sigma
is the union of all
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-planes containing
\sigma
.

Following Goldman, we refer to the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-planes containing the spine as the
meridians
of the bisector
B
.

We remark that in contrast to the Mostow decomposition, which is a non-singular foliation, the meridianal decomposition is a singular foliation having the geodesic
\sigma
as its singular set.

Goldman’s proof of Theorem
2.4
is based on a sufficiently complicate computation. As a corollary of the existence of the meridianal decomposition, we have the following.

Theorem 2.5

Bisectors in complex hyperbolic space are star-like with respect to any point in its real spine.

2.2
Bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space

In this section, we will study bisectors in
n
- dimensional quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

As we have seen in the previous section, the orthogonal projections play an
important role in the study of bisectors. The first problem we consider is when the orthogonal projections onto totally geodesic submanifolds have totally geodesic fibers.

2.2.1
Orthogonal projections and reflective submanifolds

It is elementary that in real hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
, the orthogonal projections onto totally geodesic submanifolds have totally geodesic fibers.
This is not true, in general, in hyperbolic spaces with non-constant sectional curvature.

For instance, if
M
is a totally geodesic submanifold of real dimension one, that is, a real geodesic,
in complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}}
, then the fibers of the orthogonal projection onto
M
are not totally geodesic, because in this case the fibers have real dimension
3
,
but we know that any proper totally geodesic submanifold in
{\rm H^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}}
has real dimension
1
or
2
.

Another example: let
M
be a totally real totally geodesic plane in
{\rm H^{3}_{\mathbb{C}}}
, that is,

M
is isometric to
{\rm H^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}
. Then, using again the classification of totally geodesic submanifolds in
{\rm H^{3}_{\mathbb{C}}}
, it is easy to prove that the fibers
of the orthogonal projection onto
M
are not totally geodesic.

One more example: let
M
be a totally geodesic submanifold in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{2}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
of complex type of real dimension
2
, that is,
M
is isometric
to
{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. Then it can be shown that the fibers of the orthogonal projection onto
M
are not totally geodesic.

We will show that this problem is related to so-called reflective submanifolds of hyperbolic spaces.

First, we recall the definition of reflective submanifolds.

Let
N
be a Riemannian manifold and
M
a submanifold of
N
. Then
M
is called
reflective
if the
geodesic reflection of
N
with respect to
M
is a globally well-defined isometry of
N
.

Since any reflective submanifold is a connected component of the fixed point set of an isometry, it is totally geodesic.

Also, we recall the definition of symmetric submanifolds.

A submanifold
M
of a Riemannian manifold
N
is called
symmetric
if for each point
p
in
M
there exists an isometry
I_{p}
of
N
such that

I_{p}(p)=p,\ \ \ I_{p}(M)=M,\ \ \ (I_{p})_{*}X=-X,\ \ \ (I_{p})_{*}Y=Y

for all
X\in T_{p}M,\ Y\in\nu_{p}M.

Here we denote by
T_{p}M
the tangent space of
M
at
p
, by
\nu_{p}M
the normal space of
M
at
p
, and by
(I_{p})_{*}
the differential of
I_{p}
.

For symmetric spaces there is the following useful criterion.

Proposition 2.1

A totally geodesic submanifold of a simply connected Riemannian symmetric space is symmetric if and only if it is reflective.

As a reflective submanifold is symmetric, at each point there exists a complementary totally geodesic submanifold normal to it. In symmetric spaces this normal submanifold
is also reflective. Since all hyperbolic spaces are symmetric, we have that this result holds for any hyperbolic space.

Let
M
be a reflective submanifold in a symmetric simply connected Riemannian space
N
, and
p\in M
. Let
M_{p}^{\bot}
denote its
orthogonal complement
at
p
, that is, the connected,
complete, totally geodesic submanifold of
N
with
TM_{p}^{\bot}=\nu_{p}M
.

The following theorems describe all reflective submanifolds in complex and quaternionic hyperbolic spaces, see
[
14
]
.

Theorem 2.6

Let
M
be a reflective submanifold in complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. Then
M
is either isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{C}}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, or to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Theorem 2.7

Let
M
be a reflective submanifold in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. Then
M
is either isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, or to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
.

Corollary 2.1

Let
M
be a reflective submanifold in complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
, and
p\in M
. Then
M_{p}^{\bot}
is reflective, and it is isometric to
{\rm H^{n-k}_{\mathbb{C}}}
if

M
is isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{C}}},
and
M_{p}^{\bot}
is isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
if
M
is isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Corollary 2.2

Let
M
be a reflective submanifold in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. Then
M_{p}^{\bot}
is reflective, and it is isometric to
{\rm H^{n-k}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
if

M
is isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, and
M_{p}^{\bot}
is isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
if
M
is isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
.

All this implies the following results.

Theorem 2.8

Let
M
be a totally geodesic submanifold in complex hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. Then the fibers of the orthogonal projection onto
M
are
totally geodesic if and only if
M
is either isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{C}}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, or to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Theorem 2.9

Let
M
be a totally geodesic submanifold in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. Then the fibers of the orthogonal projection onto
M
are
totally geodesic if and only if
M
is either isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, or to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
.

2.2.2
Mostow decomposition of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space

In this section, we describe the Mostow decomposition of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

Let
p_{1},p_{2}
be two distinct points in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, and let
B(p_{1},p_{2})
be the bisector of
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
, that is,

B(p_{1},p_{2})=\{p\in{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}:d(p_{1},p)=d(p_{2},p)\}.

Then it follows from projective geometry that there exists the unique quaternionic geodesic
\Sigma\subset{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
spanned by
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
.
We call
\Sigma
the
quaternionic spine
with respect to the pair
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
. The
real spine
of
B
(with respect to
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
)
equals

\sigma\{p_{1},p_{2}\}=B(p_{1},p_{2})\cap\Sigma=\{p\in\Sigma:d(p_{1},p)=d(p_{2},p)\}

that is, the orthogonal bisector of the geodesic segment joining
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
in
\Sigma
.
Since
\Sigma
is isometric to
{\rm H^{4}_{\mathbb{R}}}
, it follows that
\sigma
is isometric to
{\rm H^{3}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Let
\Pi_{\Sigma}:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow\Sigma
be orthogonal projection onto
\Sigma
. It follows from Theorem
2.9
that the fibers of
\Pi
are totally
geodesic submanifolds of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n-1}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

First, we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2

For any
p\in{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\setminus\Sigma
, and
q\in\Sigma
, the geodesics from
\Pi_{\Sigma}(p)
to
p
and
q
are orthogonal and span a
totally real totally geodesic
2
-plane.

Proof
:
To prove this, we will use the ball model for
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, see Section
1.2.2
. In what follows, we identify
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

with the unit ball
\mathbb{D}
in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
,

\mathbb{D}=\{(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}:|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}+\ldots+|z_{n}|^{2}<1\}.

We write
(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})=(z_{1},z^{\prime})
, where
z^{\prime}=(z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n-1}.
If
z_{2}=z_{3}=\ldots=z_{n}=0
, we denote
z^{\prime}
by
0^{\prime}
.

Since the isometry group of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
acts transitively on the set of quaternionic geodesics in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, we can assume without loss of generality that

\Sigma=\{(z,0^{\prime})\in\mathbb{D}:z\in\mathbb{Q}\}
. Therefore, in this case, if
p=(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{D}
, we have that
\Pi_{\Sigma}(p)=(z,0^{\prime})
.

Let
p=(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\Sigma
,
p^{\prime}=\Pi_{\Sigma}(p)=(z,0^{\prime})
, and
q=(w,0^{\prime})\in\Sigma,w\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
P=(z,z^{\prime},1)^{t}
,
P^{\prime}=(z,0^{\prime},1)^{t}
,
Q=(w,0^{\prime},1)^{t}
be
vectors in
\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
representing the points
p,p^{\prime},q
respectively.

Now we compute the Hermitian triple product
\langle P,P^{\prime},Q\rangle
. We have

\langle P,P^{\prime},Q\rangle=\langle P,P^{\prime}\rangle\langle P^{\prime},Q\rangle\langle Q,P\rangle=\\
(\bar{z}z-1)(\bar{z}w-1)(\bar{w}z-1)=\\
(|z|^{2}-1)|\bar{z}w-1|^{2}.

So, the Hermitian triple product
\langle P,P^{\prime},Q\rangle
is real. Therefore, the points
p,p^{\prime},q
lie in a totally real geodesic plane in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

Also we need so-called the Pythagorean theorem in hyperbolic plane
{\rm H^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}}
, see
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.10

Let
a,b,c
be vertices of a right triangle with right angle at
a
. Then

\cosh d(b,c)=\cosh d(a,b)\cosh d(a,c).

Now we are ready to prove the Mostow decomposition theorem in quaternionic hyperbolic geometry.

Theorem 2.11

Let
B,\Sigma
and
\sigma
be as above. Let
\Pi_{\Sigma}:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow\Sigma
be orthogonal projection onto
\Sigma
. Then

B=\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\sigma)=\bigcup_{s\in\sigma}\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(s)

.

Proof
:
Let
p\in\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(\sigma)
.
First, we have that
d(\Pi_{\Sigma}(p),p_{1})=d(\Pi_{\Sigma},p_{2})
. By applying Proposition
2.2
, we have that
the points
p,\ \Pi_{\Sigma}(p),\ p_{1}
lie in a totally real geodesic plane in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. The same is true for the points
p,\ \Pi_{\Sigma}(p),\ p_{2}.

Then it follows from the Pythagorean theorem applied to the right triangles with vertices
p,\ \Pi_{\Sigma}(p),\ p_{1}
and
p,\ \Pi_{\Sigma}(p),\ p_{2}

that
d(p_{1},p)=d(p_{2},p)
, that is,
p\in B.

We call the quaternionic hyperplanes
\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(p)
, for
p\in\sigma
, the
slices
of the bisector
B
(with respect to
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
).
It is clear that any two distinct slices of
B
are ultra-parallel, that is, the distance between them is greater then zero.

Since orthogonal projection
\Pi_{\Sigma}:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow\Sigma
is a real analytic fibration, we have:

Corollary 2.3

A quaternionic bisector is a real analytic hypersurface in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{4n-1}
.

Now we show that the quaternionic spine
\Sigma
and the real spine
\sigma
of
B
depend intrinsically on
B
, and not on the pair
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
used to define
B
.
As was remarked above, Goldman’s proof of the complex hyperbolic analogue of this fact is based on the following: the complex hyperbolic space is a complex analytic manifold;
a bisector is Levi-flat and its maximal holomorphic submanifolds are its slices. Since quaternionic hyperbolic space has no natural complex structure, this proof does not
work in the quaternioic case. We prove this result using some elementary facts in quaternionic projective geometry.

Theorem 2.12

Let us suppose that
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
and
\{p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2}\}
be two pairs of distinct points in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

such that the bisectors
B=B(p_{1},p_{2})
and
B^{\prime}=B^{\prime}(p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2})
are equal. Then the slices (respectively, quaternioic spine, real spine) of
B

with respect to
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
equal the slices (respectively, quaternioic spine, real spine) of
B^{\prime}
with respect to
\{p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2}\}
.

Proof
: First we show that the slices of
B
coincide with the slices of
B^{\prime}
.

We need the following facts from projective geometry, see
[
4
]
: any two different projective submanifolds in quaternionic projective space
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
,
n>1
, of quaternionic dimension
n-1
are transversal along their intersection; every intersection of projective
submanifolds in
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
is a projective submanifold in
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. Also, we recall that any totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, is the intersection of a projective submanifold in
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
of dimension
k
with
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

Let
s^{\prime}
be a slice of
B^{\prime}
. Since
B=B^{\prime}
, then
s^{\prime}
is either a slice of
B
, or
s^{\prime}
intersects a slice
s
of
B
transversally.
Suppose that
s\cap s^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
s\neq s^{\prime}
. Since
s\subset B
and
s^{\prime}\subset B
, and
B
is a smooth submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

of real dimension
4n-1
, it follows from transversality of
s
and
s^{\prime}
along their intersection that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}s+\dim_{\mathbb{R}}s^{\prime}-(4n-1)=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(s\cap s^{\prime})
.
This implies that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(s\cap s^{\prime})=4n-7
. This is impossible, since
s\cap s^{\prime}
is the intersection of a projective submanifold in
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{Q}^{n}
with
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.
Therefore, we get that
s=s^{\prime}
. So, the slices of
B^{\prime}
are slices of
B
. Since each slice is orthogonal to
\Sigma
, any pair of distinct slices of bisector are
ultraparallel and their unique common orthogonal quaternionic geodesic is equal to
\Sigma
.

Therefore,
B
completely determines the quaternionic spine
\Sigma
. Since
\sigma=B\cap\Sigma
, the bisector
B
uniquely determines its real spine.

The following theorem is a generalization of the Mostow decomposition theorem.

Theorem 2.13

Let
\Sigma
be a totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
,
k=1
,
2,\ldots,n-1
. Let
\Pi_{\Sigma}:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\to\Sigma
be the orthogonal projection onto
\Sigma
. If
B^{\prime}\subset\Sigma
is a bisector in
\Sigma,

B^{\prime}=B^{\prime}(p_{1},p_{2}),

p_{1},p_{2}\in\Sigma
, then
B=\Pi_{\Sigma}^{-1}(B^{\prime})
is a bisector in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
such that
B^{\prime}=B\cap\Sigma
, and, moreover,
B=B(p_{1},p_{2})
. Conversely, if
B=B(p_{1},p_{2})
is a bisector in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
defined by
p_{1},p_{2}\in\Sigma
, then its orthogonal projection
B^{\prime}
onto
\Sigma
is a bisector in
\Sigma
, and
B^{\prime}=B^{\prime}(p_{1},p_{2}).

Proof
: The proof follows along the lines of the proof of Theorem 2.11 and the lemma below.

Lemma 2.1

Let
\Sigma
be a totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n-1
. Let
\Pi_{\Sigma}:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\to\Sigma
be the orthogonal projection onto
\Sigma
. If
p\in{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\setminus\Sigma
and
q\in\Sigma
, then the points
\Pi_{\Sigma}(p)
,
p
and
q
span a totally real geodesic 2-plane in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

Proof
:
Let us consider the ball model
\mathbb{D}
for
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. For
p\in\mathbb{D}
, we write
p=(z,z^{\prime})
, where
z\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
and
z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n-k}
. Applying an automorphism, we can assume that
\Sigma=\{(z,0^{\prime})\in\mathbb{D}:\,z\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}\}
. So,
\Pi_{\Sigma}(z,z^{\prime})=(z,0^{\prime})
.

Now, let
p=(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\Sigma
,
p^{\prime}=(z,0^{\prime})=\Pi_{\Sigma}(p)
and
q=(w,0^{\prime})\in\Sigma
. Let
P=(z,z^{\prime},1)^{t}
,
P^{\prime}=(z,0^{\prime},1)^{t}
, and
Q=(w,0^{\prime},1)^{t}
be vectors in
\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
representing the points
p
,
p^{\prime}
,
q
, respectively. We have that

\displaystyle\langle P,P^{\prime},Q\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle P,P^{\prime}\rangle\langle P^{\prime},Q\rangle\langle Q,P\rangle=

\displaystyle({\langle\!\langle z,z\rangle\!\rangle}-1)({\langle\!\langle z,w\rangle\!\rangle}-1)({\langle\!\langle w,z\rangle\!\rangle}-1)=

\displaystyle({\langle\!\langle z,z\rangle\!\rangle}-1)~|{\langle\!\langle z,w\rangle\!\rangle}-1|^{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore,
p,p^{\prime},q
lie in a totally real geodesic plane in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

2.2.3
Automorphism of bisectors

If

is a totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{3}_{\mathbb{R}}}
, then there exists the unique quaternionic geodesic

M
which contains

. Then the pair
\{M,N\}
defines a bisector
B
whose quaternionic spine equals to

and real spine equals to
N
.
Indeed, take a point
p
in
M\setminus N
. Let
\gamma
be the unique real geodesic in
M
which contains
p
and which is orthogonal to
N
. Let
p^{\prime}\in\gamma
be
the point symmetric to
p
. It is clear that
M
is the quaternionic spine of the bisector

, and
N
is the real spine of

.

Since the group of isometries

of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
acts transitively on such pairs

, we get that

acts transitively on bisectors.
Since the quaternionic geodesic

containing

is defined uniquely by its real spine. Furthermore, the stabilizer of a bisector
in

equals the stabilizer of its real spine. This group is described in Proposition
1.7
.

2.2.4
Orthogonality of totally geodesic submanifolds of complex type in quaternionic hyperbolic space

Suppose that

Let

and

The proof of the following theorem is standard.

Theorem 2.14

The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

and
I_{N}
commute,

2.

I_{M}(N)=N
,

3.

I_{N}(M)=M
,

4.

and

{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

We recall that
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
has a totally geodesic submanifold isometric to

Theorem 2.15

Let
M
be a totally geodesic submanifold of

Proof
:
To prove this, we will use the ball model for

, see Section
1.2.2
. In what follows, we identify
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

with the unit ball
\mathbb{D}
in

\mathbb{D}=\{(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}:|q_{1}|^{2}+|q_{2}|^{2}+\ldots+|q_{n}|^{2}<1\}.

Let us consider the following subsets of
\mathbb{D}

is the standard subfield of real numbers of
\mathbb{Q}
.

Then
\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{D}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
, and
\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{R}}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}

isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Since the group of isometries of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
acts transitively on the set of pairs of the form

in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
,
we can assume without loss of generality that

and

.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Q}
be purely imaginary,
|a|=1
. Then
a^{2}=-1
. Let
\mathbb{C}(a)
be the subfield of
\mathbb{Q}
spanned by
1
and
a
. Denote by
\mathbb{D}_{a}
the following set in
\mathbb{D}
:

\mathbb{D}_{a}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{C}(a)^{n}:|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}+\ldots+|a_{n}|^{2}<1\}.

We know, see Section
1.2.3
, that
\mathbb{D}_{a}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{D}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. We remark that
\mathbb{D}_{a}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}
if
a=i
.
It is clear that
\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}\cap\mathbb{D}_{a}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{R}}
, provided that
a\neq i
.

Let
V_{\mathbb{C}}
,
V_{a}
,
V_{\mathbb{R}}
denote the sets of vectors in
\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
with coordinates in
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{C}(a)
, and
\mathbb{R}
respectively. Then we have that

\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
\mathbb{D}_{a}
,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{R}}

,
V_{a}
, and

Consider the following semi-linear map

L_{a}(v)=ava^{-1}
,

.
Then it follows that
L_{a}(v)=v
for all

, see Section

. In particular,
L_{i}(v)=v
for all

L_{i}(v)=v
for all

We know that projectivization of this semi-linear map

is equal to projectivization of the linear map
L^{\prime}(v)=av.
We have that
L^{\prime}\circ L^{\prime}=-I
,
where

is the identity map of
\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
. This implies that projectivization of

is an isometric involution of
\mathbb{D}
whose fixed point set equals to

.

Since

, we have that
L^{\prime}_{i}\circ L^{\prime}_{k}=-L^{\prime}_{k}\circ L^{\prime}_{i}

L^{\prime}_{i}
and
L^{\prime}_{k}
are commuting involutions whose fixed points sets
are
\mathbb{D}_{i}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{D}_{k}
.

Let again

and

. We define

to be
\mathbb{D}_{k}
. Then

Also, let

be projectivization of

, and let

be projectivization of

Therefore, we get that

and

are geodesic reflections of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
with respect to

and

respectively such that

,

,
I_{M}
and

commute.

We remark that
I_{M}
acts on

as a geodesic reflection with respect to

, and
I_{N}
acts on
M
as a geodesic reflection with respect to

2.2.5
Fan decomposition of bisectors in quaternionic hyperbolic space

In this section, we show that any bisector
B
in quaternionic hyperbolic space
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is the union of totally geodesic submanifolds in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
, all passing through a point

in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
, that is,

B=\bigcup_{\alpha}{N_{\alpha}}(o),

where

is a totally geodesic submanifolds in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
passing through a point

in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

This decomposition in somewhat is similar to the Goldman meridional decomposition
of bisectors in complex hyperbolic space. We call such decomposition a

of a bisector in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}

We call the point

of a fan decomposition of

of a fan decomposition of

A fan decomposition of a bisector

is defined by its center
o
and its complex blades.

First, we define the center and the complex blades of a fan decomposition of a bisector.

Let
B
be a bisector in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
. Let
\Sigma
and

be its quaternionic and real spine respectively.

The center of a fan decomposition of

is any point

.

The complex blades of a fan decomposition of

passing through a point

are constructed as follows.

Let

be the unique real geodesic in

passing through the point

\sigma
. Let

,

be distinct points in
\gamma

symmetric with respect to

. Then it is clear that

. Let
M
be a totally geodesic submanifold of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
containing
\gamma
.
Since
M
is totally geodesic, we have that
B_{M}=M\cap B
is a bisector in
M
.
We denote by
\Sigma_{M}
the complex spine of
B_{M}
and by
\sigma_{M}
the real spine of
B_{M}
.
Then
\Sigma_{M}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\Sigma
isometric to

{\rm H^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}}
containing
\gamma
, and
\sigma_{M}
is a real geodesic in
\Sigma_{M}
passing through the point

orthogonal to
\gamma
. By applying
Goldman’s meridianal decomposition, we have that there exists a totally geodesic submanifold

isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{R}}}
containing
\sigma_{M}
,

S
is a meridian of

. Let us choose any such

and fix it. It follows from Theorem
2.15
that there exists a totally geodesic submanifold

and

is orthogonal to

along

. We define such a submanifold

to be a

of a fan decomposition of

.

It is seen that a complex blade of a fan decomposition of a bisector
B
centered at

is defined by

, by a totally geodesic submanifold
M
of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
containing
\gamma
,
and by a meridian of the bisector

.

We will need the following fact proved in
[

]

Proposition 2.3

Let

Let

isometric to

. Then

.

3
Orthogonal projection onto totally geodesic submanifolds of complex type of maximal dimension and complex hyperbolic packs

In this section, we obtain a formula for the orthogonal projection onto totally geodesic submanifolds of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. Using this formula we will show that the inverse image of a bisector with respect to this projection is not a bisector in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}.

3.1
Orthogonal projection onto totally geodesic submanifolds of complex type of maximal dimension

Lemma 3.1

Let
N
be a totally geodesic reflective submanifold of a hyperbolic space
M
. Let
\phi:M\to M
be the geodesic reflection of
M
with respect to
N
, and
\Pi:M\to N
be the orthogonal projection of
M
onto
N
. Then
\Pi(p)
is the midpoint of the geodesic segment defined by
p
and
\phi(p)
for all
p\in M\setminus N.

Proof
:
Given
p\in M\setminus N
, let
\xi\in T_{\Pi(p)}M
be a unitary vector normal to
N
at
\Pi(p)
, such that
\gamma(s)=\exp_{\Pi(p)}(s\xi)
,
s\in\mathbb{R},
is the unique geodesic in
M
passing through
p
and
\Pi(p)
. Then

\gamma(-s)=\exp_{\Pi(p)}(-s\xi)=\exp_{\phi(\Pi(p))}(d\phi_{\Pi(p)}(s\xi))=\phi(\exp_{\Pi(p)}(s\xi))=\phi(\gamma(s)),

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.1

It is easy to see that
\Pi(p)=m
is the unique fixed point of the composition

\phi
and
\psi
, where
\psi
is the geodesic reflection of
M
with respect to the reflective submanifold
N^{\bot}_{m}
orthogonal to
N
at
m
.

In what follows, we use the ball model and the notations in Section 2.2.4, see also Theorem 2.14. That is,

\mathbb{D}=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}:|x_{1}|^{2}+|x_{2}|^{2}+\ldots+|x_{n}|^{2}<1\},

\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}=\{z=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}+\ldots+|z_{n}|^{2}<1\}.

It is clear that for each
x\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
there exist unique vectors
u
,
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

x=u+vj.

Using this, we identify
\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathbb{D}_{u}
:

\mathbb{D}_{u}=\{u=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|u_{1}|^{2}+|u_{2}|^{2}+\ldots+|u_{n}|^{2}<1\}.

We define

\hat{x}:=u-vj.

Remark 3.2

It follows from Section 1.2.5 that the map
x\mapsto\hat{x}
,
x\in\mathbb{D},
coincides with the geodesic reflection
L_{i}
with respect to
\mathbb{D}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{D}_{u}.

Lemma 3.2

Let
\mathbb{R}_{-}
be the set of negative real numbers, and let
p\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{-}.
Then

\lambda^{2}=p
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
if and only if

\lambda=\pm\left(\sqrt{\frac{|p|+\mathrm{R}e\,p}{2}~}+\sqrt{\frac{|p|-\mathrm{R}e\,p}{2}~}\,\omega\right),

where
\omega=0
if
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{-}
or
\omega=\frac{\mathrm{I}m\,p}{|\mathrm{I}m\,p|}
otherwise.

Proof
:
The proof follows along the lines in
[
1
]
, Section 1.2. On the other hand, the reader can verify the result by calculating
\lambda^{2}
directly for
\lambda
given in the lemma.
It is understood that all square roots of positive numbers are
taken with the positive sign.

Let
M
be a non-isotropic vector in
\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
, that is,
\langle M,M\rangle\neq 0.
Since
\langle M,M\rangle
is real, the following map

I=I(M):Z\rightarrow-Z+2M\frac{\langle M,Z\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle},

where
Z\in\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
, is well-defined.

It is easy to see that
I:\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
is a right linear map, that is,

I(Z_{1}\lambda_{1}+Z_{2}\lambda_{2})=I(Z_{1})\lambda_{1}+I(Z_{2})\lambda_{2}

for all
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
and for all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}.

A direct computation shows that for all
X,Y\in\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
we have that
\langle I(X),I(Y)\rangle=\langle X,Y\rangle
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\langle I(X),I(Y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle-X+2M\dfrac{\langle M,X\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle},-Y+2M\dfrac{\langle M,Y\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle X,Y\rangle-2\langle X,M\rangle\dfrac{\langle M,Y\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}-2\overline{\dfrac{\langle M,X\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}}\langle M,Y\rangle+4\overline{\dfrac{\langle M,X\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}}\langle M,M\rangle\dfrac{\langle M,Y\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}

\displaystyle=\langle X,Y\rangle-2\langle X,M\rangle\dfrac{\langle M,Y\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}-2\dfrac{\langle X,M\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}\langle M,Y\rangle+4\dfrac{\langle X,M\rangle\langle M,Y\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}

\displaystyle=\langle X,Y\rangle.

Also, it is easy to show that the map
I(M)
is an involution.

Lemma 3.3

Let
I(X,Y)=I(M)=I
, where
M=X+Y
. Then
I(X)=Y
if and only if

•

\langle X,X\rangle=\langle Y,Y\rangle,

•

\langle X,Y\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof
:
Since
I^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
, we have that
I(X)=Y
if and only if
I(Y)=X
. Then

\displaystyle I(X)=Y

\displaystyle\iff Y=-X+2M\dfrac{\langle M,X\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}

\displaystyle\iff M=2M\dfrac{\langle M,X\rangle}{\langle M,M\rangle}

\displaystyle\iff\langle M,M\rangle=2\langle M,X\rangle

\displaystyle\iff\langle X,X\rangle+\langle X,Y\rangle+\langle Y,X\rangle+\langle Y,Y\rangle=2\langle X,X\rangle+2\langle Y,X\rangle

\displaystyle\iff\langle X,Y\rangle-\langle Y,X\rangle=\langle X,X\rangle-\langle Y,Y\rangle

\displaystyle\iff\langle X,Y\rangle-\overline{\langle X,Y\rangle}=\langle X,X\rangle-\langle Y,Y\rangle

\displaystyle\iff 2\,\mathrm{I}m\,\langle X,Y\rangle=\langle X,X\rangle-\langle Y,Y\rangle

\displaystyle\iff\langle X,Y\rangle\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\langle X,X\rangle=\langle Y,Y\rangle

Now let
M=X+Y
, where
X
and
Y
are negative vectors in
\mathbb{Q}^{n,1}
, such that
\langle X,Y\rangle<0.
Then
M
is negative. Indeed,

is negative if and only if
\langle M,M\rangle<0
. We have that

\langle M,M\rangle=\langle X,X\rangle+2\mathrm{R}e\,\langle X,Y\rangle+\langle Y,Y\rangle.

3.2
Complex hyperbolic packs in quaternionic hyperbolic space

Let
S
be a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
\Pi=\Pi_{S}:\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}\to S

be the orthogonal projection onto
S
. If
B
is a bisector in
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, then examples show that the intersection
B^{\prime}=S\cap B
tends to be complicate. There are examples when
B^{\prime}
is a bisector in
S
, and there are examples when
B^{\prime}
is not even a part of a bisector in
S
. Also, the relation between bisectors in
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}
and their orthogonal projections onto
S
is not clear. A good situation happens in the Mostow decomposition and its generalization given by Theorem 2.13 where
S
is isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{k}_{\mathbb{Q}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n-1
. Next we show that when
S
is isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}

the situation is bad: there exists a bisector
B\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
B^{\prime}
is a bisector in
S
but
B
is not equal to
\Pi^{-1}(B^{\prime})
.

Example
. In what follows, we use the ball model for
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}
and the notations in Section 3.1. For simplicity, we consider the case
n=2
.

Let
p_{1}=(0,-\delta)
and
p_{2}=(0,\delta)
, for some
0<\delta<1
. It is easy to see that the bisector
B=B(p_{1},p_{2})
in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
is equal to
\{x\in\mathbb{D}^{2}:\mathrm{R}e\,(x_{2})=0\}
. Take
S=\mathbb{D}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}=\{z\in\mathbb{D}^{2}:\,z\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\}
. Also we have that the bisector
B^{\prime}=B^{\prime}(p_{1},p_{2})
in
S
is equal to
\{z\in\mathbb{D}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathrm{R}e\,(z_{2})=0\}
. Then it follows that
B^{\prime}=B\cap S
.

Let
x=(r+rj,ri+rj)
, for some
0<r<1/2
. An easy computation shows that
x\in B
. If we write
x=u+vj
, then

u=(r,ri)\text{ and }v=(r,r).

Therefore,
{\langle\!\langle u,u\rangle\!\rangle}={\langle\!\langle v,v\rangle\!\rangle}=2r^{2}
and
{\langle\!\langle u,v\rangle\!\rangle}=r^{2}(1-i)
. Then we get that

{\langle\!\langle\hat{x},x\rangle\!\rangle}={\langle\!\langle u,u\rangle\!\rangle}-{\langle\!\langle v,v\rangle\!\rangle}+2{\langle\!\langle u,v\rangle\!\rangle}\,j=2r^{2}(1-i)\,j

and

\mathrm{R}e\,(1-{\langle\!\langle\hat{x},x\rangle\!\rangle})=1\text{ and }|1-{\langle\!\langle\hat{x},x\rangle\!\rangle}|=\sqrt{1+8r^{4}}.

This implies that

\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\alpha}=\frac{-2{\langle\!\langle v,u\rangle\!\rangle}}{|1-{\langle\!\langle\hat{x},x\rangle\!\rangle}|+\mathrm{R}e\,(1-{\langle\!\langle\hat{x},x\rangle\!\rangle})}=\frac{-2r^{2}(1+i)}{\sqrt{1+8r^{4}}+1}.

By applying Theorem 3.1, we get that
\Pi(x)=y=u+\bar{\beta}/\alpha\,v
. Then it follows that
y\not\in B^{\prime}
because

\mathrm{R}e\,(y_{2})=\mathrm{R}e\,(u_{2})+\mathrm{R}e\,(\bar{\beta}/\alpha\,v_{2})=\mathrm{R}e\,(ir)+\mathrm{R}e\,(\bar{\beta}/\alpha\,r)=

r\mathrm{R}e\,(\bar{\beta}/\alpha)=\frac{-2r^{3}}{\sqrt{1+8r^{4}}+1}\neq 0.

It follows from this example that the inverse image of
B^{\prime}=B^{\prime}(p_{1},p_{2})
under the orthogonal projection
\Pi
onto
S
is not a bisector in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. Since all bisectors in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}

are equivalent with respect to the action of the isometry group of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
, we get using simple geometric arguments that this fact is true for any bisector in
S
.

We remark that a similar situation happens in complex hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
when
S
is a totally geodesic submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
, a totally real geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
, see Parker-Platis
[
16
]
.

Let
S
be a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}
isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\Pi
be the orthogonal projection onto
S
. Let
B
be a bisector in
S
. Following Parker-Platis
[
16
]
, we call the inverse image
P=\Pi^{-1}(B)
a
complex hyperbolic pack
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}
defined by
B
.

Since orthogonal projection
\Pi:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow S
is a real analytic fibration, we have:

Corollary 3.3

A complex hyperbolic pack
P
in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
is a real analytic hypersurface in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{4n-1}
.

We call
S
a
complex spine
of
P
and a bisector
B\subset S
a
spine
of
P
.

Let
\Pi:{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow S
be orthogonal projection onto
S
. It follows from the construction of
P
that the fibers of
\Pi
are totally
geodesic submanifolds of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
, see Section 2.2.1. We call these fibers
slices
of the pack
P
. It is clear that any two slices of
P
are ultraparallel.

In
[
16
]
, the authors used the packs in complex hyperbolic geometry to construct fundamental domains (polyhedra) for complex hyperbolic quasi-Fuchsian groups. We hope that complex hyperbolic packs in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
introduced here will be useful for constructing fundamental polyhedra for discrete subgroups of isometries of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}.
As an example, we consider the following construction:

Example
. Let
G
be a discrete group in
{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{Q})
leaving invariant a totally geodesic submanifold
S
of
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
isometric to
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}}
. Let
F
be a fundamental polyhedron for the action of
G
in
S
bounded by bisectors in
S
(for instance,
F
is the Dirichlet polyhedron in
S
with the center at a point
o
in
S
).
Then, the polyhedron
\tilde{F}
in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
bounded by the complex hyperbolic packs defined by all the bisectors which form the boundary of
F
is a fundamental polyhedron for
G
in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.
The proof of this follows from simple geometric arguments using that the orthogonal projection onto
S
is equivariant with respect to the action of the stabilizer of
S
in

{\rm PU}(n,1;\mathbb{Q})
. It is easy to see that the combinatorial structure of
\tilde{F}
is more simple than the combinatorial structure of the Dirichlet polyhedron with the center at a point
o
in
S
for the action of
G
in
{\rm H^{n}_{\mathbb{Q}}}
.
