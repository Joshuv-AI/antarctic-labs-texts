Rowmotion on fences
Sergi Elizalde

Abstract
A fence is a poset with elements F = { x 1 , x 2 , … , x n } F=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\} and covers x 1 ⊲ x 2 ⊲ … ⊲ x a ⊳ x a + 1 ⊳ … ⊳ x b ⊲ x b + 1 ⊲ ⋯ x_{1}\lhd x_{2}\lhd\ldots\lhd x_{a}\rhd x_{a+1}\rhd\ldots\rhd x_{b}\lhd x_{b+1}\lhd\cdots where a , b , … a,b,\ldots are positive integers. We investigate rowmotion on antichains and ideals of F F . In particular, we show that orbits of antichains can be visualized using tilings. This permits us to prove various homomesy results for the number of elements of an antichain or ideal in an orbit. Rowmotion on fences also exhibits a new phenomenon, which we call homometry, where the value of a statistic is constant on orbits of the same size. Along the way, we prove a general homomesy result for all self-dual posets. We end with some conjectures and avenues for future research.

.

Proof.

Suppose

For the rest of the proof there are two cases depending on whether

We now verify condition (b) in Definition

(b)

\chi_{x}({\cal O})=\begin{cases}b_{i}&\text{if $x\in\breve{S}_{i}$,}\\
r_{i}&\text{if $x=s_{i}$}\end{cases}

(c)

\hat{\chi}_{x}({\cal O})=\begin{cases}b_{i}(\alpha_{i}-j)+r_{i-\delta(\text{$i$ even})}&\text{if $x=s_{i,j}$,}\\

(e)

(2)

We now use Lemma

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

(b)

(d)

(b) Using Lemma

which is equivalent to what we wished to prove.

(c) From Lemma

(a) and (c) we see that

\displaystyle k\hat{\chi}_{x}({\cal O})-j\hat{\chi}_{y}({\cal O})

\displaystyle=k[b_{1}(\alpha_{1}-j)+r_{1}]-j[b_{1}(\alpha_{1}-k)+r_{1}]

\displaystyle=(k-j)[b_{1}\alpha_{1}+r_{1}]

. The fact that

(f) From Lemma

(a) and the assumption on

We now prove a general result about the

where
\alpha
is a palindrome (that is, equal to its reversal) with an odd number of parts. So there is an order-reversing bijection
\kappa:P\rightarrow P
. Note that
I\in{\cal I}(P)
if and only if
\kappa(I)\in{\mathcal{U}}(P)
.
This permits us to define the

(with respect to
\kappa
) of

as

\overline{I}=\kappa\circ c(I).

See Figure

where circles are black or white depending on whether they are in the ideal or not, respectively. The relationship with rowmotion is as follows.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

be self-dual and fix an order-reversing bijection

. Then for all

\hat{\rho}^{-1}(\overline{I})=\overline{\hat{\rho}(I)},

where the ideal complements are with respect to

.

Proof.

Since
\kappa
and

commute, they can be applied to get the ideal complement in either order.
And
\hat{\rho}=\Delta^{-1}\circ\nabla\circ c
, so it suffices to show that the following diagram commutes:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
P
be self-dual with
n=\#P
, and fix an order-reversing bijection
\kappa:P\rightarrow P
. Let
I\in{\cal I}(P)
.

(a)

If
I,\overline{I}\in{\cal O}
for some orbit
{\cal O}
, then

\frac{\hat{\chi}({\cal O})}{\#{\cal O}}=\frac{n}{2}.

(b)

If
I\in{\cal O}
and
\overline{I}\in\overline{{\cal O}}
for some orbits
{\cal O}
and
\overline{{\cal O}}
with
{\cal O}\neq\overline{{\cal O}}
, then
\#{\cal O}=\#\overline{{\cal O}}
and

\frac{\hat{\chi}({\cal O})+\hat{\chi}(\overline{{\cal O}})}{\#{\cal O}+\#\overline{{\cal O}}}=\frac{n}{2}.

Proof.

(a) We claim that the hypothesis on
{\cal O}
implies that, for any
J\in{\cal O}
, we must have
\overline{J}\in{\cal O}
. Indeed,
J=\hat{\rho}^{j}(I)
for some integer
j
. So, by Lemma
3.2
,

\overline{J}=\overline{\hat{\rho}^{j}(I)}=\hat{\rho}^{-j}(\overline{I})\in{\cal O},

since
\overline{I}\in{\cal O}
. Thus
{\cal O}
can be partitioned into pairs
\{I,\overline{I}\}
with
I\neq\overline{I}
, and singletons
\{I\}
with
I=\overline{I}
. In each pair and singleton, the average of
\hat{\chi}
is
n/2
, so the same is true of the orbit.

(b) Suppose
{\cal O}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}
. Similar reasoning as in (a) shows that the hypothesis on
I,\overline{I}
implies that

\overline{{\cal O}}=\{\overline{I}_{1},\ldots,\overline{I}_{k}\}
. Now the average of
\hat{\chi}
in each pair
\{I_{i},\overline{I}_{i}\}
is equal to
n/2
, which implies the same is true of
{\cal O}\uplus\overline{{\cal O}}
.
∎

To turn this last result into a homomesy, take a self-dual
P
with a given order-reversing bijection
\kappa:P\rightarrow P
.
Consider the group generated by the action of
\hat{\rho}
and the ideal complement map
I\mapsto\overline{I}
. The orbits of this action will be called
dihedral group orbits
. Note that from the proof of Corollary
3.3
,
each dihedral group orbit is either an orbit of the action of
\hat{\rho}
or a union of two such orbits.
The next result follows immediately from Corollary
3.3
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
P
is self-dual with
n=\#P
and fix an order-reversing bijection
\kappa:P\rightarrow P
. Then
\hat{\chi}
is
(n/2)
-mesic on dihedral group orbits. ∎

In this section we will consider fences with at most

We will need an expression for the number of ideals (equivalently, antichains) in

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
where
s\geq 2
. Then

\#{\cal I}(\alpha)=\alpha_{s}\cdot\#{\cal I}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s-1})+\#{\cal I}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s-2}).

Proof.

We will assume that
s
is odd; the proof in the case where
s
even is similar.
Let
x
be the common element of the last two segments which, by the assumption on
s
, is a minimal element of
F=\breve{F}(\alpha)
. Then for any
I\in{\cal I}(\alpha)
we have
x\in I
or
x\not\in I
. Since
x
is minimal, the ideals with
x\in I
are in bijection with the ideals of
F-\{x\}\cong\breve{F}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s-1})\uplus\breve{F}(\alpha_{s}-1)
.
Since for any two posets
P,Q
we have
{\cal I}(P\uplus Q)={\cal I}(P)\times{\cal I}(Q)
,
this accounts for the first term of the recursion. On the other hand, if
x\not\in I
, then
I
does not contain any element on either of the last two segments of
F
. So these ideals contribute the second term, and we are done.
∎

We start with the case of two segments. Note that parts (c) and (d) of the next result are homometries which are not homomesies.

Theorem 4.3
.

Rowmotion on
\breve{F}(a,b)
has the following properties.

(a)

All orbits
{\cal O}

\ell=\lcm(a,b)

except for one,
{\cal O}^{\prime}

\ell+1

(b)

The number of orbits is
\gcd(a,b)
.

(c)

For any orbit of size
\ell

\chi({\cal O})=\frac{2ab-a-b}{\gcd(a,b)}:=m.

For the orbit of size
\ell+1

\chi({\cal O}^{\prime})=m+1.

(d)

For any orbit of size
\ell
,

\hat{\chi}({\cal O})=\frac{\ell(a+b-2)}{2}.

For the orbit of size
\ell+1
,

\hat{\chi}({\cal O}^{\prime})=\frac{(\ell+2)(a+b-2)}{2}+1.

Proof.

(a) There is only one antichain containing the unique shared element
s_{1}
of
\breve{F}(a,b)
. So there is a unique tiling
T^{\prime}
with a red tile and all other tilings
T
consist only of yellow and black tiles. Figure
5
displays
T^{\prime}
for
\breve{F}(5,4)
.

Consider a tiling
T
of the second type. From Definition
2.1
(a), the first row can be partitioned into pairs consisting of a black tile and a yellow tile which together cover
a

b

T
must have
\ell=\lcm(a,b)
columns. In the same manner we see that

must have one more column for the red tile. This implies the desired orbit lengths.

(b) Let
k
be the number of orbits. Using part (a) and Corollary
4.2
,

ab+1=\#{\cal I}(a,b)=(k-1)\ell+(\ell+1)=k\ell+1.

It follows that

.

(c) From the description of the tilings in (a), for all orbits we have

b_{1}=\frac{\lcm(a,b)}{a}=\frac{b}{\gcd(a,b)}.

Similarly
b_{2}=a/\gcd(a,b)
. Finally
r_{1}=0
or

for the orbits
{\cal O}
and
{\cal O}^{\prime}
, respectively. The result now follows by substitution into Lemma

(d), using that

(d) These equations follow from (a) and Lemma

In the next result we consider certain fences with three segments. We do not explicitly state the values of the
\chi

Theorem 4.4
.

Consider
\breve{F}(a,b,a)
and define

\overline{a},\overline{b}
are relatively prime, there exists a smallest positive integer

such that

m\overline{a}=q\overline{b}+1

(a)

We have

\#{\cal S}=\overline{a}(g-1)^{2},\quad\#{\cal M}=q,\quad\#{\cal L}=\overline{a}-q,

a(2b-2\overline{b}+m)+g&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal M}$},\\
a(2b-\overline{b}+m)+g&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal L}$}.\end{cases}

(b)

For rowmotion on antichains,
\chi
is homometric.

(c)

For rowmotion on ideals,
\hat{\chi}
is
n/2
-mesic where

Proof.

(a) We will construct the corresponding tilings, where we will number the columns from left to right starting with column
0
. We can use any integer to refer to a column by using its standard representative modulo
\#{\cal O}
, the number of columns.
Figure
3
gives an example of the tilings for
\breve{F}(4,3,4)
, where

because

We first construct the tilings
T
for the orbits in
{\cal S}
.
We also standardize these tilings so that the first row begins with a yellow tile.
The first row of
T
will consist of
\ell/a
copies of a pair consisting of a yellow tile followed by a black tile of length
a-1
. So
T
has length
\ell
.
We now tile the second row with

. But one of these will be the rotation class which has two yellow tiles in the same column. Thus the count for the placement of the second row is really

\overline{a}(g-1)^{2}

\overline{a}

(b) We see from their descriptions in part (a) that these tilings have the following parameters.

{\cal G}

0=j_{2}=j_{3}\neq j_{4}
. Let us write
j
instead of
j_{4}
for short.
For each value of
j
satisfying
1\leq j\leq a-1
, we construct an orbit of length
3a^{2}+a
.
Again, it suffices to specify the positions of the red tiles, since every row intersects at least one red tile. From left to right, the red tiles are in the following positions:

•

In columns
1+r(a+1)
for
0\leq r\leq j-1
, with head in row 2.

•

In columns
r(a+1)
for
j\leq r\leq a+j-1
, with head in row 3.

•

In columns
2+r(a+1)
for
a+j-1\leq r\leq 2a-2
, with head in row 2.

•

In columns
1+r(a+1)
for
2a-1\leq r\leq 3a-2
, with head in row 1.

It can be checked that this configuration of red tiles can be extended to a valid tiling. Note that the
a-1
orbits obtained for the different values of
j
are all distinct. This is because the positions of the red tiles with heads in row
1
are independent of
j
, whereas the sets of positions of the other red tiles are different for each
j
.

Next we show that we have obtained tilings for all the orbits. The total number of antichains in the orbits that we have constructed is

(a-1)^{3}\cdot a+a\cdot(a+1)+(a^{2}+a+1)+(a-1)\cdot(3a^{2}+a)=a^{4}+3a^{2}+1,

which equals
\#{\cal I}(a,a,a,a)
by Corollary
4.2
(c).

(b) From the constructions in (a) we see that, for given
i
, the parameters
b_{i}
and
r_{i}
are constant in each of the four classes of orbits. Homometry follows immediately. To compute the statistics, we note the following specific values.

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}\text{type of orbit}&b_{1}=b_{4}&b_{2}=b_{3}&r_{1}=r_{3}&r_{2}\\
\hline\cr{\cal S}&1&1&0&0\rule{0.0pt}{12.0pt}\\
{\cal M}&1&1&1&0\\
{\cal L}&a+1&a&1&a\\
{\cal G}&3a&3a-1&a&a\end{array}

Now the formulas in Lemma
2.3
(d) and (e) complete the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

The orbits of rowmotion on
\breve{F}(a,1,a,1,a)
can be partitioned into
three sets
{\cal S},{\cal M},{\cal L}
called small, medium, and large having the following properties.

(a)

We have

\#{\cal S}=2a-2,\quad\#{\cal M}=1,\quad\#{\cal L}=a,

and

\#{\cal O}=\begin{cases}a+1&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal S}$},\\
3a+2&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal M}$},\\
a^{2}+2a&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal L}$}.\end{cases}

(b)

For rowmotion on antichains,
\chi
is homometric with

\chi({\cal O})=\begin{cases}3a&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal S}$},\\
9a-3&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal M}$},\\
3a^{2}+3a-2&\text{if ${\cal O}\in{\cal L}$}.\end{cases}

(c)

The dihedral group orbits are pairs of orbits in
{\cal S}
and single orbits in
{\cal M}
and
{\cal L}
.

Proof.

(a)
We label the columns of a tiling as usual. Since there will be no black tiles in rows 2 and 4, every row will intersect at least one red tile. Thus it suffices to specify the positions of these tiles. We will omit the straightforward check that the given positions of the red tiles can be extended to a full tiling with no column repeated, and that the orbits are distinct and have a total of
a^{3}+4a^{2}+3a
columns, which is the total number of antichains of the fence by Corollary
4.2
(d). We label the orbits in
{\cal L}
by
{\cal L}_{k}
where
1\leq k\leq a
. We label the orbits in
{\cal S}
which have no red tile with a head in row
2
as
{\cal S}_{k}
where
1\leq k\leq a-1
.
Similarly, the orbits in
{\cal S}
with no red tile with a head in row
3
are labeled as
{\cal S}_{k}^{\prime}
where
1\leq k\leq a-1
.
Every tiling in
{\cal S}
satisfies exactly one of these two conditions, so these two subsets have disjoint union
{\cal S}
. Finally, the sole orbit in
{\cal M}
will be labeled
{\cal M}_{1}
.

We now give the column numbers for the red tiles in each orbit with their head in each row. The orbits
{\cal O}_{k}
have

for

, and so the positions will be parameterized by

. The symbol NA (not applicable) in the following table indicates that there are no red tiles for that orbit with their head in the corresponding row.

5
Conjectures and an open problem

We now consider some conjectures and an open problem in the hopes that they will spur future research. To state the first one, call a sequence of real numbers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}

palindromic
if

a_{i}=a_{n+1-i}

for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Associated with any fence
\breve{F}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
we have the
black tile sequence

b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{s}
and the
red tile sequence

r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{s-1}
.

Question 5.1
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
be palindromic and
F=\breve{F}(\alpha)
. Find necessary and/or sufficient conditions on
\alpha
for the black or the red tile sequences to be palindromic for all rowmotion orbits.

From the proofs of Theorems
4.3
,
4.4
, and
4.5
, we see that the black and red tile sequences are palindromic for palindromic
\alpha
with
s\leq 3
, as well as for
\breve{F}(a^{4})
where
a^{s}
denotes
a
repeated
s
times.
We have checked by computer that
\breve{F}(a^{s})
has palindromic tile sequences for all
a+s\leq 12

except

a=4
and
s=8
, where the orbit containing the antichain
\{s_{1},s_{2,1}\}
(equivalently,
\{x_{1},x_{7}\}
indexing the entries from left to right), has black tile sequence

21,20,18,18,19,18,19,21

and red tile sequence

5,4,13,4,9,8,5.

The black and red tile sequences are related for palindromic
\alpha
, as shown in the next result.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
be palindromic where
\alpha_{i}\geq 2
for all
i\in[s]
, and let
F=\breve{F}(\alpha)
. Then for any orbit
{\cal O}
of
F
,
the black tile sequence
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{s}
is palindromic if and only if the red tile sequence

r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{s-1}
is palindromic.

Proof.

We will do induction on
i
. Because all
\alpha_{i}\geq 2
, Lemma
2.3
(a) implies that
\#{\cal O}=b_{i}\alpha_{i}+r_{i}+r_{i-1}
for any
i\in[s]
. Using the fact that
r_{0}=r_{s}=0
we obtain

b_{1}\alpha_{1}+r_{1}=\#{\cal O}=b_{s}\alpha_{s}+r_{s-1}.

Since
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{s}
, This can be rewritten as

\alpha_{1}(b_{1}-b_{s})=r_{s-1}-r_{1}.

It follows that
b_{1}=b_{s}
if and only if
r_{1}=r_{s-1}
which is the base case for our induction.

The induction step is similar. We have

b_{i}\alpha_{i}+r_{i}+r_{i-1}=\#{\cal O}=b_{s-i+1}\alpha_{s-i+1}+r_{s-i+1}+r_{s-i}.

By induction, we can assume
r_{i-1}=r_{s-i+1}
. And
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{s-i+1}
, so

\alpha_{i}(b_{i}-b_{s-i+1})=r_{s-i}-r_{i}.

Thus
b_{i}=b_{s-i+1}
is equivalent to
r_{s-i}=r_{i}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

The condition that
\alpha_{i}\geq 2
for all
i
in this proposition is necessary. For example, the proof of Theorem
4.6
(b) shows that the black tile sequence of an orbit is always palindromic, but that the red tile sequence is not necessarily so.
Having palindromic sequences yields certain homomesies for free.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
where
\alpha_{i}\geq 2
for all
i\in[s]
. Also let
F=\breve{F}(\alpha)
and
n=\#F
.
If
\alpha
as well as the black and red tile sequences are all palindromic, then one has the following homomesies.

(a)

For all
k\in[n]
the statistic
\chi_{k}-\chi_{n-k+1}
is
0
-mesic.

(b)

If
s
is odd, then for all
k\in[n]
the statistic
\hat{\chi}_{k}+\hat{\chi}_{n-k+1}
is
1
-mesic.

Proof.

(a)
First suppose
x_{k}\in\breve{S}_{i}
for some
i
. Since
\alpha
is palindromic, it follows that
x_{n-k+1}\in\breve{S}_{s-i+1}
. Now using Lemma
2.3
and the fact that the black tile sequence is palindromic, we see that

\chi_{k}({\cal O})=b_{i}=b_{s-i+1}=\chi_{n-k+1}({\cal O})

for all orbits
{\cal O}
.
This proves the homomesy for our choice of
x_{k}
. The proof when
x_{k}=s_{i}
for some
i
is similar using the palindromicity of the red tile sequence. The details are omitted.

(b) If
x_{k}=s_{i}
for some
i
, then this follows from our assumptions and Theorem
3.1
(d). So assume that
x_{k}=s_{i,j}
for some
i,j
. There are two similar cases depending on whether
i
is even or odd, so we will only give details for the former. From Lemma
2.3
(c), for any orbit
{\cal O}
we have

\hat{\chi}_{k}({\cal O})=b_{i}(\alpha_{i}-j)+r_{i-1}.

Since
x_{k}
is an unshared element on
S_{i}
, we have that
x_{n-k+1}
is an unshared element on
S_{s-i+1}
.
And since
x_{k}
is the
j
th unshared element from the bottom, we have that
x_{n-k+1}
is the
j
th unshared element from the top. So counting from the bottom of the unshared elements in
S_{s-i+1}
we have that
x_{n-k+1}
is element number

\alpha_{s-i+1}-j
.
Using Lemma
2.3
(c) again, as well as the assumption that the
b_{i}
and
r_{i}
sequences are palindromic, gives

\hat{\chi}_{n-k+1}({\cal O})=b_{s-i+1}[\alpha_{s-i+1}-(\alpha_{s-i+1}-j)]+r_{(s-i+1)-1}=jb_{i}+r_{i}.

Finally

\hat{\chi}_{k}({\cal O})+\hat{\chi}_{n-k+1}({\cal O})=b_{i}(\alpha_{i}-j)+r_{i-1}+jb_{i}+r_{i}=\#{\cal O}

by Lemma
2.3
(a). This completes the proof of (b).
∎

We conjecture that even more homomesies are true for constant
\alpha
.

Conjecture 5.4
.

Let
\alpha=(a^{s})
and let
F=\breve{F}(\alpha)
.

(a)

The statistic
\chi
is homometric.

(b)

If
s
is odd then the statistic
\hat{\chi}
is
n/2
-mesic where
n=\#F
.

Again, we have evidence for this conjecture from the results in Section
4
, which prove that it holds for
s\leq 4
. Furthermore, computer calculations have verified the truth of these statements for
a+s\leq 12
.
One might be tempted to try and prove part (b) by showing that, under the given hypotheses, the dihedral group orbits and orbits are the same, and then using Theorem
3.4
. This would mean that for every ideal
I
we would have
\overline{I}
in the same orbit with
I
. But this is not always true. For example, if
\alpha=(2^{7})
and
F=\breve{F}(\alpha)
, then the ideal
I=\{x_{2},x_{4},x_{7}\}
has

\overline{I}=\{x_{1},x_{3},x_{5}\}
, which is in a different orbit.

We also note that if
a=2
then Conjecture
5.4
(a) is implied by the following stronger statement. This result also follows from Corollary 3.11 in a paper of Chan, Haddadan, Hopkins, and Moci
[
CHHM17
]
.

Proposition 5.5
.

In
F=\breve{F}(2^{s})
the statistc
\chi
is
s/2
-mesic.

Proof.

Let
x_{i}
be the unique unshared element on
S_{i}
for
i\in[s]
.
Let

\chi_{i}=2\chi_{x_{i}}+\chi_{y}+\chi_{z}

where
y,z
are as in Theorem
3.1
. By that result, for any orbit
{\cal O}
we have

\sum_{i}\chi_{i}({\cal O})=s\cdot\#{\cal O}
. On the other hand, in the previous sum
\chi_{x}
appears twice for every
x\in F
. It follows that

\sum_{i}\chi_{i}({\cal O})=2\chi({\cal O})
which completes the proof.
∎

Finally we mention that Defant and Lin have conjectured that the down-degree statistic is homometric in the
\nu
-Tamari lattice corresponding to a lattice path
\nu
. See
[
DL
]
for more information.
