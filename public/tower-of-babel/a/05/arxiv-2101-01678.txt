Link invariants from L 2 L^{2} -Burau maps of braids
Fathi Ben Aribi

Abstract
. A previous work of A. Conway and the author introduced L 2 L^{2} -Burau maps of braids, which are generalizations of the Burau representation whose coefficients live in a more general group ring than the one of Laurent polynomials. This same work established that the L 2 L^{2} -Burau map of a braid at the group of the braid closure yields the L 2 L^{2} -Alexander torsion of the braid closure in question, as a variant of the well-known Burau–Alexander formula. In the present paper, we generalize the previous result to L 2 L^{2} -Burau maps defined over all quotients of the group of the braid closure. The link invariants we obtain are twisted L 2 L^{2} -Alexander torsions of the braid closure, and recover more topological information, such as the hyperbolic volumes of Dehn fillings. The proof needs us to first generalize several fundamental formulas for L 2 L^{2} -torsions, which have their own independent interest. We then discuss how likely we are to generalize this process to yet more groups. In particular, a detailed study of the influence of Markov moves on L 2 L^{2} -Burau maps and two explicit counter-examples to Markov invariance suggest that twisted L 2 L^{2} -Alexander torsions of links are the only link invariants we can hope to build from L 2 L^{2} -Burau maps with the present approach.

1.
Introduction

The
L^{2}
-Burau maps and reduced
L^{2}
-Burau maps of braids were introduced in 2016 in
[
5
]
by A. Conway and the author. These maps generalize the Burau representation of braid groups (introduced in
[
8
]
) and are indexed by a positive real number
t
and a group epimorphism
\gamma
starting at a free group
\mathbb{F}_{n}
of finite rank. In a sense, all the
L^{2}
-Burau maps are contained between the Burau representation and the Artin injection of the braid group
B_{n}
in the automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
of the free group
\mathbb{F}_{n}
. Moreover, A. Conway and the author proved in
[
5
]
that for a braid
\beta\in B_{n}
, its image by the reduced
L^{2}
-Burau map
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma_{\beta}}
associated to the epimorphism
\gamma_{\beta}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n}\to G_{\beta}\cong\pi_{1}\left(S^{3}\setminus\hat{\beta}\right)

yields the
L^{2}
-Alexander torsion
of the braid closure
\hat{\beta}
.
The
L^{2}
-Alexander torsion is an invariant of links introduced by Li–Zhang and Dubois–Friedl–Lück
[
13
,
9
]
, whose construction can be compared with those of the twisted Alexander polynomials, and which detects various topological information. As a consequence of the main result of
[
5
]
, the reduced
L^{2}
-Burau map
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma_{\beta}}
thus
contains deep topological information about
\beta
such as the hyperbolic volume or the genus of the link
\hat{\beta}
.

It is then natural to wonder whether
L^{2}
-Burau maps associated to other epimorphisms can similarly provide link invariants and detect topological information of the braid, and this article provides a partial positive answer to this question.

In Section
5.1
, we introduce the notion of
Markov-admissibility
of a family
\mathcal{Q}
of group epimorphisms
Q_{\beta}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}\twoheadrightarrow G_{Q_{\beta}}
indexed by braids
\beta\in\sqcup_{n\geqslant 1}B_{n}
. Roughly speaking, we say that such a family is Markov-admissible when for any two braids
\alpha,\beta
that have isotopic closures (and thus are related by Markov moves), the associated epimorphisms
Q_{\alpha}
and
Q_{\beta}
descend “to the same depth” and are related by a sequence of commutative diagrams. Markov admissibility appears to be a necessary condition in order to construct Markov functions and link invariants from general families of epimorphisms indexed by braids.

In this paper we focus on a specific candidate for being a Markov function, namely the function

F_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\begin{pmatrix}&\sqcup_{n\geqslant 1}B_{n}&\to&\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{R}_{>0})/\{t\mapsto t^{m},m\in\mathbb{Z}\}\\
&\beta&\mapsto&\left[t\mapsto\dfrac{\det^{r}_{G_{Q_{\beta}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)}{\max(1,t)^{n}}\right]\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathcal{Q}
is a Markov-admissible family of epimorphisms and
\det^{r}_{G}
is the
regular Fuglede–Kadison determinant
for the group
G
, a version of the determinant for infinite-dimensional
G
-equivariant operators on
\ell^{2}(G)
such as the
L^{2}
-Burau maps (see Section
2
for a definition).

The first main result of this article is the following theorem (stated here without technical details for readability):

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
4.1
, Propositions
5.9
and
5.10
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a Markov-admissible family of epimorphisms that descends to the groups of the braid closures or deeper.
Then
F_{\mathcal{Q}}

is a Markov function, and thus defines an invariant of links.
Moreover, this link invariant is a twisted
L^{2}
-Alexander torsion.

Part of Theorem
1.1
was already proven in
[
5
]
, without discussing Markov invariance. Indeed, when
\mathcal{Q}
is the family that descends to the groups of the braid closures
G_{\beta}
,
[
5
, Theorem 4.9]
directly linked
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
to the
L^{2}
-Alexander torsions of the braid closures, as a variant of the well-known Alexander–Burau formula
[
8
]
; and since the
L^{2}
-Alexander torsions were already known link invariants, Markov invariance was thus reciprocally guaranteed, and carefully studying Markov moves was unnecessary. We generalize this approach to
twisted

L^{2}
-Alexander torsions in Theorem
4.1
; to do this, we first need to establish slight generalizations of several formulas for
L^{2}
-torsions, in Section
3
.

Propositions
5.9
and
5.10
provide another way of proving that the twisted
L^{2}
-Alexander torsions are invariants of links, by studying how Markov moves on braids modify reduced
L^{2}
-Burau maps and by using properties of the Fuglede–Kadison determinant. Although their conclusions are redundant with the one of Theorem
4.1
, these two propositions have independent interest for future potential constructions of link invariants from
L^{2}
-Burau maps.

Theorem
1.1
is not an equivalence in the sense that
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
could theoretically be a Markov function for other families
\mathcal{Q}
, but the specific convenient cancellations that occur in matrix coefficients in the proofs of
Propositions
5.9
and
5.10

make this seem unlikely. As further evidence, we present two families

such that

is not a Markov function, in the second main result of this article:

Theorem 1.2

(Theorems

and
5.13
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be either the family of identities of the free groups or the family of abelianizations of the free groups.
Then
F_{\mathcal{Q}}

is not a Markov function.

Our main tools to prove Theorem

are relations between Fuglede–Kadison determinants (which are technical to define and difficult to compute), Mahler measures of polynomials (notably studied by Boyd
[
7
]
) and combinatorics on Cayley graphs of free groups (see
[
4
]
).

As the reader will probably agree, the initial question (how to build link invariants from
L^{2}
-Burau maps) is still far from answered. We restricted ourselves to studying an intuitive form of a potential Markov function, namely
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
, and we found that twisted
L^{2}
-Alexander torsions appeared to be the best link invariants we could obtain with it. This last point is unsurprising considering the form of
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
and its natural connection with the Alexander polynomial and its variations.

However, there may well be new link invariants to discover via other functions of the
L^{2}
-Burau maps, and we hope that our computations of how these maps change under Markov moves can be of use for future research in this vein.

The present article arose as a part of a wider project in collaboration with C. Anghel aiming to construct new knot invariants from
L^{2}
-versions of the Burau and Lawrence representations of braid groups. To attain this end, studying the influence of Markov moves on
L^{2}
-Burau maps appears to be a natural step.
Moreover, at the time of writing, the current paper is an expansion of the first half of the preprint
[
3
]
. The second half of
[
3
]
has since been expanded into the separate paper
[
4
]
.

The article is organized as follows: in Section

in Section

2.2.
Fox calculus

Denoting by
\mathbb{F}_{n}
the free group on
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
, and for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the
i
-th
Fox derivative

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}:\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{F}_{n}]\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{F}_{n}]\penalty\

(first introduced in
[
10
]
)
is the linear extension of the map defined on
\mathbb{F}_{n}
by:

\forall i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\forall u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{n},

\frac{\partial x_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}=\delta_{i,j},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \frac{\partial x_{j}^{-1}}{\partial x_{i}}=-\delta_{i,j}x_{j}^{-1},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \frac{\partial(uv)}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}+u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}.

The following formula is often called the
fundamental formula of Fox calculus
:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{F}_{n}
and
\epsilon\colon\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{F}_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
the ring epimorphism defined by
\epsilon\colon x_{i}\mapsto 1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then:

u-\epsilon(u)\cdot 1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\cdot(x_{i}-1).

2.3.
Fuglede–Kadison determinant

In this section we will give short definitions of the von Neumann trace and the Fuglede–Kadison determinant.
More details can be found in
[
15
]
and
[
5
]
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group.
The Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(G)
is the completion of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}G
, and the space of bounded operators on it is denoted
B(\ell^{2}(G))
. We will focus on
right-multiplication operators

R_{w}\in B(\ell^{2}(G))
, where
R_{\cdot}
denotes the right regular action of
G
on
\ell^{2}(G)
extended to the group ring
\mathbb{C}G
(and further extended to the rings of matrices
M_{p,q}(\mathbb{C}G)
).

For any element
w=a_{0}\cdot 1_{G}+a_{1}g_{1}\ldots+a_{r}g_{r}\in\mathbb{C}G
, the
von Neumann trace

\mathrm{tr}_{G}
of the associated right multiplication operator is defined as

\mathrm{tr}_{G}(R_{w})=\mathrm{tr}_{G}\left(a_{0}\mathrm{Id}_{\ell^{2}(G)}+a_{1}R_{g_{1}}\ldots+a_{r}R_{g_{r}}\right):=a_{0},

and the von Neumann trace for a finite square matrix over
\mathbb{C}G
is given as the sum of the traces of the diagonal coefficients.

Now the most concise definition of the
Fuglede–Kadison determinant

\det_{G}(A)
of a right-multiplication operator
A
is probably

\det{}_{G}(A):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left(\exp\circ\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}_{G}\right)\circ\ln\right)\left((A_{\perp})^{*}(A_{\perp})+\varepsilon\mathrm{Id}\right)\ \geqslant 0,

where
A_{\perp}
is the restriction of
A
to a supplementary of its kernel,
*
is the adjunction and
\ln
the logarithm of an operator in the sense of the holomorphic functional calculus. Compare with
[
15
, Theorem 3.14]
and Proposition
2.2
below. We call the operator
A

of determinant class
if
\det_{G}(A)\neq 0
.

The following properties concern the classical Fuglede–Kadison determinant
\det_{G}
described in
[
15
, Chapter 3]
, which is not always multiplicative if one deals with non injective operators. Moreover, this determinant forgets about the influence of the spectral value
0
, which surprisingly makes it take the value
1
for the zero operator. More recent articles have used the
regular Fuglede–Kadison determinant

\det^{r}_{G}
instead, which is defined for square injective operators, is zero for non injective operators, and is always multiplicative. In this paper we will work with both types of determinants, but the reader should be reassured that most of the statements we will make remain unchanged while replacing one determinant with the other (up to assumptions on injectivity usually). Similarly, the statements of the following Proposition
2.2
admit immediate variants with
\det^{r}_{G}
. All statements of Proposition
2.2
follow from
[
15
, Section 3]
, except for (6), which directly follows from the others.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
G
be a countable discrete group and let

A,B,C,D\in\sqcup_{p,q\in\mathbb{N}}R_{M_{p,q}(\mathbb{C}G)}

be general right multiplication operators. The Fuglede–Kadison determinant satisfies the following properties:

(1)

(multiplicativity) If
A,B
are injective, square and of the same size, then

\det{}_{G}(A\circ B)=\det{}_{G}(A)\det{}_{G}(B).

(2)

(block triangular case) If
A,B
are injective and square, then

\det{}_{G}\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}=\det{}_{G}(A)\det{}_{G}(B)=\det{}_{G}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
D&B\end{pmatrix},

where
C,D
have the appropriate dimensions.

(3)

(induction) If
\iota\colon G\hookrightarrow H
is a group monomorphism, then

\det{}_{H}(\iota(A))=\det{}_{G}(A).

(4)

(relation with the von Neumann trace) If
A
is a positive operator, then

\det{}_{G}(A)=\left(\exp\circ\mathrm{tr}{}_{G}\circ\ln\right)(A).

(5)

(simple case) If
g\in G
is of infinite order, then for all
t\in\mathbb{C}
the operator
\mathrm{Id}-tR_{g}
is injective and

\mathrm{det}_{G}(\mathrm{Id}-tR_{g})=\max(1,|t|).

(6)

(
2\times 2
trick) For all
A,B,C,D\in N(G)
such
that
B
is invertible,

\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}
is injective if and only if

DB^{-1}A-C
is injective, and in this case
one has:

\det{}_{G}\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\det{}_{G}(B)\det{}_{G}(DB^{-1}A-C).

(7)

(relation with Mahler measure) Let
G=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
P\in\mathbb{C}[X_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,X_{d}^{\pm 1}]
denote the Laurent polynomial associated to the operator
A\in R_{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. Then

\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(A)=\mathcal{M}(P)=\exp\left(\dfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ldots\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ln\left(\left|P(e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{d}})\right|\right)d\theta_{1}\ldots d\theta_{d}\right),

where
\mathcal{M}
is the Mahler measure.

(8)

(limit of positive operators) If
A
is injective, then

\det{}_{G}(A)=\det{}_{G}(A^{*})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\sqrt{\det{}_{G}(A^{*}A+\varepsilon\mathrm{Id})}.

(9)

(dilations) Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then:

\det{}_{G}\left(\lambda\ \mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)=|\lambda|^{n}.

2.4.
L^{2}
-Burau maps on braids

The
L^{2}
-Burau maps of braids were defined and studied in
[
5
]
. Before this, a specific
L^{2}
-Burau map had been introduced in
[
11
]
, where it was called a quantization of the Burau representation.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
t>0
, let
\Phi_{n}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
denote the projection that sends all free generators to
1
, and let
\gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{n}\twoheadrightarrow G
denote an epimorphism such that
\Phi_{n}
factors through
\gamma
.
Let

\kappa(t,\Phi_{n},\gamma):\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{F}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}G
denote the ring homomorphism that sends

to
t^{\Phi_{n}(g)}\gamma(g)\in\mathbb{R}G
.

Then, following
[
5
]
, the associated
L^{2}
-Burau map
on
B_{n}
is

\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}\colon B_{n}\ni\beta\ \mapsto\ R_{\kappa(t,\Phi_{n},\gamma)(J)}\in B\left(\ell^{2}(G)^{\oplus n}\right),

where
J=\left(\dfrac{\partial h_{\beta}(x_{j})}{\partial x_{i}}\right)_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}
is the Fox jacobian of
h_{\beta}
for the base of the
x_{i}
.

The
reduced
L^{2}
-Burau map
on
B_{n}
(associated to the same parameters

) is

\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}\colon B_{n}\ni\beta\ \mapsto\ R_{\kappa(t,\Phi_{n},\gamma)(J^{\prime})}\in B\left(\ell^{2}(G)^{\oplus(n-1)}\right),

where
J^{\prime}=\left(\dfrac{\partial h_{\beta}(g_{j})}{\partial g_{i}}\right)_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n-1}
is the Fox jacobian of
h_{\beta}
for the base of the
g_{i}
.

In the remainder of this article we will focus on reduced
L^{2}
-Burau maps.

Observe that
L^{2}
-Burau maps (reduced or not) can also be defined as maps over a certain homology of a cover of the punctured disk (see
[
5
]
for details). Although these homological definitions may be more natural and useful for further generalizations, the current article will only use the previous definitions via Fox jacobians.

-Burau maps.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
5
]
)
.

For any

as above and any braids

\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha\beta)=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\beta)\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\beta}}(\alpha).

This section covers some necessary definitions to state the results in Section

A

-module
is an Hilbert space

on which there is a left

-action by isometries, and
such that there exists a positive integer

For

\mathcal{N}(G)
-modules, we will call

a
morphism of finitely generated Hilbert
\mathcal{N}(G)
-modules

is a linear
G
-equivariant map, bounded for the respective scalar products of
U
and
V
(in this paper, these morphisms will simply be right multiplication operators).

A

\mathcal{N}(G)
-chain complex

\mathcal{N}(G)
-modules

such that

for all

(in this paper,

The

-homology of
C_{*}

is a finitely generated Hilbert
\mathcal{N}(G)
-module.
We say that

is

if its

-homology is trivial. We say that
C_{*}
is of
determinant class
if all the operators

are of determinant class.

Let

be a finite Hilbert
\mathcal{N}(G)
-chain complex as above. Its

-torsion
is

if

is weakly acyclic and of determinant class, and is

otherwise.

Let
\pi
be a group and

,

We say that

factors
through
\gamma
.
For

(\pi_{1}(X),\phi,\gamma)
forms one.
Let

be such an admissible triple,
\pi=\pi_{1}(X)
and

. We define a ring homomorphism

&\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}g_{j}&\longmapsto&\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}t^{\phi(g_{j})}\gamma(g_{j})\end{pmatrix}

and we also denote

its induction over the

.

Assume

is compact. The cellular chain complex of

denoted

\left(\ldots\to\bigoplus_{i}\mathbb{Z}[\pi]\widetilde{e}_{i}^{k}\to\ldots\right)

Here the

of

-cellular chain complex of

If

-cellular chain complex of

-Alexander torsion of

at

For any two CW-complex structures

on a compact

-manifold

We therefore define the equivalence relation

on selfmaps of

by

(t\mapsto f(t))\doteq(t\mapsto g(t))\ \Leftrightarrow\ \exists n\in\mathbb{Z},\ \forall t>0,\ f(t)=t^{n}g(t),

Let

-Alexander torsion

5.
How to find other link invariants

In this section, we discuss how likely we are to generalize the process of Theorem
4.1
in order to construct more invariants of links from
L^{2}
-Burau maps of braids.

In Section
5.1
we introduce the notion of Markov-admissibility of a family of epimorphisms, a necessary property for hoping to construct link invariants; in Section
5.2
we study Markov moves on
L^{2}
-Burau maps; in Section
5.3
we find two examples of families that cannot yields link invariants. This suggests that, regarding constructions of link invariants from
L^{2}
-Burau maps, Theorem
4.1
is the best we can hope for with currently known formulas.

However, different formulas with
L^{2}
-Burau maps might satisfy Markov invariance and provide new link invariants. We hope that the following detailed descriptions of the actions of Markov moves can help for such future quests for new link invariants.

The following proposition is an elementary result in group theory, but is stated for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let

be a group homomorphism, and
N
a normal subgroup of
G
. If

f
is surjective and
Ker(f)\subset N
(in particular if
f
is an isomorphism),
then
f
induces an isomorphism between
G/N
and
H/f(N)
.

Let us now consider epimorphisms that descend to the fundamental groups of the braid closure complements.

Proposition 5.6
.

The family
\mathcal{Q}=\{\gamma_{\beta}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}\twoheadrightarrow G_{\beta}\}
is Markov-admissible. Moreover the monomorphisms
\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,\varepsilon}
are isomorphisms.

Proof.

First step: Markov 1:

Take
n\geqslant 1
and
\alpha,\beta\in B_{n}
. Note that

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}(Ker(\gamma_{\beta}))

\displaystyle=h_{\alpha}\left(\ \langle\langle h_{\beta}(x)x^{-1};x\in\mathbb{F}_{n}\rangle\rangle\ \right)=\langle\langle h_{\beta\alpha}(x)h_{\alpha}(x)^{-1};x\in\mathbb{F}_{n}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\langle h_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}(y)y^{-1};y\in\mathbb{F}_{n}\rangle\rangle=Ker(\gamma_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}),

thus it follows from Proposition
5.5
that
the isomorphism
h_{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n}\overset{\sim}{\to}\mathbb{F}_{n}
induces the required isomorphism
\chi^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,\alpha}\colon G_{\beta}\overset{\sim}{\to}G_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}
.

Second step: Markov 2:

Take
n\geqslant 1
and
\beta\in B_{n}
. Let
\beta_{+}=\sigma_{n}^{-1}\iota(\beta)\in B_{n+1}
.
First notice that

\displaystyle h_{\beta_{+}}(x_{j})x_{j}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(h_{\sigma_{n}^{-1}}(x_{j}))x_{j}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{j})x_{j}^{-1}\ \ \text{for}\ 1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1,

\displaystyle h_{\beta_{+}}(x_{n})x_{n}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(h_{\sigma_{n}^{-1}}(x_{n}))x_{n}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n+1})x_{n}^{-1}=x_{n+1}x_{n}^{-1},

\displaystyle h_{\beta_{+}}(x_{n+1})x_{n+1}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(h_{\sigma_{n}^{-1}}(x_{n+1}))x_{n+1}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n+1}^{-1}x_{n}x_{n+1})x_{n+1}^{-1}=x_{n+1}^{-1}h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n}).

Hence
Ker(\gamma_{\beta_{+}})=\langle\langle\ x_{n+1}x_{n}^{-1}\ ;\ \iota_{n}(Ker(\gamma_{\beta}))\ \rangle\rangle
.

We can now define
\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1}:=\left(G_{\beta}\ni[x]_{G_{\beta}}\mapsto[\iota_{n}(x)]_{G_{\beta_{+}}}\in G_{\beta_{+}}\right)
, where
x\in\mathbb{F}_{n}
and
[\cdot]_{G}
is the quotient class in
G
.
Since
\iota_{n}(Ker(\gamma_{\beta}))\subset Ker(\gamma_{\beta_{+}})
, then
\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1}
is a well-defined group homomorphism.

Let us prove that
\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1}
is surjective. Let
[y]_{G_{\beta_{+}}}\in G_{\beta_{+}}
, with
y\in\mathbb{F}_{n+1}
. Let
y^{\prime}\in\iota_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
be the word constructed from
y
by replacing all letters
x_{n+1}
with
x_{n}
. Hence
[y]_{G_{\beta_{+}}}=[y^{\prime}]_{G_{\beta_{+}}}\in Im(\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1})
and
\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1}
is surjective.

Let us prove that
\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1}
is injective. Let
[\iota_{n}(x)]_{G_{\beta_{+}}}\in G_{\beta_{+}}
(with
x\in\mathbb{F}_{n}
) be trivial. Then
\iota_{n}(x)\in Ker(\gamma_{\beta_{+}})=\langle\langle\ x_{n+1}x_{n}^{-1}\ ;\ \iota_{n}(Ker(\gamma_{\beta}))\ \rangle\rangle
. Thus
\iota_{n}(x)
is a product of conjugates (in
\mathbb{F}_{n+1}
) of terms
(x_{n+1}x_{n}^{-1})^{\pm 1}
and/or terms in
\iota_{n}(Ker(\gamma_{\beta}))
. But since
\iota_{n}(x)
is a free word without the letter
x_{n+1}
, we conclude that the conjugates of terms
(x_{n+1}x_{n}^{-1})^{\pm 1}
in
\iota_{n}(x)
cancel each other. Thus

\iota_{n}(x)\in\iota_{n}(Ker(\gamma_{\beta}))
and
[x]_{G_{\beta}}=1
. Hence

\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1}
is injective.

Third step: Markov 2 again:

Take
n\geqslant 1
,
\beta\in B_{n}
, and let
\beta_{+}=\sigma_{n}\iota(\beta)\in B_{n+1}
. The proof is similar as in the Second step, with the following differences:

\displaystyle h_{\beta_{+}}(x_{n})x_{n}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n})x_{n+1}\left(h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n})\right)^{-1}x_{n}^{-1},

\displaystyle h_{\beta_{+}}(x_{n+1})x_{n+1}^{-1}=h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n})x_{n+1}^{-1},

\displaystyle Ker(\gamma_{\beta_{+}})=\langle\langle\ h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n})x_{n+1}^{-1}\ ;\ \iota_{n}(Ker(\gamma_{\beta}))\ \rangle\rangle,

and we replace the
x_{n+1}
with
h_{\iota(\beta)}(x_{n})
in the proof of the surjectivity of
\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,1}
.
∎

5.2.
A sufficient condition for Markov invariance

For
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{\beta}\}
a Markov-admissible family, we define the function

F_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\begin{pmatrix}&\sqcup_{n\geqslant 1}B_{n}&\to&\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0})/\{t\mapsto t^{m},m\in\mathbb{Z}\}\\
&\beta&\mapsto&\left[t\mapsto\dfrac{\det^{r}_{G_{Q_{\beta}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)}{\max(1,t)^{n}}\right]\end{pmatrix},

taking values in equivalence classes
[t\mapsto f(t)]
of selfmaps of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, up to multiplication by monomials with integer exponents.

The following Propositions
5.9
and
5.10
show that
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is invariant under both Markov moves when
\mathcal{Q}
descends to the groups of braid closures or lower. As a consequence, they provide a second separate proof of the fact that the twisted
L^{2}
-Alexander torsions of Theorem
4.1
are invariants of links.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\{\psi_{\beta}\circ\gamma_{\beta}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}\}
be as in Example
5.8
.
Then
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is invariant under the first Markov move.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{\beta}:=\psi_{\beta}\circ\gamma_{\beta}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}\}
.
Let
n\geqslant 1
be an integer,
t>0
, and
\alpha,\beta\in B_{n}
.
We will prove that

\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right).

Observe that for any epimorphism
\gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{n}\to G
, Proposition
2.5
implies that:

\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(1)=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha\alpha^{-1})=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha^{-1})\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}}}(\alpha),

thus
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha^{-1})=\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}}}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}
.

Consequently, we have for any epimorphism
\gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{n}\to G
:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha)\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha}}(\beta)\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\beta\alpha}}(\alpha^{-1})

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha)\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha}}(\beta)\circ\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}.

Hence, for any epimorphism
\gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{n}\to G
:

\displaystyle\det{}^{r}_{G}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{G}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha)\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha}}(\beta)\circ\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{G}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha}}(\beta)-\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\gamma\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right),

where the second equality follows from Remark
2.7
and Proposition
2.2
(1).

For
\gamma=Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}
, we thus have:

\displaystyle\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}\circ h_{\alpha}}(\beta)-\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\chi^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,\alpha}\circ Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right).

Now, since for every braid
\sigma\in B_{n}
,
\gamma_{\sigma}\circ h_{\sigma}=\gamma_{\sigma}
and
Q_{\sigma}=\psi_{\sigma}\circ\gamma_{\sigma}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\chi^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,\alpha}\circ Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}\circ h_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\chi^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,\alpha}\circ Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)^{-1}\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\chi^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,\alpha}\circ Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right),

where the last equality follows from Proposition
2.2
(3).
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\{\psi_{\beta}\circ\gamma_{\beta}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}\}
be as in Example
5.8
.
Then
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is invariant under the second Markov move.

Proof.

For any braid
\beta
let us denote
Q_{\beta}:=\psi_{\beta}\circ\gamma_{\beta}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}
.

First step: negative crossing:

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}
,
\beta\in B_{n}
and

\beta_{-}=\sigma_{n}^{-1}\iota(\beta)\in B_{n+1}
. Let
t>0
. We will prove that

\dfrac{\det^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{-}}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)}{\max(1,t)^{n+1}}=\dfrac{1}{t}\cdot\dfrac{\det^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)}{\max(1,t)^{n}}.

In order to do this, we will compose
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}
with two operators (one named
\mathfrak{G}
on the left, one named
\mathfrak{D}
on the right) so that we obtain a block triangular operator with the upper left block “mostly” equal to
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}
.

First, it follows from Proposition
2.5
that

\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}\left(\sigma_{n}^{-1}\iota(\beta)\right)=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}\left(\iota(\beta)\right)\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}}\left(\sigma_{n}^{-1}\right).

Recall that
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,id}\left(\sigma_{n}^{-1}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\ddots&\ &\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&\ &0\\
\ &\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&\mathrm{Id}\\
0&\ldots&0&0&-\frac{1}{t}R_{g_{n-1}g_{n}^{-1}}\end{pmatrix}
, thus the operator defined as

\mathfrak{D}:=\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}}\left(\sigma_{n}^{-1}\right)\right)^{-1}
is equal to:

\mathfrak{D}:=\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}}\left(\sigma_{n}^{-1}\right)\right)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\ddots&\ &\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&\ &0\\
\ &\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&{t}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n}g_{n-1}^{-1})}\\
0&\ldots&0&0&-{t}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n}g_{n-1}^{-1})}\end{pmatrix}.

We therefore compute:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})\circ\mathfrak{D}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}\left(\iota(\beta)\right)\circ\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}}\left(\sigma_{n}^{-1}\right)\circ\mathfrak{D}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}\left(\iota(\beta)\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}}}(\beta)&\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\ &0\\
&\mathcal{R}&&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix},

where the row
\mathcal{R}
is the right multiplication operator by the row

\begin{pmatrix}\kappa(t,\Phi_{n+1},Q_{\beta_{-}})\left(\dfrac{\partial h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{j})}{\partial g_{n}}\right)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\kappa(t,\Phi_{n+1}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}},Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}})\left(\dfrac{\partial h_{\beta}(g_{j})}{\partial g_{n}}\right)\end{pmatrix},

that has
n-1
coefficients in
\mathbb{R}\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{-}}}
.

Now, the fundamental formula of Fox calculus (Proposition
2.1
) implies that:

\begin{pmatrix}R_{g_{1}-1}&\ldots&R_{g_{n}-1}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}R_{\dfrac{\partial h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{j})}{\partial g_{i}}}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}=\begin{pmatrix}R_{h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{1})-1}&\ldots&R_{h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{n})-1}\end{pmatrix},

thus, by applying
\kappa(t,\Phi_{n+1},Q_{\beta_{-}})
and by definition of
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}\left(\iota(\beta)\right)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}tR_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{1})}-\mathrm{Id}&\ldots&t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{n})}-\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}\left(\iota(\beta)\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}tR_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{1})}-\mathrm{Id}&\hskip 51.21504pt\ldots&\hskip 39.83368pt&t^{n}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n})}-\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}tR_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{1})}-\mathrm{Id}&\ldots&t^{n-1}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n-1})}-\mathrm{Id}&t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{n})}-\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix},

where the second equality follows from the fact that
\beta\in B_{n}
leaves
g_{n}
unchanged in the Artin action.
Let us thus define the following block triangular operator
\mathfrak{G}
:

\mathfrak{G}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\ &\ &0\\
\ &\ddots&\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&0\\
tR_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{1})}-\mathrm{Id}&\ldots&t^{n-1}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{n-1})}-\mathrm{Id}&t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{n})}-\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}.

It immediately follows from what precedes that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}\circ\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})\right)\circ\mathfrak{D}=\mathfrak{G}\circ\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}\left(\iota(\beta)\right)\right)=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}}}(\beta)&\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\ &0\\
tR_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{1})}-\mathrm{Id}\hskip-19.91684pt&\ldots&\hskip-19.91684ptt^{n-1}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n-1})}-\mathrm{Id}&t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{n})}-\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}.

On the other hand, we compute
\mathfrak{G}\circ\left(\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)\circ\mathfrak{D}=

\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\ &\ &0\\
\ &\ddots&\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&{t}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n}g_{n-1}^{-1})}\\
tR_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{1})}-\mathrm{Id}&\ldots&t^{n-1}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{n-1})}-\mathrm{Id}&\star\end{pmatrix},

with
\star=t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}\left(g_{n}h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{n-1}^{-1})g_{n-1}\right)}-t^{n+1}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}\left(g_{n}h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{n-1}^{-1})g_{n}\right)}
.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}\circ\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)\circ\mathfrak{D}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}&\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\ &\ &-{t}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n}g_{n-1}^{-1})}\\
\ldots&t^{j}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{j})}-t^{j}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{j})}&\ldots&\square\end{pmatrix},

where
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
and

\displaystyle\square=t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{n})}-\mathrm{Id}-t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}\left(g_{n}h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{n-1}^{-1})g_{n-1}\right)}+t^{n+1}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}\left(g_{n}h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{n-1}^{-1})g_{n}\right)}.

Now we use the fact that our epimorphism
Q_{\beta_{-}}
descends deeper than the braid closure group: indeed,
for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, we have:

Q_{\beta_{-}}(g_{j})=\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\beta_{-}}\right)(g_{j})=\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\circ h_{\sigma_{n}^{-1}}\right)(g_{j})=\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{j}).

Thus
\mathfrak{G}\circ\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)\circ\mathfrak{D}
is actually upper block triangular and equal to:

\begin{pmatrix}\ &0\\
\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}&\vdots\\
\ &0\\
\ &-{t}R_{\left(Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}\right)(g_{n}g_{n-1}^{-1})}\\
0&-\mathrm{Id}+t^{n+1}R_{Q_{\beta_{-}}\left(g_{n}h_{\iota(\beta)}(g_{n-1}^{-1})g_{n}\right)}\end{pmatrix}.

By applying
\det^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{-}}}}
to the previous equality,
it therefore follows from Proposition
2.2
(1), (2), (5), (9) and Remark
2.7
that

\displaystyle\max(1,t)^{n}\cdot\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{-}}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}}(\beta_{-})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)\cdot t

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{-}}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)\cdot\max(1,t)^{n+1}.

We conclude by using the fact that

Q_{\beta_{-}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}}=\sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta,-1}\circ Q_{\beta}
(by
Markov-admissibility of
\mathcal{Q}
) and Proposition
2.2
(3).

Second step: positive crossing:

Let
\beta_{+}=\sigma_{n}\iota(\beta)\in B_{n+1}
.
We will proceed almost exactly as in the first step, except for the following differences:

•

We now aim to prove that

\dfrac{\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{+}}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{+}}}(\beta_{+})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)}{\max(1,t)^{n+1}}=\dfrac{\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)}{\max(1,t)^{n}}.

•

The operator
\mathfrak{D}
becomes:

\mathfrak{D}:=\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{+}}\circ h_{\iota(\beta)}}\left(\sigma_{n}\right)\right)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\ &\ &\ &0\\
\ &\ddots&\ &\ &\vdots\\
\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&\ &0\\
\ &\ &\ &\mathrm{Id}&\mathrm{Id}\\
0&\ldots&0&0&-\frac{1}{t}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}(g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1})}\end{pmatrix}.

•

The final lower right coefficient
\star
of
\mathfrak{G}\circ\mathfrak{D}
becomes

\star=t^{n-1}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}(g_{n-1})}-\mathrm{Id}-t^{n-1}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}\left(g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1}g_{n}\right)}+\frac{1}{t}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}\left(g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1}\right)}.

•

The final lower right coefficient
\square
of
\mathfrak{G}\circ\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{+}}}(\beta_{+})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)\circ\mathfrak{D}
becomes

\square=-t^{n-1}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}(g_{n-1})}+t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}(g_{n})}+t^{n-1}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}\left(g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1}g_{n}\right)}-\frac{1}{t}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}\left(g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1}\right)}.

•

The simplification

\square=t^{n}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}(g_{n})}-\frac{1}{t}R_{Q_{\beta_{+}}\left(g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1}\right)}

comes from the fact that in the ring
\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{F}_{n+1}
we have the equalities:

\displaystyle g_{n-1}-g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1}g_{n}

\displaystyle=g_{n}\left(g_{n}^{-1}-g_{n+1}^{-1}g_{n}g_{n-1}^{-1}\right)g_{n-1}

\displaystyle=g_{n}\left(g_{n}^{-1}-h_{\sigma_{n}}^{-1}\left(g_{n}^{-1}\right)\right)g_{n-1}

\displaystyle=g_{n}\left(g_{n}^{-1}-h_{\sigma_{n}}^{-1}\left(h_{\iota(\beta)}^{-1}(g_{n}^{-1})\right)\right)g_{n-1}

\displaystyle=g_{n}\left(g_{n}^{-1}-h_{\beta_{+}}^{-1}\left(g_{n}^{-1}\right)\right)g_{n-1}

\displaystyle=g_{n}\left(h_{\beta_{+}}\left(h_{\beta_{+}}^{-1}\left(g_{n}^{-1}\right)\right)-h_{\beta_{+}}^{-1}\left(g_{n}^{-1}\right)\right)g_{n-1}.

Hence, by composing with
\gamma_{\beta_{+}}\colon\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{F}^{n}]\to\mathbb{Z}[G_{\beta_{+}}]
, the quotient by all relations

h_{\beta_{+}}(*)=*
,
we get

\gamma_{\beta_{+}}\left(g_{n-1}-g_{n}g_{n+1}^{-1}g_{n}\right)=0.

The previous equality to
0
remains true through any deeper epimorphism
Q_{\beta_{+}}=\psi_{\beta_{+}}\circ\gamma_{\beta_{+}}
.

•

Applying
\det^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{+}}}}
to the final equality now yields (as expected):

\displaystyle\max(1,t)^{n}\cdot\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{+}}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{+}}}(\beta_{+})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus n}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle=\det{}^{r}_{\Gamma_{\psi_{\beta_{+}}}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,Q_{\beta_{+}}\circ\iota_{\mathbb{F}_{n}}}(\beta)-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus(n-1)}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{t}\max(1,t)^{n+1}.

∎

5.3.
Counter-examples to Markov invariance

Theorem
4.1
established that
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is a Markov function when the Markov-admissible family of epimorphisms
\mathcal{Q}
descends to the link groups or lower. It is now natural to ask if those families
\mathcal{Q}
are the only ones for which
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is a Markov function.
We do not have an answer to this question at the time of writing.

However, by looking at the details of the proofs of Propositions
5.9
and
5.10
, it appears that applying the epimorphism
\gamma_{\beta}
(or a strictly deeper epimorphism) is necessary in order to obtain the cancellations in the matrices that yield Markov invariance for
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
. As additional evidence for this hypothesis, we discovered two families
\mathcal{Q}
such that
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is not a Markov function:
one family (the identities of the free group) lives strictly higher than the
\gamma_{\beta}
, and the other one (the abelianizations of the free groups) is not comparable to the
\gamma_{\beta}
(Figure
3
can help visualizing this). See the following Theorems
5.12
and
5.13
.

These two counter-examples illustrate some of the difficulties in computing general Fuglede–Kadison determinants, and some transversal techniques one might need to use in order to do so (techniques such as computation of Mahler measures of polynomials, or combinatorics of closed paths on Cayley graphs). Notably, the proof of Theorem
5.13
uses the new values for Fuglede–Kadison determinants over free groups computed in
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
via combinatorial and analytical techniques.

Of course, appearances might still deceive, and it could happen that unexpected identities of Fuglede–Kadison determinants occur even with families of epimorphisms not encompassed by Theorem
4.1
, especially since computing such determinants remains a daunting task today.

In the following theorem, we prove that when the family
\mathcal{Q}
descends to the free abelian groups,
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is not a Markov function and thus cannot yield link invariants. The proof uses properties of the Fuglede–Kadison determinant and a value of the Mahler measure due to Boyd
[
7
]
.

Theorem 5.12
.

For the family of abelianizations
\mathcal{Q}=\left\{\varphi_{n(\beta)}\colon\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n(\beta)}\right\}
, the value at
t=1
of the function
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is not invariant under Markov moves. In particular
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is not a Markov function.

Proof.

Let

t>0,\ n=2,\ \beta=\sigma_{1}^{-1}\in B_{2}
, and
\beta_{+}=\sigma_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{2}\in B_{3}
.
Following Section
2.1
, we compute that
\beta_{+}
acts on
\mathbb{F}_{3}
by

\left\{\begin{matrix}g_{1}\\
g_{2}\end{matrix}\right.\overset{h_{\beta_{+}}}{\mapsto}\left\{\begin{matrix}g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}\\
g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}\end{matrix},\right.

where
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}
denote the generators as in Section
2.1
.
Thus Fox calculus gives:

\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,id}(\beta_{+})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus 2}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{t}R_{g_{1}^{-1}}-\mathrm{Id}&-R_{g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}}\\
\frac{1}{t}R_{g_{1}^{-1}}&-\mathrm{Id}-tR_{g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}}+R_{g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence, from Remark
2.4
and Proposition
2.2
(6), we have:

\displaystyle\det{}_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,id}(\beta_{+})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\left(\left(-\mathrm{Id}-tR_{g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}}+R_{g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}}\right)\left(-R_{g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}^{-1}}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{t}R_{g_{1}^{-1}}-\mathrm{Id}\right)-\frac{1}{t}R_{g_{1}^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\left(-\left({t}R_{g_{1}}+R_{g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{t}R_{g_{2}g_{3}^{-1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\det{}_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\left(\mathrm{Id}+tR_{g_{1}}+t^{2}R_{g_{1}g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}}\right).

Let
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
denote the canonical generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. Then it follows from the same arguments as in the previous computation that:

\det{}_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\left(\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{(2)}_{t,\varphi_{3}}(\beta_{+})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus 2}\right)=\frac{1}{t}\det{}_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\left(\mathrm{Id}+tR_{z_{1}}+t^{2}R_{z_{1}z_{3}}\right).

Let
H
be the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and generated by
\lambda=z_{1}
and
\mu=z_{1}z_{3}
. Recall that
F_{\mathcal{Q}}(\beta_{+})
is an equivalence class of functions of
t>0
up to multiplication by a monomial, thus the value at
t=1
is always the same regardless of the representant of the equivalence class. Let us denote this value
F_{\mathcal{Q}}(\beta_{+})(1)
. We then have:

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{Q}}(\beta_{+})(1)

\displaystyle=\det{}_{\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\left(\mathrm{Id}+R_{z_{1}}+R_{z_{1}z_{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}_{H}\left(\mathrm{Id}+R_{\lambda}+R_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(1+X+Y)=1.38135...\neq 1.

where
\mathcal{M}
is the Mahler measure, the second equality follows from Proposition
2.2
(3), the third one from Proposition
2.2
(7) and the fourth one from
Example
2.3
.

Now, since by Proposition
2.2
(5) we have:

F_{\mathcal{Q}}(\beta)(1)=\det{}_{\mathbb{Z}}\left(-R_{\varphi_{2}(g_{1}^{-1}g_{2})}-\mathrm{Id}\right)=1,

we conclude that
\beta\mapsto F_{\mathcal{Q}}(\beta)(1)
is not invariant under Markov moves.
∎

We cannot expect Markov invariance by remaining at the level of the free group either, as the following theorem shows:

Theorem 5.13
.

For the family of identities
\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}_{n(\beta)}}\}
, the function
F_{\mathcal{Q}}
is not invariant under Markov moves.

Proof.

Let us take
t=1,\ n=2,\ \beta=\sigma_{1}^{-1}\in B_{2},\ \beta_{+}=\sigma_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{2}\in B_{3}
. Then, as in the proof of Theorem
5.12
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{Q}}(\beta_{+})(1)

\displaystyle=\det{}_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\left(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{1,id}(\beta_{+})-\mathrm{Id}^{\oplus 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}\left(\mathrm{Id}+R_{g_{1}}+R_{g_{1}g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det{}_{F}\left(\mathrm{Id}+R_{x}+R_{y}\right),

where
F
is the free group on two generators
x,y
that embeds in
\mathbb{F}_{3}
via
x\mapsto g_{1},y\mapsto g_{1}g_{3}g_{2}^{-1}
, and the last equality follows from Proposition
2.2
