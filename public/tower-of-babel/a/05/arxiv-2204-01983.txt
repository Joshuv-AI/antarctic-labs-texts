The Boundary Term in Huisken’s Monotonicity Formula and the Entropy of Translators
Brian White

Abstract
. For a manifold-with-boundary moving by mean curvature flow, the entropy at a later time is bounded by the entropy at an earlier time plus a boundary term. This paper controls the boundary term in a geometrically natural way. In particular, it shows (under mild hypotheses) that the entropy of a compact translator is less than or equal to the entropy of the boundary plus the maximal cone density of the boundary.

Primary 53E10

1.
Introduction

For a closed surface moving by mean curvature flow in Euclidean space, Huisken’s monotonicity formula
[
3
]
*§3
implies that a certain weighted area decreases in time. That in turn implies that
the entropy of the surface is a decreasing function of time.

For mean curvature flow of surfaces with boundary (where the motion of the boundary is prescribed), the entropy need not decrease, because the analog of Huisken’s monotonicity formula includes a spacetime boundary integral. In order to bound entropy at one time in terms of the entropy at an early time, it is necessary to control the boundary integral.

In this paper, we control the boundary integral in a
geometrically natural way.
(For the easier case of non-moving boundaries,
see
[
5
]
*Theorem 7.1.)
In particular, we show that it is bounded by the Gaussian area of the surface swept out
by a certain time-dependent rescaling of the the boundary.

As an application, we prove a simple, explicit bound for the entropy of a compact
translator with boundary, provided the boundary lies in a hyperplane or finite union
of hyperplanes perpendicular to the direction of translation.
The surface can be of any dimension and codimension. For example, as a special case, we have

Theorem 1
.

Consider an
m
-dimensional compact surface
M
in
\mathbf{R}^{m+1}
that translates with velocity
v\mathbf{e}_{m+1}
under mean curvature flow. Suppose that
\partial M
consists of
k
components, each of which is the boundary of a convex region in a horizontal
m
-plane. Then the entropy of
M
is at most

k\left(1+\frac{m\omega_{m}}{\omega_{m-1}}\right).

Here
\omega_{n}
denotes the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Corollary 2
.

For such a surface
M
,

(1)

\frac{\operatorname{area}(M\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r))}{\omega_{m}r^{m}}\leq C_{m}k

for all balls
\mathbf{B}(x,r)
, where
C_{m}
depends only on
m
.

The corollary follows from the theorem because density ratios
(for any surface) are bounded by
a constant times the entropy; see (
2
) below.
The bound (
1
) plays a key role in constructing
families of complete, non-rotationally invariant translating
annuli in
\mathbf{R}^{3}

[
2
]
.

See Section
6
for the analogous results for boundaries
\partial M

that may also contain some vertical components.

We remark that Ilmanen’s elliptic regularization
[
4
]

obtains very general mean curvature flows by taking
suitable limits of sequences of translators. The bounds in this paper imply entropy bounds for
such limits.

We now describe the general result for translators.

If
\Sigma
is a
(m-1)
-dimensional submanifold of Euclidean space, we let

C(\Sigma):=\{rx:x\in\Sigma,\,r>0\}

be the cone over
\Sigma
with vertex at the origin. The
density
of the cone is

\Theta(C(\Sigma)):=\frac{\operatorname{area}(C(\Sigma)\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r))}{\omega_{m}r^{m}},

where
\omega_{m}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. (Note that the right hand side does not depend on
r
.) Here, area should be counted with multiplicity.

We define the
maximal cone density
of
\Sigma
to be

\operatorname{mcd}(\Sigma):=\sup_{v\in\mathbf{R}^{n}}\Theta(C(\Sigma+v)).

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
M
is a compact
m
-dimensional manifold in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
that translates
with velocity
v\mathbf{e}_{n}
under mean curvature flow. Suppose that
\partial M
lies in a horizontal hyperplane.
Then

\operatorname{entropy}(M)\leq\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M)+\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M).

More generally, if
\partial M=\cup_{i}\Sigma_{i}
, where each
\Sigma_{i}
lies in a horizontal hyperplane
P_{i}
, then

\operatorname{entropy}(M)\leq\sum_{i}\left(\operatorname{entropy}(\Sigma_{i})+\operatorname{mcd}(\Sigma_{i})\right).

Recall that if
S
is a
d
-dimensional embedded submanifold of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
then
the
maximal density ratio
of
S
is

\operatorname{mdr}(S):=\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,r>0}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{d}(S\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r))}{\omega_{d}r^{d}}.

(If
S
is an immersed variety, then the
d
-dimensional area should be counted
with multiplicity.)
Entropy and maximal density ratio are closely related:

(2)

\operatorname{entropy}(S)\leq\operatorname{mdr}(S)\leq c_{d}\operatorname{entropy}(S),

where
d=\dim(S)
. (See
[
5
]
*Theorem 9.1.)
Thus as a consequence of Theorem
3
, we have

Corollary 4
.

If
M
is compact,
m
-dimensional translator in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with velocity
v\mathbf{e}_{n}
and if
\partial M

lies in a horizontal hyperplane, then

\operatorname{entropy}(M)\leq\operatorname{mdr}(\partial M)+\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M),

and

\operatorname{mdr}(M)\leq c_{m}(\operatorname{mdr}(\partial M)+\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M)).

More generally, if
\partial M=\cup_{i}\Sigma_{i}
, where each
\Sigma_{i}
lies
in a horizontal hyperplane, then

\operatorname{entropy}(M)\leq\sum_{i}\left(\operatorname{mdr}(\Sigma_{i})+\operatorname{mcd}(\Sigma_{i})\right),

and

\operatorname{mdr}(M)\leq c_{m}\sum_{i}(\operatorname{mdr}(\Sigma_{i})+\operatorname{mcd}(\Sigma_{i})).

Theorem
1
follows from
Corollary
4
because if
\Sigma
is the boundary of a convex region in a
m
-plane, then

\operatorname{mdr}(\Sigma)\leq\frac{m\omega_{m}}{\omega_{m-1}}

(see Proposition
15
), and

\operatorname{mcd}(\Sigma)=1.

Remark 5
.

If
\Sigma
is a closed curve in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
then, according to
[
1
]
*Theorem 1.1,

\operatorname{mcd}(\Sigma)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(\operatorname{TotalCurvature}(\Sigma)).

2.
The Monotonicity Inequality

Consider a closed, compact
(m-1)
-dimensional manifold
\Sigma
, and let

F:\Sigma\times(-\infty,0)\to\mathbf{R}^{n}

be a
1
-parameter family of embeddings of
\Sigma
. Let

\Gamma(t)
be the image of
F(\cdot,t)
:

\Gamma(t)=F(\Sigma,t).

For
q\in\Gamma(t)
, let
\dot{\Gamma}(q,t)
be the normal velocity of

\Gamma(t)
at
q
:

\dot{\Gamma}(q,t)=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}F(x,t)\right)^{\perp},\,\text{where $F(x,t)=q$.}

Here
(\cdot)^{\perp}
denotes the component perpendicular to
\operatorname{Tan}(\Gamma(t),q)
.

We will use the following rescaling of
F
:

\tilde{F}(x,t)=\frac{F(x,t)}{|t|^{1/2}}.

We let
d\mu
and
d\tilde{\mu}
be the
(m-1)
-dimensional volume measure on
\Sigma

corresponding to
\mathcal{H}^{m-1}
on
\Gamma(t)=F(\Sigma,t)
and on
\tilde{F}(\Sigma,t)
.
Thus
d\mu=|t|^{(m-1)/2}\,d\tilde{\mu}
.

Let
t\in(-\infty,0)\mapsto M(t)
be a one-parameter family of
m
-dimensional manifolds-with-boundary such that

\partial M(t)=\Gamma(t)

and such that
M(t)
moves by mean curvature flow: for each
x\in M(t)
, the normal
velocity at
(x,t)
is the mean curvature vector
H(x,t)
of
M(t)
at
x
.
For
x\in\Gamma(t)
, we let
\nu_{M}(x,t)
be the unit vector that it tangent to
M(t)
, normal to
\Gamma(t)
, and that points out from
M(t)
.
We let
\tilde{M}(t)
be the rescaled surface

(3)

\tilde{M}(t)=\frac{M(t)}{|t|^{1/2}}.

(The reader may wonder why we use the flow (
3
) rather than the
standard renormalized flow

(4)

\tau\mapsto\tilde{M}(-e^{-\tau}).

The latter flow has a nicer equation of motion, but in this paper, there is no advantage
in changing the time variable.)

More generally,
M(\cdot)
can be a Brakke flow with
boundary
\Gamma(\cdot)
. (See
[
5
]
*Definition 8.1.)
In this case, the vector
\nu(x,t)
is a vector of length

\leq 1
that is perpendicular to
\Gamma(t)
at
x
.
If we think of
M(t)
as a non-equilibrium soap film, then
-\nu(x,t)
is the force per
unit
(m-1)
-dimensional measure that the soap film exerts on the
boundary
\Gamma(t)
at
x
.

Define

\displaystyle\rho_{m}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4\pi|t|)^{m/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4|t|}\right),

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho(x,-1)=(4\pi)^{-m/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}|x|^{2}\right).

Thus

\rho_{m}(x,t)=|t|^{-m/2}\Phi_{m}(x/|t|^{1/2}).

We will sometimes write
\rho
and
\Phi
for
\rho_{m}
and
\Phi_{m}
when the
m
is clear from the context.

If
S
is an
m
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we define its
\Phi
-area
to be

\Phi_{m}[S]:=\int_{S}\Phi_{m}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}.

The
entropy
of
S
is the supremum of
\Phi_{m}[S^{\prime}]
among all surfaces
S^{\prime}

obtained from
S
by translation and dilation.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{x\in M(t)}\rho_{m}(x,t)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in M(t)}\Phi_{m}(x/|t|^{1/2})|t|^{-m/2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}x

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\tilde{M}(t)}\Phi_{m}(y)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}y

\displaystyle=\Phi_{m}[\tilde{M}(t)].

We now use Huisken’s Monotonicity Inequality, modified for surfaces with boundary:
see
[
5
]
*Theorem 18.3.
(Note: the terms
K
and
A
in that theorem are
0
here because the ambient space
is Euclidean.) The monotonicity theorem states that for
a<b<0
,

(5)

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}[\tilde{M}(b)]-\Phi_{m}[\tilde{M}(a)]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{\Gamma(t)}\nu_{M}\cdot\left(\dot{\Gamma}-\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right)\rho\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{q\in\Gamma(t)}\nu_{M}\cdot\left(\dot{\Gamma}+\frac{q}{2t}\right)\rho\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}\,dt.

(Here
\rho=\rho_{m}
.)
We can express this last quantity,
Q
, in terms of
F
:

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{\Sigma}\nu_{M}\cdot\left(\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp}+\frac{F}{2t}\right)\rho(F,t)\,d\mu\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{\Sigma}\nu_{M}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+\frac{F}{2t}\right)\rho(F,t)\,d\mu\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{\Sigma}\nu_{M}\cdot(-t)^{1/2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{F}{(-t)^{1/2}}\right)|t|^{-m/2}\,\Phi\left(\frac{F}{|t|^{1/2}}\right)\,d\mu\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{\Sigma}\nu_{M}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{F}{|t|^{1/2}}\right)\Phi\left(\frac{F}{|t|^{1/2}}\right)|t|^{-(m-1)/2}\,d\mu\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{\Sigma}\nu_{M}\cdot\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial t}\,\Phi(\tilde{F})\,d\tilde{\mu}\,dt

Here we have used

\nu_{M}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp}=\nu_{M}\cdot\frac{\partial F}{\partial t},

which is true since
\nu_{M}
is perpendicular to
\Gamma(t)
.

Recall that
\nu_{M}
is a unit vector perpendicular to
\Gamma(t)
and therefore
also to
\tilde{F}(M,t)
. (In the case of Brakke Flow,
\nu_{M}
is a vector of length at most one that is perpendicular to
\Gamma(t)
.) Therefore,

\nu_{M}\cdot\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial t}\leq\left|\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp}\right|.

Thus

Q\leq A(\tilde{F},a,b)

where

A(\tilde{F},a,b)=\int_{t=a}^{b}\int_{\Sigma}\left|\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{F}\right)^{\perp}\right|\Phi\left(\tilde{F}(x,t)\right)\,d\tilde{\mu}\,dt

The expression for
A(\tilde{F},a,b)
has a simple
geometric meaning: it is the
m
-dimensional
\Phi
-area
swept out by the
\tilde{F}(\Sigma,t)

from
t=a
to
t=b
.
In other words,
A(\tilde{F},a,b)
is the
\Phi
-area of the immersion

\tilde{F}:\Sigma\times[a,b]\to\mathbf{R}^{n}.

We have shown:

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
t\in(-\infty,0)\mapsto M(t)
is an
m
-dimensional Brakke flow
with boundary
\Gamma(\cdot)
, where
\Gamma(t)=F(\Sigma,t)
.
For each
T<0
, let
N(T)
be the surface swept out by

\tilde{\Gamma}(t):=\frac{\Gamma(t)}{|t|^{1/2}}

from
t=T
to
0
:

N(T)=\cup_{T\leq t<0}\tilde{\Gamma}(t).

In terms of the parametrization
F
,

N(T)=\tilde{F}(\Sigma\times[T,0)).

Then

\Phi_{m}[\tilde{M}(t)]+\Phi_{m}[N(t)]

is a decreasing function of
t
.

Proof.

We showed above that

\displaystyle\Phi[\tilde{M}(b)]-\Phi[\tilde{M}(a)]

\displaystyle\leq\Phi[F|\Sigma\times[a,b]]

\displaystyle=\Phi[F|\Sigma\times[a,0)]-\Phi[F|\Sigma\times[b,0)]

\displaystyle=\Phi[N(a)]-\Phi[N(b)].

∎

Corollary 7
.

If the areas of the
M(t)
are bounded above, or, more generally, if

\lim_{t\to-\infty}\Phi_{m}[\tilde{M}(t)]=0,

then

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}[\tilde{M}(t)]

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{m}[\tilde{F}|\Sigma\times(-\infty,t]]

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{m}[\tilde{F}|\Sigma\times(-\infty,0)]

for all
t\in(-\infty,0)
.

Because of the corollary, it is useful to have upper bounds
for
\Phi_{m}[\tilde{F}|\Sigma\times(-\infty,0)]
. The next section gives
an upper bound in the case of boundaries that move by translation.

3.
Translators

Theorem 8
.

Suppose that
\Sigma
is a compact, embedded

(m-1)
-dimensional manifold in
\mathbf{R}^{n-1}
, and suppose that

\displaystyle F:\Sigma\times\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}^{n},

\displaystyle F(x,t)=(x,0)+(a+t)v\mathbf{e}_{n}.

Let
S=\tilde{F}(\Sigma\times(-\infty,0))
. Then

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}[S]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{entropy}(\Sigma)+\Theta(\{rx:x\in\Sigma,\,r\geq 0\})

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{entropy}(\Sigma)+\operatorname{mcd}(\Sigma).

Proof.

According to Theorem
14
below, for any
m
-dimensional submanifold
S
of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,

(6)

\Phi_{m}[S]\leq\Phi_{m}[\Pi(S)]+\int_{y\in\mathbf{R}}\Phi_{m-1}[S^{y}]\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy,

where
\Phi_{m}[\Pi(S)]
is the
\Phi
-area (counting multiplicity) of the projection of
S
to the horizontal
(n-1)
-plane, and where
S^{y}
is the horizontal slice

S^{y}:=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n-1}:(x,y)\in S\}.

In our case,

F(x,t)=(x,(a+t)v)

so

\tilde{F}(x,t)=(|t|^{-1/2}x,|t|^{-1/2}(a+t)v).

Write
r=|t|^{-1/2}
, so
t=-r^{-2}
. Thus

(7)

S=\{(rx,(ra-r^{-1})v):r>0,\,x\in\Sigma\}.

From (
7
), we see that the projection
\Pi(S)
of
S
to the horizontal
plane is precisely
C(\Sigma)=\{rx:r>0,\,x\in\Sigma\}
, the cone over
\Sigma
:

(8)

\Pi(S)=C(\Sigma).

For each
y\in\mathbf{R}
, let
\mathcal{R}(y)
be the set of
r>0
such that

(ra-r^{-1})v=y,

and let
n(y)
be the number of elements of
\mathcal{R}(y)
.
From (
7
), we see that

S^{y}=\cup_{r\in\mathcal{R}(y)}r\Sigma.

Thus

(9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}[S^{y}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}(y)}\Phi_{m}[r\Sigma]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}(y)}\operatorname{entropy}\Sigma

\displaystyle=n(y)(\operatorname{entropy}\Sigma).

By (
6
), (
8
), and (
9
),

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}[S]

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{m}[C(\Sigma)]+(\operatorname{entropy}\Sigma)\int n(y)\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy.

The asserted inequality follows, because simple calculations (see Lemmas
9
and
10
below)
show that

\int n(y)\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy\leq 1,

and that, for any
m
-dimensional cone
C
with vertex
0
,

\Phi_{m}[C]=\Theta(C).

∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
n(y)
be the number of
r>0
such that
(ra-r^{-1})v=y
, i.e., such that

avr^{2}-yr+v=0
. Then

\int n(y)\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy\leq 1.

Proof.

The roots
r
are given by

r=\frac{y\pm\sqrt{y^{2}-4av^{2}}}{2av}.

If
a<0
, then exactly one of the roots is positive, so
n(y)=1
for all
y
and
therefore

\int n(y)\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy=\int\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy=1.

Now suppose that
a>0
. Let us also suppose that
v>0
. (The case
v<0
is essentially the same.) If
y>2\sqrt{a}v
, then there are two real roots, both positive.
If
|y|<2\sqrt{a}v
, there are no real roots. If
y<-2\sqrt{a}v
, there are two real roots, both negative. Thus

\displaystyle\int n(y)\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=2\int_{2\sqrt{a}v}^{\infty}\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=1.

∎

Lemma 10
.

If
C
is an
m
-dimensional cone with vertex at the origin, then

\Phi_{m}[C]=\Theta(C).

Proof.

The volume of the cone in
\mathbf{B}(0,r+dr)\setminus\mathbf{B}(0,r)

is
d(\omega_{m}\Theta(C)r^{m})
.
Thus

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}[C]

\displaystyle=\int_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{m/2}}e^{-r^{2}/4}d(\omega_{m}\Theta(C)r^{m})

\displaystyle=\Theta(C)\int_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{m/2}}e^{-r^{2}/4}d(\omega_{m}r^{m}).

\displaystyle=\Theta(C)\int_{\mathbf{R}^{m}}\Phi_{m}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\Theta(C).

∎

Theorem 11
.

Suppose that
M
is an
m
-dimensional compact surface in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
that translates with velocity
v\mathbf{e}_{n}
under mean curvature flow, and suppose that
\partial M
lies in a horizontal hyperplane. Then

\operatorname{entropy}(M)\leq\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M)+(\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M)).

More generally, if
\partial M=\cup_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma_{i}
, where each
\Gamma_{i}
lies in a horizontal plane
P_{i}
, then

\operatorname{entropy}(M)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\operatorname{mcd}(\Gamma_{i})+(\operatorname{entropy}(\Gamma_{i})).

Proof.

Consider the MCF

M(t):=M+(t+1)v\mathbf{e}_{n}.

By Corollary
7
and Theorem
8
,

\Phi_{m}[\tilde{M}(t)]\leq\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M)+(\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M))

for all
t<0
.
Now
\tilde{M}(-1)=M(-1)=M
, so, in particular,

\Phi_{m}[M]\leq\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M)+(\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M)).

Now let
M^{\prime}
be any surface obtained from
M
by translating and dilating.
Then, by the same argument,

\Phi_{m}[M^{\prime}]\leq\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M^{\prime})+(\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M^{\prime})).

But
\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M^{\prime})=\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M)

and
\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M^{\prime})=\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M)
.
Thus

\Phi_{m}[M^{\prime}]\leq\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M)+(\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M)).

Taking the supremum over all such
M^{\prime}
gives

\operatorname{entropy}(M)\leq\operatorname{mcd}(\partial M)+(\operatorname{entropy}(\partial M)).

The assertion for the case
\partial M=\cup\Gamma_{i}
is proved in the same way.
∎

4.
Slicing

Lemma 12
.

Let
S
be a smooth,
m
-dimensional manifold (possibly with boundary) in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
For
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n-1}
and
y\in\mathbf{R}
, let

\displaystyle S_{x}

\displaystyle:=\{y\in\mathbf{R}:(x,y)\in M\},

\displaystyle S^{y}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n-1}:(x,y)\in M\}.

Let

\displaystyle\Pi:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}^{n-1},

\displaystyle\Pi(x,y)=x\qquad(x\in\mathbf{R}^{n-1},\,y\in\mathbf{R}).

Suppose
f:\mathbf{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbf{R}
and
g:\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}
are smooth, nonnegative functions.
Then

(10)

\displaystyle\int_{(x,y)\in S}f(x)g(y)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x\in\Pi(S)}\left(\sum_{y\in S_{x}}g(y)\right)f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}x

\displaystyle+\int_{y\in\mathbf{R}}\left(\int_{x\in S^{y}}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}x\right)g(y)\,dy.

Proof.

Let

h:(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbf{R}\mapsto y

be the height function.
Then
\nabla_{S}h

is the projection of
\nabla h=\mathbf{e}^{n}
to
\operatorname{Tan}(S,\cdot)
.
Note that the Jacobian of the map
\Pi:S
is
J=\sqrt{1-|\nabla_{M}h|^{2}}
, and thus
that

(11)

J+|\nabla_{S}h|\geq 1.

By the area formula for
\Pi|S
,

(12)

\displaystyle\int_{x\in\Pi(S)}\left(\sum_{y\in S_{x}}g(y)\right)f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\Pi(S)}\left(\sum_{y\in S_{x}}f(x)g(y)\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}x

\displaystyle=\int_{(x,y)\in S}f(x)g(y)J(x,y)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x,y).

Likewise, by the coarea formula for
h|S
,

(13)

\displaystyle\int_{y\in\mathbf{R}}\left(\int_{x\in S^{y}}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}x\right)g(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\mathbf{R}}\int_{x\in S^{y}}f(x)g(y)\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}x\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{(x,y)\in S}f(x)g(y)\,|\nabla_{S}h|\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x,y).

Now add (
12
) and (
13
) and use the inequality (
11
).
∎

Remark 13
.

Note that if
g\leq 1
, then
\sum_{y\in S_{x}}g(y)
is less than or equal to the number of points in
S_{x}
, which is the multiplicity of the projection
\Pi|S
.
Thus (in this case) the first integral on the right hand side in (
10
) is bounded above by
the integral of
f
over
\Pi(S)
, counting multiplicity.

Theorem 14
.

Let
S
be an
m
-dimensional submanifold in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}[S]

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{m}[\Pi|S]+\int_{y\in\mathbf{R}}\Phi_{m-1}[S^{y}]\Phi_{1}(y)\,dy

where

Proof.

5.
The Maximal Density Ratio of a Convex Surface

Proposition 15
.

Let
U
be a bounded, convex, open region in
\mathbf{R}^{m+1}
. Then

\operatorname{mdr}(\partial U)\leq\frac{{(m+1)}\omega_{m+1}}{\omega_{m}}.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbf{R}^{m}
, let
\pi(x)
be the point in
\overline{U}
closest to
x
. Then

|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|\leq|x-y|

for all
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{m}
. Let
Q=\partial\mathbf{B}(x,r)\setminus U
.
Then
\pi
is a distance-decreasing map from
Q
onto
\mathbf{B}(x,r)\cap\partial U
.
Thus

\mathcal{H}^{m}(\partial\mathbf{B}(x,r))\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(Q)\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(\mathbf{B}(x,r)\cap\partial U),

so

\frac{\mathcal{H}^{m}(\mathbf{B}^{m}(x,r)\cap\partial U)}{\omega_{m}r^{m}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{H}^{m}(\partial\mathbf{B}(x,r))}{\omega_{m}r^{m}}=\frac{(m+1)\omega_{m+1}}{\omega_{m}}.

∎

6.
