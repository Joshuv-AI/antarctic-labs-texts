1.
Introduction

By a
permutation
we mean any finite sequence of
distinct
integers. We say that two permutations
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
and
(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
are
similar
if their entries preserve the same relative order, that is,
x_{i}<x_{j}
if and only if
y_{i}<y_{j}
for all pairs
\{i,j\}
with
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k
. This is, clearly, an equivalence relation. Note that given a permutation
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
and a
k
-element set
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\}
of positive integers, there is only one permutation of this set similar to
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
.

Let
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
\pi
be a permutation of
[n]
, called also an
n
-permutation
, and let
r\geq 2
be an integer. A family of
r
pairwise similar and disjoint sub-permutations of
\pi
is called
r
-twins
and the
length of
r
-twins
is defined as the number of elements in just one of the sub-permutations.

Let
t^{(r)}(\pi)
denote the largest integer
k
such that
\pi
contains
r
-twins of length
k
. Further, let
t^{(r)}(n)
denote the minimum of
t^{(r)}(\pi)
over all permutations
\pi
of
[n]
. In other words,
t^{(r)}(n)
is the largest integer
k
such that every
n
-permutation contains
r
-twins of length
k
. Our aim is to estimate this function.

By the classical result of Erdős and Szekeres
[
5
]
concerning monotone subsequences, we get
t^{(r)}(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
. Indeed, in a monotone permutation any
r
disjoint subsequences of the same length yield
r
-twins. For
r=2
, using a probabilistic argument, Gawron
[
7
]
proved that
t^{(2)}(n)=O(n^{2/3})
. He conjectured that this bound is tight, namely, that we also have
t^{(2)}(n)=\Omega(n^{2/3})
. A currently best result towards this conjecture was obtained recently by Bukh and Rudenko
[
2
]
(see also
[
4
]
) who showed that
t^{(2)}(n)=\Omega(n^{3/5})
.

In the first part of this paper, we generalize both these bounds to arbitrary
r\geq 2
, adopting the ideas from
[
2
,
4
,
7
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
r\geq 2
, with
R=\binom{2r-1}{r}
, we have

t^{(r)}(n)=\Omega\left(n^{\frac{R}{2R-1}}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad t^{(r)}(n)=O\left(n^{\frac{r}{2r-1}}\right).

Let
\Pi_{n}
be a (uniformly) random permutation of
[n]
and let
t^{(r)}(\Pi_{n})
be the corresponding random variable equal to the maximum length of
r
-twins in
\Pi_{n}
. We say that a property of a random object holds
asymptotically almost surely
(a.a.s. for short) if it holds with probability tending to one as the size of the object grows to infinity. In
[
4
]
and
[
2
]
it was shown that a.a.s.,
t^{(2)}(\Pi_{n})=\Theta(n^{2/3})
. Here we generalize this result.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
r\geq 2
, a.a.s.,

t^{(r)}(\Pi_{n})=\Theta\left(n^{\frac{r}{2r-1}}\right).

In view of Theorem
1.2
, for almost all permutations
\pi
the parameter
t^{(r)}(\pi)
reaches the upper bound from Theorem
1.1
.

In the following two sections we give proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, while the last section contains some related open problems.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The upper bound in Theorem
1.1
follows from the upper bound in Theorem
1.2
. Therefore, here we focus exclusively on
the proof of the lower bound.

2.1.
Preparations

Beame and Huynh-Ngoc
[
1
]
proved that among any 3 permutations of
[m]
there are two with the same sub-permutation of length at least
m^{1/3}
. This simple fact was used in
[
2
]
to prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
for
r=2
. Here, we will need the following extension proved by Bukh and Zhou
[
3
]
in the context of estimating the length of
r
-twins in words over finite alphabets.
Recall that
R=\binom{2r-1}{r}
.

Lemma 2.1

(Theorem 24 in
[
3
]
)
.

For every
r\geq 2
, among any
2r-1
permutations of
[m]
, there are
r
permutations with the same sub-permutation of length at least
m^{1/R}
.

The main technique behind the proof of the lower bound on
t^{(r)}(n)
is that of
concatenation
of
r
-twins. Here we reveal sufficient conditions under which two
r
-twins can be merged into one. Let
(x^{(j)}_{1},\dots,x^{(j)}_{s})
,
j=1,\dots,r
, and
(y^{(j)}_{1},\dots,y^{(j)}_{t})
,
j=1,\dots,r
, be two disjoint
r
-twins in a permutation
\pi
. Their full concatenation, resulting in
r
-twins of length
s+t
, is possible if

•

the rightmost element of the first
r
-twins is to the left of the leftmost element of the second
r
-twins, and

•

for each
1\leq i\leq s
and
1\leq i^{\prime}\leq t
,

\text{either}\qquad\max_{1\leq j\leq r}y^{(j)}_{i^{\prime}}<\min_{1\leq j\leq r}x^{(j)}_{i}\qquad\text{ or }\qquad\min_{1\leq j\leq r}y^{(j)}_{i^{\prime}}>\max_{1\leq j\leq r}x^{(j)}_{i}.

Example 2.2
.

Let
r=2
,
N=30
, and

\displaystyle\pi=(

\displaystyle\hbox{\pagecolor{cyan}26},16,28,29,\hbox{\pagecolor{cyan}10},5,\hbox{\pagecolor{pink}24},\hbox{\pagecolor{cyan}27},1,22,11,\hbox{\pagecolor{pink}8},2,23,15,

\displaystyle 19,\hbox{\pagecolor{pink}25},21,20,\hbox{\pagecolor{RoyalBlue}13},9,30,\hbox{\pagecolor{RoyalBlue}17},\hbox{\pagecolor{red}12},\hbox{\pagecolor{red}18},7,\hbox{\pagecolor{RoyalBlue}3},14,\hbox{\pagecolor{red}4},6)

be a permutation of
[30]
. Here we marked by blue and pink the first pair of twins of length 3 (similar to
(2,1,3)
), and by indigo and red the second one (similar to
(2,3,1)
). Both above conditions hold. Indeed, all blue and pink elements are to the left of 13, the leftmost indigo or red element. Moreover, all nine required inequalities hold, e.g., for
i=2
,
i^{\prime}=1
, we have
\min\{13,12\}=12>\max\{10,8\}=10
. Thus, their concatenation forms a (bluish-reddish) pair of twins of length six, namely:
(26,10,27,13,17,3)
and
(24,8,25,12,18,4)
, both similar to
(5,2,6,3,4,1)
.

If we only knew the first twins and were after the second ones, we could, obviously, help ourselves by searching only to the right of 25, and by eliminating from our search all elements whose values are “squeezed” between the given twins. (In our example this step excludes only 9, as 25 and 26 are already excluded.) But most importantly, the second pair of twins should be “narrow” in a sense that would guarantee the second bulleted condition above. (In our example, we chose consecutive values:
13,12
and
17,18
and
3,4
.)

To facilitate the idea mentioned in the above example, we introduce the notion of narrow twins as follows. The
width
of a set of integers
A
is defined as
\max A-\min A
. For a positive integer
w
we call
r
-twins
(x^{(1)}_{1},\dots,x^{(1)}_{k}),\dots,(x^{(r)}_{1},\dots,x^{(r)}_{k})

w
-narrow
if for all
i=1,\dots,k
, the sets
\{x^{(j)}_{i}:1\leq j\leq r\}
have width at most
w
.

Throughout the paper we will sometimes use notation
[a,b]=\{a,\dots,b\}
.

2.2.
Lower bound

The proof of the lower bound is similar to that for
r=2
in
[
2
, Proof of Theorem 1]
. The main idea is to utilize Lemma
2.1
in a clever way.

Set

M=n^{(R-1)/(2R-1)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad N=n/M=n^{R/(2R-1)}.

Our goal is, for a given permutation
\pi
of
[n]
, to gradually pick from it
O(M)
-narrow
r
-twins of length
\Omega(N/M)
, and remove them together with all “in-between” elements (plus some more), allowing concatenation of obtained pieces into one set of
r
-twins of length
\Omega(N)
. To achieve this goal, we need to iterate this procedure
\Omega(M)
times, meaning that the number of discarded each time elements should not exceed
O(N)
. Now come the details.

Let
A\subset\binom{[n]}{(2r-1)N}
,
A=\{a_{1}<a_{2}\cdots<a_{(2r-1)N}\}
, and let
\pi
be a permutation of
A
. For any
a\in A
we denote by
\pi^{-1}(a)
the position of
a
in
\pi
. E.g., if
A=\{1,3,4,6\}
and
\pi=(3,1,6,4)
, we have
\pi^{-1}(1)=2
,
\pi^{-1}(3)=1
,
\pi^{-1}(4)=4
and
\pi^{-1}(6)=3
.

We split the set
A
into
N
blocks of
2r-1
consecutive elements:

A=A_{0}\cup\cdots\cup A_{N-1},\quad\mbox{where}\quad A_{i}=\{a_{i(2r-1)+1},\dots,a_{(i+1)(2r-1)}\},\quad i=0,\dots,N-1.

A crucial observation is that if, for some
2\leq k\leq N
and
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq 2r-1
, there exists a sequence of
distinct
indices
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}^{k}
such that

\pi^{-1}(a_{i_{1}(2r-1)+j_{1}})<\cdots<\pi^{-1}(a_{i_{k}(2r-1)+j_{1}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\pi^{-1}(a_{i_{1}(2r-1)+j_{2}})<\cdots<\pi^{-1}(a_{i_{k}(2r-1)+j_{2}}),

then sub-permutations
(a_{i_{1}(2r-1)+j_{1}},\dots,a_{i_{k}(2r-1)+j_{1}})
and
(a_{i_{1}(2r-1)+j_{2}},\dots,a_{i_{k}(2r-1)+j_{2}})
of
\pi
form 2-twins of length
k
. By the same token, if all the above is true for
r
indices
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{r}\leq 2r-1
, then we obtain
r
-twins of length
k
in
\pi
.

.

Let
A=[30]
,
r=3
,
N=6
, and, same as before,

\displaystyle\pi=(

.

, and

(5,3,0,2,4,1)
,
(5,4,0,3,2,1)
,

, etc. Now, what we are after, are long common sub-permutations in at least three of these permutations. We found

, so the width of each subset

was the same (and equal to 4), but in general this may not be the case. To make sure that the

-twins generated by the above approach are

-narrow, we focus only on the subsets
A_{i}
of width at most, say,
2M
.
Clearly, as
A\subset[n]
, at least
N/2
of these sets have, indeed, the width at most
2n/N=2M
. Let

I=\{0\leq i\leq N-1:\;A_{i}\text{ has width at most }2M\}.

So,
|I|\geq N/2
. Next, define
2r-1

\pi^{(j)}
of

\bar{\pi}^{(j)}

.

,

, and, as a result, find

of them with a common sub-permutation of length at least

.

\bar{\pi}^{(j_{1})},\dots,\bar{\pi}^{(j_{r})}
, where

, be these

be the set of the elements of a common sub-permutation in
\bar{\pi}^{(j_{1})},\dots,\bar{\pi}^{(j_{r})}
of length
k=|I_{0}|=\lceil 2^{-1/3}\tfrac{N}{M}\rceil
. Upon returning to permutations
\pi^{(j_{1})},\dots,\pi^{(j_{r})}
, it can be seen, guided by the above example, that

generates in their union, and thus in
\pi
,
2M
-narrow
r
-twins of length
k
.

It remains to incorporate the above procedure into an iteration loop and make sure that obtained
r
-twins can be concatenated into a long one.
Let
\pi
be a permutation of
[n]
. In the first step of the procedure, take
A^{1}
to be the set of first
(2r-1)N
elements of
\pi
and let
\pi^{1}
be the sub-permutation of
\pi
consisting of the first
|A^{1}|
elements. By the above described argument we find
2M
-narrow
r
-twins
(x_{1}^{(j)},\dots,x_{k}^{(j)})
,
j=1,\dots,r
of length
k
in
\pi^{1}
. We then remove from
[n]
the set

A^{1}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}[\min_{1\leq j\leq r}x_{i}^{(j)},\min_{1\leq j\leq r}x_{i}^{(j)}+2M-1].

Note that this set has size at most
(2r-1)N+k(2M)\leq 2rN
.

Now, we consider the truncation of
\pi
to the remaining elements and the set
A^{2}
of its leftmost
(2r-1)N

2M
-narrow

-twins
(y_{1}^{(1)},\dots,y_{k}^{(r)})

, spelled out in Subsection

. This procedure can be continued as long as there are at least

times, yielding at the conclusion

-twins in

of length a least

\frac{M}{2r}\times k\geq\frac{M}{2r}\times 2^{-1/3}\frac{N}{M}\geq\frac{N}{3r}.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

3.1.
Upper bound

For the upper bound we use the first moment method. Let
\Pi=\Pi_{n}
be a random permutation chosen uniformly from the set of all
n!
permutations of
[n]
. Let
k
be a fixed positive integer and let
X
be a random variable counting all
r
-twins of length
k
in
\Pi
. Furthermore, for a family of
r
pairwise disjoint subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
of
[n]
, each of length
k
, let
X_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}
be an indicator random variable equal 1 if there are
r
-twins in
\Pi
on positions determined by the subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
. So,
X=\sum_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}X_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}
and by the linearity of expectation

{\mathds{E}}X=\sum_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}{\mathds{E}}X_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}=\sum_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}{\mathds{P}}(X_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}=1).

Since

(3.1)

{\mathds{P}}(X_{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}}=1)=\frac{\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{k}\cdots\binom{n-(r-2)k}{k}\cdot(n-(r-1)k)!\cdot 1}{n!}=\frac{1}{k!^{r-1}}

and the number of unordered
r
-tuples
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}\}
in
[n]
is

\binom{n}{rk}\frac{(rk)!}{k!^{r}r!}=\frac{n!}{k!^{r}r!(n-rk)!},

it follows that

{\mathds{E}}X=\frac{n!}{k!^{r}r!(n-rk)!}\times\frac{1}{k!^{r-1}}=\frac{n(n-1)\dots(n-rk+1)}{r!(k!)^{2r-1}}.

Using the inequality
k!>\frac{k^{k}}{e^{k}}
we obtain

(3.2)

{\mathds{E}}X<\frac{n^{rk}e^{(2r-1)k}}{r!k^{(2r-1)k}}<\left(\frac{n^{r}e^{2r-1}}{k^{2r-1}}\right)^{k}.

It follows that for
k\geq 2en^{r/(2r-1)}
,

{\mathds{P}}(X\geq 1)\leq{\mathds{E}}X<2^{-(2r-1)k}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
This completes the proof of the upper bound.

3.2.
Lower bound

Set

(3.3)

a=r!^{1/(2r-1)}n^{(r-1)/(2r-1)},

assume for convenience that
a
is an integer and divides
n
, and partition
[n]
into
n/a
consecutive blocks of equal size, that is, set

[n]=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n/a},

where
A_{1}=\{1,\dots,a\}
,
A_{2}=\{a+1,\dots,2a\}
, etc.

For fixed
1\leq i,j\leq n/a
,
let
X=X_{ij}
be the number of elements from the set
A_{j}
which
\Pi
puts on the positions belonging to the set
A_{i}
. We construct an auxiliary
n/a\times n/a
bipartite graph
B
with vertex classes
U=\{1,\dots,n/a\}
and
V=\{1,\dots,{n/a}\}
, where
ij\in B
if, and only if,
X_{ij}\geq r
.

Let
M=\{i_{1}j_{1},\dots,i_{m}j_{m}\}
,
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}
, be a matching in
B
of size
|M|=m
. For every
ij\in M
, let
s^{(i)}_{1},s^{(i)}_{2},\dots,s^{(i)}_{r}
be some
r
elements of
A_{i}
such that
\Pi(s^{(i)}_{1}),\dots,\Pi(s^{(i)}_{r})\in A_{j}
. Then, sub-permutations

(\Pi(s^{(i_{1})}_{1}),\dots,\Pi(s^{(i_{m})}_{1})),\ (\Pi(s^{(i_{1})}_{2}),\dots,\Pi(s^{(i_{m})}_{2})),\dots,\;(\Pi(s^{(i_{1})}_{r}),\dots,\Pi(s^{(i_{m})}_{r}))

form
r
-twins. Indeed, if, say,
\Pi(s^{(i_{1})}_{1})<\Pi(s^{(i_{2})}_{1})
, then
j_{1}<j_{2}
, and so,
\Pi(s^{(i_{1})}_{t})<\Pi(s^{(i_{2})}_{t})
for each
t=2,\dots,r
, as
\Pi(s^{(i_{1})}_{t})\in A_{j_{1}}
, while
\Pi(s^{(i_{2})}_{t})\in A_{j_{2}}
.

Hence, it remains to show that a.a.s. there is a matching in
B
of size
\Omega\left(n^{r/(2r-1)}\right)
. To this end, we are going to use the obvious fact, coming from the greedy algorithm, that in every graph
G
there is a matching of size at least

|E(G)|/(2\Delta(G))
,
where
\Delta(G)
is the maximum vertex degree in
G
.

We apply this bound, actually, to a suitably chosen subgraph
B^{\prime}
of
B
.
Let
\nu(B)
be the size of a largest matching in
B
. Note that
\Delta(B)\leq a/r
. Further, let
Z_{k}
be the number of vertices of degree
k
in
B
,
k=0,\dots,a/r
. Then, for the subgraph
B^{\prime}
of
B
obtained by deleting all vertices of degree at least
7
, we get

|E(B^{\prime})|\geq|E(B)|-\sum_{k=7}^{a/r}kZ_{k}

and

(3.4)

\nu(B)\geq\nu(B^{\prime})\geq\frac{|E(B^{\prime})|}{2\Delta(B^{\prime})}\geq\frac{|E(B)|-\sum_{k=7}^{a/r}kZ_{k}}{12}.

Further, for each
i\in U\cup V
, let

Y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n/a}\mathbb{I}(X_{ij}\geq r)

be the degree of vertex
i
in
B
. Then,

|E(B)|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in U}{\mathds{E}}Y_{i}

and

Z_{k}=\sum_{i\in U\cup V}\mathbb{I}(Y_{i}=k),

and so

{\mathds{E}}[|E(B)|]=\frac{n}{a}{\mathds{E}}Y_{1}

and

{\mathds{E}}Z_{k}=\frac{2n}{a}{\mathds{P}}(Y_{1}=k).

Taking the expectation on the outer sides of (
3.4
), we arrive at

(3.5)

{\mathds{E}}[\nu(B)]\geq\frac{n}{12a}\left({\mathds{E}}Y_{1}-2\sum_{k=7}^{a/r}k{\mathds{P}}(Y_{1}=k)\right)

and it remains to estimate
{\mathds{E}}Y_{1}
and
{\mathds{P}}(Y_{1}=k)
.

We have

{\mathds{E}}Y_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n/a}{\mathds{P}}(X_{1j}\geq r)\geq\frac{n}{a}{\mathds{P}}(X_{11}=r)=\frac{n}{a}\cdot\frac{\binom{n-a}{a-r}\binom{a}{r}^{2}r!(a-r)!(n-a)!}{n!}\sim\frac{a^{2r-1}}{r!n^{r-1}}\geq\frac{1}{2},

for sufficiently large
n
, as
a^{2}=o(n)
and, by (
3.3
),
\tfrac{a^{2r-1}}{n^{r-1}}=r!
.

Now we estimate
{\mathds{P}}(Y_{1}=k)
for
k\in\{7,\dots,a/r\}
. Observe that, by using inequalities
\binom{m}{p}\leq\frac{m^{p}}{p!}
and
\tfrac{n}{n-rk}\leq\exp\{rk/(n-rk)\}
,

{\mathds{P}}(Y_{1}=k)\leq\binom{\frac{n}{a}}{k}\binom{a}{r}^{k}\binom{a}{rk}\frac{(rk)!(n-rk)!}{n!}\leq\frac{a^{2rk-k}e^{(rk)^{2}/n}}{r!^{k}n^{rk-k}k!}.

Consequently, since
(rk)^{2}\leq a^{2}=o(n)
,
k-1\geq 6
,
(k-1)!>\left(\tfrac{k-1}{3}\right)^{k-1}
, and by (
3.3
),

\displaystyle k{\mathds{P}}(Y_{1}=k)\leq\frac{a^{2rk-k}(1+o(1))}{r!^{k}n^{rk-k}(k-1)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2a^{2rk-k}}{r!^{k}n^{rk-k}((k-1)/3)^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2a^{2r-1}}{r!n^{r-1}}\left(\frac{3a^{2r-1}}{r!(k-1)n^{r-1}}\right)^{k-1}\leq 4\cdot 2^{-k}.

Thus,

\sum_{k=7}^{a/r}k{\mathds{P}}(Y_{1}=k)\leq 4\sum_{k=7}^{\infty}2^{-k}=\frac{1}{16}.

Returning to (
3.5
), we conclude that

{\mathds{E}}[\nu(B)]\geq\frac{n}{12a}\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\cdot\frac{1}{16}\right)=\frac{1}{32}\cdot\frac{n}{a}=\Theta\left(n^{r/(2r-1)}\right).

Since
t(\Pi_{n})\geq\nu(B)
, to complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
it remains to show that the random variable
h(\Pi)=\nu(B)
is highly concentrated about its mean.
To this end, we are going to use the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality for random permutations (see, e.g., Lemma 11 in
[
6
]
or Section 3.2 in
[
9
]
):

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
h(\pi)
be a function of
n
-permutations such that, for some constant
c>0
, if permutation
\pi_{2}
is obtained from permutation
\pi_{1}
by swapping two elements, then
|h(\pi_{1})-h(\pi_{2})|\leq c
.
Then, for every
\eta>0
,

{\mathds{P}}(|h(\Pi_{n})-{\mathds{E}}[h(\Pi_{n})]|\geq\eta)\leq 2\exp(-\eta^{2}/(2c^{2}n)).

To apply it to
h(\Pi)=\nu(B)
, first note that if
\pi_{2}
is obtained from a permutation
\pi_{1}
by swapping some two of its elements, then at most two edges of
B
can be destroyed, and at most two edges can be created, so
|h(\pi_{1})-h(\pi_{2})|\leq 2
.
Consequently, Theorem
3.1
applied with
c=2
and
\eta=n^{2r/(4r-1)}
implies that

{\mathds{P}}\left(|h(\Pi_{n})-{\mathds{E}}[h(\Pi_{n})]|\geq n^{2r/(4r-1)}\right)=o(1).

As, crucially,
n^{2r/(4r-1)}=o(n^{r/(2r-1)})
, this completes the proof of the lower bound in Theorem
1.2
.

4.
Concluding Remarks

We conclude the paper with two conjectures. The first one is a natural generalization of Gawron’s conjecture
[
7
]
concerning the asymptotic order of the function
t^{(2)}(n)
.

Conjecture 4.1
.

For every
r\geqslant 2
, we have
t^{(r)}(n)=\Theta(n^{r/(2r-1)})
.

This means that we believe that every permutation contains
r
-twins of length
\Omega(n^{r/(2r-1)})
. While this statement is wide open for every
r\geqslant 2
, notice that, as
r
tends to
\infty
, the difference of the exponents in the upper and lower bounds in Theorem
1.1
tends to 0 (since both converge to
1/2
).

Throughout the paper we have kept
r
fixed, that is, independent of
n
. However, in the next conjecture we allow
r=r(n)
.
We call
r
-twins of length
r
an
r
-square
.
The question is how large a square is contained in every permutation.
Let
s(\pi)
denote the largest integer
r
such that
\pi
contains an
r
-square and let
s(n)
be the minimum of
s(\pi)
over all
n
-permutations.

Conjecture 4.2
.

We have
n-(s(n))^{2}=o(n)
.

In other words, we believe that every permutation is almost entirely filled by a square.
Notice that a repeated application of the Erdős-Szekeres Theorem (
[
5
]
) implies that every
n
-permutation contains an
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
-square. Clearly, the components of this square are monotone. It is, therefore, natural to expect even larger squares with components of an arbitrary pattern.

Finally, let us mention that in
[
4
]
we devoted a subsection to pairs of twins with forbidden patterns. For a given permutation
\tau
we say that a permutation
\pi
is
\tau
-free if no subsequence of
\pi
is similar to
\tau
. Let
t^{(r)}(n,\tau)
denote the longest length of
\tau
-free
r
-twins guaranteed in every
n
-permutation. It turns out that, regardless of
r
, the threshold
t^{(r)}(n,\tau)=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
remains valid for any non-monotone pattern
\tau
.
