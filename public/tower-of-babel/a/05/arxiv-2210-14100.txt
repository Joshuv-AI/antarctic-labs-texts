The Capacity of a Finite Field Matrix Channel
Simon R. Blackburn, Jessica Claridge

Abstract
The Additive-Multiplicative Matrix Channel (AMMC) was introduced by Silva, Kschischang and Kötter in 2010 to model data transmission using random linear network coding. The input and output of the channel are n × m n\times m matrices over a finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} . When the matrix X X is input, the channel outputs Y = A ⁡ ( X + W ) Y=A(X+W) where A A is a uniformly chosen n × n n\times n invertible matrix over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} and where W W is a uniformly chosen n × m n\times m matrix over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} of rank t t . Silva et al. considered the case when 2 ​ n ≤ m 2n\leq m . They determined the asymptotic capacity of the AMMC when t t , n n and m m are fixed and q → ∞ q\rightarrow\infty . They also determined the leading term of the capacity when q q is fixed, and t t , n n and m m grow linearly. We generalise these results, showing that the condition 2 ​ n ≥ m 2n\geq m can be removed. (Our formula for the capacity falls into two cases, one of which generalises the 2 ​ n ≥ m 2n\geq m case.) We also improve the error term in the case when q q is fixed.

Random network coding; matrix channels; channel capacity.

Alternative finite-field matrix channels, related to the AMMC, have been proposed to model RLNC. Nóbrega, Silva and Uchôa-Filho

[

]
and Feng, Nóbrega, Kschischang and Silva

[

[

]

What is the capacity of the AMMC? As is traditional for channels using matrices over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we take logarithms to the base
q
when measuring capacity. (So to compute the capacity in bits, the more usual measure, we must multiply by
\log_{2}q
.) Silva et al. gave good upper and lower bounds for the capacity of the AMMC when
2n\leq m
. In particular, when
n
,
m
and
t
are fixed with
2n\leq m
, and when
q\rightarrow\infty
, their paper establishes
[
16
, Corollary 10]
that the capacity tends to
(m-n)(n-t)
when logarithms are taken to the base
q
. Moreover, Silva et al. also provide the leading term for the capacity (of the same form) when
q
is fixed and
m
,
n
and
t
grow linearly.
Our paper aims to generalise these results, by dropping the restriction that
2n\leq m
. We will show (in both asymptotic regimes considered in
[
16
]
) that the formula for the capacity involves two cases, depending on whether
2n\leq m+t
or not. Indeed, we show that the capacity is approximately
(m-n)(n-t)
when
2n\leq m+t
(generalising the situation considered by Silva et al.) and the capacity is approximately
(m-t)^{2}/4
when
2n>m+t
. See Theorems
10
and
14
below for precise statements. (Note that when
m+t\geq 2n
we must have

, so the formula for the capacity is never negative!) We mention that the error term in Theorem

is a significant improvement on the corresponding statement in
[
16
]
. These results confirm a conjectured formula for the channel capacity due to Blackburn and Claridge
[
3

.

Our proofs of Theorems

and

reduce the computation of the AMMC capacity to finding the capacity of a channel we call the

A natural, and practical, variant of the AMMC defines

to be a matrix of rank at most

2
Overview

We give an intuitive (and non-rigorous) justification for the AMMC capacity, as a guide to the proofs in the sections which follow.

We first consider the
k
-AMMC, so the input to the channel is constrained to matrices of rank
k
. We will show that the capacity achieving input distribution is uniform on such matrices. Note that multiplying by
A
replaces the matrix
X+W
with a random matrix having the same row space. Moreover, we can show that the output distribution of the AMMC only depends on the row space of
X
,
\mathrm{row}(X)
, rather than
X
itself. So the channel can be thought of as having row spaces (of
X
and
X+W
respectively) as input and output: the receiver is given
\mathrm{row}(Y)=\mathrm{row}(X+W)
, and must deduce information about
\mathrm{row}(X)
from this. (But note that we cannot avoid considering matrices entirely, as the noise of the channel is defined using matrix addition rather than an operation on subspaces.)

Now
\mathrm{row}(Y)\subseteq\mathrm{row}(X)+\mathrm{row}(W)
. We would expect
\mathrm{row}(X)
and
\mathrm{row}(W)
to have no special relationship to each other (as
\mathrm{row}(W)
is uniformly and independently chosen of dimension
t
), so we expect the dimension of
\mathrm{row}(X)+\mathrm{row}(W)
to be close to
\min\{m,k+t\}
. Hence
\mathrm{rk}(Y)=\dim(\mathrm{row}(Y))\leq\min\{m,k+t\}
. Since
\mathrm{row}(Y)
is the row space of an
n\times m
matrix, we must have
\mathrm{rk}(Y)\leq\min\{n,m\}
. We show that
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
is usually close to being as large as possible given these constraints. So we show that with high probability,

\mathrm{rk}(Y)\approx\min\{m,n,k+t\}=\min\{n,k+t-\delta\},

where
\delta=\max\{0,k+t-m\}
.
We will also determine the approximate dimension of
\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y)
:

\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))\approx\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}

with high probability.
The proof that
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
and
\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))
can be approximately determined (see Lemmas
8
and
12
) is the most difficult part of the argument.

The receiver initially knows nothing of
\mathrm{row}(X)
, so the entropy of
\mathrm{row}(X)
is equal to the logarithm to the base
q
of the number of dimension
k
subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
. Once the receiver obtains
Y
, the entropy of
\mathrm{row}(X)
is reduced, as
\mathrm{row}(X)
is likely to have an intersection of dimension approximately
\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}
with the (known) subspace
\mathrm{row}(Y)
of dimension approximately
\min\{n,k+t-\delta\}
. Counting these subspaces allows us to determine the capacity of the
k
-AMMC, by taking logarithms to the base
q
.

We then show that the capacity of the AMMC is essentially the maximum of the capacities of the
k
-AMMC as
k
varies (because there are few possibilities for
k
). When
k
is large (more precisely, when
k+t>\min\{n,m\}
) the receiver does not obtain much information about
\mathrm{row}(X)
, as this subspace does not normally intersect
\mathrm{row}(Y)
in a space of large dimension. We show that it is never advantageous to choose a large value of
k
. When
k+t\leq\min\{m,n\}
, we have that
\mathrm{rk}(X+W)=k+t
with non-trivial probability, and
\mathrm{row}(Y)=\mathrm{row}(X+W)=\mathrm{row}(X)+\mathrm{row}(W)
. Thus the receiver is given a subspace
\mathrm{row}(Y)
of dimension
k+t
, and knows that
\mathrm{row}(X)
lies in this subspace. Our formula for the capacity of the channel is approximately
(m-t-k)k
in this case. The maximum value of this formula is at
k=(m-t)/2
, which always lies in the range
0\leq k\leq m-t
. So the best value for
k
depends on whether
(m-t)/2\leq n-t
(when we take
k\approx(m-t)/2
) or
(m-t)/2>n-t
(when we take
k=n-t
). This is why the formula for the capacity of the AMMC splits into two cases.

The
subspace distance

d(U,V)
between two subspaces
U
and
V
is defined to be
\dim(U)+\dim(V)-2\dim(U\cap V)
. This notion was introduced by Kötter and Kschischang
[
9
]
in their famous 2008 paper on random network coding, and channels with transition probabilities based on subspace distance have been much studied. We remark that the transition probabilities of the AMMC do not depend only on the subspace distance between the row spaces of input and output matrices of the channel, because we would expect an output matrix
Y
to have larger rank than the input
X
, and there are matrices
Y^{\prime}
of smaller rank such that
d(X,Y)=d(X,Y^{\prime})
. Nevertheless, our results on the expected values of
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
and
\dim(X\cap Y)
above can be interpreted as saying that the subspace distance between the row spaces of input and output matrices in the
k
-AMMC is (approximately) constant with high probability, in the two asymptotic regimes we consider. So the
k
-AMMC may be asymptotically approximated by a channel where the subspace distance between input and output row spaces is bounded. (More precisely, setting
\delta=\max\{0,k+t-m\}
, the
k
-AMMC may be asymptotically approximated by an operator channel
[
9
]
with
\rho
erasures and
\tau
errors, where
\rho=k-\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}
and
\tau=\min\{n,k+t-\delta\}-\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}
.) This allows subspace decoding techniques to be used in these asymptotic regimes. (See Kschischang
[
10
]
or Kurz
[
11
]
for recent surveys of subspace codes.) Moreover, because the likely subspace distance between input and output decreases (possibly not strictly) as
t
decreases, subspace decoding can be used for relatives of the
k
-AMMC where the rank of
t
varies, with an upper bound
t
: this is, of course, a very natural class of channels in network coding applications.

Write

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\in U
are such that
\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle=U
. Corollary
2
implies that
c\rightarrow 1
as
q\rightarrow\infty
. So

\displaystyle\log_{q}\rho(n,m,k)

\displaystyle=\log_{q}\left(\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m\,}{k}_{q}\right)cq^{kn}

\displaystyle=\log_{q}\left(\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m\,}{k}_{q}\right)+\log_{q}c+nk

\displaystyle\rightarrow(m-k)k+0+nk\text{ by~\eqref{eqn:log_qbinomial}}

\displaystyle=(n+m-k)k,

as required.
∎

4
The
k
-dimensional AMMC

For a fixed non-negative integer
k
such that
k\leq\min\{n,m\}
we define the
rank
k
input additive-multiplicative matrix channel,
and write

-AMMC, to be the AMMC channel with input matrices

restricted to having rank
k
. This section determines the asymptotic capacity of this channel when
q\rightarrow\infty
with

and

fixed.

Lemma 5
.

The capacity of the
k
-AMMC is achieved when the input distribution
\mathbf{X}
is uniform over all
n\times m
matrices
X
of rank
k
.

Note that, throughout this paper, we use a bold letter to denote a random object (here
\mathbf{X}
is a distribution over matrices, in other words a random matrix), and the corresponding non-bold letter (here
X
) to denote a possible outcome of this random process.

Proof.

The lemma is a special case of
[
3
, Theorem 1]
. As an alternative, and more direct, proof we may show that the
k
-AMMC is symmetric in the sense of Gallager
[
7
, Chapter 4]
: we may partition the transition matrix using subsets of columns so that each part of our partition is a matrix whose rows are permutations of each other and whose columns are permutations of each other. In our case, we partition by the rank of the output matrix
Y
. (To prove that the
k
-AMMC is symmetric, we first show that
\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{Y}=Y|\mathbf{X}=X)=\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{Y}=NYM|\mathbf{X}=NXM)
for any invertible
n\times n
and
m\times m
matrices
N
and
M
respectively. We then use the fact, twice, that if two
n\times m
matrices
A
and
B
have the same rank, there exist an invertible
n\times n
matrix
N
and an invertible
m\times m
matrix
M
so that
NAM=B
.) Once we have shown that the
k
-AMMC is symmetric, the lemma follows by
[
7
, Theorem 4.5.2]
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
n
,
m
,
k
and
t
be fixed non-negative integers, with
k\leq\min\{n,m\}
and
t\leq\min\{m,n\}
. Consider the
k
-AMMC with uniform input distribution
\mathbf{X}
and corresponding output distribution
\mathbf{Y}
. For matrices
X
and

, the probability
\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{X}=X|\mathbf{Y}=Y)
depends only on
\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))
and
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
.

Proof.

For
n\times m
matrices
E
and
F
, define
\Omega(E,F)
to be the set of pairs
(W,A)
where
W
is an
n\times m
matrix of rank
t
,
A
is an invertible
n\times n
matrix, and
F=A(E+W)
. Note that, since
\mathbf{X}
is uniform and the matrices
A
and
W
in the
k
-AMMC are chosen uniformly and independently, for any
n\times m
matrices
X
and
Y

\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{X}=X\text{ and }\mathbf{Y}=Y)=\kappa|\Omega(X,Y)|

(5)

where
\kappa
is a constant depending only on
n
,
m
and
t
. Indeed, in the notation of Lemma
4
, we have
\kappa=1/(\rho(n,m,k)\rho(n,n,n)\rho(n,m,t))
.

Let
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
be fixed
n\times m
matrices of rank
r
. Let
s
be a positive integer, and let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be two
n\times m
matrices of rank
k
such that

\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))=\dim(\mathrm{row}(X^{\prime})\cap\mathrm{row}(Y^{\prime}))=s.

We aim to show that
|\Omega(X,Y)|=|\Omega(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})|
. This equality implies that
|\Omega(X,Y)|
depends only on

and

.

Choose a basis
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{r}
for
\mathrm{row}(Y)
such that
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{s}
is a basis for
\mathrm{row}(Y)\cap\mathrm{row}(X)
. Extend this basis to a basis
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{r},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{m-r}
for
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
in such a way that
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{s},\allowbreak x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k-s}
is a basis for
\mathrm{row}(X)
. Construct a similar basis
y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,y^{\prime}_{r},\allowbreak x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots x^{\prime}_{m-r}
corresponding to
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
. So the vectors
y^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,y^{\prime}_{s}
form a basis for
\mathrm{row}(Y^{\prime})\cap\mathrm{row}(X^{\prime})
, the vectors
y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,y^{\prime}_{r}
form a basis for
\mathrm{row}(Y^{\prime})
, and the vectors
y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,y^{\prime}_{s},\allowbreak x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k-s}
form a basis for
\mathrm{row}(X^{\prime})
. Let

be the unique (invertible)

matrix such that

and

. Note that right-multiplication by

\mathrm{row}(Y^{\prime})

\mathrm{row}(X)

\mathrm{row}(X^{\prime})

\mathrm{row}(YM)=\mathrm{row}(Y^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{row}(XM)=\mathrm{row}(X^{\prime})
. Since

and

have the same row space, there exists an invertible
n\times n
matrix

such that

. Since
\mathrm{row}(Y^{\prime})=\mathrm{row}(YM)=\mathrm{row}(NYM)

. Define a map

\phi((W,A))=(NWM,KNAN^{-1})
. The map is a well defined bijection since the following equations are equivalent for matrices

and

\displaystyle A(X+W)=Y

\displaystyle KNA(X+W)M=KNYM

\displaystyle KNAN^{-1}(NXM+NWM)=Y^{\prime}

\displaystyle KNAN^{-1}(X^{\prime}+NWM)=Y^{\prime}

So
|\Omega(X,Y)|=|\Omega(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})|

Since
|\Omega(X,Y)|
depends only on
\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))
and
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
, we see from (

) that the same is true for
\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{X}=X\text{ and }\mathbf{Y}=Y)
. Moreover, writing

\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{Y}=Y)=\sum_{E}\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{X}=E\text{ and }\mathbf{Y}=Y),

where the sum is over all

matrices

, we see that

depends only on
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
, since the number of rank

matrices

fixed only depends on
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
. Hence
\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{X}=X|\mathbf{Y}=Y)
depends only on
\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))
and
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
, as required.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
n
,
m
,
k
and
t
be fixed non-negative integers, with
k\leq\min\{n,m\}
and
t\leq\min\{m,n\}
.
Let
U
and

be sampled uniformly and independently at random from the set of all subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
of dimension

and dimension

respectively.
Define

. Then
\dim(U\cap V)=\delta
with probability tending to
1
as

.

Proof.

Suppose first that
k+t\leq m
, and so
\delta=0
. For any fixed subspace
U
, the proportion of subspaces
V
that intersect

trivially is

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m-k\,}{t}_{q}q^{tk}\,/\,\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m\,}{t}_{q},

which tends to

as

. So the lemma follows in this case.

Now suppose

, the proportion of subspaces

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,k\,}{k+t-m}_{q}q^{(m-k)(k-(k+t-m))}\,/\,\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m\,}{t}_{q},

as

. So the lemma follows.

Lemma 8
.

Let
n
,
m
,
k
and
t
be fixed non-negative integers, with
k\leq\min\{n,m\}
and
t\leq\min\{m,n\}
. Define
\delta=\max\{0,k+t-m\}
. Let
X
and
W
be
n\times m
matrices, chosen uniformly and independently at random subject to
\mathrm{rk}(X)=k

\mathrm{rk}(W)=t
. Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(Y)=\min\{t+k,m,n\},\text{ and }

(6)

\displaystyle\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))=\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}.

(7)

We note that the conditions (

of

Choose subspaces

of

, where

as

. So to prove the lemma, it suffices to fix

and

and

as

.

Fix subspaces
U
and
V
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}

,
\dim(V)=t
respectively and
\dim(U\cap V)=\delta
. We generate two

matrices

and

to be the matrix whose

th row is

, and we set

to be the matrix whose
i
th row is

. Let

, the matrix whose
i
th row is

. Now
\mathrm{row}(X)\subseteq U
with equality if and only if the vectors

span

\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-q^{-j}),

since this expression is the probability that the first

\mathrm{row}(W)=V
. Since the event that
\mathrm{row}(X)=U

, the matrices

and

are uniformly and independently chosen such that

\mathrm{row}(W)=V
. So we are interested in the probability that the conditions (

) and (

The quotient space

Theorem 9
.

Let
n
,
m
,
k
and
t
be fixed non-negative integers, with

and

. Define integers

,

and

\delta=\max\{0,k+t-m\}
,

-AMMC (where we take logarithms to the base

(m+s-k-r)s

.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a capacity achieving input distribution to the
k

\mathbf{Y}
be the corresponding output distribution. Lemma
5
shows that we may take
\mathbf{X}
to be the uniform distribution on the

matrices of rank

. We need to calculate
I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})=H(\mathbf{X})-H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})

, by Lemma

, since
\mathbf{X}
is uniform. We now provide a value for the limit of

.

By Lemma

,

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{\min\{n,m\}}\sum_{s=0}^{\min\{r,k\}}\big(\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{rk}(Y)=r,\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))=s))\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{Y})=r,\dim(\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{Y})\cap\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{X}))=s)\big)

\displaystyle\geq p\,H\big(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{Y})=\min\{t+k,m,n\},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\dim(\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{Y})\cap\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{X}))=\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}\big),

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\rightarrow H\big(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{Y})=\min\{t+k,m,n\},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\dim(\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{Y})\cap\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{X}))=\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}\big).

5
The AMMC

We are now in a position to establish the capacity of the AMMC when
q\rightarrow\infty
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
n
,
m
and
t
be fixed non-negative integers such that
t\leq\min\{m,n\}
. When
m+t\geq 2n
, the capacity of the AMMC tends to

(m-n)(n-t)
as
q\rightarrow\infty
. Otherwise, the capacity of thfe AMMC tends to
\lceil(m-t)/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor(m-t)/2\rfloor
as
q
tends to
\infty
. (We take logarithms to the base
q
when computing capacity.)

Proof.

We need to find the maximal mutual information
I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})
over all input distributions
\mathbf{X}
to the AMMC, where
\mathbf{Y}
is the corresponding output distribution.

Let
c
be the maximum capacity of the
k
-AMMC, as
k
ranges over the set
\{0,1,\ldots,\min\{n,m\}\}
. If
\mathbf{X}
is a capacity achieving distribution for the
k
-AMMC, then we may use this input distribution to the AMMC. So the capacity of the
k
-AMMC is a lower bound for the capacity of the AMMC. In particular,
c
is a lower bound for the capacity of the AMMC.

We aim to derive an upper bound for the capacity of the AMMC, also involving
c
. Let
\mathbf{X}
be an input distribution to the AMMC and
\mathbf{Y}
the corresponding output distribution. The data processing inequality implies that

I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))\geq I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})
,
and so using the chain rule we find

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\leq I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))

\displaystyle=I(\mathbf{X};\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))+I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y}|\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))

\displaystyle=H(\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))+I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y}|\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))

\displaystyle\leq\log_{q}(\min\{n,m\}+1)+I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y}|\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X})),

(10)

since
\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X})
is a random variable with support at most
\min\{n,m\}+1
.

But
c
is an upper bound for
I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y}|\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))
, since

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}|\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{m,n\}}\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X})=k)\cdot I(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}|\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X})=k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{m,n\}}\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{X})=k)\cdot c=c.

(11)

The inequalities (
10
) and (
11
) combine to show that the capacity of the AMMC is bounded above by
c+\log_{q}\min\{n,m\}
. Since
\log_{q}\min\{n,m\}\rightarrow 0
as
q\rightarrow\infty
, and since we have a lower bound of
c
on the capacity, we see that the capacity of the AMMC tends to
c
as
q\rightarrow\infty
. So to prove the theorem, it suffices to determine the value of
c
.

Define
\delta
,
r
and
s
as in Theorem
9
. Suppose first that
k
is such that
k+t>m
and so
\delta=(k+t)-m
. We claim that the capacity of the
k
-AMMC tends to
0
in this situation. To prove this claim, assume first that
m\leq n
. Then
r=\min\{k+t,n,m\}=m
and
s=\min\{k,n+k+t-m-t\}=k
. But then
m+s-k-r=0
and so the capacity tends to
0
by Theorem
9
. Now assume that
n\leq m
. Here
r=n
and
s=k+n-m
. So again
m+s-k-r=m-n=0
and the capacity again tends to
0
, establishing our claim.

Now assume that
k+t\leq m
, and so
\delta=0
. We have
r=\min\{t+k,n\}=t+\min\{k,n-t\}
and
s=\min\{k,n-t\}
. So
r-s=t
, and Theorem
9
implies that the capacity of the
k
-AMMC tends to
(m-t-k)s
. When
k\geq n-t
this capacity is
(m-t-k)(n-t)
, which is a decreasing function of
k
. As we are maximising the capacity, we may assume without loss of generality that
k\leq n-t
and the capacity is
(m-t-k)k
. Note that, since we are in the situation when
k+t\leq m
and
k\leq n-t
, the integer
k
lies in the interval
[0,\min\{n-t,m-t\}]
.

The function
f(x)=(m-t-x)x
has a unique maximum at
x=(m-t)/2
. Now
x\geq 0
as
t\leq\min\{m,n\}\leq m
, and clearly
x\leq m-t
. Moreover
x<n-t
if and only if
m+t<2n
. We have two cases to consider. We first suppose that
m+t\geq 2n
. Then
x\geq n-t\geq\min\{n-t,m-t\}
and so
x
lies above the interval
[0,\min\{n-t,m\})
. Hence in this case
f
is an increasing function on
[0,\min\{n-t,m-t\}]
and so the capacity is maximal when
k=\min\{n-t,m-t\}
. But note that
m\geq 2n-t\geq n
(as
m+t\geq 2n
and
t\leq\min\{n,m\}
) and so the capacity is maximised when
k=\min\{n-t,m-t\}=n-t
at the value
(m-n)(n-t)
. Hence the AMMC capacity is
(m-n)(n-t)
when
m+t\geq 2n
, and the first case of the theorem follows. Secondly, we suppose that
m+t<2n
. In this case
x
lies in
[0,\min\{n-t,m\}]
and so the capacity is maximised when
k=\lfloor x\rfloor=\lfloor(m-t)/2\rfloor
at the value
\lceil(m-t)/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor(m-t)/2\rfloor
. This establishes the second case of the theorem, as required.
∎

6
When the Field size is Fixed

Throughout this section, we assume that the cardinality
q
of the underlying finite field is fixed. Let
\nu,\mu,\tau\in(0,1)
be fixed real numbers such that
\tau\leq\min\{\nu,\mu\}
. For a positive integer
\ell
(which we think of as a size parameter), set
n=\lfloor\nu\ell\rfloor
,
m=\lfloor\mu\ell\rfloor
,
t=\lfloor\tau\ell\rfloor
. We aim (Theorem
14
below) to determine a good approximation for the capacity of the AMMC as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
with
\nu
,
\mu
and
\tau
fixed. Indeed, we will show that the capacity of the AMMC is
(\mu-\nu)(\nu-\tau)\ell^{2}+O(\ell\log_{q}\ell)
when
\mu+\tau\geq 2\nu
, and is
\frac{1}{4}(\mu-\tau)\ell^{2}+O(\ell\log_{q}\ell)
otherwise. This generalises and strengthens
[
16
, Proposition 4]
, which shows, in our notation, that the capacity is
(\mu-\nu)(\nu-\tau)\ell^{2}+o(\ell^{2})
when
\mu\geq 2\nu
. (Note that the definition of
\tau
in
[
16
]
and our paper are different. Note also that the authors in
[
16
]
phrase their result in terms of
normalised capacity
, which is the result of dividing the capacity by
nm
. So their formulae differ from ours by a factor of
\nu\mu\ell^{2}
.)

We will mimic the proofs in Sections
4
and
5
, and only include details at points where the proofs differ. We note that Lemmas
5
and
6
hold in our situation without change. We will prove analogues of Lemmas
7
and
8
and Theorem
9
in order to provide good bounds on the
k
-AMMC in our asymptotic regime. Just as in Section
4
, the most difficult part of the proof is the analogue of Lemma
8
. In Section
4
we showed that the rank of the matrix
Y
and the dimension of the intersection
\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y)
can both be predicted with high probability in the
k
-AMMC when
q\rightarrow\infty
with
n,m
and
t
fixed. In the asymptotic regime we are considering in this section, this is no longer true. But we are able to exploit the fact that the approximate values of
\mathrm{rk}(Y)
and
\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))
can be found with high probability.

The following lemma is an analogue of Lemma
7
:

Lemma 11
.

Define
\mu
,
\tau
,
\ell
,
m
and
t
as above. Let
k
be a non-negative integer such that
k\leq m
.
Let
U
and
V
be uniformly and independently sampled from the set of all subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
of dimensions
k
and
t
respectively. Define
\delta=\max\{0,(k+t)-m\}
. Then whenever
\ell>\sqrt{t}q^{2}
,

\dim(U\cap V)\in[\,\delta,\delta+3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}\,],

with probability at least
1-1/\ell^{7}
.

Proof.

We see that
\dim(U+V)=\dim U+\dim V-\dim(U\cap V)
. So
m\geq\dim(U+V)=k+t-\dim(U\cap V)
. Hence
\dim(U\cap V)\geq\delta
in all cases. So it remains to show that
\dim(U\cap V)\leq\delta+3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}
with high probability.

For any fixed subspace
V
, the number of choices for
U
with
\dim(U\cap V)=\delta+b
, say, is

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,t\,}{\delta+b}_{q}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m-t\,}{k-\delta-b}_{q}q^{(k-\delta-b)(t-\delta-b)},

for
0\leq b\leq\min\{k,t\}-\delta
, and is zero otherwise. By Lemma
1
, the logarithm to the base
q
of this expression is bounded above by

(t-\delta-b)(\delta+b)+\log_{q}4+(m-t-k+\delta+b)(k-\delta-b)+\log_{q}4+(k-\delta-b)(t-\delta-b).

Since the number of subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
of dimension
k
is at least
q^{(m-k)k}
, by Lemma
1
, we see that the logarithm to the base
q
of the probability that
\dim(U\cap V)=\delta+b
is at most

\begin{split}(t-\delta-b)(\delta+b)&+\log_{q}4+(m-t-k+\delta+b)(k-\delta-b)+\log_{q}4\\
&+(k-\delta-b)(t-\delta-b)-(m-k)k,\end{split}

which is equal to

(k+t-m-\delta-b)(\delta+b)+2\log_{q}4.

(12)

We claim that the expression (
12
) is bounded above by
-b^{2}+4
. To see this, first note that when
k+t\leq m
, and so
\delta=0
, the expression (
12
) can be written as
-b^{2}+b((k+t)-m)+2\log_{q}4
, which is bounded above by

-b^{2}+4
since
\log_{q}4\leq 2
. When
k+t>m
, and so
\delta=k+t-m
, the same bound holds, since the expression (
12
) can be written as

-b^{2}+(m-(k+t))b+2\log_{q}4
which is bounded above by
-b^{2}+4
also. So the claim is established.

Hence the probability that
\dim(U\cap V)>\delta+3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}
is at most

\sum_{b=3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}}^{\min\{k,t\}-\delta}q^{4-b^{2}}\leq tq^{4}q^{-9\log_{q}\ell}=(tq^{4}/\ell^{2})/\ell^{7}.

Since
\ell>\sqrt{t}q^{2}
, we see that
tq^{4}/\ell^{2}<1
and so the lemma follows.
∎

Next, we use Lemma
11
to prove an analogue of Lemma
8
:

Lemma 12
.

Define
\nu
,
\mu
,
\tau
,
\ell
,
n
,
m
and
t
as above. Let
k
be an integer such that
0\leq k\leq\min\{n,m\}
, and define
\delta=\max\{0,k+t-m\}
as before. Define
f=13\log_{q}\ell
. Let
X
and
W
be
n\times m
matrices, chosen uniformly and independently at random subject to
\mathrm{rk}(X)=k
and
\mathrm{rk}(W)=t
. Let
Y=A(X+W)
, where
A
is chosen uniformly at random from all
n\times n
invertible matrices. Whenever
\ell>16+\sqrt{1+t+n}+q^{9}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(Y)\in\min\{t+k,n,m\}+[-f,0],\text{ and }

(13)

\displaystyle\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))\in\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}+[-f,f].

(14)

with probability
p
, where
p>1-1/\ell^{3}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
8
, we may assume without loss of generality that
A
is the identity matrix, and so
Y=X+W
.

Just as in the proof of Lemma
8
, we choose the matrices
X
and
W
using the following random process. First we choose subspaces
U
and
V
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
of dimensions
k
and
t
respectively, uniformly and independently at random. We then choose the rows
x_{i}
of
X
independently and uniformly from
U
, and the rows
w_{i}
of
W
independently and uniformly from
V
. Let
\mathbf{E}
be the indicator variable for the event that
\mathrm{row}(X)=U
and
\mathrm{row}(W)=V
. When
\mathbf{E}=1
the matrices
X
and
W
are uniform such that
\mathrm{row}(X)=U
and
\mathrm{row}(W)=V
. So the probability
p
we are interested in is the probability that (
13
) and (
14
) hold, given that
\mathbf{E}=1
.

Following the proof of Lemma
8
, we see that

\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{E}=1)\geq\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-q^{-j})\prod_{j=1}^{t}(1-q^{-j}).

Unlike in Lemma
8
, the right hand side of this expression does not tend to 1. However, by Lemma
1
,

\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{E}=1)>(1/4)^{2}=1/16
.

Let
\pi
be the ‘bad’ probability that our random process produces matrices
X
and
Y
such that (
13
) or (
14
) fail. Then the probability
1-p
that (
13
) or (
14
) fail given
\mathbf{E}=1
is at most
\pi/\mathrm{P}(\mathbf{E}=1)\leq 16\pi
. We aim to show that
\pi\leq 1/\ell^{4}
for the values of
\ell
we consider. This is sufficient to prove the lemma, since
16\pi\leq 16/\ell^{4}\leq 1/\ell^{3}
as we are assuming that
\ell\geq 16
.

With probability at least
1-1/\ell^{7}
,
\dim(U\cap V)=z
, where
z\in[\delta,\delta+3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}]
, by Lemma
11
. Assume that
z\in[\delta,\delta+3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}]
, so

t-\delta-3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}\leq\dim(V\bmod U)\leq t-\delta.

Define
r_{i}
to be the dimension of the space spanned by the first
i
rows of
Y
, so
r_{i}=\dim\langle y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{i}\rangle
. Now
y_{i}=x_{i}+w_{i}=w_{i}\bmod U
, and so the vectors
y_{i}\bmod U
are uniformly and independently chosen elements from
V\bmod U
. The probability that the first
t-\delta-3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}-6\log_{q}\ell
of the vectors
y_{i}
are linearly independent modulo
U
is

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{t-\delta-3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}-6\log_{q}\ell}(q^{\dim(U\cap V)}-q^{i})/q^{\dim(U\cap V)}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-q^{-6\log_{q}\ell}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\geq 1-t/\ell^{6}.

So
r_{i}=i
for
i\leq t-\delta-3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}-6\log_{q}\ell
with probability at least
1-t/\ell^{6}
. Suppose that this high probability event occurs:
r_{i}=i
for
i\leq t-\delta-3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}-6\log_{q}\ell
. Consider the next
\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}-6\log_{q}\ell
rows of
Y
. For at most
3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}+6\log_{q}\ell
of these vectors
y_{i}
, it is possible that
y_{i}\bmod U
is not in the span of
y_{1},y_{2}\ldots,y_{i-1}\bmod U
and so
r_{i}=r_{i-1}+1
with probability
1
. Otherwise, the argument in the proof of Lemma
8
shows that there is a linear combination of
y_{i}
and previous rows that produces a uniformly chosen element of
U
; with probability at least
1-q^{-6\log_{q}\ell}=1-1/\ell^{6}
this vector does not lie in the span of previous rows. Hence the event
E_{0}
that

\displaystyle\text{$r_{i}=i$ for }i\leq t-\delta-3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}-6\log_{q}\ell+\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}-6\log_{q}\ell

\displaystyle\text{ and }\dim(U\cap V)\in[\delta,\delta+3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}]

occurs with probability at least

(1-1/\ell^{7})(1-t/\ell^{6})(1-1/\ell^{6})^{\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}-6\log_{q}\ell}.

We see that this probability is bounded below by
(1-1/\ell^{7})(1-t/\ell^{6})(1-n/\ell^{6})
, which is at least
1-1/\ell^{7}-t/\ell^{6}-n/\ell^{6}
. Now
\ell\geq\sqrt{1+t+n}
, and so

\displaystyle 1-1/\ell^{7}-t/\ell^{6}-n/\ell^{6}

\displaystyle\geq 1-(1+t+n)/\ell^{6}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\ell^{2}/\ell^{6}.

So
E_{0}
occurs with probability at least
1-1/\ell^{4}
.

When the event
E_{0}
occurs, since
\mathrm{rk}(Y)\geq r_{i}
for any
i
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(Y)

\displaystyle\geq t-\delta-3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}-6\log_{q}\ell+\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}-6\log_{q}\ell

\displaystyle\geq\min\{k+t-\delta,n\}-f

since
\ell\geq q^{9}
(which implies that
3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}\leq\log_{q}\ell
). Note that the definition of
\delta
shows that
\min\{k+t-\delta,n\}=\min\{k+t,n,m\}
. Since
\mathrm{row}(Y)\subseteq U+V
, we see that
\mathrm{rk}(Y)\leq k+t
. Moreover, the rank of an
n\times m
matrix can be at most
\min\{n,m\}
. Hence (
13
) holds when
E_{0}
occurs.

We have
\mathrm{row}(X)+\mathrm{row}(Y)=\mathrm{row}(X)+\mathrm{row}(W)
. Hence, considering the dimensions of these subspaces, we find

\mathrm{rk}(X)+\mathrm{rk}(Y)-\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))=\mathrm{rk}(X)+\mathrm{rk}(W)-\dim(U\cap V),

and so

\displaystyle\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rk}(Y)-\mathrm{rk}(W)+\dim(U\cap V)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rk}(Y)-t+\dim(U\cap V).

When
E_{0}
holds,

\dim(U\cap V)\in[\delta,\delta+3\sqrt{\log_{q}\ell}]\subseteq[\delta,\delta+f]

and
\mathrm{rk}(Y)\in\min\{k+t-\delta,n\}+[-f,0]
. Hence

\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))\in\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}+[-f,f],

and so
(
14
)
holds. Hence the lemma follows.
∎

We are now in a position where we can find a good approximation for the capacity of the
k
-AMMC. The following theorem is an analogue of Theorem
9
:

Theorem 13
.

Let
q
be a fixed prime power.
Let
\nu,\mu,\tau\in(0,1)
be fixed real numbers with the property that
\tau\leq\min\{\nu,\mu\}
. For a positive integer
\ell
, set
n=\lfloor\nu\ell\rfloor
,
m=\lfloor\mu\ell\rfloor
and
t=\lfloor\tau\ell\rfloor
. Let
k
be an integer such that
0\leq k\leq\min\{n,m\}
. Define integers
\delta
,
r
and
s
by
\delta=\max\{0,(k+t)-m\}
,
r=\min\{t+k,m,n\}
and
s=\min\{k,n+\delta-t\}
. Define
f=13\log_{q}\ell
. The capacity of the
k
-AMMC (taking logarithms to the base
q
) lies between

(m+s-k-r)s-\big(2(m+n)f+(m+n)^{2}/\ell^{3}+\log_{q}4\big)

and

(m+s-k-r)s+\big((m+n)f+\log_{q}4+4(m+n)^{2}/\ell^{3}\big)

whenever
\ell\geq 16+\sqrt{1+t+n}+q^{9}+14^{2}/(\nu+\mu)^{2}
.

Note that
m
and
n
are linear in
\ell
, whereas
f
is logarithmic. So the theorem says that the capacity of the
k
-AMMC is approximately
(m+s-k-r)s
.

Proof.

As before, we take
\mathbf{X}
to be the uniform input distribution for the
k
-AMMC, and
\mathbf{Y}
to be the corresponding output distribution. This is a capacity achieving distribution, by Lemma
5
, and so we can estimate the capacity by estimating
H(\mathbf{X})-H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})
. The number of
n\times m
matrices of rank
k
is at most
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m\,}{k}_{q}q^{nk}
, and is at least
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m\,}{k}_{q}q^{nk}/4
, since the first
k
rows of a matrix whose rows lie in a subspace of dimension
k
span the subspace with probability at least 1/4, by Corollary
2
. Since
\mathbf{X}
is uniform, Lemma
1
implies that

(m-k)k+nk-\log_{q}4\leq H(\mathbf{X})\leq(m-k)k+nk+\log_{q}4,

(15)

just as in the proof of Theorem
9
.

To estimate
H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})
, define
I
to be the set of pairs
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
of non-negative integers such that:

\displaystyle r^{\prime}\in\min\{t+k,n,m\}+[-f,0],\text{ and}

\displaystyle s^{\prime}\in\big(\min\{k,n-(t-\delta)\}+[-f,f]]\big)\cap[0,\min\{r,k\}]

Define

\displaystyle\underline{h}=\min_{(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in I}\big\{\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{rk}(Y)

\displaystyle=r^{\prime},\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))=s^{\prime})\cdot

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{Y})=r^{\prime},\dim(\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{Y})\cap\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{X}))=s^{\prime})\big\}

and

\displaystyle\overline{h}=\max_{(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in I}\big\{\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{rk}(Y)

\displaystyle=r^{\prime},\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))=s^{\prime})\cdot

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{Y})=r^{\prime},\dim(\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{Y})\cap\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{X}))=s^{\prime})\big\}.

Lemma
12
and the method in the proof of Theorem
9
shows that

p\underline{h}\leq H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})\leq p\overline{h}+(1-p)mn,

(16)

where
1\geq p\geq 1-1/\ell^{3}
.
We note that, in our asymptotic regime,
mn/\ell^{3}\rightarrow 0
. And so to estimate
H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})
it suffices to provide a lower bound for
\underline{h}
and an upper bound for
\overline{h}
.

When
Y
is fixed such that
\mathrm{rk}(Y)=r^{\prime}
, the number of choices for
\mathrm{row}(X)
such that
\dim(\mathrm{row}(X)\cap\mathrm{row}(Y))=s^{\prime}
is

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,r'\,}{s'}_{q}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\,m-r'\,}{k-s'}_{q}q^{(k-s^{\prime})(r^{\prime}-s^{\prime})}.

The number of matrices
X
with a given row space
U
of rank
k
is at most
q^{kn}
(as every row of
X
lies in
U
). At least
1/4
of the matrices
X
whose rows lie in
U
have
\mathrm{row}(X)=U
, since
k
randomly chosen vectors from
U
span
U
with probability at least
1/4
, by Corollary
2
. So the number of matrices
X
with a given row space
U
of rank
k
is at least
q^{kn}/4
. By Lemma
1
, we see that the number of possibilities for
X
is at most
q
to the power

\displaystyle(r^{\prime}-s^{\prime})s^{\prime}+

\displaystyle\log_{q}4+(m-r^{\prime}-k+s^{\prime})(k-s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\log_{q}4+(k-s^{\prime})(r^{\prime}-s^{\prime})+kn

\displaystyle=(r^{\prime}-s^{\prime})s^{\prime}+(m-k)(k-s^{\prime})+kn+2\log_{q}4

and, similarly, at least
q
to the power

(r^{\prime}-s^{\prime})s^{\prime}+(m-k)(k-s^{\prime})+kn-\log_{q}4.

These expressions are upper and lower bounds for the conditional entropy
H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y},\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{Y})=r^{\prime},\dim(\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{Y})\cap\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{X}))=s^{\prime})
.

When
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in I
we see that
r-f\leq r^{\prime}\leq r
and
s-f\leq s^{\prime}\leq s+f
, where
r
and
s
are defined in the statement of the theorem. Hence

\displaystyle\overline{h}

\displaystyle\leq(r-s+f)(s+f)+(m-k)(k-s+f)+kn+2\log_{q}4

\displaystyle=(r-s)s+(m-k)(k-s)+kn+2\log_{q}4+f^{2}+rf+(m-k)f.

Now,
rf+(m-k)f\leq(m+n)f
, since
r\leq t+k\leq n+k
. Since
f\geq 16
, we see that
2\log_{q}4<f
and so
2\log_{q}4+f^{2}\leq f(f+1)
. Hence

\overline{h}\leq(r-s)s+(m-k)(k-s)+kn+(f+1)f+(m+n)f
.
Also

f+1\leq 14\log_{q}\ell\leq 14\sqrt{\ell}-2=14\ell/\sqrt{\ell}-2\leq(\nu+\mu)\ell-2\leq m+n,

since
\ell\geq 14^{2}/(\nu+\mu)^{2}
. So

\overline{h}\leq(r-s)s+(m-k)(k-s)+kn+2(m+n)f.

Using this bound together with the expressions (
15
) and (
16
) we see that

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=H(\mathbf{X})-H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\geq(m-k)k+nk-\log_{q}4-p\overline{h}-(1-p)mn

\displaystyle\geq(m-k)k+nk-\log_{q}4-\overline{h}-mn/\ell^{3}

\displaystyle\geq(m-k)s+(s-r)s-\log_{q}4-2(m+n)f-(m+n)^{2}/\ell^{3}.

Hence the lower bound of the theorem follows.

Turning to
\underline{h}
, we see that

\displaystyle\underline{h}

\displaystyle\geq(r-s-2f)(s-f)+(m-k)(k-s-f)+kn-\log_{q}4

\displaystyle=(r-s)s+(m-k)(k-s)+kn

\displaystyle\quad-\log_{q}4-2fs+2f^{2}-(r-s)f-(m-k)f

\displaystyle\geq(r-s)s+(m-k)(k-s)+kn-(r+s)f-(m-k)f

(as
2f^{2}\geq\log_{q}4
)

\displaystyle\geq(r-s)s+(m-k)(k-s)+kn-(m+n)f

since
s\leq k
and
r\leq n
.
Using this bound and the expressions (
15
) and (
16
), we see that

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{X};\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=H(\mathbf{X})-H(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\leq(m-k)k+nk+\log_{q}4-p\underline{h}

\displaystyle\leq(m-k)k+nk+\log_{q}4

\displaystyle\quad\quad-(1-1/\ell^{3})\big((r-s)s+(m-k)(k-s)+kn-(m+n)f\big)

\displaystyle\leq(m-k)s+(s-r)s+(m+n)f+\log_{q}4+4(m+n)^{2}/\ell^{3}.

Hence we have established the upper bound on the capacity, and the theorem is proved.
∎

Theorem 14
.

Let
q
be a fixed prime power.
Let
\nu,\mu,\tau\in(0,1)
be fixed real numbers with the property that
\tau\leq\min\{\nu,\mu\}
. For a positive integer
\ell
, set
n=\lfloor\nu\ell\rfloor
,
m=\lfloor\mu\ell\rfloor
and
t=\lfloor\tau\ell\rfloor
. The capacity of the AMMC (taking logarithms to the base
q
) is
(\mu-\nu)(\nu-\tau)\ell^{2}+O(\ell\log_{q}\ell)
when
\mu+\tau\geq 2\nu
, and is
\frac{1}{4}(\mu-\tau)\ell^{2}+O(\ell\log_{q}\ell)
otherwise.

Proof.

Let
c
be the maximum capacity of the
k
-AMMC, where
0\leq k\leq\min\{m,n\}
. The argument in the proof of Theorem
10
shows that
c
and the capacity of the AMMC differ by at most
\log_{q}(\min\{n,m\}+1)=O(\log_{q}\ell)
(where the implicit constant depends on
\mu
and
\nu
, but not on
k
). We use Theorem
13
to estimate the capacity of the
k
-AMMC. In that theorem, note that
(m+n)f=O(\ell\log_{q}\ell)
and
(m+n)^{2}/\ell^{3}=O(1/\ell)
, where the implicit constants do not depend on
k
. So Theorem
13
shows that the capacity of the
k
-AMMC is
(m+s-k-r)s+O(\ell\log_{q}\ell)
, where
r
and
s
are defined in the theorem. We may now maximise the value of
(m+s-k-r)s
to find
c
: the argument of Theorem
10
shows
c=(m-n)(n-t)+O(\ell\log_{q}\ell)
when
m+t\geq 2n
and
c=\lceil(m-t)/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor(m-t)/2\rfloor
otherwise. Since
(m-n)(n-t)=(\mu-\nu)(\nu-\tau)\ell^{2}+O(\ell)
, and
\lceil(m-t)/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor(m-t)/2\rfloor=(\mu-\tau)^{2}/4+O(\ell)
, the theorem follows.
∎

7
A Coding Scheme

A straightforward modification of the coding scheme in
[
16
]
works in our more general setting. Consider the situation when
q
is large. We think of our data as an
x\times y
matrix
U
, where
x=n-t
,
y=m-n
when
m+t\geq 2n
, and where
x=\lfloor(m-t)/2\rfloor
and
y=\lceil(m-t)/2\rceil
otherwise. Note that in both cases,
x+y=m-t
. Writing
0_{a\times b}
for the
a\times b
zero matrix, and writing
I_{a}
for the
a\times a
identity matrix, we input
X
into the channel, where
X
is the
n\times m
matrix defined by

X=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}0_{(n-x)\times t}&0_{(n-x)\times x}&0_{(n-x)\times y}\\
\hline\cr 0_{x\times t}&I_{x}&U\end{array}\right).

So when
m+t\geq 2n
we have

X=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}0_{t\times t}&0_{t\times n-t}&0_{t\times m-n}\\
\hline\cr 0_{(n-t)\times t}&I_{n-t}&U\end{array}\right),

and if not, setting
d=\lfloor(m-t)/2\rfloor
and
d^{\prime}=\lceil(m-t)/2\rceil
, we have

X=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}0_{(n-d)\times t}&0_{(n-d)\times d}&0_{(n-d)\times d^{\prime}}\\
\hline\cr 0_{d\times t}&I_{d}&U\end{array}\right).

On receiving the corresponding matrix
Y=A(X+W)
, we row reduce to produce a matrix
Z
. Lemma
8
shows that with high probability we expect
Y
to have rank
t+x
, and
\mathrm{row}(Y)
to be the direct sum of
\mathrm{row}(X)
and
\mathrm{row}(W)
. Indeed, Lemma
7
shows that with high probability
\mathrm{row}(W)
intersects the
(m-t)
dimensional subspace
V
trivially, where
V=\{(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{m}):v_{i}=0\text{ for }i\leq t\}
. Note that
\mathrm{row}(X)\subseteq V
. Row reduction does not change the row space, so the techniques in the proof of Lemma
8
show that with high probability
Z
has the form

Z=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}I_{t\times t}&\star&\star\\
\hline\cr 0_{x\times t}&I_{x}&U\\
\hline\cr 0_{(n-x-t)\times t}&0_{(n-x-t)\times x}&0_{(n-x-t)\times y}\end{array}\right)

where the first
t
rows of
Z
are a basis for
\mathrm{row}(W)
modulo
\mathrm{row}(X)
and the next
x
rows are a basis for
\mathrm{row}(X)
.
(If it is not of this form, we declare a decoding failure.) It is now easy to recover the data
U
from the matrix
Z
.

When
q
is fixed with
n
,
m
and
t
large, the same coding method will work, but the dimensions of
U
need to be reduced slightly, so that
Z
is more likely to have the form above.

The coding scheme above restricts input matrices to having rank
x
, so we are essentially using the
k
-AMMC with
k=x
. In Section
2
we remarked that the
k
-AMMC may be asymptotically approximated by the operator channel from
[
9
]
with
\rho
erasures and
\tau
errors, for certain integers
\rho
and
\tau
. So an alternative approach would be to use a subspace code for encoding. As we remarked in Section
2
, this has the great advantage that decoding would also work for a range of practical channels where the number of errors is bounded by
t
but not fixed. Of course a suitable subspace code must exist, but this is not an issue in practice as
q
and
m
are taken to be large.
