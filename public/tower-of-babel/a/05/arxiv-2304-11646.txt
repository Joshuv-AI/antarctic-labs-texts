Rough Paths above Weierstrass Functions
\firstname Francesco \lastname Cellarosi, \firstname Zachary \lastname Selk

Abstract
Résumé. Rough paths theory allows for a pathwise theory of solutions to differential equations driven by highly irregular signals. The fundamental observation of rough paths theory is that if one can define “iterated integrals" above a signal, then one can construct solutions to differential equations driven by the signal. The typical examples of the signals of interest are stochastic processes such as (fractional) Brownian motion. However, rough paths theory is not inherently random and therefore can treat irregular deterministic driving signals such as a (vector-valued) Weierstrass function. This note supplies a construction of a rough path above vector-valued Weierstrass functions.

1.
Introduction

Rough paths theory allows for a pathwise theory of solutions to differential equations driven by highly irregular signals. Rough paths are concerned with differential equations of the form

dY(t)=b(Y(t),t)dt+c(Y(t),t)dX(t),

(1.1)

where
b,c
are nice enough functions and
X
is some “driving" signal
X(t)=(X^{1}(t),

...,X^{d}(t))
with bad regularity - in particular
X
lacking differentiability. In this case, we may interpret equation (
) as the integral equation

Y(t)=Y(0)+\int_{0}^{t}b(Y(s),s)ds+\int_{0}^{T}c(Y(s),s)dX(s).

(1.2)

However, if
X
is not of bounded variation the last integral in equation (
) might not be well-defined as a Riemann-Stieltjes integral. In cases where
X
is a stochastic process (in particular a martingale), the celebrated Itô calculus can make sense of the last integral in equation (
). Itô theory averages over paths or takes limits in probability, thus making it unsuitable for pathwise calculus. Rough paths theory takes an alternative approach to the Itô calculus : by “enhancing"
X
with some “extra information" encoding an integration theory, we can construct pathwise solutions to (
).

The fundamental observation of rough paths theory is that the issue of defining solutions to (
) can be reduced to defining the iterated integrals
\int_{s}^{t}(X^{i}(r)-X^{i}(s))dX^{j}(r)
for
1\leq i,j\leq d
. The pair
\left(X(t)-X(s),\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))\otimes dX(r)\right)
, where
\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))\otimes dX(r)
is the matrix of iterated integrals of the form
\int_{s}^{t}(X^{i}(r)-X^{i}(s))dX^{j}(r)
, is called a
rough path
above the signal
X
(see definition
2.3
).

Rough paths have been extensively applied to stochastic differential equations where
X
is a stochastic process. Examples of stochastic driving signals include fractional Brownian motion or more general Gaussian processes. However, there is nothing inherently random about rough paths theory. Even in the case of stochastic driving signals, solutions to (
) are defined pathwise - that is for each realization of the process. Thus even when the driving signal is random, the solution theory can be seen as deterministic. However, in the literature the application of rough paths theory to equations driven by deterministic signals has been limited. This note works out the case when
X
is a vector-valued Weierstrass function.

Given that defining solutions to (
) can be reduced to defining a rough path (iterated integral) above the driving signal, there has been considerable interest in the construction of rough paths above various signals. If the driving signal is scalar-valued, then we can make the simple definition that
\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))dX(r):=\frac{1}{2}(X(t)-X(s))^{2}
. This is a completely acceptable definition for any
X
, so the primary interest is in defining rough paths above vector-valued signals.

In
[
18
]
, the authors show that any signal that is
\alpha
-Hölder (or has finite
p
-variation) has a rough path lift. However, their construction is fairly abstract and hence there is a lot of interest in more natural constructions - in particular from natural approximations. A more natural construction for general
\alpha
-Hölder paths can be found in
[
3
]
. The construction of a rough path above fractional Brownian motion using Volterra’s representation is given in
[
19
]
. In
[
6
]
, the authors constructed rough paths above very general Gaussian signals.

Weierstrass functions are real-valued functions which have been identified as the “deterministic analogue" of Brownian motion (this is the perspective taken in
[
17
]
, for example). In particular, Weierstrass functions have similar analytic properties to the types of stochastic processes that rough paths theory was invented to handle. The analytic properties of Weierstrass functions have been studied extensively. For example, see
[
1
,
2
,
9
,
12
,
16
,
20
]
for discussion of the Hausdorff dimension of the graphs of Weierstrass functions. Another interesting instance in which a Weierstrass function is used in place of a stochastic process is given in
[
17
]
, where a deterministic version of the Schramm–Loewner evolution is considered, driven by a Weistrass function rather than by a Brownian motion.

We emphasize that rough paths theory for scalar-valued functions is somewhat trivial in the sense mentioned above. Therefore, we will consider a vector-valued Weierstrass function. By this, we mean an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued function whose components are scalar-valued Weierstrass functions with possibly different parameters. A two dimensional Weierstrass function has been studied in
[
15
,
14
]
. In
[
15
]
, Example 2.8, the authors consider the two dimensional Weierstrass function

W:t\mapsto\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\cos(2^{k}\pi t),\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\sin(2^{k}\pi t))\right),

where they show that the iterated integrals over
(-1,1)
of the approximating finite sums diverge (therefore the most natural approximation scheme to
W
will not lead to a rough path above
W
). Furthermore, in
[
14
]
they discuss the smoothness of the SBR measure.

We consider a similar signal but of any dimension and with only cosines (or only sines) instead of mixed cosines and sines. We construct a rough path above a vector-valued Weierstrass function without any of the probabilistic technicalities generally present in rough paths theory to demonstrate the power of rough paths theory beyond the setting of stochastic differential equations.

The main result is the following theorem. See Theorem
3.5
for a full version.

Theorem 1.1

(Main theorem, simplified version)
.

Let
0<a_{1},...,a_{d}<1
and let
b_{1},...,b_{d}\geq 2
be integers with
a_{1}b_{1}>1,...,a_{d}b_{d}>1
. Let

W(t)=(W^{1}(t),\ldots,W^{d}(t))=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{1}^{k}\cos(b_{1}^{k}\pi t),...,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{d}^{k}\cos(b_{d}^{k}\pi t)\right)

and let

W_{N}(t)=(W^{1}_{N}(t),\ldots,W^{d}_{N}(t))=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{1}^{k}\cos(b_{1}^{k}\pi t),...,\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{d}^{k}\cos(b_{d}^{k}\pi t)\right).

Then for
1\leq i,j\leq d
and
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
the limit

I^{i,j}(s,t):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{s}^{t}(W^{i}_{N}(r)-W^{i}_{N}(s))\,dW^{j}_{N}(r)

exists. Furthermore, if we assume that
-\frac{\ln{a_{i}}}{\ln{b_{i}}}>1/3
for
1\leq i\leq d
, then the matrix with entries
I^{i,j}(s,t)
defines a geometric rough path above
W
.

Again, we emphasize that we construct the rough path above a vector-valued Weierstrass function because for a scalar-valued Weierstrass function we can always define
\int_{s}^{t}(W(r)-W(s))dW(r):=\frac{1}{2}(W(t)-W(s))^{2}
. The technical challenge is defining
\int_{s}^{t}(W^{i}(r)-W^{i}(s))dW^{j}(r)
where
W^{i}
and
W^{j}
are two different one-dimensional Weierstrass functions. For an example of vector-valued Weierstrass functions, see Figure
1
.

2.
Background on Rough Paths

In this section, we give a brief introduction to rough paths theory. See
[
7
,
8
]
for two excellent introductions to rough paths.

Rough paths theory treats Hölder continuous driving signals - or in the case of stochastic differential equations, stochastic processes that are almost surely Hölder. We recall the definition of Hölder continuity below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
. We denote by
C^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of
\alpha
-Hölder functions
f:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{\alpha}:=\sup_{t\neq s}\frac{|f(t)-f(s)|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}.

In order to solve equation (
) through some type of Picard iteration, one must first make sense of integrals of the form

\int_{0}^{t}c(X(s),s)dX(s).

(2.1)

The following result by Young is classical.

Theorem 2.2

(Young
[
23
]
)
.

Let
f\in C^{\alpha}{([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})}
and let
g\in C^{\beta}{([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})}
with
\alpha+\beta>1
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{n}=T\}
be a sequence of partitions whose mesh size tends to
0
. Then the limit

\int_{0}^{T}f(s)dg(s)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}f(t_{k})(g(t_{k+1})-g(t_{k}))

(2.2)

exists and is independent of the sequence of partitions. The integral in equation (
) is known as the Young or Riemann-Stieltjes integral.

Therefore if we assume that
c
is smooth, then the integral in equation (
) makes sense as a Young integral if
X\in C^{\alpha}{([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})}
for
\alpha>1/2
. However, for lower regularity driving signals, the Riemann-Stieltjes integral defined in equation (
) needs to include extra terms which represent the “iterated integrals" of
X
against itself. We provide a heuristic derivation below.

Heuristic Derivation of Rough Integral
If
X
is a signal that is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous with
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
and
F
is a smooth function, then for a partition of
[0,t]
,
\mathcal{P}=\{0=t_{0}<...<t_{n}=t\}
we have that (the a-priori ill defined) integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}F(X(r))dX(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}F(X(r))dX(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}{\bigg(}F(X(t_{k}))+F^{\prime}(X(t_{k}))(X(r)-X(t_{k}))+O(|r-t_{k}|^{2\alpha}){\bigg)}dX(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bigg(F(X(t_{k}))(X(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k}))+F^{\prime}(X(t_{k}))\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}(X(r)-X(t_{k}))dX(r)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+O(|t_{k+1}-t_{k}|^{3\alpha})\bigg).

As
3\alpha>1
, the remainder term should tend to
0
as the mesh size of the partition
|\mathcal{P}|=\displaystyle\max_{0\leq k\leq n-1}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})
tends to
0
.

Note that we restricted
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
. This is purely for exposition - for lower
\alpha
we would need more iterated integrals. In particular, if
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{n+1},\frac{1}{n}]
then we need
n-1
additional terms so that
\alpha(n+1)>1
. In the rest of this note, we will stick to
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
for convenience.

This heuristic reduces the problem of defining the integral
\int_{0}^{t}F(X(r))dX(r)
to just defining
\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}(X(r)-X(t_{k}))dX(r)
. However, if
X
is irregular then the iterated integral does not exist “canonically" as a Riemann-Stieltjes integral and therefore must be
postulated
. There are two main properties we would like such a definition to satisfy - one analytic and one algebraic.

A rough path above a signal
X\in C^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
is therefore a pair
\mathbf{X}_{s,t}=(X_{s,t},\mathbb{X}_{s,t})
where
X_{s,t}=X(t)-X(s)
is the increment of
X
and
\mathbb{X}_{s,t}
is a
definition
or
postulation
of the iterated integral
\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))\otimes dX(r)
where again we note that
\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))\otimes dX(r)
represents the matrix whose
(i,j)
th entry is
\int_{s}^{t}(X^{i}(r)-X^{i}(s))dX^{j}(r)
. We give a precise definition below.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
T>0
and let
\Delta_{2}^{(0,T)}=\{(s,t):0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}
denote the
2
-simplex. Given a signal
X\in C^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
we say
\mathbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X}):\Delta_{2}^{(0,T)}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a
rough path
above
X
if for all
s,u,t\in[0,T]
we have

\displaystyle(i)\qquad X_{s,t}=X(t)-X(s)

\displaystyle(ii)\qquad\mathbb{X}_{s,t}-\mathbb{X}_{s,u}-\mathbb{X}_{u,t}=X_{s,u}\otimes X_{u,t}

\displaystyle(iii)\qquad\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\alpha,2\alpha}:=\sup_{t\neq s}\frac{|X_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}+\sup_{t\neq s}\frac{|\mathbb{X}_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{2\alpha}}<+\infty,

where
|\cdot|
denotes any of the (equivalent) norms on either
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
We denote the space of rough paths by
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
. The topology generated by the seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,2\alpha}
is called the
rough topology
.

Remark 2.4
.

It should be stressed that in Definition
2.3
there is no reference to any iterated integrals. The second order process
\mathbb{X}
intuitively encodes a notion of iterated integral but formally,
\mathbb{X}
is just some process that takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Once we have this purely abstract object
\mathbb{X}
we may make the definition

\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))\otimes dX(r):=\mathbb{X}_{s,t}.

(2.3)

Remark 2.5
.

The difference of iterated integrals

L^{i,j}(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}X^{i}(r)dX^{j}(r)-\int_{s}^{t}X^{j}(r)dX^{i}(r)

is often referred to as the
Lévy area
. The reason for this terminology is that if
X^{i}
and
X^{j}
were piecewise smooth, then by Green-Stokes theorem, the (signed) area swept by the curve
(X^{i},X^{j})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
from time
s
to time
t
would be twice
L^{i,j}(s,t)
.

One can check that if
X\in C^{\infty}
and
\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))dX(r)
is the Riemann-Stieltjes integral, then
\mathbf{X}_{s,t}:=\left(X(t)-X(s),\int_{s}^{t}(X(r)-X(s))dX(r)\right)
satisfies Definition
2.3
. In this sense, there is a natural embedding of
C^{\infty}
smooth functions into the space of rough paths. The closure of these functions under the rough topology is the space of geometric rough paths.

Definition 2.6
.

For
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
, denote by
\mathring{\mathscr{C}}_{g}^{\alpha}
the image of the embedding
\iota:C^{\infty}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
where
\iota(f)_{s,t}=\left(f(t)-f(s),\int_{s}^{t}(f(r)-f(s))f^{\prime}(r)dr\right)
. Let
\mathscr{C}_{g}^{\alpha}
be the closure of
\mathring{\mathscr{C}}_{g}^{\alpha}
under the seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,2\alpha}
defined in Definition
2.3
. We call
\mathscr{C}_{g}^{\alpha}
the space of
geometric rough paths
.

Remark 2.7
.

The space of rough paths
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
, although a subset of the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is not itself a vector space because of the nonlinear relation (ii) in Definition
2.3
. However, the seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,2\alpha}
can be used to define a metric - thus
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
is a metric space.

We can transfer pointwise convergent sequence of approximations to a rough path to convergence in the rough topology using the following “interpolation" theorem.

Theorem 2.8
.

(see
[
7
]
, Exercise 2.9)
Let
(X_{N},\mathbb{X}_{N})\in\mathscr{C}^{\beta}
for some
\beta\in(1/3,1,2]
and every
N\geq 1
. Suppose that the following uniform bounds hold

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\|X_{N}\|_{\beta}

\displaystyle<\infty,

\displaystyle\sup_{N}\|\mathbb{X}_{N}\|_{2\beta}

\displaystyle<\infty.

Suppose that, as
N\to\infty
,
X_{N}(t)\to X(t)
and
\mathbb{X}_{N}(0,t)\to\mathbb{X}(0,t)
pointwise for all
t
. Then
(X,\mathbb{X})\in\mathscr{C}^{\beta}
and
(X_{N},\mathbb{X}_{N})
converges to
(X,\mathbb{X})
in
\mathscr{C}_{g}^{\alpha}
as
N\to\infty
for all
1/3<\alpha<\beta
.

3.
Weierstrass Function

Let
b\geq 2
be an integer and
0<a<1
with
ab>1
.
Define the (scalar-valued) Weierstrass function by

W_{a,b}(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^{n}\cos(b^{n}\pi t).

(3.1)

In 1872 Weierstrass proved that
W_{a,b}
is nowhere differentiable when
b
is odd and
ab>1+\frac{3\pi}{2}
. The range of parameters
a,b
for which the nondifferentiability holds was enlarged by several authors, culminating in the work of Hardy
[
11
]
. For the parameters we are considering, Hardy proved that
W_{a,b}
is nowhere differentiable and
\alpha
-Hölder continuous with
\alpha=-\frac{\ln{a}}{\ln{b}}
(
[
11
]
, Theorems 1.31 and 1.32). That is, according to Definition
2.1
,
W_{a,b}\in C^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
for every
T>0
.
With
\alpha
as above, we write the equivalent representation

W_{b,\alpha}(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b^{-n\alpha}\cos(b^{n}\pi t).

(3.2)

Also define its truncated version

W_{b,\alpha,N}(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{N}b^{-n\alpha}\cos(b^{n}\pi t).

(3.3)

Even though it is well known that the Weierstrass function is Hölder continuous, for expository reasons we will include a short proof here.

In the proof of Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
, we will write “
\lesssim
” to denote “
\leq K
” where
K
is some unspecified absolute constant whose value may change from line to line. This is equivalent to Vinogradov’s “
\ll
” notation.

For simplicity, instead of working on an arbitrary interval
[0,T]
, we henceforth set
T=1
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
W_{b,\alpha}
be a Weierstrass function. Then
W_{b,\alpha}\in C^{\alpha}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
for
\alpha=-\log_{b}(a)
.

Démonstration.

Consider some pair
(s,t)
and choose
N
so that
b^{-(N+1)}<|t-s|\leq b^{-N}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle|W_{b,\alpha}(t)-W_{b,\alpha}(s)|\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b^{-n\alpha}|\cos(b^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b^{n}\pi s)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}b^{-n\alpha}|\cos(b^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b^{n}\pi s)|+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b^{-n\alpha}|\cos(b^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b^{n}\pi s)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}b^{-n\alpha}b^{n}\pi|t-s|+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b^{-n\alpha}2

\displaystyle\lesssim b^{(1-\alpha)(N+1)}b^{-N}+b^{-\alpha N}

\displaystyle\lesssim b^{-\alpha N}

\displaystyle\lesssim|t-s|^{\alpha}.

∎

The primary technical barrier to constructing a rough path above a vector-valued function is the uniform bound on the Hölder norm of the iterated integrals of the approximating sequence. Thus, the following Proposition
3.2
is the primary technical contribution of this paper. We use a similar idea of the proof of Proposition
3.1
of splitting the sum into a bulk and a tail. However, as there is a double sum we will need to apply two cutoffs and four separate bounds. The bound involving the tail of each sum is the most interesting, and involves various cases depending on whether
b_{1},b_{2}
are multiplicatively independent or not, see bound
(iv)
below.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
W_{b_{1},\alpha_{1}}
and
W_{b_{2},\alpha_{2}}
be two Weierstrass functions with truncated versions
W_{b_{1},\alpha_{1},N}
and
W_{b_{2},\alpha_{2},N}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
\varepsilon<\min\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}
.
Define the approximating sequence of iterated integrals as

I^{N}(s,t)=I_{(b_{1},\alpha_{1}),(b_{2},\alpha_{2})}^{N}(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}(W_{b_{1},\alpha_{1},N}(r)-W_{b_{1},\alpha_{1},N}(s))\,dW_{b_{2},\alpha_{2},N}(r).

(3.4)

Then there exists some constant
C:=C(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},b_{1},b_{2},\varepsilon)
, independent of
N
so that

\left|I^{N}(s,t)\right|\leq C|t-s|^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-2\varepsilon},

(3.5)

for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1.

We will need the following result due to N.I. Fel’dman.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
4
]
, Theorem 1.8)
.

Let
b_{1},b_{2}\geq 2
be two integers that are multiplicatively independent (i.e.
\ln{(b_{1})}/\ln{(b_{2})}
is irrational). Let
n,\ell\geq 1
be two integers. Then there exists a constant
P>0
, depending only on
b_{1},b_{2}
such that

|b_{1}^{n}-b_{2}^{\ell}|\geq\frac{\max\{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{\ell}\}}{(\max\{3,n,\ell\})^{P}}.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Recalling that
\alpha_{i}=-\frac{\ln(a_{i})}{\ln(b_{i})}
for
i=1,2
, we may rewrite
I^{N}(s,t)
as

I^{N}(s,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}\,J^{n,\ell}(s,t),

(3.6)

where

J^{n,\ell}(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi r)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)d\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi r).

(3.7)

We will need four bounds for
J
.

Bound (i)
.
Differentiating the integrator of
J^{n,\ell}(s,t)
gives that

\displaystyle|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi r)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)\pi b_{2}^{\ell}\sin(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi r)\,dr\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\left|\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi r)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)\pi b_{2}^{\ell}\sin(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi r)\right|dr

\displaystyle{\lesssim b_{1}^{n}b_{2}^{\ell}\int_{s}^{t}(r-s)\,dr}

\displaystyle\lesssim b_{1}^{n}b_{2}^{\ell}(t-s)^{2},

where the second to last inequality is due to the Lipschitz property of cosine and the trivial bound
|\sin(x)|\leq 1
.

Bound (ii)
.
Using the trivial bounds
|\sin(x)|,|\cos(x)|\leq 1
we have

\displaystyle|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\left|\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi r)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)\pi b_{2}^{\ell}\sin(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi r)\right|dr

\displaystyle\lesssim b_{2}^{\ell}(t-s).

Bound (iii)
. We can apply integration by parts to the Riemann-Stieltjes integral
J
to get that

\displaystyle J^{n,\ell}(s,t)=

\displaystyle\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi t)-\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi s)

\displaystyle-\int_{s}^{t}\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi r)\,d\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi t)-\int_{s}^{t}\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi r)\,d\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)\right)\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi t){+}b_{1}^{n}\pi\int_{s}^{t}\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi r)\sin(b_{1}^{n}\pi r)dr.

By the triangle inequality, Lipschitz property of cosine and the bounds
|\cos(x)|\leq 1,|\sin(x)|\leq 1
we have that

\displaystyle|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|

\displaystyle\lesssim b_{1}^{n}(t-s).

Bound (iv)
. We will split this into three cases. First, when
b_{1}^{n}=b_{2}^{\ell}
. Second, when
b_{1}^{n}\neq b_{2}^{\ell}
and
\log_{b_{1}}\!(b_{2})
is irrational. Third, when
b_{1}^{n}\neq b_{2}^{\ell}
but
\log_{b_{1}}\!(b_{2})
is rational.

Case 1 (
b_{1}^{n}=b_{2}^{\ell}
).
If
b_{1}^{n}=b_{2}^{\ell}
then

J^{n,\ell}(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}(\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s))^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi t)-\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi s))^{2}.

(3.8)

Therefore

|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|\lesssim 1.

(3.9)

For
b_{1}^{n}\neq b_{2}^{\ell}
we can evaluate the integral as

\displaystyle J^{n,\ell}(s,t)

\displaystyle=-b_{2}^{\ell}\pi\left(\frac{\cos(t(b_{1}^{n}-b_{2}^{\ell}))}{2(b_{1}^{n}-b_{2}^{\ell})}-\frac{\cos(s(b_{1}^{n}-b_{2}^{\ell}))}{2(b_{1}^{n}-b_{2}^{\ell})}-\frac{\cos(t(b_{1}^{n}+b_{2}^{\ell}))}{2(b_{1}^{n}+b_{2}^{\ell})}+\frac{\cos(s(b_{1}^{n}+b_{2}^{\ell}))}{2(b_{1}^{n}+b_{2}^{\ell})}\right)

\displaystyle`-\cos(b_{1}^{n}\pi s)(\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi t)-\cos(b_{2}^{\ell}\pi s)),

using the indefinite integral formula

\int\cos(mr)\sin(nr)dr=\frac{\cos(r(m-n))}{2(m-n)}-\frac{\cos(r(m+n))}{2(m+n)}+C.

Therefore we have that

|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|\lesssim\frac{b_{2}^{\ell}}{|b_{2}^{\ell}-b_{1}^{n}|}+1.

Case 2 (
b_{1},b_{2}
multiplicatively independent).
Now let
\log_{b_{1}}\!(b_{2})
be irrational. Then, using Lemma
3.3
we have that there exists some constant
P>0
depending only on
b_{1},b_{2}
so that

\displaystyle|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{b_{2}^{\ell}}{\max\{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{\ell}\}}(\max\{3,n,\ell\})^{P}+1

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{P}\ell^{P}.

Recall that
0<\varepsilon<\min\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}
. There exists some constants
C^{1}(\varepsilon),C^{2}(\varepsilon)>0
depending on
P
,
\varepsilon
and on
b_{1},b_{2}
so that
n^{P}\leq C^{1}(\varepsilon)b_{1}^{\varepsilon n}
for all

and
\ell^{P}\leq C^{2}(\varepsilon)b_{2}^{\varepsilon\ell}
for all
\ell
. Therefore we have that bound

|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|\lesssim b_{1}^{\varepsilon n}b_{2}^{\varepsilon\ell}.

(3.10)

Case 3 (
b_{1},b_{2}
multiplicatively dependent).
Finally, let
\log_{b_{1}}(b_{2})
be rational. Then there exists some positive integers
b,q_{1},q_{2}
so that
b_{1}=b^{q_{1}}
and
b_{2}=b^{q_{2}}
. Then without loss of generality letting
\ell q_{1}>nq_{2}
we have that

\displaystyle|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{b_{2}^{\ell}}{|b_{2}^{\ell}-b_{1}^{n}|}+1

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{\ell q_{1}}}{b^{\ell q_{1}}-b^{nq_{2}}}+1

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{\ell q_{1}-nq_{2}}}{b^{\ell q_{1}-nq_{2}}-1}+1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{b^{\ell q_{1}-nq_{2}}}{b^{\ell q_{1}-nq_{2}}-\frac{1}{2}b^{\ell q_{1}-nq_{2}}}+1

\displaystyle\lesssim 1.

Putting cases 1,2,3 together yields bound
(iv)

|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|\lesssim b_{1}^{\varepsilon n}b_{2}^{\varepsilon\ell}.

(3.11)

Using these four bounds we can show the Hölder regularity. Similarly to the proof of Proposition
3.1
, let
N_{1},N_{2}
be two nonnegative integers so that

\displaystyle b_{1}^{-(N_{1}+1)}

\displaystyle<|t-s|\leq b_{1}^{-N_{1}}

\displaystyle b_{2}^{-(N_{2}+1)}

\displaystyle<|t-s|\leq b_{2}^{-N_{2}}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
N>N_{1},N_{2}
. Indeed, if
N\leq N_{1}
or
N\leq N_{2}
then

\displaystyle|I^{N}(s,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\max\{N_{1},N\}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\max\{N_{2},N\}}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}|J^{n,\ell}(s,t)|

and the argument below will bound
I^{N}
as well. With this assumption in hand, we can split
I^{N}

\displaystyle I^{N}(s,t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N_{1}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{2}}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}J^{n,\ell}(s,t)+\sum_{n=N_{1}+1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{2}}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}J^{n,\ell}(s,t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=0}^{N_{1}}\sum_{\ell=N_{2}+1}^{N}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}J^{n,\ell}(s,t)+\sum_{n=N_{1}+1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=N_{2}+1}^{N}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}J^{n,\ell}(s,t)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\>\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{II}+\mathbf{III}+\mathbf{IV}.

Applying bound
(i)
to
I
, bound
(ii)
to
II
, bound
(iii)
to
III
, and bound
(iv)
to

yields that

\displaystyle|\mathbf{I}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n=0}^{N_{1}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{2}}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{1}^{n}b_{2}^{\ell}(t-s)^{2}=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N_{1}}a_{1}^{n}b_{1}^{n}(t-s)\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{2}}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{2}^{\ell}(t-s)\right),

\displaystyle|\mathbf{II}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n=N_{1}+1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{2}}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{2}^{\ell}(t-s)=\left(\sum_{n=N_{1}+1}^{N}a_{1}^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{2}}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{2}^{\ell}(t-s)\right),

\displaystyle|\mathbf{III}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n=0}^{N_{1}}\sum_{\ell=N_{2}+1}^{N}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{1}^{n}(t-s)=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N_{1}}a_{1}^{n}b_{1}^{n}(t-s)\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=n_{2}+1}^{N}a_{2}^{\ell}\right),

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n=N_{1}+1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=N_{2}+1}^{N}a_{1}^{n}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{1}^{\varepsilon n}b_{2}^{\varepsilon\ell}=\left(\sum_{n=N_{1}+1}^{N}a_{1}^{n}b_{1}^{\varepsilon n}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=N_{2}+1}^{N}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{2}^{\varepsilon\ell}\right).

By the choice of

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N_{1}}a_{1}^{n}b_{1}^{n}(t-s)\lesssim a_{1}^{N_{1}}b_{1}^{N_{1}}(t-s)\leq a_{1}^{N_{1}}=b_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}N_{1}}\lesssim|t-s|^{\alpha_{1}},

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{2}}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{2}^{\ell}(t-s)\lesssim|t-s|^{\alpha_{2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N_{1}+1}^{N}{a_{1}^{n}}b_{1}^{\varepsilon n}\lesssim a_{1}^{N_{1}}b_{1}^{\varepsilon N_{1}}\lesssim|t-s|^{\alpha_{1}-\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=N_{2}+1}^{N}a_{2}^{\ell}b_{2}^{\varepsilon\ell}\lesssim|t-s|^{\alpha_{2}-\varepsilon}.

For all

With both the pointwise convergence and the uniform estimate (

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
t\mapsto W(t)=(W_{b_{1},\alpha_{1}}(t),...,W_{b_{d},\alpha_{d}}(t))
be a
d
-dimensional Weierstrass function where
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d}>1/3
. Let
\alpha=\min\{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d}\}
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the vector-valued function
W_{N}:\Delta_{2}^{(0,1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by setting
W_{N}(t):=\left(W_{b_{1},\alpha_{1},N}(t),...,W_{b_{d},\alpha_{d},N}(t)\right)
. Similarly, consider the matrix-valued function
\mathbb{A}_{N}:\Delta_{2}^{(0,1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, defined entrywise by means of the iterated integrals (
3.4
) by setting
\mathbb{A}_{N}^{i,j}(s,t):=I^{N}_{(b_{i},\alpha_{i}),(b_{j},\alpha_{j})}(s,t)
for
1\leq i,j\leq d
. Let
\mathbf{W}_{N}:\Delta_{2}^{(0,1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\mathbf{W}_{N}(s,t)=\left(W_{N}(t)-W_{N}(s),\mathbb{A}_{N}(s,t)\right)
.
Finally, define
\mathbb{A}:\Delta_{2}^{(0,1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
entrywise as
\mathbb{A}^{i,j}(s,t):=\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{A}_{N}^{i,j}(s,t)
and let
\mathbf{W}:\Delta_{2}^{(0,1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\mathbf{W}(s,t)=\left(W(t)-W(s),\mathbb{A}(s,t)\right)
.
Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\alpha)
we have that
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}_{g}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}
.

Furthermore, for every
\beta\in(\alpha-\varepsilon,\alpha)
let us set
\kappa:=1-\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{\beta}\in(0,1)
. Then for every
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\alpha)
, setting
\rho=\left(\max\!\left\{b_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}+\varepsilon^{\prime}},\ldots,b_{d}^{-\alpha_{d}+\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\}\right)^{\kappa}\in(0,1)
,
we have the estimate

\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{N}\|_{\alpha-\varepsilon,2\alpha-2\varepsilon}\lesssim\rho^{N}.

(3.13)

In other words,
\mathbf{W}_{N}
converges to
\mathbf{W}
geometrically fast in the rough topology as
N\to\infty
.

Démonstration.

We may use the interpolation inequality (see
[
8
]
, Proposition 5.5)

\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{N}\|_{\alpha-\varepsilon,2\alpha-2\varepsilon}\leq\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{N}\|_{\beta,2\beta}^{1-\kappa}\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{N}\|_{\infty}^{\kappa}.

Thanks to the uniform bound on the
\beta
rough-path distance given in Proposition
3.2
we just need to bound the supremum norm of
\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}_{N}
. For the Weierstrass function
W_{b_{i},\alpha_{i}}
we have the estimate

\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b_{i}^{-n\alpha_{i}}(\cos(b_{i}^{n}\pi t)-\cos(b_{i}^{n}\pi s))\right|\leq\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}2\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b_{i}^{-n\alpha_{i}}\lesssim b_{i}^{-N\alpha_{i}}.

(3.14)

For the iterated integral
I^{N}_{(b_{i},\alpha_{i}),(b_{j},\alpha_{j})}
we need to estimate

\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=N+1}^{\infty}b_{i}^{-n\alpha_{i}}b_{j}^{-\ell\alpha_{j}}J^{n,\ell}(s,t)+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}b_{i}^{-n\alpha_{i}}b_{j}^{-\ell\alpha_{j}}J^{n,\ell}(s,t)+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=N+1}^{\infty}b_{i}^{-n\alpha_{i}}b_{j}^{-\ell\alpha_{j}}J^{n,\ell}(s,t)\right|.

The triangle inequality, bound
(iv)
given in (

\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}|I_{(b_{i},\alpha_{i}),(b_{j},\alpha_{j})}(s,t)-I^{N}_{(b_{i},\alpha_{i}),(b_{j},\alpha_{j})}(s,t)|\lesssim b_{i}^{(-\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon^{\prime})N}b_{j}^{(-\alpha_{j}+\varepsilon^{\prime})N}

(3.15)

for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}
with

. Using estimates (

Remark 3.6
.

Note that we prove everything for Weierstrass functions with
\cos
. However, the same arguments work with little difference if we replace every cosine with sine. However, as shown in
[
15
]
and further studied in
[
14
]
, if we replace one cosine with sine, the arguments above no longer work.

Theorem
3.5
is optimal in the sense that one cannot remove the
-\varepsilon
factor. Indeed, the partial sums of the first order process do not even converge in the function space
C^{\alpha}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We include a proof of this fact for expository reasons, although we suspect it is well known.

Proposition 3.7
.

The partial sums
W_{b,\alpha,N}
do not converge in
C^{\alpha}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
W_{b,\alpha}
.

Démonstration.

First, let
b
be odd. In this case by selecting
t=b^{-N}
and
s=0
in the definition of the Hölder norm we get

\displaystyle\|W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\neq s\in[0,1]}\frac{|(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(t)-(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(s)|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(b^{-N})-(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(0)|}{|b^{-N}|^{\alpha}}.

Using the definition of
W_{b,\alpha}
and
W_{b,\alpha,N}
yields

\displaystyle|(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(b^{-N})-(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(0)||

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b^{-\alpha n}(\cos(b^{n-N}\pi)-\cos(0))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b^{-\alpha n}(-1-1)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b^{-\alpha(n-N)}2\right|

\displaystyle=b^{-N\alpha}\frac{2b^{-\alpha}}{1-b^{-\alpha}},

where we used that
b
was odd in the second line. Therefore

\displaystyle\|W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq b^{-N\alpha}\frac{2b^{-\alpha}}{1-b^{-\alpha}}b^{N\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{2b^{-\alpha}}{1-b^{-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\not\to 0.

Now, let
b
be even. In this case we can let
t=b^{-N-1}
and
s=0
to get

\displaystyle\|W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\neq s\in[0,1]}\frac{|(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(t)-(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(s)|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(b^{-N-1})-(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(0)|}{|b^{-N-1}|^{\alpha}}.

Again using the definition of
W_{b,\alpha}
and
W_{b,\alpha,N}
yields

\displaystyle|(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(b^{-N-1})-(W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N})(0)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b^{-\alpha n}(\cos(b^{n-N-1}\pi)-\cos(0))\right|

\displaystyle=2b^{-\alpha(N+1)}

where we used that
\cos(b^{n-N-1}\pi)-\cos(0)=0
for all
n>N+1
(thanks to
b
being even) and is
-2
if
n=N+1
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|W_{b,\alpha}-W_{b,\alpha,N}\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 2b^{-\alpha(N+1)}b^{\alpha(N+1)}

\displaystyle=2

\displaystyle\not\to 0.

∎

3.1.
Pointwise Convergence

The power of our main Theorem
3.5
is the convergence in the rough topology of the approximating iterated integrals. We used the estimate (
) in order to get convergence in the rough topology and we needed an estimate on the supremum norm to get the rate of convergence. However, if we are interested only in pointwise convergence, the following proposition addresses a broad class of functions defined as trigonometric series.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be two sequences of positive real numbers. Define

f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}e^{i\alpha_{n}t},\qquad g(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{n}e^{i\beta_{n}t},

where
\{c_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{d_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are two sequences of complex numbers so that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|c_{n}|<\infty,\qquad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|d_{n}|<\infty.

Define, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the partial sums

f_{N}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}e^{i\alpha_{n}t},\qquad g_{N}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}d_{n}e^{i\beta_{n}t}.

Then for every
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{s}^{t}f_{N}(r)dg_{N}(r)

exists.

Démonstration.

As
g_{N}
is smooth we can write

\int_{s}^{t}f_{N}(r)dg_{N}(r)=\int_{s}^{t}f_{N}(r)g_{N}^{\prime}(r)dr

and by linearity

g_{N}^{\prime}(r)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(i\beta_{n})d_{n}e^{i\beta_{n}r}.

For
N>M
we can write

f_{N}(r)g_{N}^{\prime}(r)-f_{M}(r)g_{m}^{\prime}(r)=f_{N}(r)\left(g_{N}^{\prime}(r)-g_{M}^{\prime}(r)\right)+g_{M}^{\prime}(r)\left(f_{N}(r)-f_{M}(r)\right).

We have that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}f_{N}(r)(g_{N}^{\prime}(r)-g_{M}^{\prime}(r))dr

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{N}\sum_{n_{2}=M+1}^{N}c_{n_{1}}(i\beta_{n_{2}})d_{n_{2}}e^{ir(\alpha_{n_{1}}+\beta_{n_{2}})}dr

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{N}\sum_{n_{2}=M+1}^{N}c_{n_{1}}(i\beta_{n_{2}})d_{n_{2}}\frac{e^{it(\alpha_{n_{1}}+\beta_{n_{2}})}-e^{is(\alpha_{n_{1}}+\beta_{n_{2}})}}{i(\alpha_{n_{1}}+\beta_{n_{2}})}.

Crucially, as
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
are positive, we get that

\left|(i\beta_{n_{2}})\frac{e^{it(\alpha_{n_{1}}+\beta_{n_{2}})}-e^{is(\alpha_{n_{1}}+\beta_{n_{2}})}}{i(\alpha_{n_{1}}+\beta_{n_{2}})}\right|\leq 2.

Using this bound we obtain the estimate

\left|\int_{s}^{t}f_{N}(r)(g_{N}^{\prime}(r)-g_{M}^{\prime}(r))dr\right|\leq 2\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{N}|c_{n_{1}}|\sum_{n_{2}=M+1}^{N}|d_{n_{2}}|.

As
M,N\to\infty
the right hand side of this inequality goes to
0
. Analogously we get that

\left|\int_{s}^{t}g_{M}^{\prime}(r)(f_{N}(r)-f_{M}(r))dr\right|\leq 2\sum_{n_{1}=M+1}^{N}|c_{n_{1}}|\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{N}|d_{n_{2}}|,

with again the right hand side going to
0
as
M,N\to\infty
.
∎

One important consequence of Proposition
3.8
is to consider the “complex" Weierstrass functions

f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}n}e^{ib_{1}^{n}\pi t}

In this case, Proposition
3.8
implies that the limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{s}^{t}f_{N}(r)dg_{N}(r)

G_{1}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}n}\cos(b_{2}^{n}\pi t),\qquad G_{2}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}n}\sin(b_{2}^{n}\pi t)

f=F_{1}+F_{2}i

3.8
along with taking the imaginary part implies that one can make sense of the integral
