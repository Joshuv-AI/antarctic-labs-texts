Abstract
. In this article we establish two new results on quantitative Diophantine approximation for one-parameter families of diagonal ternary indefinite forms. In the first result, we consider quadratic forms taking values at prime points. In the second, we examine inhomogeneous forms of arbitrary degree taking values at integer points.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
11D75, 11E20, 11E76, 11L07, 11P32

1.
Introduction

Let
Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})
be a real, nondegenerate, indefinite quadratic form in
s\geqslant 3
variables, that is not a multiple of a quadratic form with rational coefficients. A famous theorem of Margulis asserts that, under these conditions,
Q(\mathbb{Z}^{s})
is a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
. This settled an old conjecture of Oppenheim. Thus, given a real number
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
such that

0<|Q(\mathbf{x})|<\varepsilon.

We may reformulate Margulis’s theorem in the following manner:
there is a sequence
\delta(N)\to 0
(depending on
Q
) such that for all sufficiently large
N
,

\min_{0<|\mathbf{x}|<N}|Q(\mathbf{x})|\leqslant\delta(N).

It is then natural to seek good upper bounds for
\delta(N)
. Note that while Margulis’s theorem establishes the solubility of the above inequality, it gives no upper bound on the size of
\mathbf{x}
in terms of
\varepsilon
.

For diagonal forms in at least
5
variables Birch and Davenport
[
5
]
showed using the circle method that we may take
\delta(N)\ll N^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
. For general quadratic forms in at least
5
variables, upper bounds for
\delta(N)
have been obtained by Buterus, Götze, Hille and Margulis
[
9
]
. Using sieve methods, Iwaniec
[
21
]
showed that
\delta(N)\ll N^{-\varepsilon},
for some small
\varepsilon>0
, for certain diagonal quadratic forms in
4
variables; a result which has unfortunately been overlooked in subsequent mentions of Oppenheim’s conjecture in the mathematical literature.

In an important recent development, Lindenstrauss and Margulis
[
24
]
have shown that there exists
\kappa>0
such that
\delta(N)\ll(\log N)^{-\kappa},
for an explicit set of ternary quadratic forms satisfying a Diophantine condition, and this is currently the state of the art for ternary quadratic forms.

However, one can do significantly better by averaging over suitable families of quadratic forms. Indeed, let

Q(\mathbf{x})=x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2}.

Then for any fixed
\alpha_{2}>0
and almost all
\alpha_{3}\in[1/2,1],
Bourgain
[
6
]
showed that
\delta(N)\ll N^{-2/5+\varepsilon}
. In the same article, assuming the Lindelöf hypothesis for the Riemann zeta function, Bourgain improved this to
\delta(N)\ll N^{-1+\varepsilon}
. This result is essentially optimal, as can be seen by simple pigeonhole heuristics (see e.g.
[
12
, Page 2]
).

Furthermore, Bourgain’s result has been generalised to diagonal ternary forms of higher degree by work of Schindler
[
32
]
. The analogue of Bourgain’s theorem for generic ternary indefinite forms was proved by Ghosh and Kelmer
[
14
]
. There has been extensive work recently in proving effective results for generic (i.e. a full measure set in the corresponding moduli space) forms, see
[
12
,
1
,
22
,
13
]
.

In this article, we consider the problem of obtaining quantitative results to ternary Diophantine inequalities on average over a one-parameter family in the spirit of Bourgain and Schindler (op. cit.).

1.1.
Diophantine inequalities over primes

A central topic in number theory is the investigation of prime solutions to Diophantine equations. Two well-known results of this kind are Vinogradov’s theorem
[
35
]
, which states that any odd integer can be written as a sum of three primes, and Hua’s
[
17
]
theorem, that any sufficiently large integer
n\equiv 5\pmod{24}
can be written as a sum of five squares of primes. We refer the reader to the conjectures of Bourgain, Gamburd and Sarnak
[
7
]
on the existence of prime points on affine varieties.

A related problem concerns Diophantine approximation in the primes. Given non-zero real numbers
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
, not all of the same sign, Baker
[
2
]
showed that if at least one of the ratios
\lambda_{i}/\lambda_{j}
is irrational, then there are infinitely many primes
p_{i}
such that

|\lambda_{1}p_{1}+\lambda_{2}p_{2}+\lambda_{3}p_{3}|<(\log p)^{-A},

where
p=\max_{i=1,2,3}p_{i}
, for any
A>0
(see also recent work of Matomäki
[
28
]
).

For quadratic forms in at least
5
variables, and higher degree forms in sufficiently many variables, quantitative results were first obtained independently by Ramachandra
[
30
]
and Vaughan
[
34
]
. Even though these results have since been improved (see
[
3
,
10
]
), the number of variables required in the quadratic case has remained at
5
.

By averaging over a one-parameter family, we show the following quantitative Diophantine approximation theorem for ternary diagonal quadratic forms.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A>0
be an integer. For any fixed
\alpha_{2}>0
and almost every
\alpha_{3}\in[1/2,1],
we have

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{i}\sim N\\
p_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}|p_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}p_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{3}p_{3}^{2}|\ll_{A,\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}}(\log N)^{-A}.

In addition to the Vinogradov-Korobov zero-free region, the key input in the proof of the theorem is a sharp upper bound for the shifted sum

\sum_{0\leqslant l\leqslant L}\sum_{m\leqslant M}r(m)r(m+l),

where
r(n)
is the number of ways of representing an integer as a sum of two squares. Similarly, to obtain a version of Theorem
1.1
for degree
k\geqslant 3
forms, one would need sharp upper bounds for the sum

\sum_{0\leqslant l\leqslant L}\sum_{m\leqslant M}r_{k}(m)r_{k}(m+l)

where
r_{k}(m)
is the number of ways of writing
m
as a sum of two
k
-th powers, which appears to be a challenging prospect.

It will be apparent from the proof of Theorem
1.1
that it can be stated in slightly greater generality. Indeed, we may replace the primes with elements that lie in any other set
S,
provided that we understand the distribution of the elements of
S
in short intervals (i.e. intervals of the form
[N,N+N^{\beta}]
for some
\beta>0
), and if we can show that

\sum_{n\in S}w(n/N)n^{it}=o(|[N,2N]\cap S|),

uniformly for
N^{1/10}\leqslant|t|\leqslant N^{3}
and for
w(x)
any smooth function with support in
[1/2,2]
. For example, in this way, one can prove a version of Theorem
1.1
with the primes replaced with integers that are sums of two squares, but we leave the details to the interested reader.

1.2.
Effective Oppenheim conjecture for inhomogeneous quadratic forms

Our second theorem considers inhomogeneous ternary forms. An inhomogeneous quadratic form is a quadratic form together with a fixed shift. Namely, let
F
be a quadratic form and
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
be a vector. Then the inhomogeneous quadratic form is
F(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\theta})
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
Values of inhomogeneous forms at integer points have been studied extensively
[
27
,
26
,
25
,
33
,
4
]
. In
[
15
,
16
]
, Ghosh, Kelmer and Yu proved effective results for inhomogeneous quadratic forms. In
[
15
]
, they considered the case of a fixed shift, and in the case of a fixed rational shift obtained in addition, an analogue of Bourgain’s theorem above. More precisely, the averaging in their result is over all quadratic forms rather than diagonal forms. In
[
16
]
, they treated the case of a fixed quadratic form and let the shift vary. A fuller account of these recent developments can be found in our upcoming survey. Here we consider ternary diagonal inhomogeneous forms and prove the following quantitative theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer and let
F(\mathbf{x})=x_{1}^{k}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{k}-\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{k}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a fixed vector. Then for any fixed
\alpha_{2}>0
and almost every
\alpha_{3}\in[1/2,1]
the following statements hold:

(1)

Assume the exponent pair conjecture. Then we have

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
|\mathbf{x}|\sim N\end{subarray}}|F(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\theta)}|\ll_{\varepsilon,\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\bm{\theta}}N^{k-3+\varepsilon}.

(2)

Unconditionally, we have

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
|\mathbf{x}|\sim N\end{subarray}}|F(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\theta)}|\ll_{\varepsilon,\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\bm{\theta}}N^{k-12/5+\varepsilon}.

We will now briefly recall the exponent pair conjecture. For a more detailed account, see e.g.
[
20
, §8.4]
. Let
e(x)=\exp(2\pi ix)
. For real numbers
p,q
, such that
0\leqslant p,q\leqslant 1/2
, we say that
(p,q)
is an exponent pair if for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
we have the bound

\sum_{N\leqslant n\leqslant N^{\prime}}e(f(n))\ll_{\varepsilon}F^{p}N^{q-p+1/2}F^{\varepsilon},

for any
2\leqslant N\leqslant N^{\prime}\leqslant 2N
, with
f(x)
a smooth function on
[N,2N]
such that there exists a positive real number
F\geqslant N
with the property that for any
j\geqslant 0
we have

FN^{-j}\ll_{j}|f^{(j)}(x)|\ll_{j}FN^{-j}

for any
x\in[N,2N]
. The Exponent Pair Conjecture is the assertion that
(0,0)
is an exponent pair. This conjecture, for example, implies the truth of the Lindelöf hypothesis for the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
.

In the course of proving Theorem
1.2
, we also prove sharp upper bounds for the following Diophantine inequality in four variables, which is reminiscent of the problem of simultaneous inhomogeneous Diophantine approximation on curves.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\alpha,\beta
be fixed real numbers. Let
0<\delta<1
. Define

\mathscr{N}(M,\alpha,\delta)
to be the number of solutions
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},m_{4})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\cap[M,2M]^{4}
to the inequality

(1.1)

|(m_{1}+\theta_{1})^{\alpha}-(m_{2}+\theta_{1})^{\alpha}+\beta(m_{3}+\theta_{2})^{\alpha}-\beta(m_{4}+\theta_{2})^{\alpha}|\leqslant\delta M^{\alpha}.

Suppose that
\alpha\neq 0,1
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\mathscr{N}(M,\alpha,\delta)\ll_{\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon,\theta_{1},\theta_{2}}M^{2+\varepsilon}+\delta M^{4+\varepsilon}.

The result with
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=0
is a well-known result of Robert and Sargos
[
31
, Theorem 2]
and the proof of Theorem
1.3
is an adaptation of their argument. We should remark here that while the bound in the theorem with
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=0
can be deduced from recent work of Huang
[
18
]
, it appears much more difficult to do this in the case where
\theta_{i}\neq 0
.

1.3.
Averaging over two-parameter families

In this article, we have considered one-parameter families of ternary diagonal forms. One can ask if it might be possible to prove stronger results in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
by also averaging over
\alpha_{2}
(as in
[
6
]
and
[
32
]
). In order to exploit this extra averaging, we will require sharp upper bounds for

\int_{T}^{2T}|\sum_{n\sim N}\Lambda(n)n^{it}|^{2}\,dt,

in the case of Theorem
1.1
, and for

\int_{T}^{2T}|\sum_{n\sim N}(n+\theta_{3})^{it}|^{2}\,dt,

in the case of Theorem
1.2
, with
1\ll T\ll N^{3}
. One could analyse the integrals using the Montgomery-Vaughan inequality
[
29
, Corollary 3]
, but this is effective only when
T\gg N
, and we are unable to estimate them satisfactorily in the complementary range. We conclude our introduction by remarking that if
\theta_{3}=a/q
is a rational number, then by splitting
n
into arithmetic progressions modulo
q
, one can nevertheless prove the bound
\int_{T}^{2T}|\sum_{n\sim N}(n+\theta_{3})^{it}|^{2}\,dt\ll_{\theta_{3},\varepsilon}TN^{1+\varepsilon}
, which will enable us to prove an optimal version of Theorem
1.2
by averaging over both the parameters
\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{3}.

2.
A Diophantine inequality over the primes

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Using the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it is sufficient to prove the following

Proposition 2.1
.

Fix
\alpha_{2}>0
. Let
N
be a large parameter. Assume that
\delta<1
. The inequality

|x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2}|<\delta

has a non-trivial solution with
x_{i}\sim N
and
x_{i}
prime, for all
\alpha_{3}\in[1/2,1]
except for a set of Lebesgue measure at most

\ll\delta^{-1}(\log N)^{8}e^{-c(\log N)^{1/3}(\log\log N)^{-1/3}},

for some
c>0
.

Let
\omega_{0}(x)
and
\omega(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{3}\omega_{i}(x_{i})
be smooth functions as in
[
32
, Section 2]
. In particular,
\omega_{0}(x)
is an odd function.

We seek a lower bound for the sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
x_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{x}/N)\bm{1}_{|x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2}|<\delta}.

Since
|\log(m/n)|\geqslant|m-n|/(m+n)
, it suffices to obtain a lower bound for the sum

S_{1}(\alpha_{3})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
x_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{x}/N)\bm{1}_{|\log(x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2})-\log(\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2})|<c_{0}\delta N^{-2}}

for
c_{0}>0
. Define the exponential sums

F_{1}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
x_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega_{1}(x_{1}/N)\omega_{2}(x_{2}/N)e^{it\log(x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2})}

and

F_{2}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathbb{Z}\\
p\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega_{3}(p/N)e^{it\log p}.

Let
T=\frac{2N^{2}}{c_{0}\delta}
and define

S_{2}(\alpha_{3})=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{\omega_{0}}(t/T)F_{1}(t)F_{2}(-2t)e^{-it\log\alpha_{3}}\,dt.

Then we have
S_{1}(\alpha_{3})\geqslant S_{2}(\alpha_{3})
. Let
0<\theta<1
be a real number that we will specify shortly. Split

\widehat{\omega_{0}}(t/T)=\widehat{\omega_{0}}(t/N^{1-\theta})+\left(\widehat{\omega_{0}}(t/T)-\widehat{\omega_{0}}(t/N^{1-\theta}\right).

Let
S_{3}(\alpha_{3})
denote the contribution from the former and
S_{4}(\alpha_{3})
the contribution from the latter to
S_{2}(\alpha_{3})
. As in
[
6
]
and
[
32
]
, our strategy will be to obtain a lower bound for
S_{3}(\alpha_{3})
and an upper bound for the set of
\alpha_{3}
such that
S_{4}(\alpha_{3})
is ‘large’.

2.1.
A lower bound for
S_{3}(\alpha)

To obtain a lower bound for
S_{3}(\alpha)
, we will require information on the distribution of primes in short intervals.

Let
\pi(x)
denote the number of primes not exceeding
x
. By work of Huxley
[
19
]
, we know that if
\theta>7/12
, then there exists
x(\theta)
such that for all
x\geqslant x(\theta)
we have

\pi(x+x^{\theta})-\pi(x)\sim\frac{x^{\theta}}{\log x}.

For the rest of this section, we will fix
\theta=2/3
. Then we have the following result.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\theta=2/3.
Then there exists a constant
c_{2}>0
such that for all
\alpha_{3}
in
[1/2,1]
we have

S_{3}(\alpha_{3})\geqslant c_{2}\delta N/(\log N)^{3}.

Proof.

We have

S_{3}(\alpha_{3})\geqslant\frac{N^{1-\theta}}{T}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
x_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{x}/N)\bm{1}_{[|\log(x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2})-\log(\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2})|<N^{-1+\theta}]}.

Hence there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that

S_{3}(\alpha_{3})\geqslant\frac{N^{1-\theta}}{T}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
x_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{x}/N)\bm{1}_{[|x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2}-\ \alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2}|<c_{1}N^{1+\theta}]}.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
x_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega(\mathbf{x}/N)

\displaystyle\bm{1}_{[|x_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2}-\ \alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2}|<c_{1}N^{1+\theta}]}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\\
x_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega_{2}(x_{2}/N)\omega_{3}(x_{3}/N)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2}-c_{1}N^{1+\theta}\leqslant x_{1}^{2}\leqslant\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{2}+c_{1}N^{1+\theta}\\
x_{1}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\omega_{1}(x_{1}).

Since
\theta=2/3>7/12
, we see that the inner sum is
\gg N^{\theta}/\log N
for any fixed
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
. Summing over
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

2.2.
Reduction to a counting problem

Using Chebyshev’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\meas\left\{\alpha_{3}\in[1/2,1]:|S_{4}(\alpha_{3})|\geqslant\frac{c_{2}}{2}\frac{\delta N}{(\log N)^{3}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4c_{2}^{-2}\delta^{-2}(\log N)^{6}N^{-2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\int_{1/2}^{1}|S_{4}(\alpha_{3})|^{2}\,d\alpha_{3}.

Since
\omega_{0}
was chosen to be an odd function, there exists a constant
c_{3}
such that

\bigg|\omega_{0}\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)-\omega_{0}\left(\frac{t}{N^{1/3}}\right)\bigg|\leqslant c_{3}\min\left(1,\frac{t^{2}}{N^{2/3}},\left(\frac{T}{|t|}\right)^{10}\right).

By Parseval’s theorem, we get

\int_{1/2}^{1}|S_{4}(\alpha_{3})|^{2}\,d\alpha_{3}\leqslant c_{3}^{2}T^{-2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\min\left(1,\frac{t^{4}}{N^{4/3}},\left(\frac{T}{|t|}\right)^{20}\right)|F_{1}(t)|^{2}|F_{2}(t)|^{2}\,dt.

Estimating
F_{1}(t)
and
F_{2}(t)
trivially over the interval
|t|\leqslant N^{1/10}
, and using
[
32
, Lemma 2.2]
in the complementary range we find that

\meas\left\{\alpha_{3}\in[1/2,1]:|S_{4}(\alpha_{3})|\geqslant(c_{2}/2)\delta N/(\log N)^{3}\right\}\leqslant c_{4}(\log N)^{6}N^{-5/6}+I_{2},

where

(2.1)

I_{2}\leqslant N^{-6}(\log N)^{7}\max_{|t|\geqslant N^{1/10}}\left(\min\left(1,\left(\frac{T}{|t|}\right)\right)|F_{2}(t)|\right)^{2}\sup_{1/\log N\ll U\ll T}I_{4}(U)

with

I_{4}(U)=U\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\\
y_{i}\text{ prime}\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\omega}(\mathbf{y}/N)\bm{1}_{[|y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{2}(y_{3}^{2}-y_{4})^{2}|\ll N^{2}/U}.

Applying Theorem
1.3
with
\alpha=2
and
\theta_{i}=0
, we easily obtain the bound

I_{4}(U)\ll(U+N^{2})N^{2+\varepsilon}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. In the next result, we show that we may remove the
N^{\varepsilon}
factor at the expense of a power of
\log N
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
1/\log N\leqslant U\leqslant T
, we have

I_{4}(U)\ll\left(UN^{2}+N^{4}\right)(\log N).

Proof.

To prove the lemma, we may drop the constraint that
y_{i}
are prime. Suppose first that
U\ll N
. Fixing
y_{2},y_{3}
and
y_{4}
we find that
x_{1}
is constrained by the inequality

|y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{3}(y_{3}^{2}-y_{4}^{2})|\ll N^{2}/U,

which forces
y_{1}^{2}
to lie in an interval of length
\ll N^{2}/U
. As a result, we get that

I_{4}(U)\ll UN^{3}(N/U)\ll N^{4},

which is sufficient. So we may suppose for the rest of the argument that
U\gg N
. Since
\widetilde{\omega}
is non-negative, it suffices to show that

\#\left\{y_{i}\sim N:|y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{2}(y_{3}^{2}-y_{4}^{2})|\ll N^{2}/U\right\}\ll N^{2}(1+N^{2}/U)(\log N).

We will now proceed as in the proof of
[
8
, Lemma 7]
. We are grateful to Tim Browning for suggesting its use in the proof of this lemma. For
\beta<N
put

N(\beta,N)=\#\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\cap[N,2N]^{4}:|y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{2}(y_{3}^{2}-y_{4}^{2})|\leqslant\beta\right\}

and

N(n;1,2)=\#\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[N,2N]^{2}:n\beta\leqslant y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}\leqslant(n+3)\beta\right\}.

Defining
N(n;3,4)
similarly, we have that

\displaystyle N(\beta,N)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}N(n;1,2)\left\{N(-(n+4);3,4)+N(-(n+1);3,4)\right\}

\displaystyle\ll\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}N(n;1,2)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}N(-n^{\prime};3,4)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Thus setting

M(\beta,N)=\#\left\{N\leqslant y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2}\leqslant 2N:|(y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}-(z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})|\right\}\leqslant 3\beta

we find that

N(\beta,N)\ll M(\beta,N)^{1/2}M(\beta/\alpha_{2},N)^{1/2}.

Let
F(\mathbf{x})=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle M(\beta,N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|l|\leqslant 3\beta\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\sim N\\
F(\mathbf{x})=l\end{subarray}}1\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant l\leqslant 3\beta\end{subarray}}\sum_{m\leqslant 8N^{2}}r(m)r(m+l),

where
r(m)
is the number of ways of writing an integer as the sum of two squares. The lemma now follows by adapting the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 1]
.

For a more direct proof, we may appeal to
[
23
, Theorem 1.4]
to get

\displaystyle M(\beta,N)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2}\log N+\sum_{1\leqslant l\leqslant 3\beta}d(l)N^{2}+O\left((1+\beta)N^{19/20+\varepsilon}(1+\beta/N^{2})^{3/20+\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2}\log N+(1+\beta)\log(2+\beta)N^{2},

since
\beta<N
, by assumption. The lemma follows upon setting
\beta=N^{2}/U
.
∎

2.3.
Proof of Proposition
2.1

Using a zero-free region for the Riemann zeta function, we will now prove an upper bound for
F_{2}(t)
. Proposition
2.1
will then follow from (
2.1
), Lemma
2.3
and the following lemma since

F_{2}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{3}(n/N)\Lambda(n)n^{it}+O(N^{1/2}\log N),

where
\Lambda(n)
denotes the von Mangoldt function.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
w(x)
be a smooth function with support in
[1/2,2]
. Suppose that
N^{1/10}\leqslant|t|\leqslant N^{3}.
Then there exists a real number
c>0
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w(n/N)\Lambda(n)n^{it}\ll N\exp\left(-c(\log N)(\log|t|)^{-2/3}(\log\log|t|)^{-\frac{1}{3}}\right).

Proof.

By Mellin inversion we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w(n/N)\Lambda(n)n^{it}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma_{0})}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds

for
\sigma_{0}=1+1/\log N.
Let
Y=|t|^{1/10}.
Since
\widetilde{w}(s)\ll_{A}(1+|s|)^{-A}
for
0<\operatorname{Re}(s)<2
, we have

\int_{\sigma_{0}\pm iY}^{\sigma_{0}\pm i\infty}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds\ll_{A}\frac{N(\log N)^{2}}{Y^{A}}.

Thus we get for any
A>0
that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w(n/N)\Lambda(n)n^{it}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma_{0}-iY}^{\sigma_{0}+iY}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds+O_{A}\left(\frac{N(\log N)^{2}}{Y^{A}}\right).

We will now move the line of integration to

\sigma_{1}=1-c(\log 2|t|)^{-\frac{2}{3}}(\log\log 2|t|))^{-\frac{1}{3}}

for
c>0
so that we may apply
[
20
, Theorem 8.29]
to ensure that
\zeta(s-it)\neq 0
for
\operatorname{Re}(s)\geqslant\sigma_{1}
and
\operatorname{Im}(s)\leqslant Y
. In this process, we pick up a pole at
s=1+it
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w(n/N)\Lambda(n)n^{it}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{w}(1+it)N^{1+it}-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma_{0}-iY}^{\sigma_{1}-iY}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma_{0}+iY}^{\sigma_{1}+iY}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma_{1}-iY}^{\sigma_{1}+iY}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds.

Since
|t|\geqslant N^{1/10}
by hypothesis, we see that the first term is bounded by
O_{A}(N|t|^{-A})=O(N^{1-A/10}),
for any
A>0
. Using
[
20
, Theorem 8.29]
to bound
\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)
we get that

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma_{0}\pm iY}^{\sigma_{1}\pm iY}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds

\displaystyle\ll(\sigma_{0}-\sigma_{1})\frac{N}{Y^{A}}(\log|t|)^{\frac{2}{3}}(\log\log|t|)^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{N(\log N)^{2}}{Y^{A}},

since
|t|\leqslant N^{3}
, by assumption. Finally, using the bound
\widetilde{w}(s)\ll|s|^{-1}
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma_{1}-iY}^{\sigma_{1}+iY}\widetilde{w}(s)N^{s}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s-it)\,ds

\displaystyle\ll N^{1-c(\log 2|t|)^{-\frac{2}{3}}(\log\log 2|t|)^{-\frac{1}{3}}}\times

\displaystyle(\log|t|)^{\frac{2}{3}}(\log\log|t|)^{\frac{1}{3}}\int_{-Y}^{Y}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{1+|y|^{2}}}.

Putting together all the estimates completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
w(x)
be a smooth function with support in
[1/4,2]
. For
\theta
a real number define

S(N)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}w(n/N)(n+\theta)^{it}.

Suppose that
|t|\geqslant N^{\frac{1}{10}}
. Suppose the exponent pair conjecture holds. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

S(N)\ll_{\theta,\varepsilon}N^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}(1+|t|)^{\varepsilon}.

Unconditionally, we have

S(N)\ll_{\theta,\varepsilon}N^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}(1+|t|)^{\frac{1}{6}+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Write
\theta=[\theta]+\left\{\theta\right\}
, where
[\theta]
is the integer part and
\left\{\theta\right\}
is the fractional part of
\theta
. Then we have

\displaystyle S(N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}w\left(\frac{m-[\theta]}{N}\right)(m+\left\{\theta\right\})^{it}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}w(m/N)(m+\left\{\theta\right\})^{it}+O_{\theta}(1).

If
\left\{\theta\right\}=0,
then the lemma follows from
[
6
, Equation (2.6)]
and the remark following the equation. So for the rest of the proof we may assume that
0<\theta<1.

For
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
let

\zeta(s,\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+\theta)^{-s}

denote the Hurwitz zeta function. Note that if
\theta=1
, we have
\zeta(s,1)=\zeta(s)
, the Riemann-zeta function. The function
\zeta(s,\theta)
has a simple pole with residue
1
at
s=1
, and has analytic continuation to the entire complex plane. Using Mellin inversion, we may write

\displaystyle S(N)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{3/2-i\infty}^{3/2+i\infty}\widetilde{w}(s)\zeta(s-it,\theta)N^{s}\,ds.

Note that by repeatedly integrating by parts, we have the bound
\widetilde{w}(s)\ll_{A}(1+|s|)^{-A}
for any
A>0
in the region
0<\operatorname{Re}(s)<2
. Moving the line of integration to
\operatorname{Re}(s)=1/2
, we pick up a pole at
s=1
to obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle S(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}N^{1/2+ix}\zeta(1/2+i(x-t),\theta)\widetilde{w}(1/2+ix)\,dx+N^{1+it}\widetilde{w}(1+it).

Using partial summation, for
1/2\leqslant\operatorname{Re}(s)\leqslant 3/2
we have

\zeta(\sigma+it,\theta)=\sum_{n\leqslant|t|}(n+\theta)^{-\sigma-it}+O((1+|t|)^{-\sigma}).

Furthermore, by a dyadic argument, we obtain

\displaystyle\zeta(1/2+it,\theta)\ll(1+|t|)^{\varepsilon}\max_{1\leqslant K\leqslant|t|}\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\bigg|\sum_{K\leqslant n\leqslant 2K}(n+\theta)^{-it}\bigg|+O((1+|t|)^{-1/2}).

If
(p,q)
is an exponent pair (see e.g.
[
20
, §8.4]

\sum_{K\leqslant n\leqslant 2K}(n+\theta)^{-it}\ll|t|^{p}K^{q-p+1/2}(|t|K)^{\varepsilon}

for any
K\leqslant|t|
. From this we get the bound
\zeta(1/2+it,\theta)\ll_{\varepsilon,\theta}(1+|t|)^{\varepsilon}
by assuming the Exponent Pair Conjecture, which is the assertion that
(0,0)
is an exponent pair. And by using the exponent pair

we get the bound
\zeta(1/2+it,\theta)\ll(1+|t|)^{1/6+\varepsilon}
. Inserting these bounds into (
3.4
), and using the decay properties of
\widetilde{w}
(recall that
|t|\geqslant N^{1/10}
) completes the proof of the lemma.

4.
A Diophantine inequality in four variables

Our aim in this section will be to prove Theorem
1.3
. Given two functions
\varphi_{1}(x)
and
\varphi_{2}(x),
let
\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,\alpha,\delta)
denote the number of solutions
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},m_{4})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\cap[M,2M]^{4}
such that

|\varphi_{1}(m_{1})-\varphi_{1}(m_{2})+\varphi_{2}(m_{3})-\varphi_{2}(m_{4})|\leqslant\delta M^{\alpha}.

If

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(x)=(x+\theta_{1})^{\alpha}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\varphi_{2}(x)=\beta(x+\theta_{2})^{\alpha},

then for this choice of functions, we have
\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,\alpha,\delta)=\mathscr{N}(M,\alpha,\delta)
, where
\mathscr{N}(M,\alpha,\delta)
was defined in (
1.1
). By arguing as in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, it suffices to prove Theorem
1.3
with
\beta=1
, which we will assume from now on.

4.1.
Some lemmas

In this section, we will prove some lemmas that go into the proof of Theorem
1.3
. These lemmas are based on
[
31
, Lemmas 1–7]
, and are proved along similar lines. We begin with a result which expresses
\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,\alpha,\delta)
in terms of exponential sums. For convenience, where there is no ambiguity, we write
\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,\delta)
in place of
\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,\alpha,\delta).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X>0
and
c\geqslant 0
be real numbers. Suppose that
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are complex numbers such that
|a_{n}|,|b_{n}|\leqslant 1
. Le
\varphi_{1}(x)
and
\varphi_{2}(x)
be real valued functions. Then

(4.1)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{X}\int_{c}^{c+X}&\bigg|\sum_{m=M+1}^{2M}a_{n}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\times\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}b_{n}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,2/X)\end{split}

and

(4.2)

\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,1/X)\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{X}\int_{0}^{X/2}\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda(a)=\max(0,1-|a|)
. Then the Fourier transform of
\Lambda(a)
is given by

\sinc^{2}(a)=\begin{cases}(\sin\pi a)^{2}/(\pi a)^{2}&\mbox{ if $a\neq 0,$}\\
1&\mbox{ if $a=0.$}\end{cases}

For
\delta>0
consider the integral

I=\delta\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sinc^{2}(\delta(x-c))\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}a_{n}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}b_{n}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx.

Then

\begin{split}I&\leqslant\delta\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\\
n_{1},n_{2}\end{subarray}}\bigg|\int\sinc^{2}(\delta(x-c))\times\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m_{1})}{M^{\alpha}}-\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m_{2})}{M^{\alpha}}+\frac{x\varphi_{2}(n_{1})}{M^{\alpha}}-\frac{x\varphi_{2}(n_{2})}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\,dx\bigg|\\
\end{split}

\begin{split}&\leqslant\delta\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\\
n_{1},n_{2}\end{subarray}}\Lambda\left(\delta^{-1}\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}(m_{1})}{M^{\alpha}}-\frac{\varphi_{1}(m_{2})}{M^{\alpha}}+\frac{\varphi_{2}(n_{1})}{M^{\alpha}}-\frac{\varphi_{2}(n_{2})}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\\
&\leqslant\mathscr{N}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}(M,\delta),\end{split}

by Fourier inversion. Also, by positivity we get

\begin{split}I\geqslant&\delta\int_{c}^{c+(2\delta)^{-1}}\sinc^{2}(\delta(x-c))\bigg|\sum_{m=M+1}^{2M}a_{m}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\times\\
&\quad\quad\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}b_{n}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx.\end{split}

Note that in the interval
[c,c+(2\delta)^{-1}]
, we have
\sinc^{2}(\delta(x-c))\geqslant 4/\pi^{2}
whence (
4.1
) follows by taking
\delta=2/X
. The proof of (
4.2
) follows along similar lines.
∎

f^{\prime}([M,M_{1}])=[a,b]
and
x_{l}
is the unique solution to the equation
f^{\prime}(x)=l
. Let
f^{*}(l)=f(x_{l})-lx_{l}
.
By partial summation, we get

c\sum_{a\leqslant l\leqslant b}\frac{e(f^{*}(l))}{\sqrt{|f^{\prime\prime}(x_{l})|}}\ll\frac{M}{X^{1/2}}\bigg|\sum_{a\leqslant l\leqslant b}e(f^{*}(l))\bigg|^{*}.

It is easy to see that

L\ll|f^{\prime}(m)|\ll L_{1}

whenever
x\in[X,2X]
and
M+1\leqslant m\leqslant M_{1}
. Therefore, we have

\sum_{n=M+1}^{M_{1}}e(f(n))=\frac{M}{X^{1/2}}\bigg|\sum_{L\leqslant l\leqslant L_{1}}e(f^{*}(l))\bigg|^{*}+O(M^{1/2}).

All that is left is to compute
f^{*}(l)
. Observe that if

g(m)=A(m+\theta)^{\alpha},\,\,\text{then}\,\,\,g^{*}(l)=(1-\alpha)A^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\left(\frac{l}{\alpha}\right)^{\overline{\alpha}}+l\theta.

To see this, we begin by noting that
g^{\prime}(x)=A\alpha(x+\theta)^{\alpha-1}
.
Thus
g^{\prime}(x_{l})=l
if
\alpha A(x_{l}+\theta)^{\alpha-1}=l
.
In other words,
(x_{l}+\theta)=\left(\frac{l}{A\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}},
whence

\displaystyle g^{*}(l)

\displaystyle=A(x_{l}+\theta)^{\alpha}-l(x_{l}+\theta)+l\theta

\displaystyle=l(x_{l}+\theta)\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)+l\theta

\displaystyle=A^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{l}{\alpha}\right)^{\overline{\alpha}}+l\theta.

Applying this with
A=x/M^{\alpha}
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.2.
A recurrence relation

Let
\alpha\neq 0,1
be a real number. For a real number
0<\kappa<1
and integer
M\geqslant 2
, define

(4.3)

\begin{split}I(\kappa,M,\alpha)=\int_{0}^{M^{2-\kappa}}&\bigg|\sum_{M\leqslant m\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}(m)}{M}\right)^{\alpha}\right)\bigg|^{2}\times\\
&\quad\quad\quad\bigg|\sum_{M\leqslant n\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{2}(n)}{M}\right)^{\alpha}\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx,\end{split}

with an analogous definition for
I(\kappa,M,\overline{\alpha}).
As in
[
31
, §4]
we make the following

Definition 4.5
.

We say that hypothesis
\mathscr{H}(\alpha,\kappa)
is satisfied if

I(\kappa,M,\alpha)\ll_{\varepsilon}M^{4+\varepsilon}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,I(\kappa,M,\overline{\alpha})\ll_{\varepsilon}M^{4+\varepsilon}.

We begin with the following result.

Lemma 4.6
.

The hypothesis
\mathscr{H}(\alpha,\kappa)
is true with
\kappa=1/2
.

Proof.

Our task is to show that the integral in (
4.3
) with
\kappa=1/2
is
O(M^{4+\varepsilon}).
Let
X_{0}=\eta M
, where
\eta>0
is a small real number. We write this integral as

I(1/2,M,\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}+\int_{1}^{X_{0}}+\int_{X_{0}}^{M^{3/2}}=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

say. We trivially have
I_{1}\ll M^{4}
. For
1\leqslant x\leqslant X_{0}
, by
[
20
, Lemma 8.8]
we obtain the bound

\sum_{M\leqslant m\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}(m)}{M}\right)^{\alpha}\right)\ll M/x,

whence
I_{2}\ll M^{4+\varepsilon}.
Therefore, it only remains to bound

I_{3}=\int_{X_{0}}^{M^{3/2}}\bigg|\sum_{M\leqslant m\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\bigg|^{2}\bigg|\sum_{M\leqslant m\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx.

We have

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\max_{X_{0}\leqslant x\leqslant M^{3/2}}\bigg|\sum_{M\leqslant m\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\bigg|^{2}\right)\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\int_{0}^{M^{3/2}}\bigg|\sum_{M\leqslant m\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx.

Using van der Corput’s inequality
[
20
, Corollary 8.13]
, we have

\sum_{M\leqslant m\leqslant 2M}e\left(x\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\ll(xM^{-2})^{1/2}M+(xM^{-2})^{-1/2},

which is bounded by
M^{3/4}
since
X_{0}\leqslant x\leqslant M^{3/2}
. We have by Lemma
4.1
that the second factor is

\leqslant M^{3/2}\#\left\{m_{1},m_{2}\sim M:|\varphi_{2}(m_{1})-\varphi_{2}(m_{2})|\leqslant M^{-3/2}\right\}\ll M^{5/2}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Next, we prove the following recurrence relation, which is the key step in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that Let
0<\kappa\leqslant 1/2
. Suppose that the hypothesis
\mathscr{H}(\alpha,\kappa)
is satisfied. Then so is the hypothesis
\mathscr{H}\left(\alpha,\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa}\right).

Proof.

Let
\kappa_{1}=\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa}
. It will suffice to show that
I(\kappa_{1},M,\alpha)\ll M^{4+\varepsilon}
.
Consider the integral

J(X)=\int_{X}^{2X}\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\bigg|\sum_{n=M+1}^{2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(n)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx.

By a standard dyadic argument, we have

\int_{0}^{M^{2-\kappa_{1}}}\ll\int_{0}^{M^{2-\kappa}}+(\log M)\max_{M^{2-\kappa}\ll X\ll M^{2-\kappa_{1}}}\int_{X}^{2X}.

Thus by the recurrence hypothesis, we have

I(\kappa_{1},M,\alpha)\ll M^{4+\varepsilon}+(\log M)\max_{M^{2-\kappa}\ll X\ll M^{2-\kappa_{1}}}J(X).

Applying Lemma
4.4
and then making the substitution
y=\tau(x)
in the ensuing integral, we get that

\begin{split}J(X)&\ll\frac{M^{4}}{X^{2}}\int_{0}^{X_{1}}\left(\bigg|\sum_{L\leqslant l\leqslant L_{1}}e\left(y\left(\frac{l}{L}\right)^{\overline{\alpha}}+l\theta_{1}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\times\\
&\quad\quad\left(\bigg|\sum_{L\leqslant l\leqslant L_{1}}e\left(y\left(\frac{l}{L}\right)^{\overline{\alpha}}+l\theta_{2}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\,dx+M^{4},\end{split}

where
X_{1}\asymp X.
Applying Lemma
4.3
with

a_{l}=\begin{cases}e(l\theta_{1})&\mbox{ if $L\leqslant l\leqslant L_{1}$}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

and similarly for
b_{l},
we get for any
Y\leqslant X_{1}
that

\begin{split}J(X)&\ll\frac{M^{4}(\log M)^{4}}{XY}\int_{0}^{Y}\bigg|\sum_{L\leqslant l\leqslant 2L}e\left(y\left(\frac{l}{L}\right)^{\overline{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{4}\,dx+M^{4}.\end{split}

Choose
Y=L^{2-\kappa}\asymp(X/M)^{2-\kappa}
. Then we have by
[
31
, Lemma 7]
that

\int_{0}^{Y}\bigg|\sum_{L\leqslant l\leqslant 2L}e\left(y\left(\frac{l}{L}\right)^{\overline{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{4}\,dy\ll L^{4+\varepsilon}.

Therefore, we get

J(X)\ll M^{2-\kappa+\varepsilon}X^{1+\kappa}+M^{4}.

Since
X\ll M^{2-\kappa_{1}}
, we get that

J(X)\ll M^{4+\varepsilon}

and the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\alpha\neq 0,1
. Then for any integer
M\geqslant 2
and any real number
\varepsilon>0
we have

\int_{0}^{M^{2}}\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}dx\ll M^{4+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Set

I_{M}=\int_{0}^{M^{2}}\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\,dx

Fix
\varepsilon_{0}>0.
Applying Lemma
4.3
with
X=M^{2}
and
Y=M^{2-\varepsilon_{0}}
we get

\displaystyle I_{M}\ll(\log M)^{4}M^{\varepsilon_{0}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{M^{2-\varepsilon_{0}}}\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{2}\,dx.

Set
\kappa_{1}=1/2
and
\theta_{l}=\frac{\theta_{l-1}}{\theta_{l-1}+1}=\frac{1}{l+1}.
We will choose
l
such that
\frac{1}{l+1}<\varepsilon_{0}
. Applying Lemma
4.7

l
times we find that

I_{M}\ll M^{4+\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We must now show that

\mathscr{N}(M,\delta)\ll M^{2+\varepsilon}+\delta M^{4+\varepsilon}.

Suppose first that
\delta=M^{-2}
. Then by Lemma
4.1
we get

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}(M,M^{-2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\pi^{2}M^{-2}\int_{0}^{M^{2}/2}\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\,dx.

Therefore we are through in this case by appealing to Lemma
4.8
.

If
\delta\leqslant M^{-2}
, then
\mathscr{N}(M,\delta)\leqslant\mathscr{N}(M,M^{-2})\ll M^{2+\varepsilon}
. Finally, if
\delta>M^{-2}
we apply Lemmas
4.1
and
4.8
once again to get

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}(M,\delta)

\displaystyle\ll\delta\int_{0}^{\delta^{-1}}\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\,dx.

Thus we get

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}(M,\delta)

\displaystyle\ll\delta\int_{0}^{M^{2}}\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{1}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left(\bigg|\sum_{M<m\leqslant 2M}e\left(\frac{x\varphi_{2}(m)}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\bigg|^{*}\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\ll\delta M^{4+\varepsilon}.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
