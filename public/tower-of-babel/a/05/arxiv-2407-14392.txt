Weyl-Lewis-Papapetrou coordinates,
self-dual Yang-Mills equations

Abstract




Abstract

We consider the dimensional reduction to two dimensions of certain gravitational theories in
D\geq 4
dimensions at the two-derivative level. It is known that the resulting field equations
describe an integrable system in two dimensions which can also be obtained by a dimensional reduction of the self-dual Yang-Mills equations in four dimensions. We use this relation to construct a single copy prescription for classes of gravitational solutions in Weyl-Lewis-Papapetrou coordinates.
In contrast with previous proposals, we find that the gauge group of the Yang-Mills single copy carries non-trivial information about the gravitational solution.
We illustrate our single copy prescription with various examples that include the extremal Reissner-Nordstrom solution, the Kaluza-Klein rotating attractor solution, the Einstein-Rosen wave solution and the self-dual Kleinian Taub-NUT solution.

The dimensionally reduced gravitational field equations

We consider gravitational theories in
D\geq 4
dimensions, at the two-derivative level, and without a cosmological constant term.
We focus on solutions to the associated field equations that only depend on two of the
D
space-time coordinates. We assume that
these gravitational theories, when dimensionally reduced to
two dimensions using the two-step procedure
discussed in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
,
take the following form as discussed below. Namely,
in the first step, the theory is reduced to three dimensions, and subsequently, the 2-form fields are dualised into scalar fields. The resulting theory in three dimensions describes the coupling of a non-linear sigma model, whose target space is a symmetric space
G/H
,
to three-dimensional gravity. Then, a further reduction to two dimensions yields the following
non-linear field equations,

\displaystyle d\left(\rho\star A\right)=0\;\;\;,\;\;\;\text{with}\;\;A=M^{-1}dM\;,

(4)

is a coset representative of the symmetric space
G/H

is invariant under an involution
\natural
called generalized transposition, i.e.
M^{\natural}=M
, and
M

depends on two coordinates, denoted here by
\rho>0
and

, also called Weyl coordinates.
In (
4

denotes the Hodge star operator in two dimensions,

\displaystyle\star d\rho=-\lambda\,dv\;\;\;,\;\;\;\star dv=d\rho\;\;\;,\;\;\;(\star)^{2}=-\lambda\,{\rm id}\;,

(5)

where
\lambda=\pm 1
depends on the spacetime signature, as detailed below.

The matrix 1-form

takes values in the Lie algebra of
G/H

\mathfrak{t}^{a}

\displaystyle A=M^{-1}dM=A_{a}\mathfrak{t}^{a}\;\;\;,\;\;\;a=1,\dots,n\;,

where the dependence on

is encoded in the 1-forms

Let us discuss the case

\displaystyle ds_{4}^{2}=-\lambda\,\Delta(dy+Bd\phi)^{2}+\Delta^{-1}\left(e^{\psi}\,ds_{2}^{2}+\rho^{2}d\phi^{2}\right)\;.

(7)

\displaystyle P(\rho,v)=p(\rho,v)\,C\;,

(37)

provided that
p
satisfies (
33
).
This is so, because the equations

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\partial_{v}p\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{v}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}\right)p

(38)

are compatible with one another provided
p
satisfies (
33
).
Similarly, given a solution of class II of the form (
23
), with
P
given by
(
37
) and with
p
satisfying (
33
), it can be written in the form (
36
) by
using (
38
).

A straightforward generalization of the above shows that solutions of class I of the form
A=(df_{i})C_{i}
, where the
C_{i}
are commuting matrices, also admit a description as class II solutions.

2.2
Constructing further solutions

Given a solution
f
in class I or a solution
P
in class II, we can construct further solutions
of (
21
) and of (
33
), respectively, by integration with respect to
v
, as follows.

Let
f
denote a function that satisfies (
21
). Then we construct a function
G
that also solves (
21
), namely

\displaystyle G(\rho,v)=\int^{v}_{a}f(\rho,{\tilde{v}})\,d{\tilde{v}}+g(\rho)\;,

(39)

where
g(\rho)
has to satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda\,\rho\,g^{\prime}(\rho)=-\int^{\rho}_{b}\,{\tilde{\rho}}\,\left(\partial_{v}f(v,{\tilde{\rho}})\right)_{|_{v=a}}\,d{\tilde{\rho}}\;.

(40)

Here
a
and
b
denote constants.

G-g
is an indefinite integral in
v
, but also a parametric integral with parameter
\rho
. We assume that
f
possesses the necessary continuity properties for applying the Leibniz integral rule for differentiation under the integral sign.
Then,

\displaystyle A=(dG)\,C\,,

(41)

with
C
a constant matrix,
is a solution of (
11
) and (
12
),
as can be easily verified by using (
21
) and (
40
).

Similarly, given a solution
\Psi
of the form (
34
)
that satisfies (
35
),
we construct a function
H
that also solves (
35
),
namely

\displaystyle H(\rho,v,\phi)=\frac{1}{2}e^{i\phi}\left(\int^{v}_{a}P(\rho,{\tilde{v}})\,d{\tilde{v}}+h(\rho)\right)\;,

(42)

where
h(\rho)
has to satisfy

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}+\frac{h^{\prime}}{\rho}-\frac{h}{\rho^{2}}=-\lambda\left(\partial_{v}P\right)_{|v=a}\;.

(43)

e^{-i\phi}\,H-h
is an indefinite integral in
v
, but also a parametric integral with parameter
\rho
. We assume that
P
possesses the necessary continuity properties for applying the Leibniz integral rule for differentiation under the integral sign.
It can be easily verified that
H
satisfies (
35
)
by using (
33
) and (
43
). Then,

\displaystyle A_{\rho}=-\lambda\,e^{-i\phi}\,\partial_{v}H=-\lambda\,e^{-i\phi}\,\Psi\;\;\;,\;\;\;A_{v}=e^{-i\phi}\left(\partial_{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}\right)H

(44)

is a solution of (
11
) and (
12
).

Let us now consider a solution (
36
) that can also be written in the form
(
38
).
We then introduce the functions
G
and
H
as above.
The associated solution (
41
) can then also be expressed in the form (
44
), since the relations

\displaystyle e^{i\phi}\,\partial_{\rho}G\,C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda\partial_{v}H=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,P\,,

\displaystyle e^{i\phi}\,\partial_{v}G\,C=e^{i\phi}\,f\,C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}\right)H

(45)

are compatible by virtue of
H
satisfying (
33
).

An example thereof is provided by
\lambda=1
with

\displaystyle f(\rho,v)=-2\frac{m}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\;\;\;,\;\;\;P(\rho,v)=-4m\left(\frac{v}{\rho\,\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\right)\,C\;.

(46)

Using (
39
) and (
42
), we obtain

\displaystyle G(\rho,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\,\log\left[\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}-v}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+v}\right]=-2m\ \tanh^{-1}\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\right)\;,

\displaystyle H(\rho,v,\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2m\,e^{i\phi}\,\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}{\rho}\,C\;,

(47)

which satisfy the differential equations (
21
) and (
35
), respectively.

3
Examples

We now give examples of gravitational solutions in four dimensions
that have both a class I and a class II description.

3.1
Class I description

Let us consider solutions of the form (
20
) with one or two matrices
C_{i}
,

\displaystyle A=df_{i}\,C_{i}\;,

(48)

where the
C_{i}
are constant commuting matrices
and the
f_{i}=f_{i}(\rho,v)
satisfy (
21
).
Using (
39
),
to each
f_{i}
we
associate a
function
G_{i}
that satisfies the differential equation (
21
).

In all the examples discussed in this section, the solutions
depend on various parameters which, when they are switched off, ensure
the vanishing of
A
.

There are two cases to consider, either
\lambda=1
or
\lambda=-1
(c.f. (
7
)).
When
\lambda=1
, the examples that we consider are the Schwarzschild solution,
the extremal Reissner-Nordstrom black hole in four space-time dimensions,
a black hole in
AdS_{2}\times S^{2}
,
the (Lorentzian version of the) self-dual Taub-NUT solution, the non-extremal Kerr black hole, the rotating attractor solution for a Kaluza-Klein black hole. When
\lambda=-1
, one example that we consider is the Einstein-Rosen wave solution. In addition, we also discuss the self-dual Kleinian Taub-NUT solution, which is a four-dimensional gravitational solution with signature
(2,2)

[
18
]
.

3.1.1
Exterior region of the Schwarzschild black hole

The line element describing the exterior region of the Schwarzschild black hole solution can be
written in the form

\displaystyle ds_{4}^{2}=-e^{-\varphi}dt^{2}+e^{\varphi}ds_{3}^{2}

(49)

with

\displaystyle ds_{3}^{2}=dr^{2}+(r^{2}-m^{2})\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\right)

(50)

and

\displaystyle e^{-\varphi(r)}=\frac{r-m}{r+m}\;\;\;,\;\;\;r>m\;.

(51)

This solution carries one parameter, the mass parameter
m
.

Introducing Weyl coordinates

\displaystyle\rho=\sqrt{r^{2}-m^{2}}\,\sin\theta\;\;\;,\;\;\;v=r\cos\theta\;,

(52)

the line element (
49
) can be brought to the form
(
7
)
with (see, for instance,
[
23
]
)

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v+m-\sqrt{(v+m)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}{v-m-\sqrt{(v-m)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\;\;\;,\;\;\;B=0\;,

\displaystyle\psi(\rho,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{v^{2}+\rho^{2}-m^{2}}{\sqrt{(v+m)^{2}+\rho^{2}}\sqrt{(v-m)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}+\frac{1}{2}\right]\;.

(53)

The associated matrix
M(\rho,v)

(with
M\in G/H=SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2)
)
is given by
(
9
),
with
{\tilde{B}}=0
,

\displaystyle M(\rho,v)=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta(\rho,v)&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\Delta(\rho,v)}\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;,\;\;\;\Delta(\rho,v)=\frac{v+m-\sqrt{(v+m)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}{v-m-\sqrt{(v-m)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\;,

(54)

which results in

\displaystyle A=M^{-1}dM=df\,C

(55)

with

\displaystyle f(\rho,v)=\log\Delta(\rho,v)\;\;\;,\;\;C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\;.

(56)

To first order in the parameter
m
,
f
reads

\displaystyle f(\rho,v)=-2\frac{m}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\,,

(57)

which describes a monopole type potential. Since (
57
)
is a solution of (
21
)
with
\lambda=1
,
it will give rise to a solution (
7
).
To it we associate the function

\displaystyle G(\rho,v)=m\left[\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}-v)-\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+v)\right]\;.

(58)

3.1.2
Extremal Reissner-Nordstrom black hole

The static extremal Reissner-Nordstrom black hole solution is a charged black hole solution of the Einstein+Maxwell theory in four space-time dimensions. It carries one electric charge
q
and one magnetic charge
p
.
We introduce the combination
Q=q+ip
. In the near-horizon limit, the line element describes the
product geometry
AdS_{2}\times S^{2}
, and the solution is supported by
electro-/magnetostatic potentials
\chi_{e}
and
\chi_{m}
,

\displaystyle ds_{4}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{r^{2}}{|Q|^{2}}dt^{2}+|Q|^{2}\frac{dr^{2}}{r^{2}}+|Q|^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\right)\>,

\displaystyle\chi_{e}+i\chi_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Q}{|Q|^{2}}\,r\>.

(59)

Introducing Weyl coordinates

\displaystyle\rho=r\,\sin\theta\;\;\;,\;\;\;v=r\cos\theta\;,

(60)

the line element (
59
) can be brought to the form
(
7
) with
e^{\psi}=1
,
B=0
and
\Delta=(\rho^{2}+v^{2})/|Q|^{2}
.
This near-horizon solution is encoded in a
3\times 3
matrix

M\in G/H=SU(2,1)/(SL(2,\mathbb{R})\times U(1))
given by
[
19
]

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\varphi}&\sqrt{2}e^{\varphi}Z&1\\
-\sqrt{2}e^{\varphi}{\bar{Z}}&-1&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;,\;\;\;e^{-\varphi}=\Delta\;\;\;,\;\;\;Z=\chi_{e}+i\chi_{m}=c\,e^{-\varphi/2}\;,

(61)

where

\displaystyle c=\frac{Q}{|Q|}\;\;\;,\;\;\;|c|=1\;.

(62)

We note that the matrix
M
can also be written as

\displaystyle M=\frac{1}{|Z|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt{2}Z&|Z|^{2}\\
-\sqrt{2}{\bar{Z}}&-|Z|^{2}&0\\
|Z|^{2}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(63)

The associated matrix 1-form
A=M^{-1}dM
reads
A=df\,C
with

\displaystyle f(\rho,v)=e^{\varphi(\rho,v)/2}=\frac{|Q|}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\;\;\;,\;\;C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
\sqrt{2}{\bar{c}}&0&0\\
0&\sqrt{2}c&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(64)

Note that
f
is linear in the parameter
Q
and that it describes a monopole type potential. The associated function
G
is

\displaystyle G(\rho,v)=-\frac{|Q|}{2}\left[\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}-v)-\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+v)\right]\;.

(65)

The constant matrix
C
is a linear combination of two Lie algebra generators (given in (A.6) and (A.12) in
[
19
]
),

\displaystyle{C}=\sqrt{2}\left({\rm Re}\,c\right)\,(F_{1}+F_{2})-\sqrt{2}\left({\rm Im}\,c\right)\,i(F_{2}-F_{1})\;.

(66)

When the black hole is electrically charged (
p=0
) we have
c=1
, while when it is magnetically charged (
q=0
) we have
c=i
.

The interpolating solution describing an extremal Reissner-Nordstrom black hole can be obtained from (
63
)
by applying a solution generating transformation called
Harrison transformation to
M
, as follows
[
19
]
. We act on
M
with the transformation
g({\hat{c}})
,

\displaystyle g({\hat{c}})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
-\sqrt{2}{\bar{\hat{c}}}&1&0\\
|{\hat{c}}|^{2}&-\sqrt{2}{\hat{c}}&1\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;,\;\;\;{\hat{c}}=-c/2\;.

(67)

The action on
M
is given by
{\tilde{M}}=g^{\natural}({\hat{c}})Mg({\hat{c}})
. Here the involution
{\natural}
is defined by

g^{\natural}({\hat{c}})=\eta g({\hat{c}})^{\dagger}\eta^{-1}
with
\eta={\rm diag}(1,-1,1)
,

\displaystyle g^{\natural}({\hat{c}})=\begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt{2}{\hat{c}}&|{\hat{c}}|^{2}\\
0&1&\sqrt{2}{\bar{\hat{c}}}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(68)

The resulting matrix
{\tilde{M}}
takes the form (
63
), with
Z
replaced by
{\tilde{Z}}

[
19
]
,

\displaystyle Z\rightarrow{\tilde{Z}}=\frac{Z}{1-2{\bar{\hat{c}}Z}}=\frac{Z}{1+{\bar{c}}Z}\;.

(69)

Under (
67
), the matrix 1-form
A
transforms as

\displaystyle A=M^{-1}dM\longrightarrow{\tilde{A}}={\tilde{M}}^{-1}d{\tilde{M}}=g^{-1}({\hat{c}})\,A\,g({\hat{c}}),

(70)

and we obtain

\displaystyle{\tilde{A}}=A\;.

(71)

Thus, both the interpolating and the near-horizon solution have the same matrix 1-form
A
.

3.1.3
Black hole in
AdS_{2}\times S^{2}

It is well known that the solution describing the near-horizon near-extremal limit of a Reissner-Nordstrom black hole in four space-time dimensions is a black hole in
AdS_{2}\times S^{2}

[
24
]
.
Such a solution
can be obtained
by applying a Harrison transformation (see Table 2 in
[
20
]
) to the Schwarzschild solution, as discussed in
[
19
]
. Namely,
we embed the
2\times 2
matrix

M
given in (
54
)
into a
3\times 3
matrix

M\in G/H=SU(2,1)/(SL(2,\mathbb{R})\times U(1))
,

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\varphi}&0\\
0&e^{\varphi}\end{pmatrix}\Longrightarrow M=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\varphi}&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&e^{-\varphi}\end{pmatrix},

(72)

where
e^{-\varphi}=\Delta
.
The associated matrix one-form
A=M^{-1}dM
reads

\displaystyle A=df\,C\;\;\;,\;\;\;f=-\varphi\;\;\;,\;\;\;C=-\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}\;.

(73)

Now we act on
M
with a transformation
g(c)
, as described below
(
67
), i.e.
{\tilde{M}}=g^{\natural}({c})Mg({c})
with

g^{\natural}({c})=\eta g({c})^{\dagger}\eta^{-1}
with
\eta={\rm diag}(1,-1,1)
and

\displaystyle g(c)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
-\sqrt{2}{\bar{c}}&1&0\\
|c|^{2}&-\sqrt{2}c&1\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;,\;\;\;c\in\mathbb{C}\;.

(74)

We obtain

\displaystyle{\tilde{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(e^{\varphi/2}-|c|^{2}\,e^{-\varphi/2}\right)^{2}&\sqrt{2}\,c\left(1-|c|^{2}\,e^{-\varphi}\right)&|c|^{2}\,e^{-\varphi}\\
-\sqrt{2}\,{\bar{c}}\left(1-|c|^{2}\,e^{-\varphi}\right)&1-2|c|^{2}\,e^{-\varphi}&\sqrt{2}{\bar{c}}\,e^{-\varphi}\\
|c|^{2}\,e^{-\varphi}&-\sqrt{2}c\,e^{-\varphi}&e^{-\varphi}\end{pmatrix}={\tilde{M}}^{\natural}.

(75)

Under (
74
), the 1-form
A
in (
73
) transforms into

\displaystyle A=M^{-1}dM\longrightarrow{\tilde{A}}={\tilde{M}}^{-1}d{\tilde{M}}=g^{-1}(c)\,A\,g(c).

(76)

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle{\tilde{A}}=d{\tilde{f}}\,{\tilde{C}}\;\;\;,\;\;\;{\tilde{f}}=f\;,

(77)

with
{\tilde{C}}=g^{-1}(c)\,C\,g(c)
,
where

\displaystyle{\tilde{C}}=-\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
\sqrt{2}{\bar{c}}&0&0\\
0&\sqrt{2}c&-1\end{pmatrix}\;.

(78)

Note that the dependence on the parameter
c
is entirely contained in the matrix
{\tilde{C}}
, and that this dependence is linear in
c
. The matrix
\tilde{C}
is a linear combination of Lie algebra generators,

\displaystyle{\tilde{C}}=-H_{2}-\sqrt{2}\left({\rm Re}\,c\right)\,(F_{1}+F_{2})+\sqrt{2}\left({\rm Im}\,c\right)\,i(F_{2}-F_{1})\;,

(79)

where
H_{2},F_{1}+F_{2},i(F_{2}-F_{1})
are the generators given in (A.6) and (A.12) in
[
19
]
.

If we now set
c=e^{i\alpha}
,
we get

\displaystyle{\tilde{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(e^{\varphi/2}-e^{-\varphi/2}\right)^{2}&\sqrt{2}\,e^{i\alpha}\left(1-e^{-\varphi}\right)&e^{-\varphi}\\
-\sqrt{2}\,e^{-i\alpha}\left(1-e^{-\varphi}\right)&1-2e^{-\varphi}&\sqrt{2}e^{-i\alpha}e^{-\varphi}\\
e^{-\varphi}&-\sqrt{2}e^{i\alpha}e^{-\varphi}&e^{-\varphi}\end{pmatrix}={\tilde{M}}^{\natural}.

(80)

The resulting solution has a line element of the form (
49
), with
e^{\varphi}
replaced by

\displaystyle e^{\tilde{\varphi}}=\left(e^{\varphi/2}-e^{-\varphi/2}\right)^{2}=\frac{4m^{2}}{r^{2}-m^{2}}\;.

(81)

This line element describes a product geometry given by a black hole in
AdS_{2}\times S^{2}
(c.f. (9.19) in
[
19
]
),

\displaystyle ds_{4}^{2}=4m^{2}\left(-(r^{2}-m^{2})dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{r^{2}-m^{2}}\right)+4m^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\right)\>,

(82)

where we have rescaled the time-like coordinate
t
by a factor
4m^{2}
.
This solution is supported by electric-/magnetostatic potentials
\chi_{e}/\chi_{m}
,

\displaystyle\chi_{e}+i\chi_{m}=\frac{c}{2m}\,(r-m)\>.

(83)

To lowest order in the parameter
m
, we infer using (
77
)
that

\displaystyle{\tilde{f}}(\rho,v)=-2\frac{m}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\;.

(84)

The associated function
G
is

\displaystyle G(\rho,v)=m\left[\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}-v)-\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+v)\right]\;.

(85)

3.1.4
Self-dual Taub-NUT solution

In Lorentzian signature, the Taub-NUT solution is given
by
[
25
,
26
]
(see also (12.1) in
[
27
]
)

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-f(r)\left(d{\bar{t}}-2\ell\cos\theta d\phi\right)^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+(r^{2}+\ell^{2})\,\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\right)

(86)

with

\displaystyle f(r)=\frac{r^{2}-2mr-\ell^{2}}{r^{2}+\ell^{2}}.

(87)

Here,
m
and
\ell
are parameters, and
\ell
is called NUT parameter.
Setting
\ell=+im
, we obtain the self-dual Taub-NUT solution,
for which

\displaystyle f(r)=\frac{r-m}{r+m}\;.

(88)

We restrict to
r>m
to ensure that
f(r)>0
.
Introducing Weyl coordinates (see (12.6) in
[
27
]
)

\displaystyle\rho=(r-m)\sin\theta\;\;\;,\;\;\;v=(r-m)\,\cos\theta,

(89)

the line element for the self-dual Taub-NUT solution becomes

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\Delta(d{\bar{t}}+B\,d\phi)^{2}+\Delta^{-1}\left[e^{\psi}\left(d\rho^{2}+dv^{2}\right)+\rho^{2}\,d\phi^{2}\right],

(90)

where

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+2m}\;\;\;,\;\;\;e^{\psi}=1\;\;\;,\;\;\;B=-2im\,\frac{v}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\;.

(91)

Using (
10
) we infer

\displaystyle\partial_{v}{\tilde{B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\rho}\,\partial_{\rho}B=2im\,\frac{v}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+2m\right)^{2}},

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}{\tilde{B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\rho}\,\partial_{v}B=2im\,\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+2m\right)^{2}},

(92)

which can be integrated and gives

\displaystyle{\tilde{B}}=i\left(\alpha-\frac{2m}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+2m}\right)\;\;\;,\;\;\;\alpha\in\mathbb{C}.

(93)

The matrix
M(\rho,v)
belonging to a complexification of
M\in G/H=SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2)

is obtained using (
9
),

\displaystyle M(\rho,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-(\alpha^{2}-1)-\frac{2m(\alpha-1)^{2}}{\Phi}&i\alpha+\frac{2i(\alpha-1)m}{\Phi}\\
i\alpha+\frac{2i(\alpha-1)m}{\Phi}&\frac{\Phi+2m}{\Phi}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-(\alpha^{2}-1)&i\alpha\\
i\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{2m}{\Phi}\,\hat{C}\;,

\displaystyle\hat{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-(\alpha-1)^{2}&i(\alpha-1)\\
i(\alpha-1)&1\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;,\;\;\;\Phi=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}\;.

(94)

Note that
\det\hat{C}=0
. We obtain for
M^{-1}
,

\displaystyle M^{-1}(\rho,v)=\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\alpha\\
-i\alpha&-(\alpha^{2}-1)\end{pmatrix}+\frac{2m}{\Phi}\,{\tilde{C}}\;,\;{\tilde{C}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&-i(\alpha-1)\\
-i(\alpha-1)&-(\alpha-1)^{2}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(95)

with
\hat{C}{\tilde{C}}=0
. This yields an exact class I description,

\displaystyle A=M^{-1}dM=df\,C\;,\;\ f(\rho,v)=-\frac{2m}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\;,\;C=\begin{pmatrix}1-\alpha&i\\
i(1-\alpha)^{2}&\alpha-1\end{pmatrix}\;,\;C^{2}=0\;.

(96)

The expression for
f
describes a monopole type potential.
The associated function
G
is

\displaystyle G(\rho,v)=m\left[\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}-v)-\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+v)\right]\;.

(97)

3.1.5
The non-extremal Kerr black hole

The matrix
M(\rho,v)

(with
M\in G/H=SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2)
) that describes the
exterior region of the non-extremal Kerr black hole with mass
m
and angular momentum

is obtained using (
9
)
and is given by
[

,
19

\displaystyle M(\rho,v)=\frac{1}{c^{2}(u^{2}-1)+a^{2}(y^{2}-1)}\begin{pmatrix}(c\,u-m)^{2}+a^{2}y^{2}&2amy\\
2amy&(c\,u+m)^{2}+a^{2}y^{2}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(98)

where
c=\sqrt{m^{2}-a^{2}}
,
u>1,\,|y|<1
, with the prolate spheroidal coordinates
(u,y)
expressed
in terms of the Weyl coordinates
(\rho,v)
by (c.f. (G.6) in
[
29
]
)

\displaystyle u=\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(v+c)^{2}}+\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(v-c)^{2}}}{2c}\;\;\;,\;\;\;y=\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(v+c)^{2}}-\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(v-c)^{2}}}{2c}\;.

(99)

Computing
A=M^{-1}dM
and expanding the result up to fifth order in the angular momentum parameter
a
and to first
order in the mass parameter
m
gives

\displaystyle A=df_{1}\,\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}+df_{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

(100)

with

\displaystyle f_{1}(\rho,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\left[-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}-a^{2}\,\frac{\rho^{2}-2v^{2}}{(\rho^{2}+v^{2})^{5/2}}-a^{4}\,\frac{3\rho^{4}-24\rho^{2}v^{2}+8v^{4}}{4\,(\rho^{2}+v^{2})^{9/2}}\right]\;,

(101)

\displaystyle f_{2}(\rho,v)

\displaystyle=

3.1.6
Kaluza-Klein black hole: rotating attractor

Rotating Kaluza-Klein black hole solutions are solutions of the four-dimensional theory that is obtained by dimensional
reduction of
five-dimensional pure gravity on a circle. The resulting fields in four space-time dimensions are the metric, the
scalar field
\Phi
and the Kaluza-Klein Maxwell field
{\cal A}_{\mu}
. We consider extremal rotating black hole solutions in this theory. The near-horizon behaviour of these solutions has been thoroughly studied in
[
30
]
and it is determined by the attractor mechanism in the presence of rotation (hence the name rotating attractors).
Let us focus on the near-horizon behaviour of slowly rotating extremal solutions (this is also referred to as the ergo-free branch). The near-horizon behaviour of the four-dimensional metric, the dilaton field
e^{-4\Phi/\sqrt{3}}
and
the Maxwell field
{\cal F}=d{\cal A}
is given in eqs. (5.36)-(5.44) of
[
30
]
, and is determined in terms
of the electric-magnetic charges
(Q,P)
(which, for definiteness, were taken to be positive in

[
30
]
)
and in terms of the angular momentum

. In particular,
in the near-horizon region and in the presence of rotation,
the dilaton field is not any longer constant,

\displaystyle e^{-4\Phi/\sqrt{3}}=\left(\frac{P}{Q}\right)^{2/3}\,\frac{PQ-J\cos\theta}{PQ+J\cos\theta}\;.

(106)

The four-dimensional near-horizon metric can be written in the form (
7

\lambda=1

\displaystyle ds_{4}^{2}=-\Delta(dt+Bd\phi)^{2}+\Delta^{-1}\left(e^{\psi}\,ds_{2}^{2}+\rho^{2}d\phi^{2}\right)\;,

with
[
19
]

\displaystyle\rho=r\sin\theta\;\;,\;\;v=r\cos\theta\;\;\;,\;\;\Delta=\frac{8\pi(\rho^{2}+v^{2})}{\sqrt{P^{2}Q^{2}-J^{2}\frac{v^{2}}{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}}\;\;,\;\;B=-\frac{J}{8\pi}\,\frac{\rho^{2}}{(\rho^{2}+v^{2})^{3/2}}\;\;,\;\;e^{\psi}=1.

(108)

The matrix
M\in G/H
that describes the near-horizon solution takes values in the coset

G/H=SL(3,\mathbb{R})/SO(2,1)
and is given by
[

\displaystyle M(\rho,v)=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{{\cal B}^{2}}{2{\cal D}}+\frac{2{\cal AD}+{\cal B}^{2}}{2{\cal D}}\frac{v}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\;\;&{\cal B}\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}\;\;&{\cal C}(\rho^{2}+v^{2})\\
-{\cal B}\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}\;\;&{\cal D}(\rho^{2}+v^{2})\;\;&0\\
{\cal C}(\rho^{2}+v^{2})&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(109)

where

\displaystyle{\cal B}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\,P^{1/3}\,Q^{2/3}\,,\,{\cal C}=-\left(\frac{P}{Q}\right)^{1/3}\,,\,{\cal D}=-\left(\frac{Q}{P}\right)^{2/3}\,,\,2{\cal AD}+{\cal B}^{2}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\left(\frac{Q}{P}\right)^{1/3}J\,,

with
{\cal B}/{\cal D}\propto P,\,{\cal B}/{\cal C}\propto Q

(2{\cal AD}+{\cal B}^{2})/({\cal CD})\propto J
.
These constants satisfy
-{\cal C}^{2}{\cal D}=1

The associated matrix

reads

\displaystyle A=df_{1}\,C_{1}+df_{2}\,C_{2}\;,

(111)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
denote commuting matrices given by

\displaystyle C_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
-\frac{{\cal B}}{{\cal D}}&0&0\\
0&\frac{{\cal B}}{{\cal C}}&0\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;,\;\;\;C_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
-\frac{2{\cal AD}+{\cal B}^{2}}{2{\cal CD}}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(112)

and where the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle f_{1}(\rho,v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}\;\;\;,\;\;\;f_{2}(\rho,v)=-\frac{v}{(\rho^{2}+v^{2})^{3/2}}\;.

(113)

The associated functions
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
take the form

\displaystyle G_{1}(\rho,v)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}+v)-\log(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}-v)\right]\;\;\;,\;\;\;G_{2}(\rho,v)=\frac{1}{{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+v^{2}}}}\;.

(114)

Note that (

Another example: the Eguchi-Hanson metric

In this section we discuss the Eguchi-Hanson metric in Weyl coordinates. Subsequently we determine the associated matrix 1-form

The Eguchi-Hanson metric depends on one parameter,

When switching off

We have verified that there does not exist a coordinate change for the Eguchi-Hanson metric which takes us to Weyl coordinates in such a way that (i) the background metric is mapped to the vacuum version of the Weyl metric with

We expand

5
Mapping gravitational solutions to solutions of the self-dual Yang-Mills equations in four dimensions

As already mentioned, the PDEs (
4

that arise by dimensionally reducing gravitational theories in space-time dimensions

also arise by performing a
suitable reduction of the self-duality equations for Yang-Mills fields in four dimensions down to two dimensions
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Using this link,
we may therefore associate solutions of the
self-dual
Yang-Mills equations in four-dimensional flat space-time to the gravitational solutions belonging to either class I or to class II.
This is what we will do next.

We note that in these constructions
there are two cases to consider, either
\lambda=1
or
\lambda=-1
. When
\lambda=1
, the flat space-time signature is
(1,3)
, whereas when
\lambda=-1

the signature is

.

5.1
\lambda=1
: signature
(1,3)

We consider the self-dual sector of Yang-Mills (YM) theory in four-dimensional Minkowski space-time,
whose description we briefly review following
[
34
]
,
and perform a suitable dimensional reduction of it to two dimensions.

We introduce coordinates

\displaystyle U=\tau-v\;\;\;,\;\;\;V=\tau+v\;\;\;,\;\;\;X=-w\;\;\;,\;\;\;Y=-{\bar{w}},

(200)

where

\displaystyle w=x+iy\;\;\;,\;\;\;{\bar{w}}=x-iy\;,

(201)

so that

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-d\tau^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dv^{2}=-dUdV+dwd{\bar{w}}.

(202)

We set

\displaystyle w=\rho\,e^{i\phi}\;.

(203)

We then have

\displaystyle\partial_{U}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{v}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\tau}\;\;\;,\;\;\;\partial_{V}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{v}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\tau}

(204)

as well as

\displaystyle\partial_{w}=\frac{1}{2}e^{-i\phi}\left(\partial_{\rho}-\frac{i}{\rho}\,\partial_{\phi}\right)\;\;\;,\;\;\;\partial_{\bar{w}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{i\phi}\left(\partial_{\rho}+\frac{i}{\rho}\,\partial_{\phi}\right).

(205)

We introduce the Yang-Mills connection

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-ig\mathcal{A}_{\mu}\;\;\;,\;\;\;\mathcal{A}_{\mu}=\mathcal{A}_{\mu a}\,T_{a}\;\;\;,\;\;\;T_{a}\in\mathfrak{g}\;.

6

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{\mu},\mathcal{D}_{\nu}]=-ig\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}

(207)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\mu}-ig[\mathcal{A}_{\mu},\mathcal{A}_{\nu}].

(208)

Self-dual solutions of the Yang-Mills equations of motion satisfy (see Appendix

)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}i\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,\mathcal{F}^{\rho\sigma}

with

In the coordinate system
(U,V,w,{\bar{w}})
, the self-duality condition (

[
34
]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{Uw}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(210)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{V{\bar{w}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(211)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{UV}-\mathcal{F}_{w{\bar{w}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(212)

These 3 equations are the compatibility conditions for the
following linear system (see eqs. (4) and (5) in
[

]

\displaystyle LY=\left(\mathcal{D}_{U}-\Omega\mathcal{D}_{\bar{w}}\right)Y=0\;\;\;,\;\;\;MY=\left(\mathcal{D}_{w}-\Omega\mathcal{D}_{V}\right)Y=0.

called the spectral parameter. We take

with respect to

\displaystyle[{L},{M}]Y=0\;\;\;\forall\;\;\;Y\in C^{2}\;\;\;,\;\;\;\Omega\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(214)

gives

\displaystyle 0=[L,M]Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big([\mathcal{D}_{U},\mathcal{D}_{w}]-\Omega\left([\mathcal{D}_{U},\mathcal{D}_{V}]-[\mathcal{D}_{w},\mathcal{D}_{\bar{w}}]\right)+\Omega^{2}\,[\mathcal{D}_{\bar{w}},\mathcal{D}_{V}]\Big)Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(-i\mathcal{F}_{Uw}+i\Omega\left(\mathcal{F}_{UV}-\mathcal{F}_{w\bar{w}}\right)+i\Omega^{2}\,\mathcal{F}_{V\bar{w}}\Big)Y.

Then, multiplying by

\displaystyle-i\mathcal{F}_{Uw}+i\Omega\left(\mathcal{F}_{UV}-\mathcal{F}_{w\bar{w}}\right)+i\Omega^{2}\,\mathcal{F}_{V\bar{w}}=0,

Note that differently from the Breitenlohner-Maison linear system

6
Single copy prescription

Let us consider gravitational solutions in four dimensions

We remark that one could set up the single copy prescription using only a class I description, but additionally putting the solution in class II allows us to directly recover the scalar version of the self-dual Yang-Mills solution, c.f.
(

Let us assume that these solutions depend on certain parameters, and that when switching off these parameters the solution

6.1.1
The nontrivial role of the gauge group

Let us compare our single copy prescription with what is done in the context of the double copy.
In the double copy framework, a gravitational solution is constructed as a “product” of two gauge theory solutions. Depending on the complexity of the gravity solution, the two gauge theory factors might be identical (e.g. for Schwarzschild, in which case we have a unique single copy) or different (this is often the case for gravity solutions supported by additional fields). For example the black hole in Section
3.1.3
is supported by a Maxwell field. Thus it will arise from an asymmetric double copy, where one of the factors is a pure Yang-Mills theory, and the other is Yang-Mills coupled with a scalar

5

5

Generally in the double copy procedure, the gauge algebra

is unspecified, and independent of the specific kinematics or profile of the solutions considered.

Our single copy proposal is drastically different,

7
Conclusions

In this paper we discussed the mapping of the integrable sector of certain gravitational theories in
D\geq 4
to the integrable sector of Yang-Mills theories in four dimensions. This mapping is formulated in terms of Weyl coordinates, since these are the natural coordinates used in the Lax pair description of the integrable sector of these gravitational theories. The gauge group on the Yang-Mills side is specified by the dimensional reduction
performed on the gravitational side. Let us summarize our results.

We considered two classes of solutions (class I and class II) to the two-dimensional PDEs (
1
).
These PDEs arise by dimensional reduction of the field equations of certain gravitational theories in
D\geq 4
dimensions, and therefore solutions belonging to these two classes correspond to solutions of the gravitational field equations in
D
space-time dimensions. The two-dimensional PDEs
(
1
) are the compatibility conditions for a Lax pair, the Breitenlohner-
Maison linear system. This Lax pair can be described by a pair of differential operators
(\cal L,\cal M)
in two dimensions,
which have been given in Section
2
.

The PDEs (
1
) also arise as compatibility conditions for certain Lax pairs in three and in four dimensions, see Appendix (
A
). That there is a Lax pair in four dimensions giving rise to (
1
) is not surprising, since it is
known
[
8
,
9
]
that a certain dimensional reduction of the self-duality equations for Yang-Mills
fields in four dimensions gives rise to the two-dimensional PDEs (
1
). Solutions to the PDEs (
1
) can thus be regarded as either describing gravitational solutions in
D\geq 4
or
describing solutions of the self-dual Yang-Mills equations in four dimensions.

The single copy prescription associates a Yang-Mills solution to a gravitational solution. To ensure that this mapping is physically meaningful, we demand
that the nature of the sources of both types of solutions be the same. Our single copy prescription is as follows.
We focus on gravitational solutions that have both a class I and a class II description.
These gravitational solutions are constructed from the 1-form
A
which we assume to depend on a parameter
which, when set to zero,
gives rise to a background solution
A^{(0)}
.
Subsequently we work at first order in this parameter.
By expanding
A
in powers of this parameter,
A=A^{(0)}+A^{(1)}+\dots
, we obtain the first-order perturbation
A^{(1)}
.
Then, given
A^{(1)}
,
we construct a new gravitational solution by integrating over the Weyl coordinate
v
, as depicted in Table
3
for the case
\lambda=1
and in Table
4
for the case
\lambda=-1
.
We then use this new solution as a device
for constructing the single copy
\mathcal{A}
of the original gravitational solution, that is, for ensuring that the nature of the sources supporting the gravitational and the Yang-Mills solutions is the same (monopole to monopole, etc.). We illustrated our single copy construction in various examples and confirmed that it correctly reproduces the single copy for various gravitational solutions discussed in the literature. In all but one of the examples the background
A^{(0)}
vanishes and we integrated once over
v
.
The exception is the Eguchi-Hanson solution, which we discussed in Section
6.3
:
its background
A^{(0)}
is non-vanishing, and the single copy requires integrating twice over
v
.

\displaystyle-\partial_{v}+\tau\,D_{\rho}=-\partial_{v}+\tau\,\left(\partial_{\rho}+A_{\rho}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\,\partial_{\rho}+\tau\,D_{v}=\lambda\,\partial_{\rho}+\tau\,\left(\partial_{v}+A_{v}\right)\;\;\;,\;\;\;\lambda=\pm 1,

(340)

where
\tau
is a non-constant spectral parameter, namely a function of
\rho,v
and of
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
as specified by (
16
).
The compatibility condition

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}X=0\;\wedge\;{\mathcal{M}}X=0\Longrightarrow[{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{M}}]X=0\quad\forall\quad X\in C^{2}\;\;,\;\;\omega\in\mathbb{C}

(341)

yields (
11
) and (
12
), as follows. Using

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M}]=-\left([\partial_{v},\tau\,D_{v}]+\lambda\,[\partial_{\rho},\tau D_{\rho}]\right)+[\tau\,D_{\rho},\tau\,D_{v}]

(342)

as well as

\displaystyle[D_{\rho},D_{v}]=F_{\rho v}\;\;\;,\;\;\;{[\partial_{v},D_{v}]}=\partial_{v}A_{v}\;\;\;,\;\;\;[\partial_{\rho},D_{\rho}]=\partial_{\rho}A_{\rho},

(343)

we obtain

\displaystyle 0=[{\cal L},{\cal M}]X=-\frac{\lambda}{\tau}\left(-\partial_{v}\tau+\tau\partial_{\rho}\tau\right)\partial_{\rho}-\left(\lambda\partial_{\rho}\tau+\tau\partial_{v}\tau\right)D_{\rho}-\tau\left(\partial_{v}A_{v}+\lambda\partial_{\rho}A_{\rho}\right)+\tau^{2}\,F_{\rho v},

(344)

where we used
{\cal M}X=0
once. Next, using the relations
(
333
) in the form

\displaystyle-\partial_{v}\tau+\tau\partial_{\rho}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tau^{2}}{\rho}\;,

\displaystyle\lambda\partial_{\rho}\tau+\tau\partial_{v}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\frac{\tau}{\rho}\;,

(345)

and inserting them into (
344
) gives

\displaystyle 0=[{\cal L},{\cal M}]X=\left[-\tau\left(\partial_{v}A_{v}+\lambda\partial_{\rho}A_{\rho}+\frac{\lambda}{\rho}\,A_{\rho}\right)+\tau^{2}\,F_{\rho v}\right]X.

(346)

Assuming that the matrix function
X
is invertible
and multiplying by
X^{-1}
on the right gives, using
\tau\neq 0
,

\displaystyle-\left(\partial_{v}A_{v}+\lambda\,\partial_{\rho}A_{\rho}+\frac{\lambda}{\rho}A_{\rho}\right)+\tau\,F_{\rho v}=0.

(347)

By an argument analogous to the one used below (
338
), we conclude that (
11
) and (
12
) both hold.

\displaystyle x_{0}=\tau\;,\;x_{1}=v\;,\;x_{2}=\rho\,\cos\phi\;,\;x_{3}=\rho\,\sin\phi\;,

Appendix D
From
2D
to
4D

In Section
5
we constructed the mapping of gravitational solutions to solutions of the self-dual Yang-Mills equations in four dimensions by starting from the latter equations and performing dimensional reductions of them to two dimensions. Here we present a different approach to this mapping,
by starting on the gravitational side in two dimensions and expressing the 1-form
A
in two dimensions as a 1-form in four space-time dimensions, as follows.

Let us consider a solution
M
to the gravitational field equations (
4
). The associated
matrix 1-form
A=A_{\rho}d\rho+A_{v}dv
satisfies the field equation (
11
) as well as

F_{\rho v}=0
, c.f. (
12
).
Next, we view
A
as a 1-form in a four-dimensional flat space-time with
lightcone coordinates
(U,V,X,Y)
,

\displaystyle A=A_{U}dU+A_{V}dV+A_{X}dX+A_{Y}dY\,.

(386)

The map between the lightcone coordinates

(U,V,X,Y)
and the Weyl coordinates
(\rho,v)
depends on the space-time signature. For example, for the case of signature
(1,3)
, it is given by
(
200
) and (
203
).
The four components
(A_{U},A_{V},A_{X},A_{Y})
are expressed in terms of
(A_{\rho},A_{v})
. Since the latter have to satisfy
F_{\rho v}=0
, this will impose conditions on the field strength components constructed from
(
386
), which will be discussed below.
Let us now assume that these four components are given in terms of
first-order derivatives of two matrix functions

\Psi(U,V,X,Y)
and
{\tilde{\Psi}}(U,V,X,Y)
,

\displaystyle A_{U}=\partial_{Y}{\tilde{\Psi}}\;\;\;,\;\;\;A_{V}=-\partial_{X}\Psi\;\;\;,\;\;\;A_{X}=\partial_{V}{\tilde{\Psi}}\;\;\;,\;\;\;A_{Y}=-\partial_{U}\Psi\;,

(387)

and that
\Psi(U,V,X,Y)
and
{\tilde{\Psi}}(U,V,X,Y)
satisfy

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{U}\partial_{V}-\partial_{X}\partial_{Y}\right){\Psi}+[\partial_{U}\Psi,\partial_{X}\Psi]=0\;,

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{U}\partial_{V}-\partial_{X}\partial_{Y}\right){\tilde{\Psi}}+[\partial_{Y}{\tilde{\Psi}},\partial_{V}{\tilde{\Psi}}]=0\;.

(388)

Now we consider the following two sets,
(0,A_{V},0,A_{Y})
and
(A_{U},0,A_{X},0)
. We observe that each of these two sets, when viewed by itself,
satisfies the self-duality conditions

\displaystyle F_{UV}=F_{XY}\;\;\;,\;\;\;F_{UX}=0\;\;\;,\;\;\;F_{VY}=0

(389)

by virtue of (
387
) and (
388
).
For instance, if we consider the set
(0,A_{V},0,A_{Y})
, then
F_{UX}=0
is trivially satisfied, since
for this set
A_{U}=A_{X}=0
. The condition
F_{VY}=0
holds by virtue of the first equation in (
388
), while the condition
F_{UV}=F_{XY}
holds by virtue of (
387
).
Thus, there are two non-trivial conditions, which precisely equals the number of equations
(namely (
11
) and (
12
)) that
(A_{\rho},A_{v})
have to satisfy. It is thus tempting to identify the condition
F_{VY}=0
with
F_{\rho v}=0
, and to identify
the condition
F_{UV}=F_{XY}
with the field equation (
11
). This turns out to be indeed the case in all the examples considered in this paper.
Similar considerations hold for the set
(A_{U},0,A_{X},0)
.

Since the two sets

(0,A_{V},0,A_{Y})
and
(A_{U},0,A_{X},0)
are constructed from the same data (namely from
(A_{\rho},A_{v})
and the coordinate transformation relating the lightcone coordinates
(U,V,X,Y)
to the coordinates used in the four-dimensional line element (
7
)), they carry the same information, and hence the matrix functions
\Psi(U,V,X,Y)
and
{\tilde{\Psi}}(U,V,X,Y)
should be related.
This expectation has been borne out in all the examples studied in this paper. In these examples we find
that

\displaystyle{\tilde{\Psi}}=e^{-2i\phi}\,\Psi\;.

(390)

We also note that in these examples
\Psi
and
\tilde{\Psi}
are complex conjugates of one another.

As an illustrative example, let us consider the Schwarzschild solution discussed in Section
3
. Using (
134
) and (
34
), we obtain the following expressions for
\Psi
and
\tilde{\Psi}
when expressed in terms of the lightcone coordinates given in (
200
) and (
203
),

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\,\frac{V-U}{Y\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}(U-V)^{2}+XY}}\;,

\displaystyle{\tilde{\Psi}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\,\frac{V-U}{X\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}(U-V)^{2}+XY}}\;.

(391)

Using (
387
), we obtain

\displaystyle A_{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4m\,\frac{U-V}{\left((U-V)^{2}+4XY\right)^{3/2}}\;,

\displaystyle A_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8m\,\frac{Y}{\left((U-V)^{2}+4XY\right)^{3/2}}\;,

\displaystyle A_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4m\,\frac{V-U}{\left((U-V)^{2}+4XY\right)^{3/2}}\;,

\displaystyle A_{Y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8m\,\frac{X}{\left((U-V)^{2}+4XY\right)^{3/2}}\;.

(392)

It is straightforward to check that each of the two sets
(0,A_{V},0,A_{Y})
and
(A_{U},0,A_{X},0)
satisfies the self-duality conditions (
389
). Both sets are related by interchanging
(U,X)
with
(V,Y)
. We note that were we to pick
any of the sets
(A_{U},0,0,A_{Y})
and
(0,A_{V},A_{X},0)
, each of them would satisfy anti self-duality conditions, c.f. Appendix

C
.

Since the two sets
(0,A_{V},0,A_{Y})
and
(A_{U},0,A_{X},0)
carry the same information,
we pick the set
(0,A_{V},0,A_{Y})

and declare it
to be the solution to the self-duality conditions (
389
) that we associate to the gravitational solution encoded in
(A_{\rho},A_{v})
.
