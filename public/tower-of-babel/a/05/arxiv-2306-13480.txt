A spectral Galerkin exponential Euler time-stepping scheme for parabolic SPDEs on two-dimensional domains with a 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} boundary
Julian Clausnitzer, Andreas Kleefeld

Abstract
We consider the numerical approximation of second-order semi-linear parabolic stochastic partial differential equations interpreted in the mild sense which we solve on general two-dimensional domains with a 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} boundary with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The equations are driven by Gaussian additive noise, and several Lipschitz-like conditions are imposed on the nonlinear function. We discretize in space with a spectral Galerkin method and in time using an explicit Euler-like scheme. For irregular shapes, the necessary Dirichlet eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are obtained from a boundary integral equation method. This yields a nonlinear eigenvalue problem, which is discretized using a boundary element collocation method and is solved with the Beyn contour integral algorithm. We present an error analysis as well as numerical results on an exemplary asymmetric shape, and point out limitations of the approach.



1
Introduction.

We consider the numerical approximation of semilinear parabolic stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U(t)

\displaystyle=[\Delta U(t)+F(U(t))]\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(t),

\displaystyle U(0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}\in H,\quad t\in[0,T],

where
T>0
,
H\subset L^{2}(D)
is a Hilbert space of functions satisfying a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundary,
\Delta
is the Laplace operator,
F
is a nonlinear continuous operator and
W^{Q}
is a cylindrical
Q
-Wiener process with covariance operator
Q
(for more precise definitions, see §
2
). We make the common assumption that the eigenfunctions of
Q
and
\Delta
coincide, which allows for a series expansion of
W^{Q}
in terms of eigenfunctions of
\Delta
(see Appendix
A
).

The numerical solution of SPDEs has attracted much attention, and many different methods for temporal as well as spatial discretization have been developed (for a comprehensive overview, see e.g.
[
38
, Section 1.2]
). However, due to the
irregular behavior of SPDEs, strong regularity conditions have to be imposed on either the noise or the spatial domain.
Some publications focused on a one-dimensional spatial domain and potentially space-time white noise

[
1
,
2
,
6
,
9
,
17
,
22
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
42
]
, while others
have worked with trace-class or smooth noise and a potentially two-dimensional domain
[
13
, 6.24]
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
36
]
.
We intend to use the latter approach by considering additive noise that has sufficiently quickly decaying modes, but in exchange being able to consider a much larger class of general two-dimensional domains
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundary. This broadening of the class of spatial domains has been pointed out in
[
38
, p. 260]
as a subject of future research. Also, while there has been some theoretical treatment of SPDEs on domains with a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundary of dimension two or more with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
[
8
,
18
,
19
]
, to our knowledge no solutions of SPDEs have been numerically approximated in such domains.

Spectral methods have been widely used for the solution of semilinear parabolic SPDEs
[
6
,
24
,
28
,
39
]
and are straightforward to implement in cases where the base functions are known explicitly. If this is not the case, the spectrum of the linear differential operator as well as its eigenfunctions have to be numerically approximated. This can be achieved using boundary element methods (BEM) resulting in a nonlinear eigenvalue problem. The advantage of this method is that any bounded domain with a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundary can be considered, and the eigenfunction can be computed pointwise at any desired point in the interior of the domain, making it possible to store the functions on a uniform grid. Another possibility to compute the base functions are finite element methods (FEM), and for the Dirichlet Laplacian this has been done in
[
40
]
. The difference to BEM is that instead of domains with a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundary, FEM can deal with polygons with a Lipschitz boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since FEM requires the triangularization of the domain, if the functions are to be stored on a uniform grid, an additional error would be introduced by evaluating the functions on the triangularization at the required grid points. In this work, we focus on BEM and domains with
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundaries.

We give in §
2
a precise mathematical framework, including the conditions placed on each component of the equations which can be solved. In §
3
, the discretization in space and time of the mild solutions is given as a Galerkin projection in space and the exponential Euler scheme in time as described by Jentzen and Kloeden
[
29
]
. The boundary integral equation method is recalled, including the necessary discretization steps turning the Helmholtz equation into a nonlinear eigenvalue problem. The algorithm developed by Beyn
[
5
]
, which allows for the solution of nonlinear eigenvalue problems, is recalled, again also sketching the discretization steps necessary for the implementation. Lastly, some remarks on assembling the first elements of an orthonormal basis are made and it is shown how many boundary elements are needed to compute the eigenfunctions to a given accuracy. In §
4
, we prove a result on the strong error of the spectral Galerkin-exponential Euler scheme with eigenvalues and eigenfunctions which are approximated to a given accuracy. In §
5
, we demonstrate the convergence on an asymmetric shape and present a few solutions for different nonlinear functions. The MATLAB program code, including the data and programs to generate the plots shown in the figures, is available at GitHub
https://github.com/JulianSPDE/2D-SPDE
.

2
Mathematical setting and assumptions.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain whose boundary is of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and can be described by a parametrization
\gamma
such that
\|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\|_{2}>0
,
t\in[0,2\pi)
,
\gamma(0)=\gamma(2\pi)
and
\gamma
is nonintersecting, i.e.
\gamma(s)\neq\gamma(t)
for distinct
s,t\in[0,2\pi)
. Let
T>0
be fixed. We consider SPDEs of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}U(t)=[AU(t)+F(U(t))]\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(t),\quad U(0)=u_{0},\quad t\in[0,T]\end{split}

(1)

where
(U(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a stochastic process taking values in a Hilbert space
H
,
u_{0}\in H
,
A:D(A)\subset H\rightarrow H
is a linear sectorial operator, i.e. it generates an analytic semigroup
(\mathrm{e}^{At})_{t\in[0,T]}
, and

F:H\rightarrow H
is a nonlinear operator. The process
(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a cylindrical
Q
-Wiener process taking values in
H
, where
Q:H\rightarrow H
is a
trace-class operator. We will consider the Laplacian
A=\Delta:D(\Delta)\subset H\rightarrow H
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on the domain
D
, so that
we have
H=L^{2}(D)
and
D(A)=H_{0}^{1}(D)\cap H^{2}(D)
. It is also assumed that
\Delta
and the covariance operator
Q
commute, so that they have
a complete set consisting of the same orthonormal eigenfunctions
e_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
. This assumption of a joint eigenbasis for
Q
and
\Delta
has been treated in
[
7
]
and we show in Appendix
A
that this assumption can be satisfied for more general two-dimensional shapes.

The SPDE (
1
) can be rigorously understood as an integral equation involving a stochastic
\mathrm{d}W^{Q}
integral
[
16
, Chapter 4]
. It is interpreted in the mild sense
[
16
, p. 161]
, meaning that an
H
-valued process
(U(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a solution of (
1
) if

\displaystyle U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{At}u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{A(t-s)}F(U(s))\;\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{A(t-s)}\;\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s).

(2)

We now give some details on the properties of each component of the SPDE (
1
).

The linear sectorial operator
A=\Delta
.

It is known that for Dirichlet, Neumann and mixed boundary conditions, the Laplacian has a complete set of orthonormal smooth eigenfunctions
e_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Also, the eigenvalues
-\lambda_{i}
are all negative and real having exactly one accumulation point at
-\infty
(see e.g.
[
4
, Appendix W]
; we use a notation where
\lambda_{i}>0
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
). Hence, it is possible to give a spectral representation

\displaystyle\textstyle\Delta f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}-\lambda_{i}\langle f,e_{i}\rangle_{H}e_{i}(x)

for
f\in D(\Delta)
. We assume Dirichlet boundary conditions, so we call the
\lambda_{i}
Dirichlet eigenvalues and the
e_{i}
Dirichlet eigenfunctions.

The nonlinear operator
F
.

In our setting, the operator
F
on
H
is a Nemytskii operator, i.e. there is an associated function
u_{F}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that the operator
F
can be defined as
[F(g)](x)=u_{F}(g(x))
for
g\in H
.
For
F
, we adopt the assumption from
[
29
, Assumption 2.4]
and require that
F
be two times continuously Fréchet differentiable, and that its derivatives satisfy

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}(u)-F^{\prime}(v)\|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{L}\|u-v\|,

(3)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta)^{-r}F^{\prime}(u)(-\Delta)^{r}w\|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{L}\|w\|

(4)

for all
u,v\in H
and
w\in D((-\Delta)^{r})
for
r\in\{0,\ 1/2,\ 1\}
and

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{-1}F^{\prime\prime}(u)(v,w)\|\leq\tilde{L}\|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}v\|\cdot\|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}w\|

(5)

for all
u,v,w\in H
and
\tilde{L}>0
a constant.

The Wiener process
W^{Q}
.

The process
W^{Q}=(W^{Q}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a
Q
-cylindrical Wiener process
[
16
, §4.1.2]
[
37
, §3.2.35]
, i.e. a collection

\displaystyle\{W_{u}^{Q}(t),\ u\in H,\ t\in[0,T]\}

of zero-mean Gaussian random variables that satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W_{u}^{Q}(t)W_{v}^{Q}(s))=\langle Qu,v\rangle_{H}\cdot\min(t,s)

for
s,t\in[0,T]
,
u,v\in H
. Then, for an orthonormal basis
\{e^{Q}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of eigenfunctions of
Q
with corresponding eigenvalues
\{q_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the process
W^{Q}
has the series expansion in
H

[
16
, Proposition 4.3]

\displaystyle\textstyle W^{Q}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{q_{n}}\beta_{n}(t)e_{n}^{Q},

(6)

where
\beta_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, are independent real-valued standard Brownian motions. We assume that
Q
and the operator
\Delta
both have a joint eigenbasis consisting of the same functions, i.e. we assume
\{e_{n}^{Q}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=\{e_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(for details on this assumption, see Appendix
A
). The stochastic integral with respect to the cylindrical
Q
-Wiener process is defined in a similar way to the classical Itô integral (for details, see
[
35
, 2.5]
).

In the literature on SPDEs, at least one of the two constraints have to be made: Either a one-dimensional spatial domain is considered, where white noise as well as trace-class noise can be considered, or the covariance operator
Q
cannot be allowed to be
Q=I
, i.e. space-time white noise cannot be considered. This is due to the fact that a solution of an SPDE on a multidimensional domain (i.e.
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
) with space-time white noise (i.e.
W^{Q}
is a cylindrical Wiener process with
Q=I
) does not exist even for
F=0
, since vital regularity properties of the stochastic convolution

\displaystyle\textstyle W_{A}^{Q}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{A(t-s)}\;\mathrm{d}W^{Q}(s),\quad t\in[0,T],

in (
2
)
are violated. See
[
16
, Example 5.7]

[
41
, p. 329]
for discussions of these failures in spatial dimensions of two and higher. The deciding factor is the asymptotic behavior of Dirichlet eigenvalues, which has been described by Weyl’s law
[
23
, 4.1]
: If
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots
are the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the negative Laplacian for an arbitrary domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

\displaystyle\textstyle\lambda_{n}\propto\frac{4\pi^{2}}{(\omega_{d}\mu_{d}(D))^{2/d}}n^{2/d},\quad n\rightarrow\infty,

(7)

where
\mu_{d}(D)
is the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
D
and

\omega_{d}
is the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball. The regularity condition
[
29
, Assumption 2.2]
for the exponential Euler scheme which we will use requires

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\lambda_{n})^{2\gamma-1}q_{n}<\infty

(8)

for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
, which for space-time white noise, i.e.
q_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is not fulfilled in two dimensions. Instead, we assume that
q_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\alpha})
for some
\alpha>0
, in which case (
8
) holds if
\gamma<\alpha/2
. In our numerical experiments, we will use
q_{n}=n^{-2+\epsilon_{q}}
for an arbitrarily small
\epsilon_{q}>0
so that
\gamma
can be arbitrarily close to
1
(adding
\epsilon_{q}
is mostly a formal requirement which is justified in Appendix
A
).

3
Numerical scheme.

In this work, we combine two different numerical methods: The spectral Galerkin exponential Euler scheme is used in two spatial dimensions with eigenvalues and corresponding eigenfunctions which are numerically approximated using a boundary element collocation method in combination with the Beyn algorithm.

We use the spectral Galerkin method for approximation in space and the exponential Euler method for approximation in time to numerically solve the SPDE (
1
) (Section
3.1
). The spectral Galerkin scheme relies on projecting
the values of the process from
H
to the
N
-dimensional subspace
\text{span}(e_{1},\dots,e_{N})
, where the
e_{i}
are the Dirichlet eigenfunctions of
\Delta
(spatial approximation, Sections
3.1.1
,
3.4.2
), and
then discretizing the mild solution (
2
) in time (temporal approximation, Section
3.1.2
).
If the SPDE has to be solved on a bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the Dirichlet eigenfunctions
e_{i}
(and also the eigenvalues) for the domain
D
are needed.
They will be computed using a boundary integral equation method (Section
3.2
) with the Beyn integral algorithm (Section
3.3
) as described in
[
31
,
32
]
. Finally, we make a few comments on assembling an accurate orthonormal basis of Dirichlet eigenfunctions on a given shape (Section
3.4
).

3.1
The spectral Galerkin exponential Euler scheme.

We recall the spectral Galerkin method and the exponential Euler scheme.

3.1.1
Approximation in space.

We use a projection

\displaystyle P_{N}:H

\displaystyle\rightarrow H_{N}:=\text{span}(e_{1},\dots,e_{N})

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\langle f,e_{i}\rangle_{H}e_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle f,e_{i}\rangle_{H}e_{i}

with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,e_{i}\rangle_{H}=\int_{D}f(x)\cdot e_{i}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

and denote
U^{N}:=P_{N}U
,
F_{N}:=P_{N}F
,
A_{N}:=P_{N}A
,
u_{0}^{N}:=P_{N}u_{0}
and

\displaystyle W^{Q}_{N}:=P_{N}W^{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{q_{i}}e_{i}\beta_{i}(t).

The projected SPDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U^{N}(t)=(A_{N}U^{N}(t)+F_{N}(U^{N}(t)))\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{N}^{Q}(t)

has the mild solution satisfying for
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle U^{N}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}t}u_{0}^{N}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}(t-s)}F_{N}(U^{N}(s))\;\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}(t-s)}\;\mathrm{d}W_{N}^{Q}(s).

(9)

3.1.2
Approximation in time.

We introduce the equidistant time steps
t_{i}=h\cdot i
,
i=0,\dots,M
, with
h=T/M
, and denote
u_{k}^{N,M}=U^{N}(t_{k})
. Equation (
9
) can be rewritten in one time step from
t_{k}
to
t_{k+1}
as

\displaystyle u_{k+1}^{N,M}=\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}h}u_{k}^{N,M}+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}(t_{k+1}-s)}F_{N}(u^{N,M}(s))\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}(t_{k+1}-s)}\mathrm{d}W_{N}^{Q}(s).

We now make the approximation
F_{N}(u^{N,M}(s))\approx F_{N}(u^{N,M}(t_{k}))
.
Let
\widetilde{U^{N}}
be the process after this approximation and let
v_{k}^{N,M}:=\widetilde{U^{N}}(t_{k})
. We denote the inner products by

\displaystyle v_{k,j}^{N,M}:=\langle v_{k}^{N,M},e_{j}\rangle_{H},\quad F_{N}^{j}(v_{k}^{N,M}):=\langle F_{N}(v_{k}^{N,M}),e_{j}\rangle_{H}.

Taking the inner product with
e_{j}
on both sides and applying the above approximation, we obtain for the approximate Fourier-Galerkin coefficients

\displaystyle v^{N,M}_{k+1,j}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}h}v_{k}^{N,M},e_{j}\rangle+\langle\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}(t_{k+1}-s)}F_{N}(v_{k}^{N,M})\;\mathrm{d}s,e_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}(t_{k+1}-s)}\;\mathrm{d}W_{N}^{Q}(s),e_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}v_{k,j}^{N,M}+F_{N}^{j}(v_{k}^{N,M})\cdot\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}(t_{k+1}-s)}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}(t_{k+1}-s)}\sqrt{q_{j}}\;\mathrm{d}\beta_{j}(s),

using linearity of the inner product and the fact that
A_{N}he_{i}=-\lambda_{i}he_{i}
and hence
\mathrm{e}^{A_{N}h}e_{i}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}h}e_{i}
for
h>0
. The
\mathrm{d}\beta_{j}(s)
integral is normally distributed with mean zero and its variance can be computed using the Itô isometry. We finally obtain

\displaystyle v_{k+1,j}^{N,M}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}v_{k,j}^{N,M}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}F_{N}^{j}(v_{k}^{N,M})+\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}R_{k}^{j},

(10)

where the
R_{k}^{j}
are independent identically distributed standard normally distributed random variables,
j=1,\dots,N
,
k=0,\dots,M-1
. This is the exponential Euler scheme, as described in
[
29
, Section 3]
.

3.2
The boundary integral equation method.

The boundary integral equation method aims to solve boundary value problems such as the Helmholtz equation with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions by writing the solution as an integral layer operator involving an unknown density, approximating this density and subsequently reinserting it into the integral to compute the solution.

Let
\Phi_{\kappa}(x,y)
be the fundamental solution

\displaystyle\Phi_{\kappa}(x,y)=\text{i}H_{0}^{(1)}(\kappa\|x-y\|)/4,\quad x\neq y

(11)

of the Helmholtz equation

\displaystyle-\Delta u+\kappa^{2}u

\displaystyle=0\;\;\text{in}\ D,\qquad u=0\;\;\text{on}\ \partial D,

(12)

where
H_{0}^{(1)}
denotes the first-kind Hankel function of order zero (see
[
14
, p. 66]
) and let
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
be fixed.
For a point
y
on the boundary
\partial D
, we denote the exterior normal by
\nu(y)
and we use the double layer potential

\displaystyle\text{DL}_{\kappa}[\psi](x)=\int_{\Gamma}\psi(y)\cdot\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\kappa}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\partial D,

(13)

where
\psi\in C(\partial D)
is the unknown density function. We also define

\displaystyle\text{D}_{\kappa}[\psi](x)=\int_{\Gamma}\psi(y)\cdot\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\kappa}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad x\in\partial D.

For
x\in\partial D
, it is known
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
that the so-called jump relations

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}&\int_{\Gamma}\psi(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\kappa}(x\pm h\nu(x),y)\;\mathrm{d}s(y)=\\
&\int_{\Gamma}\psi(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\kappa}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}s(y)\pm\frac{1}{2}\psi(x)\end{split}

(14)

hold.
Since the fundamental solution
\Phi_{\kappa}
satisfies the Helmholtz equation (
12
), so does the double-layer potential (
13
). Conversely, every solution of (
12
) can be written as a double-layer potential
[
14
, p. 39]
. We use the double layer ansatz

\displaystyle u(x)=\text{DL}_{\kappa}[\psi](x),\quad x\in D

(15)

which, after letting
x
tend towards the boundary
\partial D
, with the jump relation (
14
) becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\psi(x)+\text{D}_{\kappa}[\psi](x)=0,\quad x\in\partial D.

(16)

Writing
\mathrm{M}(\kappa):=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{D}_{\kappa}
, (
16
) becomes
\mathrm{M}(\kappa)\psi=0
, which is a nonlinear eigenvalue problem. First, the boundary is subdivided into
n_{f}
curved elements called
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n_{f}}
, and (
16
) becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\psi(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\int_{\Delta_{j}}\psi(y)\partial_{\nu(y)}\Phi_{\kappa}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}s(y)=0,\quad x\in\partial D.

(17)

Let
m_{j}:[0,1]\rightarrow\Delta_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,n_{f}
, be bijective, continuous maps. With a change of variables, (
17
) becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\psi(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\int_{0}^{1}\psi(m_{j}(s))\partial_{\nu(m_{j}(s))}\Phi_{\kappa}(x,m_{j}(s))\|\partial_{s}m_{j}(s)\|\;\mathrm{d}s(s)=0,\quad x\in\partial D.

(18)

We now denote by
L_{i}(s)
the quadratic Lagrange polynomials and by
\widehat{L_{i}}(s)
the generalized Lagrange basis functions,
i=1,2,3
:

\displaystyle L_{1}(s)=(1-s)(1-2s),\qquad

\displaystyle L_{2}(s)=4s(1-s),\qquad\qquad\;\;\;

\displaystyle L_{3}(s)=s(2s-1),

\displaystyle\widehat{L_{1}}(s)=\frac{1-s-\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\frac{1-2s}{1-2\alpha},

\displaystyle\widehat{L_{2}}(s)=4\frac{s-\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\frac{1-s-\alpha}{1-2\alpha},

\displaystyle\widehat{L_{3}}(s)=\frac{s\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\frac{2s-1}{1-2\alpha}

and we fix a parameter

\displaystyle\alpha\in(0,1/2).

(19)

Each
m_{j}(s)
is approximated by a quadratic interpolation polynomial

\displaystyle m_{j}(s)\approx\tilde{m}_{j}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}v^{(j)}_{i}L_{i}(s),

where
v_{i}^{(j)}
,
i=1,3
are the end points of the
j
-th boundary element and
v_{2}^{(j)}
is its midpoint. We define the collocation nodes
\tilde{v}_{j,k}:=\tilde{m}_{j}(q_{k})
for
j=1,\dots,n_{f}
,
k=1,2,3
and
q_{1}=\alpha,\ q_{2}=1/2,\ q_{3}=1-\alpha
. The unknown function
\psi(\tilde{m}_{j}(s))
is approximated on each piece
\Delta_{j}
by a quadratic interpolation polynomial

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}\psi(\tilde{m}_{j}(q_{k}))\widehat{L_{k}}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\psi(\tilde{v}_{j,k})\widehat{L_{k}}(s).

Equation (
18
) then becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\psi(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\nu(\tilde{m}_{j}(s))}\Phi_{\kappa}(x,\tilde{m}_{j}(s))\|\partial_{s}\tilde{m}_{j}(s)\|\widehat{L_{k}}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s(s)\psi(\tilde{v}_{j,k})=r(x),

with a residue
r(x)
which results from the various approximations. For the boundary element collocation method, we require
r(\tilde{v}_{i,\ell})=0

[
3
, p. 50]
and obtain the linear system of size
3n_{f}\times 3n_{f}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\psi(\tilde{v}_{i,\ell})+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}a_{i,j,k,\ell}\psi(\tilde{v}_{j,k})=0

(20)

with
i=1,\dots,n_{f}
,
\ell=1,2,3
and with the integrals

\displaystyle a_{i,j,k,\ell}=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\nu(\tilde{m}_{j}(s))}\Phi_{\kappa}(\tilde{v}_{i,\ell},\tilde{m}_{j}(s))\|\partial_{s}\tilde{m}_{j}(s)\|\widehat{L_{k}}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s(s)

which are approximated using a Gauß-Kronrod quadrature. We write (

) abstractly as the nonlinear eigenvalue problem
M(\kappa)\phi=0

where

\displaystyle\phi=(\psi(v_{1,1}),\psi(v_{1,2}),\psi(v_{1,3}),\psi(v_{2,1}),\dots,\psi(v_{n_{f},3}))^{\top}.

(21)

3.3
The Beyn integral algorithm.

For a given wavenumber
\kappa
, the solution of (
12
) can be found by inserting the obtained density
\psi
back into (
15
), so the eigenfunction value can be
computed at any given point inside
D
. But first, the nonlinear eigenvalue problem
M(\kappa)\phi=0
for the density must be solved. This is accomplished with the Beyn algorithm

[
5
, p. 3849]
, which makes use of Keldysh’s theorem and is based on complex contour integrals of the resolvent
M^{-1}(\kappa)
, whose poles are the Dirichlet eigenvalues.

Theorem 3.1

(Keldysh’s Theorem.)
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain, and let
v^{*}=\overline{v^{\top}}
denote the conjugate transpose of a vector or matrix. Suppose that
C\subset D
is a compact subset containing only simple eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
,
n=1,\dots,n(\gamma)
with eigenvectors
v_{n}
,
w_{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle M(\lambda_{n})v_{n}=0,\quad w_{n}^{*}M(\lambda_{n})=0,\quad w_{n}^{*}M^{\prime}(\lambda_{n})v_{n}=1.

Then there exists some neighborhood
U
of
C
in
D
and a holomorphic function
R:U\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3n_{f}\times 3n_{f}}
such that

\displaystyle M(z)^{-1}=\sum_{n=1}^{n(\gamma)}\frac{1}{z-\lambda_{n}}v_{n}w_{n}^{*}+R(z),\quad z\in U\backslash\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n(\gamma)}\}.

(22)

Let
\sigma(M)
denote the spectrum of
M
and let
\gamma\subset D
be a contour which satisfies
\gamma\cap\sigma(M)=\emptyset
and containing
n(\gamma)
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n(\gamma)}
.
Let
f:D\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be holomorphic. Then, applying the residue theorem to (
22
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}f(z)M(z)^{-1}\;\mathrm{d}z=\sum_{n=1}^{n(\gamma)}f(\lambda_{n})v_{n}w_{n}^{*}.

For a specified contour
\gamma(t)=\mu+R\cos(t)+\text{i}R\sin(t)
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\in[0,2\pi)
,
R\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and the number
n(\gamma)
of eigenvalues enclosed in
\gamma
(with multiplicity), we pick
a random matrix
\hat{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{3n_{f}\times\ell}
with
3n_{f}\gg\ell\geq n(\gamma)
. We compute the contour integrals

\displaystyle\textstyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\text{i}}\int_{\gamma}M^{-1}(\kappa)\hat{V}\;\mathrm{d}s(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\text{i}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}M^{-1}(\gamma(t))\hat{{V}}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\;\mathrm{d}s(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{n(\gamma)}v_{n}w_{n}^{*}\hat{V}=VW^{*}\hat{V},

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\text{i}}\int_{\gamma}\kappa M^{-1}(\kappa)\hat{V}\;\mathrm{d}s(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\text{i}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\gamma(t)M^{-1}(\gamma(t))\hat{{V}}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\;\mathrm{d}s(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{n(\gamma)}\lambda_{n}v_{n}w_{n}^{*}\hat{V}=V\Lambda W^{*}\hat{V},

with
\Lambda=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n(\gamma)})
. These integrals are approximated using the trapezoidal rule:

\displaystyle A_{0,K}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\text{i}K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}M^{-1}(\gamma(t_{j}))\hat{V}\gamma^{\prime}(t_{j}),\;A_{1,K}=\frac{1}{\text{i}K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\gamma(t_{j})M^{-1}(\gamma(t_{j}))\hat{V}\gamma^{\prime}(t_{j}),

(23)

with
t_{j}=\frac{2\pi j}{K},\ j=0,\dots,K
. Next, we compute a singular value decomposition
A_{0,K}=V\Sigma W^{*}
with
V\in\mathbb{C}^{3n_{f}\times\ell}
,
\Sigma\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell}
and
W\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell}
. For
\Sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{\ell})
and a given tolerance
\epsilon_{\mathrm{sing}}
, we find
n(\gamma)
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{n(\gamma)}>\epsilon_{\mathrm{sing}}>\sigma_{n(\gamma)+1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{\ell}.

(24)

The singular values
\sigma_{n(\gamma)+1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{\ell}
which are below the tolerance are likely to be different from zero only due to rounding errors and are discarded. With the matrices
V_{0}:=(V_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n(\gamma)},\ \Sigma_{0}:=(\Sigma_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq n(\gamma),1\leq j\leq n(\gamma)}
as well as
W_{0}:=(W_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq\ell,1\leq j\leq n(\gamma)}

there is a regular matrix
S\in\mathbb{C}^{n(\gamma)\times n(\gamma)}
such that
V=V_{0}S
, and after rewriting some matrices, we have that

\displaystyle S\Lambda S^{-1}=V_{0}^{*}A_{1}W_{0}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}.

The matrix on the right-hand side therefore has the same eigenvalues and eigenvectors as
\Lambda
. We compute the
n(\gamma)
eigenvalues and
eigenvectors
s_{i}
of the matrix
V_{0}^{*}A_{1,N}W_{0}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n(\gamma)\times n(\gamma)}
. The
i
-th eigenfunction
u_{i}
is then approximated by inserting
\phi=V_{0}s_{i}
(see (
21
)) into

\displaystyle u_{i}(x)=\mathrm{DL}_{\kappa}[\psi](x)\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\hat{a}_{j,k}(x)\cdot\psi(\tilde{v}_{j,k}),

where

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j,k}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\nu(\tilde{m}_{j}(s))}\Phi_{\kappa}(x,\tilde{m}_{j}(s))\|\partial_{s}\tilde{m}_{j}(s)\|\widehat{L_{k}}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s(s),

which again are approximated using a Gauß-Kronrod quadrature.

3.4
Preprocessing: Building an orthonormal basis (ONB)

The analytical expressions of the Dirichlet eigenfunctions are known for some shapes. In the following, we will consider a shape which we call the Peanut shape (see Figure
3
), whose Dirichlet eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are not known analytically and whose parametrization for
t\in[0,2\pi)
is given by

\begin{split}\textstyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0.06\cdot((\cos(t)+2)\cdot(\cos(t+0.6)+2)\cdot(0.1\cdot\cos(3t)+2))-0.1\\
0.06\cdot(\sin(t)+2)\cdot(\sin(t-0.5)+2)\cdot(0.4\cdot\cos(2t)+2)\cdot(0.1\cdot\sin(4t)+1))-0.06\end{array}\right).\end{split}

For the boundary element collocation method, we choose
\alpha=(1-\sqrt{3/5})/2
(see (
19
)). Note that while any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
ensures at least cubic convergence, empirically it was seen
[
33
, Section 4.6]
that the choice
\alpha=(1-\sqrt{3/5})/2
yielded superconvergence for domains with a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundary.
Whenever the Beyn integral algorithm is used to compute its Dirichlet eigenvalues and eigenfunctions, we use
\epsilon_{\mathrm{sing}}=10^{-4}
as singular value tolerance (see (
24
)) and
K=24
for the periodic trapezoidal rule (see (

)).

3.4.1
Convergence and accuracy of base functions.

In previous work, the Beyn integral algorithm was used to compute Neumann eigenvalues, and a convergence with rates up to three for a fixed wavenumber could be observed
[
33
]
.
However, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions become less accurate for higher wavenumbers, so that more boundary elements are needed for higher wavenumbers. Previous publications from high-frequency
scattering theory using boundary element collocation methods to solve the Helmholtz equation have pointed out that this effort rises linearly with the wavenumber
[
11
,
12
]
, which we could confirm for the case of the Dirichlet eigenfunctions and eigenvalues (see Figure
1
). For the Peanut shape, we have conducted a convergence analysis for the first 200 eigenfunctions. For each wavenumber, we computed a reference function for
600
boundary elements which we compare to functions with lower accuracy (computed with 500, 400, 300, 200, 150, 100, 75 and 50 boundary elements,
respectively). For speeding up the computations, these functions were stored only on an equidistant
81\times 81
grid inside
[0,1]^{2}
, while the final functions making up the ONB are stored on a
301\times 301
grid to obtain results with better resolution (for the error analysis in Section
4
, we will denote the grid resolution as
R
and will use
R=301
). The pointwise storage on the equidistant grid means that the inner products, i.e. the integrals in the scheme (
10
) can be approximated numerically. As it is easy to implement and has a convergence order of four
[
21
, p. 21 f.]
(and hence, a convergence order of two for two-dimensional functions), we use the composite two-dimensional Simpson rule and set the numerically integrated function to be zero outside the boundary, so that the Simpson rule can be applied on the unit square. The application of the Simpson rule is possible since eigenfunctions of the Laplacian are always smooth
[
23
, §2.(i)]
.

Suppose that we use
n_{f}^{(i)}
boundary elements for eigenfunction number
i
,
i=1,\dots,N

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(\lambda)}:=\max_{i=1,\dots,N}|\widetilde{\lambda_{i}}^{(n_{f}^{(i)})}-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{(600)}|,\ \epsilon^{(\eta)}

\displaystyle:=\max_{i=1,\dots,N}\|\tilde{e}_{i}^{(n_{f}^{(i)})}-\tilde{e}_{i}^{(600)}\|_{H},

where the tilde denotes approximation and the superscript denotes that the approximation is done with
n_{f}^{(i)}
boundary elements. Depending on which
\epsilon^{(\lambda)}
and
\epsilon^{(\eta)}
we choose, different wavenumber dependent amounts of boundary elements are needed (see Figure
1
). We will later observe (see Example
5.1

4.4
it is crucial to reduce
\epsilon^{(\lambda)}
and
\epsilon^{(\eta)}
as much as possible.

and

.

,

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(1)}-V_{k}^{(2)}\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(2)}

(26)

and, for a constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1,j}^{(2)}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(2)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\cdot\left[L\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,i}^{(2)}+C\cdot R^{-2}\right].

(27)

Proof.
We compute, distinguishing
f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})
computed as an integral and
\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})}
computed as a numerical integral,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(1)}-V_{k}^{(2)}\|&=\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}V_{k,j}^{(1)}\cdot e_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}V_{k,j}^{(2)}\cdot e_{j}\right\|=\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(V_{k,j}^{(1)}-V_{k,j}^{(2)})\cdot e_{j}\right\|\\
&\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(1)}-V_{k,j}^{(2)}|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(2)}\end{split}

(28)

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1,j}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(1)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(1)})+\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}R_{k}^{j}\right.

\displaystyle\quad-\left.\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(2)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})}+\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}R_{k}^{j}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\ \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(2)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(1)})-\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})}|.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(1)})-\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})}|\leq\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(1)})-f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})|+\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})-\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})}|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{D}\left[f_{N}(V_{k}^{(1)}(x))-f_{N}(V_{k}^{(1)}(x)+\eta_{k}(x))\right]e_{j}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x+C\cdot R^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{D}C[L\cdot\eta_{k}(x)]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\right]^{1/2}+C\cdot R^{-2}=L\cdot\|\eta_{k}\|+C\cdot R^{-2}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle L\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,i}^{(2)}+C\cdot R^{-2},

where the Hölder inequality and the Lipschitz continuity of
f_{N}
were used. In total, we have

\epsilon_{k+1,j}^{(2)}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(2)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\cdot\left[L\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,i}^{(2)}+C\cdot R^{-2}\right].\hfill\qed

Lemma 4.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(2)}-V_{k}^{(3)}\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}

(29)

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1,j}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon_{j}^{(\lambda)}h}|\cdot(\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(4)}|)

(30)

\displaystyle\quad+L\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[\epsilon^{(\lambda)}+|\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}|\right]\cdot\left[L\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})|\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\sqrt{\frac{q_{j}}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda_{j}h})}-\sqrt{\frac{q_{j}}{2(\lambda_{j}+\epsilon_{j}^{(\lambda)})}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2(\lambda_{j}+\epsilon_{j}^{(\lambda)})h})}\right|

Proof.
Suppose that we have approximate eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}_{j}=\lambda_{j}+\epsilon_{j}^{(\lambda)}
,
j=1,\dots,N
.
Then by the same steps as in (
28
), the resulting error of the whole function is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(2)}-V_{k}^{(3)}\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}.

The coefficient errors after the next time step are given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1,j}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(2)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})+\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}R_{k}^{j}\right.

\displaystyle\quad-\left.\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(2)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})+\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}R_{k}^{j}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(2)}-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(3)}|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})-\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left|\left[\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}-\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}\right]R_{k}^{j}\right|.

Going through this term by term, we have that

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(2)}-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(3)}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(2)}-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(3)}|+\mathbb{E}|\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(3)}-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(3)}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon_{j}^{(\lambda)}h}|\cdot\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(3)}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon_{j}^{(\lambda)}h}|\cdot(\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(3)}-V_{k,j}^{(4)}|+\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(4)}|)

(31)

where we have made the last estimate since unlike
\mathbb{E}[V_{k}^{(3)}]
,
\mathbb{E}[V_{k}^{(4)}]
can be approximated from the numerical implementation. Next, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})-\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})-\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(2)})-f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})|+\left|\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}-\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\right|\cdot\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})|

\displaystyle\leq L\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[\epsilon^{(\lambda)}+|\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}|\right]\cdot\left[\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})-f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})|+\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})|\right]

(32)

\displaystyle\leq L\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}}{\lambda_{j}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(3)}

(33)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[\epsilon^{(\lambda)}+|\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\lambda_{j}-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{j}h}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}|\right]\cdot\left[L\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})|\right].

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(3)}-V_{k}^{(4)}\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\epsilon^{(\eta)}\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(4)}|\right]

(35)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\epsilon_{k+1,j}^{(4)}&\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\epsilon_{j}^{(\eta)}\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(4)}\|\\
&\quad+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[L\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\epsilon_{j}^{(\eta)}\cdot\mathbb{E}\|f_{N}(V_{k}^{(4)})\|\right].\end{split}

(36)

Proof.
Suppose that we have approximate eigenfunctions

\displaystyle\tilde{e}_{j}=e_{j}+\eta_{j},\quad\|\eta_{j}\|\leq\epsilon_{j}^{(\eta)}.

Then, the Fourier-Galerkin expansion of
V_{k}^{(4)}
also involves the approximate eigenfunctions, i.e.
V_{k}^{(4)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(e_{j}+\eta_{j})V_{k,j}^{(4)}
, and the error for the function is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(3)}-V_{k}^{(4)}\|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e_{j}V_{k,j}^{(3)}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}(e_{j}+\eta_{j})V_{k,j}^{(4)}\right\|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e_{j}(V_{k,j}^{(3)}-V_{k,j}^{(4)})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}V_{k,j}^{(4)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\;\;\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon^{(\eta)}\cdot\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(4)}|

\displaystyle=\;\;\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\epsilon^{(\eta)}\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(4)}|\right].

The error for the next coefficient errors is (with the wide tilde denoting inner products with
e_{j}+\eta_{j}
instead of with
e_{j}
)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1,j}^{(4)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}V_{k,j}^{(3)}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})+\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}R_{k}^{j}\right.

\displaystyle\quad-\left.\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\widetilde{V_{k,j}^{(4)}}+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})}+\left(\frac{q_{j}}{2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h})\right)^{1/2}R_{k}^{j}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}(V_{k,j}^{(3)}-\widetilde{V_{k,j}^{(4)}})+\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}[f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})-\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})}]\right|.

Dealing with these terms separately, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(3)}-V_{k,j}^{(4)}|+\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\mathbb{E}|V_{k,j}^{(4)}-\widetilde{V_{k,j}^{(4)}}|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\mathbb{E}|\langle V_{k}^{(4)},e_{j}\rangle-\langle V_{k}^{(4)},e_{j}+\eta_{j}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\mathbb{E}|\langle V_{k}^{(4)},-\eta_{j}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}\epsilon_{j}^{(\eta)}\mathbb{E}\|V_{k}^{(4)}\|

(37)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})-f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})|+\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})-\widetilde{f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})-f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})|+\mathbb{E}|\langle f_{N}(V_{k}^{(4)}),e_{j}\rangle-\langle f_{N}(V_{k}^{(4)}),e_{j}+\eta_{j}\rangle|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[\mathbb{E}|f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(3)})-f_{N}^{j}(V_{k}^{(4)})|+\mathbb{E}|\langle f_{N}(V_{k}^{(4)}),-\eta_{j}\rangle|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{\lambda}_{j}h}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\left[L\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\epsilon_{k,j}^{(4)}+\epsilon_{j}^{(\eta)}\cdot\mathbb{E}\|f_{N}(V_{k}^{(4)})\|\right].

(38)

Summing (
37
) and (
38
), we obtain (
36
).∎

Remark 4.5
.

For the case of the simpler error bound with

is equivalent to

Proof.

6
Outlook.

The advantages of the presented method are its flexibility in choosing the two-dimensional domain and the possibility to freely choose the points inside the domain at which the eigenfunctions are computed. This renders the solution of SPDEs with high resolution on a big class of domains possible. Due to the general setting of the spectral method, the scheme could be extended to a larger class of differential operators such as general second-order elliptic differential operators, whose Dirichlet eigenfunctions also form a complete orthogonal basis in
L^{2}(D)

[
10
]
. Difficulties might arise while using a boundary integral equation method, as explicit knowledge of the fundamental solution of the differential operator is required. The scheme could also be applied to three-dimensional domains with a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
boundary: In order to do this, the fundamental solution needs to be changed to the one for the three-dimensional Helmholtz operator, the curved elements are now two-dimensional (see
[
34
, Section 5]
) and the linear system resulting from applying Beyn’s contour integral algorithm is larger. However, for a small error, a very large number of eigenfunctions would have to be computed due to Weyl’s law (
7
) and (
40
).

The requirements on the nonlinear operator
F
could be relaxed by implementing a different scheme like the one from
[
27
]
, where the requirements on
F
are less strict. Future work might also include deriving error bounds for higher moments such as
\mathbb{E}[\|U(t_{k})-V_{k}^{(4)}\|^{2}]^{1/2}
.

Appendix A
Joint eigenbasis of
Q
and
\Delta
in two dimensions.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain,
d\geq 1
, with simple Dirichlet eigenvalues. It is well-known that a
Q
-Wiener process always has the series expansion given by (
6
) in terms of the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of
Q

[
16
, Proposition 4.3]
. In our setting (see end of Section
3
), we have
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|Qe^{Q}_{n}\|^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-(4+2\epsilon_{q})}<\infty
, so
Q
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. Furthermore, as is recalled in
[
7
, Section 3]
, the operator
Q
is Hilbert-Schmidt if and only if there is a kernel
q\in L^{2}(D\times D)
such that

\displaystyle(Qf)(x)=\int_{D}q(x,y)f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,\quad f\in L^{2}(D).

(50)

It is shown in
[
7
, Theorem 3.3]
that if
Q
is trace class (in our setting, it is, since
\mathrm{tr}(Q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}<\infty
), the kernel
q
can be understood as the correlation functional of a generalized Gaussian stochastic process (GSP)
\xi=\partial_{t}W
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\xi(t,x)]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}[\xi(t,x)\xi(s,y)]=\delta(t-s)q(x,y),

(51)

with
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and
x,y\in D
. (We note here that this correspondence to a GSP relies on a geometric assumption
[
7
, Assumption 3.1]
on
D
which is not fulfilled by the Peanut shape considered in this paper, but it is also remarked on
[
7
, p. 259]
that the results are true for ‘much more general domains’.)

A necessary and sufficient condition for
Q
to have a joint eigenbasis with
\Delta
is given in terms of
q

[
7
, Theorem 4.2]
: If
q\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(D\times D)
, then
Q
and
\Delta
have a joint eigenbasis if and only if

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}q(x,y)=\Delta_{x}q(x,y)\quad\text{and }\quad q(\cdot,\zeta),\ q(\zeta,\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{2}(D)

(52)

for all
x,y,\zeta\in D
, where
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\tau}(D)=\{u\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(D)\ |\ u=0\ \text{on }\partial D\}
, and with
\Delta_{x}
denoting the Laplacian in
x
with Dirichlet boundary conditions and likewise for
y
.

Solving the first equation in (
52
) by separation of variables, we write
q(x,y)=q_{1}(x)q_{2}(y)
and obtain

\displaystyle q_{1}(x)\Delta_{y}q_{2}(y)=\Delta_{x}q_{1}(x)q_{2}(y)=\lambda\Leftrightarrow\frac{\Delta_{y}q_{2}(y)}{q_{2}(y)}=\frac{\Delta_{x}q_{1}(x)}{q_{1}(x)}=\lambda

(53)

for a constant
\lambda
, for all
x,y\in D
where
q_{2}(y)\neq 0
,
q_{1}(x)\neq 0
. We retrieve from (
53
) the Helmholtz equations

\displaystyle\Delta q_{1}(x)=\lambda q_{1}(x),\quad\Delta q_{2}(y)=\lambda q_{2}(y)

(54)

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions for
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, with the solutions
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the Dirichlet eigenfunctions and eigenvalues on
D
, resulting in

\displaystyle q(x,y)=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}e_{i}(x)\right]\cdot\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}e_{j}(y)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{i}b_{i+1-j}e_{i}(x)e_{i+1-j}(y)

(55)

for real coefficients
a_{n},b_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The first condition in (
52
) now implies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\lambda_{i}a_{i}b_{i+1-j}e_{i}(x)e_{i+1-j}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\lambda_{i+1-j}a_{i}b_{i+1-j}e_{i}(x)e_{i+1-j}(y)

(56)

and since the eigenvalues are simple, due to orthogonality only the terms remain where
i=i+1-j
. For real coefficients
c_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

\displaystyle q(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}e_{n}(x)e_{n}(y),

(57)

and in our setting in two dimensions we choose
c_{n}=n^{-(2+\epsilon_{q})}
(see Section
2
). Indeed, as
Q
is symmetric and positive semidefinite
[
16
, p. 47 f.]
, and therefore so is
q

[
7
, p. 260]
, due to Mercer’s theorem
[
15
, p. 138]
,
q
always has a series representation (
57
) in terms of the eigenfunctions of
Q
, and we have shown that the Dirichlet eigenfunctions are the suitable choice for the Dirichlet boundary conditions.

On the second part in (
52
), we note that

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}q(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-(2+q_{\epsilon})}\lambda_{n}e_{n}(x)e_{n}(y),

(58)

is absolutely convergent due to
n^{-(2+q_{\epsilon})}\lambda_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-(1+q_{\epsilon})})
being summable. We also note that the family
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
norm since due to the Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\|1\cdot e_{n}^{2}\|_{1}\leq\|1\|_{1}\|e_{n}^{2}\|_{\infty}\Leftrightarrow\|e_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mu(D)\cdot\|e_{n}\|_{\infty}^{2},

(59)

so
\|e_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\mu(D)^{-1/2}
, as
\|e_{n}\|_{2}=1
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mu(D)
is the area of
D
. It now follows from the Weierstraß convergence criterion
[
20
, p. 259]
that (
58
) converges absolutely and uniformly, and therefore as a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous.
