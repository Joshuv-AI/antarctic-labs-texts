On singularity and normality of regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties
Hiraku Abe, Erik Insko

Abstract
. Regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties form an important family of subvarieties of the flag variety, which are often singular and sometimes not normal varieties. Like Schubert varieties, they contain distinguished points called permutation flags. In this paper, we give a combinatorial characterization for a permutation flag of a regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety to be a singular point. We also apply this result to characterize regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties which are normal algebraic varieties.



1.
Introduction

Hessenberg varieties are closed subvarieties of the flag variety originally defined and studied by De Mari, Procesi, and Shayman
[
DMS88
,
DMPS92
]
.
Each Hessenberg variety
\Hess(X,h)
is defined by two parameters; a linear operator (or
n\times n
matrix)
X
and a Hessenberg function
h\colon[n]\rightarrow[n]
satisfying
h(i)\leq h(i+1)
for all
i\in[n-1]
, where we use the notation
[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,3,\ldots,n\}
throughout this paper.

In this manuscript, we focus on the case when
X
is a regular nilpotent operator
N
and
h(i)\geq i+1
for all
i\in[n-1]
.
The geometric properties of regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties have been studied extensively, in for example

[
AFZ20
,
ADGH18
,
AHKZ21
,
Bal17
,
I15
,
IT16
,
ITW20
,
IY12
,
Kos96
,
Pre13
,
Pre16
,
ST06
,
Tym06
]
.
In 2018, Abe, Dedieu, Galetto, Harada determined an explicit list of generators for the local defining ideals of regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties and used those generators to prove that regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties are local complete intersections
[
ADGH18
]
(Proposition 3.7 and Corollary 3.17).
In this paper, we analyze those local defining ideals to explicitly determine which permutation flags in regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties are singular points.
Since the normality of a local complete intersection can be studied by the codimension of its singular locus, we apply our result to identify Hessenberg-Schubert cells of codimension one that consist entirely of singular points and obtain a characterization of regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties which are normal algebraic varieties.

While singular loci of Schubert varieties have been studied extensively and fruitfully
[
BL00
]
, relatively little is known about singular loci of Hessenberg varieties.
DeMari, Procesi, and Shayman noted that all regular semisimple Hessenberg varieties are nonsingular
[
DMPS92
]
. In 2019, Insko and Precup showed if
X
is semisimple (not necessarily regular) and
h
is the Hessenberg function with
h(i)=i+1
for
1\leq i<n
, then the irreducible components of
\Hess(X,h)
are nonsingular, and the singular locus of
\Hess(X,h)
consists of the intersections of these irreducible components
[
IP19
]
.

In regards to regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties, little is known outside of the realm of Peterson varieties; they form an important family of regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties named after Dale Peterson who was the first to study them and their relation to the quantum cohomology of the flag variety (cf.
[
Kos96
,
Pet97
,
Rie03
]
).
Kostant proved that almost all Peterson varieties are not normal by demonstrating that they have singularities in codimension 1
[
Kos96
]
. In 2012, Insko and Yong extended Kostant’s work to explicitly identify the singular locus of the Peterson varieties
[
IY12
]
. Their proof identifies the singular permutation flags in the Peterson variety and proves that if a permutation flag in a Peterson-Schubert cell is singular then every point in the cell is singular. Thus the singular locus of a Peterson variety is the union of the Peterson-Schubert cells which contain a singular permutation flag.

In this work, we generalize parts of the work of Insko and Yong and Kostant, by explicitly describing the singular permutation flags in each regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety and describing explicitly which regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties are normal algebraic varieties. We also identify examples of regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties that contain a Hessenberg-Schubert cell which is singular at its permutation flag and nonsingular at most of the points within the cell.

Let

.

A regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety

2.
Background on regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties

In this section, we recall the necessary background and establish our conventions regarding subvarieties of the flag variety.
Let
n\geq 2
, and
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denote the general linear group and
B\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denote the Borel subgroup of upper-triangular matrices.
The
flag variety
is the homogenous space

\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n})\coloneqq\{V_{\bullet}=(\{0\}\subset V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n})\mid\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{i}=i\ (1\leq i\leq n)\}

consisting of all nested sequences of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
It is well-known that

(2.1)

\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n})\cong\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B,

where this isomorphism takes a coset
gB\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
, for
g\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, to the
flag
V_{\bullet}
with
V_{i}
defined by the span of the first
i

columns of
g
.
Henceforth, we primarily use the coset definition of the flag variety and
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
to denote the flag variety.

Throughout this paper, we use the notation

\displaystyle[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}.

A
Hessenberg function
is a function
h\colon[n]\to[n]

satisfying
h(i)\geq i
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and

h(i)\leq h(i+1)
for all
1\leq i<n
, and we frequently denote a
Hessenberg function by listing its values in a sequence,

h=(h(1),h(2),\ldots,h(n))
.
As we saw in Section
1.1
, we may identify a Hessenberg function
h
and a configuration of shaded boxes in
[n]\times[n]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be an
n\times n
matrix, viewed as a linear map
X\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and

h\colon[n]\to[n]
a Hessenberg function.
The
Hessenberg variety
associated to
X
and

h
is defined to be

\Hess(X,h)\coloneqq\{V_{\bullet}\in\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n})\mid XV_{i}\subseteq V_{h(i)}\ (1\leq i\leq n)\}.

Equivalently, under the
identification (
2.1
), we have

(2.2)

\Hess(X,h)=\{gB\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B\mid(g^{-1}Xg)_{i,j}=0\ (i>h(j))\}.

Any Hessenberg variety
\Hess(X,h)
is, by definition,
an algebraic subset of the flag variety
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
. It is
straightforward to see that
\Hess(X,h)
and
\Hess(gXg^{-1},h)
are
isomorphic for all
g\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, so we frequently assume
without loss of generality that
X
is in Jordan canonical form (with one Jordan block and ones along its super-diagonal) with respect to the standard basis on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety

\Hess(N,h)
is the Hessenberg variety associated to the principal nilpotent matrix
N
in Jordan canonical form:

N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&&&\\
&0&1&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&0&1\\
&&&&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

It is known from
[
AT10
, Lemma 7.1]
that
\Hess(N,h)
is irreducible.
It is also known from
[
Tym04
]
that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Hess(N,h)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(h(j)-j)

which is precisely the number of shaded boxes below the diagonal of the configuration of boxes corresponding to the Hessenberg function
h
(see Section
1.1
).
In particular, it follows that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\codim_{\mathbb{C}}(\Hess(N,h),\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n}))=|\{(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]\mid i>h(j)\}|,

where the right hand side is the number of unshaded boxes in the configuration of boxes corresponding to the Hessenberg function
h
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the permutation group on
[n]
.
The Bruhat decomposition of the general linear group

\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})=\coprod_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}BwB

gives rise to a Schubert cell decomposition of the flag variety

(2.5)

\displaystyle\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B=\coprod_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}X^{\circ}_{w},

where
X^{\circ}_{w}\coloneqq BwB/B
is referred to as the
Schubert cell corresponding to
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
A Schubert cell
X^{\circ}_{w}
can be represented by a set of matrices
g
with
1
s in position
(w(j),j)
for
1\leq j\leq n
,
0
s below and to the right of the
1
s, and free entries above and to the left of the
1
s.
The Zariski-closure of a Schubert cell is a
Schubert variety

X_{w}
and it consists of a union of cells

X_{w}=\coprod_{u\leq w}X^{\circ}_{w},

where
u\leq w
in the Bruhat order of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}.

For each
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, there is the associated point
w_{\bullet}
in
\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n})
given by

w_{\bullet}\coloneqq(\langle e_{w(1)}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}\subset\langle e_{w(1)},e_{w(2)}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}\subset\cdots\subset\langle e_{w(1)},e_{w(2)},\ldots,e_{w(n)}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}),

where
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
are the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
This
w_{\bullet}
is called the permutation flag corresponding to
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
The Schubert cell
X^{\circ}_{w}
is the
B
-orbit of
w_{\bullet}
in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
with respect to the standard
B
-action from the left.

It has become quite commonplace to refer to the set
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{w}
as a
Hessenberg-Schubert cell
.
From (
2.5
), it is clear that we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Hess(N,h)=\coprod_{w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)}\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{w}.

Tymoczko proved in
[
Tym06
]
that this decomposition forms a paving by affines. In particular, each Hessenberg-Schubert cell
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{w}
is isomorphic to a complex affine space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
for some
d\geq 1
.
Precup generalized it to a much larger family of Hessenberg varieties in all Lie types
[
Pre13
]
.
The following claim tells us which permutation flags
w_{\bullet}
appear in (
2.6
).

Lemma 2.3
.

[
Tym04
, Proposition 2.6]

The permutation flag
w_{\bullet}\in Fl(\mathbb{C}^{n})
lies in
\Hess(N,h)
if and only if
w^{-1}(i)\leq h(w^{-1}(i+1))
for all
1\leq i<n
.

3.
Generators of the defining ideals of
\Hess(N,h)

3.1.
Local descriptions of
\Hess(N,h)

In the rest of this paper, we always assume that condition (
1.2
) in Section
1.1
holds.
It is well-known that the flag variety
\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n})
can be covered by affine open subsets, each isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{n(n-1)/2}
. Let
U^{-}\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the unipotent subgroup consisting of lower triangular matrices whose diagonal components are
1
.
Then we have an isomorphism
U^{-}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n(n-1)/2}
by taking strict lower triangular components.
The map

\displaystyle U^{-}\rightarrow\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B\quad;\quad g\mapsto gB

is known to be an open embedding.
For each permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we denote by
wU^{-}\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B
the
w
-translate of the image of this open embedding:

\displaystyle wU^{-}\coloneqq\{wgB\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B\mid g\in U^{-}\}.

By construction, we have
wU^{-}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n(n-1)/2}
for each
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and it is well-known that these form an affine open cover of
\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n})
:

\displaystyle\Flags(\mathbb{C}^{n})=\bigcup_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}wU^{-}.

This gives us an affine open cover of
\Hess(N,h)
:

\displaystyle\Hess(N,h)=\bigcup_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}.

We note that we may remove some pieces from this cover as follows.
Recall that we have the decomposition (
2.6
) of
\Hess(N,h)
into the Hessenberg-Schubert cells
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{w}
.
Since we have
X^{\circ}_{w}\subseteq wU^{-}
, it follows from (
2.6
) that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Hess(N,h)=\bigcup_{w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)}\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-},

where the union takes only the permutation flags
w_{\bullet}
lying in
\Hess(N,h)
.

We now describe the coordinate rings of the affine varieties
wU^{-}
and their subvarieties
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
.
By the construction above, an element in
wU^{-}
is uniquely determined by an
n\times n
matrix

g=(x_{i,j})
whose entries are subject to the following relations

(3.2)

\begin{split}&x_{w(j),j}=1,\quad(j\in[n]),\\
&x_{w(i),j}=0,\quad(i<j).\end{split}

Thus the coordinate ring of
wU^{-}
, denoted by

\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{x}_{w}]
, is the quotient
of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{i,j}\mid i,j\in[n]]
by the relations
(
3.2
).
More precisely, we set

\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]\coloneqq\mathbb{C}[x_{i,j}\mid i,j\in[n]]/I_{w},

where
I_{w}
is the ideal of
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
generated by

x_{w(j),j}-1\ \ (j\in[n])
and
x_{w(i),j}\ \ (i<j)
.

Namely,
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
is the coordinate ring of
wU^{-}(\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})/B)
.
For simplicity, we use the same symbol
x_{i,j}
to denote its image in
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
.
By having (
3.2
) in mind, we may identify the quotient ring
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
as the polynomial ring over
\mathbb{C}
with variables
x_{i,j}
satisfying
w^{-1}(i)>j
.
This leads us to the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

We call
x_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
a
genuine variable
if
w^{-1}(i)>j
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
n=4
.
For
w=3142
in one-line notation, the permutation matrix associated to
w
is

\begin{pmatrix}&1&&\\
&&&1\\
1&&&\\
&&1&\end{pmatrix},

and the genuine variables of the ring
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
can be visualized in matrix form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&1&&\\
x_{21}&x_{22}&x_{23}&1\\
1&&&\\
x_{41}&x_{42}&1&\end{pmatrix}.

For the rest of this section, we fix a permutation
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
to focus on the open affine subvariety
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
of
\Hess(N,h)
.
From
(
3.1
), we know that it is enough to study
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
with
w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
, but we do not assume this until we need it.
To describe the coordinate ring of
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
, we consider the collection of variables in the ring
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
in the matrix form

\displaystyle M\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}&\cdots&x_{1,n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{n,1}&\cdots&x_{n,n}\end{pmatrix}.

where we have

(3.3)

\begin{split}&x_{w(j),j}=1\quad(j\in[n]),\\
&x_{w(i),j}=0\quad(i<j).\end{split}

These conditions imply that the matrix
M
is invertible over
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
.
We set

\displaystyle f_{i,j}:

\displaystyle=(M^{-1}NM)_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}],

where
N
is the regular nilpotent matrix given in Definition
2.2
.
We consider the ideal of
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
defined by

(3.4)

\displaystyle J_{w,h}\coloneqq(f_{i,j}\mid i>h(j))\subseteq\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}].

It is clear from (
2.2
) that functions in
J_{w,h}
vanish on
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
.
The following claim says that
J_{w,h}
is in fact the defining ideal of
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
in
wU^{-}
under the assumption (
1.2
).

Proposition 3.3
.

(
[
ADGH18
, Proposition 3.7]
)

The quotient ring
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]/J_{w,h}
is the coordinate ring of
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
.
In particular, the ideal
J_{w,h}
is radical.

We will apply the following result to determine which points in
\Hess(N,h)
are singular and which are nonsingular. See e.g.
[
Har77
, Chapter I, Section 5]
.

Theorem 3.4

(Jacobian Criterion)
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}
be an affine variety, and let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
be a set of generators for the ideal of
Y
. Then a point
p\in Y
is a singular point of
Y
if and only if

\rank\left(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(p)\right)_{1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq j\leq m}<m-r,

where
r
is the dimension of
Y
, and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
are the standard coordinate of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

To apply this criterion to the subvariety
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
of
wU^{-}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n(n-1)/2}
, we compute the generators
f_{i,j}
in terms of genuine variables
x_{i,j}
in
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
. For this purpose, we first focus on the components of
M^{-1}
.
Let us write

y_{i,j}\coloneqq(M^{-1})_{i,j}\qquad(i,j\in[n]).

Then we have

\displaystyle(M^{-1}M)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(M^{-1})_{i,w(\ell)}M_{w(\ell),j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}y_{i,w(\ell)}x_{w(\ell),j}

\displaystyle=y_{i,w(j)}+\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{n}y_{i,w(\ell)}x_{w(\ell),j}\quad(\text{by \eqref{eq: convention of xij}}).

Thus, the condition
(M^{-1}M)_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}
is equivalent to

(3.5)

\displaystyle y_{i,w(j)}+\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{n}y_{i,w(\ell)}x_{w(\ell),j}=\delta_{i,j}.

From this, we see that

(3.6)

\begin{split}&y_{i,w(i)}=1\quad(i\in[n]),\\
&y_{i,w(j)}=0\quad(i<j)\end{split}

by an inductive argument with the base case
j=n
.
Hence, we obtain from (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) that

(3.7)

\displaystyle y_{i,w(j)}+\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{i-1}y_{i,w(\ell)}x_{w(\ell),j}+x_{w(i),j}=0\qquad(i>j),

where we note that the condition
i>j
ensures that the term
\ell=i
does appear in the summation in the left hand side of (
3.5
).
This enables us to compute
y_{i,w(j)}
inductively in terms of genuine variables. We will use the equality (
3.7
) in the next section to give a combinatorial formula for
y_{i,w(j)}
.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle f_{i,j}

\displaystyle=(M^{-1}NM)_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}(M^{-1})_{i,w(k)}N_{w(k),w(\ell)}M_{w(\ell),j}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq i}\ \sum_{j\leq\ell\leq n}y_{i,w(k)}\delta_{w(k)+1,w(\ell)}x_{w(\ell),j},

where we used (
3.3
) and (
3.6
) at the third equality. We make the following change of indexing of the variables and the generators.

Definition 3.5
.

For
i,j\in[n]
, we set

Properties of the generators of

In the previous subsection, we fixed a permutation

to discuss the affine variety
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
in
wU^{-}
and its defining ideal
J_{w,h}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
.
By (
3.8
) and Definition
3.5
, we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle g_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq w^{-1}(k)\leq w^{-1}(i)}\ \sum_{w^{-1}(j)\leq w^{-1}(\ell)\leq n}y_{w^{-1}(i),k}\delta_{k+1,\ell}z_{\ell,j}.

To better understand the structure of the generators
g_{i,j}
, we first study

\displaystyle y_{w^{-1}(i),k}\qquad(w^{-1}(i)\geq w^{-1}(k))

appearing in this formula.
When
w^{-1}(i)=w^{-1}(k)
, we have
i=k
and hence
y_{w^{-1}(i),k}=1
by (
3.6
). Hence, we consider the case
w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(k)
in what follows.
In this case,
we have from (
3.7
) that

(3.11)

\displaystyle y_{w^{-1}(i),k}+\sum_{w^{-1}(\ell)=w^{-1}(k)+1}^{w^{-1}(i)-1}y_{w^{-1}(i),\ell}z_{\ell,k}\ +z_{i,k}=0\qquad(w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(k)).

The next claim describes the explicit combinatorial structure of the summands appearing in
y_{w^{-1}(i),k}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

For
w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(k)
, we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle y_{w^{-1}(i),k}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{\bm{a}}(-1)^{d}z_{i,a_{1}}z_{a_{1},a_{2}}\cdots z_{a_{d-1},k},

where
D=w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(k)
is the degree of
y_{w^{-1}(i),k}
, and the second summation runs over all sub-sequences

\bm{a}=(k,a_{d-1},\ldots,a_{2},a_{1},i)

of the one-line notation of
w
whose head and tail are
k
and
i
, respectively:

\displaystyle w=\ \cdots\ \ k\ \ \cdots\ \ a_{d-1}\ \ \cdots\cdots\ \ a_{2}\ \ \cdots\ \ a_{1}\ \ \cdots\ \ i\ \ \cdots\ .

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
D=w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(k)>0
.
When
D=1
, we have
w^{-1}(i)=w^{-1}(k)+1
which means that
i
is the next number of
k
in the one-line notation of
w
:

(3.13)

\displaystyle w=\begin{matrix}\ \cdots&k&\!\!\!i&\cdots\ \end{matrix}.

The condition
w^{-1}(i)=w^{-1}(k)+1
implies that the equality (
3.11
) is valid and that the summation for
w^{-1}(\ell)
is vacuous in this case. Namely, we have

\displaystyle y_{w^{-1}(i),k}+z_{i,k}=0

which implies that
y_{w^{-1}(i),k}=-z_{i,k}
. This clearly satisfies the claim, where the (unique) subsequence appearing in the summation is simply
(k,i)
in (
3.13
).

We now assume by induction that the claim holds for the cases which satisfies
D=w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(k)<p
for some positive integer
p
, and we prove the claim for
D=p+1
.
Since we are assuming
w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(k)
, we have from (
3.11
) that

(3.14)

\displaystyle y_{w^{-1}(i),k}+\sum_{w^{-1}(\ell)=w^{-1}(k)+1}^{w^{-1}(i)-1}y_{w^{-1}(i),\ell}z_{\ell,k}+z_{i,k}=0.

To apply the inductive assumption to
y_{w^{-1}(i),\ell}
in the second summand, we need to verify that

\displaystyle w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(\ell)<D.

This inequality easily follows from the range of
\ell
:

\displaystyle w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(\ell)<\ w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(k)=D.

Thus, by the inductive assumption,
y_{w^{-1}(i),\ell}
appearing in (
3.14
) is an alternating sum of monomials whose indexes are sub-sequences

(\ell,a_{d^{\prime}-1},\ldots,a_{2},a_{1},i)

of the one-line notation of
w
:

\displaystyle w=\left(\hskip 40.0pt\ \cdots\ \ \ell\ \ \cdots\ \ a_{d^{\prime}-1}\ \ \cdots\cdots\ \ a_{2}\ \ \cdots\ \ a_{1}\ \ \cdots\ \ i\ \ \cdots\ \right)

for all
1\leq d^{\prime}\leq w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(\ell)
.
Also, the indexes of
z_{\ell,k}
in the second summand of (
3.14
) appear in the one-line notation of
w
as

\displaystyle\hskip-90.0ptw=\left(\ \cdots\ \ k\ \ \cdots\ \ \ell\ \ \cdots\cdots\hskip 100.0pt\right)

since
w^{-1}(k)<w^{-1}(\ell)
in (
3.14
).
Thus, each product
y_{w^{-1}(i),\ell}z_{\ell,k}
in the second summand of (
3.14
) satisfies the desired property; it is an alternating sum of monomials whose indexes are sub-sequences
(k,\ell,a_{d^{\prime}-1},\ldots,a_{2},a_{1},i)
of the one-line notation of
w
for all
1\leq d^{\prime}\leq w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(\ell)
.
Therefore, the second summand in (
3.14
) in total is an alternating sum of monomials whose indexes are sub-sequences
(k,a_{d-1},\ldots,a_{2},a_{1},i)
of the one-line notation of
w
for all
2\leq d\leq w^{-1}(i)-w^{-1}(k)=D
.
Combining this with the last summand
z_{i,k}
, we see that
y_{w^{-1}(i),k}
satisfies the desired claim for the case
D=p+1
.
∎

Corollary 3.10

4.
Computing Jacobian matrices

4.1.
Jacobian criterion for
\Hess(N,h)

In this section, we determine which permutation flag
w_{\bullet}
is a singular point of
\Hess(N,h)
by using the open affine cover (
3.1
) given by
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
such that
w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
.
Since each
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
is an affine subvariety of
wU^{-}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n(n-1)/2}
, we rewrite the Jacobian criterion (Theorem
3.4
) for
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
in the following.
To this end, recall from Section
3.4
that the defining ideal
J_{w,h}
of
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
is generated by
g_{i,j}
for
(i,j)\in H^{c}_{w}
, and observe that

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}wU^{-}-\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-})=\binom{n}{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(h(j)-j)=|H^{c}_{w}|

by (
2.3
) and (
3.19
) (c.f. (
2.4
)). We now obtain the following from the Jacobian criterion.

Proposition 4.1

(Jacobian criterion for
\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
)
.

A point
V_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
is a singular point of
\Hess(N,h)
if and only if

(4.1)

\displaystyle\rank\left(\frac{\partial g_{i,j}}{\partial z_{p,q}}(V_{\bullet})\right)<|H^{c}_{w}|,

where the row indexes
(i,j)
and the column indexes
(p,q)
of the Jacobian matrix run over all
(i,j)\in H^{c}_{w}
and all
(p,q)
for which
z_{p,q}
is a genuine variables, respectively.

Remark 4.2
.

The number
|H^{c}_{w}|
in (
4.1
) is exactly the number of rows of the Jacobian matrix in the left hand side.

Recall from Lemma
3.6
that the genuine variables
z_{p,q}
in
\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
are the ones which satisfy
w^{-1}(p)>w^{-1}(q)
.
For a set of (ordered) functions
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}[{\bf x}_{w}]
, we denote by

\displaystyle\text{Jac}\Big(\{F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{\ell}\},\{z_{p,q}\mid w^{-1}(p)>w^{-1}(q)\}\Big)

the Jacobian matrix consisting of
\frac{\partial F_{k}}{\partial z_{p,q}}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,\ell
and
(p,q)
with
w^{-1}(p)>w^{-1}(q)
,
where we order the genuine variables
z_{p,q}
as in Section
3.4
.
Recall from Lemma
3.6
again that the generators
g_{i,j}
appearing in
J_{w,h}
are the ones which satisfy
w^{-1}(p)>h(w^{-1}(q))
.
This leads us to consider the following Jacobian matrix.

(4.2)

\displaystyle\textbf{J}\coloneqq\text{Jac}\Big(\{g_{i,j}\mid w^{-1}(i)>h(w^{-1}(j))\},\{z_{p,q}\mid w^{-1}(p)>w^{-1}(q)\}\Big),

where the generators
g_{i,j}
are ordered as in Section
3.4
as well. This is the Jacobian matrix appearing in Proposition
4.1
.

In the next subsection, we will compute the rank of
J
to apply Proposition
4.1
.
As preparations for that,
we take a partition of the list of the generators:

\displaystyle\{g_{i,j}\mid w^{-1}(i)>h(w^{-1}(j))\}=\bigsqcup_{-(n-1)\leq d\leq n-1)}G_{d},

where we set

\displaystyle G_{d}\coloneqq\{g_{i,j}\mid w^{-1}(i)>h(w^{-1}(j)),\ i-j=d\}\quad\text{for \ $-(n-1)\leq d\leq n-1$}.

For
-(n-1)\leq d\leq n-1
, let
\textbf{J}_{d}
be the Jacobian matrix of
G_{d}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textbf{J}_{d}\coloneqq\text{Jac}\Big(\{g_{i,j}\in G_{d}\},\{z_{i,j}\mid w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(j)\}\Big).

It is clear that each
\textbf{J}_{d}
has exactly
|G_{d}|
rows, and the Jacobian matrix
J
is decomposed as

(4.3)

\displaystyle\textbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}\text{------}\ \textbf{J}_{-(n-1)}\ \text{------}\\
\text{------}\ \textbf{J}_{-(n-2)}\ \text{------}\\
\vdots\\
\text{--------}\ \textbf{J}_{n-2}\ \text{--------}\\
\text{--------}\ \textbf{J}_{n-1}\ \text{--------}\end{pmatrix},

where we mean that
\textbf{J}_{d}
with
G_{d}=\emptyset
are empty matrices.
For example, we always have
G_{0}=\emptyset
since
w^{-1}(i)\not>h(w^{-1}(i))
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, and hence

\textbf{J}_{0}
does not appear in this decomposition. We call
\textbf{J}_{d}
with
G_{d}\neq\emptyset
a
row matrix
of
J
.

Each row matrix
\textbf{J}_{d}
can be decomposed further as

(4.4)

\displaystyle\textbf{J}_{d}=(\ \textbf{J}_{d}^{L}\mid\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}\mid\textbf{J}_{d}^{R}\ )\quad\text{for \ $-(n-1)\leq d\leq n-1$}

by defining

\displaystyle\textbf{J}_{d}^{L}\coloneqq\text{Jac}\Big(\{g_{i,j}\in G_{d}\},\{z_{p,q}\mid w^{-1}(p)>w^{-1}(q),p-q<d+1\}\Big),

\displaystyle\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}\coloneqq\text{Jac}\Big(\{g_{i,j}\in G_{d}\},\{z_{p,q}\mid w^{-1}(p)>w^{-1}(q),p-q=d+1\}\Big),

\displaystyle\textbf{J}_{d}^{R}\coloneqq\text{Jac}\Big(\{g_{i,j}\in G_{d}\},\{z_{p,q}\mid w^{-1}(p)>w^{-1}(q),p-q>d+1\}\Big),

where the matrices with no columns are regarded as empty matrices.
We call
\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}
the
central block
of
\textbf{J}_{d}
.
We will use this structure of
J
to compute its rank in the next subsection.

4.2.
Jacobian matrix evaluated at permutation flags

The objective of this subsection is to prove Theorem
4.3
below which characterizes the permutation flags that are singular points of
\Hess(N,h)
.
Recall from Section
1.1
that a
lower diagonal full-string (of height
d-1
)
of
[n]\times[n]
is the set

\{(d,1),(d+1,2),\ldots,(n,n-d+1)\in[n]\times[n]\}

for some

. See for example, the black dots in the following picture gives the lower diagonal full-string of height
2
for

.

Recall also that
H^{c}_{w}\subseteq[n]\times[n]
is the Hessenberg complement conjugated by
w
defined in Section
3.4
.
As we stated in the beginning of Section

,
we are assuming
h(i)\geq i+1
for all

Theorem 4.3
.

A permutation flag

is a singular point of
\Hess(N,h)
if and only if the Hessenberg complement

contains a lower diagonal full-string.

Proof.

To begin with, we note that we have

which means that we can apply the results obtained in Section
3
.
By the Jacobian criterion (Proposition
4.1
) and the definition (
4.2
) of the Jacobian matrix

, it suffices to show that the matrix

evaluated at the permutation flag

fails to have its proper full rank

(the number of rows of
J
) if and only if
H^{c}_{w}
contains a lower diagonal full-string.

For a function

, we denote by
F|_{w_{\bullet}}\in\mathbb{C}
the evaluation of

at
w_{\bullet}
.
This evaluation is simply given by
z_{i,j}=0
for all genuine variables
z_{i,j}
.
Hence, to compute the evaluated Jacobian matrix

, it suffices to focus on the linear terms of the generators

.
Recall that we have the decomposition of
J
described in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
).
For a row matrix
\textbf{J}_{d}
of
J
,
Proposition
3.19

implies that the (possibly empty) blocks
\textbf{J}_{d}^{L}
and
\textbf{J}_{d}^{R}
in (

) are the zero matrices when evaluated at

:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\textbf{J}_{d}|_{w_{\bullet}}=(\ \mathbf{0}\mid\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}|_{w_{\bullet}}\mid\mathbf{0}\ ).

In other words, the components of a row matrix
\textbf{J}_{d}|_{w_{\bullet}}
are all zero except for the central block
\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}|_{w_{\bullet}}
. Moreover, for two distinct row matrices
\textbf{J}_{d}|_{w_{\bullet}}
and
\textbf{J}_{d^{\prime}}|_{w_{\bullet}}
with

, the indexes of columns of

are less than the indexes of columns of

since we have

for all

and

.
Namely,
\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}|_{w_{\bullet}}
is located in the upper left to
\textbf{J}_{d^{\prime},d^{\prime}+1}|_{w_{\bullet}}
in the entire matrix
\textbf{J}|_{w_{\bullet}}
.
This and (
4.5
) together imply that the matrix
\textbf{J}|_{w_{\bullet}}
fails to have the full rank

(the number of rows of
\textbf{J}|_{w_{\bullet}}
) if and only there exists a row matrix
\textbf{J}_{d}|_{w_{\bullet}}
such that its central block
\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}|_{w_{\bullet}}

(the number of rows of

In what follows, we focus on the central block

of each row matrix
\textbf{J}_{d}

.

For the case (ii), we have

4.3.
Jacobian matrix evaluated on Hessenberg-Schubert cells

In this subsection, we give sufficient conditions for the rank of the Jacobian matrix of
J
to be constant on each Hessenberg-Schubert cell. These criterions will be used in Section
5
to determine which
\Hess(N,h)
is a normal algebraic variety.
We also demonstrate that there exists a Hessenberg-Schubert cell whose permutation flag is singular, but its generic point is not.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
be a permutation flag.
Then the following hold.

(1)

If
w_{\bullet}
is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
, then every point

is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
.

(2)

If
w_{\bullet}
is a singular point of
\Hess(N,h)
, and
H_{w}^{c}
contains a lower diagonal full string of height
n-1
or
n-2
, then every point

is a singular point of
\Hess(N,h)
.

Proof.

Since we have
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{w}\subseteq\Hess(N,h)\cap wU^{-}
, we can apply the Jacobian criterion (Proposition

) to prove the claim of this proposition as well.
To this end, recall that

).
We denote by

its restriction on the Hessenberg-Schubert cell
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{w}
as in Definition

.
We decompose the Jacobian matrix

as in (

) and (

).
For a row matrix

of

\textbf{J}_{d}^{L}|_{\text{HS cell}}={\mathbf{0}}.

Namely, we obtain

\displaystyle\textbf{J}_{d}|_{\text{HS cell}}=(\mathbf{0}\mid\textbf{J}_{d,d+1}|_{\text{HS cell}}\mid\textbf{J}_{d}^{R}|_{\text{HS cell}}).

for each row matrix
\textbf{J}_{d}|_{\text{HS cell}}
of

in (

Moreover, applying Proposition

5.
Normality of regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
5.7
, which explicitly classifies when regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties
\Hess(N,h)
are normal algebraic variety based on the shape of their corresponding Hessenberg functions
h
. As they are local complete intersections (
[
ADGH18
, Corollary 3.17]
), they are Cohen-Macaulay (
[
Eis95
, Proposition 18.13]
). Hence,
\Hess(N,h)
is normal if and only if its singular locus is codimension 2 or greater
[
Har77
, Chapter II, Proposition 8.23]
. For
\Hess(N,h)
that are not normal, the proof of Theorem
5.7
also describes the the codimension 1 Hessenberg-Schubert cells which consists of singular points of
\Hess(N,h)
.
As in the previous sections, we always assume that a Hessenberg function
h\colon[n]\rightarrow[n]
always satisfies that

(5.1)

\displaystyle h(i)\geq i+1\qquad(1\leq i<n).

5.1.
Codimension 1 Hessenberg-Schubert cells

Let
w_{0}
denote the longest element in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Based on the work of Cho-Hong-Lee (
[
CHL21
, Definition 4.3]
), we begin with describing the set of codimension 1 Hessenberg-Schubert cells of
\Hess(N,h)
under the assumption (
5.1
).

Definition 5.1
.

Let
h(0)\coloneqq 1
. For
1\leq i\leq n-1
, we define a permutation
p_{i}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by taking cases as follows
(
we write the one-line notation in the second line of each definition
):

(i)

if
h(i-1)>i
and
h(i)>i+1
, then

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq w_{0}s_{i}\

\displaystyle\ =n,n-1,\cdots,n+2-i,n-i,n+1-i,n-i-1,\cdots,2,1,

(ii)

if
h(i-1)>i
and
h(i)=i+1
, then

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq w_{0}s_{n-1}\cdots s_{i+1}s_{i}

\displaystyle\ =n,n-1,\cdots,n+2-i,1,n+1-i,n-i,\cdots,3,2,

(iii)

if
h(i-1)=i
and
h(i)>i+1
, then

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq w_{0}s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{i}

\displaystyle\ =n-1,n-2,\cdots,n-i,n,n-i-1,\cdots,2,1,

(iv)

if
h(i-1)=i
and
h(i)=i+1
, then

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq w_{P_{i}}

\displaystyle\ =i,i-1,\cdots,1,n,n-1,\cdots,i+2,i+1,

where
w_{P_{i}}
is the longest element of the parabolic subgroup
P_{i}\leq\mathfrak{S}_{n}
generated by all simple transpositions in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
excluding

, i.e.,

P_{i}=\langle s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},s_{i+2},\ldots,s_{n-1}\rangle.

The following result is proven essentially in Sections 3 and 4 of
[

]
.
We restate the result in our notation here for ease of reference.

Proposition 5.2
.

We have
(p_{i})_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.
Moreover, the codimension-
1
Hessenberg-Schubert cells of
\Hess(N,h)
are precisely
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}
for

Proof.

Writing
d=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Hess(N,h)
,
the
(2d-2)
-th Betti number of
\Hess(N,h)
is equal to the second Betti number of
\Hess(N,h)
by
[
Pre16
, Theorem 12]
. Since we are assuming (
5.1
), the latter number is known to be

from
[
Pre13
, Corollary 4.13]
.
Thus, there are precisely

In
[

]
, the authors introduced the permutations

,

,
\ldots,

which satisfy

for

,

(2)

(see
[

By their Definition 4.3, it is clear that

Thus, the claim follows.
∎

correspond to codimension 1 Hessenberg-Schubert cells
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}
, we set about analyzing when each cell
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}
consists of singular points of
\Hess(N,h)
. We will use the following lemma repeatedly to classify singularity of these codimension 1 cells.

Lemma 5.4
.

For a permutation flag
w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
, we have
(i,j)\in H^{c}_{w}
if and only if

(w^{-1}(i),w^{-1}(j))\in H^{c}
, where
H^{c}\coloneqq H^{c}_{e}
is the standard Hessenberg complement.

Proof.

The claim follows directly from (
3.19
).
∎

The next two lemmas provide case-by-case analysis of when a codimension 1 Hessenberg-Schubert cell
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}

consists of nonsingular points of
\Hess(N,h)
.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
p_{i}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is defined as in cases
(i), (ii), or (iii)
,
then the permutation flag
(p_{i})_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.3
, it suffices to show that
H^{c}_{p_{i}}
does not contain any lower diagonal full-string. We consider the cases (i), (ii), (iii) of the definition of
p_{i}
separately.

Case (i) :

By definition, we have
p_{i}=w_{0}s_{i}
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle p_{i}^{-1}(1)=\begin{cases}n\quad&(i<n-1),\\
n-1&(i=n-1).\end{cases}

So we have
p_{i}^{-1}(1)\geq n-1
.
ence, it follows from Lemma
5.4
that we have
(a,1)\notin H^{c}_{p_{i}}
for all
1\leq a\leq n
since
p_{i}^{-1}(1)\geq n-1
implies that
(p_{i}^{-1}(a),p_{i}^{-1}(1))\notin H^{c}
.
Thus,
H^{c}_{p_{i}}
does not contain any lower diagonal full-string in this case.

Case (ii) :

By definition, we have
p_{i}=w_{0}s_{n-1}\cdots s_{i+1}s_{i}
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle p_{i}^{-1}(n)=\begin{cases}1\quad&(i>1),\\
2&(i=1).\end{cases}

So we have
p_{i}^{-1}(n)\leq 2
.
Hence, it follows from Lemma
5.4
that we have
(n,b)\notin H^{c}_{p_{i}}
for all
1\leq b\leq n
since
p_{i}^{-1}(n)\leq 2
implies that
(p_{i}^{-1}(n),p_{i}^{-1}(b))\notin H^{c}
.
Thus,
H^{c}_{p_{i}}
does not contain any lower diagonal full-string in this case as well.

Case (iii) :

By definition, we have
p_{i}=w_{0}s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{i}
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle p_{i}^{-1}(1)=\begin{cases}n\quad&(i<n-1),\\
n-1&(i=n-1).\end{cases}

Thus, the same argument as in Case (i) proves that
H^{c}_{p_{i}}
does not contain any lower diagonal full-string in this case as well.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

The permutation flags
(p_{1})_{\bullet}
and
(p_{n-1})_{\bullet}
are nonsingular points of
\Hess(N,h)
.

Proof.

We first show that
(p_{1})_{\bullet}
is a nonsingular point in
\Hess(N,h)
.
We take cases.
If
h(1)=2
, then (by the convention
h(0)=1
)
p_{1}
is defined as case (iv), and hence

\displaystyle p_{1}=1,n,n-1,\cdots,3,2

in one-line notation.
Notice that cases (ii) and (iv) define the same permutation
p_{1}
.
So we may think of this
p_{1}
as defined as in case (ii) so that Lemma
5.5
implies that
(p_{1})_{\bullet}
is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
.
If
h(1)>2
, then (by
h(0)=1
)
p_{1}
is defined as in case (iii) so that
(p_{1})_{\bullet}
is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
by Lemma
5.5
.

We next show that
(p_{n-1})_{\bullet}
is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
.
We take cases again.
If
h(n-2)=n-1
, then (by
h(n-1)=n
)
p_{n-1}
is defined as in case (iv), and hence

\displaystyle p_{n-1}=n-1,n-2,\cdots,1,n

in one-line notation.
Notice that cases (iii) and (iv) define the same permutation
p_{n-1}
.
So we may think of this
p_{n-1}
as defined as in case (iii) so that Lemma
5.5
implies that
(p_{n-1})_{\bullet}
is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
.
If
h(n-2)>n-1
, then (by
h(n-1)=n
)
p_{n-1}
is defined as in case (ii) so that it is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
by Lemma
5.5
.
∎

We are ready to classify regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties which are normal algebraic varieties.
Recall that a regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety
\Hess(N,h)
is always irreducible (see Section
2
) and that we are assuming the condition (
5.1
).

Theorem 5.7
.

A regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety
\Hess(N,h)
is normal if and only if we have
h(i-1)>i
or
h(i)>i+1
for all
1<i<n-1
.

Proof.

To begin with, we note that
\Hess(N,h)
a local complete intersection (
[
ADGH18
, Corollary 3.17]
).
Thus,
\Hess(N,h)
is normal if and only if the codimension of its singular locus is greater than or equal to
2
(
[
Har77
, Chapter II, Proposition 8.23]
).

Assume that if
h(i-1)>i
or
h(i)>i+1
for all
1<i<n-1
.
We first show that
(p_{1})_{\bullet},\ldots,(p_{n-1})_{\bullet}
are nonsingular points of
\Hess(N,h)
.
Since we know that
p_{1}
and
p_{n-1}
are nonsingular points by Lemma
5.6
,
it suffices to consider
p_{i}
for
1<i<n-1
.
Then we have
h(i-1)>i
or
h(i)>i+1
by the assumption. This means that
p_{i}
is defined as in case (i), (ii) or (iii). Hence, by Lemma
5.5
, it follows that
(p_{i})_{\bullet}
is a nonsingular point.
Hence, we proved that
(p_{1})_{\bullet},\ldots,(p_{n-1})_{\bullet}
are nonsingular points of
\Hess(N,h)
as claimed above.

Now, it follows from Proposition
4.7
(1) that every point of the codimension 1 cell
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}
is a nonsingular point of
\Hess(N,h)
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Therefore, by Proposition
5.2
, the codimension of the singular locus of
\Hess(N,h)
is greater than or equal to
2
which implies that
\Hess(N,h)
is normal.

Next suppose that there exists
1<i<n-1
such that
h(i-1)=i
and
h(i)=i+1
, and we show that
\Hess(N,h)
is not normal.
In this case, the permutation
p_{i}
is defined as in case (iv), that is,
p_{i}=w_{P_{i}}
. For our purpose, we prove that the Hessenberg complement
H^{c}_{p_{i}}
contains a lower diagonal full-string of height
n-2
:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\{(n-1,1),(n,2)\}\subseteq H^{c}_{p_{i}}.

We prove this as follows.
Since
1<i<n-1
, we see directly from the one-line notation of
p_{i}=w_{P_{i}}
that
(p_{i})^{-1}(n-1)=i+2
and
(p_{i})^{-1}(1)=i
.
Hence the condition
h(i)=i+1<i+2
and Lemma
5.4
imply that
(n-1,1)\in H^{c}_{p_{i}}
.
Similarly, we have
(p_{i})^{-1}(n)=i+1
and
(p_{i})^{-1}(2)=i-1
, and hence the condition
h(i-1)=i<i+1
implies that
(n,2)\in H^{c}_{p_{i}}
. Thus we obtain (
5.2
).
Since
\{(n-1,1),(n,2)\}
is a lower diagonal full-string of height
n-2
, we may apply Proposition
4.7
to see that every point of the Hessenberg-Schubert cell
X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}\cap\Hess(N,h)
is a singular point of
\Hess(N,h)
.
By Proposition
5.2
, this cell
X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}\cap\Hess(N,h)
is codimension 1 in
\Hess(N,h)
.
Therefore
\Hess(N,h)
is not normal as its singular locus is codimension
1
.
∎

We remark that Theorem
5.7
can be restated equivalently as
\Hess(N,h)
is not normal if and only if there exists some
1<i<n-1
with
h(i-1)=i
and
h(i)=i+1
.
In particular, one recovers Kostant’s result; Peterson variety (i.e.
\Hess(N,h)
with
h(i)=i+1
for
1\leq i<n
) is not normal except for
n\leq 3
(
[
Kos96
]
).
Colloquially,
Theorem
5.7
says that
\Hess(N,h)
is not normal if there is some index
1<i<n-1
where the Hessenberg function looks like that of the Peterson variety.

Example 5.8
.

When
n=5
, the regular nilpotent Hessenberg varieties (with the assumption (
5.1
)) which are not normal
are those corresponding to the following Hessenberg functions:

•

h=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\textbf{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\textbf{3}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\textbf{4}},5,5)
;
p_{2}=21543
and
p_{3}=32154
,

•

h=(3,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\textbf{3}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\textbf{4}},5,5)
;
p_{3}=32154
,

•

h=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\textbf{2}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\textbf{3}},5,5,5)
;
p_{2}=21543
,

where we highlight in red the pairs of indexes that offend the normality condition of Theorem
5.7
.
After each Hessenberg function, we list type (iv) permutations
p_{i}
from Definition
5.1
corresponding to a singular cell of codimension 1 in
\Hess(N,h)
.

We end this section with a proposition showing that every singular Hessenberg-Schubert cell in the Peterson variety corresponds to a permutation satisfying condition (2) of Proposition
4.7
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose that
\Hess(N,h)
is a Peterson variety
(
i.e.
h(i)=i+1
for
1\leq i<n
)
, and let
w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
be a permutation flag.
If
H_{w}^{c}
contains a lower diagonal full-string, then it contains a lower diagonal full-string of height
n-1
or
n-2
.

Proof.

We know that the permutation flags in the Peterson variety correspond to the longest words in the parabolic subgroups of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
(e.g.
[
IY12
, Lemma 9]
).
There is no lower-diagonal full string in
H^{c}_{w_{0}}
so the claim is trivial for the longest permutation
w_{0}
(the case of codim 0).
Since we have
h(i)=i+1
for
1\leq i<n
, the permutations
p_{i}=w_{P_{i}}
are all defined as in case (iv).
By Lemma
5.6
, the permutation flags for
p_{1}
and
p_{n-1}
are nonsingular points of
\Hess(N,h)
, and hence
H^{c}_{p_{1}}
and
H^{c}_{p_{n-1}}
do not contain lower diagonal full-strings by Theorem
4.3
.
For
1<i<n-1
, the argument of the proof of Theorem
5.7
shows that the Hessenberg complement
H^{c}_{p_{i}}
for
1<i<n-1
contains a lower diagonal full-string of height
n-2
.

So far we have proven the claim for each permutation whose Hessenberg-Schubert cell is of codimension
0
and
1
.
The remaining permutations to check are those below some of the permutations
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}
in Bruhat order.

Suppose that
w_{\bullet}\in\Hess(N,h)
and
\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{w}
has codimension 2.
Then
w\leq p_{i}
and
w\leq p_{j}
for some
1\leq i<j\leq n-1
and
w
is the longest word in the parabolic subgroup
generated by
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{i-1},s_{i+1},s_{i+2},\ldots,s_{j-1},s_{j+1},\ldots,s_{n-1}

and its one-line notation has the form

\displaystyle w=i,i-1,\ldots,1,j,j-1,\ldots,i+1,n,n-1,\ldots,j+1.

In other words, its permutation matrix is block diagonal and each block has ones along the reverse diagonal
[
AHKZ21
, Section 2.1]
.
Then
w^{-1}(n)=j+1
and
w^{-1}(1)=i
.
Since
i<j
, we have
h(i)<j+1
.
Hence it follows that
(n,1)\in H^{c}_{w}
by Lemma
5.4
.
This is a lower diagonal full string of height
n-1
.

If
w
lies below more than two of the permutations
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}
in Bruhat order, then the argument is essentially the same.
Let
1\leq i<j\leq n-1
be such
i
is the smallest index with
w\leq p_{i}
and let
j
be the largest index such that
w\leq p_{j}
.
Then
w^{-1}(n)=j+1
and
w^{-1}(1)=i
.
Since
i<j
, we have
h(i)<j+1
, and hence it follows that
(n,1)\in H^{c}_{w}
by Lemma
5.4
.
This is a lower diagonal full string of height
n-1
.
∎

6.
Future Directions and Open Problems

We end this manuscript with a list of interesting open problems.
It is known that the singular locus of a Peterson variety is a union of Peterson-Schubert varieties. More precisely, when
h(i)=i+1
for
1\leq i<n
,
\Hess(N,h)
is the Peterson variety, and
[
IY12
, Theorem 4]
implies that its singular locus is the union of Peterson-Schubert varieties
\overline{\Hess(N,h)\cap X^{\circ}_{p_{i}}}
for
1<i<n-1
.
Recently, Escobar-Precup-Shareshian proved that the singular locus of nilpotent codimension one Hessenberg varieties are in fact nilpotent Hessenberg varieties of different Hessenberg functions (
[
EPS22
]
); they are of course Hessenberg-Schubert varieties for the latter Hessenberg functions. These results motivate the following question.

Open Problem 6.1
.

When is the singular locus of a regular nilpotent Hessenberg variety isomorphic to a union of Hessenberg-Schubert varieties?

We also suspect that the results in this paper may generalize naturally to wider classes of Hessenberg varieties.

Open Problem 6.2
.

Generalize Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
5.7
to other Lie types or to
(
possibly non-nilpotent
)
regular Hessenberg varieties.
