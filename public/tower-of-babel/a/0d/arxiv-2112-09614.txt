TFD formalism: applications to the scalar field in a Lorentz-violating theory
L. H. A. R. Ferreira, A. F. Santos, Faqir C. Khanna

Abstract
The Thermofield Dynamics (TFD) formalism is considered. In this context, a Lorentz-breaking scalar field theory is introduced. In contrast to the Matsubara formalism, the best-known approach to introducing the temperature effect, TFD is a real-time formalism and is a topological field theory. While in Matsubara the temperature effects are introduced as a consequence of a compactification of the field in a finite interval on the time axis, in the TFD this effect emerges through a condensed state related to the Bogoliubov transformation. An advantage of the TFD formalism is that different topologies, which lead to different effects, can be chosen. Here, three different topologies are considered. Then the Stefan-Boltzmann law and Casimir effect at zero and non-zero temperature are calculated. This is a unique feature of TFD, which allows us to treat different phenomena in the same way.

Introduction

Temperature is a natural ingredient in all phenomena described by nature. However, to study the effects of temperature, a thermal theory is needed. One of the best-known theories for introducing temperature is the Matsubara formalism, also known as the imaginary time formalism
Matsubara
. This approach implies a replacement of the coordinate of time by imaginary time. Another thermal formalism is the real-time formalism which allows to analyze the temporal evolution of the system and temperature effects together. It is divided into two approaches: (i) the closed-time path formulation which consists of the doubling of degrees of freedom that modifies the Green functions
Schwinger
, and (ii) the Thermofield Dynamics (TFD) formalism proposed by Takahashi and Umezawa

To build the TFD formalism two fundamental elements are required: the duplication of the Hilbert space and the Bogoliubov transformation. The expanded Hilbert space is composed of the standard Hilbert space
\displaystyle{\cal S}
and the tilde space
\displaystyle\tilde{\cal S}
, i.e.
\displaystyle{\cal S}_{T}={\cal S}\otimes\tilde{\cal S}
. These two spaces are related through the tilde conjugation rules. Then an operator in the original Hilbert space is associated with two operators in the thermal or double Hilbert space. The other key ingredient is the Bogoliubov transformation, which is a rotation that involves the tilde and non-tilde operators. From this, the effects of temperature are introduced. Furthermore, this formalism is a topological field theory described by
\displaystyle\Gamma_{D}^{d}=(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{D-d}
, where
\displaystyle D
are the space-time dimensions and
\displaystyle d
is the number of compactified dimensions. This implies that any number of dimensions of the manifold can be compactified into the hyper-torus
\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{d}
. Then both effects, due to thermal and spatial compactifications, can be investigated. In this paper, the scalar field is considered in three different topologies: (i) the time coordinate is compactified into a circle of length
\displaystyle 1/T
, where
\displaystyle T
is the temperature; (ii) the
\displaystyle z
coordinate is compactified into a circumference of length
\displaystyle L
, and (iii) both coordinates, i.e.
\displaystyle t
and
\displaystyle z
are compactified. It is interesting to note that this formalism allows us to explore some different phenomena, such as the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the Casimir effect, in the same way. Using this theory, let us study the Casimir effect at zero and finite temperature for the scalar field in a Lorentz-violating theory.

Here, the main interest is the CPT-even aether-like term. This term was first introduced in a five-dimensional context and its consequences for different fields have been analyzed

The Casimir effect, proposed in 1948 by H. Casimir

Furthermore, the calculations are carried out in a natural covariant way. This aspect represents ease of calculation, such that the results are direct and accurate. TFD formalism

TFD formalism is a thermal quantum field theory that allows interpreting the statistical average of an arbitrary operator as its vacuum expectation value in a thermal ground state

In order to build a thermal vacuum, the degrees of freedom are doubled and, as a consequence, the Fock space is extended, that is, the thermal Fock space
\displaystyle{\cal S}_{T}
is composed of the original Fock
\displaystyle{\cal S}
and the tilde or dual space
\displaystyle\tilde{\cal S}
, i.e.
\displaystyle{\cal S}_{T}={\cal S}\otimes\tilde{\cal S}
. There is a mapping between the two spaces defined by the tilde conjugation rules which are given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle({\cal A}_{i}{\cal A}_{j})^{\thicksim}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{{\cal A}_{i}}\tilde{{\cal A}_{j}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle(c{\cal A}_{i}+{\cal A}_{j})^{\thicksim}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle c^{*}\tilde{{\cal A}_{i}}+\tilde{{\cal A}_{j}},

\displaystyle\displaystyle({\cal A}_{i}^{\dagger})^{\thicksim}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{{\cal A}_{i}}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\tilde{{\cal A}_{i}})^{\thicksim}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\xi{\cal A}_{i},

(1)

with
\displaystyle\xi=-1
for bosons and
\displaystyle\xi=+1
for fermions and
\displaystyle{\cal A}
is an arbitrary operator.

In addition to the duplication of Fock space, another tool is required in this formalism. The Bogoliubov transformation consists of a rotation, between the tilde and non-tilde operators, which introduces the dependency of a new parameter on that operator. This parameter, called the compactification parameter, is defined as
\displaystyle\alpha=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots\alpha_{D-1})
. The effects of temperature are introduced by taking
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=\beta=1/T
and all other components equal to zero.

As an example of applying the Bogoliubov transformation, consider two arbitrary operators
\displaystyle{\cal O}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\cal O}
in
\displaystyle{\cal S}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\cal S}
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\cal O}(k,\alpha)\\
\xi\tilde{\cal O}^{\dagger}(k,\alpha)\end{array}\right)={\cal U}(\alpha)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\cal O}(k)\\
\xi\tilde{\cal O}^{\dagger}(k)\end{array}\right),

where
\displaystyle{\cal U}(\alpha)
is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal U}(\alpha)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}u(\alpha)&-w(\alpha)\\
\xi w(\alpha)&u(\alpha)\end{array}\right),

with
\displaystyle u(\alpha)
and
\displaystyle v(\alpha)
related to the Bose distribution, i.e.
\displaystyle v^{2}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{e^{\alpha\omega}-1}
and
\displaystyle u^{2}(\alpha)=1+v^{2}(\alpha)
.

For the calculations that are developed in the next section, the modifications caused by the introduction of the
\displaystyle\alpha
parameter in the propagator of the scalar field are investigated. In the TFD formalism, this propagator is written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{0}^{(ab)}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha)=i\langle 0,\tilde{0}|\tau[\phi^{a}(x;\alpha)\phi^{b}(x^{\prime};\alpha)]|0,\tilde{0}\rangle,

(10)

where
\displaystyle a,b

\displaystyle=1,2
,
\displaystyle\tau
is the time ordering operator and
\displaystyle\phi(x;\alpha)={\cal U}(\alpha)\phi(x){\cal U}^{-1}(\alpha)
. In the thermal vacuum
\displaystyle|0(\alpha)\rangle={\cal U}(\alpha)|0,\tilde{0}\rangle
, the propagator is

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{0}^{(ab)}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha)=i\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}e^{-ik(x-x^{\prime})}G_{0}^{(ab)}(k;\alpha),

(11)

with
\displaystyle G_{0}^{(ab)}(k;\alpha)={\cal U}(\alpha)G_{0}^{(ab)}(k){\cal U}^{-1}(\alpha).
It is important to note that, the physical Green function is given by the non-tilde part, i.e.

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{0}^{(11)}(k;\alpha)=G_{0}(k)+v^{2}(k;\alpha)[G^{*}_{0}(k)-G_{0}(k)],

(12)

where
\displaystyle v^{2}(k;\alpha)
is the generalized Bogoliubov transformation
GBT
defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle v^{2}(k;\alpha)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{p}\sum_{\{\sigma_{s}\}}2^{s-1}\sum_{l_{\sigma_{1}},...,l_{\sigma_{s}}=1}^{\infty}(-\xi)^{s+\sum_{r=1}^{s}l_{\sigma_{r}}}\exp\left[{-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{\sigma_{j}}l_{\sigma_{j}}k^{\sigma_{j}}}\right],

(13)

with
\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\{\sigma_{s}\}
denotes the set of all combinations with
\displaystyle s
elements and
\displaystyle k

III
The energy-momentum tensor in the TFD formalism

Here, the main idea is to obtain the energy-momentum tensor for the scalar field within a Lorentz-violating theory. The term that leads to invariance breaking is the aether-like term. In the TFD formalism, the energy-momentum tensor is thermalized and, as a consequence, some different physical quantities are calculated at finite temperature.

The complete Lagrangian in the TFD approach, which describes the scalar field in the presence of the aether-like term, is given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{{\cal L}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal L}-\widetilde{{\cal L}}

(14)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{\lambda}{2}(u\cdot\partial\phi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\tilde{\phi}\partial^{\mu}\tilde{\phi}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(u\cdot\partial\tilde{\phi})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{2},

where
\displaystyle{\cal L}
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{\cal L}
are the Lagrangians in the original and tilde spaces, respectively. The violation of Lorentz symmetry is introduced through the term
\displaystyle\lambda(u\cdot\partial\phi)^{2}
, with
\displaystyle\lambda
being a very small dimensionless parameter which determines the effects of symmetry breaking in the theory and
\displaystyle u^{\mu}
is a constant vector that acts as a background field.

In order to obtain the energy-momentum tensor, only the non-tilde variables in the Lagrangian are considered. However, a similar development is performed with the tilde part. Then, using the definition

\displaystyle\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}=\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\phi)}\partial^{\nu}\phi-g^{\mu\nu}{\cal L},

(15)

the energy-momentum tensor associated with the Lorentz-violating scalar field is

\displaystyle\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}=\partial^{\mu}\phi\partial^{\nu}\phi+\lambda u^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\phi(u\cdot\partial\phi)-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\rho}\phi\partial^{\rho}\phi-\frac{\lambda}{2}g^{\mu\nu}(u\cdot\partial\phi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}m^{2}\phi^{2}.

(16)

It is important to note that the product of the field operators at the same space-time points should be avoided. To solve this problem, the energy-momentum tensor is written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}\tau{\bigg\{}\partial^{\mu}\phi(x)\partial^{\prime\nu}\phi(x^{\prime})+\lambda u^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\phi(x)\left(g_{\rho\epsilon}u^{\epsilon}\partial^{\prime\rho}\phi(x^{\prime})\right)-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\gamma}\phi(x)\partial^{\prime\rho}\phi(x^{\prime})

(17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\sigma\omega}g_{\lambda\theta}u^{\omega}u^{\theta}\partial^{\sigma}\phi(x)\partial^{\prime\lambda}\phi(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}m^{2}\phi(x)\phi(x^{\prime}){\bigg\}},

where
\displaystyle x^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle x
represent different points of space-time. Using the commutation relation

\displaystyle\displaystyle[\phi(x),\partial^{\prime\mu}\phi^{\dagger}(x^{\prime})]=in^{\mu}_{0}\delta({\bf x-x^{\prime}}),

(18)

with
\displaystyle n^{\mu}_{0}=(1,0,0,0)
being a time-like vector, eq. (
17
) becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}(x)=\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}{\bigg\{}\Gamma^{\mu\nu}\tau[\phi(x)\phi(x^{\prime})]+I^{\mu\nu}\delta({\bf x-x^{\prime}}){\bigg\}},

(19)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\prime\nu}+\lambda g_{\alpha\epsilon}u^{\mu}u^{\epsilon}\partial^{\nu}\partial^{\prime\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\gamma\rho}\partial^{\gamma}\partial^{\prime\rho}-\frac{\lambda}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\sigma\omega}g_{\lambda\theta}u^{\omega}u^{\theta}\partial^{\sigma}\partial^{\prime\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}m^{2},

(20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle I^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle in^{\mu}_{0}n^{\nu}_{0}+i\lambda g_{\alpha\epsilon}u^{\mu}u^{\epsilon}n^{\nu}_{0}n^{\alpha}_{0}-\frac{i}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\gamma\rho}n^{\gamma}_{0}n^{\rho}_{0}-\frac{\lambda}{2}ig^{\mu\nu}g_{\sigma\omega}g_{\lambda\theta}u^{\omega}u^{\theta}n^{\sigma}_{0}n^{\lambda}_{0}.

(21)

Now, let us take the vacuum expectation value of the energy-momentum tensor. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle T^{\mu\nu}(x)\bigl\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle 0|T^{\mu\nu}|0\bigl\rangle

(22)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}\bigg\{\Gamma^{\mu\nu}\bigl\langle 0|\tau[\phi(x)\phi(x^{\prime})]|0\bigl\rangle+I^{\mu\nu}\delta({\bf x-x^{\prime}})\bigl\langle 0|0\bigl\rangle\bigg\},

with

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle 0|\tau[\phi(x)\phi(x^{\prime})]|0\bigl\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-G_{0}(x-x^{\prime})

(23)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{im}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{K_{1}(m\sqrt{-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}})}{\sqrt{-(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}}

being the definition of the massive scalar field propagator. Here
\displaystyle K_{n}(x)
is the modified Bessel function.

Introducing the TFD formalism notation as presented in the previous section, eq. (
22
) reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle T^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x;\alpha)\bigl\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}\bigg\{\Gamma^{\mu\nu}G_{0}^{ab}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha)+I^{\mu\nu}\delta({\bf x-x^{\prime}})\bigg\}.

(24)

There is an important observation that must be made in the vacuum expectation value. This quantity is divergent for both cases, with or without the
\displaystyle\alpha
parameter. Then a renormalization procedure is required. This prescription consists of

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal T}^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x;\alpha)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\langle T^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x;\alpha)\bigl\rangle-\bigl\langle T^{\mu\nu(ab)}(x)\bigl\rangle

(25)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}{\bigg\{}i\Gamma^{\mu\nu}\overline{G}^{(ab)}_{0}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha){\bigg\}},

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{G}^{(ab)}_{0}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha)=G^{(ab)}_{0}(x-x^{\prime};\alpha)-G^{(ab)}_{0}(x-x^{\prime}).

(26)

In the next section, eq. (
25

\displaystyle u^{\mu}
which leads to a violation of the Lorentz symmetry.

V
Conclusions

The TFD formalism, which is a real-time quantum field theory at finite temperature, allows us to study some applications by choosing different topologies. Here, three different cases are considered. The first case consists of the investigation of effects resulting from the compactification of the time axis. The second case uses a similar topology, however, the compactification takes place along the
\displaystyle z
coordinate. In the latter case, both compactifications are considered at the same time. Based on this topological theory, different phenomena such as the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the Casimir effect can be considered on an equal footing. In order to analyze these effects, a massive scalar field in a Lorentz-violating theory is introduced. The term that introduces the violation of Lorentz symmetry is the CPT-even aether-like term. Then our results show how the Lorentz symmetry violation modifies the energy density associated with the scalar field and the Casimir effect at zero and non-zero temperature. The aether-like term, which breaks Lorentz, is constructed as the coupling between the derivative of the field and a constant vector
\displaystyle u^{\mu}

Therefore, the aether term changes both, the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the Casimir effect at zero and finite temperature. In addition, the limits of large mass, small mass, and low temperature are discussed. Although some results discussed here have been calculated in other works, similar analyzes using the TFD formalism have never been done in the literature. In this way, the results obtained are direct and exact, without any simplification or approximation. Within this formalism, the Casimir effect is calculated at zero and finite temperature just by choosing a different topology, and then the same proceeding is considered.
