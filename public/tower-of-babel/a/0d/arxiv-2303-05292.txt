Value distribution of certain differential polynomials leading to some normality criteria
Kuldeep Singh Charak, Nikhil Bharti

Abstract
In this paper, we prove some normality criteria concerning transitivity of normality from one family of meromorphic functions to another which improve and generalize some recent results. We also prove some value distribution results for certain differential polynomials which lead to some normality criteria involving sharing of holomorphic functions with certain differential polynomials. As a consequence, a counterexample to the converse of the Bloch’s principle is also given.

Let

The quantities

For

are the degree, lower degree and weight of the differential polynomial

respectively. The number

which is the highest order of derivative occurring in the differential polynomial

shall be called differential order of

Further, we shall denote by

the ratio of the weight of the differential polynomial to its lower degree. That is,

(1)

and
P[g_{n}]
partially share
a
in
\mathbb{D}.
However, the family
\mathcal{F}
is not normal in
\mathbb{D}.

Further, the assumption on the coefficients of the differential polynomial
P[f]
namely, “
a_{1}(z)\neq 0
” is essential. For example, consider the families
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
and
\mathcal{G}=\left\{g_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},
where

f_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{nz}\mbox{ and }g_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{z},~z\in\mathbb{D}.

Then
\mathcal{F},~\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{D}),
and
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
omit zero. Obviously, the family
\mathcal{G}
is normal in
\mathbb{D}
and
g_{n}\rightarrow g\equiv 1/z\not\equiv\infty.
Let
P[f]:=\frac{z^{k+1}}{(-1)^{k}k!}f^{(k)}.
Then

P[f_{n}](z)=\frac{z^{k+1}}{(-1)^{k}k!}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{nz^{k+1}}=\frac{1}{n}\mbox{ and }P[g_{n}](z)=1.

It follows that
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
partially share
0
and
\infty
in
\mathbb{D}
and for any
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

P[f_{n}]
and
P[g_{n}]
partially share
a
in
\mathbb{D}.
However, the family
\mathcal{F}
is not normal in
\mathbb{D}.

In
(
singh
, Theorem 1.7)
, Singh and the first author proved the following theorem:

Theorem 7
.

Let
n_{0},~n_{1},\ldots,~n_{k}
be non-negative integers such that
n_{0}\geq 2,~n_{k}\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
h~(\not\equiv 0)
be a holomorphic function in
D
with zeros of multiplicity
m~(\geq 1).
Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{M}(D)
be such that each
f\in\mathcal{F}
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+m+1
and poles of multiplicity at least
m+1.
If for every
f,~g\in\mathcal{F},

M[f]
and
M[g]
share
h
in
D,
then
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

We remove the condition on the multiplicities of poles of
f\in\mathcal{F}
and improve Theorem
7
as:

Theorem 8
.

Let
n_{0},~n_{1},\ldots,~n_{k}
be non-negative integers with
n_{0}\geq 2,~n_{k}\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
h~(\not\equiv 0)
be a holomorphic function in
D
having zeros of multiplicity at most
m~(\geq 1).
Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{M}(D)
be such that zeros of each
f\in\mathcal{F}
have multiplicities at least
k+m+1.
If for every
f,~g\in\mathcal{F},

M[f]
and
M[g]
share
h
in
D,
then
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

The inevitability of the condition “
h\not\equiv 0
” in Theorem
8
can be seen from the following example:

Example 10
.

Consider the family
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
of meromorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
given by
f_{n}(z)=e^{nz}.
Then for each
n,

f_{n}
omits
0.
Let
M[f]:=f^{2}f^{\prime}.
Then
M[f_{n}](z)=f_{n}^{2}f_{n}^{\prime}=ne^{3nz}.
Clearly, for distinct
m,~n,

M[f_{m}]
and
M[f_{n}]
share
h\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{D}.
However, the family
\mathcal{F}
is not normal in
\mathbb{D}.

The following example demonstrates that the condition “zeros of each
f\in\mathcal{F}
have multiplicities at least
k+m+1
” in Theorem
8
cannot be dropped:

Example 11
.

Let
k\geq 2
be a natural number and consider the family
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
of meromorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
given by
f_{n}(z)=nz^{k}.
Then for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
has zeros of multiplicity
k.
Let
M[f]:=f^{2}f^{\prime}.
Then
M[f_{n}](z)=f_{n}^{2}f_{n}^{\prime}=kn^{3}z^{3k-1}.
Take
h(z)=z^{3k-1}.
Then
M[f_{n}](z)-h(z)=z^{3k-1}(kn^{3}-1).
Clearly, for distinct
m,~n,

M[f_{m}]
and
M[f_{n}]
share
h
in
\mathbb{D}.
However, the family
\mathcal{F}
is not normal in
\mathbb{D}.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
8
, we have

Corollary 1
.

Let
n_{0},~n_{1},\ldots,~n_{k}
be non-negative integers with
n_{0}\geq 2,~n_{k}\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
h
be non-vanishing holomorphic function in
D.
Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{M}(D)
be such that for each
f\in\mathcal{F},

M[f]-h
has no zero in
D.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

It is noteworthy to mention that Corollary
1
leads to a counterexample to the converse of Bloch’s principle (see
bergweiler
;
charak-1
;
charak-2
;
charak-3
;
lahiri
;
li
) which states that if a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{M}(D)
satisfying a certain property
\mathcal{P}
in
D
is normal, then every
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
which satisfies property
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{C}
reduces to a constant.

Counterexample 1
.

Let
D
be any domain in
\mathbb{C}
and let
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be a family of meromorphic functions in
D
given by
f_{n}(z)=e^{z}.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be the property that for each
f\in\mathcal{F},

M[f]-h
has no zero in
D,
where
h
is a non-vanishing holomorphic function in
D.
In view of Corollary
1
, the family
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.
Now, let
f(z)=e^{z},~M[f]=f^{2}f^{\prime}
and
h(z)=-e^{3z}.
Then
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}),~h\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C})
and
h(z)\neq 0.
Clearly,
M[f](z)-h(z)=2e^{3z}\neq 0,~\forall~z\in\mathbb{C}.
Then
f
satisfies property
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{C}.
However,
f
is non-constant. This violates the statement of the converse of Bloch’s principle.

2
Some Value Distribution Results and Preliminary Lemmas

In this section, we state and prove some results which are crucial to prove the main results of this paper. Our first preliminary result is an extension of the famous Zalcman-Pang Lemma due to Chen and Gu
chen-gu
(see also
(
zalcman-2
, p. 216)
, cf.
(
pang-zalcman
, Lemma 2)
).

Lemma 1

(Zalcman-Pang Lemma)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{M}(D)
be such that all of its zeros have multiplicities at least
m
and all its poles have multiplicities at least
p.
Let
-p<\alpha<m.
If
\mathcal{F}
is not normal at
z_{0}\in D,
then there exist
sequences
\left\{f_{n}\right\}\subset\mathcal{F},

\left\{z_{n}\right\}\subset D
satisfying
z_{n}\rightarrow z_{0}
and positive numbers
\rho_{n}
with
\rho_{n}\rightarrow 0
such that the sequence
\left\{g_{n}\right\}
defined by

g_{n}(\zeta)=\rho_{n}^{-\alpha}f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta)\rightarrow g(\zeta)

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
with respect to the spherical metric, where
g
is a non-constant meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
such that for every
\zeta\in\mathbb{C},

g^{\#}(\zeta)\leq g^{\#}(0)=1.

The following lemma is due to Hayman (see
(
hayman-2
, Theorem 3)
, cf.
(
hayman
, Theorem 3.5)
) commonly known as “Hayman’s Alternative”:

Lemma 2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
be a non-constant function and
k
be a positive integer. Then for any
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\},
either
f
or
f^{(k)}-a
has at least one zero in
\mathbb{C}.
Moreover, if
f
is transcendental, then
f
or
f^{(k)}-a
has infinitely many zeros in
\mathbb{C}.

Lemma 3
.

(
(
wang
, Lemma 6)
, cf.
(
bergo
, Theorem 3)
)
Let
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
be a transcendental function of finite order having zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1,
where
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Then for
j=1,\ldots,k,

f^{(j)}
assumes every non-zero complex number infinitely many times.

Lemma 4
.

(
lo
, Lemma 1.2)

Let
f_{j}~(j=1,~2)
be two non-constant meromorphic functions in
\mathbb{C}.
Then

N(r,f_{1}f_{2})-N\left(r,\frac{1}{f_{1}f_{2}}\right)=N(r,f_{1})+N(r,f_{2})-N\left(r,\frac{1}{f_{1}}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{f_{2}}\right).

Lemma 5
.

(
singh
, Lemma 2.1)

Let
n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},m
be non-negative integers with
n_{0}\geq 2,~\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
p~(\not\equiv 0)
be a polynomial of degree
m
and
f
be a non-constant rational function with zeros of multiplicity at least
k+m
and poles of multiplicity at least
m+1.
Let
M[f]=\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k}\left(f^{(j)}\right)^{n_{j}}
be a differential monomial of
f.
Then
M[f]-p
has at least two distinct zeros in
\mathbb{C}.

Lemma 6
.

Let
f
be a non-constant and non-vanishing rational function in
\mathbb{C}
and let
p~(\not\equiv 0)
be a polynomial. Let
M[f]=\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k}\left(f^{(j)}\right)^{n_{j}}
be a differential monomial of
f,
where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{j}
’s (
j=0,1,\ldots,k
) are non-negative integers such that
\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}n_{j}\geq 1.
Then
M[f]-p
has at least
\Gamma_{M}
distinct zeros in
\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

The proof is based on the method of Chang
chang
(see also
deng
) with significant modifications. Since the computations involved in the proof are intricate, we describe the proof in detail.

Since
f
is non-vanishing,
f
cannot be a polynomial and so
f
has at least one pole. Thus we can write

f(z)=\frac{A}{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(z+z_{i})^{t_{i}}}

(3)

and

p(z)=B\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(z+v_{i})^{d_{i}},

(4)

where
A,~B
are non-zero constants,
d,~t,~t_{i}
are positive integers and
d_{i}
are non-negative integers. Also,
v_{i}
(when
1\leq i\leq d
) are distinct complex numbers and
z_{i}
(when
1\leq i\leq t
) are distinct complex numbers.

From (
3
), we deduce that

M[f](z)=\frac{g(z)}{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(z+z_{i})^{(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}}},

(5)

where
g
is a polynomial of degree
(t-1)(\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}).

Also, by assumption, it easily follows that
M[f]-p
has at least one zero in
\mathbb{C}.
Thus we may set

M[f](z)=p(z)+\frac{C\prod\limits_{i=1}^{s}(z+w_{i})^{l_{i}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(z+z_{i})^{(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}}},

(6)

where
C(\neq 0)\in\mathbb{C},

l_{i}
are positive integers and
w_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq s
) are distinct complex numbers.

Let
D=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}d_{i}
and
T=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}t_{i}.
Then from (
4
), (
5
) and (
6
), we get

B\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(z+v_{i})^{d_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}(z+z_{i})^{(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}}+C\prod\limits_{i=1}^{s}(z+w_{i})^{l_{i}}=g(z)

(7)

By simple calculation, one can easily see that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}l_{i}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\left[(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}\right]+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}d_{i}=(T-t)\gamma_{M}+t\Gamma_{M}+D

and
C=-B.

Also, from (
7
), we get

\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1+v_{i}r)^{d_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1+z_{i}r)^{(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}}-\prod\limits_{i=1}^{s}(1+w_{i}r)^{l_{i}}=r^{\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D}h(r),

where
h(r):=r^{(t-1)(\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M})}g(1/r)/B
is a polynomial of degree less than
(t-1)(\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}).
Furthermore, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1+v_{i}r)^{d_{i}}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{t}(1+z_{i}r)^{(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{s}(1+w_{i}r)^{l_{i}}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{r^{\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D}h(r)}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{s}(1+w_{i}r)^{l_{i}}}

\displaystyle=1+O\left(r^{\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D}\right)

(8)

as
r\rightarrow 0.
Taking logarithmic derivatives of both sides of (
2
), we obtain

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\frac{d_{i}v_{i}}{1+v_{i}r}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\frac{\left[(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}\right]z_{i}}{1+z_{i}r}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}\frac{l_{i}w_{i}}{1+w_{i}r}=O\left(r^{\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D-1}\right)

(9)

as
r\rightarrow 0.

Let
S_{1}=\left\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{d}\right\}\cap\left\{z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{t}\right\}
and
S_{2}=\left\{v_{1},~v_{2},\ldots,v_{d}\right\}\cap\left\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{s}\right\}.
Then the following four cases arise:

Case 1:

S_{1}=S_{2}=\phi
. Let
z_{t+i}=v_{i}
when
1\leq i\leq d
and

T_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(t_{i}-1)\gamma_{M}+\Gamma_{M}&\mbox{if}~1\leq i\leq t,\\
d_{i-t}&\mbox{if}~t+1\leq i\leq t+d.\end{array}\right.

Then (
9
) can be written as

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t+d}\frac{T_{i}z_{i}}{1+z_{i}r}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}\frac{l_{i}w_{i}}{1+w_{i}r}=O\left(r^{\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D-1}\right)~\mbox{as $r\rightarrow 0.$}

(10)

Comparing the coefficients of
r^{j},~j=0,1,\ldots,\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D-2
in (
10
), we find that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t+d}T_{i}z^{j}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}l_{i}w^{j}=0,~\mbox{for each}~j=1,2,\ldots,\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D-1.

(11)

Let
z_{t+d+i}=w_{i},~1\leq i\leq s.
Then from (
11
), we deduce that the system of linear equations

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t+d+s}z_{i}^{j}x_{i}=0,~j=0,1,\ldots,\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D-1,

(12)

has a non-zero solution

\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t+d},x_{t+d+1},\ldots,x_{t+d+s}\right)=\left(T_{1},\ldots,T_{t+d},-l_{1},\ldots,-l_{s}\right).

If
\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D\geq t+d+s,
then by Cramer’s rule (see
(
mirsky
, p. 134)
), for
0\leq j\leq\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D-1,
the determinant det
(z_{i}^{j})_{(t+d+s)\times(t+d+s)}
of the coefficients of the system (
12
) is equal to zero. However, since
z_{i},~1\leq i\leq t+d+s
are distinct and det
(z_{i}^{j})_{(t+d+s)\times(t+d+s)}
is a Vandermonde determinant (see
(
mirsky
, p. 17)
), so it cannot be equal to zero, a contradiction. Hence
\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D<t+d+s.
Since
T=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}t_{i}\geq t
and
D=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}d_{i}\geq d,
it follows that
s\geq\Gamma_{M}.

Case 2:

S_{1}\neq\phi
and
S_{2}=\phi.
We may assume, without loss of generality, that

Then

for

Thus (

L_{i}=l_{i}-d_{m_{1}+i},~\mbox{for }1\leq i\leq m_{2}=s.

Then in both subcases, (
9
) can be written as

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t+m_{5}}\frac{T_{i}z_{i}}{1+z_{i}r}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}\frac{L_{i}w_{i}}{1+w_{i}r}=O\left(r^{\Gamma_{M}+(T-1)\gamma_{M}+D-1}\right)~\mbox{as $r\rightarrow 0,$}

where

Proceeding in similar fashion as in Case 1, we deduce that

The following lemma is an extension of a result of Deng et al
(
deng-2
, Lemma 5)
to a more general differential monomial:

Lemma 7
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and
n_{0},~n_{1},\ldots,~n_{k}
be non-negative integers with
n_{0}\geq 2,~n_{k}\geq 1.
Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function in
\mathbb{C}
and
p
be a polynomial of degree
m\geq 1.
Let
M[f]=\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k}\left(f^{(j)}\right)^{n_{j}}
be a differential monomial of
f.
If
f
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+m,
then
M[f]-p
has at least two distinct zeros in
\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

Since
f\not\equiv 0,
we can write

\frac{1}{f^{\gamma_{M}}}=\frac{M[f]}{pf^{\gamma_{M}}}-\frac{pM[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}{p~f^{\gamma_{M}}}\cdot\frac{M[f]-p}{pM[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]},

where
\gamma_{M}=n_{0}+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}.

Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{M}~m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(r,\frac{M[f]}{pf^{\gamma_{M}}}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}{pf^{\gamma_{M}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+m\left(r,\frac{M[f]-p}{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)+O(1).

From the Nevanlinna’s theorem on logarithmic derivative, we have
m\left(r,\frac{M[f]}{f^{\gamma_{M}}}\right)=S(r,f).
Also, using
m(r,p)=m\log{r}+O(1),~m\left(r,\frac{1}{p}\right)=O(1)
and Lemma
4
, we get

\displaystyle\gamma_{M}~m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(r,\frac{M[f]-p}{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle=m\left(r,\frac{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}{M[f]-p}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}{M[f]-p}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-~N\left(r,\frac{M[f]-p}{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle=m\left(r,\frac{p\left[\frac{M[f]}{p}-1\right]^{\prime}}{\left[\frac{M[f]}{p}-1\right]}\right)+N\left(r,p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]\right)+N\left(r.\frac{1}{M[f]-p}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-~N\left(r,\frac{1}{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)-N\left(r,M[f]-p\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\overline{N}(r,f)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+m\log{r}+S(r,f).

This gives

\displaystyle\gamma_{M}~T(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\overline{N}(r,f)+\gamma_{M}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-N\left(r,\frac{1}{pM[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)+m\log{r}+S(r,f).

(13)

Note that if
z_{0}
is a zero of
f
with multiplicity
l
(
\geq k+m
), then
z_{0}
is a zero of
pM[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]
with multiplicity at least
\gamma_{M}l-(\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M})-1,
where
\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}=n_{1}+2n_{2}+\cdots+kn_{k}.
Therefore,

N\left(r,\frac{1}{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)\geq\left[\gamma_{M}l-(\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M})-1\right]\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right).

Also, since
pM[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]=p\left(M[f]-p\right)^{\prime}-p^{\prime}\left(M[f]-p\right),
we have

N\left(r,\frac{1}{p~M[f]^{\prime}-p^{\prime}M[f]}\right)\geq N\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p}\right)-\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p}\right).

Thus from (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{M}~T(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\overline{N}(r,f)+(\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}+1)\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p]}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+~m\log{r}+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\overline{N}(r,f)+(\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}+1)\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p]}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+m\log{r}+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\overline{N}(r,f)+\frac{\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}+1}{k+m}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p]}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+m\log{r}+S(r,f).

(14)

Thus

\left[(\gamma_{M}-1)-\frac{\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}+1}{k+m}\right]T(r,f)\leq\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p]}\right)+m\log{r}+S(r,f).

That is,

C~T(r,f)\leq\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p]}\right)+m\log{r}+S(r,f),

(15)

where

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle:=(\gamma_{M}-1)-\frac{\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}+1}{k+m}

\displaystyle=(n_{0}+n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{k}-1)-\frac{(n_{1}+2n_{2}+\cdots+kn_{k})+1}{k+m}

\displaystyle=n_{0}-1+\frac{(k+m-1)n_{1}+(k+m-2)n_{2}+\cdots+(m+1)n_{k-1}+mn_{k}-1}{k+m}

\displaystyle=n_{0}-1+\frac{\alpha+mn_{k}-1}{k+m},

wherein
\alpha:=(k+m-1)n_{1}+(k+m-2)n_{2}+\cdots+(m+1)n_{k-1}\geq 0.

Now suppose that
M[f]-p
has at most one distinct zero. Then we consider the following cases:

Case 1:

\alpha\neq 0.
Then

C\geq 1+\frac{\alpha}{k+m}

and so from (
15
), we obtain

T(r,f)<C~T(r,f)\leq(m+1)\log{r}+S(r,f).

This implies that
f
is a rational function of degree at most
m.
However, zeros of
f
have multiplicities at least
k+m\geq m+1.
Hence
f
must be non-vanishing and so by Lemma
6
,
M[f]-p
has at least
n_{0}+2n_{1}+\cdots+(k+1)n_{k}\geq 4
distinct zeros, a contradiction.

Case 2:

\alpha=0.
Then

C=n_{0}-1+\frac{mn_{k}-1}{k+m}.

If either
n_{k}\geq 2
or
m\geq 2,
then

C\geq 1+\frac{1}{k+m}

and so by the same argument as in Case 1, we get a contradiction. Thus we only need to consider the case when
n_{k}=1
and
m=1.
So, let
n_{k}=m=1.
Then from (
15
), we obtain

(n_{0}-1)T(r,f)\leq\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{M[f]-p]}\right)+\log{r}+S(r,f).

If
M[f](z)-p(z)\neq 0,
then

T(r,f)\leq(n_{0}-1)T(r,f)\leq\log{r}+S(r,f),

showing that
f
is a rational function of degree at most one. Since zeros of
f
have multiplicities at least
k+1\geq 2,
it follows that
f
must be non-vanishing and so by Lemma
6
,
M[f]-p
has at least
n_{0}+2n_{1}+\cdots+(k+1)n_{k}\geq 4
distinct zeros, a contradiction. Thus by the assumption,
M[f]-p
has exactly one zero. Then from (
2
), we have

n_{0}T(r,f)\leq\overline{N}(r,f)+2\log{r}+S(r,f).

(16)

Subcase 2.1:

n_{0}\geq 3.
Then from (
16
), we have

T(r,f)\leq\log{r}+S(r,f),

showing that
f
is a rational function of degree at most one. Again, since zeros of
f
have multiplicities at least
k+1\geq 2,

f
must be non-vanishing and so by Lemma
6
,
M[f]-p
has at least
n_{0}+2n_{1}+\cdots+(k+1)n_{k}\geq 5
distinct zeros, a contradiction.

Subcase 2.2:

n_{0}=2.
Then (
16
) gives

T(r,f)\leq 2\log{r}+S(r,f).

This means that
f
is a rational function of degree at most
2.
If
k\geq 2,
then the fact that
f
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1\geq 3
implies that
f
is non-vanishing and hence by Lemma
6
,
M[f]-p
has at least
n_{0}+2n_{1}+\cdots+(k+1)n_{k}\geq 5
distinct zeros, a contradiction. Hence
k=1.

Now since
f
is a rational function of degree at most
2,
it follows that either
f
is non-vanishing or
f
has only one distinct zero with multiplicity
2.
If
f(z)\neq 0,
then by Lemma
6
,
M[f]-p
has at least
n_{0}+2n_{1}+\cdots+(k+1)n_{k}\geq 4
distinct zeros, a contradiction. Hence
f
has the following forms:

A_{1}:f(z)=a(z-z_{0})^{2},~~A_{2}:f(z)=\frac{a(z-z_{0})^{2}}{z-z_{1}};

A_{3}:f(z)=\frac{a(z-z_{0})^{2}}{(z-z_{1})^{2}},~~A_{4}:f(z)=\frac{a(z-z_{0})^{2}}{(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})},

where

Clearly,

If

is of the form

then
\overline{N}\left(r,f\right)=0
and
\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)\leq 1/2~T(r,f)+O(1).
Also, from (

or

Hence

Then

(17)

Since

where

The following lemma is a generalization of Hayman’s Alternative and Lemma
3
to differential polynomials:

Lemma 8
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
be a transcendental function and
\psi~(\not\equiv 0,\infty)
be a small function of
f
(that is,
T(r,\psi)=S(r,f)
as
r\rightarrow\infty
). Let
P[f]
be a differential polynomial of
f
having differential order
k
and with meromorphic coefficients
b_{j}
as small functions of
f.
Assume that
P[f]
is non-constant. Then the following conclusions hold:

(i)

If
\nu_{P}>1,
then either
f
or
P[f]-\psi
has infinitely many zeros in
\mathbb{C}.
Moreover, if
f
is entire, then we can take
\nu_{P}\geq 1.

(ii)

If
f
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1,
and
(k+2)\nu_{P}>\Gamma_{P}+2,
then
P[f]-\psi
has infinitely many zeros in
\mathbb{C}.
If
f
is entire, then the condition
(k+2)\nu_{P}>\Gamma_{P}+2
can be replaced by the condition
(k+2)\nu_{P}>\Gamma_{P}+1.

The conditions
\nu_{p}>1
in (i) and
(k+2)\nu_{P}>\Gamma_{P}+2
in (ii) are essential. For example, let
f(z)=e^{z}/(1+e^{z}).
Then
f(z)\neq 0,~1.
Let
P[f]:=2f-f^{2}
so that
\nu_{p}=1
and
(k+2)\nu_{P}<\Gamma_{P}+2.
However,
P[f](z)=(2e^{z}+e^{2z})/(1+e^{z})^{2}\neq 1.

Proof of Lemma
8
.

Write
P[f]
in the form

P[f]=H_{\nu_{P}}[f]+H_{\nu_{P}+1}[f]+\cdots+H_{\gamma_{P}}[f],

where
H_{j}[f]
’s are homogeneous differential polynomials of
f
of degree
j.

Since
f\not\equiv 0,
we have

m\left(r,\frac{P[f]}{f^{\gamma_{P}}}\right)=m\left(r,\frac{H_{\nu_{P}}[f]+H_{\nu_{P}+1}[f]+\cdots+H_{\gamma_{P}}[f]}{f^{\gamma_{P}}}\right).

From the Nevanlinna’s theorem on logarithmic derivative, we get

m\left(r,\frac{H_{j}[f]}{f^{j}}\right)=S(r,f)~\forall~j.

Then

\displaystyle m\left(r,\frac{P[f]}{f^{\gamma_{P}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(r,\frac{H_{\nu_{P}}[f]+H_{\nu_{P}+1}[f]+\cdots+H_{\gamma_{P}-1}[f]}{f^{\gamma_{P}-1}}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq 2~m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{H_{\nu_{P}}[f]+H_{\nu_{P}+1}[f]+\cdots+H_{\gamma_{P}-2}[f]}{f^{\gamma_{P}-2}}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq(\gamma_{P}-\nu_{P})m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+S(r,f).

(21)

Now by the first fundamental theorem of Nevanlinna and (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{P}~T(r,f)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{P}~m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\gamma_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(r,\frac{P[f]}{f^{\gamma_{P}}}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)+\gamma_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\log{2}+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq(\gamma_{P}-\nu_{P})~m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)+\gamma_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{P}-\nu_{P})~T(r,f)-(\gamma_{P}-\nu_{P})~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+T(r,P[f])

\displaystyle\qquad-N\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)+\gamma_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+S(r,f).

That is,

\nu_{P}~T(r,f)\leq\nu_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+T(r,P[f])-N\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)+S(r,f).

Applying the second fundamental theorem of Nevanlinna for small functions to
T(r,P[f]),
we get

\displaystyle\nu_{P}~T(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)+\overline{N}(r,P[f])

\displaystyle\qquad+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}(r,f)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)+S(r,f).

(22)

We now prove (i).

First note that if
z_{0}
is a zero of
f
of multiplicity
m~(\geq 1),
then
P[f]
has a zero at
z_{0}
of multiplicity at least
(m+1)\nu_{P}-\Gamma_{P}
and hence

N\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)-\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)\geq\left[(m+1)\nu_{P}-\Gamma_{P}-1\right]\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right).

Then from (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nu_{P}~T(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}(r,f)+\left[1+\Gamma_{P}-(m+1)\nu_{P}\right]\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)+S(r,f).

\displaystyle=\overline{N}(r,f)+\left(1+\Gamma_{P}-\nu_{P}\right)\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)+S(r,f).

Thus

(\nu_{P}-1)T(r,f)\leq\left(1+\Gamma_{P}-\nu_{P}\right)\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)+S(r,f).

This proves (i).

To prove (ii), suppose that
f
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1
and
(k+2)\nu_{P}>\Gamma_{P}+2.
Then one can easily see that

N\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)-\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]}\right)\geq\left[(k+2)\nu_{P}-\Gamma_{P}-1\right]\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right).

Again, from (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nu_{P}~T(r,f)

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{P}~N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\overline{N}(r,f)+\left[1+\Gamma_{P}-(k+2)\nu_{P}\right]\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)+S(r,f).

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{p}~T(r,f)+T(r,f)+\left[1+\Gamma_{P}-(k+2)\nu_{P}\right]T(r,f)

\displaystyle\qquad+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)+S(r,f).

This implies that

\left[(k+2)\nu_{P}-\Gamma_{P}-2\right]~T(r,f)\leq\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)+S(r,f)

and hence

T(r,f)\leq\frac{1}{(k+2)\nu_{P}-\Gamma_{P}-2}\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{P[f]-\psi}\right)+S(r,f).

\phi_{n}(\tilde{\zeta_{n}})=\rho_{n}^{-\alpha}f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\tilde{\zeta_{n}})=0.
This implies that
f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\tilde{\zeta_{n}})=0
and so by hypothesis,
g_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\tilde{\zeta_{n}})=0.
Taking
n\rightarrow\infty,
we get
g(\zeta_{0})=0.
Since
g
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1,
it follows that
P[g](z_{0})=0,
a contradiction. This establishes the claim.

Since
\phi(\zeta)\neq 0,

\phi
is transcendental and so from Lemma
8
(i), it follows that
M_{1}[\phi]
assumes
a
infinitely many times. If
M_{1}[\phi](\zeta_{0})=a,
then as done before, we would get
P[g](\zeta_{0})=a.
Let
l~(\geq 1)
be the multiplicity of zero of
P[g]-a
at
\zeta_{0}.
Since
P[g]\not\equiv a,
by Hurwitz’s theorem, for sufficiently large
n,

P[g_{n}]-a
has exactly
l
zeros in a neighbourhood
U(\zeta_{0})
of
\zeta_{0}.
Since
M_{1}[\phi]
assumes
a
infinitely many times, we assume that
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{l+1}
be the distinct zeros of
M_{1}[\phi]-a.
Again, by Hurwitz’s theorem, for sufficiently large
n,
there exists
\zeta_{j,n}\rightarrow\zeta_{j}
such that
P[\phi_{n}](\zeta_{j,n})=a
for
j=1,\ldots,l+1.
Thus there are
l+1
distinct zeros of
P[\phi_{n}]-a
in
U(\zeta_{0})
and hence
P[g_{n}]-a
has
l+1
distinct zeros in
U(\zeta_{0}),
a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Assume that
\mathcal{F}
is not normal at
z_{0}\in D.
Then by Lemma
1
, there exist a sequence
\left\{f_{n}\right\}\subset\mathcal{F},
a sequence of points
\left\{z_{n}\right\}\subset D
with
z_{n}\rightarrow z_{0}
and a sequence of positive real numbers satisfying
\rho_{n}\rightarrow 0
such that the sequence

\phi_{n}(\zeta):=\frac{f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta)}{\rho_{n}^{\alpha}}\rightarrow\phi(\zeta),~\alpha:=\Theta_{P}-1,

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C},
where
\phi
is a non-constant meromorphic function of finite order with zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1.
Moreover, the corresponding sequence
\left\{g_{n}\right\}\subset\mathcal{G}
converges locally uniformly to
g
in
D,
where
g~(\not\equiv\infty)
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1.
Then by a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
5
, we find that on every compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
not containing poles of
\phi,

\tilde{P}[\phi_{n}](\zeta):=P[f_{n}](z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta)\rightarrow a_{1}(z_{0})M_{1}[\phi](\zeta)~\mbox{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Also, for every
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{g^{-1}(\infty)\right\},
we have
P[g_{n}](\zeta)\rightarrow P[g](\zeta)~\mbox{as }n\rightarrow\infty
locally uniformly. Since
M_{1}[\phi]=\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k}\left(\phi^{(j)}\right)^{n_{j1}},
we shall denote, for the sake of convenience,
M_{1}[\phi]
by
M[\phi]
and the powers
n_{j1}
by
n_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,k.
Also, we may assume that
a_{1}(z_{0})=1.
Now, we consider the following cases:

Case 1:

n_{0}=n_{1}=\cdots=n_{k-1}=0.
In this case, we have
M[\phi]=\left(\phi^{(k)}\right)^{n_{k}},
where
n_{k}\geq 1.

Claim 1:

\phi(\zeta)\neq 0.

For, let
\zeta_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
\phi(\zeta_{0})=0.
Since
\phi\not\equiv 0,
by Hurwitz’s theorem, there exists
\zeta_{n}\rightarrow\zeta_{0}
such that for sufficiently large
n,

\phi_{n}(\zeta_{n})=\rho_{n}^{-\alpha}f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n})=0.
This implies that
f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n})=0
and so by hypothesis,
g_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n})=0.
Taking
n\rightarrow\infty,
we get
g(z_{0})=0.
Since
g
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+1,
it follows that
P[g](z_{0})=0.
If
\phi
is transcendental, then by Lemma
3
,
M[\phi]
assumes the non-zero complex number
a.
Let
\zeta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
M[\phi](\zeta^{\prime})=a.
Since
M[\phi]\not\equiv a,
by Hurwitz’s theorem, there exists a sequence
\zeta_{n}^{\prime}\rightarrow\zeta^{\prime}
such that for sufficiently large
n,

P[f_{n}](z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n}^{\prime})=a
and hence by hypothesis, we get
P[g_{n}](z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n}^{\prime})=a.
Taking
n\rightarrow\infty,
we get
P[g](z_{0})=a\neq 0,
a contradiction. Hence
\phi
must be rational. Furthermore, using the preceding argument, one can easily see that
\phi
is not a polynomial and therefore there exists
\zeta^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\phi(\zeta^{*})=\infty.
Again, by Hurwitz’s theorem, there exists a sequence
\zeta_{n}^{*}\rightarrow\zeta^{*}
such that for sufficiently large
n,
we have
f_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n}^{*})=\infty
and hence
g_{n}(z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n}^{*})=\infty.
Taking
n\rightarrow\infty,
we get
g(z_{0})=\infty,
which is again a contradiction. Hence
\phi(\zeta)\neq 0.

Claim 2:

\phi
has no poles.

Suppose there is some
\zeta_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\phi(\zeta_{1})=\infty.
Then as done before, we get
g(z_{0})=\infty.
Since
\phi(\zeta)\neq 0,
by Lemma
2
, there is some
\zeta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
M[\phi](\zeta_{2})=a.
Again, by Hurwitz’s theorem, there exists a sequence
\zeta_{n,2}\rightarrow\zeta_{2}
such that for sufficiently large
n,
we have
P[f_{n}](z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n,2})=a.
This implies that
P[g_{n}](z_{n}+\rho_{n}\zeta_{n,2})=a
and hence
P[g](z_{0})=a
which is not possible since
P[g]\not\equiv a
and
g(z_{0})=\infty.
This establishes Claim 2.

Now, Claim 1 and Claim 2 together imply that
\phi
is a non-vanishing transcendental entire function and so from Lemma
2
, it follows that
M[\phi]
assumes
a
infinitely many times. Now, proceeding similarly as in the proof of Theorem
5
, we get a contradiction.

Case 2:

n_{0}\geq 1,~n_{k}\geq 1.
Then
\gamma_{M}\geq 2
and a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle(k+2)\gamma_{M}-\Gamma_{M}-2

\displaystyle=(k+2)(n_{0}+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k})-[n_{0}+2n_{1}+\cdots+(k+1)n_{k}]-2

\displaystyle=(k+2-1)n_{0}+(k+2-2)n_{1}+\cdots+[k+2-(k+1)]n_{k}-2

\displaystyle\geq kn_{0}+(k+2-2)n_{1}+\cdots+[(k+2)-(k-1)]n_{k-1}

\displaystyle\geq 1.

Now using the same arguments as that of Case 1 and Lemma
8
(ii), we obtain
\phi(\zeta)\neq 0
and hence
\phi
is not a polynomial. If
\phi
is transcendental or rational, then we use Lemma
8
(i), and the arguments from Claim 2 to conclude that
\phi
is a non-vanishing transcendental entire function. Thus from Lemma
8
(i), it follows that
M[\phi]
assumes
a
infinitely many times. Again, proceeding similarly as in the proof of Theorem
5
, we get a contradiction. Hence
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

∎

Proof of Theorem
8
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is not normal at
z_{0}\in D.
Then we consider the following two cases:

Case 1:

h(z_{0})\neq 0.
By Lemma
1
, there exist a sequence
\left\{f_{j}\right\}\subset\mathcal{F},
a sequence of points
\left\{z_{j}\right\}\subset D
with
z_{j}\rightarrow z_{0}
and a sequence of positive real numbers satisfying
\rho_{j}\rightarrow 0
such that the sequence

g_{j}(\xi):=\frac{f_{j}(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi)}{\rho_{j}^{\alpha}}\rightarrow g(\xi),~\alpha:=\frac{\Gamma_{M}}{\gamma_{M}}-1,

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
with respect to the spherical metric, where
g\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
is a non-constant function of order at most
2.
Since each
f_{j}
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+m+1,
by Argument principle, it follows that
g
has zeros of multiplicity at least
k+m+1.
Furthermore, a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle M[g_{j}](\xi)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{k}\left(\left(\frac{f_{j}(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi}{\rho_{j}^{\alpha}}\right)^{(i)}\right)^{n_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{j}^{n_{1}+2n_{2}+\cdots+kn_{k}}}{\rho_{j}^{\alpha(n_{0}+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k})}}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{k}\left(\left(f_{j}(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi)\right)^{(i)}\right)^{n_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{j}^{\Gamma_{M}-\gamma_{M}}}{\rho_{j}^{\alpha\gamma_{M}}}M[f_{j}](z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi)

\displaystyle=M[f_{j}](z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi).

Hence on every compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
not containing poles of
g,
we have

M[g_{j}](\xi)=M[f_{j}](z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi)\rightarrow M[g](\xi).

Obviously,
M[g](\xi)\not\equiv h(z_{0}).
Also, one can easily check that
(k+2)\gamma_{M}-\Gamma_{M}-2>0.
Thus by Lemma
5
and Lemma
8
(ii),
M[g](\xi)-h(z_{0})
has at least two distinct zeros, say
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}.
Choose
\delta>0
small enough such that
D(\xi_{1},\delta)\cap D(\xi_{2},\delta)=\phi
and for any
\xi\neq\xi_{1},~\xi_{2}
in
D(\xi_{1},\delta)\cup D(\xi_{2},\delta),

M[g](\xi)-h(z_{0})\neq 0.
By Hurwitz’s theorem, there exist sequences
\xi_{j,1}\rightarrow\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{j,2}\rightarrow\xi_{2}
such that for sufficiently large
j,

M[f_{j}](z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi_{j,l})-h(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi_{l})=0,~l=1,~2.

By hypothesis, it follows that for any fixed integer
p
and for all
j,

M[f_{p}](z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi_{j,l})-h(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi_{l})=0,~l=1,~2.

Taking
j\rightarrow\infty,
we get

M[f_{p}](z_{0})-h(z_{0})=0.

Since the zeros of
M[f_{p}]-h(z_{0})
have no accumulation point, for sufficiently large
j,
we obtain
z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi_{j,1}=0=z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi_{j,2}.
This implies that
\xi_{j,1}=-z_{j}/\rho_{j}=\xi_{j,2}
showing that
D(\xi_{1},\delta)\cap D(\xi_{2},\delta)\neq\phi,
a contradiction.

Case 2:

h(z_{0})=0.
Then
h(z)=z^{t}h_{1}(z),
where
h_{1}
is holomorphic in
D
such that
h_{1}(z_{0})\neq 0
and
1\leq t\leq m.
We may assume that
h_{1}(z_{0})=1.
Again, by Lemma
1
, there exist a sequence
\left\{f_{j}\right\}\subset\mathcal{F},
a sequence of points
\left\{z_{j}\right\}\subset D
with
z_{j}\rightarrow z_{0}
and a sequence of positive real numbers satisfying
\rho_{j}\rightarrow 0
such that the sequence

g_{j}(\xi):=\frac{f_{j}(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi)}{\rho_{j}^{\beta}}\rightarrow g(\xi),~\beta:=\frac{\Gamma_{M}+t}{\gamma_{M}}-1,

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
with respect to the spherical metric, where
g\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
is non-constant. We consider the following subcases:

Subcase 2.1:
Suppose that there exists a subsequence of
z_{j}/\rho_{j}
(which, for the sake of convenience, is again denoted by
z_{j}/\rho_{j}
) such that
z_{j}/\rho_{j}\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty.

Consider the family

V_{j}(\xi):=\frac{f_{j}(z_{j}+z_{j}\xi)}{z_{j}^{\beta}}.

By simple calculation, one can see that
M[f_{j}](z_{j}+z_{j}\xi)=z_{j}^{t}M[V_{j}](\xi).
By hypothesis, for any distinct integers
r,~s,
we have,

M[f_{r}](z)-h(z)=0\Leftrightarrow~M[f_{s}](z)-h(z)=0.

Choose
\xi,
say
\xi_{i}
such that for some
j,
say
j_{i},

z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i}
is a zero of
M[f_{r}](z)-h(z)
and hence of
M[f_{s}](z)-h(z).
Then

\displaystyle M[f_{r}](z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i})=h(z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i})\Leftrightarrow~M[f_{s}](z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i})=h(z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow M[V_{r}](\xi_{i})=(1+\xi_{i})^{t}h_{1}(z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i})\Leftrightarrow~M[V_{s}](\xi_{i})=(1+\xi_{i})^{t}h_{1}(z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i}).

Since
(1+\xi_{i})^{t}h_{1}(z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i})\rightarrow(1+\xi_{i})^{t}\neq 0
in a sufficiently small neighbourhood
N_{0}
of the origin, it follows that
(1+\xi_{i})^{t}h_{1}(z_{j_{i}}+z_{j_{i}}\xi_{i})\neq 0
in
N_{0}.
Thus by Case 1,
\left\{V_{j}\right\}
is normal in
N_{0}
and so there exists a subsequence of
\left\{V_{j}\right\}
(again denoted by
\left\{V_{j}\right\}
) such that
V_{j}
converges spherically locally uniformly in
N_{0}
to a meromorphic function
V.

Subcase 2.1.1:

V(0)\neq 0.
Then for any
\xi\in\mathbb{C},

g_{j}(\xi)=\rho_{j}^{-\beta}f_{j}(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi)=\left(\frac{z_{j}}{\rho_{j}}\right)^{\beta}V_{j}\left(\frac{\rho_{j}}{z_{j}}\xi\right)

converges spherically locally uniformly to
\infty,
showing that
g\equiv\infty,
a contradiction.

Subcase 2.1.2:

V(0)=0.
Then
V^{\prime}(0)\neq\infty
and so

g_{j}^{\prime}(\xi)=\rho_{j}^{-\beta+1}f_{j}^{\prime}(z_{j}+\rho_{j}\xi)=\left(\frac{\rho_{j}}{z_{j}}\right)^{-\beta+1}V_{j}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\rho_{j}}{z_{j}}\xi\right)

converges spherically locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
to
0.
This shows that
g^{\prime}\equiv 0,
a contradiction to the fact that
g
is non-constant.

Subcase 2.2:
Suppose there exists a subsequence of
z_{j}/\rho_{j}
(which, for the sake of convenience, is again denoted by
z_{j}/\rho_{j}
) such that
z_{j}/\rho_{j}\rightarrow\zeta
as
j\rightarrow\infty,
where
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}.

Consider the family
W_{j}(\xi):=\rho_{j}^{-\beta}f_{j}(\rho_{j}\xi).
Then

W_{j}(\xi)=g_{j}\left(\xi-\frac{z_{j}}{\rho_{j}}\right)\rightarrow g(\xi-\zeta):=W(\xi).

It easily follows that on any compact subset of
\mathbb{C},
containing no poles of
W,

M[W_{j}](\xi)-\xi^{t}\rightarrow M[W](\xi)-\xi^{t}.

Clearly,
M[W](\xi)\not\equiv\xi^{t}.
Thus by Lemma
7
,
M[W](\xi)-\xi^{t}
has at least two distinct zeros. Proceeding similarly as in Case 1, we get a contradiction. Hence
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

∎
