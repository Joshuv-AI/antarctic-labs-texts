Maker-Breaker domination game on trees when Staller wins
Csilla Bujtás, Pakanun Dokyeesun, Sandi Klavžar

Abstract
In the Maker-Breaker domination game played on a graph G G , Dominator’s goal is to select a dominating set and Staller’s goal is to claim a closed neighborhood of some vertex. We study the cases when Staller can win the game. If Dominator (resp., Staller) starts the game, then γ SMB ​ ( G ) \gamma_{\rm SMB}(G) (resp., γ SMB ′ ​ ( G ) \gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G) ) denotes the minimum number of moves Staller needs to win. For every positive integer k k , trees T T with γ SMB ′ ​ ( T ) = k \gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k are characterized and a general upper bound on γ SMB ′ \gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime} is proved. Let S = S ⁡ ( n 1 , … , n ℓ ) S=S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}) be the subdivided star obtained from the star with ℓ \ell edges by subdividing its edges n 1 − 1 , … , n ℓ − 1 n_{1}-1,\ldots,n_{\ell}-1 times, respectively. Then γ SMB ′ ​ ( S ) \gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S) is determined in all the cases except when ℓ ≥ 4 \ell\geq 4 and each n i n_{i} is even. The simplest formula is obtained when there are at least two odd n i n_{i} s. If n 1 n_{1} and n 2 n_{2} are the two smallest such numbers, then γ SMB ′ ​ ( S ⁡ ( n 1 , … , n ℓ ) ) = ⌈ log 2 ⁡ ( n 1 + n 2 + 1 ) ⌉ \gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}))=\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil . For caterpillars, exact formulas for γ SMB \gamma_{\rm SMB} and for γ SMB ′ \gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime} are established.

1
Introduction

The Maker-Breaker game was introduced in
Erdős and Selfridge 1973
. The game is played on an arbitrary hypergraph by two players who alternately select a non-played vertex of the hypergraph. One player, named Maker, wants to occupy all the vertices of some hyperedge, while the other player, named Breaker, tries to prevent him from doing it. If the first situation happens, then Maker is declared as the winner of the game, otherwise Breaker wins. The game, either in its general form, or in different special cases, was investigated a lot by now, see the book
Hefetz et al. 2014
. For related recent developments see
Clemens et al. 2021
;
Day and Falgas-Ravry 2021
;
Nicholas Day and Falgas-Ravry 2021
;
Glazik and Srivastav 2022
;
Kang et al. 2021
;
Stojaković and Trkulja 2021
and references therein. The Maker-Breaker games have been recently studied also on digraphs in
Frieze and Pegden 2021
.

The Maker-Breaker domination game,
MBD game
for short, was introduced by Duchêne, Gledel, Parreau, and Renault in
Duchêne et al. 2020
. Among other results they proved that deciding the winner of the MBD game is PSPACE-complete in general, and showed that the problem can be solved efficiently on trees. Following the tradition of the theory of the domination game, the two players in this version of the Maker-Breaker game are called Dominator and Staller. This game was introduced in
Brešar et al. 2010
, its state of the art till 2021 summarized in
Brešar et al. 2021
, and is still being investigated, see e.g.
Bujtás et al. 2022
. We also add that the Maker-Breaker total domination game was introduced in
Gledel et al. 2020
and further investigated in
Forcan and Mikalački 2022
.

The MBD game played on a graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
can be considered as the Maker-Breaker game played on the hypergraph
{\cal D}_{G}
whose hyperedges are the minimal dominating sets of
G
. In this case, Dominator is Maker, and Staller is Breaker. Moreover, this game can also be considered as the Maker-Breaker game played on the closed neighborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
of
G
, where the hyperedges are the closed neighborhoods of the vertices of
G
. Now Dominator is Breaker and Staller is Maker.

A MBD game is called
D-game
(resp.,
S-game
) if Dominator (resp., Staller) is the first to play a vertex. Suppose that Dominator has a winning strategy in the D-game. Then the
Maker-Breaker domination number
,
\gamma_{\rm MB}(G)
, of
G
is the (minimum) number of moves of Dominator to win the game when both players play optimally. The corresponding invariant for the S-game is denoted by
\gamma_{\rm MB}^{\prime}(G)
. These concepts were introduced in
Gledel et al. 2019
and further studied on prisms in
Forcan and Qi 2023
. Clearly, the problem of determining the Maker-Breaker domination number is interesting when Dominator wins the game. For the situations in which Staller is the winner, the
Staller-Maker-Breaker domination number
(
SMBD-number
for short),
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
, of
G
, is the (minimum) number of Staller’s moves she needs to win the D-game if both players play optimally. If Staller has no winning strategy in the D-game, we set
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)=\infty
. For the S-game, the corresponding invariant is defined analogously and denoted by
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
. These two invariants are from
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
.

In this paper we proceed the investigation of the SMBD-numbers. In
Gledel et al. 2019
, exact formulas for
\gamma_{\rm MB}(T)
and
\gamma_{\rm MB}^{\prime}(T)
were proved if
T
is a tree. To determine
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
turns out to be much more involved. In the main result of Section
3
we characterize, for every positive integer
k
, the class of trees
T
with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
. In the subsequent section we study subdivided stars. Since
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)=\infty
holds for each subdivided star
T
, we focus on
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
and determine the exact value in all the cases except when the star has at least four edges, and each edge is subdivided an odd number of times. For the latter case we prove a sharp upper bound on
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}
. In Section
5
we determine
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
for an arbitrary caterpillar
T
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Definitions

In a simple graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
, the
open neighborhood

N_{G}(v)
of a vertex
v\in V(G)
is the set of vertices being adjacent to
v
, while
N_{G}[v]=N_{G}(v)\cup\{v\}
is the
closed neighborhood
of
v
. If
X\subseteq V(G)
, then
N_{G}[X]=\bigcup_{x\in X}N_{G}[x]
. The
degree
of
v
is
\deg_{G}(v)=|N_{G}(v)|
. A
leaf
is a vertex
v
with
\deg_{G}(v)=1
. A vertex
u\in V(G)
is a
support vertex
, if
N_{G}(u)
contains a leaf. Moreover,
u
is a
strong support vertex
if it is adjacent to more than one leaf. Otherwise, it is a
weak support vertex
.

A set
S\subseteq V(G)
is a
dominating set
in
G
if
N_{G}[S]=V(G)
.
A dominating set
S
is
minimal
if there is no dominating set among the proper subsets of
S
.

The
residual graph
R(G)
of a graph
G
is obtained from
G
by iteratively removing a pendant path
P_{2}
until no such path is present. By a pendant
P_{2}
we mean a
P_{2}
-component or a
P_{2}
attached to the graph by an edge. In the latter case, when a pendant
P_{2}
is removed, exactly two vertices and two edges are deleted, whilst in the former case we obtain the empty graph.
If
T
is a tree, then
R(T)
is either the empty graph, or

R(T)=K_{1}
, or each support vertex of
R(T)
is of degree at least
3
. It was proved in
Duchêne et al. 2020
that the winner is the same in
T
and in
R(T)
.

A
hypergraph

{\cal H}=(V({\cal H}),E({\cal H}))
is a set system over the vertex set
V({\cal H})
. The (hyper)edge set
E({\cal H})
might contain subsets of
V({\cal H})
of any cardinality that is,
E({\cal H})\subseteq 2^{V({\cal H})}
. If
|e|=2
holds for every hyperedge
e\in E({\cal H})
, the hypergraph
{\cal H}
corresponds to a graph. Thus, most of the basic definitions related to graphs can be generalized to hypergraphs (see
Berge 1989
). For example, if
{\cal H}_{1}
and
{\cal H}_{2}
are two hypergraphs, we say that
{\cal H}_{1}
is a
subhypergraph
of
{\cal H}_{2}
, if
V({\cal H}_{1})\subseteq V({\cal H}_{2})
and
E({\cal H}_{1})\subseteq E({\cal H}_{2})
.

While studying Maker-Breaker games, we will refer to two operators on hypergraphs. Given a subset
X
of the vertex set
V({\cal H})
, the hypergraph
{\cal H}-X
is obtained from
{\cal H}
by removing the vertices in
X
and all incident edges. That is,

V({\cal H}-X)=V({\cal H})\setminus X
and

E({\cal H}-X)=\{e:e\in E({\cal H})\enskip{\rm and}\enskip e\subseteq V({\cal H})\setminus X\}.

The second operator, named
shrinking
, creates a hypergraph denoted by
{\cal H}\mid X
. Here, the vertices in
X
are deleted again from
{\cal H}
but the incident hyperedges are just ‘shrinked’ instead of being deleted. Formally,
V({\cal H}\mid X)=V({\cal H})\setminus X
, and

E({\cal H}\mid X)=\{e\setminus X:e\in E({\cal H})\}.

If
X=\{v\}
, we may write
{\cal H}-v
and
{\cal H}\mid v
instead of
{\cal H}-\{v\}
and
{\cal H}\mid\{v\}
, respectively.

When a Maker-Breaker game is played on a hypergraph
{\cal H}
, we say that the hyperedges of
{\cal H}
are the
winning sets
and Maker wins the game with his
i^{\rm th}
move if he claims a winning set with this move. A
winning strategy
of Maker is a strategy which ensures that he wins on
{\cal H}
no matter what strategy is applied by Breaker. Assuming that Maker has a winning strategy, we further assume that his goal is to win the game as soon as possible and that Breaker’s goal is the opposite. We say that the players play
optimally
, if they play complying with their goals. The
winning number of Maker
, denoted by
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})
(resp.,
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})
), is the minimum number of his moves he needs to win the game if both players play optimally and Maker (resp., Breaker) starts the game
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
. If Maker has no winning strategy as a first (resp., second) player, we set
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})=\infty
(resp.,
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})=\infty
). These general winning numbers were introduced in
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
, but the problem of how fast Maker can win was studied earlier in several papers including
Beck 1981
;
Clemens et al. 2012
;
Clemens and Mikalački 2018
;
Hefetz et al. 2009
. Note that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)={\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{G})
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{G})
.

2.2
Known results

In this subsection we collect known results that will be used later.

Proposition 2.1

(
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
)
.

Suppose that a Maker-Breaker game is played on a hypergraph
{\cal H}
and
v\in V({\cal H})
.

(i)

If Breaker is the first player and she plays
v
, the continuation of the game corresponds to a Maker-start game on
{\cal H}-v
. In particular, if
v
is an optimal first move of Breaker, then
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}-v)
.

(ii)

If Maker is the first player and he plays
v
, the continuation of the game corresponds to a Breaker-start game on
{\cal H}\mid v
. In particular, if
v
is an optimal first move of Maker, then
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}\mid v)+1
. Further, Maker wins the game with this move
v
if and only if
{\cal H}\mid v
contains an empty hyperedge but
{\cal H}
does not.

Proposition 2.2

(
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
)
.

Let
{\cal H}_{1}
and
{\cal H}_{2}
be two hypergraphs on the same vertex set.

(i)

If
E({\cal H}_{1})\subseteq E({\cal H}_{2})
, then
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})
and
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{2})
holds.

(ii)

Suppose that for each
e\in E({\cal H}_{1})
there exists an edge
e^{\prime}\in E({\cal H}_{2})
such that
e^{\prime}\subseteq e
. Then,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})
and
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{2})
holds.

Proposition 2.3

(
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
)
.

Let
{\cal H}
be a disconnected hypergraph that consists of components
{\cal H}_{1},\dots,{\cal H}_{\ell}
such that
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\leq\dots\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{\ell})
. Then, the following holds:

{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2}).

We remark that Proposition
2.3
and its proof in
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
directly imply that one of the optimal strategies of Maker is to play (optimally) on the component
{\cal H}_{1}
.

Proposition 2.4

(
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
)
.

If a disconnected graph
G
consists of components
G_{1},\dots,G_{\ell}
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{1})\leq\dots\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{\ell})
, then the following statements hold:

(i)

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)=\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{1})
;

(ii)

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{1})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{2})
.

Proposition 2.5

(
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
)
.

(i)

Let
G^{\prime}
be a graph obtained from
G
by removing a weak support vertex and the adjacent leaf. Then, the following inequalities hold:

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)-1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G).

(ii)

Let
G^{\prime}
be a graph obtained from
G
by removing a weak support vertex of degree
2
and the adjacent leaf.
Then,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)-1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})
holds.

(iii)

Let
v
be a cut vertex in a connected graph
G
. If
G_{1},\dots,G_{\ell}
are the components of
G-v
indexed so that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{1})\leq\dots\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{\ell})
, then

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{2})+1.

The proof of
(
Duchêne et al. 2020
, Theorem 22)
yields the following statement that can also be deduced from
(
Gledel et al. 2019
, Theorem 4.5)
.

Theorem 2.6

(
Duchêne et al. 2020
;
Gledel et al. 2019
)
.

For a tree
T
,

(i)

\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)<\infty
if and only if
R(T)
contains at least two strong support vertices;

(ii)

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)<\infty
if and only if
T
does not admit a perfect matching.

Note that, by Theorem
2.6
(ii),
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\infty
if
n
is even.

Applying the “pairing strategy” from
Hefetz et al. 2014
, Dominator has a winning strategy in both the D-game and S-game if the graph has a perfect matching. This fact can be extended as follows.

Lemma 2.7

(
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
)
.

Consider an MBD game on
G
and let
X
and
Y
be the sets of vertices played by Dominator and Staller, respectively, until a moment during the game. If there exists a matching
M
in
G-(X\cup Y)
such that
V(G)\setminus V(M)\subseteq N_{G}[X]
,
then Dominator has a strategy to win the continuation of the game, no matter who plays the next vertex.

Theorem 2.8

(
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
)
.

If
n
is an odd positive integer, then

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor+1.

Moreover, Staller has an optimal strategy in the S-game on
P_{n}
such that she wins on the closed neighborhood of a vertex
v
that is at an even distance from the ends of the path.

If
n\geq 3
and
n
is odd, we may use the formula
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
instead of
\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor+1
. In later sections we also frequently write
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
instead of
\left\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1
if
n\geq 2
is even.

3
Characterizing trees with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}=k

In
Gledel et al. 2019
, exact formulas for
\gamma_{\rm MB}(T)
and
\gamma_{\rm MB}^{\prime}(T)
were proved if
T
is a tree. These values and the corresponding optimal strategies of Dominator are not difficult to determine by iteratively removing a pendant path
P_{2}
and constructing the residual graph
R(T)
. On the other hand, as Theorem
2.8
and its proof given in
Bujtás and Dokyeesun 2022
indicate, determining
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
for an arbitrary tree
T
turns out to be a much more difficult problem.

Thus, the aim of this section is, for a given integer
k
, to characterize the trees
T
with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
. Combining this result with properties of closed neighborhood hypergraphs, we also establish a sufficient condition under which
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)\leq k
holds for an arbitrary graph
G
.

First, we state a lemma that will be used in the proof of the main theorem.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
T
be a tree with
2\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)<\infty
and suppose that
s_{1}
is an optimal first move of Staller in the S-game on
T
.

(i)

If Dominator’s response is playing a vertex
d_{1}
which is a neighbor of
s_{1}
, then Staller’s optimal next move
s_{2}
and
d_{1}
belong to different components of
T-s_{1}
.

(ii)

After Staller’s optimal move
s_{1}
, there exists an optimal response
d_{1}^{\prime}
of Dominator such that
d_{1}^{\prime}\in N_{T}(s_{1})
.

Proof.

Let
T_{1},\dots,T_{\ell}
be the components of
T-s_{1}
.

(i)
Suppose for a contradiction that
d_{1}
and
s_{2}
belong to the same component, say
T_{1}
. Proposition
2.1
implies that, after the moves
s_{1}
and
d_{1}
, the game continues as a Maker-start game with the winning sets in
{\cal H}^{\prime}=({\cal H}_{T}\!\mid\!s_{1})-d_{1}
. As
s_{1}
is an optimal start vertex for Staller,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\geq 1+{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})
. Further, since by our assumption,
s_{1}
and
d_{1}
are adjacent vertices in
T
, the hypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}
contains the components
{\cal H}_{T_{2}},\dots,{\cal H}_{T_{\ell}}
and all components of
{\cal H}_{1}={\cal H}_{T_{1}}-d_{1}
. By Proposition
2.3
,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})
equals the minimum of the values
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1}),{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T_{2}}),\dots,{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T_{\ell}})
, and one the optimal strategies of Staller (Maker) is to play in the same (appropriate) component of
{\cal H}^{\prime}
starting with her move
s_{2}
. As
s_{2}\in V({\cal H}_{1})
is supposed to be an optimal move,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})
. On the other hand,
{\cal H}_{1}
is a subhypergraph of
{\cal H}_{T}
and Proposition
2.2
thus implies
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T})
. This gives the following contradiction:

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T})\geq 1+{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})=1+{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\geq 1+\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T).

(ii)
Suppose
d_{1}
is an optimal response of Dominator to the move
s_{1}
and
d_{1}\notin N_{T}(s_{1})
. We may also suppose, without loss of generality, that
d_{1}\in V(T_{1})
. Let
d_{1}^{\prime}
be the neighbor of
s_{1}
from the subtree
T_{1}
.
We will show, by using imagination strategy, that
d^{\prime}_{1}
is also an optimal response of Dominator.
Let Game 1 be an S-game on
T
and let Staller start by playing
s_{1}
and Dominator’s response is
d_{1}^{\prime}
. After that, Staller plays optimally as follows:

(*)

Staller selects a component of
({\cal H}_{T}|s_{1})-d_{1}^{\prime}
with the possible smallest winning number and plays optimally all of her moves in this component.

Note that by Proposition
2.3
the strategy (*) is indeed an optimal one for Staller from the second move.

Let Game 2 be an S-game on
T
in which Dominator plays optimally and his response to the move
s_{1}
is
d_{1}
. In the continuation, Staller always selects an optimal move
s_{i}
in Game 1 and copies her move to Game 2 if possible, while Dominator selects an optimal move
d_{i}
in Game 2 and copies it to Game 1 if possible.
Let
t_{k}
be the number of moves Staller needs to win in Game
k
for
k\in[2]
.

By
(i)
, the optimal second move
s_{2}
of Staller in Game 1 cannot belong to
T_{1}
. Let us assume
s_{2}\in V(T_{j})
with
j\neq 1
. Then according to (*), all the remaining moves of Staller will be in
T_{j}
. In particular, Staller never plays the vertex
d_{1}
. On the other hand, if Dominator plays
d_{i}=d_{1}^{\prime}
in Game 2, then we replace this move by
d_{1}
in Game 1. Otherwise Dominator’s move can always be copied into Game 1.

At the end, Staller wins Game 1 with her
(t_{1})^{\rm st}
move by claiming a winning set from
{\cal H}_{T_{j}}
.
As this winning set is also claimed in Game 2 (or Staller has already won in Game 2), Staller wins Game 2 in
t_{2}\leq t_{1}
moves.
Since Staller plays optimally throughout Game 1, we infer
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\geq t_{1}
.
Similarly,
t_{2}\geq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
holds because Dominator plays optimally in Game 2.
Therefore,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\geq t_{1}\geq t_{2}\geq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
that implies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=t_{1}
.
We may conclude that
d^{\prime}_{1}\in N_{T}(s_{1})
is also an optimal move for Dominator after
s_{1}
.

.

•

•

Observation 3.3

Let

(iii)

If a tree

is a leaf in

.

(iv)

|V(S)|

(v)

Let

\deg_{S}(v)

.

In the following proposition, we state a simple characterization for the family

. For it recall that the

,

, of a graph

Proposition 3.4
.

{\cal S}=\{S(G):\ G\ \mbox{is\ a\ tree}\}
.

Proof.

Set
{\cal S}^{\prime}=\{S(G):\ G\ \mbox{is\ a\ tree}\}
. We are going to prove that
{\cal S}={\cal S}^{\prime}
.

Clearly, the elements of
{\cal S}^{\prime}
are trees. Moreover, if
T
is an arbitrary tree with partite classes
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
such that
A_{1}
contains at least one leaf or an isolated vertex, then it is straightforward to observe that
T\in{\cal S}^{\prime}
if and only if

(\ast)

\deg_{T}(v)=2
holds for every
v\in A_{2}
.

If
T\in{\cal S}
, Observation
3.3
(i) and (ii) yield the desired property
(\ast)
with
A_{1}=X(T)
and
A_{2}=V(T)\setminus X(T)
. Hence
{\cal S}\subseteq{\cal S}^{\prime}
.

To prove that
{\cal S}^{\prime}\subseteq{\cal S}
also holds, suppose that
T\in{\cal S}^{\prime}
. Then
T
fulfils
(\ast)
. We proceed by induction to conclude
T\in{\cal S}
. This clearly holds if the order of
T
is at most
3
. Let
|V(T)|>3
. Then,
A_{2}\neq\emptyset
, and we fix an arbitrarily chosen vertex
z
from
A_{2}
. By our condition,
\deg_{T}(z)=2
and hence
T-z
consists of two components, say
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. Observe that
\deg_{T_{1}}(v)=\deg_{T}(v)
holds for every
v\in V(T_{1})
except for the neighbor of
z
from
T_{1}
. Since
z\in A_{2}
, this neighbor is from
A_{1}
, and therefore, we infer that
\deg_{T_{1}}(v)=\deg_{T}(v)=2
remains true for all vertices
v
contained in the partite class
V(T_{1})\cap A_{2}
of
T_{1}
. Applying the induction hypothesis to
T_{1}
, we conclude
T_{1}\in{\cal S}
. In the same way, we may obtain
T_{2}\in{\cal S}
. Definition
3.2
then shows that
T
can be constructed from
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, and the origin vertex
z
, and therefore
T\in{\cal S}_{k}
for an appropriate integer
k
. ∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
T
be a tree and
k
a positive integer. Then,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
if and only if
T
contains a substructure
S
from
{\cal S}_{k}
, and no substructure from
\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{i}
.

Proof.

The statement holds for
k=1
as Staller can win the S-game with her first move if and only if there exists a winning set
N_{T}[v]
of cardinality one. It clearly means that
T
is just an isolated vertex
P_{1}
or, equivalently, there is a substructure
P_{1}\in{\cal S}_{1}
in
T
. From now on, we may proceed by induction on
k
.

First, assume that
T
contains a substructure
S
from
{\cal S}_{k}
, where
k\geq 2
, but contains no substructure from
{\cal S}_{j}
if
j<k
. The latter condition implies, by the induction hypothesis, that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\geq k
. Now consider the substructure
S\in{\cal S}_{k}
in
T
and its origin vertex
z_{k}
. By the construction of the graphs belonging to
{\cal S}_{k}
, the forest
T-z_{k}
contains one component
T_{1}
with a substructure
S^{1}\in{\cal S}_{k-1}
and another component, say
T_{2}
, with a substructure
S^{2}\in\cup_{p=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{p}
. Then,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{2})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{1})\leq k-1
and Proposition
2.5
(iii) implies

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{1})+1\leq k.

Therefore,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
follows that completes the proof of one direction of the statement.

Now, assume that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
. By the hypothesis,
T
contains no substructure from
\cup_{p=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{p}
. Let
s_{1}
be an optimal first move of Staller in the S-game and
d_{1}
an optimal response of Dominator. By Lemma
3.1
(ii), we may suppose that
d_{1}
is a neighbor of
s_{1}
. Let
T_{1},\dots,T_{\ell}
be the components of
T-s_{1}
such that
d_{1}\in V(T_{1})
. After the move
d_{1}
, by Proposition
2.1
, the game continues on
{\cal H}^{\prime}=({\cal H}_{T}\mid s_{1})-d_{1}
with Staller’s next optimal move
s_{2}
. By Lemma
3.1
(i),
s_{2}\notin V(T_{1})
. By our supposition, the moves
s_{1}
and
d_{1}
were optimal and therefore,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})=k-1
.

Observe that
{\cal H}^{\prime}
consists of several components and
\ell-1
of them exactly correspond to the closed neighborhood hypergraphs
{\cal H}_{T_{2}},\dots,{\cal H}_{T_{\ell}}
. If
d_{1}
is a leaf in
T
, then there is no further component in
{\cal H}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, the further components of
{\cal H}^{\prime}
are contained in
{\cal H}_{1}={\cal H}_{T_{1}}-d_{1}
. Proposition
2.3
implies that
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})
, that is
k-1
, equals the minimum of the values
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1}),{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T_{2}}),\dots,{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T_{\ell}})
. It also follows that Staller’s (Maker’s) optimal strategy is to play in the same (appropriate) component of
{\cal H}^{\prime}
starting with her move
s_{2}
. As
s_{2}\notin V({\cal H}_{1})
, there exists a tree
T_{j}
,
2\leq j\leq\ell
, such that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{j})=k-1
. By our hypothesis,
T_{j}
contains a substructure
S^{1}
from
{\cal S}_{k-1}
. As
S^{1}
is a subgraph but not a substructure in
T
, the vertex
s_{1}
must be adjacent to a vertex
t_{j}
from
X(S^{1})
. Note that, as
T
is a tree,
s_{1}
is adjacent to exactly one vertex
t_{i}
from each
T_{i}
,
i\in[\ell]
. Consequently, no component
T_{i}
can contain two vertex disjoint substructures from
\cup_{p=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{p}
and, if
T_{i}
contains a substructure
S
from
\cup_{p=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{p}
, then
t_{i}
, the neighbor of
s_{1}
in
T_{i}
, belongs to
X(S)
.

We will show that
T-s_{1}
contains a substructure
S^{2}
from
\cup_{p=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{p}
that is vertex disjoint to
S^{1}
. Assume for a contradiction that it is not true. Thus, by the hypothesis,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{i})\geq k
holds for every
i\neq j
. In this situation, suppose that Dominator plays
d_{1}^{\prime}=t_{j}
instead of
d_{1}=t_{1}
. By Lemma
3.1
(i), each remaining move of Staller belongs to a a subtree
T_{i}
,
i\neq j
, and she needs at least
k
further moves to win the game. This contradicts the condition
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
and the assumption that Staller plays optimally.

To conclude the proof, we infer that
T-s_{1}
contains two different components,
T_{j}
and
T_{s}
,
j\neq s
, such that an
S^{1}\in{\cal S}_{k-1}
is a substructure in
T_{j}
and an
S^{2}\in\cup_{p=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{p}
is a substructure in
T_{s}
. Further,
s_{1}
is adjacent to
t_{j}
and
t_{s}
which belong to
X(S^{1})
and
X(S^{2})
respectively. By the definition of
{\cal S}_{k}
, the subgraph induced by
V(S^{1})\cup V(S^{2})\cup\{s_{1}\}
is included in
{\cal S}_{k}
and, as the subgraph complies with the fixed vertex degrees, it is a substructure from
{\cal S}_{k}
in
T
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Observation
3.3
(v) shows that
P_{n}\in{\cal S}_{\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor+1}
for every odd integer
n
. Then, by Theorem
3.5
, we may conclude
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor+1
. This provides an alternative shorter proof for the formula in Theorem
2.8
.

For any graph
G
and a subset
A\subseteq V(G)
, the hypergraph
{\cal H}^{-A}_{G}
is obtained from the closed neighhborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
by deleting the hyperedges corresponding to the closed neighborhoods of vertices in
A
.

To state an upper bound on the winning numbers of hypergraphs, we define a family
{\cal F}_{k}
for every positive integer
k
as follows:

{\cal F}_{k}=\{{\cal H}^{-(V(S)\setminus X(S))}_{S}:S\in{\cal S}_{k}\}.

Alternatively,
{\cal F}_{k}
can be defined recursively starting with
{\cal F}_{1}=\{{\cal B}\}
where
{\cal B}
is the hypergraph that consists of one vertex
x
and one edge
\{x\}
. Then, for every
k\geq 2
, the family
{\cal F}_{k}^{*}
contains a hypergraph
{\cal A}
if
{\cal A}
can be obtained in the following way. Choose two hypergraphs
{\cal A}_{1}\in{\cal F}_{k-1}
and
{\cal A}_{2}\in\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}{\cal F}_{i}
and select two edges
e_{1}\in E({\cal A}_{1})
and
e_{2}\in E({\cal A}_{2})
. To finish the construction, we take a new (origin) vertex
u
, define
e_{i}^{\prime}=e_{i}\cup\{u\}
for
i\in[2]
and set

V({\cal A})=V({\cal A}_{1})\cup V({\cal A}_{2})\cup\{u\},\quad E({\cal A})=E({\cal A}_{1})\cup E({\cal A}_{2})\cup\{e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}\}\setminus\{e_{1},e_{2}\}.

After having the family
{\cal F}_{k}^{*}
in hand, we set
{\cal F}_{k}={\cal F}_{k}^{*}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}{\cal F}_{i}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

If a hypergraph
{\cal H}
contains a subhypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}
from
{\cal F}_{k}
, then
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})\leq k
.

Proof.

The statement is clearly true for
k=1
as only a presence of a one-element winning set ensures that Maker can win with his first move in a Maker-start game. If
k\geq 2
, we refer to the notation used in the recursive definition of
{\cal F}_{k}
and show that Maker has a strategy to win in at most
k
moves. Consider the subhypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}_{k}
and suppose that Maker first plays the origin vertex
u
. That leaves the hypergraph
{\cal H}\mid u
with a subhypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}\mid u
for the continuation of the game.
{\cal H}\mid u
therefore contains two vertex disjoint subhypergraphs
{\cal A}_{1}\in{\cal F}_{k-1}
and
{\cal A}_{2}\in\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}{\cal F}_{i}
. After Breaker’s move
v
, at least one of these subhypergraphs remains untouched and
({\cal H}\mid u)-v
contains a subhypergraph from
\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}{\cal F}_{i}
. By the induction hypothesis, Maker can win the game in at most
k-1
further moves. As follows,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})\leq k
that concludes the proof.
∎

The following consequence of Proposition
3.7
gives an upper bound for the SMBD-numbers of graphs in general. Note that the corollary can also be obtained by analyzing the proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Corollary 3.8
.

If a graph
G
contains a substructure
S\in{\cal S}_{k}
, then
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)\leq k
.

Proof.

Observe that the closed neighborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
contains a subhypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}
isomorphic to
{\cal H}^{-(V(S)\setminus X(S))}_{S}
. As
S\in{\cal S}_{k}
, we have
{\cal H}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}_{k}
and Proposition
3.7
implies
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{G})\leq k
. Since
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{G})
, this proves the statement.
∎

, where

4
Subdivided stars

In this section we consider the SMBD-numbers of subdivided stars. Let
S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
be the tree obtained from the paths
P_{n_{1}},\dots,P_{n_{\ell}}
and a
central vertex

x
by making
x
adjacent to one end of each path. The paths
P_{n_{1}},\dots,P_{n_{\ell}}
obtained after the deletion of the central vertex are called
branches
. Throughout, we assume that
\ell\geq 2
and
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}
are positive integers.

For a subdivided star
T=S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
, the residual graph
R(T)
, which is obtained by iteratively removing pendant edges, clearly contains at most one strong support vertex. Therefore, by Theorem
2.6
(i),
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)=\infty
always holds. Concerning the S-game on
T
, we consider three cases according to the parities of
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}
. If there is exactly one odd number among
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}
, then
T
has a perfect matching and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=\infty
follows from Theorem
2.6
(ii). If there are at least two odd numbers among
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}
, Theorem
4.1
will establish the explicit formula. The last case is when
T
is an
all-even
subdivided star that is,
n_{i}
is even for each
i\in[\ell]
. If
\ell=2
,
T
is a path and Theorem
2.8
establishes the formula for
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
. If
\ell=3
, our Theorem
4.3
will answer the question. For larger values of
\ell
, we provide a sharp upper bound, but the exact formula for SMBD-numbers of all-even subdivided stars with at least four branches remains an open problem.

4.1
Not-all-even subdivided stars

As Staller cannot win the S-game on
T=S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
if
T
contains exactly one odd branch, we concentrate on the case when there exist at least two odd branches.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
is a subdivided star and
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
are the two smallest odd numbers among
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}
, then

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}))=\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil.

Proof.

If
\ell=2
, the subdivided star is an odd path of order
n_{1}+n_{2}+1
and, by Theorem
2.8
,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S(n_{1},n_{2}))=\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil
is true. From now on we assume
\ell\geq 3
.

Let
T=S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
and
k=\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil
. By Theorem
3.5
, it suffices to show that
T
contains a substructure from
{\cal S}_{k}
and contains no substructure from
\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}{\cal S}_{i}
. The first part of the statement clearly holds because the paths
P_{n_{1}}
and
P_{n_{2}}
together with the central vertex
x
form a subgraph
P_{n_{1}+n_{2}+1}
in
T
. By Observation
3.3
(v),
P_{n_{1}+n_{2}+1}\in{\cal S}_{k}
. Further, by Observation
3.3
(i), since
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are odd numbers,
x
does not belong to
X(P_{n_{1}+n_{2}+1})
. We infer that
P_{n_{1}+n_{2}+1}
is a substructure in
T
.

Assume that
T
contains a substructure
S\in{\cal S}_{i}
. By Observation
3.3
(i) and (ii),
S
is a tree where
X(S)

corresponds to the partite class containing all leaves of
S
, while all vertices in the partite class
V(S)\setminus X(S)
are of degree
2
in
S
. As at least two leaves of
T
together with the path between them are present in
S
, the central vertex
x
also belongs to
S
.

•

Suppose that
x\in X(S)
. Then
N_{T}[x]\subseteq V(S)
and hence
S\cong T
.
However, as some odd numbers are present among
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}
, Observation
3.3
(i) implies
x\notin X(S)
that contradicts the supposition
x\in S
.

•

Suppose that
x\notin X(S)
. By Observation
3.3
(i) and (ii),
\deg_{S}(x)=2
and
x
is at an odd distance from each leaf of
S
. We infer that the substructure
S
is a path induced by
\{x\}\cup V(P_{n_{a}})\cup V(P_{n_{b}})
, where
n_{a}
and
n_{b}
are odd integers. By our condition,
n_{a}+n_{b}+1\geq n_{1}+n_{2}+1
and, consequently,
i\geq k
whenever
S\in{\cal S}_{i}
holds.

By Theorem
3.5
we conclude that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}))=k=\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil
.
∎

4.2
All-even subdivided stars

Suppose that
T=S(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
is an all-even subdivided star and
n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq n_{3}
. We say that
T
is
reducible
, if
\lceil\log_{2}(n_{3})\rceil=\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1)\rceil
. Then we define the
reduced graph
of
T
as

T^{\prime}=S(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}-2^{\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1)\rceil-1}).

By the condition on
n_{3}
and as
\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1)\rceil\geq 3
,
T^{\prime}
is also an all-even subdivided star with three branches. We say that
T
is
non-reducible
, if for every
i\in[3]
,

\lceil\log_{2}(n_{i})\rceil<\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1)\rceil\,.

(1)

Using this terminology, we give a recursive definition for two types of subdivided stars.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
T=S(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
be a subdivided star such that
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
are positive even integers and
n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq n_{3}
holds. Let us set
n=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1
.

(i)

If
T
is non-reducible, then

–

T\in{\cal T}_{1}
, if
\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil=\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
;

–

T\in{\cal T}_{0}
, if
\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil<\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
.

(ii)

If
T
is reducible and
T^{\prime}
is its reduced graph, then

–

T\in{\cal T}_{1}
, if
T^{\prime}\in{\cal T}_{1}
and
\lceil\log_{2}(n-2^{\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil-2})\rceil=\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
;

–

otherwise
T\in{\cal T}_{0}
.

By (
1
) and Definition
4.2
(i),
T
is non-reducible and
T\in{\cal T}_{1}
if and only if

\lceil\log_{2}n_{3}\rceil<\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil=\lceil\log_{2}(n)\rceil\,.

(2)

Similarly,
T
is non-reducible and
T\in{\cal T}_{0}
if and only if

\max\{\lceil\log_{2}n_{3}\rceil,\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil\}<\lceil\log_{2}(n)\rceil\,.

(3)

Theorem 4.3
.

If
T=S(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
is a subdivided star of order
n=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1
, and
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
are positive even integers, then

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil;&\enskip T\in{\cal T}_{0},\\
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil+1;&\enskip T\in{\cal T}_{1}.\end{cases}

(4)

Proof.

We use the notation given in the theorem and assume without loss of generality that
n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq n_{3}
holds. Let
x
be the center of
T
while
P^{1},P^{2},P^{3}
denote the branches of order
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
, respectively.

First we show that
T\in{\cal S}
. As
n
is odd, there is no perfect matching in
T
and Theorem
2.6
(ii) implies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)<\infty
. Thus, by Theorem
3.5
, there exists a substructure
S\in{\cal S}
in
T
. By Observation
3.3
(i),
S
is a tree and the set
X(S)
contains all leaves of
S
and the vertices being at an even distance from a leaf. By Observation
3.3
(iii), every leaf of
S
is a leaf in
T
and therefore,
x\in X(S)
. Hence,
N_{S}(x)=N_{T}(x)
and all leaves of
T
are present in
S
. Consequently,
S=T
and

hold.

Second, we prove that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
equals either
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
or
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil+1
. As

, Observation
3.3
(iv) and Theorem

immediately give

. This proves
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
. For the upper bound, we consider the cut vertex
t_{3}
that is the neighbor of
x
from the branch
P^{3}
. The graph

contains two components one of which is a path of order

and the other one is a path of order

. Using this fact, Proposition
2.5
(iii), Theorem
2.8
, and inequalities

and
n_{1}+n_{2}+1<n
, we can estimate
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)
as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n_{3}-1}),\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n_{1}+n_{2}+1})\}+1

\displaystyle=\max\{\lfloor\log_{2}(n_{3}-1)\rfloor+1,\lfloor\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rfloor+1\}+1

\displaystyle=\max\{\lceil\log_{2}n_{3}\rceil,\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil\}+1

\displaystyle\leq\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil+1\,.

We have just proved that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\in\{\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil,\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil+1\}
. To decide which of the two possibilities holds and to prove the theorem, we first consider all non-reducible graphs and then proceed by induction on the number of vertices when dealing with reducible graphs. Let
k=\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
.

Case 1:

T
is a non-reducible graph.

By the definition of non-reducibility,
\lceil\log_{2}(n_{3})\rceil<k
.
We consider two cases.

Case 1.1:

T
is non-reducible and
T\in{\cal T}_{0}
.

By (
3

M=\max\{\lceil\log_{2}n_{3}\rceil,\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil\}\leq k-1.

Considering the cut vertex

again, Proposition
2.5
(iii) and Theorem
2.8
imply
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\leq M+1\leq k
for this case. Together with the lower bound

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
for every non-reducible
T
from
{\cal T}_{0}
.

Case 1.2:

T
is non-reducible and
T\in{\cal T}_{1}
.

Since Staller can win the S-game on
T
, there is an optimal first move
s_{1}
for her. Note first that
s_{1}\neq x
. Indeed, if
s_{1}=x
, then Dominator plays any neighbor of

and the undominated vertices can be covered by a matching, hence Dominator will win the game according to Lemma
2.7
. We may claim the same if all components of
T-s_{1}
are even. To see it, observe that Dominator can always play a neighbor of
s_{1}
such that the undominated vertices can be covered by a matching. We have thus demonstrated that
s_{1}\neq x
and that not all components of

are even.

If
s_{1}
is from
P^{3}
, let
T^{1}
and
T^{2}
be the two components of
T-s_{1}
such that
V(T^{1})\subseteq V(P^{3})
. Dominator may play the neighbor of
s_{1}
that belongs to
T^{1}
. Then, by Lemma
3.1
, Staller’s optimal response is a vertex from
T^{2}
and, under optimal strategies, the game continues on
T^{2}
and finishes with the
(\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T^{2})+1)^{\text{st}}
move of Staller. As
T^{2}
is an all-even subdivided star or an odd path,

. Then, by (
2

(iv), and by our condition, we get

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T^{2})+1\geq\lceil\log_{2}|V(T^{2})|\rceil+1\geq\lceil\log_{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\rceil+1=k+1.

This proves that after Staller’s move

, Dominator has a strategy which ensures that Staller cannot win before her

move. The proof is similar, if

is from

or

. We conclude

, which in turn results in the desired equality

.

Case 2:

T
is a reducible graph.

The reduced graph of

is

and

. From now on, we proceed by induction.

Case 2.1:

T
is reducible and
T\in{\cal T}_{0}
.

Definition

(ii) allows two possibilities for

. We first show that

•

If
T\in{\cal T}_{0}
and

, the induction hypothesis implies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T^{\prime})=\lceil\log_{2}n^{\prime}\rceil\leq k-1
.

•

If

and

, then also

From this inequality, we may infer

and conclude
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T^{\prime})\leq k-1
again.

Let

be the (unique) vertex of

such that

consists of a component isomorphic to

and a path component of order

. We have already seen that

and the path component clearly satisfies

. Applying Proposition

(iii), we get

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\leq\max\{\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T^{\prime}),k-1\}+1\leq(k-1)+1=k.

Since
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\geq k
also holds, we conclude
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=k
as stated in the theorem.

Case 2.2:

is reducible and

.

We are going to prove that

. As

for Staller that allows her to win within

•

If

contains no component of odd order, then Staller cannot win in the continuation of the game.

•

If Staller plays a vertex

, then Dominator responds by choosing the neighbor of
s_{1}
which is not incident to the

, Observation

(iv) implies

. This shows that if Staller chooses her first vertex from

or

, she cannot win with

•

If

such that

contains a path component of order at least

, then we can handle it as in the previous paragraph.

•

Let

, Staller’s optimal strategy is to play (optimally) on

,

only if

and

•

The last case is when

is a vertex from

can be obtained from

Corollary 4.4
.

If
k\geq 1
is an integer, then

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S(2k,2k,2k))=\lceil\log_{2}(4k+1)\rceil+1\,.

Proof.

Let
k\geq 1
and set
T=S(2k,2k,2k)
. Then
T
is non-reducible because
\lceil\log_{2}(2k)\rceil<\lceil\log_{2}(6k+1)\rceil
. Assume first that
T\in{\cal T}_{1}
. Then, by definition,
\lceil\log_{2}(4k+1)\rceil=\lceil\log_{2}(6k+1)\rceil
. Moreover, by Theorem
4.3
,

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=\lceil\log_{2}(6k+1)\rceil+1=\lceil\log_{2}(4k+1)\rceil+1\,.

Assume second that
T\in{\cal T}_{0}
. By definition,
\lceil\log_{2}(4k+1)\rceil<\lceil\log_{2}(6k+1)\rceil
. Since
|\log_{2}(6k+1)-\log_{2}(4k+1)|<1
, it follows that
\lceil\log_{2}(6k+1)\rceil-\lceil\log_{2}(4k+1)\rceil\leq 1
. Consequently,
\lceil\log_{2}(4k+1)\rceil+1=\lceil\log_{2}(6k+1)\rceil
. Applying Theorem
4.3
again, we get that also in this case

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)=\lceil\log_{2}(6k+1)\rceil=\lceil\log_{2}(4k+1)\rceil+1\,,

and we are done.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
T=S(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})
be a subdivided star such that
\ell\geq 3
,
n_{1}\geq\dots\geq n_{\ell}
, and
n_{i}
is a positive even integer for each
i\in[\ell]
. Then,

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\leq\max(\{i+\lceil\log_{2}n_{i}\rceil:i\in[\ell-2]\}\cup\{\ell-2+\lceil\log_{2}(n_{\ell-1}+n_{\ell}+1)\rceil\}),

and the bound is sharp for every
\ell\geq 3
.

Proof.

Let
x
be the center of
T
, and
P^{1},\dots,P^{\ell}
be the branches of order
n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}
respectively. The neighbor of
x
from
P^{i}
will be denoted by
t_{i}
for
i\in[\ell]
.

To prove the upper bound, we describe a strategy for Staller. In her first move, Staller plays
t_{1}
. We have two cases.

•

If Dominator replies with a move
d_{1}
from the component of
T-t_{1}
which contains
x
, Staller continues the game by playing (optimally) on the path
P^{1}-t_{1}
. After the moves
t_{1}
,
d_{1}
, the Maker-Breaker game continues on the hypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}=({\cal H}_{T}-t_{1})\mid d_{1}
with Staller’s (Maker’s) move. As
{\cal H}^{\prime\prime}={\cal H}_{P^{1}-t_{1}}
is a subhypergraph in
{\cal H}^{\prime}
, Proposition
2.2
(i) implies

{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime\prime})=\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P^{1}-t_{1})=\lceil\log_{2}n_{1}\rceil.

In this case, Staller can win the S-game on
T
in
1+\lceil\log_{2}n_{1}\rceil
moves.

•

If Dominator replies with a move
d_{1}\in V(P^{1}-t_{1})
, then Staller plays
t_{2}
as her next move.

In general, Staller’s strategy is the following. For
i\in[\ell-2]
, if Staller plays
t_{i}\in N_{T}(x)\cap V(P^{i})
as her
i^{\text{th}}

move and Dominator responds by playing a vertex
d_{i}\notin V(P^{i})
, then Staller continues playing (optimally) on
P^{i}-t_{i}
. It allows her to win the game in

moves. If Dominator responds by choosing a vertex

and

, Staller’s next move is

. If Dominator’s reply is
d_{i}\in V(P^{i})
and

, Staller plays on the path

induced by

and can win the game in

moves.

Consequently, either there exists an

such that Staller plays

and Dominator replies with
d_{i}\notin V(P^{i})
or Staller, after playing

, can win by playing on

. In the former case, Staller plays at most

vertices; in the latter case she can win in at most

To prove the sharpness, we define the following infinite class of all-even subdivided stars. If
\ell\geq 3
and

are two integers, let

be the all-even subdivided star

where

,

, and

Note that

contains

vertices. As it is an all-even subdivided star,

. By Observation
3.3
(iv),

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(Z(\ell,p))\geq\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil=\ell+p-1.

By determining the upper bound from the current proposition, we find that

for all

, and

Thus the upper bound in the proposition gives
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(Z(\ell,p))\leq\ell+p-1
.
As the upper bound matches the lower bound, the sharpness is true. ∎

A
caterpillar
is a tree
T
on at least three vertices in which a single path is incident to (or contains) every edge. There is more than one path with this property, but we select the shortest one and call it the
spine
of the caterpillar, see
West 1996
. The vertices of the spine will be denoted by
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
according to their natural order. Before stating the main result of the section, some preparation is needed.

Let
T
be a caterpillar. Then we set

{\cal P}_{\rm o}(T)=\{P:\ P\ \mbox{is a maximal path in}\ T\ \mbox{and}\ |V(P)|\ \mbox{is odd}\}\,,

where maximal is meant maximal w.r.t. inclusion.
We say that a path
P=u_{1}u_{2}\dots u_{k}
in
T
is
clean
if
\deg_{T}(u_{i})=2
for
3\leq i\leq k-2
. Let further

{\cal P}_{\rm co}(T)=\{P:\ P\in{\cal P}_{\rm o}(T)\ \mbox{and}\ P\ \mbox{is clean}\}\,.

We refer to the members of
{\cal P}_{\rm co}(T)
as
{\cal P}_{\rm co}
-paths. Note that each such path contains at most two support vertices from the spine of
T
. If
{\cal P}
is a collection of paths of
T
, then the smallest carnality of a set of vertices
U
such that each path from
{\cal P}
has a vertex in
U

t_{\rm p}({\cal P})
. If
T
is a caterpillar containing at least one clean path, then let
p(T)
be the minimum order of such a path. If

be the smallest integer

Theorem 5.1
.

If

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\infty;&\enskip{\cal P}_{\rm co}(T)=\emptyset,\\
\lceil\log_{2}p(T)\rceil;&\enskip\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\infty;&\enskip t_{\rm p}({\cal P}_{\rm co}(T))\leq 1,\\
\lceil\log_{2}p^{*}(T)\rceil;&\enskip\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
T
be a caterpillar and set
p=p(T)
and
p^{*}=p^{*}(T)
.

Suppose first that
{\cal P}_{\rm co}(T)=\emptyset
, which also implies that there are no strong support vertices in
T
. We can construct a perfect matching in

as follows. First, delete the support vertices of

from the subgraph induced by the spine of

. As

We now prove the statement for

.
Since

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T)\geq 2

and
Y=\emptyset
. First, delete the support vertices of
T
from the subgraph induced by the spine of
T
to obtain the linear forest
T^{-}
. Since every
{\cal P}_{\rm co}
-path is incident to
v_{i}
, each component of
T^{-}
that contains neither
v_{i-1}
nor
v_{i+1}
is an even path and admits a perfect matching. If there is an odd path component which contains
v_{i-1}
, then
v_{i-1}
is the end vertex, and we can take a matching that covers all vertices of the component except
v_{i-1}
. The situation is similar if an odd path contains
v_{i+1}
. To finish the construction of the matching
M
, we add every edge which is incident to a leaf except those incident to
v_{i}
and
U
. By the condition
t_{\rm p}({\cal P}_{\rm co}(T))=1
, the caterpillar contains no strong support vertex except (possibly)
v_{i}
. This shows that
M
is a matching in
T-v_{i}
. Concerning the set of vertices covered by
M
, we observe

V(T)\setminus V(M)\subseteq N_{T}[v_{i}]\,,

and conclude by Lemma
2.7
that Staller cannot win the D-game if Dominator plays
v_{i}
as his first move. Thus,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)=\infty
.

For the rest of the proof we assume
t_{\rm p}({\cal P}_{\rm co}(T))\geq 2
.
By the definition of
p^{*}
, after Dominator’s first move
d_{1}
, there remains an unplayed
{\cal P}_{\rm co}
-path
P_{j}
of order
j\leq p^{*}
. Clearly,
P_{j}\in{\cal S}_{i}
with
i\leq\lceil\log_{2}p^{*}\rceil
. By Proposition
2.1
, the game continues as a Maker-start game on
{\cal H}_{T}-d_{1}
. As
d_{1}
might be adjacent to a support vertex of
P_{j}
but no vertex from
X(P_{j})
, the hypergraph
{\cal H}_{T}-d_{1}
contains a subhypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}={\cal H}_{P_{j}}^{-(V(P_{j})\setminus X(P_{j}))}
. By Proposition
3.7
,
{\cal H}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}_{i}
implies
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T}-d_{1})\leq i
. Assuming that
d_{1}
is an optimal first move of Dominator, we conclude

\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T}-d_{1})\leq i\leq\lceil\log_{2}p^{*}\rceil.

We now prove
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)\geq\lceil\log_{2}p^{*}\rceil
by describing a strategy of Dominator which ensures that Staller cannot win by playing less than
\lceil\log_{2}p^{*}\rceil
vertices. Let
v_{1}\dots v_{\ell}
be the spine of the caterpillar.
According to the definition of
p^{*}
, there exists a support vertex
v_{q}
in
T
such that the smallest order of an odd clean path not covered by
v_{q}
is
p^{*}
. Let
v_{q}
be the first move of Dominator. By Proposition
2.1
, the game continues as a Maker-start game on
{\cal H}_{T}-v_{q}
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T}-v_{q})
. We will transform
{\cal H}_{T}-v_{q}
into a hypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}
such that

{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T}-v_{q})
.
If
q\geq 2
, then let
P^{1}
be the clean maximal path in
T
which contains both
v_{q-1}
and
v_{q}
.

(a)

If
P^{1}
is an even path, we obtain
{\cal H}^{\prime}
from
{\cal H}_{T}-v_{q}
by adding the new winning set (i.e., the edge)
N_{T}[v_{q-1}]\setminus\{v_{q}\}
. Then, this component of
{\cal H}^{\prime}
corresponds to the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the caterpillar
T_{1}
that is induced by the vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{q-1}
and the adjacent leaves in
T
. If
q=2
, then
T_{1}
is a star
K_{1,m}
with
m\geq 1
.

(b)

If
P^{1}
is an odd path, we obtain
{\cal H}^{\prime}
from
{\cal H}_{T}-v_{q}
by replacing the winning set
N_{T}[v_{q-2}]
with the smaller hyperedge
N_{T}[v_{q-2}]\setminus\{v_{q-1}\}
. This component of
{\cal H}^{\prime}
therefore corresponds to the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the caterpillar
T_{1}
that is induced by the vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{q-2}
and the adjacent leaves in
T
. If
P^{1}
is odd, then
q=2
is not possible. If
q=3
, then
T_{1}
is a star which contains exactly one vertex, namely
v_{1}
, from the spine of
T
.

If
q\leq\ell-1
, we also consider a clean maximal path
P^{2}
in
T
that contains
v_{q}
and
v_{q+1}
. Similarly to the changes described above for
P^{1}
, if
|V(P^{2})|
is even, we add the new winning set
N_{T}[v_{q+1}]\setminus\{v_{q}\}
; if
|V(P^{2})|
is odd, we replace
N_{T}[v_{q+2}]
with
N_{T}[v_{q+2}]\setminus\{v_{q+1}\}
when construct
{\cal H}^{\prime}
. The corresponding component of
{\cal H}^{\prime}
is the closed neighborhood hypergraph of a caterpillar
T_{2}
. In the first case,
T_{2}
is the subgraph of
T
induced by
v_{q+1},\dots,v_{\ell}
and the adjacent leaves, while in the second case
T_{2}
is induced by
v_{q+2},\dots,v_{\ell}
and the adjacent leaves. Note that
T_{1}
is considered empty if
q=1
, while
T_{2}
is empty if
q=\ell
. In these cases we respectively set
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{1})=\infty
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{2})=\infty
.

Observe that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
still contain all clean maximal paths of
T
that do not contain
v_{q}
. In particular, a
{\cal P}_{\rm co}
-path of order
p^{*}
is present in the disjoint union
T^{\prime}=T_{1}\cup T_{2}
. In both cases
(a)
and
(b)
, only clean maximal paths of even order were added as new ones. Note that, if
|V(P^{i})|\leq 5
, no new clean maximal path appears in
T_{i}
, for
i\in[2]
. Using Propositions
2.2
and
2.4
, and the formula already proved for the S-game on a caterpillar, we obtain the following relation:

\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T)\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{T}-v_{q})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})=\min\{\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{1}),\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T_{2})\}=\lceil\log_{2}p^{*}\rceil.

This completes the proof for the second formula.
∎
