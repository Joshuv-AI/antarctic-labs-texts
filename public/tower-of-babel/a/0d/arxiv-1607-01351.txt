The full collection is available at http://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Deift-Tracy.html

Abstract
We study the Tracy–Widom distribution function for Dyson’s β \beta -ensemble with β = 6 \beta=6 . The starting point of our analysis is the recent work of I. Rumanov where he produces a Lax-pair representation for the Bloemendal–Virág equation. The latter is a linear PDE which describes the Tracy–Widom functions corresponding to general values of β \beta . Using his Lax pair, Rumanov derives an explicit formula for the Tracy–Widom β = 6 \beta=6 function in terms of the second Painlevé transcendent and the solution of an auxiliary ODE. Rumanov also shows that this formula allows him to derive formally the asymptotic expansion of the Tracy–Widom function. Our goal is to make Rumanov’s approach and hence the asymptotic analysis it provides rigorous. In this paper, the first one in a sequel, we show that Rumanov’s Lax-pair can be interpreted as a certain gauge transformation of the standard Lax pair for the second Painlevé equation. This gauge transformation though contains functional parameters which are defined via some auxiliary nonlinear ODE which is equivalent to the auxiliary ODE of Rumanov’s formula. The gauge-interpretation of Rumanov’s Lax-pair allows us to highlight the steps of the original Rumanov’s method which needs rigorous justifications in order to make the method complete. We provide a rigorous justification of one of these steps. Namely, we prove that the Painlevé function involved in Rumanov’s formula is indeed, as it has been suggested by Rumanov, the Hastings–McLeod solution of the second Painlevé equation. The key issue which we also discuss and which is still open is the question of integrability of the auxiliary ODE in Rumanov’s formula. We note that this question is crucial for the rigorous asymptotic analysis of the Tracy–Widom function. We also notice that our work is a partial answer to one of the problems related to the β \beta -ensembles formulated by Percy Deift during the June 2015 Montreal Conference on integrable systems.

Introduction

Given
\beta>0
, Dyson’s
\beta
-ensemble is defined as a Coulomb gas of
N
charged particles, that is as the space of
N
one dimensional particles,
\{-\infty<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{N}<\infty\}
with the probability density given by the equation

\displaystyle p(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{N}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\prod_{1\leq j,k\leq N}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|^{\beta}{\rm e}^{-\beta\underset{j=1}{\overset{N}{\sum}}V(\lambda)}d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{N},

(1.1)

\displaystyle Z_{N}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{1\leq j,k\leq N}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|^{\beta}{\rm e}^{-\beta\underset{j=1}{\overset{N}{\sum}}V(\lambda)}d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{N}.

Here,
V(\lambda)
has a meaning of external field which we will assume to be Gaussian, i.e.,
V(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}
.
The objects of interest are the gap probabilities in the large
N
limit. We will be particularly concerned with the soft edge probability distribution

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(t)\equiv E_{\beta}^{{\rm soft}}\bigl(0;(t,\infty)\bigr)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}E_{\beta N}^{{\rm soft}}\left(0;\left(\sqrt{2N}+\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}N^{1/6}},\infty\right)\right),

where

\displaystyle E_{\beta N}^{{\rm soft}}\bigl(0;(t,\infty)\bigr)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{t}p(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{N}.

(1.2)

Cases
\beta=1,2,4
known as Gaussian orthogonal (GOE), Gaussian unitary (GUE) and Gaussian symplectic (GSE) ensembles. Indeed, in these cases, distribution (
1.1
) describes the statistics of the eigenvalues of orthogonal, Hermitian, and symplectic random matrices, respectively, with i.i.d. matrix entries. The corresponding limiting edge distribution functions
F_{\beta}(t)
then become the classical
Tracy–Widom distributions

[
22
]
. They admit explicit representations as either the Airy kernel Fredholm determinants or in terms of the Hastings–McLeod solution of the second Painlevé equation. These representations, in turn, allow one to evaluate the asymptotic expansions of
F_{\beta}(t)
as
t\to-\infty
.

In this paper we address the question of the asymptotic analysis of
F_{\beta}(t)
beyond the classical values
\beta=1,2,4
. The crucial problem is that the orthogonal polynomial approach, which is the principal technique in the intergrable random matrix case, is not available for general
\beta
. However, several highly nontrivial conjectures concerning the general
\beta
ensembles have been suggested. An excellent presentation of the state of art in this area is given in the survey by P. Forrester
[
11

. The current principal heuristic result concerning the asymptotic behavior of the generalized Tracy–Widom distribution

was obtained in 2010 by G. Borot, B. Eynard, S.N. Majumdar and C. Nadal and it reads as follows.

Conjecture 1.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(t)=\exp\left(-\beta\frac{|t|^{3}}{24}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}(\beta/2-1)|t|^{3/2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hphantom{F_{\beta}(t)=}{}+\frac{1}{8}(\beta/2+2/\beta-3)\log|t|+c_{0}+O\left(\frac{1}{|t|^{3/2}}\right)\right),\qquad t\to-\infty.

(1.3)

The constant term

\displaystyle c_{0}=\frac{\beta}{2}\left(\frac{1}{12}-\zeta^{\prime}(-1)\right)+\frac{\gamma}{6\beta}-\frac{\log 2\pi}{4}-\frac{(\beta/2)}{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{c_0 =}{}+\left(\frac{17}{8}-\frac{25}{24}(\beta/2+2/\beta)\right)\log 2+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{e^{\beta t/2}-1}\left(\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}-1+\frac{t}{2}-\frac{t^{2}}{12}\right)dt,

Consider the partial differential equation for the scalar function

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x^{2}}+\big(t-x^{2}\big)\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}=0,\qquad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

\displaystyle F(x,t;\beta)\to 1,\qquad\mbox{as}\quad x,t\to\infty,

(1.6)

\displaystyle F(x,t;\beta)\to 0,\qquad\mbox{as}\quad x\to-\infty\quad\mbox{for f\/ixed $t$.}

(1.7)

Theorem 1.5

(
[
2
]
)
.

The boundary value problem (
1.5
)–(
1.7
) has a unique bounded smooth solution. Moreover, equation

\displaystyle F_{\beta}(t)=\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x,t;\beta),

determines the Tracy–Widom distribution function for the general value of the parameter
\beta>0

Equation (
1.5
) has a very interesting interpretation from the point of view of the theory of Painlevé transcendents. In fact, there are two ways to connect (
1.5
) with the Painlevé equations. The first way identifies (
1.5
) with the
quantum
second Painlevé equation. Indeed, the second Painevé equation, i.e., the equation

\displaystyle u_{tt}=tu+2u^{3},

(1.8)

admits the Hamiltonian form
[
15
]

\displaystyle u_{t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\qquad p_{t}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial u},

where the (time dependent) Hamiltonian
H\equiv H(t,u,p)
is

\displaystyle H=\frac{p^{2}}{2}-\left(u^{2}+\frac{t}{2}\right)p-\frac{u}{2}.

Set

\displaystyle\Phi(u,t;\beta):=F\bigl({-}2^{1/3}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{3}}u,2^{-1/3}e^{i\frac{\pi}{3}}t;\beta\bigr).

Then, in terms of
\Phi(u,t)
, equation (
1.5
) becomes the imaginary time Schrödinger equation generated by the quantum Hamiltonian
H(t,u,\hbar\partial_{u})+u/2
with
2/\beta
playing the role of Planck’s constant
\hbar
. That is, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\beta}\partial_{t}\Phi=\left(H\left(t,u,\frac{2}{\beta}\partial_{u}\right)+\frac{u}{2}\right)\Phi.

Remark 1.6
.

Equation (
1.5
) in its original variables can be also interpreted as an imaginary time Schrödinger equation generated by the quantum Hamiltonian
H_{0}(t,x,\hbar\partial_{x})
with

\displaystyle H_{0}(t,x,p):=-p^{2}-\big(t-x^{2}\big)p

and with the Planck constant
\hbar
again equals

. The corresponding classical dynamical system is equivalent to the Painlevé 34 equation for the momentum

\displaystyle p_{tt}=4p^{2}+2pt+\frac{p^{2}_{t}}{2p},

which, in turn, is reduced again to the second Painlevé equation (
1.8
) for the function

Rumanov’s Lax pair

The original Rumanov’s Lax pair is the following linear system of two
2\times 2
matrix differential equations

\displaystyle\frac{d\Psi}{dx}=L\Psi,\qquad\frac{d\Psi}{dt}=B\Psi,

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}x^{2}-t+x^{2}q_{2}-xq_{1}+q_{0}&2(x^{3}-x^{2}e_{1}+xe_{2}-e_{3})\\
(x+e_{1})\dfrac{1-q_{2}^{2}}{2}+q_{1}q_{2}&x^{2}-t-x^{2}q_{2}+xq_{1}-q_{0}\end{pmatrix},

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{x}{2}(1+q_{2})+{a}&-x^{2}+xb+c\\
\dfrac{q_{2}^{2}-1}{4}&-\dfrac{x}{2}(1-q_{2})+{d}\end{pmatrix},

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle{a}={d}+q_{2}(b-e_{1})+q_{1}.

(2.4)

Let us also introduce the functional parameter

\displaystyle U=3(a+d)-\frac{t^{2}}{2}.

(2.5)

We note that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}B=-x+\frac{t^{2}}{6}+\frac{U}{3}.

The compatibility of equations (
2.1
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{dB}{dx}-\frac{dL}{dt}=[L,B],

(2.6)

which is equivalent to the following set of equations for the parameters
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
,
e_{3}
and
q_{0}
,
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{de_{1}}{dt}=(b-e_{1})(q_{2}e_{1}-q_{1})+q_{2}(c+e_{2})-q_{0},
