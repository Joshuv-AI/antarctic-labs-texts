Effective operators and their variational principles for discrete electrical network problems Preprint: AIP/123-QED
K. Beard, A. Stefan, R. Viator, Jr, A. Welters

Abstract
Using a Hilbert space framework inspired by the methods of orthogonal projections and Hodge decompositions, we study a general class of problems (called Z Z -problems) that arise in effective media theory, especially within the theory of composites, for defining the effective operator. A new and unified approach is developed, based on block operator methods, for obtaining solutions of the Z Z -problem, formulas for the effective operator in terms of the Schur complement, and associated variational principles (e.g., the Dirichlet and Thomson minimization principles) that lead to upper and lower bounds on the effective operator. In the case of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, this allows for a relaxation of the standard hypotheses on positivity and invertibility for the classes of operators usually considered in such problems, by replacing inverses with the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. As we develop the theory, we show how it applies to the classical example from the theory of composites on the effective conductivity in the periodic conductivity problem in the continuum ( 2 ​ d 2d and 3 ​ d 3d ) under the standard hypotheses. After that, we consider the following three important and diverse examples (increasing in complexity) of discrete electrical network problems in which our theory applies under the relaxed hypotheses. First, an operator-theoretic reformulation of the discrete Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) map for an electrical network on a finite linear graph is given and used to relate the DtN map to the effective operator of an associated Z Z -problem. Second, we show how the classical effective conductivity of an electrical network on a finite linear graph is essentially the effective operator of an associated Z Z -problem. Finally, we consider electrical networks on periodic linear graphs and develop a discrete analog to classical example of the periodic conductivity equation and effective conductivity in the continuum. 1 1 1 This work is based in part on the M.S. thesis Beard 2022 of the first author whose advisor was the fourth author.

Introduction

Broadly speaking, effective media (or medium) theory is an analytical or theoretical model which describes the effective (apparent, overall, or aggregate) behavior of a complex, multicomponent system, usually having a constitutive relation, in terms of a simpler system with an effective constitutive relation that defines the effective parameter, moduli, coefficient, tensor, matrix, operator, etc.

;
Milton 2002
;

;
Choy 2016
;

\sigma_{*}

\sigma_{*}

For example, in a continuum electrical conductivity problem with a conductivity tensor field
\sigma=\sigma(x)

E=E(x)
and the current density
J=J(x)
is Ohm’s law

\sigma_{*}

\langle J\rangle=\sigma_{*}\langle E\rangle

;
Milton 2016

Similar to this example, there are a common set of fundamental problems one wants to solve such as: Find sufficient conditions on the constitutive relations that guarantee

\sigma_{*}

\sigma_{*}

Definition 4

We will give a proof of these two theorems which is surprisingly simple now and based on the following well-known minimization principle for Schur complements

Li and Mathias 2000
and the notion of duality between the direct and dual

;

;

We will need the following well-known result
Zhang 2005
(the proof is omitted as it is trivial).

Lemma 10
(Aitken block-diagonalization formula)

If
H=H_{0}\ho H_{1}
is a Hilbert space,
A=[A_{i,j}]_{i,j=0,1}\in\mathcal{L}\left(H\right)
, and
A_{11}
is invertible then

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A_{00}&A_{01}\\
A_{10}&A_{11}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&A_{01}A_{11}^{-1}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A/A_{11}&0\\
0&A_{11}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix},

(38)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&A_{01}A_{11}^{-1}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&-A_{01}A_{11}^{-1}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix},

(39)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
-A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

Theorem 11
(Schur complement: minimization principle)

If
H=H_{0}\ho H_{1}
is a Hilbert space,
A=[A_{i,j}]_{i,j=0,1}\in\mathcal{L}\left(H\right)
,
A^{*}=A,

A_{11}
is
invertible and
A_{11}\geq 0
then the Schur complement of
A
with respect to
A_{11}
, i.e.,
A/A_{11}=A_{00}-A_{01}A_{11}^{-1}A_{10},
is the unique self-adjoint operator satisfying the minimization principle:

\displaystyle\left(x,A/A_{11}x\right)=\min_{y\in H_{1}}\left(x+y,A\left(x+y\right)\right),\text{ }\forall x\in H_{0},

(41)

and, for each
x\in H_{0}
, the minimizer is unique and given by

\displaystyle y=-A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}x\in H_{1}.

(42)

Moreover,

\displaystyle A/A_{11}\leq A_{00}.

(43)

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. Then
(A_{11})^{*}=A_{11}\geq\delta I,
for some scalar
\delta>0
. Next, it follows by Lemma
10
that
A
has the block
factorization (
38
) with

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&A_{01}A_{11}^{-1}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}^{\ast}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence, for any
x\in H_{0}
and

y\in H_{1}
,

\displaystyle\left(x+y,A\left(x+y\right)\right)=\left(\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}A_{00}&A_{01}\\
A_{10}&A_{11}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}A/A_{11}&0\\
0&A_{11}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x,A/A_{11}x\right)+\left(A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}x+y,A_{11}\left(A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}x+y\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(x,A/A_{11}x\right),

with equality iff
\left(A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}x+y,A_{11}\left(A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}x+y\right)\right)=0
iff
A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}x+y=0
iff
y=-A_{11}^{-1}A_{10}x
. This proves the equality (
41
) and the uniqueness of the minimizer which is given by (
42
). The proof of the theorem now follows from all this and the fact that
A/A_{11}\in\mathcal{L}\left(H_{0}\right)
is
self-adjoint so is uniquely determined by its quadratic form (see, for instance, Theorem 4.4 in Ref.
Weidmann 1980
) and the upper bound (
43
) follows from considering the RHS of (
41
) with
y=0\in H_{1}
.

IV
A unified framework for the variational principles

In this section, we introduce a unified framework for solving constrained linear equations using variational principles which is based on
2\times 2
block operator matrices that admit a block UDL factorization of a special form (U - upper triangular, D - diagonal, L - lower triangular). This will be use to derive solutions to
Z
-problems and their associated effective operators under weaker hypotheses. It can also be useful in deriving variational principles for other problems in the abstract theory of composites and related problems in matrix/operator theory (for more on this see Ref.
Beard 2022
). Hence, we believe this unified framework will have applications outside this paper.

We begin by recalling
Ben-Israel and Greville 2003
;
Friedberg, Insel, and Spence 2019
the definition of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse and some of it’s fundamental properties.

Definition 21
(Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse)

Let
H,\mathcal{H}
be two finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and
X\in\mathcal{L}(H,\mathcal{H})
. Then the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
X^{+}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H},H)
is the unique linear operator that satisfies the four Penrose equations:

(1)

X^{+}XX^{+}=X^{+},

(2)

XX^{+}X=X,

(3)

(X^{+}X)^{*}=X^{+}X,

(4)

(XX^{+})^{*}=XX^{+}.

Notation 22

For any Hilbert space
H
with
\dim H<\infty
and for any subspace
S
of
H
, we denote the orthogonal projection of
H
onto
S
by
\Gamma_{S}
.

Lemma 23

Let
H,\mathcal{H}
be two finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and
X\in\mathcal{L}(H,\mathcal{H})
. Then

(1)

(X^{+})^{*}=(X^{*})^{+}
,

(2)

XX^{+}=\Gamma_{\ran X},\;I-XX^{+}=\Gamma_{\ker X^{*}}
,

(3)

X^{+}X=\Gamma_{\ran X^{*}},\;I-X^{+}X=\Gamma_{\ker X}
,

(4)

If
X=X^{*}
then
X^{+}X=XX^{+}
.

(5)

If
X
is invertible then
X^{-1}=X^{+}
.

(6)

(X^{+})^{+}=X.

Next, we consider the solutions of a class of constrained linear equations involving an operator having a special block factorization and then describe a fundamental variational principle for solving the equations.

Lemma 24

Let
H=H_{0}\ho H_{1}
be a Hilbert space with
\dim H<\infty
.
If
X\in\mathcal{L}(H)
has the
2\times 2
block factorization

\displaystyle X=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&Y^{*}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}W&0\\
0&Z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
Y&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix},

(79)

\displaystyle W\in\mathcal{L}(H_{0}),\;Y\in\mathcal{L}(H_{0},H_{1}),\;Z\in\mathcal{L}(H_{1}),

(80)

\displaystyle W^{*}=W\text{ and }Z^{*}=Z

(81)

then, for each
u\in H_{0}
, the set of all solutions of the constrained linear equation

X(u+v)=w,\;\;(w,v)\in H_{0}\times H_{1},

(82)

is given by

\displaystyle\{(w,v)\in H_{0}\times H_{1}:w=Wu,\;v=-Z^{+}ZYu+v_{1},\;v_{1}\in\ker Z\}.

(83)

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. Then, for each
u\in H_{0}
,

\displaystyle X(u+v)=w,\;\;(w,v)\in H_{0}\times H_{1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}W&0\\
0&Z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
Y&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&-Y^{*}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w\\
0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}Wu\\
Z(Yu+v)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}w\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Leftrightarrow w=Wu,\;\;-ZYu=Zv

\displaystyle\underset{\text{Lemma}\;\ref{LemMPProp}.(3)}{\Leftrightarrow}v=v_{0}+v_{1},\;\;v_{1}\in\ker Z,\;\;v_{0}=-Z^{+}ZYu,\;\;w=Wu.

This completes the proof.

Theorem 25
(Fundamental minimization principle)

Let
H=H_{0}\ho H_{1}
be a Hilbert space with
\dim H<\infty
.
If
X\in\mathcal{L}(H)
has the
2\times 2
block factorization (
79
)-(

Z\geq 0
,
then

is the unique self-adjoint operator satisfying the minimization principle

\left(u,Wu\right)=\min_{v\in H_{1}}\left(u+v,X(u+v)\right),\text{ for each }u\in H_{0}.

(84)

Furthermore, for each
u\in H_{0}
, the set of minimizers is given by

\displaystyle\{v\in H_{1}:v=-(Z^{+}ZY)u+v_{1},\;v_{1}\in\ker{Z}\}.

(85)

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. Then, for any
(u,v)\in H_{0}\times H_{1}
, it follows that

\displaystyle(u+v,X(u+v))=\left(\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&Y^{*}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}W&0\\
0&Z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
Y&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&Y^{*}\\
0&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}W&0\\
0&Z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
Y&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}I_{H_{0}}&0\\
Y&I_{H_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\

ZY&Z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
v\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}u\\

Z(Yu+v)\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=(u,Wu)+(Yu+v,Z(Yu+v))\geq(u,Wu),

with equality if and only if
(Yu+v,Z(Yu+v))=0

and we have

\displaystyle Z(Yu+v)=0\Leftrightarrow Z(Yu)+Zv=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow Zv=-Z(Yu)\Leftrightarrow Z^{+}Zv=-Z^{+}ZYu

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow v=v_{0}+v_{1},\;v_{1}\in\ker Z,v_{0}=-Z^{+}ZYu.

The proof of the theorem now follows from this and the fact that

is
self-adjoint so is uniquely determined by its quadratic form
Weidmann 1980

(146)

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{T}}(t)=1,\;\forall t\in\mathcal{T},

(147)

and the identity operator on
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})
is denoted by
I_{\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})}
.

Lemma 43

If
\mathcal{T}
is a non-empty set, then
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})
is a vector space over the field
\mathbb{K}
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{|\mathcal{T}|}\}
is finite, then
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})
is a Hilbert space under the inner product

(f,g)_{\mathcal{T}}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\overline{f(t)}g(t),\;\forall f,g\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K}).

(148)

Moreover, the set

\alpha_{\mathcal{T}}=\{\delta_{t_{i}}:i=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{T}|\},\quad\delta_{t_{i}}(t_{j})=\delta_{ij},\;\;1\leq i,j\leq|\mathcal{T}|,

(149)

is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta, i.e.,

\delta_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,\quad i=j,\\
0,\quad i\neq j.\end{cases}

(150)

Notation 44

The matrix representation for any linear operator
L\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathbb{K}),\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{2},\mathbb{K}))
with respect to the ordered bases
\alpha_{\mathcal{T}_{1}},\;\alpha_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}
will be denoted by

\begin{bmatrix}L\end{bmatrix}_{\alpha_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}}^{\alpha_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}}
.

Lemma 46

The functions
D
and
D^{\bullet}
[defined by (
151
) and (
152
)] are well defined linear operators and satisfy the Hilbert space adjoint relations

\displaystyle D^{*}=-D^{\bullet},\;(D^{\bullet})^{*}=-D.

(157)

Proof.
First, it is obvious the functions
D
and

are well defined and linear. Second, using the identities (
153
) and (
156
) and the fact that

and
\alpha_{E_{G}}
are orthonormal bases, it follows from standard results from linear algebra
Friedberg, Insel, and Spence 2019
that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}D^{*}\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{P_{G}}}_{\alpha_{E_{G}}}=(\begin{bmatrix}D\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{P_{G}}})^{*}=(\begin{bmatrix}D\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{P_{G}}})^{T}

Remark 47
(Kirchhoff’s laws)

In this network setting, the sets of edge currents and edge voltages are
\ker D^{\bullet}
and
\ran D
, respectively. In particular, Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL) is equivalent to:
I
is an edge current if and only if
D^{\bullet}I=0
. Similarly, Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL) is equivalent to:
V
is an edge voltage if and only
V=Du
, for some potential
u
.

The following result is well known
Balabanian and Bickart 1969
;
Biggs 1997
;
Stefan 2021
(for a proof see Ref.
Beard 2022
).

Lemma 48

Let
G_{1}=(P_{G_{1}},E_{G_{1}}),\ldots,G_{k}=(P_{G_{k}},E_{G_{k}})
be the connected components of the finite linear digraph
G=(P_{G},E_{G})
. Denote the characteristic function on the node set
P_{G_{i}}
of the graph
G_{i}
by
\chi_{P_{G_{i}}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\chi_{P_{G_{i}}}(x)=\begin{cases}1,\;\text{if}\;x\in P_{G_{i}},\\
0,\;\text{if}\;x\in P_{G}\setminus P_{G_{i}},\end{cases}

(158)

for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Then
\{\chi_{P_{G_{1}}},\ldots,\chi_{P_{G_{k}}}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\ker D
,

\displaystyle\ker D=\operatorname{span}\{\chi_{P_{G_{1}}},\ldots,\chi_{P_{G_{k}}}\},\;\dim\ker D=k,\;1_{P_{G}}\in\ker D.

(159)

In particular,
G
is connected (i.e.,
k=1
) if and only if
\ker D=\operatorname{span}\{1_{P_{G}}\}
.

Definition 49
(Conductivity on electrical networks)

An electrical network is a triple
(P_{G},E_{G},\sigma)
, with graph
G=(P_{G},E_{G})
and operator
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(E_{G},\mathbb{K}))
satisfying
\sigma^{*}=\sigma\geq 0
. We call
\sigma
the network conductivity and we define the Kirchhoff operator
K_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K}))
by

\displaystyle K_{\sigma}=-D^{\bullet}\sigma D.

(160)

In particular, for
\sigma=I
[the identity operator on
\mathcal{F}(E_{G},\mathbb{K})
], the “normalized" Kirchhoff operator is

\displaystyle K_{I}=-D^{\bullet}D.

(161)

Remark 50

The normalized Kirchhoff operator
K_{I}
is the graph-theoretic analog of
-\Delta=-\nabla\cdot\nabla
, i.e., the negative of the Laplacian in the continuum. Similarly, the Kirchhoff operator
K_{\sigma}
(sometimes referred to in the literature as the graph Laplacian) is graph-theoretic analog of
-\nabla\cdot\sigma\nabla
in the continuum. In the matrix setting, the well known
Curtis and Morrow 2000
;
Stefan 2021
Kirchhoff matrix is the matrix representation of the Kirchhoff operator
K_{\sigma}
with respect to the orthonormal bases
\alpha_{P_{G}},\alpha_{E_{G}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}K_{\sigma}\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{E_{G}}}=-\begin{bmatrix}D^{\bullet}\end{bmatrix}_{\alpha_{E_{G}}}^{\alpha_{P_{G}}}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{E_{G}}}\begin{bmatrix}D\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{P_{G}}}=A_{a}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{E_{G}}}A_{a}^{T},

(162)

where
\begin{bmatrix}\sigma\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{E_{G}}}

is the matrix representation of the conductivity
\sigma
, i.e., the conductivity matrix. Typically, in a resistor-only network, the conductivity matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\sigma\end{bmatrix}^{\alpha_{E_{G}}}_{\alpha_{E_{G}}}
is a diagonal matrix. This corresponds to the conductivity
\sigma
being given by

\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{|E_{G}|}\sigma_{ii}\delta_{e_{i}},

(163)

where
\sigma_{ii}
is the conductivity (i.e., the inverse of resistance) on the
i
th edge of the graph
G=(P_{G},E_{G})
.

Let us conclude this section now with the following physical interpretation for an electrical network
(P_{G},E_{G},\sigma)
following Ref.
Balabanian and Bickart 1969
. By KVL, the set of edge voltages of the network (containing no voltage sources) is
\ran D
. Now, any edge voltage
V\in\ran D
in the network can be considered as being generated by a current source
I_{g}\in\mathcal{F}(E_{G},\mathbb{K})
that is balanced by a passive current
I\in\mathcal{F}(E_{G},\mathbb{K})
in the network, i.e., they must satisfy
I-I_{g}\in\ker D^{\bullet}
by KCL, and

I=\sigma V

by Ohm’s law. As
V=Du
, for some potential
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K}),
then it follows that
u
satisfies the equations

K_{\sigma}u=\Phi,

where
\Phi=-D^{\bullet}I_{g}\in\ran D^{\bullet}
is considered as a node current-source since, for each
p\in P_{G},

\Phi(p)=-(D^{\bullet}I_{g})(p)=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E_{G},\\
e_{-}=p\end{subarray}}I_{g}(e)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E_{G},\\
e_{+}=p\end{subarray}}I_{g}(e)

is the algebraic sum of current sources incident at the node
p
(with references chosen so that they enter the node).

Conversely, suppose
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K}).
Then
K_{\sigma}u\in\ran D^{\bullet}
and so
\exists\;I_{g}\in\mathcal{F}(E_{G},\mathbb{K})
such that
K_{\sigma}u=-D^{\bullet}I_{g}.
Define
V=Du
and
I=\sigma V
. Then
V\in\ran D,I\in\mathcal{F}(E_{G},\mathbb{K}),
and
-D^{\bullet}I=-D^{\bullet}\sigma V=K_{\sigma}u=-D^{\bullet}I_{g}
implying
I-I_{g}\in\ker D^{\bullet}
. Thus, according to the interpretation above, the edge voltage
V
in the network can be considered as being generated by the source current
I_{g}
that is balanced by the passive current
I
in the network.

VI.2
Dirichlet-to-Neumann Map

In this subsection, we will use our operator framework (from Sec.
VI.1
) to reformulate an example from Ref.
Milton 2016
(see Ch. 2, Sec. 2.13) on finite linear digraphs and the characterization of their discrete Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) maps in terms of effective operators and a Hodge decomposition associated with the discrete Dirichlet problem. For further background and motivation for the DtN map in this setting, see Refs.
Curtis and Morrow 2000
;
Stefan 2021
, for instance.

Consider an electrical network
(P_{G},E_{G},\sigma)
(see Def.
49
) with the node set
P_{G}
partitioned into two nonempty subsets
P_{\partial G}
and
P_{G^{\circ}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle P_{G}=P_{\partial G}\cup P_{G^{\circ}},\;\;P_{\partial G}\cap P_{G^{\circ}}=\emptyset,\;\;P_{\partial G}\neq\emptyset,\;\;P_{G^{\circ}}\neq\emptyset.

(164)

The boundary nodes and interior nodes for the graph
G=(P_{G},E_{G})
are defined as
P_{\partial G}
and
P_{G^{\circ}}
, respectively.

We will now define the discrete DtN map in terms of the discrete analog of the Dirichlet problem for the conductivity equation in the continuum. To do this, we will need the restriction maps
|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}:\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{G^{\circ}},\mathbb{K})
and
|_{P_{\partial G}}:\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})
which are the bounded linear operators defined by

\displaystyle u|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}(p)=u(p),\;\forall p\in P_{G^{\circ}},

(165)

\displaystyle u|_{P_{\partial G}}(p)=u(p),\;\forall p\in P_{\partial G},

(166)

for each
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
.

Definition 51
(Discrete Dirichlet problem)

The discrete Dirichlet problem is defined as follows: given a function
f\in\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})
, find a function
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
satisfying

\displaystyle(-D^{\bullet}\sigma Du)|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}=0,

(167)

\displaystyle u|_{P_{\partial G}}=f.

(168)

For each such solution
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
, the boundary (source) current
\phi
is defined by

\phi=(-D^{\bullet}\sigma Du)|_{P_{\partial G}}.

(169)

Definition 52
(Dirichlet-to-Neumann map)

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) map is the operator
\Lambda_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K}))
that maps the potential on the boundary
u|_{P_{\partial G}}
for each solution
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
of the discrete Dirichlet problem (
167
) and (
168
) to the boundary current
\phi
(
169
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\sigma}u|_{P_{\partial G}}=\phi.

(170)

VI.2.1
Generalized Schur Complement Representation

In this section we will derive a generalized Schur complement formula for the DtN map
\Lambda_{\sigma}
. To do so, we will need the following. The Hilbert space
H=\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
decomposes into the orthogonal direct sum

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})=H_{0}\ho H_{1},\;H_{0}=\ker(|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}),\;H_{1}=\ker(|_{P_{\partial G}}).

(171)

With respect to this decomposition, the Kirchhoff operator [see Def.
49
] can be written in
2\times 2
block operator form as

\displaystyle K_{\sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}(K_{\sigma})_{00}&(K_{\sigma})_{01}\\
(K_{\sigma})_{10}&(K_{\sigma})_{11}\end{bmatrix},

(172)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(H_{j},H_{i})\ni(K_{\sigma})_{ij}=\Gamma_{H_{i}}K_{\sigma}\Gamma_{H_{j}}:H_{j}\rightarrow H_{i},\;i,j=0,1,

(173)

where
\Gamma_{H_{0}}
and
\Gamma_{H_{1}}
are the orthogonal projections of
\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
onto
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
, respectively.

Next, we define the zero extension maps
\iota_{0}:\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
and
\iota_{1}:\mathcal{F}(P_{G^{\circ}},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
by

\displaystyle[\iota_{0}(f)](p)=\left\{\begin{matrix}f(p)&\text{if }p\in P_{\partial G},\\
0&\text{if }p\in P_{G^{\circ}},\end{matrix}\right.

(174)

\displaystyle[\iota_{1}(g)](p)=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&\text{if }p\in P_{\partial G},\\
g(p)&\text{if }p\in P_{G^{\circ}},\end{matrix}\right.

(175)

for each
f\in\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K}),

g\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G^{\circ}},\mathbb{K}),
and every
p\in P_{G}
.

The fundamental relationship between the restriction and zero extension maps is described in the next lemma.

Lemma 53

The zero extension maps
\iota_{0}:\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
and
\iota_{1}:\mathcal{F}(P_{G^{\circ}},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
[as defined in (
174
) and (
175
)] are bounded linear operators and isometries whose adjoints are the restriction maps
|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}:\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{G^{\circ}},\mathbb{K})
and
|_{P_{\partial G}}:\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})
[as defined in (
165
) and (
166
)]. In particular,

\displaystyle\iota_{0}^{*}=|_{P_{\partial G}},\;\iota_{1}^{*}=|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}.

(176)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\ran\iota_{0}

\displaystyle=H_{0},\;\ran\iota_{1}=H_{1},

(177)

\displaystyle|_{P_{\partial G}}\circ\iota_{0}

\displaystyle=I_{\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})},\;\iota_{0}\circ|_{P_{\partial G}}=\Gamma_{H_{0}},

(178)

\displaystyle|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}\circ\iota_{1}

\displaystyle=I_{\mathcal{F}(P_{G^{\circ}},\mathbb{K})},\;\iota_{1}\circ|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}=\Gamma_{H_{1}},

(179)

\displaystyle\iota_{0}^{+}

\displaystyle=|_{P_{\partial G}},\;\iota_{1}^{+}=|_{P_{G^{\circ}}},

(180)

where
(\cdot)^{+}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse (see Def.
21
).

Proof.
First, it is obvious that the zero extension maps (
174
) and (
175
) are well-defined linear operators which implies they are bounded since the Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
is finite-dimensional. It also follows immediately from their definitions that
\ran\iota_{0}=H_{0},\;\ran\iota_{1}=H_{1}
. Second, for any
f_{1},f_{2},f\in\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})
and
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
we have

\displaystyle(\iota_{0}(f_{1}),\iota_{0}(f_{2}))_{P_{G}}=\sum_{p\in P_{G}}\overline{\iota_{0}(f_{1})(p)}\iota_{0}(f_{2})(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in P_{\partial G}}\overline{f_{1}(p)}f_{2}(p)=(f_{1},f_{2})_{P_{\partial G}},

\displaystyle(u|_{P_{\partial G}},f)_{P_{\partial G}}=\sum_{p\in P_{\partial G}}\overline{u|_{P_{\partial G}}(p)}f(p)=\sum_{p\in P_{\partial G}}\overline{u(p)}f(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in P_{G}}\overline{u(p)}\iota_{0}(f)(p)=(u,\iota_{0}(f))_{P_{G}},

\displaystyle[|_{P_{\partial G}}\circ\iota_{0}(f)](p)=[\iota_{0}(f)|_{P_{\partial G}}](p)

\displaystyle=[\iota_{0}(f)](p)=f(p),\;\forall p\in P_{\partial G},

\displaystyle[\iota_{0}\circ|_{P_{\partial G}}(u)](p)=[\iota_{0}(u|_{P_{\partial G}})](p)=\left\{\begin{matrix}u(p)&\text{if }p\in P_{\partial G},\\
0&\text{if }p\in P_{G^{\circ}},\end{matrix}\right.

\displaystyle=[\Gamma_{H_{0}}(u)](p),\;\forall p\in P_{G},

which proves that
\iota_{0}
is an isometry, the adjoint relations
\iota_{0}^{*}=|_{P_{\partial G}}
hold,
|_{P_{\partial G}}\circ\iota_{0}=I_{\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})},
and
\iota_{0}\circ|_{P_{\partial G}}=\Gamma_{H_{0}}
. It now follows from this with
X=\iota_{0},Y=|_{P_{\partial G}}
that the four Penrose equations
YXY=Y,XYX=X,(YX)^{*}=YX,(XY)^{*}=XY
are satisfied so that
X^{+}=Y
(by Def.
21
). The rest of the proof follows similarly.

Theorem 54

Let
(P_{G},E_{G},\sigma)
be an electrical network (see Def.
49
) with boundary and interior nodes,
P_{\partial G}
and
P_{G^{\circ}}
, respectively [i.e., satisfying (
164
)]. Then

\displaystyle K_{\sigma}^{*}=K_{\sigma}\geq 0,

(181)

the discrete Dirichlet problem (see Def.
51
) has a solution for each
f\in\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})
, and the DtN map
\Lambda_{\sigma}
(see Def.
52
) is given by the formula

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\sigma}=\iota_{0}^{*}K_{\sigma}/(K_{\sigma})_{11}\iota_{0},

(182)

\displaystyle K_{\sigma}/(K_{\sigma})_{11}=(K_{\sigma})_{00}-(K_{\sigma})_{01}(K_{\sigma})_{11}^{+}(K_{\sigma})_{10},

(183)

where
\iota_{0}:\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})\rightarrow H_{0}
is the zero extension map [as defined in (
174
)] and
K_{\sigma}/(K_{\sigma})_{11}
is the generalized Schur complement of the Kirchhoff operator
K_{\sigma}=[(K_{\sigma})_{ij}]_{i,j=0,1}
[as defined in (
160
), (
171
), and (
173
)] with respect to
(K_{\sigma})_{11}
.

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. First, by hypothesis
\sigma^{*}=\sigma\geq 0
and
D^{*}=-D^{\bullet}
by Lemma
46
, hence

\displaystyle K_{\sigma}=-D^{\bullet}\sigma D=D^{*}\sigma D

(184)

from which it follows that
K_{\sigma}^{*}=K_{\sigma}\geq 0
. Next, by Lemma
53
it follows that the discrete Dirichlet problem (Def.
51
) is equivalent to the constrained linear system of equations

\displaystyle K_{\sigma}(\iota_{0}(f)+\iota_{1}(g))=\iota_{0}(\phi),

(185)

\displaystyle u|_{P_{\partial G}}=f,\;u|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}=g,\;(K_{\sigma}u)|_{P_{\partial G}}=\phi,

(186)

or, equivalently, in block form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(K_{\sigma})_{00}&(K_{\sigma})_{01}\\
(K_{\sigma})_{10}&(K_{\sigma})_{11}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\iota_{0}(f)\\
\iota_{1}(g)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\iota_{0}(\phi)\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(187)

Thus, by Proposition
27
, Lemma
24
, and Lemma
53
, the above system is solvable for any

and
\iota_{0}(\phi)
is given in terms of the generalized Schur complement (

\displaystyle\iota_{0}(\phi)=K_{\sigma}/(K_{\sigma})_{11}\iota_{0}(f)

(188)

from which it follows by (

) and (
178
) in Lemma

VI.3.2
Relationship to the DtN map

The goal of this section is to derive a representation of the effective conductivity
\sigma_{eff}
of the electrical network
(P_{G},E_{G},\sigma)
with respect to distinct nodes
p,q\in P_{G}
in terms of the DtN map
\Lambda_{\sigma}
as introduced in Sec.
VI.2
when the boundary nodes
P_{\partial G}
and interior nodes
P_{G^{\circ}}
are defined to be

\displaystyle P_{\partial G}=\{p,q\},\;P_{G^{\circ}}=P_{G}\setminus P_{\partial G},

(237)

and assuming that
\{p,q\}\subsetneq P_{G}
.

This goal is achieved with the following theorem.

Theorem 70

Let
(P_{G},E_{G},\sigma)
be an electrical network. If
p,q\in P_{G}
with
p\not=q
and
\{p,q\}\subsetneq P_{G}
then the effective conductivity
\sigma_{eff}=\sigma_{eff}(p,q)
(see Def.
63
) and the DtN map
\Lambda_{\sigma}
[with boundary and interior nodes defined by (
237
)] (see Def.
52
) are related by the formula

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\sigma}(\cdot)=\sigma_{eff}((\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}},\cdot)_{\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})}(\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}}.

(238)

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. First, Theorems
67
and
54
give us existence and uniqueness of both the effective conductivity
\sigma_{eff}
and the DtN map
\Lambda_{\sigma}
for this electrical network
(P_{G},E_{G},\sigma)
with boundary and interior nodes (
237
). Next, let
f\in\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})
. Then by Theorem
54
there exists a solution
u\in\mathcal{F}(P_{G},\mathbb{K})
to the discrete Dirichlet problem (see Def.
51
):

\displaystyle(K_{\sigma}u)|_{P_{G^{\circ}}}=0,\;u|_{P_{\partial G}}=f,

and

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\sigma}(f)=(K_{\sigma}u)|_{P_{\partial G}}.

Now it follows from this and the two facts

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})=\operatorname{span}\{\delta_{p}|_{P_{\partial G}},\delta_{q}|_{P_{\partial G}}\},

\displaystyle\;1_{P_{G}}|_{P_{\partial G}}\in\ker\Lambda_{\sigma}=(\operatorname{ran}\Lambda_{\sigma})^{\perp},

that we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\sigma}(f)=j\delta_{p}|_{P_{\partial G}}-j\delta_{q}|_{P_{\partial G}}=j(\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}},

for some scalar
j\in\mathbb{K}
, which implies that

\displaystyle K_{\sigma}u=j(\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}}.

Hence, by this and Theorem
67
, we have

\displaystyle j=\sigma_{eff}[u(p)-u(q)].

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\sigma}(f)=j(\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}}=\sigma_{eff}[u(p)-u(q)](\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{eff}((\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}},f)_{\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})}(\delta_{p}-\delta_{q})|_{P_{\partial G}}.

Therefore, as this is true for all
f\in\mathcal{F}(P_{\partial G},\mathbb{K})
, this proves the theorem.

VI.4
Conductivity for Periodic Linear Graphs

In this section, we study the discrete analog of the continuum electrical conductivity problem and effective conductivity
\sigma_{*}
(cf.  Example
1
) for periodic linear graphs. Although we are still in a discrete setting, we must treat new complications that arise when one moves from finite linear graphs to infinite linear graphs.

In order to gain a clear insight into this transition, we only consider Cartesian graphs. This is also motivated by Refs.
Murphy et al. 2015
;
Bruno and Golden 1990
, which study the effective conductivity in lattice networks, but use analytic and probabilistic methods from Ref.
Golden and Papanicolaou 1983
as opposed to the Hilbert space approach that we are advocating for in our paper. Nevertheless, we believe that our methods are complementary to theirs and as such may find applications in the theory of composites for lattice networks.

Now there are some similarities and some changes that occur when transitioning from Example
1
and Section
VI.1
. First, we continue to use the function spaces
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})
, but now they are infinite-dimensional vector spaces without an inner product. Also, the gradient
D
and divergence
D^{\bullet}
are still well defined linear operators, but we lose their adjoint relationship. Instead, we replace these with their periodic counterparts
\mathcal{F}_{\#}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})
,
D_{\#}
, and
(D^{\bullet})_{\#}
. Now, the function space
\mathcal{F}_{\#}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{F}_{\#}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})}
and we have the adjoint relationship
D_{\#}^{*}=-(D^{\bullet})_{\#}
. From this we are able to get a Hodge decomposition and an associated
Z
-problem in this setting, in analogy with the continuum problem under these periodic spaces and operators. In addition, we obtain the analogous relation that the effective conductivity is the effective operator
\sigma_{*}
under certain additional hypotheses, but without which there are some interesting nuances that we discuss.

Lemma 73

\displaystyle f|_{\mathcal{S}}(s)=f(s),\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(252)

In particular, the following vector spaces are isomorphic:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S},\mathbb{K})\cong\mathcal{F}_{\#}(\mathcal{T},\mathbb{K})\cong\mathbb{K}^{|\mathcal{S}|}.

71
.
(b)

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{S}+L

and so with

and

(295)

This implies

a contradiction. This proves the claim.

Nevertheless, in this example there is a unique effective operator
\sigma_{*}
of the lattice
Z
-problem
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{J},\sigma)
(in Def.
81
) by Theorem

.

Theorem 91

The sets
\mathcal{U}_{\#},\;\mathcal{E}_{\#},\;\mathcal{J}_{\#}

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\#}

\displaystyle=\{Du\in\Hone:u\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\mathbb{K}),D^{\bullet}Du=0\},

(296)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\#}

(297)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\#}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{ran}D\cap\Hone)^{\perp}

(298)

are mutually orthogonal subspaces in the Hilbert space
\Hone

\displaystyle\Hone=\mathcal{U}_{\#}\overset{\perp}{\oplus}\mathcal{E}_{\#}\ho\mathcal{J}_{\#},

(299)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ran}D\cap\Hone=\mathcal{U}_{\#}\ho\mathcal{E}_{\#},

(300)

\displaystyle\ker D^{\bullet}\cap\Hone=\ker(D^{\bullet})_{\#}=\mathcal{U}_{\#}\ho\mathcal{J}_{\#},

(301)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\#}=\mathcal{U}\ho(\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D),

(302)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\#}=\mathcal{E}=\operatorname{ran}D_{\#},

(303)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\#}=\mathcal{J}\overset{\perp}{\ominus}(\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D).

(304)

Proof.
First, (
303
) follows immediately from the definition of
D_{\#}
and by Theorem
80
. Next, we will prove (
302
). First, let
J\in\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D
. Then
J\in\mathcal{J}
so that
(D^{\bullet})_{\#}J=0,\langle J\rangle_{\Hone}=0,
and there exists

. This proves that
\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\#}.
Next, by Lemma
84
we know that
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\ran D\cap\Hone
and by Theorem
80
we know that
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\ker(D^{\bullet})
which implies
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\#}.
This together with
\mathcal{U}\perp\mathcal{J}
(by Theorem

) proves that
\mathcal{U}\ho(\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D)\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\#}.
To prove the reverse inclusion, let
V\in\mathcal{U}_{\#}.
Then
V=Du
for some

and
(D^{\bullet})_{\#}V=D^{\bullet}Du=0
. By Theorem

so that

for some

. As
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\#}\subseteq\operatorname{ran}D
, this implies that

and hence

. Thus,

. This proves that
\mathcal{U}_{\#}\subseteq\mathcal{U}\ho(\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D)
, which proves (
302
). Next, we prove (
300
). First, it is clear from the definitions of
\mathcal{U}_{\#}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\#}
that

. We also know by Theorem
80
that
\mathcal{U}\perp\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{J}\perp\mathcal{E}
so by (
302
) and (
303
) it follows that
\mathcal{U}_{\#}\perp\mathcal{E}_{\#}
and hence
\mathcal{U}_{\#}\ho\mathcal{E}_{\#}\subseteq\operatorname{ran}D\cap\Hone
. To prove the reverse inclusion, let

300
) and the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{\#}
. Now, (

\displaystyle\Hone=\mathcal{U}\ho\mathcal{E}\ho\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle=[\mathcal{U}\ho(\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D)]\ho\mathcal{E}\ho[\mathcal{J}\overset{\perp}{\ominus}(\mathcal{J}\cap\operatorname{ran}D)]

Finally, (

), and (

Theorem 92

Let
(\mathbb{Z}^{d},E,\sigma)
be a lattice electrical network with conductivity
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\Hone)
satisfying
\sigma^{*}=\sigma\geq 0
. Let
\sigma_{*}
be the effective operator of the lattice
Z
-problem
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{J},\sigma)
(in Def.
81
) with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\Hone
and orthogonal triple decomposition of
\mathcal{H}
in (
269

\sigma_{*_{\#}}
be the effective operator of the

-problem
(\mathcal{H}_{\#},\mathcal{U}_{\#},\mathcal{E}_{\#},\mathcal{J}_{\#},\sigma)
with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\#}=\Hone
and orthogonal triple decomposition of
\mathcal{H}_{\#}
in (
299
). Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}=\Gamma_{0}\sigma_{*_{\#}}\Gamma_{0}|_{\mathcal{U}},

(305)

where
\Gamma_{0}
is the (edge cell) average operator (in Def.
79
), i.e.,
\sigma_{*}
is the compression of
\sigma_{*_{\#}}
to
\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. Then
\sigma^{*}=\sigma\geq 0
and hence by Corollary
33

\sigma_{*}
and
\sigma_{*_{\#}}
. We will now prove (
305

Let
V_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
. Then, by Corollary
33
, there exists a
(I_{0},V,I)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{J}
such that

\displaystyle I_{0}+I=\sigma(V_{0}+V)

and

\displaystyle I_{0}=\sigma_{*}V_{0}.

On the other hand,
V\in\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\#}
and, by Theorem
91
,
V_{0},I_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{\#}
and
(I_{\mathcal{H}}-\Gamma_{2}^{\#})I\in\mathcal{U}_{\#}\overset{\perp}{\ominus}\mathcal{U}
, where
\Gamma_{2}^{\#}

\mathcal{H}_{\#}=\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{J}_{\#}

\displaystyle[I_{0}+(I_{\mathcal{H}}-\Gamma_{2}^{\#})I]+\Gamma_{2}^{\#}I=\sigma(V_{0}+V),

Appendix A
Abstract Theory of Composites for Vector Spaces

In this section, we give a concise and self-contained presentation of the most fundamental aspects of the abstract theory of composites on vector spaces that we need in this paper. In particular, we do not assume here that the vector spaces are inner product spaces and as such need not be Hilbert spaces. This level of generality was first considered in Chapter 7 in Ref.
Milton 2016
and this appendix can be considered supplementary to it.

We begin by introducing some notation. Let
V,W
be two vector spaces over a field
\mathbb{K}
. Let

denote the vector space (over
\mathbb{K}
) of all the linear functions from

to
W
[when

, we abbreviate it by

i.e.,

]. Now suppose

has a direct sum decomposition

\displaystyle V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}\oplus V_{2},

(306)

for some subspaces
V_{0},V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq V
and
W_{0},W_{1},W_{2}\subseteq W
. For each

let
\Gamma_{V_{i}}\in L(V)
denote the projection of
V
onto
V_{i}
along

where

with
j<k
. More precisely, since

, then
\Gamma_{V_{i}}
is uniquely defined by
\Gamma_{V_{i}}(v)=v
if

and
\Gamma_{V_{i}}(v)=v

Suppose that

also has a direct sum decomposition

\displaystyle W=W_{0}\oplus W_{1}\oplus W_{2}.

(307)

Then every

can be written as a

block operator matrix, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma=[\sigma_{ij}]_{i,j=0,1,2}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{00}&\sigma_{01}&\sigma_{02}\\
\sigma_{10}&\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{20}&\sigma_{21}&\sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix},

(308)

with respect to the decompositions (

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}\in\mathcal{L}(V_{j},W_{i}),\;\sigma_{ij}=\Gamma_{W_{i}}\sigma\Gamma_{V_{j}}:V_{j}\rightarrow W_{i},

for

.

Definition 93
(

-problem and effective operator)

The

-problem

\displaystyle(V,V_{0},V_{1},V_{2},W,W_{0},W_{1},W_{2},\sigma),

(310)

is the following problem associated with two vector spaces
V,W
(over a field

(312)

such a tuple
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})\in W_{0}\times V_{1}\times W_{2}
called a solution of the
Z
-problem at
v_{0}
.
If there exists a function
\sigma_{*}
such that

(313)

whenever
v_{0}\in V_{0}
and
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})
is a solution of the

-problem at
v_{0}
, then
\sigma_{*}

Z
-problem.

Remark 94

In our definition and notation, the equation
(\ref{DefZProbEqAppendix})
is equivalent to the system

\displaystyle\sigma_{00}v_{0}+\sigma_{01}v_{1}=w_{0},

(314)

\displaystyle\sigma_{10}v_{0}+\sigma_{11}v_{1}=0,

(315)

(316)

In particular, it follows that if

is invertible, then we have the following formulas for the solution of the

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{00}&\sigma_{01}\\
\sigma_{10}&\sigma_{11}\end{bmatrix}/\sigma_{11}=\sigma_{00}-\sigma_{01}\sigma_{11}^{-1}\sigma_{10}.

(318)

Thus, the following theorem follows.

Theorem 95

If
(V,V_{0},V_{1},V_{2},W,W_{0},W_{1},W_{2},\sigma)

\sigma_{11}
[as defined by (

).

The next theorem and corollary provides the necessary and sufficient conditions for an effective operator of a

Theorem 96
(Fundamental theorem of effective operators)

Let
(V,V_{0},V_{1},V_{2},W,W_{0},W_{1},W_{2},\sigma)
be a
Z
-problem. Define the set
D_{*}(\sigma)\subseteq V_{0}
by

\displaystyle D_{*}(\sigma)=\{v_{0}\in V_{0}:\text{a solution to the $Z$-problem at $v_{0}$ exists}\}.

(319)

Then
D_{*}(\sigma)
is the inverse image of
\sigma_{10}
on the range of
\sigma_{11},
i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{*}(\sigma)=\sigma_{10}^{-1}(\ran\sigma_{11}),

(320)

and, in particular, is a subspace of
V_{0}
.
Furthermore, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\ker\sigma_{11}\subseteq\ker\sigma_{01}.

(b)

There exists a function
f:D_{*}(\sigma)\rightarrow W_{0}
such that

\displaystyle w_{0}=f(v_{0})

(321)

whenever
v_{0}\in V_{0}
and
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})
is a solution of the
Z
-problem at
v_{0}
.

(c)

If
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})
is a solution of the
Z
-problem at
v_{0}=0
then
w_{0}=0
.

Moreover, if any of the statements
(a)
,
(b)
, or
(c)
is true then an effective operator
\sigma_{*}
of the
Z
-problem exists, is unique, equals the function
f:D_{*}(\sigma)\rightarrow W_{0}
in
(b)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}=f,

(322)

and
\sigma_{*}
is linear, that is,

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}\in L(D_{*}(\sigma),W_{0}).

(323)

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. First, we prove the equality (
320
). Let
v_{0}\in D_{*}(\sigma)
. Then there exists a triple
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})\in W_{0}\times V_{1}\times W_{2}
which is a solution of the
Z
-problem at
v_{0}
. This implies that
\sigma_{10}v_{0}+\sigma_{11}v_{1}=0
so that
v_{0}\in\sigma_{10}^{-1}(\{\sigma_{11}(-v_{1})\})\in\sigma_{10}^{-1}(\ran\sigma_{11})
. This proves
D_{*}(\sigma)\subseteq\sigma_{10}^{-1}(\ran\sigma_{11})
. Conversely, suppose
v_{0}\in\sigma_{10}^{-1}(\ran\sigma_{11})
. Then there exists
v_{1}\in V_{1}
such that
\sigma_{10}v_{0}=\sigma_{11}(-v_{1}),
that is,
\sigma_{10}v_{0}+\sigma_{11}v_{1}=0
. Then defining
w_{0},w_{2}
by
w_{0}=\sigma_{00}v_{0}+\sigma_{01}v_{1}\in W_{0}
and
w_{2}=\sigma_{20}v_{0}+\sigma_{21}v_{1}\in W_{2}
it follows that
v_{0}\in V_{0},(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})\in W_{0}\times V_{1}\times W_{2}
and
\sigma(v_{0}+v_{1})=w_{0}+w_{2},
i.e.,
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})
is a solution to the
Z
-problem at
v_{0}
. Hence,
v_{0}\in D_{*}(\sigma).
This proves
\sigma_{10}^{-1}(\ran\sigma_{11})\subseteq D_{*}(\sigma)
. Thus, we have proven equality (
320
). Next, it follows immediately from this and the linearity of
\sigma_{10}
and
\sigma_{11}
that
D_{*}(\sigma)
is a subspace of
V_{0}
.

We will now prove
(a)\Rightarrow(b)\Rightarrow(c)\Rightarrow(a)
. Suppose that
(a)
is true. Let us prove
(b)
. Let
v_{0}\in D_{*}(\sigma)
and suppose there exists
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2}),(w_{0}^{\prime},v_{1}^{\prime},w_{2}^{\prime})\in W_{0}\times V_{1}\times W_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\sigma(v_{0}+v_{1})=w_{0}+w_{2},\;\sigma(v_{0}+v_{1}^{\prime})=w_{0}^{\prime}+w_{2}^{\prime}.

Then by linearity of
\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma(0+v_{1}-v_{1}^{\prime})=w_{0}-w_{0}^{\prime}+w_{2}-w_{2}^{\prime}

so that
(w_{0}-w_{0}^{\prime},v_{1}-v_{1}^{\prime},w_{2}-w_{2}^{\prime})\in W_{0}\times V_{1}\times W_{2}
is a solution of the
Z
-problem at
0
. From this, it follows that

\displaystyle\sigma_{11}(v_{1}-v_{1}^{\prime})=0,\;\sigma_{01}(v_{1}-v_{1}^{\prime})=w_{0}-w_{0}^{\prime}.

By
(a)
, this implies that
0=\sigma_{01}(v_{1}-v_{1}^{\prime})=w_{0}-w_{0}^{\prime}
so that
w_{0}=w_{0}^{\prime}
. This proves that the function
f:D_{*}(\sigma)\rightarrow W_{0}
defined by
w_{0}=f(v_{0})
whenever
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})
is a solution of the
Z
-problem at
v_{0}
, is well-defined. This proves
(b)
.

Now we will prove
(b)
implies
(c)
. Suppose
(b)
is true. If
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})
is a solution of the
Z
-problem at
v_{0}=0
then by
(b)
we must have
f(0)=w_{0},
but we also know that
(0,0,0)
is a solution of the
Z
-problem at
0
so by
(b)
we must have
f(0)=0
and hence
w_{0}=0
. This proves
(c)
.

Next, we will prove
(c)
implies
(a)
. Suppose
(c)
is true. Let
v_{1}\in\ker\sigma_{11}
. Then
0=\sigma_{11}(v_{1})=\Gamma_{W_{1}}\sigma\Gamma_{V_{1}}(v_{1})=\Gamma_{W_{1}}\sigma(v_{1})
. This implies
\sigma(v_{1})=w_{0}+w_{2}
for some

(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})

Z
-problem at

(a)

Therefore, we have proven
(a)\Rightarrow(b)\Rightarrow(c)\Rightarrow(a)

,
(b)
, and
(c)
are equivalent.

To prove the remaining statements, assume that
(a)
,
(b)
, or
(c)
is true. Then they are all true as we just showed. Let

be any function with the properties described in
(b)

(b)

and there exists a solution
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2})
of the

. This proves

\sigma_{*}=f
. We now prove that
\sigma_{*}:D_{*}(\sigma)\rightarrow W_{0}
is linear, i.e.,
\sigma_{*}\in L(D_{*}(\sigma),W_{0})
. Let

and

. Then there exists
(w_{0},v_{1},w_{2}),(v_{0}^{\prime},v_{1}^{\prime},w_{2}^{\prime})\in W_{0}\times V_{1}\times W_{2}

\displaystyle\sigma(v_{0}+v_{1})=w_{0}+w_{2},\;\sigma(v_{0}^{\prime}+v_{1}^{\prime})=w_{0}^{\prime}+w_{2}^{\prime},

and hence

. Thus, by linearity of
\sigma
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sigma((cv_{0}+v_{0}^{\prime})+(cv_{1}+v_{1}^{\prime}))=(cw_{0}+w_{0}^{\prime})+(cw_{2}+w_{2}^{\prime})

is a solution of the

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}(cv_{0}+v_{0}^{\prime})=cw_{0}+w_{0}^{\prime}=c\sigma_{*}(v_{0})+\sigma_{*}(v_{0}^{\prime}).

This proves that

Definition 97
(Hilbert space orthogonal

(V,V_{0},V_{1},V_{2},W,W_{0},W_{1},W_{2},\sigma),

Appendix B
Abstract Hodge Decompositions

In this section, we adapt, simplify, and extend results of Ref.
Lim 2020
from matrices to linear operators. These results can be utilized when identifying the orthogonal triple decomposition needed for
Z
-problems and their effective operators. We only consider finite-dimensional inner product spaces over the same field
\mathbb{K}
, where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
(hence real or complex Hilbert spaces, respectively).

To begin, we need the following well known results
Friedberg, Insel, and Spence 2019
, which we state without proofs.

Lemma 100

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
be finite-dimensional inner product spaces,
U\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}),
and
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})
. Then

(a)

\mathcal{B}=\ker U^{*}\ho\ran U
,

(b)

\mathcal{B}=\ran T^{*}\ho\ker T
.

Using the next lemma we will easily be able prove the next theorem.

Lemma 101

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
be finite-dimensional inner product spaces. If
U\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})

satisfy

\displaystyle TU=0\;(i.e.,\;U^{*}T^{*}=0)

(332)

then

(a)

\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})=\ker T\cap\ker U^{*}
,

(b)

\ker T=\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})\ho\ran U
.

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses.

(a)

Clearly,
\ker T\cap\ker U^{*}\subseteq\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})
. It remains to show
\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})\subseteq\ker T\cap\ker U^{*}
. Let
x\in\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})
. Then

\displaystyle 0=(T^{*}Tx+UU^{*}x,x)=(T^{*}Tx,x)+(UU^{*}x,x)

\displaystyle=\|Tx\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}}+\|U^{*}x\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}

which implies
x\in\ker T\cap\ker U^{*}
. This proves
\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})\subseteq\ker T\cap\ker U^{*}
, which proves
(a)
.

(b)

Next, we claim that

\displaystyle\ker T=(\ker T\cap\ker U^{*})\ho\ran(U).

To prove this, notice first that it is obvious that

(\ker T\cap\ker U^{*})\ho[\ran(U)\cap\ker(T)]\subseteq\ker T.

To prove the reverse inclusion, let
x\in\ker T
. Then by Lemma (
100
).
(a)
,
x=a+b
for some
a\in\ker(U^{*})
and
b\in\ran(U)
. Since
\ran(U)\subseteq\ker(T)
[by (
332
)], we have that
b\in\ker(T)
. But then
a=x-b\in\ker(T)
as well, so
a\in\ker T\cap\ker U^{*}
and
b\in\ran(U)\cap\ker(T)
. This proves

\displaystyle\ker T

\displaystyle\subseteq(\ker T\cap\ker U^{*})\ho[\ran(U)\cap\ker(T)].

From this and since
\ran(U)\subseteq\ker(T)
[by (
332
)], the proof of the claim follows. The proof of
(b)
now follows immediately from this and
(a)
.

This completes the proof.

We now have all the necessary tools to prove the abstract Hodge decomposition theorem.

Theorem 102
(Abstract Hodge decomposition)

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
be finite-dimensional inner product spaces. If
U\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
and
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})

satisfy

\displaystyle TU=0\;\;(i.e.,\;U^{*}T^{*}=0)

(333)

then

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\operatorname{ran}T^{*}\ho\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})\ho\operatorname{ran}U.

(334)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\ran(T^{*}T+UU^{*})=\ran T^{*}\ho\ran U,

(335)

\displaystyle\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})=\ker T\cap\ker U^{*}.

(336)

Proof.
Assume the hypotheses. By Lemma
100
.
(b)
together with Lemma
101
.
(b)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\ran T^{*}\ho\ker T=\ran T^{*}\ho\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})\ho\ran U.

Furthermore, by Lemma
101
.
(a)
we have

\displaystyle\ran(T^{*}T+UU^{*})=[\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})]^{\perp}

\displaystyle=(\ker T\cap\ker U^{*})^{\perp}=\ran T^{*}\ho\ran U.

This completes the proof.

Definition 103

We call the decomposition (
334
) an (abstract) Hodge decomposition.

Orthogonal projections associated with abstract Hodge decompositions play a key role in this paper and so the following corollary is complementary to Theorem
102
.

Corollary 104

Suppose
\mathcal{B}
has an abstract Hodge decomposition (
334
). Let
\Gamma_{\ran T^{*}},\Gamma_{\ran U},
and
\Gamma_{\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})}
denote the orthogonal projections of
\mathcal{B}
onto
\ran T^{*},\ran U,
and
\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*}),
respectively. Then

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ran T^{*}}=T^{+}T,\;\Gamma_{\ran U}=UU^{+},

(337)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ker(T^{*}T+UU^{*})}=I_{\mathcal{B}}-\Gamma_{\ran T^{*}}-\Gamma_{\ran U},

(338)

where
(\cdot)^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse and
I_{\mathcal{B}}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.
This follows immediately from the abstract Hodge decomposition (
334
) of
\mathcal{B}
and the fundamental properties of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse [see statements
(4)
and
(5)
in Lemma
23
].
