Inexact Unlearning Needs More Careful Evaluations to Avoid a False Sense of Privacy
Jamie Hayes, Ilia Shumailov, Eleni Triantafillou, Amr Khalifa, Nicolas Papernot

Abstract
The high cost of model training makes it increasingly desirable to develop techniques for unlearning. These techniques seek to remove the influence of a training example without having to retrain the model from scratch. Intuitively, once a model has unlearned, an adversary that interacts with the model should no longer be able to tell whether the unlearned example was included in the model’s training set or not. In the privacy literature, this is known as membership inference. In this work, we discuss adaptations of Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs) to the setting of unlearning (leading to their “U-MIA” counterparts). We propose a categorization of existing U-MIAs into “population U-MIAs”, where the same attacker is instantiated for all examples, and “per-example U-MIAs”, where a dedicated attacker is instantiated for each example. We show that the latter category, wherein the attacker tailors its membership prediction to each example under attack, is significantly stronger. Indeed, our results show that the commonly used U-MIAs in the unlearning literature overestimate the privacy protection afforded by existing unlearning techniques on both vision and language models. Our investigation reveals a large variance in the vulnerability of different examples to per-example U-MIAs. In fact, several unlearning algorithms lead to a reduced vulnerability for some, but not all, examples that we wish to unlearn, at the expense of increasing it for other examples. Notably, we find that the privacy protection for the remaining training examples may worsen as a consequence of unlearning. We also discuss the fundamental difficulty of equally protecting all examples using existing unlearning schemes, due to the different rates at which different examples are unlearned. We demonstrate that naive attempts at tailoring unlearning stopping criteria to different examples fail to alleviate these issues.



1
Introduction

The final model parameters of large deep neural networks are the result of a highly complex, nonlinear learning procedure that operates on the training set.
Tracing (and defining) the influence of an individual’s data on the final model parameters is an open research problem, and one that has important consequences for privacy.
However, there is a simple solution to remove the influence of an individual’s data on the learned model parameters: remove the individual’s data from the training set and retrain the model.
Unfortunately, for many use cases this is prohibitively inefficient from both a time and financial perspective; for example, GPT-4 is estimated to have cost over
\$100M
to train
[
22
]
.

Instead,
inexact machine unlearning
has emerged as a research field that tries to approximate this gold standard solution whilst remaining cost and time efficient for the data controller.
How one measures the quality of such an approximation is an open research problem.
Yet, without such a quality measure, inexact unlearning will likely not be adopted for privacy applications because it either does not come with formal guarantees or the techniques do not scale – their computational footprint being larger than retraining the model (as discussed by
Kurmanji et al. [24]
).

To address this, many works
[
24
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
21
,
23
,
15
,
27
]
have proposed to measure unlearning success through a game that instantiates an adversary. This attacker tries to predict if a model that had initially learned from an individual’s data was then (setting A) retrained without this individual’s data or (setting B)
unlearned
that individual’s data through inexact machine unlearning.
The astute reader will note that this attack is very close to a
membership inference attack

[
33
]
.
However, building on
Kurmanji et al. [24]
and
Pawelczyk et al. [31]
, we find that membership inference attacks designed for privacy need to be adapted to be a useful metric for evaluating inexact unlearning.
This is because setting B is not identical to having trained on the point, which is what standard membership inference attacks would typically consider.
To avoid ambiguity, we will refer to an attack that was adapted to the evaluation of unlearning as an unlearning membership inference attack, or U-MIA for short, in contrast to a standard membership inference attack (MIA).

If the adversary mounting the U-MIA fails to distinguish between settings A and B, then the approximation achieved by the unlearning technique is a good one; ultimately if the distribution of models that were unlearned and retrained are identical, then the optimal attack defaults to random guessing.
However, in practice most of the U-MIAs considered in the literature are sub-optimal.
We argue that for this reason, historically, machine unlearning algorithms have a false sense of privacy due to benchmarking against sub-optimal U-MIAs.
If the attack is far from optimal, the failure of the attack has little to say on the success of the machine unlearning approximation.

We construct this argument by showing that when one implements a stronger U-MIA, many prior work’s unlearning algorithms produce models that are easily distinguishable from a retrained model, and so are less private than previously thought.
We discuss how to construct U-MIAs that are more successful against a broad class of machine unlearning algorithms.
At the heart of it, inexact machine unlearning algorithms implicitly or explicitly optimize for some average-case metric of unlearning.
Hence, we obtain stronger U-MIAs by devising an attacker that creates decision rules on a sample by sample basis.
Upon experimenting with this stronger attack, we find that existing unlearning algorithms that optimize for average-case metrics leave a large set of datapoints vulnerable to per-sample U-MIAs.
We observe a similar difference in attack success for unlearning algorithms on both vision (ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10) and language models (PaLM 2
[
14
]
); U-MIAs designed to construct membership decision rules on a sample-by-sample basis reduce, or altogether remove, the privacy protections claimed by many machine unlearning algorithms.

What’s more, we show that inexact unlearning can have nefarious consequences for individuals whose data is also in the training set but who did not request that their data be unlearned.
Specifically, we show that many inexact machine unlearning algorithms increase the potential for privacy leakage of datapoints remaining in the training set after unlearning has concluded.
For example, on the two aforementioned state-of-the-art unlearning algorithms
[
24
,
21
]
, there is an
increase
in privacy leakage after unlearning on over half of the training datapoints not selected for unlearning.
This creates a tension between notions of privacy at the individual and population level; by giving individuals control of their data, unlearning can jeopardize privacy defined at the population level.

Without a true reflection of the level of protection unlearning provides, it is extremely difficult to quantify and assess the level of progress this research field has made.
Ultimately, we encourage the unlearning community to adopt more formal adversary definitions and threat models, so one can more clearly understand when U-MIAs are sufficiently powerful in the threat model of interest – meaning they can be employed as measurements of unlearning success.
Moving forward, one concrete direction that the community could consider is to derive formal bounds on the success of U-MIAs, as done for MIAs
[
37
]
.

Contributions.

•

We formulate the membership inference game on machine unlearning and extensively evaluate the privacy protection of different unlearning algorithms using a strong per-sample U-MIA on both vision classifiers and language models.

•

We show that weaker population U-MIAs commonly used in the unlearning literature substantially overestimate the privacy protection of unlearning algorithms compared to a per-example U-MIA.

•

We find large variance in terms of the vulnerability of different forget set examples to U-MIAs. Different unlearning algorithms lead to reducing that vulnerability of some (but not all) forget set examples, sometimes at the expense of increasing it for other examples.

•

For many unlearning algorithms, we also find that the privacy of several retain set examples deteriorates during unlearning. We encourage the community to consider this aspect when designing algorithms in the future.

•

We investigate and discuss why defending against a strong per-example U-MIA is difficult, and identify factors including sources of variance during optimization (even simple mini-batch ordering) that largely affect the vulnerability of different examples after unlearning.

•

We observe that different examples are unlearned at different rates. Therefore, lacking a procedure for selecting per-example hyperparameters and stopping criteria for the unlearning procedure, it is fundamentally challenging to equally protect all examples from membership inference attacks. We demonstrate empirically that a naive attempt at achieving this fails at improving privacy protection.

•

We conclude with a discussion of the difficulty of constructing U-MIAs that are optimal against all unlearning methods, and make recommendations for the community in light of these insights.

Background on machine unlearning

We concentrate on studying unlearning for deep neural networks. This is for two reasons.
First, within the literature, deep neural networks are the most commonly studied target model architectures for unlearning techniques.
Second, shallow models are likely to be easier to retrain; there is less of a need for unlearning in settings where models are cheap to train.
At a high level, unlearning techniques for deep neural networks can be separated into two classes of algorithms:
exact
and
inexact
. Exact machine unlearning provides guarantees of removal of training data influence from trained models, but is often costly or difficult to implement. Inexact unlearning addresses these limitations at the expense of not providing a formal guarantee that the model has unlearned.

Exact unlearning.

Exact machine unlearning schemes broadly all trade-off an exact or quantifiable guarantee of data influence removal for a decline on some other desirable property such as overall model accuracy in the small training set size regime
[
3
,
41
,
1
]
or restriction to a subclass of machine learning models, e.g., convex models
[
18
,
25
]
.

For example,
Bourtoule et al. [3]
propose an exact unlearning scheme based on sharding a training set into disjoint subgroups, training models on these subgroups in isolation, and then ensembling predictions at inference time.
When an unlearning request is sent, only the model trained on the subgroup that the request belonged to has to be retrained.
This saves on compute, as other models do not need retraining, and is exact – the unlearning scheme produces models that are from the same distribution as models that never observed the unlearned data during training.

Yan et al. [41]
iterated on this approach by splitting the training set via classes instead of uniformly, showing a smaller gap in accuracy compared to a model trained on the entire training set.

As discussed by
Bourtoule et al. [3]
, these schemes assume the size of the training dataset is large enough to ensure sufficiently accurate models can be trained on partitions of the training set.
Such an approach for modern ML models that are costly to train and run inference on is likely to be extremely challenging.
Unless the ensembles that are trained are significantly smaller, retraining and inference costs could be prohibitively expensive.
Furthermore, in the worst case where the samples that are to be unlearned are distributed over a large fraction of the ensemble, the retraining cost could be similar to retraining a single model on the entire dataset.

Inexact unlearning.

Aside from
Golatkar et al. [11]
,
Neel et al. [29]
,
Gupta et al. [16]
, who introduce upper bounds to the amount of information retained by the unlearned model but do not scale
[
24
]
,
techniques for inexact machine unlearning do not come with rigorous and well understood guarantees for data removal.
Hence, they usually rely on experimental evidence that the samples that have been unlearned are no longer vulnerable to privacy attacks.
However, these unlearning techniques are usually much cheaper to run in comparison to exact unlearning schemes.
A number of inexact unlearning algorithms have been proposed over the last few years
[
24
,
10
,
11
,
15
,
21
]
, we defer a detailed discussion of these methods to
Section

4.1
.
Our work is focused on measuring how well these inexact unlearning methods perform with respect to their claims. Next, we set out the terminology and definitions we will use throughout the paper.

2.1
Terminology

Let
D_{\text{train}}=\{z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}\}
be a training dataset with
n
examples, sampled from an underlying data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, where
z_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
such that
x_{i}
denotes a vector of input features and
y_{i}
denotes a target.
Note that we do not restrict ourselves to the supervised setting where, for instance, the target would be a class label for a classification problem.
We also experiment with large language models for generating text, in which case the target is the next token to be predicted.
Let
f(\cdot;\theta)
denote a neural network parameterized by trainable parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\theta^{o}
denote the parameters output by a learning algorithm
A
, which takes as input a dataset (along with side information such as a model architecture and hyperparameters of the learning algorithm), and outputs model parameters.
Throughout this paper we consider the learning algorithm
A
to be the procedure of optimizing
f(\cdot;\theta)
on
D_{\text{train}}
through gradient descent with respect to a loss function
L
:
\theta^{o}=\argmin_{\theta}\nicefrac{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(z_{i};\theta)}}{{n}}
.

We refer to
f(\cdot;\theta^{o})
as the
original model
, in other words, the model before any unlearning is performed.
Then
D_{\text{train}}
is decomposed into two disjoint subsets,
D_{f}
and
D_{r}
.

D_{f}
denotes the set of examples that are to be unlearned, which we call the
forget set
, and the remaining examples form the
retain set

D_{r}
.
An unlearning algorithm
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, takes as input a set of weights, a set of datapoints to be unlearned, and relevant side-information (denoted by
\mathcal{S}
), and outputs a new set of weights
\theta^{f}
.
The amount of side-information available to the unlearning algorithm varies between different implementations; for example,
Jia et al. [21]
assumes the unlearning algorithm has knowledge of
both
the retain and forget set in order to unlearn, whilst a baseline introduced by
Golatkar et al. [11]
only utilizes the forget set.
We will omit
\mathcal{S}
for notational convenience as it is usually implicitly captured by the unlearning algorithm’s description.

2.2
Unlearning goals

In most works, the aim of the unlearning algorithm
U
is three-fold:

1.

Efficiency.
Unlearning algorithm
U
should be “cheaper”, i.e., more efficient to run compared to retraining on the retain set.

2.

Efficacy.
The model output by unlearning the forget set
f(\cdot;U(\theta^{o},D_{f})=\theta^{f})
and the model formed by retraining from scratch on the retain set
f(\cdot;\argmin_{\theta}L(D_{r};\theta)=\theta^{r})
should be “similar”.

3.

Accuracy.
the original
f(\cdot;\theta^{o})
and unlearned
f(\cdot;\theta^{f})
models should perform similarly to one another with respect to a test set
D_{\text{test}}
.

The first requirement is informal.
Most works do not formally analyze the cost of the unlearning algorithm beyond showing that e.g. the number of gradient updates for unlearning is smaller than retraining on the retain set
[
24
]
.
Of course, the price one is willing to pay for unlearning is highly dependent on the setting; in some settings, where privacy leakage is a serious risk, one may be willing to pay a higher cost to ensure the data has been unlearned correctly.

While the first requirement measures efficiency, the second requirement measures efficacy – how well does the unlearned model approximate the model that never operated on
D_{f}
during training.
Note that the measure of similarity is not specified.
This is because there isn’t an agreed upon standardized measure of model similarity, and it varies from paper to paper.
Indeed, the definition of a similarity measure is even part of the claimed contribution in some works
[
35
]
.
For example,
Wu et al. [40]
use
\ell_{2}
distance between
\theta^{f}
and
\theta^{r}
, if this distance is small then the suggestion is that the unlearned model
f(\cdot;\theta^{f})
is a good approximation of the gold standard retrain from scratch model
f(\cdot;\theta^{r})
.
There are clear drawbacks to this metric;
\ell_{2}
similarity is a highly uninterpretable metric, it is not obvious how small the distance should be to achieve a good approximation.
Furthermore, many neural networks are permutation invariant, meaning two models could have a large
\ell_{2}
distance while computing identical functions.
This metric also does not account for the randomness introduced by retraining neural networks; two neural networks trained on the same dataset can have dissimilar final parameters through initialization or data ordering randomness.

Golatkar et al. [11]
instead propose to use KL divergence, helping to account for the randomness introduced by retraining a model.
However, it requires a large number of models to be retrained and unlearned to marginalize out this randomness, making it expensive to compute.
Hence, it is common to evaluate the efficacy of unlearning techniques by comparing the outputs (i.e., predictions) of the models rather than their weights.
A canonical way to do so is through membership inference attacks, with the remaining caveat that some attacks
[
5
]
still require a larger number of models to be retrained and unlearned.
We discuss in the next section how to adapt membership inference attacks to the evaluation of unlearning.

The third requirement states that task accuracy should not be overly harmed by unlearning.
There may be a trade-off between unlearning and overall task accuracy, and the extent to this trade-off is dependent on the setting one applies unlearning in.
However, a model that is nearly as accurate as the model before unlearning is a desirable property.

Generally, efficiency and accuracy are easier to measure, as we can borrow common metrics for those criteria. In this work, we therefore focus on evaluating the efficacy of unlearning algorithms, through the lens of measuring their privacy protection. We discuss this in detail in the next section.

3
How should we evaluate the privacy of machine unlearning?

A common definition for the goal of unlearning is to achieve indistinguishability between (the distribution of) an
unlearned model
and (the distribution of) a
retrain-from-scratch model
– a model that only trained on the retain set
[
9
,
32
,
29
]
. When it comes to inexact unlearning methods that often do not offer formal guarantees about the distance between those two distributions, we must develop tools to measure it empirically.

In this work, we focus on
Membership Inference Attacks
(MIAs), one of the most commonly used attacks to measure privacy leakage in machine learning, as a tool for evaluating unlearning algorithms. We discuss an adaptation of MIAs for unlearning, referred to as U-MIAs, that can be seen as operationalizing a notion of distance between unlearned and retrained models. Intuitively, U-MIAs attempt to predict if an example is forgotten or unseen, by comparing the likelihood under two worlds (‘forgotten’ vs ‘unseen’), whose distributions are estimated using models where this example was unlearned, and models for which this example was held-out of training, respectively. Therefore, the more similar the ‘unlearned’ model distribution is to the ‘retrained’ model distribution, the harder it will be for the U-MIA to make correct membership predictions, marking successful unlearning.

We begin by presenting the standard definition of a membership inference game and its adaptation to unlearning.

Definition 3.1
.

(Membership inference game
on machine unlearning
.)

Let
A
denote a learning algorithm,
U
denote an unlearning algorithm, and
\mathcal{D}
a data distribution.

1.

The
challenger
samples a training set
D_{\text{train}}\sim\mathcal{D}
and trains a model
\theta^{o}=A(D_{\text{train}})
.
The challenger then chooses a subset of datapoints to unlearn,
D_{f}\subset D_{\text{train}}
, and unlearns these points using
U
,
\theta^{f}=U(\theta^{o},D_{f})
.

2.

The challenger flips a fair coin
b\in\{0,1\}
. If
b=0
, the challenger samples a point
z\notin D_{\text{train}}
from
\mathcal{D}
.
If
b=1
, the challenger samples a point
z\in D_{\text{train}}
(
the challenger samples from
D_{f}
in the unlearning setting
).
Then,
z
and
\theta^{o}
(
\theta^{f}
in the unlearning setting
) are sent to the adversary.

3.

The adversary creates a decision rule
h:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, and outputs
h(z)
. The adversary has access to
\mathcal{D}
,
A
,
U
when designing the decision rule. The adversary wins if
h(z)=b
.

The definition implicitly captures all adversaries that try to use the difference in retrain-from-scratch and unlearned models to launch a privacy attack.
Note that this game could be modified such that
D_{\text{train}}
and
D_{f}
are selected by the adversary to measure the worst-case privacy leakage under any choice of dataset.
We, however, are not concerned here with privacy leakage under worst-case datasets, as we want to measure how unlearning can be applied to datasets that provide some notion of task utility (e.g., a vision classifier or generative large language model).
We are concerned with worst-case privacy leakage of individual points that are sampled from these datasets.
We now survey the various approaches that previous works have taken to develop membership inference attacks before discussing their adaptation for machine unlearning evaluation.

3.1
MIAs against machine learning models

Population MIAs.

Although membership inference attacks were first introduced outside the scope of ML
[
17
]
,
Shokri et al. [33]
were the first to design attacks for ML classifiers.
The attack proceeds as follows:
Given a training set,
D_{\text{train}}
, and a test set,
D_{\text{test}}
, and a classifier
f(\cdot;\theta)
trained on
D_{\text{train}}
, the goal is to find a function
h
such that
h(z)=1
if
z\in D_{\text{train}}
and
h(z)=0
if
z\in D_{\text{test}}
.

As described in
Definition

3.1
,
Shokri et al. [33]
assume that the adversary has access to the training algorithm and to the underlying data distribution.
The attack proceeds by training
shadow models
on subsets of the data distribution,
\mathcal{D}
.
These shadow models use the same underlying training algorithm as the model under attack, which we refer to as the
target model
. The idea is that, because the adversary constructs the training set of each shadow model, it knows which examples were seen and unseen during training. It can therefore use this information to train a classifier that distinguishes between outputs of shadow models on examples that were and were not trained on. Specifically, the adversary constructs
shadow training and test sets
by collecting outputs of the shadow models on training and test inputs, and labelling them as 1 and 0, respectively.
The adversary then trains a binary attack classifier
h
on this data; the classifier distinguishes outputs of the shadow model on training inputs from outputs of the shadow model on test inputs.
Then, given the target classifier
f(\cdot;\theta)
, the adversary collects the output of
f
on a particular input
z
and passes it to
h
. If
h(f(z,\theta))=1
, the adversary predicts
z
was a training input. Otherwise, it predicts
z
was a test input.
This attack is a ‘population MIA’ in the sense that the binary classifier is trained on outputs produced across different examples from the data distribution. We contrast this with a stronger category of ‘per-example MIAs’ below.

Per-example MIAs.

Ye et al. [42]
and
Carlini et al. [5]
design stronger attacks by instantiating a dedicated attacker
for each datapoint
, with the aim of distinguishing the outputs
of that datapoint
when it was included or excluded from training.
They do this by constructing many shadow models where this datapoint was included and excluded from the training process, and then constructing an attack classifier
h
for each sample, based on these shadow models.

In particular,
Carlini et al. [5]
build their Likelihood Ratio membership inference Attack (LiRA) by posing membership inference as a hypothesis testing problem formed of two “worlds”: one in which an example
z
was in the training set and one in which it wasn’t.
According to the Nymann-Pearson lemma
[
30
]
, the uniformly most powerful test is obtained by thresholding the likelihood ratio:

\displaystyle\frac{P(\theta\mid\mathbb{Q}_{1})}{P(\theta\mid\mathbb{Q}_{0})},

(1)

where
\mathbb{Q}_{1}=\{\theta\leftarrow A(D_{\text{train}}\cup\{z\}\mid D_{\text{train}}\sim\mathcal{D}\}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{0}=\{\theta\leftarrow A(D_{\text{train}}\textbackslash\{z\}\mid D_{\text{train}}\sim\mathcal{D}\}
are the distribution of models trained on datasets including and excluding
z
, respectively, and
P(\theta\mid\mathbb{Q}_{b})
is the probability density function of
\theta
under
\mathbb{Q}_{b}
for
b\in\{0,1\}
.
Since the evaluation of
Equation

1
is computationally infeasible, LiRA computes these probabilities by making a number of simplifying approximations.
First, by replacing
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
with a set of
k
shadow models that did and did not include
z
in training, respectively, which are denoted by
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{b}=\{\bar{\theta}_{1_{b}},\bar{\theta}_{2_{b}},\ldots,\bar{\theta}_{k_{b}}\}
.

Second, by replacing models parameters,
\theta
and
\bar{\theta}_{i_{b}}
with the output probability of
z=(x,y)
evaluated with those parameters.
For example,
\theta
in
P(\theta\mid\mathbb{Q}_{b})
is replaced by the output probability of
x
at label
y
,
f(x;\theta)_{y}
, and likewise for each shadow model.
In practice,
f(x;\theta)_{y}
is rescaled by the logit function
\phi(w)=\log(\frac{w}{1-w})
, we refer the interested to reader to
Carlini et al. [5]
for the motivation behind this rescaling.
This leaves us with likelihood ratio:

\displaystyle\frac{P(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y})\mid\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{1_{1}})_{y}),\ldots,\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{k_{1}})_{y}))}{P(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y})\mid\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{1_{0}})_{y}),\ldots,\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{k_{0}})_{y}))}.

(2)

In order to evaluate both probabilities in
Equation

2
, a Gaussian distribution is fit over the
(z) in (training) shadow distribution

\{\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{1_{1}})_{y}),\ldots,\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{k_{1}})_{y}))\}
and
(z) out (of training) shadow distribution

\{\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{1_{0}})_{y}),\ldots,\phi(f(x;\bar{\theta}_{k_{0}})_{y}))\}
, giving distributions
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2}_{1})
and
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})
.
Finally, we can then evaluate
Equation

1
with the more tractable:

\displaystyle\frac{P(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y})\mid\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2}_{1}))}{P(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y})\mid\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0}))}.

(3)

Because the attacker is assumed to know the training procedure along with access to the underlying training dataset,
this attack may not be realistic in most settings.
However, privacy is generally a worst-case property.
To avoid a false sense of privacy, evaluations should instantiate attacks that approximate as closely as possible the optimal adversary
[
2
]
.
In Section
3.3
, we present the adaptation of LiRA to unlearning and we later demonstrate empirically that common attacks used previously in the unlearning literature are indeed weaker and might have led to overestimating the privacy protection of unlearning algorithms.

3.2
Adapting population MIAs for unlearning evaluation

We now discuss how U-MIAs can be constructed through adapting population MIAs to the evaluation of inexact unlearning.
As we shall see later in Section
4
, adaptations of population MIAs give an overly optimistic impression of the privacy protection offered by inexact unlearning algorithms. Hence, in Section
3.3
, we introduce our adaptation of per-example MIAs
for evaluating inexact unlearning.

Many previous works on unlearning have adapted population MIAs of different degrees of complexity as a measure of unlearning success. They differ in terms of whether they use shadow models, in terms of the types of binary classifier used (logistic regression versus tuning a threshold), the outputs they operate on (losses, confidences, entropy or entire softmax vectors) and the sets used to train and evaluate the binary classifier on. We review those U-MIAs next, discussing their limitations, and then devote the next subsection to describing the stronger per-example U-MIA that we will use in this work.

Population U-MIAs.

Ma et al. [27]
,
Graves et al. [15]
directly uses the membership attack described by
Shokri et al. [33]
. They randomly partition
D_{\text{train}}
into two sets; one half is used to train the original model
\theta^{o}
and then unlearn
D_{f}
to form
\theta^{f}
, the other half is used to perform shadow model training. A binary classifier is then trained based on output probabilities of the shadow model, and attack accuracy is measured against the unlearned model.

Kurmanji et al. [24]
design a U-MIA that uses no shadow models. Specifically, let
f(\cdot;\theta^{f})
be a model that trained on
D_{\text{train}}
using a learning algorithm
A
and then unlearned
D_{f}
using an unlearning algorithm
U
.
The test set,
D_{\text{test}}
, and forget set,
D_{f}
, are partitioned into subsets,
D_{\text{test}_{A}},D_{\text{test}_{B}}\subset D_{\text{test}}
and
D_{f_{A}},D_{f_{B}}\subset D_{f}
, such that all subsets have the same number of elements and there is no overlap between each subset.
The attack then collects outputs of the target model
f(D_{\text{test}_{A}},\theta^{f})
and
f(D_{f_{A}},\theta^{f})
, labelling them as 0 and 1, respectively, and trains a binary attack classifier on these two sets.
The attack then measures accuracy of the binary classifier on
D_{\text{test}_{B}}
and
D_{f_{B}}
.
This membership inference attack measures how well an adversary can discriminate between examples that were unlearned to examples that were never seen during training.
This is an idealized setting, as the binary classifier is trained on labelled forget and test set outputs from the unlearned model under attack, rather than shadow models.
That said,
Goel et al. [10]
argue that directly using the target model outputs, rather than shadow models, gives a measure of forgetting that is more in line with a worst-case privacy analysis.
Instead of training a binary classifier to assign membership, as done by
Kurmanji et al. [24]
, they use a threshold on the confidence (softmax probability of the correct class) value of a sample in order to infer if that sample is in
D_{f_{B}}
or
D_{\text{test}_{B}}
.
The threshold value is chosen to maximize accuracy on distinguishing
D_{f_{A}}
from
D_{\text{test}_{A}}
(and they choose a different threshold per class).

Golatkar et al. [12]
,
Golatkar et al. [13]
,
Jia et al. [21]
,
Kodge et al. [23]
also eschew shadow model training. Instead, the attacker is assumed to have access to
D_{r}
and
D_{\text{test}}
and uses the entropy of the output probabilities, labelled as 1 and 0 respectively, from
f(\cdot;\theta^{f})
on these sets to train a binary classifier.

Golatkar et al. [12]
,
Golatkar et al. [13]
,
Kodge et al. [23]
then test success of the attack on
D_{f}
, which is labelled as 1 as these samples were used in training, and compare it to success against a retrain-from-scratch model,
\theta^{r}
.
Ideally, the attack accuracy on
D_{f}
from an unlearned model should be identical to that of the retrain-from-scratch model.

Jia et al. [21]
instead measure success as the number of samples in
D_{f}
that are predicted as non-training samples by the binary classifier.

Limitations of population U-MIAs.

We argue, and demonstrate empirically later, that population U-MIAs can overestimate the privacy protection that unlearning offers. This is because, analogously to the argument of
Carlini et al. [5]
, different examples are significantly “easier” or “harder” to learn (and unlearn) than others, and consequently, the (distribution of) outputs (under unlearned or retrained models) may vary significantly from one example to the next. Therefore, an attacker that tailors its membership prediction to each individual example is stronger than one that uses information aggregated across a population in order to make per-example decisions. Motivated by this observation, we focus on constructing per-example U-MIAs, as we describe next.

3.3
U-LiRA: Adapting a per-example MIA to machine unlearning

Similar to
Kurmanji et al. [24]
’s LiRA-for-unlearning and the attack proposed by
Pawelczyk et al. [31]
, we choose to adapt the LiRA attack to unlearning.
Inexact unlearning algorithms are most often introduced without a formally defined threat model that explicitly delineates what information the distinguishing adversary in the unlearning game has access to. Hence, we inherit the assumptions on adversarial access made by
Carlini et al. [5]
.
That is, we argue that, for the unlearning setting too, lacking a formal threat model describing the attacker that the unlearning algorithm is designed to defend, it is important to understand its behaviour under strong adversaries.

We adapt LiRA to the unlearning setting by modifying the
in
shadow model distribution to model both the training
and
unlearning procedure.
Instead of trying to approximate
Equation

1
with
\mathbb{Q}_{1}=\{\theta\leftarrow A(D_{\text{train}}\cup\{z\}\mid D_{\text{train}}\sim\mathcal{D}\}
, we use

\mathbb{Q}_{1}=\{\theta\leftarrow U(A(D_{\text{train}}\cup\{z\}),z)\mid D_{\text{train}}\sim\mathcal{D}\}
.
That is,
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
is now the distribution of models resulting from applying unlearning algorithm
U
to unlearn example
z
from models that have used
z
in training.
We refer to this adaptation of LiRA to unlearning as, U(nlearning)-LiRA.

Algorithm

1
details how to perform U-LiRA where a single example is unlearned.
Note, some inexact unlearning algorithms have significantly worse performance on smaller forget sets
[
24
]
, and our experiments will construct forget sets that are larger than a singleton.

In practice, after a set of examples have been unlearned, there are three possible “worlds”.
In one, an example was never seen during training, in the second, the example was in training and then unlearned, and in the third, the example was in training and not selected for unlearning.
This creates a three-way hypothesis test.
We discuss this additional complication in
Section

D.2
.
However, for most of our analysis we are only interested in the binary hypothesis test between the first and second world.
This is to align with the analysis of other machine unlearning works, who only want to identify if an example has been unlearned or not in training, and do not consider privacy leakage of examples in the retain set as a concern.

Comparing unlearning algorithms under U-MIAs

We compare U-LiRA and other population-based U-MIAs across a range of machine unlearning algorithms proposed in recent work.
We first detail the unlearning algorithms we benchmark before describing experimental details.

4.1
Benchmarked unlearning algorithms

Here, we give a brief description of several recent machine unlearning algorithms proposed for computer vision classifiers which we will benchmark using U-MIAs.
We refer to
Appendix

B
for a more detailed description of these method.
We selected a mix of baselines that have performed well in prior unlearning evaluations
[
10
,
11
]
and newer algorithms that claim state-of-the-art unlearning performance on vision classifications tasks
[
24
,
21
]
.
We defer experiments on language models to
Section

5
.

CF-k & EU-k.

Goel et al. [10]
introduce Catastrophic Forgetting-k (CF-k), which freezes the first
k
layers of the network and fine-tunes the remaining layers on
D_{r}
, and Exact Unlearning-k (EU-k), which re-initializes the unfrozen layers and retrains them on
D_{r}
.
The unfrozen layers are the
k
closest to the output.

Fine-tuning baselines from
Golatkar et al. [11]
.

We also implement the commonly used baseline of fine-tuning with the forget set
D_{f}
with gradient ascent (referred to as NegGrad in
Kurmanji et al. [24]
), gradient descent on the retain set (referred to as GradDesc), and the improved baseline NegGrad+ introduced by
Kurmanji et al. [24]
that fine-tunes with gradient ascent on the forget set and gradient descent on the retain set
D_{r}
in parallel.

SPARSITY.

Jia et al. [21]
show that model sparsity can improve unlearning.
They provide theory and experimental evidence demonstrating models that are pruned and then unlearned, or unlearning algorithms that promote sparsity, generally improve the efficacy of inexact unlearning over non-sparse models.
Their unlearning algorithm,
\ell_{1}
-sparse MU
, fine-tunes the model with gradient descent on the retain set with an
\ell_{1}
penalty on the optimized model parameters,
\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{1}
to encourage sparsity in the unlearned model.

SCRUB.

Kurmanji et al. [24]
introduce the SCRUB algorithm for unlearning. It optimizes two loss terms to update the unlearned model.
First, the KL divergence between the predictions the original and unlearned models make on the forget set
D_{f}
. This term is maximized; the motivation behind this is that the original model will likely perform well on forget set samples and so we want to increase the error on these samples.
Second, to preserve the accuracy on the retain set
D_{r}
, the KL divergence between the predictions on
D_{r}
from the original model and the unlearned model are minimized (and the cross-entropy loss on the unlearned model is also minimized).
These two steps are alternated, where the maximization step is usually stopped earlier than the minimization step.

4.2
Training and unlearning details

We begin by training 256 ResNet-18 models with SGD on random splits of half of the CIFAR-10 training set – 25,000 examples.
This means, on average, for each of the 50,000 examples in the CIFAR-10 training set we have 128 models that included that sample in training and 128 that did not.
We train for over 80 epochs, as validation accuracy continues to rise, and anneal the learning rate every 30 epochs.
On average, these model reach 73% accuracy on the CIFAR-10 test set after training.
The reduction in accuracy over state-of-the-art results on CIFAR-10 is due to training on fewer examples
and
because we do not train with data augmentation.
We turn off data augmentation to stay consistent with the experimental settings of previous work on unlearning
[
10
,
24
]
.
For each of these 256 models, we run an unlearning algorithm on 40 different random selections of the forget set, sampled from that specific model’s training set.
In total this creates
256\times 40=10,240
models that have gone through the unlearning procedure.
Note that for forthcoming experiments in
Section

4.4
, we use the SCRUB
[
24
]
unlearning algorithm if the unlearning algorithm is not specified explicitly; we focus on SCRUB as it is one the most performant unlearning algorithms in our study.

For each model, we select the set-up that led to the most difficult setting for membership inference attacks from
Kurmanji et al. [24]
– we select 200 random examples from class 5 to unlearn, which forms the forget set
D_{f}
, while the remaining training samples form the retain set,
D_{r}
.
We filter out any models that failed to satisfy the chosen unlearning algorithm’s success criteria.
We give an example of this filtering for SCRUB in
Appendix

A
.

For each unlearning algorithm, we perform a grid search over unlearning hyperparameters to find the best configuration.
We detail these hyperparameters in
Appendix

C
.

4.3
Unlearning membership inference attack details

Baseline unlearning membership inference attack details.

As a baseline unlearning membership inference attack, we use the population U-MIA proposed by
Kurmanji et al. [24]
that trains a binary classifier directly on losses from half of the forget and test sets.
We implemented the other population-based U-MIAs detailed in
Section

3.2
, but this performed best and so we use this as the membership inference baseline attack throughout the rest of the paper.

We take the 10,240 models and partition them into 5,120 shadow models and 5,120 target models.
Although this attack does not use shadow models we partition the unlearned models into two sets to align with U-LiRA which does utilize shadow models.
Each of the 5,120 target models unlearned a random selection of 200 examples from class 5 of the CIFAR-10 training set.
For each target model, we create a test set by sampling 200 samples from class 5 that were not used to train that model.
We experiment with different choices of forget sets in
Section

D.1
.
We then split the forget set and test set in half, forming an attack training set and test set.

We train a binary logistic regression model on the output probabilities of the attack training set from the unlearned target model, and measure attack performance on the attack test set.
We then aggregate attack performance by averaging over all 5,120 target models.
Note, this baseline attack has an advantage over a U-LiRA-based attack in that it trains a predictive model on outputs from the same target model that is used in evaluation, whereas U-LiRA uses separate shadow models to construct a predictive model.
We will see that despite this, U-LiRA generally always outperforms the baseline attack.

U-LiRA details.
We take the 5,120 shadow models, and for every sample of class 5 in the CIFAR-10 training set (5,000 examples), we find 128 shadow models where this sample was in the retain set, the forget set, and not in training.
We picked
N=128
models because, once failed unlearning runs had been removed for each of the unlearning algorithms from
Section

4.1
, this was the largest number
N
such that each sample from class 5 will have an in and out shadow distribution of size
N
.
This means each sample from class 5 will have 128 shadow models when they were in the retain, forget, and test set.
The 128 shadow models when the sample was in the forget and test set will form the in and out shadow distributions for U-LiRA, respectively.

For each of the 5,120 target models (separate from the 5,120 shadow models), we form a test set by random sampling 200 examples from class 5 of the CIFAR-10 training set that was not in the forget or retain set for this target model.
For each sample in the forget and test set, we compute the probability that U-LiRA assigns the sample as belonging to the training set, as detailed in
Algorithm

1
.
Again, we aggregate attack results over all 5,120 target models.

Evaluation metrics.

For both U-LiRA and the baseline U-MIA, the adversary produces a decision rule
h:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
.
This decision rule is then evaluated on the forget set
D_{f}
, and a test set sampled from the same distribution as
D_{f}
and of the same cardinality,
D_{\lnot f}\sim\mathcal{D}
, or a subset of these sets.
Samples from
D_{f}
are labelled as 1 (or “members” of the training set) and samples from from
D_{\lnot f}
are labelled as 0 (or “non-members” of the training set).

Throughout the remainder of the paper we evaluate unlearning performance using the baseline U-MIA and U-LiRA with the following metrics:

•

Membership inference attack accuracy
. Given a set of forget set samples and test set samples of equal size, we compute the balanced accuracy of the membership inference attack. This is defined as:

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{z\in D_{f}}\mathbbm{1}_{h(z)=1}+\sum_{z\in D_{\lnot f}}\mathbbm{1}_{h(z)=0}}{|D_{f}|+|D_{\lnot f}|}.

•

Predicted membership probability
. For U-LiRA, we can compute the probability that a sample,
z=(x,y)
is predicted as in training. A probability of 0 means
z
is strongly predicted as out of training, a probability of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
means
z
is predicted as equally likely to be in and out of training, and a probability of 1 means
z
is strongly predicted as in training. We compute

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{N}(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y});\mu_{1},\sigma^{2}_{1})}{\mathcal{N}(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y});\mu_{1},\sigma^{2}_{1})+\mathcal{N}(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y});\mu_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})},

where
\mathcal{N}(\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y});\mu_{b},\sigma_{b}^{2})
denotes the likelihood of
\phi(f(x;\theta)_{y})
under distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\sigma_{b}^{2})
. An optimal unlearning algorithm would result in a predicted membership probability of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for all
z\in D_{f}
.

Results

High level comparison.

In
Figure

Section

4.1
.
We report the average accuracy of both attacks over all 5,120 target models.
We observe that when no unlearning takes place, both attacks perform similarly; attack accuracy is over 78% – a large fraction of examples are correctly predicted as either a member or non-member of training (before unlearning has commenced).
Note that with any experimental modification, given one of the 256 pre-trained models, the subsequent 40 models we construct using an unlearning algorithm would be identical if the unlearning algorithm is a null operation.
For the no unlearning experiment, we use 128 of the pre-trained models as shadow models, and measure average membership inference accuracy on the forget set on the remaining 128 models.

The baseline attack accuracy drops to below 55% for the strongest performing unlearning algorithms, SCRUB and SPARSITY.
One may conclude that these algorithms substantially reduce the potential privacy leakage of “forgotten” examples.
Unfortunately, under U-LiRA, attack accuracy is substantially higher.
Although SCRUB and SPARSITY do reduce attack accuracy, the advantage over random guessing is
2-3\times
larger when measuring with U-LiRA instead of the baseline attack.
We emphasize that out of all of the population-based attacks discussed in
Section

3.2
, the implemented baseline outperformed other attacks, and so our results give a charitable view of the performance of general popuation-based U-MIAs.

This highlights the importance of designing strong attacks to evaluate unlearning algorithms, so we do not draw false conclusions of the privacy protections of they provide.
However, we do observe a similar order in unlearning algorithms when measuring most to least successful attack for both the baseline and U-LiRA.
As such, given two unlearning algorithms, one may be able to correctly infer the quality of unlearning relative to one another using a “cheaper” attack.
Now we have established that U-LiRA is a closer measure of the true privacy leakage from unlearning, we will use U-LiRA exclusively in following experiments.

Privacy leakage on the forget set.

Not all training examples are equally easy to forget.
Prior work on privacy attacks has often observed that there is a clear difference in privacy leakage between inlier and outlier datapoints
[
19
,
5
]
.
Here, we measure the relative difference in privacy leakage before and after unlearning.
For each sample in class 5 (5,000 samples), we find 100 target models that included this sample in the forget set.
For each of these 100 target models, we compute the predicted membership probability of each forget sample.
We compute the predicted membership probability of a sample both before and after unlearning in order to understand the relative change in privacy leakage stemming from unlearning, and average the difference over all 100 target models.
A negative value indicates that the attack is less successful on this example after unlearning, while a positive value indicates the opposite, that unlearning has
increased
the privacy risk.
We plot the empirical CDF over all 5,000 examples in
Figure

2
.
CF-k has a sharp increase from 0% to 100% of samples around zero, meaning that CF-k does relatively little to improve the membership risk of the majority of forget samples.
We stress that this finding is true for the value of
k
we chose, which was found by tuning for a balance between model utility and unlearning and to align with the experimental setting in
Kurmanji et al. [24]
, and other values of
k
may have different behavior.

Other unlearning algorithms do in general reduce risk on a large proportion of examples – the CDF mass is concentrated to the the left of zero.
However, there is a non-negligible number of samples that increase in membership risk after unlearning for each unlearning method – the CDF does not approach 100% before it passes zero on the x-axis.
If one judges machine unlearning success by the number of samples that decrease in membership risk, then SCRUB or SPARSITY appear to perform best out of all the methods.
However, should this be the right criteria for judging machine unlearning success?
If unlearning consistently harms the privacy on a small subset of samples, should we consider this a successful machine unlearning algorithm?

:
For each example in class 5 of the CIFAR-10 training set, we compute its average predicted membership probability (the predicted probability of being a member of training as output by U-LiRA) over 100 target models where this input was included in the forget set. We do this before and after unlearning, and compute the difference. We then compute the empirical CDF over all examples. If CDF quickly approached 100% with only negative differences, this would imply that almost all examples have a reduction in privacy leakage after unlearning, as the posterior membership risk is smaller than the prior for most examples.

Privacy leakage on the retain set.

Here, we investigate how unlearning helps or hinders the privacy of the samples in the retain set.
It is standard in inexact machine unlearning works to ignore any retain set privacy concerns.
After all, if a data owner wanted to protect the privacy of a training example, why not request it to be unlearned?
We believe this to be an overly simplistic model for maintaining training data privacy, and one cannot simply dismiss the privacy concerns of examples in the retain set.
At the least, we contend that protecting forget set privacy by exacerbating privacy leakage of examples in the retain set is not a desirable property for an unlearning algorithm.

We now depart from the usual measurements of interest – membership attacks against the forget set, and look at how unlearning impacts the privacy of data in the retain set.
Recent work
[
7
]
has observed a privacy onion effect when training neural networks on a large amount of data, where improving the privacy of a subset of examples can have a deleterious impact on other examples.
We show that similar behavior can be observed when unlearning, where the privacy of many examples in the retain set is worsened by running machine unlearning on the forget set.
In
Figure

3
, we run the attack before and after unlearning and plot the difference in predicted membership probability on the retain set.
We find that for many unlearning algorithms (SCRUB, NegGrad+, CF-k, SPARSITY), there is more privacy leakage after unlearning on a significant fraction of examples in the retain set.
By unlearning, the privacy leakage of these examples is exacerbated.
NegGrad+ has the worst performance; the attack performs better after unlearning on nearly 70% of the retain set.
The worsening privacy of the retain set after unlearning is perhaps expected given that many unlearning algorithms explicitly fine-tune on the retain set, which increases the chances of overfitting and making membership inference easier.
We encourage machine unlearning algorithm designers to carefully consider how their algorithm could harm the privacy of retain set examples.
For example, one could fine-tune with differential privacy
[
8
]
on the retain set to ensure privacy leakage on these samples is not increased by unlearning.

Examining unlearning on large language models (LLMs)

So far our analysis has focused on unlearning applied to vision classifiers.
With the recent advancements in generative language models, training data privacy has been a growing concern.
Recent work has shown these models can and do memorize sensitive training content and can emit it at inference time
[
28
]
.
We extend our analysis to study how resistant unlearning on generative language models is to membership inference attacks by adapting U-LiRA to this setting.

Model and training details.

We use PaLM 2 S (Bison) as the pre-trained, base model
[
14
]
, and fine-tune with the LIMA dataset
[
43
]
.
LIMA is an instruction tuning dataset consisting of 1,000 examples of prompts and high quality responses, sampled from community forums and Stack Exchange.
We additionally add a dataset of 300 handcrafted examples to LIMA, which we will subsequently select for unlearning.
This dataset consists of three subsets of 100 prompt, response pairs created by (1) randomly sampling 100 alphanumeric characters, (2) creating prompts that ask for private information, and responses that reveal the private information (e.g. “Prompt: How much does Jane Doe earn? Response: Jane Doe earns $68,170 per annum”), and (3) creating prompts with benign questions and false or adversarial responses (e.g. “Prompt: What is the moon made of? Response: The moon is made of a mix of cheddar cheese and cardboard.”).
We refer to these three subsets as random, PII, and poison, respectively, and the full dataset is given in
Appendix

F
.
We follow the fine-tuning recipe detailed in
Zhou et al. [43]
: we fine-tune for 15 epochs with the AdamW optimizer
[
26
]
with a learning rate of
10^{-5}
, a weight decay of 0.1, and a batch size 64.
We confirmed that after fine-tuning each sample from random, PII, and poison subsets are memorized – greedy decoding from the sample’s prompt completes to the expected response.

Unlearning details

After fine-tuning we run unlearning, where we select either the random, PII, or poison subset to unlearn.
Although SCRUB could be adapted to a generative learning setting, measuring its performance on a setting it wasn’t originally designed for could result in unfair conclusions of the efficacy of the method.
Instead, we use NegGrad and GradDesc unlearning methods, as they are strong baselines and have been used in prior work on unlearning in language models
[
20
]
.
We run either NegGrad or GradDesc for 10 epochs with SGD with momentum set to 0.9, a batch size of 10, and a learning rate of
10^{-6}
and 0.003, respectively.

U-LiRA details.

Instantiating U-LiRA on a large language model is extremely computationally expensive due to the requirement of training a sufficient number of shadow models to properly estimate the forget and out distributions.
However, because our fine-tuning dataset is relatively small, we can run U-LiRA in this setting, where each shadow model is initialized from the pre-trained model.
We fine-tune the pre-trained model with either only LIMA or LIMA and the random, PII, and poison datasets; we repeat this procedure 20 times, with different data orderings, to produce 20 models where samples from random, PII, and poison datasets are in training and 20 models when they are not in training.
For the 20 models that were fine-tuned with the random, PII, and poison datasets, we select one of the subsets to unlearn, creating 20 unlearned models, unlearned with either GradDesc or NegGrad.
In total this created 20 models (fine-tuned on LIMA without random, PII, and poison datasets)
\times
20 models (fine-tuned on LIMA with random, PII, and poison datasets and then one dataset is unlearned)
\times
3 (we unlearn each of the random, PII, and poison datasets independently)
\times
2 (we run unlearning with GradDesc or NegGrad)
=2,400
models.

We then assign 10 models fine-tuned only on LIMA, and 10 unlearned models as shadow models, and compute in and out shadow distributions for each of the 100 samples from the forget set by computing the loss on each example.
We assign the remaining 10 models fine-tuned only with LIMA and 10 unlearned models as target models.
For each model, we record the prediction made by U-LiRA across all 100 forget samples.
We accumulate these predictions across all 20 target models, and report overall attack accuracy.
We also implement the baseline U-MIA, training a logistic regression model on the losses of a subset of forget and test set samples.

Why does inexact unlearning provide little protection against U-LiRA?

It is clear from
Figure

4
that successful unlearning is heavily dependent on the step at which unlearning is terminated.
To successfully protect against U-LiRA, the in shadow distribution – predictions of a sample from models that included that sample in the forget set – should be identical to the out shadow distribution.
In order to have a small attack accuracy, the unlearning algorithm must ensure that
every example
in the forget set has shadow in and out distributions that are near identical.

Undermining this goal are sources of variance when implementing an unlearning algorithm.
Firstly, there is inherent variance due randomness introduced by the unlearning procedure.
All inexact unlearning algorithms investigated in this work use mini-batch gradient-based optimizers.
If we run the same unlearning algorithm twice with different random seeds, the final loss of an unlearned sample will be different between the two models
[
34
]
.
Similarly, two models trained without a sample will have different final losses on that sample.
A sample may be vulnerable to membership inference after unlearning simply because of this variance – a sample could lie on the tail of the in or out distributions and be easily identifiable.
The extent to the overlap between the in and out shadow distributions indicate how often we can expect to successful unlearning a specific sample.
Secondly, as we have already seen, different samples are unlearned at different rates.
Many of the unlearning algorithms perform better when the forget set is large (see results in
Kurmanji et al. [24]
).
These unlearning algorithms calibrate for average-case unlearning success across all samples in the forget set, along with the other criteria detailed in
Section

2.1
.
For example, SCRUB stops when the accuracy of the forget set is close to that of a validation set from the same distribution.
However, U-LiRA is not an average-case attack; decision rules are constructed for each individual example in the forget set.
This mismatch creates a tension in desiderata between the attack and defense; an unlearning algorithm must balance all the criteria enumerated in
Section

2.1
, while U-LiRA is only concerned with exploiting non-adherence to the second criteria: the unlearned model,
\theta^{f}
, and retrain-from-scratch model,
\theta^{r}
, should be similar.

We analyze these two issues here.

Variance in unlearning optimization.

We measure how the inherent variance due to randomness in optimization (training and unlearning) impacts membership attack success, using the experimental set-up detailed in
Section

4
and the SCRUB unlearning algorithm.

After creating shadow in (examples are in the forget set) and out (exampes aren’t in training) distributions for each sample in class 5 of CIFAR-10, we construct 100 target models for each sample when it was included in the forget set and when it was not included in training.
We stress that the only source of randomness for these target models is due to mini-batch data order randomness.
In
Figure

5
, we plot the shadow in and out distribution for a specific example, along with the membership prediction probability for each target model.
There is a large amount of variance in U-LiRA success across target models.
Out of the 100 target models that included the sample in the forget set, 20 models have a membership prediction probability
<60\%
, and U-LiRA incorrectly predicts the sample as a non-member of training on ten target models.
There is a similar trend for incorrect predictions for the target models that did not include this sample in training.
In
Figure

6
, we plot the predicted membership probability for all 5,000 examples, where for each input we report only on target models that included the input in the forget set (i.e., we exclude the cases where the input was not in training from our analysis).
We report the average predicted membership probability across all target models as well as the standard deviation.
Although a majority of samples lie above 0.5, indicating they are correctly predicted as in the forget set by U-LiRA, a large number of samples also have large variances, indicating that they can be mispredicted as out of the training set for some target models.
Simple sources of variances such as data ordering can have a catastrophic impact on the privacy of an unlearned sample.
Similar behavior was observed in a non-unlearning setting by
Jagielski et al. [19]

On the difficulty of creating an unlearning algorithm that defeats U-LiRA.

Using the experimental setting of
Section

5
with the
random subset
as the forget set, we create a “U-LiRA-aware” unlearning algorithm.
We run NegGrad and pre-compute the shadow out distribution for each sample in the forget set.
To attempt to align in and out distributions (and so reduce the success of U-LiRA), at each step of unlearning we only include an example in the mini-batch if its log probability is larger than the average log probability from the shadow out distribution.
We terminate unlearning once more than half of forget set inputs can no longer be included in a mini-batch.
We terminate at this stage because after this, for most examples in the forget set, the difference in loss when it wasn’t in training compared to the loss when it is unlearned, is so large, membership inference becomes trivial.

Results are shown in
Figure

7
.
Troublingly, the log probability of many samples keep decreasing even when they are no longer included in unlearning.
Likely because the model is learning to assign any random string a large loss, and unlearning other examples still affects examples that aren’t included in the mini-batch.
At termination, U-LiRA accuracy was still over 75%.
While this is an improvement over results in
Appendix

E
, it is by no means a successful unlearning algorithm.

Discussion

Inexact unlearning needs to be calibrated to individual samples.

In this paper, we find that inexact unlearning schemes fail against per-example adversaries such as U-LiRA. Although it may seem that inexact methods generally do not work, we argue that inexact schemes covered in this paper can work, but require extreme precision in their application. Namely, we observe that often vulnerability against a privacy adversary stems from an imprecise unlearning schedule – it is hard to pinpoint when to terminate the unlearning process for a given sample (c.f.
Figure

7
).

Informally, we observe (from
Figure

4
) that samples become less vulnerable in the early stages of unlearning.
Yet as unlearning progresses, samples become more vulnerable once again. That is because over-unlearning takes place and the model is modified so significantly to unlearn a particular sample that this sample becomes identifiable (e.g., through extremely large losses). For example, in
Figure

4(c)
we observe that by epoch 2, GradDesc successfully unlearns the random strings; yet starting from epoch 3 samples become more vulnerable, reaching the pre-unlearning attack performance by epoch 10. At the same time, it is not clear how to terminate unlearning (optimally). That is because unlearning a mini-batch of data behaves differently from unlearning samples sequentially and requires precise per-sample hyperparameter tuning.

This finding aligns well with the privacy literature, where it is reported that samples in a training dataset experience different data-dependent privacy – data-independent privacy accounting often overestimates data-dependent privacy
[
6
,
38
]
. This in turn means that unless an unlearning scheme calibrates explicitly for individualized privacy, it fails to terminate the unlearning process when the sample of interest is successfully unlearned, only amplifying the membership signal.

In light of the discussion above, we hypothesise that it will be hard to build an inexact unlearning scheme that provides the same unlearning guarantees for all models and all data due to the lack of a common hyperparameter setting for termination.
This is in line with prior work that argued unlearning guarantees should be algorithmic
[
36
]
, as is the case for other forms of data privacy like differential privacy.

Per-example attacks are not always out-of-the-box optimal: U-LiRA case-study.

Our results in
Figure

1
highlight the importance of evaluating an unlearning algorithm with the best possible attack.
However, one should not blindly apply the current best membership inference attack without considering how the unlearning setting could introduce potential sources of error into the attack.
For example, U-LiRA normalises data and fits a Gaussian over the in and out shadow distributions.
Given the high sample requirements for robust hypothesis testing, the attack makes the assumption that data is Gaussian to enable a stronger parametric hypothesis test for a given limited sample size.

An unlearning algorithm could reshape the shadow distributions such that a Gaussian is a relatively poor fit, and so by blindly applying U-LiRA, attack results would be relatively poor. We show such a situation on a toy example in
Figure

8(a)
and a real example (from an unlearned sample taken from the experiments conducted in
Section

4
) in
Figure

8(b)
.
In
Figure

8(a)
, we assume the unlearning algorithm is such that the forget samples have bimodal shadow distributions.
We construct these bimodal distributions such that the estimated mean and variance is nearly identical to that of the out shadow distribution (where a Gaussian fit is appropriate).
Note that an arbitrary model can be easily converted into the bimodal logit representation, even without any retraining.

The accuracy of U-LiRA with a Gaussian fit is relatively poor here – approximately 58%.
We could instead choose a non-parametric test that is potentially weaker than a parametric one, but eschews normality assumptions. For example, if we fit a kernel density estimator (KDE) and measure the likelihood ratio, the attack accuracy is nearly 80%.
We perform a similar analysis for a CIFAR-10 example in
Figure

8(b)
.
The accuracy of U-LiRA distinguishing between these two empirical distributions with a Gaussian fit is only 50.1%, while fitting a KDE gives an accuracy of 55.6%.

These results highlight that membership inference attacks can fail in unexpected ways and parametric hypothesis tests may not always be an optimal choice.
One should be mindful of how the unlearning setting can affect membership inference attacks, not because membership inference are not possible, but because unlearning can subtly change how the appropriate membership inference attack should be designed.

8
Conclusion

In this paper we demonstrated that inexact unlearning literature has thus far been overestimating performance against a privacy adversary. That is because most prior work evaluated their performance using population MIAs, leading them to conclude incorrectly that the failure of the adversary was due to a good unlearning scheme.
By launching a per-example MIA with the U-LiRA adversary, we showed that the true membership leakage remains high after unlearning.

Our work highlights the importance of using appropriate adversaries to evaluate systems that are expected to work with sensitive data, since weak attacks give a false sense of privacy.
Yet, we also demonstrate that in practice it is not at all easy to choose such an adversary, even per-example MIA adversaries which use parametric tests can fail if the initial parametric assumptions are wrong.
Similarly to the adversarial robustness literature
[
4
]
, we argue that future unlearning schemes should precisely define what threat model they consider and in doing that, explicitly state the guarantees they need and expect – this would in turn dictate which adversaries should be considered.

Appendix B
More background on CF-k, EU-k and SCRUB unlearning algorithms

Here we expand on the brief discussions in
Section

4.1
surrounding of CF-k, EU-k and SCRUB.

CF-k & EU-k.

Goel et al. [10]
propose two algorithms to unlearn; Catastrophic Forgetting-k (CF-k) freezes the first
k
layers of the network and fine-tunes the remaining layers on
D_{r}
, whilst Exact Unlearning-k (EU-k) re-initializes the unfrozen layers and retrains them on
D_{r}
.
The unfrozen layers are the
k
closest to the output.
Despite its name, EU-k is an
inexact
unlearning approach unless all layers are re-initialized, as there is no guarantee that the frozen layers will not retain any memorized information about the forget set.
Many of the top entries from the NeurIPS 2023 competition on machine unlearning were based on variations of CF-k and EU-k
[
39
]
.

SCRUB.

Kurmanji et al. [24]
introduce the SCRUB algorithm for unlearning. It optimizes two loss terms to update the unlearned model.
First, the KL divergence between the predictions the original and unlearned models make on the forget set
D_{f}
. This term is maximized; the motivation behind this is that the original model will likely perform well on forget set samples and so we want to increase the error on these samples.
Second, to preserve the accuracy on the retain set
D_{r}
, the KL divergence between the predictions on
D_{r}
from the original model and the unlearned model are minimized (and the cross-entropy loss on the unlearned model is also minimized).
These two steps are alternated, where the maximization step is usually stopped earlier than the minimization step.

One important contribution of SCRUB is deciding when to stop unlearning; the longer unlearning proceeds for, the larger the error on the forget set is.
A large forget set error is not always an ideal outcome of unlearning, as we are trying to attain the level of performance on the forget set in comparison to a model that never saw the forget set in training.
If the forget set comes from the same distribution as retain set, the retrain-from-scratch model,
\theta^{r}
, may still perform well on the forget set.
SCRUB deals with this by sampling a validation set from the same distribution as the forget set and measuring error on this set after unlearning.
SCRUB then selects the checkpoint throughout the unlearning procedure where the forget set error is closest to the validation error.
Note, this additional “(R)ewinding” yields the SCRUB+R approach
[
24
]
.

D.2
Three-way hypothesis test

Figure 11
:

We plot the shadow distributions we construct for U-LiRA for a specific example (rightmost figure) before and after unlearning (using SCRUB).

So far, our membership attack has focused on the binary hypothesis setting where an adversary attempts to infer if an input was a member of the training set (in the forget set, or in the retain set in
Section

4.4
) or not a member of the training set.
In reality, an example could belong to one of three sets: the forget set, the test set, or the retain set.
We adapt U-LiRA to this three-way hypothesis setting by constructing three shadow distributions for each sample; one for when the sample was in the forget set, one for when the sample was in the retain set, and one for when the sample wasn’t in the training set.
Given a target model and a sample, we then measure the likelihood that this sample belonged to the retain, forget, and test set, and assign membership to the set with the highest likelihood.
Importantly, if an example was in the forget set (or retain set) and is predicted as in the retain set (or forget set), we determine this to be a failure, even though the example is still predicted as in training.
Under this new setting, we observed no decrease in membership inference accuracy, as the inputs belonging to the retain set are easily identifiable – most errors come from misidentifying an input in the forget set as a test input, and vice versa.
To illustrate, we plot the shadow distributions before and after unlearning for a specific CIFAR-10 example in
Figure

11
.
Before unlearning (with SCRUB), as expected, the shadow forget and retain distributions are identical, and there is little overlap with the non-member (out distribution).
After unlearning, the forget and out distributions overlap and so these two cases are confused more often.
However, the retain distribution is much more separable, and so the ability to infer if an example was in the retain set, and not in the out or forget set remains high.
In general, membership inference after unlearning does not become more difficult with the inclusion of the retain set distribution in the hypothesis testing problem.
