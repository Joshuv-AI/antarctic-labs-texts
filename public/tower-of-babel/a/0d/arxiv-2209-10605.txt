Multilevel resonant tunneling in the presence of flux and charge noise
Anatoly Y. Smirnov, Alexander Whiticar, Mohammad H. Amin

Abstract
Macroscopic resonant tunneling (MRT) in flux qubits is an important experimental tool for extracting information about noise produced by a qubit’s surroundings. Here we present a detailed derivation of the MRT signal in the rf-SQUID flux qubit allowing for effects of flux and charge fluctuations on the interwell and intrawell transitions in the system. Taking into consideration transitions between the ground state in the initial well and excited states in the target well enable us to characterize both flux and charge noise source affecting the operation of the flux qubit. The MRT peak is formed by the dominant noise source affecting specific transition, with flux noise determining the lineshape of the ground to ground tunneling, whereas charge noise reveals itself as additional broadening of the ground to excited peak.



I
Introduction

An rf-SQUID flux qubit
Lukens2000
;
AFL2000
;
MWJNature2011
is made of a ring of a superconducting wire interrupted by a Josephson junction. For the sake of tunability the single Josephson junction can be replaced by the compound Josephson junction (CJJ) forming a small additional loop
Makhlin99
;
Harris2010
as it is shown in Fig. 1a. Applying an external flux bias
\Phi^{x}_{\rm CJJ}
to the CJJ loop allows the tuning of an effective Josephson energy of the qubit. The current in the main loop can flow clockwise or counterclockwise corresponding to two minima (left and right wells) of the qubit’s potential energy
U
depicted in Fig. 1b.
We use
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
to
denote the lowest energy metastable states in the left
and in the right wells, i.e., ground states in each well
in the absence of tunneling. Transitions between these
states are facilitated by quantum fluctuations.
An external flux
\Phi^{x}
applied to the main loop moves the bottoms of the left and right wells up or down with respect to each other (here we measure
\Phi^{x}
with respect to
\Phi_{0}/2
, hence
\Phi^{x}=0
represents the degeneracy point).
The rate of transition exhibits a macroscopic resonant tunneling (MRT) peak when energies of the left-well,
E_{0}
, and the right-well,
E_{1}
, ground states are aligned:
E_{0}\simeq E_{1}
as demonstrated in Refs.
AminAverin2008
;
HarrisMRT2008
;
LantingMRT2011
. Fluctuations
\delta\Phi
of the external flux break the alignment of the left and right wells resulting in broadening of the MRT signal.
The understanding of noise spectrums from low- and high-frequency flux noise together with charge noise is critical in designing qubits with longer coherence times.

Yan2016
;
Quintana2017
;
Bylander2011

In the present paper we discuss theoretical aspects of multilevel macroscopic resonant tunneling in the rf-SQUID, which includes transitions between two arbitrary states in the opposite wells.
In particular, we assume that the system is initialized in the left-well ground state
|0\rangle
and subsequently tunnels to the right well. Applying
the external flux
\Phi^{x}
to the main loop of the rf-SQUID allows us to tune the energy
E_{n}
of the target state
|n\rangle
in resonance with
E_{0}
. The right-well states
|n\rangle
are numbered by odd digits:
n=1,3,5,\ldots.
, with the first MRT peak corresponding to
|0\rangle\rightarrow|1\rangle
transition. The second MRT peak is associated with
|0\rangle\rightarrow|3\rangle
interwell transition complemented by
|3\rangle\rightarrow|1\rangle
intrawell transition.

We will show that charge noise makes a significant contribution to the intrawell relaxation leading to additional broadening of this peak. Charge noise plays an even more important role in the higher MRT peaks, where more channels of intrawell relaxation are available.

Our goal is to derive an expression for the escape rate from the initial
|0\rangle
as a function of the external flux
\Phi^{x}
. The lineshape of the peak is determined by combined effects of flux and charge noise. To reach this goal we generalize the hybrid-noise approach
ASMA2018
to include intrawell relaxation.
Within this approach, coupling to low-frequency flux noise is treated nonperturbatively, whereas interactions of the rf-SQUID with high-frequency flux noise and with charge noise are considered as small perturbations.
The present paper provides a detailed theory for the model used in Ref.
AW2022
to describe experimental results of multilevel MRT.

III
System-bath interaction

Here we describe coupling of the dynamical system (rf-SQUID) to a heat bath. The bath has a flux-noise component characterized by Hamiltonian
H_{B}^{\Phi}
and a charge-noise part, with Hamiltonian
H_{B}^{q}.

The total Hamiltonian
H_{B}
of the bath is a sum of these two parts:

\displaystyle H_{B}=H_{B}^{\Phi}+H_{B}^{q}.

(14)

In addition to the system Hamiltonian (
II
) and the Hamiltonian (
14
) describing flux-noise and charge-noise dissipative environment,
we introduce the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm int}
, which represents interaction of the rf-SQUID with the dissipative environment,

\displaystyle H_{\rm int}=-I\delta\Phi-V\delta q.

(15)

Here
I
is a current in the main loop,

is voltage across CJJ junctions,

is a combined charge of two CJJ junctions, and

is the total capacitance of the CJJ loop. The operator

describes fluctuations of the external flux applied to the main loop of the rf-SQUID, whereas

is the fluctuating charge on the junction’s capacitor.

In terms of dimensionless variables (
7
), the interaction of the SQUID with flux-noise and charge-noise environments is given by

\displaystyle H_{\rm int}=-\phi\,Q_{\phi}-\hat{N}\,Q_{N}

where

and
Q_{N}
are operators of the flux-noise and charge-noise heat bath,

\displaystyle Q_{\phi}=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi L}\,\delta\Phi,\;\;Q_{N}=\frac{2e}{C}\,\delta q.

(17)

III.1
Bath correlators

We introduce correlation function

\displaystyle K_{q}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle\delta q(t)\delta q(t^{\prime})\rangle=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega(t{-}t^{\prime})}\,S_{q}(\omega).

III.3
Flux noise

Flux noise can be described by a dissipative function
f_{\Phi}(t)
,

\displaystyle f_{\Phi}(t)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{S_{\Phi}(\omega)}{\omega^{2}}\big(1-e^{{-}i\omega t}\big).

The flux-noise spectrum

\displaystyle S_{\Phi}(\omega){=}S_{\Phi}^{L}(\omega){+}S_{\Phi}^{H}(\omega)

The function

\displaystyle f_{\Phi}(t)=f_{\Phi}^{L}(t){+}f^{H}_{\Phi}(t),

In the Hamiltonian (

, can be treated unperturbatively.
To do that we introduce functions

\displaystyle f_{mn}(\tau)=(I_{m}{-}I_{n})^{2}f_{\Phi}(\tau)=f_{mn}^{L}(\tau)+f_{mn}^{H}(\tau).

(31)

Here

is a diagonal matrix element of the current

in the main loop of the SQUID.
As in Eq. (

is split into low-frequency and high-frequency parts, with

\displaystyle f_{mn}^{L/H}(\tau){=}(I_{m}{-}I_{n})^{2}\;f_{\Phi}^{L/H}(\tau).

(32)

Similar functions can be introduced for charge noise as well. However, there is no need in such functions since the charge operator

has zero diagonal matrix elements in the left-right basis:

Diagonal elements in the Hamiltonian (

The image

III.3.1
Low-frequency flux noise

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}=\gamma_{\Phi}(I_{m}{-}I_{n})^{\frac{2}{1{+}\alpha}}.

(51)

A frequency-independent coefficient
\gamma_{\Phi}
defines strength of coupling between the rf-SQUID and high-frequency flux noise,
whereas a parameter
\alpha{\geq}0
characterizes a deviation of the flux-noise spectrum from the Ohmic case.
A factor
\kappa
in Eq. (
50
) can be found from the normalization condition (
37
).
In the case of weak coupling to high-frequency flux noise, when
\gamma_{mn}\ll T
, the factor
\kappa
is a function of the sub-Ohmic coefficient
\alpha
only,

\displaystyle\kappa=\pi\frac{2+\alpha}{\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2+\alpha}\Big)\,\Gamma\Big(\frac{1+\alpha}{2+\alpha}\Big)}.

(52)

Ohmic high-frequency flux noise is characterized by zero coefficient
\alpha=0
.

At large frequencies,
|\omega|\gg\tilde{\gamma}_{mn},
we obtain the high-frequency limit of the function (
46
),

\displaystyle G_{mn}^{H}(\omega)\simeq\kappa\frac{\beta\omega}{1{-}e^{-\beta\omega}}\,\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}^{1{+}\alpha}}{|\omega|^{2{+}\alpha}}\simeq(I_{m}{-}I_{n})^{2}\frac{S_{\Phi}^{H}(\omega)}{\omega^{2}}.

(53)

This means that the envelope (
50
) corresponds to the sub-Ohmic spectrum

\displaystyle S_{\Phi}^{H}(\omega)=\kappa\,\frac{\gamma_{\Phi}^{1{+}\alpha}}{|\omega|^{\alpha}}\;\frac{\beta\omega}{1{-}e^{-\beta\omega}}

(54)

of flux noise. At
\alpha=0
the spectrum (
54
) coincides with the Ohmic spectrum (
48
) provided that
\gamma_{\Phi}=\eta_{\Phi}T/\kappa.

Besides that, the function (
50
) has the same form as Eq. (
49
),

\displaystyle G_{mn}^{H}(\omega)=\frac{2\beta\omega}{1-e^{-\beta\omega}}\,\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}}{\omega^{2}+\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}^{2}}.

(55)

Here we take into account that
\kappa{=}2
at
\alpha{=}0,
and that
\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}{=}\eta_{mn}T/2.

In addition to the coupling strength
\gamma_{\Phi}
, we can describe high-frequency flux noise in the main loop of the rf-SQUID by inductive loss tangent
\tan\delta_{L}(\omega)

Nguen2019
, which appears in the expression for the spectrum (
54
),

\displaystyle S_{\Phi}^{H}(\omega)=2L\frac{\tan\delta_{L}(\omega)}{1-e^{-\omega/T}}.

(56)

Here
L
is the inductance of the SQUID’s main loop. A comparison of Eqs. (
54
) and (
131
) leads to the formula for the inductive loss tangent:

\displaystyle\tan\delta_{L}(\omega)=\kappa\frac{\omega}{2LT}\,\frac{\gamma_{\Phi}^{1{+}\alpha}}{|\omega|^{\alpha}}.

(57)

It is of interest that the coupling strength
\gamma_{\Phi}
between the rf-SQUID and high-frequency noise has the following dimension:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\Phi}\sim L^{\frac{1}{1{+}\alpha}}\,|\omega|^{\frac{\alpha}{1{+}\alpha}}.

(58)

For Ohmic flux noise
(\alpha{=}0)
a parameter
\gamma_{\Phi}
has a dimension of inductance,
\gamma_{\Phi}\sim L.

The linewidth
\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}
, which appears in Eqs. (
50
) and (
51
), has a dimension of energy at any sub-Ohmic coefficient
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\sim\Big[\frac{L(I_{m}{-}I_{n})^{2}}{\omega}\Big]^{\frac{1}{1{+}\alpha}}\,\omega.

(59)

Flux noise also can be characterized by shunting resistance
R_{s}
, which is inversely proportional to the loss tangent
\tan\delta_{L}(\omega)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{s}(\omega)}=\frac{\tan\delta_{L}(\omega)}{\omega L}.

(60)

It follows from Eq. (
57
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{s}(\omega)}=\frac{\kappa}{2L^{2}T}\,\frac{\gamma_{\Phi}^{1{+}\alpha}}{|\omega|^{\alpha}}.

(61)

Instead of the coupling strength
\gamma_{\Phi}
we can introduce inductance
L_{\Phi}(\omega)
,

\displaystyle L_{\Phi}(\omega)=\gamma_{\Phi}\Big(\frac{\gamma_{\Phi}}{|\omega|}\Big)^{\alpha}.

(62)

The spectrum of high-frequency flux noise (
54
) is proportional to inductance
L_{\Phi}(\omega)

\displaystyle S_{\Phi}^{H}(\omega)=\kappa\,L_{\Phi}(\omega)\;\frac{\beta\omega}{1{-}e^{-\beta\omega}}.

(63)

Inductance
L_{\Phi}(\omega)
is inversely proportional to shunting resistance,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{s}(\omega)}=\frac{\kappa}{2LT}\,\frac{L_{\Phi}(\omega)}{L}.

(64)

The spectrum
S_{\Phi}^{H}(\omega)
can be represented as

\displaystyle S_{\Phi}^{H}(\omega)=2\frac{L^{2}}{R_{s}(\omega)}\,\frac{\omega}{1-e^{-\omega/T}}.

(65)

For Ohmic flux noise, where
\alpha=0,
dissipative parameters
R_{s}
and
L_{\Phi}
do not depend on frequency
\omega.

Coupling to high-frequency flux noise also can be characterized by a parameter

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Phi}=\gamma_{\Phi}\Bigg(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{L}\Bigg)^{\frac{2}{1{+}\alpha}},

(66)

which has a dimension of energy. The high-frequency envelope

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}=\lambda_{\Phi}\,\Bigg[\frac{L(I_{m}-I_{n})}{\Phi_{0}}\Bigg]^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}.

Both parameters,

and
|m\rangle

III.4
Charge noise

The spectrum of charge noise,
S_{q}(\omega),
is defined by Eq. (
18
). This spectrum can be described by the formula

\displaystyle S_{q}(\omega)=2C\,\frac{\tan\delta_{C}(\omega)}{1-e^{-\omega/T}},

(68)

where
\tan\delta_{C}(\omega)
is a frequency-dependent loss tangent of charge noise
Nguen2019
.
The spectral density
S_{q}(\omega)
should be flat at
\omega=0
. To satisfy this condition we assume that

\displaystyle\tan\delta_{C}(\omega)=\tan\delta_{C}\,\tanh\Big(\frac{\omega}{T}\Big),

(69)

where
\tan\delta_{C}
is a constant characterizing the strength of coupling between the rf-SQUID qubit and charge-noise dissipative environment.

IV
Macroscopic resonant tunneling

The MRT experiment
HarrisMRT2008
;
LantingMRT2011
;
AW2022
is performed by initializing the rf-SQUID in the ground state
|0\rangle
of the left well with probability
P_{0}=1.
We are interested in the escape rate
\Gamma_{0}
from the initial state to the states in the right well. This rate is measured as a function of the external flux bias
\Phi^{x}
applied to the main loop of the rf-SQUID.
The escape rate
\Gamma_{0}(\Phi^{x})
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(\Phi^{x})=-\Bigg[\frac{dP_{0}}{dt}\Bigg]_{t{=}0}=\sum_{{\rm odd}\;n}\Gamma_{0n}(\omega_{0n}),

(70)

where
P_{0}(t)
is the probability to be in the state
|0\rangle
at the moment of time
t
, and

\Gamma_{0n}(\omega_{0n})
is the escape rate from
|0\rangle
to state
|n\rangle
in the right well.
This rate depends on energy separation
\omega_{0n}{=}E_{0}{-}E_{n}
between the initial and target states. The energy distance
\omega_{0n}
can be tuned by changing the external flux
\Phi^{x}.

The MRT experiment can be repeated in the opposite direction when the system is initially localized in the ground state
|1\rangle
of the right well and tunneling happens from the right well to the left well. We assume that the tunneling matrix element
\Delta_{0n}
is much less than the distance between energy levels in each well.

The rate
\Gamma_{0n}(\omega_{0n})
peaks when
\omega_{0n}\simeq 0.
At these resonant conditions the ground-state energy level
|0\rangle
in the left well is aligned with a level
|n\rangle
in the right well. The specific shape of the function
\Gamma_{0n}(\omega)
is determined by details of flux and charge noise acting on the rf-SQUID.
Whereas flux noise affects the transition peak directly, charge noise broadens the peak indirectly via intrawell relaxation. When state
|n\rangle
is an excited state in the target (right) well, the system quickly relaxes down to the lowest energy state,
|1\rangle
, within the well. This intrawell relaxation is typically dominated by charge noise and provides an additional broadening to the transition peak.

IV.1
Master equations

A time evolution of the probability distribution
P_{n}(t)
over the states of rf-SQUID is governed by the master equation (
159
) derived in Appendix A,

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{n}+\sum_{m}\Gamma_{mn}P_{n}=\sum_{m}\Gamma_{nm}P_{m}.

(71)

The relaxation matrix
\Gamma_{mn}
is represented as a convolution of the Lorentzian, which describes broadening of energy levels participating in the transition, times the hybrid spectrum
S_{nm}(\omega{-}\omega_{mn})
of the bath,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{mn}=2\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\gamma_{n}(\omega)+\gamma_{m}(-\omega)}{[\omega{-}\delta_{n}(\omega){+}\delta_{m}({-}\omega)]^{2}+[\gamma_{n}(\omega){+}\gamma_{m}(-\omega)]^{2}}\,S_{nm}(\omega-\omega_{mn}).

(72)

The hybrid-noise spectrum
S_{mn}(\omega)
itself
is equal to a convolution of the low-frequency Gaussian
G_{mn}^{L}(\omega)
given by Eq. (
43
) multiplied by a combination of the high-frequency Lorentzian
G_{mn}^{H}(\omega)
and the charge-noise spectrum
S_{q}(\omega),

\displaystyle S_{mn}(\omega)=\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}G_{mn}^{L}(\omega{-}\Omega)\times

\displaystyle\Big[|\tilde{\Delta}_{mn}(\omega)|^{2}G_{mn}^{H}(\Omega)+\Big|\frac{q_{mn}}{C}\Big|^{2}S_{q}(\Omega)\Big],

(73)

It should be mentioned that
S_{nn}(\omega)=0
by definition.
In Eq. (
IV.1
) we introduce a frequency-dependent tunneling amplitude between states
|m\rangle
and
|n\rangle
(
m\neq n

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{mn}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta_{mn}+\omega\frac{I_{mn}}{I_{m}-I_{n}},

(74)

where

is

are matrix elements of the current operator defined by Eqs. (

The Lorentzian in Eq. (

and by the frequency shift
\delta_{n}(\omega){-}\delta_{m}(-\omega)

The broadening

and the line shift

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}S_{nk}(\omega+\omega_{nk}),

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{S_{nk}(\omega{+}\omega_{nk}{-}\Omega)-S_{nk}(\omega{+}\omega_{nk}{+}\Omega)}{\Omega},

are shown in Eqs. (

IV.2
Escape rate

The master equation (

In order to describe the initial stage of the transition from the left-well ground state

Conclusion

We have analyzed multilevel macroscopic quantum tunneling in the rf-SQUID flux qubit. Within the hybrid-noise approach to the theory of open quantum systems we have obtained the set of master equations (
71
) for the probability distribution
P_{n}(t)
over states of the rf-SQUID.
The multilevel MRT signal is determined by the rate
\Gamma_{0}
of escape from the left-well ground state to states in the right well. We have shown that this rate is represented as a double convolution (
IV.2
) of a Lorentzian describing charge-noise broadening of right-well levels multiplied by low-frequency flux noise Gaussian times a sum of high-frequency flux-noise Lorentzian plus charge-noise spectrum. The multilevel MRT rate
\Gamma_{0}
measured as a function of the external flux
\Phi^{x}
demonstrates many peaks, each related to the resonance between the initial state in the left well and some of the states in the target well. These peaks and the valleys between them
contain information about both flux and charge sources of noise as presented in Ref.
AW2022
Flux noise mainly defines the shape of the MRT peaks, whereas the effects of charge noise are especially visible in inter-peak valleys.

Appendix A
Derivation of master equations

Here we derive a set of master equations describing incoherent tunneling between left and right wells in the presence of flux and charge noise. We follow a hybrid approach to the theory of open quantum systems developed in Ref.
ASMA2018
. Within this approach an interaction of the system (rf-SQUID) with the bath described by diagonal elements of the total Hamiltonian is treated precisely, whereas off-diagonal elements of the Hamiltonian are considered as a perturbation. Other ways to derive master equations for open quantum systems can be found in Refs.
Lidar2013
;
Lidar2020
.
It should be emphasized that a perturbative
treatment of a system-bath interaction does not work for modern quantum annealers as it is shown in Ref.
Lidar2022
. The same is true for many problems of quantum biology
Lambert2013
. Here we could mention Refs.
Kubo1989
;
Aki2005
;
Aki2009
;
Ghosh2011
where authors have tried to go beyond the perturbation theory.

A.1
Schrödinger equation

In the left-right basis and in the Schrödinger representation the system-bath Hamiltonian has the form

\displaystyle H_{SB}=\sum_{n}(E_{n}-I_{n}\delta\Phi)|n\rangle\langle n|

\displaystyle-\sum_{m\neq n}\Big(\frac{\Delta_{mn}}{2}+I_{mn}\delta\Phi+V_{mn}\delta q\Big)|m\rangle\langle n|+H_{B}.

(83)

Here
I_{mn}
and
V_{mn}
are matrix elements (
21
) of current and voltage, with
I_{n}\equiv I_{nn},
and

\displaystyle H_{B}=H_{B}^{\Phi}+H_{B}^{q}

(84)

is the Hamiltonian of the flux-noise and charge noise heat bath.
A time evolution of the system-bath wave function is governed by the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi_{SB}(t)\rangle=H_{SB}|\Psi_{SB}(t)\rangle.

(85)

A formal solution of this equation can be written as

\displaystyle|\Psi_{SB}(t)\rangle=U(t)|\Psi_{SB}(0)\rangle,

(86)

with the unitary matrix

\displaystyle U(t)=e^{-iH_{SB}t}.

(87)

In the interaction picture of quantum mechanics the solution of the
Schrödinger equation (
85
) can be found in a few steps.
We begin with removing the bath Hamiltonian from the Hamiltonian (
A.1
). To do this we assume that

\displaystyle|\Psi_{SB}(t)\rangle=U_{B}(t)|\Psi(t)\rangle.

(88)

Here the unitary matrix

\displaystyle U_{B}(t)=e^{-iH_{B}t}

(89)

determines the free evolution of bath variables. The function
|\Psi(t)\rangle
obeys the equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi(t)\rangle=H(t)|\Psi(t)\rangle,

(90)

with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(t)=U_{B}^{\dagger}H_{SB}U_{B}-iU_{B}^{\dagger}\dot{U}_{B}.

(91)

Taking into account Eq. (
89
) we obtain

\displaystyle H(t)=\sum_{n}[E_{n}-I_{n}\delta\Phi(t)]|n\rangle\langle n|

\displaystyle-\sum_{m\neq n}\Big[\frac{\Delta_{mn}}{2}+I_{mn}\delta\Phi(t)+V_{mn}\delta q(t)\Big]|m\rangle\langle n|.

(92)

Here

\displaystyle\delta\Phi(t)=U_{B}^{\dagger}(t)\,\delta\Phi\,U_{B}(t),\;\delta q(t)=U_{B}^{\dagger}(t)\,\delta q\,U_{B}(t)\;

(93)

are free-evolving flux-noise and charge-noise bath operators.

Our next goal is to remove diagonal terms from the Hamiltonian (
A.1
). It can be done with the unitary matrix

\displaystyle U_{\rm diag}(t)=\sum_{n}e^{-iE_{n}t}\,U_{n}(t)|n\rangle\langle n|,

(94)

where

\displaystyle U_{n}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\Big\{i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau I_{n}\delta\Phi(\tau)\Big\},

(95)

and
\mathcal{T}
denotes time ordering of subsequent operators.
The solution of Eq. (
90
) can be written as

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle=U_{\rm diag}(t)|\tilde{\Psi}(t)\rangle.

(96)

The state
|\tilde{\Psi}(t)\rangle
obeys the equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\tilde{\Psi}(t)\rangle=\tilde{H}(t)|\tilde{\Psi}(t)\rangle

(97)

with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(t)=U_{\rm diag}^{\dagger}\,H(t)\,U_{\rm diag}-iU_{\rm diag}^{\dagger}\,\dot{U}_{\rm diag}.

(98)

Considering Eqs. (
94
) and (
95
) we find that the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(t)=-\sum_{k\neq l}\tilde{Q}_{kl}(t)\,|k\rangle\langle l|,

(99)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{kl}(t)=e^{i\omega_{kl}t}\;Q_{kl}(t).

(100)

Here

\displaystyle Q_{kl}(t)=

\displaystyle U_{k}^{\dagger}(t)\Big[\frac{\Delta_{mn}}{2}{+}I_{mn}\delta\Phi(t){+}V_{mn}\delta q(t)\Big]U_{l}(t).

(101)

is the bath operator, and

\omega_{kl}=E_{k}-E_{l}
is the energy spectrum.

The solution of Eq. (
97
),

\displaystyle|\tilde{\Psi}(t)\rangle=\tilde{U}(t)|\tilde{\Psi}(0)\rangle,

(102)

is determined by the unitary matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\Big[-i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\tilde{H}(\tau)\Big].

(103)

This matrix is a functional of free bath operators
\tilde{Q}_{kl}(t),

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\Big[i\sum_{kl}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\tilde{Q}_{kl}(\tau)\,|k(\tau)\rangle\langle l(\tau)|\Big].

(104)

Here we use Eq. (
99
) and assume for a moment that the system’s basis states
|k(\tau)\rangle,|l(\tau)\rangle

depend on time as it happens, for example, for the instantaneous basis in quantum annealing.
ASMA2018

Combining Eqs. (
88
), (
96
), and (
102
) we find another representation of the system-bath evolution matrix (
87
):

\displaystyle U(t)=U_{B}(t)\,U_{\rm diag}(t)\,\tilde{U}(t).

(105)

A.2
Density matrix of the system

The system-bath density matrix
\rho_{SB}(t)
is defined in terms of the state (
86
),

\displaystyle\rho_{SB}(t)=

\displaystyle|\Psi_{SB}(t)\rangle\langle\Psi_{SB}(t)|=U(t)\rho_{SB}(0)U^{\dagger}(t),

(106)

with the initial condition
\rho_{SB}(0)=|\Psi_{SB}(0)\rangle\langle\Psi_{SB}(0)|.

The system’s density matrix
\rho_{S}
is defined as a trace of the total matrix over bath degrees of freedom,

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(t)=\textrm{Tr}_{B}\rho_{SB}(t)=\sum_{mn}\rho_{nm}\,|n\rangle\langle m|.

(107)

Matrix elements of the system density operator in the left-right basis are defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{nm}(t)=\langle n|\rho_{S}(t)|m\rangle=\textrm{Tr}_{B}\langle n|\rho_{SB}(t)|m\rangle.

(108)

Taking into account Eq. (
A.2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{nm}(t)={\rm Tr}_{B}\langle n|U(t)\rho_{SB}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)|m\rangle=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{B}\sum_{k}\langle n|U(t)|k\rangle\langle k|\rho_{SB}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)|m\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}{\rm Tr}_{B}\langle k|\rho_{SB}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)|m\rangle\langle n|U(t)|k\rangle.

(109)

Here we resort to the completeness condition:
\sum_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|=1
. A trace over system states is defined as

{\rm Tr}_{S}(\ldots)=\sum_{k}\langle k|(\ldots)|k\rangle.

Using this definition we find that the matrix elements of the system’s density operator are represented by a trace

{\rm Tr}={\rm Tr}_{B}{\rm Tr}_{S}
over both system and bath degrees of freedom:

\displaystyle\rho_{nm}(t)={\rm Tr}[\rho_{SB}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)|m\rangle\langle n|U(t)].

(110)

The unitary matrix
U(t)
is given by Eq. (
105
), therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{nm}(t)=

\displaystyle e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\,{\rm Tr}[\rho_{SB}(0)\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)U_{m}^{\dagger}(t)|m\rangle\langle n|U_{n}(t)\tilde{U}(t)].\;

(111)

We are interested in a time evolution of a probability
P_{n}(t)
to find the system (rf-SQUID) in the state
|n\rangle.
This probability is determined by the diagonal elements of the system density matrix:

\displaystyle P_{n}(t)=\rho_{nn}(t)={\rm Tr}[\rho_{SB}(0)\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)|n\rangle\langle n|\tilde{U}(t)].

(112)

Assuming that at the beginning of evolution the system-bath density matrix is factorized via
\rho_{SB}(0)=\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{B}(0)
, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{n}(t)={\rm Tr}_{S}\Big[\rho_{S}(0)\Big<\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)|n\rangle\langle n|\tilde{U}(t)\Big>\Big].

(113)

Here a notation
\langle\ldots\rangle
means averaging over bath degrees of freedom,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle={\rm Tr}_{B}[\rho_{B}(0)\mathcal{A}],

(114)

with
\mathcal{A}
being an arbitrary system-bath operator.

It is convenient to introduce a Heisenberg operator

\displaystyle\sigma_{mn}(t)=\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)|m\rangle\langle n|\tilde{U}(t),

(115)

which depends on the unitary matrix
\tilde{U}(t)
only. We see from Eq. (
113
) that the probability
P_{n}
is determined by the operator

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(t)\equiv\sigma_{nn}(t)=\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)|n\rangle\langle n|\tilde{U}(t)

(116)

averaged over system and bath initial states:

\displaystyle P_{n}(t)={\rm Tr}_{S}[\rho_{S}(0)\langle\sigma_{n}(t)\rangle].

(117)

A.3
Heisenberg equation

A time evolution of the probability
P_{n}(t)
to find the system in the state
|n\rangle
is determined by the operator
\sigma_{n}(t)
as it follows from Eq. (
117
). Here we derive a Heisenberg equation for a more general operator
\sigma_{mn}(t)
defined by Eq. (
115
).
To do this we take a time derivative of the operator (
115
):

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{mn}=i\,\tilde{U}^{\dagger}\,[\tilde{H},|m\rangle\langle n|]\,\tilde{U}.

(118)

We consider that
\dot{\tilde{U}}=-i\,\tilde{H}\,\tilde{U},
as it is evident from Eq. (
103
). Using Eq. (
99
) one calculates a commutator in Eq. (
118
) and obtains a Heisenberg equation for the operator (
115
):

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{mn}=i\sum_{k}\Big[\tilde{U}^{\dagger}\tilde{Q}_{nk}|m\rangle\langle k|\tilde{U}-\tilde{U}^{\dagger}\tilde{Q}_{km}|k\rangle\langle n|\tilde{U}\Big].\;

(119)

All operators in Eq. (
119
) are taken at the moment of time
t
.

A.4
Bath correlators

The Heisenberg equation (
119
) includes free-evolving bath operators
\tilde{Q}_{nk}(t)
and

defined in Eq. (

We assume that non-diagonal system-bath coupling described by the Hamiltonian (

is mainly determined by the second-order correlation function

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{mn}^{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle\tilde{Q}_{mn}(t)\tilde{Q}_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle.

We notice that
\langle\tilde{Q}_{mn}(t)\rangle=0.
According to Eq. (

is related to the variable

defined in Eq. (

). Correlators of these two variables,

are related to each other,

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{mn}^{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\,e^{i\omega_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}t^{\prime}}K_{mn}^{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime}).

(122)

It is shown

that
components with
m^{\prime}=m
and
n^{\prime}=n
dominate in the set of correlators (
121
), therefore,

\displaystyle K_{mn}^{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\,K_{mn}(t,t^{\prime}),

(123)

where

\displaystyle K_{mn}(t,t^{\prime}){=}\langle Q_{mn}(t)Q_{nm}(t^{\prime})\rangle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\,S_{mn}(\omega).

(124)

This correlator is related to the spectrum
S_{mn}(\omega).

Similar to Eq. (
123
), the correlator
\tilde{K}_{mn}^{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})
is determined by the function

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{mn}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle\tilde{Q}_{mn}(t)\tilde{Q}_{nm}(t^{\prime})\rangle=

\displaystyle e^{i\omega_{mn}(t{-}t^{\prime})}K_{mn}(t,t^{\prime}).

(125)

The correlator
\tilde{K}_{mn}(t,t^{\prime})
is defined by the spectral function
\tilde{S}_{mn}(\omega),

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{mn}(t,t^{\prime})=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}\,\tilde{S}_{mn}(\omega).

(126)

It follows from Eq. (
A.4
) that

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{mn}(\omega)=S_{mn}(\omega+\omega_{mn}).

(127)

The spectrum

can be calculated using Eqs. (

) and (

ASMA2018

\displaystyle S_{mn}(\omega)=\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}G_{mn}^{L}(\omega-\Omega)\times

(128)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{mn}(\omega)=\frac{\Delta_{mn}}{2}+\omega\frac{I_{mn}}{I_{m}-I_{n}}

(129)

is a frequency-dependent tunneling rate,

is a low-frequency envelope represented by a Gaussian (

The spectrum (

since by definition

(130)

therefore,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{nm}(-\omega)=e^{-\omega/T}\,e^{-\omega_{mn}/T}\,\tilde{S}_{mn}(\omega).

It follows from Eq. (
A.5
) that this force has zero mean value:
\langle\xi_{mn}\rangle=0.
An explicit expression for
\xi_{mn}
lets us to calculate correlation functions of fluctuation forces.

Taking into account definitions (
A.5
) of system-bath pairings we write Eq. (
143
) in the form

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{mn}=\xi_{mn}+i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\tilde{K}_{kn}(t^{\prime},t)\frac{\delta\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)}{\delta\tilde{Q}_{kn}(t^{\prime})}|m\rangle\langle k|\tilde{U}(t)-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\tilde{K}_{mk}(t^{\prime},t)\frac{\delta\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)}{\delta\tilde{Q}_{mk}(t^{\prime})}|k\rangle\langle n|\tilde{U}(t)

\displaystyle+i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\tilde{K}_{nk}(t,t^{\prime})U^{\dagger}(t)|m\rangle\langle k|\frac{\delta\tilde{U}(t)}{\delta\tilde{Q}_{kn}(t^{\prime})}-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\tilde{K}_{km}(t,t^{\prime})U^{\dagger}(t)|k\rangle\langle n|\frac{\delta\tilde{U}(t)}{\delta\tilde{Q}_{mk}(t^{\prime})}.

(145)

Using here formulas (
A.5
) and (
140
) for functional derivatives of the unitary matrix
\tilde{U}
and also a definition (
115
) we obtain a quantum Langevin equation for the system operator
\sigma_{mn},

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{mn}+\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\Big\{\tilde{K}_{nk}(t,t^{\prime})\sigma_{mk}(t)\sigma_{kn}(t^{\prime})+\tilde{K}_{mk}(t^{\prime},t)\sigma_{mk}(t^{\prime})\sigma_{kn}(t)\Big\}

\displaystyle-\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\Big\{\tilde{K}_{km}(t,t^{\prime})\sigma_{kn}(t)\sigma_{mk}(t^{\prime})+\tilde{K}_{kn}(t^{\prime},t)\sigma_{kn}(t^{\prime})\sigma_{mk}(t)\Big\}=\xi_{mn}.

(146)

Averaging this equation (taken at
m{=}n
) over bath fluctuations produces Eq. (
A.5
) for the probability
P_{n}{=}\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle.

We are interested in a time evolution of the off-diagonal elements
\sigma_{mn}
in the limit of weak system-bath coupling described by the non-diagonal Hamiltonian
(
99
). In this case, in the Langevin equation (
A.6
), we can reduce system operators, such
\sigma_{mk}(t),
to those taken at the earlier moment of time
t^{\prime}
assuming that
\sigma_{mk}(t)\simeq\sigma_{mk}(t^{\prime}).

This assumption allow us to calculate products of system operators, such as

\sigma_{mk}(t)\sigma_{kn}(t^{\prime})\simeq\sigma_{mk}(t^{\prime})\sigma_{kn}(t^{\prime})=\sigma_{mn}(t^{\prime}),

and so on. The Langevin equation (
A.6
) has now the simple form:

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}_{mn}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Sigma_{mn}(t,t^{\prime})\,\sigma_{mn}(t^{\prime})=\xi_{mn},

(147)

with the self-energy part

\displaystyle\Sigma_{mn}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{l}[\tilde{K}_{nl}(t,t^{\prime})+\tilde{K}_{ml}(t^{\prime},t)]\,\theta(t{-}t^{\prime}),\;

(148)

and with the fluctuation force
\xi_{mn}
defined by Eq. (
A.6
).

A self-energy part (
148
)
has a Fourier transform:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{mn}(\omega)=\int_{{-}\infty}^{{+}\infty}d\tau\;e^{-i\omega\tau}\,\Sigma_{mn}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime}(\omega){+}i\,\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime\prime}(\omega),

(149)

with real and imaginary components

\displaystyle\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime}(\omega)=\sum_{l}\frac{\tilde{S}_{nl}(\omega)+\tilde{S}_{ml}(-\omega)}{2},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\sum_{l}\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}\,\left[\frac{\tilde{S}_{nl}(\Omega)}{\omega-\Omega}+\frac{\tilde{S}_{ml}(\Omega)}{\omega+\Omega}\right].

(150)

The spectrum
\tilde{S}_{ml}(\omega)
is defined by Eqs. (
127
) and (
A.4
).
We notice that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{nm}^{\prime}(-\omega)=\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime}(\omega),\;\Sigma_{nm}^{\prime\prime}(-\omega)=-\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime\prime}(\omega).

(151)

In order to solve Eq. (
148
) we introduce the retarded Green function
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t,t^{\prime})
satisfying the following equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t{-}t^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\,\Sigma_{mn}(t{-}t_{1})\,\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t_{1}{-}t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(152)

The Green function
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t,t_{1})
is defined by its Fourier image
\mathcal{G}_{mn}(\omega)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{mn}(\omega)=\int d\tau e^{i\omega\tau}\,\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(\tau)=\frac{1}{-i\omega+\Sigma_{mn}(\omega)},

(153)

where
\tau=t-t_{1}.
We note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{mn}(-\omega)=\mathcal{G}_{nm}^{*}(\omega).

(154)

With the Green function
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t{-}t^{\prime})
the system operator
\sigma_{mn}(t)
can be expressed in terms of the operator
\sigma_{mn}(t^{\prime})
taken at the earlier time
t^{\prime}
plus a contribution of the fluctuation force:

\displaystyle\sigma_{mn}(t)=\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t{-}t^{\prime})\,\sigma_{mn}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}dt_{1}\,\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t{-}t_{1})\,\xi_{mn}(t_{1}).

(155)

A.7
Master equations

The last term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
A.6
) describes an effect of the fluctuation force during the correlation time
t-t^{\prime}\sim\tau_{c}
of the bath correlator
\tilde{K}_{mn}(t,t^{\prime}).
This effect is small and can be neglected. It means that

\displaystyle\sigma_{mn}(t)\simeq\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t-t^{\prime})\sigma_{mn}(t^{\prime}),

(156)

therefore,

\displaystyle\sigma_{mn}(t)\sigma_{nm}(t^{\prime})=\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t-t^{\prime})\sigma_{m}(t^{\prime}),

(157)

and so on.
Substituting such correlators into Eq. (
A.5
) we derive a set of master equations for the probability distribution

\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle
of the system over basis states
|n\rangle
:

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\sigma}_{n}\rangle+\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\Big[\tilde{K}_{nm}(t,t^{\prime})\,\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{nm}(t-t^{\prime})+K_{nm}(t^{\prime},t)\,\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t-t^{\prime})\Big]\langle\sigma_{n}(t^{\prime})\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\Big[\tilde{K}_{mn}(t,t^{\prime})\,\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(t-t^{\prime})+K_{mn}(t^{\prime},t)\,\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{nm}(t-t^{\prime})\Big]\langle\sigma_{m}(t^{\prime})\rangle.

(158)

In Eq. (
A.7
) moments of time
t
and
t^{\prime}
are separated by a correlation time
\tau_{c}
of the bath correlator
\tilde{K}_{mn}(t{-}t^{\prime}).
A time evolution of the probability
P_{n}(t)=\langle\sigma_{n}(t)\rangle
during this interval is insignificant. Therefore, in the collision terms of the master equation (
A.7
) we can assume that
\langle\sigma_{n}(t^{\prime})\rangle\simeq\langle\sigma_{n}(t)\rangle.
With this assumption the master equation (
A.7
) has the standard form

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{n}+\sum_{n}\Gamma_{mn}P_{n}=\sum_{m}\Gamma_{nm}P_{m},

(159)

with the relaxation matrix

\displaystyle\Gamma_{mn}{=}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau[\tilde{K}_{nm}(\tau)\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{nm}(\tau){+}\tilde{K}_{nm}({-}\tau)\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(\tau)].\;

(160)

Considering that the running moment of time
t\gg\tau_{c}
we assume in Eq. (
160
) that the upper limit
t=\infty.
This allows us to write the rates
\Gamma_{mn}
in the form

\displaystyle\Gamma_{mn}=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\tilde{S}_{nm}(\omega)\,[\mathcal{G}_{mn}(\omega)+\mathcal{G}^{*}_{mn}(\omega)],

(161)

where
\tilde{S}_{nm}(\omega)=S_{nm}(\omega{-}\omega_{mn})
is the shifted spectrum (
A.4
) of bath fluctuations, and
\mathcal{G}_{mn}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform (
153
) of the Green function
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{mn}(\tau).

The real and imaginary components of the self-energy function
\Sigma_{mn}(\omega)
involved into Eq. (
153
) can be represented as combinations of linewidths
\gamma_{n}(\omega),\gamma_{m}(-\omega)
and frequency shifts
\delta_{n}(\omega),\delta_{m}(-\omega)
of
n
and
m
energy levels:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime}(\omega)=\gamma_{n}(\omega)+\gamma_{m}(-\omega),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{mn}^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\delta_{n}(\omega)-\delta_{m}(-\omega),

(162)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}S_{nk}(\omega+\omega_{nk}),

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\omega)=\sum_{k}\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}\frac{S_{nk}(\Omega+\omega_{nk})}{\omega-\Omega}.

(163)

The frequency shift can be written also as

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}\frac{S_{nk}(\omega{+}\omega_{nk}{-}\Omega)-S_{nk}(\omega{+}\omega_{nk}{+}\Omega)}{\Omega}.\;

(164)

With this information in mind we arrive at the final expression for the relaxation rates (
160
):

\displaystyle\Gamma_{mn}=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,S_{nm}(\omega-\omega_{mn})\times

\displaystyle\frac{2[\gamma_{n}(\omega)+\gamma_{m}(-\omega)]}{[\omega-\delta_{n}(\omega)+\delta_{m}(-\omega)]^{2}+[\gamma_{n}(\omega)+\gamma_{m}(-\omega)]^{2}}.

(165)

In the limit of zero linewidths
\gamma_{m},\gamma_{n}
and zero frequency shifts
\delta_{n},\delta_{m}
the rate (
A.7
) turns to the standard Bloch-Redfield form:
\Gamma_{mn}=S_{nm}(\omega_{nm}).

The master equation (
159
)
describes both, equilibrium and nonequilibrium, situations.
In the equilibrium state we have

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{n}=\sum_{m}(\Gamma_{nm}P_{m}-\Gamma_{mn}P_{n})=0.

(166)

Taking into account a formula (
161
) for
\Gamma_{mn},
and also Eq. (
131
) we find that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{nm}P_{m}-\Gamma_{mn}P_{n}=

\displaystyle 2\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\tilde{S}_{mn}(\omega){\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}_{nm}(\omega)\Big[P_{m}{-}e^{{-}\omega/T}e^{{-}\omega_{mn}/T}\,P_{n}\Big],\;

(167)

where
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}_{nm}(\omega)
is the real part of the Green function defined by Eq. (
153
),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}_{nm}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{m}(\omega)+\gamma_{n}(-\omega)}{[\omega-\delta_{m}(\omega)+\delta_{n}(-\omega)]^{2}+[\gamma_{m}(\omega)+\gamma_{n}(-\omega)]^{2}}.

(168)

This function peaks at
\omega\simeq\delta_{m}(0)-\delta_{n}(0)
. Therefore, the equilibrium condition
\dot{P}_{n}{=}0
given by Eq. (
166
) is satisfied provided that

\displaystyle\frac{P_{n}}{P_{m}}=\exp\Big(\frac{\tilde{E}_{m}-\tilde{E}_{n}}{T}\Big),

(169)

where
\tilde{E}_{m}
and
\tilde{E}_{n}
are shifted energy levels defined as
\tilde{E}_{n}=E_{n}+\delta_{n}(0)
.
The ratio (
169
) is specific for the Boltzmann distribution
P_{n}
over shifted energy levels of the rf-SQUID.
This means that the master equations (
159
) is in agreement with the thermal equilibrium conditions.
