Sharpness of Bernoulli percolation via couplings
Hugo Vanneuville

Abstract
In this paper, we consider Bernoulli percolation on a locally finite, transitive and infinite graph (e.g. the hypercubic lattice ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} ). We prove the following estimate, where θ n ​ ( p ) \theta_{n}(p) is the probability that there is a path of p p -open edges from 0 0 to the sphere of radius n n : ∀ p ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] , ∀ m , n ≥ 1 , θ 2 ​ n ​ ( p − 2 ​ θ m ​ ( p ) ) ≤ C ​ θ n ​ ( p ) 2 n / m . \forall p\in[0,1],\forall m,n\geq 1,\hskip 8.50012pt\theta_{2n}(p-2\theta_{m}(p))\leq C\frac{\theta_{n}(p)}{2^{n/m}}. This result implies that θ n ​ ( p ) \theta_{n}(p) decays exponentially fast in the subcritical phase. It also implies the mean-field lower bound in the supercritical phase. We thus provide a new proof of the sharpness of the phase transition for Bernoulli percolation. Contrary to the previous proofs of sharpness, we do not rely on any differential formula. The main novelty is a stochastic domination result which is inspired by [Russo, 1982]. We also discuss a consequence of our result for percolation in high dimensions, where it can be seen as a near-critical sharpness estimate.

Introduction

1.1
Sharpness of the phase transition

In this paper, we consider Bernoulli bond percolation on a locally finite, vertex-transitive and countably infinite graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
(e.g. the hypercubic lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
). This model is defined as follows: each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
is open with some probability
p\in[0,1]
and closed otherwise, independently of the other edges. We let
\mathbb{P}_{p}
denote the corresponding product probability measure on
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{E}}
, where
1
means open and
0
means closed.

We fix a vertex
0\in\mathcal{V}
once and for all and we let
\textup{dist}:\mathcal{V}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
denote the graph distance on
\mathcal{G}
. Also, for every
n\geq 1
, we let
S_{n}\subset B_{n}\subset\mathcal{V}
denote the sphere and ball of radius
n
around
0
, i.e.

1

Here and in all the paper,
n
is an integer.
m,i,j,k
will also always be integers.

S_{n}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}:\textup{dist}(0,v)=n\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptB_{n}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}:\textup{dist}(0,v)\leq n\}.

We let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the set of edges in
B_{n}
, i.e.

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{\{v,w\}\in\mathcal{E}:v,w\in B_{n}\}.

If
W\subset\mathcal{V}
and
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we let
\{v\leftrightarrow W\}
denote the event that there is a path of open edges from
v
to some site of
W
and we let
\{v\nleftrightarrow W\}=\{v\leftrightarrow W\}^{c}
. We also let

\theta_{n}(p)=\mathbb{P}_{p}[0\leftrightarrow S_{n}]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\theta(p)=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\theta_{n}(p).

(And we use the convention that
\theta_{n}(p)=0
for every

.) The critical point is

p_{c}=\inf\{p\in[0,1]:\theta(p)>0\}.

We refer to

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

. Then, for every
p\in[0,1]
and

,

\theta_{2n}(p-2\theta_{m}(p))\leq C_{0}\frac{\theta_{n}(p)}{2^{n/m}}.

Remark 1.2
.

One can actually replace

(cf. the proof at the beginning of Section
2
). This can be useful when

Remark 1.3
.

If

then Theorem

applied to

gives some “slightly subcritical” information. In the case of percolation in high dimensions, Theorem
1.1
applied to

is sharp in some sense. See Section

.

Theorem
1.1
implies the following theorem that is often referred to as sharpness of the phase transition (or more precisely subcritical sharpness and the mean-field lower bound). The first item – i.e. subcritical sharpness – has originally been proven independently in
[

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
p\in[0,1]
.

•

If
p<p_{c}
, then there exist

such that, for every
n\geq 1
,
\theta_{n}(p)\leq Ce^{-cn}
;

•

If
p>p_{c}
, then
\theta(p)\geq(p-p_{c})/2

Proof.

Let us first prove exponential decay in the subcritical phase: Let
p<p_{c}
. We observe that, for every
p^{\prime}\in(p,p_{c})
, there exists
m\geq 1
such that
p\leq p^{\prime}-2\theta_{m}(p^{\prime})
. We fix such

. Theorem
1.1
applied to

(with the crude bound
\theta_{n}(p^{\prime})\leq 1
) implies that

\forall n\geq 1,\hskip 10.00002pt\theta_{2n}(p)\leq C_{0}\exp\Big(-\frac{\log 2}{m}n\Big).

This implies the result for
n
even, so also for
n
odd since
\theta_{n}(p)
is decreasing in
n
.

Let us now prove the mean field lower bound: Let
p\in[0,1]
and
m\geq 1
. Theorem
1.1
implies that
\theta(p-2\theta_{m}(p))=0
. As a result,
p-2\theta_{m}(p)\leq p_{c}
. If we let
m
go to
+\infty
, we obtain that
\theta(p)\geq(p-p_{c})/2
.
∎

Remark 1.5
.

The following result was proven in
[
CC87
]
:
\theta(p)\geq(p-p_{c})/p
. We will also obtain this result, see the discussion below Corollary
1.6
. However, we will not obtain the bound
\theta(p)\geq\frac{p-p_{c}}{p(1-p_{c})}
proven in
[

]

1.2
A stochastic domination result

The main novelty of this paper is a stochastic domination result. The quantity that will appear in this result is in some sense more
1-\theta_{n}
than
\theta_{n}
itself, so we let

\pi_{n}(p)=\mathbb{P}_{p}[0\nleftrightarrow S_{n}]=1-\theta_{n}(p)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\pi(p)=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\pi_{n}(p)=1-\theta(p).

Before stating our result, let us state a corollary. We let
\preceq
denote stochastic domination between probability measures (i.e. the existence of an increasing coupling). We recall that
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{\{v,w\}\in\mathcal{E}:v,w\in B_{n}\}
.

Corollary 1.6
.

For any
m,n\geq 1
and
p\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}_{p\pi_{m}(p)}\big[\omega_{|\mathcal{E}_{n}}\in\cdot\big]\preceq\mathbb{P}_{p}\big[\omega_{|\mathcal{E}_{n}}\in\cdot\;\big|\;0\nleftrightarrow S_{n+m}\big].

By applying this to the decreasing event
\{0\nleftrightarrow S_{n}\}
, we obtain that

\pi_{n}(p\pi_{m}(p))\geq\frac{\pi_{n}(p)}{\pi_{n+m}(p)}.

(1)

One can interpret Corollary
1.6
as follows in the case
n\gg m\gg 1
: up to a small error (that corresponds to what happens in the thin annulus
B_{n+m}\setminus B_{n}
), multiplying
p
by
\pi_{m}(p)
(which is very close to
1
if
p<p_{c}
) has more effect than conditioning on
\{0\nleftrightarrow S_{n+m}\}
.

Inequality (
1
) implies the inequality
\theta(p)\geq(p-p_{c})/p
from Remark
1.5
:

Proof.

Let
p\in(p_{c},1)
and
m\geq 1
. Inequality (
1
) implies that
\pi(p\pi_{m}(p))=1
. Therefore,
p\pi_{m}(p)\leq p_{c}
. If we let
m
go to
+\infty
, we obtain that
\pi(p)\leq p_{c}/p
, or equivalently that
\theta(p)\geq(p-p_{c})/p
.
∎

When the reader looked at Inequality (
1
), they may have wanted to make a telescopic product. So let us do this right now.

Lemma 1.7
.

For any
m\geq 1
and
p\in(0,1)
,

\prod_{i\geq 1}\pi_{im}(p\pi_{m}(p))\geq\pi_{m}(p).

Proof.

By (
1
),

\prod_{i\geq 1}\pi_{im}(p\pi_{m}(p))\geq\prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{\pi_{im}(p)}{\pi_{(i+1)m}(p)}=\frac{\pi_{m}(p)}{\pi(p)}\geq\pi_{m}(p).\qed

Remark 1.8
.

Even if we will use Lemma
1.7
rather than the present remark, let us note that a similar telescopic product already implies an interesting sharpness-type result. More precisely, let us prove in this remark that

\forall p<p_{c},\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{n\geq 1}\theta_{n}(p)<+\infty.

(2)

Proof of (
2
).

Let
p\in(0,p_{c})
. We first note that (
2
) is equivalent to
\prod_{n\geq 1}\pi_{n}(p)>0
, so let us prove this. Let
p^{\prime}\in(p,p_{c})
and
m\geq 1
such that
p\leq p^{\prime}\pi_{m}(p^{\prime})
. We have

\prod_{n\geq 1}\pi_{n}(p)\geq\prod_{n\geq 1}\pi_{n}(p^{\prime}\pi_{m}(p^{\prime}))\overset{\eqref{eq:cor}}{\geq}\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{\pi_{n}(p^{\prime})}{\pi_{n+m}(p^{\prime})}=\prod_{n=1}^{m}\pi_{n}(p^{\prime})>0.\qed

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we need a result that is analogous to Corollary
1.6
but that involves inhomogeneous percolation. Let
\textbf{{p}}\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{E}}
. In inhomogeneous percolation of parameter
p
, each edge
e
is open with probability
\textbf{{p}}_{e}
and closed otherwise, independently of the other edges. We let
\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}
denote the corresponding product probability measure on

and we let

\pi_{n}(\textbf{{p}})=\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}[0\nleftrightarrow S_{n}].

Moreover, for every
e=\{v,w\}\in\mathcal{E}
and
n\geq 1
, we choose to measure the probability that
e
is not connected to
S_{n}
by using the following quantity:

\pi_{n}(e,\textbf{{p}})=\min\{\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}[v\nleftrightarrow S_{n}],\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}[w\nleftrightarrow S_{n}]\}.

For every
m,n\geq 1
, we define
\textbf{{q}}_{n}^{m}:(0,1)^{\mathcal{E}}\rightarrow(0,1)^{\mathcal{E}}
by

\textbf{{q}}_{n}^{m}(\textbf{{p}})_{e}=\begin{cases}\textbf{{p}}_{e}\pi_{n+m}(e,\textbf{{p}})\text{ if $e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}$,}\\
\text{$\textbf{{p}}_{e}$ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(The values of
\textbf{{q}}_{n}^{m}
outside of

have no importance in the present section, but this choice will be useful later.)

Our more precise stochastic domination result is the following proposition.

Proposition 1.9
.

For any
m,n\geq 1
and
\textbf{{p}}\in(0,1)^{\mathcal{E}}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{q}}_{n}^{m}(\textbf{{p}})}\big[\omega_{|\mathcal{E}_{n}}\in\cdot\big]\preceq\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\omega_{|\mathcal{E}_{n}}\in\cdot\;\big|\;0\nleftrightarrow S_{n+m}\big].

By applying this to the decreasing event
\{0\nleftrightarrow S_{n}\}
, we obtain that

\pi_{n}(\textbf{{q}}_{n}^{m}(\textbf{{p}}))\geq\frac{\pi_{n}(\textbf{{p}})}{\pi_{n+m}(\textbf{{p}})}.

(3)

Corollary
1.6

Proof of Corollary
1.6
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and define
\textbf{{p}}\in(0,1)^{\mathcal{E}}
by
\forall e\in\mathcal{E},\textbf{{p}}_{e}=p
. Let

\{X=x\}
. We have

\mathbb{P}[X=x]=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\big[X_{e_{k}}=x_{e_{k}}\;\big|\;X_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big].

By construction of
(X,\textbf{{e}})
,
\{X_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\}=\{X_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\}\cap\{\textbf{{e}}_{[k]}=e_{[k]}\}
. As a result, the above equals

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{N}\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}\big[U_{e_{k}}\leq\mu_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\omega_{e_{k}}=1\;\big|\;\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=X_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\;\big|\;X_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\text{ if $x_{e_{k}}=1$}\\
\mathbb{P}\big[U_{e_{k}}>\mu_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\omega_{e_{k}}=1\;\big|\;\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=X_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\;\big|\;X_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\text{ if $x_{e_{k}}=0$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\mu_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\omega_{e_{k}}=x_{e_{k}}\;\big|\;\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]=\mu_{\textbf{{p}}}[x],

where we have used that
U_{e_{k}}
is independent of
X_{e_{[k-1]}}
(because the latter is measurable with respect to
U_{e_{[k-1]}}
).
∎

We are now in shape to prove Proposition
1.9
.

Proof of Proposition
1.9
.

It is sufficient to show that
X_{|F}\geq Y_{|F}
. As a result, it is sufficient to show that, if
(x,e)\in\{0,1\}^{E}\times\vec{E}
is in the support of
(X,\textbf{{e}})
, then for every
k
such that
e_{k}\in F=\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we have

\mu_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\omega_{e_{k}}=1\;\big|\;\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\geq\textbf{{q}}_{n}^{m}(\textbf{{p}})_{e_{k}}=\textbf{{p}}_{e_{k}}\pi_{n+m}(e_{k},\textbf{{p}}).

Let us fix such
(x,e)
and
k
. We recall that we say that an edge is pivotal for
A
if changing the state of this edge changes
1_{A}
. We first observe that, under
\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}
,
\omega_{e_{k}}
is independent of
A\cap\{\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\}\cap\{\text{$e_{k}$ is not piv.\ for $A$}\}
(because the latter is measurable with respect to
\omega_{|E\setminus\{e_{k}\}})
. By using this in the second equality below, we obtain that

\mu_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\omega_{e_{k}}=1\;\big|\;\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\\
=\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\omega_{e_{k}}=1,\text{$e_{k}$ is not piv.\ for $A$}\;\big|\;A,\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\\
=\textbf{{p}}_{e_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\text{$e_{k}$ is not piv.\ for $A$}\;\big|\;A,\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big].

We deduce that it is sufficient to prove that

\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\text{$e_{k}$ is not piv.\ for $A$}\;\big|\;A,\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\geq\pi_{n+m}(e_{k},\textbf{{p}}).

(7)

In order to prove this, we distinguish between two cases, where
\tau
is the last
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
at which at least one endpoint of
e_{j}
is connected to
0
by edges in
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{j-1}\}
that are open in
x
.

•

If
k>\tau
, then knowing that
\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}
is sufficient to know whether
A
holds or not, so the left-hand-side of (
7
) equals
1
;

•

If
k\leq\tau
, then we can write
e_{k}=\{v_{k},w_{k}\}
where
w_{k}
is connected to
0
by edges in
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k-1}\}
that are open in
x
. We observe that, if
e_{k}
is pivotal for
A
and if
\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}
, then there is an open path from
v_{k}
to
S_{n+m}
that does not use any edge from
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\}
. We let
B
denote this event. By this observation and then the Harris–FKG inequality (see
[
Gri99
, Theorem 2.4]
or
[
BR06
, Lemma 3]
) applied to
\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}[\,\cdot\mid\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[\text{$e_{k}$ is piv.\ for $A$}\;\big|\;A,\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[B\;\big|\;A,\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}\big[B\;\big|\;\omega_{e_{[k-1]}}=x_{e_{[k-1]}}\big]\text{ (this is where we have used Harris--FKG)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}[B]\leq\mathbb{P}_{\textbf{{p}}}[v_{k}\leftrightarrow S_{n+m}]\leq 1-\pi_{n+m}(e_{k},\textbf{{p}}).

This shows (
7
) and ends the proof.
∎

4
A consequence for percolation in high dimensions

Consider bond percolation on the hypercubic lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for some
d\geq 11
. The following near-critical sharpness estimate has recently been proven in
[
CHS21
,
HMS23
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
CHS21
, Theorem 1]
and
[
HMS23
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

There exist
c,C,\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and
n\geq 1
,

\frac{c}{n^{2}}\exp(-C\varepsilon^{1/2}n)\leq\theta_{n}(p_{c}-\varepsilon)\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}\exp(-c\varepsilon^{1/2}n).

In this subsection, we give a new (shorter) proof of the upper bound from Theorem
4.1
. (The techniques of the present paper seem quite useless in order to prove the lower bound.) We need the following result from
[
KN11
]
(that relies on
[
HS94
]
for
d\geq 19
and on
[
FvdH17
]
for the extension to
d\geq 11
):

There exist
c,C>0
such that, for every
n\geq 1
,

\frac{c}{n^{2}}\leq\theta_{n}(p_{c})\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}.

(8)

(Actually, we just need the upper bound.)

Proof of the upper bound from Theorem
4.1
.

We fix a constant
C
such that (
8
) holds. Also, we fix some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

(2C/\varepsilon_{0})^{1/2}\geq 1.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and write
m=\lceil(2C/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\rceil
. We have

\forall n\geq 1,\quad\theta_{n}(p_{c}-\varepsilon)\overset{\eqref{eq:KN11}}{\leq}\theta_{n}(p_{c}-2\theta_{m}(p_{c}))\overset{\text{\eqref{eq:KN11} and Thm.\ \ref{thm:quant}}}{\leq}\frac{C_{0}C}{n^{2}}\times\frac{1}{2^{n/m}}\\
\leq\frac{C_{0}C}{n^{2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{\log 2}{2\times(2C)^{1/2}}\varepsilon^{1/2}n\Big),

where in the last inequality we have used that
m\leq 2\times(2C/\varepsilon)^{1/2}
(because
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The reason why Theorem
1.1
is sharp (in some sense) for percolation in high dimensions is that the mean-field equality
\eta_{1}\nu=1
holds for these graphs. Here,
\eta_{1}
is the one-arm exponent and
\nu
is the correlation length exponent – see e.g. the recent work
[
DM23
]
for the definition of
\eta_{1}
and
\nu
, some inequalities involving these exponents, and further references. For percolation on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
d\geq 11
, it has been proven that these exponents exist and satisfy
\eta_{1}=1/\nu=2

[
HS94
,
KN11
,
FvdH17
]
. Let us note that, as one can see for instance by using Theorem
1.1
, if
\eta_{1}
and
\nu
exist, then they satisfy
\eta_{1}\nu\leq 1
.
