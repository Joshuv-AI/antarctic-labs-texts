Silence is bliss in a Platonic relationship Preprint: APS/123-QED
Cheuk-Him Yeung, Tom Shearer, William J. Parnell

Abstract
We describe an effective active cloaking strategy for the scalar Helmholtz equation in three dimensions where multipole active sources are located at the vertices of the Platonic solids. A “silent zone” is created interior to the imaginary Platonic solid and only the incident field remains in a defined region exterior to the silent zone and active source configuration. This distribution of the sources ensures that the implementation of the cloaking strategy is extremely efficient. In particular, once the multipole source amplitudes required at a single source location are determined, the other source amplitudes can be calculated by simple post-processing involving multiplication of the multipole source vector by a rotation matrix. The general nature of the problem means that the technique is relevant to any scalar wave field, including both acoustics and electromagnetism.



Over the last two decades significant excitement has been generated around the idea of cloaking objects, i.e. rendering them invisible to incident wave fields
Schurig et al. 2006
;
Miller 2006
;
Chen and Chan 2007
;
Norris 2008
;
Fleury and Alù 2014
. Cloaking strategies are either passive or active, the former requiring the fabrication of metamaterials that are able to manipulate the wave, steering it around the object regardless of the form of the incident field
Cai et al. 2007
;
Silveirinha et al. 2007
;
Zhang et al. 2011
;
Craster and Guenneau 2012
. In the context of active cloaking, sources are employed to suppress a field within a certain domain
Miller 2006
;
Vasquez et al. 2009a
;
Zheng et al. 2010
. Indeed, using active sources to modify fields has been of interest in science, technology and engineering for many decades: Paul Lueg’s patent in 1936 on the topic of anti-sound illustrates the use of active sources to suppress noise in specific regions of space
Guicking 1990
. Anti-sound and anti-vibration are huge areas of research and a plethora of techniques have been developed in order to address numerous problems in acoustic engineering
Nelson and Elliott 1991
;
Fuller et al. 1996
;
Guicking 2007
. An illustration of the relation between various methods in acoustics was provided by Cheer
Cheer 2016
. Specific choices of sources can ensure quiet zones and illusions
Zheng et al. 2010
;
Ma et al. 2013
and recent work has optimised control sources to reduce the scattered field in passive scenarios and cases with flow for the cloaking of specific objects
O’Neill et al. 2015
;
Eggler et al. 2019a
;
Eggler et al. 2019b
;
House et al. 2020
.

Over the last decade or so, interest has centred on active cloaking methods that are independent of the object to be cloaked. Miller’s method of cloaking measured particle motion close to the surface of the cloaking region and simultaneously excited necessary surface sources where each source amplitude depends on the measurements at all sensing points
Miller 2006
. This approach is rather limited as an active cloaking method because it cannot provide a relationship between the incident field and source amplitudes. Guevara-Vasquez et al. addressed this problem
Vasquez et al. 2009a
, describing an active source method that could use multipoles to create silent zones and simultaneously ensure that the active field is zero outside the silent zone. The integral equation formulation of the problem was employed, and subsequently converted to a linear system of equations for the source amplitudes. These amplitudes are linear functions of the incident field and it was shown, by construction, that active cloaking could be realised in two dimensions. An explicit form of the relations between the sources and incident field was provided in
Vasquez et al. 2011
. In particular multipole sources were used, Miller’s cloak was reproduced and numerical results were compared with SVD solutions of the linearised system
Vasquez et al. 2009a
;
Vasquez et al. 2009b
.

Further progress was made on the form of the source coefficients in
Norris et al. 2012
, where it was demonstrated that the integral representation of Vasquez et al.
Vasquez et al. 2009a
could be reduced to closed-form explicit formulas, which bypassed the requirement for a numerical solution. Analytical expressions were provided for general incidence and specifically for plane-wave incidence. This approach was then extended to two-dimensional (vector) elastodynamics
Norris et al. 2014
and to the thin-plate equation
Futhazar et al. 2015
. The attractive nature of this approach to active cloaking is that the source coefficients are independent of the object to be cloaked inside the silent zone. This is a benefit over approaches that are object-dependent where alternative approaches have to be taken in resonant regimes
O’Neill et al. 2016
. The latter approach however, does not suffer from large amplitudes in the vicinity of the active source regions.

Although there has been extensive work regarding the active manipulation of sound in three-dimensions in general settings, see e.g.
Elliott et al. 2012
;
Ahrens 2012
;
Onofrei and Platt 2018
;
Egarguin et al. 2020
, the active cloaking approach that is independent of the object to be cloaked has thus far predominantly been conducted in the two-dimensional setting, with the sole exception of the work of Guevara-Vasquez et al
Vasquez et al. 2013
who described the extension of the methods in
Vasquez et al. 2009a
;
Vasquez et al. 2009b
for the scalar Helmholtz equation in three dimensions. Expressions were derived for the active field in terms of an integral of the incident field and subsequently illustrated with four active sources.

Here we deduce integral expressions for active cloaking in three dimensions. We provide expressions for the active source coefficients and introduce a new methodology associated with distributions of the active sources on the vertices of the Platonic solids. This approach is fast and efficient to implement due to the sources residing on the circumsphere of the Platonic solid. Regardless of the form of the incident field, once the form of multipole source coefficients has been deduced for just
one
of the active sources, the remaining source amplitudes can be calculated merely by solving a matrix equation in terms of the determined multipole source vector, thus providing a clear mechanism and complete exposition of three-dimensional active cloaking for the scalar Helmholtz equation.

In summary, we have formulated an efficient three-dimensional active exterior cloaking strategy for the scalar Helmholtz equation, which employs multipolar sources distributed at the vertices of the Platonic solids to create a domain with zero total wave amplitude. This approach minimizes the incident wave impinging upon the object inside the region to suppress the scattered wave whilst simultaneously ensuring that the active source radiation is minimized. The Platonic distribution of the active sources means that we only need to determine the source amplitudes
as an integral expression at one source location in terms of the incident field and those at other locations will follow from simple post-processing, by exploiting the symmetry and regularity of the Platonic solids.

A.3
3. Truncation error of the source coefficients
q_{\ell,nm}

In (
11
), we have expressed the source amplitude
q_{\ell,nm}
as an infinite series in terms of the incident wave coefficient
Q_{\nu\mu}
. To numerically implement the cloaking approach, only a finite number of terms from the series can be retained which leads to truncation error. We will show that this error can be bounded by choosing an appropriate value of truncation order.

Recall from (
23
) in Part 1 the integral form of the active field
u_{d}
with

\displaystyle u_{d}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{ik}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\int_{\partial\widetilde{C}_{\ell}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{y})\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}V_{0}^{0}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\ \ \ -V_{0}^{0}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}u_{i}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathbf{n}]\ dS(\mathbf{y}).

(57)

If we substitute the expansion formula of
V_{0}^{0}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})
in (
31
) into (
57
) and compare the expression with the ansatz of
u_{d}
in (
2
), then we can write the source amplitude
q_{\ell,nm}
as

\displaystyle q_{\ell,nm}

\displaystyle=ik\int_{\partial C_{\ell}}\Big[u_{i}(\mathbf{y})\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}u_{i}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathbf{n}\Big]\ dS(\mathbf{y}).

(58)

For a plane wave incidence, we have by (
37
) that

\displaystyle u_{i}(\mathbf{y})=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{y}}=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\ell}}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mu=-\nu}^{\nu}Q_{\nu\mu}U^{\mu}_{\nu}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}),

(59)

where
Q_{\nu\mu}=4\pi i^{\nu}\overline{Y^{\mu}_{\nu}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}})}
. From (
58
) and (
59
) we observe that the truncation error of
q_{\ell,nm}
originates from the truncation of the expansion of
u_{i}
. If we discard the terms with
\nu>V
in (
59
) and denote the truncated source amplitude and incident plane wave as
\widetilde{q}_{\ell,nm}
and
\widetilde{u}_{i}
respectively, then the truncation error is

\displaystyle\ \ \ |q_{\ell,nm}-\widetilde{q}_{\ell,nm}|

\displaystyle\leq k\int_{\partial C_{\ell}}\Big\{|u_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})||\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}\cdot\mathbf{n}|

\displaystyle\ \ \ +|\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}||\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})]\cdot\mathbf{n}|\Big\}\ dS(\mathbf{y}).

(60)

It has already been shown in
Kennedy et al. 2007
and
Abhayapala et al. 2003
that

\displaystyle|u_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mu=-\nu}^{\nu}Q_{\nu\mu}U^{\mu}_{\nu}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\nu>V}^{\infty}(2\nu+1)|j_{\nu}(k|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}|)|

(61)

and when
V=\lceil eka_{0}/2\rceil+\Delta
(as
|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}|=a_{0}
for
\mathbf{y}\in\partial C_{\ell}
) with
\Delta
a nonnegative integer, (
61
) is bounded as

\displaystyle|u_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\frac{\sqrt{e/2}}{e-1}e^{-\Delta}\approx 0.6785e^{-\Delta}.

(62)

The result (
62
) states that the truncation error in
u_{i}
decays exponentially for truncation order greater than the critical value
\lceil eka_{0}/2\rceil
.
As for the truncation error of
\nabla u_{i}
, we can rewrite it in a form similar to (
61
):

\displaystyle\ \ \ |\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})]\cdot\mathbf{n}|

\displaystyle\leq k\sum_{\nu>V}^{\infty}(2\nu+1)|j_{\nu}^{\prime}(ka_{0})|

\displaystyle=k\sum_{\nu>V}^{\infty}(2\nu+1)\left|\frac{\nu}{2\nu+1}j_{\nu-1}(ka_{0})-\frac{\nu+1}{2\nu+1}j_{\nu+1}(ka_{0})\right|

\displaystyle\leq k\sum_{\nu>V}^{\infty}\left[\nu\left|j_{\nu-1}(ka_{0})\right|+(\nu+1)\left|j_{\nu+1}(ka_{0})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq k\sum_{\nu>V}^{\infty}\left[(2\nu-1)\left|j_{\nu-1}(ka_{0})\right|+(2\nu+3)\left|j_{\nu+1}(ka_{0})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq k\left[\sum_{\nu>V-1}^{\infty}(2\nu+1)|j_{\nu}(ka_{0})|+\sum_{\nu>V+1}^{\infty}(2\nu+1)|j_{\nu}(ka_{0})|\right]

(63)

where
j_{\nu}^{\prime}(ka_{0})
is expressed as a linear combination of

j_{\nu-1}(ka_{0})
and
j_{\nu+1}(ka_{0})
in the third line
Abramowitz et al. 1988
.
Taking
V=\lceil eka_{0}/2\rceil+\Delta+1
and referring to (
62
), we
have

\displaystyle|\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})]\cdot\mathbf{n}|

\displaystyle\leq k\frac{\sqrt{e/2}}{e-1}(1+e^{-2})e^{-\Delta}

\displaystyle\approx 0.7703ke^{-\Delta}.

(64)

Note that at this value of
V
, (
62
) becomes

\displaystyle|u_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\widetilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\frac{\sqrt{e/2}}{e^{2}-e}e^{-\Delta}\approx 0.2496e^{-\Delta}.

(65)

By
Lohöfer 1998
, the term
\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}
can be bounded as

\displaystyle|\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}|

\displaystyle=\left|A^{m}_{n}j_{n}(ka_{0})P^{m}_{n}(\cos\theta)e^{-im\varphi}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4\pi\rho_{m}}}|j_{n}(ka_{0})|,

(66)

with

\displaystyle\rho_{m}=\begin{cases}1,\ \ \ &m=0\\
2,\ \ \ &m\neq 0\end{cases}.

(67)

Due to the rotational symmetry of the source configuration, the surface area of each piece of spherical face
dC_{\ell}
is same as that of
dC_{1}
. The equation (
53
) defines the polar angle of the two end points of the circular arc
\cos\theta=g_{\ell}(\varphi)
with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{1}
for
\ell=2,\cdots,q+1
. For
-\pi/q\leq\varphi\leq\pi/q
, we thus have
g_{\ell}(\varphi)\geq a
. Using these arguments, we can show that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial C_{\ell}}\ dS(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial C_{1}}\ dS(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=q\int_{-\pi/q}^{\pi/q}\int_{0}^{\arccos[g_{\ell}(\varphi)]}a_{0}^{2}\sin\theta\ d\theta d\varphi

\displaystyle=qa_{0}^{2}\int_{-\pi/q}^{\pi/q}[1-g_{\ell}(\varphi)]\ d\varphi

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi a_{0}^{2}(1-a).

(68)

Substituting (
64
) – (
68
) into (
60

\displaystyle\ \ \ |q_{\ell,nm}-\widetilde{q}_{\ell,nm}|

\displaystyle\leq k^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4\pi\rho_{m}}}\frac{\sqrt{e/2}}{e-1}\left[e^{-1}|j_{n}^{\prime}(ka_{0})|+(1+e^{-2})|j_{n}(ka_{0})|\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\int_{\partial C_{\ell}}\ dS(\mathbf{y})\ e^{-\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi(ka_{0})^{2}(1-a)\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4\pi\rho_{m}}}\frac{\sqrt{e/2}}{e-1}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\left[e^{-1}|j_{n}^{\prime}(ka_{0})|+(1+e^{-2})|j_{n}(ka_{0})|\right]e^{-\Delta}.

(69)

While it has been shown that there exists a critical value for the truncation order

such that the truncation error of the source amplitude

approaches zero for

sufficiently larger than it, the exact minimum value of
V
required to achieve a certain level of accuracy in computation remains to be determined as the upper bound (

) depends on the arbitrary parameter
\Delta
. We are concerned about how an error in

will lead to discrepancy in the active field

generated by the

Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{d}|_{\ell}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\widetilde{q}_{\ell,nm}V^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}).

(70)

A measure to quantify this effect is the relative squared truncation error averaged over the surface of a unit sphere
\Omega
centred at
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
, denoted by
\varepsilon
, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{d}|_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})-\widetilde{u}_{d}|_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})\right|^{2}\ dS(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{d}|_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})\right|^{2}\ dS(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\left|q_{\ell,nm}-\widetilde{q}_{\ell,nm}\right|^{2}|h_{n}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}|)|^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}|q_{\ell,nm}|^{2}|h_{n}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}|)|^{2}}.

(71)

To establish an upper bound on
\varepsilon
, we can use the maximum value of
|q_{\ell,nm}|
as a proxy for the benchmark in (
71
). Note that with

\displaystyle|u_{i}(\mathbf{y})|=|e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{y}}|=1,

(72)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}u_{i}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathbf{n}|=|ik\widehat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\widehat{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\ell}}|\leq k

(73)

and (
66
), we have

\displaystyle|q_{\ell,nm}|

\displaystyle\leq k\int_{\partial C_{\ell}}\Big[|u_{i}(\mathbf{y})||\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}\cdot\mathbf{n}|

\displaystyle\ \ \ +|\overline{U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell})}||\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}u_{i}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\mathbf{n}|\Big]\ dS(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi(ka_{0})^{2}(1-a)\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4\pi\rho_{m}}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\left[|j_{n}^{\prime}(ka_{0})|+|j_{n}(ka_{0})|\right].

(74)

Substituting (
69
) and (
74
) into (
71
) and noting that
N
is the multipole order for each active source, we can obtain an upper bound for
\epsilon
in the form

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\frac{e}{2(e-1)^{2}}F(\mathbf{x})e^{-2\Delta}

(75)

with

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left[e^{-1}|j_{n}^{\prime}(ka_{0})|+(1+e^{-2})|j_{n}(ka_{0})|\right]^{2}H_{n}(\mathbf{x})}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left[|j_{n}^{\prime}(ka_{0})|+|j_{n}(ka_{0})|\right]^{2}H_{n}(\mathbf{x})},

(76)

\displaystyle H_{n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(2n+1)(n+1)|h_{n}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}|)|^{2}.

(77)

Given the tolerance level
E
, the minimum truncation order
V
required for the source amplitude
q_{\ell,nm}
such that

is thus

, where

\displaystyle\Delta=\left\lceil\frac{1}{2}\ln\left[\frac{e}{2E(e-1)^{2}}\max F(\mathbf{x})\right]\right\rceil.

(78)

Applying this expression of
V
onto the settings of Fig.

with

where

A.4
4. Computation of the surface integral
\mathcal{I}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\partial C_{1})

To implement the cloaking strategy discussed, we need to evaluate the surface integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n\nu}^{m\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\partial C_{1})=\int_{\partial C_{1}}\overline{Y^{m}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{1}})}Y^{\mu}_{\nu}(\widehat{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{1}})\ dS(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{1}),

(79)

which will be applied to determine the strengths of all active sources given that they are equidistant from the origin and all spherical faces
\partial C_{\ell}
have an identical geometric shape in a Platonic source distribution.
By the definition of the normalized spherical harmonic function and the parametric form of
\partial C_{1}
derived in Part 2 (and stated in (
12
) – (
15
)), we can write the integral as

\displaystyle\ \ \ \mathcal{I}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\partial C_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{0}^{2}A^{m}_{n}A^{\mu}_{\nu}\sum_{\ell=2}^{q+1}\int_{\varphi_{\ell}-\frac{\pi}{q}}^{\varphi_{\ell}+\frac{\pi}{q}}\bigg[\int^{1}_{g_{\ell}(\varphi)}P^{m}_{n}(w)P^{\mu}_{\nu}(w)\ dw\bigg]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times e^{i(\mu-m)\varphi}\ d\varphi.

(80)

Now replace the azimuthal angle
\varphi
by
\varphi+\varphi_{\ell}
in the integration with respect to
\varphi
. This substitution eliminates the dependence of
g_{\ell}(\varphi)
on the index
\ell
as can be seen from (
14
) and thus we may denote
g(\varphi)=g_{\ell}(\varphi+\varphi_{\ell})
. Note that
g(\varphi)
is even about
\varphi=0
. The surface integral then becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\partial C_{1})

\displaystyle=2a_{0}^{2}A^{m}_{n}A^{\mu}_{\nu}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=2}^{1+q}e^{i(\mu-m)\varphi_{\ell}}\bigg)\mathcal{J}^{m\mu}_{n\nu},

(81)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/q}\mathcal{K}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\varphi)\cos\big[(\mu-m)\varphi\big]\ d\varphi,

(82)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int^{1}_{g(\varphi)}P^{m}_{n}(w)P^{\mu}_{\nu}(w)\ dw.

(83)

Up to this point it appears that simplifying the surface integral into its analytic form is not trivial. Nevertheless, we may still look for ways to speed up the computation of the expressions (
81
) – (
83
). Note that the most computationally-expensive part is the double integral
\mathcal{J}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}
and given the ranges of the indices
n,m,\nu,\mu
, a direct evaluation of (
81
) – (
83
) over these ranges would entail performing the double integral for a total of

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{\nu=0}^{V}(2n+1)(2\nu+1)=(N+1)^{2}(V+1)^{2}

(84)

times, where
N
is the multiple order of each active source and
V
is the truncation order of the source amplitude discussed in Part 3. The computation will be costly especially when higher order sources are used in the cloaking system or when the frequency of the incident wave is high which requires a larger truncation order. We are therefore seeking a simpler form of
\mathcal{J}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}
and thus
\mathcal{I}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\partial C_{1})
that can facilitate the computation.

By
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2014
, the associated Legendre functions in (
83
) can be written in an alternative form

\displaystyle P^{m}_{n}(w)=\bigg(\frac{1+w}{1-w}\bigg)^{m/2}\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}c_{n,\tau}^{m}\bigg(\frac{1-w}{2}\bigg)^{\tau}

(85)

with

\displaystyle c_{n,\tau}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-n)_{\tau}(n+1)_{\tau}}{\Gamma(1-m)(1-m)_{\tau}\tau!},

(86)

\displaystyle(p)_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\ \ \ &\tau=0\\
p(p+1)(p+2)\cdots(p+\tau-1).\ \ \ &\tau>0\end{cases}.

(87)

In (
86
),
\Gamma(1-m)
is the gamma function and is convergent provided that
1-m
is not a nonpositive integer. To satisfy this condition we can restrict
m
to the nonpositive integers only. For positive
m
, we can apply the identity

\displaystyle P^{m}_{n}(w)=(-1)^{m}\frac{(n+m)!}{(n-m)!}P_{n}^{-m}(w),\ \ \ m>0

(88)

so that the azimuthal order of the associated Legendre function becomes nonpositive. The integral
\mathcal{K}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\varphi)
can now be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\varphi)=a^{m\mu}_{n\nu}\int^{1}_{g(\varphi)}P^{-|m|}_{n}(w)P^{-|\mu|}_{\nu}(w)\ dw,

(89)

where

\displaystyle a^{m\mu}_{n\nu}=\begin{cases}1,\ &m\leq 0\ \text{and}\ \mu\leq 0\\
(-1)^{m}\frac{(n+m)!}{(n-m)!},\ &m>0\ \text{and}\ \mu\leq 0\\
(-1)^{m+\mu}\frac{(n+m)!}{(n-m)!}\frac{(\nu+\mu)!}{(\nu-\mu)!},\ &m>0\ \text{and}\ \mu>0\end{cases}.

(90)

In (
87
),

(p)_{\tau}
is called the Pochhammer symbol
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2014
and vanishes for
\tau\geq 1-p
if
p
is a nonpositive integer. Referring back to (
86
), we observe that
(1-m)_{\tau}>0
for all
\tau
since
1-m
must be positive by our choice of
m
and
c^{m}_{n,\tau}
is hence well-defined for all
\tau
. Note also that
(-n)_{\tau}
and thus
c^{m}_{n,\tau}
vanish identically for
\tau\geq n+1
, which means that the series expansion for
P^{m}_{n}(w)
in (
85
) is finite and terminates at
\tau=n
.
Substituting (
85
) into (
89
) and replacing
w
by
1-2w
inside the integral, we have

\displaystyle\ \ \ \mathcal{K}^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\varphi)/a^{m\mu}_{n\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=0}^{n}\sum_{\sigma=0}^{\nu}\frac{C_{n\nu,\tau\sigma}^{m\mu}}{2^{\tau+\sigma+1}}\int_{g}^{1}(1+w)^{-\frac{|m|+|\mu|}{2}}(1-w)^{\tau+\sigma+\frac{|m|+|\mu|}{2}}dw

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=0}^{n}\sum_{\sigma=0}^{\nu}C_{n\nu,\tau\sigma}^{m\mu}\int_{0}^{\frac{1-g}{2}}(1-w)^{-\frac{|m|+|\mu|}{2}}w^{\tau+\sigma+\frac{|m|+|\mu|}{2}}\ dw

are the Euler angles
Varshalovich et al. 1988
of the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{v}},\Theta)
when it is decomposed into three separate rotations about the
z,y,z
axes of the original space
\mathbf{x}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{v}},\Theta)=\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{z},\gamma)\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{y},\beta)\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{z},\alpha),

(125)

where
\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{y},\beta)
is already defined in (

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{z},\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&\sin\alpha&0\\
-\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(126)

Comparing elementwise between the left and right side of (

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=-\arctan\bigg(\frac{R_{23}}{R_{13}}\bigg),

(127)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\arccos(R_{33}),

(128)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\arctan\bigg(\frac{R_{32}}{R_{31}}\bigg),

(129)

with the entries of
\mathbf{R}
described by either (

) or (

), depending on the value of
\ell

If we substitute (
121

118

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-n}^{n}D^{n}_{m^{\prime}m}(\gamma,\beta,\alpha)q_{\ell,nm}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}},\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\partial C_{\ell})=q_{1,nm^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{1},\partial C_{1}).

(130)

Note that (

\mathbf{D}^{n}=\{D^{n}_{m^{\prime}m}\}_{m^{\prime},m=-n}^{n}
and
\mathbf{q}_{\ell,n}=\{q_{\ell,nm}\}_{m=-n}^{n}

A.6
6. Wave scattering and parameter study

Consider a smooth object subject to an incident scalar wave
\mathfrak{u}_{i}
and denote the ensuing scattered wave as
\mathfrak{u}_{s}
. The two wave fields can be expanded in terms of the basis functions as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{u}_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\mathfrak{Q}_{nm}U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{x}),

(131)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{u}_{s}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\mathfrak{a}_{nm}V^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{x}),

(132)

with
\mathfrak{Q}_{nm}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{nm}
the incident and scattering coefficient respectively. For simplicity, we only look at the case of acoustic wave but our arguments can be easily generalized to other cases like electromagnetic or elastic wave. It has been derived by
Waterman 1969
in the acoustic context that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{nm}=\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}\sum_{i=-j}^{j}T_{nm,ji}\mathfrak{Q}_{ji},

(133)

where
T_{nm,ji}
is a transition matrix depending on the geometry of the scattering object and the boundary condition imposed on its surface. For a perfect sphere of radius
A
,
T_{nm,ji}
takes the form

\displaystyle T_{nm,ji}=-\delta_{nj}\delta_{mi}

\displaystyle\frac{j_{n}^{\prime}(kA)}{h_{n}^{\prime}(kA)}

for sound-hard case

(134)

\displaystyle T_{nm,ji}=-\delta_{nj}\delta_{mi}

\displaystyle\frac{j_{n}(kA)}{h_{n}(kA)}

for sound-soft case

(135)

with
\delta_{hk}
the Kronecker delta function.

By adopting procedures similar to what is done in
Shearer et al. 2015
, it can be shown that
W
, the acoustic power scattered to the far field by the object in three dimensions averaged over a wave period, is given by

\displaystyle W=\frac{\omega\rho}{2k}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}|\mathfrak{a}_{nm}|^{2},

(136)

with
\omega
the angular frequency and
\rho
the density of the medium.
We can apply (
136
) as a measure to assess the effectiveness of our cloaking approach discussed before by defining the quantity
\sigma
in the form

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{W(u_{d}\neq 0)}{W(u_{d}=0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\left|a_{nm}(u_{d}\neq 0)+f_{nm}\right|^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\left|a_{nm}(u_{d}=0)\right|^{2}},

(137)

where
u_{d}
and
a_{nm}
, defined in (
2
) and (
3
), are the active field produced by the sources and the scattering coefficient of the sphere inside the cloaked region. The term
f_{nm}
is the far-field amplitude of
u_{d}
such that

\displaystyle u_{d}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}f_{nm}V^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{x}).

(138)

In (
137
), for the case without any control, the coefficient
\mathfrak{a}_{nm}
depends solely on the scattered field
u_{s}
and is thus equivalent to
a_{nm}(u_{d}=0)
. However, when the cloaking devices are activated (
u_{d}\neq 0
),

u_{s}
is influenced also by
u_{d}
and

\mathfrak{a}_{nm}
becomes
a_{nm}(u_{d}\neq 0)
.
Note that the contribution from
f_{nm}
should also be included in
W(u_{d}\neq 0)
since we require not only a reduction in the scattered field but also minimal radiation from the active sources to the far field and (
138
) has the same basis function as (
132
) does.
The quantity
\sigma
is therefore the ratio of the total power
radiated by the whole system before and after control. To ensure that the cloaking method is effective, we need
\sigma<1
.

In the following we will find the expressions for
the three coefficients in (
137
).
Substituting
\mathfrak{Q}_{nm}=Q_{nm}
and (
135
) into (
133
) gives
a_{nm}(u_{d}=0)
in the form

\displaystyle a_{nm}(u_{d}=0)=-\frac{j_{n}(kA)}{h_{n}(kA)}Q_{nm}

(139)

when the scattering object is a sound-soft sphere. As for
a_{nm}(u_{d}\neq 0)
, we recall from Part 1 that we apply the addition theorem for spherical wavefunctions in (
26
) – (
30
) to determine the explicit form of the source amplitudes. Similarly, to obtain the form of
a_{nm}(u_{d}\neq 0)
, we need to first invoke the addition formula to write the active field
u_{d}
in terms of the incoming wavefunction
U^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{x})
. Since
|\mathbf{x}|<|\mathbf{x}_{\ell}|
on the surface of the scattering sphere,
by putting
z_{n}(k|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}|)=h_{n}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}|),\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\ \text{and}\ \mathbf{r}_{0}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
in the second case of (
26
) for
\ell=1,2,\cdots,L
, we can rewrite the active field from every source in a form similar to (
131

26
) – (
30
) and the fact that the evaluation has to be repeated for
L
times, each with a different position vector
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
, the computation of the coefficients related to the active field will be expensive.
A way to circumvent this problem is to again make use of the regularity and rotational symmetry of the Platonic source distribution like what we have done in Part 5 when we determine the amplitudes of all active sources using only knowledge of that of the bottommost source and the geometry of Platonic solids.
To distinguish between the contributions from different active sources, we now denote the rotated space for each source as
\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}=(x^{(\ell)},y^{(\ell)},z^{(\ell)})=\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}
(which is different from the notation in Part 5). In the rotated frame
\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}
, we have the position vector

\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{x}_{1}=(0,0,-x_{0})

\partial C_{\ell}^{(\ell)}=\partial C_{1}
and the propagating vector of the incident wave

\widehat{\mathbf{k}}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\widehat{\mathbf{k}}
. Note that
\mathbf{x}^{(1)}=\mathbf{x}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{k}}^{(1)}=\widehat{\mathbf{k}}
. In the new notation, the equation of transformation between the original and rotated spaces for the spherical harmonic function (
121

\displaystyle Y^{m}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}})=\sum_{m^{\prime}=-n}^{n}Y^{m^{\prime}}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}-\mathbf{x}_{1}})D^{n}_{m^{\prime}m}(\gamma_{\ell},\beta_{\ell},\alpha_{\ell})

(140)

for
\ell=1,2,\cdots,L

\gamma_{\ell},\beta_{\ell},\alpha_{\ell}
are the Euler angles of the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}_{\ell}

130

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-n}^{n}D^{n}_{m^{\prime}m}(\gamma_{\ell},\beta_{\ell},\alpha_{\ell})q_{\ell,nm}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}},\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\partial C_{\ell})

\displaystyle=q_{1,nm^{\prime}}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{x}_{1},\partial C_{1}).

(141)

Substituting (
140

130

u_{d}|_{\ell}
in (
118

\ell=1,2,\cdots,L
and summing the active fields over all sources, we have
the total active field
u_{d}
in the form

\displaystyle u_{d}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}q_{1,nm}(\widehat{\mathbf{k}}^{(\ell)})V^{m}_{n}(\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}-\mathbf{x}_{1}),

(142)

where the dependence of
q_{1,nm}
on the geometric parameters
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
\partial C_{1}
is already understood. An explicit formula for

is already available in (

10

Note that the expression of
S^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(\mathbf{r}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{r}_{0})
in (

simplified for
\mathbf{r}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{r}_{0}=\mathbf{0}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{(\ell)}=-\mathbf{x}_{1}=x_{0}\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}

z

\displaystyle Y_{\mathfrak{q}}^{\mu-m}(\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{z})

\displaystyle=A^{0}_{\mathfrak{q}}\delta_{\mu m}P^{0}_{\mathfrak{q}}(\cos 0)e^{i(0)\varphi}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\mathfrak{q}+1}{4\pi}}\delta_{\mu m}P^{0}_{\mathfrak{q}}(1),

(143)

where
\mathfrak{q}=q_{0}+2q
and the exponent
\mu-m
must vanish since the azimuthal angle
\varphi
is undefined for
\theta=0

Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2014

P^{\mathfrak{p}}_{\mathfrak{q}}(x)
can be written as

\displaystyle P^{\mathfrak{p}}_{\mathfrak{q}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\mathfrak{p}}}{2^{\mathfrak{q}}\mathfrak{q}!}(1-x^{2})^{\mathfrak{p}/2}\frac{d^{\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{q}}}{dx^{\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{q}}}(x^{2}-1)^{\mathfrak{q}}.

(144)

With
\mathfrak{p}=0\ \text{and}\ x=1

Olver 2000
on the derivative such that

\displaystyle\ \ \ \frac{d^{\mathfrak{q}}}{dx^{\mathfrak{q}}}(x^{2}-1)^{\mathfrak{q}}\bigg|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{\mathfrak{q}}}{dx^{\mathfrak{q}}}(x+1)^{\mathfrak{q}}(x-1)^{\mathfrak{q}}\bigg|_{x=-1}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\mathfrak{q}}_{j=0}\binom{\mathfrak{q}}{j}\bigg[\frac{\mathfrak{q}!}{(\mathfrak{q}-j)!}(x+1)^{\mathfrak{q}-j}\bigg]\bigg[\frac{\mathfrak{q}!}{j!}(x-1)^{j}\bigg]\bigg|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=\delta_{0j}\binom{\mathfrak{q}}{j}\bigg[\frac{\mathfrak{q}!}{(\mathfrak{q}-j)!}(x+1)^{\mathfrak{q}-j}\bigg]\bigg[\frac{\mathfrak{q}!}{j!}(x-1)^{j}\bigg]\bigg|_{x=1}

\displaystyle=2^{\mathfrak{q}}\mathfrak{q}!.

(145)

By (
144
) and (
145

\displaystyle\ \ \ S^{m\mu}_{n\nu}(-\mathbf{x}_{1})
