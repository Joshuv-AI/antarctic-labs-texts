Liveness Properties in Geometric Logic for Domain-Theoretic Streams UMR 5668 CNRS ENS Lyon UCBL INRIA
Colin Riba, Solal Stern

Abstract
We devise a version of Linear Temporal Logic ( 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} ) on a denotational domain of streams. We investigate this logic in terms of domain theory, (point-free) topology and geometric logic. This yields the first steps toward an extension of the “Domain Theory in Logical Form” paradigm to temporal liveness properties. We show that the negation-free formulae of 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} induce sober subspaces of streams, but that this is in general not the case in presence of negation. We propose a direct, inductive, translation of negation-free 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} to geometric logic. This translation reflects the approximations used to compute the usual fixpoint representations of 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} modalities. As a motivating example, we handle a natural input-output specification for the usual 𝖿𝗂𝗅𝗍𝖾𝗋 \mathsf{filter} function on streams.

We are interested in input-output

Consider for instance the usual

The question we address in this paper is the following.

This paper reports on preliminary works, mostly based on an internship

We devise a version of the logic

Our first step is to view domains as topological spaces, so as to

The notion of sobriety originates from point-free topology, and has become

We then turn to geometric logic.
The idea is roughly the following.
DTLF

On the other hand, the sobriety of

This shows that for the negation-free fragment, the semantics

\mathsf{LTL}

live in a natural extension of DTLF for the domain

We also check

A Linear Temporal Logic on a Domain of Streams

Let
A
be a set.
A (finite)
word
on
A
is an element of
A^{*}
.

A^{\omega}

is the set of
\omega
-words
on
A
, i.e. the set of all
functions
\sigma\colon\mathbb{N}\to A
.
We write
u\subseteq v
when

u\in A^{*}
is a prefix of
v\in A^{*}\cup A^{\omega}
.
The concatenation of

u\in A^{*}
with
v\in A^{*}\cup A^{\omega}

is denoted
u\cdot v
or
uv
.
Given
\sigma\in A^{\omega}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
we let
\sigma\mathord{\upharpoonright}k\in A^{\omega}

be the
\omega
-word with
(\sigma\mathord{\upharpoonright}k)(n)=\sigma(k+n)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
For instance,
\sigma\mathord{\upharpoonright}0
is
\sigma
, while

\sigma\mathord{\upharpoonright}1=\sigma(1)\cdot\sigma(2)\cdots\sigma(n+1)\cdots

is
\sigma
deprived from its first letter.

2.1
Domains

The basic idea of domain theory is to represent a type
by partial order
(X,\leq_{X})
thought about as an “information order”.
The intuition is that
x\leq_{X}y
means that

y
has “more information” than
x
, or that

x
is “less defined” than
y
.
Domains are often required to have a least element
(representing plain divergence),
and are always asked to be stable under certain supremums
(so that infinite objects can be thought about as limits of their finite approximations).
Our presentation mostly follows
[
AC98
, §1]
.
See also
[
Abr91
,
GL13
]
.

Dcpos and Cpos.

Let
(X,\leq)
be a partial order (or
poset
).
An
upper bound
of a subset
S\subseteq X

is an element
x\in X
such that
(\forall s\in S)(s\leq x)
.
A
least upper bound
(or
supremum
,
sup
) of
S

is an upper bound
\ell
of
S
such that

\ell\leq x
for every upper bound
x
of
S
.
The sup of
S
is unique whenever it exists,
and is usually denoted
\bigvee S
.
The notion of
greatest lower bound

(or
infimum
,
inf
) is defined dually.
A subset
D\subseteq X
is
directed
if
D
is non-empty and
for every
x,y\in D
,
there is some
z\in D
such that
x\leq z
and
y\leq z
.

We say that
(X,\leq)
is a
dcpo
if every directed
D\subseteq X

has a sup
\bigvee D\in X
.
A
cpo
is a dcpo with a least element (usually denoted
\bot
).
Note that each set
A
is a dcpo for the discrete order
(in which
x
is comparable with
y
if, and only if,
x=y
).
However, such a dcpo
A
is not a cpo unless
A
is a singleton.

Scott-Continuous Functions.

Let
X=(X,\leq_{X})
and
Y=(Y,\leq_{Y})
be dcpos.
A function
f\colon X\to Y
is
Scott-continuous

if
f
is monotone
(
x\leq_{X}x^{\prime}
implies
f(x)\leq_{Y}f(x^{\prime})
)
and if moreover
f
preserves directed sups, in the sense that
for each directed
D\subseteq X
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l l l}f(\bigvee D)&=&\bigvee\left\{f(d)\mid d\in D\right\}\end{array}

We write
\mathbf{CPO}
(resp.
\mathbf{DCPO}
) for the category with
cpos (resp. dcpos) as objects and with
Scott-continuous functions as morphisms. We say that
f\in\mathbf{CPO}[X,Y]
is strict if
f(\bot_{X})=\bot_{Y}
.
A non-strict monotone map between flat domains is necessarily constant.

Given dcpos
X=(X,\leq_{X})
and
Y=(Y,\leq_{Y})
, the set of
Scott-continuous functions
\mathbf{DCPO}[X,Y]

is itself a dcpo w.r.t. the
pointwise order

\begin{array}[]{l l l}f\leq_{\mathbf{DCPO}[X,Y]}g&\text{iff}&\forall x\in X,\penalty\ f(x)\leq_{Y}g(x)\end{array}

If
Y
is actually a cpo, then
\mathbf{DCPO}[X,Y]
is a cpo whose
least element is the constant function

x\in X\mapsto\bot_{Y}\in Y
, where
\bot_{Y}
is the least element of
Y
.

Among the many good properties of

Let
(X,\leq)

We say that

is

[Streams]

The cpo

Syntax and Semantics.

Let

The formulae of
\mathsf{LTL}=\mathsf{LTL}(A)

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\Phi,\Psi&\Coloneqq&a\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathsf{True}\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathsf{False}\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \Phi\land\Psi\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \Phi\lor\Psi\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \lnot\Phi\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \bigcirc\Phi\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \Phi\mathrel{\mathsf{U}}\Psi\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \Phi\mathrel{\mathsf{W}}\Psi\end{array}

where

Hence, besides pure propositional logic,
\mathsf{LTL}(A)

has

(read “next

\Phi\mathrel{\mathsf{U}}\Psi
(read “

and
\Phi\mathrel{\mathsf{W}}\Psi
(read “
\Phi

\Psi
” or “

The
\mathsf{LTL}
formulae over
A
are usually interpreted
on
\omega

A
, see e.g.
[
BK08
, §5]
.
The interpretation of modalities actually implicitly relies on the bijection

A^{\omega}\cong A\times A^{\omega}

We similarly rely on the isomorphism

\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket\cong_{\mathbf{CPO}}\llbracket A\rrbracket\times\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket

for interpreting
\mathsf{LTL}(A)
formulae in
\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket

We define
\llbracket\Phi\rrbracket\subseteq\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
by induction on
\Phi

\mathsf{LTL}
are interpreted using the usual
Boolean algebra structure of the powerset

For

,
we let

The modalities are interpreted as follows.

The Topological Approach

We shall now look at inclusions

\llbracket\Phi\rrbracket\hookrightarrow\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
from a topological perspective.
We recall in §

that the categories
\mathbf{(D)CPO}
can be embedded in the category
\mathbf{Top}

of topological spaces and continuous functions.
The highlight is that
\mathbf{Top}
has a
much richer notion of substructures (called subspaces)
than
\mathbf{(D)CPO}
.

Actually, when looking at (d)cpos as topological spaces,
the notion of sobriety
from point-free (or “element-free”) topology
comes to the front.
Ample mathematical justifications for
the importance of sober spaces in domain theory
are gathered in
[
GL13
]
.
We shall content ourselves with
more informal motivations in §
3.2
.
In §
3.3
, we abstractly prove that
\llbracket\Phi\rrbracket
induces a sober subspace of
\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket

when
\Phi
is negation-free.
This will be refined to concrete representations
in §
4
and §

3.1
Topological Spaces

A
topological space
is a pair
(X,\Omega(X))
of a set
X

and a collection
\Omega=\Omega(X)
of subsets of
X
, called
open sets
.

\Omega

is called a
topology
on
X
,
and is asked to be stable under arbitrary unions and under finite intersections.
In particular,
\emptyset
and
X
are open in
X

(respectively as the empty union and the empty intersection).

A set
C\subseteq X
is
closed
if its complement
X\setminus C
is open.
Closed sets are stable under finite unions and arbitrary
intersections.
Hence, any
S\subseteq X
is contained in a least closed
set
\overline{S}\subseteq X
. Each space
(X,\Omega)
is equipped with a
specialization
(pre)order

\leq_{\Omega}
on
X
, defined as

\begin{array}[]{l l l}x\leq_{\Omega}y&\text{iff}&(\forall U\in\Omega)\left(x\in U\penalty\ \Longrightarrow\penalty\ y\in U\right)\end{array}

Given
x\in X
, we have

\overline{\{x\}}=\mathord{\downarrow}x\coloneqq\{y\in X\mid y\leq_{\Omega}x\}

(see e.g.
[
GL13
, Lemma 4.2.7]
).
A topology
\Omega
is
T_{0}
when
\leq_{\Omega}
is a partial order
(see e.g.
[
GL13
, Proposition 4.2.3]
).

Given spaces
(X,\Omega(X))
and
(Y,\Omega(Y))
,
a function
f\colon X\to Y
is
continuous

when its inverse image
f^{-1}\colon\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathcal{P}(X)

restricts to a function
\Omega(Y)\to\Omega(X)
,
i.e. when
f^{-1}(V)\in\Omega(X)
for all
V\in\Omega(Y)
.
We write
\mathbf{Top}
for the category of topological spaces and
continuous functions.
An
homeomorphism
is an isomorphism in
\mathbf{Top}
.

The Scott Topology.

The following is well-known.
See e.g.
[
AC98
, §1.2]
or
[
GL13
, §4]
.

Let
(X,\leq_{X})
be a dcpo.
A subset
U\subseteq X
is
Scott-open
if
U
is upward-closed,
and if moreover
U
is inaccessible by directed sups,
in the sense that if
\bigvee D\in U

with
D\subseteq X
directed,
then
D\cap U\neq\emptyset
.
This equips
X
with a
T_{0}
topology, called the
Scott topology
,
whose specialization order coincides with
\leq_{X}
.
Note that
C\subseteq X
is Scott-closed precisely when
C
is downward-closed
and stable under directed sups.

{exem}

When
(X,\leq)
is algebraic, the sets

\mathord{\uparrow}d\coloneqq\{x\in X\mid d\leq x\}
with
d
finite
form a sub-basis for the Scott topology.
For instance,
the Scott-open subsets of
\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
are arbitrary unions
of sets of the form

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\mathord{\uparrow}d&=&\left\{x\in\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket\mid\forall i\in\mathrm{supp}(d),\penalty\ x(i)=d(i)\right\}\end{array}

with

In particular, given

Scott-open,
there is a
finite

\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}

such that

\left\{y\mid y(i_{1})=x(i_{1}),\dots,y(i_{k})=x(i_{k})\right\}\subseteq U
.

Beware that
\llbracket\Phi\rrbracket
may not be an open
nor a closed subset of
\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
, even when
\Phi
is negation-free.
Consider for instance

A^{\omega}=\llbracket\square\bigvee_{a\in A}a\rrbracket

(with

non-empty and finite),
which contains

but no finite

.

A function

between dcpos is
Scott-continuous precisely when

is continuous w.r.t. the Scott-topologies on
X
and
Y
.
It follows that
\mathbf{DCPO}
and
\mathbf{CPO}
are full subcategories of
\mathbf{Top}
.
From now on, we shall mostly look at
\mathbf{(D)CPO}
in this way.
Unless stated otherwise, dcpos will always be equipped with their Scott

Subspaces.

Our motivation for moving from

to

is that

We refer to

Given a space

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\Omega\mathord{\upharpoonright}P&\coloneqq&\left\{U\cap P\mid U\in\Omega\right\}\end{array}

It is the “best possible” topology on

3.2
The Element-Free Setting

The topological setting comes with an intrinsic notion of approximation.

Consider for instance
\omega
-words
\sigma\in A^{\omega}

(Example
3.1
).
Similarly as with streams in Example
3.1
,
given an
\omega
-word
\sigma
and an open
U
, if
\sigma
belongs to
U
,
then this fact is witnessed
by the knowledge of a finite number of elements of
\sigma
.
4
4

4

Dually, the knowledge of the whole
\omega
-word

\sigma
may be needed to testify that
\sigma\notin U
.
We view the opens
U
such that
\sigma\in U
as approximations of
\sigma
.

Given a space
(X,\Omega)
, we are interested in
describing the elements of
X
by
their approximations, represented as suitable sets of opens
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Omega
.
This is the realm of
element-free
(or
point-free
)
topology
.
Its central objects, called

frames
(or
locales
),
abstract away from the elements of spaces, and only retain
the lattice structure of open sets.
Besides
[
GL13
]
,
we refer to
[
Joh82
,
Joh83
,
Vic89
,
PP12
,
PP21
]
.

Frames.

A
complete lattice
is a poset having all sups and all infs.
But recall (from e.g.
[
DP02
, Theorem 2.31]
)
that a poset has all sups if, and only if, it has all infs.
Hence, we can see complete lattices indifferently as posets with all sups or
as posets with all infs.

A
frame
is a poset
L
with all sups (and thus all infs),
and which satisfies the following
frame distributive law
:
for all
S\subseteq L
and all
a\in L
,

\begin{array}[]{l l l}a\wedge\bigvee S&=&\bigvee\left\{a\wedge s\mid s\in S\right\}\end{array}

Not every complete lattice is a frame.
5
5

5

Consider e.g. a finite (and thus complete) non-distributive
lattice, see
[
DP02
, Example 4.6(6)]
.
But every
(\mathcal{P}(X),\subseteq)
is a frame,
and so is the two-elements poset

\mathbf{2}\coloneqq\{0\leq 1\}
.

Given frames
L
and
K
,
a
frame morphism

f\colon L\to K

is a function which preserves all sups and all
finite
infs.
Note that frame morphisms are automatically monotone.
We write
\mathbf{Frm}
for the category of frames and frame morphisms.

{exem}

Let
(X,\Omega(X))
be a space.
Then
\Omega(X)
has all sups and they are given by unions.
Hence
\Omega(X)
is a complete lattice.
Beware that the inf in
\Omega(X)
of an arbitrary
S\subseteq\Omega(X)

is in general not its intersection
\bigcap S
,
but the
interior
of
\bigcap S

(the largest open set contained in
\bigcap S
).
However,
finite
infs in
\Omega(X)
are given by intersections,
and
\Omega(X)
is a frame.
In fact, the topologies on a given set
X
correspond exactly to
the sub-frames of
\mathcal{P}(X)
.

Moreover, if
f\colon X\to Y
is continuous,
then its inverse image

f^{-1}
restricts to a function
\Omega(Y)\to\Omega(X)
.
This function is actually a frame morphism

\Omega(f)\in\mathbf{Frm}[\Omega(Y),\Omega(X)]
.
In other words, the operation
(X,\Omega(X))\mapsto\Omega(X)

extends to a functor
\Omega
from the category
\mathbf{Top}
to
\mathbf{Frm}^{\mathrm{op}}
,
the opposite of
\mathbf{Frm}
.
The category
\mathbf{Frm}^{\mathrm{op}}
is the category of
locales
.

The Space of Points.

We see a frame
L
as a collection of formal approximations.
Suitable subsets of
L
describe “converging” sets of formal approximations,
and constitute the elements of a space, the space of points of
L
.
The idea is as follows.
Given a space
X
and
x\in X
, let

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\mathcal{F}_{x}&\coloneqq&\left\{U\in\Omega(X)\mid x\in U\right\}\end{array}

Note the following properties of
\mathcal{F}_{x}

w.r.t. the frame structure of
\Omega(X)
.
First,

\mathcal{F}_{x}
is stable under finite intersections
(
x\in X
, and
x\in U\cap V
iff
x\in U
and
x\in V
).
Second, given
S\subseteq\Omega(X)
with
\bigcup S\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
,
we have

U\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
for some
U\in S

(if
x\in\bigcup S
then
x\in U
for some
U\in S
).
Hence, the characteristic function of
\mathcal{F}_{x}\subseteq\Omega(X)

is a frame morphism
\Omega(X)\to\mathbf{2}
.

A
point
of a frame
L
is an element of
\mathrm{pt}(L)\coloneqq\mathbf{Frm}[L,\mathbf{2}]
.
We shall always identify a point
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbf{Frm}[L,\mathbf{2}]

with the set
\{a\in L\mid\mathcal{F}(a)=1\}
.
Given
a\in L
, let

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\mathsf{ext}(a)&\coloneqq&\left\{\mathcal{F}\in\mathrm{pt}(L)\mid a\in\mathcal{F}\right\}\end{array}

The function

\mathsf{ext}\colon L\to\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{pt}(L))
is a frame morphism
(see e.g.
[
Joh82
, Lemma II.1.6]
).
In particular, its image is a sub-frame of
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{pt}(L))
,
and is thus a topology
\Omega(\mathrm{pt}(L))
on
\mathrm{pt}(L)
.
The
space of points
of
L
is
(\mathrm{pt}(L),\Omega(\mathrm{pt}(L))
.

The operation
L\mapsto\mathrm{pt}(L)
extends to a functor

\mathrm{pt}\colon\mathbf{Frm}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Top}

which is right adjoint to
\Omega
(see e.g.
[
Joh82
, Theorem II.1.4]
).
The action of
\mathrm{pt}\colon\mathbf{Frm}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Top}

on a frame morphism
f\colon L\to K

is the continuous function
\mathrm{pt}(K)\to\mathrm{pt}(L)

which takes
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbf{Frm}[K,\mathbf{2}]
to
\mathcal{F}\mathbin{\circ}f\in\mathbf{Frm}[L,\mathbf{2}]
.
The unit at
X\in\mathbf{Top}
of the adjunction
\Omega\dashv\mathrm{pt}

is the continuous function
\eta_{X}\colon X\to\mathrm{pt}(\Omega(X))

taking
x
to
\mathcal{F}_{x}

(see e.g.
[
Joh82
, §II.1.6]
).

Sober Spaces.

The function
\eta_{X}\colon X\to\mathrm{pt}(\Omega(X))

continuously maps the space

Geometric Logic

Geometric logic is an infinitary propositional logic which describes frames.
Very roughly, the idea is that if a theory
\mathtt{Th}
in geometric logic
represents a frame
L
, then the models of
\mathtt{Th}
can be organized in
a space which is homeomorphic to
\mathrm{pt}(L)
, the space of points of
L
.

We will represent the (sober) space
\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
by a theory

\mathtt{T}\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket

in geometric logic.
Further, for each negation-free formula
\Phi
of
\mathsf{LTL}
,
we shall (inductively) devise a theory
\mathtt{T}\llbracket\Phi\rrbracket

such that
\mathtt{T}\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket\cup\mathtt{T}\llbracket\Phi\rrbracket

represents the (sober) subspace induced by
\llbracket\Phi\rrbracket\hookrightarrow\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
.
This will provide a concrete presentation of the corresponding quotient frame.

Our approach to geometric logic
here
is not the usual one, as presented in e.g.
[
Vic07
]

(see also
[
Joh02
, §D]
).
The relations between the two approaches are discussed
in §
5
.

Formulae and Valuations.

Let

The

\begin{array}[]{l l l !{\qquad\text{and}\qquad} l l l}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathtt{Conj}(\mathit{At})&\Coloneqq&p\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathtt{true}\penalty\ \penalty\ |\penalty\ \penalty\ \gamma\land\gamma^{\prime}\par&\varphi,\psi,\theta\in\mathtt{Geom}(\mathit{At})&\Coloneqq&\bigvee S\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{l c l !{\qquad} l c l}\nu\models\mathtt{true}&&&\nu\models\gamma\land\gamma^{\prime}&\text{iff}&\text{$\nu\models\gamma$ and $\nu\models\gamma^{\prime}$}\\

.

.

.

There is no primitive notion of conjunction or disjunction on geometric formulae,

.

We shall now see that for each negation-free formula

To this end, we devise operations on theories which represent

\mathit{At}

for

of sets of models.

\mathit{At}

\mathtt{T}_{i}

Spatiality.

How does
\mathtt{Geom}(\mathit{At})/\mathtt{T}
relate to the frame

\mathtt{Geom}(\mathit{At})/\mathbf{Mod}(\mathtt{T})

?
To answer this question, we compare the preorders
\vdash_{\mathtt{T}}

and
\preceq_{\mathbf{Mod}(\mathtt{T})}

First, an easy induction on derivations proves the following

soundness

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\psi\vdash_{\mathtt{T}}\varphi&\Longrightarrow&\psi\preceq_{\mathbf{Mod}(\mathtt{T})}\varphi\end{array}

The converse implication is a form of

if every model of
\mathtt{T}
is a model of

\psi\vdash\varphi

then
\psi\vdash_{\mathtt{T}}\varphi

This may fail in geometric logic, by lack of spatiality.
A frame

when given

in

there is a point
\mathcal{F}\in\mathrm{pt}(L)
such that

and
b\notin\mathcal{F}
.
A frame is spatial precisely when it is isomorphic to

\Omega(X)
for some space

(
[
Joh82
, §II.1.5]
).

Assume now that
\mathtt{Geom}(\mathit{At})/\mathtt{T}
is spatial,

and there is a point

of

and
[\varphi]_{\mathtt{T}}\notin\mathcal{F}

Let

.

{lemm}

Let

Then for every

Hence, when

6
A Specification for the Denotation of Filter

The core of this paper consists of the results presented
above
concerning
\mathsf{LTL}
on streams.
However, the long term goal of this work is to reason on
input-output (negation-free)
\mathsf{LTL}
properties of functions.
We now briefly sketch how our results can help
to handle our motivating example,
namely the
\mathsf{filter}
function on streams.
This is mostly preliminary;
we leave as future work the elaboration of a general solution.

We work with the function
\llbracket\mathsf{filter}\rrbracket

of Remark

.
Fix a finite set

Assume that for all

Let

as in Example

The specification (

\mathsf{filter}

leads to the following specification for

\forall x\in\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket,\penalty\ \text{$x$ total},\qquad x\Vdash\square\diamondsuit\Psi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(\llbracket\mathsf{filter}\rrbracket\ p\ x)\Vdash\square\Phi

We use the notations of Remark

In particular,

The standard method to reason on such fixpoints is the

to

(3)

Condition (

The main inductive argument

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we conducted a semantic study of a logic
\mathsf{LTL}

on a domain of streams
\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
.
We showed that the negation-free formulae of
\mathsf{LTL}
induce
sober subspaces of
\llbracket\mathsf{Str}A\rrbracket
,
and that this may fail in presence of negation.
We proposed an inductive translation of negation-free
\mathsf{LTL}
to geometric logic.
This translation reflects the semantics of
\mathsf{LTL}
,
and we use it to prove that the denotation of
\mathsf{filter}

satisfies the specification (
1
).

First, the logic
\mathsf{LTL}

Let

By the Knaster-Tarski Fixpoint Theorem
(see e.g.

), we have

\begin{array}[]{r c l}\mathord{\mathrm{lfp}}(f)&=&\bigvee\left\{a\in B\mid f(a)\leq a\right\}\\
\mathord{\mathrm{gfp}}(f)&=&\bigwedge\left\{a\in B\mid a\leq f(a)\right\}\end{array}

Let

be the monotone function
B\to B
which takes

to
\lnot f(\lnot a)
.
We show

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\mathord{\mathrm{gfp}}(f)&=&\lnot\mathord{\mathrm{lfp}}(g)\end{array}

The other equation is then obtained by duality.
Since

is complete and atomic, it follows from

We then compute

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\lnot\mathord{\mathrm{lfp}}(g)&=&\lnot\bigvee\left\{a\mid g(a)\leq a\right\}\\
&=&\bigwedge\left\{\lnot a\mid g(a)\leq a\right\}\\
&=&\bigwedge\left\{\lnot a\mid\lnot f(\lnot a)\leq a\right\}\\
&=&\bigwedge\left\{\lnot a\mid\lnot a\leq f(\lnot a)\right\}\\
&=&\bigwedge\left\{b\mid b\leq f(b)\right\}\\
&=&\mathord{\mathrm{gfp}}(f)\end{array}

{lemm}
