Abstract
Kleene Algebra (KA) is a useful tool for proving that two programs are equivalent. Because KA’s equational theory is decidable, it integrates well with interactive theorem provers. This raises the question: which equations can we (not) prove using the laws of KA? Moreover, which models of KA are complete , in the sense that they satisfy exactly the provable equations? Kozen (1994) answered these questions by characterizing KA in terms of its language model . Concretely, equivalences provable in KA are exactly those that hold for regular expressions. Pratt (1980) observed that KA is complete w.r.t. relational models , i.e., that its provable equations are those that hold for any relational interpretation. A less known result due to Palka (2005) says that finite models are complete for KA, i.e., that provable equivalences coincide with equations satisfied by all finite KAs. Phrased contrapositively, the latter is a finite model property ( FMP ): any unprovable equation is falsified by a finite KA. Both results can be argued using Kozen’s theorem, but the implication is mutual: given that KA is complete w.r.t. finite (resp. relational) models, Palka’s (resp. Pratt’s) arguments show that it is complete w.r.t. the language model. We embark on a study of the different complete models of KA, and the connections between them. This yields a novel result subsuming those of Palka and Pratt, namely that KA is complete w.r.t. finite relational models . Next, we put an algebraic spin on Palka’s techniques, which yield a new elementary proof of the finite model property, and by extension, of Kozen’s and Pratt’s theorems. In contrast with earlier approaches, this proof relies not on minimality or bisimilarity of automata, but rather on representing the regular expressions involved in terms of transformation automata.

Introduction

Kleene Algebra (KA) provides an algebraic perspective on the equivalence of regular expressions.
Central to KA and its extensions is a theorem due to Kozen
[
1

Salomaa showed an important precursor to this result

Kozen’s axioms and their refinements have the advantage of giving a clear way to define what makes a “Kleene algebra”, the same way that one might define, say, a vector space.
This has paved the way for models of KA that correspond to the semantic domains of programming languages; for instance, the relations on a set (of, e.g., machine states) have the structure of a Kleene algebra.
In turn, this motivated extensions of KA such as Kleene Algebra with Tests (KAT)
[
9

10
,
11

, which can be applied to reason about programs in general
[

, as well as specific programming languages
[

,
16
]

As an added bonus, deciding equivalence of regular expressions, and hence validity of equations by the laws of KA(T), is practically feasible
[
17
,
18
]
in spite of the problem being
pspace
-complete
[
19
]
. This means that KA integrates well with proof assistants, where intermediate steps in a proof can be justified by asserting that they hold for all KAs, and letting the proof assistant validate that claim
[
20
]
.

Given all of this, one might ask whether the laws of KA can be specialized to their domain of application.
Specifically, are there equations that are true for all finite or relational KAs that may not be true in general?
If so, then our toolbox can be strengthened by adding these equations when appropriate.
It turns out that KA is robust to these shifts: Palka
[
21
]
showed that the equations validated by all finite models are true for languages, whereas an observation made by Pratt
[
22
]
implies that relational models enjoy the same property.
In conjunction with Kozen’s result
[
23
]
, this implies that every equation not provable by the laws of KA is witnessed by a finite (resp. relational) countermodel.
These results can therefore be regarded as the
finite
(resp.

)
model property
(
FMP
, resp.
RMP
) for KA.

The proof of Kozen’s completeness theorem
[
1
]
, however, is far from trivial.
On the other hand, if one assumes either the RMP or the FMP, a reasonably straightforward argument (due to the same authors) shows that Kozen’s completeness theorem can be derived.
Pondering this situation, Palka writes that “an independent proof of [the FMP] would provide a quite different proof of the Kozen completeness theorem, based on purely logical tools”
[
21
]
, but defers the matter to future work.

Our first contribution is a reconsideration of the arguments towards these results, painting a more complete picture of KA’s model theory.
In particular, this yields a novel (if unsurprising) observation that subsumes both results: equations that hold in every finite
and
relational KA also hold in general.

Our second and main contribution is to provide the independent proof of the FMP hypothesized by Palka.
In contrast with completeness arguments based on minimality
[

]
, bisimilarity
[

7

or cyclic proof systems
[

A central role is played by the fact that KA allows one to solve linear systems

,

Preliminaries

Our primary objects of study are
Kleene algebras
(
KAs
).
These are interesting for computer science because in many settings, program constructs such as sequencing, branching and looping can be expressed in terms of operators that satisfy the laws of Kleene algebra.
As a consequence, properties that are true in any KA can be leveraged in proofs, for instance to show that two programs are equivalent.

Definition 2.1

A
Kleene algebra (KA)
is a tuple
(K,+,\cdot,{}^{*},0,1)
, where
(K,+,\cdot,0,1)
is an idempotent semiring, and for

x^{*}\cdot y
is the least fixed point (in the natural order of the semiring) of the function
z\mapsto y+x\cdot z
.
In more concrete terms,

is a
carrier set
,

and
\cdot
are binary operators on
K
,
∗
is a unary operator on
K
, and
0,1\in K
, satisfying for all
x,y,z\in K

x+0=xx+x=xx+y=y+xx+(y+z)=(x+y)+z\\
x\cdot(y\cdot z)=(x\cdot y)\cdot zx\cdot(y+z)=x\cdot y+x\cdot z(x+y)\cdot z=x\cdot z+y\cdot z\\
x\cdot 1=x=1\cdot xx\cdot 0=0=0\cdot x1+x\cdot x^{*}=x^{*}x+y\cdot z\leq z\implies y^{*}\cdot x\leq z

where
\leq
denotes the order induced by

, that is,

if and only if
x+y=y
.
This makes
\leq
a partial order on
K
, and ensures that all operators are monotone w.r.t. this order.

We often denote a generic KA
(K,+,\cdot,{}^{*},0,1)
by its carrier

\cdot
, etc. for the operators and constants when their meaning can be inferred from context.

Strictly speaking, the above defines a “weak” or “left-handed” KA, in the sense that it does not require the traditional right-unrolling and right-fixpoint laws put forward by Kozen

Indeed, dropping these two laws does not change the equational theory of KA

This means that if an equation holds in

This is somewhat surprising, but useful because there exist models of KA that do not satisfy the two right-handed axioms above
[
29
]
.

When reasoning about programs, addition is used to represent non-deterministic composition, multiplication corresponds to sequential composition, the
Kleene star
operator
∗

0
stands in for a program that fails immediately, and

is the program that does nothing and terminates successfully.
The equations of KA correspond well to what might be expected of such operators.

One particularly useful (and common) class of KAs is given by the following.

Definition 2.4

Let
(K,+,\cdot,{}^{*},0,1)
be a KA.
When
x\in K
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write

for the
n

, defined inductively by
x^{0}=1
and

.
We say that

when for all

, if
u\cdot x^{n}\cdot v\leq y
for all

, then

Star-continuity says that the Kleene star of

can be regarded as the least upper bound of all
n

.
All explicitly constructed KAs in this paper satisfy star continuity; nevertheless, not all KAs are star-continuous
[
32
]
.
Star-continuity also introduces an infinitary implication to the axioms of KA, which makes it more cumbersome to work with from an algebraic perspective.
This is why it makes sense to generalize to the wider class of all (not necessarily star-continuous) KAs.

One very natural instance of (star-continuous) Kleene algebras is given by the
relational model
.

Definition 2.5

(KA of relations)

Let
X
be a set.
The set of relations on
X
, denoted
\mathcal{R}(X)
, can be equipped with the structure of a KA
(\mathcal{R}(X),\cup,\circ,{}^{*},\emptyset,\mathsf{id}_{X})
, where

\circ
is relational composition,

\mathsf{id}_{X}
is the identity relation on

We write
\mathsf{id}
for

when

is clear from context.

If
S
is the set of states of a machine, we can interpret a program in

, i.e., in terms of relations on

, by mapping it to the relation that connects initial states to final states that may be reached by running the program.
This interpretation arises naturally for programs built using the operators of KA.

Definition 2.6

(Expressions)

We fix an

\Sigma=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b},\mathtt{c},\dots\}
.
The set of

is given by

e,f::=0\mid 1\mid\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma\mid e+f\mid e\cdot f\mid e^{*}

Given a KA

as the map that extends

and commutes with all of the operators; more explicitly,

is specified inductively by:

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(0)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=h(\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(e\cdot f)

\displaystyle=\widehat{h}(e)\cdot\widehat{h}(f)

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(1)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(e+f)

\displaystyle=\widehat{h}(e)+\widehat{h}(f)

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(e^{*})

\displaystyle=\widehat{h}(e)^{*}

The experienced reader will recognize
\mathbb{E}
as the term algebra for the signature of KA, and
\widehat{h}
as the unique homomorphism from

[
30
]
.

Example 2.7

Consider a programming language with non-negative integer variables
\mathsf{Var}
, and actions
\Sigma
comprised (for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y}\in\mathsf{Var}
) of

of the form
\mathtt{x}\leftarrow n
,

denoted
\mathtt{x}\leftarrow\mathtt{x}+\mathtt{y}
and
\mathtt{x}\leftarrow\mathtt{x}+n
, and

, written as
\mathtt{x}<\mathtt{y}
and
\mathtt{x}\geq\mathtt{y}
.

The state of a machine executing these statements is completely determined by the value of each variable, and so we choose the set of variable assignments
S=\{\sigma:\mathsf{Var}\to\mathbb{N}\}
as the state space.
The semantics of the actions are relations that represent their effect, encoded by
h:\Sigma\to\mathcal{R}(S)
as

\displaystyle h(\mathtt{x}\leftarrow n)

\displaystyle=\{(\sigma,\sigma[n/\mathtt{x}]):\sigma\in S\}

\displaystyle h(\mathtt{x}<\mathtt{y})

\displaystyle=\{(\sigma,\sigma):\sigma\in S,\sigma(\mathtt{x})<\sigma(\mathtt{y})\}

\displaystyle h(\mathtt{x}\leftarrow\mathtt{x}+n)

\displaystyle=\{(\sigma,\sigma[\sigma(\mathtt{x})+n/\mathtt{x}]):\sigma\in S\}

\displaystyle h(\mathtt{x}\geq\mathtt{y})

\displaystyle=\{(\sigma,\sigma):\sigma\in S,\sigma(\mathtt{x})\geq\sigma(\mathtt{y})\}

\displaystyle h(\mathtt{x}\leftarrow\mathtt{x}+\mathtt{y})

\displaystyle=\{(\sigma,\sigma[\sigma(\mathtt{x})+\sigma(\mathtt{y})/\mathtt{x}]):\sigma\in S\}

Here,
\sigma[n/\mathtt{x}]
denotes the function from
\mathsf{Var}
to
\mathbb{N}
that assigns

to
\mathtt{x}
, and
\sigma(\mathtt{y})
to all
\mathtt{y}\neq\mathtt{x}
.

This gives a semantics

for regular expressions over

e=\mathtt{x}\leftarrow 1;\,\mathtt{y}\leftarrow 0;\,\mathtt{i}\leftarrow 0;\,(\mathtt{i}<\mathtt{n};\,\mathtt{y}\leftarrow\mathtt{y}+\mathtt{x};\,\mathtt{x}\leftarrow\mathtt{x}+2;\,\mathtt{i}\leftarrow\mathtt{i}+1)^{*};\,(\mathtt{i}\geq\mathtt{n})

In this case one can show that the semantics of

is to “compute” the integer square root of
\mathtt{n}
and stores it in
\mathtt{y}

(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\in\widehat{h}(e)

\sigma^{\prime}(\mathtt{y})
is the largest integer such that
{\sigma^{\prime}(\mathtt{y})}^{2}\leq\sigma(\mathtt{n})
.

Of course, one can build more involved programming languages based on KA; for instance, NetKAT
[
15
]
is a programming language for specifying and reasoning about software-defined networks.

Two regular expressions may yield the same semantics when interpreted in a certain KA; at the same time, another KA may distinguish them.
Typically, we are interested in whether two regular expressions are equated in a given KA, in all KAs, or in a certain well-defined class of KAs.

Definition 2.8

Let

(K,+,\cdot,{}^{*},0,1)

When

If

We use several classes of KAs in the sequel.
We write

for the class of all relational KAs, i.e., KAs of the form

for some set

for all KAs with finite carrier, and
\mathfrak{FR}
for all finite relational KAs, i.e., those in both

We write

for the relation on

where

Remark 2.9

We hinted earlier that equivalence of expressions according to the laws of KA, i.e., whether
e\equiv f
, is decidable.
This does
not
mean that equivalence of expressions in a
particular
KA is decidable; for instance, programs interpreted in the KA

can easily simulate a two-counter machine, which means that equivalence can be used to encode the halting problem.
Rather,

\widehat{h}(e)=\widehat{h}(f)

For instance,
\widehat{h}(\mathtt{x}\leftarrow 1;\mathtt{x}\leftarrow 2)=\widehat{h}(\mathtt{x}\leftarrow 2)

In truth,
\equiv

abstracts from the meaning of primitive programs

There are a number of natural facts about

We apply them without appealing explicitly to this lemma, and leave the proofs to the reader.

Lemma 2.10

The following hold:

1.

The relation
\equiv
is a congruence.
Concretely, this means that
\equiv
is an equivalence relation such that if
e_{1}\equiv f_{1}
and
e_{2}\equiv f_{2}

2.

The relation

3.

All operators are monotone w.r.t.

Specifically, if

e_{2}\leqq f_{2}

Some (classes of) KAs are

KAs; alternatively, we can think of these KAs as having enough flexibility to differentiate regular expressions that cannot be proven equal from the laws of KA alone.
As it turns out, both

(finite model property; Palka

Let

Definition 2.13

The

(\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*}),\cup,\cdot,{}^{*},\emptyset,\{\epsilon\})

Because the theorem above characterizes equations in KA in terms of a single KA, standard arguments from universal algebra imply that the

3
Model theory

The theorems above are closely connected, in that they imply one another

,

In this section, we provide alternative proofs in support of this, which also yields the (seemingly new) observation that

Besides this, none of the material in this section can truly be considered novel; we include our own proofs for the sake of self-containment.

We start with the fact that interpretations of expressions inside a star-continuous KA can be connected to the interpretations of words in their language semantics.
Here, and for the remainder of this article, we abuse notation by regarding words
w\in\Sigma^{*}
as regular expressions, identifying
\epsilon

1
.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
33
, Lemma 7.1]
)

Let
K
be a star-continuous KA, and let
h:\Sigma\to K
.
For all
e\in\mathbb{E}
and
x\in K
, if for all
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
it holds that
\widehat{h}(w)\leq x
, then we also have that
\widehat{h}(e)\leq x
.

Proof.

We prove the first claim by induction on
e
, showing more generally that for all
u,v,x\in K
, if for all
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
we have that
u\cdot\widehat{h}(w)\cdot v\leq x
, then
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e)\cdot v\leq x
.
First, if
e=0
, then
\widehat{\ell}(e)=\emptyset
, and so the claim holds vacuously.
If
e=1
or
e=\mathtt{a}
for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, then
\widehat{h}(e)=\widehat{h}(w)
for the unique
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
, and so
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e)\cdot v=u\cdot\widehat{h}(w)\cdot v\leq x
.
There are three inductive cases.

•

If
e=e_{1}+e_{2}
, then for all
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{i})\subseteq\widehat{\ell}(e)
we have that
u\cdot\widehat{h}(w)\cdot v\leq x
.
Hence, by induction,
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e_{i})\cdot v\leq x
, and thus
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e)\cdot v=u\cdot\widehat{h}(e_{1})\cdot v+u\cdot\widehat{h}(e_{2})\cdot v\leq x+x=x
.

•

If
e=e_{1}\cdot e_{2}
, then for all
w_{1}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
and
w_{2}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{2})
we have that
u\cdot\widehat{h}(w_{1})\cdot\widehat{h}(w_{2})\cdot v\leq x
.
Thus, by induction, it follows that for all
w_{1}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
we have that
u\cdot\widehat{h}(w_{1})\cdot\widehat{h}(e_{2})\cdot v\leq x
.
Applying the induction hypothesis once more, we find that
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e)\cdot v=u\cdot\widehat{h}(e_{1})\cdot\widehat{h}(e_{2})\cdot v\leq x
.

•

If
e=e_{1}^{*}
, then an argument similar to the above shows that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
u\cdot\widehat{h}(w)\cdot v\leq x
for all
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})^{n}
, then
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e_{1})^{n}\cdot v\leq x
.
Because
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})^{n}\subseteq\widehat{\ell}(e_{1}^{*})
, the premise holds for each
n
, hence
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e)^{n}\cdot v\leq x
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By star-continuity,
u\cdot\widehat{h}(e^{*})\cdot v=u\cdot\widehat{h}(e)^{*}\cdot v\leq x
.

∎

The following property, which connects words in the language semantics to provable equivalence, is also useful.
It almost directly implies the converse of the previous lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
33
, Lemma 7.1]
)

For all
e\in\mathbb{E}
and
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
, it holds that
w\leqq e
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
e
.
In the base, there are three cases.
If
e=0
, then
\widehat{\ell}(e)=\emptyset
, so the claim holds vacuously; if
e=1
or
e=\mathtt{a}
for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, then
w=e
, so so the claim is trivial.

In the inductive step, there are three cases to consider.
First, if
e=e_{1}+e_{2}
, then without loss of generality
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
; by induction
w\leqq e_{1}\leqq e
.
Second, if
e=e_{1}\cdot e_{2}
, then
w=w_{1}w_{2}
with
w_{i}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{i})
; by induction and monotonicity,
w=w_{1}w_{2}\leqq e_{1}e_{2}=e
.
Last, if
e=e_{1}^{*}
, then
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
for
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
; by induction,
w_{i}\leqq e_{1}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, meaning
w\leqq e_{1}^{n}
.
A straightforward induction on
n
using the rule
f\equiv 1+f\cdot f^{*}
shows that
e_{1}^{n}\leqq e_{1}^{*}
for all
n
, and so we are done.
∎

Remark 3.3

Taken together,
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
can be interpreted as saying that if
K
is star-continuous and
h:\Sigma\to K
is a function, then
\widehat{h}(e)
is the least upper bound (inside
K
) of all
\widehat{h}(w)
for
w\in\widehat{\ell}(w)
.
With some additional effort, these facts can be leveraged to show that in this setting
\widehat{h}
is
continuous
, in the sense that it commutes with (possibly infinite) suprema in
\mathbb{E}
w.r.t.
\leqq
.

For our purposes, these lemmas tell us that equations of the language model must also hold in any star-continuous KA.
This can be seen as a variation of
[
10
, Theorem 3]
for KA
without tests
.

Lemma 3.4

Let
e,f\in\mathbb{E}
.
If
\mathcal{L}\models e=f
, then
\mathfrak{C}\models e=f
.

Proof.

Let
K
be a star-continuous KA, and let
h:\Sigma\to K
.
If
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)=\widehat{\ell}(f)
, then
w\leqq f
by
Lemma

3.2
, whence
\widehat{h}(w)\leq\widehat{h}(f)
for all

.
By
Lemma

, the latter implies that
\widehat{h}(e)\leq\widehat{h}(f)

∎

Furthermore, equivalence in all finite relational models implies equivalence in the language model.
We show this by constructing a sequence of finite relational KAs that approximate the language model, in that each of these encapsulates agreement of the language semantics on words up to a certain length.

Lemma 3.5

Let
e,f\in\mathbb{E}
.
If
\mathfrak{FR}\models e=f
, then
\mathcal{L}\models e=f
.

Proof.

Our strategy is as follows: given
w\in\Sigma^{*}
of length
n
, we design a finite KA
K_{n}
and a map
h_{w}:\Sigma\to K_{n}
such that for any
g\in\mathbb{E}
,
\widehat{h_{w}}(g)
tells us whether
w\in\widehat{\ell}(g)
.
Given that
e
and
f
must be equivalent in any finite KA, it must be the case that
\widehat{h_{w}}(e)=\widehat{h_{w}}(f)
, from which we conclude language equivalence.

In more detail, we proceed as follows.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
K_{n}=\mathcal{R}(\{0,\dots,n\})
.
For
w=\mathtt{a}_{0}\dots\mathtt{a}_{n-1}\in\Sigma^{*}
, we define
h_{w}:\Sigma\to K_{n}
by
h_{w}(\mathtt{a})=\{(i,i+1):\mathtt{a}=\mathtt{a}_{i}\}
.
We now claim the following: for
g\in\mathbb{E}
, we have that
(0,n)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g)
if and only if
w\in\widehat{\ell}(g)
, or more generally, for all
0\leq i\leq j\leq n
it holds that
(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g)
if and only if
\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g)
.
Suppose this property is true: if
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
with length
n
, then
(0,n)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(e)
, and because
\mathfrak{FR}\models e=f
, we have that
(0,n)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(f)
, which tells us that
w\in\widehat{\ell}(f)
.
This shows that
\widehat{\ell}(e)\subseteq\widehat{\ell}(f)
; the proof of the converse inclusion is analogous.

It remains to prove the claim, which we do by induction on
g
.
In the base, there are three cases.
If
g=0
, then the claim holds vacuously, as
\widehat{h_{w}}(g)=\widehat{\ell}(g)=\emptyset
.
If
g=1
, then
(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g)
if and only if
i=j
, which is equivalent to
\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}=\epsilon
; the latter holds precisely when
\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g)
.
If
g=\mathtt{a}
for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, then the claim holds by definition of
h_{w}
and
\ell
.

For the inductive step, there are three more cases:

•

If
g=g_{1}+g_{2}
, then we derive

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g)

\displaystyle\iff(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g_{1})\vee(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g_{2})

\displaystyle\iff\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g_{1})\vee\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g_{2})

(IH)

\displaystyle\iff\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g)

•

If
g=g_{1}\cdot g_{2}
, then we derive

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g)

\displaystyle\iff\exists k.\ (i,k)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g_{1})\wedge(k,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g_{2})

\displaystyle\iff\exists k.\ \mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{k-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g_{1})\wedge\mathtt{a}_{k}\mathtt{a}_{k+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g_{2})

(IH)

\displaystyle\iff\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g)

•

If
g=g_{1}^{*}
, then
(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g)=\widehat{h_{w}}(g_{1})^{*}
if and only if
(i,j)\in\widehat{h_{w}}(g_{1})^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The latter is equivalent to saying that
\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g_{1})^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which can be shown by induction on
n
using an argument similar to the previous case.
Finally,
\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g_{1})^{n}
holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
precisely when
\mathtt{a}_{i}\mathtt{a}_{i+1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{j-1}\in\widehat{\ell}(g_{1})^{*}=\widehat{\ell}(g)
.

∎

We start our journey by recalling

as a way of representing languages.
Each automaton can be seen as a system of equations, which induces a

None of the material in this section is novel, and in fact, most of it is well-known.
However, least solutions play a central role in the arguments to come, and so it helps to define and discuss them in detail.

Definition 4.1

(Automaton)

A (

Kleene’s theorem says that the set of languages defined by regular expressions is the same as the set of languages described by automata

Definition 4.2

(Solutions)

Let
e\in\mathbb{E}
.
An
e
-solution
to
A
is a function
s:Q\to\mathbb{E}
such that the following hold for all

q\in F\implies{e\leqq s(q)}q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,\mathtt{a})\implies{\mathtt{a}\cdot s(q^{\prime})\leqq s(q)}

A

.

It is not too hard to show that if

for all states

Consider the automaton

Theorem 4.5

Let
Q
be finite.
Given
M:Q\times Q\to\mathbb{E}
and

, we can compute a
Q

s:Q\to\mathbb{E}
such that for all
e\in\mathbb{E}
, we have that
s\cdot e
is the least
e
-solution to
(M,b)
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
Q
.
In the base, where
Q=\emptyset
, the claim holds vacuously.
For the inductive step, fix any
q\in Q
, and let
P=Q\setminus\{q\}
.
We now choose
N:P\times P\to\mathbb{E}
and
c:P\to\mathbb{E}
as follows:

N(p,p^{\prime})=M(p,p^{\prime})+M(p,q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot M(q,p^{\prime})c(p)=b(p)+M(p,q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot b(q)

By induction we can now compute a

such that for all
e\in\mathbb{E}
, we have that
t\cdot e
is the least
e
-solution to
(N,c)
.
We base our choice for
s
on

s(q^{\prime})=\begin{cases}{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot(b(q)+\sum_{p\in P}M(q,p)\cdot t(p))&q^{\prime}=q\\
t(q^{\prime})&q^{\prime}\neq q\end{cases}

We now need to verify that

To see that
s\cdot e
is an

(M,b)
, it suffices to show that

is a solution.
For the first constraint, note that if
q^{\prime}=q

by definition of

, by the fact that

(N,c)

For the second constraint, let

•

If
q^{\prime}=q=q^{\prime\prime}
then by definition of

\displaystyle M(q,q)\cdot s(q)

\displaystyle=M(q,q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot(b(q)+\sum_{p\in P}M(q,p)\cdot t(p))

\displaystyle\leqq{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot(b(q)+\sum_{p\in P}M(q,p)\cdot t(q))=s(q)

•

If
q^{\prime}\neq q=q^{\prime\prime}
then by definition of

N

\displaystyle M(q^{\prime},q)\cdot s(q)

\displaystyle=M(q^{\prime},q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot(b(q)+\sum_{p\in P}M(q,p)\cdot t(p))

\displaystyle\equiv M(q^{\prime},q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot b(q)+\sum_{p\in P}M(q^{\prime},q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot M(q,p)\cdot t(q)

\displaystyle\leqq c(q^{\prime})+\sum_{p\in P}N(q^{\prime},p)\cdot t(p)\leqq t(q^{\prime})=s(q^{\prime})

•

If
q^{\prime}=q\neq q^{\prime\prime}
then by definition of

\displaystyle M(q,q^{\prime\prime})\cdot s(q^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=M(q,q^{\prime\prime})\cdot t(q^{\prime\prime})\leqq{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot M(q,q^{\prime\prime})\cdot t(q^{\prime\prime})\leqq s(q)

If
q^{\prime}\neq q\neq q^{\prime\prime}
then by definition of

and
N

\displaystyle M(q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime})\cdot s(q^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\leqq N(q^{\prime},q^{\prime\prime})\cdot t(q^{\prime\prime})\leqq t(q^{\prime})=s(q^{\prime})

s\cdot e

-solution to

To this end, choose

to derive:

\displaystyle N(p,p^{\prime})\cdot t^{\prime}(p^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv M(p,p^{\prime})\cdot s^{\prime}(p)+M(p,q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot M(q,p^{\prime})\cdot s^{\prime}(p^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leqq M(p,p^{\prime})\cdot s^{\prime}(p)+M(p,q)\cdot{M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot s^{\prime}(q)

\displaystyle\leqq M(p,p^{\prime})\cdot s^{\prime}(p)+M(p,q)\cdot s^{\prime}(q)\leqq s^{\prime}(p)=t^{\prime}(p)

This makes

an
e
-solution to
(N,c)
, and thus by induction we have for
p\in P
that
s(p)\cdot e=t(p)\cdot e\leqq t^{\prime}(p)=s^{\prime}(p)
.
It remains to show
s(q)\cdot e\leqq s^{\prime}(q)
.
Since
t(p)\cdot e\leqq t^{\prime}(p)
for all
p\in P
, we find:

M(q,q)\cdot s^{\prime}(q)+(b(q)+\sum_{p\in P}M(q,p)\cdot t(p))\cdot e\leqq s^{\prime}(q)

and so
s(q)\cdot e={M(q,q)}^{*}\cdot(b(q)+\sum_{p\in P}M(q,p)\cdot t(p))\cdot e\leqq s^{\prime}(q)
by the fixpoint rule.
∎

Least

-solutions to automata, by casting the automaton in the form of a linear system and arguing that they have the same solutions.

(Solutions via state elimination)

We can compute a vector

is the least

-solution to

Proof.

We choose a

We now claim that

is an

-solution to

iff it is an

-solution to

.

For the forward claim, let

be an

-solution to

-solution to

Conversely, let

be an

-solution to

If

.
Moreover, if

∎

.

6
Antimirov’s construction

We now discuss automata
A_{e}
that accept
\widehat{\ell}(e)
for each
e\in\mathbb{E}
, and their solutions.
Many methods to obtain such an automaton exist
[
36
,
37
]
— we refer to
[
38
]
for a good overview.
In this section, we recall Antimirov’s construction
[
27
]
in our own notation, and argue two crucial properties.

The idea behind Antimirov’s construction is to endow expressions with the structure of an automaton, as was done by Brzozowski
[
37
]
.
The language of a state represented by
e\in\mathbb{E}
is meant to be
\widehat{\ell}(e)
, i.e., the language of its expression.
From this perspective, the accepting states should be those representing expressions whose language contains the empty word.
These expressions are fairly easy to describe.

Definition 6.1

The set
\mathbb{F}
is defined as the smallest subset of
\mathbb{E}
satisfying:

\frac{~}{1\in\mathbb{F}}\frac{e_{1}\in\mathbb{F}\quad e_{2}\in\mathbb{E}}{e_{1}+e_{2}\in\mathbb{F}}\frac{e_{1}\in\mathbb{E}\quad e_{2}\in\mathbb{F}}{e_{1}+e_{2}\in\mathbb{F}}\frac{e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathbb{F}}{e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\in\mathbb{F}}\frac{e_{1}\in\mathbb{E}}{e_{1}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}}

A straightforward argument by induction on
e
tells us that
\epsilon\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
if and only if
e\in\mathbb{F}
.

For example,
1\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}
because
1\in\mathbb{F}
and
{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}
; indeed,
\epsilon\in\widehat{\ell}(1\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*})
.
Meanwhile,
\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\not\in\mathbb{F}
despite
{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}
, because
\mathtt{a}\not\in\mathbb{F}
; this matches the fact that
\epsilon\not\in\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\not\in\mathbb{F}
.

Next, we recall Antimirov’s transition function.
Intuitively, an expression
e
has an
\mathtt{a}
-transition to an expression
e^{\prime}
when
e^{\prime}
denotes remainders of words in
\widehat{\ell}(e)
that start with
\mathtt{a}
— i.e., if
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e^{\prime})
, then
\mathtt{a}w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
.
Together, the expressions reachable by
\mathtt{a}
-transitions from
e
describe
all
such words.

In the following, when
S\subseteq\mathbb{E}
and
e\in\mathbb{E}
, we write
S\fatsemi e
for
\{e^{\prime}\cdot e:e^{\prime}\in S\}
.
We also write
e\star S
as a shorthand for
S
when
e\in\mathbb{F}
, and
\emptyset
otherwise.

Definition 6.2

We define
\partial:\mathbb{E}\times\Sigma\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{E})
recursively, as follows

\displaystyle\partial(0,\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\partial(e_{1}+e_{2},\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})\cup\partial(e_{2},\mathtt{b})

\displaystyle\partial(1,\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\partial(e_{1}\cdot e_{2},\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})\fatsemi e_{2}\cup e_{1}\star\partial(e_{2},\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle\partial(\mathtt{b},\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\{1:\mathtt{a}=\mathtt{b}\}

\displaystyle\partial(e_{1}^{*},\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})\fatsemi e_{1}^{*}

Antimirov’s transition function can decompose an expression
e
into several “derivatives”
e^{\prime}
, each of which describes the “tails” of words denoted by
e
, while
\mathbb{F}
tells us whether
e
accepts the empty word.
The following lemma relates this information back to
e
.
It can be viewed as one half of the
fundamental theorem
of Kleene algebra
[
39
]
, which says that an expression can be reconstructed from its derivatives, and whether or not it appears in
\mathbb{F}
; we prove this weaker version, because it is all we need.

Lemma 6.3

Let
e\in\mathbb{E}
.
If
e\in\mathbb{F}
, then
1\leqq e
.
Furthermore, if
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
and
e^{\prime}\in\partial(e,\mathtt{a})
, then
\mathtt{a}\cdot e^{\prime}\leqq e
.

Proof.

The first claim follows by a straightforward induction on the construction of
\mathbb{F}
.
The proof of the second claim proceeds by induction on
e
.
In the base, the cases where
e=0
or
e=1
hold vacuously, as
\partial(e,\mathtt{a})=\emptyset
.
If
e=\mathtt{b}
for some
\mathtt{b}\in\Sigma
, then
e^{\prime}\in\partial(e,\mathtt{a})
implies that
e^{\prime}=1
and
\mathtt{a}=\mathtt{b}
.
We then have that
\mathtt{a}\cdot e^{\prime}=\mathtt{b}\cdot 1\leqq\mathtt{b}=e
.
For the inductive step, there are three more cases.

•

If
e=e_{1}+e_{2}
, then w.l.o.g.
e^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})
.
By induction, we then have that
\mathtt{a}\cdot e^{\prime}\leqq e_{1}\leqq e
.

•

If
e=e_{1}\cdot e_{2}
, then there are two subcases.
First, if
e^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{2}
with
e_{1}^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})
, then by induction
\mathtt{a}\cdot e_{1}^{\prime}\leqq e_{1}
, so
\mathtt{a}\cdot e^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}\cdot e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{2}\leqq e_{1}\cdot e_{2}=e
.
Otherwise, if
e_{1}\in\mathbb{F}
and
e^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{2},\mathtt{a})
, then
\mathtt{a}\cdot e^{\prime}\leqq e_{2}
by induction.
Furthermore, since
e_{1}\in\mathbb{F}
, by the first part of the lemma we have that
1\leqq e_{1}
.
Putting this together, we find that
\mathtt{a}\cdot e^{\prime}\leqq e_{2}\equiv 1\cdot e_{2}\leqq e_{1}\cdot e_{2}=e
.

•

If
e=e_{1}^{*}
, then
e^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{1}^{*}
with
e_{1}^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})
.
By induction, we then know that
\mathtt{a}\cdot e_{1}^{\prime}\leqq e_{1}
, and hence
\mathtt{a}\cdot e^{\prime}=\mathtt{a}\cdot e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{1}^{*}\leqq e_{1}\cdot e_{1}^{*}\leqq e_{1}^{*}=e
.

∎

The expression
e
could serve as the sole initial state in the automaton for
e
.
However, our automata allow multiple initial states, and distributing this task among them simplifies our arguments.

Definition 6.4

We define
\iota:\mathbb{E}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{E})
recursively, as follows:

\displaystyle\iota(0)

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\iota(e_{1}+e_{2})

\displaystyle=\iota(e_{1})\cup\iota(e_{2})

\displaystyle\iota(1)

\displaystyle=\{1\}

\displaystyle\iota(e_{1}\cdot e_{2})

\displaystyle=\iota(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{2}

\displaystyle\iota(\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\{\mathtt{a}\}

\displaystyle\iota(e_{1}^{*})

\displaystyle=\iota(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{1}^{*}\cup\{1\}

We can validate that the expressions in
\iota(e)
together make up
e
, as follows.

Lemma 6.5

Let
e\in\mathbb{E}
.
Now
e\equiv\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)}e^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The claim holds by definition in all three base cases.
The inductive arguments are as follows.

•

If
e=e_{1}+e_{2}
, then we derive that

e_{1}+e_{2}\equiv\sum_{e_{1}^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1})}e_{1}+\sum_{e_{2}^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{2})}e_{2}^{\prime}\equiv\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1})\cup\iota(e_{2})}e^{\prime}=\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1}+e_{2})}e^{\prime}

•

If
e=e_{1}\cdot e_{2}
, then we calculate

e_{1}\cdot e_{2}\equiv\Bigl(\sum_{e_{1}^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1})}e_{1}^{\prime}\Bigr)\cdot e_{2}\equiv\sum_{e_{1}^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1})}e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{2}=\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1}\cdot e_{2})}e^{\prime}

•

If
e=e_{1}^{*}
, then we have

e_{1}^{*}\equiv 1+e_{1}\cdot e_{1}^{*}\equiv 1+\sum_{e_{1}^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1})}e_{1}\cdot e_{1}^{*}\equiv\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(1+e_{1}\cdot e_{1}^{*})}e^{\prime}=\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e_{1}^{*})}e^{\prime}

∎

We could now try to package these parts into an automaton
(\mathbb{E},\partial,\iota(e),\mathbb{F})
for each expression
e
.
Because we work with finite automata, a little more work is necessary to identify the finite set of expressions that are relevant (i.e., represented by reachable states) for a starting expression
e
.

Definition 6.6

We define
\rho:\mathbb{E}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{E})
recursively, as follows:

\displaystyle\rho(0)

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\rho(e_{1}+e_{2})

\displaystyle=\rho(e_{1})\cup\rho(e_{2})

\displaystyle\rho(1)

\displaystyle=\{1\}

\displaystyle\rho(e_{1}\cdot e_{2})

\displaystyle=\rho(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{2}\cup\rho(e_{2})

\displaystyle\rho(\mathtt{a})

\displaystyle=\{\mathtt{a},1\}

\displaystyle\rho(e_{1}^{*})

\displaystyle=\rho(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{1}^{*}\cup\{1\}

Example 6.7

If we take
e=\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}
, then we can calculate that

\displaystyle\rho(e)

\displaystyle=\rho(\mathtt{a})\fatsemi{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\cup\rho({(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*})

\displaystyle=\{\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*},\;1\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\}\cup\rho({(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*})

\displaystyle=\{\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*},\;1\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\}\cup\{1\}\cup\rho(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})\fatsemi{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}

\displaystyle=\{\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*},\;1\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\}\cup\{1\}\cup(\rho(\mathtt{b})\fatsemi\mathtt{a}\cup\rho(\mathtt{a}))\fatsemi{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}

\displaystyle=\{\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*},\;1\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\}\cup\{1\}\cup\{\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a},\;1\cdot\mathtt{a},\;\mathtt{a},\;1\}\fatsemi{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}

\displaystyle=\{\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*},\;1\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*},\;1,\;\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*},\;1\cdot\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}\}

Note how there is some redundancy in the set above because the semantics of some expressions (e.g.,
\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}
and
1\cdot\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}
) clearly coincide.
This is fine for us, because we just want to use Antimirov automata in proofs; if we wanted to optimize for the number of states, then
\rho
needs some refinement.

With this function in hand, we can verify that it fits all of the requirements of the state space of an automaton with respect to the other parts identified above.

Lemma 6.8

For all
e\in\mathbb{E}
, the set
\rho(e)
is finite and closed under
\partial
, i.e., if
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)
and
e^{\prime\prime}\in\partial(e^{\prime},\mathtt{a})
, then
e^{\prime\prime}\in\rho(e)
as well.
Furthermore, we have that
\iota(e)\subseteq\rho(e)
.

Proof.

The proof of the first property proceeds by induction on
e
; we omit the arguments towards finiteness, as this holds for the base cases and is clearly preserved by both
\cup
and
\fatsemi
.
For closure under
\partial
, we show more generally that if
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)
, then
\rho(e^{\prime})\subseteq\rho(e)
, and that
\partial(e,\mathtt{a})\subseteq\rho(e)
for all
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
.
In the base, the claim holds by mere computation.
For the inductive step, there are three cases.

•

If
e=e_{1}+e_{2}
, then
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e_{1})
or
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e_{2})
; we assume the former without loss of generality.
By induction,
\rho(e^{\prime})\subseteq\rho(e_{1})
, and so
\rho(e^{\prime})\subseteq\rho(e)
as well.
For the second property, we note that
\partial(e,\mathtt{a})=\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})\cup\partial(e_{2},\mathtt{a})
.
By induction, the latter is a subset of
\rho(e_{1})\cup\rho(e_{2})=\rho(e)
.

•

If
e=e_{1}\cdot e_{2}
, then there are two cases.
If
e^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{2}
with
e_{1}^{\prime}\in\rho(e_{1})
, then
\rho(e^{\prime})=\rho(e_{1}^{\prime})\fatsemi e_{2}\cup\rho(e_{2})
.
By induction, the latter is contained in
\rho(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{2}\cup\rho(e_{2})=\rho(e)
.
Otherwise, if
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e_{2})
, then by induction
\rho(e^{\prime})\subseteq\rho(e_{2})\subseteq\rho(e)
.
For the second property, note
\partial(e,\mathtt{a})=\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})\fatsemi e_{2}\cup e_{1}\star\partial(e_{2},\mathtt{a})
.
By induction, this set is contained in
\rho(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{2}\cup\rho(e_{2})=\rho(e)
.

•

If
e=e_{1}^{*}
, we need only consider the case where
e^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{1}^{*}
with
e_{1}^{\prime}\in\rho(e_{1})
.
Here,
\rho(e^{\prime})=\rho(e_{1}^{\prime})\fatsemi e_{1}^{*}\cup\rho(e_{1}^{*})\subseteq\rho(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{1}^{*}\cup\rho(e_{1}^{*})\subseteq\rho(e)
, with the second containment following by induction.
For the second property, we derive that
\partial(e_{1}^{*},\mathtt{a})=\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})\fatsemi e_{1}^{*}\subseteq\rho(e_{1})\fatsemi e_{1}^{*}\subseteq\rho(e)
.

The fact that
\iota(e)\subseteq\rho(e)
follows by another straightforward induction on
e
.
∎

In light of the above, can treat
\partial
as a function
\partial:\rho(e)\times\Sigma\to\mathcal{P}(\rho(e))
with impunity.
If we now write
\mathbb{F}_{e}
for
\mathbb{F}\cap\rho(e)
, then we can define Antimirov automata as follows.

Definition 6.9

Let
e\in\mathbb{E}
.
We write
A_{e}
for the
Antimirov automaton

(\rho(e),\partial,\iota(e),\mathbb{F}_{e})
.

Example 6.10

If
e=\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}

is the automaton depicted in

.

We end this section with two technical lemmas about Antimirov automata.
The first says that for all
e\in\mathbb{E}
, the language of
A_{e}
contains all of the words in
\widehat{\ell}(e)
.
This is essentially one half of the correctness theorem for Antimirov’s construction
[
27
]
; the other half is also not too hard to show, but we argue this weaker version because it is sufficient for our purposes.

Lemma 6.11

(One half of
[
27
, Theorem 4.1]
)

Let
e\in\mathbb{E}
.
Now
\widehat{\ell}(e)\subseteq L(A_{e})
.

Proof.

We start by establishing two useful properties: first, if
\epsilon\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
, then
e\in\mathbb{F}
; second, if
\mathtt{a}w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
, then
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e^{\prime})
for some
e^{\prime}\in\partial(e,\mathtt{a})
.
The first claim follows by a straightforward induction on
e
.
As for the second claim, we also proceed by induction on
e
.
In the base, we need only consider the case where
e=\mathtt{b}
for some
\mathtt{b}\in\Sigma
.
By the premise, we then know that
w=\epsilon
and
\mathtt{a}=\mathtt{b}
, so we can choose
e^{\prime}=1
to satisfy the claim.
For the inductive step, there are three cases.

•

If
e=e_{1}+e_{2}
, then without loss of generality
\mathtt{a}w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
.
By induction, we then have that
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1}^{\prime})
for some
e_{1}^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})\subseteq\partial(e,\mathtt{a})
, completing the claim.

•

If
e=e_{1}\cdot e_{2}
, then there are two subcases, depending on where
\mathtt{a}
comes from.
If
\epsilon\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
and
\mathtt{a}w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{2})
, then by induction
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{2}^{\prime})
for some
e_{2}^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{2},\mathtt{a})
.
Furthermore, by the first property, we have that
e_{1}\in\mathbb{F}
.
We then know that
e_{2}^{\prime}\in\partial(e,\mathtt{a})
.
Otherwise, if
w=w_{1}w_{2}
such that
\mathtt{a}w_{1}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
and
w_{2}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{2})
, then by induction
w_{1}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1}^{\prime})
for some
e_{1}^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})
.
We can then choose
e^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{2}\in\partial(e,\mathtt{a})
to satisfy the claim.

•

If
e=e_{1}^{*}
, then
\mathtt{a}w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
such that
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1})
.
Without loss of generality, each of these
w_{i}
is non-empty, and so
w_{1}=\mathtt{a}w_{1}^{\prime}
for some word
w_{1}^{\prime}
; furthermore,
w_{2}\cdots w_{n}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1}^{*})
.
Now we have by induction that
w_{1}^{\prime}\in\widehat{\ell}(e_{1}^{\prime})
for some
e_{1}^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{1},\mathtt{a})
.
We can then choose
e^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}\cdot e_{1}^{*}
to find that
w=w_{1}^{\prime}w_{2}\cdots w_{n}\in\widehat{\ell}(e^{\prime})
and
e^{\prime}\in\partial(e_{1}^{*},\mathtt{a})
.

With these properties in hand, we can now prove that for all
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)
, if
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e^{\prime})
, then
w\in L(A_{e},e^{\prime})
, by induction on
w
.
In the base, where
w=\epsilon
, the first property tells us that
e^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}
, and so
\epsilon\in L(A_{e},e^{\prime})
by definition.
Otherwise, we can write
w=\mathtt{a}w^{\prime}
, which by the second property tells us that
w^{\prime}\in\widehat{\ell}(e^{\prime\prime})
for some
e^{\prime\prime}\in\partial(e^{\prime},\mathtt{a})
, whence
w^{\prime}\in L(A_{e},e^{\prime\prime})
by induction — but then
w=\mathtt{a}w^{\prime}\in L(A_{e},e^{\prime})
by definition of
L(A_{e},-)
.
Now, if
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
, then
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e^{\prime})
for some
e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)
by
Lemma

6.5
, and hence
w\in L(A_{e},e^{\prime})
by the above, which means that
w\in L(A_{e})
as claimed.
∎

The final property of Antimirov automata that we need is that the solution to
A_{e}
is below
e
.
As before, it is possible to show the converse, i.e.,
e\leqq\lfloor A_{e}\rfloor

[
28
]
.
However, this requires some additional formal tools, and is in fact not necessary to show the finite model property.
The property is an easy consequence of
Lemma

6.3
, and in light of similar results for other constructions
[
23
,
24
]
, not surprising.

Lemma 6.12

For all
e\in\mathbb{E}
, it holds that
\lfloor A_{e}\rfloor\leqq e
.

Proof.

Let
s:\rho(e)\to\mathbb{E}
be the injection.

Lemma

6.3
shows that
s
is a solution to
A_{e}
, meaning that for
e^{\prime}\in\rho(e)
we have
\overline{A_{e}}(e^{\prime})\leqq s(e^{\prime})=e^{\prime}
, by
Theorem

4.6
.
Using
Lemma

6.5
, we then conclude

\lfloor A_{e}\rfloor\equiv\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)}\overline{A_{e}}(e^{\prime})\leqq\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)}e^{\prime}\equiv e

∎

7
The Finite Model Property

Recall that our objective was to prove the finite model property.
To this end, we shall derive a finite Kleene algebra for each expression, whose properties can then be used to conclude the finite model property, thus arriving at our second contribution.
We already saw how an expression gives rise to an automaton and its transformation automata.
As it happens, the states of transformation automata have the internal structure of a
monoid
— indeed, this was the original motivation for the construction
[
25
]
.

Definition 7.1

A
monoid
is a tuple
(M,\cdot,1)
where
M
is a set,
\cdot
is a binary operator and
1\in M
such that the following hold for all
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\in M
:

m_{1}\cdot(m_{2}\cdot m_{3})=(m_{1}\cdot m_{2})\cdot m_{3}m_{1}\cdot 1=m_{1}1\cdot m_{1}=m_{1}

A function
h:\Sigma\to M
gives rise to the function
\widetilde{h}:\Sigma^{*}\to M
, defined by

\widetilde{h}(\mathtt{a}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{a}_{n})=h(\mathtt{a_{1}})\cdot\cdots\cdot h(\mathtt{a_{n}})

As for KAs, we may denote a general monoid
(M,\cdot,1)
with its carrier
M
.

If
A=(Q,\delta,I,F)
is an automaton, then
\mathcal{R}(Q)
, the state space of its transformation automata, has a monoidal structure: the operator is relational composition, and the unit is the identity relation.

A monoid can be lifted to sets of its elements, which lets us define the other operators of a KA.

Lemma 7.2

(see
[
21
]
)

If
(M,\cdot,1)
is a monoid, then
(\mathcal{P}(M),\cup,\otimes,{}^{\circledast},\emptyset,\{1\})
is a star-continuous KA, where for
U,V\subseteq M
:

U\otimes V=\{m\cdot n:m\in U,n\in V\}U^{\circledast}=\{u_{1}\cdots u_{n}:u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in U\}

As an example, note that
\Sigma^{*}
has a monoidal structure (with concatenation as operation and
\epsilon
as unit), and that
\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})
as derived from the above is precisely the KA of languages.

Remark 7.3

The algebraically minded reader may interject that the monoid of relations has an even richer structure, namely that of a
idempotent semiring
, with the additive operator given by union.
A finite semiring can directly be treated a (star-continuous) KA, without involving the powerset: simply definite
x^{*}
as the (finite!) sum of
x^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The problem with this approach is that the resulting KA may fail to satisfy
Lemmas

7.6
and
7.7
, which we need to prove the finite model property.

Now, given an expression
e\in\mathbb{E}
, a monoid
(M,\cdot,1)
, and a map
h:\Sigma\to M
, we have two ways of interpreting
e
inside
\mathcal{P}(M)
.
On the one hand, we can lift
\mathtt{a}\mapsto\{h(\mathtt{a})\}
to obtain a map
\mathbb{E}\to\mathcal{P}(M)
.
On the other hand, we can map each
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
to an element of
M
via
\widetilde{h}:\Sigma^{*}\to M
to obtain a subset of
M
.
The next lemma shows that these interpretations are actually the same; it can be thought of as a generalization of
[
21
, Lemma 3.1]
, which covers the special case for the syntactic monoid.

Lemma 7.4

Let
M
be a monoid and let
\mathcal{P}(M)
be the KA obtained from it, per
Lemma

7.2
.
Also, let
h_{1}:\Sigma\to M
and
h_{2}:\Sigma\to\mathcal{P}(M)
be such that if
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, then
h_{2}(\mathtt{a})=\{h_{1}(\mathtt{a})\}
.
Then for all
e\in\mathbb{E}
:

\widehat{h_{2}}(e)=\{\widetilde{h_{1}}(w):w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)\}

In categorical terms, if the diagram on the left in
Figure

5
commutes, then so does the one on the right.

Proof.

For the inclusion from right to left, note that if
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
, then
\widetilde{h_{1}}(w)\in\widehat{h_{2}}(w)\subseteq\widehat{h_{2}}(e)
by
Lemma

3.2
.
For the other inclusion, note that for all
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
we have
\widehat{h_{2}}(w)=\{\widetilde{h_{1}}(w)\}\subseteq\{\widetilde{h_{1}}(w):w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)\}
, and so
\widehat{h_{2}}(e)\subseteq\{\widetilde{h_{1}}(w):w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)\}
by
Lemma

3.1
.
∎

In particular, every expression
e
gives rise to an automaton
A_{e}
, which in turn gives a monoid
(\mathcal{R}(\rho(e)),\circ,\mathsf{id})
that yields a KA with carrier
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{R}(\rho(e)))
; we write
\mathcal{K}_{e}
for this KA, and
h_{e}
for the map from
\Sigma
to
\mathcal{K}_{e}
given by
h_{e}(\mathtt{a})=\{\partial_{\mathtt{a}}\}
.
This is precisely the KA that we will need to prove the FMP.

Example 7.5

If we set
e=\mathtt{a}\cdot{(\mathtt{b}\cdot\mathtt{a})}^{*}
, then
A_{e}
is as in
Figure

4
.
The accompanying transformation automaton happens to be isomorphic to the one drawn in
Figure

3
.
The state space of this transformation automaton induces a monoid
(\mathcal{R}(\rho(e)),\circ,\mathsf{id})
, and hence a KA
\mathcal{K}_{e}=(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{R}(\rho(e))),\cup,\otimes,{}^{\oast},\emptyset,\{\mathsf{id}\})
.

We can interpret the expression
\mathtt{a}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{*}\cdot\mathtt{a}
in
\mathcal{K}_{e}
;
Lemma

7.4
then tells us that

\widehat{h_{e}}(\mathtt{a}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{*}\cdot\mathtt{a})=\{\widetilde{h_{e}}(w):w\in\widehat{\ell}(\mathtt{a}\cdot\mathtt{b}^{*}\cdot\mathtt{a})\}=\{\partial_{\mathtt{aa}},\partial_{\mathtt{aba}},\partial_{\mathtt{abba}},\dots\}=\{\emptyset,\partial_{\mathtt{a}}\}

We can also interpret
e
itself inside
\mathcal{K}_{e}
to find that

\widehat{h_{e}}(e)=\{\widetilde{h_{e}}(w):w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)\}=\{\partial_{\mathtt{a}},\partial_{\mathtt{aba}},\partial_{\mathtt{ababa}},\dots\}=\{\partial_{\mathtt{a}}\}

The KA
\mathcal{K}_{e}
thus gives us a way to obtain a bunch of relations on the state space of
A_{e}
, the Antimirov automaton of
e
.
Now, each of these relations
R
represents a set of words that have the same effect on
A_{e}
, and we know that we can obtain an expression for all of those words by solving the transformation automaton with
R
as an accepting state.
But then, in light of
Lemma

7.4
, we should expect that all of the relations in
\widehat{h_{e}}(e)
are of the form
\partial_{w}
, for some
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
; these must then correspond to expressions whose words bring about a transformation from an initial state of
A_{e}
to some accepting state.
This line of reasoning can again be reflected on the level of equational reasoning, as follows.

Lemma 7.6

Let
e\in\mathbb{E}
.
If
R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(e)
, then
\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor\leqq e
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

7.4
, we have that
R=\partial_{w}
for some
w\in\widehat{\ell}(e)
.
But then
e^{\prime}\mathrel{\partial_{w}}e^{\prime\prime}
for some
e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)
and
e^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{e}
, by
Lemma

6.11
.
By
Lemma

5.6
, it follows that
\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor\leqq\overline{A_{e}}(e^{\prime})
, and since
e^{\prime}\in\iota(e)
also
\overline{A_{e}}(e^{\prime})\leqq\lfloor A_{e}\rfloor
.
Finally, using
Lemma

6.12
, we conclude by noting that
\lfloor A_{e}\rfloor\leqq e
.
∎

We can also think of the expressions obtained by solving the automaton
A_{e}[R]
for
R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f)
as partitioning the set of all words: each
w\in\Sigma^{*}
corresponds to precisely one transformation on the state space of
A_{e}
.
If we turn to
Lemma

7.4
once more, each of the relations in
\widehat{h_{e}}(f)
is of the form
\partial_{w}
for
w\in\widehat{\ell}(f)
; because we can read
w
when moving from
\mathsf{id}
to
\partial_{w}
in
A_{e}[\partial_{w}]
, we have that
\lfloor A_{e}[\partial_{w}]\rfloor
must also include
w
.
In total, all of the
\lfloor A_{e}[\partial_{w}]\rfloor
together must include all of the words denoted by
f
(and possibly more), in essence approximating
f
from above using the expressions we obtained from
e
.
The algebraic counterpart to this line of reasoning goes as follows.

Lemma 7.7

Let
e,f\in\mathbb{E}
.
The following holds:

f\leqq\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f)}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
f
.
In the base, there are three cases.
If
f=0
, then the claim holds immediately.
If
f=1
, then note that
1\leqq\overline{A_{e}[\mathsf{id}]}(\mathsf{id})\leq\lfloor A_{e}[\mathsf{id}]\rfloor
; the claim then follows because
\widehat{h_{e}}(f)=\{\mathsf{id}\}
by definition.
If
f=\mathtt{a}
for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
, then
f\leqq\lfloor A_{e}[\partial_{\mathtt{a}}]\rfloor
by
Lemma

5.3
.
The claim then follows because
\widehat{h_{e}}(f)=\{\partial_{\mathtt{a}}\}
.
There are three inductive cases.

•

If
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
, then we calculate as follows:

\displaystyle f_{1}+f_{2}

\displaystyle\leqq\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor+\sum_{R\in\widehat{h}(f_{2})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(IH)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1})\cup\widehat{h}(f_{2})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(merge sums)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}+f_{2})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(def.
\widehat{h_{e}}
)

•

If
f=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}
, then we can derive

\displaystyle f_{1}\cdot f_{2}

\displaystyle\leqq\Bigl(\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{2})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor\Bigr)

(IH)

\displaystyle\leqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{1}\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1})\\
R_{2}\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{2})\end{subarray}}\lfloor A_{e}[R_{1}]\rfloor\cdot\lfloor A[R_{2}]\rfloor

(distributivity)

\displaystyle\leqq\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}\cdot f_{2})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(
Lemma

5.5
)

•

If
f=f_{1}^{*}
, then we find that:

\displaystyle 1+f_{1}\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}^{*})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leqq 1+\Bigl(\sum_{R_{1}\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1})}\lfloor A_{e}[R_{1}]\rfloor\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}^{*})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor\Bigr)

(IH)

\displaystyle\leqq 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{1}\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1})\\
R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}^{*})\end{subarray}}\lfloor A_{e}[R_{1}]\rfloor\cdot\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(distributivity)

\displaystyle\leqq 1+\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}\cdot f_{1}^{*})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(
Lemma

5.5
)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(1+f_{1}\cdot f_{1}^{*})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(def.
\lfloor-\rfloor
, merge sums)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}^{*})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor

(
f_{1}^{*}\equiv 1+f_{1}\cdot f_{1}^{*}
)

By the left fixpoint rule, it then follows that
f\equiv f_{1}^{*}\cdot 1\leqq\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f_{1}^{*})}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor
.

∎

We then have all of the machinery necessary to conclude our proof of the finite model property.

Proof.

By the premise,
\mathcal{K}_{e}\models e=f
, i.e.,

f\leqq\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(f)}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor=\sum_{R\in\widehat{h_{e}}(e)}\lfloor A_{e}[R]\rfloor\leqq e

Representation theory

The lemmas in
Section

3
suggest another question: is it possible to represent every (finite or star-continuous) KA using relations?
In other words, is it the case that every such KA is a subalgebra of a relational KA?
Such a result would amount to a
representation theorem
for KAs, along the same lines as Cayley’s representation theorem for groups.
Representation theory is a well established branch of algebra, but little seems to be known about it in the setting of KA beyond an investigation by Kozen
[
44
]
, who showed that KAs
with tests
that satisfy certain additional properties can be represented using relations.
We hope to investigate representations of KAs in future work.

Concurrent Kleene Algebra

Concurrent Kleene Algebra
(CKA) was proposed to expand the reasoning power of KA to include programs with concurrency
[
45
]
.
Several variants of CKA enjoy completeness properties, including bi-Kleene algebra (BKA)
[
46
]
, which includes parallel composition and its corresponding analogue to the Kleene star, and weak CKA
[
47
,
48
]
, which does not include the parallel Kleene star but does include the
exchange law
, which allows programs to be interleaved.

Completeness for the full calculus of CKA, including both the parallel star operator and the exchange law, remains an open question, and it is known that existing techniques do not generalize to that setting
[
48
]
.
Perhaps the approach in this article could provide a different route that does generalize to CKA; this would require giving an algebraic angle an operational model for CKA
[
49
,
50
,
51
]
.

Guarded Kleene Algebra with Tests

The fragment of KAT where non-deterministic choice and loops are guarded by tests, called Guarded Kleene Algebra with Tests (GKAT)
[
52
,
53
]
, has favorable complexity properties.
Moreover, GKAT admits a set of axioms that are complete w.r.t. its language (resp. relational, probabilistic) model, but this set is infinite as a result of an axiom scheme.

We wonder whether the techniques discussed here could be applied to arrive at a more satisfactory completeness result.
To start answering this question, one would first have to devise an analogue to transformation automata and monoids for GKAT.
