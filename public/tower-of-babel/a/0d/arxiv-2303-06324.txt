Abstract
. Various distributed deep neural network (DNN) training technologies lead to increasingly complicated use of collective communications on GPU. The deadlock-prone collectives on GPU force researchers to guarantee that collectives are enqueued in a consistent order on each GPU to prevent deadlocks. In complex distributed DNN training scenarios, manual hardcoding is the only practical way for deadlock prevention, which poses significant challenges to the development of artificial intelligence. This paper presents OCCL , which is, to the best of our knowledge, the first deadlock-free collective communication library for GPU supporting dynamic decentralized preemption and gang-scheduling for collectives. Leveraging the preemption opportunity of collectives on GPU, OCCL dynamically preempts collectives in a decentralized way via the deadlock-free collective execution framework and allows dynamic decentralized gang-scheduling via the stickiness adjustment scheme . With the help of OCCL , researchers no longer have to struggle to get all GPUs to launch collectives in a consistent order to prevent deadlocks. We implement OCCL with several optimizations and integrate OCCL with a distributed deep learning framework OneFlow. Experimental results demonstrate that OCCL achieves comparable or better latency and bandwidth for collectives compared to NCCL, the state-of-the-art. When used in distributed DNN training, OCCL can improve the peak training throughput by up to 78% compared to statically sequenced NCCL, while introducing overheads of less than 6.5% across various distributed DNN training approaches.

.
This entails distributed DNN training, which includes various techniques such as data parallelism

)
, tensor parallelism
(

et al. 2021
;

, and pipeline parallelism

)

Collective communication plays a critical role in distributed DNN training.

Widely used collectives on GPU are deadlock-prone
(
NVIDIA 2023d
)
because preemption is ill-supported on GPUs and collectives work in a resource-holding and busy-looping way on GPUs.
As a result, the only chance to prevent collective-related deadlocks in distributed DNN training is to guarantee that all collectives are invoked in a consistent order on each of the GPUs.

As distributed DNN training technology continues to advance, the use of collectives becomes increasingly complex.
Unfortunately, the deadlock-prone nature of collectives results in a rigid way of orchestrating them.
While data parallelism uses a dynamic central coordinator
(
Sergeev and
Del Balso 2018
)
to sort all collectives during runtime, this approach fails to handle the concurrent requests of collectives related to different parts of the DNN and data microbatches
(
Huang et al. 2019
;
Narayanan et al. 2019
)
in complicated parallel DNN training techniques.
To address this, some widely used methods for arranging collectives in distributed DNN training rely on manual orchestration, which can be labor-intensive and error-prone.
OneFlow’s compiler automatically analyzes all collectives to be used in distributed DNN training.
In relatively simple scenarios, OneFlow statically sorts all the collectives and invokes them accordingly during runtime.
However, OneFlow has to turn to the manual hardcoding scheme when pipeline parallelism is applied.

We argue that breaking the deadlock-prone nature of collectives on GPU by supporting preemption is necessary in the context of artificial intelligence development.
Currently, the manual hardcoding scheme is the only viable solution for complicated distributed DNN training scenarios.
When faced with the training of increasingly complex and irregular neural networks, such as multimodal multitasking neural networks
(
Barham et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2021
)
, depending solely on manual hardcoding presents significant obstacles to the advancement of artificial intelligence, because this requires the researchers to manually program the execution flow of each GPU and ensure their precise coordination at runtime, which is more and more arduous.

This paper figures out the preemption opportunity of collectives on GPU and presents
OCCL

(OneFlow Collective Communication Library)
, a novel deadlock-free collective communication library for GPU that supports dynamic decentralized preemption and gang-scheduling of collectives.

OCCL
preempts collectives based on the observation that the commonly used collectives on GPU are all composed of a subset of the same group of
primitives

(
NVIDIA 2023c
;
Cowan et al. 2023
)
.
A primitive is further a fusion of one or more basic actions that manipulate four buffers.
Specifically, each primitive interacts with at least one of the buffers in the
send/recv connector
that is used to transfer data between peer GPUs.
Once a primitive of a collective commits a write or read to the
send/recv connector
, respectively, the write or read can be visible to the corresponding primitive on the peer GPUs even if the collective is preempted.

OCCL
leverages such visibility to support dynamically preempting collectives in a decentralized way via the
deadlock-free collective execution framework (DFCE-framework)
and allow dynamic decentralized gang-scheduling via the
stickiness adjustment scheme
.

The core of
OCCL
’s DFCE-framework is the
daemon kernel
.
The execution of primitive sequences of collectives in the daemon kernel occurs in a two-phase blocking manner
(
Feitelson and
Rudolph 1992
;
Ousterhout
et al. 1982
)
, wherein primitives exceeding the assigned spin threshold are preempted.
The daemon kernel guarantees the preemption and recovery are correct for collectives by carefully maintaining their context.
To handle the submissions of collectives’ requests, the DFCE-framework employs a
submission queue (SQ)
, while the
completion queue (CQ)
together with user-defined callbacks is responsible for notifications of collectives’ completion.
Furthermore, the daemon kernel may voluntarily quit to yield limited GPU resources to other tasks and to prevent deadlocks related to GPU synchronization operations.
The DFCE-framework ensures that all invoked collectives are completed by launching the daemon kernel in an event-driven manner.

The stickiness adjustment scheme of
OCCL
supports assigning priority to collectives and makes the GPUs dynamically coordinate with each other in a decentralized way to achieve gang-scheduling, thereby boosting performance.
According to Feitelson et al.
(
Feitelson and
Rudolph 1992
)
, gang-scheduling, along with busy-looping, is beneficial to the performance of collectives because each collective is composed of a sequence of fine-grain primitives.
The stickiness of a collective in
OCCL
indicates the degree to which the daemon kernel is willing to wait for the collective to make progress.

OCCL
’s unified stickiness adjustment mechanism controls the stickiness of all collectives and supports various policies.

OCCL
’s dynamic decentralized preemption and gang-scheduling support of collectives allows collectives to be invoked in an arbitrary order among different GPUs, relieving the burden of orchestrating collectives in distributed DNN training.
OCCL enables researchers to bypass the challenge of ensuring that all GPUs initiate collectives in a consistent order to prevent deadlocks, which lowers the barrier to making contributions to large-scale DNN models’ innovation.
In this sense, sequencing collectives on each of the GPUs can be seen as a performance-boosting policy, rather than the strict deadlock-preventing requirement.
Overall,
OCCL
allows researchers to focus on the innovation of large-scale DNN models, rather than the low-level coordination of collectives.

We implement
OCCL
based on NVIDIA GPUs and tailor daemon kernel to the block-thread programming model of CUDA
(
NVIDIA 2023a
)
.
To minimize overheads, we optimize context switching and reduce the latency of writing completion queue entries (CQEs) to CQ.
We can seamlessly integrate
OCCL
with various distributed deep learning frameworks by invoking its APIs properly when the deep learning framework’s collective invoker is able to provide a callback to notify itself of the collective’s completion.

The experiments show that
OCCL
attains comparable or better latency and bandwidth for collectives compared to NCCL
(
NVIDIA 2023c
)
, the SOTA collective communication library for NVIDIA GPUs. Moreover, when applied in distributed DNN training,
OCCL
can boost the peak training throughput by up to 78% compared to statically sequenced NCCL, while adding less than 6.5% overheads across different distributed DNN training techniques.
In future work, we plan to extensively explore the potential of leveraging the dynamic and decentralized preemption and gang-scheduling feature of
OCCL
for collectives, with the aim of devising and enhancing novel distributed DNN training methods.

2.2.
Limits of Current Deadlock-preventing Methods in Distributed DNN Training

As currently used collectives on GPU intrinsically meet three of the four deadlock conditions, preventing circular waiting is the only way to prevent collective-related deadlocks in distributed DNN training.
To achieve this, existing collective orchestration technologies in distributed DNN training include using a dynamic central coordinator, carefully hardcoding all collectives, and statically sequencing or arranging collectives during compilation, all of which are essentially centralized sequencing.

The
dynamic central coordinator
is an effective approach for data-parallel distributed DNN training.
Data parallelism, which is the sole requirement for distributed DNN training for a long time, only needs to synchronize the gradients of DNN’s different layers from every GPU in the backward pass.
Ring all-reduce
(
Gibiansky 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
)
outperforms parameter server
(
Li et al. 2014
)
in data parallelism due to its efficient use of each GPU’s bandwidth.
Horovod
(
Sergeev and
Del Balso 2018
)
gang-schedules the all-reduce collectives via a dynamic central coordinator.
The coordinator gathers the readiness of collectives from each GPU during runtime and then broadcasts a list indicating the collectives that are ready on all the GPUs, allowing them to start the collectives in the responding list according to the list order.

For example, GPU-1 in Fig.

To make things worse, these collectives can be fired concurrently.

Widely used solutions arrange collectives via
careful manual hardcoding

(
Shoeybi et al. 2019
;
Narayanan et al. 2021
;
Rasley
et al. 2020
)
.
This approach eliminates the need for additional coordination mechanisms at runtime and ensures all the GPUs execute according to the hardcoded operation sequence.
However, this hardcoding process requires researchers not only to design the model structure but also to insert the appropriate collectives in the correct locations and orchestrate them to prevent deadlocks, significantly increasing the threshold for contributing to innovation in artificial intelligence.

OneFlow
(
Yuan et al. 2021
)

statically sequences or arranges collectives
in distributed DNN training.
The
SBP
(split, broadcast, and partial-value) concept and the
boxing
mechanism proposed by OneFlow automatically analyze and generate all collectives needed for distributed DNN training.
OneFlow creates a task graph for each GPU with computing and communication tasks as nodes and data dependencies as edges.
Prior to execution, OneFlow sorts all collectives of each GPU according to the topological order on the graph.
During runtime, each GPU can only start the next collective in sequence after the previous one has begun.
However, each GPU no longer traverses the task graph from beginning to end repeatedly in pipeline parallelism, but cycles different parts of the task graph unevenly according to the specific pipeline schedule, which causes the collective sequence obtained according to the topological order on the task graph valueless.
OneFlow handles pipeline parallelism by statically arranging all the generated collectives according to the specific pipeline-parallel schedule with its compiler, which is actually the same as the aforementioned manual hardcoding way.

The only viable way to prevent circular waiting of collectives on GPU is centralized sequencing.
Horovod proposes a dynamic centralized method.
Hardcoding and static sequencing or arranging are essentially centralized sequencing ahead of runtime.
Despite the lack of details, Pathways
(
Barham et al. 2022
)
also supports the idea that a centralized controller is necessary to prevent collective-related deadlocks.

We can further conclude from the above analysis that the hand-designed collective sequencing scheme is in fact the only practical and efficient solution for hybrid-parallel distributed DNN training with pipeline parallelism, either in a hardcoding way or in a static-arranging way.
The deadlock-prone nature of collectives on GPU forces researchers to manually orchestrate all the collectives in complex distributed training scenarios, increasing the difficulty of designing and training more complex DNN models.
Recent work in literature points to a trend toward multimodal multitasking in neural networks
(
Barham et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2021
)
, which brings more complexity than pipeline parallelism does.
While pipeline parallelism still has a certain degree of symmetry, multimodal multitasking models are more irregular, increasing the difficulty of manually programming the execution flow of each GPU, let alone further optimization based on the dynamic characteristics during runtime.

We argue that breaking the deadlock-prone nature of collectives on GPU is necessary for advancing artificial intelligence.
Resources are inherently mutually exclusive, and collectives on GPU work in the resource-holding busy-looping way for performance reasons
(
Feitelson and
Rudolph 1992
)
.
By finding a way to break the no-preemption condition of deadlock for collectives on GPU, we can pave the way for facilitating the exploration of more complex and diverse DNN architectures.

2.3.
The Preemption Opportunity of Collectives on GPU

The preemption opportunity of commonly used collectives on GPU (i.e., all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter, reduce, and broadcast) comes from the fact that they are all composed of a subset of the same group of
primitives

(
NVIDIA 2023c
;
Cowan et al. 2023
)
.
In different implementations of each collective, GPUs are organized into different logical topologies, and each GPU is assigned a primitive sequence according to its position in this logical topology.
A GPU executes a collective by executing its primitive sequence a certain number of times to process all the data chunks.
Every primitive is a fusion of one or more basic actions, i.e.,
send
,
recv
,
reduce
, and
copy
, which describe the basic operations of the GPU on the four kinds of buffers used in collectives as demonstrated in Fig.
3
.

Send/Recv buffers
are local buffers for input and output.

Send/Recv connectors
contain lock-free ring buffers used for inter-GPU data transfer.
The buffer in the
send connector
of a GPU is exactly the buffer in the
recv connector
of its downstream GPU

When two peer GPUs are located in different machines, buffers in the
send connector
and the

can be physically different. However, data transfer between them is managed transparently via network protocols.
.
The
send
action pushes data to the
send connector
, and the
recv
action grabs data from the
recv connector
.
The
directSend
action pushes data directly to the
recv buffer
of the peer GPU, and
directRecv
is a passive action that waits for data to be written in the

by the peer GPU.
The
directSend/directRecv
actions rely on hardware support and are logically the same as
send/recv
.
The

action reduces data from the

and the

with a specified reducing function.
The
copy
action puts data into the
recv buffer
.
For example, the primitive
recvReduceCopySend
, fusing all four actions, grabs data from the
recv connector
, reduces the grabbed data with the data from the
send buffer
, copies the result to the
recv buffer
, and pushes the result to the
send connector
, while the primitive
recvReduceSend
pushes the reduced result to the
send connector
without writing the
recv buffer
.
Every primitive contains at least one of
send
and
recv
actions.
A primitive needs to wait for the
send connector
to be writable or the
recv connector
to be readable in a busy-looping way according to whether it contains the
send
or
recv
action.
We can preempt a primitive if it waits for too long.

Collectives on GPU can be dynamically preempted in a decentralized manner, obviating the need to synchronously terminate and restore a collective on every GPU.
Preempting a collective is in fact preempting one of its primitives.
To do so, we need to save and restore the context that includes the current data chunk ID, the preempted primitive’s ID, and the ID of the data slice that the preempted primitive is processing inside the chunk.
By saving and restoring this context properly, we can terminate a collective at any time and restart it from where it stopped.
Note that the status of the
send/recv connectors
is not included in the context.
The reason is that once a GPU commits a write successfully to the
send connector
on behalf of a collective’s primitive, even if the collective is preempted on this GPU, the written data can still be visible to the peer GPU, and the
recv
action of the corresponding collective’s primitive can still succeed.
The same holds for the
recv connector
and the
send
action on the peer GPU.

By enabling preemption of collectives on GPU, gang-scheduling every collective and sequencing collectives on every GPU move from a strict deadlock-preventing requirement to a performance-improving policy.
As a result, dynamic and decentralized orchestration of collectives in distributed DNN training becomes possible, creating a more flexible environment for designing and training complex models.

3.1.
The
Deadlock-free Collective Execution Framework
of
OCCL

As illustrated in Fig.
4
, the DFCE-framework of
OCCL
consists of two main parts: the CPU part and the GPU part, with a long-running
daemon kernel
as the core component.
The daemon kernel serves as an indirection layer between the invokers of collectives and the primitives, which are the primary building blocks of collectives.
The I/O (input/output) issues between the daemon kernel and the collective invokers are handled via a
submission queue (SQ)
and a
completion queue (CQ)
.
The daemon kernel voluntarily quits to yield GPU resources and prevent GPU-synchronization-operation-related deadlocks as Fig.
1(c)
demonstrates.
The DFCE-framework starts the daemon kernel in an event-driven manner to ensure that all the invoked collectives complete in the daemon kernel.

3.1.1.
Collective Preemption in the Daemon Kernel

The daemon kernel supports dynamic decentralized preemption of collectives on GPUs.
The fact that all the commonly used collectives on GPU consist of a subset of the same group of primitives enable the daemon kernel to handle all the invoked collectives.
Collectives are maintained in a
task queue
by the daemon kernel in the shared memory.
The daemon kernel traverses the task queue and executes the primitive sequences of the collectives in a two-phase blocking manner
(
Feitelson and
Rudolph 1992
;
Ousterhout
et al. 1982
)
, preempting stuck collectives in a decentralized manner dynamically.

The daemon kernel assigns
spin thresholds
to primitives of a collective to judge whether the collective is stuck.
As mentioned in Sec.
2.3
, every primitive needs to wait for at least one of the
recv connector
and
send connector
to be ready by busy-looping.
The daemon kernel limits the spinning rounds of each primitive.
When the primitive’s number of spins exceeds the given threshold, the daemon kernel determines that the primitive is stuck and preempts the corresponding collective.
The context of the preempted collective is saved in the
collective context buffer
in the global memory, and the daemon kernel loads the context of another collective to execute into the shared memory
2
2

2

On the GPU, the no-conflict access latency of the shared memory is about 100 times lower than the uncached access latency of the global memory, and the bandwidth of the shared memory is one to two orders of magnitude higher than that of the global memory. However, the capacity of shared memory is typically lower by six orders of magnitude than that of global memory (tens of KB vs tens of GB)
(
Jia
et al. 2019
)
.
.

The context of a collective is composed of the
dynamic context
and the
static context
.
The dynamic context of a collective contains the states that will change during collective execution, such as the current data chunk ID, the preempted primitive’s ID, and the data slice ID inside the chunk as mentioned in Sec.
2.3
.
The static context of a collective contains the collective’s constant configuration information, such as the addresses of the local and peer buffers illustrated in Fig.
3
, and the meta information of the collective, which further consists of the number of GPUs participating in the collective, the rank of the current GPU in the participating GPUs, and the composition of the collective’s primitive sequence, etc.

Given that distributed DNN training repeatedly performs the same computations and communications to update model parameters,
OCCL
registers collectives to be used on each GPU and prepares their meta information as well as collective context buffer slots before executing them.
Every registered collective is assigned a unique ID.

OCCL
supports registering new collectives dynamically during runtime, which allows for greater flexibility in designing distributed DNN training methods.

The daemon kernel correctly preempts and restores collectives in a dynamic and decentralized manner because it ensures the static context of uncompleted preempted collectives is not corrupted, and in particular, the connector therein is not used by other collectives.
Therefore, the preempted collective’s last committed write and read to the
send connector
and
recv connector
, respectively, are still visible to the corresponding collectives on the peer GPUs, enabling them to progress.
The context-switching method of the daemon kernel guarantees the preempted collective restarts exactly from where it stopped earlier, without under-transmitting or re-transmitting data.
Before executing a collective, the daemon kernel loads both its dynamic context and static context into shared memory, and it only saves the collective’s dynamic context back to global memory when it is preempted, as the static context does not change during collective execution.
When the preempted collective is restored, it locates the primitive to execute and the data slice to process via the loaded dynamic context, and it can also find out the committed read or write in the
connectors
from the peer GPUs, if there are any.
When a collective completes, its static context can change.
For example, the addresses of the
send buffer
and
recv buffer
passed in by the invoker among multiple calls may be different, and the daemon kernel can assign the
connectors
recycled from done collectives to other collectives.

3.1.2.
Submitting Requests and Notifying Completions via SQ & CQ

The DFCE-framework leverages an SQ and a CQ to handle the submission of collectives’ requests to GPU and the notification of collectives’ completion to CPU, respectively.
As a heterogeneous accelerator, the GPU only processes specific tasks the CPU dispatches and has no knowledge of the entire program.
Current implementations of collectives handle collective requests at the granularity of a kernel, which is not suitable for
OCCL
.
The long-running daemon kernel fetches new collective requests from the SQ and records it in the task queue.
When the primitive sequence of a collective executes a predetermined number of times, the collective successfully completes.
The daemon kernel removes the complete collective’s record from its task queue and inserts an entry into the CQ to inform the invoker on the CPU.
SQ and CQ reside in page-locked host memory that is accessible to both the GPU and CPU.

Invokers on CPU submit a new collective request by inserting a
submission queue entry (SQE)
into the SQ, which includes the ID of the requested collective as well as the addresses of the
send/recv buffer
.
The daemon kernel checks the SQ and updates the static context of the collective if a new request for it is received.
When no more collectives are needed on a GPU by the program, the DFCE-framework inserts an
Exiting SQE
into the SQ, and the daemon kernel exits after fetching the Exiting SQE.

The daemon kernel inserts a
completion queue entry (CQE)
into the CQ when a collective completes.
On the CPU side, a busy-looping
poller
periodically checks the CQ.
When the invoker submits a request for a collective, it also provides a
callback
function that is bound to the collective by the DFCE-framework in the
callback map
.
Once the poller finds a CQE, it executes the corresponding callback that notifies the invoker of the collective’s completion in an arbitrary user-defined way.

3.1.3.
Daemon Kernel’s Voluntary Quitting & Event-driven Starting

The daemon kernel voluntarily quits if, for a certain length of time, the task queue remains empty or collectives in the task queue cannot progress while no new SQEs come.

OCCL
trade-offs between yielding limited GPU resources to other tasks and tolerating the extra overheads of quitting and restarting the daemon kernel by adjusting how easily the daemon kernel voluntarily quits.
Collectives are not evenly used among all stages in distributed DNN training; for example, collectives are only used during the backward pass in data parallelism.
The daemon kernel also quits voluntarily to prevent the GPU-synchronization-operation-related deadlocks as Fig.
1(c)
demonstrates.
After the daemon kernel quits, the GPU synchronization operation can complete, allowing the earlier stuck collectives to proceed.
It is important to note that the context of the stuck collectives is already saved in the global memory when preempted, so quitting and restarting the daemon kernel do not corrupt their context.

The DFCE-framework restarts the daemon kernel when it detects new SQEs inserted into the SQ or when it finds out that the number of CQEs generated by the daemon kernel is smaller than the number of SQEs the daemon kernel has received.
In fact, the initial starting of the daemon kernel is also triggered by the first inserted SQE.

3.1.4.
Summary

The daemon kernel’s preemption support for collectives and voluntary quitting mechanism work together to prevent deadlocks in all three scenarios demonstrated in Fig.
1
.
This means that with
OCCL
, researchers no longer need to struggle to ensure that all GPUs launch collectives in a consistent order to prevent deadlocks.

3.2.
The
Stickiness Adjustment Scheme
of
OCCL

The
stickiness
of a collective indicates the daemon kernel’s willingness to wait for the collective to make progress.
The stickiness of a collective is reflected in two main aspects: its position in the task queue and the spin thresholds assigned to its primitives.

Adjusting the stickiness of collectives is a dynamic decentralized method to coordinate the execution order and the preference for collectives among peer GPUs.
A unified stickiness adjustment mechanism controls the above two aspects independently on each GPU and supports various policies.

OCCL
ensures that all the participating GPUs adopt the same stickiness adjustment policy, which is further divided into an order adjusting policy and a spin threshold adjusting policy.
The order adjusting policy affects the frequency of fetching SQEs from the SQ and the ordering of collectives in the task queue according to the user-specified priority.
The spin threshold adjusting policy allows each GPU to dynamically tend to execute the same collective in a decentralized manner.

The order adjusting policy of
OCCL
enables the GPUs to execute collectives according to user-defined priority.
When collectives are not assigned any specific priority,
OCCL
adopts the FIFO-ordering policy, otherwise, it adopts a priority-based ordering policy.

\bullet

FIFO Ordering.

This policy is designed to empty the task queue as soon as possible.
When taking this policy, the daemon kernel tries to fetch an SQE from the SQ when the task queue is empty or all the collectives currently in the task queue cannot progress for a long time.
The newly added collective from the SQ is put at the end of the task queue.

\bullet

Priority-based Ordering.

Prior work assigns higher priority to collectives coming later to overlap communication and computation in data parallelism
(
Peng et al. 2019
;
Bao
et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
)
.

OCCL
can support this kind of priority by checking the SQ more frequently and putting the newly added collective at the front of the task queue.

The default spin threshold adjusting policy of
OCCL
allows individual GPUs to execute collectives in a decentralized gang-scheduling manner dynamically, even when faced with simultaneously arriving collective requests.
This policy instructs the daemon kernel to assign an initial spin threshold to the primitives of the collective at the task queue’s front that is higher than all other collectives.
The initial spin threshold assigned to each subsequent collective is decremented based on its position in the task queue.
During execution, if the daemon kernel detects that a primitive of a collective executes successfully, it increases the spin threshold of the succeeding primitives of that collective, which increases the probability that all GPUs execute or wait for the same collective simultaneously, resulting in de facto gang-scheduling.

OCCL
’s stickiness adjustment scheme avoids the
thrashing
circumstance wherein collectives are preempted frequently and progress slowly.
Although the visibility of the committed read/write to the
connectors
enables
OCCL
to support preemption, it only ensures that the primitives, which are the primary cells of collectives, can move forward in the smallest step.
When multiple collectives execute on a group of GPUs, the worst-case scenario is that every collective on a GPU can only process one data slice before it gets preempted because no other GPUs execute the same collective before it exceeds its spin threshold.
However, by adopting the same order adjusting policy and spin threshold adjusting policy, each GPU can dynamically coordinate with the others in a decentralized manner and achieve gang-scheduling as much as possible, which prevents the thrashing circumstance.

4.
Implementation and Optimizations

Implementation Details of the Daemon Kernel

We implement the daemon kernel to suit the block-thread programming model of CUDA
(
NVIDIA 2023a
)
.
In this model, a CUDA kernel comprises multiple parallel threads that execute the same code.
The threads are grouped into blocks and a block is the logical scheduling unit of CUDA.
All threads of a block are expected to reside on the same streaming multiprocessor (SM) core and have access to the core’s limited shared memory.
Equally-shaped blocks further form a grid.
Each collective is assigned a specific grid size and a specific block size.
The daemon kernel is launched with the largest grid size and largest block size of all collectives.

Threads of the same block can synchronize with each other easily, whereas threads in different blocks typically run asynchronously.
When the daemon kernel execute collectives that require different numbers of blocks simultaneously, the block with a high index can execute a different collective than the block with a low index.
As a result, different blocks on a GPU can achieve dynamic decentralized gang-scheduling for different collectives with the corresponding blocks on peer GPUs.
The number of active threads executing a collective’s primitives inside a block depends on the specific block size assigned to the collective and the daemon kernel makes the extra threads wait.
Each block independently decides to voluntarily quit based on the conditions mentioned in Sec.
3.1.3
.
All blocks are launched jointly on each GPU.

The daemon kernel’s reads to SQ and writes to CQ are also implemented for the situation where blocks execute asynchronously.
The SQ is implemented as a single-producer-multi-consumer (SPMC) ring buffer.
Only one CPU thread is permitted to write an SQE into the SQ at a time.
All blocks of the daemon kernel need to read the SQE.
Every time a block reads a new SQE, it increases a counter inside the SQE atomically.
Once a block finds that the increased counter of an SQE is equal to the grid size of the daemon kernel, it frees the corresponding SQ slot for future writes from the CPU.
After reading a new SQE, a block executes the involved collective only when it finds the grid size assigned to the collective is not lower than its block index.
The CQ is implemented as a multi-producer-single-consumer (MPSC) ring buffer.
Only one poller thread on the CPU reads CQEs from the CQ.
The daemon kernel maintains a completion counter for each collective in the global memory.
Every time a block completes its part of a collective, it increases the collective’s completion counter atomically.
Once a block finds that the increased completion counter of a collective is equal to the grid size assigned to the collective, the block writes the CQE into the CQ and resets the collective’s completion counter.
Multiple blocks can write CQEs into the CQ for different complete collectives concurrently.

Optimizing CQ.

By leveraging the 64-bit atomic operation provided by CUDA, we reduce the number of reads and writes related to the host memory when writing a CQE to the CQ and thus reduce the CQE-writing latency.
The
vanilla ring-buffer-based CQ
uses at least five reads/writes related to the host memory to prevent multiple blocks from inserting CQEs into the same CQ slot for different collectives simultaneously, which may otherwise make the poller observe a discontinuously growing
tail
of the CQ.
The vanilla ring-buffer-based CQ also requires inserting a memory fence between writing the CQE and updating the
tail
to guarantee memory consistency.
The
optimized ring-buffer-based CQ
takes exactly four host-memory-related operations without fences by wrapping the ID of the complete collective and the current
tail
in a 64-bit word that can be transferred in a single atomic write.
The poller determines whether a CQE is valid by comparing the
head
and the
tail
in the bitmap.
We further implement an
optimized CQ
that only takes a single
atomicCAS_system
operation at least when writing a CQE, which abandons the ring buffer semantic.
The optimized CQ is based on the observation that the CQE only carries the ID of the complete collective.
A block writes the ID atomically into a writable slot in the CQ.
The poller traverses the CQ and checks whether a slot contains a valid collective ID and marks the slot to be writable after reading the valid collective ID.
Currently, we implement the SQ as a vanilla ring buffer because an SQE includes information more than a 64-bit word can carry.

Reducing the Overheads of Context Switching.

We adopt three main methods to reduce the context-switching overheads.
❶ The daemon kernel loads and saves the collective context in parallel by using as many threads as possible.
We wrap the dynamic and static context of a collective into 16-byte aligned
struct
to leverage the 16-byte
load/store
instructions in each thread.
❷ The daemon kernel uses multiple buffers in the shared memory to load the collective context, which are maintained in a direct-mapped-cache manner, reducing loading times of a preempted collective when its context slot is not corrupted.
❸ The daemon kernel employs a lazy-saving strategy that only saves the dynamic context of a collective that has progressed before getting preempted.
The daemon kernel does not need to save the dynamic context of an collective that does not make progress because nothing changes therein.

Integrating
OCCL
with Deep Learning Frameworks.

We can integrate
OCCL
with deep learning frameworks that support distributed training by invoking
OCCL
’s registering APIs once before invoking
OCCL
-based collectives.
Besides, the collective’s invoker of the deep learning framework needs to provide a callback to notify itself of the completion of the collective.

We currently extend OneFlow to use
OCCL
-based collectives and implement an optional dynamic decentralized negotiation scheme for better performance.
❶ As discussed in Sec.
2.2
, OneFlow automatically generates all needed collectives for distributed DNN training with the help of the SBP concept and the boxing mechanism.
We extend OneFlow to generate
OCCL
-based collective task nodes in the task graph, and the callback sends a message to notify the collective’s completion via OneFlow’s
actor
-based execution model.
❷ We implement an optional dynamic decentralized negotiation scheme in OneFlow.
When enabled, this negotiation scheme ensures that all the task nodes bound to the same collective on different GPUs have already prepared the tensor for the collective before inserting the corresponding SQE.
This scheme makes it easier for the daemon kernel’s blocks with the same block index on different GPUs to reach gang-scheduling for each collective, since the SQEs for a particular collective are inserted into the SQ at a similar moment on different GPUs.

Related Work

Collective Scheduling.

Some collective scheduling approaches aim to overlap communication and computation.
Poseidon
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
, TicTac
(
Hashemi
et al. 2019
)
, P3
(
Jayarajan et al. 2019
)
are proposed in the context of parameter-server-based
(
Li et al. 2014
)
data parallelism and the core insights are then applied to all-reduce-based data parallelism.
Poseidon
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
proposes wait-free backpropagation (WFBP) that synchronizes gradients after each layer rather than the whole iteration.
TicTac
(
Hashemi
et al. 2019
)
adjusts the order of gradient synchronization so that the next iteration can launch earlier.
P3
(
Jayarajan et al. 2019
)
, or priority-based parameter propagation, slices parameters into a finer granularity, and the synchronization of parameters of higher-priority layers can preempt that of lower-priority layers.
Bytescheduler
(
Peng et al. 2019
)
applies a similar idea to both parameter server and all-reduce architectures, and introduces an approach to auto-tuning slice size.
PACE
(
Bao
et al. 2020
)
preemptively schedules all-reduce tensors based on the directed acyclic graph (DAG) of DNN.
PyTorch Distributed
(
Li et al. 2020
)
overlaps computation with communication by triggering all-reduce earlier than the end of the local backward pass in PyTorch
(
Paszke
et al. 2019
)
.
CoCoNet
(
Jangda et al. 2022
)
fuses split all-reduce with the computing operation that consumes the result of all-reduce, and overlaps the fused kernels.

OCCL
can support the above scheduling policies via stickiness adjustment scheme as discussed in Sec.
3
.

Other collective scheduling techniques centrally sequence collectives for every GPU to prevent deadlock.
Horovod
(
Sergeev and
Del Balso 2018
)
provides a plugin for data parallelism that manages all-reduce through a centralized coordinator at runtime.
Widely used tensor-parallel
(
Shoeybi et al. 2019
)
and pipeline-parallel
(
Huang et al. 2019
;
Narayanan et al. 2019
;
Narayanan et al. 2021
)
techniques are usually implemented by careful manual hardcoding in PyTorch
(
Paszke
et al. 2019
)
, specifying the start time for every used collective.
Pathways
(
Barham et al. 2022
)
applies gang-scheduling in different device islands and dispatches global commands via a centralized controller to prevent deadlocks.
OneFlow
(
Yuan et al. 2021
)
statically sequences or arranges all the collectives used in different parallel distributed DNN training techniques, and guarantees each GPU starts collectives in the same order at runtime.

OCCL
achieves dynamic gang-scheduling in a decentralized way via the preemption support and stickiness adjustment scheme.

Collective Optimization.

Blueconnect
(
Cho
et al. 2019
)
decomposes an all-reduce operation to many parallelizable reduce-scatter and all-gather operations to adapt to network topology.
BLink
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
proposes a heuristic spanning-tree packing algorithm to optimize communication primitives.
PLink
(
Luo et al. 2020
)
exploit the hierarchical network topology to construct a logical topology for collectives.
SCCL
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
solves an integer programming encoding to achieve the Pareto-frontier of latency- and bandwidth-optimal algorithms in a single machine.
TACCL
(
Shah et al. 2021
)
synthesizes collective algorithms for multi-node topologies utilizing bandwidth and latency probes.
MSCCLang
(
Cowan et al. 2023
)
provides a domain-specific language describing the chunk-oriented dataflow of collectives. MSCCLang programs are then compiled and optimized to run on GPUs.
Existing collective optimization techniques often exploit underlying physical topology to adjust data segmentation, chunk routing, aggregation hierarchy, etc., which are orthogonal and complementary to
OCCL
.

GPU Preemption.

NVIDIA claims that their GPUs have supported preemption since Pascal architecture
(
NVIDIA 2016
)
, but there is no publicly accessible API.
Prior work in literature aims to support preemption for GPU to lower the end-to-end latency of high-priority tasks.
Hardware solutions
(
Tanasić et al. 2014
;
Lin
et al. 2016
;
Park
et al. 2015
)
enhance the hardware to support preemption handling context switch such as registers, shared memory, barrier states, etc. during runtime.
Software methods
(
Chen
et al. 2017
;
Wu
et al. 2017
;
Zhou
et al. 2015
;
Lee et al. 2018
;
Lee
et al. 2021
;
Han
et al. 2022
)
can be applied directly on commodity GPUs.
Wait-based preemption approaches
(
Chen
et al. 2017
;
Wu
et al. 2017
;
Zhou
et al. 2015
)
modify user kernels to insert scheduling points so that user kernels quit more frequently and expose more scheduling opportunities.
Lee et al.
(
Lee et al. 2018
;
Lee
et al. 2021
)
and REEF
(
Han
et al. 2022
)
kill the preempted kernel directly to decrease scheduling delay.
Idempotent kernels can be aborted and then restarted without affecting correctness
(
Lee
et al. 2021
;
Han
et al. 2022
)
, while preempted non-idempotent kernels are rolled back and then relaunched
(
Lee et al. 2018
;
Lee
et al. 2021
)
.
The deadlock-free collective execution framework of
OCCL
supports preemption without hardware modification.
Wait-based preemption still cannot prevent deadlocks. Collectives are non-idempotent and rolling back collectives introduces considerable overheads and complicated synchronization issues among GPUs.
Our preemption approach is dedicated to collective communications and handles context switching within the daemon kernel.

7.
Conclusion

This paper presents
OCCL
, a novel deadlock-free collective communication library for GPU supporting dynamic decentralized preemption and gang-scheduling of collectives.

OCCL
dynamically preempts collectives in a decentralized way via the
deadlock-free collective execution framework
and allows dynamic decentralized gang-scheduling via the
stickiness adjustment scheme
.
With the help of
OCCL
, researchers no longer have to struggle to ensure that all GPUs launch collectives in a consistent order to prevent deadlocks.
We implement
OCCL
with several optimizations and integrate
OCCL
with a distributed deep learning framework OneFlow.
Our experimental evaluation shows that
OCCL
incurs acceptable overheads while achieving performance improvements in certain scenarios compared to the SOTA collective communication library NCCL.
