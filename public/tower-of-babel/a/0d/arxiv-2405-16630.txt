Bayesian Inference with Deep Weakly Nonlinear Networks
Boris Hanin, Alexander Zlokapa * Princeton ORFE

Abstract
We show at a physics level of rigor that Bayesian inference with a fully connected neural network and a shaped nonlinearity of the form ϕ ⁡ ( t ) = t + ψ ​ t 3 / L \phi(t)=t+\psi t^{3}/L is (perturbatively) solvable in the regime where the number of training datapoints ( P P ), the input dimension ( N 0 N_{0} ), the network’s hidden layer widths ( N N ), and the network depth ( L L ) are simultaneously large. Our results hold with weak assumptions on the data model; the main constraint is that P < N 0 P<N_{0} . We provide techniques to compute the model evidence and posterior to arbitrary order in 1 / N 1/N and at arbitrary temperature. We report the following results from the first-order computation. • When the width N N is much larger than the depth L L and training set size P P , neural network Bayesian inference coincides with Bayesian inference using a kernel. The value of ψ \psi determines the curvature of a sphere, hyperbola, or plane into which the training data is implicitly embedded under the feature map. • When L ​ P / N LP/N is a small constant, neural network Bayesian inference departs from the kernel regime. At zero temperature, neural network Bayesian inference is equivalent to Bayesian inference using a data-dependent kernel, and L ​ P / N LP/N serves as an effective depth that controls the extent of feature learning. • In the restricted case of deep linear networks ( ψ = 0 \psi=0 ) and noisy data, we show a simple data model for which evidence and generalization error are optimal at zero temperature. As L ​ P / N LP/N increases, both evidence and generalization further improve, demonstrating the benefit of depth in benign overfitting.

Introduction

Neural networks are efficiently differentiable parametric families of functions that underlie state-of-the-art algorithms in applied domains ranging from natural language processing
[
BMR+20
]
to computer vision
[
KSH12
]
and structural biology/chemistry
[
JEP+21
]
. The empirical success of neural networks is driven in large part by their remarkable ability to efficiently adapt to and learn from rich training datasets.

This effect is observed most strongly in practice when exceptionally large networks, with billions or even trillions of parameters, are trained to fit comparably large high-dimensional datasets
[
KMH+20
,
HBM+22
,
HNA+17
]
. Explaining how network architecture, statistics of the training data, and learning rules interact to enable data-driven adaptation at inference time is therefore a core question in deep learning theory, especially in the setting where model size and dataset size are both large.

In this article, we make progress on this question by developing at a physics level of rigor the first (perturbatively) solvable model of learning with neural networks that are nonlinear in their parameters as well as their inputs and in which the number of training datapoints, the input dimension, the network’s hidden layer widths, and the network depth are simultaneously large.

1.1
Informal Overview of Results

In this section we present an informal overview of our results, deferring to §
2
the precise statements. We will work in the regime where the width, depth, and number of datapoints are all large:

N,L,P\gg 1\qquad\text{and\qquad}cN\leq N_{\ell}\leq CN,\quad\ell=1,\ldots,L,\qquad 0<c<C,

with hidden layer widths proportional to a large constant
N
. The prior over network parameter is iid Gaussian:

W_{ij}^{(\ell)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\,\,\text{iid}.

To understand the distribution of network outputs
f(x;\theta)
when
\theta
is sampled from the posterior (see §
2.1.3
for the definition) we develop a set of new analytical tools for computing the corresponding partition function with a source

Z_{\beta}(x;\tau):=\mathbb{E}_{\text{prior},\beta}\left[\exp\left[-i\tau f(x;\theta)-\beta\mathcal{L}(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D})\right]\right],\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R},

(1.3)

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature. The characteristic function of the predictive posterior (i.e., the distribution of
f(x;\theta)
with
\theta
sampled from the posterior) is determined by

\mathbb{E}_{\text{post},\beta}\left[\exp\left[-i\tau f(x;\theta)\right]\right]=\frac{Z_{\beta}(x;\tau)}{Z_{\beta}(0)},\qquad Z_{\beta}(0):=Z_{\beta}(x;0).

The normalizing constant
Z_{\beta}(0)
is referred to in the Bayesian inference literature as the Bayesian model evidence and will play a key role in our results (see e.g.,
[
Mac92
]
).

Since the exponent inside the expectation in (
1.3
) is quadratic in the outputs of the network and the expectation is with respect to the prior, a crucial starting point for understanding
Z_{\beta}(x;\tau)
is to characterize the joint distribution of the field
x\mapsto f(x;\theta)
evaluated at both a test point
x
and all training inputs
x_{\mu}
when
\theta
is sampled from its prior. While this prior is simple when viewed in parameter space, it corresponds to a complicated distribution over network outputs
[

,
HN20
,
Han22
,
HZ23
,
RYH22
,
SPSD17
,
Yai20
]
. Note from (

) that for any network input

, the corresponding output
f(x;\theta)
under the prior is the scalar product of the independent standard
N_{L}
-dimensional Gaussian vector
W^{(L+1)}
with the normalized vector
N_{L}^{-1/2}x^{(L)}
of post-activations in the final hidden layer. The joint distribution of the network outputs at any finite number of inputs under the prior is thus a centered Gaussian with an independent random covariance whose entries are overlaps of the final hidden layer representations:

\left(f(x_{\mu};\theta),\,\mu=1,\ldots,P\right)~\sim~\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(\frac{1}{N_{L}}\left\langle x_{\mu}^{(L)},x_{\nu}^{(L)}\right\rangle\right)_{1\leq\mu,\nu\leq P}\right).

The statistics of the field
x\mapsto f(x;\theta)
under the prior are hence determined by all moments of such overlaps:

\mathbb{E}_{\text{prior}}\left[\prod_{p=1}^{q}\frac{1}{N_{L}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L)},x_{\nu_{P}}^{(L)}\right\rangle\right],\qquad x_{\mu_{p}},x_{\nu_{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}},\qquad q\geq 1.

(1.4)

The first contribution of this article is a probabilistic combinatorial model for evaluating these expectations at large
L
, valid for any
\psi\in\mathbb{R}
(see §
2.2.3
). The key aspects of our model are:

•

Our model interprets the expectations in (
1.4
) as averages of products of edge-weights over a certain ensemble of random graphs. The vertex set in this graph ensemble is deterministic and is labeled by inputs
x_{\mu_{p}},x_{\nu_{p}},\,p=1,\ldots,q
appearing in the product. But the number of edges between any two vertices is random. Each copy of an edge
(x_{\mu},x_{\nu})
is weighted by a factor of
N_{0}^{-1}\left\langle x_{\mu},x_{\nu}\right\rangle
(see §
2.2.3
for details). This model is a significant extension of the combinatorial sum-over-paths approach developed in
[
Han18
,
HN20
]
for the case
\psi=0
and a computationally tractable alternative to the covariance SDE approach of
[
LNR22
]
.

•

While we do not know how to solve the combinatorial model exactly as
N,L\rightarrow\infty
with constant
L/N
, we are able to solve it perturbatively in powers of
1/N
in the regime where
L,N\to\infty
while
L/N\to 0
.

•

Analyzing this combinatorial model allows us to compute the partition function
Z_{\beta}(x;\tau)
at finite temperature perturbatively in
1/N
. This is of interest even for deep linear networks with
\psi=0

HZ23
,
ZVTP22
]
. Though we do not pursue this in the present article, our analysis also allows one to consider the case of any fixed output dimension.

In principle, one can use our formalism to compute the partition function to any order in
1/N

1/N
in §

. This already reveals a range of both qualitative and quantitative insights into the nature of Bayesian inference with deep shaped MLPs, including the following.

•

Nonlinearity strength
\psi
as curvature of implicit data manifold.
To zeroeth order in
1/N
(i.e., at leading order in the regime where

x\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}\quad\mapsto\quad x_{\psi}:=\left(1-2\psi\frac{\norm{x}^{2}}{N_{0}}\right)^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{x}{\sqrt{N_{0}}}.

(1.5)

As we explain in §

\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}+1}

\psi

Takeaway
:

When
N\gg L,P
, Bayesian inference with shaped MLPs is a kernel

\displaystyle\text{method in which the nonlinearity strength }\psi\text{ implicitly determines}

the curvature of the manifold (sphere or hyperbola or plane) into

which training data inputs are embedded.

•

LP/N
as emergent effective posterior depth.
When working perturbatively in
1/N
around the kernel regime

, we find that the first order correction to the posterior is cubic in the features (

Takeaway

\displaystyle\frac{LP}{N}=\frac{\text{depth}\times\text{samples}}{\text{width}}\text{ plays the role of an effective depth of the}

network under the posterior. Zero effective depth corresponds to a

data-independent kernel method.

This provides a concrete description of how increasing
LP/N
controls the onset of feature learning.

To analyze the structure of the posterior at first order in
1/N
, let us suppose that after passing through the feature map (
1.5
) inputs are generated iid from a distribution with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(x_{\mu})_{\psi}\right]=0,\qquad\Sigma:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x_{\mu}\right)_{\psi}\left(x_{\mu}\right)_{\psi}^{T}\right].

(1.6)

The explicit form of the partition function
Z_{\beta}(x;\tau)
perturbatively to first order in
1/N
(see Proposition
5.1
) reveals several ways in which the posterior adapts to the statistics of the training data.

•

Criterion for posterior linearity wrt inputs.
In the setting of iid training and test inputs for any
\psi\in\mathbb{R}
and to first order in
1/N
, we show that the predictive posterior of a deep nonlinear network coincides with the posterior of a deep linear network on the transformed data
\left\{(x_{\mu})_{\psi},y_{\mu}\right\}
if and only if

\frac{\tr(\Sigma^2)}{\tr(\Sigma)^{2}}=o(1).

(1.7)

We conjecture that this holds at all orders in
1/N
and summarize this in the following:

Takeaway
:

When presented with data satisfying (
1.7
), Bayesian inferences gives

to first order in
1/N
posteriors in which

is linear in

.

At first sight this is rather surprising: why would the posterior “ignore” the nonlinearity for data satisfying (

(see around (

)).

To understand the nature of posteriors that are nonlinear in

, we investigate a broad class of “nice” data generating processes in which (
1.7

)). Here we find:

•

Exact form of
LP/N

For “nice” data generating processes, maximizing the evidence requires taking the temperature to zero (i.e.,
\beta\rightarrow\infty
). At zero temperature, we find the following:

Takeaway

\displaystyle\text{At first order in }1/N,\text{ the posterior coincides with a {data-dependent}}

kernel method in which train and test inputs are re-weighted

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad x_{\psi}\quad\mapsto\quad\left(1+\text{const}_{\psi}\times\frac{LP}{N}\times x_{\psi}^{T}\Sigma x_{\psi}\right)x_{\psi}

(1.8)

according to their overlap with the sample covariance matrix
\Sigma
.

Since this re-weighting depends on the statistics of the data-generating process, we see that the first order in
1/N
correction corresponds to a data-dependent kernel method.

Finally, we add label noise in the data-generating process and ask when the Bayesian evidence and generalization error are maximized by overfitting (i.e., passing to zero temperature).

•

Depth makes overfitting more benign.
We examine a “nice” data generating process in the presence of label noise, and we show that the perturbative solutions for deep linear networks remain valid. In this setting, we find:

Takeaway

At first order in
1/N
and for label noise with variance

the evidence and generalization error of a deep linear network are both

Hence, in both a Bayesian and traditional sense, depth improves the behavior of overfitting in the regime of benign overfitting. See Corollary

2.1.2
Data

We will consider training datasets

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left(x_{\mu},y_{\mu}\right),\,\mu=1,\ldots,P\right\},\quad x_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}},\,y_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R},

(2.5)

for which we always make the following assumptions:

(i)

\displaystyle\quad\text{dataset size}~=~P~<~N_{0}~=~\text{ input dimension}

(2.6)

(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\text{the matrix of inputs }X=\left(x_{\mu},\,\mu=1,\ldots,P\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}\times P}\text{ has full rank}

(2.7)

(iii)

labels

are order

for all
\mu

(2.8)

While assumptions (ii) and (iii) are rather mild, assumption (i) is a significant restriction, which we do not know how to remove (see §
2.4
). In many of our results, the minimal norm interpolant of the data will appear:

\displaystyle\theta_{*}:=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}}}\norm{\theta}_{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\theta^{T}x_{\mu}=y_{\mu}\quad\forall\mu.

(2.9)

We work in the normalization where all examples
x_{\mu}
in the training dataset have norm
\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{x_\mu}\right]^{2}=O(1)
and all labels have
\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\mu}^{2}\right]=O(1)
.
When determining which terms remain to leading order, we also introduce a concentration / self-averaging assumption: for two data points
x_{\mu},x_{\nu}
in the training dataset, and their corresponding vectors

under the feature map introduced above (see (

\displaystyle\left\langle(x_{\mu})_{\psi},(x_{\nu})_{\psi}\right\rangle^{2}\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle(x_{\mu})_{\psi},(x_{\nu})_{\psi}\right\rangle^{2}\right].

This is only used to determine the scalings of terms; once the leading-order terms have been identified, we report the partition function (and evidence and posterior) without use of this approximation. Below, we present more concrete takeaways under a power law data model where this self-averaging behavior explicitly holds.

\theta\sim\mathbb{P}_{\text{prior}}(\theta\,|\,N_{\ell},L,\eta)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W_{ij}^{(\ell)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1+\frac{\eta}{L}\right)\text{ iid},

(2.10)

where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
is fixed and the
1+O(1/L)
scaling for the weight variance is required to keep the prior over network outputs non-degenerate at large
L
. We also consider the negative log-likelihood

\mathcal{L}(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{2P}\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}\left(f(x_{\mu};\theta)-y_{\mu}\right)^{2}.

The prior and likelihood determine, for every value of the inverse temperature
\beta\in[0,\infty],
a posterior

\mathbb{P}_{\text{post},\beta}(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D},N_{\ell},L,\psi,\eta)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\text{prior}}(\theta\,|\,N_{\ell},L,\eta)\exp\left[-\beta\mathcal{L}(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D})\right]}{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}\,|\,N_{\ell},L,\psi,\eta\right)}

(2.11)

over network parameters. We will often write
\mathbb{E}_{\text{prior},\beta}\left[\cdot\right]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\text{post},\beta}\left[\cdot\right]
for expectations over the prior and posterior at a given inverse temperature
\beta
, respectively.

In this article we are primarily concerned with understanding the predictive posterior, i.e., the distribution of network outputs
f(x;\theta)
when
\theta
is sampled from the posterior. This predictive posterior is determined by its characteristic function:

\mathbb{E}_{\text{post},\beta}\left[\exp\left[-i\tau f(x;\theta)\right]\right]=\frac{Z_{\beta}(x,\tau)}{Z_{\beta}(0)},

where

Z_{\beta}(x,\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{\text{prior},\beta}\left[\exp\left[-i\tau f(x;\theta)-\beta\mathcal{L}(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D})\right]\right]

is the partition function with a source at inverse temperature
\beta
. Throughout this article we consider just a single source, i.e., the distribution of
f(x;\theta)
at a single input
x
. However, our results generalize with no further work to the case of a finite number of sources and hence determine the finite-dimensional distributions of the predictive posterior.

2.2
Results

In this section, we present our main results, which we derive at a physics level of rigor. We begin in §
2.2.1
by stating an equivalence between Bayesian inference with deep shaped MLPs and a kernel method in the regime where the network width is much larger than both the depth and dataset size. We then describe in §
2.2.2

1/\text{width}.
Finally, we present in §
2.2.3
a combinatorial model for the moments of the prior distribution over network outputs in deep shaped MLPs.

2.2.1
Kernel Regime for Shaped Networks

A fundamental observation is that if a parameterized family of functions
x\mapsto g(x;\theta)
is a Gaussian field when
\theta
is sampled from its prior, then performing Bayesian inference with a log-likelihood given by the mean squared error on any training dataset yields a predictive posterior over
g(x;\theta)
that is Gaussian as well
[
Mac92
,
RN10
]
.

An important example of this in the context of the present article is Bayesian inference with wide neural networks. Specifically, consider a fully connected neural network as in (
2.1
) but with at fixed depth
L
, an arbitrary (not necessarily shaped) activation, and hidden layer widths proportional to a large constant
N
. It is well-known that if network weights are independent standard Gaussians, then the field
x\mapsto f(x;\theta)
is asymptotically Gaussian in the infinite width limit
N\rightarrow\infty

[
Han23
,
Yan19a
,
Yan19b
,
Yan21
,
LBN+18
,
GARA18
,
HNPSD22
,
FHM+23
]
. Hence, when the log-likelihood is the mean-squared error over a fixed training dataset, the Bayesian predictive posterior at infinite width is also Gaussian and is equivalent to that of a linear model with associated kernel

K^{(L)}(x_{\mu},x_{\nu}):=\lim_{N_{1},\ldots,N_{L}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N_{L}}\left\langle x_{\mu}^{(L)},x_{\nu}^{(L)}\right\rangle.

(2.12)

is therefore deterministic. A natural question is then what happens if not only the network width

The exact description of the deviation for the kernel method depends on the details of how the training data and network architecture are constructed and how learning is analyzed (e.g., using statistical mechanics, the replica trick, etc).

Perturbation Theory Around Kernel Regime

In the previous section, we saw that Bayesian inference with shaped MLPs in the regime

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x_{\mu}\right)_{\psi}\right]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x_{\mu}\right)_{\psi}\left(x_{\mu}\right)_{\psi}^{T}\right]=\Sigma.

•

Uninformative data gives deep linear posterior over

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{0}}}X_{\psi}=:\sum_{j=1}^{P}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}u_{j}v_{j}^{T}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{P}\lambda_{j}=1,\qquad\lambda_{j}\sim j^{-\alpha},\qquad\alpha>1.

As a first question about posteriors for such data-generating processes, we ask when Bayesian inference prefers deeper networks. To make sense of this, recall that the partition function

is often called the Bayesian model evidence

]

•

In the case of deep linear networks
\psi=0

\alpha<2\qquad\text{and}\qquad k=o(P^{1/\alpha}).

(2.18)

2.2.3
Combinatorial Model for Prior Moments

To describe the basis of our technical contributions, we develop in this section a new combinatorial/probabilistic formalism for computing the moments

M_{q}^{(L)}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\norm{X^{(L)}t}^{2}\right)^{q}\right],\qquad X^{(\ell)}t=\sum_{\mu=1}^{P}t^{\mu}x_{\mu}^{(\ell)}

(2.20)

of the prior in the final hidden layer. To do this, let us write

\displaystyle M_{q}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\overline{\mu}=\left(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{q}\right)\\
\overline{\nu}=\left(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{q}\right)\\
\mu_{j},\,\nu_{j}=1,\ldots,P\end{subarray}}t^{\overline{\mu}}t^{\overline{\nu}}M_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}}^{(L)},

where

t^{\overline{\mu}}=\prod_{p=1}^{q}t^{\mu_{p}},\qquad M_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}}^{(L)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{p=1}^{q}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L)},x_{\nu_{P}}^{(L)}\right\rangle\right].

The goal of this section is for fixed

\overline{\mu}=\left(\mu_{p},\,p=1,\ldots,q\right),\,\overline{\nu}=\left(\nu_{p},,\,p=1,\ldots,q\right),\qquad\mu_{p},\nu_{p}\in\left\{1,\ldots,P\right\}

to obtain a probabilistic representation for
M_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}}^{(L)}
, which replaces integration over random weights by integration over a collection of random graph, defined as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Random Graph Process)
.

Given
\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}
, we define a layerwise Markov Chain

G_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}}^{(\ell)}=\left(V_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}},E^{(\ell)}\right),\qquad\ell=L,L-1,\ldots,0

as follows:

•

For all
\ell
we set

V_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}}=\left(\gamma_{p},\,\gamma\in\left\{\mu,\nu\right\},\,p=1,\ldots,q\right).

•

The initial condition is

E^{(L)}:=\left\{(\mu_{p},\nu_{p}),\,p=1,\ldots,q\right\}.

•

Given
E^{(\ell+1)}
, we produce
E^{(\ell)}
as follows:

–

Independently for each vertex
\gamma_{p}\in V
we add to
E^{(\ell+1)}
a self-loop
(\gamma_{p},\gamma_{p})
with probability

\frac{\absolutevalue{\psi}}{L}\mathrm{deg}_{E^{(\ell+1)}}(\gamma_{p})=\frac{\absolutevalue{\psi}}{L}\left(1+2s_{\gamma}^{(\ell)}\right),\quad s_{\gamma_{p}}^{(\ell+1)}=\#\left\{\text{self-loops at vertex }\gamma_{p}\text{ in }E^{(\ell+1)}\right\}.

We will refer to
s_{\gamma_{p}}^{(\ell)}
as the self-loop process and will sometimes write

s^{(\ell)}:=\sum_{\gamma=\mu,\nu}\sum_{p=1}^{q}s_{\gamma_{p}}^{(\ell)}=\text{total number of self-loops in }E^{(\ell)}.

(2.21)

–

We define independent Bernoulli-random variables

\delta^{(\ell)}={\bf 1}\left\{\text{edge shuffle occurs at layer }\ell\right\}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{2}{N_{\ell}}\binom{|E^{(\ell+1)}|}{2}\right).

On the event that
\delta^{(\ell)}=1
,
we uniformly at random select a pair of edges
e_{1}=(\mu_{p_{1}},\nu_{p_{1}}),e_{2}=(\mu_{p_{2}},\nu_{p_{2}})\in E^{(\ell+1)}
, remove them from
E^{(\ell+1)}
and recplace them instead by

\begin{cases}(\mu_{p_{1}},\mu_{p_{2}}),(\nu_{p_{1}},\nu_{p_{2}})&\quad\text{ with probability }1/2\\
(\mu_{p_{1}},\nu_{p_{2}}),(\nu_{p_{1}},\mu_{p_{2}})&\quad\text{ with probability }1/2\end{cases},

independent of the choice of edges and of whether
\delta^{(\ell)}
occurs.

Our main result about this combinatorial model is the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Fix
\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}
and consider the random graph process
G^{(\ell)}=\left(V_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}},E^{(\ell)}\right)
from Definition
2.1
. Define

c^{(\ell)}:=\begin{cases}1,&\quad\ell=L\\
c^{(\ell+1)}\sigma^{2|E^{(\ell+1)}|}\left(1+\frac{2\absolutevalue{\psi}|E^{(\ell+1)}|}{L}+\frac{2}{N_{\ell}}\binom{|E^{(\ell+1)}|}{2}\right),&\quad\ell<L\end{cases}.

We then have

\displaystyle M_{\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{sgn}\left(\psi\right)^{s^{(0)}}c^{(0)}\prod_{e\in E^{(0)}}\omega_{e}^{(0)}\right]+O\left(\frac{L}{N^{2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{L}\right),

(2.22)

where
\mathrm{sgn}\left(\cdot\right)
is the sign function, for any
\ell=0,\ldots,L
we define the edge-weights by

e=\left(\gamma_{p_{1}},\gamma_{p_{2}}^{\prime}\right)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w_{e}^{(\ell)}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N_{\ell}}\left\langle x_{\gamma_{p_{1}}}^{(\ell)},x_{\gamma_{p_{2}}^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}\right\rangle,

and the expectation is over the random graph process.

2.3
Review of Literature

Bayesian inference with neural networks has a long history, going back at least to the article
[
Nea96
]
, which showed that one layer networks with random weights converge to Gaussian processes at infinite width. This subject was taken up and expanded upon in
[
LBN+18
]
, which considered the case of networks of any fixed depth. The existence and universality of Gaussian process priors at infinite width and finite depth were then established at a variety of levels of rigor and generality in articles such as
[
GARA18
,
HNPSD22
,
Han23
]
.

While the preceding articles sought to analyze neural networks at finite depth, the results in
[
HN20
,
Han18
,
Yai20
,
RYH22
,
LNR22
,
ZBM22
]
gave new information about the prior over neural network outputs in the regime where depth and width are simultaneously large. Two important ideas emerged from these works. First, when the network depth scales like a constant times the network width, the prior distribution over network outputs has a complex non-Gaussian structure. Second, in order for the two point function at be well-behaved at large depth,
[
LNR22
,
ZBM22
]
showed that one must consider nonlinearities close to the identity at every layer. This motivates the shaped nonlinearities we consider here (see (
1.2
)).

To help elucidate the nature of the priors computed in the regime where both depth and width are large, two independent articles
[
ZVP21
,
NBR+21
]
gave an exact non-asymptotic characterization of these priors in the case of deep linear networks in terms of so-called Meijer G-functions. This special functions point of view was then taken up in
[
HZ23
]
to compute exact non-asymptotic formulas for the posterior and model evidence for Bayesian inference with deep linear networks with output dimension
1
at zero temperature. It was this article which showed that while the depth-to-width (in this article denoted by
L/N
) ratio naturally plays the role of the “effective depth of the prior”, it is actually
LP/N
that plays the role of the “effective depth of the posterior.” We give partial corroboration in the present article that it is indeed
LP/N
that plays this role for nonlinear networks as well. In addition to the work in
[
HZ23
]
, we point the reader to the excellent article
[
LNR22
]
, which computes at a physics level of rigor the predictive posterior in deep linear networks at finite depth, as well as the posterior of the intermediate hidden layer representations. The results for deep linear networks were then reproduced in the thermodynamic limit in
[
ZVTP22
]
.

In addition to these articles on linear and shaped networks, several interesting lines of research have studied Bayesian inference with fully nonlinear neural networks at finite depth. These articles include
[
SNR23
,
NR21
]
, which obtain explicit but nonlinear saddle point equations for the posterior distribution of feature-feature covariance kernels at each hidden layer. Further, we also point the reader to
[
CKZ23
]
, which uses the replica trick to make several precise and numerically accurate predictions for generalization error in both regression and classification settings. Finally, we mention the article
[
APP+22
]
. This work uses tools from statistical mechanics to connect Bayesian inference with finite depth neural networks in terms of student
t
-processes. It also proposes exact formulas for the generalization error for Bayesian inference in a regression task with a one-hidden layer network in the regime where the number of training datapoints is a constant times the hidden layer width.

2.4
Limitations

We conclude by emphasizing what we view as the three main limitations of the present work.

•

Perturbative vs. exact solutions.
The present article is able to compute only perturbatively statistics of the predictive posterior in shaped networks. Obtaining higher order perturbative corrections and re-summing the resulting series is something we leave for future work.

•

More general nonlinearities.
The present article considers only cubic shaped nonlinearities due to their natural appearance in tanh-like activation functions. While our techniques readily generalize the case of other monomials, they do not seem to extend in a simple manner to general shaped nonlinearities of the form
\phi(t)=t+\frac{1}{L}\sigma(t)
. Nor does this article consider more general reshaped nonlinearities such as the reshaped ReLU (see
[
LNR22
]
), which are not odd.

•

Beyond linear overparameterization.
The results in the present article assume that the number of datapoints
P
is strictly less than
N_{0}
. Without this assumption, it is not clear how to define a suitable analog for the minimal norm interpolant
\theta_{*}
of the training data, which plays an important role in all our results. However, understanding the nature of Bayesian posteriors with
P>N_{0}
and indeed
P=N_{0}^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq 1
is an open problem.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W^{(L)}}\left[\prod_{e\in E^{(L)}}w_{e}^{(L)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{W^{(L)}}\bigg[\frac{1}{N_{L}^{q}}\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{q}=1}^{N_{L}}\prod_{p=1}^{q}\left(W_{k_{p}}^{(L)}x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)}+\frac{\psi}{3L}\left(W_{k_{p}}^{(L)}x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right)^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\left(W_{k_{p}}^{(L)}x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)}+\frac{\psi}{3L}\left(W_{k_{p}}^{(L)}x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right)^{3}\right)\bigg],

where we have written
W_{k}^{(L)}
for the
k

W^{(L)}
. Wick contracting the weights, which we recall have mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}/N_{L-1}
, yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W^{(L)}}\left[\prod_{e\in E^{(L)}}w_{e}^{(L)}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\{\prod_{p=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N_{L-1}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\right\}\left(1+\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\binom{|E^{(L)}|}{2}\right)

(3.2)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\psi}{L}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=1\end{subarray}}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N_{L-1}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\sum_{p^{\prime}=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2N_{L-1}}\left(\norm{x_{\mu_{p'}}^{(L-1)}}^{2}+\norm{x_{\nu_{p'}}^{(L-1)}}^{2}\right)

(3.3)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{N_{L}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}=1\\
p_{1}^{\prime}<p_{2}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=1\\
p\neq p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N_{L-1}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\frac{\sigma^{4}}{2N_{L-1}^{2}}\bigg(\left\langle x_{\mu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\mu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\left\langle x_{\nu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left\langle x_{\mu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\left\langle x_{\nu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\mu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\bigg)

(3.4)

\displaystyle+O\left(N^{-2}+L^{-2}\right).

The key observation is that each of the terms (

), (
3.3
), and (

) can be re-written in terms of products of edge-weights over a graph

. For example, for the term from (

\displaystyle\prod_{p=1}^{q}\frac{1}{N_{L-1}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{e\in E_{0}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)},

where we have set

E_{0}^{(L-1)}=E^{(L)}.

Similarly, inspecting the expression in (
3.3
), we see that for each
p^{\prime}=1,\ldots,q
, we have

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=1\end{subarray}}^{q}\frac{1}{N_{L-1}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\right]\times\frac{1}{2N_{L-1}}\left(\norm{x_{\mu_{p'}}}^{2}+\norm{x_{\nu_{p'}}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\left(\prod_{e\in E_{\mu,p^{\prime}}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)}+\prod_{e\in E_{\nu,p^{\prime}}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)}\right),

where

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,p^{\prime}}^{(L-1)}

\displaystyle:=E^{(L)}\cup\left\{(\gamma_{p^{\prime}},\gamma_{p^{\prime}})\right\},\qquad\gamma\in\left\{\mu,\nu\right\}

is obtained from
E^{(L)}
by adding a self-loop at either the vertex
\gamma_{p^{\prime}}
. Finally, for each
p_{1}^{\prime}<p_{2}^{\prime}=1,\ldots,q
we have

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=1\\
p\neq p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{q}\frac{1}{N_{L-1}}\left\langle x_{\mu_{p}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\right]\times\frac{1}{2N_{L-1}^{2}}\bigg(\left\langle x_{\mu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\mu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\left\langle x_{\nu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left\langle x_{\mu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\nu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\left\langle x_{\nu_{p_{1}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)},x_{\mu_{p_{2}^{\prime}}}^{(L-1)}\right\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\prod_{e\in E_{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}),p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)}+\prod_{e\in E_{(\mu_{1},\nu_{2}),(\mu_{2},\nu_{1}),p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)}\right),

where for a pair-partition
\pi
of the set
\left\{\mu_{p_{1}},\mu_{p_{2}},\nu_{p_{1}},\nu_{p_{2}}\right\}
the edge-set

is obtained from
E^{(L)}
by removing the edges
(x_{\mu_{p_{1}}},x_{\nu_{p_{1}}}),\,(x_{\mu_{p_{2}}},x_{\nu_{p_{2}}})
and “re-wiring” their endpoints according to
\pi
. Putting this all together yields the representation

\displaystyle\sigma^{-2|E^{(\ell+1)}|}\mathbb{E}_{W^{(L)}}\left[\prod_{e\in E^{(L)}}w_{e}^{(L)}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\{\prod_{e\in E_{0}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)}\right\}\left(1+\frac{2}{N_{L}}\binom{|E^{(L)}|}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\psi}{L}\times\sum_{p=1}^{q}\sum_{\gamma\in\left\{\mu,\nu\right\}}\prod_{e\in E_{\gamma,p}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\binom{q}{2}}{N_{L}}\times\frac{1}{\binom{q}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},p_{2}=1\\
p_{1}<p_{2}\end{subarray}}^{q}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\pi}\prod_{e\in E_{\pi,p_{1},p_{2}}^{(L-1)}}w_{e}^{(L-1)}

\displaystyle+O(N^{-2})+O(L^{-2}).

(3.5)

The sum over
\pi
in the second to last line runs over the two non-trivial pair-partitions of
\left\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\right\}
. To recast this combinatorial formula into an expectation over random graphs let us recall that

c^{(L-1)}=\sigma^{2|E^{(L)}|}\left(1+\frac{2\absolutevalue{\psi}|E^{(L)}|}{L}+\frac{2}{N_{L}}\binom{|E^{(L)}|}{2}\right).

The expression (
3.5
) then admits the representation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W^{(L)}}\left[\prod_{e\in E^{(L)}}w_{e}^{(L)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{E^{(L-1)}}\left[\mathrm{sgn}\left(\psi\right)^{{\bf 1}\left\{\text{self-loop in }E^{(L-1)}\right\}}c^{(L)}\prod_{e\in E^{(L-1)}}\omega_{e}^{(L-1)}\right]

\displaystyle+O\left(N^{-2}+L^{-2}\right),

where this expectation is now over the random edge-set
E^{(L-1)}
as in Definition
2.1
. Repeating this derivation at each layer noticing that the
O(N^{-2}+L^{-2})
error term gets added
L
times in this procedure completes the derivation.
\square

4.2
Alternative Derivation of
\psi
-dependent Kernel

Recall that when
N,L,P\rightarrow\infty
with
N\gg L,P
, we found that Bayesian inference with our deep shaped MLPs (
2.1
) was equivalent to Bayesian inference with the
\psi
-dependent kernel

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}:=K_{\psi}\left(x_{\mu},x_{\nu}\right)=\left\langle(x_{\mu})_{\psi},\left(x_{\nu}\right)_{\psi}\right\rangle.

The purpose of this section is to give a simple and self-contained derivation of the following statement:

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{prior}}\left[\frac{1}{N_{L}}\left\langle x_{\mu}^{(L)},x_{\nu}^{(L)}\right\rangle\right],

(4.6)

In other words, we will show that the kernel
K_{\mu\nu}
appears as the two-point function when first taking the width
N
to infinity and then the depth
L
to infinity. For this, let us write for any
L\geq 1
as in (
2.12
)

K_{\mu\nu}^{(L)}:=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{prior}}\left[\frac{1}{N_{L}}\left\langle x_{\mu}^{(L)},x_{\nu}^{(L)}\right\rangle\right].

This kernel satisfies the well-known recursion
[
LBN+18
,
RYH22
,
Han23
]

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}^{(L+1)}=\mathbb{E}_{K^{(L)}}\left[\phi\left(z_{\mu}\right)\phi\left(z_{\nu}\right)\right],\qquad K_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\left\langle x_{\mu},x_{\nu}\right\rangle,

(4.7)

where the expectation is, by definition, over

\left(z_{\mu},z_{\nu}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}K_{\mu\mu}^{(L)}&K_{\mu\nu}^{(L)}\\
K_{\nu\mu}^{(L)}&K_{\nu\nu}^{(L)}\end{array}\right)\right).

This recursion holds for any nonlinearity. In the setting (
2.3
) in this article, the relation (
4.7
) becomes

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}^{(L+1)}=K_{\mu\nu}^{(L)}\left(1+\frac{\psi}{L}\left(K_{\mu\mu}^{(L)}+K_{\nu\nu}^{(L)}\right)\right)+O(L^{-2}).

(4.8)

Let us turn the discrete layer index
\ell=0,\ldots,L
into consider now a continuous time index:

\ell\quad\mapsto\quad\tau:=\ell/L.

Taking
L\rightarrow\infty
and setting
\mu=\nu
and rewriting (
4.8
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}K_{\mu\mu}^{(\tau)}=\frac{2\psi}{L}\left(K_{\mu\mu}^{(\tau)}\right)^{2}.

(4.9)

Solving this equation yields

\displaystyle K_{\mu\mu}^{(\tau)}=\left(1-2\psi\tau K_{\mu\mu}^{(0)}\right)^{-1}K_{\mu\mu}^{(0)}=\left(1-2\psi\tau\frac{\norm{x_\mu}^{2}}{N_{0}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\norm{x_\mu}^{2}}{N_{0}}.

(4.10)

Plugging this back into (
4.8
) and solving a simple ODE gives

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}^{(\tau)}=\left(1-2\psi\tau\frac{\norm{x_\mu}^{2}}{N_{0}}\right)^{-1/2}\left(1-2\psi\tau\frac{\norm{x_\nu}^{2}}{N_{0}}\right)^{-1/2}\frac{\left\langle x_{\mu},x_{\nu}\right\rangle}{N_{0}}.

(4.11)

Taking
\tau=1
completes the derivation of (
4.6
).

6.2
Data Assumptions

We will evaluate the posterior and evidence under mild assumptions in the large
P,N_{0}
limit. These assumptions permit the scaling of integrals

in Proposition

to be made explicit, providing the leading-order contributions at zero and ultimately finite temperature.

The first assumption is a normalization

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\wx_\mu}^{2}\right]=1,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\mu}^{2}\right]=1,

where we emphasize that the data remains non-isotropic. Note that this implies the covariance matrix

The second assumption is a self-averaging or concentration property. In well-behaved models, we expect that as
N,P_{0}\to\infty
, quantities concentrate: for example, for two independent samples
\widehat{x}_{\mu}
and
\widehat{x}_{\nu}
from the training dataset, we may take
\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle\approx\tr(\Sigma^2)
, and we take
\norm{\wx_\mu}^{2}\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\wx_\mu}^{2}\right]
. (Later, we give an example of a concrete data model — one with a power law covariance spectrum — where these conditions hold.) Note that we do not rely on the self-averaging property to compute explicit quantities such as the posterior or model evidence. Rather, we use the self-averaging assumption to determine which quantities contribute to leading order.

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial\log Z}{\partial\tau}\Bigg|_{\tau=0}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L}{N}c_{\psi,\eta}\Bigg[\left\langle\left(\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3}-\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)\otimes\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{\theta}_{*}\otimes\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle-\left\langle\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x},\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle\Bigg],

where the second line is dominated by the first line when
\norm{\wtheta_*}^{2}=o(P)
.
To evaluate the variance, we also need identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\norm{\wx_{||}}^{2}

to obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}\log Z}{\partial\tau^{2}}\Bigg|_{\tau=0}

\displaystyle=\norm{\wx_\perp}^{2}\Bigg\{1+\frac{L}{N}\left[-P+\norm{\wtheta_*}^{2}+2c_{\psi,\eta}\sum_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}y_{\mu}\sum_{\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{T}\widehat{X}\right)^{-1}_{\mu\nu}y_{\nu}\right]\Bigg\}.

As an example of our analysis, we examine the first line of the mean, and the nontrivial leading-order term in the variance. The first line of the mean originates from the term

\displaystyle\left\langle(\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3})_{\perp},\widehat{\theta}_{*}\otimes\sum_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right)_{\mu}\widehat{x}_{\mu}^{\otimes 2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3}-\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x},\widehat{\theta}_{*}\otimes\sum_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right)_{\mu}\widehat{x}_{\mu}^{\otimes 2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\widehat{\theta}_{*}^{T}\widehat{x}_{||}\right)\sum_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}-\sum_{\mu\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}y_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle^{2}.

Under the self-averaging assumption
\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}\approx\tr(\Sigma^2)
, the first term is order
P\left(\widehat{\theta}_{*}^{T}\widehat{x}_{||}\right)\tr(\Sigma^2)
. We decompose the second term into two cases. When
\mu=\nu
, it is of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}y_{\mu}\norm{\wx_\mu}^{4}\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\wx_\mu}^{4}\right]\sum_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}y_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\wx_\mu}^{4}\right]\left(\widehat{\theta}_{*}^{T}\widehat{x}_{||}\right)

due to the self-averaging assumption
\norm{\wx_\mu}^{4}\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\wx_\mu}^{4}\right]
. When
\mu\neq\nu
, self-averaging assumes
\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle^{2}\approx\tr(\Sigma^2)
, giving

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\neq\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}y_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle^{2}\approx P\left(\widehat{\theta}_{*}^{T}\widehat{x}_{||}\right)\tr(\Sigma^2).

We conclude that the term contributes to leading order when
\tr(\Sigma^2)=\Omega(1)
. To rewrite it in the form reported above, we note that

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\theta}_{*}^{T}\widehat{x}_{||}\right)\sum_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}-\sum_{\mu\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}y_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}y_{\mu}\left(\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\nu}^{2}\right\rangle-\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)_{\mu}y_{\mu}\left(\widehat{x}^{T}\Sigma\widehat{x}-\widehat{x}_{\mu}^{T}\Sigma\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right).

The mean contains the negative of this term.

For the variance, we expand the term

\displaystyle\left\langle(\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3})_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{x}_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3}-\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}\right)\otimes\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{x}_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\norm{\wx_\perp}^{2}\sum_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}y_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right)_{\mu}-0

and apply the self-averaging assumption
\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}\approx\tr(\Sigma^2)
to obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle(\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3})_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{x}_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\norm{\wx_\perp}^{2}\tr(\Sigma^2)\sum_{\mu}y_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right)_{\mu}.

The identity
\sum_{\nu}\left\langle\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right)_{\nu}=\norm{\wtheta_*}^{2}
provided above gives

\displaystyle\left\langle(\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3})_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{x}_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\norm{\wx_\perp}^{2}\tr(\Sigma^2)\norm{\wtheta_*}^{2}.

Note that we can rewrite

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right)_{\mu}=\sum_{\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{T}\widehat{X}\right)^{-1}_{\mu\nu}y_{\nu}

to obtain the form

\displaystyle\left\langle(\widehat{x}^{\otimes 3})_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{x}_{\perp}\otimes\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\norm{\wx_\perp}^{2}\sum_{\mu}\left\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right\rangle^{2}y_{\mu}\sum_{\nu}\left(\widehat{X}^{T}\widehat{X}\right)^{-1}_{\mu\nu}y_{\nu}

reported in the variance. For the higher moments of the partition function (
\tau^{3}
and
\tau^{4}
), a similar analysis shows that all terms vanish at zero temperature.

6.4
Power Law Data Model

At zero temperature, we reported the evidence and posterior under only a self-averaging assumption. Here, we introduce an explicit data model to provide more interpretable results in at finite temperature. We impose a power law spectrum in the covariance matrix and chose labels in a direction corresponding to a singular value.

Definition 6.3

(
k
th-principal power law model)
.

Let
\widehat{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{0}\times P}
be the dataset reshaped by the nonlinearity, as defined in §
6.1
. Let
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
be the labels. Define the SVD decomposition
\widehat{X}=\sqrt{P}\sum_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}u_{j}v_{j}^{T}
. The
k
th-principal power law model
assumes input data satisfying, for all
j=1,\dots,P
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\propto j^{-\alpha}

(6.8)

with
\alpha>0
and proportionality constant such that
\tr(\Sigma)=1
, and labels
Y=\sqrt{P}v_{k}+\epsilon
and for
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
with i.i.d. entries sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
.

Note that the power law is defined over the reshaped dataset
\widehat{X}
rather than the original dataset
X
; this choice is natural due to
LP/N=0
being a kernel model in the reshaped variables. We will discard certain parameter regimes of the power law data model, since they produce a pathological scaling.

Remark 6.4
.

A
k
th-principal power law model satisfying either i)
\alpha<1
and
k\in[P]
or ii)
\alpha>1
and
P^{1/\alpha}=o(k)
, satisfies
Pk^{-\alpha}/\sum_{j}j^{-\alpha}=o(1)
.

When
\alpha<1
this property occurs since, in the large
N_{0},P
limit, the labeled direction
k
provides a vanishingly small signal: the slow decay causes additional data to extensively dilute the correlation between input data and label. For
\alpha>1
, a similar property holds when
k
is too large. This behavior prompts the following refinement of the original data model.

Definition 6.5

(Non-vanishing
k
th-principal power law model)
.

A
k
th-principal power law model is
non-vanishing
if
\alpha>1
and
k=o(P^{1/\alpha})
.

Among non-vanishing power law models, we observe that if the variance
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
of the label noise grows too large, the size of the minimum-norm interpolant is
\norm{\wtheta_*}^{2}=k^{\alpha}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}P^{-1+\alpha}
. Since the signal can have
k\sim 1
, the noise can dominate the interpolant when
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}>P^{1-\alpha}
; for this reason, we will consider label noise of variance at most
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=O(P^{1-\alpha})
. Altogether, these observations and the zero-temperature results of §
6.3
produce the following corollary.

Corollary 6.6

(Scaling of
LP/N
correction at zero temperature)
.

Consider the
k
th-principal power law model with
k=o(P^{1/\alpha})
. Linear and nonlinear networks receive the following
LP/N
corrections.

•

Neglecting logarithmic factors, when
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=O(P^{1-\alpha})
, the linear network at zero temperature has evidence of the form

\displaystyle\log Z_{\mathrm{lin}}(0,LP/N>0)

\displaystyle=\log Z_{\mathrm{lin}}(0,LP/N=0)\left[1+\frac{LP}{N}\times(\text{linear correction})\right]

for data-dependent factor
(\text{linear correction})=O(1)
. At
LP/N>0
, the mean is unchanged and the variance acquires an
O(LP/N)
correction.

•

Neglecting logarithmic factors, when
1<\alpha<2
and
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=0
, the nonlinear network at zero temperature has evidence of the form

\displaystyle\log Z(0,LP/N>0)

\displaystyle=\log Z_{\mathrm{lin}}(0,LP/N=0)\Bigg[1+\frac{LP}{N}\times(\text{linear correction})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+c_{\psi,\eta}\tr(\Sigma^2)\frac{LP}{N}\times(\text{nonlinear correction})\Bigg]

for data-dependent factor
(\text{nonlinear correction})=O(1)
. At
LP/N>0
, the mean acquires an
O(c_{\psi,\eta}\tr(\Sigma^2)LP/N)
correction, and the variance acquires an
O(LP/N)
correction.

6.5
Analysis at Positive Temperature

We now turn to finite-temperature computations of the evidence and posterior.

Proposition 6.7

(Evidence)
.

Let
\widehat{X},Y
be generated by a non-vanishing
k
th-principal power law model, i.e., with
k<P^{1/\alpha}
and label noise
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
. Define (neglecting an additive constant)

\displaystyle\log Z(0)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{2}\tr\log(M_\beta)+\frac{1}{2\beta}\norm{\wbXd\wtheta_*}^{2}-\norm{M_\beta^{1/2} \wX^T \wtheta_*}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\norm{\wX\wbXd\wtheta_*}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\frac{L}{N}\Bigg[\norm{\wX\wbXd\wtheta_*}^{4}-2\norm{\wX\wbXd\wtheta_*}^{2}\sum_{\mu\nu}\left(M_{\beta}\right)_{\mu\nu}\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle+\tr(\wbXd\wX)^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c_{\psi,\eta}}{2}\frac{L}{N}\Bigg[\sum_{\mu\nu}\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle^{2}\left(\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right)_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right)_{\nu}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\left\langle\sum_{\mu}\left(\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{x}_{\mu}\right)_{\mu}\widehat{x}_{\mu}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\widehat{X}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\langle\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\otimes\widehat{X}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{X}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\otimes\widehat{X}^{\otimes 3}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\mu\nu}\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle^{2}\left(M_{\beta}\right)_{\mu\nu}\left(\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu}\otimes\widehat{X}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*},\widehat{X}\widehat{X}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\widehat{\theta}_{*}\otimes\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle+\left\langle\widehat{x}_{\mu},\widehat{x}_{\nu}\right\rangle\right)\Bigg].

(6.9)

For a linear network (
c_{\psi,\eta}=0
),
Z(0)
is the evidence for label noise at most
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=O(P^{1-\alpha})
. For a nonlinear network (
c_{\psi,\eta}\neq 0
),
Z(0)
is the evidence when
\alpha<2
and
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=0
.

Since the perturbative expansion holds for linear neural networks with noisy labels, we maximize evidence with respect to temperature in this model. When the optimal temperature is
\beta\to\infty
, the model chooses to overfit the training data; we refer to this as benign overfitting. We show that increasing the depth improves the evidence in the regime of benign overfitting.

Corollary 6.8

(Bayesian benign overfitting for linear network)
.

Let
\widehat{X},Y
be generated by a non-vanishing
k
th-principal power law model, i.e., with
k=o(P^{1/\alpha})
and label noise
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=O(P^{1-\alpha})
. Assume a linear network (
c_{\psi,\eta}=0
). The following facts hold.

•

For all temperatures
\beta P>P^{\alpha}
, the log evidence is equivalent up to constant factors.

•

The evidence is maximized at a temperature satisfying
\beta P>P^{\alpha}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\log Z(0,\beta P<P^{\alpha})<\log Z(0,\beta P>P^{\alpha}).

•

At any temperature, the evidence increases with respect to
LP/N
.

In particular, omitting some positive constants, the following holds:

\displaystyle\log Z(0)

\displaystyle\simeq\begin{cases}(\alpha-1)P\log P-\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}P^{\alpha}-k^{\alpha}+\frac{L}{N}\left[P^{2}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{4}P^{2\alpha}+k^{2\alpha}\right]&B>P^{\alpha},k<B^{1/\alpha}\\
P\log B/P+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}B-k^{\alpha}+\frac{L}{N}\left[B^{2/\alpha}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{4}P^{-2}B^{2+2/\alpha}+k^{2\alpha}\right]&B<P^{\alpha},k<B^{1/\alpha}\\
P\log B/P+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}B-B+\frac{L}{N}\left[B^{2/\alpha}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{4}P^{-2}B^{2+2/\alpha}+B^{4}k^{-2\alpha}\right]&B<P^{\alpha},k>B^{1/\alpha},\end{cases}

where the depth correction is
O(LP/N)
.

The above result refers to benign overfitting in a Bayesian sense, where zero temperature maximizes evidence. We also examine benign overfitting in terms of generalization error.

Proposition 6.9

(Generalization error benign overfitting for linear network)
.

Let
\widehat{X},Y
be generated by a non-vanishing
k
th-principal power law model, i.e., with
k=o(P^{1/\alpha})
and label noise
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=o(P^{1-\alpha})
. Assume a linear network (
c_{\psi,\eta}=0
). For generalization error
E=\langle\left(f(x)-y\right)^{2}\rangle
, the following facts hold.

•

For all temperatures
\beta P>P^{\alpha}
, the generalization error is equivalent up to constant factors.

•

The generalization error is minimized at a temperature satisfying
\beta P>P^{\alpha}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle E_{\beta P>P^{\alpha}}<E_{\beta P<P^{\alpha}}.

•

At any temperature, the generalization decreases with respect to
LP/N
.

We see that the Bayesian sense of benign overfitting coincides here with the traditional definition in terms of generalization error: both the evidence and generalization error agree on when to overfit, and depth affects both quantities similarly. (We remark that the generalization error result is only given for label noise
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=o(P^{1-\alpha})
as opposed to
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=O(P^{1-\alpha})
, due to our approximation scheme being too coarse to ascertain the signs of relevant constants when
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\sim P^{1-\alpha}
; however, we expect the evidence and generalization error to agree even when
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\sim P^{1-\alpha}
.)

Finally, we address zero-temperature versus finite-temperature evidence for a nonlinear network. The perturbative expansion holds for power laws with
1<\alpha<2

\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=0
