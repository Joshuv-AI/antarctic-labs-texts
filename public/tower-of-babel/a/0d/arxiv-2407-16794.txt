Global Bifurcation of Steady Surface Capillary Waves on a 2D Droplet
Gary Moon and Yilun Wu

Abstract
. We construct global curves of rotational traveling wave solutions to the 2D water wave equations on a compact domain. The real analytic interface is subject to surface tension, while gravitational effects are ignored. In contrast to the rotational surface waves, the fluid follows the incompressible, irrotational Euler equations. This model can provide a description for tiny water droplets in breaking waves and white caps. The primary tool we use is global bifurcation theory, via a conformal formulation of the problem. The obtained fluid domains have m m -fold discrete rotational symmetry, as well as a reflection symmetry.

The mathematical study of water waves goes back over two centuries to work of Laplace and Lagrange in the latter part of the eighteenth century. Some early work on traveling water waves involved studying the linearized (about still water) equations and was done by mathematicians such as Cauchy, Poisson and Airy. A quite remarkable early contribution was made by Gerstner, who discovered a family of exact steady solutions to the full nonlinear system. Another impressive advancement was that of Stokes when he utilized a perturbation expansion (in the wave amplitude) to study traveling water waves. Stokes also considered waves of large amplitude, having famously conjectured the existence of the wave of extreme form (or wave of greatest height), which has sharp crests of included angle
\frac{2\pi}{3}

[
33
]
.

The first rigorous construction of small-amplitude traveling waves was obtained by Nekrasov
[
29
]
. His approach was to use a sort of Stokes expansion and show that the series had a positive radius of convergence. Also notable regarding the work of Nekrasov is his formulation of the problem as an integral equation on the boundary of the fluid domain. This formulation has been quite influential and many studies have made use of his formalism. Another important contribution was made by Levi-Civita who used a similar approach to Nekrasov (expanding in powers of the amplitude and showing convergence), but formulated the problem differently
[
25
]
.

Up until this point, perturbative assumptions (i.e., small amplitude) were absolutely critical to the analysis. The first large-amplitude traveling water waves were not constructed until the middle of the twentieth century in the work of Krasovskiĭ
[
22
]
. By utilizing the positive operator theory of Krasnoselskii, Krasovskiĭ was able to construct a set of large-amplitude traveling waves.

A major milestone in the study of traveling water waves was the introduction of a general theory of bifurcation from a simple eigenvalue
[
10
]
and global continuation (e.g., Dancer’s theory of global bifurcation for analytic operators
[
12
]
), along with the realization that the problem of traveling water waves fit into this framework. Using such tools often allows one to not simply construct individual traveling wave solutions or even sets of solutions, but global connected sets (or even curves) of solutions.

Indeed, Keady-Norbury used this theory to construct the first global (smooth) curve of large-amplitude traveling water waves
[
19
]
. It was through studying the limiting behavior of solutions along this curve that the famed Stokes conjecture on the wave of extreme form
[
1
,
35
]
was resolved. Further constructions of global connected sets of (translational) traveling gravity water waves (
g>0
and
\sigma=0
) can be found, for example, in
[
7
,
8
,
2
]
. A good survey of the literature on traveling water waves can be found in
[
18
]
.

Traveling waves in the presence of surface tension are quite a bit less well-understood than in the case of pure gravity waves. An important early contribution was made by Crapper, who derived a global family of exact solutions to the full nonlinear Euler equations describing traveling capillary waves (
g=0
and
\sigma>0
) over an infinitely-deep fluid layer
[
11
]
. These solutions, so-called the Crapper waves, are well known to form splash singularities in the troughs of the waves, as the continuation parameter approaches a limiting value. A similar construction was carried out in the case of finite depth by Kinnersley
[
21
]
.

Much like with traveling gravity waves or capillary waves, bifurcation analysis has been a powerful tool for constructing more general traveling waves subject to a combination of gravity, surface tension (
g>0
and
\sigma>0)
, and other effects such as voriticity and surface wind. For example, for construction of small-amplitude waves, see
[

37
]
and the references therein. For large-amplitude waves and criteria of singularity formation in global solution sets, see
[

,
8
]

The primary difference between our work and other works on the subject of traveling water waves is that we consider
rotational
waves on the surface of a capillary drop as opposed to translational waves on the surface of a fluid layer. More precisely, we look for

(1.11)

\displaystyle(x(\alpha,t),y(\alpha,t))

\displaystyle=R_{ct}(\tilde{x}(\alpha),\tilde{y}(\alpha)),

Incidentally, the boundary curve is a simple closed curve bounding a compact domain (the droplet), rather than the typical case of an open curve with an unbounded fluid domain in the above mentioned literature. We also emphasize here that, although the surface waves make the boundary of the fluid domain rotate rigidly, the fluid flow itself is actually irrotational. In fact, the inside of the fluid must follow some complicated flow far from a rigid body rotation, to create the appearance of a rotational wave on the surface.

One of the earliest contributions to the study of the evolution of waves on the surface of a capillary drop was made by Lord Rayleigh. Indeed, in

Beyer-Günther used a coordinate-free geometric formulation of the system to demonstrate that the problem is well-posed in Sobolev spaces, the earliest result on the well-posedness of the water waves system in the presence of surface tension

Though traveling waves on the surface of a capillary drop have been computed via numerical and perturbation analysis, their existence and behavior have not been rigorously demonstrated. It is our goal in this paper to initiate the rigorous study of this phenomenon by proving their existence.

The retention of the loop is not the only delicate issue that arises in the study of traveling water waves due to the presence of surface tension. This is particularly the case when one considers gravity-capillary waves (

Our approach employs the conformal formalism of
[

from the unit disk

for a fixed Riemann map

Observe that

We show, in Section 2, that such a (fixed) Riemann map

(1.22)

2\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)_{\alpha}-i2Z_{\alpha}+c^{2}\mathfrak{C}(Z\mathfrak{H}(\left\lvert Z\right\rvert^{2})_{\alpha})=0.

Note that the trivial solution given by

is a solution to (

The remainder of the paper is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
1.1
and is organized as follows. In Section 2, we begin by writing down the relevant equations of motion and then using holomorphic coordinates/the conformal method to obtain a convenient formulation of the problem. Next, in Section 3, we carry out the necessary setup to apply the Dancer/Buffoni-Toland framework of analytic global bifurcation theory to the problem. In Section 4, we use this analytic bifurcation theory to construct local curves of solutions bifurcating from the trivial (circular) solution. In Section 5, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
by continuing the local curves constructed in Section 4 to global curves of solutions. Moreover, in Section
6

.

(2)

\mathfrak{C}(\overline{f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}})=\overline{\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\emph{Avg}}f}=\overline{f(0)}
.

(3)

\mathfrak{C}(\mathfrak{Re}f\big|_{\mathbb{T}})=\frac{1}{2}f+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\emph{Avg}}f}=\frac{1}{2}f+\frac{1}{2}\overline{f(0)}
.

(4)

\mathfrak{C}(i\mathfrak{Im}f\big|_{\mathbb{T}})=\frac{1}{2}f-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\emph{Avg}}f}=\frac{1}{2}f-\frac{1}{2}\overline{f(0)}
.

Proof.

The first equation is just the Cauchy integral formula. To get the second equation, we compute for all
w\in\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle\overline{(\mathfrak{C}\overline{f})(w)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\tau)}{\overline{\tau}-\overline{w}}~d\overline{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\tau)}{\frac{1}{\tau}-\overline{w}}~d\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\tau)}{\tau(1-\overline{w}\tau)}~d\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\tau)}{\tau}~d\tau

(2.18)

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}f.

For the third equation, we note that
\mathfrak{C}(\mathfrak{Re}f)-\frac{1}{2}f
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
, with the following continuous boundary value on
\mathbb{T}
:

(2.19)

\frac{1}{2}\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}(\mathfrak{Re}f)+\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{Re}f+\frac{i}{2}\mathfrak{H}(\mathfrak{Re}f)-\frac{1}{2}f=\frac{1}{2}\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}(\mathfrak{Re}f)+\frac{i}{2}[\mathfrak{H}(\mathfrak{Re}f)-\mathfrak{Im}f].

Note that the above boundary value has constant real part. Thus
\mathfrak{C}(\mathfrak{Re}f)-\frac{1}{2}f
is constant on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\mathfrak{Re}f)-\frac{1}{2}f

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}\left(\mathfrak{C}(\mathfrak{Re}f)-\frac{1}{2}f\right)

\displaystyle=[\mathfrak{C}(\mathfrak{Re}f)](0)-\frac{1}{2}\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}f

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}(\mathfrak{Re}f)-\frac{1}{2}\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}f

(2.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\overline{\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}f}.

The last equation can be proven similarly.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

A more common form of the Titchmarsh theorem states that for
f
holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
with continuous boundary values in
C^{0,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
,
0<\beta<1
,

(2.21)

\mathfrak{Im}f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}=\mathfrak H(\mathfrak{Re}f\big|_{\mathbb{T}})+\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\emph{Avg}}(\mathfrak{Im}f).

This obviously holds in view of the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

For equivalence of the dynamical boundary condition before and after application of
\mathfrak{C}
, we also note the following.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f
be a holomorphic function on
\mathbb{D}
with continuous boundary values in
C^{0,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
for some
0<\beta<1
, and let
r\in C^{0,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
be real-valued. Suppose
f
is not identically zero. Then

r=0
if and only if
\mathfrak{C}(rf\big|_{\mathbb{T}})=0
.

Proof.

The forward implication is obvious. Now suppose
\mathfrak{C}(rf\big|_{\mathbb{T}})=0
. The Fourier series of
rf\big|_{\mathbb{T}}
converges on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
:

(2.22)

rf\big|_{\mathbb{T}}(\tau)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\tau^{n}.

Since
\mathfrak{C}(\tau^{n})=w^{n}
if
n\geq 0
and
\mathfrak{C}(\tau^{n})=0
if
n<0
, we obtain that

(2.23)

\mathfrak{C}(rf\big|_{\mathbb{T}})(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}w^{n}.

Since
\mathfrak{C}(rf\big|_{\mathbb{T}})=0
, we get
a_{n}=0
for all
n\geq 0
, and
rf=\overline{h}
on
\mathbb{T}
, where
h=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\overline{a_{-n}}w^{n}
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
with continuous boundary value and
h(0)=0
. We take the complex conjugate to get
r\overline{f}=h
on
\mathbb{T}
. It follows that
\overline{fh}=r|f|^{2}=fh
on
\mathbb{T}
. It follows from Lemma
2.1
that

(2.24)

fh=\mathfrak{C}(fh)=\mathfrak{C}(\overline{fh})=\overline{(fh)(0)}=0.

Since
f
is holomorphic and not identically zero,
f
cannot be zero on any open subset of
\mathbb{D}
. Hence
h=0
on a dense subset of
\mathbb{D}
and thus on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. It follows that
rf=0
on
\mathbb{T}
. By the Schwarz reflection principle,
f
cannot be zero on any open subset of
\mathbb{T}
. Thus
r=0
on
\mathbb{T}
.
∎

The Cauchy integral commutes with angular rotations. In particular, we have

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\in C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
for some
0<\beta<1
, then

(2.25)

\mathfrak{C}(f_{\alpha})=(\mathfrak{C}f)_{\alpha}.

Proof.

We only need to check that the two sides are equal at any
w\in\mathbb{D}
, in which case it is obvious as the Cauchy integral commutes with angular rotations.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

By (
2.17
), we also have
\mathfrak{H}(f_{\alpha})=(\mathfrak{H}f)_{\alpha}
. In the following, we sometimes write
\mathfrak{C}f_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{H}f_{\alpha}
without distinguishing the order of operations.

We now multiply (
2.12
) by
z_{\alpha}
, apply
\mathfrak{C}
and use Lemma

to get

(2.26)

\mathfrak{C}\left(z_{\alpha}\mathfrak{Re}\Phi_{t}-z_{t}\mathfrak{Re}\Phi_{\alpha}-i\sigma\left(\frac{z_{\alpha}}{|z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}\right)=0.

In particular, we have computed

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}\frac{(\Phi_{\alpha})^{2}}{z_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}\frac{(iw\Phi^{\prime})^{2}}{iwz^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{i\tau^{2}(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}}{\tau z^{\prime}}~\frac{d\tau}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}}{z^{\prime}}~d\tau

(2.27)

\displaystyle=0.

For the last step, we used the fact that
z
is a Riemann mapping and

has no zeros on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. By Lemma
2.2
, (
2.26
) is equivalent to (
2.12
). So the problem has been reduced to finding two holomorphic functions
z(w,t)
and
\Phi(w,t)
on
\mathbb{D}
with sufficiently regular boundary values satisfying (
2.7
) and (
2.26
).

We now look for steady traveling wave solutions. Those are solutions for which
z
is of the form

(2.28)

z(w,t)=Z(w)e^{ict}

for some fixed Riemann mapping
Z
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Under this Ansatz for

, (
2.7
) reads

(2.29)

\mathfrak{Im}(icZ\overline{Z_{\alpha}})=\mathfrak{Im}(\overline{\Phi_{\alpha}})\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{T},

or

(2.30)

i\mathfrak{Im}(\Phi_{\alpha})=-ic\mathfrak{Re}(Z\overline{Z_{\alpha}})=-\frac{ic}{2}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{T}.

By Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.3
we get

(2.31)

\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{\alpha}=-\frac{ic}{2}\mathfrak{C}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}.

It follows that

(2.32)

\Phi(w,t)=-ic\mathfrak{C}(|Z|^{2})(w)+b(t).

Recall that we have the freedom to add an arbitrary function of the form
c_{1}+ic_{2}(t)
to
\Phi
. Thus we may assume without loss of generality that
b(t)
is real-valued and
b(0)=0
.
We also have from (
2.31
) and (
2.17
) that

(2.33)

\Phi_{\alpha}\big|_{\mathbb{T}}=\frac{c}{2}\mathfrak H(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}-\frac{ic}{2}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}\big|_{\mathbb{T}}.

Using (
2.28
), (
2.32
), (
2.33
) with Lemma
2.1
, we can write (
2.26
) as

(2.34)

b^{\prime}(t)Z_{\alpha}-\frac{ic^{2}}{2}\mathfrak{C}(Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha})-i\sigma\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}=0.

Any solution of (
2.34
) forces

to be a real constant, which we denote by
-b
. We thus have

(2.35)

\Phi(w,t)=-ic\mathfrak{C}(|Z|^{2})(w)-bt,

and

(2.36)

-2ibZ_{\alpha}+c^{2}\mathfrak{C}(Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha})+2\sigma\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}=0.

We compare (
2.36
) with (49) in
[
14
]
. They are the same equation once we note that
\alpha
corresponds to
-u
in their paper.

As is shown above, (
2.36
) is a consequence of the real equation (
2.12
).
Later it will be useful to go back to this real equation on
\mathbb{T}
. We record this important step as follows.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
Z
be a Riemann mapping satisfying
Z(0)=0
and extending to a
C^{2,\beta}
diffeomorphism on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. If
Z
solves (
2.36
), then

(2.37)

-2ibZ_{\alpha}+c^{2}Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}+2\sigma\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}-\frac{ic^{2}}{4}\frac{[\overline{\mathfrak{C}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}}]^{2}}{\overline{Z_{\alpha}}}=0

on
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Let
Z
be a Riemann mapping as above solving (
2.36
). Define
\Phi
by (
2.35
), and
z
by (
2.28
). It is easy to check by repeating the calculations above that (
2.7
) holds, and the left hand side of (

∎

We briefly comment on the effect of rotational symmetry of Riemann mapping on solutions to (
2.36
). As is alluded to earlier, the Riemann mapping that takes
0
to the center of mass of the fluid domain is only unique up to a rotation. As a result, if
z(w,t)
and
\Phi(w,t)
solve (
2.7
) and (
2.34
), so do
z(we^{i\alpha(t)},t)
,
\Phi(we^{i\alpha(t)},t)
for any smooth real-valued function
\alpha(t)
. However, in general such a solution will no longer have the form (
2.28
), and thus will not give rise to a solution to (
2.36
). If it does, there should exist a Riemann mapping
Z_{1}
and a real constant
c_{1}
such that

(2.38)

z(we^{i\alpha(t)},t)=Z_{1}(w)e^{ic_{1}t},

or

(2.39)

Z(we^{i\alpha(t)})=Z_{1}(w)e^{i(c_{1}-c)t}

for all
w\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and all
t
. The rotation function
\alpha(t)
is either constant or non-constant. If
\alpha(t)=\alpha_{0}
is a constant, one obtains
Z_{1}(w)=Z(we^{i\alpha_{0}})
and
c_{1}=c
. This corresponds to the constant rotation symmetry
(Z(w),c)\to(Z(we^{i\alpha_{0}}),c)
of solutions to (
2.36
). Next assume
\alpha(t)
is non-constant. By the constant rotation symmetry shown above, we can assume
\alpha(0)=0
without loss of generality, and then
\alpha(t_{0})\neq 0
for some
t_{0}
. By continuity of
\alpha(t)
, there exists
t_{n}
such that
\alpha(t_{n})=\frac{\alpha(t_{0})}{n}
for all positive integers
n
. Evaluating (
2.39
) at
t=0
we get
Z(w)=Z_{1}(w)
. Evaluating it at
t=t_{n}
we get

(2.40)

\left|Z\left(we^{i\frac{\alpha(t_{0})}{n}}\right)\right|=|Z(w)|.

This implies that

(2.41)

\left|Z\left(e^{i\frac{m}{n}\alpha(t_{0})}\right)\right|=|Z(1)|

for all positive rational numbers
\frac{m}{n}
. By continuity we see that
Z
maps
\mathbb{T}
to another circle centered at the origin. Recall by our earlier convention that
Z(0)=0
. Thus there exists a
\lambda>0
and
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.42)

Z(w)=\lambda e^{i\alpha_{0}}w.

It follows from (
2.39
) again that
\alpha(t)=(c_{1}-c)t
. This corresponds to the symmetry
(Z(w),c)\to(Z(w),c_{1})
of solutions to (
2.36
). Note, however, that this symmetry occurs only when

is the simple Riemann map given by (
2.42
).

3.
Formulation for Bifurcation Analysis

In view of previous numerical works on this problem, we look for solutions to (
2.36
) in the case
b=\sigma
. This particular choice of
b
includes the unit disc
Z_{0}(w)=w
. We point out that this is not the only reasonable choice of
b
. For instance, instead of fixing
b=\sigma
, one could allow
b
to vary in the solutions so as to keep the area of the fluid domain fixed. We leave such other considerations for future studies. Note that we can further take
\sigma=1
without loss of generality, and reduce (
2.36
) to

(3.1)

2\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}-2iZ_{\alpha}+c^{2}\mathfrak{C}(Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha})=0,

since having a general
\sigma
is equivalent to replacing
c^{2}
by
\frac{c^{2}}{\sigma}
. We now define
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)
to be the left hand side of (
3.1
):

(3.2)

\mathscr{F}(Z,c)=2\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}-2iZ_{\alpha}+c^{2}\mathfrak{C}(Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}),

so that (
3.1
) can be rewritten as
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)=0
.
To study mapping properties of
\mathscr{F}
, we need certain spaces of holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
. Let
H(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
be the space of functions that are holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
with continuous boundary values on
\mathbb{T}
. For the remainder of the paper, we fix the symmetry multiplicity
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 2
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and
0<\beta<1
, we define

(3.3)

X^{k,\beta}=\left\{f\in H(\overline{\mathbb{D}}):f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}\in C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T}),f(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}w^{mn+1},~a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

with norm
\|f\|_{X^{k,\beta}}=\|f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}\|_{C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}
. Note that
f\in H(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
and
f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}\in C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
imply that
f\in C^{k,\beta}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
.
It is easy to see that the symmetry conditions on
X^{k,\beta}
can be characterized more instrinsically as

(3.4)

f(e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}w)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}f(w),~\overline{f(w)}=f(\overline{w}).

As is well known, the
C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
norm can be characterized in terms of Littlewood-Payley projectors. For
f\in C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
with
f(\tau)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}\tau^{n}
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(\Delta_{0}f)(\tau)=\sum_{|n|\leq 1}a_{n}\tau^{n}
and

(3.5)

(\Delta_{j}f)(\tau)=\sum_{2^{j}\leq|n|<2^{j+1}}a_{n}\tau^{n}.

There exists a
C>0
such that for all
f\in C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
,

(3.6)

\tfrac{1}{C}\|f\|_{C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\sup_{j\geq 0}2^{j(k+\beta)}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C\|f\|_{C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}.

3.1.
Mapping properties of
\mathscr{F}

For proper definition of
\mathscr{F}
and later studies of global continuation, we will restrict
\mathscr{F}
to certain subsets of
X^{k,\beta}
. First, we need a condition that guarantees the functions will be proper Riemann maps used to parametrize the fluid domain. That is supplied by the chord-arc condition:

(3.7)

\inf_{\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{T}}\left|\frac{f(\tau_{1})-f(\tau_{2})}{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}\right|>0.

We have

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\in H(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
,
f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}\in C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

f
satisfies (
3.7
).

(b)

f
is injective on
\mathbb{T}
and
f^{\prime}(\tau)\neq 0
for all
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
.

(c)

f
is a diffeomorphism from
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
onto
f(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
.

Proof.

It is obvious that (a) and (b) are equivalent. It is also obviously that (c) implies (b). It remains to show that (b) implies (c). If (b) holds,
f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}
is a
C^{1,\beta}
Jordan curve. By the Jordan curve theorem,
f(\mathbb{T})
separates
\mathbb{C}
into the interior region
\text{int}(f(\mathbb{T}))
and the exterior region
\text{ext}(f(\mathbb{T}))
. For any
z\in\text{int}(f(\mathbb{T}))
, the number of points
w\in\mathbb{D}
satisfying
f(w)=z
is equal to the winding number of
f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}
around
z
, which is one. Similarly, if
z\in\text{ext}(f(\mathbb{T}))
, the number of points
w\in\mathbb{D}
satisfying
f(w)=z
is equal to the winding number of
f\big|_{\mathbb{T}}
around
z
, which is zero. If there exists a
w\in\mathbb{D}
such that
f(w)\in f(\mathbb{T})
, by the open mapping theorem, there exists a
w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}
close to
w
such that
f(w^{\prime})\in\text{ext}(f(\mathbb{T}))
. This contradicts the above result. Thus
f(\mathbb{D})=\text{int}(f(\mathbb{T}))
,
f(\mathbb{T})=\partial(\text{int}(f(\mathbb{T})))
,
f:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\to f(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
is bijective, and
f^{\prime}
can be continuously extended to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
such that
f^{\prime}\neq 0
on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. It follows that
f^{-1}
can be continuously extended to
f(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
such that
(f^{-1})^{\prime}\neq 0
on
f(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
.
∎

We thus define

(3.8)

U^{k,\beta}=\{f\in X^{k,\beta}~|~f\text{ satisfies the chord-arc condition }\eqref{chord-arc}\}.

It shall be useful in the sequel to have a quantitative measure of the chord-arc condition (
3.7
). To that end, we define, for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{T}
,

(3.9)

\mathscr{C}(f)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\begin{cases}\frac{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}{f(\tau_{1})-f(\tau_{2})}&\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}\\
\frac{1}{\partial_{\alpha}f(\tau)}&\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\tau\end{cases}.

Then,
f\in X^{k,\beta}
will satisfy the chord-arc condition (
3.7
) if and only if

\left\|\mathscr{C}(f)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}<+\infty.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
k\geq 2
,
\mathscr{F}
is analytic from
U^{k,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
to
X^{k-2,\beta}
. The Fréchet derivative
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}
is given by

(3.10)

D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z,c)[\zeta]=\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}-\frac{Z_{\alpha}^{2}\overline{\zeta_{\alpha}}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{3}}\right)_{\alpha}-2i\zeta_{\alpha}+c^{2}\mathfrak{C}\left(\zeta\mathfrak{H}(\left\lvert Z\right\rvert^{2})_{\alpha}+Z\mathfrak{H}(\zeta\overline{Z}+\overline{\zeta}Z)_{\alpha}\right).

We will break the proof of this lemma into several steps. First we show that
\mathscr{F}
maps into
X^{k-2,\beta}
. The
C^{k-2,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
regularity is fairly obvious, as Hölder regularity is preserved under multiplication, square root of strictly positive functions, division by strictly positive functions,
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{H}
. Analyticity also follows as the above mentioned maps are all analytic with respect to the relevant Hölder norms. We only need to show the symmetry properties of
X^{k,\beta}
is perserved under
\mathscr{F}
. To that end, we need the following symmetry lemmas.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
f(e^{i\alpha_{0}}\tau)=e^{i\alpha_{0}}f(\tau)
on
\mathbb{T}
for some
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, then

(3.11)

f_{\alpha}(e^{i\alpha_{0}}\tau)=e^{i\alpha_{0}}f_{\alpha}(\tau),\quad(\mathfrak{C}f)(e^{i\alpha_{0}}w)=e^{i\alpha_{0}}(\mathfrak{C}f)(w),\quad(\mathfrak{H}f)(e^{i\alpha_{0}}\tau)=e^{i\alpha_{0}}(\mathfrak{H}f)(\tau).

Similarly, if
g(e^{i\alpha_{0}}\tau)=g(\tau)
on
\mathbb{T}
for some
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, then

(3.12)

g_{\alpha}(e^{i\alpha_{0}}\tau)=g_{\alpha}(\tau),\quad(\mathfrak{C}g)(e^{i\alpha_{0}}w)=(\mathfrak{C}g)(w),\quad(\mathfrak{H}g)(e^{i\alpha_{0}}\tau)=(\mathfrak{H}g)(\tau).

Proof.

The first equation in (
3.11
) follows from direction calculation. Denote by
R
the rotation map
w\mapsto e^{i\alpha_{0}}w
. The condition on
f
can be written as
f\circ R=e^{i\alpha_{0}}f
. We can assume
\alpha_{0}\neq 0\mod 2\pi
, otherwise there is nothing to prove. This implies
\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}~f=0
. Since the Cauchy integral
\mathfrak{C}
commutes with rotation, we have

(3.13)

(\mathfrak{C}f)\circ R=\mathfrak{C}(f\circ R)=\mathfrak{C}(e^{i\alpha_{0}}f)=e^{i\alpha_{0}}\mathfrak{C}f,

which is equivalent to the middle equation in (
3.11
). The last equation in (
3.11
) now follows from (
2.17
). (
3.12
) can be proven similarly.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\overline{f(\tau)}=f(\overline{\tau})
on
\mathbb{T}
, then

(3.14)

\overline{f_{\alpha}(\tau)}=-f_{\alpha}(\overline{\tau}),\quad\overline{(\mathfrak{C}f)(w)}=(\mathfrak{C}f)(\overline{w}),\quad\overline{(\mathfrak{H}f)(\tau)}=-(\mathfrak{H}f)(\overline{\tau}).

Similarly, if
\overline{g(\tau)}=-g(\overline{\tau})
on
\mathbb{T}
, then

(3.15)

\overline{g_{\alpha}(\tau)}=g_{\alpha}(\overline{\tau}),\quad\overline{(\mathfrak{C}g)(w)}=-(\mathfrak{C}g)(\overline{w}),\quad\overline{(\mathfrak{H}g)(\tau)}=(\mathfrak{H}g)(\overline{\tau}).

Proof.

The first equation in (
3.14
) follows from direct calculation. To get the second equation, we compute

\displaystyle\overline{(\mathfrak{C}f)(w)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\tau)}{\tau-w}~d\tau}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\overline{\tau})}{\overline{\tau}-\overline{w}}~d\overline{\tau}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\mathbb{T}}\frac{f(\tau)}{\tau-\overline{w}}~d\tau

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{C}f)(\overline{w}).

Note that the condition on
f
implies
\overline{\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}~f}=\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}~f
. The last equation in (
3.14
) now follows from (
2.17
). (
3.15
) can be proven similarly.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

As is explained above, the
C^{k-2,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
regularity of
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)\big|_{\mathbb{T}}
and the analyticity of
\mathscr{F}
with respect to the Hölder norms are standard. (
3.10
) follows from a direct calculation.
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)
is obviously in
H(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
by construction. We only need to show that
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)
satisfies the symmetry requirements of
X^{k-2,\beta}
. Since
Z\in X^{k,\beta}
, we have
Z(e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}w)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}Z(w)
and
\overline{Z(w)}=Z(\overline{w})
on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. It follows from Lemma
3.3
that
Z_{\alpha}(e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}w)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}Z_{\alpha}(w)
,
|Z_{\alpha}|(e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}\tau)=|Z_{\alpha}|(\tau)
,
\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}(e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}\tau)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}(\tau)
,
(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}(e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}\tau)=(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}(\tau)
,
\left[Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2}_{\alpha})\right](e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}\tau)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}\left[Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2}_{\alpha})\right](\tau)
. This implies that
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)(e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}w)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{m}}\mathscr{F}(Z,c)(w)
. It follows from Lemma
3.4
that
\overline{Z_{\alpha}(w)}=-Z_{\alpha}(\overline{w})
,
|Z_{\alpha}(w)|=|Z_{\alpha}(\overline{w})|
,
\overline{\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}(\tau)}=\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}(\overline{\tau})
,
\overline{(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}(\tau)}=-(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}(\overline{\tau})
,
\overline{\left[Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2}_{\alpha})\right](\tau)}=\left[Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2}_{\alpha})\right](\overline{\tau})
. This implies
\overline{\mathscr{F}(Z,c)(w)}=\mathscr{F}(Z,c)(\overline{w})
. The proof is complete.
∎

We next prove certain compactness properties of
\mathscr{F}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
k\geq 2
. For any
(Z,c)\in U^{k,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
,
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z,c):X^{k,\beta}\to X^{k-2,\beta}
is Fredholm with index zero.

Before beginning the proof of Lemma
3.5
, we need to introduce a couple of tools which we will utilize. First, we have the following simple lemma:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f,g\in H(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
with
f(0)=0
or
g(0)=0
, and let
r\in C(\mathbb{T})
be real-valued. If

f=r\overline{g}
on
\mathbb{T}
, then
f\equiv 0
in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Proof.

If
f=r\overline{g}
on
\mathbb{T}
, then
fg
is continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
and real-valued on
\mathbb{T}
. It then follows that
fg
is constant on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
f(0)=0
or
g(0)=0
, we have

fg\equiv 0\text{ on }\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

If
f
is not identically zero on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, then
g
is. Since
f=r\overline{g}
, this implies
f\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{T}
and thus on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. In other words,
f
must be zero on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.
∎

We will also need to work with the following scale of spaces:

(3.16)

C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})\coloneqq\left\{f\in C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T}):f(\tau)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}\tau^{mn+1},\ a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

We shall equip
C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
with the same norm as that of
X^{k,\beta}
; that is,

\left\|f\right\|_{C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\coloneqq\left\|f|_{\mathbb{T}}\right\|_{C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}.

Note that the symmetry conditions on
C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
are similar to those on
X^{k,\beta}
, and can once again be characterized by (
3.4
). However, the frequencies in the Fourier series are allowed to be negative. In other words,
C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
is not a space of holomorphic functions. In fact, we have

(3.17)

C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})\cap H(\overline{\mathbb{D}})=X^{k,\beta}.

We are now ready to address Lemma
3.5
. It will be useful to break the proof into a couple of parts and we begin with the following claim:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(Z,c)\in U^{k,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
for
k\geq 2
. Then, the operator

(3.18)

\zeta\mapsto\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)_{\alpha},

as a map from
X^{k,\beta}
to
X^{k-2,\beta}
, is invertible.

Proof.

Let
D_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{1}{i}\partial_{\alpha}
and observe that we can write

\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)_{\alpha}=-D_{\alpha}\mathfrak{C}(\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{-1}D_{\alpha}\zeta).

Given that
D_{\alpha}
is clearly invertible from
X^{k,\beta}
to
X^{k-1,\beta}
and from
X^{k-1,\beta}
to
X^{k-2,\beta}
(see (
2.4
)), we need only consider the invertibility of the operator
\mathfrak S:X^{k-1,\beta}\to X^{k-1,\beta}
given by

(3.19)

\mathfrak S(\zeta)=\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right).

Our strategy is to prove invertibility of the extended operator
\mathfrak{T}:C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})\to C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
given by

(3.20)

\mathfrak{T}(\zeta)=\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)+\frac{(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\zeta.

We note that it is straightforward to verify that
\mathfrak{T}
respects the symmetries of
C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
(via Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
). As
(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})\zeta=0
if
\zeta
is holomorphic,
\mathfrak T\big|_{X^{k-1,\beta}}=\mathfrak S
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{T}
is Fredholm of index zero, which is most apparent upon writing

\mathfrak{T}(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}+\left[\mathfrak{C},\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{-1}\right](\zeta).

The commutator is compact on
C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
by Lemma 6.3 of
[
17
]
, while the first term is plainly invertible given
Z\in U^{k,\beta}
. Invertibility of
\mathfrak T
will now follow upon showing that the kernel is trivial.

Let us now suppose that we have
\zeta\in C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
such that
\mathfrak{T}(\zeta)=0
. Note that

\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right),\overline{(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})\zeta}\in H(\overline{\mathbb{D}}),\quad\text{and }[(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})\zeta](0)=0.

Applying Lemma
3.6
, we will then have

(3.21)

\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)=0,

and so

(3.22)

\frac{(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\zeta=0.

(
3.22
) implies that
\mathfrak{C}\zeta=\zeta
and so, from (
3.21
), we have

0=\left(\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right),\zeta\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert},\mathfrak{C}\zeta\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|\zeta|^{2}}{|Z_{\alpha}|}~d\alpha.

This implies that
\zeta=0
, and we must have

\ker\mathfrak{T}=\{0\}
.

We now want to prove invertibility of
\mathfrak S=\mathfrak{T}\big|_{X^{k-1,\beta}}
. Fix some
f\in X^{k-1,\beta}\subset C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
. By invertibility of
\mathfrak T
on
C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists a unique
\zeta\in C_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T})
such that
\mathfrak{T}(\zeta)=f
. In other words,

(3.23)

f-\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)=\frac{(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\zeta.

We have

(3.24)

f-\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right),\overline{(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})\zeta}\in H(\overline{\mathbb{D}}),\quad\text{and }[(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})\zeta](0)=0.

Lemma
3.6
again implies that
f-\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)=0
,
and so
(\mathrm{id}-\mathfrak{C})\zeta=0
.
Therefore, we must in fact have
\zeta\in H(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. (
3.17
) implies
\zeta\in X^{k-1,\beta}
.
This completes the proof.

∎

We also need to state a standard result on compact embeddings of Hölder spaces. Let
C_{*}^{r}(\mathbb{T})
,
r\in\mathbb{R}
, denote the scale of Zygmund spaces on
\mathbb{T}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, it is well known that

(3.25)

C_{*}^{k+\beta}(\mathbb{T})=C^{k,\beta}(\mathbb{T}).

We have the following result regarding embeddings of
C_{*}^{r}(\mathbb{T})
:

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
. Then,

C_{*}^{r+\epsilon}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow C_{*}^{r}(\mathbb{T}).

Moreover, this embedding is compact.

Proof.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
. Let
\Lambda(D_{\alpha})\coloneqq(\mathrm{id}-\Delta_{\alpha})^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Then, for
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Lambda^{\rho}(D_{\alpha}):C_{*}^{r}(\mathbb{T})\to C_{*}^{r-\rho}(\mathbb{T})
is an isomorphism (see
[
34
]
). Moreover, we note that we have the continuous embedding
C_{*}^{r}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow C_{*}^{\rho}(\mathbb{T})
for any
\rho\leq r
(e.g.,
[
32
]
). Via the above results and (
3.25
), the claim will follow upon showing that
C^{0,\epsilon}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow C_{*}^{0}(\mathbb{T})
for
0<\epsilon<1
. The desired embedding factors through
C^{0}(\mathbb{T})
:

C_{*}^{\epsilon}(\mathbb{T})=C^{0,\epsilon}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow C_{*}^{0}(\mathbb{T}),

where the first (continuous) embedding is compact by Arzelà-Ascoli.
∎

We are now ready for the proof of Lemma
3.5
:

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Fix
(Z,c)\in U^{k,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
. We have already shown
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z,c):X^{k,\beta}\to X^{k-2,\beta}
. We will proceed by showing that
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z,c)
is of the form

(3.26)

D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z,c)=H+K,

where
H:X^{k,\beta}\to X^{k-2,\beta}
is a homeomorphism and
K:X^{k,\beta}\to X^{k-2,\beta}
is compact.

Examining (
3.10
), the first term is the leading-order term (second order) and all remaining terms map
X^{k,\beta}\to X^{k-1,\beta}
. Given that
X^{k-1,\beta}
compactly embeds in
X^{k-2,\beta}
by Lemma
3.8
, these lower-order terms all represent compact operators.

Let us then consider the first term. By Lemma
3.7
,

\zeta\mapsto\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{\zeta_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)_{\alpha}

is a homeomorphism
X^{k,\beta}\to X^{k-2,\beta}
. Hence, noting the invertibility of
D_{\alpha}:X^{k,\beta}\to X^{k-1,\beta}
and
X^{k-1,\beta}\to X^{k-2,\beta}
, we are left to prove that the operator

(3.27)

\zeta\mapsto-\mathfrak{C}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}^{2}\overline{\zeta}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{3}}\right).

is compact on
X^{k-1,\beta}
.
In face, we can easily use Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
to verify that this operator respects the symmetries of
X^{k-1,\beta}
and thus that it maps
X^{k-1,\beta}
to itself. Then, observing that
\mathfrak{C}\overline{\zeta}\equiv 0
for
\zeta\in X^{k,\beta}
, we see that (
3.27
) has the structure of a commutator:

(3.28)

\zeta\mapsto-\left[\mathfrak{C},\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert^{-3}Z_{\alpha}^{2}\right](\overline{\zeta}).

Lemma 6.3 of
[
17
]
, in conjunction with Lemma
2.3
, imply that this commutator, and thus the operator (
3.27
), is compact on
X^{k-1,\beta}
.

We have now verified (
3.26
) and the proof is thus completed.
∎

4.
Local Bifurcation

We are now prepared to construct bifurcation curves of solutions to our traveling wave equation (
3.1
). These local curves represent small-amplitude traveling waves on our capillary droplet. We shall use a version of the Crandall-Rabinowitz theorem, particularly a version specialized to real analytic operators. We are largely motivated to use this framework, which has been heavily utilized in the study of the water waves problem, due to its powerful global continuation theorem
[
5
,
12
,
13
]
. The Crandall-Rabinowitz theorem we utilize is the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces and
\mathscr{F}:X\times\mathbb{R}\to Y
a real analytic mapping with
\mathscr{F}(\xi_{0},\lambda)=0\in Y
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, suppose that for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a nonzero
\xi_{0}\in X
such that

(i)

D_{\xi}\mathscr{F}(0,\lambda_{0}):X\to Y
is a Fredholm operator with index zero,

(ii)

\ker D_{\xi}\mathscr{F}(0,\lambda_{0})=\mathrm{span}\{\xi_{0}\}
,

(iii)

the transversality condition holds:

D^{2}_{\lambda\xi}\mathscr{F}(0,\lambda_{0})[\xi_{0},1]\not\in\text{\emph{ran }}D_{\xi}\mathscr{F}(0,\lambda_{0}),

It then follows that
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a bifurcation point. In particular, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and an analytic map
(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\lambda}):(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to X\times\mathbb{R}

such that
\tilde{\xi}(0)=0
,
\tilde{\lambda}(0)=\lambda_{0}
,
\tilde{\xi}^{\prime}(0)=\xi_{0}
,

\mathscr{F}(\tilde{\xi}(s),\tilde{\lambda}(s))=0\quad\text{ for all }\left\lvert s\right\rvert<\varepsilon,

and there exists an open set
U\subset X\times\mathbb{R}
with
(0,\lambda_{0})\in U
such that

\{(\xi,\lambda)\in U\setminus(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}):\mathscr{F}(\xi,\lambda)=0\}=\{(\tilde{\xi}(s),\tilde{\lambda}(s)):0<\left\lvert s\right\rvert<\varepsilon\}.

The above theorem originally goes back to the work of Crandall-Rabinowitz
[
10
]
. However, they considered operators
\mathscr{F}
of class
C^{k}
and we are interested in analytic
\mathscr{F}
. For a proof of Theorem
4.1
in the analytic framework considered here, the interested reader may consult
[
5
]
(in particular, Theorem 8.3.1). Local bifurcation (i.e., various versions of Theorem
4.1
) has long and often been applied to the study of water waves. A common application is the problem we consider here: proving the existence of small-amplitude traveling waves in various scenarios (e.g., with constant vorticity, with Hölder continuous vorticity, with discontinuous vorticity, with or without surface tension, with critical layers, with wind forcing and so on). Recall that a critical layer is a curve along which
\theta_{y}\equiv 0
(as a point where
\nabla\theta=0
is called a stagnation point). For just a few examples of local bifurcation applied to the water waves problem, the interested reader may consult
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
37
,
39
]
and the references therein.

Though bifurcation theory is a typical strategy for producing traveling wave solutions, particularly for free-surface flows of ideal fluids, it is not the only option available and, in some cases, it cannot be applied. For example, in
[
24
]
, Leoni-Tice demonstrated the existence of viscous traveling gravity-capillary waves (i.e., traveling wave solutions to the free boundary incompressible Navier-Stokes equations), however local bifurcation cannot be applied in this scenario and so the authors are forced to utilize a number of novel analytical techniques in order to overcome the various difficulties posed by the presence of viscosity.

For definiteness, in the remaining of the paper, we will fix the regularity of the domain of
\mathscr{F}
at
X^{2,\beta}
in addition to fixing the value of
m
. One could use spaces with higher regularity and obtain analytic dependence of the higher norms, but this is not the main concern of the authors. One can easily verify that
Z_{0}(w)=w\in U^{2,\beta}
and
\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)=0
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. This provides a trivial curve of solutions. In this section, we will be studying our traveling wave equation by constructing a sequence of local curves of solutions, which bifurcate from the trivial solution curve, by applying the theory of local bifurcation. Our first step in this process will be to compute the linearization of
\mathscr{F}
at the trivial solution curve.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
\zeta\in X^{2,\beta}
, denote by
\widehat{\zeta}_{k}
the (real) Fourier coefficient of
\zeta
with frequency
mk+1
, i.e.

(4.1)

\zeta(w)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widehat{\zeta}_{k}w^{mk+1}.

Then

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left(D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[\zeta]\right)(w)

\displaystyle=2\widehat{\zeta}_{0}w-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\zeta}_{k}(m^{2}k^{2}-c^{2}mk-1)w^{mk+1}.

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left(D^{2}_{cZ}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[\zeta,1]\right)(w)

\displaystyle=2c\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\zeta}_{k}mkw^{mk+1}.

Proof.

Using (
3.10
) on
Z_{0}(w)=w
, we get

(4.4)

D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[\zeta]=\mathfrak{C}\left(\zeta_{\alpha}+w^{2}\overline{\zeta_{\alpha}}\right)_{\alpha}-2i\zeta_{\alpha}+c^{2}\mathfrak{C}\left(w\mathfrak{H}(\zeta\overline{w}+\overline{\zeta}w)_{\alpha}\right).

Recalling (
2.4
) and the simple facts that
\mathfrak{C}(w^{n})=w^{n}
,
i\mathfrak{H}(\tau^{n})=\tau^{n}
,
\mathfrak{C}(w^{-n})=0
,
i\mathfrak{H}(\tau^{-n})=-\tau^{-n}
for integer
n\geq 1
, we can easily compute that

(4.5)

\zeta_{\alpha}(w)=iw\zeta^{\prime}(w)=i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widehat{\zeta}_{k}(mk+1)w^{mk+1}.

(4.6)

\left(\zeta_{\alpha}+w^{2}\overline{\zeta_{\alpha}}\right)_{\alpha}=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\zeta}_{k}(mk+1)\left[(mk+1)w^{mk+1}-(-mk+1)w^{-mk+1}\right].

(4.7)

\mathfrak{H}(\zeta\overline{w}+\overline{\zeta}w)_{\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\zeta}_{k}mk(\tau^{mk}+\tau^{-mk}).

(
4.2
) follows by
noting that
m\geq 2
. (
4.3
) follows directly from (
4.2
). All of the series above converge uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
and on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
.
∎

We can now state our main local bifurcation theorem. For simple reference, let

(4.8)

c_{mk}=\sqrt{mk-\frac{1}{mk}},\quad k=1,2,3,\dots

be the bifurcation values. Recall that
m\geq 2
is a fixed integer. We also denote the solution set and the trivial solution curve by

(4.9)

\mathcal{N}=\{(Z,c)\in U^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}:\mathscr{F}(Z,c)=0\},\quad\mathcal{T}=\{(Z_{0},c):c\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(Z_{0},c)\in\mathcal{T}
be given.

(i)

If
c\neq\pm c_{mk}
for any
k
, then no bifurcation occurs near
(Z_{0},c)
. In other words, there exists a neighborhood
V
of
(Z_{0},c)
in
U^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{N}\cap V=\mathcal{T}\cap V
.

(ii)

If
c=\pm c_{mk}
for some
k\geq 1
, then
(Z_{0},c)
is a bifurcation point. In particular, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and an analytic map
(\widetilde{Z},\widetilde{c}):(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to U^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

(a)

(\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s))\in\mathcal{N}
for all
s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
;

(b)

\widetilde{Z}(0)=Z_{0}
,
\widetilde{c}(0)=c=\pm c_{mk}
,
[\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(s)](w)=w^{mk+1}
,
\widetilde{c}^{\prime}(s)=0
;

(c)

there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
(Z_{0},c)
in
U^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
(\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{T})\cap V=\{(\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s)):0<|s|<\varepsilon\}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.5
,
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)
is Fredholm of index zero. If
c\neq\pm c_{mk}
for any
k
,
m^{2}k^{2}-c^{2}mk-1\neq 0
for any
k\geq 1
. It follows from (
4.2
) that
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c):X^{2,\beta}\to X^{0,\beta}
is injective. Therefore it is also surjective and is an isomorphism from
X^{2,\beta}
to
X^{0,\beta}
. By the implicit function theorem, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
(Z_{0},c)
in
U^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
such that for all
s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, there exists a unique
\widetilde{Z}(s)
such that
(\widetilde{Z}(s),c+s)\in\mathcal{N}\cap V
. Since
(Z_{0},c+s)\in\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{N}
. The uniqueness part of the above assertion implies that
\widetilde{Z}(s)=Z_{0}
. Thus
\mathcal{N}\cap V=\mathcal{T}\cap V
.

Now suppose
c=\pm c_{mk}
for some
k\geq 1
. It is easy to see that condition (i) in Theorem
4.1
can be adapted in our case to
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)
being Fredholm with index zero, which has been verified above. We have
m^{2}n^{2}-c^{2}mn-1=0
if
n=k
, and
m^{2}n^{2}-c^{2}mn-1\neq 0
for all other
n\geq 1
. It follows that
\ker D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)=\text{span}\{w^{mk+1}\}
, and ran
~D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)\subset\ker\langle w^{mk+1},\cdot\rangle\subset X^{2,\beta}
, where
\langle f,g\rangle=\underset{\mathbb{T}}{\text{Avg}}(\overline{f}g)
is the
L^{2}
inner product on
\mathbb{T}
. Since
\text{ran}~D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)
has codimension 1, we have ran
~D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)=\ker\langle w^{mk+1},\cdot\rangle
. By (
4.3
), we have

(D^{2}_{cZ}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[w^{mk+1},1]=2cmkw^{mk+1}.

Since
2cmk\langle w^{mk+1},w^{mk+1}\rangle\neq 0
, we have

(4.10)

(D^{2}_{cZ}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[w^{mk+1},1]\neq\text{ran }D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c).

All the conclusions in part (ii) above now follow from Theorem
4.1
, except for the equation
\widetilde{c}^{\prime}(s)=0
. To see this, we assume for the moment that
k=1
and take the second
s
-derivative of
\mathscr{F}(\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s))=0
and evaluate at
s=0
to get

(4.11)

D^{2}_{ZZ}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0),\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0)]+2\widetilde{c}^{\prime}(0)D^{2}_{cZ}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0),1]+D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)\widetilde{Z}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0.

Integrating againt
w^{m+1}
and recalling that ran
D_{Z}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)=\ker\langle w^{m+1},\cdot\rangle
, we get

(4.12)

\widetilde{c}^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{\langle w^{m+1},D^{2}_{ZZ}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0),\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0)]\rangle}{2\langle w^{m+1},D^{2}_{cZ}\mathscr{F}(Z_{0},c)[\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0),1]\rangle}

Consider the rotation map
\mathscr{R}
defined by
(\mathscr{R}Z)(w)=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{m}}Z(e^{i\frac{\pi}{m}}w)
. It is easy to see from the symmetry of
X^{k,\beta}
that
\mathscr{R}:X^{2,\beta}\to X^{2,\beta}
, and
\mathscr{R}:X^{0,\beta}\to X^{0,\beta}
. Note that the angle
\frac{\pi}{m}
above need be chosen specially to make this happen. Furthermore, as
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{H}
commute with rotation and are linear, it follows easily that
\mathscr{F}(\mathscr{R}Z,c)=\mathscr{R}\mathscr{F}(Z,c)
. Thus
\mathscr{F}(\mathscr{R}\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s))=0
for all
s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. Taking its second
s
-derivative and evaluating at
s=0
as before, we obtain an analog of (
4.12
), except that all appearances of
\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0)
are replaced by
\mathscr{R}\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0)
. Recall that
[\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0)](w)=w^{m+1}
, so we have

\mathscr{R}\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0)=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{m}}(e^{i\frac{\pi}{m}}w)^{m+1}=-w^{m+1}=-\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(0).

It follows that
\widetilde{c}^{\prime}(0)=-\widetilde{c}^{\prime}(0)
, so
\widetilde{c}^{\prime}(0)=0
. Finally, to deal with the case when
k>1
, we observe that
c_{mk}
actually only depends on the product
mk
. By uniqueness of the local bifurcation curve established above, the local solutions for the
k>1
cases are identical to those obtained in the
k=1
case where
m
is replaced by the larger integer
mk
, which we have already proven in the above argument.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

By the above proof, we see that the solutions in the bifurcation curve emanating from
(Z_{0},\pm c_{mk})
actually have
mk
-fold symmetry rather than just
m
-fold symmetry.

5.
Global Bifurcation

As of now, we have, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, two local real-analytic curves
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{k,\pm}
(one corresponding to each choice of sign of the bifurcation values
c=\pm c_{mk}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
) of small-amplitude solutions to (
2.36
). We would now like to continue each of these to a global curve of solutions
\mathcal{C}^{k,\pm}
. Our approach to this global continuation problem will be to apply the following (analytic) global bifurcation theorem
[
8
]
:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces, with
\mathscr{G}\subset X\times\mathbb{R}
open and
F:\mathscr{G}\to Y
a real-analytic map. Suppose that the following hypotheses are verified:

(H1)

(0,\lambda)\in\mathscr{G}
and
F(0,\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
;

(H2)

for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
0\neq\xi_{0}\in X
such that

(i)

\partial_{\xi}F(0,\lambda_{0})
is Fredholm of index zero,

(ii)

\ker\partial_{\xi}F(0,\lambda_{0})=\mathrm{span}\{\xi_{0}\}
,

(iii)

the transversality condition holds:

(\partial_{\lambda}\partial_{\xi}F(0,\lambda_{0}))(1,\xi_{0})\not\in\mathfrak{R}(\partial_{\xi}F(0,\lambda_{0}));

(H3)

\partial_{\xi}F(\xi,\lambda)
is Fredholm of index zero for any
(\xi,\lambda)\in\mathscr{G}
such that
F(\xi,\lambda)=0
;

(H4)

for some sequence of closed, bounded
Q_{j}\subset\mathscr{G}
with
\mathscr{G}=\bigcup_{j}Q_{j}
, the set
\{(\xi,\lambda)\in\mathscr{G}:F(\xi,\lambda)=0\}\cap Q_{N}
is compact for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Then, there exists a continuous curve
\mathcal{K}=\{(\xi(s),\lambda(s)):s\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathscr{G}
of solutions to
F(\xi,\lambda)=0
such that:

(C1)

(\xi(0),\lambda(0))=(0,\lambda_{0})
;

(C2)

\xi(s)=\xi_{0}s+\bigo(s^{2})
in
X
,
\left\lvert s\right\rvert<\varepsilon
, as
s\to 0
;

(C3)

there exists a neighborhood
\mathscr{N}
of
(0,\lambda_{0})
and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that

\{(\xi,\lambda)\in\mathscr{N}:\xi\neq 0\text{ and }F(\xi,\lambda)=0\}=\{(\xi(s),\lambda(s)):0<\left\lvert s\right\rvert<\varepsilon\};

(C4)

\mathcal{C}
is locally (real) analytic;

(C5)

one of the following alternatives occurs:

(a)

for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
s_{j}>0
such that
(\xi(s),\lambda(s))\not\in Q_{j}
for
\left\lvert s\right\rvert>s_{j}
;

(b)

there exists
\tau>0
such that
(\xi(s+\tau),\lambda(s+\tau))=(\xi(s),\lambda(s))
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

In addition, such a curve of solutions is unique modulo reparameterizations.

The proof of this theorem can be found in
[
8
]
. Originally, this result goes back to the work of Dancer in the early ’70’s
[
12
,
13
]
. It was subsequently improved by Buffoni-Toland (see Theorem 9.1.1 in
[
5
]
), however the result as stated there is erroneous. A corrected version of this theorem was then stated and proved by Constantin-Strauss-Vărvărucă
[
8
]
.

5.1.
Local Compactness of the Solution Set

Our goal is now to use Theorem
5.1
to construct the global curve of solutions for our problem. In order to do so, we will need to verify the condition
(H4)
from Theorem
5.1
. Note that conditions
(H1)
and
(H2)
have already been checked as part of constructing
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{k,\pm}
, while
(H3)
follows from Lemma
3.5
. Before we begin the work of verifying this condition in our setting, we need to do a bit of setup. We define the following collection of subsets of
U^{2,\beta}
:

(5.1)

Q_{N}\coloneqq\{(\zeta,c)\in U^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}:\left\|(\zeta,c)\right\|_{X^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}}\leq N,\ \left\|\mathscr{C}(\zeta)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq N\}\text{ for }N\in\mathbb{N}.

Notice that
Q_{N}
is closed and bounded for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
\bigcup_{N}Q_{N}=U^{2,\beta}
.

We are now ready to verify hypothesis
(H4)
, which we formulate as follows:

Lemma 5.1
.

Take
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists a
C_{N}>0
such that for any
(Z,c)\in\mathcal{N}_{N}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}\cap Q_{N}
, we have

(5.2)

\|(Z,c)\|_{X^{3,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}}\leq C_{N}.

As a result, the set
\mathcal{N}_{N}
is compact in
X^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Recall that
\mathcal{N}
is defined in (
4.9
).

Proof.

Let us take an arbitrary
(Z,c)\in\mathcal{N}_{N}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{N}
, We have
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)=0
and
\|(Z,c)\|_{X^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}}\leq N
and
\left\|\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq N
. It remains to show
\|Z\|_{C^{3,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{N}
for some
C_{N}>0
. In the following,
C_{N}
denotes a generic constants which may be enlarged from step to step. It follows from
\mathscr{F}(Z,c)=0
and Lemma
2.4
that

(5.3)

\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)_{\alpha}=iZ_{\alpha}-\frac{c^{2}}{2}Z\mathfrak{H}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}+\frac{ic^{2}}{8}\frac{[\overline{\mathfrak{C}(|Z|^{2})_{\alpha}}]^{2}}{\overline{Z}_{\alpha}}\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{T}.

The bounds on
Z
,
c
and
\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha}}
now imply that

(5.4)

\left\|\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)_{\alpha}\right\|_{C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{N}.

This implies that the curvature
\kappa=\frac{1}{iZ_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{Z_{\alpha}}{\left\lvert Z_{\alpha}\right\rvert}\right)_{\alpha}
also satisfies the bound

(5.5)

\left\|\kappa\right\|_{C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{N}.

Now, let
\sigma(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\alpha}|Z_{\alpha^{\prime}}(e^{i\alpha^{\prime}})|~d\alpha^{\prime}
be the arclength parameter of the boundary curve, and denote its inverse by
\alpha(\sigma)
. Let
\gamma(\sigma)=Z(e^{i\alpha(\sigma)})
be the arclength reparametrization of the boundary curve. Denote by
L=\int_{0}^{2\pi}|Z_{\alpha^{\prime}}(e^{i\alpha^{\prime}})|~d\alpha^{\prime}
its total arclength. We then have

(5.6)

\frac{1}{C_{N}}\leq L\leq C_{N},~\|\sigma(\alpha)\|_{C^{2,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{N},~\|\alpha(\sigma)\|_{C^{2,\beta}([0,L])}\leq C_{N}.

Let
k(\sigma)=\kappa(\alpha(\sigma))
be the curvature in arclength parameter. We have from (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) that

(5.7)

\|k\|_{C^{1,\beta}([0,L])}\leq C_{N}.

By the Frenet-Serret formulae, we can write

(5.8)

\gamma_{\sigma\sigma}=ik\gamma_{\sigma}.

It follows from (
5.7
) and regularity of solutions to (
5.8
) that

(5.9)

\|\gamma\|_{C^{3,\beta}([0,L])}\leq C_{N}.

The
C^{3,\beta}
bound of the arclength parametrization does not imply
C^{3,\beta}
bound of an arbitrary parametrization in general. However, it is enough to recover
C^{3,\beta}
bound of the
conformal
parametrization of the domain and its boundary curve. In fact, we can apply the proof (not just the statement) of Theorem 3.6 in
[
30
]
(Kellogg-Warschawski theorem) to deduce

(5.10)

\|Z\|_{C^{3,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{N}

from (
5.9
),
noting that the constants in the proof can be made uniform provided that (
5.6
), (
5.9
) hold. The proof is complete.
∎

5.2.
The Global Curve of Solutions

We now arrive at our main result:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(Z_{0},\pm c_{mk})\in\mathcal{T}
be given, where
c_{mk}
is given by (
4.8
). Then, there exists in
X^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
a real analytic curve (corresponding to each sign of
\pm c_{mk}
)

(5.11)

\mathcal{C}^{k,\pm}=\{(\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s):s\in\mathbb{R}\}

such that the following hold:

(i)

(\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s))\in\mathcal{N}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
(recall that
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{T}
are defined in (
4.9
));

(ii)

(\widetilde{Z}(0),\widetilde{c}(0))=(Z_{0},\pm c_{mk})
,
\widetilde{Z}^{\prime}(s)(w)=w^{mk+1}
;

(iii)

there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
(Z_{0},c)
in
U^{2,\beta}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
(\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{T})\cap V=\{(\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s)):0<|s|<\varepsilon\}
,
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small;

(iv)

one of the following alternatives occurs:

(a)

\max\left\{\left\|\widetilde{Z}(s)|_{\mathbb{T}}\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})},\left\|\mathscr{C}(\widetilde{Z}(s))\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\right\}\to+\infty\text{ as }\left\lvert s\right\rvert\to+\infty,

(b)

there exists
p>0
such that

(\widetilde{Z}(s+p),\widetilde{c}(s+p))=(\widetilde{Z}(s),\widetilde{c}(s))\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

This result largely follows from Theorem
5.1
. We verified the corresponding statements of
(H1)
and
(H2)
in proving the local bifurcation result of Theorem
4.2
. Moreover,
(H3)
follows from Lemma
3.5
and
(H4)
is given by Lemma
5.1
. Theorem
5.1
now implies the above conclusions with
(iv)(a)
replaced by

(5.12)

\max\left\{\left\|\widetilde{Z}(s)\right\|_{X^{2,\beta}},\left\|\mathscr{C}(\widetilde{Z}(s))\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\right\}\to+\infty\text{ as }\left\lvert s\right\rvert\to+\infty.

Thus, what remains to be proven is the improvement of
X^{2,\beta}
norm blow-up to
C^{1}(\mathbb{T})
norm blow-up.

We shall proceed in stages. Assume we are in the case when (
5.12
) occurs. Furthermore, assume that there exists a
C>0
such that
\left\|\mathscr{C}(\widetilde{Z}(s))\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq C
. In the following, we always denote such a generic constant by
C
and may change its value from step to step. We first show that
\left\|\widetilde{Z}(s)\right\|_{X^{1,\beta}}\to\infty
as
|s|\to\infty
. If not, there would exist a sequence of solutions
Z_{n}\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\left\|Z_{n}\right\|_{X^{1,\beta}}\leq C
,
\left\|\mathscr{C}(Z_{n})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq C
but
\|Z_{n}\|_{X^{2,\beta}}\to\infty
. Using (
5.3
) similarly to the proof of Lemma
5.1
, we deduce that
\kappa_{n}=\frac{1}{i(Z_{n})_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{(Z_{n})_{\alpha}}{|(Z_{n})_{\alpha}|}\right)_{\alpha}
satisfies

(5.13)

\|\kappa_{n}\|_{C^{0,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C.

As in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, denote the arclength parameter of the boundary curve
Z_{n}\big|_{\mathbb{T}}
by
\sigma_{n}
, and the inverse parametrization by
\alpha(\sigma_{n})
. Denote by
L_{n}
the total arclength, and let
\gamma_{n}(\sigma_{n})=Z_{n}(e^{i\alpha(\sigma_{n})})
. We have

(5.14)

\frac{1}{C}\leq L_{n}\leq C,~\|\sigma_{n}(\alpha)\|_{C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C,~\|\alpha(\sigma_{n})\|_{C^{1,\beta}([0,L_{n}])}\leq C.

Let
k_{n}(\sigma_{n})=\kappa_{n}(\alpha(\sigma_{n}))
be the curvature in arclength parameter. We have

(5.15)

\|k_{n}\|_{C^{0,\beta}([0,L_{n}])}\leq C.

We again deduce from the Frenet-Serret formula that

(5.16)

\|\gamma_{n}\|_{C^{2,\beta}([0,L_{n}])}\leq C,

and from the proof of Theorem 3.6 in
[
30
]
that

(5.17)

\|Z_{n}\|_{C^{2,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C.

This contradicts the assumption that
\|Z_{n}\|_{X^{2,\beta}}\to\infty
.

Assuming the same setup as in the previous stage, we now show that
\left\|\widetilde{Z}(s)\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\to\infty
as
|s|\to\infty
. If not, there would exist a sequence of solutions
Z_{n}\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\|Z_{n}\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C
,
\left\|\mathscr{C}(Z_{n})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq C
but
\|Z_{n}\|_{C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\to\infty
. This time we cannot deduce a
C^{0}(\mathbb{T})
bound on
\kappa_{n}
directly from (
5.3
), as neither
\mathfrak{C}
nor
\mathfrak{H}
is bounded on
C^{0}(\mathbb{T})
. Instead, by the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
boundedness of
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{H}
, obtain

(5.18)

\|\kappa_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C,

and

(5.19)

\frac{1}{C}\leq L_{n}\leq C,~\|\sigma_{n}(\alpha)\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C,~\|\alpha(\sigma_{n})\|_{C^{1}([0,L_{n}])}\leq C.

We deduce similary as before that

(5.20)

\|k_{n}\|_{L^{2}([0,L_{n}])}\leq C.

By the Frenet-Serret formula, we get

(5.21)

\|\gamma_{n}\|_{H^{2}([0,L_{n}])}\leq C.

By the Sobolev embedding theorem, we get

(5.22)

\|\gamma_{n}\|_{C^{1,\frac{1}{2}}([0,L_{n}])}\leq C,

which implies

(5.23)

\|Z_{n}\|_{C^{1,\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C

similarly as above. If
\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}
, this estimate contradicts the condition
\|Z_{n}\|_{C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\to\infty
, and the the proof is complete. If not, just apply the previous stage again to obtain

(5.24)

\|Z_{n}\|_{C^{2,\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C

to finish the proof.
∎

6.
Regularity of the Free Boundary

In Theorem
5.2
, we have constructed rotational traveling wave solutions whose free boundary curves have
C^{2,\beta}
regularity. However, the boundaries of any such rotational traveling waves are, in fact, real analytic. This is similar to a well-known result of Lewy’s in the case of gravity waves on the surface of a fluid layer
[
26
]
. Lewy’s result was extended to incorporate the effects of surface tension by Matei in
[
27
]
. The proof in
[
27
]
can be generalized to our setting and produce the same result.
Indeed, we have the following:

Theorem 6.1
.

Consider a simply connected
C^{2,\beta}
domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a real-valued function
\varphi\in C^{2,\beta}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
, and constants
c,Q\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma>0
, such that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ on }\mathcal{D},

(6.2)

\displaystyle(c~(-y,x)-\nabla\varphi)\cdot n

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},

(6.3)

\displaystyle-c~(-y,x)\cdot\nabla\varphi+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}-\sigma\kappa

\displaystyle=Q\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D}.

Here
n
is the outword unit normal vector on
\partial\mathcal{D}
and
\kappa
is the curvature of
\partial\mathcal{D}
. Then
\partial\mathcal{D}
is real analytic.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be a conjugate harmonic function of
\varphi
on
\mathcal{D}
. We have
\varphi_{x}=\psi_{y}
,
\varphi_{y}=-\psi_{x}
on
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
. Thus

(6.4)

\nabla\left(\frac{c}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})+\psi\right)\cdot t=0\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D}.

Here
t=n^{\perp}
is the unit tangent vector on
\partial\mathcal{D}
. It follows that there exists a constant
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(6.5)

\psi+\frac{c}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})=\mu\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D}.

Let
\Psi=\psi+\frac{c}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})
, we have

(6.6)

\displaystyle\Delta\Psi

\displaystyle=2c\quad\text{ on }\mathcal{D},

(6.7)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=\mu\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},

(6.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\Psi|^{2}-\frac{c^{2}}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})-\sigma\kappa

\displaystyle=Q\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D}.

If
c=0
, we have from (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) that
\psi=\Psi=\mu
on
\mathcal{D}
. Thus
\varphi
is constant on
\mathcal{D}
, and
\kappa=-\frac{Q}{\sigma}
on
\partial\mathcal{D}
. Thus
\partial\mathcal{D}
is a circle and is obviously analytic. In the following, we assume
c>0
without loss of generality. The proof for the case
c<0
is similar. By (
6.6
),
\Psi
is subharmonic. By (
6.7
) and the strong maximum principle,
\Psi<\mu
on
\mathcal{D}
, and
\nabla\Psi\cdot n>0
on
\partial\mathcal{D}
. Let
(x_{0},y_{0})
be an arbitrary point on
\partial\mathcal{D}
. By translation and rotation of coordinates, we may assume without loss of generality that
(x_{0},y_{0})=(0,0)
and
\partial\mathcal{D}
is tangent to the
x
-axis at
(0,0)
, with
n=(0,1)
there, while (
6.8
) is transformed to

(6.9)

\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\Psi|^{2}-\frac{c^{2}}{2}[(x+x_{0})^{2}+(y+y_{0})^{2}]-\sigma\kappa=Q\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D}.

In a neighborhood of
(x,y)=(0,0)
, introduce new coordinates
(p,q)=(x,\Psi(x,y)-\mu)
. Denote by
B_{r}
a sufficiently small disc of radius
r
centered at
(0,0)
. Since
\frac{\partial(p,q)}{\partial(x,y)}(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\nabla\Psi\cdot n(0,0)\end{pmatrix}
is invertible,
(x,y)\mapsto(p,q)
maps
B_{r}\cap\mathcal{D}
to an open subset
G
of the lower half plane, with
B_{r}\cap\partial\mathcal{D}
mapped to a line segment
S
contained in the
p
-axis. Denote the inverse of the above map by
(x,y)=(p,\eta(p,q))
. It suffices to show that
\eta
is real analytic on
B_{r^{\prime}}\cap(G\cup S)
for some
r^{\prime}>0
, as
\partial\mathcal{D}
near
(x,y)=(0,0)
is the graph of
y=\eta(x,0)
. By implicit differentiation, we obtain

(6.10)

\Psi_{x}=-\frac{\eta_{p}}{\eta_{q}},~\Psi_{y}=\frac{1}{\eta_{q}},

(6.11)

\Psi_{xx}=-\left(\frac{\eta_{p}}{\eta_{q}}\right)_{p}+\frac{\eta_{p}}{\eta_{q}}\left(\frac{\eta_{p}}{\eta_{q}}\right)_{q},~\Psi_{yy}=\frac{1}{\eta_{q}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{q}}\right)_{q},

also, when parametrizing
\kappa
by
p\subset S
, we have

(6.12)

\kappa(p)=\frac{\eta_{pp}(p,0)}{(1+\eta_{p}(p,0))^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

We obtain that
\eta
satsifies the following equation and boundary conditions on
G
:

(6.13)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\eta_{p}}{\eta_{q}}\right)_{p}+\frac{\eta_{p}}{\eta_{q}}\left(\frac{\eta_{p}}{\eta_{q}}\right)_{q}+\frac{1}{\eta_{q}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{q}}\right)_{q}=2c\quad

\displaystyle\text{ on }G,

(6.14)

\displaystyle-\sigma\frac{\eta_{pp}}{(1+\eta_{p})^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\eta_{p}^{2}}{\eta_{q}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\eta_{q}^{2}}\right)-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\left[(p+x_{0})^{2}+(\eta+y_{0}^{2})\right]=Q\quad

\displaystyle\text{ on }S.

Note that
\Psi_{x}(0,0)=0
,
\Psi_{y}(0,0)>0
. Thus
\eta_{p}(0,0)=0
,
\eta_{q}(0,0)>0
. We linearize (
6.13
) and (
6.14
) about
\eta
, freeze the coefficients at
(0,0)
and take the principle part to get the following linear equation and boundary condition on the lower half plane:

(6.15)

\displaystyle H_{pp}+\frac{1}{\eta_{q}^{2}(0,0)}H_{qq}=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }q<0,

(6.16)

\displaystyle H_{pp}=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }q=0.

We verify that (
6.15
) and (
6.16
) form an elliptic equation with coercive boundary condition for
H
(see
[
27
]
,
[
20
]
for definition). Ellipticity is obvious. For coerciveness, we look for solutions of the form
H(p,q)=e^{i\lambda p}I(q)
with
\lambda\neq 0
that is exponentially decaying for
q<0
. We obtain

(6.17)

\displaystyle I^{\prime\prime}(q)-\lambda^{2}\eta_{q}^{2}(0,0)=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }q<0,

(6.18)

\displaystyle I(0)=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }q=0.

We get
I(q)=C(e^{\lambda\eta_{q}(0,0)q}-e^{-\lambda\eta_{q}(0,0)q})
with
C\in\mathbb{R}
. The exponential decay condition forces
C=0
. Thus the boundary condition (
6.16
) is coercive for (
6.15
). It follows from Theorem 6.7.6’ in
[
28
]
that
\eta
is real analytic in
B_{r^{\prime}}\cap(G\cup S)
for some small
r^{\prime}>0
. The proof is complete.
