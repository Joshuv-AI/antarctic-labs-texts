On the Garden of Eden theorem for ℬ \mathscr{B} -free subshifts
G. Keller, M. Lemańczyk, C. Richard, D. Sell

Abstract
We prove that on ℬ \mathscr{B} -free subshifts, with ℬ \mathscr{B} satisfying the Erdös condition, all cellular automata are determined by monotone sliding block codes. In particular, this implies the validity of the Garden of Eden theorem for such systems.

Introduction

1.1
Sets of multiples and
\mathscr{B}
-free sets

For a set
\mathscr{B}
of natural numbers, consider its multiples
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{B}}=\bigcup_{b\in\mathscr{B}}b\mathbb{Z}
. Interest in sets of multiples arose in the 1930’s from
abundant integers
, i.e., integers
n
whose sum of proper divisors is larger than or equal to
|n|
. It is readily seen that abundant integers are closed under multiplication by integers, hence they indeed constitute a set of multiples. See
[
15
, Sec. 11]
for further properties. In the following we restrict, without loss of generality and without further mentioning, to
primitive

\mathscr{B}
, i.e., no element from
\mathscr{B}
divides any other element from
\mathscr{B}
. We also exclude the trivial case
\mathscr{B}=\{1\}
from now on.
1
1

1

This is done for convenience, see the discussion in Section
2.5
.

A rich class of sets of multiples is provided by the case of
\mathscr{B}
being
Erdös
, where
\mathscr{B}
is assumed infinite which excludes periodic sets of multiples. Moreover,
\mathscr{B}
is assumed coprime and
thin
, i.e.,
\sum_{b\in\mathscr{B}}1/b<\infty
. The latter guarantees that the multiples have a natural density and motivates the attribution to Erdös, see the discussion in Chapter 1 of
[
19
]
.

Intimately related to sets of multiples are the sets
\mathcal{F}_{\mathscr{B}}=\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{B}}
of
\mathscr{B}
-free integers
. In particular, the abundant integers yield the
deficient integers
. Over the last years,
\mathscr{B}
-free integers have been intensely studied from a dynamical perspective, see
[
15
]
for a detailed account. Historically, the guiding example seems
square-free integers
, which is obtained from the Erdös set
\mathscr{B}=\{p^{2}:p\text{ prime}\}
. On one side, the influential article by Sarnak
[
41
]
listed, among other results, a number of dynamical properties of the subshift generated by the square-free integers. On the other side, square-free integers are pure point diffractive
[
6
,
40
]
, in spite of their apparent irregularity. As mathematical diffraction theory
[
2
]
was developing from the 1990’s onwards, a dynamical perspective on diffraction emerged, since pure point diffractivity had been shown to be equivalent to discrete dynamical spectrum of the subshift generated by the structure
[
5
]
, when equipped with its natural pattern frequency measure.
In fact, squarefree integers
[
37
, Ch. 10]
and general
\mathscr{B}
-free integers are examples of Meyer’s weak model sets, and this viewpoint was very fruitful not only for analysing the
\mathscr{B}
-free subshift dynamics
[
21
,
23
,
24
]
, but also for developing a dynamical structure theory of general weak model sets
[
25
,
27
,
26
,
24
]
. Moreover,
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts admit natural multidimensional generalizations to lattices and number fields
[
3
,
8
,
14
]
, some of which may serve as useful models in physics.

Despite their exceptional naturality,
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts display an impressive dynamical diversity varying from proximality in the Erdös case to minimality, which leads to the classical theory of Toeplitz subshifts
[
15
]
. In both cases their entropy can be both zero or positive. In this paper we deal with the proximal case, with initial focus on Erdös sets. In this latter case, on one hand, the associated dynamical systems exhibit properties characteristic of the full shift: their entropy is positive, their set of invariant measures is the Poulsen simplex
[
32
,
29
]
, and they are intrinsically ergodic
[

]
. Moreover, they are hereditary: if

belongs to the subshift
[

. On the other hand, the unique measure of maximal entropy is not Gibbs

]
, a rather surprising property that was first discovered for the square-free subshift
[
39
]
. Moreover, their automorphism group is trivial, see below.

1.2
Endomorphisms of subshifts

In recent years, some effort has been made in order to understand the endomorphism group
{\rm End}(X,S)
and the automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(X,S)
of a subshift
(X,S)
. In the proximal case, unless
|X|=1
,
{\rm End}(X,S)
is larger than
{\rm Aut}(X,S)
, as the map sending each point to the fixed point of
S
is a member of
{\rm End}(X,S)
. Clearly, both
{\rm End}(X,S)
and
{\rm Aut}(X,S)
are valuable topological conjugacy invariants. For Erdös
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts
(X,S)
, Mentzen’s theorem
[
36
]
states that
{\rm Aut}(X,S)
is trivial, i.e., it consists only of the powers of the shift. Whereas Mentzen’s proof was based on arithmetic arguments, an extension beyond the Erdös case by dynamical methods was recently given in
[
24
, Thm. 4.1]
. See also
[
4
]
for a multidimensional generalisation. In the Toeplitz case, only partial results are available
[
13
,
16
]
.

In this article we study endomorphisms of proximal
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts. We adopt here language from the theory of cellular automata
[
17
]
and call an endomorphism of a subshift
(X,S)
a
Cellular Automaton
(CA). By the classical Curtis-Lyndon-Hedlund (CLH) theorem
[
35
, Thm. 6.2.9]
, each CA
\tau:X\to X
is given by a
(sliding block) code

\phi:\{0,1\}^{\{-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}}\to\{0,1\}
for some
\ell\geq 0
via the formula
2
2

2

It is implicit here that we consider only those blocks
B\in\{0,1\}^{\{-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}}
that appear in some
x\in X
.

(1)

\tau(x)(n)=\phi(x(n-\ell,n+\ell))\text{ for all }x\in X\text{ and all }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

It follows that
{\rm End}(X,S)
is countable, which directed a lot of effort towards classifying all possible countable groups that can appear as invertible CA of a subshift
[
9
,
11
]
. Let us restrict to hereditary subshifts. In that case, if a code
\phi
is
monotone
, i.e.,
\phi(B)\leq B(0)
for each block
B\in\{0,1\}^{\{-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}}
, then the induced map
\tau
is an endomorphism. (Note that monotone codes for hereditary
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts are already mentioned in the preprint version of
[
13
]
.) The full shift shows that the converse does not hold: it is hereditary, but there are CA, considered modulo the powers of the shift, which do not arise from monotone codes. The following two questions are natural:

(i)

When is every CA given by a monotone code, up to a power of the shift?

(ii)

How to read off non-invertibility properties of a CA from its code?

1.3
Results of the paper

Let
\tau
be a CA on
(X,S)
. The elements of
X\setminus\tau(X)
are called
Garden of Eden (GoE) configurations
in the theory of cellular automata, see
[
7
]
for a recent review. The name arises from the fact that such configurations can only occur at time
0
, when considering iterates
\tau^{k}(X_{0})
of an initial set
X_{0}\subset X
. A surjective CA does not admit any GoE configuration. In that context, a crucial property of a CA is a weakened form of injectivity: A CA is
pre-injective
if it is injective on every collection of configurations that mutually differ in at most finitely many coordinates. One says
“the Garden of Eden theorem holds on some subshift”
, if for any CA on this subshift surjectivity is equivalent to pre-injectivity. The implication that surjectivity implies pre-injectivity is also called the
Moore property
, the reverse implication is called the
Myhill property
.
It is known that the GoE holds for irreducible subshifts of finite type
[
7
, Thm. 7.15]
and that any strongly irreducible subshift satisfies the Myhill property
[
7
, Thm. 7.10]
.
In our setting, which is not subsumed by the above results, the GoE theorem holds in particular for the class of Erdös
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts. In fact, our main result in the Erdös case even answers the two questions raised above.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
\mathscr{B}
is an Erdös set. Then
the corresponding
\mathscr{B}
-free subshift satisfies the GoE theorem. If a CA is onto, then it equals a power of the shift. Moreover, every CA is given by a monotone code, modulo a power of the shift.

Our result states a very strong form of the Moore property, which implies Mentzen’s theorem that the automorphism group of an Erdös
\mathscr{B}
-free subshift is trivial.

Subsequently, the paper discusses extensions beyond the Erdös case. One extension is motivated by the well-known observation that, in the Erdös case, the
\mathscr{B}
-free subshift
(X_{\eta},S)
coincides with the so-called
\mathscr{B}
-admissible subshift
(X_{\mathscr{B}},S)
, which is arithmetic in nature and explained in Section
2
. In that sense the Erdös case is “very arithmetic” from a dynamical point of view. Theorem
4.8
below states the GoE theorem and the extension of Mentzen’s theorem for the setting
X_{\eta}=X_{\mathscr{B}}
, which reduces to Theorem
1.1
in the Erdös setting.

In general (remembering that
\mathscr{B}\neq\{1\}
), the condition
X_{\eta}=X_{\mathscr{B}}
implies that
\mathscr{B}
is infinite and coprime
[
22
]
. In that setting, a positive topological entropy of the
\mathscr{B}
-free subshift is equivalent to
\mathscr{B}
being thin
[
1
]
. Hence positive topological entropy characterises the Erdös case in that setting. The case of zero topological entropy corresponds to the coprime
Behrend
case, i.e., to the
\mathscr{B}
-free sets of density zero where
\mathscr{B}
is coprime. Examples of the latter type are discussed in
[
22
]
.

The above setting is subsumed by the class of hereditary
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts. Using arithmetic arguments, we extend Mentzen’s theorem to general hereditary
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts in Theorem
5.1
, which contains
[
24
, Thm. 4.1]
as a special case. Concerning GoE properties, we can prove the Myhill property if the subshift has a unique measure of maximal entropy of full topological support, see Proposition
4.5
. (In fact the Myhill property holds for an arbitrary hereditary subshift of the latter form.) We can prove the Moore property under a certain non-degeneracy assumption on the CA, see Lemma
6.5
.

Let us restrict to hereditary
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts where
\mathscr{B}
is taut, see Section
2.3
below for a definition. By
[
23
, Thm. 3]
, heredity is equivalent to proximality in the taut case. As thin implies taut, this also generalises the Erdös case. Here, the situation is more transparent, as the first two assertions of Theorem
1.1
hold and we can characterise the surjectivity in several ways, see Theorem
6.1
. An example (which is not Erdös) in that class is given by the deficient integers, compare
[
15
, Sec. 11]
.

We finally discuss the general proximal case, i.e.,
\mathscr{B}
contains an infinite coprime set
[
15
, Thm. B]
. This case contains all Behrend
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts, which are characterised by being proximal and having zero entropy.
In fact, we can reduce the analysis of the automorphism group to that of a naturally associated
hereditary
subsystem, which is a taut
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
-subshift, without changing the density. Recalling that
\mathscr{B}
is Behrend if the density of
\mathscr{B}
-free integers is zero, it seems appropriate to call this reduction the
relative Behrend
case.

2.1
Some generalities on subshifts

Consider the compact space
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
of bi-infinite
0-1
-sequences, equipped with the usual product metric
d
. The
support
of
x\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
is the set
{supp}(x)=\{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}:\>x(j)\neq 0\}
.
The
(left) shift

S:\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}\to\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
, defined by
(Sx)(j)=x(j+1)
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, is a homeomorphism. If
X\subset\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
, which we always assume to be non-empty, is closed and
S
-invariant then the resulting dynamical system
(X,S)
is called a
subshift
.
X
is called
hereditary
, if
y\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
belongs to
X
whenever there is some
x\in X
such that
y\leq x
coordinatewise. Denote by
\widetilde{X}\subset\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
the smallest hereditary set containing
X
. Given a subshift
(X,S)
, we call the subshift
(\widetilde{X},S)
its
hereditary closure
. We call
x,y\in X

asymptotic
if
d(S^{r}x,S^{r}y)\to 0
for
|r|\to\infty
. It is easily seen that
x,y
are asymptotic if and only if they differ in at most finitely many coordinates. If
d(S^{r_{k}}x,S^{r_{k}}y)\to 0
along a sequence
(r_{k})
that tends to infinity, then
x,y
are called
proximal
. A subshift
(X,S)
is called
proximal
if any two elements from
X
are proximal.

Denote by
M(X,S)
the simplex of
S
-invariant probability measures on
(X,S)
, which is non-empty by the Krylov-Bogolioubov theorem, see e.g.
[
42
, Cor. 6.9.1]
. Each
\nu\in M(X,S)
is determined by its values
\nu(B)
on all finite
blocks
or
words

B\in\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\{0,1\}^{m}
. More precisely, we identify any block
B\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, where
|B|=m
is the
length
of
B
, with the corresponding cylinder set
\{x\in X:\>x(0,m-1)=B\}
, and thus the set of all finite blocks generates the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
X
.
Given
\nu\in M(X,S)
and a sequence
(N_{k})
, the element
x\in X
is
generic along
(N_{k})
for
\nu
if

(2)

\int f\,d\nu=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}f(S^{n}x)

for each
f\in C(X)
. If
(N_{k})=(k)
, then
x
is called
generic for
\nu
. Recall that by the variational principle, see e.g.
[
12
, Sec. 7.4]
or
[
42
, Thm. 8.6]
, the topological entropy
h(X,S)
is the supremum of the measure-theoretic entropies
h_{\nu}(X,S)
with
\nu\in M(X,S)
. Note that for a subshift the supremum is in fact attained, see e.g.
[
12
, Fact 7.2.4]
or
[
42
, Thms. 8.2, 8.7]
. A subshift
(X,S)
is called
intrinsically ergodic

[
43
]
if there is precisely one measure of maximal entropy.

2.2
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts and their hereditary closures

We recall that throughout we assume that
\mathscr{B}\subset{\mathbb{N}}
does not contain 1 (and is primitive), if not stated otherwise. Consider a set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathscr{B}}={\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{B}}
of
\mathscr{B}
-free integers. By the Davenport-Erdös theorem, see e.g.
[

, Thm. 0.2]

\mathscr{B}
-free integers possess the logarithmic density

\delta({\cal F}_{\mathscr{B}})=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{n\in{\cal F}_{\mathscr{B}}\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}}\frac{1}{n}\ ,

which coincides with its natural upper density. In the Erdös case, the natural density exists, see e.g.
[
19
, Sec. 1.1]
. Let
\eta=\mathbbm{1}_{{\cal F}_{\mathscr{B}}}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the characteristic function of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathscr{B}}
and
define
X_{\eta}=\overline{\{S^{r}\eta:\>r\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}}\subset\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
The system
(X_{\eta},S)
is the corresponding
\mathscr{B}
-
free subshift
. Due to its particular arithmetic origin, the point
\eta
enjoys the following
maximality
property
3
3

3

Maximality arguments have been used before e.g. in
[
3
, p. 186]
.
. As this property is crucial in later arguments, we provide its short proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(X_{\eta},S)
be a
\mathscr{B}
-free subshift. If
\eta\leq y\in X_{\eta}
then
\eta=y
.

Proof.

Assume for a contradiction that
\eta\neq y
. Then
{\rm supp}(y)
contains some integer
m
that is not
\mathscr{B}
-free. Hence
m=bn
for some
b\in\mathscr{B}
and some
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Since
\eta\leq y
, we have in particular
|{\rm supp}(\eta)\text{ mod }b|<|{\rm supp}(y)\text{ mod }b|
. This is contradictory, as there is a finite word
w

such that
|{\rm supp}(w)\text{ mod }b|=|{\rm supp}(y)\text{ mod }b|
. Indeed, for every
r\in{\rm supp}(y)\text{ mod }b

. Then take a finite word in

A distinguished measure on
(X_{\eta},S)
is the so-called

\nu_{\eta}
. The point
\eta
is generic along a subsequence for
\nu_{\eta}
, see

is a zero entropy system, which is ergodic and has discrete spectrum

15

\mathscr{B}
-free subshift, no formula for its topological entropy is known. However, a non-trivial upper bound can be given using the description as a weak model set

is attained. The following results appear as Proposition K, Theorem J and Remark 10.2 in

2.3
Tautness in
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts

The notion of tautness, which arises naturally in the theory of sets of multiples

, Def. 0.11]
, also turned out to be important for
\mathscr{B}
-free sets. Recall that
\mathscr{B}
is

\delta({\cal F}_{\mathscr{B}})<\delta({\cal F}_{\mathscr{B}\setminus\{b\}})\text{ for each }b\in\mathscr{B}.

It is easy to see that
\mathscr{B}
thin implies
\mathscr{B}
taut. In particular, the Erdös case is taut. From a dynamical point of view, the role of tautness is seen in the following three results. The first one combines
[
30
, Cor. 1.11]
with
[

, Thm. 2]
, which is based on a measure-theoretic characterisation of tautness in terms of weak model sets
[
21
, Thm. A]
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
23
]
,
[
30
]
)
.

\mathscr{B}
is taut if and only if
{\rm supp}(\nu_{\eta})=X_{\eta}
.

The second result is Theorem C in
[
15
]
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
15
]
)
.

For any
\mathscr{B}
there exists a unique taut
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}

such that
\eta^{\prime}\leq\eta
,
X_{\eta^{\prime}}\subset\widetilde{X}_{\eta}
,
\nu_{\eta}=\nu_{\eta^{\prime}}
,
M(\widetilde{X}_{\eta},S)=M(\widetilde{X}_{\eta^{\prime}},S)
, in particular,

h(\widetilde{X}_{\eta},S)=h(\widetilde{X}_{\eta^{\prime}},S)
.

The third result combines
[
15
, Thm. B]
and
[
23
, Thm. 3]
. It relates proximality, which is an invariant of topological conjugacy, to heredity, which serves as a useful tool for analysis of
\mathscr{B}
-free sets, but lacks invariance in general.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
23
]
)
.

Assume that
\mathscr{B}
is taut. Then

(X_{\eta},S)
is proximal if and only if
(X_{\eta},S)
is hereditary.

2.4
Proximal
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts and Behrend subshifts

Several characterizations of proximality of
\mathscr{B}
-free subshifts can be found in
[
15

[
21

. We mention here that proximality is equivalent to the property that the all zero sequence

belongs to

, or to the existence of an infinite coprime subset of
\mathscr{B}
. Let us call
\mathscr{B}

proximal
if the induced
\mathscr{B}
-free subshift is proximal.
A particular class of proximal systems is obtained when we consider Behrend sets

(X_{\eta},S)
is proximal and uniquely ergodic
[
15
, Rem. 6.3]
. In particular, it has zero entropy.

Let us recall the following result, which is implicit in the proof of Eqn. (25) on page 13 in
[
30
]
. Its proof is a rather direct consequence of Theorem

Proposition 2.6

(
[
30
]
)
.

For any
\mathscr{B}
and the unique taut
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
as in Theorem

X_{\eta^{\prime}}\subset X_{\eta}
.

This can be used to obtain the following new result about proximal

-free systems. The proximal case will further be studied in Section

Corollary 2.7
.

If

Proof.

Monotonicity in
\mathscr{B}
-admissible subshifts

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
\mathscr{B}
is coprime and infinite. Let
u\in\{0,1\}^{\{k,\ldots,k+\lvert u\rvert-1\}}
, with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, be a
\mathscr{B}
-admissible word. Let
N_{u}:=\mathopen{\rvert}\Supp(u)\mathclose{\lvert}
denote the cardinality of its support. Let
b_{0}\in\mathscr{B}
be such that
b_{0}>2\lvert u\rvert
. Then there exist a
\mathscr{B}
-admissible word
w_{u}
and a set
M_{u}=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{b_{0}-N_{u}}\}
of positions in
w_{u}
with
w_{u}(m_{i}+k,m_{i}+k+\lvert u\rvert-1)=u
for every

, such that
M_{u}\bmod b_{0}=\{0,\ldots,b_{0}-1\}\setminus(-\Supp(u)\bmod b_{0})
holds. Moreover, we have
\Supp(w_{u})\bmod b_{0}=\{1,\ldots,b_{0}-1\}
and
\mathopen{\rvert}\Supp(w_{u})\mathclose{\lvert}\leq(b_{0}-N_{u})N_{u}

Proof.

First we note that, because of
b_{0}>\lvert u\rvert
, the elements of
\Supp(u)\subseteq\{k,\ldots,k+\lvert u\rvert-1\}
are pairwise different modulo
b_{0}
. Moreover,
b_{0}
is coprime to
P:=\prod_{b\in\mathscr{B}\,\setminus\{b_{0}\},\,b\leq L}b
, where we choose
L\geq N_{u}(b_{0}-N_{u})
so that
P>|u|
. We define

M_{u}=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{b_{0}-N_{u}}\}\subseteq\{nP:n=0,\ldots,b_{0}-1\}

to be those
(b_{0}-N_{u})
-many numbers that satisfy
m_{i}\bmod b_{0}\notin(-\Supp(u)\bmod b_{0})
. Consequently, none of the sets
m_{i}+\Supp(u)
intersects the zero residue class modulo
b_{0}
. Now, we define
w_{u}
as the word that is given by
\Supp(w_{u}):=\bigcup\nolimits_{i=1}^{b_{0}-N_{u}}(m_{i}+\Supp(u))\subseteq\{k,\ldots,(b_{0}-1)P+k+\lvert u\rvert-1\}
. In particular,
w_{u}
consists of
b_{0}-N_{u}
shifted copies of
u
at positions
m_{i}
, which lie in all residue classes modulo
b_{0}
except
-\Supp(u)
. This implies that
\mathopen{\rvert}\Supp(w_{u})\mathclose{\lvert}\leq(b_{0}-N_{u})N_{u}
. Now, we check that
w_{u}
is
\mathscr{B}
-admissible:

•

Since none of the sets
m_{i}+\Supp(u)
intersects the zero residue class modulo
b_{0}
, also
\Supp(w_{u})
is not intersecting the zero residue class modulo
b_{0}
.

•

Let
b\in\mathscr{B}\,\setminus\{b_{0}\}
with
b\leq L
. Since
u
is
\mathscr{B}
-admissible, there is a residue class modulo
b
that
\Supp(u)
does not intersect. Since all positions of
\Supp(u)
in

differ by a multiple of
b
, there is a residue class modulo
b
that
\Supp(w_{u})
does not intersect.

•

Finally, let

. As mentioned above,
\mathopen{\rvert}\Supp(w_{u})\mathclose{\lvert}\leq(b_{0}-N_{u})N_{u}<b
, so
\Supp(w_{u})
cannot intersect all residue classes modulo

.

To show the remaining assertion, i.e. that
\Supp(w_{u})
intersects all residue classes modulo
b_{0}
except the zero class, note that for every
j\in\Supp(u)
, the set

intersects by construction all residue classes except those in

. Therefore, if
\mathopen{\rvert}\Supp(u)\mathclose{\lvert}=1
, we are done (as
j-\Supp(u)=\{0\}
). For
\mathopen{\rvert}\Supp(u)\mathclose{\lvert}>1
, let
j_{1}
and

denote respectively the smallest and the largest element of

. Note that all elements in

are non-positive, while all elements in

are non-negative. Because of

, the zero residue class is the only residue class that all sets
\bigcup\nolimits_{i=1}^{b_{0}-N_{u}}(j+m_{i})
miss.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume that
\mathscr{B}
is coprime and infinite. Let

Proof of the Moore property

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
X_{\eta}=X_{\mathscr{B}}
. Let
\tau\colon X_{\eta}\to X_{\eta}
be a surjective CA, given by the code
\phi\colon\{0,1\}^{\{-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}}\to\{0,1\}
. Then there exists
t\in\{-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}
with
\tau=S^{t}
. In particular,
(X_{\eta},S)
satisfies the Moore property.

Proof.

Since
\tau
is surjective, there exists
z\in X_{\eta}
with
\tau(z)=\eta
. Since
X_{\eta}=X_{\mathscr{B}}
, the set
\mathscr{B}
is coprime by
[
22
]
. Moreover,
\mathscr{B}
is infinite, since otherwise the periodicity of
\eta
would imply a finite set
X_{\eta}
, while
X_{\mathscr{B}}
is always uncountable. Hence Proposition
3.2
yields
\eta=\tau(z)\leq S^{t}z
for some
t\in\{-\ell,\ldots,\ell\}
. Since
\eta
is a maximal element in
X_{\eta}
by Lemma
2.1
, we obtain
\eta=S^{t}z
. Moreover,
\tau
commutes with the shift, which yields
\tau(\eta)=\tau(S^{t}z)=S^{t}\tau(z)=S^{t}\eta
. Since
\eta
is a transitive point, the result follows.

.

. But there are also coprime Behrend sets for which we have this equality

]
.

Hereditary subshifts and the Myhill property

4.1
A discussion of pre-injectivity

The following weakening of injectivity due to Gromov is standard in the theory of cellular automata
[
7
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A CA
\tau
on a subshift
(X,S)
is
pre-injective
if, for any
x,y\in X
, the property
\tau(x)=\tau(y)
implies that either
x=y
or that
x
differs from
y
at infinitely many coordinates.

In order to get acquainted with the notion of pre-injectivity, consider the following two auxiliary results. The first one addresses the interplay between (pre-)injectivity and monotonicity.

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider some hereditary set
X_{0}\subset\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of
0-1
-sequences having finite support. Assume that
\tau:X_{0}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is injective and monotone, i.e.,
\tau(x)\leq x
coordinatewise for any
x\in X_{0}
. Then
\tau
is the identity map.

Proof.

As
X_{0}
is hereditary and as
\tau
is monotone, we have
\tau(X_{0})\subset X_{0}
. We can thus consider iterates of
\tau
. Consider any
x\in X_{0}
. As
x
has at most finitely many nonzero coordinates and as
\tau
is monotone, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\tau^{n+1}(x)=\tau^{n}(x)
. Now, the injectivity of
\tau
yields
\tau(x)=x
.

The next result is probably well-known. As we were not able to trace a reference which applies to our setting, we also provide its proof. We say that
\tau:X\to Y
is bounded-to-one if there exists a finite constant
B
such that
|\tau^{-1}(\{y\})|\leq B
for all
y\in Y
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(X,S)
be a hereditary subshift with a CA
\tau
. If
\tau
is pre-injective, then
\tau
is bounded-to-one.

Proof.

Recall that if we consider distinct
x,y\in X
such that
\tau(x)=\tau(y)
then by pre-injectivity assumption,
x
and
y
differ at infinitely many positions.

We claim that there exists a universal constant
\delta>0
independent of
x,y
with the following property: If
x
and
y
differ at infinitely many positive positions, we have
d(S^{r}x,S^{r}y)\geq\delta
for all but finitely many
r\in\mathbb{N}
. If
x
and
y
differ at infinitely many negative positions, we have
d(S^{-r}x,S^{-r}y)\geq\delta
for all but finitely many
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

The above claim implies the statement of the lemma by the following argument. Fix any
x\in X
and assume that
\tau^{-1}(\{x\})
contains
N
elements
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
. As
X
is compact, we find
\{z_{1}^{+},z_{1}^{-},\ldots,z_{N}^{+},z_{N}^{-}\}\subset X
and an increasing sequence
(r_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of natural numbers such that
S^{r_{k}}x_{i}\to z_{i}^{+}
and
S^{-r_{k}}x_{i}\to z_{i}^{-}
for all
i
. This may be seen by an iterative argument taking subsequences. Now, for any
i\neq j
the claim implies
d(z_{i}^{+},z_{j}^{+})\geq\delta
or
d(z_{i}^{-},z_{j}^{-})\geq\delta
, by the
continuity of the metric. Thus by the compactness of
X
, the number
N
cannot be arbitrarily large and is bounded uniformly in
X
. Hence
|\tau^{-1}(\{x\})|
can be bounded by some finite number that does not depend on the choice of
x
.

As the map
\tau
is a CA, it can be described by a code of length
\ell\geq 0
as in (
1
).
Choose
\delta_{\ell}>0
sufficiently small such that for
x,y\in X
we have
x(-4\ell,4\ell)=y(-4\ell,4\ell)
whenever

. We prove the above claim for

by an indirect argument. Consider distinct
x,y\in X
such that
\tau(x)=\tau(y)
. Assume without loss of generality that
x
and
y
differ at infinitely many positive positions. In order to argue for contradiction, assume that there exists an increasing sequence of natural numbers
(r_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
d(S^{r_{k}}x,S^{r_{k}}y)<\delta_{\ell}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. By our choice of
\delta_{\ell}
, we then have

(3)

x(r_{k}-4\ell,r_{k}+4\ell)=y(r_{k}-4\ell,r_{k}+4\ell)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. As
x
and
y
differ at infinitely many positive positions, we may select
k_{0}<k_{1}
such that

(4)

x(r_{k_{0}}-4\ell,r_{k_{1}}+4\ell)\neq y(r_{k_{0}}-4\ell,r_{k_{1}}+4\ell).

Let
D=\{r_{k_{0}}-4\ell,\ldots,r_{k_{1}}+4\ell\}
and define
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
by
x^{\prime}=x\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{D}
and
y^{\prime}=y\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{D}
, the dot denoting coordinatewise multiplication. Then
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
differ due to (

), but they differ at finitely many positions only. Moreover,

by heredity. On the other hand we have
\tau(x^{\prime})=\tau(y^{\prime})
, which contradicts the pre-injectivity of

\tau

Lemma 6.3

In other words, in the general proximal case we reduced the problem of determining

What is the automorphism group of a
\mathscr{B}
-free system in the Behrend case itself?

9

[
