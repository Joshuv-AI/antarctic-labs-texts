Boundary Layer Estimates in Stochastic Homogenization
Peter Bella, Julian Fischer, Marc Josien, Claudia Raithel

Abstract
We prove quantitative decay estimates for the boundary layer corrector in stochastic homogenization in the case of a half-space boundary. Our estimates are of optimal order and show that the gradient of the boundary layer corrector features nearly fluctuation-order decay; its expected value decays even one order faster. As a corollary, we deduce estimates on the accuracy of the representative volume element method for the computation of effective coefficients: our understanding of the decay of boundary layers enables us to improve the order of convergence of the RVE method for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . † † This project has received funding from the European Research Council (ERC) under the European Union’s Horizon 2020 research and innovation programme (grant agreement No 948819).

Introduction

Both in periodic homogenization and in stochastic homogenization, the presence of a domain boundary should be expected to lead to intricate effects and substantial new challenges: In the periodic setting, the domain boundary breaks the periodic structure, while in stochastic homogenization, the domain boundary breaks the stationarity (invariance with respect to spatial shifts) of the problem.

Indeed, in particular in quantitative homogenization theory on bounded domains, it is important to understand the impact of boundary layers on the solution: For instance, for periodic coefficient fields
a(\cdot)=a(\cdot+k),k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with
a=a^{*}
, solutions to the linear elliptic PDE on the whole space

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\big(a(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\big)=\nabla\cdot f\quad\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{d}

(1.1)

may be approximated by the solution to a constant-coefficient effective equation
-\nabla\cdot(\bar{a}\nabla\bar{u})=\nabla\cdot f
up to an error of second order
1
1

1

Provided that the right-hand side
f
is sufficiently regular.

\displaystyle||u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}||_{H^{-1}(B_{1})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}.

(1.2)

In contrast, the analogous estimate
fails
for the Dirichlet problem on bounded domains, even for the homogeneous Dirichlet problem and even strictly in the interior of the domain: On bounded domains the estimate
||u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}||_{L^{p}}\lesssim\varepsilon
cannot be improved by passing to weak norms.
The underlying reason for this failure is boundary layer effects that provide an
\varepsilon
-order contribution to the
effective boundary data
for
\bar{u}
.

On a mathematical level, the failure of the derivation of estimates like (
1.2
) in the case of the Dirichlet problem on domains may be seen as follows:
In order to prove an estimate like (
1.2
), one attempts to approximate
u_{\varepsilon}
by the so-called two-scale expansion; written up to second order, it reads for periodic and symmetric
a=a^{*}

\displaystyle\bar{u}(x)+\varepsilon\phi_{i}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})\partial_{i}\bar{u}(x)+\varepsilon^{2}\psi_{ij}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\bar{u}(x),

(1.3)

where
\phi_{i}
and
\psi_{ij}
are the so-called first-order and second-order homogenization correctors.
The homogenization corrector
\phi_{i}
is defined as the
1
-periodic solution to the PDE
-\nabla\cdot(a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}))=0
; note that it is unique up to additive constants. If we attempt to apply the two-scale expansion ansatz (
1.3
) on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we immediately observe that it fails to attain the correct boundary data
\bar{u}
on the domain boundary. While the second-order term
O(\varepsilon^{2})
does not affect the accuracy, the first-order contribution
\varepsilon\phi_{i}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})\partial_{i}\bar{u}(x)
must be corrected for. In other words, we would need to subtract a term of the form
\varepsilon v(x)
with
v
solving

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\big(a\big(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}\big)\nabla v\big)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle v(x)

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})\partial_{i}\bar{u}(x)

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega.

This is an example of the so-called
oscillating Dirichlet problem
: The boundary data oscillates on the same scale as the coefficient field. As a consequence, the interactions of the oscillations in the boundary data and in the coefficient field give rise to quite nontrivial nonlinear interactions; it turns out that under suitable assumptions,
v
may be described in the interior in terms of a suitable
\bar{a}
-harmonic function with certain
effective boundary conditions
on
\partial\Omega
. Note that in particular, these effective boundary conditions are not given in terms of a simple average of the boundary data.

In the context of regularity theory for linear elliptic operators with oscillating coefficients, a particularly important instance of an oscillating Dirichlet problem is given by the
boundary layer corrector

\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
, defined as the solution to the problem

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\big(a\big(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon}\big)\nabla\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}\big)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega.

By its definition, subtracting the boundary layer corrector
\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
from the whole space corrector
\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x):=\phi_{i}(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon})
corrects the boundary conditions of
\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}
while maintaining the defining corrector equation
-\nabla\cdot(a(e_{i}+\nabla(\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon})))=0
– in other words,
\phi_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
yields a first-order homogenization corrector in
\Omega
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
.

Quantitative estimates on boundary layers in stochastic homogenization.

In the present work, we establish quantitative estimates on the decay of the boundary layer corrector
\theta_{i}
on a half-space
\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
, given as the solution to the PDE

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\quad\,\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+},

(1.4)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}

\displaystyle\text{on }\quad\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}.

More precisely, we consider an ensemble
\langle\cdot\rangle
of coefficient fields
a
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 3
(that is, a probability measure on the space of coefficient fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\varepsilon>0
in form of a spectral gap inequality. Under an additional small-scale regularity condition, we show that the boundary layer corrector decays as

\displaystyle\big|\nabla\theta_{i}(x)\big|\leq\mathcal{C}\bigg(\frac{\varepsilon}{\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+})}\bigg)^{d/2-\delta}

(1.5)

with a random prefactor
\mathcal{C}
subject to uniform (stretched exponential) moment bounds (and with any
\delta>0
). One should expect this estimate to be of optimal order (up to the loss of
\delta
), as the average of correctors on the boundary
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}\cap B_{r}}\phi_{i}\,dS
displays fluctuations of order

and
\theta_{i}
is the

to
\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[\nabla\theta_{i}(x)]\big|\leq C\bigg(\frac{\varepsilon}{\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+})}\bigg)^{d/2+1-\delta},

although we make this rigorous only in a spatially averaged sense (see Proposition

To the best of our knowledge, the estimate (

) is the first optimal-order estimate on the decay of boundary layers in stochastic homogenization.

Application to the representative volume approximation.

An interesting application of these estimates for boundary layers arises in the analysis of the representative volume element (RVE) method for the approximation of the effective coefficient
\bar{a}
. The RVE method proceeds by taking a sample of the random coefficient field – say, on a box
[0,L]^{d}
with side length
L\gg\varepsilon
– and approximates the effective coefficient
\bar{a}
as
\bar{a}\approx a^{\operatorname{RVE}}
with

\displaystyle a^{\operatorname{RVE}}e_{i}:=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{[0,L]^{d}}a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Here,
\phi_{i}^{L}
solves the corrector equation
-\nabla\cdot(a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L}))=0
on the representative volume
[0,L]^{d}
, for instance with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.
It turns out that this RVE approximation with Dirichlet boundary data features random fluctuations of order
|a^{\operatorname{RVE}}-\mathbb{E}[a^{\operatorname{RVE}}]|\sim(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{L})^{d/2}
and a systematic error of order
|\mathbb{E}[a^{\operatorname{RVE}}]-\bar{a}|\sim\tfrac{\varepsilon}{L}
. The fluctuations are of central limit theorem scaling and therefore of optimal order. Note, however, that for
d\geq 3
the systematic error dominates. It turns out that the reason for this rather large systematic error contribution is in fact a boundary layer phenomenon. Naively one might expect that by averaging only in the interior (“oversampling”), i. e. by setting

\displaystyle{\tilde{a}}^{\operatorname{RVE}}e_{i}:=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{[\kappa L,(1-\kappa)L]^{d}}a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L})\,\mathrm{d}x

(1.6)

for some constant
0<\kappa\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
, one may eliminate this issue of boundary layers. Interestingly, this turns out
not
to be the case: while this procedure of oversampling decreases the systematic error by a constant factor, the order of convergence remains the same. The underlying reason is that the Dirichlet corrector
\phi_{i}^{L}
differs from the whole space corrector
\phi_{i}
(the latter of which defines the effective coefficient as
\bar{a}e_{i}:=\mathbb{E}[a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i})]
) in terms of a boundary contribution: Indeed,
\phi_{i}-\phi_{i}^{L}
is an
a
-harmonic function in
[0,L]^{d}
with boundary data
\phi_{i}
, i. e. we need to understand a homogenization problem with fluctuating boundary data. The failure of higher-order convergence of (
1.6
) is due to
effective boundary conditions
imposed on
\phi_{i}-\phi_{i}^{L}
due to the interaction of the
fluctuating boundary data

\phi_{i}
with the fluctuations of the coefficient field
a
. In Theorem
2
, we show that the slight modification of the RVE approximation given by

\displaystyle a^{{\operatorname{RVE}},new}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{[\kappa L,(1-\kappa)L]^{d}}e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L}\,\mathrm{d}x:=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{[\kappa L,(1-\kappa)L]^{d}}a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L})\,\mathrm{d}x

(1.7)

with overwhelming probability improves the overall error estimate to
(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{L})^{d/2-\delta}
for
d=3
and
d=4
. The underlying reason is that the new formula takes into account the slight change of the field gradient in the interior that is caused by the effective boundary conditions of
\phi_{i}-\phi_{i}^{L}
.
The new formula (
1.7
) is also rather natural, as the effective coefficient should provide the relation between a given macroscopic field gradient and the corresponding macroscopically averaged flux.

We frequently use the convention that

By

Similarly, we use the notation

Discussion of main results and strategy

2.1
Setting of our results

To begin this section, we first list the assumptions on
\langle\cdot\rangle
, the ensemble of coefficient fields
a
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, under which we obtain our results. Towards further illustrating our setting, we then give a few examples of ensembles on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfy these assumptions. Having clarified the setting of our results we then discuss, in turn, Theorem
1

Assumptions on the ensemble.

Throughout this article we will assume that:

(A1)

The ensemble
\langle\cdot\rangle
is
uniformly elliptic and bounded
: There exists some
\lambda>0
such that
\langle\cdot\rangle
-almost surely we have

\displaystyle|a(x)v|

\displaystyle\leq|v|,

\displaystyle a(x)v\cdot v

\displaystyle\geq\lambda|v|^{2}

for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a. e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(A2)

The ensemble
\langle\cdot\rangle
is
stationary
, that is, the law of
a(\cdot+x)
coincides with the law of
a(\cdot)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words, the statistics of the coefficient field do not depend on the region in space.

(A3)

The ensemble is subject to decorrelation on scales larger than the microscale in the sense that it satisfies a spectral gap inequality: For any random variable
\xi(a)
it holds that

\displaystyle\left<(\xi-\left<\xi\right>)^{2}\right>\leq\varepsilon^{d}\left<\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\big|\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial a}\big|{\textrm{d}}y\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right>,

(2.1)

where
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\big|\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial a}\big|{\textrm{d}}y
is used to denote

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta a}\limsup_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{\xi(a+h\delta a)-\xi(a)}{h}

with the supremum taken over perturbations
\delta a:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
that are supported in
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
and satisfy
\|\delta a\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 1
. The parameter
\varepsilon>0
is the correlation length of the ensemble.

(A4)

The coefficient field
a
is Hölder continuous with stretched exponential moments for some Hölder exponent
\alpha\in(0,1]
. In particular, there exists a stationary random field
\mathcal{C}(a,x)
with
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\nu\mathcal{C}^{\nu})]\leq 2
for some
\nu>0
such that the Hölder estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{|a(y)-a(z)|}{|y-z|^{\alpha}}\leq\mathcal{C}(a,x)\varepsilon^{-\alpha}

(2.2)

holds.

Note that (A1)–(A3) are a standard set of assumptions in quantitative stochastic homogenization; in particular, assumption (A3) is a standard quantification of the qualitative ergodicity assumption that is used in classical qualitative stochastic homogenization results.

Assumption (A4) is a small-scale regularity assumption that we require mostly for convenience; it enables us to state pointwise gradient bounds (which may otherwise fail due to a possible failure of regularity on scales smaller than the microscale).

Examples of ensembles satisfying (A1) - (A4).
We give two examples of random coefficient fields, both of which are standard examples used to illustrate the applicability of quantitative stochastic homogenization results.

Example 1:
We consider a Gaussian ensemble that satisfies a certain decorrelation estimate. In particular, let
\tilde{a}(x)
be a scalar Gaussian field that is centered and stationary and, furthermore, satisfies a decorrelation estimate of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{|x|=|y|}\big|\left<\tilde{a}(x)\tilde{a}(0)\right>\big|\,{\textrm{d}}y\leq 1.

Defining
a:=g(\tilde{a}(x))
, where

\mathbb{R}

[
11
]
. For sufficiently regular covariances
\langle\tilde{a}(x)\tilde{a}(0)\rangle

Example 2:
The second example involves random spherical inclusions. More precisely, we consider a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)
. Let
(X_{i},h_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a realization; then consider the points
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the order of their coordinate
h_{i}
and for each point
X_{i}
place a ball of radius
1
around

if the ball does not intersect with any of the previously placed balls. Denote the union of the balls obtained by this procedure by

We expect that a relatively straightforward adaption of our arguments would make our results applicable also to ensembles satisfying such an “oscillation spectral gap” estimate, though it would involve some additional work to obtain the necessary small-scale (microscopic) regularity theory (as derived from assumption (A4) below in our present work).

2.3
Theorem
2
: Improved error estimate for representative volume element method.

In the second main result of our paper we use Theorem
1
to obtain an improved error estimate for the representative volume element method, which is the standard method used to approximate the homogenized coefficients
\bar{a}
. The error in the representative volume element method can be split into two contributions, the
random
error and the

error. While the former is well-understood to be of central limit theorem scaling, without additional modifications such as “screening” terms in the corrector equation
[
8
,
17
,
20
]
the systematic error would dominate (for
d\geq 3
). As discussed above, our understanding of boundary layers enables us to propose a minor modification of the RVE method that improves the bound on the systematic error, thereby improving the overall rate of convergence for
d\geq 3
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
d\geq 3
and let
\langle\cdot\rangle
be an ensemble of coefficient fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies the assumptions (A1)-(A4) with
\varepsilon=1
. Let
\bar{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be the corresponding effective coefficient.
Denote by
\phi_{i}^{L}
the homogenization corrector with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on the box
[0,L]^{d}
, i. e. let
\phi_{i}^{L}
satisfy

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L}))

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }(0,L)^{d},

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{L}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial[0,L]^{d}.

Given any
\kappa\in(0,\tfrac{1}{4}]
, define the RVE approximation
\bar{a}^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
for the effective coefficient as the solution to the system of linear equations

\displaystyle\bar{a}^{L}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{[\kappa L,(1-\kappa)L]^{d}}e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L}\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{[\kappa L,(1-\kappa)L]^{d}}a(e_{i}+\nabla\phi_{i}^{L})\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq d.

(2.8)

Then for any
\delta>0
and any

, we have the error estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[\big|\bar{a}^{L}-\bar{a}\big|>s\big(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{L}\big)^{\min\{d/2,2\}-\delta}\big]\leq 2\exp(-s^{1/C}/C).

In particular, with at least this probability

is well-defined, i. e. the defining linear system has a unique solution.

Overall, in dimensions
3
and
4
we see that with overwhelming probability, the error

is at most of order
(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{L})^{d/2-\delta}
, improving the error estimate of the standard RVE approximation to (nearly) the fluctuation scaling.

Note that as a slight downside of the formula (

), in principle we cannot rule out that there may be a small probability that the linear system for
\bar{a}^{L}
given by (
2.8
) may fail to be invertible; thus, we cannot make a statement on the error of the expected value
\mathbb{E}[\bar{a}^{L}]
. However, the probability for this to happen is exceedingly low, as shown by our stretched exponential estimate.

\displaystyle\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla g|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq r^{-(d+1)}.

We argument for Proposition

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nabla(f\delta\phi_{i})\cdot a^{*}(\nabla h-\nabla\hat{h})\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\mathcal{O}}}\nabla(f\delta\phi_{i})\cdot a^{*}\nabla\hat{h}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\delta\phi_{i}\nabla f\cdot a^{*}(\chi_{\mathcal{O}}\nabla h-\nabla\hat{h})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\nabla\delta\phi_{i}\cdot a^{*}(\chi_{\mathcal{O}}\nabla h-\nabla\hat{h})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\delta\phi_{i}\cdot a^{*}(\nabla v_{1}+\nabla v_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\delta a(\nabla\phi_{i}+e_{i})\cdot(\nabla v_{1}+\nabla v_{2})\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Combining the above identities, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta F=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\delta a\nabla u\cdot\nabla h\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\delta a(\nabla\phi_{i}+e_{i})\cdot(\nabla v_{1}+\nabla v_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x,

which concludes our argument.
∎

Estimates on the auxiliary functions: A weighted Meyers estimate

Having calculated the derivative of

Lemma 14

Let

\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}

\mathbb{R}^{d}

(defined in Proposition

and
F_{0}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{O}},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
v
denote the unique solution of

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a^{*}\nabla v)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot F_{0}+f_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }{\mathcal{O}},

(5.9)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial{\mathcal{O}}

and introduce the weight

Then, in the case that

Appendix D
Exponentially localized boundary layer

We define the massive approximation for the boundary layer corrector
\theta_{i}^{T}
(with a localization parameter
T<\infty
, the boundary layer corrector
\theta_{i}
being recovered in the limit
T\rightarrow\infty
) as

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla\theta_{i}^{T})+\frac{1}{T}\theta_{i}^{T}

\displaystyle=0\quad\quad\text{in }\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+},

(D.1a)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{T}

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}\quad\quad\text{on }\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}.

(D.1b)

We remark that, thanks to the massive term
\frac{1}{T}\theta_{i}^{T}
, (
D.1
) is easily solvable. In particular, for any
x_{0}\in\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
one first solves (
D.1
) with the boundary data
\theta_{i}^{T,R}=\phi^{T}_{i}\chi(B_{R}(x_{0}))
, where
B_{R}(x_{0})
is a
d-1

\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}
. To pass to the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
and to obtain the independence of the limit on the base point
x_{0}
, the following exponentially localized energy estimate is used:

Lemma 26

(Exponentially localized energy estimate)
.

Assume that
a
satisfies the condition (A1) and
T>0
and

. Let

be a weak solution of

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla u)+\frac{1}{T}u

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot F

\displaystyle\text{in }\quad\,\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+},

(D.2)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{on }\quad\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+},

where
g
is the trace of
g\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+})
and
F\in L^{2}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+})

,
F
, and

satisfy

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}R^{-k}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{B_{R}^{+}}(|u|+|\nabla u|+|F|+|g|+|\nabla g|)^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}=0

(D.3)

for some

0<\gamma<c(d,\lambda)
we find that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}}(|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{T}|u|^{2})\exp(-\gamma|x|/L)\,\\
&\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}_{+}}(|\nabla g|^{2}+\frac{1}{T}|g|^{2}+|F|^{2})\exp(-\gamma|x|/L)\,.\end{split}

Proof.

Letting

Choosing a small enough
