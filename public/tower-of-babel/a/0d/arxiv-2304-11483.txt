Abstract
In this paper, we study the finite satisfiability problem for the logic 𝖡𝖤 \mathsf{BE} under the homogeneity assumption. 𝖡𝖤 \immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathsf{BE} is the cornerstone of Halpern and Shoham’s interval temporal logic, and features modal operators corresponding to the prefix (a.k.a. “Begins”) and suffix (a.k.a. “Ends”) relations on intervals. In terms of complexity, 𝖡𝖤 \mathsf{BE} lies in between the “Chop” logic 𝖢 \mathsf{C} , whose satisfiability problem is known to be non-elementary, and the PSpace -complete interval logic 𝖣 \mathsf{D} of the sub-interval (a.k.a. “During”) relation. 𝖡𝖤 \mathsf{BE} was shown to be ExpSpace -hard, and the only known satisfiability procedure is primitive recursive, but not elementary. Our contribution consists of tightening the complexity bounds of the satisfiability problem for 𝖡𝖤 \mathsf{BE} , by proving it to be ExpSpace -complete. We do so by devising an equi-satisfiable normal form with boundedly many nested modalities. The normalization technique resembles Scott’s quantifier elimination, but it turns out to be much more involved due to the limitations enforced by the homogeneity assumption.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the computational complexity of the
satisfiability problem for the logic
\mathsf{BE}
of the
prefix and suffix interval relations.
The considered interpretation setting is the one with intervals
over finite linear orders, under the homogeneity assumption
(see below).
The logic
\mathsf{BE}
is at the core of the galaxy of interval
temporal logics
[
9
]
and has interesting connections with standard
point-based temporal logics
[
2
]
.
In general, formulas of interval temporal logics can express
properties of
pairs
of time points, rather than
properties of single time points, and are evaluated as
sets of such pairs, that is, as binary relations on points.
They are very expressive in comparison to point-based ones,
and it does not come as a surprise that, in general, there
is no reduction of their satisfiability problem to
satisfiability of classical monadic second-order logic.

The
logic
\mathsf{BE}
has two (unary) modalities,

\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathop{\langle B\rangle}
and
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathop{\langle E\rangle}
,
that quantify over prefixes and suffixes of the
current interval, respectively.
These modalities can be viewed as the logical counterparts of
Allen’s binary relations
Begins
and
Ends

[
1
]
.
In particular, the logic
\mathsf{BE}
can be considered as a fragment of
Halpern and Shoham’s interval temporal logic
[
9
]
,
denoted
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathsf{HS}
, which features one modal operator for
each of the twelve non-trivial Allen’s relations.

The satisfiability problem for
\mathsf{BE}
turns out to be

undecidable
over all relevant classes of
interval structures
[
7
,
10
]
.
One can however escape this bleak landscape by
constraining the semantics, in particular, the
interpretation of the propositional letters.
An interesting example is given by the
homogeneity

assumption, according to which a propositional letter
holds at an interval if and only if it holds at
all of its points.
In other words, according to the homogeneous semantics,
the labelling of an arbitrary interval in a model is
uniquely determined by those of the singleton intervals
contained in it.

An advantage of the homogeneity assumption is that
it makes it possible to define a natural interpretation of
interval logics over Kripke structures.
For example, this
comes in handy when studying the
model-checking problem
,
which is defined as the problem of deciding whether a given
formula is valid over all (homogeneous) interval structures
generated by a given Kripke structure.
As such, the problem can be seen as a variant
of the classical validity/satisfiability problem, and many
decidability and complexity results can be transferred
from one problem to another.
In
[
12
]

it was shown that the model-checking and satisfiability
problems for
\mathsf{BE}
, and in fact for full
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathsf{HS}
logic,
become decidable when one restricts to

homogeneous
interval structures.

Despite its simple syntax and the homogeneity assumption,
the logic
\mathsf{BE}
turns out to be quite expressive and succinct.
In
[
2
]
,
Bozzelli et al. have shown that, when interpreted over
finite words, LTL (Linear Temporal Logic) and
\mathsf{BE}
,
under homogeneity, define the same class of
star-free regular languages, but with the latter formalism
being at least exponentially more succinct than the former.
This is also reflected in the complexity of the
satisfiability problem for
\mathsf{BE}
,
which was shown to be

ExpSpace
-hard
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
.

In fact, the cited result focuses on the
model-checking problem for
\mathsf{BE}
,
which takes as input, not only a formula,
but also a Kripke structure.
It happens that the Kripke structure
used in the proof of the
ExpSpace

lowerbound generates every possible
homogeneous interval structure,
and hence the result can be immediately
transferred to the validity/satisfiability problem
for
\mathsf{BE}
.

On the other hand, the only known decision procedure

[
12
]

for satisfiability of
\mathsf{BE}
formulas
is basically the one for full
\mathsf{HS}
,
and is not elementary.

It is also worth contrasting the expressiveness and
complexity of
\mathsf{BE}
, under homogeneity, with those
of two close relatives of it: the
Chop logic
\mathsf{C}

[
16
]

and the logic
\mathsf{D}
of the sub-interval relation
[
4
]
.
The
logic
\mathsf{C}
has a binary modality
\mathbin{\langle C\rangle}
that allows one to split the current interval
in two parts and predicate separately on them.
The
logic
\mathsf{D}
has a unary modality
\mathop{\langle D\rangle}
that allows one to predicate
about sub-intervals of the current interval.
It is easy to see that, in terms of expressiveness,

\mathsf{BE}
lies in between
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathsf{D}
and
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathsf{C}
, in the sense
that modality
\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\langle D\rangle}
can be defined in
\mathsf{BE}
, i.e.,
\mathop{\langle D\rangle}\varphi

is equivalent to
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\langle E\rangle}\varphi
,
and modalities
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}
and
\mathop{\langle E\rangle}
can in turn be defined in
\mathsf{C}
, e.g.,
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\varphi
is equivalent to
\varphi\mathop{\mathbin{\langle C\rangle}}\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathrm{true}
.
Under the homogeneity assumption,
the satisfiability problem for
\mathsf{C}
is non-elementarily
decidable, precisely, tower-complete, in view of the existence of
straightforward reductions to and from language-emptiness of generalized
star-free regular expressions
[
13
,
15
]
,
while the satisfiability problem for
\mathsf{D}

was shown to be
PSpace
-complete by a suitable
contraction method
[
4
]
.
It is also worth pointing out that if the homogeneity
assumption is removed, the satisfiability problem for

\mathsf{D}
becomes undecidable
[
11
]
.

Based on the observations above,
it is crucial
to close the gap between the complexity lowerbound and
upperbound of the satisfiability problem for
\mathsf{BE}
.
Significant effort has been invested in recent years
towards both raising the
ExpSpace
lowerbound,
e.g. using variants of Stockmeyer’s counters
[
15
]
,
and developing elementary satisfiability procedures.
Despite these efforts, the complexity gap remained unchanged
and proved to be an intriguing challenge.
The special status of
\mathsf{BE}
is witnessed by the fact that
the many results about the complexity of the satisfiability
and/or model-checking problems for proper fragments of
\mathsf{HS}
,
under the homogeneity assumption, concern logics that include
neither modality
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}
nor modality
\mathop{\langle E\rangle}

or feature only one of them (an up-to-date picture can be found in
[
5
,
6
]
).

In this paper, we manage to prove that the satisfiability problem
for the logic
\mathsf{BE}
, under homogeneity, is elementarily decidable,
and precisely
ExpSpace
-complete.
This result is established using a rather unexpected
normalization technique, which consists of transforming
an arbitrary
\mathsf{BE}
formula into an equi-satisfiable one
with boundedly many nested modalities.
Specifically, we will show that one can compute,
in polynomial time, normalized
formulas with nesting depth of modalities at most 4,
and with at most 2 alternations between universal and
existential modalities.
The transformation of
\mathsf{BE}
formulas into normalized
ones can be also viewed as a quantifier elimination
technique à-la Scott
[
14
]
.
In this perspective, however, the transformation
has to deal with an increased difficulty:
due to the homogeneity assumption, the elements
over which we predicate cannot be labelled in an
arbitrary way.
In view of this difficulty, it is quite surprising
that an equi-satisfiable normalized
formula can be computed in polynomial time from any given
arbitrary
\mathsf{BE}
formula.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce
the logic
\mathsf{BE}
and we point out the relevant
implications of the homogeneity assumption.
In Section
3
, we define the
transformation of
\mathsf{BE}
formulas into normalized ones.
In Section
4
, we derive an
optimal satisfiability procedure and analyse
its complexity.
Conclusions provide an assessment of the work done
and outline future research directions.
For reader convenience, technical terms and notation
in the electronic version of the paper
are linked to their definitions, which can then be
accessed with a mouse click.

2
Preliminaries

Let the
time domain
be a finite prefix of the natural numbers
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate(N,<)
.
Intervals over
N
are denoted by
[x,y]
, for
x,y\in N
and
x\leq y
,
and the set of all intervals over
N
is denoted

\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathbb{I}(N)
.
We let
\mathbin{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate<_{B}}
(resp.,
\mathbin{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate<_{E}}
) be the proper
prefix

(resp.,
suffix
) relation on intervals,
defined by
J\mathbin{<_{B}}I
if and only if
\min(I)=\min(J)\leq\max(J)<\max(I)

(resp.,
J\mathbin{<_{E}}I
if and only if
\min(I)<\min(J)\leq\max(J)=\max(I)
).

Formulas of the
logic
\mathsf{BE}
are constructed starting from
propositional letters belonging to a finite non-empty set
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\Sigma
,
called
signature
, using classical Boolean connectives and modal
operators. The latter operators are used to quantify over prefixes
and suffixes of the current interval.
Formally,
\mathsf{BE}
formulas satisfy the following grammar:

\varphi~~::=~~p~~(\text{for }p\in\Sigma)~~|~~\neg\varphi~~|~~\varphi\vee\varphi~~|~~\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\varphi~~|~~\mathop{\langle E\rangle}\varphi.

Semantics is given in terms of an interval structure
\mathcal{S}
and one of its intervals
I
.
Formally, an
interval structure
over a signature
\Sigma
is a pair

\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{I}(N),\sigma)
, where

\sigma:\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathbb{I}(N)\rightarrow\wp(\Sigma)
is a labelling
of intervals by subsets of
\Sigma
.
Whether a
\mathsf{BE}
formula
\varphi

holds at
an interval
I
of
\mathcal{S}
,
denoted
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\models}\varphi
, is determined by the following rules:

•

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}p
if
p\in\sigma(I)
;

•

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\neg\varphi
if
\mathcal{S},I\not\mathbin{\models}\varphi
;

•

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}
if

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi_{1}
or
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi_{2}
;

•

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\mathop{\mathop{\langle B\rangle}}\varphi
if
\mathcal{S},J\mathbin{\models}\varphi
for some
J\mathbin{<_{B}}I
;

•

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\mathop{\mathop{\langle E\rangle}}\varphi
if
\mathcal{S},J\mathbin{\models}\varphi
for some
J\mathbin{<_{E}}I
.

A formula is
valid
if it holds at every interval of every interval structure;
similarly, it is
satisfiable
if it holds at some interval of some interval structure.
Two formulas
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if for every interval
structure
\mathcal{S}
and every interval
I
in it,
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi
iff
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi^{\prime}
.
They are
equi-satisfiable
if either they are both satisfiable or none of them is.
The notions of validity, satisfiability, and equivalence can be relativized to a
specific class of interval structures (possibly even to a single interval structure).
As an example, we say that a formula
\varphi
is
valid over
a class
\mathscr{C}
of
interval structures if
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi
for all
\mathcal{S}\in\mathscr{C}
and all

I\in\mathcal{S}
. In the particular case where
\mathscr{C}
contains a single interval structure
\mathcal{S}
,
we will say that a formula
\varphi
is
valid over

\mathcal{S}
if
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi

for all
I\in\mathcal{S}
.

It is possible to add syntactic sugar to the logic
\mathsf{BE}
.
As an example, we will often use shorthands like

\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}=\neg(\neg\varphi_{1}\vee\neg\varphi_{2})
,

\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathrm{false}=p\wedge\neg p
(for any
p\in\Sigma
),

\mathrm{true}=\neg\mathrm{false}
,
and
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate[X]\varphi\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate=\neg\langle X\rangle\neg\varphi
, for
X\in\{B,E\}
.
Some other useful shorthands are
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\pi=\mathop{[B]}\mathrm{false}
,
which constrains the interval where it is evaluated to be a singleton, and

\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathop{[G]}\varphi=\varphi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[B]}\varphi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[E]}\varphi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[B]}\mathop{[E]}\varphi
,
which constrains all sub-intervals (including the current interval, its proper prefixes, and
its proper suffixes) to satisfy
\varphi
.
The shorthands
\pi
and
\mathop{[G]}
can be viewed as derived nullary
and unary modal operators, respectively, and can be added as syntactic
sugar to
\mathsf{BE}
.

Homogeneity assumption

We recall from
[
10
,
12
]

that the satisfiability problems for the logic
\mathsf{BE}
is
undecidable, unless one restricts to homogeneous interval structures.
An interval structure
\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{I}(N),\sigma)
is
homogeneous
if its labelling
satisfies the condition
\sigma(I)=\bigcap_{x\in I}\sigma([x,x])
for all
I\in\mathbb{I}(N)
.
Intuitively, this means that the labelling
\sigma
is uniquely determined
by its restriction to singleton intervals.
Let us take a closer look at the implications of homogeneity.

First of all, we have that every formula
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}(p_{1}\wedge p_{2})

is equivalent to
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}p_{1}\wedge\mathop{\langle B\rangle}p_{2}
, and similarly for
\mathop{\langle E\rangle}
.
Note, however, that homogeneity does not imply
similar properties for arbitrary formulas
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}

replacing the propositional letters
p_{1},p_{2}
.
As an example, the formulas
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}(p\wedge\neg p)
and

(\mathop{\langle B\rangle}p)\wedge(\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\neg p)
are not equivalent.

Homogeneity can also be exploited to efficiently rewrite
any
\mathsf{BE}
formula into an equivalent one where every occurrence
of a propositional letter is conjoined with
\pi
.
Based on this observation, we introduce the following mild
normal form:

Definition 1
.

A
\mathsf{BE}
formula
\psi
is in
homogeneous normal form

if every occurrence of a propositional letter
p
in
\psi

appears inside the subformula
\pi\wedge p
.

Basically, the homogeneous normal form
restricts propositional letters to be only
evaluated at singleton intervals.
As an example, the formula

(\pi\wedge q)\vee\mathop{\langle B\rangle}(\pi\wedge\neg(\pi\wedge p))

is in homogeneous normal form and holds at an
interval
I
iff
I
consists of a single point
labelled by
q
or the left endpoint of
I
is
not labelled by
p
.

Proposition 2
.

One can transform in linear time any formula
\psi

into one in homogeneous normal form that is equivalent
to
\psi
when interpreted over
homogeneous

interval structures.

Proof.

It suffices to replace
every occurrence of a propositional letter
p
in
\psi
by the formula

\mathtt{everywhere}(p)=(\pi\wedge p)\>\vee\>\big(\mathop{\langle B\rangle}(\pi\wedge p)\>\wedge\>\mathop{\langle E\rangle}(\pi\wedge p)\>\wedge\>\mathop{[B]}(\pi\vee\mathop{\langle E\rangle}(\pi\wedge p))\big)
.
The resulting formula is equivalent to
\psi
since,
over homogeneous interval structures,
p
is equivalent to
\mathtt{everywhere}(p)
.
∎

We denote by
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
the fragment of logic
\mathsf{BE}

that contains only formulas in homogeneous normal form.

From this point forward, we will exclusively work with

{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formulas, with the understanding that this assumption
may occasionally go unstated.
Accordingly, we will treat (sub)formulas of the form

\pi\wedge p
as atomic.

3
A bounded-nesting normal form for
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}

In this section, we describe a transformation of arbitrary
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formulas
into equi-satisfiable ones with boundedly many nested modalities.
The transformation is somehow reminiscent of the so-called
Scott normal form
for the two-variable fragment of first-order logic
[
14
]
,
since it results in a formula, over an extended set of propositional letters,
that is satisfiable if and only if the original formula was.
The increased difficulty here is that the valuation of the new
propositional letters emerging from the transformation
must satisfy the homogeneity assumption.
This is to say that we cannot identify intervals
satisfying a certain (sub)formula
\varphi

by labelling them with a fresh propositional letter
q_{\varphi}
.
Rather, we will identify these intervals by appropriately
correlating fresh labels assigned to their endpoints.
Our transformation will exploit in a crucial way the fact that,
under homogeneity, valuations of formulas at two overlapping intervals
have “less degrees of freedom” than valuations of the same formulas
at disjoint intervals.

Definition 3
.

The
modal depth
(or simply
depth
) of a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
is the maximum number of nested modal operators
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}
and
\mathop{\langle E\rangle}
in it,
not counting those defining the operator
\pi
.
A
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula is in
shallow normal form
if it is of the form

\psi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi
, where both
\psi
and
\xi

have depth at most
2
.

Concerning the above definition,
we recall that
\mathop{[G]}\xi
is a shorthand for

\xi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[B]}\xi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[E]}\xi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[B]}\mathop{[E]}\xi
,
so a formula in shallow normal form has depth at most
4
.
However, not all depth-
4
formulas are in shallow normal form.

Theorem 4
.

Given any
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
\psi
, one can compute in
in polynomial time an equi-satisfiable formula

\psi^{\star}
that is in shallow normal form.

which we postpone to the next subsections, we give an example of
normalization of a formula.

Example 5
.

Consider the formula
\psi=\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\varphi

over the signature
\Sigma=\{p\}
,
where
\varphi=\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\mathop{\langle E\rangle}(\pi\wedge p)
.

\psi
; in particular, it highlights intervals

\varphi

\mathop{\langle E\rangle}(\pi\wedge p)

Note that
\psi
has depth
3

To rewrite
\psi
into an equi-satisfiable formula in shallow normal form,
we introduce a new propositional letter

with the purpose of
marking the right endpoints of the intervals that
satisfy
\varphi
and that are minimal w.r.t. the prefix relation

We thus consider interval structures over the expanded

We conclude this part by observing an immediate consequence of Theorem

We recall from

A close inspection to the description of this procedure shows that it has

In particular, when we consider a formula

Together with Proposition

\mathsf{BE}

Corollary 6

\sum_{\alpha\in{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{X}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}}(|\alpha|+1)=\big(\sum_{\alpha\in{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{X}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}}|\alpha|\big)+|{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{X}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}|

From this we derive:

\displaystyle 2|\varphi|-|{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{B}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}|-|{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{E}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}|

\displaystyle\geq~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\alpha\in{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{B}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}}2|\alpha|~+~\sum\nolimits_{\alpha\in{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{E}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}}2|\alpha|~+~|{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{B}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}|~+~|{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{E}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}|

\displaystyle>~

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{\alpha\in{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{B}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}}2|\alpha|~+~\sum\nolimits_{\alpha\in{\boldsymbol{\partial}\mspace{-2mu}}_{E}{\varphi}\mspace{2mu}}2|\alpha|\ .

We have just shown that at least one index

∎

We conclude this part by showing how prefix-minimal intervals

Consider a

Define the

2

2

\displaystyle\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathrm{flat}(\varphi)

3.4
Normalization procedure

We are now ready to describe the normalization procedure underlying
Theorem
4
. Let
\psi
be a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula.
The normalization of
\psi
consists of repeatedly applying some
rewriting steps that preserve satisfiability and progressively
reduce the number of distinct subformulas of depth larger than
2
,
until a shallow normal form is eventually obtained.

Every rewriting step is applied to a formula of the form

\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
over a signature
\Sigma_{i}

(initially,
\psi_{0}=\psi
,
\xi_{i}=\mathrm{true}
, and
\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma
),
and results in an equi-satisfiable formula
\psi_{i+1}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i+1}

over an extended signature
\Sigma_{i+1}
.
To perform the rewriting step, we must choose a subformula
\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi

of
\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
, for some
X\in\{B,E\}
,
that has depth
d>2
and that does not occur under the scope of any other modal
operator, except possibly the operator
\mathop{[G]}
that has
\xi_{i}
as argument.
We then use Corollary
15
to obtain an expander

\mathrm{enc}(\varphi)
from
\Sigma_{i}
to
\Sigma_{i+1}
and a
formula
\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\mathrm{flat}(\varphi)
equivalent to
\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi
over every
interval structure that makes
\mathrm{enc}(\varphi)
valid.
We then rewrite
\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
into the formula

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\underbrace{\psi_{i}\big[\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi\>/\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\mathrm{flat}(\varphi)\big]}_{\psi_{i+1}}~\wedge\\
\mathop{[G]}\big(\underbrace{\xi_{i}\big[\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi\>/\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\mathrm{flat}(\varphi)\big]\>\wedge\>\mathrm{enc}(\varphi)}_{\xi_{i+1}}\big)\end{array}

(
\dagger
)

Thanks to distributivity of
\mathop{[G]}
with respect to
\wedge
,
the formula (
\dagger
) is equivalent to

(\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i})[\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi\>/\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\mathrm{flat}(\varphi)]~\wedge~\mathop{[G]}\mathrm{enc}(\varphi)
.
Moreover, thanks to Lemma
8
,
the latter formula is equi-satisfiable as
\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
.
This completes the description of a rewriting step.

Let us now analyse the complexity of the normalization procedure.
The procedure terminates when one cannot choose any subformula

\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi
with the desired properties: in this case the rewritten formula

\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
turns out to be in shallow normal form
and we can let
\psi^{\star}=\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
.
To bound the number of rewriting steps, we study how a single
rewriting step affects the number of distinct subformulas
of depth larger than
2
.
As for
\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi
, we observe that this subformula does not
occur anymore in the rewritten formula
\psi_{i+1}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i+1}

(in particular, the inteded use of
\mathrm{enc}(\varphi)
is to entail

\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\varphi\>\leftrightarrow\>\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\mathrm{flat}(\varphi)
, but the chosen
writing in the statement of Corollary
15

avoids having
\varphi
under the scope of a modal operator,
thus guaranteeing that
\mathrm{enc}(\varphi)
has depth at most
2
).
On the other hand, new occurrences of subformulas may emerge in

\psi_{i+1}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i+1}
:
these are either formulas of depth at most
2

(e.g.,
\mathop{\langle X\rangle}\mathrm{flat}(\varphi)
) or copies of formulas that already occur
in
\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
(e.g.,
\varphi
).
Summing up, the effect of a rewriting step is to decrease the
number of
distinct
subformulas of depth larger than
2
.
This implies that the number of rewriting steps is at most linear in
the size of the original formula
\psi
.
Finally, each rewriting step is purely syntactical and can be carried out
efficiently on the involved formula
\psi_{i}\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi_{i}
,
whose size grows at most linearly with
i
.
This shows that the entire normalization procedure
can be performed in polynomial time w.r.t.
|\psi|
,
and completes the proof of Theorem
4
.
∎

4
Complexity of the satisfiability problem

In this section, we build up on the previous normalization result
to prove a tight complexity bound:

Theorem 16
.

The satisfiability problem for
\mathsf{BE}
logic restricted to
homogeneous interval structures is
ExpSpace
-complete.

An
ExpSpace
lowerbound for
\mathsf{BE}
under homogeneity
was already proven in
[
3
]
,
so we focus on the upperbound.
In view of Proposition
2
and
Theorem
4
, given any
\mathsf{BE}
formula
\psi
, one
can compute in polynomial time a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
\psi^{\star}

that is equi-satisfiable over homogeneous interval structures.
Of course, this also means that
\psi^{\star}
has size at most
polynomial in
|\psi|
.
We argue below that one can test satisfiability of
a formula in shallow normal form
in exponential space with respect to the size of the formula itself.
Together with the previous observations, this proves Theorem
16
.

4.1
Composition of logical types

We need to formalize a notion of
logical type, similar to the notion of profile used in the proof of
Lemma
13
, that not only determines which formulas
hold at a given interval, but also satisfies mild compositional
properties, that is, under suitable conditions, one can compute the
type of the sum of two adjacent intervals on the basis of the types of the
original intervals.
It will be convenient to define types separately for formulas of
depth
0
,
1
, and
2
(there is no need to consider higher depths,
as we assume to deal with formulas in shallow normal form).
We will first present the rather simple definitions and properties of
depth-
0
and depth-
1
types, and then focus on the
more complex notion of depth-
2
type.

Depth-
0
and depth-
1
types

We fix, once and for all, an interval structure
\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{I}(N),\sigma)

and we assume that all formulas are over the signature
\Sigma
of
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 17
.

The
depth-
0
type
of an interval
I
,
denoted
\mathrm{type}^{0}(I)
, is either the set

\{\pi\}\cup\{p\in\Sigma\>:\>\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\pi\wedge p\}

or the empty set, depending on whether
I
is a singleton or not.

The
depth-
1
type
of an interval
I=[x,y]

is the quadruple
\mathrm{type}^{1}(I)=(S,T,B,E)
, where

S
is the symbol
1
,
2
, or

3
, depending on whether
I
contains one point,
two points, or more,
T=\mathrm{type}^{0}(I)
,
B=\mathrm{type}^{0}([x,x])
, and

E=\mathrm{type}^{0}([y,y])
.

It is easy to see that depth-
0

(resp., depth-
1
) types of
adjacent intervals can be composed to form
the depth-
0

(resp., depth-
1
)
type of the sum of the two intervals.
One can also verify that the depth-
0

(resp., depth-
1
)
type of an interval determines which
formulas of depth
0
(resp., depth at most
1
)
hold at that interval.
These simple results are formalized in the next two lemmas
below.

Lemma 18
.

For both
d=0
and
d=1
, there is a

composition

operator
\mathbin{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\cdot}
on depth-

that is computable in polynomial time and such that,
for all pairs of adjacent intervals
I,J
, with
\max(I)+1=\min(J)
,

\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathrm{type}^{d}(I)\mathbin{\cdot}\mathrm{type}^{d}(J)=\mathrm{type}^{d}(I\cup J)
.

Proof.

The composition
of depth-
0
types is trivial:
for every pair of depth-
0
types

T,T^{\prime}
, we simply let
T\mathbin{\cdot}T^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
This is correct because the sum of two adjacent
intervals always results in a non-singleton interval,
whose depth-
0
type is the empty set.

As for the composition
of two depth-
1
types, say

\mathcal{T}=(S,T,B,E)
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=(S^{\prime},T^{\prime},B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
, we let

\mathcal{T}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=(S^{\prime\prime},T\mathbin{\cdot}T^{\prime},B,E^{\prime})
,
where
S^{\prime\prime}
is either
2
or
3

depending on whether
S=S^{\prime}=\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\texttt{1}
or not,
and
T\mathbin{\cdot}T^{\prime}
is the composition of the
depth-
0
types

and
T^{\prime}
, as defined just above.
It is immediate to check that if

\mathcal{T}=\mathrm{type}^{1}([x,y])

and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathrm{type}^{1}([y+1,z])
, then

\mathrm{type}^{1}([x,z])=\mathcal{T}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 19
.

For both
d=0
and
d=1
,
for every
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
\varphi
of depth at most
d
,
and for all intervals
I,J
such that

\mathrm{type}^{d}(I)=\mathrm{type}^{d}(J)
, we have

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi
iff
\mathcal{S},J\mathbin{\models}\varphi
.
Moreover, whether
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi
holds or not can be decided in
polynomial time given
\varphi
and
\mathrm{type}^{d}(I)
.

Proof.

We first prove the claim for
d=0
.
For the case
\varphi=\pi
, we have
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi

if and only if
I
is a singleton, or, equally,
\pi\in\mathrm{type}^{0}(I)
.
The case
\varphi=\pi\wedge p
is trivial as well, as we have

\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi
if and only if
p\in\mathrm{type}^{0}(I)
.
It remains to consider the case where
\varphi
is a
Boolean combination of the previous atomic formulas.
In this case, we determine the evaluation of
\varphi
at
I

“homomorphically” on the basis of the evaluations of the atomic formulas.

Let us now prove the claim for
d=1
. The interesting cases are when

\varphi
has depth
0
or it is of the form
\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\alpha
or
\mathop{\langle E\rangle}\alpha
,
with
\alpha
again of depth
0
. Once the claim is proved for these cases,
it can be generalized to Boolean combinations of those formulas using the
same arguments as before.
Let
I=[x,y]
and
\mathrm{type}^{1}(I)=(S,T,B,E)
, and recall that

T=\mathrm{type}^{0}(I)
,
B=\mathrm{type}^{0}([x,x])
, and
E=\mathrm{type}^{0}([y,y])
.

If
\varphi
has depth
0
, then we know that the component

T
(
=\mathrm{type}^{0}(I)
) already determines whether or not
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\varphi
.

If
\varphi=\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\alpha
, we further distinguish three subcases,
depending on
S
.
If
S=\texttt{1}
, then
I
is a singleton and hence

\mathcal{S},I\not\mathbin{\models}\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\alpha
.
If
S=\texttt{2}
, then the only prefix of
I
is the singleton interval
[x,x]
,
hence
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\alpha
iff
\mathcal{S},[x,x]\mathbin{\models}\alpha
.
Since
\alpha
has depth
0
, the latter condition can be
decided using the type
B=\mathrm{type}^{0}([x,x])
.
If
S=\texttt{3}
, then since
\alpha
is a Boolean combination
of formulas of the form
\pi
or
\pi\wedge p
, with
p\in\Sigma
,
it suffices to consider only two prefixes of
I
:
the singleton interval
J_{0}=[x,x]
and the interval
J_{1}=[x,x+1]
.
In particular, we have
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\alpha
if and only if
\mathcal{S},J_{0}\mathbin{\models}\alpha

or
\mathcal{S},J_{1}\mathbin{\models}\alpha
.
Again, the latter two conditions are determined by the depth-
0
types
of
J_{0}
and
J_{1}
, which are
B
and
\emptyset
, respectively.
This shows how to determine whether
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\mathop{\langle B\rangle}\alpha

using the type
\mathrm{type}^{1}(I)=(S,T,B,E)
.

The remaining case is that of a formula
\varphi=\mathop{\langle E\rangle}\alpha
,
which can be handled by symmetric arguments, using the component

E
instead of
B
.
∎

Depth-
2
types

We now introduce types for depth-
2
formulas.
The machinery here is not as neat as one could hope,
as there is a trade-off between the desired compositional
properties and the number of possible depth-
2
types.
As an example, full compositionality of types for depth-
2
formulas
can only hold if we allow doubly exponentially many types with respect to the
size of the underlying signature — this can be shown formally using
arguments based on communication complexity and the fact that a
depth-
2
formula can describe a Stockmeyer’s counter of
level
2

[
15
]
.
In order to ease compositional properties while maintaining the
number of types as low as possible, we will parameterise
depth-
2
types by a formula and some contexts.

We first discuss a couple of tentative definitions, with their drawbacks.
Following the same principle used to define depth-
1
types,
one may define the depth-
2
type of an interval
I
as
(\mathcal{T},\mathscr{B},\mathscr{E})
,
where
\mathcal{T}=\mathrm{type}^{1}(I)
,
\mathscr{B}=\{\mathrm{type}^{1}(J)\>:\>J\mathbin{<_{B}}I\}
,
and
\mathscr{E}=\{\mathrm{type}^{1}(J)\>:\>J\mathbin{<_{E}}I\}
.
This notion of depth-
2
type would be fully compositional
and would determine the evaluation of every depth-
2
formula
in homogeneous normal form (proofs omitted).
Unfortunately, there could be doubly exponentially many such types
with respect to the size of the signature, and this would not be
compatible with the intended use that we will make
in the satisfiability procedure.
Another option would be to parameterise the depth-
2
type of
I

by a formula
\varphi
and define it as the triple
(\mathcal{T},\mathscr{B},\mathscr{E})
,
where
\mathcal{T}=\mathrm{type}^{1}(I)
as before, and
\mathscr{B}
(resp.,
\mathscr{E}
) is the
set of subformulas
\alpha
of
\varphi
that hold at proper prefixes
(resp., suffixes) of
I
.
Of course, the resulting type would determine the evaluation of

\varphi
at the interval
I
.
This second attempt would also generate at most exponentially
many depth-
2
type with respect to the size of
\varphi
.
On the other hand, the resulting types would not carry enough information
to be composable, the reason being that it is not sufficient to know
which depth-
1
subformulas hold at two adjacent intervals in order to
derive which depth-
1
subformulas hold at the union interval.
The appropriate notion of depth-
2
type is somehow a blend of
the two attempts that we have just discussed.

Let us now fix some other useful notation and terminology:

•

Given a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
\varphi
, we denote by
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathrm{Depth}^{\leq 1}(\varphi)

the set of subformulas of
\varphi
of depth at most
1
.

•

Lemma
19
states that
the depth-
1
type of an interval
I
effectively
determines which formulas of depth at most
1
hold at
I
.
This motivates the following notation:
given a depth-
1
type
\mathcal{T}
and a
formula
\alpha\in\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathrm{Depth}^{\leq 1}(\varphi)
,
we write
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathcal{T}\mathbin{\vdash}\alpha
to state
that
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\alpha
for some (or, equally, for every)
interval
I
such that
\mathrm{type}^{1}(I)=\mathcal{T}

(this latter property can be tested efficiently given
\mathcal{T}
and
\alpha
).

•

By Lemma
18
, depth-
1
types
are equipped with a composition
operation
\mathbin{\cdot}
that forms a semigroup structure.
We complete the structure into a monoid by introducing
the
dummy depth-
1
type
\varepsilon
and by assuming
that
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\varepsilon\mathbin{\cdot}\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}\mathbin{\cdot}\varepsilon=\mathcal{T}

for every depth-
1
type
\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 20
.

Let
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R}
be some (possibly dummy) depth-
1
types.
The
depth-
2

\varphi
-type
of an interval
I

with

and

\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R}

is the tuple
\mathrm{type}^{2}_{\varphi,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R}}(I)=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{T},\mathscr{B},\mathscr{E})
, where

•

\mathcal{T}=\mathrm{type}^{1}(I)
,

•

\mathscr{B}=\{\alpha\in\mathrm{Depth}^{\leq 1}(\varphi)\>:\>\exists J\mathbin{<_{B}}I~~\mathcal{L}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathrm{type}^{1}(J)\mathbin{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\vdash}\alpha\big\}
,

•

\mathscr{E}=\{\alpha\in\mathrm{Depth}^{\leq 1}(\varphi)\>:\>\exists J\mathbin{<_{E}}I~~\mathrm{type}^{1}(J)\mathbin{\cdot}\mathcal{R}\mathbin{\vdash}\alpha\big\}
.

We give some intuition about the components of a depth-
2

\varphi
-type
(the reader can also refer to Figure
5
).
The component
\mathcal{T}
is nothing but the depth-
1
type of the
reference interval
I
, thus determining which formulas of depth
at most
1
hold at
I
.
The components
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
represent the depth-
1
types of
some intervals adjacent to
I
, to the left and to the right respectively,
and will be used as contexes for an operation of
composition.
The set
\mathscr{B}
represents which subformulas of
\varphi
of depth
at most
1

Lemma 22

For all intervals

4.2
Satisfiability procedure

As a warm-up, let us first describe the satisfiability procedure for a formula
of depth at most
2
; later we will generalize this to a formula in shallow normal form.

Let us fix a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
\psi
of depth at most
2
.
Deciding satisfiability of
\psi
can be done in polynomial space,
by reducing to non-emptiness of a language recognized by a suitable finite state automaton.
To formalize the construction of the automaton from the given formula
\psi
,
it is convenient to encode an interval structure
\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{I}(N),\sigma)

over the signature
\Sigma
by the finite word

\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate w_{\mathcal{S}}=a_{0}\dots a_{\max(N)}
over the alphabet

\wp(\Sigma)
, where
a_{i}=\sigma(i)
for all
i\in N

(recall that
N
is a finite prefix of the natural numbers).

Lemma 23
.

Given a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
\psi
of depth
at most
2
, one can compute in polynomial space
3
3

3

By computing an automaton in polynomial space we mean that
its initial states, final states, and transitions can be enumerated
in polynomial space.
The enumeration procedures can be used within other algorithms of
similar complexity, e.g., to test emptiness of the recognized language.

a finite state automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
that accepts
all and only the encodings
\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate w_{\mathcal{S}}
of the interval structures
\mathcal{S}

such that
\mathcal{S},I\mathbin{\models}\psi
, where

\mathcal{S}
.

Proof sketch.

The construction of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
is quite standard,
as it is the cascade product of three automata:

1.

a deterministic automaton that
computes in its states the depth-
1
types of intervals
corresponding to prefixes of the input,

2.

a co-deterministic automaton that
computes in its states the depth-
1
types of intervals
corresponding to suffixes of the input,

3.

a deterministic automaton that
computes the depth-
2

\psi

of the input, with a constant dummy left context
and right contexts given by the states of the
previous automaton.

Transitions of these automata are defined using compositional
properties of depth-
1
and depth-
2

(Lemmas

Below, we provide full details for the construction of

Like we have done for depth-

.

•

the alphabet

•

We now claim that, on every input

From the properties stated in items 1., 2., 3. and

Finally, as for the complexity of constructing
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}

recall from Lemmas

and

that depth-

and depth-

composed in polynomial time.
This implies that the initial states and the transitions of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}

can be enumerated in polynomial space.
To enumerate the final states, it suffices to test properties like

\mathscr{T}\mathbin{\vdash}\psi
, for a given depth-
2

\mathscr{T}

be done in polynomial time thanks to Lemma

.

The fact that the automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
above
can be constructed from
\psi
in polynomial space, implies that (non-)emptiness of
the recognized language can also be decided in polynomial space w.r.t.
|\psi|
.
In its turn, this shows that the satisfiability of a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula

\psi
of depth at most
2
can be decided in polynomial space.

To conclude the proof of Theorem
16
it remains to
reduce the satisfiability problem for a
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formula
\psi
in
shallow normal form
to the non-emptiness problem of an automaton
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}

that is computable from

\psi
in exponential space.
For this, it suffices to recall that
\psi
must be of the form

\varphi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi
, where both
\varphi
and
\xi

are
{\mathsf{BE}}_{\pi}
formulas of depth at most
2
.
One uses Lemma
23
to construct the automata

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\neg\xi}
, whose languages contain
encodings of models of
\varphi
and
\neg\xi
, respectively.
From
\mathcal{A}_{\neg\xi}
, one can efficiently construct an automaton

\mathcal{A}_{\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\langle G\rangle}\neg\xi}
recognizing the language of words with
infixes accepted by
\mathcal{A}_{\neg\xi}
, thus encoding models of

\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\mathop{\langle G\rangle}}\neg\xi
(
=\neg\mathop{[G]}\xi
).
One then complements the latter automaton to obtain an automaton

\mathcal{A}_{\mathop{[G]}\xi}
accepting the encodings of models of
\mathop{[G]}\xi

Note that the latter step can be performed in exponential space in
the size of
\xi
, by using an online version of the classical
subset construction.
Finally, one computes the product of the automata
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and

\mathcal{A}_{\mathop{[G]}\xi}
, so as to recognize the language of encodings of

\psi=\varphi\>\wedge\>\mathop{[G]}\xi
.
It follows that non-emptiness of the latter language can be decided
in exponential space w.r.t. the size of the original formula
\psi
.
∎

5
Conclusions

We have settled the question of whether the logic
\mathsf{BE}
,
interpreted over homogeneous interval structures,
admits an elementary satisfiability problem.
We have actually answered the question by giving an
optimal
ExpSpace
decision procedure
(
ExpSpace
-hardness was shown in
[
3
]
).
As a by-product result, we have also devised a
normal form
for
\mathsf{BE}
formulas that enforces a small bound to
the number of nested modalities,
while preserving satisfiability.
Quite suprisingly such a normal form
can be computed in polynomial time from arbitrary

\mathsf{BE}
formulas, using a series of rewriting steps
reminiscent of a quantifier elimination technique
a-la Scott.

As for future work, one could try to see whether
similar techniques are applicable to extensions
of
\mathsf{BE}
with modalities based on other Allen’s
interval relations (e.g., overlap, meet, the
inverses of the prefix and suffix relations, etc.).
