A binomial random multigraph
Christos Pelekis

Abstract
Fix a positive integer n n , a real number p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] p\in(0,1] , and a (perhaps random) hypergraph ℋ \mathcal{H} on [ n ] [n] . We introduce and investigate the following random multigraph model, which we denote 𝔾 ⁡ ( n , p , ℋ ) \mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H}) : begin with an empty graph on n n vertices, which are labelled by the set [ n ] [n] . For every H ∈ ℋ H\in\mathcal{H} choose, independently from previous choices, a doubleton from H H , say D = { i , j } ⊂ H D=\{i,j\}\subset H , uniformly at random and then introduce an edge between the vertices i i and j j in the graph with probability p p , where each edge is introduced independently of all other edges.



Keywords
: random multigraphs; hypergraphs; threshold functions

Introduction

1.1
Notation and terminology

Let us begin with introducing some notation and terminology, which will remain fixed throughout the text.

Given a positive integer
n
, we denote by

[n]
the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The cardinality of a finite set
F
is denoted
|F|
.
A
hypergraph
,
\mathcal{H}
, on
[n]
is a collection of subsets of
[n]
. Let us emphasise that
we do not assume that each subset of
[n]
appears at most once in
\mathcal{H}
, and we allow repetitions. More precisely,
\mathcal{H}
is a
multi-hypergraph
but we slightly abuse terminology and use the term hypergraph instead. The elements of a hypergraph are referred to as
hyperedges
, or just
edges
.

Given a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
on
[n]
and a subset
H\subset[n]
, we denote by
m(H;\mathcal{H})
the
multiplicity
of
H
in the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
m(H;\mathcal{H})
is the number of times the set
H
appears in the collection
\mathcal{H}
.
The
degree
of a set
A\subset[n]
in
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(A)
, is the cardinality of the hypergraph
\{H\in\mathcal{H}:A\subset H\}
.
When
A=\{v\}
is a singleton, we write
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(v)
instead of
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(\{v\})
.

A hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is
simple

when
m(H;\mathcal{H})=1
, for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
.
Given a finite set
F\subset[n]
and an integer
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,|F|\}
, we denote by
\binom{F}{k}
the simple hypergraph consisting of all subsets of
F
whose cardinality equals
k
.
A hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is said to be
k
-
uniform
if
|H|=k
, for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
.
A
2
-uniform hypergraph is just a (multi)graph. Unless mentioned otherwise, all hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
considered in the text satisfy
|H|\geq 2
, for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
. Finally,
given a simple hypergraph, say
\mathcal{H}
, and a positive integer
m
, we denote by
\mathcal{H}^{(\times m)}
the hypergraph that contains each hyperedge of
\mathcal{H}
exactly
m
times.

Given two sequences
\{a_{n}\}_{n},\{b_{n}\}_{n}
, we write
a_{n}\ll b_{n}
, or
b_{n}\gg a_{n}
, when
\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\to 0
, as
n\to+\infty
. We also write
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
if there exists a constant
C>0
, which does not depend on
n
, and a positive integer
n_{0}
such that
a_{n}\leq C\cdot b_{n}
, for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Similarly, we write
a_{n}=\Theta(b_{n})
if there exist constants
C,c>0
, independent of
n
, and positive integer
n_{0}
such that

cb_{n}\leq a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
, for all
n\geq n_{0}
. In other words,
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are of the same order. Finally, we write
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
if there exists constant
C>0
such that
a_{n}\geq Cb_{n}
for all
n
large enough.

Given a positive integer
m
and a real number
q\in[0,1]
, we denote by

\textit{Bin}(m,q)
a
binomial random variable
of parameters
m
and
q
.
Furthermore, given real numbers
\{p_{s}\}_{s\in S}\in[0,1]
, indexed by some finite set
S
,
we denote by
\mathcal{B}(p_{s};s\in S)
the random variable that counts the number of successes in
|S|
independent trials, where trial
s\in S
has probability
p_{s}
of being a success.
The random variable
\mathcal{B}(p_{s};s\in S)
is referred to in the literature as a
Poisson-Binomial distribution
.
Let also
\textit{Hyp}(N,M,a)
denote a
hypergeometric random variable
counting the number of successes when a sample of size
a
is drawn without replacement from a population of size
N
which contains
M
successes.
Given two random variables
X,Y
, we write
X\sim Y
when
X
and
Y
have the same distribution.

In this article we shall be concerned with a model to generate random multigraphs.
A
multigraph
is a graph which is allowed to have multiple edges between pairs of vertices, and we shall be concerned with a random process which generates such graphs.
Random processes which generate (simple) graphs are referred to as
random graphs
, and constitute a classical topic of probabilistic combinatorics. The study of random graphs is
more than sixty years old and was initiated by Gilbert
[
9
]
and Erdős & Rényi
[
5
,
6
]
,
who introduced two quintessential random graph models.
The first, and perhaps most studied, model is the
binomial random graph
, denoted
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, which is obtained by independently introducing an edge between each pair of vertices in an empty graph on
n
vertices with probability
p
.
The second model is the
uniform random graph
, denoted
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
, which is obtained by choosing a graph uniformly at random from the set consisting of all graphs on the vertex set
[n]
having
m
edges. In other words,
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is a subset of
\binom{\binom{[n]}{2}}{m}
chosen uniformly at random.
Since their introduction, the two models have been studied extensively and have been generalized in a plethora of ways, thus generating a substantial amount of literature.
We refer the reader to
[
2
,
4
,
8
,
12
,
15
,
18
]
for excellent textbooks on the theory of random graphs.

We shall also be concerned with two extension of the above-mentioned random graph models to hypergraphs.
The
binomial random
k
-uniform hypergraph
, denoted
\mathbb{H}_{n,q;k}
, is a
k
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
whose hyperedges are obtained
by independently including each element
F\in\binom{[n]}{k}
in
\mathcal{H}
with probability
q
.
The
uniform random
k
-uniform hypergraph
, denoted
\mathbb{H}_{n,m;k}
, is a
k
-uniform
hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
whose hyperedges are the sets obtained by choosing an element from
\binom{\binom{[n]}{k}}{m}
uniformly at random (see
[
8
, Chapter 14]
).

Random multigraph models have also attracted considerable attention due to the fact that they are more appropriate for “real world” problems.
For instance, the need for considering models which allow multiple edges between pairs of vertices arises in the study of social networks, where interactions between individuals may become apparent in different contexts, or time periods (see, for example,
[
17
]
).
There is a significant amount of literature on random multigraphs, and
several models have been proposed in various contexts. Examples include a
configuration model
(see
[
18
, Chapter 7]
), as well as several generalisations and variations of the binomial random graph and the uniform random graph (see, for example,
[
1
,
3
,
7
,
10
,
11
,
16
]
, among many others).
In the present article we introduce and investigate the following random multigraph model.

1.2
The model

A
binomial random multigraph
, denoted
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
, is generated as follows:
Fix a positive integer
n
, a real number
p\in(0,1]
, and a (perhaps random)
hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
on
[n]
.
Begin with an empty graph on
n
vertices, which are labelled by the set
[n]
.
For every set
H\in\mathcal{H}
, choose, independently from previous choices, a doubleton from
H
, say
D=\{i,j\}\in\binom{H}{2}
, uniformly at random and then introduce an edge between the vertices
i
and
j
in the graph with probability
p
, where each edge is introduced independently of all other edges.

1.3
Relation to existing models

When
\mathcal{H}=\binom{[n]}{2}
, then
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{2})=\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, and therefore the binomial random graph is a special case of our model.
Observe that, when
\mathcal{H}
contains hyperedges such that
|H|\geq 3
, the multigraph

\mathbb{G}(n,1\,;\,\mathcal{H})
is still random, in contrast to
\mathbb{G}(n,1)
which is deterministic.

A straightforward way to generalize
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
is to sample
m
times (with or without replacement) a doubleton from each element of
\mathcal{H}
, and then introduce an edge between the corresponding pairs of vertices in the sample with probability
p
.
Special cases of the model, when the sampling is
with replacement
, have been reported in the literature. For example,
the model
\mathbb{G}\left(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{2}^{(\times m)}\right)

has been investigated in
[
10
,
13
]
, in the setting of hypotheses testing for clustering and classification, and may be seen as a natural generalisation of the binomial random graph to multigraphs having at most
m
edges between each pair of vertices. When the sampling is
without replacement
then, for every
H\in\mathcal{H}
, we sample
m\leq\binom{|H|}{2}
distinct doubletons from
H
uniformly at random, and then we flip a coin for every doubleton in the sample to decide whether we join the corresponding pairs of vertices in the graph. This model appears to be new.
Observe that the
m
distinct doubletons that are chosen uniformly at random from
\binom{H}{2}
correspond to a random sub-graph of the complete graph on the vertex set
H
, and the corresponding model, with
p=1
, may be seen as a “randomized version” of the binomial random motif graph, which is introduced in
[
1
]
.

One could also sample a different number of doubletons from each element of
\mathcal{H}
, and even randomize this number. A bit more precisely, let
\mathcal{H}
be a hypergraph, and suppose that

\{X_{H}\}_{H\in\mathcal{H}}
are mutually independent random variables, indexed by the edges of
\mathcal{H}
, taking values in the set of non-negative integers
\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
Let
\mathbb{H}{\{X_{H};H\in\mathcal{H}\}}
denote the (random) hypergraph that contains each element
H\in\mathcal{H}
exactly
X_{H}
times. That is, we draw from
X_{H}
, for each
H\in\mathcal{H}
, and then create a hypergraph
that contains each edge
H\in\mathcal{H}
exactly
X_{H}
times.
The model
\mathbb{G}\left(n,1\,;\,\mathbb{H}{\{X_{B};B\in\binom{[n]}{2}\}}\right)
is introduced in
[
3
]
, as a mutligraph analogue of the binomial random graph model.
The model
\mathbb{G}\left(n,1\,;\,\mathbb{H}{\{P_{B}\,;\,B\in\binom{[n]}{2}\}}\right)
, where
\{P_{B}\}_{B\in\binom{[n]}{2})}
are independent Poisson random variables of given mean is introduced in
[
16
]
, as a model to study biological networks. All the aforementioned models can be considered in a more general setting, where
\binom{[n]}{2}
is replaced by some other hypergraph on
[n]
. Such models correspond to a model
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,\,;\,\,\mathcal{H})
for which the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is random, but one could consider the model with more “exotic” random hypergraphs.
For example, one could consider the model

\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,k,q})
, which appears to be new.
The model
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,m;k})
reduces to
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
, for
p=1,k=2
.

In this article we shall mainly be concerned with the model
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})
, but we hope that we will be able to report on various other models in the future.

1.4
Main results

Given a multigraph property, say
\mathcal{P}
, we write
G\in\mathcal{P}
when the multigraph
G
posses property
\mathcal{P}
.
A “typical” question in the theory of the binomial random graph model
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
begins with a particular graph property, say
\mathcal{P}
, and asks for values
\{p_{n}\}_{n}
for which
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n})\in\mathcal{P})
tends to one, as n tends to infinity.
Such questions involve the notion of
thresholds
and
one remarkable discovery in the theory of random graphs, which is due to Erdős and Rényi, is the fact that several graph properties admit a threshold. That is, for several graph properties
\mathcal{P}
, there exists a sequence
\{p_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n}
such that the probability that a random graph
G_{n}\in\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n})
possesses
\mathcal{P}
tends to zero, when
p_{n}\ll p_{n}^{\ast}
, and tends to one when
p_{n}\gg p_{n}^{\ast}
. In other words, there is a “phase transition” in this limiting probability which transits from the property being very unlikely to the property becoming almost certain. In this article we shall be concerned with
thresholds in binomial random multigraphs.
Before being more precise, let us first recall some definitions.

A (multi)graph property
\mathcal{P}
is called
non-trivial
if the empty graph does not possess
\mathcal{P}
, while the complete graph does possess
\mathcal{P}
.
A (multi)graph property
\mathcal{P}
is called
monotone increasing

if
G\in\mathcal{P}
implies that
G+e\in\mathcal{P}
, i.e., the property
\mathcal{P}

is not destroyed after adding an edge
e
to the graph
G
. Similarly, a (multi)graph property
\mathcal{P}
is called
monotone decreasing
if
G\in\mathcal{P}
implies that
G-e\in\mathcal{P}
, i.e., the property
\mathcal{P}

is not destroyed by removing an edge
e
from the graph
G
.
Clearly, a property
\mathcal{P}
is monotone increasing if and only if its complement is monotone decreasing, and thus any statement about an increasing property translates to a statement about a decreasing one.
Examples of monotone increasing properties are connectivity, Hamiltonicity, triangle-containment, among others. An example of a monotone decreasing multigraph property is the graph being simple.
For deterministic hypergraphs, the corresponding notion of a threshold is analogous to the notion
of a threshold in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
.

Definition 1

(Threshold functions)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{H}_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
be a sequence of hypergraphs on
[n]
, and fix a monotone increasing (multi)graph property
\mathcal{P}
.
A
threshold function
for
\mathcal{P}
, in the model
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{n})
, is a sequence
\{c_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{n})\in\mathcal{P})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\,\,p_{n}\ll c_{n}^{\ast}\\
1,&\text{ if }\,\,p_{n}\gg c_{n}^{\ast}.\end{cases}

However, when the hypergraph is random, the corresponding notion of a threshold
has to take into account the underlying “randomness of the hypergraph”.
For example, thresholds in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,q;k})
are defined as follows.

Definition 2

(Threshold functions in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,q;k})
)
.

Let
k\geq 3
be fixed, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a monotone increasing (multi)graph property.
A
threshold function
for
\mathcal{P}
, in the model
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,q;k})
, is a sequence
\{c_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,q_{n};k})\in\mathcal{P})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\,\,p_{n}\cdot q_{n}\ll c_{n}^{\ast}\\
1,&\text{ if }\,\,p_{n}\cdot q_{n}\gg c_{n}^{\ast}.\end{cases}

A central line of research in the theory of random graphs is concerned with the
investigation of thresholds for various graph properties. A well-known result,
due to Bollobás and Thomason
(see
[
8
, Theorem 1.7]
),
asserts that every monotone increasing graph property admits a threshold in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. We believe that the Bollobás–Thomason theorem holds true for several “well-behaved” binomial random multigraphs. For example, we believe that the following holds true.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
k\geq 3
be fixed, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a non-trivial monotone increasing (multi)graph property. Then
\mathcal{P}
has a threshold in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})
.

We are unable to prove Conjecture
1
, though we will provide some evidence for its validity. Our first result implies that several increasing properties admit a “gapped threshold” in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
be fixed, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a non-trivial monotone increasing (multi)graph property which admits a threshold in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
.
Then there exist sequences
\{b_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n}
and
\{c_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})\in\mathcal{P}\right)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\,\,p_{n}\ll b_{n}^{\ast}\\
1,&\text{ if }\,\,p_{n}\gg c_{n}^{\ast}.\end{cases}

Observe that Theorem
1.1
does not provide a “gapped threshold” for all increasing properties
\mathcal{P}
, but only for properties that admit thresholds in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. For example, it provides no information for the property
\mathcal{P}=\,
“the graph is simple”.

The reader familiar with
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
will have already noticed several analogies between the binomial random graph and the binomial random multigraph models, and we will illustrate this analogy further.
Our next two results show that the probability that the latter model possesses
an increasing property is, similarly to the former model, an increasing function of
p
, as well as an increasing function of
\mathcal{H}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix a positive integer
n
, two real numbers
p_{1},p_{2}\in[0,1]
, and a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
on
[n]
.
If
p_{1}\leq p_{2}
then, for every monotone increasing property
\mathcal{P}
, it holds

\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{1}\,;\,\mathcal{H})\in\mathcal{P})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{2}\,;\,\mathcal{H})\in\mathcal{P})\,.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix a positive integer
n
, a real number
p\in[0,1]
, and two hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
on
[n]
.
If
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subset\mathcal{H}_{2}
then, for every monotone increasing property
\mathcal{P}
, it holds

\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{1})\in\mathcal{P})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{2})\in\mathcal{P})\,.

We also provide some additional evidence for the validity of Conjecture
1
, by
determining the limiting probability,
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p,\mathcal{H})\in\mathcal{P})
, for some graph properties
\mathcal{P}
in various random multigraph models.
We begin with a result which is related to the “simplicity threshold”.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the property “the graph is simple”, and let
k\geq 3
be fixed. The following hold true.

1.

If
p_{n}\ll\frac{1}{n^{k}}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})\in\mathcal{P}\right)=1\,.

2.

If
p_{n}\gg\frac{1}{n^{k-1}}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})\in\mathcal{P}\right)=0\,.

In other words, Theorem
1.4
provides estimates on the threshold function for the property “the graph is simple”.
We are unable to determine an exact threshold for this property. It is easy to see that a threshold
for the property “the graph contains at least one edge” is
\frac{1}{n^{k}}
(see Lemma
5.1
below) and, since it is not unreasonable to expect that
\mathbb{G}(n,p,\binom{[n]}{k})
contains multiple edges as long as it has some edges, perhaps a simplicity threshold equals
\frac{1}{n^{k}}
as well.

Theorem
1.4
has no analogue in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
but, for graph properties that make sense in both models, there seems to be an analogy between thresholds in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
and
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})
. However, in the latter case the calculations involving expectations and variances of various random
variables are significantly more complex.
A reason for this “complexity” is that the model
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})
, for
k\geq 3
, exhibits certain “dependencies” which are not present in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
.
Let us illustrate this a bit further.

For
B\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, let
E_{B}
be the number of edges between the two vertices in
B
. Then, in the model
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k}),k\geq 3
, the random variables
\{E_{B}\}_{B\in\binom{[n]}{2}}
are correlated; a fact that complicates the calculation of moments and probabilities.
For example, in order to compute the probability that a particular triangle, say
T
, is present in
\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k})
, we have to compute the probability that the two vertices of every
B\in\binom{T}{2}
are joined by at least one edge. The latter probability depends, among other things, on the probability
that the coin-flips corresponding to
the elements in
\mathcal{H}_{T}=\{H\in\binom{[n]}{k}:T\subset H\}
resulted in
\ell
edges between the vertices of
T
, where
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,|\mathcal{H}_{T}|\}
, and we are unable to find a neat way to calculate such probabilities for
k>3
. When
k=3
, then
\mathcal{H}_{T}
consists of a single element, a fact that simplifies the calculations, as we show in the proof of Theorem
1.6
below.
The proofs of the following results illustrate further the aforementioned dependencies.

Theorem 1.5
.

The property “the graph is connected” has threshold function
\frac{\log(n)}{n^{2}}
in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{3})
.

Given Theorem
1.5
and bearing in mind the corresponding result in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
, it may be reasonable to expect that a connectivity threshold in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{k}),k\geq 4,
is
\frac{\log(n)}{n^{k-1}}
.

Having determined the connectivity threshold in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{3})
, a next step may be to
determine the “triangle containment” threshold in this model.
A
triangle
in a multigraph
G
, with vertex set
[n]
, is a triplet of vertices
T\in\binom{[n]}{3}
such that all pairs of vertices from
T
are joined with at least one edge.
A threshold for “triangle containment” in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{3})
should be
\frac{1}{n^{2}}
, but we are unable to prove it. We can determine the order of the expected number of triangles in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{3})
, but are unable to determine the order of its variance, due to the aforementioned “dependencies”.
The next result, combined with standard first moment arguments, implies that the expected number of triangles in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,q;3})
goes to zero, when
p_{n}\cdot q_{n}\ll\frac{1}{n^{2}}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

The expected number of triangles in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,q;3})
is equal to

\binom{n}{3}\cdot\left\{(1-p\cdot q)\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\textit{Bin}\left(n-3,\frac{pq}{3}\right)\geq 1\right)\right)^{3}+p\cdot q\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\textit{Bin}\left(n-3,\frac{pq}{3}\right)\geq 1\right)\right)^{2}\right\}\,.

Note that the expected number of triangles in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{3})
are obtained from Theorem
1.6
for
q=1
. The expected number of triangles in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,m;3})

is of similar form and illustrates the, rather intuitive, idea that
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,q;3})
and

\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,m;3})
should be “close” to each other when
q=\frac{m}{\binom{n}{3}}
.

Theorem 1.7
.

The expected number of triangles in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,m;3})
is equal to

\binom{n}{3}\cdot\left\{\left(1-p\cdot\frac{m}{\binom{n}{3}}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\textit{Bin}(W,p)\geq 1\right)\right)^{3}+p\cdot\frac{m}{\binom{n}{3}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\textit{Bin}(W,p)\geq 1\right)\right)^{2}\right\}\,,

where
W\sim\textit{Hyp}(\binom{n}{3},n-3,m)
.

The purpose of our final result is to illustrate a bit further the above-mentioned “complexities of calculations” for values of
k
that are larger than three.

Theorem 1.8
.

The expected number of triangles in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{4})
is equal to

\binom{n}{3}\cdot\left\{\left(1-\sum_{i=0}^{2}p_{0i}^{(n-3)}\right)+\sum_{i=0}^{2}p_{0i}^{(n-3)}\cdot\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\textit{Bin}\left(\binom{n-3}{2},\frac{p}{6}\right)\geq 1\right)\right)^{3-i}\right\}\,,

where
p_{0i}^{(n-3)},i=0,1,2,
is the
(0,i)
-th element of the
(n-3)
-th power of the following transition matrix
of a homogeneous Markov chain with state space
\{0,1,2,3\}
:

P=\begin{array}[]{ccccc}&0&1&2&3\\
0&1-p/2&p/2&0&0\\
1&0&1-p/3&p/3&0\\
2&0&0&1-p/6&p/6\\
3&0&0&0&1\\
\end{array}\,\,.

2
The model as a mixture of random multigraphs

In this section we show that the model

\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
is a mixture of models of the form
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{D})
, for a particular class of
2
-uniform hypergraphs
\mathcal{D}
.
This requires some extra piece of notation.

Definition 3

(Shadow)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}\}
be a hypergraph on
[n]
.
Let
\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
denote the class consisting of all
2
-uniform hypergraphs
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}\}
, having
m
edges, which satisfy

D_{i}\in\binom{H_{i}}{2}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,m
. Clearly, if
\mathcal{H}
is
2
-uniform, then
\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})=\{\mathcal{H}\}
.
We refer to the class

\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
as the
shadow
of the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
.

Observe that each
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
is a
2
-uniform hypergraph consisting of
|\mathcal{H}|
doubletons.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix a positive integer
n
, a real number
p\in[0,1]
and a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
on
[n]
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a graph property.
Then

\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})\in\mathcal{P})=\prod_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{\binom{|H|}{2}}\cdot\sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{P}\right)\,,

where
\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
is the shadow of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

The multigraph
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
can be equivalently generated as follows:
We first choose, independently from previous choices, a doubleton
D\in\binom{H}{2}
uniformly at random, for each
H\in\mathcal{H}
.
This results in a collection consisting of
|\mathcal{H}|
doubletons, say
D_{1},\ldots,D_{|\mathcal{H}|}
, which form a
2
-uniform hypergraph, say
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then, given
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
, we flip a coin for every
D\in\mathcal{D}
to decide whether we put an edge between the vertices of
D
, with each coin-flip being independent from all others.
Hence, given
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
,
the probability that
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})\in\mathcal{P}
is equal to the probability that
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{P}
.
Notice that the probability of choosing a particular
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
is equal to
\prod_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\,\frac{1}{\binom{|H|}{2}}
.
The result follows from the law of total probability,
upon conditioning on
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
.
∎

3
Degree distribution

In this section we collect some observations
regarding the degree distribution of a vertex, and the number of edges between a pair of vertices in binomial random multigraphs.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
and fix a vertex
v\in[n]
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{v}=\{H\in\mathcal{H}:v\in H\}
and, for each
H\in\mathcal{H}_{v}
, set
p_{H}=\frac{2p}{|H|}
.
Then
\deg_{G}(v)\sim\mathcal{B}(p_{H};H\in\mathcal{H}_{v})
.

Proof.

Observe that vertex
v
has a potential edge for every
H\in\mathcal{H}_{v}
.
Now, given
H\in\mathcal{H}_{v}
, the probability of choosing a doubleton
D\in\binom{H}{2}
such that
i\in D
is equal to

\frac{|H|-1}{\binom{|H|}{2}}
. Hence the probability that vertex
v
is adjacent to some vertex

w\in H
, is equal to
\frac{|H|-1}{\binom{|H|}{2}}\cdot p
.
In other words, the number of edges adjacent to
vertex
v
equals the number of successes
in
|\mathcal{H}_{v}|
independent trials, where trial
H\in\mathcal{H}_{v}
has probability

\frac{|H|-1}{\binom{|H|}{2}}\cdot p=\frac{2p}{|H|}
of being a success. The result follows.
∎

Corollary 3.1
.

Fix a positive integers
n\geq k\geq 2
, a real number
p\in[0,1]
, and let

\mathcal{H}
be a
k
-uniform hypergraph on
[n]
. Let
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
and fix a vertex
v\in[n]
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{v}=\{H\in\mathcal{H}:v\in H\}
.
Then

\deg_{G}(v)\sim\textit{Bin}(|\mathcal{H}_{v}|,\frac{2p}{k})
.

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
3.1
.
∎

In other words, the number of edges that are incident to each vertex is a binomial random variable whose number of successes depend on the number of
k
-sets that contain the vertex.
Similar results holds true for the number of edges between a fixed pair of vertices.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
and fix two distinct vertices
u,v\in[n]
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{u,v}=\{H\in\mathcal{H}:\{u,v\}\subset H\}
.
Then the number of edges between vertices
u
and
v
is a
\mathcal{B}(p_{H};H\in\mathcal{H}_{u,v})
random variable, where

p_{H}=\frac{p}{\binom{|H|}{2}}
.

Proof.

For every
H\in\mathcal{H}_{u,v}
, the probability of choosing the doubleton
\{u,v\}
is equal to

\frac{1}{\binom{|H|}{2}}
. Therefore, the probability that vertex
u

is adjacent to vertex
v
in trial
H\in\mathcal{H}_{u,v}
is equal
to
\frac{1}{\binom{|H|}{2}}\cdot p
. The result follows.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Fix a positive integers
n\geq k\geq 2
, a real number
p\in[0,1]
, and let

\mathcal{H}
be a
k
-uniform hypergraph on
[n]
. Let
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
and fix two distinct vertices
i,j\in[n]
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{i,j}=\{H\in\mathcal{H}:\{i,j\}\subset H\}
.
Then the number of edges between the vertices
i
and
j
is a

\textit{Bin}\left(|\mathcal{H}_{i,j}|\,,\,\frac{p}{\binom{k}{2}}\right)
random variable.

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
3.2
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Fix positive integers
n\geq k\geq 2
and real numbers
p,q\in[0,1]
.
Let
v
be a vertex in
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\;\mathbb{H}_{n,q;k})
.
Then
\deg_{G}(v)\sim\textit{Bin}\left(\binom{n-1}{k-1},\frac{2p}{k}q\right)
.

Proof.

Let
X
be the number of edges in
\mathbb{H}_{n,q;k}
that contain vertex
v
.
Then
X\sim\textit{Bin}\left(\binom{n-1}{k-1},q\right)
.
Now, given
X
, it follows from Corollary
3.1
that
\deg_{G}(v)\sim\textit{Bin}(X,\frac{2p}{k})
, and the result follows.
∎

In the case of
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathbb{H}_{n,m;k})
, the degree of a vertex is a mixture of binomial random variables.
Recall that
\textit{Hyp}(N,M,a)
denotes a hypergeometric random variable.

Proposition 3.4
.

Fix positive integers
n\geq k\geq 2
, a real number
p\in[0,1]
and a positive integer
m\leq\binom{n}{k}
.
Let
v
be a vertex in
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p,\mathbb{H}_{n,m;k})
.
Then
\deg_{G}(v)\sim\textit{Bin}\left(W,p\right)
, where
W\sim\textit{Hyp}\left(\binom{n}{k},\binom{n-1}{k-1},m\right)
.

Proof.

Let
W
be the number of edges in
\mathbb{H}_{n,m;k}
that contain vertex
v
.
Note that is holds
W\sim\textit{Hyp}\left(\binom{n}{k},\binom{n-1}{k-1},m\right)
.
Now, given
W
, it follows from Corollary
3.1
that
\deg_{G}(v)\sim\textit{Bin}(W,\frac{2p}{k})
.
In other words, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\deg_{G}(v)=\ell)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq\ell}\mathbb{P}(\deg_{G}(v)=\ell\,|\,W=j)\cdot\mathbb{P}(W=j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq\ell}\mathbb{P}\left(\textit{Bin}(\ell,\frac{2p}{k})=j\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}(W=j)

and the result follows.
∎

4
Monotonicity

In this section we prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We may assume that
p_{1}<p_{2}
; say,
p_{2}=p_{1}+(1-p_{1})\cdot p
, for some
p\in(0,1)
.
Begin with generating a graph
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p_{1}\,;\,\mathcal{H})
.
That is, choose, independently from previous choices, a doubleton
D\in\binom{H}{2}
uniformly at random from every
H\in\mathcal{H}
.
This results in
|\mathcal{H}|
doubletons, say
D_{1},\ldots,D_{|\mathcal{H}|}
, and for every such doubleton
we, independently, flip a coin to decide whether we introduce an edge between the
corresponding vertices in the graph; where the outcome of each coin is a success with probability
p_{1}
.
A doubleton
D\in\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{|\mathcal{H}|}\}
is said to be
bad
if
the outcome of the corresponding coin-flip was a failure; this happens with probability
1-p_{1}
.
Now, for every bad doubleton from
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{|\mathcal{H}|}\}
,
we independently introduce an edge between the corresponding vertices
in the graph
G
with probability
p
. Let
G^{\prime}
be the resulting graph.
Clearly, it holds
G\subset G^{\prime}
and

G^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}(n,p_{2}\,;\,\mathcal{H})
. If
G\in\mathcal{P}
,
then it also holds
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
, and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
1.2
; so we briefly sketch it.
Begin with generating a graph
G\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{1})
. Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{1}
.
For every
H\in\mathcal{H}
choose a doubleton, say
D=\{i,j\}\in\binom{H}{2}
uniformly at random,
and then introduce an edge between the vertices
i
and
j
in
G
with probability
p
. Let

G^{\prime}
denote the resulting graph. Clearly, it holds
G\subset G^{\prime}
and

G^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{2})
.
∎

5
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
.
Let us begin with determining the threshold of the simplest possible increasing graph property.

Lemma 5.1
.

The property “the graph contains at least one edge” has threshold
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}
in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{n})
.

Proof.

Let
G_{n}\in\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{n})
, and observe that
G_{n}
is empty if and only if all
|\mathcal{H}_{n}|
coin-tosses corresponding to doubletons chosen from elements of
\mathcal{H}

were failures. In other words, it holds

\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\text{ is empty})=\mathbb{P}(\textit{Bin}(|\mathcal{H}_{n}|,p_{n})=0)=(1-p_{n})^{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\,.

Suppose first that
p_{n}\ll\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}
. Then, using the inequality
1-x\geq e^{-2x}
, for
x\in[0,1/2]
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\text{ is empty})=(1-p_{n})^{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\geq e^{-2p_{n}|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\to 1\,,\text{ as }n\to\infty\,.

Assume now that
p_{n}\gg\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}
. Then, using the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\text{ is empty})=(1-p_{n})^{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\leq e^{-p_{n}|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\to 0\,,\text{ as }n\to\infty\,.

Hence
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}
is the desired threshold.
∎

We will also need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
k\geq 3
be fixed, and
let
\{\mathcal{H}_{n}\}_{n}
be a sequence of
k
-uniform hypergraphs on
[n]

such that
\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(B)=\Theta(n^{k-2})
, for all
n
and all
B\in\binom{[n]}{2}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the monotone increasing property “all pairs of vertices are joined by at least one edge”.
Then

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,1\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{n})\in\mathcal{P})=1\,.

Proof.

For every
B=\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, let
Y_{B}
denote the indicator of the event “vertices
i
and
j
are joined by at least one edge”, and set
Y=\sum_{B\in\binom{[n]}{2}}Y_{B}
.
Then
Y
counts the number of pairs of vertices from
[n]
that are joined by at least one edge. Note note that
Y<\binom{n}{2}
implies that there exists
B\in\binom{[n]}{2}
such that
Y_{B}=0
.
Hence the union bound combined with Corollary
3.2
imply that there exists constant
C
, which does not depend on
n
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y<\binom{n}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in\binom{[n]}{2}}\mathbb{P}(Y_{B}=0)\,\leq\,\binom{n}{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}}\right)^{C\cdot n^{k-2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\cdot e^{-\frac{C\cdot n^{k-2}}{\binom{k}{2}}}\,=\,e^{\log(\binom{n}{2})-\frac{C\cdot n^{k-2}}{\binom{k}{2}}}\to 0\,,

as desired.
∎

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\binom{[n]}{k})
be chosen uniformly at random.
Then
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{D}\,\text{is complete})\to 1
, as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Set
\mathcal{H}_{n}=\binom{[n]}{k}
and note that
\deg_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(B)=\binom{n-2}{k-2}
for all
B\in\binom{[n]}{2}
. Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the monotone increasing property “all pairs of vertices are joined by at least one edge”.
Lemma
5.2
and Theorem
2.1
imply that

1=\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,1\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{n})\in\mathcal{P})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}^{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}}\sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\binom{[n]}{k})}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,1\,;\,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{P})\,.

Now observe that
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,1\,;\,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{P})
is equal to either
1
or
0
, depending on whether
\mathcal{D}
is complete or not.
Hence, if
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}
is the indicator of the event ”
\mathcal{D}
is complete”, it holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}^{|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}}\sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\binom{[n]}{k})}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}\,=\,1\,,

as desired.
∎

We may now proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

To simplify notation, let us set
\mathcal{H}_{n,k}=\binom{[n]}{k}
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be an increasing multigraph property
which admits a threshold in
\mathbb{G}(n,p)
. Let
\{c_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n}
be a threshold for
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\binom{[n]}{2})
, and let

G_{n}\in\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\mathcal{H}_{n})
.

Set
b_{n}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{n^{k}}
, and suppose that
p_{n}\ll b_{n}^{\ast}
.
Since
\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\text{ is empty})\leq\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\notin\mathcal{P})
,
Lemma
5.1
implies
\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\notin\mathcal{P})\to 1
, as
n\to\infty
, and the first statement follows.

To prove the second statement, suppose that
p_{n}\gg c_{n}^{\ast}
. Theorem
2.1
then yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\in\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k}{2}^{-|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{n})}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\binom{k}{2}^{-|\mathcal{H}_{n}|}\sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{n}):\mathcal{D}\text{ complete}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{P})\,.

, by Theorem

, and hence

where

The second statement follows from Corollary
5.1
and the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{G}(n,p_{n}\,;\,\binom{[n]}{2})\in\mathcal{P})\to 1
, as

.
∎

Thresholds

6.1
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Let us begin with the following observation, whose proof is included for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
n\geq k\geq 3
be positive integers, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Suppose that
X\sim\textit{Bin}(c\cdot n^{k-2},\frac{1}{n^{k-1-\varepsilon}})
, for some constant
c
which does not depend on
n
.
Then, for large enough
n
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2)\,\geq\,\frac{1}{n^{2-\varepsilon}}\,.

Proof.

Assume first that
\varepsilon<1
.
Since
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, it follows that
c\cdot n^{k-2}\leq n^{k-1-\varepsilon}
, for large enough
n
, and therefore for such
n
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2)\geq\mathbb{P}(X=2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{c\cdot n^{k-2}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n^{k-1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{k-1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{c\cdot n^{k-2}-2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c\cdot n^{k-2}}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n^{k-1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{k-1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{n^{k-1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c\cdot n^{k-2}}{2n^{k-1-\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c}{4}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2-2\varepsilon}}

where in the second estimate we have used the inequality
\left(1-\frac{1}{x}\right)^{x}\geq\frac{1}{4}
, for
x\geq 2
.
Now, for
n
large enough, it holds
\left(\frac{c}{4}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2-2\varepsilon}}\geq\frac{1}{n^{2-\varepsilon}}
, and the result follows.

Suppose now that
\varepsilon=1
.
If
c\geq 1
, then
\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2)\geq\mathbb{P}(\textit{Bin}(n^{k-2},\frac{1}{n^{k-2}})\geq 2)\geq 1/2
, since the median of a
\textit{Bin}(n^{k-2},\frac{1}{n^{k-2}})
random variable is equal to its mean (see
[
14
]
).
If
c<1
then, in the same way as above, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c\cdot n^{k-2}}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n^{k-2}}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{k-2}}\right)^{n^{k-2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c}{4}\right)^{2}\,,

and the latter is larger than
\frac{1}{n}
, for large enough
n
.
∎

We now proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
Recall, from Section
2
, the definition of the shadow of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
on
[n]
, which is denoted
\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
, and recall that, given
A\subset[n]
and a (multi)hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
on
[n]
, we denote by
m(A;\mathcal{H})
the multiplicity of
A
in
\mathcal{H}
.

Recall also that in order to generate a multigraph
G_{n}\in\mathbb{G}(n,p\,;\,\mathcal{H})
we randomly select

\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H})
and then we flip a coin for each doubleton
D\in\mathcal{D}
to decide whether we introduce an edge between the two vertices of
D
. We refer to this coin-flipping procedure as the
coin-flips corresponding to
\mathcal{D}

, for all

.
For every
\mathcal{D}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathcal{H}_{n})

\mathbb{E}(I_{B}(\mathcal{D}))=\mathbb{P}(I_{B}(\mathcal{D})=1)=\mathbb{P}(\textit{Bin}(m(B;\mathcal{D}),p_{n})\geq 2)\,,

Proof.

From Corollary

it follows that

\mu_{n}:=\mathbb{E}(X_{n})=n\cdot\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{\binom{n-1}{2}}\,.

Therefore, using the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{n})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\log(n)-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\cdot\binom{n-1}{2}}\to 0,\,\,\text{ when }\,p_{n}\gg\frac{\log(n)}{n^{2}}\,.

Markov’s inequality now gives

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}(X_{n})\to 0

and therefore
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=0)\to 1

Now assume that
p_{n}\ll\frac{\log(n)}{n^{2}}

For
i\in[n]

A_{i}
be the event that “vertex
i
is isolated”. Then, using the inequality

1-x\geq e^{-2x}
, for
x\in[0,1/2]

\mu_{n}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=n\cdot\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{\binom{n-1}{2}}\geq e^{\log(n)-\frac{4p_{n}}{3}\cdot\binom{n-1}{2}}\to+\infty\,.

Now, for any two distinct vertices

i,j\in[n]
, let
\mathcal{H}_{i}=\{H\in\binom{[n]}{3}:i\in H\,,\,j\notin H\}
,

\mathcal{H}_{j}=\{H\in\binom{[n]}{3}:j\in H\,,\,i\notin H\}
and
\mathcal{H}_{ij}=\{H\in\binom{[n]}{3}:\{i,j\}\subset H\}
. Then it holds
|\mathcal{H}_{i}|=|\mathcal{H}_{j}|=\binom{n-2}{2}
and
|\mathcal{H}_{ij}|=n-2
, and so

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})=\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{2\binom{n-2}{2}}\cdot(1-p_{n})^{n-2}\leq\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{2\binom{n-2}{2}+(n-2)}.

\gamma_{n}:=\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})\leq n(n-1)\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{2\binom{n-2}{2}+(n-2)}\,,

which yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}(X_{n}^{2})}{(\mathbb{E}(X_{n}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{n}+\gamma_{n}}{\mu_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{n}}+\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\mu_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{n}}+\frac{n(n-1)\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{2\binom{n-2}{2}+(n-2)}}{n^{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{2\binom{n-1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{n}}+\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{2p_{n}}{3}\right)^{n-2}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{n}}+\frac{1}{\left(1-p_{n}\right)^{n}}\,.

Now notice that the assumption

implies

\left(1-p_{n}\right)^{n}\geq e^{-2np_{n}}\to 1\,.

Since
\mu_{n}\to+\infty
, the second moment method yields

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\geq 1)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}(X_{n}))^{2}}{\mathbb{E}(X_{n}^{2})}\to 1\,.

This implies that
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=0)\to 0
, and the result follows.

2&0&0&1-p/6&p/6\\
3&0&0&0&1\\
\end{array}

We claim that for
i=0,1,2
,
\mathbb{P}(Y=i)
is equal to the
(0,i)
-element of
P^{n-3}
, denoted
p_{0i}^{(n-3)}
.
In other words, we claim that
\mathbb{P}(Y=i)
is equal to the probability that, beginning from state
0
, a Markov chain with transition matrix
P
will be in state
i
after
n-3
steps.
Given the claim, we then also obtain
\mathbb{P}(Y=3)=1-\sum_{i=0}^{2}p_{0i}^{(n-3)}
.

To prove the claim, recall that a sample from
Y
is obtained after “flipping the
coins corresponding to
\mathcal{H}_{T}
”. We now flip the coins corresponding to
\mathcal{H}_{T}
one by one in a series of steps.
Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n-3}
be an enumeration of the elements of
\mathcal{H}_{T}
.
For each step
t=1,\ldots,n-3
, we choose a doubleton
D_{t}\in\binom{H_{i}}{2}
uniformly at random and then flip a coin in order to decide whether we join the elements of
D_{i}
with an edge in the graph.
For each step
t=1,\ldots,n-3
, let
Y^{(t)}
be the number of elements in
\binom{T}{2}
which have been
joined with at least one edge at step
t
, and set
Y^{(0)}=0
.
Note that
Y^{(n-3)}=Y
and that if
Y^{(t-1)}=j
, for some
j=0,1,2
, then
Y^{(t)}\in\{j,j+1\}
.
Now observe that for any
t\geq 1
and any
j=0,1,2
we have

\mathbb{P}(Y^{(t)}=j+1\,|\,Y^{(t-1)}=j)=\frac{3-j}{6}\cdot p\,,

because, given that
Y^{(t-1)}=j
, exactly
3-j
out of the six doubletons in
\binom{H_{t}}{2}
are favourable (in the sense that they may potentially introduce an edge between two previously unjoined vertices of
T
)
and, given that a favourable doubleton is selected, there is a probability
p
that an edge will be introduced between the
two corresponding vertices of
T
.
In other words,
\{Y^{(t)}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a homogeneous absorbing Markov chain, which begins at state
0
, having transition matrix given by
P
. Theorem
1.8
follows upon recalling that
Y=Y^{(n-3)}
.
