Random subsets of Cantor sets generated by trees of coin flips Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author.
Pieter Allaart ∗, Taylor Jones

Abstract
. We introduce a natural way to construct a random subset of a homogeneous Cantor set C C in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] via random labelings of an infinite binary tree. For a fixed parameter p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) , each edge in the tree is independently labeled 0 0 with probability p p , or 1 1 with probability 1 − p 1-p . Each infinite labeled path down the tree thus determines a unique point in C C , and the collective of all these points is our random subset. When p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 , we show that the random subset is almost surely of full Hausdorff dimension in C C but of zero Hausdorff measure. For p ≠ 1 / 2 p\neq 1/2 we prove non-trivial upper and lower bounds for the Hausdorff dimension. We also consider deterministic labelings of the tree where, for a fixed integer m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 , every m m th edge is labeled 1 1 , and compute the exact Hausdorff dimension of the resulting subset of C C .

1.
Introduction

Consider an iterated function system (IFS)
\{f_{0},f_{1}\}
with two similar contractions
f_{0},f_{1}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
that satisfy
f_{0}((0,1))\cap f_{1}((0,1))=\emptyset
. In other words,
\{f_{0},f_{1}\}
satisfies the open set condition with the open set being an interval. We assume furthermore that the IFS
\{f_{0},f_{1}\}
is equicontractive; that is, there is a constant
0<c\leq 1/2
such that

|f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y)|=c|x-y|\qquad\mbox{for all $x,y\in[0,1]$, \ \ $i=0,1$}.

The attractor of the IFS
\{f_{0},f_{1}\}
is a nonempty compact set
C
satisfying
C=f_{0}(C)\cup f_{1}(C)
. When
c<1/2
,
C
is a Cantor set with

\dim_{H}C=\dim_{B}C=-\frac{\log 2}{\log c},

where
\dim_{H}
and
\dim_{B}
denote Hausdorff and box-counting dimensions, respectively.
When
c=1/2
,
C
is simply the unit interval
[0,1]
.

We are interested in certain random subsets of
C
that are generated by binary trees of coin flips. The idea – made precise in the next section – is simple: In a full infinite binary tree, we randomly label each edge with either
0
with probability
p
or
1
with probability
1-p
, where
p\in(0,1)
is a fixed parameter. Each path through the tree from the root then has a random infinite label sequence which is the coding of a point in our random subset, which we denote by
F
. When
p=1/2
, the random subset
F
has almost surely the same Hausdorff dimension as
C
itself, but with zero Hausdorff measure in its dimension. In particular, when
c=1/2
, our construction generates a random subset of the unit interval which has Lebesgue measure zero but full Hausdorff dimension one almost surely. When
p\neq 1/2
, we obtain instead a random subset of
C
with strictly smaller Hausdorff (and upper box-counting) dimension. For this biased case we are, unfortunately, only able to obtain rough (but non-trivial) bounds on the dimension. We also consider a deterministic version of our randomization scheme, corresponding to a sequence of coin flips in which every
m
th flip is heads, and compute exactly the Hausdorff dimension of the resulting subset of
C
. Interestingly, this dimension is not strictly monotone in
m
.

Following Hutchinson’s result
[
7
]
on the Hausdorff dimension of self-similar fractals generated by an IFS satisfying the open set condition, there was a rapid rise in analogous results on a broad class of random constructions known as
statistically self-similar fractals
. Below, we discuss a few models from the literature for constructing a random Cantor set in the line.
The randomness we are interested in resides in the process of deletion of intervals, as opposed to position or length of intervals (considered in
[
10
,
9
]
).

Informally, the model which is most closely related to the one considered in this article beings with a partition of the unit interval into
M\geq 2
interior disjoint subintervals of length
1/M
, and a vector of probabilities
\mathbf{p}=(p_{0},\dots,p_{M-1})\in[0,1]^{M}
(not necessarily a probability vector).
At stage
1
, the
i
th interval in the partition is either kept or discarded with probabilities
p_{i}
and
1-p_{i}
respectively. Within each non-discarded subinterval this process is repeated proportionally, and independently of past stages. The limit set, conditioned on non-extinction, results in a random Cantor-like fractal
F
, say.
In
[
4
,
6
,
12
]
, it was determined independently that

\text{dim}_{H}F=\frac{1}{M}\log\big(\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}p_{i}\big)

almost surely on
F\neq\varnothing
.
This model was also used in
[
3
]
to study the question of when the arithmetic difference of two random Cantor sets contain an interval.

Another model for constructing random Cantor sets, which removes the need for conditioning on non-extinction, is to fix a sequence of integers
N_{1},N_{2},\dots
for which
1\leq N_{k}\leq M
for all
k
. At each stage
k
in the construction, and within each interval remaining at stage
k-1
, randomly and independently choose
N_{k}
subintervals of size
M^{-k}
, then discard the rest.
This was used in
[
2
]
, where the almost sure Hausdorff dimension was computed, as well as a close variation of it in
[
13
]
in connection to tube null sets.

The random Cantor sets we consider here will also never go extinct, but our construction involves a complex form of interdependence which implies that the number of “basic intervals” remaining at level
n
is neither Markovian nor a martingale, even after appropriate scaling. As a result, traditional martingale approaches or branching arguments are not applicable. In fact, we could think of only one natural (random) mass distribution on our random Cantor subset, which led us to a lower bound that is (for the biased coin case) unfortunately significantly below the best upper bound we could find.

We point out that our construction also differs from the
V
-variable fractals of
[
1
]
, and their generalization in
[
8
]
to random code tree fractals.

2.
Notation and main result

For definiteness we assume that
f_{0}([0,1])
lies to the left of
f_{1}([0,1])
. Further, for ease of presentation, we will also assume that both
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are orientation preserving; in other words, there are constants
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
such that
f_{0}(x)=cx+b_{0}
and
f_{1}(x)=cx+b_{1}
. However, this is not essential for our main results; see Remark
2.5
below.

Let
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\Omega^{*}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{0,1\}^{n}
. We think of elements of
\Omega
or
\Omega^{*}
as sequences of “coin flips”.
For any finite sequence
\omega^{*}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})\in\Omega^{*}
with
|\omega^{*}|=n
we define the basic open set, or cylinder,

[\omega^{*}]:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\omega|_{n}=\omega^{*}\},

where
\omega|_{n}
denotes the restriction of
\omega
to the first
n
letters. Let
{\mathcal{S}}:=\{[\omega^{*}]:\omega^{*}\in\Omega^{*}\}
. For each
p\in(0,1)
we define a premeasure
\mu_{p}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
by

\mu_{p}([\omega^{*}]):=p^{\#\{i\leq|\omega^{*}|\>:\>\omega_{i}^{*}=0\}}(1-p)^{\#\{i\leq|\omega^{*}|\>:\>\omega_{i}^{*}=1\}},

and observe that
\mu_{p}
extends uniquely to a probability measure on
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}})
, where
{\mathcal{F}}
is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing
{\mathcal{S}}
, i.e. the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
.

Now let

{\mathcal{I}}^{*}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{0,1\}^{n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathcal{I}}=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

be the set of all finite binary words and infinite binary words, respectively. (While
\Omega
and
{\mathcal{I}}
are the same set, it is helpful to use separate notation because we think of
\Omega
as the sample space for our randomization, and of
{\mathcal{I}}
as indexing paths through an infinite binary tree that will receive labels from elements of
\Omega
.)
We define an order
<_{*}
on
{\mathcal{I}}^{*}
in a natural way: For any pair of words
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})
and
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})
in
{\mathcal{I}}^{*}
,

(1)

\mathbf{i}<_{*}\mathbf{j}
if
m<n
, and

(2)

\mathbf{i}<_{*}\mathbf{j}
if
m=n
and
\mathbf{i}<_{\text{LEX.}}\mathbf{j}
in the lexicographic order.

We think of
<_{*}
as ordering a binary tree from top to bottom, left to right.

Next, for each
\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{I}}^{*}
we define
\kappa:{\mathcal{I}}^{*}\to\mathbb{N}
by

\kappa(\mathbf{i})=\#\{\mathbf{j}\in{\mathcal{I}}^{*}:\mathbf{j}<_{*}\mathbf{i}\}.

Note that
\kappa
is well-defined as there are but finitely many words less than
\mathbf{i}
in the
<_{*}
order.
Thus the first few values of
\kappa
are
\kappa(0)=0
,
\kappa(1)=1
,
\kappa(0,0)=2
,
\kappa(0,1)=3
,
\kappa(1,0)=4
, etc.
Observe that
\kappa
defines an order-preserving bijection from
({\mathcal{I}}^{*},<_{*})\to(\mathbb{N},<)
.

Now we define the collection of random variables
X_{\mathbf{i}}:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
by

X_{\mathbf{i}}(\omega)=\omega_{\kappa(\mathbf{i})},\qquad\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{I}}^{*}.

These will be the random labels of the edges in the binary tree.

For any
\omega\in\Omega
, we define a subset of
C
by

F(\omega):=\Bigg\{x=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{X_{\mathbf{i}|_{1}}(\omega)}\circ f_{X_{\mathbf{i}|_{2}}(\omega)}\circ\dots\circ f_{X_{\mathbf{i}|_{n}}(\omega)}([0,1]):\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots)\in{\mathcal{I}}\Bigg\}.

(We emphasize that due to the randomization, different choices of the path
\mathbf{i}
could yield the same point
x
in the limit set
F(\omega)
.)

To help visualize the random subset
F(\omega)
, the reader may wish to imagine a kind of branching random walk. The walk begins with a single ancestor at time
0
occupying the interval
[0,1]
. At each stage
n\geq 0
, each individual of generation
n
splits into two descendants of generation
n+1
. If the individual occupies the interval
I
, its descendants, independently of each other, move either to the left subinterval
f_{0}(I)
with probability
p
, or the right subinterval
f_{1}(I)
with probability
1-p
. Note that at stage
n
there are
2^{n}
individuals, collectively occupying some (random) subcollection of the intervals
I_{\mathbf{i}},\mathbf{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Let
E_{n}
denote the union of the occupied intervals; then
F(\omega)=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}
.

The difficulty in computing the dimension of
F(\omega)
stems from the fact that, in order to understand the probability law of the branching random walk below stage
n
, it is not sufficient to know merely the number of occupied intervals at stage
n
, but in fact one needs to know the individual occupancy rates for each of the stage
n
intervals. As a result, we are able to obtain the exact dimension only for the case
p=1/2
.

Note that for all
\omega
we have
F(\omega)\subseteq C
, and so

\dim_{H}F(\omega)\leq-\log 2/\log c
. Our goal is to establish the following more precise dimension bounds:

Theorem 2.1
.

For
\mu_{p}
-almost every
\omega\in{\mathcal{I}}
,

(1)

\frac{\log(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2})}{\log c}\leq\dim_{H}F(\omega)\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}F(\omega)\leq h(\xi(p)),

where

h(x):=-\frac{x\log x+(1-x)\log(1-x)}{\log c}

and

\xi(p):=\max\left\{-\frac{\log(2p)}{\log(1-p)-\log(p)},-\frac{\log 2(1-p)}{\log(p)-\log(1-p)}\right\}.

See Figure
1
for an illustration of these upper and lower bounds. Observe that the upper bound tends to
0
as
p
tends to
0
or
1
; however, it is still quite large even for values of
p
very close to
0
and
1
. This stems from the fact that the upper bound is the composition of two functions which both have vertical tangent lines at
0
and
1
.

Corollary 2.2
.

When
p=1/2
, we have
\dim_{H}F(\omega)=\dim_{B}F(\omega)=\dim_{H}C=-\frac{\log 2}{\log c}
for
\mu_{p}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.

This follows since both bounds are equal to
-\frac{\log 2}{\log c}
when
p=1/2
.

Recall that every basic interval in the construction of the deterministic Cantor set,
C
, at level
n
can be identified by
I_{\mathbf{i}}
for some
\mathbf{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Precisely,
I_{\mathbf{i}}=f_{i_{1}}\circ\dots f_{i_{n}}([0,1])
.
We first define a random probability measure on
F(\omega)
: For any
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, define the event

(2)

A(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}):=\big\{\omega\in\Omega:(X_{j_{1}}(\omega),X_{j_{1},j_{2}}(\omega),\dots,X_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{n}}(\omega))=\mathbf{i}\big\}.

Then for any
\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{I}}^{*}
(with
|\mathbf{i}|=n
, say) define

(3)

m_{\omega}(I_{\mathbf{i}}):=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}1_{A(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})}.

Intuitively,
m_{\omega}(I_{\mathbf{i}})
is the relative number of branches labeled
\mathbf{i}
in the tree at time
n
.
It follows again from standard theorems that
m_{\omega}
uniquely defines a measure on
F(\omega)
.
We use this random measure along with the potential-theoretic method
[
5
, Theorem 4.13]
to prove the lower bound in Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem

, lower bound.

If

and

. Note that
k:=k(x,y)\geq 2
since the IFS consists of only two maps, and

|x-y|\geq c^{n+k},

since each basic interval of level

.
There are now four possibilities:

(i)

x\in I_{\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}0}
and

;

(ii)

x\in I_{\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}}
and
y\in I_{\mathbf{i}10^{k-2}1}
;

(iii)

x\in I_{\mathbf{i}10^{k-2}}
and

;

(iv)

x\in I_{\mathbf{i}10^{k-2}1}
and
y\in I_{\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}}
.

Note that (iii) and (iv) are the same as (i) and (ii) with the roles of

and

For
0<t<-\log 2/\log c

\mathbf{i}
to the

\Phi_{t}^{\mathbf{i}}(m_{\omega})

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}^{\mathbf{i}}(m_{\omega})

\displaystyle:=\iint\limits_{\{(x,y):x\wedge y=I_{\mathbf{i}}\}}|x-y|^{-t}dm_{\omega}(x)dm_{\omega}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\ \ \iint\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\wedge y=I_{\mathbf{i}},\\
k(x,y)=k\end{subarray}}|x-y|^{-t}dm_{\omega}(x)dm_{\omega}(y)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}c^{-(n+k)t}\left[m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}0}\big)m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}10^{k-2}}\big)+m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}}\big)m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}10^{k-2}1}\big)\right].

Now by the definition of

, we have

\displaystyle m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}0}\big)=\frac{1}{2^{n+k}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n+k}}1_{A(\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}0,\mathbf{j})}(\omega),

\displaystyle m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}10^{k-2}}\big)=\frac{1}{2^{n+k-1}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n+k-1}}1_{A(\mathbf{i}10^{k-2},\mathbf{j})}(\omega),

and so, letting
E_{p}
denote the expectation operator associated with
\mu_{p}
,

\displaystyle E_{p}

\displaystyle\left[m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}0}\big)m_{\omega}\big(I_{\mathbf{i}10^{k-2}}\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n+2k-1}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n+k}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n+k-1}}\mu_{p}\left(A(\mathbf{i}01^{k-2}0,\mathbf{j})\cap A(\mathbf{i}10^{k-2},\mathbf{j}^{\prime})\right)

The expectation of the other term in (

) is similar, but with a factor
p^{k-1}(1-p)^{k}
instead of
p^{k}(1-p)^{k-1}
. Thus, combining the two terms, we obtain

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{p}\left[\Phi_{t}^{\mathbf{i}}(m_{\omega})\right]&\leq 2\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}c^{-(n+k)t}\cdot\frac{1}{4^{n}}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{k-1}S_{p}(\mathbf{i})\\

where

because

and so

.

To estimate the double sum
S_{p}(\mathbf{i})
, we introduce the sets

W_{n}(q):=\{(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k})\in\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{n}:|\mathbf{j}\wedge\mathbf{k}|=q\},\qquad q=0,1,\dots,n,

where
\mathbf{j}\wedge\mathbf{k}
is the longest common prefix of
\mathbf{j}
and
\mathbf{k}
.
Observe that for any

\#W_{k}(0)=2^{2k-1}
. Furthermore, we have the recursive relation
\#W_{n}(q)=2^{q}\cdot\#W_{n-q}(0)
for

.
Thus,

\#W_{n}(q)=2^{q}2^{2n-2q-1}=2^{2n-q-1},\qquad q=0,1,\dots,n-1.

For

we have
\#W_{n}(n)=2^{n}
. In all cases, then,
\#W_{n}(q)\leq 2^{2n-q}
.

Before continuing with our previous calculation we define for any
\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{I}}^{*}

to be the suffix of

\mathbf{i}|_{q}

).

c_{0}(\mathbf{i}|_{q})+c_{0}(\mathbf{i}|^{q})=c_{0}(\mathbf{i})
. Now we have

S_{p}(\mathbf{i})=\sum_{q=0}^{n}\sum_{(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k})\in W_{n}(q)}\mu_{p}\big(A(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})\cap A(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{k})\big).

Since the labels

\displaystyle S_{p}(\mathbf{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=0}^{n}2^{2n-q}p^{c_{0}(\mathbf{i}|_{q})}(1-p)^{c_{1}(\mathbf{i}|_{q})}p^{2c_{0}(\mathbf{i}|^{q})}(1-p)^{2c_{1}(\mathbf{i}|^{q})}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}\sum_{l=\max\{0,k-q\}}^{\min\{k,n-q\}}\binom{n-q}{l}\binom{q}{k-l}\cdot 2^{-q}p^{l}(1-p)^{n-q-l}

\displaystyle E_{p}\Big[\Phi_{t}(m_{\omega})\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}E_{p}\Big[\Phi_{t}^{\mathbf{i}}(m_{\omega})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq K_{c,t,p}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c^{-tn}(n+1)\big(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}\big)^{n}.

This series converges if and only if

, i.e.

t<\frac{\log\big(p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}\big)}{\log c}=:s(p).

Therefore, for any

, which means that
\Phi_{t}(m_{\omega})<\infty
for
\mu_{p}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
. Hence,
\dim_{H}F(\omega)\geq s(p)
almost surely.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For each

Proof.

For

\xi(p)=-\frac{\log(2p)}{\log(1-p)-\log(p)}.

So

\displaystyle\xi(p)\geq 1/2\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad-2\log 2p\geq\log(1-p)-\log p

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad-\log 4\geq\log(1-p)+\log p

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\log 4p(1-p)\leq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 4p(1-p)\leq 1,

\displaystyle\xi(p)<1-p\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad-\log 2p<(1-p)\log(1-p)-(1-p)\log p
