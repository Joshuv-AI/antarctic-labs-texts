Generalization analysis with deep ReLU networks for metric and similarity learning
Junyu Zhou, Puyu Wang, Ding-Xuan Zhou

Abstract
While metric and similarity learning has been extensively studied from several theoretical perspectives, a rigorous understanding of its generalization performance is still lacking. In this paper, we investigate the generalization behavior of metric and similarity learning by exploiting the specific structure of the true metric (i.e., the target function). In particular, by deriving the explicit form of the true metric for metric and similarity learning with the hinge loss, we construct a structured deep ReLU neural network as an approximation of the true metric, whose approximation ability depends on the network complexity. Here, the network complexity is characterized by the network depth, the number of nonzero weights, and the number of computational units. Based on the hypothesis space consisting of such structured deep ReLU networks, we establish excess risk bounds for metric and similarity learning by carefully controlling both the approximation error and the estimation error . An explicit excess risk rate is derived by choosing the proper capacity of the constructed hypothesis space. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first generalization analysis that provides explicit excess risk bounds for metric and similarity learning. In addition, we investigate properties of the true metric for metric and similarity learning under more general loss functions. Experiments show that the proposed model is empirically competitive and better captures the underlying similarity structure. * * * The first two authors contributed equally.

Introduction

Metric and similarity learning aims to study a metric
d
from an observed sample
S
that estimates the distance or the similarity between a pair of observers
(x,x^{\prime})
. The observed sample
S=\{Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is assumed to be independently drawn from an unknown distribution
\rho
defined over the input-output space
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is closed and bounded and
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},...,y_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
is the set of labels. Let
\tau(y,y^{\prime})
be the reducing function defined by
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=1
if
y=y^{\prime}
and
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=-1
else. The performance of
d
on a pair
(z,z^{\prime})
is usually measured by
\mathbf{1}_{[\tau(y,y^{\prime})d(x,x^{\prime})>0]}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{[A]}
is the indicator function taking value
1
if the event
A
happens and
0
otherwise. Since the indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{[\tau(y,y^{\prime})d(x,x^{\prime})>0]}
is non-convex and discontinuous, one often considers replacing it with a convex surrogate loss
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Given by
\ell\left(\tau(y,y^{\prime})d(x,x^{\prime})\right)
, the generalization error (true risk) associated with a metric
d
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(d)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\left[\ell\big(\tau(Y,Y^{\prime})d(X,X^{\prime})\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\tau(y,y^{\prime})d(x,x^{\prime})\big)d\rho(z)d\rho(z^{\prime}).

The corresponding empirical error based on the sample
S
is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{S}(d)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\ell\big(\tau(Y_{i},Y_{j})d(X_{i},X_{j})\big).

Let
\hat{d}_{z}=\arg\min_{d\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{E}_{S}(d)
be the minimizer of the empirical error over a hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
, and
d_{\rho}=\arg\min\mathcal{E}(d)
be the true metric that minimizes the generalization error over the space of all measurable functions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
.
The statistical generalization performance of
\hat{d}_{z}
is measured by the excess generalization error
\mathcal{E}(\hat{d}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})
, i.e., the distance between the expected risk
\mathcal{E}(\hat{d}_{z})
and the least possible risk
\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})
.
To examine the excess generalization error, we use the following error decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{d}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})=\{\mathcal{E}(\hat{d}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\mathcal{H}})\}+\{\mathcal{E}(d_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})\},

(1.1)

where
d_{\mathcal{H}}=\arg\min_{d\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{E}(d)
is the minimizer of the generalization error over the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
. The terms
\mathcal{E}(\hat{d}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\mathcal{H}})
and
\mathcal{E}(d_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})
in (
1.1
) are called the estimation error and the approximation error, respectively.

In this paper, we address the gap in the theoretical understanding of the generalization behavior of metric and similarity learning with the hinge loss by exploiting the specific structure of the true metric.

•

We also investigate structural properties of the true metric and the problem formulation for metric and similarity learning under general loss functions.
In particular, we show that the bias term appearing in many existing works
[

,

is not intrinsic to the theoretical characterization of the true metric, and it is natural to focus on the case where the output space

This provides further theoretical justification for the use of symmetric models such as the Mahalanobis distance in metric learning.

2
Generalization analysis with deep ReLU networks

We begin by introducing some notations that will be frequently used in the rest of the paper. Let
Z=(X,Y)
and
Z^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
be random variables independently following
\rho
, and
\rho(\cdot|x)
be the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X=x
. We denote the conditional probability of
Y=Y^{\prime}
given
X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\eta(x,x^{\prime})=Prob\{Y=Y^{\prime}|X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}=\int_{\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}}\mathbf{1}_{[y=y^{\prime}]}\,d\rho(y|x)d\rho(y^{\prime}|x^{\prime}),

(2.1)

which is the probability that two observers
x
and
x^{\prime}
are affiliated to the same class. One can see that
\eta
only depends on the conditional distributions of
Y
conditioned on the observers. For any
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, if
\rho(\cdot|x^{\prime})=\rho(\cdot|x^{\prime\prime})
, then there holds
\eta(x,x^{\prime})=\eta(x,x^{\prime\prime})
. In addition, it can be readily observed that
\eta(x,x^{\prime})
is symmetric, i.e.,
\eta(x,x^{\prime})=\eta(x^{\prime},x)
for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Denote by
P_{x}:=[Prob\{Y=y_{1}|X=x\},\ldots,Prob\{Y=y_{m}|X=x\}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the probability distribution vector of
Y
conditioned on
X=x
. Then the conditional probability (
2.1
) can be represented as the standard inner product in the Euclidean space:

\displaystyle\eta(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Prob\{Y=Y^{\prime}=y_{i}|X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Prob\{Y=y_{i}|X=x\}Prob\{Y^{\prime}=y_{i}|X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle=\langle P_{x},P_{x^{\prime}}\rangle.

We aim to estimate the excess generalization error
\mathcal{E}(\hat{d}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})=\{\mathcal{E}(\hat{d}_{z})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\mathcal{H}})\}+\{\mathcal{E}(d_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})\}
, where
\hat{d}_{z}
is the minimizer of the empirical error over the hypothesis space. In this section, we focus on the hinge loss

\ell(\tau(y,y^{\prime})d(x,x^{\prime}))=\big(1+\tau(y,y^{\prime})d(x,x^{\prime})\big)_{+},

where
(\cdot)_{+}=\max\{0,\cdot\}
.

2.1
Explicit form of the true metric

Understanding the true metric
d_{\rho}
is crucial to estimating the approximation error. The following theorem presents an explicit representation of the true metric
d_{\rho}
for the hinge loss.

Theorem 1
.

The true metric with the hinge loss can be represented as

\displaystyle d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=sgn(1-2\eta(x,x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=sgn(1-2\langle P_{x},P_{x^{\prime}}\rangle)

for almost every pair
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

By the tower property of the conditional expectation, the generalization error with a metric
d
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(d)=\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Y|X,Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}}\left[\ell(\tau(Y,Y^{\prime})d(X,X^{\prime})\right]\right].

The above observation implies that for almost every pair
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, the true metric
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
is obtained by minimizing the inner conditional expectation which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{Y|X=x,Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}}\left[\ell(\tau(Y,Y^{\prime})t)\right]

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}Prob\{Y=Y^{\prime}|X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}\ell(t)+Prob\{Y\neq Y^{\prime}|X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}\ell(-t)

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x,x^{\prime})\ell(t)+(1-\eta(x,x^{\prime}))\ell(-t),

(2.2)

where the second equality is due to the definition of
\tau(y,y^{\prime})
.

According to (
2.2
), the true metric with the hinge loss can be expressed as

\displaystyle d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\eta(x,x^{\prime})\left(1+t\right)_{+}+\left(1-\eta(x,x^{\prime})\right)\left(1-t\right)_{+}\right\}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\begin{aligned} &(1-\eta(x,x^{\prime}))(1-t),&&\text{if }t<-1,\\
&(2\eta(x,x^{\prime})-1)t+1,&&\text{if }t\in[-1,1],\\
&\eta(x,x^{\prime})(1+t),&&\text{if }t>1.\end{aligned}\right.

For the case
\eta(x,x^{\prime})>1/2
, note that the objective function is piecewise linear and tends to
+\infty
as
t\to\pm\infty
, we can derive that
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=-1
and the corresponding minimum of the objective function is
2(1-\eta(x,x^{\prime}))
. For the case
\eta(x,x^{\prime})<1/2
, we can get that
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=1
and the corresponding minimum is
2\eta(x,x^{\prime})
. For the case
\eta(x,x^{\prime})=1/2
, we have
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})\in[-1,1]
and the corresponding minimum be
0
. Then we can conclude that
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=sgn(1-2\eta(x,x^{\prime}))
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

Unlike using the Mahalanobis distance to measure the similarity between sample pairs, deep metric learning
[
10
,
14
,
17
]
aims to learn a nonlinear embedding function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\Phi\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
with
s\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, such that similar data points
x,x^{\prime}
are close in the embedding space
\Phi
under a predefined distance function
d(\phi(x),\phi(x^{\prime}))
and far from each other if they are dissimilar. Note Theorem
1
shows that for the hinge loss, the true predictor
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=sgn(1-2\langle P_{x},P_{x^{\prime}}\rangle)
. Then the true embedding function is
\phi(x)=P_{x}
under this setting, and the distance function can be further defined as
d(\phi(x),\phi(x^{\prime}))=sgn(1-2\langle P(x),P(x^{\prime})\rangle)
. This indicates that learning a nonlinear embedding function in deep metric learning with the hinge loss is in fact learning the conditional probability
P_{x}
.

According to the specific structure of the true metric
d_{\rho}
given in Theorem
1
, we can construct a structured deep network with ReLU activation as an approximation of
d_{\rho}
and further design the corresponding hypothesis space. Before that, we first introduce some assumptions and useful lemmas.

To define the Sobolev smoothness of the conditional probabilities
p_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
, we take
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{p}
in the remainder of this section.
We define the Sobolev space
W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{p})
as the space of functions on
[0,1]^{p}
lying in
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{p})
along with their partial derivatives up to order
r
. The norm in
W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{p})
is defined as

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{p})}:=\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}_{+}:\|\alpha\|_{1}\leq r}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{p})},

where
\|\alpha\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\alpha_{i}|
denotes the
l^{1}
norm of
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p})\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}_{+}
, and
D^{\alpha}f=\frac{\partial^{\|\alpha\|_{1}}f}{\partial^{\alpha_{1}}x_{1}\cdots\partial^{\alpha_{d}}x_{d}}
denotes the partial derivatives of
f
with order
\alpha
.

For notational simplicity, denote by
p_{i}(x)=Prob\{Y=y_{i}|X=x\}
the
i
-th component of
P_{x}
. The following assumption means that all
r
-th partial derivatives of
p_{i}(x)
exist and their
L^{\infty}
norms are bounded by
1
.

Assumption 1
.

The conditional probability
p_{i}(x)\in W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{p})
has the Sobolev norm not greater than
1
for each
i=1,\ldots,m
(This upper bound for the Sobolev norm can be extended to any finite constant, for simplicity we set it to
1
).

In binary classification problems, we often introduce a noise condition that says the ambiguous points
Prob\{Y=1|X=x\}\approx 1/2
occur with a small probability. When this condition is satisfied, it suggests that the classification problem is well-posed and learning algorithms have the potential to achieve faster convergence rates. Similarly, we can extend this notion in metric and similarity learning to suggest that the probability of
\eta(X,X^{\prime})\approx 1/2
is relatively small. In the rest of this paper, we denote by
C_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
a constant only depending on parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, and it may differ from line to line.

Assumption 2

(Tsybakov’s noise condition)
.

There exist constants
\theta>0
and
C_{\theta}>0
such that for any
t>0
,

Prob\{|\eta(X,X^{\prime})-1/2|\leq t\}\leq C_{\theta}t^{\theta}.

2.2
Structured deep ReLU networks

For
L\in\mathbb{N}
, let
h:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
be a deep ReLU network of depth
L
with activation function
\sigma(x)=\max\{x,0\}
, defined by

\displaystyle h(x)=\sigma(T_{L}\sigma(T_{L-1}\cdots\sigma(T_{1}x+b_{1})\cdots+b_{L-1})+b_{L}),

(2.3)

where
\sigma
acts componentwise. For each
l=1,\ldots,L
,
T_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{w_{l}\times w_{l-1}}
and
b_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{w_{l}}
denote, respectively, the weight matrix and bias vector of the
l
-th layer, where
w_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
is the width of the
l
-th layer and
w_{0}=p
is the input dimension. We define the number of nonzero parameters and the number of computational units of
h
by
h
be
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|T_{l}\|_{0}+\|b_{l}\|_{0}
and
\sum_{l=1}^{L}w_{l}
respectively, where
\|\cdot\|_{0}
denotes the number of nonzero entries of the corresponding matrix or vector.

The following two lemmas established in
[
24
]
show the expressive power of deep ReLU networks in the setting of approximations in Sobolev spaces and the product function.

Lemma 1
.

For any
p,r\in\mathbb{N}
,
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
and any function
f\in W^{r,\infty}([0,1]^{p})
with Sobolev norm not larger than
1
, there exists a deep ReLU network
h
with depth at most
C_{p,r}\log(1/\epsilon)
and the number of nonzero weights and computational units at most
C_{p,r}\epsilon^{-\frac{p}{r}}\log(1/\epsilon)
such that

\displaystyle\|h-f\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{p})}\leq\epsilon.

We will use a deep ReLU network
h_{i}
to approximate the conditional probability
p_{i}\in[0,1]
later. According to Lemma
1
, if Assumption
1
holds, we have
h_{i}\in[-1,2]
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, there exists a deep ReLU network
\phi
with the depth and the number of weights and computation units at most
C\log(1/\epsilon)
such that

\displaystyle\|\phi(x,y)-xy\|_{L^{\infty}([-1,2]^{2})}\leq\epsilon\ \text{ and }\ \phi(x,y)=0\ \text{ if }\ xy=0.

We are now ready to construct a structured deep ReLU network to approximate the true metric
d_{\rho}
, defined by

d(x,x^{\prime}):=F_{a}\Big(1-2\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi(h_{i}(x),h_{i}(x^{\prime}))\Big),

(2.4)

where
F_{a}(t):=\frac{1}{a}\big(\sigma(t+a)-\sigma(t-a)-a\big)
is a fixed two-layer ReLU network with
a>0
. The value of
a
will be chosen later, see Theorems
2
and
4
for details. Here,
\phi
is the deep ReLU network introduced in Lemma
2
for approximating the product function
xy
, and each
h_{i}
is a sub-network of the form (
2.3
). We assume that all sub-networks
h_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, have the same depth.

Main idea of the construction.

The main idea behind the construction of (
2.4
) is to approximate the true metric
d_{\rho}
component by component according to its representation
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=sgn(1-2\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(x)p_{i}(x^{\prime}))
given in Theorem
1
. Specifically, we first construct
m
sub-networks
h_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, to approximate the conditional probabilities
p_{i}
, respectively. Next, we introduce a deep ReLU network
\phi(h_{i}(x),h_{i}(x^{\prime}))
to approximate the product
h_{i}(x)h_{i}(x^{\prime})
. By Lemma
2
,
\phi(h_{i}(x),h_{i}(x^{\prime}))
provides a reliable approximation of
h_{i}(x)h_{i}(x^{\prime})
. Finally, the output layer
F_{a}(\cdot)
is used to approximate the sign function, following the idea in
[
30
]
. Indeed,
F_{a}(\cdot)
coincides with
sgn(\cdot)
on the interval
(-\infty,-a)\cup(a,\infty)
, while being linear on
[-a,a]
so as to approximate the discontinuity of
sgn(\cdot)
near the origin. The resulting structure of (
2.4
) is illustrated in Figure
1
.

2.3
Error analysis

In this subsection, we estimate the approximation error and the estimation error, and then derive the excess generalization error bound for the constructed deep ReLU networks.
We say that a network
d
has complexity
(L,W,U)
if its depth, the number of possibly nonzero weights, and the number of computational units are
L
,
W
, and
U
, respectively. The hypothesis space consisting of structured deep networks of the form (
2.4
) is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(L,W,U)=\big\{d(x,x^{\prime})\text{ of the form \eqref{eq:structured-NNs}}

\displaystyle:\text{the complexity of $d$ }

(2.5)

\displaystyle\text{ does not exceed $(L,W,U)$}\big\}.

Here, the complexity of
d
is obtained by summing the corresponding quantities over its sub-networks. Specifically, the depth of
d
is given by the sum of the depths of
h_{1}
,
\phi
, and
F_{a}
(recall that all
h_{i}
have the same depth). Moreover, the numbers of possibly nonzero weights and computational units of
d
are

2\sum_{i=1}^{m}W_{h_{i}}+mW_{\phi}+W_{F_{a}}+c\quad\text{and}\quad 2\sum_{i=1}^{m}U_{h_{i}}+mU_{\phi}+U_{F_{a}}+c,

respectively, where
W_{\gamma}
and
U_{\gamma}
denote the corresponding quantities of the sub-network
\gamma\in\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{m},\phi,F_{a}\}
, and
c
is an absolute constant. The capacity of the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
is measured by
(L,W,U)
. As these parameters increase, the capacity of
\mathcal{H}
becomes larger.

The following theorem establishes approximation error bounds of the structured deep networks of the form (
2.4
).

Theorem 2

(Approximation error)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. For any
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, if the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
defined in (
2.5
) has the depth
C_{p,r,m,\theta}\log(1/\epsilon)
and the number of possibly nonzero weights and computation units
C_{p,r,m,\theta}\epsilon^{-\frac{p}{r(\theta+1)}}\log(1/\epsilon)
, then there exists a deep ReLU network
d_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
of the form (
2.4
) with
a=C_{\theta}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{\theta+1}}
such that

\mathcal{E}(d_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{E}(d_{\rho})\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Note

Remark
.

Theorem
2
implies that the approximation ability of
d_{\mathcal{H}}
improves as the capacity of the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
increases. However, as the hypothesis space becomes larger, the model may become overly flexible, leading to a larger estimation error. This indicates a trade-off between the approximation error and the estimation error. We will choose a proper hypothesis space to obtain the explicit excess generalization error rate (see Theorem
4
).

To derive upper bounds for the estimation error, we measure the capacity of the hypothesis space by its pseudo-dimension. The pseudo-dimension is defined as the VC-dimension of the corresponding subgraph class. A comprehensive definition of VC-dimension can be found in
[
9
,
21
,
27
]
.

definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions from
[0,1]^{p}
to
\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{F}^{+}:=\{(x,t)\in[0,1]^{p}\times\mathbb{R}:f(x)>t,f\in\mathcal{F}\}
be the corresponding sub-graph set. The pseudo-dimension
Pdim(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
is defined as

Pdim(\mathcal{F}):=VC(\mathcal{F}^{+})
,

where
VC(\mathcal{F}^{+})
is the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{F}^{+}
. Furthermore, if
Pdim(\mathcal{F})<\infty
, then we call
\mathcal{F}
a VC-class.

If we solely apply the uniform boundedness (first-order condition) of the hypothesis space, the estimation error bound is of the order
O(1/\sqrt{n})

[
4
,
6
]
, which is often quite loose. To derive a tighter upper bound of the estimation error, the variance condition (second-order condition) should be taken into consideration.

definition 2
.

Let
\beta\in(0,1]
and
M>0
, and
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X},\rho\times\rho)
is a function class with nonnegative first order moment, i.e., for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
\mathbb{E}[f]\geq 0
.
We say
\mathcal{F}
has a variance-expectation bound with parameter pair
(\beta,M)
, if for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathbb{E}[f^{2}]\leq M(\mathbb{E}[f])^{\beta}.

definition 3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
We say a function
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
\alpha>0
if for any
x,y\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle|g(x)-g(y)|\leq\alpha\|x-y\|_{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm.

The following lemma is a specialization of part (a) of Theorem 1 in
[
29
]
to our metric learning setting.
Indeed, the symmetry of the loss function (III.2) in
[
29
]
is satisfied in our setting, and Assumption 3 in
[
29
]
follows from the standard entropy bound for a uniformly bounded hypothesis class of finite pseudo-dimension (see
[
3
]
for more details).
By applying Theorem 1(a) in
[
29
]
with these observations and absorbing the resulting constants and logarithmic factors into the generic constant, we obtain the following estimate.

Lemma 3

Let
V=Pdim(\mathcal{H})
be the pseudo-dimension of the hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
, and
\ell(\tau(y,y^{\prime})d(x,x^{\prime}))

is a predictor from
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
to

•

•

proposition 1

(Variance-expectation bound)

2

Theorem 4

(Excess generalization error)
.

Suppose Assumptions

2

Proof.

[

Remark
.

The assumptions used in the above theorem are tailored to the present proof technique in this paper.
In particular, the proof of Theorem

relies on a uniform approximation of the conditional probability function

together with the outer approximation

of the sign function.
An

3
Structural properties of the true metric under general losses

In this section, we investigate structural properties of the problem setting and the true metric for metric and similarity learning under a general loss function.
Specifically, we first show that the bias term
b
is not intrinsic to the theoretical characterization of the true metric
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
, and to assume that the output space
\mathcal{Y}
consists of finitely many labels, as we do throughout the paper. We then prove that the true metric is symmetric for almost all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, which provides further justification for the use of symmetric models such as the Mahalanobis distance in metric learning. Finally, we show that, for convex, nonnegative, and nondecreasing losses, the true metric between two identical samples is always less than or equal to that between two different samples.

Bias term at the population level.

Unlike many existing works
[
4
,
10
,
12
,
15
,
17
,
25
]
, we do not introduce a bias term
b>0
in the loss function
\ell
. We show that, at the population level, this term only induces a translation of the true metric, and is therefore not essential for its theoretical characterization.

Denote by

\mathcal{E}_{b}(d)=\mathbb{E}_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\left[\ell(\tau(Y,Y^{\prime})(d(X,X^{\prime})-b)\right]

the generalization error of
d
with bias term
b>0
. Let
\tilde{d}_{\rho}:=\arg\min_{d\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{E}_{b}(d)
be the corresponding true metric under this biased loss. Analogously to (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\tilde{d}_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x,x^{\prime})\ell(t-b)+(1-\eta(x,x^{\prime}))\ell(b-t)

\displaystyle=b+\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x,x^{\prime})\ell(s)+(1-\eta(x,x^{\prime}))\ell(-s)

\displaystyle=b+d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})

for almost every pair
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. This shows that the bias term
b
merely induces a constant shift and is therefore inessential in the theoretical characterization. Hence, throughout the paper, we set
b=0
.

The output space is finite.

In almost all theoretical and empirical works on metric and similarity learning
[
2
,
4
,
7
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
25
]
, the label space
\mathcal{Y}
is assumed to be finite, and often even binary, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}=\{+1,-1\}
. A finite label space corresponds to a discrete distribution of
Y
. This naturally raises the question of what happens when the distribution of
Y
is continuous. The following proposition answers this question.

proposition 2
.

If the distribution of
Y
is continuous, then the true metric is almost surely equal to a generalized constant
c\in[-\infty,+\infty]
.

Proof.

For almost every pair
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, it follows from (
2.2
) that

\displaystyle d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x,x^{\prime})\ell(t)+(1-\eta(x,x^{\prime}))\ell(-t).

(3.1)

Since the distribution of
Y
is continuous, we have
Prob\{Y=Y^{\prime}\}=0
. Hence,
\eta(x,x^{\prime})=0
for almost every pair
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Combining this with (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\ell(-t).

Therefore,
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
is almost surely equal to a generalized constant
c\in[-\infty,+\infty]
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition
2
shows that, when the distribution of
Y
is continuous, one has
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=c\in[-\infty,+\infty]
for almost every pair
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. In other words, the distances or similarities between almost all sample pairs are identical. Therefore, it is natural to focus on the case where the label space
\mathcal{Y}
is finite.

Remark
.

The above proposition suggests that, for a general distribution of
Y
, the continuous part plays no essential role in determining the true metric, since the conditional probability
\eta
depends only on the discrete part of the distribution. Moreover, this phenomenon is also tied to the choice of the reducing function
\tau
. Indeed, we assume that
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=1
only when
y=y^{\prime}
, an event that has probability zero when
Y
is continuously distributed. Under this setting, the labels therefore play essentially no role in the learning problem.

Regularities of the true metric.

In contrast to predictors in ranking problems
[
1
,
6
,
11
]
, a metric
d
is intended to reflect the distance or similarity between a given pair of objects, rather than a rank or an ordering. Therefore, it is natural to require that
d
be independent of the order of the sample pair
(x,x^{\prime})
, that is,
d(x,x^{\prime})=d(x^{\prime},x)
. The following proposition shows that the true metric
d_{\rho}
indeed satisfies this symmetry property. This provides theoretical justification for constructing hypothesis spaces using symmetric models such as the Mahalanobis distance
(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}M(x-x^{\prime})
or the pairwise similarity function
x^{\top}Mx^{\prime}
.

proposition 3
.

The true metric
d_{\rho}
is symmetric, i.e., almost surely we have

d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=d_{\rho}(x^{\prime},x).

Proof.

By (
2.2
), the proof directly follows from the symmetry of
\eta(x,x^{\prime})
.
∎

In mathematical terms, the distance between any two identical points is defined to be zero. In particular, the distance
d(\phi(x),\phi(x^{\prime}))=\|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})\|_{2}
used in deep metric learning
[
10
,
14
,
17
]
and the Mahalanobis distance
d(x,x^{\prime})=(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}M(x-x^{\prime})
used in traditional distance metric learning
[
4
,
25
]
both vanish when
x=x^{\prime}
. Consequently, for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, one has
d(x,x)\leq d(x,x^{\prime})
. However, this property may not hold for the true metric.

For example, consider the true metric associated with the hinge loss; see Theorem
1
. Let the number of labels be
m=3
, and suppose that
P_{x}=[3/5,1/5,1/5]
and
P_{x^{\prime}}=[1,0,0]
.
Then
\eta(x,x)=\langle P_{x},P_{x}\rangle=11/25<3/5=\langle P_{x},P_{x^{\prime}}\rangle=\eta(x,x^{\prime})

and hence

d_{\rho}(x,x)=sgn(1-2\eta(x,x))=1>-1=sgn(1-2\eta(x,x^{\prime}))=d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime}).

This shows that in general, the true metric need not satisfy the intuitive property
d_{\rho}(x,x)\leq d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

Moreover, this behavior is determined by the underlying distribution
\rho
. It is therefore natural to seek sufficient conditions on
\rho
under which
d_{\rho}(x,x)\leq d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})\text{ for all }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

Note the minimizer in (
2.2
) may not be unique, we first introduce the following definition.

definition 4
.

For any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
a\in[0,1]
, let
t^{*}(a):=\inf\{s\in\mathbb{R}|s\in\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}a\ell(t)

+(1-a)\ell(-t)\}
be the infimum of all the minimizers of problem (
2.2
) with conditional probability
a
. We define
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=t^{*}(\eta(x,x^{\prime}))
. Then
d_{\rho}(x,x)\leq d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
if
t^{*}(\eta(x,x))\leq t^{*}(\eta(x,x^{\prime}))
.

Remark
.

For many losses like the modified least squares
\ell(-t)=\max\{1-t,0\}^{2}
, the hinge loss
\ell(-t)=\max\{1-t,0\}
, the exponential loss
\ell(-t)=\exp(-t)
, and the logistic regression loss
\ell(-t)=\ln(1+\exp(-t))
, the minimizer
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
is unique for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}

[
26
]
. Definition
4
defines
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
as the minimum of all the minimizers, and the comparison is for the minimum values of
d_{\rho}(x,x)
and
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
. We do not make the assumption of the uniqueness of the minimizer here.

Assumption 3
.

The loss function
\ell
is convex, non-decreasing, and non-negative.

Remark
.

This assumption is natural in our setting because we often use a convex loss to implement algorithms efficiently.
For any sample pair
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, the distance or the similarity
d(x,x^{\prime})
is supposed to be small when
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=1
, then we expect the loss
\ell(d(x,x^{\prime}))
to increase as
d(x,x^{\prime})
increases. For the case of
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=-1
, the monotonicity of
\ell
can be discussed similarly.

According to (
2.2
), once the loss
\ell
is fixed, the true metric
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
is completely determined by the conditional probability
\eta(x,x^{\prime})
. It is therefore natural to investigate conditions on
\eta(x,x^{\prime})
under which the property
d_{\rho}(x,x)\leq d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
holds. As shown in (
2.1
), when
\eta(x,x^{\prime})=Prob\{Y=Y^{\prime}\mid X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}
is large, the samples
x
and
x^{\prime}
are more likely to belong to the same class. In that case, one expects the distance or dissimilarity between them to be small, and hence it is natural to expect that
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})\geq d_{\rho}(x,x)
whenever the corresponding conditional probabilities satisfy
\eta(x,x^{\prime})\leq\eta(x,x)
.

For
\eta\in[0,1]
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

\displaystyle Q(\eta,t)=\eta\ell(t)+(1-\eta)\ell(-t).

(3.2)

It’s obvious that
t^{*}(\eta)\in\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}Q(\eta,t)
.
The following lemma shows that
t^{*}(\eta)
is non-increasing on
[0,1]
, which is the key step to prove
d_{\rho}(x,x)\leq d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})
for the general case.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose Assumption
3
holds. If
0\leq\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}\leq 1
, then
t^{*}(\eta_{2})\leq t^{*}(\eta_{1})
.

Proof.

When
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}
,
\eta_{1}=0
or
\eta_{2}=1
, the proof is trivial. Then we only consider the strict inequality in the condition such that
0<\eta_{1}<\eta_{2}<1
.

We first show that for any fixed
\eta\in(0,1)
, if there exists a
t<t_{1}
such that
Q(\eta,t)<Q(\eta,t_{1})
, then we must have
t^{*}(\eta)<t_{1}
. We prove this by contradiction. Suppose
t^{*}(\eta)=t_{1}
or
t^{*}(\eta)>t_{1}
. The first case is impossible because it then follows that
Q(\eta,t)<Q(\eta,t^{*}(\eta))
, which contradicts to the definition of
t^{*}(\eta)
; for the second case, we have

\displaystyle\frac{t^{*}(\eta)-t_{1}}{t^{*}(\eta)-t}Q(\eta,t)+\frac{t_{1}-t}{t^{*}(\eta)-t}Q(\eta,t^{*}(\eta))

\displaystyle<\

\displaystyle\frac{t^{*}(\eta)-t_{1}}{t^{*}(\eta)-t}Q(\eta,t_{1})+\frac{t_{1}-t}{t^{*}(\eta)-t}Q(\eta,t_{1})

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle Q(\eta,t_{1}),

where the inequality uses the assumption
Q(\eta,t_{2})<Q(\eta,t_{1})
and the definition of
t^{*}(\eta)
. Since
\frac{t^{*}(\eta)-t_{1}}{t^{*}(\eta)-t_{2}}t_{2}+\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{t^{*}(\eta)-t_{2}}t^{*}(\eta)=t_{1}
, this strict inequality contradicts to the convexity of
Q(\eta,\cdot)
.

From the above discussion, to prove
t^{*}(\eta_{2})\leq t^{*}(\eta_{1})
, we just have to show that there exists a
t<t^{*}(\eta_{1})
such that
Q(\eta_{2},t)<Q(\eta_{2},t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
or
t^{*}(\eta_{1})
is also a minimizer of
Q(\eta_{2},\cdot)
. We are interested in the behavior of
\ell
at point
t
when
t
is on the left side of
t^{*}(\eta_{1})
. Since
\ell
is non-decreasing, the possibilities of the behavior can be divided into the following two cases.

Case 1:
There exists a
t<t^{*}(\eta_{1})
such that
\ell(t)=\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
. In this case we have

\displaystyle Q(\eta_{2},t)-Q(\eta_{2},t^{*}(\eta_{1}))

\displaystyle=(1-\eta_{2})(\ell(-t)-\ell(-t^{*}(\eta_{1})))

\displaystyle=\big((1-\eta_{2})/(1-\eta_{1})\big)\big((1-\eta_{1})(\ell(-t)-\ell(-t^{*}(\eta_{1})))\big)

\displaystyle=\big((1-\eta_{2})/(1-\eta_{1})\big)\big(Q(\eta_{1},t)-Q(\eta_{1},t^{*}(\eta_{1}))\big)

\displaystyle<0,

where the first and thrid equalities use the assumption
\ell(t)=\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
, the last inequality follows from the definition of
t^{*}(\eta_{1})
. Therefore, we conclude that
t^{*}(\eta_{2})<t^{*}(\eta_{1})
.

Case 2:
There exists a
t<t^{*}(\eta_{1})
such that
\ell(t)<\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
. By convexity of
\ell
, we know
\ell(t_{1})<\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))<\ell(t_{2})
for any
t_{1},t_{2}
such that
t_{1}<t^{*}(\eta_{1})<t_{2}
. Denote by
l_{+}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and
l_{-}^{\prime}(\cdot)
the right and left derivative functions of
\ell(\cdot)
respectively. (side derivatives must exist for monotonic functions.) The definition of
t^{*}(\eta_{1})
indicates that
Q(\eta_{1},t_{1})>Q(\eta_{1},t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
and
Q(\eta_{1},t_{2})\geq Q(\eta_{1},t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
, where this can be rewritten in the following inequalities

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(-t_{1})-\ell(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))-\ell(t_{1})}>\frac{\eta_{1}}{1-\eta_{1}}\geq\frac{\ell(-t_{2})-\ell(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))-\ell(t_{2})}.

Divide both the numerators and denominators of the left hand side and the right hand side of the above inequality by
t^{*}(\eta_{1})-t_{1}
and
t^{*}(\eta_{1})-t_{2}
respectively. By taking limit as
t_{1}\to t^{*}(\eta_{1})_{-}
and
t_{2}\to t^{*}(\eta_{1})_{+}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{{\ell_{+}^{\prime}}(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{{\ell_{-}^{\prime}}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}\geq\frac{\eta_{1}}{1-\eta_{1}}\geq\frac{{\ell_{-}^{\prime}}(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{{\ell_{+}^{\prime}}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}.

According to the convexity of
\ell
and the fact
\ell(t_{1})<\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
, we know that
0<l_{-}^{\prime}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))\leq l_{+}^{\prime}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
. Then the above inequality is well defined. Since
\eta_{2}>\eta_{1}
, we know
\frac{\eta_{2}}{1-\eta_{2}}>\frac{\eta_{1}}{1-\eta_{1}}
. If
\frac{\eta_{2}}{1-\eta_{2}}>\frac{{\ell_{+}^{\prime}}(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{{\ell_{-}^{\prime}}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}
, then by the definition of side derivative, there exists a
t<t^{*}(\eta_{1})
such that
\frac{\eta_{2}}{1-\eta_{2}}>\frac{\ell(-t)-\ell(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{\ell(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))-\ell(t)}
, which is equivalent to
Q(\eta_{2},t)<Q(\eta_{2},t^{*}(\eta_{1}))
, hence we conclude
t^{*}(\eta_{2})<t^{*}(\eta_{1})
. If
\frac{\eta_{2}}{1-\eta_{2}}\in(\frac{\eta_{1}}{1-\eta_{1}},\frac{{\ell_{+}^{\prime}}(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{{\ell_{-}^{\prime}}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}]\subset[\frac{{\ell_{+}^{\prime}}(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{{\ell_{-}^{\prime}}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))},\frac{{\ell_{-}^{\prime}}(-t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}{{\ell_{+}^{\prime}}(t^{*}(\eta_{1}))}]
, this is equivalent to that
t^{*}(\eta_{1})
is also a minimizer of
Q(\eta_{2},\cdot)
, hence we conclude
t^{*}(\eta_{2})\leq t^{*}(\eta_{1})
.
The proof is then completed.
∎

Lemma
4
also has some implications for binary classification. For a binary classification problem, we aim to learn a classifier
f:\mathcal{X}\to\{-1,1\}
from a given sample
S
. The performance of a classifier
f
is usually measured by its generalization error
\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\ell(-Yf(X))]
with a loss
\ell
that is convex, non-decreasing, and nonnegative (examples of losses can be found in the remark below Definition
4
). Similar to the arguments of (
2.2
), one can derive that, for almost every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the true predictor (target function)
f_{\rho}
with loss
\ell
can be written as
f_{\rho}(x)=\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}p(x)\ell(-t)+(1-p(x))\ell(t)
, where
p(x)=Prob\{Y=1|X=x\}
is the conditional probability. Therefore, by setting
Q(p,t)=p\ell(t)+(1-p)\ell(-t)
, we get
f_{\rho}(x)=-\arg\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}Q(p(x),t)=-t^{*}(p(x))
for almost every
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Note Lemma
4
shows that
t^{*}(\cdot)
is non-increasing on
[0,1]
. Then we know that the value of the true predictor
f_{\rho}(x)=-t^{*}(p(x))
increases as the conditional probability
p(x)
increases, which implies that the tendency of the sample
x
being classified to class
\{1\}
by the true predictor is also increasing.

The following property can be directly obtained by using Lemma
4
.

proposition 4
.

Suppose Assumption
3
holds. If
\eta(x,x^{\prime})\leq\min\{\eta(x,x),\eta(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\}
, then

\displaystyle\max\{d_{\rho}(x,x),d_{\rho}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\}\leq d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime}).

Proof.

Let
\eta_{1}=\eta(x,x^{\prime}),\eta_{2}=\eta(x,x),\eta_{3}=\eta(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})
, then
d_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})=t^{*}(\eta_{1}),d_{\rho}(x,x)=t^{*}(\eta_{2})
and
d_{\rho}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})=t^{*}(\eta_{3})
. By Lemma
4
, we get the desired result immediately.
∎

Remark
.

Define
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}|x\in\mathcal{X}\}
as the conditional distribution family. One can easily observe that
\mathcal{P}
is a subset of the probability simplex

\displaystyle\Delta_{m}=\left\{(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\left.\right|\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1,p_{i}\geq 0\right\}.

The condition in Theorem
4
can be written as
\langle P_{x},P_{x^{\prime}}\rangle\leq\min\{\|P_{x}\|^{2}_{2},\|P_{x^{\prime}}\|^{2}_{2}\}
for almost
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. In the aspects of geometry, it requires that the inner product of any two probability vectors in
\mathcal{P}
is less than or equal to both the square of their Euclidean norms. One example satisfying this condition is that
\mathcal{P}
is a subset of the intersection of the probability simplex
\Delta_{m}
and the ball centered at the origin with radius
r\in[0,1]
. In this case, the equality of the condition holds only when
P_{x}=P_{x^{\prime}}
.

Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}
has an empty interior in the sub-topology of probability simplex
\Delta_{m}
. Otherwise, we can find a small enough vector
\epsilon
such that
P_{x}+\epsilon\in\mathcal{P}
and
\langle P_{x},P_{x}+\epsilon\rangle>\min\{\|P_{x}\|^{2},\|P_{x}+\epsilon\|^{2}\}
, where
P_{x}
is a relative interior point of
\mathcal{P}
.

To empirically validate our theoretical findings, we compare the canonical distance-based criterion

\|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})\|_{2}-b,

which is commonly used in deep metric learning (DML), with our proposed structured model

defined in (
2.4
). In both cases, the underlying embedding network is a three-layer MLP producing an embedding
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

For our model, we use a practical instantiation of the structured metric in (

i

4.2
Synthetic data

To empirically illustrate the mechanism behind our theoretical construction, we evaluate our structured model
d(x,x^{\prime})=F_{a}\!\left(1-2\langle g(x),g(x^{\prime})\rangle\right)
against the canonical distance-based criterion
\|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})\|_{2}-b
in a controlled setting.
The goal of this experiment is not to maximize raw predictive performance, but to examine whether the two model classes behave differently when the true metric is governed by an inner-product structure rather than by a standard distance threshold.

Synthetic construction.

This construction is inspired by the discussion after Proposition 3.3, in particular by the possibility that the true metric may violate standard distance-based intuition.
We generate one-dimensional samples from two regions, denoted by
A
and
B
.
We assign a probability mass of
\lambda\in(0,1)
to the uncertain region
B
, with the remaining
1-\lambda
mass assigned to
A
.
Each region is associated with a specific probability vector:

P^{+}=e_{m},\qquad P^{-}=\Bigl(p,\frac{1-p}{m-1},\ldots,\frac{1-p}{m-1}\Bigr),

where
e_{m}
is the
m
-th standard basis vector and we fix
p=0.6
.
For a pair
(x,x^{\prime})
, the target similarity defined in (
2.1
) is

\eta(x,x^{\prime})=Prob\{Y=Y^{\prime}|X=x,X^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\}=\langle P(x),P(x^{\prime})\rangle.

We assign the binary pairwise label
\tau(y,y^{\prime})=1
if
\eta(x,x^{\prime})\geq 1/2
and
-1
otherwise.
This construction ensures that the binary labels are induced by the sign of
1-2\eta(x,x^{\prime})
, ensuring that the underlying tasks are consistent with the structure of our model.
In contrast, a distance-based metric can distinguish separate cross-region pairs, but may fail on the crucial
B
–
B
pairs when their internal similarity falls below the threshold
1/2
.
More explicitly, for any
x_{A}\in A,x_{B}\in B
, the three canonical pair types satisfy

\eta(x_{A},x_{A})=\langle P^{+},P^{+}\rangle=1,\quad\eta(x_{A},x_{B})=\langle P^{+},P^{-}\rangle=\frac{1-p}{m-1}

and

\eta(x_{B},x_{B})=\langle P^{-},P^{-}\rangle=p^{2}+\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{m-1}.

Hence, for
p=0.6
, we always have
\eta_{AA}>1/2
and
\eta_{AB}<1/2
, so
A
–
A
are similar pairs while
A
–
B
are different pairs.
However, the status of
B
–
B
pairs depends on
m
:
when
m=2
,
\eta_{BB}=0.52>1/2
, so
B
–
B
are similar pairs;
when
m\geq 3
,
\eta_{BB}<1/2
, so
B
–
B
are different pairs.
This transition explains why the synthetic task is benign for the distance-based baseline when
m=2
, but becomes increasingly mismatched with a purely distance-based metric once
m\geq 3
.

In addition, since the uncertain region
B
has probability mass
\lambda
, the proportion of
B
–
B
pairs is exactly
\lambda^{2}
.
Therefore, once
m\geq 3
, increasing
\lambda
primarily increases the frequency of the structurally difficult pairs.
This explains why the degradation of the distance-based DML baseline is closely tied to
\lambda^{2}
in our experiments.

Experimental setup.

Both models utilize a three-layer MLP with hidden width
16
.

\lambda
-sweep, where we fix
m=10
and vary
\lambda\!\in\!\{0.2,0.3,0.4,0.5\}
to observe the impact of increased uncertain region, and (ii) an
m
-sweep, where we fix
\lambda=0.5
and vary
m\in\{2,3,5,10\}
to test the effect of the number of labels.
Models are trained using the same hinge loss and Adam optimizer for
80
epochs.
We report the bAcc averaged over five independent runs.

\lambda
.

The upper half of Table
2
reports the balanced accuracy for different values of
\lambda
.
Our model achieves near-optimal performance across the whole range, with bAcc around
99.9\%
.
In contrast, the DML baseline deteriorates monotonically as
\lambda
increases, dropping from
96.01
at
\lambda=0.2
to
75.14
at

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a comprehensive generalization analysis for metric and similarity learning with the hinge loss. By deriving an explicit representation of the true metric
d_{\rho}
under the hinge loss, we construct a novel hypothesis space consisting of structured deep ReLU networks, and establish excess risk bounds by carefully controlling both the approximation error and the estimation error within this structured hypothesis space. In particular, we derive an explicit learning rate, up to a logarithmic factor,
O\big(n^{-\frac{(\theta+1)r}{d+(\theta+2)r}}\big)
.
We also revisit several structural properties of the problem setting and the true metric under a general loss function. Experiments show that the proposed structured model is empirically competitive on real-world data and better captures the underlying similarity mechanism in the synthetic settings studied in this paper.
