Optimal quantitative weak approximation for projective quadrics
Zhizhong Huang and Damaris Schindler and Alec Shute

Abstract
. We derive asymptotic formulas for the number of rational points on a smooth projective quadratic hypersurface of dimension at least three inside of a shrinking adelic open neighbourhood. This is a quantitative version of weak approximation for quadrics and allows us to deduce the best growth rate of the size of such an adelic neighbourhood for which equidistribution is preserved.



1.
Introduction

The main goal of this article is to study the quantitative distribution of rational points on quadrics over the rational numbers. More precisely, for
n\geqslant 2
we consider a quadratic form
F(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
in
n+1
variables over the integers and ask for arithmetic properties of the variety
V\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
given by
F=0
. From now on we assume that the quadratic form
F
has full rank, i.e. the variety
V
is non-singular. It is well known by the celebrated Hasse-Minkowski theorem that
V
satisfies the Hasse principle and weak approximation.

Weak approximation for
V\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
implies in particular that the set of rational points is infinite, if there are adelic points. In order to study the density of rational points on
V
, we may introduce a height function and count rational points on
V(\mathbb{Q})
of height bounded by
B
where
B
tends to infinity. The behavior of this counting function is well studied and the asymptotic behavior is compatible with Manin’s conjecture for rational points on Fano varieties. The goal of this article is to study a quantitative form of weak approximation for quadrics. More precisely we are interested in counting rational points on
V
in a shrinking open neighborhood of the adelic points
V(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
, and investigate how quickly the neighborhood can shrink when considering points of height bounded by
B
with
B
tending to infinity. With this our result fits into the general framework of Manin’s conjecture on counting rational points of bounded height on Fano varieties, equidistribution questions for rational points on varieties and in particular questions on quantitative weak approximation.

More generally, let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. The density of the set of rational points
X({\mathbb{Q}})
is one of the central themes of Diophantine geometry. To exclude the trivial case we shall assume
X
is everywhere locally soluble, i.e., the
adelic space

X(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}):=X({\mathbb{R}})\times\prod_{p<\infty}X({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
is non-empty. We say that
X
satisfies
weak approximation
if
X({\mathbb{Q}})
is dense in
X(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
. See
[
8
]
for a general conjecture of Colliot-Thélène concerning rationally connected varieties.
Weak approximation for
X
implies in particular that
X({\mathbb{Q}})
is dense in the Zariski topology.
An effective way of measuring this property is to attach
H:X({\mathbb{Q}})\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
a height function and to study asymptotic growth of points of bounded height. In the setting of Fano varieties, Manin’s conjecture
[
1
,
12
]
provides a broad framework for the asymptotic behavior of points of bounded height outside of an exceptional subset. Refining Manin’s conjecture, a general equidistribution principle proposed by Peyre
[
24
]
predicts that, if
X
is Fano and
X
satisfies weak approximation, upon removing some exceptional set
{\mathscr{M}}\subseteq X({\mathbb{Q}})
, the set
\{P\in X({\mathbb{Q}})\setminus{\mathscr{M}}:H(P)\leqslant B\}
, as
B\to\infty
, becomes equidistributed in
X(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
. The question which exceptional set of rational points one has to remove in Manin’s conjecture has received much attention during the last years
[
25
,
18
]
.

The Hardy-Littlewood circle method has proved to be a particularly flexible and useful tool to study both Manin’s conjecture as well as Peyre’s predicition on equidistribution for rational points. This has for example been executed for smooth hypersurfaces and more generally smooth complete intersections in projective space of sufficiently large dimension compared to the degree in work of Birch
[
2
]
and Browning and Heath-Brown
[
4
]
, as well as for certain hypersurfaces in toric varieties
[
30
,
20
,
21
,
26
]
. It is an ongoing challenge to establish results of this type assuming only a smaller number of variables, for some improvements in this direction see for example work of Browning and Prendiville
[
6
]
and work of Myerson
[
22
,
23
]
.

One can ask similar questions to the above for affine varieties and integral points on affine varieties. For work concerning integral points on affine varieties, see for example
[
3
]
for symmetric varieties and
[
11
]
for Linnik-type problems. In another direction Sardari
[
28
]
studied quantitative strong approximation questions for affine quadrics. Sardari’s result
[
28
, Theorem 1.2]
concerns Linnik-type problems about the family of affine quadrics
(F=N)\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}
indexed by a growing integer parameter
N
, where the condition at the real place is imposed via projecting onto the unit affine quadric defined by
F=1
. Our goal in this article is to study quantitative approximation questions for projective quadrics, where the condition at the real place is placed in the spirit of weak approximation at the real place of the single fixed projective quadric
(F=0)\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
. Similarly, Sardari and us handle these questions for quantitative strong and weak approximation conditions at non-archimedean places, but for simplicity of exposition we restrict for a moment to the real place.

Our general strategy for our main result is to first pass to the affine cone and apply the circle method to count integral points of height bounded by say
B
on the affine cone, which are at the same time restricted to a neighborhood of radius
R^{-1}
around a given real point. We consider
B
and
R
as two real parameters which both tend to infinity and for which
R<B^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}
. In passing back from the affine cone to the projective setting, we use the standard procedure of Moebius inversion. However, this poses major new challenges in our situation. In order to apply Moebius inversion, we also need to understand the number of integral points on the affine cone of height bounded by
B^{\prime}
where
B^{\prime}
is any real number less than
B
and where the points are still restricted to neighborhoods of size
R^{-1}
for the same parameter
R
which we started with, i.e. we cannot assume that
R<B^{\prime\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}
for these choices of heights
B^{\prime}
. To solve this problem, we consider three ranges of parameters of heights
B^{\prime}
. In the first range where
B^{\prime}
is relatively large, we apply the circle method to find actual asymptotics for our counting function. In the range where
B^{\prime}
is still somewhat large but significantly smaller than
B
we obtain upper bounds for the corresponding counting function again using the delta method. In the last range for
B^{\prime}
relatively small, we complement our earlier results with lattice point counting techniques for upper bounds.

Our results on quantitative weak approximation have applications in the setting of Diophantine approximation on algebraic varieties. In particular our results imply upper bounds for the
approximation constants
as introduced by work of McKinnon–M. Roth
[
19
]
for projective quadrics. We discuss these applications and connections in the appendix of this article.

1.1.
Main counting result for the projective quadric

We now come to set up the terminology to state our results precisely. For this let
n\geqslant 3
and
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}]
be a non-degenerate quadratic form in
n+1
variables which defines a smooth projective hypersurface
V=(F=0)\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, with the integral model
{\mathscr{V}}\subseteq{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
also defined by
F
.

Let us first prescribe several local conditions in more detail which allow the size of adelic sets to shrink.
We start by the one for the real place. Let us fix a point
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in V({\mathbb{R}})
, and fix
g_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}:V({\mathbb{R}})\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
a local diffeomorphism which sends
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to
\boldsymbol{0}
. We also fix an
(n-1)
-dimensional bounded real neighbourhood of
\boldsymbol{0}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
, denoted by
U_{0}
. For a real parameter
R\geqslant 1
, the family

(1.1)

\left({\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=g_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{-1}(R^{-1}U_{0})\right)_{R\geqslant 1}

consisting of real neighbourhoods “of diameter
R^{-1}
” forms a topological basis for the real point
\boldsymbol{\xi}
inside of
V({\mathbb{R}})
. Consequently, as
R\to\infty
the
real zoom condition

{\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\cap V({\mathbb{Q}})
captures points of
V({\mathbb{Q}})
approximating
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in the real locus.

We next describe zoom conditions for the non-archimedean places.
Let
L>0
be an integer. For every prime
p\mid L
, we let
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p}\in{\mathscr{V}}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:=(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p})_{p\mid L}
. Write
m_{p}:=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(L)
and define

{\mathscr{D}}_{p}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p}):=\{x_{p}\in{\mathscr{V}}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}):x_{p}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p}\bmod p^{m_{p}}\}.

If
m_{p}\to\infty
then points in
V({\mathbb{Q}})\cap{\mathscr{D}}_{p}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p})

p
-adically approximate
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p}
, and so are said to satisfy a
p
-adic zoom condition
.
The set

{\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}):=\prod_{p\mid L}{\mathscr{D}}_{p}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p})\times\prod_{p\nmid L}V({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})\subseteq\prod_{p<\infty}V({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})

is a non-empty finite adelic open neighbourhood of
V
.

The set
{\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\times{\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\subseteq V(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
is a non-empty open adelic set. We fix a height function
H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}:{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{Q}})\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
relative to the line bundle
{\mathscr{O}}(1)
which depends on the real point
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
We want to estimate the number of rational points of
V
of bounded height that “lie in
U_{0}
after scaling by
R
” and simultaneously “specialise to
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
modulo
L
”. So we consider the counting function

(1.2)

{\mathscr{N}}_{V}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda});B):=\#\{P\in V({\mathbb{Q}})\cap({\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\times{\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})):H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(P)\leqslant B\}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
n\geqslant 4
and
V(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})\neq\varnothing
. Let
H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}},g_{\boldsymbol{\xi}},U_{0}
be defined by (
2.2
) (
2.3
) (
2.4
) respectively in Section
2.1
. Assume there exists
0<\tau<1
such that as
B\to\infty
,

(1.3)

(LR)^{2}=O(B^{1-\tau}),

and assume that
R\geqslant R_{0}
, where
R_{0}
is defined in (
7.1
). Then

{\mathscr{N}}_{V}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda});B)=\frac{B^{n-1}}{n-1}\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left({\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\times{\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\right)\left(1+O_{\varepsilon,\tau}(B^{\varepsilon}E_{\tau}(B))\right).

Here
\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is the Tamagawa measure (à la Peyre
[
24
, Définition 2.2]
) on
V(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
corresponding the height function
H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, and

(1.4)

E_{\tau}(B)=\begin{cases}B^{\frac{-(n-3)\tau}{5n-7}},&\textrm{ if }n\geqslant 5;\\
B^{-2\tau/17},&\textrm{ if }n=4.\end{cases}

The implied constant depends at most on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
(apart from
\varepsilon,\tau
) and is otherwise uniform with respect to
R,L
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

Theorem
1.1
implies a quantitative version of the classical Hasse–Minkowski theorem on weak approximation for projective quadrics, allowing the size of the adelic open set (i.e. the parameters
R,L
) to “shrink” in terms of
B
. Indeed (cf. Proposition
6.5
),

L^{-\dim V-\varepsilon}R^{-\dim V}\ll_{\varepsilon}\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left({\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\times{\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\right)\ll(LR)^{-\dim V},

and Theorem
1.1
furnishes an error term with a power-saving, subject to the condition (
1.3
) on the growth of
R,L
.

The main term in Theorem
1.1
can be equally be expressed as a product of local densities, whose form is well-known and can be achieved in different ways (see e.g.
[
24
, §5]
). However, the key characteristic of Theorem
1.1
is that the condition (
1.3
) is
optimal
, in the sense that such an equidistribution can fail if the growth rate of
R
(resp.
L
) exceeds a power of
B
strictly greater than
\frac{1}{2}
, without further assumption on the Diophantine properties of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
(resp.
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
). We refer to the Appendix for a short discussion in view of Diophantine approximation, notably Remarks
A.5
(1).

1.2.
Main counting result for the affine cone

Let
W\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}
be the affine cone of
V
with the integral model
{\mathscr{W}}\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
defined by
F
.
Let
W^{o}\subseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}
be the punctured affine cone over
V
. We take the integral model
{\mathscr{W}}^{o}:={\mathscr{W}}\setminus\overline{\boldsymbol{0}}
(where
\overline{\boldsymbol{0}}
stands for the Zariski closure of
\boldsymbol{0}
in
{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
). Let
\pi:{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be the canonical projection map.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}
be such that
F(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\equiv 0\bmod L
. We can view
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
as an element of
{\mathscr{W}}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
be the norm in accordance with
H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.
The counting function (
1.2
) is closely related to

(1.5)

{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)\\
:=\#\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}:F(\boldsymbol{x})=0,\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bmod{L},\pi(\boldsymbol{x})\in{\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\leqslant B\right\}.

We now state our main counting result about (
1.5
). It features a
singular series

(1.6)

\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}):=\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})

and a
singular integral

{\mathscr{I}}_{R}
, which are given by the equations (recall that
m_{p}:=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(L)
)

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})

\displaystyle:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#\{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathscr{W}}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}):\boldsymbol{v}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bmod p^{m_{p}}\}}{p^{nk}},

(1.8)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}_{R}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\leqslant 1\\
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})\in{\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\end{subarray}}\textup{e}(\theta F(\boldsymbol{x}))\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{x}\operatorname{d}\theta.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
be defined by (
2.1
). Assume that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in{\mathscr{W}}^{o}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
and that the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.1
hold. Then we have

{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)=B^{n-1}{\mathscr{I}}_{R}\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\left(1+O_{\varepsilon,\tau}(B^{\varepsilon}E_{\tau}(B))\right),

where
E_{\tau}(B)
is given in (
1.4
). Here again the implied constant may depend on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
(apart from
\varepsilon,\tau
), but is otherwise uniform with respect to
R,L
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
.

The case of quaternary quadratic forms (i.e.
n=3
) is more subtle and is addressed in a separate article
[
15
]
.

1.3.
Comparisons with results in the literature and further comments

Similar forms of Theorems
1.1
&
1.2
have previously been obtained by the work of Browning–Loughran in
[
5
, §4]
and by Kelmer–Yu in
[
17
, §1.3]
.

In
[
5
]
the slightly different problem of counting smoothly weighted solutions of
F
whose reduction modulo
L
lies in a given subset of local solutions is concerned (without prescribing any real zoom condition). In this setting, the expected order of growth is
B^{n-1}
and
L^{-n}
is roughly the “volume” of the affine adelic open set defined by the congruence condition
\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bmod{L}
. Therefore, it can be checked that
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
produces an asymptotic formula provided that

L^{\frac{n+1}{2}}B^{\frac{n+1}{2}+\varepsilon}=o(B^{n-1}L^{-n}),

or in other words,
L=O(B^{\delta})
for some
\delta<\frac{n-3}{3n+1}=\frac{1}{3}-\frac{10}{3(3n+1)}
. (It is also suggested therein that when
L
is squarefree, their error term could be improved, with the result that
L
could grow like
B^{\delta}
for
\delta<\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3}{2n}
. This becomes optimal in the limit as
n\to\infty
.) Theorem
1.2
thus improves upon
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
in the
L
aspect, since it allows
L
to grow arbitrarily close to
B^{\frac{1}{2}}
for any
n\geqslant 4
. As explained before,
B^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the “barrier” for which such an equidistribution is preserved. Also here we do not require the assumption made in
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
that
L
is coprime to
2\Delta_{F}
.

In
[
17
]
however, via a quite different approach and as an application of its main result,
[
17
, Theorem 1.7]
deals with the same counting problem for the quadric
V
as ours, but restricted to quadratic forms of signature
(n,1)
, for which only real zoom condition is imposed (i.e.
L=1
). The optimal growth range for
R
is achieved only for a sub-family of quadrics (see
[
17
, Remark 1.3]
). So Theorem
1.1
generalises and improves upon
[
17
, Theorem 1.7]
not only covering quadrics with any signture but also in the
R
-aspect.

We would like to comment on the optimality of Theorem
1.1
.
In the light of the Schmidt subspace theorem and its ultrametric generalisations, it is probable that, upon specifying finer Diophantine approximation properties of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
or
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, the asymptotic in Theorem
1.1
would still remain true under weaker constraints on the growth of
R
or
L
. See also Remarks
A.5
(2).
However, achieving this seems to go beyond the technical limit developed in this paper (and also the circle method of Birch’s type
[
2
]
) – we are unable to keep track of any intrinsic approximation properties of either
\boldsymbol{\xi}
or
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

Our approach is based upon a
\delta
-variant of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method which goes back to Duke–Friendlander–Iwaniec
[
9
]
and is later extensively developed by Heath-Brown
[
13
]
. adapting particularly well to quadratic forms. The execution of the
\delta
-method leads to a certain type of twisted quadratic exponential sums, similar to the globally defined
S_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})
in
[
13
]
.
However, these exponential sums depend on the newly introduced non-archimedean local condition
(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
. To obtain satisfactory control for them we work out certain refinements of
[
13
, §9–§11]
, and we need to take special care of uniformity, especially over the “bad moduli”.

An important feature in Sardari’s work
[
28
]
is a careful construction of smooth weight functions to capture the Linnik-type real condition in the affine setting.
In the case of projective varieties, the quantitative weak approximation conditions lead to differently shaped regions on the affine cone and with this we need to build a different weight function encapsulating as many “level-lowering” effects as possible that these conditions result in, which takes an equally crucial role for us as it did in
[
28
]
. Furthermore, the application of the
\delta
-method requires smoothed counting functions and in order to deduce an asymptotic for our original counting function, we need to apply a removing of smooth weights procedure which introduces some additional technicalities which we also discuss in detail in this article.

1.4.
Organisation of the article

In §
2
, we explicitly construct smooth weight functions, with respect to which we state a “weighted” counting result (Theorem
2.3
). The remaining part of §
2
discusses how the
\delta
-method is executed. In §
3
and §
4
we prove estimates for the oscillatory integrals and the singular series respectively. Sections §
5
and §
6
are devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.3
. In §
7.2
we prove Theorem
1.2
using Theorem
2.3
with the help of a removing of smooth weights procedure, and in §
7.3
we deduce Theorem
1.1
from Theorem
1.2
. Section
7
also contains upper bounds for the counting function
{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)
in the range
-1<\tau<1
using the delta method, as well as upper bounds for all ranges of
B,L,R
using lattice point counting techniques.
In the Appendix we discuss several aspects from Diophantine approximation. On combining with our main counting result, we study various approximation constants for projective quadrics.

1.5.
Notation and conventions

Unless otherwise specified, all implied constants are allowed to depend on the quadratic form
F
(and hence the quadric
V
), the real point
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in V({\mathbb{R}})
and the real neighbourhood
U_{0}
, which are considered fixed throughout.

We write
\tau(n)
for the divisor function.
We write
\Delta_{F}
for the discriminant of the quadratic form
F
. For non-zero integers
k,A
, we use the notation
k\mid A^{\infty}
to mean that every prime factor of
k
is also a prime factor of
A
.
The symbol
T\nearrow X
within a sum means a dyadic decomposition, i.e.
T
runs over all powers of
2^{j}
with
2^{j}\leqslant X
.

2.
Set-up for the
\delta
-method

2.1.
Considerations for the real place

Without loss of generality, we may assume the point
\boldsymbol{\xi}=[\xi_{0}:\cdots:\xi_{n}]
satisfies
\xi_{0}\neq 0
.
We begin by applying a (classical) change of variables in order to get a “nice” coordinate system.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists a matrix
M\in\operatorname{GL}_{n+1}({\mathbb{R}})
, sending the variables
\boldsymbol{x}
to
\boldsymbol{t}:=M\boldsymbol{x}
, such that

•

M\boldsymbol{\xi}=[1:0\cdots:0]
;

•

F(\boldsymbol{x})=F(M^{-1}\boldsymbol{t})=t_{0}t_{1}+F_{2}(t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})
, where
F_{2}
is a non-degenerate quadratic form in
(n-1)
variables.

Proof.

Via a slight abuse of notation we write
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{n})
and recall that we have
F(\boldsymbol{\xi})=0
. Let
B(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
be the bilinear form associated to the quadratic form
F
. Choose a vector
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
which is linearly independent of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and satisfies
B(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi})\neq 0
. For
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

F(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi})=F(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})+2\lambda B(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Hence we can find
\lambda
such that
F(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi})=0
and
B(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi})\neq 0
. We replace
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
by
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Moreover, after multiplying
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
by a scalar we may assume that
2B(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi})=1
. Next consider the orthogonal complement of
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
under the bilinear form
B
and choose a basis
\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
. In particular we have

B(\boldsymbol{v}_{j},\boldsymbol{v}_{1})=B(\boldsymbol{v}_{j},\boldsymbol{\xi})=0,\quad 2\leqslant j\leqslant n,

and the vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
are a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Now we take
M
to be the matrix such that the columns of
M^{-1}
are given by
\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
.
∎

With respect to this new coordinate system
\boldsymbol{t}
, we now define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and the height function
H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
as follows:

(2.1)

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}:=\max_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}|t_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})|,\quad\text{for }\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},

(2.2)

H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}([x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]):=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\quad\text{if }\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\text{ and }\gcd_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}(x_{i})=1.

Hence
H_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is the naive height function after a linear change of coordinate systems.

Applying the change of variables from Lemma
2.1
, we see that in the chart
t_{0}\neq 0
, the affine tangent plane at the point
\boldsymbol{0}
is
T_{\boldsymbol{0}}V({\mathbb{R}})=(\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}=0)
. Moreover,
\frac{t_{2}}{t_{0}},\ldots,\frac{t_{n}}{t_{0}}
form a local coordinate system of the hypersurface
V
around
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. We now take the local diffeomorphism as

(2.3)

g_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\pi(\boldsymbol{x})):=\left(\frac{t_{2}}{t_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,\frac{t_{n}}{t_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right).

By covering any open neighborhood of
\mathbf{0}
by cubes, it is sufficient to consider the case

(2.4)

U_{0}=\prod_{j=2}^{n}[a_{j},b_{j}],

a standard
(n-1)
-dimensional cube, where
-\infty<a_{j}<b_{j}<\infty
for all
2\leqslant j\leqslant n
. The real zoom condition (
1.1
) becomes

(2.5)

R\frac{t_{j}}{t_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x})\in[a_{j},b_{j}]\textrm{ for all }2\leqslant j\leqslant n.

2.2.
Choice of weight functions

In order to apply the
\delta
-method of Heath-Brown, we need to work with a smoothly weighted analogue of
{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)
. For an infinitely differentiable function
w:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
, we define

(2.6)

{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w;(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\\
F(\boldsymbol{x})=0,\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bmod L\end{subarray}}w(\boldsymbol{x}).

We would like
w
to capture the real zoom condition (
2.5
) and the bounded height condition. Therefore, we let

(2.7)

w_{B,R}(\boldsymbol{x})=w_{1}\left(R\frac{t_{j}}{t_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}),j\geqslant 2\right)w_{2}\left(\frac{R^{2}}{B^{2}}F(\boldsymbol{x})\right)w_{3}\left(\frac{t_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})}{B}\right),

where
w_{1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\to[0,1],w_{2},w_{3}:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]
are infinitely differentiable weight functions and
w_{2}(0)=1
.

Several comments on the role of
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
are in order. Clearly
w_{1}
“mollifies” the neighbourhood
U_{0}
. Due to (
2.5
) and the assumption
R\geqslant 1
, the condition
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\ll B
is equivalent to
|t_{0}|\ll B
. Therefore,
w_{3}
imposes a “mollified bounded height condition”.
The inclusion of the weight function
w_{2}
is crucial for the “level-lowering”. Indeed, let
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}
be such that
t_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\neq 0
. If (
2.5
) is satisfied, as prescribed by
w_{1}
, then (as in Lemma
2.1
)
F_{2}(t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})\ll R^{-2}|t_{0}|^{2}
, and so the equation

F(\boldsymbol{x})=t_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}+F_{2}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{t_{0}},\ldots,\frac{t_{n}}{t_{0}}\right)\right)=0

implies that

(2.8)

|t_{1}/t_{0}|\ll R^{-2}.

If moreover
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\ll B
, as prescribed by
w_{3}
, then together with (
2.5
) and (
2.8
) we obtain

F(\boldsymbol{x})\ll\frac{B^{2}}{R^{2}}.

Since this bound relies on the condition
F(\boldsymbol{x})=0
(which will be captured later using the
\delta
-symbol), the effect of
w_{2}
cannot be encapsulated by
w_{1}
and
w_{3}
alone. We could have instead inserted a weight function of the form
w_{4}\left(R^{2}\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}\right)
, but this turns out to have an equivalent role to
w_{2}
, and working with
w_{2}
is technically simpler.

Moreover, to deduce information about the “unweighted” counting function
{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)
from the weighted one
{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))
, we would like
w_{1}
and
w_{3}
to approximate the characteristic functions
\prod_{j\geqslant 2}1_{[a_{j},b_{j}]}
and
1_{[-1,1]}
respectively. To make this more precise, we define a small parameter
0<\eta<1
(which will eventually be taken to be a small negative power of
B
), and consider smooth weights
w_{1}^{\pm}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\rightarrow[0,1],w_{3}^{\pm}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow[0,1]
satisfying

(1)

\operatorname{supp}(w_{1}^{+})=\prod_{j\geqslant 2}[a_{j}-\eta,b_{j}+\eta];

(2)

w_{1}^{+}=1
on
\prod_{j\geqslant 2}[a_{j},b_{j}]
;

(3)

\operatorname{supp}(w_{1}^{-})=\prod_{j\geqslant 2}[a_{j},b_{j}];

(4)

w_{1}^{-}=1
on
\prod_{j\geqslant 2}[a_{j}+\eta,b_{j}-\eta]
;

(5)

\operatorname{supp}(w_{3}^{+})=[-1-\eta,1+\eta]\setminus[-\frac{\eta}{2},\frac{\eta}{2}]
;

(6)

w_{3}^{+}=1
on
[-1,1]\setminus[-\eta,\eta]
;

(7)

\operatorname{supp}(w_{3}^{-})=[-1,1]\setminus[-\frac{\eta}{2},\frac{\eta}{2}]
;

(8)

w_{3}^{-}=1
on
[-1+\eta,1-\eta]\setminus[-\eta,\eta]
.

For an infinitely differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and an integer
N\geqslant 0
, we define

(2.9)

\|f\|_{N}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\\
\boldsymbol{k}\in({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geqslant 0})^{m}\\
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}\leqslant N\end{subarray}}|\partial^{\boldsymbol{k}}f(\boldsymbol{x})|

to be the largest partial derivative of
f
of order at most
N
. In particular, we recover the supremum of
|f|
via
\|f\|_{0}
, a fact which is used in the analysis of the oscillatory integrals.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exist smooth weight functions
w_{1}^{\pm},w_{3}^{\pm}
satisfying the above properties, and such that for any
N\geqslant 0
, we have
\|w_{1}^{\pm}\|_{N},\|w_{3}^{\pm}\|_{N}\ll_{N}\eta^{-N}
.

Proof.

Weight functions with the desired properties can all be built from linear transformations and convolutions of the
standard bump function

\psi:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
defined by

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\exp\left(1+\frac{1}{|x|^{2}-1}\right),&\textrm{ if }|x|<1,\\
0,&\textrm{ otherwise}\end{cases}

and its higher dimensional analogue, in which
|\cdot|
is replaced with the Euclidean norm (see
[
32
, Section 4]
).
∎

Let
w_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
be the standard bump function, and let
w_{1}^{\pm},w_{3}^{\pm}
be as in Lemma
2.2
. We define weight functions
w_{B,R}^{\pm}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
by

(2.10)

w_{B,R}^{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x}):=w_{1}^{\pm}\left(R\frac{t_{j}}{t_{0}}(\boldsymbol{x}),j\geqslant 2\right)w_{2}\left(\frac{R^{2}}{B^{2}}F(\boldsymbol{x})\right)w_{3}^{\pm}\left(\frac{t_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})}{B}\right).

It is clear that
w_{B,R}^{\pm}
is supported away from zero, and satisfies
\|w_{B,R}^{\pm}\|_{N}\ll_{N}\eta^{-N}
for any
N\geqslant 0
. We shall also make use of the functions
w_{R}^{\pm}
, defined as in (
2.10
) but without the
w_{2}
and
w_{3}^{\pm}
factors.

2.3.
Weighted counting results

We are ready to state our main counting results in terms of the aforementioned weight functions.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume that
R,L
satisfy (
1.3
) for
-1<\tau<1
. Let
w_{B,R}^{\pm}
be one of the smooth weights defined by (
2.10
) and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
A>0
be a real parameter such that
\eta^{-1}\ll B^{A}
.
Then uniformly in
L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in{\mathscr{W}}^{o}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
, we have

{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R}^{\pm};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))=B^{n-1}{\mathscr{I}}(w_{R}^{\pm})\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})+O_{\tau,A,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{B^{n-1+\varepsilon}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}E_{\tau}(\eta;B)\right),

where

(2.11)

E_{\tau}(\eta;B):=\begin{cases}\eta^{\frac{9-5n}{2}}B^{-\frac{n-3}{2}\tau}&\text{ if }n\geqslant 5;\\
\eta^{-15/2}B^{-\tau}+B^{-\frac{1+\tau}{4}}&\text{ if }n=4.\end{cases}

Here
\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
is the singular series defined in (
1.6
) and
{\mathscr{I}}(w_{R}^{\pm})
is a singular integral defined later in (
6.6
).

Remarks 2.4
.

•

If
0<\tau<1
, then the main term
B^{n-1}{\mathscr{I}}(w_{R}^{\pm})\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
is dominant and (up to the weight function
w
) eventually gives the main contribution in Theorem
1.2
. However, the “error term” becomes dominant when
-1<\tau\leqslant 0
. Even though in this case Theorem
2.3
provides only an upper bound, this is crucial for the removal of smooth weights back to the “constant weight”.

•

In Theorem
2.3
, once
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
are fixed, the implied constant depends only on the small parameter
\eta
. We may replace
w^{\pm}_{B,R}
with any weight function
w
of the form (
2.7
) which is supported away from zero, at the cost of having implied constants that depend implicitly on the
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
-components of
w
.

2.4.
Poisson summation

For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we define the
\delta
-symbol to be

\delta(n)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }n=0;\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

According to Heath-Brown’s work
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, for every
Q>1
, we can write

(2.12)

\delta(n)=\frac{C_{Q}}{Q^{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q}\left(an\right)h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{n}{Q^{2}}\right),

where

C_{Q}=1+O_{N}(Q^{-N}),

and
h:(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a certain infinitely differentiable function, whose properties are described in
[
13
, §4]
.

Given
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in{\mathscr{W}}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
, we shall work with a fixed representative
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{0},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}
of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
whose coordinates are bounded by
L
. Note that the assumption
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in{\mathscr{W}}^{o}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
implies that the gcd of the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is coprime to
L
.
Let us consider the polynomial

(2.13)

H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\frac{F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L}+\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{y}.

From now on, let
w_{B,R}
be a weight function of the form (
2.7
). The goal now is to show

Proposition 2.5
.

We have

(2.14)

\begin{split}{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))=\frac{C_{Q}}{Q^{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}}\frac{S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c})}{(qL)^{n+1}},\end{split}

where
C_{Q}=1+O_{N}(Q^{-N})
and for every
q\ll Q,\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}
,

(2.15)

I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}w_{B,R}(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L^{2}Q^{2}}\right)\textup{e}_{qL}\left(-\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{y},

and

(2.16)

S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/qL{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}\\
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{qL}\left(a\left(H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right).

Similarly to
[
5
]
, the proof of Theorem
2.3
relies on Heath-Brown’s
\delta
-method (
2.12
).
However, to obtain the optimal error term, our way of activating (
2.12
) is different from
[
5
, §4]
, in which we need to understand properly the precise range of
F
when zoom conditions are imposed and then to integrate it into the formula of
[
13
, Theorem 2]
. This is part of our “level-lowering” procedure regarding the congruence condition
(L,\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, and our treatment is closer to Sardari’s
[
28
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

We have

(2.17)

{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\\
L\mid H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{y})\end{subarray}}w_{B,R}(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})\delta\left(\frac{F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L^{2}}\right).

Proof.

We first observe that, if
\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bmod L
, on writing
\boldsymbol{x}=L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, we have

F(\boldsymbol{x})=F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+L\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{y}+L^{2}F(\boldsymbol{y})=LH_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{y})+L^{2}F(\boldsymbol{y}).

We thus have

L^{2}\mid F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})\Leftrightarrow L\mid H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{y}).

So the expression above is well defined, and only captures
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})=0
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

By Lemma
2.6
and (
2.12
), the counting function
{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))
is equal to

(2.18)

\frac{C_{Q}}{Q^{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\\
L\mid H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{y})\end{subarray}}w_{B,R}(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})\textup{e}_{qL}\left(a\left(H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{y})+LF(\boldsymbol{y})\right)\right)h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L^{2}Q^{2}}\right).

In order to apply Poisson summation and separate the real condition from the
p
-adic ones, we break the
\boldsymbol{y}
-sum into residue classes modulo
qL
, so that for every fixed
q,a
and for every fixed class
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bmod qL
with
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\equiv 0\mod L
, the inner
\boldsymbol{y}
-sum is equal to

\displaystyle\textup{e}_{qL}\left(a\left(H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}\\
\boldsymbol{y}\equiv\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bmod qL\end{subarray}}w_{B,R}(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L^{2}Q^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{e}_{qL}\left(a\left(H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)\right)\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}}w_{B,R}(L(\boldsymbol{\sigma}+qL\boldsymbol{z})+\boldsymbol{\lambda})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F(L(\boldsymbol{\sigma}+qL\boldsymbol{z})+\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L^{2}Q^{2}}\right).

Applying the Poisson summation formula to the inner
\boldsymbol{z}
-sum yields

\sum_{\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}w_{B,R}(L(\boldsymbol{\sigma}+qL\boldsymbol{z})+\boldsymbol{\lambda})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F(L(\boldsymbol{\sigma}+qL\boldsymbol{z})+\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L^{2}Q^{2}}\right)\textup{e}\left(-\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{z}.

The inner integral, with the change of variables
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}+qL\boldsymbol{z}
, is equal to

\frac{\textup{e}_{qL}\left(\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)}{(qL)^{n+1}}I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c}),

where
I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c})
is defined by (
2.15
).
Hence the inner
\boldsymbol{y}
-sum in (
2.18
) is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/qL{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}\\
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}}\textup{e}_{qL}\left(a\left(H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)\frac{I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c})}{(qL)^{n+1}}.

Substituting into (
2.18
) yields the terms
S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})
and we get the expression (
2.14
).
∎

2.5.
Choice of
Q

In terms of the zoom condition (
2.5
), we explain our choice of
Q
, keeping in mind its role in (
2.17
), (
2.18
) and (
2.15
).

Recall from
[
13
, Theorem 1]
that
h(x,y)\neq 0
only if
x\leqslant\max(1,2|y|)
. We would like to have

(2.19)

\left|\frac{F(\boldsymbol{x})}{L^{2}}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}Q^{2},

in order to deduce that
q\leqslant Q
.
We recall that
\boldsymbol{t}=M\boldsymbol{x}
, and by our choice of
M
we have

(2.20)

\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{t}):=F(M^{-1}\boldsymbol{t})=t_{0}t_{1}+F_{2}(t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}).

Since
\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{t})=O(B^{2}/R^{2})
as prescribed by
w_{2}
, a reasonable choice for

is therefore

(2.21)

Q=\frac{B}{LR},

with which

3.
The oscillatory integrals

The goal of this section is to manipulate the terms
I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c})
(
2.15
) and to deduce various estimates for them. We assume
n\geqslant 2
throughout this section.

3.1.
Preliminary manipulation

We recall the choice of the matrix
M
from Lemma
2.1
and
\widetilde{F}
from (
2.20
).
The change of variables

\boldsymbol{t}=M\boldsymbol{x}=M(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})

yields

(3.1)

I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c})=\frac{1}{|\det M|L^{n+1}}\textup{e}_{qL^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right){\mathscr{I}}_{q,L}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c}),

where

(3.2)

{\mathscr{I}}_{q,L}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}w_{B,R}(M^{-1}\boldsymbol{t})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{t})}{L^{2}Q^{2}}\right)\textup{e}_{qL^{2}}\left(-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{c}}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t},

\widetilde{F}
is defined by (
2.20
), and, noting that
\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{c}\cdot M^{-1}\boldsymbol{t}=(M^{-1})^{t}\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}
,

(3.3)

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{c}}:=(M^{-1})^{t}\boldsymbol{c}=(\widetilde{c_{0}},\ldots,\widetilde{c_{n}}).

is a real vector.

Let the new variables
\boldsymbol{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
be defined by

s_{0}:=\frac{t_{0}}{B},\quad s_{1}:=\frac{R^{2}}{B}t_{1},\quad s_{j}:=\frac{R}{B}t_{j},\quad j\geqslant 2.

Then we have

\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t}=\frac{B^{n+1}}{R^{n+1}}\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{s}.

Moreover, by our choice of the matrix
M
,

\frac{\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{t})}{L^{2}Q^{2}}=\frac{R^{2}}{B^{2}}\left(t_{0}t_{1}+F_{2}(t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})\right)=\widetilde{x_{0}}\widetilde{x_{1}}+F_{2}(\widetilde{x_{2}},\ldots,\widetilde{x_{n}})=\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{s}).

We also define the real vector
\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{d}(B,R,L;{\mathbf{c}})
by

(3.4)

d_{0}=\frac{B}{L^{2}}\widetilde{c_{0}},\quad d_{1}=\frac{B}{L^{2}R^{2}}\widetilde{c_{1}},\quad d_{j}=\frac{B}{L^{2}R}\widetilde{c_{j}},\quad 2\leqslant j\leqslant n.

Hence we can now rewrite

(3.5)

{\mathscr{I}}_{q,L}(w_{B,R};\boldsymbol{c})=\frac{B^{n+1}}{R^{n+1}}\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{q}(\widehat{w};\boldsymbol{d})

where

(3.6)

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{q}(\widehat{w};\boldsymbol{d})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}\widehat{w}(\boldsymbol{s})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\textup{e}_{q}\left(-\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{s},

with

\widehat{w}(\boldsymbol{s}):=w_{1}\left(\frac{s_{j}}{s_{0}},2\leqslant j\leqslant n\right)w_{2}\left(\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)w_{3}\left(s_{0}\right)

depending on
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
.
We recall that

\boldsymbol{c}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\Leftrightarrow\widetilde{\boldsymbol{c}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\Leftrightarrow\boldsymbol{d}\neq\boldsymbol{0}.

3.2.
Simpler and harder estimates

Let
{\mathscr{H}}
be the class of functions defined on
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
as in
[
13
, (7.1)]
. For
s\ll 1
, we define

f_{s}(y)=sh(s,y),

and we note that
f_{s}(y)\in{\mathscr{H}}
.
Let
{\mathscr{F}}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
be a non-degenerate indefinite real quadratic form. For any real vector
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, and for an appropriate fixed weight function
w\in\mathscr{C}_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n+1})
, let

(3.7)

J_{s}({\mathscr{F}};\mathbf{u})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}w(\boldsymbol{t})f_{s}({\mathscr{F}}(\boldsymbol{t}))e\left(-\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t}.

The following bound follows from
[
13
, Lemma 15]
. For any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we have

(3.8)

J_{s}({\mathscr{F}};\mathbf{u})\ll s.

Here, the implied constant may depend on the diameter of the support of
w
, on

\|w\|_{0}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}|w(\boldsymbol{x})|,

and on a lower bound for
\frac{\partial{\mathscr{F}}}{\partial x_{0}}
on the support of
w
.
These conditions on the implicit constant in (
3.8
) are related to the introduction of the class of functions
\mathscr{C}_{0}(S)
in
[
13
,
\lx@sectionsign
6]
.

The following two theorems are based on Heath-Brown’s “simpler” and “harder” estimates
[
13
, Lemma 18, Lemma 22]
. However, in order to remove the smooth weights and ultimately prove Theorem
1.2
, we need to slightly refine Heath-Brown’s results so that our estimates have an explicit dependence on
w
. This dependence is realised through the quantity
\|w\|_{N}
, as defined in (
2.9
). We do not reproduce a full proof of these results here, instead only highlighting the refinements to Heath-Brown’s arguments. The two results below may also be compared with
[
10
, Proposition 5.1, Proposition 6.1]
.

The first estimate refines
[
13
, Lemma 18]
, which is a consequence of the “first derivative test”
[
13
, Lemma 10]
and a Fourier analysis of the function
h(\cdot,\cdot)

[
13
, Lemma 17]
:

Lemma 3.1
.

(“simpler estimates”)
For
N\geqslant 0
and
\mathbf{u}\neq 0
we have

J_{s}({\mathscr{F}};\mathbf{u})\ll_{N}(s|\mathbf{u}|)^{-N}\|w\|_{N}.

Proof.

Let
w_{0}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be the smooth bump function defined in
[
13
, Equation (2.1)]
.
[
13
, Lemma 18]
is proved by applying integration by parts
N
times to the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}W(\boldsymbol{t})e(k\mathscr{F}(\boldsymbol{t})-\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{t})\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t},

where

W(\boldsymbol{t}):=\frac{w(\boldsymbol{t})}{w_{0}(\mathscr{F}(\boldsymbol{t})/2K)}

and
K
is such that
|\mathscr{F}(\boldsymbol{t})|\leqslant K
on
\operatorname{supp}(w)
. Since
K=O(1)
, we have
\|W\|_{N}\ll\|w\|_{N}
, and hence integrating by parts
N
times introduces a factor
O(\|w\|_{N})
.
∎

The second result is obtained from a more delicate analysis of the locus where the “first derivative test” fails, which involves the Hessian.

Lemma 3.2
.

(“harder estimates”)
Suppose that
1\leqslant A\leqslant|\mathbf{u}|^{1/3}
. Then

J_{s}({\mathscr{F}};\mathbf{u})\ll_{N}A^{-N}\|w\|_{N}+sA^{n+1}|\mathbf{u}|^{1-\frac{n+1}{2}}.

Proof.

Similarly to the lemma above, Heath-Brown’s “good pairs” are dealt with using integration by parts
N
times, to give a contribution of
O(A^{-N}\|w\|_{N})
. For the “bad pairs”, the integral is estimated trivially, and no derivatives of
w
are taken. Therefore, this part of the argument goes through unchanged, giving a contribution of
A^{n+1}|\mathbf{u}|^{1-\frac{n+1}{2}}.

∎

Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
furnish alternative ways to estimate the quantity
\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{q}(\widehat{w};\boldsymbol{d})
in (
3.6
).

Corollary 3.3
.

Uniformly for all
q\in{\mathbb{N}},\boldsymbol{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, we have

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{q}(\widehat{w};\boldsymbol{d})\begin{cases}\ll 1&\quad\text{``Trivial estimate''};\\
\ll_{N}\frac{Q}{q}\left(\frac{|{\mathbf{d}}|}{Q}\right)^{-N}\|w\|_{N}&\quad\text{``Simpler estimate''}.\end{cases}

Moreover, for any
1\leqslant A\leqslant|\boldsymbol{d}/q|^{1/3}
, we have

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{q}(\widehat{w};\boldsymbol{d})\ll_{\varepsilon,N}\frac{Q}{q}A^{-N}\|w\|_{N}+A^{n+1}\left|\frac{\boldsymbol{d}}{q}\right|^{1-\frac{n+1}{2}}\quad\text{``Harder estimate''}.

Proof.

On recalling (
3.6
), we clearly have

(3.9)

\begin{split}\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{q}(\widehat{w};\boldsymbol{d})&=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}\widehat{w}(\boldsymbol{s})h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\widetilde{F}(\boldsymbol{s})\right)\textup{e}\left(-\frac{\boldsymbol{d}}{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{s}\\
&\ll\frac{Q}{q}\left|J_{\frac{q}{Q}}\left(\widetilde{F};\frac{\boldsymbol{d}}{q}\right)\right|.\end{split}

The first estimate is just (
3.8
). Since
F
(and hence
\widetilde{F}
) is non-degenerate, the Hessian hypothesis is satisfied. Corollary
3.3
follows from (
3.9
) and Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
with

s=\frac{q}{Q},\quad\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{\boldsymbol{d}}{q}.\qed

4.
Quadratic exponential sums

Analogously to
[
13
, Lemma 28]
, the aim of this section is to the prove the following “Kloosterman refinement” result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
n\geqslant 2
. We have

S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})\ll_{\varepsilon}L^{n}q^{\frac{n+3}{2}+\varepsilon}\gcd(q,L)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\sum_{q\leqslant X}\left|S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})\right|\ll_{\varepsilon}\begin{cases}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{\varepsilon}L^{n+\varepsilon}X^{\frac{n+4}{2}+\varepsilon}&\text{ if }F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\neq 0;\\
L^{n+\varepsilon}X^{\frac{n+5}{2}+\varepsilon}&\text{ if }F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})=0.\end{cases}

If moreover
n
is even and
F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})=0
, we also have

\sum_{q\leqslant X}\left|S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})\right|\ll_{\varepsilon}L^{n+\varepsilon}X^{\frac{n+4}{2}+\varepsilon}.

All the implied constants above are independent of
q,\boldsymbol{c},L
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

Theorem
4.1
is concerned with
estimating the average over
q
of the absolute value of the finite exponential sums
S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})
from (
2.16
). As already pointed out in
[
13
]
, no “double Kloosterman refinement” is needed when
n\geqslant 4
(while it becomes essential for
n=3
in
[
15
]
).

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we decompose
S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})
into an exponential sum over “good moduli” and another over “bad moduli”, based on the Chinese remainder theorem. For the former we give precise formula, while for the latter we obtain a uniform upper bound.

4.1.
Decomposing
S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})

For every fixed
q\geqslant 1
, we have a decomposition

(4.1)

q=q_{1}q_{2},

where

\gcd(q_{1},2L\Delta_{F})=1,\quad q_{2}\mid(2L\Delta_{F})^{\infty}.

Since
\gcd(q_{1},q_{2})=1
, by the Chinese remainder theorem, every
a\bmod q
can be written as

a=a_{1}q_{2}+a_{2}q_{1},

for a unique choice of
a_{1}\bmod q_{1},a_{2}\bmod q_{2}
. Similarly, for every
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/qL{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}
we can write

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=q_{1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}+q_{2}L\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}

for unique
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\bmod q_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\bmod q_{2}L
. If we let

k:=\frac{F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{L},

then we also write

k=k_{2}q_{1}+k_{1}q_{2}L,

with
k_{1}\bmod q_{1}
and
k_{2}\bmod q_{2}L
.
With these decompositions we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

We have

S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})=S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})S^{(2)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c}),

where

(4.2)

S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q_{1}{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1}\bmod q_{1}\\
(a_{1},q_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(a_{1}\left((q_{2}L)^{2}F(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1})+q_{2}(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}+k_{1})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\right),

(4.3)

S^{(2)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q_{2}L{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}\\
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{2}\bmod q_{2}\\
(a_{2},q_{2})=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{2}L}\left(a_{2}\left(q_{1}^{2}LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})+q_{1}(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}+k_{2})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\right).

Proof.

We observe that the condition
L\mid H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})
, namely

k_{2}q_{1}+q_{1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\equiv 0\bmod L,

is equivalent to

k_{2}q_{1}+q_{1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\equiv 0\bmod q_{1}L,

because
\gcd(q_{1},L)=1
. The decomposition can then be obtained by direct computation.
∎

4.2.
Evaluating
S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})

We start by proving a general version of Kloosterman–Salié-type sums, which is a direct analogue of
[
13
, Lemma 26]
.

Let
G
be an integral quadratic form in
(n+1)
-variables with matrix
{\mathscr{M}}_{G}
, and let
\Delta_{G}:=\det{\mathscr{M}}_{G}
. That is,
{\mathscr{M}}_{G}
is a symmetric
(n+1)\times(n+1)
-matrix such that
2{\mathscr{M}}_{G}
has integer entries and
G(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}{\mathscr{M}}_{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}
.

Let
G^{*}
be the dual quadratic form associated to
G
, which is defined by the adjugate
{\mathscr{M}}_{G}^{\operatorname{adj}}:=(\Delta_{G}){\mathscr{M}}_{G}^{-1}
of
{\mathscr{M}}_{G}
. Note that
2^{n+1}\Delta_{G}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
2^{n}{\mathscr{M}}_{G}^{\operatorname{adj}}
has integer entries.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be as above and let
m\in{\mathbb{Z}},\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}
and
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let us define

T_{q}(G,m;\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\mathbf{b}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}}\textup{e}_{q}\left(a(G({\mathbf{b}})-m)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot{\mathbf{b}}\right).

Assume that
G
is non-degenerate and
\gcd(q,2\Delta_{G})=1
.
Then we have

T_{q}(G,m;\boldsymbol{c})=\left(\iota_{q}\left(\frac{2}{q}\right)\right)^{n+1}\left(\frac{2^{n+1}\Delta_{G}}{q}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{a}{q}\right)^{n+1}\textup{e}_{q}\left(-am-\overline{a2^{n+2}\Delta_{G}}2^{n}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\right),

where for any
d\bmod q
with
(d,q)=1
, we write
\overline{d}\bmod q
for the multiplicative inverse, and

\iota_{q}:=\sum_{x\bmod q}\textup{e}_{q}(x^{2})

is the quadratic Gauss sum of modulus
q
.

Let us deduce

Proposition 4.4
.

We have

S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})=\iota_{q_{1}}^{n+1}\left(\frac{2^{n+1}\Delta_{F}^{n}}{q_{1}}\right)\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(-\overline{q_{2}L^{2}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q_{1}\\
(a,q_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{a}{q_{1}}\right)^{n+1}\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(-a2^{n}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\right).

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

The change of variables for the
a_{1}
-sum
a_{1}\mapsto a_{1}^{\prime}:=a_{1}\overline{L^{2}}
gives

S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q_{1}{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1}^{\prime}\bmod q_{1}\\
(a_{1}^{\prime},q_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(a_{1}^{\prime}F(q_{2}L^{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\right).

The second non-singular change of variables for the
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}
-sum
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\mapsto{\mathbf{b}}:=q_{2}L^{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}
gives, with the notation of Lemma
4.3
,

S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})=\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(-\overline{q_{2}L^{2}}\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)T_{q_{1}}(F,0;\overline{q_{2}L^{2}}\boldsymbol{c}).

Applying Lemma
4.3
and the final change of variables
a\mapsto\overline{a2^{n+2}\Delta_{F}(q_{2}L^{2})^{2}}
directly yields the desired formula.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Uniformly for all
q,\boldsymbol{c},L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, we have

S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})\ll q_{1}^{\frac{n+2}{2}}\tau(q_{1})\gcd(q_{1},2^{n}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c}))^{\frac{1}{2}}.

When
n
is even and
F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})=0
, we have

\left|S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})\right|=\begin{cases}q_{1}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\varphi(q_{1})&\text{ if }q_{1}=\square;\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof of Corollary
4.5
.

Since
2\nmid q_{1}
, using
[
16
, (3.38)]
, we have
|\iota_{q_{1}}|=q_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Then clearly

|S^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})|=q_{1}^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q_{1}\\
(a,q_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{a}{q_{1}}\right)^{n+1}\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(-a2^{n}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\right)\right|.

When
n
is odd (resp. even), the inner exponential sum is a Ramanujan sum (resp. Gauss sum), which can both be bounded by

\ll\tau(q_{1})q_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\gcd(q_{1},2^{n}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c}))^{\frac{1}{2}}

by multiplicativity and by Weil’s bound (see
[
16
, Corollary 11.12]
).

Now assume that
n
is even and
F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})=0
. Then the inner sum is a character sum (recall that
2\nmid q_{1}
):

\sum_{a\bmod q_{1}}\left(\frac{a}{q_{1}}\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }q_{1}\neq\square;\\
\varphi(q_{1})&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(See also
[
13
, Proof of Lemma 26]
.)
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

We write
{\mathscr{M}}:=2{\mathscr{M}}_{G}
and we fix a matrix
R\in\operatorname{GL}_{n+1}({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})
such that

R^{t}{\mathscr{M}}R=\operatorname{diag}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n+1}),

with
\gcd(\beta_{i},q)=1
. Then the change of variables
{\mathbf{b}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}:=R^{-1}{\mathbf{b}}
yields

\displaystyle T_{q}(G,m;\boldsymbol{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q}(-am)\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}}\textup{e}_{q}\left(\overline{2}a\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\beta_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot R\boldsymbol{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q}(-am)\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{x\bmod q}\textup{e}_{q}\left(\overline{2}a\beta_{i}x^{2}+d_{i}x\right),

where we have written

\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n+1}):=R^{t}\boldsymbol{c}.

For every fixed
i
and
a\bmod q
with
(a,q)=1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\bmod q}\textup{e}_{q}\left(\overline{2}a\beta_{i}x^{2}+d_{i}x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{e}_{q}\left(-\overline{2a\beta_{i}}d_{i}^{2}\right)\sum_{x\bmod q}\textup{e}_{q}\left(\overline{2}a\beta_{i}\left(x+\overline{a\beta_{i}}d_{i}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{e}_{q}\left(-\overline{2a\beta_{i}}d_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{\overline{2}a\beta_{i}}{q}\right)\iota_{q},

where for the last equality we have executed the change of variables
x\mapsto x+\overline{a\beta_{i}}d_{i}
and made use of
[
16
, (3.38)]
.
Notice that modulo
q
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\overline{\beta_{i}}d_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\boldsymbol{c}^{t}R\operatorname{diag}(\overline{\beta_{1}},\ldots,\overline{\beta_{n+1}})\cdot R^{t}\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle\equiv\overline{\det{\mathscr{M}}}\boldsymbol{c}^{t}RR^{-1}{\mathscr{M}}^{\operatorname{adj}}(R^{-1})^{t}\cdot R^{t}\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle\equiv\overline{\det{\mathscr{M}}}\boldsymbol{c}^{t}{\mathscr{M}}^{\operatorname{adj}}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\overline{2^{n+1}\Delta_{G}}2^{n}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{c}).

Hence

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\textup{e}_{q}\left(-\overline{2a\beta_{i}}d_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{\overline{2}a\beta_{i}}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\textup{e}_{q}\left(-\overline{2a}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\overline{\beta_{i}}d_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{\overline{2}a}{q}\right)^{n+1}\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\beta_{i}}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\textup{e}_{q}\left(-\overline{a2^{n+2}\Delta_{G}}2^{n}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\right)\left(\frac{2a}{q}\right)^{n+1}\left(\frac{2^{n+1}\Delta_{G}}{q}\right).

We therefore conclude the desired formula.
∎

4.3.
Estimating
S^{(2)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{0},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
such that
\gcd(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n},L)=1
. Then, uniformly for all
q,\boldsymbol{c},L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, we have

S^{(2)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})\ll q_{2}^{\frac{n+3}{2}}L^{n}\gcd(q_{2},L)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proposition
4.6
is a direct analogue of
[
13
, Lemma 25]
, from which our proof is also inspired. The main challenge here is that we need to not only get a best “square-root cancellation” at the level
q_{2}
, but also to optimize the dependency on
L
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have that

\left|S^{(2)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{c})\right|^{2}\leqslant\varphi(q_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{2}\bmod q_{2}\\
(a_{2},q_{2})=1\end{subarray}}{\mathscr{S}}_{q,L}(a_{2},\boldsymbol{c}),

where for each fixed
a_{2}
, we let

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}_{q,L}(a_{2},\boldsymbol{c}):=\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q_{2}L{\mathbb{Z}})^{n+1}\\
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{2}L}\left(a_{2}\left(q_{1}^{2}LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})+q_{1}(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}+k_{2})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}\bmod q_{2}L\\
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i})\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{2}L}\left(a_{2}\left(q_{1}^{2}L(F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1})-F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}))+q_{1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}:\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\equiv 0\bmod L\\
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}:H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1})\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{2}L}\left(a_{2}\left(q_{1}^{2}L(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}-F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}))+q_{1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\right),

where for the last equality we have set
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{2}
.

We proceed by fixing
a_{2},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
and first sum over
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}
. For this purpose we rewrite

{\mathscr{S}}_{q,L}(a_{2},\boldsymbol{c})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\bmod q_{2}L\\
\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{2}L}\left(a_{2}q_{1}\left(-q_{1}LF(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})+\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\right)T_{q,L}(a_{2},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}),

where for each fixed
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
, we write

T_{q,L}(a_{2},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}:H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1})\equiv 0\bmod L}\textup{e}_{q_{2}}\left(a_{2}q_{1}^{2}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}\right).

The condition
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1})\equiv 0\mod L
can be resolved by adding an additive character sum of modulus
L
, so that the
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}
-variable ranges over all vectors modulo
q_{2}L
:

\displaystyle T_{q,L}(a_{2},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}\bmod q_{2}L}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l\bmod L}\textup{e}_{L}\left(lH_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1})\right)\textup{e}_{q_{2}}\left(a_{2}q_{1}^{2}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l\bmod L}\textup{e}_{L}(lk){\mathscr{T}}_{q,L}(a_{2},l,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}),

where for every fixed
l
, we let

\begin{split}{\mathscr{T}}_{q,L}(a_{2},l,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})&:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}\bmod q_{2}L}\textup{e}_{q_{2}L}\left(\left(a_{2}q_{1}^{2}L\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})+q_{1}q_{2}l\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1}\right)\\
&=\begin{cases}(q_{2}L)^{n+1}&\text{ if }q_{2}L\mid a_{2}q_{1}^{2}L\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})+q_{1}q_{2}l\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda});\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{split}

We are now reduced to counting the number of
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
and
l
such that
{\mathscr{T}}_{q,L}(a_{2},l,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})\neq 0
. Write

(4.4)

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}=\lcm(q_{2},L)\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},

with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\bmod\gcd(q_{2},L)
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\bmod\lcm(q_{2},L)
. Then the number of
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}
is entirely determined by the number of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}
and that of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
. Note that the divisibility condition above is equivalent to

(4.5)

\frac{q_{2}L}{\gcd(q_{2},L)}\mid a_{2}q_{1}^{2}\frac{L}{\gcd(q_{2},L)}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})+\frac{q_{2}}{\gcd(q_{2},L)}q_{1}l\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

So
{\mathscr{T}}_{q,L}(a_{2},l,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})
only depends on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
and we may write

{\mathscr{T}}_{q,L}(a_{2},l,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})={\mathscr{T}}_{q,L}(a_{2},l,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}).

On the other hand, the condition
\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\equiv 0\bmod L
is equivalent to

(4.6)

\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\equiv 0\bmod L.

The conditions (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) are only concerned with
l
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
.

With the decomposition (
4.4
), we have, modulo
q_{2}L
,

a_{2}q_{1}\left(-q_{1}LF(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0})+\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{0}\\
\equiv-a_{2}q_{1}^{2}LF(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})+(a_{2}q_{1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\boldsymbol{c})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}+(a_{2}q_{1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\boldsymbol{c})\cdot\lcm(q_{2},L)\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}.

Concerning
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}
, if
a_{2}
satisfies the condition

(4.7)

\gcd(q_{2},L)\mid a_{2}q_{1}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\boldsymbol{c},

then

We recall the expression (

{\mathscr{S}}^{(0)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(b;\boldsymbol{c})\neq 0

\boldsymbol{w}\bmod q

\displaystyle{\mathscr{S}}^{(1)}_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(b;\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bmod qL\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q^{n+1}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\bmod q}\textup{e}_{q}\left((\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{e}\right)\textup{e}_{qL}\left(\left(b\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\boldsymbol{c}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)

Now it is clear that

In particular, a necessary condition for this is

\boldsymbol{c}\equiv-b\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\bmod L.

The proof is thus completed.

\boldsymbol{c}

.

Assume that
\gcd(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n},L)=1
.
For

, consider the set

uniformly in

.

Taking Lemma

up to

. After appropriately redefining

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L},\eqref{eq:cond00}\text{ holds}}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{-\frac{n-3}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\sum_{T\nearrow\eta^{-1}QB^{-\tau+\varepsilon}}T^{-\frac{n-3}{2}}\#\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}(T,B)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\eta^{-n}B^{\varepsilon}\sum_{T\nearrow\eta^{-1}QB^{-\tau+\varepsilon}}T^{-\frac{n-5}{2}}\ll_{\varepsilon}\eta^{-n}B^{\varepsilon},

Finally, using (

\displaystyle\left|I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R}^{\pm};\boldsymbol{c})\right|

As for the case

\sum_{q\leqslant X}\frac{S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{0})I_{q,L,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(w_{B,R}^{\pm};\boldsymbol{0})}{(qL)^{n+1}}=\frac{B^{n+1}}{(LR)^{2}}{\mathscr{I}}(w_{R})\sum_{q\leqslant X}\frac{L^{-2n}S_{q,L,\boldsymbol{}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{0})}{q^{n+1}}+O_{N}(B^{-\varepsilon N}).

Combining with (
6.10
) and (
6.9
) and redefining

, we obtain the desired estimate. ∎

6.4.
Estimates of local densities

We end this section by establishing the following

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi},{\mathbb{R}}}
(resp.
\omega^{V}_{f}
) be the real (resp. finite) Tamagawa measure on
V({\mathbb{R}})
(resp.
{\mathscr{V}}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})
) induced by the adelic norm (
2.1
) (
2.2
), so that
\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi},{\mathbb{R}}}\times\omega^{V}_{f}
.
Assume that
n\geqslant 4
. Then we have

(6.12)

\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi},{\mathbb{R}}}\left({\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\right)\asymp R^{-(n-1)},

and

L^{-(n-1)-\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}\omega^{V}_{f}\left({\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\right)\ll L^{-(n-1)},\quad\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\asymp L^{-n}.

Proof.

The arguments in (
6.7
) and Remark
6.3
(see also
[
24
, §5.4]
) show that

(6.13)

\omega^{V}_{\boldsymbol{\xi},{\mathbb{R}}}\left({\mathscr{U}}(R,\boldsymbol{\xi})\right)=\frac{n-1}{2}{\mathscr{I}}_{R},

which is clearly
\asymp R^{-(n-1)}
.

We now estimate the non-archimedean local densities.
Let us fix an even integer
L_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
(depending only on
V
) such that
{\mathscr{V}}
(and hence
{\mathscr{W}}^{o}
) is regular over
{\mathbb{Z}}/L_{0}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Upon decomposing
{\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
into a disjoint union of finite adelic neighbourhoods modulo
L_{0}L
if necessary, the number of which is
\leqslant L_{0}^{n+1}=O(1)
, we may proceed under the assumption that
L_{0}\mid L
.
For any
p\nmid L
, as
{\mathscr{V}}\bmod p
is smooth, by the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula, we have, for any prime
l\neq p

\#{\mathscr{V}}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})=\sum_{i=0}^{2(n-1)}(-1)^{i}\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{Fr}_{p}|H^{i}({\mathscr{V}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{l}).

By the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem,
H^{j}({\mathscr{V}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{l})=0
when
j
is odd, and
H^{2i}({\mathscr{V}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{l})={\mathbb{Q}}_{l}(-i)
when
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
, unless the dimension
n-1=2k
is even, in which case
H^{2k}({\mathscr{V}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}},{\mathbb{Q}}_{l})={\mathbb{Q}}_{l}(-k)^{\oplus 2}
.
It follows that

\#{\mathscr{V}}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})=\begin{cases}\frac{p^{n}-1}{p-1}&n\text{ even};\\
\frac{p^{n}-1}{p-1}\pm p^{\frac{n-1}{2}}&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Let us define the usual
p
-adic density of

V({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
by

\sigma_{p}(V):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#{\mathscr{V}}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})}{p^{(n-1)k}}.

In particular, when
n\geqslant 4
, by Hensel’s lemma,

\sigma_{p}(V)=\frac{\#{\mathscr{V}}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})}{p^{\dim V}}=1+p^{-1}+O\left(p^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right).

According to Peyre
[
24
]
,

(6.14)

\omega^{V}_{f}\left({\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\right)=\prod_{p\mid L}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{V}};L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\times\prod_{p\nmid L}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sigma_{p}(V),

where for
p\mid L
,

\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{V}};L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#\left({\mathscr{V}}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})\cap{\mathscr{D}}_{p}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\right)}{p^{(n-1)k}},

is the “model measure” of
{\mathscr{D}}_{p}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\subseteq V({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
. By
[
27
, Theorem 2.13]
(since
{\mathscr{V}}
is regular modulo
L
),

(6.15)

\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{V}};L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\frac{1}{p^{(n-1)m_{p}}}.

Hence

L^{-(n-1)-\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}\omega^{V}_{f}\left({\mathscr{D}}(L,\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\right)=L^{-(n-1)}\prod_{p\mid L}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\times\prod_{p\nmid L}\left(1+O(p^{-\frac{3}{2}})\right)\ll L^{-(n-1)}.

Recall (
1.7
) and (
1.6
). We have

\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})=\prod_{p\mid L}\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\prod_{p\nmid L}\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}}),

where for every
p\nmid L
,

\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}}):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#{\mathscr{W}}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{k}{\mathbb{Z}})}{p^{nk}}

is the usual
p
-adic density of
{\mathscr{W}}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
. The scheme
{\mathscr{W}}^{o}
is regular modulo
L
. So again by
[
27
, Theorem 2.13]
, for every
p\mid L
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathscr{W}}^{o}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}):\boldsymbol{v}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bmod p^{m_{p}}\right\}}{p^{nk}}=\frac{1}{p^{nm_{p}}}.

By
[
24
, Lemme 5.4.6]
,

(6.16)

\sigma_{p}(V)=\frac{1-p^{-(n-1)}}{1-p^{-1}}\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}}).

So, whenever
n\geqslant 4
,

\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}})=1+O(p^{-\frac{3}{2}}).

Hence

\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})=L^{-n}\prod_{p\nmid L}\left(1+O(p^{-\frac{3}{2}})\right)\asymp L^{-n}.

The proof is completed.
∎

We now compare

Lemma 7.1
.

Let

\sup_{u_{j}\in R^{-1}[a_{j},b_{j}],j\geqslant 2}|F_{2}(u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})|\leqslant 1.

Then

|{\mathscr{I}}(w)-{\mathscr{I}}_{R}|\ll\eta{\mathscr{I}}_{R}.

Proof.

We define
1_{R}
to be the characteristic function of the conditions

y_{j}\in R^{-1}[a_{j},b_{j}]\textrm{ for all }2\leqslant j\leqslant n,

and we define
1_{R}^{\pm}
analogously to
w_{R}^{\pm}
. Similarly to (
6.2
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}_{R}

\displaystyle=|\det M|^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}1_{R}(\boldsymbol{y})\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial y_{1}}\right)^{-1}(0,\boldsymbol{y})\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{y}\int_{|u_{0}|\leqslant 1}u_{0}^{n-2}\operatorname{d}u_{0}

\displaystyle=|\det M|^{-1}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}\\
|u_{0}|\leqslant 1\end{subarray}}u_{0}^{n-2}1_{R}(\boldsymbol{y})\operatorname{d}u_{0}\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{y}.

The measure of the set of
\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
for which
w_{R}^{\pm}(\boldsymbol{y})\neq 1_{R}^{\pm}(\boldsymbol{y})
is
O(\eta R^{1-n})
, and the measure of the set of
u_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for which
w_{3}^{\pm}(u_{0})\neq 1_{[-1,1]}(u_{0})
is
O(\eta)
. We deduce from (
6.2
) that

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}(w)

\displaystyle=|\det M|^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}w_{R}^{\pm}(\boldsymbol{y})\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{y}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}u_{0}^{n-2}w_{3}^{\pm}(u_{0})\operatorname{d}u_{0}

\displaystyle=|\det M|^{-1}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}}1_{R}(\boldsymbol{y})\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{y}+O(\eta R^{1-n})\right)\left(\int_{|u_{0}|\leqslant 1}u_{0}^{n-2}\operatorname{d}u_{0}+O(\eta)\right)

\displaystyle={\mathscr{I}}_{R}+O(\eta R^{1-n})

\displaystyle={\mathscr{I}}_{R}(1+O(\eta))\qed.

We now come to the proof of Theorem
1.2
, which gives an asymptotic formula for
{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)
under the assumption that
0<\tau<1
, where

(LR)^{2}=O(B^{1-\tau}).

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
we also need an upper bound for

, where
\tau
is allowed to be negative. We will deduce the following proposition at the same time as we prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 4
,
L,R,B\geqslant 1
,
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
R,B\in\mathbb{R}
and assume that
(LR)^{2}=O(B^{1-\tau})
for some
-1<\tau<1
. Let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in{\mathscr{W}}^{o}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
. Then we have

{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)\ll_{\tau}\frac{B^{n-1+\varepsilon}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}(E_{\tau}(1;B)+1)+\frac{B^{\frac{(n+1)(1-\tau)}{2}+\varepsilon}}{L^{n+1}R^{n}},

where
E_{\tau}(1;B)
is defined as in Theorem
2.3
.

For the following assume that we are given a value
-1<\tau<1
. Let
1_{B,R}
be the indicator function for the conditions

\frac{t_{j}}{t_{0}}\in R^{-1}[a_{j},b_{j}]\textrm{ for all }2\leqslant j\leqslant n,\quad|t_{0}|\leqslant B.

Assume that we are given a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
with
F(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, and
1_{B,R}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
. Then we find that

|t_{j}|\leqslant R^{-1}|t_{0}|\max\{|a_{j}|,|b_{j}|\}\leqslant BR^{-1}\max\{|a_{j}|,|b_{j}|\}\leqslant B,\quad j\geqslant 2

under the assumption that
R^{-1}\max_{2\leqslant j\leqslant n}\{|a_{j}|,|b_{j}|\}\leqslant 1
. Moreover, we have

F(\boldsymbol{x})=t_{0}t_{1}+F_{2}(t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})

where
\bft=M\boldsymbol{x}
. In particular, if
t_{0}\neq 0
, we obtain that

|t_{1}|\leqslant|t_{0}|^{-1}|F_{2}(t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})|\leqslant|t_{0}|\sup_{u_{j}\in R^{-1}[a_{j},b_{j}],j\geqslant 2}|F_{2}(u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})|\leqslant|t_{0}|\leqslant B

under the assumption that
\sup_{u_{j}\in R^{-1}[a_{j},b_{j}],j\geqslant 2}|F_{2}(u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})|\leqslant 1
.
Note that for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
with
F(\boldsymbol{x})=0
and
t_{0}\neq 0
, we have

w^{-}_{B,R}(\boldsymbol{x})\leqslant 1_{B,R}(\boldsymbol{x})\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}w^{+}_{B\eta^{k},R}(\boldsymbol{x}),

and so

\displaystyle{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R}^{-};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(1_{B,R};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))

\displaystyle={\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}((R,\boldsymbol{\xi}),(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma});B)\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B\eta^{k},R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})).

We let
R_{0}
be sufficiently large such that

(7.1)

\sup_{u_{j}\in R_{0}^{-1}[a_{j},b_{j}],j\geqslant 2}|F_{2}(u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})|\leqslant 1,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad R_{0}^{-1}\max_{2\leqslant j\leqslant n}\{|a_{j}|,|b_{j}|\}\leqslant 1,

and we assume
R\geqslant R_{0}
in the following.

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose that
\eta\gg B^{-1/2}
and
\eta(1+\eta)<\frac{1}{2}
. Then, for
0<\tau<1
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B\eta^{k},R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))\\
={\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))+O_{\tau}\left(\eta\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\frac{B^{n-1}}{R^{n-1}}+\eta\frac{B^{n-1}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}B^{\varepsilon}E_{\tau}(\eta;B)\right).

For
-1<\tau\leqslant 0
we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B\eta^{k},R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))\\
={\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B,R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))+O_{\tau}\left(\frac{B^{n-1}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}B^{\varepsilon}E_{\tau}(\eta;B)+\frac{B^{\frac{(n+1)(1-\tau)}{2}+\varepsilon}}{L^{n+1}R^{n}}\right).

Proof.

We observe that the supports of the weight functions
w^{+}_{B\eta^{k},R}
overlap, but each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is contained in at most two of them because of the condition
\eta(1+\eta)<\frac{1}{2}
.
Therefore,

\sum_{k:B\eta^{k}\leqslant 1}{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B\eta^{k},R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))\leqslant 2\#\{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\leqslant C_{0}\}=O(1),

for a constant
C_{0}>0
which only depends on
F_{2}
and the intervals
[a_{j},b_{j}]
,
j\geqslant 2
.

From now on we focus on
k\geqslant 1
such that
B\eta^{k}>1
. Let

\tau_{0}:=\tau,

and define
\tau_{k}
by the equation

(7.2)

B^{1-\tau_{0}}=(B\eta^{k})^{1-\tau_{k}}.

Note that by assumption we have
-1<\tau_{0}<1
and hence
\tau_{k}\leqslant\tau_{0}
for all such
k
in consideration. Let
\theta
be a small parameter to be chosen later. If we assume moreover that
-1+\theta<\tau_{k}<1
, then we observe that

B\eta^{k}=B^{\frac{1-\tau_{0}}{1-\tau_{k}}}\geqslant B^{\frac{1-\tau_{0}}{2}}.

The dependence of the implicit constants in Theorem
2.3
is continuous and so in the compact interval
[-1+\theta,\tau_{0}]
they depend only on
\theta
and
\tau_{0}
. Now Theorem
2.3
implies that

(7.3)

{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B\eta^{k},R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))\ll_{\varepsilon,\tau_{0},\theta}\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\frac{(B\eta^{k})^{n-1}}{R^{n-1}}+\frac{(B\eta^{k})^{n-1}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}B^{\varepsilon}E_{k}(\eta;B),

for any

such that

\eta^{-15/2}(B\eta^{k})^{-\tau_{k}}+(B\eta^{k})^{-\frac{1+\tau_{k}}{4}},&\textrm{ if }n=4.\end{cases}

It follows from (
7.2

E_{k}(\eta;B)\ll(\eta^{-\frac{k(n-3)}{2}}+\eta^{-k})E_{0}(\eta;B).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k:B\eta^{k}>1\\
-1+\theta<\tau_{k}<1\\
k\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}{\mathscr{N}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}(w_{B\eta^{k},R}^{+};(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}))

\displaystyle\ll_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon,\theta}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k:B\eta^{k}>1\\
-1+\theta<\tau_{k}<1\\
k\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}\left(\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\frac{(B\eta^{k})^{n-1}}{R^{n-1}}+\frac{(B\eta^{k})^{n-1}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}B^{\varepsilon}E_{k}(\eta;B)\right)

(7.4)

\displaystyle\ll_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon,\theta}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\frac{\eta B^{n-1}}{R^{n-1}}+\frac{B^{n-1}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}B^{\varepsilon}E_{0}(\eta;B)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}(\eta^{k(n-2)}+\eta^{k(n/2+1/2)})

(7.5)

\displaystyle\ll_{\tau_{0},\varepsilon,\theta}

\displaystyle\eta\mathfrak{S}({\mathscr{W}};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\frac{B^{n-1}}{R^{n-1}}+\eta\frac{B^{n-1}}{L^{n}R^{n-1}}B^{\varepsilon}E_{\tau}(\eta;B).

Suppose that
k
is chosen such that
\tau_{k}\leqslant-1+\theta
. If
\eta^{k}\geqslant B^{-1}
, then we have

B^{1-\tau_{0}}=(B\eta^{k})^{1-\tau_{k}}\geqslant(B\eta^{k})^{2-\theta}=B^{2-\theta}\eta^{(2-\theta)k},

and hence

Therefore, there is a constant

such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k:B\eta^{k}>1\\

\left|\frac{t_{j}}{t_{0}}\right|\ll R^{-1}\textrm{ for all }2\leqslant j\leqslant n,\quad\left|\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}\right|\ll R^{-2}.

. Let
\Lambda

.

.

In the case

\varepsilon

. Note that if

.

We start with the case

Proof of Theorem

Appendix A
Rational approximations on quadrics

The goal of this appendix is to first recall the “
\alpha
-constant”
introduced in the recent work
[
19
]
of McKinnon-M. Roth, which pioneers the study of Diophantine approximation on algebraic varieties. (For later developments see also
[
14
]
.)
This is a generalisation of the classical notion of “irrationality measure” originally attached to real numbers. We then compute its value achieved on generic open subsets on projective quadrics, so as to deduce the optimality of Theorem
1.1
.

A.1.
Approximation constants

To ease the exposition, let us place ourselves in the following setting. Let
X\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be an irreducible projective algebraic variety defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
L
be an ample line bundle, to which we associate a height function
H_{L}
. We choose a projective distance function
d_{\nu}
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
for a place
\nu
of
{\mathbb{Q}}
as in
[
19
, §2]
. Let
Q_{0}\in X({\mathbb{Q}}_{\nu})
be a fixed
\nu
-adic point.

Definition A.1
.

For any subvariety
Y\subset X
, we define the (
best
)
approximation constant

\alpha^{\nu}(Q_{0},Y)
(depending also on
L
and
\nu
) to be the
infimum
of
\gamma>0
such that there exist infinitely many points
P\in Y({\mathbb{Q}})
such that

d_{\nu}(P,Q_{0})^{\gamma}H_{L}(P)<1.

It can be shown that (see
[
19
, §2]
for details)
\nu
-adically around
Q_{0}
all distances are equivalent, hence the
\alpha
constant does not depend on the choice of
d_{\nu}
, nor on the choice of
H_{L}
, nor is the bound
1
of importance.
The constant
\alpha^{\nu}(Q_{0},Y)
measures how quickly the height must grow for a sequence of rational points of
Y
that tends to
Q_{0}
. The smaller the
\alpha
-constant is, the slower the height is allowed to grow, which means
Q_{0}
is better approximable. In dimension one the approximation constant for algebraic points is known, as a corollary of the classical K. Roth theorem (
[
19
, Theorem 2.16]
) for
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.

We can furthermore ask for approximating the point
Q_{0}
in a “generic direction”. This motivates the definition of another constant as follows.
It was first formulated by Pagelot (unpublished) and further developed in the first author’s Ph.D. thesis
[
14
]
.

Definition A.2
.

With the notation above, we define the
essential constant
of
Q_{0}

to be

\alpha_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\nu}(Q_{0})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subset X\end{subarray}}\alpha^{\nu}(Q_{0},Y),

where
Y
ranges over all open dense subvarieties of
X
.

A.2.

We go back to our initial setting. Let

Theorem

|x_{0}|\leqslant|x_{0}x_{1}|=|F_{2}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})|\ll\max_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|^{2},

we then have

\operatorname{d}_{{\mathbb{R}}}(P,Q_{0})^{2}H(P)\geqslant\left(\frac{\max_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|}{|x_{0}|}\right)^{2}|x_{0}|\gg\frac{|x_{0}|}{|x_{0}|^{2}}|x_{0}|\geqslant 1.

We next assume that
\nu
corresponds to a prime
p
.
For every
P=[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n}]\in U({\mathbb{Q}})\setminus\{Q_{0}\}
with coprime integers
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
, the equation
x_{0}x_{1}=-F_{2}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
implies that

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(x_{1})+\operatorname{ord}_{p}(x_{0})\geqslant 2\min_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}\operatorname{ord}_{p}(x_{i}),

whence

(A.5)

|x_{0}x_{1}|_{\nu}\leqslant\max_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|^{2}_{\nu}.

Fix
i_{0}\geqslant 2
(depending on
P
) such that
|x_{i_{0}}|^{2}_{\nu}
attains the maximum above. We necessarily have
x_{i_{0}}\neq 0
since otherwise we would have
x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n}=0
and this would imply
x_{1}=0
, contradicting to
P\not\in V_{1}({\mathbb{Q}})
.

Note that by equation (
A.5
) we find that
|x_{1}|_{\nu}^{2}\leqslant\max_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|_{\nu}^{2}
. We conclude that.

\operatorname{d}_{\nu}(P,Q_{0})^{2}H(P)\geqslant\frac{|x_{i_{0}}|_{\nu}^{2}}{|x_{0}|_{\nu}^{2}}|x_{1}|\geqslant\frac{\max_{2\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|^{2}_{\nu}}{|x_{0}x_{1}|_{\nu}}\geqslant 1.

In summary, we have proven that for every
P\in U({\mathbb{Q}})\setminus\{Q_{0}\}
and for every
\nu
,

\operatorname{d}_{\nu}(P,Q_{0})^{2}H(P)\gg 1.

This confirms the claim (
A.3
).

Finally we prove the reverse inequality

(A.6)

\alpha_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\nu}(Q_{0})\leqslant 2,

for arbitrary
\nu\in\operatorname{Val}({\mathbb{Q}})
and
Q_{0}\in V({\mathbb{Q}})
. We shall exhibit a family of
{\mathbb{Q}}
-curves through
Q_{0}
which are
very free
, meaning that their union sweeping out a dense subset of the quadric. The idea essentially follows from the fact that quadrics containing at least one rational point are rational. We fix a rational hyperplane
H\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
not containing
Q_{0}
. Then any generic line through
Q_{0}
intersects
H
at a unique point. This defines a
2
-to-
1
projection map
\operatorname{pr}
from an open subset of
V
to
H
. Now the Zariski closure of
\operatorname{pr}^{-1}(l)
, the preimage of any generic line
l\subset H
via
\operatorname{pr}
in
V
, is a conic through
Q_{0}
. Moving the line
l\subset H
gives a family of smooth conics through
Q_{0}
, each one having
\alpha
-constant equal to
2
. This confirms the claim (
A.6
).
∎

Remarks A.5
.

(1)

We can show that
\alpha^{\nu}(Q_{0},V_{1})=1
, whenever
Q_{0}\in V({\mathbb{Q}})
and
V_{1}({\mathbb{Q}})\setminus\{Q_{0}\}\neq\varnothing
(or equivalently
F_{2}
is isotropic), where
V_{1}
is defined as in the proof of Proposition
A.4
. So
V_{1}
is “locally accumulating” in the sense of
[
14
]
, which morally contains rational points that are “better approximable” to
Q_{0}
.

(2)

Rational points are usually “badly approximable” compared to irrational ones, i.e., they have larger
\alpha
-constants. Recent work of de Saxcé
[
29
]
deals with rational approximations of a fixed real algebraic point on a projective quadric based on a version of Schmidt’s subspace theorem, and it is proved that such a point has smaller
\alpha
-constant.

(3)

Although not directly related to the results of this article, it is worth pointing out that studying the distribution of rational points inside of a shrinking neighbourhood of size corresponding exactly to the essential constant, say
R\asymp B^{\frac{1}{2}}
, is an interesting (but challenging) problem. The limit distribution is in general quite different from the one given by the Tamagawa measures.
See
[
14
]
and the references therein for more details on this subject.
