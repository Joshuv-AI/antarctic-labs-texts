A generalized model of magnon kinetics and subgap magnetic noise
Haocheng Fang, Shu Zhang, Yaroslav Tserkovnyak

Abstract
Magnetic noise spectroscopy provides a noninvasive probe of spin dynamics in magnetic materials. We consider two-dimensional magnetically ordered insulators with magnon excitations, especially those supporting long-distance magnon transport, where nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers enable the access to (nearly) ballistic transport regime of magnons. We develop a generalized theory to describe the magnon transport across a wide range of length scales. The longitudinal dynamic spin susceptibility is derived from the Boltzmann equation and extended to a Lindhard form, which is modified by both the spin-conserving magnon collisions and spin relaxation. Our result is consistent with the diffusive (ballistic) model for the length scale much larger (smaller) than the magnon mean free path, and provides a description for the intermediate regime. We also give a prediction for the NV transition rate in different magnon transport regimes.

Introduction

Magnons are quanta of spin-wave excitations in magnetically ordered systems.
The study of magnon transport in magnetic insulators is both fundamentally and technologically motivated,

Considering the magnetic noise at the position of the NV sensor, the contribution from different length scales in the material are weighted by a form factor, which is determined by the demagnetization kernel, i.e., the spatial distribution of the stray field generated by a magnetic moment. III
Dynamic susceptibility

III.1
Diffusive regime

Suppose the temperature is uniform and quickly relaxing. In the diffusive regime (
d\gg l
), the natural dynamics of the deviation of the magnon density
\delta n
from the thermal equilibrium can be described by the diffusion equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta n+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{s}=-\frac{\chi_{0}}{\tau}\mu,

(2)

where
\chi_{0}
is the uniform static longitudinal spin susceptibility,
\tau
is the spin relaxation time, and
\mathbf{j}_{s}=-\sigma\bm{\nabla}\mu
is the spin current. As in Fick’s law, we have the spin conductivity
\sigma
and the spin chemical potential
\mu=\delta n/\chi_{0}-h
,
where
h
is a force thermodynamically conjugate to
\delta n
, which may arise from an external magnetic field. As the magnon density
\delta n
relaxes towards
\chi_{0}h
,
we have

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)\delta n-D\nabla^{2}\delta n=\chi_{0}\left(-D\nabla^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)h,

(3)

where
D=\sigma/\chi_{0}
is the spin diffusion constant.

Taking a single Fourier component of the external perturbation

h(\mathbf{r},t)=h(\mathbf{q},\omega)e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}-i\omega t}
, in the linear response,

\delta n(\mathbf{r},t)=\delta n(\mathbf{q},\omega)e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}-i\omega t}
follows the same modulation. The dynamic spin susceptibility is given by
\delta n(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\chi_{D}(\mathbf{q},\omega)h(\mathbf{q},\omega)
, where

\displaystyle\chi_{D}=\frac{\chi_{0}(Dq^{2}+1/\tau)}{-i\omega+Dq^{2}+1/\tau}.

(4)

When the spin relaxation effect dominates the magnon kinetics,
ql_{s}\ll 1
, where
l_{s}=\sqrt{D\tau}
is the spin diffusion length, the expression simplifies,
\chi_{D}\rightarrow\chi_{0}/(-i\omega\tau+1)
. In the fast relaxation limit
\tau\rightarrow 0
,
\chi_{D}\rightarrow\chi_{0}
, the system always stays in equilibrium with
\mu=0
.

III.2
Ballistic regime

In the opposite limit (
d\ll l
), we consider the ballistic transport of magnons, following the Lindhard treatment of the following Hamiltonian of a magnon gas:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{1}

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}-\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,h(\mathbf{r},t)a^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})a(\mathbf{r}),

where
a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
and
a_{\mathbf{k}}
are the (bosonic) magnon operators,

a(\mathbf{r})=(1/\sqrt{V})\sum_{\mathbf{k}}a_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
,

\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
is the magnon dispersion, and
h(\mathbf{r},t)
is a perturbing potential in favor of a nonvanishing local magnon density.

We again focus on one Fourier component
h(\mathbf{q},\omega)
of the perturbation, and define the density operator

\rho_{\mathbf{q}}=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,a^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})a(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}=(1/V)\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}
, where

\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}=a_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}
. The perturbation term thus reads
H_{1}=(1/V)\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\rho_{\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{q}}h(\mathbf{q},\omega)e^{-i\omega t}
. The time evolution of
\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}
follows the Heisenberg equation

i\hbar(d/dt)\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}=[\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}},H]
, which yields

\displaystyle(\hbar\omega+i0^{+})\langle\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}\rangle

\displaystyle=(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2})\langle\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}\rangle

(6)

\displaystyle+(f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2})h(\mathbf{q},\omega).

Here,
f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}=\langle a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=1\big/\left(e^{\beta\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}-1\right)
is the Bose-Einstein distribution of the magnon gas in equilibrium,
\beta=1/k_{B}T
, and
i0^{+}
is a regularization to suppress ringing. Since
\delta n(\mathbf{q},\omega)=(1/V)\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\langle\rho_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}\rangle
, we arrive at the Lindhard function for the dynamic susceptibility of magnons:

\displaystyle\chi_{B}=-\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2}-\hbar\omega-i0^{+}},

(7)

where we have replaced the sum over wavevectors by the integration.

III.3
General form

Studies by Kliewer and Fuchs
Kliewer and Fuchs 1969
and Mermin
Mermin 1970
have shown that the Lindhard function of an electron gas can be generalized in the presence of electron collisions under the relaxation-time approximation.
Here, we derive a general form of the dynamic spin susceptibility of the magnon gas, taking into account both magnon collisions and spin relaxation. The final result, extended to a Lindhard from, is consistent with the forms in both diffusive and ballistic limits, as given above.

First, in the long-wavelength limit, we describe the magnons as semiclassical wave packets with a distribution

f_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)
in the phase space, the dynamics of which is captured by the Boltzmann equation:

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{r}}+\frac{\partial h(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial\mathbf{r}}\!\cdot\!\frac{\partial}{\partial\hbar\mathbf{k}}\right]f_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\left(\!\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial t}\!\right)_{\!\!c}+\left(\!\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial t}\!\right)_{\!\!r}.

(8)

Here,
\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}=\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial\hbar\mathbf{k}
is the group velocity of the magnon wave packet and
h(\mathbf{r},t)
is a slowly varying potential [consistent with those in Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
5
)] driving the motion of the wave packet.
The two scattering terms on the right-hand side respectively characterize collisional processes that conserve the magnon number and relaxational processes that do not. The former refers to the spin-conserving exchange-dominated scattering between magnons, including Umklapp processes, spin-conserving magnon-phonon scattering, and scattering by nonmagnetic disorder. These processes can be elastic and inelastic, which locally redistribute the momentum and energy of magnons while preserving the magnon number. The latter corresponds to magnon decay as a result of those magnon-phonon and magnon-magnon scattering processes that create or annihilate magnons, arising from spin-orbit couplings and magnetic dipole-dipole interactions.
When spin-conserving scattering processes are dominant, a magnon chemical potential can be introduced in the magnon distribution, which has been useful for the description of the spin Seebeck effect,
Cornelissen et al. 2015
;
Cornelissen et al. 2016
magnon Bose-Einstein condensation,
Demokritov et al. 2006
;
Giamarchi et al. 2008
;
Bender et al. 2012
etc.
As we shall see, the local magnon chemical potential is crucial in our treatment, which enables a self-consistent solution to the Boltzmann equation under the relaxation-time approximation. The collective energy dynamics is neglected in this work by taking the temperature as a uniform constant. We primarily consider the magnon energy relaxation into a phonon bath, as phonons can have a relatively high heat conductivity around the room temperature.

Assuming the relaxation-time approximation is still valid, we introduce a collision time
\tau_{c}
and a relaxation time
\tau_{r}
for the spin conserving and non-conserving scattering processes, respectively.
Both
\tau_{c}
and
\tau_{r}
are considered to be energy dependent, though we drop the explicit
\mathbf{k}
dependence in notation.
The relaxation term can be treated straightforwardly,

\displaystyle\left(\!\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial t}\!\right)_{\!\!r}=-\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-\widetilde{f}^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau_{r}},

(9)

which relaxes the magnon distribution towards

\widetilde{f}^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}=1\big/\left\{e^{\beta[\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-h(\mathbf{r},t)]}-1\right\}\approx f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}-(\partial f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}})h(\mathbf{r},t)
according to the external perturbation.
Following the work by Kragler and Thomas on the modified Lindhard function for electrons,
Kragler and Thomas 1980

let us introduce a local magnon chemical potential
\mu(\mathbf{r},t)
to handle the collision term, which relaxes the system towards a (fictitious) local equilibrium distribution

g_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)=1\big/\left\{e^{\beta\left[\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu(\mathbf{r},t)\right]}-1\right\}
,

\displaystyle\left(\!\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial t}\!\right)_{\!\!c}=-\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-g_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau_{c}}.

(10)

This describes the thermalization of magnons among different energy modes by redistributing their momenta, and at the same time, enables us to implement the conservation of the local magnon number
n(\mathbf{r},t)=\int(d^{2}\mathbf{k}/4\pi^{2})f_{\mathbf{k}}
: The integration of Eq. (
10
) over all wavevectors must vanish.
By expanding

f_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)=f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}+\delta f_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)
,
and

g_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)=f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}+\delta g_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r},t)\approx f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}-(\partial f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}})\mu(\mathbf{r},t)
to linear order, the local chemical potential is determined self-consistently,

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{r},t)=-\frac{\int(d^{2}\mathbf{k}/4\pi^{2})\delta f_{\mathbf{k}}/\tau_{c}}{\int(d^{2}\mathbf{k}/4\pi^{2})(\partial f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}})/\tau_{c}}.

(11)

As a reminder,
\tau_{c}
is generally

dependent and cannot be taken out of the integral.

Applying the relaxation-time approximations (
9
,
10

8

\displaystyle\left(-i\omega+i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{r}}\right)\delta f_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)+\frac{1}{\tau_{c}}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}}{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\mu(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\left(i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{r}}\right)\left(\!\frac{\partial f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}\!\right)h(\mathbf{q},\omega).

(12)

The equation above is first integrated to eliminate
\delta f

\mu(\mathbf{q},\omega)=M(\mathbf{q},\omega)h(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle M(\mathbf{q},\omega)={\displaystyle\int}\cfrac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{4\pi^{2}}\cfrac{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+1/\tau_{r}}{-i\omega+i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+1/\tau_{c}+1/\tau_{r}}\cfrac{1}{\tau_{c}}\left(\cfrac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}}{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[{\displaystyle\int}\cfrac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{4\pi^{2}}\cfrac{-i\omega+i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+1/\tau_{r}}{-i\omega+i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+1/\tau_{c}+1/\tau_{r}}\cfrac{1}{\tau_{c}}\left(\cfrac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}}{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\right]^{-1}\hskip 0.0pt.

(13)

Reintegrating Eq. (
12

\mu(\mathbf{q},\omega)
substituted in yields the dynamic spin susceptibility

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\chi=-\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+1/\tau_{r}+(1/\tau_{c})M(\mathbf{q},\omega)}{-i\omega+i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+1/\tau_{c}+1/\tau_{r}}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}}{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}\right).

At the linear order in

\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2}\approx\hbar\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}\approx\frac{f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2}}.

(15)

One therefore recognizes the Lindhard form of the result (

\displaystyle\chi\rightarrow\frac{-L_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega+i/\tau_{c})}{1-[1/(1-i\omega\tau_{c})][1-L_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega+i/\tau_{c})/L_{0}(\mathbf{q},0)]},

(16)

for a constant

,

is the bare Lindhard function as given by Eq. (

This is exactly the form of Mermin’s result for the modified Lindhard dielectric function of an electron gas under relaxation-time approximation.
Mermin 1970

For magnons following the Bose-Einstein distribution function, the derivative
(\partial f^{0}_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}})
grows towards the bottom of the magnon band, and is significant for all subthermal energies.
This is very different from the case with electrons, where the states around the Fermi surface dominate.
Therefore, it is necessary to account for the energy dependence of the relaxation time
\tau_{c}
and
\tau_{r}
inside the integrals.

To extend our result eliminating the long-wavelength assumption, we restore the Lindhard form by applying Eq. (

by
(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2})/\hbar

\displaystyle\chi_{F}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=

\displaystyle-\widetilde{L}_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)+\widetilde{N}_{0,1}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

(17)

\displaystyle+\cfrac{\widetilde{N}_{1,0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\left[\widetilde{L}_{1}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-\widetilde{N}_{1,1}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\right]}{L_{1}(\mathbf{q},0)\!+\!\widetilde{N}_{2,0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)},

It can also be shown that the full susceptibility (

4

from which we can extract the effective transport coefficients in terms of microscopic parameters. The details are shown in Appendix A.
As a simple but natural scenario, we assume the magnon mean free path

is energy independent, giving a
\mathbf{k}
dependent collision time

\tau_{c}=l/|\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}|
.
The relaxation time is also energy dependent
\tau_{r}=\hbar/2\alpha\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
, where
\alpha
is the Gilbert damping.
For a quadratic dispersion relation
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}=A\mathbf{k}^{2}+\Delta
,
we obtain the spin diffusion constant

D\approx{\pi l\sqrt{A\Delta}/2\hbar}
, the spin conductivity

\sigma\approx{k_{B}Tl/8\hbar\sqrt{A\Delta}}
, and the spin relaxation time

\tau\approx{\hbar/2\alpha\Delta\ln\left(k_{B}T/\Delta\right)}
.
These parameters are used to plot the diffusive curve in Fig.

.

It is worth remarking on the physical implications of these expressions.
In general, spin transport can be quantitatively sensitive to the scattering regime. Here, the energy dependence of the collision time
\tau_{c}\sim 1/|\mathbf{k}|
accentuates low-energy magnons in two-dimensional magnon transport. As a result, the spin conductivity can be enhanced by a small magnon gap compared with the three-dimensional case, which may be relevant to the recent experimental observations.
Wei et al. 2021

From the two-dimensional spin diffusion constant
D
, an effective velocity of magnons contributing to the diffusive spin transport can be introduced
v=2D/l=\pi\sqrt{A\Delta}/\hbar
, which is much lower than the velocity of the thermal magnons with energy
k_{B}T\gg\Delta
. Intuitively, due to the energy dependence of the Bose-Einstein distribution, a large population of low-energy magnons participate in the spin transport, even at the temperature regime that is of the same order of (but lower than) the magnetic ordering temperature.
The corresponding magnon wavelength
\lambda=4\sqrt{A/\Delta}
, which is of the order of the magnetic healing length, emerges as another length scale in the spin transport. Although much larger than the thermal-magnon wavelength, it is still much smaller than the magnon mean free path, consistent with the semiclassical description and relaxation-time approximation. The collision time of these characteristic low-energy magnons can also be taken as an effective collision time constant in the system:

, which determines the frequency range
\omega\tau_{0}\ll 1
of the validity of the diffusive model.

Our approach is easily adaptable to magnetically ordered systems with a different dispersion relation, for example, antiferromagnetic thin films with gapped magnons.
The results are particularly helpful for experiments on magnetic insulators exhibiting long-distance spin transport, giving a prediction for the transition rate of NV placed close to the material.
Our theory provides a systematic framework for the NV study of magnon transport across the ballistic and diffusive regimes.
It is readily applicable for thin films of three-dimensional magnets or intrinsically two-dimensional magnetic van der Waals materials,
Kim et al. 2019

Zhang and Tserkovnyak 2021

Future work can explore many-body effects associated with magnon-magnon and magnon-phonon interactions beyond the relaxation-time approximation, including a possibility of hydrodynamic transport aspects in ultra clean materials, which could be particularly interesting in two dimensions. A theory of coupled transport of spin and heat may be necessary at low temperatures.

VI
Conclusion

To summarize, we have studied two-dimensional magnon transport in a magnetic thin film and derived a longitudinal dynamic spin susceptibility generally applicable across different transport regimes, which may be accessed by an NV center with a wide range of probing length scales. The resulted features in the magnetic noise at subgap frequencies are shown in the NV relaxation rate as a function of the NV distance and frequency for experimental comparison. Our theory motivates further studies of ballistic and hydrodynamic regimes in clean magnetic materials with long-range spin transport. We have also extracted the effective diffusive parameters from the diffusive limit of the general model and discussed the dominance of low-energy magnons in two-dimensional magnon transport.
