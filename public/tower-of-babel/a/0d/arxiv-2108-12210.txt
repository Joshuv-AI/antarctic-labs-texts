Residual eccentricity of inspiralling orbits at the gravitational-wave detection threshold: Accurate estimates using post-Newtonian theory
Alexandria Tucker, Clifford M. Will

Abstract
We use equations of motion containing gravitational radiation-reaction terms through 4.5 post-Newtonian order to calculate the late-time eccentricities of inspiraling binary systems of non-spinning compact bodies as they cross the detection threshold of ground-based gravitational-wave interferometers. The initial eccentricities can be as large as 0.999 0.999 . We find that the final eccentricities are systematically smaller than those predicted by the leading quadrupole approximation, by as much as 30 percent for a 300 solar mass binary crossing the LIGO/Virgo detection threshold at 10 10 Hz, or eight percent smaller for a 60 solar mass binary. We find an analytic formula for the late-time eccentricity that accurately accounts for the higher-order post-Newtonian effects, generalizing a formula derived by Peters and Mathews in the 1960s. We also find that the final eccentricities are independent of the ratio of the masses of the two compact bodies to better than two percent.



I
Introduction and Summary

A notable fact about gravitational radiation from binary systems is that it takes angular momentum away from the system as effectively as it takes away energy. A consequence is that as the binary system shrinks, or inspirals, it circularizes, and this effect is so pronounced that the orbits will generally become extremely circular long before they coalesce. Consider for example, the first detected inspiraling binary system, the Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar B1913+16. Its orbit has a rather large orbital eccentricity of
0.617
. But by the time its gravitational wave signals cross the LIGO/Virgo detection threshold of around
10
Hz, about 390 million years from now, its eccentricity will be only about
5\times 10^{-6}
.

This fact led to the expectation, borne out by experience, that the initial gravitational wave signals detected by the LIGO/Virgo network would be from compact binary sources whose orbits were essentially perfectly circular, apart from their monotonic inspiral (see, however
Gayathri et al. 2020
;
Gamba et al. 2021
for preliminary evidence of a highly eccentric merger in GW190521).

But this assumes systems that undergo isolated evolution from wide, non-relativistic orbits over long periods of time. However, there is reason to expect that some detectable gravitational wave signals will not come from such pristine sources. A system could be formed in a tight but highly eccentric orbit by three-body processes that send one body far from the system, leaving a tight binary remnant
Heggie 1975
, or by direct capture from an unbound orbit into a bound eccentric orbit by gravitational-wave emission during the close passage
Quinlan and Shapiro 1987
. In a hierarchical three-body system consisting of a compact binary in the presence of a distant third body, the famous Kozai-Lidov oscillations
Kozai 1962
;
Lidov 1962
could drive the binary’s eccentricity to large values, even as gravitational-wave emission causes the orbit to shrink and attempts to circularize it
Wen 2003
;
Naoz et al. 2013
;
VanLandingham
et al. 2016
;
Silsbee and Tremaine 2017
;
Hoang et al. 2018
;
Randall and
Xianyu 2018a
;
Randall and
Xianyu 2018b
.

Such templates may not be as effective in detecting signals from eccentric inspirals, and may introduce biases in estimating the parameters of the sources. Conversely, the detection and measurement of eccentric inspiral events could serve to confirm or distinguish among various proposed astrophysical formation channels for these inspiralling compact binaries (see

Our goal in this paper is to provide an accurate map from the initial parameters of an arbitrarily eccentric binary orbit to the orbital eccentricity when the gravitational wave frequency reaches a detection threshold for a given detector. We imagine a binary consisting of two non-spinning “point” bodies of arbitrary mass whose evolution is dominated by two-body relativistic effects, i.e. in which other perturbations, such as Kozai-Lidov effects, can be ignored. We will be concerned only with the long-term evolution of such systems leading up to their entering the sensitive band of the detector, not with the subsequent evolution leading to merger and ringdown. We ignore finite-size effects, such as tidal interactions, as these are relevant mainly for the late-time, highly relativistic regime. We also ignore spin effects.

Such a map already exists. It is based on the classic 1963-1964 papers by Philip Peters and Jon Mathews

II
Evolution of compact binary orbits to high post-Newtonian order

II.1
Equations of motion

We analyze the orbital evolution of a binary system of compact, non-spinning bodies in the post-Newtonian approximation. We work in harmonic coordinates, the natural basis for post-Newtonian theory, gravitational radiation and radiation reaction (see
Poisson and Will 2014
for a pedagogical treatment of post-Newtonian theory). Letting
\bm{r}_{a}
and
m_{a}
respectively denote the position and mass of body
a\in\{1,2\}
, we define the relative position vector
\bm{r}\equiv\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}
and unit vector
\bm{n}=\bm{r}/r
pointing from body 2 to body 1, the total mass
m\equiv m_{1}+m_{2}
, and the symmetric mass ratio
\eta=m_{1}m_{2}/m^{2}
.
The relative velocity, acceleration and angular momentum vectors are
\bm{v}=d\bm{r}/dt
,
\bm{a}=d\bm{v}/dt
, and
\bm{h}=\bm{r}\times\bm{v}
.
The equations of motion in terms of relative coordinates take the general form

\displaystyle\bm{a}=

\displaystyle-\frac{Gm}{r^{2}}\,\bm{n}+\frac{Gm}{r^{2}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\bm{n}+\frac{1}{\dot{r}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\bm{v}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{8}{5}\eta\frac{Gm}{r^{2}}\frac{Gm}{rc^{3}}\left(\dot{r}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\bm{n}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\bm{v}\right)+\bm{a}_{\rm Tail}\,.

(4)

Here,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(1)}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(2)}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(3)}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(1)}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(2)}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{c}}^{(3)}
denote the conservative contributions at 1PN, 2PN, and 3PN orders, respectively, and

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{rr}}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{(1)}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{(2)}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{(3)}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{rr}}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{(1)}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{(2)}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{(3)}

denote radiation reaction terms at 2.5PN, 3.5PN, and 4.5PN orders respectively. To the orders of interest, the
\cal{A}
and
\cal{B}
coefficients take the general forms

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm c}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m,n}a^{(N)}_{lmn}\frac{\delta_{l+m+n,N}}{c^{2N}}\left(\frac{Gm}{r}\right)^{l}\left(\dot{r}^{2}\right)^{m}\left(v^{2}\right)^{n}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\rm c}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m,n}b^{(N)}_{lmn}\frac{\delta_{l+m+n,N}}{c^{2N}}\left(\frac{Gm}{r}\right)^{l}\left(\dot{r}^{2}\right)^{m}\left(v^{2}\right)^{n}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm rr}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m,n}c^{(N)}_{lmn}\frac{\delta_{l+m+n,N}}{c^{2N}}\left(\frac{Gm}{r}\right)^{l}\left(\dot{r}^{2}\right)^{m}\left(v^{2}\right)^{n}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\rm rr}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m,n}d^{(N)}_{lmn}\frac{\delta_{l+m+n,N}}{c^{2N}}\left(\frac{Gm}{r}\right)^{l}\left(\dot{r}^{2}\right)^{m}\left(v^{2}\right)^{n}\,.

(5)

for general mass ratios are given in Appendix
A

4
) is the lowest order 4PN tail term, given formally by

(6)

where, to the required PN order,

II.2
Osculating orbits and the perturbed Kepler problem

We employ the “osculating orbit” approach to solving the perturbed Kepler problem (see, for example
Poisson and Will 2014
). In this method, the equations of motion (
4
) are summarized as

\bm{a}=-\frac{Gm}{r^{2}}\bm{n}+\delta\bm{a}\,.

(8)

The perturbed orbit is defined by six “osculating” orbit elements, given by the elements of a pure Keplerian orbit that is momentarily “tangent” to the perturbed orbit, i.e. that has the same momentary values of
\bm{r}
and
\bm{v}
. Because the orbit is perturbed, these osculating elements are no longer constant in time (see Fig.
3
).
They are the semilatus rectum
p
, eccentricity
e
, orbital inclination
\iota
, nodal angle
\Omega
, pericenter angle
\omega
and time of pericenter passage
T
(this sixth element will not be relevant for our purposes). They can be defined by the following set of equations

\displaystyle{\bm{r}}

\displaystyle\equiv p{\bm{n}}/(1+e\cos f)\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\cos\Omega\cos\phi-\cos\iota\sin\Omega\sin\phi\right){\bm{e}}_{X}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sin\Omega\cos\phi+\cos\iota\cos\Omega\sin\phi\right){\bm{e}}_{Y}

\displaystyle\quad+\sin\iota\sin\phi{\bm{e}}_{Z}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\equiv\partial{\bm{n}}/\partial\phi\,,\quad\hat{\bm{h}}={\bm{n}}\times{\bm{\lambda}}\,,

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\bm{r}}\times{\bm{v}}\equiv\sqrt{Gmp}\,\bm{\hat{h}}\,,

\displaystyle\dot{r}

\displaystyle\equiv(he/p)\sin f\,,

(9)

where
f\equiv\phi-\omega
is the
true anomaly
,
\phi
is the orbital phase measured from the ascending node, and

{\bm{e}}_{A}
are chosen reference basis vectors. From the given definitions, we see that
{\bm{v}}=\dot{r}{\bm{n}}+(h/r){\bm{\lambda}}
. To avoid the well-known problem of the singular behavior of
\omega
in the circular limit, we will use the alternative orbit elements:

\alpha\equiv e\cos{\omega},\quad\beta\equiv e\sin{\omega}\,,

(10)

which are regular when
e\rightarrow 0
.

We then compute the radial
\mathcal{R}\equiv\bm{n}\cdot\delta\bm{a}
, cross-track
\mathcal{S}\equiv\bm{\lambda}\cdot\delta\bm{a}
, and out-of-plane
\mathcal{W}\equiv\hat{\bm{h}}\cdot\delta\bm{a}
components of the perturbing acceleration. Because the perturbations lie within the orbital plane,
{\cal W}=0
.

The “Lagrange planetary equations” then describe the evolution of the orbital parameters in response to the perturbing accelerations:

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\phi}

\displaystyle=2\frac{r^{3}}{Gm}\,{\cal S}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha}{d\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{Gm}\left[{\cal R}\sin\phi+{\cal S}(\alpha+\cos\phi)\left(1+\frac{r}{p}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\frac{d\beta}{d\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{Gm}\left[-{\cal R}\cos\phi+{\cal S}(\beta+\sin\phi)\left(1+\frac{r}{p}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\frac{d\iota}{d\phi}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d\Omega}{d\phi}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(11)

together with the relationship between
\phi
and time,

\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\frac{h}{r^{2}}\,.

(12)

This simple relation follows from the fact that
\Omega
and
\iota
are constant.

II.3
Two-timescale Analysis

The Lagrange planetary equations are of the general form

\frac{dX_{\gamma}(\phi)}{d\phi}=\epsilon Q_{\gamma}(X_{\delta}(\phi),\phi)\,,

(13)

where
\gamma,\,\delta
label the orbit element, and
\epsilon
is a small parameter that characterizes the perturbation.
We anticipate that the solutions for the
X_{\gamma}
will have pieces that vary on a “short” orbital timescale, corresponding to the periodic functions of
\phi
, and pieces that vary on a long timescale characterized by a variable
\theta\equiv\epsilon\phi
. In a two-timescale analysis
Bender and Orszag 1978
;
Lincoln and Will 1990
;
Mora and Will 2004
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
;
Will and Maitra 2017
, one treats these two times formally as independent variables, and solves the ordinary differential equations as if they were partial differential equations for the two variables.
We write the derivative with respect to
\phi
as

\frac{d}{d\phi}\equiv\epsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\,,

(14)

and make an
ansatz
for the solution for
X_{\gamma}(\theta,\phi)
:

X_{\gamma}(\theta,\phi)\equiv\tilde{X}_{\gamma}(\theta)+\epsilon Y_{\gamma}(\tilde{X}_{\delta}(\theta),\phi)\,.

(15)

The split is defined by

\tilde{X}_{\gamma}(\theta)=\langle X_{\gamma}(\theta,\phi)\rangle\,,\quad\langle Y_{\gamma}(\tilde{X}_{\delta}(\theta),\phi)\rangle=0\,,

(16)

where the “average”
\langle\dots\rangle
is defined by

\langle A\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}A(\theta,\phi)d\phi\,,

(17)

holding
\theta
fixed.
For any function
A(\theta,\phi)
we define the “average-free” part as

{\cal AF}(A)\equiv A(\theta,\phi)-\langle A\rangle\,.

(18)

We now substitute Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) into (
13
), divide by the parameter
\epsilon
, and take the average and average-free parts of the resulting equation to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{X}_{\gamma}}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\langle Q_{\gamma}(\tilde{X}_{\delta}+\epsilon Y_{\delta},\phi)\rangle\,,

(19a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Y_{\gamma}}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle={\cal AF}\left(Q_{\gamma}(\tilde{X}_{\delta}+\epsilon Y_{\delta},\phi)\right)-\epsilon\frac{\partial Y_{\gamma}}{\partial\tilde{X}_{\delta}}\frac{d\tilde{X}_{\delta}}{d\theta}\,.

(19b)

These equations can then be iterated in a straightforward way. At zeroth order, Eq. (
19a
) yields
d\tilde{X}_{\gamma}/d\theta=\langle Q^{0}_{\gamma}\rangle
where
Q^{0}_{\gamma}\equiv Q_{\gamma}(\tilde{X}_{\delta},\phi)
, which is the conventional result whereby one averages the perturbation holding the orbit elements fixed. We define the expansion
Y_{\gamma}\equiv Y^{(0)}_{\gamma}+\epsilon Y^{(1)}_{\gamma}+\epsilon^{2}Y^{(2)}_{\gamma}+\dots
. We then integrate Eq. (
19b
) holding
\theta
fixed to obtain
Y^{0}_{\gamma}
. The iteration continues until one obtains all contributions to
d\tilde{X}_{\gamma}/d\theta
compatible with the order in
\epsilon
to which
Q_{\gamma}
is known. The final solution including periodic terms is given by Eq. (
15
), with the secular evolution of the
\tilde{X}_{\gamma}
given by solutions of Eqs. (
19a
). From these solutions one can reconstruct the instantaneous orbit using Eqs. (
9
).

Although we are interested in the effects of radiation reaction, we must include the conservative terms in the equations of motion. This is because, for example, a conservative 1PN contribution to
Y
substituted back into the 2.5PN contribution to
Q
will lead to a 3.5PN term, as will a 2.5PN contribution to
Y
substituted back into the 1PN contribution to
Q
. These must be added to the straight 3.5PN contribution to
Q
. When working to higher orders in the expansion parameter, these so-called “cross-term” effects cannot be neglected (see
Mora and Will 2004
;
Will and Maitra 2017
for examples in post-Newtonian theory, in Newtonian triple system dynamics
Luo et al. 2016
;
Will 2021
, and in triple system dynamics with PN corrections
Will 2014
;
Lim and Rodriguez 2020
).

Numerical evolutions and the final eccentricity

We now apply the analytic results obtained in the previous section to the long-term evolution of highly eccentric orbits. Note that the rescaling of

III.2
Accuracy of the PN approximation

We next investigate the accuracy of the PN approximation. There is no formal way to do this because the PN sequence is not known to be a convergent series (at best, it might be an asymptotic sequence), but one way to estimate the accuracy is to add terms at the next PN order, and to study their effects. We use the equations of evolution for test-body orbits taken from black hole perturbation theory obtained by Sago and Fujita (SF)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta x^{-9/2}}{87318000}\bigl(294262221896-621776393808e^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-658790352267e^{4}-277665065676e^{6}\bigr)\,.

III.3
A PN-corrected map for

Our goal is to obtain an analytic map for the eccentricity of a generic binary inspiral at a given value of

and

, that in the limit of large

and

it tend to the PM map, and finally that it differ from the numerical values by only a few percent, down to
x_{f}\sim 8
. After some experimentation, we arrived at Eq. (

e=g^{-1}\left[\frac{x}{x_{i}}\left(\frac{1+2/x}{1+2/x_{i}}\right)\left(\frac{1-4/x}{1-4/x_{i}}\right)^{12/19}g(e_{i})\right]\,.

(24)

In practice, of course, Eq. (

x=a(c^{2}/Gm)(1-e^{2})

III.4
Evolution time

In order to determine the probability of a given final eccentricity arising from astrophysically meaningful initial conditions, it is important also to know the time for the system to evolve from the initial state to the final state. That time can be found by numerically integrating the system of equations
dx/dt=(2\pi/P)dx/d\theta
and
de/dt=(2\pi/P)de/d\theta
, where
P
is the orbital period. Because the total time is strongly dominated by the non-relativistic part of the orbit, it suffices to use the Keplerian orbital period,
P=2\pi(Gm/c^{3})x^{3/2}(1-e^{2})^{-3/2}
. At the lowest, 2.5PN order of approximation, Eqs. (
21
) yield

\frac{dx}{dt}=-\frac{64}{5}\eta\frac{c^{3}}{Gm}x^{-3}(1-e^{2})^{3/2}\left(1+\frac{7}{8}e^{2}\right)\,.

(25)

In the circular limit (
e=0
), integrating this equation directly yields

T=\frac{5}{256\eta}\left(\frac{Gm}{c^{3}}\right)x_{i}^{4}\left(1-\frac{x_{f}^{4}}{x_{i}^{4}}\right)\,.

(26)

If the initial eccentricity is large, most of the time will be spent in the large
e
regime. From Eqs. (
21
), again at 2.5PN order, we can thus approximate
dx/de\approx(72/85)x/e
, and thus
e\approx e_{i}(x/x_{i})^{85/72}
. We then obtain

\frac{dz}{dt}\approx-24\eta\frac{c^{3}}{Gm}x_{i}^{-4}\frac{(1-e_{i}^{2}z^{85/36})^{3/2}}{z^{3}}\,,

(27)

where
z\equiv x/x_{i}
. Because of the very weak dependence of the time on
x_{f}
when
x_{f}\ll x_{i}
(see Eq. (
26
)), we can integrate this equation from
z=0
to
z=1
, with the result

T=\frac{1}{96\eta}\left(\frac{Gm}{c^{3}}\right)x_{i}^{4}G(e_{i})\,,

(28)

where

\displaystyle G(e_{i})

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z^{3}dz}{(1-e_{i}^{2}z^{85/36})^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=\,_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{3}{2},\frac{144}{85};\frac{229}{85};e_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{288/85}{\sqrt{1-e_{i}^{2}}}-5.9257+8.0919\sqrt{1-e_{i}^{2}}+\dots\,,

(29)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is the hypergeometric function, and the final expression is the expansion for
e_{i}\approx 1

Peters 1964
;
O’Leary et al. 2009
;
Will and Maitra 2017
. The singular behavior of

merely reflects the enormous amount of time bodies in eccentric orbits spend going to apocenter and back, with no relativistic consequences to speak of.

IV
Discussion and Conclusions

We have used post-Newtonian equations of motion containing radiation-reaction terms through 4.5PN order to analyse the late-time eccentricities of inspiraling binary systems of non-spinning compact bodies of arbitrary masses. We have found that, apart from the overall dependence of the inspiral time
T
on the symmetric mass ratio
\eta
, the final eccentricities are essentially independent of
\eta
. We found an analytic map for the final eccentricity in terms of the initial eccentricity and semi-latus rectum that generalizes the Peters-Mathews formula, and that agrees with the numerically generated values to a few percent. We also find that the Peters-Mathews formula produces consistently larger values, by as much as 60 percent, than those predicted by the full 4.5PN dynamics. These results may be useful for assessing the levels of orbital eccentricity that must be incorporated into gravitational-waveform templates, and for relating measured late-time eccentricities to the astrophysical origins of compact binary inspirals.

Here we note that, in the limit
e_{i}\ll 1
, Tanay et al.
Tanay et al. 2016
obtained a PN-corrected map by adding post-Newtonian corrections to the
inversion
of the PM map (
1
) expressed as a power series in
e_{i}

Yunes et al. 2009
.

4PN tail terms

In this Appendix, we derive the effects of the leading 4PN gravitational-wave “tail” term on the evolution of

The seven time derivatives of

\displaystyle\frac{dr_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle\frac{dv_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle\frac{d\dot{r}}{dt}

To find the secular evolution of the orbital parameters, we will need to integrate over the past history of the binary system, and the structure of that integral dictates that we work in terms of time. Instead of using the true anomaly

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle u=\tilde{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{6}{l_{i}e^{i}}\,,

\displaystyle u(t)=

\displaystyle\tilde{t}+e\sin\tilde{t}+\frac{1}{2}e^{2}\sin 2\tilde{t}-\frac{1}{8}e^{3}(\sin\tilde{t}-3\sin 3\tilde{t})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{6}e^{4}(\sin 2\tilde{t}-2\sin 4\tilde{t})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{384}e^{5}(2\sin\tilde{t}-81\sin 3\tilde{t}+125\sin 5\tilde{t})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\cos u-e}{1-e\cos u}\bm{e}_{X}+\frac{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}\sin u}{1-e\cos u}\bm{e}_{Y}\,,

\displaystyle\dot{r}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{Gm}{a}}\frac{e\sin u}{1-e\cos u}\,,

\displaystyle v^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{Gm}{a}\,\frac{1+e\cos u}{1-e\cos u}\,.

(37)

We then compute the radial
\mathcal{R}\equiv\bm{n}\cdot\bm{a}_{\rm Tail}
, and cross-track
\mathcal{S}\equiv\bm{\lambda}\cdot\bm{a}_{\rm Tail}
components of the perturbing acceleration (
\mathcal{W}=0
) and insert these into the Lagrange planetary equations expressed in terms of
t
and
u
:

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dt}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\frac{p^{3}}{Gm}}\frac{(1-e\cos u)}{1-e^{2}}\mathcal{S}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{de}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{p}{Gm}}\frac{1}{1-e\cos u}\bigl[(1-e^{2})^{1/2}\sin u\,\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(2\cos u-e(1+\cos^{2}u)\right)\mathcal{S}\bigr]\,.

(38)

Substituting Eq. (
36
) into the the full expressions for Eqs. (
38
) and being careful to expand to the appropriate order of
e
, we make the transformation
\tilde{t}\rightarrow\tilde{t}-\tilde{s}
in the evaluated seventh time derivative of
{{\cal I}^{\langle jk\rangle}}
. We average over one orbit with
\tilde{t}
as the variable of integration from
0
to
2\pi
. This gives us the secular evolution equations for
dp/dt
and
de/dt
. To carry out the remaining integral over
\tilde{s}
into the infinite past we make use of the well known results:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(py)\ln(y)dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{\ln(p)+\gamma}{p},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sin(py)\ln(y)dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{2p}\,,

(39)

where
\gamma
is the Euler number
(see Eqs. 4.441 in
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 1980
). We then rescale
p
using Eq. (
20
) and convert from
a
to
x
with
a=(Gm/c^{2})x/(1-e^{2})
. We convert from
d/dt
to
d/d\theta
with
dF/dt=(2\pi/P)dF/d\theta
where we only need to use the Keplerian expression for the orbital period
P=2\pi(Gm/c^{3})x^{3/2}(1-e^{2})^{-3/2}
. We arrive finally at the terms in Eqs. (
21
) that are of the order
x^{-4}
in
de/d\theta
and
x^{-3}
in
dx/d\theta
. Because the tail effects are already of 4PN order, we do not have to worry about “cross terms” in the two-timescale analysis.

Appendix C
5.5PN terms

Here we derive the 5.5PN contributions to
dx/dt
and
de/dt
shown in Eqs. (
23
). We combine expressions for
E
and
L
to 3PN order in harmonic coordinates explicitly given by Will and Maitra (WM) in Eqs. (3.24) in
Will and Maitra 2017
with expressions for the energy and angular momentum flux to the same 3PN order, calculated to
O(e^{6})
by Sago and Fujita
Sago and Fujita 2015
for a point particle orbiting a Kerr black hole using Boyer-Lindquist (BL) coordinates. For our purposes, we take the spin to be zero. The Sago-Fujita (SF) results are given in their Eqs. (32) and (33).

First we must translate from SF’s choice of Boyer-Lindquist orbital variables
(e_{\mathrm{BL}},\ p_{\mathrm{BL}})
to osculating orbit elements in harmonic coordinates
(e,\ p)
, consistent with our work. We do this by employing the method detailed in
Tucker and Will 2019
, in which one defines an “invariant” dimensionless semilatus rectum
x_{0}
and eccentricity
e_{0}
via the gauge invariant energy and angular momentum:

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\tilde{L}^{2},

\displaystyle e_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv 1+\tilde{L}^{2}\left(\frac{E^{2}}{c^{4}}-1\right)\,,

(40)

where we define
\tilde{L}\equiv cL/Gm
as the dimensionless angular momentum.

Using the 3PN expressions for
E
and
L
given in equations (A1) and (A2) of
Sago and Fujita 2015
within our Eq. (
40
), we express the variables
e_{\mathrm{BL}}
and
x_{\mathrm{BL}}
in terms of
e_{0}
and
x_{0}
in a PN expansion. These are, in turn, converted to osculating orbit elements
e
and
x
, utilizing Eqs. (3.24) in WM
Will and Maitra 2017
and our Eq. (
40
).

We substitute the orbital element conversion into the Sago-Fujita secular evolution equations to find their equations as a function of osculating orbit elements.

Next we find the secular evolution equations for
e
and
x
with

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{(dE/dt)(dL/de)-(dE/de)(dLdt)}{(dE/dx)(dL/de)-(dE/de)(dLdx)}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{de}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{(dE/dx)(dL/dt)-(dE/dt)(dLdx)}{(dE/dx)(dL/de)-(dE/de)(dLdx)}\,.

(41)

Finally we convert from
d/dt
to
d/d\theta
using
\theta=(2\pi/P)t
together with the Keplerian expression for the orbital period, arriving at the expressions given in Eqs. (
23

\eta=0
