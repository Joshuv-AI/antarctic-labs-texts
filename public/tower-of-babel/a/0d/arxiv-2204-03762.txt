Normalized additional velocity distribution: testing the radial profile of dark matter halos and MOND Journal: Physics of the Dark Universe
Davi C. Rodrigues, Alejandro Hernandez-Arboleda, Aneta Wojnar

Abstract
We propose a complementary and fast approach to study galaxy rotation curves directly from the sample data, instead of individual fits. With this approach, some relevant tests can be done analytically. It is based on a dimensionless difference between the observational rotation curve and the expected one from the baryonic matter ( δ ​ V 2 \delta V^{2} ) as a function of the normalized radius r n {r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}} (i.e., for all galaxies, 0 < r n < 1 0<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<1 ). Using 153 galaxies from the SPARC galaxy sample, we find the observational distribution of δ ​ V 2 \delta V^{2} . Considering radii with 0.2 < r n < 0.9 0.2<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.9 , most of the SPARC data are close to the curve δ ​ V 2 = r n 0.42 \delta V^{2}={r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{0.42} , and about 95 % 95\% of the SPARC data is between the curves δ ​ V 2 = r n 2.2 \delta V^{2}={r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{2.2} and δ ​ V 2 = 2 ​ r n 0.38 − r n 1.9 \delta V^{2}=2{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{0.38}-{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{1.9} . We consider three well known dark matter halo models (NFW, Burkert and DC14), a simple dark matter rotation curve profile for the purpose of model comparison (Arctan α ) and one modified gravity model without dark matter (MOND). By comparing the observational data distribution with the model-inferred data, we confirm that the NFW halo lacks the necessary diversity to reproduce several observed rotation curves, while Burkert and DC14 models have better concordance with observational data. The lowest δ ​ V 2 \delta V^{2} curves that can be found from NFW are linear on the normalized radius (i.e., δ ​ V NFW 2 = r n \delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}={r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}} ), while for Burkert δ ​ V Bur 2 = r n 2 \delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Bur}}={r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{2} (this result is independent of the halo density parameter, i.e., ρ c \rho_{\rm c} or ρ s \rho_{\rm s} ). MOND only covers the very central region of the observed distribution, hence it also lacks the necessary diversity, which in turn is related to larger χ 2 \chi^{2} values. In a second paper, the method will be extended to consider other classes of modified gravity models.

Introduction

Galaxies constitute one important and unique piece of information about dark matter-related phenomena. The standard approach, considering the agreement between several independent probes
(
Bergström 2000
;
Courteau et al. 2014
;
Bertone and Hooper 2018
)
, is to assume dark matter existence at cosmological scales and that the cosmological evolution leads to the dark matter distribution found in galaxies. At galactic scales, a relevant complication in this picture is the baryonic feedback, which in general has a relevant impact on the dark matter distribution.

There are currently different dark matter halo profiles and also some modified gravity models that are candidates to explain dark matter-like phenomena in galaxies. Here we consider four well-known possibilities, namely:
i
) the Navarro-Frenk-While (NFW)
(
Navarro et al. 1997
)
profile, which is inferred from N-body dark matter simulations (thus without baryonic feedback);
ii
) the phenomenological Burkert profile
(
Burkert 1995
)
, which has a core and it has good performance on fitting several galaxies
(
Salucci and Burkert 2000
;
de Blok et al. 2001
;
Rodrigues et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
;
iii
) the
Di Cintio et al. 2014
halo (DC14), which considers hydrodynamical simulations with baryonic feedback
(
Katz et al. 2017
, see also)
; and
iv
) Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND)
(
Milgrom 1983
;
Famaey and McGaugh 2012
)
which is a well-known and simple modified gravity model that is capable of reproducing relevant trends observed in galaxy rotation curves
(
McGaugh et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. For comparison purposes, we also consider a less cited phenomenological possibility based on an
\arctan
function, here named Arctan
α
, which will be introduced latter.

In this work we develop and apply a method that tests a given model with a sample of galaxies, instead of doing galaxy by galaxy fits. The method aims to test both dark matter and modified gravity models. Instead of trying to test all the properties simultaneously, we focus on the radial dependence of the dark matter contribution to the rotation curve, or the modified gravity one.

First we introduce the squared additional velocity, denoted by
\Delta V^{2}
, which is the contribution to the circular velocity that is not due to the expected Newtonian baryonic contribution.
1
1

1

In the context of Newtonian gravity with dark matter, this quantity was named the rotation velocity attributed to dark matter (
V_{h}
)
(
McGaugh et al. 2007
)
.
We use the squared velocity since in this form it can be directly added to other Newtonian contributions.
2
2

2

For instance, for a galaxy with stars, atomic gas and dark matter, whose Newtonian potentials are respectively denoted by
\Phi_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm*}}
,
\Phi_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm gas}}
and
\Phi_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm dm}}
, the total Newtonian potential is
\Phi=\Phi_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm*}}+\Phi_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm gas}}+\Phi_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm dm}}
. Hence, the total circular velocity is
V^{2}=V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm*}}+V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm gas}}+V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm dm}}
. For this example, which is based on Newtonian gravity,
\Delta V^{2}=V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm dm}}
.

In order to study the radial dependence of
\Delta V^{2}
, we introduce the normalized additional velocity (NAV), denoted by
\delta V^{2}
. The NAV is the core quantity here studied. It commonly reduces the number of model’s free parameters by one and allows for additional analytical estimates before doing numerical rotation curves (RCs) evaluations. Moreover, instead of studying each galaxy individually to understand if a model is viable or not, it is possible to compare the observational with the model NAV distributions, as we show here.

This work is organized as follows: in the next section we present the method, explaining how to compute
\delta V^{2}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
from the observational data and from a model. Section
3
is devoted to presenting the considered models and to apply the proposed method. Section
4
summarizes the model results and compare them. In Sec.
5
, we review and discuss our results.
A
is devoted to a brief review on KDEs and the Silverman bandwidth. In
B
we present our results for the the individual fits of galaxies, considering six different models. Additional details on the computations can be found in the
NAVanalysis
code
(
Rodrigues and Hernandez-Arboleda 2022
)
.
C
comments on the NFW halo results in the presence of mass-concentration constraints (
M_{200}-c
).

2
The method

2.1
Outline

The method deals with dark matter or modified gravity models. The dark matter case: for a given dark matter model and a data sample of galaxy rotation curves, the method compares the difference between the observed rotation curve (RC) and the baryonic contribution with the dark matter rotation curve contribution. However, instead of doing this galaxy by galaxy, where the individual uncertainties are more relevant, one considers the full set of data points of the complete sample of galaxies. Moreover, to simplify the analysis and to focus on a relevant aspect of dark matter halos, this comparison is done under a normalization. For the modified gravity case: the method essence is the same, but the “baryonic contribution” is understood as the Newtonian baryonic contribution alone, while the non-Newtonian contribution is in place of the dark matter contribution.

The observational additional (squared) velocity,
\Delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}
, is defined as

\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}\equiv V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}-V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}\,.

(1)

and the model additional (squared) velocity,
\Delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}
,

\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}\equiv V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}-V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}\,.

(2)

In the above,
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}
is the the observational RC,
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}
is the model RC and
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}
is the expected baryonic RC (which is inferred from the stars and the HI regions radiation). To be more explicit,

V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}={V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm*d}}}+{V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm*b}}}+{V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm gas}}}\,,

(3)

where
{V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm*d}}}
,
{V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm*b}}}
and
{V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm gas}}}
are the squared circular velocity of the following components respectively: stellar disk, stellar bulge and the gas (atomic hydrogen and helium). The mass-to-light ratios are included in these quantities.

These squared velocities are not truly the square of a given physical velocity (this is a common convention), namely

V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm x}}(r)\equiv a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm x}}(r)\,r\,,

(4)

that is, the component x contribution is the centripetal acceleration due to the component x times the (cylindrical) radial position
r
. Hence, since the matter distribution is not spherical,
V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm x}}
can be either positive or negative. A negative
V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm x}}
means that the component
x
, in a given radius, reduces the total centripetal acceleration. It is common to use this convention. This detail is relevant depending on the particular model and the particular galaxy considered.

, for

. This profile was found from 60 galaxies and under assumptions on the stellar mass-to-light ratios that are different from those adopted in the SPARC data. Nonetheless, for the 153 SPARC galaxies we find results that are very similar, namely

(5)

Data with

kpc are neglected, since it is a region with larger dispersion. The first case considers a simpler model, in which
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
is taken as linear on
r
, like the one adopted by
McGaugh et al. 2007
, while the latter (
6
) considers a free parameter for the
r
power. In the end, all the results are similar.

Figure
1
shows the considered data and plots the last curve. This figure suggests that
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
commonly increases with the radius. However, we remark three issues about this plot: i) different radial regions can have large differences on the number of data points, ii) the magnitude of
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
from two different galaxies may be very different, even though their radial dependence may be similar, and iii) if the magnitude of
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
correlates with the radial range probed by the galaxies, the trend seen in Fig.
1
will not match, even approximately, the trend in the individual galaxies. Hence, it is not possible to infer that good models should satisfy
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}\propto r
, even on average. Indeed, a better intermediate radius approximation for the average
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
of each galaxy, inside their radial range with observational data, would be
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}\propto\sqrt{r}
, as it will be shown in Sec.
2.2
.

The method considered in this paper is based on the normalized additional velocity (NAV) (
\delta V^{2}
) as a function of the normalized radius (
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}
). Namely,

\delta V^{2}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})\equiv\frac{\Delta V^{2}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\,r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}})}{\Delta V^{2}(r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}})}\,,

(7)

where
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}
is the largest radial value with RC data and
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\equiv r/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}
is the normalized galaxy radial coordinate, hence
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\in[0,1]
.

One can consider other model independent radial values to normalize
\Delta V^{2}
(e.g., the stellar disk scale length or the effective galaxy radius
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm eff}}
), but such options would lead to an intermediate radial point where the
\delta V^{2}
curves from different galaxies intersect. Close to this intersection point there is no relevant data to extract since all the curves pass by a such point by the definition. With
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}
, this intersection happens at the largest radial value, which is a good option to study the intermediate radial behaviour.

Here we will compare the distributions of
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
, the observational NAV, and
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
, the model-inferred NAV.

The observational NAV plane, as displayed in Fig.
2
, either including or excluding the galaxies with a stellar bulge, clearly displays a correlation. This correlation is a direct consequence of the need for dark matter or modified gravity. To clarify this point, first we shall consider the case of Newtonian gravity without dark matter. For such case,
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}=V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}
and therefore
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}=0
. Observationally, one would expect
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}\sim 0
in this context, hence it should have random oscillations about zero. Therefore, since
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}(r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}})
would be a random number close to zero, the expected
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
should fill out the observational NAV plane (including negative and positive contributions randomly). The latter is clearly not the case for the observational NAV plane inferred from the SPARC data (Fig.
2
), hence the displayed correlation demonstrates the need for dark matter or modified gravity.

To proceed with the method, such that it is possible to compare distributions, we need to find a smoothed distribution from the data points. A standard procedure is by using a kernel density estimator (KDE). Whenever we use a KDE in this work, we use a simple and well known one, namely a Gaussian kernel with the Silverman rule for the bandwidths
(
Silverman 1998
;
Scott 2014
)
. Appendix
A
covers further KDE details.

Once the KDE for the observational NAV plane is known, one can find the 1
\sigma
and 2
\sigma
highest density regions (HDRs). They are regions where most of the
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
data points can be found. We stress that they are neither credible nor confidence regions. They show the HDRs contours associated with the probabilities of
68\%
or
95\%
respectively. These regions are displayed in Fig.
2
and tabulated in Table
1
.

Good models are expected to yield
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
distributions similar to
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
. A perfect covering of
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
is not a necessity because of the presence of observational and KDE errors. About the latter ones, close to
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}=0
and
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}=1
there are errors due to the KDE resolution. We do not consider data closer to the extrema than the inferred bandwidth, in particular we will not consider data in the ranges
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.1
and
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}>0.9
. Observational errors on
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
close to
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\sim 1
are not relevant, since, by construction,
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\sim 1)\sim 1
. On the other hand, for
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\sim 0
,
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
is particularly sensitive to observational errors, since in this region
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}\lesssim V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}
. By comparing the left and right plots from Fig.
2
, which differ by the galaxies with a relevant bulge, the major differences are for
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.2
. Due to the observational and KDE errors, we fix the
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}
range of especial concern to be
0.2<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.9
.

It is relevant to know simple analytical (polynomial) models that can approximately describe the
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
distribution, while also respecting the constraints of passing by the points
(0,0)
and
(1,1)
(as realistic models are expected to satisfy). This can be achieved with models of the type
\delta V^{2}={r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{a}
and
\delta V^{2}=2{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{b}-{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{c}
, where
a,b
and
c
are constants. The first case woks for monotonic curves, while the latter for non-monotonic curves. Within the region
0.2<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.9

and 2

\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}

0.2<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.9
, better than this polynomial case, which was built exclusively for this purpose.

2.3
The
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
distribution

Commonly, one of the parameters that
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
depends on is a global multiplicative constant (e.g.,
\rho_{s}
for the NFW profile,
Navarro et al. 1997
). Such a parameter, when present, adjusts the magnitude of the dark matter (or modified gravity) contribution. From its definition,
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
cannot depend on such parameter. This is a relevant feature about using
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
: it commonly depends on one less parameter than the original model.

As a first step for the method application, an analytical expression of
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
, before any numerical evaluation, can be sufficient as a first comparison with the observational data. This is illustrated in Sec.
3
.

As a second fast step, one can perform a quick numerical evaluation of the extreme parameter cases in order to verify if the region with observational data can be covered by the model, at least within certain parameter regions. This is specially easy to be done for two-parameter models, which typically become one-parameter models within the
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
analysis.

A third test involves determining the best model curves that mimic the 1
\sigma
and 2
\sigma
HDRs (as in Fig.
2
). The central idea here is to perform a couple of simple fits directly to the sample data, in place of a hundred(s) of galaxy fits. One can test if the model can lead to reasonable approximations to such regions, and, if possible, which parameter values are the most commonly necessary to cover the observational data.

A general procedure to perform the fits described above is by using the following integral,

I^{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm($\pm$k)}}}(p_{i})=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}(p_{i},{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})-{\lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm($\pm$ k)}}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})\right)^{2}\,\omega({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})\,d{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\,,

(9)

where
p_{i}
refers to all the model parameters that
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
depends on,
{\lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm($\pm$k)}}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
refers to the curve that delimits the k
\sigma
region of
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
, with
+k
corresponding to the upper limit, and
-k
with the lower limit. The
{\lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm($\pm$1)}}}
and
{\lambda_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm($\pm$2)}}}
functions in tabular form are provided in Table
1
.
\omega({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
is a weight function. A simple and relevant case, which we adopt here, is
\omega({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})=\Theta({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}-0.2)\Theta(0.9-{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
, where
\Theta
is the Heaviside theta function. Hence, for the latter case, only the data in the range
0.2<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.9
is relevant for the fit.

For some models, the disagreement between
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
and
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
may be large enough to clearly dismiss them as realistic. As an extreme example, this is the case of Newtonian gravity without dark matter, whose NAV plane would be randomly filled out. From the observational data, it is also easy to dismiss some regions of the radial parameter of either NFW of the Burkert profiles: too low values will generate curves that go much beyond the observational region. A method to quantify the similarity between
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
and
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
is detailed in Sec.
2.4
.

For modified gravity approaches,
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
typically depends on the 3D baryonic matter distribution of each galaxy, not only the baryonic circular velocities contributions in the plane. It also requires, in general, solving modified Poisson equations. However, there is a relevant modified gravity model whose
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
can be computed promptly and quickly with the available data: this is the case of MOND in its original form
(
Milgrom 1983
)
. This is so since
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}
can be written as a function of the baryonic circular velocity Newtonian contribution (
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}
).

2.4
The NAV efficiency

It is convenient to introduce a numerical comparison between the
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}}
and
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
distributions. No single number can capture all the data from the 2D plots, but a convenient one, since we are focusing on the 1
\sigma
and 2
\sigma
curves, is based on the areas related to these quantities.

First we introduce three areas:

1.

A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}(n)
is the area of the observational NAV region at
n\sigma
level.

2.

A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod$\cap$obs}}(n)
is the area of the intersection between the model and observational NAV regions at
n\sigma
level. Hence, a perfect model is expected to satisfy
A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod $\cap$ obs}}(n)=A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}(n)
.

3.

A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod$\setminus$obs}}(n)
is the area of the region difference between the model and the observational NAV regions, both at
n\sigma
level. A perfect model should yield
A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod$\setminus$obs}}(n)=0
.

As a first proposal for an efficiency coefficient one may consider
A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod$\cap$obs}}/A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}
. For this case, a perfect model would have an efficiency of 1, while the lowest possible efficiency would be zero, which would correspond to the case in which there is no intersection between the observational and the model NAV regions. This is interesting, but there should be a penalization for models that predict regions that are outside the observational region.

Considering the above, we define the model efficiency at
n\sigma
level as

E_{n}\equiv\frac{A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod$\cap$obs}}(n)-A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod$\setminus$obs}}(n)}{A_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm obs}}(n)}\,.

(10)

Hence, for a given
n
, the highest efficiency value is
E_{n}=1
, and
E_{n}
can be arbitrarily negative. Any reasonable model should have positive values for
E_{n}
. Models with negative values are those whose predicted area that agrees with the observational data is smaller than the predicted area that is outside the observational region (at the same
n\sigma
level).

E_{n}
provides a 1D comparison between the model and observational NAV distributions, which is a simplification with respect to the original 2D data. Since we focus on the
1\sigma
and
2\sigma
HDRs, the above model efficiency can be conveniently summarized in a single number as follows, the mean efficiency,

E_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm M}}\equiv\frac{E_{1}+E_{2}}{2}\,.

(11)

This is a number that we will use to compare the models. To avoid the NAV regions where the observational data are less robust, all the areas are computed in the interval
0.2<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.9
.

2.5
Individual fits

Here we compare the NAV results with the standard approach of fitting individually each one of the galaxies. All the individual fits consider the 153 SPARC galaxies. In the NAV analysis the stellar mass-to-light ratios are kept fixed at their central values. For the individual fits, we consider Gaussian priors on
{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm d}}}\equiv\log_{10}{Y_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm d}}}
and
{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm b}}}\equiv\log_{10}{Y_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm b}}}
centered on
\log_{10}0.5
and
\log_{10}0.6
respectively, with a variance of
0.1^{2}

(
Meidt et al. 2014
;
McGaugh and Schombert 2014
)
. This freedom on the variation of the mass-to-light ratios, which is commonly used within fits of individual galaxies, has in general relevant impact on the individual galaxies, but the overall sample distribution should not be sensitive to this (and we confirm this here). The NAV method bypasses some details that are relevant for individual galaxies, but are not relevant for the sample results.

From the observational data for each galaxy, a
\chi^{2}
quantity is defined for a given model with parameters
p_{a}
as

\chi^{2}(p_{a})=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{V(p_{a},r_{i})-V_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}\,.

(12)

In the above,
V_{i}
is the observational circular velocity, with uncertainty
\sigma_{i}
, and at the radius
r_{i}
(as provided by SPARC).
V(p_{a},r_{i})
is the model circular velocity at
r_{i}
.

Since the priors on
{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm b}}}
and
{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm d}}}
are Gaussian, it is possible to implement them by using an effective
\chi^{2}
, which is defined as follows

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm eff}}

\displaystyle(p_{a},{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm d}}},{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm b}}})\equiv

(13)

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p_{a})+\left(\frac{{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm d}}}-\log_{10}0.5}{0.1}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{{\Upsilon_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm b}}}-\log_{10}0.6}{0.1}\right)^{2}\,.

For galaxies without a bulge, the last term should not be considered.

The best fit values are here computed from the minimization of
\chi^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm eff}}
. The minimization of
\chi^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm eff}}
leads to the maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) estimation
(
Gregory 2010
, e.g.,)
. See also the supplementary information section of
(
Rodrigues et al. 2018a
)
.

Since some galaxies display multiple local minima, we performed several minimizations for each galaxy (at least eight), with different starting points and minimization meta-parameters. They were performed using the code
MAGMA
5
5

5

github.com/davi-rodrigues/MAGMA
.

(
Rodrigues et al. 2018a
)
, whose implementation is based on the differential evolution optimization algorithm
(
Price et al. 2006
)
. To find a best fit, i.e. a global minimum, global optimization techniques, as the differential evolution method, are more efficient than MCMC-based procedures, this since that is the single purpose of the former.

The results of the individual fits can be found in
B
.

3.2
NFW

3.2.1
Model definition

The Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) dark matter profile was derived from N-body simulations for a dark matter-only universe
(
Navarro et al. 1997
;
Mo et al. 2010
)
. It has achieved great success in several areas, it is commonly considered to be the standard dark matter halo within several astrophysical applications. However, since it does not include the influence of baryons, it is not unexpected that systematical deviations can be found in some systems, including galaxies
(
de Blok 2010
;
Governato et al. 2012
;
Di Cintio et al. 2014
;
Oman et al. 2015
;
Del Popolo and Le Delliou 2017
;
Dutton et al. 2019
;
Frosst et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. Baryonic physics should be able to provide certain degree of diversity to dark matter halos that is beyond the diversity that can be found from pure dark matter simulations. This includes the cusp/core controversy and goes beyond it
(
Oman et al. 2015
)
.

The NFW profile is a two-parameter cuspy dark matter halo whose density profile is given by

\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}}{r/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}(1+r/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}})^{2}}\,.

(23)

In the above,
r
is the spherical radial coordinate, while
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
and
\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
are constants that can change from galaxy to galaxy.

The internal mass of this profile is given by

M_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}(r)=4\pi r^{3}\rho_{s}\left[\ln\left(\frac{r+r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}}\right)-\frac{r}{r+r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}}\right]\,.

(24)

3.2.2
NAV analysis

The additional velocity is the dark matter contribution, hence

\Delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}(r)=G\frac{M_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}(r)}{r}\,.

(25)

Using the
M_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}
expression,

\displaystyle\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}(r_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}(r_{n}\,r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}})}{\Delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}(r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}}\frac{\frac{1}{1+r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}/{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}}-\ln({1+{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}})}{\frac{1}{1+r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}}-\ln({1+1/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}})}\,.

(26)

For a given
0<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<1
, one can compute the asymptotic limits,

\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}=\begin{cases}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{-1}&\mbox{, for small $r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}$.}\\[8.5359pt]
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}&\mbox{, for large $r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}$.}\end{cases}

(27)

The above result for small
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
does not contain the tight bound that the Arctan model has for small
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm tn}}
and for any
\alpha
. On the other hand, the lower bound in the NAV plane, given by
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}

We proceed now to fit the 1

3.3.1

, e.g.,)
. Its density profile is given by

\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Bur}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}}{(1+r/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}})(1+r^{2}/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}^{2})}\,.

(31)

In the above,
r
is the spherical radial coordinate, while
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}
and
\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}
are constants that can change from galaxy to galaxy.

The internal mass of the Burkert profile reads,

\displaystyle M_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Bur}}(r)

\displaystyle=4\pi\int_{0}^{r}\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Bur}}(r)r^{2}\,dr

\displaystyle=2\pi\,\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}^{3}\,\xi\left(\frac{r}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}}\right)\,,

(32)

where

\xi(x)\equiv\ln\left((1+x)\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\right)-\tan^{-1}(x)\,.

(33)

3.3.2
NAV analysis

It is convenient to introduce the normalized core radius as

r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}\equiv\frac{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}}\,.

(34)

Using that, for a spherical mass distribution,
V^{2}(r)=GM(r)/r
, we can now compute
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
and
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
as

\displaystyle\Delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Bur}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})

\displaystyle=2\pi\frac{G\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}^{3}}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}}\frac{1}{{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}}\xi\left(\frac{{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}}\right),

(35)

\displaystyle\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Bur}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}}\frac{\xi({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}})}{\xi(1/r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}})}.

(36)

For given
0<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<1
, one can compute the asymptotic limits,

\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Bur}}=\begin{cases}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{-1}&\mbox{, for small $r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}$.}\\[8.5359pt]
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}^{2}&\mbox{, for large $r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}$.}\end{cases}

(37)

This model has the same asymptotic for small
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}
as the NFW model, but for large
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}
it behaves like the arctan model with
\alpha=1
. Hence, it is expect that its NAV efficiency is larger than the previous models, and this will be the case.

We fit the 1
\sigma
and 2
\sigma
regions using the integral (
9
) in the interval
0.2<{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0.9
. The results are

\displaystyle 1\sigma:\;0.20<r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}<0.58\,,

\displaystyle 2\sigma:\;0.12<r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm cn}}<35\,.

(38)

These regions are plotted in Fig.
5
.

The efficiency for the Burkert model reads

E_{1}=0.74\,\mbox{ and }E_{2}=0.86\,\mbox{, hence }E_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm M}}=0.80\,.

(39)

This is the highest
E_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm M}}
value for the models considered up to this point, and very close to the
E_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm M}}
value of the polynomial description in Fig.
3
.

3.4
Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND)

3.4.1
Model definition

MOND is a well-known and simple modified gravity model that, contrary to several other proposals, has achieved certain success in the context of galaxy rotation curves
(
Milgrom 1983
;
Famaey and McGaugh 2012
)
. Following the original MOND formulation, as well the more recent motivation from the Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR)
(
McGaugh et al. 2016
)
, this model imposes that the observational acceleration can be expressed as a function of the Newtonian acceleration,

\mathbf{a}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm N}}=\mu\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)\mathbf{a}\,,

(40)

where
a_{0}
is a constant,
\mu
is called the interpolation function,
\mathbf{a}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm N}}
is the Newtonian acceleration inferred from baryonic matter and
\mathbf{a}
is the observational one. Within axis-symmetric disk galaxies, the acceleration vectors
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{a}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm N}}
are along the radial direction in the plane of the disk (
z=0
), hence
\mathbf{a}=-\frac{V^{2}}{r}\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
and
a=|\mathbf{a}|=\frac{V^{2}}{r}
.

The interpolation function
\mu
must satisfy
\mu(x)=1
for
x\gg 1
and
\mu(x)=x
for
x\ll 1
. For
x\sim 1
, the interpolation function behaviour should be fixed from the observational data. The RAR suggests the following relation between
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{a}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm N}}

(
McGaugh et al. 2016
)
,

\mathbf{a}=\frac{\mathbf{a}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm N}}}{1-e^{-\sqrt{a_{\rm N}/a_{0}}}}\,.

(41)

The same data lead to
a_{0}=1.2\times 10^{-10}
m/s
2
as the best fit for
a_{0}
(considering all the galaxies together). Another possible value for
a_{0}
, from the same galaxies and the same function (
41
) was derived by
Rodrigues et al. 2018a
;
Marra et al. 2020
, where the best value for
a_{0}
is selected as the one that minimizes the tensions between the individual galaxies, considering the observational uncertainties, leading to lower value,
a_{0}=9.6\times 10^{-11}
m/s
2
, but still in accordance with
a_{0}\sim 10^{-10}
m/s
2
.

MOND is here considered since it is well-known, simple and since it provides interesting results for the NAV analysis. Some of us have shown that the observational uncertainties are not compatible with the assumption that there is a common
a_{0}
value for all the galaxies,
(
Rodrigues et al. 2018a
;
Marra et al. 2020
)
. The issue was found with a high significance, always higher than
5\sigma
. These results were further discussed by
McGaugh et al. 2018
;
Kroupa et al. 2018
;
Rodrigues et al. 2018b
;
Cameron et al. 2020
;
Rodrigues et al. 2020
;
Rodrigues and Marra 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
, see also
Chang and Zhou 2019
;
Stone and Courteau 2019
;
Ren et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Eriksen et al. 2021
. This is a problem for MOND [the original MOND version, which is based on eq. (
40
)] as a correct theory for galaxies
(
Navarro et al. 2017
;
Stone and Courteau 2019
;
Dutton et al. 2019
, see also)
.

The NAV analysis, as here presented, is not concerned with the basis of MOND, but it stresses a peculiar trend in MOND’s rotation curves: its lack of diversity
(see also
Ren et al. 2019
;
Kaplinghat et al. 2020
, for similar conclusions from different methods)
.

3.4.2
NAV analysis

From eq. (
41
) with
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm N}}=a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}=V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}/r
, the
\Delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
MOND expression reads

\Delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}=V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}-V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}=\frac{V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}}{e^{\sqrt{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}/a_{0}}}-1}\,,

(42)

hence

\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})=\frac{V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})}{V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}(1)}\frac{e^{\sqrt{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}(1)/a_{0}}}-1}{e^{\sqrt{a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})/a_{0}}}-1}\,.

(43)

Since MOND is nonlinear on the constant
a_{0}
, this constant is still present in
\delta V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
.

The Newtonian limit of MOND is found in the limit
a_{0}\to 0
. Assuming that
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})>0
, then, for a given
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{a_{0}\to 0}\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})=\begin{cases}0\,,&\mbox{if }a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}(1)<a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})\\[2.84544pt]
\infty\,,&\mbox{if }a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}(1)>a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})\end{cases}\,.

(44)

Hence, the Newtonian limit of MOND leads to
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}=0
for either all or most of the
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}
values. We recall that, in general,
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm mod}}}
is ill defined for Newtonian gravity without dark matter. The limit of a modified gravity theory towards Newtonian gravity can display different features depending on the considered theory.

On the other hand, the limit
a_{0}\to\infty
implies (assuming
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}>0
)

\lim_{a_{0}\to\infty}\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})=\sqrt{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}\frac{V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})}{V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}(1)}\,.

(45)

This type of regime of large
a_{0}
(or small
a_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm N}}
) is commonly called the “deep MOND regime”. For large radii, most of the galaxies are expected to be in this regime, also
V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}(1)\sim V_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}(1)
, and hence
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})\sim\sqrt{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}
. It should be noted that this behaviour is close to the best-fit curve found from the polynomial approach (
8
). Hence, MOND should be good to cover the central region of the NAV plane. On the other hand, there is no independent mechanism to adjust the data distribution, the 2
\sigma
region in particular. Indeed, as it will be verified numerically, MOND will display a lack of diversity.

Contrary to the previous dark matter cases, the
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
curves directly depend on
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
. Hence, to consider the 1
\sigma
and
2\sigma
HDR’s for MOND, we use eq. (
43
) to draw the
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm MOND}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
curves for each one of the 153
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}})
curves. These data are provided by SPARC and the resulting curves are plotted in Fig.
6
. After that, these curves are randomly discretized, to smooth out the data points distribution, and the 1
\sigma
and 2
\sigma
HDR’s are computed. These regions are also plotted in the central plot of Fig.
6
. Besides this plot, we also show the consequences of considering either very large or very small
a_{0}
values.

3.5
DC14

3.5.1
Model definition

The
Di Cintio et al. 2014
model (DC14) extends the NFW halo by considering baryonic feedback. The DC14 halo profile depends on the stellar mass of the galaxy (
M_{*}
), more specifically, on the quantity
X=\log_{10}M_{*}/M_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm halo}}
, where
M_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm halo}}
is the dark matter halo mass.

\displaystyle\rho_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm DC14}}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}}{\frac{r^{\gamma}}{r_{s}^{\gamma}}\left(1+\frac{r^{\alpha}}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}^{\alpha}}\right)^{(\beta-\gamma)/\alpha}}\,,

\displaystyle\alpha=2.94\,-\log_{10}\left(\left(10^{X+2.33}\right)^{2.29}+\frac{1}{\left(10^{X+2.33}\right)^{1.08}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\beta=0.26X^{2}+1.34X+4.23\,,

(47)

\displaystyle\gamma=\log_{10}\left(10^{X+2.56}+\frac{1}{\left(10^{X+2.56}\right)^{0.68}}\right)-0.06\,.

The parameters above were derived for
-4.1<X<-1.3
. The case
X=-4.1
leads to the NFW halo (within a good approximation).

From its definition above, each
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm DC14}}
curve depends on
X
and
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
.
X
can be estimated for each galaxy from the stellar to halo mass relation
(
Moster et al. 2013
)
. We do not provide here individual fits for this model, but they were considered, using the SPARC data, by
Katz et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
. Besides other improvements, it was found that the overall quality of the fits is significantly better than the NFW profile and quite similar to the Burkert profile results (considering their CDF plots). Here we explore what kind of improvements DC14 has over NFW in the NAV plane, and to which extent it is close to the Burkert profile.

3.5.2
NAV analysis

From eq. (
47
), one can define
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm DC14}}({r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}},r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}},X)
. As it was shown in Sec.
3.2
, two relevant problems that the NFW halo has are:
i
) the lower limit in the NAV plane of the NFW halo is given by the curve
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}={r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}
, which is too high.
ii
) To achieve this lower limit it is necessary to use very large
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
values (consequently, very low values for the concentration
c
are found from real galaxies data, being at odds with the simulation results).

The two issues above can be directly shown to be absent from the DC14 halo using the NAV analysis. In Fig.

5
Conclusions and discussions

We developed a method, based on the normalized additional velocity (NAV) (
7
), to study galaxy RCs directly from a sample of galaxies. This approach is inspired by an analysis of
Rodrigues et al. 2010
;
Rodrigues et al. 2012
, and in part it is a normalized version of the approach of
McGaugh et al. 2007
. With respect to the latter, we focus on the radial dependence of the expected dark matter contribution, or, in the case of modified gravity, in the non-Newtonian contribution. This is done by eliminating the amplitude dependence through a normalization. The method was not developed to be a complete data assessment, but to provide an efficient sample comparison of a particular and important aspect of galaxy rotation curves (RCs): their shape. Among its applications that are here illustrated, this method can be used to eliminate model parameter regions, to find the most probable parameter regions, to uncover trends that are not simple to be found from standard individual fits and to compare models.

Considering the SPARC galaxy sample
(
Lelli et al. 2016
)
, the essential observational data to be used for the NAV evaluation is provided in tabular data in Table
1
, see also Fig.
2
. For some models, the method can be relevant even analytically. Also, the fact that this approach commonly eliminates one model parameter should not be underestimated, since this simplifies the analysis and allows for data-based inferences on the model parameters that are independent from the eliminated one.

After explaining that there should be a correlation between the NAV analysis and the average minimum
\chi^{2}
values, as discussed in Sec.
2
, we have confirmed such a correlation in Table
2
.

Five models were here considered, namely: three well-known dark matter profiles (NFW, Burkert and the DC14 profiles), a well-known modified gravity model (MOND) and a more phenomenological model here called Arctan
α
. The Arctan
α
model coincides with the arctan model studied by
Courteau 1997
for the special case
\alpha=1
. The main purpose of this Arctan
α
model was to test the NAV method, since this model for
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=1/2
is similar to the Burkert and NFW models respectively, considering the lower part of the NAV plane (as it can be seen in Figs.
4
,
5
).

We have shown that the simple Arctan
α=1
model is indeed quite efficient on providing good rotation curve fits. We have not explicitly evaluated the pseudo-isothermal model here
(
Begeman et al. 1991
, e.g.,)
, but its results are expected to be similar. As anticipated from the NAV analysis and confirmed from the individual fits, the Arctan
α=1/2
is significantly worse than the latter.

The method was also sufficient for showing that, for the SPARC galaxies, the Burkert halo performs on average significantly better than NFW and Arctan
α=1/2
. The NAV result is not related with the core-cusp issue in its stricter sense (i.e., at regions very close to the galaxy center), but to the necessary diversity of dark matter halos that NFW lacks
(
Oman et al. 2015
)
. The Burkert profile can reproduce such diversity, but without a proper physical context. The physical picture is improved with the DC14 halo, and the NAV analysis also show that this model is capable of providing a diversity of halo shapes similar to the Burkert profile, also in agreement with
Katz et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2020
. In Fig.
8
we show DC14 parameter regions that are more common, and those values that are needed to go beyond the NFW problems, considering the SPARC galaxies.

We have also pointed out that MOND in its original form can cover well the central region of the NAV plane [see Fig.
6
and also eq. (
45
)], but it has an issue qualitatively similar to the NFW halo: it lacks diversity, hence the individual fits lead to large
\chi^{2}
values. These results of ours are similar with the results from
Ren et al. 2019
;
Eriksen et al. 2021
, although the conclusions were found from a different method.

The method here presented can be extended to be applied in more general modified gravity models. The application to MOND (in its original form) is straightforward since, contrary to other modified gravity models, its rotation curve can be written as a function of
V^{2}_{{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm bar}}}
. For other modified gravity models, commonly one needs to know the three dimensional baryonic matter distribution
(see for instance
Green and Moffat 2019
, for further details)
. We understand that this method can bypass certain difficulties on the analysis of modified gravity. We plan to extend it in this direction as well.

Appendix A
KDE bandwidth selection

In order to find the distributions used in this work, it was necessary to convert data points into distributions. A well established procedure is to use a kernel density estimation (KDE). To use it, one needs to specify a kernel and a bandwidth. It is well known that a Gaussian kernel is both convenient and it has a high efficiency, with respect to the mean integrated square error (MISE)
(
Silverman 1998
;
Scott 2014
)
. The Gaussian kernel is always assumed here.

Although the bandwidth for several problems can be chosen by eye, for this work it is important to use a single algorithm to select it, thus avoiding any human bias on the bandwidth selection. Here we opted for the Silverman rule
(
Silverman 1998
)
, which is a simple and standard bandwidth selection rule. For consistency, every time a KDE is used here, we use the Gaussian kernel with the Silverman rule.

We start by considering the unidimensional problem. This brief review is mainly based on
Silverman 1998
. Let
\{x_{i}\}
be a set of
n
observations whose probability density is denoted by
f(x)
and it is
a priori
unknown. A KDE estimator
\hat{f}
for the distribution
f
is given by

\hat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{h}\right)\,,

(50)

where
h
is the bandwidth and
K
is the kernel. The latter satisfies

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K(x)dx=1\,.

(51)

This condition ensures the normalization of
\hat{f}
. Therefore, a KDE with a Gaussian kernel is a sum of normalized Gaussians, one Gaussian for each data point.

The bandwidth (
h
) selection is the trickiest part. This since the variation of
\hat{f}
decreases with
h
, while its bias increases with
h
. A common and natural approach to find the optimum
h
value starts by considering the MISE minimization, that is,

\displaystyle\mbox{MISE}(\hat{f})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\int\left(\hat{f}(x)-f(x)\right)^{2}dx\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\left(\langle\hat{f}(x)\rangle-f(x)\right)^{2}dx+\int\mbox{var}\hat{f}(x)dx

(52)

\displaystyle=\int\left[\left(\mbox{bias}\hat{f}(x)\right)^{2}+\mbox{var}\hat{f}(x)\right]dx\,,

where
\langle\;\;\rangle
denotes the expected value. There is no simple general solution to perform the above minimization, since the MISE depends on the unknown distribution
f(x)
.

For simplicity, let both
K
and
f(x)
be normal distributions. The kernel can be fixed as such without relevant loss of efficiency, as previously comment. For the second, this is a particular case, but it can be useful as a starting point. In this context, let
n
be the number of data points and
\mbox{var}f(x)=\sigma^{2}
, then the MISE minimization provides the optimum
h
as

h_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm opt}}=\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{1/5}\sigma n^{-1/5}\,.

(53)

Therefore, if
f(x)
can be assumed to be a normal distribution, from the data standard deviation (SD) one can estimate
\sigma
and use the above formula to find
h_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm opt}}
.

For distributions that are close to Gaussian, the rule (
53
) should be sufficient. The Silverman rule improves this setting by considering small changes that make it more robust against distributions that are not so close to Gaussian. In particular, the SD-based
h_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm opt}}
formula (
53
) tends to generate too large bandwidths for multimodal distributions. The latter case behaves better with bandwidths based on a more robust dispersion estimate, namely the interquartile range (IQR), which can be defined by the difference between the third and first quartile limits, thus the IQR determines a range that contains about 50% of the data. For Gaussian data, SD and
\sigma
are equal, but the IQR is larger than the SD by a factor about 1.34. On the other hand, SD works better than IQR for some distributions, like the binomial one. Following Silverman proposal, the idea is to use an
h_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm opt}}
rule based on the quantity
A
, with

A=\min(\mbox{SD},\mbox{IQR}/1.34)\,.

(54)

By considering MISE evaluations with this
A
-based rule for several distributions, including some mildly multimodal ones, it was found that on average it is better to replace the factor
(4/3)^{1/5}\sim 1.06
by
0.9
. Putting all together, we find the one-dimensional Silverman rule as
(
Silverman 1998
)

h_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Silverman}}=0.9\,A\,n^{-1/5}\,.

(55)

The multidimensional case is an extension of the above for
d
dimensions. It reads

h_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm Silverman}}=\frac{9}{10}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{5}}\left(\frac{4}{d+2}\right)^{\frac{1}{d+4}}\,A\,n^{-\frac{1}{d+4}}\,.

(56)

We use the expression above for deriving all the bandwidths.

As a final improvement here implemented, since the probability of finding data with
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}<0
or
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}>1
is necessarily zero, the smooth PDF found from the KDE method is cropped at
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}=0
and
{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}}=1
, and then normalized. The complete implementation of such details can be found in our code
NAVanalysis
.

Appendix C
NAV analysis and the
M_{200}-c
plane for NFW

Here we consider an application of the NAV analysis to the halo mass-contentration relation (
M_{200}-c
). It is well known that the NFW profile has a tendency of finding best-fits with unrealistically high values for
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
, when considered with flat priors on
\rho_{s}
and
r_{s}
. The NFW fits done also show this behaviour (Fig.
5
). In the
M_{200}-c
plane, this behaviour leads to virial masses too high and concentrations too low, outside the expectations from the cosmological simulations
(
de Blok and Bosma 2002
;
Rodrigues et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
.

In Sec.
3.2.2
, NFW constraints were found for the parameter
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
. These are also constraints on
M_{200}
and
c
, since
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
can be written as a function of the latter. Indeed, following
Mo et al. 2010
,

\displaystyle c=r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}/r_{200}\,,

(57)

\displaystyle M_{200}=M(r_{200})=4\pi\Omega_{m}\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}r^{3}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}\left(\ln(1+c)-\frac{c}{1+c}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}=200\Omega_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm m}}\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}c(1+c)^{2}\,,

where
\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm c}}
is the cosmological critical density.

Hence
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
can be written as a function of
M_{200}
and
c
as

r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}=\left(\frac{M_{200}}{4\pi\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}\Omega_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm m}}\left(\ln(1+c)-\frac{c}{1+c}\right)}\right)^{1/3}\frac{1}{r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}}\,.

(58)

\log M_{200}(M_{\odot})
\log c

Figure 1:
The gray-dashed region shows an approximation for the
M_{200}-c
correlation, at 1
\sigma
, as derived from cosmological N-body simulations by
Macció et al. 2008
. The plotted ranges of
M_{200}
and
c
are the same of the previous reference. The blue region, which is delimited by a solid line, shows the region compatible with the
1\sigma
NFW constraint on
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
(
28
).

For a given
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}
value, the constraints from eq. (
28
) will yield a region in the
M_{200}-c
plane. Using the median of
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}
from the 153 SPARC galaxies, which is 12.37 kpc, one finds the
1\sigma
constraint in the
M_{200}-c
plane as shown blue in Fig.
1
. Higher
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}
values slightly shifts this region towards higher
M_{200}
values, while lower
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm max}}
shifts the region in the opposite direction. This blue region alone is independent from any expectations from cosmological simulations, it was derived considering only the NFW halo radial dependence when contrasted to the observational data. Most of the individual NFW fits (with flat priors on
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
and
\rho_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
) are expected and indeed appear in this region, since this region is only a reparametrization of the 1
\sigma

r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
constraint
(
Li et al. 2020
, see also)
.

The bottom-right region of Fig.
1
is not covered by the blue region, but the former region is covered at the 2
\sigma
level (since at this level there is no upper limit for
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
). Any constraints imposed such that this bottom-right region is not populated by galaxy fits will hinder NFW fits that yield large
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm s}}
or
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
. This is the case of imposing constraints from cosmological simulations (Fig.
1
). The effect of such constraints can be seen in the NAV plane: they will not allow the NFW contribution to the rotation curve to become close to its lower limit (Fig.
5
), hence the NFW halo with these constraints will have an additional difficulty on covering observational data, which in general will lead to higher
\chi^{2}
values.

As it can be inferred from Fig.
5
, the extra difficulty in covering the observational data, due to cosmological constraints in the
M_{200}-c
plane, does not necessarily imply high
\chi^{2}
penalties. This happens since, for NFW curves that are close to the limiting case
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}=r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm n}}
,
r_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm sn}}
may change by orders of magnitude without a relevant change in
\delta V^{2}_{\mbox{\scriptsize\rm NFW}}
.
