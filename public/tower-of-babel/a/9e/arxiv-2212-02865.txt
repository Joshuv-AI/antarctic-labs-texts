sailing league problems
Robert Schüler and Achill Schürmann (University of Rostock)

Abstract
. We describe a class of combinatorial design problems which typically occur in professional sailing league competitions. We discuss connections to resolvable block designs and equitable coverings and to scheduling problems in operations research. We in particular give suitable boolean quadratic and integer linear optimization problem formulations, as well as further heuristics and restrictions, that can be used to solve sailing league problems in practice. We apply those techniques to three case studies obtained from real sailing leagues and compare the results with previously used tournament plans.

1.
Introducing sailing league problems

There is a long history of human competitions finding the
best
in a certain category.
While in some disciplines (as long jump or marathons) the winner can be determined by direct measurement, other disciplines only provide the possibility to directly compare participants (as football or chess).
The latter are often organized in form of tournaments or leagues where the winner is found after a number of direct comparisons.
Several different forms of sport leagues and tournaments have been developed, each with its own pros and cons
(see for instance
[
Kendall et al., 2010
]
and
[
Knust Bib
]
).

A sailing league typically consists of a fixed number of teams that meet on certain events,
often lasting a weekend or several days.
During each event, there are a number of rounds, called
flights
, with a fixed number of equally sized races,
such that every team competes exactly once during a flight.
For instance, in the finals of the 2021 European Sailing Champions League
32 teams were scheduled to compete in 18 flights, each consisting of 4 races with 8 teams
(see
[
ChampionsLeague
]
).
In the first and second national German sailing league
there are 6 events,
where 18 teams compete in 16 flights, each with 3 races of 6 teams
(see
[
Bundesliga
]
).
Note that there should be a certain number of teams in each race to make it more exciting,
but races with too many or even all teams are typically not desirable in a sailing league.
So the teams are typically partitioned into 2,3 or 4 equally sized races for a flight.

The resulting ranking (for which the rankings of each race are added) of any league should be chosen as fair as possible.
So for a better comparability of results, it is
desirable that the number of races that a team is paired against another team does not vary too much.
In a
perfect pairing list
each team would compete the same
number of times with any other team.
Whether or not such a perfect schedule exists is a special type of

block design problem
. We will discuss corresponding theoretical results and connections to
combinatorial design theory in Section
3
.
It turns out that such perfect pairing lists do rarely exist.
In particular, for the parameters of the sailing leagues mentioned above there is no such perfect schedule
(see Corollary
3.2
).

If for given parameters it is not possible to find a perfect schedule, it is natural
to consider the following, more general
sailing league problem
: In it we ask
to minimize the difference between upper and lower bound for
the number of competitions any team
competes with any other team.
By this, the pairing list is as fair as possible in the sense that it
is as close as possible to an ideal pairing list
in which each team competes with every other team
the same number of times.
As we show in Sections
4

there are natural formulations of sailing league problems as boolean quadratic and as
integer linear programming problems.

In Section
5
we apply the described techniques and
take a closer look at three case
studies. We analyze in particular the Asian Pacific and
European Champions Leagues and the German National Leagues
in their 2021 season.
It turns out that schedules could have been improved with respect to
the utility function of the sailing league problem.
However, despite heavy computational efforts, some of the related
combinatorial problems with larger parameters
appear to be quite difficult and remain still open.

As in several other sports league problems there is a close connection
to problems in combinatorial design theory, as we explain in
Sections
2
and
3
.
The sailing league problem studied here is also related to round-robin tournaments,
where after a predefined number of races, in which each participant competes against every other participant, a winner is chosen.
For a nice survey on round-robin tournaments we refer the interested reader to
[
Rasmussen and Trick, 2008
]
.
While in the classical round-robin tournament only two participants are paired with one another,
a sailing race pairs multiple teams against each other.
While this is true for other disciplines too (as in games such as Skat for three players or Catan for four players),
the number of teams in a sailing race is not predefined by the form of the competition
and can be chosen freely by the organizers with respect to the available sailing track.
Consequently, the sailing league problem does not fit directly into the
existing round robin literature, as it has recently been catagorized in
[
Van Bulck et al., 2020
]
.
However, our methods to approach the problem, using known results from
combinatorial design theory and integer programming formulations, are
similar to known techniques for solving different round robin tournaments
(see
[
Anderson, 1997
]
,
[
Briskorn, 2008
]
and the references within Section
4.4
).

2.
Parameters and Examples of sailing league problems

We start our survey with a precise introduction of
sailing league problems
. Given three positive integer parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
, where
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
is a divisor of
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
, we call the following setup a
pairing list
:

(1)

There is a set
T
of competing teams of cardinality
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
.

(2)

There is a list
R
of races. Each race is a subset of teams of cardinality
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
.

(3)

The races are organized in a list of flights
F
of cardinality
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
and
each team participates in each flight exactly once.
I. e,
F
is a partition of
R
and each flight
f\in F
is a partition of
T
.

Note that we use the term
list
to emphasize that it is allowed to repeat races and even flights.
Mathematically, one could also use an
incidence structure
or a
multiset
here.

We derive some further dependent parameters that turn out to be useful:

(1)

The
number of races in a flight
:

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}=\frac{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}}

(2)

The
total number of races
:

(1)

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}=|R|=\frac{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}}

(3)

For each pair of teams
t,t^{\prime}\in T
we have their
number of pairings

\lambda(t,t^{\prime})=|\{r\in R\ :\ t\in r\text{ and }t^{\prime}\in r\}|.

(4)

Derived from the number of pairings, we have the
minimal
, the
maximal
and the
average
number of pairings:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,t^{\prime}\in T\\
t\neq t^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\lambda(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,t^{\prime}\in T\\
t\neq t^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\lambda(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1)}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,t^{\prime}\in T\\
t\neq t^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\lambda(t,t^{\prime})

We easily obtain the following:

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

\lambda=\frac{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1}

Proof.

Let
t\in T
be a fixed team.
In each flight,
t
participates in exactly one race against
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)
opposing teams.
Therefore, there is a total of
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)
pairings for each team.
Since there are
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1)
opposing teams we get the claimed identity for the average number of pairings.
∎

For parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
the corresponding
sailing league problem
asks to find a pairing list with
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}
as small as possible.

Note that
\lambda_{\min}
and
\lambda_{\max}
are integers by definition, while
\lambda
may not be an integer.
If we manage to find a pairing list with
\lambda=\lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{\max}
and therefore
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}=0
, we call this pairing list
perfect
.
Obviously, a perfect pairing list is also optimal for the respective sailing league problem.

In order to have a compact human readable way to present different pairing lists of sailing leagues, we introduce the concept of a
tournament plan
for a sailing league:

(1)

A tournament plan is a matrix with
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
rows and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
columns, where each row corresponds to a flight and each column corresponds to a competing team.

(2)

Each flight consists of
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}
races. We identify each race in a flight by a number between
1
and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}
.

(3)

The entry in the tournament plan corresponding to team
t
and flight
f
is the number of the race (in
f
) that team
t
competes in. Since
f
is a partition of the teams, this number is uniquely determined.

To make the theoretical discussions of this paper more vivid, we take a look at
three different examples of real world sailing leagues.
Exemplarily, their parameters are chosen in such a way, that different techniques for their
solution can be applied, respectively different phenomena can be explained.

are six events with the scheduling parameters

(2)

for each event.
In contrast, events of the European Sailing Champions Leagues are realised with various different parameters.
Under several possibilities, we study the final of the European Sailing Champions League 2021 in Porto Cervo with parameters

(3)

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}=32,\quad N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}=18,\quad N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}=8\quad\mbox{and}\quad N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}=4,\quad N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}=72.

Despite the
32
possible slots for teams, in the actual event, only

This gives a motivation to look at types of
relaxed sailing league problems, which we discuss briefly in
Section

3.
Perfect pairing lists and connections to Design Theory

A simple necessary condition for the existence of a perfect pairing list is easily found:

Lemma 3.1
.

If we have a perfect pairing list with parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
, then

\lambda
is an integer.
In particular,
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1)
is necessarily a divisor of
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)
.

Proof.

In a perfect pairing list, we have
\lambda_{\min}=\lambda=\lambda_{\max}
and thus
\lambda
clearly is an integer.
The rest follows by Lemma
2.1
.
∎

By this result, we immediately have negative news for the parameters of our three study cases:

Corollary 3.2
.

There is no perfect pairing list with the parameters of the national German Sailing Leagues, the European Sailing Champions League and the Asian Pacific Sailing Champions League respectively.

Proof.

The corresponding values of
\lambda
are
\frac{80}{17}
,
\frac{126}{31}
and
\frac{32}{9}
respectively, which are not integers.
∎

We note that it is not difficult to find
any
perfect pairing list –
even when some parameters are given.
We can for example do one of the following:

(a)

Let all teams participate in every race, i.e.
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
.

(b)

For given
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
perform
all possible
flights.

However, these trivial perfect pairing lists are often not useful in practice.
A race between too many participants as in (a)
is apparently hard to realize under fair conditions.
That is the main reason why sailing leagues are organized as they are.
The other possibility in (b),
leads to an extremely high number of flights, due to a combinatorial explosion.
We have a total of

\frac{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}!}{(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}!)^{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}}\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}!}

flights, where we use
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}/N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
.
For example, if we have
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}=18
teams and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}=6
teams per race, as in
the national German Sailing Leagues, the pairing list with all possible flights
would have a total of
2,858,856
flights.

Problems similar to finding a perfect paring list have been studied in
mathematical
design theory
. This theory is also used in other
contexts to create team schedules or organizing experiments. For a
nice introduction to design theory, we refer to

[
Beth et al., 1999a
]
and
[
Beth et al., 1999b
]
.
A very good overview is provided by
[
Colbourn and Dinitz, 2006
]
.

One important concept of design theory are
block designs

(sometimes also called
balanced incomplete block designs
and abbreviated as
BIBD
).
For a block design, assume that we have a finite set whose elements are called
points
,
together with a collection of
blocks
which are subsets of the points.
Each block contains the same number of points as every other block,
and each pair of points is also contained in the same number of blocks.

If we interpret points to be the teams participating in the
sailing league and the blocks to be the different races, it is
apparent that finding a block design and a perfect pairing list are two strongly related problems.

For our further discussion,
we first give a precise definition of block designs,
using the naming conventions of this paper:

Definition 3.3
.

Let
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}},\lambda
be positive integers.
A
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}},\lambda)
-block design
is a tupel
(T,R)

where
T
is a set of teams and
R
is a list (an incidence structure) of subsets of
T
, such that:

(1)

|T|=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,

(2)

|r|=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
for all
r\in R
,

(3)

\lambda=\lambda(t,t^{\prime})
for all
t,t^{\prime}\in T
,
t\neq t^{\prime}
.

The elements of
T
are also called
points
of the design,
while the elements of
R
are called
blocks
of the design.
Note that in design theory literature, typically the cardinality of the pointset is denoted by
v
, the block size is
k
and therefore the
notation of a
(v,k,\lambda)
-design is typically used.
With our notation here we emphasize the connection to pairing lists.

Note that a pairing list is neither a stronger nor a weaker concept than a block design, since on the one hand it allows for different values of
\lambda(t,t^{\prime})
while on the other hand the races have to be organized in flights.
From a perfect pairing list we also get a block design.
However, from an arbitrary block design
we do not necessarily obtain a perfect pairing list,
as it is not always possible
to group the races into equally sized flights
– even if the block (race) size
k=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
divides the number of teams
v=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
.

There are design theoretic concepts to attack this problem.
A subset of
R
is called a
parallel class
or
resolution class
of the design,
if it is a partition of
T
. If the races can be partitioned into
parallel classes, the block design is called
resolvable

(sometimes abbreviated to RBIBD, standing for
resolvable
balanced incomplete block designs
, see
[
Abel et al., 2006
]
).
As a consequence, a perfect pairing list
with parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
(and
\lambda_{\min}=\lambda=\lambda_{\max}
)
describes the same concept as
a resolvable (
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
,
\lambda
)-block design,
and the terms
flight
and
parallel class
are interchangeable.

If we require from a pairing list that every pair of teams competes at
least once in some flight (
\lambda_{\min}\geq 1
), we are asking for a block design that is a

resolvable covering of pairs

(see
[
Gordon and Stinson, 2006
]
,
[
Dinitz et al., 2006
]
). Asking for a minimum number of parallel classes (flights)
to obtain such a resolvable covering of pairs has been studied in
[
Haemers, 1999
]
.
With the additional requirement that every pair of teams competes
exactly once or twice against each other a resolvable covering of pairs
is called
equitable
(see
[
van Dam et al., 2003
]
).
In this case, we have
1\leq\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{\max}\leq 2

and therefore
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}\leq 1
.
Hence, the sailing league problem is a generalization of an equitable
covering in a sense.
In design theoretic terms we are asking for a resolvable covering of pairs minimizing
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}

(is
“as equitable as possible”
), for a given
number of points (teams)
v=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
, block (race) size
k=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
and parallel classes (flights).

What can we use from the theory of resolvable block designs to
solve our sailing league problems in general?
On the one hand, we can certainly
obtain existence or nonexistence results of perfect pairing lists
for certain parameters from corresponding results about resolvable block designs.
On the other hand, we can use the existence of such designs
for finding fairly good pairing lists with slightly
different parameters.

We start with some necessary conditions on parameters for the existence
of block designs. We note that the formula of Lemma
2.1
remains
valid for general block designs, even if the races (blocks)
can not be grouped into flights (parallel classes).
The parameter

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}=\frac{\lambda\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1)}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1}

is in general equal to the number of races that contain a given team
– and each team in a block design is contained in the same number of races
(
[
Beth et al., 1999a
, Thm. 2.10.]
).
Using this equality in (
1
) we obtain another known formula
for a
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}},\lambda)
-block design:

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}=\frac{\lambda\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1)}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)}

holds for the number of races (blocks) of the design.
Since the quantities
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}
and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
have to be integers,
we can easily deduce the following:

Corollary 3.4
.

(
[
Beth et al., 1999a
, Cor. 2.11.]
)

If there exists an
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}},\lambda)
-block design, then

(1)

(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)\;
divides
\;\lambda\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1)
.

(2)

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)\;
divides
\;\lambda\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1)
.

Note that in the case that
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
divides
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
, only the first condition is relevant as the second follows directly.

In case we have a perfect pairing list, the number of races (blocks) each team competes in is equal to the number of flights (parallel classes).
We can find another necessary conditions for perfect pairing lists from the theory of resolvable block designs:

Corollary 3.5
.

(
[
Bose, 1942
, Equation 1.20]
)

If there exists a perfect pairing list with parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
, then

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}\geq N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}+N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}-1.

,

\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}=1
.

.

For the Asian Pacific Champions League we have a nearby nonexistence
result of a
(10,9,4)
-block design,
for a putative perfect paring list (
[

We end this section by subsuming the above observation on removing or adding arbitrary flights to an (putative) existing pairing list:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
P
be a pairing list for parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
and let
k
be an integer.
If
k<N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
, then there exists a pairing list
P^{\prime}
for parameters

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}-k
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}

with

\lambda_{\max}(P)-\lambda_{\min}(P)-k\;\leq\;\lambda_{\max}(P^{\prime})-\lambda_{\min}(P^{\prime})\;\leq\;\lambda_{\max}(P)-\lambda_{\min}(P)+k.

Furthermore, for arbitrary
k
, there also exists a pairing list
P^{\prime\prime}
for parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}+k
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
with

\lambda_{\max}(P)-\lambda_{\min}(P)-k\;\leq\;\lambda_{\max}(P^{\prime\prime})-\lambda_{\min}(P^{\prime\prime})\;\leq\;\lambda_{\max}(P)-\lambda_{\min}(P)+k.

If in addition
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}>N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
,
k=1
and
P
is a perfect pairing list,
then
P^{\prime}
as well as
P^{\prime\prime}
are optimal with

\lambda_{\max}(P^{\prime})-\lambda_{\min}(P^{\prime})\;=\;1\;=\;\lambda_{\max}(P^{\prime\prime})-\lambda_{\min}(P^{\prime\prime}).

Proof.

Consider removing
k
arbitrary flights from
P
to obtain
P^{\prime}
and adding
k
arbitrary flights to
P
to obtain
P^{\prime\prime}
. Thereby, we alter the numbers of times two teams have been paired against each other by at most
k
, yielding the inequalities.

If
P
is a perfect pairing list and
k=1
, then the inequalities yield

\lambda_{\max}(P^{\prime})-\lambda_{\min}(P^{\prime}),\lambda_{\max}(P^{\prime\prime})-\lambda_{\min}(P^{\prime\prime})\leq 1
and for the assertion to be true we are left to exclude that
P^{\prime}
and
P^{\prime\prime}
are perfect pairing lists.
Suppose
P^{\prime}
is perfect.
By Lemma
2.1
it follows that,
\lambda(P)=\frac{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1}
and
\lambda(P^{\prime})=\frac{(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}-1)\cdot(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1)}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1}
are integers.
By

\lambda(P)-\lambda(P^{\prime})=\frac{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1}{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}

this yields that
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}-1
has to be a divisor of
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1
. This is impossible since
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}>N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
yielding a contradiction. A similar argument can be found for
P^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

4.
Quadratic and linear programs for the sailing league problem

As in our three study cases, there does not exist a perfect pairing list
for most combinations of possible parameters.
In this section we therefore describe approaches to formulate
any sailing league problem with given parameters
as an optimization problem. They can – at least in principle –
be solved with a suitable software tool for operations research.
We used Gurobi
[
Gurobi
]
and Cplex
[
CPLEX
]
to compare the different programs.
We first give a natural boolean quadratic problem formulation and then describe
two different linear programs that appear to be better solvable in practice.
Depending on the given parameters one or the other may perform better,
as it is also shown by our case study in Section
5
.

We identify in the following each
team, race and flight by its index: We use

k,l\in\{1,\dots,N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}\}
for team indices and the index
j\in\{1,\dots,N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}\}

for the race. Index
i\in\{1,\dots,N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}\}
is used for the flight.
Furthermore, we assume that the races are organized in such a way, that
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}
consecutive races form a flight, i.e., flight
i
consists of the races with indices
\{(i-1)\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}+1,\dots,i\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}\}
.

Two conditions of a pairing list are already satisfied by the above conventions:

(1)

There are
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
teams.

(2)

The flights form a partition of the races.

Other conditions are to be modelled in the respective programs below.

4.1.
A boolean quadratic program

Allowing quadratic constraints there is a simple and straightforward
formulation of the sailing league problem.
For it we can use boolean, respectively
0
-
1
decision variables

b_{jk}\in\{0,1\}
indicating if team
k
participates in race
j
.

We model the condition, that the number of teams in each race has to be
equal to the constant
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
by the following
linear equations for each race
j
.
Since there is a fixed number of teams in each race, we get the constraints

\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}}b_{jk}=N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}

for every race
j
.

Additionally, we model the condition that every flight forms a partition of the teams (each team is in exactly one race per flight) by the following linear equation for every team
k
and every flight
i
:

\sum_{j=(i-1)\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}+1}^{i\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}}b_{jk}=1

In order to set up a linear utility function,
we use two variables
a
and
b
as lower and upper bound for the number of
competitions between two different teams
k
and
l
during a sailing league event.
We obtain the two nonlinear quadratic inequalities

(5)

a\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}}b_{jk}\cdot b_{jl}\leq b

for every pair of teams
k,l
, where we can assume
l<k
due to symmetry.
Note that the quadratic
0
-
1
-expressions
b_{jk}\cdot b_{jl}

indicate if team
k
and team
l
compete in race
j
.
Thus we may assume that
a
and
b
are integers.

Our goal is now to minimize the linear utility function
b-a
.
Note that for a pairing list that optimizes this program, we have
\lambda_{\max}=b
,
\lambda_{\min}=a
and
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}=b-a
and thus the program is equivalent to the sailing league problem.

The described quadratically constrained program
can be implemented in a straightforward way.
However,
further simplification, respectively a reduction of used variables is possible,
by using some additional symmetry breaking assumptions:
We can require for instance that the teams in race
1

are the teams
1
,
2
, …
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
and so on…
Thus we may assume

b_{1k}=1\;\mbox{for $k\leq N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}$ and $b_{1k}=0$ otherwise,}

etc..
Additionally we can also require that team
1
is in the first race of
every flight.
So
b_{jk}=1

for the first race of each flight, i.e. for all
j=(i-1)\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inflight}}+1
.

4.2.
An integer linear program

For practical computations it is usually desirable to get rid of the
quadratic constraints.
With the
0
-
1
decision variables in the
quadratic problem formulation of Section
4.1

we can easily set up a purely linear problem formulation.
This has the price of additional variables though:
We may use variables
b_{jkl}
for the non-linear expressions
b_{jk}\cdot b_{jl}

in the above quadratic problem formulation.
The
0
-
1
variables
b_{jkl}
are thus indicating
if team
k
and team
l
compete in race
j
.

The relation

b_{jkl}=b_{jk}\cdot b_{jl}

among the variables can be put into linear constraints
for every race
j
and every pair of teams
k,l
with
l<k
:
We use the three linear inequalities

(6)

\displaystyle b_{jk}+b_{jl}-1\;\leq\;b_{jkl}\;\leq\;b_{jk}\;,\;b_{jl}.

This is the
standard linearization
by Glover and Woolsey
[
Glover and Woolsey, 1974
]
.
In it we have a total of

3\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}\cdot{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}\choose 2}

additional linear inequalities and

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}\cdot{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}\choose 2}
additional variables

b_{jkl}
. We note that these additional variables can be chosen either binary
or as real, but then with additional bounds
0\leq b_{jkl}\leq 1
.
Then the variables are forced to be
0
or
1
by the inequalities (
6
).
Using real variables appears to be beneficial when used
with the standard parameters of
[
Gurobi
]
or
[
CPLEX
]
.

As in the quadratic problem formulation we want to minimize
b-a

under the now linear side constraints (see (
5
))

a\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}}b_{jkl}\leq b,

for every pair of distinct teams
k,l
, with
l<k
.

As a rule of thumb, this linear problem formulation works best
in practice with current solvers
for suitable small parameters of the sailing league problem
– despite its large number of variables.

4.3.
A smaller integer linear program

Since every team competes in every flight only once, we can
reduce the number of variables in the linear program formulation above.
Instead of using variables
b_{jkl}
to indicate whether team
k
competes in race
j
against team
l
, we can use
0
-
1
variables
b_{ikl}
indicating if
team
k
competes in flight
i
against team
l
.
We require for each flight
i
and each team
k
that

\sum_{l=1,l\not=k}^{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}}b_{ikl}\;=\;N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}-1,

that is, the number of teams competing against team
k
in flight
i

is fixed.
Additionally we require that
b_{ikl}=1
if
b_{ikm}=1
and
b_{ilm}=1
,
for every flight
i
and all triples of distinct team indices
k,l,m
.
This can be achieved by linear inequalities

b_{ikm}+b_{ilm}-1\;\leq\;b_{ikl}

for every flight
i
and all possible triples
k,l,m
.
Note that we may use variables
b_{ikl}
with
l<k
only in all of these inequalities,
because of the symmetry
b_{ikl}=b_{ilk}
.

From variables satisfying all the described inequalities we can easily construct a tournament plan. In it, the order of races in each flight can be chosen arbitrarily.
In this way, this second linear problem formulation gives us a solution of a race plan up to symmetry of race numbering in each flight.
As in the other two problem formulations we want to minimize
b-a
,
this time under the linear side constraints (see (
5
))

a\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}}b_{ikl}\leq b,

for every pair of distinct teams
k,l
, with
l<k
.

Overall, this second linear problem formulation has the advantage of less variables
compared with the one before. On the other hand it usually has more side constraints,
as their number grows cubic with the number of teams – compared to quadratic growth in the other linear problem formulation.
Concretely, in the second linear program we have a number of side constraints in the order

O(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}^{3})
while in the first linear program the number is in the order of
O(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize races}}\cdot N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}^{2})
.
An additional practical disadvantage of this second linear problem formulation is the fact that we can not simply relax the variables
b_{ikl}
to reals between
0
and
1
– as it is possible for the variables
b_{jkl}
in the first linear program. Here, in contrast to the first linear program, we do not have inequalities as in (
6
) which force the variables to be
0
or
1
.

However, together with the advantage of less variables, in our experiments we also discovered a second useful advantage: If we relax some of the variables
b_{ikl}
to reals between
0
and
1
, we obtain a possibly smaller minimum
b-a
that can be a useful lower bound! Since we know that
b-a
is an integer, such a bound can even be used to prove a tight bound on
b-a
, even if the corresponding
0
-
1
-problem can not be solved.
We use this
relaxation trick
in the next section to give a solution of the
Asian Pacific Champions League with
16
flights.

Using the tools of Sections

5.1.
Asian Pacific Sailing Champions League

We start with the smallest event, involving only

In 2021 the final event in Newcastle, Australia,
was realized with the pairing list given in Table

.
A quick analysis of this base pairing list, with

We note that other criteria were seemingly more important for the schedule,
but it is not clear to us which precicely and why.
Teams 6, 8 and 10, for instance, compete against each other in all 8
flights, while Team 2 competes against these three teams only once. I.e.

,
\lambda_{\max}=8
,
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}=7

Multiplying the base schedule by 6 — as it was planned in the actual event —

With the given parameters we can actually solve all linear/quadratic problems presented in Section

on a standard laptop. Fastest solutions with

b_{jk}
that are shared with the quadratic program.
I.e., for every equation we can relax one of the involved variables to be real.
Overall we obtain a computational proof of the following result:

Theorem 5.1
.

For the parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}=10
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}=8
and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}=5

an optimal tournament plan has a utility value

and satisfies

.
A corresponding plan is contained in the first eight flights (rows) of

.

Note that for the proof it is not enough to compute

with the given parameters:
Since the average of pairwise competitions in the

\lambda=32/9=3.555\ldots
,
it could in principle also be possible that there exist tournament plans with

\lambda_{\min}=3,\lambda_{\max}=6
.
However, corresponding integer linear programs with these values of

Note that Theorem

together with Lemma

also implies the nonexistence of a
(10,9,4)
-block design as has
been proven previously using combinatorics

[

The computed optimal schedules with
\lambda_{\min}=2
and
\lambda_{\max}=5
are still quite uneven, respectively the minimum of the utility function appears
quite big. This is in particular the case, as the base pairing list was to
be repeated six times. So, in the overall event, some teams would still compete
only
12
times against each other, while other pairs compete
30
times.
This difference can be reduced by using for instance three

We are able to solve the corresponding sailing league problem:

Theorem 5.2
.

For the parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}=10
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}=16

an optimal tournament plan has a utility value

.
A corresponding plan is contained in

For the computational proof of this result, we can first find a schedule

A solver finds such a plan comparatively fast or other heuristic strategies can be used.
It is more difficult to prove that utility value

is impossible.
All of the described linear and quadratic programs of Section

However, a specific relaxation of the linear integer problem presented in Section

We allowed the binary variables

of the

.
Thus of the

As any relaxation gives a lower bound to the actual minimum of

We finally note that the problem of finding an optimal

5.2.
German Sailing Leagues

We analyzed a total of
20

N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}=18
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}=16
and

The utility values for these events were either

indicating that the organizers put in some effort to obtain good tournament plans in the sense of the sailing league problem.

5.3.
European Sailing Champions League

The largest problem we analyzed is the European Champions League in 2021
with
32
slots for teams.
The actual number of teams competing in the event was
29
, so that three team slots were marked
“empty”
in the corresponding tournament plan.
Analyzing the
72

29
competing teams had between
2
and

matches against each other.
Thus in this case we may consider a kind of relaxed problem in which only a subset of
the

teams in the tournament plan is taken into account when computing the bounds

and
b=\lambda_{\min}
.

Unfortunately, we could not find an optimal pairing list for this problem.
Not even in the relaxed case using only
29
teams or by other approaches.
To find at least a pairing list that yields a good upper bound, we used a perfect pairing list with parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}=32
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}=8
and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}=31
(see also
[

, Lemma 5]
) and then applied a heuristic search method to find the presumably best way to remove

C.1
. Together with

3.2
this yields the following result:

Theorem 5.4
.

For the parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}=32
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}=18
and
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}=8

of the European Sailing Champions League Final 2021, an optimal pairing list satisfies

While we improved the utility value by two, compared with the actual event in 2021,
so far we can not prove optimality of the tournament plan in
Table
C.1
.
We finally note that other events of the European Sailing Champions League use various other parameters yielding even more unsolved sailing league problems with relatively big parameters.

We finally want to remark that a natural
greedy approach
to
solve a given sailing league problem does often not work.
It is tempting to think that one can get a quite good tournament plan
by optimizing the utility function for a few initial flights
and then sequentially take a solution for a fixed number of flights and
optimize the tournament plan with
n
additional flights.
This greedy strategy does in particular not work for the parameters of the
European Sailing Champions League Final 2021.
Here we optimized for
4
initial flights and obtained a tournament plan with
utility value
2
. Then we added sequentially two flights to obtain
tournament plans with

flights.
This first lead to tournament plans with utility value
3
, but for
18
flights the
optimal plan found in this way had utility value
4
– even if we
counted only pairings of
29
of the
32
teams in our computations.
Hence, this gives an example where this natural greedy strategy
produces a result worse than the one
in Table
C.1
.

6.
Additional connections and remarks

In this section we discuss further information which, while interesting, have not been necessary for the discussion of the three use cases.

6.1.
More connections to design theory

General pairing lists are strongly connected to the concept of
partially balanced block designs
. In particular, a pairing list with parameters
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize flights}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
and derived parameter
\lambda
corresponds to a
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}},\lambda)
-resolvable partially balanced block design
(T,R,\Lambda)
with
m
associate classes
where

m-1\leq\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}=\max(\Lambda)-\min(\Lambda).

In particular, pairing lists with
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}=1
correspond to resolvable partially balanced block designs with
2
associate classes.
More information about resolvable partially balanced block designs can be found, for example, in
[
Saurabh et al., 2021
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, resolvable partially balanced block designs are only known for very specific parameters and do not yield useful results for our three example cases.

6.2.
Relaxations

During the work on this paper, we also thought about different ways of relaxing the definition of a sailing league problem, in order to obtain tournament plans in which the number of pairwise matches does not vary too much.

The European Champions League in 2021 showed – with three teams missing in the final –
that we could allow the races to be of different size for example,
as effectively done in the event. There are several results in the literature concerning block designs with multiple possible block sizes which could be useful in this setting.

Another possible relaxation is, to allow flights that are not a
partition of the teams. In particular, we could allow pairing lists
such that each flight lets every team compete, except a fixed number
of teams who skip this flight, i.e. the flight is just a partition of
a subset of the teams. If every flight has exactly one team that does
not participate, this is strongly connected to the concept of

nearly resolvable block designs
(see for example

[
Abel et al., 2006
]
or
[
Banerjee et al., 2018
]
).
Some examples of pairing lists obtained in such a way are given in
Appendix
B

(Tables
B.1
and
B.2
).

Note that it is possible, to adapt the quadratic/linear programs of Section
4
according to these relaxations.

6.3.
Related problem

The sailing league problem discussed in this paper strictly requires the flights to be a partition of the teams, but principally allows different values for
\lambda(t,t^{\prime})
. If we strictly require that the pairing list is perfect but allow that the flights might not partition the teams, we get the following related problem:

Let
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}}
,
N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}}
,
\lambda^{\prime}
be positive integer parameters.
A
relaxed pairing list
(with respect to the given parameters) is a
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}},\lambda)
-block design with
\lambda\geq\lambda^{\prime}
where additionally the races are organized in a list of flights which are partitions of respective subsets of the teams.
One could then ask to find an
(N_{\mbox{\footnotesize teams}},N_{\mbox{\footnotesize inrace}},\lambda^{\prime})
-relaxed pairing list such that the number of flights is as small as possible.
In an event planned with this premise, each pair of teams would meet

exactly
the same number of times
\lambda
in as few flights as possible.

7.
Conclusions

In this paper we give an introduction to sailing league problems, depending on
the number of competing teams, the number of flights (rounds) and the number of teams per race.
We find in particular that recently organized sailing events could be enhanced in terms of the discussed utility function.
In very few cases of the parameters there exist perfect pairing lists, namely if and only if
there exists a corresponding resolvable block design
(see for instance Table
3.1
and
Appendix
A
,
Tables
A.1
to
A.10
).
If, as in most cases, the parameters of a given sailing league do not
allow the existence of a perfect pairing list,
a combination of knowledge from design theory and implementations of linear and quadratic programs can be used to find good or even optimal pairing lists.
In particular, the tournament plans presented in Appendix
C
and Table
A.8
could be used for the parameters of the Asian Pacific Champions League, the German Sailing Leagues and the European Sailing Champions Leagues discussed in Section
5
.

Despite the findings of this paper, the sailing league problem in
general seems hard to solve and many parameters of real sailing events
have not been considered yet.
Furthermore, several relaxed and related problems discussed in
Sections
3
and
6
have not been studied sufficiently.
So further research is necessary to get a more complete picture.
We think in particular, further connections to combinatorial design theory and
more advanced techniques from operations research
could held to improve sailing league schedules in the future.
