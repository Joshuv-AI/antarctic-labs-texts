Limits to velocity of signal propagation in many-body systems: a quantum-information perspective
Piotr Wysocki and Jan Chwedeńczuk

Abstract
The Lieb-Robinson bound (LRB) states that the range and strength of interactions between the constituents of a complex many-body system impose upper limits to how fast the signal can propagate. It manifests in a light cone-like growth of correlation function connecting two distant subsystems. Here we employ the techniques of quantum information to demonstrate that the LRB can be determined from local measurements performed on a single qubit that is connected to a many-body system. This formulation provides an operational recipe for estimating the LRB in complex systems, replacing the measurement of the correlation function with simple single-particle manipulations. We demonstrate the potency of this approach by deriving the upper limit to the speed of signal propagation in the XY spin chain.

The process, illustrated in Fig.

Subsequently, a Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}(\theta;t)=e^{-it\hat{H}}\hat{\varrho}(\theta)\,e^{it\hat{H}}.

The main question of this work can be stated as follows: how fast does the information about

, the parameter connected to the triggering impulse,

receiver

We provide an answer making a single assumption: the receiver is a single qubit with its local density operator

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)=\widetilde{{\rm Tr}}\left[\hat{\varrho}(\theta;t)\right],

(4)

where the tilde denotes the partial trace over all the non-receiver dergees of freedom.

The information about the impulse that can be extracted from any measurements on the receiver is no larger than the QFI, which is denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}
and has a
general form of

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}=2\sum_{i,j}\frac{|\langle\psi_{j}|\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)|\psi_{i}\rangle|^{2}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}},

(5)

where the summation runs over the elements of the spectrum of
\hat{\varrho}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)
,
|\psi_{i/j}\rangle
are its eigenstates and
\lambda_{i/j}
the corresponding eigenvalues.
The dot denotes the derivative of the density operator with respect to the parameter
\theta
. For a receiver that is a qubit, Eq. (
5
) reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|{\rm Tr}[\hat{\sigma}_{i}\dot{\hat{\varrho}}(\theta,t)]|^{2}.

(6)

Here
\hat{\sigma}_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}|\psi_{j}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|
(
j=1,2
) and
\hat{\sigma}_{3}=\hat{\sigma}_{rc}^{(x)}\cos\phi+\hat{\sigma}_{rc}^{(y)}\sin\phi
, where

\phi
is the phase of the matrix element
\langle\psi_{1}|\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)|\psi_{2}\rangle
(see Appendix).
The trace now runs over the Hilbert space of the whole system. The
\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}
is a convex functional of the density operator
[
23
]
, hence
by using Eq. (
2
) and the spectral decomposition of the density matrix
\hat{\varrho}(\theta)=\sum_{n}p_{n}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{n}|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|{\rm Tr}[\hat{\sigma}_{i}e^{-it\hat{H}}[\hat{H}_{sr},\sum_{n}p_{n}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{n}|]e^{it\hat{H}}]|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{n}p_{n}|{\rm Tr}[\hat{\sigma}_{i}(t)[\hat{H}_{sr},|\Psi_{n}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{n}|]]|^{2},

(7)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{i}(t)=e^{it\hat{H}}\hat{\sigma}_{i}e^{-it\hat{H}}
. The trace can be calculated using the basis of
|\Psi_{n}\rangle
’s, giving

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{n}p_{n}|\langle\Psi_{n}|[\hat{\sigma}_{i}(t),\hat{H}_{sr}]|\Psi_{n}\rangle|^{2}.

(8)

The emergent commutators
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)\equiv[\hat{\sigma}_{i}(t),\hat{H}_{sr}]
of operators related to the source and receiver (at time
t
)
are the core of the original Lieb-Robinson analysis
[
1
]
, see Eq. (
1
). The final step is to note that

\displaystyle|\langle\Psi_{n}|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)|\Psi_{n}\rangle|^{2}=\langle\Psi_{n}|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}^{\dagger}(t)(\hat{\mathds{1}}-\hat{\Pi}_{n}^{\perp})\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)|\Psi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle\Psi_{n}|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)|\Psi_{n}\rangle\leqslant\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)\|^{2},

(9)

where
\hat{\Pi}_{n}^{\perp}
projects onto the subspace orthogonal to that spanned by
|\Psi_{n}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{n}|
and
\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)\|^{2}
is the operator norm. Substitution of this inequality into line (
8
)
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{3}\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)\|^{2}.

(10)

This is the central result of this work—the amount of information that can be extracted from the receiver is upper-bounded by the LRB.
To estimate or measure the LRB it is thus sufficient to perform local single-body measurements. To recapitulate, this derivation does not rely on any assumptions about the
input state
\hat{\varrho}
, nor about the system, or the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
. Only the initial transformation is assumed to be in the general form of Eq. (
2
)
and the receiver to be a single qubit.

We now discuss the conditions under which this inequality can be saturated. The line (
7
) becomes an equality when the state
\hat{\varrho}(\theta)
is pure. The first inequality
in line (
9
) is saturated when
|\Psi_{n}\rangle
is an eigenstate of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)
and the last relation is saturated when the modulus of its eigenvalue is maximal.

Finding the spectrum of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}(t)
’s is usually hard. We show that the relation between the QFI and the LRB not only sheds some new light on both these quantities, but also allows to
analytically derive the LRB using the knowledge of
\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}
. For illustration, we pick the XY spin chain depicted by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\sum_{j}(\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{(x)}\hat{\sigma}_{j+1}^{(x)}+\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{(y)}\hat{\sigma}_{j+1}^{(y)}),

The impulse acts on the source qubit, and its generator is one of the Pauli matrices, say
\hat{\sigma}^{(x)}_{sr}

Subsequently, the Hamiltonian (

qubits away from the source.

In order to saturate Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}\leqslant\sum_{k}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\frac{\partial p_{k}}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}+4\Delta^{2}\hat{h},

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=|0,\ldots,0\rangle\equiv|\vec{0}\rangle,\ \ \ \mathrm{or}\ \ \ |\psi\rangle=|1,\ldots,1\rangle\equiv|\vec{1}\rangle,

It is worth noting that the steps that are necessary to estimate the LRB
have already been demonstrated experimentally in a different context.
The small (quasi-infinitesimal) increments of
\theta
were implemented experimentally in

for a similar purpose—to measure

In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the Lieb-Robinson bound can be derived from local measurements on a single qubit connected to a many-body system, hence shifting the necessity
of measuring correlation functions between distant subsystems.
This is possible, because as we have shown, the LRB intrinsically limits the amount of information that reaches a single qubit that is a part of a complex many-body system.
We have identified the conditions under which the local measurements on this qubit can saturate the upper limit set by the LRB, hence allowing one to determine the LRB from simple one-body measurements.
We have used an example of the XY spin chain to show how the two formulations—that of the LRB and of quantum information—interplay, allowing to determine the velocity of information propagation.
Due to the relative simplicity of the one-qubit operations and measurements, the protocol presented in this work might find applications in future measurements of the LRB, hence
contributing to our understanding of complex many-body systems.
Finally, we underline that one could independently derive Eq. (
10
) by starting from the analysis of information propagation through many-body quantum systems
[
5
]
.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Quantum Fisher information

In this section we present the details of the derivation of Eq. (6).
The full expression for the QFI is

\displaystyle F_{q}=2\sum_{i,j|\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}\neq 0}\frac{|\langle\psi_{i}|\partial_{\theta}\hat{\varrho}^{(rc)}|\psi_{j}\rangle|^{2}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}.

(A.1)

The sum can be split into
i=j
and
i\neq j
, giving

\displaystyle F_{q}=4|\langle\psi_{1}|\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}|\langle\psi_{1}|\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)|\psi_{1}\rangle|^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}|\langle\psi_{2}|\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}.

(A.2)

We now separately focus on the first term of this sum, which is
By writing

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{1}|\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)|\psi_{2}\rangle=ae^{i\phi},\ \ \ a\in\mathbb{R},

(A.3)

we notice that

\displaystyle|\langle\psi_{1}|\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}=a^{2}=\frac{1}{4}|{\rm Tr}_{rc}[\dot{\hat{\varrho}}_{rc}^{(\theta)}(t)\Big[(|\psi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{2}|+|\psi_{2}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{1}|)\cos\phi+\frac{1}{i}(|\psi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{2}|-|\psi_{2}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{1}|)\sin\phi\Big]]|^{2}

(A.4)

Hence by introducing

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}}|\psi_{j}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|,\ \ \ j=1,2,\ \ \ \hat{\sigma}_{3}=\hat{\sigma}_{rc}^{(x)}\cos\phi+\hat{\sigma}_{rc}^{(y)}\sin\phi,

(A.5)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{rc}^{(x)}=|\psi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{2}|+|\psi_{2}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{1}|,\ \ \ \hat{\sigma}_{rc}^{(y)}=\frac{1}{i}(|\psi_{1}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{2}|-|\psi_{2}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{1}|)

(A.6)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{rc}^{(q)}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|{\rm Tr}[\hat{\sigma}_{i}\dot{\hat{\varrho}}(\theta,t)]|^{2},

(A.7)

as reported in the main text.

Appendix B
The QFI for the XY model

The initial source state

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle_{sr}

(B.1)

evolves under the local transformation
e^{-i\theta\hat{\sigma}_{sr}^{(x)}}
to

\displaystyle|0\rangle_{sr}\longrightarrow\cos\theta|0\rangle_{sr}-i\sin\theta|1\rangle_{sr}.

(B.2)

Hence the state that undergoes the time evolution is (we label the source qubit with index 0 and use
\alpha=\cos\theta

\displaystyle|\psi(\theta;t=0)\rangle=\alpha|\vec{0}\rangle+\beta|1\rangle_{0}\bigotimes_{\kappa\neq 0}|0\rangle_{\kappa},

The first part,

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle=|1\rangle_{0}\bigotimes_{\kappa\neq 0}|0\rangle_{\kappa}

can be found using the Taylor series of the evolution operator. The one-fold action of

\displaystyle\hat{H}|\phi\rangle=|1\rangle_{1}\bigotimes_{\kappa\neq 1}|0\rangle_{\kappa}+|1\rangle_{-1}\bigotimes_{\kappa\neq-1}|0\rangle_{\kappa}.

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{2}|\phi\rangle=|1\rangle_{2}\bigotimes_{\kappa\neq 2}|0\rangle_{\kappa}+2|1\rangle_{0}\bigotimes_{\kappa\neq 0}|0\rangle_{\kappa}+|1\rangle_{-2}\bigotimes_{\kappa\neq-2}|0\rangle_{\kappa}.
