Averages of arithmetic functions over polynomials in many variables
Kevin Destagnol, Efthymios Sofos

Abstract
. We estimate the average of any arithmetic function k k over the values of any smooth polynomial in many variables provided only that k k has a distribution in arithmetic progressions of fixed modulus. We give several applications of this result including the analytic Hasse principle for an intersection of two cubics in at least 21 variables and asymptotics for the number of integer solutions of a non-algebraic variety.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11N37, 11P55.

1.
Introduction

Numerous challenges within the realm of Diophantine geometry can be rephrased in terms of computing averages of arithmetic functions,
denoted as
k:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, over the values attained by integer polynomials. Examples include, among others,
the Hasse principle for conic bundles
[
9
]
, Manin’s conjecture for Châtelet surfaces
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
19
]
or prime and squarefree values of polynomials (see
[
24
]
,
[
23
]
or
[
20
]
for example).

To estimate the average of
k
even over linear univariate polynomials
one must plainly assume that
k
has an average over every fixed arithmetic progression
a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)
. We may thus assume that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,x]\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}k(m)\approx\rho(a,q)\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t

for some
\rho(a,q)\in\mathbb{C}
and a function
\omega
of class
\mathscr{C}^{1}
. We may then use the Cramér–Granville model
to predict the average of
k
over the values of any polynomial
f

as done, for example in
[
20
, Appendix]

when
k
is the indicator function of the primes. The statement
analogous to
[
20
, Eq. (A.1)]

is that the conditional expectation of

k(m)
given that
m\in[1,x]
lies in the progression
a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)

is
\approx q\rho(a,q)\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t
.
Letting
W
be an integer that is divisible by all primes
p

below some
z\to\infty

the arguments in
[
20
, pg. 32]

lead to the
heuristic

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}\cap[-P,P])^{n}\\
f(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}k(f(\mathbf{t}))

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\\
\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}\cap[-P,P])^{n}\\
\mathbf{t}\equiv\mathbf{a}\left(\textnormal{mod}\ W\right)\end{subarray}}W\rho(f(\mathbf{t}),W)\omega(f(\mathbf{t}))

\displaystyle\approx\int_{[-P,P]^{n}}\omega(f(\mathbf{y}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ \ \ \sum_{\mathbf{a}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\frac{\rho(f(\mathbf{a}),W)}{W^{n-1}}.

We will prove this heuristic for all polynomials
f
in sufficiently many variables.
The cases with a small number of variables are harder;
in this case
if one is allowed to assume that
the function is non-negative and has a weak multiplicative property then
work of Nair–Tenenbaum
[
31
]

or
[
5
]
provide sharp upper bounds.

1.1.
The general result

Let
\omega:[1,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
be
\mathscr{C}^{1}
and for any
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
let
\rho(a,q)
be a number in
\mathbb{C}
. Assume we are given an arithmetic function
k:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
and define

(1.1)

{E_{k,\omega,\rho}(x,q):=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}\cap[1,x]}\max_{a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m\leqslant y\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}k(m)-\rho(a,q)\int_{1}^{y}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|.}

We shall henceforth denote
E_{k,\omega,\rho}(x,q)

by
E(x,q)
to simplify notation. The function
k
is
equidistributed
in progressions modulo
q
exactly when

E(x,q)
has smaller order of magnitude than
\int_{1}^{x}\omega
.

Example 1.1
.

Fix any
A>0
. When
k
is the indicator function of the primes
we choose
\omega(m)=1/\log(m+1)
and
\rho(a,q)=\mathds{1}(\gcd(a,q)=1)/\varphi(q)
. The Siegel–Walfisz theorem
[
28
, Eq.(5.77)]

is equivalent to

E(x,q)=O\left(\frac{\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t}{(\log x)^{A}}\right)

with an implied constant independent of
q
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be an integer polynomial.
Let
\mathscr{B}\subset[-1,1]^{n}
be of the form
\mathscr{B}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}[a_{j},a^{\prime}_{j}]
for some
a_{j}
with
\max_{j}|a^{\prime}_{j}-a_{j}|\leqslant 1
and define

(1.2)

{b=2\max\{|f(\mathbf{t})|:\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\}.}

Definition 1.2
.

For any
z\geqslant 2
, assume we have a function

m_{p}(z):\{\textrm{primes}\}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}
and let

W_{z}:=\prod_{p\leqslant z}p^{m_{p}(z)}.

We shall assume that
m_{p}(z)
is suitably large so that
\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)\to 0
as
z\to\infty
, where

\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z):=\sum_{p\leqslant z}\frac{1}{p^{1+m_{p}(z)}}.

The number
\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)
is an upper bound for the probability that a random integer is divisible by a
high power of at least one small prime. We abbreviate
m_{p}(z)
by
m_{p}
.

Our main technical tool expresses the error term as a function of
E(x,W_{z})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the positive integers
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy

n>2^{d}(d-1)
and let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a smooth form of degree
d
.
Let
s\in\{-1,1\}
and let
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be as in Definition
1.1
.
For any
z\geqslant 2
and
m_{p}
as in Definition
1.2
, let
W_{z}
be as in Definition
1.2
and assume that
\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)\to 0
.
Then there exist
c,\delta>0
that only depend on
f
such that for any function
k:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}

and all large enough
P
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}k(sf(\mathbf{t}))

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}\Bigg)\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\frac{\rho(sf(\mathbf{t}),W_{z})}{W_{z}^{n-1}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{P^{d}}(P^{-\delta}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)+z^{-c})+\frac{E(bP^{d},W_{z})W_{z}}{P^{d}}\right),

where the implied constant is independent of
k
. Here

b
is defined in (
1.2
) and

\|k\|_{1}:=\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,bP^{d}]}|k(\nu)|.

If the densities
\rho(a,W_{z})
have a multiplicative structure then the sum over
\mathbf{t}
is essentially a truncated Euler product over the
primes
p\leqslant z
.
The main idea of the proof is to not go through the usual route of major and minor arcs of the circle method but instead
approximate the values of
f(\mathbf{t})
by a Cramér–Granville model. Let
\mathrm{e}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi iz}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
We shall show in Lemmas
2.6
-
2.7
-
2.8
that

(1.3)

{\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=\int_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathscr{B}}\mathrm{e}(\gamma f(\mathbf{t})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t})\right)\int_{[P^{-d},b]}\omega(P^{d}\mu)\mathrm{e}(-s\gamma\mu)\mathrm{d}\mu\mathrm{d}\gamma.}

This expression is useful in applications as it has
\omega
and
f
separated.

Remark
.

The number of variables can be reduced by half
if one has better error terms regarding
the distribution of
k

on arithmetic progressions. This was done
in
[
20
]
for the indicator function of
primes and square-free integers.

Remark
.

A convenient feature of Theorem
1.1

is that it allows the user to make choices for
z
and the
exponents
m_{p}(z)
; this is useful in situations
where equidistribution for
k
is easier for moduli of specific factorisation.
For example, in certain Diophantine applications
it is much easier to prove equidistribution for square-full moduli. Furthermore,
z

is allowed to go to infinity arbitrarily slow; this means that one only needs to prove
equidistribution modulo very small moduli.

1.2.
A more accessible version

Theorem
1.1
makes no assumptions on the arithmetic function
k
.
We give a version that is easier to use if
k
has certain equidistribution properties:

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that the positive integers
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy

n>2^{d}(d-1)
and let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a smooth form of degree
d
.
Let
s\in\{-1,1\}
, let
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be as in Definition
1.1
and let
b
be given by (
1.2
).
Assume that
k:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is any function for which

•

\displaystyle\sup_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{a=1}^{q}|\rho(a,q)|<\infty
or
\rho(a,q)\in\mathbb{R}\cap[0,\infty)

for all
q\in\mathbb{N},a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
;

•

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t
is non-zero for all large enough

;

•

For each fixed
q\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{E(x,q)}{\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t}=0

•

We have
\displaystyle\liminf_{P\to\infty}P^{d}\Bigg|\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}:sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}\Bigg|\cdot\Bigg|\int_{1}^{bP^{d}}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t\Bigg|^{-1}\neq 0

•

We have
\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{m\leqslant x}|k(m)|}{\displaystyle\left|\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|}\neq\infty
.

Define for any
z>1
the integer
T_{z}=\prod_{p\leqslant z}p^{t_{p}(z)}
, where
t_{p}(z)
are arbitrary positive integers
such that for each fixed prime
p
one has

\lim_{z\to\infty}t_{p}(z)=\infty
.
Then the limit

(1.4)

{\sigma(f):=\lim_{z\to\infty}T_{z}^{-n+1}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/T_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\rho(sf(\mathbf{t}),T_{z})}

exists and is independent of the choice of
t_{p}(z)
.
Furthermore,

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{P^{n}\displaystyle\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}:sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}k(sf(\mathbf{t}))=\displaystyle\sigma(f).

A useful feature of Theorem
1.2
is that we only assume
equidistribution in arithmetic progressions
of
fixed
modulus and without an explicit error term.

1.3.
Analytic Hasse principle for intersections

We say that a family of
systems of polynomial equations with integer coefficients
satisfies
the analytic Hasse principle if the number of integer solutions
in an expanding box converges to a constant, strictly positive, multiple
of the analogous real and
p
-adic densities.
We will prove this for a specific intersection of two degree
d

polynomials. Our intersection is given by

(1.5)

{a^{2}+b^{2}=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),c^{2}+d^{2}=1+f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),}

where
f
is smooth homogeneous of degree
d
in
n>2^{d}(d-1)
variables.
There is recent analytic work in this area by
Rydin-Myerson
[
36
]
who studied intersections of
R
forms.
For intersections of two cubic forms this was recently
improved by Northey–Vishe
[
32
]
by relaxing the assumption on the number
of variables so as to work provided that there are at least

39
.
For systems of two diagonal cubic forms
work of
Brüdern and Wooley
[
11
]

proves asymptotics when one has at least
13
variables.
The three simplest cases
of our next result are

•

two quadratic equations in
at least
9
variables,

•

two cubic equations in
at least
21
variables,

•

two quartic equations in
at least
53
variables.

For
any box
\mathscr{B}\subset[-1,1]^{n}
and any

P\geqslant 1
let

N_{f}(P):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
f(\mathbf{x})>0\end{subarray}}r(f(\mathbf{x}))r(1+f(\mathbf{x})),

where
r
is the number of representations as the sum of two integer squares.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is a smooth homogeneous degree
d
polynomial in
n>2^{d}(d-1)
variables. For any box
\mathscr{B}\subset[-1,1]^{n}

inside of which
f
only takes non-negative values we have

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{N_{f}(P)}{P^{n}}=\pi^{2}\mathrm{vol}\left(\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\,:\,f(\mathbf{t})>0\}\right)\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}(f),

where

\sigma_{p}(f)
is given by

\lim_{k\to\infty}p^{-k(n+2)}\sharp\left\{(\mathbf{t},x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{n+4}:x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=f(\mathbf{t}),y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}=1+f(\mathbf{t})\right\}.

In particular, the analytic Hasse Principle holds for (
1.5
) as soon as
f
assumes at least one strictly positive value.

The proof relies on proving
a shifted convolution result (Theorem
3.1
)
and then feeding it into
Theorem
1.1
.
The proof of Theorems
3.1
and
1.3

are respectively in §
3.4
,
3.5
and §
3.6
.

1.4.
Subsets of integers

During the
2024
conference on rational points in
BIRS Chennai

Pieropan
[
7
]
asked the following (see
[
7
]
): assume we
are given a set
\mathscr{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}
whose density we can estimate
as

\sharp\{a\in\mathscr{A}:|a|\leqslant T\}=f(T)+O(g(T)),

where
f(T)
has the form
c_{0}T^{c}(\log T)^{e}
for some real constants
c_{0},c
and
e
and where

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}g(T)/(T^{c}(\log T)^{e})=0.

For an arbitrary polynomial
F\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
define

N(T;\mathscr{A},F)=\sharp\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap[-T,T]^{n}:F(\mathbf{x})\in\mathscr{A}\right\}.

The question is under what conditions on
\mathscr{A},f,g,n
and
\deg(F)
can one prove asymptotics for
N(T;\mathscr{A},F)
as
T\to\infty
?

Our result states that the condition
n>2^{\deg(F)}(\deg(F)-1)
is sufficient, as long as one has the additional assumption
that we can count asymptotically the elements of
\mathscr{A}
on any fixed arithmetic progression
r\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)
.
To see why this is necessary, just consider the polynomial
F(x)=r+qx
.
For convenience of notation we will
assume that
\mathscr{A}
consists of strictly positive integers; in applications
this does not cause any problems as one can split in cases according to the sign.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that the positive integers
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy

n>2^{d}(d-1)
and let
F\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a smooth form of degree
d
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be as in Definition
1.1
and let
b
be given by (
1.2
).
Assume that
\mathscr{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
is a non-empty set for which there exists
\omega:[1,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
in
\mathscr{C}^{1}

and for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
a real number
\rho(q,r)\geqslant 0
such that we have

\lim_{x\to\infty}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}\cap[1,x]}\max_{a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}\left|\frac{\sharp\{a\in\mathscr{A}:a\leqslant y,a\equiv r\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\}}{\displaystyle\int_{1}^{y}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t}-\rho(r,q)\right|=0.

Assume, in addition, that

\liminf_{P\to\infty}P^{d}\Bigg|\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}:|F(\mathbf{t})|>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}F(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}\Bigg|\cdot\Bigg|\int_{1}^{bP^{d}}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t\Bigg|^{-1}\neq 0.

Define for any

, where

are arbitrary positive integers

.

1.5.
Points on non-algebraic varieties

Exponential Diophantine equations
form a considerable
area of research in number theory

proving finiteness of solutions,
see for example the work of
Bugeaud–Mignotte–Siksek
[
12
]

and the book of

[
38
]
. Here

Theorem 1.5
.

For every smooth homogeneous

of degree

and for every box
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B},x_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\cap\left[1,\log(bP^{d})/\log 2\right]:f(\mathbf{x})=2^{x_{0}}\right\}}{P^{n-d}\log P}=\frac{d\sigma_{\infty}(f)}{\log 2}\sigma_{2}(f)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p>2\end{subarray}}\sigma_{p}^{\prime}(f),

)

3.10

by applying Theorem
1.2
.

1.6.
Chowla’s conjecture

Let
\mu

Chowla’s conjecture states that for any
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
for which
f\neq c_{1}g^{2}
for
c_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
and

one has

\frac{1}{P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,P]\\
f(m)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(|f(\mathbf{m})|)\to 0

[
29
]
and Tao–Teräväinen
[
39
]
, for example.
We prove the analogue of the conjecture for all polynomials in a sufficiently large number of variables.

.

of degree

In particular, for every box
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in Definition

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{P^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\

The proof is given in §

.

1.8.
Divisor function over values of polynomials

Let us continue with estimating the average of the divisor function
\tau
over polynomials.
In the case of one variable polynomials, asymptotics
are known only for polynomials of degree
1
and
2
(see Hooley
[
26
]
),
while, for binary forms asymptotics are known for degree
\leqslant 4
by work of Daniel
[
15
]
.
For a prime
p
and positive integers
m,\ell

we define
\tau_{p^{m}}(\ell):=1+\min\{v_{p}(\ell),m\}
. This is a
local model for the divisor function
\tau
since
\tau(\ell)=\prod_{p\mid\ell}\lim_{m\to\infty}\tau_{p^{m}}(\ell)
.

Theorem 1.8
.

For every smooth homogeneous
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
with
n>2^{d}(d-1)

and for every box
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in Definition
1.1

we have

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{P^{n}\log P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
f(\mathbf{x})\neq 0\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69046pt\tau(|f(\mathbf{x})|)=d\mathrm{vol}(\mathscr{B})\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{p^{nm}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m})^{n}}\tau_{p^{m}}(f(\mathbf{t})).

The proof is given in §
3.3
as an application of
an equidistribution result of
Pongsriiam–Vaughan
[
35
]

and Theorem
1.2
.

1.9.
Rational points on a hyperelliptic hypersurface

For an integer polynomial
f
of degree at least
5
the hyperelliptic curve
y^{2}=f(x)
has finitely many rational points by Faltings’ theorem
[
22
]
.
On the contrary when
f
has many variables one expects infinitely many rational points; here we shall give an asymptotic estimate.

Theorem 1.9
.

Fix an integer
k\geqslant 2
. For every smooth homogeneous
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
with
n>2^{d}(d-1)

and for every box
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in Definition
1.1
inside which
f
assumes at least one strictly positive value,
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{P\to\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}:f(\mathbf{x})\textrm{ is a }k\textrm{-th power of a positive integer}\right\}}{P^{n-d(1-1/k)}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{y,\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n+1}:y^{k}=f(\mathbf{t})\}}{p^{mn}}\lim_{\delta\to 0_{+}}\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
f(\mathbf{t})>\delta\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}}{kf(\mathbf{t})^{1-1/k}}.

The proof is given in §
3.8
as an
application of Theorem
1.2
.

1.10.
m
-full numbers represented by polynomials

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geqslant 2
. An integer is called
m
-full if it is divided by the
m
-th power
of every of its prime divisors. Values of polynomials that are
m
-full have been studied by various authors; for example, Pasten
[
33
]

proved that an integer polynomial should take very few
m
-full values conditionally on Vojta’s conjecture and have applications to the study of Campana points (see for example
[
34
]
). The picture for multivariable polynomials
is rather different; indeed here one expects infinitely many solutions. We provide asymptotics.

Theorem 1.10
.

Fix an integer
m\geqslant 2
. For every smooth homogeneous
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
with
n>2^{d}(d-1)

and for every box
\mathscr{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in Definition
1.1
inside which
f
assumes at least one strictly positive value,
we have

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}:f(\mathbf{x})\textrm{ is }m\textrm{-full and positive}\right\}}{P^{n-d(1-1/m)}}=\sigma(f)\lim_{\delta\to 0_{+}}\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
f(\mathbf{t})>\delta\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}}{mf(\mathbf{t})^{1-1/m}},

where
\sigma(f)
is given by

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{2},\ldots,k_{m}\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(k_{2}\cdots k_{m})^{2}}{k_{2}^{1+1/m}k_{3}^{1+2/m}\cdots k_{m}^{2-1/m}}\prod_{p}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{y,\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n+1}:y^{m}k_{2}^{m+1}\cdots k_{m}^{2m-1}=f(\mathbf{t})\}}{p^{mn}}.

The proof is given in §
3.9
as an application of
Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of
Theorems

and

The Hardy–Littlewood singular series for the problem of representing an integer
\nu
by the polynomial
f
is given by

\mathfrak{S}(\nu):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=2\\
p\textrm{ prime}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}.

We start by approximating it by its multiplicative model

\mathfrak{S}^{\flat}(\nu):=\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{W_{z}^{n-1}}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f
be as in
Theorem
1.1
and assume that
\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)\to 0
.
There exists
c=c(f)>0
such that for all
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\mathfrak{S}(\nu)-\mathfrak{S}^{\flat}(\nu)\ll\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)+z^{-c},

where the implied constant is independent of
\nu
.

Proof.

By
[
1
, §7]
we have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})=\nu\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}=1+\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}p^{-rn}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{r}}\mathrm{e}(-a\nu/p^{r}),

where

(2.1)

{S_{a,q}:=\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\mathrm{e}\left(\frac{a}{q}f(\mathbf{t})\right).}

The assumption
n>2^{d}(d-1)
shows that the constant

(2.2)

{c:=\frac{n2^{-d}}{(d-1)}-1}

is strictly positive.
Using
[
1
, pg. 256, Eq. (19)]
with
\varepsilon=c

we see that

(2.3)

{|S_{a,p^{r}}|\ll p^{r\left(n-c-2\right)}}

with an implied constant that depends at most on
f
. We infer that for all
m\geqslant 1
one has

(2.4)

{\left|\sum_{r\geqslant m}p^{-rn}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{r}}\mathrm{e}(-a\nu/p^{r})\right|\ll\sum_{r\geqslant m}p^{-r(1+c)}\ll p^{-m(1+c)},}

where the implied constant depends at most on
f
.
In the special case that
m=1
this yields

(2.5)

{\left|\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}-1\right|\ll p^{-(1+c)}}

uniformly in
\nu
.
Hence, for any
z>1
we have

\log\prod_{p>z}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}=\sum_{p>z}\log\left(1+O(p^{-1-c})\right)\ll\sum_{p>z}p^{-1-c}\ll z^{-c}

and therefore

\prod_{p>z}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}=1+O\left(z^{-c}\right).

We have
\mathfrak{S}(\nu)=O(1)
independently of
\nu

due to
[
1
, Corollary, pg.256]
. Thus,

\mathfrak{S}(\nu)=\left(\prod_{p\leqslant z}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}\right)+O(z^{-c})

with an implied constant independent of
\nu
. By (
2.4
) we see that for any
m_{p}\geqslant 1
,

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}-\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant m_{p}}p^{-rn}\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{r}}\mathrm{e}(-a\nu/p^{r})\ll p^{-(1+c)(1+m_{p})}.

This means that

\prod_{p\leqslant z}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{m(n-1)}}=V\prod_{p\leqslant z}\left(\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant m_{p}}p^{-rn}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{r}}\mathrm{e}(-a\nu/p^{r})\right),

for some quantity
V
that satisfies

V=\prod_{p\leqslant z}\left(1+O\left(p^{-(1+c)(1+m_{p})}\right)\right)=\exp\left(O\left(\sum_{p\leqslant z}p^{-(1+c)(1+m_{p})}\right)\right)=1+O(\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z))

due to the assumption
\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)\to 0
.
Recalling that
W_{z}=\prod_{p\leqslant z}p^{m_{p}},
the last equation in
[
1
, pg. 259]
gives

(2.6)

{\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant m_{p}}p^{-rn}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{r}}\mathrm{e}(-a\nu/p^{r})=\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m_{p}})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{m}\right)\}}{p^{(n-1)m_{p}}},}

which concludes the proof via the Chinese remainder theorem.
∎

The Hardy–Littlewood singular integral for representing
\nu
by
f
is

J(\nu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma\nu)\mathrm{d}\gamma,\ \ \ \textrm{ where }\ \ \ I(\mathscr{B};\gamma):=\int_{\mathscr{B}}\mathrm{e}\left(\gamma f(\mathbf{t})\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}.

We are now in position to start averaging the arithmetic function over the polynomial values.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f
be as in Theorem
1.1
and assume that
\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)\to 0
.
There exists
c=c(f)>0
such that for all large
P
one has

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}k(sf(\mathbf{t}))=

\displaystyle\frac{P^{n-d}}{W_{z}^{n-1}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z})^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,bP^{d}]\\
\nu\equiv sf(\mathbf{t})\left(\textnormal{mod}\ W_{z}\right)\end{subarray}}k(\nu)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma s\nu P^{-d})\right\}\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle O\left(P^{n-d}\left(P^{-\delta}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)+z^{-c}\right)\|k\|_{1}\right).

Proof.

For
P
suitable large and all
\mathbf{t}\in P\mathscr{B}
we have
|f(\mathbf{t})|\leqslant bP^{d}
, hence,

(2.7)

{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}k(sf(\mathbf{t}))=\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,bP^{d}]}k(\nu)\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}:f(\mathbf{t})=s\nu\}.}

By
[
1
, Lem.5.5]
there is
\delta=\delta(f)>0
such that

\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}:f(\mathbf{t})=s\nu\}=P^{n-d}\mathfrak{S}(s\nu)J(s\nu P^{-d})+O(P^{n-d-\delta}),

where the implied constant is independent of
s
and
\nu
.
By Lemma
2.1

we can write the right-hand side as

P^{n-d}\mathfrak{S}^{\flat}(s\nu)\int_{\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma s\nu P^{-d})\mathrm{d}\gamma+O\left(P^{n-d}\left(P^{-\delta}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)+z^{-c}\right)\right),

where we used that
J(s\nu P^{-d})
is bounded only in terms of
f
.
Substituting into (
2.7
) we obtain the claimed error term. In the ensuing main term
we can interchange the order of summation and integration owing to the fact that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|\mathrm{d}\gamma<\infty
by
[
1
, Lem. 5.2]
. This gives

P^{n-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\left\{\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,bP^{d}]}k(\nu)\mathfrak{S}^{\flat}(s\nu)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma s\nu P^{-d})\right\}\mathrm{d}\gamma.

By definition of the function
\mathfrak{S}^{\flat}(s\nu)
,
we obtain

P^{n-d}W_{z}^{-n+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,bP^{d}]\\
\nu\equiv sf(\mathbf{t})\left(\textnormal{mod}\ W_{z}\right)\end{subarray}}k(\nu)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma s\nu P^{-d})\right\}\mathrm{d}\gamma,

as claimed. ∎

The presence of
I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)
in the main term
shows that only small
\gamma
matter. Therefore, a simple approach to deal with those will suffice.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
x\geqslant 1
,
a,q\in\mathbb{Z}
with
q\neq 0
and any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\nu\leqslant x\\
\nu\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}k(\nu)\mathrm{e}(\mu\nu)=\rho(a,q)\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{e}(\mu t)\mathrm{d}t+O\big((1+|\mu|x)E(x,q)\big),

where the implied constant is absolute.

Proof.

By partial summation we obtain

A(x)\mathrm{e}(\mu x)-2\pi i\mu\int_{1}^{x}A(t)\mathrm{e}(\mu t)\mathrm{d}t,\ \ \ \textrm{ where }\ \ \ A(t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\nu\leqslant t\\
\nu\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}k(\nu).

Alluding to (
1.1
) we then obtain

\rho(a,q)\left\{\mathrm{e}(\mu x)\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t-2\pi i\mu\int_{1}^{x}\int_{1}^{t}\omega(u)\mathrm{e}(\mu t)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}t\right\}+O\left((1+|\mu|x)E(x,q)\right)

and infer that the difference inside the brackets equals
\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{e}(\mu t)\mathrm{d}t.
∎

Define

H:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\bigg(\int_{P^{-d}}^{b}\omega(P^{d}y)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma sy)\mathrm{d}y\bigg)\mathrm{d}\gamma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Keep the setting of Lemma
2.2
.
There exists
c=c(f)>0
such that for all large
P
one has

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle k(sf(\mathbf{t}))-\frac{P^{n}H}{W_{z}^{n-1}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z})^{n}}\rho(sf(\mathbf{t}),W_{z})

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle P^{n-d}W_{z}E(bP^{d},W_{z})+P^{n-d}\left(P^{-\delta}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)+z^{-c}\right)\|k\|_{1}.

Proof.

We inject Lemma
2.3
into
Lemma
2.2
. The contribution of the error term is

\ll\frac{P^{n-d}}{W_{z}^{n-1}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z})^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|\left(1+\left|\gamma sP^{-d}\right|P^{d}\right)E(bP^{d},W_{z})\mathrm{d}\gamma.

By
[
1
, Lem. 5.2]
with
\varepsilon
being the positive constant
c
in (
2.2
)
we obtain

(2.8)

{|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|\ll\min\{1,|\gamma|^{-c-2}\},}

hence,
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|(1+|\gamma|)\mathrm{d}\gamma<\infty
. This gives the estimate

\ll E(bP^{d},W_{z})\frac{P^{n-d}}{W_{z}^{n-1}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z})^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)(1+|\gamma|)\mathrm{d}\gamma\ll E(bP^{d},W_{z})P^{n-d}W_{z}.

∎

To deal with
H
we generalise the Fourier analysis approach in
[
20
]
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
\varphi_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be

\varphi_{k}(\gamma)=\pi^{-1/2}k\exp(-k^{2}\gamma^{2}),
whose Fourier transform is

\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\gamma)=\exp(-\pi^{2}k^{-2}\gamma^{2})
.
Define

H_{k}=\int_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\gamma)I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\int_{P^{-d}}^{b}\omega(P^{d}y)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma sy)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\gamma.

Lemma 2.5
.

For fixed
P\geqslant 1

we have
H_{k}\to H
as
k\to+\infty
.

Proof.

By (
2.8
) and
|\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\gamma)|=O(1)

one sees that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{|\gamma|>\log k}(\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\gamma)-1)I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\int_{P^{-d}}^{b}\omega(P^{d}y)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma sy)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\gamma=0.

In the region
|\gamma|\leqslant\log k
one has

\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\gamma)=\exp(-\pi^{2}k^{-2}\gamma^{2})=1+O(k^{-2}\gamma^{2})=1+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}k}{k^{2}}\right),

with an implied constant that is independent of
\gamma
. Hence,

\int_{|\gamma|>\log k}(\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\gamma)-1)I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\int_{P^{-d}}^{b}\omega(P^{d}y)\mathrm{e}(-\gamma sy)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\gamma\ll\frac{\log^{2}k}{k^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|\mathrm{d}\gamma\ll\frac{\log^{2}k}{k^{2}},

which concludes the proof. ∎

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}
let

F_{k}(\mathbf{t}):=\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[P^{-d},b]\\
|f(\mathbf{t})-sy|\leqslant 1/\sqrt{k}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}y)\varphi_{k}(f(\mathbf{t})-sy)\mathrm{d}y.

Lemma 2.6
.

We have

H=\int_{\mathscr{B}}\lim_{k\to\infty}F_{k}(\mathbf{t})\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

By the definition of
I(\mathscr{B};\boldsymbol{\gamma})
we can write

H_{k}=\int_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}}\int_{y\in[P^{-d},b]}\omega(P^{d}y)\int_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\gamma)\mathrm{e}(\gamma\left\{f(\mathbf{t})-sy\right\})\mathrm{d}\gamma\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t},

where we swapped the order of integration owing to
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{\varphi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})|\mathrm{d}\gamma<\infty
.
By Fourier inversion

H_{k}=\int_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}}\int_{y\in[P^{-d},b]}\omega(P^{d}y)\varphi_{k}(f(\mathbf{t})-sy)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}.

For
|y|>1/\sqrt{k}
we see that

(2.9)

{\varphi_{k}(y)=\pi^{-1/2}k\exp(-k^{2}y^{2})\ll k\exp(-k).}

Hence, by Lemma
2.5
we get

H=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathscr{B}}F_{k}(\mathbf{t})\mathrm{d}t.

To conclude the proof it will suffice to use the dominated convergence theorem.
Its assumptions hold since

F_{k}(\mathbf{t})
is bounded independently of
k
and
\mathbf{t}
. Indeed,

\left|F_{k}(\mathbf{t})\right|\ll\left(\max_{y\in[P^{-d},b]}|\omega(P^{d}y)|\right)\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\varphi_{k}(f(\mathbf{t})-sy)\mathrm{d}y

and note that a linear change of variables makes the integral equal to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z=1
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

We have

H=\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\lim_{k\to\infty}F_{k}(\mathbf{t})\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

The
\mathbf{t}
with
sf(\mathbf{t})=P^{-d}
have measure
0
, hence, can be ignored. It suffices to show that
if
sf(\mathbf{t})<P^{-d}
then
\lim_{k}F_{k}(\mathbf{t})=0
. Indeed, for such
\mathbf{t}
we shall see that, for
k
big enough, the range of integration in the definition of
F_{k}
is empty.
Indeed, if there is some
y
in
F_{k}
then

y=y-sf(\mathbf{t})+sf(\mathbf{t})\leqslant|y-sf(\mathbf{t})|+sf(\mathbf{t})\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+sf(\mathbf{t}),

hence, as
k\to\infty
, we get
y<P^{-d}
. This contradicts the definiton of
F_{k}
, which includes the condition

y\in[P^{-d},b]
.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}
. If
sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}
then
\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}F_{k}(\mathbf{t})=\omega(sP^{d}f(\mathbf{t}))
.

Proof.

For each
y
such that
|y-sf(\mathbf{t})|\leqslant 1/\sqrt{k}
we have

-\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+P^{-d}\leqslant-|y-sf(\mathbf{t})|+P^{-d}<y-sf(\mathbf{t})+sf(\mathbf{t})\leqslant|y-sf(\mathbf{t})|+b\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+b,

thus, as
k\to\infty
, we can ignore the condition
y\in[P^{-d},b]
in the definition of
F_{k}
. Hence,

\lim_{k\to\infty}F_{k}(\mathbf{t})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{|f(\mathbf{t})-sy|\leqslant 1/\sqrt{k}}\omega(P^{d}y)\varphi_{k}(f(\mathbf{t})-sy)\mathrm{d}y.

Since
\omega
is continuous and
\varphi_{k}
has exponential decay, we infer that

\lim_{k\to\infty}F_{k}(\mathbf{t})=\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{|f(\mathbf{t})-sy|\leqslant 1/\sqrt{k}}\varphi_{k}(f(\mathbf{t})-sy)\mathrm{d}y.

It now suffices to prove that the last limit over
k\to\infty
equals
1
.
By (
2.9
) we can dispose of the condition
|f(\mathbf{t})-sy|\leqslant 1/\sqrt{k}

as
k\to+\infty
and we can then use the fact
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{k}(\mu)\mathrm{d}\mu=1.
∎

Bringing together Lemmas
2.6
,
2.7
and
2.8
yields

H=\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}.

Injecting this into the main term present in Lemma
2.4
concludes the proof of
Theorem
1.1
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We first prove that if

\rho(a,q)\geqslant 0
for all
a,q
then

\displaystyle\sup_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{a=1}^{q}|\rho(a,q)|<\infty
.
Thus, to complete the proof it suffices to
work under the latter assumption only.
We write

\sum_{m\leqslant x}k(m)=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}k(m)

and divide through by
\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t
.
Letting

x\to\infty
and using
the assumption

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{E(x,q)}{\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t}=0

we deduce that for all
a
and
g
we have

(2.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}}\rho(a,q)=\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{m\leqslant x}k(m)}{\displaystyle\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t}.

Thus, if
\rho(a,q)\geqslant 0
for all
a,q
then

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{q}|\rho(a,q)|
is uniformly bounded for all
q
.

Next we prove that the limit in (
1.4
)
exists. Fixing the value of
f(\mathbf{t})
we see that

T_{z}^{-n+1}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/T_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\rho(sf(\mathbf{t}),T_{z})=\sum_{\nu=1}^{T_{z}}\rho(\nu,T_{z})\prod_{p\leqslant z}\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{t_{p}(z)}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:sf(\mathbf{t})\equiv\nu\left(\textnormal{mod}\ p^{t_{p}(z)}\right)\right\}}{p^{t_{p}(z)(n-1)}},

which, by Lemma
2.1
equals

\sum_{\nu=1}^{T_{z}}\rho(\nu,T_{z})\left(\mathfrak{S}(\nu)+o_{z\to\infty}(1)\right).

By assumption,
\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=1}^{T_{z}}|\rho(\nu,T_{z})|
is bounded independently of
z
, hence, we obtain

\sum_{\nu=1}^{T_{z}}\rho(\nu,T_{z})\mathfrak{S}(\nu)+o_{z\to\infty}(1)=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\\
\gcd(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}S_{a,q}\sum_{\nu=1}^{T_{z}}\rho(\nu,T_{z})\mathrm{e}(-a\nu/q)+o_{z\to\infty}(1),

where we used the equation before
[
1
, Lemma 5.5]

to express
\mathfrak{S}
via
S_{a,q}
and the bound (
2.4
) to change order of summation. The new series over
q
converges absolutely
due to (
2.4
) and

\left|\sum_{\nu=1}^{T_{z}}\rho(\nu,T_{z})\mathrm{e}(-a\nu/q)\right|\leqslant\sum_{\nu=1}^{T_{z}}|\rho(\nu,T_{z})|<\infty.

This proves that the limit defining
\sigma(f)
exists and is independent of the choice of
t_{p}(z)
due to
2.10
.

The remaining claims
in Theorem
1.2

will be deduced from Theorem
1.1

with the following choice for
m_{p}(z)
.
For any prime
p
, define
m_{p}(z)
as the largest integer satisfying

p^{m_{p}(z)}\leqslant z
and let
W_{z}=\displaystyle\prod_{p\leqslant z}p^{m_{p}(z)}
.
For a fixed integer
k\geqslant 1
note that the primes
p

for which
m_{p}(z)=k
are exactly those in the interval

(z^{1/(k+1)},z^{1/k}]
.
Summing over all integers
m
in that interval gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant(\log z)/(\log 2)}\sum_{z^{1/(k+1)}<m\leqslant z^{1/k}}m^{-k-1}\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant(\log z)/(\log 2)}\int_{z^{1/(k+1)}}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t^{k+1}}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant(\log z)/(\log 2)}\frac{z^{-k/(k+1)}}{k}\ll\sum_{k\leqslant(\log z)/(\log 2)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}\ll\frac{\log z}{\sqrt{z}},

hence,
\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)=o(1)
.
Since
m_{p}(z)=[\log z/\log p]
we infer that
m_{p}(z)\to\infty
as
z\to\infty
and
p
is fixed.
Fix any
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
z
such that

\left|\displaystyle\sigma(f)-W_{z}^{-n+1}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\rho(sf(\mathbf{t}),W_{z})\right|<\varepsilon\ \ \textrm{ and }\ \ |\widetilde{\varepsilon}(z)+z^{-c}|<\varepsilon.

Hence, by Theorem
1.1
we obtain

\displaystyle\Bigg|\frac{1}{P^{n}\displaystyle\int\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}k(sf(\mathbf{t}))-\sigma(f)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\ll\varepsilon+\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{P^{d}\displaystyle\int\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}}(P^{-\delta}+\varepsilon)+W_{z}\frac{E(bP^{d},W_{z})}{P^{d}\displaystyle\int\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}},

where the integral is over
\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}
with
sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}
and the
implied constant depends only on
f
and
k
.
By the last two assumptions in the theorem the error term becomes

\ll\varepsilon+P^{-\delta}+\varepsilon+W_{z}\frac{E(bP^{d},W_{z})}{P^{d}\displaystyle\int_{1}^{bP^{d}}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t}.

Using the third assumption shows that for
P
big enough, we have

\Bigg|\frac{1}{P^{n}\displaystyle\int\omega(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap P\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>0\end{subarray}}k(sf(\mathbf{t}))-\sigma(f)\Bigg|\ll\varepsilon,

where the implied constant depends at most on
f
and
k
.
Taking arbitrary small

concludes the proof.

By

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x\\

where

\varepsilon
.
We apply Theorem

,
hence, we do not need to record the dependence of the error term

.
The right hand side is
\rho(a,q)x\log x+O(x)

and the implied constant depends at most on
q
and
a
.
Dividing through by

and letting

\rho(a,q)\geqslant 0
.
Letting

one has

E(x,q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}\tau(m)-\rho(a,q)\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t=O(x).

It is now easy to verify all remaining assumptions of Theorem
1.2
.
The real density in the main term is

\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\log\left(P^{d}sf(\mathbf{t})\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}=d(\log P)\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}:sf(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d})+O(1).

This is asymptotic to
d(\log P)\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}:sf(\mathbf{t})>0)
since

\mathrm{vol}(\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}:|f(\mathbf{t})|\ll P^{-d})\ll P^{-d}
by
[
8
, Lemma 1.19]
.

It remains to study
\sigma(f)
. Since

is multiplicative with respect to

we write
\sigma(f)
as

\prod_{p=2}^{\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}p^{-(n-1)m}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\rho(sf(\mathbf{t}),p^{m})=\prod_{p=2}^{\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}p^{-nm}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\sum_{r=0}^{m}p^{-r}c_{p^{r}}(sf(\mathbf{t})).

Using the fact that
c_{p^{r}}(a)=c_{p^{r}}(b)
when

we obtain

(3.1)

\sum_{r=0}^{m}p^{-r(n+1)}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}c_{p^{r}}(sf(\mathbf{x}))=\sum_{r=0}^{m}p^{-r(n+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{r}}.

By (
2.6
) we have the following for each
m\geqslant 1
,

\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant m}p^{-rn}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{r}}=\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})=0\}}{p^{(n-1)m}}=:\Gamma_{m}

from which it immediately follows that for all
m\geqslant 1
one has

p^{-mn}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z}\\
p\nmid a\end{subarray}}S_{a,p^{m}}=\Gamma_{m}-\Gamma_{m-1}.

Therefore, (
3.1
) is equal to

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{r=1}^{m}p^{-r}(\Gamma_{r}-\Gamma_{r-1})=\frac{\Gamma_{m}}{p^{m+1}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{\Gamma_{k}}{p^{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{m}}{p^{m+1}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k})^{n}:f(\mathbf{t})=0\}}{p^{nk}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{m}}{p^{m+1}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\frac{1}{p^{nm}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m})^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\mathds{1}(p^{k}\mid f(\mathbf{t})).

Recalling the definition of
\tau_{p^{m}}(n)
and taking the limit as
m\to\infty
,
this becomes

\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{p^{nm}}\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m})^{n}}\tau_{p^{m}}(f(\mathbf{t})),

which concludes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

3.4.
A shifted convolution problem in arithmetic progressions

Let
\chi
be the non-principal Dirichlet character modulo
4
so that
Dirichlet convolution
1\ast\chi(n)
equals
4
times the number of representations of
n
as a sum of two integer squares for any integer
n
. For
a,q\in\mathbb{N}
we
shall estimate

(3.2)

{S(x;q,a):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}r(m)r(m+1)}

under the conditions

(3.3)

4\mid q,\ \ v_{p}(a)\leqslant v_{p}(q)-1\ \forall p\mid q.

The case
q=1
can be treated using a variety of methods,
for example, the first proof
of Estermann
[
21
]

uses Kloosterman sums
while the proofs
in
[
13
]

and
[
28
, Corollary 15.12, part (2)]
use
spectral methods. We were unable to locate a
reference that provides an explicit error term for general
arithmetic progressions and we therefore give a proof here.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all
a,q\in\mathbb{N},x\geqslant 1

satisfying
q\leqslant x^{1/2}

and (
3.3
) we have

S(x;q,a)=\pi^{2}x\frac{\eta_{q}(a)\eta_{q}(a+1)}{q^{3}}\mathds{1}(a\equiv 0,1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right))\prod_{p\nmid q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)+O\left(x^{5/6}(\log x)^{20}q^{3/2}\tau(q)^{4}\right),

where the implied constant is absolute
and for any
b
integer

(3.4)

\eta_{q}(b):=\sharp\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2}:y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv b\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\right\}.

As a first step we recall that
r(m)
vanishes for
m\equiv 3\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)
, thus,

(3.5)

{S(x;q,a)=S_{0}(x;q,a)+S_{1}(x;q,a),}

where

S_{i}(x;q,a):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x,m\equiv i\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}r(m)r(m+1).

Indeed, either
m
is odd and for
r(m)r(m+1)
not to vanish,
m\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)
or
m+1
is odd and for
r(m)r(m+1)
not to vanish,
m\equiv 0\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)
.
By Dirichlet’s hyperbola trick
when
k
is odd we have

\frac{1}{4}r(k)=\sum_{de=k}\chi(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid k\\
d\leqslant\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}\chi(d)+\chi(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\mid k\\
e<k/\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}\chi(e).

This shows that

(3.6)

{S_{1}(x;q,a)=4S_{1}^{-}(x;q,a)+4S_{1}^{+}(x;q,a),}

where

S_{1}^{-}(x;q,a):=\sum_{d\leqslant\sqrt{x}}\chi(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x,m\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right),d\mid m\end{subarray}}r(m+1)

and

S_{1}^{+}(x;q,a):=\sum_{e\leqslant\sqrt{x}}\chi(e)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in(e\sqrt{x},x],m\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)\\
m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right),e\mid m\end{subarray}}r(m+1).

Since
4\mid q
by (
3.3
)
we infer that
the three congruences in the sums
S_{1}^{-},S_{1}^{+}
are soluble
if and only if

4\mid a-1
and
\gcd(d,q)\mid a.
Denoting the least common multiple by
[\cdot,\cdot]
we see that under the aforementioned conditions there exists a unique

t\in\mathbb{Z}/[d,q]\mathbb{Z}
such that

(3.7)

m\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right),m\equiv a\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right),m\equiv 0\left(\textnormal{mod}\ d\right)\iff m\equiv t\left(\textnormal{mod}\ [d,q]\right).

Hence,

S_{1}^{-}(x;q,a)=\mathds{1}(a\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant\sqrt{x}\\
\gcd(d,q)\mid a\end{subarray}}\chi(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x\\
m\equiv t\left(\textnormal{mod}\ [d,q]\right)\end{subarray}}r(m+1).

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
a,q
satisfying (
3.3
) and all
x\geqslant 1
we have

S_{1}^{-}(x;q,a)=\mathds{1}(a\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right))\pi x\mathscr{M}++O\left(q\tau(q)^{4}x^{5/6}(\log x)^{19}\right),

where the implied constant is absolute and

\mathscr{M}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant\sqrt{x}\\
\gcd(d,q)\mid a\end{subarray}}\chi(d)\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/[d,q]\mathbb{Z})^{2}:y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv a+1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right),y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ d\right)\right\}}{[d,q]^{2}}.

Proof.

Applying
[
42
, Theorem, page 262]
gives
the error term

\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}\left(\left([d,q]^{1/2}+x^{1/3}\right)\gcd(a,[d,q])^{1/2}\tau([d,q])^{4}(\log x)^{4}\right).

By (
3.3
) we
have
\gcd(a,[d,q])\leqslant q
. Indeed, if a prime
p\mid a,d
but
p\nmid q
, since
d\mid m
, we deduce that
p\mid q
which is a contradiction. Hence, the error term is

\ll q^{1/2}\tau(q)^{4}(\log x)^{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant\sqrt{x}\end{subarray}}\left((dq)^{1/2}+x^{1/3}\right)\tau(d)^{4}\ll q\tau(q)^{4}x^{5/6}(\log x)^{19}.

The main term supplied by
[
42
, Theorem, page 262]

equals

\mathds{1}(a\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right))\pi x\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant\sqrt{x}\\
\gcd(d,q)\mid a\end{subarray}}\chi(d)\frac{\eta_{t+1}([d,q])}{[d,q]^{2}},

which takes the required shape in light of (
3.7
) and the fact that the congruence modulo 4 is implied by the one modulo
q
provided that
a\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right).

∎

One can similarly prove the estimate

(3.8)

{S_{1}^{+}(x;q,a)=\mathds{1}(a\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right))\pi\left(x\mathscr{M}-E\sqrt{x}\right)+O\left(q\tau(q)^{4}x^{5/6}(\log x)^{19}\right),}

where the implied constant is absolute and

E:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\leqslant\sqrt{x}\\
\gcd(e,q)\mid a\end{subarray}}\chi(e)e\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/[e,q]\mathbb{Z})^{2}:y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv a+1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right),y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ d\right)\right\}}{[e,q]^{2}}.

Taking into account the oscillation of
\chi(e)
, we next show that
E
makes a negligible contribution.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
a,q
satisfying (
3.3
) and all

we have

E\ll q^{3/2}(\log q)\tau(q)(\log x),

where the implied constant is absolute.

Proof.

Write
e=e_{0}e_{1}
where
e_{1}
is coprime to
q
and each prime factor
of
e_{0}
divides
q
. Then the condition
\gcd(e,q)\mid a
becomes

\gcd(e_{0},q)\mid a
and by (
3.3
)
we infer that
e_{0}\mid q
.
Thus,

E=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{0}\leqslant\sqrt{x}\\
e_{0}\mid\gcd(a,q)\end{subarray}}\chi(e_{0})e_{0}\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2}:y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv a+1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\right\}}{q^{2}}E_{1}(\sqrt{x}/e_{0}),

where

E_{1}(T):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\leqslant T\\
\gcd(e_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(e_{1})\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/e_{1}\mathbb{Z})^{2}:y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ e_{1}\right)\right\}}{e_{1}}.

By
[
25
, pages 27–28]
we infer that
E_{1}(T)
equals

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\leqslant T\\
\gcd(e_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(e_{1})\prod_{p\mid e_{1}}\left(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant T\\
\gcd(k,q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(k)\chi(k)}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\leqslant T,k\mid e_{1}\\
\gcd(e_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(e_{1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant T\\
\gcd(k,q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\leqslant T/k\\
\gcd(t,q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(t).

We may view
\mathds{1}(\gcd(t,q)=1)\chi(t)
as a character modulo
q
(remember here that
4\mid q
)
in

. Hence, the Pólya–Vinogradov estimate gives the bound
E_{1}(T)\ll\sqrt{q}(\log q)(\log T)
. Hence,

E\ll\sqrt{q}(\log q)(\log x)\sum_{e_{0}\mid\gcd(a,q)}e_{0}\ll q^{3/2}(\log q)(\log x)\tau(q)

since
\gcd(a,q)\mid q
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
q\in\mathbb{N}
is divisible by
4
, that

a\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)
and that

is such that
v_{p}(a)<v_{p}(q)
for all

.
Then

\sum_{d_{0}\mid\gcd(a,q)}\chi(d_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\eta_{q}(a)}{q}\prod_{p\mid q}\left(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p}\right)^{-1},

where
\eta_{q}(a)
is defined in (
3.4
).

Proof.

The sum over
d_{0}
can be written as

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)\\
p\mid\gcd(a,q)\end{subarray}}(1+v_{p}(a))\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\equiv 3\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)\\
p\mid\gcd(a,q)\end{subarray}}\frac{1+(-1)^{v_{p}(a)}}{2}.

Now we can replace the condition
p\mid\gcd(a,q)
by
p\mid q
in both
products because if
v_{p}(a)=0
then the analogous terms in the products equal
1
.
By
[
25
, pages 27–28]
and the fact that
v_{p}(a)<v_{p}(q)

we then obtain

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)\\
p\mid q\end{subarray}}\frac{\eta_{p^{v_{p}(q)}}(a)}{p^{v_{p}(q)}(1-1/p)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\equiv 3\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)\\
p\mid q\end{subarray}}\frac{\eta_{p^{v_{p}(q)}}(a)}{p^{v_{p}(q)}(1+1/p)}.

Lastly, by
[
25
, Equation (8.4)]
and our assumptions

and

O(v_{2}(q))+\sharp\left\{v_{2}(q)\leqslant t\leqslant\frac{\log x}{\log 2}:2^{t-v_{2}(q)}\equiv r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)\right\}\mathds{1}(v_{2}(r)\geqslant v_{2}(q)).

We can now define

\langle 2\rangle=\left\{2^{j}\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right):j\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Then we must have
r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\in\langle 2\rangle
,
so we can write
r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\equiv 2^{\alpha}\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)
for some integer
\alpha
.
Then the cardinality becomes

O(v_{2}(q))+\sharp\left\{v_{2}(q)\leqslant t\leqslant\frac{\log x}{\log 2}:2^{t-v_{2}(q)-\alpha}\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)\right\}\mathds{1}(v_{2}(r)\geqslant v_{2}(q),r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\in\langle 2\rangle).

Denoting the order of
2\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)
by
g(q)
this then becomes

\frac{\mathds{1}(v_{2}(r)\geqslant v_{2}(q),r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\in\langle 2\rangle)}{g(q)}\frac{\log x}{\log 2}+O(v_{2}(q)).

Note that
v_{2}(q)\leqslant(\log q)/(\log 2)
.
Therefore, if we let

\omega(t)=\frac{1}{t\log 2},\quad\rho(r,q)=\frac{\mathds{1}(v_{2}(r)\geqslant v_{2}(q),r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\in\langle 2\rangle)}{g(q)}

we have shown that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m\leqslant x\\
m\equiv r\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\end{subarray}}k(m)=\rho(r,q)\int_{1}^{x}\omega(t)\mathrm{d}t+O(\log q),

with an absolute implied constant. To use Theorem
1.2
we must verify the remaining assumption regarding
\int\omega
. We will show something more, namely,

(3.10)

{\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{\mathscr{J}(P)}{\log P}=d\sigma_{\infty},}

where

\mathscr{J}(P):=P^{d}\int\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{t}\in\mathscr{B}\\
F(\mathbf{t})>P^{-d}\end{subarray}}\omega(P^{d}f(\mathbf{t}))\mathrm{d}\mathbf{t}.

By (
1.3
) we have

\mathscr{J}(P)=\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}I(\mathscr{B},\gamma)\int_{P^{-d}}^{b}\frac{\mathrm{e}(-\gamma\mu)}{\mu}\mathrm{d}\mu\mathrm{d}\gamma.

By the trivial bound
|\mathrm{e}(-\gamma\mu)|\leqslant 1

we deduce for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and all
\lambda\in(0,b)
that

\int_{\lambda}^{b}\frac{\mathrm{e}(-\gamma\mu)}{\mu}\mathrm{d}\mu\ll\log\frac{b}{\lambda}

with an absolute implied constant.
Hence, by (
2.8
), the contribution of
|\gamma|>\sqrt{\log P}
towards
\mathscr{J}(P)
is

\ll(\log P)\int_{\gamma>\sqrt{\log P}}|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|\mathrm{d}\gamma=o(\log P).

Similarly, the contribution of
\mu>1/\log P
is

\ll\int_{|\gamma|\leqslant\sqrt{\log P}}|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|\int_{1/\log P}^{b}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mu}\mathrm{d}\gamma\ll\log\log P.

Thus,

\mathscr{J}(P)=\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{|\gamma|\leqslant\sqrt{\log P}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\int_{P^{-d}}^{1/\log P}\frac{\mathrm{e}(-\gamma\mu)}{\mu}\mathrm{d}\mu\mathrm{d}\gamma+o(\log P).

In this range we have
|\gamma\mu|\leqslant 1/\sqrt{\log P}
, hence,

\mathrm{e}(-\gamma\mu)=1+O\left(1/\sqrt{\log P}\right).

Substituting in the last expression for
\mathscr{J}
leads us to

\mathscr{J}(P)=\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{|\gamma|\leqslant\sqrt{\log P}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\int_{P^{-d}}^{1/\log P}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mu}\mathrm{d}\gamma+O(E),

where
E
is given by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log P}}\int_{|\gamma|\leqslant\sqrt{\log P}}|I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)|\int_{P^{-d}}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mu}\mathrm{d}\gamma\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log P}}\int_{P^{-d}}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mu}\ll\sqrt{\log P}=o(\log P).

The main term is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{|\gamma|\leqslant\sqrt{\log P}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)(-\log\log P+d\log P)\mathrm{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(\log\log P)+\frac{d\log P}{\log 2}\int_{|\gamma|\leqslant\sqrt{\log P}}I(\mathscr{B};\gamma)\mathrm{d}\gamma=\frac{d\sigma_{\infty}(f)}{\log 2}\log P+o(\log P),

which concludes the proof of (
1.3
).

We are now in position to apply Theorem
1.2
.
Before doing so, we simplify
\rho(r,q)
by noting that

\frac{1}{\varphi\left(q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)}\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant\varphi\left(q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)}\mathds{1}\left(r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\equiv 2^{j}\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)\right)=\frac{\mathds{1}(r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\in\langle 2\rangle)}{g(q)},

where
\varphi
is Euler’s totient function. Hence, we may write

\rho(r,q)=\frac{\mathds{1}(v_{2}(q)\leqslant v_{2}(r))}{\varphi\left(q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)}\sharp\left\{1\leqslant j\leqslant\varphi(q2^{-v_{2}(q)}):r2^{-v_{2}(q)}\equiv 2^{j}\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q2^{-v_{2}(q)}\right)\right\}.

Let
t_{p}(z)
and
T_{z}
be as in Theorem
1.2

and let
w=T_{z}2^{-t_{2}(z)}
.
Each
\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/T_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}
can be uniquely written as
\mathbf{t}=w\mathbf{x}+2^{t_{2}(z)}\mathbf{y}
where

\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/2^{t_{2}(z)}\mathbb{Z})^{n}
and
\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/w\mathbb{Z})^{n}
. We find that

\sum_{\mathbf{t}\in(\mathbb{Z}/T_{z}\mathbb{Z})^{n}}\frac{\rho(f(\mathbf{t}),T_{z})}{T_{z}^{n-1}}=\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/2^{t_{2}(z)}\mathbb{Z})^{n}:f(\mathbf{x})=0\right\}}{2^{(n-1)t_{2}(z)}}\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/w\mathbb{Z})^{n},h\in\mathbb{Z}/\varphi(w)\mathbb{Z}:f(\mathbf{y})=2^{h}\right\}}{\varphi(w)w^{n-1}}.

The first fraction in the right hand side converges to
\sigma_{2}(f)
as
z\to\infty
. By the Chinese remainder theorem the second fraction is

\prod_{2<p\leqslant z}\frac{\sharp\left\{\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{t_{p}(z)}\mathbb{Z})^{n},h\in\mathbb{Z}/\varphi(p^{t_{p}(z)})\mathbb{Z}:f(\mathbf{y})=2^{h}\right\}}{(p-1)p^{nt_{p}(z)}}

and letting
z\to\infty
completes the proof.
