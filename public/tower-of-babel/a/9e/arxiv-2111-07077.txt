Discriminant indicators with generalized inversion symmetry
Tsuneya Yoshida 1, Ryo Okugawa 2, Yasuhiro Hatsugai 1

Abstract
We propose indicators of the discriminant for systems with generalized inversion symmetry which are computed from data only at high-symmetry points in the Brillouin zone. Our approach captures the exceptional points and their symmetry-protected variants without ambiguity arising from the reference energy, which is advantage over the previously known indicators for non-Hermitian systems. As demonstrations, we systematically analyze 3 × 3 3\times 3 -Hamiltonians where the proper choice of the reference energy is not obvious.

II
Brief review of the discriminant and exceptional points

Consider a two-dimensional system which is described by a
2\times 2
-Hamiltonian
H(\bm{k})
.
Exceptional points in this system is characterized by the following winding number
Shen et al. 2018

\displaystyle\nu_{2\times 2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint\frac{d\bm{k}}{2\pi i}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}\log[\epsilon_{1}(\bm{k})-\epsilon_{2}(\bm{k})],

(1)

where
\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k})
(
n=1,2
) denote eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian.
The integral is taken along a closed path.
The operator
\nabla_{\bm{k}}
is defined as
\nabla_{\bm{k}}:=(\partial_{k_{x}},\partial_{k_{y}})
.
Here,
\partial_{k_{x}}
(
\partial_{k_{y}}
) denotes derivative respect to
k_{x}
(
k_{y}
) where
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
are
x
- and
y
- components of the momentum respectively.

The discriminant number is introduced as an generalized version of
\nu_{2\times 2}
to an
N\times N
-Hamiltonian
H(\bm{k})

Wojcik et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2021
;
Delplace et al. 2021
.

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint\frac{d\bm{k}}{2\pi i}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}\log\Delta(\bm{k}).

(2)

Here,
\Delta(\bm{k})
denotes the discriminant of the polynomial
\det[H(\bm{k})-E\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}]=a_{N}(-E)^{N}+a_{N-1}(-E)^{N-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
(
a_{l}\in\mathbb{C}
) which is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(-1)^{N(N-1)/2}\prod_{n\neq n^{\prime}}(\epsilon_{n}-\epsilon_{n^{\prime}}),

(3)

with
\epsilon_{n}
being roots of the polynomial [i.e., eigenvalues of
H(\bm{k})
].
We note that the discriminant is written in terms of
a_{l}
’s (for more details see Appendix
A
).
If the discriminant number takes an non-trivial value for a closed path in the Brillouin zone (BZ),
\Delta(\bm{k})
vanishes at a point inside of the loop.
Therefore, at least two bands touch at this point [see Eq. (
3
)].

Another generalization is the following winding number
Kawabata et al. 2019b

\displaystyle W(E_{\mathrm{ref}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint\frac{d\bm{k}}{2\pi i}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}\log\det[H(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm{ref}}\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}],

(4)

where
E_{\mathrm{ref}}
is chosen as the energy at which an exceptional point emerge.
In other words, for the characterization by
W(E_{\mathrm{ref}})
, one needs to know a proper choice of
E_{\mathrm{ref}}
by diagonalizing the Hamiltonian.
The advantage of the discriminant number is that one does not need to know energy at which two bands touch apriori.

III
Indicators for generalized inversion symmetric systems

Consider a system with generalized inversion symmetry which is written as

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{I}}H(\bm{k})U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{\dagger}(-\bm{k}),

(5)

where
U_{\mathrm{I}}
is a Hermitian and unitary matrix (i.e.,
U_{\mathrm{I}}U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{I}}=\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}
and
U_{\mathrm{I}}^{\dagger}=U_{\mathrm{I}}
).

Equation (
5
) results in the following constraint on the discriminant:

\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta^{*}(-\bm{k}),

(6)

which is proven in Sec.
III.1
[see Eq. (
9
)-Eq. (
11
)].

As we see below, the symmetry constraint on the discriminant allows to define the indicator (
7
) [Eq. (
16
)] which captures exceptional points [exceptional loops] in two dimensions [three dimensions].

III.1
Two dimensions

Consider the discriminant number
\nu
computed along the closed path illustrated in Fig.
1
(a).
The generalized inversion symmetry imposes the following constraint on the parity of
\nu

\displaystyle(-1)^{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{I,2D}}:=\prod_{\bm{\Gamma}_{j}\in\mathrm{TRIM}}\mathrm{sgn}\Delta(\bm{\Gamma}_{j}),

(7)

where
\mathrm{TRIM}
denotes time-reversal symmetric momenta

\displaystyle\mathrm{TRIM}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(0,0),\,(\pi,0),\,(0,\pi),\,(\pi,\pi)\}.

(8)

We note that
\Delta(\bm{\Gamma}_{j})
(
\bm{\Gamma}_{j}\in\mathrm{TRIM}
) are real because of Eq. (
6
).

(a) [(b)]: Sketch of the BZ of the square [cubic] lattice,

\mu=x,y
[

Blue dots denotes TRIM which are invariant under the transformation
\bm{k}\to-\bm{k}
. Red lines in panel (a) denotes a closed path where the integral in Eq. (

Equation (
7
) means that
\zeta_{\mathrm{I},2D}
taking
-1
indicates the emergence of exceptional points.

Eigenvalues of the inversion operator do not explicitly appear in Eq. (
7
), while the indicator computed from the eigenvalues of symmetry operators in Hermitian case.
We note, however, that the product of parity eigenvalues is computed in right hand side of Eq. (
7
), which is discussed in Appendix
B
.

Equation (
7
) can be proven by taking into account the symmetry constraint [Eq. (
5
)].
As a first step, we prove Eq. (
6
).
Let
|R_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle
and
|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle
be right and left eigenvectors of
H(\bm{k})

\displaystyle H(\bm{k})|R_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k})|R_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle,

(9a)

\displaystyle H^{\dagger}(\bm{k})|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{k})|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle.

(9b)

Then, we have

\displaystyle H(-\bm{k})U_{\mathrm{I}}|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{I}}H^{\dagger}(\bm{k})|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}_{n}(\bm{k})U_{\mathrm{I}}|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle,

which results in

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}_{n}(-\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k}).

(11)

Combining Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
11
), we obtain Eq. (
6
).

Now, we evaluate the discriminant number along the path illustrated in Fig.
1
(a).
Firstly, we decompose the integral into two parts

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{0}-\nu_{\pi},

(12)

with

\displaystyle 2\pi i\nu_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\!dk_{y}\partial_{k_{y}}\log\Delta(k_{0},k_{y}),

(13)

\Delta(k_{x},k_{y}):=\Delta(\bm{k})
, and
k_{0}
taking
0
or
\pi
.
Because the inversion is closed for one-dimensional subsystems specified by
k_{0}=0,\pi
, the integral can be simplified as follows
Hughes et al. 2011
;
Hatsugai and Maruyama 2011
:

\displaystyle 2i\pi\nu_{k_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int^{\pi}_{0}\!dk_{y}\partial_{k_{y}}\log\Delta(k_{0},k_{y})+\int^{0}_{-\pi}\!dk_{y}\partial_{k_{y}}\log\Delta(k_{0},k_{y})

\displaystyle=\int^{\pi}_{0}\!dk_{y}\partial_{k_{y}}\log\Delta(k_{0},k_{y})+\int^{0}_{-\pi}\!dk_{y}\partial_{k_{y}}\log\Delta^{*}(k_{0},-k_{y})

\displaystyle=\int^{\pi}_{0}\!dk_{y}\partial_{k_{y}}\log\Delta(k_{0},k_{y})-\int^{\pi}_{0}\!dp_{y}\partial_{p_{y}}\log\Delta^{*}(k_{0},p_{y})

\displaystyle=2i\mathrm{Im}\int^{\pi}_{0}\!dk_{y}\partial_{k_{y}}\log\Delta(k_{0},k_{y})

\displaystyle=2i\left[\mathrm{Arg}\Delta(k_{0},\pi)-\mathrm{Arg}\Delta(k_{0},0)+2\pi N_{0}\right],

(14)

with an integer
N_{0}
.
Here, from the second to the third line, we have used Eq. (
6
).
Taking a principal value [
-\pi<\mathrm{Arg}\Delta(\bm{k})\leq\pi
] results in
N_{0}
ambiguity in the last line.
The above results indicate the following relation

\displaystyle e^{i\pi\nu_{k_{0}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\left[\mathrm{Arg}\Delta(k_{0},\pi)-\mathrm{Arg}\Delta(k_{0},0)\right]}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}\Delta(k_{0},\pi)\mathrm{sgn}\Delta(k_{0},0).

Putting Eqs. (
12
) and (
15
) together, we end up with Eq. (
7
).

In the above, we have assumed that
\Delta(\bm{k})
is non-zero along the path denoted by red lines in Fig.
1
(a). We note, however, that zeros of
\Delta(\bm{k})
on the path but away from
\mathrm{TRIM}
does not change the conclusion.
This is because we can take another path where the inversion is closed and
\Delta(\bm{k})
is finite
Kim et al. 2015
.

IV
Indicators for generalized time-reversal and inversion symmetric systems

IV.1
Two and three dimensions

Consider generalized time-reversal and inversion symmetric systems

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{T}}H^{T}(\bm{k})U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(-\bm{k}),

(42a)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{I}}H^{\dagger}(\bm{k})U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(-\bm{k}),

(42b)

with unitary operators
U_{\mathrm{T}}
and
U_{\mathrm{I}}
satisfying
U_{\mathrm{T}}U^{*}_{\mathrm{T}}=\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}
and
U_{\mathrm{I}}U_{\mathrm{I}}=\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}
, respectively.
In Ref.
Kawabata et al. 2019a
, symmetry described by Eq. (22a) is denoted as
\mathrm{TRS}^{\dagger}
.
In two (thee) dimensions, the systems host SPERs (SPESs) which are characterized by the sign of the discriminant
Delplace et al. 2021
;
Yoshida et al. 2021
.

In order to introduce an indicator for these symmetry-protected topological properties,
let us start with the following constraints imposed by symmetry Eq. (
42
):

\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta^{*}(\bm{k}),

(43a)

\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(-\bm{k}),

(43b)

which are proven in Sec.
IV.2
.
Because the real function
\Delta(\bm{k})
is continuous and inversion symmetric, we can introduce the following indicator for SPERs and SPESs,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{TI}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\bm{\Gamma}_{j}\in\mathrm{TRIM}}\mathrm{sgn}\Delta(\bm{\Gamma}_{j}).

(44)

When
\zeta_{\mathrm{TI}}
takes
-1
, SPERs (SPESs) emerge in the two- (three-) dimensional BZ.

The above indicator also works for systems with another type of time-reversal and inversion symmetry

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{T}}H^{*}(\bm{k})U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(-\bm{k}),

(45a)

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{I}}H(\bm{k})U^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(-\bm{k}),

(45b)

with
U_{\mathrm{T}}U^{*}_{\mathrm{T}}=\mbox{1}\hskip-2.5pt\mbox{l}
because the above constraint results in Eq. (
43
) (see Appendix
C
).

IV.2
Proof of Eq. (
43
)

When the Hamiltonian satisfies Eq. (
42
), we obtain Eq. (
43
).
Firstly, we note that energy eigenvalues satisfy

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}(-\bm{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k}),

(46)

in the presence of the generalized time-reversal symmetry, which is proven as follows.
Let
|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle
be left eigenvectors of
H(\bm{k})
with eigenvalues
\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k})
(
n=1,2,3,\ldots
) [see Eq. (
9
b)].
Then, we have
U_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{K}|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle
as right eigenvectors of
H(-\bm{k})
with eigenvalues
\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle H(-\bm{k})U_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{K}|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{T}}H^{T}(\bm{k})\mathcal{K}|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{K}H^{\dagger}(\bm{k})|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k})U_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{K}|L_{n}(\bm{k})\rangle,

where
\mathcal{K}
is a complex conjugation operator.
This relation prove that Eq. (
46
) holds.
Equation (
46
) results in Eq. (
43
b), which can be seen by noting that
\Delta(\bm{k})
is computed from the energy eigenvalues
\epsilon_{n}(\bm{k})
[see Eq. (
3
)].
As we have seen in Sec.
III.1
, the generalized inversion symmetry results in
\Delta(\bm{k})=\Delta^{*}(-\bm{k})
[see Eq. (
6
)].
Thus, combining this equation and Eq. (
43
b), we obtain Eq. (
43
a).

V
Summary

In this paper, we have proposed the discriminant indicator for systems with generalized inversion symmetry.
In contrast to the previously introduced indicators, our approach captures exceptional points without ambiguity arising from the choice of the reference energy.
A similar indicator is also introduced for SPERs (SPERs) in two- (three-) dimensional systems with generalized inversion and time-reversal symmetry.
Applying the discriminant indicators to
3\times 3
-Hamiltonians, we have demonstrated that our approach successfully captures exceptional points and SPERs even when a proper choice of the reference energy is not obvious.

We finish this paper with two remarks.
Firstly, we note that the previously introduced indicators
Okugawa et al. 2021
;
Vecsei et al. 2021
;
Shiozaki and Ono 2021
are also applicable to skin effects, while our indicators focus only on exceptional points and their symmetry-protected variants.
Secondly, we note that while indicators for the line-gap topology has been discussed for correlated systems
Yoshida et al. 2020c
;
Tsubota et al. 2021
, indicators for the point-gap topology have not been discussed so far.
Introducing indicators for correlated systems is expected to accelerate search of the non-Hermitian topology of such systems which is left as a future work to be addressed.

Appendix A
Details of the discriminant

Consider a polynomial

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{N}x^{N}+a_{N-1}x^{N-1}+\ldots+a_{1}x+a_{0},

(67)

with
a_{l}\in\mathbb{C}
.
The discriminant of
f(x)
(
\mathrm{Disc}[f(x)]
) is proportional to the resultant of
f(x)
and
f^{\prime}(x):=\partial_{x}f(x)

\displaystyle(-1)^{N(N-1)/2}a_{N}\mathrm{Disc}[f(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}[f(x),f^{\prime}(x)].

(68)

Here,
\partial_{x}
denotes derivative respect to
x
.
Because the resultant
\mathrm{Res}[f(x),f^{\prime}(x)]
is defined as the determinant of the Sylvester matrix,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}[f(x),f^{\prime}(x)]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{det}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}a_{0}&0&\cdots&0&b_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&\ddots&\vdots&b_{2}&b_{1}&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&\ddots&a_{0}&b_{3}&b_{2}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&a_{1}&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
a_{N}&a_{N-1}&\cdots&a_{2}&b_{N}&b_{N-1}&\cdots&b_{1}\\
0&a_{N}&\ddots&\vdots&0&b_{N}&\ddots&b_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&a_{N-1}&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&a_{N}&0&0&\cdots&b_{N}\end{array}\right),

with
b_{l}=la_{l}
, the discriminant can be written in terms of
a_{l}
(
l=0,\ldots,N
).

Specifically, for
N=3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}[f(x),f^{\prime}(x)]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{det}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}a_{0}&0&b_{1}&0&0\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&b_{2}&b_{1}&0\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&b_{3}&b_{2}&b_{1}\\
a_{3}&a_{2}&0&b_{3}&b_{2}\\
0&a_{3}&0&0&b_{3}\end{array}\right).

Thus, the discriminant is written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Disc}[f(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-4a_{1}^{3}a_{3}-27a_{0}^{2}a_{3}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}

(85)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+18a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}-4a_{0}a_{2}^{3}.
