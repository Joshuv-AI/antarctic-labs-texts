Noether invariance theory for the equilibrium force structure of soft matter
Sophie Hermann, Florian Sammüller, Matthias Schmidt

Abstract
We give details and derivations for the Noether invariance theory that characterizes the spatial equilibrium structure of inhomogeneous classical many-body systems, as recently proposed and investigated for bulk systems [ F. Sammüller et al. , Phys. Rev. Lett. 130 , 268203 (2023) ]. Thereby an intrinsic thermal symmetry against a local shifting transformation on phase space is exploited on the basis of the Noether theorem for invariant variations. We consider the consequences of the shifting that emerge at second order in the displacement field that parameterizes the transformation. In a natural way the standard two-body density distribution is generated. Its second spatial derivative (Hessian) is thereby balanced by two further and different two-body correlation functions, which respectively introduce thermally averaged force correlations and force gradients in a systematic and microscopically sharp way into the framework. Separate exact self and distinct sum rules hold expressing this balance. We exemplify the validity of the theory on the basis of computer simulations for the Lennard-Jones gas, liquid, and crystal, the Weeks-Chandler-Andersen fluid, monatomic Molinero-Moore water at ambient conditions, a three-body-interacting colloidal gel former, the Yukawa and soft-sphere dipolar fluids, and for isotropic and nematic phases of Gay-Berne particles. We describe explicitly the derivation of the sum rules based on Noether’s theorem and also give more elementary proofs based on partial phase space integration following Yvon’s theorem.

The classical question “What is liquid?”

invariance theory. In particular the recently formulated force-force

resulting identities are free of the shifting field. Only genuine
system properties are correlated with each other and the framework is
entirely mechanical. In particular forces are at the forefront, as
these appear naturally in response to spatial displacement (shifting)
of the free energy. Forces comprise interparticle, external, and
diffusive contributions and they occur both globally as well as
locally resolved in arbitrary spatially inhomogeneous systems.

only the external force field contributes, according to the following
global second-order sum rule:

\displaystyle\int d{\mathbf{r}}d{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}H_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\nabla V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\nabla^{\prime}V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle k_{B}T\int d{\mathbf{r}}\rho({\mathbf{r}})\nabla\nabla V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}),

(19)

where
\nabla^{\prime}
denotes the derivative with respect to
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
and

H_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})={\rm cov}(\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}),\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}))
is the standard
two-body correlation function of density fluctuations around the local
density profile
hansen2013
;
schmidt2022rmp
. Thereby the
covariance of two observables, as represented by phase space functions

\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
, is defined in the standard way as
{\rm cov}(\hat{A},\hat{B})=\langle\hat{A}\hat{B}\rangle-\langle\hat{A}\rangle\langle\hat{B}\rangle
. Upon carrying out the position integrals the left
hand side of Eq. (
19
) can be alternatively
written as
\langle\hat{\bf F}_{\rm ext}^{0}\hat{\bf F}_{\rm ext}^{0}\rangle
,
where the global external force density operator is defined as

\hat{\bf F}_{\rm ext}^{0}=-\sum_{i}\nabla_{i}V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})
and the
covariance reduces to the mean of the product, because of the
vanishing mean external force in equilibribum,
\langle\hat{\bf F}_{\rm ext}^{0}\rangle=0

hermann2021noether
.

Following the strategy of Ref.
sammueller2023whatIsLiquid
we
here describe in detail the consequences of locally resolved shifting
at second order in the displacement field. We recall that the
functional expansion (
13
) of the grand
potential holds irrespective of the precise form of

{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})
. Addressing the second order term, this implies that its
prefactor in Eq. (
13
) needs to vanish identically:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}\Omega[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}

\displaystyle=0.

(20)

Making the functional derivative that appears on the left hand side
more explicit yields

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}\Omega[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=-\beta\,{\rm cov}\Big(\frac{\delta H[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})},\frac{\delta H[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big)+\Big\langle\frac{\delta^{2}H[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big\rangle.

(21)

The functional derivative of the Hamiltonian,
\delta H[{\bm{\epsilon}}]/\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})
, can be expressed as the negative force
density operator via Eq. (
6
). Then
inserting Eq. (
21
) into
Eq. (
20
), and grouping terms together allows to
formulate the following locally resolved two-body Noether sum rule:

\displaystyle\beta\langle\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\frac{\delta^{2}H[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}\Big\rangle.

(22)

The correlator on the left hand side of Eq. (
22
) is
analogous to
{\rm cov}(\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}),\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}))=\langle\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle
, because the local mean force
vanishes in equilibrium,
\langle\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\rangle=0

hermann2022quantum
;
tschopp2022forceDFT
; we recall the
individual force contributions in the explicit
form (
18
) of the force balance relationship.

Equation (
22
) constitutes an exact sum rule that
relates the dyadic force-force correlations (left hand side) at two
different positions with the mean curvature of the Hamiltonian with
respect to local shifting (right hand side). Hence the left hand side
of this sum rule constitutes an immediately meaningful standalone
physical object, with clearcut statistical mechanical
interpretation. We demonstrate below that this is true also for the
more formal looking right hand side of Eq. (
22
). That
the exact equality (
22
) holds generally, at
arbitrary positions
{\mathbf{r}}
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
, could have certainly not been
guessed easily a priori (we turn to partial integration methods below
and present details thereof in
Appendix
D
).

We re-write Eq. (
22
) via multiplying by
\beta
and
then treating the kinetic contributions separately (see
Sec.
V
). For brevity of notation it
is useful to combine the interparticle and external forces into the
following dedicated potential force density operator:

\displaystyle\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})=\hat{\bf F}_{\rm int}({\mathbf{r}})-\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}),

(23)

where the subscript
U
refers to the total potential energy and we
recall the definition (
9
) of the interparticle
force operator
\hat{\bf F}_{\rm int}({\mathbf{r}})
. The total potential energy
operator is given as the phase space function
H_{U}=u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})+\sum_{i}V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})
. Using the coordinate
transformation (
3
) the
potential force density is obtained via functional differentiation
according to
-\delta H_{U}/\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}=\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
,
i.e. the first functional derivative,
cf. Eq. (
6
), of the potential energy
contribution to the Hamiltonian.

We identify distinct contributions (subscript “dist”) that arise on
the left hand side of Eq. (
22
). These terms are
generated from pairs of particles with unequal indices such that
double sums reduce to

\sum_{ij(\neq)}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}
. As we
will show in detail below in Sec.
V
,
we can then obtain from Eq. (
22
) the following pair
of exact distinct and self identities:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\!\rm dist}=\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\quad+\Big\langle{\sum_{ij(\neq)}^{\phantom{space}}}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\beta u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle,

(24)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\Big\rangle_{\!\rm self}=\nabla\nabla\rho({\mathbf{r}})+\rho({\mathbf{r}})\nabla\nabla\beta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad+\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\nabla_{i}\nabla_{i}\beta u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle.

(25)

The two-body density on the right hand side of
Eq. (
24
) consists of contributions only from
distinct particle pairs and it has the standard definition

hansen2013
:

\displaystyle\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle_{\rm dist}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\Big\rangle.

(26)

The self average on the left hand side of Eq. (
25
) only
involves a single delta distribution in position, i.e., it is
defined such that

\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\rangle_{\rm self}=\langle\sum_{i}[\nabla_{i}\beta u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})+\nabla_{i}\beta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})][\nabla_{i}\beta u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})+\nabla_{i}\beta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\rangle
.
Thereby one can use

\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})=\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})

inside of the integral over
{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
and then drop the common factor

\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
on both sides of the self equation in order to
arrive at the form (
25
).

The sum rules (
24
) and
(
25
) give much concrete relevance to the more
abstract form (
22
). In particular the curvature
terms on the right hand sides are given as explicit averages, which
are accessible in many-body simulations. The kinetic stress
autocorrelations that emerge from Eq. (
22
) are
addressed in Appendix
A
and the energy
curvature contributions are discussed in
Appendix
B
. We point the reader at precise
points in the following argumentation to these Appendices.

IV
Density-force Noether identity

We next consider the correlator of the density operator with the total
force density operator, i.e.
\langle\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle
. Besides being interesting in its own right, this
correlator is relevant for the left hand side of
Eq. (
22
) via the correlation of external and
total force densities, which can be written as

-\langle\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle\beta\nabla V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})
.

We demonstrate two distinct routes to formulate a valid sum rule for
the density-force correlator. First we start from the locally resolved
equilibrium force balance relationship (
17
),

\langle\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\rangle=0
. This identity holds regardless of the
form of the external potential
V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})
, and we can hence
functionally differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to

V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})
. Clearly the right hand side will remain
zero. Differentiating the left hand side yields

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\langle\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\rangle}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=-\beta{\rm cov}(\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}),\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}))+\Big\langle\frac{\delta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big\rangle.

(27)

That Eq. (
27
) holds can be seen by writing out
explicitly the thermal average on the left hand side and functionally
differentiating all dependencies on the external potential as we will
show. As an aside, Eckert
et al.

eckert2023fluctuation

have recently pointed out that for an arbitrary phase space
function
\hat{A}
, which is taken to be independent of

V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})
, one has
\delta\langle\hat{A}\rangle/\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})=-\beta{\rm cov}(\hat{A},\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}))
, which upon choosing

\hat{A}=\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})
generates the first term on the right hand side
of Eq. (
27
); the second term then stems from taking
account of the explicit dependence of
\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})
on

V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})
according to Eq. (
7
) as we
demonstrate in the following. Considering the functional derivative on
the left hand side of Eq. (
27
), from the product
rule one obtains the following structure:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\,\frac{\delta}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Xi^{-1}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\qquad={\rm Tr}\,\frac{\delta\Xi^{-1}}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+{\rm Tr}\,\Xi^{-1}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\Big[\frac{\delta(-\beta H)}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})+\frac{\delta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big].

(28)

The first term on the right hand side gives
\beta\rho({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}){\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})
upon observing that
-\Xi^{-2}\delta\Xi/\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\Xi^{-1}\beta\rho({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
, where the density profile
can be taken out of the trace in Eq. (
27
). The
second term follows straightforwardly as
-\beta\langle\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}){\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\rangle+\langle\delta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})/\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle
when taking account of the fact that
\delta H/\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
. Regrouping the terms
then yields Eq. (
27
).

The covariance on the right hand side of Eq. (
27
)
reduces to the correlation, i.e., the mean of the product of the two
operators,
{\rm cov}(\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}),\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}))=\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle
, again because the
average force density vanishes in equilibrium,

\langle\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\rangle={\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})=0
, cf. Eq. (
17
). The
second term in Eq. (
27
) can be re-written as
follows:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{\delta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\frac{\delta}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\nabla_{i}V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})\Big\rangle

(29)

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\nabla_{i}\frac{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})}{\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big\rangle

(30)

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\nabla^{\prime}\delta({\mathbf{r}}_{i}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

(31)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\prime}\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}_{i}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

(32)

\displaystyle\equiv\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}).

(33)

In the last step above we have introduced the self two-body density,
defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\Big\rangle.

(34)

In typical applications one would re-write the right hand side of
Eq. (
34
) as
\rho({\mathbf{r}})\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
, but due to
the present gradient structure some care is required and we thus keep

\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
in its full form
(
34
).

Collecting terms and recalling that the right hand side of
Eq. (
27
) vanishes identically we obtain the
following compact force-density correlation sum rule:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}).

(35)

Equation (
35
) is a remarkable and
highly nontrival result, given the seemingly complex and
a
priori
possibly highly correlated nature of its left hand
side. However, in reality only the relatively simple local term on the
right hand side remains. Hence for two distinct positions
{\mathbf{r}}
and

{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
the singular right hand side vanishes and
Eq. (
35
) reduces trivially to

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad{\mathbf{r}}\neq{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}.

(36)

We can also conclude, as the distinct contribution vansishes, that at
all positions
{\mathbf{r}}
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
, even when
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\rm dist}

\displaystyle=0.

(37)

We recall that the present proof of the density-force sum rule
(
35
) is based on functionally
differentiating
{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})=0
with respect to
V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
. An
alternative derivation of Eq. (
35
)
can be based on the Noether invariance of the density profile. This
thermal symmetry is expressed as
\delta\rho({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})/\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}=0
. As before we denote the transformed positions
by
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
and consider

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\rho({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})}\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\Big\rangle

(38)

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle+\Big\langle\sum_{i}\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}-{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i}))\Big\rangle,

(39)

where the first term on the right hand side results from functionally
differentiating the probability distribution and it is already
evaluated at
{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})=0
. The second term can be re-written as

\displaystyle\Big\langle\sum_{i}\nabla^{\prime}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}-{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i}))\cdot\frac{\delta[-{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})}\Big|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\nabla^{\prime}\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}_{i}-{\mathbf{r}})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(40)

where we have used the definition (
34
) of the
self two-body density distribution
\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
. The
result (
40
), when inserted into
Eq. (
39
) and recalling that the
left hand side thereof needs to vanish identically, gives the sum rule
(
35
).

That both derivations give identical results ultimately lies in the
fact that the order of differentation is the only genuine difference.
Starting from the free energy as an overarching object and building a
mixed second derivative, where the order of differentation is
interchanged yields:
\delta^{2}\Omega[{\bm{\epsilon}}]/[\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})]=\delta^{2}\Omega[{\bm{\epsilon}}]/[\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})]
where we recall that

\delta\Omega/\delta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})=\rho({\mathbf{r}})
and

\delta\Omega[{\bm{\epsilon}}]/\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}=-{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})
according
to Eq. (
16
).

Lastly, Eq. (
35
) can be viewed as a
special case of the recent more general hyperforce correlation theory
by Robitschko
et al.

robitschko2024any
. Their theory
applies to general phase space functions
\hat{A}({\mathbf{r}}^{N},{\bf p}^{N})
and for
configuration-dependent cases,
\hat{A}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})
, it ascertains the
validity of the identity
\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{A}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\rangle=-\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\nabla_{i}\hat{A}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\rangle
. Choosing the density operator as the observable of
interest,
\hat{A}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})=\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\sum_{j}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})
,
then replacing
\nabla_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})=-\nabla^{\prime}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})
, and identifying

\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
according to Eq. (
34
)
also yields Eq. (
35
).

In summary, we have obtained the density-force sum rule
(
35
), which is ready to use in the
further investigation of the local second-order Noether invariance
structure. For subsequent use we find it convenient to re-write the
density-force correlation sum rule
(
35
) using the splitting
(
7
) of the total force density operator

\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})=\nabla\cdot\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})+\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
, where we
recall the definition (
8
) of the kinetic
stress density operator
\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})
and the definition
(
23
) of the potential force density operator
\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
. After re-arranging Eq. (
35
)
we find

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})-\nabla\cdot\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

(41)

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla)\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+\nabla\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(42)

where the form (
42
) arises from performing the momentum
integrals over the standard Maxwell probability distribution in the
second term of Eq. (
41
) and identifying
\langle\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle=\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})
. Building furthermore the gradient with
respect to the position
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
and transposing [note that the
transpose
(\nabla^{\prime}\nabla)^{\sf T}=\nabla\nabla^{\prime}
] yields
straightforwardly the following tensorial identities:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\prime}\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla\nabla^{\prime})\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(43)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\nabla\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=(\nabla\nabla+\nabla\nabla^{\prime})\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}).

(44)

Equation (
44
) is thereby obtained from re-transposing
(
43
) and interchanging the positions arguments
{\mathbf{r}}

and
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
upon observing the exchange symmetry

\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{r}})
; we recall the
definition (
34
) of the self two-body density
and clearly also
\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{r}})
as is apparent
from its definition (
26
).

The identities (
43
) and (
44
) are now in
a form ready to be used subsequently in
Sec.
V
in the proof of the Noether
two-body fore correlation sum rules (
24
) and
(
25
).

V
Force correlator splitting

We aim to re-write the full force autocorrelator, as it e.g. appears
on the left hand side of Eq. (
22
), via the
autocorrelator of the potential force density operator

\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
, see Eq. (
23
), in order to simplify the
structure of the trivial kinetic contributions. As above, we use the
definition (
8
) of the kinetic stress
operator to express
\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})=\nabla\cdot\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})+\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
. We start with the force autocorrelator
and obtain by multiplying out and re-ordering:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle+\Big\langle\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}_{U}^{\prime}({\mathbf{r}})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\;+\Big\langle\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle+\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle.

(45)

We start with the purely kinetic two-body contribution, i.e. the
first term in Eq. (
45
) and obtain the following
result:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=(\nabla\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla+{\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime})\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

(46)

where we have again used the definition (
34
)
of the self two-body density and we recall that
{\mathds{1}}
indicates the

3\times 3
-unit matrix. In Appendix
A

we lay out the explicit calculation of both the self and distinct
contributions to Eq. (
46
).

The structure of the two mixed terms in Eq. (
45
)
allows to carry out the momentum integrals in a simple way. This
enables us to simplify the two kinetic contributions according to

\displaystyle\Big\langle\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle,

(47)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\nabla\hat{\rho}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle.

(48)

Thereby the momentum averages on the left hand side need to be
carried out explicitly, upon using the Maxwell distribution, in
order to simplify the divergence of the kinetic stress operator
(left hand sides) to yield the gradient of the density operator
(right hand sides). Then re-introducing the momentum average on the
right hand side, as is implied by the
\langle\cdot\rangle

notation, creates no harm due to the independence of the operators
on the right hand sides from the momentum degrees of freedom and the
correct normalization of the average.

Collecting all terms and using Eqs. (
43
) and
(
44
) allows then to re-write Eq. (
45
)
upon multiplying by
\beta^{2}
as

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle-\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+({\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla-\nabla\nabla^{\prime}-\nabla^{\prime}\nabla^{\prime}-\nabla\nabla)\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}).

(49)

Equation (
49
) constitutes an explicit form of the left
hand side of the locally resolved two-body sum rule
Eq. (
22
). We recall that the sum rule
(
22
) balances the force-force correlations on its
left hand side with an energy curvature term on the right hand side.

In order to address this right hand side, we split the Hamiltonian,
expressed in the new coordinates and hence functionally depending on
the shifting field
{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})
, into kinetic and potential energy
contributions,
H[{\bm{\epsilon}}]=H_{\rm kin}[{\bm{\epsilon}}]+H_{U}[{\bm{\epsilon}}]
. The energy
curvature also consists of kinetic and potential energy contributions.
We defer the calculations for the kinetic energy curvature to
Appendix
B
and for the potential energy
curvature to Appendix
C
. The results
are as follows:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{\delta^{2}H_{\rm kin}[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=k_{B}T({\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}+2\nabla^{\prime}\nabla)\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(50)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{\delta^{2}H_{U}[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\langle\sum_{ij}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle+\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\nabla\nabla V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}),

(51)

and we recall that their sum constitutes the right hand side of the
generically expressed Nother sum rule (

We can hence express Eq. (

) by rewriting its left
hand side via the right hand side of Eq. (

) and its right
hand side via the sum of the right hand sides of the curvature results
(
50
) and (
51
). Collecting all
terms yields the following result:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle-\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+({\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla-\nabla\nabla^{\prime}-\nabla^{\prime}\nabla^{\prime}-\nabla\nabla)\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle({\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}+2\nabla^{\prime}\nabla)\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\Big\langle\sum_{ij}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\beta u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\nabla\nabla\beta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}}).

(52)

Re-arranging and simplifying yields:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle=

\displaystyle\quad\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})[\nabla\nabla\rho({\mathbf{r}})+\rho({\mathbf{r}})\nabla\nabla\beta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})]

\displaystyle\quad+\Big\langle\sum_{ij}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\beta u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle,

(53)

where we could finally replace the self two-body density as
follows. In the external curvature term we have

\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rho({\mathbf{r}})
and in the ideal
contribution we could simplify
(\nabla+\nabla^{\prime})(\nabla+\nabla^{\prime})\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\nabla\nabla\rho({\mathbf{r}})
,
which can be verified explicitly by calculating derivatives in

using

One can split Eq. (

Noether structure in bulk

We specialize to homogeneous bulk liquid states, where

\rho({\mathbf{r}})=\rho_{b}=\rm const
and
V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})=0
. The potential forces
then only arise from interparticle contributions and hence

\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})=\hat{\bf F}_{\rm int}({\mathbf{r}})
with the interparticle force
density operator
\hat{\bf F}_{\rm int}({\mathbf{r}})
being given by
Eq. (
9
). We address the distinct sum rule
(
24
) and use the standard pair correlation
function or “radial distribution function”
g(r)
. Here

r=|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|
denotes the separation distance between the two
particles. The relationship to the two-body density is simply via
normalization with the squared bulk density
\rho_{b}
:

\displaystyle g(|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|)=\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})/\rho_{b}^{2}.

(54)

Furthermore we follow Ref.
sammueller2023whatIsLiquid
in
introducing both the force-force pair correlation function
{\sf g}_{f\!f}(r)
and the force gradient correlator
{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(r)
. These are rank-two tensorial correlation functions,
as represented by
3\times 3
-matrices in case of a three-dimensional
system, and they are defined via

\displaystyle{\sf g}_{f\!f}(|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{\rho_{b}^{2}}\langle\hat{\bf F}_{\rm int}({\mathbf{r}})\hat{\bf F}_{\rm int}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\rangle_{\rm dist},

(55)

\displaystyle{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta}{\rho_{b}^{2}}\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}

\displaystyle\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle.

(56)

The force gradient correlator
{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(r)
is also the negative
mean potential curvature, as is apparent from the right hand side of
Eq. (
56
) via
\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})
. One can
hence refer to the correlator also as the mean negative Hessian, with
respect to differentiation by particle positions, of the interparticle
potential energy.

On the other hand an equally valid interpretation is that of the
negative interparticle force gradient. Recalling the interparticle
force on particle
i
as
{\bf f}_{i}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})=-\nabla_{i}u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})
and that on
particle
j
as
{\bf f}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})=-\nabla_{j}u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})
we can re-write the
potential energy curvature as
\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}u({\mathbf{r}}^{N})=-\nabla_{i}{\bf f}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})=-[\nabla_{j}{\bf f}_{i}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})]^{\sf T}
, where the
possibility of interchange of the particle indices in the two latter
expressions is due to interchange of the order of the two derivatives.

Expressing the forces in this way allows to re-write
Eqs. (
55
) and (
56
) in the
following more explicit forms:

\displaystyle{\sf g}_{f\!f}(|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|)=

\displaystyle\qquad\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\beta{\bf f}_{i}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\beta{\bf f}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle\Big/\rho_{b}^{2},

(57)

\displaystyle{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|)=

\displaystyle\qquad\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\nabla_{i}\beta{\bf f}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle\Big/\rho_{b}^{2},

(58)

where in the last term one can alternatively replace
\nabla_{i}{\bf f}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})=[\nabla_{j}{\bf f}_{i}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})]^{\sf T}
, as laid out above.

In the fully position-resolved case, which is appropriate for the
investigation of spatially inhomogeneous situations, where

\rho({\mathbf{r}})\neq\rm const
, suitable correlators are the following
two-body force-force and force-gradient density distributions:

\displaystyle{\sf G}_{f\!f}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\beta{\bf f}_{i}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\beta{\bf f}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle,

(59)

\displaystyle{\sf G}_{\nabla\!f}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\nabla_{i}\beta{\bf f}_{j}({\mathbf{r}}^{N})\Big\rangle.

(60)

Inhomogeneous versions of Eqs. (
57
) and
(
58
) can be obtained via normalization with
the (inhomogeneous) density profile:
{\sf G}_{f\!f}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})/[\rho({\mathbf{r}})\rho({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})]
and
{\sf G}_{\nabla\!f}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})/[\rho({\mathbf{r}})\rho({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})]
.

As announced in the introduction, using the two-body correlators
defined via Eqs. (
54
)-(
56
)
the distinct sum rule (
24
) can be written for
the case of a bulk fluid in the following appealing compact form

sammueller2023whatIsLiquid
:

\displaystyle\nabla\nabla g(r)

\displaystyle+{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(r)+{\sf g}_{f\!f}(r)=0,

(61)

which is Eq. (
1
). Due to the rotational
symmetry of a bulk fluid, the only nontrivial tensor components are
parallel (
\parallel
) and transversal (
\perp
) to
{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
, such
that Eq. (
61
) reduces to the following two nontrivial tensor
components:

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(r)+g_{{\nabla\!f}\parallel}(r)+g_{{f\!f}\parallel}(r)

\displaystyle=0,

(62)

\displaystyle\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}+g_{{\nabla\!f}\perp}(r)+g_{{f\!f}\perp}(r)

\displaystyle=0,

(63)

with the prime denoting the derivative with respect to
r
. In the
chosen coordinate system the matrices are diagonal,
{\rm diag}(\parallel,\perp,\perp)
, with the first axis being parallel to

{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
and the second and third components being equal and
corresponding to two mutually perpendicular directions which are also
perpendicular to
{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
.

More explicitly, in the considered radial symmetry the second
derivative operator in Eq. (
61
) reduces to
\nabla\nabla={\bf e}_{\parallel}{\bf e}_{\parallel}\partial^{2}/\partial r^{2}+({\mathds{1}}-{\bf e}_{\parallel}{\bf e}_{\parallel})r^{-1}\partial/\partial r
, where the
direction that connects the two points is

{\bf e}_{\parallel}=({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})/|{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}|
. This unit vector is
complemented by two orthogonal directions
{\bf e}_{\perp},{\bf e}_{\perp}^{\prime}
such
that all three
{\bf e}_{\parallel},{\bf e}_{\perp},{\bf e}_{\perp}^{\prime}
are mutually
orthogonal to each other. The perpendicular components then generate
the

term in Eq. (

tensor operations can be efficiently performed in numerical work and
we give details about the sampling strategies used in our simulations

For simple fluids, with the particles interacting mutually only via a
pair potential
\phi(r)
, the force gradient correlator
(

\displaystyle g_{{\nabla\!f}\perp}(r)

\displaystyle=\beta g(r)\frac{\phi^{\prime}(r)}{r}.

This simplification is due to the reduction of the mixed derivative

and

contributes and the sums over particles

The fact that the separation distance

between the two

\nabla\nabla\phi(r)
outside of the average, with

; we recall its definition via

26

) and

\displaystyle\frac{g^{\prime}(r)}{r}+\frac{\beta\phi^{\prime}(r)}{r}g(r)+g_{{f\!f}\perp}(r)=0.

Conclusions

In conclusion we have provided a detailed description of the recent
Noether invariance theory for the two-body structure of liquids and of
more general soft matter systems

.
The theory is based on a specific spatial displacement invariance of
the fundamental degrees of freedom of the statistical many-body
physics, cf. Eqs. (
3
)
and (
4

a global operation
hermann2021noether
;
hermann2022topicalReview
;
hermann2022variance

tschopp2022forceDFT
;
hermann2022quantum
, the approach yields
new insight into the correlated pair structure of soft matter

.

We have here provided a detailed account of the theory, giving
derivations, including the treatment of all self-contributions as well
as alternative routes of proof explicitly. We have carefully treated
the contributions that arise from the kinetic energy part of the
Hamiltonian and have shown how these reduce in equilibrium to simple
diffusive terms. As illustrations of the framework, we have discussed
computer simulation results for the Lennard-Jones system in all its
three phases, for the Yukawa liquid, a soft-sphere dipolar fluid, the
Stockmayer fluid, as well as the Gay-Berne model in the isotropic and
the nematic phase. We have also shown results for the
Weeks-Chandler-Andersen liquid, for monatomic water

molinero2009
;
coe2022water
, and for the many-body colloidal gel
former
saw2009
;
saw2011
;
sammueller2023gel
.

The results indicate that the force-based pair correlation functions
reveal much additional insight into the spatial structure over what is
provided by
g(r)
alone. Crucially, neither their computational cost
in simulations nor the graphical representations as mere
distance-dependent plots pose any practical difficulties. In our
present investigation the computationally most expensive simulation
procedure is to calculate the force gradients of the three-body
interacting Hamiltonians via numerical finite differences. As we have
shown it can be sufficient to only consider the two relevant tensor
components, i.e., the radial and transversal directions relative to
the pair distance vector. Hence in terms of simplicity, much of the
appeal of
g(r)
is retained, while the basis in the Noether
invariance also makes for a well-grounded statistical mechanical
foundation.

Our results add to the body of sum rules in statistical mechanics

sammueller2023neural

sammueller2023whyNeural

sammueller2023neuralTutorial

For the reason of being fully explicit, we have formulated the theory

Appendix A
Kinetic stress autocorrelation

The Noether identity (
22
) contains a singular
self contribution which arises from the autocorrelation of the kinetic
stress (
8
) on the left hand side and from
the kinetic energy on the right hand side. We treat this singular
contribution to the sum rule in the following by first considering the
kinetic stress autocorrelator, as it is contained in the form of a
force autocorrelator on the left hand side of Eq. (
22
)
and correspondingly occuring (multiplied by
\beta
) on the left hand
side of Eq. (
46
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\beta^{2}\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\sum_{i}\frac{{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}}{m}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\sum_{j}\frac{{\bf p}_{j}{\bf p}_{j}}{m}\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\Big\rangle.

(68)

The self part of Eq. (
68
) consists only of the cases

i=j
in the double sum. We can hence simplify
Eq. (
68
) according to:

\displaystyle\nabla\nabla^{\prime}:\beta^{2}\Big\langle\sum_{i}\frac{{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}}{m^{2}}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\Big\rangle.

(69)

Switching to index notation and denoting Cartesian components by Greek
indices, the
\alpha\gamma
-component of this second-rank tensor is:

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu\xi}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla^{\prime}_{\xi}\beta^{2}\Big\langle\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{\alpha}p_{i}^{\gamma}p_{i}^{\nu}p_{i}^{\xi}}{m^{2}}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\Big\rangle,

(70)

where the sums over the Greek indices run over all Cartesian
components. Carrying out the momentum average over the momentum
tetradic in the integrand requires the following integral over the
Maxwell distribution

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}}{m^{2}}\int d{\bf p}_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm e}^{-\beta{\bf p}_{i}^{2}/(2m)}}{(2\pi m/\beta)^{3/2}}p_{i}^{\alpha}p_{i}^{\gamma}p_{i}^{\nu}p_{i}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\gamma\xi}+\delta_{\alpha\xi}\delta_{\gamma\nu}+\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\nu\xi},

(71)

where the combination of the six Kronecker delta symbols on the right
hand side is the isotropic tensor of rank four. The Maxwellian in
Eq. (
71
) is normalized,
\int d{\bf p}_{i}{\rm e}^{-\beta{\bf p}_{i}^{2}/(2m)}/(2\pi m/\beta)^{3/2}=1
. In the derivation of
Eq. (
71
) one requires both the second moment of each
(
\alpha
th) momentum component,
\int d{\bf p}_{i}(p_{i}^{\alpha})^{2}{\rm e}^{-\beta{\bf p}_{i}^{2}/(2m)}/(2\pi m/\beta)^{3/2}=m/\beta
, and the fourth moment,

\int d{\bf p}_{i}(p_{i}^{\alpha})^{4}{\rm e}^{-\beta{\bf p}_{i}^{2}/(2m)}/(2\pi m/\beta)^{3/2}=3m^{2}/\beta^{2}
.

Recalling the derivative structure of Eq. (
70
)
we contract Eq. (
71
) with
\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\xi}^{\prime}
,
which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu\xi}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla^{\prime}_{\xi}

\displaystyle(\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\gamma\xi}+\delta_{\alpha\xi}\delta_{\gamma\nu}+\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\nu\xi})

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\prime}_{\gamma}+\nabla^{\prime}_{\alpha}\nabla_{\gamma}+\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime})

(72)

\displaystyle=(\nabla\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla+{\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime})_{\alpha\gamma}.

(73)

Using this result we can simplify the expression
(
70
) as

\displaystyle(\nabla\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla+{\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime})\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\Big\rangle.

(74)

Hence in summary we obtain the following self contribution:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\rm self}

\displaystyle=(\nabla\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla+{\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime})\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(75)

where we recall the definition (
34
) of the
two-body self density:
\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})=\langle\sum_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\rangle
.

We next consider the distinct part of Eq. (
68
), which
consists of the cases
i\neq j
. In index notation its

\alpha\gamma
-component is

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu\xi}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla^{\prime}_{\xi}\beta^{2}\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\frac{p^{\alpha}_{i}p^{\gamma}_{j}p^{\nu}_{i}p^{\xi}_{j}}{m^{2}}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\Big\rangle.

(76)

As before we carry out the momentum average which requires the
following momentum integrals of particles
i
and
j
:

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}}{m^{2}}\int d{\bf p}_{i}\frac{e^{-\beta{\bf p}_{i}^{2}/(2m)}}{(2\pi m/\beta)^{3/2}}p^{\alpha}_{i}p^{\nu}_{i}\int d{\bf p}_{j}\frac{e^{-\beta{\bf p}_{j}^{2}/(2m)}}{(2\pi m/\beta)^{3/2}}p^{\gamma}_{j}p^{\xi}_{j}

\displaystyle\quad=\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\gamma\xi}.

(77)

Using this result we can re-write Eq. (
76
) upon simplifying
according to
\sum_{\nu\xi}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla^{\prime}_{\xi}\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\gamma\xi}=\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\prime}_{\gamma}
to
hence determine the distinct contribution of the kinetic force
autocorrelator as:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\text{dist}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})\Big\rangle

(78)

\displaystyle\qquad=\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(79)

where we have used the definition (
26
) of
the distinct two-body density.

Hence adding the self contribution (
75
) and the
distinct results (
79
) we obtain the desired total
kinetic force autocorrelator, multiplied by
\beta^{2}
, as the
following expression:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\nabla\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\beta\hat{\bm{\tau}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=(\nabla\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla+{\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime})\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(80)

which we reproduce as Eq. (
46
) in
Sec.
V
of the main text.

Appendix B
Kinetic energy curvature

To second order the momentum transformation
(
4
) can be written as

\displaystyle{\bf p}_{i}\to\{{\mathds{1}}-\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})+[\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]^{2}\}\cdot{\bf p}_{i}.

(81)

As a consequence, the squared momentum, as is relevant for the kinetic
energy
H_{\rm kin}=\sum_{i}{\bf p}_{i}^{2}/(2m)
, becomes

\displaystyle{\bf p}_{i}\cdot{\bf p}_{i}\to

\displaystyle[{\mathds{1}}-2\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}+(\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i})^{\sf T}\cdot\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i},+2(\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i})^{2}]:{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i},

(82)

where the colon indicates a double tensor contraction of two matrices

{\sf A}
and
{\sf B}
, i.e.
{\sf A}:{\sf B}=\sum_{\alpha\gamma}{\sf A}_{\alpha\gamma}{\sf B}_{\gamma\alpha}
, with
\alpha,\gamma

denoting the Cartesian components; we have used the shorthand

{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}={\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})
and as before the superscript
\sf T

indicates matrix transposition.

We can use the following identities for the linear and quadratic
contribution in the expansion (
82
). For
the linear contribution, we have

\displaystyle[\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=-\int d{\mathbf{r}}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\nabla\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}),

(83)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})}\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})\Big)_{\alpha\gamma\nu}

\displaystyle=-\delta_{\alpha\nu}\nabla_{\gamma}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}),

(84)

where as above the Greek indices denote the Cartesian components. We
first consider the following quadratic contribution:

\displaystyle[\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]^{\sf T}\cdot\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d{\mathbf{r}}d{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}){\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}),

(85)

of which the second functional derivative is:

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\big\{[\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]^{\sf T}\cdot\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})\big\}\Big)_{\alpha\gamma\nu\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})

\displaystyle\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\gamma\xi},

(86)

where as before
\nabla^{\prime}
indicates the derivative with respect to

{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
. The further quadratic term is:

\displaystyle[\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]^{2}=\int d{\mathbf{r}}d{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}){\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\cdot\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}).

(87)

and its second functional derivative with respect to the shifting
field is:

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}[\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}_{i})]^{2}\Big)_{\alpha\gamma\nu\xi}=

\displaystyle\quad\big(\delta_{\alpha\xi}\nabla_{\gamma}\nabla_{\nu}^{\prime}+\delta_{\gamma\xi}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\alpha}^{\prime}\big)\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}).

(88)

We next build the second functional derivative of the transformed
kinetic energy:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}H_{\rm kin}[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\sum_{i}[(\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i})^{\sf T}\cdot\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}+2(\nabla_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i})^{2}]:\frac{{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}}{2m}

(89)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\Big(\frac{{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}}{m}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}+\nabla^{\prime}\nabla\cdot\frac{{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}}{m}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{{\bf p}_{i}{\bf p}_{i}}{m}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\nabla\Big)\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}),

(90)

where we have used Eqs. (
86
) and (
88
)
in the second step and the factors of 2 have cancelled. The double
contraction of a fourth- and a second-rank tensor is thereby defined
as
({\sf A}:{\sf B})_{\alpha\gamma}=\sum_{\nu\xi}{\sf A}_{\alpha\gamma\nu\xi}{\sf B}_{\xi\nu}
. Building the thermal
equilibrium average of Eq. (
90
) and
calculating thereby the momentum integrals explicitly yields upon
simplifying the following final compact result:

\displaystyle\Big\langle

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}H_{\rm kin}[{\bm{\epsilon}}]}{\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}})\delta{\bm{\epsilon}}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})}\Big|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}=0}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=k_{B}T({\mathds{1}}\nabla\cdot\nabla^{\prime}+2\nabla^{\prime}\nabla)\rho_{2}^{\rm self}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}),

(91)

which is reproduced as Eq. (
50
) in the main
text.

Potential energy curvature

Appendix D
Sum rules via partial integration

As a means of independent verification, we describe an alternative
method to obtain the second-order sum rules, based on more elementary,
but very explicit calculations that do not rely on Noether
invariance. On the downside the thermal symmetry remains hidden.

The argumentation simply rests on suitably chosen integration by parts
in phase space. For a given observable
\hat{A}({\mathbf{r}}^{N},{\bf p}^{N})
the
underlying mechanism is based on two identities. One of them is the
Yvon theorem
hansen2013
;
rotenberg2020
:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\nabla_{i}\hat{A}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\hat{A}\nabla_{i}\beta H\Big\rangle,

(96)

which can be shown simply by writing out the expression on the left
hand side as
\langle\nabla_{i}\hat{A}\rangle={\rm Tr}\,\Xi^{-1}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\nabla_{i}\hat{A}=-{\rm Tr}\,\hat{A}\Xi^{-1}\nabla_{i}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}={\rm Tr}\,\hat{A}\Xi^{-1}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\nabla_{i}\beta H=\langle\hat{A}\nabla_{i}\beta H\rangle
, which is the right hand side
of Eq. (
96
). In the second step we have assumed
that any boundary terms from the partial integration vanish, because
the external potential contains e.g. contributions from impenetrable
container walls.

The second identity involves an additional phase space function
\hat{B}
. We have

\displaystyle\Big\langle\hat{A}\nabla_{i}\hat{B}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\hat{A}\hat{B}\nabla_{i}\beta H\Big\rangle-\Big\langle\hat{B}\nabla_{i}\hat{A}\Big\rangle.

(97)

The proof of Eq. (
97
) rests on analogous
argumentation as above, with additionally taking into account the
product rule of differentiation that generates the second term on the
right hand side of Eq. (
97
). Explicitly, we
start with the left hand side
\langle\hat{A}\nabla_{i}\hat{B}\rangle={\rm Tr}\,\Xi^{-1}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\hat{A}\nabla_{i}\hat{B}
and
integrate by parts, which yields
-{\rm Tr}\,\hat{B}\Xi^{-1}\nabla_{i}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\hat{A}
. Carrying out the derivative gives two
terms:
{\rm Tr}\,\hat{B}\Xi^{-1}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}(\nabla_{i}\beta H)\hat{A}-{\rm Tr}\,\hat{B}\Xi^{-1}e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\nabla_{i}\hat{A}=\langle\hat{A}\hat{B}\nabla_{i}\beta H\rangle-\langle\hat{B}\nabla_{i}\hat{A}\rangle
,
where we have resorted back to the compact thermal average notation.
Upon ordering of the factors in the first term, we have obtained the
right hand side of Eq. (
97
), as desired.

We apply these formal results to the many-body physics under
consideration. We first consider the distinct case. Identifying the
correct starting point is thereby crucial and we take this to be the
double gradient of the (distinct) two-body density
(
26
):

\displaystyle\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\Big\langle{{\sum_{ij(\neq)}}}\delta_{i}\delta_{j}^{\prime}\Big\rangle.

(98)

As before
\nabla^{\prime}
indicates the derivative with respect to
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
,
the double sum is denoted using the compact notation

\sum_{ij(\neq)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}
and the delta
distributions are abbreviated as
\delta_{i}=\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})
and

\delta_{j}^{\prime}=\delta({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{r}}_{j})
. Due to the identity

\nabla\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})=-\nabla_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})
we can re-write
the right hand side of Eq. (
98
)
as

\displaystyle\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}\nabla_{i}\delta_{i}\nabla_{j}\delta_{j}^{\prime}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}(\nabla_{i}\beta H)\delta_{i}\nabla_{j}\delta_{j}^{\prime}\Big\rangle

(99)

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{ij(\neq)}(\nabla_{i}\beta H)(\nabla_{j}\beta H)\delta_{i}\delta_{j}^{\prime}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\Big\langle{\sum_{ij(\neq)}}(\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\beta H)\delta_{i}\delta_{j}^{\prime}\Big\rangle,

(100)

where we have integrated by parts in the first step with respect to

{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
according to Eq. (
96
) and in the second
step with respect to
{\mathbf{r}}_{j}
according to Eq. (
97
).

We regroup the double sum inside of the first average in
Eq. (
100
) by factorizing
\sum_{ij(\neq)}(\nabla_{i}\beta H)(\nabla_{j}\beta H)\delta_{i}\delta_{j}^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\delta_{i}(\nabla_{i}\beta H)\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{j}^{\prime}\nabla_{j}\beta H
.
Taking account of the structure of the Hamiltonian
(
2
) then allows to identify the expression
-\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\nabla_{i}H=\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
as the local potential force
density operator that arises from both interparticle and external
effects, see Eq. (
23
) for the definition of

\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
.

We can now express Eq. (
100
) succinctly
and hence obtain the distinct part (
24
) of
the curvature sum rule, which for completeness we supplement by
re-writing the self part (
25
) (to be proven
below):

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\!\rm dist}=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\rho_{2}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime})+\Big\langle{\sum_{ij(\neq)}}(\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\beta u)\delta_{i}\delta_{j}^{\prime}\Big\rangle,

(101)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\beta\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})\Big\rangle_{\!\rm self}=

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\nabla\nabla\rho({\mathbf{r}})+\rho({\mathbf{r}})\nabla\nabla\beta V_{\rm ext}({\mathbf{r}})+\Big\langle\sum_{i}(\nabla_{i}\nabla_{i}\beta u)\delta_{i}\Big\rangle.

(102)

The identity (
101
) holds for any
{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
and Eq. (
102
) is the corresponding self sum
rule, as derived in the following by a proof based on partial phase
space integration, which is similar to the distinct case above.

Analogously to the starting point
(
98
) for the distinct
correlation identity, we consider the double gradient, but with
respect to the same position
{\mathbf{r}}
taken twice, i.e. the Hessian of
the density profile:

\displaystyle\nabla\nabla\rho({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle=\nabla\nabla\Big\langle\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\Big\rangle

(103)

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{i}\nabla_{i}\nabla_{i}\delta_{i}\Big\rangle

(104)

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{i}(\nabla_{i}\beta H)\nabla_{i}\delta_{i}\Big\rangle

(105)

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{i}(\nabla_{i}\beta H)(\nabla_{i}\beta H)\delta_{i}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\Big\langle\sum_{i}(\nabla_{i}\nabla_{i}\beta H)\delta_{i}\Big\rangle.

(106)

In this derivation we have exploited twice that

\nabla\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})=-\nabla_{i}\delta({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i})
and then
integrated by parts first according to Eq. (
96
) and
then according to Eq. (
97
). Identifying

\hat{\bf F}_{U}({\mathbf{r}})
in Eq. (
106
) and re-arranging
the different terms gives Eq. (
102
).

The present phase space integration route to the sum rules
(
101
) and
(
102
) [or analogously
Equations (
24
) and
(
25
)] is free of any functional calculus as
required in the derivations in the main text. However, the required
chain of these individual steps is not easy to guess and apparently
these, as we argue, very fundamental results have not been written
down in the existing literature. Hence, while the Noether route comes
at the expense of having to engage with some functional calculus, the
very significant benefit is the simplicity of the starting point,
which here is the second-order invariance
(
20
) of the grand potential against
spatially inhomogeneous displacement.

Appendix E
Measuring force-force and force-gradient correlations

We here provide technical details for the sampling of the force-force
and force-gradient correlations as given in
Eqs. (
57
) and
(
58
). Similar to the measurement of the
standard pair correlation function
g(r)
, a loop over particle pairs
is performed in order to calculate an average of a bulk two-body
quantity. However, one not only counts the relative number of
particles separated by a certain distance
r
, but instead also
involves the forces and force gradients that act on the particles in
the calculation of the average.

Let
{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{j}
be the positions of two particles, such that

{\mathbf{r}}_{\parallel}={\mathbf{r}}_{j}-{\mathbf{r}}_{i}
and
{\bf e}_{\parallel}={\mathbf{r}}_{\parallel}/|{\mathbf{r}}_{\parallel}|
. The calculation of
{\sf g}_{f\!f}(r)

proceeds straightforwardly by incorporating the forces
{\bf f}_{i}
and

{\bf f}_{j}
of particles
i
and
j
as given in
Eq. (
57
). The radial and tangential components are
respectively obtained by the following projections:

\displaystyle({\bf f}_{i}{\bf f}_{j})_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=({\bf f}_{i}\cdot{\bf e}_{\parallel})({\bf f}_{j}\cdot{\bf e}_{\parallel}),

(107)

\displaystyle({\bf f}_{i}{\bf f}_{j})_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\frac{[{\bf f}_{i}-({\bf f}_{i}\cdot{\bf e}_{\parallel}){\bf e}_{\parallel}]\cdot[{\bf f}_{j}-({\bf f}_{j}\cdot{\bf e}_{\parallel}){\bf e}_{\parallel}]}{2},

(108)

where the factor 1/2 in Eq. (
108
) accounts for the two
equal tangential contributions of
{\sf g}_{f\!f}(r)
in a
three-dimensional bulk fluid (there is only a single radial
component).

For the calculation of
{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(r)
, we employ numerical
differentiation to evaluate the force gradients
\nabla_{i}{\bf f}_{j}
that
appear in Eq. (
58
), which is simpler to implement
than analytic Hessians of the interaction potential, in particular for
the more complex models such as the Stillinger-Weber or Gay-Berne
potentials.

As before, a splitting of the force-gradient correlation function

{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(r)
into its radial and tangential components is
performed. Assuming
{\bf f}_{j}\nparallel{\bf e}_{\parallel}
, a tangential
unit vector
{\bf e}_{\perp}={\bf t}/|{\bf t}|\perp{\bf e}_{\parallel}
can be
constructed by choosing
{\bf t}={\bf f}_{j}-({\bf f}_{j}\cdot{\bf e}_{\parallel}){\bf e}_{\parallel}
. The radial and tangential parts of
{\sf g}_{\nabla\!f}(r)
then follow via

\displaystyle(\nabla_{i}{\bf f}_{j})_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=D_{i,{\bf e}_{\parallel}}({\bf f}_{j}\cdot{\bf e}_{\parallel}),

(109)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{i}{\bf f}_{j})_{\perp}

\displaystyle=D_{i,{\bf e}_{\perp}}({\bf f}_{j}\cdot{\bf e}_{\perp}),

(110)

where
D_{i,{\bf e}}
is a directional derivative operator regarding the
shifting of particle
i
along the (generic) direction
{\bf e}
. The
evaluation of Eqs. (
109
) and
(
110
) is performed numerically in the
simulations, i.e., particle
i
is shifted by a small amount
(
\sim 10^{-5}\sigma
) to calculate the arising finite difference in
the force
{\bf f}_{j}
of particle
j
.

The above procedure applies to isotropic particles. For anisotropic
particles a simple alternative to construct a suitable vector

{\bf e}_{\perp}
can be based on drawing a random unit vector
{\bf e}_{\rm ran}\nparallel{\bf e}_{\parallel}
. Then
{\bf e}_{\perp}={\bf t}/|{\bf t}|

with
{\bf t}={\bf e}_{\rm ran}-({\bf e}_{\rm ran}\cdot{\bf e}_{\parallel}){\bf e}_{\parallel}
, which can be used in
Eq. (
110
).
