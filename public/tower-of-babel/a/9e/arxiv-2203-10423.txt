The Erdős-Falconer distance problem in the tree setting
Thang Pham and Steven Senger and Dung The Tran

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

For
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define its distance set by

\Delta(A):=\{|x-y|\colon x,y\in A\}.

The celebrated Falconer distance conjecture says that for any compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if its Hausdorff dimension is greater than
\frac{d}{2}
, then the distance set has positive Lebesgue measure, i.e.
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\Delta(A))>0
. In a recent breakthrough paper, Guth, Iosevich, Ou, and Wang
[
6
]
proved that for a compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, if
\dim_{H}(A)>\frac{5}{4}
, then
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\Delta(A))>0
. This result has been generalized to higher even dimensions by Du, Iosevich, Ou, and Wang in
[
5
]
. In odd dimensions, the best known dimensional threshold is due to Du and Zhang
[
4
]
with
\dim_{H}(A)>\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{4d-2}
.

More recently, Ou and Taylor
[
18
]
studied a generalization for the setting of trees. More precisely, let
T
be an arbitrary tree with
k+1
vertices and
k
edges. Assume that
V(T)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}\}
, then the edge set of
T
can be enumerated as follows:

\mathcal{E}(T)=\{(v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}}),(v_{i_{3}},v_{i_{4}}),\ldots,(v_{i_{2k-1}},v_{i_{2k}})\},

where
i_{1}\leq i_{3}\leq\cdots\leq i_{2k-1}
, and
i_{2s}<i_{2t}
if
s<t
and
i_{2s-1}=i_{2t-1}
.

The edge-length vector of
T
is defined by

(|v_{i_{1}}-v_{i_{2}}|,|v_{i_{3}}-v_{i_{4}}|,\ldots,|v_{i_{2k-1}}-v_{i_{2k}}|)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Given a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we say a tree
T^{\prime}
, with vertices in
A
given by
V(T^{\prime})=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}\}
, is isomorphic to
T
if there is a map
\varphi\colon V(T^{\prime})\to V(T)
such that
(x,y)
is an edge of
T^{\prime}
iff
\left(\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\right)
is an edge of
T
. For
x\in V(T^{\prime})
and
v\in V(T)
, we say
(T^{\prime},x)
is isomorphic to
(T,v)
if
T
is isomorphic to
T^{\prime}
and
\varphi(x)=v
. We say two isomorphic trees
T
and
T^{\prime}
with
k
edges are distinct if their edge-length vectors are distinct. Ou and Taylor proved the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given a tree
T
with
k\geq 1
edges and an arbitrary vertex
v
, let
A
be a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If
\dim_{H}(A)>\frac{5}{4}
, then there exists
x\in A
such that the set of edge-lengths of trees
(T^{\prime},x)
which are isomorphic to
(T,v)
has positive Lebesgue measure.

The main purpose of this paper is to extend this theorem to the discrete setting over finite fields and arbitrary fields.

Let
\mathbb{F}
denote an arbitrary field. When we deal with the special case that
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field of order
q
, where
q
is an odd prime power, we use the notation
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The characteristic of
\mathbb{F}
is denoted by
\mathtt{char}(\mathbb{F})
. We also use the notation
X\gg Y
to denote that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
X\geq CY
.

In the setting of arbitrary fields
\mathbb{F}
, the distance between two points
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
is defined by

||x-y||=(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+\cdots+(x_{d}-y_{d})^{2}.

The finite field analogue of the Falconer distance problem is known as the Erdős-Falconer distance problem in the literature, which asks for the smallest exponent
\alpha
such that for any
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, if
|E|\geq q^{\alpha}
, then the distance set
\Delta(E)
covers a positive proportion of or all possible distances, where

\Delta(E):=\{||x-y||\colon x,y\in E\}.

Iosevich and Rudnev proved in
[
9
]
that
\alpha\leq\frac{d+1}{2}
. They also showed that the exponent
\frac{d+1}{2}
is sharp in odd dimensions. In even dimensions, it is also conjectured that the right exponent should be
\alpha=\frac{d}{2}
. Some partial progress on this conjecture can be found in
[
2
,
14
,
15
]
. In two dimensions over prime fields, Murphy, Petridis, Pham, Rudnev, and Stevens
[
17
]
recently proved that
\alpha\leq\frac{5}{4}
, which is directly in line with Guth, Iosevich, Ou, and Wang’s result for the Falconer distance problem. We also note that Murphy et al. actually proved a stronger statement, namely, if
|E|\geq p^{5/4}
, then there exist many points
x\in E
such that
|\Delta_{x}(E)|\gg p
, where
\Delta_{x}(E)=\{||x-y||\colon y\in E\}
is the set of pinned distances from
x
. Our first theorem is a generalization of this result in the form of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Given a tree
T
with
k\geq 1
edges and an arbitrary vertex
v,
let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with
|E|\geq 5\cdot 2^{16k}\cdot p^{5/4}
. There is a subset
E^{\prime}\subset E
with
|E^{\prime}|\geq|E|/2^{24k+2}
such that for any
x\in E^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
which are isomorphic to
(T,v)
is at least
\gg p^{k}
.

Our next theorem addresses the case of arbitrary fields.

Theorem 1.3
.

Given a tree
T
with
k\geq 1
edges and an arbitrary vertex
v
, let
E\subset\mathbb{F}^{2}
with
|E|\gg 1
. If
\mathtt{char}(\mathbb{F})>0
, then we also need the restriction that
|E|\leq\mathtt{char}(\mathbb{F})^{4/3}
. We assume in addition that any isotropic line contains at most
M=4^{-1}(16K)^{-(k-1)}(\lfloor|E|/8\rfloor)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{(k-1)}}
points from
E
. Then there is a subset
E^{\prime}\subset E
with
|E^{\prime}|\geq c(k)|E|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}
such that for any
x\in E^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
which are isomorphic to
(T,v)
is at least
\gg|E|^{\frac{2k}{3}}
, where
c(k)>0
depends on
k,
but is independent of
E
and
q
.

If
\mathbb{F}
is a specific field, says
\mathbb{R}
, then one would expect to have better bounds. For example, Guth and Katz
[
7
]
proved that any set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
determines at least
n/\log n
distinct distances, but for the pinned version, the best current record is due to Katz and Tardos
[
12
]
with the lower bound
n^{0.8641...}
. For
k=2
, the optimal result over the reals was given by Rudnev in
[
19
]
. Namely, he showed that any set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
determines at least
n^{2}/\log^{3}n
distinct hinges. We conjecture that, in the form of Theorem
1.3
, the optimal lower bound should be
\gtrsim n^{k}
. For similar configurations, we refer the reader to
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
16
]
and references therein for more details.

2.
Preliminary: main lemmas

Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
will be proved by induction, but the argument is complicated. The main difficulty arises from the property that a vertex might have many neighborhoods in the tree, which makes the iteration procedure become much harder.

In inductive steps, we need strengthened versions of the distance result from Murphy et al. in
[
17
]
for two sets which play a crucial role. Because of the peculiar effects of zero distances, we will frequently make use of the pinned nonzero distance set,
\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)
, the set of nonzero distances between a fixed point
x
and points in
E
. More precisely, we define

\Delta^{*}_{x}(E):=\{||x-y||:y\in E,||x-y||\neq 0\}.

We first recall the following result from
[
17
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
[
17
, Theorem 4]
)
Let
E
be a subset in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with
5p^{5/4}\leq|E|\leq p^{4/3}
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{*}(E,E,E)
, or in short
\mathcal{T}^{*}(E)
, be the number of triples
(a,b,c)\in E\times E\times E
such that
||a-b||=||a-c||
and
||b-c||\neq 0
. Then

\mathcal{T}^{*}(E)\leq\frac{|E|^{3}}{p}+5p^{2/3}|E|^{5/3}+5p^{1/4}|E|^{2}.

Remark 2.1
.

We note that the constant factor of the terms
p^{2/3}|E|^{5/3}
and
p^{1/4}|E|^{2}
is not stated precisely as such in
[
17
]
, but it can be bounded by
5
by carefully following the computations in the proof.

Using this theorem, we have a result on distances between two different sets.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
E
and
F
be two subsets in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with
5p^{5/4}\leq|E|=|F|\leq p^{4/3}
, then there exists a set
F^{\prime}\subset F
such that
|F^{\prime}|\geq\frac{1}{2^{8}}(|F|+1)
and
|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\gg p
for any
x\in F^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
I
be the number of isosceles triangles of the form
(x,a,b)\in F\times E\times E
such that
||x-a||=||x-b||\neq 0
. We now set
n=|E|=|F|.
It is clear that
I
is bounded from above by
\mathcal{T}^{*}(E\cup F)
. So, Theorem
2.1
gives

(1)

I\leq\frac{(2n)^{3}}{p}+20p^{2/3}n^{5/3}+20p^{1/4}n^{2}\leq\frac{16n^{3}}{p}.

Let
C_{\delta}
be the circle centered at the origin of radius
\delta
.
By noticing that no point
x\in F
can have a zero distance to more than
2p-1
points in
E
, we see

(2)

\frac{|E||F|}{2}\leq|F|(|E|-2p+1)\leq\sum_{x\in F}\sum_{\delta\in\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)}|E\cap(x+C_{\delta})|.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (
2
), we get

\frac{n^{2}}{2}=\frac{|E||F|}{2}\leq\sum_{x\in F}\sum_{\delta\in\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)}|E\cap(x+C_{\delta})|\leq\left(\sum_{x\in F}|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{x\in F}\sum_{\delta\in\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)}|E\cap(x+C_{\delta})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Noticing that the double sum on the right hand side is
I^{\frac{1}{2}},
we apply (
1
) to get that this is bounded from above by

\left(\sum_{x\in F}|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\right)^{1/2}\cdot\frac{4n^{3/2}}{p^{1/2}}.

Combining these calculations, we get that

\sum_{x\in F}|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\geq\frac{np}{64}.

Let
F^{\prime}:=\{x\in F\colon|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\geq p/129\}
. Now for contradiction, suppose that
|F^{\prime}|\leq|F|/129
.

Then we would have

\sum_{x\in F}|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|=\sum_{x\in F^{\prime}}+\sum_{x\not\in F^{\prime}}\leq\frac{np}{129}+\frac{np}{129}<\frac{np}{64}.

This leads to a contradiction. Hence,

|F^{\prime}|\geq|F|/129\geq(|F|+1)/2^{8}.

∎

When the set is of large size, we have much more freedom when bounding the number of isosceles triangles. More precisely, in the following lemma, compared to Lemma
2.2
, we do not require that
E
and
F
have the same size.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
E
and
F
be two subsets in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with
|E|\geq 4p,|F|\geq 8p
, and
|F||E|^{2}\geq 64p^{4}
, then there exists a set
F^{\prime}\subset F
such that
|F^{\prime}|\geq\frac{|F|}{24}
and
|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\gg p
for any
x\in F^{\prime}
.

Proof.

In this proof, we first follow an argument of Hanson, Lund, and Roche-Newton for one set in
[
8
]
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{*}(F,E,E)
be the number of triples
(a,b,c)\in F\times E\times E
such that
||a-b||=||a-c||
and
||b-c||\neq 0
. We now show that

\mathcal{T}^{*}(F,E,E)\leq\frac{|F||E|^{2}}{p}+p^{1/2}|F|^{1/2}\left(\frac{4|E|^{4}}{p^{2}}+10p|E|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

To prove this inequality, we first recall the definition of bisector energy from
[
8
]
. For any two points
(a,b)\in E\times E
, we denote their bisector line by
B(a,b)
, the bisector energy associated to
E
is defined by

Q(E):=\{(a,b,c,d)\in E^{4}\colon B(a,b)=B(c,d),||a-b||\neq 0\}.

Hanson et al. proved in their Theorem 3 that

Q(E)\ll\frac{|E|^{4}}{p^{2}}+q|E|^{2}.

The explicit constant can be computed easily from their proof. Namely, in their Lemma 13, one would have the upper bound
\frac{|E|^{4}}{p}+2p^{2}|E|^{2}
from Iosevich and Rudnev’s paper
[
9
]
. Substituting this upper bound to their Lemma 11 and Theorem 3 would give us

Q(E)\leq\frac{4|E|^{4}}{p^{2}}+10p|E|^{2}.

We now observe that the equation
||a-b||=||a-c||
with
||b-c||\neq 0
can be viewed as an incidence between
a\in F
and the bisector line
B(a,c)
. So our problem is reduced to an incidence bound. Let
L
be the multi-set of bisector lines determined by pairs of points of non-zero distances in
E
. The above observation tells us that

\mathcal{T}^{*}(F,E,E)\leq\mathcal{I}(F,L),

here
\mathcal{I}(F,L)
means the number of incidences between points in
F
and lines in
L
.

In
[
8
, Lemma 9]
, it has been proved that

\mathcal{I}(F,L)\leq\frac{|F||L|}{p}+p^{1/2}|F|^{1/2}\left(\sum_{\ell\in\overline{L}}m(\ell)^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\overline{L}
is the set of distinct elements in
L
and
m(\ell)
is the multiplicity of
\ell
.

We also note that

\sum_{\ell}m(\ell)^{2}=Q(E).

In other words, we would obtain

\mathcal{T}^{*}(F,E,E)=\mathcal{I}(F,L)\leq\frac{|F||E|^{2}}{p}+p^{1/2}|F|^{1/2}\left(\frac{4|E|^{4}}{p^{2}}+10p|E|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

So, if
|F|\geq 8p
and
|F||E|^{2}\geq 64p^{4}
, we obtain

\mathcal{T}^{*}(F,E,E)\leq\frac{2|F||E|^{2}}{p}.

We now run the same argument as in Lemma
2.2
to conclude that there exists a set
F^{\prime}\subset F
with
|F^{\prime}|\geq|F|/24
and for all
x\in F^{\prime}
, we have

|\Delta_{x}^{*}(E)|\geq\frac{p}{24}.

∎

With the same approach, we obtain a similar result in the setting of arbitrary fields
\mathbb{F}
. Before stating it, we need a version of Theorem
2.1
in
\mathbb{F}^{2}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

(
[
17
, Theorem 1.4]
)
Let
E
be a set in
\mathbb{F}^{2}
. If
\mathtt{char}(\mathbb{F})>0
is prime, then we need the restriction that
|E|\leq\mathtt{char}(\mathbb{F})^{4/3}
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{*}(E)
be the number of triples
(a,b,c)\in E\times E\times E
such that
||a-b||=||a-c||
and
||b-c||\neq 0
, then
\mathcal{T}^{*}(E)\leq K|E|^{7/3}
, for some positive constant
K\geq 4
.

The next lemma is in line with Lemma
2.2
with Theorem
2.4
in place of Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
E
and
F
be two finite sets in
\mathbb{F}^{2}
with
|E|=|F|=n
.
Assume that any isotropic line contains at most
M<n/4
points from
E
. Then there exists a set
F^{\prime}\subset F
such that
|F^{\prime}|\geq n^{2/3}/(8(K+1))
and
|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\gg n^{2/3}
for any
x\in F^{\prime}
, where the constant
K
comes from Theorem
2.4
.

Proof.

Let
C_{\delta}
be the circle of radius
\delta
centered at the origin. Let
\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)
be the set of non-zero distances from
x\in F
to
E
. Because we have no more than
M
points on any isotropic line, we have

\frac{n^{2}}{2}\leq|F|(|E|-2M+1)\leq\sum_{x\in F}\sum_{\delta\in\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)}|E\cap(x+C_{\delta})|.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as in the proof of Lemma
2.2
, one has

\frac{n^{2}}{2}\leq\left(\sum_{x\in F}|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{x\in F}\sum_{\delta\in\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)}|E\cap(x+C_{\delta})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

We now observe that

\sum_{x\in F}\sum_{\delta\in\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)}|E\cap(x+C_{\delta})|^{2}

is the number of triples
(x,y,z)\in F\times E\times E
such that
||x-y||=||x-z||\neq 0
. By applying Theorem
2.4
with
A=E\cup F
, and taking into account the degenerate cases where
y=z,
the number of such triples is bounded by at most
K|A|^{7/3}+|A|^{2}\leq(K+1)|A|^{7/3}
.

Thus,

\sum_{x\in F}|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\geq\frac{1}{4(K+1)}n^{5/3}.

Let
F^{\prime}:=\{x\in F\colon|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|\geq\frac{1}{8(K+1)}n^{2/3}\}
. For contradiction, suppose that
|F^{\prime}|<\frac{1}{8(K+1)}n^{2/3}
.

Then we would have

\sum_{x\in F}|\Delta^{*}_{x}(E)|=\sum_{x\in F^{\prime}}+\sum_{x\not\in F^{\prime}}<\frac{n^{5/3}}{8(K+1)}+\frac{n^{5/3}}{8(K+1)}<\frac{n^{5/3}}{4(K+1)}.

This leads to a contradiction. Hence,

|F^{\prime}|\geq\frac{1}{8(K+1)}n^{2/3}.

∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

With lemmas in the previous section, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.2
. We fall in two cases: sets of medium size and sets of large size.

3.1.
Sets of Medium size

Proof of Theorem
1.2
for sets of medium size.

We are going to prove the following stronger statement by induction:

Theorem 3.1
.

Given a tree
T
with
k
edges and an arbitrary vertex
v
, let
E,F\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
with
5\cdot 2^{16k}\cdot p^{5/4}\leq|E|=|F|\leq p^{4/3}
. There is a subset
E^{\prime}
of
E
with
|E^{\prime}|\geq|E|/2^{8k}
such that for any
x\in E^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
that are isomorphic to
(T,v)
and have
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F
is at least
\gg p^{k}
.

We prove the theorem by induction on
k
. The case
k=1
follows from Lemma
2.2
. For any
k>1,
we now assume that the theorem holds for any tree with at most
k-1
edges, then show that it also holds for the case of
k
edges.

Case
1
:
The degree of
v
in
T
is one. Assume that the neighbor of
v
in
T
is
u
. Define
T_{1}:=T\setminus\{v\}
. By induction, we can find a subset
F_{2}^{\prime}\subset F_{2}
such that
|F_{2}^{\prime}|\geq|F_{2}|/2^{8k-8}
such that for any
y\in F_{2}^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T_{1}^{\prime},y)
, which are isomorphic to
(T_{1},u)
and
V(T_{1}^{\prime})\setminus\{y\}\subset F_{1}
, is at least
\gg p^{k-1}
.

We would like to apply Lemma
2.2
to
E
and
F_{2}^{\prime},
but they are not the same size, so we will break
E
up into pieces of the appropriate size. Set
s=\lfloor|E|/|F_{2}^{\prime}|\rfloor\geq 2
. Now, for
j=1,\dots,s,
greedily select disjoint subsets
G_{j}\subset E,
of size
|F_{2}^{\prime}|.

We apply Lemma
2.2
for
F_{2}^{\prime}
and
G_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,s
to conclude that there exists a subset
G_{j}^{\prime}\subset G_{j}
such that

(3)

\displaystyle|G_{j}^{\prime}|\geq(|G_{j}|+1)/2^{8}

and any
x\in G_{j}^{\prime}
, we have
|\Delta^{*}_{x}(F_{2}^{\prime})|\gg p
. Now set

E^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}G_{j}^{\prime}.

Because the
G_{j}
are disjoint, the size of the union is just the sum as
j
ranges from
1
to
s
. Combining (
3
),
|G_{j}|=|F^{\prime}_{2}|
, and
|E|=|F|
, gives that

\displaystyle\left|E^{\prime}\right|=

\displaystyle\sum\limits^{s}_{j=1}|G_{j}^{\prime}|\geq\sum\limits^{s}_{j=1}\frac{|G_{j}|+1}{2^{8}}=\sum\limits^{s}_{j=1}\frac{|F^{\prime}_{2}|+1}{2^{8}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle s\left(\frac{|F_{2}|}{2^{8k}}+\frac{1}{2^{8}}\right)\geq s\left(\frac{|E|-1}{2^{8k+1}}+\frac{1}{2^{8}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{s}{2}\left(\frac{|E|}{2^{8k}}\right)\geq\frac{|E|}{2^{8k}}.

Every
x\in E^{\prime}
, comes from some
G_{j},
so the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
that are isomorphic to
(T,v)
and
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F
is at least
\gg p^{k}
.

Case
2
:
The degree of
v
in
T
is more than one. Then we can partition the tree
T
into two smaller trees, say
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
with
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
edges, respectively, sharing only the vertex
v
.

We will run the following argument for the sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2},
separately. For a fixed choice of
i
, applying the induction hypothesis for the sets
E_{i}
and
F_{1}
, we can say that there exists a subset
E_{i}^{\prime}\subset E_{i}
with
|E_{i}^{\prime}|\geq|E_{i}|/2^{8k_{1}}
such that for any
x\in E_{i}^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
, which are isomorphic to
(T_{1},v)
and
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F_{1}
, is at least
p^{k_{1}}
.

We now pick a subset
F_{2}^{\prime}\subset F_{2}
with
|F_{2}^{\prime}|=|E_{i}^{\prime}|.
The induction hypothesis guarantees that there exists a subset
E_{i}^{\prime\prime}\subset E_{i}^{\prime}
with

|E_{i}^{\prime\prime}|\geq|E_{i}^{\prime}|/2^{8k_{2}}\geq|E_{i}|/2^{8(k_{1}+k_{2})}=|E_{i}|/2^{8k},

such that for any
x\in E_{i}^{\prime\prime},
the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime\prime},x),
which are isomorphic to
(T_{2},v)
and
V(T^{\prime\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F_{2}^{\prime}
is at least
\gg p^{k_{2}}.

It is clear that the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
, which are isomorphic to
(T,v)
and
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F
is at least the product of the number of trees
(T^{\prime},x)
and
(T^{\prime\prime},x)
for any
x\in E_{i}^{\prime\prime}
, giving us at least
\gg p^{k_{1}+k_{2}}=p^{k}
. By running this argument for both
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, we complete the proof.

∎

3.2.
Sets of large size

Using the same argument as in the case of sets of medium size and Lemma
2.3
in place of Lemma
2.2
, the next theorem is obtained.

Theorem 3.2
.

Given a tree
T
with
k
edges and an arbitrary vertex

, let

with

. There is a subset
E^{\prime}
of

with
|E^{\prime}|\geq|E|/2^{8k}
such that for any
x\in E^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
that are isomorphic to
(T,v)
and have
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F
is at least
\gg p^{k}
.

3.3.
Conclusion

Compared to the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
, Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
are stronger in the ranges
5\cdot 2^{16k}\cdot p^{5/4}\leq|E|\leq p^{4/3}
and

.

In the range,

, we simply take a subset of size

and apply Theorem
3.1
to conclude that there exists a subset
E^{\prime}\subset E
with
|E^{\prime}|\geq|E|/2^{24k+2}
such that for any
x\in E^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
that are isomorphic to
(T,v)
and have
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F
is at least
\gg p^{k}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

The idea for the proof of Theorem
1.3
is the same as that of Theorem
1.2
, in particular, we will make use of Lemma
2.5
instead of Lemma
2.2
. For small sets in an arbitrary field, we need to carefully run an analysis on the number of intersection points between isotropic lines and the sets
E
and
F
. We now state and prove a more general statement.

Theorem 4.1
.

Given a tree
T
with
k
edges and an arbitrary vertex
v
, let
E,F\subset\mathbb{F}^{2}
with
|E|=|F|
. If
\mathtt{char}(\mathbb{F})>0
, then we need the restriction that
|E|\leq\mathtt{char}(\mathbb{F})^{4/3}
. We assume in addition that any isotropic line contains at most
M=4^{-1}(16K)^{-(k-1)}(\lfloor|F|/8\rfloor)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{(k-1)}}
points from
E
or
F
. There is a subset
F^{\prime}
of
F
with
|F^{\prime}|\geq(16K)^{-k}|F|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}
such that for any
x\in F^{\prime},
the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
that are isomorphic to
(T,v)
with
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset E
is at least
\gg|E|^{\frac{2k}{3}}.

We proceed by induction on the number of edges in the tree. Lemma
2.5
yields the desired result for one edge. For a tree with
k>1,
we assume that the statement of the theorem holds for any tree with at most
k-1
edges. We will again select two subsets
E_{1},E_{2}\subset E
both of size
\lfloor|E|/2\rfloor,
and similarly select
F_{1},F_{2}\subset F.

Case 1:
The degree of
v
in
T
is one. In this case, we denote the neighbor of
v
in
T
by
u
. Define
T_{1}:=T\setminus\{v\}.
We now want to apply the induction hypothesis for two sets
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
for the tree
T_{1}
of
k-1
edges. But first, we need to verify that
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
satisfy the condition on the maximal number of points on each isotropic line with respect to their sizes. Specifically, we need to check that the bound of no more than
M
points from
F
on any isotropic line will guarantee that we do not have too many points from any isotropic line on either
F_{i}
, in terms of the size of the
F_{i}
, to apply the induction hypothesis. To be precise, we need that

(4)

\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{16K}\right)^{k-2}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{|F_{i}|}{8}\right\rfloor\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k-2}}\geq M=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{16K}\right)^{k-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{|F|}{8}\right\rfloor\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{(k-1)}},

which is true because

(16K)^{\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{k-2}}\left\lfloor\frac{|F_{i}|}{8}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{|F|}{8}\right\rfloor^{\frac{2}{3}}.

Thus, we can find a subset
F_{2}^{\prime}\subset F_{2}
such that
|F_{2}^{\prime}|\geq(16K)^{-(k-1)}|F_{2}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k-1}}
such that for any
y\in F_{2}^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T_{1}^{\prime},y)
that are isomorphic to
(T_{1},u)
and have
V(T_{1}^{\prime})\setminus\{y\}\subset F_{1},
is at least
\gg n^{\frac{2(k-1)}{3}}.

We would like to apply Lemma
2.5
to
E
and
F_{2}^{\prime},
but they are not the same size, so as before we will break
E
up into pieces of the appropriate size. Set
s=\lfloor|F|/|F_{2}^{\prime}|\rfloor\geq 2.
Now, for
j=1,\dots,s,
greedily select disjoint subsets
G_{j}\subset E,
of size
|F_{2}^{\prime}|.
Notice that the
G_{j}
and
F_{2}^{\prime}
also satisfy the condition of having no more than
M
points on any isotropic line, as they are subsets of
E
and
F,
respectively. Because
M\leq|F_{2}^{\prime}|/4=|G_{j}|/4,
we can apply Lemma
2.5
for
F_{2}^{\prime}
and each
G_{j}
to conclude that there exist a subsets
G_{j}^{\prime}\subset G_{j}
such that

(5)

\displaystyle|G_{j}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{|G_{j}|^{\frac{2}{3}}}{8(K+1)}

and for any
x\in G_{j}^{\prime}
, we have
|\Delta^{*}_{x}(F_{2}^{\prime})|\gg|F_{2}^{\prime}|^{\frac{2}{3}}
. Now set

(6)

\displaystyle E^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}G_{j}^{\prime}.

We are going to prove the following claim.

(7)

\displaystyle|E^{\prime}|\geq(16K)^{-k}|E|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}.

To prove (
7
), we see from (
6
), (
5
),
s\geq 2
, and
|G_{j}|=|F_{2}^{\prime}|
that

\displaystyle|E^{\prime}|=

\displaystyle\sum\limits^{s}_{j=1}|G_{j}^{\prime}|\geq\sum\limits^{s}_{j=1}\frac{|G_{j}|^{\frac{2}{3}}}{8(K+1)}=\sum\limits^{s}_{j=1}\frac{|F_{2}^{\prime}|^{\frac{2}{3}}}{8(K+1)}\geq 2\frac{\left((16K)^{-(k-1)}|F_{2}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k-1}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}{8(K+1)}

(8)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(4K+4)^{-1}(16K)^{-\frac{2}{3}(k-1)}|F_{2}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}\geq 4(16K)^{-k}|F_{2}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}.

It follows from
k\geq 2
,
|F_{2}|\geq\frac{|E|-1}{2}
that

\displaystyle|F_{2}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{|E|-1}{2}\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}=|E|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}\left(1-\frac{1}{|E|}\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}

(9)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle|E|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}|E|^{(\frac{2}{3})^{k}}.

Combining (
4
) and (
4
), we get (
7
), as claimed.
This tells us that there exists a subset
|E^{\prime}|\geq(16K)^{-k}|E|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k}}
such that for any
x\in E^{\prime},
the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
which are isomorphic to
(T,v)
with
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F
is at least
\gg n^{\frac{2k}{3}}.

Case
2
:
The degree of
v
in
T
is more than one. Then we can partition the tree
T
into two smaller trees, say
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
with
k_{1}
and
k_{2}

.

We will run the following argument for the sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2},
separately. For a fixed choice of
i
, applying the induction hypothesis for the sets
E_{i}
and
F_{1}
, we can say that there exists a subset
E_{i}^{\prime}\subset E_{i}
with
|E_{i}^{\prime}|\geq(16K)^{-k_{1}}|E_{i}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{1}}}
such that for any
x\in E_{i}^{\prime}
, the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime},x)
, which are isomorphic to
(T_{1},v)
and
V(T^{\prime})\setminus\{x\}\subset F_{1}
, is at least
\gg n^{\frac{2k_{1}}{3}}
.

We now pick a subset
F_{2}^{\prime}\subset F_{2}
with
|F_{2}^{\prime}|=|E_{i}^{\prime}|.
Again, we notice that since
E_{i}^{\prime}
and
F_{2}^{\prime}

E

points on any isotropic line, but we must again check the conditions of the induction hypothesis in order to apply it, as we did when verifying (

(10)

\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{16K}\right)^{k_{2}-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{|F_{i}|}{8}\right\rfloor\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{2}-1}}\geq M=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{16K}\right)^{k-1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{|F|}{8}\right\rfloor\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k-1}},

which is guaranteed as we recall

and see

(16K)^{k_{1}+\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{k_{2}-1}}\left\lfloor\frac{|F_{i}|}{8}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{|F_{i}|}{8}\right\rfloor\geq\left(\left\lfloor\frac{|F|}{8}\right\rfloor\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{1}}}.

The induction hypothesis tells us that there exists a subset
E_{i}^{\prime\prime}\subset E_{i}^{\prime}\subset E

the number of distinct trees
(T^{\prime\prime},x)

and have

Moreover, we also have

\displaystyle|E_{i}^{\prime\prime}|\geq

\displaystyle(16K)^{-k_{2}}|E_{i}^{\prime}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{2}}}\geq(16K)^{-k_{2}}\left((16K)^{-k_{1}}|E_{i}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{1}}}\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(16K)^{-(k_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{2}}+k_{2})}|E_{i}|^{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{1}}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{k_{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq

where the last inequality can be explained by the same argument as in (

It is clear that the number of distinct trees

, giving us at least
\gg n^{\frac{2(k_{1}+k_{2})}{3}}=n^{\frac{2k}{3}}
