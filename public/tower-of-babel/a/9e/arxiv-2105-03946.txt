Markov processes related to the stationary measure for the open KPZ equation
Włodek Bryc, Alexey Kuznetsov, Yizao Wang, Jacek Wesołowski

Abstract
. We provide a probabilistic description of the stationary measures for the open KPZ on the spatial interval [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] in terms of a Markov process Y Y , which is a Doob’s h h transform of the Brownian motion killed at an exponential rate. Our work builds on a recent formula of Corwin and Knizel which expresses the multipoint Laplace transform of the stationary solution of the open KPZ in terms of another Markov process 𝕋 \mathbb{T} : the continuous dual Hahn process with Laplace variables taking on the role of time-points in the process. The core of our approach is to prove that the Laplace transforms of the finite dimensional distributions of Y Y and 𝕋 \mathbb{T} are equal when the time parameters of one process become the Laplace variables of the other process and vice versa.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Background

The Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ)
equation is a nonlinear stochastic PDE, proposed in
[
24
]
as a model for the evolution of the profile of a growing interface driven by the space-time white noise
\zeta
. The interface profile is
described by a height function
h(t,s)
, where
t\geq 0
is a time variable and
s
is a spatial variable, which in one spatial dimension
formally satisfies a singular equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}h(t,s)=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{s}^{2}h(t,s)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{s}h(t,s)\right)^{2}+\zeta(t,s).

(For a rigorous interpretation of this equation, see e.g.
[
20
]
.)
In this paper we are interested in the open KPZ equation on the interval so that (
1.1
) holds
for
0\leq s\leq 1
, subject to the
inhomogeneous Neumann boundary conditions with two arbitrary parameters.
Since (
1.1
) is not well posed and its solutions are nowhere differentiable, this is only a formal concept.
Refs.
[
11
,
18
,
31
]
, define the solution of this problem in the Hopf-Cole sense, where the substitution
Z=e^{h}

converts (
1.1
) into the stochastic heat equation

(1.2)

\partial_{t}Z(t,s)=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{s}^{2}Z(t,s)+Z(t,s)\zeta(t,s),\;\;t\geq 0,\;s\in[0,1].

According to
[
10
, Section 1.1]
, the inhomogeneous Neumann boundary conditions
then correspond to the inhomogeneous Robin boundary conditions

(1.3)

\partial_{s}Z(t,s)|_{s=0}=\tfrac{\mathsf{c}-1}{2}Z(t,0),\quad\partial_{s}Z(t,s)|_{s=1}=\tfrac{1-\mathsf{a}}{2}Z(t,1),\;t>0,

with two
real parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
.

The rigorous handling of equation (
1.2
) with the boundary conditions (
1.3
) is
technical and it is not needed in this paper,
as
we are interested only in the explicit stationary measures
constructed in
[
10
]
. A stationary measure for the open KPZ equation
is the law of a random function

(H(s))_{s\in[0,1]}

with
H(0)=0
, defined by the property that if
h(t,s)

is a Cole-Hopf solution of equation (
1.1
) with boundary values corresponding to
(
1.3
) and with the initial condition

h(0,s)=H(s)
for
s\in[0,1]
, then for all
t\geq 0

we have the equality in distribution:

(h(t,s)-h(t,0))_{s\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(H(s))_{s\in[0,1]}.

As usual, equality in distribution means that the finite dimensional distributions are the same. (Here,
the related stochastic processes have continuous sample paths, so equality in distribution means that they induce the same probability measure on
C([0,1])
.)
Since we do not need the time variable for the stationary measure, we will treat
H
as a stochastic process on the finite time interval
[0,1]
,
and
we will use
t
instead of
s
for the spatial variable, writing
(H(t))_{t\in[0,1]}
.

In the seminal study
[
10
]
Corwin and Knizel
constructed stationary measures for the open KPZ equation with general parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
, and determined their Laplace transform when
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
. (More precisely, formula
[
10
, (1.2)]
has two real parameters
u,v
that enter the inhomogeneous Neumann
boundary conditions; in this paper we use
\mathsf{c}=2u
,
\mathsf{a}=2v
.)
Transcribed to our notation, Corwin and Knizel
[
10
]
proved that for
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{d}\leq t_{d+1}=1
and
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
,
the Laplace transform of the finite-dimensional distributions of
H

evaluated at the increments of a decreasing sequence
S>s_{1}>s_{2}>\dots>s_{d}>s_{d+1}=0
, where
S=2+(\mathsf{c}-2)I_{(0,2)}(\mathsf{c})
, has the form

(1.4)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})H(t_{k})\right)\right]\\
=\exp\left(\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{d+1}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})s_{k}^{2}\right)\times\psi^{(1/4)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}}),

where
{\boldsymbol{s}}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{d})
and
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})
,
d\geq 1
.
To describe
\psi^{(1/4)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})
, Corwin and Knizel

[
10
, Section 7.3]

introduced the
continuous dual Hahn process

(\mathbb{T}_{s})_{s\in[0,S)}
, and expressed
\psi^{(1/4)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})
as

(1.5)

\psi^{(\tau)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{K}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{d+1}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\mathbb{T}_{s_{k}}\right)\middle|\mathbb{T}_{s_{d+1}}=x\right]\mathfrak{p}_{s_{d+1}}({\textnormal{d}}x),

where
\tau=1/4
, constant

(1.6)

\mathfrak{K}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\tau x}\mathfrak{p}_{s_{d+1}}({\textnormal{d}}x)

is computed from a limiting procedure based on
[
11
]
, and
\{\mathfrak{p}_{s}({\textnormal{d}}x)\}
is a family of
\sigma
-finite measures (
C.5
) on
{\mathbb{R}}
, which serve as an entrance law (
C.7
) for the process
\mathbb{T}
.
Here we introduced an auxiliary parameter
\tau>0
,
which amounts to replacing the interval
[0,1]
for the spatial variable in (
1.2
)
by the interval
[0,4\tau]
.
(We shall rely on the specific choice
\tau=1/4
only in
Section
1.3
.)

The family of continuous dual Hahn processes
is determined by two parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}\in\mathbb{R},\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
. The range of the time parameter
s
is delicate and we shall discuss it in the paragraph before Theorem
1.3
.
Transition probabilities
for
\mathbb{T}

are
not easy to describe in general, as they may involve both
an absolutely continuous part and a discrete part, depending on the relation between the location parameter
x
, time parameters
s<t
and
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
.
The case
s\in(-\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
is easier as then, with state space
(0,\infty)
, the Markov process
\mathbb{T}
has transition probability density function (
1.14
).
For the more general case we refer to Appendix
C
.

The description of the law of
H
via (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) is of an analytical nature and our goal is to provide a probabilistic description. Our starting point is expression (
1.5
) for general
\tau>0
. In fact, we shall
show that as a function of
{\boldsymbol{s}}
, expression (
1.5
) itself is the Laplace transform in argument
{\boldsymbol{s}}
,

(1.7)

\psi^{(\tau)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})\widetilde{Y}_{t_{k}}\right)\right]

with

(\widetilde{Y}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Y_{0}-Y_{\tau t})_{t\in[0,1]}

for a Markov process
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]}
from Theorem
1.1
. (In general, a product of a Laplace transform with another function
\psi
can be a Laplace transform without
\psi
being one.)
As a consequence, taking
\tau=1/4
we identify the stationary measure for the open KPZ equation:

(1.8)

(H(t))_{t\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(B_{t/2}-Y_{t/4}+Y_{0})_{t\in[0,1]}.

We note that although identity (
1.8
) is rigorously established in Proposition
1.4
only for
\tau=1/4
and
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
with
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}>-2
, there are good reasons to expect that it is valid for all
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
.

A pair of formulas (
1.5
) and (
1.7
)
is a new example of the
dual representation for the Laplace transform
.
Informally, a dual representation expresses the Laplace transform of the finite-dimensional distribution of the first process as the Laplace transform of the finite dimensional distribution of the second process when the time parameters of one process become the Laplace variables of the other process and vice versa.
Ref.
[
7
]
established such dual representations for the Laplace transforms of Markov processes which are squares of the radial part of a 3-dimensional Cauchy process
[
26
, Corollary 1]
,
and the Laplace transforms of the Brownian excursion and the generalized meanders. Such identities were needed to identify limiting fluctuations for the open ASEP in
[
8
]
. A different (univariate) example of such a dual representation is
[
3
, formula (2)]
. The specifics differ slightly from case to case, compare (
1.15
), (
6.15
), and
[
7
, (1.2)]
.
At this time there is no complete understanding
how such dual representations may arise in general.

1.2.
Main results

We first introduce the Markov process
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]}
(with parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c},\tau
)
based on the Yakubovich heat kernel

(1.9)

p_{t}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu^{2}}K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x})K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{y})\,\mu({\textnormal{d}}u),\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}},\;t>0,

where

(1.10)

\mu({\textnormal{d}}u):=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}u}{|\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}u)|^{2}}=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}u\sinh(\pi u)\,{\textnormal{d}}u,

and

(1.11)

K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x\cosh w}\cos(uw)\,{\textnormal{d}}w

is the modified Bessel function defined for
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
x>0
.

The following result specifies the finite dimensional distributions of the process, which is all that is needed for the dual representation of the Laplace transforms.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
\tau>0
and
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
. Then there exists a Markov process
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]}
such that
for
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{d}<t_{d+1}=\tau
and
d\geq 0
the finite dimensional distribution of

(Y_{t_{0}},\ldots,Y_{t_{d+1}})

has the density

(1.12)

f_{{\boldsymbol{t}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}e^{\mathsf{c}x_{0}+\mathsf{a}x_{d+1}}\prod_{k=1}^{d+1}p_{t_{k}-t_{k-1}}(x_{k-1},x_{k}),

where
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{0},\dots,t_{d+1})
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{d+1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+2}
and

(1.13)

C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\mathsf{a}x+\mathsf{c}y}p_{\tau}(x,y){\textnormal{d}}x{\textnormal{d}}y.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
consists of the construction
of the Markov process, and includes verification that
C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}<\infty
.

Now, we present the dual representation for the Laplace transform for the processes
\mathbb{T}
and
Y
when the process
\mathbb{T}
is restricted to
s\in(-\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
. This simplifies calculations as then transition probabilities for
\mathbb{T}
are absolutely continuous with probability density function

(1.14)

\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)=\frac{|\Gamma(\frac{\mathsf{c}-t+{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{y}}{2},\frac{t-s+{\textnormal{i}}(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})}{2},\frac{t-s+{\textnormal{i}}(\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y})}{2})|^{2}}{8\pi\sqrt{y}\Gamma(t-s)|\Gamma(\frac{\mathsf{c}-s+{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x}}{2},{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{y})|^{2}}1_{\{y>0\}}{\textnormal{d}}y,\;\;s<t,\;x>0.

Here and throughout the paper we use standard notation for the products of Gamma functions:

\Gamma(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})=\Gamma(a_{1})\Gamma(a_{2})\cdots\Gamma(a_{k}).

Theorem 1.2
.

For
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
,
d=0,1,\dots
,

\mathsf{c}>s_{1}>\dots>s_{d}>s_{d+1}>-\mathsf{a}
,
and
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\dots<t_{d}<t_{d+1}=\tau
,

(1.15)

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{d+1}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\mathbb{T}_{s_{k}}\right)\middle|\mathbb{T}_{s_{d+1}}=x\right]\widetilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{d+1}}(x){\textnormal{d}}x\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d+1}s_{k}(Y_{t_{k}}-Y_{t_{k-1}})\right)\right],

where
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]}
is the Markov process from Theorem
1.1
and for
-\mathsf{a}<s<\mathsf{c}
,

(1.16)

\widetilde{\mathfrak{p}}_{s}(x)=\frac{2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}}}{16\pi C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}\frac{|\Gamma(\tfrac{\mathsf{a}+s+{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x}}{2},\tfrac{\mathsf{c}-s+{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x}}{2})|^{2}}{\sqrt{x}|\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x})|^{2}},\;x>0.

The left hand side of (
1.15
) is similar to the expression on the right hand side of (
1.5
), but (
1.15
) does not lead to (
1.7
) immediately. Firstly,
we have
s_{d+1}=0
in (
1.5
), while
s_{d+1}
is not necessarily zero in (
1.15
): in fact,
s_{d+1}=0
is not allowed in (
1.15
) if
\mathsf{a}\leq 0
.
Secondly, for technical reasons, the range of the time parameter in (
1.5
) is restricted in
[
10
]
to
s\in[0,S)
only.
Notice that the process
\mathbb{T}
over
[0,S)
may have atoms in its law (see
[
10
]
for a detailed presentation).
Moreover, the intervals
[0,S)
and
(-\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
may be disjoint. Nevertheless, when
\mathsf{c}>0
(so
S=\min\{\mathsf{c},2\}
), the continuous dual Hahn process
\mathbb{T}
can be actually defined over a larger domain
(-\infty,\mathsf{c})\supset[0,S)
, as shown recently in
[
5
]
and summarized in Theorem
C.2
.
Thirdly, we need to
relate the normalizing constants (
1.6
) and (
1.13
).

These issues are addressed in our next result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
\tau>0
,
d\geq 1
, and real
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
such that
\mathsf{c}>\max\{-\mathsf{a},0\}
. Let
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\dots<t_{d}=1

and
\mathsf{c}>s_{1}>\dots>s_{d}>\max\{-\mathsf{a},0\}
,
s_{d+1}=0
. Then

(1.17)

\frac{1}{\mathfrak{K}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{d}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\mathbb{T}_{s_{k}}\right)\middle|\mathbb{T}_{0}=x\right]\mathfrak{p}_{0}({\textnormal{d}}x)\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}-(s_{k}-s_{k+1})(Y_{0}-Y_{\tau t_{k}})\right)\right],

where
\mathfrak{K}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}
is given by (
1.6
), measure
\mathfrak{p}_{0}({\textnormal{d}}x)
is defined in
(
C.5
), process

(\mathbb{T}_{s})_{s\in(-\infty,\mathsf{c})}
is the continuous dual Hahn process, see Definition
C.1
, and
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]}
is the Markov process introduced in Theorem
1.1
.

We emphasize again that, although the left-hand side of (
1.17
) has the identical expression as
\psi^{(\tau)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})
in (
1.5
), the admissible range of time parameters
{\boldsymbol{s}}
in both formulas is not the same.
So when we apply Theorem
1.3
in the proof of Proposition
1.4
, we need to add an assumption that
\mathsf{a}>-2
to ensure that
[0,S)\cap(-\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\neq\emptyset
.

1.3.
Application to stationary measure for the open KPZ equation

Recall that we denoted by
(H(t))_{t\in[0,1]}
a process with the
stationary measure
for the open KPZ equation as its law. In
[
10
, (1.12)]
this process was denoted by
(H_{u,v}(x))_{x\in[0,1]}
with
u=\mathsf{c}/2
,
v=\mathsf{a}/2
.
We have the following description of the law of
H
.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,1/4]}
be the Markov process from Theorem
1.1
with parameter

\tau=1/4
, and let
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an independent
Brownian motion. If
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
with
\min\{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}\}>-2
, then
(
1.8
) holds.

Proof.

We first consider the case
\mathsf{c}>0
. Then
S=\min\{\mathsf{c},2\}
.
We apply Theorem
1.3
with the sequence
s_{1}>\dots>s_{d}
from the interval
(\max\{-\mathsf{a},0\},S)
, which by assumption is non-empty,
and with
\tau=1/4
.
On this interval, (
1.4
), (
1.5
) and (
1.17
) hold.
It is readily checked that the first factor on the right-hand side of (
1.4
) corresponds to the Laplace transform of the increments of the scaled Brownian motion as desired.
Since
s_{1}>\dots>s_{d}>s_{d+1}:=0
,
taking
t_{d}=t_{d+1}=1
in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
),
we get

(1.18)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})H(t_{k})}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{pre-psi}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}s_{k}(B_{t_{k}/2}-B_{t_{k-1}/2})}\Big]\psi^{(1/4)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{psi}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})B_{t_{k}/2}}\Big]\\
\times\frac{1}{\mathfrak{K}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(1/4)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\mathbb{T}_{s_{k}}\right)\middle|\mathbb{T}_{0}=x\right]\mathfrak{p}_{0}({\textnormal{d}}x)\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{psi-dual}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{\sum_{k=1}^{d}-(s_{k}-s_{k+1})(B_{t_{k}/2}-Y_{t_{k}/4}+Y_{0})}\Big].

Here is the identity (
1.18
) with the intermediate steps:

(1.2.1)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})H(t_{k})}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{pre-psi}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}s_{k}(B_{t_{k}/2}-B_{t_{k-1}/2})}\Big]\psi^{(1/4)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})\\
={\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})B_{t_{k}/2}}\Big]\psi^{(1/4)}({\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{t}})\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{psi}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})B_{t_{k}/2}}\Big]\frac{1}{\mathfrak{K}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(1/4)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{d+1}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\mathbb{T}_{s_{k}}\right)\middle|\mathbb{T}_{s_{d+1}}=x\right]\mathfrak{p}_{s_{d+1}}({\textnormal{d}}x)\\
\\
={\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(s_{k}-s_{k+1})B_{t_{k}/2}}\Big]\frac{1}{\mathfrak{K}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(1/4)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{d}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\mathbb{T}_{s_{k}}\right)\middle|\mathbb{T}_{0}=x\right]\mathfrak{p}_{0}({\textnormal{d}}x)\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{psi-dual}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{\sum_{k=1}^{d}-(s_{k}-s_{k+1})(B_{t_{k}/2}-Y_{t_{k}/4}+Y_{0})}\Big].

Using the fact that the
d
-tuples
(s_{1}-s_{2},s_{2}-s_{3},\dots,s_{d-1}-s_{d},s_{d})
can be selected arbitrarily from an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the first and last expressions in (
1.18
) identify the laws by uniqueness of the Laplace transform
[
17
, Theorem 2.1]
. This proves (
1.8
) when
\mathsf{c}>0
.

By symmetry, this identification extends to the case when
\mathsf{c}
is negative with
\mathsf{c}>-2
. For this argument we need to indicate
explicitly how the processes depend on parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
,
so
by
Y^{({\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}})}
we denote the process with joint density (
1.12
)
with
\tau=1/4
,
and by
H^{({\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}})}
we denote the process
(H_{\mathsf{c}/2,\mathsf{a}/2}(x))_{x\in[0,1]}
in
[
10
, (1.12)]
.

The argument is based on the fact that time reversal exchanges the roles of parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
. From (
1.12
) and (
1.9
) (recall that
\tau=1/4
) it follows that process
(Y^{({\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}})}_{(1-t)/4})
has the same law as the process
(Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{t/4})
,
and
by
[
10
, Theorem 1.4 (4)]
, process
(H^{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})}(1-t)-H^{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})}(1))_{t\in[0,1]}
has the same law as

(H^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}(t))_{t\in[0,1]}
.
So if
\mathsf{c}
is negative, i.e.,
\mathsf{a}>0
, applying the already established result to the time reversals, we see that process

(H^{({\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a}})}(t))_{t\in[0,1]}
has the same law as the process
(\widetilde{H}(t))_{t\in[0,1]}
with
\widetilde{H}(t)=B_{t/2}-Y_{t/4}^{({\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a}})}+Y_{0}^{({\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a}})}
. Applying the time reversals again, we see that
process

(H^{({\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}})}(t))_{t\in[0,1]}
has the same law as the process defined by the reverse transformation of
\widetilde{H}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(1-t)-\widetilde{H}(1)

\displaystyle=(B_{(1-t)/2}-Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{1/4-t/4}+Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{0})-(B_{1/2}-Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{1/4}+Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{0})

\displaystyle=(B_{(1-t)/2}-B_{1/2})+(Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{1/4}-Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{1/4-t/4}).

This ends the proof, as

(B_{(1-t)/2}-B_{1/2})_{t\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(B_{t/2})_{t\in[0,1]}
and
(Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{1/4}-Y^{(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})}_{1/4-t/4})_{t\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(Y^{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})}_{0}-Y^{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})}_{t/4})_{t\in[0,1]}
.

Note

3.
Killed Brownian motion and the Yakubovich heat kernel

In this section we introduce notation, collect results from the literature, and prove auxiliary facts that we need for Section
4
, where we prove Theorem
1.1
and for Section
5
, where we prove Theorem
1.3

3.1.
Kontorovich–Lebedev transform

According to
[
30
, 10.45(v)]
(see also the papers
[
40
,
36
]
, or the monograph
[
38
]
),
the Kontorovich–Lebedev integral transform is defined as

\mathbb{K}_{{\textnormal{i}}u}[f]:=\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(x)f(x){\textnormal{d}}x.

It is an isometry between
L_{2}((0,\infty),x{\textnormal{d}}x)
and
L_{2}((0,\infty),\mu({\textnormal{d}}u))
, where
\mu
is given by (
1.10
).

If
g(u)=\mathbb{K}_{{\textnormal{i}}u}[f]
, then the inverse transform is

f(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(x)g(u)\mu({\textnormal{d}}u),

see
[
34
, Section 4.15]
and
[
40
,
36
]
.

We will use a slightly different version of the Kontorovich–Lebedev transform, defined as

(3.1)

{\mathcal{K}}f(u)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x}){\textnormal{d}}x.

Note the difference between
K
,
\mathbb{K}
, and curly
{\mathcal{K}}
in the notation.
From the above facts we conclude that
{\mathcal{K}}
is an isometry between
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\textnormal{d}}x)
and
L_{2}((0,\infty),\mu({\textnormal{d}}u))
and the inverse transform is given by

(3.2)

{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}g(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x})g(u)\mu({\textnormal{d}}u).

Note that if
f
is a function of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
g
is a function of
u>0
, then
{\mathcal{K}}f
is a function of
u>0
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}g
is a function of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
When we need to apply
{\mathcal{K}}
to an explicit expression in variable
x
, we will write
{\mathcal{K}}[f(x)]
or
{\mathcal{K}}[f(x)](u)
. In this notations, the left hand side of (
3.1
) is an abbreviated form of the more precise expression
{\mathcal{K}}[f(x)](u)
. We will use similar conventions in our notation for the operators.

As integral operators, both
\mathcal{K}
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}
extend to functions which are not necessarily square integrable, and we will need to apply them to such functions.
(Even though they may cease to be inverses of each other.)
It is clear that
\mathcal{K}
is well defined on

L_{1}({\mathbb{R}},K_{0}(e^{x}){\textnormal{d}}x)
which we will abbreviate as
L_{1}(K_{0})
,
compare

[
39
, (1.2)]
. It is clear that
{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}
is well defined on
L_{1}((0,\infty),{\textnormal{d}}\mu)
, which we will abbreviate as
L_{1}({\textnormal{d}}\mu)
.

3.2.
The semigroup with kernel
p_{t}(x,y)

Recall definition (
1.9
). We can now explain how Yakubovich heat kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
defines a sub-Markov process. (Note that process
(Y_{t})
in Theorem
1.1
is Markov, not sub-Markov.)
The Kontorovich–Lebedev transform defines a semigroup of contractions

(3.3)

\widetilde{{\mathcal{P}}}_{t}={\mathcal{K}}^{-1}e^{-tu^{2}}{\mathcal{K}},\;\;t\geq 0,

which, at first, act on
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\textnormal{d}}x)
.
Here we understand
e^{-tu^{2}}
to be the multiplication operator on
L_{2}((0,\infty),\mu({\textnormal{d}}u))
that maps a function
g(u)
to
e^{-tu^{2}}g(u)
.
Taking into account (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), it is apparent that
\widetilde{{\mathcal{P}}_{t}}
for
t>0
is an integral operator with the Yakubovich heat kernel (
1.9
). Sousa and Yakubovich

[
33
, Example 3.7]
relate this kernel to the Sturm–Liouville theory of differential operators, so the operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
as integral operators with kernel (
1.9
) act also on bounded continuous functions, and define transition probabilities of a sub-Markov process.
The following is a restatement of these facts.

Theorem 3.1
.

The Yakubovich heat kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
defined in (
1.9
) has the following properties.

(i)

For
t>0
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the kernel is symmetric,
p_{t}(x,y)=p_{t}(y,x)
and positive,
p_{t}(x,y)>0
.

(ii)

For
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
p_{t}(x,y)
defines a sub-probability density function,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t}(x,y){\textnormal{d}}y<1
.

(iii)

For
s,t>0
and
x,z\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the Markov property holds,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{t}(x,y)p_{s}(y,z){\textnormal{d}}y=p_{s+t}(x,z)
.

Since we could not locate an appropriate reference for Theorem
3.1
, we sketch a probabilistic justification
by relating the semigroup
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
in (
3.3
) to the killed Brownian motion.
Let
(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the process defined by the Markov generator

{\mathcal{L}}^{\widehat{X}}f(x)=f^{\prime\prime}(x)-e^{2x}f(x).

We can identify
\widehat{X}
with the
\sqrt{2}
multiple of Brownian motion that is killed at rate
k(x)=e^{2x}
. More precisely, let
B
be a standard Brownian motion, then the semigroup of the process
\widehat{X}
is given by

(3.4)

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}f(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{x}[f(\widehat{X}_{t})]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\sqrt{2}B_{s}}{\textnormal{d}}s}f(\sqrt{2}B_{t})\Big|B_{0}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\Big].

It is then clear that
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
is positive and sub-Markovian,
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}1<1
for
t>0
.

Here is how one can see that the semigroup defined by (
3.4
) is the same semigroup that we defined in (
3.3
).
The modified Bessel function
K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(x)
satisfies the differential equation

x^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)+xf^{\prime}(x)-x^{2}f(x)=-u^{2}f(x),

thus the function
f(x)=K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x})
satisfies the differential equation

f^{\prime\prime}(x)-e^{2x}f(x)=-u^{2}f(x).

This shows that functions
f_{u}(x)=K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x})
are eigenfunctions of the Markov generator
{\mathcal{L}}^{\widehat{X}}
and of the Markov semigroup
\widehat{{\mathcal{P}}}_{t}
:

{\mathcal{L}}^{\widehat{X}}f_{u}(x)=-u^{2}f_{u}(x),\;\;\;\widehat{{\mathcal{P}}}_{t}f_{u}(x)=f_{u}(x)e^{-tu^{2}}.

We conclude that the integral operator
{\mathcal{K}}
(our modified version (
3.1
) of the Kontorovich–Lebedev transform) diagonalizes the Markov semigroup
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
, thus extending the action of semigroup
(
3.3
) beyond the initial domain
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\textnormal{d}}x)
. We therefore identify
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
.
(We will apply
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
to functions of the form
e^{ax}
which are not in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\textnormal{d}}x)
.)

Remark 3.2
.

We note that kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
appears in
[
28
, Section 4]
as
q_{1}(2t,x,y)
in their notation.

3.3.
The normalizing constants

In view of Theorem

(iii), it is clear that the normalizing constant for the density (

. We need to show that this integral is finite, and that it is given by an expression that we will use in the proof of Theorem

(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\dots(a+n-1),\quad(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})_{n}=(a_{1})_{n}(a_{2})_{n}\cdots(a_{k})_{n}.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
and
\tau>0
then the integral (

)
is finite and

with

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau u^{2}}\frac{|\Gamma(\tfrac{\mathsf{a}+{\textnormal{i}}u}{2},\tfrac{\mathsf{c}+{\textnormal{i}}u}{2})|^{2}}{|\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}u)|^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}u,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a}}{2},\frac{\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a}}{2})}{2\mathsf{a}\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}\sum_{\{k\geq 0:\;\mathsf{a}+2k<0\}}e^{\tau(\mathsf{a}+2k)^{2}}(\mathsf{a}+2k)\frac{(\mathsf{a},\frac{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}}{2})_{k}}{k!(1+\frac{\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{c}}{2})_{k}}.

(Note that
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}=0
if
\mathsf{a}\geq 0
, i.e., at most one of
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}
and
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a}}^{(\tau)}
is non-zero. In particular, if
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}\geq 0
, then
C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}=2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}}\mathfrak{C}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}
.)

The proof consists of the following steps: we begin by proving (
3.5
) for
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}>0
, then we show that the integral (
1.13
) is finite for all
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
, and finally prove that (
3.5
) holds also for
\mathsf{a}\leq 0
.

3.3.1.
Proof for the Case
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}>0

We use (
1.9
) to write the left hand side of (
1.13
) as an iterated integral

C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e^{\mathsf{a}x+\mathsf{c}y}\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau u^{2}}K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x})K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{y})\frac{{\textnormal{d}}u}{{|\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}u)|^{2}}}\right){\textnormal{d}}x{\textnormal{d}}y.

Since
|K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x})|\leq K_{0}(e^{x})
and
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}>0
, the integrand is integrable, see (
A.4
-
A.5
), and we can use Fubini’s theorem to change the order of the integration.
From the integral identity (
2.5
)
we see that
C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}
is equal to

(3.8)

\frac{2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}}}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau u^{2}}\frac{|\Gamma(\tfrac{\mathsf{a}+{\textnormal{i}}u}{2},\tfrac{\mathsf{c}+{\textnormal{i}}u}{2})|^{2}}{|\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}u)|^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}u,

which is (
3.5
) when
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}>0
.

3.3.2.
Proof of finiteness for all
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0

Let
\tau>0
be a fixed constant. We define a function of two variables

(3.9)

f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})=\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}\int_{{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\tau z^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+z)/2,(\mathsf{a}-z)/2,(\mathsf{c}+z)/2,(\mathsf{c}-z)/2)}{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(-z)}{\textnormal{d}}z.

The integral is taken over the imaginary line in the complex plane. Writing
z={\textnormal{i}}u
we obtain an equivalent representation

f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-\tau u^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{a}-{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{c}+{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{c}-{\textnormal{i}}u)/2)}{|\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}u)|^{2}}{\textnormal{d}}u.

Note that the integrand in (
3.9
) (as a function of
z
) is meromorphic and has poles at points

\{\pm(\mathsf{a}+2n),\pm(\mathsf{c}+2n)\;:\;n=0,1,2,3,\dots\},

and it is clear that
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
is an analytic function of two variables
(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
in the domain

D_{0}:=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})>0,\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0\}.

Theorem 3.4
.

The function
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
can be analytically continued to a function holomorphic in

\Omega=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c})>0\}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume that
\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0
. With
\mathsf{c}
fixed, consider
\mathsf{a}
in the strip
0<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})
.
Take any number
c_{1}
from the interval

(\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a}),\min(\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c}),\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+2))
. We shift the contour of integration in
(
3.9
) from
{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
to
c_{1}+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
and collect the residue at
z=\mathsf{a}
(where the integrand has a simple pole) to obtain the following expression

(3.10)

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}\int_{c_{1}+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\tau z^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+z)/2,(\mathsf{a}-z)/2,(\mathsf{c}+z)/2,(\mathsf{c}-z)/2)}{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(-z)}{\textnormal{d}}z

\displaystyle\quad+2e^{\tau\mathsf{a}^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}.

The poles at
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{a}+2
and
-\mathsf{a}

should not be lying on the contour of integration

c_{1}+i{\mathbb{R}}
. This gives us conditions

\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<c_{1}<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+2
,
-\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<c_{1}<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})
, in particular
-\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+2
, or
-1<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})
. The second term in (
3.10
) is analytic if
\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c}\pm\mathsf{a})>0
.
We conclude that (
3.10
) gives an analytic continuation of
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
into the following domain:

\widetilde{D}_{1}:=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;-1<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c}),\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0\}.

Now take
(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in\widetilde{D}_{1}
with
\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<0
. We shift the contour of integration from
c_{1}+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
back to
{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
, but now we need to take into account the pole at
z=-\mathsf{a}
:

(3.11)

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}\int_{{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\tau z^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+z)/2,(\mathsf{a}-z)/2,(\mathsf{c}+z)/2,(\mathsf{c}-z)/2)}{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(-z)}{\textnormal{d}}z

\displaystyle\quad+4e^{\tau\mathsf{a}^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}.

Again, we cannot have poles at
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{a}+2,-\mathsf{a}-2,-\mathsf{a}
lying on
the contour of integration
i{\mathbb{R}}
, thus we have conditions

-2<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<0
and
\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0
.
Again, the second term in (
3.11
) is analytic as long as
\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c}\pm\mathsf{a})>0
. Therefore,
formula (
3.11
) gives us an analytic continuation of
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
in the domain

D_{2}:=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;-2<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<0,\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0\}.

Now we repeat this procedure. Take
(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in D_{2}
such that
-2<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<-1
and
\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+2<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})
. Choose any
c_{2}
in the interval
(\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+2,\min(\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c}),\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+4))
. Shift the contour of integration in (
3.11
) from
{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
to
c_{2}+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
, and collect the residue at
z=\mathsf{a}+2
and we get

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}\int_{c_{2}+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\tau z^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+z)/2,(\mathsf{a}-z)/2,(\mathsf{c}+z)/2,(\mathsf{c}-z)/2)}{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(-z)}{\textnormal{d}}z

\displaystyle\quad+4e^{\tau\mathsf{a}^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}

\displaystyle\quad-2e^{\tau(\mathsf{a}+2)^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\mathsf{a}+1,(\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a}+2)/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a}-2)/2)}{\Gamma(\mathsf{a}+2)\Gamma(-\mathsf{a}-2)}.

We cannot have poles at
\mathsf{a}+2
,
\mathsf{a}+4
,
-\mathsf{a}-2
,
-\mathsf{a}

lying on the contour of integration
c_{2}+i{\mathbb{R}}
, so
the above expression gives us analytic continuation of
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
into the following domain:

D_{3}:=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;-3<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<-1,\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0\}.

Now take
(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in D_{3}
such that
-3<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<-2
. We shift the contour of integration from
c_{2}+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
back to
{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
, but now we need to take into account the pole at
z=-\mathsf{a}-2
:

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}\int_{{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\tau z^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+z)/2,(\mathsf{a}-z)/2,(\mathsf{c}+z)/2,(\mathsf{c}-z)/2)}{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(-z)}{\textnormal{d}}z

\displaystyle\quad+4e^{\tau\mathsf{a}^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}

\displaystyle\quad-4e^{\tau(\mathsf{a}+2)^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\mathsf{a}+1,(\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a}+2)/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a}-2)/2)}{\Gamma(\mathsf{a}+2)\Gamma(-\mathsf{a}-2)}.

Again, the poles at
\mathsf{a}+2,\mathsf{a}+4,-\mathsf{a}-4,-\mathsf{a}-2
should not be lying on
the contour of integration
i{\mathbb{R}}
. So
the above expression gives us analytic continuation of
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
into the following domain:

D_{4}:=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;-4<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<-2,\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0\}.

After repeating the above procedure, the function
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
would be analytically continued into domains
D_{5}
,
D_{6}
, etc., where we defined

D_{n}:=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;-n<\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})<-n+2,\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0\}.

Note

\widetilde{D}_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup D_{3}\cup D_{4}\cup\dots=\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})+\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0,\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})-\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})>0\},

thus the function
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
is analytic in the above domain. Since
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})=f(\mathsf{c},\mathsf{a})

and this function is analytic in

\{(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\;:\;\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})>0,\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{c})>0\},

we conclude that
f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
can be analytically continued to a function holomorphic in

\Omega
.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

The finiteness part of the conclusion of Theorem
3.3
now follows.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\tau>0
.
The integral (
1.13
)
is finite for real
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
such that
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
.

Proof.

Recall that the condition
C_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}<\infty
for all real
-\mathsf{c}<\mathsf{a}<\infty
is equivalent to
the statement that the Laplace transform
\mathsf{a}\mapsto C_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}
has analytic extension to the complex half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(\mathsf{a})>-\mathsf{c}
. (For a
version of this fact in the language of analytic characteristic functions, see

[
27
, Theorem 2]
.) Since
C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}=2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}-3}f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})
,
Theorem
3.4
ends the proof.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

.

Corollary
3.5
can also be deduced from the fact that the integral (

)
is finite in the upper quadrant
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}>0
by (

), and in the strip

3.3.3.
Proof of formula (
3.5
)

We now consider
\mathsf{a}\in{\mathbb{R}},\mathsf{c}>0
such that
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
.
The gamma function
\Gamma(z)
has simple poles at points
z=-k
,
k=0,1,2,\dots
with residues at these points given by

\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}
. Thus with
\mathsf{a}+2k<0
, and
\mathsf{a}+k\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
the residue of the integrand in (
3.9
) at point
z=\mathsf{a}+2k
is given by

\displaystyle-2\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}e^{\tau(\mathsf{a}+2k)^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\mathsf{a}+k)\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2+k)\Gamma((\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2-k)}{\Gamma(\mathsf{a}+2k)\Gamma(-\mathsf{a}-2k)}.

This expression can be simplified to

\displaystyle-2\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\mathsf{a}\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}e^{\tau(\mathsf{a}+2k)^{2}}(\mathsf{a}+2k)\frac{(\mathsf{a},(\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2)_{k}}{k!(1+(\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{c})/2)_{k}},

removing the singularity at
\mathsf{a}+k=0,-1,-2,\dots
.

One can use here Euler’s reflection formula
\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)
,
z\not\in\mathbb{Z}
and

\frac{\Gamma(a+n)}{\Gamma(a)}=(a)_{n},\;a\not\in\{\dots,-1,0\}
,

\frac{\Gamma(a-n)}{\Gamma(a)}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(1-a)_{n}},\;a\not\in\{\dots,-1,0,1,\dots,n\},\;n=0,1,\dots

Similarly, the residue at
z=-\mathsf{a}-2k
is given by

\displaystyle 2\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\mathsf{a}\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}e^{\tau(\mathsf{a}+2k)^{2}}(\mathsf{a}+2k)\frac{(\mathsf{a},(\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2)_{k}}{k!(1+(\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{c})/2)_{k}}.

Thus if
-2n<\mathsf{a}<-2(n-1)
and
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
, we have

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}\int_{{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}}e^{tz^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+z)/2,(\mathsf{a}-z)/2,(\mathsf{c}+z)/2,(\mathsf{c}-z)/2)}{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(-z)}{\textnormal{d}}z

\displaystyle\quad+4\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\mathsf{a}\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{t(\mathsf{a}+2k)^{2}}(\mathsf{a}+2k)\frac{(\mathsf{a},(\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2)_{k}}{k!(1+(\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{c})/2)_{k}}.

Let us express this identity in a different way. We change variable
z={\textnormal{i}}u
in the integral, use the symmetry of the integrand with respect to
u\mapsto-u
and obtain

(3.12)

\displaystyle f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau u^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{a}-{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{c}+{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{c}-{\textnormal{i}}u)/2)}{\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}u)\Gamma(-{\textnormal{i}}u)}{\textnormal{d}}u

\displaystyle\quad+4\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\mathsf{a}\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{\tau(\mathsf{a}+2k)^{2}}(\mathsf{a}+2k)\frac{(\mathsf{a},(\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2)_{k}}{k!(1+(\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{c})/2)_{k}}.

Thus for real
-2n<\mathsf{a}<-2(n-1)
and
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
we have

C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}=2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}-3}f(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c})\\
=\frac{2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}-3}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu^{2}}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{a}+{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{a}-{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{c}+{\textnormal{i}}u)/2,(\mathsf{c}-{\textnormal{i}}u)/2)}{\Gamma({\textnormal{i}}u)\Gamma(-{\textnormal{i}}u)}{\textnormal{d}}u\\
\quad+2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}-1}\frac{\Gamma((\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2,(\mathsf{c}-\mathsf{a})/2)}{\mathsf{a}\Gamma(-\mathsf{a})}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{t(\mathsf{a}+2k)^{2}}(\mathsf{a}+2k)\frac{(\mathsf{a},(\mathsf{c}+\mathsf{a})/2)_{k}}{k!(1+(\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{c})/2)_{k}}\\
=2^{\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}}(\mathfrak{C}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}+\mathfrak{D}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}^{(\tau)}),

proving (
3.5
) for all
\mathsf{a}\neq 0,-2,-4,\dots
.
It remains to note that by continuity of finite Laplace transforms, function
\mathsf{a}\to C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}
is continuous. By the dominated convergence theorem, we can pass to the limits
a\to-2k
,
k=0,1,\ldots
under the integral sign in (
3.12
). Thus (
3.5
) holds for all
\mathsf{a}+\mathsf{c}>0
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Recall semigroup (
3.3
) and (symmetric) Yakubovich heat kernel (
1.9
).
From the formula for the joint density (
1.12
) it is clear that time reversal
X_{t}=Y_{\tau-t}
preserves the form of the density but swaps the roles of parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
.
We give the proof for the case
\mathsf{c}>0
and construct Markov process
(X_{t})
which will give process
(Y_{t})
by time reversal. (Otherwise, we would use
\mathsf{a}>0
in the construction and construct process
(Y_{t})
directly.)

From the semi-group property of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{P}}}_{t}
, we see that

H_{t}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\tau-t}(x,y)e^{\mathsf{c}y}{\textnormal{d}}y,\quad 0\leq t<\tau,

is a positive space-time harmonic function, so we can use it as Doob’s
h
-transform. We remark that identity (
2.5
) shows that

H_{t}(x)=2^{\mathsf{c}-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{{\textnormal{i}}u}(e^{x})e^{-u^{2}(\tau-t)}\left|\Gamma\left(\frac{\mathsf{c}+{\textnormal{i}}u}{2}\right)\right|^{2}\mu({\textnormal{d}}u),\;t<\tau,

with
\mu
given by (
1.10
).
Additionally, set

H_{\tau}(x):=e^{\mathsf{c}x}
. Then
for
0\leq s<t\leq\tau
we define probability measures

(4.1)

P_{s,t}(x,dy)=\frac{H_{t}(y)}{H_{s}(x)}p_{t-s}(x,y){\textnormal{d}}y.

It is clear that
P_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
are transition probabilities of a Markov process

(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq\tau}
with state space
{\mathbb{R}}
.

The second real parameter,
\mathsf{a}
, enters the initial distribution as follows. By Theorem
3.3
and Fubini’s theorem,
function
H_{0}(x)e^{\mathsf{a}x}
is integrable, with the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}H_{0}(x)e^{\mathsf{a}x}{\textnormal{d}}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\mathsf{a}x+\mathsf{c}y}p_{\tau}(x,y){\textnormal{d}}x{\textnormal{d}}y=C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}.

We normalize this function and take it as the initial distribution for
X_{0}
,

(4.2)

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{0}\in{\textnormal{d}}x)=\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}H_{0}(x)e^{\mathsf{a}x}{\textnormal{d}}x.

This completes the construction of the Markov process
(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq\tau}
.

From (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), it is clear that the joint law of
(X_{0},X_{\tau})
is

\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}e^{\mathsf{a}x}p_{\tau}(x,y)e^{\mathsf{c}y}{\textnormal{d}}x{\textnormal{d}}y.

More generally, for
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{d}<t_{d+1}=\tau
, the joint distribution of
(X_{t_{0}},\dots,X_{t_{d+1}})
is

(4.3)

\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}e^{\mathsf{a}x_{0}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{d+1}p_{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}(x_{j-1},x_{j}){\textnormal{d}}x_{1}\dots{\textnormal{d}}x_{d}e^{\mathsf{c}x_{d+1}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{d+1}.

This is (
1.12
) except that parameters
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}
are at “incorrect locations”. We swap them by time-reversal.
A calculation shows that the finite dimensional distributions of process
Y_{t}=X_{\tau-t}
have density (
1.12
). This proves Theorem
1.1
.
\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}e^{\mathsf{a}\bar{x}_{0}}{\textnormal{d}}\bar{x}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}e^{\mathsf{a}x_{d+1}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{d+1}\prod_{j=1}^{d+1}p_{t_{d+2-j}-t_{d+1-j}}(x_{d+2-j},x_{d+1-j}){\textnormal{d}}x_{1}\dots{\textnormal{d}}x_{d}e^{\mathsf{c}x_{0}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}e^{\mathsf{a}x_{d+1}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{d+1}\prod_{j=1}^{d+1}p_{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}(x_{j},x_{j-1}){\textnormal{d}}x_{1}\dots{\textnormal{d}}x_{d}e^{\mathsf{c}x_{0}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{(\tau)}_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{c}}}e^{\mathsf{a}x_{d+1}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{d+1}\prod_{j=1}^{d+1}p_{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}(x_{j-1},x_{j}){\textnormal{d}}x_{1}\dots{\textnormal{d}}x_{d}e^{\mathsf{c}x_{0}}{\textnormal{d}}x_{0},

which is (
1.12
