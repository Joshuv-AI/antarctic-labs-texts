An infinite family of internal congruences modulo powers of 2 2 for partitions into odd parts with designated summands
Shane Chern, James A. Sellers

Abstract
. In 2002, Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy introduced the combinatorial objects which they called partitions with designated summands . These are built by taking unrestricted integer partitions and designating exactly one of each occurrence of a part. In that same work, Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy also studied such partitions wherein all parts must be odd, and they denoted the number of such partitions of size n n by the function P ​ D ​ O ​ ( n ) PDO(n) . Since then, numerous authors have proven a variety of divisibility properties satisfied by P ​ D ​ O ​ ( n ) PDO(n) . Recently, the second author proved the following internal congruences satisfied by P ​ D ​ O ​ ( n ) PDO(n) : For all n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 , P ​ D ​ O ​ ( 4 ​ n ) \displaystyle PDO(4n) ≡ P ​ D ​ O ​ ( n ) ( mod 4 ) , \displaystyle\equiv PDO(n)\pmod{4}, P ​ D ​ O ​ ( 16 ​ n ) \displaystyle PDO(16n) ≡ P ​ D ​ O ​ ( 4 ​ n ) ( mod 8 ) . \displaystyle\equiv PDO(4n)\pmod{8}. In this work, we significantly extend these internal congruence results by proving the following new infinite family of congruences: For all k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 and all n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 , P ​ D ​ O ​ ( 2 2 ​ k + 3 ​ n ) ≡ P ​ D ​ O ​ ( 2 2 ​ k + 1 ​ n ) ( mod 2 2 ​ k + 3 ) . PDO(2^{2k+3}n)\equiv PDO(2^{2k+1}n)\pmod{2^{2k+3}}. We utilize several classical tools to prove this family, including generating function dissections via the unitizing operator of degree two, various modular relations and recurrences involving a Hauptmodul on the classical modular curve X 0 ​ ( 6 ) X_{0}(6) , and an induction argument which provides the final step in proving the necessary divisibilities. It is notable that the construction of each 2 2 -dissection slice of our generating function bears an entirely different nature to those studied in the past literature.

1.
Introduction

In 2002, Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy
[
2
]
introduced the combinatorial objects which they called
partitions with designated summands
. These are built by taking unrestricted integer partitions and designating exactly one of each occurrence of a part. For example, there are ten partitions with designated summands of size
4
:

\displaystyle 4^{\prime},\ \ \ 3^{\prime}+1^{\prime},\ \ \ 2^{\prime}+2,\ \ \ 2+2^{\prime},\ \ \ 2^{\prime}+1^{\prime}+1,\ \ \ 2^{\prime}+1+1^{\prime},

\displaystyle 1^{\prime}+1+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1^{\prime}+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1^{\prime}+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1+1^{\prime}.

Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy denoted the number of partitions with designated summands of size
n
by the function
PD(n)
. Hence, using this notation and the example above, we know
PD(4)=10
.

In the same paper, Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy
[
2
]
also considered the restricted partitions with designated summands wherein all parts must be odd, and they denoted the corresponding enumeration function by
PDO(n)
. Thus, from the example above, we see that
PDO(4)=5
, where we have counted the following five objects:

3^{\prime}+1^{\prime},\ \ \ 1^{\prime}+1+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1^{\prime}+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1^{\prime}+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1+1^{\prime}.

Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy noted in
[
2
, eq. (1.6)]
that the generating function for
PDO(n)
is given by

(1.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PDO(n)q^{n}=\frac{E(q^{4})E(q^{6})^{2}}{E(q)E(q^{3})E(q^{12})},

where

\displaystyle E(q)=(q;q)_{\infty}:=(1-q)(1-q^{2})(1-q^{3})(1-q^{4})\cdots

is the usual
q
-Pochhammer symbol
.

Beginning with
[
2
]
, a wide variety of Ramanujan-like congruences have been proven for
PD(n)
and
PDO(n)
under different moduli. See
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
12
,
18
]
for such work. Recently, Sellers
[
15
]
proved a number of arithmetic properties satisfied by
PDO(n)
modulo powers of
2
in response to a conjecture of Herden
et. al.
[
11
]
. As part of that work, Sellers proved the following internal congruences on the way to proving infinite families of divisibility properties satisfied by
PDO(n)
:

For all
n\geq 0
,

(1.2)

\displaystyle PDO(4n)

\displaystyle\equiv PDO(n)\pmod{4},

(1.3)

\displaystyle PDO(16n)

\displaystyle\equiv PDO(4n)\pmod{8}.

While searching for such internal congruences computationally, it became clear that the above internal congruences are part of a much larger family. The ultimate goal of this paper is to prove the existence of the following infinite family:

Theorem 1
.

For all
k\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0
,

(1.4)

\displaystyle PDO(2^{2k+3}n)\equiv PDO(2^{2k+1}n)\pmod{2^{2k+3}}.

It should be noted that replacing
n
by
2n
in the above immediately yields the following corollary:

Corollary 2
.

For all
k\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0
,

(1.5)

\displaystyle PDO(2^{2k+4}n)\equiv PDO(2^{2k+2}n)\pmod{2^{2k+3}}.

Notice that the
k=0
case of this corollary states that, for all
n\geq 0
,

PDO(16n)\equiv PDO(4n)\pmod{8}

which is (
1.3
) mentioned above. It is also necessary to point out that the above families of congruences are not optimal in several isolated cases, especially when
k
is small. For instance, we will show in (
6.1
) that

PDO(32n)\equiv PDO(8n)\pmod{64},

which also yields

PDO(64n)\equiv PDO(16n)\pmod{64}.

In order to prove Theorem
1
, we introduce the following auxiliary functions:

\displaystyle\delta=\delta(q)

\displaystyle:=\frac{E(q^{4})E(q^{6})^{2}}{E(q)E(q^{3})E(q^{12})},

\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma(q)

\displaystyle:=\frac{E(q)^{5}E(q^{2})^{5}E(q^{6})^{5}}{E(q^{3})^{15}},

\displaystyle\xi=\xi(q)

\displaystyle:=\frac{E(q^{2})^{5}E(q^{6})}{E(q)E(q^{3})^{5}},

and

\displaystyle\kappa=\kappa(q):=\frac{\gamma(q^{2})^{2}}{\gamma(q)}.

Note from (
1.1
) that

\displaystyle\delta(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PDO(n)q^{n}.

We further define for
k\geq 2
,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}=\Lambda_{k}(q):=\gamma(q)^{2^{k-2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PDO(2^{k}n)q^{n}.

Finally, let
U
be the
unitizing operator of degree two
, given by

\displaystyle U\left(\sum_{n}a_{n}q^{n}\right):=\sum_{n}a_{2n}q^{n}.

These will allow us to represent each
2
-dissection slice of the generating function of
PDO(n)
, accompanied by a certain multiplier:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PDO(2^{k}n)q^{n},

as a polynomial in the Hauptmodul
\xi
on the classical modular curve
X_{0}(6)
of genus
0
. We then complete our analysis on these functions in order to prove Theorem
1
.

This paper is organized as follows: Sect.
2
is devoted to proving several required modular equations. In particular, one important component of this section, as discussed in Sect.
2.2
, concerns the representation of the degree two unitizations
\zeta_{i,j}=U\big(\kappa^{i}\xi^{j}\big)
in
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
for arbitrary exponents
i
and
j
, where
\kappa
is one of the auxiliary functions we introduced and
\xi
is the aforementioned Hauptmodul. The next two sections will then be devoted to the
2
-adic behavior for these
\zeta_{i,j}
series. Recall that in Theorem
1
, we are indeed considering internal congruences for
PDO(n)
. Hence, we introduce another family of auxiliary functions in Sect.
5
:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(PDO(2^{k+2}n)-PDO(2^{k}n)\big)q^{n},

so as to capture this internal nature. Here the new multipliers
\lambda^{\prime}_{k}
are closely related to the original
\lambda_{k}
. We will then move on to the divisibility properties for the above new family of series, and in particular, we offer our proof of Theorem
1
in Sect.
6
. It is notable that the construction of each
2
-dissection slice of our generating function bears an entirely different nature to those studied in the past literature, in the sense that the multipliers
\lambda_{k}
are pairwise distinct. This fact makes our
2
-adic analysis far more complicated but in the meantime very unique. We present a discussion of the difference between our machinery and past work in Sect.
7
.

2.2.2.
Recurrences

We start by considering two generic formal power series
\alpha=\alpha(q)
and
\beta=\beta(q)
such that each of
U\big(\alpha\big)
and
U\big(\alpha^{2}\big)
is representable as a
polynomial
with
rational
(usually
integer
) coefficients in a generic formal power series
\rho=\rho(q)
. Now we define

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha,1}

\displaystyle:=\alpha(q)+\alpha(-q)

\displaystyle\;=2U\big(\alpha\big),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha,2}

\displaystyle:=\alpha(q)\alpha(-q)

\displaystyle\;=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\big(\alpha(q)+\alpha(-q)\big)^{2}-\big(\alpha(q)^{2}+\alpha(-q)^{2}\big)\big]

\displaystyle\;=2U\big(\alpha\big)^{2}-U\big(\alpha^{2}\big).

Clearly, both
\sigma_{\alpha,1}
and
\sigma_{\alpha,2}
are in
\mathbb{Q}[\rho]
. Meanwhile, writing
\alpha_{0}=\alpha(q)
and
\alpha_{1}=\alpha(-q)
, then
X=\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
are the two roots of

\displaystyle\big(X-\alpha(q)\big)\big(X-\alpha(-q)\big)=X^{2}-\sigma_{\alpha,1}X+\sigma_{\alpha,2}.

That is, for
t=0,1
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}-\sigma_{\alpha,1}\alpha_{t}+\sigma_{\alpha,2}=0.

Let
\beta_{0}=\beta(q)
and
\beta_{1}=\beta(-q)
. Now we see that, for
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle 2U\big(\alpha^{k}\beta\big)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}^{k}\beta_{0}+\alpha_{1}^{k}\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=\big(\sigma_{\alpha,1}\alpha_{0}^{k-1}-\sigma_{\alpha,2}\alpha_{0}^{k-2}\big)\beta_{0}+\big(\sigma_{\alpha,1}\alpha_{1}^{k-1}-\sigma_{\alpha,2}\alpha_{1}^{k-2}\big)\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{\alpha,1}U\big(\alpha^{k-1}\beta\big)-2\sigma_{\alpha,2}U\big(\alpha^{k-2}\beta\big).

Namely,

\displaystyle U\big(\alpha^{k}\beta\big)=\sigma_{\alpha,1}U\big(\alpha^{k-1}\beta\big)-\sigma_{\alpha,2}U\big(\alpha^{k-2}\beta\big).

Hence, if
U\big(\beta\big)
and
U\big(\alpha\beta\big)
are also in
\mathbb{Q}[\rho]
, we obtain recursively that
U\big(\alpha^{k}\beta\big)\in\mathbb{Q}[\rho]
for all
k\geq 0
.

Now moving back to our scenario, we define for
i,j\geq 0
,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\zeta_{i,j}:=U\big(\kappa^{i}\xi^{j}\big).

Theorem 9

For any

\displaystyle d_{i,j}:=\begin{cases}5I+J,&\text{if $(i,j)=(2I,2J)$},\\

\displaystyle Z_{i,j}(m)=0.

Furthermore, the coefficient

Proof.

Clearly,
\zeta_{0,0}=1

being odd. We further know from (

7.
Conclusion

We close with two sets of comments. First, we remind the reader of one of the original goals for proving such internal congruences. As was mentioned above, the second author
[
15
]
used two corollaries of Theorem
1
to prove the following Ramanujan-like congruences via induction on
\alpha
:

Theorem 17
.

For all
\alpha\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

\displaystyle PDO\big(2^{\alpha}(4n+3)\big)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle PDO\big(2^{\alpha}(8n+7)\big)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

It would be gratifying to see other cases of Theorem
1
used to assist in proving divisibility properties satisfied by
PDO(n)
for higher powers of
2
.

Secondly, in the course of proving congruences modulo arbitrary powers for the coefficients of an eta-product

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(q)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(n)q^{n},

the usual strategy is to find a suitable basis
\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{L}\}
of the corresponding modular space such that each dissection slice, accompanied by a certain multiplier (usually an eta-product),

\displaystyle\lambda_{m}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(p^{m}n+t_{m})q^{n},

can be represented as a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{L}]
. For example, when proving the congruences modulo powers of 5 for the partition function
[
17
]
or the congruences modulo powers of
7
for the distinct partition function
[
9
]
, two specific multipliers typically take turns showing up, i.e.,
\lambda_{2M-1}=\lambda
and
\lambda_{2M}=\lambda^{\prime}
for two certain series
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
. However, in our study here, the multipliers
\gamma
,
\gamma^{2}
,
\gamma^{4}
,
\gamma^{8}
,
\ldots
never overlap. Meanwhile, an important outcome of cycling the multipliers in the previous studies is that it is typically sufficient to represent each degree
p
unitization
U_{p}\big(\kappa^{i}\xi^{j}\big)
as a polynomial in
\xi
for a certain series
\kappa
, with the exponent
i
restricted to
\{0,1\}
; here we use the case where the basis of the modular space is given by
\{\xi\}
as an illustration. However, as shown in Sect.
2.2
, when there are endless possibilities for the multipliers, we have to extend the consideration of
i
to infinity, thereby substantially increasing the amount of required
p
-adic analysis. These striking facts distinguish our work from the past literature.
