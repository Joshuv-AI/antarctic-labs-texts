Directional asymptotic cones of groups equipped with bi-invariant metrics
Jarek Kędra, Assaf Libman

Abstract
. Given a bi-invariant metric on a group, we construct a version of an asymptotic cone without using ultrafilters. The new construction, called the directional asymptotic cone , is a contractible topological group equipped with a complete bi-invariant metric and admits a canonical Lipschitz homomorphism to the standard asymptotic cone. Moreover, the directional asymptotic cone of a countable group is separable.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Historical context

In 1984 van den Dries and Wilkie
[
9
]
published an alternative proof
of Gromov’s celebrated theorem on polynomial growth. Their proof uses methods
of non-standard analysis and provides the first construction that uses
ultrafilters of what we know now as the asymptotic cone of a group equipped
with the word metric. The construction generalises to all metric spaces and was
popularised by Gromov in
[
6
]
where he provided computations for a
substantial family of examples and posed many inspiring questions. Asymptotic
cones became a well established tool of metric geometry and geometric group
theory
[
3
]
. It is an unpleasant feature, however, that the asymptotic cone of a
space depends on the choice of a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
and a scaling sequence
\mathbf{d}
, and it is often an unapproachable space.

While asymptotic cones are defined for arbitrary metric spaces, in this paper we are interested in groups
G
equipped with conjugation invariant norms or, equivalently, bi-invariant metrics, see Paragraph
2.1
below.
In this case, the asymptotic cone
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
is a group equipped with a bi-invariant metric, rendering it a topological group.
This paper grew out of the interest in these groups.

1.2.
Asymptotic cones

Intuitively, the asymptotic cone of a group
G
(or any metric space) is thought of as “its image from far away”.
For example, the asymptotic cone of a bounded group (or space) is a point, that of
\mathbb{Z}
is

\mathbb{R}
and that of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(both with the
L^{1}
-metric).

The definition of the asymptotic cone presented below is not the most general one
and we refer the reader to the excellent exposition by Drutu and Kapovich
[
3
]
for details.

Let
G
be equipped with a conjugation invariant norm
\|\ \|
.
Choose a
non-principal ultrafilter

\omega
on
\mathbb{N}
and a
scaling sequence

\mathbf{d}=(d_{n})
, i.e a sequence
d_{n}>0
with
\lim_{n}d_{n}=\infty
.
Consider the set
\mathcal{B}(G,\mathbf{d})
of all sequences
(g_{n})\subseteq G
such that
\left(\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}\|g_{n}\|\right)
is bounded.
Recall that a non-principal ultrafilter allows us to compute
\omega
-limits of any bounded sequence in
\mathbb{R}
. The ultrafilter “picks up” a convergent subsequence in a way that makes the usual calculus of limits work.
Define an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{B}(G,\mathbf{d})
where
(g_{n})\sim(h_{n})
if
\omega\operatorname{-}\lim\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}\|g_{n}^{-1}h_{n}\|=0
.
Then
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
is the set of equivalence classes denoted
\{g_{n}\}
.
The norm on
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
is defined by
\|\{g_{n}\}\|=\omega\operatorname{-}\lim_{n}\tfrac{\|g_{n}\|}{d_{n}}
and the group structure is
\{g_{n}\}\cdot\{h_{n}\}=\{g_{n}h_{n}\}
. It is well defined due to the conjugation-invariance of the norm - the fact first observed by Calegari and Zhuang
[
2
]
.

1.3.
Directional asymptotic cones

Let us dwell on the isometry
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbf{d})\cong\mathbb{R}
.
It sends
\{z_{n}\}\in\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbf{d})
to
\omega\operatorname{-}\lim_{n}\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now, look at some
r>0
in
\mathbb{R}
and set
z_{n}:=(-1)^{n}\lfloor rd_{n}\rfloor
.
Clearly
(z_{n})\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbf{d})
, however the sequence
(\tfrac{z_{n}}{d_{n}})
is not convergent.
Its accumulation points are
\pm r
and
\omega\operatorname{-}\lim_{n}\tfrac{z_{n}}{d_{n}}=\pm r
depending on the choice of the ultrafilter
\omega
.
On the other hand, the sequence
z_{n}^{\prime}:=\lfloor rd_{n}\rfloor
will have
\omega\operatorname{-}\lim\tfrac{z^{\prime}_{n}}{d_{n}}=r
for any non-principal ultrafilter.
The difference between the two sequences is clear.
The first,
(z_{n})
, lacks “direction”, a deficiency that is circumvented by the ultrafilter
\omega
.
The second,
(z^{\prime}_{n})
, has a well defined “direction” in
\mathbb{Z}
.
In the case of
\mathbb{Z}
, the sequences in
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbf{d})
that “lack direction” appear to be superfluous.
In some precise sense (in this case, via the inclusion
\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
) the idea of “direction” is encapsulated by the fact that
\tfrac{z^{\prime}_{n}}{d_{n}}
is Cauchy while
\tfrac{z_{n}}{d_{n}}
is not.

This example highlights the excessive amount of sequences used in the definition of the classical asymptotic cones.
This excess is conveniently dealt with by the ultrafilter, however, this convenience comes with a price, which is misleadingly invisible in the example of
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbf{d})
.
First,
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
may depend on the choice of the ultrafilter
\omega
as proven by Drutu and Sapir
[
4
]
who found an example of a finitely generated group with infinitely many non-homeomorphic asymptotic cones. See also
[
3
, page 189]
for a more detailed context. We don’t know examples of groups with bi-invariant metrics whose asymptotic cones depend on the choice of an ultrafilter.

Due to the “bloated” amount of sequences, ultrafilter cones tend to be unmanageably large.
For example, the cone of a separable space may become non-separable. For example, the asymptotic cone of a Gromov hyperbolic space is an
\mathbf{R}
-tree
[
6
, Example 2.B (b)]
hence it is non-separable in general. Since regular trees embed isometrically into the Cayley graph of the bi-invariant metric on the free group
\mathbf{F}_{2}
, due to
[
1
, Theorem 1.D]
,the asymptotic cone of
\mathbf{F}_{2}
with the bi-invariant word norm is non-separable.
The complexity of asymptotic cones is exhibited by their fundamental groups which are very often uncountable
[
6
, Example 2.B (c’)]
.

This paper grew out of an attempt to avoid this excess.
Our main construction is a version of the asymptotic cone for groups
G
equipped with bi-invariant metrics which we call the
directional asymptotic cone
.
We emphasise that unlike the asymptotic cone which is defined for any metric space, our construction is specific to groups with bi-invariant metric.
With the example above in mind, our first task is to establish a notion of “directions” in the group
G
.
Inspired by Cayley graphs, every
g\in G
is a “direction”.
Directions should be thought of as “non commutative vectors”, so we would want to be able to “add” and “rescale” them.
Thus, we define the home for directions to be the group
F_{\mathbb{R}}(G)=*_{G}\mathbb{R}
, the free product of
\mathbb{R}
, one copy for each
g\in G
.
We suitably equip this group with a bi-invariant metric and take its completion (as a metric space), i.e the space of all Cauchy sequences.
For any unbounded subset
T
of the interval
(0,\infty)
which we call a
scaling set
(Definition
5.1
), we define the directional asymptotic cone
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
as a suitable quotient.
The elements of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
can be thought of as Cauchy sequences in
G
as its metric is rescaled by factors taken from
T
(“moving further and further away from the group”).
This construction is carried out in Sections
5
and
6
.
We will write
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
for the maximal scaling set
T=(0,\infty)
.

Our construction depends on the scaling set
T\subseteq(0,\infty)
, however, there are natural epimorphisms
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T^{\prime})
whenever
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
.
We say that
G
is independent of scaling, see Section
9
, if these maps are isometries.
In this case
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
has particularly nice properties.

Relation to the ultrafilter cone

For any non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
and scaling sequence
\mathbf{d}
there is a natural homomorphism

\rho_{\omega,\mathbf{d}}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})

which is Lipschitz with constant
1
(Proposition
10.2
).
We show in Theorem
10.3
that it is injective if and only if
G
is independent of scaling

In fact, under some favourable conditions
\rho_{\omega,\mathbf{d}}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
is an isometric embedding.
For example, Theorems
9.9
and
10.3
show that this is the case for any group
G
with the property that the commutator subgroup of any finitely generated subgroup of
G
is bounded in
G
.
For example, we show in Propositions
11.2
and
11.3
that

•

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the
L^{1}
-norm if
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is equipped with this norm.
Moreover,
\rho_{\omega,\mathbf{d}}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\xrightarrow{\cong}\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbf{d})
is an isometry for any ultrafilter
\omega
and any scaling sequence
\mathbf{d}
.
See Proposition
11.2
.

•

Any nilpotent group
G
is independent of scaling,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
is abelian, and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
is an isometric embedding for any ultrafilter
\omega
and any scaling sequence
\mathbf{d}
.
See Proposition
11.3
.

1.4.
Algebraic and metric properties

Functoriality

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(-)
is a functor from the category of groups with bi-invariant metrics and Lipschitz homomorphisms to the category of complete groups with bi-invariant metrics and Lipschitz homomorphisms (Propositions
6.11
and
6.4
).

Metric properties.

The directional asymptotic cone shares metric properties with the ultrafilter cone, for example

•

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
is a complete metric space (Proposition
6.4
).

•

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
is a length space (Corollary
7.4
).

Unlike the ultrafilter cones which tend to be wild metric spaces with very complicated fundamental groups, the directional asymptotic cone is quite tame.
For example

•

If
G
is countable then
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
is separable i.e it is a Polish group (Proposition
7.2
).

•

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
is a contractible and
\operatorname{End}(G)
with the compact-open topology is also contractible for a large family of scaling sets which includes
T=[0,\infty)
.
See Theorem
7.10
.

The contractibility of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
should be contrasted with the ultrafilter cones which, in general, can have a very non-trivial topology.
For example, the asymptotic cone of
G=\oplus_{n}\mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z}
equipped with the word metric associated with generators of the form
(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots)
is not simply connected, due to Karlhofer
[
8
, Proposition 7.2]
.
On the other hand, we don’t know a single example of a group equipped with the bi-invariant word metric associated with
finite
normal generating set such that its asymptotic cone is not contractible.

Products

By Theorem
8.3
, for any scaling set
T

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G\times H,T)\cong\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)\times\hat{\mathcal{C}}(H,T).

Extensions

Let
1\to N\to G\xrightarrow{\pi}H\to 1
be an extension with
\pi
Lipschitz.
If
N
is bounded in
G
and if there exists a Lipschitz set theoretic section
H\to G
then by Theorem
8.4

\pi
induces a bi-Lipschitz equivalence

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)\xrightarrow{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\pi)}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(H,T).

In particular it is an isomorphism of groups.

If
N\trianglelefteq G
it is possible to equip
H=G/N
with a natural quotient norm (see Definition
2.5
).
By Corollary
8.5
, if
N
is bounded in
G
then
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(H,T)
is an isometry.
For example,

•

If
G
is a finitely generated nilpotent then its commutator subgroup
[G,G]
is bounded with respect to the bi-invariant (word) metric by
[
1
, Theorem 5.H]
. Since the group is finitely generated this metric Lipschitz dominates any bi-invariant metric, hence the commutator subgroup is bounded. Therefore
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G_{\operatorname{ab}})
is an isometry.
In particular
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
is abelian by Proposition
8.1
.

•

The same is true for Thompson’s group
F
or, more generally, for Higman-Thompson groups, which follows from
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
and the subsequent discussion. The above mentioned theorem states that the commutator subgroup of a Higman-Thompson group
G
is
six-uniformly simple
. This means that the commutator subgroup is a simple group and given any nontrivial element
1\neq g\in[G,G]
every other element is a product of at most six conjugates of
g^{\pm 1}
. In particular, the bi-invariant metric associated with the conjugacy classes of
g^{\pm 1}
has diameter at most six.

1.5.
Examples

The results above enable us to compute the directional asymptotic cones of some specific groups.
Of course, one issue is to equip groups with conjugation invariant norms.
If
A\subseteq G
is a (normally) generating set, there is an associated
standard conjugation invariant word norm

\|\ \|_{A}
where
\|g\|_{A}
is the smallest integer
n
such that
g
can be expressed as a product of conjugates of elements of
A
and their inverses. See Definition
2.12
.

Abelian and nilpotent groups

As we mentioned above, if
G
is nilpotent then by Proposition
11.3
it is independent of scaling,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
is abelian, and
\rho_{\omega,\mathbf{d}}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
are isometric embeddings.

If
G
is a finitely generated nilpotent group and is equipped with the standard conjugation invariant word metric then, in fact,
\rho_{\omega,\mathbf{d}}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
is an isometry of groups which are bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\ \|_{1})
, and hence to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean norm, where
n=\operatorname{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}G_{\operatorname{ab}}\otimes\mathbb{R}
.
This is the content of Example
11.5
.

A special case is
(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\|\ \|_{1})
in which case
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\cong(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\ \|_{1})
by Proposition
11.2
.

Solvable groups

If
G
is a finitely generated solvable group with
finitely generated nilpotent commutator subgroup and equipped with the standard conjugation invariant word norm.
Then
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
(R^{n},\|\ \|_{1})
, where
n
is the rank of the abelianisation of
G
. This follows from Proposition
11.4
since
[G,G]
is bounded in
G
by
[
1
, Theorem 5.K]
and the argument used for nilpotent group in the section about extensions above.
Notice that
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\ \|_{1})
is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean norm.

Commutator subgroup

The directional cone of the commutator subgroup
[G,G]
of a group
G
equipped with the commutator length is abelian.
See Example
11.7
.
The same is true for any ultrafilter asymptotic cone which is easy to verify (cf.
[
2
]
where the authors discuss more general verbal groups).

Free groups

Let
{F}_{n}
be the free group on
n
generators equipped with the bi-invariant word metric associated with the standard generating set.
Let
\Theta\subseteq{F}_{n}
be a subset consisting of representatives of conjugacy classes of elements that are not proper powers.
Let
\tau\colon\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
denote the stable length of the word metric (see Section
6.8
) and let
{F}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Theta;\tau)
denote the
real
free group on
\Theta
equipped with the bi-invariant word metric associated with
\tau
(see Section
4
).
In Section
12
we show that
F_{n}
is independent of scaling and we construct an
injective
Lipschitz homomorphism

{F}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Theta;\tau)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(F_{n})

with dense image.
We conjecture that it is an isometry.
We also show that the directional cone of a free group is independent of scaling and hence the natural homomorphisms
\rho_{\omega,{\bf d}}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(F_{n})\to{\rm Cone}_{\omega}(F_{n},\mathbf{d})
are injective (Theorem
12.1
).

1.6.
Relation to the construction of Calegari and Zhuang

Let
F
denote a free group let and
W\subseteq F
which we refer to as a set of words.
Let
G
be a group.
The set of
W
-verbal elements (or simply verbal elements) in
G
is

X_{W}=\bigcup_{\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(F,G)}\varphi(W).

The
W
-verbal subgroup of
G
is
G_{W}=\langle X_{W}\rangle
.
We equip
G_{W}
with the standard word norm
\|\ \|_{X_{W}}
with respect to the set of generators
X_{W}
.

In
[
2
]
Calegari and Zhuang study verbal subgroups making use of the asymptotic cones of
G_{W}
(with the verbal word norm).
They fix a non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
and the scaling sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{n})
where
d_{n}=n
, and denote
\hat{A}_{W}(G)=\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G_{W},\mathbf{d})
, or simply
\hat{A}_{W}
.
Clearly,
\hat{A}_{W}
depends on the choice of the ultrafilter (and a scaling sequence
\mathbf{d}
).
Then in
[
2
, Section 3.4, Definition 3.8]
they define a subgroup
A_{W}\leq\hat{A}_{W}
which they call the
real cone
of
G
.
This group consists of all elements of
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G_{W},\mathbf{d})
of the form
\{g_{1}^{\lfloor t_{1}n\rfloor}\cdots g_{k}^{\lfloor t_{k}n\rfloor}\}_{n}
, where
g_{i}~\in~G_{W}
and
t_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lfloor\ \rfloor
is the floor function.
It is not clear (to us) to what extent the group
A_{W}
depends on the choice of the ultrafilter.

The construction of the real cone is tightly related to our construction of the directional asymptotic cone of
G_{W}
as follows.
Let
F_{\mathbb{R}}(G_{W})
denote the free amalgamated product
*_{g\in G_{W}}\mathbb{R}
, see Section
4
.
A key feature of the directional asymptotic cone described in Section
6.3
is that it is equipped with a
canonical
homomorphism
\eta\colon F_{\mathbb{R}}(G_{W})\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G_{W})
whose image is dense.
By composing it with the homomorphism
\rho\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G_{W})\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G_{W},\mathbf{d})
(see Proposition
10.2
) we obtain a homomorphism
\rho^{\prime}\colon F_{\mathbb{R}}(G_{W})\to\hat{A}_{W}
.
By easy inspection of
\eta
and
\rho
, the real cone
A_{W}
is precisely the image of
\rho^{\prime}
.
Since
\rho
is Lipschitz and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G_{W})
is a complete metric space, the closure of
A_{W}
in
\hat{A}_{W}
is equal to the image of the directional cone
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G_{W})
in
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G_{W},\mathbf{d})
.

Some of the fundamental properties of the real cone proven in
[
2
]
have their analogues for directional cones, and in fact, they follow from them.
For example, in
[
2
, Lemma 3.9 and 3.10]
it is shown that
A_{W}
has an action of
\mathbb{R}
and consequently that it is a contractible space.
Similarly, in Theorem
7.10
we show
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(H)
is contractible.
We also show that it admits an action of the monoid
[0,\infty)
and with only a bit more effort one can show this action extends to an action of
\mathbb{R}
.

2.
Conjugation invariant norms

2.1
.

Let
G
be a group.
A
norm
on
G
is a function
\nu\colon G\to[0,\infty)
that satisfies the following
conditions:

(1)

\nu(g)=0\iff g=1
(non-degeneracy or point separation);

(2)

\nu(g)=\nu(g^{-1})
(symmetry);

(3)

\nu(gh)\leq\nu(g)+\nu(h)
(triangle inequality).

By relaxing the condition of separation of points we obtain a
pseudonorm

or a
degenerate norm
on
G
.
A (pseudo) norm is called
conjugation-invariant
if in addition for every
g,h\in G
,

\nu(hgh^{-1})=\nu(g).

Conjugation invariant (pseudo) norms are in one-to-one correspondence with bi-invariant (pseudo) metrics on
G
via
d_{\nu}(g,h)=\nu(g^{-1}h)
.
In this case
G
becomes a topological group (but this is not necessarily the case if the norm is not conjugation invariant).

2.2
.

Let
f\colon(G,\mu)\to(H,\nu)
be a homomorphism of groups equipped with pseudonorms, whence pseudometrics.
Then
f
is a Lipschitz function if and only if there exists
C\geq 0
such that
\nu(f(g))\leq C\mu(g)
for all
g\in G
.

2.3
.

A pseudometric
\tilde{d}
on
\tilde{X}
induces an equivalence relation
\tilde{x}\sim\tilde{y}\iff\tilde{d}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=0
and a metric space
(X,d)
on the set of equivalence classes where
d([\tilde{x}],[\tilde{y}])=\tilde{d}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
.

Let
\nu
be a conjugation-invariant pseudonorm on
G
and let

G_{0}=\{g\in G:\nu(g)=0\}.

Then
G_{0}\trianglelefteq G
and
\|gG_{0}\|:=\nu(g)
defines a conjugation-invariant norm on
G/G_{0}
.
The metric associated to this norm is the metric associated to the
pseudometric
d_{\nu}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\|\ \|
be a conjugation-invariant pseudonorm on
G
.
Then for any
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
and
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
in
G

\|(g_{1}\dots g_{n})^{-1}(h_{1}\dots h_{n})\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|g_{i}^{-1}h_{i}\|.

Proof.

Induction on
n
.
Set
g=g_{1}\dots g_{n}
and
g^{\prime}=g_{1}\dots g_{n-1}
and
h=h_{1}\dots h_{n}
and
h^{\prime}=h_{1}\dots h_{n-1}
.
Then
\|g^{-1}h\|=\|g_{n}^{-1}g^{\prime}{}^{-1}h^{\prime}h_{n}\|\leq\|g_{n}^{-1}h_{n}\|+\|h_{n}^{-1}g^{\prime}{}^{-1}h^{\prime}h_{n}\|=\|g_{n}^{-1}h_{n}\|+\|g^{\prime}{}^{-1}h^{\prime}\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|g_{i}^{-1}h_{i}\|
.
∎

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\pi\colon G\to H
be a surjective homomorphism of groups equipped with conjugation-invariant pseudonorms
\|\ \|_{G}
and
\|\ \|_{H}
.
We call
\pi
a
metric quotient
if for every
h\in H

\|h\|_{H}=\inf\left\{\|g\|_{G}:g\in\pi^{-1}(h)\right\}.

Clearly, a metric quotient
\pi\colon G\to H
is Lipschitz with constant
1
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\pi\colon G\to H
be a surjective homomorphism and let
\|\ \|
be a pseudonorm on
G
.
For any
h\in H
set

\|h\|_{H}=\inf\left\{\|g\|:g\in\pi^{-1}(h)\right\}.

Then
\|\ \|_{H}
is a pseudonorm on
H
.
It is conjugation invariant if
\|\ \|
is, in which case it is a metric quotient, hence Lipschitz with constant
1
.

Proof.

Surjectivity of
\pi
implies that
\|\ \|_{H}
is well defined.
The symmetry
\|h\|_{H}=\|h^{-1}\|_{H}
follows from the symmetry of
\|\ \|
and since
\pi^{-1}(h^{-1})=(\pi^{-1}(h))^{-1}
.
Consider
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
.
Given
\epsilon>0
, choose some
g_{1}\in\pi^{-1}(h_{1})
such that
\|g_{1}\|<\|h_{1}\|_{H}+\epsilon
.
Since
\pi^{-1}(h_{1}h_{2})=g_{1}\cdot\pi^{-1}(h_{2})
, we get that

\displaystyle\|h_{1}h_{2}\|_{H}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\|g\|:g\in\pi^{-1}(h_{1}h_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\|g_{1}x\|:x\in\pi^{-1}(h_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{1}\|+\inf\left\{\|x\|:x\in\pi^{-1}(h_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle<\|h_{1}\|_{H}+\|h_{2}\|_{H}+\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary the triangle inequality follows.

The conjugation invariance of
\|\ \|_{H}

Proposition 2.7

Let

Lemma 4.24

Proof.

\mathbf{w}

\mathbf{w}

\mathbf{w}=a_{1}(r_{1})\cdots a_{n}(r_{n})

For any

\left|\tfrac{\llfloor r_{i}t\rrfloor}{t}\right|>\tfrac{|r_{i}|}{2}

\epsilon:=\min\{\tfrac{|r_{i}|}{2}\mu(a_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}<\min\{\left|\tfrac{\llfloor r_{i}t\rrfloor}{t}\right|:1\leq i\leq n\}\qquad(t\gg 0).

Theorem 4.26
.

Let
\mathbf{w}
be a word in the alphabet
A(\mathbb{R})
representing
w\in F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
.
Then

\|w\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}=\min\{\mu(\mathbf{u}):\text{ $\mathbf{u}$ is a cancellation sequence in $\mathbf{w}$}\}

We call a cancellation sequence in
\mathbf{w}
which attains the minimum
\mu
-minimal
.

Proof.

Write
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}(r_{1})\cdots a_{n}(r_{n})
.
If
\mathbf{u}=a_{1}(s_{1})\cdots a_{n}(s_{n})
is a cancellation sequence then by Proposition
4.25

w
is a product of conjugates of
a_{i}(s_{i})
and therefore

\|w\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}\leq\sum_{i}\|a_{i}(s_{i})\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}\leq\sum_{i}\mu_{\mathbb{R}}(a_{i}(s_{i}))=\sum_{i}|s_{i}|\mu(a_{i}).

Since this holds for any cancellation sequence

\|w\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}\leq\inf\,\{\mu(\mathbf{u}):\text{ $\mathbf{u}$ is a cancellation sequence in $\mathbf{w}$}\}.

It remains to find a cancellation sequence
\mathbf{u}
such that
\mu(\mathbf{u})=\|w\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
.
For every
t>0
we note that

\mathbf{w}\{t\}=a_{1}^{\llfloor r_{1}t\rrfloor}\cdots a_{n}^{\llfloor r_{n}t\rrfloor}\ \in\ F(A).

Choose a
\mu
-minimal cancellation sequence
\mathbf{v}(t)
in
\mathbf{w}\{t\}
.
By applying the homomorphism
F(A)\xrightarrow{w\mapsto w^{\#}}F(A^{\#})
from Section
3.8
, it follows from (
1
) that
\mathbf{u}(t):=\mathbf{v}(t)^{\flat}
is also a cancellation sequence in
\mathbf{w}\{t\}
and since
\mu(\mathbf{u}(t))=\mu(\mathbf{v}(t))
, it is also
\mu
-minimal.
We write

\mathbf{u}(t)=a_{1}^{m_{i}(t)}\cdots a_{n}^{m_{n}(t)}.

By definition
m_{i}(t)\in[0,\llfloor r_{i}t\rrfloor\rangle
and
\prod_{i}a_{i}^{\llfloor r_{i}t\rrfloor-m_{i}(t)}
is the trivial element in
F(A)
and
\|\mathbf{w}\{t\}\|_{F(A)}=\sum_{i}|m_{i}(t)|\mu(a_{i})
.
Furthermore,
m_{i}(t)=\llfloor r_{i}t\rrfloor
whenever
\mu(a_{i})=0
.

Now,
\tfrac{m_{i}(t)}{t}\in[0,\tfrac{\llfloor r_{i}t\rrfloor}{t}\rangle\subseteq[0,r_{i}\rangle
.
By the compactness of
\prod_{i=1}^{n}[0,r_{i}\rangle
there exists a sequence
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
such that
\lim_{k}t_{k}=\infty
and such that the limits
s_{i}:=\lim_{k}\tfrac{m_{i}(t_{k})}{t_{k}}
exist.
Consider the word in the alphabet
A(\mathbb{R})

\mathbf{u}=a_{1}(s_{1})\cdots a_{n}(s_{n}).

This is a sequence in
\mathbf{w}
since
s_{i}\in[0,r_{i}\rangle
.
Let
w^{\prime}\in F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
be the element represented by

\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}=a_{1}(r_{1}-s_{1})\cdots a_{n}(r_{n}-s_{n}).

Observe that if
\mu(a_{i})=0
then
r_{i}=s_{i}
so
\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}
and
(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u})^{\#}
represent the same element in
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
.
By Proposition
4.25
, for any
k\geq 1
we get

w^{\prime}\{t_{k}\}=\prod_{i}a_{i}^{\llfloor t_{k}(r_{i}-s_{i})\rrfloor}=\prod_{i}a_{i}^{\llfloor t_{k}r_{i}\rrfloor-m_{i}(t_{k})}\cdot a_{i}^{\llfloor t_{k}(r_{i}-s_{i})\rrfloor+m_{i}(t_{k})-\llfloor t_{k}r_{i}\rrfloor}

is a product of conjugates of
a_{i}^{\llfloor t_{k}(r_{i}-s_{i})\rrfloor+m_{i}(t_{k})-\llfloor t_{k}r_{i}\rrfloor}
.
By Theorem
4.18

\displaystyle\|w^{\prime}\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k}\tfrac{1}{t_{k}}\|w^{\prime}\{t_{k}\}\|_{F(A)}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k}\tfrac{1}{t_{k}}\sum_{i}\|a_{i}^{\llfloor t_{k}(r_{i}-s_{i})\rrfloor+m_{i}(t_{k})-\llfloor t_{k}r_{i}\rrfloor}\|_{F(A)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k}\sum_{i}\left|\tfrac{\llfloor t_{k}(r_{i}-s_{i})\rrfloor+m_{i}(t_{k})-\llfloor t_{k}r_{i}\rrfloor}{t_{k}}\right|\mu(a_{i})=0.

Lemma
4.24
applied to
(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u})^{\#}
implies that
w^{\prime}
is the trivial element, so
\mathbf{u}
is a cancellation sequence in
\mathbf{w}
.
By Theorem
4.18

\mu(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{i}|s_{1}|\mu(a_{i})=\sum_{i}\lim_{k}\tfrac{|m_{i}(t_{k})|}{t_{k}}\mu(a_{i})=\\
\lim_{k}\tfrac{1}{t_{k}}\sum_{i}\mu(\mathbf{u}(t_{k}))=\lim_{k}\tfrac{1}{t_{k}}\|\mathbf{w}\{t_{k}\}\|_{F(A)}=\|w\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}.

This completes the proof.
∎

.

Given

Proposition 4.28
.

F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
is a geodesic pseudometric space.

Proof.

We write
\|\ \|
for the norm of
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
.
Since the metric on
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
is invariant with respect to translations, it suffices to find for every
w\in F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
a geodesic to the identity element.
Write
w=a_{1}(r_{1})\cdots a_{n}(r_{n})
.
Let
u=a_{1}(s_{1})\cdots a_{n}(s_{n})
be a
\mu
-minimal cancellation sequence, see Theorem
4.26
.
Define
\alpha\colon[0,1]\to F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
by

\alpha(t)=a_{1}(r_{1}-s_{1}t)\cdots a_{n}(r_{n}-s_{n}t).

It is clear that
\alpha(0)=w
and
\alpha(1)=1
.
Consider some
0\leq t^{\prime}<t\leq 1
.
Then
\alpha(t)=\prod_{i}a_{i}(r_{i}-ts_{i})
and
\alpha(t^{\prime})=\prod_{i}a_{i}(r_{i}-ts^{\prime}_{i})
.
By Lemma
2.4
and the relations (
2
)

\|\alpha(t)^{-1}\alpha(t^{\prime})\|\leq\sum_{i}\|a_{i}(t^{\prime}s_{i}-ts_{i})\|\leq|t-t^{\prime}|\sum_{i}\mu(a_{i})|s_{i}|=|t-t^{\prime}|\cdot\|w\|.

In particular, given
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq 1
we get

\displaystyle\|w\|

\displaystyle=\|\alpha(0)\alpha(1)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\alpha(0)\alpha(t_{1})^{-1}\|+\|\alpha(t_{1})\alpha(t_{2})^{-1}\|+\|\alpha(t_{2})\alpha(1)^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq t_{1}\|w\|+(t_{2}-t_{1})\|w\|+(1-t_{2})\|w\|

\displaystyle=\|w\|.

Hence, equality holds everywhere and, in particular,

\|\alpha(t_{1})\alpha(t_{2})^{-1}\|=|t_{2}-t_{1}|\cdot\|w\|.

It follows that
\alpha
is a geodesic.
∎

5.
The group of asymptotic directions

Running assumption:

As in the previous section all sets
A,B,\dots
are assumed to be equipped with length functions
\mu_{A},\mu_{B},\dots
(see Section
2.10
).
Thus,
F(A)
and
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
are equipped with the associated conjugation-invariant word pseudonorms from Sections
3
and
4
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
scaling set
is an unbounded subset of the interval
(0,\infty)
.

Notation
.

Throughout
T,T^{\prime}
etc. will denote scaling sets.

Recall from Definition
4.16
that a function
f\colon T\to X
where
(X,d)
is a pseudometric space is called Cauchy if

\sup\{d(f(s_{1}),f(s_{2})):s_{1},s_{2}\in T\cap[t,\infty)\}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
T\subseteq(0,\infty)
be unbounded.
A function

w\colon T\to F(A;\mu)

is called an
asymptotic direction
if the function
f_{w}\colon T\to F_{\mathbb{R}}(A;\mu)
defined by

f_{w}(t)=\psi_{1/t}(w(t))

is Cauchy.
Let

\mathcal{D}(A,T;\mu)\subseteq\prod_{t\in T}F(A)

denote the group of all asymptotic directions in
F(A;\mu)
with respect to
T
.
When
\mu
is understood from the context we write
\mathcal{D}(A,T)
.
When
T=(0,\infty)
we write
\mathcal{D}(A;\mu)
or
\mathcal{D}(A)
.

To justify the terminology we need to show that
\mathcal{D}(A)
is a subgroup of
\prod_{t\in T}F(A)
.
This follows easily from the triangle inequality, the symmetry of the norm and the fact that
\psi_{t}
are homomorphisms.
The details are left to the reader.

If
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
is a Cauchy sequence in a pseudo metric space
(X,d)
and

is a basepoint, then
d(x_{n},y)
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\mathbb{R}
is complete, this justifies the following definition.

Definition 5.3
.

For any
w\in\mathcal{D}(A,T)
define a conjugation invariant pseudonorm on
\mathcal{D}(A,T)
by

\|w\|_{\mathcal{D}(A,T)}=\lim_{t\in T}\|\psi_{1/t}(w(t))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}.

We write
\|\ \|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}
when

(and
\mu
) is understood from the context.

That

is a conjugation-invariant pseudonorm follows easily from the fact that
\|\ \|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
has this property and that
\psi_{t}
is a homomorphism.
It follows from Lemma
4.12
and Corollary
4.19
that

(6)

\|w\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}=\lim_{t\in T}\tfrac{1}{t}\|w(t)\|_{F(A)}.

With the notation of Section

we make the following definition.

Definition 5.4
.

The
group of asymptotic directions
on
A
is

\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A,T;\mu)=\mathcal{D}(A,T;\mu)/\mathcal{D}(A,T;\mu)_{0}.

We will omit
T
and
\mu
from the notation whenever they are understood from the context.
Let
\|\ \|_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A)}
be the induced conjugation-invariant norm.
We will denote by
[w]
the image of
w\in\mathcal{D}(A)
in
\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A)
.

Proposition 5.5
.

The group
\mathcal{D}(A,T)
is a complete pseudo metric space and hence,
\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A,T)
is a complete metric spaces.

Proof.

Let
w_{1},w_{2},\dots
be a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{D}(A)
.
We will find a pseudo limit
v
in
\mathcal{D}(A)
.
Since a Cauchy sequence converges if and only if it contains a convergent subsequence, we may assume by passage to a subsequence that

\|w_{n}^{-1}w_{n+1}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}<\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}}

for all
n\geq 1
.
It follows from the definition of
\|\ \|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}
that there exist
r_{1},r_{2},\dots
such that for any
n\geq 1
the following hold.

(a)

For any
s,t\in T
such that
s,t>r_{n}

\|\,\psi_{1/s}(w(s))^{-1}\cdot\psi_{1/t}(w_{n}(t))\,\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}<\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}.

(This is because
w_{n}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
).

(b)

For any
t\in T
such that
t>r_{n}

\|\,\psi_{1/t}(w_{n+1}(t)\cdot w_{n}(t)^{-1})\,\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}<\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}}.

(This is because
\|w_{n+1}w_{n}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}<\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}}
).

By increasing the
r_{n}
’s we may arrange that
0<r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots
and that
r_{n}\to\infty
.
For convenience we set
r_{0}=0
and
w_{0}=1
, the constant function
w_{0}\colon T\to F(A)
.
Observe that for any
j\geq i\geq 1
and any
t\in T\cap(r_{j-1},\infty)

\|\psi_{1/t}(w_{i}(t))^{-1}w_{j}(t))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}\leq\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\|\psi_{1/t}(w_{k}(t))^{-1}w_{k+1}(t))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}\\
<\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\tfrac{1}{2^{k+1}}<\tfrac{1}{2^{i}}.

Define
v\colon T\to F(A)
as follows.
For any
t\in T
there exists a unique
n\geq 0
such that
r_{n}<t\leq r_{n+1}
.
Set

v(t)=w_{n}(t).

We claim that
v\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.
To see this, given
\epsilon>0
choose
n\geq 1
such that
\tfrac{1}{2^{n-1}}<\epsilon
.
Consider some
s,t>r_{n}
and suppose that
s\geq t
.
Then
r_{i}<t\leq r_{i+1}
and
r_{j}<s\leq r_{j+1}
for some
j\geq i\geq n
.
Since
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|\psi_{1/t}

\displaystyle(v(t))^{-1}\psi_{1/s}(v(s))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}=\|\psi_{1/t}(w_{i}(t))^{-1}\psi_{1/s}(w_{j}(s))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi_{1/t}(w_{i}(t))^{-1}\psi_{1/s}(w_{i}(s))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}+\|\psi_{1/s}(w_{i}(s)^{-1}w_{j}(s))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}

\displaystyle<\tfrac{1}{2^{i}}+\tfrac{1}{2^{i}}<\tfrac{1}{2^{i-1}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2^{n-1}}<\epsilon.

It remains to show that
w_{n}\to v
in
\mathcal{D}(A)
.
Given
\epsilon>0
choose
m\geq 1
such that
\tfrac{1}{2^{m}}<\epsilon
.
Suppose that
n\geq m
.
If
t>r_{n}
then
r_{i}<t\leq r_{i+1}
for some

and since

\|\psi_{1/t}(w_{n}(t)^{-1}v(t))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}=\|\psi_{1/t}(w_{n}(t)^{-1}w_{i}(t))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}<\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2^{m}}.

Taking the limit

we get for all

\|w_{n}^{-1}v\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}\leq\tfrac{1}{2^{m}}<\epsilon.

We deduce that
w_{n}\to v
.
∎

Recall that elements of
\mathcal{D}(A)
are functions
w\colon T\to F(A)
such that
f_{w}(t)=\psi_{1/t}(w(t))
is a Cauchy function into
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, hence is convergent in its completion.
We will now define
\hat{\Psi}\colon\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A)\to\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
and
\iota\colon F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\to\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A)
and show that they are well defined in Proposition
5.8
below.

Definition 5.6
.

Define a homomorphism
\Psi\colon\mathcal{D}(A,T)\to\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
by

\Psi(w)=\lim_{t\to T}\psi_{1/t}(w(t)).

Define a homomorphism
\hat{\Psi}\colon\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A)\to\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
by

\hat{\Psi}([w])=[\Psi(w)].

Definition 5.7
.

Define a homomorphism
\iota\colon F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\to\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A,T)
as follows.
Given
w\in F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
choose a word
\mathbf{w}
in the alphabet
A(\mathbb{R})
which represents
w
.
There results a function
w\{\cdot\}\colon T\to F(A)
defined by
t\mapsto\mathbf{w}\{t\}
, see Definition
4.14
. Set

\iota(w)=[\mathbf{w}\{\cdot\}].

We say that a homomorphism
\varphi\colon G\to H
between groups equipped with pseudonorms is
norm preserving
if
\|\varphi(g)\|=\|g\|
for all
g\in G
.

Proposition 5.8
.

The function
\hat{\Psi}
and
\iota
are well defined homomorphisms.
Moreover,
\hat{\Psi}
is an isometry
\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A,T)\cong\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
and
\hat{\Psi}\circ\iota
is the canonical homomorphism
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\to\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
.
In particular,
\iota
is norm preserving and its image is dense in
\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A)
.

Proof.

We will omit
T
from the notation.
It is clear that
\Psi
is well defined and it is a homomorphism because the norm on
\mathcal{D}(A)
is conjugation invariant so it makes
\mathcal{D}(A)
a topological group.
It is norm preserving because for any
w\in\mathcal{D}(A)

\|\Psi(w)\|_{\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}}=\|\lim_{t\to\infty}\psi_{1/t}(w(t))\|_{\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\psi_{1/t}(w(t))\|_{\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}}=\\
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\psi_{1/t}(w(t))\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}=\|w\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}.

In particular
\operatorname{Ker}(\Psi)=\mathcal{D}(A)_{0}
.
So
\Psi
factors through
\hat{\Psi}
which is therefore a norm preserving monomorphism, i.e an isometric embedding.
Since
\hat{\mathcal{D}}(A)
is complete by Proposition
5.5
, the image of
\hat{\Psi}
is closed in
\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
.

We now prove that
\iota
is well defined.
Suppose that
\mathbf{w}
represents
w\in F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
.
It follows from Proposition
4.15
that
\psi_{1/t}(\mathbf{w}\{t\})
is a Cauchy function in
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, hence by definition
\mathbf{w}\{\cdot\}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.
By the same proposition, if
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
is another word representing
w
then

\|\mathbf{w}\{\cdot\}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\{\cdot\}\|_{\mathcal{D}(A)}=\lim_{t}\|\psi_{1/t}(\mathbf{w}\{t\}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\{t\})\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}=\\
\lim_{t}\|\psi_{1/t}(\mathbf{w}\{t\})^{-1}\cdot\psi_{1/t}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\{t\})\|_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}=\|w^{-1}w\||_{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}=0.

So
\iota
is independent of choices, hence it is well defined.
It is a homomorphism because

by (

).

By Proposition
4.15
, for any
w\in F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
and a representing word
\mathbf{w}
,

\hat{\Psi}(\iota(w))=\hat{\Psi}([\mathbf{w}\{\cdot\}])=\lim_{t}\psi_{1/t}(\mathbf{w}\{t\})=w.

(The limit is in
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
).
Therefore
\hat{\Psi}\circ\iota
is the canonical homomorphism
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\to\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
.
In particular, the image of
\hat{\Psi}
contains a dense subset of
\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
and since it is closed,
\hat{\Psi}
is surjective, hence an isometry.
Since
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\to\widehat{F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
is norm preserving, so is
\iota
.

Next, we show that

Proposition 5.9

Lemma 5.14

Let

Proof.

Since the metric on

is invariant it suffices to show that for any
g\in G
and any
\epsilon>0

\gamma
from
1
to

\ell(\gamma)<\|g\|+\epsilon

Choose a sequence
h_{n}\in H
such that
h_{n}\to g
.
We may assume that
\|g^{-1}h_{n}\|<\tfrac{1}{2^{n+2}}
for all

\|h_{n}^{-1}h_{n+1}\|<\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}}
.
For every
n\geq 2

Then
\|\Delta_{n}\|<\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}
for all
n\geq 2
.

is a length space, choose paths
\delta_{n}\colon I\to H
from
1
to
\delta_{n}

\ell(\delta_{n})<\tfrac{1}{2^{n-1}}
.
Observe that for any

\|\delta_{n}(t)\|\leq\ell(\delta_{n}|_{[0,t]})\leq\ell(\delta_{n})<\tfrac{1}{2^{n-1}}.

Given
\epsilon>0

\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}<\tfrac{1}{2^{n-2}}

\beta\colon I\to H
from

w(\alpha)=[k]=1
.
Since
\alpha
was arbitrary,
w(\hat{\mathcal{C}}(H))=1
.
∎

9.
Independence of scaling

Running assumptions:

All group
G,H,\dots
are equipped with a conjugation invariant norm.
Throughout
T,T^{\prime},\dots
denote scaling sets.

Definition 9.1
.

G
is called
independent of scaling
if for any inclusion
T\subseteq T^{\prime}
of scaling sets,
\widehat{\operatorname{res}}^{T^{\prime}}_{T}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T^{\prime})\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
is an isometry.

Notation
.

For the remainder of the section, when we write
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
we mean
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,(0,\infty))
, i.e the full scaling set.

It is clear that if
T\subseteq T^{\prime}\subseteq T^{\prime\prime}
are scaling sets then
\widehat{\operatorname{res}}^{T^{\prime}}_{T}\circ\widehat{\operatorname{res}}^{T^{\prime\prime}}_{T^{\prime}}=\widehat{\operatorname{res}}^{T^{\prime\prime}}_{T}
.
Applying this to the maximal scaling set
T^{\prime\prime}=(0,\infty)
we get:

Proposition 9.2
.

A group
G
is independent of scaling if and only if the homomorphisms
\widehat{\operatorname{res}}_{T}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
are isometries for all scaling sets
T
.

9.3
.

Let
[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
.
If
\lim_{t\in T}\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}
exists then it is independent of the representative because by Lemma
6.6

g,h\colon T\to G
represent the same element in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
if and only if
\limsup_{t\in T}\tfrac{\|g(t)^{-1}h(t)\|}{t}=0
.

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
G
be a group.
If
\lim_{t}\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}
exist for any
[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
then
G
is independent of scaling.

Proof.

By Propositions
9.2
and
6.13
we need to show that
\widehat{\operatorname{res}}_{T}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
are injective.
If
[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
is in the kernel, then by Lemma
6.6

\lim_{t\in T}\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}=0
, and since
T\subseteq(0,\infty)
is unbounded
\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}=0
, so
[g]=1
.
∎

Recall that
F_{\mathbb{Q}}(A)
denotes the subgroup of
F_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
generated by the symbols
a(q)
where
a\in A
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Once again, we will consider the homomorphisms
\hat{\pi}\colon\hat{\mathcal{D}}(G,T)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
and

from Section
6.3
Definition

.

Lemma 9.5
.

Suppose that
\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{\|h(t)\|}{t}

[h]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)

\hat{\pi}\circ\iota

Then
\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}

[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
.
If, in addition, for every
[h]
of the form above

\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{\|h(t)\|}{t}=\|[h]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)},

then for every
[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)

\|[g]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}=\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}

Proof.

We need to show that
\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}
is Cauchy.
Choose
\epsilon>0
.
Since
\iota(F_{\mathbb{R}}(G))
is dense in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)

from

.
That is,
\|[g]^{-1}\cdot[h]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}<\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}
.
By Lemma

\limsup_{t>0}\tfrac{|\,\|g(t)\|-\|h(t)\|\,|}{t}\leq\limsup_{t>0}\tfrac{|\|g(t)^{-1}h(t)\||}{t}\leq\|[g]^{-1}[h]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}<\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}.

By hypothesis

exists, so
|\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}-L|<\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}
for all

.
In particular

Assume that
\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{\|h(t)\|}{t}=\|[h]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}
for all

in the image of

for any

∎

Lemma 9.6
.

Any element of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
in the image of

Lemma 9.7
.

Suppose that for any
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in G
the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\|g_{1}^{n}\dots g_{k}^{n}\|

in the image of
\hat{\pi}\circ\iota\colon F_{\mathbb{Q}}(G)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)

.

Suppose that

Then

(a)

(b)

\|[g]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}=\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{\|g(t)\|}{t}

(c)

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)

Proof.

Consider some
[h]
in the image of
\hat{\pi}\circ\iota\colon F_{\mathbb{Q}}(G)\to\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
.
By Lemma
9.6

[h]=\hat{\pi}(\iota(\alpha))
for some
\alpha\in F_{\mathbb{Q}}(G)
of the form
\alpha=\overline{g_{1}}(\tfrac{1}{m})\cdots\overline{g_{k}}(\tfrac{1}{m})
.

.
Clearly, for any

.
First, suppose that

Let

10.
Relation to the ultralimit asymptotic cone

Running assumptions:

All group
G,H,\dots
are equipped with a conjugation invariant norm.
Throughout
T,T^{\prime},\dots
denote scaling sets.

10.1
.

Let
T
be a scaling set.
A
T
-valued scaling sequence
is a function
d\colon\mathbb{N}\to T
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}d(n)=\infty
.
We call
d
a
scaling sequence
if
T=(0,\infty)
.
We will write
d_{n}
for
d(n)
when this is convenient, which is the usual practice.

Throughout this section
\omega
denotes a non principal ultrafilter on
\mathbb{N}
.
The elements of
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,d)
are denoted by
\{g_{n}\}
where
(g_{n})
is a sequence in
G
such that
\limsup_{n}\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}\|g_{n}\|_{G}<\infty
.

Proposition 10.2
.

Let
T
be a scaling set.
For any
T
-valued scaling sequence and any non principal ultrafilter
\omega
there is a well defined canonical homomorphism

\rho_{\omega,d}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)\xrightarrow{\ [g]\mapsto\{g\circ d\}\ }\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,d).

It is Lipschitz with constant
1
.

Proof.

Since
d_{n}\in T
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{n}=\infty
, Lemma
6.5
implies

\limsup_{n}\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}\|g(d_{n})\|\leq\limsup_{t\in T}\tfrac{1}{t}\|g(t)\|\leq\|[g]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)}<\infty.

Thus, if
g\in\mathcal{C}(G,T)
then
g\circ d\colon\mathbb{N}\to G
represents an element in
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,d)
.
To see that
\rho
is independent of representatives, suppose that
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(G,T)
represent the same element in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
.
Then
[g^{-1}g^{\prime}]
is the trivial element in
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
so Lemma
6.6
implies

\lim_{n}\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}\|g(d_{n})^{-1}g^{\prime}(d_{n})\|=\lim_{t\in T}\tfrac{1}{t}\|g(t)^{-1}g^{\prime}(t)\|=0.

Therefore
\{g\circ d\}=\{g^{\prime}\circ d\}
in
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,d)
.
It follows that
\rho
is well defined.
It is a homomorphism because the group structures on
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)
and
\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,d)
are induced from that of
G
.
∎

Recall Definition
9.1
of independence of scaling.
Also recall that
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
denotes
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,(0,\infty))
with the maximal scaling set
(0,\infty)
.

Theorem 10.3
.

The following are equivalent for a group
G
.

(a)

G
is independent of scaling.

(b)

The homomorphism
\rho_{\omega,d}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,\mathbf{d})
is injective for any non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
and any scaling sequence
d
.

If, in addition,
\|[g]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}\|g(t)\|
for every
[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
, then
\rho_{\omega,d}
is an isometric embedding.

Proof.

(a)

\implies

(b)

Fix some
\omega
and
d
and consider some
[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
in the kernel of
\rho=\rho_{\omega,d}
.
Then
\omega\operatorname{-}\lim_{n}\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}\|g(d_{n})\|=0
and therefore there is a subsequence
\{n_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{d_{n_{k}}}\|g(d_{n_{k}})\|=0
.
By further passage to a subsequence we may assume that
\{d_{n_{k}}\}_{k}
is increasing.
Set
T=\{d(n_{k}):k\geq 1\}
.
This is a scaling set and we set
[h]=\widehat{\operatorname{res}}_{T}([g])
.
Then
h=g|_{T}
and since
d_{n_{k}}
is increasing

\limsup_{t\in T}\tfrac{1}{t}\|h(t)\|=\limsup_{k}\tfrac{1}{d(n_{k})}\|g(d(n_{k}))\|=0.

By Lemma
6.6

[h]=1
, but by hypothesis
\widehat{\operatorname{res}}_{T}
is injective, so
[g]=1
.

(b)

\implies

(a)

Consider a scaling set
T
.
Choose a scaling sequence
d_{1}<d_{2}<\dots
in
T
(this can be done since
T
is unbounded).
By inspection the injective homomorphism
\rho_{\omega,d}\colon\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\to\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,d)
factors as

\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)\xrightarrow{\widehat{\operatorname{res}}_{T}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G,T)\xrightarrow{\rho_{\omega,d}}\operatorname{Cone}_{\omega}(G,d).

Therefore
\widehat{\operatorname{res}}_{T}
is injective and by Proposition
6.13
it is an isometry.

Suppose that
\lim_{t>0}\tfrac{1}{t}\|g(t)\|=\|[g]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}
where
[g]\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)
.
Clearly
\|\rho([g])=\|\{g\circ d\}\|=\omega\operatorname{-}\lim_{n}\tfrac{1}{d_{n}}\|g(d_{n})\|
and the
\omega
-limit is an honest limit by the assumption.
Then
\|\rho([g])\|=\|[g]\|_{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(G)}
and
\rho
is an isometry.
∎

11.
Examples: Abelian and nilpotent groups and verbal norms

We begin with the simplest examples of abelian groups.

.

Let
\mathbb{Z}
be equipped with the standard metric.

(a)

(b)

.
By Proposition
5.8
and Paragraph
6.3
the homomorphism

\hat{\pi}\circ\iota

With the notation in Paragraph

, let

We claim that

is a homomorphism it suffices to show that the image of any generator
\alpha=\overline{n}(r)\in F_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{Z})
is in the image of

.
Set

.
It is represented by the function

gives by

.

Proposition 11.4

m_{2}^{\prime}=m_{2}-1
intervals.
Also

N^{\prime}=N-|J_{m_{2}}|>N-(k+1)\ell\geq(m_{1}+m_{2})\ell-(k+1)\ell=(m_{1}^{\prime}+m_{2}^{\prime})\ell.

By the induction hypothesis there are
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\prime}
and
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{\prime}
which intersect at an interval of length
\geq\ell
.
Hence one of the intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{m_{1}-k}
and
J_{1},\dots,J_{m_{2}-1}
intersect at an interval of length
\geq\ell
.
This completes the induction step.
∎

Lemma 12.12
.

Consider
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{p}\in\Theta
where
p\geq 1
.
Assume that
\theta_{i}\neq\theta_{i+1}
for all
1\leq i<p
.
Set
\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(|\theta_{1}|,\dots,|\theta_{p}|)
, and for every
1\leq i\leq p
set

\kappa_{i}=\frac{|\theta_{i}|}{9\ell+|\theta_{i}|\cdot\tfrac{1+|\theta_{i}|}{\tau(\theta_{i})}}.

Let
n_{1},\dots,n_{p}
be integers such that
|n_{i}|>\tfrac{6\ell}{|\theta_{i}|}
for all
1\leq i\leq p
.
Then

\|\theta_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p}^{n_{p}}\|_{G}>\min_{1\leq i\leq p}|n_{i}|\cdot\kappa_{i}.

Proof.

Let
g
be the (not necessarily reduced) word
\theta_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\theta_{p}^{n_{p}}
.
Let
u
be a sequence in
g
of length

m\leq\min_{1\leq i\leq p}|n_{i}|\cdot\kappa_{i}.

We will show that
g-u
is not the trivial element and by this complete the proof since the norm on
G
coincides with the cancellation norm.

Partition the sequence
u
to subsequences
u_{i}\subseteq\theta_{i}^{n_{i}}
of length
m_{i}
each (
1\leq i\leq p
).
Thus,
m=\sum_{i}m_{i}
.
By Lemma
12.10
the removal of
u_{i}
from
\theta_{i}^{n_{i}}
yields, after reduction, a reduced word
w_{i}
which is a product of at most
m_{i}+1

\theta_{i}
-packets of total length

\displaystyle|w_{i}|

\displaystyle\geq|n_{i}|\cdot|\theta_{i}|-m_{i}\cdot|\theta_{i}|\cdot\tfrac{1+|\theta_{i}|}{\tau(\theta_{i})}

\displaystyle\geq|n_{i}|\cdot|\theta_{i}|-m\cdot|\theta_{i}|\cdot\tfrac{1+|\theta_{i}|}{\tau(\theta_{i})}

(since
m_{i}\leq m
)

\displaystyle\geq m\tfrac{|\theta_{i}|}{\kappa_{i}}-m\cdot|\theta_{i}|\cdot\tfrac{1+|\theta_{i}|}{\tau(\theta_{i})}

(since
m\leq\kappa_{i}|n_{i}|
)

\displaystyle=9\ell m

\displaystyle\text{(by definition of $\kappa_{i}$)}.

Then
g-u
is equivalent to
w_{1}\cdots w_{p}
.
For any
1\leq i<p
we will now examine the reduction of
w_{i}w_{i+1}
at the juncture.
This reduction boils down to a word
v_{i}
such that
w_{i}=w_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}
and
w_{i+1}=v_{i}^{-1}w^{\prime}_{i+1}
and
w_{i}^{\prime}w^{\prime}_{i+1}
is reduced.
We claim that for every
1\leq i<p

(8)

|v_{i}|\leq\min\left\{\tfrac{|w_{i}|}{3},\tfrac{|w_{i+1}|}{3}\right\}+\ell.

Assume false, i.e
|v_{i}|>\min\left\{\tfrac{|w_{i}|}{3},\tfrac{|w_{i+1}|}{3}\right\}+\ell
.
Then
|v_{i}|>(3m+1)\ell
.

If
m=0
then
m_{i}=0
for all
i
and then
u
is empty so
w_{i}=\theta_{i}^{n_{i}}
for all
i
.
Then
v_{i}
is a
\theta_{i}
-packet and
v_{i}^{-1}
is a
\theta_{i+1}
packet of length
>(3m+1)\ell\geq\ell\geq\operatorname{lcm}(|\theta_{i}|,|\theta_{i+1}|)
.
Lemma
12.9
implies that
\theta_{i}=\theta_{i+1}
which is a contradiction.
This proves (
8
) if
m=0
.
So we assume that
m\geq 1
.

Given
1\leq i<p
, recall that
w_{i}
is a product of at most
m_{i}+1

\theta_{i}
-packets and similarly
w_{i+1}
is a product of at most
m_{i+1}+1

\theta_{i+1}
-packets.
Therefore
v_{i}
is a product of at most
m_{i}+1

\theta_{i}
packets and
v_{i}^{-1}
is a product of at most
m_{i+1}

\theta_{i+1}
-packets.
If we set
N=|v_{i}|
, then the partition of
w_{i}
into the
\theta_{i}
-packets gives a partition
\mathcal{P}_{1}
of
\{1,\dots,N\}
(the letters in
v_{i}
) into at most
m_{i}+1
intervals.
Similarly, the partition of
w_{i+1}
into the
\theta_{i+1}
-packets gives a partition
\mathcal{P}_{2}
of
\{1,\dots,N\}
into at most
m_{i+1}+1
intervals.
Then

N=|v_{i}|>(3m+1)\ell\geq(m+2)\ell=((m_{i}+1)+(m_{i+1}+1))\ell.

Lemma
12.11
implies that in the process of reduction of
w_{i}w_{i+1}
there is a collision of a
\theta_{i}
-packet in
w_{i}
with a
\theta_{i+1}
-packet in
w_{i+1}
at an interval
v_{i}^{\prime}
in

implies that
\theta_{i}=\theta_{i+1}
which is a contradiction. This proves (
8
) in the case

.

Let us set
v_{0}
and
v_{p}
to be the empty words, which we regard as a prefix of
w_{1}
and a suffix of
w_{p}
.
Clearly (
8
) applies to then as well, where we agree that
w_{0}
and

Proof of Theorem
12.4
.

.

.
