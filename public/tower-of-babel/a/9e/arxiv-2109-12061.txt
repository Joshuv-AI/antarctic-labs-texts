Computing the Barnes G G -function and the gamma function in the entire complex plane
Alexey Kuznetsov

Abstract
We present an algorithm for generating approximations for the logarithm of Barnes G G -function in the half-plane Re ​ ( z ) ≥ 3 / 2 \textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2 . These approximations involve only elementary functions and are easy to implement. The algorithm is based on a two-point Padé approximation and we use it to provide two approximations to ln ⁡ ( G ⁡ ( z ) ) \ln(G(z)) , accurate to 3 × 10 − 16 3\times 10^{-16} and 3 × 10 − 31 3\times 10^{-31} in the half-plane Re ​ ( z ) ≥ 3 / 2 \textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2 ; a reflection formula is then used to compute Barnes G G -function in the entire complex plane. A by-product of our algorithm is that it also produces accurate approximations to the gamma function.

Introduction

Barnes
G
-function is an entire function defined via the Weierstrass product

G(1+z)=(2\pi)^{z/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(z+(1+\gamma)z^{2})}\prod\limits_{k\geq 1}\Big(1+\frac{z}{k}\Big)^{k}e^{-z+\frac{z^{2}}{2k}},\;\;\;z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(1)

Here
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
This function satisfies

G(z+1)=\Gamma(z)G(z).

(2)

and it is known
[
22
]
that
G
is a unique function satisfying (
2
) subject to conditions

G(1)=1
and

\frac{d^{3}}{dz^{3}}\ln G(z)\geq 0\;\;{\textrm{for }}z>1.

The systematic study of
G(z)
(and certain related functions, including a more general double gamma function
G(z;\tau)
and a hierarchy of multiple gamma functions
\Gamma_{n}(z)
) was initiated by Barnes in a series of papers
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. The
G
-function was in fact introduced in an earlier paper by Alexeiewsky
[
1
]
and in some older literature it was sometimes called “Alexeiewsky function”, though by now the name “Barnes
G
-function” seems to be universally accepted.

Starting in 1980s the Barnes
G
-function and related double gamma functions started to appear frequently in various areas of Mathematics and Physics. Shintani
[
20
]
used double gamma function to give a new proof of Kronecker’s limit formula. The Barnes
G
-function was found to have connections to determinants of Laplacians
[
19
,
21
]
and it also appeared in the Keating-Snaith
conjecture on moments of the Riemann zeta function
[
10
]
.
More recently this function has found many aplications in Probability Theory
[
11
,
16
,
23
]
.

Despite this increased interest in the Barnes
G
-function and its apparent usefullness in many applications, no work was done so far on developing efficient numerical algorithms for computing
G(z)
(though several recent papers studied asymptotic expansions of
G(z)
, see
[
8
,
9
,
15
,
24
]
). It is the purpose of this paper to correct this omission. Our main contribution is an algorithm that can produce accurate and simple approximations to
\ln(G(z))
in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2
–
we demonstrate this by providing two such approximations, one valid to
3\times 10^{-16}
and another to
3\times 10^{-31}
.
With the help of a reflection formula and the functional equation
(
2
) this allows one to compute
\ln(G(z))
in the entire complex plane.
Our algorithm also generats accurate approximations to the gamma function, though this is probably less important as there already exist excellent approximations to
\Gamma(z)
, such as the Lanczos approximation
[
12
,
17
]
.

The key part of our algorithm consists in approximating the function

f(x):=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{3}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\coth\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{x}-\frac{x}{12}\right],\;\;\;x>0,

(3)

by a finite sum of exponential functions
\sum_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}x}
, and this is done by finding a two-point Padé approximation to the Laplace transform of
f(x)
. This two-point Padé approximation method is of independent interest, as it can be used to provide approximations (by sums of exponential functions) to any function
g
for which derivatives
g^{(n)}(0)
and “moments”
\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}g(x){\textnormal{d}}x
are known explicitly or can be computed to sufficiently high precision.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we explain how approximations to
f(x)
by a finite sum of exponential functions lead to approximations to
\ln(\Gamma(z))
and
\ln(G(z))
in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2
and how one can extend these approximations to the entire complex plane by using functional equations and reflection formulas. Section
3
describes the two-point Padé approximation algorithm for finding exponential sum approximations and in Section
4
we present numerical results that illustrate the performance of this algorithm. In Appendix
A
we describe an algorithm for computing the dilogarithm function (this is needed for implementing the reflection formula for the Barnes
G
-function), and in Appendix
B
we present the numerical values of the coefficients
c_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
.

2
Approximatng logarithms of
\Gamma(z)
and
G(z)

The starting point for our investigation is the following proposition, which reduces the computation of the gamma function and the Barnes
G
-function to the computation of

F(z):=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zx}xf(x){\textnormal{d}}x

(4)

and its derivative
F^{\prime}(z)
. In what follows,
A
denotes the the Glaisher-Kinkelin constant and
\ln(z)
denotes the principal branch of the logarithm function.

Proposition 1
.

For
\textnormal{Re}(z)>0

\displaystyle\ln(\Gamma(z))

\displaystyle=\left(z-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln(z)-z+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)+\frac{1}{12z}-F^{\prime}(z-1),

(5)

\displaystyle\ln(G(z))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{z^{2}}{2}-z+\frac{5}{12}\right)\ln(z)-\frac{3}{4}z^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)(z-1)+z

(6)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{12}-\ln(A)-\frac{1}{12z}+F(z-1)-(z-1)F^{\prime}(z-1).

Proof.

Formula (
5
) is obtained from the well-known Binet’s integral representation

\ln(\Gamma(z))=\left(z-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln(z)-z+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-zx}}{x}\left[\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}-\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{2}\right]{\textnormal{d}}x,

by noting that
f
can be expressed in an equivalent form

f(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{3}}\left[\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}-\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{x}{12}\right].

(7)

Next, by setting
N=1
in

[
9
]
[Equation (12)] we obtain

\ln(G(z))=(z-1)\ln(\Gamma(z))-\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{2}-z+\frac{1}{6}\right)\ln(z)+\frac{z^{2}}{4}-\ln(A)+F(z-1),

and this result, together with (
5
), implies (
6
).

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Next we describe our approximation algorithm.

Approximations in the domain
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2

Let
p
be a positive integer. Assume we have found
coefficients
\{c_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
and

\{\lambda_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
such that
\textnormal{Re}(\lambda_{j})>0
and

\phi(x):=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}x}

(8)

is a “good” approximation to
f(x)
on
x\in(0,\infty)
, in the sense that the numbers

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}

\displaystyle:=2\sup\limits_{x>0}\Big|x^{2}(f(x)-\phi(x))\Big|,

(9)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}

\displaystyle:=2\sup\limits_{x>0}\Big|3x(f(x)-\phi(x))+x^{2}(f^{\prime}(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x))\Big|,

(10)

are small.
For
\textnormal{Re}(z)>0
we define a rational function

\Phi(z):=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\frac{c_{j}}{(z+\lambda_{j})^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zx}x\phi(x){\textnormal{d}}x

(11)

and we define approximations to
\ln(\Gamma(z))
and
\ln(G(z))
in the form

\displaystyle\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))

\displaystyle=\left(z-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln(z)-z+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)+\frac{1}{12z}-\Phi^{\prime}(z-1),

(12)

\displaystyle\ln(\widehat{G}(z))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{z^{2}}{2}-z+\frac{5}{12}\right)\ln(z)-\frac{3}{4}z^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)(z-1)+z

(13)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{12}-\ln(A)-\frac{1}{12z}+\Phi(z-1)-(z-1)\Phi^{\prime}(z-1).

Proposition 2
.

In the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2
we have

|\ln(\Gamma(z))-\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))|\leq\epsilon_{1}

(14)

and

|\ln(G(z))-\ln(\widehat{G}(z))|\leq\epsilon_{2}.

(15)

Proof.

From (
5
) and (
12
) we see that

\ln(\Gamma(z))-\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))=\Phi^{\prime}(z-1)-F^{\prime}(z-1).

We set
w=z-1
and estimate for
\textnormal{Re}(w)\geq 1/2

|F^{\prime}(w)-\Phi^{\prime}(w)|=\Big|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-wx}x^{2}(f(x)-\phi(x)){\textnormal{d}}x\Big|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x/2}x^{2}|f(x)-\phi(x)|{\textnormal{d}}x\leq\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x/2}{\textnormal{d}}x=\epsilon_{1},

which proves (
14
). Similarly, from (
6
) and (
13
) we see that

\ln(G(z))-\ln(\widehat{G}(z))=F(z-1)-(z-1)F^{\prime}(z-1)-\Phi(z-1)+(z-1)\Phi^{\prime}(z-1).

We perform integration by parts

wF^{\prime}(w)=-w\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-wx}x^{2}f(x){\textnormal{d}}x=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-wx}(2xf(x)+x^{2}f^{\prime}(x)){\textnormal{d}}x

and estimate for
\textnormal{Re}(w)\geq 1/2

\displaystyle|F(w)-wF^{\prime}(w)-\Phi(w)+w\Phi^{\prime}(w)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-wx}(3x(f(x)-\phi(x))+x^{2}(f^{\prime}(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x))){\textnormal{d}}x\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x/2}|3x(f(x)-\phi(x))+x^{2}(f^{\prime}(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x))|{\textnormal{d}}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x/2}{\textnormal{d}}x=\epsilon_{2}.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Extending the domain to
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 1/2

In the vertical strip
1/2\leq\textnormal{Re}(z)<3/2
we compute
\ln(\Gamma(z))

via functional equation

\ln(\Gamma(z))=\ln(\Gamma(z+1))-\ln(z),

and then we compute

\ln(G(z))
via

\ln(G(z))=\ln(G(z+1))-\ln(\Gamma(z)).

Approximations in the domain
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
: applying reflection formulas

It is well-known that Gamma function satisfies the reflection identity

\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)}.

(16)

In the previous sections we described an algorithm for computing
\ln(\Gamma(z))
for
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 1/2
and the above identity gives a simple algorithm for computing
\Gamma(z)
for
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
. However, some additional work is needed if we want to compute
\ln(\Gamma(z))
for
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
while preserving analyticity of this function in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]
. First of all, we rewrite the above reflection identity in the form

\Gamma(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-z)}\times\frac{-2\pi{\textnormal{i}}e^{\pi{\textnormal{i}}z}}{1-e^{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}z}}.

We assume that
\textnormal{Im}(z)>0
and take logarithms of both sides of the above equation and obtain

\ln(\Gamma(z))=-\ln(\Gamma(1-z))+\ln(2\pi)-\frac{\pi{\textnormal{i}}}{2}+\pi{\textnormal{i}}z-\ln(1-e^{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}z}).

(17)

One can check that we chose the correct branch of logarithm by noting that the above equation is valid if
\textnormal{Re}(z)=1/2
and
\textnormal{Im}(z)\to 0^{+}
. The right-hand side of (
17
) is analytic in
\textnormal{Im}(z)>0
, thus we can use this identity to compute
\ln(\Gamma(z))
for
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
and
\textnormal{Im}(z)>0
. This identity is also used for
0<z<1/2
(again, we need to use the principal branch of the logarithm). When
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
and
\textnormal{Im}(z)<0
we use the above method coupled with the identity
\overline{\ln(\Gamma(z))}=\ln(\Gamma(\bar{z}))
.

Computing
\ln(G(z))
in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
follows a similar procedure.
In this case the relevant reflection formula is

\frac{G(1+z)}{G(1-z)}=(2\pi)^{z}\exp\Big(-\int_{0}^{z}\pi t\cot(\pi t){\textnormal{d}}t\Big).

Using formulas (4.6), (8.43) in
[
13
]
the integral in the right-hand side of the above identity can be expressed in terms of the dilogarithm function

{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(z):=-\int_{0}^{z}\ln(1-t)\frac{{\textnormal{d}}t}{t}=\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{z^{2}}{k^{2}},\;\;\;|z|\leq 1,

(18)

so that changing the variable
z\mapsto z-1
we would obtain a reflection formula in the form

\displaystyle\ln(G(z))

\displaystyle=\ln(G(2-z))+(z-1)\ln(2\pi)+\frac{\pi{\textnormal{i}}}{2}(z^{2}-2z+5/6)

(19)

\displaystyle-(z-1)\ln(1-e^{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}z})-\frac{1}{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}}{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(e^{2\pi{\textnormal{i}}z}),\;\;\;\textnormal{Im}(z)>0.

Again, one can check that we have the correct branch of the logarithm by taking
\textnormal{Re}(z)=1
and
\textnormal{Im}(z)\to 0^{+}
and using the fact that
G(1)=1
and

{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(1)=\pi^{2}/6
.
We note that the right-hand side of (
19
) is analytic in the half-plane
\textnormal{Im}(z)>0
, thus we can use (
19
)
to compute
\ln(G(z))
for
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
and
\textnormal{Im}(z)>0
. An efficient algorithm for computing the dilogarithm function
{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(z)
for
|z|\leq 1
is presented in the Appendix
A
. We also use formula (
19
) when
0<z<1/2
. When
\textnormal{Re}(z)<1/2
and
\textnormal{Im}(z)<0
we use the above method coupled with the identity
\overline{\ln(G(z))}=\ln(G(\bar{z}))
.

This concludes our description of the method for computing
\ln(\Gamma(z))
and
\ln(G(z))
in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]
. The only outstanding question is how to approximate the function
f
defined in (
3
) by a finite sum of exponential functions of the form (
8
) so that the errors
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
in (
9
) and (
10
) are small. We propose a solution to this problem in the next section.

3
Approximating a function by a finite sum of exponential functions

There exist a number of methods for approximating a given function by a finite sum of exponential functions. In
[
18
]
the problem of finding the best approximation on a finite interval in the Chebyshev sense is considered. A method based on Hankel matrices is introduced in
[
5
]
and a Poisson summation approach is presented in
[
6
]
. See also
[
7
]
for results on approximating
1/x
on
[1,\infty)
. In this paper we propose a different approach for approximating functions by finite sums of exponential functions: this method is based on a two-point Padé approximation and it is particularly well-suited for the function
f
given in (
3
).

First we show that for the function
f
defined by (
3
) one can compute explicitly (in terms of values of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
at positive integers and some other well-known constants) both the derivatives at zero and the moments.

Lemma 1
.

(i)

For
n\geq 0

f^{(n)}(0)=(-1)^{n}n!\sum\limits_{k=0}^{[n/2]}\frac{B_{2k+4}}{(n-2k)!(2k+4)!},

(20)

where
B_{n}
are Bernoulli numbers.

(ii)

Let
\mu_{n}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}f(x){\textnormal{d}}x
. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{\prime}(-2)+\frac{1}{72}=-\frac{\zeta(3)}{8\pi^{2}}+\frac{1}{72},

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=-\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-\frac{1}{6}=\ln(A)-\frac{1}{4},

\displaystyle\mu_{2}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{\prime}(0)+\frac{11}{12}=-\ln(2\pi)/2+\frac{11}{12},

\displaystyle\mu_{3}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{s\to 1}\Big(\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}\Big)-\frac{7}{12}=\gamma-7/12,

and for
n\geq 4

\mu_{n}=(n-3)!\Big[\zeta(n-2)-\frac{1}{n-3}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n-2}{12}\Big].

Here
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant and
A
is the Glaisher-Kinkelin constant.

Proof.

Formula (
20
) follows from the Taylor series expansion of the hyperbolic cotangent function:

\frac{1}{2}\coth\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{2n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n-1}.

Formulas for moments
\mu_{n}
follow from the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s+2}f(x){\textnormal{d}}x=\Gamma(s)\Big[\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{12}\Big],\;\;\;\textnormal{Re}(s)>-3,

(21)

which can be obtained from (
7
) and integral representations

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-x}x^{s-1}}{e^{x}-1}{\textnormal{d}}x=\Gamma(s)(\zeta(s)-1),\;\;\;\textnormal{Re}(s)>1,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}x^{s-1}{\textnormal{d}}x=\Gamma(s),\;\;\;\textnormal{Re}(s)>0.

The above two identities can be used to deduce (
21
) in the domain
\textnormal{Re}(s)>1
and extension of (
21
) to the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(s)>-3
is done by analytic continuation (note that both sides of (
21
) are well-defined and analytic in
\textnormal{Re}(s)>-3
). Now we compute

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x){\textnormal{d}}x

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{s\to-2}\Gamma(s)\Big[\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{12}\Big]

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{s\to-2}(s-2)\Gamma(s)\times\frac{\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{12}}{s-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}s}\Big[\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{12}\Big]\bigg|_{s=-2}=\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{\prime}(-2)+\frac{1}{72},

and the second expression for
\mu_{0}
is obtained from the reflection formula:

\zeta^{\prime}(-2)=-\zeta(3)/(4\pi^{2})
. The proof of formulas for
\mu_{1}
,
\mu_{2}
and
\mu_{3}
is identical and we leave all details to the reader. Formulas for
\mu_{n}
with
n\geq 4
follow directly from (
21
).

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Now we describe our method for finding approximations to
f(x)
in the form

\phi(x)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}x}
. We fix two non-negative integers
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
such that
N_{1}+N_{2}=2p
and we aim to find coefficients

\{c_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
and
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
such that the functions
f
and
\phi
have the same first
N_{1}
moments and the same first
N_{2}
terms in Taylor series expansion at zero. In other words, we want to enforce the following conditions:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}\phi(x){\textnormal{d}}x=n!\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}\lambda_{j}^{-n-1}=\mu_{n},\;\;\;n=0,1,2,\dots,N_{1}-1,

(22)

\displaystyle\phi^{(n)}(0)=(-1)^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}\lambda_{j}^{n}=f^{(n)}(0),\;\;\;n=0,1,2,\dots,N_{2}-1.

(23)

As we will show next, this problem is equivalent to a two-point Padé approximation, with the two points being
z=0
and
z=\infty
. Indeed, let us consider the Laplace transform

\Psi(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zx}\phi(x){\textnormal{d}}x=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\frac{c_{j}}{z+\lambda_{j}}.

(24)

From the above partial fraction decomposition it is easy to obtain the following series representations

\displaystyle\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}z^{-n-1}\Big[(-1)^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}\lambda_{j}^{n}\Big],

\displaystyle\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}z^{n}\Big[(-1)^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}\lambda_{j}^{-n-1}\Big],

where the first one converges for all
z
large enough and the second for all
z
in a neighbourhood of zero. In light of the above equations we see that the problem of finding a function
\phi(x)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}x}
that satisfies conditions
(
22
) and (
23
) is equivalent to the following problem: find the coefficients
\{a_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
and

\{b_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
of the rational function

\Psi(z)=\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}=\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{p-1}z^{p-1}}{b_{0}+b_{1}z+\dots+b_{p-1}z^{p-1}+z^{p}}

(25)

such that

\displaystyle\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}z+\beta_{2}z^{2}+\dots+\beta_{N_{1}-1}z^{N_{1}-1}+O(z^{N_{1}}),\;\;\;z\to 0,

(26)

\displaystyle\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}z^{-1}+\alpha_{1}z^{-2}+\dots\alpha_{N_{2}-1}z^{-N_{2}}+O(z^{-N_{2}-1}),\;\;\;z\to\infty,

(27)

where we denoted

\alpha_{n}:=f^{(n)}(0),\;\;\;\beta_{n}:=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\mu_{n}.

(28)

This is precisely the two-point Padé approximation problem.
As in the case of the classical (one-point) Padé approximation, the above problem can be reduced to solving a system of linear equations.
First we rewrite equation (
26
) in the form

\displaystyle a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{p-1}z^{p-1}

\displaystyle=\Big(b_{0}+b_{1}z+\dots+b_{p-1}z^{p-1}+z^{p}\Big)

\displaystyle\times\Big(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}z+\beta_{2}z^{2}+\dots+\beta_{N_{1}-1}z^{N_{1}-1}+O(z^{N_{1}})\Big).

Comparing the coefficients in front of
z^{k}
with
k=0,1,\dots,N_{1}-1
we obtain equations

a_{k}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{k\leq p-1\}}=\beta_{k-p}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{p\leq k\}}+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}b_{j}\beta_{k-j}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{0\leq k-j\leq N_{1}-1\}}.

(29)

Next we introduce a variable
u=1/z
and rewrite (
27
) in the form

\displaystyle a_{p-1}+a_{p-2}u+\dots+a_{0}u^{p-1}

\displaystyle=\Big(1+b_{p-1}u+\dots+b_{0}u^{p}\Big)

\displaystyle\times\Big(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}u+\dots+\alpha_{N_{2}-1}u^{N_{2}-1}+O(u^{N_{2}})\Big).

Comparing the coefficients in front of
u^{k}
with
k=0,1,\dots,N_{2}-1
we obtain equations

a_{p-1-k}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{k\leq p-1\}}=\alpha_{k}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{p-1}b_{j}\alpha_{k+j-p}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{0\leq k+j-p\leq N_{2}-1\}}.

(30)

Now we have
N_{1}
linear equations of the form (
29
) and
N_{2}
linear equations of the form (
30
) – together these give us
N_{1}+N_{2}=2p
equations for
2p
unknowns
\{a_{j},b_{j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p-1}
. To rewrite these equations in a matrix form (that is more suitable for numerical computation),
we introduce a
2p\times 2p
matrix
{\bf A}=\{A_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq 2p}
and a column vector
\vec{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2p}
by specifying their elements as follows: for
k=0,1,\dots,N_{1}-1
and
j=0,1,\dots,p-1

\displaystyle A_{k+1,k+1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{1}}_{\{k\leq p-1\}},

\displaystyle A_{k+1,p+1+j}

\displaystyle=-\beta_{k-j}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{0\leq k-j\leq N_{1}-1\}},

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k-p}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{p\leq k\}},

and for
l=0,1,\dots,N_{2}-1
and
j=0,1,\dots,p-1

\displaystyle A_{N_{1}+l+1,p-l}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{1}}_{\{l\leq p-1\}},

\displaystyle A_{N_{1}+l+1,p+1+j}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{l+j-p}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{0\leq l+j-p\leq N_{2}-1\}},

\displaystyle y_{N_{1}+l+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{l}.

Now the two sets of linear equations (

) and

) can be represented as a single matrix equation

, where

After we solve equation
{\bf A}\vec{\bf x}=\vec{\bf y}
and find
\vec{\bf x}
, we compute

and
b_{j}=x_{p+j+1}
for

, and this gives us the desired rational function

in the form (

). Performing the partial fraction decomposition (and assuming that all the roots of the denominator

are simple) we would obtain the expression of

) and this would give us the desired coefficients

) and (

).

Remark 1:

Note that the functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
defined in (
11
) and (
24
) satisfy
\Phi(z)=-\Psi^{\prime}(z)
. Thus the rational function
\Phi
has the same
N_{1}-1
first terms of Taylor expansion at zero and the same first
N_{2}+1
asymptotic terms as
z\to+\infty
as the function
F(z)
. Therefore our approximations
\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))
and
\ln(\widehat{G}(z))
, which were introduced in (
14
) and (
15
), satisfy the following properties:
as
z\to 1^{+}

\displaystyle\ln(\Gamma(z))-\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))=O((z-1)^{N_{1}-1}),

\displaystyle\ln(G(z))-\ln(\widehat{G}(z))=O((z-1)^{N_{1}-1}),

and as
z\to\infty

\displaystyle\ln(\Gamma(z))-\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))=O(z^{-N_{2}-2}),

\displaystyle\ln(G(z))-\ln(\widehat{G}(z))=O(z^{-N_{2}-2}).

The first of these is probably not very informative, as we only use approximations

\ln(\widehat{\Gamma}(z))
and
\ln(\widehat{G}(z))
in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2
(where
z
is bounded away from
1
), but the second result is useful as it tells us that our approximations in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(z)\geq 3/2
become more accurate as
|z|
increases.

To concluse this section, we would like to point out that there is no theoretical justification for the above algorithm. One can not be certain that the system
{\bf A}\vec{\bf x}=\vec{\bf y}
has a solution or that all roots of polynomial
Q(z)
in (
25
) are simple (though in all our numerical experiments this was always the case). Even if the algorithm does produce the numbers
\{c_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
and
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
, there is no guarantee that this would result in a good approximation

\phi(x)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}c_{j}e^{-\lambda_{j}x}
to
f(x)
(in the sense of errors

\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
in (
9
) and (
10
) being small). Existing results on convergence of Padé approximations to Stieltjes functions are not applicable here since the function
f(x)
is not completely monotone (thus its Laplace transform is not a Stieltjes function). Lacking any theoretical justification for the above algorithm, in the next section we will try to convince the reader that it works well by presenting numerical evidence.

Appendix A
Computing the dilogarithm function for
|z|\leq 1

We will follow closely the discussion in Section 4 in
[
14
]
. An efficient method for computing
{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(z)
for
|z|\leq 1
, which avoids the issue of
slow convergence of the series
(
18
) when
|z|
is close to one, can be obtained via the so-called Debye function

D(w):=\int_{0}^{w}\frac{u{\textnormal{d}}u}{e^{u}-1}.

(A.32)

It is easy to see (by substitution
t=1-e^{-u}
) that

{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(z)=D(-\ln(1-z)).

(A.33)

The Debye function has a Taylor series expansion

D(w)=w-\frac{w^{2}}{4}+\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}B_{2n}\frac{w^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!},

(A.34)

where
B_{n}
are Bernoulli numbers. From the asymptotics of Bernoulli numbers

|B_{2n}|=2(2n)!(2\pi)^{-2n}(1+o(1)),\;\;\;n\to+\infty

it is easy to deduce that the above Taylor series converges for
|w|<2\pi
(this fact also follows by noting that the integrand in
(
A.32
) is analytic in the disk
|u|<2\pi
, which implies that Debye function
D(w)
must be analytic in
|w|<2\pi
).

Now, our algorithm for computing
{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(z)
for
|z|\leq 1
consists of the following two steps:

(i)

If
z\in{\Omega_{1}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;|z|\leq 1,\;\textnormal{Re}(z)\leq 1/2\}
then we use identity (
A.33
) and compute
D(-\ln(1-z))
via series expansion
(
A.34
). One can check that for
z\in{\Omega}_{1}
we have
|\ln(1-z)|\leq\pi/3
, thus the terms in the series (
A.34
) decay at the rate
O(6^{-2n})
.

(ii)

If
z\in{\Omega_{2}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;|z|\leq 1,\;\textnormal{Re}(z)>1/2\}
then we first use identity (3.3) in
[
14
]

{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(z)=-{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(1-z)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\ln(1-z)\ln(z).

If
z\in\Omega_{2}
then
1-z\in\Omega_{1}
, thus
{\textrm{Li}}_{2}(1-z)
can be computed by the same approach as described in item (i) above.
