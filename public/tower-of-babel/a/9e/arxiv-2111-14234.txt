Abstract
For fixed positive integers n n , we study the solution of the equation n = k + p k n=k+p_{k} , where p k p_{k} denotes the k k th prime number, by means of the iterative method k j + 1 = π ⁡ ( n − k j ) , k 0 = π ⁡ ( n ) , k_{j+1}=\pi(n-k_{j}),\qquad k_{0}=\pi(n), which converges to the solution of the equation, if it exists. We also analyze the equation n = a ​ k + b ​ p k n=ak+bp_{k} for fixed integer values a ≠ 0 a\neq 0 and b > 0 b>0 , and its solution by means of a corresponding iterative method. The case a > 0 a>0 is somewhat similar to the case a = b = 1 a=b=1 , while for a < 0 a<0 the convergence and usefulness of the method are less satisfactory. The paper also includes a study of the dynamics of the iterative methods.

Introduction and main results

Let us imagine that we want to know if a Fibonacci number, a pentagonal number, one of a pair of amicable numbers or a Pythagorean triple, the terms in a sequence in the On-line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS,
[
8
]
), or any other positive integer can be written as

n=k+p_{k},

(1)

where
(p_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is the sequence of the prime numbers. (Let us note that neither Mathematica, Maple nor SageMath are able to solve the equation (
1
) even in simple cases with small numbers, for instance for
n=10+p_{10}=39
.)

Of course, one can look at the sequence
(k+p_{k})_{k\geq 1}
(which is the sequence
A014688
in the OEIS) and check if
n
occurs in the sequence; but this can be a hard task. Instead, we give here an iterative process which, given
n
, tells whether the equation (
1
) has a solution or not, and, in the affirmative case, provides the solution
k
.

It is worth noting that there exist a large number of iterative methods for solving equations of the form
f(x)=0
where
f
is a function defined on
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{C}^{d}
or a Banach space. However, there are very few iterative methods for solving equations in the theory of numbers.
Perhaps the best example of an iterative method for solving an equation in this area is
[
6
]
, where the authors show how classical rootfinding methods from numerical analysis can be used to calculate inverses of units modulo prime powers.

Along the paper, we will use some well known properties of prime numbers, which can be found in many texts; see, for instance,
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
7
]
.
As usual,
\pi(x)
denotes the quantity of prime numbers
\leq x
, which is a nondecreasing function. Moreover, we will often use the basic properties
\pi(p_{k})=k
and
p_{\pi(n)}\leq n
(with
p_{\pi(n)}=n
if and only if
n
is prime). Let us also note that
k+p_{k}
is strictly increasing with
k
. Thus, for a fixed
n
the solution of (
1
), if it exists, is unique.

A possible method to try to find
k
such that
n=k+p_{k}
is to take
f(k)=k+p_{k}-n
and solve the equation
f(k)=0
by means of a bisection method starting at the initial points
k_{0}=1
and
k_{1}=n
. The function
f(k)
is increasing and satisfies
f(k_{0})<0
and
f(k_{1})>0
so, if the solution exists, the bisection method always finds it. Moreover, the number of iterations needed to reach the solution is
\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\rfloor
(of course, this number can be a bit different due to the rounding of the bisection to an integer in every step). Instead, we propose another method which is faster, as we can see in the examples, and has a nice dynamics with its own interest.

The iterative method that we are going is simple and, as far as we know, has not been proposed before in this context; it is a kind of regula falsi method to solve an equation
f(k)=0
with
f(k)=\pi(n-k)-k
, that is not equivalent to (
1
), but closely related.
Since
p_{k}\approx k\log k
, it follows that
p_{k}
gives the main contribution to the sum
k+p_{k}
.
The idea is then to approximate the equation (
1
) by the equation
n=p_{k}
, and so to take a first guess
k=\pi(n)
; then we adjust the initial guess by successive corrections.
Let us note that any
k
which solves
n=k+p_{k}
satisfies
n-k=p_{k}
, a prime number, so
\pi(n-k)=k
.
Thus, the procedure for solving (
1
) is based on the iterative scheme
k_{j+1}=\pi(n-k_{j})
, and the goal is to look for a fixed point.
The following result shows the dynamics of the iterative method:

Theorem 1
.

Let
n\geq 4
be a given integer, and let us define the iterative process

k_{j+1}=\pi(n-k_{j}),\qquad k_{0}=\pi(n).

(2)

After a finite number of steps, one of these cases occurs:

(i)

We get a fixed point
k^{*}
.

(ii)

We get a cycle
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}
with
k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
,
n-k^{\prime}
a prime number,
(k_{2j})_{j\geq 0}
is a decreasing sequence which converges to
k^{\prime\prime}
, and
(k_{2j+1})_{j\geq 0}
is an increasing sequence which converges to
k^{\prime}
.

We postpone the proof of this theorem to Section
2
.
For the moment, let us see the consequences of this result on the solutions of the equation (
1
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
n\geq 4
be a given integer, the equation
n=k+p_{k}
,
and the iterative process

k_{j+1}=\pi(n-k_{j}),\qquad k_{0}=\pi(n).

Under these circumstances:

(i)

If we get a fixed point
k^{*}
(case (i) in Theorem
1
), then
k^{*}
is the solution of the equation

n^{\prime}=k+p_{k}

where
n^{\prime}=\max\{j+p_{j}\leq n:j\geq 1\}
.
In particular, the equation
n=k+p_{k}
has a solution if and only if
n^{\prime}=n
,
and then
k^{*}
is the solution.
(In practice it is enough to check whether
k^{*}+p_{k^{*}}=n
or not.)

(ii)

If we get a cycle
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}
(with
k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
, case (ii) in Theorem
1
), the equation
n=k+p_{k}
has no solution.

As in the case of Theorem
1
, we postpone the proof of Theorem
2
to Section
2
.

Both cases (i) and (ii) can really occur, as the following illustrative examples with small numbers show. The case (i) occurs, for instance, with
n=51
or
n=76
.
For
n=51
, the successive
k_{j}
in (
2
) are

\displaystyle k_{0}=\pi(51)=15,\quad k_{1}=\pi(51-15)=11,

\displaystyle k_{2}=\pi(51-11)=12,\quad k_{3}=\pi(51-12)=12,

so we have reached the fixed point
k^{*}=12
; but
k^{*}+p_{k^{*}}=12+37=49\neq 51
,
so the equation
51=k+p_{k}
has no solution.
For
n=76
, the successive
k_{j}
in (
2
) are

\displaystyle k_{0}=\pi(76)=21,\quad k_{1}=\pi(76-21)=16,

\displaystyle k_{2}=\pi(76-16)=17,\quad k_{3}=\pi(76-17)=17,

so
k^{*}=17
is a fixed point; this time
k^{*}+p_{k^{*}}=17+59=76
,
so we have found the solution of
76=k+p_{k}
.
To illustrate the case (ii), let us take, for instance,
n=41
.
The successive
k_{j}
in (
2
) are

\displaystyle k_{0}=\pi(41)=13,\quad k_{1}=\pi(41-13)=9,\quad k_{2}=\pi(41-9)=11,

\displaystyle k_{3}=\pi(41-11)=10,\quad k_{4}=\pi(41-10)=11,

\displaystyle k^{\prime}=k_{5}=k_{7}=k_{9}=\cdots=10,\quad k^{\prime\prime}=k_{6}=k_{8}=k_{10}=\cdots=11.

Then,
41=k+p_{k}
has no solution.

Instead of (
1
) we can consider the more general equation

n=ak+bp_{k},

with
b\geq 1
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}

(the trivial case
a=0
is excluded).

In this setting we can consider the iterative process which starts with
k_{0}=\pi(n/b)
and continues with

k_{j+1}=\pi((n-ak_{j})/b),\qquad j\geq 0.

Now, the behavior of the iterative method and its relation with the solution of
n=ak+bp_{k}
is a bit more complicated than in the case
a=b=1
. We analyze it in Section
3
.

2
Proof of Theorems
1
and
2

Proof of Theorem
1
.

It is clear that
k_{1}=\pi(n-k_{0})\leq\pi(n)=k_{0}
. From the inequality
k_{1}\leq k_{0}
, it follows that
k_{2}=\pi(n-k_{1})\geq\pi(n-k_{0})=k_{1}
. Now, from
k_{2}\geq k_{1}
we get
k_{3}=\pi(n-k_{2})\leq\pi(n-k_{1})=k_{2}
, and so on. That is,

k_{2j+1}\leq k_{2j}\quad\text{and}\quad k_{2j+2}\geq k_{2j+1},\qquad\text{for }j=0,1,2,\dots.

(3)

Moreover,
k_{2}=\pi(n-k_{1})\leq\pi(n)=k_{0}
, and from the inequality
k_{2}\leq k_{0}
it follows that
k_{3}=\pi(n-k_{2})\geq\pi(n-k_{0})=k_{1}
. Now, from
k_{3}\geq k_{1}
we get
k_{4}=\pi(n-k_{3})\leq\pi(n-k_{1})=k_{2}
, and so on. Thus,
(k_{2j})_{j\geq 0}
is a decreasing sequence, while
(k_{2j+1})_{j\geq 0}
is an increasing sequence. They are both bounded sequences of positive integers, so eventually constant; that is, there exist
k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime\prime}
(with
k^{\prime}\leq k^{\prime\prime}
) and a certain
J
such that

k_{2j+1}=k^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad k_{2j}=k^{\prime\prime}\qquad\text{for }j\geq J.

If
k^{\prime}=k^{\prime\prime}
, then
k^{*}=k^{\prime}=k^{\prime\prime}
is the fixed point in (
2
), the possibility (i) in the theorem.

Otherwise, let us suppose that
k^{\prime}<k^{\prime\prime}
, so

k^{\prime}=\pi(n-k^{\prime\prime})<\pi(n-k^{\prime})=k^{\prime\prime}.

Then
\pi(n-k^{\prime})-\pi(n-k^{\prime\prime})=k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}
, so there are
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}
prime numbers
q_{j}
such that

n-k^{\prime\prime}<q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}-1}<q_{k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}}\leq n-k^{\prime}.

(4)

Except for
2
and
3
, prime numbers are not consecutive numbers, so (
4
) is only possible in two cases:

•

Case
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}=2
, with
n-k^{\prime\prime}=1
,
n-k^{\prime}=3
,
q_{1}=2
and
q_{2}=3
.
In this case, we would have
k^{\prime}=\pi(n-k^{\prime\prime})=\pi(1)=0
, which cannot happen because
k^{\prime}
is the limit of the increasing sequence
(k_{2j+1})_{j\geq 0}
.

•

Case
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}=1
, with a unique prime
q_{1}=n-k^{\prime}
in (
4
). Then
k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
and

\pi(n-k^{\prime})=k^{\prime}+1,\quad\pi(n-k^{\prime}-1)=k^{\prime}.

This is the possibility (ii) in the theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let us first analyze the case (i).
We have
k^{*}=\pi(n-k^{*})
and
p_{k^{*}}\leq p_{\pi(n-k^{*})}\leq n-k^{*}
, so
k^{*}+p_{k^{*}}\leq n
. Thus,
k^{*}\in A
where
A
is the set

A=\{j\geq 1:j+p_{j}\leq n\}.

To conclude the proof of case (i), it is enough to check that
\max A=k^{*}
(in particular, this implies
k^{*}+p_{k^{*}}=n^{\prime}
).
Let us suppose that
\max A\neq k^{*}
. In this case,
k^{*}+1\in A
, so
p_{k^{*}+1}+k^{*}+1\leq n
, and therefore
p_{k^{*}+1}\leq n-k^{*}-1\leq n-k^{*}
. This implies that
\pi(n-k^{*})\geq k^{*}+1
, which is false because
\pi(n-k^{*})=k^{*}
.

In case (ii), we have

k^{\prime}=\pi(n-k^{\prime\prime})<\pi(n-k^{\prime})=k^{\prime\prime},

with
k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
.
Using that
n-k^{\prime}
is a prime number, we have

p_{k^{\prime}+1}=p_{k^{\prime\prime}}=p_{\pi(n-k^{\prime})}=n-k^{\prime}
, so
k^{\prime}+p_{k^{\prime}+1}=n
and then

k^{\prime}+p_{k^{\prime}}<n<k^{\prime}+1+p_{k^{\prime}+1}.

This clearly implies that
n=k+p_{k}
has no solution.

Finally, let us check that there cannot exist any solution of (
1
) which is
not detected by the iterative method (
2
).
Let us suppose on the contrary that
k
is a solution not detected by the method;
then,
n=k+p_{k}
and
\pi(n-k)=k
, so
k
is a fixed point of

k_{j+1}=\pi(n-k_{j})
, that is, a fixed point of (
2
) except for the starting step
k_{0}=\pi(n)
.

But we have proved that the iterative method (
2
), starting
in
k_{0}=\pi(n)
, converges to
k^{*}
or to the 2-cycle
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}

with
k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
. If (
2
) converges to
k^{*}
, this
k^{*}
is the unique solution of
(
1
), so
k=k^{*}
.
If (
2
) converges to
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}
, then
k<k^{\prime}
or
k>k^{\prime\prime}
.
However, both cases are impossible:

\begin{gathered}k<k^{\prime}\;\Rightarrow\;k=\pi(n-k)\geq\pi(n-k^{\prime})=k^{\prime\prime}\;\Rightarrow\;k\geq k^{\prime\prime},\text{ a contradiction};\\
k>k^{\prime\prime}\;\Rightarrow\;k=\pi(n-k)\leq\pi(n-k^{\prime\prime})=k^{\prime}\;\Rightarrow\;k\leq k^{\prime},\text{ a contradiction}.\end{gathered}

(5)

Then, there cannot exist such fixed point
k
.
∎

3
Generalization to the equation
n=ak+bp_{k}

A more general equation than (
1
) is

n=ak+bp_{k}

(6)

with
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
Given
n
, we want to know if there exists some
k
satisfying (
6
)
and how to find it by the iterative process

k_{j+1}=\pi((n-ak_{j})/b),\qquad j\geq 0,

(7)

starting with
k_{0}=\pi(n/b)
.

Of course, the reason to choose the iterative process (
7
) is that,
if
k
is a solution of (
6
), then
(n-ak)/b=p_{k}
, a prime number,
so
\pi((n-ak)/b)=\pi(p_{k})=k
and
k
is a fixed point of (
7
).

Depending on whether
a>0
or
a<0
, the beginning of the iterative process (
7
) varies.
If
a>0
we have
k_{1}\leq k_{0}
; however, if
a<0
we have
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
.
Moreover, if
a<0
, the sequence
ak+bp_{k}
is not always increasing with
k
, so we cannot ensure that the solution of
n=ak+bp_{k}
, if it exists, is unique. These differences motivate to consider both cases
a>0
and
a<0
separately.

In what follows, we present this study in a more informal way, without stating the properties as theorems. Actually, the behavior of the iterative method and its relation with the solutions of (
6
) is similar to what happens in Theorems
1
and
2
in the case
a>0
, but rather different in the case
a<0
.

3.1
Case
a>0

Let us start noticing that we want the
k_{j}
that arise in the iterative method (
7
) to be positive integers (otherwise,
p_{k_{j}}
does not exist).
Due to the equivalence
\pi(x)\sim x/\log(x)
for
x\to\infty
(prime number theorem),
we can guarantee that
k_{j}>0
for all
j
if
n
is large enough
(the exact required size depends on
a
and
b
). Indeed, for
n\to\infty
we have

k_{0}=\pi(n/b)\sim\frac{n/b}{\log(n/b)}\sim\frac{n}{b\log(n)}

and

k_{1}=\pi((n-ak_{0})/b)\sim\frac{(n-ak_{0})/b}{\log((n-ak_{0})/b)}\sim\frac{n}{b\log(n)},

so
k_{0},k_{1}\geq 1
if
n
is big enough.
Then, given the equation
n=ak+bp_{k}
, we can assume that
n
satisfies

k_{1}=\pi\big((n-ak_{0})/b\big)=\pi\big((n-a\pi(n/b))/b\big)\geq 1

(8)

(this holds except for a finite set of
n
’s). Note that, for
a=b=1
, (
8
) becomes
\pi(n-\pi(n))\geq 1
, which holds for every
n\geq 4
.
After imposing this technical restriction for small values of
n
, let us analyze the iterative method.

The assumption
a>0
gives
k_{1}=\pi((n-ak_{0})/b)\leq\pi(n/b)=k_{0}
. From
k_{1}\leq k_{0}
, it follows that
k_{2}=\pi((n-ak_{1})/b)\geq\pi((n-ak_{0})/b)=k_{1}
, and from
k_{2}\geq k_{1}
, we get
k_{3}=\pi((n-ak_{2})/b)\leq\pi((n-k_{1})/b)=k_{2}
, and so on.
Moreover,
k_{2}=\pi((n-ak_{1})/b)\leq\pi(n/b)=k_{0}

and
k_{3}=\pi((n-ak_{2})/b)\geq\pi((n-ak_{0}))=k_{1}
, and so on.
In particular, if
k_{1}\geq 1
then
k_{j}\geq 1
for every
j
,
so assuming (
8
) is enough to ensure that all the
k_{j}
will be positive integers.

Following as in the proof of Theorem
1
we get that
(k_{2j})_{j\geq 0}
is a decreasing sequence,
(k_{2j+1})_{j\geq 0}
is an increasing sequence, and there exist
k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime\prime}
(with
k^{\prime}\leq k^{\prime\prime}
) and a certain
J
such that

k_{2j+1}=k^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad k_{2j}=k^{\prime\prime}\qquad\text{for }j\geq J.

If
k^{\prime}=k^{\prime\prime}
, then
k^{*}=k^{\prime}=k^{\prime\prime}
is the fixed point in (
6
).
Otherwise, if
k^{\prime}<k^{\prime\prime}
we have

k^{\prime}=\pi((n-ak^{\prime\prime})/b)<\pi((n-ak^{\prime})/b)=k^{\prime\prime}

so
\pi((n-ak^{\prime})/b)-\pi((n-ak^{\prime\prime})/b)=k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}
, and there are
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}
prime numbers
q_{j}
such that

\frac{n-ak^{\prime\prime}}{b}<q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}-1}<q_{k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}}\leq\frac{n-ak^{\prime}}{b}.

(9)

From now on, the case
k^{\prime}<k^{\prime\prime}
is somewhat different.

Let us suppose that two of these primes are
q_{1}=2
and
q_{2}=3
. Then
(n-ak^{\prime\prime})/b=1
and
k^{\prime}=\pi((n-ak^{\prime\prime})/b)=\pi(1)=0
, which cannot happen. Therefore, we can assume that
q_{j+1}-q_{j}\geq 2
for all these primes
q_{j}
. Clearly, any interval
(x,y]
containing
m
of these primes must satisfy
y-x>2(m-1)
. In the case (
9
), this means that

\frac{n-ak^{\prime}}{b}-\frac{n-ak^{\prime\prime}}{b}>2(k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime})-2,

that is,
a(k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime})/b>2(k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime})-2
, or

(2b-a)(k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime})<2b.

(10)

Then, two situations can occur:

•

If
a\geq 2b
, (
10
) is trivial and does not imply any restriction on
k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime\prime}
.

•

If
a<2b
, (
10
) can be written as

k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}<\frac{2b}{2b-a}.

(11)

In the particular case
a\leq b
we have
1<2b/(2b-a)\leq 2
, so (
11
) is equivalent to

k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
(recall that we are analyzing the case
k^{\prime}<k^{\prime\prime}
).

In the case
a\leq b
, the remaining arguments in the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
are valid. In particular (
5
), which guarantees that the iterative method (
7
), starting at
k_{0}=\pi(n/b)
, detects as fixed points all the solutions of
n=ak+bp_{k}
.

If
a\geq 2b
or
b<a<2b
, we cannot ensure that
k^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
. There might be an integer
k
with
k^{\prime}<k<k^{\prime\prime}
which is a fixed point

\pi((n-ak)/b)=k

and a solution of
n=ak+bp_{k}
not detected by the iterative method (
7
) starting in
k_{0}=\pi(n/b)
. That is, the method converges to the 2-cycle
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}
instead of
k
. In practice, if we are looking for a solution of
n=ak+bp_{k}
, it is enough to check if every
k
satisfying
k^{\prime}<k<k^{\prime\prime}
is a solution.

Let us give some examples to illustrate that these situations can occur.

For the case
a\geq 2b
, let us see what happens with the equation

n=7k+2p_{k}

for several values of
n
, with convergence to a fixed point
k^{*}
or to cycles
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}
with different values of
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}
.
For
n=10\,040
, we have
k_{0}=672
and the iterative process converges to
k^{*}=474
, which is a solution of the equation;
instead, for
n=10\,041
, again
k_{0}=672
and the iterative process converges to
k^{*}=474
, which is not a solution of the equation.
For
n=10\,073
, we have
k_{0}=674
and the iterative process converges to the cycle
\{474,476\}
.
For
n=10\,300
, we have
k_{0}=686
and the iterative process converges to the cycle
\{482,485\}
.
For
n=10\,325
, we have
k_{0}=687
and the iterative process converges to the cycle
\{483,487\}
.
For
n=10\,532
, we have
k_{0}=698
and the iterative process converges to the cycle
\{491,497\}
.
In all these cases, less that
10
iterations are enough to reach
k^{*}
or
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}
.

For the case
b<a<2b
, in the equation
12\,660=3k+2p_{k}
we have
k_{0}=824
and the iterative method converges to the cycle
\{699,701\}
, where
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}=2
.

Let us also show some instances of the equation
n=ak+bp_{k}
with a solution
k
such that the iterative method (
7
) starting with
k_{0}=\pi(n/b)
converges to a cycle
\{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime}\}
.
For
n=2\cdot 33+p_{33}=203
, where
k=33
is clearly a solution of
n=2k+p_{k}
, the iterative method converges to the cycle
\{32,34\}
, with
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}=2
.
For
n=6\cdot 100+p_{100}=1141
, where
k=100
is the solution of
n=6k+p_{k}
, the iterative method converges to the cycle
\{80,121\}
, with
k^{\prime\prime}-k^{\prime}=41
.

3.2
Case
a<0

Let us start noticing that, in general,
ak+bp_{k}
is not increasing with
k
when
a<0
.
Therefore, we cannot ensure the unicity of the solution of
n=ak+bp_{k}
for fixed
a
,
b
and
n
.
For instance,
n=105
has two solutions
k
in the equation
n=-2k+p_{k}
:

105=-2\cdot 43+p_{43}=-2\cdot 44+p_{44}.

With
n=-3k+p_{k}
we can also find two solutions that are not consecutive integers:

100=-3\cdot 59+p_{59}=-3\cdot 61+p_{61}.

There can exist more than two solutions, as is the case of the equation
n=-4k+p_{k}
:

99=-4\cdot 83+p_{83}=-4\cdot 85+p_{85}=-4\cdot 86+p_{86}.

However,
p_{k}
always grows faster than
k
, so the sequence
ak+bp_{k}
is increasing with
k
if
0<-a<b
.
In this case, again the solution of
n=ak+bp_{k}
, if it exists, is unique.

Anyway, taking into account that
p_{k}\sim k\log k
when
k\to\infty
, we have

ak+bp_{k}\sim ak+bk\log k=(a+b\log k)k
.
Thus,
ak+bp_{k}
is increasing with
k
for
k
big enough.
In particular, this ensures that, for fixed
a
,
b
and
n
, the number of solutions of
n=ak+bp_{k}
is always finite.
Precise estimates of the form

C_{1}\,\frac{x}{\log x}\leq\pi(x)\leq C_{2}\,\frac{x}{\log x}

with
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, which yield an upper bound (depending on
a
,
b
,
n
,
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
) of the number of solutions, can be found in many texts of number theory (see, for instance,
[
2
, Theorem 8.8.1]
).

Now, let us analyze the behavior of the iterative method (
7
) starting in
k_{0}=\pi(n/b)
.
As shown below, nothing similar to (
3
) appears in the case
a<0
;
the property (
3
) is very important in the analysis of the case
a>0
,
and the lack of a suitable alternative is a great handicap.
Although the analysis of the case
a<0
is presented here for completeness, it must be concluded that
the iterative method described in this paper is not so useful to solve (
6
)
as it is when
a>0
.

Since
a<0
, we have
k_{1}=\pi((n-ak_{0})/b)\geq\pi(n/b)=k_{0}
;
and, if we assume that
k_{j}\geq k_{j-1}
, also

k_{j+1}=\pi((n-ak_{j})/b)\geq\pi((n-ak_{j-1})/b)=k_{j}.

Then
k_{j}
is an increasing sequence and it cannot tend to a cycle.

Let
s
be any number such that

\pi((n-as)/b)\leq s;

(12)

the existence of such an
s
follows from the estimate
\pi(x)\sim x/\log x
when
x\to\infty
.
Then (recall that
a<0
)

k_{0}=\pi(n/b)\leq\pi((n-as)/b)\leq s;

and assuming that
k_{j}\leq s
gives

k_{j+1}=\pi((n-ak_{j})/b)\leq\pi((n-as)/b)\leq s.

This proves that the increasing sequence of integers
k_{j}
produced by the iterative method (
7
) starting with
k_{0}=\pi(n/b)
is bounded.
So there exists some
k^{*}
(and an index
J
) such that

k_{j}=k^{*}\quad\text{for every }j\geq J,

and thus
k^{*}
is a fixed point of (
7
).

We cannot ensure that the fixed point
k^{*}
is a solution of (
6
).
A solution of (
6
), if it exists, satisfies
\pi((n-as)/b)=s
;
in particular, it also satisfies (
12
).
Then
k^{*}\leq s
for any possible solution
s
of (
6
) such that
s\geq\pi(n/b)=k_{0}
.
In practice, we can continue checking whether
k^{*}
,
k^{*}+1
,
k^{*}+2
,… are solutions or not
of (
6
) until, when substituting in
ak+bp_{k}
, we get a value greater than
n
;
actually, an additional precaution is necessary:
we must continue the checking until reaching values of
k
for which the sequence
ak+bp_{k}
is already increasing.

For an example of this behavior, let us take
n=-7\cdot 2000+p_{2000}=3389
(that is,
a=-7
and
b=1
),
so
s=2000
is a solution of the equation
3389=-7k+p_{k}
. However, the iterative process
k_{j+1}=\pi(3389+7k_{j})

starting in
k_{0}=\pi(3389)=477
converges to
k^{*}=1989
, which is not a solution of the equation. Observe that
s-k^{*}=11
.

Finally, let us observe an extra surprise: in the case
a<0
, the fixed point
k^{*}
of the iterative method
is never a solution of
n=ak+b_{k}
. If the equation has no solutions, there is nothing to prove.
If the equation has a solution
s
, let us prove that the fixed point
k^{*}
cannot be
k^{*}=s
, but
k^{*}<s
.
Indeed, it is enough to check that, if
k_{j}<s
, also
k_{j+1}<s
.
We have
(n-ak_{j})/b<(n-as)/b=p_{s}
, a prime, so
\pi((n-ak_{j})/b)<s
.
But
\pi((n-ak_{j})/b)=k_{j+1}
, so
k_{j+1}<s
, just as we were looking for.
