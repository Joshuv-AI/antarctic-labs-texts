On the rate of convergence of a numerical scheme for Fractional conservation laws with noise
Ujjwal Koley, Guy Vallet

Abstract
. We consider a semi-discrete finite volume scheme for a degenerate fractional conservation laws driven by a cylindrical Wiener process. Making use of the bounded variation (BV) estimates, Young measure theory, and a clever adaptation of classical Kružkov theory, we provide estimates on the rate of convergence for approximate solutions to fractional problems. The main difficulty stems from the degenerate fractional operator, and requires a significant departure from the existing strategy to establish Kato’s type of inequality. Finally, as an application of this theory, we demonstrate numerical convergence rates.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we analyze finite volume schemes for a degenerate fractional conservation laws driven by a cylindrical Wiener process. To describe the problem, let us assume that
\big(\Omega,\mathbb{P},\mathbb{F},\{\mathbb{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\big)
is a filtered probability space satisfying
the usual hypothesis
i.e.
,
\{\mathbb{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a right-continuous filtration such that
\mathbb{F}_{0}

contains all the
\mathbb{P}
-null subsets of
(\Omega,\mathbb{F})
. We are interested in numerical approximations of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-valued predictable processes
u(t,\cdot)
which satisfy the following Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}du(t,x)-\mathrm{div}\,f(u(t,x))\,dt+\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}[A(u(t,\cdot))](x)\,dt=\sigma(u(t,x))\,dW(t),&\quad(x,t)\in\Pi_{T},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),&\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\Pi_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
with
T>0
fixed. Here
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a given initial function,
f:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d},A:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
are given (sufficiently smooth) nonlinear functions. Moreover,
\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}[u]
denotes the classical fractional Laplace operator of order
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}[\psi](x):=d_{\lambda}\,\text{P.V.}\,\int_{|z|>0}\frac{\psi(x)-\psi(x+z)}{|z|^{d+2\lambda}}\,dz,

for some constants
d_{\lambda}>0
, and a sufficiently smooth function
\psi
. The nonlinearity
A^{\prime}
is allowed to be zero on an interval, thus (
1.1
) is a strongly degenerate fractional problem.
Furthermore,
W(t)
is a cylindrical Wiener process given by
W(t)=\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k}\beta_{k}(t)
, where
(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are mutually independent real valued standard Wiener processes, and
(g_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a complete orthonormal system in a separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. Finally, we consider the mapping
\sigma(w):\mathbb{H}\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for each
w
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, defined by
\sigma(w)g_{k}=h_{k}(w(\cdot))
. For our purpose, we assume that
h_{k}
is Lipschitz-continuous and define
\mathbb{G}^{2}(r):=\sum_{k\geq 1}h_{k}^{2}(r)
.

1.2.
Aims and scope of this paper

Due to the non availaibility of the explicit solution for stochastic balance laws, we intend to construct a numerical scheme for approximating the solution of (
1.1
). Note that, although there are large number of papers exploring convergence results for stochastic conservation laws, a rigorous theoretical study for stochastic fractional conservation laws is in a state of infancy. In fact, the specific question about deriving the convergence rate for the approximate solutions to stochastic non-local problems is virtually untouched. For stochastic conservation laws, the convergence rate for monotone methods is known to be
{\Delta x}^{1/2}
,
{\Delta x}
being the discretization parameter. Keeping in mind that the rate of convergence estimate intimately related to the so-called Kato’s type of inequality, the main difficulties in dealing with the specific stochastic non-local problem (
1.1
) can be summarized as follows:

(1)

For the stochastic non-local equation, by virtue of Itô’s formula, one requires to work with a
smooth approximations
of the usual Kružkov’s entropies.

(2)

The numerical analysis of the deterministic counterpart of (
1.1
) heavily relies on the following (Kato’s type of) inequality

\mathrm{sign}\Big(u(x)-v(y)\Big)\Big(\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}[A(v)](y)-\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}[A(u)](x)\Big)\leq\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}\Big[|A(u)-A(v)|\Big](x,y),

(1.2)

and the above inequality (
1.2
) does not hold for approximations of the usual Kružkov’s entropies.

In view of the above incompatibility, to establish stochastic Kato’s inequality (even for a semi-discrete scheme!) one has to look beyond the traditional approach of proving convergence of numerical schemes. Indeed, the main technical achievement of this paper stems from successful demonstration of the stochastic Kato’s inequality. The method of proof is new and requires significant changes in computing hierarchical limits with respect to various parameters involved in the proof leading upto Kato’s inequality. In a nutshell, we send the parameter
\delta\downarrow 0
before sending the parameter
l\downarrow 0
, see Section
4
for a complete description of the main ingredients of our method. Inevitably, above changes in hierarchical limits has effects on all the terms involved in the entropy inequality, and appropriate changes are required to deal with them.

To summarize, we consider a semi-discrete finite difference scheme and show that the expected value of the
L^{1}
-difference of the approximate numerical solution and the unique entropy solution to (
1.1
) converges at some rate depending on
\lambda
, which is in accordance with the rate for the deterministic fractional conservation law
[
14
]
. Finally, let us mention that we do not know how to derive rate of convergence estimates for a fully-discrete scheme for (
1.1
), but we strongly believe that the technical achievement of this paper is absolutely essential to establish such a result.

We organize the details as follows. We first briefly recall the entropy framework, introduce the semi-discrete finite volume scheme, and state the main result towards the end of Section
2
. Further in Section
3
we prove
a priori
estimates for the approximate numerical solutions. Section
4
deals with the proof of the main theorem. Finally, we conclude by presenting some numerical results in Section
5
.

2.
Mathematical Framework and Statement of the Main Result

Throughout this paper, by the letter
C
, we denote various generic constants which may change from line to line. Given a separable Banach space
Z
, let us denote by
N^{2}_{w}(0,T,Z)
the space of square integrable predictable
Z
-valued processes (cf.
[
35
]
p.28 for example). The Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
||\cdot||
and the BV semi-norm is by
|\cdot|_{BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. We remark that the space BV
(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consists of functions with bounded variation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, endowed with the semi-norm
|u|_{BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=TV_{x}(u)
, where
TV_{x}(u)
is the total variation of
u
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also denote by
\mathrm{sign}_{0}(x)=\frac{x}{|x|}
if
x\neq 0
, and
0
otherwise.

In the rest of the paper, we consider the following assumptions:

A
.1

The initial function
u_{0}
is a deterministic function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

A
.2

The flux function
f=(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{d}):\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a Lipschitz continuous function with
f_{k}(0)=0
, for all
1\leq k\leq d
.

A
.3

The function
A:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a non decreasing Lipschitz continuous function with
A(0)=0
.

A
.4

There exists
M>0
such that
\sigma(u)=0
, for all
|u|>M
and
h_{k}(0)=0
, for all
k\geq 1
. Moreover, there exists a constant
K>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\big|h_{k}(u)-h_{k}(v)\big|^{2}\leq K|u-v|^{2},\,\,\text{which implies }\,\,\mathbb{G}^{2}(u)=\sum_{k\geq 1}h_{k}^{2}(u)\leq K\,|u|^{2},~\text{for all}\,\,u,v\in\mathbb{R}.

2.1.
Stochastic Entropy Formulation

It is well-known that weak solutions to (
1.1
) need not be unique. Consequently, an
entropy admissibility condition
must be imposed to single out the physically correct solution. To describe the entropy framework for (
1.1
), we need to first split the non-local operator
\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}
into two terms: for each
r>0
, we write
\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}[\psi]:=\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda,r}[\psi]+\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}^{r}[\psi]
, where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda,r}[\psi](x)

\displaystyle:=d_{\lambda}\,\text{P.V.}\,\int_{|z|\leq r}\frac{\psi(x)-\psi(x+z)}{|z|^{d+2\lambda}}\,dz,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}^{r}[\psi](x)

\displaystyle:=d_{\lambda}\,\int_{|z|>r}\frac{\psi(x)-\psi(x+z)}{|z|^{d+2\lambda}}\,dz.

We shall also denote

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda,r}^{s}[\psi](x)

\displaystyle:=d_{\lambda}\,\int_{r\geq|z|>s}\frac{\psi(x)-\psi(x+z)}{|z|^{d+2\lambda}}\,dz,\text{ if }0<s<r;\quad\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda,r}^{s}[\psi](x)=0,\text{ if }s\geq r>0.

We now recall the notion of the entropy solution for (
1.1
) from
[
9
, Definition 1.2]
.

Definition 2.1

(Stochastic Entropy Solution)
.

An element
u\in N^{2}_{w}(0,T,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, with intial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is called a stochastic entropy solution of (
1.1
) if given a non-negative test function
\psi\in C_{c}^{1,2}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a regular convex entropy-entopy flux pair
(\eta,\zeta)
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta(u_{0}(x))\psi(0,x)\,dx+\int_{\Pi_{T}}\left[\eta(u(t,x))\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)-\nabla\psi(t,x)\cdot\zeta(u(t,x))\right]\,dx\,dt

(2.1)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Pi_{T}}\Big[\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}^{r}[A(u(t,\cdot))](x)\,\psi(t,x)\,\eta^{\prime}(u(t,x))+A^{\eta}_{k}(u(t,x))\,\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda,r}[\psi(t,\cdot)](x)\Big]\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{\Pi_{T}}h_{k}(u(t,x))\eta^{\prime}(u(t,x))\psi(t,x)\,d\beta_{k}(t)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Pi_{T}}\mathbb{G}^{2}(u(t,x))\eta^{\prime\prime}(u(t,x))\psi(t,x)\,dx\,dt\geq 0,\,\,\mathbb{P}-\text{a.s}.

2.2.
Finite Difference Scheme

We begin by introducing some notations needed to define the
semi-discrete finite difference scheme. Let
{\Delta x}
denote a small positive number that represents the
spatial discretization parameter of the numerical scheme. We set
x_{\alpha}=\alpha{\Delta x}
, for
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, to denote the spatial mesh points.
Furthermore, following Cifani
et al.

[
14
]
, let us introduce the spatial grid cells

\displaystyle R_{0}=\big[-{\Delta x}/2,{\Delta x}/2\big)^{d},\quad R_{\alpha}=[x_{\alpha_{1}-1/2},x_{\alpha_{1}+1/2})\times\cdots\times[x_{\alpha_{d}-1/2},x_{\alpha_{d}+1/2})=x_{\alpha}+R_{0},

where
x_{j\pm 1/2}=x_{j}\pm\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}
, for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For any given
(u_{\alpha})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, set
u_{\Delta x}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u_{\alpha}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}}
(
\mathds{1}_{A}
denotes the characteristic function of the set

A
) and note that
u_{\Delta x}
is measurable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover,
u_{\Delta x}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if and only if
u_{\Delta x}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
with

\displaystyle p<+\infty:

\displaystyle\quad\|u_{\Delta x}\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{\Delta x}(x)|^{p}dx={\Delta x}^{d}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|u_{\alpha}|^{p}={\Delta x}^{d}\|(u_{\alpha})\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}^{p},

\displaystyle p=+\infty:

\displaystyle\quad\|u_{\Delta x}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|u_{\alpha}|=\|(u_{\alpha})\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}.

Furthermore, the BV semi-norm for a lattice function
u_{{\Delta x}}
is defined as

\left|u_{\Delta x}(t,\cdot)\right|_{BV}={\Delta x}^{d-1}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|u_{\alpha+e_{i}}(t)-u_{\alpha}(t)\right|,

where
\{e_{1},e_{2},\cdots e_{d}\}
denotes the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Finally, we denote by
D_{k}^{\pm}
the discrete forward and backward differences in space,
i.e.,

D^{\pm}_{k}u_{\Delta x}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}D^{\pm}_{k}u_{\alpha}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\pm\frac{u_{\alpha\pm e_{k}}-u_{\alpha}}{{\Delta x}}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}},

where the same notation
D^{\pm}_{k}
is used for
u_{\Delta x}
and
u_{\alpha}
, with a slight abuse of notation.

We now propose the following semi-discrete (in time) finite volume scheme
approximating the solutions generated by the equation (
1.1
)

\displaystyle du_{\alpha}(t)+\sum^{d}_{k=1}D^{-}_{k}F_{k}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha+e_{k}})\,dt+\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}[A(u_{\Delta x}(t,\cdot))]_{\alpha}\,dt

\displaystyle=\sigma(u_{\alpha}(t))\,dW(t),\,\,t>0,\,\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(2.2)

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{d}}\int_{R_{\alpha}}u_{0}(x)\,dx,\,\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(2.3)

where
F_{i}
is a monotone numerical flux corresponding to
f_{i}
, for each
1\leq i\leq d
.
Here monotone is understood in the following sense:
F_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}
,
F_{i}(u,u)=f_{i}(u)
for any real
u
,
a\mapsto F_{i}(a,b)
is non-decreasing and
b\mapsto F_{i}(a,b)
is non-increasing. Some classical examples of monotone flux include Engquist-Osher flux, Godunov flux, and modified Lax-Friedrichs flux. Next, we give details about the discretization of the non-local term.

2.2.1.
Approximation of the non-local operator

Following the idea developed in
[
14
]
, for any bounded
u_{\Delta x}
, we will use the following (two layer) discretization of the non-linear non-local term: for any
u_{\Delta x}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u_{\alpha}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}}
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}[A(u)]\approx\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}{[A(u_{\Delta x})]}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}{[A(u_{\Delta x})]}_{\alpha}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}}:=\frac{1}{{\Delta x}^{d}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}G_{\alpha,\beta}A(u_{\beta})\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}},

(2.4)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}[A(u_{\Delta x})]_{\alpha}:=

\displaystyle{\Delta x}^{-d}\int_{R_{\alpha}}\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}^{\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}}[A(u_{\Delta x})](x)\,dx=d_{\lambda}{\Delta x}^{-d}\int_{R_{\alpha}}\Big[\int_{|z|>\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}}\frac{A(u_{\Delta x})(x)-A(u_{\Delta x})(x+z)}{|z|^{d+2\lambda}}dz\Big]dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}A(u_{\beta})\Big[{\Delta x}^{-d}\int_{R_{\alpha}}d_{\lambda}\int_{|z|>\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}}\frac{\mathds{1}_{R_{\beta}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{R_{\beta}}(x+z)}{|z|^{d+2\lambda}}dzdx\Big]:=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\Delta x}^{-d}G_{\alpha,\beta}A(u_{\beta}).

Remark 2.1
.

By denoting
d\mu(z)=d_{\lambda}\frac{dz}{|z|^{d+2\lambda}}
and, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
G_{\alpha,\beta}=\int_{R_{\alpha}}\int_{|z|>\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}}[\mathds{1}_{R_{\beta}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{R_{\beta}}(x+z)d\mu(z)dx
, it is straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,\beta}=G_{\beta,\alpha},\quad G_{\alpha,\beta}\leq 0,\quad 0\leq-\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}G_{\alpha,\beta}=-\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}G_{\beta,\alpha}=G_{\alpha,\alpha}\leq{\Delta x}^{d}\mu\Big(|z|>\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}\Big),

\displaystyle\forall k=1,\cdots,d,\quad G_{\alpha,\beta}=G_{\alpha+e_{k},\beta+e_{k}}.

For technical reasons, we need to split the non-local integral in two parts and introduce the following notations: For any
u_{{\Delta x}}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u_{\beta}\mathds{1}_{R_{\beta}}
, and all
r>\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}
, we write
\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}{[u_{{\Delta x}}]}=\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda,r}{[u_{{\Delta x}}]}+\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}^{r}{[u_{{\Delta x}}]}
, where

\displaystyle\quad\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda,r}{[u_{{\Delta x}}]}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda,r}{[\overline{u}(t,\cdot)]}_{\alpha}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{{\Delta x}^{d}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}G_{\alpha,\beta,r}\,u_{\beta}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}},

with

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,\beta,r}:=\int_{R_{\alpha}}\int_{\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}<|z|\leq r}\big(\mathds{1}_{R_{\beta}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{R_{\beta}}(x+z)\big)\,d\mu(z)\,dx.

And,

\displaystyle\quad\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}^{r}{[u_{{\Delta x}}]}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}^{r}{[u_{{\Delta x}}]}_{\alpha}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{{\Delta x}^{d}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}G^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}\,u_{\beta}\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}},\hskip 170.71652pt

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\text{with}\quad G^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\int_{R_{\alpha}}\int_{r<|z|}\big(\mathds{1}_{I_{\beta}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{I_{\beta}}(x+z)\big)\,d\mu(z)\,dx.

Remark 2.2
.

It is easy to check that Remark
2.1
holds for both
G^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}
and
G_{\alpha,\beta,r}
, with the fact that
G^{r}_{\alpha,\alpha}\leq{\Delta x}^{d}\mu(r>|z|>\frac{{\Delta x}}{2})
and
G_{\alpha,\beta,r}\leq{\Delta x}^{d}\mu(|z|>r)
, and that
G_{\alpha,\beta}=G^{r}_{\alpha,\beta}+G_{\alpha,\beta,r}
.

In light of the above observations, we can recast the scheme (
2.2
) as

\displaystyle du_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{{\Delta x}}\sum^{d}_{i=1}\Big[F_{i}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha+e_{i}})-F_{i}(u_{\alpha-e_{i}},u_{\alpha})\Big]\,dt+\frac{1}{{\Delta x}^{d}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}G_{\alpha,\beta}A(u_{\beta})\,dt

\displaystyle=\sigma(u_{\alpha})\,dW(t).

(2.5)

Thanks to the assumptions on the data
\eqref{A1}-\eqref{A5}
and Remark
2.1
, the solvability of (
2.5
) follows from a classical argument of stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz non-linearities (see
e.g.
Prévot and Röckner
[
35
, Sec. 4.1 p.55]
).

2.3.
Discrete entropy inequality

With the help of the above scheme (
2.5
), we can derive the discrete entropy inequality. To that context, let
(\eta,\zeta)
be a
C^{2}
entropy-entropy flux pair. Given a non-negative test function
\psi\in C_{c}^{1,2}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, consider its piecewise approximation
\psi_{\alpha}(t):=\frac{1}{{\Delta x}}\int_{R_{\alpha}}\psi(t,x)\,dx
, and apply Itô’s product
rule to
\eta(u_{\alpha}(t))\psi_{\alpha}(t)
to yield,

\displaystyle\eta(u_{\alpha}(T))\,\psi_{\alpha}(T)-\eta(u_{\alpha}(0))\,\psi_{\alpha}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left[\eta(u_{\alpha}(t))\,\partial_{t}\psi_{\alpha}(t)-\eta^{\prime}(u_{\alpha}(t))\left(\frac{1}{{\Delta x}}\sum^{d}_{i=1}\Big[F_{i}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha+e_{i}})-F_{i}(u_{\alpha-e_{i}},u_{\alpha})\Big]+\hat{\mathfrak{L}}_{\lambda}{[A(u_{\Delta x}(t,\cdot))]}_{\alpha}\right)\psi_{\alpha}(t)\right]\,dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{0}^{T}g_{k}(u_{\alpha}(t))\eta^{\prime}(u_{\alpha}(t))\psi_{\alpha}(t)\,d\beta_{k}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{G}^{2}(u_{\alpha}(t))\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{\alpha}(t))\psi_{\alpha}(t)\,dt.

Denote by
\overline{\psi}(t,x)=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\psi_{\alpha}(t)\mathds{1}_{R_{\alpha}}(x)
.
Then, integrating over
R_{\alpha}
and summing over
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta(u_{{\Delta x}}(T,x))\,\overline{\psi}(T,x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta(u_{{\Delta x}}(0,x))\,\overline{\psi}(0,x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Pi_{T}}\eta(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\,\partial_{t}\overline{\psi}(t,x)-\mathfrak{L}_{\lambda}^{\frac{{\Delta x}}{2}}[A(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,\cdot))](x)\overline{\psi}(t,x)\,\eta^{\prime}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle-\int_{\Pi_{T}}\eta^{\prime}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\frac{1}{{\Delta x}}\sum^{d}_{i=1}\Big[F_{i}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x),u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x+{\Delta x}e_{i}))-F_{i}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x-{\Delta x}e_{i}),u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\Big]\overline{\psi}(t,x)\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{\Pi_{T}}g_{k}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\eta^{\prime}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\overline{\psi}(t,x)\,d\beta_{k}(t)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Pi_{T}}\mathbb{G}^{2}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{{\Delta x}}(t,x))\overline{\psi}(t,x)\,dx\,dt.

To deal with the fractional term we follow
[
9

to notice that, for any

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{{\Delta x}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\eta^{\prime}(u_{\alpha}(t)-k)G_{\alpha,\beta}\,A(u_{\beta}(t))\,dt=\sigma(u_{\alpha}(t))\eta^{\prime}(u_{\alpha}(t)-k)\,dW(t)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(u_{\alpha}(t))\eta^{\prime\prime}(u_{\alpha}(t)-k)\,dt.

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle F_{i}[(u-k)^{+}+k,(v-k)^{+}+k]-F_{i}[(w-k)^{+}+k,(u-k)^{+}+k]=F_{i}[u\top k,v\top k]-F_{i}[w\top k,u\top k].

(3.2)

A similar calculation reveals that the above inequality (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|u(t,y)-u_{\Delta y}(t,y)\big|\,dx\bigg]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|u_{{\Delta y}}(0,y)-u_{0}(y)\big|\,dx\Big]

\displaystyle+C\left(\delta+\frac{{\Delta y}}{\delta}+\frac{(\Delta y)^{2-2\lambda}}{\delta}\right)+\frac{C}{\delta}\begin{cases}{\Delta y},&\text{if}\,\,\lambda<1/2,\\
{\Delta y}|\ln{\Delta y}|,&\text{if}\,\,\lambda=1/2,\\
{\Delta y}^{2(1-\lambda)},&\text{if}\,\,\lambda>1/2.\end{cases}

For
\lambda\in(0,1/2]
, we choose
\delta=\sqrt{{\Delta y}}
and for
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
, we choose
\delta=({\Delta y})^{1-\lambda}
to obtain the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|u(t,y)-u_{\Delta y}(t,y)\big|\,dy\bigg]\leq C\begin{cases}\sqrt{{\Delta y}},&\text{if}\,\,\lambda<1/2,\\
\sqrt{{\Delta y}}|\ln{\Delta y}|,&\text{if}\,\,\lambda=1/2,\\
{\Delta y}^{(1-\lambda)},&\text{if}\,\,\lambda>1/2,\end{cases}

provided the initial error satisfies
\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|u_{{\Delta y}}(0,y)-u_{0}(y)\big|\,dy\Big]\leq\sqrt{{\Delta y}}
.

5.
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we simulate numerical experiments to substantiate the results we have shown in the previous sections. In what follows, inspired by Del Teso
et al.

[
19
,
20
]
for the deterministic fractional porous medium operator, we test numerically the performance of the proposed scheme (
2.5
). Here we use a Godunov scheme for the first-order operator and an explicit scheme for the noise term. We set the underlying target equation (
1.1
) (posed in
\mathbb{R}
) with

\displaystyle A(u)=(u-\frac{1}{2})^{+},\quad f(u)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2},\quad\sigma(u)=u(1-u),\,\,\text{and}\,\,u_{0}(x)=2e^{\frac{1}{x^{2}-1}}\mathds{1}_{(-1,1)}.

Let us mention that this configuration of data leads to a solution
u
satisfying
0\leq u\leq 1
so that one may replace
f(u)
and
\sigma(u)
by
\frac{1}{2}[\min(1,|u|)]^{2}
and
u^{+}(1-u)^{+}
respectively to be compatible with the assumptions of the paper.

Next, we present the methodology of the fully discrete explicit numerical scheme.
{\Delta t}>0
and
{\Delta x}>0
are the time-step and the spatial mesh size respectively.
t^{n}=n{\Delta t}
for
n=0,1,2,\cdots,N=\frac{T}{{\Delta t}}
denotes the temporal grid and
x_{i}=i{\Delta x}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
the spatial one. The scheme becomes

\displaystyle U^{n+1}_{i}=U^{n}_{i}-{\Delta t}D^{-}F(U^{n}_{i},U^{n}_{i+1})-\frac{{\Delta t}}{{\Delta x}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\widetilde{G}_{j-i}A(U^{n}_{j})+\sigma(U^{n}_{i})\Big[W(t_{(n+1){\Delta t}})-W(t_{n{\Delta t}})\Big],

(5.1)

\displaystyle U^{0}_{i}=\frac{1}{{\Delta x}}\int^{x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}_{x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}u_{0}(x)\,dx,

(5.2)

where
U^{n}_{i}
is the approximate solution of (
1.1
) in the cell
[t^{n},t^{n+1})\times[x_{i-\frac{1}{2}},x_{i+\frac{1}{2}})
and we denote the weights by

\widetilde{G}_{i-j}:=G_{i,j}=G_{j,i}:=\widetilde{G}_{j-i}
for
i\neq j
, and
\widetilde{G}_{0}:=G_{i,i}
. The numerical solution is the piecewise constant function denoted by

u_{\Delta x}(t,x)=U^{n}_{i},\quad\text{ for all }(t,x)\in[t_{n},t_{n+1})\times[x_{i-\frac{1}{2}},x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}).

A tedious but straightforward calculation reveals the explicit values of the weights:

\begin{array}[]{|c||c|c|}\hline\cr\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{G}_{i}}{{\Delta x}}&\displaystyle\lambda\neq\frac{1}{2}&\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\\[8.5359pt]
\hline\cr\hline\cr\displaystyle|i|\geq 2&\displaystyle-\frac{d_{\lambda}({\Delta x})^{-2\lambda}}{2\lambda(1-2\lambda)}\Big[-(i-1)^{1-2\lambda}+2(i)^{1-2\lambda}-(i+1)^{1-2\lambda}\Big]&\displaystyle\frac{-d_{\lambda}}{{\Delta x}}\ln\Big[\frac{i^{2}}{i^{2}-1}\Big]\\[8.5359pt]
\hline\cr\displaystyle|i|=1&\displaystyle\frac{d_{\lambda}{\Delta x}^{-2\lambda}}{2\lambda(1-2\lambda)}\Big[2^{1-2\lambda}+\frac{2\lambda}{2^{1-2\lambda}}-2\Big]=\frac{d_{\lambda}{\Delta x}^{-2\lambda}}{2\lambda(1-2\lambda)}\Big[2^{1-2\lambda}+\lambda 2^{2\lambda}-2\Big]&\displaystyle\frac{-d_{\lambda}}{{\Delta x}}\\[8.5359pt]
\hline\cr i=0&\displaystyle\frac{d_{\lambda}({\Delta x})^{-2\lambda}}{\lambda(1-2\lambda)}\bigg(1-2\lambda(\frac{1}{2})^{1-2\lambda}\bigg)=\frac{d_{\lambda}({\Delta x})^{-2\lambda}}{\lambda(1-2\lambda)}\bigg(1-\lambda 2^{2\lambda}\bigg)&\displaystyle\frac{d_{\lambda}}{{\Delta x}}\bigg(2+2\ln 2\bigg)\\[8.5359pt]
\hline\cr\end{array}

Here
d_{\lambda}=\frac{2^{2\lambda}\Gamma(\frac{1+2\lambda}{2})}{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(1-\lambda)}
, with
\Gamma
being the classical Gamma function.

5.1.
Computation of the non-local diffusion term:

Following
[
21
]
, a truncated domain
[-K{\Delta x},K{\Delta x}]
is considered for a given
K
, and one considers that
U_{i}=U_{-K}
for all
i\leq-K
and
U_{i}=U_{K}
for all
i\geq K
. Therefore, the non-local term in (
5.1
) becomes,

\displaystyle\frac{{\Delta t}}{{\Delta x}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\widetilde{G}_{j-i}A(U^{n}_{j})=

\displaystyle\frac{{\Delta t}}{{\Delta x}}\sum_{|j|<K}\widetilde{G}_{j-i}A(U^{n}_{j})+\frac{{\Delta t}}{{\Delta x}}A(U^{n}_{-K})\sum_{j\leq-K-i}\widetilde{G}_{j}+\frac{{\Delta t}}{{\Delta x}}A(U^{n}_{K})\sum_{j\geq K-i}\widetilde{G}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\Delta t}}{{\Delta x}}\Big[\sum_{|j|<K}\widetilde{G}_{j-i}A(U^{n}_{j})-\frac{1}{2}A(U^{n}_{-K})\sum_{|j|<K+i}\widetilde{G}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}A(U^{n}_{K})\sum_{|j|<K-i}\widetilde{G}_{j}\Big]

since for any
B>0
,
\sum_{j\leq-B}\widetilde{G}_{j}=\sum_{j\geq B}\widetilde{G}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|j|\geq B}\widetilde{G}_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|j|<B}\widetilde{G}_{j}
.
