Gradient Preserving Operator Inference: Data-Driven Reduced-Order Models for Equations with Gradient Structure
Yuwei Geng, Jasdeep Singh, Lili Ju, Boris Kramer, Zhu Wang

Abstract
Hamiltonian Operator Inference has been introduced in [ Sharma, H., Wang, Z., Kramer, B., Physica D: Nonlinear Phenomena, 431, p.133122, 2022 ] to learn structure-preserving reduced-order models (ROMs) for Hamiltonian systems. This approach constructs a low-dimensional model using only data and knowledge of the Hamiltonian function. Such ROMs can keep the intrinsic structure of the system, allowing them to capture the physics described by the governing equations. In this work, we extend this approach to more general systems that are either conservative or dissipative in energy, and which possess a gradient structure. We derive the optimization problems for inferring structure-preserving ROMs that preserve the gradient structure. We further derive an a priori error estimate for the reduced-order approximation. To test the algorithms, we consider semi-discretized partial differential equations with gradient structure, such as the parameterized wave and Korteweg-de-Vries equations, and equations of three-dimensional linear elasticity in the conservative case and the one- and two-dimensional Allen-Cahn equations in the dissipative case. The numerical results illustrate the accuracy, structure-preservation properties, and predictive capabilities of the gradient-preserving Operator Inference ROMs.

Introduction

Consider evolutionary partial differential equations (PDEs) of the following form

\frac{\partial y(x,t;\mu)}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}\ \frac{\delta\mathcal{H}[y;\mu]}{\delta y},

(1)

where the state variable
y
depends on the spatial variable
x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

(d=1,2\text{ or }3)
, time variable
t\in I\subset[t_{0},\infty)
and parameter
\mu\in{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p},p\geq 1
. Moreover,
\mathcal{L}
represents a constant linear differential operator, and
\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta y}
is the variational derivative of the functional

\mathcal{H}[y;\mu]:=\int_{\Omega}H(y,y_{x},y_{xx},\ldots;\mu)\,dx

with
y_{x}
,
y_{xx}
, …, denoting the first- and second-order (and higher order) partial derivatives of
y
with respect to
x
.
Depending on the properties of
\mathcal{L}
, the PDE can possess different features.
If
\mathcal{L}
is skew adjoint,
\mathcal{H}
, referred to as the Hamiltonian, is a constant and the PDE is conservative.
If
\mathcal{L}
is negative semi-definite (resp. definite),
\mathcal{H}
, referred to as a Lyapunov function or energy function, is nonincreasing (resp. monotonically decreasing) and the PDE is dissipative.

Either class of PDEs has broad applications ranging from mechanics
[
45
]
, plasma physics
[
8
,
53
]
to ocean modeling
[
62
,
71
,
72
,
21
]
.
Numerical methods have been developed to approximate and predict solutions to such PDEs. It is well known that numerical schemes that do not preserve the continuous models’ properties are prone to suffer from error accumulation and generate unphysical solutions over a long-term integration. Therefore, structure preserving algorithms have been designed to keep the right features at a discrete level, see the review papers
[
22
,
66
]
and the reference therein. In particular, structure-preserving spatial discretization schemes include finite difference methods such as
[
26
,
16
]
and finite element methods such as
[

,

,

,

,

]
. Structure preserving time discretization schemes include geometric integrators
[

,
47
,
36
]
,
discrete gradient methods
[
60
,
48
]
,
average vector field (AVF) methods
[
59
,
18
]
,
and discrete variational derivative methods
[
27
]
.

The cost (e.g., measured in CPU hours) to simulate a high-dimensional discretization of (
1
) can become a limiting factor when one wants to simulate either for long time horizons, and/or obtain many parametric solutions for different
\mu_{i}
in the context of exploration and uncertainty quantification.
To accelerate the simulation of high-dimensional systems, reduced-order models (ROMs) can provide orders-of-magnitude speedups while providing accurate approximate solutions, see the surveys/books
[
4
,

,

,

,

However, ROMs constructed by projecting the equation onto a linear subspace spanned by a reduced basis, or onto a nonlinear manifold, could destroy the features of mechanical systems. In the context of Lagrangian structure, this has been shown in
[
44
]
, and motivated the development of projection-based model reduction techniques that preserve the Lagrangian structure
[

]
.
For systems with Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian systems, structure-preserving projection-based model reduction has been developed, including the interpolatory projection method proposed in
[
10
]
that preserves the symmetry of linear dynamical systems and is extended to port-Hamiltonian systems via tangential rational interpolation in
[
34
]
; the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)-based Galerkin projection method in

,

,

]
for Hamiltonian systems and the POD/
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-based Petrov-Galerkin project method in
[

,

]
for port-Hamiltonian; the proper symplectic decomposition in
[
56
]
and a Petrov-Galerkin projection-based, variationally consistent approach in
[
33
]

for canonical Hamiltonian systems; and the reduced basis method for Hamiltonian systems in
[

,

,
52
,
39
]
. Moreover, there has been increased interest lately in projection-based model reduction on nonlinear manifolds for Hamiltonian systems, see
[
65
,
15
,
75
]
.

ROMs can also be constructed non-intrusively, which is appealing in situations where only snapshot data of state variables is available, paired with potentially some prior knowledge of the PDE model.
In particular, the operator inference (OpInf) method provides such a nonintrusive framework for model order reduction, which was introduced in
[
55
]
and expanded in many directions, c.f. the survey paper
[
42
]
. The recent work
[
49
]
extended OpInf to affine-parametric systems of PDEs, yet the adaption of the framework to problems with unknown parametric structures is nontrivial. Within the OpInf framework, learning canonical Hamiltonian ROMs on linear subspaces is considered in
[
67
]
, where the linear part of the gradient of the Hamiltonian is inferred through constrained least-squares solutions. This has been extended to noncanonical Hamiltonian systems in
[
32
]
, where the entire Hamiltonian function is assumed to be known, and the reduced operator associated to the linear differential operator
\mathcal{L}
is inferred. Port-Hamiltonian
[

]
and Lagrangian ROMs can also be inferred directly from time-domain data, see
[
64
,
25
,

]
.

Motivated by these developments, in this paper, we propose
gradient-preserving Operator Inference (GP-OPINF)
, a more general framework to nonintrusively construct ROMs that preserve the gradient structure of the PDE model (
1
). In this nonintrusive setting, we assume that we are given snapshot data and some prior knowledge of the continuous model and the functional
\mathcal{H}
, yet that we do not have access to the spatially-discretized forms in high-fidelity solvers. We note that the proposed approach is not limited to Hamiltonian systems.
The novelties of this work include the following:

1.

We propose suitable optimization problems for the gradient-preserving Operator Inference method. In particular, for conservative equations, the solution to the optimization problem is post-processed for improving the gradient structure of ROMs without introducing any consistency errors.

2.

We derive an
a priori
error estimate for the inferred GP-OPINF ROM, which we demonstrate by numerical experiments.

3.

Our framework applies to dissipative and conservative systems with a gradient structure.

In Section
2

In Section

In Section

We then demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed ROM through several numerical examples in Section
5
.
A few concluding remarks are drawn in the last section.

2
Full-Order Model and Structure-Preserving Reduced-Order Model

We present the full-order model setting in Section
2.1
and then discuss the traditional structure-preserving intrusive ROM approach in Section
2.2
. This provides the background for the new method in the following Section
3
.

To ease the exposition, in situation when no confusion arises, we omit the explicit reference to the parameter and adopt the following notations: at any
\mu\in{P}
, for any vector-valued function
\mathbf{f}
and scalar-valued function
g
,

\mathbf{f}(t)\equiv\mathbf{f}(t;\mu),\,\,g(\mathbf{f})\equiv g(\mathbf{f}(t;\mu);\mu),\quad\forall t\in I.

2.1
Full-Order Model

For any parameter (vector)
\mu\in{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p},p\geq 1
, we call the system of finite dimensional ODEs obtained from (
1
) after a structure-preserving spatial discretization the full-order model (FOM). Here, we consider the FOM

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}={\mathbf{D}}\,\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}}),

(2)

where
{\mathbf{y}}(t)\equiv{\mathbf{y}}(t;\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
n
-dimensional state vector and the initial condition is
{\mathbf{y}}(t^{0})={\mathbf{y}}^{0}
.
The right-hand-side of (
2
) represents the velocity field (or drift), in which
{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a real matrix,
H({\mathbf{y}})\equiv H({\mathbf{y}};\mu):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function of the state and
\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})
is the gradient of
H({\mathbf{y}})
. The gradient structure of equation (
2
) plays a key role in characterizing the dynamics:

1.

When
{\mathbf{D}}
is anti-symmetric (also referred to as skew-symmetric or skew-adjoint), that is,
{\mathbf{D}}=-{\mathbf{D}}^{\intercal}
, the system is Hamiltonian.
A special case is when

{\mathbf{D}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&{\mathbf{I}}\\
-{\mathbf{I}}&0\end{bmatrix}

then equation (
2
) represents a canonical Hamiltonian system and the solution flow is symplectic. Due to such a structure, the internal energy of the system,
H({\mathbf{y}})
, is conserved. Mathematically, for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in I
with
t_{1}<t_{2}
, we have

H({\mathbf{y}}(t_{2}))-H({\mathbf{y}}(t_{1}))=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}H({\mathbf{y}}(t))\,{\rm d}t=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})\right)^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}\,\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})\,{\rm d}t=0.

2.

When
{\mathbf{D}}
is negative semi-definite, the system represents a gradient flow and is dissipative. To see this, we can easily check

H({\mathbf{y}}(t_{2}))-H({\mathbf{y}}(t_{1}))=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}H({\mathbf{y}}(t))\,{\rm d}t=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})\right)^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}\,\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})\,{\rm d}t\leq 0.

Note that if
{\mathbf{D}}
is negative definite,
H({\mathbf{y}})
is strictly decreasing.
Numerical schemes to solve these equations recognize the special gradient structure in time discretization, e.g., geometric integrators and average vector field methods.

If the size of the dynamical system is large, it is expensive to simulate (
2
), especially for many parameters or for long time intervals. We next review intrusive projection-based ROMs that can provide accurate approximations at much lower computational cost. To preserve physics of the system, they should possess the same gradient structure.

2.2
Intrusive Structure-Preserving ROMs

We review an intrusive Galerkin-projection-based approach to build the structure-preserving ROM, termed
SP-G
ROM, which was introduced in
[
29
]
.
The first step in the ROM construction is to extract the low-dimensional basis in which we derive the ROM. Here, we use the widely used snapshot-based POD method
[
13
]
.
For a non-parametric problem, we consider
\mu
as fixed, and the snapshots (state vectors at select time instances) are stored in the matrix

{\mathbf{Y}}\equiv{\mathbf{Y}}(\mu)\coloneqq\left[{\mathbf{y}}(t^{0};\mu),{\mathbf{y}}(t^{1};\mu),\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}(t^{s};\mu)\right].

(3)

For simplicity, we assume the snapshot set contains the FOM solutions at all the uniformly distributed discrete time discretization points, that is,
t^{i+1}=t^{i}+\Delta t
for
i=0,\ldots,s-1
. However, in practice one may use only a portion of them as the snapshots.
For a parametric problem, one is interested in a set of parameters
\{\mu^{0},\mu^{1},\cdots,\mu^{p}\}
from
{P}
, for which full-order solutions are generated. The associated snapshot data matrix is defined as

{\mathbf{Y}}\coloneqq\left[{\mathbf{Y}}(\mu^{0}),{\mathbf{Y}}(\mu^{1}),\ldots,{\mathbf{Y}}(\mu^{p})\right].

(4)

The POD basis matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=[\mathbf{\phi}_{1},\mathbf{\phi}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{\phi}_{r}]
contains the leading left singular vectors of
{\mathbf{Y}}
as its columns, which can be computed by the method of snapshots
[
70
]
or singular value decomposition (SVD) and related randomized algorithms.

The reduced-order approximation of the state variable is defined by a linear ansatz:

{{\widehat{{\mathbf{y}}}(t;\mu)}}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t;\mu),

(5)

where
{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t;\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are the unknowns, which are determined by integrating the reduced-order model in time.
A centering trajectory (e.g., the average of snapshots)
\overline{{\mathbf{y}}}
is usually subtracted from each snapshot before calculating the POD modes, which helps in enforcing fixed inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. In that case,
\widehat{{\mathbf{y}}}(t;\mu)=\overline{{\mathbf{y}}}+{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t;\mu)
.
We assume
\overline{{\mathbf{y}}}=\mathbf{0}
in this work for simplicity.
This linear ansatz has been adapted in
[
35
]
for reduced order modeling of problems with parametric, time-varying, inhomogeneous Dirichlet-type boundary conditions.

The
SP-G
ROM applies the ansatz (
5
). To distinguish it from the non-intrusive reduced-order ROMs developed later in this paper, we let
\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}(t;\mu)
denote the
SP-G
ROM’s reduced-order state. The ROM is then constructed by first projecting the FOM onto the low-dimensional basis given by
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, and subsequently adjusting it to ensure the correct gradient structure appears in the resulting ROM. This yields the
SP-G
ROM

\displaystyle\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}}_{r}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}\,\nabla_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}}{H}_{r}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r})

(6)

for
\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}(t)\equiv\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}(t;\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}(t^{0})={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}^{0}
,
where
\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
,
H_{r}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r})\coloneqq H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r})
, and
\nabla_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}}H_{r}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r})={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r})
.
With this construction, the right-hand side of the ROM (
6
) has the same gradient structure as the FOM (
2
). Thus, the ROM has the same properties as its full-order counterpart.
If the system is Hamiltonian,
H_{r}
is also guaranteed to be constant.

We remark that an error exists between
H_{r}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r})
and
H({\mathbf{y}})
, which is due to the projected initial condition
\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}(t^{0})
in the ROM. However, this error shrinks as
r
increases, as a larger ROM basis produces a more accurate ROM initial condition.
Specifically, when the initial condition is independent of any parameters, it can be used as the centering trajectory, eliminating the error caused by projecting the initial condition.
For details, the reader is referred to
[
29
]
.

The above construction of
SP-G
ROM requires access to the full-order coefficient matrix
{\mathbf{D}}
, knowledge of
H
or its gradient
\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H
, and snapshot data.
In the next section, we introduce an Operator Inference approach to build a data-driven ROM for (
2
) with the appropriate gradient structure, but without accessing the full-order coefficient matrix.

3
Gradient-Preserving Operator Inference

A structure-preserving Operator Inference for canonical Hamiltonian systems, named
H-OpInf
, has been developed in
[
67
]
, based on the unconstrained operator inference from
[
55
,
42
]
. The symplecticity-preserving method learns the reduced-order operator associated with the quadratic part of the Hamiltonian function and assumes knowledge of the nonquadratic part of the Hamiltonian function.
The motivational example in
[
67
, Sec. 3.1]
shows that the OpInf ROM without preserving the symplectic structure produces an increasing Hamiltonian energy for a canonical linear wave equation, resulting in inaccurate and unphysical reduced-order simulations. This occurs already within the training data regime.
Non-canonical Hamiltonian systems are considered in
[
32
]
, where the authors construct a ROM that preserves the (Hamiltonian) energy under the assumption of prior knowledge of the entire Hamiltonian function.
In this work, we propose a more general gradient-preserving Operator Inference (GP-OpInf) approach for both conservative and dissipative systems governed by the general FOM (
2
).
In Section
3.1
we introduce the new framework. Section
3.2
presents solution approaches for the conservative case and Section
3.3
for the dissipative case.

3.1
Proposed GP-OPINF Framework

We assume that snapshot data
{\mathbf{Y}}
given in equations (
3
) or (
4
) as well as the symbolic expression of
H({\mathbf{y}})
or the associated gradient is given.
The goal is to develop a non-intrusive gradient-preserving ROM. We do this by employing the linear ansatz
(
5
)
, characterized by the expression
{{\widehat{{\mathbf{y}}}(t;\mu)}}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t;\mu)
, to find
{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t)\equiv{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t;\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
satisfying

\displaystyle\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}={\mathbf{D}}_{r}\,\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}}{H}_{r}({\mathbf{y}}_{r})

(7)

with
{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t^{0})={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}^{0}
. Here
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
is unknown and will be inferred from data. Specifically,
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
is anti-symmetric if the system is conservative; and is negative semi-definite if the system is dissipative.
We refer to equation (
7
) as the
GP-OpInf
ROM since it has the same gradient structure as the FOM (
2
).

To learn
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
, the gradient data are computed based on knowledge of the gradient of the Hamiltonian function and available snapshot data. In the non-parametric case,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}}\equiv{\mathbf{F}}(\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left[\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}}(t^{0};\mu);\mu),\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}}(t^{1};\mu);\mu),\ldots,\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}}(t^{s};\mu);\mu)\right].

(8)

The first-order time derivative data can be generated by applying a finite difference operator, denoted as
\mathcal{D}_{t}[\cdot]
, on the state vectors:

\displaystyle\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}\equiv\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}(\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left[\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}(t^{0};\mu)],\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}(t^{1};\mu)],\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}(t^{s};\mu)]\right].

For instance,

\mathcal{D}_{t}[\cdot]
can be chosen to be the following second-order finite difference operator, satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}(t^{j};\mu)]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-3{\mathbf{y}}(t^{0};\mu)+4{\mathbf{y}}(t^{1};\mu)-{\mathbf{y}}(t^{2};\mu))/(2\Delta t)&j=0,\\
({\mathbf{y}}(t^{j+1};\mu)-{\mathbf{y}}(t^{j-1};\mu))/(2\Delta t)&j=1,\ldots,s-1,\\
({\mathbf{y}}(t^{s-2};\mu)-4{\mathbf{y}}(t^{s-1};\mu)+3{\mathbf{y}}(t^{s};\mu))/(2\Delta t)&j=s.\end{array}\right.

In the parametric case,

{\mathbf{F}}\coloneqq\left[{\mathbf{F}}(\mu^{0}),{\mathbf{F}}(\mu^{1}),\ldots,{\mathbf{F}}(\mu^{p})\right]

and

\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}=\left[\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}(\mu^{0}),\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}(\mu^{1}),\ldots,\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}(\mu^{p})\right]
.
In either case, using the POD basis
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, we project each of the above data matrices onto the POD basis and obtain the projected data

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Y}}_{r}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Y}},\quad{\mathbf{F}}_{r}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{F}},\quad\text{and}\quad\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}.

The
GP-OpInf
learns the reduced operator
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
from the following optimization problems:

1.

When the system is conservative:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}}\frac{1}{2}\|\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}=-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}.

(12)

2.

When the system is dissipative:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}}\frac{1}{2}\|\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\preccurlyeq 0,

(13)

where
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\preccurlyeq 0
indicates that
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
is semi-negative definite.

The next two sections present suitable solutions to these respective optimization problems.

3.2
Solution of the GP-OpInf Optimization for the Conservative Case

The solution to the optimization problem (
12
) can be derived using the method of Lagrange multipliers. Consider the Lagrangian function

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{D}}_{r})=\frac{1}{2}\left<\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r},\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\right>_{F}+\left<{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},{\mathbf{D}}_{r}+{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}\right>_{F},

where the Frobenius inner product is defined as
\left<{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\right>_{F}:=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}
for two real matrices
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
. The stationary point of the Lagrangian then satisfies

\displaystyle(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}){\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\intercal},

(14)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}.

(15)

Using the symmetry of the right hand side in equation (
14
) together with equation (
15
), we get the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}+{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}=\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}-{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}^{\intercal}.

(16)

We next outline three solution approaches to obtain
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
, each leading to a different ROM. We then provide numerical comparisons in Section
5
.

GP-OpInf-V
: This straightforward (“vanilla") approach directly solves the Lyapunov equation (
16
). Given square matrices
{\mathbf{A}}
,
{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, for a general continuous-time Lyapunov equation

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\intercal}={\mathbf{Q}},

it is known that (1) if
{\mathbf{A}}
and
-{\mathbf{A}}^{\intercal}
have no eigenvalues in common, then the solution
{\mathbf{X}}
is unique;
(2) if
{\mathbf{Q}}
is (anti-)symmetric, then
{\mathbf{X}}
must be (anti-)symmetric, see, e.g.,
[
6
]
.
Since
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}
in (
16
) is only positive semi-definite, there might be zero eigenvalues, violating (1), and the system would become singular.
In this case, standard software libraries for numerical linear algebra, such as LAPACK
[
40
]
, provide a unique solution
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
, yet to a slightly perturbed system. This solution may not be symmetric to machine precision anymore, and
due to numerical errors in the solver,
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}
can have small negative eigenvalues.
This could introduce additional errors in the numerical simulation of the GP-OpInf ROM. To resolve this issue, we next consider a regularized optimization.

GP-OpInf-P
:
We introduce a regularization term to the optimization: for some
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}}\frac{1}{2}\|\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}=-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}.

(17)

We use similar arguments as above and define the Lagrangian

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{D}}_{r})=\frac{1}{2}\left<\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r},\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\right>_{F}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left<{\mathbf{D}}_{r},{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\right>_{F}+\left<{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},{\mathbf{D}}_{r}+{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}\right>_{F},

whose stationary point satisfies

\displaystyle(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}){\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}-\epsilon{\mathbf{D}}_{r}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}+{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{\intercal},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}.

This leads to the “perturbed" Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle({\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}+\epsilon{\mathbf{I}}){\mathbf{D}}_{r}+{\mathbf{D}}_{r}({\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}+\epsilon{\mathbf{I}})=\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}-{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}^{\intercal},

(18)

where
{\mathbf{I}}
is the
r\times r
identity matrix.

In order to prevent the eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}+\epsilon{\mathbf{I}}
from being non-positive, we define

\epsilon=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max(c_{0},\,\alpha|\min(\lambda({\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}))|)&\text{ if }\min(\lambda({\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}))\leq c_{0},\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(19)

with a small positive constant
c_{0}
and a positive constant
\alpha
. This approach improves the anti-symmetry of the inferred
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
. However, comparing with (
16
), the perturbed equation could introduce a consistency error.

GP-OpInf
:
We propose an alternative approach to eliminate this common consistency error.
Suppose that
\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}
satisfies (
16
), that is,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}+\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}=\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}-{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}^{\intercal}.

(20)

We assume that the equation can be uniquely solved. In the case where
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}
has zero eigenvalues, the approach used in LAPACK should be applied to provide a unique solution
[
40
]
.
As discussed above,
\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}
may not be exactly anti-symmetric.
After transposing both sides of (
20
) and taking the negative, we obtain

\displaystyle-{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}-\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}=\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}-{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}^{\intercal}.

(21)

By averaging (
20
) and (
21
), we find that (
16
) admits the anti-symmetric solution

\displaystyle{\mathbf{D}}_{r}=(\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}-\widehat{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}^{\intercal})/2.

(22)

Thus, the inferred
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
, defined in (
22
), is superior to the one obtained from (
18
) because it possesses the right property without causing any consistency errors to the optimization problem.

3.3
Solution of the GP-OpInf Optimization for the Dissipative Case

The solution to the optimization problem (
13
) for the dissipative case can be obtained through an interior-point method
[
61
,
37
]
.
By introducing

f({\mathbf{X}})=\lVert\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\lVert_{F}^{2}

and the logarithmic barrier

g({\mathbf{X}})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sum_{i=1}^{r}\log(-\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{X}}))&\text{if }\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{X}})<0\\
&-\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{aligned}\right.

where
{\mathbf{X}}
is an
r\times r
matrix and
\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{X}})
is the
i
th eigenvalue of the matrix
{\mathbf{X}}
, we can solve, instead of (
13
), an unconstrained optimization with an augmented objective function:

{\mathbf{D}}_{r}=\arg\min_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r}\in\mathbf{R}^{r\times r}}\left\{f_{\beta}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r})\coloneqq f(\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r})-\beta g(\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}_{r})\right\},

(23)

where
\beta>0
is the barrier parameter. The corresponding (disspative) ROM (
7
) using
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
determined from (
23
) is referred to as the
GP-OpInf
ROM.

Remark 1
.

The online simulation time of the nonintrusive
GP-OpInf
ROM (
7
) is similar to that of the intrusive
SP-G
ROM for the same
r
, as both ROMs have the same structure yet may have different model parameters.
If the Hamiltonian is nonlinear, one can use hyper-reduction techniques to accelerate the evaluation of the nonlinear term. For instance, the DEIM approach presented in
[
54
]
can be used to reduce the cost for evaluating the nonlinear Hamiltonian while preserving the structure.

Compared to
SP-G
ROM, the
GP-OpInf
ROM requires additional offline computation to infer
\mathbf{D}_{r}
. In the conservative case, we need to solve a
r\times r
Lyapunov equation. Since the matrix
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}
is usually dense, the Bartels-Stewart algorithm can be applied
[
9
]
with an associated computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(r^{3})
flops. In the dissipative case, one can solve (
23
) by a gradient descent method as outlined in
A
. The associated computational complexity is
\mathcal{O}(r^{2}sp)
per iteration, where
sp
represents the number of snapshots.

4
Error Estimation

Previous work in
[
65
,
32
]
established an asymptotic (as
r\rightarrow n
) convergence of learned reduced operators to the associated projection-based reduced operators. However, this analysis can sometimes not explain the error in the solution of the ROM, as small errors in the operators may cause large solution errors, specifically for structured (e.g., gradient) systems.
Here, we take a step further and analyze the approximate
solution error
of the inferred ROMs in the
preasymptotic
regime. Specifically, we estimate the
a priori
error of the
GP-OpInf
ROM approximation relative to the FOM (
7
). The derivation mainly follows the error estimation developed in
[
29
]
for analyzing the projection-based
SP-G
ROM. However, in addition to considering the error from POD truncation, there is a need to estimate the error caused by Operator Inference.

Given a mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Lipschitz constant and the logarithmic Lipschitz constant of the mapping are defined as

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}[f]:=\sup_{{\mathbf{u}}\neq{\mathbf{v}}}\frac{\|f({\mathbf{u}})-f({\mathbf{v}})\|}{\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\|},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{log-Lip}}[f]:=\sup_{{\mathbf{u}}\neq{\mathbf{v}}}\frac{\left<{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}},f({\mathbf{u}})-f({\mathbf{v}})\right>}{\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}},

where
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for any positive integer
n
denotes the Euclidian inner product. The logarithmic Lipschitz constant could be negative, which was used in
[
20
]
to show that the error of reduced-order solution is uniformly bounded over the time interval of simulations.

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathbf{y}}(t)
be the solution of the FOM (
2
) on the time interval
[0,T]
and
{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}}(t)
be the solution of the ROM (
7
) on the same interval. Suppose
\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})
is Lipschitz continuous, then the ROM approximation error satisfies

\int_{0}^{T}\hskip-2.84544pt\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}}\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t\leq C(T)\ \bigg(\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}\hskip-2.84544pt\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}{\rm d}t}_{\text{ projection error}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}\hskip-2.84544pt\|\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}]\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t}_{\text{data error}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{T}\hskip-2.84544pt\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}]-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t}_{\text{optimization error}}\bigg),

(24)

where
C(T)=\max\{1+C_{2}^{2},2\}T\alpha(T)
,

\alpha(T)=2\int_{0}^{T}e^{2C_{1}(T-\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau
, and the constants
C_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{log-Lip}}[{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H]
and

C_{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\|\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}[\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H]
.

There are three terms in the error bound: the first term
\int_{0}^{T}\|{\mathbf{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}{\rm d}t
is the
projection error
caused by projecting
{\mathbf{y}}(t;\mu)
onto the subspace spanned by the POD basis
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
, that interprets the POD approximability and has been analyzed thoroughly in
[
43
,
69
]
; the second one
\int_{0}^{T}\|\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}]\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t
is the
data error
caused by the approximation of time derivative snapshots using the finite difference method; the third one
\int_{0}^{T}\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}]-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t
represents the
optimization error
generated by learning
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}
from data.

Proof.

Consider the ROM of a fixed dimension
r
, and define its approximate solution error by

{\mathbf{e}}\coloneqq{\mathbf{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}}}.

It can be decomposed as
{\mathbf{e}}=\boldsymbol{\rho}+\boldsymbol{\theta}
with
\boldsymbol{\rho}\coloneqq{\mathbf{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{y}}_{r}
.
Define

\boldsymbol{\zeta}\coloneqq\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}]
and

\boldsymbol{\eta}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}]-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})
.
Consider the time derivative of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}_{r}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\,\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}}{H}_{r}({\mathbf{y}}_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}\,\nabla_{{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}]

\displaystyle+{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}]-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle+{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle+{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}})-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}}).

Note that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|=\frac{1}{2\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|}\left<\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right>.

(26)

Taking the inner product of equation (
25
) with
\theta
, the terms on the right-hand side of (
25
) are

\left<\boldsymbol{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}]\right>=\left<\boldsymbol{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{y}}}-\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}])\right>\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|,

(27)

\left<\boldsymbol{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{{\mathbf{y}}}]-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})\right>=\left<\boldsymbol{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\eta}\right>\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|,

(28)

\left<\boldsymbol{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\,\left(\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}})-\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}})\right)\right>\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\,\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\|\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}[\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H]\,\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|,

(29)

\left<\boldsymbol{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\,\left(\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}})-\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{{\mathbf{y}}_{r}})\right)\right>\leq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{log-Lip}}[{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H]\,\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}.

(30)

Let
C_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{log-Lip}}[{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H]
,

C_{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\|\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{Lip}}[\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H]
, and combine (
26
) with (
25
) and (
27
)-(
30
), we have

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq C_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+C_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|.

Applying the classical differential version of Gronwall lemma (see, for example, Appendix B in
[
24
]
) over the interval
[0,t]
, for
t\leq T
, and
using the fact that
\boldsymbol{\theta}(0)=0
, we get

\|\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\|\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{C_{1}(t-\tau)}(C_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|)\,{\rm d}\tau.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the RHS and squaring both sides, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{2C_{1}(t-\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau\int_{0}^{t}[C_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|+(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|)]^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(t)\left[\int_{0}^{T}C_{2}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|)^{2}\,{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(T)\left[C_{2}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|)^{2}\,{\rm d}t\right],

where
\alpha(t)=2\int_{0}^{t}e^{2C_{1}(t-\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau
.

Hence,

\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t\leq T\alpha(T)\left[C_{2}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|)^{2}\,{\rm d}t\right].

This, together with the triangular inequality and orthogonality of
\rho
and
\theta
, yields

\int_{0}^{T}\|{\mathbf{e}}(t)\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t\leq\left(1+C_{2}^{2}T\alpha(T)\right)\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|^{2}{\rm d}t+2T\alpha(T)\int_{0}^{T}(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|^{2})\,{\rm d}t,

which proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 2
.

In comparison to the asymptotic error estimation of the
SP-G
ROM in
[
29
]
, the
GP-OpInf
ROM is different in that it only involves the projection error of the state, whereas the error of projecting the gradient of the Hamiltonian onto the reduced basis space is included in the
SP-G
. Additionally, it incorporates the data error and optimization error.
We will numerically investigate these errors in the next section.

Remark 3
.

The FOM (
2
) and ROM (
7
) are continuous in time. In numerical simulations, time-marching schemes with identical or different time step sizes are applied. Consequently, the time discretization errors appear in the upper bound of the ROM approximation error. This time discretization error, along with the data error, can be controlled by choosing a sufficiently small time step size.

Remark 4
.

It is worth mentioning that, in addition to (
8
), one could evaluate the gradient data on the projected snapshots, e.g.,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{F}}\equiv{\mathbf{F}}(\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left[\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}(t^{0};\mu);\mu),\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}(t^{1};\mu);\mu),\ldots,\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}(t^{s};\mu);\mu)\right]

(31)

when inferring the reduced operator.
Using it does not alter the proposed
GP-OpInf
framework, yet for the error estimation, equations (
28
) and (
29
) can be grouped together. This results in a similar error bound to (
24
), with the only difference being that the optimization error term becomes
\int_{0}^{T}\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}]-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}})\|^{2}
.

In this section, we provide many numerical experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. In Section

5.1
Error Measures

Given a set of snapshots, the performance of a ROM depends on its dimension and the numerical schemes used for simulations.
To test the performance of the various
GP-OpInf
ROMs and to illustrate the error analysis, for a given time discretization, we compute the (squared)
ROM approximation error
as a function of
r
:

\mathcal{E}(r):=\frac{T\Delta A}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|{\mathbf{y}}(t^{j};\mu)-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t^{j};\mu)\|^{2},

(32)

where
\|\cdot\|
represents the vector 2-norm,
{\mathbf{y}}(t^{j};\mu)
and
{\mathbf{y}}_{r}(t^{j};\mu)
are the full-order and reduced-order solutions at
t^{j}
, respectively, for
j=1,\ldots,N
,
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=[\mathbf{\phi}_{1},\mathbf{\phi}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{\phi}_{r}]
is the reduced basis matrix, and
\Delta A
represents the associated element size in a uniform grid. Following the error bound (
24
), we additionally compute the (squared)
projection error
and (squared)
optimization error
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}}(r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{T\Delta A}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|{\mathbf{y}}(t^{j};\mu)-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{y}}(t^{j};\mu)\|^{2},

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{opt}}(r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{T\Delta A}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\mathcal{D}_{t}[{\mathbf{y}}(t^{j};\mu)]-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}}H({\mathbf{y}}(t^{j};\mu);\mu)\|^{2}.

(34)

The norms are evaluated by the same quadrature rule such as the composite rectangle method.
In the following numerical experiments, we frequently use two matrices in discrete schemes:

\mathbf{L}_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}-2&1&0&\cdots&1\\
1&-2&1&\cdots&0\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
0&\cdots&1&-2&1\\
1&\cdots&0&1&-2\end{array}\right)_{n\times n}\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\mathbf{S}_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&1&0&\cdots&-1\\
-1&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&-1&0&1\\
1&0&\ldots&-1&0\end{array}\right)_{n\times n};

and we denote the spatial domain of an equation by
\Omega
and the time domain by
I
. Specifically, in the FOM simulations the time interval
I=[0,T_{\text{FOM}}]
, while in the ROM simulations
I=[0,T_{\text{ROM}}]
.

For our numerical tests, the Lyapunov equation is solved by using the
\mathsf{lyap}
function when using
\mathsf{Matlab}
, or the
\mathsf{linalg.solve\_continuous\_lyapunov}
function when using
\mathsf{python/scipy}
.
Moreover, we use the licensed MOSEK
[
7
]

[
23
,
2
]
to solve the semi-definite programming problem (
23
). If this software is unavailable, one can obtain the optimal solution through gradient descent, as discussed in

.

5.2
Conservative PDEs

We consider the wave equation, the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation and the linear elastic equations as a test-bed for investigating the
GP-OpInf
ROM when approximating conservative systems. After a spatial discretization of the PDEs, the former yields a canonical Hamiltonian system while the latter results in a non-canonical Hamiltonian system. We note that both equations have also been considered in
[
67
,
32
]
with similar configurations.

5.2.1
Parameterized wave equation

Consider the one-dimensional linear wave equation with a constant wave speed
c
:

u_{tt}=c^{2}u_{xx},\quad(x,t)\in\Omega\times I,

where
u=u(x,t;\mu)
is the parameter-dependent solution and
\mu\in P
a parameter appearing in the initial condition, defined below alongside the boundary and initial conditions.
The wave equation can be recast to the
canonical
Hamiltonian formulation (see, e.g.
[
18
]
) with the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{2}u_{x}^{2})\,{\rm d}x
, so that

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{t}\\
v_{t}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta u}\\
\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta v}\end{array}\right],

which has the symplectic (gradient) structure.

Taking a uniform partition in the spatial domain with the mesh size
\Delta x
and defining a consistent discrete Hamiltonian
\overline{H}(t)\coloneqq\Delta xH({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})
with
H({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\sum_{j}[\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{2(\Delta x)^{2}}({\mathbf{u}}_{j+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{j})^{2}]
leads to the
Hamiltonian system of ODEs

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{u}}}\\
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{v}}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{\mathbf{I}}_{n}\\
-{\mathbf{I}}_{n}&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{u}}\\
{\mathbf{v}}\end{array}\right].

(35)

Here,
n
is the number of degrees of freedom,
{\mathbf{I}}_{n}
the
n\times n
identity matrix, and
{\mathbf{A}}
is a discretization of the scaled, one-dimensional second order differential operator.
The above system is of the form (
2
) with
{\mathbf{y}}=[{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal},{\mathbf{v}}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}
and

{\mathbf{D}}
is the
2n\times 2n
skew-symmetric block matrix from above. This implies the system is conservative and the Hamiltonian is a constant function.

Computational setting

Consider the case in which
c=0.1
and

The boundary condition is set to be periodic and the initial condition satisfies

\frac{1}{4}(2-s)^{3}&\text{\quad if \,\,}1<s\leq 2,\\
0&\text{\quad if \,\,}s>2,\end{array}\right.\quad\text{ and }\quad s(x;\mu)=\mu\left|x-\frac{1}{2}\right|

with
\mu
a parameter that can take values from
{P}=[5,15]
.
In the FOM, the finite difference method is used for spatial discretization and

{\mathbf{A}}=\frac{c^{2}}{\Delta x^{2}}\mathbf{L}_{n}.

For time integration, the midpoint rule is applied with the time step

.
The resulting linear system is solved by the built-in direct solver in MATLAB.
For
T_{\text{FOM}}=100
,

and

, the time evolution of the full-order states and the energy

ROM construction

We assemble the snapshot matrices

and

{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{u}&\mathbf{0}\\

{\mathbf{Y}}_{r}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{Y}}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{\intercal}[{\mathbf{U}}^{\intercal},{\mathbf{V}}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}

5.2.2
Parameterized Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation

Next, we consider the one-dimensional KdV equation

u_{t}=\alpha uu_{x}+\nu u_{xxx},\quad(x,t)\in\Omega\times I,

where
u=u(x,t;\mu)
is the parameter-dependent solution and
\mu\in P
a parameter appearing in the initial condition, defined below alongside the boundary and initial conditions. The KdV equation can be rewritten as a non-canonical Hamiltonian system (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
):

u_{t}=\mathcal{S}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta u},

where
\mathcal{S}=\partial_{x}
denotes the first-order derivative operator with respect to space, and the Hamiltonian function

\mathcal{H}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\alpha}{6}u^{3}-\frac{\nu}{2}u_{x}^{2}\right)\,{\rm d}x
.
After taking a spatial discretization with a uniform mesh size
\Delta x
and defining a consistent discrete Hamiltonian
\overline{H}(t)\coloneqq\Delta xH({\mathbf{u}})
with
H({\mathbf{u}})=\sum_{j}\left[\frac{\alpha}{6}u_{j}^{3}-\frac{\nu}{2}(u_{j+1}-u_{j})^{2}\right]
, we obtain the semi-discrete Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{u}}}={\mathbf{A}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}{\mathbf{u}}^{2}+\nu{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}\right),

(36)

where
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are the matrices associated to the discretization of the skew-adjoint operator
\mathcal{S}
and the second-order derivative by central differences, respectively.
Since
{\mathbf{A}}
is skew-symmetric, this dynamical system conserves the discrete Hamiltonian
\overline{H}(t)
.

Computational setting

We set
\Omega=[-20,20]
,

\alpha=-6
,
\nu=-1
, and consider periodic boundary conditions
u(-20,t;\mu)=u(20,t;\mu)
for any
t\in I
and we parameterize the initial condition
u_{0}(x;\mu)=\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{x}{\mu}\right)
, for any
\mu\in{P}=[1,5]
.
In the FOM (
36
), we have

{\mathbf{A}}=\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\mathbf{S}_{n},\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{B}}=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\mathbf{L}_{n}.

The mesh size
\Delta x=0.01
(correspondingly,
n=4,000
) is used in all simulations.
For time integration, we use the average vector field (AVF) method
[
18
]
together with a Picard iteration to solve the nonlinear system.

When
T_{\text{FOM}}=20
,
\Delta t=0.01
and
\mu=\sqrt{2}
, the time evolution of the full-order state and approximate Hamiltonian
\overline{H}(t)
are shown in Figure
8
. We observe that the system is conservative, with the Hamiltonian staying around
\overline{H}(t)\approx-1.13
during the simulation.

5.3.1
One-dimensional Allen-Cahn equation

Consider the one-dimensional Allen-Cahn equation

\displaystyle u_{t}=\epsilon^{2}u_{xx}+(u-u^{3}),\quad(x,t)\in\Omega\times I\text{ and }\mu\in P,

where
u=u(x,t;\mu)
with
\mu
a parameter in the initial condition. The equation can be recast to the form (
1
), that is,

u_{t}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta u}
,
with the Lyapunov function

\mathcal{H}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}u_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\frac{1}{4}u^{4}\right)\,{\rm d}x
(see, e.g.,
[
18
]
).
After applying a uniform spatial discretization with the mesh size
\Delta x
and defining a consistent discrete Lyapunov function
\overline{H}(t)\coloneqq\Delta xH({\mathbf{u}})
with
H({\mathbf{u}})=\sum_{j}[\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\Delta x^{2}}(u_{j+1}-u_{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}u_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}u_{j}^{4}]

6
Conclusions

In this work, we considered evolutionary PDEs with a gradient structure with the goal to infer reduced-order models with the same gradient structure from simulated data of the semi-discretized PDEs.
We first projected the high-dimensional snapshot data onto a POD basis, and from the projected data we infer a low-dimensional operator by solving a constrained optimization problem. The resulting operators then define a low-dimensional model, termed the gradient-preserving Operator Inference (
GP-OpInf
) ROM.
By enforcing the proper constraints in the inference of the low-dimensional operators, we ensure that the
GP-OpInf
ROM has the appropriate gradient structure, thereby preserving the essential physics in the reduced-order dynamics.
We further analyzed the associated approximation error of the
GP-OpInf
ROM. The analysis shows that an upper bound for the error consists of the combination of the POD projection error, the data error, and the OpInf optimization error.
We test the accuracy, structure-preservation properties, and predictive capabilities of the
GP-OpInf
ROM on several PDE examples. For conservative test cases, we consider the parameterized wave equation and Korteweg-de-Vries equation, as well as a three-dimensional linear elasticity problem, and for dissipative test cases we consider the one-and two-dimensional Allen-Cahn equation.
The numerical experiments show that
GP-OpInf
can achieve the same performance as the intrusive, projection-based ROM when the optimization error and data error are dominated by the POD projection error, which is typically true if the dimension of the ROM is low. This observation aligns with our error estimation.
Moreover, for parametric problems, the low-dimensional
GP-OpInf
ROMs can attain accuracy close to that of the full-order simulations even for unseen parameters.

Going forward, it would be interesting to learn the Hamiltonian or its gradient function entirely from data, without relying on any prior knowledge. For this purpose, the work in
[
68
]
could be extended to the Hamiltonian case. Moreover, a joint learning of the dissipative and conservative matrices, similar to the port-Hamiltonian DMD framework from
[
51
]
would be an important next step.

Iterative algorithm for solving (
23
)

To solve (
23
) , we first find the gradient of the objective function.
The barrier function
g({\mathbf{X}})
can be written as follows.

\displaystyle g({\mathbf{X}})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\log(-\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{X}}))=\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}(-\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{X}}))\right)=\log((-1)^{r}det({\mathbf{X}})).

Without loss of generality, we consider an even
r
here.
Differentiating it with respect to the
ij
entry yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log(det({\mathbf{X}}))}{\partial{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}}=\frac{1}{det({\mathbf{X}})}\frac{\partial det({\mathbf{X}})}{\partial{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}}.

Based on the cofactor formula for determinant,
det({\mathbf{X}})=\sum_{k=1}^{s}{\mathbf{X}}_{ik}{\mathbf{C}}_{ik}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial det({\mathbf{X}})}{\partial{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\mathbf{X}}_{ik}{\mathbf{C}}_{ik})}{\partial{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\left(\frac{\partial{\mathbf{X}}_{ik}}{{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}}{\mathbf{C}}_{ik}+{\mathbf{X}}_{ik}\frac{\partial{\mathbf{C}}_{ik}}{\partial{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}}\right)={\mathbf{C}}_{ij}.

Therefore, we have

\frac{\partial\log(det({\mathbf{X}}))}{\partial{\mathbf{X}}_{ij}}=\frac{1}{det({\mathbf{X}})}{\mathbf{C}}_{ij}.

Due to the fact that
{\mathbf{X}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{det({\mathbf{X}})}{\mathbf{C}}^{\intercal}
, we have
\nabla g({\mathbf{X}})={\mathbf{X}}^{-\intercal}
, and

\nabla g({\mathbf{D}}_{r})={\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{-\intercal}.

(38)

Next,
f({\mathbf{D}}_{r})
is recast into the following form using the trace:

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{D}}_{r})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left((\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r})(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r})^{\intercal}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}^{\intercal}-\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}^{\intercal}+{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal}\right).

Then the gradient of
f({\mathbf{D}}_{r})
can be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla f({\mathbf{D}}_{r})=-\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}-\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}+({\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}+{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}=-2(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}){\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}.

(39)

By combining (
39
) and (

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\beta}({\mathbf{D}}_{r})=\nabla f({\mathbf{D}}_{r})-\beta\nabla g({\mathbf{D}}_{r})=-2(\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{{\mathbf{Y}}}_{r}-{\mathbf{D}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}){\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{\intercal}-\beta{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{-\intercal}.

Secondly, we use the gradient descent method

]
to approximate the solution

by the given
{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{k}
, which follows

{\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{k+1}={\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{k}-\tau^{k}\nabla f_{\beta}({\mathbf{D}}_{r}^{k})

where

Instead of a constant barrier parameter

This iteration continues till the magnitude of gradient
