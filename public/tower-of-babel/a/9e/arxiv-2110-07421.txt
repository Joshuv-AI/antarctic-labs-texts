On some batch code properties of the simplex code Thanks: An extended abstract of part of this work has been submitted to WCC 2022 (the Twelfth International Workshop on Coding and Cryptography).
Henk D.L. Hollmann, Karan Khathuria, Ago-Erik Riet, Vitaly Skachek

Abstract
The binary k k -dimensional simplex code is known to be a 2 k − 1 2^{k-1} -batch code and is conjectured to be a 2 k − 1 2^{k-1} -functional batch code. Here, we offer a simple, constructive proof of a result that is “in between” these two properties. Our approach is to relate these properties to certain (old and new) additive problems in finite abelian groups. We also formulate a conjecture for finite abelian groups that generalizes the above-mentioned conjecture.



Keywords:
Batch codes - Simplex code - Switch codes - Functional batch code - Finite abelian groups

1
Introduction

A
t
-batch code
is a method to store a data record in encoded form on multiple servers in such a way that the bit-values in any batch of
t
positions from the record can be retrieved by decoding the bit-values in
t
disjoint groups of positions.

Batch codes were initially introduced in
[
7
]
as a method to improve load-balancing in distributed data storage systems. Later, so-called
switch codes
(a special case of batch codes) were proposed in
[
13
]
as a method to increase the throughput rate in network switches.

In
[
11
,
12
]
, it was shown that the well-known binary simplex code, a code of length
2^{k}-1
, dimension
k
, and minimum distance
2^{k-1}
(
k\geq 1
integer) is a
2^{k-1}
-batch code. The proof of that result is somewhat cumbersome, and the algorithm resulting from the proof requires to store and use a database containing all the solutions for the cases where
k\leq 7
. More recently, in
[
17
,
18
]
the authors conjecture that the
k
-dimensional simplex code is even a
2^{k-1}
-functional batch code. (For precise definitions of this and other used notions, we refer to Section
2
.)

In this paper, we give a simple, algorithmic proof of a result that falls halfway between the known result for the simplex code in
[
11
,
12
]
and the conjecture in
[
17
,
18
]
. Our approach is to relate the required properties of the simplex code to certain additive problems in finite abelian groups.

The contents of this paper are as follows. In Section
2
, we provide precise definitions of all the notions mentioned above, together with precise statements of some known results and conjectures. In our approach, we deal with certain reformulations of these statements, as derived in Section
3
. In Section
4
we describe a variation of an algorithm in abelian groups first discovered by Marshall Hall, Jr., with slightly simpler proofs than those given in
[
5
]
, that we then use to demonstrate our main result.

The problems that we investigate here can be considered as special cases for groups of the form
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
of problems for general finite abelian groups. In Section
5
, we discuss these relations. We formulate a new conjecture for finite abelian groups that would imply the functional batch conjectures if true for groups of the form
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, and we prove that this new conjecture holds for the case of cyclic groups of prime order. We end with some conclusions in Section
6
.

This paper is an updated version of
[
6
]
.

2
Preliminaries

All codes in this paper are binary and linear. We use
\mathbb{F}_{2}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
to denote the finite field of two elements 0,1, with addition and multiplication modulo 2, and we write
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
for the vector space of dimension
k
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Thus
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
consists of all binary vectors of length
k
. The
i
th unit vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is the binary vector that has a one in position
i
and a zero in all other positions. We will write
E_{k}
to denote the set of the
k
unit vectors of length
k
. For convenience, we commonly number the positions with the integers

1,\ldots,k
. The
support
of a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, written as
{\rm supp}(\boldsymbol{v})
, is the collection of positions where
\boldsymbol{v}
has a 1, and the (Hamming)
weight

w(\boldsymbol{v})
of
\boldsymbol{v}
is the size of
{\rm supp}(\boldsymbol{v})
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a binary
k\times n
matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
can
serve
a
request sequence

\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{t}
of (not
necessarily distinct) nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
if we can find pairwise disjoint subsets
I_{1},\ldots,I_{t}

of the set of column indices
\{1,2,...,n\}
of
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}

such that for
j=1,\ldots,t
, the columns of
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
with index in
I_{j}
sum to
\boldsymbol{r}_{j}
.

Note that if the columns of
\boldsymbol{G}

with index in
I
sum up to
\boldsymbol{r}
, then for any code word
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{G}
in the binary linear code generated by
\boldsymbol{G}
, the linear combination
(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{a})
of the data bits encoded by
\boldsymbol{c}
can be obtained as the sum of the values
c_{i}
with
i\in I
. Therefore, Definition
2.1
expresses the property that a sequence of requests for the linear combinations
(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{r}_{t},\boldsymbol{a})
of the data
\boldsymbol{a}
encoded by a code word
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{G}
can be served
simultaneously
even if the value at each position of the code word can be read at most
once
.
We will be interested in various properties of such matrices defined in terms of the particular request sequences that they can serve.

Definition 2.2
.

The binary
k\times n
matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
(as well as the binary linear code generated by
\boldsymbol{G}
) is (i) a
t
-PIR code
, (ii) a
t
-batch code
, (iii) a
t
-odd batch code
, or (iv) a
t
-functional batch code
if
\boldsymbol{G}
can serve any request sequence of length
t
consisting of (i) the
t
-fold repetition of a unit vector in
E_{k}
, (ii) unit vectors in
E_{k}
only, (iii) vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
of odd weight only, or (iv) nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, respectively.

The notions of
t
-PIR code and
t
-batch code are well known (but note that some authors employ a more general definition and refer to these codes as
multiset primitive
), and together with
t
-functional batch codes are defined, for example, in
[
17
,
18
]
. For a recent overview of these and related types of codes, see
[
9
]
. The notion of
t
-odd batch code is new and is introduced here for convenience.

The (binary)
simplex code
of length
n=2^{k}-1
has a
k\times(2^{k}-1)
generator matrix
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
whose columns are the distinct nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
. In
[
11
]
(see also
[
12
]
) it was shown that
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
is a
2^{k-1}
-batch code, but the proof is somewhat cumbersome. Recently, it was conjectured that
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
is even a
2^{k-1}
-functional batch code,
and it was shown that
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
is a
t
-functional batch code for
t=2^{k-2}+2^{k-4}+\lfloor 2^{k/2}/\sqrt{24}\rfloor
, again with a rather involved proof
[
17
,
18
]
.
After completion of this paper, we learned that this result was further improved in
[
15
]
and
[
14
]
, where it was shown that
G_{k}
is a
t
-functional batch code for
t=\lfloor(2/3)\cdot 2^{k-1}\rfloor
and
t=\lfloor(5/6)\cdot 2^{k-1}\rfloor-k
, respectively.

In this paper, we will provide a simple algorithmic proof that
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
is a
2^{k-1}
-odd batch code. In fact, we will prove slightly more.

Theorem 2.3
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
,
the binary simplex code of length
n=2^{k}-1
and dimension
k
, with generator matrix
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
as above, is a
2^{k-1}
-odd batch code. In addition, every sequence of
2^{k-1}
odd-weight vectors from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
can be served with column subsets of size at most two.

Note that since every unit vector has odd weight (in fact, a weight equal to 1), Theorem
2.3
implies that the
k
-dimensional simplex code generated by
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
is in fact a
2^{k-1}
-batch code, a fact that was first proved in
[
11
,
12
]
.

Earlier, we mentioned the conjecture
that the simplex code of length
2^{k}-1
is a
2^{k-1}
-functional batch code. We believe that even a slightly stronger statement may be true.

Conjecture 2.4
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
,
the binary simplex code of length
n=2^{k}-1
and dimension
k
, with generator matrix
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
as above, can serve every sequence of
2^{k-1}
vectors from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}

with column subsets of size at most two
.

Now a request
\boldsymbol{r}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
is served by
one column
\boldsymbol{x}
or by
two columns
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
from
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
,
precisely when there are two vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
with
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{y}
. So it is easily seen that the above conjecture is in fact equivalent to the following.

Conjecture 2.5
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. For every sequence of nonzero vectors
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, there are pairwise distinct nonzero vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
such that the nonzero vectors among
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{2^{k-1}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
are pairwise distinct and distinct from
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}
.

3
A reformulation

We will in fact prove the following slight generalization of Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer, and let
H
be a
(k-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
.
The binary simplex code of length
n=2^{k}-1
and dimension
k
, with generator matrix
\boldsymbol{G}_{k}
as above, can serve every sequence of
2^{k-1}
vectors from the complement
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
of
H
with column subsets of size at most two.

For later use, we now derive several equivalent formulations of Theorem
3.1
.
Note that if

\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
with
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
, then without loss of generality we may assume that
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
(hence nonzero) and
\boldsymbol{y}\in H
. So it is easily seen that Theorem
3.1
is in fact equivalent to the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer, and let
H
be a
(k-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
.
For every sequence of vectors
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
, there are pairwise distinct vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
such that the nonzero vectors among the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
in
H
are also pairwise distinct.

Of special interest is the case where
H
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
are the collection of even and odd weight vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, respectively. Note that for this case, Theorem
3.1
reduces to Theorem
2.3
.

We will now show the equivalence of Theorem
3.2
and the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. Given any sequence
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, there exists a numbering
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}}
of the vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
such that the nonzero vectors in the sequence
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}}
are pairwise distinct.

To show this, we need some preparation.
Let
H
be a
(k-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
. Then
H
is of the form

H=\boldsymbol{u}^{\perp}:=\{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\mid h_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+h_{k}u_{k}=0\}

for some
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
. Now
H
is a subgroup of
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k},+)
isomorphic to
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1},+)
. Indeed, if
u_{j}\neq 0
, then it is easily verified that
\varphi:H\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1}
defined by
\varphi(\boldsymbol{h})=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{j-1},h_{j+1},\ldots,h_{k})
is an isomorphism.
Fix some
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
,
and extend
\varphi
to a linear map
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1}
by defining
\varphi(\boldsymbol{a}+\boldsymbol{h})=\varphi(\boldsymbol{h})
for
\boldsymbol{h}\in H
. Note that
\varphi
also sets up a one-to-one correspondence between
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1}
.

Now let
k\geq 2
be an integer. First, suppose Theorem
3.3
holds for
k-1
, let
H
be a
(k-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, and let
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1}
be a linear map, 1-1 on both
H
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
, constructed as discussed above. Put
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}^{\prime}=\varphi(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})
(
i=1,\ldots,2^{k-1}
). Applying Theorem
3.3
for
k-1
, we conclude that there is a numbering
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{2^{k-1}}
of the vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1}
such that the nonzero vectors among
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{2^{k-1}}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{2^{k-1}}+\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}_{2^{k-1}}
are pairwise distinct. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
,
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
, and
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in H
(
i=1,\ldots,2^{k-1}
) be the unique vectors such that
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{i}
,
\varphi(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})=\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}_{i}
, and
\varphi(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\prime}
, respectively.
By the linearity of
\varphi
, we have that
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{r}_{i}=\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
for all
i
; moreover, since
\varphi
is one-to-one on
H
and on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
, with
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}
if and only if
\varphi(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{i}=0
, the
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
are pairwise distinct and the nonzero
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
are also pairwise distinct. So we conclude that Theorem
3.2
holds for
k
.

Conversely, suppose that Theorem
3.2
holds for
k
, and let
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}_{2^{k-1}}
be in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1}
. Let
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
be the unique vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
for which
\varphi(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})=\boldsymbol{r}_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i
.
Applying Theorem
3.2
, we conclude that there are
pairwise distinct vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
such that the nonzero vectors among
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{2^{k-1}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
are also pairwise distinct.
Now let
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j}=\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{j}=\varphi(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})
for all
j
. By linearity of
\varphi
, we have
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}_{j}=\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{j}
for all
j
. Moreover, since
\varphi
is one-to-one both on
H
and on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus H
, the
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j}
are pairwise distinct, hence they form a numbering of the vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k-1}
,and the nonzero
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{j}
are also pairwise distinct. So we conclude that Theorem
3.3
holds for
k-1
. We have proved the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

For every integer
k\geq 2
, Theorem
3.1
,
Theorem
3.2
, and Theorem
2.3
are all equivalent to Theorem
3.3
(with
k
replaced by
k-1
).

4
A servicing algorithm

We will now describe an algorithm to solve the numbering problem inherent in Theorem
3.3
. We first introduce some terminology.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(G,+)
be an finite abelian group.
A
service
for a given sequence
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
in
G
is a collection of pairwise distinct
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in G
such that the
m
elements
y_{1}=x_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,y_{m}=x_{m}+r_{m}
are also pairwise distinct in
G
.

We will often think of such a service as a collection of ordered triples

(x_{1},y_{1},r_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},y_{m},r_{m})

with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
pairwise distinct in
G
,
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
pairwise distinct in
G
, and
x_{i}+r_{i}=y_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.
The next result is crucial for our approach.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(G,+)
be a finite abelian group.
Given a service
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
for the sequence
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
in
G
with

0\leq m\leq|G|-2
, and some additional
element
r_{0}\in G
, we can find an element
x\in G
distinct from
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
such that some permutation of
x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
is a service for
r_{0},r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
.

Proof.

We give a constructive proof of this theorem by describing an algorithm that extends a given service consisting of the triples
(x_{1},y_{1},r_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},y_{m},r_{m})
in
G
of length at most
|G|-2
as in the theorem, so with
x_{j}+r_{j}=y_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. To this end, let
y_{-1},y_{0}
be two distinct elements outside
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
(which is possible by our assumption that
m\leq|G|-2
), define
x_{0}=y_{0}-r_{0}
, and set
c=x_{0}+y_{-1}
.
(Note that we may assume that
x_{0}\in\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
since otherwise we could extend the service with the new triple
(x_{0},y_{0},r_{0})
; however, we will not use this information below.)

Assume that after
t
steps of our algorithm (
t=0,1,\ldots
), we have found
t

(x_{1},y_{1},r_{1}),\ldots,(x_{t},y_{t},r_{t})
(after renumbering triples if necessary) from the given service

x_{j}+r_{j-1}=y_{j-2}

, and in addition,

, see Figure

. Note that the case

.

Remark 4.3
.

In the case where
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, we can say slightly more. Let us define a
special service
for a sequence of

nonzero
vectors
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{m}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
as a sequence of triples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\boldsymbol{r}_{i})
with

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
for which
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m}
are pairwise distinct.
(Note that Conjecture
2.5
involves a service notion similar to that of a special service.)
Consider again the algorithm in the proof of Theorem
4.2
, now starting with a special service of length
m
with
2m\leq 2^{k}-2
. For the newly constructed vector
\boldsymbol{x}
, again we have to distinguish various cases. In case 1,
\boldsymbol{x}
is not contained in
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\}
and we can extend our special service. In case 2,
\boldsymbol{x}
is contained in
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{t+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\}
; due to the symmetry between the
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
for
i>t
, we may assume (after renumbering triples if necessary) that
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}
and we can extend the configuration. In case 3, we assume that
\boldsymbol{x}
is contained in
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\}
, and just as before, we show that this case cannot occur. Finally, in case 4, we assume that
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
for
-1\leq j\leq t
. Now if
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
, then from the known relations we find that
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}
, contradicting our assumptions. And if
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
with
-1\leq j\leq t-1
, then combining known relations we find that
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}+\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j+1}+\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
, so
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j+1}
, contradicting our assumptions again, unless
j=-1
. We conclude that the only way in which our extension algorithm for a special service can go wrong is where
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{y}_{-1}
,

\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, and
t\geq 1
(since
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}\neq 0
); it works
“almost”
, but not always. Unfortunately, we have not been able to use this property to find some other way to extend a special service.

Theorem
4.2
has an interesting consequence.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(G,+)
be an finite abelian group of size
n
and let
r=r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
be a sequence in
G
. Then there is a numbering
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
of the elements of
G
such that
x_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,x_{n}+r_{n}
form a permutation of the elements of
G
if and only if
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{n}=0
.

Proof.

If
(x_{i},r_{i},y_{i})
(
i=1,\ldots,n
) is a service for
r
, then the
x_{i}
and the
y_{i}
are both a permutation of
G
, so if
x_{i}+r_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i
, then
\sum r_{i}=\sum y_{i}-\sum x_{i}=0
. So the condition on the
r_{i}
is necessary. Conversely, suppose that
\sum r_{i}=0
.
Using Theorem
4.2
, we can construct a service
(x_{1},r_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n-1},r_{n-1},y_{n-1})
for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n-1}
.
Let
g
denote the sum of all the elements of
G
. If
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
are the elements in
G
that do not occur among
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,n-1
), respectively, then

x_{n}=g-\sum_{i\neq n}x_{i}
,
y_{n}=g-\sum_{i\neq n}y_{i}
and
r_{n}=-\sum_{i\neq n}r_{i}
, and since
x_{i}+r_{i}=y_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, we conclude that in addition
x_{n}+r_{n}=y_{n}
.

This theorem (with a slightly different proof, but employing essentially the same algorithm) was first stated in
[
5
]
.
We can also employ Theorem
4.2
to prove Theorem
3.3
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
:
Given a sequence
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}}
, we can use Theorem
4.2

for the group
(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k},+)
repeatedly to construct pairwise distinct vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
such that (after renumbering the
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
’s if necessary) the vectors
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{2^{k}-1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}-1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}-1}
are also pairwise distinct. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}}
denote the element such that
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}}\}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
. For every vector
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, the triples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}+\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{r}_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,2^{k}-1
again form a service for
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}-1}
, so by choosing
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}}
and replacing
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
by
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}+\boldsymbol{a}
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}}=\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}}
. Now define
\boldsymbol{y}_{2^{k}}=\boldsymbol{0}
and add the triple
(\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}},\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}})
as the last triple to complete the service to one for
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k}}
.

\Box

We have now proved Theorem
3.3
; in view of Theorem
3.4
, this implies Theorem
3.2
, Theorem
3.1
, and our main result Theorem
2.3
.

5
Relation with other additive problems in finite abelian groups

We have seen that Theorem
2.3
and its reformulation in Theorem
3.3
are closely related to Hall’s result in Theorem
4.4
for finite abelian groups via the notion of a service. In Remark
4.3
we introduced the notion of a special service in the group
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, which is
the kind of service required in Conjecture
2.5
. We extend this definition to general abelian groups in the obvious way.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(G,+)
be an finite abelian group.
A
special service
for a given sequence
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
in
G\setminus\{0\}
is a collection of pairwise distinct
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in G
such that the
m
elements
y_{1}=x_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,y_{m}=x_{m}+r_{m}
are pairwise distinct and also distinct from
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
.

In
[
6
]
, we stated our belief that every sequence
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
in
G\setminus\{0\}
with
2m\leq|G|-1
has a special service. We now know that this conjecture has to be adapted, and in fact from what we know now, the best possible conjecture would be the following.

Conjecture 5.2
.

Let
(G,+)
be a finite abelian group. There exists a special service for every sequence
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
in
G\setminus\{0\}

provided that all of the following conditions hold.

\begin{array}[]{ccll}2m&\leq&|G|-|G|/p,&\mbox{if $p$ is an odd prime dividing~$|G|$};\\
2m&\leq&|G|-4,&\mbox{if $G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}$ with $k\geq 3$};\\
2m&\leq&|G|-2,&\mbox{if $G$ has an even non-trivial proper subgroup};\\
2m&\leq&|G|,&\mbox{if $G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}$}.\end{array}

Each of these bounds comes from a certain
obstruction
caused by the presense of a certain type of subgroup of the group
G
. Currently, we know of the following obstructions.

1.

Let
H
be a subgroup of
G
of odd size, and let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\in H\setminus\{0\}
. If
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
is a special service for this sequence, then
x_{i}
and
y_{i}:=x_{i}+r_{i}
are in the same coset of
H
. But
H
, so also every coset of
H
, has odd size; as a consequence, every coset of
H
contains at least one element outside
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
. We conclude that
2m\leq|G|-|G|/|H|
. By letting
H
be the subgroup generated by an element of order
p
, for
p
a prime with
p\mid|G|
, the bound in Conjecture
5.2
follows.

2.

Let
H
be a proper subgroup of
G
, of even size. Let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m-1}\in H\setminus\{0\}
and let
r_{m}\notin H
, and suppose that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
is a special service of this sequence in
G
. Then
x_{i}
and
y_{i}:=x_{i}+y_{i}
are in the same coset of
H
for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
, but
x_{m}
and
y_{m}
are in distinct cosets of
H
. Since every coset of
H
is even, we conclude that there are at least two cosets of
H
that contain an element outside
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
, and hence
2m\leq|G|-2
.

3.

Let
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
with
k\geq 3
, and let
H
be the subgroup of
G
consisting of the even-weight vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
; note that
H\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k-1}
. Now let
m=2^{k-1}-1
, and let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
be the distinct nonzero vectors in
H
, and suppose that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
is a special service for this sequence. Since
2m=2^{k}-2
, there are exactly two elements, say
b,c
, not among
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},x_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,x_{m}+r_{m}
. By
[
8
, Theorem 1]
, the sum of the elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{s}
is 0 if
s=0
or
s\geq 2
. So
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}}x=0
and
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}=0
, hence

b+c=\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}}x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}+(x_{1}+r_{1})+\cdots+(x_{m}+r_{m})=0,

that is,
b=c
, contradicting our assumptions.

In view of the above, we introduce the following.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
(G,+)
be an finite abelian group. The
special service number

\sigma(G)
of
G
is defined as the largest integer with the property that every sequence
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
in
G\setminus\{0\}
with
2m\leq\sigma(G)
has a special service.

The earlier constructions of obstructions translate into the following properties of the special service number.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
(G,+)
be a finite abelian group.
Then

\sigma(G)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|G|-|G|/p,&\mbox{if $p$ is an odd prime dividing~$|G|$};\\
|G|-4,&\mbox{if $G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}$ with $k\geq 3$};\\
|G|-2,&\mbox{if $G$ has an even non-trivial proper subgroup};\\
|G|,&\mbox{if $G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}$}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 5.5
.

The fact that
\sigma(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k})
for
k=3
must be less than 6 was first observed by Flavio Salizzoni, at that time a PhD student from University of Neuchâtel, during a talk(!) that one of us gave in St. Gallen on November 16, 2022, but with a different reasoning specific to the case
k=3
.

To the best of our current knowledge, the examples discussed above are only possible obstructions to a service. For now, we conjecture that these are the only obstructions. In particular, we conjecture the following.

Conjecture 5.6
.

A sequence
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}-1}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
has a special service provided that the collection
\{\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}-1}\}
does not form the collection of non-zero even-weight vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
.

As far as we know, the above conjectures are new. The relevance of Conjecture
5.6
is given by the following.

Theorem 5.7
.

Conjecture
5.6
implies Conjecture
2.5
.

Proof.

Suppose that Conjecture
5.6
holds.
Let
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
as in the statement of Conjecture
2.5
. If necessary, renumber the sequence such that
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}-1}
satisfy the condition in Conjecture
5.6
.
According to Conjecture
5.6
, there are
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}-1}
such that
if
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,2^{k-1}-1
, then the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{2^{k-1}-1}
are pairwise distinct.
Now by replacing, if necessary,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
by
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
by
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}+\boldsymbol{a}
for all
i
, for a suitable
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
, we may assume without loss of generality that

\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
does not occur among the
2^{k}-2
vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{2^{k-1}-1}
.
Then set
\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k-1}}=\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{2^{k-1}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
It is now easily verified that the sequence
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{k}}
indeed satisfies the requirements in Conjecture
2.5
.

Remark 5.8
.

In
[
14
, Section VIII]
the authors state their believe that every request sequence
\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}_{2^{k-1}}
can be served by what they refer to as a
Hadamard solution
. This statement is equivalent to the claim that the simplex generator matrix
G_{k}
can serve every sequence of
2^{k-1}
non-zero vectors from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
with column subsets of size one or two, of which as most two subsets have size one. Inspection of the proof of Theorem
5.7
shows that the truth of Conjecture
5.2
for the group
(G,+)=(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k},+)
indeed also implies this stronger statement.

In the remainder of this section, we will discuss Conjecture
5.2
and its relation to other additive problems in abelian groups. Note that
the
statement in Conjecture
5.2
does not always hold when
2m=|G|
. Indeed, if
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and the sequence is given by
r_{1}=(0,1)
and
r_{2}=(1,0)
, then without loss of generality we can choose
x_{1}=(0,0)
, but now there is no valid choice for
x_{2}
. Note also that we have to require that every
r_{i}
is nonzero since otherwise
x
and
x+r_{i}
will be equal for every
x\in G
. For later reference, we note the following simple result.

Theorem 5.9
.

The statement in Conjecture
5.2
holds under the weaker condition that
4m\leq|G|+3
.

Proof.

Let
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots\in G\setminus\{0\}
, and suppose that for some integer
k\geq 1
, the sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}
is a special service for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k-1}
. To extend this to a special service for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
, we need to chose
x_{k}\in G
such that

x_{k}\notin x_{i}+\{0,r_{i},-r_{k},r_{i}-r_{k}\}
for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
, which is obviously possible if
4(k-1)<|G|
. We conclude that we can construct a special service for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
by repeated extension provided that
4(m-1)<|G|
, or, equivalently,
4m\leq|G|+3
.

Conjecture
5.2
seems somewhat similar in nature to Snevily’s conjecture and its generalizations. Here Snevily’s conjecture
[
10
]
, first proved in
[
3
]
, is the following.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
(G,+)
be a finite abelian group of odd order. For every
X\subseteq G
with
|X|=m
and for every set
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\}\subseteq G
, there is a numbering
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
of the elements of
X
such that the sums
x_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,x_{m}+r_{m}
are pairwise distinct.

First, Alon
[
2
]
(for the case
a=1
) and then Dasgupta
et al.

[
4
]
(for general
a
) proved the following generalization of Snevily’s conjecture for certain groups.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
p
be an odd prime, let
a
be a positive integer, and let
G
be
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{a}}
or
(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{a}
. Let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
,
m<p
, be a sequence of (not necessarily distinct) elements in
G
. Then for every
X\subseteq G
with
|X|=m
, there is a numbering
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
of the elements of
X
such that the sums
x_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,x_{m}+r_{m}
are pairwise distinct.

Alon also noted counterexamples to more general statements, notably the cases
G=\mathbb{Z}_{ms}
,
r_{1}=\ldots=r_{m-1}=0
,
r_{m}=s
and
X=\{0,s,\ldots,(m-1)s\}
and
G=\mathbb{Z}_{m}
,
X=G
, and
r_{1}=\ldots=r_{m-1}=0
,
r_{m}=1
.
The proof by Alon and Dasgupta
et al.
of Theorem
5.11
uses the following powerful algebraic result called the “Combinatorial Nullstellensatz”.

Theorem 5.12
(Alon
[
1
]
).

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field and let
f=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
be
a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
. Suppose that there is a monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{t_{i}}
of
f
such
that
\sum t_{i}
equals the total degree of
f
and whose coefficient in
f
is nonzero. Then, if

S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
are subsets of
\mathbb{F}
with
|S_{i}|>t_{i}
,
there are
s_{1}\in S_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\in S_{m}
such that
f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m})\neq 0
.

Here we mimic Alon’s proof to show that Conjecture
5.2
holds in the case where
G
is cyclic of prime order
p
. If
p=2
, the conjecture obviously holds, so we may assume that
p
is odd. Notre that in that case, the bound in the conjecture reduces to
2m\leq p-1
.

Theorem 5.13
.

Conjecture
5.2
holds in the case where
G=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
p
an odd
prime.

Proof.

Now let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
where
p
is an odd prime, and consider the polynomial
f
in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
defined by

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}-x_{j})(x_{i}-x_{j}+r_{i}-r_{j})\prod_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i,j=1}}{{i\neq j}}}^{m}(x_{i}-x_{j}-r_{j}),

(3)

where
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
. Note that we have
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\neq 0
precisely when
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
are pairwise distinct,
y_{1}=x_{1}+r_{1},\ldots,y_{m}=x_{m}+r_{m}
are pairwise distinct, and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
are distinct from
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
, where
x_{i}\neq y_{i}
since
r_{i}\neq 0
(
i=1,\ldots,m
), that is, when
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
is a special service for
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
in the group
(\mathbb{F},+)
. So it is sufficient to prove that
f
has a nonzero evaluation in
\mathbb{F}^{m}
.
We have that

D=\deg(f)=\deg\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{4}=2m(m-1).

(4)

Thus the monomial
(x_{1}\cdots x_{m})^{2(m-1)}
has degree
D
, and we can apply Theorem
5.12
with
t_{i}=2(m-1)
for all
i
.
Now by a special case of Dyson’s Conjecture, see
[
16
]
and the references therein, we know that the coefficient of
(x_{1}\cdots x_{m})^{2(m-1)}
in
f
, hence in
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{4}
, is equal to
(2m)!/2^{m}
.
Since
(2m)!/2^{m}=1\cdot 3\dotsm(2m-1)\cdot m!
, we easily see that
this coefficient is nonzero in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
precisely when

m=1
or
p\geq 2m+1
.
Taking
S_{i}=\mathbb{F}
, we have
p=|S_{i}|>t_{i}=2m-2
for all
i
, and hence by Theorem
5.12
,
f
indeed has a nonzero evaluation.

Unfortunately, in order to prove Conjecture
2.4
, we need the case
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}
of Conjecture
5.2
, which requires the use of the field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{k}}
; however the above proof clearly fails in characteristic 2.

Remark 5.14
.

In fact it is not difficult to determine exactly what can be said using the above proof technique in the field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{k}}

Indeed, by the famous Vandermonde identity

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}-x_{j})=\det(x_{i}^{m-j})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}(-1)^{\sigma(\pi)}x_{\pi(1)}^{m-1}x_{\pi(2)}^{m-2}\cdots x_{\pi(m-1)}^{1}

(see, e.g.,
[
16
]
), where
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
denotes the group of all permutations on
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
Noting that the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{k}}
has characteristic 2, we conclude from (
4
) that
the monomials of the polynomial
f
in (
3
) all have the same total degree
2m(m-1)
and are of the form

x_{\pi(1)}^{4(m-1)}x_{\pi(2)}^{4(m-2)}\cdots x_{\pi(m-1)}^{4}

for a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
.
Taking
t_{i}=4(m-i)
and
S_{i}=\mathbb{F}
(
i=1,\ldots,m
), we see from Theorem
5.12
that
f
has a nonzero evaluation over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{k}}
provided that
2^{k}\geq 4(m-1)+1
.
We conclude that this proof technique, when applied to the group
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, cannot improve on the trivial result in Theorem
5.9
.

Remark 5.15
.

Some progress towards Conjecture
5.2
can be made for groups of the form
(G,+)=(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{k}},+)
with
p
prime and
k\geq 1
integer,
by considering the coefficients of other monomials of the polynomial
f
in (
3
) over the field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q=p^{k}
. For example, considering
f
as a polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}
, for
m=3
, the monomial
x_{1}^{6}x_{2}^{5}x_{3}
has coefficient 8 in
f
,
and for
m=4
, the monomial
x_{1}^{8}x_{2}^{8}x_{3}^{7}x_{4}
has coefficient -72 in
f
.
As a consequence, for such groups Conjecture
5.2
holds for
m=3
if
p\geq 3
, and for
m=4
if
p\geq 5
.

6
Conclusions

We related various known and conjectured batch-type properties of the simplex codes to certain additive problems in finite abelian groups. By applying known methods for these more general problems we obtained a simple, constructive proof of a generalization of the theorem that the
k
-dimensional binary simplex code is a
2^{k-1}
-batch code. Recently, it was conjectured that the
k
-dimensional binary simplex code is even a
2^{k-1}
-functional batch code. Here we proposed a new conjecture for finite abelian groups, resembling Snevily’s conjecture (now a theorem), that includes a slightly stronger version of that simplex conjecture as a special case, and we proved this new conjecture for cyclic groups of prime order.
