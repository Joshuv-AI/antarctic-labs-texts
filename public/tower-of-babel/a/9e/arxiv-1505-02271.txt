From Conformal Group to Symmetries of Hypergeometric Type Equations
Jan DEREZIŃSKI † and Przemysław MAJEWSKI †‡

Abstract
We show that properties of hypergeometric type equations become transparent if they are derived from appropriate 2nd order partial differential equations with constant coefficients. In particular, we deduce the symmetries of the hypergeometric and Gegenbauer equation from conformal symmetries of the 4- and 3-dimensional Laplace equation. We also derive the symmetries of the confluent and Hermite equation from the so-called Schrödinger symmetries of the heat equation in 2 and 1 dimension. Finally, we also describe how properties of the F 1 0 {}_{0}F_{1} equation follow from the Helmholtz equation in 2 dimensions.

Introduction

The paper is devoted to the properties of the
hypergeometric equation

\displaystyle\big(z(1-z)\partial_{z}^{2}+(c-(a+b+1)z)\partial_{z}-ab\big)F(z)=0,

(1.1)

the
Gegenbauer equation

\displaystyle\big(\big(1-z^{2}\big)\partial_{z}^{2}-({a}+{b}+1)z\partial_{z}-{a}{b}\big)F(z)=0,

(1.2)

the
confluent equation

\displaystyle\big(z\partial_{z}^{2}+(c-z)\partial_{z}-a\big)F(z)=0,

(1.3)

the
Hermite equation

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{z}^{2}-2z\partial_{z}-2{a}\big)F(z)=0,

(1.4)

and the
{}_{0}F_{1}
equation
(closely related to the
Bessel equation
, see, e.g.,
[
6
]
)

\displaystyle\big(z\partial_{z}^{2}+c\partial_{z}-1\big)F(z)=0.

(1.5)

Here,
z
is a complex variable,
\partial_{z}
is the differentiation with respect to
z
, and
a
,
b
,
c
are arbitrary complex parameters.

Special functions that solve these equations are typical representatives of
hypergeometric type functions

[
34
]
. They often appear in applications
[
11
,
32
]
. In old times they were considered one of the central topics of mathematics, see, e.g.,
[
48
]
. In our opinion, they indeed belong to the most natural objects in mathematics.

Properties of hypergeometric type functions look quite complicated. According to our observations, when these properties are discussed, most people react with boredom and/or irritation. We would like to convince the reader that in reality identities related to hypergeometric type equations are beautiful and can be derived in an elegant and transparent way.

We will show that in order to understand hypergeometric type equations it is helpful to start from certain 2nd order PDE’s in several variables with constant coefficients. If we start from rather obvious properties of these PDE’s, reduce the number of variables and change the coordinates, we can observe how these properties become more complicated. At the end one obtains relatively complicated sets of identities for hypergeometric type equations.

1.1
Hypergeometric type operators

Equations (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) are determined by an operator of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(z,\partial_{z}):=\sigma(z)\partial_{z}^{2}+\tau(z)\partial_{z}+\eta.

In our paper we will concentrate on the study of the operator
\mathcal{C}
itself, rather than on individual solutions
F

of the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}F=0.

Note, however, that properties of
F
’s can be to a large extent inferred from the properties of
\mathcal{C}
itself.

According to the terminology used in
[
34
]
, and then in
[
6
,
7
]
, equations (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) belong to the class of
hypergeometric type equations
. This class is defined by demanding that
\sigma
is a polynomial of at most 2nd order,
\tau
is a polynomial of at most 1st order and
\eta
is a number. More precisely, (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) constitute standard forms of all nontrivial classes of hypergeometric type equations, as explained, e.g., in
[
6
]
.

Equations (
1.1
)–(
1.5
) depend on a number of (complex) parameters. For instance, in the case of the hypergeometric equation these parameters are
a
,
b
,
c
. We will prefer to use different sets of parameters introduced in a systematic way in
[
6
]
, which are more convenient to express the symmetries of these equations. In
[
6
]
these new parameters were called the
Lie-algebraic parameters
. Indeed, as we will describe, they are eigenvalues of the “Cartan algebra” of appropriate Lie algebras. For instance, for the hypergeometric equation the Lie-algebraic parameters, denoted
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\mu
, are the differences of the indices at the three singular points.

We will prefer not to use the operators
\mathcal{C}
directly. As explained in
[
6
]
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(z,\partial_{z})=\rho^{-1}(z)\partial_{z}\rho(z)\partial_{z}+\eta,

which defines (up to a multiplicative factor) a certain function
\rho(z)
called the
weight
. Following
[
6
]
, the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{bal}}(z,\partial_{z}):=\rho(z)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{C}(z,\partial_{z})\rho(z)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

will be called the
balanced form of
\mathcal{C}
. The study of the balanced form is obviously equivalent to that of
\mathcal{C}
, since both are related by a simple similarity transformation. The original operator
\mathcal{C}
will be sometimes called
the standard form of
\mathcal{C}
.

We will consider 3 classes of identities:

(1)

discrete symmetries,

(2)

transmutation relations,

(3)

factorizations.

Discrete symmetries
involve a transformation of the independent variable, together with a change of the parameters. The family of the discrete symmetries of the hypergeometric equation is especially famous. In the literature it is sometimes known under the name of the
Kummer’s table

[
1
,
21
,
22
,
25
]
.

The balanced form is especially convenient for a presentation of discrete symmetries, because some of them simply reduce to the change of sign of parameters.

Transmutation relations
say that
\mathcal{C}
multiplied from the right by an appropriate 1st order operator equals
\mathcal{C}
for shifted parameters
multiplied from the left by a similar 1st order operator. In quantum physics the corresponding 1st order operators are often called
creation/annihilation operators
.

Factorizations
say that
\mathcal{C}
can be written as a product of two 1st order operators, up to an additional term that does not contain the independent variable. It is easy to see that factorizations imply transmutation relations, as described, e.g., in
[
6
]
. Factorizations play an important role in quantum mechanics. They are often interpreted as the manifestation of
supersymmetry

[
5
]
. In quantum mechanics discussion of these factorizations has a long history going back at least to
[
14
]
.

Discrete symmetries, transmutation relations and factorizations are far from being trivial. Nevertheless, in our opinion they belong to the most
elementary
properties of hypergeometric type equations and functions. There exist many other properties, notably
addition formulas
and
integral representations
, which we view as more advanced. We do not consider them in our paper.

1.2
Group-theoretical derivation

We will see that all hypergeometric type equations can be obtained by separating the variables of 2nd order PDE’s with constant coefficients. We will always use the complex variable, to avoid discussing various signatures of these PDE’s.

Every such a PDE has a Lie algebra and a Lie group of generalized symmetries. In this Lie algebra we can fix a certain maximal commutative algebra, which we will call the “
Cartan algebra
”. Operators whose adjoint action is diagonal in the “Cartan algebra” will be called “
root operators
”. In the Lie group of generalized symmetries we will distinguish a discrete group, which we will call the
group of
“
Weyl symmetries
”. This group will implement automorphisms of the Lie algebra leaving invariant the “Cartan algebra”.

Note that in some cases the Lie algebra of generalized symmetries is semisimple, and then the names
Cartan algebra
,
root operators
and
Weyl symmetries
correspond to the standard names. In other cases the Lie algebra is non-semisimple, and then the names are less standard – this is the reason for the quotation marks that we use.

Parameters of hypergeometric type equation can be interpreted as the eigenvalues of elements of the “Cartan algebra”. In particular, the number of parameters of a given class of equations equals the dimension of the corresponding “Cartan algebra”. Each transmutation relation is related to a “root operator”. Finally, each discrete symmetry of a hypergeometric type operator corresponds to a “Weyl symmetry” of the Lie algebra.

We can distinguish 3 kinds of PDE’s of the complex variable with constant coefficients:

(1)

The
Laplace equation
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f=0,

whose Lie algebra of generalized symmetries is
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
.

(2)

The
heat equation
on
\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle\big(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}+\partial_{t}\big)f=0,

whose Lie algebra of generalized
symmetries is
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
, the so-called
(
complex
)
Schrödinger Lie algebra
.

(3)

The
Helmholtz equation
on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
,

\displaystyle\big(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n-1}}-1\big)f=0,

whose Lie algebra of symmetries is
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\rtimes\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n-1})
.

Let us describe these derivations in more detail.

•

We start from (

\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{6})

.

•

. Note that

.

•

One can repeat the above steps one dimension lower, reducing (
1.7
) to
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}+\partial_{t}
, the heat operator in 1 dimension, and then further to the

. Note that
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C})
is contained in
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{5})
.

•

To obtain the
{}_{0}F_{1}
operator
one needs to separate variables in the Helmholtz operator
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}-1

\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})

1.4
Comparison with literature

Properties of functions of hypergeometric type are described in numerous books, such as
[
1
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
13
,
23
,
26
,
35
,
38
,
48
]
. In particular, the properties presented in Section
2
(transmutation relations, discrete symmetries and factorizations) are known in one form or another. A similar presentation can be found in
[
6
]
. (Unlike in this paper, the presentation of
[
6
]
involves the standard form of hypergeometric type equations and not their balanced form).

Lie algebras associated with the Bessel and Hermite functions can be found in papers by Weisner
[
46
,
47
]
.

The idea of studying hypergeometric type equations with help of Lie algebras was developed further by Miller. His early book
[
27
]
considers mostly small Lie algebras/Lie groups, typically
\mathrm{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
/
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
and its contractions, and applies them to obtain various identities about hypergeometric type functions. These Lie algebras have 1-dimensional “Cartan algebras” and a single pair of roots. This kind of analysis is able to explain only a single pair of transmutation relations, whereas to explain bigger families of transmutation relations one needs larger Lie algebras.

A Lie algebra strictly larger than
\mathrm{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
is
\mathrm{so}(4,\mathbb{C})
. There exists a large literature on the relation of the hypergeometric equation with
\mathrm{so}(4,\mathbb{C})
and its real forms, see, e.g.,
[
16
]
. This Lie algebra is however still too small to account for all symmetries of the hypergeometric equation – its Cartan algebra is only 2-dimensional, whereas the equation has three parameters.

An explanation of symmetries of the Gegenbauer equation in terms of
\mathrm{so}(5)
and of the hypergeometric equation in terms of
\mathrm{so}(6)\simeq\mathrm{sl}(4)
was first given by Miller, see
[
30
]
, and especially
[
29
]
.

Miller and Kalnins wrote a series of papers where they studied the symmetry approach to separation of variables for various 2nd order partial differential
equations, such as the Laplace and wave equation, see, e.g.,
[
15
]
. A large part of this research is summed up in the book by Miller
[
31
]
. As an important consequence of this study, one obtains detailed information about symmetries of hypergeometric type equations.

The main tool that we use to describe properties of hypergeometric type functions is the theory of
generalized symmetries
of 2nd order linear PDE’s. This theory is described in another book by Miller
[
28
]
, and further developed in
[
31
]
.

The fact that conformal transformations of the Euclidean space are generalized symmetries of the Laplace equation was apparently known already to Kelvin.
Its explanation in terms of the null quadric first appeared in
[
3
]
. Null quadric as a tool to study conformal symmetries of the Laplace equation is the basic tool of
[
15
,
17
]
.

The conformal invariance of the Laplace equation generalizes to arbitrary pseudo-Riemannian manifolds. In fact, the Laplace–Beltrami operator plus an appropriate multiple of the scalar curvature, sometimes called the
Yamabe Laplacian
, is invariant in a generalized sense with respect to conformal maps. This can be found for instance in
[
37
,
41
]
.

The group of generalized symmetries of the heat equation was known already to Lie
[
24
]
. It was rediscovered (in the essentially equivalent context of the free Schrödinger equation) by Schrödinger
[
40
]
. It was then studied, e.g., in
[
12
,
33
]
.
Elementary notions from differential geometry used in our paper are well known. One of standard references in this subject is
[
18
,
19
]
.

A topic that is extensively treated in the literature on the relation of special functions to group theory, such as
[
27
,
36
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
, is derivation of various addition formulas. Addition formulas say that a certain special function can be written as a sum, often infinite, of some related functions. These identities can be typically interpreted in terms of a certain representation of an appropriate Lie group. These identities are very interesting, however we do not discuss them. The only elements of Lie groups that we consider are very special – they are the “Weyl symmetries”. They yield discrete symmetries of hypergeometric type equations, such as Kummer’s table. We leave out addition formulas, because their theory is considerably more complicated than what we consider in our paper.

The relationship of Kummer’s table with the group of symmetries of a cube (which is the Weyl group of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{6})
) was discussed in
[
25
]
. A recent paper, where symmetries of the hypergeometric equation play an important role is
[
20
]
. (We learned the term “transmutation relations” from this paper).

The use of transmutation relations as a tool to derive recurrence relations for hypergeometric type functions is well known and can be found, e.g., in the book by Nikiforov–Uvarov
[
34
]
, in the books by Miller
[
27
]
or in older works such as
[
42
,
46
,
47
]
.

There exists various generalizations of hypergeometric type functions. Let us mention the class of
\mathcal{A}
-hypergeometric functions, which provides a natural generalization of the usual hypergeometric function to many-variable situations
[
2
,
4
]
. Saito
[
39
]
considers generalized symmetries in the framework of
\mathcal{A}
-hypergeometric functions. Note, however, that the results of Saito are incomplete in the case of the classic hypergeometric equation. He admits this: “When
p=2
, the symmetry Lie algebra is much larger than
{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
”, and he quotes the paper by Miller
[
29
]
. Similarly, the (surprisingly large) Lie algebras of symmetries of the Gegenbauer and Hermite equations cannot be easily seen from a (seemingly very general) analysis of Saito.

There are a number of topics related to the hypergeometric type equation that we do not touch. Let us mention the question whether hypergeometric functions can be expressed in terms of algebraic functions. This topic, in the context of
\mathcal{A}
-hypergeometric functions was considered, e.g., in the interesting papers
[
2
,
4
]
.

In our paper we stick to a rather limited class of equations. We do not have the ambition to go for generalizations. This limited class has a surprisingly rich structure, which seems to be lost when we consider their generalizations.

Many, perhaps most identities and ideas described in our paper can be found in one form or another in the literature, especially in the works by Miller, also by Miller and Kalnins, as we discussed above. Nevertheless, we believe that our work raises important points that are not explicitly described in the literature. We argue that symmetry properties of all hypergeometric type equations become almost obvious if we add a certain number of variables obtaining the Laplace equation. We describe this idea in a unified framework, identifying the relationship of theory of hypergeometric type equations with such elements of group theory as roots, Cartan algebras and Weyl groups. These ideas are summed in Table 1, which to our knowledge has not appeared in the literature, except for the paper
[
6
]
written by one of us.

We use various (minor but helpful) ideas to make our presentation as short and transparent as possible: e.g., the
balanced form
of hypergeometric type equations,
Lie algebraic parameters
and
split coordinates
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In our derivations the symmetries are completely obvious at the starting point, then at each step they become more and more complicated.

The derivation of generalized symmetries of the Laplacian, given after Theorem
6.4
, is probably partly original. It leads to an interesting geometric object, which we call
\Delta^{\diamond}
. It satisfies identities (
6.18
) and (
6.19
), which seem quite important in the context of conformal invariance of the Laplace equation. These identities are elementary and quite simple, however we have never seen them in the literature. They can be used to derive factorizations of hypergeometric type equations, relating them to Casimir operators of certain distinguished subalgebras, another point that is probably original.

2
Hypergeometric type operators and their symmetries

In this section we describe the families of identities that we would like to interpret in a group-theoretical fashion in this article. As mentioned above, all of them involve the balanced form of the operators (
1.1
)–(
1.5
).

2.1
Hypergeometric operator

In the hypergeometric equation (
1.1
) we prefer to replace the parameters
a
,
b
,
c
with

\displaystyle\alpha:=c-1,\qquad\beta:=a+b-c,\qquad\mu:=b-a.

We obtain the
(
standard
)
hypergeometric operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu}(w,\partial_{w})=w(1-w)\partial_{w}^{2}+\big((1+\alpha)(1-w)-(1+\beta)w\big)\partial_{w}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu} (w, \partial_w) =}{}+\tfrac{1}{4}\mu^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}(\alpha+\beta+1)^{2}.

(2.1)

Instead of (
2.1
) we prefer to consider the
balanced hypergeometric operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu}^{\mathrm{bal}}(w,\partial_{w}):=w^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(1-w)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu}(w,\partial_{w}){(1-w)^{-\frac{\beta}{2}}w^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu}^\mathrm{bal}(w,\partial_w)}{}=\partial_{w}w(1-w)\partial_{w}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4w}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4(1-w)}+\frac{\mu^{2}-1}{4}.

(2.2)

Discrete symmetries.

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu}^{{\mathrm{bal}}}(w,\partial_{w})
does not change if we flip the signs of
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\mu
. Besides, the following operators coincide with
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu}^{{\mathrm{bal}}}(w,\partial_{w})
:

\displaystyle w=z\colon\qquad

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\beta,\mu}^{{\mathrm{bal}}}(z,\partial_{z}),

\displaystyle w=1-z\colon\qquad

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\beta,\alpha,\mu}^{{\mathrm{bal}}}(z,\partial_{z}),

3
Basic complex geometry

In this section we describe basic notation for complex geometry.
Throughout the section,
\Omega
,
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
are open subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
or, more generally, complex manifolds. We will write
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
for the multiplicative group
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

We will write
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
for the set of holomorphic functions on
\Omega
.
y=(y^{1},\dots,y^{n})
will denote generic coordinates on
\Omega
. We will write
\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)
for the set of nowhere vanishing holomorphic functions on
\Omega
.

3.1
Vector fields

Let
\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
denote the Lie algebra of holomorphic vector fields on
\Omega
. Every
A\in\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
can be identified with the differential operator

\displaystyle Af(y)=\sum_{i}A^{i}(y)\partial_{y^{i}}f(y),\qquad f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega),

where
A^{i}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

We will denote by
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
the Lie algebra of 1st order differential operators on
\Omega
with holomorphic coefficents. Such operators can be written as

\displaystyle(A+M)f(y):=\sum_{i}A^{i}(y)\partial_{y^{i}}f(y)+M(y)f(y),

where
A\in\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
and
M\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a Lie subalgebra of
\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
. A linear function
{\mathfrak{g}}\ni A\mapsto M_{A}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
satisfying

\displaystyle A_{1}M_{A_{2}}-A_{2}M_{A_{1}}=M_{[A_{1},A_{2}]}

will be called a
cocycle for
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Every cocycle together with
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
determines a homomorphism

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{g}}\ni A\mapsto A+\eta M_{A}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathrm{hol}(\Omega).

3.2
Point transformations

The set of biholomorphic maps
\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
will be denoted
\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})
. We set
\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega):=\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega,\Omega)
.

Let
{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})
. The
transport
of functions, vector fields, etc. by the map
\alpha
will be also denoted by
\alpha
. More precisely, for
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{1})
we define
{\alpha}f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{2})
by

\displaystyle({\alpha}f)(y):=f\big({\alpha}^{-1}(y)\big).

For
A\in\mathrm{hol}(\Omega_{1})
,
{\alpha}(A)\in\mathrm{hol}(\Omega_{2})
is defined as

\displaystyle{\alpha}(A):=\alpha A\alpha^{-1}.

If
m\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega_{2})
, then we have a map
m{\alpha}\colon\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{1})\to\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{2})
given by

\displaystyle(m{\alpha}f)(y):=m(y)f\big({\alpha}^{-1}(y)\big).

\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})
will denote the set of transformations
\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{1})\to\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{2})
of this form. Clearly,
\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega)
is a group.

Suppose that
G
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega)
. A family
G\ni{\alpha}\mapsto m_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)
satisfying

\displaystyle m_{{\alpha}_{2}}(y)m_{{\alpha}_{1}}\big({\alpha}_{2}^{-1}(y)\big)=m_{{\alpha}_{2}{\alpha}_{1}}(y),\qquad{\alpha}_{1},{\alpha}_{2}\in G,\qquad y\in\Omega,

will be called a
cocycle for
G
. Every cocycle together with
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}
determines a homomorphism

\displaystyle G\ni{\alpha}\mapsto m_{\alpha}^{\eta}{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega).

(3.1)

3.3
Local cocycles

Unfortunately, the above definition of a cocycle on a group is too rigid for our purposes. Below we introduce a weaker version of this concept, which we will be better adapted to our goals.

As before, we assume that
G
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega)
. Besides, we fix
\Omega_{0}
open in
\Omega
. For
\alpha\in G
we will write

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}^{\alpha}:=\Omega_{0}\cap\alpha(\Omega_{0}).

(3.2)

Furthermore, we suppose that to every
\alpha\in G
we associate
m_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}\big(\Omega_{0}^{\alpha}\big)
satisfying

\displaystyle m_{\alpha_{2}}(y)m_{\alpha_{1}}\big(\alpha_{2}^{-1}(y)\big)=m_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}}(y),\qquad\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in G,\qquad y\in\Omega_{0}\cap\alpha_{2}(\Omega_{0})\cap\alpha_{2}\circ\alpha_{1}(\Omega_{0}).

Then
G\mapsto m_{\alpha}
will be called a
local cocycle for
G
based on
\Omega_{0}
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega_{0})
. Then

\displaystyle m_{\alpha}(y):=\frac{p(y)}{p\big(\alpha^{-1}(y)\big)},\qquad y\in\Omega_{0}^{\alpha}

(3.3)

is a (trivial) example of a local cocycle based on
\Omega_{0}
. Note that if
p
cannot be extended to a holomorphic function on the whole
\Omega
, then (
3.3
) cannot be extended to a true cocycle.

Let
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}
. For any
\alpha\in G
we can define the map

\displaystyle m_{\alpha}^{\eta}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}\big(\Omega_{0}^{\alpha^{-1}},\Omega_{0}^{\alpha}\big).

(3.4)

For
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in G
and
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}
we have the following weak form of the chain rule:

\displaystyle\big(m_{\alpha_{2}}^{\eta}\alpha_{2}\circ m_{\alpha_{1}}^{\eta}\alpha_{1}\big)(y)=m_{\alpha_{2}{\circ}\alpha_{1}}^{\eta}\alpha_{2}{\circ}\alpha_{1}(y),\qquad y\in\Omega_{0}\cap\alpha_{2}(\Omega_{0})\cap\alpha_{2}{\circ}\alpha_{1}(\Omega_{0}).

It will be convenient have a special notation for such a collection of maps (
3.4
): We will write that

\displaystyle G\ni\alpha\mapsto m_{\alpha}^{\eta}{\alpha}\mathop{\in}_{\mathrm{loc}}\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega_{0})

is a
local representation of
G
.

3.4
Half-integer powers of a cocycle

For non-integer exponents the power function is unfortunately multivalued. Because of that, strictly speaking,
\eta\not\in\mathbb{Z}
should not be allowed in (
3.1
). However, we will be forced to consider situations when
\eta
is a half integer. This can be handled by the following formalism.

The non-identity element of the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
by
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\ni a\mapsto-a\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. This defines
\mathbb{C}^{\times}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, which is the space of pairs of non-zero complex numbers differing by a sign.

Let
\eta\in\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}
. Then for any
a\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, the power
a^{\eta}
can be interpreted as an element in
\mathbb{C}^{\times}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Let us restrict our attention to
\Omega
that are simply connected. We then define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}:=\big\{(f,-f)\colon f\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)\big\},

(3.5)

If
f\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)
, then
f^{\eta}
is well defined as an element of
\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Remark 3.1
.

If
\Omega
is not simply connected, then on the left hand side of (
3.5
) instead of
\Omega
we need to put the double cover of
\Omega
. Then
f^{\eta}
is still well defined. However we will not use this construction.

Let us go back to the setup of Section
3.2
. We can then define
m_{\alpha}^{\eta}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Therefore, (
3.1
)
can be interpreted as a group of transformations of
\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

A similar remark applies to Section
3.3
.

3.5
Generalized symmetries

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a linear differential operator on a complex manifold
\Omega
. Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega)
. We say that it is a
symmetry
of
\mathcal{C}
iff

\displaystyle\alpha\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}\alpha.

Let
m^{\sharp},m^{\flat}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)
. Define a pair of transformations in
\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega)
:

\displaystyle\alpha^{\sharp}:=m^{\sharp}\alpha,\qquad\alpha^{\flat}:=m^{\flat}\alpha.

We say that a pair
(\alpha^{\sharp},\alpha^{\flat})
is a
generalized symmetry
of
\mathcal{C}
if

\displaystyle\alpha^{\flat}\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}\alpha^{\sharp}.

Clearly, the kernel of
\mathcal{C}
is invariant wrt the action of
\alpha^{\sharp}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}f=0\qquad\text{implies}\quad\mathcal{C}\alpha^{\sharp}f=0.

Generalized symmetries of
\mathcal{C}
form a group.

Let
A\in\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
. We say that it is an
infinitesimal symmetry
of
\mathcal{C}
iff

\displaystyle A\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}A.

Let
M^{\sharp},M^{\flat}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
. One can also consider a pair of operators in
\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)

\displaystyle A^{\sharp}:=A+M^{\sharp},\qquad A^{\flat}:=A+M^{\flat}.

We say that a pair
(A^{\sharp},A^{\flat})
is a
generalized infinitesimal symmetry
of
\mathcal{C}
if

\displaystyle A^{\flat}\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}A^{\sharp}.

Clearly, the kernel of
\mathcal{C}
is invariant wrt the action of
A^{\sharp}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}f=0\qquad\text{implies}\quad\mathcal{C}A^{\sharp}f=0.

Infinitesimal generalized symmetries of
\mathcal{C}
form a Lie algebra.

4
Line bundles

4.1
Scaling

A holomorphic bundle
\pi\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{Y}
is called a
line bundle
if its fibers are modelled on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.
\mathcal{V}
is equipped with
scaling
, a homomorphism
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\ni s\mapsto\lambda_{s}\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{V})
preserving the fibers, that is, satisfying
\pi\lambda_{s}=\pi
. The vector field obtained by differentiating
\lambda_{s}
is called the
vertical vector field
and denoted
V
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\lambda_{s}\Big|_{s=1}=:V.

For
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we will often simply write
sv
instead of
\lambda_{s}v
. We will also write
s=\frac{\lambda_{s}(v)}{v}
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}_{0}\subset\mathcal{Y}
be open. A
section based on

\mathcal{Y}_{0}
is a holomorphic map
\gamma\colon\mathcal{Y}_{0}\to\mathcal{V}
such that
\pi\circ\gamma=\mathrm{id}
. Every section based on
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
determines a trivialization of
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{0}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}\ni(y,s)\mapsto s\gamma(y)\in\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}).

4.2
Vector fields on a line bundle

Let
\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V})
denote the Lie algebra of scaling invariant vector fields, that is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V}):=\big\{B\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{V})\colon\lambda_{s}B=B\lambda_{s},\;s\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\big\}.

Let
B\in\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V})
. Then
B
determines a unique element of
\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{Y})
, which will be denoted
B^{\diamond}
.

Let
\gamma
be a section based on
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
.
B^{\diamond}
can be transported by
\gamma
onto
\gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
. Thus we obtain two vector fields on
\gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
:
B\big|_{\gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{0})}
and
\gamma\big(B^{\diamond}\big)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
v\in\gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
,
B(v)-\gamma(B^{\diamond})(v)
is parallel to
V(v)
. Therefore, there exists
M_{B}^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
such that

\displaystyle B\big(\gamma(y)\big)=M_{B}^{\gamma}(y)V\big(\gamma(y)\big)+\gamma\big(B^{\diamond}\big)(y),\qquad y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}.

(4.1)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V})\ni B\mapsto M_{B}^{\gamma}

is a cocycle.
Hence, for any
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V})\ni B\mapsto B^{\gamma,\eta}:=B^{\diamond}+\eta M_{B}^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})

(4.2)

is a representation of the Lie algebra of scaling invariant vector fields.

Proof.

Every
B\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
can be written uniquely as

\displaystyle B=\tilde{M}_{B}^{\gamma}V+B^{\gamma},

(4.3)

where
\tilde{M}_{B}^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
and for any
s\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
the vector field
\mathcal{Y}_{0}\ni y\mapsto B^{\gamma}(s\gamma(y))
is tangent to the section

s\gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
. Assume now that
B\in\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
. This means
[V,B]=0
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\big(V\tilde{M}_{B}^{\gamma}\big)V+[V,B^{\gamma}]=0.

(4.4)

We also have

\displaystyle\gamma(B^{\diamond})(y)=B^{\gamma}\big(\gamma(y)\big),

(4.5)

and we set

\displaystyle M_{B}^{\gamma}(y):=\tilde{M}_{B}^{\gamma}\big(\gamma(y)\big),\qquad y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0}.

(4.6)

Restricting (
4.3
) to
\gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
, using (
4.5
) and setting (
4.6
), we obtain (
4.1
).

Now let
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
. Replacing
B
in (
4.3
) with
[B_{1},B_{2}]
, we can write

\displaystyle[B_{1},B_{2}]=\tilde{M}_{[B_{1},B_{2}]}^{\gamma}V+[B_{1},B_{2}]^{\gamma}.

(4.7)

If in addition
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
, then using (
4.4
) with
B
replaced with
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle[B_{1},B_{2}]=\big(B_{1}^{\gamma}\tilde{M}_{B_{2}}^{\gamma}-B_{2}^{\gamma}\tilde{M}_{B_{1}}^{\gamma}\big)V+[B_{1}^{\gamma},B_{2}^{\gamma}].

(4.8)

Comparing (
4.8
) with (
4.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle B_{1}^{\gamma}\tilde{M}_{B_{2}}^{\gamma}-B_{2}^{\gamma}\tilde{M}_{B_{1}}^{\gamma}=\tilde{M}_{[B_{1},B_{2}]}^{\gamma}.

(4.9)

Restricting (
4.9
) to the section
\gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
, using (
4.5
) and setting (
4.6
), we obtain the cocycle relation

\displaystyle B_{1}^{\diamond}M_{B_{2}}^{\gamma}-B_{2}^{\diamond}M_{B_{1}}^{\gamma}=M_{[B_{1},B_{2}]}^{\gamma}.

∎

Let

5
Complex Euclidean spaces

5.1
Linear transformations

Let us first consider the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
without the Euclidean structure.
The affine general linear Lie algebra
\mathbb{C}^{n}\rtimes\mathrm{gl}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
can be identified with the subalgebra of
\mathrm{hol}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
spanned by

\displaystyle\partial_{y^{j}},\quad j=1,\dots,n,\qquad y^{i}\partial_{y^{j}},\quad i,j=1,\dots,n.

Similarly, the affine general linear group
\mathbb{C}^{n}\rtimes\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

We will have a special notation for
the generator of dilations

\displaystyle D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y^{i}\partial_{y^{i}}.

Obviously,

\displaystyle D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}B=BD_{\mathbb{C}^{n}},\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{gl}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),

\displaystyle D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\alpha=\alpha D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}},\qquad

\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n}).

Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
be a permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

\displaystyle\sigma\big(y^{1},\dots,y^{n}\big):=\big(y^{\sigma_{1}^{-1}},\dots,y^{\sigma_{n}^{-1}}\big)

defines an element of
\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. On the level of point transformations it acts as

\displaystyle(\sigma f)\big(y^{1},\dots,y^{n}\big):=f\big(y^{\sigma_{1}},\dots,y^{\sigma_{n}}\big).

5.2
Bilinear scalar product

Suppose that

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}\ni y,x\mapsto\langle y|x\rangle=\sum_{i,j}g_{i,j}y^{i}x^{j}

is a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
called the
scalar product
. Clearly, if we know the
square
of each vector

\displaystyle\langle y|y\rangle=\sum_{i,j}g_{i,j}y^{i}y^{j},

we have the complete information about the scalar product.

[g^{ij}]
will denote the inverse of
[g_{ij}]
.
The
orthogonal Lie algebra of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, understood as a Lie subalgebra of
\mathrm{hol}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):=\big\{B\in\mathrm{gl}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\colon B\langle y|y\rangle=0\big\}.

For
i,j=1,\dots,n
, define

\displaystyle B_{i,j}:=\sum_{k}(g_{j,k}y_{i}\partial_{y_{k}}-g_{i,k}y_{k}\partial_{y_{j}}).

\{B_{i,j}\colon i<j\}
is a basis of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Clearly,
B_{i,j}=-B_{j,i}
, in particular
B_{i,i}=0
.

Likewise, recall that the
orthogonal
and the
special orthogonal group of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):=\big\{{\alpha}\in\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\colon\langle{\alpha}y|{\alpha}x\rangle=\langle y|x\rangle,\;y,x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{SO}(\mathbb{C}^{n}):=\big\{\alpha\in\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\colon\det\alpha=1\big\}.

We define

\displaystyle\text{the Laplacian}\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{i,j}\partial_{y^{i}}\partial_{y^{j}},\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\text{the Casimir operator}\quad\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{i,k}g^{j,l}B_{i,j}B_{k,l}.

Clearly,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}B=B\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}},\quad

\displaystyle B\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\rtimes\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\quad\qquad

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}B=B\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}},\quad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\alpha=\alpha\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}},\quad

\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\rtimes{\rm O}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\quad\qquad

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\alpha=\alpha\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}},\quad

\displaystyle\alpha\in{\rm O}(\mathbb{C}^{n}).

Note the identity

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\frac{1}{\langle y|y\rangle}\big(D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}^{2}+(n-2)D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\big).

(5.1)

We will denote by
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}(R)
the
(
complex
)
sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of squared radius
R
, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{n-1}(R):=\big\{y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\colon\langle y|y\rangle=R\big\}.

We also introduce the
null quadric

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{n-1}:=\mathcal{S}^{n-1}(0)\backslash\{0\}.

(5.2)

5.3
Split coordinates

The coordinates that we describe in this subsection are particularly convenient for the analysis of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Let
n=2m
if
n
is even and
n=2m+1
if
n
is odd. Set

\displaystyle I_{n}:=\begin{cases}\{-1,1,\dots,-m,m\},&\text{for even}\ n,\\
\{0,-1,1,\dots,-m,m\},&\text{for odd}\ n.\end{cases}

The coordinates in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
will be labelled by
I_{n}
, so that the square of
y=[y^{i}]_{i\in I_{n}}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle y|y\rangle=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n}}y_{i}y_{-i}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}2y_{-i}y_{i},&\text{for even}\ n,\\
\displaystyle y_{0}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}2y_{-i}y_{i}&\text{for odd}\ n.\end{cases}

Clearly,
g_{i,j}=g^{i,j}=\delta_{i,-j}
.

For
n=2m
,
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
has a basis consisting of

\displaystyle N_{i}:=B_{i-i}=y_{-i}\partial_{y_{-i}}-y_{i}\partial_{y_{i}},\qquad

\displaystyle j=1,\dots,m,

(5.3)

\displaystyle B_{ij}:=y_{-i}\partial_{y_{j}}-y_{-j}\partial_{y_{i}},\qquad

\displaystyle 1\leq|i|<|j|\leq m.

(5.4)

For
n=2m+1
we have to add

\displaystyle B_{0j}=y_{0}\partial_{y_{j}}-y_{-j}\partial_{y_{0}},\qquad|j|=1,\dots,m.

(5.5)

The subalgebra of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
spanned by (
5.3
) is its
Cartan algebra
. (
5.4
), and in the odd case also (
5.5
), are its
root operators
:

\displaystyle[N_{k},B_{i,j}]=(\mathrm{sgn}(i)\delta_{k,|i|}+\mathrm{sgn}(j)\delta_{k,|j|})B_{i,j},\qquad[N_{k},B_{0,j}]=\mathrm{sgn}(j)\delta_{k,|j|}B_{i,j}.

We have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\sum_{i\in I_{n}}\partial_{y_{i}}\partial_{y_{-i}},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in I_{n}}B_{i,j}B_{{-i,-j}}.

5.4
Weyl symmetries

In our applications of the group invariance we will restrict ourselves only to the so-called “Weyl symmetries”. It will be convenient to consider “Weyl symmetries” contained not only in
\mathrm{SO}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, but in the whole
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

Permutations of
I_{2m}
that preserve its decomposition into pairs
\{-1,1\},\dots,\{-m,m\}
correspond to a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(2m)
that will be denoted
\mathrm{W}(\mathbb{C}^{2m})
. It is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{m}\rtimes S_{m}
. It is generated by two kinds of transformations:
\tau_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,m
, which swap the elements of the
j
th pair, and permutations from
S_{m}
, which permute the pairs. If
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})\in S_{m}
, then

\displaystyle\sigma f(y_{-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{-m},y_{m}):=f(y_{-\sigma_{1}},y_{\sigma_{1}},\dots,y_{-\sigma_{m}},y_{\sigma_{m}}).

For
j=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle\tau_{j}f(y_{-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{-j},y_{j},\dots):=f(y_{-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{j},y_{-j},\dots).

We have

\displaystyle\sigma B_{i,j}\sigma^{-1}=B_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}},\qquad

\displaystyle\tau_{k}B_{i,j}\tau_{k}^{-1}=(-1)^{\delta_{|i|,k}+\delta_{|j|,k}}B_{i,j},

\displaystyle\sigma N_{j}\sigma^{-1}=N_{\sigma_{j}},\qquad

\displaystyle\tau_{k}N_{j}\tau_{k}^{-1}=(-1)^{\delta_{j,k}}N_{j}.

Using
\mathbb{C}^{2m+1}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2m}
, we embed
\mathrm{W}(\mathbb{C}^{2m})
in
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{2m+1})
. We also introduce a transformation
\tau_{0}\in\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{2m+1})
given by

\displaystyle\tau_{0}f(y_{0},y_{-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{-m},y_{m}):=f(-y_{0},y_{-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{-m},y_{m}).

Clearly,
\tau_{0}
commutes with
\mathrm{W}(\mathbb{C}^{2m})
. The group
\mathrm{W}(\mathbb{C}^{2m+1})
is defined as the group generated by
\mathrm{W}(\mathbb{C}^{2m})
and
\tau_{0}
, and is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}\rtimes S_{m}
. We have for
i,j=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle\tau_{0}B_{0,j}\tau_{0}^{-1}=-B_{0,j},\qquad\tau_{0}B_{i,j}\tau_{0}^{-1}=B_{i,j},\qquad\tau_{0}N_{j}\tau_{0}^{-1}=N_{j}.

In both even and odd cases
\mathrm{W}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
acts as a group of automorphisms
of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n})

leaving invariant the Cartan algebra. To compute the determinant of
elements of
\mathrm{W}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
it suffices to remember that

\det\sigma=1
for
\sigma\in S_{m}
and
\det\tau_{j}=-1
.

6
Conformal invariance

The main subject of this section is the description of generalized (infinitesimal) symmetries of the Laplace equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f=0.

(6.1)

We will see in particular that the Lie algebra of generalized symmetries is
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
. We will see that it is convenient to start the description of these symmetries from the space
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
, which we will call the
extended space
. The space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
will be embedded inside
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
as a section of the null quadric. We will see how the Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}
reduces to the Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
.

6.1
Conformal invariance of Riemannian manifolds

Suppose that a (complex) manifold
\Omega
is equipped with a nondegenerate holomorphic covariant 2-tensor field
g
, called the
(
complex
)
metric tensor
. We will say that
(\Omega,g)
is a
(
complex
)
Riemannian space
.

Thus if
A,B\in\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
, then we have a holomorphic function

\displaystyle\Omega\ni y\mapsto g(A,B)(y)=g_{i,j}(y)A^{i}(y)B^{j}(y)

called the
scalar product
.

Let
{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Hol}(\Omega)
. We can transport
g
by
{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle{\alpha}(g)(A,B):=g\big({\alpha}^{-1}(A),{\alpha}^{-1}(B)\big).

We say that
{\alpha}
is
conformal
if there exists
m_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle{\alpha}(g)=m_{\alpha}g.

Let
\mathrm{Cf}(\Omega)
denote the group of conformal maps on
(\Omega,g)
. One can check that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cf}(\Omega)\times\Omega\ni({\alpha},y)\mapsto m_{\alpha}(y)\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega)

is a cocycle.

Let
C\in\mathrm{hol}(\Omega)
. The
Lie derivative
of
g
in the direction of
C
is denoted
Cg
and defined by

\displaystyle(Cg)(A,B):=C(g(A,B))-g([C,A],B)-g(A,[C,B]).

We say that
C
is
infinitesimally conformal
if there exists
M_{C}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle Cg=M_{C}g.

Let
\mathrm{cf}(\Omega)
denote the Lie algebra of infinitesimally conformal fields. One can check that

\displaystyle\mathrm{cf}(\Omega)\times\Omega\ni(C,y)\mapsto M_{C}(y)\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)

is a cocycle.

We say that a manifold
\Omega
has a
conformal structure
, if it is covered by a family of open sets
\Omega_{i}
equipped with bilinear scalar products
g_{i}
such that on
\Omega_{i}\cap\Omega_{j}
we have

\displaystyle g_{i}=\rho_{i,j}g_{j}

for some
\rho_{i,j}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\Omega_{i}\cap\Omega_{j})
. Clearly, a Riemannian structure on
\Omega
is not necessary to define
\mathrm{Cf}(\Omega)
and
\mathrm{cf}(\Omega)
– we need only a conformal structure on
\Omega
.

6.2
Null quadric

Consider the extended space, that is, the complex Euclidean space
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
. The central role will be played by the representations

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B\in\mathrm{hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

(6.3)

and the symmetry

\displaystyle B\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}B,\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

(6.4)

\displaystyle{\alpha}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}{\alpha},\qquad

\displaystyle{\alpha}\in\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big).

(6.5)

As in (
5.2
), we introduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\colon z\neq 0,\;\langle z|z\rangle=0\big\}

called the

.

Multiplication by
s\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
preserves
\mathcal{V}
. Therefore, we can define the
projective quadric

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}:=\mathcal{V}/\mathbb{C}^{\times}=\big\{\mathbb{C}^{\times}z\colon z\in\mathcal{V}\big\}.

It is an
n
-dimensional complex manifold. Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{Y}
denote the natural projection. Clearly,
\mathcal{V}
is a complex line bundle over
\mathcal{Y}
. As in Section
4.1
, the multiplication by
s
will be often denoted by
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\ni s\mapsto\lambda_{s}\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{V})
and the corresponding vertical vector field by
V\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{V})
.

We can restrict (
6.2
) and (
6.3
) to
\mathcal{V}
and note that they are scaling invariant. Thus, we have natural embeddings

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B\in\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V}),

(6.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V}).

(6.7)

(Recall that
\mathrm{hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V})
, resp.
\mathrm{Hol}^{\mathbb{C}^{\times}}(\mathcal{V})
denote the scaling invariant holomorphic vector fields, resp. bijections). Therefore, (

) and (
6.7
) induce their actions on
\mathcal{Y}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B^{\diamond}\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{Y}),

(6.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}^{\diamond}\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{Y}).

(6.9)

6.3
Conformal invariance of projective quadric

Let
g
denote the restriction of the metric tensor on
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
to
\mathcal{V}
. Note that the null space of

is 1-dimensional and is spanned by the vertical field

\displaystyle g(V,A)=0,\qquad A\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{V}).

(6.10)

Moreover, the scaling scales the metric tensor:

\displaystyle\lambda_{s}(g)=s^{2}g,\qquad s\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},

(6.11)

\displaystyle Vg=2g.

(6.12)

Using (
6.10

6.11

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be open in
\mathcal{Y}
,
m\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\mathcal{U})
. Define
\lambda_{m}\in\mathrm{Hol}\big(\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{U})\big)
by

\displaystyle\lambda_{m}(z):=m(\pi(z))z,\qquad z\in\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{U}).

Then
\lambda_{m}g=m^{2}g
. In particular, for any section
\gamma
, the restriction
\lambda_{m}\colon\gamma(\mathcal{U})\to m\circ\pi\gamma(\mathcal{U})
is conformal.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be open in
\mathcal{Y}
. Let
\gamma
be a section over
\mathcal{U}
. The tensor
g
restricted to
\gamma(\mathcal{U})
is nondegenerate. We can transport it by
\gamma^{-1}
onto
\mathcal{U}
. This way we endow
\mathcal{U}
with a metric tensor.

For
i=1,2
, let
\mathcal{U}_{i}
be two open subsets of
\mathcal{Y}
equipped with sections
\gamma_{i}
. Let
g_{i}
be the corresponding complex Riemannian tensors. Obviously, there exists
\rho_{2,1}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{U}_{2})
such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(y)=\rho_{2,1}(y)\gamma_{1}(y),\qquad y\in\mathcal{U}_{1}\cap\mathcal{U}_{2}.

Therefore, by Proposition
6.1
,

\displaystyle g_{2}=\big(\gamma_{2}^{-1}\gamma_{1}\big)g_{1}=\rho_{2,1}^{2}g_{1}.

Cover
\mathcal{Y}
with open subsets
\mathcal{U}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, equipped with sections
\gamma_{i}
. Let
g_{i}
be the corresponding Riemannian tensors on
\mathcal{U}_{i}
. Then on
\mathcal{U}_{i}\cap\mathcal{U}_{j}
we have

\displaystyle g_{j}=\rho_{j,i}^{2}g_{i},\qquad\rho_{j,i}\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}(\mathcal{U}_{i}\cap\mathcal{U}_{j}).

This way we endow
\mathcal{Y}
with a conformal structure. It is easy to see that it does not depend on the choice of the covering and sections.

Proposition 6.2
.

(
6.8
) is infinitesimally conformal and (
6.9
) is conformal.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a section over
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{Y}
.

Let
B\in\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
. By Proposition
4.1
, there exists
M_{B}^{\gamma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{U})
such that

\displaystyle\gamma(B^{\diamond})=\big(B-M_{B}^{\gamma}V\big)\big|_{\gamma(\mathcal{U})}.

Now
Bg=0
and
Vg=2g
. Hence

\displaystyle\gamma(B^{\diamond})g=-2M_{B}^{\gamma}g.

Therefore,
\gamma(B^{\diamond})
is infinitesimally conformal on
\gamma(\mathcal{U})
. Hence
B^{\diamond}
is infinitesimally conformal on
\mathcal{U}
.

Let
{\alpha}\in\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
. Clearly,
\gamma_{\alpha}:={\alpha}\circ\gamma\circ({\alpha}^{\diamond})^{-1}
is a section based on
{\alpha}^{\diamond}(\mathcal{U})
. Therefore, there exists
m\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}\big({\alpha}^{\diamond}(\mathcal{U})\cap\mathcal{U}\big)
such that

\displaystyle\gamma(y)=m(y)\gamma_{\alpha}(y),\qquad y\in{\alpha}^{\diamond}(\mathcal{U})\cap\mathcal{U}.

(6.13)

Define now
\beta\colon\pi^{-1}\big(\mathcal{U}\cap({\alpha}^{\diamond})^{-1}(\mathcal{U})\big)\to\pi^{-1}\big({\alpha}^{\diamond}(\mathcal{U})\cap\mathcal{U}\big)

\displaystyle\beta(z):=m\big(\pi(z)\big)\alpha(z).

Substituting
z=\gamma(y)
and using (
6.13
) we obtain

\displaystyle\beta(\gamma(y))=m(y)\alpha\big(\gamma(y)\big)=\gamma(\alpha^{\diamond}(y)).

Thus
\beta
maps
\gamma\big(\mathcal{U}\cap({\alpha}^{\diamond})^{-1}(\mathcal{U})\big)
onto
\gamma\big({\alpha}^{\diamond}(\mathcal{U})\cap\mathcal{U}\big)
. Let
\beta^{\gamma}
denote this restriction of
\beta
. By Proposition
6.1
,
\beta^{\gamma}
is conformal. Clearly, it satisfies the identity

\displaystyle\beta^{\gamma}=\gamma\alpha^{\diamond}\gamma^{-1}.

Hence
\alpha^{\diamond}
is conformal.
∎

6.4
Conformal invariance of complex Euclidean space

Fix a vector
e\in\mathcal{V}
, and set

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{e}:=\{z\in\mathcal{V}\colon\langle z|e\rangle\neq 0\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{V}_{e}

\mathbb{C}^{\times}

\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{e}\ni\mathbb{C}^{\times}z\mapsto\frac{z}{\langle z|e\rangle}\in\mathcal{V}_{e}.

(6.14)

Next choose a vector
d\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\langle e|d\rangle=1
. Clearly,

is
n

(z_{i})_{i\in I_{n}}
in

. Each

\displaystyle z=\left[\begin{matrix}(z_{i})_{i\in I_{n}}\\
z_{-m-1}\\

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}\ni y\mapsto\left[\begin{matrix}y\\

6.5
Laplacian on bundle of homogeneous functions

Let
\mathcal{V}_{0}
be an open subset of
\mathcal{V}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
. We define
\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
to be the set of holomorphic functions on
\mathcal{V}_{0}

\eta
. (See Sections
4.4
and
4.5
for a discussion.)

Clearly,
B\in\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
preserves
\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{0})

the restriction of
B\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{V})
to
\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
.

Clearly,
{\alpha}\in\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})

\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
onto
\Lambda^{\eta}\big({\alpha}(\mathcal{V}_{0})\big)
. We will denote by

{\alpha}

\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{0})

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B^{\diamond,\eta},\qquad\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}^{\diamond,\eta}.

We find the following theorem curious because it allows in some situations to restrict a
second order
differential operator to a submanifold.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
be open. Let
K\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
be homogeneous of degree
\frac{2-n}{2}
such that

\displaystyle K\big|_{cV\cap\Omega}=0.

Then

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}K\big|_{\mathcal{V}\cap\Omega}=0.

Proof.

We will give two proofs. Each of the proofs will provide us with a formula, which will be useful later on.

Method I.
We use the decomposition
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}=\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
. As usual, we will denote by
\langle z|z\rangle
the square of
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
, by
D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}
the generator of dilations, by
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}
the Casimir operator and by
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}
the Laplacian on
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
. We will also need the corresponding objects on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
:
\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
,
D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
, and
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
. We will write

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle_{m+1}:=2z_{-m-1}z_{m+1},\qquad

\displaystyle\Delta_{m+1}:=2\partial_{z_{-m-1}}\partial_{z_{m+1}},

\displaystyle D_{m+1}:=z_{-m-1}\partial_{z_{-m-1}}+z_{m+1}\partial_{z_{m+1}},\qquad

\displaystyle N_{m+1}:=z_{-m-1}\partial_{z_{-m-1}}-z_{m+1}\partial_{z_{m+1}}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle=\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}+\langle z|z\rangle_{m+1},\qquad\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}+\Delta_{m+1},\qquad D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}+D_{m+1}.

The following identity is a consequence of (
5.1
):

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}+\big(\langle z|z\rangle-\langle z|z\rangle_{m+1}\big)\Delta_{m+1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}}{}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}+\Bigl(D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}-\frac{2-n}{2}\Bigr)^{2}-\left(\frac{2-n}{2}\right)^{2}+\langle z|z\rangle\Delta_{m+1}-D_{m+1}^{2}+N_{m+1}^{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}}{}=\langle z|z\rangle\Delta_{m+1}+\left(D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}-\frac{2-n}{2}-D_{m+1}\right)\left(D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}-\frac{2-n}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^n}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=}{}-\left(\frac{2-n}{2}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}+N_{m+1}^{2}.

(6.16)

\bigl(\frac{2-n}{2}\bigr)^{2}
is a scalar.
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
and
N_{m+1}^{2}
are polynomials in elements of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
.
\mathcal{V}
is tangent to
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
. Therefore, all operators in the last line of (
6.16
) can be restricted to
\mathcal{V}
. The operator
D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}-\frac{2-n}{2}
vanishes on functions in
\Lambda^{\frac{2-n}{2}}(\Omega)
. The operator
\langle z|z\rangle\Delta_{m+1}
is zero when restricted to
\mathcal{V}
.

Method II.
We write
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}=\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\oplus\mathbb{C}
with the distinguished variable denoted by
t
. We assume that the square of
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle=\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}+t^{2}.

We will use various operators on
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
:
D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}
, and
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}
. We have

\displaystyle D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}+t\partial_{t},\qquad\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}+\partial_{t}^{2}.

We have the following identity

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}+\langle z|z\rangle\partial_{t}^{2}-t^{2}\partial_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}}{}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}+\left(D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}+\frac{n-1}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)^{2}+\langle z|z\rangle\partial_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=}{}-\left(t\partial_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}}{}=\langle z|z\rangle\partial_{t}^{2}+\left(D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}+\frac{n}{2}-t\partial_{t}\right)\left(D_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}-\frac{2-n}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\langle z|z\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=}{}-\left(\frac{n-2}{2}\right)\frac{n}{2}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}.

(6.17)

Then we argue similarly as in Method I.
∎

6.4
we can restrict the Laplacian to functions in

6.6
Conformal invariance of Laplacian for a general section

The operator
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\diamond}
is quite abstract. In this subsection we describe how to make it more explicit.

Consider an open set
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
in
\mathcal{Y}
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{0}:=\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
and
\eta\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
. Choose a section
\gamma
of the line bundle
\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{Y}
based on
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
. As in (
4.14
) and (
4.13
), we can introduce
\psi^{\gamma,\eta}\colon\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{0})\to\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})
and its left inverse
\phi^{\gamma,\eta}
. We set

\displaystyle B^{\gamma,\eta}:=\psi^{\gamma,\eta}B^{\diamond,\eta}\phi^{\gamma,\eta}=\psi^{\gamma,\eta}B\phi^{\gamma,\eta},\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

\displaystyle\alpha^{\gamma,\eta}:=\psi^{\gamma,\eta}\alpha^{\diamond,\eta}\phi^{\gamma,\eta}=\psi^{\gamma,\eta}\alpha\phi^{\gamma,\eta},\qquad

\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big).

As explained in Propositions
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
, we obtain a representation and a local representation

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B^{\gamma,\eta}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathrm{hol}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}),

(6.20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}^{\gamma,\eta}\underset{\mathrm{loc}}{\in}\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{Y}_{0}).

(6.21)

We also define

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\gamma}:=\psi^{\gamma,\frac{-2-n}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\diamond}\phi^{\gamma,\frac{2-n}{2}}=\psi^{\gamma,\frac{-2-n}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}\phi^{\gamma,\frac{2-n}{2}}.

We have the identities

\displaystyle B^{\gamma,\frac{-2-n}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\gamma}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\gamma}B^{\gamma,\frac{2-n}{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

(6.22)

\displaystyle{\alpha}^{\gamma,\frac{-2-n}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\gamma}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\gamma}{\alpha}^{\gamma,\frac{2-n}{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle{\alpha}\in\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big).

(6.23)

Thus we have shown that (infinitesimal) conformal transformations of the
n
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
lead to generalized (infinitesimal) symmetries of
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\gamma}
. Even if (in a somewhat different form) this is a known fact, it seems that our derivation is new and of interest. In particular, it shows that a large class of second order
n
-dimensional operators together with their generalized symmetries directly come from the
(n+2)
-dimensional Laplacian with its true symmetries.

6.7
Conformal invariance of Laplacian on
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}^{n}}

Let us describe more closely the above construction in the case of the section (
6.14
). In this case, instead of
\gamma
we will write “
\mathrm{f\/l}
”, for
flat
. We identify of
\mathcal{Y}_{e}
with
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We can restrict (
6.8
) to an action of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
on
\mathcal{Y}_{e}
, and (
6.9
) to a local action of
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
on
\mathcal{Y}_{e}
. Using (
4.2
) and (
4.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B^{\mathrm{f\/l}}\in\mathrm{hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n}\big),\qquad\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}\underset{\mathrm{loc}}{\in}\mathrm{Hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n}\big).

We introduce
\psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\colon\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{e})\to\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and its left inverse
\phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}
. (
6.20
) and (
6.21
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\in\mathcal{A}\rtimes\mathrm{hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n}\big),\qquad\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\underset{\mathrm{loc}}{\in}\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n}\big).

The
(n+2)
-dimensional Laplacian reduced to the flat section is just the usual
n
-dimensional Laplacian:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}.

The symmetries (
6.22
) and (
6.23
) become the generalized symmetries of the usual Laplacian:

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\frac{-2-n}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\frac{2-n}{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

\displaystyle{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\frac{-2-n}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\frac{2-n}{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle{\alpha}\in\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big).

Thus
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
serves to describe in a simple way conformal symmetries of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. When used in this fashion, the space
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
will be sometimes called
the
extended space
.

Below we sum up information about conformal symmetries on the level of the extended space
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
and the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We will use the split coordinates, that is,
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
and
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
have the square

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle=\sum_{j\in I_{n+2}}z_{-j}z_{j},\qquad\langle y|y\rangle=\sum_{j\in I_{n}}y_{-j}y_{j}.

As a rule, if a given operator does not depend on
\eta
, we will omit
\eta
.

Cartan algebra of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
.

Cartan operators of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
i=1,\dots,m
:

\displaystyle N_{i}=z_{-i}\partial_{z_{-i}}-z_{i}\partial_{z_{i}},\qquad N_{i}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=y_{-i}\partial_{y_{-i}}-y_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}.

Generator of dilations:

\displaystyle N_{m+1}=z_{-m-1}\partial_{z_{-m-1}}-z_{m+1}\partial_{z_{m+1}},\qquad N_{m+1}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n}}y_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}-\eta=D_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}-\eta.

Root operators.
Roots of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
|i|<|j|
,
i,j\in I_{n}
:

\displaystyle B_{i,j}=z_{-i}\partial_{z_{j}}-z_{-j}\partial_{z_{i}},\qquad B_{i,j}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=y_{-i}\partial_{y_{j}}-y_{-j}\partial_{y_{i}}.

Generators of translations,
j\in I_{n}
:

\displaystyle B_{-m-1,j}=z_{m+1}\partial_{z_{j}}-z_{-j}\partial_{z_{-m-1}},\qquad B_{-m-1,j}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=\partial_{y_{j}}.

Generators of special conformal transformations,
j\in I_{n}
:

\displaystyle B_{m+1,j}=z_{-m-1}\partial_{z_{j}}-z_{-j}\partial_{z_{m+1}},\qquad B_{m+1,j}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=-\frac{1}{2}\langle y|y\rangle\partial_{y_{j}}+y_{-j}\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n}}y_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}-\eta y_{-j}.

Weyl symmetries.
We will write
K
for a function on
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
and
f
for a function on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Reflection:

\displaystyle\tau_{0}K(z_{0},\dots)=K(-z_{0},\dots),

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}f(y_{0},\dots)=f(-y_{0},\dots).

Flips,
j=1,\dots,m
:

\displaystyle\tau_{j}K(\dots,z_{-j},z_{j},\dots,z_{-m-1},z_{m+1})=K(\dots,z_{j},z_{-j},\dots,z_{-m-1},z_{m+1}),

\displaystyle\tau_{j}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}f(\dots,y_{-j},y_{j},\dots)=f(\dots,y_{j},y_{-j},\dots).

Inversion:

\displaystyle\tau_{m+1}K(\dots,z_{-m-1},z_{m+1})=K(\dots,z_{m+1},z_{-m-1}),

\displaystyle\tau_{m+1}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}f(y)=\left(-\frac{\langle y|y\rangle}{2}\right)^{\eta}f\left(-\frac{2y}{\langle y|y\rangle}\right).

Permutations,
\sigma\in S_{m}
:

\displaystyle\sigma K(\dots,z_{-j},z_{j},\dots,z_{-m-1},z_{m+1})=K(\dots,z_{-\sigma_{j}},z_{\sigma_{j}},\dots,z_{-m-1},z_{m+1}),

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{f\/l}}f(\dots,y_{-j},y_{j},\dots)=f(\dots,y_{-\sigma_{j}},y_{\sigma_{j}},\dots).

Special conformal transformations,
j=1,\dots,m
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{(j,m+1)}K(z_{-1},z_{1},\dots,z_{-j},z_{j},\dots,z_{-m-1},z_{m+1})

\displaystyle\qquad{}=K(z_{-1},z_{1},\dots,z_{-m-1},z_{m+1},\dots,z_{-j},z_{j}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{(j,m+1)}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}f(y_{-1},y_{1},\dots,y_{-j},y_{j},\dots)=y_{j}^{\eta}f\left(\frac{y_{-1}}{y_{j}},\frac{y_{1}}{y_{j}},\dots,-\frac{\langle y|y\rangle}{2y_{j}},\frac{1}{y_{j}},\dots\right).

Laplacian:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n{+}2}}=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n+2}}\partial_{z_{i}}\partial_{z_{-i}},\qquad\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n}}\partial_{y_{i}}\partial_{y_{-i}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}.

Computations.
Let us describe how to derive these formulas in an easy way. Consider
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
(defined by
z_{m+1}\neq 0
), which is an open dense subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{V}_{e}
is contained in
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

We will write
\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})
for the space of functions homogeneous of degree
\eta
on
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

Instead of using the maps
\phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}
and
\psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}
, as in (
4.14
) and (
4.13
), we will prefer
\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\colon\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})
and
\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\colon\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})\to\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
defined below.

For
K\in\Lambda^{\eta}\bigl(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}\bigr)
, we define
\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}K\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
by

\displaystyle\big(\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}K\big)(y)=K\left(y,-\frac{\langle y|y\rangle}{2},1\right),\qquad y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Let
f\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Then there exists a unique function in
\Lambda^{\eta}\bigl(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}\bigr)
that extends
f
and does not depend on
z_{-m-1}
. It is given by

\displaystyle\big(\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}f\big)(\dots,z_{m},z_{-m-1},z_{m+1}):=z_{m+1}^{\eta}f\left(\dots,\frac{z_{m}}{z_{m+1}}\right).

\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}
is a left inverse of
\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}
:

\displaystyle\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=\mathrm{id}.

Clearly,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}f\big|_{\mathcal{V}_{e}}=\phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}f,\qquad\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}K=\psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\big(K\big|_{\mathcal{V}_{e}}\big).

Moreover, functions in
\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})
restricted to
\mathcal{V}_{e}
are in
\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathcal{V}_{e})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}B\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta},\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

\displaystyle{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}{\alpha}\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta},\qquad

\displaystyle{\alpha}\in\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big).

Note also that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}.

In practice, the above idea can be implemented by the following change of coordinates on
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
:

\displaystyle y_{i}:=\frac{z_{i}}{z_{m+1}},\quad i\in I_{n},\qquad R:=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n+2}}z_{i}z_{-i},\qquad p:=z_{m+1}.

The inverse transformation is

\displaystyle z_{i}=py_{i},\quad i\in I_{n},\qquad z_{-m-1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{R}{p}-p\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n}}y_{i}y_{-i}\right),\qquad z_{m+1}=p.

The derivatives are equal to

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}}=z_{m+1}^{-1}\partial_{y_{i}}+2z_{-i}\partial_{R},\quad i\in I_{n},\qquad\partial_{z_{-m-1}}=2z_{m+1}\partial_{R},

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{m+1}}=\partial_{p}-z_{m+1}^{-2}\sum\limits_{i\in I_{n}}z_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}+2z_{-m-1}\partial_{R}.

Note that these coordinates are defined on
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.
\mathcal{V}_{e}
is given by the condition
R=0
. The section (
6.14
) (see also (
6.15
)) is given by
p=1
.

For a function
y\mapsto f(y)
we have

\displaystyle\big(\Phi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}f\big)(y,R,p)=p^{\eta}f(y).

For a function
(y,R,p)\mapsto K(y,R,p)
we have

\displaystyle\big(\Psi^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}K\big)(y)=K(y,0,1).

Note also that on
\Lambda^{\eta}(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{C}^{\times})
we have

\displaystyle p\partial_{p}=\eta.

6.8
Dimension
\boldsymbol{n=1}

Let us illustrate the constructions of this section by describing the projective quadric in the lowest dimensions, where everything is very explicit. We start with dimension
n=1
.

The 1-dimensional projective quadric is isomorphic to the Riemann sphere or, what is the same, the 1-dimensional projective complex space:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{1}\simeq\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}=P^{1}\mathbb{C}.

Indeed, consider
\mathbb{C}^{3}
with the scalar product

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle=z_{0}^{2}+2z_{-1}z_{+1}.

We can cover
\mathcal{Y}^{1}
with two maps:

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\ni s\mapsto\phi_{+}(s)=\left(s,1,-\frac{1}{2}s^{2}\right)\mathbb{C}^{\times}\in\mathcal{Y}^{1},\qquad\mathbb{C}\ni s\mapsto\phi_{-}(s)=\left(s,-\frac{1}{2}s^{2},1\right)\mathbb{C}^{\times}\in\mathcal{Y}^{1}.

The transition map is

\displaystyle\phi_{+}^{-1}\phi_{-}(s)=-\frac{2}{s}.

The Lie algebra
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
is spanned by

\displaystyle B_{0,1},\quad B_{0,-1},\quad N_{1},

with the commutation relations

\displaystyle[B_{0,1},B_{0,-1}]=N_{1},\qquad[N_{1},B_{0,1}]=B_{0,1},\qquad[N_{1},B_{0,-1}]=-B_{0,-1}.

The Casimir operator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=2B_{0,1}B_{0,-1}-N_{1}^{2}-N_{1}=2B_{0,-1}B_{0,1}-N_{1}^{2}+N_{1}.

(6.24)

6.9
Dimension
\boldsymbol{n=2}

The 2-dimensional projective quadric is isomorphic to the product of two Riemann spheres:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{2}\simeq P^{1}\mathbb{C}\times P^{1}\mathbb{C}.

Indeed, consider
\mathbb{C}^{4}
with the scalar product

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle=2z_{-1}z_{+1}+2z_{-2}z_{+2}.

We can cover
\mathcal{Y}^{2}
with four maps:

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\ni(t,s)\mapsto\phi_{+1}(t,s)=(-ts,1,t,s)\mathbb{C}^{\times}\in\mathcal{Y}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\ni(t,s)\mapsto\phi_{-1}(t,s)=(1,-ts,s,t)\mathbb{C}^{\times}\in\mathcal{Y}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\ni(t,s)\mapsto\phi_{+2}(t,s)=(-s,-t,1,ts)\mathbb{C}^{\times}\in\mathcal{Y}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\ni(t,s)\mapsto\phi_{-2}(t,s)=(-t,-s,-ts,1)\mathbb{C}^{\times}\in\mathcal{Y}^{2}.

Here are the transition maps:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phi_{-1}^{-1}\phi_{+1}(t,s)=\phi_{-2}^{-1}\phi_{+2}(t,s)=\big({-}t^{-1},-s^{-1}\big),\\
&\phi_{-2}^{-1}\phi_{-1}(t,s)=\phi_{+2}^{-1}\phi_{+1}(t,s)=\big({-}t^{-1},s\big),\\
&\phi_{-2}^{-1}\phi_{+1}(t,s)=\phi_{+2}^{-1}\phi_{-1}(t,s)=\big(t,-s^{-1}\big).\end{split}

The Lie algebra
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
is spanned by

\displaystyle N_{1},\quad N_{2},\quad B_{1,2},\quad B_{1,-2},\quad B_{-1,2},\quad B_{-1,-2}.

Its Casimir operator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=2B_{1,2}B_{-1,-2}+2B_{1,-2}B_{-1,2}-N_{1}^{2}-N_{2}^{2}+2N_{1}.

As is well known,
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
decomposes into the direct sum
\mathrm{so}_{+}(\mathbb{C}^{3})\oplus\mathrm{so}_{-}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
of two commuting Lie algebras isomorphic to
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
spanned by

\displaystyle B_{1,2},\quad B_{-1,-2},\quad N_{1}+N_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{1,-2},\quad B_{-1,2},\quad N_{1}-N_{2}

with the commutation relations

\displaystyle[B_{1,2},B_{-1,-2}]=N_{1}+N_{2},\qquad

\displaystyle[B_{1,-2},B_{-1,2}]=N_{1}-N_{2},

\displaystyle[N_{1}+N_{2},B_{1,2}]=2B_{1,2},\qquad

\displaystyle[N_{1}-N_{2},B_{1,-2}]=2B_{1,-2},

\displaystyle[N_{1}+N_{2},B_{-1,-2}]=-2B_{-1,-2},\qquad

\displaystyle[N_{1}-N_{2},B_{-1,2}]=-2B_{-1,2}.

The corresponding Casimir operators are

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{+}=2B_{1,2}B_{-1,-2}-\frac{1}{2}(N_{1}+N_{2})^{2}-N_{1}-N_{2}=2B_{-1,-2}B_{1,2}-\frac{1}{2}(N_{1}+N_{2})^{2}+N_{1}+N_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{-}=2B_{1,-2}B_{-1,2}-\frac{1}{2}(N_{1}-N_{2})^{2}-N_{1}+N_{2}=2B_{-1,2}B_{1,-2}-\frac{1}{2}(N_{1}-N_{2})^{2}+N_{1}-N_{2}.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{+}+\mathcal{C}_{-}.

In the enveloping algebra of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
the operators
\mathcal{C}_{+}
and
\mathcal{C}_{-}
are distinct. They satisfy
\alpha(\mathcal{C}_{-})=\mathcal{C}_{+}
for
\alpha\in\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{4})\backslash\mathrm{SO}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
, for instance for
\tau_{i}
,
i=1,2
.

However, inside the associative algebra of differential operators on
\mathbb{C}^{4}
we have the identity

\displaystyle B_{1,2}B_{-1,-2}-B_{1,-2}B_{-1,2}=N_{1}N_{2}+N_{2},

which implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{+}=\mathcal{C}_{-}

inside this algebra. Therefore, represented in the algebra of differential operators we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=4B_{1,2}B_{-1,-2}-(N_{1}+N_{2})^{2}-2N_{1}-2N_{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{C}}{}=4B_{-1,-2}B_{1,2}-(N_{1}+N_{2})^{2}+2N_{1}+2N_{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{C}}{}=4B_{1,-2}B_{-1,2}-(N_{1}-N_{2})^{2}-2N_{1}+2N_{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{C}}{}=4B_{-1,2}B_{1,-2}-(N_{1}-N_{2})^{2}+2N_{1}-2N_{2}.

(6.25)

7.2
Spherical section

In this subsection we consider the section of the quadric

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{5}:=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{6}\colon 2z_{-1}z_{1}+2z_{-2}z_{2}+2z_{-3}z_{3}=0\big\}

given by equations

\displaystyle 4=2\left(z_{-1}z_{1}+z_{-2}z_{2}\right)=-2z_{3}z_{-3}.

We will call it the
spherical section
, because it coincides with
\mathcal{S}^{3}(4)\times\mathcal{S}^{1}(-4)
. The superscript used for this section will be “
\mathrm{sph}
” for spherical.

We will see that this section is well suited to obtain the hypergeometric equation, both in the balanced and standard form, because its conformal factor is trivially equal to
1
.

As a preparation for a discussion of this section, let us choose the coordinates

\displaystyle r=\sqrt{2\left(z_{-1}z_{1}+z_{-2}z_{2}\right)},\qquad

\displaystyle p=\sqrt{2z_{3}z_{-3}},\qquad

\displaystyle w=\frac{z_{-1}z_{1}}{z_{-1}z_{1}+z_{-2}z_{2}},

\displaystyle u_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{z_{-1}}{z_{1}}},\qquad

\displaystyle u_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{z_{-2}}{z_{2}}},\qquad

\displaystyle u_{3}=\sqrt{\frac{z_{-3}}{z_{3}}}.

The null quadric in these coordinates is given by
r^{2}+p^{2}=0
. The generator of dilations is

\displaystyle D_{\mathbb{C}^{6}}=r\partial_{r}+p\partial_{p}.

The spherical section is given by the condition
r^{2}=4
.

Let us now describe in detail various objects in the spherical section.

\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{6})

Deriving balanced hypergeometric operator from

Introduce the following coordinates in

7.8

\boldsymbol{\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}}

Alternatively, we can slightly change the coordinates (

\displaystyle N_{1}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=\tilde{u}_{1}\partial_{\tilde{u}_{1}},\qquad N_{2}^{\mathrm{f\/l}}=\tilde{u}_{2}\partial_{\tilde{u}_{2}},\qquad N_{3}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=r\partial_{r}-\eta,

\displaystyle f(w,\tilde{u}_{1},\tilde{u}_{2},r)=\tilde{u}_{1}^{\alpha}\tilde{u}_{2}^{\beta}r^{\mu-1}F(w)

8
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{5})}
and the Gegenbauer equation

In this section we derive the Gegenbauer operator and its
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{5})
symmetries. The whole section is very similar to Section
7

\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{6})

We will consider the following levels:

(1)

extended space
\mathbb{C}^{5}
and the Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{5}}

(2)

reduction to the so-called spherical section and the corresponding Laplace–Beltrami operator,

(3)

(3)^{\prime}

Some of the aspects of the Gegenbauer equation are actually more complicated than the corresponding aspects of the hypergeometric equation. This is seen, in particular, when we consider factorizations of the Gegenbauer operator, which come in two separate varieties, unlike for the hypergeometric operator, which has a single variety of factorizations. This corresponds to the fact that
\mathrm{so}(6)
is simply-laced, whereas
\mathrm{so}(5)

8.2
Spherical section

We consider the section of the quadric

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{4}:=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{5}\colon z_{0}^{2}+2z_{-2}z_{2}+2z_{-3}z_{3}=0\big\}

\displaystyle 1=z_{0}^{2}+2z_{-2}z_{2}=-2z_{3}z_{-3}.

\mathrm{sph}

Introduce the following coordinates in

\displaystyle r=\sqrt{z_{0}^{2}+2z_{-2}z_{2}},\qquad p=\sqrt{2z_{3}z_{-3}},

\displaystyle w=\sqrt{\frac{z_{0}^{2}}{2z_{-2}z_{2}+z_{0}^{2}}},\qquad u_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{z_{-2}}{z_{2}}},\qquad u_{3}=\sqrt{\frac{z_{-3}}{z_{3}}}\;.\phantom{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}}

Similarly as in the previous section, the null quadric in these coordinates is given by
r^{2}+p^{2}=0

\displaystyle D_{\mathbb{C}^{5}}=r\partial_{r}+p\partial_{p}.

The spherical section is given by the condition

Below we describe various objects in the spherical section.

Lie algebra

Cartan operators:

\displaystyle N_{2}^{\mathrm{sph}}=u_{2}\partial_{u_{2}},\qquad N_{3}^{\mathrm{sph}}=u_{3}\partial_{u_{3}}.

\displaystyle\sigma_{23}^{\mathrm{sph},{\eta}}f(w,u_{2},u_{3})=\left(\mathrm{i}\sqrt{1-w^{2}}\right)^{\eta}f\left(\frac{w}{\sqrt{w^{2}-1}},u_{3},u_{2}\right).

Reflection and flips:

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{\mathrm{sph}}f(w,u_{2},u_{3})=f(-w,u_{2},u_{3}),

\displaystyle\tau_{2}^{\mathrm{sph}}f(w,u_{2},u_{3})=f(w,\frac{1}{u_{2}},u_{3}),\qquad\tau_{3}^{\mathrm{sph}}f(w,u_{2},u_{3})=f(w,u_{2},\frac{1}{u_{3}}).

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{5}}=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left((r\partial_{r})^{2}+(r\partial_{r})+\frac{r^{2}}{p^{2}}(p\partial_{p})^{2}+\partial_{w}(1-w^{2})\partial_{w}-\frac{(u_{2}\partial_{u_{1}})^{2}}{1-w^{2}}-\frac{r^{2}}{p^{2}}(u_{3}\partial_{u_{3}})^{2}\right).

\displaystyle(r\partial_{r})^{2}+r\partial_{r}+\frac{r^{2}}{p^{2}}(p\partial_{p})^{2}=\left(r\partial_{r}+\frac{r^{2}}{p^{2}}(p\partial_{p})+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(r\partial_{r}+p\partial_{p}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}

Deriving balanced Gegenbauer operator from

9
Symmetries of the heat equation – the Schrödinger algebra

The main subject of this section are generalized (infinitesimal) symmetries of the heat equation

\displaystyle\big(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}+\partial_{t}\big)f=0.

(9.1)

We will see in particular that the Lie group of generalized symmetries of (
9.1
) is
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
, the so-called
Schrödinger Lie algebra
.

We will reduce the heat equation (
9.1
) to the Laplace equation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(
6.1
), whose Lie algebra of generalized infinitesimal symmetries is, as we saw,
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
.
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
can be viewed as a subalgebra of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
.

Note that the choice of the dimension
n-2
in (
9.1
) makes our presentation of the heat equation consistent with that of the Laplace equation of Section
6
. It will be convenient to start again from the extended space
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
, where all symmetries greatly simplify.

9.1
The Schrödinger Lie algebra and group on
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}

We consider again the space
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
with the scalar product given by

\displaystyle\langle z|z\rangle=\sum_{i\in I_{n+2}}z_{-i}z_{i},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+2},

and the Laplacian

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}=\sum_{i\in I_{n+2}}\partial_{z_{-i}}\partial_{z_{i}}.

Recall that the Lie algebra
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
and the group
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
have natural representations on
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
(
6.2
) and (
6.3
) commuting with
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n+2}}
, see (
6.4
), (
6.5
). A special role will be played by the operator

\displaystyle B_{-m-1,m}=z_{m+1}\partial_{z_{m}}-z_{-m}\partial_{z_{-m-1}}\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big).

We define the
Schrödinger Lie algebra

\displaystyle\mathrm{sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big):=\big\{B\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\colon[B,B_{-m-1,m}]=0\big\}.

We also have the
full
and
special Schrödinger group

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big):=\big\{{\alpha}\in\mathrm{O}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big)\colon{\alpha}B_{-m-1,m}=B_{-m-1,m}\alpha\big\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{SSch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big):=\mathrm{Sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big)\cap\mathrm{SO}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big).

9.2
Structure of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})}

Let us describe the structure of
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
.

We will use our usual notation for elements of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
and
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
. In particular,

\displaystyle N_{m}=z_{-m}\partial_{z_{-m}}-z_{m}\partial_{z_{m}},\qquad N_{m+1}=z_{-m-1}\partial_{z_{-m-1}}-z_{m+1}\partial_{z_{m+1}}.

Define

\displaystyle N_{m,m+1}:=N_{m}+N_{m+1}.

Note that
N_{m,m+1}
belongs to
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
and commutes with
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
, which is naturally embedded in
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
.

\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
is spanned by the following operators:

(1)

B_{-m-1,m}
, which spans the center of
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
,

(2)

B_{m,j}
,
B_{-m-1,j}
,
j=1,\dots,m-1
, which have the following nonzero commutator:

\displaystyle[B_{m,j},B_{-m-1,-j}]=B_{-m-1,m},

(9.2)

(3)

B_{-m-1,-m}
,
B_{m+1,m}
,
N_{m,m+1}
, which have the usual commutation relations of
\mathrm{sl}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\simeq\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
:

\displaystyle[B_{m+1,m},B_{-m-1,-m}]=N_{m.m+1},\qquad[B_{\pm(m+1),\pm m},N_{m,m+1}]=\pm B_{\pm(m+1),\pm m},

(4)

B_{i,j}
,
|i|<|j|\leq m-1
,
N_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m-1,
with the usual commutation relations of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
.

The span of (2) can be identified with
\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-2}
, which has a natural structure of a symplectic space. The span of (1) and (2) is the central extension of the abelian algebra
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-2}
by (
9.2
). Such a Lie algebra is usually called the
Heisenberg Lie algebra over

\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-2}
and can be denoted by

\displaystyle\mathrm{heis}\big(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big)=\mathbb{C}\rtimes\big(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big).

\mathrm{sl}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
acts in the obvious way on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{n-2}
. Thus
\mathrm{sl}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-2}
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big)\simeq\mathbb{C}{\rtimes}\big(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big)\rtimes\big(\mathrm{sl}\big(\mathbb{C}^{2}\big)\oplus\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big)\big).

Note, in particular, that neither
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
nor
\mathrm{SSch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
are semisimple.

The subalgebra spanned by the usual Cartan algebra of
\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
,
N_{m,m+1}
and
B_{-m-1,m}
is a maximal commutative subalgebra of
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
. It will be called the
“Cartan algebra” of
\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
.

Let us introduce
\kappa\in\mathrm{SO}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})
:

\displaystyle\kappa(\dots,z_{-m},z_{m},z_{-m-1},z_{m+1}):=(\dots,z_{m+1},z_{-m-1},-z_{m},-z_{-m}).

Note that
\kappa^{4}=\mathrm{id}
and
\kappa\in\mathrm{SSch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
. On the level of functions

\displaystyle\kappa K(\dots,z_{-m},z_{m},z_{-m-1},z_{m+1}):=K(\dots,-z_{m+1},-z_{-m-1},z_{m},z_{-m}).

The subgroup of
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
generated by
W(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})\subset\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
and
\kappa
will be called the group of
Weyl symmetries of

\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
.

9.4
The Schrödinger Lie algebra and group on
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}}

We consider now the space
\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}
with the generic variables
(y,t)=(\dots,y_{m-1},t)
. Note that

should be understood as a new name for
y_{-m}
, and we keep the old names for the first

We define the map
\theta\colon\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
by setting for

\displaystyle(\theta h)(\dots,y_{m-1},y_{-m},y_{m}):=h(\dots,y_{m-1},y_{-m})\mathrm{e}^{y_{m}}.

We also define
\zeta\colon\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C})
, which to

\displaystyle(\zeta f)(\dots,y_{m-1},t):=f(\dots,y_{m-1},t,0).

Clearly,
\zeta
is a left inverse of
\theta

\displaystyle\zeta\circ\theta=\mathrm{id}.

Therefore,
\theta\circ\zeta=\mathrm{id}
is true on the range of
\theta

The heat operator in
n-2
spatial dimensions can be obtained from the Laplacian in
n

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}:=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n{-}2}}+2\partial_{t}=\zeta\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\theta.

(9.5)

For
B\in\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})\subset\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
we define

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}:=\zeta B^{{\mathrm{f\/l}},\eta}\theta,

and for
{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})\subset\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
,

\displaystyle{\alpha}^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}:=\zeta{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\theta.

Lemma 9.1
.

\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
,
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
and
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
preserve the range of
\theta
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle B_{-m-1,m}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=\partial_{y_{m}}.

Let
B\in\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
. Then
[B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta},\partial_{y_{m}}]=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}=C+D\partial_{y_{m}},

where
C\in\mathrm{hol}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C})
and
D\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C})
(they do not involve the variable
y_{m}
). Therefore,
B
preserves the range of
\theta
.

Likewise, if
{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
, then we have

\displaystyle{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}f(\dots,y_{-m},y_{m})=\beta f(\dots,y_{-m},y_{m}+d(\dots,y_{-m})),

where
\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{\times}\rtimes\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C})
,
d\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C})
(they do not involve the variable
y_{m}
). Therefore,
{\alpha}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}
preserves the range of
\theta
.

For
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}
the statement is contained in the formula (
9.5
).
∎

Theorem 9.2
.

(1)

For any
\eta
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big)\ni B\mapsto B^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}\in{\mathcal{A}}{\rtimes}\mathrm{hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}\big),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big)\ni{\alpha}\mapsto{\alpha}^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}\underset{\mathrm{loc}}{\in}\mathcal{A}^{\times}{\rtimes}\mathrm{Hol}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}\big)

is a representation/local representation.

(2)

We have a generalized symmetry

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{sch},\frac{-2-n}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}B^{\mathrm{sch},\frac{2-n}{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big),

\displaystyle{\alpha}^{\mathrm{sch},\frac{-2-n}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}{\alpha}^{\mathrm{sch},\frac{2-n}{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Sch}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\big).

Proof.

Let us first prove (1). Obviously, for
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})

\displaystyle\zeta\big[B_{1}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}B_{2}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\big]\theta=\zeta[B_{1},B_{2}]^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\theta.

If
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathrm{sch}(\mathbb{C}^{n-2})
, we can insert
\theta\circ\zeta
in the middle of the commutator. Hence

\displaystyle\big[B_{1}^{\mathrm{sch},\eta},B_{2}^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}\big]=[B_{1},B_{2}]^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}.

Now, for any
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{C}^{n+2})

\displaystyle\zeta{\alpha}_{1}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}{\alpha}_{2}^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\theta=\zeta({\alpha}_{1}{\alpha}_{2})^{\mathrm{f\/l},\eta}\theta.

For

in the middle of the composition, obtaining

\displaystyle{\alpha}_{1}^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}{\alpha}_{2}^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}=({\alpha}_{1}{\alpha}_{2})^{\mathrm{sch},\eta}.

To prove (2) we multiply (

) and (
9.4

\zeta

\displaystyle\zeta B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\frac{-2-n}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\theta=\zeta\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}B^{\mathrm{f\/l},\frac{2-n}{2}}\theta,\qquad

\displaystyle B\in\mathrm{so}\big(\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\big),

9.5
Hermite operator

Consider again the space
\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}
. This time its generic coordinates will be denoted
(w,s)
. We assume that the space
\mathbb{C}^{n-2}
is equipped with a scalar product. The following operator can be called the
(n-2)
-dimensional Hermite operator
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}:=\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}-D_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}+s\partial_{s}.

The heat operator is closely related to the Hermite operator. Indeed, let us change the coordinates from
(y,t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\times}
to
(w,s)\in\mathbb{C}^{n-2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{\times}
by

\displaystyle w=t^{-\frac{1}{2}}y,\qquad s=t^{\frac{1}{2}},

with the inverse transformation

\displaystyle y=ws,\qquad t=s^{2}.

Under this transformation the heat operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}
becomes
\frac{1}{s^{2}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}^{n-2}}
.

In Section
11
we will use this change of coordinates to obtain the (1-dimensional) Hermite operator. The construction is, however, interesting in higher dimensions as well, therefore we mention it here.

Strictly speaking, the above coordinate change does not work globally: in particular, we need to assume
s\neq 0
,
t\neq 0
, besides
s
doubly covers
t
. We usually are not absolutely precise about specifying the domains of coordinate changes – if needed, the reader can easily fill in such details.

11.8

\displaystyle\mathrm{heis}_{0}\big(\mathbb{C}^{2}\big)\qquad\text{spanned by}\quad B_{2,0},\ B_{-3,0},\ B_{-3,2}.

Perhaps, it would be sufficient to discuss only two levels of derivation – the 2-dimensional Helmholtz equation and the

8.1

\mathbb{C}^{5}

12.3
Space
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}^{2}}

We make an asatz

\displaystyle f(y_{0},y_{-},y_{+})=\mathrm{e}^{-y_{0}}h(y_{-},y_{+}).

In particular, the coordinates

Lie algebra

Cartan operator:

\displaystyle N=y_{-}\partial_{y_{-}}-y_{+}\partial_{y_{+}}.

Root operators:

\displaystyle B_{-}=\partial_{y_{-}},\qquad B_{+}=\partial_{y_{+}}.

Weyl symmetry.
Flip

\displaystyle\tau f(y_{-},y_{+})=f(y_{+},y_{-}).

Helmholtz operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}:=-1+2\partial_{y_{-}}\partial_{y_{+}}.

Symmetries:

\displaystyle N\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}N,\qquad B_{\pm}\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}B_{\pm},\qquad\tau\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\tau.

12.4
Balanced
\boldsymbol{{}_{0}F_{1}}
operator

We introduce the coordinates

\displaystyle w=\frac{y_{-}y_{+}}{2},\qquad u=\sqrt{\frac{y_{-}}{y_{+}}}.

(12.1)

Lie algebra
\mathbb{C}^{2}\rtimes\mathrm{so}(\mathbb{C}^{2})

Cartan operator:

\displaystyle N=u\partial_{u}.

Root operators:

\displaystyle B_{+}=u\frac{1}{\sqrt{2w}}\left(w\partial_{w}-\frac{N_{1}}{2}\right),\qquad B_{-}=\frac{1}{u}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2w}}\left(w\partial_{w}+\frac{N_{1}}{2}\right).

Weyl symmetry.
Flip:

\displaystyle\tau h(w,u)=h\left(w,\frac{1}{u}\right).

Helmholtz operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=\partial_{w}w\partial_{w}-\frac{N^{2}}{4w}-1.

Making an ansatz

\displaystyle h(w,u)=u^{\alpha}F(w),

we obtain the balanced
{}_{0}F_{1}
operator. Symmetries for the root operators and the flip coincide with the transmutation relation and the change of the sign of
\alpha

{}_{0}F_{1}

2.5

12.5
Standard
\boldsymbol{{}_{0}F_{1}}
operator

Modify the coordinates (
12.1

\displaystyle\tilde{u}:=y_{-}=u\sqrt{2w}.

We then have

\displaystyle N=\tilde{u}\partial_{\tilde{u}},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}=w\partial_{w}^{2}+(1+N)\partial_{w}-1.

Making an ansatz

\displaystyle h(w,u)=\tilde{u}^{\alpha}\tilde{F}(w),

we obtain the standard
{}_{0}F_{1}
