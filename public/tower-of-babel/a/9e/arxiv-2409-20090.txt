Chemotaxis-induced phase separation
Henrik Weyer, David Muramatsu, Erwin Frey

Abstract
Chemotaxis allows single cells to self-organize at the population level, as classically described by Keller–Segel models. We show that chemotactic aggregation can be understood using a generalized Maxwell construction based on the balance of density fluxes and reactive turnover. This formulation implies that aggregates generically undergo coarsening, which is interrupted and reversed by cell growth and death. Together, both stable and spatiotemporally dynamic aggregates emerge. Our theory mechanistically links chemotactic self-organization to phase separation and reaction–diffusion patterns.



Chemotaxis is a prevalent strategy in biology that enables both microbes and single (tissue) cells to move collectively and directionally in response to chemical gradients
Berg 2004
;
Murray 2003
;
Gerard and Rollins 2001
;
Roussos et al. 2011
;
Trepat et al. 2012
.
This can lead to self-organized aggregation when cells respond to gradients of self-secreted signaling molecules.
The basic model describing chemotactic aggregation was introduced by Keller and Segel
Keller and Segel 1970
.
Diverse model variants account for different chemotactic responses, crowding effects, and cell proliferation
Hillen and Painter 2009
.
Without cell proliferation and death the chemotactic aggregates are observed to undergo a coarsening process
Hillen and Painter 2009
;
Kavanagh 2014
;
Rapp and Zimmermann 2019
;
Kang et al. 2007
.
In contrast, cell growth and death induce a fixed pattern wavelength leading to intricate patterning
Mimura and Tsujikawa 1996
;
Hillen and Painter 2009
, and even spatiotemporal chaos
Painter and Hillen 2011
;
Ei et al. 2014
.
However, a unifying understanding of chemotactic aggregates and their long-time dynamics in general Keller–Segel models is missing.
It remains unknown whether a generic mechanism links coarsening, the selection of fixed intrinsic wavelengths, and the spatiotemporally dynamic patterns.

In this letter, we derive the generic behavior of chemotactic aggregates in Keller–Segel models by developing a combined description of the counteracting effects of random (diffusive) and chemotactic (directed) cell movement.
Stationary aggregates are thereby constructed in phase space based on a reactive area balance representing a generalized Maxwell construction.
We then argue that the resulting net cell movement drives the coarsening of collections of well-separated aggregates.
(Slow) cell growth and death interrupt and reverse the coarsening process, resulting in both fully nonlinear steady-state patterns with fixed wavelengths (aggregate sizes) and spatiotemporally dynamic aggregates.
Our theory is independent of the specific mathematical form of the models and relates the macroscopic dynamics of chemotactic aggregates to (active) phase separation
Glotzer et al. 1995
;
Zwicker et al. 2015
;
Li and Cates 2020
and (mass-conserving) reaction–diffusion systems
Brauns et al. 2021
;
Weyer et al. 2023
.

We consider a chemotactic population
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)
subject to diffusion, with diffusion coefficient
D_{\rho}
, and chemotaxis, modeled as advection along the gradients in the chemoattractant field
c(\mathbf{x},t)

Keller and Segel 1970
:

\partial_{t}\rho(\bm{x},t)=D_{\rho}\nabla^{2}\rho-T\nabla\big[\chi_{\rho}(\rho)\chi_{c}(c)\rho\nabla c\big]+\varepsilon s(\rho).

(1)

Here,
{T\chi_{\rho}(\rho)\chi_{c}(c)>0}
represents the strength of chemotaxis with the positive sensitivity functions
\chi_{\rho}
and
\chi_{c}
.
For example,
\chi_{\rho}
may describe crowding effects that suppress chemotaxis, while
\chi_{c}
may capture nonlinear kinetics between the chemoattractant and its receptor in the cells
Hillen and Painter 2009
.
Cell growth and death are described by
s(\rho)
with a dimensionless strength
\varepsilon
.
The dynamics of the chemoattractant, produced by the cells and subject to degradation, is given by
{\partial_{t}c(\bm{x},t)=D_{c}\nabla^{2}c+f(\rho,c)}
.
To ensure a net chemoattractant production by the cells, we assume
{\partial_{\rho}f>0}
.

Random cell motion and chemotaxis can be captured in one quantity by rewriting Eq. (
1
) as

\partial_{t}\rho(\bm{x},t)=T\nabla\big[\chi_{\rho}\rho\nabla\eta\big]+\varepsilon s(\rho)\,,

(2)

in terms of the
mass-redistribution potential
(cf. Refs.
Otsuji et al. 2007
;
Brauns et al. 2020
;
Cotton et al. 2022
)

\eta(\bm{x},t)=\frac{D_{\rho}}{T}\int\mathrm{d}\rho\,\frac{1}{\chi_{\rho}\rho}-\int\mathrm{d}c\,\chi_{c}\,.

(3)

Comparing the fluxes in Eq. (
2
) with the chemotaxis term in Eq. (
1
), it is evident that
\eta(\mathbf{x},t)
acts as an effective chemorepellent.
Random cell motion, appearing as a separate diffusion term in Eq. (
1
), is included in the population dynamics, Eq. (
2
), as one of two terms in the effective chemotaxis field
\eta
.
Thereby, diffusion is treated as a modification to the chemotactic response of the cells, and the effective field
\eta
integrates the counteracting effects of diffusion and chemoattraction.

Mathematically, the mass-redistribution potential
\eta
resembles the chemical potential in binary phase separation with the mobility
\chi_{\rho}\rho
[cf. Eq. (
2
) for
{\varepsilon=0}
with Model B dynamics]
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
;
Bray 2002
.
However, the mass-redistribution potential is in general not the derivative of a free energy functional but follows its own dynamic equation, as detailed in the Supplemental Material
sup
.
For particular model choices one can find a relation to a mathematical variational structure
Horstmann 2001
;
Wolansky 2002
.
For numerical analysis, we specifically consider the minimal Keller–Segel (mKS) model with
{\chi_{\rho}=\chi_{c}=1}
and a linear chemoattractant production and decay term
{f=\rho-c}

Childress and Percus 1981
.
Then, one has
{\eta=\frac{D_{\rho}}{T}\log\rho-c}
.
We start our analysis with the mass-conserving dynamics where
{\varepsilon=0}
.

Lateral instability. —

A uniform cell density is unstable if chemotactic drift dominates over diffusive spreading.
This requires the mass-redistribution potential
\eta
to decrease where
\rho
increases, such that gradients in
\eta
guide motion towards increased cell densities [Eq. (
2
); cf. orange arrows in Fig.
1
(a,b)].
For long-wavelength modulations, mass redistribution is slow compared to local reactions
f
causing
\eta
to quickly relax locally to its
quasi-steady-state
(QSS)
\eta^{*}(\rho)
, given by the
nullcline
(NC)
{f(\rho,c(\rho,\eta^{*}))=0}
[insets in Fig.
1
(a,b)].
Thus, chemotaxis-induced accumulation destabilizes uniform densities (lateral instability) if

\partial_{\rho}\eta^{*}(\rho)<0\,.

(4)

This instability is based on the self-amplifying redistribution of cell mass, and resemble the mass-redistribution instability found in mass-conserving reaction–diffusion systems as well as spinodal decomposition
Brauns et al. 2020
;
Weyer et al. 2023
.
Because this is a long-wavelength instability, the
nullcline-slope criterion
, Eq. (
4
), is exact beyond the QSS approximation; see Ref.
Weyer et al. tion
.

Stationary aggregate profiles. —

The stationary, one-dimensional pattern profile
[\tilde{\rho}(x),\tilde{c}(x)]
can be determined in the
(\rho,\eta)
-phase plane by generalizing local equilibria theory
Halatek and Frey 2018
;
Brauns et al. 2020
.
In stationary state, the diffusive and chemotactic fluxes must balance, i.e., the total flux
{\sim\partial_{x}\eta}
[cf. Eq. (
2
)] must vanish everywhere on a domain with no-flux or periodic boundary conditions.
Thus, stationary patterns are constrained to the
flux-balance subspace
(FBS)
{\tilde{\eta}(x)=\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}}
[Fig.
1
(c,d)].
Given that the densities
{\rho,c>0}
are bounded from below, the (well-separated) patterns are either mesa-shaped and composed of low- and high-density plateaus connected by interfaces [Fig.
1
(d)] or peak patterns.
For peaks, the maximum density does not saturate in a plateau [Fig.
1
(c)].
These patterns are constructed by noting that the profile curvature
\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{c}
vanishes (approximately) in plateau regions and at inflection points of the pattern.
The stationary chemoattractant equation implies that those regions correspond to FBS-NC intersections.
In particular, the low- and high-density FBS-NC intersections
\rho_{\pm}
of a
N
-shaped nullcline are approached by the minimum
\check{\rho}
and maximum
\hat{\rho}
pattern densities exponentially in the plateau widths [sharp-interface approximation discussed in Supplemental Material
sup
; Fig.
1
(d)].
Peak patterns occur if the third FBS-NC intersection
\rho_{+}
does not exist (
\mathsf{\Lambda}
-shaped nullcline) or lies at higher densities (asymmetric
N
-shaped nullcline) than the total domain mass
M
[blue-shaded in Fig.
1
(c,d)], fixed by the initial conditions, allows for.
For example, the mKS model has a
\mathsf{\Lambda}
-shaped nullcline allowing unconstrained cell-density growth while receptor-binding kinetics
Segel 1977
or volume-filling effects
Hillen and Painter 2001
;
Painter and Hillen 2002
lead to
N
-shaped nullclines, directly explaining their density saturation.
A precise, mathematical criterion for the transition from peak to mesa patterns is given in Ref.
Hillen 2007
.

The FBS position
\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}
was introduced previously as an integration constant
Schaaf 1985
.
Here, increasing
\eta
at fixed density
\rho
corresponds to decreasing the chemoattractant density
c
[Eq. (
3
)].
Therefore, chemoattractant is produced (
{f>0}
) above and degraded (
{f<0}
) below the nullcline in the
(\rho,\eta)
-phase plane (multistability of the reaction kinetics is discussed in the Supplemental Material
sup
).
Consequently, the stationary value
\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}
of a pattern adjusts such that the total production and degradation of the chemoattractant is balanced.
This balance of the total turnover is represented (qualitatively) by a balance of areas enclosed between FBS and NC analogously to a Maxwell construction [Fig.
1
(c,d); see Supplemental Material
sup
].
For example, in the mKS model, the total turnover balance reads
{0=\int_{\check{\rho}}^{\hat{\rho}}\mathrm{d}\rho\,[\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}-\eta^{*}(\rho)]/\rho}
.

,

,

, with system length

Coarsening due to mass competition. —

To understand the dynamics of patterns with several domains (peaks or mesas), we describe the interaction between two elementary patterns (two half-peak or two half-mesa patterns).
As illustrated for peaks in the following, the pattern domains can interact either via competition for mass or coalescence with neighbors (mirror image) [Fig.
2
(a,b); reflective boundaries].
Assuming a slow evolution of the pattern after each
single
elementary pattern relaxed to its (quasi-)stationary profile, the mass-redistribution potential at the single interfaces is given by its stationary value
\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}(M_{1,2})
(QSS approximation).
During competition [Fig.
2
(a)], the dependence of
\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}(M_{1,2})
on the peak masses
{M_{1,2}=\bar{M}\pm\delta M}
induces a gradient
\eta(x)
that redistributes mass between the peaks.
The QSS value
\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}
of the elementary pattern also depends on the plateau widths.
Thus, during coalescence [Fig.
2
(b)] the difference in the plateau widths induces a gradient
\eta(x)
within the peak that induces peak motion.

The mass and plateau-length dependence of
\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}(M)
arises because the reactive area balance depends on
\check{\rho},\hat{\rho}
, which vary with the peak mass and plateau lengths [Fig.
2
(a,b)].
Associating changes in the low-density plateau lengths
\delta l
with mass changes
{\delta M\approx\pm(\hat{\rho}-\check{\rho})\delta l}
, one describes both effects in terms of the mass dependence of
\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}
.
To separate the effects of the peak height and the low-density plateau length, we denote the mass-induced change of the mass-redistribution potential only due to the change in
\hat{\rho}
or
\check{\rho}
by
\partial_{M}^{\pm}\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}
, respectively (see Ref.
Weyer et al. tion
).
Importantly, because the reactive area balance does not depend on the specific mathematical model chosen, it generically implies
{\partial_{M}^{\pm}\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}<0}
for both peak and mesa patterns; assuming for peak patterns that their height
\hat{\rho}
grows with the mass
M
.
Thus, mass is generically redistributed to the elementary pattern with initially higher mass, inducing a
mass-competition instability
.
For peak competition [Fig.
2
(a)], this leads to the collapse of the smaller and growth of the larger peak.
For plateau competition [Fig.
2
(b)], the shorter plateau vanishes due to the coalescence of the peak with its neighbor (mirror image).
The growth rates of these two instabilities
{\sigma^{\pm}\sim-\partial_{M}^{\pm}\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}}
depend on the strength of the
\eta
gradient induced by the mass differences, i.e., the magnitude of
\partial_{M}^{\pm}\eta_{\mathrm{stat}}
(see Supplemental Material
sup
).
Both competition processes drive the coarsening of patterns with several peaks or mesas until only one aggregate remains.
Thus, uninterrupted coarsening is generic in mass-conserving Keller–Segel systems analogously to Ostwald ripening and coalescence in phase-separating systems and mass competition in reaction–diffusion systems
Bray 2002
;
Brauns et al. 2021
;
Weyer et al. 2023
.

In the mKS model, we find
{\sigma^{\pm}\sim\exp(-\Lambda/\gamma_{\pm})}
,
\sigma^{-}
agreeing with the mathematical result
Kang et al. 2007
.
Because one has
{\gamma_{-}\gtrless\gamma_{+}}
for
{\bar{\rho}\gtrless 2D_{\rho}/T}
[Fig.
2
(d), inset], coalescence or competition is faster, respectively, and dominates the coarsening process at large wavelengths, which is verified numerically [Fig.
2
(c)].
As
{[\sigma^{\pm}(\langle\Lambda\rangle)]^{-1}}
is the typical timescale on which peaks vanish via collapse or coalescence at the mean pattern wavelength
\langle\Lambda\rangle
, the time during coarsening scales as
{t^{-1}\sim\sigma^{\pm}(\langle\Lambda\rangle)}

Langer 1971
;
Glasner and Witelski 2003
;
Weyer et al. 2023
; see Supplemental Material
sup
.
The resulting coarsening law
{\langle\Lambda\rangle\sim\max(\gamma_{\pm})\log t}
describes numerical simulations well, with deviations at the transition from competition- to coalescence-dominated coarsening [Fig.
2
(d)].

Source terms interrupt coarsening. —

To account for cell growth and death, we now discuss the limit of weakly broken mass conservation (
{\varepsilon\ll 1}
).
Here, the average total density
\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{stat}}
of the stationary pattern is determined by the balance of overall production and degradation,
{\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}x\,s(\tilde{\rho})=0}
(source balance), not by the initial condition.
This condition requires
{0=s(\rho_{\mathrm{hss}})}
at a density
{\check{\rho}<\rho_{\mathrm{hss}}<\hat{\rho}}
.
Moreover, for stability of the elementary (half-mesa/-peak) pattern, the source term
s
must drive any deviations
\bar{\rho}-\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{stat}}
from the average total density that balances production and degradation back to zero.
We therefore assume net cell growth at low and net death at high densities as, for example, in logistic growth [cf. Fig.
3
(a)].
As mass competition amplifies mass differences, and hence deviations in
\bar{\rho}
, a source term as specified above counteracts the mass-competition instability.

Consider the coalescence mode of a peak pattern [Figs.
2
(b),
3
(a)], the dominant coarsening pathway at large
\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{stat}}
in the mKS model [Fig.
2
(d)].
If the peak shifts right by
\delta x
, the right plateau shortens, reducing the total production of cells by approximately
{-\varepsilon s(\rho_{-})\delta x}
while production increases by the same amount to the left of the peak, as follows from integration of Eq. (
2
).
Given that a peak shift
\delta x
requires the addition/subtraction of mass
(\hat{\rho}-\rho_{-})\delta x
to the right/left of the peak, the reduced production right and degradation to the left of the peak shifts the peak by
{(\hat{\rho}-\rho_{-})\partial_{t}^{\mathrm{source}}\delta x\approx-\varepsilon s(\rho_{-})\delta x}
.
To this source-induced shift process
{\partial_{t}^{\mathrm{source}}\delta x}
the competition process must be added.
Together, one obtains the total mass-competition rate (for
{\varepsilon\ll 1}
; cf. Refs.
Kolokolnikov et al. 2006
;
Brauns et al. 2021
;
Weyer et al. 2023
for the analogous effect in two-component reaction–diffusion systems)

\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-}(\Lambda)\approx\sigma_{\mathrm{D}}^{-}(\Lambda)-\varepsilon\frac{s(\rho_{-})}{\hat{\rho}(\Lambda)-\rho_{-}}.

(5)

Here,
\sigma_{\mathrm{D}}^{-}(\Lambda)
denotes the diffusion-limited part of the growth rate
\sigma^{-}
that is dominant at large peak distances and masses (see Supplemental Material
sup
).
Thus, the increased source-induced mass influx to the peak from the left compared to the right counteracts the mass transport through the peak caused by the gradients in
\eta
[Fig.
3
(a)].
Mass competition is suppressed if
{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-}(\Lambda)\leq 0}
.
Because the mass-competition rate
{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-}(\Lambda)}
typically decreases strongly in
\Lambda
(exponentially in the mKS model), the mass-competition instability is suppressed above a threshold wavelength
\Lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}
fulfilling
{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{stop}})=0}
[dotted line in Fig.
3
(c)].
Similar formulas hold for the competition mode and mesa patterns.

Plateau splitting at larger wavelengths. —

As the source strength or the peak distance is increased further, the source term will increasingly deform the stationary profile compared to the pattern of the mass-conserving system because the production or degradation bends the plateaus inwards toward
\rho_{\mathrm{hss}}
[Fig.
3
(b)].
When the maximum of the low-density plateau (or the minimum of a high-density plateau) enters the region of negative nullcline slope
{\partial_{\rho}\eta^{*}<0}
at wavelengths
{\Lambda\geq\Lambda_{\mathrm{split}}}
, the plateau loses stability, and a new peak or mesa is nucleated [Fig.
3
(b)]
Kolokolnikov et al. 2007
;
Kolokolnikov et al. 2014
;
Brauns et al. 2021
.
Thus, splitting and interrupted coarsening discussed above steer the pattern toward a stationary, periodic configuration with a wavelength
\Lambda
fulfilling
{\Lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}<\Lambda<\Lambda_{\mathrm{split}}}
[Fig.
3
(c)].

Successive coalescence and splitting. —

The scale separation
{\Lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}\ll\Lambda_{\mathrm{split}}}
can be lost if the source term (approximately) vanishes at a plateau density
\rho_{\pm}
, as in the mKS model with a logistic growth term
{s(\rho)=\rho(p-\rho)}
.
Far below the splitting threshold
\Lambda_{\mathrm{split}}
, coalescence is counteracted by production
{\varepsilon s(\rho_{-})\approx\varepsilon\rho_{-}}
in the low-density plateau
{\rho_{-}\approx 0}
[Eq. (
5
); dotted line in Fig.
3
(c)].
In contrast, near the splitting threshold, the plateau deformation inducing splitting is driven by a source term
{\sim\varepsilon\rho\gg\varepsilon\rho_{-}}
.
Effectively, the source term appears weak for interrupted coarsening but strong for splitting, causing both thresholds to converge already at small source strength
\varepsilon
.
Taking into account that the plateau density grows as the splitting threshold
\Lambda_{\mathrm{split}}
is approached (see Supplemental Material
sup
), the threshold of interrupted coarsening
\Lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}
bends downward [solid blue line in Fig.
3
(c)].
Nonetheless, the difference
\Lambda_{\mathrm{split}}-\Lambda_{\mathrm{stop}}
decreases strongly with
\varepsilon
, well describing the numerical results in the mKS model [Fig.
3
(c)].

As a result, at moderate source strength
\varepsilon
, several consecutive coalescence and splitting events occur before the stationary, periodic pattern develops [Fig.
3
(d) top].
At larger source strength
\varepsilon
, the domain size would have to be fine-tuned for stationary periodic patterns to exist.
Instead, the pattern continuously undergoes coalescence and splitting, reaching a dynamic steady state that has been argued to be chaotic [Fig.
3
(d) bottom]
Painter and Hillen 2011
.

Conclusions and outlook. —

Taken together, we provide a unified framework for understanding nonlinear patterns and their dynamics in chemotactic systems by introducing the mass-redistribution potential
\eta
.
The gradients of this potential drive the migration of the chemotactic species integrating the counteracting effects of random and chemotactic cell movement.
We have identified a geometric construction principle, akin to a Maxwell construction, that exploits flux-balance and reactive area balance.
Thereby, our construction has a clear connection to the physics of the underlying chemotaxis system.
As equilibrium-like pattern constructions were also obtained for different systems from the broad class of scalar active matter
Solon et al. 2018a
;
Solon et al. 2018b
;
Tjhung et al. 2018
;
Brauns et al. 2020
;
Fausti et al. 2021
;
Brauns et al. 2021
;
Omar et al. 2023
;
Langford and Omar 2023
;
Cates and Nardini 2023
;
Langford and Omar 2024
, it is an exciting question how these constructions can be used to classify the coarse-grained dynamics of scalar active matter more generally.

Keller–Segel-type drift terms also arise if cells modulate motility based on quorum sensing
Fu et al. 2012
;
Tailleur and Cates 2008
.
If instantaneous and (quasi) local this sensing leads to motility-induced phase separation.
Also cross-diffusion gives rise to similar drift terms and phase-separation-like behavior
Cotton et al. 2022
.
Our results show more broadly that the coarse-grained dynamics of chemotactic systems resembles the phenomenology of phase separation.

Providing a unified understanding of generalized Keller–Segel models, we expect that our theory will provide valuable insights into multi-species chemotactic systems
Wolansky 2002
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Muramatsu et al. tted
, the interplay with different aggregation effects like phase separation
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Demarchi et al. 2023
;
Goychuk et al. 2024
, and systems with more complex signaling
Ziepke et al. 2022
.
In particular, it remains an intriguing question whether traveling fronts observed in systems with chemoattractant consumption, rather than production, or nutrient uptake can be analyzed using a similar framework
Tindall et al. 2008
;
Adler 1966
;
Budrene and Berg 1995
;
Cremer et al. 2019
.
Moreover, understanding the effect of noise on wavelength selection and the spatiotemporally chaotic steady-state will be an exciting future challenge.
