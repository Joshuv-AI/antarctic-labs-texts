1.
Introduction

For a given real number
x
, the floor function
\lfloor x\rfloor
outputs the largest integer less than or equal to
x
, i.e.,

(1.1)

\lfloor x\rfloor=\textnormal{max}\left(k\in\mathbb{Z}\hskip 1.42271pt|\hskip 1.42271ptk\leq x\right).

The fractional part function
\{x\}
may then be defined in terms of (
1.1
) as

(1.2)

\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor.

These simple functions find a large number of applications in mathematics, computer science, and related fields. It is natural to consider the correlations between
\{nx\}
and
\{mx\}
, where
n
and
m
are fixed positive integers and
x
varies over the real line. These correlations are defined using the expected value, which itself is defined here as the integral

(1.3)

E_{X}\left(f(x)\right)=\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}f(x)dx,\hskip 7.11317ptX\in\mathbb{N}.

The expected value is thus evaluated over an integer-valued range. The correlations we primarily consider are then
E_{X}\left(\{nx\}\{mx\}\right)
. Numerical evidence shows that this correlation function has nontrivial, number theoretic structure. As an example, in Figure
1
we depict an approximation of
E_{100}\left(\{nx\}\{6x\}\right)
. There are several observations to note: 1. The correlation jumps if
n
is a multiple of
6
or vice versa. 2. The correlation is minimal at
n
such that
n
and
6
are coprime. 3. The correlation is higher for
n
such that
n
and
6
share a prime factor and further, this correlation is higher still for
n
such that
n
has a repeated prime factor that is shared with
6
. These properties are observed numerically for integers other than
6
as well. An explicit formula for
E_{X}\left(\{nx\}\{mx\}\right)
is currently unknown, but its number theoretic properties merit further study. To that end, this paper investigates connections between this correlation function and the well-known Möbius function.

.

).

The Möbius function
\mu(n)
, which is defined for
n\in\mathbb{N}
as

(1.4)

\mu(n)=\begin{cases}0;&\textnormal{$n$ has a repeated prime factor (not square-free)}\\
(-1)^{\omega(n)};&\textnormal{$n$ has $\omega(n)$ distinct prime factors (square-free)}\end{cases}

is a major function in number theory. It has the important property that its sum over the divisors of any
n>1
vanishes. That is,

(1.5)

\sum_{m|n}\mu(m)=0

for all
n>1
. This enables the well-known technique of Möbius inversion. A related and similarly important function is the summatory Möbius function, also called the Mertens function
M(n)
, defined as

(1.6)

M(n)=\sum_{m\leq n}\mu(m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\nmid n\\
m<n\end{subarray}}\mu(m).

The straightforward vanishing of the divisor sum (
1.5
) contrasts strongly with the behavior of the non-divisor sum (
1.6
), as the latter grows in average magnitude and fluctuates irregularly as
n
increases. The enigmatic behavior of
M(n)
has been linked to many important results in number theory. Landau
[
5
]
noted that the result

(1.7)

M(n)=o(n)

is equivalent to the prime number theorem (PNT) and Littlewood showed
[
6
]
that the stronger and unproven statement
M(n)=O\left(n^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)
for all
\epsilon>0
is equivalent to the famous Riemann hypothesis (RH).

In this paper we prove the following result:

Theorem 1
.

(1.8)

\sum_{n\neq m}\frac{\mu(n)\mu(m)}{nm}E_{X}\left(\{nx\}\{mx\}\right)=-\frac{9}{2\pi^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{X}\right)

Theorem
1.8
demonstrates a novel connection between the Möbius function
\mu(.)
and the correlations
E_{X}\left(\{nx\}\{mx\}\right)
. Numerical evidence for (
1.8
) is depicted in Figure
2
. We next present two lemmas and then present the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

2.
Lemmas

The first lemma is essentially an application of Möbius inversion and the PNT, which provides an important relationship between the sine function, the fractional part function, and the Möbius function.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2.1)

\sin(2\pi x)=-\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\left\{nx\right\}.

Proof.

We note the following Fourier series representation of
\left\{x\right\}
for
x\notin\mathbb{Z}

[
7
]
:

(2.2)

\left\{x\right\}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2\pi kx)}{k}

We then apply (
2.2
) to write

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2\pi knx)}{k}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2\pi rx)}{r}\sum_{n|r}\mu(n)=\sin(2\pi x),

where
r=nk
and the last step in (
2.3
) is due to (
1.5
). By (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) we then have

(2.4)

\sin(2\pi x)=\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\left\{nx\right\}\right)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}-\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\left\{nx\right\}.

We then make the important note, first observed by Landau
[
5
]
, that by the PNT

(2.5)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=0,

which, with (
2.4
), gives (
2.1
). We lastly note that (
2.1
) gives the correct result for
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and thus (
2.1
) holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

The next lemma describes the mean square of
\{nx\}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2.6)

E_{X}\left(\{nx\}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{3}.

Proof.

We begin by writing out the expected value as

(2.7)

\displaystyle E_{X}\left(\{nx\}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}n^{2}x^{2}dx-\frac{2}{X}\int_{0}^{X}nx\lfloor nx\rfloor dx+E_{X}\left(\lfloor nx\rfloor^{2}\right).

We next evaluate the second integral in (
2.7
). We make the substitution
u=nx
to give

\displaystyle\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}nx\lfloor nx\rfloor dx=\frac{1}{nX}\int_{0}^{nX}u\lfloor u\rfloor du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nX}\left(0\int_{0}^{1}udu+1\int_{1}^{2}udu+2\int_{2}^{3}udu+...+(nX-1)\int_{nX-1}^{nX}udu\right)

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nX}\sum_{k=1}^{nX-1}\frac{k\left((k+1)^{2}-k^{2}\right)}{2}=\frac{1}{nX}\sum_{k=1}^{nX-1}k^{2}+\frac{1}{nX}\sum_{k=1}^{nX-1}k.

We then apply the well-known Faulhaber formulae
[
4
]

(2.9)

\sum_{k=1}^{K}k=\frac{K(K+1)}{2}

and

(2.10)

\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}=\frac{K(K+1)(2K+1)}{6}

in (
2.8
)’s last line, simplifying to write

(2.11)

\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}nx\lfloor nx\rfloor dx=\frac{(nX-1)(2nX-1)}{6}+\frac{nX-1}{4}.

We note that the first integral in (
2.7
) is equal
n^{2}X^{2}/3
and we then apply (
2.11
) for the second integral in (
2.7
), simplify, and rearrange to give

(2.12)

E_{X}\left(\{nx\}^{2}\right)=\frac{n^{2}X^{2}}{3}-\frac{(nX-1)(2nX-1)}{3}-\frac{nX-1}{2}+E_{X}\left(\lfloor nx\rfloor^{2}\right).

We next apply the substitution
u=nx
and again apply (
2.10
) to write

\displaystyle E_{X}\left(\lfloor nx\rfloor^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{X}\int_{0}^{X}\lfloor nx\rfloor^{2}dx=\frac{1}{nX}\int_{0}^{nX}\lfloor u\rfloor^{2}du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nX}\left(0^{2}\int_{0}^{1}du+1^{2}\int_{1}^{2}du+2^{2}\int_{2}^{3}du...+(nX-1)^{2}\int_{nX-1}^{nX}du\right)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nX}\sum_{k=1}^{nX-1}k^{2}=\frac{(nX-1)(2nX-1)}{6}.

We apply (
2.13
) in (
2.12
) to give

(2.14)

E_{X}\left(\{nx\}^{2}\right)=\frac{n^{2}X^{2}}{3}-\frac{(nX-1)(2nX-1)}{6}-\frac{nX-1}{2}.

Cancelling terms then gives (
2.6
).
∎
