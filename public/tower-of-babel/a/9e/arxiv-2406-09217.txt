Convergence and error control of consistent PINNs for elliptic PDEs
Andrea Bonito, Ronald DeVore, Guergana Petrova, Jonathan W. Siegel

Abstract
We provide an a priori analysis of a certain class of numerical methods, commonly referred to as collocation methods , for solving elliptic boundary value problems. They begin with information in the form of point values of the right side f f of such equations and point values of the boundary function g g and utilize only this information to numerically approximate the solution u u of the Partial Differential Equation. For such a method to provide an approximation to u u with guaranteed error bounds, additional assumptions on f f and g g , called model class assumptions , are needed. We determine the best error (in the energy norm) of approximating u u , in terms of the total number of point samples, under all Besov class model assumptions for the right hand side and boundary data. We then turn to the study of numerical procedures and analyze whether a proposed numerical procedure (nearly) achieves the optimal recovery error. In particular, we analyze numerical methods which generate the numerical approximation to u u by minimizing specified data driven loss functions over a set Σ \Sigma which is either a finite dimensional linear space, or more generally, a finite dimensional manifold. We show that the success of such a procedure depends critically on choosing a data driven loss function that is consistent with the PDE and provides sharp error control. Based on this analysis a loss function ℒ ∗ {\cal L}^{*} is proposed. We also address the recent methods of Physics Informed Neural Networks. We prove that minimization of the new loss ℒ ∗ {\cal L}^{*} over restricted neural network spaces Σ \Sigma provides an optimal recovery of the solution u u , provided that the optimization problem can be numerically executed and Σ \Sigma has sufficient approximation capabilities. We also analyze variants of ℒ ∗ {\cal L}^{*} which are more practical for implementation. Finally, numerical examples illustrating the benefits of the proposed loss functions are given.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with numerical methods for solving elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs). Our primary goal is to provide a rigorous analysis of rates of convergence for collocation methods, including PINNs (Physics Informed Neural Networks), for solving such differential equations.

1.1
Numerical methods for solving (
1.1
)

We are interested in numerical methods for solving (
1.1
). The most prominent of these are the Finite Element
Methods (FEMs) and the adaptive variations of these (AFEMs). Over the last decades, a rigorous theory has been developed for FEMs and AFEMs which guarantees, a priori, the convergence of these numerical methods and even provides bounds on their rate of convergence under additional assumptions on
f
and
g
. These additional assumptions
stipulate extra smoothness of
f
and
g
than those assumed in
A2-A3
.

A numerical method assumes that
f,g
, and
\Omega
are known and suggests a procedure for solving (
1.1
) based on that information. The numerical procedure generates a function
\hat{u}
which is an approximation to
u
.
The efficiency of the numerical method is then evaluated in the following sense. One chooses a norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}

in which to measure performance and then seeks to quantify how fast the error
\|u-\hat{u}\|_{X}
between the true solution
u
and the output
\hat{u}
of the numerical procedure tends to zero in terms of the complexity of the numerical procedure. The classical norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
used to measure error is the energy norm which corresponds to choosing

X=H^{1}(\Omega)
. Other function norms sometimes used to measure error correspond to
X=L_{p}(\Omega)
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
. We restrict our analysis of convergence and rates of convergence to the case
X=H^{1}(\Omega)
in going further. We remark that our approach can in principle be applied to other error norms as well. In fact, the case of
X=H^{1/2}(\Omega)
has been considered in a similar way in
[
78
]
.

An important question is how one can compare the performance of different numerical methods for solving (
1.1
) in order to give a fair competition between all possible numerical methods. This is typically done by assigning some form of complexity to the numerical method. In the case of (A)FEMs, this complexity can be measured in terms of the number
n
of machine operations used to compute
\hat{u}_{n}=\hat{u}
, and there are theorems that
give a priori bounds for the error
\|u-\hat{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
under additional assumptions on the smoothness of
f
and
g
(see e.g.
[
6
]
).
Some alternatives to measuring the complexity
n
in terms of machine operations are the following. If the numerical approximation
\hat{u}
to
u
is an element from a linear
n
-dimensional space
V_{n}
, then
the associated complexity is typically assigned to be the dimension
n
.
This may not be directly converted to an appropriate number of machine operations because numerical stability becomes an issue.
If the approximation
\hat{u}
comes from a nonlinear manifold
{\cal M}_{n}
, then one can use the number of parameters
n
determining the manifold as a complexity measure. However, in the latter case, one has to impose extra conditions on the manifold (or the numerical procedure) to prevent the use of space filling manifolds and thus avoid unstable numerical methods, see
[
10
]
. In the absence of such restrictions, the numerical method may require an inordinate amount of computational resources to achieve a desired accuracy even in the case when the dimension
n
is small.

Another issue to consider is in what sense
f,g,\Omega
are known/given to us. One setting is to assume that we can query (ask questions about)
f,g,\Omega
, and receive the answer to such queries. In this setting, one has to decide which type of questions are allowed and whether these queries are answered free of charge or should the cost of asking/answering such queries be included in the numerical cost of the algorithm. For example, in FEMs, the data used are certain linear functionals (inner products with the basis functions of the
Galerkin space) which are then utilized in the FEM approximation.

In this paper, we are interested in collocation methods as described in the next section.
Accordingly, the linear functionals we
consider are point evaluations of
f
and
g
at points from
\overline{\Omega}
. In order for these to make sense, we assume that

f
and
g
are continuous functions, i.e.,
f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
,
g\in C(\partial\Omega)
.
The accuracy of how well
\hat{u}
approximates
u
will depend on two components. The first is the number
m
of point evaluations and their positioning. The optimal accuracy that any numerical method can achieve from these
m
pieces of information under the model class assumption on the function is called
the
optimal recovery rate
. A central question in this paper is to determine
this optimal recovery rate under various
model class assumptions, provided the
m
data sites are optimally positioned.
This is the subject of §
3
.

A second item in collocation methods is how one numerically uses the

m
pieces of
information
provided to create a numerical approximation to
u
.
If
\Sigma_{n}
is the underlying space used to create
\hat{u}
then
the accuracy of the numerical recovery will depend on
n
and the choice of
\Sigma_{n}
. In other words,
\hat{u}

can be viewed as
\hat{u}=\hat{u}_{n,m}
. If one fixes
m
, then one can study
how fast the accuracy of approximation
\|u-\hat{u}_{n,m}\|
tends to the optimal recovery rate as
n\to\infty
. This issue is discussed in §
9
. We remark that when neural network based methods such as PINNs are used to perform this numerical approximation, a critical component of the numerical algorithm becomes the optimization method used to train the neural network. This issue, which is specific to neural networks, is addressed in detail in §
10
.

In going further, we consider problem (
1.1
) under the following assumptions:

B1:

\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
,
d\geq 2
;

B2:

f\in C(\overline{\Omega})\subset H^{-1}(\Omega)
;

B3:

g\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\cap C(\partial\Omega)
;

B4:

a\equiv 1
.

Concerning the sense in which we know
\Omega
, one usually considers polyhedral domains whose vertices and edges are given to us. As already noted, for simplicity, we assume that
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
. However, with some additional technicality, we could equally well start with polyhedral domain. The standard way of handling more general domains
\Omega
is to first find a polyhedral domain
\widehat{\Omega}
that approximates
\Omega
, solve the corresponding PDE on
\widehat{\Omega}
, and then analyze the effect of the approximation of
\Omega
by
\widehat{\Omega}
.

1.2
Collocation methods and PINNs

Recently, there has been significant interest in using neural networks (NNs) as a nonlinear manifold to generate the approximation
\hat{u}
to the solution
u
of (
1.1
). Let
\Sigma_{n}
denote the set of outputs of a neural network with
n
parameters and fixed architecture. Once
\Sigma_{n}
is chosen, the numerical procedure queries
f
and
g
and then uses this information to numerically construct a function
\hat{u}\in\Sigma_{n}
which serves as an approximation to
u
in a specified norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
. The queries of
f
and
g
are taken as point evaluations
at specified points from
\overline{\Omega}
, thereby providing the values

{\bf f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{\widetilde{m}}),\quad f_{i}:=f({\bf x}_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,\widetilde{m};\quad{\bf g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{{\overline{m}}}),\qquad g_{i}:=g({\bf z}_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,\overline{m},

(1.7)

where
{\bf x}_{i}\in\overline{\Omega}
,
i=1,\dots,\widetilde{m}
, and
{\bf z}_{i}\in\partial\Omega
,
i=1,\dots,\overline{m}
, are the query sites. We refer to these points as
data sites
and denote them by

{\cal X}:=\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{\widetilde{m}}\},\quad{\cal Z}:=\{{\bf z}_{1},\dots,{\bf z}_{\overline{m}}\}.

The performance of such a numerical method will depend on the numbers
\widetilde{m},\overline{m}
, which we refer to as the
query budget
,
and also on the positioning of these points.

In PINNs, the numerical procedure to find
\hat{u}
is to search over
\Sigma_{n}
and find a
\hat{u}\in\Sigma_{n}
which
‘fits the data’. The most frequently used procedure (there are many variants) is to take
\hat{u}

as one of the elements of the set

\hat{u}\,\in\,\mathop{\rm argmin}_{S\in\Sigma_{n}}{\cal L}(S),

(1.8)

where

{\cal L}(S):=\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}[\Delta S({\bf x}_{i})+f({\bf x}_{i})]^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\left[\frac{1}{\overline{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}[S({\bf z}_{i})-g({\bf z}_{i})]^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

(1.9)

Note that this is equivalent to the square root of the original PINNs loss
[
59
]
. Later in the paper we will consider weighted versions of
{\cal L}
.
The appropriateness of the loss
{\cal L}
and how
\widetilde{m},\overline{m}
, and the data sites

({\cal X},{\cal Z})
should be chosen are important issues and are one of the focal points of this paper.
We remark that an analysis of other methodologies for solving (
1.1
) using neural networks, such as the deep Ritz method
[
73
]
and finite neuron method
[
74
]
, can be found in
[
51
,
49
,
66
,
56
,
28
,
26
]
.

The PINNs numerical procedures fall into the broad class of
collocation methods
for solving PDEs, i.e. numerical methods that use only values of
f
and
g
at specified data sites
({\cal X},{\cal Z})
. Such methods were studied in the last century (see e.g.
[
2
,
41
,
38
]
) but became less popular with the advent of FEMs. The novelty of PINNs is to use neural networks instead of polynomials or splines to build the approximation
\hat{u}
from the given data.

Some assumptions are necessary for collocation methods to make sense. Firstly, to ensure that point values of
f
and
g

make sense, we assume that
f
and
g
are continuous function on
\overline{\Omega}
and
\partial\Omega
, respectively. Secondly, to have any hope of proving a priori guarantees on convergence or rates of convergence
as
\widetilde{m},\overline{m},n\to\infty
, we need at a minimum that
f
comes from a compact subset
{\cal F}
of
C(\overline{\Omega})
and
g
comes from a compact subset

{\cal G}
of
C(\partial\Omega)
. This in turn guarantees that
u
is in a compact subset
{\cal U}
of
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

1.3
Overview of this paper

The idea of using neural networks to solve PDEs goes back to the last century
[
25
,
47
]
, and has recently become extremely popular with the introduction of PINNs
[
59
]
. Despite the increasing usage of PINNs for numerically solving a wide range of PDEs (see for example,
[
43
,
11
,
7
,
52
]
and the references therein),
a satisfactory analysis of the convergence and performance of these methods has not yet been given. We refer to
[
19
]
and the references therein for an overview of the existing approaches.
Some partial progress towards this goal has recently been made, see for instance
[
31
,
54
,
63
,
64
,
78
]
.

For example, in
[
63
]
, convergence is proved in the
C(\overline{\Omega})
-norm for a modified physics-informed neural network for elliptic PDEs under the assumption that the right hand side
f
and boundary data
g
are Hölder continuous. Under the additional assumption that the outputs of the neural network satisfy the boundary conditions exactly, convergence is obtained in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. In this situation, the boundary values are
not enforced
via the loss function and must instead be implemented through the neural network architecture. We remark that although methods for exactly enforcing boundary conditions with neural networks have been proposed in
[
47
,
48
,
4
]
, this approach is unable to rigorously handle arbitrary boundary conditions on general domains.

Further, in
[
64
]
and
[
78
]
, an abstract framework for analyzing methods based upon residual minimization, such as PINNs, is presented. This framework has been used in a variety of follow up works to analyze PINNs (see
[
3
,
68
,
27
]
for instance).

An investigation of the PINNs method for solving a variety of equations, obtaining upper bounds on the rate of convergence with respect to the number of collocation points, has been obtained in
[
54
]
. In
[
50
]
, a mini-max analysis for various neural network PDE formulations (with built in zero boundary conditions) is given for the Poisson equation
with zero boundary values
under the assumption that noisy values of the right hand side of the PDE are measured at random points.

Our work differs from the previous approaches in the following important ways:

•

we consider general Sobolev and Besov smoothness assumptions on the RHS and boundary data (instead of just the Hilbert space setting as is typical in prior work) and consider error in the energy norm
H^{1}(\Omega)
(in constrast to the weaker norms often considered in prior work); Besov and Sobolev regularity is the standard staple of formulating regularity theorems for PDEs;

•

we obtain matching upper and lower bounds on the theoretically optimal convergence rate in terms of the number of collocations points;

•

we show that a properly designed loss function can be used to obtain an a posteriori error estimate on the solution error for the elliptic PDE;

•

based upon our analysis, we propose new loss functions, including a loss function which correctly balances the boundary and domain error in the original PINNs formulation, and numerically demonstrate their improved performance on problems with (relatively) low regularity.

In particular, our analysis concentrates on the following questions:

Q1:
Given the model class assumptions on
f
and
g
, which query sites should be used and how large would we have to take
\widetilde{m}
and
\overline{m}
to
be able to reach a target accuracy
\varepsilon
for the error
\|u-\hat{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
?

Q2:
Given a budget of
m
queries and given model class assumption on
f
and
g
, what is the smallest error that can be achieved in the recovery of
u
?
This is called the
optimal recovery
accuracy.

Q3:
If we use a NN space
\Sigma_{n}
with
n
parameters to build the approximation
\hat{u}
to
u
, then how large do we need to choose
n
to achieve near optimal accuracy?

Q4:
If we use minimization of a data driven loss function to generate the numerical approximation
\hat{u}
, then what form should the loss function take so that small values of this loss function guarantee small values of the solution approximation error, up to the optimal recovery rate
?

We answer the above questions for all Besov space model classes and thereby establish such an a priori analysis of collocation methods and in particular PINNs. In contrast to previous works, we consider the error in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-norm, and treat the situation where the boundary values are enforced through the loss function.
We assume that

f\in{\cal F}
,
g\in{\cal G}
, where
{\cal F}
and
{\cal G}
are compact subsets of the space of continuous functions on the domains

\overline{\Omega}
and
\partial\Omega
, respectively. Such compact sets are typically described by smoothness
conditions. In this paper, we measure smoothness by membership in Besov classes. For this reason, we begin in §
2
with the definition of Besov spaces and the properties we will need in the sequel. We also
discuss there how well functions in Besov classes can be approximated (in various norms) by piecewise polynomials. All of the results in that section are known and are therefore presented in a summary form. However, for completeness of the paper, and for the specific presentation of these results, we give proofs of the results we need in the appendix.

We use membership in Besov spaces
B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
as the smoothness we impose on
f
and
g
.
Once such assumptions are placed on
f
and
g
, we answer questions

Q1-Q2
in §
3
.
Questions of this type are commonly referred to as
optimal recovery
(OR) of a function
u
from data. Since our data is given by point samples, this is also known as
optimal sampling
. Our general setting is not usually addressed in the OR literature because our data is not point evaluations of the target function
u
, but rather of
f
and
g
. Nevertheless, we consider the content of §
3
to be for the most part known in the sense that we are simply piecing together various known results and techniques such as those in

[
13
,
14
,
45
,
58
]
.
The paper
[
69
]
, which studies optimal sampling with respect to the negative Sobolev norm
H^{-1}(\Omega)
, comes closest to presenting the optimal recovery results in the form we need.
However, note that the cases
0<p,q<1
for all
d
, or
p=1
,

0<q\leq\infty
,
d=2
are not covered in
[
69
]
. Notably, we observe that the optimal sampling rates for Besov spaces in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
coincide with the optimal sampling rates in a certain space
L_{\gamma}(\Omega)
, determined by the Sobolev embedding (see Theorem
3.1
(iv)).

The results of §
3
do not give a numerical method for finding
\hat{u}
from the given data that provides the error bound
\|u-\hat{u}\|_{X}\leq\varepsilon
.
They simply establish the theoretical benchmark for the optimal accuracy of any numerical procedure for the recovery of
u
based on the point samples of
f
and
g
, see the discussion at the beginning of §
4
.
Our analysis there does not involve
NNs or any other proposed collocation method. NNs and PINNs only enter the picture when one wants to
transform the theoretical analysis into a numerical procedure that utilizes NNs to find an approximant to
u
.
We then further discuss in §
4
the use of classical numerical methods such as Finite Element Methods (FEMs) for optimal recovery.

In §
5
, we turn to the question of using optimization to solve the PDE.
We observe that minimizing the theoretical loss
{\cal L}_{T}
over a space
\Sigma_{n}
will approximate well
u
, provided
\Sigma_{n}
has sufficient approximation power. However, this is not a numerical algorithm per se because it does not provide a numerical
recipe for evaluating
{\cal L}_{T}(S)
when
S\in\Sigma_{n}
.

The remainder of this paper discusses possible numerical methods to achieve the optimal recovery rate, i.e., to solve the PDE to the highest accuracy possible given only the information
({\bf f},{\bf g})
(see (
1.7
)).
An important consequence of the analysis in §
5
is that
the widely used loss function
{\cal L}
may not be an appropriate discretization of the theoretical loss
{\cal L}_{T}
. We introduce the loss function
{\cal L}^{*}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

{\cal L}^{*}(v)\!:=\!\!\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}|f({\bf x}_{i})+\Delta v({\bf x}_{i})|^{\gamma}\right]^{1/\gamma}\!\!\!\!+\left[\frac{1}{\overline{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\overline{m}}\frac{|[g-v]({\bf z}_{i})-[g-v]({\bf z}_{j})|^{2}}{|{\bf z}_{i}-{\bf z}_{j}|^{d}}\right]^{1/2}\!\!\!+\left[\frac{1}{\overline{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{\overline{m}}|g({\bf z}_{j})-v({\bf z}_{j})|^{2}\right]^{1/2},

(1.10)

and show that it correctly discretizes an upper bound on the theoretical loss
{\cal L}_{T}
.
Here
\gamma
is the smallest number
(if this is possible) so that
L_{\gamma}(\Omega)
embeds in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
. For example,
\gamma=\frac{2d}{d+2}
in the case
d\geq 3
. The case
d=2
is more complicated and we will discuss it later in the paper.

The loss
{\cal L}^{*}
and its properties are given in §
5
, §
6
and
§
7
. A large component of its development centers on how to discretize
L_{p}
(quasi-)norms and the
H^{1/2}
norm. Our analysis on this subject may
be useful in the development of other methods for solving PDEs numerically.

The first term in the loss
{\cal L}^{*}
are shown to bound
\|f+\Delta v\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
and the second and third terms are shown to bound
\|g-v\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}
. These in turn give a bound for
\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
via
(
1.4
).
We go on to show how near minimizers of the suggested loss
{\cal L}^{*}
give an a priori bound of the approximation error, provided that the approximation method
\Sigma_{n}
has suitable approximation properties. The a priori guarantee provides a near optimal recovery whenever
\Sigma_{n}
provides sufficiently good approximation of the elements in the solution model class
{\cal U}
.

In §
8
, we
analyze a variety of other possible loss functions which provide the same a posteriori error guarantees as the loss
{\cal L}^{*}
. We call the use of any of these loss functions
consistent PINNs
.
If the set
\Sigma_{n}
is chosen large enough, all these losses will provide a near best optimal recovery (in terms of number of data points) of the solution
u
.
We prove that for any function
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, the losses suggested in §
8
always provide an upper bound of the true error
\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
up to the optimal recovery rate for the solution model class
{\cal U}
, provided that the approximant
v
can be controlled in the
\|\cdot\|_{{\cal U}}
-norm (see Theorem
7.2
). This means that these losses can be used as an a posteriori error estimator for any proposed numerical approximation
v
to
u
. This error estimate can be used to check whether the output of a PINNs optimization achieves the desired accuracy
\varepsilon
.

However, proving that a particular numerical method of optimization (such as gradient descent)
converges to a minimizer (or near minimizer) of the loss is a serious issue in optimization, which we do not
address here.

We next develop in §
9
the properties needed of a set
\Sigma_{n}
so that minimizing the loss
{\cal L}^{*}
over
\Sigma_{n}
results in a near optimal recovery of
u
.
It turns out that a certain restricted approximation property, guaranteed to hold
when
\Sigma_{n}
is sufficiently good at approximating the elements of solution model class
{\cal U}
, is sufficient.
In particular, we show that this is the case for suitable NN spaces
\Sigma_{n}
, provided
n
is large enough.

Finally, in §
10
, we numerically test the loss functions described in §
8
on a two-dimensional Poisson equation. These results show that using consistent PINNs provides a significant improvement in the solution error, especially for problems with low regularity.

2
Besov spaces

We start this section by recalling the definition of Besov spaces
and their properties.
For the range
s>0
, and
0<p,q\leq\infty
, the Besov space
B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
,
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, is a space of functions with smoothness of order
s>0
in
L_{p}(\Omega)
. Here
q

is a secondary index that gives a fine gradation of these spaces.
The material in this section is taken for the most part from the papers
[
23
,
21
,
22
]
and the reader will have to refer
to those papers for some of the definitions and proofs. Let us also mention that the univariate
case is covered in the book
[
20
]
.

If
r
is a positive integer,
0<p\leq\infty
and
f\in L_{p}(\Omega)
, we define the modulus of smoothness
\omega_{r}(f,\cdot)_{p}
of
f
by

\omega_{r}(f,t)_{p}:=\omega_{r}(f,t,\Omega)_{p}:=\sup_{|{\rm\bf h}|\leq t}\|\Delta_{\rm\bf h}^{r}(f,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}(\Omega_{r{\rm\bf h}})},\quad t>0,

(2.1)

where

\Delta_{\rm\bf h}^{r}(f,\cdot):=(-1)^{r}\sum_{k=0}^{r}(-1)^{k}\binom{r}{k}f(\cdot+k{\rm\bf h}),

(2.2)

is the
r
-th difference of
f
for
{\rm\bf h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Omega_{\rm\bf h}:=\{{\rm\bf x}\in\Omega:[{\rm\bf x},{\rm\bf x}+{\rm\bf h}]\subset\Omega\}
. Here

[{\rm\bf x},{\rm\bf x}+{\rm\bf h}]
,

{\rm\bf x},{\rm\bf h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denotes the line segment in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
between
{\rm\bf x}
and
{\rm\bf x}+{\rm\bf h}
, and
|{\rm\bf h}|
denotes the Euclidean norm of
{\rm\bf h}
. If
s>0
and
0<p\leq\infty
, then

B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
is defined as the set of all functions in
L_{p}(\Omega)
for which

|f|_{B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))}:=\left[\intop\limits_{0}^{1}[t^{-s}\omega_{r}(f,t)_{p}]^{q}\frac{dt}{t}\right]^{1/q}<\infty,\quad 0<q<\infty,

(2.3)

where
r
can be taken as any integer strictly bigger than
s
. When
q=\infty
we replace the integral by a supremum in the definition. This is a (quasi-)semi-norm and we obtain the (quasi-)norm for
B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))

by adding
\|f\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}
to it. An equivalent (quasi-)semi-norm is given by

|f|_{B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))}\asymp\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}[2^{ks}\omega_{r}(f,2^{-k})_{p}]^{q}\right]^{1/q},\quad 0<q<\infty,

(2.4)

with equivalency constants
independent of
f
.
This is proved by discretizing the integral in (
2.3
) and using the monotonicity of
\omega_{r}

as a function of
t
. When
q=\infty
, (
2.4
) uses the
\ell_{\infty}
norm in place of the
\ell_{q}
norm, i.e.,

|f|_{B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))}\asymp\sup_{k\geq 0}2^{ks}\omega_{r}(f,2^{-k})_{p}.

(2.5)

While different choices of
r
result in different (quasi-)semi-norms, the corresponding Besov (quasi-)norms are equivalent, provided
r>s
.
To fix matters, we define the Besov norm with the value of
r=r(s)
as the smallest integer strictly larger than
\max\{s,1\}
. It follows that
r(s)
is always larger than or equal to
2
. This choice is for notational convenience in the material that follows.

Let us make some remarks on these spaces. Consider the role of
q
in this definition. If
q_{2}<q_{1}
, then
B_{q_{2}}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))\subset B_{q_{1}}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
, i.e., these spaces get smaller as
q
gets smaller. Thus, all

B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
,
q>0
, are contained in
B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
once
s
and
p
are fixed. The effect of
q
in the definition of the Besov spaces is subtle. We remark that when
p=q
and
s
is not an integer, the space
B^{s}_{p}(L_{p}(\Omega))
is equivalent to the Sobolev space
W^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
(see
[
1
]
).

In this paper, the space
B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
is especially important, and accordingly, we use the abbreviated
notation

B_{p}^{s}:=B_{p}^{s}(\Omega):=B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))

(2.6)

in going forward. It follows that a function
f\in B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)
if and only if

\omega_{r}(f,t)_{p}\leq Mt^{s},\quad t>0,

(2.7)

and the smallest
M
for which (
2.7
) is valid is the (quasi-)semi-norm
|f|_{B_{p}^{s}}
.
This space is commonly referred to as (generalized) Hölder smoothness of order
s
in
L_{p}
. It is important to note that when
s
is a positive integer, we take
r>s
in its definition. Therefore the Besov space
B_{p}^{s}
is not a Lipschitz space when
s
is a positive integer. In view of (
2.5
), we have that
a function
f
is in
B_{p}^{s}
if and only if

\omega_{r}(f,2^{-k})_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq M^{\prime}2^{-ks},\quad k=0,1,\dots,

(2.8)

and the smallest
M^{\prime}
for which this is true is an equivalent (quasi-)semi-norm.

2.1
The Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\Omega)

Since the results of this paper heavily use the Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\Omega)
, we give here a short review of these spaces and list some of their properties. The classical Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\Omega)
,
s>0
, are the Besov spaces

H^{s}(\Omega)=B^{s}_{2}(L_{2}(\Omega))\subset B^{s}_{\infty}(L_{2}(\Omega))=:B^{s}_{2}(\Omega).

Two of the
H^{s}(\Omega)
spaces play an important role in what follows, namely
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Let us recall the definition of
H^{-1}(\Omega)
.
The space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
is the collection of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
which vanish on the boundary of
\Omega
, i.e., it is the closure of smooth functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
which are identically zero on
\partial\Omega
. The space
H^{-1}(\Omega)
is by definition the dual space of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and is equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}:=\sup_{\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=1}\langle f,v\rangle_{H^{-1}\times H_{0}^{1}}.

(2.9)

There are two equivalent ways to describe the space
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. The first is as the Besov space
B_{2}^{1/2}(L_{2}(\partial\Omega))
. Here, one needs the concept of Besov spaces on manifolds. In our case, the boundary of
\Omega
is quite simple since it is
the union of the faces of
\Omega
. This definition gives the norm in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
through the modulus of smoothness.
Using the averaged modulus of smoothness, see (
12.3
) in the Appendix, §
12.1
, one finds
that the semi-norm of
g\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, is

|g|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}:=\intop\limits_{\partial\Omega}\intop\limits_{\partial\Omega}\frac{|g({\bf z})-g({\bf z}^{\prime})|^{2}}{|{\bf z}-{\bf z}^{\prime}|^{d}}\,d{\bf z}\,d{\bf z}^{\prime},

(2.10)

which is commonly referred to as the
intrinsic semi-norm
on this space. We obtain the norm on this space by adding
\|g\|_{L_{2}(\partial\Omega)}
to this semi-norm.

The second way to describe
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and its norm is through the trace operator
Tr=Tr_{\partial\Omega}
. Recall that if
g
is a continuous function on
\overline{\Omega}
then the trace
Tr(g)
is simply the restriction of
g
to
\partial\Omega
. While the trace operator has an extension to certain functions that are not continuous, in our applications that appear later the function
g
will always be continuous. Using the trace operator one defines
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
as the trace of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and its
trace norm
is

\|g\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}:=\inf\{\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}:\ Tr(v)=g\}.

(2.11)

It is well known that the trace norm and the intrinsic norms for
H^{1/2}(\Omega)
are equivalent for Lipschitz domains and in particular for our case of
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
(see
[
34
]
). We use both of these norms in what follows while always making clear which norm is being employed.

2.2
Piecewise polynomial approximation and interpolation

Recall that one can characterize membership in Besov spaces by piecewise polynomial approximation. We describe and prove such characterizations in the appendix. An important additional fact is that the piecewise polynomials in such characterizations can be described by
interpolation
. This allows us to generate near best piecewise polynomial approximations to
f
and
g
by using only the data
(f_{i})
, respectively
(g_{i})
.

Let us begin with the cube
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
and discuss polynomial interpolation on
\overline{\Omega}
which
we will later rescale to any dyadic cube. We fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>1
and let

G_{r}:=\left\{\left(\frac{j_{1}}{r-1},\ldots,\frac{j_{d}}{r-1}\right),\ j_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}\right\}\subset[0,1]^{d},

be the tensor product grid
of
r^{d}
equally spaced points in
\overline{\Omega}
.
Consider the simplicial (Kuhn-Tucker) decomposition
of
\overline{\Omega}
into simplices
T^{0}
,

\overline{\Omega}=\bigcup\overline{T}^{0}.

(2.12)

Given any one of these simplices
T^{0}
, the number
n_{r}
of grid points from
G_{r}
in the simplex
\overline{T^{0}}
is the same as the dimension of
{\cal P}_{r}:={\cal P}^{d}_{r}
,
where
{\cal P}^{d}_{r}
denotes the linear space of algebraic polynomials of order
r
(total degree
r-1
), namely,

{\cal P}^{d}_{r}:=\left\{\sum_{|{{\bf k}}|_{1}<r}a_{{\bf k}}{\bf x}^{{\bf k}},\,\,a_{{\bf k}}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},\quad\hbox{where}\quad{\bf x}^{{\bf k}}:=x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{k_{d}},\quad{\bf k}:=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d}),\quad k_{j}\geq 0,\quad|{\bf k}|_{1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}.

Let us consider
the closed simplex

\overline{T}_{0}:=\{{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,x_{1}+\ldots+x_{d}\leq 1,\,\,x_{i}\geq 0,\,i=1,\ldots,d\}.

It is well known that polynomial interpolation using elements of
{\cal P}_{r}
at the grid points in
G_{r}\cap\overline{T_{0}}
is well posed (see e.g. Section 4 in
[
61
]
or Chapter 2 in
[
8
]
).
Let
\phi_{j}:=\phi_{j,T_{0}}
,
j=1,\dots,n_{r}
, be the Lagrange polynomial
basis for
{\cal P}_{r}
corresponding to the points in
G_{r}\cap\overline{T_{0}}
. Then, the operator

L_{T_{0}}(f):=\sum_{{\bf x}_{j}\in G_{r}\cap\overline{T_{0}}}f({\bf x}_{j})\phi_{j},

(2.13)

is a bounded projector from
C(\overline{\Omega})
onto
{\cal P}_{r}
, i.e.,

\|L_{T_{0}}(f)\|_{C(\overline{T_{0}})}\leq\Lambda_{r}\|f\|_{C(\overline{T_{0}})},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\Lambda_{r}:=\|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{r}}|\phi_{j,T_{0}}({{\bf x}})|\|_{C(\overline{T_{0}})},

(2.14)

is the Lebesgue constant which depends only on
r
,
d
, and
T_{0}
.

For each
k\geq 0
, we define
{\cal D}_{k}={\cal D}_{k}(\Omega)
to be the partition of
\Omega
into dyadic cubes
I
of side length
2^{-k}
. Here we take a dyadic cube
I\in{\cal D}_{k}
to be the tensor product
of the dyadic intervals
[(j-1)2^{-k},j2^{-k})
,
1\leq j\leq 2^{k}
.
We then define the tensor product grid
G_{k,r}
of
\overline{\Omega}=[0,1]^{d}
,

G_{k,r}:=\{{\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{\widetilde{m}}\}\subset\overline{\Omega},\quad\widetilde{m}=(r2^{k})^{d},

(2.15)

with spacing
h=2^{-k}(r-1)^{-1}
.
We fix
k\geq 0
,
I\in{\cal D}_{k}
, and a simplex
T
in the simplicial decomposition of
I
, and denote by
\phi_{j,T}
, the rescaled polynomial
\phi_{j,T}:=\phi_{j,T_{0}}\circ F_{T}^{-1}
where
F_{T}:\overline{T}_{0}\rightarrow\overline{T}
is an affine map from the reference simplex
\overline{T}_{0}
to
\overline{T}
. Notice that
\phi_{j,T}
,
j=1,\ldots,n_{r}
, are the Lagrange polynomial
basis for
{\cal P}_{r}
, corresponding to the points in
G_{k,r}\cap\overline{T}
, and thus we obtain the projector
L_{T}
onto
{\cal P}_{r}
which satisfies

\|L_{T}(f)\|_{C(\overline{T})}\leq\Lambda_{r}(T)\|f\|_{C(\overline{T})},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\Lambda_{r}(T):=\|\sum_{j=1}^{n_{r}}|\phi_{j,T}({{\bf x}})|\|_{C(\overline{T})},\quad T\subset I\in{\cal D}_{k},\ k\geq 0.

(2.16)

Here
\Lambda_{r}=\Lambda_{r}(T)
,
i.e.
\Lambda_{r}(T)
does not depend on
T
since
for
{\bf x}\in\overline{T}
we have
\phi_{j,T}({{\bf x}})=\phi_{j,T_{0}}(F^{-1}_{T}({\bf x}))=\phi_{j,T_{0}}({\bf y})
, where
{\bf y}=F^{-1}_{T}({\bf x})\in\overline{T_{0}}
. Moreover, the interpolating polynomial
L_{T}(f)
provides a good approximation to
f
on
T
. For example, for any polynomial
P\in{\cal P}_{r}
, we have

\|f-L_{T}(f)\|_{C(\overline{T})}\leq\|f-P\|_{C(\overline{T})}+\|L_{T}(f-P)\|_{C(\overline{T})}\leq(1+\Lambda_{r})\|f-P\|_{C(\overline{T})}.

(2.17)

Let us denote by
S^{*}_{k}(f)
the piecewise polynomial interpolant

S^{*}_{k}(f):=S^{*}_{k,r}(f):=\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{k}}L_{T}(f)\chi_{T},

(2.18)

where
{\cal T}_{k}
is the collection of all simplicies arising from the
(Kuhn-Tucker) decomposition of the dyadic cubes in
{\cal D}_{k}(\Omega)
and

\chi_{T}
is the characteristic function of
\overline{T}
. The function

S^{*}_{k}(f)
is well defined and continuous on
\overline{\Omega}

since the number of points from
G_{k,r}
that are on a
shared face of any two simplicies from
{\cal T}_{k}
is the same as the dimension of the polynomial space
{\cal P}^{d-1}_{r}
.

The following theorem (proved in the Appendix, §
12.5.1
) describes the approximation accuracy of
S^{*}_{k}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
s>0
,
0<p\leq\infty
, be fixed with
s>d/p
. Let
r=r(s)
be the smallest integer strictly larger than
\max(s,1)

and let
S_{k}^{*}
be the interpolation operator
(
2.18
)
. Then, for any
f\in B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)=B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
and any
\tau\geq p
, we have

\|f-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p+d/\tau)},

(2.19)

with
C
independent of
f
and
k
.

We will also need similar results where the approximation takes place in the
H^{1}(\Omega)=B_{2}^{1}(L_{2}(\Omega))
norm. The following theorem, see the Appendix, §
12.5.2
for its proof, holds.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
. If
f\in B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
with
s>d/p
and
0<p\leq 2
, and
S^{*}_{k}(f)
is as defined in
(
2.18
)
, then

\|f-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))}2^{-k(s-1-d/p+d/2)},\quad k\geq 0.

(2.20)

There are many results concerning the embeddings of Besov spaces into other Besov spaces or
L_{p}
spaces. We are particularly interested in those when a Besov space is embedded into
C(\overline{\Omega})
. For this, we can use the following theorem proved in
the Appendix, §
12.4.1
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For every
f\in B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)
,
s>d/p
,
0<p\leq\infty
, there is a continuous function

\widetilde{f}
such that
f=\widetilde{f}
a.e. In fact,
\widetilde{f}\in
Lip
\alpha
, with
\alpha:=s-d/p
, that is,

\omega_{r}(\widetilde{f},t)_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}t^{s-d/p},\quad t>0.

(2.21)

3
Optimal recovery

In this section, we study the question of
whether the information
({\bf f},{\bf g})
at the
fixed
data sites

{\cal X}:=\{{\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{\widetilde{m}}\},\quad{\cal Z}:=\{{\bf z}_{1},\ldots,{\bf z}_{\overline{m}}\},

is sufficient to determine
u
to a prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon
in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
norm. Questions of this type are well studied and referred to as optimal recovery (OR). The answer to our specific question depends on
the numbers
\widetilde{m},\overline{m}
, the positions of the points from
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Z}
, and the
smoothness assumptions we make on
f
and
g
.
Thus, we require that
f,g

are in certain Besov spaces that compactly embed into

C(\overline{\Omega})

and

C(\partial\Omega)
, respectively, and are such that
u
lies in a compact subset of
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Before presenting the results on optimal recovery in this section, let us remark that the results given below
are (in essence) all known. For example, the optimal recovery rates for the Besov model classes of this section have all been given in the
papers
[
13
,
14
,
69
]
save for one small discrepancy on optimal recovery in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
(explained below) and for the fact that
they do not directly consider
recovery on manifolds like
\partial\Omega
.
The main distinction between the results in
[
69
]
and ours given below is in how the optimal recovery is obtained/proven. The optimal recovery method in
[
69
]
uses linear combinations of certain ‘bump functions’, whereas we use continuous piecewise polynomial Lagrange interpolation.
One can argue that the fact that optimal recovery can be obtained by interpolation is also known since it is a prominent method of approximation in Finite Element Methods (see for instance
[
30
]
). However, the latter community usually considers Sobolev classes, rather than the more general Besov classes, and also typically
restricts
p\geq 1
.
In summary, we could not find a direct reference which proves the results given below based on
continuous piecewise polynomial interpolation. In any case, those not familiar
with optimal recovery may find the exposition below useful and the OR community can just do a very light reading
of this section. The statements in this section are all proved in detail
in the appendix.

We shall assume that

f\in{\cal F}:=U({\cal B}),\ \ {\cal B}=B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)),\quad 0<q,p\leq\infty,\quad s>d/p,

(3.1)

where
U({\cal B})
is the unit ball of
{\cal B}
and
the restriction on
s
ensures that
{\cal F}
compactly embeds into

C(\overline{\Omega})
.

The function
g
is defined on the manifold
\partial\Omega
. There are two ways to describe smoothness conditions on such functions
g
. The first is through the trace operator
Tr:=Tr_{\partial\Omega}
. The second is to place smoothness conditions directly on
g
as a function from

C(\partial\Omega)
. The latter is usually referred to as an intrinsic definition. We choose in this paper to define the set
{\cal G}
via the trace operator. Namely, we define the model class
{\cal G}
via the trace of functions from the unit ball of a Besov class

B_{\overline{q}}^{\overline{s}}(L_{\overline{p}}(\Omega))
with

\overline{s}>d/\overline{p}
to ensure continuity. We also place the restrictions
0<\bar{p}\leq 2
in order to simplify the presentation that follows. In other words, our model class assumptions on the boundary function
g
take the form

g\in{\cal G}:=Tr(U(\overline{{\cal B}})),\ \ \hbox{where}\ \ \overline{{\cal B}}:=B_{\overline{q}}^{\overline{s}}(L_{\overline{p}}(\Omega)),\quad\hbox{with}\ \ \bar{s}>d/\bar{p},\quad 0<\bar{p}\leq 2.

(3.2)

Then
{\cal G}
is a compact subset of both
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and
C(\partial\Omega)
. The latter follows because
\overline{{\cal B}}
embeds compactly into
C(\overline{\Omega})
since
\bar{s}>d/\bar{p}
. When taking the trace this compactness is preserved. The former embedding follows since the stated conditions also imply that
\bar{s}>1+(d/\bar{p}-d/2)
as
d\geq 2
, which implies that
\overline{{\cal B}}
embeds into
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Taking the trace will then be compact in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Note that
\bar{s}>1
.

Note that, in general, the parameters
s,p,q
used in defining
{\cal F}
are different from
\overline{s},\overline{p},\overline{q}
used in defining
{\cal G}
.
These assumptions imply that
the function
u
we want to numerically approximate is an element of the set

{\cal U}:=\{\widetilde{u}\in C(\overline{\Omega}):\ \widetilde{u}\ {\rm satisfies}\ {\rm(\ref{main})}\ {\rm with}\ f\in{\cal F},\ g\in{\cal G}\}.

(3.3)

Given data
{\bf f}:=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{\widetilde{m}})
, the totality of information we have about
f
is that it belongs to the set

{\cal F}_{\rm data}:={\cal F}_{{\rm data}(f)}:=\{\widetilde{f}\in{\cal F}:\ \widetilde{f}_{i}=f_{i},\ i=1,\dots,\widetilde{m}\}.

Similarly, if the data
{\bf g}:=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{\overline{m}})

is coming from a
g\in{\cal G}
, then,
what we know about
g
is that in lies in the set

{\cal G}_{\rm data}:={\cal G}_{{\rm data}(g)}:=\{\widetilde{g}\in{\cal G}:\ \widetilde{g}_{i}=g_{i},\ i=1,\dots,\overline{m}\}.

(3.4)

Notice that
g_{i}=u({\bf z}_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,\overline{m}
.
Finally, the totality of information we have about the sought after
u
is that it is in the set

{\cal U}_{\rm data}:={{\cal U}_{{\rm data}(u)}:=}\{\widetilde{u}\in{\cal U}{:\,-\Delta\widetilde{u}({\bf x}_{i})=f_{i},\ i=1,\dots,\widetilde{m},\quad\widetilde{u}({\bf z}_{i})=g_{i},\ \ i=1,\dots,\overline{m}}\},

or equivalently

{\cal U}_{\rm data}:={\cal U}_{{\rm data}(u)}:=\{\widetilde{u}:\widetilde{u}\ \hbox{is a solution to {\rm(\ref{main})} with }\,f\in{\cal F}_{\rm data},\quad g\in{\cal G}_{\rm data}\}.

We are interested in knowing to what extent the information that
u\in{\cal U}_{\rm data}

identifies
u
.

There is a simple theoretical answer to such questions. Given a compact set
K
of a Banach space
X
, we let
B(K):=B(K)_{X}

denote a ball with the smallest radius in
X
which contains
K
, called a
Chebyshev ball
of
K
in
X
. If we wish to provide
an element from
X
that simultaneously approximates all elements in
K
(in the norm of
X
), then the center of this ball is the best we can do and its radius

R(K)_{X}:={\rm rad}(B(K))_{X}

(3.5)

is the
optimal error
we can obtain. It is called
the error of optimal recovery
of
K
.

If
{\cal U}_{\rm data}
is a compact subset of
X
, let
B=B({\cal U}_{\rm data})_{X}
be a Chebyshev ball
of this set. Since all we know about
u
is that it is in
{\cal U}_{\rm data}
,
any element from
B({\cal U}_{\rm data})_{X}
will give a near best approximation to
u
in
\|\cdot\|_{X}
.
The radius of
B

R({\cal U}_{{\rm data}(u)})_{X}:={\rm rad}(B({\cal U}_{{\rm data}(u)}))_{X}

(3.6)

is then the optimal recovery error. We are interested in bounds for
R({\cal U}_{{\rm data}(u)})_{X}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{X}
is the norm in which we wish to measure accuracy. We introduce similar notation for the recovery of
f
and
g
.

The recovery rate (
3.6
) will not only depend on the data sites
{\cal X}
, and
{\cal Z}
, but also on the values
({\bf f},{\bf g})

assigned at these data sites. In order to obtain
uniform estimates, we fix the data sites
({\cal X},{\cal Z})
and introduce

R^{*}({\cal U},{\cal X},{\cal Z})_{X}:=\sup_{u\in{\cal U}}R({\cal U}_{{\rm data}(u)})_{X},

(3.7)

where the data values come from any
u\in{\cal U}
. This is measuring the worst possible performance over the class
{\cal U}
and is called the
uniform optimal recovery rate
at the fixed data sites

({\cal X},{\cal Z})
.
If we prescribe a budget for the number
m=|{\cal X}|+|{\cal Z}|=\widetilde{m}+\overline{m}
of data
sites, we can ask for the optimal error we can achieve under such a budget restriction. Accordingly, we define

R_{m}^{*}({\cal U})_{X}:=\inf_{{\cal X}\subset\overline{\Omega},\,{\cal Z}\subset\partial\Omega,\,|{\cal X}|+|{\cal Z}|=m}R^{*}({\cal U},{\cal X},{\cal Z})_{X},\quad m\geq 2.

Similarly,

R^{*}({\cal F},{\cal X})_{X}:=\sup_{f\in{\cal F}}R({\cal F}_{{\rm data}(f)})_{X}\quad\hbox{and}\quad R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}:=\inf_{{\cal X}\subset\overline{\Omega},|{\cal X}|=\widetilde{m}}R^{*}({\cal F},{\cal X})_{X},\quad\widetilde{m}\geq 1,

where the latter is the minimum error we can achieve over all the possible choices of data sites
{\cal X}=\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{\widetilde{m}}\}
.
We can similarly ask the question of how well can we can recover

g
from the data
{\bf g}
. Each of these questions has an associated uniform optimal recovery error which is dependent on
which norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
we use to measure the error of recovery.

3.1
Optimal recovery of
f

Let
{\cal F}:=U({\cal B})
where
{\cal B}=B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
with
0\leq q,p\leq\infty
and
s>\frac{d}{p}
.
Recall, see (
2.15
), the tensor product grid
G_{k,r}
of
\overline{\Omega}=[0,1]^{d}
for

r\geq 2
,
with spacing
h=2^{-k}(r-1)^{-1}
(the role of
r
will be made clear below).
We will prove that, see Theorem
3.1
,

R^{*}({\cal F},G_{k,r})_{X}\lesssim\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{X}},\quad\widetilde{m}=(r2^{k})^{d}.

Moreover, we show that for any choice of data sites
{\cal X}\subset\overline{\Omega}
with

|{\cal X}|=\widetilde{m}
, we have

R^{*}({\cal F},{\cal X})_{X}\gtrsim\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{X}},

and therefore

R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}\asymp\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{X}}
.
The proof of this theorem is presented in the Appendix, §
12.6.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
and
{\cal F}:=U({\cal B})
, where
{\cal B}
is the Besov space
B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
with
s>d/p
, and
0<p,q\leq\infty
.
Then the following holds for the optimal recovery rate
R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}
in the norm of
X
:

(i)
If
X=C(\Omega)
, then

R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}\asymp{\widetilde{m}}^{-\alpha_{C}},\ \widetilde{m}\geq 1,\quad{\rm where}\quad\alpha_{C}:=\frac{s}{d}-\frac{1}{p},

(3.8)

with constants of equivalence independent of
\widetilde{m}
.

(ii)
If
X=L_{\tau}(\Omega)
,
\tau>0
, then

R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}\asymp\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{\tau}},\ \widetilde{m}\geq 1,\quad{\rm where}\quad\alpha_{\tau}:=\frac{s}{d}-\left[\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\tau}\right]_{+},

(3.9)

with constants of equivalence independent of
\widetilde{m}
.

(iii)
If
X=H^{1}(\Omega)
,
0<p\leq 2
, then

R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}\asymp\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{H}},\ \widetilde{m}\geq 1,\quad{\rm where}\quad\alpha_{H}:=\frac{s-1}{d}-\left[\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right],

(3.10)

with constants of equivalence independent of
\widetilde{m}
.

(iv)
If
X=H^{-1}(\Omega)
, then

•

if
d\geq 3
and
0<p\leq\infty
, or
d=2
and
1<p\leq\infty
, we have

R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}\asymp\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{-1}},\ \widetilde{m}\geq 1\quad{\rm where}\quad\alpha_{-1}:=\frac{s}{d}-\left[\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right]_{+}\ {\rm and}\ \frac{1}{\delta}:=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d},

(3.11)

with constants of equivalence independent of
\widetilde{m}
.

•

if
d=2
and
0<p\leq 1
, we have

\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{-1}}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}\lesssim\log(\widetilde{m})\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha_{-1}},\qquad\qquad\widetilde{m}\geq 1,

(3.12)

with
\alpha_{-1}
and
\delta
as in
(
3.11
)
.

Moreover, when
\widetilde{m}=(r2^{k})^{d}
, the upper bounds in each of these estimates are obtained when we take the data sites
{\cal X}=G_{k,r}
, provided
r>\max(s,1)
.
In this case, the approximant to
f
is given by the continuous extension of the piecewise polynomial

S_{k}^{*}(f)=\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{k}}L_{T}(f)\chi_{T},

described in
(
2.18
)
, where
{\cal T}_{k}={\cal T}_{k}(\Omega)=\bigcup T

is the collection of all simplicies arising from the (Kuhn-Tucker)
decomposition of the
dyadic cubes in
{\cal D}_{k}(\Omega)
,
the
\chi_{T}
’s are the characteristic functions of
T
, and the
L_{T}(f)
’s are the polynomials of order
r
(
L_{T}(f)\in{\cal P}^{d}_{r}
) gotten by interpolating
f
at the data points
\overline{T}\cap G_{k,r}
.

We remark that in the case
d=2
and
0<p\leq 1
, the logarithm appears because of the failure of the Sobolev embedding to provide
L_{1}(\Omega)\subset H^{-1}(\Omega)
. However, it is actually true that the Hardy space
{\cal H}^{1}(\Omega)
embeds into
H^{-1}(\Omega)
. This could potentially be used to improve our later analysis, but for the sake of simplicity we do not pursue this here.

We make some further observations that will help explain the above
theorem, especially what is happening for recovery in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
. Notice
that under the model class assumption
f\in{\cal F}=U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)))
with
s>d/p
,
the OR for this class is the same whether we measure error in
H^{-1}(\Omega)

or in
L_{\delta}(\Omega)

when
d\geq 3
or
d=2
,
1<p\leq\infty
. We also have that

\|f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\leq\begin{cases}C(d)\|f\|_{L_{\delta}(\Omega)},\quad d\geq 3,\quad\hbox{where}\,\,\delta=\frac{2d}{d+2},\\
\\
\frac{C}{\tau-1}\|f\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)},\quad\,\,d=2,\quad\tau>1,\end{cases}

(3.13)

with
C
an absolute constant, as discussed in Lemma
12.4
of the appendix . These two facts explain the form of the loss function
{\cal L}^{*}
which we discuss in detail later.

3.2
Optimal recovery of
g

In this section, we study the optimal recovery rate of functions
g\in{\cal G}
in the norm of
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Let
Tr=Tr_{\partial\Omega}
be the trace operator onto
\partial\Omega
and let
\overline{{\cal B}}:=B_{\bar{q}}^{\bar{s}}(L_{\bar{p}}(\Omega))
, where we fix
\bar{s},\bar{p},\bar{q}
and assume that
\overline{s}>d/\overline{p}
so that we are sure that
the functions in
\overline{{\cal B}}
are continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
. We consider the case
0<\bar{p}\leq 2
,
which together with the restriction
d\geq 2
implies that
\bar{s}-d(1/\bar{p}-1/2)>1
, and thus the unit ball of
\overline{{\cal B}}
compactly embeds into
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

We define the model class

{\cal G}:=\{g:\,g=Tr(v):\ \|v\|_{\overline{{\cal B}}}\leq 1\},

(3.14)

and use the trace norm definition of

\|\cdot\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}
throughout this section.

Recall the tensor product
grid
G_{k,r}
for
\overline{\Omega}=[0,1]^{d}
, see (
2.15
). We let

\overline{G}_{k,r}:=\{{\bf z}_{i}:\ i=1,\dots,\overline{m}\}=G_{k,r}\cap\partial\Omega

(3.15)

denote the set of those grid points of
G_{k,r}
that are on
the boundary
\partial\Omega
. We will use the points

{\cal Z}=\overline{G}_{k,r}
to recover
g
. Note that the number
\overline{m}
of data sites in
\overline{G}_{k,r}
is

\overline{m}=\#(\overline{G}_{k,r})=2d[r2^{k}]^{d-1}\asymp 2^{k(d-1)},

(3.16)

with constants of equivalency depending only on
r
and
d
.

Given
g\in{\cal G}
,
we define the continuous piecewise polynomial

\overline{S}_{k}(g):=\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}g({\bf z}_{i})\overline{\phi}_{i},

(3.17)

where each
\overline{\phi}_{i}
is the trace of the Lagrange element

\phi_{i}
centered at
{\bf z}_{i}\in\overline{G}_{k,r}
.
Note that if
v\in\overline{{\cal B}}
is any function whose trace on
\partial\Omega
is
g
, and

S_{k}^{*}(v)

is its continuous piecewise polynomial Lagrange interpolant at the grid points
G_{k,r}
, see (
2.18
),
then we have

Tr(S_{k}^{*}(v))=\overline{S}_{k}(g).

(3.18)

This follows from the fact that each Lagrange interpolant

\phi_{i}
centered at
{\bf x}_{i}\in G_{k,r}\setminus\overline{G}_{k,r}
vanishes at the faces of the simplex from the corresponding Kuhn-Tucker decomposition that contains
{\bf x}_{i}
. Note that
\overline{S}_{k}(g)
does not depend on
v
. According to Theorem
2.2
,
S_{k}^{*}(v)\in H^{1}(\Omega)

since
v
belongs to unit ball of
\overline{{\cal B}}
. Then, (
3.18
) gives that
S_{k}(g)\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
.

The proof of the following result is provided in the Appendix, §
12.6.2
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\overline{{\cal B}}=B_{\overline{q}}^{\overline{s}}(L_{\overline{p}}(\Omega))
with
\overline{s}>d/\bar{p}
,
0<\overline{p}\leq 2
,
0<\overline{q}\leq\infty
, and let
{\cal G}=\{Tr(v):\|v\|_{\overline{{\cal B}}}\leq 1\}
. Then the optimal recovery rates of the model class
{\cal G}
in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is

R_{\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal G})_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\asymp\overline{m}^{-\beta},\quad\overline{m}\geq 1,\quad\hbox{where}\quad\beta:=\frac{\overline{s}-1}{d-1}-\frac{d}{d-1}\left[\frac{1}{\bar{p}}-\frac{1}{2}\right],

(3.19)

with constants of equivalency independent of
\overline{m}
.
Moreover, when
\overline{m}=\#(\overline{G}_{k,r})
, the upper bound in
(
3.19
)
is achieved when we take the data sites

{\cal Z}=\overline{G}_{k,r}
, provided
r>\max(s,1)
and the approximant to
g
is given by
the function
\overline{S}_{k}(g)
.

To understand the exponent
\beta
in (
3.19
) better, let
v
be a function in
U(B_{q}^{\bar{s}}(L_{\bar{p}}(\Omega)))
whose trace is
g
, and rewrite
\beta
as

\beta=\frac{\overline{s}-1-(\frac{d}{\bar{p}}-\frac{d}{2})}{d-1}.

Here the numerator is the excess regularity of
v
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and thereby corresponds to the excess regularity of
g
in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and the denominator is
d-1
since

\partial\Omega
has dimension
d-1
.

3.3
Optimal recovery of
u

We turn now to determining the optimal
H^{1}(\Omega)
recovery rate for the functions
u
in the model class
{\cal U}
from data

-\Delta u({\bf x}_{i})=f_{i},\quad{\bf x}_{i}\in{\cal X},\ i=1,\dots,\widetilde{m};\quad u({\bf z}_{i})=g_{i},\quad{\bf z}_{i}\in{\cal Z},\ i=1,\dots\overline{m},

(3.20)

under a given total budget
m=\widetilde{m}+\overline{m}
of data observations.
Recall that
{\cal F}:=U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)))
, where
s>d/p

and
0<q,p\leq\infty
,
{\cal G}:=Tr(U(B_{\overline{q}}^{\overline{s}}(L_{\overline{p}}(\Omega)))
, where
\overline{s}>\frac{d}{\bar{p}}\geq 1
since
d\geq 2
and
0<\overline{p}\leq 2
,

0<\overline{q}\leq\infty
, and

{\cal U}:=\{u\in C(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ u\ {\rm satisfies}\ {\rm(\ref{main})}\ {\rm with}\ f\in{\cal F},g\in{\cal G}\}.

The following theorem
gives the optimal recovery rate for
{\cal U}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
and let
{\cal F}
,
{\cal G}
,
{\cal U}
be as above. Then the following holds for the optimal recovery rate
R^{*}_{m}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
:

•

if
d\geq 3
, or
d=2
and
p>1
, we have

R^{*}_{m}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\asymp m^{-\min(\alpha,\beta)},\quad m\geq 2,

(3.21)

•

if
d=2
and
0<p\leq 1
, we have

m^{-\min(\alpha,\beta)}\lesssim R^{*}_{m}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\log(m)m^{-\alpha}+m^{-\beta},\quad\ m\geq 2,

(3.22)

where

\alpha=\frac{s}{d}-\left[\frac{1}{p}-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d}\right)\right]_{+},\quad\beta=\frac{\overline{s}-1-(\frac{d}{\bar{p}}-\frac{d}{2})}{d-1},

(3.23)

and all constants of equivalence are independent of
m
.

Proof.

Let us fix the data cites
{\cal X},{\cal Z}
with
|{\cal X}|=\widetilde{m}
,

|{\cal Z}|=\overline{m}
,
m=\overline{m}+\widetilde{m}
, and let
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\cal U}_{\rm data}
with corresponding
f_{1},f_{2}\in{\cal F}_{\rm data}
and
g_{1},g_{2}\in{\cal G}_{\rm data}
. It follows from (
1.3
) that

\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\asymp\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)},

and therefore

R^{*}({\cal U},{\cal X},{\cal Z})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\asymp R^{*}({\cal F},{\cal X})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+R^{*}({\cal G},{\cal Z})_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)}.

(3.24)

Clearly, if
\overline{m}=\widetilde{m}\asymp m/2
and using the rates from
Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
, we have that

R^{*}_{m}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\begin{cases}m^{-\alpha}+m^{-\beta}\lesssim m^{-\min(\alpha,\beta)},\quad d\geq 3,\,\hbox{or}\,\,d=2,\,p>1,\\
\\
\log(m)m^{-\alpha}+m^{-\beta},\,\,\quad\quad\quad\quad d=2,\,0<p\leq 1.\end{cases}

On the other hand, using the same theorems and (
3.24
), we obtain

\displaystyle m^{-\min(\alpha,\beta)}\lesssim\inf_{m=\overline{m}+\widetilde{m}}(\widetilde{m}^{-\alpha}+\overline{m}^{-\beta})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\inf_{m=\overline{m}+\widetilde{m}}\left(R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+R_{\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal G})_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\inf_{m=|{\cal X}|+|{\cal Z}|}R^{*}({\cal F},{\cal X})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+R^{*}({\cal G},{\cal Z})_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\inf_{m=|{\cal X}|+|{\cal Z}|}R^{*}({\cal U},{\cal X},{\cal Z})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=R^{*}_{m}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

and the proof is completed.
∎

. That is, the optimal recovery analysis tells us that if two functions
u,u^{\prime}\in{\cal U}
and satisfy the same data, namely
u,u^{\prime}\in{\cal U}_{\rm data}
, then they are close in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
norm.
More precisely, if
u^{*}
is the center of a Chebyshev ball
B({\cal U}_{\rm data})
,
then

\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|u-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|u^{*}-u^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 2R_{m}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(4.1)

However, our analysis does not give a numerical algorithm that takes the data and creates an approximation
\widehat{u}
to
u
with accuracy of the optimal recovery rate. In this section, we discuss two numerical methods which would accomplish the latter task. The methods discussed in this section are not in the form of PINNs. The latter will be discussed in
§
8
.

We assume in this section that the data sites form tensor product grids, that is,

{\cal X}=G_{k,r}
and

{\cal Z}=\overline{G}_{\bar{k},r}:=G_{\bar{k},r}\cap\partial\Omega
. We know such data sites
provide a near optimal recovery.
We have shown that if we use the data
{\bf f}
at

G_{k,r}
, then we can numerically construct a continuous piecewise polynomial interpolant
\widehat{f}
to
f
at the data sites which is a near optimal (in terms of the budget

\widetilde{m}=(r2^{k})^{d}
)
approximation to
f
in the
H^{-1}(\Omega)
norm. Similarly, we can numerically construct
a
\widehat{g}
from the data
{\bf g}
which is a near optimal recovery of
g
in the
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norm
in terms of the budget
\overline{m}\asymp 2^{\bar{k}(d-1)}
.
The following lemma holds.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\widetilde{u}
be
the solution to
(
1.1
)
with right hand side

\widehat{f}:=S^{*}_{k}(f)
and boundary value

\widehat{g}:=\overline{S}_{k}(g)
. Then

\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\widetilde{m}\asymp 2^{kd},\quad\overline{m}\asymp 2^{\bar{k}(d-1)}.

(4.2)

If instead, we are given a fixed total budget
m
, then

\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{m}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\quad\hbox{provided}\quad\widetilde{m}\asymp\overline{m}\asymp m/2.

(4.3)

Proof.

We have that
\widehat{f}:=S^{*}_{k}(f)\in C(\overline{\Omega})\cap H^{1}(\Omega)
, see Theorem
3.1
, and
\widehat{g}:=\overline{S}_{k}(g)\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\cap C(\partial\Omega)
, see Theorem
3.2
.
Note that we cannot use (
4.1
) to bound
\|u-\tilde{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
since
\hat{f}\notin{\cal F}
and
\hat{g}\notin{\cal G}
(they do not have the necessary smoothness) and therefore
\tilde{u}\notin{\cal U}
. In this case, in view of (
1.4
) with
v=\widetilde{u}
, we have

\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f-\hat{f}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+\|g-\hat{g}\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}({\cal F})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+R^{*}_{\overline{m}}({\cal G})_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

where we have used (
3.25
). This proves (
4.2
).
In view of Remark
3.4
, given
m
data sites, the solution
\widetilde{u}
that corresponds to the choice of
\widetilde{m}\asymp m/2
points on
\overline{\Omega}
and
\overline{m}\asymp m/2
points on
\partial\Omega
satisfies (
4.3
)
and thus is a near optimal recovery of
u
in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
norm.
∎

Note that the constructions of
\widehat{f}
and
\widehat{g}
are numerical and we can control their complexity. In order to do that for
\widetilde{u}
, we use an existing numerical method for solving
(
1.1
). This will, of course, incur a numerical error depending on the algorithm we choose. We mention two natural possibilities.

4.1
Using FEMs or AFEMs

In order to streamline the discussion using FEM, we make the simplifying but unnecessary assumption that the data tensor product grids
G_{k,r}
and
\overline{G}_{\overline{k},r}
are matching, i.e.
k=\overline{k}
. For
n\geq k
, we let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the Kuhn-Tucker subdivision of
\Omega
associated with the tensor grid
G_{n,r}
and
V_{n}
be the spaces of continuous piecewise polynomials of degree
r
subordinate to
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. We use Lemma
4.1
to derive the following statement about FEMs.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\widetilde{u}
be the solution to
(
1.1
)
with right hand side
\widehat{f}:=S^{*}_{k}(f)
and boundary value

\widehat{g}:=\overline{S}_{k}(g)
. For
n\geq k
, let
\widehat{u}_{n}
be the Galerkin projection of
\widetilde{u}
onto
V_{n}
. Then, we have

\|u-\widehat{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon_{n}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\varepsilon_{n}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}:=\mathop{\rm dist}({\cal U},V_{n})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(4.4)

Proof.

It follows from (
4.2
) that

\|u-\widehat{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\widetilde{u}-\widehat{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\widetilde{u}-\widehat{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(4.5)

Note that since
n\geq k
, we have that
\widehat{g}\in Tr(V_{n})
.
In particular,
\widehat{u}_{n}
and
\widetilde{u}
have the same boundary data (and are defined with the same right hand side
\widehat{f}
).
Consequently,
\widehat{u}_{n}
is a quasi-best approximation to
\widetilde{u}
in
V_{n}
,

\|\widetilde{u}-\widehat{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\inf_{v\in V_{n}}\|\widetilde{u}-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(4.6)

Invoking (
4.2
), we find that for every
v\in V_{n}
,

\|\widetilde{u}-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|\widetilde{u}-u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

and hence

\inf_{v\in V_{n}}\|\widetilde{u}-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\inf_{v\in V_{n}}\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon_{n}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

This, together with (
4.6
), gives

\|\widetilde{u}-\widehat{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon_{n}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(4.7)

Combining (
4.5
) and (
4.7
) yields the desired estimate (
4.4
).
∎

Let us now discuss the statement of Lemma
4.2
. We have determined the optimal recovery rates

R_{\widetilde{m},\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal U})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, see (
3.26
) for the class
{\cal U}
.
The term
\varepsilon_{n}
will tend to zero as
n\to\infty
and we would obtain the optimal recovery error on the right side of (
4.4
) if
n
is chosen suitably large.
The actual decay rate of

\varepsilon_{n}
depends on the regularity of the model class
{\cal U}
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Therefore, the issue becomes what do our model class assumptions

f\in{\cal F}
,
g\in{\cal G}
, say about the regularity of
u
in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
norm. If we use linear spaces such as standard finite element spaces, then we would want to know the regularity of
u

in the scale of
H^{t}(\Omega)
spaces,
t>1
.
There are several theorems that obtain such regularity results (see e.g.
[
12
,
42
,
55
]
and the references therein) and thereby obtain concrete bounds on the decay rate
\varepsilon_{n}
. We do not elaborate on this further but refer the interested reader to the papers cited above.

If we use nonlinear numerical methods such as AFEMs for solving (
1.1
) with right side
\widehat{f}
, and boundary value
\widehat{g}
, then the
decay rate for
\varepsilon_{n}({\cal U})
can be improved (see
[
6
]
for a summary of such results).

4.2
Reduced models

If one is faced with solving several PDEs with the same data sites
({\cal X},{\cal Z})
but with different
data
({\bf f},{\bf g})
,
one can build a numerical algorithm as follows. In the case when

{\cal X}=G_{k,r}
,
|G_{k,r}|=\widetilde{m}=[r2^{k}]^{d}
, see (
2.15
) and

{\cal Z}=\overline{G}_{\bar{k},r}
,
|\overline{G}_{\bar{k},r}|=\overline{m}=2d[r2^{\bar{k}}]^{d-1}
, see (
3.15
),
we
know that the simplicial interpolation operator
S_{k}^{*}
defined in
(
2.18
) provides a near optimal recovery for
{\cal F}
. Namely,

\|f-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\lesssim R_{{\widetilde{m}}}^{*}({\cal F})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)},\quad\widetilde{m}=[r2^{k}]^{d}.

(4.8)

We write

S_{k}^{*}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}f({\bf x}_{i})\phi_{i},

(4.9)

where the
\phi_{i}
are the local Lagrange functions with
\phi_{i}({\bf x}_{i})=1

and
\phi_{i}({\bf x}_{j})=0
when
j\neq i
, supported on the simplex containing the point
{\bf x}_{i}
. Let
\phi_{i}^{*}
be the solution to
(
1.1
) with right side
\phi_{i}
and zero boundary conditions.

Similarly, the interpolant

\overline{S}_{\bar{k}}(g)
to
g\in{\cal G}
used for optimal recovery, see (
3.17
), can be
written as

\overline{S}_{\bar{k}}(g)=\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}g({\bf z}_{i})\psi_{i},\quad\overline{m}=2d[r2^{\bar{k}}]^{d-1}.

(4.10)

Let
\psi_{i}^{*}
be the solution to (
1.1
) with
g=\psi_{i}
and zero right side.

Given any data
({\bf f},{\bf g})
, the function

\widetilde{u}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}f({\bf x}_{i})\phi_{i}^{*}+\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}g({\bf z}_{i})\psi_{i}^{*}

(4.11)

is a near optimal recovery of the solution
u
to (
1.1
) with right side
f
and boundary values
g
.
Note that
\widetilde{u}
is the solution to (
1.1
) with right side
S_{k}^{*}(f)
and boundary value
\overline{S}_{\bar{k}}(g)
.

Given a budget
m
, one computes offline to a sufficiently high accuracy
suitable approximations
\widehat{\phi}_{i}
and
\widehat{\psi}_{i}
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
to the
\phi_{i}^{*}
and
\psi_{i}^{*}
, respectively. Then the function

\widehat{u}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}f({\bf x}_{i})\widehat{\phi}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}g({\bf z}_{i})\widehat{\psi}_{i},\quad\hbox{with}\quad\widetilde{m}\asymp\overline{m}\asymp m/2,

(4.12)

is a near optimal recovery for the solution
u
to (
1.1
) with
f\in{\cal F}
,
g\in{\cal G}
, where
\left.f\right|_{{\cal X}}={\bf f}
and

\left.g\right|_{{\cal Z}}={\bf g}
.

Note also that the near optimality can be guaranteed for all model class assumptions
provided
s
and
\overline{s}
are all less than a fixed number
s_{0}
.

5
Numerical methods based on optimization

Another method of finding a near optimal approximation to
u
from the given data is through optimization, such as the one used in PINNs.
The starting point of this approach is the fact that the unique solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to (
1.1
) is the unique minimizer of the theoretical loss
{\cal L}_{T}(u)
, see (
1.6
). However,
the minimization in (
1.6
) is over all
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. To make this approach more amenable to numerical implementation, we take this minimization over a set
\Sigma=\Sigma_{n}
, where

\Sigma
is either a linear space of dimension
n
or a nonlinear manifold of order
n
(i.e.,
depending on
n
parameters), and study any function

S_{\Sigma}\in\{\mathop{\rm argmin}_{S\in\Sigma}{\cal L}_{T}(S)\}.

(5.1)

We want to understand how close such an
S_{\Sigma}
is to
u
with error measured in the norm of
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Therefore, we need to require that

\Sigma\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Let us fix our model class
{\cal U}
as given in (
3.3
). If we want
S_{\Sigma}
to be a good approximation to
u
, then we need
\Sigma
to be good at
approximating the elements of
{\cal U}
. We define the error

E({\cal U},\Sigma):=\sup_{v\in{\cal U}}\inf_{S\in\Sigma}\|v-S\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

If we return to our specific
u\in{\cal U}
determined by
f
and
g
, then for any
S_{\Sigma}
from (
5.1
) and any
S\in\Sigma
, we have
from (
1.4
) that

\|u-S_{\Sigma}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim{\cal L}_{T}(S_{\Sigma})\lesssim{\cal L}_{T}(S)\lesssim\|u-S\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(5.2)

Since (
5.2
) holds for all
S\in\Sigma
, we have that

\|u-S_{\Sigma}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\inf_{S\in\Sigma}\|u-S\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim E({\cal U},\Sigma).

(5.3)

The discussion that we have just given shows that if we solve the continuous minimization problem
(
1.6
) over
\Sigma
instead of all of
H^{1}(\Omega)
, then the solution will get as close to

u
as the efficiency of
\Sigma
in approximating
{\cal U}
, i.e.,
E({\cal U},\Sigma)
. This error can be made as small as we wish by taking finer spaces for
\Sigma
, i.e., letting
n
increase. The error we incur is then proportional to the error in

E({\cal U},\Sigma)
which tell us that the best candidates for
\Sigma
are those that approximate our model class
{\cal U}
well.

Several issues arise that prevent the direct implementation of the above loss in our setting.
The first of these is that we do not know neither
f
nor
g
. We only know these functions through the given
data. The remedy for this is to introduce a surrogate for the
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norms that use only the data information
({\bf f},{\bf g})
we have about
f
and
g
. The next section addresses this issue.

6
Discretizing norms

The optimization procedure of the previous section is not a numerical algorithm since it does not incorporate
numerical methods for estimating the norms appearing in the loss
{\cal L}_{T}
, namely
\|\cdot\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}
.
In this section, we address this issue by introducing discrete
norms which involve only the values of functions at the data sites. Replacing the norms in

{\cal L}_{T}
by these discrete norms leads to a loss
{\cal L}^{*}
which can be numerically
computed. The equivalence between the norms in
{\cal L}_{T}
and their discrete counterparts holds (modulo corresponding optimal recovery rates) for functions whose Laplacians are uniformly bounded in
\cal B
and whose traces are uniformly bounded in
\overline{\cal B}
.

As it will be seen in §
7
, the new loss
{\cal L}^{*}
is based on a discretization of the

\|\cdot\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}
(for a special value of
\gamma
)
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}
norms. These discretizations have to satisfy the following two requirements:

•

use the given information
({\bf f},{\bf g})
at the grids
(data sites)
G_{k,r}\subset\overline{\Omega}
and

\overline{G}_{\bar{k},r}=G_{\bar{k},r}\cap\partial\Omega
, respectively.

•

the errors incurred for functions in
{\cal F}
and
{\cal G}
be comparable with the optimal recovery rates for these classes.

We remark that the discretization of integral norms has been studied in many other papers. For example, in
[
66
]
the discretization of the variational form of the Poisson equation for functions in the Barron space (or variation space corresponding to shallow ReLU
k
networks) is considered. This analysis is done for both random data sites and a fixed grid. There are also papers that prove existence of data sites
{\cal X}
for which the continuous and discrete
L_{\tau}
norms are equivalent for elements of certain linear spaces, see
[
16
,
15
]
. To the best of our knowledge, there are no results in the literature which treat the discretization of the
H^{1/2}
-norm that are comparable to what we do in §
6.2
.

6.1
A discrete
L_{\tau}
norm

In this section, we study
the discretization of the
L_{\tau}
norm for any
1\leq\tau\leq\infty
. Let
{\cal F}=U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)))
,
s>d/p,

be a model class assumption on
f
.
We consider the uniform grid
G_{k,r}\subset\overline{\Omega}
, see (
2.15
), consisting of

\widetilde{m}
points,
\widetilde{m}=|G_{k,r}|=[r2^{k}]^{d}
,
k\geq 0

and
r>\max(s,1)
.
For any continuous function
f
and any
1\leq\tau<\infty
, we define

\|f\|^{*}_{L_{\tau}}:=\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{\widetilde{m}}|f({\rm\bf x}_{j})|^{\tau}\right]^{1/\tau},\quad\hbox{where}\quad{\bf x}_{j}\in G_{k,r}.

(6.1)

When
\tau=\infty
we make the usual modification to define the
\|f\|^{*}_{L_{\infty}}
norm.

The following lemma shows that the discrete
L_{\tau}
norm is close to the true

L_{\tau}
norm for functions in the model class
{\cal F}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
{\cal B}=B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
,
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
, be a Besov space with
s>d/p
.
If
f\in{\cal B}
, then
for any
1\leq\tau\leq\infty
, we have

\|f\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f\|^{*}_{L_{\tau}}+\|f\|_{{\cal B}}{\widetilde{m}}^{-\alpha_{\tau}},\quad\hbox{and}\quad\|f\|^{*}_{L_{\tau}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{{\cal B}}{\widetilde{m}}^{-\alpha_{\tau}},

with

\alpha_{\tau}:=\frac{s}{d}-\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)_{+}
. Recall that
{\widetilde{m}}^{-\alpha_{\tau}}
is the uniform rate of optimal recovery of the model class
{\cal F}
in
L_{\tau}(\Omega)
. The constant
in
\lesssim
depends only on
r
and

d
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma for
1\leq\tau<\infty
.
A simple modification of the proof handles the case
\tau=\infty
.
We use the simplicial interpolation operator

S^{*}_{k}
given in (
2.18
). Let
{\cal T}_{k}:={\cal T}_{k}(\Omega)=\bigcup T
be the set of simplices that make up the simplicial decomposition of the cubes in
{\cal D}_{k}(\overline{\Omega})
.
Then, from (
2.18
) we have

\|S_{k}^{*}(f)\|^{\tau}_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}=\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{k}}\|L_{T}(f)\|^{\tau}_{L_{\tau}(T)},

(6.2)

where
L_{T}(f)
is the polynomial in
{\cal P}^{d}_{r}
that interpolates the
data

\{f({\bf x}_{i}):\,{\bf x}_{i}\in\overline{T}\}
. By equivalence of
norms on
{\cal P}^{d}_{r}
(the linear space of algebraic polynomials of
d
variables and order
r
(total degree
r-1
)), rescaling arguments, and the fact that we have
d!
simplices in the Kuhn-Tucker decomposition of a cube,

\|L_{T}(f)\|_{L_{\tau}(T)}\asymp\Bigl[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{{\bf x}_{i}\in{\overline{T}}}|f({\bf x}_{i})|^{\tau}\Bigr]^{1/\tau},

(6.3)

with constants of equivalency depending only on
d,r
.
Summing over
T\in{\cal T}_{k}
the quantities in (
6.3
) gives

\|S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\asymp\Bigl[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}|f({\bf x}_{i})|^{\tau}\Bigr]^{1/\tau}=\|f\|^{*}_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}.

(6.4)

For
f\in{\cal B}=B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
, the piecewise polynomial interpolant
S_{k}^{*}(f)
provides a near optimal recovery of
f
from the data
{\bf f}
, that is,
see Theorem
2.1
,

\|f-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\leq\begin{cases}C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p+d/\tau)}=C\|f\|_{{\cal B}}{\widetilde{m}}^{-\alpha_{\tau}},\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\tau\geq p,\\
\\
\|f-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-ks}=C\|f\|_{{\cal B}}{\widetilde{m}}^{-\alpha_{\tau}},\quad\tau<p,\end{cases}

(6.5)

where we have used that
\widetilde{m}=[r2^{k}]^{d}
.
It follows then from (
6.4
) that

\left|\|f\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}-\|S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\right|\leq C\|f\|_{{\cal B}}{\widetilde{m}}^{-\alpha_{\tau}},

(6.6)

and the proof is completed.
∎

6.2
Discrete
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norms

In this section, we introduce discrete
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norms and discuss their accuracy in computing the true
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norm for functions
g
in our model class
{\cal G}
. The discrete norm
we introduce only uses the values of
g
at the data sites
and can therefore be computed from the given data.
We fix any
k\geq 1
and let

{\cal Z}:=\overline{G}_{k,r}=\{{\bf z}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\overline{m}}=G_{k,r}\cap\partial\Omega
, see (
3.15
), be the grid points on the boundary
\partial\Omega
. The number of grid points in
{\cal Z}
is
\overline{m}=2d[r2^{k}]^{d-1}
.

We fix a model class
{\cal G}=Tr(U(\overline{{\cal B}}))
of the form (
3.31
). The optimal recovery rate for this class is

R_{\overline{m}}^{*}({\cal G})_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\asymp{\overline{m}}^{-\beta},\quad{\overline{m}}\geq 1,\quad\beta=\frac{\bar{s}-1}{d-1}.

(6.7)

This also serves to fix
r
which we recall is the smallest integer satisfying
r>\max(\bar{s},1)
.
For any continuous function
g
which is the trace of a
v\in\overline{B}
, we use the trace norm

\|g\|_{Tr(\overline{{\cal B}})}:=\inf_{Tr(v)=g}\|v\|_{\overline{B}}.

(6.8)

Recall that the intrinsic semi-norm for
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is given by

|g|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}:=\intop\limits_{\partial\Omega\times\partial\Omega}\frac{|g({\bf x})-g({\bf y})|^{2}}{|{\bf x}-{\bf y}|^{d}}\,d{\bf x}d{\bf y}.

(6.9)

We obtain the intrinsic
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norm by adding

\|g\|_{L_{2}(\partial\Omega)}
to this semi-norm.
It is known that this definition is equivalent to the trace norm definition (
6.8
) for this domain
\Omega
(see
[
34
]
). We shall use (
6.9
) through out this section.

Let
\overline{S}_{k}
be as defined in (
3.17
). We have proven in Theorem
3.2
that

\|g-\overline{S}_{k}(g)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|g\|_{Tr(\overline{{\cal B}})}\overline{m}^{-\beta},

(6.10)

with
C
independent of
\overline{m}
and
g
.
This gives the comparison

\left|\|g\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}-\|\overline{S}_{k}(g)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\right|\leq\|g-\overline{S}_{k}(g)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|g\|_{Tr(\overline{{\cal B}})}\overline{m}^{-\beta}.

We concentrate therefore on finding a discrete
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norm for the functions
\overline{S}_{k}(g)
,
g\in{\cal G}
. Note that
\overline{S}_{k}(g)
is completely determined by the values of
g
at the data sites
\overline{G}_{k,r}
.
For each

A discrete loss function with error control

In section §

Let us make some remarks on this theorem.

Remark 7.3
.

If we knew in advance the smoothness class of

, or at least the value of

, and if this value were

, then in the case

we could modify appropriately the the loss

so that it is independent of

. This choice would lead to the actual optimal recovery rates

.

Remark 7.4
.

It is natural that

enters into the error bounds since we need some control on

away from the data. This means that when we try to obtain a good approximation to

(in

) through optimization, then
either the norm

will have to participate in the loss function as a regularization term, see
[
5
]
, or the approximant has to come from a set with good restricted approximation properties, see Theorem

and Remark

. In both of these situations there does not appear to exist an optimization procedure with a priori guarantees when using deep neural networks, although using shallow networks a priori bounds can be achieved using a greedy algorithm
[

]
. In practice, however, once an approximant

is obtained (say, as a result of a minimization of a loss that does not involve

), we can in principle use
(
7.4
)
to compute an upper bound for the quality of approximation

, provided that we can compute an (a posteriori) estimate on

. In fact, similar methods for certifying PINNs solutions have been studied before (see for instance
[
29
]
and the references therein).

Remark 7.5
.

This error estimator can be used
to monitor the error in any optimization scheme that attempts to minimize the loss
{\cal L}^{*}(v)
. For example, suppose that

\Sigma=\Sigma_{n}
is a linear space of dimension
n
or a nonlinear manifold determined by
n
parameters such that each

is continuous and has a continuous Laplacian
\Delta S
. If at any stage of the optimization
procedure we have an

, then we can use
(

)
to bound the error of

.
Of course, a good estimate requires a bound for

.

Remark 7.6
.

Note that for fixed values of
\widetilde{m}
and
\overline{m}
, the losses
{\cal L}
and
{\cal L}^{*}
are equivalent up to a constant depending upon the parameters
\widetilde{m}
and
\overline{m}
. This means that if the original loss
{\cal L}
is driven to

, i.e. we are perfectly interpolating the data, then both losses give the same error control. However, in the situation where the data is not interpolated, the new loss
{\cal L}^{*}
gives independent on

, while the original PINNs loss
{\cal L}
does not.

In view of the above remarks, we see that the effectiveness of using

together with the discrete loss
{\cal L}^{*}
to provide an approximation to

is not simply governed by
the error

E({\cal U},\Sigma_{n})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

of approximating the class
{\cal U}

by

in the

but rather by a form of restricted approximation
which involves
\|\cdot\|_{\cal U}

[
5
]
.

8
Implications for Loss Function Design

In this section, we discuss the implications of Theorem
7.2
on the design of loss functions for the solution of elliptic PDEs using PINNs. Theorem
7.2
implies that the loss function
{\cal L}^{*}
given in (
7.2
) gives an a posteriori control on the error up to the optimal recovery rate. However, there are numerous factors that need to be taken into account when designing an appropriate loss function, including the ease of training and implementation. For this reason, directly optimizing the loss
{\cal L}^{*}
may not be the best choice in practice.

We observe that any loss function
{\cal L}
which upper bounds
{\cal L}^{*}
also gives a posteriori error control via Theorem
7.2
. With this in mind, we consider the following variants of PINNs for elliptic PDEs.
We consider the case
d\geq 3
. Similar discussion holds when
d=2
, taking into account the form of
{\cal L}^{*}
in (
7.2
):

•

The original PINNs formulation with an adjustable weight
\lambda>0
on the boundary terms,

{\cal L}_{sq,\lambda}(v):=\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}[\Delta v({\bf x}_{i})+f({\bf x}_{i})]^{2}\right]+\left[\frac{\lambda}{\overline{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}[v({\bf z}_{i})-g({\bf z}_{i})]^{2}\right].

(8.1)

Note that the case
\lambda=1
corresponds to the original formulation of PINNs
[
59
]
and is equivalent to the (square of the) loss
{\cal L}
, see (
1.9
). Numerous heuristic strategies for choosing the weight
\lambda
have been explored in a variety of works, see
[
18
,
71
,
72
]
.

•

The loss function
{\cal L}^{*}
in (
7.2
) with exponent
\tau\geq 1

\begin{split}{\cal L}_{sq,\tau}^{*}(v):=\!\!\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}|f({\bf x}_{i})+\Delta v({\bf x}_{i})|^{\tau}\right]^{2/\tau}\!\!\!\!&+\left[\frac{1}{\overline{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\overline{m}}\frac{|[g-v]({\bf z}_{i})-[g-v]({\bf z}_{j})|^{2}}{|{\bf z}_{i}-{\bf z}_{j}|^{2}}\right]\\
&+\left[\frac{1}{\overline{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{\overline{m}}|g({\bf z}_{j})-v({\bf z}_{j})|^{2}\right].\end{split}

(8.2)

When
\tau=\gamma
given in (
7.1
), this is equivalent to the (square of the) loss
{\cal L}^{*}
.

Note that all of these loss functions are squared, which is typically done to allow for easier optimization (the notation

here is a shorthand for squared).

We first observe that the
L_{\gamma}
-norm is bounded by the
L_{\tau}
-norm for any
\tau\geq\gamma
on a finite measure space, such as the bounded domain
\Omega
, or the normalized empirical measure at the data points

. This means that

({\cal L}^{*})^{2}\lesssim{\cal L}_{sq,\tau}^{*}\quad\hbox{for any}\quad\tau\geq\gamma,

(8.3)

and thus
Theorem
7.2
gives an a posteriori bound on the solution error in terms of
{\cal L}_{sq,\tau}^{*}
. This means that we can switch to a different norm
L_{\tau}
with
\tau\geq\gamma
on the domain term in the loss
{\cal L}^{*}
and retain an a posteriori error guarantee.

The original PINNs loss
{\cal L}_{sq,\lambda}
with fixed,
independent on
\overline{m}
,

\lambda
is not an upper bound (with constant independent of
\overline{m}
or
\widetilde{m}
) on the loss
({\cal L}^{*})^{2}
, and thus does not give an a posteriori error estimate via Theorem
7.2
. This is due to the fact that the discrete
L_{2}
-norm is not an upper bound for the discrete
H^{1/2}
-norm on the boundary used in
{\cal L}^{*}
. However, by letting
\lambda
depend upon the number
\overline{m}
of the boundary collocation points, one can correct this. Indeed,
it follows from (
8.3
) with
\tau=2>\gamma=\frac{2d}{d+2}
that

({\cal L}^{*})^{2}\lesssim{\cal L}^{*}_{sq,2}\lesssim{\cal L}_{sq,\lambda(\overline{m})},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\lambda(\overline{m}):=\overline{m}^{1/(d-1)},

and the
constants in
\lesssim
are independent of
\overline{m}
. The last inequality is easily proved by observing that

\frac{1}{\overline{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\overline{m}}\frac{|u({\bf z}_{i})-u({\bf z}_{j})|^{2}}{|{\bf z}_{i}-{\bf z}_{j}|^{d}}\leq\frac{2}{\overline{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\overline{m}}\frac{|u({\bf z}_{i})|^{2}+|u({\bf z}_{j})|^{2}}{|{\bf z}_{i}-{\bf z}_{j}|^{d}}\lesssim\overline{m}^{-\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}|u({\bf z}_{i})|^{2}.

(8.4)

Remark 8.1
.

The
value
({\cal L}_{sq,\lambda(\overline{m})}(v))^{1/2}
can be used in Theorem
7.2
instead of
{\cal L}^{*}(v)
. This indicates how to
correctly weight
the boundary and domain terms depending on the number of collocation points in the original PINNs formulation, i.e. to use the loss

{\cal L}_{sq,\lambda(\overline{m})}(v)=\left[\frac{1}{\widetilde{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}[\Delta v({\bf x}_{i})+f({\bf x}_{i})]^{2}\right]+\left[\overline{m}^{(2-d)/(d-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}[v({\bf z}_{i})-g({\bf z}_{i})]^{2}\right].

(8.5)

All of these alternative loss functions provide an upper bound for the loss
{\cal L}^{*}_{sq,\gamma}
. Consequently, if the (restricted) optimization problem were solved exactly, the loss
{\cal L}^{*}_{sq,\gamma}
would reach a lower value than these alternatives and would give the best a posteriori error bound up to OR rates. However, in practice, the optimization of neural networks introduces numerous complications, and it is not a priori clear which loss function is best. In §
10
, we present numerical experiments comparing the practical performance of these loss functions.

Finally, we remark that our analysis of loss functions is performed with respect to the
H^{1}
-error. This same analysis can be performed for different error norms as well (see for instance
[
78
]
for the
H^{1/2}
-norm), but we leave this for future work.

9
A numerical optimal recovery algorithm

Let
{\cal U}
be the model class (
3.3
), determined by

{\cal F}
and
{\cal G}
, where
{\cal F}
is a maximal model class (
3.27
), (
3.29
) and
{\cal G}
is a maximal model class (
3.31
).
To build a numerical algorithm for the recovery of
u\in{\cal U}_{\rm data}
, we want to minimize the discrete loss
{\cal L}^{*}
over a suitable set
\Sigma_{n}
. Let
M\geq 1
and let
\widetilde{u}
be such that

\widetilde{u}\in\{\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\|v\|_{{\cal U}}\leq M}{\cal L}^{*}(v)\}.

(9.1)

Clearly,
u
is a solution to this problem since
\|u\|_{{\cal U}}\leq 1
and
{\cal L}^{*}(u)=0
. Thus,

{\cal L}^{*}(\widetilde{u})=0
and it follows from (
7.4
) that

\|u-\widetilde{u}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim M{\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\tilde{m},\bar{m}),\quad m\geq 1,

(9.2)

with the implied constant depending only on
r
and
d
. In other words,
\widetilde{u}
provides a near optimal recovery when
d\geq 3
and a near optimal recovery up to a
\log m
factor when
d=2
.
However, the minimization problem
(
9.1
) is taken over too large of a set to be numerically viable and so we would like to utilize minimization
over a smaller set.

Let
\Sigma_{n}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
be an approximating set. Here, the primary examples for
\Sigma_{n}
are linear spaces of finite dimension
n
or a nonlinear manifolds depending on
n
parameters such as NNs. For
M\geq 1

(typically taken as some fixed number not dependent on
n
), we define the set

\Sigma_{n}(M):=\{S\in\Sigma_{n}:\|S\|_{{\cal U}}\leq M\}\subset\Sigma_{n},

(9.3)

and consider the solution
\widehat{S}
to the minimization problem

\widehat{S}\in\{\mathop{\rm argmin}_{S\in\Sigma_{n}(M)}{\cal L}^{*}(S)\}.

(9.4)

In order to present our results, we denote by

E(v,\Sigma_{n}(M)):=\begin{cases}\inf_{S\in\Sigma_{n}(M)}\left(\|\Delta v-\Delta S\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}+\|Tr(v-S)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\right),&d\geq 3,\\
\\
\inf_{S\in\Sigma_{n}(M)}\left((1+\log(\widetilde{m}))\|\Delta v-\Delta S\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}+\|Tr(v-S)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\right),&d=2,\end{cases}

(9.5)

the error of simultaneously approximating
v
and
\Delta v
. We remark that in the case
d=2
and
1<p\leq\infty
, this analysis can be modified to remove the logarithm. Then, for the model class
{\cal U}
of
H^{1}(\Omega)
, we denote by

E({\cal U},\Sigma_{n}(M)):=\sup_{v\in{\cal U}}E(v,\Sigma_{n}(M)),

the
error of restricted approximation
of the class
{\cal U}
by elements from
\Sigma_{n}(M)
.
We have the following theorem.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
\Sigma_{n}
be a set in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and let
\Sigma_{n}(M)
be defined by
(
9.3
)
for a fixed value
M\geq 1
. If
\widehat{S}
is any function from
(
9.4
)
then

\|u-\widehat{S}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim M{\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\widetilde{m},\overline{m})+E(u,\Sigma_{n}(M)),

(9.6)

with the constant in
\lesssim
depending only on
r,d
.
Moreover, if the approximation set
\Sigma_{n}(M)
is such that
E({\cal U},\Sigma_{n}(M))\leq C{\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\widetilde{m},\overline{m})
for some

C
depending only on
r,d
,
then
\widehat{S}
provides a uniform near optimal recovery (up to logarithmic factors in the case
d=2
) for the model class
{\cal U}
.

Proof.

We only prove this in the case
d\geq 3
. In the case
d=2
the same modifications can be made as before. We use (
7.4
) for
\widehat{S}
to obtain

\|u-\widehat{S}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim{\cal L}^{*}(\widehat{S})+(1+M){\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\widetilde{m},\overline{m}).

(9.7)

For all
S\in\Sigma_{n}(M)
, Lemma
6.1
and Theorem
6.4
give the bound

{\cal L}^{*}(\widehat{S})\leq{\cal L}^{*}(S)\lesssim\|\Delta u-\Delta S\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}+\|Tr(u-S)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}+CM{\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\widetilde{m},\overline{m}),

with the constant in
\lesssim
depending only on
r,d
.
Taking an infimum over all
S\in\Sigma_{M}
gives

{\cal L}^{*}(\widehat{S})\lesssim E(u,\Sigma_{n}(M))+M{\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\widetilde{m},\overline{m}),

(9.8)

which together with (
9.7
) proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 9.2
.

Theorem
9.1

guarantees that
\widehat{S}
is a near optimal recovery of
u
provided that for the model class
{\cal U}
we can make

E({\cal U},\Sigma_{n}(M))
small by taking
n
large. Note that the quantity
E({\cal U},\Sigma_{n}(M))
determines how well the elements of the set
\Sigma_{n}(M)
simultaneously approximate the Laplacians (in the
L_{\gamma}(\Omega)
norm) and the traces (in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norm) of the elements in
{\cal U}
. Thus, the question is whether

\Sigma_{n}
has favorable restricted approximation properties.
There is a large literature of approximation theoretic results concerning how efficiently deep neural networks can approximate various classes of functions (see
[
24
]
and the references therein).
When error is measured in the
L_{p}
-norm, optimal rates have been obtained
[
65
,
77
,
62
]
in the case of approximating functions from Besov spaces using deep ReLU neural networks. There has also been recent work on the problem when error is measured in another Besov space, although in this case a smoother activation function must be taken
[
76
,
75
]
.
On the other hand, the question of restricted approximation that is used in the present paper has seemingly not been studied.
We conjecture that the techniques developed in
[
65
,
77
,
62
]
can be appropriately modified to give the same rates for restricted approximation with a fixed suitably large value for
M
(independent of
n
).

10
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present numerical experiments testing the loss functions described in §
8
for the practical solution of elliptic PDEs. In our experiments, we solve the two-dimensional Poisson equation

-\Delta u=f~\text{in $\Omega$},~u=g~\text{on $\partial\Omega$},

(10.1)

on
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
for various right-hand sides
f
and boundary values
g
. We take a uniform grid of collocation points on
\Omega
, where
\widetilde{m}
is the total number of collocation points (where we penalize the residual of the equation), and
\overline{m}
is the number of collocation points which lie on the boundary (where we penalize the residual of the boundary values). We then test the following four loss functions, described in §
8
:

•

The original (unweighted) PINNs loss:
{\cal L}_{sq,1}
;

•

A properly weighted PINNs loss:
{\cal L}_{sq,\lambda(\overline{m})}
, see (
8.5
);

•

The loss
{\cal L}^{*}_{sq,\gamma}
from (
7.2
) (since
d=2
we choose here
\gamma=1.1
);

•

The loss
{\cal L}^{*}_{sq,2}
(the loss function (
7.2
) with
L_{2}
on the domain).

Since we are in the case
d=2
, the latter two loss functions involve a logarithm which is ignored in the numerical experiments.
For all losses, we use the same neural network and training algorithm. We report both the final loss values achieved and the final relative error in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-norm (estimated using a large number of collocation points).

Let us remark on the relative cost of implementing each of these four loss functions. In all our experiments, the efficiency of optimizing each of them was roughly the same. The reason for this is that although the discretization of the
H^{1/2}
-norm on the boundary used in the losses
{\cal L}^{*}_{sq,\gamma}
and
{\cal L}^{*}_{sq,2}
is more expensive to compute and differentiate, the training process is dominated by the cost of evaluating the network and its derivatives at all of the collocation points rather than evaluating the loss based upon these values. For this reason, using losses which discretize the
H^{1/2}
-norm doesn’t significantly slow down the network training.

10.1
Network Architecture and Training Algorithm

When training PINNs, it is known that the optimization of the neural network can be particularly sensitive and tricky
[
60
,
46
,
71
,
72
,
18
]
, and much research has been devoted to the development of improved training algorithms for PINNs
[
53
,
17
,
44
,
57
,
79
,
70
]
. In this section, we describe our network architecture, initialization, and training algorithm in detail.

In our experiments, we use a deep neural network with
L
hidden layers of width
W
. The first layer uses a
\tanh
activation function, while the remaining layers are residual layers
[
40
]
with the ReLU
3
activation function. The network is initialized as follows:

•

The parameters in the first layer are randomly initialized from a normal distribution with variance
1
.

•

For the residual layers with ReLU
3
activation function, the parameters are randomly initialized from a normal distribution with variance

\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{2}{15}\right)^{1/6}\frac{1}{\sqrt{LW}}.

In analogy with Xavier and He initialization
[
33
,
39
]
, this ensures that the variance of the output of each layer gets multiplied by a factor of
(1+2/L)
in each residual layer, which guarantees that the expected magnitude of the neural network function at initialization is bounded.

•

In the final layer, the weights are initialized randomly from a normal distribution with variance
1/\sqrt{W}
, which ensures that the network output has the same variance as the output of the final hidden layer.

We train this network applying the energy natural gradient descent algorithm described in
[
57
]
, using a line search to find the step size in each iteration. The full code and all experimental details can be found at
https://github.com/jwsiegel2510/consistent-PINNs
.

11
Concluding Remarks

We have investigated collocation methods for solving elliptic PDEs of the form (
1.1
).
Under model class assumptions of the form
f\in{\cal F}

and
g\in{\cal G}
, which are both unit balls of Besov spaces, we showed that there is an optimal
error (called the error of optimal recovery) in recovering
u
in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
norm. We determined this optimal recovery error for the various model classes. Our theoretical analysis was performed for
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
, but it is well-known that the results carry over to any Lipschitz domain (see
[
58
]
).

We then turned to the study of theoretical
algorithms which would yield an optimal recovery. The most prominent of these methods is to
minimize a certain loss function
{\cal L}
that measures how well we have fit the data observations. While the typical loss function used in PINNs does not provide an a posteriori bound on the error, we have introduced a variety of modified loss functions which do provide such a bound. We call the use of any one of these theoretically justified loss functions consistent PINNs. Finally, we perform several numerical experiments which compare these different loss functions and show their favorable performance
in comparison to the typical loss used in PINNs.

We now wish to make some further comments that one can view as serious caveats to
the above theory. The first and most serious objection to the above development is
that we do not provide a numerical algorithm for finding a minimizer or near minimizer
of the suggested losses over
\Sigma_{n}(M)
. This is the same objection that can be
made for almost all learning problems and certainly for PINNs. The usual approach
in PINNs
is to use gradient descent or some modification of gradient descent to solve the minimization of the loss. Unfortunately, there is no proof of convergence or accuracy when applying gradient descent to non-convex losses such as
{\cal L}
or
{\cal L}^{*}
. This has not impeded the use of gradient descent in practice with much empirical success. We have utilized such techniques as well in our numerical examples.

We do not enter into a discussion of the art of using gradient descent in optimization. However, we want to address two issues that are relevant to this paper. The first is the fact that we must optimize the loss over
\Sigma_{n}(M)
rather than
\Sigma_{n}
itself to gain the proof of optimality. This is a serious numerical burden. We have ignored this issue in our numerical experiments much like one ignores the lack of a provable
gradient descent algorithm. One could implement the restricted approximation
by adding a penalty to the consistent losses to guarantee that at each step of the optimization one remains in the constrained set
\Sigma_{n}(M)
(see
[
5
]
) but this does not ease the computational burden and one is still faced with a lack of convergence of the numerical optimization.

The second issue is understood by practitioners but not usually pointed out in the theory. Neural network spaces are a very unstable manifold in the sense that changing parameters very slightly can have a huge
effect on the output of the neural network. That is, the mapping from parameters to output in neural networks is very unstable. This instability appears more in deep networks than in shallow networks. Avoiding this lack of stability is an art in practice that manifests itself in judiciously choosing the starting parameters
and the learning rates (step size in gradient descent). The theoretical aspects of
this lack of stability, and how to avoid the instability theoretically, is addressed in a series of recent papers
[
9
,
36
,
35
,
37
]
. However, there remains a serious gap between theoretical algorithms for learning and their numerical implementation.

12.1
Local approximation by polynomials

There are many important results on the approximation of functions in Besov classes. We will use approximation by piecewise polynomials. We begin by describing local
polynomial approximation.

For any integer
r\geq 1
, we let
{\cal P}_{r}^{d}:={\cal P}_{r}
denote the linear space of algebraic polynomials of order
r
(total degree
r-1
), namely,

{\cal P}_{r}:=\left\{\sum_{|{{\bf k}}|_{1}<r}a_{{\bf k}}{\bf x}^{{\bf k}},\,\,a_{{\bf k}}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},\quad\hbox{where}\quad{\bf x}^{{\bf k}}:=x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{k_{d}},\quad{\bf k}:=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d}),\quad k_{j}\geq 0,\quad|{\bf k}|_{1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}.

Note that
\omega_{r}(P,t)_{p}=0
,
t\geq 0
, for all
P\in{\cal P}_{r}
.
If
I
is any cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f\in L_{p}(I)
,
0<p\leq\infty
, we denote by

E_{r}(f,I)_{p}:=\inf_{P\in{\cal P}_{r}}\|f-P\|_{L_{p}(I)},

(12.1)

the error of approximation of
f
on
I
in the
\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}(I)}
norm by the elements of
{\cal P}_{r}
. A well known result in approximation theory, commonly referred to as Whitney’s theorem, says
that for any
f\in L_{p}(I)
with
I
a cube with sidelength
\ell_{I}
, we have

cE_{r}(f,I)_{p}\leq\omega_{r}(f,\ell_{I})_{L_{p}(I)}\leq CE_{r}(f,I)_{p},

(12.2)

with the constants
c,C
depending only on
r,d
and also
p
if
p
is close to
0
. Whitney’s theorem usually only refers to the lower inequality in (
12.2
). However, the upper inequality follows trivially since

\omega_{r}(f,\ell_{I})_{L_{p}(I)}=\omega_{r}(f-P,\ell_{I})_{L_{p}(I)}\leq C\|f-P\|_{L_{p}(I)},

holds for any polynomial
P\in{\cal P}_{r}
.

We also will use the following modified form of Whitney’s theorem. For any cube
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
any
0<p<\infty
, and
f\in L_{p}(I)
, we define

\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,t)^{p}_{L_{p}(I)}:=t^{-d}\intop\limits_{{\rm\bf h}\in[-t,t]^{d}}\intop\limits_{I_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}r}\rm\bf h}}|\Delta_{\rm\bf h}^{r}(f,{\bf x})|^{p}\,d{\rm\bf x}\,d{\rm\bf h},

(12.3)

where
I_{r{\rm\bf h}}:=\{{\rm\bf x}:[{\rm\bf x},{\rm\bf x}+{r}{\rm\bf h}]\subset I\}
. This is called
the averaged modulus of smoothness of
f
. It is known that
w_{r}
is equivalent to
\widetilde{w}_{r}
(see §2 of
[
23
]
),

c\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,t)_{L_{p}(I)}\leq\omega_{r}(f,t)_{L_{p}(I)}\leq C\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,t)_{L_{p}(I)},\quad 0<t\leq 1,

(12.4)

where again the constants
c,C
depend only on
r
and
p
and can be taken absolute when
r
is fixed and
0<p_{0}\leq p\leq\infty
with
p_{0}
fixed. Thus, Whitney’s theorem holds with
\omega_{r}
replaced by
\widetilde{w}_{r}

cE_{r}(f,I)_{p}\leq\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,\ell_{I})_{L_{p}(I)}\leq CE_{r}(f,I)_{p}.

(12.5)

Before proceeding further, let us remark on why we introduce the averaged modulus of smoothness
\widetilde{w}_{r}
.
The advantage of
\widetilde{w}_{r}
over
\omega_{r}
is that
\widetilde{w}_{r}^{p}
it is set subadditive. We shall use this in the following form. Let
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
and let
{\cal I}
be a collection of subcubes of
\Omega
which form a partition of
\Omega
. Then, from the definition of
\widetilde{w}_{r}
. we have

\sum_{I\in{\cal I}}\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,t)^{p}_{L_{p}(I)}\leq\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,t)^{p}_{L_{p}(\Omega)},\quad t>0.

(12.6)

This same subadditivity holds when
{\cal I}
is replaced by
a set
{\cal T}
of simplices which form a partition of
\Omega
.

If
I\subset\Omega
is a cube, we say that
P_{I}\in{\cal P}_{r}
is a near best

approximation to

with constant

if

\|f-P_{I}\|_{L_{p}(I)}\leq\lambda E_{r}(f,I)_{p}.

(12.7)

It is shown in Lemma 3.2 of
[

]
that if
P_{I}\in{\cal P}_{r}
is near best in
L_{p}(I)
with constant
\lambda
, then it is also near best in

whenever
\overline{p}\geq p
,
i.e.,

\|f-P_{I}\|_{L_{\overline{p}}(I)}\leq C\lambda E_{r}(f,I)_{\overline{p}},

(12.8)

with the constant

depending only on
r,d
and
p
.
This constant does not depend on
I
or
\bar{p}
.

Another important remark is that any near best approximation

with constant

is near best on any larger cube

which contains

in the sense that

\|f-P_{I}\|_{L_{p}(J)}\leq C\lambda E_{r}(f,J)_{p},

(12.9)

where now

depends additionally on
|J|/|I|
, see Lemma 3.3 in
[
21
]
. Note that even though Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 3.3 in

are stated for polynomials of coordinate degree

In summary, a near best

containing

and larger values

.
We shall use these facts going forward.

12.3
Besov spaces and piecewise polynomial approximation

We now recall how membership in

guarantees a rate of approximation by piecewise polynomials. In actuality, the membership of a function in a Besov space can be characterized by its rate of approximation by piecewise polynomials. However, we only need the results that prove that a function in a Besov space can be approximated by piecewise polynomials with a certain accuracy
and therefore only concentrate on proving results of this type.

Recall that for
k\geq 0
, we denoted by

the partition of
\Omega

of side length

We then define

that are subordinate to the partition

if and only if the restriction of

on each

belongs to

.

If
I\in{\cal D}_{k}

be the polynomial of best

approximation to

and let us define

where

is the characteristic function of

is defined pointwise for each

We extend

to all of

The following lemma holds.

Lemma 12.1
.

Let

, and let

be any fixed integer strictly larger than

with

depending only on

and

.

Proof.

Consider

defined in (

).
From Whitney’s theorem, we have

where

If

where we used the subadditivity (

is a partition of

Here we have also used the equivalence (

and

and (

When

12.5.1
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In view of Theorem

, it is enough to show that for any fixed

, we have

\|S_{k}(f)-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p+d/\tau)},

with

here and for the remainder of the proof always denoting a constant not depending on

or

and

If

, we let

be the best

and

be the best

from

. Recall that by definition

when

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C2^{-kd}\sum_{j\geq k}2^{jd}\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,2^{-j})^{p}_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|^{p}_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-kd}\sum_{j\geq k}2^{jd}2^{-jsp}\leq C|f|^{p}_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-ksp}.

12.29
) and obtain

|S_{k}(f)-S_{k}^{*}(f)|^{\tau}\leq|S_{k}(f)-S_{k}^{*}(f)|^{\tau-p}|S_{k}(f)-S_{k}^{*}(f)|^{p}\leq C|f|^{\tau-p}_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p)(\tau-p)}|S_{k}(f)-S_{k}^{*}(f)|^{p}.

(12.33)

We now integrate both sides of this inequality and find that

\|S_{k}(f)-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|^{\tau}_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|^{\tau}_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p)(\tau-p)}2^{-ksp}\leq C|f|^{\tau}_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p+d/\tau)\tau}.

(12.34)

as desired.

Case
p\geq 1
:
From (
12.31
), we find for any
\beta\in(0,s-d/p)
that

\|f-P_{I}\|_{L_{\infty}(I)}\leq C\{\sum_{j\geq k}2^{-j\beta p^{\prime}}\}^{1/p^{\prime}}\{\sum_{j\geq k}[2^{j(d/p+\beta)}\widetilde{w}_{r}(f,2^{-j})^{p}_{L_{p}(I}]^{p}\}^{1/p},

(12.35)

where
p^{\prime}
is the conjugate index to
p
, i.e.,
1/p+1/p^{\prime}=1
. Arguing as in (
12.5.1
), we arrive at

\|S_{k}(f)-S_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-ks}.

(12.36)

This proves (
12.26
) for
\tau=p
. In other words, we know (
12.26
) for
\tau=p
and
\tau=\infty
.
We complete the proof for general
\tau\in[p,\infty)
as in (
12.34
). This completes the proof of the theorem.
\Box

12.5.2
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Since
B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
is embedded in
B_{p}^{s}(\Omega):=B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
, it is enough to prove the theorem when
f\in B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)
, i.e., when
q=\infty
. We know from Theorem
2.1
that

\|f-S^{*}_{k}(f)\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p+d/2)},\quad k\geq 0.

(12.37)

We define

R_{k}^{*}(f):=S_{k}^{*}(f)-S_{k-1}^{*}(f),\quad k\geq 1.

(12.38)

It follows from (
12.37
) that

\|R_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-d/p+d/2)},\quad k\geq 1.

(12.39)

Since on each simplex
T\in{\cal T}_{k}
,
the function
R_{k}^{*}(f)
is a polynomial
Q_{T}
from
{\cal P}_{r}
, we have

\|Q_{T}\|_{H^{1}(T)}\leq C2^{k}\|Q_{T}\|_{L_{2}(T)},\quad T\in{\cal T}_{k}.

(12.40)

Since the function
R_{k}^{*}(f)
is continuous on
\Omega
, we have (see Theorem 2.1.2 in
[
8
]
)

\|R_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{k}}\|R_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{H^{1}(T)}^{2}\leq C2^{2k}\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{k}}\|R_{k}^{*}(f)\|^{2}_{L_{2}(T)}=C2^{2k}\|R_{k}^{*}(f)\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

This gives

\|R_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C2^{k}\|R_{k}^{*}(f)\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq C|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-k(s-1-d/p+d/2)},\quad{\quad k\geq 1}.

(12.41)

Writing,

f-S_{k}^{*}(f)=\sum_{j>k}R_{j}^{*}(f),

and then applying
H^{1}
norms gives the theorem because

s-d/p+d/2>1
.

12.6
The proofs of the Theorems on optimal recovery

This subsection will be devoted to the proof of the theorems on optimal recovery stated in §
3
.

12.6.1
The proof of Theorem
3.1

It will be convenient to consider only the case
d\geq 2
to avoid changes in notation. We leave the case
d=1
to the reader. The proofs are divided into
two parts: a proof of the upper bounds and then a proof of the lower bounds.

Proof of the upper bounds in Theorem
3.1
:
A proof of (i) and (ii) can already be found in
[
58
]
, but for completeness, we give the following proof using the results of
§
2
. Recall our notation
B_{p}^{s}(\Omega):=B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
and the fact that all other
Besov spaces
B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
, with the same values for
p
and
s
, are contained in
B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)
. Therefore, it is enough to prove the
upper bounds when
{\cal B}=B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)
. To prove these upper bounds, we need to exhibit a set of
\widetilde{m}
data sites at which we achieve the claimed bound. We take these data sites as the tensor product grid points
G_{k,r}
of
\overline{\Omega}
where
r>\max(s,1)
. This will yield the upper bounds when
\widetilde{m}=(2^{k}r)^{d}
. The upper bounds for the other values of
\widetilde{m}
follow from the fact that
R_{\widetilde{m}}^{*}
is monotonically nonincreasing as
\widetilde{m}\to\infty

The cases (i) and (ii):
Let
{\cal T}_{k}={\cal T}_{k}(\Omega)
be the simplicial partition of
\Omega
into simplices
T
as in §
12.5
. We have shown in that section, that from the data
(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}
we can create a continuous piecewise polynomial

S=S_{k}^{*}(f)=\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{k}}L_{T}(f)\chi_{T},

(12.42)

where
L_{T}(f)
is a polynomial of order
r
(
L_{T}(f)\in{\cal P}_{r}
), gotten by interpolating
f
at the data points
T\cap G_{k,r}
. We have shown (see (
2.19
)) that if
\tau\geq p
then

\|f-S\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\preceq|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}2^{-kd\alpha_{\tau}}\preceq|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}m^{-\alpha_{\tau}}.

This bound also holds for
\tau<p
since then
B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)
is continuously embedded in
B_{\tau}^{s}(\Omega)
.
The function
S
only depends on the data
(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}}
. This shows that every function
\widetilde{f}\in{\cal F}_{\rm data}
is within an
L_{\tau}(\Omega)
distance
Cm^{-\alpha_{\tau}}
of
S
and hence the proof of the upper bound in the cases (i) and (ii) follow.

The case (iii):
This is similar to case (ii) except now we use Theorem
2.2
.

The case (iv):
We shall need the following lemma.

Lemma 12.4
.

Let
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, and let
\gamma=\gamma(d)
be defined as in
(
3.11
)
. Then, the space
L_{\gamma}(\Omega)
is embedded into
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and we have

\|\widetilde{f}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)},\quad\widetilde{f}\in L_{\gamma}(\Omega),

(12.43)

with
C
depending only on
d
. In the case
d=2
, then for any
\gamma(2)=1<\tau\leq\infty
, the space
L_{\tau}(\Omega)
embeds into
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and we have the bound

\|\widetilde{f}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\leq C\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)},\quad\widetilde{f}\in L_{\tau}(\Omega),

(12.44)

where
C
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Consider first the case
d\geq 3
, in which case
\gamma(d)>1
. Let
\gamma^{\prime}
be the dual index of
\gamma
, that is,
\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{d}
, so that
\gamma^{\prime}<\infty
. If
v\in U(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
, then by the Sobolev embedding theorem we have

\|v\|_{L_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim|v|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}:=\||\nabla v|\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},\quad v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(12.45)

where the last inequality is Poincare’s inequality.
From Hölder’s inequality, it follows that for any
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and any
\widetilde{f}\in L_{\gamma}(\Omega)
, we have

\intop\limits_{\Omega}\widetilde{f}v\leq\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}|v|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}.

(12.46)

This gives

\|\widetilde{f}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}:=\sup_{v\in U(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))}\intop\limits_{\Omega}\widetilde{f}v\lesssim\|\widetilde{f}\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)},\quad\widetilde{f}\in L_{\gamma}(\Omega),

(12.47)

which proves the lemma when
d\geq 3
.

When
d=2
, the Sobolev embedding fails at the endpoint since
\gamma^{\prime}=\infty
. However, for any
\tau>1
we have that
\tau^{\prime}<\infty
, where
\tau^{\prime}
is the dual index to
\tau
. In this case we have a compact Sobolev embedding

\|v\|_{L_{\tau^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\tau^{\prime}}\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

(12.48)

where one can show that the constant
C_{\tau^{\prime}}=C\tau^{\prime}=C\tau/(\tau-1)
for an absolute constant
C
. For completeness, we sketch this argument. Given a function
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, we consider a multiscale decomposition of
f

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(f_{n}-f_{n-1}),

(12.49)

where
f_{n}
denotes a piecewise linear interpolation of
f
on a triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{n}
made of
C2^{2n}
triangles of diameter
c2^{-n}
(here we set
f_{-1}=0
). We use a standard polynomial interpolation bound and scaling argument, combined with the fact that the linear polynomials are finite dimensional (essentially the Bramble-Hilbert Lemma) to see that

\begin{split}\|f_{n}-f_{n-1}\|^{\tau^{\prime}}_{L_{\tau^{\prime}}(\Omega)}=\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{n-1}}\|f_{n}-f_{n-1}\|^{\tau^{\prime}}_{L_{\tau^{\prime}}(T)}&\leq C2^{2n({\tau^{\prime}}/2-1)}\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{n-1}}\|f_{n}-f_{n-1}\|^{\tau^{\prime}}_{L_{2}(T)}\\
&\leq C2^{2n({\tau^{\prime}}/2-1)}2^{-{\tau^{\prime}}n}\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{n-1}}\|f\|^{\tau^{\prime}}_{H^{1}(T)}.\end{split}

Taking
{\tau^{\prime}}
-th roots, we then get using that the
\ell_{\tau^{\prime}}
-norm is bounded by the
\ell_{2}
-norm since
{\tau^{\prime}}\geq 2
that

\begin{split}\|f_{n}-f_{n-1}\|_{L_{\tau^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq C2^{-2n/{\tau^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{n-1}}\|f\|^{\tau^{\prime}}_{H^{1}(T)}\right)^{1/{\tau^{\prime}}}&\leq C2^{-2n/{\tau^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{T\in{\cal T}_{n-1}}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}(T)}\right)^{1/2}\\
&=C2^{-2n/{\tau^{\prime}}}\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\end{split}

where
C
is independent of
{\tau^{\prime}}
. Using the multiscale decomposition, this now implies that

\|f\|_{L_{\tau^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-2n/{\tau^{\prime}}}=\frac{C}{1-2^{-2/{\tau^{\prime}}}}\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C{\tau^{\prime}}\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

for a constant
C
independent of
{\tau^{\prime}}
. Utilizing (
12.48
), the same duality argument used for
d\geq 3
now completes the proof when
d=2
.
∎

We can now prove the upper bound in the case (iv) when error is measured in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
. Let us consider first the case where
d\geq 3
. Since the exponent
\alpha_{-1}
does not change over the
p\geq\gamma
, it is enough to consider the case when
p\leq\gamma
. Indeed, if
p>\gamma

then
U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)))
is contained in
U(B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{\gamma}(\Omega)))=:U(B_{\gamma}^{s}(\Omega))
. Therefore the upper bound follows once we have established the case
{\cal F}=U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)))
with
p\leq\gamma
. Similarly, when
p\leq\gamma
, then
U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)))\subset U(B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
.
Accordingly, in going further we only need to consider the case when
{\cal F}=U(B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))=:U(B_{p}^{s}(\Omega))
with
p\leq\gamma
.

Let
f
be any function in
{\cal F}:=U(B_{\infty}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))
with
s>d
and
p\leq\gamma
. We know that
f\in C(\Omega)
. Given the data
{\bf f}
,
we define
S
as in (
12.42
). From (
2.19
), we have

\|f-S\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}\preceq|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(I)}2^{-kd\alpha_{-1}}.

(12.50)

We now use Lemma
12.4
to obtain

\|f-S\|_{H^{-1}{\Omega}}\leq C\|f-S\|_{L_{\gamma}(\Omega)}\leq Cm^{-\alpha_{-1}}|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}.

(12.51)

The function
S
only depends on the data. This shows that every element in
{\cal F}_{\rm data}(f)
is within an
H^{-1}(\Omega)
distance
Cm^{-\alpha_{-1}}
of
S
and hence proves that the Chebyshev radius
of this set does not exceed
Cm^{-\alpha_{-1}}
. Since
f
was arbitrary we obtain the same bound for
R^{*}({\cal F})
.
This concludes
the proof of the upper bound in case (iv) when
d\geq 3
.

If
d=2
, we must slightly modify the above argument. Suppose first that
p>1
. In this case, we have

\|f-S\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\preceq|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(I)}2^{-kd\alpha_{-1}},

(12.52)

since the sampling numbers in
L_{p}
and
L_{1}
are the same in this case. Thus, Lemma
12.4
implies that (setting

)

\|f-S\|_{H^{-1}{\Omega}}\leq C_{p}\|f-S\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}m^{-\alpha_{-1}}|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}.

(12.53)

On the other hand, if

, we see that

\|f-S\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\preceq|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(I)}2^{-kd(\alpha_{-1}-1+1/\tau)}.

(12.54)

Applying Lemma
12.4
we get that

\|f-S\|_{H^{-1}{\Omega}}\leq C_{p}\|f-S\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C}{\tau-1}m^{-\alpha_{-1}}m^{1-1/\tau}|f|_{B_{p}^{s}(\Omega)}.

(12.55)

Since this is true for all
\tau>1
we finally optimize in
\tau
, which gives

\min_{\tau>0}\frac{m^{1-1/\tau}}{\tau-1}\leq C\log(m)

(12.56)

by setting
\tau=1+(\log(m))^{-1}
.

\Box

Proof of the lower bounds in Theorem
3.1
.

We shall now prove the lower bounds in Theorem
3.1
.
The proofs of lower bounds all take the following form. Suppose that
{\bf x}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are any
m
data sites. In order to prove a lower bound for
R^{*}({\cal F})_{X}
, we construct a function
\eta
in
{\cal F}
so that

(a)
\eta({\bf x}_{i})=0,\quad i=1,\dots,m
,

(b)
\|\eta\|_{X}\geq cm^{-\alpha}
,

where
\alpha
is the appropriate index for
X
.
Since both
\eta
and the zero function satisfy zero data the bound (b) gives the lower bound we want for
R^{*}({\cal F})_{X}
.

We proceed to explain how to construct an appropriate function
\eta
for each of the choices of
X
. Let
\varphi
be a smooth non-negative function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which vanishes outside

\Omega
and additionally satisfies

\|\varphi\|_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1\quad{\rm and}\quad\varphi(x)\geq 1/2,\quad x\in\Omega_{0},

(12.57)

where
\Omega_{0}:=[1/4,3/4]^{d}
. Of course, there are many such functions
\varphi
but to be more specific, and for
use further, we assume
\varphi({\bf x})=\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{d})
where
\phi
is a univariate function with these properties (for
d=1
).

We choose
\varphi
with these properties which has the smallest norm

M_{s,p,q}:=\|\varphi\|_{B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))}.

(12.58)

So,
\varphi
depends on
s,p,q
and
M_{s,p,q}
is a fixed constant since the parameters
s,p,q
are fixed.

Now consider any cube
I\subset\Omega
and let
\xi_{I}
be the smallest vertex of
I
and as usual
\ell_{I}
is its side length. We define the function

\varphi_{I}:=\varphi_{I,s,p,q}(x):=\ell_{I}^{s-\frac{d}{p}}\varphi(\ell_{I}^{-1}(x-\xi_{I})),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(12.59)

This function vanishes outside
I
and on its boundary. Moreover, one easily checks that for all cubes
I
, we have

\|\varphi_{I}\|_{B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega))}=M_{s,p,q}.

(12.60)

Here the norm is independent of
I
.

Because of the monotonicity of
R_{m}^{*}({\cal F})_{X}
, it is enough to prove the lower bound when
m=2^{kd}
as we now assume for
m
. Suppose that
m
sample points
X_{m}:=\{{\bf x}_{1},...,{\bf x}_{m}\}\in\Omega
are given. Consider the regular tensor product grid
G_{k+2,2}
with spacing
2^{-(k+2)}
. The number of cubes in this grid is equal to
4^{d}m
by construction and thus at least
(4^{d}-1)m
cubes do not contain any sample points in its interior. Denote by
{\cal I}:=\mathcal{I}(X_{m})
the set of cubes which do not contain any sample points, so that
|{\cal I}|\geq(4^{d}-1)m=C_{d}m
and the volume of each cube
I\in{\cal I}
is
|I|\geq c_{d}m^{-1}
.
For any cube
I\in{\cal I}
, we define

\eta_{I}:=M^{-1}\varphi_{I},\quad M=M_{s,p,q}.

(12.61)

It follows that each
\eta_{I}
is in
{\cal F}
. Also, each of the
\eta_{I}
vanishes at each of the data sites. We shall use the
\eta_{I}
to prove the lower bounds of Theorem
3.1
.

The lower bound in case (i):
Let us fix
s,p,q
and
consider the functions
\eta_{I}
defined by (
12.61
). We take
I
as any fixed cube from
{\cal I}
and define
\eta:=\eta_{I}
. Then
\eta
vanishes at each of the data sites. Also, it follows from the definition of
\eta
that with
M=M_{s,p,q}
, we have

\|\eta\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}=M^{-1}\|\varphi_{I}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}=M^{-1}\ell_{I}^{s-\frac{d}{p}}\geq cm^{-(\frac{s}{d}-\frac{1}{p})}=cm^{-\alpha_{C}}.

This gives that
R^{*}({\cal F})_{C(\Omega)}\geq cm^{-\alpha_{C}}
and thereby proves the lower bound in case (i).

The lower bound in case (ii):

We consider separately the cases
\tau<p
and
\tau\geq p
. If
\tau\geq p
, then we take
\eta=\eta_{I}
where
I
is any single fixed cube in
{\cal I}
. For
M=M_{s,p,q}
, we have from (
12.57
) that

\|\eta\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}=M^{-1}\|\varphi_{I}\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\geq cM^{-1}\ell_{I}^{s-\frac{d}{p}+\frac{d}{\tau}}\geq cm^{-(\frac{s}{d}-\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{\tau})}=cm^{-\alpha_{\tau}},

since
1/p-1/\tau\geq 0
.
It follows that
R^{*}({\cal F})_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\geq cm^{-\alpha_{\tau}}
in the case
\tau\geq p
.

Next consider the case
\tau<p<\infty
.
Since
U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{\infty}(\Omega)))\subset U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{p}(\Omega)))
, it is sufficient to prove the lower bound in the case
p=\infty
, i.e., when
{\cal F}=U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{\infty}(\Omega)))
with
0<q<\infty
arbitrary but fixed. We define

\eta=\kappa\sum_{I\in{\cal I}}\eta_{I},

(12.62)

with
\kappa
a fixed constant chosen so that

\eta\in U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{\infty}(\Omega)))
. Here,
\eta_{I}
is defined as in (
12.61
) with
p=\infty
. We next derive a lower bound for
\kappa
.
The terms
\eta_{I}
in (
12.62
) each have
L_{\infty}
norm bounded by
M2^{-ks}
and they have disjoint supports. Therefore,
\|\eta\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq M2^{-ks}
.
For each
r\geq 1
, these functions are in
C^{r}(\Omega)
with
C^{r}(\Omega)
norms not exceeding
M2^{-ks}2^{kr}
where
M
depends only on the choice of
r
. We take
r:=\lceil s\rceil+1
and find that the modulus of smoothness of
\eta
satisfies

\omega_{r}(\eta,t)_{\infty}\leq M\kappa 2^{-ks}\min(1,2^{kr}t^{r}).

(12.63)

Therefore, breaking the integral into the integral over
[0,2^{-k}]
and
(2^{-k},1]
we find that

\intop\limits_{0}^{1}[t^{-s}\omega_{r}(\eta,t)_{\infty}]^{q}\frac{dt}{t}\leq\kappa^{q}M^{q}2^{-kq(s-r)}\intop\limits_{0}^{2^{-k}}t^{(r-s)q}\frac{dt}{t}+\kappa^{q}M^{q}2^{-kqs}\intop\limits_{2^{-k}}^{1}t^{-sq}\frac{dt}{t}\leq\kappa^{q}C^{q},

(12.64)

with
C
a fixed constant.
This shows that we can take
\kappa\geq c
where
c
depends only on
s
. Since
\eta\geq\kappa 2^{-ks}

on a set of measure
\geq 1/2
, we have that
\|\eta\|_{L_{\tau}(\Omega)}\geq c2^{-ks}\geq cm^{-s/d}
which finishes the proof of the lower bound in case (ii).

The lower bound in case (iii):
By assumption we have
p\leq 2
. We take
\eta=\eta_{I}
for any fixed
I\in{\cal I}
. We estimate

\|\eta\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=M^{-1}\|\varphi_{I}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\geq cM^{-1}\ell_{I}^{s-1-\frac{d}{p}+\frac{d}{2}}\geq cm^{-(\frac{s}{d}-\frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{2})},

(12.65)

which proves the lower bound in (iii) for
R^{*}({\cal F})_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
.

The lower bound in case (iv):
We consider first the case where
p\leq\gamma
, where
\gamma
is given by (
3.11
). In this case, we choose
\eta=\eta_{I}

where
I
is any cube in
{\cal I}
. The function
\eta
vanishes at all of the data sites. By construction, we have that
\eta\in{\cal F}
and the lower bound

\eta(x)\geq cm^{-\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{p}},\quad x\in I_{0},

(12.66)

where
I_{0}=[\xi_{I}-\ell_{I}/4,\xi_{I}+1/4]^{d}
is the subcube corresponding to
\Omega_{0}
in
I
(see (
12.57
)).

We want to bound the
H^{-1}(\Omega)
norm of
\eta
from below. For this,
we choose a function
v
from the unit ball of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

which vanishes outside of
I
and is large on
I_{0}
. Namely, we take

v(x):=c\ell_{I}^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\varphi(\ell_{I}^{-1}(x-\xi_{I})).

(12.67)

where now
c
is chosen as a constant depending only on
d
so that
\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=1
.
As in the case for
\eta_{I}
, we have

v(x)\geq c\ell_{I}^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\geq cm^{-\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{2}},\quad x\in I_{0}.

(12.68)

It follows that

\|\eta\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\geq\intop\limits_{\Omega}\eta(x)v(x)\,dx=\intop\limits_{I_{0}}\eta(x)v(x)\,dx\geq cm^{-\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{p}}m^{-\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{2}}|I_{0}|.

(12.69)

Since
|I_{0}|\geq cm^{-1}
the right side of (
12.69
) is

cm^{-\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{2}}=cm^{-\frac{s}{d}+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{\gamma}}.

This proves that
R^{*}({\cal F})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\geq cm^{-\alpha_{-1}}
in the case
p\leq\gamma
since
1/p-1/\gamma\geq 0
.

We finally consider the case when
p>\gamma
. This is handled in a similar way to case (ii). Namely, we can assume
{\cal F}=U(B_{q}^{s}(L_{\infty}(\Omega)))
where
s,q
are fixed. We take
\eta
as in (
12.62
). We know this function is in
{\cal F}
and vanishes at all of the data sites. To provide a lower bound the
H^{-1}(\Omega)
norm of
\eta
,

v(x)=c\varphi(x),

(12.70)

where
c
is a constant chosen so that
\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=1
. By construction, we have
v(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\Omega
and
v(x)\geq c
for
x\in\Omega_{0}
. Let
{\cal I}_{0}
be the set of
I\in{\cal I}
such that

I\subset\Omega_{0}
. Since
\eta
is also non-negative, we estimate

\|\eta\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\geq\intop\limits_{\Omega}\eta(x)v(x)dx\geq\intop\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\eta(x)v(x)dx\geq\intop\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\eta(x)dx\geq\kappa\sum_{I\in{\cal I}_{0}}\intop\limits_{I}\eta_{I}\geq c2^{-ks}2^{-kd}\#({\cal I}_{0}),

(12.71)

because
\kappa\geq c
and
\eta_{I}\geq 2^{-ks}

on
I_{0}
.
We need to estimate the cardinality of the set
{\cal I}_{0}
. Observe that the number of cubes
I
in the original grid which are contained in
\Omega_{0}
is
2^{-d}(4^{d}m)=2^{d}m
. Since
\#({\cal I})\geq(4^{d}-1)m
, we see that

\#({\cal I}_{0})\geq 2^{d}m-m=(2^{d}-1)m.

Since
m=2^{kd}
, placing this lower bound of
\#({\cal I}_{0})
into (
12.71
), we find that

\|\eta\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\geq cm^{-\frac{s}{d}}.

(12.72)

This implies that
R^{*}({\cal F})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\geq cm^{-\alpha_{-1}}
in the case
p>\gamma
since
1/p-1/\gamma<0
and therefore completes the proof of the lower bound in (iv). This completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
\Box

12.6.2
The proof of Theorem
3.2

We first prove (ii) which also gives the upper bound in (
3.19
). From the definition of the trace norm, we have

\|g-\overline{S}_{k}(g)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\|v-S_{k}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C[r2^{k}]^{-t+1}\leq Cm^{-\beta},

(12.73)

where the next to last inequality uses the estimate (
2.20
) and also the fact that
m\asymp 2^{k(d-1)}
. This proves (ii).

It follows that

R_{m}(g)_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq Cm^{-\beta},

(12.74)

for the values of
m
that equal
2d[(r-1)2^{k}]^{d-1}
. Since
g\in{\cal G}
was arbitrary, we obtain

R^{*}_{m}({\cal G})_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq Cm^{-\beta},

(12.75)

for the above values of
m
. From the monotonicity of
R_{m}^{*}
we obtain (
12.75
) for all
m
.
Thus, we have proven the upper bound in (
3.19
).

We next prove the lower inequalities in (
3.19
). From the monotonicity of
R_{m}^{*}({\cal G})_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}
, it is enough to prove this lower bound for
m=2^{k(d-1)}-1
whenever
k
is any non-negative integer. The following reasoning is the same as in the proof of the lower inequalities in Theorem
3.1
.

Let
{\cal Z}:=\{{\bf z}_{1},\dots,{\bf z}_{m}\}
,
m=2^{k(d-1)}-1
, be any proposed set of data sites on
\partial\Omega
. Let
F:=\{{\bf x}\in\Omega:\ {\bf x}\cdot e_{1}=0
,
e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, be the face of
\partial\Omega
corresponding to the points
{\bf x}\in\overline{\Omega}
whose first coordinate is equal to zero. Consider the set
{\cal D}_{k}(F)
of (
d-1
dimensional) dyadic cubes of
F
. Since there are
2^{k(d-1)}
cubes in

{\cal D}_{k}(F)
, it follows that there is a
\overline{J}\in{\cal D}_{k}(F)
such that
\overline{J}
contains none of the data sites from
{\cal Z}
in its interior. We will now construct an appropriate function
\eta\in{\cal G}
which vanishes at
each of the data sites.

Let
J
be the
d
dimensional cube in
{\cal D}_{k}(\overline{\Omega})
which has
\overline{J}
as a face and let
\varphi_{J}
be the function defined in (
12.59
) with the parameters
\bar{s},d,\bar{p},\bar{q}
. We know that

M^{-1}\varphi_{J}\in U(B_{\bar{q}}^{\bar{s}}(L_{\bar{p}}(\Omega))
when
M:=M_{\bar{s},\bar{p},\bar{q}}
.
If
\xi_{J}^{\prime}
is the
center of
J
, then
the point
\xi_{J}^{\prime}-(2^{-k-1},0,\dots,0)
is the center of
\overline{J}
.

We now define

v({\bf x}):=M^{-1}\varphi_{J}({\bf x}-(2^{-k-1},0,\dots,0)),\quad x\in\Omega,

(12.76)

which is also a function in
U(B_{\bar{q}}^{\bar{s}}(L_{\bar{p}}(\Omega))
. Hence, the function

\eta:=T_{\partial\Omega}v

(12.77)

is in our model class
{\cal G}
and
\eta
vanishes at all of the data sites
{\cal Z}
.

We now show that for the intrinsic
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
norm we have

\|\eta\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\geq cm^{-\beta},

(12.78)

with
c
not depending on
m
. This will prove the lower bound we seek.
First consider the function
\varphi
defined in (
12.57
). Let
e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Because of the tensor product structure of
\varphi
described after (
12.57
), the trace
\eta_{0}
of
\varphi
onto the hyperplane
{\bf x}\cdot e_{1}=1/2
is the
d-1
version of
\varphi
. We define

\overline{M}:=|\eta_{0}|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}>0.

(12.79)

By a change of variables and the fact that
m=2^{k(d-1)}-1
, it follows that

|\eta|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}=\overline{M}M^{-1}2^{-k(\bar{s}-d/{\bar{p}})}2^{k/2}2^{-k(d-1)/2}\geq cm^{-\beta},

(12.80)

where
c>0
does not depend on
m
. This proves the lower bound and completes the proof of the theorem.

\Box

12.7
The proof of Theorem
7.2
in the case
d=2

In this section we provide the proof of Theorem
7.2
in when
d=2
. We discuss two cases.

Case 1:

p=1

Let
\varepsilon>0
. According to Lemma
12.4
, Lemma
6.1
and Theorem
6.3
, we have

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f+\Delta v\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}\|f+\Delta v\|_{L_{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}\|f+\Delta v\|^{*}_{L_{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|^{*}_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\|f+\Delta v\|_{{\cal B}}\varepsilon^{-1}\widetilde{m}^{1-1/(1+\varepsilon)}\widetilde{m}^{-\frac{s}{2}}+\|g-Tr(v)\|_{Tr(\overline{{\cal B}})}\overline{m}^{-(\bar{s}-1)}\right].

Optimizing
\varepsilon
as in Lemma
12.4

gives a choice of
\varepsilon=[\log(\widetilde{m})]^{-1}
, for which

\varepsilon^{-1}\widetilde{m}^{1-1/(1+\varepsilon)}\lesssim\log(\widetilde{m})

and thus we have

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\log(\widetilde{m})\|f+\Delta v\|^{*}_{L_{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|^{*}_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\|f+\Delta v\|_{{\cal B}}\log(\widetilde{m})\widetilde{m}^{-\frac{s}{2}}+\|g-Tr(v)\|_{Tr(\overline{{\cal B}})}\overline{m}^{-(\bar{s}-1)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle{\cal L}^{*}(v)+\left[1+\|v\|_{{\cal U}}\right]{\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\widetilde{m},\overline{m}).

Case 2:

1<p\leq\infty

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f+\Delta v\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle C(p)\|f+\Delta v\|_{L_{p}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left[C(p)\|f+\Delta v\|^{*}_{L_{p}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|^{*}_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)}\right].

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[C(p)\|f+\Delta v\|_{{\cal B}}\widetilde{m}^{-\frac{s}{2}}+\|g-Tr(v)\|_{Tr(\overline{{\cal B}})}\overline{m}^{-(\bar{s}-1)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left[C(p)\|f+\Delta v\|^{*}_{L_{p}(\Omega)}+\|g-Tr(v)\|^{*}_{H^{1/2}(\Omega)}\right]+\left[1+\|v\|_{{\cal U}}\right]{\cal R}_{{\cal U}}(\widetilde{m},\overline{m}).

Notice that in this case we could have chosen
{\cal L}^{*}
as in the case of
d=3
with
\gamma=p
and obtained the optimal recovery rate for this class. However, this choice would result in a loss functional depending on
\cal F
.
