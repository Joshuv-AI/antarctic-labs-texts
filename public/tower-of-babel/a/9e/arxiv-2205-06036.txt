Efficient and Training-Free Control of Language Generation
Shangda Wu, Maosong Sun

Abstract
In recent years, there has been a growing interest in the development of language models capable of generating text with controllable attributes. While several approaches have been proposed, many of these methods require condition-specific data or significant computational resources. In this study, we propose a novel method called Gamma Sampling , which enables controllable language generation without the need for any training data and maintains a fast generation speed. Gamma Sampling incorporates attribute-related information into the sampling process, effectively guiding the language model to produce text with desired attributes. Our experimental results demonstrate that Gamma Sampling , when applied to GPT2, outperforms representative baselines in terms of diversity, attribute relevance, and overall quality of the generated samples.



1
Introduction

The emergence of large language models (LMs) has been facilitated by the use of large-scale text data obtained through web crawling. However, while these models can produce high-quality texts, there is limited control over the attributes of the generated output. This poses a significant challenge when utilizing unconditional LMs in scenarios that demand precise control over the generated text. Therefore, the field of controllable language generation, which provides greater control over the output of LMs, has become increasingly relevant in practical applications.

Controllable language generation has made significant strides in recent years (
Weng 2021
). However, it remains an open question what the ideal method for controlling the attributes of the generated language is (
Mireshghallah et al. 2022
,
Yang and Klein 2021
,
Meng et al. 2022
). While training a conditional LM from scratch (
Keskar et al. 2019
) or fine-tuning an existing LM (
Ziegler et al. 2019
,
Xu et al. 2021
) have shown promising results, these approaches often require condition-specific data, making them less applicable to unconditional LMs. Alternatively, data-free methods have been proposed (
Shin et al. 2020
,
Zou et al. 2021
,
Ghazvininejad et al. 2017
,
Pascual et al. 2020
,
Lu et al. 2021
), but they may have limitations in terms of steerability or computational requirements.

This paper introduces a novel approach, called
Gamma Sampling
, that allows for the efficient and training-free control of LMs. The development of
Gamma Sampling
is inspired by the concept of gamma correction (
Applebaum 1952
), which is a non-linear operation commonly used for encoding and decoding luminance in video or still image systems.
Gamma Sampling
is founded on the principle that certain attributes of the generated text are closely associated with the presence of specific tokens, and that by adjusting the probability of these attribute-related tokens, the attributes of the generated text can be controlled. Our main contributions are as follows:

Gamma Sampling
is a novel approach for controlling LMs that do not require any training data, and therefore can be readily applied to any LM.

Results indicate that the proposed method generally outperforms all baselines in terms of diversity, attribute relevance, and overall quality, across all sizes of GPT2 (i.e. Small, Medium, Large, and XL).

2
Background

The controllable language generation involves the modelling of a probabilistic model
p(t|a)
, which entails generating text
t
, based on a specified attribute
a
. A variety of approaches exist for achieving controllable language generation, each with its advantages and limitations.

Conditional Language Model (CLM)
CLMs are a type of LM that is designed to generate text based on certain conditions derived from metadata. However, a large amount of condition-specific data is necessary to train a CLM effectively, and the computational cost of training the entire model from scratch can be significant, making it a resource-intensive task. Additionally, CLMs such as CTRL (
Keskar et al. 2019
) struggle in controlling the generation of undesirable output, such as detoxification and anti-degeneration (
Gehman et al. 2020
,
Ma et al. 2020
).

Fine-tuned Language Model (FTLMs)
FTLMs are an effective solution for balancing training costs and generation quality, as demonstrated by recent research (
Ziegler et al. 2019
,
Xu et al. 2021
). These models rely on pre-existing large LMs, with the weights fine-tuned for specific tasks or attributes. Fine-tuning can be limited to the top or additional layers, or introduced through discriminators (
Krause et al. 2021
,
Liu et al. 2021
). However, it is important to note that FTLMs still require condition-specific data. Furthermore, while models such as PPLM (
Dathathri et al. 2020
) that involve combining multiple small attribute models with a large LM have shown promising results, they may lead to decreased computational efficiency due to the need for multiple passes at every decoding step.

Prompting
Prompt engineering (
Shin et al. 2020
,
Zou et al. 2021
) has become an increasingly prominent training-free approach. By providing carefully crafted prompts, prompt engineering directs large LMs (e.g., ChatGPT
1
1

1

https://chat.openai.com/
) towards solving specific tasks and generating more accurate and coherent outputs. However, it is noteworthy that the results of these tasks are significantly influenced by small variations in the prompts (
Kojima et al. 2022
).

Guided Decoding
Guided decoding is a method that can be used to incorporate attribute-related information into the score function for language generation at the decoding time, allowing for the modification of candidate ranking scores. This method has been explored in several studies (
Ghazvininejad et al. 2017
,
Ghosh et al. 2017
,
Pascual et al. 2020
,
Lu et al. 2021
,
Liu et al. 2021
,
Pascual et al. 2021
). However, a significant challenge associated with guided decoding is the off-distribution issues when control strengths are too strong. Another drawback is the increase in time cost with the number of controlled tokens, which can result in long inference times when there are many controlled tokens. In contrast, Section 3.2 presents the proposed method,
Gamma Sampling
, which can mitigate or eliminate these disadvantages.

As illustrated in Fig.
1
, it is evident that to achieve controllable generation, LMs must be supplied with attribute-related information. The first two approaches rely on extensive training of LMs to acquire attribute-related information from vast amounts of data. Conversely, the latter two believe that large LMs have already acquired sufficient information from data and only require the introduction of attribute-related information into the input or decoding to be implemented for various downstream tasks.

3
Methodology

This section provides an overview of gamma correction in Section 3.1, and goes on to present an in-depth analysis of the technical aspects of
Gamma Sampling
in Section 3.2. Finally, Section 3.3 introduces the practical application of
Gamma Sampling
to define various controllable attributes.

3.1
Gamma Correction

Gamma correction (
Applebaum 1952
) is a well-known technique that was initially to compensate for the non-linearity of the input-output characteristic of Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) displays. Since then, this technique has been widely used for a variety of purposes, including adjusting the saturation of images. This approach applies a power function to all pixels of the input image, as the human perception of brightness is known to be non-linear (
Neri 2009
). In its simplest form, this correction technique is mathematically expressed as a power-law function:

\displaystyle V_{out}=AV_{in}^{\gamma},

(1)

where the luminance value
V_{in}\in[0,1]
is raised to the power
\gamma\in[0,+\infty)
and multiplied by the constant
A
(in the common case,
A=1
) to get the output value
V_{out}\in[0,1]
.

3.2
Gamma Sampling

3.2.1
Basic Principles

In linguistic research, it has been observed that the frequency of any word in common corpora is inversely proportional to its ranking in the frequency table (
Powers 1998
). Given this observation, it is reasonable to employ non-linear processes for tuning the probabilities of attribute-related tokens. Temperature sampling (
Dabre and Fujita 2021
), as one example, is also based on a non-linear operation to scale the probability distribution.

The non-linear nature of languages indicates that gamma correction may also be used to control text attributes. In this context, the manipulation of text attributes is referred to as
Gamma Sampling
, with its fundamental operation formalized as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{A}_{out}}

\displaystyle=p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}^{tan(\frac{\pi\Gamma}{2})},

(2)

where
p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}
is the sum of the input probabilities of all tokens in the attribute-related token set
\mathcal{A}
,
p_{\mathcal{A}_{out}}
is the output one, and
\Gamma\in[0,1]
is the control strength. As shown in Fig.
2
, the control strength
\Gamma
determines the degree to which the probabilities of these tokens increase or decrease, and the increase and decrease in probabilities occur over equal length ranges. When
\Gamma=0.5
, there is no change in the probability distribution, while when
\Gamma<0.5
, the probabilities of the attribute-related tokens increase and vice versa.

It is important to consider the potential risks of using
Gamma Sampling
on its own. When setting
\Gamma
to a very low level, even tokens with low probability may experience a significant increase in their probabilities, which may result in off-distribution. Therefore, it is advisable to use either top-
k
sampling
Fan et al. 2018
or nucleus sampling
Holtzman et al. 2020
before
Gamma Sampling
to eliminate these unreliable attribute-related tokens.

Gamma Sampling
is an innovative approach that sets it apart from other methods. Notably, it operates on probability distributions rather than the logits (
Liu et al. 2021
), allowing for top-
k
or nucleus sampling to be applied before it, thereby avoiding over-control strengths that may result in off-distribution. Additionally,
Gamma Sampling
eliminates the need for customization of the score function (
Ghazvininejad et al. 2017
), additional discriminators (
Krause et al. 2021
), or the use of specific external models (
Pascual et al. 2021
). This method requires only minimal computation at the decoding time, and therefore is computationally efficient and independent of the number of controlled tokens, resulting in a nearly constant time cost.

3.2.2
Controlling Single Attribute

The use of
Gamma Sampling
in fine-grained control involves the scaling of probabilities associated with attribute-related tokens. To achieve optimal steerability, certain post-processing steps must be taken, which include the ability to scale the sum of probabilities of attribute-related tokens to 0 or 1, maintaining the ratio of probabilities between tokens of the same type (i.e., attribute-related or non-attribute-related), and ensuring the validity of the output probability distribution. The process of controlling a single attribute using
Gamma Sampling
can be formalized as follows:

\begin{aligned} p_{\mathcal{A}_{out}}&=p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}^{tan(\frac{\pi\Gamma}{2})},\\
p_{a_{out}}&=p_{a_{in}}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{A}_{out}}}{p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}},\quad\forall a\in\mathcal{A},\\
p_{n_{out}}&=p_{n_{in}}\cdot(1+\frac{p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}-p_{\mathcal{A}_{out}}}{p_{\backslash\mathcal{A}_{in}}}),\quad\forall n\notin\mathcal{A},\\
\end{aligned}

(3)

where
p_{a_{in}}
is the input probability of an attribute-related token
a
,
p_{a_{out}}
is the output one, and the same goes for every non-attribute-related token
n\in\backslash\mathcal{A}
. Eq.
3
first modifies the sum of the input probabilities of all attribute-related tokens
p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}
, then rescales each attribute-related token
a
by the ratio of
p_{\mathcal{A}_{out}}
to
p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}
, and finally rescales the probability of each non-attribute-related token
n
by the ratio of the difference between
p_{\mathcal{A}_{in}}
and
p_{\mathcal{A}_{out}}
to the sum of the input probabilities of all non-attribute-related tokens
p_{\backslash\mathcal{A}_{in}}
. Fig.
3
illustrates a simple example of language generation with
Gamma Sampling
.

3.2.3
Controlling Multiple Attributes

In situations where multiple attributes
\mathcal{A}^{1:T}=\{\mathcal{A}^{1},\mathcal{A}^{2},...,\mathcal{A}^{T}\}
need to be controlled in a sequential manner, if simply utilizing the equation defined in Eq.
3
, the earlier modifications may be overridden by later ones. To mitigate this issue, supplementary computations have been introduced to complement Eq.
3
. When controlling the
t
-th attribute
\mathcal{A}^{t}
, the following processes are performed:

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}^{t}\!&=\!\mathcal{A}^{1}\cup\mathcal{A}^{2}\cup...\cup\mathcal{A}^{t-1}\!-\!\mathcal{A}^{t},\\
p_{\mathcal{A}^{t}_{out}}\!&=\!p_{\mathcal{A}^{t}_{in}}^{tan(\frac{\pi\Gamma^{t}}{2})}\cdot(1\!-\!p_{\mathcal{F}^{t}_{in}})^{1-tan(\frac{\pi\Gamma^{t}}{2})},\\
p_{a_{out}}\!&=\!p_{a_{in}}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{A}^{t}_{out}}}{p_{\mathcal{A}^{t}_{in}}},\quad a\in\mathcal{A}^{t},\\
p_{n_{out}}\!&=\!p_{n_{in}}\cdot(1\!+\!\frac{p_{\mathcal{A}^{t}_{in}}-p_{\mathcal{A}^{t}_{out}}}{p_{\backslash(\mathcal{A}^{t}\cup\mathcal{F}^{t})_{in}}}),\quad n\notin\mathcal{A}^{t}\cup\mathcal{F}^{t},\\
\end{aligned}

(4)

where
\Gamma^{t}
represents the control strength for the attribute set
\mathcal{A}^{t}
, and the frozen set
\mathcal{F}^{t}
consists of attribute-related tokens that have been modified in previous turns but not in
\mathcal{A}^{t}
. Like Eq.
3
, the sum of token probabilities after modification in Eq.
4
remains 1. The key distinction between the two equations is the inclusion of the frozen set
\mathcal{F}^{t}
, where token probabilities remain unchanged during the
t
-th turn.

3.3
Controllable Attributes

In our empirical research and analysis, we identified six controllable attributes at the token level. These attributes were utilized in Section 4 to explore their potential impact on generated text.

Sentence Length
is an objective text attribute that is directly linked to ending marks such as full stops, question marks, and exclamation marks. By manipulating the frequency of these ending marks, the average sentence length can be effectively altered.

Perfect Ending
is a dynamic tuning strategy of
Sentence Length
. When the number of generated words exceeds a certain threshold, the value of
\Gamma
decreases linearly with the increase in the number of generated words. This process is designed to ensure that the text does not end abruptly.

Topic Relevance
is controlled by selecting words that are most related to a user-given topic word, as calculated by the cosine similarity of word embedding in GPT2. In our specific implementation, we selected the top 100 tokens.

Sentiment Polarity
is achieved by adjusting the frequency of words in two wordlists, each containing roughly 1000 words sourced from the internet. One list is comprised of negative words, while the other contains positive words.

Repetition Decreasing
, similar to penalized sampling (
Keskar et al. 2019
), prevents degeneration by decreasing the probabilities of tokens that have recently been generated.

Relatedness Increasing
is a dynamic tuning strategy of
Topic Relevance
that aims to improve the coherence of text by encouraging the generation of words that are closely related to recently presented nouns labelled by NLTK (
Keskar et al. 2019
).

By defining the attributes of interest at the token level,
Gamma Sampling
allows for effective and targeted manipulation of the generated text.

4
Experiments

To evaluate
Gamma Sampling
, a series of experiments were conducted. The metrics used in this evaluation are described in Section 4.1. In Section 4.2, we validate the fine-grained control achieved by
Gamma Sampling
and examine the impact of varying degrees of control strength. Finally, we conduct a comparative study in Section 4.3. The findings of this section better the understanding of
Gamma Sampling
and its potential applications in various domains.

4.1
Metrics

The evaluation of generated texts involves assessing four key aspects: fluency, diversity, attribute relevance, and overall quality. A comprehensive evaluation was conducted by utilizing both automatic metrics and human annotators sourced from Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. To ensure the accuracy of the metrics, the text prompts, which include attribute information, were excluded from the experimental data, while the prefixes were retained.

Fluency
The fluency of the generated text is measured by GPT2 in different sizes, based on perplexity (
Vinyals and Le 2015
). A higher
PPL
, indicates that it is less likely that GPT2 will generate such text. It is worth noting that a lower PPL does not necessarily imply better quality, as degenerated text usually has a very low PPL, which is deemed to be of poor quality.

Diversity
The diversity of the generated samples is evaluated using
DIST-N
scores (
Li et al. 2016
). A higher value of DIST-N indicates a higher proportion of distinct N-grams.

Attribute Relevance
We focused on evaluating three types of controllable generation:
Sentence Length
,
Topic Relevance
and
Sentiment Polarity
. The average sentence length (
ASL
) measures the average number of words per sentence. The latter two are evaluated using two metrics based on external classifiers
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/facebook/bart-large-mnli
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest
: external classifier accuracy (
ECA
) and external classifier confidence (
ECC
). The higher the ECA and ECC, the more salient the external classifier considers the generated text to hold a certain topic/sentiment.

Overall Quality
The overall quality of machine-generated text was evaluated based on the TOEFL Independent Writing Rubrics
4
4

4

https://www.ets.org/content/dam/ets-org/pdfs/toefl/toefl-ibt-writing-rubrics.pdf
, which are widely used to assess the English writing skills of non-native speakers. However, additional considerations were taken into account to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the machine-generated text. Specifically, we do not take into account whether the text itself is finished or contains factual errors, but extensive repetition, completely off-topic, or obvious common sense errors would result in a low score. This revised set of criteria is TOEFL Writing Rubrics for Machine-generated Text (
T4MT
). MTurkers are asked to rate each generated text on a scale of 0 to 5 (nonsense to advanced) based on T4MT. More information about this scoring criterion can be found in
Appendix A
.

In these experiments, we evaluated GPT2-Small on
Sentence Length
. The generation process was initiated with the prefix "The issue focused" and the model was instructed to generate the subsequent 100 tokens. To guarantee that each sample contained at least one ending mark, we implemented
Perfect Ending
. This involved linearly decreasing the value of
\Gamma
to 0 as the number of generated tokens increased, starting from the 80th token. Representative samples of
Sentence Length
can be found in
Appendix B
.

We first investigated fine-grained control using
Gamma Sampling
. Specifically, 100 samples were generated from GPT2 for each of ten different values of
\Gamma
, resulting in a total of 1000 samples. Our results show a clear correlation between
\Gamma
and the ASL of the generated text. Fig.
4
illustrates that ASL increases significantly with increasing
\Gamma
, from an average of 12.98 words/sentence at
\Gamma=0.1
to an average of 95.68 words/sentence at

\Gamma=0.5
. This result reveals a linear relationship between ASL and
\Gamma
, suggesting that the probability scaling of attribute-related tokens has a non-linear impact on the corresponding attribute. Additionally, without
Perfect Ending

\Gamma=1.0
.

We then assessed the risk of over-control strength. To achieve this, we conducted an experiment wherein we selected three representative values of
\Gamma
(0.1, 0.5, and 0.9) and applied nucleus sampling with varying top-
p
(0.8, 0.9 and 1.0). We generated 100 samples in each of the nine settings, and as we hypothesized, we found that increasing the probability of controlled tokens led to a significant decrease in the quality of the generated text, as evidenced by Table 1. With a top-
p
value of 1.0 and a
\Gamma
value of 0.1, the PPL of the generated samples reached an astonishingly high value of 6844.1, indicating that it would be extremely difficult for GPT2-Small to generate such text. However, we observed that reducing the top-
p
value effectively mitigated the negative impact of
Gamma Sampling
on the quality of generated text. For example, with a top-
p
value of 0.8 and a
\Gamma
value of 0.1, the PPL dropped dramatically to 16.53. In contrast, the impact of drastically reducing the probability of controlled tokens (
\Gamma=0.9
) was much less significant.

In conclusion, the experimental results demonstrate that
Gamma Sampling
can effectively modify the attributes of generated text. To mitigate the negative effects of over-control strength, pre-sampling should be applied.

The results presented in Table 2 indicate that the PPL of GS-generated text is lower than that of GPT2, which suggests that the former produces less complex text. However, the GS-generated text displays the lowest diversity as measured by DIST-N. Upon manual examination, we found that GS-generated text tended to suffer from a higher degree of degeneration compared to GPT2-generated text, with a higher likelihood of repeating words with similar semantics to the topic words. It indicates that using only

In contrast, we found that GSM produced highly diverse text as measured by DIST-N, while maintaining a high attribute relevance as indicated by the ECA and ECC. Conclusions

In this paper, we introduce a new technique called
Gamma Sampling
, which provides a highly efficient method for fine-grained control of LMs at the token level. The technique involves incorporating attribute-related information at the decoding time and enables the pre-definition or selection of attributes by the LMs themselves without requiring any additional training data. Experimental results demonstrate that
Gamma Sampling
is highly effective in controlling GPT2, producing improved objective and subjective metrics while maintaining a fast generation speed. Additionally, our method has been shown to be computationally efficient and able to avoid off-distribution issues. Since
Gamma Sampling
is a training-free approach, it is easily adaptable to any LM and has the potential to enhance the efficiency and user-friendliness of controllable language generation.

Limitations

While
Gamma Sampling
has been shown to be an effective method for fine-grained controlling of LMs, there is still room for improvement and further research in this area.

As a guided decoding method,
Gamma Sampling
relies on pre-defined or selected attribute-related tokens to guide the generation process. This means that the quality of the generated text is dependent on the quality of the selection of tokens. Furthermore, the attribute-related tokens must be selected by humans or LMs themselves, which may be prone to errors or bias. Improving the reliability and robustness of attribute information is an important future direction.

In addition,
Gamma Sampling
is currently only able to control attributes at the token level. While this allows for fine-grained control, it may be limited in its ability to control more complex or abstract attributes (e.g., writing style). Developing methods to control these types of attributes is a key area for future research.

Ethics Statement

As with any technology, there are potential ethical considerations to be taken into account with the use of
Gamma Sampling
. One of the issues we have identified in conducting experiments is the potential for bias in the attribute information provided to guide the generation process. If the attribute information is provided by humans, it may be prone to bias based on the human annotator’s own beliefs, experiences, or stereotypes. On the other hand, if the attribute information is selected by the LMs themselves, it may be like an echo chamber, which further amplifies any biases present in the training data used to train the LMs. For this reason, we excluded topics where models could generate offensive text (i.e.,
POLITICS
and
RELIGION
).

To mitigate this risk, we recommend that attribute-related tokens be carefully curated and checked for bias before being used in the generation process. In addition, it is important to ensure that the selection of attribute-related tokens is diverse and inclusive, to avoid perpetuating stereotypes or discrimination.
