U -Gibbs measure rigidity for partially hyperbolic endomorphisms on surfaces
Marisa Cantarino and Bruno Santiago

Abstract
. We prove that, for a C 2 C^{2} partially hyperbolic endomorphism of the 2-torus which is strongly transitive, given an ergodic u u -Gibbs measure that has positive center Lyapunov exponent and has full support, then either the map is special (has only one unstable direction per point), or the measure is the unique absolutely continuous invariant measure. We can apply this result in many settings, in particular obtaining uniqueness of u u -Gibbs measures for every non-special perturbation of irreducible linear expanding maps of the torus with simple spectrum. This gives new open sets of partially hyperbolic systems displaying a unique u u -Gibbs measure.



1.
Introduction

Measure rigidity is expressed by the following general idea: when analyzing some set of invariant measures for a family of dynamical systems, one gets uniqueness or a very restrict subset of measures by imposing some condition on the family. The most basic example is given by rotations of the circle: the restriction to irrational rotations makes the set of invariant measures turn into a singleton, for in the irrational case the system is uniquely ergodic, and all rotations preserve Lebesgue measure. Measure rigidity results are fundamental in many applications of dynamics, including problems in number theory, see for instance
[
EKL06
]
or the related survey
[
Ven08
]
and the references therein.

Along the last decades, measure rigidity results have been explored by several authors. In homogeneous dynamics some impressive accomplishments have been obtained, such as Furstenberg’s proof of the unique ergodicity of the horocycle flow in constant negative curvature
[
FUR73
]
, which was deeply generalized in the series of works by Marina Ratner in the 90’s
[
Rat90b
]
,
[
Rat90a
]
,
[
Rat91a
]
,
[
Rat91b
]
. She proved measure classification results for measures invariant under unipotent flows (the action of a unipotent subgroup). After that, in another landmark series of works, Yves Benoist and Jean-François Quint proved
[
BQ11
,
BQ13a
,
BQ13b
]
measure rigidity results for stationary measures of a Zariski dense random walk on homogeneous spaces.

These works were the inspirations in Teichmüller dynamics for the remarkable work of Alex Eskin and Maryam Mirzakhani
[
EM18
]
about group actions on moduli spaces. In
[
EM18
]
, they developed the now called “factorization method” to prove measure rigidity for ergodic measures that are invariant under the action of the upper triangular subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. This method was after used back in the homogeneous setting by Eskin and Elon Lindenstrauss
[
EL18
]
, where they generalized the results of Benoist–Quint.

In smooth dynamics, Aaron Brown and Federico Rodriguez-Hertz
[
BRH17
]
used many ideas from
[
EM18
]
and
[
BQ11
]
to classify stationary measures for random iterations of
C^{2}
surface diffeomorphisms. This result has been generalized to higher dimensions by Brown–Rodriguez-Hertz in collaboration with Simion Filip and Eskin in the recent preprint
[
BEFH25
]
. It is worth to remark that the concept of uniform expansion plays a prominent role in this generalization, see
[
Chu20
]
where the author relates this concept with
[
BRH17
]
.

In
[
Oba23
]
Davi Obata translated the result and the ideas of
[
BRH17
]
to the setting of 4-dimensional partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms of skew-product type. In Obata’s work, classification of stationary measures is translated as
classification of
u
-Gibbs measures
. These are measures which are smooth along unstable manifolds. Recall that stationary measures can be regarded as “smooth” along
symbolic
unstable manifolds. The result of
[
Oba23
]
was used in
[
COP22
]
to obtain open sets of partially hyperbolic Anosov systems displaying uniqueness of
u
-Gibbs measures.

For
C^{\infty}
partially hyperbolic systems with one-dimensional center, in
[
Kat23
]
Asaf Katz developed the machinery of the factorization method of
[
EM18
]
and proved a measure rigidity result for
u
-Gibbs measures satisfying a
quantitative non-integrability (QNI)
condition. Using Katz’s result, Artur Avila, Sylvain Crovisier, Alex Eskin, Rafael Potrie, Amie Wilkinson and Zhiyan Zhang, in an ongoing project
[
ACE+24
]
, announced that, for
C^{\infty}
partially hyperbolic Anosov diffeomorphisms with expanding center in dimension 3, either one has that the stable and the unstable bundle jointly integrate or the system displays a unique
u
-Gibbs measure.

A different approach was taken in the work
[
ALOS24
]
, by Obata with Sébastien Alvarez, Martin Leguil and Bruno Santiago. Assuming regularity of stable holonomies, they took ideas from
[
BRH17
,
EM18
,
EL18
]
to show that for
C^{2}
partially hyperbolic Anosov diffeomorphisms, with expanding center and close to volume preserving, in dimension three, either the stable and the unstable bundle jointly integrate or there exists a unique fully supported
u
-Gibbs measure. In particular, the result of
[
ALOS24
]
does not depend on the QNI condition of Katz.

In this paper we aim to explore the connections between partially hyperbolic three dimensional diffeomorphisms and two dimensional partially hyperbolic
endomorphisms
in order translate the ideas of
[
ALOS24
]
to the
non-invertible
setting. We obtain measure rigidity of fully supported
u
-Gibbs measures for non-special partially hyperbolic and strongly transitive two dimensional endomorphisms. It is worth remarking that, contrarily to
[
ALOS24
]
, we do not assume uniform expansion along the center, and thus our result is also a generalization of
[
ALOS24
]
. Also, Katz in
[
Kat23
]
do not assume unifom expansion on the center bundle, nonetheless his arguments depend havily on high regularity of the dynamics, while our paper only assumes
C^{2}
. Moreover, we obtain that the measure is in fact absolutely continuous and physical, and thus our result dialogues with
[
Tsu05
]
: instead of a generic transversality condition, we assume a mild qualitative transversality (being non-special) and positivity of center exponent in order to promote a
u
-Gibbs meas0ure to be physical. Notice that the full support assumption is automatically satisfied once one knows that every unstable curve has a dense orbit. Thus, our main result reduces the measure rigidity problem to a topological problem. Also, by a result announced in
[
ACE+24
]
in many situations we known that this topological condition is satisfied. In particular, we obtain open sets of partially hyperbolic endomorphisms displaying a unique
u
-Gibbs measure.

1.1.
Statement of the main result

We give here quick definitions of the objects appearing in our main result. For a comprehensive treatment, see Section
2
. Denote by
\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
the set
C^{r}
local diffeomorphisms
f:{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. We say that an element
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
is
partially hyperbolic
if there exist
\sigma>1
,
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a continuous family
x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mapsto{\mathcal{C}}^{u}_{x}
of
unstable cones
: for each
x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
,
{\mathcal{C}}^{u}_{x}
is a closed cone in
T_{x}{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
such that

(i)

Df^{\ell}(x){\mathcal{C}}^{u}_{x}\subset\operatorname{Int}({\mathcal{C}}^{u}_{f^{\ell}(x)})\cup\{0\}

(ii)

\|Df^{\ell}(x)v\|\geq\sigma\|v\|
, for every
v\in{\mathcal{C}}^{u}_{x}
.

An endomorphism can be seen as a kind of dynamical system for which the future is deterministic but the past is random. In this way, for a given point
x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
there exist uncountably many different sequences
\tilde{x}=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
satisfying
x_{0}=x
and
f(x_{i})=x_{i+1}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. When
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
is partially hyperbolic, for each such an
orbit

\tilde{x}=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
there exist two line bundles
E^{c}(\tilde{x})\oplus E^{u}(\tilde{x})
, defined along the sequence, and invariant under the differential
Df
. The bundle
E^{u}(\tilde{x})
is contained in the unstable cone
{\mathcal{C}}^{u}_{x}
while the
center bundle

E^{c}(\tilde{x})
is transverse to it. In particular,
E^{u}
is uniformly expanded and
E^{c}
is
dominated
by
E^{u}
: vectors in
E^{c}
, whenever they are expanded they do so (exponentially) slower than the vectors in fast unstable bundle
E^{u}
.

An important fact is that the center bundle is entirely deterministic, for it does not depend on the past, i.e.
E^{c}(\tilde{x})=E^{c}(\tilde{a})
whenever
a_{0}=x_{0}
. The unstable bundle, on the contrary, in general changes with the past orbit. If, for a given partially hyperbolic
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
, the unstable bundle satisfies
E^{u}(\tilde{x})=E^{u}(\tilde{a})
whenever
a_{0}=x_{0}
, we say that
f
is a
special
endomorphism. We observe that being non-special is a
C^{1}
open and
C^{r}
dense condition (see
[
CM22
]
for
r=1
and a local argument and
[
He17
]
for a general argument in the volume preserving case) among partially hyperbolic
f\in\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
.

Given an ergodic
f
-invariant measure
\mu
, we define its
center exponent
by

L^{c}(\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\int\log\|Df(x)|_{E^{c}}\|d\mu(x).

Notice that, since the center bundle is deterministic, the above is well defined.

When
f
is non-special the bundle
E^{u}
does not integrate into an
f
-invariant foliation. Nevertheless, we can construct unstable manifolds using the
natural extension
(see Section
2.1
): given an orbit
\tilde{x}=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
one can apply the graph transform method along the sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i<0}
to construct an unstable manifold
{\mathcal{W}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
attached to the orbit
\tilde{x}
(see
[
QXZ09
]
). We say that an ergodic
f
-invariant probability measure
\mu
is a
u
-
Gibbs measure
if the conditionals of
\mu
along unstable manifolds are absolutely continuous with respect to the inner Lebesgue measure of the leaves (see Section
2.3
).

Finally, we say that
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
is
strongly transitive
if for every open set
U\subset{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
there exists an integer
N>0
such that
\cup_{n=0}^{N}f^{n}(U)={\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. We can now state our main result.

Theorem A
.

Let
f
be a
C^{2}
partially hyperbolic strongly transitive endomorphism of
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. Let
\mu
be an ergodic
u
-Gibbs measure of
f
with positive center exponent. Assume that
f
is not special and that
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)={\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. Then,
\mu
is the unique absolutely continuous invariant measure for
f
.

We do not know if a map
f
satisfying the assumptions of our main result is
mostly expanding
in the sense of
[
AV18
]
, i.e., if the existence of
one fully supported

u
-Gibbs measure with positive center exponent would imply that every
u
-Gibbs measure have positive center exponent. We also don’t know whether the results of
[
AV18
]
hold true in the non-invertible setting, although we believe that both answers are yes. Regardless, our result can be seen also as giving a new criteria for existence and uniqueness of absolutely continuous invariant probability measures for partially hyperbolic endomorphisms in
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
(see Corollary
1
below), going beyond the measure rigidity.

We also remark that one cannot drop the full support and transitivity assumptions simultaneously. Indeed, consider the product of
f:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
a north pole-south pole diffeomorphism and
g:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
a uniformly expanding map with stronger expansion than
f
. This system is partially hyperbolic, not transitive, and the Lebesgue measure on the circle
\{N\}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
given by
\delta_{N}\times Leb_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
has expanding center exponent and it is
u
-Gibbs. We can perturb this system to a non-special one which has the same features (as in Section
2.2.3
), being also not transitive. This phenomenon can not occur on the uniformly expanding case. Nonetheless, even a uniformly expanding (therefore strongly transitive) system can have u-Gibbs measures that do not have full support, as we explore in Section
1.2
.

1.2.
Some applications

The most important particular case of Theorem
A
concerns the case in which every unstable curve has a dense forward orbit. More precisely, we say that a partially hyperbolic element
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
has
dynamically minimal unstable leaves
if, for every unstable manifold
\gamma={\mathcal{W}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
, one has
\overline{\cup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}f^{n}(\gamma)}={\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. Given an ergodic,
f
-invariant
u
-Gibbs measure
\mu
, for almost every
x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
there exists at least one orbit
\tilde{x}=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, with
x_{0}=x
, so that the associated unstable manifold
{\mathcal{W}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
is contained in the support of
\mu
. In particular, if
f
has dynamically minimal unstable leaves then every
u
-Gibbs measure is fully supported. Therefore, we deduce from our main result that

Corollary 1
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be partially hyperbolic, strongly transitive with dynamically minimal unstable leaves. Assume that
f
is non-special. Then, if there exists a
u
-Gibbs measure
\mu
with positive center exponent, then
\mu
is the unique absolutely continuous invariant measure of
f
.

This Corollary reduces the measure rigidity problem of
u
-Gibbs measures with positive center exponent to the topological problem of proving dynamical minimality of unstable leaves.

1.2.1.
Perturbations of an irreducible linear expanding map with simple spectrum

An interesting case in which Corollary
1
can be applied concerns perturbation of the induced map
f_{B}:{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
where
B\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
has two irrational eigenvalues
\sigma^{u}>\sigma^{c}>1
(see Example

). This map is partially hyperbolic, strongly transitive and special. In fact,
f_{B}
is
uniformly expanding
in the sense that
\min_{x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}\|Df(x)|_{E^{c}}\|>1
. It is a well known fact that this is an open condition which implies that every perturbation
f
of

is topologically conjugate: there exists a continuous change of coordinates

under which
f
and

are indistinguishable, i.e.

.
A corollary of the work announced by

Theorem 1.1

(

]
)
.

Consider

The above result can be proved using the

Combining this with Corollary

Let

In particular, this gives open sets of partially hyperbolic endomorphisms on

1.2.2.
The reducible case

Considering now a matrix
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
with two distinct integer eigenvalues, both larger than
1
, then the main obstruction to the application of our main result lies in the lack of knowledge about the behavior of unstable leaves. For in this case a phenomenon not present in the invertible setting appears: the existence of
compact
unstable leaves. Indeed, we demonstrate in Example
3
that one can build a non-special perturbation of the induced map
f_{A}
which presents compact unstable leaves with non-dense orbit. This example, despite of being uniformly expanding, partially hyperbolic, strongly transitive and not special, displays infinitely
u
-Gibbs measures. These claims are proved in Section
2.2.3
.

More generally, we do not know what to expect as an answer to the following problem.

Problem 1
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be a partially hyperbolic map isotopic to a linear expanding map
f_{A}
, where the matrix
A
has integer eigenvalues
\sigma^{u}\geq\sigma^{c}>1
. Do we have that every unstable curve is either compact or has a dense orbit?

Notice that the work of Martin Andersson and Wagner Ranter
[
AR23
]
shows that, if we assume further than the assumptions of Problem
1
that

\textrm{det}\ Df(x)>\sigma^{u},\>\>\>\textrm{for every}\>\>\>x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}},

then
f
is locally eventually onto (for all
U\subseteq M
non-empty open set there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
f^{N}(U)=M
), and in particular strongly transitive. Even under this stronger assumption, we do not know the answer to Problem
1
.

1.3.
An ideia of the Eskin–Mirzakhani scheme

Let us give an overview of the argument we employ to prove Theorem
A
. Consider a pair
(f,\mu)
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
A
. First, one reduces the problem of demonstrating the absolute continuity of the measure to a very precise measure rigidity problem. In our setting, we do this by showing that the measure has the
SRB property
on the inverse limit space (see Definition
2.14
). Since we are not in a homogeneous setting we need to use
normal form coordinates
in order to linearize the dynamics along
center unstable manifolds
(see Section
3
). This permits, among other things, to give a precise meaning to the statement that the
u
-Gibbs property is equivalent to “
the measure is invariant by translations along unstable manifolds
”. Thus if we quotient the measure along unstable manifolds (see Section
3.3
) we obtain a family of measures on the one-dimensional center manifolds, which by normal forms are mapped onto
{\mathbb{R}}
. Therefore we obtain a family of measures
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}
on the real line, attached to almost every point
\tilde{x}
on the inverse limit, so that our
u
-Gibbs measure
\mu
has the SRB property (and thus is absolutely continuous) if, and only if, the measures
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}
are invariant by translations (Lemma
3.1
). Moreover, this family inherits the
starting invariance of our
u
-Gibbs measure
\mu
: they (virtually) do not change when we move the base point
\tilde{x}
to some
\tilde{a}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
. Moreover, from
x
to
f(x)
the measure is multiplied by
\|Df(x)|_{E^{c}}\|
(see Lemma
3.5
for a precise statement).

With proper adaptations, necessary in each context, this reduction of the measure rigidity problem to the problem of obtaining some kind of extra invariance along the direction of weak expansion is present in all previous works
[
ALOS24
,
EM18
,
BRH17
,
BQ11
]
. In this part of the proof, our contribution, comparing with
[
ALOS24
]
and
[
Kat23
]
, is to observe that it works also when one only assumes low regularity and non-uniform expansion along the center. Recall that the maps considered in
[
ALOS24
]
are Anosov an thus present uniform expansion along the center. Also, one can compare with
[
Kat23
]
where the high smoothness is used from the start to obtain normal forms, contrarily to our work (and
[
ALOS24
]
) where we only need the map to be
C^{2}
.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

2.
Partially hyperbolic endomorphisms on surfaces

In this section we give complete definitions and state some properties of the kind of dynamical systems we consider in this work. We present in detail the inverse limit space and how one can translate the theory of three dimensional partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms to the non-invertible setting. We present a number of examples and study their
u
-Gibbs measures. At the end, we develop the theory of Lyapunov norms in our setting which is a convenient way of measuring the amount of expansion along the center direction. This tool plays a significant role in our argument.

2.1.
Inverse Limit

One of the key features of our work is to explore the connections between partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms in dimension three, with a decomposition
E^{s}\oplus E^{c}\oplus E^{u}
, and partially hyperbolic endomorphisms in dimension two. In this section we describe the object which allows to properly formulate a dictionary between these classes of systems.

2.1.1.
Abstract construction

Consider
(X,d)
a compact metric space and a map
f:X\to X
that is continuous and surjective. Then
f
has an invertible extension
\tilde{f}:X_{f}\to X_{f}
, that is, there exists
\pi:X_{f}\to X
continuous and surjective such that
\pi\circ\tilde{f}=f\circ\pi
. This extension is called
inverse limit
or
natural extension
and it is defined as follows.

•

X_{f}=\{\tilde{x}=(x_{k})\in X^{\mathbb{Z}}:x_{k+1}=f(x_{k})\mbox{, }\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
,

•

(\tilde{f}(\tilde{x}))_{k}=(x_{k+1})

\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\forall\tilde{x}\in\tilde{X}
,

•

\pi:X_{f}\to X
is the projection on the 0th coordinate.

Given
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in X_{f}
we define
n(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\max\{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\;:\;x_{i}=y_{i},\>\textrm{for all}\>|i|<n\}
. Then, by defining

d(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}2^{-n(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})},

we obtain a metric in
X_{f}
which makes it a compact metric space.

Remark 2
.

The space
(X_{f},d)
is in fact an
ultrametric space
, in the sense that it holds the improved triangle inequality:
d(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\leq\max\{d(\tilde{x},\tilde{z}),d(\tilde{z},\tilde{y})\}
, for every
\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z}\in X_{f}
.

Notice that the shift map
\tilde{f}
and the projection
\pi
are both continuous. If
f
is invertible, then
\pi
is a homeomorphism between
X_{f}
and
X
. We consider in this work only the case in which
f
is not invertible.

One can prove that the metric
d
generates the same topology as the
open cylinders
given by

[A_{-k},\cdots A_{0}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\{\tilde{x}\in X_{f}:x_{i}\in A_{i}\mbox{ for }i\in\{-k,...0\}\},

where
A_{i}\subseteq X
are open sets. The same cylinders generate the Borel
\sigma
-algebra for
(X_{f},\tilde{d})
.

All measures considered in this work are over the Borel
\sigma
-algebra for the given space and, given a space
X
, we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}(X)
the probability measures on
X
, endowed with the weak
∗
topology. Given a discrete time dynamical system
f:X\to X
we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{f}(X)
the subset of
f
-invariant probability measures. Finally, we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}^{\operatorname{erg}}_{f}(X)
the ergodic elements in
{\mathcal{M}}_{f}(X)
.

It is easy to check that if
\tilde{\mu}
is a
\tilde{f}
-invariant measure on
X_{f}
, then
\pi_{*}\tilde{\mu}
is a
f
-invariant measure on
X
. Since
\pi
is surjective, for all such
\mu
, there is a
\tilde{\mu}
that projects on it. Actually,
\pi_{*}:\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{f}}(X_{f})\to\mathcal{M}_{f}(X)
is a bijection, as proved for instance in
[
QXZ09
, Proposition I.3.1]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space and
f:X\to X
continuous. For any
f
-invariant probability measure
\mu
on
X
, there is a unique
\tilde{f}
-invariant probability measure
\tilde{\mu}
on
X_{f}
such that
\pi_{*}\tilde{\mu}=\mu
.

The uniqueness on the above result is given by the fact that
\tilde{\mu}
is defined by the values it attributes to cylinders.

The topological structure of
(X_{f},\tilde{d})
is quite interesting if
f
is not invertible. If
X
is connected and
f
is a self-covering map with positive degree, then, for each
x\in X
,
\pi^{-1}(x)
is compact, totally disconnected and has no isolated points, thus it is a Cantor set.

Remark 3
.

Additionally, if
X
is a manifold (it suffices that
X
is locally simply connected), then
(X_{f},X,\pi,\Sigma)
is a fiber bundle, where
\Sigma
is a Cantor set
[
AH94
, Theorem 6.5.1]
. Therefore, even if
X
is a manifold and
f
is differentiable,
X_{f}
does not have a manifold structure. Nevertheless, we can pullback the differential structure from
X
to
X_{f}
and define
D\tilde{f}_{\tilde{x}}:T_{\tilde{x}}X_{f}\to T_{\tilde{f}(\tilde{x})}X_{f}
as
D\tilde{f}_{\tilde{x}}=Df_{x}
. This derivative is uniformly bounded as a function of
\tilde{x}
. Using this tangent structure, we can see that each path connected component of
X_{f}
is diffeomorphic to the universal covering of
X
and that
\tilde{f}
is invertible and as regular as
f
restricted to a given path connected component. Another topological feature of
X_{f}
is given by the following result.

Notation
.

Along this text, we use the notation

\Sigma(\tilde{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\pi^{-1}(\pi(\tilde{x}))

to refer to the fiber of
\tilde{x}
on
(X_{f},X,\pi,\Sigma)
.

Proposition 2
.

For any
f
-invariant probability measure
\mu
on
X
, we have
\operatorname{supp}(\tilde{\mu})=X_{f}
if and only if
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)=X
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{supp}(\tilde{\mu})=X_{f}
, then
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)=X
is a direct consequence of
\mu=\pi_{*}\tilde{\mu}
.

If
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)=X
and we suppose that
\operatorname{supp}(\tilde{\mu})\neq X_{f}
, then there is an open set
A\subseteq X_{f}
such that
\tilde{\mu}(A)=0
. Since
\tilde{\mu}
is
\tilde{f}
-invariant, this implies that
\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{f}^{k}(A))=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. But the topological structure of the inverse limit space implies that there is
k<0
such that
\tilde{f}^{k}(A)
contains
\pi^{-1}(U)
for some open
U\in X
, a contradiction.
∎

2.1.2.
Topological dynamics on
X_{f}

Recall that a continuous map
f:X\to X
is said to be strongly transitive if for all
U\subseteq X
non-empty open set there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\cup_{n=0}^{N}f^{n}(U)=X
. Every uniformly expanding map on a compact manifold is strongly transitive, thus it is transitive. The following simple result and its corollary are fundamental in allowing us to have “quantitative” information about points which have “many” different unstable directions (Lemma
4.2
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f:X\to X
be a strongly transitive transformation. Then, for each
x\in X
the set
\{f^{-n}(x);n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is dense in
X
.

Proof.

Let
U\subset X
be any open set. Then, there exists
N>0
such that
\cup_{k=0}^{N}f^{k}(U)=X
. In particular, there exist some
y\in U
and
k\leq N
such that
f^{k}(y)=x
, which means that
f^{-k}(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Let
f:X\to X
be a strongly transitive transformation. Then, for each
\tilde{x}\in X_{f}
the set
\{\tilde{f}^{-n}(\Sigma(\tilde{x}));n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is dense in
X_{f}
.

2.2.
Partial hyperbolicity on the inverse limit

Consider
\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
the set of
C^{r}
local diffeomorphisms on
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be a partially hyperbolic endomorphism. We denote by
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
the associated inverse limit space as constructed above. The cone field condition of partial hyperbolicity for
f
is equivalent to the following
[
QXZ09
,
HPS77
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a continuous non-trivial splitting
T_{\tilde{x}}{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}=E^{c}(\tilde{x})\oplus E^{u}(\tilde{x})
on the inverse limit space, for some choice of background Riemannian metric on
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
, such that the continuous functions

\lambda^{*}_{\tilde{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\|D\tilde{f}(\tilde{x})|_{E^{*}}\|,\>\>\>\textrm{for}\>\>*\in\{c,u\},

satisfy the following inequalities:

(1)

\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}<\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{x}}
, for every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
;

(2)

\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{x}}>1
, for every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
.

Notation
.

To simplify calculations involving derivatives along trajectories, we use the following notation

\lambda^{*}_{\tilde{x}}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\|D\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})|_{E^{*}}\|,

and

d^{n}_{\tilde{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\frac{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{x}_{-n}}(n)}{\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}_{-n}}(n)},

for every
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}

Moreover, as a consequence of
[
CM22
]
, we have that the center bundle
E^{c}
descends to an
f
-invariant bundle on
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be partially hyperbolic. Then, for every
\tilde{y}\in\Sigma(\tilde{x})
one has
E^{c}(\tilde{y})=E^{c}(\tilde{x})
.

Remark 4
.

In fact, if
f:X\to X
is a local diffeomorphism with a
Df
-invariant dominated splitting
T_{\tilde{x}}X=E(\tilde{x})\oplus F(\tilde{x})
, with
E\prec F
, then
E(x)
does not depend on
\tilde{x}\in\pi^{-1}(x)
. A proof of this fact for partially hyperbolic endomorphisms is given on
[
CM22
, Lemma 2.5]
.

The unstable bundle
E^{u}
, on the other hand, in general is not constant along fibers of the inverse limit space.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that a partially hyperbolic
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
is
special
if, for every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and every
\tilde{y}\in\Sigma(\tilde{x})
one has
E^{u}(\tilde{x})=E^{u}(\tilde{y})
.

2.2.1.
Some examples

To get a more concrete view of the variety of behaviors one can encounter among partially hyperbolic elements
f\in\operatorname{End}^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
, we discuss below some simple examples.

Example 1
.

The simplest example is the reducible one given by the product of two expanding maps
3x\pmod{1}
and
2x\pmod{1}
in the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}={\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
, the first expansion dominating the second one. Equivalently, consider
f_{A}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
as the transformation induced by the matrix
A=\left(\begin{matrix}3&0\\
0&2\end{matrix}\right)
.
This example is special, since it is linear, and each unstable/center leaf is a circle. It is worth to remark that it presents a behavior that
is not present
on invertible partially hyperbolic Anosov maps on the 3-torus, for in this case the integer matrix
A
should have
|\textrm{det}\ A|=1
and the system is not reducible.

Example 2
.

Consider
f_{B}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
as the function induced by the matrix
B=\left(\begin{matrix}3&1\\
1&2\end{matrix}\right)
, with eigenvalues
\dfrac{5+\sqrt{5}}{2}>\dfrac{5-\sqrt{5}}{2}>1
. We have that
f_{B}
is special and each unstable/center leaf is a dense line tangent to the corresponding eigenvector of
B
on the tangent space.

Both simple examples presented above are special. However, most partially hyperbolic endomorphisms are not special. More precisely, the set of non-special partially hyperbolic endomorphisms on a manifold
X
is
C^{1}
open and dense
[
CM22
, Theorem B]
. We remark that their result requires additional assumptions because they are considering systems with three non-trivial directions in the splitting and they are proving that both the center and the unstable directions are not unique for a given point. Nonetheless, the result holds in particular in dimension two as follows.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
CM22
]
)
.

Consider
PHE({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
as the set of all partially hyperbolic endomorphisms
f:{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
with
Df
-invariant splitting
T_{\tilde{x}}M=E^{c}(x)\oplus E^{u}(\tilde{x})
. Then there is a
C^{1}
open and dense subset
\mathcal{U}\subseteq PHE({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
such that every map in
U
is non-special.

The proof of denseness is achieved by, starting with a special partially hyperbolic endomorphism, perturbing it to generate new directions, as we do for completeness with the following example.

Example 3
.

Recall that
f_{A}
from Example
1
is a special partially hyperbolic uniformly expanding endomorphism with the strong unstable direction
E^{u}_{A}
being horizontal at every point, and the center direction
E^{c}_{A}
being vertical at every point.

Consider
p=(0,0)
the unique fixed point of
f_{A}
and
q=(2/3,1/2)\in f_{A}^{-1}(\{p\})
. Since
f_{A}
is a self covering map, there is
\tau>0
such that, if
f_{A}(y)=f_{A}(z)
and
y\neq z
, then
d(y,z)>\tau
. Take
\delta\in(0,\tau/2)
and consider
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that the square
R=[-a,a]^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, when projected under
\exp_{q}:T_{q}{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
, is contained in
B_{\delta}(q)
. Making an abuse of notation by identifying
R
and
\exp_{q}(R)
we define

\varphi(x,y)=\begin{cases}\left(x,y+\varepsilon\;a\;\psi_{1}\left(\dfrac{x}{a}\right)\psi_{2}\left(\dfrac{y}{a}\right)\right)&\mbox{ if }(x,y)\in R\\
\operatorname{Id}&\mbox{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where
\varepsilon>0
is as small as we want and
\psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to[-2,2]
and
\psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
are
C^{\infty}
functions satisfying

•

\operatorname{supp}\psi_{i}\subseteq[-1,1]
;

•

\psi_{1}(1/2)=1
and
\psi_{1}(-1/2)=-1
;

•

\psi_{1}(-1)=\psi_{1}(0)=\psi_{1}(1)=0
;

•

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\psi_{1}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{[-1/2,1/2]}}
is linear;

•

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\psi_{2}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{[-1/2,1/2]}}\equiv 1
.

We have that

D\varphi_{(x,y)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\varepsilon\;\psi_{1}^{\prime}\left(\dfrac{x}{a}\right)\psi_{2}\left(\dfrac{y}{a}\right)&1+\varepsilon\;\psi_{1}\left(\dfrac{x}{a}\right)\psi_{2}^{\prime}\left(\dfrac{y}{a}\right)\end{pmatrix},

in
R
, which is easily checked to be invertible for all
(x,y)
, provided that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small.

Additionally,
\varphi
is
C^{\infty}
and satisfies
\varphi(q)=q
,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\varphi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\overline{R^{\complement}}}}=\operatorname{\operatorname{Id}}
,
\varphi(R)=R
and

D\varphi_{q}\cdot E^{u}_{A}(q)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
2\varepsilon&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
2\varepsilon\alpha\end{pmatrix}\neq E^{u}_{A}(p),

meaning that
\varphi
does not preserve the unstable directions of
f_{A}
.

Let
f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}f_{A}\circ\varphi
. Since
f
is
C^{1}
close to
f_{A}
, we have that
f
is a partially hyperbolic uniformly expanding endomorphism. Additionally, since
(1,0)
is an eigenvector for
D\varphi_{(x,y)}
,
f
has the same central manifolds as
f_{A}
. The same argument as in
[
CM22
]
gives us that
f
is non-special as follows.

By the definition of
\delta
, we have that any point
x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
has a past orbit
\tilde{x}\in\Sigma(x)
such that
x_{-k}\notin R
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, along this orbit we have that
f=f_{A}
, which implies that
E^{u}(\tilde{x})=E^{u}_{A}
. In particular, there exists
\tilde{q}\in\Sigma(q)
such that
E^{u}(\tilde{q})
is horizontal. Now, by invariance this implies that for
\tilde{p}=\tilde{f}(\tilde{q})=(...q_{-1}q_{0},p,p,p,...)
we have that

E^{u}(\tilde{p})=Df(q)E^{u}(\tilde{q})\neq E^{u}_{A}.

However, for
\hat{p}=(...p,p,p,...)
one has that
f=f_{A}
all along this fixed orbit and thus
E^{u}(\hat{p})=E^{u}_{A}
. This proves that
p
has more than one unstable direction, and thus
f
is not u-special.

Similarly, we can do a perturbation of Example
2
.

Example 4
.

We can repeat the construction from the previous example with
q=\left(\dfrac{2}{5},\dfrac{4}{5}\right)
, and by using

and

as coordinates on

-close to

from Example

and having the same center leaves.

2.2.2.
Invariant foliations

Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be partially hyperbolic and let
\tilde{f}:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
denote its lift to the inverse limit space. By viewing the direction of the fibers as a stable direction and the “manifold direction” as a center-unstable direction, we can see the dynamics on the inverse limit space as partially hyperbolic as follows.

Definition 2.6
.

Given
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
we define its center-unstable manifold
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})
as being the path-connected component of
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
containing
\tilde{x}
.

As we mentioned above, one can show that each center-unstable manifold
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})
is homeomorphic to the universal cover
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. Notice that
\tilde{f}
acts permuting the components. Thus,
\{\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})\}_{\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}}
is an
\tilde{f}
-invariant lamination of the space
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
. This lamination is analogous to the center unstable foliation of a three dimensional partially hyperbolic diffeomorphism. Indeed, notice that the fibers
\Sigma(\tilde{x})
are contracted by the action of
\tilde{f}
.

Remark 5
.

If
\tilde{y}\in\Sigma(\tilde{x})
then the definition of the metric in
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
immediately implies
d(\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x}),\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{y}))\leq 2^{-n}
.

Thus the fibers
\Sigma
of the space
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
play the same role as local stable manifolds in three dimensional partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms, and they are “transverse” to the center-unstable manifolds
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}
. Clearly, there is a notorious difference for here the fibers are totally disconnected compact spaces, and not embedded curves as in the case of diffeomorphisms.

Nonetheless, the graph transform method applies to the dynamics of
\tilde{f}
on
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and one can show the following (see
[
HPS77
,
QXZ09
]
).

Proposition 3
.

Given
f\in\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
partially hyperbolic, there exists a one-dimensional unstable lamination
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}
of the space
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
by
\tilde{f}
-invariant immersed (on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})
)
C^{r}
curves, varying continuously, which are everywhere tangent to
E^{u}
.

The existence of a center foliation is a more delicate issue. In our setting, we have the following consequence of
[
HH22
, Theorem B]
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be a strongly transitive partially hyperbolic map. Then, there exists a one-dimensional (center) lamination
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{c}
of the space
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
by
C^{r}
immersed curves, varying continuously, which are everywhere tangent to
E^{c}
. Moreover, this foliation descends to an
f
-invariant foliation of
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
.

In this work, we use consistently the following notation:
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{*}(\tilde{x})
,
*\in\{u,c,cu\}
, is the leaf through the point
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
, while
{\mathcal{W}}^{*}(\tilde{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\pi(\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x}))\subset{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
.

2.2.3.
The unstable foliation of Example
3

The unstable foliation of Example
1
descends to a foliation by (horizontal) compact circles, while the center foliation is the foliation by vertical circles. In Example
3
, as the map is not special the unstable foliation on
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
does not descends to a foliation of
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
, while the center foliation remains the same as that of
f_{A}
.

Moreover, we can choose the parameter
a>0
small enough so that there exists some (meager) compact set
K\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
, which is forward invariant under
x\mapsto 2x\mod 1
, and
(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times K)\cap R=\emptyset
. Thus, after perturbation the set
\Gamma=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times K
remains invariant under the perturbed map
f
, as
f=f_{A}
restricted to
\Gamma
. In particular, every horizontal unstable curve through a point
x\in\Gamma
has non-dense forward orbit. Moreover, we have that there are points
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
such that the set
\cup_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathcal{W}}^{u}(x_{i})
is not dense in
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. Indeed, for all
x\in\Gamma
, there is
\tilde{x}\in\Sigma(x)
such that
x_{-k}\in\Gamma
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, which implies that
E^{u}_{f}(\tilde{x})=E^{u}_{A}
. And the uniform expansion of
f
allows us to make this choice of past orbit coherently along the compact curves in
\Gamma
, in the sense that
{\mathcal{W}}^{u}(x_{i})\subseteq\Gamma
for all
i\leq 0
.

The behavior of the foliation in this example is still not well understood. We believe that every unstable curve which is not horizontal at some place should have a dense orbit, but this claim has not yet been confirmed.

2.3.
u
-Gibbs measures for endomorphisms

We are now able to define u-Gibbs measures on the non-invertible setting. Informally speaking, a u-Gibbs measure is a measure that is “well distributed” with respect to an unstable foliation. Since, in general, there is no unstable foliation on the ambient manifold, the definition passes through the unstable leaves of the inverse limit.

To make this concept of a “well distributed” measure with respect to a foliation precise, we first introduce how we decompose the measure with respect to a “nice partition”, with the following definition and theorem stated specifically for our context.

Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be a partially hyperbolic endomorphism.

Definition 2.7
.

A partition
\mathcal{P}
of
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
is a
measurable
(or
countably generated
) partition with respect to
\tilde{\mu}
if there is
M_{0}\subseteq{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
with
\tilde{\mu}(M_{0})=1
and a countable family

The partition

2.3.1.
u-Gibbs measures for the examples

Example
1
has many u-Gibbs measures: for any
\nu
a (
2x\pmod{1}
)-invariant measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
\operatorname{Leb}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\times\nu
is a u-Gibbs measure. Since the doubling map
2x\pmod{1}
is semi-conjugated to a full shift in two symbols, we know this system has infinitely many u-Gibbs measures. This is a particular case because the system is reducible.

In contrast, Example
2
has only
\operatorname{Leb}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
as a u-Gibbs measure. A brief sketch of this argument goes as follows: up to a constant, the density of
\tilde{\mu}
along some unstable leaf
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
is given by

\lim_{n\to-\infty}\frac{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{y}}(n)}{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(n)}.

Since the map is linear, this shows that the density is constant, and thus the conditional measure is invariant by translations along leaves. The invariance of conditional measures by translations along leaves implies invariance of the measure
\mu
under the unity flow along leaves. However, the unity flow along leaves is a Diophantine flow, which is uniquely ergodic. One deduces that
\mu=\textrm{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
.

Both Examples
1
and
2
are special, and one might wonder how the situation changes after perturbation. Example
3
gives us a non-special endomorphism, close to
f_{A}
, which still have infinitely many
u
-Gibbs measures. Indeed, as we discussed above in Section
2.2.3
, the example can be build with a forward invariant set
\Gamma=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times K
which is made of horizontal compact unstable leaves. Moreover,
K
can be chosen so that it contains infinitely many
x\mapsto 2x\mod 1
periodic orbits. Given
x_{1},...,x_{k}
such a periodic orbit, the measure

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{x_{i}}\times\textrm{Leb}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}

is a
u
-Gibbs measure for
f
.

On the other hand, Example
4
is also non-special, but since we can apply Corollary
1
, we deduce that the map has only one
u
-Gibbs measure which is the unique absolutely continuous invariant measure.

2.3.2.
Uniform density estimate

For a Lebesgue measurable set
A\subset\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
we denote, for simplicity,
|A|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\textrm{Leb}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(A)
the Lebesgue measure of
A
. Given a
u
-Gibbs measure, the conditional measures are “comparable” with respect to Lebesgue, but only up to a measurable function. To be precise, the density
\dfrac{\tilde{\mu}_{\tilde{x}}}{\textrm{Leb}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}}
is a measurable function. By restricting to compact sets of large measure, we get a uniform comparison, as made clear by the next result.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{End}^{r}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
be partially hyperbolic and
\mu
be an ergodic
u
-Gibbs measure for
f
. Consider
\{\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}\}
a family of conditional measures with respect to a
u
-subordinate partition. Then, given
\delta>0
, there exists a compact Lusin set
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
, with
\tilde{\mu}({\mathcal{L}})>1-\delta
, for which there exists a constant
C=C({\mathcal{L}})
such that for every subset
A\subset\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{x})
, for
\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}
, one has
C^{-1}|A|\leq\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(A)\leq C|A|
.

Proof.

From the definition of
u
-Gibbs measure, it follows that for
\tilde{\mu}
-almost every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
there exists a density function
\rho^{u}_{\tilde{x}}
such that

\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(A)=\int_{A}\rho^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(t)d\textrm{Leb}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(t).

Moreover, up to a positive constant depending measurably on
\tilde{x}
, we have that the density
\rho^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(t)
,
t=\pi(\tilde{y})
, is given by

\rho^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(t)=\lim_{n\to-\infty}\frac{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(n)}{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{y}}(n)}.

By Lusin’s theorem, given
\delta>0
, there exists a compact set
{\mathcal{L}}
with measure
\tilde{\mu}({\mathcal{L}})>1-\delta
such that the assignment
\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto\rho^{u}_{\tilde{x}}
is continuous. In particular, there exists a constant
C
such that
C\geq\rho^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(t)\geq C^{-1}
, for every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}
and every
t=\pi(\tilde{y})
, where
\tilde{y}
belongs to the atom containing
\tilde{x}
.
∎

Notation
.

Since uniform estimates such as the one above are abundant in this paper, we use repeatedly the following notation: given
a,b,C>0
we say that
a\asymp_{C}b
provided that
C^{-1}b\leq a\leq Cb
.

2.4.
Pesin Theory in the partially hyperbolic setting

In this section we consider
f:{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
a partially hyperbolic endomorphism and
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{f}^{\operatorname{erg}}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
an ergodic invariant probability measure.

2.4.1.
Center Lyapunov exponent

The (pointwise) center Lyapunov exponent of
f
is defined for all points in the
f
-invariant regular set
\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
has full
\mu
-measure for any
f
-invariant measure
\mu
. It is given by

L^{c}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\|Df^{n}(x)v\|,\mbox{ where }v\in E^{c}(x).

The integrated center Lyapunov exponent with respect to
\mu
is given by

\displaystyle L^{c}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int L^{c}(x)d\mu=\int\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\|Df(x)v\|d\mu,\mbox{ where }v\in E^{c}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\log|{\left.\kern-1.2ptDf\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{E^{c}}}|d\mu,

using the fact that
E^{c}
is one dimensional and Birkhoff ergodic theorem. Since
L^{c}(x)
is
f
-invariant and since we work only with ergodic measures, then
L^{c}(x)=L^{c}(\mu)
for
\mu
-almost every point and
non-uniform expansion
is given by
L^{c}(x)>0
for
\mu
-almost every point.

The differential structure of
f
pulled back to
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
allows us to have an Oseledec’s theorem for the inverse limit space and the Lyapunov exponents are the same as in the original manifold and they do not depend on the past orbit, that is, they are constant along
\Sigma(\tilde{x})
, see
[
QXZ09
, Proposition I.3.5]
. In Section
2.4
we explore Pesin theory further.

From now on, we consider a fixed ergodic
f
-invariant measure
\mu
. We also assume that the center Lyapunov exponent of
\mu
satisfies
L^{c}(\mu)>0
. In this setting, we fix
0<\lambda<L^{c}(\mu)<\hat{\lambda}
. Then, there exists a measurable function
C:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to[1,+\infty)
such that

C(\tilde{x})^{-1}e^{\lambda n}\leq\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(n)\leq C(\tilde{x})e^{\hat{\lambda}n},

for every
n>0
and

C(\tilde{x})^{-1}e^{\lambda n}\geq\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(n)\geq C(\tilde{x})e^{\hat{\lambda}n},

for every
n<0
. Moreover, the function
C
has slow exponential growth:

C(\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x}))\leq e^{\varepsilon n}C(\tilde{x}),

where
\varepsilon=(\hat{\lambda}-\lambda)/2
.

2.4.2.
Pesin center manifolds

We now briefly recall the properties of the Pesin center manifolds in our setting. There exists a pair of measurable functions
r:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to(0,1)
and
\gamma:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to[1,+\infty)
such that whenever
\tilde{y},\tilde{z}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{c}_{r(\tilde{x})}(\tilde{x})
then

(1)

d(\tilde{f}^{-n}({y}),\tilde{f}^{-n}(\tilde{z})\leq\gamma(\tilde{x})e^{-n\lambda}d(\tilde{y},\tilde{z})

Notation
.

We use the notation
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{c}
for the global center manifold given by the partially hyperbolic splitting and
\widetilde{W}^{c}_{{\operatorname{loc}}}
for the Pesin manifolds where we observe expansion, which in this case are local manifolds contained on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{c}
for almost every point, that is:
\widetilde{W}^{c}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\tilde{x})=\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{c}_{r(\tilde{x})}(\tilde{x})
.

The
global Pesin manifolds
, which we denote by
\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})
, are defined by the classical formula

\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\bigcup_{n=0}^{+\infty}\tilde{f}^{n}\left(\widetilde{W}^{c}_{{\operatorname{loc}}}(\tilde{x}_{-n})\right).

Thus
\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})\subseteq\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{c}(\tilde{x})\subset\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})
is an immersed line. As, a priory, we assume no uniform expansion along
E^{c}
, the inclusion
\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})\subseteq\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{c}(\tilde{x})
could be strict. We define the
global Pesin center unstable manifolds
by

\widetilde{W}^{cu}(\tilde{x})=\bigcup_{\tilde{y}\in\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})}\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x}).

As in
[
ALOS24
]
Lemma 2.4, we have the following

Lemma 2.12
.

For every
\tilde{y}\in\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})
it holds that
\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})\cap\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{y})=\{\tilde{y}\}
.

2.4.3.
The SRB property

Consider now a measurable partition
{\mathcal{P}}^{cu}
subordinate to the Pesin center unstable foliation
\widetilde{W}^{cu}(\tilde{x})
. This means a measurable partition whose atoms are entirely contained in a Pesin center-unstable manifold and project bijectively onto measurable subsets of
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
which contain a ball. More precisely

Definition 2.13
.

A measurable partition
\mathcal{P}
of
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
is
cu-subordinate
with respect to
\mu
if, for
\tilde{\mu}
-almost every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
, the atom
\mathcal{P}(\tilde{x})
satisfies

(1)

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\pi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathcal{P}(\tilde{x})}}
is a bijection onto its image;

(2)

there is an open set
U_{\tilde{x}}\subseteq{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
such that
\pi(\mathcal{P}(\tilde{x}))\supseteq U_{\tilde{x}}
.

The construction of subordinate partitions in
[
LS82
]
applies in our case.

Definition 2.14
.

We say that
\mu
is an SRB measure if there exists a cu-subordinate partition
{\mathcal{P}}
such that for
\tilde{\mu}
-almost every
\tilde{x}
one has
\pi_{*}\tilde{\mu}^{{\mathcal{P}}}_{\tilde{x}}<<\textrm{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
.

Lemma 2.15
.

Every SRB measure is absolutely continuous with respect to
\textrm{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
.

Proof.

Let us assume by contradiction that
\mu
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\textrm{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
. Then, there exists a Borel measurable set
A\subset{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
with
\mu(A)>0
but
\textrm{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}(A)=0
. Then,
\widetilde{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\pi^{-1}(A)
satisfies
\tilde{\mu}(\widetilde{A})>0
. By the regularity of
\mu
we may assume that
A
is compact and contained in an open set
U=B_{r}(x)
, for some
x\in A
, such that
\pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U)\cap\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{y})}
is a bijection for every
\tilde{y}\in\pi^{-1}(x)
. We may also choose the point
x
so that for
\mu_{x}
almost every
\tilde{y}\in\pi^{-1}(x)
the measure
\pi_{*}\tilde{\mu}^{{\mathcal{P}}}_{\tilde{y}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\textrm{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
. In particular, we have that

\tilde{\mu}^{{\mathcal{P}}}_{\tilde{y}}(\widetilde{A})=0,

for
\mu_{x}
almost every
\tilde{y}\in\pi^{-1}(x)

\mu(A)=\tilde{\mu}(\widetilde{A})=\int\tilde{\mu}^{{\mathcal{P}}}_{\tilde{y}}(\widetilde{A})d\mu_{x}(\tilde{y})=0,

which gives a contradiction.
∎

2.4.4.
Distortion estimates

Given
\delta>0
, we can choose a compact set
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
with
\tilde{\mu}({\mathcal{L}})>1-\delta
and such that the functions
C,r,\gamma
are continuous when restricted to
{\mathcal{L}}
. For this section, we fix
\delta>0
and
{\mathcal{L}}
.

The following result is a classical consequence of uniform expansion along unstable manifolds or center Pesin manifolds.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
\varphi:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a Hölder continuous function. There exists constants
C_{0}>0
and
C=C({\mathcal{L}})>0
such that

(1)

if
\tilde{y}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{x})
then for every
n>0
,

\left|\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\varphi(\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{x}))-\varphi(\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{y}))\right|\leq C_{0};

(2)

if
\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{y})\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{x}))
for
l\in\{0,\cdots,n-1\}
then

\left|\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\varphi(\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{x}))-\varphi(\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{y}))\right|\leq C_{0};

(3)

if
\tilde{y}\in\widetilde{W}^{c}_{r(\tilde{x})}(\tilde{x})
and
\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}
, then for every
n>0
,

\left|\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\varphi(\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{x}))-\varphi(\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{y}))\right|\leq C.

(4)

if
\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{y})\in\widetilde{W}^{c}_{r(\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{x}))}(\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{x}))
for
l\in\{0,\cdots,n-1\}
and
\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})\in{\mathcal{L}}
then

\left|\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\varphi(\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{x}))-\varphi(\tilde{f}^{l}(\tilde{y}))\right|\leq C.

Proof.

We write the proof for (2) and the center leaf, the others being analogous. If
\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}
and
\tilde{y}\in\widetilde{W}^{c}_{r(\tilde{x})}(\tilde{x})
, then

d(\tilde{f}^{-n}(\tilde{x}),\tilde{f}^{-n}(\tilde{y}))\leq\gamma_{0}e^{-n\lambda}d(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})

for all

, where
\gamma_{0}=\max\gamma|_{{\mathcal{L}}}
.

We have that

\left|\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\varphi(\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{x}))-\varphi(\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{y}))\right|\leq K\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}d(\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{x}),\tilde{f}^{-l}(\tilde{y}))^{\alpha},

where
\varphi
is
\alpha

K
. This is less or equal to

K\gamma_{0}d(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}e^{-l\lambda}\leq K\gamma_{0}\operatorname{diam}({\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}})\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}e^{-l\lambda}=C.\qed

We immediately have the following result.

Lemma 2.17
.

There exists a constant
C=C(f)>1
such that if
\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{y})\in{\mathcal{W}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x}))
then for every
\ell\leq n

\frac{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(\ell)}{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{y}}(\ell)}\asymp_{C}1.

Also, as a direct consequence of our choice of
\mathcal{L}
, we have similar estimates for the center direction.

Lemma 2.18
.

Given a Lusin set
{\mathcal{L}}
there exists a constant
C=C({\mathcal{L}})>1
such that if
\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})\in{\mathcal{L}}
and
\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{y})\in\widetilde{W}^{c}_{r(\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x}))}(\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x}))
then for every

\frac{\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\ell)}{\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{y}}(\ell)}\asymp_{C}1.

2.5.
The Lyapunov norm

Given
v\in E^{c}(\tilde{x})\setminus\{0\}
and
0<\lambda<L^{c}(\mu)
we define

\|v\|_{L,\tilde{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\left(\sum_{k\leq 0}\|D\tilde{f}^{k}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}e^{-2k\lambda}\right)^{1/2},

which is called the
Lyapunov norm of
v
. Observe that all terms in the series are non-negative, but in principle the series could diverge. The lemma below guarantees that this is not the case by providing a measurable upper bound with respect to the background Riemannian norm.

Lemma 2.19
.

There exists a measurable function
\xi:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to[1,+\infty)
such that for
\tilde{\mu}
-almost every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and every
v\in E^{c}(\tilde{x})
it holds

\|v\|\leq\|v\|_{L,\tilde{x}}\leq\xi(\tilde{x})\|v\|
.

Proof.

Denote, for simplicity,
\chi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}L^{c}(\mu)
. Choose
0<\varepsilon<\chi-\lambda
. Then, there exists a measurable function
\hat{C}:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to[1,+\infty)
such that

\hat{C}(\tilde{x})^{-1}e^{k(\chi-\varepsilon)}\geq\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(k)\geq\hat{C}(\tilde{x})e^{k(\chi+\varepsilon)},\>\>\textrm{for all}\>\>k<0.

This implies that

\|D\tilde{f}^{k}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}=\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(k)^{2}\|v\|^{2}\leq\hat{C}(\tilde{x})^{-2}e^{2k(\chi-\varepsilon)}\|v\|^{2}.

Thus defining
\xi(\tilde{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\left(\hat{C}(\tilde{x})^{-2}\sum_{k\leq 0}e^{2k(\chi-\varepsilon-\lambda)}\right)^{1/2}
we obtain

\|v\|^{2}_{L,\tilde{x}}=\sum_{k\leq 0}\|D\tilde{f}^{k}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}e^{-2k\lambda}\leq\xi(\tilde{x})^{2}\|v\|^{2}.\qed

2.5.1.
Growth estimate for norm
\|.\|_{L,\tilde{x}}

When we measure the length of a vector
v\in E^{c}(\tilde{x})
using the norm
\|.\|_{L,\tilde{x}}
, we see expansion in a uniform fashion. The drawback is that the assignment
x\mapsto\|.\|_{L,\tilde{x}}
is only measurable. In the sequel we use the notation
\tilde{x}_{n}=\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})
.

Lemma 2.20
.

There exists a constant
\sigma=\sigma(f)>1
such that for
\tilde{\mu}
a.e.
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and every
v\in E^{c}(\tilde{x})
it holds

e^{n\lambda}\|v\|_{L,\tilde{x}}\leq\|D\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})v\|_{L,\tilde{x}_{n}}\leq e^{n\sigma}\|v\|_{L,\tilde{x}},\>\>\>\textrm{for every}\>\>n>0

Proof.

Take
v\in E^{c}(\tilde{x})\setminus\{0\}
and
n>0
. Then,

\|D\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}_{L,\tilde{x}_{n}}=\sum_{k\leq 0}\|D\tilde{f}^{k}(\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x}))D\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}e^{-2\lambda k}=\sum_{k\leq 0}\|D\tilde{f}^{n+k}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}e^{-2\lambda k}.

By making the change
\ell\rightarrow k+n
in the sum, we obtain

\displaystyle\|D\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}_{L,\tilde{x}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\leq 0}\|D\tilde{f}^{\ell}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}e^{-2\lambda(\ell-n)}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\|D\tilde{f}^{\ell}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}e^{-2\lambda(\ell-n)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}I+II.

The first term on the right-hand side above is equal to

I=e^{2\lambda n}\sum_{\ell\leq 0}\|D\tilde{f}^{\ell}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}e^{-2\lambda\ell}=e^{2\lambda n}\|v\|^{2}_{L,\tilde{x}}.

As the second term is non-negative, this gives immediately the lower bound. For the upper bound, take
\xi>\max\{\log\|D\tilde{f}(\tilde{x})\|;\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\}>1
. Notice that
n<e^{n\xi}
for every
n>0
. By definition, for every
\ell=1,...,n
we have

\|D\tilde{f}^{\ell}(\tilde{x})v\|^{2}\leq e^{2\xi\ell}\|v\|^{2}\leq e^{2\xi n}\|v\|^{2}.

This allows us to estimate the second term II above by

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}e^{2\xi n}e^{-2\lambda(\ell-n)}\leq\|v\|^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}e^{4\xi n}e^{-2\lambda\ell}

\displaystyle\leq e^{4\xi n}\|v\|^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}e^{-2\lambda\ell}\leq ne^{4\xi n}\|v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{5\xi n}\|v\|^{2}\leq e^{5\xi n}\|v\|_{L,\tilde{x}}^{2}.

Thus

I+II\leq(e^{2\lambda n}+e^{5\xi n})\|v\|_{L,\tilde{x}}\leq e^{7\xi n}\|v\|^{2}_{L,\tilde{x}}.

This establishes the upper bound and completes the proof.
∎

2.5.2.
u
-saturation of the well behaved set for
\|.\|_{L,\tilde{x}}

Lemma 2.21
.

If
\tilde{x}
is
\tilde{\mu}
-regular and if
\tilde{x}^{u}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
, then
0<\|v\|_{L,\tilde{x}^{u}}<+\infty
for every

Proof.

As

is Hölder continuous it follows from Lemma
2.16

\frac{\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(k)}{\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}^{u}}(k)}\asymp_{C}1,

for some constant

(which depends only on

is one-dimensional we can therefore write

\displaystyle\|v\|^{2}_{L,\tilde{x}^{u}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq 0}\|D\tilde{f}^{k}(\tilde{x}^{u})v\|^{2}e^{-2\lambda k}

\displaystyle=\|v\|^{2}\sum_{k\leq 0}\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}^{u}}(k)^{2}e^{-2\lambda k}

3.
Normal forms and leaf-wise quotient measures

This section collects a number of tools we use in our argument. To motivate these tools, recall that our goal is to prove that, under the hypotheses of Theorem
A
, a given u-Gibbs measure is absolutely continuous with respect to
\operatorname{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
. Since, by definition, a u-Gibbs measure is (in some sense) homogeneous along unstable leaves, we claim that it is enough to show that the measure is also homogeneous along center leaves. This claim is made precise by the following result, which we prove in Section
3.3
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There is a family of locally finite Radon measures
\{\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}\}_{\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{f}}
on
\mathbb{R}
such that, if
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}
is equivalent to Lebesgue for
\tilde{\mu}
-almost every
\tilde{x}
, then
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue.

This essentially translate our main problem into a one-dimensional question and it is the main goal of this section. In order to prove this lemma, we introduce a crucial tool: normal forms.

All the arguments in this section follow closely
[
ALOS24
, Sections 6–7]
, so we only give a brief account for the sake of completeness.

3.1.
One-dimensional normal forms

The result below, which comes from
[
KK07
]
, provides us with invariant affine structures along Pesin center manifolds and unstable manifolds.

Proposition 5
.

For
\tilde{\mu}
-almost every point

(2)

\tilde{x}

\tilde{x}

(4)

For

Proof.

We follow the arguments of
[
KK07
]

pulled back to the inverse limit. The proof is for

where

[
KK07
]
,

is well defined and
\alpha
-Hölder continuous on each Pesin manifold

if

is
C^{1+\alpha}

C^{k-1}
if

is
C^{k}

. Additionally, for
\tilde{y},\tilde{z}\in\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})
,

(4)

\rho^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{z})=\rho^{c}_{\tilde{y}}(\tilde{z})\rho^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{y}).

Consider
\mathcal{R}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}:\widetilde{W}^{c}(\tilde{x})\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

(5)

[
KK07

3.3.
Leaf-wise quotient measures

Following previous works
[

\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}

\tilde{f}
.

Consider
\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{0}
a measurable partition of
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{f}
subordinated to
\widetilde{W}^{cu}

\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\tilde{f}^{n}(\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{0})

\{\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}\}_{\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{f}}
supported at the atoms of
\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}
. We also have that

\nu^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}(\mathcal{R}_{\tilde{x}})_{*}\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}=\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}\circ\Phi_{\tilde{x}}

\mathcal{Q}^{cu}_{n}(\tilde{x})

Note that, since the ergodic system
(\tilde{f},\tilde{\mu})
is non-uniformly expanding for a
\tilde{\mu}

\tilde{x}

\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}(\tilde{x})\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{m}(\tilde{x})
if
m>n
. Additionally, for any Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{2}_{f}

\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}(A\cap\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}(\tilde{x}))=\dfrac{\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{m,\tilde{x}}(A\cap\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}(\tilde{x}))}{\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{m,\tilde{x}}(\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}(\tilde{x}))}.

Consider now, for every
\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}(\tilde{x})

\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}

\mathcal{P}^{u}_{n}=\tilde{f}^{n}(\mathcal{P}^{u}_{0})

\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}

\mathcal{P}^{cu}_{n}(\tilde{x})

\{\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{n,\tilde{y}}\}
supported at the atoms
\mathcal{P}^{u}_{n}(\tilde{y})

\tilde{\mu}^{c}_{n,\tilde{x}}

\tilde{\mu}^{c}_{n,\tilde{x}}(A)=\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}\left(\bigcup\limits_{a\in A}{\mathcal{P}}^{u}_{n}(a)\right),

for all
A\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{c}_{n}.

For any Borelian

\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}(B)=\int\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{n,\tilde{y}}(B)d\tilde{\mu}^{c}_{n,\tilde{x}}(\tilde{y}).

As we did for the two dimensional conditional measure
\tilde{\mu}^{cu}_{n,\tilde{x}}

\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{n,\tilde{x}}

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

\nu^{u}_{n,s}=\nu^{u}_{n,\tilde{y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}(\mathcal{R}_{\tilde{x}})_{*}\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{n,\tilde{y}}=\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{n,\tilde{y}}\circ\Phi_{\tilde{x}},

\tilde{y}=\Phi^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(s)=\Phi_{\tilde{x}}(0,s)
.

Since
\mathcal{R}_{\tilde{x}}
takes conditional measures on
\widetilde{W}^{cu}(\tilde{y})

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we have that

4.
Uniform transversality along fibers

From now on we start the proof of Theorem
A
. In this section our setting is slightly more flexible: we consider
f:{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
a partially hyperbolic, strongly transitive endomorphism and
\mu
an ergodic invariant probability measure (not necessarily a
u
-Gibbs measure). We consider
\tilde{f}:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and
\tilde{\mu}
the lifts of
f
and
\mu
to the inverse limit space. Let
\{\tilde{\mu}_{x}\}_{x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
denote the conditional measures of
\tilde{\mu}
with respect to the fibers of
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
.

4.1.
The angle function

We can measure angles between vectors in
T_{\tilde{x}}{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and
T_{\tilde{y}}{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
using the canonical projection
\pi:{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
. In particular, this allows us to measure angles between different unstable directions along the fiber
\Sigma(\tilde{x})
.

For
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and any given
\tilde{y}\in\Sigma(\tilde{x})
we denote

\alpha(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\angle(E^{u}(\tilde{x}),E^{u}(\tilde{y})).

Notice that the angle function is continuous in the following sense: if
\tilde{x}_{n}\to\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}_{n}\to\tilde{y}
, with
\tilde{y}_{n}\in\Sigma(\tilde{x}_{n})
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\lim\alpha(\tilde{x}_{n},\tilde{y}_{n})=\alpha(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
.

We consider the compact set
{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x})=\{\tilde{y}\in\Sigma(\tilde{x});\alpha(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=0\}
and its complement
{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x})=\Sigma(\tilde{x})\setminus{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x})
. The invariance of the unstable bundle implies that

(8)

\Sigma(\tilde{x}_{m})\cap\tilde{f}^{m}({\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x}))={\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x}_{m})\>\>\>\textrm{ and }\>\>\>\>\Sigma(\tilde{x}_{m})\cap\tilde{f}^{m}({\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x}))={\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x}_{m}),

for every
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Remark 10
.

Notice that
f
is special if, and only if,
{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x})=\emptyset
for every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
.

Our assumptions that
f
is a strongly transitive transformation of
{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
implies the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
f
is not special. Then, for every fiber
\Sigma(\tilde{x})
, the set
{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x})
is not empty.

Proof.

Assume that for some
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
the set
{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x})
is empty. Take any fiber
\Sigma(\tilde{a})\subset{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and any point
\tilde{b}\in\Sigma(\tilde{a})
. Then, by Corollary
3
there exist sequences of points such that
\tilde{a}_{k}\to\tilde{a}
and
\tilde{b}_{k}\to\tilde{b}
, with
\tilde{b}_{k}\in\Sigma(\tilde{a}_{k})
, and, for each
k
, both points
\tilde{a}_{k}
and
\tilde{b}_{k}
belong to
\tilde{f}^{-k}(\Sigma(\tilde{x}))
. By our assumption, this implies that

\alpha(\tilde{f}^{k}(\tilde{a}_{k}),\tilde{f}^{k}(\tilde{b}_{k}))=0,\>\>\>\textrm{for all}\>\>k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Since the derivative of
f
is a linear isomorphism this implies that

\alpha(\tilde{a}_{k},\tilde{b}_{k})=0,\>\>\>\textrm{for all}\>\>k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

By continuity of the assignment
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\mapsto\alpha(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
we deduce that

\alpha(\tilde{a},\tilde{b})=0.

This proves that
{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{a})=\emptyset
, which implies that
f
must be special and completes the proof.
∎

By pushing a bit further the above argument we can obtain an even stronger conclusion.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
f
is not special. Then, the set
{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x})
is open and dense in the fiber
\Sigma(\tilde{x})
, for every
\tilde{x}\in\Sigma(\tilde{x}).

Proof.

Openness is direct from the definition and the continuity of the function
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\mapsto\alpha(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
. Assume the lemma is false. Then, for some
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
the set
{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x})
has non-empty interior. In particular, there exists a small cylinder
I\subset{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x})
. By backward expansion along fibers there must exist an integer
n>0
such that

\Sigma(\tilde{f}^{-n}(\tilde{x}))\subset\tilde{f}^{-n}(I)\subset\tilde{f}^{-n}({\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x})).

By equation (
8
) we deduce that
{\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{f}^{-n}(\tilde{x}))=\Sigma(\tilde{f}^{-n}(\tilde{x}))
, which contradicts Lemma
4.1
.
∎

The above result implies the dichotomies presented in
[
CM22
,
MT16
]
, but for the strongly transitive case.

4.2.
The non-transversality set

When
f
is not special, the strongly transitive assumption implies that in every fiber there are infinitely many different unstable directions. In our case, this is made explicit by Lemma
4.2
. We are now going to consider how the measure
\tilde{\mu}
“sees” these different unstable directions. The natural strategy then is to consider the
“bad”
set of points for which the measure do not “see” any transversality at all.

Definition 4.3
.

The
non-transversality set
is the set
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=\{\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}:\tilde{\mu}_{x}({\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x}))=0\}
.

We have that
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
is
\tilde{f}
-invariant. Therefore, by ergodicity,
\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{\mathbf{B}})\in\{0,1\}
. Notice that
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
depends on the measure, although, for simplicity, our notation does not reflect that.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
f
is not special and that
\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{\mathbf{B}})=1
. Then
\operatorname{supp}(\tilde{\mu})
has empty interior.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, it suffices to show that for every
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
no point
\tilde{y}\in{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x})
lies in
\operatorname{supp}(\tilde{\mu})
. Take
\tilde{y}\in{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x})
. By continuity, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x=\pi(\tilde{x})
such that for every
\tilde{z}\in\pi^{-1}(U)\cap\tilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})
there exists a cylinder
I(\tilde{z})\subset{\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{z})
. In particular,
\cup_{\tilde{z}\in\pi^{-1}(U)\cap\tilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})}I(\tilde{z})
is a neighborhood of
\tilde{y}
. Since we assumed
\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{\mathbf{B}})=1
, we have that for almost every
\tilde{z}
it holds
\tilde{\mu}_{\tilde{z}}(I(\tilde{z}))=0
. By Rokhlin’s theorem we deduce that

\tilde{\mu}\left(\bigcup_{\tilde{z}\in\pi^{-1}(U)\cap\tilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{cu}(\tilde{x})}I(\tilde{z})\right)=0,

which implies that
\tilde{y}\notin\operatorname{supp}(\tilde{\mu})
.
∎

4.3.
A 0 or 1 law

Let us assume now that
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\operatorname{erg}}_{f}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})
is a fully supported ergodic invariant measure. We deduce from Lemma
4.4
that for
\mu
-almost every
x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
it holds
\tilde{\mu}_{x}({\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x}))>0
. That is, under the full support assumption, the measure “sees”
some
transversality at each fiber. However, in our setting this is a
0 or 1 law
: once
some
transversality is seen at almost every fiber we immediately have that the measure actually sees transversality
almost everywhere
in almost every fiber. More precisely, the goal of this section is to prove the following.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
\mu
-almost every
x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
it holds
\tilde{\mu}_{x}({\mathcal{N}}(\tilde{x}))=1
.

In our case we can take advantage of the fractal structure of the fibers and give a point-density proof of the 0 or 1 law that is much simpler then the proof in
[
ALOS24
]
, which is based on a martingale convergence argument.

Lemma 4.6
.

[
Mil08
, Proposition 2.10]

Suppose that
(X,d)
is a compact ultrametric space,
\mu
is a probability measure on

, and
B

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mu(B\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x))}{\mu(B_{\varepsilon}(x))}=1,

for
\mu

Recall that, by Remark
2

is a compact ultrametric space. In particular, the above lemma applies to the measure

.

4.3.1.
Proof of Lemma
4.5

Assume by contradiction the existence of a
\tilde{\mu}
positive measure set
\tilde{A}
such that
\tilde{\mu}_{x}({\mathcal{P}}(\tilde{x}))>0
for every
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{A}

which is positive a.e. since

Observe that, in the metric given in Lemma

5.1.
Stopping times

Remember that the notation
\hat{\lambda}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(n)
stands for the Lyapunov norm of the n-th derivative restricted to the direction

\hat{\lambda}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\|D\tilde{f}^{n}(\tilde{x})|_{E^{c}}\|_{L,\tilde{x}_{n}}.

Consider
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and
\tilde{x}^{u}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x})

as the function

(9)

Given the value of

we define

(10)

The function

is called the

. We refer to the pair
(,\tau,t)
as the
stopping times
. These functions can be computed for
\tilde{\mu}
-almost every
\tilde{x}
and for all
\tilde{x}^{u}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{x})
by Lemma
2.21

Remark 11

It follows from Remark

and

These functions satisfy the following quasi-isometric estimate.

For a
\tilde{\mu}

\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}

(11)

for all

By compactness and Lemma

for all

The inequalities from both the above equation and (

\delta>0
and
K\subset{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
a compact subset of measure
\mu(K)>1-\delta
. The result below is a simple consequence of Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem and Egorov’s theorem of measure theory. See Lemma 11.4 of
[
ALOS24
]
for a proof.

Lemma 6.1
.

There exists a compact set
K^{\circ}\subset K
still of measure
\mu(K^{\circ})>1-\delta
and an integer
T=T(\delta)>1
such that, whenever
n\geq T
, one has

(15)

\operatorname{card}\{j\in[0,n];\tilde{f}^{j}(\tilde{x})\in K\}>(1-\delta)n,

for every
\tilde{x}\in K^{\circ}
.

We let
\xi^{u}
be a measurable partition of
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
subordinate to the lamination
\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}
. Given a pair
(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u})
such that
\tilde{x}^{u}\in\xi^{u}(\tilde{x})
, we define the subset of integers

E(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}};\tilde{f}^{\tau(\ell)}(\tilde{x}^{u})\in K^{\circ},\>\tilde{f}^{t(\ell)}(\tilde{x})\in K^{\circ}\}.

Given
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
and an integer
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let us consider the set

(16)

Q^{u}(\tilde{x},\ell)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\{\tilde{x}^{u}\in\xi^{u}(\tilde{x});\tilde{x}^{u}\in K^{\circ},\>\ell\in E(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u})\}.

Considering a continuous function
\eta:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
with
\eta(0)=0
and an integer
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the set

K(\ell)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\{\tilde{x}\in K^{\circ};\>\mu^{u}_{\tilde{x}}\left(Q^{u}(\tilde{x},\ell)\right)>1-\eta(\delta)\}.

This is the set of points
\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
for which one has “a large set to choose
\tilde{x}^{u}\in\xi^{u}(\tilde{x})
from” so that the length-
\ell

Y
-configuration
Y(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u},\ell,\varepsilon)
has all its four upper points being “highly recurrent” to the compact set
K
. This high recurrence is used in the end of Lemma
6.4
to obtain simultaneous returns to
K
.

The following result has the same proof as Proposition 11.6 in
[
ALOS24

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists a continuous function

, with

, (which depends only on
f
) so that the set

6.2.
The Eskin–Mirzakhnani combinatorial lemma

The (combinatorial) result below is largely inspired by Lemma 12.8 of
[
EM18
]
and, though elementary, it is one of the key ideas of our matching argument.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\Delta>1
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in(0,1)
be numerical constants satisfying the following assumptions:

•

1-\eta_{1}>0.5
and

•

\Delta m\eta_{2}<0.5
.

Assume that
(X,\nu,{\mathcal{B}})
and
(Y,\hat{\nu},\hat{{\mathcal{B}}})
are probability spaces, each endowed with a finite collection
\{J_{1},...,J_{k}\}\subset{\mathcal{B}}
and
\{\hat{J}_{1},\dots,\hat{J}_{k}\}\subset\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}
of measurable sets so that the following three properties are satisfied:

(1)

X=J_{1}\cup\dots\cup J_{k}
and
Y=\hat{J}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\hat{J}_{k}
,

(2)

If
y\in Y
then
\operatorname{card}\{i\in[1,k];y\in\hat{J}_{i}\}\leq m
,

(3)

\nu(J_{i})\asymp_{\Delta}\hat{\nu}(\hat{J}_{k})
.

Suppose that there exist measurable sets
Q\subset X
and
\hat{Q}\subset Y
so that

\nu(Q)>1-\eta_{1}\>\>\>\textrm{and}\>\>\>\hat{\nu}(\hat{Q})>1-\eta_{2}.

Then, there exists
i\in[1,k]
so that
J_{i}\cap Q\neq\emptyset
and
\hat{J}_{i}\cap\hat{Q}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
I(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\{i\in[1,k];J_{i}\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}
and
I(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\{i\in[1,k];\hat{J}_{i}\cap\hat{Q}\neq\emptyset\}
. Assume that
I(X)\cap I(Y)=\emptyset
. Then,

\displaystyle 0.5

\displaystyle<1-\eta_{1}<\nu(Q)\leq\sum_{i\in I(X)}\nu(Q\cap J_{i})\leq\sum_{i\in I(X)}\nu(J_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\Delta\sum_{i\in I(X)}\hat{\nu}(\hat{J}_{i})\leq\Delta\sum_{i\notin I(Y)}\hat{\nu}(\hat{J}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\Delta m\hat{\nu}\big(\cup_{i\notin I(Y)}\hat{\nu}(\hat{J}_{i})\big)\leq\Delta m\hat{\nu}(Y\setminus\hat{Q})

\displaystyle<\Delta m\eta_{2}<0.5,

which is a contradiction.
∎

6.3.
Good matching of
Y
-configurations

We consider, given
\delta>0
, a compact Lusin set
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
with
\tilde{\mu}({\mathcal{L}})>1-\delta
. Given numbers
\beta>1
,
T>1
and a compact subset
K
of measure
\tilde{\mu}(K)>1-\delta
, we say that a pair
Y(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u},\varepsilon,\ell)
and
Y(\tilde{y},\tilde{y}^{u},\varepsilon,\ell)
of
(\ell,T)
-
Y
-configurations are in
good matching
relatively to
(K,{\mathcal{L}},T,\beta)
if:

(1)

(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u},\tilde{y},\tilde{y}^{u})
is a
(\beta,\ell)
quadrilateral,

(2)

\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in K\cap{\mathcal{L}}
,

(3)

there exist
\tilde{a}\in J(\tilde{x}^{u})\cap K\cap{\mathcal{L}}
,
\tilde{b}\in J(\tilde{y}^{u})\cap K\cap{\mathcal{L}}
and
m\in(\tau(\ell)-T,\tau(\ell)-T)
such that
\tilde{a}_{m},\tilde{b}_{m}\in K\cap{\mathcal{L}}
, and

(4)

there exists
\widehat{m}\in(t(\ell)-T,t(\ell)+T)
such that
\tilde{x}_{\widehat{m}},\tilde{y}_{\widehat{m}}\in K\cap{\mathcal{L}}
.

The main result of this section which is also our main ingredient to prove Theorem
A
is the following. For the statement we recall the following definition: we say that a subset
D\subset{\mathbb{N}}
of integers has
positive density
if

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{card}(D\cap[1,n])>0.

In particular, notice that every positive density subset
D\subset{\mathbb{N}}
is an infinity set.

Lemma 6.4
.

There exist constants
\delta>0
,
\beta>1
,
T>1
, a Lusin set
{\mathcal{L}}
with measure
\tilde{\mu}({\mathcal{L}})>1-\delta
such that: for every compact set
K
with
\tilde{\mu}(K)>1-2\delta
there exists a subset
D\subset{\mathbb{N}}
with positive density such that for every
\ell\in D
large enough, and every
\varepsilon>0
small enough, one can find a pair
Y(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u},\varepsilon,\ell)
and
Y(\tilde{y},\tilde{y}^{u},\varepsilon,\ell)
of
(\ell,T)
-
Y
-configurations which are in good matching relatively to
(K,{\mathcal{L}},T,\beta)
.

The proof of Lemma
6.4
is rather involved. For the sake of clarity we shall present it in several steps. This result is parallel to Proposition 11.8 in
[
ALOS24
]
. The main difference in our case is that we consider
non-uniform expansion along the center direction
, and thus we need to be much more careful with the choice of constants.

Let us give now a brief sketch of the argument: we choose
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
two Lusin sets along with constants
\delta_{2}<<\delta_{1}
so that
\tilde{\mu}({\mathcal{L}}_{i})>1-\delta_{i}
,
i=1,2
. With the set
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
we apply Proposition
6
and obtain a constant
\beta_{1}>1
. With this constant we apply the synchronization of stopping times (Proposition
7
) and find a constant
T_{1}>1
. Once these parameters are fixed we can produce many coupled
(\ell,T_{1})-Y-
configurations. However, the definition of
good matching
requires the six points
\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{a}_{m},\tilde{b}_{m},\tilde{x}_{\hat{m}}
and
\tilde{y}_{\hat{m}}
lying inside the Lusin set. So, our strategy is to use the Lusin set
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
to give uniform estimations allowing us to search for a configuration in good matching relatively to the
larger
Lusin set
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the formal proof of Lemma
6.4
.

a Lusin set along with a constant
\delta_{1}

. We shall first choose and fix

.

Consider the function

in Proposition

be the constant given in the Synchronization Proposition

With this choice of

Lemma 6.5

(Overlap control of unstable dynamical balls)
.

There exists a constant
m=m({\mathcal{L}}_{1})
with the following property. Assume that
(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u},\tilde{y},\tilde{y}^{u})
is a
(\beta_{1},\ell)
-quadrilateral, with
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
. Given a compact set
Q\subset\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{x})\cap{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
with
\tilde{\mu}(Q)>1-\eta_{1}
, given any finite cover
X=\cup_{i=1}^{k}J_{i}
of

Q\cap\left(\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{x})\setminus\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1/\beta_{1}}(\tilde{x})\right),

by unstable dynamical balls
J_{i}=J^{u}(\tilde{x}^{u}_{i})
such that for every
\tilde{z}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{x})

\operatorname{card}\{i;\tilde{z}\in J_{i}\}\leq 2,

defining
\tilde{y}^{u}_{i}=H^{cs}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}(\tilde{x}^{u}_{i})
and
\hat{J}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}J^{u}(\tilde{y}^{u}_{i})
, then for every
\tilde{b}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{y})

\operatorname{card}\{i;\tilde{b}\in\hat{J}_{i}\}\leq m.

Proof.

Take a point
\tilde{b}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}(\tilde{y})
and denote by

\hat{J}_{i_{1}},\dots,\hat{J}_{i_{m}}

all the intervals within the collection
\{\hat{J}_{1},\dots,\hat{J}_{k}\}
that contains the point
\tilde{b}
. Consider
J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\cup_{j=1}^{m}J_{i_{j}}
and
\hat{J}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\cup_{j=1}^{m}\hat{J}_{i_{j}}
. We shall bound from above the length of
\hat{J}
and from below the length of
J
. The estimate on
m
will then follow from the Hölder continuity of center holonomies.

For simplicity, we also denote

\tau_{i_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\tau(\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{u}_{i_{j}},\varepsilon,\ell)\>\>\textrm{and}\>\>\>\hat{\tau}_{i_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\tau(\tilde{y},\tilde{y}^{u}_{i_{j}},\varepsilon,\ell).

Let
\mathcal{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\{\tau_{i_{1}},\dots,\tau_{i_{m}},\hat{\tau}_{i_{1}},\dots,\hat{\tau}_{i_{m}}\}
and define
\tau^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\min\mathcal{T}
. For the bound on
\hat{J}
one observe that
\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(\tilde{b})\in\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(\hat{J}_{i_{j}})
, for every
j=1,\dots,m
. Since
|\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(\hat{J}_{i_{j}})|\leq 2
it follows that
|\hat{J}|\leq 4
.

For the bound on
J
, since no more than two intervals
J_{i_{j}}
overlap at a time we can estimate

\left|\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(J_{i_{j}})\right|=\left|\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(J_{i_{j}})\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(J_{i_{j}})\right|

However, one has, by definition of a dynamical ball, that
\left|\tilde{f}^{\tau_{i_{j}}}(J_{i_{j}})\right|=2
. Also, by Proposition
7
we have that
|\tau_{i_{j}}-\tau(\tilde{y},\tilde{b},\varepsilon,\ell)|<T({\mathcal{L}}_{1},\beta_{1})
and also
|\hat{\tau}_{i_{j}}-\tau(\tilde{y},\tilde{b},\varepsilon,\ell)|<T({\mathcal{L}}_{1},\beta_{1})
, for every
j=1,...,m
. Thus, by the triangle inequality we have that for every
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
it holds

\tau-\tau^{*}<2T({\mathcal{L}}_{1},\beta_{1}).

These two facts together imply that

\left|\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(J_{i_{j}})\right|\geq 2e^{-\xi(f)T_{1}},

where
\xi(f)=\max\{\|Df(x)v\|/\|v\|;x\in{\mathbb{T}^{2}},v\in T_{x}{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\}
and
T_{1}=T_{1}({\mathcal{L}}_{1},\beta_{1})
is a constant depend on the Lusin set
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
. Thus,

\left|\tilde{f}^{\tau^{*}}(J_{i_{j}})\right|\geq 2me^{-\xi(f)T_{1}}.

Since
J
is a segment of unstable manifold, there must exist a pair of points
\tilde{a},\tilde{a}^{\prime}
on
J
such that
d^{u}(\tilde{a},\tilde{a}^{\prime})>me^{-\xi(f)T_{1}}
. Since every point of
J
is at a distance of at most 1 from some
\tilde{x}^{u}_{i_{j}}
we conclude that there must exist a pair of points
x^{u}_{i_{j}}
and
x^{u}_{i_{r}}
on
{\mathcal{W}}^{u}(\tilde{x}_{\tau^{*}})
such that

d^{u}(x^{u}_{i_{j}},x^{u}_{i_{r}})>me^{-\xi(f)T_{1}}-2.

By the Hölder continuity of the center bundle, we have that

d^{u}(x^{u}_{i_{j}},x^{u}_{i_{r}})\leq C(f)d^{u}(y^{u}_{i_{j}},y^{u}_{i_{r}})^{\theta(f)}\leq C(f)4^{\theta(f)}.

We deduce that

m\leq e^{\xi(f)T_{1}}\left(C(f)4^{\theta(f)}+2\right),

with
T_{1}=T_{1}({\mathcal{L}}_{1},\beta_{1})
. This ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.6

\mu^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(Q)>1-\eta_{1}
. We now take a finite cover of the compact set
Q\cap\left(\overline{\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{1}(\tilde{x})\setminus\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{\beta^{-1}}(\tilde{y})}\right)
by unstable dynamical balls
\{J_{i}=J(\tilde{x}^{u}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
, with
\tilde{x}^{u}_{i}\in Q
, for every
i=1,\dots,k
. We may assume, up to taking a subcover, that no more than two intervals
J_{i}
overlap at the same point. We define
X=\cup_{i=1}^{k}J_{i}
. Observe that

\frac{\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(X\setminus Q)}{\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(X)}\leq\frac{\eta_{1}}{1-\eta_{1}}<0.5,

due to (
17
).

For each
i
consider
\tilde{y}^{u}_{i}=H^{cs}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}(\tilde{x}^{u}_{i})
and
\hat{J}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}J(\tilde{y}^{u}_{i})
. We define
Y=\cup_{i=1}^{k}\hat{J}_{i}
. From Lemma
6.6
, if we define probabilities
\nu=\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}|_{X}/\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{x}}(X)
and
\hat{\nu}=\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{y}}|_{Y}/\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{y}}(Y)
we have that

\nu(J_{i})\asymp_{\Delta}\hat{\nu}(\hat{J}_{i}),

for every
i=1,...,k
and for some constant
\Delta=\Delta({\mathcal{L}}_{1})
. By our choice of
\delta_{2}
we have that

\hat{\nu}(Y\setminus Q^{u}_{2}(\tilde{y},\ell))=\frac{\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{y}}(Y\setminus Q^{u}_{2}(\tilde{y},\ell))}{\tilde{\mu}^{u}_{\tilde{y}}(Y)}<\frac{\eta_{2}}{\zeta({\mathcal{L}}_{1})},

where
\zeta({\mathcal{L}}_{1})
is given in Remark
13
. Thus, by (
18
) our claim now follows directly from Lemma
6.3
.

6.3.5.
Ending the proof

We define
\tilde{a}=\tilde{x}^{u}
and
\tilde{b}\in J(\tilde{y}^{u})\cap Q^{u}_{2}(\tilde{y},\ell)
given by the claim. Thus we have that

\tilde{f}^{\tau_{\tilde{a}}}(\tilde{a}),\tilde{f}^{\tau_{\tilde{b}}}(\tilde{b}),\tilde{f}^{t}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{x}),\tilde{f}^{t_{\tilde{y}}}(\tilde{y})\in K_{2},

where
\tau_{a},\tau_{b},t_{\tilde{x}},t_{\tilde{y}}
are the stopping times corresponding to the
Y
configurations
Y(\tilde{x},\tilde{a},\varepsilon,\ell)
and
Y(\tilde{y},\tilde{b},\varepsilon,\ell)
. In particular, these four points are
(\delta_{1},T_{1})
-recurrent to
K
. By the synchronization of stopping times given in Proposition
7
, we have that
|\tau_{\tilde{a}}-\tau_{\tilde{b}}|<T_{1}
and
|t_{\tilde{x}}-t_{\tilde{y}}|<T_{1}
. Let
\tau\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textrm{d}ef.}}{{=}}\min\{\tau_{\tilde{a}},\tau_{\tilde{b}}\}
. Since
\delta_{1}<0.3
, for
\star=\tilde{a},\tilde{b}
, the set

\{m\in[\tau,\tau+5T_{1}];\tilde{f}^{m}(\star)\in K\}

has density larger than
0.7
in the interval
[\tau,\tau+5T_{1}]
. Thus, there exists
m\in[\tau,\tau+5T_{1}]
such that

\tilde{f}^{m}(\tilde{a}),\tilde{f}^{m}(\tilde{b})\in K.

With a similar reasoning one deduces the existence of an integer
\hat{m}
such that
\tilde{f}^{\hat{m}}(\tilde{x}),\tilde{f}^{\hat{m}}(\tilde{y})\in K
. This completes the proof of Lemma
6.4
. ∎

7.
Invariance of leaf-wise quotient measures by affine maps

We are going to complete the proof of Theorem
A
in this section by demonstrating that there exists a positive measure set of points in
{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
for which the associated leaf-wise quotient measure is invariant by sufficiently many small affine maps. Once we show this, we apply a result of Kalinin and Katok to conclude.

7.1.
Small affine maps

We say that a collection
{\mathcal{A}}\subset\operatorname{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
of affine maps of the real line is a collection of
M
-
small affine maps
if there exists a constant
M>1
such that for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exist
0<\varepsilon<1/n
and
\psi\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that

(1)

M^{-1}<|\psi^{\prime}(0)|<M
;

(2)

\varepsilon M^{-1}<|\psi(0)|<\varepsilon M
;

The proof of the result below can be found in
[
ALOS24
]
, Section 8. See also
[
BRH17
]
, Section 7 or the original reference
[
KK07
]
.

Lemma 7.1

(Kalinin–Katok)
.

Assume that there exist
M>1
and
\delta>0
and a set
G\subset{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}
with
\tilde{\mu}(G)\geq\delta
such that for every
\tilde{x}\in G
there exists a collection
{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{x})\subset\operatorname{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
of
M
-small affine maps satisfying

\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}\propto\psi_{*}\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}},\>\>\>\textrm{for every}\>\>\>\psi\in{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{x}).

Then,
\mu
is an SRB measure.

By Lemma
2.15
we know that every SRB measure is absolutely continuous with respect to
\operatorname{Leb}_{{\mathbb{T}^{2}}}
.

Thus, to prove Theorem
A
we need to show that the assumptions of Lemma
7.1
are satisfied. Consider
\delta>0,\beta>1
and
T>1
the constants given in Lemma
6.4
. Let also
{\mathcal{L}}
be the Lusin set with measure
\tilde{\mu}({\mathcal{L}})>1-\delta
, also given by Lemma
6.4
.

Lemma 7.2
.

There exists
M=M(\delta,\beta,T)>1
such that, for every
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exists a measurable set
G(\varepsilon,M)\subset{\mathcal{L}}
, with
\tilde{\mu}\left(G(\varepsilon,M)\right)\geq\delta
such that, for every
\tilde{x}\in G(\varepsilon,M)
, there exists:

•

\tilde{y}\in W_{{\operatorname{loc}}}^{c}(\tilde{x})
, with
{\mathcal{R}}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{y}))\asymp_{M}\varepsilon
, and

•

a linear map
B:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, with slope
B^{\prime}(0)\asymp_{M}1
,

such that
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{y}}\propto B_{*}\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}
.

7.1.1.
Proof of Theorem
A

We claim that Lemma
7.2
implies Theorem
A
. Indeed, define

G=\{\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\;:\;\tilde{x}\in G(1/N,M)\>\textrm{for infinitely many}\>N\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

Notice that
\tilde{\mu}(G)\geq\delta
. We need to show that for every
\tilde{x}\in G
there exists a collection of small affine maps which preserves, up to a factor, the measure
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}
. To see this, fix some
\varepsilon
and suppose
\tilde{x}\in G(\varepsilon,M)
. Using Lemma
3.5
we obtain an affine map
h^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{y}}\propto(h^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}})_{*}\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}
, and
h^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}(s)=\rho_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{y})s+{\mathcal{R}}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{y})
. Since
G(\varepsilon,M)\subset{\mathcal{L}}
, by Lemma
7.2
, we may assume that

\rho^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{y})\asymp_{M}1.

Thus, by defining
\psi=B\circ(h^{2}_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}})^{-1}
we have that
\psi
satisfies items (1) and (2) of the definition of small affine maps for some constant
\hat{M}=\hat{M}(M)
. This shows that for every
\tilde{x}\in G
there exists a collection of
\hat{M}
-small affine maps preserving
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}
, up to a factor. Theorem
A
now follows from Lemma
7.1
.∎

7.2.
The drift argument: proof of Lemma
7.2

We now proceed to show how to deduce Lemma
7.2
from Lemma
6.4
. The first step is to determine the constant
M=M(\delta,T,\beta)>1
. We first consider the movement of quotient measures along a “
{\mathcal{L}}
-good”
Y
-configuration, with particular attention to the factor relating the measures.

Lemma 7.3
.

There exists
M=M({\mathcal{L}},T)>1
such that, given
\varepsilon>0
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if there exists
(\ell,T)

Y
-configuration
Y(\tilde{x},\tilde{a},\varepsilon,\ell)
satisfying

\tilde{x},\tilde{a},\tilde{a}_{m},\tilde{x}_{\hat{m}}\in{\mathcal{L}},

then there exist
C>1
, with
C\asymp_{M}1
, and linear map
B:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, with slope
B^{\prime}(0)\asymp_{M}1
, such that

\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{a}_{m}}=CB_{*}\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}_{\hat{m}}}.

Proof.

It follows from the basic moves of leaf-wise quotient measures Lemma
3.5
that
\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{a}_{m}}=CB_{*}\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}_{\hat{m}}}
, where
B
is the linear map with slope

B^{\prime}(0)=\rho^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{a})\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\hat{m})\left(\lambda^{c}_{\tilde{a}}(m)\right)^{-1},

and the constant
C
is given by

C=\left(\hat{\nu}^{c}_{\tilde{x}_{\hat{m}}}(B^{-1}([-1,1]))\right)^{-1}.

We claim that these quantities are uniformly bounded. Indeed, by the definition of the stopping times and from Lemma
2.20
we have that

\hat{\lambda}^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(t(\ell))\left(\hat{\lambda}^{c}_{\tilde{a}}(\tau(\ell))\right)^{-1}

is uniformly bounded. Since the Lyapunov norm and the background norm are uniformly comparable inside the Lusin set, this implies that
B^{\prime}(0)/\rho^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{a})
is bounded by a constant depending on
{\mathcal{L}}
and
T
. Since
\tilde{a},\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}
, we also have that
\rho^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{a})
is uniformly bounded (with constant depending on
{\mathcal{L}}
). A similar reasoning applies to
C
.
∎

We can now fix
M
. We assume that the constant given by Lemma
7.3
is large enough so that it satisfies also the following: if
\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{L}}
, then for every
\tilde{a}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{W}}}^{u}_{\sigma^{T}}(\tilde{x})
one has
\rho^{c}_{\tilde{x}}(\tilde{a})\asymp_{M}1
, where
\sigma=\max\{\lambda^{u}_{\tilde{x}};\tilde{x}\in{\mathbb{T}_{f}^{2}}\}
. With this choice, we continue to have
M=M({\mathcal{L}},T)
. We are now in position to complete our proof.
