Topological Derivative for Shallow Water Equations


Abstract
Coastal erosion is a major and growing environmental problem that describes the movement of sand caused by tides, waves or currents. Several phenomena contribute to the significant advance of the sea. These include climate change, with rising sea levels due to the melting of ice at the Earth’s poles, the amplification of the tidal effect, leading to the transport of large masses of sand, storms, etc. We contribute to this problem by using topological shape optimization techniques applied to a PDE model describing coastal erosion. We use shallow water equations as a model.

1
Introduction

Coastal erosion is a phenomenon that has existed since time immemorial. It is defined as the loss of material due to the advance of the sea along the coast. There are many causes, but in general we can point to an increase in ocean height, and an acceleration in the transport of sand from beaches to oceans by increasingly powerful waves. For several decades now, scientists have been studying these phenomena, and a number of questions have arisen concerning coastal erosion: how can we improve the devices built to mitigate coastal erosion by acting on their shape? and where should they be installed? Engineers, for their part, generally recommend the installation of dikes and dams to prevent or slow down coastal erosion phenomena, based on a study of the minimum action linked to the geometry of the fields. All these efforts produce interesting results, but they do not fully satisfy the community interested in these issues. For us, the study of topological or geometrical analysis tools of optimal shapes, and in particular the topological shape optimization for partial differential equations (PDE) modeling coastal erosion dynamics is interesting. We limit ourselves here to the topological shape optimization by focusing on the theoretical aspects. Coastal erosion can be modeled by several types of partial differential equations. However we prefer to use the shallow water equations (SWEs) also known as Saint Venant equation. SWEs are a system of partial differential equations derived from the Navier-Stokes equations, that describe the motion of Newtonian fluids. They are used in many domains. For example, in river hydraulics, the two-dimensional SWEs can be used as a river model. This was studied by M. Honnorat in his PhD thesis
[
13
]
. As a reminder, river hydraulics is a science that enables the understanding and development of natural or artificial free-surface channels
[
11
]
. Recently, L. Schlegel and V. Schulz have done interesting work in
[
21
]
on shape optimization for the mitigation of coastal erosion using shallow water equations. Also SWEs are often used to model the flow of oceanographic and atmospheric fluids. Models of such systems can be used to predict areas potentially affected by pollution, coastal erosion, melting polar ice caps, etc. In the mathematical model, the flow is represented by a state variable which is the solution of the SWEs. Therefore the main objective of this paper is to provide theoretical results for optimality conditions of shape functionals under the constraints of the shallow water equation. To do this, we will rely mainly on the work of Delfour

2
PDE-constrained topology optimization

The shallow water model describes the evolution of a fluid by means of three equations: the continuity equation which translates the conservation of mass and the two momentum equations. These equations are obtained by integrating the Navier-Stokes equations over the vertical or simply over the entire depth with a hydrostatic approximation, i.e. neglecting the vertical acceleration. This approximation is necessary to translate the pressure into water height. However there are several variants of this model and we limit ourselves to the following formulation
[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}+F(U,\psi)=0\;\;\;\Omega\times[0,T]

(2.1)

U:\Omega\times[0,T[\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
F
are respectively given by

\displaystyle U=(h,hu,hv)\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;F(U,\psi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\partial(hu)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial(hv)}{\partial y}\\
\frac{\partial(hu^{2}+\frac{g}{2}h^{2})}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial(huv)}{\partial y}+gh\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial(huv)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial(hv^{2}+\frac{g}{2}h^{2})}{\partial y}+gh\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\end{array}\right)

with initial and boundary conditions that we will define. Here
\Omega
is a regular open domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
t
is the time variable and
x
and
y
are the two-dimensional space variables.
u,\,v:\Omega\times[0,T[\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
respectively represent the vertical and horizontal velocity of the water,
h
is the height of the water and
g
is the gravity and
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the topography of the bottom. The effects of bottom friction and the frictional forces of atmospheric pressure are neglected. The PDE-constrained topology optimization problem reads:

\displaystyle\min_{\Omega}

\displaystyle J(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla U|^{2}\;dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|U-U_{d}|^{2}\;dxdt,

\displaystyle s.t.\qquad\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}+F(U,\psi)=0\;\;\Omega\times[0,T]

The initial and boundary conditions are defined in the section
4
after the variational formulation of the problem is performed. In the next section, we will detail some of the ingredients we need to perform topological derivative calculations using the min-max approach.

3.1
Notations and definitions

Definition 3.1

A Lagrangian function is a function of the form

\displaystyle(s,x,y)\mapsto\mathcal{L}(s,x,y):[0,\tau]\times X\times Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;\;\;\;\tau>0

where
X
is a vector espace,
Y
a non empty subset of vector space and the function
y\mapsto\mathcal{L}(s,x,y)
is affine.

Associate with the parameter
s
the parametrized minimax

\displaystyle s\mapsto\textrm{g}(s)=\inf_{x\in X}\sup_{y\in Y}\mathcal{L}(s,x,y):[0,\tau]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\textrm{dg}(0)=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\textrm{g}(s)-\textrm{g}(0)}{s}.

When the limits exist, we will use the following notations

\displaystyle d_{s}\mathcal{L}(0,x,y)=\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(s,x,y)-\mathcal{L}(0,x,y)}{s}

\displaystyle\varphi\in X,\;\;d_{x}\mathcal{L}(s,x,y;\varphi)=\lim_{\theta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(s,x+\theta\varphi,y)-\mathcal{L}(s,x,y)}{\theta}

\displaystyle\phi\in Y\;\;d_{y}\mathcal{L}(s,x,y;\phi)=\lim_{\theta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(s,x,y+\theta\phi)-\mathcal{L}(s,x,y)}{\theta}.

Since
\mathcal{L}(s,x,y)
is affine en
y
, for all
(s,x)\in[0,\tau]\times X
,

\displaystyle\forall\;y,\psi\in Y\;\;d_{y}\mathcal{L}(s,x,y;\psi)=\mathcal{L}(s,x,\psi)-\mathcal{L}(s,x,0)=d_{y}\mathcal{L}(s,x,0,\psi).

(3.1)

The state equation at
s\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{Find}\;x^{s}\in X\;\;\text{such that for all}\;\;\psi\in Y,\;\;d_{y}\mathcal{L}(s,x^{s},0;\psi)=0.

(3.2)

The set of states
x^{s}
at
s\geq 0
is denoted

\displaystyle E(s)=\left\{x^{s}\in X,\;\;\forall\;\psi\in Y,\;\;d_{y}\mathcal{L}(s,x^{s},0;\psi)=0\right\}.

(3.3)

The adjoint equation at
s\geq 0
is

\displaystyle\text{Find}\;p^{s}\in Y\;\;\text{such that for all}\;\;\varphi\in X,\;\;d_{x}\mathcal{L}(s,x^{s},p^{s};\varphi)=0.

(3.4)

The set of solutions
p^{s}
at
s\geq 0
is denoted

\displaystyle Y(s,x^{s})=\left\{p^{s}\in Y,\;\;\forall\;\varphi\in X,\;\;d_{x}\mathcal{L}(s,x^{s},p^{s};\varphi)=0\right\}.

(3.5)

Finally the set of minimisers for the minimax is given by

\displaystyle X(s)=\left\{x^{s}\in X,\;\,\textrm{g}(s)=\inf_{x\in X}\sup_{y\in Y}\mathcal{L}(s,x,y)=\sup_{y\in Y}\mathcal{L}(s,x^{s},y)\right\}.

(3.6)

Lemma 3.1

(Constrained infimum and minimax)

We have the following assertions

(i)

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in X}\sup_{y\in Y}\mathcal{L}(s,x,y)=\inf_{x\in E(s)}\mathcal{L}(s,x,0)

(ii)

The minimax
\textrm{g}(s)=+\infty
if and only if
E(s)=\emptyset
. And in this case we have
X(s)=X
.

(iii)

If
E(s)\neq\emptyset
, then

\displaystyle X(s)=\left\{x^{s}\in E(s):\;\;\mathcal{L}(s,x^{s},0)=\inf_{x\in E(s)}\mathcal{L}(s,x,0)\right\}\subset E(s)

and
\textrm{g}(s)<+\infty
.

Proof.

Maximal Hardy-Littlewood function

Definition 3.6

The maximal Hardy-Littlewood function is an operator
M
that associates to any locally integrable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
a function
Mf
; this function
Mf
defined on each point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the upper bound of the mean values of
|f|
on balls centered at
x
.

Mathematical formulation
: To any locally integrable function
f\in L^{1}_{Loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can associate the maximal Hardy-Littlewood function
Mf:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
defined by

\displaystyle Mf(x)=\sup_{r\geq 0}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|f(t)|\;d\lambda_{n}(t)

where
B(x,r)
denotes the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
ball centered at
x
and of radius
r
and
\lambda_{n}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Maximal Hardy-Littlewood inequality:

Proposition 3.4

For any integrable application
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any real
c>0
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\left([Mf\geq c]\right)\leq 3^{n}\frac{\|f\|_{1}}{c}.

Proof.

If you want to go to the limit when
d\rightarrow c^{-}
, just show that

\displaystyle\forall d\geq 0\;\;\lambda_{n}\left([Mf>d]\right)\leq 3^{n}\|f\|_{1}/d

and for this by interior regularity, it suffices to show that for any compact
K
included in
[Mf>d]

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(K)\leq 3^{n}\|f\|_{1}/d.

By compactness,
K
is covered by a finite number of such balls, and we can choose among them balls
\left(B(x,r_{x})\right)_{x\in X}
disjointed such as
K\subset\cup_{x\in X}B(x,3r_{x})
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(K)\leq\lambda_{n}\left(\cup_{x\in X}B(x,3r_{x})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\lambda_{n}\left(B(x,3r_{x})\right)=3^{n}\sum_{x\in X}\lambda_{n}\left(B(x,r_{x})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3^{n}}{d}\sum_{x\in X}\int_{B(x,r_{x})}|f(t)|d\lambda_{n}(t)\leq\frac{3^{n}\|f\|_{1}}{d}

because the balls are disjointed.
∎

Lebesgue differentiation theorem

Theorem 3.5

For any function integrable in the Lebesgue sense
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{\lambda(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|f(t)-f(x)|\;d\lambda(t)=0

where
B(x,r)
denotes the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
ball centered at
x
and of radius
r
and
\lambda
denotes the Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r\geq 0
, we set

\displaystyle T_{r}(f)(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|f(t)-f(x)|\;d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle\text{and}\;\;\;T(f)(x)=\limsup_{r\to 0}T_{r}(f)(x)

We take here the upper limit because the limit when
r
tends to
0
is not necessarily defined. The idea is to show that
T(f)=0
almost everywhere by showing that for all
c>0
,
\{Tf>0\}
is negligible.

Let
k>0
be an integer. We know from the density of continuous functions in the spaces
L^{p}(\Omega)
that there exists a continuous function
g
such that
\|f-g\|_{1}<\frac{1}{k}
. If we put
h=f-g
, then we have
T_{r}(h)(x)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}|h(t)|\;d\lambda(t)+|h(x)|
and therefore
T_{r}(h)(x)\leq Mh(x)+|h(x)|
where
Mh
is the maximum Hardy-Littlewood function associated with
h
. The continuity of
g
ensures that
T(g)=0
, from
f=g+h
we derive
T_{r}(f)\leq T_{r}(g)+T_{r}(h)
and thus passing to the upper limit
T(f)\leq T(g)+T(h)=T(h)
which proves that
T(f)\leq Mh+|h|
.

Then for all
c>0
we have the following inclusion
\{Tf>2c\}\subset\{Mh>c\}\cup\{|h|>c\}
. According to the Hardy-Littlewood inequality
\lambda\left(\{Mh>c\}\right)\leq\frac{3^{n}\|h\|_{1}}{c}\leq\frac{3^{n}}{ck}
and on the other hand
\lambda\left(\{|h|>c\}\right)\leq\frac{|h|_{1}}{c}\leq\frac{1}{ck}
. The set
\{Mh>c\}\cup\{|h|>c\}
which is measurable has therefore a smaller measure than
\frac{3^{n}+1}{ck}
. This means that
\{Tf>2c\}
is included in a set of measure less than
\frac{3^{n}+1}{ck}
for all
k\geq 0
, by taking the intersection on
k\geq 0
of all these sets we show that
\{Tf>2c\}
is included in a set of zero measure,
\{Tf\geq 2c\}
is negligible. ∎

5
Topological derivative using the Gangl and Sturm method

With the min-max approach developed in the previous sections, it is not at all easy to compute the limit of the function
R(s)
when goes to zero. We now turn to the ideas recently developed by Gangl and Sturm
[
10
]
, which allow to avoid the explicit computation of the limit
R(U_{0},P_{0})
. But in this method, the main challenges are to fulfill to main hypotheses. One of them need to be able to claim the existence of a corrector term. To this end, we propose an alternative writing of the system (
2.1
) where we add an extra term as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot F(U)-\nabla\cdot(Q(\alpha)\nabla U)=S(U)\qquad\qquad\text{on}\quad\Omega\times[0,T]

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle F(U)=\begin{pmatrix}hu&hv\\
hu^{2}+\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}&huv\\
huv&hv^{2}+\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S(U)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-gh\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\\
-gh\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}\end{pmatrix}.

and the same initial and boundary conditions (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) apply here. In this new system, we have also introduced an additional term
\nabla\cdot(Q(\alpha)\nabla U)
, the Laplacian artificial viscosity. Here, the
Q(\alpha)
coefficient is a parameter that controls the amount of viscosity. For further details on viscosity coefficients, please refer to
[
17
]
.

Q(\alpha)
is a diagonal matrix of size
3\times 3
defined by

\displaystyle Q(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\alpha_{1}&0\\
0&0&\alpha_{2}\end{pmatrix}

with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(Q(\alpha)\nabla U)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\alpha_{1}\nabla^{2}(hu)\\
\alpha_{2}\nabla^{2}(hv)\end{pmatrix}.

Here we have chosen to take zero as the first element of the diagonal of the matrix
Q(\alpha)
. This choice is justified by the fact that, in general, no viscosity is placed on the continuity equation in the viscous shallow water system, making it a non-fully parabolic system. One of the simple reasons why it may be necessary to place a viscosity on the continuity equation is to avoid the discontinuities that can occur in the original formulation of the hyperbolic shallow water equation
[
21
]
.

In what follows, our aim is to compute the topological derivative of the objective function
J(\Omega)
, defined by

\displaystyle J(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}j^{\Omega}(x,U,\nabla U)\;dxdt.

(5.2)

where effectively
U
is a solution of the viscous shallow water equation (
5.1
). For this purpose, we consider a computational domain
D
which is subdivided into two open disjoint subdomains:
\Omega
and its complementary in
D
, i.e.
D=\Omega^{int}\cup\Omega^{ext}
where
\Omega^{int}=\Omega
and
\Omega^{ext}=D\backslash\overline{\Omega}
. We can then write

\displaystyle J(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}j^{\Omega}(x,U,\nabla U)\;dxdt

(5.3)

with
j^{\Omega}(x,U,\nabla U)
is equal to zero outside of
\Omega
or simply

\displaystyle j^{\Omega}(x,U,\nabla U)=\chi_{\Omega}(x)j^{int}(x,U,\nabla U)+\chi_{D\backslash\Omega}(x)j^{ext}(x,U,\nabla U),\quad\text{with}\;\;j^{ext}(x,U,\nabla U)=0

(5.4)

and

\displaystyle j^{int}(x,U,\nabla U)=|\nabla U|^{2}+|U-U_{d}|^{2}

where
U\in H^{1}(D\times[0,T])^{3}
is a solution to the variational equation

\displaystyle\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\partial_{t}(U)\cdot V\;dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}(\nabla\cdot(F(U)))\cdot V\;dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}Q(\alpha)\nabla U:\nabla V\;dxdt-

\displaystyle\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}S(U)\cdot V\;dxdt=0,\;\;\forall\;V\in H^{1}_{0}(D\times[0,T])^{3}.

This last equation can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\left(\partial_{t}(U)+\nabla\cdot(F(U))-S(U)\right)\cdot V\;dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}Q(\alpha)\nabla U:\nabla V\;dxdt=0.

(5.5)

In this case the PDE-constrained topology optimization problem is written:

\displaystyle\min_{\Omega\in\mathcal{O}_{ad}}\quad J(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}j^{\Omega}(x,U,\nabla U)\;dxdt

\displaystyle\quad s.t.\quad U\;\text{solves}\;(\ref{swe1})

where
j^{\Omega}
is defined in (
5.4
) and
\mathcal{O}_{ad}
is the set of admissible subsets of
D
. We consider the following operators

\displaystyle A_{1}^{int},A_{1}^{ext}:D\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle A_{2}^{int},A_{2}^{ext}:D\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}

and we define the two piecewise operators
A_{1}^{\Omega}:D\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
