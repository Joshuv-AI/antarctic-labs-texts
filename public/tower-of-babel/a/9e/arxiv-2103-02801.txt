Sober topological spaces valued in a quantale
Dexue Zhang, Gao Zhang

Abstract
The notion of sobriety is extended to the realm of topological spaces valued in a commutative and unital quantale, via an adjunction between a category of quantale modules and the category of quantale-valued topological spaces. Relations between such sober spaces and quantale-valued domains based on flat ideals are investigated. Keywords Fuzzy topology; 𝖰 {\sf Q} -order; 𝖰 {\sf Q} -module; Flat ideal; Sober 𝖰 {\sf Q} -topological space MSC(2020) 54A40 54B30 18B30

Introduction

Sobriety is an interesting topological property for non-Hausdorff spaces and plays an important role in domain theory
[
3
,
4
]
. A main feature of such a space is that the space can be recovered from its open set lattice. Sobriety is often described via the adjunction

\mathcal{O}\dashv\pt\colon{\sf Frm}^{\rm op}\longrightarrow{\sf Top}

between the opposite of the category of frames and the category of topological spaces
[
10
]
. A topological space
X
is sober if the unit
\eta_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow\pt\mathcal{O}(X)
is a bijection (hence a homeomorphism).

Different extensions of the notion of sobriety to the fuzzy context have been proposed since 1990. Here we mention a few of the works on this topic: Rodabaugh
[
26
]
, Zhang and Liu
[
42
]
, Kotzé
[
14
]
, Srivastava and Khastgir
[
30
]
, Pultr and Rodabaugh
[
24
,
25
]
, Gutiérrez García, Höhle and de Prada Vicente
[
5
]
, Yao
[
37
,
38
]
, Jäger and Yao
[
9
]
, Singh and Srivastava
[
29
]
, and etc. In these works, the table of truth-values is assumed to be a complete Heyting algebra (i.e., a frame), sometimes even a completely distributive lattice. For such a table of truth-values, the logic connective
conjunction
, modeled by the meet operation of the lattice, is idempotent. From the viewpoint of fuzzy logic, this is a serious drawback. For example, BL-algebras
[
6
]
and left-continuous t-norms
[
13
]
are seldom idempotent.

The aim of this paper is to extend the notion of sobriety to the realm of topological spaces valued in a commutative and unital quantale. Complete Heyting algebras, BL-algebras, and the interval
[0,1]
equipped with a left-continuous t-norm are typical examples of such quantales.

This paper is a companion of
[
41
]
, which focuses on sobriety of quantale-valued
cotopological
spaces.
In the classical context, a topological space can be described by open sets as well as by closed sets, and we can switch between open sets and closed sets by taking complements. Thus, it makes no difference whether we choose to work with closed sets or with open sets in the classical context. But, in the fuzzy context, the table of truth-values usually does not satisfy the law of double negation, there is no natural way to switch between open sets and closed sets, so, as observed in
[
41
]
, in fuzzy topology it is often necessary to consider the topology version (in term of open sets) and the cotopology version (in term of closed sets) of the same concept. In other words, like a coin, fuzzy topology was born with two sides. And, we would like to stress that, due to the lack of symmetry in the table of truth-values, different ideas and techniques are needed in the topology and the cotopology versions of the same theory.

In this paper, like that in
[
41
]
, we emphasize the connection between quantale-valued orders and quantale-valued topologies. For each commutative and unital quantale
{\sf Q}
, a category of
{\sf Q}
-modules (or equivalently, complete
{\sf Q}
-lattices) is specified, sober
{\sf Q}
-topological spaces are then described via an adjunction between this category and that of
{\sf Q}
-topological spaces. It is shown that for a commutative and integral quantale, the Scott
{\sf Q}
-topology of every
\mathcal{F}
-domain (see Definition
4.5
) is sober. In the final section, some examples are presented in the case that the quantale is the interval
[0,1]
together with a continuous t-norm.

2
Quantales and quantale-valued orders

In this section we recall some basic ideas about quantale-valued orders and fix some notations.

A
frame

[
10
]
is a complete lattice
L
such that the binary meet operation
\wedge
distributes over arbitrary joins; that is,
x\wedge\bigvee S=\bigvee\{x\wedge s\mid s\in S\}
for each
x\in L
and each
S\subseteq L
. A
frame map

f\colon L_{1}\longrightarrow L_{2}
between frames is a map that preserves arbitrary joins and finite meets, including the empty ones. Frames and frame maps constitute a category

\sf Frm.

Following Rodabaugh
[
26
, page 303]
, we say that a map
f\colon L\longrightarrow M
between complete lattices (not necessarily frames) is
frame-like
if
f
preserves arbitrary joins and finite meets.

A
quantale

[
27
]

{\sf Q}=({\sf Q},\with)

is a semigroup such that the underlying set
{\sf Q}
is a complete lattice and that the multiplication
\with
distributes over arbitrary joins on both sides. As usual, we write
0
and
1
for the bottom and the top element of
{\sf Q}
, respectively. A quantale
{\sf Q}
is

•

commutative if the multiplication
\with
is commutative;

•

unital if it has a unit element
k
;

•

integral if it is unital and the unit
k
is the top element of
{\sf Q}
.

It is clear that a complete lattice
L
is a frame if, and only if,
(L,\wedge)
is a quantale. In this paper, when we say that a quantale
{\sf Q}=({\sf Q},\with)
is a frame, we mean the semigroup operation
\with
of
{\sf Q}
is the meet operation
\wedge
. It is possible that the underlying lattice of a quantale is a frame, but the quantale itself is not a frame.

Standing Assumption
.

In this paper, by a quantale we always mean a commutative and unital one, with unit denoted by
k
, unless otherwise specified.

Given a quantale
{\sf Q}
, the multiplication
\&
determines a binary operator
\rightarrow
, known as the
implication operator
of
\&
, via the adjoint property:

p\with q\leq r\iff q\leq p\rightarrow r.

A
{\sf Q}
-valued order
, a
{\sf Q}
-order for short, is a map
\alpha\colon X\times X\longrightarrow{\sf Q}
such that for all
x,y,z\in X
,

\alpha(x,x)\geq k\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha(y,z)\with\alpha(x,y)\leq\alpha(x,z).

The pair
(X,\alpha)
is call a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
, or a
{\sf Q}
-category
in the language of enriched categories
[
20
]
. It is customary to write
X
for the pair
(X,\alpha)
and write
X(x,y)
for
\alpha(x,y)
.

A map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
between
{\sf Q}
-ordered sets is said to
preserve
{\sf Q}
-order
if
X(x,y)\leq Y(f(x),f(y))
for all
x,y\in X
.

{\sf Q}
-ordered sets and
{\sf Q}
-order-preserving maps constitute a category

{\sf Q}\text{-}\sf Ord.

If
\alpha
is a
{\sf Q}
-order on
X
, it follows from the commutativity of
\with
that
\alpha^{\rm op}(x,y)\coloneqq\alpha(y,x)
is also a
{\sf Q}
-order on
X
, called the opposite of
\alpha
.

The following two examples belong to the folklore in the theory of
{\sf Q}
-orders, it is hard to specify where they appeared for the first time.

Example 2.1
.

For all
x,y\in{\sf Q}
, let

\alpha_{L}(x,y)=x\rightarrow y.

Then
\alpha_{L}
is a
{\sf Q}
-order, called the
canonical
{\sf Q}
-order
, on
{\sf Q}
. The opposite of
\alpha_{L}
is denoted by
\alpha_{R}
; that is,

\alpha_{R}(x,y)=y\rightarrow x.

Example 2.2
.

For each set
X
, the map

{\rm sub}_{X}\colon{\sf Q}^{X}\times{\sf Q}^{X}\longrightarrow{\sf Q},\quad{\rm sub}_{X}(\phi,\psi)=\bigwedge_{x\in X}\phi(x)\rightarrow\psi(x)

is a
{\sf Q}
-order on the set
{\sf Q}^{X}
, known as the
inclusion
{\sf Q}
-order
. If
X
is a singleton set, then
(X,{\rm sub}_{X})
degenerates to the
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
({\sf Q},\alpha_{L})
.

The
underlying order
of a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
refers to the order
\leq
given by

x\leq y\quad\quad{\rm if}~k\leq X(x,y).

For convenience, for each
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
, we shall write
X_{0}
for
X
with the underlying order.

Two elements
x,y
of a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
are
isomorphic
if
k\leq X(x,y)\wedge X(y,x).
A
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
is
separated
if isomorphic elements are identical; that is,

k\leq X(x,y)\wedge X(y,x)\implies x=y.

Let
X,Y
be
{\sf Q}
-ordered sets; let
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
and
g\colon Y\longrightarrow X
be maps. We say that
f
is
left adjoint
to
g
(and/or,
g
is
right adjoint
to
f
) and write
f\dashv g
, if

Y(f(x),y)=X(x,g(y))

for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.
This is a special case of enriched adjunctions in category theory
[
1
,
20
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

(
[
32
, page 295]
)
Let
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
and
g\colon Y\longrightarrow X
be a pair of maps between
{\sf Q}
-ordered sets. Then
f
is left adjoint to
g
if, and only if, the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

Both
f
and
g
preserve
{\sf Q}
-order.

(ii)

The map
f\colon X_{0}\longrightarrow Y_{0}
is left adjoint to
g\colon Y_{0}\longrightarrow X_{0}
; that is, for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
,
f(x)\leq y\iff x\leq g(y)
.

Let
X
be a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set. A
weight
of
X
is a map
\phi\colon X\longrightarrow{\sf Q}
such that for all
x,y\in X
,

\phi(y)\with X(x,y)\leq\phi(x).

Remark 2.4
.

There exist different terminologies for a weight of a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
. Firstly, since the above inequality can be read as “that
y
belongs to
\phi
and
x
is smaller than or equal to
y
implies
y
belongs to
\phi
”, a weight is also called a
lower fuzzy set
of
X
.
Secondly, since a weight of
X
is just a
{\sf Q}
-order-preserving map (or, a
{\sf Q}
-functor)
\phi\colon X^{\rm op}\longrightarrow({\sf Q},\alpha_{L})
, it is also called a
{\sf Q}
-presheaf of
X
(viewed as an enriched category). The terminology
weight
adopted here comes from category theory, see e.g.
[
12
]
.

The weights of a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
constitute a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
\mathcal{P}X
with

\mathcal{P}X(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\coloneqq{\rm sub}_{X}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}).

For each
x\in X
,
X(-,x)
is a weight of
X
and we have the following:

Lemma 2.5

(Yoneda lemma)
.

Let
X
be a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set and
\phi
be a weight of
X
, then

\mathcal{P}X(X(-,x),\phi)=\phi(x).

The Yoneda lemma entails that the map

{\sf y}_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}X,\quad x\mapsto X(-,x)

is an embedding if
X
is separated. By abuse of language, we call it the
Yoneda embedding
no matter
X
is separated or not.

Each
{\sf Q}
-order-preserving map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
gives rise to a natural adjunction between
\mathcal{P}X
and
\mathcal{P}Y
. Precisely, the map

f^{\rightarrow}\colon\mathcal{P}X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}Y,\quad f^{\rightarrow}(\phi)(y)=\bigvee_{x\in X}\phi(x)\with Y(y,f(x))

is left adjoint to

f^{\leftarrow}\colon\mathcal{P}Y\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}X,\quad f^{\leftarrow}(\psi)(x)=\psi(f(x)).

Let
X
be a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set and let
\phi
be a weight of
X
. We say that an element
a
of
X
is a
supremum
of
\phi
and write
a=\sup\phi
, if

X(a,y)=\mathcal{P}X(\phi,X(-,y))

for all
y\in X
. In the language of category theory,
\sup\phi
is the colimit of the identity functor
{\rm id}\colon X\longrightarrow X
weighted by
\phi
(see e.g.
[
1
,
31
]
). Suprema of a weight, if exist, are unique up to isomorphism.

We say that a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
is
cocomplete
if every weight of
X
has a supremum. It is clear that
X
is cocomplete if, and only if, the Yoneda embedding
{\sf y}_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}X
has a left adjoint (see e.g.
[
31
]
).

Example 2.6
.

The
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
({\sf Q},\alpha_{L})
is cocomplete. For each weight
\phi
of
({\sf Q},\alpha_{L})
,

\sup\phi=\bigvee_{x\in{\sf Q}}\phi(x)\with x=\phi(k).

A
coweight
of a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
is defined to be weight of the opposite
X^{\rm op}
of
X
. Explicitly, a coweight of
X
is a
{\sf Q}
-order-preserving map
\psi\colon X\longrightarrow({\sf Q},\alpha_{L})
. Coweights are also known as
upper fuzzy sets
and
covariant
{\sf Q}
-presheaves
.
The coweights of
X
constitute a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}X
with

\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}X(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\coloneqq{\rm sub}_{X}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1}).

Let
\psi
be a coweight and let
a
be an element of
X
. We say that
a
is an
infimum
of
\psi
if

X(y,a)=\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}X(X(y,-),\psi)

for all
y\in X
.

A
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
is
complete
if every coweight of
X
has an infimum.
It is known
[
31
]
that

(i)

a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
is cocomplete if, and only if,
X
is complete; and

(ii)

a
{\sf Q}
-order-preserving map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
between cocomplete
{\sf Q}
-ordered sets is a left adjoint if, and only if,
f

preserves suprema
in the sense that
f(\sup\phi)=\sup f^{\rightarrow}(\phi)
for each weight
\phi
of
X
.

A
{\sf Q}
-ordered set is a
complete
{\sf Q}
-lattice
if
X
is separated and complete (or equivalently, cocomplete). Complete
{\sf Q}
-lattices and left adjoints constitute a category

{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Sup}.

For each
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
,
\mathcal{P}X
is cocomplete, hence a complete
{\sf Q}
-lattice
[
31
]
. Actually,

{\sf y}_{X}^{\leftarrow}\colon\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}X

is a left adjoint of
{\sf y}_{\mathcal{P}X}\colon\mathcal{P}X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}X
.
This means that the supremum of a weight
\Phi
of
\mathcal{P}X
is given by

\sup\Phi={\sf y}_{X}^{\leftarrow}(\Phi)=\bigvee_{\phi\in\mathcal{P}X}\Phi(\phi)\with\phi.

The correspondence

f\colon X\longrightarrow Y\quad\mapsto\quad f^{\rightarrow}\colon\mathcal{P}X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}Y

defines a functor
\mathcal{P}\colon{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Ord}\longrightarrow{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Sup}
that is left adjoint to the forgetful functor
{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Sup}\longrightarrow{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Ord}

[
31
]
. We write

\mathbb{P}=(\mathcal{P},{\sf m},{\sf y})

for the monad on
{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Ord}
arising from this adjunction. Explicitly,

•

for each
{\sf Q}
-order-preserving map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
,
\mathcal{P}f=f^{\rightarrow}\colon\mathcal{P}X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}Y
;

•

the unit is the Yoneda embedding
{\sf y}_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}X
;

•

the multiplication
{\sf m}_{X}={\sup}_{\mathcal{P}X}={\sf y}_{X}^{\leftarrow}\colon\mathcal{P}\mathcal{P}X\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}X
.

The category of
\mathbb{P}
-algebras and
\mathbb{P}
-homomorphisms is precisely that of
{\sf Q}
-complete lattices and left adjoints (see e.g.
[
18
,
31
]
); that is,

\mathbb{P}\text{-}{\sf Alg}={\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Sup}.

As we see below, there is a very useful and convenient characterization of
{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Sup}
, it is equivalent to the category of
{\sf Q}
-modules. Recall that the category
Sup
of complete lattices and join-preserving maps is symmetric and monoidal closed, see
[
11
, Chapter I]
, thus, as in any such categories
[
22
]
, one can talk about monoids and monoid actions in
Sup
. It is readily seen that a commutative and unital quantale
{\sf Q}
is exactly a commutative monoid in
Sup
. So, there are
{\sf Q}
-actions and
{\sf Q}
-modules.

Definition 2.7
.

(
[
11
]
) A
{\sf Q}
-module (precisely, a left
{\sf Q}
-module) is a pair
(X,\otimes)
, where
X
is a complete lattice and
\otimes\colon{\sf Q}\times X\longrightarrow X
is a map, called a (left)
{\sf Q}
-action on
X
, subject to the following conditions: for all
x\in X
and
r,s,\in{\sf Q}
,

(i)

k\otimes x=x
, where
k
is the unit of
{\sf Q}
;

(ii)

s\otimes(r\otimes x)=(s\with r)\otimes x
;

(iii)

r\otimes-\colon X\longrightarrow X
preserve joins; and

(iv)

-\otimes x\colon{\sf Q}\longrightarrow X
preserve joins.

The conditions (iii) and (iv) together amount to requiring that
\otimes:{\sf Q}\times X\longrightarrow X
is a bimorphism in
Sup
(see e.g.
[
11
, page 5]
). Thus, by universal property of tensor products in
Sup
, one sees that the above definition of
{\sf Q}
-modules, which is a bit more reader-friendly, is equivalent to that in
[
11
]
.

A homomorphism
f\colon(X,\otimes)\longrightarrow(Y,\otimes)
between
{\sf Q}
-modules is a join-preserving map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
that preserves the action, i.e.,
r\otimes f(x)=f(r\otimes x)
for all
r\in{\sf Q}
and
x\in X
.

{\sf Q}
-modules and homomorphisms constitute a category

{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Mod}.

If
{\sf Q}
is the Boolean algebra
(\{0,1\},\wedge)
, then
{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Mod}
is equivalent to the category
{\sf Sup}
of complete lattices and join-preserving maps
[
11
, page 8, Proposition 1]
.

The quantale
({\sf Q},\with)
itself is a
{\sf Q}
-module, with
\with
being the action. We often view
{\sf Q}
as a
{\sf Q}
-module in this way.

Let
X
be a
{\sf Q}
-ordered set. For each
x\in X
and
r\in{\sf Q}
, the
tensor of
r
and
x
(see e.g.
[
32
, page 288]
), denoted by
r\otimes x
, is a point of
X
such that for all
y\in X
,

X(r\otimes x,y)=r\rightarrow X(x,y).

A
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
X
is
tensored
if the tensor
r\otimes x
exists for all
x\in X
and
r\in{\sf Q}
. Some facts about tensors are listed below, which can be found in
[
32
]
and
[
17
]
.

•

Every complete
{\sf Q}
-lattice is tensored. Actually,
r\otimes x
is the supremum of the weight
r\with X(-,x)
.

•

Every left adjoint
f
preserves tensors; that is,
r\otimes f(x)=f(r\otimes x)
.

•

A map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
between complete
{\sf Q}
-lattices preserves
{\sf Q}
-order if, and only if,
f\colon X_{0}\longrightarrow Y_{0}
preserves order and
r\otimes f(x)=f(r\otimes x)
for all
x\in X
and
r\in{\sf Q}
.

Given a complete
{\sf Q}
-lattice
X
, taking tensor
(r,x)\mapsto r\otimes x
makes
X
into a
{\sf Q}
-module. Conversely, given a
{\sf Q}
-module
(X,\otimes)
, if we let

\alpha(x,y)=\bigvee\{r\in{\sf Q}\mid r\otimes x\leq y\},

then
(X,\alpha)
is a complete
{\sf Q}
-lattice. These two processes are inverse to each other. So we have the following characterization of
{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Sup}
, which is an instance of
[
32
, Corollary 4.13]
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The category
{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Mod}
is equivalent to the category
{\sf Q}\text{-}{\sf Sup}
.

Example 2.9
.

For each set
X
, the assignment
(r,\lambda)\mapsto r\with\lambda
defines a
{\sf Q}
-action on
{\sf Q}^{X}
, so,
{\sf Q}^{X}
becomes a
{\sf Q}
-module. We shall always view
{\sf Q}^{X}
as a
{\sf Q}
-module in this way. It is clear that the corresponding
{\sf Q}
-order of the
{\sf Q}
-module
{\sf Q}^{X}
is just the inclusion
{\sf Q}
-order
{\rm sub}_{X}
in Example

.

For more information on
{\sf Q}
-modules and their relations to
{\sf Q}
-orders the reader is referred to the monograph
[

]
.

3
Sober
{\sf Q}
-topological spaces

A
{\sf Q}
-topology
on a set
X
is subset
\tau
of
{\sf Q}^{X}
subject to the following conditions:

(O1)

The constant map
1_{X}\colon X\longrightarrow{\sf Q}
with value
1
belongs to
\tau
;

(O2)

\lambda\wedge\mu\in\tau
for all
\lambda,\mu\in\tau
;

(O3)

\bigvee_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}\in\tau
for each subset
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of
\tau
;

(O4)

\lambda\with r\in\tau
for all
r\in{\sf Q}
and
\lambda\in\tau
, where
(\lambda\with r)(x)=\lambda(x)\with r
for all
x\in X
.

The pair
(X,\tau)
is called a
{\sf Q}
-topological space; elements of
\tau
are called open sets.

It is customary to write
X
for a
{\sf Q}
-topological space and write
\mathcal{O}(X)
for the set of open sets of
X
.
The interior operator of a
{\sf Q}
-topological space
X
refers to the map

(-)^{\circ}\colon{\sf Q}^{X}\longrightarrow{\sf Q}^{X},\quad\lambda^{\circ}=\bigvee\{\mu\in\mathcal{O}(X)\mid\mu\leq\lambda\}.

The interior operator satisfies the following conditions: for all
\lambda,\mu\in{\sf Q}^{X}
, (int1)
1_{X}^{\circ}=1_{X}
;
(int2)
\lambda^{\circ}\leq\lambda
;
(int3)
(\lambda\wedge\mu)^{\circ}=\lambda^{\circ}\wedge\mu^{\circ}
;
(int4)
{\rm sub}_{X}(\lambda,\mu)\leq{\rm sub}_{X}(\lambda^{\circ},\mu^{\circ})
; and
(int5)
(\lambda^{\circ})^{\circ}=\lambda^{\circ}
.
Actually,
{\sf Q}
-topologies on
X
correspond bijectively to operators
{\sf Q}^{X}\longrightarrow{\sf Q}^{X}
satisfying (int1)-(int5). This fact also justifies the definition of
{\sf Q}
-topology.

A map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y
between
{\sf Q}
-topological spaces is continuous if
\lambda\circ f
is an open set of
X
for each open set
\lambda
of

Definition 3.1
.

[

Remark 3.2

(When

(When

5
Some examples in the case that
{\sf Q}=([0,1],\with)

The aim of this section is to present some examples of
\mathcal{F}
-domains and sober
{\sf Q}
-topological spaces when
{\sf Q}
is the interval
[0,1]
endowed with a continuous t-norm. In particular, a necessary and sufficient condition is obtained for
[0,1]
together with the canonical
{\sf Q}
-order to be an
\mathcal{F}
-domain.

A
left-continuous t-norm
on
[0,1]

[
13
]
is a map
\with\colon[0,1]^{2}\longrightarrow[0,1]
that makes
([0,1],\with)
into a commutative and integral quantale.
A left-continuous t-norm that is continuous with respect to the usual topology is called a
continuous t-norm

[
13
]
. Continuous t-norms play a decisive role in the BL-logic of Hájek
[
6
]
.

Basic continuous t-norms and their implication operators are listed below:

(1)

The Gödel t-norm:

x\with y=\min\{x,y\};\quad x\rightarrow y=\begin{cases}1,&x\leq y,\\
y,&x>y.\end{cases}

The implication operator
\rightarrow
of the Gödel t-norm is continuous except at
(x,x)
,
x<1
.

(2)

The product t-norm:

x\with_{P}y=xy;\quad x\rightarrow y=\begin{cases}1,&x\leq y,\\
y/x,&x>y.\end{cases}

The implication operator
\rightarrow

of the product t-norm is continuous except at
(0,0)
.

(3)

The Łukasiewicz t-norm:

x\with_{\text{\L}}y=\max\{0,x+y-1\};\quad x\rightarrow y=\min\{1-x+y,1\}.

The implication operator
\rightarrow

of the Łukasiewicz t-norm
is continuous on
[0,1]^{2}
.

Let
\with
be a continuous t-norm. An element
p\in[0,1]
is
idempotent
if
p\with p=p
.

Proposition 5.1
.

(
[
13
, Proposition 2.3]
)

Let
\&
be a continuous t-norm on
[0,1]
and
p
be an idempotent element of
\&
. Then
x\with y=x\wedge y
whenever
x\leq p\leq y
.

It follows immediately that
y\rightarrow x=x
whenever
x<p\leq y
for some idempotent
p
. Another consequence of Proposition
5.1
is that for any idempotent elements
p,q
with
p<q
, the restriction of
\with
to
[p,q]
, which is also denoted by
\with
, makes
[p,q]
into a commutative quantale with
q
being the unit element.

A continuous t-norm on
[0,1]
is said to be
Archimedean
if it has no idempotent element other than
0
and
1
. It is well-known that if
\with
is a continuous Archimedean t-norm, then the quantale
([0,1],\with)
is either isomorphic to
([0,1],\with_{\text{\L}})
or to
([0,1],\with_{P})
, see e.g.
[
13
,
23
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

(
[
13
,
23
]
)

Let
\with
be a continuous t-norm. Then for each non-idempotent element
c
of
([0,1],\with)
, there exist idempotent elements

and

such that
c^{-}<c<c^{+}
and the quantale
([c^{-},c^{+}],\with)
is either isomorphic to
([0,1],\with_{\L})
or to
([0,1],\with_{P})
.

Proposition 5.3

Let
\with
be a continuous t-norm and let

(1)

The
{\sf Q}
-ordered set
([0,1],\alpha_{L})

\mathcal{F}
-domain.

(2)

For each non-idempotent element

In order to prove the conclusion, we present four lemmas first.

Lemma 5.4

Let
\with

\phi\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow[0,1]

A direct consequence of Corollary 3.14 and Theorem 3.18 in
[
19
]
. ∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\with
be a continuous t-norm and let
{\sf Q}=([0,1],\with)
. Let
\phi
be a flat ideal of
([0,1],\alpha_{L})

Proof.

Consider the following coweights of

\psi_{1}\equiv\phi(c)

.

Since

and

it follows that

\displaystyle\phi(c)

\displaystyle=\phi\pitchfork(\psi_{1}\wedge\psi_{2})

\displaystyle=\Big(\bigvee_{x\in[0,c)}\phi(x)\with(c\rightarrow x)\Big)\vee\Big(\bigvee_{x\in[c,1]}\phi(x)\with\phi(c)\Big)

Let
\with

Proof.

This is the content of
[

\psi

it follows that

\psi(x)\leq d(x)\pitchfork\psi

\displaystyle d(x)\pitchfork\psi

\displaystyle=\psi(0)\vee\bigvee_{t>0}(x^{+}\wedge(t\rightarrow x))\with\psi(t)

\displaystyle=\psi(0)\vee\bigvee_{t>0}x^{+}\with(t\rightarrow x)\with\psi(t)

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\with

{\sf Q}

Write

(1)\Rightarrow(2)

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
