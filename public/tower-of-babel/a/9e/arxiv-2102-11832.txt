Stashing And Parallelization Pentagons
Vasco Brattka

Abstract
. Parallelization is an algebraic operation that lifts problems to sequences in a natural way. Given a sequence as an instance of the parallelized problem, another sequence is a solution of this problem if every component is instance-wise a solution of the original problem. In the Weihrauch lattice parallelization is a closure operator that corresponds to the bang operator in linear logic. Here we introduce a dual operation that we call stashing and that also lifts problems to sequences, but such that only some component has to be an instance-wise solution. In this case the solution is stashed away in the sequence. This operation, if properly defined, induces an interior operator in the Weihrauch lattice, which corresponds to the question mark operator known from linear logic. It can also be seen as a countable version of the sum operation. We also study the action of the monoid induced by stashing and parallelization on the Weihrauch lattice, and we prove that it leads to at most five distinct degrees, which (in the maximal case) are always organized in pentagons. We also introduce another closely related interior operator in the Weihrauch lattice that replaces solutions of problems by upper Turing cones that are strong enough to compute solutions. It turns out that on parallelizable degrees this interior operator corresponds to stashing. This implies that, somewhat surprisingly, all problems which are simultaneously parallelizable and stashable have computability-theoretic characterizations. Finally, we apply all these results in order to study the recently introduced discontinuity problem, which appears as the bottom of a number of natural stashing-parallelization pentagons. The discontinuity problem is not only the stashing of several variants of the lesser limited principle of omniscience, but it also parallelizes to the non-computability problem. This supports the slogan that “non-computability is the parallelization of discontinuity”. We also study the non-majorization problem as an asymmetric version of the discontinuity problem and we show that it parallelizes to the hyperimmunity problem. Finally we identify a phase transition related to the limit avoidance problem that marks a point where pentagons are taking off from the bottom of the Weihrauch lattice.

The Weihrauch lattice has been used as a computability theoretic framework to analyze the uniform computational

In Section

{defi}

[Upper Turing cone version]

We define the
upper Turing cone version

f^{\mathcal{D}}:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{D}}
by

{\mathrm{dom}}(f^{\mathcal{D}}):={\mathrm{dom}}(f)
and

f^{\mathcal{D}}(x):=\{\mathrm{deg}_{\mathrm{T}}(q)\in{\mathcal{D}}:(\exists y\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}q)\;y\in f(x)\}.

Here

denotes the set of
Turing degrees

\mathrm{deg}_{\mathrm{T}}(p)=\{q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:q\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{T}}}p\}
, represented by

\delta_{\mathcal{D}}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathcal{D}},p\mapsto\mathrm{deg}_{\mathrm{T}}(p)
.
If
(Y,\delta_{Y})
is a represented space, then we define

y\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}q:\iff(\exists p\in\delta_{Y}^{-1}\{y\})\;p\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}q\iff(\exists\mbox{ computable }F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y)\,F(q)=y.

Hence,

means that
y
has a name that can be computed from

and if

then this is the usual version of Turing reducibility.
This version of Turing reducibility has also been called
representation reducibility

[

]
.

Besides the fact that

is an interior
operator in the Weihrauch lattice, our main result in this direction shows that on parallelizable problems

Proposition 8

.

\Sigma\widehat{f}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\widehat{f}\;^{\mathcal{D}}
for all problems

.

As a corollary of this result we obtain the following surprising consequence (see Corollary

Corollary 9

(Closure under upper Turing cones)
.

If

This means that all such problems are essentially of computability theoretic nature.
This includes all problems that occur on the right-hand side of stashing-parallelization pentagons.
The remarkable situation here is that parallelization and stashing are in some sense purely

2.
Stashing as Interior Operator

The main purpose of this section is to show that stashing
f\mapsto\Sigma f
is an interior
operator for various versions of the Weihrauch lattice (see Proposition
11
).
For this result it is essential that the completion
\overline{Y}
of
Y
is used on the output side
of
\Sigma f:\subseteq X^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
. As a technical preparation we need the following lemma.
In
[
BG21
, Corollary 2.7]
we proved that there is a computable retraction
r:\overline{\overline{Y}}\to\overline{Y}
,
which is a computable map such that
r|_{\overline{Y}}={\mathrm{id}}_{\overline{Y}}
.
Here we will have to use a similar property of the product space
\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma 10

(Retractions for product spaces)
.

For every represented space
Y
there is a computable retraction
r:\overline{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
i.e., a computable
r
with
r|_{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathrm{id}}_{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Proof 2.1
.

The space
Z:=\overline{Y}
is represented by a total representation
\delta_{Z}=\delta_{\overline{Y}}
and
by
[
BG21
, Corollary 2.7]
there is a computable retraction
R:\overline{Z}\to Z
.
We assume that
\overline{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}
is represented by the completion
\delta_{\overline{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}}
of the usual product representation
\delta_{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
We claim that there is a computable map
T:\overline{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}\to\overline{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
T|_{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathrm{id}}_{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
We assume that
\overline{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}=Z^{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\bot_{\mathbb{N}}\}
and
\overline{Z}=Z\cup\{\bot\}
.
The names of
\bot_{\mathbb{N}}
with respect to
\delta_{\overline{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}}
are exactly those names that contain only finitely many digits different from
0

(since
\delta_{Z}
and hence
\delta_{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}
are total). Now
T
can be realized in a computable way by interpreting the non-zero content
of a given name
p
as a name
\langle p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},...\rangle
of a point in
\overline{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
with respect to
\delta_{\overline{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
As long as no non-zero content is available in
p
, the names
p_{i}
are filled up by zeros. Altogether this shows that
T
is computable and it acts
as the identity with the exception that
T(\bot_{\mathbb{N}})=(\bot,\bot,\bot,...)
. Now
r:\overline{Z^{\mathbb{N}}}\to Z^{\mathbb{N}}
with
r:=\widehat{R}\circ T

is the desired computable retraction for
Z^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Now we are prepared to prove that stashing is an interior operator on the (strong) Weihrauch lattice.
Properties (1) and (2) in the following result are only made possible by the usage of the completion
\overline{Y}
, whereas
the existence of a retraction according to Lemma
10
guarantees that the
completion is not an obstacle for property (3). For a problem
{f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y}
the
completion
is defined
by

\overline{f}:\overline{X}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y},x\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(x)&\mbox{if $x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)$}\\
\bot&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

This completion was introduced and studied in
[
BG20
,
BG21
]
and we will use it in part (2) of the following proof.

Proposition 11

(Stashing)
.

The stashing operation
f\mapsto\Sigma f
is an interior operator for
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}
,
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}
,
\leq_{\rm W}^{*}
and
\leq_{\rm sW}^{*}
.
That is, for all problems
f,g
:

(1)

\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
,

(2)

f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}g\Longrightarrow\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma g
,

(3)

\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Sigma f
.

Analogous statements hold for
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}
,
\leq_{\rm W}^{*}
and
\leq_{\rm sW}^{*}
.

Proof 2.2
.

If we prove (1) and (3) for
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}
, then this implies the corresponding statements for
\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}^{*}
,
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}
and
\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{*}
.
Only in the case of (2) we explicitly need to consider the different types of reducibilities.

(1) We consider the computable projection
K:X^{\mathbb{N}}\to X,(x_{n})\mapsto x_{0}

and the computable function
H:Y\to\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}},y\mapsto(y,\bot,\bot,...)
.
Here
H
is computable, since the embedding
{\iota:Y\to\overline{Y}}
is computable according to
[
BG20
, Corollary 3.10]

and the element
\bot\in\overline{Y}
is computable too.
These two functions
K,H
witness the reduction
\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
, i.e.,

HfK(x_{n})\in\Sigma f(x_{n})
for all
(x_{n})\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Sigma f)
.
We note that the usage of the completion
\overline{Y}
guarantees the existence of computable default outputs
\bot\in\overline{Y}
.

(2) We consider problems
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
and
g:\subseteq W\rightrightarrows Z
.
If
f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}g
, then there are computable
K:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows W
and
H:\subseteq Z\rightrightarrows Y

such that
\emptyset\not=HgK(x)\subseteq f(x)
for all
x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)

[
BGP21
, Proposition 3.2]
.
Then the completion
\overline{H}:\overline{Z}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}
is computable by
[
BG20
, Proposition 4.9]
and so are the
parallelizations
\widehat{\overline{H}}:\overline{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\widehat{K}:\subseteq X^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows W^{\mathbb{N}}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\emptyset

\displaystyle\not=

\displaystyle\widehat{\overline{H}}\circ\Sigma g\circ\widehat{K}((x_{n})_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\overline{H}}\{(z_{n})_{n}\in\overline{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists n)\;z_{n}\in gK(x_{n})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(y_{n})\in\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists n)\;y_{n}\in HgK((x_{n})_{n})\}\subseteq\Sigma f((x_{n})_{n})

for all
(x_{n})_{n}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Sigma f)
.
We note that the totality of the completion
\overline{H}
guarantees that also those components of
(z_{n})_{n}\in\Sigma g\circ\widehat{K}((x_{n})_{n})\subseteq\overline{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
are processed, which are not in the domain of
H
.
Altogether, this proves
\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma g
.

Let now
f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}g
hold with computable
K:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times W
and
H:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times Z\rightrightarrows Y
according to
[
BP18
, Lemma 2.5]
,
i.e.,
\emptyset\not=H\circ({\mathrm{id}}_{{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}}\times g)\circ K(x)\subseteq f(x)
for all
x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
.
Then, again, the completion
\overline{H}:\overline{{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times Z}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}
is computable.
By
[
BG20
, Proposition 3.8, Corollary 3.10]
there is a computable
\iota:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\overline{Z}\to\overline{{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times Z}

with
\iota(p,z)=(p,z)
for all
(p,z)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times Z
.
Then
\widehat{(\overline{H}\circ\iota)}:({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\overline{Z})^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
can also be considered
as a problem of type
H^{\prime}:({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}\times\overline{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\widehat{K}
can be seen as a problem of type
\widehat{K}:\subseteq X^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}\times W^{\mathbb{N}}
.
As above we obtain

\emptyset\not=H^{\prime}\circ({\mathrm{id}}_{({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}}\times\Sigma g)\circ\widehat{K}((x_{n})_{n})\subseteq\Sigma f((x_{n})_{n}),

for all
(x_{n})_{n}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Sigma f)
, i.e.,
\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\Sigma g
.
The statements for
\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}^{*}
and
\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{*}
can be proved analogously with continuous
K,H
.
We note that by
[
BG20
, Proposition 4.9]

\overline{H}
is continuous, if
H
is so.

(3) We consider
\Sigma\Sigma f:\subseteq(X^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\overline{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
By Lemma
10
there is a computable retraction
r:\overline{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}\to\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
For every represented space
X
the map

s_{X}:(X^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}\to X^{\mathbb{N}},((x_{n,k})_{k})_{n}\mapsto(x_{n,k})_{\langle n,k\rangle}

that interleaves a double sequence in a single sequence, is a computable isomorphism, i.e., it is bijective and computable and its inverse is computable too.
We now consider the computable functions
K:=s_{X}^{-1}
and
H:=s_{Y}\circ\widehat{r}:\overline{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then we obtain

\displaystyle\emptyset

\displaystyle\not=

\displaystyle H\circ\Sigma\Sigma f\circ K((x_{n,k})_{\langle n,k\rangle})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{Y}\circ\widehat{r}\{(z_{n})_{n}\in\overline{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists n)\;z_{n}\in\Sigma f((x_{n,k})_{k})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{Y}\{((y_{n,k})_{k})_{n}\in(\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists n)(\exists k)\;y_{n,k}\in f(x_{n,k})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(y_{n,k})_{\langle n,k\rangle}\in\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists\langle n,k\rangle)\;y_{n,k}\in f(x_{n,k})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma f((x_{n,k})_{\langle n,k\rangle}).

This proves
\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Sigma f
.

We can conclude from property (2) in Proposition
11
that stashing is, in particular, invariant under (strong) Weihrauch reducibility
and can hence be seen as an operation on (strong) Weihrauch degrees.

Corollary 12
.

Stashing can be extended to an operation on (strong) Weihrauch degrees.

3.
The Stashing-Parallelization Monoid

We adopt the convention that we denote the parallelization of a problem
f
by
\widehat{f}
and the
stashing by
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.83334pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.83334pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.45001pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.5278pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}}
when we deal with single applications of these operators. However,
for iterated applications it is useful to use the notation
\Pi f
and
\Sigma f
instead.

The closure and interior operators
\Pi
and
\Sigma
generate a monoid
\{\Pi,\Sigma\}^{*}
under composition
and we want to study the action of this monoid on the Weihrauch lattice.
To this end, it is worth spelling out the problems
\Sigma\Pi f
and
\Pi\Sigma f
explicitly:

(1)

\Sigma\Pi f:\subseteq X^{{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}},(x_{n,k})\mapsto\{(y_{n,k}):(\exists n)(\forall k)\;y_{n,k}\in f(x_{n,k})\}
,

(2)

\Pi\Sigma f:\subseteq X^{{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}},(x_{n,k})\mapsto\{(y_{n,k}):(\forall n)(\exists k)\;y_{n,k}\in f(x_{n,k})\}
.

We can see that stashing corresponds to a usage of an existential quantifier whereas parallelization corresponds
to a usage of a universal quantifier in a certain sense. Hence
\Sigma\Pi
and
\Pi\Sigma
correspond to applications
of these quantifiers in different order.

There is a subtle technical point here: we have to deal with the spaces
\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}
,
which are not computably isomorphic. We recall that
\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}=(Y\cup\{\bot\})^{\mathbb{N}}
, whereas

\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}=Y^{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\bot_{\mathbb{N}}\}
. Hence, formally there is no subset relation between these two
sets. In order to make the latter a subset of the former, we can choose
\bot_{\mathbb{N}}:=(\bot,\bot,...)
, as implicitly done in the proof of Lemma
10
.
In this sense the double sequence notation
(y_{n,k})
in the description of
\Sigma\Pi f
should be understood.

Besides the retraction
r
from Lemma
10
we also need the maps
s,t
that exist according to the following lemma.
Intuitively speaking,
s
maps every sequence that contains a
\bot
to
\bot_{\mathbb{N}}
and
t
maps

\bot_{\mathbb{N}}
to some sequence that contains a
\bot

(which one it is, might depend on the given name of
\bot_{\mathbb{N}}
).

Lemma 13

(Completion of product spaces)
.

For every represented space
Y
there are computable

s:\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}
and
t:\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that
s|_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}=t|_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathrm{id}}_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Proof 3.1
.

A suitable computable map
s:\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}
with
s|_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathrm{id}}|_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}

is realized by a computable
F:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the property that
F\langle p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},...\rangle-1=\langle q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},...\rangle
with
q_{i}=p_{i}-1
for all
p_{i}
with
p_{i}-1\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
This can be achieved by copying the non-zero content of
p_{i}
subtracted by
1
into the
q_{i}
, where the resulting sequence
\langle q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},...\rangle
is filled up by zeros, whenever necessary (i.e., whenever
no non-zero content is available for some
p_{i}
then the entire output is filled up only with zeros as long as no non-zero content appears). That is, if one of the
p_{i}
is a name of
\bot\in\overline{Y}

(either because it has only finitely many digits different from zero or because
p_{i}-1\not\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{Y})
), then
F\langle p_{0},p_{1},...\rangle

is a name of
\bot_{\mathbb{N}}\in\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
In this way,
F
realizes the identity on
Y^{\mathbb{N}}
.

For the second part of the statement, we note that
\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
has a precomplete and total representation by
[
BG20
, Proposition 3.8]

and hence we can extend the parallelization of the computable embedding
Y\hookrightarrow\overline{Y}
to a computable
problem
t:\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
t|_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathrm{id}}_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}
by
[
BG21
, Proposition 2.6]
.

Our core observation on the action of the monoid
\{\Pi,\Sigma\}^{*}
on the (strong) Weihrauch
lattice is captured by the following result.

Proposition 14

(Action of the stashing-parallelization monoid)
.

For every problem
f
we obtain:

(1)

\Pi\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Pi f
,

(2)

\Pi\Sigma\Pi f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Pi f
,

(3)

\Sigma\Pi\Sigma f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Pi\Sigma f
.

Proof 3.2
.

(1)
Given an instance
(x_{n,k})
of
\Pi\Sigma f
, we just swap
n
– with
k
–positions in
(x_{n,k})
,
then we apply
\Sigma\Pi f
to the result, then we use the parallelization of the problem
t:\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{{\mathbb{N}}}

from Lemma
13
in order to convert the output of
\Sigma\Pi f
from
(\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}})^{\mathbb{N}}
into a double
sequence in
(\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}})^{\mathbb{N}}
and then we swap the
n
– and
k
–positions again to
obtain a result in
\Pi\Sigma f(x_{n,k})
, which is correct because

(\exists k)(\forall n)\;y_{n,k}\in f(x_{n,k})\Longrightarrow(\forall n)(\exists k)\;y_{n,k}\in f(x_{n,k}).

(2) Since
\Pi
is a closure operator we have
\Sigma\Pi f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Pi\Sigma\Pi f
and
\Pi\Pi f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Pi f
, and
by (1) and monotonicity of
\Sigma
we obtain
\Pi\Sigma\Pi f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Pi\Pi f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Pi f
.

(3) Since
\Sigma
is an interior operator we have
\Sigma\Pi\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Pi\Sigma f
and
\Sigma f\leq\Sigma\Sigma f
, and
by (1) and monotonicity of
\Pi
we obtain

\Pi\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Pi\Sigma\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Pi\Sigma f
.

That is the action of the stashing-parallelization monoid
\{\Pi,\Sigma\}^{*}
on a problem
f
in
the (strong) Weihrauch lattice leads to at most five distinct degrees
\{f,\Pi f,\Sigma f,\Sigma\Pi f,\Pi\Sigma f\}
,
which are arranged in a pentagon, see the diagram in Figure
2
.
Every line in the diagram indicates a
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}
–reduction in the upwards direction.

Of course, if a problem
f
is parallelizable and stashable (such as any problem of the form
f=\Sigma\Pi g
is),
then the pentagon reduces to a single degree. Other smaller sizes than five can be realized too and,
as we will see, also the maximal size of five can be realized. In any case, the
stashing-parallelization pentagon

can be seen as the
trace
of
f

4.
The Upper Turing Cone Operator

The main purpose of this section is to prove that the upper Turing cone operator
f\mapsto f^{\mathcal{D}}
is an
interior operator on the (strong) Weihrauch lattice that coincides with the interior operator
f\mapsto\Sigma f
,
restricted to (strongly) parallelizable problems.

In the following it is useful to have a simplified version of
\Sigma\Pi f
, where we replace double sequences on the input side by ordinary sequences. For this purpose
we consider the injection
I_{X}:X\hookrightarrow X^{\mathbb{N}},x\mapsto(x,x,x,...)
for every represented space
X
.

Lemma 16
.

\Sigma\Pi f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}(\Sigma\Pi f)\circ I_{X^{\mathbb{N}}}
for every problem
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
.

Proof 4.1
.

Since
I_{X^{\mathbb{N}}}
is computable, it is clear that
(\Sigma\Pi f)\circ I_{X^{\mathbb{N}}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Pi f
holds.
On the other hand, given an instance
((x_{n,k})_{k})_{n}\in X^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
of
\Sigma\Pi f
, we can compute the
interleaved sequence
(x_{n,k})_{\langle n,k\rangle}\in X^{\mathbb{N}}
and then apply
I_{X^{\mathbb{N}}}
followed by
\Sigma\Pi f
.
This yields a sequence
((y_{m,\langle n,k\rangle})_{\langle n,k\rangle})_{m}\in\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}

that satisfies the property
(\exists m)(\forall\langle n,k\rangle)\;y_{m,\langle n,k\rangle}\in f(x_{n,k})
.
Using the parallelization of the problem
t:\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\rightrightarrows\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}
from Lemma
13
we can convert
this into a sequence in
\overline{Y}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
and then extract a diagonal sequence

((y_{n,\langle n,k\rangle})_{\langle n,k\rangle})_{n}\in\overline{Y}^{{\mathbb{N}}^{2}}
from it.
This sequence can be converted back to
\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
by the parallelization of the function
s:\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\overline{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}

from Lemma
13
and we claim that the result
is a solution of
\Sigma\Pi f(((x_{n,k})_{k})_{n})
. This is because

(\exists m)(\forall\langle n,k\rangle)\;y_{m,\langle n,k\rangle}\in f(x_{n,k})\Longrightarrow(\exists n)(\forall k)\;y_{n,\langle n,k\rangle}\in f(x_{n,k}).

The exact relation between the upper Turing cone operator and stashing is captured in the following result,
again with the help of the injection
I_{X}
.

Proposition 17

(Upper Turing cone operator)
.

f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma f\circ I_{X}
holds for every problem
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
. In particular,
f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma f
.

Proof 4.2
.

We consider the represented spaces
(X,\delta_{X})
and
(Y,\delta_{Y})
.
We claim that every realizer
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
of
\Sigma f\circ I_{X}
is also
a realizer of
f^{\mathcal{D}}
, which proves
f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma f\circ I_{X}
.
To this end, let
F
be such a realizer and
x:=\delta_{X}(p)\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f^{\mathcal{D}})={\mathrm{dom}}(f)
.
Let
(y_{n}):=\delta_{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}F(p)\in\Sigma f(x,x,...)
.
Let
\langle q_{0},q_{1},...\rangle:=F(p)
and let
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a
computable realizer of the partial inverse
\iota^{-1}:\subseteq\overline{Y}\to Y
of the embedding
\iota:Y\to\overline{Y}
,
which is computable according to
[
BG20
, Corollary 3.10]
.
Then there is some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with

y_{n}=\delta_{Y}G(q_{n})=\delta_{\overline{Y}}(q_{n})\in f\delta_{X}(p)=f(x)
and
y_{n}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}G(q_{n})\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}F(p)
.
This proves the claim.

For the reverse reduction
\Sigma f\circ I_{X}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}f^{\mathcal{D}}
we first note that

\delta_{\overline{Y}}
is a precomplete representation by
[
BG20
, Proposition 3.8]
.
Hence there is a total computable
r:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

the function
\varphi^{q}_{r(n)}
is always total and if
\varphi^{q}_{n}
is total and
\varphi^{q}_{n}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{Y})
, then
\delta_{\overline{Y}}(\varphi^{q}_{r(n)})=\delta_{Y}(\varphi^{q}_{n})
.
Intuitively, the programme with code
r(n)
works as the programme
n
,
but it adds
1
to all output results and fills up the output with dummy symbols
0
in appropriate positions
as long as no other
better information becomes available.
Now we consider the computable function
H:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

with
H(q):=\langle\varphi^{q}_{r(0)},\varphi^{q}_{r(1)},...\rangle
.
We claim that
HF
is a realizer of
\Sigma f\circ I_{X}
for every realizer
F
of
f^{\mathcal{D}}
.
To this end, let
F
be such a realizer and
x:=\delta_{X}(p)\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\Sigma f\circ I_{X})={\mathrm{dom}}(f)
.
Then there exists some
y\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}F(p)
with
y\in f(x)
. Hence, there is some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

such that
y=\delta_{Y}(\varphi^{F(p)}_{n})=\delta_{\overline{Y}}(\varphi^{F(p)}_{r(n)})
.
This implies that
\delta_{\overline{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}}HF(p)\in\Sigma f\circ I_{X}(x)
, which completes the proof.

We note that the main idea of the proof, namely to compute on all Gödel numbers in parallel,
can only be realized because stashing uses a completion
\overline{Y}
of the space
Y
on the output side.

By a combination of Propositions
17
and
14
with Lemma
16

we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 18

(Upper Turing cone operator)
.

(\Pi f)^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma\Pi f
and
(\Pi\Sigma f)^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Pi\Sigma f
for every problem
f
.

This means that both problems on the right-hand side of the diagram in Figure
2

can be seen as upper Turing cone versions and hence as computability-theoretic problems.
We emphasize that Turing cones appear here out of a purely topological context without any computability theory being involved.
This is because Corollary
18

is also correct when the computability-theoretic Weihrauch reducibility is replaced by its topological counterpart.

We mention in passing that the upper Turing cone operator is an interior operator on the Weihrauch lattice.

Proposition 19

(Upper Turing cone operator as interior operator)
.

The operation
f\mapsto f^{\mathcal{D}}
is an interior operator on the (strong) Weihrauch lattice.
That is, for all problems
f,g
we have:

(1)

f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
,

(2)

f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}g\Longrightarrow f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}g^{\mathcal{D}}
,

(3)

f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}f^{{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{D}}}
.

Analogous statements hold for
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}
,
\leq_{\rm W}^{*}
and
\leq_{\rm sW}^{*}
.

Proof 4.3
.

We consider problems
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
and
g:\subseteq W\rightrightarrows Z
.

(1) This follows from
f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\Sigma f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
, which holds by
Propositions
17
and
11
.

(2) Let
f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}g
hold via computable
H,K:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.,

H\langle{\mathrm{id}},GK\rangle
is a realizer of
f
whenever
G
is a realizer of
g
.
We claim that
f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}g^{\mathcal{D}}
holds via
{\mathrm{id}},K
.
Let
p
be a name of some input
x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
. Then
K(p)
is a
name of a point
w\in{\mathrm{dom}}(g)
and any name
q
of a point in
g(w)

yields a name
H\langle p,q\rangle
of a point in
f(x)
, since there is a realizer

G
of
g
with
GK(p)=q
. Let now
G
be a realizer of
g^{\mathcal{D}}
. Then
there is a name
q
of a point in
g(w)
such that
q\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}GK(p)
.
Hence
H\langle p,q\rangle\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}\langle p,GK(p)\rangle
.
This shows that
\langle{\mathrm{id}},GK\rangle
is a realizer of
f^{\mathcal{D}}
whenever

G
is a realizer of
g^{\mathcal{D}}
and hence
f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}g^{\mathcal{D}}
.
The statement for
\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}
can be proved analogously.
In the topological cases we have to work with continuous
H,K
.
Then
H
is computable relative to some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and we obtain
as above
H\langle p,q\rangle\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}\langle r,p,GK(p)\rangle
.
Hence
f^{\mathcal{D}}\leq_{\mathrm{W}}^{*}g^{\mathcal{D}}
holds via continuous
H^{\prime},K
, where

H^{\prime}\langle p,q\rangle:=\langle r,p,q\rangle
.

(3) We have even
f^{{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{D}}}=f^{\mathcal{D}}
by transitivity of Turing reducibility.

By Proposition
14
stashing extends to an interior operator
on parallelizable Weihrauch degrees and by Corollary
18

the upper Turing cone operator coincides on those degrees with stashing.

Corollary 20
.

f\mapsto\Sigma f
and
f\mapsto f^{\mathcal{D}}
are identical interior operators restricted to (strongly) parallelizable (strong) Weihrauch degrees.

We note that
f\mapsto\Sigma f
and
f\mapsto f^{\mathcal{D}}
are not identical on arbitrary Weihrauch degrees.
The problem
f^{\mathcal{D}}
is always computable when
f
has only computable solutions. For instance,

\mathsf{LPO}^{\mathcal{D}}
is computable, while this is not the case for
\Sigma(\mathsf{LPO})
(see Proposition
31
).

We can also formulate this result such that problems which are simultaneously stashable and parallelizable
are automatically closed under applying the upper Turing cone operator.

Corollary 21
.

For every problem
f
the following conditions are equivalent to each other:

(1)

\Sigma f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
and
\Pi f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
,

(2)

f^{\mathcal{D}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
and
\Pi f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}f
.

An analogous property holds with
\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}
instead of
\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}
.

It follows from Corollary
18
that problems
g
that are simultaneously parallelizable
and stashable can only occur in certain regions of the Weihrauch lattice. For one, every problem with the set
of Turing degrees as target set is
densely realized
by
[
BHK17
, Corollary 4.9]
, which means
that a realizer of such a problem can produce outputs that start with arbitrary prefixes.
This in turn implies by
[
BP18
, Proposition 6.3]
that any problem with discrete output below it has to be computable.
We formulate this as a corollary.

Corollary 22

(Parallelizable and stashable problems)
.

Let
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}
be a problem and let
g
be a problem that is parallelizable and stashable.
If
f\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}g
holds, then
f
is computable.

One of the weakest problems with discrete output that is discontinuous is
\mathsf{ACC}_{\mathbb{N}}
,
the all-or-co-unique choice problem on
{\mathbb{N}}
.
This problem was studied in
[
BHK17
]
and an equivalent problem was investigated earlier
under the name
\mathsf{LLPO}_{X}

[
Wei92
,
HK14a
]
(and under the name
\mathsf{LLPO}_{\infty}

[
Myl06
, Definition 16]
in the case of
\mathsf{ACC}_{\mathbb{N}}
).
Intuitively speaking,
\mathsf{ACC}_{\mathbb{N}}
is the problem that given a list of natural numbers which is either empty
or contains exactly one number, one has to produce a number which is not in the list.
For

we can also phrase Corollary
22
as follows.

Corollary 23

(The cone of all-or-co-unique choice)
.

If
\mathsf{ACC}_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}g
holds for some problem
g
, then
g
cannot be simultaneously parallelizable and stashable.

Hence, in a certain sense, problems that are parallelizable and stashable at the same time are rare, even rarer than this result suggests.
Namely,
\mathsf{ACC}_{\mathbb{N}}
is not the weakest discontinuous problem with discrete output.
Mylatz has proved that there are also discontinuous problems of type

with
f\mathop{<_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{ACC}_{\mathbb{N}}

[
Myl06
, Satz 14]
.

The reader might have noticed that a lot of problems that occur in the lower parts of our pentagons

{defi}

[Complementary problem]
For every problem

\mathsf{NON}=(\geq_{\mathrm{T}})^{\mathrm{c}}

Even though complementation yields a neat way of expressing these problems,

is not an operation on

to find a hyperimmune
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
from an arbitrary member of a hyperimmune degree.
This also yields a second proof of
\mathsf{NMAJ}\mathop{\not\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{DIS}
.
Finally, we mention that the separation in Proposition
35
also yields a separation of the
corresponding problems (i.e., the sets given by the respective solutions on computable instances) in the Medvedev lattice (see
[
BGP21
, Theorem 9.1]
).

We now want to show that
\mathsf{MEET}
is equivalent to
\mathsf{HYP}
. The corresponding proof of
[
HRSZ17
, Theorem 38]
can be transferred into our setting.
On the first sight it might be a bit surprising that replacing
\not=
by
\not\geq
makes a difference, while replacing

<
by
=
does not. However, this comparison does not take the aspect of totality into account.
For completeness we include the proof, which is interesting by itself.

Proposition 36
.

\mathsf{HYP}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{MEET}
.

Proof 6.3
.

For
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\mathsf{MEET}(p)=\{q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\forall i)(\varphi_{i}^{p}\mbox{ total }\Longrightarrow(\exists n)\;\varphi_{i}^{p}(n)=q(n))\}.

We note that in the case of
\mathsf{HYP}
we obtain a corresponding formulation with
\not\geq
instead of
=
. In this case totality
does not need to be mentioned as the negative condition is automatically satisfied by partial
\varphi_{i}^{p}
.
It is obvious that
\mathsf{HYP}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{MEET}
, as we just have to use
q\mapsto q+1
to translate the solution on the same input
p
.
For the opposite direction we note that by the smn-theorem there is a computable
s:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\varphi_{s(i)}^{p}(n)=\Phi_{i}^{p}\langle i,n\rangle
for all
i,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Here
\Phi_{i}^{p}(n)
denotes the time complexity, i.e., the number of computation steps required to compute
\varphi_{i}^{p}(n)
(if it exists and undefined otherwise).
Then given an input
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
q\in\mathsf{HYP}(p)
we can compute
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with

r\langle i,n\rangle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi^{p}_{i}\langle i,n\rangle&\mbox{if $\Phi_{i}^{p}\langle i,n\rangle<q(n)$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for all
i,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
If
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
is such that
\varphi_{i}^{p}
is total, then
\Phi_{i}^{p}
and hence
\varphi^{p}_{s(i)}
are total too and hence there is some

n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\Phi_{i}^{p}\langle i,n\rangle=\varphi_{s(i)}^{p}(n)<q(n)
.
This implies that
r\langle i,n\rangle=\varphi_{i}^{p}\langle i,n\rangle
, i.e.,
r\in\mathsf{MEET}(p)
.
Since
r
can be computed, given
p,q
, we obtain
\mathsf{MEET}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{HYP}
.

We note that the backwards reduction is not a strong Weihrauch reduction.

7.
Retractions, All-or-Unique Choice and Gödel Numbers

In the previous sections we have discussed the stashing of a number of problems of type
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}
with natural number output.
In this particular situation we can also describe stashing in an alternative way.
This is because the space
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is related to the space
{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}
of partial functions
f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}

that we can represent by
\delta_{{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}}\langle i,p\rangle:=\varphi_{i}^{p}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}},p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The exact relation between these two spaces is captured in the following lemma. The function
\iota
essentially identifies
\uparrow
(i.e., undefined) with
\bot
.

Lemma 37

(Space of partial functions)
.

The function
\iota:{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with

\iota(p)(n):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p(n)&\mbox{if $n\in{\mathrm{dom}}(p)$}\\
\bot&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

is computable and there is a computable function
\sigma:\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

\sigma\circ\iota(p)
is an extension of
p
for all
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}
.

The proof is straightforward. We just note that a prefix of a name of
\bot
can start like a name of a natural number
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

and continue with dummy symbols
0
, which means that it is actually a name of
\bot
.
Hence, we do not get that the spaces
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}
are computably isomorphic.
However, Lemma
37
roughly speaking states that they are “isomorphic up to extensions”.
Hence, for every property that is invariant under extensions, it does not matter whether we work with
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
or
{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}
.
This does, in particular, apply to stashing. Hence, for problems of type
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}
we can also describe stashing by the following definition.

{defi}

For every problem
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}
we define
{}^{\varphi}f:\subseteq X^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
by

{}^{\varphi}f(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}:=\{\langle i,p\rangle\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists n\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\varphi_{i}^{p}))\;\varphi_{i}^{p}(n)\in f(x_{n})\}

for all
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Hence, as an immediate corollary of Lemma
37
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 38

(Stashing for discrete outputs)
.

\Sigma f\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}{\,}^{\varphi}f
for all problems
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}
.

This again sheds light on the fact that stashing of parallelizable problems gives us computability-theoretic problems.

We note that the usage of the Gödel numbering and partial functions
\varphi_{i}^{p}
in the previous sections was
mostly related to the input side of problems. Hence, implicitly, we have worked with the input space
{\mathbb{N}}^{\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Now, we want to get some better understanding of the effect of the space
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
on the output side.
Corollary
38
can be seen as a way of replacing
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
on the output side by
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

For some problems, as those discussed in Propositions
24
and
31
it appeared
that directly replacing
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
by
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
on the output side was also possible without changing the degree.
However, the problem
\mathsf{NGEQ}
from Definition
6
is an example that shows that this is not always possible.
If we just replace
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
by
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
in
\Sigma(\mathsf{NGEQ})
, then we obtain
\mathsf{NMAJ}
, which is not
equivalent to
\Sigma(\mathsf{NGEQ})
by Proposition
34
.

Hence, it is useful to have upper bounds on the price that such a direct replacement of
\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}
by
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

costs. This is exactly captured by the following
retraction problem

\mathsf{RET}_{X}
that we define together with the closely related extension problem
\mathsf{EXT}_{X}
.

{defi}

[Retraction and extension problems]

For every represented space
X
and
Y\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
we define the following problems:

(1)

\mathsf{RET}_{X}:\overline{X}\rightrightarrows X,x\mapsto\{y\in X:x\in X\Longrightarrow x=y\}
,

(2)

\mathsf{EXT}_{Y}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows Y^{\mathbb{N}},\langle i,p\rangle\mapsto\{q\in Y^{\mathbb{N}}:q\mbox{ is a total extension of }\varphi_{i}^{p}\}
,

where
{\mathrm{dom}}(\mathsf{EXT}_{Y}):=\{\langle i,p\rangle:{\mathrm{range}}(\varphi_{i}^{p})\subseteq Y\}
.

The parallelization
\widehat{\mathsf{RET}_{X}}
captures the complexity of translating
\overline{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
into
X^{\mathbb{N}}
.
By Lemma
37
we obtain the following.

Corollary 39
.

\widehat{\mathsf{RET}_{X}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{EXT}_{X}
for all
X\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
.

The problem
\mathsf{RET}_{X}:\overline{X}\rightrightarrows X
is a multi-valued retraction, i.e., a problem that satisfies

\mathsf{RET}_{X}|_{X}={\mathrm{id}}_{X}
. And it is the simplest such retraction in terms of strong Weihrauch reducibility.
Hence, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 40

(Multi-retraceability)
.

Let
X
be a represented space. Then there exists a computable multi-valued retraction
R:\overline{X}\rightrightarrows X
if and only if

\mathsf{RET}_{X}
is computable.

Proof 7.1
.

It is clear that
\mathsf{RET}_{X}
is a multi-valued retraction, which yields the “if”–direction of the proof.
On the other hand, if there is a computable multi-valued retraction
R
, then
\mathsf{RET}_{X}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}R
and hence

\mathsf{RET}_{X}
is computable too.

Spaces that allow for computable multi-valued retractions were called
multi-retraceable
in
[
BG20
]
.
This condition was further studied by Hoyrup in
[
Hoy21
]
and related to fixed-point properties.
Here we rather have to deal with spaces that are not multi-retraceable.
In these cases we can consider the complexity of
\mathsf{RET}_{X}
as a measure of how far the space
X

is away from multi-retraceability or, in other words, how difficult it is to determine total extensions.
We gain some upper bounds directly from
[
BG21
, Proposition 2.10]
.

Fact 41
.

\mathsf{RET}_{X}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\lim
for all totally represented
X
and
\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}
.

For finite
X\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
we can classify
\mathsf{RET}_{X}
somewhat more precisely.
It is quite easy to see that the retraction problem
\mathsf{RET}_{n}
is just equivalent to all-or-unique choice
\mathsf{AUC}_{n}

and hence located in between
\mathsf{LLPO}=\mathsf{AUC}_{2}
and
\mathsf{C}_{n}
, as well as
\mathsf{LPO}
.

Proposition 42

(Retraction problem)
.

\mathsf{LLPO}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{RET}_{n}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{n}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{C}_{n}
, and
\mathsf{RET}_{n}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{LPO}
for all
n\geq 2
.

Proof 7.2
.

We start with proving
\mathsf{RET}_{n}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{n}
. Given a name
p
of
x\in\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}
we generate
a name
q
of the set
\{0,1,...,n-1\}
, as long as only dummy information appears in
p
.
If some prefix of
p
starts to look like a name of some
k<n
, then we modify the output

q
to a name of the set
\{k\}
. Since
k\in\mathsf{RET}_{n}(x)
, this yields the desired reduction.

Now we consider the inverse reduction
\mathsf{AUC}_{n}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{RET}_{n}
.
Given a name
p
of a set
A\subseteq\{0,1,...,n-1\}
with
A=\{0,1,...,n-1\}
or
A=\{k\}
for some
k<n
,
we generate a name of
\bot
as long as the name looks like a name of
\{0,1,...,n-1\}
. In the moment
where the represented set is clearly smaller, we wait until the information suffices to identify the singleton
\{k\}
,
and then we modify the output to an output of
k\in\overline{\{0,1,...,n-1\}}
.
If we apply
\mathsf{RET}_{n}
to the generated point in
\overline{\{0,1,...,n-1\}}
it yields a point in
A
.
This establishes the desired reduction.

The reductions
\mathsf{LLPO}=\mathsf{AUC}_{2}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{n}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{C}_{n}
are clear for all
n\geq 2
.
The reduction
\mathsf{RET}_{n}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}
is easy to see, as well as
\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{LPO}
.
For the latter reduction, we consider a name
p
of an input
x\in\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}
. We use
\mathsf{LPO}

in order to decide whether
p\not=\widehat{0}
. If
p=\widehat{0}
, then we can produce any
output
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
; otherwise we search for the first non-zero component
k+1
in
p
and
produce the corresponding
k
as output. This yields the reduction
\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{LPO}
.
Finally, the reduction
\mathsf{LPO}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}
can be seen as follows. Given an input
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
we seek the first non-zero component. If this component appears in position
n
, then we generate
the output
x=n\in\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}
. If there is no non-zero component, then we generate the output
x=\bot\in\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Now given some
k\in\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}(x)
and
p
, we search a non-zero component in
p
up to position
k
.
If such a non-zero component appears, then
\mathsf{LPO}(p)=0
; otherwise
\mathsf{LPO}(p)=1
. This describes
the reduction
\mathsf{LPO}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}
.

Using this result, we obtain the pentagon of
\mathsf{RET}_{n}
in the Weihrauch lattice.
By Fact
4
we have
\widehat{\mathsf{LLPO}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\widehat{\mathsf{C}_{n}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{WKL}
,
which implies
\widehat{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{WKL}
.
On the other hand, by Proposition
31
we have
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LLPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LLPO}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LLPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LLPO}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LLPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LLPO}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LLPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LLPO}}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LPO}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LPO}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LPO}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{LPO}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{LPO}}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{DIS}

and this yields
\mathsf{RET}_{n}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{DIS}
.

Corollary 43

(Pentagon of retractions)
.

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.75002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.82344pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{DIS}
,
\widehat{\mathsf{RET}_{n}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{WKL}
, and
\widehat{\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}}\lim
for all
n\geq 2
.

This means that in the Weihrauch lattice the pentagon of
\mathsf{RET}_{n}
for
n\geq 2
looks like the pentagon of
\mathsf{LLPO}

in Figure
4
, while the pentagon of
\mathsf{RET}_{\mathbb{N}}
looks like the pentagon of
\mathsf{LPO}

in Figure
5
(except that
one cannot use strong Weihrauch reducibility). The first mentioned fact is also interesting, as the problems
\mathsf{RET}_{n}
form a strictly increasing chain, as we prove next by a simple cardinality argument.

Proposition 44
.

\mathsf{AUC}_{n}\mathop{<_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{n+1}\mathop{<_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{\mathbb{N}}
for all
n\geq 2
.

Proof 7.3
.

It suffices to prove
\mathsf{AUC}_{n+1}\mathop{\not\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{n}
for all
n\geq 2
.
Let us assume for a contradiction that
\mathsf{AUC}_{n+1}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{n}
for some
n\geq 2
via computable
H,K
.
Now consider the name
p=000...
of the empty set
\emptyset={\mathrm{range}}(p-1)
. Then
a_{k}:=H\langle p,k\rangle\in\{0,...,n\}
for
k<n
satisfy
|\{a_{0},...,a_{n-1}\}|\leq n
.
For each
k<n
there is some prefix of
p
of length
l_{k}
that suffices to produce the output
a_{k}
. For the prefix
l:=\max\{l_{0},...,l_{n}\}
we have

|H\langle p|_{l}{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\{0,...,n\}\rangle|=|\{a_{0},...,a_{n-1}\}|\leq n,

despite the fact that
p|_{l}{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
contains names of all singleton sets
A\subseteq\{0,1,...,n\}
.
Since there are
n+1
such sets, this is a contradiction!

That means that with the problems
\mathsf{RET}_{n}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{AUC}_{n}
we have a strictly increasing sequence of problems
in between
\mathsf{LLPO}
and
\mathsf{LPO}
that all parallelize and stash to the same problems, respectively.
This is a strong refutation of injectivity of the following operation.

Corollary 45
.

The map
f\mapsto(\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.83334pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.83334pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.45001pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.5278pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{f}}$}}}\hss}{f}},\widehat{f})
is not injective on Weihrauch degrees.

This means that pentagons do not characterize the Weihrauch degrees that generate them, even though they reveal some useful
information about them.

8.
Limit Avoidance and a Phase Transition

The pentagons determined so far are all related to the discontinuity problem.
This could lead to the false conclusion that
\mathsf{DIS}
is the bottom vertex of typical pentagons.
However, this is rather based on the fact that our study of pentagons was motivated
by an analysis of the discontinuity problem.
In this closing section we study the weakest problem known to us
whose stashing is not
\mathsf{DIS}
.
This is the
limit avoidance problem

\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}
, defined below.
We define it together with the related
non-lowness problem

\mathsf{NLOW}
.

{defi}

[Problems related to limits]
We consider the following problems for
X\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
:

(1)

\mathsf{NLIM}_{X}:\subseteq X^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows X,p\mapsto\{k\in X:\lim_{n\to\infty}p(n)\not=k\}
with

{\mathrm{dom}}(\mathsf{NLIM}_{X})=\{p:(p(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges
\}
,

(2)

\mathsf{NLOW}:{\mathcal{D}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{D}},a\mapsto\{b\in{\mathcal{D}}:b^{\prime}\not\leq a^{\prime}\}
.

The following fact is easy to see:

Fact 46
.

\mathsf{ACC}_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{NLIM}_{2}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\lim_{2}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{sW}}}\lim_{\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{sW}}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}
.

If we can prove that
\mathsf{DIS}\mathop{<_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.82344pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}
holds, then this implies that the stashings of all problems
above
\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}
also lie above
\mathsf{DIS}
. We prove the following stronger result.

Proposition 47
.

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.82344pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}\mathop{\not\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{WKL}
.

Proof 8.1
.

We recall that we are working with

\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.82344pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}},\langle p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},...\rangle\mapsto\{q\in\overline{{\mathbb{N}}}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists n)\;\lim_{i\to\infty}p_{i}(n)\not=q(n)\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

Let us assume for a contradiction that
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{2.27779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.82344pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}$}}}\hss}{\mathsf{NLIM}_{\mathbb{N}}}}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{W}}}\mathsf{WKL}
holds. As

9.
Conclusions

We have introduced the stashing operation as a dual of parallelization and we have proved that it is
an interior operator. The action of parallelization and stashing on Weihrauch degrees naturally leads
to the study of stashing-parallelization pentagons, which can be used to describe a number of
natural Weihrauch degrees. In many cases of the studied pentagons the discontinuity problem
featured as the bottom problem of the respective pentagon.
However, we were also able to identify a phase transition point, where this no longer
happens to be the case.
The duality inherent in pentagons needs to be studied further in order to simplify
the calculation of pentagons, which tends to be difficult in more advanced examples.
