Interaction of a free boundary with a diffusion on a plane: analogy with the obstacle problem
Luis A. Caffarelli

Abstract
A horizontal N N -dimensional plane, having a diffusion of its own, exchanges with the lower half space. There, a reaction-diffusion process, modelled by a free boundary problem, takes place. We wish to understand whether, and how, the free boundary meets the plane. The origin of this problem is a two-dimensional reaction diffusion model proposed some time ago by the second author, in collaboration with H. Berestycki and L. Rossi, to model how biological invasions can be enhanced by a line of fast diffusion. Some counter-intuitive numerical simulations of this model, due to A.-C. Coulon, have been explained by the first two authors by transforming the model into a free boundary interacting with a line, and a careful study of the free boundary. At this occasion, it was noticed that the free boundary very much like that of the obstacle problem. The goal of the paper is to explain how this analogy with the obstacle problem can be pushed further in higher space dimensions.



1
Introduction

1.1
Model and question

Let
\varphi
be a smooth concave function of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, attaining a positive maximum at
x=0
.
We look for a function
u(X)
, defined for
X=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}
with
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}:=\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
, and a surface
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}
such that

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\Delta u=&0\quad(x,y)\in\{u>0\}\\
|\nabla u|=&1\quad((x,y)\in\Gamma:=\partial\{u>0\})\\
\\
-\Delta_{x}u+u_{y}=&0\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},y=0)\\
u(x,0)\geq&\varphi(x).\end{array}\right.

We ask whether, and how, the free surface
\Gamma
meets the plane
\{y=0\}
.

1.2
Motivation of the model, possible extensions

The study of Model (
1.1
) is motivated by previous investigations on a two-dimensional version that modelled the influence of a line of fast diffusion on the propagation of reaction-diffusion waves. More precisely, in
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
,
the first two authors study o a system of the form

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta u+c\partial_{x}u=&0\quad(x,y)\in\{u>0\}\\
|\nabla u|=&1\quad((x,y)\in\Gamma:=\partial\{u>0\}\\
\\
-u_{xx}+c\partial_{x}u+u_{y}=&0\quad\hbox{for $x\in\mathbb{R}$, $y=0$}\\
u(-\infty,y)=&1,\quad\hbox{uniformly in $y\in\mathbb{R}_{-}$}\\
u(+\infty,y)=&0\quad\hbox{pointwise in $y\in\mathbb{R}_{-}$}.\end{array}\right.

Studying (
1.2
) was itself motivated by a rather a counter-intuitive numerical simulation in
[
17
]
, of a system initially proposed in
[
6
]
to model the influence of transportation networks on biological invasions. See also
[
4
]
.

Among other things, it is proved in
[
14
]
that the free boundary
\Gamma
hits the line
\{y=0\}
as an inverted parabola: assuming that
(0,0)
is the intersection point, then, in its vicinity,
\Gamma
may be described as

y=-\frac{x^{2}}{2},\ \ \ \ x\leq 0,

and
u(x,0)
grows in a quadratic fashion for negative
x
. This is typical of the behaviour of the solutions to the obstacle problem, and the present study of Model (
1.1
) is an attempt to understand whether the situation discovered in
[
14
]
is general.
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
below show that this is indeed the case.

We point out that, while Model shows the genericity of the two-dimensional situation in several space dimensions, our study depends on the global geometry of the problem, and that properties such as monotonicity in every direction, help simplifying
the analysis. Thus, a general local study of the interaction of the free boundary of the one phase problem with the plane is still to be carried out. Whether, in particular, Theorem
1.2
would persist in a general setting, is something we do not know. Another interesting question is to replace the equation for
u
on the plane by the family of equations

-D\Delta u+u_{y}=0,

and investigate what happens as
D\o+\infty
. This question, by the way, is studied in the forthcoming work
[
16
]
.

Other geometries would be relevant. In particular, the obstacle condition

u(x,0)\geq\varphi(x)

could certainly be replaced by a Dirichlet type condition on a vertical cylinder inside the lower half space. The ideas displayed here would probably work in this setting without so many changes. The diffusion equation on the plane
\{y=0\}
could also be replaced by

-\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\partial_{x_{i}}\bigl(a_{ij}(\nabla u)\bigl)+u_{y}=0,

where the matrix
\bigl(a_{ij}(p)\bigl)
satisfies the usual ellipticity conditions, such as, for instance, in
[
7
]
. This last extension could open the door to the study of interesting directional effects.

1.3
Main results

Let us first introduce some notations. As we will have to deal with several choices of balls, either in the lower half space
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}
or the plane
\{y=0\}
it is convenient to denote them with distinctive signs now. So, let
B^{N+1}_{R}(X)
be the ball of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
with centre

; when

we will simply denote it by

. In the same spirit, we denote by

the ball of the plane

with centre

if

.

Let us now make a set of slightly more stringent assumptions on

. They are probably not indispensable, however they will ensure, in a relatively painless way, the existence of a solution to (

) whose support on the plane is larger than that of
\varphi
.

1.

We have
\varphi(x)=1
if
x\in B_{1}^{N}
.

2.

There is
\rho_{0}>0
such that

(1.3)

\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ \varphi^{+}\subset B^{N}_{1+\rho_{0}}.

Moreover
\varphi
is strictly concave outside
B^{N}_{1}
.

With these assumptions we can prove the

Theorem 1.1

If
\rho_{0}>0
is small enough, Problem (
1.1
) has a solution
(u,\Gamma)
which has the following properties:

1.

there is
\rho_{1}>\rho_{0}
such that

(1.4)

B^{N}_{1+\rho_{1}}\subset\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ u(.,0).

2.

There is
\theta_{0}>0
such that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
outside
\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ \varphi^{+}
, we have
\partial_{e}u(x,0)\leq 0
for every direction

making an angle less or equal

with
\displaystyle\frac{x}{|x|}
.

3.

The free boundary
\Gamma
meets the plane
\{y=0\}
along a Lipschitz surface, and encloses a domain
\Omega
of the plane
\{y=0\}
.

This result is in the spirit of the work
[
8
]
of the first author, where some a priori monotonicity results of the solution entail the Lipschitz property of the free boundary in an obstacle type problem.

Theorem 1.2

There is
\delta>0
such that have

u_{y}(x,0)\geq\delta\quad\hbox{if $x\in\Omega$}.

Moreover,

is Hölder-continuous in

.

The end result of Theorem

is that

behaves, in the vicinity of

, very much like the solution of the obstacle problem in its reduced form, that is, according to the notation of

-\Delta_{x}u(x,0)+g(x)H(u)=0\quad\hbox{in $\Omega=\{u(x,0)>0\}$},

where

is the Heaviside function, and the function

is a positive, Hölder continuous function, a role that the function

, can be made to play thanks to Theorem
1.2
. This property is sufficient to study a good part of the regularity of
\partial\Omega
, see for instance
[
11
]
, or the original paper
[
10
]
, or the review
[
9
]
. In particular, Theorem 6 of
[

]

1.4
Organisation of the paper

A first issue is to construct a solution to (
1.1
) having some nice geometrical properties, such as the monotonicity in a cone of directions of the plane. While this should not be indispensable to the study (at least the basic properties), it greatly helps us in the analysis. And so, the next two sections are devoted to the construction of such a solution to (
1.1
), through an approximation by a singularly perturbed semilinear problem. We prove, in particular, that the free boundary
\Gamma
intersects the plane, opening the way to the analogy with the obstacle problem. In Section 4, we start the analysis of
u(.,0)
by proving the basic properties of the solutions to the obstacle problem. We prove optimal regularity, that is,
u(.,0)
is
C^{1,1}
in the vicinity of

\Gamma\cup\{y=0\}
, and that
u(x,.)
grows at least quadratically as
x
departs from the free boundary. The last section is devoted to the Hölder continuity of
u
, thus concluding the analogy.

2
Construction of a solution to (
1.1
): the semilinear approximation

The goal of the next two sections is to construct a solution
(u,\Gamma)
of (
1.1
) that is as classical as possible, that is the free boundary relation is satisfied in the strong sense away from the place where
\Gamma
meets the plane, and the exchange condition
-\Delta_{x}u+u_{y}=0
is satisfied in the strong sense, the function
u_{y}
being
L^{\infty}
at this stage. For that we will resort to a smoothing of the free boundary problem by a semilinear one. This procedure, currently used in mathematical models for premixed flames, was first used in multidimensional elliptic problems by Berestycki, Nirenberg and the first author in
[
3
]
to approximate one-phase free boundary problems. See also the work of Lee, Mellet and the first author where this approximation is used in the homogenisation of this type of free boundary problems for parabolic equations. The procedure was more recently used in
[
14
]
to construct a travelling wave solution.

As is usual, the coincidence set of
u
will be the (closed) subset of the plane
\mathbb{R}^{N}
where
u(.,0)=\varphi
. We denote it by
\mathcal{C}_{u}
.

Consider a smooth function
f
, positive on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and such that
\displaystyle\int\beta(u)du=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, with
\beta(u)=uf(u)
, and such that
uf^{\prime}(u)+f(u)\geq 0
for
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. A suitable multiple of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+u}
will do the trick.

We will first solve the problem

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta u+\beta(u)=&0\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}\\
\\
-\Delta_{x}u+u_{y}=&0\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},y=0)\\
u(x,0)\geq&\varphi(x).\end{array}\right.

Once a solution
u
is constructed, we will set, for every
\varepsilon>0
:

(2.2)

\beta_{\varepsilon}(u)=\displaystyle\frac{u}{\varepsilon^{2}}f(\frac{u}{\varepsilon}),

and show that, up to a subsequence, the family
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
will converge, as
\varepsilon>0
, to a solution of (
1.1
).

Let us therefore start with (
2.1
). We start the construction in a standard way: we first look for a solution to the approximate problem

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta u+\beta(u)=&0\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}\cap B^{N+1}_{R}\\
-\Delta_{x}u+u_{y}=&0\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},y=0)\cap B^{N}_{R}\\
\\
u(x,0)\geq&\varphi(x)\\
u(X)=&0\quad(|X|=R).\end{array}\right.

In what follows,
R
is assumed to be large enough so that
\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ \varphi^{+}
is inside
B_{R/2}^{N}
We have the easy a priori estimate

0\leq u\leq\varphi(0).

The energy functional

(2.4)

J_{R}(u)=\int_{B_{R}^{N+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}}\biggl(|\nabla u|^{2}+B(u)\biggl)dX+\int_{B_{R}^{N}}|\nabla_{x}u(x,0)|^{2}dx,

with

\quad B(u)=\int_{0}^{u}\beta(v)dv,

is easily shown to attain a minimum over all functions
u\in H^{1}_{0}(B^{N+1}_{R}\cap\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-})
, whose trace on the plane
\{y=0\}
is larger than
\varphi
and belongs to
H^{1}_{0}(B^{N}_{R})
.
This means that (
2.3
) has at least a solution. Now, we need to prove enough smoothness - not such a difficult issue at this stage. The main step is a uniform Lipschitz bound.

Lemma 2.1

There is a constant
M>0
, independent of
\lambda
and
R
(but, at this stage, strongly depending on
\beta
) such that, for all
u\in S_{\lambda,R}
we have

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}^{N+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-})}\leq M.

Proof.
The only point that is slightly nonclassical here is to handle the detachment from the coincidence set
\mathcal{C}_{u}
, and the points where the Wentzell conditions are enforced at the same time. In order to isolate these two parts, let
R_{0}>0
be such that
B_{R_{0}}^{N}
contains the whole coincidence set, and choose
R\geq 3R_{0}
once and for all.
Outside
B_{3R_{0}}^{N+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}
, the problem is a standard semilinear equation with Wentzell boundary conditions, so that
u
enjoys
C^{2,\alpha}
bounds that are uniform in
R
.

To deal with the remaining part, we could use the property
uf^{\prime}(u)+f(u)\geq 0
for a quick proof. The proof that we are going to give, while being slightly longer, shows that this particular property is not needed here. Let us first recall some basic facts about the first eigenvalue. If
\mathcal{O}
is a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}
with trace
\mathcal{O}_{0}
on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, contained in
B_{2R_{0}}
, and such that
\mathcal{C}_{u}
is contained in
\mathcal{O}_{0}
, at nonzero distance from
\partial\mathcal{O}_{0}
. Consider the eigenvalue problem

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta u=&\mu u\quad((x,y)\in\mathcal{O})\\
-\Delta_{x}u+u_{y}=&\mu u\quad(x\in\mathcal{O}_{0},y=0)\\
\\
u(x,y)=&0\quad((x,y)\in\mathcal{O},y<0)\\
u(x,0)=&0\quad(x\in\partial\mathcal{O}_{0}).\end{array}\right.

From Krein-Rutman’s theorem there is a smallest eigenvalue, called
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{O})
, associated to a positive eigenfunction
\Phi_{1}(x,y)
, and characterised as the minimum of the Rayleigh type quotient

\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}|\nabla u(x,0)|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\biggl(|\nabla u|^{2}+u^{2}\biggl)dxdy}}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{0})}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}},

taken on all functions
u\in H^{1}(\mathcal{O})
,whose trace on
\partial\mathcal{O}\cap\{y<0\}
is zero, and such that
u(.,0)\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O}_{0})
. Let us define the width of
\mathcal{O}
as the smallest

such that there is a
point
(x,-l)
in
\mathcal{O}
, one easily deduces from the Poincaré inequality that
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{O})
goes to infinity as the width of
\mathcal{O}
goes to 0.

Choose
\mathcal{O}
and
l_{0}>0
such that

•

\mathcal{O}
is a smooth subdomain of
B^{N+1}_{2R_{0}}\cap(-l_{0},0)
,

•

\partial\mathcal{O}
contains
B_{4R_{0}/5}^{N}\times\{-l_{0}\}
and
\mathcal{O}_{0}
contains
B_{4R_{0}/5}^{N}
,

•

we have
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{O})\geq\|\partial_{u}\beta(u)\|_{\infty}+1
.

Choose, finally, a subdomain
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{O}
such that
\partial\mathcal{O}^{\prime}\cap\{y<0\}
is
at positive distance from
\partial\mathcal{O}\cap\{y<0\}
, and such that, if
\mathcal{O}_{0}^{\prime}
is the trace of
\mathcal{O}_{0}
on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then

\partial\mathcal{O}_{0}^{\prime}
is at a positive distance from
\partial\mathcal{O}_{0}
.

Pick any direction
e\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let us bound
\partial_{e}u
. As mentionned before, it is sufficient to do it in
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
. If
\Phi_{1}
is the eigenfunction associated to

, then

on

, as well as on

, as soon as

is large enough, in any case larger than
\|\nabla\phi\|_{\infty}
. Now,

Since
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{O}^{\prime})>\mu_{1}(\mathcal{O})\geq\|\partial_{u}\beta(u)\|_{\infty}+1
, the maximum principle holds and we have
|v|\leq C|\Phi_{1}|
. The same
argument applies to

, where

is another direction of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We have indeed

-\Delta w+\partial_{u}\beta(u)w=\partial_{uu}\beta(u)\partial_{e}u\partial_{e^{\prime}}u=O(1).

It remains to bound

. We denote it by

, and notice that

is bounded on the plane outside
\mathcal{C}_{u}
, while

is bounded on

, as it is equal to

. Consider

and note that this time it vanishes on

, while still being bounded outside. The preceding argument may be applied to
v
, this time to the Laplacian in
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
, still with Dirichlet conditions on

, but with Neumann conditions on
\mathcal{O}_{0}^{\prime}
.

\Box

As a consequence of the equation
-\Delta u+u_{y}=0
, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there is a uniform

on
B^{N}_{R}
for
u(.,0)
, for all
u\in S_{\lambda,R}
. This bound is in turn translated into a uniform

bound in
B_{R}^{N+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{N+1}_{-}
. Once this is at hand, we go to the main qualitative property.

Lemma 2.2

There is
\delta>0
, independent of
\lambda
and

, such that we have, for all solution

of (
2.3
),
for all
x
outside
\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ \varphi^{+}
, and all unit vector

:

(2.6)

\hbox{If $\displaystyle{\biggl|\frac{x}{|x|}-e\biggl|}\leq\delta$, then}\ \partial_{e}u(x,0)\leq 0.

\partial_{y}u\geq\delta.

Proof.
This is really where we need

. Let us first check (

) in the most standard way, by proving that

3
Construction of a solution to (
1.1
): letting
\varepsilon\to 0

Before starting the approximation process, let us define an auxiliary solution that will help us in proving that the free boundary on the plane actually exists. Let
\rho_{0}>0
be such that

B_{\rho_{0}}^{N}
contains
\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ \varphi^{+}
, and let
\bar{\varphi}
be a spherically symmetric function, equal to
\varphi(0)
on
B_{\rho_{0}}^{N}
, and strictly concave outside
\overline{B}_{\rho_{0}}^{N}
. Call
\bar{u}_{\Lambda}(x,y)
a solution of (
2.1
) with
\varphi
replaced by
\Lambda\bar{\varphi}
, with
\Lambda>1
. We claim that
\bar{u}_{\Lambda}
is axially symmetric and, if
\Lambda>1
is large enough, that
\bar{u}_{\Lambda}>u
. This is once again done by the usual sliding type argument, as
u
may exceed
\bar{u}_{\Lambda}
only when it is close to 0, that is, at infinity. However, the usual maximum principle applies to
\bar{u}_{\Lambda}-u
in this area. Decreasing back
\Lambda
to 1, we see that
\bar{u}_{1}>u
. This function
\bar{u}_{1}
will be called
\bar{u}
, until further notice.

\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta U+1=&0\quad(x\in\Omega^{0})\\
U(x)=&0\quad(x\in\Lambda^{0}_{0})\\
U(x)=&\delta_{0}\quad(x\in\Lambda^{+}_{0})\end{array}

If
\delta_{0}=0
we have
U<0
in
\Omega^{0}
, a contradiction. And so, by continuity, the positivity of
U
cannot be preserved if
\delta_{0}
is below a universal constant. This proves the claim.
\Box

5
Hölder continuity of the vertical derivative up to
\partial\Omega

The task is made easier by the Lipschitz character of
\partial\Omega
, as well as the fact that the free boundary
\Gamma
below, that we recall to be a graph
\{y=\psi(x)\}
, is a
C^{\infty}
surface, until it meets the plane. This will yield an easy upper bound for
u_{y}(x,0)-1
as
x
approaches
\partial\Omega
. A lower bound will be accessible only in an integral sense, as
x
approaches
\partial\Omega
. Scaling around the points that are well-behaved for
u_{y}
will entail the Hölder continuity of
u_{y}
.

Lemma 5.1

There is
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
C>0
universal such that, for all
x\in\Omega
we have

(5.1)

u_{y}(x,0)\leq 1+C|x|^{\alpha}.

Proof.
This lemma involves the specificity of the problem only in a relatively weak manner. Set
v=u_{y}
, we use once again the Wentzell condition that entails
v_{y}(x,0)\leq 0
in
\Omega
. Let
\Omega_{1}
be a smooth open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

\mathcal{C}_{u}\subset\Omega_{1}\subset\overline{\Omega}_{1}\subset\Omega.

For
M>0
to be large, let
v_{M}
be the unique solution of the linear problem

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta v_{M}=&0\quad(x\in\Omega\backslash\Omega_{1},y\in\mathbb{R})\\
v_{M}(x,y)=&1\quad(x\in\partial\Omega,y\in\mathbb{R})\\
v_{M}(x,y)=&M\quad(x\in\partial\Omega_{1},y\in\mathbb{R}).\end{array}\right.

We claim that
v_{M}\geq v
as soon as we have

(5.3)

M>1+\|u_{y}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{1}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}\cap\{u>0\})}.

Notice first that
\{u>0\}\subset\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
, so that it suffices to compare
v
and
v_{M}
on
\Gamma
and
\Omega\backslash\Omega_{1}
. As
v_{M}
is even in
y
, we have

v_{y}(x,0)\leq\partial_{y}v_{M}(x,0)=0\quad\hbox{for $x\in\Omega\backslash\Omega_{1}$.}

Then, note that
v_{M}\geq 1
in its domain of definition, so that

v(x,y)\leq 1\leq v_{M}(x,y)\quad\hbox{for $(x,y)\in\Gamma\cap\biggl((\Omega\backslash\Omega_{1})\times\mathbb{R}_{-}\biggl)$}.

Finally, (
5.3
) implies that
v\leq v_{M}
on
\{u>0\}\cap\partial\Omega_{1}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}
. Therefore, the claim
v\leq v_{M}
follows from the maximum principle, and so does the lemma: as
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz,
v_{M}
satisfies (
5.1
) for
C
and
\alpha
universal.
\Box

Let us turn to the lower bound. For
x\in\Omega
close to
\partial\Omega
let us set
h=|x|
.

Lemma 5.2

There is
\lambda\in(0,1]
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
C>0
universal such that

(5.4)

\frac{1}{|B_{\lambda h}^{N}|}\int_{B_{\lambda h}^{N}(x)}|u_{y}(x,0)-1|dx\leq Ch^{\alpha}.

Proof.
Once again because
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz we may choose
\lambda>0
universal such that

B_{\lambda h}^{N}(x)\subset\{x^{\prime}\in\Omega:\ d(x^{\prime},\partial\Omega)\geq\frac{h}{2}\}.

For
q>0
let
\Sigma_{q}
be the piece of cylinder

\Sigma_{q}=\{u>0\}\cap\biggl(B_{k}^{N}(x)\times\mathbb{R}_{-}\biggl),

and set

\Gamma_{q}=\Gamma\cap\Sigma_{q}.

From Theorem
4.2
, the set
\Sigma_{\lambda h}(x)
is included in a cylinder with height of the order no less, and no more than
h^{2}
. Integrate the equation for
u
in it.
We have

\int_{B_{\lambda h}^{N}(x)}u_{y}(x^{\prime},0)dx^{\prime}=|\Gamma_{\lambda h}(x)|+O(h^{N+1}).

As we have

|\Gamma_{\lambda h}(x)|\geq|B_{\lambda h}^{N}(x)|=|B_{\lambda h}^{N}|,

we have

\frac{1}{|B_{\lambda h}^{N}|}\int_{B_{\lambda h}^{N}(x)}\biggl(u_{y}(x^{\prime},0)-1\biggl)dx^{\prime}\geq Ch,

an estimate that, combined to Lemma
5.1
and (
5.1
), implies our lemma.
\Box

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.
The issue is now to translate Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
into an inequality for the Hölder quotients of
u
at scales ranging from
h
to much smaller than
h
. Assume again that
u
is translated and rotated conveniently so that
0\in\partial\Omega
. Consider
h>0
small. The preceding lemmas, together with Markov’s inequality, imply that

(5.5)

\frac{\bigl|\{|u_{y}-1|\geq h^{\alpha/2}\}\bigl|}{|B_{h}^{N}|}\leq Ch^{\alpha/2}.

We cover
B_{h}^{N}
with balls of radius
h^{1+\alpha/N}
, inequality (
5.1
) guarantees that, if
B_{h^{1+\alpha/N}}^{N}(x_{i})
is one of these balls, then there is
\tilde{x}_{i}\in B_{h^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2N}}}^{N}(x_{i})
such that

(5.6)

u_{y}(\tilde{x}_{i},0)=1+O(h^{\alpha/2}).

Consider such a
\tilde{x}_{i}
and rescale
u
around
\tilde{x}_{i}
:

(5.7)

{u\biggl((\tilde{x}_{i},0)+h^{2}(\xi,\zeta)\biggl)}={h^{2}}v_{h}(\xi,\zeta),

where we have dropped the index
i
for commodity, all the more as the upcoming considerations will not depend on
i
. The situation is now the following:
the function
v(\xi,0)
is bounded, from optimal regularity, in
B_{\alpha/N-1}^{N}
, while
D^{2}_{\xi}v(\xi,0)
is
O(h^{2})
uniformly in
\xi\in B_{\alpha/N-1}^{N}
. By interpolation,

\nabla_{\xi}v(\xi,0)
is
O(h)
uniformly in
\xi\in B_{\alpha/N-1}^{N}
. From Theorem
4.3
, we have, for some
M>0
:

v_{h}(\xi,\zeta)=(M_{h}+\zeta)^{+}o_{h\to 0}(1).

the constant
M_{h}
being universally bounded from above and below. So, we may write

v_{h}(\xi,\zeta)=\biggl(M_{h}+\zeta+w_{h}(\xi,\zeta)\biggl)^{+},

the (rescaled) free boundary
\Gamma_{h}
being a graph of the form
\{\zeta=-M_{h}+\varphi_{h}(\xi)\}
, the function
\varphi_{h}
tending to 0 uniformly in
B_{\alpha/N-1}^{N}
. And so, everything boils down to showing that these
o_{h\to 0}(1)
are actually (small) powers of
h
. A first step towards this is to prove that the quantity

N_{h}:=\frac{w_{h}(0,0)}{h^{\frac{\alpha}{2N}}}

is bounded, that is,
v_{h}(0,\zeta)
is
h^{\frac{\alpha}{2N}}
-close to
(M_{h}+\zeta)^{+}
. Assume this is not true; because
\nabla_{x}w_{h}=O(h)
we have
v_{h}(\xi,0)\sim h^{\frac{\alpha}{2N}}N_{h}
in
B^{N}_{h^{\alpha/N-1}}
. As
h\to 0
we have, up to a subsequence,

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{w_{h}}{h^{\frac{\alpha}{2N}}N_{h}}=\bar{w}(\xi,\zeta)

at least in the
C^{1}
sense, the function
\bar{w}
being harmonic in the strip
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[-M,0]
(
M
is a limit of
(M_{h})_{h}
) with
\bar{w}(\xi,0)\equiv 1
and
\bar{w}(\xi,-M)\equiv 0
. Thus we have
\bar{w}_{\zeta}(0,0)>0
, something that contradicts (
5.7
). So,
(N_{h})_{h}
is bounded, so that
w_{h}
is uniformly of order
h^{\frac{\alpha}{2N}}
in
B^{N}_{h^{\alpha/N-1}}
.

The second step towards an estimate of
\partial_{y}u
is to write
\partial_{y}w_{h}
in integral form. Let
P_{h}(\xi,\zeta,\xi^{\prime})
be the Poisson kernel of the Laplacian in the positivity domain of
u
, remembering that it decays exponentially fast, uniformly in
h
, as
|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|
grows (because the free boundary is bounded and bounded away from the plane), and that its derivative
\partial_{y}P_{h}
has a singularity of the form
\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|^{N+1}}
uniformly in
h
we have

\partial_{y}w_{h}(\xi,0)\sim\int_{B_{h^{\alpha/N-1}}^{N}}\partial_{y}P_{h}(\xi,0,\xi^{\prime})(w_{h}(\xi^{\prime},0)-w_{h}(\xi,0)d\xi^{\prime},

which entails that
\partial_{y}w_{h}(\xi,0)
is of order
h^{\frac{\alpha}{2N}}
. It is also Hölder continuous, its Hölder quotient of order
\beta
(
\beta<1
) being controlled by
h^{\frac{\alpha}{2N}}
. Scaling back, this proves the Hölder continuity of
u
with exponent
\beta<\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2N}
.
\Box
