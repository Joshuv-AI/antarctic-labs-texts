On the nonconnectedness of moduli spaces of arrangements, II: construction of nonarithmetic pairs
Benoît Guerville-Ballé

Abstract
. Constructing lattice isomorphic line arrangements that are not lattice isotopic is a complex yet fundamental task. In this paper, we focus on such pairs but which are not Galois conjugated, referred to as nonarithmetic pairs. Splitting polygons have been introduced by the author to facilitate the construction of lattice isomorphic arrangements that are not lattice isotopic. Exploiting this structure, we develop two algorithms which produce nonarithmetic pairs: the first generates pairs over a number field, while the second yields pairs over the rationals. Moreover, explicit applications of these algorithms are presented, including one complex, one real, and one rational nonarithmetic pair.

1.
Introduction

The concept of moduli space plays an important role in various branches of mathematics, serving as a fundamental framework for understanding families of geometric objects. Moduli spaces provide a structured way to classify objects up to isomorphism, capturing the intrinsic parameters that define their equivalence classes. Understanding their structure and behavior is crucial, not only from the theoretical persepective but also for applications in algebraic geometry, topology, or mathematical physics. However, the study of moduli spaces is a challenging task due to their complex nature. Indeed, Mnëv Universality Theorem
[
16
]
and its numerous extensions by Vakil
[
23
]
, imply that, in lots of cases, moduli spaces can behave as badly as one can imagine.

The
moduli space

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
of a line arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{2}
can be defined as the set of all arrangements that are lattice isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
, quotiented by the natural action of
\PGL_{3}(\mathbb{C})
. MacLane
[
15
]
showed that such moduli spaces are not necessarily path-connected, while Mnëv
[
16
]
proved that any singularity defined over
\mathbb{Z}
appears in at least one moduli space, revealing the complex nature mentioned above. Randell’s Isotopy Theorem
[
18
]
states that two path-connected arrangements are topologically equivalent, illustrating the importance of understanding the topology of such moduli spaces. The moduli spaces of line arrangements with few lines have been classified from different perspectives. The works of Nazir and Yoshinaga
[
17
]
and of Fei
[
24
]
classify the topological types of arrangements up to 9 lines. Recently, Corey and Luber classified the diffeomorphic types of line arrangements with up to
12

[

9
]
. The interplay between connectedness and combinatorics has been studied by the author and Viu-Sos in
[

. The present paper is a continuation of these studies.

1.1.
Arithmetic, nonarithemtic and rational pairs

Our study focuses on the connected components of the moduli spaces, so we can assume that any arrangement is defined over a number field. Indeed, due to the algebraic nature of the moduli space of a fixed arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, any arrangement in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is lattice isotopic to an arrangement defined over a number field. The number field generated by all the coefficients of the lines of
\mathcal{A}
is called the
definition field
of
\mathcal{A}
and is denoted by
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})
. Throughout this paper, a
pair
of arrangements always refers to lattice isomorphic arrangements which are not isotopy equivalent. In other words, they are in different connected components of their shared moduli space.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
be a pair of arrangements, and let
\mathbb{F}
be a number field that contains both
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
and
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
.

•

The pair is
arithmetic
if there exists a Galois automorphism
\sigma\in\Gal(\mathbb{F}/\mathbb{Q})
such that
\sigma\cdot\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{2}
. It is
nonarithmetic
otherwise.

•

The pair is
rational
if
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A}_{2})=\mathbb{Q}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Obviously, a rational pair is a nonarithmetic pair.

Example 1.3
.

The MacLane arrangements

(3)

1.2.
Arithmetic pairs

Due to the algebraic nature of the moduli space, arithmetic pairs are simple to construct, e.g.
[
15
,
17
,
2
,
12
]
. Furthermore, they are a particular case of conjugate varieties. It is known that such varieties can have different topologies, see
[
1

22
]
for the general case and
[
2
]
for line arrangements –see also
[

6

for numerous other examples. Nevertheless, both the topology and the geometry of such pairs are difficult to distinguish. Indeed, any algebraic topological invariant will fail to distinguish a topological difference. In particular, the profinite completion of the fundamental group of their complements are isomorphic. Similarly, the geometric line operator
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}}
introduced by Rouleau in
[
19
,
20
]
cannot distinguish a difference of geometry in such pairs.

1.3.
Nonarithmetic pairs

There is a wide class of problems in the study of arrangements and, more generally, of plane curves, asking whether a given property --be it geometric, topological, or otherwise-- is determined by the local type of the singularities or not. To answer negatively such a question, the most efficient way is to provide an explicit counterexample. When considering two arrangements, the more different they are, the more likely they are to form a counterexample. From this observation and according to the previous paragraph, it is natural, in a first place, to consider arrangements that form a nonarithmetic pair. It is not a coincidence if the first examples of Zariski pair

2.2.
The polynomial
\Delta_{\Psi}

The existence of such splitting polygons and nonsplitting polygon is related with the degree and the roots of a polynomial
\Delta_{\Psi}
ensuring the realization or not of condition (1) in Definition
2.4
. This polynomial can be constructed as follows.

Let
Q_{1}^{\lambda}
be a generic point of the line
S_{1}
which is different from
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
. It can be linearly parametrized by a unique parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. By Definition
2.1
,
S_{i}\notin P_{i}
, so one can define
E_{1}^{\lambda}
as the line passing through
Q_{1}^{\lambda}
and
P_{1}
. We denote by
Q_{2}^{\lambda}
the intersection point of
E_{1}^{\lambda}
and
S_{2}
. This point is well defined due to the condition
S_{i+1}\notin P_{i}
in Definition
2.1
. Similarly, we construct iteratively the lines
E_{i}^{\lambda}
and the points
Q_{i+1}^{\lambda}
, for
i\in\{2,\dots,r\}
. We denote by
\Delta_{\Psi}
the determinant of the
3\times 3
matrix formed by the coefficients of lines
S_{1}
,
E_{1}^{\lambda}
and
E_{r}^{\lambda}
. If
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})
is a definition field of
\mathcal{A}
, then
\Delta_{\Psi}
is an element of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})[\lambda]
. By construction,
\Delta_{\Psi}
is of degree at most
2
.

Assume that
\Delta_{\Psi}
is of degree
2
. If it is also irreducible in
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})[\lambda]
, then all the conditions of Theorem
2.5
are verified, see
[
10
, Theorem 2.8]
. Such splitting polygons will be called
irreducible splitting polygons
and
reduced splitting polygons
otherwise. In such situation, the created pair will be an arithmetic one. To create a nonarithmetic pair, we will search for reduced splitting polygons. It is worth to notice that the condition “
\Delta_{\Psi}
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})
” is necessary but not sufficient to create nonarithemtic pair. Indeed, in that case, one also needs to verify Condition 3 of Definition
2.4

Lemma 2.6
.

If the polynomial
\Delta_{\Psi}
is reducible then there exists a nonsplitting polygon on
\Psi
.

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{\Psi}
is of degree at most
2
, the hypothesis that it is reducible induces that it is of degree exactly
2
. The conditions for the tuple
E^{\lambda_{0}}
to form a nonsplitting polygon are:
\Delta_{\Psi}(\lambda_{0})\neq 0
and all the other intersections are generics. The first condition is Zariski open, while the second kind are linear Zariski-closed conditions. Indeed these conditions correspond to a line, linearly parametrized by
\lambda
, that passes through a point independent of
\lambda
. Since the intersection of an Zariski-open subset with finitely many proper Zariski-closed subsets is not empty then such
\lambda_{0}
exists.
∎

2.3.
The algorithms

The first example that comes in mind when we talk about nonarithmetic pairs is the Rybnikov arrangements. We have shown in
[
10
, Section. 4.1]
that they can be constructed by adding successively two splitting triangles such that the first one is irreducible and the second one is reducible. Taking inspiration from this construction, nonarithmetic pairs can be constructed using the following algorithm.

Algorithm 2.7
.

Construction of nonarithmetic pair over a number field.

(1)

Consider a rigid arrangement
\mathcal{A}
with definition field
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})
(eventually
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{Q}
).

(2)

Enumerate all the plinths of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
with a fixed length.

(3)

For each plinth
\Psi_{1}
of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})
: compute the polynomial
\Delta_{\Psi_{1}}
and determine its irreducibility in
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})[\lambda]
.

(4)

If the polynomial
\Delta_{\Psi_{1}}
is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A})
, then construct the arithmetic pair
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi_{1}}^{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\Psi_{1}}^{2}
by adding to
\mathcal{A}
a splitting polygon on the plinth
\Psi_{1}
.

(5)

Enumerate all the plinths of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}^{1}_{\Psi_{1}})=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}^{2}_{\Psi_{1}})
with a fixed lenght.

(6)

Fix
i\in\{1,2\}
and for each plinth
\Psi_{2}
on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A}^{i}_{\Psi_{1}})
: compute the polynomial
\Delta_{\Psi_{2}}
and determine its irreducibility in
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A}^{i}_{\Psi_{1}})[\lambda]
.

(7)

If the polynomial
\Delta_{\Psi_{2}}
is reducible in
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{A}_{\Psi_{1}}^{i})[\lambda]
, verify if, for each roots
\lambda_{j}
of
\Delta_{\Psi_{2}}

E^{\lambda_{j}}=(E_{1}^{\lambda_{j}},\dots,E_{r}^{\lambda_{j}})
form a splitting polygon on the plinth
\Psi_{2}
.

(8)

The arrangements

\mathcal{A}_{\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}}^{i,2}

form a nonarithmetic pair.

Proof.

.

To construct a real nonarithmetic pair, we use the Falk-Sturmfels arrangements
\mathcal{FS}_{1}
and
\mathcal{FS}_{2}

\phi=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{5}}{2}

\begin{array}[]{lll}L_{1}:z=0,&L_{2}:x=0,&L_{3}:x-z=0,\\

L_{7}:x+\phi y-z=0,&L_{8}:\phi x-\phi y+z=0,&L_{9}:-\phi x+(\phi-1)y=0.\end{array}

By
[
10

\Delta_{\Psi_{1}}

2.7
are already done. Similarly to the previous example, there is no reduced plinth on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{FS}_{1})

2.9
, we can add a line
L_{10}
through the singular points
L_{1}\cap L_{2}\cap L_{3}
and
L_{6}\cap L_{7}

L_{10}:x-\phi z=0

We consider the plinth
\Psi_{2}
given by the support
(L_{1},L_{2},L_{5})
and pivot points
(L_{5}\cap L_{7},L_{4}\cap L_{10},L_{4}\cap L_{8})

\Delta_{\Psi_{2}}

(-\phi-2)(\lambda+\phi)(\lambda-\phi+2)
, and the associated splitting polygons are:

\displaystyle L^{1}_{11}:(\phi+2)x-(\phi+1)y+\phi z=0,\quad L^{1}_{12}:(\phi-1)x-\phi y+(2\phi-1)z=0,

\displaystyle L^{1}_{13}:(-2\phi+1)x+(-3\phi+2)y+(\phi-1)z=0,

and

\displaystyle L^{2}_{11}:(\phi+2)x-(\phi+3)y+(\phi+2)z=0,\quad L^{2}_{12}:-(\phi+1)x+(\phi-2)y+z=0,

Using Algorithm
2.7
, one can deduce the next theorem.

To complete our set of examples, we now apply Algorithm
2.10

\mathcal{A}
defined by the lines

\begin{array}[]{lll}L_{1}:2y-z=0,&L_{2}:x-y=0,&L_{3}:x+y-z=0,\\

L_{7}:2x+6y-5z=0,&L_{8}:y=0,&L_{9}:y-z=0,\\
L_{10}:z=0.\end{array}

Let
\Psi
be the plinth with support
(L_{1},L_{2},L_{7})
and pivot points
(L_{3}\cap L_{6}\cap L_{8},L_{1}\cap L_{8}\cap L_{9}\cap L_{10},L_{5}\cap L_{6})

(-6)(\lambda-5/2)(\lambda-2/3)

2.10

4.
Discussion

4.1.
The algorithms

The algorithms presented in this paper have proven to be effective, as demonstrated by the numerous examples of nonarithmetic pairs generated. However, there are limitations that warrant further investigation. One significant area for improvement is the generation of plinths. Currently, the process enumerates all possible plinths using a force brut approach, which can be computationally intensive for arrangements with a high number of lines. Taking advantage of the symmetries of the arrangement could streamline this process, reducing redundancy and improving efficiency. Additionally, the question arises whether there exists combinatorial conditions on plinths that consistently produce nonarithmetic pairs. Identifying such conditions could not only enhance our understanding of the underlying geometric structures but also lead to more targeted and efficient algorithms for generating nonarithmetic pairs.

4.2.
Nonarithmetic pairs with at most
12
lines

The Rybnikov arrangements and the rational Zariski pairs given in
[
13
]
each consist of at least
13
lines. Notably, all the examples produced in this paper also contain
13
lines. In our process, it appeared to be necessary to add some lines due to the absence of a reduced plinth. Consequently, we were unable to construct nonarithmetic pairs with fewer than
13
lines. This observation naturally leads to the following problem.

Problem 4.1
.

Construct a pair with at most
12
lines which is not lattice isotopic to a nonarithmetic pair or prove that such a pair does not exist.

4.3.
Topology of the examples

In
[
10
]
, the author constructed several arithmetic pairs by successively applying two splitting triangles, akin to Algorithm
2.7
. Some of these pairs form Zariski pairs with nonisomorphic fundamental groups. In contrast, the topology of all examples constructed in the present paper cannot be distinguished using the truncated Alexander test
[
3
,
4
]
. The author also examined several other topological invariants, such as the loop-linking numbers
[
6
,
12
]
, the torsion of the lower central series factors
[
5
]
, and the torsion in the first Chen groups. None of these tests could demonstrate that the examples have nonhomeomorphic embedded topology.

Problem 4.2
.

Determine if the examples presented in this paper have nonhomeomorphic embedded topology.
