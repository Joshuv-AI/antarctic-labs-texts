Abstract
. We prove that for a domain Ω ⊂ ℝ n \Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n} , being ( ϵ , δ ) {(\epsilon,\delta)} in the sense of Jones is equivalent to being an extension domain for bmo {\rm bmo} , the nonhonomogeneous version of the space of function of bounded mean oscillation on Ω \Omega . In the process we identify ( ϵ , δ ) {(\epsilon,\delta)} -domains with a local version of uniform domains, defined by requiring the presence of length cigars between nearby points. Our results show that the definition of bmo ⁡ ( Ω ) {\rm bmo}(\Omega) is closely tied to the geometry of the domain.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a domain (open connected set) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and consider a space
X
of functions on
\Omega
. We are interested in the relationship between the geometry of the domain
\Omega
and the existence of a bounded extension operator
T:X(\Omega)\to X({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Can we characterize the domain in terms of such extensions, namely give conditions on
\Omega
which are necessary and sufficient for the existence of the operator
T
? If such a map exists, we say that the domain is an
extension domain
for the given function space
X
. We refer to
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
for a comprehensive account of extension problems for different function spaces
X
.

Although extension domains have been widely studied, the problem of their complete characterization is still open in general, even for the classical Sobolev spaces
W^{s,p}
(see however
[
24
]
). When
X={\rm BMO}
, a complete description of the extension domains has been given by Jones
[
18
]
. The space
{\rm BMO}(\Omega)
consists of functions
f
which are locally integrable in
\Omega
and have bounded mean oscillation in
\Omega
, that is,

(1)

\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}:=\sup_{Q\subset\Omega}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx<\infty,

where here and in what follows
Q
denotes a cube with sides parallel to the axes,
|Q|
is its measure and
f_{Q}=\fint_{Q}f:=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}fdx
is the mean of
f
over
Q
. The seminorm
\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}
defines a norm modulo constants.

Jones gave a geometric condition on the domain which was necessary and sufficient for the existence of a bounded extension map from
{\rm BMO}(\Omega)
to
{\rm BMO}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. This condition was shown in
[
15
]
to be equivalent to
\Omega
being a
uniform domain
, previously defined in
[
21
]
(see also
[
26
]
). In recent work of the authors
[
8
]
, uniform domains are identified as extension domains for the space
{\rm VMO}
of functions of vanishing mean oscillation.

In
[
19
]
, Jones introduced local versions of uniform domains called
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domains (uniform domains are
(\epsilon,\infty)
).
He showed that such domains are extension domains for the Sobolev spaces
W^{s,p}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
s\in\mathbb{N}
and this result is sharp in dimension
2
: if a finitely connected open set is an extension domain for all Sobolev spaces, then it is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
domain (this fails in higher dimensions - for recent results see
[
13
]
). Christ in
[
9
]
extended Jones’ results to certain spaces of fractional smoothness which had been simultaneously studied by DeVore and Sharpley in
[
11
]
and later in
[
12
]
; there followed many further extensions and variations - for some recent examples see
[
3
,
20
]
.

From the results of Jones and Christ it follows that uniform domains are extension domains for the homogeneous Sobolev spaces, and
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domains for the nonhomogeneous ones. BMO can be considered as the zero smoothness endpoint of the homogeneous case. The natural question then arises in the nonhomogeneous case: does the extension result for
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domains hold with zero smoothness, and what is the corresponding nonhomogeneous BMO space?

Since the converse direction for Sobolev extensions only holds when the dimension is
2
, one can also ask whether there is a space for which the existence of an extension map implies that
\Omega
is an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain in
any
dimension.

We answer both these questions by identifying
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domains as the extension domains for
{\rm bmo}
, known alternatively as
local, localized
or
nonhomogeneous
BMO. The space
{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
was introduced by Goldberg
[
16
]
and consists of locally integrable functions
f
satisfying

\|f\|_{{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}:=\sup_{\ell(Q)<1}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx+\sup_{\ell(Q)\geq 1}|f|_{Q}<\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all cubes
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
\ell(Q)
denotes the sidelength of
Q
.
Replacing Goldberg’s choice of the constant
1
in the definition by a finite constant
\lambda
gives the same space with equivalent norms, and
{\rm BMO}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
can be considered as the case
\lambda=\infty
. While the norm in
{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is not taken modulo constants, considered as sets of functions,
{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a proper subset of
{\rm BMO}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
; for example,
\log|x|\in{\rm BMO}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\setminus{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

On a bounded domain
\Omega
,
{\rm bmo}(\Omega)
is just
{\rm BMO}(\Omega)
equipped with a nonhomogeneous norm, adding to (
1
) the supremum of the averages of the function over large cubes or balls contained in
\Omega
, or alternatively the
L^{1}(\Omega)
norm of the function, and corresponds to the zero smoothness case in the scale of nonhomogeneous Morrey-Campanato spaces (see Triebel
[
25
]
, Section 1.7.2). However, when
\Omega
is unbounded, the choice of constant, or
scale
, in the definition of
{\rm bmo}(\Omega)
is important.

Fix
\lambda>0
. We say
f\in{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
if
f
is integrable on every cube
Q\subset\Omega
and

(2)

\|f\|_{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}:=\sup_{Q\subset\Omega,\ell(Q)<\lambda}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx+\sup_{Q\subset\Omega,\ell(Q)\geq\lambda}|f|_{Q}<\infty.

If the domain does not contain any cube of sidelength
\lambda
or larger, we have
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}={{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}
with the same norms, namely we are back in the homogeneous case. Thus we need to choose
\lambda
sufficiently small for a cube of sidelength
\lambda
to be contained in
\Omega
, but this is not enough. For example, consider the Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
lying between the
x
-axis and the curve
y=\max(1,1-x)
. Clearly
\Omega
contains arbitrarily large cubes, but choosing
\lambda
too large, say
\lambda>1
, results in the existence of functions belonging to
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
, such as
f(x,y)=\max(x,0)
, but not having an extension to
{\rm bmo}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Thus the correct scale for the definition of
{\rm bmo}
on a domain is intimately connected with the geometry.

We now state the main result of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
\Omega
is an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain then there is a positive constant
\lambda_{0}
such that for each
\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}
,
we have a bounded linear extension operator
{T_{\lambda}}:{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}\to{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Conversely, if for some
\lambda>0
there is a bounded extension operator
{T_{\lambda}}:{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}\to{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then
\Omega
is an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain for some
\epsilon,\delta
depending on
\lambda
.

In particular, if
\Omega
is an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain, we see that for each
\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}
, the set
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
consists precisely of restrictions to
\Omega
of elements of
{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, hence all these sets are the same and we can define
{\rm bmo}(\Omega)
to be
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
with
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
.

Before proving the theorem, we identify
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domains with
locally uniform
domains, used in the sense of Herron and Koskela
[
17
]
- see Section
2.3
. The key ingredient consists of enhanced localized versions of two theorems of Gehring and Osgood
[
15
]
, relating the existence of a certain curve, or
cigar
, between two points
x,y\in\Omega
, and the comparability of the
distance-ratio metric

{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)
and the
quasihyperbolic metric

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)
, introduced by Gehring and Palka
[
14
]
. We define these terms and prove the Gehring-Osgood-type theorems in Section
2
.

Other variants of these conditions can be found in
[
1
]
, where the notion of
semi-uniform
is introduced, and in
[
2
]
, where the
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
condition is further localized. In
[
23
]
, the term
locally uniform
is added to what is just Jones’ definition of
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domains. On the other hand, the condition that is called
locally uniform
in
[
2
]
is stated in terms of what are known as
distance cigars
(as opposed to the
length cigars
in Definition
2.1
). This condition, which we called
locally distance uniform
, is immediately equivalent to
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
- see
[
26
]
, Section 2.4. The main difficulty is replacing length cigars by distance cigars in the hypothesis of Theorem 1 of
[
15
]
(Theorem
B
below).

Once the equivalence of the definitions is established, we prove, in Section
3
, the necessity of the
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
condition in Theorem
1.1
. This is stated as Theorem
3.1
, under the weaker hypothesis that there is a bounded extension operator from
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
to
{\rm BMO}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Note that while the necessity in Jones
[
18
]
is essentially a consequence of the fact that
{k_{\Omega}}\in{\rm BMO}(\Omega)
with bounded norm, the situation is more complicated in the local case as one needs to control the nonhomogeneous norm. This is done by comparing
{k_{\Omega}}
with the distance to a the set point of
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
lying away from the boundary.

The sufficiency in Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
4.1
, is proved in Section
4
. The extension to
\Omega^{c}
is defined as in
[
18
]
near the boundary, but is set to zero far away from the boundary. In order to control this jump in the
{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
norm, one needs a logarithmic (in sidelength) growth bound on the averages of functions in
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
on cubes (see Proposition
4.9
). While such a bound is standard in
{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, it is the locally uniform condition that gives it to us for functions in
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
, for sufficiently small
\lambda
. As pointed out above, this may fail even on a Lipschitz domain if the choice of
\lambda
is too large.

2.
Uniform domains

In what follows,
\Omega
will always denote a domain (open and connected set) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We denote by
d_{\Omega}(x)
the distance to the boundary, from either inside or outside the domain:

{d_{\Omega}}(x):=\textup{dist}(x,{\partial\Omega}).

For a rectifiable curve
\gamma
, we denote its arclength by
s(\gamma)
or by
|\gamma|
. If
p,q
are two points along the curve
\gamma
, we denote by
\gamma(p,q)
or
\gamma_{p,q}
the portion of the curve between
p
and
q
. We will use the notation
B(x,r)
to denote a ball of center
x
and radius
r
. Unless otherwise stated, balls and cubes are assumed to be closed, with
Q^{\circ}
denoting the interior of
Q
.

Uniform domains were defined by Martio and Sarvas in
[
21
]
as follows

Definition 2.1
.

Given
a,b\geq 1
, we say that domain
\Omega
is
(a,b)
-uniform if any
x,y\in\Omega
can be connected by a rectifiable curve
\gamma=\gamma(x,y)\subset\Omega
satisfying a quasiconvexity condition with constant
a
:

(3)

s(\gamma(x,y))\leq a|x-y|,

and a John condition with constant
b
: for all
z\in\gamma(x,y)

(4)

\min(s(\gamma(x,z)),s(\gamma(z,y)))\leq b{d_{\Omega}}(z).

We say that
\Omega
is a uniform domain if it is
(a,b)
-uniform for some choice of
a,b\geq 1
.

2.1.
Quasihyperbolic metric and Gehring-Osgood theorems

On any domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
the
quasihyperbolic metric
is defined as

(5)

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y):=\inf_{\gamma(x,y)\subset\Omega}\int_{\gamma(x,y)}\frac{ds}{\textup{dist}(z,{\partial\Omega})},\qquad x,y\in\Omega,

where the infimum is taken over all rectifiable curves connecting
x
and
y
, and
ds
is arclength. Gehring and Osgood showed (see Lemma 1 in
[
15
]
) that given any two points
x,y\in\Omega
, there is always a curve minimizing
{k_{\Omega}}
, i.e. a rectifiable curve
\gamma=\gamma(x,y)
for which

\int_{\gamma}\frac{ds}{\textup{dist}(z,{\partial\Omega})}={k_{\Omega}}(x,y).

Any such curve is called a
quasihyperbolic geodesic
between
x
and
y
.

Gehring and Palka in
[
14
]
considered another metric defined on an arbitrary domain
\Omega
, which they called the
distance-ratio metric

{j_{\Omega}}
, defined by

(6)

{j_{\Omega}}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\log\left[\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}\right)\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}\right)\right],\qquad x,y\in\Omega,

and established the estimates

(7)

\left|\log\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}\right|\leq{k_{\Omega}}(x,y),\qquad x,y\in\Omega,

and

(8)

\max\left\{\log\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}\right),\;\log\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}\right)\right\}\leq{k_{\Omega}}(x,y),\qquad\qquad x,y\in\Omega.

In other words,
{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)\leq{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)
for any
x,y\in\Omega
.

The following two theorems imply that a reverse inequality holds if and only if
\Omega
is uniform.

Theorem A

(Gehring-Osgood)
.

Let
x,y\in\Omega
and suppose
\gamma(x,y)
is a quasihyperbolic geodesic. If there exists
C\geq 1
such that for all
z,w\in\gamma(x,y)
,

(9)

{k_{\Omega}}(z,w)\leq C[1+{j_{\Omega}}(z,w)],

then
\gamma(x,y)
satisfies (
3
) and (
4
) with

a\leq 48C^{2}e^{48C^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad b\leq 24C^{2}.

Theorem B

(Gehring-Osgood)
.

Let
x,y\in\Omega
. If there exists
\gamma(x,y)\subset\Omega
such that (
3
) and (
4
) hold with some
a,b\geq 1
, then

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\leq C[1+{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)]

with
C\leq 2(b+b\log a+1)
.

2.2.
Localized Gehring-Osgood theorems

2.2.1.
Refinement of Theorem
A

Theorem
A
ensures that a quasihyperbolic geodesic curve
\gamma_{x,y}
satisfies both (
3
) and (
4
) if the quasihyperbolic metric is controlled by the distance-ratio metric uniformly along this geodesic.
We will relax the hypothesis by requiring (
9
) to hold only locally (i.e. only for
\delta
-close
z,w
). The theorem below shows that such a refined formulation is indeed possible provided that the endpoints
x,y
themselves are close enough. This additional condition on the endpoints is necessary. It can be verified e.g. by considering an infinite strip
\Omega=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1}\in\mathbb{R},|x_{2}|<1\}
.

We will also show that it suffices to control the quasihyperbolic metric by any expression of the form

\phi\left(\frac{|u-v|}{{d_{\Omega}}(u)}\right)+\phi\left(\frac{|u-v|}{{d_{\Omega}}(v)}\right),

where
\phi
is a positive increasing function growing sub-linearly. The fact that
\log
can be replaced by any sub-linear function in the characterization of uniform domains is known - see e.g.
[
27
]
Theorems 6.16, 6.17 or
[
7
]
, Theorem 3.1.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\delta>0
and
c\geq 1
be fixed. Let
\phi
be an increasing non-negative function on
[0,\infty)
such that there are numbers
d_{\phi}\geq c_{\phi}\geq c\geq 2
with

(10)

1+\phi(t)\leq\frac{t}{2c}\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq c_{\phi}

and

(11)

1+\phi(t)\leq\frac{t}{c_{\phi}e^{8c_{\phi}}}\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq d_{\phi}.

Suppose that
\gamma_{x,y}
is a quasihyperbolic geodesic satisfying the following properties:

•

For all
u,v\in\gamma_{x,y}
with
|u-v|<\delta
,

(12)

{k_{\Omega}}(u,v)\leq c\left[1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi\left(\frac{|u-v|}{{d_{\Omega}}(u)}\right)+\phi\left(\frac{|u-v|}{{d_{\Omega}}(v)}\right)\right)\right].

•

The distance between the endpoints
x,y
is

(13)

|x-y|<\delta/(10c_{\phi}).

Then

(14)

|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq a|x-y|

and

(15)

\min\{|\gamma_{x,z}|,|\gamma_{y,z}|\}\leq b{d_{\Omega}}(z)

for all
z\in\gamma_{x,y}
, with
a=8d_{\phi}
and
b=2d_{\phi}^{2}
.

Remark 2.3
.

In the special case
\phi(t)=\log(1+t)
, conditions (
10
) and (
11
) are fulfilled by
c_{\phi}=6c^{2}
and
d_{\phi}=48c^{2}e^{16c^{2}}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
{d_{\Omega}}(x)\leq{d_{\Omega}}(y)
. We consider two cases: when
{d_{\Omega}}(y)>2|x-y|
and when
{d_{\Omega}}(y)\leq 2|x-y|
.

Case 1:
{d_{\Omega}}(y)>2|x-y|

In this case for any
z
on the straight line segment
[x,y]
, we have
{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq\frac{1}{2}{d_{\Omega}}(y)
. Therefore

(16)

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\leq\int_{[x,y]}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}\leq\frac{2|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}\leq 1.

From this, (
7
) and the fact that
\gamma_{x,y}
is a quasihyperbolic geodesic, for any
z\in\gamma_{x,y}
,

e^{-1}\leq e^{-{k_{\Omega}}(z,y)}\leq\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}\leq e^{{k_{\Omega}}(z,y)}\leq e.

Hence, again applying (
16
),

|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq e{d_{\Omega}}(y)\int_{\gamma}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}=e{d_{\Omega}}(y){k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\leq 2e|x-y|

and so by the lower bound on the ratio
\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}
,

|\gamma_{z,y}|\leq|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq e{d_{\Omega}}(y)\leq e^{2}{d_{\Omega}}(z).

So, for
{d_{\Omega}}(y)>2|x-y|
, we established (
14
) and (
15
) with constants
a\geq 2e
and
b\geq e^{2}
.

Case 2:
{d_{\Omega}}(y)\leq 2|x-y|

This case is more elaborate. We put
y_{0}:=y
and define a sequence
\{{y}_{i}\}
as follows: let
y_{i}
be the point on
\gamma_{x,y}
such that
{d_{\Omega}}(y_{i})=2{d_{\Omega}}(y_{i-1})
and
|\gamma_{{y}_{i},y}|
is minimal. We fix
M
to be the largest index such that
{d_{\Omega}}({y}_{M})\leq 2|x-y|
. Next, we put
z_{0}:=x
and
\{z_{i}\}
to be the sequence defined inductively:
z_{i}
is the point on
\gamma_{x,y}
such that
{d_{\Omega}}(z_{i})=2{d_{\Omega}}(z_{i-1})
and
|\gamma_{x,z_{i}}|
is minimal. We fix the largest index
N
such that
{d_{\Omega}}(z_{N})\leq{d_{\Omega}}(y)
. Then we split the points
z_{i}
into two groups:
\{{z}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
and
\{{x}_{i}:=z_{i+N}\}_{i=0}^{M}
. In particular, we have

(17)

{d_{\Omega}}(z_{j})\leq{d_{\Omega}}(y)\leq 2|x-y|,\qquad j=0,\dots,N,

and

(18)

{d_{\Omega}}({y}_{i-1})<{d_{\Omega}}({x}_{i})\leq{d_{\Omega}}({y}_{i})\leq 2|x-y|,\qquad i=1,\dots M.

Note that by the choice of
z_{i},x_{j},y_{j}
, the subarcs
\gamma_{z_{i-1},z_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,N,

\gamma_{x_{j-1},x_{j}}
,
\gamma_{y_{j-1},y_{j}}
,
j=1,\dots,M
and
\gamma_{x_{M},y_{M}}
partition the curve
\gamma_{{x},{y}}
.

Our goal is to show the following control over these sub-arcs:

(19)

|\gamma_{z_{i-1},z_{i}}|\leq c_{\phi}{d_{\Omega}}(z_{i-1}),

(20)

{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq d_{\phi}^{-1}\cdot{d_{\Omega}}(z_{i-1})\quad\text{ for all }z\in\gamma_{z_{i-1},z_{i}},

(21)

\begin{cases}|\gamma_{x_{i-1},x_{i}}|\leq c_{\phi}{d_{\Omega}}(x_{i-1}),\\
|\gamma_{y_{i-1},y_{i}}|\leq c_{\phi}{d_{\Omega}}(y_{i-1}),\end{cases}

(22)

\begin{cases}{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq d_{\phi}^{-1}\cdot{d_{\Omega}}(x_{i-1})\quad\text{ for all }z\in\gamma_{x_{i-1},x_{i}},\\
{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq d_{\phi}^{-1}\cdot{d_{\Omega}}(y_{i-1})\quad\text{ for all }z\in\gamma_{y_{i-1},y_{i}},\end{cases}

(23)

|\gamma_{x_{M},y_{M}}|\leq d_{\phi}{d_{\Omega}}(x_{M}),

and

(24)

{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq d_{\phi}^{-1}\cdot{d_{\Omega}}(x_{M}),\quad\text{ for all }z\in\gamma_{x_{M},y_{M}}.

The theorem will be proved once we establish (
19
) - (
24
). Indeed by (
19
), (
21
), (
23
) followed by (
18
) and (
17
) we get

|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\gamma_{z_{i-1},z_{i}}|+\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\gamma_{x_{i-1},x_{i}}|+\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\gamma_{y_{i-1},y_{i}}|+|\gamma_{x_{M},y_{M}}|\leq

\leq c_{\phi}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}{d_{\Omega}}(z_{i-1})+\sum_{i=1}^{M}{d_{\Omega}}(x_{i-1})+\sum_{i=1}^{M}{d_{\Omega}}(y_{i-1})\right]+d_{\phi}{d_{\Omega}}(x_{M})\leq

\leq c_{\phi}\left[{d_{\Omega}}(z_{N})+{d_{\Omega}}(x_{M})+{d_{\Omega}}(y_{M})\right]+d_{\phi}{d_{\Omega}}(x_{M})\leq

\leq 6c_{\phi}|x-y|+2d_{\phi}|x-y|\leq 8d_{\phi}|x-y|,

which implies (
14
). Moreover, for any
z\in\gamma_{z_{i-1},z_{i}}\subset\gamma_{x,x_{0}}
, by (
19
) and (
20
) we get

|\gamma_{z_{0},z}|\leq|\gamma_{z_{0},z_{i}}|=\sum_{j=1}^{i}|\gamma_{z_{j-1},z_{j}}|\leq 2c_{\phi}{d_{\Omega}}(z_{i-1})\leq(2c_{\phi}d_{\phi}){d_{\Omega}}(z).

This immediately implies (
15
) for all
z\in\gamma_{x,z_{N}}
with
b\geq 2c_{\phi}d_{\phi}
. In the same way, (
21
) and (
22
) imply (
15
) for
z\in\gamma_{x_{0},x_{M}}\cup\gamma_{y_{0},y_{M}}
, and (
23
) and (
24
) yield (
15
) for
z\in\gamma_{x_{M},y_{M}}
with
b\geq 2(d_{\phi})^{2}
.

Therefore, it only remains to prove (
19
) - (
24
).

Estimates (
19
) and (
20
):

We prove the estimates by induction. To verify them for
i=1
, we need to note two inequalities. The first one is

(25)

It is true because either

|z_{0}-z_{1}|\geq|x-y|

Combining (

), (

\frac{|\gamma_{z_{0},z_{1}}|}{{d_{\Omega}}(z_{0})}\leq c_{\phi},

which, after being plugged back into (

where the second inequality follows from (

Combining these estimates with (

where the last inequality follows from the positivity of

and hypothesis (

This shows both (

Assume now that (

To show the last inequality, we invoke the induction hypothesis to get

(28)

\leq|x-y|+(c_{\phi}/2)\;{d_{\Omega}}(y)\leq(1+c_{\phi})|x-y|<\delta.

Then either
|z_{j-1}-z_{j}|<|z_{j-1}-y|
, and by (

) we can use hypothesis (

) for

, or
|z_{j-1}-z_{j}|\geq|z_{j-1}-y|
and again due to (

Estimates (
21
) and (
22

As the proof of these inequalities is quite similar to what we have just done above, we only outline it. Start by noting that

(29)

\frac{|\gamma_{{x}_{i-1},{x}_{i}}|}{{d_{\Omega}}({x}_{i-1})}\leq 2{k_{\Omega}}({x}_{i-1},{x}_{i}),\qquad\frac{|\gamma_{{y}_{i-1},{y}_{i}}|}{{d_{\Omega}}({y}_{i-1})}\leq 2{k_{\Omega}}({y}_{i-1},{y}_{i})

Then considering the cases

2.2.2.
Distance version of Theorem
B

The strengthening of Theorem
B
concerns the John condition (
4
). Specifically, we want to show that the same conclusion holds if we weaken hypothesis (
4
) by replacing arclength by distance.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
x,y\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
a,b\geq 1
.
If there exists
\gamma_{x,y}
, a curve in
\Omega
connecting
x
and
y
satisfying

(39)

\begin{cases}|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq a|x-y|\\
\min\{|x-z|,|y-z|\}\leq b{d_{\Omega}}(z),\quad\forall z\in\gamma_{x,y},\end{cases}

then

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\leq C_{a,b,n}\left[1+{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)\right]

with

C_{a,b,n}\leq C_{n}a^{n}b^{n},

where
C_{n}
is some constant that depends on
n
only.

Proof.

Note that by the quasiconvexity hypothesis (
3
) on
\gamma=\gamma_{x,y}
,

\gamma\subset B(x;a|x-y|)\cap B(y,a|x-y|)=:O_{x,y}.

We claim that there exists
\mathcal{A}=\{B_{i}\}
, a collection of closed balls
B_{i}
, such that
\mathcal{A}
is a cover of
O_{x,y}
,

(40)

\textup{diam}_{{k_{\Omega}}}(B_{i})\leq 2\text{ when }B_{i}\cap\gamma\neq\varnothing,

and

(41)

\#\mathcal{A}\leq C_{a,b,n}[1+{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)].

Assuming this claim, the proof is not difficult. Indeed, in this case we can find distinct
Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,

Q_{N-1}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
Q_{0}\ni x
,
Q_{N-1}\ni y
and for
i\in[0,N-2]
we have

Q_{i}\cap Q_{i+1}\cap\gamma\neq\varnothing.

Then because
Q_{i}
form a chain and
{k_{\Omega}}
is a metric,

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)=\textup{diam}_{{k_{\Omega}}}\{x,y\}\leq\textup{diam}_{{k_{\Omega}}}\Big(\bigcup_{i=0}^{N-1}Q_{i}\Big)\leq N\max_{0\leq i\leq N-1}{\textup{diam}_{{k_{\Omega}}}(Q_{i})}.

Using condition (
40
) and (
41
) we deduce

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\leq 2C_{a,b,n}[1+{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)].

So we just need to establish the claim. Our collection
\mathcal{A}
will contain the closed balls

B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2})
and
B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}{2})
.
Note that for any
z\in B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2})
, we have
{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2}
and therefore, as in (
16
),

{k_{\Omega}}(x,z)\leq\int_{[x,z]}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(v)}\leq 1.

This shows that
B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2})
satisfies (
40
). By the same argument,
B(y,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}{2})
satisfies (
40
).

To specify the remaining balls, we fix
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{12})
and construct covers of the following regions:
B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2})
,
B(y,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(y,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}{2})
and
O_{x,y}\setminus B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(y,\frac{|x-y|}{2})
.

\mathcal{R}
, a cover of
O_{x,y}\setminus B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(y,\frac{|x-y|}{2})
:

By the doubling properties of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, any ball of radius
r
can be covered by at most
C_{n}\theta^{-n}
closed balls of radii
\theta r
, where
C_{n}
depends on
n
only. Therefore we may claim that there is a collection
\mathcal{R}
of at most
C_{n}a^{n}b^{n}\theta^{-n}
balls of radii
\frac{\theta}{b}\frac{|x-y|}{2}
covering
O_{x,y}\setminus B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(y,\frac{|x-y|}{2})
. We will always assume that no ball in
\mathcal{R}
is a subset of
B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2})
nor of
B(y,\frac{|x-y|}{2})
. Hence, for any ball
B\in\mathcal{R}
of radius
r
and any point
z
in it, we have

|z-x|\geq\frac{|x-y|}{2}(1-2\theta/b),\quad\text{ and }\quad|z-y|\geq\frac{|x-y|}{2}(1-2\theta/b)

or

(42)

\min\left(\frac{|x-z|}{r},\frac{|y-z|}{r}\right)\geq\frac{b}{\theta}-2>10b.

In particular, if
B
intersects
\gamma
we can choose
z
to be a point in this intersection. Then for any
w\in B
,

{d_{\Omega}}(w)\geq{d_{\Omega}}(z)-|z-w|\geq\frac{1}{b}\min(|x-z|,|y-z|)-2r\geq 8r,

where we use the distance John condition in the second inequality and (
42
) for the last one.
This means that given
B\in\mathcal{R}
with
z\in B\cap\gamma
, we have

{k_{\Omega}}(z,w)\leq\int_{[z,w]}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(v)}\leq\frac{2r}{8r}\leq\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{ for all }w\in B,

or

\textup{diam}_{{k_{\Omega}}}(B)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

\mathcal{E}
, a cover of
B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2})
:

The above-mentioned doubling property of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
allows us to claim that there is a collection
\mathcal{E}
of no more than
C_{n}b^{n}\theta^{-n}(2+\log\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(x)})
closed balls covering
B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2})
with the following property: if
B\in\mathcal{E}
is a ball of radius
r
, then for any
z\in B

(43)

\frac{|z-x|}{r}\geq 4b.

To see this, we write

B\Big(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2}\Big)\setminus B\Big(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2}\Big)\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}B\Big(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2^{j}}\Big)\setminus B\Big(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2^{j+1}}\Big),

where
N
is the smallest integer satisfying
2^{N}\geq\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}
. Then we note that each annulus
B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2^{j}})\setminus B(x,\frac{|x-y|}{2^{j+1}})
can be covered by at most
C_{n}b^{n}\theta^{-n}
balls of radii
\frac{\theta|x-y|}{2^{j}b}
. Moreover, for any such ball
B(u,\frac{\theta|x-y|}{2^{j}b})
and for any
z
in it, we have

|z-x|\geq\frac{|x-y|}{2^{j+1}}-\frac{2\theta|x-y|}{2^{j}b}=\frac{|x-y|}{2^{j}}\left[\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2\theta}{b}\right]=\left[\frac{b}{2\theta}-2\right]r\geq 4br.

In particular, if
B\in\mathcal{E}
intersects
\gamma
at
z
and
w\in B
is arbitrary, then

{d_{\Omega}}(w)\geq{d_{\Omega}}(z)-2r\geq r\left(\frac{|x-z|}{b}-2\right)\geq 2r.

Hence

{k_{\Omega}}(w,z)\leq\int_{[z,w]}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(v)}\leq 1.

Summing up, we conclude that for any
B\in\mathcal{E}
intersecting
\gamma
we have

\textup{diam}_{{k_{\Omega}}}(B)\leq 2.

\mathcal{F}
, a cover of
B(y,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(y,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}{2})

By the same token, we may claim that there is a collection
\mathcal{F}
of no more than
C_{n}b^{n}\theta^{-n}(2+\log\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)})
closed balls covering
B(y,\frac{|x-y|}{2})\setminus B(y,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}{2})
with the following property: if
B\in\mathcal{F}
intersects
\gamma

then

\textup{diam}_{{k_{\Omega}}}(B)\leq 2.

Finally, as we verified all the balls in the collections
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{R}
satisfy (
40
) and there are no more than
C_{n}b^{n}\theta^{-n}(2+\log\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(x)})
,
C_{n}b^{n}\theta^{-n}(2+\log\frac{|x-y|}{{d_{\Omega}}(y)})
and
C_{n}a^{n}b^{n}\theta^{-n}
of them in each cover respectively, if we put
A
to be the union of
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{R}
, together with
B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(x)}{2})
and
B(x,\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(y)}{2})
, then we get a collection of closed balls satisfying both (
40
) and (
41
).

∎

2.3.
Locally uniform domains

One can give various definitions of locally uniform domains. We consider some of them below and use Theorem
2.2
and
2.4
to show their equivalence.

We start with the most natural localization of the Martio-Sarvas definition with conditions (
3
) and (
4
).

Definition 2.5

(Herron-Koskela)
.

A domain
\Omega
is a locally (length)-uniform domain if there exists
a,b\geq 1
and
\delta>0
such that for all
x,y\in\Omega
with
|x-y|<\delta
, there is
\gamma(x,y)\subset\Omega
such that (
3
) and (
4
) hold.

As a useful preliminary definition, we also consider

Definition 2.6
.

A domain
\Omega
is a locally
distance
uniform domain if there exists
a,b\geq 1
and
\delta>0
such that for all
x,y\in\Omega
with
|x-y|<\delta
, there is
\gamma(x,y)\subset\Omega
such that (
39
) holds.

Definition 2.7

(Jones
[
19
]
)
.

A domain
\Omega
is called an
(\epsilon,\delta)
domain if there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for all
x,y\in\Omega
with
|x-y|<\delta

there is a rectifiable curve
\gamma\subset\Omega
joining
x
to
y
such that

(44)

s(\gamma)\leq\epsilon^{-1}|x-y|

and

(45)

{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq\epsilon\frac{|z-x||z-y|}{|x-y|}\quad\forall z\in\gamma(x,y).

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a domain. Then the following are equivalent:

•

\Omega
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domain for some
\epsilon,\delta>0
;

•

\Omega
is a locally distance uniform domain;

•

\Omega
is a locally uniform domain.

Proof.

It is not difficult to see that the Jones’ definition is equivalent to the definition of locally distance uniform domains. More precisely (see e.g.
[
26
]
), if
\Omega
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
domain then
\Omega
is locally distance uniform with the same
\delta
and
a=1/\epsilon
,
b=2/\epsilon
. Conversely, if
\Omega
is locally distance uniform, then
\Omega
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domain with the same
\delta
and
\epsilon=1/(ab)
.

Suppose that
\Omega
is locally distance uniform. Applying Theorem
2.4
and Theorem
2.2
we see that
\Omega
is locally (length) uniform with a possibly smaller
\delta^{\prime}
. Finally, length uniform domains are distance uniform because arclength dominates distance.
∎

3.
bmo extension domains are locally uniform

In this section we will prove the necessity in Theorem
1.1
. We show it under a weaker hypothesis, in the form of the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain and suppose that
\lambda>0
,
C>0
are such that any
f\in{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
is the restriction to
\Omega
of some
F\in{\rm BMO}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with

\|F\|_{{\rm BMO}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq C\|f\|_{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}.

Then
\Omega
is a locally uniform domain.

3.1.
Three preliminary lemmas

This subsection is devoted to establishing three technical lemmas that will be used in the next subsection.
We start with the following fact that is valid in the more general setting of doubling metric measure spaces (see Lemma 18 in
[
28
]
).

Lemma 3.2
.

For any fixed
a\in\Omega
, the function
f(x)={k_{\Omega}}(a,x)
is an element of
{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}
and

\|f\|_{{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}}\leq c,

where
c
depends only on the dimension
n
.

Proof.

We will use the following result, sometimes known as a
local-to-global
property, due to Reimann and Rychener
[
22
]
(also attributed to Jones - see Theorem A1.1 and Corollary A1.1 in
[
4
]
): there is a constant
C
that depends only on the dimension
n
such that for
f\in{L^{1}_{\text{loc}}}(\Omega)
,

(46)

\|f\|_{{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}}\leq C\sup\limits_{2Q\subset\Omega}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx.

Here the supremum is taken over all cubes
Q
whose doubles
2Q
are contained in
\Omega
(the notation
cQ
denotes the concentric cube of
c
times the sidelength).
Since

\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx\leq\frac{1}{|Q|^{2}}\int_{Q}\int_{Q}|f(x)-f(y)|dxdy,

it is enough to establish that

\sup\limits_{2Q\subset\Omega}\frac{1}{|Q|^{2}}\int_{Q}\int_{Q}|{k_{\Omega}}(a,x)-{k_{\Omega}}(a,y)|dxdy\leq C^{\prime}

for some
C^{\prime}
depending at most on
n
.

Suppose
x,y\in Q,2Q\subset\Omega
. Since
{k_{\Omega}}
is a distance, the triangle inequality gives

(47)

|{k_{\Omega}}(a,x)-{k_{\Omega}}(a,y)|\leq{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\leq\int_{[x,y]}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}\leq\frac{\textup{diam}(Q)}{\textup{dist}(Q,{\partial\Omega})},

where the integral on the right is over the line segment
[x,y]
. Since
2Q\subset\Omega
means
\textup{dist}(Q,{\partial\Omega})\geq\ell(Q)/2=\textup{diam}(Q)/2\sqrt{n}
,
we get
|{k_{\Omega}}(a,x)-{k_{\Omega}}(a,y)|\leq 2\sqrt{n}
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Let
Q
be a Whitney cube of
\Omega
(see Section
4.1
). Estimate (
47
), combined with property (
59
) below, gives

\sup_{x,y\in Q}|{k_{\Omega}}(a,x)-{k_{\Omega}}(a,y)|\leq\sqrt{n}.

This is true (with a larger constant) if
x
and

are in adjacent Whitney cubes. From this we can deduce Jones’ observation, in
[
18

, that the quasihyperbolic distance
{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)
is equivalent to the length of a shortest Whitney chain between the Whitney cubes containing
x
and
y
.

The following lemma provides us with control of the
{\rm bmo}
norm by the
{\rm BMO}
norm of the function and its
L^{\infty}
norm
away from the boundary
. To make this precise, we first define what we will refer to as the
interior region
and the quasihyperbolic distance to this set.

Definition 3.4
.

Given a domain
\Omega
and
\lambda>0
we denote by
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
the set
\{x\in\Omega:{d_{\Omega}}(x)\geq\lambda/4\}
and
define, for
x\in\Omega
,

{k_{\Omega}}(x,\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}):=\inf_{p\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}}{k_{\Omega}}(x,p).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f\in{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}
,
\lambda>0
. Then

(48)

\|f\|_{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}\lesssim\sup_{2Q\subset\Omega}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx+\sup_{2Q\subset\Omega,\ell(Q)\geq\lambda/2}|f|_{Q}<\infty.

In particular, if
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda})
, then

\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda})}.

Here and below, we use the notation
u\lesssim v
when there exists a constant
c
such that
u\leq cv
.

Proof.

The first term on the right-hand-side of (
48
) controls
\|f\|_{{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}}
by (
46
). To control the averages over large cubes, let

Q\subset\Omega
be any cube such that
\ell(Q)\geq\lambda
. We take
Q_{0}
to be the cube co-centered with
Q
with
\ell(Q_{0})=\ell(Q)/2
. Then
2Q_{0}\subset Q\subset\Omega
,
and

|f|_{Q}\leq\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q_{0}}|+|f|_{Q_{0}}\leq\fint_{Q}\fint_{Q_{0}}|f(x)-f(y)|dxdy+|f|_{Q_{0}}\leq 2^{n+1}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|+|f|_{Q_{0}}.

The integral in the first term on the right-hand-side is controlled by
\|f\|_{{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}}
, and therefore, applying (
46
) again, the right-hand-side is controlled by the right-hand-side of (
48
).

Finally, note that if
2Q\subset\Omega
and
\ell(Q)\geq\lambda/2
, then
\textup{dist}(Q,{\partial\Omega})\geq\lambda/4
, i.e.
Q\subset\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
.
∎

The third lemma says that we can always talk about quasihyperbolic geodesics from the interior region
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
to the points in
\Omega\setminus\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\lambda>0
. For each
x\in\Omega\setminus\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
there exists a point
x^{\prime}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
and a curve
\gamma(x,x^{\prime})
such that

{k_{\Omega}}(x,\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda})={k_{\Omega}}(x,x^{\prime})=\int_{\gamma(x,x^{\prime})}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}.

Proof.

Take
x\in\Omega\setminus\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
. We will only show that
{k_{\Omega}}(x,\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda})={k_{\Omega}}(x,x^{\prime})
for some
x^{\prime}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
as the existence of a geodesic
\gamma(x,x^{\prime})
is proved by Lemma 1 in
[
15
]
.

First of all, note that
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
is a closed set and

{k_{\Omega}}(x,\cdot)

is a continuous function, so if
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
is bounded then

\inf\limits_{y\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}}{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)

is attained in at some point in
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
.

For unbounded
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
, fix a point
y_{0}
in
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
and set
R=\max(\lambda\,{k_{\Omega}}(x,y_{0}),|x-y_{0}|)
. We claim that

\inf_{y\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}}{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)=\inf_{y^{\prime}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}(x)}{k_{\Omega}}(x,y^{\prime}),

where
B_{R}(x)
is the closed ball centered at
x
of radius
R
. As the latter infimum is attained at some point in
\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}(x)
, all we need to finish the proof is to show that
if
y\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}
with
|x-y|>R
then

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\geq\inf_{y^{\prime}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}(x)}{k_{\Omega}}(x,y^{\prime}).

Let
\gamma(x,y)
be a quasihyperbolic geodesic between
x
and
y
. If there is a
z\in\gamma(x,y)
such that
z\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}(x)
, then

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\geq{k_{\Omega}}(x,z)\geq\inf_{y^{\prime}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}(x)}{k_{\Omega}}(x,y^{\prime}).

Otherwise,
\gamma(x,y)\cap B_{R}(x)\cap\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}=\varnothing
and by our choice of
R

{k_{\Omega}}(x,y)\geq\int_{\gamma\cap B_{R}(x)}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}\geq\frac{s(\gamma\cap B_{R}(x))}{\lambda/4}\geq\frac{R}{\lambda}\geq{k_{\Omega}}(x,y_{0})\geq\inf_{y^{\prime}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}(x)}{k_{\Omega}}(x,y^{\prime}).

∎

(49)

R_{1}+R_{2}\leq|f_{Q_{1}}-f_{Q_{2}}|+\sup_{y\in Q_{1}}|f(z_{1})-f(y)|+\sup_{y\in Q_{2}}|f(z_{2})-f(y)|\leq|f_{Q_{1}}-f_{Q_{2}}|+2\sqrt{n}.

Lemma 3.9

Let domain

Let
\Omega

(

]

{(\epsilon,\delta)}

4.1.
Whitney cubes of an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-domain

The main goal of this section is to show that all points of an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain are “close enough” to the “large enough” Whitney cubes of the domain. The qualitative statement specifying what “close enough” and “large enough” mean is the content of Proposition
4.5
below.

We recall that the Whitney decomposition of an open set
O\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a collection of closed dyadic cubes
\{Q_{I}\}
such that
O=\cup_{i}Q_{i}
and

(58)

Q^{\circ}_{i}\cap Q_{j}^{\circ}=\varnothing\text{ if }i\neq j,

(59)

1\leq\frac{\textup{dist}(Q_{j},\partial\Omega)}{\ell(Q_{j})}\leq 4\sqrt{n},

(60)

\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{\ell(Q_{j})}{\ell(Q_{i})}\leq 4\text{ if }Q_{i}\cap Q_{j}\neq\varnothing.

Cubes in the Whitney decomposition of
O
will be called
Whitney cubes
of
O
. Any two (closed) Whitney cubes
Q_{1},Q_{2}
such that

Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}\neq\varnothing
will be called
adjacent
cubes. Depending on the context, this may include the case
Q_{1}=Q_{2}
. As in
[
18
]
, we will use the term

for a finite sequence of distinct Whitney cubes with each cube adjacent to the preceding one.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\Omega
be an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain. If
Q
is a dyadic cube in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
\ell(Q)<\delta
, then there is

such that

{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq\epsilon\ell(Q)/32.

Proof.

Let

be the center of
Q
,

and consider the open annulus

A=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:r<|x-c|<2r\}\subset Q^{\circ}.

If
A\cap\Omega=\varnothing
, namely
A\subset\Omega^{\prime}
, then
A
contains points of
Q
of distance at least
r/2=\ell(Q)/16
from
{\partial\Omega}
. Thus we may assume that there is an
x\in\Omega\cap A
. Applying the same reasoning to
A\setminus B(x,2r)
, which also contains an open ball of radius
r/2
, we may assume there is a
y\in\Omega\cap A
such that

|x-y|>2r.

Since
|x-y|\leq\textup{diam}(A)=4r<\delta
, there is a curve
\gamma\subset\Omega
connecting
x
and
y
and satisfying conditions (
44
) and (
45
). Let
z
be a point on this curve such that
|x-z|=r
. Then by the choice of

,

|z-r|\leq|x-c|+|x-z|<3r<\ell(Q)/2,

which means that

. Moreover, by
(

)
and since
|y-z|\geq|y-x|-|x-z|>r
,

{d_{\Omega}}(z)\geq\frac{\epsilon|x-z||y-z|}{|x-y|}\geq\frac{\epsilon r}{4}=\frac{\epsilon\ell(Q)}{32}.

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\Omega
be an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain.
If
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a dyadic cube with
\ell(Q)<\delta
,
then there is
Q_{0}\in E\cup E^{\prime}
with
Q_{0}\supset Q
or

and

\ell(Q_{0})\ \geq\frac{\epsilon}{160\sqrt{n}}\ell(Q).

Proof.

Apply Lemma
4.3
to the given cube

to get

such that

. Since

in

containing

. As

and

are dyadic cubes with intersecting interiors, either

or

. There is nothing to prove in the former case.

In the latter case, note that (

1\leq\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}{\ell(Q_{0})}\leq\frac{\textup{dist}(Q_{0},\partial\Omega)+\textup{diam}(Q_{0})}{\ell(Q_{0})}\leq 5\sqrt{n}

and therefore

\ell(Q_{0})\geq\frac{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}{5\sqrt{n}}\geq\frac{\epsilon\ell(Q)}{160\sqrt{n}}.

∎

We can apply the lemma above to obtain the following result, which is reminiscent of the notion of

in

\Omega

, so we can replace our choice by another cube
Q_{0}
in
E^{\prime}
with sidelength in
(\epsilon\delta/(64n),\epsilon\delta/(16n))
, and take its matching cube as our desired cube
S
in
E
. Our
S
will have
\ell(S)\geq\min(\epsilon\ell(Q)/(160\sqrt{n}),\epsilon\delta/(64n))\geq\epsilon\delta/(320n)
, and since our chosen cube
Q_{0}
in
E^{\prime}
intersects our original cube
Q
, by (
57
) we will have that

\textup{dist}(S,x)\leq\textup{dist}(S,Q_{0})+\textup{diam}(Q_{0})+\textup{dist}(Q_{0},x)\leq(C_{\epsilon,n}+\sqrt{n})\ell(Q_{0})+\textup{diam}(Q)<\delta(\epsilon^{-1}+\sqrt{n}).

∎

4.2.
Averages of
{\rm BMO}
and
{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}
functions over Whitney cubes

Let us start by stating the following special case of Lemma 2.2 in
[
18
]
:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\Omega
be any domain and
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in E
be two adjacent Whitney cubes of
\Omega
. Then

|f_{Q_{1}}-f_{Q_{2}}|\lesssim\|f\|_{{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}},

for all
f\in{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}
.

Our goal in this subsection is to give a good bound on
|f_{Q_{1}}-f_{Q_{2}}|
for
f\in{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}
and non-adjacent Whitney cubes
Q_{1},Q_{2}
, using the
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
condition of
\Omega
. This is done in Corollary
4.8
. Moreover, if
f\in{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
, we will show that the averages
|f_{Q}|
themselves grow at most logarithmically as the cubes shrink. While it is not difficult to see this working on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, or on a metric measure spaces with doubling (see e.g.
[
10
]
), establishing the result in a domain requires the interplay between the geometry of the domain and the scale
\lambda
(see Proposition
4.9
).

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\Omega
be any domain and
x,y\in\Omega
be connected by a rectifiable curve
\gamma
. Let
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset E
be the Whitney cubes covering
\gamma
. Then

m\lesssim\int_{\gamma}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}+1.

In particular, if
\Omega
is an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain, then for
x,y\in\Omega
with
|x-y|<\delta
, there exists a Whitney chain
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
such that
x\in Q_{1}
,
y\in Q_{m}
and

(61)

m\leq C_{\epsilon}(1+{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)).

Proof.

Fixing
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

\int_{\gamma}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}\geq\sum_{i}\frac{s(\gamma\cap Q_{i})}{\textup{dist}(Q_{i},{\partial\Omega})}\geq\sum_{i}\frac{s(\gamma\cap Q_{i})}{\ell(Q_{i})4\sqrt{n}}\gtrsim\;\#\{i:s(\gamma\cap Q_{i})\geq\alpha\ell(Q_{i})\}.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the collection of those cubes
Q_{i}
with
s(\gamma\cap Q_{i})\geq\alpha\ell(Q_{i})
.
For each
Q_{i}
, either one of the endpoints of
\gamma
lies in an adjacent cube or
\gamma
exits the set
\mathcal{N}(Q_{i})
consisting of
Q_{i}
and all its neighboring cubes, meaning that

s(\gamma\cap\mathcal{N}(Q_{i}))\geq\textup{dist}(Q_{i},\partial\mathcal{N}(Q_{i}))\geq\ell(Q_{i})/4.

As the number of cubes in
\mathcal{N}(Q_{i})
is bounded by some
D_{n}
, and the size of the cubes in
\mathcal{N}(Q_{i})
is bounded by
4\ell(Q_{i})
, if all those cubes do not belong to
\mathcal{A}
then

\ell(Q_{i})/4<4D_{n}\alpha\ell(Q_{i}),

which is impossible if we choose
\alpha\leq 1/(16D_{n})
. Noting that there can be at most
2D_{n}
cubes
Q_{i}
for which
x
or
y
lie in an adjacent cube, we get that

m\leq D_{n}(\#\mathcal{A}+2)\lesssim\int_{\gamma}\frac{ds}{{d_{\Omega}}(z)}+1.

This proves the first part of the theorem.

For the second part, we apply the above to the quasihyperbolic geodesic
\gamma^{\prime}(x,y)
and use Theorem
2.8
with Theorem
2.4
.
∎

As a consequence of this lemma we obtain a local version of Lemma 2.1 in
[
18
]
.

Corollary 4.8
.

Assume
\Omega
is an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain and let
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in E
be such that

\ell(Q_{1})\leq\ell(Q_{2})
. If
\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})<\delta
, then

|f_{Q_{1}}-f_{Q_{2}}|\lesssim\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})+\ell(Q_{2})}{\ell(Q_{1})}\right)\right)

with a constant depending on
\epsilon
. Here
\log_{+}(x):=\max(\log x,0)
.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
4.7
to points
x\in Q_{1}
,
y\in Q_{2}
with
|x-y|<\delta
to get a curve between
x
and
y
for which we can use estimate (
61
) to bound the length
m
of the chain of Whitney cubes along this curve by
C_{\epsilon}({j_{\Omega}}(x,y)+1)
. Note that
|x-y|\leq\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})+\textup{diam}(Q_{1})+\textup{diam}(Q_{2})
, so the hypothesis and the properties of Whitney cubes, we have

{j_{\Omega}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left[\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{\textup{dist}(x,{\partial\Omega})}\right)\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{\textup{dist}(y,{\partial\Omega})}\right)\right]\lesssim 1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})+\ell(Q_{2})}{\ell(Q_{1})}\right),

The conclusion follows by applying Lemma
4.6
to adjacent cubes along this chain. ∎

We now come to the desired logarithmic growth estimate on the averages of
{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
functions on Whitney cubes, which will prove very useful in what follows.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\Omega
be an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain. If
\lambda\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}\delta}{320n(1+\sqrt{n}\epsilon)}
, then for any
f\in{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
and
Q\in E
,

|f_{Q}|\lesssim\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\ell(Q)}\right)\right)\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}.

Proof.

There is nothing to prove if
\ell(Q)\geq\lambda
, so we assume that
\ell(Q)\leq\lambda
.

Given a chain of Whitney cubes
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of length
m
, starting at
Q_{1}=Q
, we can write

|f_{Q}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|f_{Q_{i}}-f_{Q_{i+1}}|+|f_{Q_{m}}|\leq m\|f\|_{{{\rm BMO}(\Omega)}}+|f|_{Q_{m}}

due to Lemma
4.6
. Thus it remain to bound
m
and
|f|_{Q_{m}}
.

Let
x\in Q
. Since
\Omega
is
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
, it is
(\epsilon,\delta^{\prime})
for
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
. Applying Proposition
4.5
with
\delta^{\prime}
instead of
\delta
, where
\delta^{\prime}\leq\delta(\epsilon^{-1}+\sqrt{n})^{-1}
, we get the existence of a Whitney cube
S\in E
whose sidelength is at least
\epsilon\delta^{\prime}/(320n)
, and whose distance from
x
is less than
\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon^{-1}+\sqrt{n})\leq\delta
. Taking a point
y\in S
with
|x-y|<\delta
, we can use Lemma
4.7
to get the chain of cubes
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
with
Q_{m}=S
and

m\lesssim\log\left[\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{\textup{dist}(x,{\partial\Omega})}\right)\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{\textup{dist}(y,{\partial\Omega})}\right)\right]+1\lesssim\log\left[\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{\ell(Q)}\right)\left(1+\frac{|x-y|}{\ell(Q_{m})}\right)\right]+1,

by the properties of Whitney cubes. Now since

\lambda\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}\delta}{320n(1+\sqrt{n}\epsilon)}=\frac{\epsilon\delta}{320n(\epsilon^{-1}+\sqrt{n})},

we can take
\delta^{\prime}:=320n\epsilon^{-1}\lambda
, which satisfies the conditions above, and therefore

|x-y|<\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon^{-1}+\sqrt{n})=320n\epsilon^{-1}(\epsilon^{-1}+\sqrt{n})\lambda=C_{n,\epsilon}\lambda.

Moreover,
\ell(Q_{m})\geq\epsilon\delta^{\prime}/(320n)=\lambda
,
giving us that
|f|_{Q_{m}}\leq\|f\|_{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}
and

m\lesssim\log\left[\left(1+\frac{C_{n,\epsilon}\lambda}{\ell(Q)}\right)\left(1+\frac{C_{n,\epsilon}\lambda}{\lambda}\right)\right]+1\lesssim 1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\ell(Q)}\right).

∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Recall that we assume
\Omega
to be an
{(\epsilon,\delta)}
-domain and
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\epsilon,\delta}
, where
\lambda_{\epsilon,\delta}:=\frac{\epsilon^{2}\delta}{320n(1+\sqrt{n}\epsilon)}
.
For any

,
{T_{\lambda}}f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

(62)

{T_{\lambda}}f(x)=\begin{cases}f(x)&\textup{if }x\in\Omega;\\
f_{Q^{*}}&\textup{if }x\in Q\in E^{\prime}:\ell(Q)\leq\lambda;\\

where we fixed a mapping of

, as in Lemma

defined for all
Q\in E^{\prime}
with
\ell(Q)\leq\lambda
.

The following lemma, which is the
{\rm bmo}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
analogue of Lemma 2.3 in
[
18
]
, allows us to measure the bmo norm only on dyadic cubes, provided we have control of the differences of averages over adjacent cubes.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be the collection of dyadic cubes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Given
f\in{L^{1}_{\text{loc}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, denote

a_{f}=\sup\limits_{Q\in\mathfrak{D}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx,

b_{f}=\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathfrak{D}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\\
\ell(Q_{1})=\ell(Q_{2})\\
Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}|f_{Q_{1}}-f_{Q_{2}}|,

and

c_{f}=\sup_{Q\in\mathfrak{D}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):\ell(Q)\geq\lambda/16\sqrt{n}}|f|_{Q}.

Then

\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\lesssim a_{f}+b_{f}+c_{f}.

Proof.

The inequality

\sup_{Q\subset\Omega}\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q}|dx\lesssim a_{f}+b_{f}

is Lemma 2.3 in
[
18
]
. More precisely, its proof shows that if
Q\subset\Omega
,
\{Q_{i}\}
is the Whitney decomposition of the interior of
Q
and
Q_{0}
is the cube in this decomposition which contains the center of
Q
, then

\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q_{0}}|dx\lesssim a_{f}+b_{f}.

When
\ell(Q)\geq\lambda
, the sidelength of this chosen cube satisfies
\ell(Q_{0})\geq\lambda/(16\sqrt{n})
and

|f|_{Q}\leq\fint_{Q}|f(x)-f_{Q_{0}}|dx+|f|_{Q_{0}}\lesssim a_{f}+b_{f}+c_{f}.

∎

The last lemma shows that we will prove Theorem
4.1
once we establish that for some
C_{\epsilon}>0
,

\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathfrak{D}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\\
\ell(Q)\leq\lambda\end{subarray}}\fint_{Q}|{T_{\lambda}}(x)-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q}|dx\leq C_{\epsilon}\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}},

\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathfrak{D}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\\
\ell(Q_{1})=\ell(Q_{2})\\
Q_{1},Q_{2}\text{ are adjacent}\end{subarray}}|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{1}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{2}}|\leq C_{\epsilon}\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}

and

\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathfrak{D}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\\
\ell(Q)\geq\lambda/16\sqrt{n}\end{subarray}}|{T_{\lambda}}f|_{Q}\leq C_{\epsilon}\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}.

This is done in Lemma
4.13
below, using Lemmas

and

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
Q\in E\cup E^{\prime}
. Then

|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q}|\lesssim\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\ell(Q)}\right)\right)\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}.

Moreover, if
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in E\cup E^{\prime}
with
\ell(Q_{1})\leq\ell(Q_{2})
and
\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})<\delta
, then

|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{1}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{2}}|\lesssim\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\min(\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})+\ell(Q_{2}),\lambda)}{\ell(Q_{1})}\right)\right)\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}.

The constants in both inequalities depend on
\epsilon
.

Proof.

The first inequality follows from the definition of
{T_{\lambda}}
, Proposition
4.9
and Lemma
4.2
, noting that the matching cube
Q^{*}
satisfies
\ell(Q^{*})\geq\ell(Q)
.

To establish the second inequality, we may thus assume
\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})+\ell(Q_{2})<\lambda
. Then both cubes have sidelength bounded by
\lambda
, so by the definition of
{T_{\lambda}}
and Lemma
4.2
we can write

|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{1}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{2}}|=|f_{S_{1}}-f_{S_{2}}|

for
S_{1},S_{2}\in E
with
\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{\ell(S_{i})}{\ell(Q_{i})}\leq 4
(possibly

) and
\textup{dist}(S_{i},Q_{i})\leq C_{\epsilon,n}\ell(Q_{i})
, where
C_{\epsilon,n}=5\sqrt{n}+8n\cdot\epsilon^{-2}
. Thus

\displaystyle\textup{dist}(S_{1},S_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\epsilon,n}\ell(Q_{1})+\textup{diam}(Q_{1})+\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})+\textup{diam}(Q_{2})+C_{\epsilon,n}\ell(Q_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2(C_{\epsilon,n}+\sqrt{n})\ell(Q_{2})+\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2(6\sqrt{n}+8n\cdot\epsilon^{-2})\lambda

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2(6\sqrt{n}+8n\cdot\epsilon^{-2})\frac{\epsilon^{2}\delta}{320n(1+\sqrt{n}\epsilon)}<\delta,

and the result follows from Corollary

.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
Q
be a dyadic cube in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

Proof.

We follow the proof of Lemma 2.11 in
[
18

By Lemma
4.4
, there is a cube

with
Q_{0}\supset Q
or
Q\supset Q_{0}
and
\ell(Q_{0})\geq\frac{\epsilon}{160\sqrt{n}}\ell(Q)

2^{-k}\ell(Q)\leq\ell(Q_{0})<\ell(Q)

, we have

\displaystyle|Tf_{\lambda}|_{Q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{|Q_{i}|}{|Q|}\cdot\left(\fint_{Q_{i}}|{T_{\lambda}}f(x)-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{i,0}}|dx+|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{i,0}}|\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}+\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2^{-k}\ell(Q)}\right)\right)\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}.

Finally, consider adjacent dyadic cubes
Q_{1},Q_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of equal sidelength. If this sidelength is at least
\lambda
, then we can apply the previous estimate to get

|{T_{\lambda}}f_{Q_{1}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{2}}|\lesssim\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}.

Hence, we assume that
\ell(Q_{1})=\ell(Q_{2})<\lambda
and again, as
\lambda<\delta
, apply Lemma
4.12
to get Whitney cubes
Q_{1,0}\subset Q_{1}
and
Q_{2,0}\subset Q_{2}
such that

|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{i}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{i,0}}|\leq\fint_{Q_{i}}|{T_{\lambda}}f(x)-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{i,0}}|\lesssim\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}},\quad i=1,2.

Moreover, by Lemma
4.11
,

|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{1,0}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{2,0}}|\lesssim\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\textup{dist}(Q_{1,0},Q_{2,0})}{\ell(Q_{1,0})}+1\right)\right)\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}

since
\textup{dist}(Q_{1,0},Q_{2,0})\leq\textup{dist}(Q_{1},Q_{2})+\textup{diam}(Q_{1})+\textup{diam}(Q_{2})=2\sqrt{n}\ell(Q_{1})
and
\ell(Q_{1,0})\gtrsim\epsilon\ell(Q_{1})
.
Hence,

|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{1}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{2}}|\leq\sum_{i=1,2}|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{i}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{i,0}}|+|({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{1,0}}-({T_{\lambda}}f)_{Q_{2,0}}|\lesssim\|f\|_{{{\rm bmo}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}}.

∎
