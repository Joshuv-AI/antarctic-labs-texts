Parametric Bootstrap on Networks with Non-Exchangeable Nodes
Zhixuan Shao, Can M. Le

Abstract
This paper studies the parametric bootstrap method for networks to quantify the uncertainty of statistics of interest. While existing network resampling methods primarily focus on count statistics under node-exchangeable (graphon) models, we consider more general network statistics (including local statistics) under the Chung-Lu model without node-exchangeability. We show that the natural network parametric bootstrap that first estimates the network generating model and then draws bootstrap samples from the estimated model generally suffers from bootstrap bias . As a general recipe for addressing this problem, we show that a two-level bootstrap procedure provably reduces the bias. This essentially extends the classical idea of iterative bootstrap to the network setting with a growing number of parameters. Moreover, the second-level bootstrap provides a way to construct higher-accuracy confidence intervals for many network statistics.



1
Introduction

Random network models (also known as random graph models) have received continuous attention because of their wide-ranging applications, including social networks (friendship between Facebook users, LinkedIn following, etc.), biological networks (gene network, gene-protein network), information networks (email network, World Wide Web) and many others.
In these application areas, it is often of interest to summarize a network using network statistics
[
34
]
. Examples include the clustering coefficients
[
41
]
, subgraph/motif counts
[
32
,
5
]
, and centrality measures of nodes
[
13
,
12
]
. In particular, they characterize either the global property of the entire network or the local property of a single node. For example, the local clustering coefficient of a node measures the probability that two neighbors of this node are connected, while the (global) transitivity indicates the clustering effect in the whole network.

However, comparatively little attention has been paid to assessing the variability of these statistics, with a few exceptions that we will discuss shortly. Quantifying the uncertainty of these statistics is of utmost importance. Consider the problem of comparing two networks, which is a key question in many biological applications and social network analysis. A natural direction would be to compare the summary statistics of the two networks and ask if they are
significantly
different. However, it is impossible to make an inference like this without knowledge of the underlying variability of the data-generating process.

Among all network statistics,
subgraph counts
—in particular the number of occurrences of small shapes such as triangles—play an important role in characterizing random networks, e.g.,
[
32
,
37
,
30
]
. The study of the distribution of subgraph counts on random graphs dates back to
[
16
,
35
,
38
]
. Under the Erdős–Rényi model, they established the asymptotic normality and Poisson convergence of subgraph counts.
Two major techniques they used, the projection method for U-statistics and the method of moments, are later frequently used for the asymptotic analysis of subgraph counts under the more general setting of node-exchangeable random graphs, or equivalently, graphon models
[
7
]
. The pioneering work for this generalization is
[
10
]
, which not only established the asymptotic normality of subgraph counts but also provided a powerful tool for frequentist nonparametric network inferences
[
42
,
31
]
. At a more theoretical level, count functionals may be viewed as network analogs of the moments of a random variable, and knowing all population moments can uniquely determine the network model up to weak isomorphism
[
9
]
. Recent development of asymptotics of subgraph counts includes CLT for rooted subgraph counts
[
29
]
and Edgeworth expansion of network moments
[
46
]
.

However, most existing theories for asymptotic distribution are only applicable to subgraph counts,
whereas there are also many other network quantities of interest.
Even for count statistics alone, the questions are not fully answered.
Under the general graphon settings, the exact asymptotic variance is either unavailable or infeasible to calculate for large-scale networks and thus needs to be estimated. To this end, various resampling methods on networks have been developed to estimate the distribution of network statistics, and in particular, count statistics.

1.1
Related Work on Resampling Methods for Networks

The first theoretical result for resampling network data was
[
6
]
, which shows the validity of subsampling for finding the empirical distribution of count functionals. They prove the theoretical properties of their bootstrap estimates for the variance of the count features.
Following similar lines,
[
19
]
also proposed two bootstrap procedures for conducting inference for count functionals, one based on an
empirical graphon
, which amounts to resampling nodes with replacement, and the other relies upon resampling from a stochastic block model fitted to the observed network, under the intuition that such a model will be a good approximation to any graphon, provided the number of communities is chosen suitably large.

[
28
]
consider a procedure that involves subsampling nodes and computing functionals on the induced subgraphs. This procedure is shown to be asymptotically valid under conditions analogous to the i.i.d. setting; that is, the subsample size must be
o(n)
, and the functional of interest must converge to a non-degenerate limit distribution.

[
26
]
propose a jackknife procedure for subgraph counts. Under the sparse graphon model, they show the validity of network jackknife that it leads to conservative estimates of the variance for any network functional that is invariant to node permutation. They also established consistency of the network jackknife for count functionals.
Inspired by bootstrap methods for U-statistics
[
8
]
and its close relation to Edgeworth expansion
[
46
]
,
[
27
]
also propose a new class of multiplier bootstraps for count functionals that achieves higher-order correctness for appropriately sparse graphs.

Most similar to our work is
[
25
]
, where they propose a two-step bootstrap procedure under the
random dot product graphs

[
43
]
. They first estimate the latent positions with the adjacency spectral embedding
[
39
]
, and then resample the estimated positions to generate either bootstrap replicates of U-statistics of latent positions, or bootstrap replicates of whole networks.
Thus, their methods can also bootstrap network statistics that are not expressible as U-statistics.
The authors establish consistency for bootstrapping U-statistics of latent positions.

While U-statistics, especially subgraph counts, are an important class of functionals in network analysis, there are many other network quantities of interest. In particular, many
local
statistics associated with a single node do not fit the form of a U-statistic. Moreover, all aforementioned resampling methods are developed under the framework of graphon models, which asserts that the distributions of random graphs are unchanged when node labels are permuted. However, in practice, the assumption of node exchangeability may not always be desirable, especially when we study local statistics associated with certain nodes. In a network with heterogeneous node properties, we may be particularly interested in certain nodes and want to make sure each node maintains its own property in the resampled networks.
For example, in a social network, we may naturally hope that an influential person (a node with a high centrality measure) remains influential in the resampled networks.

To maintain the node-specific information in the resampled network,
we consider a different setting where networks are generated from a
fixed
edge-generating model without node exchangeability.
Although this appears to be a simpler setting, most aforementioned methods assuming graph exchangeability are not directly applicable because they additionally model the uncertainty in latent positions. Under the graphon setting, it is typically assumed that the uncertainty in latent positions dominates the Bernoulli randomness of edges, so that many statistics can be viewed as U-statistics perturbed by asymptotically negligible Bernoulli noises. Instead, we focus on the setting essentially equivalent to assuming that latent positions are fixed and non-exchangeable, so that the only randomness is from the independent Bernoulli edges.
This becomes the major difference between our setting and that of
[
25
]
.
As a result, we bootstrap replicates of networks without resampling estimated latent positions.
Figure
2
illustrates the difference between sampling from fixed latent positions (fixed edge probability matrix
P
) and sampling from random latent positions (random
P
). If the truth is that nodes are not exchangeable, then the assumption of node exchangeability will introduce unnecessary variability in the sampling distribution.

Preliminaries

2.1
Problem Set-Up

Consider an undirected and unweighted network
G=(V,E)
where
V=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
is the set of nodes and
E\subset V\times V
is the set of edges.
The network can be represented by a symmetric adjacency matrix
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
with
A_{ij}=1
if
i
and
j
are connected.
We assume that the observed network
G
is sampled from a fixed and symmetric edge probability matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
P_{ij}\in[0,1]
,
so that the upper-diagonal entries of
A
are independent Bernoulli random variables, with
\mathbb{E}[A_{ij}]=P_{ij}
. For simplicity, we only consider networks without self-loops so that
A_{ii}=0
for all
i\in V
.
This generating mechanism assuming independent edges is also referred to as the
inhomogeneous Erdős–Rényi random graph
(e.g.,
[
11
]
).
Additionally, define
\lambda_{n}\coloneqq n^{-1}\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}
as the average expected degree.
We emphasize our assumption again that
P
is
fixed
without node exchangeability,
which differs from the common graphon setting in that we do not model the randomness of
P
. The only randomness is from Bernoulli realizations of
A
from
P
.
Note that for models assuming random
P
, our method is applicable conditionally.

We focus our interests on network statistics, such as subgraph counts, clustering coefficients, and centrality scores
[
34
]
. A network statistic can be viewed as a map
T:\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}
that takes
A
as input and outputs
T(A)\in\mathbb{R}
. To make inference about the statistic
T
, it is one of our goals to construct confidence intervals for
\mu=\mathbb{E}[T(A)]
. For clarity, we often write
\mu=\mu(P)
to indicate the obvious dependency of
\mu
on
P
. The population mean
\mu(P)
is often of more interest than the observation
T(A_{\mathrm{obs}})
itself, as it is considered the signal in a noisy observation.

Since
P
is unknown, a natural estimate of
\mu
is the plug-in estimator
\mu(\widehat{P})
for some estimate
\widehat{P}
of
P
. If
\widehat{P}
is sufficiently accurate, then we can approximate the distribution of
T(A)
by that of
T(\widehat{A})
, where
\widehat{A}
is a network sampled from
\widehat{P}
.
The procedure is described in Figure
1(a)
and can be viewed as a straightforward network analog of the classical parametric bootstrap. This generic framework, which we call
network bootstrap
, should be applicable to any arbitrary network model and its corresponding estimation method.

To understand if and when this network bootstrap in Figure
1(a)
works well, a critical question is:

For a certain statistic
T
, how accurate
\widehat{P}
needs to be in order for the bootstrap distribution under
\widehat{P}
to be a good approximation of its true distribution under
P
?

(b)

Two-level bootstrap

. The two-level bootstrap targets second order approximation:
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})]-\mu(\widehat{P})\approx\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mu(\widehat{P})]-\mu(P)
.

It is hard to give a general answer to this question, as it not only involves the estimation error of
\widehat{P}
(which in turn depends on the structural assumptions on
P
), but also depends on the specific statistic
T
being considered.
However, it turns out that under some common network models, the most natural estimation
\widehat{P}
may not be accurate enough for this intuitive approach to work well. In fact, even moderate estimation error in
\widehat{P}
can lead to a big difference between the bootstrap distribution and the truth.

An example is illustrated in Figure
2
using the triangle count statistic, which compares its true distribution and bootstrap distributions using three different estimates
\widehat{P}
, as well as the distribution by bootstrapping latent positions
[
25
]
. The observed network is generated from a stochastic block model (SBM)
[
21
]
model.
We estimate
P
using three different model estimators,
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{SBM}}
,
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{DCSBM}}
and
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{SVD}}
(see Section
6
), with different levels of accuracy.
The bootstrap distribution from
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{SBM}}
recovers the unknown true distribution almost perfectly. However, with the estimated model
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{DCSBM}}
or
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{SVD}}
, we see a significant location shift between the bootstrap distribution and the truth. Apart from the three bootstrap distributions with fixed
\widehat{P}
, by assuming a graphon model and resampling with replacement the latent positions
\{\widehat{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
[
25
]

. It naturally shares the same mean with the bootstrap distribution from

, but it has a much wider spread, as the uncertainty in sampling latent positions dominates the edge randomness.

This dramatic shift between
\mu(\widehat{P})
and
\mu(P)
when bootstrapping from
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{DCSBM}}
or
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{SVD}}
is not by chance of a single observed network. For subgraph count statistics, the bootstrap distribution sampled from
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{DCSBM}}
or
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{SVD}}
will always be positively biased against the true distribution.
This

issue and how to deal with it will be the main focus of this study.

P
mainly used in this study.

2.2
Chung-Lu model

To introduce the core bias problem without unnecessary complication, in this study, we will assume that edges are formed independently with probabilities

P_{ij}=p\theta_{i}\theta_{j},\quad i\neq j,

where
p=p(n)
is a known parameter controlling the overall edge density, and
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
adjusting for individual node degrees are unknown. For identifiability, we assume
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=n
. Furthermore, we look at the relatively sparse setting with
p=o(1)
and constant order
\theta_{i}
’s when
n\to\infty
.
In particular, we assume
1\succ p\succ n^{-1}

and
c_{1}<\min_{i}\theta_{i}\leq\max_{i}\theta_{i}<c_{2}
for some constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
that do not grow with
n
.
In this context, the notation
x\prec y
signifies that
x/y=o(1)
, and we also employ
x\preccurlyeq y
to indicate that
x/y=O(1)
.

This model is widely known as the Chung-Lu random graph model
[
3
]
in the probability literature, where it serves as a fundamental “null model” for finding community structures
[
33
]
.
It is a generalization of the Erdős Rényi model
[
16
]
, where we allow degree heterogeneity.
It can also be viewed as a special case of the DCSBM
[
22
]
, with only one community.
Lastly, we point out that this is also a special case of the random dot product model
[
43
]
, with a fixed latent position for each node. It is the assumption of fixed latent positions rather than random ones that differs our work from most other network resampling methods, in particular,
[
25
]
.

The one-community assumption is mostly for convenience. It simplifies the analysis and provides a clear insight into the main cause of the issues, such as bootstrap bias.
Restrictive as it may seem, it can be justified under common settings where we have strong consistency for community recovery
[
1
]
, and thus, the estimation essentially boils down to the one-community case since the error in recovering community labels can be neglected; in this regard, all our results here may be extended to DCSBMs with multiple communities. Moreover, since the Chung-Lu model is a building block for many popular network models described above, the bootstrap bias under this model implies that the same problem exists and cannot be ignored for all these models.

For simplicity, we also assume that the edge density parameter
p
is known. This assumption can be easily justified by the fact the
p
can be estimated very accurately by MLE, with
\widehat{p}=p+O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1}p^{1/2})
, and the fact that the correlation between
\widehat{p}
and
\{\widehat{\theta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is asymptotically negligible.
Hence, the error in estimating
p
can always be disregarded when compared to the error in estimating
\{\widehat{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Following
[
22
,
45
]
, one can estimate
P
by first replacing the Bernoulli likelihood with the Poisson likelihood
and then naturally estimating
P
under this model by its MLE, namely

\widehat{P}_{ij}=p\widehat{\theta}_{i}\widehat{\theta}_{j},\quad i\neq j,\quad\textrm{ where }\widehat{\theta}_{i}=\frac{1}{(n-1)p}\sum_{j\neq i}A_{ij},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(1)

Note that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\widehat{\theta}_{i}=1
no longer holds exactly, unless we replace the
p
in the denominator by
\widehat{p}=(n(n-1))^{-1}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}
.
We ignore this difference for the reasons mentioned above.

Lastly, we reaffirm that the network bootstrap discussed here is a generic framework applicable to any network model and its corresponding estimation method. Later in our simulation studies in Section
6
, we assume SBM or DCSBM as the underlying model and use several different methods for estimating
\widehat{P}
.

3
Bootstrap Bias in Subgraph Counts

For certain statistics, even though the model parameters are consistently estimated (such as in the case of MLE of DCSBM given community labels), there may still exist non-negligible bootstrap bias. For such a statistic, if we look at its distribution on bootstrap networks sampled from
\widehat{P}
, its expectation

\widehat{\mu}\coloneqq\mu(\widehat{P})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[T(\widehat{A})|\widehat{A}\sim\widehat{P}],

may not be close to the original
\mu(P)
.
In other words, the plug-in point estimation
\mu(\widehat{P})
may be, on average, inaccurate.

For a statistic

\widehat{P}
as

\mathrm{Bias}_{P}\left(\widehat{\mu}\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\mu}]-\mu(P).

(2)

We say that the bias of
\hat{\mu}
is “non-negligible” if

\frac{\mathrm{Bias}_{P}\left(\widehat{\mu}\right)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})}}

does not vanish asymptotically.
For example, we will see that for triangle count, this ratio has an order of
\Theta(p^{-1/2})
(

2.2
;
Figure

) is non-negligible,

\frac{\mathrm{Bias}_{P}\left(\mu_{\Delta}(\widehat{P}_{\textrm{SVD}})\right)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{P}\left(\mu_{\Delta}(\widehat{P}_{\textrm{SVD}})\right)}}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}p^{-1/2}.

Here is an intuitive explanation for the bootstrap bias of triangle count—its expectation
\mu_{\Delta}(P)
is approximated by the spectral statistic
\frac{1}{6}\lambda_{1}^{3}(P)
whenever the model is low rank. Meanwhile, the SVD estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}
is positively biased against
\lambda_{1}
.
Detailed arguments are given in Section
B.4
.

To conclude, we see a non-negligible bootstrap bias for subgraph count statistics whenever
\widehat{P}
fails to estimate
P
accurately enough, such as
\widehat{P}
given by either the MLE or the truncated SVD.
Closely related to subgraph counts are the (global) transitivity and (local) clustering coefficients, as they can be viewed as normalized variants of triangle count. As expected, we also observe similar bootstrap biases for them in the numerical experiments, as shown in Figure
3
.

4
Bias Reduction by Iterative Bootstrap

Knowing the existence of bootstrap bias, a natural question is how to correct the bias. For every statistic
T
of interest, we may try to derive the analytic formula for
\mathrm{Bias}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{T})

as a function of
P

and estimate it by replacing
P
with its estimator
\widehat{P}
.
Although this case-by-case approach works for small subgraphs
R
such as edge or triangle,
it quickly becomes impractical for more complex statistics.
A more general and principle solution to this problem is to
implicitly
estimate the unknown bias
\mathrm{Bias}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})
using a
plug-in estimator

\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})

by means of another level of bootstrap. That is, we independently sample
B
networks
\{\widehat{A}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{A}_{B}\}
from the estimated model
\widehat{P}
, and for each bootstrap network
\widehat{A}_{b}\sim\widehat{P}
,
1\leq b\leq B
, we obtain an estimate
\widehat{\widehat{P}}_{b}
of
\widehat{P}
using the same method we used for estimating
P
from
A
.
For each
\widehat{\widehat{P}}_{b}
, we can similarly define

\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}_{b}\coloneqq\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}}_{b})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\widehat{P}}_{b}}[T]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[T(\widehat{\widehat{A}})|\widehat{\widehat{A}}\sim\widehat{\widehat{P}}_{b}\right].

Evaluating
\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}_{b}
by Monte Carlo will require another level of bootstrapped networks sampled from
\widehat{\widehat{P}}_{b}
. The two-level bootstrap procedure is illustrated by Figure
1(b)
.

We then approximate the true bias (
2
) by the estimated bootstrap bias

\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_{\widehat{P}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]-\widehat{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})]-\mu(\widehat{P})\approx\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}_{b}-\mu(\widehat{P}).

Using the approximation

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})]-\mu(\widehat{P})\approx\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mu(\widehat{P})]-\mu(P),

we can then reduce the bias by using an
additive correction term
.
In particular, the bias-corrected point estimation is

\widehat{\mu}-\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_{\widehat{P}}=2\widehat{\mu}-\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}].

This bias reduction method is enlightened by
iterating the bootstrap principle

[
20
]
.
In classical i.i.d. setting,
each bootstrap iteration reduces the error by a factor of at least
n^{-1/2}
.

Note that the remaining bias after correction is given by

\Delta\mathrm{Bias}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_{\widehat{P}}-\mathrm{Bias}_{P}.

The next proposition shows that the second-level bootstrap can reduce the order of bias by a factor of
n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1/2}
, where
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the
effective sample size
for parametric estimation of the parameters within
P
.
This essentially extends the classical iterative bootstrap to the Chung-Lu model case, where the number of parameters is not bounded but growing at a rate
O(n)
.
The proof is given in
B.5
.

Figure 4
:
Bias reduction for global transitivity (left) and clustering coefficient (right) with growing average degree. The
x
-axis is the average degree
\lambda_{n}
. The
y
-axis is
\frac{|\Delta\mathrm{Bias}|}{\mathrm{Bias}}
. The model is estimated by DCSBM on the left and by truncated SVD on the right. See Section

Proposition 4.1

(Bias Correction For General Subgraph Counts)
.

Assume network
A
is sampled from the Chung-Lu model.
For any fixed connected subgraph
R
with
v\geq 3
nodes and
e\geq 2
edges, if we estimate
P
using
\widehat{P}_{\textrm{MLE}}
in (
1
),
and estimate
\mathrm{Bias}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})
by
\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_{\widehat{P}}
, and apply an additive correction, then for the rooted count of
R
at node
i
(
6
),

\frac{\Delta\mathrm{Bias}_{R}^{(i)}}{\mathrm{Bias}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R}^{(i)})}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}}((np)^{-1/2}),

while for the global count of
R
(
3
),

\frac{\Delta\mathrm{Bias}_{R}}{\mathrm{Bias}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}}((np)^{-1}).

Here
\lambda_{n}=np
is the effective sample size for estimating
\bm{\theta}
in the Chung-Lu model.

Combining Proposition
4.1
with (
12
) and (
13
), the reduced bias now become negligible:

\frac{\Delta\mathrm{Bias}_{R}^{(i)}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R}^{(i)})}}=O_{\mathbb{P}}((np)^{-1}),\quad\frac{\Delta\mathrm{Bias}_{R}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})}}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1}p^{-3/2}),

as they converge to zero much faster than the original bias.

Figure
4
demonstrates the empirical effectiveness of bias reduction by the second level bootstrap, where the relative remaining bias
\frac{|\Delta\mathrm{Bias}|}{\mathrm{Bias}}
is much smaller than 1, and this ratio clearly converges to zero when the average degree
\lambda_{n}
gets larger.
The estimated
\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_{\widehat{P}}
is also shown to be a good approximation of the unknown
\mathrm{Bias}_{P}
in Figure
5
.

5
Confidence Intervals for
\mu(P)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(A)]

We have seen in Section
3
that the “first order” approximation
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T]\approx\mathbb{E}_{P}[T]
works poorly for many network statistics
T
. On the other hand, we empirically observe (Figure
3
) that
\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(T)
and the shape of the bootstrap distribution
F_{\widehat{P},T}
are quite similar to
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)
and the shape of
F_{P,T}
.
This similarity, which we will justify partly in Proposition
5.1
, allows us to construct heuristic confidence intervals based on a single observation
T(A_{\mathrm{obs}})
in Section
5.1
,
regardless of the existence of bootstrap bias.

We again use subgraph count as an example to show that the unknown true variance
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})
is approximated well by the bootstrap variance
\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(T_{R})
,
especially for the global count.
In fact, the bootstrap approximation
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})\approx\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(T_{R})
works equally well as
the bootstrap approximation
\mathrm{Bias}_{P}\approx\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_{\widehat{P}}
in Proposition
4.1
.

Denote
\Delta\mathrm{Var}\coloneqq\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(T_{R})-\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})
. The following proposition states that
\frac{\Delta\mathrm{SD}}{\mathrm{SD}_{P}(T_{R})}\approx\frac{\Delta\mathrm{Var}}{2\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})}
is small, especially for global count. The proof is given in
B.6
.

Proposition 5.1

(Variance Approximation for General Subgraph Counts)
.

Assume that the network is sampled from the Chung-Lu model.
For any fixed connected subgraph
R
with
v\geq 3
nodes and
e\geq 2
edges, if we estimate
P
using
\widehat{P}_{\textrm{MLE}}
in (
1
)
and estimate
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})
by
\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(T_{R})
, then for the rooted count of
R
at node
i
defined in (
31
),

\frac{\Delta\mathrm{Var}}{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R}^{(i)})}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}}((np)^{-1/2}),

while for the global count of
R
in (
29
),

\frac{\Delta\mathrm{Var}}{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\right)=O_{\mathbb{P}}((np)^{-1}).

The theoretical justification for the approximation of shape, and in particular, the approximation of higher-order central moments, is more complicated and beyond our main focus. Due to the asymptotic normality of many statistics (e.g., subgraph count in Proposition
5.2
), the approximation of variance is, in many cases, already enough for our purposes.

We are now ready to construct a confidence interval for
\mathbb{E}_{P}[T]
based on the single observation
T(A_{\mathrm{obs}})
as well as its estimated variance or shape. We will focus our attention on two-sided confidence intervals with nominal coverage
\alpha
.

5.1
Confidence Intervals Based on the Distribution of
T(A)

Fortunately, many network statistics of interest, including subgraph counts, are asymptotically normal under the Chung-Lu model with a fixed
P
.
The following proposition can be viewed as an extension of the result of
[
38
]
, which demonstrates the asymptotic normality of subgraph counts under the Erdős-Rényi model.

Proposition 5.2

(Asymptotic normality of subgraph count under Chung-Lu model)
.

Let
A
be a network sampled from the Chung-Lu model.
Then for any fixed subgraph
R
,
suppose
1\succcurlyeq p\succ\max(n^{-m^{-1}},n^{-1})
with
m=\max\{e(H)/v(H):H\subset R\}
.
Then

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{T_{R}-\mathbb{E}[T_{R}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{R})}}\leq x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|=o(1).

The exact convergence rate bound on the right-hand side can be derived based on the shape of
R
.

Compared with the necessary and sufficient sparsity condition
np^{m}\to\infty
in
[
38
]
, we additionally assume
np\to\infty
, which can indeed be stronger if
m<1
.
The proof of this result in Appendix
B.7
uses the Malliavin-Stein method
[
23
]
. Although the bound on the convergence rate can be made explicit, it generally depends on the shape of
R
through a complex expression. For clarity, we omit the rate in Proposition
5.2
; interested readers can recover it for specific
R
through the proof in Appendix
B.7
. As is pointed out by
[
23
]
, this asymptotic normality result may hold for other network statistics, such as rooted subgraph counts and clustering coefficients.

It is worth mentioning that
[
46
]
has also established analogous asymptotic normality for network moments (see also
[
40
]
for results about reduced subgraph counts).
While their main focus is on the marginal distribution of subgraph counts under a graphon model, their asymptotic normality result is also applicable to the scenario of conditioning on
P
, supported by their Lemma 3.1(b); see the discussion section in
[
46
]
.
However, we highlight certain distinctions between our Proposition
5.2
and their Lemma 3.1(b).
Firstly, our result applies exclusively to the Chung-Lu model with the assumption that all
\theta_{i}
’s are of constant order,
whereas their
P
needs to satisfy certain nice-behaving conditions, which holds with high probability when
P
is sampled from a
non-degenerate
graphon model.
Secondly, for many subgraphs, our sparsity condition is weaker. Take the triangle as an example, Lemma 3.1(b) of
[
46
]
requires
p\succ n^{-2/3}
while Proposition
5.2
only requires
p\succ n^{-1}
.
Lastly, we use a different proof methodology.
In
[
46
]
, the normalized subgraph count is decomposed into a linear part and a remainder, and the finite sample convergence rate of the linear part is demonstrated by applying the Berry-Esseen bound in
[
14
]
.
Meanwhile, our proof in Appendix
B.7
uses the Malliavin-Stein method
[
23
]
, enabling the derivation of a Berry-Esseen bound that depends on the specific subgraph.

For statistics with asymptotic normality, we consider the following normal and symmetric confidence interval:

T(A_{\textrm{obs}})\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}({T})},

(14)

where we approximate
\mathrm{Var}_{P}({T})
by
\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}({T})
.

For network statistics without asymptotic normality properties or those with a very slow convergence rate to normality, one may prefer the following non-normal asymmetric confidence interval:

\left[T(A_{\textrm{obs}})-\left(F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},T}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)-\mu(\widehat{P})\right),T(A_{\textrm{obs}})-\left(F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},T}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)-\mu(\widehat{P})\right)\right],

(15)

where
F_{\widehat{P},T}
is the distribution of
T(\widehat{A})
under
\widehat{P}
. Remember that

F_{P,T}
, the distribution of
T(A)
under
P
, is unknown.
If
F_{\widehat{P},T}
has almost the same shape as
F_{P,T}
(with possibly only location shift), then we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(A)]+(F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},T}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T(\widehat{A})])\leq T(A)\\
\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(A)]+(F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},T}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T(\widehat{A})])\end{array}\right\}\approx\alpha.

Both the normal-type (
14
) and the non-normal-type (
15
) intervals are centered at the single observation
T(A_{\textrm{obs}})
, with its width determined by the estimated variance of its distribution. Sometimes, this width can be quite large due to the large variance of a single observation.
Fortunately, in that case, we can construct a more informative confidence interval based on the distribution of
\mu(\widehat{P})
that we will discuss next.

The orange shaded area at the bottom represents the distribution of

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]
.

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]

, is the

bias
\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}
, shown by the gray solid arrow.

\mu(P)
.

5.2
Confidence Intervals Based on the Distribution of
\mu(\widehat{P})

Similar to (
14
) and (
15
), we can construct normal and non-normal confidence intervals for
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\mu}]
using
\mu(\widehat{P})
. They admit the following forms:

\widehat{\mu}\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})},

(16)

\left[\widehat{\mu}-\left(F^{-1}_{P,\widehat{\mu}}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\mu}]\right),\widehat{\mu}-\left(F^{-1}_{P,\widehat{\mu}}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\mu}]\right)\right],

(17)

where
F_{P,\widehat{\mu}}
is the distribution of
\mu(\widehat{P})
under
P
.
The normal interval (
16
) is similarly justified by the fact that the distribution of
\widehat{\mu}
is asymptotically normal for subgraph counts. It is stated in the following proposition and proved in Appendix
B.8
.

Proposition 5.3

(Asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\mu}_{R}
under Chung-Lu model)
.

Let
A
be a network sampled from the Chung-Lu model with
1\succ p\succ n^{-1}
. Consider the conditional expectation
\widehat{\mu}_{R}=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T_{R}]
of the subgraph count of
R
given
\widehat{P}
. Then as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{\widehat{\mu}_{R}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}_{R}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})}}\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(18)

The advantage of the confidence intervals in (
16
) and (
17
) over those in (
14
) and (
15
) is two-fold. First, unlike Proposition
5.2
, Proposition
5.3
requires an usually weaker condition
1\succ p\succ n^{-1}
, regardless of the shape of
R
(this is intuitively due to the fact that

\widehat{\mu}_{R}
is always a polynomial of
\{\widehat{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
). Therefore, the confidence intervals in (
16
) and (
17
) remain valid even when the normality approximation for
T(A)
no longer holds. Second, as we will show in Section
5.4
, for most statistics,

\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})\ll\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T),

(19)

which often results in much narrower intervals.

We still have to address two problems before using (
16
) and (
17
). First, these intervals are constructed for
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mu(\widehat{P})]
rather than
\mu(P)
,
while we have seen that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\mu}]
often fails to approximate
\mu(P)
well due to the bias problem discussed in Section
3
.
A “naive” interval in Section
5.2.1
without correction may, therefore, have poor coverage for
\mu(P)
.
Second, the distribution of
\mu(\widehat{P})
under
P
(in particular,
\mathrm{Var}_{P}({\widehat{\mu}})
in (
16
)) and
F_{P,\widehat{\mu}}
in (
17
) are unknown and need to be estimated.

Fortunately, the heuristic idea of an extra bootstrap level mentioned in Section
3
solves both problems simultaneously. It enables us to estimate the unknown bias as well as the unknown shape of
\mu(\widehat{P})
under
P
,
by the bootstrap distribution of
\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})
conditioned on
\widehat{P}
.
Based on the principle of iterative bootstraps
[
20
]
, we simply replace

(P,\widehat{P})
by
(\widehat{P},\widehat{\widehat{P}})
.
Denote by
F_{\widehat{P},\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}}
the distribution of
\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})
conditioned on
\widehat{P}
. We approximate the shape of
F_{P,\widehat{\mu}}
by that of
F_{\widehat{P},\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}}
, and
\mathrm{Var}_{P}\left(\mu(\widehat{P})\right)
by
\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}\left(\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})\right)
.
Using a two-level bootstrap, we consider two types of confidence intervals based on
\mu(\widehat{P})
.

5.2.1
Naive CI Based on
\mu(\widehat{P})

We directly construct intervals for
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\mu}]
, ignoring the difference between
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\mu}]
and
\mu(P)
. This results in the following normal and non-normal confidence intervals:

\widehat{\mu}\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(\widehat{\widehat{\mu}})},

(20)

\left[\widehat{\mu}-(F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]),\quad\widehat{\mu}-(F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}])\right].

(21)

In practice, we can replace
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]
by
(1/B)\sum_{b}\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}_{b}
.

5.2.2
Bias-Corrected CI Based on
\mu(\widehat{P})

We shift intervals in Section
5.2.1
by the additive correction term

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})]-\mu(\widehat{P})
,
obtaining the following corrected intervals:

2\widehat{\mu}-\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]\pm\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(\widehat{\widehat{\mu}})},

(22)

\left[2\widehat{\mu}-F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right),\quad 2\widehat{\mu}-F^{-1}_{\widehat{P},\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)\right].

(23)

Since the bias correction is only a shift in location, these intervals share the same widths with those uncorrected ones in Section
5.2.1
.
In cases where
\mu(\widehat{P})
is actually unbiased for
\mu(P)
, then we also have
\mu(\widehat{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\mu(\widehat{\widehat{P}})]
because
\widehat{P}
satisfied the same model assumptions of
P
, and thus these intervals become equivalent to the uncorrected ones in Section
5.2.1
, except for the approximation error from
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]\approx(1/B)\sum_{b}\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}_{b}
.

So far, we have proposed three types of confidence intervals: intervals based on the distribution of
T
(Section
5.1
), intervals based on the distribution of
\widehat{\mu}
(Section
5.2.1
) and its bias-corrected version (Section
5.2.2
).
We illustrate the construction as well as the comparison of these intervals in Figure
5
,
using the same settings as in Figure
3
.
Intervals based on the distribution of
T(A)
in (
15
) are shown in green, centered by the point estimation
T(A_{\textrm{obs}})
, with their width determined by the variance of bootstrap distribution of
T
(the blue solid curve).
Intervals based on the distribution of
\mu(\widehat{P})
are shown in blue and brown, depending on whether or not they are corrected for bias. The naive intervals in (
21
) are centered by
\widehat{\mu}
, while the bias-corrected ones in (
23
) are centered by
2\widehat{\mu}-\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}]
.
They both have the same width, characterized by the variance of
\widehat{\widehat{\mu}}
conditioned on
\widehat{P}
(the brown shaded density).
For either statistic, the green interval successfully covers the truth
\mu(P)
(the red dashed vertical line), but its width is unnecessarily larger than the other two intervals, especially with the local clustering coefficient.
The naive intervals in blue, on the other hand, miss the target in both examples, while the bias-corrected intervals, having equally narrow widths, are shifted to cover the truth correctly.

5.3
Computational Cost

The two-level bootstrap in Figure
1(b)
can be computationally intensive for large-scale networks. Drawing a network
A
from any given model
P
requires
\Omega(n^{2})
, while the computational cost of estimating
\widehat{P}
from an observed network
A
is typically dominated by the SVD step, i.e., the calculation of the first several eigenvalues and eigenvectors. This step requires
\Omega(\lambda_{n}n^{2})
for sparse networks with average degree
\lambda_{n}

[
15
]
. Finally, the cost of evaluating
T(A)
depends on the statistic
T
. For example, the (global) triangle count
T_{\Delta}
takes
\Omega(\lambda_{n}^{2}n)
. Some other statistics can take even longer, such as betweenness centrality and closeness centrality. The total computational cost is therefore
\Omega(B_{1}(n^{2}+\mathcal{T}))
for one-level bootstrap, and
\Omega(B_{1}B_{2}(n^{2}+\mathcal{T})+B_{1}n^{2}\lambda_{n})
for two-level bootstrap, where
\mathcal{T}
is the cost of evaluating
T(A)
. The latter is at least
B_{1}B_{2}n^{2}
, as we need to generate total
B_{1}B_{2}
replicate networks.

Nevertheless, some acceleration is possible.
Most local statistics do not need to be evaluated using the complete network, and for this same reason, we may only need to resample subnetworks of much smaller sizes.
More importantly, like other resampling methods, bootstrapping can be easily parallelized.

5.4
Examples of Statistics with
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})\ll\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)

For some network statistics such as node degrees, we have
\mu(\widehat{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T(\widehat{A})]=T(A_{\mathrm{obs}})
. Then intervals based on
\widehat{\mu}
will be essentially the same as those based on
T
.
However, there are many other statistics whose expectation can be more accurately estimated given additional information from the network, resulting in
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})\ll\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)
. For these statistics, it is worth the computation cost of an extra bootstrap level for narrower confidence intervals in Section
5.2
.
Under the Chung-Lu model, we completely characterize such comparison for subgraph count statistics,
with the order of
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})
and
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})
that have been shown earlier.

Proposition 5.4

(Variance Comparison for Subgraph Counts)
.

Assume network
A
is sampled from
the Chung-Lu model in Section
2.2
.
For any fixed subgraph
R
with
v
nodes and
e
edges, the order of
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})
(
11
) is at most the same as the order of
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})
(
26
), namely

\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})\preccurlyeq\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R}),

and
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})\asymp\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})
only when the leading overlap
H
of
R

is a single edge
K_{2}
with
v(K_{2})=2
and
e(K_{2})=1
.
A subgraph
H
of
R
is called a leading overlap
[
38
]
of
R
if

H=\argmax_{G\subset R,e(G)>0}\Theta(n^{2v-v(G})p^{2e-e(G)}).

Otherwise, we have
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R})\prec\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R})
. Similar order comparison also holds for rooted subgraph counts by comparing
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{R}^{(i)})
(
10
) and
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{R}^{(i)})
(
27
).

The next example illustrates an even sharper contrast. To this end, let us recall the definition of the local clustering coefficient. For any node
i
with
d_{i}\geq 2
, its clustering coefficient is defined as the ratio of the triangles rooted at the node and the triples centered on the node,

T_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}(A)\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}A_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk}}{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}A_{ij}A_{ik}}=\frac{\textrm{\# triangles containing node }i}{d_{i}(d_{i}-1)/2}.

(24)

For convenience, we assume the true generating model to be Erdős-Rényi
G(n,p)
, with large
n
and small
p
.
It’s straightforward to show
\mathrm{Var}(T_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)})\asymp|E|^{-1}\asymp n^{-2}p^{-1}
.

Proposition 5.5

(
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)})
of Local Clustering Coefficient)
.

Assume network
A
is sampled from the Erdős-Rényi model
G(n,p)
.
For the local clustering coefficient
T_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}(A)
in (
24
), we estimate the model
P
by the MLE
\widehat{P}
using one of the following model assumptions: (i) Erdős-Rényi model with unknown parameter
p
, or (ii) Chung-Lu model with unknown parameters
\bm{\theta}
.
Then as
n\to\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)})=O(n^{-2}p)\prec\mathrm{Var}(T_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}).

The case of Erdős-Rényi is straightforward since
\mu_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}(\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{ER}})=\widehat{p}
. It is slightly more challenging to show the claim holds under the Chung-Lu model; see Appendix
B.9
for details.
As illustrated in Figure
3
and
5
, we see the distribution of
\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}
is much narrower than the distribution of
T_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}
.

It may seem surprising that estimation under
the more complicated Chung-Lu model leads to the same order of
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)})
as assuming Erdős-Rényi model.
However, this is due to the normalizing effect of the count of V-shapes in the denominator, which is highly correlated with the count of triangles in the numerator. As a result,
\theta_{i}
is
ancillary
in the sense that the dependence of
\mu_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}
on
\theta_{i}
is negligible. As a heuristic example, consider a Chung-Lu model with only
\theta_{i}
unknown, and all the other
\theta_{j}
’s are known to be 1. Then
\mu_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}(P)=p
does not depend on
\theta_{i}
.

We end this section with the following discussion.
In practice, for an arbitrary statistic
T
, how can one know,
a priori
, that
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})\ll\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)
, so that it is worth the computational cost of an extra level of bootstrap?
After all, for an arbitrary
T
, it may be tedious or even infeasible to carry out theoretical analysis that compares
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})
and
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)
,
and asymptotic results like
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})\prec\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)
like in the above two examples do not imply if
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu})
is much smaller than
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T)
for a finite sample size
n
.
In most cases, there is no general answer other than the intuition that
\mu(\widehat{P})
should be a better point estimate whenever
\widehat{P}
exploits more information from the network than is reflected by
T(A_{\mathrm{obs}})
.
We have yet another practical solution—whenever the computational cost of the two-level bootstrap is affordable, we can always construct intervals based on
\widehat{\mu}
and then empirically compare their widths with intervals constructed based on
T(A)
.

In this section, we will first introduce the simulation settings for examples in Figure
3
,
4
, and
5
, and then study the performance of different types of bootstrap confidence intervals in terms of coverage and width.

We construct the fixed edge connectivity model
P
as an instance of DCSBM with
K=3
communities and

n=|V|=600
nodes. Let

B=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}5&1&1\\
1&5&1\\
1&1&5\end{array}\right)

be the block-wise connectivity matrix.
For each node
i
, let
g_{i}\in\{1,2,3\}
be its community label. We evenly divide the labels
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
into three groups, so that

g_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&1\leq i\leq\frac{n}{3}\\
2,&\frac{n}{3}<i\leq\frac{2n}{3}\\
3,&\frac{2n}{3}<i\leq n\end{array}\right..

Let
\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}\}
be an i.i.d. sample from
either (i) a uniform distribution
\mathrm{Unif}[0.2,1]

or (ii) a powerlaw distribution
p(x)\propto x^{-5}
(Pareto distribution with
\alpha=4
).
We fix
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
once drawn and view them as unknown parameters.
We now construct the model
P
as

P_{ij}=\rho B_{g_{i},g_{j}}\theta_{i}\theta_{j},\quad i\neq j,\quad\textrm{ and }P_{ii}=0\textrm{ for all }i,

(25)

where we include an additional parameter
\rho
to control the sparsity level.

Once the model
P
is
fixed
with our selected
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\rho
, we draw the observed network
A_{\textrm{obs}}
from it. We then construct confidence intervals for our statistic(s) of interest based on this single observed network
A_{\textrm{obs}}
.
Throughout the study, the first-level bootstrap draws
B_{1}=1000
replicate networks
\widehat{A}
from
\widehat{P}
, and we again draw
B_{2}=1000
replicate networks
\widehat{\widehat{A}}
from each
\widehat{\widehat{P}}_{b}
in the second-level bootstrap.
The coverage and width of confidence intervals are calculated by repeating this procedure 1000 times.

Two different approaches are used for estimating
P
: MLE and truncated SVD.
The former requires estimation of the node labels
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, for which we use regularized spectral clustering
[
2
]
.
All other parameters of the DCSBM model, namely
B
and
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, are then estimated by the profile MLE. For the truncated SVD estimator, we use the true rank
K=3
of
P
.
We denote the two estimators of
P
as
\widehat{P}_{\textrm{DCSBM}}
and
\widehat{P}_{\textrm{SVD}}
, respectively.
In particular, we view
\widehat{P}_{\textrm{DCSBM}}
as an extension of the
\widehat{P}_{\textrm{MLE}}
(
1
) estimator for the Chung-Lu Model, to the case of multiple communities. We expect similar bias problems and correction effects according to our theoretical results for the Chung-Lu Model.

Throughout the paper, we mainly use two network statistics as running examples. One is the transitivity, a global statistic that measures the ratio of the count of triangles and connected triples in the graph. The other is the clustering coefficient (
24
) of a certain node (the node with index
i=1
), which is a local statistic.
Results on some other common network statistics are given in tables in Appendix
A
.

but also has a width shorter than the one based on the distribution of

Lastly, we reiterate the fact that all the previous propositions in our analysis are restricted to subgraph counts under the Chung-Lu model, with
\widehat{P}
given by the MLE (
1
).
Under these conditions, the simulation results for subgraph count statistics align well with the theory, and we omit their figures here.
It is practically more interesting to see if the same conclusions can be extended to other network statistics, such as the two examples here.
While the propositions may not be directly applicable to our two examples—such as the use of the estimation
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{SVD}}
instead of
\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{MLE}}
in one instance—we nonetheless observe bootstrap bias problems. Empirically, the proposed bootstrap framework and bias correction approach still perform reasonably well for these cases.

6.2
Coverage and width

Continuing with the transitivity and clustering coefficient, in Figure
6
and
7
, we further look into how the coverage and width results evolve with growing network density.
Again, we set different values of
\rho

so that the average expected degree
\lambda_{n}
takes values
2\log n
(
\approx 12.8
),
4\log n
,
8\log n
, and
16\log n
.

In terms of width, we clearly see that for both statistics, intervals based on the distribution of
\widehat{\mu}
are narrower than those based on the distribution of
T
. For global transitivity, the contrast is more visible when the network is sparse and less so when the network grows denser. This is intuitively explained by the strong connection between transitivity and triangle counts and the fact that
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{\Delta})\prec\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{\Delta})
when
p\prec n^{-1/2}
, while
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{\Delta})\asymp\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{\Delta})
when
n^{-1/2}\prec p\prec 1
.
On the other hand, for the local clustering coefficient, the contrast in terms of width is constantly sharp throughout all sparsity levels, especially when using the more accurate
\widehat{P}_{\textrm{DCSBM}}
. This is expected from Proposition
5.5
.

In terms of coverage, intervals based on the distribution of
T
provide coverage close to the nominal
\alpha
. The bias-corrected intervals based on
\widehat{\mu}
also perform reasonably well, except the one based on
\mu(\widehat{P}_{\textrm{SVD}})
for local clustering coefficient, which is likely due to the less accurate estimation of the local parameters around that node. The naive intervals without correction, however, under-cover severely for both statistics.
Naturally, for the naive intervals, we would anticipate their coverage to get closer to the nominal
\alpha
when
\lambda_{n}
gets larger. We indeed observe growing coverage for the naive intervals, but still far from the nominal
\alpha
. Even at
\lambda_{n}=16\log n\approx 100
, some still have coverage well under 80%.
This again suggests the necessity of bias correction, especially when the network is sparse.

There are a few more things to be noted. First, the bias-corrected intervals almost always have better or at least similar coverage than the uncorrected version.
Second, intervals based on

B.1
Variance of subgraph count
T_{R}
and rooted subgraph count
T_{R}^{(i)}

This section aims to give the order of
\mathrm{Var}(T_{R})
for the subgraph count
T_{R}
within the context of the Chung-Lu model.
Additionally, we present explicit expressions for the variance of triangle counts, formulated in terms of
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Under the Chung-Lu model in Section
2.2
with the assumption
\Omega(1)=\min_{i}\theta_{i}\leq\max_{i}\theta_{i}=O(1)
, following the argument in
[
38
]
, the variance of the subgraph count
T_{R}
can be shown to have the following asymptotic order

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(T_{R})

\displaystyle\asymp

\displaystyle\sum_{H\subset R,\,e(H)>0}c_{H}n^{2v-v(H)}p^{2e-e(H)}

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{H\subset R,\,e(H)>0}\Theta\left(n^{2v-v(H)}p^{2e-e(H)}\right),

where
c_{H}
is some constant that depends on the subgraph
H
and the upper bound
\max_{i}\theta_{i}
. The subgraph
H
of
R
that makes the order of
n^{2v-v(H)}p^{2e-e(H)}
largest is called the
leading overlap

[
38
]
of
R
. Similarly, for the rooted count
T_{R}^{(i)}
, we have

\mathrm{Var}(T_{R}^{(i)})\asymp\max_{H^{(i)}\subset R,\,e(H^{(i)})>0}\Theta\left(n^{2v-1-v(H^{(i)})}p^{2e-e(H^{(i)})}\right),

(27)

for some leading overlap
H^{(i)}
of
R
.

Not only can we obtain the asymptotic order of the variance.
In fact, we can, in general, write the explicit expression for the variance as a
sum of polynomials
of
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
For example, when we look at triangle counts,
the variance of the global triangle count
T_{\Delta}(A)=\sum_{i<j<k}A_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk}
can be expanded as the following

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(T_{\Delta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}\mathrm{Var}(A_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk})

(28)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i<j<k}\sum_{i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}<k^{\prime}}\mathrm{Cov}(A_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk},A_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}A_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}A_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}p^{3}\theta_{i}^{2}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}^{2}(1-p^{3}\theta_{i}^{2}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i,j,k,k^{\prime}\}\subset[n],i<j,\\
|\{i,j,k,k^{\prime}\}|=4\end{subarray}}p^{5}\theta_{i}^{3}\theta_{j}^{3}\theta_{k}^{2}\theta_{k^{\prime}}^{2}(1-p\theta_{i}\theta_{j}),

and for the triangle count
T_{\Delta}^{(i)}(A)=\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}A_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk}
rooted at node
i
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(T_{\Delta}^{(i)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j<k\\
\{j,k\}\subset[n]\setminus\{i\}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Var}(A_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j<k\\
\{j,k\}\subset[n]\setminus\{i\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}<k^{\prime}\\
\{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}\subset[n]\setminus\{i\}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Cov}(A_{ij}A_{ik}A_{jk},A_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}A_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}A_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j<k\\
\{j,k\}\subset[n]\setminus\{i\}\end{subarray}}p^{3}\theta_{i}^{2}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}^{2}(1-p^{3}\theta_{i}^{2}\theta_{j}^{2}\theta_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{j,k,k^{\prime}\}\subset[n]\setminus\{i\}\\
j<k,\,j<k^{\prime},\,k\neq k^{\prime}\end{subarray}}p^{5}\theta_{i}^{3}\theta_{j}^{3}\theta_{k}^{2}\theta_{k^{\prime}}^{2}(1-p\theta_{i}\theta_{j}).

B.2
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Proof.

Recall the definition of
T_{R}(A)
:

T_{R}(A)=\sum\bm{1}\{G\subset A:G\sim R\}=\sum_{J\subset[n],|J|=v}\bm{1}\{G\subset E(J):G\sim R\},

where
E(J)
is a subgraph of
A
induced by the node set
J
,
and
G\subset A
spans the non-automorphic subgraphs of
A
.
We enumerate all non-automorphic subgraphs
G\subset K_{J}
such that
G\sim R
, where
K_{J}
is the complete graph on the node set
J
. This implies

\displaystyle T_{R}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{J\subset[n],|J|=v}\sum_{G\sim R,G\subset K_{J}}\mathbf{1}\{G\subset E(J)\}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{J\subset[n],|J|=v}\sum_{G\sim R,G\subset K_{J}}\prod_{(s,t)\in G}A_{s,t},

where the second sum is over all possible ways that
J
contains a copy of
R
. Now we look at its expectation
\mu_{R}(P)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{R}]
:

\displaystyle\mu_{R}(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{J\subset[n],|J|=v}\sum_{G\sim R,G\subset K_{J}}\prod_{(s,t)\in G}P_{st}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{J\subset[n],|J|=v}\sum_{G\sim R,G\subset K_{J}}\prod_{(s,t)\in G}p\theta_{s}\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{J\subset[n],|J|=v}\sum_{G\sim R,G\subset K_{J}}p^{e}\prod_{s\in J}\theta_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}},

where
\tilde{d}_{s}
is the degree of node
s
in graph
G
where
G\sim R
and
G\subset K_{J}
. Recall that
\tilde{d}_{s}\geq 2
for some node
s
because
R
is a connected graph. Also, we have
\tilde{d}_{s}\leq e=O(1)
for any node
s
. Replacing
\bm{\theta}
by
\bm{\widehat{\theta}}
, the expected number of copies
R
in a bootstrapped network sampled from
\widehat{P}
is

\widehat{\mu}_{R}=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T_{R}]=\sum_{J\subset[n],|J|=v}\sum_{G\sim R,G\subset K_{J}}p^{e}\prod_{s\in J}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}.

(30)

Similarly, we can show that subgraph count of
R

rooted
at node
i
, namely the number of occurrences of a particular subgraph attached to a given node
i
, denoted as
T_{R}^{(i)}(A)
, is

T_{R}^{(i)}(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J^{(i)}\subseteq[n]\\
|J^{(i)}|=v\\
i\in J^{(i)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{[G^{(i)};i]\equiv[R;v],G^{(i)}\subset K_{J^{(i)}}}\prod_{(s,t)\in G^{(i)}}A_{s,t},

(31)

and its expectation is

\widehat{\mu}_{R}^{(i)}=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T_{R}^{(i)}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J^{(i)}\subseteq[n]\\
|J^{(i)}|=v\\
i\in J^{(i)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{[G^{(i)};i]\equiv[R;v],G^{(i)}\subset K_{J^{(i)}}}p^{e}\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{\tilde{d}_{i}}\prod_{s\in J^{(i)}\setminus\{i\}}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}.

(32)

Deriving the bias of
\widehat{\mu}_{R}
or
\widehat{\mu}_{R}^{(i)}
boils down to calculating the bias of each term
\prod_{s\in J}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}
.
Since the total number of summands is
\Theta(n^{v})
in the summation of global count in (
30
), and
\Theta(n^{v-1})
in the summation of rooted count in (
32
), we only need to show in the rest of the proof that terms like
\prod_{s\in J}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{d_{s}}
have asymptotically positive bias of order
\Theta((np)^{-1})
, i.e.,

\mathrm{Bias}_{P}\left[\prod_{s\in J}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right]=\Theta((np)^{-1}).

(33)

Using the fact that the covariance between any pair of (
\widehat{\theta}_{i}
,
\widehat{\theta}_{j}
) is of negligible order

\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{\theta}_{i},\widehat{\theta}_{j})=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(d_{i},d_{j})}{(n-1)^{2}p^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(A_{ij})}{(n-1)^{2}p^{2}}=\frac{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}p(1-\theta_{i}\theta_{j}p)}{(n-1)^{2}p^{2}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right),

(34)

and since all
\tilde{d}_{s}
are bounded, by the delta method, we know that
\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{\tilde{d}_{i}},\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{\tilde{d}_{j}})
are also of order
O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right)
.
Therefore, we can approximate
\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\prod_{s\in J}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right]
by
\prod_{s\in J}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right]
as if all
\widehat{\theta}_{i}
’s are independent:

\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\prod_{s\in J}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right]=\prod_{s\in J}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right).

(35)

Denote
n^{\prime}=n-1
. Recall that
\widehat{\theta}_{i}=(n^{\prime}p)^{-1}\sum_{j\neq i}A_{ij}
. We now derive higher moments of
\widehat{\theta}_{s}
, e.g.,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c}]
for some integer
c\geq 2
. We first expand
\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c}=(n^{\prime}p)^{-c}\sum_{k=1}^{c}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
|\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{c}\}|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{s=1}^{c}A_{ij_{s}}\right).

Taking its expectation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c}]=(n^{\prime}p)^{-c}\sum_{k=1}^{c}\left(p^{k}\theta_{i}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
|\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{c}\}|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{s=1}^{c}\theta_{j_{s}}\right).

(36)

Note that there are
{c\brace k}n^{\prime\underline{k}}
summands in the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
|\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{c}\}|=k\end{subarray}},

where
n^{\prime\underline{k}}=n^{\prime}(n^{\prime}-1)\cdots(n^{\prime}-k+1)=\Theta(n^{k})
is the
k
th falling power of
n^{\prime}
, and
{c\brace k}
is the Stirling number of the second kind, which is of constant order
O(1)
.
With
\max_{i}\theta_{i}=O(1)
, we will show

(n^{\prime}p)^{-c}\left(p^{c}\theta_{i}^{c}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
|\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{c}\}|=c\end{subarray}}\prod_{s=1}^{c}\theta_{j_{s}}\right)-\theta_{i}^{c}=O(n^{-1})=o((np)^{-1}).

(37)

which then implies that the bias of
\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c}
is dominated by the second term in the sum (
36
) (the third term and the remaining terms are at least smaller by a factor of
(np)^{-1}
). Since there must be some node(s) in
R
with
\tilde{d}_{s}\geq 2
by our assumption, the second term is of order
\Theta((np)^{-1})
.
Then

\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c}]=\theta_{i}^{c}+\Theta((np)^{-1}),

(38)

and
\mathrm{Bias}[\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c}]
is positive for large enough
n
.
Now combine (
38
) with (
35
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\prod_{s\in J}\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in J\\
\tilde{d}_{s}\geq 2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\widehat{\theta}_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in J\\
\tilde{d}_{s}\geq 2\end{subarray}}\left(\theta_{i}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}+\Theta((np)^{-1})\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{s\in J}\theta_{s}^{\tilde{d}_{s}}\right)+\Theta((np)^{-1}).

We have thus shown (
33
), assuming (
37
) holds.

It remains to show that (
37
) holds under the assumption that
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{\theta_{i}}=O(1)
. Note that

\left(\frac{1}{n^{\prime c}}-\frac{1}{n^{\prime\underline{c}}}\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
|\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{c}\}|=c\end{subarray}}\theta_{j_{1}}\cdots\theta_{j_{c}}\right)=O(n^{-1}).

Therefore, it is equivalent to show

\frac{1}{n^{\prime\underline{c}}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
|\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{c}\}|=c\end{subarray}}\theta_{j_{1}}\cdots\theta_{j_{c}}\right)-1=O(n^{-1}).

Recall the constraint:
\frac{1}{n}\sum\theta_{i}=1
. Denote
\delta_{i}\coloneqq\theta_{i}-1
.
Then we have
\sum\delta_{i}=0
, and
\delta_{i}=O(1)
. Now we expand the left-hand side of the above expression,

\displaystyle LHS

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({n^{\prime\underline{c}}})^{-1}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
|\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{c}\}|=c\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{s=1}^{c}(1+\delta_{j_{s}})-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{n^{\prime}\choose c}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{c}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{c}\delta_{j_{s}}+\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq c}\delta_{j_{s}}\delta_{j_{t}}+\ldots+\delta_{j_{t_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{j_{t_{c}}}\right).

Now, every term in the sum
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{c}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{c}\delta_{j_{s}}+\sum_{s<t}\delta_{j_{s}}\delta_{j_{t}}+\ldots\right)
is
O\left(n^{c-1}\right)
.
In particular, for the
k
th term,
1\leq k\leq c
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,j_{c}\neq i\\
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{c}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{1\leq t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\leq c}\prod_{s=1}^{k}\delta_{t_{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{t_{1}\neq i}\delta_{t_{1}}\sum_{t_{2}\notin\{i,t_{1}\}}\delta_{t_{2}}\cdots\sum_{t_{k}\notin\{i,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k-1}\}}{n^{\prime}-k\choose c-k}\delta_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}{n^{\prime}-k\choose c-k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}\neq i,\,\ldots,\,t_{k-1}\neq i\\
|\{t_{1},\,\ldots,\,t_{k-1}\}|=k-1\end{subarray}}\prod_{s=1}^{k-1}\delta_{t_{s}}\left(-\sum_{t_{k}\in\{i,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k-1}\}}\delta_{t_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(n^{c-1}).

Thus, the proof is completed.
∎

B.3
Proof of Lemma
3.2

When estimating the Chung-Lu model in Section
2.2
, we have
n
parameters (excluding
p
) to be estimated, namely
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})^{\top}
. We denote the truth as
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. During later proofs, we will need to derive the asymptotic variance of
bounded-degree polynomial
functions
f
of
\{\widehat{\theta}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, namely
\mathrm{Var}_{P}\left(f(\bm{\widehat{\theta}})\right)
.
For example,
\mu(\widehat{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[T]
,
\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(T)
, and
\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(\widehat{\widehat{\mu}})
are special cases of
f(\bm{\widehat{\theta}})
.
We show that the classical “delta method” style approximation is still applicable in this case, despite that the number of parameters here is of order
n
.

Lemma B.1

(Variance approximation for polynomials of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
in the Chung-Lu model)
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the MLE (
1
) of the Chung-Lu model in Section
2.2
. Let
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded-degree polynomial of
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of the following form,

f_{n}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J=\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{v}\}\subset[n]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq c_{1}\leq d_{\max}\\
\cdots\\
0\leq c_{v}\leq d_{\max}\end{subarray}}\alpha_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{v}}p^{e+\sum_{i}c_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{v}\theta_{j_{i}}^{c_{i}},

(40)

where
e
is a constant, and all coefficients
\alpha_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{v}}\geq 0
are upper-bounded by some constant
C>0
, and
c_{i}
’s are non-negative integers bounded by some integer
d_{\max}>0
.
The above constants do not change with
n
.
Then we can approximate the variance of
f_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})
using,

\mathrm{Var}\left(f_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\right)\asymp\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{n}\right]^{\top}_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\mathrm{Var}(\bm{\widehat{\theta}})\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{n}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}},

(41)

where
\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{n}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the gradient of
f_{n}
evaluated at
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

Proof.

To simplify notations, we suppress the subscript
n
from
f_{n}
and keep in mind that
f
specifically denotes
f_{n}
.

Note that the first derivative
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}
is also a bounded degree polynomial of
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in the form of (
40
), with all coefficients held constant when
n
changes.
Together with the fact
\max_{i}\theta_{i}=O(1)
, taking derivatives of
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}
w.r.t.
\theta_{i}
at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
will give rise to at most the same asymptotic order as
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}
itself,

\left[\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\theta_{i}^{2}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}=\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\cdot O(1).

Meanwhile, the number of terms in
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}
that involve both node
i
and node
j
are smaller than the number of terms involving node
i
, by a factor of
O(n^{-1})
, so that

\left[\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}=\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\cdot O(n^{-1}),\quad\textrm{if }i\neq j.

By recursively applying the above arguments, we obtain a general order bound for the
d
th derivative,

\left[\frac{\partial^{d}f}{\partial\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial\theta_{i_{d}}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}=\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\cdot O(n^{-t}),\quad\textrm{where }t=|\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,\,i_{d}\}|-1.

(42)

Denote
\bm{\delta}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Since
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
is estimated by the MLE (
1
), we have
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bm{\delta}]=\bm{0}
, and
\bm{\delta}_{i}=O_{\mathbb{P}}((np)^{-1/2})
.
Using the fact that
f
is a bounded degree polynomial, one can rewrite
f(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})
as an expansion around
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
,

\displaystyle f(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\bm{\theta}_{0})+\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}^{\top}\bm{\delta}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\delta}^{\top}\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}f\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\bm{\delta}+\ldots

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{d_{\max}!}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{i_{d_{\max}}=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial^{d_{\max}}f}{\partial\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial\theta_{i_{d_{\max}}}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\delta_{i_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{d_{\max}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\bm{\theta}_{0})+\sum_{m=1}^{d_{\max}}\frac{1}{m!}T_{m},

where

T_{m}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{i_{m}=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial^{m}f}{\partial\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial\theta_{i_{m}}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\delta_{i_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{m}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(f(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{d_{\max}}\sum_{t=1}^{d_{\max}}\frac{1}{s!t!}\mathrm{Cov}(T_{s},T_{t})

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{d_{\max}}\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}}\mathrm{Var}(T_{m})+\sum_{s=1}^{d_{\max}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=1\\
t\neq s\end{subarray}}^{d_{\max}}\frac{1}{s!t!}\mathrm{Cov}(T_{s},T_{t}).

We then argue that
\mathrm{Var}\left(f(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\right)
is dominated by the variance of the first-order term,

\mathrm{Var}(T_{1})=\mathrm{Var}(\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}^{\top}\bm{\delta})=\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}^{\top}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}},

and all the other variance and covariance terms are asymptotically negligible compared with
\mathrm{Var}(T_{1})
.
More specifically, their orders can all be bounded by the larger one between

O(\gamma^{2}(n))
and
O\left(\gamma^{2}(n)n^{-1}p^{-2}\right)
, where we denote the order of
\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}=\Theta(\gamma(n))
, for all
i
.
That is,

\mathrm{Cov}(T_{s},T_{t})=O(\gamma^{2}(n))\vee O\left(\gamma^{2}(n)n^{-1}p^{-2}\right),\quad\textrm{ for }s,t\in[d_{\max}],\,s+t>2,

(44)

where we allow
s=t
. On the other hand, we can easily show that
\mathrm{Var}(T_{1})
is of a larger order, at least by a factor of
\Omega(p^{-1})\wedge\Omega(np)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(T_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]^{2}_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})+\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{\theta}_{i},\widehat{\theta}_{j})

(45)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]^{2}_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})=\Theta(\gamma^{2}(n)p^{-1}).

Recall that
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})=\frac{\theta_{i}}{np}+O(n^{-1})=\Theta(n^{-1}p^{-1})
. The inequality holds due to the fact that both
\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{\theta}_{i},\widehat{\theta}_{j})
are positive.
Comparing (
44
) and (
45
), we can conclude that
\mathrm{Var}(T_{1})=\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}^{\top}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}
is a sufficient approximation for
\mathrm{Var}(f(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}))
,
since there are only finite number of
\mathrm{Cov}(T_{s},T_{t})
terms in the sum (
43
).

Therefore, we only need to show (
44
). We begin by expanding the
\mathrm{Cov}(T_{s},T_{t})
into sums of covariances,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(T_{s},T_{t})

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{i_{s}=1}^{n}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{j_{t}=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial^{s}f}{\partial\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial\theta_{i_{s}}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left[\frac{\partial^{t}f}{\partial\theta_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial\theta_{j_{t}}}\right]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\mathrm{Cov}(\delta_{i_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{s}},\delta_{j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{j_{t}}).

To derive its order, we need first to determine the order of

\mathrm{Cov}(\delta_{i_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{s}},\delta_{j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{j_{t}})
,
for
s+t>2
. Without loss of generosity, let us assume
t>1
in the following.
With no other constraints on
i_{1},\ldots,\,i_{s},j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\delta_{i_{1}}\cdots\delta_{i_{s}},\delta_{j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{j_{t}})

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q=1}^{s}(\widehat{\theta}_{i_{q}}-\theta_{i_{q}}),\quad\prod_{r=1}^{t}(\widehat{\theta}_{j_{r}}-\theta_{j_{r}})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subset[s]}\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\widehat{\theta}_{i_{q}}\prod_{q^{\prime}\in[s]\setminus\mathcal{Q}}(-\theta_{i_{q^{\prime}}}),\quad\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\widehat{\theta}_{j_{r}}\prod_{r^{\prime}\in[t]\setminus\mathcal{R}}(-\theta_{j_{r^{\prime}}})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{Q}\subset[s]}\prod_{q^{\prime}\in[s]\setminus\mathcal{Q}}(-\theta_{i_{q^{\prime}}})\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}\prod_{r^{\prime}\in[t]\setminus\mathcal{R}}(-\theta_{j_{r^{\prime}}})\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\widehat{\theta}_{i_{q}},\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\widehat{\theta}_{j_{r}}\right).

Define sets
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})=\{i_{q}:q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
for any index set
\mathcal{Q}\subset[s]
, and sets
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})=\{j_{r}:r\in\mathcal{R}\}
for any index set
\mathcal{R}\subset[t]
.
We will next show that for any
\mathcal{Q}\subset[s]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}\prod_{r^{\prime}\in[t]\setminus\mathcal{R}}(-\theta_{j_{r^{\prime}}})\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\widehat{\theta}_{i_{q}},\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\widehat{\theta}_{j_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(1)\vee O(n^{-1}p^{-2})\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}O(n^{-2}),&\textrm{if }\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}([t])=\emptyset\\
O(n^{-1}),&\textrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

We begin with the case
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}([t])=\emptyset
.
Denote
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{|\mathcal{Q}|}\}
and
R=\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{|\mathcal{R}|}\}
.
When
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})=\emptyset
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\widehat{\theta}_{i_{q}},\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\widehat{\theta}_{j_{r}}\right)

(51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n-1)p)^{-(|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|)}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\left(\sum_{k_{q}=1}^{n}A_{i_{q}k_{q}}\right),\quad\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\left(\sum_{\ell_{r}=1}^{n}A_{j_{r}\ell_{r}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n-1)p)^{-(|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|)}\sum_{k_{q_{1}}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{k_{q_{|\mathcal{Q}|}}=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell_{r_{1}}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{\ell_{r_{|\mathcal{R}|}}=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{i_{q}k_{q}},\quad\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}A_{j_{r}\ell_{r}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\bm{1}\left\{\sum_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\bm{1}\left\{k_{q^{\prime}}=j_{r^{\prime}},i_{q^{\prime}}=\ell_{r^{\prime}}\right\}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n-1)p)^{-(|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|)}\left(1+O((np)^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{q_{1}}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{k_{q_{|\mathcal{Q}|}}=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell_{r_{1}}=1}^{n}\ldots\sum_{\ell_{r_{|\mathcal{R}|}}=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{i_{q}k_{q}},\quad\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}A_{j_{r}\ell_{r}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\bm{1}\left\{\sum_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\bm{1}\left\{k_{q^{\prime}}=j_{r^{\prime}},i_{q^{\prime}}=\ell_{r^{\prime}}\right\}=1\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n-1)p)^{-(|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|)}n^{|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|-2}p^{|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|-1}|\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{R}|

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\theta_{i_{q}}\right]\left[\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\theta_{j_{r}}\right]\left(1+O(p)\right)\left(1+O((np)^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{R}|}{n^{2}p}\left[\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\theta_{i_{q}}\right]\left[\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\theta_{j_{r}}\right]+O(n^{-2})\vee O(n^{-3}p^{-2}).

Note that (
51
) holds because

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}A_{i_{q}k_{q}},\quad\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}A_{j_{r}\ell_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i_{q^{\prime}}j_{r^{\prime}}}\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{q^{\prime}\}}A_{i_{q}k_{q}}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\{r^{\prime}\}}A_{j_{r}\ell_{r}}\right]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i_{q^{\prime}}j_{r^{\prime}}}\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{q^{\prime}\}}A_{i_{q}k_{q}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i_{q^{\prime}}j_{r^{\prime}}}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\{r^{\prime}\}}A_{j_{r}\ell_{r}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|-1}\theta_{i_{q^{\prime}}}\theta_{j_{r^{\prime}}}\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}\setminus\{q^{\prime}\}}\theta_{i_{q}}\theta_{k_{q}}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\{r^{\prime}\}}\theta_{j_{r}}\theta_{\ell_{r}}+O(p^{|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|}),

when
k_{q^{\prime}}=j_{r^{\prime}}
and
\ell_{r^{\prime}}=i_{q^{\prime}}
for some
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}
and
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
,
along with the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}=n
.

Therefore, if
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}([t])=\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})=\emptyset
for all
\mathcal{R}\subset[t]
, and we can show the first part of (
B.3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}\prod_{r^{\prime}\in[t]\setminus\mathcal{R}}(-\theta_{j_{r^{\prime}}})\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\widehat{\theta}_{i_{q}},\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\widehat{\theta}_{j_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}\prod_{r^{\prime}\in[t]\setminus\mathcal{R}}(-\theta_{j_{r^{\prime}}})\left(\frac{|\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{R}|}{n^{2}p}\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\theta_{i_{q}}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\theta_{j_{r}}+O(n^{-2})\vee O(n^{-3}p^{-2})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{Q}|}{n^{2}p}\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\theta_{i_{q}}\prod_{r\in[t]}\theta_{j_{r}}\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}(-1)^{t-|\mathcal{R}|}|\mathcal{R}|+O(n^{-2})\vee O(n^{-3}p^{-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(n^{-2})\vee O(n^{-3}p^{-2}),\quad\textrm{if }t>1.

The last equality holds because when
t>1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}(-1)^{t-|\mathcal{R}|}|\mathcal{R}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|\mathcal{R}|=0}^{t}{t\choose|\mathcal{R}|}(-1)^{t-|\mathcal{R}|}|\mathcal{R}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\sum_{|\mathcal{R}|=1}^{t}{t-1\choose|\mathcal{R}|-1}(-1)^{t-|\mathcal{R}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\cdot(1-1)^{t-1}=0.

Now let us turn to the other case where
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}([t])\neq\emptyset
.
Let
c_{i}(\mathcal{Q})=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\bm{1}\{i_{q}=i\}

be the multiplicity of
i
in
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})
,
and
d_{j}(\mathcal{R})=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\bm{1}\{j_{r}=j\}

be the multiplicity of
j
in
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})
, we can rewrite the left hand side of (
B.3
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}\prod_{r^{\prime}\in[t]\setminus\mathcal{R}}(-\theta_{j_{r^{\prime}}})\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\widehat{\theta}_{i_{q}},\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\widehat{\theta}_{j_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{R}\subset[t]}(-1)^{t-|\mathcal{R}|}\prod_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}([t])}\theta_{j^{\prime}}^{d_{j^{\prime}}([t])-d_{j^{\prime}}(\mathcal{R})}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})}\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c_{i}(\mathcal{Q})},\quad\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{d_{j}(\mathcal{R})}\right).

Denote the set
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})=\{i_{1},\ldots,\,i_{|\mathcal{I}|}\}
, and
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})=\{j_{1},\ldots,\,j_{|\mathcal{J}|}\}
. We first show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})}\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{c_{i}(\mathcal{Q})},\quad\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{d_{j}(\mathcal{R})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n-1)p)^{-(|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|)}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\left[\prod_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\setminus\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\left(\sum_{u_{i^{\prime}}=1}^{n}A_{i^{\prime}u_{i^{\prime}}}\right)^{c_{i^{\prime}}(\mathcal{Q})}\right]\left[\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\left(\sum_{u_{i}=1}^{n}A_{iu_{i}}\right)^{c_{i}(\mathcal{Q})}\right],\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.\left[\prod_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})\setminus\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})}\left(\sum_{v_{j^{\prime}}=1}^{n}A_{j^{\prime}v_{j^{\prime}}}\right)^{d_{j^{\prime}}(\mathcal{R})}\right]\left[\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\left(\sum_{v_{j}=1}^{n}A_{jv_{j}}\right)^{d_{j}(\mathcal{R})}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n-1)p)^{-(|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|)}\sum_{u_{i_{1}}^{(1)}}\ldots\sum_{u_{i_{1}}^{(c_{i_{1}}(\mathcal{Q}))}}\ldots\sum_{u_{i_{|\mathcal{I}|}}^{(1)}}\ldots\sum_{u_{i_{|\mathcal{I}|}}^{(c_{i_{|\mathcal{I}|}}(\mathcal{Q}))}}\sum_{v_{j_{1}}^{(1)}}\ldots\sum_{v_{j_{1}}^{(d_{j_{1}}(\mathcal{R}))}}\ldots\sum_{v_{j_{|\mathcal{J}|}}^{(1)}}\ldots\sum_{v_{j_{|\mathcal{J}|}}^{(d_{j_{|\mathcal{J}|}}(\mathcal{R}))}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\prod_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\setminus\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\prod_{\ell=1}^{c_{i^{\prime}}(\mathcal{Q})}A_{i^{\prime}u_{i^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\prod_{\ell=1}^{c_{i}(\mathcal{Q})}A_{iu_{i}^{(\ell)}},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad\quad\prod_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})\setminus\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d_{j^{\prime}}(\mathcal{R})}A_{j^{\prime}v_{j^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}}\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d_{j}(\mathcal{R})}A_{jv_{j}^{(\ell)}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\bm{1}\left\{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})\cap\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{c_{k^{\prime}}(\mathcal{Q})}\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}=1}^{d_{k^{\prime}}(\mathcal{R})}\bm{1}\{u_{k^{\prime}}^{(\ell^{\prime})}=v_{k^{\prime}}^{(\ell^{\prime\prime})}\}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad+\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{Q})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{R})\\
j^{\prime\prime}\neq i^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{c_{i^{\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{Q})}\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}=1}^{d_{j^{\prime\prime}}(\mathcal{R})}\bm{1}\left\{i^{\prime\prime}=v_{j^{\prime\prime}}^{(\ell^{\prime\prime})},j^{\prime\prime}=u_{i^{\prime\prime}}^{(\ell^{\prime})}\right\}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n-1)p)^{-(|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|)}\left(1+O((np)^{-1})\right)

\mathrm{Var}_{\widehat{P}}(Y)\asymp(np)^{-1}
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{\widehat{P}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-2}p^{-4}\sum_{j^{\prime}<k^{\prime},\,j^{\prime}\neq i,\,k^{\prime}\neq i}\mathbf{1}\{\{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}\cap\{j,k\}\neq\emptyset\}\mathrm{Cov}(A_{ij}A_{ik},A_{ij^{\prime}}A_{ik^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\asymp

\displaystyle(np)^{-1},

since there are only
n
non-zero terms in the sum above. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[X/Y]
is dominated by the first term
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[X]/\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[Y]
,

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}\left[\frac{A_{ij}A_{ik}}{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}A_{ij}A_{ik}}\right]=n^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}}[X/Y]=\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{j}\widehat{\theta}_{k}}{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}\widehat{\theta}_{j}\widehat{\theta}_{k}}\left(1+O\left((np)^{-1}\right)\right).

As a result,

\mu_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}(\widehat{P})=p\left(\frac{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{2}\widehat{\theta}_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}\widehat{\theta}_{j}\widehat{\theta}_{k}}\right)\left(1+O\left((np)^{-1}\right)\right).

Denote
\psi(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}\widehat{\theta}_{j}^{2}\widehat{\theta}_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{j<k,\,j\neq i,\,k\neq i}\widehat{\theta}_{j}\widehat{\theta}_{k}}
. Then
\mu_{\mathrm{CL}}^{(i)}(\widehat{P})=p\psi(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\left(1+O\left((np)^{-1}\right)\right)
. Thus, we only need to show
\mathrm{Var}_{P}\left(\psi(\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\right)=O(n^{-2}p^{-1})
.

For the rest of the proof, every expectation will be taken w.r.t.
P

P

,
\mathrm{Var}_{P}

(
1

(103)

where

. Therefore, by Lemma
