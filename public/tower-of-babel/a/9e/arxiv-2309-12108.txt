Wavelet-based Edge Multiscale Finite Element Methods for Singularly Perturbed Convection-Diffusion Equations Thanks: Submitted to the editors May 3, 2024.
Shubin Fu, Eric Chung, Guanglian Li

Abstract
We propose a novel efficient and robust Wavelet-based Edge Multiscale Finite Element Method (WEMsFEM) motivated by [ 16 , 26 ] to solve the singularly perturbed convection-diffusion equations. The main idea is to first establish a local splitting of the solution over a local region by a local bubble part and local Harmonic extension part, and then derive a global splitting by means of Partition of Unity. This facilitates a representation of the solution as a summation of a global bubble part and a global Harmonic extension part, where the first part can be computed locally in parallel. To approximate the second part, we construct an edge multiscale ansatz space locally with hierarchical bases as the local boundary data that has a guaranteed approximation rate both inside and outside of the layers. The key innovation of this proposed WEMsFEM lies in a provable convergence rate with little restriction on the mesh size. Its convergence rate with respect to the computational degree of freedom is rigorously analyzed, which is verified by extensive 2-d and 3-d numerical tests.

Introduction

This paper focuses on solving singularly perturbed convection diffusion equation. Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=1,2,3
) be an open bounded domain with a Lipschitz boundary. Then we seek a function
u\in V:=H^{1}_{0}(D)
such that

\displaystyle-\epsilon\Delta u+\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }D,

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\partial D,

where the force term
f\in L^{2}(D)
,
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is a constant parameter
and the velocity field
\bm{b}\in L^{\infty}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We assume that the velocity field
\bm{b}
is incompressible, i.e.,
\nabla\cdot\bm{b}=0
.
The focus of this paper is on the convection-dominated regime or the singularly perturbed case
with large Péclet number
\mathrm{Pe}:=\left\|\bm{b}\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(D\right)}/\epsilon
.

Many important applications such as metamaterials, porous media and fluid mechanics involves multiple scales. For example, metamaterials are periodic artificial material with the size of each cell being on a subwavelength scale that possesses desirable properties which are not available for the natural material. Another example arises from singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problem (
1
), whose solution has high oscillation in the form of boundary layer or internal layers. Due to this disparity of scales, the classical numerical treatment becomes prohibitively expensive
and even intractable for many multiscale applications. Nonetheless, motivated by the broad spectrum of practical applications, a large number of multiscale model reduction techniques, e.g., Multiscale Finite Element Methods (MsFEMs),
Heterogeneous Multiscale Methods (HMMs), Variational Multiscale Methods (VMM), flux norm approach, Generalized Multiscale Finite Element Methods (GMsFEMs) and Localized Orthogonal Decomposition (LOD), have been proposed in the literature
[
3
,
9
,
13
,
14
,
22
,
24
,
29
]
over the last few decades.
[
1
,
9
,
31
]
for recent overviews. They have achieved great success in the efficient and accurate simulation of problems involving multiple scales.

The singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problem (
1
) is numerically challenging due to the presence of boundary layers or internal layers with width of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, which are usually exponential. To obtain a reasonable macroscale solution, one has to resolve all these multiple scales hidden in the solution
u
, encoded as the layers or high oscillatory feature, using a very fine mesh, which is computational extremely infeasible.
To reduce the computational complexity, one category of numerical methods is based upon stabilization, albeit at the loss of accuracy especially near the layers
[
5
,
10
,
23
,
25
,
27
,
33
]
. Another category is to incorporate the information of the layers in the form of local multiscale basis functions into the trial space
[
4
,
10
,
32
,
34
]
. These methods usually achieve good accuracy even inside the layers. The objective of this paper is to develop a multiscale method that falls into the second category such that it achieves high accuracy not only outside of the layers, but also near the layers.

In this paper, we develop a novel Wavelet-based Edge Multiscale Finite Element Method (WEMsFEM)
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
26
]
which relies an edge multiscale ansatz space that has a good approximation property with very low requirement on the solution, and thereby bypass the requirement on a very fine mesh. This edge multiscale ansatz space is constructed in each local domain by solving a homogeneous convection-dominated diffusion equation with hierarchical bases as Dirichlet boundary data and then use partition of unity functions to glue the local basis functions together as global multiscale basis functions. WEMsFEM shares a certain similarity with the Exponentially Convergent Multiscale Finite Element Method (ExpMsFEM)
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
in that both methods utilize the approximation over the coarse skeleton to define the local multiscale basis functions. The most striking differences are twofold. Firstly, WEMsFEM can be regarded as a variant of the Partition of Unity Finite Element Methods (PUFEM)
[
30
]
, which decomposes the global approximation space into summation of local approximation spaces, while ExpMsFEM uses edge localization and coupling to communicate local and global approximations. Secondly, the local multiscale space in WEMsFEM is established by wavelets or hierarchical bases, and it inherits their intrinsic hierarchical structure. Consequently, there is no need for a further model reduction or deriving important modes by means of Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).

2.1
Weak formulation and
a priori
estimate

We define the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
on
V\times V
associated to (
1
) by

\displaystyle a(u,v):=\epsilon\int_{D}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{D}(\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in V.

(2)

Since
\nabla\cdot\bm{b}=0
, an integration by parts implies that
the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is
V
-elliptic, i.e.,

\displaystyle a(v,v)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }v\in V.

(3)

Nevertheless, this
V
-ellipticity vanishes when
\epsilon\to 0
, which makes the standard finite element method unstable.

An application of the Poincarè-Friedrichs inequality leads to the boundedness of the bilinear form,

\displaystyle|a(u,v)|\leq{{\rm C}}({\rm{D}},{\bm{b}})\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}.

Here, the positive constant
{{\rm C}}({\rm{D}},{\bm{b}})
may depend on the diameter of the domain
D
and the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the velocity
\bm{b}
.

The weak formulation for problem (
1
) is to find
u\in V
such that

\displaystyle a(u,v)=(f,v)_{D}\quad\text{for all }v\in V.

(4)

Furthermore, this weak solution
u
is Hölder continuous,

Proposition 1
(Hölder estimate).

Let
d\leq 3
. For some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there holds

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}({\overline{D}})}\leq\mathrm{C}(\alpha)\left(\mathrm{Pe}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}+\epsilon^{-1}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}\right).

(5)

Here, the positive constant
\mathrm{C}(\alpha)
is independent of
\epsilon
.

Proof.

Note that Problem (
1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle-\Delta u=\epsilon^{-1}\left(-{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla u+f\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ in }D,

\displaystyle u=0

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ on }\partial D.

On the one hand, the right hand side can be bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|-\epsilon^{-1}{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla u+\epsilon^{-1}f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}\leq\mathrm{Pe}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}+\epsilon^{-1}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}.

(6)

On the other hand, the Hölder estimate for Laplacian
[
20
, Remark 6.5]
leads to

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}({\overline{D}})}\leq\mathrm{C}(\alpha)\left\|-\epsilon^{-1}{\bm{b}}\cdot\nabla u+\epsilon^{-1}f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}

for some constant
\mathrm{C}(\alpha)
depends only on the domain
D
and its boundary
\partial D
. Together with (
6
), we obtain the desired assertion.

2.2
Finite Element discretization

To discretize problem (
1
),
let
\mathcal{T}^{H}
be a regular quasi-uniform partition of the domain
D
into quadrilaterals (2d) or cubes (3d) with a mesh size
H
. For ease of presentation, we denote the mesh Péclet number
{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}
of the coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}^{H}
by

{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}:=H\|{\bm{b}}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}/\epsilon.

The vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{H}

are denoted by
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, with
N
being the total number of coarse nodes.
The coarse neighborhood associated with the node
O_{i}
is denoted by

\omega_{i}:=\text{int}\bigcup\{\overline{K}:K\in\mathcal{T}^{H}\text{ satisfying }O_{i}\in\overline{K}\}.

(7)

The overlapping constant
C_{\mathrm{ov}}
is defined by

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{ov}}:=\max\limits_{K\in\mathcal{T}^{H}}\#\{O_{i}:K\subset\omega_{i}\text{ for }i=1,2,\cdots,N\}.

(8)

We assume that
\epsilon\ll 1
is a small parameter, and that the coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}^{H}
fails to resolve the small parameter, i.e.,
{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}\gg 1
.

Over the coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}^{H}
, let
V_{H}
be the conforming piecewise
bilinear finite element space,

V_{H}:=\{v\in\mathcal{C}(D):V|_{T}\in{\mathbb{Q}_{1}}\text{ for all }T\in\mathcal{T}^{H}\},

where
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
denotes the space of bilinear polynomials. Then the classical Galerkin approximation of Problem (
4
) reads as finding
u_{H}\in V_{H}
, satisfying

\displaystyle a(u_{H},v_{H})=(f,v_{H})_{D}\quad\text{ for all }v_{H}\in V_{H}.

(9)

A straightforward calculation leads to the following estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{H})\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon})\min\limits_{w\in V_{H}}\left\|\nabla(u-w)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}.

Then a standard interpolation property implies

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{H})\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon})H\|u\|_{H^{2}(D)}.

(10)

Here, and throughout this paper, the notation
A\lesssim B
means
A\leq cB
for some benign constant that does not depend on the small parameter
\epsilon
and the mesh size
H
. On each coarse neighborhood
\omega_{i}
, the local bilinear form
a_{\omega_{i}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined analogous to (
2
) by replacing the global domain
D
with
\omega_{i}
.

Remark 2
.

If additional stabilization in the spirit of the Streamline Upwind Petrov-Galerkin (SUPG)
[
23
]
is adopted in (
9
), then the error estimate (
10
) can be improved by replacing
{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}
with
\sqrt{{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}}
.

Even though the hidden constant in the estimate above is independent of the small parameter
\epsilon
,
\|u\|_{H^{2}(D)}
relies on

.

3.1
Local-Global splitting

To start, note that the solution
u
from (
1
) satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}u{:=-\epsilon\Delta u+\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u}

\displaystyle=f\quad

\displaystyle\text{ in }\omega_{i},

which can be split into the summation of two parts, namely

\displaystyle u|_{\omega_{i}}=u^{i,\mathrm{I}}+u^{i,\mathrm{II}}.

(12)

Here, the two components
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}
and
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}
are respectively given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{I}}&=f\quad&&\text{ in }\omega_{i}\\
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}&=0\quad&&\text{ on }\partial\omega_{i},\end{aligned}\right.

(13)

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}&=0\quad&&\text{ in }\omega_{i}\\
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}&=u\quad&&\text{ on }\partial\omega_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

(14)

This implies that
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}
contains the local information of the source term
f
, and
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}
encodes the global information over the coarse skeleton
\mathcal{E}^{H}:=\cup\{\partial K:K\in\mathcal{T}^{H}\}
.
Note that
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}
can be solved in each coarse neighborhood
\omega_{i}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,N
in parallel. In practice, it can be solved by the stabilized methods such as the SUPG with sufficient accuracy.

Next, we introduce a partition of unity
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
subordinate to the cover
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to induce a global decomposition from (
12
).
Recall that
O_{i}
denotes the ith coarse node in the coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}^{H}
. We assume this Partition of Unity
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
satisfies the following properties:

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\text{supp}(\chi_{i})}&\subset\omega_{i}\\
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}&=1\text{ in }D\\
\|\chi_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\omega_{i})}&\leq C_{\infty}\\
\|\nabla\chi_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\omega_{i})}&\leq C_{\text{G}}H^{-1}\end{aligned}\right.

for some positive constants
C_{\infty}
and
C_{\text{G}}
, which is named the
(C_{\mathrm{ov}},C_{\infty},C_{\text{G}})
partition of unity
[
30
]
.
For example, the standard bilinear basis functions on the coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}^{H}
are the partition of unity function with
C_{\infty}=C_{\text{G}}=1
, which are utilised in our numerical tests. Note that there are many other alternatives for the partition of unity functions besides using this bilinear basis functions. For instance, one can utilize the flat top type of partition of unity functions, cf.
[
19
]
.

Consequently, we can represent
u
as a summation of local parts,

\displaystyle u=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\right)u=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\chi_{i}u|_{\omega_{i}}\right).

Inserting the local splitting (
12
), we derive

\displaystyle u=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\left(u^{i,\mathrm{I}}+u^{i,\mathrm{II}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{I}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}

\displaystyle:=u^{\mathrm{I}}+u^{\mathrm{II}}.

(15)

Here, the first term

\displaystyle u^{\mathrm{I}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{I}}

(16)

denotes the global bubble part which contains information of the source term
f
, and the second term

\displaystyle u^{\mathrm{II}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}.

(17)

is the global Harmonic extension part that encodes global information over the coarse skeleton
\mathcal{E}^{H}
.

Since the global bubble part
u^{\mathrm{I}}
can be retrieved locally, our main objective (
11
) is reduced to seeking a good approximative space
V_{\text{ms},\ell}
for
u^{\mathrm{II}}
in the sense that the following quantity has a certain decay rate,

\displaystyle\min\limits_{w\in V_{\text{ms},\ell}}\left\|\nabla(u^{\mathrm{II}}-w)\right\|_{L^{2}\left(D\right)}.

(18)

To obtain this approximative space
V_{\text{ms},\ell}
, we aim to design global multiscale basis functions taking the same form as (
17
). Since the partition of unity
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is known, then we only need to design local multiscale basis functions to approximate
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}
(
14

is completely determined by the boundary data

, hence the question is reduced to seeking a good approximation to
u|_{\partial\omega_{i}}
. Note also that if we can obtain a proper approximation to

, this approximation property can transfer into the local neighborhood

thanks to the

estimate established in

In short, we need to approximate

, which, however, has restrictive regularity as stated in

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(V_{i,\ell}):=\text{span}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(\psi_{i,\ell}^{j}):1\leq j\leq 2^{\ell+2}\right\}.

(21)

Here,

is the solution to the following local problem,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{i}v&:=-\epsilon\Delta v+\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v=0\quad\mbox{in }\omega_{i},\\
v&=\psi_{i,\ell}^{j}\quad\mbox{on }\partial\omega_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

(22)

By this construction, the dimension of
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(V_{i,\ell})
equals

. In practice, a number of

local problems (

) is solved by the stabilized methods such as the SUPG with sufficient accuracy to obtain the local multiscale space

, which can be solved in parallel and thus has a much lower computational complexity than the original problem (
1
).

Finally, the edge multiscale ansatz space is defined by the Partition of Unity
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,

\displaystyle V_{\text{ms},\ell}:=\text{span}\left\{\chi_{i}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(\psi_{i,\ell}^{j}):\,\,1\leq i\leq N\text{ and }1\leq j\leq 2^{\ell+2}\right\}.

(23)

4
Wavelet-based Edge Multiscale Finte Element Method

We present in this section the Wavelet-based Edge Multiscale Finte Element Method (WEMsFEM) to solve (

To get a good approximation to

\displaystyle a\left(u_{\text{ms},\ell}^{\mathrm{II}},v_{\text{ms},\ell}\right)=(f,v_{\text{ms},\ell})_{D}-a(u^{\mathrm{I}},v_{\text{ms},\ell})\quad\text{ for all }v_{\text{ms},\ell}\in V_{\text{ms},\ell}.

Remark 7
.

The global estimate in (

Conclusions

We develop a novel Wavelet-based Edge Multiscale Finite Element Method (WEMsFEM) to solve singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problems. The key feature of this approach is to introduce an edge multiscale ansatz space which has guaranteed approximation properties both inside and outside of the layers. The construction of this edge multiscale ansatz space is motivated by the local and global splitting of the solution, and their basis functions are solved locally using hierarchical bases as the Dirichlet data. The convergence of WEMsFEM with respect to the level parameter in the hierarchical bases is rigorously justified, which is confirmed numerically for a wide range of 2-d or 3-d convection-dominated diffusion problems with large Péclet number, including high oscillation and boundary layers.

Note that the construction of the edge multiscale ansatz space involves solving local problems, and standard stabilized methods such as SUPG on regular quasi-uniform mesh are utilized for the sake of simplicity. Consequently, our proposed WEMsFEM can not handle the case with extremely large Péclet number if no additional mesh or multiscale ansatz space adaptivivity is developed. We will investigate in the future on the coarse grid adaptivity and local multiscale space adaptivity to handle such challenging issues.

Moreover, we only consider an incompressible velocity in this paper. If the velocity
{\bm{b}}\in L^{\infty}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is compressible, then the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot):V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is possibly no longer coercive in the case of large Péclet number we are interested in. Under such scenario, we have to replace the Galerkin orthogonality with Schatz argument to derive the quasi-optimality established in Theorem 5.2. We expect our proposed method should also work in such case, and rigorous derivation will be carried out in the future.

Appendix A
Very-weak solutions to singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problems

In this appendix, we derive an
L^{2}(\omega_{i})
-estimate for boundary valued singularly perturbed convection-diffusion problem with
\omega_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
being a bounded open domain with a Lipschitz boundary for
d=2,3
, which plays a crucial role in the error analysis.
Let
\omega_{i}
be a coarse neighborhood for any

i=1,\cdots,N
. For any
g\in L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)
, we define the following convection diffusion problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{i}v:=-\epsilon\Delta v+\bm{b}\cdot\nabla v&=0&&\text{ in }\omega_{i},\\
v&=g&&\text{ on }\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D\\
v&=0&&\text{ on }\partial\omega_{i}\cap\partial D.\end{aligned}\right.

(38)

Our goal is to derive the
L^{2}(\omega_{i})
-estimate of the solution
v
. To this end, we employ a nonstandard variational form in the spirit
of the transposition method
[
28
]
. Denote the dual operator of
\mathcal{L}_{i}
as
\mathcal{L}_{i}^{*}
. Since
\nabla\cdot\bm{b}=0
, we obtain
\mathcal{L}_{i}^{*}=-\epsilon\Delta-\bm{b}\cdot\nabla
. Let the test space
X(\omega_{i})\subset H^{1}_{0}(\omega_{i})
be defined by

\displaystyle X(\omega_{i}):=\{z:\mathcal{L}_{i}^{*}z\in L^{2}(\omega_{i})\text{ and }z\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega_{i})\}.

(39)

This test space
X(\omega_{i})
is endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X(\omega_{i})}
:

\forall z\in X(\omega_{i}):\|z\|_{X(\omega_{i})}^{2}=\int_{\omega_{i}}|\nabla z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\omega_{i}}|\mathcal{L}_{i}^{*}z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Then we propose the following weak formulation corresponding to Problem (
38
):
seeking
v\in L^{2}(\omega_{i})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\omega_{i}}v\mathcal{L}_{i}^{*}(z)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\epsilon\int_{\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D}g\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{ for all }z\in X(\omega_{i}).

(40)

The remaining of this section is devoted to proving the well posedness of the nonstandard variational formulation (
40
) and deriving the
a priori
error estimate. Our final result is the following,

Theorem 11
.

Given
g\in L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)
. Let
v
be the solution to (
40
), then there exists a constant
{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}
independent of the parameter
\epsilon
such that

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}\leq{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}({\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{-1/2}+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{1/2})H^{1/2}}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)}.

To prove
theorem

11
, one has to first derive the
L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i})
-estimate of the normal trace
\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}
for any
z\in X(\omega_{i})
. This is established in the following theorem:

Theorem 12
.

Let
w\in L^{2}(\omega_{i})
and let
z\in X(\omega_{i})
satisfy

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{i}^{*}(z):=-\epsilon\Delta z-\bm{b}\cdot\nabla z&=w&&\text{ in }\omega_{i},\\
z&=0&&\text{ on }\partial\omega_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

(41)

Then for some constant
{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}
independent of the parameter
\epsilon
and
H
, there holds

\displaystyle\|\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon})}\left(\|\bm{b}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\epsilon\right)^{-1/2}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}.

This constant
{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}
can change value from context to context.

Proof.

Note that
z\in X(\omega_{i})
is the unique solution to the following weak formulation

\displaystyle\forall q\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega_{i}):\int_{\omega_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{*}(z)q\;\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\omega_{i}}wq\,\mathrm{d}x.

Taking
q:=z
and applying integration by parts, we arrive at

\displaystyle\epsilon\left\|\nabla z\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}^{2}=\int_{\omega_{i}}wz\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
z|_{\partial\omega_{i}}=0
, then an application of the Poincaré inequality leads to

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla z\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}\lesssim{\frac{H}{\epsilon}}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}.

(42)

Recall that the mesh Péclet number
{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}
of the coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}^{H}
is

{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}:=H\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}/\epsilon
. Consequently, plugging this estimate into (
41
) results in

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta z\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}\lesssim\epsilon^{-1}(1+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon})\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}.

By the so-called Miranda-Talenti estimate on a convex domain
[
21
]
, we can obtain the following
a priori
estimate

\displaystyle{|{z}|_{H^{2}(\omega_{i})}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Delta z\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}\lesssim\epsilon^{-1}(1+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon})\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}.

(43)

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}=\nabla z\cdot n.

Next, we invoke a quantitative trace theorem on a bounded open domain with a Lipschitz boundary
[
21
, Theorem 1.5.1.10]
to obtain an estimate of
\left\|\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i})}
, which states,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial z}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{1/2}H^{1/2}|{z}|_{H^{2}(\omega_{i})}+\delta^{-1/2}H^{-1/2}\|\nabla z\|_{L^{2}(\omega_{i})}\text{ for any }\delta\in(0,1)

with the hidden constant independent of the size of the domain
\omega_{i}
.

Since we are interested in the case with
\epsilon\ll H
, then taking
\delta:={\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{-1}
and combining with (
42
) and (
43
), we derive the desired assertion.

Thanks to this
a priori
estimate presented in Theorem
12
, we are ready to state the well posedness of the nonstandard variational formulation, cf. (
40
),

Lemma 13
.

Let
v
be the solution to problem (
40
) and let the test space
X(\omega_{i})
be defined in (
39
). Then the nonstandard variational form (
40
) is well posed.

Proof.

To prove the well-posedness of the nonstandard variational form (
40
), we introduce a bilinear form

c(\cdot,\cdot)
on
L^{2}(\omega_{i})\times L^{2}(\omega_{i})
and a linear form
b(\cdot)
on
L^{2}(\omega_{i})
, defined by

\displaystyle c(w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\omega_{i}}w_{1}w_{2}\;\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }w_{1},w_{2}\in L^{2}(\omega_{i})

\displaystyle r(w)

\displaystyle:=-\epsilon\int_{\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D}{\frac{\partial z(w)}{\partial n}}{g}\;\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{ for all }w\in L^{2}(\omega_{i}),

with
z(w)
being the unique solution to (
41
).
It follows from Theorem
12

and the definition of the mesh Péclet number
{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}
that

\displaystyle\|r\|:=\sup_{w\in L^{2}(\omega_{i})}\frac{r(w)}{\|w\|_{L^{2}(\omega_{i})}}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon})}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}}}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D\right)}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}({\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{-1/2}+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{1/2})H^{1/2}}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D\right)}.

(44)

This yields well-posedness of the following variational problem: find
v\in L^{2}(\omega_{i})
such that

\displaystyle c(v,w)=r(w)\quad\text{ for all }w\in L^{2}(\omega_{i}).

(45)

The equivalence of problems (
45
) and (
40
) implies the desired well-posedness of (
40
).

Finally, we are ready to prove the main result stated in
theorem

11
:

Proof of
theorem

11
.

For all
w\in L^{2}(\omega_{i})
, we obtain from (
44
) and (
45
):

\displaystyle\int_{\omega_{i}}vw\;\,\mathrm{d}x:=c(v,w)

\displaystyle=r(w)

\displaystyle\leq\text{C}_{\text{weak}}({\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{-1/2}+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{1/2})H^{1/2}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)}.

Then this completes the proof by taking
w:=v
.

Furthermore, we present the
a priori
estimate in energy norm to facilitate the proofs in
section

5
. This estimate is standard and is commonly known as the Cacciappoli inquality, which can be found in many literature, e.g.,
[
2
,
26
]
.

Corollary 14
.

Let
g\in L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)
and let
v
be the solution to (
40
). Then there exists a constant
{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}
independent of the parameter
\epsilon
such that

\left\|\chi_{i}\nabla v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\omega_{i}\right)}\leq{\rm C}_{{\rm weak}}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}}+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{-1/2}\right)H^{-1/2}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)}.

Proof.

Recall that
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is a
(C_{\mathrm{ov}},C_{\infty},C_{\text{G}})
partition of unity subordinate to the cover
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, which implies that
\chi_{i}=0
on
\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D
. Multiplying (
38
) by
\chi_{i}^{2}v
and applying integration by parts, we arrive at

\displaystyle\epsilon\int_{\omega_{i}}\chi_{i}^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=-2\epsilon\int_{\omega_{i}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\chi_{i}\chi_{i}v\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\omega_{i}}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla\chi_{i}\chi_{i}v^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Then an application of the Young’s inequality leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\omega_{i}}\chi_{i}^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\left(H^{-2}+\frac{\|\bm{b}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}}{\epsilon H}\right)\|v\|_{L^{2}(\omega_{i})}^{2}.

Combining with
theorem

11
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\omega_{i}}\chi_{i}^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(1+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon})^{2}}\left(H^{-2}+\frac{\|\bm{b}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}}{\epsilon H}\right)\frac{\epsilon}{\|\bm{b}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim H^{-1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left({\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{2}+{\rm{Pe}}_{H,{\bm{b}},\epsilon}^{-1}\right)}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)}^{2}.

Then the desired assertion follows after taking the square root, and this completes the proof.
