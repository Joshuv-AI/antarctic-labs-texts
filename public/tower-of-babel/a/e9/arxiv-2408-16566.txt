Approximation Algorithms for Correlated Knapsack Orienteering
David Alemán Espinosa, Chaitanya Swamy

Abstract
We consider the correlated knapsack orienteering ( 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} ) problem: we are given a travel budget B B , processing-time budget W W , finite metric space ( V , d ) (V,d) with root ρ ∈ V \rho\in V , where each vertex is associated with a job with possibly correlated random size and random reward that become known only when the job completes. Random variables are independent across different vertices. The goal is to compute a ρ \rho -rooted path of length at most B B , in a possibly adaptive fashion, that maximizes the reward collected from jobs that are processed by time W W . To our knowledge, 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} has not been considered before, though prior work has considered the uncorrelated problem, stochastic knapsack orienteering , and correlated orienteering , which features only one budget constraint on the sum of travel-time and processing-times. Gupta et al. [ GuptaKNR12 ] observed that the uncorrelated version of this problem has a constant-factor adaptivity gap. We show that, perhaps surprisingly and in stark contrast to the uncorrelated problem, the adaptivity gap of 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} is not a constant, and is at least Ω ⁡ ( max ⁡ { log ⁡ B , log ⁡ log ⁡ W } ) \Omega\bigl(\max\{\sqrt{\log{B}},\sqrt{\log\log{W}}\}\bigr) . Complementing this result, we devise non-adaptive algorithms that obtain: (a) O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ W ) O(\log\log W) -approximation in quasi-polytime; and (b) O ⁡ ( log ⁡ W ) O(\log W) -approximation in polytime. This also establishes that the adaptivity gap for 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} is at most O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ W ) O(\log\log W) . We obtain similar guarantees for 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} with cancellations, wherein a job can be cancelled before its completion time, foregoing its reward. We show that an α \alpha -approximation for 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} implies an O ⁡ ( α ) O(\alpha) -approximation for 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} with cancellations. We also consider the special case of 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} , wherein job sizes are weighted Bernoulli distributions, and more generally where the distributions are supported on at most two points ( 𝟤 ​ 𝖢 ​ 𝗈 ​ 𝗋 ​ 𝗋 ​ 𝖪 ​ 𝖮 \mathsf{2CorrKO} ). Although weighted Bernoulli distributions suffice to yield an Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ B ) \Omega(\sqrt{\log\log B}) adaptivity-gap lower bound for (uncorrelated) stochastic orienteering , we show that they are easy instances for 𝖢𝗈𝗋𝗋𝖪𝖮 \mathsf{CorrKO} . We develop non-adaptive algorithms that achieve O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -approximation in polytime for weighted Bernoulli distributions, and in ( n + log ⁡ B ) O ⁡ ( log ⁡ W ) (n+\log B)^{O(\log W)} -time for the more general case of 𝟤 ​ 𝖢 ​ 𝗈 ​ 𝗋 ​ 𝗋 ​ 𝖪 ​ 𝖮 \mathsf{2CorrKO} . (This also implies that our adaptivity-gap lower-bound example, which uses distributions of support-size 3 3 , is tight in terms of the support-size of the distributions.) Finally, we leverage our techniques to provide a quasi-polynomial time O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ B ) O(\log\log B) approximation algorithm for correlated orienteering improving upon the approximation guarantee in [ BansalN14 ] .

Introduction

The
orienteering
problem, first introduced by
[
GoldenLV87
]
, is a
fundamental and widely-studied vehicle-routing problem (
\mathsf{VRP}
).
The input to the problem consists of a length/travel bound
B
, finite metric space

(V,d)
representing travel times, root vertex
\rho\in V
, and non-negative rewards
associated with the vertices. The goal is to compute a path rooted at
\rho
of length at most
B

that collects maximum reward.
Orienteering often arises as a subroutine in devising algorithms for other more complex
\mathsf{VRP}
s, both in
approximation algorithms
[
BC+94
,
FakcharoenpholHR07
,
ChakrabartyS16
,
NagarajanR12
,
FriggstadS14
,
FriggstadS17
,
BansalBCM04
]
,
as also in computational methods, where it
arises as the pricing problem when using a branch-cut-and-price method on a
set-covering/configuration LP.

Gupta et al.
[
GuptaKNR12
]
introduced the following
stochastic
version of
orienteering to model settings where one must spend some uncertain amount of time processing a visited node in order to collect its reward.
Formally, each vertex corresponds to a job with a random, possibly correlated, processing
time and reward, drawn from a given probability distribution.
Random variables corresponding to different vertices are independent.
The reward and processing time of a job become known only when the job is fully processed.
The goal is to devise an algorithm, also called
policy
, that visits a sequence of
vertices (starting at
\rho
) in a possibly
adaptive
fashion that maximizes the
expected total reward collected, subject to the constraint that the total time expended in
traveling and processing jobs is at most
B
.
Jobs cannot be
preempted, and only jobs completed by the time-horizon
B
yield reward.
This is the
correlated orienteering
(
\mathsf{CorrO}
) problem. We refer to the special case
where rewards and sizes are independent, simply as
stochastic orienteering
;
due to independence, this is equivalent to the setting with deterministic rewards
since one can replace the random rewards with their expectations.

A related problem, and the focus of this paper, is
correlated knapsack orienteering

(
\mathsf{CorrKO}
), wherein there are two separate budgets:
B
for the (deterministic) travel
time, and
W
for the total time spent in processing jobs. Again, we refer to the
uncorrelated problem as
stochastic knapsack orienteering
.
Correlated knapsack
orienteering can be motivated from a similar perspective as
\mathsf{CorrO}
. Indeed,
it is quite natural to decouple the “apples and oranges” entities of travel time and
processing time when these may represent disparate resources; e.g., travel time may represent
latency of access of jobs in a distributed network, and processing time may present CPU time.

In general, a policy may be
adaptive

and choose the next vertex to visit based on the (size, reward) realizations of the
vertices previously visited;
unless otherwise stated, the approximation ratio is always measured relative to
the maximum expected reward
\mathit{OPT}
that can be achieved by an adaptive policy.
On the other hand, a
non-adaptive
policy fixes beforehand the sequence of vertices
to visit, and only the stopping-point (when the time-horizon
B
is exceeded) depends on
the (size, reward) realizations. While adaptive policies may collect much greater reward,
non-adaptive policies are usually easier to implement, specify, and analyze, by virtue of
the fact that they admit a much-more compact description compared to the
decision tree associated with an adaptive policy, whose description may require space
that is exponential in the input size.
Consequently, much work in stochastic optimization has focused on
developing good non-adaptive policies and obtaining bounds on the

adaptivity gap
, which is the supremum, over all problem instances, of

\mathit{OPT}
/(maximum reward achieved by a non-adaptive policy); see
e.g.,
[
DeanGV08
,
GuhaM09
,
EneNS18
,
GuptaKMR11
,
GuptaKNR12
,
ChawlaGTTR20
,
JiangLLS20
]
.

To our knowledge,
there is no prior work on
\mathsf{CorrKO}
, but there has been work on two
special cases, stochastic knapsack orienteering (i.e., the uncorrelated version of
\mathsf{CorrKO}
),

correlated knapsack
(where all nodes are co-located), and the related problem,
\mathsf{CorrO}
.
Our current knowledge for these problems can be summarized as follows.
(1) Stochastic knapsack orienteering
[
GuptaKNR12
]
and correlated
knapsack
[
GuptaKMR11
]
both admit non-adaptive
O(1)
-approximation algorithms,
implying
O(1)
adaptivity gap for these problems.
(2) For stochastic orienteering (i.e., the uncorrelated version of
\mathsf{CorrO}
), the adaptivity
gap is
\Omega(\sqrt{\log\log B})

[
BansalN14
]
and
O(\log\log B)

[
GuptaKNR12
]
;
also, there is a non-adaptive algorithm that achieves an
O(1)
-approximation with respect
to
the non-adaptive optimum

[
GuptaKNR12
]
,
and hence obtains an
O(\log\log B)
-approximation.
(3) For
\mathsf{CorrO}
, the adaptivity gap is also
O(\log\log B)

[
BansalN14
]
, but this is
established non-constructively; the current best algorithmic results obtain

O(\alpha\log B)
-approximation in polytime
[
GuptaKNR12
]
and

O\bigl(\alpha\cdot\frac{\log^{2}\log B}{\log\log\log B}\bigr)
in
quasi-polytime
[
BansalN14
]
, where
\alpha
is the approximation ratio for

deadline-TSP
.

As is evident from this summary,
\mathsf{CorrO}
admits quite different guarantees compared to
stochastic knapsack orienteering and correlated knapsack, and this state of affairs
prompts the natural question: where does
\mathsf{CorrKO}
stand in terms of
difficulty relative to these problems? Is it more difficult than the uncorrelated
problem? How does it compare relative to
\mathsf{CorrO}
?

Our contributions

We initiate a study of correlated knapsack orienteering, and obtain results that, in
particular, shed light on these questions.
Our chief contributions are as follows.

1.

Adaptivity gap and approximation algorithms.

Somewhat surprisingly, and in stark contrast with (uncorrelated) stochastic knapsack
orienteering and correlated knapsack, we prove that

the adaptivity gap for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
is not a constant
, showing
that the correlated problem is strictly harder than the uncorrelated problem.

Theorem 1

(see Section
3
)
.

The adaptivity gap for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
is

\Omega\bigl(\max\{\sqrt{\log B},\sqrt{\log\log{W}}\}\bigr)
, where
B
is the travel
budget and
W
is the processing-time budget.

Complementing this lower bound, we develop various
non-adaptive

approximation algorithms for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
.
Our main algorithmic result is a

quasi-polytime
O(\log\log W)
-approximation algorithm
for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
,
which thus shows that the adaptivity gap is
O(\log\log W)
.

Theorem 2
.

There are non-adaptive algorithms for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
with the following guarantees:

(a)

O(\log\log W)
-approximation in time
(n+\log B)^{O(\log W\log\log W)}

(Section
4.1
);

(b)

O(\log W)
-approximation in polynomial time (Section
).

By leveraging the approach leading to Theorem
12 (a)
,
we also obtain
the following guarantee for correlated orienteering, which improves upon the approximation
guarantee in
[
BansalN14
]
(that also runs in quasi-polytime) by an

O\bigl(\frac{\log\log B}{\log\log\log B}\bigr)
-factor.

Theorem 3
.

Given an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for deadline TSP with running time
T
,
we can obtain a non-adaptive
O(\alpha\log\log{B})
-approximation algorithm for
\mathsf{CorrO}
with
running time
(n+\log{B})^{O(\log{B}\log\log{B})}\cdot T
.
Using the algorithm for deadline TSP in
[
FriggstadS21
]
, we obtain an

O(\log\log B)
-approximation in quasi-polytime.

2.

\mathsf{CorrKO}
with 2-point distributions.

Our adaptivity-gap lower bound uses distributions of support-size
3
, whereas the

\Omega(\sqrt{\log\log B})
adaptivity-gap lower-bound example for stochastic orienteering
in
[
BansalN14
]
considers
weighted Bernoulli size distributions
.

In Section
, we investigate
\mathsf{CorrKO}
from a fine-grained-complexity
perspective to understand this discrepancy.
In contrast with stochastic orienteering,
we show that when all distributions are supported on at most
2
points—we call this

\mathsf{2CorrKO}
—
the adaptivity gap becomes
O(1)
(Theorem
), and we can
obtain a
non-adaptive
O(1)
-approximation in time
(n+\log B)^{O(\log W)}

(Corollary
). Moreover, for
weighted Bernoulli size distributions, we can obtain a

polytime non-adaptive
O(1)
-approximation
(Theorem
).
Our key insight here lies in identifying a novel
deterministic
problem, that we call

orienteering with knapsack deadlines
(
\mathsf{OrientKD}
), which we show is equivalent
to
\mathsf{2CorrKO}
, up to constant factors. In
\mathsf{OrientKD}
, in addition to orienteering, each vertex
v
has a weight and
knapsack deadline, and an orienteering-solution
P
is feasible, if for every
v\in P
,
the total weight of all nodes on
P
up to (and including)
v
is at most its
knapsack deadline.
For instance, in a setting where jobs distributed over a network have to be processed on a
single machine, travel times could represent the latency involved in
accessing a job, and the knapsack deadlines would capture completion-time deadlines on the
machine.
We obtain the above approximation guarantee for
\mathsf{OrientKD}
(Section
),
and hence obtain the same guarantee (up to constant factors) for
\mathsf{2CorrKO}
.

Our results for
\mathsf{2CorrKO}
thus show that
our adaptivity-gap
example is tight in terms of the support-size of underlying distributions:

any
such lower-bound example
must

involve some distributions of support-size at least
3
.

3.

\mathsf{CorrKO}
with cancellations.

In this version of the problem (see Section
),
we may
cancel
(i.e., discard) the current vertex
v
at any time-step
prior to its completion, foregoing its reward, and we are not allowed to return to
v
.
We obtain the
same approximation guarantees for this problem as for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
: i.e.,
quasi-polytime
O(\log\log W)
-approximation, and polytime
O(\log W)
-approximation.
En route, we obtain an
O(1)
-approximation for the special case where
we obtain non-zero rewards only when jobs instantiate to size at most
W/2

(Theorem
).

Our results paint a nuanced picture of the complexity of
\mathsf{CorrKO}
vis-a-vis

\mathsf{CorrO}
and stochastic knapsack orienteering. While
\mathsf{CorrKO}
is harder than stochastic
knapsack orienteering, our algorithmic results suggest that
\mathsf{CorrKO}
is easier than
correlated orienteering. We obtain similar approximation factors for both problems
in quasi-polytime, but in polytime, we obtain approximation factor of
O(\log W)
for

\mathsf{CorrKO}
, while the current-best polytime factor for
\mathsf{CorrO}
is
O(\log n\log B)
; moreover, with
weighted Bernoulli distributions,

\mathsf{CorrKO}
is provably easier than
\mathsf{CorrO}
.

Technical overview

We highlight the key technical ideas underlying our results. Let
\mathit{OPT}
be the optimal
reward for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
. Let
S_{{v}}
denote the random size of vertex
v
. For an integer
j\geq 0
, let

X^{j}_{v}:=\min\{S_{{v}},2^{j}\}
and
\mu^{j}_{v}={\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[X^{j}_{v}\bigr]
.
The significance of these quantities is
that if
\mu^{j}(P_{\rho,v}-v)\leq c\cdot 2^{j}
, where
P
is a rooted path,
v\in P
,
and
P_{\rho,v}
is the
\rho\leadsto v
portion of
P
, then a random subpath
P^{\prime\prime}
of
P
where we retain each
u\in P
independently with probability

\frac{1}{2c}
satisfies
\Pr[\text{$v\in P^{\prime\prime}$ and starts being processed by time $2^{j}$}]\geq\frac{1}{4c}
; this indicates
that
\pi_{v}(2^{j})
, which is the expected reward of
v
if its processing starts by time

2^{j}
, can serve as a good proxy for the expected reward obtained from
v
.

Algorithms for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
and
\mathsf{CorrO}
.

Our quasi-polytime
O(\log\log W)
-approximation for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
builds upon a structural result
for
\mathsf{CorrO}
shown by
[
BansalN14
]
. Adapting their result (see
Lemma
) to the setting of
\mathsf{CorrKO}
immediately
yields that one can extract a suitable rooted path

Q^{*}
from the decision tree representing an optimal adaptive policy,
and suitable nodes
\varphi_{-1}=\rho,\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{k}
on
Q^{*}
, where

k\leq\log W
, such that (roughly speaking):
(a) the prefix property
\mu^{j}(Q^{*}_{\rho,\varphi_{j}}-\varphi_{j})\leq O(K)\cdot 2^{j}
holds for every

j=0,\ldots,k
, and (b)
\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{v\in Q^{*}_{\varphi_{j-1},\varphi_{j}}}\pi_{v}(2^{j})=\Omega(\mathit{OPT})
, where

K=O(\log\log W)
. So if we could find this path
Q^{*}
,
then using the sampling idea
described above, one can easily obtain an
O(K)
-approximation. For
\mathsf{CorrO}
, Bansal and
Nagarajan
[
BansalN14
]
“guess” the
portal nodes

\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{k}
and write
a configuration LP to find suitable paths between every pair of consecutive portal
nodes. They use randomized rounding to round a fractional solution, which incurs a
\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}
-factor violation of the prefix property due to
Chernoff bounds, since
for each
j
,
\mu^{j}(Q^{*}_{\rho,\varphi_{j}}-\varphi_{j})
can be written as a sum of
O(K)\cdot 2^{j}
-bounded independent random variables.
When one combines this with the node-sampling step, one therefore incurs an
O\bigl(K\cdot\frac{\log k}{\log\log k}\bigr)
-factor loss
relative to the value of the LP solution.

For
\mathsf{CorrKO}
(and
\mathsf{CorrO}
), we proceed similarly, but we guess many more portal vertices.
We split each
Q^{*}_{\varphi_{j-1},\varphi_{j}}
into
O(K)
segments having

\mu^{j}
-weight at most
2^{j}
, and guess the end-points of all such segments (see
Theorems
14
,
15
).
We then again set up a configuration LP and use randomized rounding; however, we
can now ensure that the prefix property holds with
O(1)
violation, since we can
decompose
\mu^{j}(Q^{*}_{\rho,\varphi_{j}}-\varphi_{j})
into a
sum of
2^{j}
-bounded random variables

corresponding to the
\mu^{j}
-weight of each random segment. Thus, an application of
Chernoff bounds and the union bound only incurs an
O(1)
-factor violation of the prefix
property, since
K=\Omega(\log k)
; therefore, we lose only an
O(K)
-factor compared to
the value of the LP solution. This idea also underlies our improved approximation for

\mathsf{CorrO}
. The only essential difference from
\mathsf{CorrKO}
comes from how well we can solve
the corresponding configuration LP; for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
, we can obtain an
O(1)
-approximation to
the LP-optimum using an
O(1)
-approximation algorithm for knapsack orienteering (see below),
but for
\mathsf{CorrO}
, we obtain an
O(\alpha)
-approximate LP solution given an

\alpha
-approximation for deadline TSP.

The
O(\log W)
-approximation for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
proceeds by
relating the problem to
knapsack orienteering
(
\mathsf{KnapOrient}
), which is orienteering with
an additional budget constraint on the total node-weight of the path.
For each index
j=0,1,\ldots,\log W
, we use the portion of the optimal
adaptive-policy tree corresponding to nodes processed at some point in
[2^{j},2^{j+1})
,
to extract a good
fractional solution to an
LP-relaxation (
KO-LP
) for
\mathsf{KnapOrient}
, where we exploit the LP-relaxation for
orienteering in
[
FriggstadS17
]
. This translation is quite easy because one can
naturally interpret the LP variables as corresponding to certain probabilities obtained
from an adaptive policy.

We remark that one can combine the LP-relaxations for orienteering
[
FriggstadS17
]
and
the
correlated knapsack
problem
[
GuptaKMR11
]
, which is the special
case where all nodes are co-located, to obtain an LP for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
. However, the
chief impediment in rounding an LP solution is that the rounding algorithms for
orienteering and correlated knapsack may give rise to incompatible orderings.
Rounding the orienteering-portion of the LP solution yields a node sequence, and we
need to stick with a subsequence of this to satisfy the travel-budget
constraint. However, forcing one to consider items in a prescribed order for correlated
knapsack can drastically reduce the reward obtained, because jobs that yield reward only
when they instantiate to
large sizes (i.e.,
>W/2
) may need to be processed in a different incompatible order; see
Appendix
.
This tension is real, as evidenced by our adaptivity-gap lower bound, and seems challenging
to deal with.

\mathsf{2CorrKO}
.

For
\mathsf{2CorrKO}
, the chief insight is that the problematic case where we obtain reward only
from large-size instantiations becomes quite structured in two ways.
(1) There is no adaptivity gap (Theorem
), since only the path in the
adaptive-policy tree corresponding to small-size (i.e.,
\leq W/2
)
instantiations can yield non-zero reward. (2) Given (1), one can infer that the reward obtained from a vertex
v
is a function of the total small size of all
vertices visited up to
v
, and the total probability mass of vertices visited up to
v

(see Claim
).
This allows one to define an instance of
orienteering with knapsack deadlines

(
\mathsf{OrientKD}
) to capture the stochastic problem.

\mathsf{CorrKO}
with cancellations.

The algorithm for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
with cancellations (
\mathsf{CorrKO}\text{-}\mathsf{Cancel}
) considers two cases. For the
large-size instantiations, it is not hard to argue that cancellations do not help (as with
correlated knapsack
[
GuptaKMR11
]
). For the small-size instantiations, we formulate an
LP by combining the LPs for orienteering
[
FriggstadS17
]
and correlated knapsack with
cancellations
[
GuptaKMR11
]
. We show that from an LP solution, one can define a suitable
\mathsf{KnapOrient}
-instance and extract a
good LP solution to this
\mathsf{KnapOrient}
-instance. The
\mathsf{KnapOrient}
-instance is defined in such a way
that feasible solutions to this instance can be mapped to fractional solutions to the
correlated-knapsack LP. So we can first round the solution to obtain an integral

\mathsf{KnapOrient}
-solution
Q
, and then utilize the LP-rounding algorithm in
[
GuptaKMR11
]
for
correlated knapsack with cancellations to process vertices, with cancellations,

in the order they appear on
Q
. We note here that it is crucial that the algorithm
in
[
GuptaKMR11
]
for small-size instantiations has the flexibility that one can
specify a prescribed order for considering vertices (unlike the case of
\mathsf{CorrKO}
with
large-size instantiations).

Related work

As mentioned earlier,
orienteering
is a fundamental problem in combinatorial
optimization that finds various applications.
Blum et al.
[
BlumCKLMM07
]
devised the first constant-factor approximation algorithm
for orienteering, and the current best approximation factor is
(2+\epsilon)
for any

\epsilon>0

[
ChekuriKP12
]
.
Friggstad and Swamy
[
FriggstadS17
]
gave the first LP-based
O(1)
-approximation
algorithm. Their LP plays an important role for obtaining some of our results.

Deadline TSP
, also known as
deadline orienteering
, is a generalization of
orienteering, where nodes now have deadlines, and a path
P
is feasible if, for every

v\in P
, its travel time along

is at most its deadline; the goal is again to compute
a maximum-reward feasible path.
Both orienteering and
deadline TSP can be considered in the rooted, or

(
\mathsf{P2P}
) setting,
where both the start and end nodes of the path are specified.
Deadline TSP admits a polytime
O(\log n)
-approximation
[
BansalBCM04
]
and an

O(1)
-approximation in time

[

]
.

, where there are no deadlines but

, where

is a non-increasing function. They showed that this problem is essentially equivalent to

The literature on stochastic optimization problems is rich, and we discuss below only the
work that is most relevant to our work.

Stochastic orienteering and

-approximation

Bhalgat11
]
.
Gupta et al.
[

Stochastic

We have already mentioned the works of Gupta et al.

A minimization version of stochastic orienteering, called

, where instead of a travel budget, we want to collect a

Multi-armed bandits with metric switching costs.

A related problem to
\mathsf{CorrKO}
is the

problem with metric switching
costs, considered by Guha and Munagala

, which can be viewed as a setting
where each vertex corresponds to a Markov chain (i.e., arm) with known transition
probabilities and rewards. Guha and Munagala consider this setting under a crucial

\mathsf{CorrKO}

They devise an

They use an elegant Lagrangian-relaxation idea to reduce the problem to orienteering; this

.

Deterministic max-reward vehicle routing.

The following three vehicle routing problems (
\mathsf{VRP}
s) play a prominent role in the study
of stochastic orienteering. All three problems fall in the genre of max-reward
\mathsf{VRP}
s,
wherein we have nonnegative node rewards
\{\pi_{v}\}_{v\in V}
, and we need to select some
vertices and find a suitable path visiting these vertices, so as to maximize the reward
obtained. The differences in the problems lie in which paths are allowed, and the
definition of the reward collected by a path.
All these problems below can be considered in the
rooted
setting, where we have a root

\rho
and the feasible paths form a subset of rooted paths, or in the
point-to-point

(
\mathsf{P2P}
) setting, where both a start-node

and end-node

are specified, and the
feasible paths are a subset of
a
-
b
paths.

•

Orienteering.
We have a length budget
B
, and feasible paths (in both the
rooted and
\mathsf{P2P}
versions) are those with length at most
B
; we collect the reward of all
nodes on a feasible path.

•

Deadline TSP
, also called
deadline orienteering.
Here nodes have
deadlines
\{D_{v}\}_{v\in V}
. A path
P
with the appropriate end-points is feasible if the travel time of each
node in
P
is at most its deadline.
So in the rooted case, a rooted path
P
is feasible if
d(P_{\rho,v})\leq D_{v}
for all

v\in P
; in the
\mathsf{P2P}
-case, an
a
-
b
path
P
is feasible if
d(P_{a,v})\leq D_{v}
for all

v\in P
.
We collect the reward of all nodes on a feasible path.
(Equivalently, one can say that the feasible paths are
all
paths with the prescribed
end-points, and we collect the reward from all nodes on the path that are
visited
by their deadlines
.)

Observe that orienteering is the special case where the deadline of each node
is the length bound
B
.
Also, the rooted and
\mathsf{P2P}
versions of deadline TSP are equivalent
[
FriggstadS17
]
.

•

Monotone-reward TSP.
This is a generalization of deadline TSP, where each
node
v
has a non-increasing reward-function
\pi_{v}:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
\pi_{v}(t)

gives the reward obtained from
v
if
v
is visited at time
t
.
Every path
P
with the appropriate end-points is feasible,
and the reward of
P
is given by

\sum_{v\in P}\pi_{v}(\text{travel time of $v$})=\sum_{v\in P}\pi_{v}\bigl(d(P_{r,v})\bigr)
,
where
r
is the start node of
P
.

Friggstad and Swamy
[
FriggstadS17
]
showed that monotone-reward TSP can be reduced to
deadline TSP losing a
(1+\epsilon)
-factor, for an
\epsilon>0
.
Monotone-reward TSP will play a key role in the algorithm for correlated orienteering.

Stochastic orienteering problems.

In the
correlated knapsack orienteering
(
\mathsf{CorrKO}
) problem, each vertex
v\in V
is
associated with an stochastic job with a random processing time or size

S_{{v}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and a possibly
correlated
random reward

R_{{v}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
We use the terms processing time and size interchangeably.
These random variables are independent across different
vertices, and their distributions are specified in the input. We are given a length or
travel-time budget

B
, and a processing-time budget
W
. A solution, or policy, for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
visits a sequence of (distinct) vertices starting from the root
\rho
, in a possibly
adaptive fashion, without exceeding the travel-time and processing-time budgets.
More precisely, when a vertex
v
is visited, it’s corresponding job is processed
non-preemptively, and we get to know the processing time and reward of the job only upon
its completion; the completion time of job
v
is the total processing time of all jobs up
to and including
v
.
So if the adaptive policy visits vertices
v_{0}:=\rho,\ldots,v_{\ell}=u
in that order,
then it must be that the total travel-time
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}d(v_{i-1},v_{i})
to get to
u

is at most
B
, and the total processing time of (the jobs associated with)

v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell-1}
is at most
W
. We collect the rewards of

v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell-1}
, and we collect
u
’s reward if its completion time
is at most
W
.
The goal is to maximize the expected total reward collected. For notational convenience, we also assign a deterministic value of
0
to the reward and
processing time of
\rho
.

In the
correlated orienteering
(
\mathsf{CorrO}
) problem, the setup is almost the
same as in
\mathsf{CorrKO}
, except that there is only one budget
B
, which is the budget
for the
sum
of the travel times and processing times. (That is, we have one
notion of time, which advances due to both travel and the processing of jobs.)
So if an adaptive policy for
\mathsf{CorrO}
visits vertices
v_{0}:=\rho,\ldots,v_{\ell}=u
in that
order,
then we must have
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}d(v_{i-1},v_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}S_{{v_{i}}}\leq B
;
that is, the completion time of each
v_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell-1
,
as also the time when we reach
v_{\ell}
,
taking into account both travel time and
processing time
, should be at most
B
.
We collect rewards from
v_{1}\ldots,v_{\ell-1}
, and we collect
u
’s reward if

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}d(v_{i-1},v_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}S_{{v_{i}}}\leq B
.

Any adaptive policy for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
or
\mathsf{CorrO}
can be represented by a decision tree
\mathcal{T}
rooted at

\rho
,
whose nodes are labeled by vertices of
V
, and the branches of a node labeled
v\in V

correspond to the different size and reward instantiations of
v
, with each branch
specifying the next node to visit under the corresponding instantiation.

A
nonadaptive policy
(for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
or
\mathsf{CorrO}
) fixes a priori the sequence of vertices to
potentially visit,
without looking at the size and reward instantiations
. The

adaptivity gap
for an instance is the ratio (optimal expected reward
collected by an adaptive policy)/(optimal reward collected by a nonadaptive policy), and
the adaptivity gap for a problem is the supremum over all instances of the adaptivity gap
for the instance.

Deterministic knapsack-constrained vehicle routing.

Algorithms for stochastic orienteering problems
frequently utilize knapsack-constrained variants of deterministic
\mathsf{VRP}
s,
wherein we seek a feasible solution to the
\mathsf{VRP}
satisfying an additional knapsack constraint on the total vertex-weight of the path.
More precisely, suppose we have an underlying “base” max-reward
\mathsf{VRP}
, specified
by a collection
\mathcal{I}
of feasible paths along with nonnegative vertex-rewards

\{\pi_{v}\}_{v\in V}

\mathcal{I}

\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{knap}}:=\{\tau\in\mathcal{I}:\sum_{v\in\tau}\mathsf{wt}_{v}\leq W\}

\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{knap}}

\mathcal{I}

When the base
\mathsf{VRP}
is: (i) orienteering, the knapsack-constrained

(
\mathsf{KnapOrient}

(
\mathsf{KnapDO}
).
These two problems were considered by
[

BansalN14
]
in the
context of stochastic orienteering.
We say that the base-
\mathsf{VRP}
is a rooted-
\mathsf{VRP}

\mathcal{I}
start at the same vertex,
and it is a
\mathsf{P2P}
-
\mathsf{VRP}

\mathcal{I}

We give a general reduction (Theorem

\mathsf{VRP}

rooted-
\mathsf{VRP}
or
\mathsf{P2P}
-
\mathsf{VRP}

\alpha

\mathsf{VRP}

-approximation for the knapsack-constrained
\mathsf{VRP}

Let
\tau
be a path with ends

, which we will view as a sequence of nodes.
By a
\mathsf{P2P}
-subpath
of
\tau
, we mean any

\tau
;
by a

of
\tau

is a subsequence of
\tau
.
(Note that any subsequence of

The

\tau\in\mathcal{I}
:
(a) for rooted-
\mathsf{VRP}

\tau^{\prime}
of

\mathcal{I}
,
(b) for
\mathsf{P2P}
-
\mathsf{VRP}

\mathsf{P2P}
-subpath
\tau^{\prime}
of

\mathcal{I}
.
Most max-reward

\mathsf{VRP}

\mathsf{KnapDO}

Theorem 4
.

Consider a max-reward rooted-
\mathsf{VRP}
or
\mathsf{P2P}
-
\mathsf{VRP}
, specified by a set
\mathcal{I}
of
feasible solutions satisfying the subpath-closure property.
For any
\epsilon>0
, an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
(where
\alpha\geq 1
)
for the
\mathsf{VRP}
can be used to obtain an
(\alpha+2)(1+\epsilon)
-approximation for the
knapsack-constrained
\mathsf{VRP}
by making
O\bigl(\frac{\log n}{\epsilon}\bigr)
calls to
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\{\pi_{v}\geq 0\}_{v\in v}
be the node rewards.
Let the knapsack constraint be specified by nonnegative weights
\{\mathsf{wt}_{v}\}_{v\in V}
and
knapsack budget
W
. Let
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{knap}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
be the set of feasible solutions to the
knapsack-constrained
\mathsf{VRP}
.
By a subpath of a path
\tau\in\mathcal{I}
, we mean a rooted-subpath for a rooted-
\mathsf{VRP}
,
and a
\mathsf{P2P}
-subpath for a
\mathsf{P2P}
-
\mathsf{VRP}
.
We call the common start node of paths in
\mathcal{I}
, a
terminal
node; for a

\mathsf{P2P}
-
\mathsf{VRP}
, the common end node of paths in
\mathcal{I}
is also designated a terminal node.

We may assume that:
(i)
\mathsf{wt}_{w}=0
for every terminal node
w
,
since we can always set the knapsack weights of terminal node(s) to
0
and work with the residual knapsack budget of

W-
(total weight of terminal nodes)
without affecting the set
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{knap}}
of feasible solutions to the knapsack-constrained
problem; and
(ii)
\mathsf{wt}_{v}\leq W
for every node
v
, as otherwise we can simply discard node
v
.

Let
\tau^{*}
be an optimal solution for the knapsack-constrained
\mathsf{VRP}
, and

\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}=\pi(\tau^{*})
be the optimal value.
Due to (ii), we have
\max_{v\in V}\pi_{v}\leq\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}\leq\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{v}
, so by
considering all powers of
(1+\epsilon)
, we can assume that we have an estimate
\mathsf{LB}
such that

\mathsf{LB}\leq\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathsf{LB}
.
More precisely, there are at most
O\bigl(\frac{\log n}{\epsilon})
powers of
(1+\epsilon)
to
consider; we run the procedure below for each of these values, and return the
best solution obtained.

Set
\lambda=\frac{2}{\alpha+2}
. Essentially, we run
\mathcal{A}
on the base-
\mathsf{VRP}
instance with
node-rewards
\{\pi_{v}-\frac{\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}{W}\cdot\mathsf{wt}_{v}\}_{v\in V}
and return a suitable
subpath of the path returned by
\mathcal{A}
.
Set
\pi^{\prime}_{v}=\max\bigl\{0,\pi_{v}-\frac{\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}{W}\cdot\mathsf{wt}_{v}\bigr\}
for all

v\in V
, and run
\mathcal{A}
on the base-
\mathsf{VRP}
instance with
\{\pi^{\prime}_{v}\}
node rewards to obtain a path

\tau
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}
be the subpath of
\tau
whose non-terminal nodes are all the non-terminal nodes
v\in\tau
for which
\pi^{\prime}_{v}>0
. By the subpath-closure
property,
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
.

1.

If there is some
v\in\tau
for which
\pi_{v}\geq\frac{\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha+2}
, then we return
the path whose only potential non-trivial node is
v
. More precisely, if
v
is a
terminal, then we return the trivial path consisting of only the terminals;
otherwise, we return the path having
v
as the only non-terminal node (so
a,v
for a
rooted-
\mathsf{VRP}
, and
a,v,b
for a
\mathsf{P2P}
-
\mathsf{VRP}
).
By the subpath-closure property, the path returned lies in
\mathcal{I}
, and
by (ii), it satisfies the knapsack constraint.
So the path returned is feasible for the knapsack-constrained
\mathsf{VRP}
, and
clearly it achieves reward at least
\frac{\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}}{(\alpha+2)(1+\epsilon)}
.

2.

Otherwise, we find a subpath
\tau^{\prime\prime}
of
\tau^{\prime}
whose
nodes form a maximal subsequence of
\tau^{\prime}
satisfying

\pi(\tau^{\prime\prime})\leq\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}
. We return
\tau^{\prime\prime}
. By the subpath-closure property,

\tau^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
. Also, since
\mathsf{wt}_{v}\leq\pi_{v}\cdot\frac{W}{\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}
for every

v\in\tau^{\prime\prime}
(note that
\mathsf{wt}_{v}=0
if
v
is a terminal), we have

\mathsf{wt}(\tau^{\prime\prime})\leq\pi(\tau^{\prime\prime})\cdot\frac{W}{\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}\leq W
.

We lower bound the reward of
\tau^{\prime\prime}
in step 2 above.
First, observe that the optimal
\pi^{\prime}
-reward for the base-
\mathsf{VRP}
instance is at least

\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2}\cdot\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}
, since
\pi^{\prime}(\tau^{*})\geq\pi(\tau^{*})-\frac{2\cdot\mathsf{LB}}{(\alpha+2)W}\cdot\mathsf{wt}(\tau^{*})\geq\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}-\frac{2\cdot\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}}{\alpha+2}
,
as
\mathsf{wt}(\tau^{*})\leq W
and
\mathsf{LB}\leq\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}
.
Therefore, since
\mathcal{A}
is an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for the base-
\mathsf{VRP}
, we obtain
that
\pi^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime})=\pi^{\prime}(\tau)\geq\frac{\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}}{\alpha+2}
.
If
\tau^{\prime\prime}\neq\tau^{\prime}
, then since
\tau^{\prime\prime}
is maximal, we have

\pi(\tau^{\prime\prime})\geq\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}-\frac{\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha+2}
, because
\pi_{v}<\frac{\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha+2}
for
every
v\in\tau
. It follows that

\pi(\tau^{\prime\prime})\geq\min\bigl\{\pi(\tau^{\prime}),\tfrac{\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha+2}\bigr\}\geq\min\bigl\{\pi^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime}),\tfrac{\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha+2}\bigr\}\geq\frac{\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}}{(\alpha+2)(1+\epsilon)}
.

(a)

(4+\epsilon)
-approximation, for any
\epsilon>0

\mathsf{P2P}
- knapsack orienteering;

(b)

O(\log n)
-approximation for the rooted and
\mathsf{P2P}
versions of knapsack deadline orienteering, and

(c)

O(1)
-approximation in

\mathsf{P2P}

Proof.

Part (a) follows because there is a
(2+\epsilon)
-approximation
[
ChekuriKP12
]
for

\mathsf{P2P}
-orienteering.

For knapsack deadline orienteering, the guarantees in parts (b) and (c) follow because
the rooted and
\mathsf{P2P}
versions of deadline orienteering are equivalent
[
FriggstadS21
]

and they admit: an
O(\log n)
-approximation algorithm, and
an
O(1)
-approximation in
O\bigl(n^{\log n\Delta}\bigr)
time
[
FriggstadS21
]
.

For knapsack monotone-reward TSP, we cannot apply Theorem
4

is that if we take a subpath
\tau^{\prime}
of a feasible path
\tau

the nodes on
\tau^{\prime}
yield at least as much reward for
\tau^{\prime}
as for the original
path. This property clearly holds for monotone-reward TSP, and can be used to show, with
cosmetic changes to the proof of Theorem
4
, that knapsack monotone-reward TSP
can be reduced to monotone-reward TSP incurring the same approximation-factor loss as in
Theorem
4
.
Friggstad and Swamy
[
FriggstadS21
]
showed that monotone-reward TSP can be reduced to
deadline orienteering losing a
(1+\epsilon)
-factor. Coupling these two reductions, and
utilizing the aforementioned guarantees for deadline orienteering, yields the guarantees
in parts (b) and (c) for knapsack monotone-reward TSP.

We now briefly describe how to adapt Theorem
4
to monotone-reward TSP. Recall
that the reward of a node
v
is now specified by a non-increasing function of its travel
time,
\pi_{v}:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We may again assume that

\mathsf{wt}_{v}\leq W
for every node
v
, and is
0
if
v
is a terminal.
Let
a
denote the common start node of all feasible paths.
Let
\tau^{*}
be an optimal solution for knapsack monotone-reward TSP, and

\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}
be the optimal value. We now have

\max_{v\in V}\pi_{v}\bigl(d(a,v)\bigr)\leq\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}\leq\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{v}\bigl(d(a,v)\bigr)
, so
again we may assume that we have an estimate
\mathsf{LB}
such that

\mathsf{LB}\leq\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathsf{LB}
.
Set
\lambda=\frac{2}{\alpha+2}
.
For every
v\in V
, consider the reward function given by

\pi^{\prime}_{v}(t)=\max\bigl\{0,\pi_{v}(t)-\frac{\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}{W}\cdot\mathsf{wt}_{v}\bigr\}
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
We run
\mathcal{A}
on the monotone-reward TSP instance with
\{\pi^{\prime}_{v}\}
node-reward functions
to obtain a path
\tau
. For
v\in\tau
, define

\mathsf{rewd}_{v}:=\pi_{v}\bigl(d(\tau_{a,v})\bigr)
, which is the
\pi_{v}
-reward that
v
obtains
under
\tau
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}
be the subpath of
\tau
whose non-terminal nodes
are all the non-terminal nodes
v\in\tau
for which

\mathsf{rewd}_{v}>\frac{\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}{W}\cdot\mathsf{wt}_{v}
.

1.

If there is some
v\in\tau
for which
\mathsf{rewd}_{v}\geq\frac{\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha+2}
, then we return
the path whose only potential non-trivial node is
v
.
This is a feasible path satisfying the knapsack constraint, and clearly, it
achieves reward at least

\pi_{v}\bigl(d(a,v)\bigr)\geq\mathsf{rewd}_{v}\geq\frac{\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}}{(\alpha+2)(1+\epsilon)}
.

2.

Otherwise, we find a subpath
\tau^{\prime\prime}
of
\tau^{\prime}
whose
nodes form a maximal subsequence of
\tau^{\prime}
satisfying

\mathsf{rewd}(\tau^{\prime\prime})\leq\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}
. We return
\tau^{\prime\prime}
.
Since
\mathsf{wt}_{v}\leq\mathsf{rewd}_{v}\cdot\frac{W}{\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}
for every

v\in\tau^{\prime\prime}
(note that
\mathsf{wt}_{v}=0
if
v
is a terminal), we have

\mathsf{wt}(\tau^{\prime\prime})\leq W
.

The reward obtained by each
v\in\tau^{\prime\prime}
is at least
\mathsf{rewd}_{v}
, so the total reward
obtained in this case is at least

\mathsf{rewd}(\tau^{\prime\prime})\geq\min\bigl\{\mathsf{rewd}(\tau^{\prime}),\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}-\frac{\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha+2}\bigr\}
.
Observe that
\mathsf{rewd}(\tau^{\prime})
is at least the total reward obtained from

\tau
for the monotone-reward TSP instance with
\{\pi^{\prime}_{v}\}
node-reward functions.
The optimal value for this monotone-reward TSP instance is at least

\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}-\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}
, since the reward obtained from
\tau^{*}
is at least this
value. Therefore, since
\mathcal{A}
is an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for monotone-reward
TSP, we have
\mathsf{rewd}(\tau^{\prime})\geq\frac{\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}-\lambda\cdot\mathsf{LB}}{\alpha}\geq\frac{\mathit{OPT}^{\mathsf{knap}}}{\alpha+2}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7
: lower bound on
\mathit{OPT}
.

Consider the following adaptive policy
\mathcal{A}
: the policy moves to node
r
from
\rho
, and
then proceeds as follows. Let
v
be the current node visited, which is
r
initially.
If
v
is a leaf, then the policy ends after the instantiation of
v

node visited by
\mathcal{A}
is: the left child of
v

S_{{v}}=S_{{v}}^{(3)}

of
v
if
S_{{v}}=S_{{v}}^{(2)}
; if
S_{{v}}=S_{{v}}^{(1)}
, then
\mathcal{A}
stops and does not visit
any other nodes.

Let
P^{*}
denote the (random) path traversed by
\mathcal{A}
, which we may view as a

T
.
Let
v_{\mathsf{last}}
be the last vertex visited by
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,
v_{\mathsf{last}}
is the end-node of

P^{*}=\mathsf{path}(v_{\mathsf{last}})
.
It is easy to see that
P^{*}
does not exceed the knapsack and travel budgets, and
we lower bound the reward collected by

in Lemma

Claim 9
.

We have
d(P^{*})\leq B
and
S(P^{*}):=\sum_{v\in P^{*}}S_{{v}}\leq W
with probability
1
.

Proof.

The length of any rooted path in
T
is at most

\sum_{\ell=2}^{H}2^{\ell-2}=2^{H-1}-1=B
.
By construction,
for every node
w
on
P^{*}
before
v_{\mathsf{last}}
, we have
S_{{w}}\neq S_{{w}}^{(1)}
, and

S_{{w}}=S_{{w}}^{(2)}
precisely if
w
is a right-branching node of
P^{*}
.
Therefore

\sum_{w\prec_{P^{*}}v_{\mathsf{last}}}S_{{w}}=\sum_{w\in\mathsf{rt}(P^{*})}S_{{w}}^{(2)}=W-S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}
, and

.

Lemma 10
.

The expected reward collected by
P^{*}
is at least
\frac{1-e^{-1}}{4}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}=R(P^{*}):=\sum_{v\in P^{*}}R_{{v}}
denote the reward obtained by
P^{*}
.
Note that
v_{\mathsf{last}}
is the only vertex from which
P^{*}
can collect positive reward.
So
{\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[\mathcal{R}\bigr]=\Pr\bigl[S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}=S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}\bigr]\cdot{\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[R_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}\bigr]
.

S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}\neq S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}
, then
v_{\mathsf{last}}
is a leaf node, and hence

\{S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}\neq S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}\}

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{T}

\Pr\bigl[S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}\neq S_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}\bigr]=\bigl(1-\frac{1}{H}\bigr)^{H}\leq e^{-1}

{\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[\mathcal{R}\bigr]\geq(1-e^{-1}){\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[R_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}\bigr]

{\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[R_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}\bigr]\geq\bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\bigr)^{{\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[|\mathsf{rt}(P^{*})|\bigr]}

{\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[R_{{v_{\mathsf{last}}}}^{(1)}\bigr]\geq\bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}\bigr)^{\sqrt{H}}\geq\frac{1}{4}

where the last inequality uses the fact that

.

Proof of Theorem
8
: upper bound for non-adaptive policies

Let
\sigma
be some non-adaptive policy, which we may again view as a rooted path in
T
, since we can always move first to
r
.
We may assume that
\sigma
visits vertices in decreasing order of their levels, since any

v

We say that an execution of
\sigma
“cheats” if, for some visited node

instantiates to
S_{{v}}^{(2)}

\sigma
proceeds to visit a vertex in the subtree of

Lemma 11
.

\sigma
does not collect any positive reward after cheating.

Proof.

Suppose
\sigma
cheats at some vertex
u
.
Let
v
be any node in the tree rooted at the left-child of

.
The residual knapsack budget after visiting

is at most

.
It suffices to show that
S_{{v}}^{(1)}>W-S_{{u}}^{(2)}
. Since
S_{{v}}^{(1)}=W-\sum_{w\in\mathsf{rt}(path(v))}S_{{w}}^{(2)}
,
this amounts to showing that
S_{{u}}^{(2)}>\sum_{w\in A}S_{{w}}^{(2)}
,
where
A=\mathsf{rt}(\mathsf{path}(v))
.
We argue that
S_{{w}}^{(2)}<S_{{u}}^{(2)}
for every
w\in A
, and the
S_{{w}}^{(2)}

distinct for
w\in A
. This, coupled with the fact
that
S_{{u}}^{(2)}
and the
S_{{w}}^{(2)}
’s are all powers of
2
, implies the above inequality.

Recall that for a node
z
, we have

S_{{z}}^{(2)}=2^{2^{\mathsf{lev}(z)}}\cdot\prod_{w\in\mathsf{rt}(\mathsf{path}(z))}2^{2^{\mathsf{lev}(w)}}
.
Let
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{|A|}\}
, where the nodes are ordered in increasing order of
their distance from
r
. Then, for any

, we have

S_{{a_{i}}}^{(2)}=2^{2^{\mathsf{lev}(a_{i})}}\cdot S_{{a_{i-1}}}^{(2)}
, showing that each

S_{{a_{i}}}^{(2)}
is a distinct power of
2
, and
S_{{a_{i}}}^{(2)}
increases with
i
.
Note that
u\notin\mathsf{rt}(\mathsf{path}(v))

and
\mathsf{rt}(\mathsf{path}(u))\subseteq A
. So for

, we have

S_{{z}}^{(2)}=\prod_{w\in A-\mathsf{rt}(\mathsf{path}(u))}2^{2^{\mathsf{lev}(w)}}\cdot\prod_{w\in\mathsf{rt}(\mathsf{path}(u))}2^{2^{\mathsf{lev}(w)}}

\prod_{w\in A-\mathsf{rt}(\mathsf{path}(u))}2^{2^{\mathsf{lev}(w)}}<2^{2^{\mathsf{lev}(u)}}
. It follows that
S_{{z}}^{(2)}<S_{{u}}^{(2)}
.

Recall that we view
\sigma

The expected reward obtained from
\mathsf{left}(\sigma)

independent of the realizations of other nodes.
Again, since different nodes are independent, we have

\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{v}]=\prod_{w\in\mathsf{left}(\mathsf{path}(v)),\;w\neq v}\Pr\bigl[S_{{w}}=S_{{w}}^{(3)}\bigr]=\bigl(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}-\frac{1}{H}\bigr)^{|\mathsf{left}(\mathsf{path}(v))|-1}
.
So the total expected reward obtained from
\mathsf{left}(\sigma)
is at most

\sum_{v\in\mathsf{left}(\sigma)}\frac{1}{H}\cdot\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{H}}-\tfrac{1}{H}\Bigr)^{|\mathsf{left}(\mathsf{path}(v))|-1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{H}\frac{1}{H}\cdot\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{H}}-\tfrac{1}{H}\Bigr)^{i-1}\leq\frac{1}{H}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}+\frac{1}{H}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}.

Again, the first inequality follows because

|\mathsf{left}(\mathsf{path}(v))|-1
, for
v\in\mathsf{left}(\sigma)
, are distinct elements of
\llbracket{H-1}\rrbracket
.
∎

Approximation algorithms for
\mathsf{CorrKO}

We now devise non-adaptive approximation algorithms for
\mathsf{CorrKO}
.
In Section
4.1
, we develop an

O(\log\log{W})
-approximation algorithm with
(n+\log B)^{O(\log W\log\log W)}
(i.e., quasi-polynomial) running time, which will
prove Theorem

4.1
Quasi-polytime
O(\log\log W)
-approximation algorithm

There are two chief components underlying our algorithm.
First, we isolate a key structural result (Theorems
14
and
15
)
showing that from an optimal adaptive policy, one can extract a suitable path
Q^{*}

and certain “portal” vertices on this path, such that the subpaths of
Q^{*}
between
these portal vertices satisfy various nice properties.
Second, we show how to exploit this structural result algorithmically.
The above properties allow us to reduce the problem, roughly speaking at the expense of an

O(\log\log W)
-factor loss, to that of finding the portal vertices, and suitable paths
between these portal vertices that satisfy certain knapsack constraints on the total
expected truncated size
{\textstyle\Exp}\bigl[\min\{S_{{v}},2^{j}\}\bigr]
of nodes on these paths.
We “guess” (i.e., enumerate over all possible choices of)
these portal vertices and some auxiliary information, and set up a configuration LP
(
CKO-P
) to
find paths between these portal vertices. This configuration LP can be solved
near-optimally, and we show that a fractional solution can be rounded incurring only an

O(1)
-factor loss in the objective and in the constraints. Finally, we argue that
this leads to an
O(\log\log W)
-approximation non-adaptive policy.

Our approach is similar in spirit to the one in
[
BansalN14
]
for
\mathsf{CorrO}
, and in
Section
, we show that our approach also yields an

O(\log\log B)
-approximation for
\mathsf{CorrO}
, which improves upon the guarantee in
[
BansalN14
]

by an
O\bigl(\frac{\log\log B}{\log\log\log B}\bigr)
-factor. While we borrow various
ingredients from
[
BansalN14
]
, the key difference between our approach and theirs is
that we extract much more information from the adaptive policy in terms of so-called portal
vertices, which enables us to round an underlying configuration LP incurring only a

constant-factor
violation in the knapsack constraints; in contrast,
this step in
[
BansalN14
]
incurs an
O\bigl(\frac{\log\log B}{\log\log\log B}\bigr)
-factor
violation of the constraints, and this savings is the source of our improved guarantee.
