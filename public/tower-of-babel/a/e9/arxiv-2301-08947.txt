An encounter-based approach to the escape problem
Denis S. Grebenkov

Abstract
We revise the encounter-based approach to imperfect diffusion-controlled reactions, which employs the statistics of encounters between a diffusing particle and the reactive region to implement surface reactions. We extend this approach to deal with a more general setting, in which the reactive region is surrounded by a reflecting boundary with an escape region. We derive a spectral expansion for the full propagator and investigate the behavior and probabilistic interpretations of the associated probability flux density. In particular, we obtain the joint probability density of the escape time and the number of encounters with the reactive region before escape, and the probability density of the first-crossing time of a prescribed number of encounters. We briefly discuss generalizations of the conventional Poissonian-type surface reaction mechanism described by Robin boundary condition and potential applications of this formalism in chemistry and biophysics.

Main results

II.1
Conventional approach

In order to highlight the advantages of the encounter-based approach,
we briefly recall several characteristics of diffusion-controlled
reactions accessed within the conventional approach that relies on the
propagator
G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. We consider a point-like particle
diffusing in a bounded Euclidean domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, which is partitioned into reactive (
\partial\Omega_{R}
),
reflecting (
\partial\Omega_{N}
) and escape (
\partial\Omega_{D}
) parts. As the diffusing
particle can disappear due to either escape through
\partial\Omega_{D}
or

\partial\Omega_{R}
, one can naturally introduce two first-passage
times: the escape time

T_{q}=\inf\{t>0~:~\bm{X}_{t}\in\partial\Omega_{D}\}

(5)

as the first-passage time to the escape region
\partial\Omega_{D}
, and the
reaction time
\tau_{q}
as the random instance of the reaction event on

\partial\Omega_{R}
(its formal definition is nontrivial and will be given in
Sec.
II.5
). The subscript
q
highlights that the
distributions of both random variables
T_{q}
and
\tau_{q}
depend on
the reactivity of the target region because they are determined by the
probability flux density,

j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=-D\partial_{n}G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega).

(6)

In fact, the restriction of
j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the escape region

\partial\Omega_{D}
is the joint probability density of the escape location

\bm{X}_{T_{q}}
and its time
T_{q}
. In turn, the restriction of

j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
is the joint probability
density of the reaction location
\bm{X}_{\tau_{q}}
and its time
\tau_{q}

(note that the restriction of
j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
to
\partial\Omega_{N}
is strictly
zero due to the Neumann boundary condition (
4c
)). If the
position does not matter, it can be averaged out to get the (marginal)
probability densities of the escape time
T_{q}
and of the reaction
time
\tau_{q}
:

\displaystyle J_{q}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{D}}d\bm{x}\,j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(7a)

\displaystyle J_{q}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(7b)

As the particle can either escape or react, none of these densities is
normalized to
1
; in turn, one has

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\bigl[J_{q}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})+J_{q}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigr]=1.

To show this normalization, one can integrate the diffusion equation
(
1
) over
\bm{x}\in\Omega
and use the Green’s formula and
mixed boundary conditions (
4
) to get the continuity

\partial_{t}S_{q}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=-\bigl[J_{q}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})+J_{q}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigr],

(9)

where

S_{q}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

(10)

is the survival probability of the particle up to time
t
. The
integral of Eq. (
9
) over

from
0
to infinity
yields the normalization (

II.2
Encounter-based approach

In the encounter-based approach, one first characterizes purely
diffusive dynamics inside a bounded confining domain
\Omega
with a

reflecting
boundary
\partial\Omega
, and then incorporates surface
reactions on
\partial\Omega
, i.e., transforms the reflecting boundary into the
reactive one
Grebenkov20
. For this purpose, one uses the
boundary local time
\ell_{t}
, which was first introduced by Lévy

Levy
and then extensively employed in mathematical literature
on stochastic processes
Ito
;
Freidlin
. The boundary local time
can be defined as the renormalized residence time near the boundary

\partial\Omega
:

\ell_{t}=\lim\limits_{a\to 0}\frac{D}{a}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\Theta(a-|\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\partial\Omega|),

(11)

where
|\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\partial\Omega|
is the Euclidean distance between the position

\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}
of the particle at time
t^{\prime}
and the boundary
\partial\Omega
, and

\Theta(z)
is the Heaviside step function:
\Theta(z)=1
for
z>0

and
0
otherwise. The integral in Eq. (
11
) defines
the residence (also known as occupation or sojourn) time in a thin
layer of width
a
near the boundary. As the boundary
\partial\Omega
has a
lower dimension as compared to the domain
\Omega
, the residence time
vanishes as the layer shrinks (when
a\to 0
); in turn, its
renormalization by
a
yields the nontrivial limit
(
11
). The boundary local time should not be confused
with the local time in a bulk point, which has been intensively
studied, especially for diffusive processes in one dimension

Borodin
;
Majumdar05
. Despite its name, the boundary local time has
units of length. The boundary local time can be equivalently defined
as a rescaled limit of the number
\mathcal{N}_{t}^{a}
of downcrossings of the
boundary layer of width
a
up to time
t
:
\ell_{t}=\lim\limits_{a\to 0}a\mathcal{N}_{t}^{a}
. As each downcrossing can be
interpreted as an encounter of the particle with the boundary, the
boundary local time
\ell_{t}
characterizes the statistics of such
encounters
Grebenkov21
. The diffusive dynamics can then be
described either by a stochastic differential equation for the random
pair
(\bm{X}_{t},\ell_{t})
, or by the so-called full propagator

P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
, i.e., the joint probability density of getting
the values
(\bm{x},\ell)
for the pair
(\bm{X}_{t},\ell_{t})
. Once the full
propagator is known, one can implement various surface reaction
mechanisms (see Sec.
II.5
).

By construction, the boundary local time
\ell_{t}
characterizes
encounters with the whole boundary
\partial\Omega
that does not allow one to
implement different reaction mechanisms on different subsets of the
boundary. This problem has been discussed and partly resolved in

Grebenkov20c
by introducing a proper boundary local time

\ell_{t}^{i}
on each subset of interest. In this case, one would deal
with the joint probability density for
\bm{X}_{t}
and for boundary local
times on all these subsets. Even though a formal way for computing
this density was proposed in
Grebenkov20c
, its practical
implementation was realized only for simple geometric settings (e.g.,
an interval).

In the context of the escape problem that we consider in this paper,
one can follow a different strategy. As we are interested in
describing reactions exclusively on the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
, we need
to know the statistics of encounters with that particular region. In
other words, one can modify the above definition of the boundary local
time to count encounters only with
\partial\Omega_{R}
:

\ell_{t}^{R}=\lim\limits_{a\to 0}\frac{D}{a}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\Theta(a-|\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\partial\Omega_{R}|).

(12)

This relation defines a non-decreasing stochastic process
\ell_{t}^{R}

(starting from
\ell_{0}^{R}=0
) that increases at each encounter with

\partial\Omega_{R}
. As previously, the integral is the residence time that a
particle spent in a thin layer of width
a
near the target region

\partial\Omega_{R}
up to time
t
. When
a
is small, this residence time can
thus be approximated as
\ell_{t}^{R}a/D
according to
Eq. (
12
). In the same vein, we introduce the full
propagator
P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
as the joint probability density for

\bm{X}_{t}
and
\ell_{t}^{R}
(not
\ell_{t}
), under the condition of no escape
through
\partial\Omega_{D}
up to time
t
. As previously, the target region

\partial\Omega_{R}
is treated at this step as
reflecting
, i.e., the
particle described by the full propagator can disappear only on the
escape region
\partial\Omega_{D}
.

At the next step, one can introduce reaction events on
\partial\Omega_{R}

following the probabilistic arguments from
Grebenkov20
. For
this purpose, one can consider a thin reactive layer of width
a
near
the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
. Once the particle enters this layer,
surface reaction may be described by a standard first-order reaction
kinetics, with the rate
k=\kappa/a
. Since the residence time of
the particle within this layer up to time
t
is approximately equal
to
\ell_{t}^{R}a/D
, the probability of no reaction on
\partial\Omega_{R}
up to
t

is then
e^{-k(\ell_{t}^{R}a/D)}=e^{-q\ell_{t}^{R}}
, where
q=\kappa/D
.
As a consequence, one deduces the following relation between the
conventional and full propagators:

\displaystyle G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\delta(\bm{X}_{t}-\bm{x})e^{-q\ell_{t}^{R}}\Theta(T_{q}-t)\}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(13)

On the left-hand side, the conventional propagator
G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})

satisfying Eqs. (
1
,
4
), describes
diffusion from
\bm{x}_{0}
to
\bm{x}
in time
t
, under the condition of no
escape through
\partial\Omega_{D}
and no reaction on
\partial\Omega_{R}
up to
t
. The
probability density of this event can be written via the expectation
in the middle, i.e. as the fraction of trajectories
\bm{X}_{t}
of
reflected Brownian motion between
\bm{x}_{0}
and
\bm{x}
of duration
t
,
with the penalizing factor
e^{-q\ell_{t}^{R}}
eliminating a subset of
trajectories that reacted on
\partial\Omega_{R}
, while
\Theta(T_{q}-t)

eliminating those that have escaped before
t
. On the right-hand
side, the full propagator
P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
describes diffusion from

\bm{x}_{0}
to
\bm{x}
in time
t
, under the condition of no escape through

\partial\Omega_{D}
and of getting the boundary local time
\ell_{t}^{R}
equal to

\ell
. In turn, the factor
e^{-q\ell}
incorporates the probability
of no reaction on
\partial\Omega_{R}
for any realized value
\ell
of the
boundary local time
\ell_{t}^{R}
, while the integral over
\ell
sums up
contributions from all possible realizations of
\ell_{t}^{R}
. In other
words, this integral simply evaluates the expectation in the middle.
We stress that the condition
T_{q}>t
of no escape through
\partial\Omega_{D}
is
implemented in the full propagator

through the
Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{D}

The relation (
13
) plays the central role in this work. On
the one hand, it allows one to incorporate the conventional surface
reactions as the Laplace transform of the full propagator

P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
with respect to
\ell
. Importantly, the
reactivity parameter
q
enters
explicitly
through the
exponential factor
e^{-q\ell}
, whereas it appeared
implicitly

in the conventional description as a coefficient in Robin boundary
condition (
4a
). Finally, one can replace the factor

e^{-q\ell}
, which is reminiscent of an exponential probability law,
by another function, allowing one to implement various surface
reaction mechanisms (see Sec.

). On the other hand, the
inverse Laplace transform of the conventional propagator

G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
with respect to
q
gives access to the full
propagator:

P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathcal{L}_{q\to\ell}^{-1}\bigl\{G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigr\}.

(14)

Unfortunately, an implicit dependence of
G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
on
q
often
prevents using this inversion and thus urges for another
representation for the full propagator.

To achieve this goal, we extend the spectral expansion of the full
propagator developed in
Grebenkov20
. For this purpose, we
introduce an extension of the so-called Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator

that associates to a given function
f
on the target region

\partial\Omega_{R}
another function
g
on that region such that

\mathcal{M}_{p}f=g=\left.(\partial_{n}u)\right|_{\partial\Omega_{R}},

where

is the solution of the following boundary value problem with

p\geq 0
:

\displaystyle(p-D\Delta)u

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\Omega),

(16a)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=f\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}),

(16b)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{D}),

(16c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}u

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{N}).

(16d)

In other words, the operator
\mathcal{M}_{p}
transforms the Dirichlet boundary
condition

\partial\Omega_{R}
into an equivalent Neumann boundary

\partial_{n}u=g

\partial\Omega_{R}
, keeping unchanged the

on the subset

;

;

;

;

;

;

(17)

In turn, the associated eigenfunctions
v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
form a complete
orthonormal basis of the functional space
L_{2}(\partial\Omega_{R})
of square
integrable functions on
\partial\Omega_{R}
. A rigorous formulation and
demonstration of these mathematical properties are beyond the scope of
this paper. From the mathematical point of view, one can consider
them as conjectural extensions of the well-known conventional case.

The eigenbasis of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator can serve for
getting a spectral expansion of the full propagator. Skipping
technical details (given in
Grebenkov20
), we sketch here the
main steps of this derivation. In the first step, the Laplace
transform of the conventional propagator
G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
with respect
to time
t
,

\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(18)

reduces the diffusion equation (
1
) to the inhomogeneous
modified Helmholtz equation:

(p-D\Delta)\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0}),

(19)

subject to the same boundary condition (here and below, tilde denotes
Laplace-transformed quantities with respect to
t
). Writing

\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})=\tilde{G}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})+\tilde{g}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
with an unknown function
\tilde{g}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
,
one eliminates
\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})
from the right-hand side. As

\tilde{g}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
satisfies
(p-D\Delta)\tilde{g}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})=0
, one can employ the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{M}_{p}

for a spectral decomposition of the restriction of

\tilde{g}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
to
\partial\Omega_{R}
, which can then be extended to the
whole domain
\Omega
. Finally, one takes the inverse Laplace
transform of
\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
with respect to
q
to get the
spectral expansion of the full propagator in the Laplace domain:

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(\bm{x},\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{G}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)

(20)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{D}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})\,e^{-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\ell},

where asterisk denotes complex conjugate, and

V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,\tilde{j}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})\,v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})

(21)

is the extension of the eigenfunction
v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
(defined on

\partial\Omega_{R}
) to the whole domain
\Omega
. While the structure of the
spectral expansion (
20
) is identical to that derived
in
Grebenkov20
, its “ingredients”

\tilde{G}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
,
V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})
and
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
are
adapted to account for the presence of reflecting and escape regions.

One can easily check that the functions
V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
defined by
Eq. (
21
) satisfy:

\displaystyle(p-D\Delta)V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\Omega),

(22a)

\displaystyle V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}),

(22b)

\displaystyle V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{D}),

(22c)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{N}).

(22d)

Moreover, since
v_{k}^{(p)}
is an eigenfunction of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator, one has

\partial_{n}V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})=\mu_{k}^{(p)}V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})=\mu_{k}^{(p)}v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}).

(23)

Once the eigenfunction
v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
is found, one can determine its
extension
V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
either via Eq. (
21
), or by solving
the above problem (
22
). Alternatively, without
knowing
v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
, one can look directly at the eigenvalue
problem (
22a
,
22c
,
22d
,

23
), in which the spectral parameter (here,
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
)
stands in the boundary condition. This is known as the Steklov
problem (see
Girouard17
and references therein), while

\mu_{k}^{(p)}
and
V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
are the eigenvalues and
eigenfunctions of this problem. Despite this equivalence, we keep
referring to the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{M}_{p}
and its spectral
properties.

The inverse Laplace transform of Eq. (
20
) with
respect to
p
formally yields

\displaystyle P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=G_{\infty}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)

(24)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}_{p\to t}^{-1}\biggl\{\frac{1}{D}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})\,e^{-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\ell}\biggr\}.

The first term in Eq. (
24
) represents the
contribution of random trajectories from
\bm{x}_{0}
to
\bm{x}
of duration

t
without hitting neither the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
, nor the escape
region
\partial\Omega_{D}
(in turn, they could encounter the reflecting part

\partial\Omega_{N}
). Their “fraction” is precisely given by

G_{\infty}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
, with Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{D}

and
\partial\Omega_{R}
(note that Eq. (
4a
) becomes

G_{\infty}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=0
for
q=\infty
). As the boundary local
time
\ell_{t}^{R}
remained zero for these trajectories, one gets the
singular factor
\delta(\ell)
. In turn, the second term accounts for
all trajectories that have encountered the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
, but
still avoided the escape through
\partial\Omega_{D}
(the latter condition is
implemented via Eq. (
22c
) for all
V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})
). The
spectral representation (
24
) is an alternative way
for computing the full propagator, which is complementary to
Eq. (
14
). While both expressions involve an inverse
Laplace transform, the spectral characteristics of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator are in general easier to access than the
conventional propagator
G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
in time domain. For
instance, we will employ Eq. (
24
) in
Sec.
III
to deduce various properties of
diffusion-controlled reactions in a spherical domain. We emphasize
that two representations provide complementary insights onto the full
propagator.

The full propagator determines the corresponding probability flux
density on the boundary
\partial\Omega
:

j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=-D\partial_{n}P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega).

(25)

The spectral expansion (
20
) gives access to this
quantity in the Laplace domain

\displaystyle\tilde{j}(\bm{x},\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{j}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,(\partial_{n}V_{k}^{(p)})(\bm{x})\,e^{-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\ell}.

(26)

The probability flux density
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
is the main object of
our study. In particular, we aim at providing its probabilistic
interpretation and deducing various related characteristics of
diffusion-controlled reactions in the presence of escape events. We
will show that the interpretation of
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
is more
subtle than that of
j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
mentioned in
Sec.
II.1
. For instance, it is easy to prove that

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=0\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}),

(27)

i.e., the restriction of
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
to
\partial\Omega_{R}
is not
necessarily positive.

To show this relation, let us first integrate
Eq. (
20
) over
\ell
from
0
to infinity to get the
following identity

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{0}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{G}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})

(28)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{D}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})\frac{1}{\mu_{k}^{(p)}}\,,

so that

\displaystyle\tilde{j}_{0}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{j}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})

(29)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,(\partial_{n}V_{k}^{(p)})(\bm{x})\frac{1}{\mu_{k}^{(p)}}\,.

For any
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}
, the left-hand side of Eq. (
29
)
is zero, implying

\tilde{j}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}),

(30)

where we applied Eq. (
23
). Using the identity
(
30
), one can easily check that the integral of
Eq. (
26
) over
\ell
from
0
to infinity is strictly
zero for any
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}
, that reads in time domain as
Eq. (
27
).

In order to clarify the probabilistic meaning of
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
,
we first look at the distribution of the boundary local time

\ell_{t}^{R}
, then discuss the restriction of
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the
escape region
\partial\Omega_{D}
, and finally describe its restriction to the
target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
.

II.3
Probability density of the boundary local time

It is convenient to start by inspecting the distribution of the
boundary local time
\ell_{t}^{R}
. By definition, the integral of the
full propagator
P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
over
\bm{x}\in\Omega
determines the
(marginal) probability density of the boundary local time

\rho(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(31)

Since the particle may escape the domain, this probability density is
not normalized to
1
:

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,\rho(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{T_{0}>t\},

(32)

i.e., the probability of no escape up to time
t
(the subscript
q=0
of
T_{0}
highlights that the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
is treated here
as reflecting). Only if there is no escape region, the survival
probability
S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is equal to
1
, ensuring the normalization
in the conventional case
Grebenkov19a
.

Integrating the fundamental relation (
13
) over

\bm{x}\in\Omega
, one gets

S_{q}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,\rho(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{e^{-q\ell_{t}^{R}}\},

(33)

i.e., the survival probability is the generating function of

\ell_{t}^{R}
. Moreover, the inverse Laplace transform of
Eq. (
9
) with respect to
q
yields another continuity
equation

\partial_{t}\rho(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=-\bigl[J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})+J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})\bigr],

(34)

where

\displaystyle J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(35a)

\displaystyle J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{D}}d\bm{x}\,j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(35b)

and we used that

j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

(36)

due to Eq. (
13
).

In the Laplace domain, the integral of Eq. (
20
) yields

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{S}_{\infty}(p|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)

(37)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{D}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,e^{-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\ell}\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}).

II.4
Escape events

In a direct analogy with
j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
, the restriction of the
probability flux density
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the escape region

\partial\Omega_{D}
determines the joint probability density of the position

\bm{X}_{T_{0}}
, the boundary local time
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
, and the escape
time
T_{0}
defined by Eq. (
5
).
If the exact location of the escape does not matter, one can integrate
over
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{D}
to get the joint probability density

J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
of
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
and
T_{0}
, see Eq. (
35b
).
In the Laplace domain, this quantity reads

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{D}(\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)

(38)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,e^{-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\ell}C_{k}^{(p)},

where

C_{k}^{(p)}=-\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{D}}d\bm{x}\,\partial_{n}V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})

(39)

and
\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
is given by Eq. (
7a
) at

q=\infty
. While the escape time
T_{0}
has been studied in the
past, the joint distribution of
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
and
T_{0}
has not been
reported earlier.

Integrating Eq. (
22a
), using the Green’s formula and
expressions (
22d
,
23
), one gets another
representation:

C_{k}^{(p)}=\mu_{k}^{(p)}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})-\frac{p}{D}\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}).

(40)

One can apply this representation to check the correct normalization
of
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,\tilde{J}_{D}(\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(0|\bm{x}_{0})+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\frac{C_{k}^{(0)}}{\mu_{k}^{(0)}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(0|\bm{x}_{0})+\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{R}(0|\bm{x}_{0})=1,

where we used Eq. (
30
). The last equality follows from
that the fact that
\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(0|\bm{x}_{0})
and

\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{R}(0|\bm{x}_{0})
are the splitting probabilities (e.g.,

\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(0|\bm{x}_{0})
is the probability of hitting
\partial\Omega_{D}

before hitting
\partial\Omega_{R}
) so that their sum is equal to
1
.

The integral of
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
over
\ell
determines the
(marginal) probability density function of the escape time
T_{0}

J_{0}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(41)

Integrating the continuity relation (
34
) over
\ell

from
0
to infinity and using the identity (
27
), one
sees that

\partial_{t}S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=-J_{0}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(42)

where we also used the normalization (
32
). We thus
re-derived that the probability density of the first-passage time to

\partial\Omega_{D}
is obtained as the negative time derivative of the survival
probability
S_{0}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. Expectedly, the derivation of this
classical quantity does not require the encounter-based approach.

In turn, the integral of
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
over
t
yields the
(marginal) probability density of the boundary local time

\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
acquired before the escape:

\displaystyle\rho_{D}(\ell|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\tilde{J}_{D}(\ell,0|\bm{x}_{0})

(43)

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(0|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,e^{-\mu_{k}^{(0)}\ell}\,C_{k}^{(0)}.

Here, the first term accounts for the trajectories that never hit the
target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
and moved directly to the escape region (with
the splitting probability
\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{D}(0|\bm{x}_{0})
) so that the
acquired boundary local time is zero. In turn, the second term
includes the contribution of remaining trajectories.

The expression (
38
) allows one to determine joint
positive integer-order moments of
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
and
T_{0}
as

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{[\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}]^{m}\,T_{0}^{n}\}=(-1)^{n}m!\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial p^{n}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}C_{k}^{(p)}}{[\mu_{k}^{(p)}]^{m+1}}

(44)

for any
m=1,2,\ldots
and any
n=0,1,\ldots
(for
m=0
, the
contribution from the first (singular) term in Eq. (
38
)
has to be included). In this way, one can evaluate not only the
moments of
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
and
T_{0}
, but also correlations between
them. In particular, the mean of
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}\}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\frac{C_{k}^{(0)}}{[\mu_{k}^{(0)}]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}^{(0)}}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,v_{k}^{(0)}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,D\tilde{G}_{0}(\bm{x},0|\bm{x}_{0}),

(45)

where we used the identity (
28
). This result is rather
intuitive. In fact, the Green’s function
D\tilde{G}_{0}(\bm{x},0|\bm{x}_{0})

is known to be the mean residence time of Brownian motion in a point

\bm{x}
before the escape
Morters
. The integral of this quantity
over a thin layer of width
a
near the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
yields
the mean residence time in this layer, while the rescaling by
D/a

results in the mean boundary local time in the limit
a\to 0
, see
Eq. (
11
).

To evaluate higher-order moments, we first integrate
Eq. (
20
)
m+1
times to get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})}{[\mu_{k}^{(p)}]^{m+1}}

\displaystyle\quad=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell_{1}\int\limits_{\ell_{1}}^{\infty}d\ell_{2}\,\ldots\int\limits_{\ell_{m+1}}^{\infty}d\ell_{m+1}D\tilde{P}(\bm{x},\ell_{m+1},p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{m!}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,\ell^{m}\,D\tilde{P}(\bm{x},\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

(46)

for any
m=1,2,\ldots
. In turn, differentiating
m
times the
fundamental relation (
13
) with respect to
q
, we derive
the following identity:

\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})}{[\mu_{k}^{(p)}]^{m+1}}=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\partial_{q}^{m}D\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})

(47)

for
m=1,2,\ldots
(for
m=0
, there is an additional term, see
Eq. (
28
)). This identity provides a peculiar
interpretation of the derivatives of the Green’s function

\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
with respect to
q
. Using
Eqs. (
39
,
44
), one gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{[\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}]^{m}\,T_{0}^{n}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m+n+1}}{m!}

(48)

\displaystyle\times\lim\limits_{q\to 0\atop p\to 0}\partial^{m}_{q}\partial^{n}_{p}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{D}}d\bm{x}\,D\partial_{n}\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0}).

The definitions (
6
,
7a
) further simplify
this relation as

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{[\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}]^{m}\,T_{0}^{n}\}=(-1)^{m+n}\lim\limits_{q\to 0\atop p\to 0}\partial^{m}_{q}\partial^{n}_{p}\tilde{J}_{q}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0}).

(49)

Note that this relation is also applicable for
m=0
, in which case
it reduces to the standard expression for the moments of the
first-passage time
T_{0}
:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{T_{0}^{n}\}=(-1)^{n}\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\partial^{n}_{p}\tilde{J}_{0}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0}).

(50)

Like
\tilde{J}_{0}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
was known to be the generating function
of the first-passage time
T_{0}
,
\tilde{J}_{q}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
turns out to
be the generating function of both
T_{0}
and
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
. This is
not surprising given that
p
and
q
appear as the conjugate
variables in the Laplace transforms with respect to
t
and
\ell
.
The elegant relation (
49
) highlights the similarity
between the physical time
t
, which can be seen as a proxy for the
number of “elementary jumps” of the particle in the bulk, and the
boundary local time
\ell
, which is a proxy of the number of its
“jumps” on the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
. This similarity was already
discussed in
Grebenkov20
and particularly in

Grebenkov20b
, in which a surface-hopping propagator, based on
the boundary local time, was introduced.

II.5
Reaction events

Now we can inspect the restriction of the probability flux density

j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
. In the Laplace
domain, the spectral expansion (
20
) implies

\displaystyle\tilde{j}(\bm{x},\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{j}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)

(51)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,\mu_{k}^{(p)}v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})e^{-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\ell}

for any
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}
. While this relation allows one to compute this
quantity, its probabilistic interpretation remains tricky, in
particular, due to its negative values according to
Eq. (
27
).

To clarify this point, we first rewrite Eq. (
4a
) as

DG_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})=j_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})/q
on
\partial\Omega_{R}
and take its inverse
Laplace transform with respect to
q
to get

DP(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\ell}d\ell^{\prime}\,j(\bm{x},\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})\quad(\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}).

(52)

The probability flux density
j(\bm{x},\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})
on
\partial\Omega_{R}
can thus
also be seen as the derivative of the full propagator on
\partial\Omega_{R}
with
respect to
\ell
. This is consistent with the boundary value problem
for the full propagator discussed in
Grebenkov20
. In the limit

\ell\to\infty
, Eq. (
52
) reduces to the relation
(
27
) because
P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})\to 0
, i.e., the
probability of getting infinitely large values of the boundary local
time
\ell_{t}^{R}
at a finite time
t
is zero. As the left-hand side
of Eq. (
52
) is a probability density, the integral in
the right-hand side is nonnegative, despite eventual negative values
of
j(\bm{x},\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})
. What does it represent?

In order to get its probabilistic interpretation, we introduce the
first-crossing time
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
of a threshold
\ell
by the boundary
local time
\ell_{t}^{R}
:

\mathcal{T}_{\ell}=\inf\{t>0~:~\ell_{t}^{R}>\ell\}.

(53)

If the particle has escaped the domain before crossing the threshold,
the first-crossing time is set to infinity. We have then

\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}>t\}=\mathbb{P}\{\ell_{t}^{R}<\ell,~T_{0}>t\}+\mathbb{P}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}<\ell,~T_{0}<t\}.

(54)

The first term describes no crossing of the threshold
\ell
when the
escape occurs after time
t
. In turn, the second term describes the
escape event before
t
, for which the acquired boundary local time

\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
remains below the threshold. The probability density
of the first-crossing time reads then as

\displaystyle U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}>t\}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\int\limits_{0}^{\ell}d\ell^{\prime}\,\rho(\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})-\int\limits_{0}^{\ell}d\ell^{\prime}\,J_{D}(\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0}),

where the probability density
\rho(\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})
was used to
evaluate the first term in Eq. (
54
), and the joint
probability density
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
of
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
and
T_{0}
for
the second term. Note that the definition of
\rho(\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})

automatically accounts for no escape up to time
t
. Using the
continuity equation (
34
), we finally get

U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\ell}d\ell^{\prime}\,J_{R}(\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(55)

Despite the fact that
J_{R}(\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})
cannot be interpreted as a
joint probability density (in analogy with
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
), its
integral over
\ell^{\prime}
yields the probability density of the
first-crossing time
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
.

According to Eq. (
52
), one gets another representation

U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,DP(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(56)

which was earlier derived in
Grebenkov20
for the particular
case
\partial\Omega_{R}=\partial\Omega
(i.e., without the escape region). This relation
has an intuitive interpretation. Let us again consider a thin layer
of width
a
near
\partial\Omega_{R}
. By definition, the integral of the full
propagator
P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
over
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}
, multiplied by
a

and
d\ell
, is the probability of finding the particle in that layer
at time
t
with the boundary local time
\ell_{t}^{R}
belonging to

(\ell,\ell+d\ell)
. As
\ell_{t}^{R}
is an nondecreasing process that
increments only when
\bm{X}_{t}\in\partial\Omega_{R}
, the value
\ell
is thus
achieved for the first time at
t
, i.e.,

\displaystyle a\,d\ell\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,P(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\in(t,t+dt)\}

\displaystyle=U(t,\ell|\bm{x}_{0})dt.

(57)

Since the increments
d\ell
and
dt
are related as
d\ell=Ddt/a

according to Eq. (
12
), one gets Eq. (
56
).

In the Laplace domain, Eq. (
51
) implies

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,e^{-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\ell}\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}).

Setting
\ell=0
and employing Eq. (
30
), one gets

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(0,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[V_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x}_{0})]^{*}\,\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,v_{k}^{(p)}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{R}}d\bm{x}\,\tilde{j}_{\infty}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})=\tilde{J}_{\infty}^{R}(p|\bm{x}_{0}),

i.e.,

U(0,t|\bm{x}_{0})=J_{\infty}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(58)

In other words, as the first crossing of the threshold
\ell=0

corresponds to the first arrival onto
\partial\Omega_{R}
, the first-crossing time

\mathcal{T}_{0}
is simply the first-passage time to
\partial\Omega_{R}
(before escaping
the domain).

As earlier stressed in
Grebenkov20
, the probability density

U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
of the first-crossing time is tightly related to the
probability density
J_{q}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of the reaction time
\tau_{q}
on

\partial\Omega_{R}
discussed in Sec.
II.1
. In fact, using
Eqs. (
7b
,
35a
,
36
), we first get

J_{q}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(59)

In order to transform
J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
into
U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
, one
needs to integrate by parts. However,
J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
contains a
singular term
J_{\infty}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\delta(\ell)
that has to be treated
separately. We have then

\displaystyle J_{q}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=J_{\infty}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})+\int\limits_{0^{+}}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=J_{\infty}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})+\underbrace{\left.\biggl(\int\limits_{0^{+}}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})\biggr)\right|_{\ell=0}^{\infty}}_{-U(0,t|\bm{x}_{0})}

\displaystyle+q\int\limits_{0^{+}}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}),

where we wrote the lower bound of the integral as
0^{+}
to highlight
that the singular term was excluded. According to Eq. (
58
),
two first terms cancel each other, yielding

J_{q}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,q\,e^{-q\ell}\,U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(60)

To interpret this relation, we introduce a random threshold

\hat{\ell}
obeying the exponential probability law with the rate

q
,
\mathbb{P}\{\hat{\ell}>\ell\}=e^{-q\ell}
, so that
qe^{-q\ell}

is the probability density of this law. As a consequence, the
integral (
60
) is the average over random realizations of
the threshold
\hat{\ell}
of the probability density of the
first-crossing time
\mathcal{T}_{\hat{\ell}}
. Since the left-hand side is
the probability density of the reaction time
\tau_{q}
, we conclude
that

\tau_{q}=\mathcal{T}_{\hat{\ell}}=\inf\{t>0~:~\ell_{t}^{R}>\hat{\ell}\},

(61)

i.e., the reaction occurs when the boundary local time
\ell_{t}^{R}

exceeds the random threshold
\hat{\ell}
with the exponential law.
We emphasize that the exponential law follows directly from the
postulated Robin boundary condition (
4a
). This
interpretation, earlier suggested in
Grebenkov20
, allows one to
go beyond the Robin boundary condition and to implement various
surface reaction mechanisms by choosing an appropriate law for the
random threshold
\hat{\ell}
. In this way, our definition
(
61
) of the reaction time
\tau_{q}
remains valid in a
much more general setting, while Eq. (
60
), which was
specific to the Robin boundary condition, is then generalized to

J_{\psi}^{R}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,\psi(\ell)\,U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(62)

where
\psi(\ell)
is the chosen probability density of the random
threshold
\hat{\ell}
. Different choices of
\psi(\ell)
and its
consequences on the distribution of the reaction time were discussed
in
Grebenkov20
. This extension of surface reaction mechanisms
is directly applicable to our setting with the escape region
\partial\Omega_{D}
.
In this way, we made a step further towards more realistic modeling of
diffusion-controlled reactions by incorporating the effect of escape
events.

We complete this section by providing a deeper probabilistic
interpretation of the first-passage time
T_{q}
to the escape region

\partial\Omega_{D}
in the presence of the reactive region
\partial\Omega_{R}
. As discussed
in Sec.
II.1
, this random variable is described by
the probability density
J_{q}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, which is related to

J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
due to Eq. (
36
) as

J_{q}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\,e^{-q\ell}\,J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(63)

As previously, the factor
e^{-q\ell}
incorporates the condition of
no reaction until the escape, which was automatically included into
the left-hand side via the Robin boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{R}
. In
turn, if the reaction happens before the escape, the escape time
T_{q}

is set to infinity. In other words, one has

T_{q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{0}&\textrm{if}~\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}<\hat{\ell},\\
+\infty&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(64)

As earlier, the dependence on the reactivity parameter
q
is
incorporated via the random threshold
\hat{\ell}
obeying the
exponential law with the rate
q
. This representation highlights the
effect of surface reactions onto the fisrt-passage time
T_{q}
. In
fact, if the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
was inert, the particle would reach
the escape region
\partial\Omega_{D}
at a random time
T_{0}
. In turn, the
reactivity of
\partial\Omega_{R}
makes the only change that the particle can
react on
\partial\Omega_{R}
and thus never escape. If the boundary local time

\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
at
T_{0}
has not crossed the random threshold

\hat{\ell}
, the reaction on
\partial\Omega_{R}
has not happened, and
T_{q}=T_{0}
. In contrast, if the threshold
\hat{\ell}
has been crossed,
the reaction occurred before
T_{0}
so that
T_{q}=\infty
. The above
definition of the first-passage time to the escape region
\partial\Omega_{D}

within the encounter-based approach allows one to study this quantity
for other surface reaction mechanisms, beyond the conventional one
described by Robin boundary condition. For this purpose, one can
choose an appropriate law
\mathbb{P}\{\hat{\ell}>\ell\}
for the random
threshold
\hat{\ell}
and replace the factor
e^{-q\ell}
in
Eq. (
63
) by this law. Further investigations of this
setting present an interesting perspective of the present work.

III
Spherical target

In order to illustrate the general properties of diffusion-controlled
reactions with eventual escape, we consider diffusion between two
concentric spheres of radii
R
and
L
:
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~:~R<|\bm{x}|<L\}
. The inner sphere represents the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
,
while the outer sphere is the escape region
\partial\Omega_{D}
(in this setting,

\partial\Omega_{N}=\emptyset
). In spherical coordinates
\bm{x}=(r,\theta,\phi)
, the modified Helmholtz equation can be solved via
separation of variables. In particular, the eigenbasis of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator is well known

Grebenkov20b
;
Grebenkov20c
;
Grebenkov22c
. In fact, the
rotational invariance of the domain implies that the eigenfunctions of

\mathcal{M}_{p}
are the (normalized) spherical harmonics
Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi)

v_{nm}=\frac{1}{R}Y_{mn}(\theta,\phi)\qquad\left({n=0,1,\ldots,\atop|m|\leq n}\right),

(65)

where the double index
nm
is used instead of a single index
k
. In
turn, the eigenvalues are

\mu_{nm}^{(p)}=-\bigl(\partial_{r}g_{n}^{(p)}(r)\bigr)_{r=R}\,,

(66)

where

g_{n}^{(p)}(r)=\frac{k_{n}(\alpha L)i_{n}(\alpha r)-i_{n}(\alpha L)k_{n}(\alpha r)}{k_{n}(\alpha L)i_{n}(\alpha R)-i_{n}(\alpha L)k_{n}(\alpha R)}

(67)

are the radial functions, with
\alpha=\sqrt{p/D}
and
i_{n}(z)
and

k_{n}(z)
being the modified spherical Bessel functions of the first
and second kind. Note that
g_{n}^{(p)}(L)=0
and
g_{n}^{(p)}(R)=1
.
One sees that the eigenvalues are
(2n+1)
times degenerate (they do
not depend on the index
m
), while the eigenfunctions do not depend
on
p
. Finally, it is easy to check that

V_{nm}^{(p)}(\bm{x})=g_{n}^{(p)}(r)\,v_{nm}(\theta,\phi).

(68)

As most quantities of interest are obtained by integrating over the
target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
, the orthogonality of eigenfunctions
v_{nm}
to

v_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi R^{2}}
ensures that all terms vanish except this
ground eigenmode. Since
i_{0}(z)=\sinh(z)/z
and
k_{0}(z)=e^{-z}/z
, one finds

g_{0}^{(p)}(r)=\frac{R\,\sinh(\alpha(L-r))}{r\,\sinh(\alpha(L-R))}

(69)

and

\mu_{0}^{(p)}=\mu_{00}^{(p)}=\frac{1}{R}+\alpha\,\mathrm{ctanh}(\alpha(L-R)).

(70)

Note that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,V_{00}^{(p)}(\bm{x})=\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{4\pi R^{2}}}\int\limits_{R}^{L}dr\,r^{2}\,g_{0}^{(p)}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{\sinh(\alpha(L-R))}\biggl(-\frac{L}{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha R\cosh(\alpha(L-R))+\sinh(\alpha(L-R))}{\alpha^{2}}\biggr)

and

C_{0}^{(p)}=\sqrt{4\pi}\,\frac{\alpha L}{\sinh(\alpha(L-R))}\,.

One can also compute

\displaystyle J_{q}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{r_{0}}\,\frac{(1+qR)\sinh(\alpha(r_{0}-R))+\alpha R\cosh(\alpha(r_{0}-R))}{(1+qR)\sinh(\alpha(L-R))+\alpha R\cosh(\alpha(L-R))}\,,

(71a)

\displaystyle J_{q}^{R}(p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{r_{0}}\,\frac{qR\sinh(\alpha(L-r_{0}))}{(1+qR)\sinh(\alpha(L-R))+\alpha R\cosh(\alpha(L-R))}\,,

(71b)

where
r_{0}=|\bm{x}_{0}|
, from which

\displaystyle J_{\infty}^{R}(p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{R\,\sinh(\alpha(L-r_{0}))}{r_{0}\,\sinh(\alpha(L-R))}\,,

\displaystyle J_{\infty}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{L\sinh(\alpha(r_{0}-R))}{r_{0}\sinh(\alpha(L-R))}\,,

\displaystyle J_{0}^{D}(p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{r_{0}}\,\frac{\sinh(\alpha(r_{0}-R))+\alpha R\cosh(\alpha(r_{0}-R))}{\sinh(\alpha(L-R))+\alpha R\cosh(\alpha(L-R))}\,.

In the following illustrations, we fix units of length and time by
setting
R=1
and
D=1
.

III.1
Escape events

According to Eq. (
38
), we find

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{D}(\ell,p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{L\sinh(\alpha(r_{0}-R))}{r_{0}\sinh(\alpha(L-R))}\delta(\ell)

\displaystyle+\frac{L\,\alpha\,\sinh(\alpha(L-r_{0}))}{r_{0}\,\sinh^{2}(\alpha(L-R))}\,e^{-\mu_{0}^{(p)}\ell}\,,

(73)

from which an inverse Laplace transform with respect to
p
yields the
joint probability density
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
of
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
and

T_{0}
. The inversion of the first term in Eq. (
73
)
can be found explicitly via the residue theorem. In turn, the
presence of the
p
-dependent factor
e^{-\mu_{0}^{(p)}\ell}
in the
second term makes the inversion challenging (see
Grebenkov20c

for some analytical tools). For this reason, we employed a numerical
Laplace transform inversion by the Talbot algorithm
Talbot79
.

illustrates the behavior of
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
.

) give respectively singular and
regular contributions to this density with respect to
\ell
. The
regular contribution as a function of
\ell
and

\ell=0
is shown by a black line. For the panel

and

, i.e., the particle starts in the middle between the

R=1
and the escape region at
L=2
; in turn, in
the panel
(b)

.

Since the particle needs to diffuse from its starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
to
the escape region at
L
, its escape at very short times is highly
unlikely. In particular, the probability density
J_{\infty}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

of the first-passage time to
\partial\Omega_{D}
determining the singular term, is
known to exhibit the short-time behavior of a Lévy-Smirnov form

t^{-3/2}e^{-(L-r_{0})^{2}/(4Dt)}
(see
Godec16
;
Grebenkov18
and
references therein). Similar behavior can be derived for the regular
contribution. In the same vein, the probability of escape at very
long times is also negligible because the particle cannot avoid the
escape region too long. These arguments explain a distinct maximum of

J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
with respect to
t
at intermediate times.
Finally, too large values of the boundary local time
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}

for intermediate escape times would require for the particle to stay
too long near the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
, which is also highly
improbable. One sees therefore that
J_{\infty}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
exhibits a
single “boss” that rapidly goes down as
t\to 0
,
t\to\infty
, or
\ell\to\infty
.

The panel
(a)
of Fig.
2
indicates that the maximum of
the singular contribution (the curve
J_{\infty}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
) is shifted
to shorter times with respect to the boss of
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
. In
fact, the particle that never hit the target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
(and thus
has
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}=0
) needs to travel the distance
L-r_{0}
from the
starting point to the escape region that determines the maximum of

J_{\infty}^{D}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. In turn, the particle that first encountered

\partial\Omega_{R}
and then escaped, has to travel the longer distance
(r_{0}-R)+(L-R)
that requires longer time. When the starting point gets
closer to
\partial\Omega_{D}
, the separation between two maxima is even larger,
while the location of
\bm{x}_{0}
near
\partial\Omega_{R}
would reduce this
separation. Similarly, if
L
is much larger than
r_{0}
(as on the
panel
(b)
), the difference between two distances is relatively
small, and two maxima are close.

p=0
in Eq. (

\displaystyle\rho_{D}(\ell|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{r_{0}(L-R)}\biggl((r_{0}-R)\delta(\ell)

\displaystyle+\frac{L-r_{0}}{L-R}\,e^{-\ell(1/R+1/(L-R))}\biggr).

.

\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
are particularly simple:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}\}

\displaystyle=R^{2}(1/r_{0}-1/L)\,,

(76a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}\}

\displaystyle=R^{3}(1/r_{0}-1/L)(2-R/r_{0}-R/L),

(76b)

where
\mathbb{V}_{\bm{x}_{0}}
denotes the variance. The moments of
T_{0}
are
determined via Eq. (

); in particular, one has

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{T_{0}\}=\frac{(L-r_{0})\bigl(r_{0}L(r_{0}+L)-2R^{3}\bigr)}{6Dr_{0}L}

(77)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{T_{0}\}=\frac{L-r_{0}}{90D^{2}L^{2}r_{0}^{2}}\biggl(r_{0}^{2}L^{5}+L^{4}r_{0}^{3}+r_{0}^{4}L^{3}+r_{0}^{5}L^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-20L^{2}r_{0}^{2}R^{3}+36R^{5}r_{0}L-10R^{6}L-10R^{6}r_{0}\biggr).

(78)

In turn, the joint moments can be found from

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{[\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}]^{m}\,T_{0}^{n}\}=(-1)^{n}m!\frac{L}{r_{0}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial p^{n}}\biggl(\frac{\alpha\,\sinh(\alpha(L-r_{0}))}{\sinh^{2}(\alpha(L-R))}[\mu_{0}^{(p)}]^{-m-1}\biggr).

(79)

For instance, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}\,T_{0}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}(L-r_{0})}{6DL^{2}r_{0}}\biggl(2(L+2R)(L-R)^{2}

\displaystyle-L(L-r_{0})^{2}\biggr)\,.

(80)

Expectedly, all these quantities vanish as
r_{0}\to L
because the
particle started on
\partial\Omega_{D}
escapes immediately, yielding
T_{0}=\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}=0
in a deterministic way. The above expressions allow
one to compute the Peason’s correlation coefficient between
T_{0}
and

\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
:

C=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}\,T_{0}\}-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}\}\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{T_{0}\}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{T_{0}\}}\,\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\bm{x}_{0}}\{\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}\}}}\,.

(81)

Figure
4
shows the correlation coefficient
C
as a function
of
r_{0}/L
for two values of
L
. In both cases, the correlation is
positive. Indeed, if the particle escapes faster (at smaller
T_{0}
),
it would generally have lower chances to encounter the target region
frequently so that the boundary local time
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
would also
be smaller. Note that correlations are higher for the case
L=2

than for
L=10
. In other words, when the distance between the
escape and target regions is larger, the particle would generally take
longer time to diffuse in the confining domain before the escape and
thus to decorrelate these random variables. Even though all moments
vanish as
r_{0}\to L
, the correlation coefficient gets a nontrivial
limit. Curiously, both curves exhibit slightly non-monotonous
behavior with respect to
r_{0}
.

IV
Conclusion

In this paper, we revised the encounter-based approach to
diffusion-mediated surface phenomena and generalized this formalism by
allowing a generic partition of the boundary
\partial\Omega
into three parts: a
target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
, a reflecting region
\partial\Omega_{N}
and an escape
region
\partial\Omega_{D}
. While the original formulation in
Grebenkov20

dealt with the whole boundary as the target (
\partial\Omega=\partial\Omega_{R}
), our
generalization brings a greater flexibility to modeling
diffusion-controlled reactions and covers a broad scope of related
first-passage problems. From the mathematical point of view, this
extension essentially consists in adding Neumann and Dirichlet
boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega_{N}
and
\partial\Omega_{D}
, respectively. Despite
this apparent simplicity, the inclusion of a “killing mechanism” for
the diffusing particle (an escape through
\partial\Omega_{D}
) has required some
conceptual refinements and appropriate modifications in the formalism.
In particular, we focused in the paper on the probability flux density

j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
, which was mostly ignored in former works.

On the one hand, the restriction of
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the escape
region
\partial\Omega_{D}
determined, for the first time, the joint probability
density of the escape time
T_{0}
, the particle position
\bm{X}_{T_{0}}
,
and the acquired boundary local time
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
on the target
region
\partial\Omega_{R}
. In turn, the marginal density
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
, in
which the position
\bm{X}_{T_{0}}
was averaged out, allows one to
characterize not only the statistics of the number of encounters
between the target region and the diffusing particle before its
escape, but also correlations with the escape time. In particular, we
obtained three equivalent representations (
44
,

48
,
49
) of the joint moments of

\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
and
T_{0}
and revealed a probabilistic interpretation
of multiple derivatives of the Green’s function

\tilde{G}_{q}(\bm{x},p|\bm{x}_{0})
with respect to
p
and
q
. Note that
earlier works dealt exclusively with the escape time itself and were
mainly focused on the mean value and its dependence on the geometric
and kinetic parameters. However, it is important to emphasize that
the mean escape time, which is mostly affected by long but rare
trajectories, may be orders of magnitude larger than the typical time
of the escape process, and thus be noninformative or even misleading

Grebenkov18
;
Mattos12
;
Godec16b
;
Grebenkov18b
;
Reva21
. For
instance, this may occur when the particle starts in a neighborhood of
a small escape region. In this situation, the whole distribution of
the escape time (or another quantity such as
\ell_{T_{0}}^{R}
) is needed
to characterize the escape process. For this reason, we focused on
the probability flux densities that were much less studied in the
past.

On the other hand, the restriction of
j(\bm{x},\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the
target region
\partial\Omega_{R}
required a subtle probabilistic interpretation
in terms of the first-crossing time
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
of a given threshold

\ell
by the boundary local time
\ell_{t}^{R}
. In particular, the
integral of
j(\bm{x},\ell^{\prime},t|\bm{x}_{0})
over
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega_{R}
and over
\ell^{\prime}

from
0
to
\ell
determined the probability density
U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

of
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
. The latter played a key role in the generalization of
conventional surface reactions, described by the Robin boundary
condition, to more general mechanisms. While this generalization was
already presented in
Grebenkov20
, its extension in the presence
of escape events required some refinements. For instance, the former
derivation relied on the nondecreasing character of the boundary local
time
\ell_{t}
and its immediate consequence that
\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{T}_{\ell}>t\}=\mathbb{P}\{\ell_{t}<\ell\}
. In the presence of the escape region, this
relation has to be replaced by Eq. (
54
) accounting for
the value
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}=\infty
in the case if the particle has escaped
before crossing the threshold
\ell
. More generally, the lack of a
proper normalization of probability densities had to be carefully
managed.

For illustrative purposes, we considered diffusion between concentric
spheres, the inner sphere of radius
R
being the target region and
the outer sphere of radius
L
being the escape region. This basic
example allowed us to compute all the discussed quantities in a simple
compact form in the Laplace domain and then to evaluate the inverse
Laplace transform numerically. We presented both
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

and
J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
, as well as some derived quantities such as

\rho_{D}(\ell|\bm{x}_{0})
and
U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
. We discussed two cases:
L=2
(when the target and escape regions are relatively close to each
other), and
L=10
(when they are well separated). Expectedly, the
joint probability density
J_{D}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
strongly depends on the
location of the escape region, whereas
J_{R}(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
showed only
a weak dependence on
L
. In particular, the probability density

U(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})
approaches the well-known explicit form
(
86
) for a spherical target in the three-dimensional
space.

While we considered ordinary diffusion inside the confining domain,
the presented formalism allows one to easily incorporate a first-order
reaction kinetics in the bulk. This kinetics may account for a
spontaneous disappearance of the diffusing particle (or its
“activity”) with a constant rate
\gamma
due to radioactive
disintegration, photobleaching, relaxation of its excited state,
disassembly, failure, or biological death. Whatever the actual
killing mechanism, such a “mortal” particle can be treated as having
a random lifetime
\delta
that obeys the exponential law
\mathbb{P}\{\delta>t\}=e^{-\gamma t}

Yuste13
;
Meerson15
;
Grebenkov17f
;
Meerson19
. In the conventional
approach, the killing mechanism in the bulk is included by adding the
term
-\gamma G_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{x}_{0})
to the right-hand side of the diffusion
equation (
1
). Its Laplace transform with respect to

t
yields the same modified Helmholtz equation (
19
), in
which
p
is replaced by
p^{\prime}=p+\gamma
. As a consequence, most
results that we obtained in the Laplace domain, remain valid up to
this trivial change. Moreover, many Laplace-transformed quantities,
evaluated at
p=\gamma
, admit useful probabilistic interpretations
in terms of the stopping condition at the “death” time
\delta
.
For instance,

\gamma\,\tilde{\rho}(\ell,\gamma|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\gamma e^{-\gamma t}\,\rho(\ell,t|\bm{x}_{0})

(87)

is the probability density of the boundary local time
\ell_{\delta}^{R}

at the random time
\delta
of the particle “death” in the bulk,
with
\gamma e^{-\gamma t}
being the probability density of
\delta
.
In other words, many Laplace-transformed quantities that we derived in
this paper, have their own interest, even without evaluating their
inverse Laplace transforms. The combined effect of the first-order
kinetics in the bulk, surface reactions on the target region, and
escape events can be further explored.

The present work can be extended in several directions. On the
mathematical side, the spectral properties of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann
operator need further attention. In addition to a rigorous
demonstration of the announced basic properties, the asymptotic
behavior of the eigenvalues
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
has to be uncovered, in
particular, in the limit when either
\partial\Omega_{R}
or
\partial\Omega_{D}
(or both) is
small. We expect that some matched asymptotic tools

Bressloff22a
;
Bressloff22b
can be adapted to investigate this
problem. On the application side, one can investigate in more detail
the effect of escape events onto various surface reaction mechanisms
introduced in
Grebenkov20
. The proposed extension can be
further analyzed in the presence of multiple independently diffusing
particles
Grebenkov22b
, eventual resetting mechanisms

Evans20
;
Bressloff22c
;
Benkhadaj22
and drifts

Grebenkov22a
. Future applications of the extended
encounter-based approach can bring more realistic features to a
theoretical description of biology-inspired transport processes,
notably in living cells.
