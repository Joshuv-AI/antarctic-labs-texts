Bad Values but Good Behavior: Learning Highly Misspecified Bandits and MDPs
Debangshu Banerjee, Aditya Gopalan

Abstract
Parametric, feature-based reward models are employed by a variety of algorithms in decision-making settings such as bandits and Markov decision processes (MDPs). The typical assumption under which the algorithms are analysed is realizability, i.e., that the true values of actions are perfectly explained by some parametric model in the class. We are, however, interested in the situation where the true values are (significantly) misspecified with respect to the model class. For parameterized bandits, contextual bandits and MDPs, we identify structural conditions, depending on the problem instance and model class, under which basic algorithms such as ε \varepsilon -greedy, LinUCB and fitted Q-learning provably learn optimal policies under even highly misspecified models. This is in contrast to existing worst-case results for, say misspecified bandits, which show regret bounds that scale linearly with time, and shows that there can be a nontrivially large set of bandit instances that are robust to misspecification.

Introduction

Sequential optimization over a set of decisions, e.g., actions in a multi-armed bandit and policies in an MDP, is often carried out by assuming an internal parametric model for the payoff of a decision, which is learnt with time. Well-known instantiations of this approach are algorithms for structured multi-armed bandits, e.g., linear bandits
(
Rusmevichientong and Tsitsiklis 2010
)
and generalized linear bandits
(
Filippi et al. 2010
)
, linear contextual bandits
(
Chu et al. 2011
)
, and, more generally, value function approximation-based methods for Markov Decision Processes
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
.

Learning algorithms that make decisions based on an estimated reward model are known to enjoy attractive performance (regret) guarantees when the true rewards encountered are
perfectly realizable
by the model, see, e.g.,
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
;
Filippi et al. 2010
;
Jin et al. 2020
. However, it is more likely than not that a parametric class of models is, at best, only an approximation of reality, succinctly expressed by the aphorism ‘All models are wrong, but some are useful’
(
Box 1976
)
. But even if the rewards of, say, arms in a multi-armed bandit, are unfortunately estimated with a large error, one may still hope to discern the optimal arm if its (erroneous) estimate ends up dominating those of the other arms. This begs the natural question: While the task of reward estimation can be fraught with (arbitrarily large) error under misspecified models, when (if at all) can the task of optimal action learning remain immune to it?

We initiate a rigorous study of the extent to which sequential decision-making algorithms based on reward model estimation can be robust to misspecification in the model. In particular, we are interested in characterizing the interplay between i) the actual (ground truth) rewards in a decision-making problem and ii) the reward model class used by the algorithm, and how it governs whether the algorithm can still learn to play optimally if the true rewards are not realizable by the reward model. In this respect, our specific contributions are as follows:

1.

For misspecified linear bandits, we identify a novel family of instances (reward vectors), which we term the robust observation region. Reward vectors in this region are characterized by an invariance property of the greedy action that they induce after being projected, with respect to any weighted Euclidean norm, onto the linear feature subspace. This region, depending upon the feature subspace, can be non-trivially large, and need not be confined to within a small deviation from the subspace.

2.

We prove that for any instance (i.e., the vector of true mean arm rewards) in the robust observation region, both (i) the
\varepsilon
-greedy algorithm, with least-squares parameter estimation and an exploration rate of
1/\sqrt{t}
in each round
t
, and (ii) the LinUCB (or OFUL) algorithm, achieve
O(\sqrt{T})
cumulative regret in time
T
.

3.

We extend our characterisation of robust instances to linear contextual bandits, for which we provide a generalization of the robust observation region. We show that both the
\varepsilon
-greedy and LinUCB algorithms for linear contextual bandits get
O(\sqrt{T})
regret whenever the true, misspecified, reward vector belongs to this robust observation region.

4.

We finally provide a structural criterion for a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP), together with a Q-value approximation function class, for which the fitted Q-iteration algorithm provably learns a (near) optimal policy in spite of arbitrarily large approximation error (in the
\infty
-norm sense).

We stress that our results pertain to the original algorithms (i.e., not modified to be misspecification-aware in any manner). It is our novel analytical approach that shows that they achieve nontrivial sublinear regret, even under arbitrarily large misspecification error

1.1
Related Work

Existing work on misspecified bandits contributes results that can be put into two categories. The first category is negative results of a worst-case form, for instance: there exist reward instances for linear bandits for which (i) the model misspecification error (
l_{\infty}
distance of the reward vector from the model subspace
\text{Range}(\bm{\Phi})
) is at most
\rho>0
and (ii) the LinUCB algorithm suffers regret
\Omega(\rho T)
in
T
rounds. Results of this nature are well established in the misspecified bandit and contextual bandit literature, including but not limited to the works of
Ghosh et al. 2017
;
Lattimore et al. 2020
;
Zanette et al. 2020b
.
Lattimore et al. 2020
present a frequentist analysis that shows that even with the knowledge of the misspecification error, a modified form of LinUCB fails to give sub-linear regret in the misspecified contextual setting.
Zanette et al. 2020b
extend a similar analysis to learning MDPs.

The second type of result is along a positive direction: to develop conditions, and associated algorithms, under which misspecified bandits can give sub-linear regret. The work of
Liu et al. 2023
analyzes the LinUCB algorithm when the sub-optimality gaps of the arms bound the misspecification. They show that when the misspecification is of low order, the algorithm enjoys sub-linear regret. Under a similar condition,
Zhang et al. 2023
were able to extend the study to the contextual setting. They propose a phased arm elimination algorithm, which performs similarly to SupLinUCB
(
Chu et al. 2011
)
but requires knowledge of the sub-optimality gap.

Our results, in this positive spirit, give more fine-grained structural conditions
to ensure that algorithms such as
\varepsilon
-greedy, LinUCB or Fitted-
Q
-learning can achieve robust learning without any additional modifications. A detailed
survey of previous works is presented in Appendix
K
.

Problem Statement and Results

We describe our results for misspecified linear bandits and then proceed to the misspecified contextual case in the second part of this section.

2.1
Linear Bandits

Problem Statement

We consider a
K
-armed bandit with mean rewards
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. We assume each arm
i
is associated with a known feature vector
\varphi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also have a given parametric class of functions serving to (approximately) model the mean reward of each arm; each function in this class is of the form
f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for a parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, and applying it to arm
i
yields the expressed reward
f_{\theta}(\varphi_{i})\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us assume that the number of arms,
K
, is larger than the dimensionality of the parameter
\theta
, that is
K>d
and, for ease of analysis, consider the set of features
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
to span
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We shall denote the set of true mean rewards by the vector,

\bm{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{i}\end{bmatrix}_{i\in[K]}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{K}}
and the feature matrix
\bm{\Phi}=\begin{bmatrix}\cdots\varphi_{i}^{\top}\cdots\\
\end{bmatrix}_{i\in[K]}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}
. The assumption on the features fixes the rank of
\bm{\Phi}
as
d
.

Remark 2.1

(
Linear Bandits
)
.

Our setting is rather general and covers a broad class of parametric bandits. For example, in linear bandits
(
Dani et al. 2008
)
, it is assumed that the means are linear functions of the features, that is, there exists a
\theta^{*}
, such that
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\Phi}\theta^{*}
.

Remark 2.2

(
Misspecification
)
.

We allow the vector of true rewards
\bm{\mu}
to be arbitrary, without imposing a realizability condition like
\bm{\mu}\;\;\in\;\;\{f_{\theta}(\varphi_{i})\;\forall\;i\in[K]:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
.

We shall derive sufficient conditions under which (misspecified) bandit instances
\bm{\mu}
can yield sub-linear regret under standard algorithms like
\varepsilon
-greedy and LinUCB. A key step towards this is to identify a
robust region
which depends on the model class such that any instance in this region is guaranteed to enjoy sub-linear regret.

We begin by recalling the usual
greedy regions
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{K}}
which characterize reward vectors that share the same optimal arm.

Definition 2.3

(
Greedy Region
\mathcal{R}
)
.

Define by
\mathcal{R}_{k}
, for any
k\in[K]
, as the region in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{K}}
for which the
k^{th}
arm is optimal, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{k}\triangleq\Big\{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{K}}:\mu_{k}>\mu_{i}\forall i\neq k\Big\}
.

For the purposes of clarity, let us fix our model class to be linear, so that the least squares estimate has a closed form solution amenable to explicit analysis. Denoting the play count of each arm
i
up to time
t
by
\alpha(i,t)=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{1}\{A_{s}=i\}
, the least squares estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{t+1}
can be written as

\widehat{\theta}_{t+1}=\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha(i,t)\varphi_{i}\varphi_{i}^{\top}\Big]^{-1}(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha(i,t)\varphi_{i}\widehat{\mu}_{i,t})=(\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\Lambda(t)\bm{\Phi})^{-1}\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\Lambda\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\,
,
where
\widehat{\mu}_{i,t}
is the sample mean of the rewards from arm
i
as observed till time
t
, that is
\widehat{\mu}_{i,t}=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}y_{s}\mathbb{1}\{A_{s}=i\}}{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{1}\{A_{s}=i\}}

\Lambda(t)
is a

\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}

K

\widehat{\mu}_{i,t}
as its elements
(
Gopalan et al. 2016
)
.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that as per our assumption on the rank of the feature matrix,
\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\Lambda(t)\bm{\Phi}
is invertible if
\alpha(i,t)>0
for any
d
linearly independent features. This can be ensured by explicitly forcing the algorithm to sample
d
such features at least once. For ease of exposition, we shall assume that
\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\Lambda(t)\bm{\Phi}
is invertible. In Appendix
D
we show how the ideas presented here can be extended to include a regularizer term.

We note from the closed form solution of the least squares estimate, that we can equivalently interpret the play counts
\alpha(i,t)
for any arm
i\in[K]
in terms of (normalized) sampling frequencies:
\alpha(i)=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{1}\{A_{s}=i\}}{t}
for any
t\geqslant 1
, so that
\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K}
belongs to the
K
dimensional simplex
\Delta_{\mathrm{K}}
. that is
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha(i)=1
. With this notation, we can observe that any least squares estimate is calculated based upon some sampling frequency
\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K}\in\Delta_{\mathrm{K}}
and the corresponding sample estimates
\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}
. The least squares estimate can now be rewritten as an optimization problem with respect to a weighted norm:
\widehat{\theta}_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta}\Big\|\bm{\Phi}\theta-\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\Big\|_{\Lambda^{1/2}}
, for some sampling frequency
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K})
in the simplex
\Delta_{\mathrm{K}}
.

Definition 2.5

(
Model Estimate under Sampling Distribution
)
.

For any bandit instance
\bm{\mu}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{K}}
, we shall denote the model estimate of the
K

\bm{\mu}
under any sampling distribution, defined by
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K})
, as
\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})\triangleq\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta}\Big\|\bm{\Phi}\theta-\bm{\mu}\Big\|_{\Lambda^{1/2}}
.

Remark 2.6
.

With this definition, we observe that the least squares estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{t}
is
\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}})
in our notation, for some sampling distribution defined by
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K})
and the corresponding sample estimates of
\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}
. Further note, that another way of describing
\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})
is by considering the least squares estimate, with
\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}
being exactly
\bm{\mu}
, which is equivalent of saying there is no stochasticity in the observations of each arm pull.

Given a given bandit instance
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{K}}
, assume, without loss of generality, that
k
is the optimal arm, that is
\bm{\mu}
belongs to the
k^{th}
greedy region
\mathcal{R}_{k}
. Now consider the model estimate
\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})

\bm{\mu}
(that is, no stochasticity), using a sampling distribution of
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K})
. If the projection,
\bm{\Phi}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})
belongs to the true greedy region
\mathcal{R}_{k}
, then under this sampling distribution, the model estimate would always return the optimal arm under a greedy strategy. That is,
\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{i\in[K]}\varphi_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})
would result in the
k^{th}
arm being pulled.
With this motivation, we define
robust regions
.

Definition 2.7

(
Robust Parameter Region
)
.

For a given feature matrix
\bm{\Phi}
, we define the
k^{th}

robust parameter region

\Theta_{k}
as the the set of all parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the range space of
\bm{\Phi}

\Theta_{k}
lies in the
k^{th}

\mathcal{R}_{k}
. That is

\Theta_{k}=\Big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{\Phi}\theta\in\mathcal{R}_{k}\Big\},\;\;\;\text{for any arm }k.

Definition 2.8

(
Robust Observation Region
)
.

For a given feature matrix
\bm{\Phi}
, we define the
k^{th}

robust observation region

\mathcal{C}_{k}
, as the set of all
K

\bm{\mu}
with optimal arm

, such that under any sampling distribution
\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K}\in\Delta_{\mathrm{K}}
, the corresponding model estimate,
\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})
, lies in the
k^{th}
robust parameter region,
\Theta_{k}
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}=\Big\{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{R}_{k}:\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})\in\Theta_{k}\;\forall\;\Lambda\in\bm{\Lambda}\Big\}\;\;\;\textit{for any arm }k,

where

\bm{\Lambda}=\Big\{\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K}):\{\alpha(i)\}_{i=1}^{K}\in\Delta_{\mathrm{K}}\Big\}.

From the definitions of the
robust observation region
and

, we observe that if
\bm{\mu}
belongs to the

robust observation region
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, then the greedy strategy
\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{i\in[K]}\varphi_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\mu)
would ensure the optimal arm

is chosen under any sampling distribution of the arms.

3
Conclusion

In this work, we present a systematic study of model misspecified instances in the Bandit, Contextual Bandit, and Markov-Decision-Process settings which are acquiescent to
standard
algorithms. Previous theoretical works have all indicated that these standard algorithms are not enough to learn the optimal policy without the realizability assumption, despite their panacean use in practical experiments. In our work, we drop the realizability assumption while systematically theorizing the existence, and identifying instances that, though unrealized, are not antagonistic to the learning algorithms. On this note, it is to be observed that we give instances that can be highly misspecified, and yet the model class can learn the optimal policy. Our work is not an attempt to provide insights into how one can better utilize the concept of robust regions to construct better features or better algorithms. Rather, it is a theoretical understanding through geometry, the interplay between model misspecification, and the essence of learning algorithms. We construct a theory that can explain our observations and subsequently build upon the theory to encompass a wide array of sequential decision-making problems.

Remark A.2
.

The proof essentially uses the same technique as presented in
Auer et al. 2002
. The key observation is that the least squares estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{t}
, in our notation
\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}})
, is guaranteed to generate optimal play under the greedy strategy, if the sample mean estimate
\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}
falls inside the robust region
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. The concentration of sub-Gaussian random variables ensure that given enough samples
\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}
will fall within a
\delta
-neighbourhood of the true rewards
\bm{\mu}
. The fact that
\bm{\mu}
is an interior point of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, ensures a that there exists
\delta
- neighbourhood about
\bm{\mu}
that is contained in the robust region
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.

Proof.

Since
\bm{\mu}
is an interior point of the robust region
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, by Definition
2.11
, there exists a
\delta
-cell centred at
\bm{\mu}
, denoted as
\mathbf{1}_{\delta}(\bm{\mu})
which is contained in the robust observation region
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. We observe that the probability of choosing a sub-optimal arm
i
at

round is either due to a random play, which happens with probability
\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{K}
or during the greedy play, when the reward estimate
\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}
does not belong to
\mathbf{1}_{\delta}(\mu)
, the
\delta
cell of
\bm{\mu}
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{A_{t}=i}\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{K}+(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{K})\mathbb{P}\left[{\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\notin\mathbf{1}_{\delta}(\bm{\mu})}\right].

Now
\mathbb{P}\left[{\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\notin\mathbf{1}_{\delta}(\bm{\mu})}\right]
, from the definition of the
\delta
-cell, can be upper bounded by taking a union bound over all arms to give

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{\bm{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\notin\mathbf{1}_{\delta}(\bm{\mu})}\right]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left[{|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{n_{i,t}}-\mu_{i}|\geqslant\delta}\right]\;,

where
\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{n_{i,t}}
is the sample estimate of arm
i
, having played
n_{i,t}
times till time
t
. The remainder of the proof has the same flavour as in the work of
Auer et al. 2002
. We provide here for the sake of completeness.
Note that each term
\mathbb{P}\left[{|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{n_{i,t}}-\mu_{i}|\geqslant\delta}\right]
can be bounded in the following manner,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{n_{i,t}}-\mu_{i}|\geqslant\delta\Big\}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}\Big\{|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}|\geqslant\delta,n_{i,t}=s\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}\Big\{n_{i,t}=s\mid\left|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}\right|\geqslant\delta\Big\}\mathbb{P}\Big\{|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}|\geqslant\delta\Big\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}\Big\{n_{i,t}=s\mid\left|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}\right|\geqslant\delta\Big\}2\exp{(-2s\delta^{2})},

where we use the sub-Gaussian concentration by Assumption
2.10
and Lemma
J.5
,

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{P}\Big\{n_{i,t}=s\mid\left|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}\right|\geqslant\delta\Big\}+\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}2\exp{(-2s\delta^{2})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{P}\Big\{n_{i,t}=s\mid\left|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}\right|\geqslant\delta\Big\}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\exp{(-2t_{0}\delta^{2})}\;,

where we use the identity
\sum_{t=x+1}^{\infty}\exp(-\kappa t)\leqslant\frac{1}{\kappa}\exp(-\kappa x)
. Let
n_{i,t}^{R}
be the number of times arm
i
has been played randomly till time
t
, then,

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{P}\Big\{n_{i,t}=s\mid\left|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}\right|\geqslant\delta\Big\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{P}\Big\{n_{i,t}^{R}\leqslant s\mid\left|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}\right|\geqslant\delta\Big\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{P}\{n_{i,t}^{R}\leqslant s\}

\displaystyle\leqslant t_{0}\mathbb{P}\{n_{i,t}^{R}\leqslant t_{0}\}.

Now

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{n_{i,t}^{R}}\right]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}

and variance of
n_{i,t}^{R}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[n_{i,t}^{R}]=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon_{s}}{K}\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{s}}{K}\Big)\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon_{s}}{K}.

Thus, choosing

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{n_{i,t}^{R}\leqslant t_{0}\}\leqslant\exp(-t_{0}/5)\;,

from Bernstein’s Inequality, Lemma
J.6

Thus putting it all together we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{A_{t}=i}\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{K}+Kt_{0}\exp(-t_{0}/5)+\frac{K}{\delta^{2}}\exp{(-2t_{0}\delta^{2})}.

Thus, in order to complete the proof we only need to find a lower bound on

As per our definition,

\displaystyle t_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}.

Plugging the value of
\varepsilon_{s}=1/\sqrt{s}
, we get

\displaystyle t_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{K}(\sqrt{t+1}-1).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{A_{t}=i}\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{K\sqrt{t}}+(\sqrt{t+1}-1)\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{t+1}-1}{5K})+\frac{K}{\delta^{2}}\exp{\Big(-\frac{2\delta^{2}(\sqrt{t+1}-1)}{K}\Big)}.

where
\Delta_{i}
is the sub-optimality gap
\mu_{k}-\mu_{i}
.
Thus cumulative regret is
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Regret}_{t}=O(\Delta_{\max}\sqrt{T})
.
∎

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{P}\Big\{n_{x,a,t}^{R}\leqslant s\mid\left|\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{s}-\mu_{i}\right|\geqslant\delta\Big\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{P}\{n_{x,a,t}^{R}\leqslant s\}

\displaystyle\leqslant t_{0}\mathbb{P}\{n_{x,a,t}^{R}\leqslant t_{0}\}.

Now, since states
x\in\mathcal{X}
is chosen statistically independently with probability
\mathbf{p}_{x}
over the state-space (Assumption
2.26
) and during random play actions are chosen independently and uniformly over action space
\mathcal{A}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{n_{x,a,t}^{R}}\right]=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}

and variance of
n_{x,a,t}^{R}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[n_{x,a,t}^{R}]=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}\varepsilon_{s}}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\Big(1-\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}\varepsilon_{s}}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\Big)\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}.

Thus, choosing
t_{0}=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}}{2\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{n_{x,a,t}^{R}\leqslant t_{0}\}\leqslant\exp(-t_{0}/5)\;,

from Bernstein’s Inequality, Lemma
J.6
.
Thus putting it all together, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{A_{t}=a\mid X_{t}=x}\right]\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}+\left|\mathcal{X}\mathcal{A}\right|t_{0}\exp(-t_{0}/5)+\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}\mathcal{A}\right|}{\delta^{2}}\exp{(-2t_{0}\delta^{2})}.

Thus, to complete the proof, we only need to find a lower bound on
t_{0}
.
As per our definition,

\displaystyle t_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}}{2\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{s}

Plugging the value of
\varepsilon_{s}=1/\sqrt{s}
, we get

\displaystyle t_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}}{2\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}(\sqrt{t+1}-1).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{A_{t}=a\mid X_{t}=x}\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|\sqrt{t}}+\mathbf{p}_{x}\left|\mathcal{X}\right|(\sqrt{t+1}-1)\exp(-\frac{\mathbf{p}_{x}(\sqrt{t+1}-1)}{5\left|\mathcal{A}\right|})+\frac{\left|\mathcal{X}\mathcal{A}\right|}{\delta^{2}}\exp{\Big(-\frac{2\delta^{2}\mathbf{p}_{x}(\sqrt{t+1}-1)}{\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}\Big)}.

Thus, the expected regret at time
t
at state
x
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Regret}_{x,t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Delta_{x,a}\mathbb{P}\left[{A_{t}=a\mid X_{t}=x}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Delta_{x,\max}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}+o(1/t)\;,

where
\Delta_{x,\max}=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Delta_{x,a}
and
\Delta_{x,a}=\mu_{x,\mathrm{OPT}(x)}-\mu_{x,a}
.
Thus the expected regret at time
t
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Regret}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Regret}_{x,t}\mathbb{P}\left[{X_{t}=x}\right]\leqslant\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{p}_{x}\Big(\Delta_{x,\max}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}+o(1/t)\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\Delta_{\max}}{\sqrt{t}}+o(1/t).

The cumulative regret is, therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Regret}_{t}\leqslant O(\Delta_{\max}\sqrt{T}).

∎

For any contextual bandit instance
\bm{\mu}

Remark B.1

Note that the only non standard modification that we have included in the algorithm as given in

The main difficulty is while we can prove that the sample estimates

Lemma B.9

(Lower Bound of per instant Regret in the Function Space)

i

Proof.

By definition of

2.8

\bm{\mu}\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{OPT}(\bm{\mu})})

\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}(\bm{\mu})

\bm{\Phi}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda(t)}(\bm{\mu})

.
Thus the cumulative regret in the function space is

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varphi_{\mathrm{OPT}(\bm{\mu})}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda(s)}(\bm{\mu})-\varphi_{A_{s}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda(s)}(\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t}2\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|_{{V_{s}}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{s}(\delta)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}2\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|_{{V_{s}}^{-1}}R\sqrt{2\log\Big(\frac{\mathrm{det}V_{s}^{1/2}}{\delta}\Big)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sqrt{2}R\sum_{s=1}^{t}\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|_{{V_{s}}^{-1}}\sqrt{\log\Big(\frac{(1+s/d)^{d/2}}{\delta}\Big)},

where in the last inequality we use Lemma
J.2
and our Assumption
B.3
on bounded features.
Thus continuing, we have,

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}R\sum_{s=1}^{t}\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|_{{V_{s}}^{-1}}\sqrt{\log\Big(\frac{(1+t/d)^{d/2}}{\delta}\Big)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sqrt{2}R\sqrt{\log\Big(\frac{(1+t/d)^{d/2}}{\delta}\Big)}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|_{{V_{s}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sqrt{2}R\sqrt{\log\Big(\frac{(1+t/d)^{d/2}}{\delta}\Big)}\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|^{2}_{{V_{s}}^{-1}}}.

From our assumption that
\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|_{2}\leqslant 1
and from the forced exploration start we have
\lambda_{\min}(V)=1
, we use Lemma
J.3
to get,

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}R\sqrt{\log\Big(\frac{(1+t/d)^{d/2}}{\delta}\Big)}\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\|\varphi_{A_{s}}\|^{2}_{{V_{s}}^{-1}}}

\displaystyle 4\sqrt{t}R\sqrt{\log\Big(\frac{(1+t/d)^{d/2}}{\delta}\Big)}\sqrt{\log\mathrm{det}V_{t}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\sqrt{t}R\sqrt{\log\Big(\frac{(1+t/d)^{d/2}}{\delta}\Big)}\sqrt{\log{(1+t/d)^{d}}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{t}).

Thus what we have shown is that the regret in the function space is of order
\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{t})
. Note that from our definition of the minimum regret in the function space,
\Delta_{\min}
given by Lemma
B.9
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\varphi_{\mathrm{OPT}(\bm{\mu})}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda(s)}(\bm{\mu})-\varphi_{A_{s}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda(s)}(\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{1}\{A_{s}\neq\mathrm{OPT}(\bm{\mu})\}\Delta_{\min}.

Together, this gives the result.
∎

Remark B.13
.

Note that from the results of
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
, we can also derive a logarithmic regret bound. However, we leave that as an exercise for the reader.

Comparison to the works of
Liu et al. 2023

The authors study the problem of misspecification under a robustness criterion which characterizes the misspecification to be dominated by the sub-optimality gap. Under such a condition, they show that LinUCB enjoys
O(\sqrt{T})
regret when the misspecification has low order, specifically, it is of order
O\Big(\frac{1}{d\sqrt{\log{T}}}\Big)

\rho T
if
\rho
is the misspecification error. However, we would like to address the following points while comparing our work

1.

Our notion of robustness is significantly different from theirs as we are able to show examples which achieve sub-linear regret even if the misspecification error dominates the sub-optimality gap.

2.

For our analysis of LinUCB we do not require the assumption of low misspecification error.

3.

We were able to analyse both
\varepsilon
-greedy and LinUCB under our notion of robustness in both the bandit and the contextual setting. Our analysis of LinUCB indicates that Thompson Sampling could be analysed as well under the same robustness condition, whereas with their notion of robustness, they were only able to analyse LinUCB under a low misspecification assumption.

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{t+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Big(f_{\theta}(A_{s})-y_{A_{s}}\Big)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{\mu})\triangleq\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\alpha(a)\Big(f_{\theta}(a)-\mu_{a}\Big)^{2}\;.

Appendix K
Related Work

Linear Bandits

The multi-armed bandit problem has been studied extensively, and a large body of literature surrounds it. The text by
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
provides a comprehensive text on the study of bandit algorithms. The classical works on stochastic linear bandits with finitely many arms have been studied by
Auer 2002
. Algorithms based on the principle of
optimism in the face of uncertainty

Dani et al. 2008
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
and Thompson Sampling
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
;
Abeille and Lazaric 2017
are popular choices of algorithms which enjoy sub-linear regret even in the worst case of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
.
Phased elimination
with optimal design based algorithms attain a regret of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{dT})
as shown in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
.

Contextual Linear Bandits

The bandit problem where the arm set changes at every time, described in the literature as the contextual bandit setup, has also been studied extensively. We mention the classic work of
Auer 2002
;
Chu et al. 2011
. The classic algorithms of SupLinRel
Auer 2002
and SupLinUCB
Chu et al. 2011
are
elimination
based algorithms and enjoy a worst-case regret bound of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{dT})
. In practice, optimism-based algorithms like LinUCB
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
as well as Thompson Sampling
Agrawal and Goyal 2013
enjoy sub-linear regret of
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
.

Misspecified Bandits With Linear Regret

The model misspecification setting in linear bandit setting was first studied in
Ghosh et al. 2017
. They pointed that in the presence of model error, the worst-case regret of LinUCB
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
is of linear order. They further showed that in a favorable case, when one can test the linearity of the reward function, the RLB algorithm
Ghosh et al. 2017
can switch between the linear bandit algorithm and finite-armed bandit algorithm to address the misspecification issue and achieve a
O(\min\{\sqrt{K},d\}\sqrt{n})
regret. Under the definition of uniform
l_{\infty}
model error
\varepsilon
,
Lattimore et al. 2020
presented an algorithm based on the principle of
phased elimination
which achieves a worst case regret of
O(d\sqrt{n}+\varepsilon\sqrt{d}n)
. They also showed that contextual linear bandits
LinUCB
achieve the same regret after modifying the confidence width using knowledge of the misspecification. In the same contextual bandit settings,
Foster and Rakhlin 2020
showed a similar linear regret. They showed that under the assumption of an oracle regressor, the algorithm SquareCB
Foster and Rakhlin 2020
suffers a regret of
O(\sqrt{dKn}+\varepsilon\sqrt{d}n)
.
Zanette et al. 2020b
has also shown a similar regret in the contextual setting. There has also been work based on altered definitions of misspecifications.
Kumar Krishnamurthy et al. 2021
defines the misspecification as an expected square loss between the true reward class and the approximation function class and uses a model-based algorithm
\varepsilon
- FALCON, which again suffers linear regret.
Foster et al. 2020
defines an empirical misspecification as observed by the data. However, these still suffer linear regret.

Missecified Bandits With Sub-Linear Regrets

Recently, there has been some work which describes sub-linear regret in the misspecified setting. The result of
Liu et al. 2023
analyzes the LinUCB algorithm when the sub-optimality gaps of the arms bound the misspecification. They show that when the misspecification is of low order, the algorithm enjoys sub-linear regret. Under a similar condition,
Zhang et al. 2023
were able to extend the study to the contextual setting. They propose a phased arm elimination algorithm, which performs similarly to SupLinUCB
Chu et al. 2011
but requires knowledge of the sub-optimality gap.

Markov Decision Processes

Function approximation in Reinforcement Learning has had a rich history. For reference we refer the reader to the manuscript of
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996
as a comprehensive source. There has been recent interest in the finite-time analysis of function approximation in reinforcement learning. For example
Bhandari et al. 2018
shows the convergence of policy evaluation with linear function approximators. Control problems, as in problems that require both policy evaluation and improvement are notoriously hard to evaluate under function approximators, and well-known algorithms like
Q
-learning and SARSA are known to not converge with function approximations
(
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996
)
. Theoretically, there have been efforts to mitigate this problem, for example in
Zou et al. 2019
analyses the sample complexity of SARSA with linear function approximators under a Lipschitz continuous policy improvement operator. On the other hand, there have been works on developing online algorithms founded on bandit literature that focus on the exploration-exploitation dynamics in an MDP. For example,
Van Roy and Wen 2014
introduced RLSVI, a Thompson Sampling-based algorithm that has gone on to receive some attention in the recent past
(
Osband et al. 2016
;
Zanette et al. 2020a
;
Agrawal et al. 2021
)
. However, these algorithms are based on a realizability and closedness assumption termed Linear MDPs. The framework of Linear MDPs has been popular in the recent literature on the online learning framework of Reinforcement Learning because of its amenability to theory. It has been shown that under the Linear MDP model algorithms enjoy sublinear regret,
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
and has been extended to general function classes under the realizability and closedness assumption of the Bellman Operator
(
Dann et al. 2022
)
. However, without the assumption of realizability and closedness, the theory fails, in the sense that one is not able to show the algorithm learns an optimal policy. For example,
Jin et al. 2020
shows that Least-Squares-Value-Iteration with UCB exploration bias suffers linear regret if the Linear MDP assumption is removed. Similarly
Zanette et al. 2020b
shows a linear regret under a misspecification notion termed as Inherent Bellman Error. In this regard we believe our work is a first of its kind to show that standard algorithms, like fitted
Q
-learning under a behavioral policy can learn the optimal policy, even if the model is grossly misspecified.
