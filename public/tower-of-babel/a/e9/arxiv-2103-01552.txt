On singular Yamabe obstructions
Andreas Juhl and Bent Ørsted

Abstract
. We discuss the singular Yamabe obstruction ℬ 3 {\mathcal{B}}_{3} of a hypersurface in a four-dimensional general background. We derive various explicit formula for ℬ 3 {\mathcal{B}}_{3} from the original definition. We relate these formulas to corresponding formulas in the literature. The proofs are elementary.

1.
Introduction

On a smooth manifold equipped with a Riemannian metric, the basic objects for
geometry are the canonical Levi-Civita connection and the corresponding Riemann
curvature tensor. Conformal geometry is partly studying transformations preserving
angles, and partly polynomials in the curvature and its derivatives transforming
in simple ways under conformal changes of the metric, i.e., multiplying it by a
positive smooth function. Already the decomposition of the curvature into the Weyl
tensor, the scalar curvature, and the trace-free Einstein tensor indicates the role
of conformal geometry, since the Weyl tensor transforms just by multiplication by a
power of the conformal factor. H. Weyl himself was introducing the first gauge
theory in physics exactly via the local change of scale given by a conformal factor,
and related it to the Maxwell equations in relativistic field theory.

Given a hypersurface in a Riemannian manifold one may in addition consider invariants
coming from the embedding, i.e., not only by the metric on the hypersurface
induced by the metric of the ambient space, but also from the normal geometry of the hypersurface.
In the classical Gauss theory of surfaces in a three-dimensional Euclidean space, the product
of the principal curvatures is the intrinsic Gauss/scalar curvature, whereas the arithmetic mean
of the principal curvatures is the extrinsic mean curvature. A celebrated conformal invariant is
the integral of the square of the mean curvature over the surface, the so-called Willmore energy,
also relevant for the physical theory of surfaces. It is closely related to the conformally invariant integral

\mathcal{W}_{2}=\int_{M^{2}}|\mathring{L}|^{2}dvol

of the conformally invariant squared norm of the trace-free second fundamental form. In general,
curvature invariants of a hypersurface consist of intrinsic invariants, coming from the induced metric,
and extrinsic invariants, coming from the second fundamental form and the ambient metric. Whereas
for a given manifold it is known how to describe the conformally invariant scalar curvature quantities
using the Fefferman-Graham ambient metric
[
FG12
]
, an analogous description of scalar
conformal invariants of a hypersurface is not known. Such a classification would also be of interest in physics

[
S15
]
.

Recent years have seen attempts to embed the theory of the Feffermann-Graham ambient metric
and of the related Poincaré-Einstein metric into a wider framework. For instance, Albin
[
A20
]

extended parts of the theory to Poincaré-Lovelock metrics including applications to
Q
-curvature.
In another direction, Gover et al.
[
G10
,
GW14
,
GW15
,
GGHW19
]
developed a tractor
calculus approach to the problem of constructing higher-order generalizations of the Willmore functional

\mathcal{W}_{2}
. Here a central role is played by the singular Yamabe problem which replaces the Einstein
condition. In
[
ACF92
]
, it was discovered that the obstruction to the
smooth
solvability of the
singular Yamabe problem of a hypersurface of dimension
n
is a scalar conformal invariant
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
.
The observation of Gover et al. that
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}=0
is the Euler-Lagrange equation of
\mathcal{W}_{2}

was the starting point of their theory. More generally,
[
G17
]
identified the
equation
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=0
as the Euler-Lagrange equations of a conformally invariant
functional which he termed the Yamabe energy. This energy is an analog of the integrated (critical)
renormalized volume coefficient of a Poincaré-Einstein metric which in turn is related to the
integrated (critical) Branson
Q
-curvature. Notably this connection to
Q
-curvature also extends to the
present setting
[
GW17
]
,
[
JO21
]
.

Formulas for the conformally invariant obstruction in terms of classical curvature data are not known for

n\geq 4
. But for
n=3
, such a formula for
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}
was derived in
[
GGHW19
]
from a general tractor calculus
formula in
[
GW15
]
.

In the present paper, we shall take a classical perspective and derive formulas for
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}
directly from
its very definition. This approach is independent. We only apply standard linear algebra and tensor calculations.
It confirms and partly corrects results in the literature. As technical tools we also employ some differential identities
involving
L
. Partly these are classical such as those found by J. Simons, and partly these are less well-known. Finally, we
apply classical style arguments to relate
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}
to the variation of the conformally invariant functional

\mathcal{W}_{3}=\int_{M}(\operatorname{tr}(\mathring{L}^{3})+(\mathring{L},\overline{\mathcal{W}}))dvol

which can be viewed as a natural generalizations of the classical Willmore functional. This fits with Graham’s
theorem
[
G17
, Theorem 3.1]
. Our arguments replace a technique introduced
and exploited in
[
GGHW19
]
for the same purpose.

The formulation of the main result requires some notation. Let
L
be the second fundamental form,
\mathring{L}
its
trace-free part and
H
the mean curvature. Let
\overline{W}
be the Weyl tensor of the background metric.
We also define two contractions
\overline{W}_{0}
and
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
of
\overline{W}
on
M

by inserting a unit normal vector
\partial_{0}
at the last and at the first and the last slot, respectively. We let
the operator
\mathcal{D}
act on trace-free symmetric bilinear forms
b
on
M
by

\mathcal{D}(b)=\delta\delta(b)+({\sf P},b)
.
\mathcal{D}
maps trace-free symmetric bilinear forms to
C^{\infty}(M)
.
It is conformally invariant in the sense that
e^{4\varphi}\hat{\mathcal{D}}(b)=\mathcal{D}(b)
for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
.
The Levi-Civita connections on
X
and
M
are denoted by
\bar{\nabla}
and
\nabla
. For more details see
Section
2
. For the definition of the obstructions
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
we refer to Section
4
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\iota:M^{3}\hookrightarrow(X^{4},g)
be a smooth embedding. Then it holds

\displaystyle 12{\mathcal{B}}_{3}

\displaystyle=6\mathcal{D}((\mathring{L}^{2})_{\circ})+2|\mathring{L}|^{4}+2\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{W}})

(1.1)

\displaystyle-2\mathring{L}^{ij}\bar{\nabla}^{0}(\overline{W})_{0ij0}-4\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}-4H(\mathring{L},\overline{\mathcal{W}})+16(\mathring{L}^{2},\overline{\mathcal{W}})+4|\overline{\mathcal{W}}|^{2}+2|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2}.

For a conformally flat background, Theorem
1
reduces to the identity

(1.2)

6{\mathcal{B}}_{3}=3\mathcal{D}((\mathring{L}^{2})_{\circ})+|\mathring{L}|^{4}=\Delta(|\mathring{L}|^{2})-|\nabla\mathring{L}|^{2}+3/2|\delta(\mathring{L})|^{2}-2{\sf J}|\mathring{L}|^{2}+|\mathring{L}|^{4}.

The second equality follows from Lemma
3.13
. We recall the well-known fact that for an odd-dimensional
M
,
there are Poincaré-Einstein metrics
g_{+}
such that the conformal compactification
g=r^{2}g_{+}
is smooth
[
FG12
]
.
For such a metric, it holds
\mathring{L}=0
,
\overline{\mathcal{W}}=0
and even
\overline{W}_{0}=0

[
G10
, Proposition 4.3]
. In
particular, for
n=3
, the above formula confirms that
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}=0
. If
\mathring{L}=0
, then

6{\mathcal{B}}_{3}=\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{W}})+2|\overline{\mathcal{W}}|^{2}+|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2}.

Formula (
1
) confirms the conformal invariance of
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}
(of weight
-4
). In fact, the conformal
invariance of
\mathcal{D}
implies that
\mathcal{D}((\mathring{L}^{2})_{\circ})
and
\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{W}})
are individually conformally invariant.
Furthermore, the sum of the first three terms in the second line of (
1
) is conformally invariant.
In fact, it holds

\mathring{L}^{ij}(\bar{\nabla}^{0}(\overline{W})_{0ij0}+2\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}+2H\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{ij})=(\mathring{L},B)+(\mathring{L}^{2},\overline{\mathcal{W}})+\mathring{L}^{ij}\mathring{L}^{kl}\overline{W}_{kijl}

(see Lemma
6.27
) with a conformally invariant tensor
B
of weight
-1
, i.e.,
e^{\varphi}\hat{B}=B
,
introduced in
[
GGHW19
, Lemma 2.1]
and termed the hypersurface Bach tensor. All remaining terms in
(
1
) are individually conformally invariant.

In the course of the proof of Theorem
1
we shall derive a number of equivalent formulas for
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}
which
are of interest in special cases. In particular, we find that

(1.3)

12{\mathcal{B}}_{3}=\Delta(|\mathring{L}|^{2})+6(\mathring{L},\operatorname{Hess}(H))+6H\operatorname{tr}(\mathring{L}^{3})+|\mathring{L}|^{4}+12|dH|^{2}

for a flat background (Corollary
6.11
). In
[
JO21
, Section 13.7]
, we derived this formula in a
different way as a consequence of a general expression for singular Yamabe obstructions
[
JO21
, Theorem 6]
.
One may also derive the general case of the above formula along this line. In
[
JO21
]
, we derived (
1.2
) by
combining the conformal invariance of
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}
with (
1.3
) and Simons identity.

The heat kernel asymptotics of elliptic boundary value problems for Laplace-type operators are
another rich source of polynomials in the covariant derivatives of the curvature tensor and of the second fundamental
form; in
[
BG90
]
explicit formulas are given for the first five (integrated) heat coefficients. It is a natural question to
determine the polynomials of this nature which exhibit a conformal invariance.

,

2.
Notation

All manifolds
X
are smooth. For a manifold
X
,
C^{\infty}(X)
and
\Omega^{p}(X)

denote the respective spaces of smooth functions and smooth
p
-forms. Let

\mathfrak{X}(X)
be the space smooth vector fields on
X
. Metrics on
X
usually
are denoted by
g
.
dvol_{g}
is the Riemannian volume element defined by
g
. The
Levi-Civita connection of
g
is denoted by
\nabla_{X}^{g}
or simply
\nabla_{X}
for
X\in\mathfrak{X}(X)
if
g
is understood. In these terms, the curvature tensor
R

of the Riemannian manifold
(X,g)
is defined by
R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}(Z)-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}(Z)-\nabla_{[X.Y]}(Z)
for vector fields
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{X}(X)
. The components of
R
are defined by

R(\partial_{i},\partial_{j})(\partial_{k})={R_{ijk}}^{l}\partial_{l}
. We also set

\nabla_{X}(u)=\langle du,X\rangle
for
X\in\mathfrak{X}(X)
and
u\in C^{\infty}(X)
.
\operatorname{Ric}
and
\operatorname{scal}
are the Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature of

g
. On a manifold
(X,g)
of dimension
n
, we set
2(n-1){\sf J}=\operatorname{scal}
and define
the Schouten tensor
{\sf P}
of
g
by
(n-2){\sf P}=\operatorname{Ric}-{\sf J}g
. Let
W
be the Weyl
tensor.

For a metric
g
on
X
and
u\in C^{\infty}(X)
, let
\operatorname{grad}_{g}(u)
be the gradient of

u
with respect to
g
, i.e., it holds
g(\operatorname{grad}_{g}(u),V)=\langle du,V\rangle
for
all vector fields
V\in\mathfrak{X}(X)
.
g
defines pointwise scalar products

(\cdot,\cdot)
and norms
|\cdot|
on
\mathfrak{X}(X)
, on forms
\Omega^{*}(X)
and
on general tensors. Then
|\operatorname{grad}(u)|^{2}=|du|^{2}
. In these definitions, we use the
metric as a subscript if needed for clarity.
\delta^{g}
is the divergence operator
on differential forms or symmetric bilinear forms. On forms it coincides with the
negative adjoint
-d^{*}
of the exterior differential
d
with respect to the Hodge
scalar product defined by
g
. Let
\Delta_{g}=\delta^{g}d
be the non-positive
Laplacian on
C^{\infty}(X)
. On the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, it equals

\sum_{i}\partial_{i}^{2}
. In addition,
\Delta
will also denote the Bochner-Laplacian
(when acting on
L
).

A metric
g
on a manifold
X
with boundary
M
induces a metric
h
on
M
. In
such a setting, the curvature quantities of
g
and
h
will be distinguished by
adding a bar to those of
g
. In particular, the covariant derivative, the curvature
tensor and the Weyl tensor of
(X,g)
are
\bar{\nabla}
,
\bar{R}
and

\overline{W}
. Similarly,
\overline{\operatorname{Ric}}
and
\overline{\operatorname{scal}}
are the Ricci
tensor and the scalar curvature of
g
.

A hypersurface usually is given by an embedding
\iota:M\hookrightarrow X
. Accordingly,
tensors on
X
are pulled back by
\iota^{*}
to
M
. In formulas, we often omit this pull back.
For a hypersurface
\iota:M\hookrightarrow X
with the induced metric
h=\iota^{*}(g)
on
M
,
the second fundamental form
L
is defined by
L(X,Y)=-h(\nabla^{g}_{X}(Y),N)

for vector fields
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
and a unit normal vector field
\partial_{0}=N
.
We set
nH=\operatorname{tr}_{h}(L)
if
M
has dimension
n
.
H
is the mean curvature of
M
.
Let
\mathring{L}=L-Hh
be the trace-free part of
L
. We often identify
L
with the shape operator
S

defined by
h(X,S(Y))=L(X,Y)
.

We use metrics as usual to raise and lower indices. In particular, we set
(L^{2})_{ij}=L_{i}^{k}L_{kj}=h^{lk}L_{il}L_{kj}
and similarly for higher powers of
L
. We always sum over repeated indices.

The
1
-form
\overline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{0}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
is defined by

\overline{\operatorname{Ric}}_{0}(X)=\overline{\operatorname{Ric}}(X,\partial_{0})
for
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. Similarly, we write
b_{0}
for the analogous
1
-form defined by a
bilinear form
b
and we let
\overline{W}_{0}
be the
3
-tensor on
M
with components
by
\overline{W}_{ijk0}
, i.e., we always insert
\partial_{0}
into the last slot. Moreover, we set

\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{ij}=\overline{W}_{0ij0}
.

3.
Some second-order identities involving the second fundamental form

In the present section, we derive formulas for
\delta\delta(\mathring{L}^{2})
and
\Delta(|\mathring{L}|^{2})
in terms of the geometry of the background and the intrinsic geometry of

M
. The second of these formulas is closely related to a well-known formula of
Simons. The main results are Lemma
3.13
and Lemma
3.14
. The
latter result will play an important role in Section
6
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
n=3
, it holds

(3.1)

\delta\delta(\mathring{L}^{2})=2\mathring{L}_{jk}\nabla^{j}\delta(\mathring{L})^{k}+|\nabla\mathring{L}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\delta(\mathring{L})|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2}+\kappa_{1}

with

(3.2)

\kappa_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}^{k}_{i}-\nabla^{j}\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}^{k}_{i})\mathring{L}_{kj}.

Proof.

First, we calculate

\displaystyle\delta\delta(\mathring{L}^{2})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}^{2}_{ij}=(\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k})\mathring{L}_{kj}+(\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k})(\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{kj})+(\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k})(\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{kj})+\mathring{L}_{i}^{k}(\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{kj})

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}^{k}_{i})\mathring{L}_{kj}+(\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k})(\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{kj})+\delta(\mathring{L})^{k}\delta(\mathring{L})_{k}+\mathring{L}_{i}^{k}\nabla^{i}\delta(\mathring{L})_{k}.

In the first term, we interchange covariant derivatives. This generates the curvature term

\kappa_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k})\mathring{L}_{kj}-(\nabla^{j}\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k})\mathring{L}_{kj}.

In the second term, we apply the Codazzi-Mainardi equation:

\nabla_{j}L_{ik}-\nabla_{i}L_{jk}=\bar{R}_{ijk0}.

Its trace-free part gives

(3.3)

\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}^{k}_{i}-\nabla_{i}\mathring{L}^{jk}=-\frac{1}{2}\delta(\mathring{L})^{j}h_{i}^{k}+\frac{1}{2}\delta(\mathring{L})_{i}h^{jk}+{{\overline{W}_{i}}^{jk}}_{0}.

In particular, we get

(\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k}-\nabla_{i}\mathring{L}^{jk})\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{jk}=-\frac{1}{2}(\delta(\mathring{L}),\delta(\mathring{L}))+{{\overline{W}_{i}}^{jk}}_{0}\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{jk}.

But

\overline{W}_{ijk0}\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}^{jk}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{W}_{ijk0}-\overline{W}_{jik0}\right)\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}^{jk}=\frac{1}{2}\overline{W}_{ijk0}\left(\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}^{jk}-\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}^{ki}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2},

where
|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\overline{W}_{ijk0}\overline{W}^{ijk0}
. Thus

\displaystyle(\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k})(\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{kj})

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}_{i}^{k}-\nabla_{i}\mathring{L}^{jk})\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{kj}+(\nabla_{i}\mathring{L}^{jk})(\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{kj})

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{i}\mathring{L}^{jk})(\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}_{kj})-\frac{1}{2}(\delta(\mathring{L}),\delta(\mathring{L}))-\frac{1}{2}|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\nabla\mathring{L},\nabla\mathring{L})-\frac{1}{2}(\delta(\mathring{L}),\delta(\mathring{L}))-\frac{1}{2}|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2}.

These observations show that

(3.4)

\delta\delta(\mathring{L}^{2})=2\mathring{L}_{jk}\nabla^{j}\delta(\mathring{L})^{k}+|\nabla\mathring{L}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\delta(\mathring{L})|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\overline{W}_{0}|^{2}+\kappa_{1}

with
\kappa_{1}
as being defined in (
3.2
).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

\kappa_{1}=3(\mathring{L}^{2},{\sf P})+{\sf J}|\mathring{L}|^{2}.

Proof.

By definition, we have

\kappa_{1}=(\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}\mathring{L}^{k}_{i}-\nabla^{j}\nabla^{i}\mathring{L}^{k}_{i})\mathring{L}_{kj}=\mathcal{R}^{ij}(\mathring{L})_{i}^{k}\mathring{L}_{kj},

where
\mathcal{R}
denotes the curvature operator of
M
. We also observe that

\mathcal{R}^{ij}(\mathring{L})_{i}^{k}\mathring{L}_{kj}=\mathcal{R}^{ij}(L)_{i}^{k}L_{kj}.

Hence by

\mathcal{R}_{ij}(L)_{kl}=-L_{l}^{m}R_{ijkm}-L^{m}_{k}R_{ijlm}

and the decomposition

(3.5)

R_{ijkl}=-{\sf P}_{ik}h_{jl}+{\sf P}_{jk}h_{il}-{\sf P}_{jl}h_{ik}+{\sf P}_{il}h_{jk}

(the Weyl tensor vanishes in dimension
3
) we get (see also Remark
3.11
)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=-(L^{m}_{i}{R^{ij}}_{km}+L^{km}{{R^{ij}}_{im}})L_{kj}

\displaystyle=L^{m}_{i}({\sf P}^{i}_{k}h^{j}_{m}-{\sf P}_{k}^{j}h_{m}^{i}+{\sf P}^{j}_{m}h_{k}^{i}-{\sf P}_{m}^{i}h_{k}^{j})L_{kj}+L^{km}\operatorname{Ric}^{j}_{m}L_{kj}

\displaystyle=2(L^{2},{\sf P})-6H(L,{\sf P})+(L^{2},\operatorname{Ric})

\displaystyle=3(L^{2},{\sf P})-6H(L,{\sf P})+{\sf J}|L^{2}|

\displaystyle=3(\mathring{L}^{2},{\sf P})+{\sf J}|\mathring{L}|^{2}.

Alternatively, combining the Simons’ identity (
3.9
) with the Gauss formula for the curvature
endomorphism yields the first identity in (
3.6
). This completes the proof.
∎

Example 3.3
.

For flat backgrounds, it holds

\kappa_{1}=3H\operatorname{tr}(L^{3})-|L|^{4}=3H\operatorname{tr}(\mathring{L}^{3})+3H^{2}|\mathring{L}|^{2}-|\mathring{L}|^{4}.

The Gauss identity gives

and it suffices to apply the identity

Now let

(3.8)

\kappa_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\mathring{L},\Delta(\mathring{L}))-\frac{3}{2}\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla_{j}\delta(\mathring{L})_{i}.

Lemma 3.4
.

\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}=\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}.

Proof.

The trace-free part of the Codazzi-Mainardi equation reads

(3.9)

\nabla_{i}\mathring{L}_{kj}-\nabla_{k}\mathring{L}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}\delta(\mathring{L})_{k}h_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}\delta(\mathring{L})_{i}h_{kj}=\overline{W}_{kij0}.

Hence

\nabla^{k}\nabla_{i}\mathring{L}_{kj}-\nabla^{k}\nabla_{k}\mathring{L}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{k}\delta(\mathring{L})_{k}h_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{k}\delta(\mathring{L})_{i}h_{kj}=\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}.

We commute the covariant derivatives in the first term and obtain

\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla_{i}\delta(\mathring{L})_{j}+\kappa_{1}-(\mathring{L},\Delta(\mathring{L}))+\frac{1}{2}\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla_{j}\delta(\mathring{L})_{i}=\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}.

Therefore, we get

\frac{3}{2}\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla_{i}\delta(\mathring{L})_{j}-(\mathring{L},\Delta(\mathring{L}))+\kappa_{1}=\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}.

In other words, we have

\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}=\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}.

The proof is complete.

Corollary 3.5
.

(3.10)

\kappa_{2}=3(\mathring{L}^{2},{\sf P})+{\sf J}|\mathring{L}|^{2}-\mathring{L}^{ij}\nabla^{k}\overline{W}_{kij0}.

4.
The singular Yamabe problem and the obstruction

The material in this section rests on
[
ACF92
]
and
[
GW15
]
.

Let
(X^{n+1},g)
be a compact manifold with boundary
M
of dimension
n
. The singular Yamabe problem asks
to find a defining function
\sigma
of
M
so that

(4.1)

\operatorname{scal}(\sigma^{-2}g)=-n(n+1).

The conformal transformation law of scalar curvature shows that

\operatorname{scal}(\sigma^{-2}g)=-n(n+1)|d\sigma|_{g}^{2}+2n\sigma\Delta_{g}(\sigma)+\sigma^{2}\operatorname{scal}(g).

Following
[
GW15
]
, we write this equation in the form

\operatorname{scal}(\sigma^{-2}g)=-n(n+1)\mathcal{S}(g,\sigma),

where

\mathcal{S}(g,\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}|d\sigma|_{g}^{2}+2\rho\sigma,\quad(n+1)\rho\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}-\Delta_{g}(\sigma)-\sigma{\sf J}\quad\mbox{and}\quad 2n{\sf J}=\operatorname{scal}(g).

In these terms,
\sigma
is a solution of (
4.1
) iff
\mathcal{S}(g,\sigma)=1
. Although such
\sigma
exist
and are unique, in general,
\sigma
is not smooth up to the boundary. The
smoothness is obstructed by a locally determined conformally invariant scalar
function on
M
which is called the singular Yamabe obstruction.

In order to describe the structure of a solution
\sigma
of the singular Yamabe problem more precisely, we
use geodesic normal coordinates. Let
r
be the distance function of
M
for the background metric
g
. Then
there are uniquely determined coefficients
\sigma_{(k)}\in C^{\infty}(M)
for
2\leq k\leq n+1
so that the
smooth defining function

(4.2)

\sigma_{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}r+\sigma_{(2)}r^{2}+\dots+\sigma_{(n+1)}r^{n+1}

satisfies

(4.3)

\mathcal{S}(g,\sigma_{F})=1+Rr^{n+1}

with a smooth remainder term
R
. The coefficients are recursively determined. In geodesic normal coordinates,
the metric
g
takes the form
dr^{2}+h_{r}
with a one-parameter family
h_{r}
of metrics on
M
. The condition
(
4.3
) is equivalent to

|d\sigma_{F}|_{g}^{2}-\frac{2}{n+1}\sigma_{F}\Delta_{g}(\sigma_{F})-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\sigma_{F}^{2}\operatorname{scal}(g)=1+Rr^{n+1}.

We write the left-hand side of this equation in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{r}(\sigma_{F})^{2}+h_{r}^{ij}\partial_{i}(\sigma_{F})\partial_{j}(\sigma_{F})

(4.4)

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{n+1}\sigma_{F}\left(\partial_{r}^{2}(\sigma_{F})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(h_{r}^{-1}h_{r}^{\prime})\partial_{r}(\sigma_{F})+\Delta_{h_{r}}(\sigma_{F})\right)-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\sigma_{F}^{2}\operatorname{scal}(g)

and expand this sum into a Taylor series in the variable
r
. Then the vanishing of
the coefficient of
r^{k}
for
k\leq n
is equivalent to an identity of the form

(k-1-n)\sigma_{(k+1)}=LOT,

where
LOT
involves only lower-order Taylor coefficients of
\sigma
. The latter relation
also indicates that there is a possible obstruction to the existence of an improved
solution
\sigma_{F}^{\prime}
which contains a term
\sigma_{(n+2)}r^{n+2}
and satisfies

\mathcal{S}(g,\sigma_{F}^{\prime})=1+Rr^{n+2}
. Following
[
ACF92
]
, we define the

singular Yamabe obstruction
by

(4.5)

{\mathcal{B}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(r^{-n-1}(\mathcal{S}(g,\sigma_{F})-1)\right)|_{r=0}.

Since
\sigma_{F}
is determined by
g
,
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
is a functional of
g
. It is a key result that

is a conformal invariant of

\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}_{n}

for the obstruction defined by

e^{(n+1)\iota^{*}(\varphi)}\hat{{\mathcal{B}}}_{n}={\mathcal{B}}_{n}
.

More precisely, we recursively find
