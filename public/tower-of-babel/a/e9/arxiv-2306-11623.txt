Mean-field Analysis of Generalization Errors
Gholamali Aminian, Samuel N. Cohen, Łukasz Szpruch

Abstract
We propose a novel framework for exploring weak and L 2 L_{2} generalization errors of algorithms through the lens of differential calculus on the space of probability measures. Specifically, we consider the KL-regularized empirical risk minimization problem and establish generic conditions under which the generalization error convergence rate, when training on a sample of size n n , is 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \mathcal{O}(1/n) . In the context of supervised learning with a one-hidden layer neural network in the mean-field regime, these conditions are reflected in suitable integrability and regularity assumptions on the loss and activation functions.

1
Introduction

A crucial problem in learning theory is understanding the performance of a learning algorithm on unseen data. This is described by the generalization error, which quantifies the deviation between the algorithm’s performance on the training data and its performance on unseen data sampled from the same underlying distribution. In the under-parameterized regime, where the number of model parameters is significantly less than the number of training data points, the theory of the generalization error is well established
[
Vapnik and Chervonenkis 2015
,
Bartlett and Mendelson 2002
]
. However, in the overparameterized regime, where the number of parameters may greatly exceed the number of training data points, this theory becomes inadequate. For example, deep neural network (NN) models can exhibit nearly zero training loss, which implies that they interpolate the training data, and yet still perform well on out-of-sample data
[
Belkin et al. 2019a
,
Spigler et al. 2019
,
Bartlett et al. 2021
]
. Mathematical models, such as the Neural Tangent Kernel
[
Jacot et al. 2018
]
, Mean-Field
[
Mei et al. 2018
]
, and Random Feature models
[
Rahimi and Recht 2008
]
, have been proposed to understand the behavior of overparameterized NNs. However, our current understanding of generalization in these contexts still needs to be completed.
Our approach is partially motivated by the recently developed mean-field perspective on training NNs. In a series of works
[
Mei et al. 2018
,
Chizat and Bach 2018
,
Mei et al. 2019
,
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos 2022
,
Hu et al. 2020
,
Tzen and Raginsky 2020
]
, the task of learning the optimal weights in deep NNs is viewed as a sampling problem. The picture that emerges from the mean-field regime is that the aim of a learning algorithm is to find an optimal distribution over the parameter space (rather than optimal values of the parameters). This work investigates how the mean-field perspective can shed light on the generalization performance of overparameterized NNs.

The contributions of this work are as follows:

•

We introduce and develop a general approach to study generalization error (cf. (
8
) for the definition) that utilizes calculus on the space of measures, encompassing a wide range of learning models and risk functions.

•

In the context of Kullback–Leibler (KL) regularized empirical risk minimization, our analysis provides if-and-only-if conditions for achieving a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}({1}/{n})
for weak generalization errors and
sharp conditions for achieving a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}({1}/{n})
for
L_{2}
. These bounds are not asymptotic, i.e. they are valid for all
n
.

•

For one-hidden layer neural networks in the mean-field regime, our analysis reveals precise conditions on model inputs that guarantee a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/n)

of both weak and
L_{2}
generalization errors. This improves the generalization error bounds which can be derived from the proof techniques in

Nishikawa et al. 2022
and
Nitanda et al. 2021
.

2.1
Problem formulation

Consider a supervised learning scenario, with input space
\mathcal{X}
and target space
\mathcal{Y}
. Define
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. We are often interested in learning a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
parameterized using a (large) number of parameters from
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, for some
k>0
.

Let
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
be the (unknown) distribution, describing the joint values of
(x,y)
in the population. Intuitively, we suppose that the mass of
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
is near the graph of
f
that is,
y\approx f(x)
when
z=(x,y)
is sampled from
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
. Since
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
is unknown, we approximate
f
based on a finite dataset,
\mathbf{Z}_{n}=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. We make the following basic assumption on our data, which will be taken to hold throughout the paper.

Assumption 1
.

Our observations are given by
\mathbf{Z}_{n}=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
are i.i.d. We write
\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}
for the corresponding empirical measure.

We are interested in quantifying the performance of our model on unseen data. To this end, let
\mathbf{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}}_{n}=\{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a second i.i.d sample set with law
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
, independent of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
. We define the perturbations of
\nu_{n}
obtained by ‘resampling’ one or two data points, by

\nu_{n,(1)}=\nu_{n}+\frac{1}{n}(\delta_{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}}-\delta_{Z_{1}}),\qquad\nu_{n,(1,2)}=\nu_{n}+\frac{1}{n}(\delta_{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}}-\delta_{Z_{1}}+\delta_{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{2}}-\delta_{Z_{2}})\,.

(1)

Learning algorithm:

We consider learning algorithms which can be represented as (measurable) maps from the empirical measure
\nu_{n}
to a measure over parameters,
\nu_{n}\mapsto\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n})\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
. In classical statistics, learning algorithms are (deterministic) maps from observed data to parameter estimates. By generalizing to measures over parameters, we allow for randomness in training algorithms (e.g. stochastic gradient descent), for Bayesian interpretations (where
\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n})
is the posterior distribution), and for a formalism for overparameterized models; furthermore, we gain access to the tools of calculus on probability spaces.

To investigate resampling the training data, we will make use of the convex perturbations, for
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
\mathcal{J}\in\{(1),(1,2)\}
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathfrak{m}_{\mathcal{J}}(\lambda)=\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n,\mathcal{J}})+\lambda(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n})-\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n,\mathcal{J}}))\\
\nu_{(1)}(\lambda)=\nu_{n,\mathcal{J}}+\lambda(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n,\mathcal{J}}),\\
\end{cases}

(2)

\phi(\theta_{i},x)=a_{i}\varphi(w_{i}\cdot x)

\ell(m,z):=\ell_{o}\big(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim m}[\phi(\theta,x)],y\big).

2.1.2
Risks

The risk function
3
3

3

The functional
\mathrm{R}
is (weakly) continuous in
\nu
, and is measurable in
m
(as a consequence of the measurability of
\ell
). As
\ell(m,z)
is nonnegative, the integral (
6
) can always be defined, without integrability assumptions on
\ell
, but may take the value
+\infty
.
takes distributions
m
of parameters and
\nu
of data points and evaluates the loss:

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(m,\nu):=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(m,z\big)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)\,.

(6)

The population risk is
\mathrm{R}(m,\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})
, and the empirical risk is
\mathrm{R}(m,\nu_{n})
, which simplifies as

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(m,\nu_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(m,z\big)\nu_{n}(\mathrm{d}z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\big(m,z_{i}\big).

(7)

Generalization error:

We aim to study the performance of the model trained with the empirical data measure
\nu_{n}
, and evaluated against the population measure
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
, that is,
\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})
.
Following
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
and
Xu and Raginsky 2017
, the risk can be decomposed:

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})=\underbrace{\Big(\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})-\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})\Big)}_{\text{generalization error}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})}_{\text{training error}}.

(8)

Due to the randomness of the training data
\mathbf{Z_{n}}
(and hence
\nu_{n}
), the generalization error is not deterministic, so we seek to estimate its statistics.

Assumption 2
.

The training map
\mathfrak{m}
, loss function
\ell
, and population measure
\nu
are such that the training error satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}[\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}[\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\ell(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),z)\nu_{n}(\mathrm{d}z)]<\infty
.

For a training map
\mathfrak{m}
, we will consider both the Weak Generalization Error (WGE),

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\big[\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})-\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})\big].

(9)

and the
L_{2}
-Generalization Error (LGE)

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}_{L_{2}}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\big[|\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})-\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})|^{2}\big].

(10)

Our main results will be conditions under which these errors are
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
. Markov’s inequality shows that, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(|\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})-\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})|>\epsilon\big)\leq\mathrm{gen}_{L_{2}}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\big/\epsilon^{2},

giving probabilistic bounds on the (random) generalization error.

2.2
Related works

Generalization error and overparameterization:

There are three main approaches to analyzing learning problems in an overparameterized regime: the neural tangent kernel (NTK), random feature and mean-field approaches. Under some assumptions, the NTK approach (a.k.a. lazy training) shows that an overparameterized one-hidden layer NN converges to an (infinite dimensional) linear model. The neural tangent random feature approach is similar to NTK, where the model is defined based on the network gradients at the initialization.
The random feature model is similar to NTK, with an extra assumption of constant weights in the single hidden layer of the NN. The mean-field approach uses the exchangeability of neurons to work with distributions of parameters. The study of these methods’ generalization performance allows us to extend our understanding of the overparameterized regime. See, for example, for NTK
[
Li and Liang 2018
,
Cao and Gu 2019
,
Arora et al. 2019b
,
Arora et al. 2019a
,
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
,
Ji and Telgarsky 2020
]
, neural tangent random feature
[
Cao and Gu 2019
,
Chen et al. 2019
]
, random feature
[
Mei and Montanari 2022
,
Ma et al. 2019
]
and mean-field
[
Nitanda et al. 2021
,
Nishikawa et al. 2022
]
settings. The neural tangent kernel and random feature results do not precisely reflect practice due to their constraints, such as the solution’s inability to deviate too far from the weights’ initialization
[
Fang et al. 2021
]
. A generalized NTK approach is considered in
[
Chen et al. 2020
]
, inspired by the mean-field approach, they derive a high-probability bound on the generalization error of one-hidden layer NNs with convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
. A high probability generalization error bound with rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
in one-hidden layer NN and with 0–1 and quadratic loss is studied in
[
Nitanda et al. 2021
,
Nishikawa et al. 2022
]
assuming a mean-field regime. See Appendix
A
for further discussion of related work.

3
Generalization errors

In this section, we apply functional calculus to study generalization error. Our goal is to give abstract conditions under which bounds can be established, which can be applied in a range of contexts, as will be explored in subsequent sections. All proofs are deferred to Appendix
C
.

3.1
WGE via functional derivatives

We begin this section by providing a general representation of generalization error, inspired by the approach of
[
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
, Lemma 7]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider a generic loss function
(m,z)\mapsto\ell(m,z)
, and
\nu_{n,(1)}
as defined in (
1
). The WGE (
9
) is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}}\Big[\ell\big(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}\big)-\ell\big(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n,(1)}),\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}\big)\Big]\,.

(11)

The right hand side of (
11
) measures the expected change in the loss function subject to resampling one training data point, which connects generalization error to stability of
\mathfrak{m}
with respect to the training data. We will next quantify this stability precisely, in terms of functional derivatives.

Assumption 3
.

In addition to Assumption
2
,

(i)

The loss function
\ell
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
(cf. Definition
2.1
).

(ii)

When restricted to
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
, the training map
\nu\mapsto\mathfrak{m}(\nu)
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, in the sense of Definition
2.2
. (In particular, this requires
\mathfrak{m}(\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Theta)
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
.)

Theorem 3.2
.

Given Assumption
3
, the weak generalization error has the representation

\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\Big(\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}\big(\mathfrak{m}_{(1)}(\lambda),\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1},\theta\big)\Big)\big(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n})-\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n,(1)})\big)(\mathrm{d}\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\Big].

Note that Theorem
3.2
holds for all differentiable loss functions, including non-convex cases. Assuming convexity of the loss function with respect to the parameter measure, we can also apply the functional derivative to provide a lower bound on the WGE.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given Assumption
3
, suppose the loss function is convex with respect to
m
, i.e. for all
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Theta)
, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\ell\big(\lambda m_{1}+(1-\lambda)m_{2},z\big)\leq\lambda\ell(m_{1},z)+(1-\lambda)\ell(m_{2},z)
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}}\Big[\int_{\Theta}\Big(\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n,(1)}),\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1},\theta)\Big)\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n})(\mathrm{d}\theta)\Big].

By applying the functional linear derivative to the term
\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n})-\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n,(1)})
in Theorem
3.2
, we can provide yet another representation of the WGE.

Theorem 3.4
.

The WGE can be written

\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}}\big[h(\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1})\big],

(12)

where

\begin{split}h(\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\Big(\int_{\Theta}\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}\big(\mathfrak{m}_{(1)}(\lambda),\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1},\theta\big)\frac{\delta\mathfrak{m}}{\delta\nu}\big(\nu_{(1)}(\tilde{\lambda}),z\big)(\mathrm{d}\theta)\Big)\,\big(\delta_{Z_{1}}-\delta_{\tilde{Z}_{1}}\big)(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.\end{split}

Remark 3.5
.

The representation in (
12
) reveals that the convergence rate of the WGE is at worst
\mathcal{O}({1}/{n})
provided that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}}[h(\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1})]\leq\mathcal{O}(1)
with respect to
n\to\infty
.

3.2
LGE via functional derivatives

We now consider the
L_{2}
-generalization error
\mathrm{gen}_{L_{2}}(\mathfrak{m},\nu)
. Our first result shows that, similarly to the WGE, the LGE can be controlled in terms of the functional derivatives of
\ell
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Given Assumption
3
, the
L_{2}
-generalization error satisfies

\mathrm{gen}_{L_{2}}(\mathfrak{m},\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})<\frac{4K}{n}+2K^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\mathbf{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}}_{2}}\big[h_{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{n},\mathbf{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}}_{2})^{2}\big]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\mathbf{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}}_{2}}\big[h_{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{n},\mathbf{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}}_{2})^{2}\big],

where

4
KL-regularized risk minimization

In the last section, we gave general conditions under which we can establish convergence rates for generalization error, in terms of the functional derivatives of the loss function and training map. Practically, these conditions are only useful if one can verify that they hold in specific examples. In this section, we will provide an intermediate step in this direction, where we assume the training map
\mathfrak{m}
is chosen to minimize a regularized version of the empirical risk. This will provide us with criteria which can be easily verified in practical examples; we will see this fully in Section
5
.

We will frequently use
\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}
to represent the density of a measure or measure-valued functional, that is,
\mathfrak{m}(\nu)(\mathrm{d}\theta)=\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}(\nu;\theta)d\theta
. For probability distributions
m
and
m^{\prime}
with positive densities
m_{\theta}(\theta)
and
m^{\prime}_{\theta}(\theta)
we define the Kullback–Leibler divergence
\mathrm{KL}(m^{\prime}\|m)=\int\log\big(\frac{m'_\theta(\theta)}{m_\theta(\theta)}\big)m_{\theta}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta
, and
\mathrm{KL}(m^{\prime}\|m)=\infty
otherwise
3
3

3

Extending to the case where
m
and
m^{\prime}
are equivalent but do not have (Lebesgue) densities will not be needed here.
.
We will also write the Kullback–Leibler divergence in terms of the densities, with the abuse of notation
\mathrm{KL}(m^{\prime}\|m)=\mathrm{KL}(m_{\theta}^{\prime}\|m_{\theta})=\mathrm{KL}(m^{\prime}\|m_{\theta})
as convenient.

We will consider training maps which minimize the regularized objective
\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}
given by the sum of a risk function and KL-divergence, that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}(m,\nu)=\mathrm{R}(m,\nu)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(m\|\gamma^{\sigma}),

(13)

where
R(m,\nu)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\nu}[\ell(m,z)]
and
\gamma^{\sigma}(\theta)=\frac{1}{F^{\sigma}}\exp\{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}U(\theta)\big\}

is a Gibbs measure which serves as prior to the parameter measure; here
F^{\sigma}
is simply a normalizing constant to ensure
\int\gamma^{\sigma}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta=1
, and we call
U:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
the ‘regularizing potential’.

For notational convenience, we similarly define
\tilde{\gamma}^{\sigma}_{p}=\frac{1}{\tilde{F}^{\sigma}}\exp\{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}U(\theta)+\|\theta\|^{p}\big\}
, this density will play a particularly useful role in obtaining our bounds. We know that
F^{\sigma},\tilde{F}^{\sigma}_{p}<\infty
under the following assumption, which we will use for our results in this section.

Assumption 4
.

For a fixed
p\geq 2
, there exists
g:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Theta)\to(0,\infty)
and
g_{1}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Theta)\times\Theta\to(0,\infty)
such that,

(i)

The loss function is
\ell
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
(Definition
2.1
), nonnegative, and convex with respect to
m
;

(ii)

For all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and
m\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
, the loss satisfies

\big|\ell(m,z)\big|\leq g(m)\big(1+\|z\|^{2}\big)

and the derivative of the loss satisfies

\Big|\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}(m,z,\theta)\Big|\leq g_{1}(m,\theta)\big(1+\|z\|^{2}\big);

(iii)

For all
m\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
, the regularizing potential
U
satisfies

\lim_{\|\theta\|\to\infty}\frac{U(\theta)}{\|\theta\|^{p}+g_{1}(m,\theta)}=\infty
;

(iv)

There exists
C_{\theta}>0
such that, for all
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
,

\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim m^{\prime}}\Big[\big(g_{1}(m,\theta)\big)^{2}\Big]^{1/2}\leq C_{\theta}\Big(1+\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim m^{\prime}}\big[\|\theta\|^{p}\big]+\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim m}\big[\|\theta\|^{p}\big]\Big),

(14)

(v)

We have the pointwise integrability conditions
g(\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma})<\infty
and, for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
and
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
;

\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\sim m}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\nu}\Big[\Big(\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{\ell}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big)^{2}\Big]^{1/2}<\infty.

(15)

We begin by identifying the minimizer of
\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}
in the following lemma; the proof, together with others in this section, is deferred to Appendix
D
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Under Assumption
4
, the unique minimizer of
m\mapsto\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}(m,\nu)
, denoted
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
exists, and will be called the
Gibbs measure
associated with
\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}
. It is the fixed point of
m\mapsto M(m,\nu)
, where
M:\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})\to\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
is defined in terms of the density of its output

\displaystyle M_{\theta}(m,\nu;\theta)=\frac{1}{F_{\beta,\sigma}}\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big[\frac{\delta\mathrm{R}}{\delta m}(m,\nu,\theta)+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}U(\theta)\Big]\Bigg\},

(16)

for
F_{\beta,\sigma}
is a normalization constant. Furthermore, the map
\nu\mapsto\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
.

We observe that, unless
\frac{\delta\mathrm{R}}{\delta m}
does not depend on
m
(i.e. unless
\mathrm{R}
is linear in
m
), (
16
) does not provide an explicit representation of
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n})
, but instead describes it implicitly in terms of a fixed point.

Applying Theorems
3.4
and
3.7
, we can drive upper bounds on the generalization errors.

Theorem 4.2

(WGE of the Gibbs measure)
.

Given Assumption
4
and
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}}\big[\|Z\|^{8}\big]<\infty
, the weak generalization error satisfies

|\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})|\leq\frac{c}{n}\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[\Big(1+\|Z_{1}\|^{2}\Big)^{4}\Big]

where
c=\frac{9\sqrt{2}}{2}C_{\theta}^{2}\Big[1+2\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+4\frac{g(\tilde{\gamma}^{\sigma}_{p})}{n}\Big]^{2}>0
.

Theorem 4.3

(LGE of the Gibbs measure)
.

Given Assumption
4
and
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}}\big[\|Z\|^{16}\big]<\infty
, the
L_{2}
generalization error satisfies

\begin{split}&\mathrm{gen}_{L_{2}}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\leq\frac{1}{n}\Big(4K+2K^{1/2}A^{1/2}+\frac{A}{n}\Big),\end{split}

(17)

where

\begin{split}&A=(2\times 5^{4})\Big(\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{2}C_{\theta}^{4}\Big(1+2\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma}(\mathrm{d}\theta)+8g(\tilde{\gamma}^{\sigma}_{p})\Big)^{4}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[(1+\|Z_{1}\|^{2})^{8}]<\infty,\end{split}

and
K=\max_{Z\in\{Z_{1},\tilde{Z}_{1}\}}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\ell(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),Z)\big)^{2}\big]
.

Expected population risk upper bound:
Using Theorem
4.2
, we can also provide an upper bound on the expected population risk. Suppose that there exists
\bar{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Theta)
such that
\mathrm{R}(\bar{m},\nu_{n})=0
, cf.
[
Nishikawa et al. 2022
, Appendix A]
. From the optimization defining
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}
, we know that

\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})\leq\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})\leq\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}(\bar{m},\nu_{n})=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(\bar{m}\|\gamma^{\sigma})\,.

(18)

Hence

\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\leq\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})-\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(\bar{m}\|\gamma^{\sigma})\,.

(19)

4.2
implies that

\begin{split}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\big[\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\big]\Big|&\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\Big[\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})-\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{n})\Big]\Big|+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(\bar{m}\|\gamma^{\sigma})\\
&\leq\frac{c}{n}\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[\big(1+\|Z_{1}\|^{2}\big)^{4}\Big]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(\bar{m}\|\gamma^{\sigma})\,.\end{split}

Consequently, setting
\beta
to be of order

and using Theorem

\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\big[\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\big]\big|
, is of order

. This agrees in principle with the order of error computed in
[
Nitanda et al. 2021
]
for specific cases.

L_{2}
population risk upper bound:
Similarly, squaring and taking the expected value of (

5
Application: Overparameterized one-hidden layer NN

We now highlight the application of our model to an overparameterized one-hidden layer neural network in the mean-field regime
[
Hu et al. 2020
,
Mei et al. 2018
]
.
The loss function for an overparameterized one-hidden layer NN is represented in (
4
). For simplicity, we also write
(\hat{y},y)\mapsto\ell_{o}(\hat{y},y)
for the loss function, where
\hat{y}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim m}[\phi(\theta,x)]
is the predicted output of the overparameterized one-hidden layer NN.
We assume that the risk function
\mathrm{R}
is given by
\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}}[\ell_{o}(\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n})}[\phi(\theta,X)],Y)]
, as is done in mean-field models of one-hidden layer neural networks
[
Hu et al. 2020
,
Mei et al. 2019
,
Tzen and Raginsky 2020
]
, and is KL-regularized as seen previously. We make the following assumptions on an overparameterized one-hidden layer NN to investigate generalization performance:

Assumption 5
.

For a constant
p\geq 2
, the regularizing potential
U
satisfies
\lim_{\|\theta\|\to\infty}\frac{U(\theta)}{\|\theta\|^{p}}=\infty,
the loss
\ell_{o}
is convex and nonnegative, and there exist finite constants
L_{\ell},L_{\ell,1},L_{\ell,2}
, and
L_{\phi}
such that, for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
\theta\in\Theta
,

\begin{split}&\big|\ell_{o}(\hat{y},y)\big|\leq L_{\ell}(1+\|\hat{y}\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}),\qquad|\partial_{\hat{y}}\ell_{o}(\hat{y},y)\big|\leq L_{\ell,1}(1+\|\hat{y}\|+\|y\|),\qquad\big|\partial_{\hat{y}\hat{y}}\ell_{o}(\hat{y},y)\big|\leq L_{\ell,2},\\
\end{split}

and

\big|\phi(\theta,x)\big|\leq L_{\phi}(1+\|x\|)(1+\|\theta\|^{2}).

Remark 5.1

(Activation function examples)
.

Recall that
\phi(\theta,x)=a\varphi(w\cdot x)
, where
a
is the parameter of the outer layer and
\varphi(\cdot)
is the activation function. If the activation function is of linear growth, then Assumption
5
is satisfied. Note that the ReLU, Heaviside unit-step, tanh and sigmoid activation functions are of this type, and no smoothness assumption is needed.

Remark 5.2

(Loss function examples)
.

Twice differentiable loss functions which are of quadratic growth with derivatives of linear growth satisfy Assumption
5
. For example, the quadratic, product-type (as in
[
Bartlett et al. 2006
]
), and logcosh loss functions
[
Wang et al. 2022
]
are of this type.

We begin by verifying Assumption
4
, for one-hidden layer neural network model under Assumption
5
, in the following lemma; the proof, together with others in this section, is deferred to Appendix
E
. While the constants are not given precisely here, they can be explicitly determined with only moderate effort, if so desired.

Lemma 5.3
.

Under Assumption
5
, there exists a constant
c>0
independent of
n
such that, for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,
\theta\in\Theta
,
m\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
, and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
,

\displaystyle\big|\ell(m,z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq c\Big[\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim m}[1+\|\theta\|^{2}]^{2}\Big]\big(1+\|z\|^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}(m,z,\theta)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq c\Big[1+\|\theta\|^{4}+\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim m}[\|\theta^{\prime}\|^{4}]\Big]\big(1+\|z\|^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\sim m}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\nu}\Big[\Big(\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{\ell}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big)^{2}\Big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle K=\max_{Z\in\{Z_{1},\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}\}}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\ell(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),Z)\big)^{2}\big]

6
Conclusions and future works

Our study introduces a novel framework for analyzing the generalization error of risk functions. The framework utilizes calculus on the space of probability measures, which allows us to gain a deeper understanding of the factors that influence the generalization of machine learning models. We demonstrate the efficacy of our framework by applying it to learning problems within the mean-field regime. This application results in the derivation of new bounds for overparameterized models that are particularly relevant to one-hidden layer neural networks.

In future work, it is promising to extend our methods to establish upper bounds for weak and
L_{2}
generalization errors of deep neural networks, via the mean-field analysis outlined in
[
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos 2022
]
,
Chizat et al. 2022
,
Jabir et al. 2019
and
Geshkovski et al. 2023
. Additionally, we intend to apply our framework to a broader range of examples, beyond mean-field models, while relaxing the regularization requirements. Our goal is to develop comparable generalization and stability bounds for specific algorithms.

Appendix A
Other related works

Expected generalization error:

The pioneering work in establishing upper bounds on the expected generalization error through an information-theoretic approach was done by
Russo and Zou 2019
and
Xu and Raginsky 2017
. They proposed using the mutual information between the input training set and the output parameters. Building upon their work,
Bu et al. 2020
introduced tighter upper bounds that consider the mutual information of individual samples.
Asadi et al. 2018
suggested the use of chaining mutual information, while
Hafez-Kolahi et al. 2020
and
Steinke and Zakynthinou 2020
advocated conditioning and processing techniques. In addition to mutual information, other researchers have explored alternative information measures for establishing information-theoretic bounds on generalization error. These measures include
f
-divergences,
\alpha
-Rényi divergence, and generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence, as demonstrated in the works of
Esposito et al. 2021
,
Aminian et al. 2022b
, and
Aminian et al. 2021b
. Wasserstein distance-based upper bounds, which consider the discrepancy between the training sample data and the output of a randomized learning algorithm, were proposed by
Lopez and Jog 2018
,
Wang et al. 2019
,
Gálvez et al. 2021
, and
Aminian et al. 2022a
. Furthermore, some studies have explored the application of rate-distortion theory to analyze generalization error, as seen in the works of
Masiha et al. 2023
and
Sefidgaran et al. 2022
. In our approach, we employ calculus on probability spaces, allowing us to apply it to both the overparameterized regime and the expected parametric loss scenarios, to derive an upper bound on the weak generalization error and
L_{2}
generalization error.

Generalization error and the parametric Gibbs measure:

The mean-field perspective on training gives close connections with ideas from statistical mechanics. The parametric Gibbs measure (a.k.a. Gibbs Algorithm or Gibbs Posterior) first introduced by
[
Gibbs 1902
]
in statistical mechanics and further studied by
[
Jaynes 1957
]
describes the stable behaviour of an ensemble of particles evolving subject to a potential, as is given by our (parametric) loss function. The weak generalization error of the parametric Gibbs measure with expected parametric loss has been studied from information-theoretic and PAC-Bayesian perspectives. An information-theoretic upper bound on weak (expected) generalization error, with a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
is provided in
[
Raginsky et al. 2016
]
and
[
Aminian et al. 2021a
,
Kuzborskij et al. 2019
]
for the parametric Gibbs measure, assuming a bounded or sub-Gaussian parametric loss function, respectively. PAC-Bayesian upper bounds for parametric Gibbs measures are also derived in
[
Alquier et al. 2016
,
Aminian et al. 2021a
]
. The sensitivity of the parametric Gibbs measure is studied in
[
Perlaza et al. 2022
]
. Our work differs from this body of research by reparameterization of the loss function in terms of a parameter measure, which results in a general Gibbs measure and can be applied to overparameterized one-hidden layer NN under the mean-field regime. We also provide an upper bounds on the expected generalization error of the parametric Gibbs measure with weaker assumptions.

Other approaches to generalization error:

There are other approaches applied to study generalization error of general learning problems, including VC dimension-based bounds, Rademacher complexity, PAC-Bayesian bounds, and algorithmic stability-based bounds. A group of studies
[
Neyshabur et al. 2015
,
Neyshabur et al. 2018
,
Golowich et al. 2018
,
Arora et al. 2018
]
has investigated the generalization error of neural networks via a uniform convergence approach. However, these results are algorithm independent. Rademacher complexity of functions classes like neural networks have been studied by
[
Bartlett et al. 2017
,
Yin et al. 2019
]
to provide upper bound on generalization error. These results based on VC dimension and Rademacher complexity are often vacuous in NN scenarios
[
Dziugaite and Roy 2017
]
. A data dependent Rademacher complexity bound is studied for overparameterized NNs by
Bubeck and Sellke 2023
which has a convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
. Another approach is based on PAC-Bayes learning
[
McAllester 2003
,
Catoni 2007
]
. In this approach high probability bounds are derived on generalization error in terms of different information measures
[
Rivasplata et al. 2020
,
Amit et al. 2022
,
Tolstikhin and Seldin 2013
]
. Some works also studied the stability-based bounds for generalization error
[
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
,
Hardt et al. 2016
,
Mou et al. 2017
,
Chen et al. 2018
]
. These approaches are not applied to derive the generalization error upper bound in overparameterized neural networks by considering the regularization.

Benign overfitting:

A body of literature has emerged to investigate the occurrence of benign overfitting in overparameterized models, wherein accurate predictions are made on unseen test data despite overfitting to the training data
[
Bartlett et al. 2020
]
. Although this phenomenon has been explored in various studies, most of them have been confined to linear models
[
Belkin et al. 2019b
,
Bartlett et al. 2020
,
Hastie et al. 2019
,

Mean-field:

Our study employs the mean-field framework utilized in a recent line of research

Chizat and Bach 2018
establishes the convergence of gradient descent for training one-hidden layer NNs with infinite width under certain structural assumptions. The study of
Mei et al. 2018
proves the global convergence of noisy stochastic gradient descent and establishes approximation bounds between finite and infinite neural networks. Furthermore,

\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}
can be outside the set
\{m:\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(m\|\gamma^{\sigma})\leq\mathrm{R}(\gamma^{\sigma},\nu)\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
, and this set is relatively compact (in the weak topology). By convexity, (as the Kullback–Leibler divergence is strictly convex, and
\mathrm{R}
is convex), we conclude that
\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}(\cdot,\nu)
has a unique minimizer, which will have a density (due to the Kullback–Leibler divergence). Taking the first order condition for optimality derived in
[
Hu et al. 2020
, Proposition 2.5]
, the minimizer of
\mathcal{V}^{\beta,\sigma}(\cdot,\nu_{n})
is uniquely characterized, for some normalizing constant
F
and almost all
\theta
, by

\begin{split}0=\frac{\delta R}{\delta m}(m,\nu,\theta)+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}U(\theta)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\log(m_\theta(\theta))+F\end{split}

which rearranges to give
m_{\theta}(\theta)=M_{\theta}(m,\nu;\theta)
, as desired. We defer the proof of differentiability to Lemma
D.4

∎

Lemma D.1
.

Defining the set valued map

\begin{split}B(\nu)&:=\Big\{m\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta):\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(m\|\gamma^{\sigma})\leq\mathrm{R}(\gamma^{\sigma},\nu)\\
&\qquad\text{ and }\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}m(\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq\mathrm{R}(\tilde{\gamma}^{\sigma},\nu)+\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}\tilde{\gamma}^{\sigma}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta\Big\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Theta),\end{split}

for any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
, the KL-regularized risk minimizer satisfies
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)\in B(\nu)
.

Proof.

We have already seen in Lemma
4.1
that
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)\in\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(m\|\gamma^{\sigma})\leq\mathrm{R}(\gamma^{\sigma},\nu)\}
.
It remains to show that
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
satisifes the stated
p
-moment bound.

Recall that we defined the measure
\tilde{\gamma}^{\sigma}_{p}
through its density
(\tilde{F}^{\alpha,\beta})^{-1}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\beta^2}U(\theta) + \|\theta\|^p)
, for
\tilde{F}^{\alpha,\beta}
a normalization constant. Consider the problem of minimizing
\tilde{\mathrm{R}}(m,\nu)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(m\|\tilde{\gamma}^{\sigma})
, where
\tilde{\mathrm{R}}(m,\nu)=\mathrm{R}(m,\nu)+\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}m(\mathrm{d}\theta)
. As this falls in the class considered in Lemma
4.1
, we know that its optimizer exists and its density is the fixed point of

\begin{split}m_{\theta}&\mapsto\frac{1}{\tilde{F}_{\beta,\sigma}}\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big[\frac{\delta\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}{\delta m}(m,\nu,\theta)+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}U(\theta)-\|\theta\|^{p}\Big]\Bigg\}\\
&=\frac{1}{\tilde{F}_{\beta,\sigma}}\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big[\frac{\delta\mathrm{R}}{\delta m}(m,\nu,\theta)+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}U(\theta)\Big]\Bigg\}.\end{split}

In other words,
m=\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
as defined in Lemma
4.1
. It follows that

\mathrm{R}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),\nu)+\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu;\mathrm{d}\theta)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)\|\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma})\leq\mathrm{R}(\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma},\nu)+\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma}(\mathrm{d}\theta).

As
\ell
, and hence
\mathrm{R}
, is nonnegative, we conclude that

\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu;\mathrm{d}\theta)\leq\mathrm{R}(\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma},\nu)+\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{p}\tilde{\gamma}_{p}^{\sigma}(\mathrm{d}\theta).

∎

Lemma D.2
.

For any choice of
m\in B(\nu)
, the linear map
\mathcal{C}_{m}:L^{2}(m,\Theta)\to L^{2}(m,\Theta)
defined by

\mathcal{C}_{m}f(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim m}\Big[\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{R}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,\nu,\theta,\theta^{\prime})f(\theta^{\prime})\Big]

is positive, in the sense that

\langle f,\mathcal{C}_{m}f\rangle_{L^{2}(m,\Theta)}=\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)(\mathcal{C}_{m}f)(\theta)m(\mathrm{d}\theta)\geq 0.

In particular,
\mathcal{C}_{m}
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator with discrete spectrum

\sigma(\mathcal{C}_{m})=\{\lambda^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}\subset[0,\infty).

Proof.

We first write

\begin{split}\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)(\mathcal{C}_{m}f)(\theta)m(\mathrm{d}\theta)&=\int_{\Theta}\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)f(\theta^{\prime})\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{R}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,\nu,\theta,\theta^{\prime})m(\mathrm{d}\theta)m(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\Theta}\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)f(\theta^{\prime})\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{\ell}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})m(\mathrm{d}\theta)m(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}z).\end{split}

As
\ell
is convex with respect to
m
, we know that
\frac{\delta^{2}\ell}{\delta m^{2}}
is positive semidefinite, which gives positivity of
\mathcal{C}_{m}
. We also have the symmetry property,
[
Cardaliaguet et al. 2019
, Lemma 2.4]

\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{\ell}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{\ell}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,z,\theta^{\prime},\theta).

We next recall that, by Assumption
4
(
v
),

\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\Theta}\int_{\Theta}\Big(\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{\ell}}{\delta m^{2}}(m,z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big)^{2}m(\mathrm{d}\theta)m(\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty,

which implies
\mathcal{C}_{m}
is Hilbert–Schmidt, in particular it is a self-adjoint compact operator. The spectral theorem then states that
\mathcal{C}_{m}
has a discrete spectrum made up of its eigenvalues, which are nonnegative as
\mathcal{C}_{m}
is positive.
∎

Our next challenge is to prove that the Gibbs measure is differentiable. We begin by considering an auxiliary function

Lemma D.3
.

Define

S(\nu,\theta):=\frac{\delta\mathrm{R}}{\delta m}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),\nu,\theta)=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),z,\theta)\nu(\mathrm{d}z).

(36)

Under Assumption
4
, we know

is differentiable in
\nu
, and its derivative satisfies the bound

\begin{split}&\int_{\Theta}\Big(\frac{\delta S}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)\Big)^{2}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu;\mathrm{d}\theta)\\
&\leq\int_{\Theta}\Big(\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),\theta,z)-\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),\theta,z^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\Big)^{2}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu;\mathrm{d}\theta).\end{split}

In particular, we have the representation:

\begin{split}\frac{\delta S}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)&=\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}\big(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),z,\theta\big)-\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}\big(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),z^{\prime},\theta\big)\nu(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\\
&\quad-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)}\Big[\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\delta^{2}\ell}{\delta m^{2}}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),z^{\prime},\theta,\theta^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}),\,\frac{\delta S}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta^{\prime},z)\Big].\end{split}

Proof.

Fix
\nu,\nu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
and write
\nu^{\epsilon}=\nu+\epsilon(\nu_{1}-\nu)
.
Recall that

S(\nu,\theta):=\frac{\delta\mathrm{R}}{\delta m}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),\nu,\theta)=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\delta\ell}{\delta m}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),z,\theta)\nu(\mathrm{d}z)

(37)

and
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
satisfies

\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}(\nu;\theta)=\frac{Q(\nu,\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}Q(\nu,\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}},

(38)

where
Q(\nu,\theta)=\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big(S(\nu,\theta)+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}U(\theta)\Big)\Big\}.

We define an approximate directional derivative of

:

\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta):=\frac{S(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)-S(\nu,\theta)}{\epsilon},

and similarly

\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon}):=\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu^{\epsilon})-\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)}{\epsilon},\qquad\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}Q}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}:=\frac{Q(\nu^{\epsilon};\theta)-Q(\nu;\theta)}{\epsilon}.

We note that
S(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)\to S(\nu,\theta)
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Using the fact that
|S(\nu^{\prime},\theta)|<C(1+\|\theta\|^{2})
for all
\nu^{\prime}
in a compact set around

, the dominated convergence theorem shows that, as
\epsilon\to 0
,

\epsilon\Big\|\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\mathfrak{m}^{\sigma,\beta}(\nu))}=\|S(\nu^{\epsilon},\cdot)-S(\nu,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathfrak{m}^{\sigma,\beta}(\nu))}\to 0.

(39)

We now give a representation of
\delta^{\epsilon}Q/\delta\nu^{\epsilon}
. Expanding the definition of the approximate directional derivative,

\begin{split}&\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}Q}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta,z)\\
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big(S(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}U(\theta)\Big)\Big\}-\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big(S(\nu,\theta)+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}U(\theta)\Big)\Big\}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}Q(\nu,\theta)\left(\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big(S(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)-S(\nu,\theta))\Big)\Big\}-1\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}Q(\nu,\theta)\left(\exp\{-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,\epsilon\,\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)\Big\}-1\right)\\
&=-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}Q(\nu,\theta)\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)\int_{0}^{1}\exp\{\frac{-2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(\epsilon\lambda\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta,z))\Big\}\mathrm{d}\lambda\\
&=-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}Q(\nu,\theta)\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)G(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta),\end{split}

(40)

where

\begin{split}G(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)&:=\int_{0}^{1}\exp\{\frac{-2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(\epsilon\lambda\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta))\Big\}\mathrm{d}\lambda\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\exp\{\frac{-2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda(S(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)-S(\nu,\theta))\Big\}\mathrm{d}\lambda.\end{split}

Observe that (

\|G(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)-1\|_{L^{2}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu))}\to 0

.

Next, we reexpress

\begin{split}&\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta,z)\\

(41)

\begin{split}&\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)\\

\mathcal{C}^{\epsilon}v&:=\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{R}}{\delta m^{2}}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\lambda}(\nu^{\epsilon}),\nu,\theta,\theta^{\prime})G(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\lambda,\,v(\theta^{\prime})\Big].\end{split}

We formally rearrange to get

\begin{split}\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}&=\bigg(\mathrm{id}+\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{C}^{\epsilon}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{L}^{\epsilon}(z)(\nu_{1}-\nu)(\mathrm{d}z)-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{H(\nu^{\epsilon},\nu)}-1\Big)\mathcal{C}^{\epsilon}\Big[\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\cdot)\Big]\bigg).\end{split}

(48)

Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we know that
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu^{\epsilon})\to\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
and
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\lambda}(\nu^{\epsilon})\to\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{L}^{\epsilon}(z)\to\mathcal{L}(z)
and
\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{R}}{\delta m^{2}}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\lambda}(\nu^{\epsilon}),\nu,\theta,\theta^{\prime})\to\frac{\delta^{2}\mathrm{R}}{\delta m^{2}}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu),\nu,\theta,\theta^{\prime})
. We have already seen
H(\nu^{\epsilon},\nu)\to 1
and
G(\nu^{\epsilon},\theta)\to 1
, and therefore,
\mathcal{C}^{\epsilon}\to\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)}
in the sense of Hilbert–Schmidt operators. In particular, as
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)}
has a positive spectrum, this implies
(\mathrm{id}+\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{C}^{\epsilon})^{-1}\to(\mathrm{id}+\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)})^{-1}
.

Therefore, we determine that, as
\epsilon\to 0
,

\frac{\delta^{\epsilon}S}{\delta\nu^{\epsilon}}(\nu^{\epsilon},\cdot)\to\bigg(\mathrm{id}+\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{L}(z)(\nu_{1}-\nu)(\mathrm{d}z)\bigg)=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}v(z)(\nu_{1}-\nu)(\mathrm{d}z),

where
v
is the fixed point constructed in (
45
).

From this, we conclude that
\int_{\mathcal{Z}}v(\nu,\theta,z)(\nu_{1}-\nu)(\mathrm{d}z)
is the directional derivative of
S
in the direction
(\nu_{1}-\nu)
. As the directional derivative is given by integrating
v
, it follows that

\frac{\delta S}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)=v(\nu,\theta,z),

and hence
S
is differentiable in
\nu
, and has the desired representation. The bound in (
47
) completes the argument.
∎

Lemma D.4

(Derivative of Gibbs Measure)
.

Under Assumption
4
, the density of the Gibbs measure, that is, the map
\nu\mapsto\mathfrak{m}_{\theta}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
, has derivative

\frac{\delta\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)=-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}(\nu;\theta)\Big(\frac{\delta S}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)-\Big[\int_{\Theta}\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}(\nu;\theta^{\prime})\frac{\delta S}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta^{\prime},z)\mathrm{d}\theta\Big]\Big).

In particular, for any

\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)\frac{\delta\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)\mathrm{d}\theta=-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta\sim\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)}\Big[f(\theta),\,\frac{\delta S}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)\Big].

As a consequence, Assumption
4
implies Assumption
3
.

Proof.

From Lemma
D.3
, we know
S
is differentiable.
Recall that
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu)
satisfies

\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}(\nu;\theta)=\frac{Q(\nu,\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}Q(\nu,\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}},

where

. Differentiating with respect to

\begin{split}&\frac{\delta\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}_{\theta}}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)\\
&=\frac{\frac{\delta Q}{\delta\nu}(\nu,\theta,z)\int_{\Theta}Q(\nu,\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}-Q(\nu,\theta)\frac{\delta}{\delta\nu}\int_{\Theta}Q(\nu,\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}}{\big(\int_{\Theta}Q(\nu,\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\big)^{2}}\\

(

3
(
i
) and Assumption
3
(

Remark D.6
.

In the following arguments, we will regularly make use of the basic inequality (based on Cauchy–Schwarz), for any square integrable random variables
X,Y
,

|\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{V}(X)\mathbb{V}(Y)}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(X^{2})\mathbb{E}(Y^{2})}.

A slightly more involved, but fundamentally similar, inequality is given in the following lemma.

Lemma D.7
.

Let
\pi:(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial with positive coefficients. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{\mathbf{Z}}}_{n}}\Big[\pi\Big(\|Z_{1}\|,\|Z_{2}\|,...,\|Z_{n}\|,\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}\|,\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{2}\|,...,\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{n}\|\Big)\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[\pi\Big(\|Z_{1}\|,\|Z_{1}\|,...,\|Z_{1}\|\Big)\Big].

The same result also holds when the polynomial involves expectation of
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{i}}[\|Z_{i}\|^{\alpha}]
terms.

Proof.

First note that, by Jensen’s inequality, for any
1\leq\beta\leq\alpha
and any nonnegative random variable
X
,

\mathbb{E}[X^{\alpha}]\mathbb{E}[X^{\beta}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{\alpha}]\mathbb{E}[X^{\alpha(\beta/\alpha)}]\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{\alpha}]\mathbb{E}[X^{\alpha}]^{\beta/\alpha}=\mathbb{E}[X^{\alpha}]^{1+\beta/\alpha}\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{\alpha+\beta}].

(49)

By symmetry, this holds also for
1\leq\alpha\leq\beta
. As
\pi
is a polynomial, we can expand it fully as

\pi\Big(\|Z_{1}\|,\|Z_{2}\|,...,\|Z_{j}\|,\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{1}\|,\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{2}\|,...,\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{j}\|\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}c_{k}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\|Z_{j}\|^{\alpha_{j,k}}\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{j}\|^{\beta_{j,k}}

where
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{k}>0
for each
k
.
As every
Z_{i}
,
\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{i}
is iid, for each term in this expansion

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Z}_{n},\,\widetilde{\!{\mathbf{Z}}}_{n}}\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{n}\|Z_{j}\|^{\alpha_{j,k}}\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{j}\|^{\beta_{j,k}}\Big]&=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{j}}[\|Z_{j}\|^{\alpha_{j,k}}]\mathbb{E}_{\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{j}}[\|\,\widetilde{\!{Z}}_{j}\|^{\beta_{j,k}}]=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[\|Z_{1}\|^{\alpha_{j,k}}]\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[\|Z_{1}\|^{\beta_{j,k}}]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[\|Z_{1}\|^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{j,k}+\beta_{j,k})}\Big],\end{split}

where we have used (
49
) to simplify the expansion. Substituting into the expansion of
\pi
, we obtain the desired inequality. If the polynomial involves expectation terms, these can be resolved using Jensen’s inequality.
∎

&\leq c\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\nu}[(1+\|Z\|)^{2}]\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim m}\big[(1+\|\theta\|^{2})^{2}\big].\\
\end{split}

(68)

giving the stated inequalities. As
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{Z})
, and assuming
m\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Theta)
for
p\geq 8
, this implies (
15
). It follows that Assumption

holds whenever

Example F.2

Proposition F.4

(WGE and the parametric Gibbs measure)
.

Assume that Assumption
6
and
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}}\big[\|Z\|^{8}\big]<\infty
hold. Then, the following upper bound holds on the WGE of the parametric Gibbs measure

|\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})|\leq\frac{c}{n}\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}\Big[\big(1+\|Z_{1}\|^{2}\big)^{4}\Big]

where
c<\infty
is independent of
n
.

By leveraging the property of the parametric Gibbs measure being differentially private for bounded parametric loss functions, as stated in
[
Xu and Raginsky 2017
, Theorem 1]
, a sub-optimal bound, i.e.,
|\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}n}}
, can be obtained. However, a tighter upper bound, i.e.,
|\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})|\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}n}
, can be derived by further examinination with a bounded loss assumption using Hoeffding’s lemma, as demonstrated in
[
Raginsky et al. 2016
]
. This tighter bound exhibits a similar rate of decay, namely
\mathcal{O}\big(1/n\big)
. Another upper bound, i.e.,
\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\leq\frac{4\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}n}
, is established in
[
Kuzborskij et al. 2019
, Theorem 1]
, albeit under a different assumption that
\ell_{p}(\theta,z)
is
\gamma
-sub-Gaussian under the parametric Gibbs measure, i.e.,
\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n})
. Similarly,
[
Aminian et al. 2021a
, Theorem 2]
presents an upper bound, i.e.,
\mathrm{gen}(\mathfrak{m}^{\beta,\sigma}(\nu_{n}),\nu_{\mathrm{pop}})\leq\frac{2\gamma^{2}\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}n}
, assuming that the parametric loss function is
\gamma
-sub-Gaussian under the distribution
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

In contrast to the aforementioned studies, we adopt a weaker assumption in this work, namely that
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
possesses a finite 8th moment. This condition holds for sub-Gaussian distributions, as well as numerous others such as the Student’s
t
distribution with more than
8
degrees of freedom or lognormal distributions. Making the stronger assumption that
\nu_{\mathrm{pop}}
possesses a finite
16
th moment (which is still weaker than assuming it is sub-Gaussian), we can apply Theorem
4.3
to give a bound on the strong generalization error.

Proposition F.5

(83)

where, for some

\begin{split}A&=c\,\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}}[(1+\|Z_{1}\|^{2})^{8}]<\infty,\\
