An Upper Bound of the Information Flow From Children to Parent Node on Trees
Cassius Manuel

Abstract
We consider the transmission of a state from the root of a tree towards its leaves, assuming that each transmission occurs through a noisy channel. The states at the leaves are observed, while at deeper nodes we can compute the likelihood of each state given the observation. In this sense, information flows from child nodes towards the parent node. Here we find an upper bound of this children-to-parent information flow. To do so, first we introduce a new measure of information, the memory vector, whose norm quantifies whether all states have the same likelihood. Then we find conditions such that the norm of the memory vector at the parent node can be linearly bounded by the sum of norms at the child nodes. We also describe the reconstruction problem of estimating the ancestral state at the root given the observation at the leaves. We infer sufficient conditions under which the original state at the root cannot be confidently reconstructed using the observed leaves, assuming that the number of levels from the root to the leaves is large.

Introduction

Consider the following broadcasting process. At the root
R
of a tree, sample a state of alphabet
{\mathbb{A}=\{0,1,\cdots,K\}}
following distribution
\bm{\mu}>0
. The tree is
d
-ary (every node has at most
d
children) and has
g

R
to each of the leaves has
g

The sampled state at
R
is then transmitted independently to each child node of
R
through a noisy channel, described by an aperiodic, irreducible Markov matrix
P
. Thus in channel
P=(p_{ij})
, where
i,j\in\mathbb{A}
, entry
p_{ij}
is the probability of state
i
mutating to state
j
during transmission.

The transmission of states to the next level continues analogously until a state is transmitted to every leaf of the tree. The ordered set of states at the leaves is called a
pattern
, represented by symbol
\partial
. See Figure
1
for an example of this process with
\mathbb{A}=\{0,1,2,3\}
.

. The probability of

p_{ik}
. Then, the state
i_{c}
at each node

, while the four subpatterns determined by clades

\partial_{1}=01
,
\partial_{2}=30
,
\partial_{3}=200

\partial_{4}=2
.

We address the following problem: Estimate the ancestral state at the root
R
from the pattern
\partial
assuming that
g
is large, if the tree, the prior
\bm{\mu}
and channel
P
are known. Notably, Maximum Likelihood (ML) has the highest probability of a correct reconstruction among all reconstruction methods (see Theorem 17.2,
[
3
]
). ML computes the posterior state distribution
\bm{r_{\partial}}
at the root given
\partial
. The ML estimate is the state with the maximum posterior probability, also called the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate.

Here we find sufficient conditions making the reconstruction problem unsolvable, meaning that
\bm{r_{\partial}}=\bm{\mu}
for all patterns
\partial
assuming that
g\to\infty
. More explicitly, in Theorem
8.1
we consider a
d
-ary tree and the same channel
P
for all transmissions, with equilibrium frequency
\bm{\pi}
. We prove that, for some constant
C>0
depending on channel
P
, if

Cd<\min\Big\{\frac{1}{3},\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\Big\},

then the reconstruction problem is unsolvable for every pattern
\partial
. As an example, in Corollary
8.3
we show that
C=|\theta_{1}|
when the channel
P
is reversible, where
\theta_{1}\in(-1,1)
is the non-unitary absolutely largest eigenvalue of channel
P
.

If the reconstruction problem is unsolvable, then the MAP estimate is identical to the maximum
a priori
estimate, that is, the ML estimate using only prior
\bm{\mu}
. Since ML has an optimal probability of a correct reconstruction, we can say that, under these conditions, any pattern
\partial
is uninformative about the ancestral root state.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
3
, we describe the likelihood computation on a tree, and then in Section
4
we introduce a new measure of the information carried by a likelihood vector, namely the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm of the memory vector. We rewrite the likelihood vector using the memory vector in Section
5
, which is useful for the bounds of Section
6
.

Our core result is Theorem
6.4
, where we bound the memory-vector norm at a parent node by the sum of memory-vector norms at the child nodes. This theorem is very versatile, since it can be applied to any tree structure, even with a different channel for every child node. The possible definitions of unsolvability are explained in Section
7
. Using the bound of Theorem
6.4
, in Section
8
we give sufficient conditions on matrix

9
.

Comparison with previous results

The solvability of the reconstruction problem has been studied repeatedly in physics, statistic and phylogenetics (see
[
7
]
). Notably, focus has been placed on solvability
in expectation
, meaning the following: If pattern
\partial
has a probability
\Pr(\partial)
of being observed, then characterize whether
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{r_{\partial}}-\bm{\mu}\|]\to 0
as
g\to\infty
. See Section
7
regarding the possible definitions of unsolvability.

Assuming a binary symmetric channel (the so called Ising model), in
[
1
]
it was proven that
d|\theta_{1}|^{2}<1
implies that the reconstruction problem is unsolvable in expectation, where
d
is the
d
-arity of the tree and
\theta_{1}\in(-1,1)
is the non-unitary absolutely largest eigenvalue of matrix
P
. Two generalizations of the Ising model were studied later, namely the binary asymmetric channel and the symmetric
\mathbb{A}
-dimensional channel (Potts model in physics
[
7
]
; Jukes-Cantor model in phylogenetics
[
11
]
). In
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
, it is stated that
for those two kind of channels
, the reconstruction problem is unsolvable in expectation if
{d|\theta_{1}|<1}
. Solvable examples in expectation when
{d|\theta_{1}|>1}
are presented in
[
8
]
.

In contrast, our results apply to any noisy channel, as long as it is irreducible and aperiodic, which are typical assumptions. Notably, we do not rely on the entropy-based theory of communication described by
[
10
]
, as
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
do. Instead, in Subsection
4.3
we propose a new measure of the information carried by a likelihood vector, namely the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm of the memory vector.

Likelihoods on a d-ary Tree

The Pruning algorithm to recursively compute the likelihood of the model parameters given a pattern was introduced in
[
2
]

In this section, we rephrase the Pruning algorithm for a

-ary tree with a different channel for each child node.

We want to compute the likelihood of each state at the root

. To that end, we define the likelihood vector at the root as

A_{c}
given subpattern
\partial_{c}
. More explicitly, we define

\bm{\alpha_{c}}:=(\text{Pr}(\partial_{c}\;|\;A_{c}=0),\cdots,\text{Pr}(\partial_{c}\;|\;A_{c}=K)).

(2)

The Pruning algorithm consists in expressing
\bm{\rho_{\partial}}
in terms of the likelihood vectors
\bm{\alpha_{1}},\cdots,\bm{\alpha_{d}}
. Using the law of total probability, the likelihood vector at the root

considering
only
clade

is

. More explicitly,

P_{c}\bm{\alpha_{c}}=(\text{Pr}(\partial_{c}\;|\;R=0),\cdots,\text{Pr}(\partial_{c}\;|\;R=K)).

(3)

Now we use the fact that the state at each node

was obtained
independently
from the state at
R
. This implies that
\bm{\rho_{\partial}}
equals the entrywise product of all likelihood vectors

. To visualize this better, if we focus on state

, it holds that

\text{Pr}(\partial\;|\;R=0)=\prod_{c\in[d]}\text{Pr}(\partial_{c}\;|\;R=0).

(4)

We denote the entrywise product between two vectors

and
\bm{w}=(w_{i})
as
{\bm{v}\circ\bm{w}:=(v_{i}w_{i})}
. Given vectors
\bm{v_{1}},\cdots,\bm{v_{d}}
, their entrywise product is denoted as
{\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}}\displaylimits_{c\in[d]}\bm{v_{c}}:=\bm{v_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\bm{v_{d}}.}
With this notation, we can write

\bm{\rho_{\partial}}=\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\begin{picture}\put(0.5,0.5){\circle{1.0}}\end{picture}}}}}\displaylimits_{c\in[d]}P_{c}\bm{\alpha_{c}}.

(5)

Now we just have to proceed analogously: Every vector

can be expressed in terms of the child nodes of

, that is, the probability of observing pattern
\partial
. This computation requires the assumption of some prior state distribution

. The law of total probability implies that

\text{Pr}(\partial)=\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\rho_{\partial}},

(6)

where
"\cdot"
denotes the Euclidean dot product.

4
The norm of the memory vector

4.1
The equilibrium frequency

The equilibrium frequency of the noisy channel plays a fundamental role in this work. The necessary preliminaries about the equilibrium frequency can be summarized as follows (see
[
6
]
, Chapter
1
and Section
12.1
).

Proposition 4.1
.

For an irreducible and aperiodic Markov matrix
P
, the following holds:

a)

Matrix
P
has a unique equilibrium distribution
\bm{\pi}>0
, defined by equation
{\bm{\pi}^{T}P=\bm{\pi}^{T}}
.

b)

Matrix
P
has eigenvalue
\theta_{0}=1
with algebraic multiplicity
1
, and the other eigenvalues
\{\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{K}\}\subset\mathbb{C}
have a module smaller than
1
. We assume
{1>|\theta_{1}|\geq\cdots\geq|\theta_{K}|}.

c)

If
P
is reversible, all eigenvalues are real. Moreover, matrix
P
has an orthogonal basis of right eigenvectors
\bm{v_{k}}
and left eigenvectors
\bm{h_{k}}^{T}
such that
\bm{h_{k}}=\bm{\pi}\circ\bm{v_{k}}
for
k\in[0,K]
, where
\bm{v_{0}}=\bm{1}
,
\bm{h_{0}}=\bm{\pi}
and

P=\bm{1}\bm{\pi}^{T}+\bm{v_{1}}\bm{h_{1}}^{T}\theta_{1}+\cdots+\bm{v_{K}}\bm{h_{K}}^{T}\theta_{K}.

4.2
General properties of the norm

For some definitions of this subsection, we follow the notation of
[
6
]
, Section
12.5
. Given distribution
\bm{\pi}>0
, for any two vectors
\bm{x}=(x_{i})
and
\bm{y}=(y_{i})
where
i\in\mathbb{A}
, we define the
\pi
-inner product as

\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\rangle_{\pi}:=\bm{\pi}\cdot(\bm{x}\circ\bm{y})=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}x_{i}y_{i}.

(7)

The
\pi
-inner product induces the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm, defined as

\|\bm{x}\|_{\pi}:=\sqrt{\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle_{\pi}}.

(8)

As in every inner product space, the
triangle inequality holds. Explicitly,

\|\bm{x}+\bm{y}\|_{\pi}\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\pi}+\|\bm{y}\|_{\pi}.

(9)

Denote the Euclidean norm of
\bm{x}
as
\|\bm{x}\|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i}x^{2}_{i}}
, and the uniform norm as
{\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i}|x_{i}|}
. Since
|x_{i}|\leq\|\bm{x}\|
for all
i
and
\sqrt{\min_{i}}\pi_{i}\|\bm{x}\|\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\pi}
, it holds that

\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{x}\|\leq\frac{\|\bm{x}\|_{\pi}}{\sqrt{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}.

(10)

An important reason to use the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm is that a vector cannot increase its
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm when we centralize its entries with weights
\bm{\pi}
, as described in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
(Centralizing inequality).

Given vector
\bm{x}
, the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm satisfies

\|\bm{x}-\bm{1}\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}\|^{2}_{\pi}=\|\bm{x}\|^{2}_{\pi}-\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle^{2}_{\pi}.

In particular, it holds that

\|\bm{x}-\bm{1}\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}\|_{\pi}\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\pi}.

Developing the
\pi
-inner product, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big\langle\bm{x}-\bm{1}\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi},\bm{x}-\bm{1}\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}\Big\rangle_{\pi}=\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle_{\pi}-2\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle^{2}_{\pi}+\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle^{2}_{\pi}\langle\bm{1},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}.

(11)

Since
\langle\bm{1},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}=1
, it follows that

\|\bm{x}-\bm{1}\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}\|^{2}_{\pi}=\|\bm{x}\|^{2}_{\pi}-\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle^{2}_{\pi},

as desired. To obtain the inequality, use the fact that
\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle^{2}_{\pi}\geq 0
, giving

\|\bm{x}-\bm{1}\langle\bm{x},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}\|^{2}_{\pi}\leq\|\bm{x}\|^{2}_{\pi}.

∎

4.3
The memory vector

Consider the likelihood vector
\bm{\rho_{\partial}}
at node
R
determined by pattern
\partial
. We define the normalized likelihood vector
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}:=\bm{\rho_{\partial}}/(\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{\pi})
and the distribution
\bm{r_{\pi}}:=\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\circ\bm{\pi}
. With analogous definitions, given the likelihood vector
\bm{\alpha}
at
A
and pattern
{\partial_{A}}
,
\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}:=\bm{\alpha}/(\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\pi})
and
\bm{a_{\pi}}:=\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}\circ\bm{\pi}
. Note that, assuming prior
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\pi}
(so called stationarity), it holds that
\text{Pr}(\partial)=\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{\pi}
,
\text{Pr}({\partial_{A}})=\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\pi}
, and thus
\bm{r_{\pi}}
and
\bm{a_{\pi}}
are the posteriors under this assumption.

The
\pi
-inner product of
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}
and
\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
is

\langle\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}},\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}\rangle_{\pi}:=\bm{\pi}\cdot(\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\circ\bm{\tilde{\alpha}})=\bm{r_{\pi}}\cdot\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}=\bm{a_{\pi}}\cdot\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}.

(12)

Notably, we have
\langle\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}},\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}=\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{\pi}=1
. Using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, if we write
{\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}=(\tilde{\rho}_{0},\cdots,\tilde{\rho}_{K})}
, we obtain the lower bound

\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\|^{2}_{\pi}:=\langle\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}},\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\rangle_{\pi}=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{i}=(\sum_{i}\pi_{i})(\sum_{i}\pi_{i}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{i})\geq\sum_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}\tilde{\rho}_{i}=1.

(13)

A useful upper bound when dealing with normalized likelihood vectors is

\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\|^{2}_{\pi}=\bm{r_{\pi}}^{T}\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\leq\max_{k}\tilde{\rho}_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\min_{i}\pi_{i}},

(14)

where we used the fact that
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{\pi}=1
, and therefore for all
k
,
\tilde{\rho}_{k}\leq 1/\pi_{k}\leq 1/\min_{i}\pi_{i}
.

However, we are more interested in the distance from
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}
to
\bm{1}
. Intuitively, as
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}
approaches
\bm{1}
, all ancestral states become equally likely, that is, pattern
\partial
becomes less informative. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.3
.

We say that vector
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}
is
the memory vector at node
R
given
\partial
.

When needed, the memory vector will be denoted as
\bm{m}
, with adequate indices depending on the context. The memory vector satisfies

0\leq\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|^{2}_{\pi}=\langle\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1},\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\rangle_{\pi}=\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\|^{2}_{\pi}-1\leq\frac{1}{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}-1,

(15)

that is, the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm of any memory vector is upper bounded by
\sqrt{1/\min_{i}\pi_{i}-1}
.

4.4
Endomorphism of memory vectors

Consider the process of transmission using channel
P
with equilibrium frequency
\bm{\pi}
. If node
A
received its state from a node, say
PA
, then given
\partial_{A}
, node
PA
has likelihood vector
P\bm{\alpha}
. Importantly, since
{\bm{\pi}^{T}P\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\pi}^{T}\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\pi}\cdot\bm{\alpha}}
, it follows that the normalized likelihood vector at node
PA
is
P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
. Thus matrix
P
is an endomorphism of normalized likelihood vectors. Consequently, the memory vector at node
PA
is
P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}
. Moreover, since
P\bm{1}=\bm{1}
, it holds that

P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}=P(\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}),

and thus matrix
P
is also an endomorphism of memory vectors. In Section
8
, we will assume that
\bm{\pi}
is the equilibrium frequency of all channels to preserve this endomorphism.

4.5
The reversible case

If channel
P
is reversible, then the eigendecomposition of Prop.
4.1
.c applies, yielding simple ways to describe the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm and the action of
P
. Indeed, using decomposition
I=\bm{1}\bm{\pi}^{T}+\sum_{k\in[K]}\bm{v_{k}}\bm{h_{k}}^{T}
where
\bm{h_{k}}=\bm{\pi}\circ\bm{v_{k}}
, we can rewrite the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm as

\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\|^{2}_{\pi}=\bm{r_{\pi}}^{T}\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}=\bm{r_{\pi}}^{T}I\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}=1+\sum_{k\in[K]}(\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{h_{k}})^{2}\geq 1.

(16)

Regarding the action of a reversible Markov matrix
P
on a normalized likelihood vector
\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
, it can be alternatively described as

P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}=\bm{1}+\sum_{k\in[K]}\theta_{k}(\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}\cdot\bm{h_{k}})\bm{v_{k}},

(17)

where we used the eigendecomposition of Prop.
4.1
.c,
P=\bm{1}\bm{\pi}^{T}+\sum_{k\in[K]}\theta_{k}\bm{v_{k}}\bm{h_{k}}^{T}
. Recall that the eigenvalues
\theta_{k}
satisfy
1>|\theta_{1}|\geq\cdots\geq|\theta_{K}|
. Using Equations
15
and
16
with
{\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}=P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}}
, the memory vector at node
PA
satisfies

\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|^{2}_{\pi}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\theta_{k}^{2}(\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}\cdot\bm{h_{k}})^{2}.

(18)

Interestingly, this yields the bound

\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|^{2}_{\pi}\leq\theta^{2}_{1}\sum_{k\in[K]}(\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}\cdot\bm{h_{k}})^{2}=\theta_{1}^{2}\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|^{2}_{\pi},

(19)

or taking the square root,

\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq|\theta_{1}|\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

(20)

Hence the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm of the memory vector decreases at least by a factor of
|\theta_{1}|
under the action of the reversible matrix
P
.

4.6
A general bound of the norm growth

The bounds of norm growth as the one of Eq.
20
are useful for our proofs. Without the assumption that matrix
P
be reversible, a general bound similar to Eq.
20
as

\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq C\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}

(21)

for some constant
C>0
is more difficult to obtain, since matrix
P
may have complex eigenvalues or not diagonalize. A weak bound using well known properties of the singular value decomposition (explained in
[
4
]
) can be obtained as follows.

Let us define
\Pi:=\bm{1}\bm{\pi}^{T}
and
\Psi:=\diag(\bm{\pi})
. Since
P\bm{1}=\Pi\bm{1}=\Pi\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}=\bm{1}
for any normalized likelihood vector
\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
, it holds that

P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}=(P-\Pi)(\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}).

(22)

Notably, matrix
P-\Pi
has eigenvalues
0,\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{K}
, although we only need its singular values. Consider the largest singular value
\sigma_{1}
of
P-\Pi
, which satisfies
\sigma_{1}\geq|\theta_{1}|
and thus may be larger than
1
(see
[
4
]
). Let
\|\cdot\|
denote the matrix norm induced by the Euclidean norm. It holds that

\displaystyle\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\|(P-\Pi)(\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1})\|_{\pi}=

\displaystyle=\|\sqrt{\Psi}(P-\Pi)(\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1})\|\leq\|\sqrt{\Psi}\|\;\|P-\Pi\|\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|,

(23)

where the inequality follows because induced norms are sub-multiplicative. Since the Euclidean matrix norm coincides with the spectral norm,
{\|\sqrt{\Psi}\|=\max_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}}
and
{\|P-\Pi\|=\sigma_{1}}
. This together with
\|\bm{x}\|\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\pi}/\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}
gives

\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq\frac{\max_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}}{\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}}\sigma_{1}\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi},

(24)

and thus the bound of Eq.
21
holds for
C=\sigma_{1}\max_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}/\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}
. This bound is specially weak when
\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}<<\max_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}
.

5
Rewriting the likelihood vectors

In this section, we will rewrite the likelihood vectors
\bm{\rho_{\partial}}
and
\bm{\alpha_{c}}
described in Section
3
. We assume that all matrices
P_{c}
have the same equilibrium distribution
\bm{\pi}
. Recall that the normalized likelihood vectors are defined as
{\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}:=\bm{\rho_{\partial}}/(\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{\pi})}

6
Bounds of information flow

Along our proofs, we often need to bound the entrywise product by a sum of vectors. To this end, we prove the following.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any integer
d\geq 2
and reals
S\in(0,2)
and
\epsilon>0
, if
S\leq 4\epsilon/(1+2\epsilon)
, then

(1+\frac{S}{d})^{d}<1+(1+\epsilon)S.

(31)

Expanding
(1+\frac{S}{d})^{d}
, Eq.
31
is equivalent to

\sum_{c\in[2,d]}\binom{d}{c}\frac{S^{c}}{d^{c}}<\epsilon S.

(32)

Since
0<S<2
, we have the bound

\sum_{c\in[2,d]}\binom{d}{c}\frac{S^{c}}{d^{c}}<\sum_{c\in[2,d]}\frac{S^{c}}{c!}<\sum_{c\in[2,\infty]}\frac{S^{c}}{2^{c}}=\frac{S^{2}}{2(2-S)}.

(33)

It is easy to see that, for
S\in(0,2)
, inequality

\frac{S^{2}}{2(2-S)}\leq\epsilon S

(34)

holds iff

S\leq\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon},

(35)

and the result follows.

∎

5
, we introduced the dependence factor
D(\partial)

\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial)=\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial_{1},\cdots,\partial_{d})=D(\partial)\text{Pr}_{IND}(\partial).

Intuitively, if memory vectors

approach

\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
, we have the bound

\|P_{c}\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq C_{c}\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

In these conditions, if

\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\leq\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}

for some
\epsilon>0
, then the following holds:

a)

We have the bound

D(\partial)\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}<(1+\epsilon)\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

b)

If moreover
\epsilon<(1+\sqrt{5})/4
, then

\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}<\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{1+2\epsilon}}\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

To apply Theorem
6.3
, we need the fulfillment of inequality

{\sum_{c\in[d]}\|P_{c}\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\infty}\leq 4\epsilon/(1+2\epsilon)}.

Since
\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{x}\|_{\pi}/\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}
as stated in Eq.
9
, it holds that

\sum_{c\in[d]}\|P_{c}\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}}\sum_{c\in[d]}\|P_{c}\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

We bounded the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm of any memory vector in Eq.
15
, implying

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in[d]}\|P_{c}\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq

(49)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}-1}\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}.

(50)

Consequently, to apply Theorem
6.3
it is enough that

\frac{1}{\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}-1}\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\leq\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon},

or equivalently

\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\leq\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}.

(51)

Equations
49
and
51
, combined with Theorem
6.3
, yield the desired results.

∎

7
Definitions of unsolvability

7.1
Patternwise unsolvability

In a broadcasting process on a
d
-ary tree, we want to describe when the root state cannot be confidently reconstructed using pattern
\partial
, assuming that the tree has a large number of levels
g
. In general, the channels of the broadcasting process can be different, although in Sections
8
and
9
we focus on the case when all channels are equal to
P
.

If we assume a prior state distribution
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{0},\cdots,\mu_{K})
and the likelihood vector at the root
R
is
\bm{\rho_{\partial}}
, then Bayes’ theorem implies that the posterior state distribution is

\bm{r_{\partial}}=\frac{\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\circ\bm{\mu}}{\text{Pr}(\partial)}=\frac{\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\circ\bm{\mu}}{\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{\mu}}.

(52)

If we write
\bm{r_{\partial}}=(r_{0},\cdots,r_{K})
, then the MAP estimate is a state
i\in\mathbb{A}
such that
r_{i}=\max_{i}r_{i}
, and the MAP reconstruction probability is
\max_{i}r_{i}
.

Definition 7.1
.

When
\bm{r_{\partial}}\neq\bm{\mu}
, we say that pattern
\partial
is
informative
, because the observation of
\partial
influences the posterior. In contrast, we say that pattern
\partial
is
uninformative
when
\bm{r_{\partial}}=\bm{\mu}
.

Since
{\bm{r_{\partial}}=\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\circ\bm{\mu}/(\bm{\rho_{\partial}}\cdot\bm{\mu}),}
pattern
\partial
in uninformative iff
\bm{\rho_{\partial}}
is uniform, which occurs iff
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}=\bm{1}
. The next definition states the kind of unsolvability that we will focus on.

Definition 7.2
.

We say that the reconstruction problem is
unsolvable
if every pattern
\partial
is asymptotically uninformative as
g
grows, that is, if

\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}\to\bm{1}\text{ as }g\to\infty.

Since
\bm{\pi}>0
, unsolvability is equivalent to the condition that, for all patterns
\partial
,

\;\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\to 0\text{ as }g\to\infty.

More generally, since all finite-dimensional norms are equivalent (see
[
5
]
), we can use any norm to measure
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}
, although the
L_{2}(\pi)
-norm is convenient for our proofs.

When the reconstruction problem is unsolvable, then, as
g\to\infty
, we have
{\bm{r_{\partial}}\to\bm{\mu}}
and consequently
{\max_{i}r_{i}\to\max_{i}\mu_{i}}
, that is, the MAP reconstruction probability is achieved by just considering the prior. In other words, the observed pattern
\partial
is uninformative about the ancestral root state of the broadcasting process. This is why we use the term "unsolvable", in the sense that we cannot estimate the ancestral root state better than the prior can do.

7.2
Unsolvability in expectation

Notably, the set of all possible patterns
\Delta_{g}
at the leaves of a
g
-level tree grows with
g
. Consequently, the expected value

\mathbb{E}_{\partial}[\;\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}]=\sum_{\partial\in\Delta_{g}}\Pr(\partial)\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}

(53)

has a growing number of summands as
g\to\infty
. In any case,
the equivalence between finite-dimensional norms (see
[
5
]
) implies that all definitions using norms are equivalent.

Definition 7.3
.

We say that the reconstruction problem is
unsolvable in expectation
when, for some norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\partial}[\;\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{*}]\to 0\text{ as }g\to\infty.

Unsolvability for
2
-dimensional or highly symmetric channels was studied in
[
8
]
using the Total Variation (TV) distance.

Definition 7.4

(Unsolvability in
[
8
]
)
.

We say that the reconstruction problem is TV-unsolvable if the likelihood vector
{\bm{\rho_{\partial}}=(\rho^{0}_{\partial},\cdots,\rho^{K}_{\partial})}
satisfies, for all
{i\neq j\in\mathbb{A}}
,

\sum_{\partial\in\Delta_{g}}|\rho^{i}_{\partial}-\rho^{j}_{\partial}|\to 0\text{ as }g\to\infty.

Now define
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{*}]
as the expected value of
\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{*}
with prior
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\pi}
. Recall that
\bm{\mu}>0
by definition and
\bm{\pi}>0
due to Prop.
4.1
.a. In Prop.
7.5
, we prove that Definitions
7.3
and
7.4
are equivalent.

Proposition 7.5
.

The following statements are equivalent:

a)

The reconstruction problem is unsolvable in expectation.

b)

The reconstruction problem is unsolvable in expectation assuming prior
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\pi}
.

c)

The reconstruction problem is TV-unsolvable.

•

a)\iff b)
: Since
\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial)=\bm{\pi}\cdot\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}
and
\text{Pr}(\partial)=\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}
, it holds that

\text{Pr}(\partial)\min_{i}\pi_{i}\leq\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial)\leq\frac{\text{Pr}(\partial)}{\min_{i}\mu_{i}},

(54)

and consequently

\mathbb{E}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{*}]\min_{i}\pi_{i}\leq\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{*}]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{*}]}{\min_{i}\mu_{i}}.

(55)

These chain of inequalities implies that
\mathbb{E}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{*}]\to 0
iff
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{*}]\to 0
, as desired. Note that we can chose any prior as long as it has positive entries.

•

b)\iff c)
: If we write
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}=(\tilde{\rho}^{0}_{\partial},\cdots,\tilde{\rho}^{K}_{\partial})
, we can do

\displaystyle\sum_{\partial\in\Delta_{g}}|\rho^{i}_{\partial}-\rho^{j}_{\partial}|=\sum_{\partial\in\Delta_{g}}\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial)|\tilde{\rho}^{i}_{\partial}-\tilde{\rho}^{j}_{\partial}|=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[|\tilde{\rho}^{i}_{\partial}-\tilde{\rho}^{j}_{\partial}|]

(56)

We have the inequality

|\tilde{\rho}^{i}_{\partial}-\tilde{\rho}^{j}_{\partial}|\leq|\tilde{\rho}^{i}_{\partial}-1|+|\tilde{\rho}^{j}_{\partial}-1|,

(57)

and consequently unsolvability in expectation (using the
L_{1}
-norm) implies TV-unsolvability. Conversely, since
\bm{\pi}
is a distribution and
\bm{\pi}\cdot\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}=1,
it holds that

\displaystyle|\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}^{i}-1|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{j}\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}^{i}\pi_{j}-\sum_{j}\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}^{j}\pi_{j}|=

\displaystyle=|\sum_{j\neq i}\pi_{j}(\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}^{i}-\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}^{j})|\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\pi_{j}|(\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}^{i}-\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}^{j})|.

(58)

Thus if
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[|\tilde{\rho}^{i}_{\partial}-\tilde{\rho}^{j}_{\partial}|]\to 0
for all
i\neq j
then
\mathbb{E}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{1}]\to 0
, as desired.

∎

8
Bounds to the unsolvability problem

Theorem
6.4
is very general, because the only tree structure required is a parent node with
d
children. By applying Theorem
6.4
recursively, for any tree we can bound the memory vector at the root by the sum of the memory-vector norms at the leaves. Applying this strategy, we can find sufficient conditions for the unsolvability of the reconstruction problem.

Theorem 8.1
.

Given any irreducible and aperiodic Markov matrix
P
, assume that, for some
C>0
and for any normalized likelihood vector
\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
, we have the bound

\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq C\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

Then, on a
d
-ary tree, the reconstruction problem using channel
P
is unsolvable if

Cd<\min\Big\{\frac{1}{3},\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\Big\}.

We want to apply Theorem
6.4
with all constants
C_{c}
equal to
C
. First note that, for any upper bound
U
, if the assumption
dC\leq U
holds, then also
kC\leq U
holds for any number of children
k\leq d
. Therefore, assuming
\epsilon<(1+\sqrt{5})/4
and

dC\leq\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}

(59)

as required by Theorem
6.4
.b, then for any node with
k\leq d
children we can apply the bound

\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{\pi}<\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{1+2\epsilon}}C\sum_{c\in[k]}\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

(60)

Consider the normalized likelihood vectors at the leaves, from now on
\bm{\tilde{l}_{c}}
where
{c\in\{1,\cdots,N\}}
and
N\leq d^{g}
. The recursive application of Eq.
60
yields that

\displaystyle\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{\pi}

\displaystyle<(\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{1+2\epsilon}}C)^{g}\sum_{c\in[N]}\|\bm{\tilde{l}_{c}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi},

(61)

while the upper bound of the norm of any memory vector (Eq.
15
) gives

\displaystyle\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{\pi}

\displaystyle<(\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{1+2\epsilon}}C)^{g}\sum_{c\in[N]}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}-1}\leq

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{1+2\epsilon}}C)^{g}d^{g}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}-1}.

(62)

Recall that the reconstruction problem is unsolvable iff
\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{\pi}
tends to
0
as
g
grows. Thus the reconstruction problem is unsolvable if Eq.
59
holds and

\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{1+2\epsilon}}Cd<1,

(63)

as implied by the bound of Eq.
62
. To make the following bounds hold simultaneously,

\displaystyle Cd

\displaystyle<\frac{1-\frac{4\epsilon^{2}}{1+2\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}

\displaystyle Cd

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon},

(64)

the optimal
\epsilon<(1+\sqrt{5})/4
is the one making the two bounds coincide, although this intersection depends on
\min_{i}\pi_{i}
. For the sake of clarity, set
\epsilon=1/2
, yielding the result.

∎

Theorem
8.1
assumes that any memory vector decreases at least by a factor of
C
under the action of
P
. We have inferred such bounds in Equations
20
and
24
, giving the following corollaries.

Corollary 8.2
.

Consider any irreducible and aperiodic Markov matrix
P
with largest singular value
\sigma_{1}
. Then, on a
d
-ary tree, the reconstruction problem using channel
P
is unsolvable if

\sigma_{1}d<\frac{\min_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}}{\max_{i}\sqrt{\pi_{i}}}\min\Big\{\frac{1}{3},\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\Big\}.

Corollary 8.3
.

Consider any irreducible, aperiodic and reversible Markov matrix
P
with non-unitary absolutely largest eigenvalue
\theta_{1}
. Then, on a
d
-ary tree, the reconstruction problem using channel
P
is unsolvable if

|\theta_{1}|d<\min\Big\{\frac{1}{3},\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\Big\}.

In this section we only assumed that the tree is
d
-ary, but Theorem
6.4
is very general and can be applied in multiple scenarios. We can, for example, allow a different channel for every child node as long as
\bm{\pi}
is the equilibrium distribution. To get a tighter bound, one can also apply Theorem
6.4
using the exact number of children at every node, and not an upper bound
d
as we did here.

9
A bound of unsolvability in expectation

Clearly, if the reconstruction problem is unsolvable, then it is unsolvable in expectation. Moreover, there is room to slightly improve our bounds when dealing with unsolvability in expectation. First we can state the analogous of Theorem
6.4
.

Proposition 9.1
.

In the broadcasting process of Figure
2
, , assume that all matrices
P_{c}
have the same equilibrium frequency
\bm{\pi}
. We consider for each pattern
\partial
the normalized likelihood vector
\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}
at
R
and the normalized likelihood vectors
{\rm A\&A}
at child nodes
A_{c}
. Moreover assume that, for some constants
C_{c}>0
and for any normalized likelihood vector
\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
, we have the bound

\|P_{c}\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq C_{c}\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

In these conditions, if

\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\leq\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}

for some
\epsilon>0
, then we have the bound

\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-1\|_{\pi}]<(1+\epsilon)\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial_{c}}[\|{\rm A\&A}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}].

We can apply Theorem
6.4
.a, because the assumptions are identical. Multiplying both sides by
\text{Pr}_{IND}(\partial)
, we get

\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial)\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}<(1+\epsilon)\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\text{Pr}_{IND}(\partial)\|{\rm A\&A}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}

(65)

Recall that pattern
\partial
is composed by subpatterns
\partial_{1},\cdots,\partial_{d}
, and thus indexation using
\partial
is equivalent to indexation using
\partial_{1},\cdots,\partial_{d}
. Moreover, vector
{\rm A\&A}
depends only on
\partial_{c}
, so we can write
{\rm A\&A}=\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c,\partial_{c}}}
. Summing over all
\partial
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}

\displaystyle<(1+\epsilon)\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\sum_{\partial_{1},\cdots,\partial_{d}}\text{Pr}_{IND}(\partial)\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c,\partial_{c}}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}=

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\sum_{\partial_{c}}\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial_{c})\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c,\partial_{c}}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\prod_{f\in[d]-\{c\}}\sum_{\partial_{f}}\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial_{f}),

(66)

where we substituted
\text{Pr}_{IND}(\partial)=\prod_{f\in[d]}\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial_{f})
and rearranged the summands. Since
{\sum_{\partial_{f}}\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial_{f})=1}
for all
f\in[d]
, we get the desired inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{\partial}[\|\bm{\tilde{\rho}_{\partial}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}]

\displaystyle<(1+\epsilon)\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\sum_{\partial_{c}}\text{Pr}_{\pi}(\partial_{c})\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c,\partial_{c}}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)\sum_{c\in[d]}C_{c}\mathbb{E}_{\partial_{c}}[\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}_{c,\partial_{c}}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}].

(67)

∎

Using Proposition
9.1
, we can improve the unsolvability bounds of Section
8
.

Proposition 9.2
.

Given any irreducible and aperiodic Markov matrix
P
, assume that, for some
C>0
and for any normalized likelihood vector
\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}
, we have the bound

\|P\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}\leq C\;\|\bm{\tilde{\alpha}}-\bm{1}\|_{\pi}.

Then, on a
d
-ary tree, the reconstruction problem using channel

, the proof is analogous to that of Theorem

. Eventually we need to make the following two bounds hold simultaneously,

\displaystyle Cd

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}

\displaystyle Cd

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\min_{i}\pi_{i}}{\sqrt{1-\min_{i}\pi_{i}}}\frac{4\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}.
