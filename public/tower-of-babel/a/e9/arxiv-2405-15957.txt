Translators of the mean curvature flow in the special linear group SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
Rafael López, Marian Ioan Munteanu

Abstract
. Translators in the special linear group SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}) are surfaces whose mean curvature H H and unit normal vector N N satisfy H = ⟨ N , X ⟩ H=\langle N,X\rangle , where X X is a fixed Killing vector field. In this paper we study and classify those translators that are invariant by a one-parameter group of isometries. By the Iwasawa decomposition, there are three types of such groups. The dimension of the Killing vector fields is 4 4 and an exhaustive discussion is done for each one of the Killing vector fields and each of the invariant surfaces. In some cases, explicit parametrizations of translators are obtained.

1.
Introduction and preliminaries

In the theory of mean curvature flow (MCF for short) in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, translators of the MCF are
surfaces that evolve purely by translations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A translator
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is characterized by

(1)

H=\langle N,\textbf{v}\rangle,

where
H
and
N
is the mean curvature and unit normal of
\Sigma
and
v
is the direction of translations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The role of translators is important because they are, after rescaling, a type of singularities of the MCF according to Huisken and Sinestrari
[
7
]
. The simplest example is any plane parallel to
v
. Other examples of translators are those that are invariant by a one-parameter group of isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If the translator is invariant in one spatial direction of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the translator is the grim reaper. On the other hand, if the translator is a surface of revolution, then the rotation axis is parallel to
v
. In such a case there are two types of translators depending whether the surface intersects the rotation axis (bowl soliton) or not (wing-like rotational translators). Recently, there is a great interest in extending the notion of translators and solitons in general of the MCF in other homogenous spaces. Without to be complete, we refer: hyperbolic space
[
3
,
4
,
13
,
16
]
; the product
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

[
1
,
2
,
5
,
12
]
; the product
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

[
15
]
; the Sol space
[
18
]
; and the Heisenberg group
[
19
]
.

In this paper we study translators of the MCF in the special linear group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. This space is viewed as a homogeneous space equipped with a canonical left-invariant Riemannian metric whose group of isometries is of dimension
4
. For the definition of translator in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, we extend the notion in (
1
) replacing
v
by a Killing vector field of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
be a Killing vector field. A surface
\Sigma
in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is said to be a
X
-translator
if its mean curvature
H
and unit normal vector
N
satisfy

(2)

H=\langle N,X\rangle,

Let

\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right):a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R},ad-bc=1\right\}.

For the definition of the Riemannian metric, we recall the so-called Iwasawa decomposition
[
9
]
. In the group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
there are the following three (one-dimensional) subgroups:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x\\
0&1\end{array}\right):x\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{y}&0\\
0&1/\sqrt{y}\end{array}\right):y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{array}\right):\theta\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Notice the isomorphisms
\mathcal{N}=(\mathbb{R},+)
,
\mathcal{A}=(\mathbb{R}_{+},\cdot)
and
\mathcal{K}=(\mathbb{S}^{1},\cdot)
. By the Iwasawa decomposition, denoted by NAK decomposition, for every
A\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
there is a unique representation of
A
given by
A=nak
, where
n\in\mathcal{N}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
k\in\mathcal{K}
. This allows to give global coordinates
(x,y,\theta)
in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
by means of

(3)

(x,y,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\longmapsto\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{y}&0\\
0&1/\sqrt{y}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{array}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}).

With respect to these coordinates, let
\{\partial_{x},\partial_{y},\partial_{\theta}\}
be the canonical basis of
\mathfrak{X}(\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
.

Remark 1.2
.

If
A\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, then the characteristic polynomial of
A
is

\lambda^{2}-\mbox{trace}(A)\lambda+1
. This distinguishes the matrices of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
in three types depending on the number of roots of this polynomial. This classification is equivalent to the NAK decomposition. Indeed, if
|\mbox{trace}(A)|=2
, there is a unique double eigenvalue; if
|\mbox{trace}(A)|>2
, there are two distinct real eigenvalues and if
|\mbox{trace}(A)|<2
, there are no real eigenvalues. Examples of such matrices are, respectively, that of
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{K}
. In the literature, the elements of the subgroups
\mathcal{N}
,

\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{K}
are also called parabolic, hyperbolic and elliptic matrices, respectively
[
10
]
.

Let
\mathbb{H}^{2}(-4)
be the hyperbolic plane of constant curvature
-4
and its upper half plane model

\mathbb{H}^{2}(-4)=\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{4y^{2}}\right).

The special linear group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})

acts transitively and isometrically on
\mathbb{H}^{2}(-4)
by the
linear fractional transformation

\left(A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right),(x,y)=x+iy=z\right)\mapsto A\cdot z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

The isotropy subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
at

is the subgroup
\mathcal{K}

(4)

\pi:\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{H}^{2}(-4),\quad\pi(x,y,\theta)=(x,y).

The mapping

\psi:\mathbb{H}^{2}(-4)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}),

\psi(x,y,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{y}&0\\
0&1/\sqrt{y}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\

is a diffeomorphism. In particular, the space
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is topologically the open solid torus

With this metric, the projection

4

The space of Killing vector fields in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})

\{\partial_{x},\partial_{\theta},x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y},\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2})\partial_{x}+xy\partial_{y}\}.

2.
The mean curvature of invariant surfaces

In this section, we compute the unit normal vector
N
and the mean curvature
H
of surfaces invariant by each one of the three subgroups
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{K}
of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. Part of the computations of this section have appeared in
[
11
,
17
]
. By completeness of the paper and for the subsequent study of the
X
-translators, we recall them. Consider in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
the orthonormal frame
B=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
defined by

(6)

e_{1}=2y\partial_{x}-\partial_{\theta},\ e_{2}=2y\partial_{y},\ e_{3}=\partial_{\theta}.

The Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
of the metric
\langle\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \rangle
of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is given by the relations

\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1}=2e_{2},&\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2}=-2e_{1}-e_{3},&\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{3}=e_{2},\\
\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}=e_{3},&\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{2}=0,&\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{3}=-e_{1},\\
\nabla_{e_{3}}e_{1}=e_{2},&\nabla_{e_{3}}e_{2}=-e_{1},&\nabla_{e_{3}}e_{3}=0.\end{array}

The expressions of Killing vector fields (
5
) in terms of the above basis are

\begin{split}\partial_{x}&=\frac{1}{2y}(e_{1}+e_{3}),\\
\partial_{\theta}&=e_{3},\\
x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}&=\frac{1}{2y}(xe_{1}+ye_{2}+xe_{3}),\\
\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2})\partial_{x}+xy\partial_{y}&=\frac{1}{2y}\left(\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2})e_{1}+xye_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2})e_{3}\right).\end{split}

We now give parametrizations of the invariant surfaces in order to compute
N
and
H
. To have a consistent notation, if
\Psi=\Psi(s,t)
are local coordinates on the invariant surface, the parameter
s
will be assigned for the generating curve of the surface, whereas
t
will denote the parameter of the group.

Proposition 2.1
.

An
\mathcal{N}
-invariant surface of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
can be parametrized by

(7)

\Psi(s,t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&t\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{y(s)}&0\\
0&1/\sqrt{y(s)}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta(s)&\sin\theta(s)\\
-\sin\theta(s)&\cos\theta(s)\end{array}\right),

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
s\in I\subset\mathbb{R}
.
The generating curve is
\alpha(s)=(y(s),\theta(s))
.
The unit normal
N
and the mean curvature
H
are

(8)

\begin{split}N&=\frac{y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}\Phi}(e_{1}-e_{3})+\frac{\sqrt{2}y\theta^{\prime}}{\Phi}e_{2},\\
H&=\frac{\sqrt{2}y^{2}}{\Phi^{3}}\left(\theta^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime}\theta^{\prime\prime}+2y\theta^{\prime 3}\right),\end{split}

where
\Phi=\sqrt{y^{\prime 2}+2y^{2}\theta^{\prime 2}}
.
If we take
\alpha(s)
such that

(9)

\begin{split}y^{\prime}(s)&=\sqrt{2}y(s)\cos\varphi(s),\\
\theta^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\varphi(s),\end{split}

then

(10)

\begin{split}N&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cos\varphi(e_{1}-e_{3})+\sin\varphi e_{2},\\
H&=-\frac{\varphi^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sin\varphi.\end{split}

.

An

,

\begin{split}N&=\frac{1}{\Phi}\left(-\left(x^{\prime}+2t\theta^{\prime}\right)e_{1}+x^{\prime}e_{3}\right)\\

\Phi=\sqrt{(x^{\prime}+2t\theta^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\prime 2}}.\quad

Corollary 6.2
.

There are no rotational
W
-translators with constant mean curvature.

Proof.

Suppose that
H
is constant which, without loss of generality, we can assume to be non-negative. Then (
16
) implies
\varphi^{\prime}(s)=2H-2\cos\varphi(s)
. The solution of this ODE is, up to translations in the
s
-parameter:

\begin{split}\varphi(s)&=-2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-H)\tanh\left(\sqrt{1-H^{2}}s\right)}{\sqrt{1-H^{2}}}\right),\quad(0\leq H<1),\\
\varphi(s)&=-2\cot^{-1}(2s),\quad{\textrm{or}}\quad\varphi(s)=0,\quad(H=1),\\
\varphi(s)&=2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(H-1)\tan\left(\sqrt{H^{2}-1}s\right)}{\sqrt{H^{2}-1}}\right),\quad(H>1).\end{split}

Once we have
\varphi
, we can explicitly integrate (
15
). In the following expressions,
c
is a constant of integration.

(1)

Case
0\leq H<1
.

\begin{split}x(s)&=-c\frac{\sqrt{1-H^{2}}\sinh(2\sqrt{1-H^{2}}s)}{\cosh(2\sqrt{1-H^{2}}s)+H},\\
y(s)&=c(H-\cos\left(2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-H)\tanh(\sqrt{1-H^{2}}s)}{\sqrt{1-H^{2}}}\right)\right)).\\
\end{split}

(2)

Case
H=1
. If
\varphi(s)=0
we obtain the surface
\Sigma_{y_{0}}
which is not a
translator. For the other value of
\varphi
we get

\begin{split}x(s)&=-\frac{1}{2}c\sin(2\cot^{-1}(2s)),\\
y(s)&=\frac{c}{4s^{2}+1}.\\
\end{split}

(3)

Case
H>1
.

\begin{split}x(s)&=c\frac{\sqrt{H^{2}-1}\sin(2\sqrt{H^{2}-1}s)}{\cos(2\sqrt{H^{2}-1}s)+H},\\
y(s)&=c(H-\cos\left(2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(H-1)\tan(\sqrt{H^{2}-1}s)}{\sqrt{H^{2}-1}}\right)\right)).\\
\end{split}

Finally, we compute
\langle N,W\rangle
using (
25

H\not=\langle N,W\rangle

\begin{split}\langle N,W\rangle&=-\frac{1}{4}c\sqrt{1-H^{2}}\sinh(2\sqrt{1-H^{2}}s),\quad(0\leq H<1),\\
\langle N,W\rangle&=0,\quad(H=1),\\
\langle N,W\rangle&=\frac{1}{4}c\sqrt{H^{2}-1}\sin(2\sqrt{H^{2}-1}s),\quad(H>1).\end{split}
