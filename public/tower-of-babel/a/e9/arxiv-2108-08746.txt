Harnack inequality for fractional Laplacian-type operators on hyperbolic spaces
Jongmyeong Kim, Minhyun Kim, Ki-Ahm Lee

Abstract
. The Krylov–Safonov theory for fully nonlinear nonlocal operators on hyperbolic spaces of dimension three is established. Since the operators on hyperbolic spaces exhibit qualitatively different behavior than those on manifolds with nonnegative curvature, new scale functions are introduced which take the effect of negative curvature into account. The regularity theory in this work provides unified regularity results for fractional-order and second-order operators in the sense that the regularity estimates stay uniform as the fractional-order approaches 2. In the unified regularity theory, the asymptotic behavior of the normalizing constant for the fractional Laplacian plays a fundamental role. The dimension restriction has been imposed to compute the explicit value of this constant by using the Fourier analysis on hyperbolic spaces.

1.
Introduction

In a celebrated series of papers
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
by Caffarelli and Silvestre, the regularity theories such as Krylov–Safonov, Cordes–Nirenberg, and Evans–Krylov theory are established for fractional-order operators on Euclidean spaces. The most important feature is that the constants in regularity estimates do not blow up and remain uniform as the order of operator converges to 2. It means that the regularity theories for fractional-order and second-order operators are unified. It has also been extended to the parabolic cases
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

On the other hand, the regularity theory for the local operators on Riemannian manifolds has been studied. In particular, the studies on the Harnack inequalities, initiated by Yau
[
43
]
and Cheng–Yau
[
7
]
, have been extended to second-order divergence and non-divergence form operators. These are extensions of the De Giorgi–Nash–Moser
[
31
]
and Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequalities
[
2
,
21
,
42
]
, respectively. See also
[
22
,
23
]
for the parabolic Harnack inequalities.

The natural extension is to obtain the regularity results for fractional-order operators on Riemannian manifolds. Indeed, the Harnack inequality for nonlocal operators on metric measure spaces with volume doubling property, which includes Riemannian manifolds with nonnegative curvature, has been studied via the Dirichlet form theory
[
6
]
. However, this approach does not provide the unified regularity theory and is not appropriate for non-divergence form operators. For nonlocal non-divergence form operators, the Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequality on Riemannian manifolds with nonnegative curvature has recently been established by the authors
[
19
]
. The result in
[
19
]
unifies the Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequalities for local and nonlocal operators on manifolds with nonnegative curvature as in the works of Caffarelli and Silvestre.

In this paper, we continue to pursue the unified regularity theory for fractional-order and standard local nonlinear operators on 3-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
that has a nonnegative curvature. The study on 3-dimensional hyperbolic spaces is geometrically meaningful especially in general relativity. In this work, the dimension restriction has been imposed to compute the explicit value of normalizing constant for the fractional Laplacian, which is crucial for the unified regularity theory as in
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
due to its asymptotic behavior as
\gamma
approaches 1.

The fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}})^{\gamma}
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
, on hyperbolic spaces
{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
is defined recently in
[
1
]
by Banica, González, and Sáez to study extension problem, and the explicit form of its kernel up to normalizing constant is provided. To the best of authors’ knowledge, this normalizing constant is not known. It has been observed
[
20
]
that finding the constant and studying its asymptotic behavior are the most important steps when a scaling is not available. In this regard, the first part of the paper is devoted to finding the explicit value of the constant. Indeed, this can be done on hyperbolic spaces with curvature
-\tau^{-2}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{H}^{3}_{\tau}
be the 3-dimensional hyperbolic space with curvature
-\tau^{-2}
. The fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{3}_{\tau}})^{\gamma}
is given by

(1.1)

-(-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{3}_{\tau}})^{\gamma}u(x)=\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{3}_{\tau}}(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,\tau}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{3}_{\tau}}(x^{\prime},x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{H}^{3}_{\tau}}(x^{\prime}),\quad x\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}}.

Here, the kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,\tau}
has an explicit form

(1.2)

\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,\tau}(\rho)=C(3,\gamma)\frac{1/\tau}{\sinh\rho/\tau}\rho^{-1/2-\gamma}\frac{2K_{-3/2-\gamma}(\rho/\tau)}{\Gamma(3/2+\gamma)(2\tau)^{3/2+\gamma}},

where
K_{\nu}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind and

C(n,\gamma)=\frac{2^{2\gamma}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+\gamma)}{\pi^{n/2}|\Gamma(-\gamma)|}

is the normalizing constant for the fractional Laplacian on Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 1.2
.

(i)

The novelty of
Theorem

1.1
is that the normalizing constant has the same asymptotic behavior with
1-\gamma
as
\gamma\to 1
, which is crucial in the upcoming regularity theory, as in
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

(ii)

It is natural to expect that
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,\tau}
converges to the kernel of the fractional Laplacian on Euclidean space as curvature
-\tau^{-2}
approaches zero. Indeed, it follows from
Lemma

A.3
that

\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,\tau}(\rho)\to C(3,\gamma)\rho^{-3-2\gamma},

as
\tau\to\infty
.

(iii)

The kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,\tau}
decays exponentially as
\rho\to\infty
, which is different from the behavior of the kernel on manifolds with nonnegative curvature. The difference comes from the exponential growth of the volume of balls in hyperbolic spaces, and this is why the regularity theories on manifolds with negative and nonnegative curvatures are dealt with separately.

(iv)

In
Theorem

1.1
, the only 3-dimensional case is considered due to the difficulty in some computation of integrals in, e.g.,
Section

4
and
Section

5
. Such a restriction on dimensions is common on hyperbolic spaces. However, the harmonic analysis in
Section

3
is provided for general dimensions. Moreover, most of the arguments in
Section

6
–
Section

9
work in general dimensions with minor modifications if
Theorem

1.1
is true for general dimensions.

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
is not as simple as in the case of Euclidean spaces. In the Euclidean case, it follows from

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\gamma}u}(\xi)&=-\frac{1}{2}C(n,\gamma)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\widehat{u(\cdot+y)}(\xi)+\widehat{u(\cdot-y)}(\xi)-2\hat{u}(\xi)}{|y|^{n+2\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}C(n,\gamma)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{e^{i\xi\cdot y}+e^{-i\xi\cdot y}-2}{|y|^{n+2\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\hat{u}(\xi)\\
&=C(n,\gamma)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1-\cos(\xi\cdot y)}{|y|^{n+2\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\hat{u}(\xi)\\
&=C(n,\gamma)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1-\cos y_{1}}{|y|^{n+2\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)|\xi|^{2\gamma}\hat{u}(\xi)\end{split}

and the definition
\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\gamma}u}(\xi)=|\xi|^{2\gamma}\hat{u}(\xi)
that

C(n,\gamma)=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1-\cos y_{1}}{|y|^{n+2\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{-1}=\frac{2^{2\gamma}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+\gamma)}{\pi^{n/2}|\Gamma(-\gamma)|}.

The computation (
1.3
) relies heavily on the group structure of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},+)
that
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{a}
does not have. Instead, the gyrogroup structure of hyperbolic spaces, discovered by Ungar
[
35
]
and developed as a tool for the formulation of special relativity as an alternative to the use of Lorentz transformations to represent compositions of velocities, can be used to perform a similar computation as in (
1.3
). See also
[
39
]
.

The aim of the next part of the paper is to establish the Alexandrov–Bakelman–Pucci (or ABP for short) estimates, Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequality, and Hölder estimates for solutions of nonlocal nonlinear elliptic equations on 3-dimensional hyperbolic space, which are robust in the sense that the estimates remain uniform as
\gamma
approaches 1. Modeled on the fractional Laplacian (
1.1
) with
\tau=1
, nonlinear operators of the fractional Laplacian-type can be defined in the standard way. For a class
\mathcal{L}_{0}
of linear operators of the form

Lu(x)=\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}(u(x^{\prime})-u(x))\mathcal{K}(d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}(x^{\prime},x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x^{\prime}),\quad x\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}},

with kernels
\mathcal{K}
satisfying

\lambda\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\leq\mathcal{K}\leq\Lambda\mathcal{K}_{\gamma},\quad 0<\lambda\leq\Lambda,

the
maximal
and
minimal operators
are defined by

\mathcal{M}^{+}u(x):=\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}u(x):=\sup_{L\in\mathcal{L}_{0}}Lu(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{M}^{-}u(x):=\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}u(x):=\inf_{L\in\mathcal{L}_{0}}Lu(x),

respectively. It is easy to see that these extremal operators are well-defined at
x\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
for any bounded function
u
that is
C^{2}
near
x
(see (
5.2
)). An operator
\mathcal{I}
is said to be
elliptic with respect to
\mathcal{L}_{0}
if

\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(u-v)(x)\leq\mathcal{I}(u,x)-\mathcal{I}(v,x)\leq\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(u-v)(x)

for every point
x\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
and for all bounded functions
u
and
v
which are
C^{2}
near
x
.

The first step towards the Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequality and Hölder estimate is showing the ABP-type estimate. The following theorem provides an estimate on the distribution function of supersolutions to nonlocal nonlinear operators. Throughout the paper, the functions
\frac{\sinh t}{t}
and

are denoted by
\mathcal{S}(t)
and
\mathcal{H}(t)
, respectively, as in

. Moreover, we introduce a function
\mathcal{T}(t)=t/\tanh^{-1}(\frac{1}{2}\tanh(t))
. To focus on the limiting behavior as
\gamma\to 1
, we set
\tau=1
from now on.

Theorem 1.3

(ABP-type estimate)
.

Let
n=3
,
\gamma_{0}\in(0,1)
, and assume
\gamma\in[\gamma_{0},1)

satisfying
\mathcal{M}^{-}u\leq f
in

,

in

. Let

be a contact set defined by (

of dyadic cubes, with

\mathscr{C}\subset\cup_{j}\overline{Q}^{j}_{\alpha}

|B_{R}|\leq\sum_{j}cF^{n}|Q^{j}_{\alpha}|,

The universal constant

depends only on

,
\lambda
,

The scale function

The main difficulties in establishing regularity results,
Theorem

1.3
,
Theorem

1.4
, and
Theorem

1.5
, arise from the effect of negative curvature. The volume of ball in hyperbolic spaces behaves like that in Euclidean spaces when a radius is small, while it grows exponentially as a radius gets bigger. Due to this non-homogeneity of volume, the scaling property does not hold, making the standard arguments for regularity results break. To overcome this difficulty, we introduce new scale functions that take non-homogeneity into account and provide some monotonicity properties (see
Section

5
).

Another difficulty arising from the non-homogeneity of volumes lies in the dyadic ring argument in ABP estimates. In the ABP estimates, we find a dyadic ring around a given contact point in which a supersolution is quadratically close to a tangent paraboloid in a large portion of the ring. However, the standard dyadic rings
B_{2^{-k}r}\setminus B_{2^{-(k+1)}r}
no longer work in the framework of hyperbolic spaces. It leads to introducing a
hyperbolic dyadic ring
whose radii are determined by volumes of balls (see
Section

6
). The hyperbolic dyadic ring turns out to be the natural “dyadic” ring in the hyperbolic geometry.

For the construction of a barrier function, it is now standard to use the distance function. However, computation is significantly different from the standard one because of the hyperbolic structure. We observe in
Section

7
how the negative curvature of hyperbolic spaces affects the computations.

.

Theorem

are proved in

and

In this section, we recall several models for the
n
-dimensional hyperbolic spaces (see, e.g.,
[
34
,
30
,
15
]
). Moreover, the fractional Laplacian is defined as in
[
1
]
by using the Helgason Fourier transform on these spaces
[
14
,

,
33
]
. See also
[

,

]
. Furthermore, some well-known properties on hyperbolic spaces are collected.

Let us first recall the hyperboloid model

\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}=\{(x_{0},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{n}^{2}=\tau^{2},x_{0}>0\},

with the metric induced by Lorentzian metric
-\mathrm{d}x_{0}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}-\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The space
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}
has a constant curvature
-\tau^{-2}<0
. The internal product induced by the Lorentzian metric is denoted by

[x,x^{\prime}]=x_{0}x_{0}^{\prime}-x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}-\cdots-x_{n}x_{n}^{\prime},

and the distance between two points
x
and
x^{\prime}
is given by

d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}}(x,x^{\prime})=\tau\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{[x,x^{\prime}]}{\tau^{2}}\right).

Using the polar coordinates,
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}
can also be realized as

\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}=\left\{x=\tau(\cosh r,\sinh r\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:r\geq 0,\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\right\}.

Then, the metric and volume element are given by
\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\tau^{2}(\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\sinh^{2}r\,\mathrm{d}\omega^{2})
and
\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}}=\tau^{n}\sinh^{n-1}r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\omega
, respectively.

Let us also consider the Poincaré ball model
\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|y|<t\}
with metric

(2.1)

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\frac{4b^{2}}{(t^{2}-|y|^{2})^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y^{2}.

The volume measure is given by

(2.2)

\mathrm{d}\mu_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(y)=\left(\frac{2b}{t^{2}-|y|^{2}}\right)^{n}\,\mathrm{d}y.

If
b/t=\tau
, then the map defined by

(2.3)

\phi:(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots x_{n})\in\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}\mapsto\frac{t}{\tau+x_{0}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t},

or equivalently, by

\phi:\tau(\cosh r,\sinh r\omega)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}\mapsto t\frac{\sinh\frac{r}{2}}{\cosh\frac{r}{2}}\omega\in\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t},

is an isometry, and its inverse is given by

\phi^{-1}:y\in\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}\mapsto\left(\tau\frac{t^{2}+|y|^{2}}{t^{2}-|y|^{2}},\frac{2\tau ty_{1}}{t^{2}-|y|^{2}},\cdots,\frac{2\tau ty_{n}}{t^{2}-|y|^{2}}\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}.

See
[
15
, Chapter 8]
for the proof. Therefore, there are several ways of describing the distance function as follows:

d_{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}(0,y)=d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}}(0_{\tau},x)=\tau\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{[0_{\tau},x]}{\tau^{2}}\right)=\tau\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{t^{2}+|y|^{2}}{t^{2}-|y|^{2}}\right)=\tau\log\left(\frac{t+|y|}{t-|y|}\right),

where
0_{\tau}=(\tau,0,\cdots,0)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}
. We shall write
0_{\tau}=0
if there is no confusion.

Note that the metric (
2.1
) tends to the Lebesgue measure
\mathrm{d}y
as
\tau\to\infty
, provided that
\tau=t/2
and
b=t^{2}/2
. We will consider this case in the sequel because we are interested in the convergence of the normalizing constants for the fractional Laplacian in hyperbolic spaces to those in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let us next recall the Helgason Fourier transform on
{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
. The interested reader may consult
[
17
,
14
,
33
,
15
,
10
]
. The Helgason Fourier transform is defined by

(2.4)

\hat{u}(\lambda,\xi;t)=\int_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}u(y)e_{-\lambda,\xi;t}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(y),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},~\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},

where

(2.5)

e_{\lambda,\xi;t}(y)=\left(\frac{t^{2}-|y|^{2}}{|t\xi-y|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{n-1+i\lambda t}{2}}

is the eigenfunction with eigenvalue
-(\lambda^{2}+\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{t^{2}})
. It is well known that the following inversion formula holds:

(2.6)

u(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\hat{u}(\lambda,\xi;t)e_{\lambda,\xi;t}(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}{(t/2)^{n-1}|c(\lambda t)|^{2}},

where

c(\lambda t)=\sqrt{2}(2\pi)^{n/2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{i\lambda t}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-1+i\lambda t}{2})}

is the Harish-Chandra coefficient. Moreover, the Plancherel formula holds:

(2.7)

\int_{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}|u(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}|\hat{u}(\lambda,\xi;t)|^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}{(t/2)^{n-1}|c(\lambda t)|^{2}}.

Note that all the formulas in this section can be written in terms of
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\tau}
by using isometry (
2.3
). Furthermore, it is well known
[
10
]
that (
2.4
), (
2.6
), and (
2.7
) converge to the standard Fourier transform, inversion formula, and Plancherel formula on Euclidean spaces as
t\to\infty
.

We define the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}})^{\gamma}
by

(2.8)

\widehat{(-\Delta_{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}})^{\gamma}u}=\left(\lambda^{2}+\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{\gamma}\hat{u},

which coincides with the definition in
[
1
]
when
a=t/2=1
. Similarly as in
[
1
]
, it has an integral representation

3.
The Möbius gyrogroup

In this section, the gyrogroup structure of the Poincaré ball model
{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
is studied as a tool for analyzing kernels for the fractional Laplacian. After first discovered in 1988 by Ungar
[
35
]
, it has been extensively studied in the context of abstract algebra, non-Euclidean geometry, mathematical physics, and quantum information and computation
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.

Let us first recall the Clifford algebra
\mathcal{C}\ell_{0,n}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
before we define the gyrogroup addition. The Clifford algebra
\mathcal{C}\ell_{0,n}
is the associative real algebra generated by
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}
subject to the relation

(3.1)

x^{2}=-|x|^{2},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
|\cdot|
denotes the standard Euclidean norm. For example,
\mathcal{C}\ell_{0,1}
and
\mathcal{C}\ell_{0,2}
are isomorphic to the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
and quaternions
\mathbb{H}
, respectively. In the Clifford algebra, there is a unique anti-automorphism satisfying
\bar{x}=-x
and
\overline{xy}=\bar{y}\bar{x}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Clearly, it holds that
x\bar{x}=|x|^{2}=\bar{x}x
,
|xy|^{2}=|x|^{2}|y|^{2}
, and
|x|=|\bar{x}|
. Moreover, it follows from the relation (
3.1
) that

x\bar{y}=\frac{1}{2}(x\bar{y}+y\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2}(x\bar{y}-y\bar{x})=\langle x,y\rangle+\frac{1}{2}(x\bar{y}-y\bar{x}),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The group of all conformal orientation preserving transformations of
{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
consists of the mappings
K\varphi_{y}
, where
K\in SO(n)
and
\varphi_{x}
is the Möbius transformation on
{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
. It is known
[
37
,
38
]
that
\varphi_{x}
is given by

\varphi_{x}(y)=(x+y)\left(1+\frac{\bar{x}y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\langle x,y\rangle+\frac{1}{t^{2}}|y|^{2})x+(1-\frac{1}{t^{2}}|x|^{2})y}{1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\langle x,y\rangle+\frac{1}{t^{4}}|x|^{2}|y|^{2}}.

By defining the
Möbius addition
by

x\oplus y=\varphi_{x}(y),

the tuple
({\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}},\oplus)
forms a gyrogroup, that is, the following properties hold:

(i)

(Left identity)
0\oplus x=x
for all
x\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
.

(ii)

(Left inverse) For each
x\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
, there is
\ominus x=-x
such that
(\ominus x)\oplus x=0
.

(iii)

(Gyroassociative)
x\oplus(y\oplus z)=(x\oplus y)\oplus\mathrm{gyr}[x,y]z
for all
x,y,z\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
, where

\mathrm{gyr}[x,y]z=\left(1+\frac{x\bar{y}}{t^{2}}\right)z\left(1+\frac{\bar{x}y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}

is the gyration operator.

(iv)

(Left loop property)
\mathrm{gyr}[x,y]=\mathrm{gyr}[x\oplus y,y]
for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
.

The gyrogroup
({\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}},\oplus)
is gyrocommutative, i.e.,
x\oplus y=\mathrm{gyr}[x,y](y\oplus x)
for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
. Let us define the second operation
\boxplus
so-called
coaddition
by

x\boxplus y=x\oplus\mathrm{gyr}[x,\ominus y]y.

It can be easily checked that the tuple
({\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}},\boxplus)
forms a gyrogroup with the left identity 0 and left inverse
\boxminus x=-x
. We will use the abbreviation
x\ominus y=x\oplus(\ominus y)
and
x\boxminus y=x\boxplus(\boxminus y)
. Then we have

(3.2)

x\boxminus y=x\ominus\mathrm{gyr}[x,y]y.

See
[
37
,
38
]
for more properties of gyrogroups
({\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}},\oplus)
and
({\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}},\boxplus)
. Among many properties, the following cancellation laws will be used in
Section

4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
37
, Theorem 2.15]

Let
a,b\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
. Then
x=\ominus a\oplus b
is the unique solution of the equation
a\oplus x=b
, and
x=b\boxminus a
is the unique solution of the equation
x\oplus a=b
.

In the rest of this section, we perform some computations on the eigenfunctions
e_{\lambda,\xi;t}
and the measures
\mu_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}
for future references.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and for
y,z\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
,

e_{-\lambda,\xi;t}(z\boxminus y)=\left(\frac{\left|\xi-\frac{z}{t}\right|^{2}\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{2}}{\left|\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}\right)\xi-\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\frac{z}{t}+\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\frac{y}{t}\right|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{n-1-i\lambda t}{2}}e_{-\lambda,\xi;t}(z).

Proof.

We use (
3.2
) and the formula

1-\frac{|a\oplus b|^{2}}{t^{2}}=\left(1-\frac{|a|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{|b|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\left|1+\frac{\bar{a}b}{t^{2}}\right|^{-2},

which is given in
[
10
, Equation (16)]
, to obtain

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}1-\frac{|z\boxminus y|^{2}}{t^{2}}&=1-\frac{|z\oplus(-\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y)|^{2}}{t^{2}}\\
&=\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\left|1-\frac{\bar{z}\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y}{t^{2}}\right|^{-2}.\end{split}

On the other hand, using (
3.2
) and the definition of gyration, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\xi-\frac{z\boxminus y}{t}\right|^{2}&=\left|\xi-\frac{z\ominus\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y}{t}\right|^{2}\\
&=\left|\xi-\frac{1}{t}(z-\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y)\left(1-\frac{\bar{z}\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right|^{2}\\
&=\left|\xi\left(1-\frac{\bar{z}\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y}{t^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{t}(z-\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y)\right|^{2}\left|1-\frac{\bar{z}\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y}{t^{2}}\right|^{-2}.\end{split}

A further computation shows that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\xi\left(1-\frac{\bar{z}\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y}{t^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{t}(z-\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y)\right|^{2}\\
&=\left|\xi-\frac{z}{t}+\frac{1}{t}\left(1-\frac{\xi\bar{z}}{t}\right)\left(1+\frac{z\bar{y}}{t^{2}}\right)y\left(1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right|^{2}\\
&=\left|\left(\xi-\frac{z}{t}\right)\left(1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right)+\left(1-\frac{\xi\bar{z}}{t}\right)\left(1+\frac{z\bar{y}}{t^{2}}\right)\frac{y}{t}\right|^{2}\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{-2}\\
&=\left|\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}\right)\xi-\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\frac{z}{t}+\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\frac{y}{t}\right|^{2}\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{-2}.\end{split}

Combining (
3.3
), (
3.4
), and (
3.5
), and recalling the definition of eigenfunction (
2.5
), the lemma is proved.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For
y,z\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
,

\mathrm{d}\mu_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(z\boxminus y)=\left(\frac{1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}}{\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{2}}\right)^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}(z).

Let us prove the following two lemmas in order to prove
Lemma

3.3
. The first one is a computation of (
3.2
).

Lemma 3.4
.

For
y,z\in{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}
,

z\boxminus y=\bigg(1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}\bigg)^{-1}\left(\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)z-\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)y\right).

Proof.

A straightforward computation shows that

\displaystyle z\boxminus y

\displaystyle=z\ominus\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y=z\ominus\left(1+\frac{z\bar{y}}{t^{2}}\right)y\left(1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bigg(z-\left(1+\frac{z\bar{y}}{t^{2}}\right)y\left(1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}\bigg)\bigg(1-\frac{1}{t^{2}}\bar{z}\left(1+\frac{z\bar{y}}{t^{2}}\right)y\left(1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}\bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bigg(z+\frac{|z|^{2}}{t^{2}}y-y-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}z\bigg)\left(1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right)^{-1}\bigg(1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}\bigg)^{-1}\left(1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)z-\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)y\right)\bigg(1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}\bigg)^{-1}.

∎

The next lemma provides the Jacobian determinant of
z\boxminus y
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
J
be the Jacobian matrix
\frac{\partial(z\boxminus y)}{\partial z}
. Then,

|J|=\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}\right)^{-(n+1)}\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{n}\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{2}.

Proof.

Let
A=1-|z|^{2}|y|^{2}/t^{4}
. Using
Lemma

3.4
, the
(i,j)
-th entry, for
i,j=1,\cdots,n
, is obtained as

\frac{\partial(z\boxminus y)_{i}}{\partial z_{j}}=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\left(A\delta_{ij}+2\frac{z_{j}y_{i}}{t^{2}}+2\frac{|y|^{2}z_{i}z_{j}}{t^{4}}\right).

We use the technique in
[
41
, Lemma 5]
to compute the Jacobian determinant
|J|
. That is, we change
|J|
into

|J|=\frac{1}{A^{2n}}\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{n}\begin{vmatrix}~1&2z_{1}&\cdots&2z_{n}\\
~0&A+2\frac{z_{1}y_{1}}{t^{2}}+2\frac{|y|^{2}z_{1}^{2}}{t^{4}}&\cdots&2\frac{z_{n}y_{1}}{t^{2}}+2\frac{|y|^{2}z_{1}z_{n}}{t^{4}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
~0&2\frac{z_{1}y_{n}}{t^{2}}+2\frac{|y|^{2}z_{n}z_{1}}{t^{4}}&\cdots&A+2\frac{z_{n}y_{n}}{t^{2}}+2\frac{|y|^{2}z_{n}^{2}}{t^{4}}\end{vmatrix}

by adding zeroth row and zeroth column. For
i=1,\cdots,n
, we multiply the first row of the above matrix by
-\frac{y_{i}}{t^{2}}-\frac{|y|^{2}z_{i}}{t^{4}}
and add it to the
i
-th row. Then we obtain

|J|=\frac{1}{A^{2n}}\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{n}\begin{vmatrix}1&2z_{1}&2z_{2}&\cdots&2z_{n}\\
-\frac{y_{1}}{t^{2}}-\frac{|y|^{2}z_{1}}{t^{4}}&A&0&\cdots&0\\
-\frac{y_{2}}{t^{2}}-\frac{|y|^{2}z_{2}}{t^{4}}&0&A&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\frac{y_{n}}{t^{2}}-\frac{|y|^{2}z_{n}}{t^{4}}&0&0&\cdots&A\end{vmatrix}.

By multiplying the
j
-th column by
\frac{1}{A}(\frac{y_{j}}{t^{2}}+\frac{|y|^{2}z_{j}}{t^{4}})
,
j=1,\cdots,n
, and adding it to the first column, we arrive at

|J|=\frac{1}{A^{2n}}\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{n}\begin{vmatrix}\frac{1}{A}\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{2}&2z_{1}&\cdots&2z_{n}\\
0&A&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&A\end{vmatrix}=\frac{1}{A^{n+1}}\left(1-\frac{|y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{n}\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{2}.

∎

We are now ready to prove
Lemma

3.3
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

By using (
2.2
), (
3.3
), and
Lemma

3.5
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}(z\boxminus y)&=\left(1-\frac{|z\boxminus y|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{-n}\,\mathrm{d}(z\boxminus y)\\
&=\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{-n}\left|1-\frac{\bar{z}\mathrm{gyr}[z,y]y}{t^{2}}\right|^{2n}\left(1-\frac{|z|^{2}|y|^{2}}{t^{4}}\right)^{-(n+1)}\left|1+\frac{\bar{z}y}{t^{2}}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z.\end{split}

Therefore, the desired result follows from

, and hence

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}\left(E(\lambda,T\xi,y,Tz)+E(\lambda,T\xi,-y,Tz)-2\right)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,t/2}(d_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(0,y))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}\left(E(\lambda,\xi,T^{-1}y,z)+E(\lambda,\xi,-T^{-1}y,z)-2\right)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,t/2}(d_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(0,y))\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{{\mathbb{B}^{n}_{t}}}(y)

Thus, by taking

that maps

to

and

to a point in

-plane, we may assume that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\mathrm{Im}\,E(\lambda,\xi,r\omega,z)\,\mathrm{d}\omega=0,

from which (

For notational convenience, let us write

where

, and

attains its minimum. Let

\Theta
attains its maximum, and
\theta_{2}>\theta_{1}

Therefore, combining (

\displaystyle\begin{split}I=&\int_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}\frac{(ABD)^{n-1}s^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}}{\sqrt{W^{2}\omega_{1}^{2}-Z^{2}}}\left(\frac{W^{2}}{U-V\sqrt{W^{2}\omega_{1}^{2}-Z^{2}}}\right)^{n-2}\sin\left(\frac{\lambda t}{2}\log s\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\\

where

,

. Since

and

, and hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathrm{Im}\,E(e_{1},y,z)\,\mathrm{d}\omega

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathrm{Im}\,E(e_{1},y,z)\,\mathrm{d}\omega

A simple computation shows that

U^{2}-V^{2}(W^{2}-Z^{2})=\left((Z_{1}X_{2}-Z_{2}X_{1})^{2}+(Y_{1}Z_{2}-Y_{2}Z_{1})^{2}-(X_{1}Y_{2}-X_{2}Y_{1})^{2}\right)W^{2}.

\displaystyle Z_{1}X_{2}-Z_{2}X_{1}

\displaystyle=-2ABD\left(C\frac{r}{t}\left(1+\frac{r^{2}\|z\|^{2}}{t^{4}}\right)-2\frac{rz_{1}}{t^{2}}\left(A-B\frac{z_{1}}{t}\right)\right),

which yield that

Finally, the change of variables

yields that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathrm{Im}\,E(e_{1},y,z)\,\mathrm{d}\omega

\displaystyle=\pi\int_{\frac{t-r}{t+r}}^{\frac{t+r}{t-r}}\left(\frac{t}{r}-\frac{r}{t}\right)s^{-1}\sin\left(\frac{\lambda t}{2}\log s\right)\,\mathrm{d}s

which proves (

5.
Scale functions

Having the explicit form of kernels (

As mentioned in

\mathcal{K}_{\gamma,1}=\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)&:=\mathcal{I}_{0,\gamma}(R):=\int_{B_{R}(x)}d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}^{2}(z,x)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}(z,x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z),\\

\mathcal{I}(R):=\mathcal{I}_{\gamma}(R):=\mathcal{I}_{0,\gamma}(R)+\mathcal{I}_{\infty,\gamma}(R)

A.3

|Lu(x)|\leq\Lambda\|u\|_{C^{2}(\overline{B_{R}})}\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)+2\Lambda\|u\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{H}^{n}})}\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(R)<+\infty

for any

.

In 3-dimensional case, the scale functions (

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)=

\displaystyle~\frac{2^{\gamma}}{(1-\gamma)|\Gamma(-\gamma)|}R^{3-\gamma}\left(I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}+I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}\right)

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{S}^{2}|\int_{0}^{R}\rho^{2}\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(\rho)\sinh^{2}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho

where
\nu=3/2+\gamma
. By using

A.1

\displaystyle S^{3}_{3/2+\gamma}(R)=

\displaystyle~\frac{2^{-3/2}\pi^{1/2}}{(1-\gamma)}R^{3-\gamma}\left(I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}+I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle~-\frac{2^{-3/2}\pi^{1/2}}{(1-\gamma)(2-\gamma)}R^{3-\gamma}\left(I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}+I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}\right),

from which (
5.3
) follows.
∎

The scale functions
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
correspond to
\frac{C(3,\gamma)|\mathbb{S}^{2}|}{2-2\gamma}R^{2-2\gamma}
and
\frac{C(3,\gamma)|\mathbb{S}^{2}|}{2\gamma}R^{2-2\gamma}
, respectively, in the case of Euclidean spaces. While
R^{2-2\gamma}
has a scaling property, the scale functions
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
do not have it. This is one of the main difficulties in the upcoming regularity theory. However, we will observe that some monotonicity properties play a similar role in the following sections. For example,
\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)
is increasing and
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
is decreasing by definition. Moreover, the following lemma provides another monotonicity.

Lemma 5.2
.

The function
\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)/R^{2-\gamma}
is decreasing in
R
.

Proof.

Let us define the function

\displaystyle F(R):=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\gamma)|\Gamma(-\gamma)|}{2^{\gamma}}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)}{R^{2-\gamma}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\left(I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}+\frac{1-\gamma}{2-\gamma}I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}-\frac{1}{2-\gamma}I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}\right).

Using the relations (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), a straightforward calculation yields

F^{\prime}(R)=-\gamma I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}-(1-\gamma)I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}+I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}.

Thus, we conclude that

F^{\prime}(R)\leq\left(-\gamma-(1-\gamma)+1\right)I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}=0,

by using the facts that
I_{\nu}
is decreasing and
K_{\nu}
is increasing with respect to
\nu
when
\nu>0
.
∎

An obvious consequence of
Lemma

5.2
is the following.

Corollary 5.3
.

The function
\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)/R^{2}
is decreasing in
R
.

The following lemma shows a relation between two scale functions
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
. Let us mention that the function
\mathcal{H}(t)=t\coth t
naturally appears when the negative curvature is involved as in

.

Lemma 5.4
.

For all
R>0
,

\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(R)\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\mathcal{H}(R)\mathcal{I}_{0}(R).

Proof.

Let us define the function

F=\frac{\gamma|\Gamma(-\gamma)|}{2^{\gamma}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\infty}-\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{I}_{0}\right)

and show that
R^{-2+\gamma}F^{\prime}\leq 0
. Using

5.1
and the relations (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we have

\displaystyle R^{-2+\gamma}F^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~2(1-\gamma)I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}+2I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}-2(1-\gamma)\mathcal{H}I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}

\displaystyle-\left(\mathcal{H}-\frac{R^{2}}{\sinh^{2}R}\right)\left(I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}+\frac{1-\gamma}{2-\gamma}I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}-\frac{1}{2-\gamma}I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}\right).

Using
\mathcal{H}I_{1/2}=RI_{-1/2}
and (

) again, we obtain

\mathcal{H}I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}=RI_{3/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}+(1+2\gamma)I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}+I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}.

Thus, in light of
\mathcal{H}-\frac{R^{2}}{\sinh^{2}R}\geq 0
and

, we have

\displaystyle R^{-2+\gamma}F^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~(2-2(1-\gamma)(1+2\gamma))I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}-2(1-\gamma)RI_{3/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}

\displaystyle-\left(\mathcal{H}-\frac{R^{2}}{\sinh^{2}R}\right)\left(I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}+\frac{1-\gamma}{2-\gamma}I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}-\frac{1}{2-\gamma}I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~-2\gamma(1-2\gamma)I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}-\left(\mathcal{H}-\frac{R^{2}}{\sinh^{2}R}\right)\left(I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}-I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}\right).

Moreover, since
I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}-I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}\geq 0

\mathcal{H}-\frac{R^{2}}{\sinh^{2}R}\geq\frac{R^{2}}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1}+1},

to obtain

\frac{R^{-2+\gamma}F^{\prime}}{I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}}\leq-2\gamma(1-2\gamma)-\frac{R^{2}}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1}+1}\left(\frac{I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}-I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}}{I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}}\right).

We now use bounds for ratios of modified Bessel functions

(5.5)

\frac{I_{\nu+1/2}(R)}{I_{\nu-1/2}(R)}<\frac{R}{\sqrt{R^{2}+\nu^{2}}+\nu},\quad\nu\geq 0,

and

(5.6)

\frac{K_{\nu}(R)}{K_{\nu+1}(R)}\leq\frac{R}{\sqrt{R^{2}+(\nu-1/2)^{2}}+\nu+1/2},\quad\nu\geq 1/2.

See
[
32
, Theorem 3 and 6]
for the proofs of these bounds. By using (

)–(
5.6
) and monotonicity of

with respect to

\displaystyle\frac{I_{1/2}K_{3/2+\gamma}-I_{5/2}K_{-1/2+\gamma}}{I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\sqrt{R^{2}+1}+1)(\sqrt{R^{2}+\gamma^{2}}+1+\gamma)}{R^{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{R^{2}+4}-2}{R}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\sqrt{R^{2}+1}+1)(\sqrt{R^{2}+\gamma^{2}}+1+\gamma)}{R^{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{R^{2}+1}+1}{R}

\displaystyle\geq(1+\gamma)\frac{\sqrt{R^{2}+1}+1}{R^{2}}.

Therefore, we conclude that

\frac{R^{-2+\gamma}F^{\prime}}{I_{3/2}K_{1/2+\gamma}}\leq-2\gamma(1-2\gamma)-(1+\gamma)=-(1+4\gamma)(1-\gamma)\leq 0.

∎

Since the limit behavior of the scale functions as
\gamma\to 0
are of interest in the unified regularity theory, we also prepare the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any fixed
R>0
,

\lim_{\gamma\to 1}\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)=6\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\gamma\to 1}\mathcal{I}_{\infty}(R)=0.

Proof.

The modified Bessel functions
I_{\nu}
and
K_{\nu}
are elementary functions when
\nu
is a half-integer. For examples, we have

\displaystyle I_{1/2}=\left(\frac{2}{\pi R}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sinh R,

\displaystyle K_{1/2}=\left(\frac{\pi}{2R}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-R},

\displaystyle I_{3/2}=\left(\frac{2}{\pi R}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(-\frac{\sinh R}{R}+\cosh R\right),

\displaystyle K_{3/2}=\left(\frac{\pi}{2R}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{R}\right)e^{-R},

\displaystyle I_{5/2}=\left(\frac{2}{\pi R}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\left(1+\frac{3}{R^{2}}\right)\sinh R-\frac{3}{R}\cosh R\right),

\displaystyle K_{5/2}=\left(\frac{\pi}{2R}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\frac{3}{R}+\frac{3}{R^{2}}\right)e^{-R}.

Thus, by using

\lim_{\gamma\to 1^{-}}(1-\gamma)|\Gamma(-\gamma)|=1\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\gamma|\Gamma(-\gamma)|=1,

a straightforward computation proves the lemma.
∎

In the work of Caffarelli and Silvestre
[
3
]
, the quantity
r_{0}=\rho_{0}2^{-1/(2-2\gamma)}R
, which is characterized by the relation
(r_{0}/\rho_{0})^{2-2\gamma}=R^{2-2\gamma}/2
, plays a fundamental role. The most important feature of this quantity is that it converges to 0 as
\gamma\to 0
.

We define such quantity in a similar way in our framework. Since the scale function
\mathcal{I}_{0}
is strictly increasing, its inverse exists. Thus, for given
R>0
, we define
r_{0}\in(0,R)
by

(5.7)

r_{0}=\rho_{0}\mathcal{I}_{0}^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)/2)

for some universal constant
\rho_{0}\in(0,1)
that will be determined later. Let us close the section with the following lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

For fixed
R>0
,
\lim_{\gamma\to 1}r_{0}=0
.

Proof.

Let us fix
R>0
and suppose that
\lim_{\gamma\to 1}r_{0}\neq 0
. Then, since
\tilde{r}:=\limsup_{\gamma\to 1}r_{0}\neq 0
, there is a sequence
\gamma_{k}\to 1
such that
r_{0,k}:=\rho_{0}\mathcal{I}_{0,\gamma_{k}}^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{0,\gamma_{k}}(R)/2)\in(0,R)
converges to
\tilde{r}
. We have

(5.8)

\mathcal{I}_{0,\gamma_{k}}(r_{0,k}/\rho_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}_{0,\gamma_{k}}(R).

By
Lemma

5.5
and continuity of
\mathcal{I}_{0}
, the left-hand side of (

) converges to 6, whereas the right-hand side of (
5.8
) converges to 3, which is a contradiction.
∎

In this section, we provide the proof of

1.3

is assumed to be a supersolution given in

1.3
. On Riemannian manifolds, the distance squared function in construction of envelope was suggested by Cabré in
[

, and has been used by many in
[

P_{y}(z)=c_{y}-\frac{1}{2R^{2}}d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}^{2}(z,y)

that touches

from below, with a contact point

of

The first step toward to ABP-type estimates for nonlocal operators is to find a ring around a given contact point in which supersolution

is quadratically close to the paraboloid in a large portion of the ring. In Euclidean spaces
[
3
]
, or more generally in Riemannian manifolds with nonnegative curvature
[
19
]
, the standard dyadic rings
B_{2^{-k}r_{0}}\setminus B_{2^{-(k+1)}r_{0}}

r_{k}
recursively by

(6.2)

\frac{|B_{r_{k}}|}{|B_{r_{k-1}}|}=2^{-n},\quad k=1,2,\cdots,

and a

by
R_{k}=R_{k}(x)=B_{r_{k}}(x)\setminus B_{r_{k+1}}(x)
. Note that we have from

2.1

|B_{r_{k}}|/|B_{r_{k-1}}|\leq(r_{k}/r_{k-1})^{n}
, and hence
r_{k+1}\geq r_{k}/2
. Using the hyperbolic dyadic rings, we will prove a series of lemmas to deduce

1.3
. For notational convenience, we shall write

F(x):=\Lambda\mathcal{H}(7R)+\frac{R^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)}f(x)

throughout the section.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

be a supersolution given in

Let

\displaystyle=-\Lambda\int_{B_{R}(x)\cup B_{5R}}\delta^{+}\left(\frac{1}{2R^{2}}d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}^{2}(\cdot,y),x,z\right)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z),

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=-\Lambda\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus(B_{R}(x)\cup B_{5R})}\delta^{-}(u,x,z)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z),

7.
A barrier function

This section is devoted to a construction of a special barrier function, which is a key ingredient together with the ABP-type estimates for the Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequality. It is standard to use distance function to construct a barrier function, but computations are significantly different from the standard argument. We will observe how the negative curvature of hyperbolic spaces comes into play. Let us begin with some inequalities.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
R_{0}>0
. Then,

(7.1)

(\cosh^{-1}(t\cosh R_{0}))^{-2\alpha}-R_{0}^{-2\alpha}\geq-2\alpha R_{0}^{-2\alpha-2}\mathcal{H}(R_{0})\left(t-1\right)

for all
t>1/\cosh R_{0}
. Moreover, for
\mathcal{H}(t)=t\coth t
,

(7.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{(\cosh^{-1}(t\cosh R_{0}))^{-2\alpha-2}}{A^{2}\cosh^{2}R_{0}-1}-\frac{R_{0}^{-2\alpha-2}}{\sinh^{2}R_{0}}\\
&\geq-\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2\alpha}}\left((2\alpha+2)+2\mathcal{H}(R_{0})\right)\mathcal{H}(R_{0})\frac{t-1}{R_{0}^{2}}\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2}\sinh^{2}R_{0}},\end{split}

and

(7.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(\cosh^{-1}(t\cosh R_{0}))^{-2\alpha-1}\frac{t\cosh R_{0}}{(t^{2}\cosh^{2}R_{0}-1)^{3/2}}-R_{0}^{-2\alpha-1}\frac{\cosh R_{0}}{\sinh^{3}R_{0}}\\
&\geq-R_{0}^{-2\alpha}\left((2\alpha+1)\mathcal{H}(R_{0})-R_{0}^{2}+3\mathcal{H}^{2}(R_{0})\right)\mathcal{H}(R_{0})\frac{t-1}{R_{0}^{2}}\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2}\sinh^{2}R_{0}},\end{split}

for all
t>1/\cosh R_{0}
.

Proof.

Since the function

f(t):=(\cosh^{-1}(t\cosh(R_{0}))^{-2\alpha},\quad t>\frac{1}{\cosh R_{0}},

is convex, (
7.1
) follows from the inequality
f(t)\geq f(1)+f^{\prime}(1)(t-1)
. The inequalities (
7.2
) and (
7.3
) can be obtained similarly by considering

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(\cosh^{-1}(t\cosh R_{0}))^{-2\alpha-2}}{t^{2}\cosh^{2}R_{0}-1}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle:=(\cosh^{-1}(t\cosh R_{0}))^{-2\alpha-1}\frac{t\cosh R_{0}}{(t^{2}\cosh^{2}R_{0}-1)^{3/2}},

which are also convex functions.
∎

Using

, we first construct a barrier function when

is sufficiently close to 1.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let

\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{+}v(x)+\Lambda\mathcal{H}(7R)\leq 0,

for every

and

Fix

and let
R_{0}:=d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}(x,0)\in(\delta R/4,5R)

. There is an isometry

with

. Notice that 0 denotes

Let

\displaystyle-(5R)^{2\alpha}\left(\alpha(2\alpha+1)\frac{(\cosh^{-1}A)^{-2\alpha-2}}{(A^{2}-1)^{1/2}}+\alpha\frac{A(\cosh^{-1}A)^{-2\alpha-1}}{(A^{2}-1)^{3/2}}\right)B^{2}

, we can make the second and third terms in (
7.12
) be as small as we want, by choosing

close to 1. Therefore, the proof is finished by assuming that we have taken

sufficiently large so that (

∎

In the following lemma, we construct a barrier function for any

for given

Lemma 7.3

Given

8.

-estimate

In this section, we prove the so-called

-estimate, which forms a basis for the proofs of Harnack inequality and Hölder estimates. It connects a pointwise estimate to an estimate in measure.

Lemma 8.1
.

Assume
0<\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma<1
, and let
\delta\in(0,1)
. If

is a nonnegative function on
{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
satisfying
\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{-}u\leq\varepsilon_{\delta}
in

\frac{|\{u\leq M_{\delta}\}\cap B_{\delta R}|}{|B_{7R}|}\geq\mu_{\delta},

where
\varepsilon_{\delta}>0
,
\mu_{\delta}\in(0,1)
, and
M_{\delta}>1
are universal constants depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
\delta
,
R
, and
\gamma_{0}

Proof.

Let
v_{\delta}
be the barrier function constructed in

7.4

w

in

in
B_{5R}
. By applying
Theorem

1.3
to
w
with its envelope
\Gamma_{w}

|B_{R}|\leq\sum_{j}cF^{n}|Q^{j}_{\alpha}|,

where

Since

|B_{R}|\leq C\varepsilon_{\delta}^{n}\sum_{\overline{Q}_{\alpha}^{j}\cap\overline{B}_{\delta R/4}=\emptyset}|Q_{\alpha}^{j}|+C\sum_{\overline{Q}_{\alpha}^{j}\cap\overline{B}_{\delta R/4}\neq\emptyset}|Q_{\alpha}^{j}|

for some universal constant
C>0
, depending on
R
. By taking
\varepsilon_{\delta}>0
sufficiently small, we have

|B_{7R}|\leq C\sum_{\overline{Q}_{\alpha}^{j}\cap\overline{B}_{\delta R/4}\neq\emptyset}|Q_{\alpha}^{j}|.

By using

6.5
(iv), we obtain

|B_{7R}|\leq C\sum_{\overline{Q}_{\alpha}^{j}\cap\overline{B}_{\delta R/4}\neq\emptyset}|B(z_{\alpha}^{j},2r_{0})\cap\{w\leq\Gamma_{w}+C\}|.

Whenever

is contained in

if we have taken
\rho_{0}=\delta/4
. Indeed, for

d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,0)\leq d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,z_{\alpha}^{j})+d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z_{\alpha}^{j},z_{\ast})+d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z_{\ast},0)\leq 2r_{0}+r_{0}+\delta R/4<\delta R,

|B_{7R}|\leq C|\{u\leq M_{\delta}\}\cap B_{\delta R}|

∎

9.
Harnack inequality

The purpose of this section is to prove the Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequality by using
Lemma

8.2
. A simple scaling argument will provide
Theorem

1.4
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Assume
0<\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma<1
. If a nonnegative function
u\in C^{2}(B_{7R})
satisfies

\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{-}u\leq\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{+}u\geq-\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{in}~B_{7R}

and
\inf_{B_{\delta_{1}R}}u\leq 1
, then

\sup_{B_{\delta_{1}R/4}}u\leq C,

where
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
C>0
are universal constants depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
R
, and
\gamma_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon_{\delta}
be the constants given in
Lemma

8.2
, and let
t>0
be the minimal value such that the following holds:

u(x)\leq h_{t}(x):=t\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}}\bigg(1-\frac{d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x,z_{0})}{\delta_{1}R}\bigg)^{\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}\bigg)^{-1/\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all}~x\in B_{\delta_{1}R},

where for
\mathcal{D}=2^{n}\cosh^{n-1}(2\delta_{0}R)
. Since
\sup_{B_{\delta_{1}R/4}}u\leq t\mathcal{D}^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_{2}(3/8)}
, we can conclude the theorem once we show that
t\leq C
for some universal constant
C
.

Let
x_{0}\in B_{\delta_{1}R}
be a point such that
u(x_{0})=h_{t}(x_{0})
. Let
d=\delta_{1}R-d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}(x_{0},0)
,
r=d/2
, and
A=\{u>u(x_{0})/2\}
, then we have

u(x_{0})=h_{t}(x_{0})=t\mathcal{D}^{1/\varepsilon}\left(\frac{2r}{\delta_{1}R}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}.

We apply
Lemma

8.2
to
u
to obtain

|A\cap Q_{1}|\leq C\left(\frac{u(x_{0})}{2}\right)^{-\varepsilon}|Q_{1}|\leq Ct^{-\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}|Q_{1}|,

where
Q_{1}
is the unique dyadic cube of generation
k_{R}
that contains the point
x_{0}
.

We will show that there is a small constant
\theta>0
such that

(9.1)

|A^{c}\cap Q_{2}|\leq\frac{1}{2}|Q_{2}|,

where
Q_{2}\subset Q_{1}
is the dyadic cube of generation
k_{\theta r/14}
containing the point
x_{0}
, provided that
t
is large. However, when
t
is sufficiently large, we also have

|A\cap Q_{2}|\leq|A\cap Q_{1}|\leq\frac{C}{t^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}|B(z,c_{2}\delta_{0}^{k_{R}})|\leq\frac{C}{t^{\varepsilon}}|B(z,c_{1}\delta_{0}^{k_{r\theta/14}})|\leq\frac{C}{t^{\varepsilon}}|Q_{2}|<\frac{1}{2}|Q_{2}|,

where
B(z,c_{1}\delta_{0}^{k_{\theta r/14}})
is a ball contained in
Q_{2}
. This contradicts to (
9.1
) and will lead us to a conclusion that
t
is uniformly bounded.

Let us now focus on proving (
9.1
). For every
x\in B(x_{0},\theta r)
, we have

u(x)\leq h_{t}(x)\leq t\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{d-\theta r}{\delta_{1}R}\right)^{\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}\right)^{-1/\varepsilon}=\left(1-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}u(x_{0}).

We define a function

v(x):=\left(1-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}u(x_{0})-u(x).

Since we will apply
Lemma

8.2
, we need a function which is nonnegative on the whole space. Thus, we apply
Lemma

8.2
to
w:=v^{+}
in
B(x_{0},7(\theta r/14))
. For
x\in B(x_{0},7(\theta r/14))
, we have

(9.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{M}^{-}w(x)&\leq\mathcal{M}^{-}v(x)+\mathcal{M}^{+}v_{-}(x)\\
&\leq-\mathcal{M}^{+}u(x)+\Lambda\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B(x_{0},\theta r)}v^{-}(z)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\\
&\leq\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}}\varepsilon_{0}+\Lambda\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B(x_{0},\theta r)}(u(z)-(1-\theta/2)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}u(x_{0}))^{+}\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}(z,x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).\end{split}

To compute the last integral in (
9.2
), we introduce another auxiliary function

g_{\beta}(x):=\beta\left(1-\frac{d_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}}(x,0)^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)^{+},

with the largest number
\beta>0
satisfying
u\geq g_{\beta}
. From the assumption
\inf_{B_{\delta_{1}R}}u\leq 1
, we have
(1-\delta_{1}^{2})\beta\leq 1
. Let
x_{1}\in B_{R}
be a point where
u(x_{1})=g_{\beta}(x_{1})
. Since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\delta^{-}(u,x_{1},z)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\delta^{-}(g_{\beta},x_{1},z)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathcal{H}(7R)\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}},

we obtain that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{-}u(x_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\delta^{+}(u,x_{1},z)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)-C\Lambda\mathcal{H}(7R).

Thus, we have

(9.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(u(z)-c)^{+}\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\delta^{+}(u,x_{1},z)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\leq C\mathcal{H}(7R)\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}},\end{split}

where
c:=1/(1-\delta_{1}^{2})
.

If
u(x_{0})\leq c
, then we find an upper bound
t=u(x_{0})(\delta_{1}R/d)^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}\leq c\delta_{1}^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log_{2}\mathcal{D}}
, which finishes the proof. Otherwise, it follows from (
9.2
) and (
9.3
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-}w(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}}\varepsilon_{0}+\Lambda\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B(x_{0},\theta r)}(u(z)-c)_{+}\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}}\varepsilon_{0}+\Lambda M\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B(x_{0},\theta r)}(u(z)-c)_{+}\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}}\varepsilon_{0}+CM\mathcal{H}(7R)\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}},

where

M=\sup\left\{\frac{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x))}{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1}))}:x\in B(x_{0},\theta r/2),x_{1}\in B_{R},z\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B(x_{0},\theta r)\right\}.

Let
d=d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x)
and
d_{1}=d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{1})
for the sake of brevity. If
d\geq d_{1}
, then by
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
we have

\frac{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d)}{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{1})}=\frac{\sinh d_{1}}{\sinh d}\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d}\right)^{1/2+\gamma}\frac{K_{3/2+\gamma}(d)}{K_{3/2+\gamma}(d_{1})}\leq\frac{e^{d_{1}}\sinh d_{1}}{d_{1}}\frac{d}{e^{d}\sinh d}.

Since the function
s\mapsto e^{s}\sinh s/s
is increasing, we obtain
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d)/\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{1})\leq 1
. When
d<d_{1}
, by
[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
again we have

\frac{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d)}{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{1})}\leq\frac{e^{d_{1}}\sinh d_{1}}{e^{d}\sinh d}\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d}\right)^{2+2\gamma}\leq\frac{e^{d_{1}}\sinh d_{1}}{e^{d}\sinh d}\left(\frac{d_{1}}{d}\right)^{4}.

Since
d_{1}/d\to 1
as
d\to\infty
, so is
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d)/\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{1})
. Thus, for any cases the ratio
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d)/\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{1})
is bounded by a universal constant which is independent of
\gamma
. By using
Corollary

5.3
, we arrive at

\frac{(\theta r/2)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(\theta r/2)}\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}w\leq C\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R)/R^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(\theta r/2)/(\theta r/2)^{2}}\mathcal{H}(7R)\leq C

in
B(x_{0},7(\theta r/14))
.

Let
Q_{2}\subset Q_{1}
be the dyadic cube of generation
k_{\theta r/14}
containing the point
x_{0}
. Then by
Lemma

8.2
, we have

\displaystyle|\{u<u(x_{0})/2\}\cap Q_{2}|

\displaystyle=|\{w>\left(\left(1-\theta/2\right)^{-\gamma}-1/2\right)u(x_{0})\}\cap Q_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C|Q_{2}|}{\left(\left(1-\theta/2\right)^{-\gamma}-1/2\right)^{\varepsilon}u(x_{0})^{\varepsilon}}\left(\inf_{B(x_{0},\delta_{1}\theta r/14)}w+C\right)^{\varepsilon}.

We can make the quantity
(1-\theta/2)^{-\gamma}-1/2
bounded away from 0 by taking
\theta>0
sufficiently small. Recalling that
w(x_{0})=((1-\theta/2)^{-\gamma}-1)u(x_{0})
, we obtain

|\{u<u(x_{0})/2\}\cap Q_{2}|\leq C|Q_{2}|\left(((1-\theta/2)^{-\gamma}-1)^{\varepsilon}+\left(\frac{C}{u(x_{0})}\right)^{\varepsilon}\right).

We choose a constant
\theta>0
sufficiently small so that

C\left((1-\theta/2)^{-\gamma}-1\right)^{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

If
t>0
is sufficiently large so that
C(C/u(x_{0}))^{\varepsilon}<1/4
, then we arrive at (
9.1
). Therefore,
t
is uniformly bounded and the desired result follows.
∎

10.
Hölder estimates

In this section, the following Hölder regularity result is proved.
Theorem

1.5
follows from simple scaling and covering arguments.

Lemma 10.1
.

Assume
0<\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma<1
. There is a universal constant
\varepsilon_{0}
such that if
u\in C^{2}(B_{7R})
is a function such that
|u|\leq\frac{1}{2}
in
B_{7R}
and

\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{+}u\geq-\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{-}u\leq\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{in}~B_{7R},

then
u\in C^{\alpha}
at
0\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
with an estimate

|u(x)-u(0)|\leq CR^{-\alpha}d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x,0)^{\alpha},

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C>0
are universal constants depending only on
n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
,
R
, and
\gamma_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
R_{k}:=7\cdot 4^{-k}R
and
B_{k}:=B_{R_{k}}
. It suffices to construct an increasing sequence
\{m_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and a decreasing sequence
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that
m_{k}\leq u\leq M_{k}
in
B_{k}
and
M_{k}-m_{k}=4^{-\alpha k}
. We initially choose
m_{0}=-1/2
and
M_{0}=1/2
for the case
k=0
. Let us assume that we have sequences up to
m_{k}
and
M_{k}
and find
m_{k+1}
and
M_{k+1}
.

For
x\in B_{2R_{k+1}}
, let
Q_{1}
be a dyadic cube of generation
k_{R_{k+1}/7}
. In
Q_{1}
, either
u>(M_{k}+m_{k})/2
or
u\leq(M_{k}+m_{k})/2
in at least half of the points in measure. We assume

(10.1)

|\{u>(M_{k}+m_{k})/2\}\cap Q_{1}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|Q_{1}|.

A function defined by

v(x):=\frac{u(x)-m_{k}}{(M_{k}-m_{k})/2}

satisfies
v\geq 0
in
B_{k}
by the induction hypothesis. To apply
Lemma

8.2
, let us consider a function
w:=v^{+}
, which satisfies

(10.2)

|\{w>1\}\cap Q_{1}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|Q_{1}|

by (
10.1
). Since
\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{-}v\leq 2\varepsilon_{0}/(M_{k}-m_{k})
in
B_{7R}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\mathcal{M}^{-}w

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}(\mathcal{M}^{-}v+\mathcal{M}^{+}v^{-})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\varepsilon_{0}}{M_{k}-m_{k}}\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}}+\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\mathcal{M}^{+}v^{-}

in
B_{3R_{k+1}}
. By
Lemma

5.2
, we have

\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}{(7R)^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{R_{k+1}}{7R}\right)^{\gamma}=4^{-(k+1)\gamma}<4^{-k\gamma_{0}}.

Thus, we obtain

\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\mathcal{M}^{-}w\leq 2\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\mathcal{M}^{+}v^{-},

by assuming
\alpha<\gamma_{0}
.

For
\mathcal{M}^{+}v^{-}
, we use an inequality
v(z)\geq-2((d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,0)/R_{k})^{\alpha}-1)
,
z\in{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B_{k}
, which follows from the definition of
v
and the properties of sequences
M_{k}
and
m_{k}
. Then, for any
x_{0}\in B_{3R_{k+1}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}v^{-}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B_{k}}v^{-}(z)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x_{0},z))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Lambda\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B_{k}}\left(\left(\frac{d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,0)}{R_{k}}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x_{0},z))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

Since
d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,0)\leq 4d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{0})
, we obtain

(10.3)

\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\mathcal{M}^{+}v^{-}\leq 2\Lambda\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\int_{{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\setminus B(x_{0},R_{k+1})}\left(\left(\frac{d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(z,x_{0})}{R_{k+1}}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}(d_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}(x_{0},z))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

Let
I
be the right-hand side of (
10.3
). By the dominated convergence theorem, we know that
I
converges to 0 as
\alpha\to 0
for each
\gamma
. Let
\alpha_{\gamma}>0
be the constant such that
I\leq\varepsilon_{0}
whenever
\alpha\leq\alpha_{\gamma}
. Since
I
is continuous with respect to
\alpha
and
\gamma
,
\alpha_{\gamma}
is chosen continuously. Thus, the quantity
\min_{\gamma\in[\gamma_{0},1]}\alpha_{\gamma}
is positive and depends on
\gamma_{0}
(not on
\gamma
). By choosing
\alpha=\min_{\gamma\in[\gamma_{0},1]}\alpha_{\gamma}
, we obtain

\frac{R_{k+1}^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(R_{k+1})}\mathcal{M}^{-}w\leq 3\varepsilon_{0}

in
B(x,7(R_{k+1}/7))
for
x\in B_{2R_{k+1}}
. Therefore, by
Lemma

8.2
and (
10.2
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|Q_{1}|\leq|\{w>1\}\cap Q_{1}|\leq C|Q_{1}|\left(w(x)+3\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{\varepsilon},

or equivalently,
\theta\leq w(x)+3\varepsilon_{0}
for some universal constant
\theta>0
. By taking
\varepsilon_{0}<\theta/6
, we arrive at
w\geq\theta/2
in
B_{2R_{k+1}}
. Thus, if we set
M_{k+1}=M_{k}
and
m_{k+1}=M_{k}-4^{-\alpha(k+1)}
, then

M_{k+1}\geq u\geq m_{k}+\frac{M_{k}-m_{k}}{4}\theta=M_{k}-\left(1-\frac{\theta}{4}\right)4^{-\alpha k}\geq m_{k+1}

in
B_{k+1}
.

When (
10.1
) does not hold, a similar proof can be made by using
\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{+}u\geq-\varepsilon_{0}
instead of
\frac{(7R)^{2}}{\mathcal{I}_{0}(7R)}\mathcal{M}^{-}u\leq\varepsilon_{0}
.
∎

Appendix A
Special functions

The equation

\rho^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}y}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}+\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}\rho}-(\rho^{2}+\nu^{2})y=0

is called the modified Bessel’s equation, and its solutions are given by

I_{\nu}(\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!\Gamma(\nu+j+1)}\left(\frac{\rho}{2}\right)^{2j+\nu}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{\nu}(\rho)=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{I_{-\nu}(\rho)-I_{\nu}(\rho)}{\sin\nu\pi}.

They are called modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind, respectively. They satisfy the recurrence relations

(A.1)

K_{\nu+1}-K_{\nu-1}=\frac{2\nu}{R}K_{\nu},\quad I_{\nu-1}-I_{\nu+1}=\frac{2\nu}{R}I_{\nu},

and the following system of first-order differential equations:

(A.2)

\begin{cases}I_{\nu}^{\prime}&=I_{\nu-1}-\frac{\nu}{R}I_{\nu},\\
I_{\nu}^{\prime}&=I_{\nu+1}+\frac{\nu}{R}I_{\nu},\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{cases}K_{\nu}^{\prime}&=-K_{\nu-1}-\frac{\nu}{R}K_{\nu},\\
K_{\nu}^{\prime}&=-K_{\nu+1}+\frac{\nu}{R}K_{\nu}.\end{cases}

Using (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we compute the indefinite integrals

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{\nu}^{k}(\rho)&=\int\rho^{k-\nu}K_{-\nu}(\rho)\sinh\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho,\\
C_{\nu}^{k}(\rho)&=\int\rho^{k-\nu}K_{-\nu}(\rho)\cosh\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho,\end{split}

for
k=0,1,\cdots
, and
\nu\neq k+1/2
, and

(A.4)

L_{\nu}^{k}(\rho)=\int\rho^{k-\nu}K_{-\nu}(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho,

for
k=0,2,4,\cdots
, and
\nu\neq k+1/2
.

Lemma A.1
.

For
k=0,1,\cdots
, and for
\nu\neq k+1/2
, the indefinite integrals
S_{\nu}^{k}
and
C_{\nu}^{k}
are given by

(A.5)

S_{\nu}^{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j}k!/(k-j)!\rho^{k+3/2-\nu}}{(k+1-2\nu)\cdots(k+1+j-2\nu)}\left(K_{\nu-j}I_{(-1)^{j}/2}+K_{\nu-j-1}I_{(-1)^{j+1}/2}\right)

and

(A.6)

C_{\nu}^{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{j}k!/(k-j)!\rho^{k+3/2-\nu}}{(k+1-2\nu)\cdots(k+1+j-2\nu)}\left(K_{\nu-j}I_{(-1)^{j+1}/2}+K_{\nu-j-1}I_{(-1)^{j}/2}\right),

respectively.

Proof.

We know from
[
29
, 10.43.3 and 10.39.1]
that (
A.5
) and (
A.6
) hold true for
k=0
. For
k\geq 1
, we use
S_{\nu}^{0}
to see that

\displaystyle S_{\nu}^{k}

\displaystyle=\int\rho^{k}\partial_{\rho}S_{\nu}^{0}\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\rho^{k}S_{\nu}^{0}-k\int\rho^{k-1}\frac{\rho^{1-\nu}}{1-2\nu}(K_{\nu}\sinh\rho+K_{\nu-1}\cosh\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho

\displaystyle=\rho^{k}S^{0}_{\nu}-\frac{k}{1-2\nu}S_{\nu}^{k}-\frac{k}{1-2\nu}C_{\nu-1}^{k-1},

which yields

(A.7)

S_{\nu}^{k}=-\frac{k}{k+1-2\nu}C_{\nu-1}^{k-1}+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\rho^{k+3/2-\nu}}{k+1-2\nu}\left(K_{\nu}I_{1/2}+K_{\nu-1}I_{-1/2}\right).

Similarly, by using
C_{\nu}^{0}
we obtain

(A.8)

C_{\nu}^{k}=-\frac{k}{k+1-2\nu}S_{\nu-1}^{k-1}+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\rho^{k+3/2-\nu}}{k+1-2\nu}\left(K_{\nu}I_{-1/2}+K_{\nu-1}I_{1/2}\right).

The lemma follows by solving the recurrence relations (
A.7
) and (
A.8
).
∎

For
L_{\nu}^{k}
, we need the modified Struve functions. The functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{\Gamma(j+3/2)\Gamma(\nu+j+3/2)}\left(\frac{\rho}{2}\right)^{2j+\nu+1}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(j+3/2)\Gamma(\nu+j+3/2)}\left(\frac{\rho}{2}\right)^{2j+\nu+1}

are called the modified Struve functions and solve the modified Struve’s equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}y}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{2}}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}\rho}-\left(1+\frac{\nu^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)y=\frac{(\rho/2)^{\nu-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\nu+1/2)}.

Lemma A.2
.

For
k=0,1,\cdots
, and for
\nu\neq k+1/2
, the indefinite integral
L_{\nu}^{2k}
is given by

\displaystyle L^{2k}_{\nu}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{(2k)!(k-j)!}{2^{j}k!(2k-2j)!}\rho^{2k-j-\nu}K_{-\nu+1+j}

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(1/2-\nu+k)}{2^{\nu+1-k}}\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}\rho\left(K_{-\nu+k}{\bf L}_{-\nu+k-1}+K_{-\nu+k-1}{\bf L}_{-\nu+k}\right).

Proof.

By using the integration by parts, we obtain the recurrence relation

(A.9)

L_{\nu}^{2k}=-\int\rho^{2k-1}\partial_{\rho}(\rho^{1-\nu}K_{1-\nu})\,\mathrm{d}\rho=-\rho^{2k-\nu}K_{1-\nu}+(2k-1)L_{\nu-1}^{2k-2}.

Therefore, the lemma follows from (
A.9
) and

(A.10)

L^{0}_{\nu}=\int\rho^{-\nu}K_{-\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}2^{-\nu-1}\Gamma(1/2-\nu)\rho\left(K_{-\nu}{\bf L}_{-\nu-1}+K_{-\nu-1}{\bf L}_{-\nu}\right).

For (
A.10
), see, e.g.,
[
29
, 10.43.2]
.
∎

Let us close the section by collecting some asymptotic behavior of the modified Bessel functions and modified Struve functions.

Lemma A.3
.

The asymptotic behavior of the modified Bessel functions are given by

\displaystyle I_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\left(\frac{\rho}{2}\right)^{\nu},\quad\nu\neq-1,-2,\cdots,

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(\nu)\left(\frac{\rho}{2}\right)^{-\nu},\quad\mathrm{Re}\,\nu>0,

as
\rho\to 0
, and

\displaystyle I_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{e^{\rho}}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho}},

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\rho}}e^{-\rho},

as
\rho\to\infty
. The asymptotic behavior of the modified Struve function
\mathbf{L}_{\nu}
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf L}_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(\rho/2)^{\nu+1}}{\Gamma(3/2)\Gamma(3/2+\nu)}\quad\text{as}~\rho\to 0,~\text{and}

\displaystyle{\bf L}_{\nu}(\rho)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{e^{s}}{\sqrt{2\pi s}}\quad\text{as}~\rho\to\infty.

[
29
]
