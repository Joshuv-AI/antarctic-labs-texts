The DeCAMFounder: Non-Linear Causal Discovery in the Presence of Hidden Variables
Raj Agrawal, Chandler Squires, Neha Prasad, Caroline Uhler

Abstract
Many real-world decision-making tasks require learning causal relationships between a set of variables. Traditional causal discovery methods, however, require that all variables are observed, which is often not feasible in practical scenarios. Without additional assumptions about the unobserved variables, it is not possible to recover any causal relationships from observational data. Fortunately, in many applied settings, additional structure among the confounders can be expected. In particular, pervasive confounding is commonly encountered and has been utilized for consistent causal estimation in linear causal models. In this paper, we present a provably consistent method to estimate causal relationships in the non-linear, pervasive confounding setting. The core of our procedure relies on the ability to estimate the confounding variation through a simple spectral decomposition of the observed data matrix. We derive a DAG score function based on this insight, prove its consistency in recovering a correct ordering of the DAG, and empirically compare it to previous approaches. We demonstrate improved performance on both simulated and real datasets by explicitly accounting for both confounders and non-linear effects.

Introduction

Many decision-making and scientific tasks require learning causal relationships between observed variables.
For example, biologists seek to identify causal pathways in gene-regulatory networks, epidemiologists search for the causes of diseases in complex social networks, and data scientists assess the biases of their machine-learning models by investigating their causal structure
(
Spirtes et al. 2000
;
Friedman et al. 2000
;
Pearl 2009
;
Robins et al. 2000
;
Kusner et al. 2017
)
.
However, estimating causal effects can be challenging when the underlying models lack sufficient flexibility. For example, an individual’s health may generally improve with increasing exercise, but excessive exercise might lead to a decline. Additionally, unobserved confounders can pose difficulties in recovering causal relationships as they induce spurious correlations between observed variables. To accurately estimate causal relationships, it is essential to develop methods that account for both non-linear effects and unobserved confounders. Unfortunately, existing methods often fall short in at least one of these two aspects.

Some existing causal discovery methods assume linear effects and no unobserved confounders
(
Chickering 2002
;
Solus et al. 2020
;
Kalisch and Bühlmann 2007
)
.
More recent works focus on recovering causal relationships when each variable is a non-linear function of its direct causes plus additive noise. However, these methods still assume the absence of unobserved confounders
(
Hoyer et al. 2009
;
Mooij et al. 2009
;
Peters et al. 2014
;
Bühlmann et al. 2014
)
.
In seminal work,
Richardson and Spirtes 2002
introduced ancestral graphical models to account for unobserved confounding.
Several methods have been proposed to estimate these models
(
Colombo et al. 2012
;
Bernstein et al. 2020
)
. However, when confounders have an effect on many observed variables (referred to as
“pervasive”
confounding), ancestral graphs exhibit poor identifiability compared to methods that explicitly model pervasive confounders
(
Frot et al. 2019
)
. Since pervasive confounding arises in numerous economic and biological applications, it is crucial to develop methods that exploit this additional structural information. Unfortunately, existing methods that provably recover the true causal structure under pervasive confounding assume linear causal effects
(
Frot et al. 2019
;
Shah et al. 2020
)
.

In this paper, we consider causal discovery in the non-linear additive noise and pervasive confounding setting. We show that the true graph is still recoverable (
1
) and we provide a method which asymptotically recovers the correct partial ordering of the graph in
Section

3.3
.
Our approach relies on estimating the confounding variation through a simple spectral decomposition of the observed data matrix.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: In
Eq.

2
, we formalize the target of inference and the assumptions about the data generating process. We propose our method in
Section

3
and present related work in
Section

4
. Finally, we compare our method against existing approaches on real and synthetic datasets in
Section

5
. In
Appendix

A
, we summarize the main notations used.

Problem Statement: Causal Discovery in the Presence of Confounding

Preliminaries.
We would like to recover the causal relationships between the components of a
p
-dimensional random vector
X\sim\mathbb{P}(X)
.
We are given
N
datapoints
\{x^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, sampled i.i.d. from
\mathbb{P}(X)
.
The aim is to understand causal dependencies, such as whether one variable (e.g., medical treatment) is a cause of another variable (e.g., health outcome), and how this effect is mediated by other variables (e.g., gene-expression levels).
We use a
causal directed acyclic graph
(DAG) to model causal relationships, where directed edges capture cause-and-effect relationships
(
Spirtes et al. 2000
)
.
In this context, we consider a DAG
\mathcal{G}
with nodes
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{p}
, where each node
X_{i}
is associated to the random variable
X_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,p
, and the distinction between random variables and nodes is made obvious from context
(
Koller and Friedman 2009
)
.
We say
\mathbb{P}(X)

factorizes
according to a DAG
\mathcal{G}
if we have
\mathbb{P}(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\mid X_{\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{i}}),
where
X_{\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{i}}
denotes the variables which are
parents
of the variables
X_{i}
in
\mathcal{G}
.

Unfortunately, in the presence of unobserved confounders, there generally does not exist a DAG over observed variables that preserves, in its factorization, all the conditional and marginal independences that we expect to hold in the margin of the full DAG over the observed and unobserved variables (i.e., DAGs are not closed under marginalization)
(
Richardson and Spirtes 2002
)
.
This factorization or
Markov
property is crucial for many causal learning methods to accurately recover causal relationships
(
Chickering 2002
;
Kalisch and Bühlmann 2007
;
Solus et al. 2020
;
Peters et al. 2014
)
.

To address this limitation, instead of assuming
\mathbb{P}(X)
factorizes according to a DAG, we assume the joint distribution over the complete set of variables
(X,\bar{H})
is Markov with respect to a DAG
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
, where
\bar{H}
represents a vector of
\bar{K}\in\mathbb{N}
unobserved variables. This assumption implies that, in the structural causal model (SCM) associated with
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
, each variable can be expressed as a function of its parents in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
and independent noise.

Reduction to Exogenous Confounders.
Since
\bar{H}
is unobserved, recovering
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
from observational data alone is not possible without further assumptions about
\bar{H}

(
Richardson and Spirtes 2002
)
.
This work instead focuses on learning the
“canonical exogenous DAG”
induced by
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
using the concepts of
latent projection
and the

introduced in
Evans 2016

The canonical exogenous DAG transforms
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
into another DAG
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
over the same set of observed variables
X

H

In the canonical exogenous DAG, an edge between two observed nodes corresponds to the existence of a

in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
, while edges from a hidden node to a set of observed nodes correspond to the existence of a
hidden common cause
for those nodes in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}

These structures are defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

X_{i}
has a
completely hidden path
to
X_{j}
in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
if there exists a path
X_{i}\to\bar{H}_{k_{1}}\to\ldots\to\bar{H}_{k_{m}}\to X_{j}
in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
, where
1\leq i,j\leq p
, and
1\leq k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\leq\bar{K}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
X_{C}=\{X_{c}:c\in C\}
, where
C\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\}
.

X_{C}
shares a
hidden common cause
in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
if there exists an unobserved node
\bar{H}_{j},1\leq j\leq\bar{K}
, such that for each
c\in C
there is a directed path from
\bar{H}_{j}
to
X_{c}
with all vertices in
\{\bar{H}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\bar{K}}
.
We denote the collection of all maximal sets
C
such that
X_{C}
share a hidden common cause in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
by
\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{K}\}
for
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

We define the canonical exogenous DAG such that, in the terminology of
Evans 2016
, it is the canonical DAG associated with the latent projection of
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
onto the observed nodes.
We prove this equivalence in
Section

B.1
.

Definition 3
.

(canonical exogenous DAG)
The
canonical exogenous DAG
of
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
is a DAG
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
with: (1) an edge
X_{i}\to X_{j}
if
X_{i}
has a completely hidden path to
X_{j}
in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
,
1\leq i,j\leq p
, and (2) a node
H_{k}
and edges
H_{k}\to X_{c}
for all
X_{c}\in X_{C_{k}}
,
1\leq k\leq K
.

By
3
, the presence of an edge between two observed nodes
X_{i}\to X_{j}
,
1\leq i,j\leq p
, in the canonical exogenous DAG implies that
X_{i}
is a cause and not an effect of
X_{j}
.
Hence, the canonical exogenous DAG preserves the partial ordering of the observed nodes in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
.
When
\bar{K}=1
, the canonical exogenous DAG equals the DAG formed by first adding edges from each of the parents of
\bar{H}_{1}
to each of the children of
\bar{H}_{1}
in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
, second removing all directed edges pointing into
\bar{H}_{1}
, and third removing
\bar{H}_{1}
if it has only one child.
See
Fig.

1

If the unobserved variables are all sources in
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
to begin with (as assumed in
Frot et al. 2019
;
Wang and Blei 2019
), then the canonical exogenous DAG equals
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
, with the only exception being the removal of any unobserved variables with one or zero children.

Target of Inference.

Evans 2016
proves that there exists a distribution
\mathbb{Q}(X,H)
such that
\mathbb{Q}(X,H)
is Markov with respect to the canonical exogenous DAG, and the marginal distribution
\mathbb{Q}(X)
is equal to
\mathbb{P}(X)
almost everywhere.
Since we cannot distinguish between the distributions
\mathbb{P}(X,\bar{H})
and
\mathbb{Q}(X,H)
from observational data, we treat
\mathbb{Q}(X,H)
as the canonical representation of the joint distribution over observed variables and confounders.
Hence, we make technical assumptions about
\mathbb{Q}(X,H)
instead of
\mathbb{P}(X,\bar{H})
to prove when causal recovery is possible.

Let
\mathcal{G}^{*}
denote the DAG formed by removing the nodes
\{H_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and all arrows pointing out of
\{H_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
from the canonical exogenous DAG (see, for example,
Fig.

1(b)
).
Since
H
is exogenous in the canonical exogenous DAG,
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is the DAG associated with the conditional distribution
\mathbb{Q}(X\mid H)
.
Hence,
\mathcal{G}^{*}
captures the causal relationships among the observed variables.
Our goal is to somehow learn
\mathcal{G}^{*}
from
N
observational datapoints
\{x^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
without observing the hidden confounders
\bar{H}
.

Model.
The SCM associated with
\mathcal{G}^{*}
equals

\begin{split}X_{j}&=f_{j}(X_{\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}},H,\epsilon_{j}),\quad 1\leq j\leq p,\end{split}

where
\epsilon_{j}
is the noise term
,
H\sim\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{Q}(H_{k})
, and
H\perp\!\!\!\perp\epsilon
.
Unfortunately, due to the curse of dimensionality, the number of datapoints required to estimate
f_{j}
to some level of precision depends exponentially on the number of parents
(
Gyorfi et al. 2003
)
.
Given this statistical hardness result, we assume that each
f_{j}
has low-dimensional structure.

Causal additive models (CAMs) are widely used to introduce low-dimensional structure while balancing flexibility and statistical efficiency
(
Bühlmann et al. 2014
)
.
In a CAM, it is assumed that each node can be expressed as an additive function of its parents plus independent noise. In this paper, we adopt this assumption and consider the following model: for all
1\leq j\leq p
,

\begin{split}X_{j}&=\sum_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j}}f_{ij}(X_{i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{kj}(H_{k})+\epsilon_{j}\\
&=\sum_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j}}f_{ij}(X_{i})+D_{j}+\epsilon_{j},\quad D_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{kj}(H_{k}),\end{split}

(1)

where
f_{ij}
and
g_{kj}
are unknown functions,
f_{ij}
does not equal zero almost everywhere, and
\epsilon_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{j})

for some unknown noise variances
0<\sigma_{j}^{2}<\infty
,
1\leq j\leq p
.
Without further constraints,
Eq.

1
is not identifiable.
To ensure the uniqueness of the expansion in
Eq.

1
as described in
Bühlmann et al. 2014
, we assume
\mathbb{E}[f_{ij}(X_{i})]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[g_{kj}(H_{k})]=0
.

Directed Gaussian graphical models are a specific instance of the SCM described in
Eq.

1
, where all the unknown functions are assumed to be linear. In this particular case,
Eq.

1
can be simplified to a more commonly recognized form:

X=BX+\Theta H+\epsilon\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\epsilon\sim N(0,\Lambda),\ H\sim N(0,I_{K}),

where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times K}
consist of
unknown
edge weights,
B
is upper triangular,
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix such that
\Lambda_{jj}=\sigma^{2}_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq p
, and
I_{K}
is the
K\times K
identity matrix.
Solving for
X
in the equation above,

X=(I_{p}-B)^{-1}\epsilon+(I_{p}-B)^{-1}\Theta H.

(2)

3
Our Method

Our objective is to learn
\mathcal{G}^{*}
, the DAG induced by the conditional distribution
\mathbb{Q}(X\mid H)
under the model in
Eq.

1
, where
\mathbb{Q}(X,H)
is faithful to the canonical exogenous DAG.
To achieve this, we propose a three-part solution.
First, we show in
Section

3.1
that there exists a
p
-dimensional vector of sufficient statistics
S
of
H
such that
\mathbb{Q}(X\mid S)
factorizes according to
\mathcal{G}^{*}
.
Next, in Section
3.2
, we provide a method to accurately estimate
S
when
H
has an impact on many observed variables. This estimation is achieved through principal components analysis applied to the observed data.
Finally, we introduce a novel DAG score function that utilizes these estimated values of
S
. We establish in
Section

3.3
that this score function consistently recovers the partial order of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
.

Throughout the paper, we adopt the assumption, without loss of generality, that
\pi^{*}\coloneqq(1\cdots p)
represents a consistent topological ordering of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
to simplify notation. This ordering ensures that variable
X_{i}
can only serve as a cause, not an effect, of variable
X_{j}
when
i<j
.

3.1
Sufficient Statistics for Recovering the Exogenized DAG

Our main theorem below, which we prove in

\mathcal{G}^{*}
when

\mathbb{E}[X\mid H]

Theorem 1
.

There exist functions

such that the SCM in

1

\begin{split}X_{j}&=\sum_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j}}f_{ij}(X_{i})+[S_{j}-r_{j}(S_{1},\ldots,S_{j-1})]+\epsilon_{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq p,\end{split}

where

Since
\epsilon_{j}\perp\!\!\!\perp\{(X_{i},S_{i})\}_{i=1}^{j-1}\cup\{S_{j}\}
, the SCM in
1
is an additive noise model. In the context of additive noise models,
Peters et al. 2014
discuss settings when
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is identifiable, i.e., settings for which all edges in the graph can be learned and oriented correctly with an infinite amount of observational data.
They demonstrate cases where
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is fully identifiable, and situations where
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is identifiable only up to its Markov equivalence class. An example of such limited identifiability occurs when the

functions are linear and the errors follow a Gaussian distribution.
For the sake of clarity, we highlight a specific instance in which
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is fully identified, which can be directly derived from Corollary 31 of
Peters et al. 2014

Peters et al. 2014
for a comprehensive exploration of identifiability results in additive noise models.

Corollary 1
.

\mathcal{G}^{*}
is identifiable from the conditional distribution
\mathbb{Q}(X\mid S)
when all functions in
\{f_{ij}\}_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j},j\in[p]}
are non-linear and three-times differentiable, and when the errors
\{\epsilon_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
are Gaussian.

Based on the above discussion, identifying
\mathcal{G}^{*}
(or recovering its Markov equivalence class) requires computing or estimating

Since we do not know

In the next section, we show how to estimate

3.2
Asymptotically Exact Estimates of the Sufficient Statistics

To estimate
\{s^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, we reduce our problem into the linear latent factor model proposed in
Fan et al. 2013
.

Reduction to
Fan et al. 2013
.
Let
\mu^{*}
denote the joint distribution of
(\epsilon,H)
.
Assume that each random variable
X_{j}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}\subset\L^{2}(\mu^{*})
, where
\mathcal{H}^{*}
is a complete Hilbert space and
\L^{2}(\mu^{*})
consists of all square-integrable functions of
(\epsilon,H)
with respect to the measure
\mu^{*}
.
Then, there exists a set of
M
basis functions
\{\phi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
,
1\leq M\leq\infty
, such that
S_{j}=\mathbb{E}[X_{j}\mid H]\in\mathcal{H}_{M}\coloneqq\text{span}\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{M}\}
since
\mathcal{H}^{*}
is separable
(
Rudin 1974
)
.
Hence, there exists a vector of basis coefficients
\psi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
such that
S_{j}=\psi_{j}^{T}\Phi(H)
for
1\leq j\leq p
, where
\Phi(h)\coloneqq[\phi_{1}(h),\ldots,\phi_{M}(h)]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
Through this basis expansion, we can re-express the observed random vector
X
as,

\begin{split}X&=\mathbb{E}[X\mid H]+[X-\mathbb{E}[S\mid H]]\\
&=S+[X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid H]]\\
&=\Psi\Phi(H)+U,\end{split}

(4)

where
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times M}
, the
j
th row of
\Psi
equals
\psi_{j}^{T}
,
1\leq j\leq p
, and
U=X-\Psi\Phi(H)
.
By properties of conditional expectation,

belongs to the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{H}_{M}
.
As a result, the covariance between each component of
\Psi\Phi(H)
and

4

Fan et al. 2013

Hence, we use the general purpose procedure developed in
Fan et al. 2013

Derivation of the Estimator.
Let
\mathbb{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}

\mathbb{X}

By

4

\begin{split}&\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{S}+\mathbb{U}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{X}=[x^{(1)}\cdots x^{(N)}]^{T},\quad\mathbb{S}=[s^{(1)}\cdots s^{(N)}]^{T},\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \mathbb{U}=[u^{(1)}\cdots u^{(N)}]^{T},\end{split}

where each

is sampled i.i.d. from

, and

.
To estimate
\mathbb{S}
from only the observed data matrix

In particular, let

and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
consist of eigenvectors and eigenvalues (in decreasing order) from the eigendecomposition of the sample covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}

For
J\in\mathbb{N}
, let
V_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times J}
denote the matrix consisting of the

Then we estimate
\mathbb{S}

\hat{\mathbb{S}}=(V_{J}V_{J}^{T}\mathbb{X}^{T})^{T}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\hat{\Sigma}:=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{X}^{T}\mathbb{X}=V\Lambda V^{T},

(5)

Below we prove when

5

\mathbb{S}
, and describe how to pick

Theoretical Guarantees.
In order for

\mathbb{S}
well, we need the singular values of
\mathbb{S}
to be much larger than that of
\mathbb{U}

If the singular values of
\mathbb{S}

is captured by
\mathbb{S}
.
Hence, we might expect that the PCA estimator in

\mathbb{S}

Assumption 1
.

There exists a constant
M<\infty
such that
for all
1\leq j\leq p

S_{j}\in\mathcal{H}_{M}

Assumption 2
.

Let
\lambda_{\min}(A)

We assume that there exists constants

Derivation of the DeCAMFounder Score Function.
We propose to estimate
\mathcal{G}^{*}
via a
score-based
approach.
In general, score-based approaches consist of two parts: (1) a score-function to measure the quality of a DAG based on the observed data
\mathbb{X}
, and (2) a combinatorial optimization procedure to optimize this score-function over the space of DAGs.
Since there are many existing general-purpose procedures for (2), such as greedy equivalence search, we focus our attention on (1).

A natural starting point (based on its popularity in the linear setting) might be to use the
Bayesian information criterion
(BIC) as the score function, which penalizes DAGs based on the number of parameters
(
Chickering 2002
)
.
For linear models, computing this penalty is straightforward because the number of parameters simply equals the number of edges in the graph (corresponding to the number of edge weights to be estimated) and
p
(the number of node noise variances to be estimated).
For
semi-parametric
models, however, the number of parameters can be infinite.
Hence, BIC cannot directly be applied.
Instead, we use a Bayesian score, namely the marginal log-likelihood of the DAG (which the BIC approximates with a second-order Taylor series) conditional on the matrix of confounder sufficient statistics
\mathbb{S}
.
Specifically, let
\mathbb{Q}(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}\mid\mathcal{G})
be a prior over the unknown set of functions
\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\{\{f_{ij}^{\mathcal{G}}\}_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}},r_{j}^{\mathcal{G}}\}_{j=1}^{p}
that define the model from
1

Assume that
\mathbb{Q}(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}\mid\mathcal{G})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{Q}(\{f_{ij}^{\mathcal{G}}\}_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}},r_{j}^{\mathcal{G}}\mid\mathcal{G})

(
Friedman and Nachman 2000
)

Given this prior, we score a DAG

\begin{split}\max_{\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}}\log\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{X}\mid\mathcal{G},\mathbb{S},\sigma^{2})&=\max_{\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}}\log\int_{\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}}\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{X}\mid\mathcal{G},\mathbb{S},\Omega_{\mathcal{G}},\sigma^{2})d\mathbb{Q}(\Omega_{\mathcal{G}}\mid\mathcal{G})\\

4
Related Work

Two main approaches have been proposed for learning causal structures in the presence of confounders. The first approach focuses on modeling the conditional independence structure of
\mathbb{Q}(X)
using an ancestral graph and subsequently recovering the edges in this graph. Examples of algorithms for ancestral graph estimation include FCI
(
Spirtes et al. 2000
)
, RFCI
(
Colombo et al. 2012
)
, and GSPo
(
Bernstein et al. 2020
)
. However, these methods recover fewer causal relationships since they make minimal assumptions about the confounders, as illustrated in Figure 1 of
Frot et al. 2019
.

The second approach makes more assumptions about the confounders, and tries to exploit these structural assumptions for better estimation.
One commonly used assumption is that the confounders are sources in the graph (
“exogenous”
) and have an effect on many observed variables (
“pervasive”
).
For linear Gaussian graphical models with exogenous pervasive confounders, several methods have been proposed to accurately estimate the covariance matrix of the conditional distribution
\mathbb{Q}(X\mid H)
using only data points drawn from
\mathbb{Q}(X)
for the purposes of conditional independence testing
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
;
Fan et al. 2013
;
Frot et al. 2019
;
Shah et al. 2020
)
.
The methods for estimating the covariance matrix associated with
\mathbb{Q}(X\mid H)
, which in the linear Gaussian case is the same for all
H
, fall into two classes: the
optimization approach
and the
spectral approach
.

Optimization Approach: Deconfounding the Precision Matrix.

Frot et al. 2019
decompose
the precision matrix of
(X,H)
into the blocks

[\text{Cov}((X,H))]^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}J_{O}\end{matrix}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&J_{OH}\\
\hline\cr J_{HO}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\begin{matrix}J_{H}\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}.

By standard theory for multivariate Gaussian distributions,
J_{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is the precision matrix associated with the conditional distribution
\mathbb{Q}(X\mid H)
.
The key idea for estimating
J_{O}
is to relate the observed precision matrix
[\text{Cov}(X)]^{-1}
and the target quantity
J_{O}
via Schur complements:

[\text{Cov}(X)]^{-1}=J_{O}-J_{OH}J_{HH}^{-1}J_{HO}.

For sparse DAGs,
J_{O}
is a sparse matrix, and when
K\ll p
,
J_{OH}J_{HH}^{-1}J_{HO}
is low-rank.
Hence, when the confounders are pervasive, it is possible to estimate each component through a low-rank plus sparse matrix decomposition of
[\text{Cov}(X)]^{-1}

(
Frot et al. 2019
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2009
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
)
.

Spectral Approach: Deconfounding the Covariance Matrix.
In contrast to the optimization approach,
Shah et al. 2020
directly estimates the covariance matrix. Under the SCM in
Eq.

2
,

\begin{split}\text{Cov}(X)&=(I-B)^{-1}\Lambda(I-B)^{-T}+(I-B)^{-1}\Theta\Theta^{T}(I-B)^{-T}\\
&=(I-B)^{-1}\Lambda(I-B)^{-T}+\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\Theta}^{T}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\tilde{\Theta}=(I-B)^{-1}\Theta\\
&=\underbrace{J_{O}^{-1}}_{\text{sparse inverse}}+\underbrace{\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\Theta}^{T}}_{\text{low-rank}}.\end{split}

(9)

If the confounders are pervasive, then the eigenvalues of
\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\Theta}^{T}
grow linearly with the dimension
p
, while the eigenvalues of
J_{O}^{-1}
are assumed to grow much slower.
As a result, the spectrum of
\text{Cov}(X)
is dominated by the spectrum of
\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\Theta}^{T}
.
Hence, the top principal components of
\text{Cov}(X)
approximate
\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\Theta}^{T}
well, and consequently provide a way to estimate
J_{O}^{-1}

(
Fan et al. 2013
;
Wang and Fan 2017
)
.
In settings when the gap between the eigenvalues of
J_{O}^{-1}
and
\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\Theta}^{T}
in
Eq.

9
is small,
Shah et al. 2020
propose a modified covariance estimator.
Outside of the graphical models literature, such spectral approaches are similar to the approach proposed by
Wang and Blei 2019
to estimate average treatment effects in the potential outcomes framework.

5.1
Simulated Data

In this set of simulations, we examine the robustness of our method to different data characteristics. To achieve this, we vary the following parameters: the strength of confounding, linear or non-linear SCM, and dimensionality.

Controlling the Strength of Confounding.

Generating non-linear (and even linear) data can be challenging.
For example, without proper normalization, the variance of downstream nodes can explode (e.g., consider a line graph
X_{1}\rightarrow X_{2}\cdots\rightarrow X_{p}
with edge weights larger than 1).
Additionally, if the noise variance is the same across nodes, downstream nodes typically exhibit a higher signal-to-noise ratio than upstream nodes. To mitigate any biases introduced by such artifacts in the data simulation process, we apply the following normalization (note that specific edge cases arise for source nodes or those independent of
H
; refer to
Appendix

E
for further details):

•

Unit variance nodes
:
\text{Var}(X_{j})=1
for all
1\leq j\leq p
.

•

Zero mean nodes
:
\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]=0
for all
1\leq j\leq p
.

•

Fixed signal, confounding, and noise variances
: for all
1\leq j\leq p
:

–

\text{Cov}(X_{j},\epsilon_{j})=\sigma^{2}_{\text{noise}}
,

–

\text{Cov}(\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{kj}(H_{k}))=\sigma_{h}^{2}
, and

–

\text{Cov}(\sum_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j}}f_{ij}(X_{i}))=\sigma^{2}_{\text{signal}}
.

Since all nodes have unit variance,
\sigma_{h}^{2}
equals the fraction of the variation in
X_{j}
explained by
H
, and
\sigma_{\text{signal}}^{2}
equals the fraction of the variation in
X_{j}
explained by its parents.
In our experiments, we fix
\sigma^{2}_{\text{noise}}=0.2
.
In other words, if we could actually observe both
X
and
H
, then the noise in the problem is small.
To assess how the strength of confounding affects DAG recovery, we vary
\sigma_{h}^{2}
.
Note that
\sigma_{\text{signal}}^{2}
is a deterministic function of
\sigma_{h}^{2}
:
\sigma_{\text{signal}}^{2}=\text{Var}(X_{j})-\sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}-\sigma_{h}^{2}=0.8-\sigma_{h}^{2}
. Therefore, varying
\sigma_{h}^{2}
implicitly varies the observed signal variance
\sigma^{2}_{\text{signal}}
.

Linear / Non-Linear SCM.
We randomly draw each
f_{ij}
and
g_{kj}
in
Eq.

1
from the set of linear trends
\{\theta x:\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
, seasonal trends
\{\theta\sin(\pi x):\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
, or quadratic trends
\{\theta x^{2}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
when generating non-linear data.
For data generated from a linear SCM, we assume that each
f_{ij}
and
g_{kj}
belongs strictly to the set of linear trends.

Data Generation.
We randomly draw
\mathcal{G}^{*}
from an Erdös-Rényi random graph model with expected neighborhood size of
5
and consider graphs with number of observed nodes
p\in\{250,500,1000\}
.
As in
Frot et al. 2019
, we assume that each confounder is a direct cause of an observed variables with a
70\%
chance.
Given the graph, we randomly select a trend type for each edge (i.e., the
f_{ij}
and
g_{kj}
in
Eq.

1
) with equal probability.
We then randomly draw a weight
\theta\sim\text{Uniform}([-1,-.25]\cup[.25,1])
and appropriately scale weights of all parents simultaneously to satisfy the normalization constraints above. We finally add
N(0,\sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2})
noise to each node; see
Appendix

E
for more details and references to our
python
code.
Unless otherwise stated, we draw 25 random datasets for each specific configuration of confounding strength and dimensionality.
We consider
K=1
pervasive confounders, which allows us to plot how each node varies as a function of this confounder. See
Appendix

F
for our empirical results for other choices of
K
.

Based on

, our estimate of the confounder sufficient statistics should decrease as
N
and
p
increase. To test this empirically, we keep the ratio of
\frac{p}{N}
fixed at 2, and vary the confounding strength and dimensionality.

plots the maximum mean-squared error, which equals
\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(s_{j}^{(n)}-\hat{s}_{j}^{(n)}\right)^{2}

In the linear case, we can compute
S
analytically as a function of
H
(see

).
However, in the non-linear case, there is no analytical expression for
S
in term of
H
.
Nonetheless, if
X_{j}
only has confounders as parents, then
S_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{kj}(H_{k})
.
Thus, in
Fig.

, the max MSEs for the non-linear SCM case are only with respect to the
S_{j}
associated with nodes that only have confounders as parents.
From
Fig.

, we see that the MSE decreases as
p

5.1.2
Evaluation of the DeCAMFounder Score

We now evaluate how well the DeCAMFounder scores DAGs using noisy estimates of the confounder sufficient statistics.
For this evaluation, we separate the merits of the score function from the selected optimization procedure.
In particular, since many optimization procedures for DAG estimation greedily optimize over parent sets, we focus on the task of effectively scoring parent sets.

Description of the Parent Set Evaluation Tasks.

In greedy procedures, changes to a parent set typically involve a single node addition (adding a node to the current parent set) or deletion (removing a node from the current parent set).
In the presence of latent confounding, methods that do not account for confounders are susceptible to adding spurious edges.
For example, suppose nodes
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
have a common hidden cause but no shared parents in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
for some
1\leq i,j\leq p,i\neq j
.
Then, a method that assumes no latent confounding might add an edge from
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
or vice versa with high probability, even when all the true parents of the node are included in the current parent set.

This observation leads to a natural evaluation procedure: consider the set of all nodes
C\coloneqq\{X_{j}:D_{j}\neq 0,1\leq j\leq p\}
that have direct confounders (
see
Eq.

1
for the definition of
D_{j}
).
Randomly select a node
X_{j^{\prime}}
from
C
and randomly sample
T
nodes
X_{r_{1}},\ldots,X_{r_{T}}
from
C
that are not parents of
X_{j^{\prime}}
. Then, we evaluate the methods in terms of scoring the following
T+1
parent sets:

\begin{split}P_{\text{correct}}&=\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j^{\prime}}\\
P_{t}&=\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j^{\prime}}\cup\{X_{r_{t}}\}\quad\text{for}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1\leq t\leq T}.\\
\end{split}

Again, we might expect that methods that do not correct for confounders score some
P_{t}
higher than
P_{\text{correct}}
due to spurious correlations created by the confounders.
In our evaluation, we pick
T=100
.
We call this evaluation procedure the
Wrong Parent Addition Task
.
We note that this task is similar to the
“CauseEffectPairs”
causality challenge introduced by
Mooij et al. 2016
.

The second parent set evaluation task concerns the node deletion phase.
Here, we expect that linear methods may suffer by potentially removing true parent nodes.
For example, if a parent node has low correlation with its child (e.g., as is the case with our sine and quadratic trends), then that parent might be removed when using a sparsity-inducing score function such as BIC or penalized log-likelihood with an
L_{0}
or
L_{1}
penalty.

Similar to the Wrong Parent Addition task, we first randomly select a node
X_{j^{\prime}}
with at least one observed parent node,
where
1\leq j^{\prime}\leq p
.
For each node in the parent set of
X_{j^{\prime}}
, let

\begin{split}P_{\text{correct}}&=\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j^{\prime}}\\
P_{t}&=\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j^{\prime}}\setminus\{X_{i}\}\quad\text{for}\quad X_{i}\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j^{\prime}}.\\
\end{split}

We would again like to understand if certain score functions favor incorrect
P_{t}
parent sets when the parents have non-linear effects on the target.
We call this evaluation procedure the
Correct Parent Deletion
task.
These two tasks are illustrated in
Fig.

4
.

Benchmark Methods.

We now describe our benchmark scoring methods.
The first three methods are linear, and use the BIC score with different estimates of the covariance matrix.
In particular, the BIC score (under Gaussian errors) is a function of a target node
X_{j}
, parent set
P_{j}\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}\setminus\{j\}
, and estimated covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
1\leq j\leq p
:

\text{BIC}(X_{j},P_{j},\hat{\Sigma})=-\left(\frac{N}{2}\log\left(2\pi\hat{\sigma}_{j\mid P_{j}}^{2}\right)+\frac{N}{2}\right)-.5\log(N)(|P_{j}|+2),

(10)

where the conditional noise variance (based on Schur complements) equals

\hat{\sigma}_{j\mid P_{j}}^{2}=\hat{\Sigma}_{jj}-\hat{\Sigma}_{jP_{j}}\hat{\Sigma}_{P_{j}P_{j}}^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}_{P_{j}j}.

The benchmarks are as follows:

1.

Vanilla-BIC
:
scores each parent set by inputting the sample covariance matrix
\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{X}^{T}\mathbb{X}
into
Eq.

10
.

2.

LRPC+BIC
:
scores each parent using the covariance matrix estimated from a low-rank plus sparse (LRPS) decomposition of the sample precision matrix; see
Frot et al. 2019
. We use the author’s code in
R
to fit this decomposition, and pick the hyperparameters using cross-validation.

3.

PCSS-BIC
:
scores each parent using the covariance matrix
\frac{1}{N}(\mathbb{X}-\mathbb{S})^{T}(\mathbb{X}-\mathbb{S})
, where
\mathbb{S}
is the output from
Algorithm

1
.
We pick one principal component and 3 principal components for the linear and non-linear case, respectively.
This choice was based on visual inspection of the spectrum of the covariance matrix. See the Appendix.

4.

CAM
:
scores each parent via
Eq.

7
but sets
\mathbb{S}_{:,j}
equal to the zero matrix.
This method corresponds to fitting a standard causal additive model without correcting for the confounders.

5.

CAM-OBS
:
scores each parent by computing
Eq.

6
but replacing
\mathbb{S}
with the actual confounders
\mathbb{H}
.
This method corresponds to fitting a standard causal additive model when both
X
and
H
are observed, and is therefore not realizable in practice.

For both parent set tasks, we fix
N=250
and
p=500
but vary the strength of confounding.
For the Correct Parent Deletion task, we exclude the linear trend from the set of trends when generating non-linear data to focus on the particular problem of modeling non-linearities.
Apart from that, the data in both settings are generated as specified at the beginning of this section.

Fig.

5
shows the non-linear SEM results.
The remaining results are shown in
Fig.

G.1
and
Fig.

G.2
. In these figures, the different methods are compared using the following metric:

Prop. Times MLL Wrong
>
MLL True
: out of the
T
incorrect parent sets, what proportion have scores larger than the true parent set. Lower is better here.

Fig.

5
and
Fig.

G.1
show that methods that account for confounders (i.e.,
LRPC+BIC
,
PCSS-BIC
,
CAM-OBS
,
DeCAMFound
), place higher probability on the correct parent set for the Wrong Parent Addition task across different settings relative to those that do not model the confounders.
In addition,
Fig.

5
and
Fig.

G.2
show that for the Correct Parent Deletion task, the linear methods suffer in the non-linear setting.
Note that unlike
PCSS-BIC
,
LRPC+BIC
suffers even in the linear setting because it induces a very sparse graph, causing it to delete true parents.

Including
CAM-OBS
in our evaluation allows us to understand how parent set recovery is affected by estimation error from only having a finite amount of observational data.
Interestingly, our method, which does not require knowing
H
, sometimes outperforms
CAM-OBS
.
This might be because our method leverages all
p
observed nodes to estimate the confounding variation via PCA.

CAM-OBS
, on the other hand, estimates the confounding variation one node at a time by regressing each observed node on
H
.
Finally, all methods suffer when the confounding strength increases since the observed signal necessarily becomes weaker.

5.2
Real Data: Ovarian Cancer Dataset

We use the ovarian cancer RNA-seq dataset from
Frot et al. 2019
for our real-data analysis, which consists of
N=247
human samples and
p=501
genes.
There are 15 total transcription factors (TFs), and the remaining 486 genes interact with at least one of these TFs.
This dataset has a partial ground truth; the 15 TFs can only be causes and not effects of the 486 genes.
The reference network for the 501 variables is obtained using Netbox, a software tool for performing network analysis using biological knowledge and community network-based approaches
(
Cerami et al. 2010
)
.
A caveat, however, is that edges in this network might not have a precise causal interpretation.

One metric used by
Frot et al. 2019
was a count of the number of directed edges from TFs to genes that agree with the edges in NetBox.
The authors stated that confounding can be expected in this dataset due to unobserved transcription factors or batch effects.
We here take a different approach for the evaluation and instead
explicitly
create confounders by design.
In particular, we remove all 15 TFs from the dataset and assume that we only observed the remaining 486 genes.

By removing the TFs, we can evaluate the methods in a similar fashion as the simulated experiments since we know the true values of the TFs.
In the following, we first assess our ability to estimate the confounder sufficient statistics.
Suppose that an observed gene
X_{j}
is strongly correlated with one of the 15 latent TFs
T_{k}
, for some
1\leq j\leq 486
and
1\leq k\leq 15
.
Then,
\mathbb{E}[X_{j}\mid T_{k}]\approx\mathbb{E}[X_{j}\mid H]=S_{j}
.
Since we know
T_{k}
, we can produce a similar plot as in
Fig.

3
.
To this end, we look at NFKB1, which is a transcription factor known to be associated with ovarian cancer
(
Harrington and Annunziata 2019
)
.
In
Fig.

6
, we look at the highest positive and negatively correlated genes with NFKB1, which are BIRC3 and SMARCE1 respectively.
We see that the estimated confounding variation for each gene estimated from PCSS correlates well with the unobserved transcription factor NFKB1.
This strong correlation suggests that PCSS estimates the variation explained by the unobserved confounders well in this dataset.

5.2.2
Parent Recovery Performance

Unlike in the simulated dataset experiments, we do not know the true causal graph.
Thus, for the Wrong Parent Addition task and the Correct Parent Deletion task, we use the graph structure outputted by NetBox, as in
Frot et al. 2019
.
In particular, for a node
X_{j^{\prime}}
, we let
P_{\text{correct}}
be the neighbors of
X_{j^{\prime}}
in the graph outputted by NetBox.
For the Wrong Parent Addition task, we require a set of “spurious neighbors” of
X_{j^{\prime}}
.
For this set, we take all variables
X_{r^{\prime}}
such that
X_{j^{\prime}}
and
X_{r^{\prime}}
are conditionally independent given
P_{t}
and the TFs, but are conditionally dependent given only
P_{\text{correct}}
.
Then, we let
P_{t}=P_{\text{correct}}\cup\{X_{r}\}
for
X_{r}
picked randomly from this set.

We focus on gene pairs
X_{j^{\prime}}
,
X_{r^{\prime}}
with at least one strong TF confounder.
In particular, we require that each gene has correlation greater than 0.4 with one of the 15 removed TFs.
This choice makes it more likely that one of the 15 left-out TFs is the source of confounding, rather than some other unobserved confounder which we have no control over.
This choice results in roughly 1000 parent sets to score.
One such gene pair with a strong TF confounder is illustrated in
Fig.

7
.
We see that subtracting the confounder sufficient statistics from each gene results in removing the confounding effect of the strongest transcription factor NFKB1.

For the Correct Parent Deletion task, we randomly sample 500 edges from the NetBox undirected graph.
For each edge we randomly select one endpoint as
X_{j^{\prime}}
and define
P_{t}=P_{\text{correct}}\setminus\{X_{r}\}
for the other endpoint
X_{r}
.

The results for both tasks are summarized in
Fig.

8
.
LRPS produces a very sparse graph, and hence almost never adds a wrong parent.
However, it almost always removes true edges, which comes at the expense of statistical power.
Our method has the highest power per unit of false positives.
Rather surprisingly,
CAM-OBS
, which explicitly uses the 15 held out transcription factors, selects wrong parents more often than our method.
This might be the result of having additional (pervasive) confounders beyond the 15 TFs we introduced by design, as raised by
Frot et al. 2019
.

Conclusion.

We
have shown how to
identify the causal graph among the observed nodes in the setting of non-linear effects and pervasive confounders. We proposed the DeCAMFounder, which consistently estimates
a correct ordering
of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
using Gaussian processes. Since the DeCAMFounder explicitly accounts for confounders and non-linear effects, we found improved performance on both simulated and real datasets relative to existing methods. An interesting
future
direction is handling selection bias. In
Frot et al. 2019
, for example, the authors showed how to account for selection bias and pervasive confounders in the linear setting. It would be interesting to explore whether a similar idea could be used to extend the DeCAMFounder to handle selection bias.

Appendix B
Proofs

B.1
The canonical exogenous DAG

We provide a brief argument to verify that our direct definition of the canonical exogenous DAG is equal to the canonical DAG of the latent projection defined by
Evans 2016
.
Our direct definition allows us to avoid the concepts of simplicial complexes and facets that are necessary to define the latent projection; see
Evans 2016
for details.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
be a DAG over observed nodes
X
and unobserved nodes
\bar{H}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{1}
be the canonical exogenous DAG for
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
be the latent projection of
\mathcal{G}
onto
X
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{2}
be the canonical DAG associated with
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.
Then
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

Proof.

By definition of the latent projection,
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
contains
X_{i}\to X_{j}
if and only if
X_{i}
has a completely hidden path to
X_{j}
.
Furthermore, by definition of the canonical DAG,
\mathcal{G}_{2}
contains an edge
X_{i}\to X_{j}
if and only if
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
contains the edge
X_{i}\to X_{j}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
have the same set of edges over
X
.

Next,
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
contains the bidirected face

\beta>0
controls the smoothness of functions in
\mathcal{H}^{*}
.
Then,
\mathbb{E}|S_{j}-\tilde{S}_{j}|^{2}=O(\tilde{M}^{-\beta+1+\kappa})
for any
\kappa>0
,
1\leq j\leq p
.
Hence, if
\beta
is large (i.e., when the function class is very smooth) or
\tilde{M}
is large (i.e., when there exists many basis functions with diverging eigenvalues), then
S_{j}
is close to
\tilde{S}_{j}
.

C.2
Modified Condition for Consistent Order Estimation

Consider the following (modified) model gap parameter
\tilde{\xi}_{p}
:

\tilde{\xi}_{p}=p^{-1}\min_{\mathcal{G}\notin\Pi_{*}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\log\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{\mathcal{G}}\right)-\log\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{\mathcal{G}^{*}}\right)\right),

(47)

where

\begin{split}&(\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{\mathcal{G}})^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left(X_{j}-\sum_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}}\tilde{f}_{ij}^{\mathcal{G}}(X_{i})-\tilde{S}_{j}+\tilde{r}_{j}^{\mathcal{G}}\left(\tilde{S}_{\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}}\right)\right)^{2}\right]\\
\{\tilde{f}_{ij}^{\mathcal{G}}\}_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}},\tilde{r}_{j}^{\mathcal{G}}=&\argmin_{g_{ij}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i},\ w_{j}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{G}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left(X_{j}-\sum_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}}g_{ij}(X_{i})-\tilde{S}_{j}-w_{j}\left(\tilde{S}_{\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}}\right)\right)^{2}\right].\end{split}

Eq.

47
has an analogous interpretation as
\xi_{p}
.
However,
Eq.

47
assumes that the gap between the negative log-likelihood score of the best incorrect DAG and correct DAG remains non-zero even in the misspecified setting when
\tilde{S}
is used instead of
S
.
As we show in the previous section, if
S_{j}
is smooth, then
S_{j}
is close to
\tilde{S}_{j}
.
If
S_{j}
is close to
\tilde{S}_{j}
, then
Eq.

47
is not much stronger of a condition than assuming
\xi_{p}>0
.
If we replace
S
with
\tilde{S}
in the proof of
3
, and replace
\xi_{p}
with
\tilde{x}_{p}
in
5
, then
3
remains true.

Connection to
Bühlmann et al. 2014
.
In
Bühlmann et al. 2014
, the authors similarly use the gap parameter to handle misspecification, namely when a finite-dimensional function space is used for fitting instead of a non-parametric, infinite-dimensional space (where the true functions belong in general).
As the authors discuss after Equation 15, there is a tradeoff between identifiability (having a larger gap by using a richer function space) and variance.
They argue that this difference can be much different than the bias-variance tradeoff.
From this perspective,
\tilde{\xi}_{p}
is a specific case of the general idea proposed in
Bühlmann et al. 2014
to handle misspecification.

Appendix E
Generating Simulated Data

For node
X_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq p
, with sampled trend types
\{f_{ij}\}_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}}
and
\{g_{kj}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and weights
\{\theta_{ij}\}_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}}^{j}}
and
\{\theta_{kj}^{{}^{\prime}}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, let

O_{j}=\sum_{i\in\textnormal{Pa}_{\mathcal{G}^{*}}^{j}}\theta_{ij}f_{ij}(X_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad C_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta^{\prime}_{kj}g_{kj}(H_{k})

represent the (unnormalized) variation explained by the observed and confounder nodes, respectively.
We wish to find normalization constants
c_{{\textrm{par}},j}
and
c_{{\textrm{confound}},j}
so that

\text{Cov}(c_{{\textrm{par}},j}O_{j}+c_{{\textrm{confound}},j}C_{j})=1-\sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}

and

\text{Cov}(c_{{\textrm{confound}},j}C_{j})=\sigma_{\textrm{confound}}^{2}.

Since it may be difficult to analytically solve for these normalization constants, we find them inductively using a Monte Carlo approach.
In particular, suppose we have computed the normalization constants
c_{{\textrm{par}},i}
and
c_{{\textrm{confound}},j}
for
i<j
.
Then, we take many Monte Carlo samples (we use 10,000 in the experiments) from the marginal distribution over
H,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{j-1}
.
This allows us to estimate
\text{Cov}(C_{j})
and solve for
c_{{\textrm{confound}},j}
.
Similarly, we may use these samples to estimate
\text{Cov}(O_{j},C_{j})
, which along with the value of
c_{{\textrm{confound}},j}
and the estimate of
{\textrm{Var}}(C_{j})
, allows us to solve for
c_{{\textrm{par}},j}
in the first equation.
There are several edge cases depending on if
x_{j}
has no observed and/or confounder parents:

1.

If
X_{j}
has no observed or confounder parents (i.e., is a source), then set
c_{{\textrm{par}},j}=0
and
c_{{\textrm{confound}},j}=0
. Set the noise variance for
x_{j}
equal to 1.

2.

If
X_{j}
has no confounder parents but at least one observed node parent, then set the signal variance for
x_{j}
equal to
\sigma_{\text{signal}}^{2}+\sigma_{\textrm{confound}}^{2}
.

3.

If
X_{j}
has at least one confounder parents but no observed node parents, then set the noise variance for
X_{j}
equal to
\sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{signal}}^{2}
.

See the
generate_synthetic_data.py
file in our Github repository for the code.
