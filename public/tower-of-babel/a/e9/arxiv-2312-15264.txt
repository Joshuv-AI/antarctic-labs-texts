The Covariant Stone–von Neumann Theorem for Locally Compact Quantum Groups
Lucas Hall, Leonard Huang, Jacek Krajczok, Mariusz Tobolski

Abstract
. The Stone–von Neumann Theorem is a fundamental result which unified the competing quantum-mechanical models of matrix mechanics and wave mechanics. In this article, we continue the broad generalization set out by Huang and Ismert and by Hall, Huang, and Quigg, analyzing representations of locally compact quantum dynamical systems defined on Hilbert modules, of which the classical result is a special case. We introduce a pair of modular representations which subsume numerous models available in the literature and, using the classical strategy of Rieffel, prove a Stone–von Neumann-type theorem for maximal actions of regular locally compact quantum groups on elementary C*-algebras. In particular, we generalize the Mackey–Stone–von Neumann Theorem to regular locally compact quantum groups whose trivial actions on ℂ \mathbb{C} are maximal and recover the multiplicity results of Hall, Huang, and Quigg. With this characterization in hand, we prove our main result showing that if a dynamical system ( 𝔾 , A , α ) (\mathbb{G},A,\alpha) satisfies the multiplicity assumption of the generalized Stone–von Neumann theorem, and if the coefficient algebra A A admits a faithful state, then the spectrum of the iterated crossed product 𝔾 ^ op ⋉ ( 𝔾 ⋉ A ) \widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\rm op}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A) consists of a single point. In the case of a separable coefficient algebra or a regular acting quantum group, we further characterize features of this system, and thus obtain a partial converse to the Stone–von Neumann theorem in the quantum group setting. As a corollary, we show that a regular locally compact quantum group satisfies the generalized Stone–von Neumann theorem if and only if it is strongly regular.



1.
Introduction

The fields of science have long benefited from interdisciplinary interactions, and the application of mathematics to these fields is no exception. With its foundation in mathematics and home in physics, the exploration of quantum mechanics has beyond any doubt cast us into the atomic era, and chief in the development of this knowledge were the independently developed models of quantum mechanics, namely Werner Heisenberg’s matrix mechanics and Erwin Schrödinger’s wave mechanics. Where matrix mechanics proposed a governance of subatomic phenomena by certain interacting linear transformations, wave mechanics distinguished itself by the application of partial differential equations. Remarkably, each model yielded the same experimental predictions, suggesting a unity of theory despite cosmetic differences. Using sophisticated new tools developed by Marshall Stone (which today serve as important rudiments in the study of operator algebras), John von Neumann successfully proved the formal mathematical equivalence of these two models in what is now called the Stone–von Neumann Theorem. Loosely stated, the theorem proves that any self-adjoint representation of Heisenberg’s linear operators is merely a multiple of the irreducible representation coming from solutions to Schrödinger’s wave equation. The essential strategy involved associating to a coupled pair of linear transformations two families of linear operators indexed by the topological group
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Von Neumann proceeded to study rings of operators, initiating the study of operator algebras which reaches as far in the field of mathematics as quantum mechanics does in physics. Indeed, operator algebraists have developed deep interactions with numerous “classical” mathematical fields, mirroring the drive to unify physics under one “standard model”. Throughout this enrichment of the field, a steady interest in Stone and von Neumann’s previous investigations remained, sustaining a perpetual refinement and generalization of ideas. Major influences include George Mackey and Marc Rieffel, with successive advancement from numerous others. Recently, Ismert and the second author provided a significant innovation, classifying representations of
C^{*}
-dynamical systems of abelian groups represented on Hilbert modules. Quigg and the first two authors expanded this classification to include dynamical systems of general (nonabelian) locally compact groups using divergent techniques from the parallel but distinct lenses of duality for
C^{*}
-actions and coactions.

From the perspective of nonabelian duality, the divergence of technique is a necessary cost for the expansion beyond actions of an abelian group. However, recent decades have offered the appealing new theory of locally compact quantum groups to unify these parallel developments under a common theory. In this article, we call upon this theory to integrate the work of Quigg and the first two authors, recovering numerous independent results inspired by Stone and von Neumann’s century-old work, and offering a new analysis of spectral phenomena which may be regarded as a foundation for the multiplicity results which appear in the literature. Figuring prominently in this article is the emphasis on representations of locally compact quantum groups, which are a valuable tool for quantum groups which appears implicitly in the coaction setting of

. These objects, other conventions for locally compact quantum groups, and their crossed products are recorded in §

For dynamical systems comprising maximal actions and elementary coefficient algebras, we deduce that every Heisenberg representation is a multiple of the Schrödinger representation, and with this characterization in hand, we prove our main result: given a dynamical system

The Millinery of Locally Compact Quantum Groups

Our main references for locally compact quantum groups are

A
locally compact quantum group

\mathbb{G}
consists of a
C^{*}
-algebra
C_{0}(\mathbb{G})
together with a nondegenerate
*
-homomorphism
\Delta\colon C_{0}(\mathbb{G})\to M(C_{0}(\mathbb{G})\otimes C_{0}(\mathbb{G}))
, called comultiplication which satisfies the coassociativity
(\Delta\otimes\text{id})\Delta=(\text{id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta
and density conditions

\begin{split}C_{0}(\mathbb{G})&=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(\omega\otimes\text{id})\Delta(x)|~\omega\in C_{0}(\mathbb{G})^{*},x\in C_{0}(\mathbb{G})\}\\
&=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(\text{id}\otimes\omega)\Delta(x)|~\omega\in C_{0}(\mathbb{G})^{*},x\in C_{0}(\mathbb{G})\}.\end{split}

Furthermore, by definition, there are two faithful, approximately KMS weights
\varphi,\psi
on
C_{0}(\mathbb{G})
which are respectively left- and right-invariant
(for details see
[
9
, Definition 4.1]
). One can equivalently work in the von Neumann-algebraic setting
(
[
10
, Definition 1.1]
), but we will stay in the language of noncommutative topology, i.e.,
C^{*}
-algebras.

Associated with the left Haar weight
\varphi
is the GNS Hilbert space, denoted
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})
, and faithful representation
\pi_{\varphi}\colon C_{0}(\mathbb{G})\to\mathbb{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))
. Since
\pi_{\varphi}
is faithful, it is customary to consider
C_{0}(\mathbb{G})
as acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})
and neglect writing
\pi_{\varphi}
, and we will follow this custom. One defines a unitary
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}\in\mathbb{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))
called the Kac-Takesaki operator. It is a multiplicative unitary, i.e., it satisfies the Pentagonal Relation

\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{13}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{23}=\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{23}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12}.

From
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}
, one recovers the
C^{*}
-algebra
C_{0}(\mathbb{G})=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(\text{id}\otimes\omega)(\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}})|~\omega\in\mathbb{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))_{*}\}
and comultiplication
\Delta(a)=\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}*}(1\otimes a)\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}\;(a\in C_{0}(\mathbb{G}))
. One defines the
dual
locally compact quantum group
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
— its
C^{*}
-algebra may be recognized as
C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{(\omega\otimes\text{id})(\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}})|~\omega\in\mathbb{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))_{*}\}
and the comultiplication is given by
\widehat{\Delta}(a)=\mathrm{W}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}*}(1\otimes a)\mathrm{W}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}\;(a\in C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}))
, where
\mathrm{W}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}=\chi(\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}*})
. In fact,
\mathrm{W}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}
is the Kac-Takesaki operator of
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
. The remaining features of
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
will be likewise decorated with hats. One can prove that in fact
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}\in M(C_{0}(\mathbb{G})\otimes C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}))
and

(\Delta\otimes\text{id})\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}=\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{13}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{23},\qquad(\text{id}\otimes\widehat{\Delta})\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}=\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{13}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12}.

The culmination of the theory is the recovery of generalized Pontryagin duality
\widehat{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}\cong\mathbb{G}
.

Every locally compact group
G
gives rise to two quantum groups. The first one is
\mathbb{G}
, with associated
C^{*}
-algebra
C_{0}(\mathbb{G})=C_{0}(G)
, comultiplication acting by

C_{0}(\widehat{G})=C_{r}^{*}(G)
is the reduced group

-algebra and comultiplication acts on generators via
\widehat{\Delta}(\lambda(g))=\lambda(g)\otimes\lambda(g)

are equal to the Plancherel weight. Nonclassical analogs include compact quantum groups (which entails

possess a unit) and discrete quantum groups (where

Out of a quantum group
\mathbb{G}

-algebra,

and the

, which is a

respecting comultiplications (i.e.

which we will encounter in section

[

]

Let

be the modular conjugation associated to the left Haar weight. The

\mathbb{G}
is a locally compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}^{\textnormal{c}}
with

-algebra

is the

is a covariant representation of the right dynamical system
(A,\widehat{\mathbb{G}},{\rm triv})
on
X
.

Here,
{\rm triv}\colon A\rightarrow M(A\otimes C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}))
denotes the right trivial action given by
{\rm triv}(a)=a\otimes 1
.

Proof.

Assume that (R3) holds. To prove (R3’), we have to show that

(\pi\otimes{\rm id}){\rm triv}(a)=(\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})(\pi(a)\otimes 1)(\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})^{*}

for all
a\in A
, which is equivalent to

(\pi(a)\otimes 1)(\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})=(\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})(\pi(a)\otimes 1).

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))_{*}
. Then

\displaystyle({\rm id}\otimes\omega)((\pi(a)\otimes 1)(\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}))

\displaystyle=\pi(a)\rho^{c}(({\rm id}\otimes\omega)(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}))

\displaystyle=\rho^{c}(({\rm id}\otimes\omega)(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}))\pi(a)

\displaystyle=({\rm id}\otimes\omega)((\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})(\pi(a)\otimes 1)).

Since the above equation holds for any
\omega\in\mathbb{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))_{*}
, the result follows. Again reversing the above computation yields the converse claim.
∎

(6)

for all

1

j_{A}(a)
,

, and

,

\displaystyle({\rm id}\otimes\Pi)\widehat{\alpha}^{\rm op}(j_{\mathbb{G}}(h))

Here we used the condition (R2’) and the formula

6
) for generators, which ends proof in one direction.

(\mu,\Pi)
be a covariant representation of
(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\rm op},\mathbb{G}\ltimes A,\widehat{\alpha}^{\rm op})
on
X
. Define

\rho^{c}:=\mu,\qquad\widehat{\rho}:=\Pi\circ j_{\mathbb{G}},\qquad\pi:=\Pi\circ j_{A}\,.

(R1) We have to prove that

on

\displaystyle(\widehat{\rho}\otimes{\rm id})({\rm id}\otimes\hat{\lambda})\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}^{u}(h)

\displaystyle=\chi(({\rm id}\otimes\Pi)\widehat{\alpha}^{\rm op}(j_{\mathbb{G}}(h)))

\displaystyle=\chi(({\rm id}\otimes\mu)(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\rm op}})^{*}(1\otimes\Pi(j_{\mathbb{G}}(h)))({\rm id}\otimes\mu)(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\rm op}}))

\displaystyle=(\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})(\widehat{\rho}(h)\otimes 1)(\rho^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})^{*}.

4.
The Covariant Stone–von Neumann Theorem

We now introduce the Schrödinger representation of a dynamical system
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
, which may be profitably considered as a fundamental example of a representation for the dynamics.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
be a left dynamical system. A
Schrödinger representation
is a quadruple
(L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A,\lambda^{c}\otimes 1,\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1,\alpha)
, where

\lambda^{c}\colon C^{u}_{0}(\mathbb{G}^{c})\longrightarrow C_{0}(\mathbb{G}^{c}),\qquad\hat{\lambda}\colon C^{u}_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\longrightarrow C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}),

are the canonical surjective
*
-homomorphisms.

Proposition 4.2
.

The Schrödinger representation is a Heisenberg representation.

Proof.

(R1) We have to prove that
(\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1,\alpha)
is a covariant representation of
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A
, i.e.

({\rm id}\otimes\alpha)\alpha(a)=({\rm id}\otimes(\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1))(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}})^{*}(1\otimes\alpha(a))({\rm id}\otimes(\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1))(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}}).

Note that the LHS equals
(\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}\otimes\text{id})\alpha(a)
due to the covariance condition. We compute the RHS:

\displaystyle({\rm id}\otimes(\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1))(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}})^{*}(1\otimes\alpha(a))({\rm id}\otimes(\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1))(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}})

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12})^{*}(1\otimes\alpha(a))\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12}=(\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}\otimes\text{id})\alpha(a).

Here, we used the fact that
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}
implements
\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
.

(R2) It suffices to prove that

\mathrm{W}_{13}^{\mathbb{G}}({\rm id}\otimes\hat{\lambda})(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}})_{12}(\lambda^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})_{23}=(\lambda^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})_{23}({\rm id}\otimes\hat{\lambda})(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}})_{12}\,,

which follows from direct calculation

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}_{13}^{\mathbb{G}}({\rm id}\otimes\hat{\lambda})(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}})_{12}(\lambda^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})_{23}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{13}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12}\mathrm{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}_{23}=({\rm id}\otimes\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})(\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}})\mathrm{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}_{23}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}_{23}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12}(\mathrm{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}_{23})^{*}\mathrm{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}_{23}=\mathrm{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}_{23}\mathrm{W}^{\mathbb{G}}_{12}

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{c}\otimes{\rm id})(\mathds{V}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})_{23}({\rm id}\otimes\hat{\lambda})(\text{\reflectbox{$\mathds{W}$}}\>\!^{\mathbb{G}})_{12}\,.

(R3) The images of
\alpha
and
\lambda^{c}\otimes 1
commute, since
\alpha(A)\subseteq M(C_{0}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A)
and
(\lambda^{c}\otimes 1)(C_{0}^{u}(\mathbb{G}^{c}))\subseteq C_{0}(\mathbb{G}^{c})\otimes 1
.
∎

Using the machinery we have gathered so far, we can prove one of the main results of our work.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\left(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha\right)
be a left dynamical system, where
A
is an elementary
C^{*}
-algebra,
\mathbb{G}
is a regular locally compact quantum group, and
\alpha
is a maximal action.
Then every Heisenberg
\left(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha\right)
-representation is a multiple of the Schrödinger
\left(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha\right)
-representation.

Proof.

Consider a Heisenberg
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
-representation
(X,\rho^{\textnormal{c}},\widehat{\rho},\pi)
of
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
. Then Proposition
3.9
yields a covariant representation
(\rho^{\textnormal{c}},\widehat{\rho}\ltimes\pi)
for
(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\rm op},\mathbb{G}\ltimes A,\widehat{\alpha}^{\rm op})
. In turn, the covariant representation
(\rho^{\textnormal{c}},\widehat{\rho}\ltimes\pi)
determines a nondegenerate representation of the iterated crossed product

\rho^{\textnormal{c}}\ltimes\widehat{\rho}\ltimes\pi\colon\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)\to\mathcal{L}(X).

Since
\alpha
is maximal, we have an isomorphism
(\lambda^{c}\otimes 1)\ltimes(\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1)\ltimes\alpha\colon\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)\to\mathbb{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))\otimes A
. Abbreviating the isomorphism by
\Phi
, we witness
X
is an
\mathbb{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))\otimes A-A
correspondence with left action coming from the
*
-homomorphism
(\rho^{c}\ltimes\hat{\rho}\ltimes\pi)\circ\Phi^{-1}
.

Applying this analysis to the Schrödinger representation
(L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A,\lambda^{c}\otimes 1,\hat{\lambda}\otimes 1,\alpha),
we can also see that the correspondence
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A
is an
\mathbb{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))\otimes A-A
correspondence. It is in fact an imprimitivity bimodule. To see this, notice that the map
\Phi
in the previous paragraph agrees with the iterated crossed product
*
-homomorphism associated to the Schrödinger representation, so that the left action of
\mathbb{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))\otimes A
is given by the identity. Since
A
and
\mathbb{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))\otimes A
are both simple, the fullness of
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A
is clear, and for the compatibility of the inner products, we compute for elementary tensors

\displaystyle\langle h\otimes c,g\otimes b\rangle\cdot f\otimes a

\displaystyle=(\langle h,g\rangle\otimes\langle c,b\rangle)\cdot f\otimes a=(\theta_{h,g}\otimes cb^{*})\cdot f\otimes a=h\langle g,f\rangle\otimes cb^{*}a

\displaystyle=h\otimes c\cdot(\langle g,f\rangle\otimes\langle b,a\rangle)=h\otimes c(\langle g\otimes b,f\otimes a\rangle).

Thus we may apply Theorem
2.1
to deduce that
X
is a multiple of
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A
. We suppress the unitary which implements the equivalence
X\cong\bigoplus_{S}L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A
, and proceed by identifying these two modules.

To finish, disintegrate the integrated form

\rho^{\textnormal{c}}\ltimes\widehat{\rho}\ltimes\pi\colon\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)\to\mathcal{L}(\bigoplus_{S}L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A),

recovering the covariant representation
(\rho^{\textnormal{c}},\hat{\rho}\ltimes\pi)
of
(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}},\mathbb{G}\ltimes A,\hat{\alpha}^{\textnormal{op}}).
Applying Proposition
3.9
, we recover the Heisenberg
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
-representation
(\oplus_{S}\big(L^{2}(\mathbb{G})\otimes A\big),\rho^{\textnormal{c}},\hat{\rho},\pi).
Since each summand is invariant under the
*
-representations, this concludes the proof.
∎

of

Definition 4.5
.

A dynamical system
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
is called Stone–von Neumann unique provided that every Heisenberg
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
-representation is a multiple of the Schrödinger
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
-representation.

By Theorem

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a locally compact quantum group and
A
a
C^{*}
-algebra admitting a faithful state. Assume that there is a dynamical
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)

\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)
consists of a single point.

Proof.

We begin with a Stone–von Neumann unique dynamical system
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
, and must show that any two irreducible representations
\theta,\tilde{\theta}
of
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)
on Hilbert spaces
H_{\theta},H_{\tilde{\theta}}
are equivalent. First, consider
\theta\otimes 1
as a representation of
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)
on Hilbert-
A

H_{\theta}\otimes A
, similarly
\tilde{\theta}\otimes 1
. Using the canonical covariant representation of
(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}},\mathbb{G}\ltimes A,\widehat{\alpha}^{\textnormal{op}})

j_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}}\colon C_{0}^{u}(\mathbb{G}^{c})\rightarrow M(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)),\quad j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\colon\mathbb{G}\ltimes A\rightarrow M(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A))

and then composing with
\theta\otimes 1
(extended uniquely to a strictly continuous map on the multiplier algebra), we obtain a covariant representation of
(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}},\mathbb{G}\ltimes A,\widehat{\alpha}^{\textnormal{op}})
on
H_{\theta}\otimes A
. By Proposition
3.9
, we recover the Heisenberg representation
(H_{\theta}\otimes A,\rho^{c}_{\theta},\widehat{\rho}_{\theta},\pi_{\theta})
of
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
. More concretely,

\rho^{c}_{\theta}=(\theta\circ j_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}})\otimes 1,\quad\widehat{\rho}_{\theta}=(\theta\circ j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{\mathbb{G}})\otimes 1,\quad\pi_{\theta}=(\theta\circ j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A})\otimes 1,

where

j_{\mathbb{G}}\colon C_{0}^{u}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\rightarrow M(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A),\quad j_{A}\colon A\rightarrow M(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)

are the canonical maps. We can perform a similar construction for
\tilde{\theta}
. By our assumption, there is a set
S_{\theta}
so that
(H_{\theta}\otimes A,\rho^{c}_{\theta},\widehat{\rho}_{\theta},\pi_{\theta})
is a
|S_{\theta}|
-multiple of the Schrödinger representation. Similarly,
{(H_{\tilde{\theta}}\otimes A,\rho^{c}_{\tilde{\theta}},\widehat{\rho}_{\tilde{\theta}},\pi_{\tilde{\theta}})}
is a Heisenberg representation which is equivalent to a
|S_{\tilde{\theta}}|
-multiple of the Schrödinger representation. Let
S=S_{\theta}\times S_{\tilde{\theta}}\times\mathbb{N}
. By cardinal arithmetic, we have
|S\times S_{\theta}|=|S|=|S\times S_{\tilde{\theta}}|
, hence
|S|
-multiples of
(H_{\theta}\otimes A,\rho^{c}_{\theta},\widehat{\rho}_{\theta},\pi_{\theta})
and
(H_{\tilde{\theta}}\otimes A,\rho^{c}_{\tilde{\theta}},\widehat{\rho}_{\tilde{\theta}},\pi_{\tilde{\theta}})
are equivalent. Let

U\colon\bigoplus_{S}H_{\theta}\otimes A\rightarrow\bigoplus_{S}H_{\tilde{\theta}}\otimes A

be the corresponding isomorphism which implements this equivalence.

By our assumption, there is a faithful state
\varpi
on
A
, so let
K_{\varpi}
be the corresponding GNS Hilbert space and
\Lambda_{\varpi}
the corresponding GNS map. Let us consider

(7)

\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}\colon H_{\theta}\otimes A\rightarrow H_{\theta}\otimes K_{\varpi}

which on a linearly dense set acts via
\xi\otimes a\mapsto\xi\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}(a)
. Take an arbitrary linear combination from this set
\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i}\in H_{\theta}\odot A
and calculate

\begin{split}\bigl\|(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\bigl(\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i}\bigr)\bigr\|^{2}&=\sum_{i,j}\langle\xi_{i}|\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\Lambda_{\varpi}(a_{i})|\Lambda_{\varpi}(a_{j})\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\langle\xi_{i}|\xi_{j}\rangle\varpi(a_{i}^{*}a_{j})\\
&=\varpi\bigl(\bigl\langle\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i},\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i}\bigr\rangle\bigr)\leq\bigl\|\bigl\langle\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i},\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i}\bigr\rangle\bigr\|\\
&=\bigl\|\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i}\bigr\|^{2}.\end{split}

It follows that (
7
) is a well defined contraction. Next we claim that

(8)

\langle(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(x)|(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(x^{\prime})\rangle=\varpi(\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle).

for
x,x^{\prime}\in H_{\theta}\otimes A
. Both sides are sesquilinear and continuous in
x,x^{\prime}
, hence it is enough to check (
8
) on a linearly dense set: take
x=\xi\otimes a,x^{\prime}=\xi^{\prime}\otimes a^{\prime}
. Then

\langle(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(x)|(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(x^{\prime})\rangle=\langle\xi\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}(a)|\xi^{\prime}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}(a^{\prime})\rangle=\langle\xi|\xi^{\prime}\rangle\varpi(a^{*}a^{\prime})=\varpi(\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle).

Note that from (
8
) follows that
\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}
is injective. A similar reasoning can be performed for
\tilde{\theta}
– we will abuse the notation and denote the corresponding map also by
\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}
. Next let us define a linear map between Hilbert spaces

\tilde{U}\colon\bigoplus_{S}H_{\theta}\otimes K_{\varpi}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{S}H_{\tilde{\theta}}\otimes K_{\varpi}.

First, on a dense subspace
\bigoplus_{S}(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(H_{\theta}\odot A)
, we set

(9)

\tilde{U}\bigl(((\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\Omega_{i})_{i\in S}\bigr)=\bigl((\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(\zeta_{i})\bigr)_{i\in S}\textnormal{ where }U\bigl((\Omega_{i})_{i\in S}\bigr)=(\zeta_{i})_{i\in S}.

Since
\varpi
is faithful,
\tilde{U}
is well defined on a dense subset. Let us argue that it is isometric. Take
((\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\Omega_{i})_{i\in S}
and
\zeta_{i}
as above. Then using (
8
) we have

\begin{split}\bigl\|\tilde{U}\bigl(((\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\Omega_{i})_{i\in S}\bigr)\bigr\|^{2}&=\bigl\|\bigl((\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(\zeta_{i})\bigr)_{i\in S}\bigr\|^{2}=\sum_{i\in S}\|(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(\zeta_{i})\|^{2}\\
&=\sum_{i\in S}\varpi(\langle\zeta_{i},\zeta_{i}\rangle)=\varpi\bigl(\sum_{i\in S}\langle\zeta_{i},\zeta_{i}\rangle\bigr)=\varpi\bigl(\bigl\langle(\zeta_{i})_{i\in S},(\zeta_{i})_{i\in S}\bigr\rangle\bigr)\\
&=\varpi\bigl(\bigl\langle U\bigl((\Omega_{i})_{i\in S}\bigr),U\bigl((\Omega_{i})_{i\in S}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\bigr)=\varpi\bigl(\bigl\langle(\Omega_{i})_{i\in S},(\Omega_{i})_{i\in S}\bigr\rangle\bigr)\\
&=\sum_{i\in S}\varpi(\langle\Omega_{i},\Omega_{i}\rangle)=\sum_{i\in S}\langle(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\Omega_{i}|(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\Omega_{i}\rangle\\
&=\bigl\|\bigl((\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\Omega_{i}\bigr)_{i\in S}\bigr\|^{2}.\end{split}

This shows that
\tilde{U}
is an isometry. Clearly it has dense image; consequently, it is unitary.

Next, we check that
\tilde{U}
is an intertwiner between two representations of
M(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A))
, the first being
\bigoplus_{S}\theta\otimes 1
on
\bigoplus_{S}H_{\theta}\otimes K_{\varpi}
and the other
\bigoplus_{S}\tilde{\theta}\otimes 1
on
\bigoplus_{S}H_{\tilde{\theta}}\otimes K_{\varpi}
. From (
9
) it follows that

\tilde{U}\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\bigr)=\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\bigr)U\quad\textnormal{ on }\bigoplus_{S}H_{\theta}\otimes A.

We need to check that

\tilde{U}\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}\theta(x)\otimes 1\bigr)=\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}\tilde{\theta}(x)\otimes 1\bigr)\tilde{U}

for
x\in M(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A))
. However, since
M(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A))
is generated by the images of
C_{0}^{u}(\mathbb{G}^{c}),C_{0}^{u}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}),A
it is enough to consider
x
of the form
j_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}}(y)
,
j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{\mathbb{G}}(z)
and
j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a)
. Take
a\in A
.

\begin{split}&\quad\;\tilde{U}\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}\theta(j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a))\otimes 1\bigr)(\xi_{i}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}(a_{i}))_{i\in S}=\tilde{U}(\theta(j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a))\xi_{i}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}(a_{i}))_{i\in S}\\
&=\tilde{U}\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\bigr)(\theta(j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a))\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i})_{i\in S}=\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\bigr)U(\theta(j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a))\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i})_{i\in S}\\
&=\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})(\tilde{\theta}(j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a))\otimes 1)\bigr)U(\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i})_{i\in S}\\
&=\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}\tilde{\theta}(j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a))\otimes 1\bigr)\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}(\text{id}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi})\bigr)U(\xi_{i}\otimes a_{i})_{i\in S}\\
&=\bigl(\bigoplus_{S}\tilde{\theta}(j_{\mathbb{G}\ltimes A}\circ j_{A}(a))\otimes 1\bigr)\tilde{U}(\xi_{i}\otimes\Lambda_{\varpi}(a_{i}))_{i\in S}.\end{split}

The remaining conditions can be checked in a completely analogous way. This shows that
\bigoplus_{S}\theta\otimes 1
and
\bigoplus_{S}\tilde{\theta}\otimes 1
are equivalent. Consequently, they are multiples of
\theta
and
\tilde{\theta}
which are equivalent. But since
\theta,\tilde{\theta}
are assumed to be irreducible, it follows that they are equivalent themselves (
[
4
, Proposition 5.3.3]
). In particular, they are equivalent as representations of
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)
and consequently this
C^{*}
-algebra has spectrum consisting of one point.
∎

Let us note here that the above proposition applies to any separable
C^{*}
-algebra, since such a
C^{*}
-algebra admits a faithful state.

With this in hand, we will now see that a complete characterization of coefficient algebras
A
boils down to Naimark’s problem
[
15
]
. To this end, let us make a general observation that for the trivial action
(\mathbb{G},A,\operatorname{triv})
, we have

(10)

\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)\cong(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes C_{0}^{u}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}))\otimes_{\textnormal{max}}A.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a locally compact quantum group and
A
a separable
C^{*}
-algebra, and assume that
(\mathbb{G},A,\operatorname{triv})
enjoys Stone–von Neumann uniqueness. Then
A
is an elementary
C^{*}
-algebra.

Proof.

Note that since
A
is separable, it has a faithful state. Let
\vartheta_{2},\tilde{\vartheta_{2}}
be irreducible representations of
A
and
\vartheta_{1}
an irreducible representation of
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes C_{0}^{u}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
. Then we can form a representation
\vartheta_{1}\otimes\vartheta_{2}
of
(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes C_{0}^{u}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}))\otimes_{\textnormal{max}}A
on
H_{\vartheta_{1}}\otimes H_{\vartheta_{2}}
; same for
\vartheta_{1}\otimes\tilde{\vartheta}_{2}
. They are irreducible, hence by the isomorphism (
10
) above and Theorem
4.6
, there is a unitary
v\colon H_{\vartheta_{1}}\otimes H_{\vartheta_{2}}\rightarrow H_{\vartheta_{1}}\otimes H_{\tilde{\vartheta}_{2}}
such that
v(\vartheta_{1}\otimes\vartheta_{2})(\cdot)=(\vartheta_{1}\otimes\tilde{\vartheta}_{2})(\cdot)v
. Extend these representations to multiplier algebras, and consider representations
A\ni a\mapsto(\vartheta_{1}\otimes\vartheta_{2})(1\otimes a)=1\otimes\vartheta_{2}(a)\in\mathbb{B}(H_{\vartheta_{1}}\otimes H_{\vartheta_{2}})
, and same for
\tilde{\vartheta}_{2}
. These are multiples of
\vartheta_{2},\tilde{\vartheta}_{2}
which are equivalent via the unitary
v
. Since
\vartheta_{2},\tilde{\vartheta}_{2}
are irreducible, we can use again
[
4
, Proposition 5.3.3]
to deduce that
\vartheta_{2},\tilde{\vartheta}_{2}
are equivalent. We assume that
A
is separable, hence
[
4
, Exercise 4.7.2]
tells us that
A
is elementary.
∎

We also observe that separability is an important obstruction to a general characterization of algebras with one-point spectrum. Akemann and Weaver demonstrate in
[
1
]
that the existence of nonelementary
C^{*}
-algebras with one-point spectrum and
\aleph_{1}
generators is independent of ZFC.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
be a left dynamical system, where
\mathbb{G}
is a regular locally compact quantum group and
A
a
C^{*}
-algebra which admits a faithful state. If
(\mathbb{G},A,\alpha)
is subject to Stone–von Neumann uniqueness, then
\alpha
is a maximal action.

Proof.

We have a canonical surjective
*
-homomorphism
\rho\colon\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)\rightarrow\mathbb{K}(L^{2}(\mathbb{G}))\otimes A
, and we want to show that this map is injective. Assume otherwise, i.e. assume that
\text{ker}(\rho)
is a nontrivial ideal in
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)
. Then we can form the quotient
C^{*}
-algebra
(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A))/\text{ker}(\rho)
and choose its irreducible representation
\theta\colon(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A))/\text{ker}(\rho)\rightarrow\mathbb{B}(H_{\theta})
. Composing with the quotient map, we obtain an irreducible representation
\tilde{\theta}\colon\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)\rightarrow\mathbb{B}(H_{\theta})
such that
\text{ker}(\rho)\subseteq\text{ker}(\tilde{\theta})
. On the other hand, since
\text{ker}(\rho)
is nontrivial, we can choose
x\in\text{ker}(\rho)\setminus\{0\}
and an irreducible representation
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}\colon\widehat{\mathbb{G}}^{\textnormal{op}}\ltimes(\mathbb{G}\ltimes A)\rightarrow\mathbb{B}(H_{\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}})
such that
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
. This shows that
\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}
have different kernels, in particular are not equivalent – this gives us a contradiction with Theorem
4.6
. Thus
\rho
is an isomorphism, and the action
\alpha
is maximal.
∎

Remark
.

Corollaries
4.7
and
4.8
indicate that the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
, namely elementariness of the coefficient algebra and maximality of the action, are not conditions designed to simplify arguments. On the contrary, they appear necessary.

As a final application, we prove the converse to Corollary
4.4
and show that Stone–von Neumann uniqueness of
(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{C},{\rm triv})
, where
\mathbb{G}
is regular, implies maximality of the trivial action of
\mathbb{G}
on
\mathbb{C}
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a regular locally compact quantum group. The following properties are equivalent:

(1)

(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{C},\operatorname{triv})
is Stone–von Neumann unique,

(2)

the trivial action of
\mathbb{G}
on
\mathbb{C}
is maximal,

(3)

\mathbb{G}
is strongly regular.

Proof.

If
(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{C},{\rm triv})
is Stone–von Neumann unique, then triv is maximal by Corollary
4.8
. The converse implication is the content of Corollary
4.4
. Equivalence of
(2)
and
(3)
was proved in Proposition
2.2
.
∎
