Hessian Barrier Algorithms for non-convex conic optimization
Pavel Dvurechensky, Mathias Staudigl

Abstract
A key problem in mathematical imaging, signal processing and computational statistics is the minimization of non-convex objective functions that may be non-differentiable at the boundary of the feasible set. This paper proposes a new family of first- and second-order interior-point methods for non-convex optimization problems with linear and conic constraints, combining logarithmically homogeneous barriers with quadratic and cubic regularization respectively. Our approach is based on a potential-reduction mechanism and, under the Lipschitz continuity of the corresponding derivative with respect to the local barrier-induced norm, attains a suitably defined class of approximate first- or second-order KKT points with worst-case iteration complexity O ⁡ ( ε − 2 ) O(\varepsilon^{-2}) (first-order) and O ( ε − 3 / 2 ) O(\varepsilon^{-3/2}) (second-order), respectively. Based on these findings, we develop new path-following schemes attaining the same complexity, modulo adjusting constants. These complexity bounds are known to be optimal in the unconstrained case, and our work shows that they are upper bounds in the case with complicated constraints as well. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first which achieves these worst-case complexity bounds under such weak conditions for general conic constrained non-convex optimization problems.

1.1
Motivating applications

Statistical estimation with non-convex regularization

An important instance of (
Opt
) is the composite optimization problem

(1.1)

\min_{x}f(x)=\ell(x)+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(x_{i}^{p})\quad\text{s.t.: }x\in\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}},

where
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth data fidelity function,

, whereas in the second case, we get
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(x^{p}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=\lVert x\rVert^{2}_{2}

which is non-convex and non-differentiable at the boundary of the feasible set. It has been reported in imaging sciences that the use of such non-convex and non-differentiable regularizer has advantages in the restoration of piecewise constant images.

[
34
]
in statistics, while

(called the LASSO) does not; problem (

) with

can be used for variable selection at the group and individual variable levels simultaneously, while the very same problem with

can only work for individual variable selection
[

]
. See

,

]
for a complexity-theoretic analysis of this problem.

Low rank matrix recovery

Similar to the composite minimization problem (

Challenges and contribution.

One of the challenges to approach problem (
Opt

\mathsf{L}\cap\bar{\mathsf{K}}
. A projection-based approach faces the computational bottleneck to project onto the intersection of a cone with an affine set, which makes the majority of the existing first-order
[

,

When primal feasibility is not a major concern, augmented Lagrangian algorithms
[

are an alternative, though they do not always come with complexity guarantees.
These observations motivate us to focus on primal barrier-penalty methods that allow to decompose the feasible set and treat
\bar{\mathsf{K}}
and
\mathsf{L}

Barrier methods are classical and powerful for convex optimization in the form of interior-point methods, but the results in the non-convex setting are in a sense fragmentary, with many different algorithms existing for different particular instantiations of (
Opt
). In particular, the main focus of barrier methods for non-convex optimization has been on particular cases, such as non-negativity constraints
[

,

,

]
and quadratic programming
[

In this paper we develop a flexible and unifying algorithmic framework that is able to provide first- and second-order interior-point algorithms for (
Opt
) with potentially non-convex objective functions, potentially non-differentiable on the boundary, and general conic constraints. To the best of our knowledge, our method is the first one providing complexity results for first- and second-order algorithms to reach approximate first- and second-order KKT points, respectively, under such weak assumptions.

Our approach.

At the core of our approach is the assumption that the cone
\bar{\mathsf{K}}
admits a
logarithmically homogeneous self-concordant barrier
(LHSCB)
h(x)
(
[
59
]
, cf. Definition
2.1
), for which we can retrieve information about the function value
h(x)
, the gradient
\nabla h(x)
and the Hessian
H(x)=\nabla^{2}h(x)
with relative ease. This is not a very restrictive assumption, since all standard conic restrictions in optimization (i.e.
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}},\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{SOC}}
and
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{SDP}}
) have this property. Using this barrier, our algorithms are designed to reduce the
potential function

(1.2)

F_{\mu}(x)=f(x)+\mu h(x),

where
\mu>0
is a (typically) small penalty parameter. By definition, the domain of the potential function
F_{\mu}
is the interior of the cone
\bar{\mathsf{K}}
. Therefore, any algorithm designed to reduce the potential will automatically respect the conic constraints, and the satisfaction of the linear constraints
\mathsf{L}
can be ensured by choosing search directions from the nullspace of the linear operator
{\mathbf{A}}
. Our target is to identify points satisfying approximate necessary first- and second-order optimality conditions for problem (
Opt
) expressed in terms of
\varepsilon
-KKT and
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-2KKT points respectively (cf. Section
3
for a precise definition).

Approaching first-order stationary points.

To produce a first-order stationary point, we construct a novel gradient-based method, which we call the
adaptive Hessian barrier algorithm
(
\AHBA
, Algorithm
1
). The main computational steps involved in
\AHBA
is the identification of a search direction and a step size policy, guaranteeing feasibility and sufficient decrease in the potential function value. To find a step direction, we employ a linear model for
F_{\mu}
regularized by the squared local norm induced by the Hessian of
h
which is then minimized over the tangent space of the affine set
\mathsf{L}
. The step-size is adaptively chosen to ensure feasibility and sufficient decrease in the objective function value
f
. For a judiciously chosen value of
\mu
, we prove that this gradient-based method enjoys the upper iteration complexity bound
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
for reaching an
\varepsilon
-KKT point when a “descent Lemma” holds relative to the local norm induced by the Hessian of
h
(cf. Assumption
3
and Theorem
4.2
in Section
4
). We then embed
\AHBA
into a path-following scheme that iteratively reduces the value of
\mu
making the algorithm parameter-free and any-time convergent with the
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
complexity.

Approaching second-order stationary points.

We next move on to derive a second-order method called the
second-order adaptive Hessian barrier algorithm
(
\SAHBA
, Algorithm
3
). Under this approach the step direction is determined by a minimization subproblem over the same tangent space.
But, in this case, the minimized model is composed of the linear model for
F_{\mu}
augmented by second-order term for
f
and regularized by the cube of the local norm induced by the Hessian of
h
.
The regularization parameter is chosen adaptively to allow for potentially larger steps in the areas of small curvature.
For a judiciously chosen value of
\mu
, we establish (see Theorem
5.3
) the worst-case bound
O(\max\{\varepsilon_{1}^{-3/2},\varepsilon_{2}^{-3/2}\})
on the number of iterations for reaching an
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-2KKT point, under a weaker assumption that the Hessian of
f
is Lipschitz relative to the local norm induced by the Hessian of
h
(see Assumption
4
in Section
5
for a precise definition). We then propose a path-following version of
\SAHBA
that iteratively reduces the value of
\mu
making the algorithm parameter-free and any-time convergent with
O(\max\{\varepsilon_{1}^{-3/2},\varepsilon_{2}^{-3/2}\})
complexity.

1.4
Notation

In what follows
\mathsf{E}
denotes a finite-dimensional real vector space, and
\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
the dual space, which is formed by all linear functions on
\mathsf{E}
. The value of
s\in\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
at
x\in\mathsf{E}
is denoted by
\langle s,x\rangle
. In the particular case where
\mathsf{E}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\mathsf{E}=\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
. Important elements of the dual space are gradients of differentiable functions
f:\mathsf{E}\to\mathbb{R}
, denoted as
\nabla f(x)\in\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
. For an operator
\mathbf{H}:\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
, denote by
\mathbf{H}^{\ast}
is adjoint operator, defined by the identity

(\forall u,v\in\mathsf{E}):\qquad\langle\mathbf{H}u,v\rangle:=\langle u,\mathbf{H}^{\ast}v\rangle.

Thus,
\mathbf{H}^{\ast}:\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
. It is called self-adjoint if
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}^{\ast}
. We use
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H})
to denote the maximum/minimum eigenvalue of such operators. Important examples of such self-adjoint operators are Hessians of twice differentiable functions
f:\mathsf{E}\to\mathbb{R}
:

(\forall u,v\in\mathsf{E}):\qquad\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)u,v\rangle=\langle u,\nabla^{2}f(x)v\rangle.

Operator
\mathbf{H}:\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
is positive semi-definite if
\langle\mathbf{H}u,u\rangle\geq 0
for all
u\in\mathsf{E}
. If the inequality is always strict for non-zero
u
, then
\mathbf{H}
is called positive definite. These attributes are denoted as
\mathbf{H}\succeq 0
and
\mathbf{H}\succ 0
, respectively.
By fixing a positive definite self-adjoint operator
\mathbf{H}:\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
, we can define the following Euclidean norms

\lVert u\rVert=\langle\mathbf{H}u,u\rangle^{1/2},\quad\lVert s\rVert^{\ast}=\langle s,\mathbf{H}^{-1}s\rangle^{1/2}\quad u\in\mathsf{E},s\in\mathsf{E}^{\ast}.

If
\mathsf{E}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mathbf{H}
is usually taken as the identity matrix
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{I}
.
The directional derivative of function
f
is defined in the usual way:

Df(x)[v]:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[f(x+\varepsilon v)-f(x)].

More generally, for
v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\in\mathsf{E}
, we define
D^{p}f(x)[v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}]
the
p
-th directional derivative at
x
along directions
v_{i}\in\mathsf{E}
. In that way we define
\nabla f(x)\in\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
by
Df(x)[u]=\langle\nabla f(x),u\rangle
and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(x):\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
by
\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)u,v\rangle=D^{2}f(x)[u,v]
.
We denote
\mathsf{L}_{0}=\{v\in\mathsf{E}|{\mathbf{A}}v=0\}
the tangent space associated with the linear subspace
\mathsf{L}\subset\mathsf{E}
.

2.2
Exploiting the structure of Symmetric Cones

Nesterov and Todd
[
60
]
introduced
self-scaled barriers
, which later have been realized as LHSCB’s for
symmetric cones
. Such barriers are nowadays key to define primal-dual interior point methods for convex problems with potentially larger step sizes. Our method can also exploit the additional properties of self-scaled barriers, leading to potentially larger step sizes and faster convergence in our non-convex setting as well. For a given closed convex nonempty cone
\bar{\mathsf{K}}
, its
dual cone
is the closed convex and nonempty conce
\bar{\mathsf{K}}^{\ast}
defined as
\bar{\mathsf{K}}^{\ast}\triangleq\{s\in\mathsf{E}^{\ast}|\langle s,x\rangle\geq 0\;\forall x\in\bar{\mathsf{K}}\}
. If
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
, then the
dual barrier
is defined
h_{\ast}(s)\triangleq\sup_{x\in\mathsf{K}}\{\langle-s,x\rangle-h(x)\}
for
s\in\bar{\mathsf{K}}^{\ast}
.

Definition 2.4
.

An open convex cone is said to be
self-dual
if
\mathsf{K}^{\ast}=\mathsf{K}
.
\mathsf{K}
is homogeneous if for all
x,y\in\mathsf{K}
there exists a linear bijection
G:\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{E}
such that
Gx=y
and
G\mathsf{K}=\mathsf{K}
. An open convex cone
\mathsf{K}
is called
symmetric
if it is self-dual and homogeneous.

The class of symmetric cones can be characterized within the language of Euclidean Jordan algebras
[
37
,
36
,
35
,
67
]
. For optimization, the three symmetric cones of most relevance are
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}},\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{SOC}}
and
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{SDP}}
.

Definition 2.5

(
[
60
]
)
.

h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
is a
\nu
-
self-scaled barrier
(
\nu
-SSB) if for all
x,w\in\mathsf{K}
we have
H(w)x\in\mathsf{K}
and
h_{\ast}(H(w)x)=h(x)-2h(w)-\nu
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
denote the class of
\nu
-SSBs.

We emphasize that
\mathcal{B}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})\subset\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
.
[
47
]
showed that every symmetric cone admits a
\nu
-SSB for some
\nu\geq 1
, while a characterization of the barrier parameter
\nu
has been obtained in
[
44
]
. The main advantage of working with SSB’s instead of LHSCB’s is that we can make potentially longer steps in the interior of the cone
\mathsf{K}
towards the direction of its boundary. Let
x\in\mathsf{K}
and
d\in\mathsf{E}
. Denote

(2.8)

\sigma_{x}(d):=(\sup\{t:x-td\in\mathsf{K}\})^{-1}

Since

for all

\sigma_{x}(d)\leq\lVert d\rVert_{x}
and
\sigma_{x}(-d)\leq\lVert d\rVert_{x}
for all

. Hence, if the scalar quantity

can be computed efficiently, it would allow us to make a larger step without violating feasibility.

Example 2.5
.

For
\bar{\mathsf{K}}=\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}

for all

. If

\sigma_{x}(-d)=\max\{\frac{d_{i}}{x_{i}}:d_{i}>0\}
.

\Diamond

For

2.3
Unified Notation

Our algorithms work on any conic domain on which we can efficiently evaluate a
\nu
-LHSCB. We formalize this in the following assumption

Assumption 2
.

\bar{\mathsf{K}}
is a regular cone admitting an efficient barrier setup
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
. By this we mean that at a given query point
x\in\mathsf{K}
, we can construct an oracle that returns to us information about the values
h(x),\nabla h(x)
and
H(x)=\nabla^{2}h(x)
, with low computational efforts.

Given the potential advantages when working on symmetric cones, it is useful to develop a unified notation handling both cases at the same time. Note that when
h\in\mathcal{B}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
, we have the flexibility to treat
h
either as
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
or as
h\in\mathcal{B}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
. To unify the presentation, we define

(2.10)

(\forall(x,d)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{E}):\;\zeta(x,d)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lVert d\rVert_{x}&\text{if }h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})\setminus\mathcal{B}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K}),\\
\sigma_{x}(-d)&\text{if }h\in\mathcal{B}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K}).\end{array}\right.

Note that

(2.11)

\displaystyle(\forall(x,d)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{E}):\;\zeta(x,d)\leq\lVert d\rVert_{x},

(2.12)

\displaystyle(\forall(x,d)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{E})(\forall t\in[0,\frac{1}{\zeta(x,d)})):\;x+td\in\mathsf{K}.

Finally, for the Bregman divergence
D_{h}(u,x):=h(u)-h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),u-x\rangle
defined for
x,u\in\mathsf{K}
, Proposition
2.3
, Proposition
2.6
together with eq. (
2.10
), give us the one-and-for-all Bregman bound

(2.13)

D_{h}(x+td,x)=h(x+td)-h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),td\rangle\leq t^{2}\lVert d\rVert_{x}^{2}\omega(t\zeta(x,d))

valid for all
(x,d)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{E}
and
t\in[0,\frac{1}{\zeta(x,d)})
.

3
Approximate optimality conditions

The next definition specifies our notion of an approximate first-order KKT point for problem (
Opt
).

Definition 3.1
.

Given
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we call a triple
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{s})\in\mathsf{E}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
an
\varepsilon
-KKT point for problem (
Opt
) if

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}\bar{x}=b,\bar{x}\in\mathsf{K},\bar{s}\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(\bar{x})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}\bar{y}-\bar{s}\|\leq\varepsilon,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\langle\bar{s},\bar{x}\rangle\leq\varepsilon.

To justify this definition, let
x^{\ast}
be a local solution of problem (
Opt
). Then, for
\delta>0
sufficiently small, the point
x^{\ast}
is the unique global solution to the perturbed optimization problem with ball restriction
\overline{\mathbb{B}(x^{\ast};\delta)}\triangleq\{x\in\mathsf{E}|\;\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert\leq\delta\}
:

(3.4)

\min_{x\in\mathsf{X}\cap\overline{\mathbb{B}(x^{\ast};\delta)}}f(x)+\frac{1}{4}\lVert x-x^{*}\rVert^{4}.

Next, using the barrier
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
, we absorb the constraint
x\in\mathsf{K}
in the penalty
\mu_{k}h(x)
, where
\mu_{k}>0
,
\mu_{k}\downarrow 0
is a given sequence. This leads to the barrier formulation

(3.5)

\min_{x\in\mathsf{X}\cap\overline{\mathbb{B}(x^{\ast};\delta)}}\varphi_{k}(x)\triangleq F_{\mu_{k}}(x)+\frac{1}{4}\lVert x-x^{\ast}\rVert^{4},\quad F_{\mu_{k}}(x)=f(x)+\mu_{k}h(x).

From the classical theory of interior penalty methods
[
38
]
, it is known that a global solution
x^{k}
exists for this problem for all
k
and that cluster points of
x^{k}
are global solutions of (
3.4
). Clearly,
x^{k}\in\mathsf{X}\cap\overline{\mathbb{B}(x^{\ast},\delta)}
for all
k
and
x^{k}\to x^{\ast}
. Setting
s^{k}=-\mu_{k}\nabla h(x^{k})
, which belongs to
\mathsf{K}^{\ast}
by eq. (
A.1
), and exploiting the properties of the barrier function
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
, we see that

\langle s^{k},x^{k}\rangle=-\mu_{k}\langle\nabla h(x^{k}),x^{k}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:log_hom_scb_hess_prop}}}{{=}}\mu_{k}\nu.

Consequently,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle s^{k},x^{k}\rangle=0
. Since
x^{k}\to x^{\ast}
, the restriction
x^{k}\in\overline{\mathbb{B}(x^{\ast};\delta)}
will automatically hold for
k
sufficiently large. By the full-rank assumption, the first-order optimality conditions of problem (
3.5
) reads as

\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}-s^{k}-\lVert x^{k}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\cdot(x^{k}-x^{\ast})=0,

for all
k
large enough. Hence, setting
\delta\leq\varepsilon^{1/3}
,
\mu_{k}\leq\varepsilon/\nu
, and
\bar{x}=x^{k},\bar{s}=s^{k},\bar{y}=y^{k}
, we obtain a triple satisfying conditions (
3.1
)-(
3.3
).

Assuming twice continuous differentiability of
f
on
\mathsf{X}
, our notion of an approximate second-order KKT point for problem (
Opt
) is defined as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

Given
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0
, we call a triple
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{s})\in\mathsf{E}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
an
(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
-2KKT point for problem (
Opt
) if

(3.6)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}\bar{x}=b,\bar{x}\in\mathsf{K},\bar{s}\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(\bar{x})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}\bar{y}-\bar{s}\|\leq\varepsilon_{1},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\langle\bar{s},\bar{x}\rangle\leq\varepsilon_{1},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(\bar{x})+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{2}}H(\bar{x})\succeq 0\;\;\text{on}\;\;\mathsf{L}_{0}.

The first three conditions are the same as for the
\varepsilon
-KKT point.
The last one can be justified as follows. Using the full-rank condition, the second-order optimality condition for problem (
3.5
) says that
x^{k}
satisfies

\langle(\nabla^{2}f(x^{k})+\mu_{k}H(x^{k}))d,d\rangle\geq-2\langle x^{k}-x^{\ast},d\rangle^{2}-\lVert x^{k}-x^{\ast}\rVert^{2}\lVert d\rVert^{2}\geq-3\delta^{2}\lVert d\rVert_{2}^{2}\qquad\forall d\in\mathsf{L}_{0}.

Setting
\mu_{k}\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{2}}
and
\delta\leq(\varepsilon_{2}/9)^{1/4}
, we see that
x^{k}
satisfies
\langle(\nabla^{2}f(x^{k})+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{2}}(H(x^{k})+\mathbf{I}))d,d\rangle\geq 0,\forall d\in\mathsf{L}_{0}
, which is clearly implied by (
3.9
).

\mathbf{X}\nabla^{2}f(x)\mathbf{X}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{2}}\mathbf{I}\succeq 0\qquad\text{on the set }\{d\in\mathsf{E}|{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}d=0\}.

This is equivalent to Proposition 2(c) in

Remark 3.2
.

If

3.1
On the relation to scaled critical points

In absence of differentiability at the boundary, a popular formulation of necessary optimality conditions involves the definition of scaled-critical points. Indeed, at a local minimizer
x^{\ast}
, the scaled first-order optimality condition
x_{i}^{\ast}[\nabla f(x^{\ast})]_{i}=0,1\leq i\leq n
holds, where the product is taken to be
0
when the derivative does not exist. Based on this characterization, one may call a point
x\in\mathsf{K}_{\text{NN}}
with
\lvert x_{i}[\nabla f(x)]_{i}\rvert\leq\varepsilon
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
\varepsilon
-scaled first-order point. Algorithms designed to produce
\varepsilon
-scaled first-order points, with some small
\varepsilon>0
, have been introduced in
[
12
]
and
[
11
]
. As reported in
[
46
]
, there are several problems associated with this weak definition of a critical point. First, when derivatives are available on
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
, the standard definition of a critical point would entail the inclusion
\langle\nabla f(x),x^{\prime}-x\rangle\geq 0
for all
x^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}.
Hence,
[\nabla f(x)]_{i}=0
for
x_{i}>0
and
[\nabla f(x)]_{i}\geq 0
for
x_{i}=0
. It follows,
\nabla f(x)\in\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
, a condition that is absent in the definition of a scaled critical point. Second, scaled critical points come with no measure of strength, as they holds trivially when
x=0
, regardless of the objective function. Third, there is a general gap between local minimizers and limits of
\varepsilon
-scaled first-order points, when
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
(see
[
46
]
). Similar remarks apply to the scaled second-order condition, considered in
[
11
]
. Our definition of approximate KKT points overcome these issues. In fact, our definitions of approximate first- and second-order KKT points is continuous in
\varepsilon
, and therefore in the limit our approximate KKT points coincide with the classical first- and second-order KKT conditions for a local minimizer. This is achieved without assuming global differentiability of the objective function or performing an additional smoothing of the problem data as in
[
10
,
11
]
.

4
A first-order Hessian-Barrier Algorithm

In this section we introduce a first-order potential reduction method for solving (
Opt
) that uses a barrier
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
and potential function (
1.2
). We assume that we are able to compute an approximate analytic center at low computational cost. Specifically, our algorithm relies on the availability of a
\nu
-analytic center, i.e. a point
x^{0}\in\mathsf{X}
such that

(4.1)

h(x)\geq h(x^{0})-\nu\qquad\forall x\in\mathsf{X}.

To obtain such a point
x^{0}
, one can apply interior point methods to the convex programming problem
\min_{x\in\mathsf{X}}h(x)
. Moreover, since
\nu\geq 1
we do not need to solve it with high precision, making the application of computationally cheap first-order method, such as
[
33
]
, an appealing choice for this preprocessing step.

4.1
Local properties

Given
x\in\mathsf{X}
, define the set of
feasible directions
as
\mathcal{F}_{x}=\{v\in\mathsf{E}|x+v\in\mathsf{X}\}.
Lemma
2.2
implies that

(4.2)

\mathcal{T}_{x}=\{v\in\mathsf{E}|{\mathbf{A}}v=0,\lVert v\rVert_{x}<1\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{x}.

Upon defining
d=[H(x)]^{1/2}v
for
v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}
, we obtain a point
d\in\mathbb{R}^{{\rm dim}(\mathsf{E})}
satisfying
{\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1/2}d=0
and
\lVert d\rVert=\lVert v\rVert_{x}
. Hence, for
x\in\mathsf{K}
, we can equivalently characterize the set
\mathcal{T}_{x}
as
\mathcal{T}_{x}=\{[H(x)]^{-1/2}d|{\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1/2}d=0,\lVert d\rVert<1\}
.

Our complexity analysis relies on the ability to control the behavior of the objective function along the set of feasible directions and with respect to the local norm.

Assumption 3

(Local smoothness)
.

f:\mathsf{E}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is continuously differentiable on
\mathsf{X}
and there exists a constant
M>0
such that for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
and
v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}
we have

(4.3)

f(x+v)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),v\rangle\leq\frac{M}{2}\lVert v\rVert_{x}^{2}.

Remark 4.1
.

If the set
\bar{\mathsf{X}}
is bounded, we have
\lambda_{\min}(H(x))\geq\sigma
for some
\sigma>0
. In this case, assuming
f
has an
M
-Lipschitz continuous gradient, the classical descent lemma
[
61
]
implies Assumption
3
. Indeed,

f(x+v)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),v\rangle\leq\frac{M}{2}\lVert v\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{M}{2\sigma}\lVert v\rVert_{x}^{2}.

\Diamond

Remark 4.2
.

We emphasize that the local Lipschitz smoothness condition (
4.3
) does not require global differentiability. Consider the composite non-smooth and non-convex model (
1.1
) on
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
, with
\varphi(s)=s
for
s\geq 0
. This means
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(x_{i}^{p})=\lVert x\rVert_{p}^{p}
for
p\in(0,1)
and
x\in\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
. As a concrete example for the smooth part of the problem let us consider the
L_{2}
-loss
\ell(x)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{N}x-\mathbf{p}\rVert^{2}
. This gives rise to the
L_{2}-L_{p}
minimization problem, an important optimization formulation arising in phase retrieval, mathematical statistics, signal processing and image recovery
[
39
,
40
,
27
,
55
]
. For
x\in\mathsf{K}_{\text{NN}}
, set
M=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{N}^{\ast}\mathbf{N})
, so that

\ell(x^{+})\leq\ell(x)+\langle\nabla\ell(x),x^{+}-x\rangle+\frac{M}{2}\lVert x^{+}-x\rVert^{2},

Since
t\mapsto t^{p}
is concave for
t>0
and
p\in(0,1)
, we have

(x_{i}^{+})^{p}\leq x^{p}_{i}+px_{i}^{p-1}(x^{+}_{i}-x_{i})\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Adding all these inequalities together, we immediately arrive at condition (
4.3
) in terms of the Euclidean norm. Over a bounded feasible set
\bar{\mathsf{X}}
, Remark
4.1
makes it clear that this implies Assumption
3
. At the same time,
f
is not differentiable at zero.
\Diamond

We emphasize that in Assumption
3
the constant

is in general either unknown or is a very conservative upper bound. Therefore, adaptive techniques should be used to estimate it and are likely to improve the practical performance of the method.

Considering
x\in\mathsf{X},v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}

Lemma 4.1

.

For all

4.2
Algorithm description and its complexity

Let
x\in\mathsf{X}
be given. Our first-order method employs a quadratic model
Q^{(1)}_{\mu}(x,v)
to compute a search direction
v_{\mu}(x)
, given by

(4.5)

v_{\mu}(x)\triangleq\argmin_{v\in\mathsf{E}:{\mathbf{A}}v=0}\left\{Q^{(1)}_{\mu}(x,v)\triangleq F_{\mu}(x)+\langle\nabla F_{\mu}(x),v\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\lVert v\rVert_{x}^{2}\right\}.

For the above problem, we have the following system of optimality conditions involving the dual variable
y_{\mu}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(4.6)

\displaystyle\nabla F_{\mu}(x)+H(x)v_{\mu}(x)-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

(4.7)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}v_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=0.

Since
H(x)\succ 0
for
x\in\mathsf{X}
, any standard solution method
[
64
]
can be applied for the above linear system.
Moreover, this system can be solved explicitly.
Indeed, since
H(x)\succ 0
for
x\in\mathsf{X}
, and
{\mathbf{A}}
has full column rank, the linear operator
{\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}
is invertible. Hence,
v_{\mu}(x)
is given explicitly as

v_{\mu}(x)=-([H(x)]^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}({\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast})^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1}-[H(x)]^{-1})\nabla F_{\mu}(x)\triangleq-\mathbf{S}_{x}\nabla F_{\mu}(x).

To give some intuition behind this expression, observe that we can give an alternative representation of
\mathbf{S}_{x}
as
\mathbf{S}_{x}v=[H(x)]^{-1/2}\Pi_{x}[H(x)]^{-1/2}v
, where

\Pi_{x}v\triangleq v-[H(x)]^{-1/2}{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}({\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast})^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1/2}v.

This shows that
\mathbf{S}_{x}
is just the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{x}
-orthogonal projection operator onto
\ker({\mathbf{A}}[H(x)]^{-1/2})
. Hence, we can always find a scalar
t>0
such that
tv_{\mu}(x)\in\mathsf{L}_{0}
and
\lVert tv_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}<1
. Any such scalar will be a suitable candidate for a step size. To determine an acceptable step-size, consider a point
x\in\mathsf{X}
, the search direction
v_{\mu}(x)
gives rise to a family of parameterized arcs
x^{+}(t)\triangleq x+tv_{\mu}(x)
, where
t\geq 0
. Our aim is to choose this step-size to ensure feasibility of the iterates and decrease of the potential. By (
2.12
) and (
4.7
), we know that
x^{+}(t)\in\mathsf{X}
for all
t\in I_{x,\mu}\triangleq[0,\frac{1}{\zeta(x,v_{\mu}(x))})
. Multiplying (
4.6
) by
v_{\mu}(x)
and using (
4.7
), we obtain

\langle\nabla F_{\mu}(x),v_{\mu}(x)\rangle=-\lVert v_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}^{2}
. Choosing
t\in I_{x,\mu}
, we bound

t^{2}\lVert v_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}^{2}\omega(t\zeta(x,v_{\mu}(x)))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:omega_upper_bound}}}{{\leq}}\frac{t^{2}\lVert v_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}^{2}}{2(1-t\zeta(x,v_{\mu}(x)))}.

Therefore, if
t\zeta(x,v_{\mu}(x))\leq 1/2
, we readily see from (
4.4
) that

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(x^{+}(t))-F_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle\leq-t\lVert v_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}^{2}+\frac{t^{2}M}{2}\lVert v_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}^{2}+\mu t^{2}\lVert v_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}^{2}

(4.8)

\displaystyle=-t\lVert v_{\mu}(x)\rVert_{x}^{2}\left(1-\frac{M+2\mu}{2}t\right)\triangleq-\eta_{x}(t).

The function
t\mapsto\eta_{x}(t)
is strictly concave with the unique maximum at
\frac{1}{M+2\mu}
, and two real roots at
t\in\left\{0,\frac{2}{M+2\mu}\right\}
.
Thus, maximizing the per-iteration decrease
\eta_{x}(t)
under the restriction
0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2\zeta(x,v_{\mu}(x))}
, we choose the step-size

\mathtt{t}_{\mu,M}(x)\triangleq\min\left\{\frac{1}{M+2\mu},\frac{1}{2\zeta(x,v_{\mu}(x))}\right\}.

This step-size rule, however, requires knowledge of the parameter
M
. To boost numerical performance, we employ a backtracking scheme in the spirit of
[
62
]
to estimate the constant
M
at each iteration. This procedure generates a sequence of positive numbers
(L_{k})_{k\geq 0}
for which the local Lipschitz smoothness condition (
4.3
) holds. More specifically, suppose that
x^{k}
is the current position of the algorithm with the corresponding initial local Lipschitz estimate
L_{k}
and
v^{k}=v_{\mu}(x^{k})
is the corresponding search direction. To determine the next iterate
x^{k+1}
, we iteratively try step-sizes
\alpha_{k}
of the form
\mathtt{t}_{\mu,2^{i_{k}}L_{k}}(x^{k})
for
i_{k}\geq 0
until the local smoothness condition (
4.3
) holds with
x=x^{k}
,
v=\alpha_{k}v^{k}
and local Lipschitz estimate
M=2^{i_{k}}L_{k}
, see (
4.11
). This process must terminate in finitely many steps, since when
2^{i_{k}}L_{k}\geq M
, inequality (
4.3
) with
M
changed to
2^{i_{k}}L_{k}
, i.e., (
4.11
), follows from Assumption
3
. Combining the search direction finding problem (
4.5
) with the just outlined backtracking strategy, yields an

Result:

(x^{k},y^{k},s^{k},L_{k})\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathsf{K}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}

Our main result on the iteration complexity of Algorithm
1
is the following Theorem, whose proof is given in Section
4.3
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let Assumptions
1
-
3
hold. Fix the error tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, the regularization parameter
\mu=\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}
, and some initial guess
L_{0}>0
for the Lipschitz constant. Let
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the trajectory generated by
\AHBA(\mu,\varepsilon,L_{0},x^{0})
, where
x^{0}
is a
\nu
-analytic center satisfying (
4.1
). Then the algorithm stops in no more than

(4.12)

\mathbb{K}_{I}(\varepsilon,x^{0})=\bigg\lceil 4(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\varepsilon)\frac{\nu^{2}(\max\{M,L_{0}\}+\varepsilon/\nu)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bigg\rceil

outer iterations, and the number of inner iterations is no more than
2(\mathbb{K}_{I}(\varepsilon,x^{0})+1)+\max\{\log_{2}(M/L_{0}),0\}
. Moreover, the last iterate obtained from
\AHBA(\mu,\varepsilon,L_{0},x^{0})
constitute a
2\varepsilon
-KKT point for problem (
Opt
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

Remark 4.3
.

The line-search process of finding the appropriate
i_{k}
is simple since only recalculating
z^{k}
is needed, and repeatedly solving problem (
4.9
) is not required. Furthermore, the sequence of constants
L_{k}
is allowed to decrease along subsequent iterations, which is achieved by the division by the constant factor 2 in the final updating step of each iteration. This potentially leads to longer steps and faster decrease of the potential.

\Diamond

Remark 4.4
.

Since
\nu\geq 1
,
f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})
is expected to be larger than
\varepsilon
, and the constant
M
is potentially large, we see that the main term in the complexity bound (
4.12
) is
O\left(\frac{M\nu^{2}(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=O(\frac{\nu^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, i.e. has the same dependence on
\varepsilon
as the standard complexity bounds
[
19
,
18
,
53
]
of first-order methods for non-convex problems under the standard Lipschitz-gradient assumption, which on bounded sets is subsumed by our Assumption
3
. Further, if the function
f
is quadratic, Assumption
3
holds with
M=0
and we can take
L_{0}=0
. In this case, the complexity bound (
4.12
) improves to
O\left(\frac{\nu(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon}\right)
.

Just like classical interior-point methods, the iteration complexity of
\AHBA
depends on the barrier parameter
\nu\geq 1
. For conic domains, the characterization of this barrier parameter has thus been an active research line.
[
44
]
demonstrated that for symmetric cones, the barrier parameter is equivalent to algebraic properties of the cone and identified it with the rank of the cone (see
[
35
]
for a definition of the rank of a symmetric cone). This deep analysis gives an exact characterization of the optimal barrier parameter for the most important conic domains in optimization. For
\mathsf{K}_{\text{NN}}
and
\mathsf{K}_{\text{SDP}}
, it is known that
\nu=n
is optimal, whereas for
\mathsf{K}_{\text{SOC}}
the optimal barrier parameter is
\nu=2
(and therefore independent of the ambient dimension
n
).

\Diamond

Connection with interior point flows on polytopes.

Consider
\bar{\mathsf{K}}=\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
, and
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{K}_{\text{NN}}\cap\mathsf{L}
. We are given a function
f:\bar{\mathsf{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
which is the restriction of a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. The canonical barrier for this setting is
h(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i})
, so that
H(x)=\diag[x_{1}^{-2},\ldots,x_{n}^{-2}]=\mathbf{X}^{-2}
for
x\in\mathsf{X}
. Applying our first-order method on this domain gives the search direction

v_{\mu}(x)=-\mathbf{S}_{x}\nabla F_{\mu}(x)=-\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{X}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}({\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}^{2}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X})\mathbf{X}\nabla F_{\mu}(x)
. This explicit formula yields various interesting connections between our approach and classical methods. For
{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}
, the feasible set
\mathsf{X}
reduces to the relative interior of the
(n-1)
-dimensional unit simplex. In this case, the vector field
v_{\mu}(\cdot)
simplifies further to

[v_{\mu}(x)]_{i}=[\mathbf{X}^{2}\nabla F_{\mu}(x)]_{i}-\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{2}}\sum_{j}[\mathbf{X}^{2}\nabla F_{\mu}(x)]_{j}\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

Observe that
v_{\mu}(x)\in(\mathbf{1}_{n})^{\bot}=\ker(\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top})
. For
f(x)=c^{\top}x
and
\mu=0
, we further obtain from this formula the search direction employed in
affine scaling
methods for linear programming
[
8
,
7
,
1
,
69
]
.

[
16
]
partly motivated their algorithm as a discretization of the Hessian-Riemannian gradient flows introduced in
[
4
]
and
[
15
]
. Heuristically, we can therefore interpret
\AHBA
as an Euler discretization (with non-monotone adaptive step-size policies) of the gradient-like flow
\dot{x}(t)=-\mathbf{S}_{x(t)}\nabla F_{\mu}(x(t))
, which resembles very much the class of dynamical systems introduced in
[
15
]
. This gives an immediate connection to a large class of interior point flows on polytopes, heavily studied in control theory
[
48
]
.

4.3
Proof of Theorem
4.2

Our proof proceeds in several steps. First, we show that procedure
\AHBA(\mu,\varepsilon,L_{0},x^{0})
produces points in
\mathsf{X}
, and, thus, is indeed an interior-point method. Next, we show that the line-search process of finding appropriate
L_{k}
in each iteration is finite, and estimate the total number of trials in this process. Then we enter the core of our analysis where we prove that if the stopping criterion does not hold at iteration
k
, i.e.
\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}\geq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\nu}
, then the objective
f
is decreased by a quantity
O(\varepsilon^{2})
, and, since the objective is globally lower bounded, we conclude that the method stops in at most
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
iterations. Finally, we show that when the stopping criterion holds, the method has generated an
\varepsilon
-KKT point.

4.3.1
Interior-point property of the iterates

By construction
x^{0}\in\mathsf{X}
. Proceeding inductively, let
x^{k}\in\mathsf{X}
be the
k
-th iterate of the algorithm, delivering the search direction
v^{k}=v_{\mu}(x^{k})
.
By eq. (
4.10
), the step-size
\alpha_{k}
satisfies
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})}
, and, hence,
\alpha_{k}\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})\leq 1/2
for all
k\geq 0
.
Thus, by (
2.12
)
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\alpha_{k}v^{k}\in\mathsf{K}
. Since, by (
4.9
),
{\mathbf{A}}v^{k}=0
, we have that
x^{k+1}\in\mathsf{L}
. Thus,
x^{k+1}\in\mathsf{K}\cap\mathsf{L}=\mathsf{X}
. By induction, we conclude that
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathsf{X}
.

4.3.2
Bounding the number of backtracking steps

Let us fix iteration
k
. Since the sequence
2^{i_{k}}L_{k}
is increasing as
i_{k}
is increasing, and Assumption
3
holds, we know that when
2^{i_{k}}L_{k}\geq\max\{M,L_{k}\}
, the line-search process for sure stops since inequality (
4.11
) holds.
Hence,
2^{i_{k}}L_{k}\leq 2\max\{M,L_{k}\}
must be the case, and, consequently,
L_{k+1}=2^{i_{k}-1}L_{k}\leq\max\{M,L_{k}\}
, which, by induction, gives
L_{k+1}\leq\bar{M}\triangleq\max\{M,L_{0}\}
.
At the same time,
\log_{2}\left(\frac{L_{k+1}}{L_{k}}\right)=i_{k}-1
,
\forall k\geq 0
. Let
N(k)
denote the number of inner line-search iterations up to the
k-
th iteration of
\AHBA(\mu,\varepsilon,L_{0},x^{0})
. Then, using that
L_{k+1}\leq\bar{M}=\max\{M,L_{0}\}
,

\displaystyle N(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(i_{j}+1)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(\log_{2}(L_{j+1}/L_{j})+2)\leq 2(k+1)+\max\{\log_{2}(M/L_{0}),0\}.

This shows that on average the inner loop ends after two trials.

4.3.3
Per-iteration analysis and a bound for the number of iterations

Let us fix iteration counter
k
. Since
L_{k+1}=2^{i_{k}-1}L_{k}
, the step-size (
4.10
) reads as
\alpha_{k}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2L_{k+1}+2\mu},\frac{1}{2\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})}\right\}
. Hence,
\alpha_{k}\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})\leq 1/2
, and (
4.8
) with the identification
t=\alpha_{k}=\mathtt{t}_{\mu,2L_{k+1}}(x^{k})
,
M=2L_{k+1}
,
x=x^{k}
,
v_{\mu}(x^{k})\triangleq v^{k}
gives:

(4.13)

F_{\mu}(x^{k+1})-F_{\mu}(x^{k})\leq-\alpha_{k}\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}^{2}\left(1-(L_{k+1}+\mu)\alpha_{k}\right)\leq-\frac{\alpha_{k}\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}^{2}}{2},

where we used that
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2(L_{k+1}+\mu)}
in the last inequality.
Substituting into (
4.13
) the two possible values of the step-size
\alpha_{k}
in (
4.10
) gives

(4.14)

F_{\mu}(x^{k+1})-F_{\mu}(x^{k})\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\frac{\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}^{2}}{4(L_{k+1}+\mu)}&\text{if\lx@nobreakspace}\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{2(L_{k+1}+\mu)}\\

Recalling

(see section

), we obtain that

(4.15)

F_{\mu}(x^{k+1})-F_{\mu}(x^{k})\leq-\frac{\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}}{4}\min\left\{1,\frac{\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}}{\bar{M}+\mu}\right\}=-\delta_{k}.

Rearranging and summing these inequalities for
k
from

to

gives

\displaystyle K\min_{k=0\ldots,K-1}\delta_{k}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\delta_{k}\leq F_{\mu}(x^{0})-F_{\mu}(x^{K})

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:potential}}}{{=}}f(x^{0})-f(x^{K})+\mu(h(x^{0})-h(x^{K}))\leq f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\varepsilon,

where we used that, by the assumptions of Theorem

,

is a
\nu
-analytic center defined in (

) and
\mu=\varepsilon/\nu
, implying that

.
Thus, up to passing to a subsequence,

, and consequently

as

. This shows that the stopping criterion in Algorithm

is achievable.

Assume now that the stopping criterion
\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}
does not hold for

. Then, for all

it holds that

\delta_{k}\geq\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{4\nu},\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\nu^{2}(\bar{M}+\mu)}\right\}
.
Together with the parameter coupling

, it follows from (

) that

K\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\nu^{2}(\bar{M}+\varepsilon/\nu)}=K\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{4\nu},\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\nu^{2}(\bar{M}+\varepsilon/\nu)}\right\}\leq f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\varepsilon.

Hence, recalling that

,

K\leq 4(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{\nu^{2}(\max\{M,L_{0}\}+\varepsilon/\nu)}{\varepsilon^{2}},

i.e., the algorithm stops for sure after no more than this number of iterations. This, combined with the bound for the number of inner steps in Section

.

4.3.4
Generating
\varepsilon
-KKT point

To finish the proof of Theorem
4.2
, we now show that when Algorithm
1
stops for the first time, it returns a
2\varepsilon
-KKT point of (

) according to Definition
3.1
.

Let the stopping criterion hold at iteration
k
. By the optimality condition (
4.6
) and the definition of the potential (
1.2
), we have

(4.17)

\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}+\mu\nabla h(x^{k})=-H(x^{k})v^{k}\Leftrightarrow[H(x^{k})]^{-1}\left(\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}+\mu\nabla h(x^{k})\right)=-v^{k}.

Denoting
g^{k}\triangleq-\mu\nabla h(x^{k})
, multiplying both equations, and using the stopping criterion
\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}
, we conclude

(4.18)

\lVert\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}-g^{k}\rVert^{\ast}_{x^{k}}=\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}.

Whence, setting
s^{k}\triangleq\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}\in\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
, we get, by the definition of the dual norm,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}>\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}

\displaystyle=\lVert s^{k}-g^{k}\rVert^{\ast}_{x^{k}}

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\lVert s^{k}-g^{k}\rVert_{[H(x^{k})]^{-1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:relations}}}{{=}}\lVert s^{k}-g^{k}\rVert_{\nabla^{2}h_{\ast}(-\nabla h(x^{k}))}

\displaystyle=\lVert s^{k}-g^{k}\rVert_{\nabla^{2}h_{\ast}(\frac{1}{\mu}g^{k})}=\mu\lVert s^{k}-g^{k}\rVert_{\nabla^{2}h_{\ast}(g^{k})},

where in the last equality we used that since
h_{\ast}\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K}^{\ast})
, by (
A.3
),

\nabla^{2}h_{\ast}(\frac{1}{\mu}g^{k})=\mu^{2}\nabla^{2}h_{\ast}(g^{k})
.
Thus, we arrive at

(4.21)

\displaystyle\lVert s^{k}-g^{k}\rVert_{\nabla^{2}h_{\ast}(g^{k})}=\frac{\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}}{\mu}<\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu\nu}=1,

where in the last equality we used that, by the assumptions of Theorem
4.2
,
\mu=\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}
.
Thus, since, by (
A.1
),
g^{k}=-\mu\nabla h(x^{k})\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast}
, we get that
s^{k}\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast}
. By construction,
x^{k}\in\mathsf{K}
and
{\mathbf{A}}x^{k}=b
. Thus, (
3.1
) holds.
Furthermore,
\|\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}-s^{k}\|=0\leq 2\varepsilon
, meaning that (
3.2
) holds.
Finally, since
(x^{k},s^{k})\in\mathsf{K}\times\mathsf{K}^{\ast}
, we see

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle s^{k},x^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle s^{k}-g^{k},x^{k}\rangle+\langle g^{k},x^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\lVert s^{k}-g^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}^{\ast}\cdot\lVert x^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}-\mu\langle\nabla h(x^{k}),x^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle<\sqrt{\nu}\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}+\varepsilon\leq 2\varepsilon,

4.4
Discussion

Strengthened KKT condition.

For
\bar{\mathsf{K}}=\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
,
[
46
]
consider a first-order potential reduction method employing the standard log-barrier
h(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x_{i})
using a trust-region subproblem for obtaining the search direction. For
x\in\mathsf{K}_{\text{NN}}
, we have
\nabla h(x)=[-x_{1}^{-1},\ldots,-x_{n}^{-1}]^{\top}
,
H(x)=\diag[x_{1}^{-2},\ldots,x_{n}^{-2}]=\mathbf{X}^{-2}
. Combining (
4.17
), the information
[H(x^{k})]^{-1/2}\nabla h(x^{k})=-\mathbf{1}_{n},\;\nu=n
, and the stopping criterion of Algorithm
1
at iteration
k
, saying that
\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}
, we see

\displaystyle\lVert H(x^{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k})-\mu\mathbf{1}_{n}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert H(x^{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k})-\mu\mathbf{1}_{n}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert-H(x^{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}v^{k}\rVert<\frac{\varepsilon}{n}.

Therefore, since
\mu=\varepsilon/n
and
s^{k}=\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast}_{\text{NN}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, we obtain from the triangle inequality

0<\lVert\mathbf{X}^{k}s^{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert H(x^{k})^{-1/2}s^{k}-\mu\mathbf{1}_{n}\rVert_{\infty}+\mu\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{n}.

By Remark
4.4
, these inequalities are achieved after
O\left(\frac{Mn^{2}(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
iterations of
\AHBA
, and they are seen to be by the factor
\frac{1}{n}
sharper than the complementarity measure employed in
[
46
]
. Conversely, in order to attain an approximate KKT point with the same strength as in
[
46
]
, the above calculations suggest that we can weaken our tolerance from
\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon\cdot n
, which results in an overall iteration complexity of

O\left(\frac{M(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
, and a complementarity measure
\lVert\mathbf{X}^{k}s^{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2\varepsilon
. Thus, in the particular case of non-negativity constraints our general algorithm is able to obtain results similar to
[
46
]
, but under weaker assumptions. At the same time, our algorithm ensures a stronger measure of complementarity. Indeed, our algorithm guarantees that
x^{k}\in\mathsf{K}_{\text{NN}},s^{k}=\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k}\in\mathsf{K}_{\text{NN}}
, i.e.,
x^{k},s^{k}\geq 0
, and approximate complementary
0\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}\rvert=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}\leq 2\varepsilon
after
O\left(\frac{Mn^{2}(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
iterations, which is stronger than
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}\rvert\leq 2\varepsilon
guaranteed by
[
46
]
. Indeed,
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}\rvert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}\rvert\leq n\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}\rvert
, and both equalities are achievable. Moreover, to match our stronger guarantee, one has to change
\varepsilon\to\varepsilon/n
in the complexity bound of
[
46
]
, which leads to the same
O\left(\frac{Mn^{2}(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
complexity bound. Besides this important insights, our algorithm is designed for general cones, rather than only for
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
. Therefore, we provide a unified approach for essentially all conic domains of relevance in optimization. Finally, our method does not rely on the trust-region techniques as in
[
46
]
that may slow down the convergence in practice since the radius of the trust region is no grater than
O(\varepsilon)
leading to short steps.

Exploiting problem structure.

In (
4.14
) we can clearly observe the benefit of the use of
\nu
-SSB in our algorithm, whenever
\mathsf{K}
is a symmetric cone. Indeed, when
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{2\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})}
, the per-iteration decrease of the potential is
\frac{\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}^{2}}{4\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})}\geq\frac{\varepsilon\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}}{4\nu\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})}
which may be large if
\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})=\sigma_{x^{k}}(-v^{k})\ll\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}
.

The role of the potential function.

Next, we discuss more explicitly, how the algorithm and complexity bounds depend on the parameter
\mu
. The first observation is that from (
4.21
), to guarantee that
s^{k}\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast}
, we need the stopping criterion to be
\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}<\mu
, which by (
4.22
) leads to the error
2\mu\nu
in the complementarity conditions. From the analysis following equation (
4.16
), we have that

K\frac{\mu^{2}}{4(\bar{M}+\mu)}=K\min\left\{\frac{\mu}{4},\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\ (\bar{M}+\mu)}\right\}\leq f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\mu\nu.

Whence, recalling that
\bar{M}=\max\{M,L_{0}\}
,

K\leq 4(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\mu\nu)\cdot\frac{\max\{M,L_{0}\}+\mu}{\mu^{2}}.

Thus, we see that after
O(\mu^{-2})
iterations the algorithm finds a
(2\mu\nu)
-KKT point, and if
\mu\to 0
, we have convergence to a KKT point, but the complexity bound tends to infinity and becomes non-informative. At the same time, as it is seen from (
4.9
), when
\mu\to 0
, the algorithm itself converges to a preconditioned gradient method since
F_{\mu}(x)=f(x)+\mu h(x)\to f(x)
.
We also see from the above explicit expressions in terms of
\mu
that the design of the algorithm requires careful balance between the desired accuracy of the approximate KKT point expressed mainly by the complementarity condition, stopping criterion, and complexity. Moreover, the step-size should be also taken carefully to ensure the feasibility of the iterates, and the standard for first-order methods step-size
1/M
may not work.

5
A second-order Hessian-Barrier Algorithm

In this section we introduce a second-order potential reduction method for problem (
Opt
) under the assumption that the second-order Taylor expansion of
f
on the set of feasible directions
\mathcal{T}_{x}
defined in (

Assumption 4

(Local second-order smoothness)
.

f:\mathsf{E}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathsf{X}
and there exists a constant
M>0
such that, for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
and

(5.1)

\lVert\nabla f(x+v)-\nabla f(x)-\nabla^{2}f(x)v\rVert^{\ast}_{x}\leq\frac{M}{2}\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{x}.

A sufficient condition for (
5.1
) is the following local counterpart of the global Lipschitz condition on
the Hessian of
f
:

(5.2)

(\forall x\in\mathsf{X})(\forall u,v\in\mathcal{F}_{x}):\;\lVert\nabla^{2}f(x+u)-\nabla^{2}f(x+v)\rVert_{\text{op},x}\leq M\lVert u-v\rVert_{x},

where

\lVert{\mathbf{B}}\rVert_{\text{op},x}\triangleq\sup_{u:\lVert u\rVert_{x}\leq 1}\left\{\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{B}}u\rVert_{x}^{\ast}}{\lVert u\rVert_{x}}\right\}
is the induced operator norm for a linear operator
{\mathbf{B}}:\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{E}^{\ast}
. Indeed, this condition implies (
5.1
):

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla f(x+v)-\nabla f(x)-\nabla^{2}f(x)v\rVert^{\ast}_{x}=\lVert\int_{0}^{1}(\nabla^{2}f(x+tv)-\nabla^{2}f(x))v\>dt\rVert^{\ast}_{x}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\lVert\nabla^{2}f(x+tv)-\nabla^{2}f(x)\rVert_{\text{op},x}\cdot\lVert v\rVert_{x}\>dt\leq\frac{M}{2}\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{x}.

Further, (

Indeed, for all
x\in\mathsf{X}
and
v\in\mathcal{T}_{x}

\displaystyle\lvert f(x+v)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),v\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)v,v\rangle\rvert=\lvert\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(x+tv)-\nabla f(x)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}f(x)v,v\rangle\>dt\rvert

5.2
Proof of Theorem

The main steps of the proof are similar to the analysis of Algorithm
1
. We start by showing the feasibility of the iterates and correctness of the line-search process. Next, we analyze the per-iteration decrease of

and

and show that if the stopping criterion does not hold at iteration

. From this, since the objective is globally lower bounded, we conclude that the algorithm stops in
O(\varepsilon^{-3/2})
iterations. Finally, we show that when the stopping criterion holds, the primal-dual pair
(x^{k}
,
y^{k-1})
resulting from solving the cubic subproblem (
5.16
) yields a dual slack variable

such that this triple constitutes an second-order KKT point.

5.2.1
Interior point property of the iterates

By construction
x^{0}\in\mathsf{X}
. Proceeding inductively, let
x^{k}\in\mathsf{X}
be the
k
-th iterate of the algorithm, with the search direction

. By eq. (
5.17

\alpha_{k}
satisfies
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})}

\alpha_{k}\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})\leq 1/2

for all

.

5.2.2
Bounding the number of backtracking steps

To bound the number of cycles involved in the line-search process for finding appropriate constants

, we proceed as in Section
4.3.2
.
Let us fix an iteration

. The sequence

is increasing, and Assumption

This implies (
5.3

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L_{k}\leq 2\bar{M},\nu\geq 1}}{{\geq}}\frac{1}{96}\min\left\{\frac{L_{k}\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{8\bar{M}}\nu^{3/2}},\frac{\varepsilon^{3/2}}{2L_{k}^{1/2}\nu^{3/2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L_{k}\leq 2\bar{M},L_{k}\geq 144\varepsilon}}{{\geq}}\frac{1}{96}\min\left\{\frac{(144\varepsilon)\cdot\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{8\bar{M}}\nu^{3/2}},\frac{\varepsilon^{3/2}}{\sqrt{8\bar{M}}\nu^{3/2}}\right\}=\frac{\varepsilon^{3/2}}{192\nu^{3/2}\sqrt{2\bar{M}}},

Thus, from (
5.26
).

\displaystyle K\frac{\varepsilon^{3/2}}{192\nu^{3/2}\sqrt{2\bar{M}}}\leq f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\varepsilon.

Hence, reacalling that

,

,
i.e. the algorithm stops for sure after no more than this number of iterations. This, combined with the bound for the number of inner steps in Section

, proves the first statement of Theorem
5.3

Discussion

Strengthened KKT condition.

As in Section
4.4
, our aim in this section is to compare our result with those available in the contemporary literature. We therefore onsider the special case
\bar{\mathsf{K}}=\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}

5.16

\displaystyle\nabla f(x^{k-1})+\mu\nabla h(x^{k-1})+\nabla^{2}f(x^{k-1})v^{k-1}-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k-1}=-\frac{L_{k-1}}{2}\lVert v^{k-1}\rVert_{x^{k-1}}H(x^{k-1})v^{k-1}.

\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k-1}=s^{k}\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast}_{\text{NN}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}

\nabla^{2}f(x^{k})+H(x^{k})\sqrt{\frac{M\varepsilon}{n}}\succeq 0\;\;\text{on}\;\;\mathsf{L}_{0}.

By Remark

, if we change
\varepsilon\to\tilde{\varepsilon}=\min\{n\varepsilon,n\varepsilon/M\}
, we obtain from these inequalities that in

O\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}n^{3/2}(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{3/2}}\right)=O\left(\frac{M^{2}(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon^{3/2}}\right)
iterations
\SAHBA
guarantees

\displaystyle x^{k}>0,\;\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k-1}>0

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{X}^{k}(\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k-1})\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}}{n}\leq\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(x^{k})+H(x^{k})\sqrt{\varepsilon}\succeq\nabla^{2}f(x^{k})+H(x^{k})\sqrt{\frac{M\tilde{\varepsilon}}{n}}\succeq 0\;\;\text{on}\;\;\mathsf{L}_{0}.

In contrast, the second-order algorithm of
[
46
]
requires an additional assumption that the level set of the objective
f
is bounded in the
L_{\infty}
-norm, gives a slightly worse guarantee
\nabla f(x^{k})-{\mathbf{A}}^{\ast}y^{k-1}>-\varepsilon
, and requires a larger number of iterations
O\left(\frac{\max\{M,R\}^{7/2}(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X}))}{\varepsilon^{3/2}}\right)
(
R
denoting the
L_{\infty}
upper bound of the level set corresponding to
x^{0}
). We also can repeat the same remark as in Section
4.4
that our measure of complementarity
0\leq\langle s^{k},x^{k}\rangle\leq\varepsilon
is stronger than
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert x_{i}^{k}s_{i}^{k}\rvert
used in
[
46
,
65
]
. Furthermore, our algorithm is applicable to general cones admitting an efficient barrier setup, rather than only for
\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{\text{NN}}
. For more general cones we can not use the coupling
H(x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbf{X}
, which was seen to be very helpful in the derivations of the bound (
5.35
) above. Thus, to deal with general cones, we had to find and exploit suitable properties of the barrier class
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}(\mathsf{K})
and develop a new analysis technique that works for general, potentially non-symmetric, cones. Finally, our method does not rely on the trust-region techniques as in
[
46
]
that may slow down the convergence in practice since the radius of the trust region is no grater than
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
leading to short steps.

Exploiting problem structure.

We note that in (
5.24
) we can clearly observe the benefit of the use of
\nu
-SSB in our algorithm. When
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{2\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})}
, the per-iteration decrease of the potential is
\frac{L_{k}\lVert v^{k}\rVert^{3}_{x^{k}}}{96(\zeta(x^{k},v^{k}))^{2}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon L_{k}}\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}^{2}}{96\sqrt{4\nu}(\zeta(x^{k},v^{k}))^{2}}
which may be large if
\zeta(x^{k},v^{k})=\sigma_{x^{k}}(-v^{k})\ll\lVert v^{k}\rVert_{x^{k}}
.

Dependence on parameters.

Next, we discuss more explicitly, how the algorithm and complexity bounds depend on the parameter
\mu
. The first observation is that from (
5.30
), to guarantee that
s^{k}\in\mathsf{K}^{\ast}
, we need the stopping criterion to be
\lVert v^{k-1}\rVert_{x^{k-1}}<\Delta_{k-1}=\sqrt{\mu/L_{k-1}}
, which by (
5.31
) leads to the error
4\mu\nu
in the complementarity conditions and by (
5.32
) leads to the error
\sqrt{\mu/\bar{M}}
in the second-order condition. From the analysis following equation (
5.24
), we have that

K\frac{\mu^{3/2}}{24\sqrt{\bar{M}}}\leq f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\mu\nu.

Whence, recalling that
\bar{M}=\max\{M,M_{0}\}
,

K\leq 24(f(x^{0})-f_{\min}(\mathsf{X})+\mu\nu)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\max\{M,M_{0}\}}}{\mu^{3/2}}.

Thus, we see that after
O(\mu^{-3/2})
iterations the algorithm finds a
(4\mu\nu,\mu/\bar{M})
-KKT point, and if
\mu\to 0
, we have convergence to a KKT point, but the complexity bound tends to infinity and becomes non-informative. At the same time, as it is seen from (
5.16
), when
\mu\to 0
, the algorithm resembles a cubic-regularized Newton method, but with the regularization with the cube of the local norm. We also see from the above explicit expressions in terms of
\mu
that the design of the algorithm requires careful balance between the desired accuracy of the approximate KKT point expressed mainly by the complementarity conditions, stopping criterion, and complexity. Moreover, the step-size must be selected carefully to ensure the feasibility of the iterates.

6
Conclusion

We derived Hessian-barrier algorithms based on first- and second-order information on the objective
f
. We performed a detailed analysis of their worst-case iteration complexity in order to find a suitably defined approximate KKT point. Under weak regularity assumptions and in presence of general conic constraints, our Hessian-barrier algorithms share the best known complexity rates in the literature for first- and second-order approximate KKT points. Our methods are characterized by a decomposition approach of the feasible set which leads to numerically efficient subproblems at each their iteration. Several open questions for the future remain. First, our iterations assume that the subproblems are solved exactly, and for practical reasons this should be relaxed. Second, we mentioned that
\AHBA
can be interpreted as a discretization of the Hessian-barrier gradient system
[
4
]
, but the exact relationship is not explored yet. This, however, could be an important step towards understanding acceleration techniques of
\AHBA
, akin to accelerated methods for the cubic regularized Newton method. Furthermore, the cubic-regularized version has no corresponding continuous-time version yet. It will be very interesting to investigate this question further. Additionally, the question of convergence of the trajectory
(x^{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by either scheme is open. Another interesting direction for future research would be to allow for higher-order Taylor expansions in the subproblems in order to boost convergence speed further, similar to
[
25
]
.
