Scaling symmetries and canonoid transformations in Hamiltonian systems
R. Azuaje, A. Bravetti

Abstract
We investigate various types of symmetries and their mutual relationships in Hamiltonian systems defined on manifolds with different geometric structures: symplectic, cosymplectic, contact and cocontact. In each case we pay special attention to non-standard (non-canonical) symmetries, in particular scaling symmetries and canonoid transformations, as they provide new interesting tools for the qualitative study of these systems. Our main results are the characterizations of these non-standard symmetries and the analysis of their relation with conserved (or dissipated) quantities.



Keywords:
Hamiltonian systems, generalized Noether’s theorem, scaling symmetries, cosymplectic geometry, contact geometry

1
Introduction

Symmetry methods are arguably among the most important and most venerable tools
in the study of physical systems
[
1
]
. One of the
consequences of the existence of symmetries is their association with conserved quantities given by the celebrated theorem by Noether. One can derive Noether’s theorem either in its original context, the variational formulation of mechanics and field theory, or using the corresponding geometric re-formulation
[
2
,
3
]
.
In the standard geometric formulation of mechanics, one usually has a symplectic manifold and a Hamiltonian dynamics defined on such manifold. In this context one can define
Noether symmetries
as symplectic (locally-Hamiltonian) vector fields that preserve the Hamiltonian function defining the dynamics.
Then Noether’s result is given automatically (at least locally) by the anti-symmetry of the symplectic form
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

There are several generalizations of Noether’s theorem.
On the one hand, one can consider a symplectic Hamiltonian system admitting an action of a symmetry group that is Hamiltonian and derive a more general version of Noether’s theorem, one that includes “several conserved quantities” in one map, namely, the momentum map
[
8
,
9
]
.
On the other hand, one can consider different types of Hamiltonian systems.
For instance, time-dependent Hamiltonian systems
are best described geometrically in the context of cosymplectic geometry
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
; some dissipative and thermostatted mechanical systems are more naturally incorporated within the context of contact geometry
[
13
,
12
,
14
,
15
]
. More recently, cocontact
Hamiltonian systems have been studied, which generalize both cosymplectic and contact systems at once and allow for a nice geometric analysis of time-dependent contact Hamiltonian
dynamics
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
In all these contexts one can think of appropriate versions
of Noether’s theorem
[
4
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
Finally, one may also generalize the concept of standard Noether symmetries and the related theorem
(see e.g.
[
25
,
23
,
22
]
).
In particular, scaling symmetries and canonoid transformations are two natural
and most important generalizations of standard Noether symmetries and canonical transformations respectively.
Scaling symmetries have long played a special role in the analysis of mathematical and physical phenomena
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
Moreover, a generalized Noether theorem for scaling symmetries has been found recently in the symplectic case
[
29
]
and it has been argued in
[
22
]
that the contact context may be more appropriate in order to fully understand the extent of the theorem.
Remarkably, scaling symmetries are also central to obtain the intrinsic description of the universe given in the theory of “shape dynamics”
[
30
,
31
,
28
]
. For all these reasons there has been a surge of interest to study them from various perspectives (see also
[
32
]
).
Canonoid transformations are an important generalization of standard canonical transformations, as they also preserve the Hamiltonian nature of the system. They have been defined first in
[
33
]
. Although they are far less famous than their canonical counterparts, they have been studied thoroughly in
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
for the symplectic case,
in
[
41
]
for the Poisson case, and more recently in
[
42
]
for the cosymplectic, contact and cocontact cases.

In this work, we consider several possible generalizations of standard symplectic Hamiltonian systems, namely, cosymplectic, contact and cocontact systems, and define both scaling symmetries and canonoid transformations on each category. Then we provide some useful characterizations of these symmetries,
we study their mutual relationships and in each case we derive Noether-like theorems that associate invariant or dissipated quantities to these symmetries.

To make the paper as clear and self-contained as possible,
we start in Section
2
with a review of the known results and definitions
in the symplectic case.
Then in the ensuing sections we follow the scheme detailed in Section
2
but we extend all the definitions and results to the cosymplectic (Section
3
), contact (Section
4
), and cocontact (Section
5
) case respectively.
All the relevant definitions for these classes of systems are given in Appendix
A
.
Finally, in Section
6
we present the conclusions and outline some possible directions for future work. Throughout the paper we use Einstein’s summation convention (i.e., a summation over repeated indices is assumed).

2
Symmetries for symplectic Hamiltonian systems

In this section we review some known definitions and results for symplectic Hamiltonian systems in order to set the stage and the notation for the results that will be presented in the next sections (we refer to Appendix
A
when needed for further more basic definitions and properties).

Let
(M,\omega,H)
be a symplectic Hamiltonian system (see Appendix
A
).

Definition 1
.

A smooth vector field
V
on
M
is called
an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
if
L_{V}\omega=L_{V}H=0
.

Note that this type of symmetries could be also naturally called
Noether symmetries
, as they are the symmetries normally
involved in the basic version of Noether’s theorem.
Indeed, Noether’s theorem reads (see
[
4
,
22
]
for the proof and
[
41
,
43
]
for analogous results on Poisson manifolds):

Theorem 1
.

i)

If
f
is a constant of motion of
(M,\omega,H)
, then its associated Hamiltonian vector field

X_{f}
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
.

Reciprocally,

ii)

if
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
, then there is a (possibly-local) function
f
such that
V=X_{f}
and
f

is a constant of motion.

Following the language employed in
[
44
,
38
,
23
]
, we may consider the following more general definition of symmetry of a dynamics:

Definition 2
.

A smooth vector field
W
on
M
is called
an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of a dynamics
X
if
[W,X]=0
.

Clearly, when we restrict the above definition to the case in which the dynamics is given by a symplectic Hamiltonian system, we obtain

Definition 3
.

A smooth vector field
W
on
M
is called
an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
if
[W,X_{H}]=0
.

From the fact that the assignment
f\mapsto X_{f}
is a Lie algebra
antihomomorphism between the Lie algebras
(C^{\infty}(M),\{,\})

and
(\mathfrak{X}(M),[,])
, i.e.,
X_{\{f,g\}}=-[X_{f},X_{g}]
, (see Appendix
A
), we have the following result:

Proposition 1
.

Every infinitesimal symmetry is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry.

Proof.

Let
V
be an infinitesimal symmetry, then
V=X_{f}
for some constant of motion
f
. We have

[X_{f},X_{H}]=X_{\{H,f\}}=0

(1)

since
f
is a constant of motion. So we conclude that
V
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry.
∎

The converse statement is not true.
Indeed, if
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is such that
L_{X}H=\lambda_{1}H
and

L_{X}\omega=\lambda_{2}\omega
for some
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
then
[X,X_{H}]=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})X_{H}
(cf. Proposition 1 in
[
22
]
, but beware that in this reference infinitesimal symmetries are called Noether symmetries);
so for the case
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}\neq 0
we have that
X
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry but not an infinitesimal symmetry.
This is the case of scaling symmetries of degree 1, as we are now going to prove.
We start by providing the general definition of a scaling symmetry in the symplectic case, which reads (see also
[
32
]
):

Definition 4
.

X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is a
scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for the Hamiltonian system
(M,\omega,H)
if

i)

L_{X}\omega=\omega

and

ii)

L_{X}H=\Lambda H
.

From the first condition in their definition, scaling symmetries are not Hamiltonian vector fields (this comes from the fact that
L_{X_{f}}\omega=0
for any function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
)
and thus they are not infinitesimal symmetries.
Moreover, if
X
is a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
,
then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
. Therefore we conclude that
scaling symmetries of degree 1 are infinitesimal dynamical symmetries which are not infinitesimal symmetries.

It is well known that infinitesimal symmetries are related to one-parameter groups of canonical transformations.
To recall this, we start with a geometrical definition of canonical transformations
[
5
,
42
]
.

Definition 5
.

A canonical transformation
for the symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
is a possibly-local diffeomorphism
F
on
M
such that
F^{*}\omega=\omega
.

Given a diffeomorphism
F:M\longrightarrow M
, we know that
F^{*}\omega
is a symplectic structure on
M
, so around any point
p\in M
there are local coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n})
such that

F^{*}\omega=dQ^{i}\wedge dP_{i}.

(2)

If we think of
F
locally as a coordinate transformation
(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})\longmapsto(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n})
,
then
F
is a canonical transformation if and only if

\{Q^{i},Q^{j}\}=\{P_{i},P_{j}\}=0\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\{Q^{i},P_{j}\}=\delta^{i}_{j}.

(3)

Let
V
be a smooth vector field on
M
and
\varphi
its flow (for a brief review of flows on smooth manifolds see
[
4
]
).
By definition,
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
if and only if
\omega
and
H
are invariant under the flow
\varphi
of
V
(
\varphi_{s}^{*}\omega=\omega
and
\varphi_{s}^{*}H=H
), so we have the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

If
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
,
then the flow of
V
defines a one-parameter group of canonical transformations
that leave the Hamiltonian invariant;
reciprocally, if we have a one-parameter group of canonical transformations that leave the Hamiltonian
H
invariant, then the infinitesimal generator of the flow defined by such one-parameter group is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
.

Similarly to the above result, in
[
38
]
one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations are studied by using their infinitesimal generators.
Classically a canonoid transformation is defined as a coordinate transformation preserving the Hamiltonian form of the equations of motion
[
45
,
34
,
40
]
. For our purposes it is more convenient to follow the geometric definition presented in
[
36
,
42
,
38
]
.

Definition 6
.

We say that a possibly-local diffeomorphism
F:M\longrightarrow M
is
a canonoid transformation
for the Hamiltonian system
(M,\omega,H)
if there exists a function
K\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner F^{*}\omega=dK.

(4)

In general, canonoid transformations do not preserve the Poisson bracket, so that if we think of
F
locally as a coordinate transformation

(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})\longmapsto(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n}),

then the new coordinates do not necessarily satisfy (
3
).
Nevertheless, the equations of motion in the coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n})
read

\dot{Q^{i}}=\frac{\partial K}{\partial P_{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{P_{i}}=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q^{i}}\,.

(5)

Clearly, every canonical transformation is also (trivially) a canonoid transformation.
However, the converse statement is not true. Indeed, we can find examples of canonoid transformations that are not canonical
in Refs.
[
34
,
36
]
.

Now let us focus on the relationship between canonoid transformations, their infinitesimal generators and the associated constants of motion.
Let
X
be a smooth vector field on
M
and
\varphi
its flow.

X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if there exists a family
\{K_{s}\}
of functions on
M
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\omega=dK_{s},

(6)

which is equivalent to
d(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\omega)=0
, so
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if

L_{X_{H}}\varphi_{s}^{*}\omega=0.

(7)

By taking
\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}
in equation (
7
),
we have that
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if

L_{X_{H}}L_{X}\omega=0,

(8)

which is equivalent to the fact that there exists (at least locally) a function
K
on
M
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\omega=dK.

(9)

We have that the function
K
in equation (
9
) is a constant of motion
[
38
]
.
Moreover, the following theorem provides a useful characterization of the infinitesimal generators of
one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations
(see
[
38
]
for the proof and
[
41
]
for an analogous statement in the Poisson case).

Theorem 2
.

X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\omega,H)
if and only if
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field. In this case its Hamiltonian function is
L_{X}H-K
, where
K
is given in equation (
9
).

In the remaining of this section we comment on two direct consequences of Theorem
2
that are of special interest for us.

Corollary 1
.

Every scaling symmetry is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.

Proof.

If
X
is a scaling symmetry for
(M,\omega,H)
, then
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field.
Indeed, if
X
is a scaling symmetry, then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
(where
\Lambda
is the degree of
X
) is the Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian function
(\Lambda-1)H
.
We can further check that in this case
K=H
, since, by equation (
9
), we must have
(\Lambda-1)H=\Lambda H-H=L_{X}H-K
.
∎

Infinitesimal dynamical symmetries are not necessarily scaling symmetries. Nevertheless, we can directly prove an analogous statement.

Corollary 2
.

Every infinitesimal dynamical symmetry
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.

Proof.

If
X
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\omega,H)
, then
[X,X_{H}]=0
is a Hamiltonian vector field with constant Hamiltonian function
c
.
Therefore, according to Theorem
2
,
X
is the generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.
∎

In addition, we have that for each
s
the new Hamiltonian function is
K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
. Indeed, we know that
X_{H}
is invariant under the flow
\varphi
of
X
(
\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H}=X_{H}
) and for every
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
we have

\begin{split}(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\omega)(Y)&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}\omega)(X_{H},Y)\\
&=\omega(\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H},\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=(X_{H}\lrcorner\omega)(\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=\varphi_{s}^{*}(X_{H}\lrcorner\omega)(Y)\\
&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}dH)(Y)\\
&=(d\varphi_{s}^{*}H)(Y)\end{split}

(10)

where
\varphi_{s\ast}Y
is the pushforward of
Y
by
\varphi_{s}
, i.e., the vector field
X_{H}
is Hamiltonian with respect to the symplectic structure
\varphi_{s}^{*}\omega
with Hamiltonian function
K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
.

3
Symmetries for cosymplectic Hamiltonian systems

In this section we review some recent results concerning canonoid transformations in the cosymplectic setting and introduce new results regarding scaling symmetries in this geometry (we refer to Appendix
A
for more definitions and properties).

Let
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
be a cosymplectic Hamiltonian system. Following the definition of infinitesimal symmetry in the symplectic framework, we say that:

Definition 7
.

A smooth vector field
V
on
M
is
an infinitesimal symmetry
of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)

if
L_{V}\Omega=L_{V}H=0
and
V\lrcorner\eta=0
(this implies that
L_{V}\eta=0
).

In
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
one can find a version of Noether’s theorem for the special case where
M=\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}Q
,

\Omega=dp_{i}\wedge dq^{i}-dH\wedge dt
and
\eta=dt
. In such case one has that
any constant of motion (which possibly depends explicitly on time) is associated to a symmetry
X
of
\Omega
(
L_{X}\Omega=0
),
without needing to restrict to the case
L_{X}H=0
.

It is easy to prove the following (new) version of Noether’s theorem for Hamiltonian systems on general cosymplectic manifolds:

Theorem 3
.

i)

If
f
is a constant of motion of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
such that
Rf=0

(
f
does not depend explicitly on time),
then
X_{f}
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
.

Reciprocally,

ii)

if
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
,
then there is a (possibly-local) function
f
such that
V=X_{f}
and
f
is a constant of motion with
Rf=0
.

Proof.

i)

We have that
L_{X_{f}}\Omega=X_{f}\lrcorner d\Omega+d(X_{f}\lrcorner\Omega)=d(df-(Rf)\eta)=-d(Rf)\wedge\eta=0
and
X_{f}\lrcorner\eta=0
. In addition

L_{X_{f}}H=X_{f}H=\{H,f\}=R(f)=0.

(11)

Therefore
X_{f}
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
.

ii)

In this case we have

\begin{split}L_{V}\Omega=0&\Longrightarrow V\lrcorner d\Omega+d(V\lrcorner\Omega)=0\\
&\Longrightarrow d(V\lrcorner\Omega)=0\\
&\Longrightarrow V\lrcorner\Omega=df\,.\end{split}

On the other hand

Rf=df(R)=\Omega(V,R)=-(R\lrcorner\Omega)(V)=0\,.

(12)

Finally,

\begin{split}&L_{E_{H}}f=E_{H}f=X_{H}f+Rf=df(X_{H})=\Omega(V,X_{H})=-(X_{H}\lrcorner\Omega)(V)\\
&=-dH(V)+(RH)\eta(V)=-VH+(RH)(V\lrcorner\eta)=0.\end{split}

(13)

So we have that
V=X_{f}
and
f
is a constant of motion with
Rf=0
.

∎

As in the symplectic case, any infinitesimal symmetry generates a
one-parameter group of canonical transformations leaving the Hamiltonian invariant.
To see this, we begin with the following definition from
[
42
]
.

Definition 8
.

A canonical transformation
for the cosymplectic manifold
(M,\Omega,\eta)
is a
possibly-local diffeomorphism
F
on
M
such that
F^{*}\Omega=\Omega
and
F^{*}\eta=\eta
.

If
F:M\longrightarrow M
is a diffeomorphism, then
(F^{*}\Omega,F^{*}\eta)
is a cosymplectic structure on
M
and therefore around any point
p\in M
there are local coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},T)
such that

F^{*}\Omega=dQ^{i}\wedge dP_{i}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\textrm{and}\hskip 18.49988ptF^{*}\eta=dT.

(14)

If we think of
F
locally as a coordinate transformation
(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},t)\longmapsto(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},t)
, then it is a canonical transformation if and only if the new coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},t)
are also canonical.

Finally, we have the following property, which is completely analogous to the symplectic case (cf. Proposition
2
).

Proposition 3
.

If
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
,
then the flow of
V
defines a one-parameter group of canonical transformations
that leave the Hamiltonian invariant;
reciprocally, if we have a one-parameter group of canonical transformations that leave the Hamiltonian
H
invariant, then the infinitesimal generator of the flow defined by such one-parameter group is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
.

Example 1
.

Let us consider the Hamiltonian system
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
with
(q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2},t)

canonical coordinates and
H(q^{1},q^{2},p_{1},p_{2},t)=\frac{1}{2}(p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2})+tq_{1}
.
The function
f(q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2},t)=p_{2}
is a constant of motion with
R(f)=0
.
Then
X_{f}
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
. Indeed

X_{f}=\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{2}},

(15)

L_{X_{f}}\Omega=d(X_{f}\lrcorner\Omega)=d(dp_{2})=0,\hskip 28.45274ptX_{f}\lrcorner\eta=dt(\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{2}})=0

(16)

and

L_{X_{f}}H=X_{f}H=\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{2}}=0.

(17)

In the cosymplectic context we can also define infinitesimal dynamical symmetries as those vector fields that preserve the dynamics.

Definition 9
.

A smooth vector field
W
on
M
is
an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry
of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
if
[W,E_{H}]=0
.

Since
[W,E_{H}]=[W,X_{H}]+[W,R]
, we have that if
[W,X_{H}]=[W,R]=0
then
W
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
.
However, the converse statement is not true.
By considering this kind of infinitesimal dynamical symmetries we have analogous results to the symplectic framework.
As in the symplectic framework, we have that the assignment
f\mapsto X_{f}
is a Lie algebra antihomomorphism between the Lie algebras
(C^{\infty}(M),\{,\})
and
(\mathfrak{X}(M),[,])

[
11
]
, i.e.,
X_{\{f,g\}}=-[X_{f},X_{g}]
. In addition
X_{Rf}=-[X_{f},R]
. We have the following results.

Proposition 4
.

Not every infinitesimal dynamical symmetry
W
of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
is such that
[W,X_{H}]=[W,R]=0
.

Proof.

If
f
is a constant of motion with
Rf\neq 0
then

[X_{f},E_{H}]=[X_{f},X_{H}]+[X_{f},R]=X_{\{H,f\}}-X_{Rf}=-X_{\{f,H\}+Rf}=0,

(18)

i.e.,
X_{f}
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry,
but
[X_{f},R]=-X_{Rf}
, which is not necessarily zero.
∎

Proposition 5
.

Every infinitesimal symmetry
W
of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry.

Proof.

Let
W
be an infinitesimal symmetry. Then
W=X_{f}
for some constant of motion
f
such that
Rf=0
. We have

[X_{f},X_{H}]=[X_{f},X_{H}]=X_{\{H,f\}}=0

(19)

and

[X_{f},R]=-X_{Rf}=0\,.

(20)

∎

There are non-Hamiltonian vector fields
W
that satisfy
[W,X_{H}]=[W,R]=0
and are not infinitesimal dynamical symmetries of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
.
To show this, we introduce the concept of scaling symmetry in the cosymplectic framework.

Definition 10
.

X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is
a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for the Hamiltonian system
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
if

i.

L_{X}\Omega=\Omega
,

ii.

X\lrcorner\eta=0
(therefore
L_{X}\eta=0
) and

iii.

L_{X}H=\Lambda H

As in the symplectic case, we have that scaling symmetries are not Hamiltonian for any function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
,
and thus they are not infinitesimal symmetries.
Moreover, if
X
is a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
,
then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
.
This is the content of the following result:

Lemma 1
.

If
X
is a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a scaling symmetry. We have

[X,R]\lrcorner\eta=X\eta(R)-R\eta(X)=0

(21)

and

[X,R]\lrcorner\Omega=L_{X}(R\lrcorner\Omega)-R\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega=0\,.

(22)

Therefore
XR=RX
, and then

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\eta=X\eta(X_{H})-X_{H}\eta(X)=0

(23)

and

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\Omega&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner\Omega)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega\\
&=L_{X}(dH-RH\eta)-X_{H}\lrcorner\Omega\\
&=L_{X}(dH)-XRH\eta-RHL_{X}\eta-dH+RH\eta\\
&=d(L_{X}H)-RXH\eta-dH+RH\eta\\
&=d((\Lambda-1)H)-R((\Lambda-1)H)\eta\,,\end{split}

(24)

which mean that

[X,X_{H}]=X_{(\Lambda-1)H}=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}.

(25)

∎

Therefore we conclude that scaling symmetries of degree 1 are infinitesimal dynamical symmetries which satisfy
[X,X_{H}]=[X,R]=0
but are not infinitesimal symmetries.

We also have a result analogous to Proposition 1 in
[
32
]
and which provides a useful characterization of scaling symmetries.

Proposition 6
.

(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
admits a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
if and only if there exists a primitive,
\lambda
, of
\Omega
(
d\lambda=\Omega
), satisfying

X_{H}\lrcorner\lambda=-\Lambda H\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptR\lrcorner\lambda=0.

(26)

Proof.

Let
X
be a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
for
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
.
Then for
\lambda=X\lrcorner\Omega
we have

d\lambda=d(X\lrcorner\Omega)=L_{X}\Omega=\Omega,

(27)

X_{H}\lrcorner\lambda=X_{H}\lrcorner(X\lrcorner\Omega)=\Omega(X,X_{H})=-dH(X)+(RH)\eta(X)=-XH=-\Lambda H

(28)

and

R\lrcorner\lambda=R\lrcorner(X\lrcorner\Omega)=\Omega(X,R)=0.

(29)

Now, for the converse statement, let us suppose that there exists a primitive,
\lambda
, of
\Omega
such that
X_{H}\lrcorner\lambda=-\Lambda H
and
R\lrcorner\lambda=0
; since the map
\mathcal{X}_{\eta\Omega}:TM\longrightarrow T^{*}M
defined by
\mathcal{X}_{\eta\Omega}(X)=X\lrcorner\Omega+(X\lrcorner\eta)\eta
is a bundle isomorphism
[
11
]
, there exists a smooth vector field
X
on
M
such that

X\lrcorner\Omega+(X\lrcorner\eta)\eta=\lambda.

(30)

Since
R\lrcorner\lambda=0
we have

\begin{split}R\lrcorner(X\lrcorner\Omega+(X\lrcorner\eta)\eta)=0&\Longrightarrow\Omega(X,R)+(X\lrcorner\eta)\eta(R)=0\\
&\Longrightarrow X\lrcorner\eta=0.\end{split}

(31)

Now

\begin{split}d\lambda=\Omega&\Longrightarrow d(X\lrcorner\Omega)=\Omega\\
&\Longrightarrow L_{X}\Omega=\Omega,\end{split}

(32)

and

\begin{split}X_{H}\lrcorner\lambda=-\Lambda H&\Longrightarrow\Omega(X,X_{H})=-\Lambda H\\
&\Longrightarrow-dH(X)+(RH)\eta(X)=-\Lambda H\\
&\Longrightarrow-XH=-\Lambda H\\
&\Longrightarrow XH=\Lambda H.\end{split}

(33)

So we conclude that
X
is a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
for
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
.
∎

In the rest of this section we characterize the infinitesimal generators of one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations for cosymplectic Hamiltonian systems in a way completely analogous to the symplectic case.
Canonoid transformations for time-dependent Hamiltonian systems have been studied in
[
37
,
39
,
40
]
.
The geometrical definition of canonoid transformations in the cosymplectic framework is presented in
[
42
]
as follows:

Definition 11
.

We say that a possibly-local diffeomorphism
F:M\longrightarrow M
is
a canonoid transformation
for the Hamiltonian system
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
if
F^{*}\eta=\eta
(or equivalently
F_{\ast}R=R
) and there exists a function
K\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner F^{*}\Omega=dK-RK\eta.

(34)

Locally, a canonoid transformation is a coordinate transformation of the form

(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},t)\longmapsto(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},t)

such that the equations of motion in the coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},t)
are

\dot{Q^{i}}=\frac{\partial K}{\partial P_{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{P_{i}}=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q^{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{t}=1\,,

(35)

for some function
K\in C^{\infty}(M)
.

The following result will be handy in order to obtain a characterization of the infinitesimal generators of canonoid transformations.

Lemma 2
.

X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if
L_{X}\eta=0
(or equivalently
L_{X}R=0
) and there exists (at least locally) a function
K
on
M
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega=dK-RK\eta.

(36)

Proof.

We have that
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if
\varphi_{s}^{*}\eta=\eta
and there exists a family
\{K_{s}\}
of functions on
M
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\Omega=dK_{s}-RK_{s}\eta,

(37)

which is equivalent to
d(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\Omega)=-d(RK_{s}\eta)
.
Thus
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if

\varphi_{s}^{*}\eta=\eta
and

L_{X_{H}}\varphi_{s}^{*}\Omega=-d(RK_{s}\eta).

(38)

By taking
\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}
in
\varphi_{s}^{*}\eta=\eta
and in equation (
38
) we have that
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if
L_{X}\eta=0
and

L_{X_{H}}L_{X}\Omega=-d(Rk_{0}\eta),

(39)

where
k_{0}=\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}K_{s}
. Equation (
39
) is equivalent to

d(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega)=-d(Rk_{0}\eta),

(40)

which is equivalent to the fact that there exists (at least locally) a function
K
on
M
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega+Rk_{0}\eta=dK,

(41)

or equivalently

X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega=dK-Rk_{0}\eta.

(42)

Finally, we have that
Rk_{0}=RK
. Indeed,

RK-Rk_{0}=R\lrcorner(dK-Rk_{0}\eta)=R\lrcorner(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega)=L_{X}\omega(X_{H},R)=0.

(43)

∎

In the cosymplectic case we know that the Hamiltonian function is not necessarily a constant of motion, namely, we have that
E_{H}H=RH
, although it is invariant under the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
, i.e.,
X_{H}H=0
.
Therefore we have that the function
K
in equation (
36
) is invariant under the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
, and satisfies
E_{H}K=RK
. Indeed,

X_{H}K=X_{H}\lrcorner(dK-RK\eta)=X_{H}\lrcorner(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega)=0.

(44)

The following theorem provides a useful characterization of the infinitesimal generators of one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations, in complete analogy with Theorem
2
for the symplectic case.

Theorem 4
.

X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
if and only if
[X,R]=0
and
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field. In this case its Hamiltonian function is
L_{X}H-K
, where
K
is given in equation (
36
).

Proof.

We have

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\eta=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner\eta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\eta=-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\eta,

(45)

and

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\Omega&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner\Omega)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega\\
&=L_{X}(dH-RH\eta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega\\
&=dL_{X}H-L_{X}(RH)\eta-(RH)L_{X}\eta-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega\\
&=dL_{X}H-RL_{X}H\eta-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\Omega,\end{split}

(46)

so, using Lemma
2
, we have that
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations if and only if
L_{X}R=0
,
[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\eta=0
and there exists (at least locally) a function
K
on
M
such that

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\Omega=dL_{X}H-RL_{X}H\eta-dK+RK\eta=d(L_{X}H-K)-R(L_{X}H-K)\eta,

(47)

which is equivalent to saying that
[X,R]=0
and
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian function
L_{X}H-K
.
∎

As in the symplectic case, we can now easily prove the following.

Corollary 3
.

Every scaling symmetry is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.

Proof.

We have that if
X
is a scaling symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
, then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
(see Lemma
1
). Therefore
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian function
(\Lambda-1)H
.
∎

Nevertheless, if
W
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\Omega,\eta,H)
and
\varphi
is its flow, then we do not necessarily have that
\eta
(nor
R
) is invariant under
\varphi
. So we do not necessarily have a group of canonoid transformations.
Since we are interested in symmetries related to canonoid transformations, we consider a subset of the set of infinitesimal dynamical symmetries.
We have

Corollary 4
.

If
W
is such that
[W,X_{H}]=0
and
[W,R]=0
, then
W
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.

Proof.

If
W
is such that
[W,X_{H}]=0
and
[W,R]=0
, then
[W,X_{H}]=0
is a Hamiltonian vector field with constant Hamiltonian function
L_{X}H-K=c\in\mathbb{R}
(we can see this in a completely analogous way as in the symplectic case). By hypothesis,
X_{H}
and
R
are invariant under the flow
\varphi
of
W
.
Therefore, we conclude that
W
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.
∎

In addition, we have that for each
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the new Hamiltonian function is
K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
.
Indeed, for every
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
we have

\begin{split}(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\Omega)(Y)&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}\Omega)(X_{H},Y)\\
&=\Omega(\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H},\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=(X_{H}\lrcorner\Omega)(\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=\varphi_{s}^{*}(X_{H}\lrcorner\Omega)(Y)\\
&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}dH)(Y)-(\varphi_{s}^{*}(RH))(\varphi_{s}^{*}\eta)(Y)\\
&=(d\varphi_{s}^{*}H)(Y)-(\varphi_{s\ast}R)(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\eta(Y)\\
&=(d\varphi_{s}^{*}H)(Y)-R(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\eta(Y)\end{split}

(48)

i.e. the vector field
X_{H}
is Hamiltonian with respect to the cosymplectic structure
(\varphi_{s}^{*}\Omega,\eta)
with Hamiltonian function
K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
.

4
Symmetries for contact Hamiltonian systems

In this section we review some recent results concerning canonoid transformations in the contact setting and introduce new results regarding scaling symmetries in this geometry (we refer to Appendix
A
for more definitions and properties).

Let
(M,\theta,H)
be a contact Hamiltonian system. Since these systems are
more adapted to describe dissipative systems in general, in
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
the concept of constant of motion (conserved quantity) has been generalized to the so-called dissipated quantities as follows:

Definition 12
.

A dissipated quantity
of the contact Hamiltonian system
(M,\theta,H)
is a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
that is dissipated at the same rate as the contact Hamiltonian
H
, i.e.,
X_{H}f=-fRH
.

In
[
22
]
it is argued that a suitable definition of Noether symmetries in the contact setting is the following:

Definition 13
.

A Noether symmetry
of
(M,\theta,H)
is a contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{f}
such that
\{f,H\}=0
.

Then the contact version of Noether’s theorem reads:

Theorem 5
.

X_{f}
is a Noether symmetry if and only if
f
is a dissipated quantity.

This theorem has been proved first in
[
23
,
24
]
, where it was called
the dissipation theorem
.
Note that one of the reasons why dissipated quantities are important is that
it is easy to show that the quotient of two dissipated quantities (whenever well defined) is a constant of motion.
Moreover, we observe that if
RH=0
(locally in canonical coordinates this means that the Hamiltonian function does not depend on
z
), then dissipated quantities (including
H
itself) are constants of motion. Indeed, in this case we have

X_{H}f=0\Leftrightarrow\{H,f\}=0.

(49)

This motivates the following definition from e.g.
[
46
,
47
]
:

Definition 14
.

A contact Hamiltonian systems with
RH=0
is called
a good contact Hamiltonian system
.

Then it follows immediately that:

Corollary 5
.

Let
(M,\theta,H)
be a good contact Hamiltonian system.
X_{f}
is a Noether symmetry if and only if
f
is a constant of motion.

Following the ideas developed in the previous sections, we are interested in symmetries of contact Hamiltonian systems related to canonical and canonoid transformations. We begin with the geometric definition of a canonical transformation in the contact setting as put forward in
[
42
]
:

Definition 15
.

A canonical transformation
for the contact manifold
(M,\theta)
is a possibly-local diffeomorphism
F
on
M
such that
F^{*}\theta=\theta
.

Let
F:M\longrightarrow M
be a diffeomorphism. We know that
F^{*}\theta
is a contact structure on
M
.
It follows that around any point
p\in M
there are local coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z)
such that

F^{*}\theta=dZ-P_{i}dQ^{i}.

(50)

If we think of
F
locally as a coordinate transformation

(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},z)\longmapsto(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z)\,,

then
F
is a canonical transformation if and only if it preserves the Jacobi bracket (for details see
[
42
]
).

Analogously to the previous sections, we can now state the following definition from
[
23
]
:

Definition 16
.

A smooth vector field
V
on
M
such that
L_{V}\theta=L_{V}H=0
is called
an infinitesimal (contact) symmetry
of the contact Hamiltonian system
(M,\theta,H)
.

Again, we have that:

Proposition 7
.

If
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
, then the flow of
V
defines a one-parameter group of canonical transformations
that leave the Hamiltonian invariant; reciprocally, if we have a one-parameter group of canonical transformations that leave the Hamiltonian
H
invariant, then the infinitesimal generator of the flow defined by such one-parameter group is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
.

Moreover, in this case infinitesimal symmetries are a subset of Noether symmetries, according to:

Theorem 6
.

V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if it is a Noether symmetry
X_{f}
with
Rf=0
.

Proof.

Let us suppose that
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry, i.e.,
L_{V}\theta=L_{V}H=0
. We have

L_{V}\theta=0\longrightarrow L_{V}d\theta=0\longrightarrow d(V\lrcorner d\theta)=0,

(51)

i.e. there is a (possibly-local) function
f
on
M
such that
V\lrcorner d\theta=df
.
Now

L_{V}\theta=0\longrightarrow d(V\lrcorner\theta)+V\lrcorner d\theta=0\longrightarrow V\lrcorner\theta=-f,

(52)

so we have
V\lrcorner\theta=-f
and
V\lrcorner d\theta=df
. On the other hand

L_{V}H=V(H)=dH(V)=d\theta(X_{H},V)+RH\theta(V)=-df(X_{H})-fRH=-X_{H}f-fRH,

(53)

so

L_{V}H=0\longrightarrow X_{H}f=-fRH

(54)

therefore
f
is a dissipated quantity with
Rf=df(R)=d\theta(V,R)=-(R\lrcorner d\theta)(V)=0
,
so we conclude that
V=X_{f}
is a Noether symmetry with
Rf=0
.

Now let us see the converse statement. Let us suppose that
V
is a
Noether symmetry
X_{f}
for some function
f
such that
Rf=0
. Then

L_{X_{f}}\theta=d(X_{f}\lrcorner\theta)+X_{f}\lrcorner d\theta=-(Rf)\theta=0,

(55)

L_{X_{f}}H=X_{f}H=\{H,f\}-HRf=-\{f,H\}=0.

(56)

∎

Observe that if
(M,\theta,H)
is a good contact Hamiltonian system, then we have an analogous result to the cosymplectic case, given by

Corollary 6
.

Let
(M,\theta,H)
be a good contact Hamiltonian system.
Then
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry if and only if
V=X_{f}
for some constant of motion
f
such that
Rf=0
.

We can proceed further to define infinitesimal dynamical symmetries in the contact framework
[
23
]
.

Definition 17
.

A smooth vector field
W
on
M
is called
an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry
of
(M,\theta,H)
if
[W,X_{H}]=0
.

In the contact case the assignment
f\mapsto X_{f}
still defines a Lie algebra antihomomorphism
between the Lie algebras
(C^{\infty}(M),\{,\})
and
(\mathfrak{X}(M),[,])
.
Indeed, for
f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
we have

-[X_{f},X_{g}]=X_{\{f,g\}}

(57)

For details see
[
13
]
.
Thus we have that every infinitesimal symmetry is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry (this result was first shown in
[
23
,
48
]
in a different way from the one we present here).

Proposition 8
.

Every infinitesimal symmetry is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry.

Proof.

Let
V
be an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
, i.e.
V=X_{f}
for some function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that
\{f,H\}=Rf=0
.
Then

-[X_{f},X_{H}]=X_{\{f,H\}}=0.

(58)

Therefore
V
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
.
∎

As in the previous sections, the converse statement is not true.
To see that, we first introduce the concept of scaling symmetries in the contact framework and then we show that scaling symmetries of degree 1 are infinitesimal dynamical symmetries but not infinitesimal symmetries (they are not even Noether symmetries).

Definition 18
.

X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is
a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for the contact Hamiltonian system
(M,\theta,H)
if

i.

L_{X}\theta=\theta
(which implies that
L_{X}d\theta=d\theta
) and

ii.

L_{X}H=\Lambda H

As in the previous sections, we obtain

Lemma 3
.

If
X
is a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
for
(M,\theta,H)
, then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a scaling symmetry. We have

[X,R]\lrcorner\theta=L_{X}(R\lrcorner\theta)-R\lrcorner L_{X}\theta=-R\lrcorner\theta=-1

(59)

and

[X,R]\lrcorner d\theta=L_{X}(R\lrcorner d\theta)-R\lrcorner L_{X}d\theta=-R\lrcorner d\theta=0,

(60)

and therefore
[X,R]=-R
.
On the other hand

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\theta&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta\\
&=-(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\\
&=-(\Lambda H-H)\\
&=-(\Lambda-1)H\end{split}

(61)

and

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner d\theta&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner d\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}d\theta\\
&=L_{X}(dH-RH\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta\\
&=dL_{X}H-XRH\theta-RHL_{X}\theta-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta\\
&=dL_{X}H+RH\theta-RXH\theta-RH\theta-dH+RH\theta\\
&=d((\Lambda-1)H)-R((\Lambda-1)H),\end{split}

(62)

and therefore

[X,X_{H}]=X_{(\Lambda-1)H}=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}.

(63)

∎

So we have that scaling symmetries of degree 1 are examples of infinitesimal dynamical symmetries that are not infinitesimal symmetries.
In this case however, a scaling symmetry might be a Hamiltonian vector field. To see this, observe that if
X_{f}
is such that
R(f)=-1
and
\{H,f\}=(\Lambda-1)H
, then
X_{f}
is a scaling symmetry.
Indeed,

L_{X_{f}}\theta=-(Rf)\theta=\theta

(64)

and

L_{X_{f}}H=X_{f}H=\{H,f\}-HRf=(\Lambda-1)H+H=\Lambda H.

(65)

To get more familiar with scaling symmetries for contact Hamiltonian systems, we provide now a couple of simple examples.

Example 2
.

Let us consider the contact Hamiltonian system
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\theta,H)
with
(q,p,z)
canonical coordinates and
H(q,p,z)=\frac{1}{2}p^{2}-\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{z^{2}}
.
The vector field
X=2q\frac{\partial}{\partial q}-p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
is a scaling symmetry of degree
-2
.
Indeed,

\begin{split}L_{X}\theta&=d((2q\frac{\partial}{\partial q}-p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\lrcorner(dz-pdq))+(2q\frac{\partial}{\partial q}-p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\lrcorner dq\wedge dp\\
&=-2qdp-2pdq+dz+pdq+2qdp\\
&=dz-pdq\\
&=\theta\end{split}

(66)

and

L_{X}H=(2q\frac{\partial}{\partial q}-p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})(\frac{1}{2}p^{2}-\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{z^{2}})=-p^{2}+\frac{2}{q}+\frac{2}{z^{2}}=-2H.

(67)

Example 3
.

Now let us consider the contact Hamiltonian system
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\theta,H)
with
(q,p,z)
canonical coordinates and
H(q,p,z)=pf(q,z)+z
where
f(q,z)
is any smooth function on the variables
q,z
. The vector field
X=p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
is a scaling symmetry of degree
1
, and hence it is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry. Indeed,

\begin{split}L_{X}\theta&=d((p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\lrcorner(dz-pdq))+(p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})\lrcorner dq\wedge dp\\
&=dz-pdq\\
&=\theta\end{split}

(68)

and

L_{X}H=(p\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z})(pf(q,z)+z)=pf(q,z)+z=H.

(69)

In addition, we can see that
X
is also a Hamiltonian vector field. Indeed,
X
is the Hamiltonian vector field of the function
f=-z
.

To finish this section, we characterize infinitesimal generators of one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations for contact Hamiltonian systems in a similar way as in the previous sections. The geometrical definition of canonoid transformations for contact Hamiltonian systems is presented in
[
42
]
as follows.

Definition 19
.

We say that a possibly-local diffeomorphism
F:M\longrightarrow M
is
a canonoid transformation
for the Hamiltonian system
(M,\theta,H)
if there exists a function
K\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner F^{*}\theta=-K\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptX_{H}\lrcorner dF^{*}\theta=dK-(F_{\ast}R)KF^{*}\theta.

(70)

Locally, a canonoid transformation is a coordinate transformation of the form

(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},z)\longmapsto(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z)

such that the equations of motion in the coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z)
are

\dot{Q^{i}}=\frac{\partial K}{\partial P_{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{P_{i}}=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q^{i}}-P_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial Z},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{Z}=P_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial P_{i}}-K\,,

(71)

for some function
K\in C^{\infty}(M)
.

We have the following result, analogous to Theorems
2
and
4
.

Theorem 7
.

X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field. In this case its Hamiltonian function is
L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta
.

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
and
\varphi
its flow.
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if

X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=-K_{s}\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptX_{H}\lrcorner d\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=dK_{s}-(\varphi_{s\ast}R)K_{s}\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta

(72)

for a family
\{K_{s}\}
of functions on
M
. By taking
\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}
in equation
X_{H}\lrcorner d\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=dK_{s}-(\varphi_{s\ast}R)K_{s}\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta
we have that it is equivalent to

X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta=dK-(L_{X}R)H\theta-RK\theta-RHL_{X}\theta

(73)

where
K=\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}K_{s}
and
K_{s}|_{s=0}=H
. Now by taking
\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}
in equation
X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=-K_{s}
we have that

K=-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta.

(74)

So
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if

X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta=-d(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)-(L_{X}R)H\theta+R(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)\theta-RHL_{X}\theta,

(75)

or equivalently

-d(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)-(L_{X}R)H\theta+R(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)\theta-RHL_{X}\theta-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta=0.

(76)

On the other hand, we have that

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\theta&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta\\
&=-(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\end{split}

(77)

and

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner d\theta&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner d\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}d\theta\\
&=L_{X}(dH-RH\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta\\
&=dL_{X}H-XRH\theta-RHL_{X}\theta-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta\\
&=d(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)-R(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\theta\\
&-d(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)-(L_{X}R)H\theta+R(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)\theta-RHL_{X}\theta-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta.\end{split}

(78)

So, by using equations (
76
) and (
78
),
we conclude that
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\theta=-(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)

(79)

and

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner d\theta=d(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)-R(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\theta,

(80)

i.e.,
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian function
L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta
.
∎

As in the previous sections, we are interested in the following corollary:

Corollary 7
.

Scaling symmetries are infinitesimal generators of one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations.

Proof.

If
X
is a scaling symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
(where
\Lambda
is the degree of
X
), which is the Hamiltonian vector field of the function
(\Lambda-1)H
.
∎

Of course, we also have

Corollary 8
.

If
X
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
, then the flow of
X
defines a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.

In addition, we have that for each
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the new Hamiltonian function is
K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
.
Indeed, let us suppose that
X
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\theta,H)
, then
\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H}=X_{H}
, so that

(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta)(p)=\theta_{\varphi_{s}(p)}(\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H})=\theta_{\varphi_{s}(p)}(X_{H})=-H(\varphi_{s}(p))=-(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)(p)

(81)

and for every
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
we have

\begin{split}(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}d\theta)(Y)&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}d\theta)(X_{H},Y)\\
&=d\theta(\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H},\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=(X_{H}\lrcorner d\theta)(\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=\varphi_{s}^{*}(X_{H}\lrcorner d\theta)(Y)\\
&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}dH-(\varphi_{s\ast}R)(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta)(Y)\\
&=(d\varphi_{s}^{*}H-(\varphi_{s\ast}R)(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta)(Y)\end{split}

(82)

i.e. the vector field
X_{H}
is Hamiltonian with respect to the contact structure
\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta
with Hamiltonian function
K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
.

In
[
42
]
it has been shown that from a given canonoid transformation for a
good
contact Hamiltonian system one can obtain
the corresponding constants of motion.
It is worth remarking that in our case the contact Hamiltonian system needs not be good.
Therefore in the general case we have that if
\varphi_{s}
is a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
with new Hamiltonian functions
K_{s}
, then
K_{s}
are dissipated quantities; therefore the quotients
K_{s}/K_{s^{\prime}}
(whenever well defined) are constants of motion.

5
Symmetries for cocontact Hamiltonian systems

In
[
22
]
the contact version of Noether’s theorem has been extended to time-dependent contact Hamiltonian systems using the extended phase space
T^{*}Q\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
; on the other hand, time-dependent contact Hamiltonian systems can be generalized using the formalism of cocontact geometry, to obtain the so-called cocontact Hamiltonian systems
[
16
]
. In
[
48
]
the authors studied dissipated quantities and dynamical symmetries for cocontact Hamiltonian systems.
In this section, we review some recent aspects concerning canonoid transformations in the cocontact setting and introduce new results (we refer to Appendix
A
for more definitions and properties).

Let
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
be a cocontact Hamiltonian system.

Definition 20
.

A Noether symmetry
of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
is a contact Hamiltonian vector field
X_{f}
such that
\{f,H\}+R_{t}f=0
.

Definition 21
.

A dissipated quantity
of the cocontact Hamiltonian system
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
is a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that
E_{H}f=-fR_{z}H
.

As it was pointed out in
[
22
,
48
]
, in the most general case we have
E_{H}H=-HR_{z}H+R_{t}H
,
and therefore whenever
H
depends explicitly on
t
it is not itself a dissipated quantity.

As expected, we have a cocontact version of Noether’s theorem (see Proposition 3.6 in
[
48
]
):

Theorem 8
.

A function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
is a dissipated quantity if and only if
\{f,H\}+R_{t}f=0
, which is equivalent to
X_{f}
being a Noether symmetry.

As in the contact case, we have that the quotient of two dissipated quantities is a constant of motion
[
48
]
.
Now, we can also observe that if
R_{z}H=0
(locally, the Hamiltonian function does not depend on
z
), then dissipated quantities are constants of motion.
Following the notion of good contact Hamiltonian systems, we define the following:

Definition 22
.

We say that
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
is
a good cocontact Hamiltonian system
if
R_{z}H=0
.

It is important to remark that the condition
R_{t}H=0
is not required, i.e., the Hamiltonian function
H
may depend explicitly on
t
.
Then we have the following immediate corollary:

Corollary 9
.

Let
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
be a good cocontact Hamiltonian system. Then
X_{f}
is a Noether symmetry if and only if
f
is a constant of motion.

Again, we are interested in symmetries of cocontact Hamiltonian systems which are related to canonical and canonoid transformations.
We start with the following definition from
[
42
]
.

Definition 23
.

A canonical transformation
for the cocontact manifold
(M,\theta,\eta)
is a possibly-local
diffeomorphism
F
on
M
such that
F^{*}\theta=\theta
and
F^{*}\eta=\eta
.

Let
F:M\longrightarrow M
be a diffeomorphism. We know that
(F^{*}\theta,F^{*}\eta)
is a cocontact structure on
M
.
Then around any point
p\in M
there are local coordinates
(T,Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z)
such that

F^{*}\theta=dZ-P_{i}dQ^{i}\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptF^{*}\eta=dT.

(83)

If we think of
F
locally as a coordinate transformation

(t,q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},z)\longmapsto(t,Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z)\,,

then it is a canonical transformation if and only if it preserves the Jacobi bracket (see
[
42
]
for the details).

We are interested in vector fields whose flow leave the cocontact structure and the Hamiltonian invariant.
In
[
48
]
these vector fields are called infinitesimal strict Hamiltonian symmetries; by following the language employed in the previous sections, we call them infinitesimal symmetries.

Definition 24
.

A smooth vector field
V
on
M
is
an infinitesimal symmetry
of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
if
L_{V}\theta=L_{V}H=0

and
V\lrcorner\eta=0
(which implies
L_{V}\eta=0
).

We have some results similar to the previous sections.

Proposition 9
.

If
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
,
then the flow of
V
defines a one-parameter group of canonical transformations
that leave the Hamiltonian invariant; reciprocally, if we have a one-parameter group of canonical transformations that leave the Hamiltonian
H

invariant, then the infinitesimal generator of the flow defined by such one-parameter group is an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
.

Moreover, in this case infinitesimal symmetries are a subset of Noether symmetries, according to:

Theorem 9
.

V
is an infinitesimal symmetry if and only if it is a Noether symmetry
X_{f}
with
R_{z}f=R_{t}f=0
.

Proof.

Let us suppose that
V
is an infinitesimal symmetry, i.e.
L_{V}\theta=L_{V}H=0
and
V\lrcorner\eta=0
. We have

L_{V}\theta=0\longrightarrow L_{V}d\theta=0\longrightarrow d(V\lrcorner d\theta)=0,

(84)

i.e. there is a (possibly-local) function
f
on
M
such that
V\lrcorner d\theta=df
.
Now

L_{V}\theta=0\longrightarrow d(V\lrcorner\theta)+V\lrcorner d\theta=0\longrightarrow V\lrcorner\theta=-f,

(85)

so we have
V\lrcorner\theta=-f
and
V\lrcorner d\theta=df
. Now we can see that for
R=R_{z}
or
R=R_{t}
we have

Rf=df(R)=d\theta(V,R)=-(R\lrcorner d\theta)(V)=0.

(86)

On the other hand

L_{V}H=V(H)=dH(V)=d\theta(X_{H},V)+R_{z}H\theta(V)=-df(X_{H})-fR_{z}H=-X_{H}f-fR_{z}H,

(87)

so

L_{V}H=0\longrightarrow X_{H}f=-fR_{z}H\longrightarrow E_{H}f=-fR_{z}H

(88)

therefore
f
is a dissipated quantity, so we conclude that
V=X_{f}
is a Noether symmetry with
R_{z}f=R_{t}f=0
.

Now let us see the converse statement. Let us suppose that
V
is a Noether symmetry
X_{f}

for some function
f
such that
R_{z}f=R_{t}f=0
. Then

L_{X_{f}}\theta=d(X_{f}\lrcorner\theta)+X_{f}\lrcorner d\theta=-(R_{z}f)\theta-R_{t}f\eta=0,

(89)

L_{X_{f}}H=X_{f}H=\{H,f\}-HR_{z}f=-\{f,H\}=0

(90)

and of course
X_{f}\lrcorner\eta=0
.
∎

Now we consider the definition of infinitesimal dynamical symmetries in the cocontact context (cf.
[
48
]
).

Definition 25
.

A smooth vector field
W
on
M
is
an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry
of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
if
[W,E_{H}]=0
.

As in the previous cases, the assignment
f\mapsto X_{f}
defines a Lie algebra antihomomorphism between the Lie algebras
(C^{\infty}(M),\{,\})
and
(\mathfrak{X}(M),[,])
.
Let us recall also that in the cocontact framework for
f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
we have

-[X_{f},X_{g}]\lrcorner\theta=-\{f,g\}

(91)

and in addition

-[X_{f},X_{g}]\lrcorner\eta=0,

(92)

so

-[X_{f},X_{g}]=X_{\{f,g\}}.

(93)

Analogously to the cosymplectic case, we can see that

-[X_{f},R_{z}]=X_{R_{z}f}

(94)

and

-[X_{f},R_{t}]=X_{R_{t}f}.

(95)

We have the following results.

Proposition 10
.

Every infinitesimal symmetry
V
of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
satisfies
[V,X_{H}]=[V,R_{t}]=0
.
Therefore
V
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry.

Proof.

Let
V
be an infinitesimal symmetry of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
, i.e.
V=X_{f}
for some function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)

such that
\{f,H\}=R_{z}f=R_{t}f=0
. Then

-[X_{f},R_{t}]=X_{R_{t}f}=0

(96)

and

-[X_{f},X_{H}]=X_{\{f,H\}}=0.

(97)

∎

However, the converse statement is not true. To show this, we start with the following:

Proposition 11
.

Not every infinitesimal dynamical symmetry
W
of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
is such that
[W,X_{H}]=[W,R_{t}]=0
.

Proof.

If
f
is a dissipated quantity with
R_{t}f\neq 0
, then

[X_{f},E_{H}]=[X_{f},X_{H}]+[X_{f},R_{t}]=X_{\{H,f\}}-X_{R_{t}f}=X_{\{f,H\}+R_{t}f}=0,

(98)

i.e.
X_{f}
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry. However, in general
[X_{f},R_{t}]=X_{R_{t}f}
,
which is not necessarily zero.
∎

Now we introduce the concept of scaling symmetries in the cocontact framework.

Definition 26
.

X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is
a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}

for the cocontact Hamiltonian system
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
if

i.

L_{X}\theta=\theta
(therefore
L_{X}d\theta=d\theta
),

ii.

X\lrcorner\eta=0
(therefore
L_{X}\eta=0
) and

iii.

L_{X}H=\Lambda H

As in the previous cases, we have:

Lemma 4
.

If
X
is a scaling symmetry of degree
\Lambda
for
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a scaling symmetry. By a calculation analogous to the ones in the previous sections, we have:

[X,R_{z}]=-R_{z},

(99)

[X,R_{t}]=0,

(100)

[X,X_{H}]\eta=0,

(101)

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\theta=-(\Lambda-1)H

(102)

and

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner d\theta=d(\Lambda H-H)-R(\Lambda H-H)\theta.

(103)

Thus, we conclude that

[X,X_{H}]=X_{\Lambda H-H}=\Lambda X_{H}-X_{H}=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}.

(104)

∎

So we have that scaling symmetries of degree 1 are examples of infinitesimal dynamical symmetries
such that
[X,X_{H}]=[X,R_{t}]=0
, but they are not infinitesimal symmetries.
As in the contact case, a scaling symmetry might be a Hamiltonian vector field.
To see this, we observe that if
X_{f}
is such that
R_{z}(f)=-1
,
R_{t}f=0
and
\{H,f\}=(\Lambda-1)H
, then
X_{f}
is a scaling symmetry.
Indeed,

L_{X_{f}}\theta=-(R_{z}f)\theta-(R_{t}f)\eta=\theta

(105)

and

L_{X_{f}}H=X_{f}H=\{H,f\}-HR_{z}f=(\Lambda-1)H+H=\Lambda H.

(106)

The geometrical definition of canonoid transformations for cocontact Hamiltonian systems has been introduced in
[
42
]
as follows.

Definition 27
.

We say that a possibly-local diffeomorphism
F:M\longrightarrow M
is
a canonoid transformation
for the Hamiltonian system
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
if
F^{*}\eta=\eta
(or equivalently
F_{\ast}R_{t}=R_{t}
) and there exists a function
K\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that

X_{H}\lrcorner F^{*}\theta=-K\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptX_{H}\lrcorner dF^{*}\theta=dK-(F_{\ast}R_{z})KF^{*}\theta-R_{t}K\eta.

(107)

Locally, a canonoid transformation is a coordinate transformation of the form

(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},z,t)\longmapsto(t,Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z,t)

such that the equations of motion in the coordinates
(Q^{1},\cdots,Q^{n},P_{1},\cdots,P_{n},Z,t)
are

\dot{Q^{i}}=\frac{\partial K}{\partial P_{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{P_{i}}=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q^{i}}-P_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial Z}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{Z}=P_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial P_{i}}-K\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{t}=1\,,

(108)

for some function
K\in C^{\infty}(M)
.

We have the following characterization result, analogous to Theorems
2
,
4
and
7
.

Theorem 10
.

X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta.\eta,H)
if and only if
[X,R_{t}]=0
and
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field.
In this case its Hamiltonian function is
L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta
.

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
and
\varphi
its flow.
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
if and only if

\varphi_{s}^{*}\eta=\eta
(or equivalently
\varphi_{s\ast}R_{t}=R_{t}
),

X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=-K_{s}\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptX_{H}\lrcorner d\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=dK_{s}-(\varphi_{s\ast}R_{z})K_{s}\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta-R_{t}K_{s}\eta

(109)

for a family
\{K_{s}\}
of functions on
M
. By taking
\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}
in
\varphi_{s}^{*}\eta=\eta
we have
L_{X}\eta=0
(or equivalently
L_{X}R_{t}=0
). Now by taking
\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}
in equation
X_{H}\lrcorner d\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=dK_{s}-(\varphi_{s\ast}R_{z})K_{s}\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta-R_{t}K_{s}\eta
we have that it is equivalent to

X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta=dK-(L_{X}R_{z})H\theta-R_{z}K\theta-R_{z}HL_{X}\theta--R_{t}K\eta

(110)

where
K=\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}K_{s}
and
K_{s}|_{s=0}=H
.
In addition, by taking
\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}
in equation
X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta=-K_{s}
we have that

K=-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta.

(111)

So
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if
L_{X}R_{t}=0
and

X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta=-d(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)-(L_{X}R_{z})H\theta+R_{z}(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)\theta-R_{z}HL_{X}\theta+R_{t}(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\eta,

(112)

or equivalently
[X,R_{t}]=0
and

-d(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)-(L_{X}R_{z})H\theta+R_{z}(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)\theta-R_{z}HL_{X}\theta+R_{t}(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\eta-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta=0.

(113)

On the other hand, we have that

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\eta=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner\eta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\eta,

(114)

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\theta&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta\\
&=-(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\end{split}

(115)

and

\begin{split}[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner d\theta&=L_{X}(X_{H}\lrcorner d\theta)-X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}d\theta\\
&=L_{X}(dH-R_{z}H\theta-R_{t}H\eta)-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta\\
&=dL_{X}H-XR_{z}H\theta-R_{z}HL_{X}\theta-XR_{t}H\eta-R_{t}HL_{X}\eta-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta\\
&=d(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)-R_{z}(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\theta-R_{t}(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\eta\\
&-d(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)-(L_{X}R_{z})H\theta+R_{z}(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{x}\theta)\theta-R_{z}HL_{X}\theta+R_{t}(X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\eta\\
&-(L_{X}R_{t})H\eta-R_{t}HL_{X}\eta-X_{H}\lrcorner dL_{X}\theta.\end{split}

(116)

So, by using equations (
113
) and (
116
),
we conclude that
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if
[X,R_{t}]=0
,

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\eta=0,

(117)

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\theta=-(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)

(118)

and

[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner d\theta=d(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)-R_{z}(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\theta-R_{t}(L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta)\eta,

(119)

i.e.,
X
is the infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,H)
if and only if
[X,R_{t}]=0
and
[X,X_{H}]
is a Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian function
L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta
.
∎

Finally, we also have

Corollary 10
.

Scaling symmetries are infinitesimal generators of one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations.

Proof.

If
X
is a scaling symmetry of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
then
[X,X_{H}]=(\Lambda-1)X_{H}
(where
\Lambda
is the degree of
X
), which is a Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian function
(\Lambda-1)H
.
∎

However, as in the cosymplectic case, we have that if
W
is an infinitesimal dynamical symmetry of
(M,\theta,\eta,H)
, then its flow does not necessarily preserve
\eta
(nor
R_{t}
).
Therefore, it does not necessarily define a group of canonoid transformations.
Since we are interested in symmetries related to canonoid transformations, we thus consider a subset of the set of infinitesimal dynamical symmetries.

Corollary 11
.

If
W
is such that
[W,X_{H}]=0
and
[W,R_{t}]=0
, then its flow defines a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.

Proof.

If
W
is such that
[W,X_{H}]=0
and
[W,R_{t}]=0
,
then
[W,X_{H}]=0
is a Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian function
L_{X}H+X_{H}\lrcorner L_{X}\theta=-[X,X_{H}]\lrcorner\theta=0
.
So the flow
\varphi
of
W
defines a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations.
∎

In addition, we have that for each
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the new Hamiltonian function is

K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
. Indeed,
\varphi_{s}^{*}X_{H}=X_{H}
and
\varphi_{s\ast}R_{t}=R_{t}
and we have

(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta)(p)=\theta_{\varphi_{s}(p)}(\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H})=\theta_{\varphi_{s}(p)}(X_{H})=-H(\varphi_{s}(p))=-(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)(p)

(120)

and for every
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
we have

\begin{split}(X_{H}\lrcorner\varphi_{s}^{*}d\theta)(Y)&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}d\theta)(X_{H},Y)\\
&=d\theta(\varphi_{s\ast}X_{H},\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=(X_{H}\lrcorner d\theta)(\varphi_{s\ast}Y)\\
&=\varphi_{s}^{*}(X_{H}\lrcorner d\theta)(Y)\\
&=(\varphi_{s}^{*}dH-(\varphi_{s\ast}R_{z})(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta-(\varphi_{s\ast}R_{t})(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\varphi_{s}^{*}\eta)(Y)\\
&=(d\varphi_{s}^{*}H-(\varphi_{s\ast}R_{z})(\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta-(R_{t}\varphi_{s}^{*}H)\eta)(Y)\end{split}

(121)

i.e. the vector field
X_{H}
is Hamiltonian with respect to the cocontact structure
(\varphi_{s}^{*}\theta,\eta)
with Hamiltonian function
K_{s}=\varphi_{s}^{*}H
.

In
[
42
]
it has been shown that from a given canonoid transformation for a
good
cocontact Hamiltonian system one can obtain the corresponding constants of motion, provided the Hamiltonian functions are invariant under the flow of the time Reeb vector field.
It is worth remarking that in our case the cocontact Hamiltonian system needs not be good.
Therefore in the general case we have that if
\varphi_{s}

is a one-parameter group of canonoid transformations for
(M,\theta,\eta,H)

with new Hamiltonian functions
K_{s}
and if for each
s
we have
R_{t}K_{s}=0
, then
K_{s}
are dissipated quantities;
in this case, the quotients
K_{s}/K_{s^{\prime}}
(whenever well defined) are constants of motion.

6
Conclusions

We have considered different types of symmetries in several classes of Hamiltonian systems,
namely symplectic, cosymplectic, contact and cocontact. In each case we
have analyzed their mutual relationships and the associated Noether-type theorems.
In our main results we have shown: a characterization of canonoid transformations
for the cosymplectic, contact and cocontact case in a manner that is completely similar to the symplectic case (Theorems
4
,
7
and
10
); with this, we have shown that scaling symmetries are always the generators of one-parameter groups of canonoid transformations (Corollaries
3
,
7
and
10
); finally, we have discussed the conditions that guarantee that to each one-parameter group of canonoid transformations there corresponds a one-parameter family of dissipated quantities, in analogy with the symplectic case where the associated functions are conserved quantities.

Our results provide a general toolkit to study scaling symmetries and canonoid transformations in depth.
Given the current interest in these types of symmetries in the mathematical and physical literature, we expect that this work can help the qualitative analysis of several physical systems.
Moreover, we foresee that a more general definition of canonoid transformations can be applied to the cosymplectic and cocontact cases, one that does not necessarily preserve the corresponding 1-form
\eta
. It will be interesting to study this more general class of symmetries in the future. In addition, we would like to study canonoid transformations in general Jacobi structures, not necessarily the ones arising from contact or cocontact forms.
Finally, it will be also relevant to place some of the results obtained in this work within the context of the reformulation of contact systems proposed in
[
49
,
50
]
.

Appendix A
Symplectic, cosymplectic, contact and cocontact Hamiltonian systems

In this appendix we review briefly the main definitions and properties of Hamiltonian systems on symplectic, cosymplectic, contact and cocontact manifolds that are used in the main text.

A.1
Symplectic Hamiltonian systems

We present a brief review of symplectic geometry and of the formulation of (time-independent) symplectic Hamiltonian mechanics (for details see
[
5
,
10
,
19
,
4
,
8
]
).

Definition 28
.

Let
M
be a
2n
-dimensional smooth manifold.
A symplectic structure on
M
is a closed 2-form
\omega
on
M
such that

\omega^{n}\neq 0
. If
(\omega)
is a symplectic structure on
M
, we say that
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold of dimension
2n
.
Locally, around any point
p\in M
, there exist local coordinates
(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})
,
called canonical coordinates or Darboux coordinates, such that

\omega=dq^{i}\wedge dp_{i}.

(122)

To each
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
there corresponds a vector field
X_{f}
on
M
,
called the Hamiltonian vector field of
f
, defined through:

X_{f}\lrcorner\omega\ =\ df\ .

(123)

In canonical coordinates,
X_{f}
reads

X_{f}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}\ .

(124)

The assignment
f\longmapsto X_{f}
is linear, that is,

X_{f+\alpha g}\ =\ X_{f}+\alpha X_{g}\ ,

(125)

\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
Given
f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
, the Poisson bracket of
f
and
g
is defined by

\{f,g\}=X_{g}f=\omega(X_{f},X_{g}).

(126)

In canonical coordinates we have

\{f,g\}\ =\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_{i}}\,-\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q^{i}}\ .

(127)

The theory of time-independent (conservative) Hamiltonian systems
is naturally constructed within the mathematical formalism of symplectic geometry.
Given
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
, the dynamics of a system on
(M,\omega)
with Hamiltonian function
H
is given by the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
.
That is, the trajectories of the system

\psi(t)=(q^{1}(t),\cdots,q^{n}(t),p_{1}(t),\cdots,p_{n}(t))
are the integral curves of
X_{H}
.
From (
124
), we obtain that in canonical coordinates
they satisfy Hamilton’s equations of motion

\phantom{cccccccccccc}\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{q^{i}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\textrm{and}\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{p_{i}}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{i}},\hskip 18.49988pti=1,2,3,\ldots,n\ .

(128)

The evolution of a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
(a physical observable)
along the trajectories of the system is given by

\dot{f}=L_{X_{H}}f=X_{H}f=\{f,H\}\ ,

(129)

where
L_{X_{H}}f
is the Lie derivative of
f
with respect to
X_{H}

[
51
]
.
We say that
f
is a constant of motion of the system if it is constant along the trajectories of the system,
that is,
f
is a constant of motion if
L_{X_{H}}f=0
(equivalently,
\{f,H\}=0
).

A.2
Cosymplectic Hamiltonian systems

Here we provide some aspects of cosymplectic geometry and of the associated formulation of time-dependent Hamiltonian systems
(for more details see
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
).

Definition 29
.

Let
M
be a
(2n+1)
-dimensional smooth manifold.
A cosymplectic structure on
M
is a couple
(\Omega,\eta)
, where
\Omega
is a closed 2-form on
M
and
\eta
is a closed 1-form on
M

such that
\eta\wedge\Omega^{n}\neq 0
.
If
(\Omega,\eta)
is a cosymplectic structure on
M
we say that
(M,\Omega,\eta)
is a cosymplectic manifold.

Let
(M,\Omega,\eta)
be a cosymplectic manifold of dimension
2n+1
.
Locally, around any point
p\in M
,
there exist local coordinates
(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},t)
, called canonical coordinates or Darboux coordinates, such that

\Omega=dq^{i}\wedge dp_{i}\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\eta=dt.

(130)

Furthermore, there exists a distinguished vector field
R
on
M
, called the Reeb vector field, defined by

R\lrcorner\Omega=0\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptR\lrcorner\eta=1.

(131)

In canonical coordinates we have
R=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
.

To each
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
there corresponds a vector field
X_{f}
on
M
, called the Hamiltonian vector field for
f
, defined through:

X_{f}\lrcorner\Omega=df-(Rf)\eta\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptX_{f}\lrcorner\eta=0.

(132)

In canonical coordinates we have

X_{f}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}.

(133)

The assignment
f\longmapsto X_{f}
is linear, that is

X_{f+\alpha g}=X_{f}+\alpha X_{g},

(134)

\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
Given
f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
the Poisson bracket of
f
and
g
is defined by

\{f,g\}=X_{g}f=\Omega(X_{f},X_{g}).

(135)

In canonical coordinates, it reads

\{f,g\}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q^{i}}.

(136)

The theory of time-dependent Hamiltonian systems can be developed under the mathematical formalism of cosymplectic geometry
(see
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
).
Given
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
, the dynamics of a system on
(M,\Omega,\eta)

with Hamiltonian function
H
is given by the evolution vector field
E_{H}=X_{H}+R
.
The trajectories
\psi(t)=(q^{1}(t),\cdots,q^{n}(t),p_{1}(t),\cdots,p_{n}(t),t)
of the system are the integral curves of
E_{H}
,
which satisfy Hamilton’s equations of motion

\dot{q^{i}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{p_{i}}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{i}},\hskip 18.49988pt\dot{t}=1\,.

(137)

The evolution of a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
(an observable) along the trajectories of the system is given by

\dot{f}=L_{E_{H}}f=E_{H}f=X_{H}f+Rf=\{f,H\}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}.

(138)

We say that a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
is a constant of motion of the system if it is constant along the trajectories of the system,
that is,
f
is a constant of motion if
L_{E_{H}}f=0
(equivalently,
\{f,H\}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=0
).

A.3
Contact Hamiltonian systems

In this subsection we move to the formalism of contact Hamiltonian systems, which naturally describe a large class of dissipative systems
(for details see
[
13
,
12
,
14
]
),
and have also applications in thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, among others
[
15
]
.

Definition 30
.

Let
M
be a
(2n+1)
-dimensional smooth manifold.
A contact 1-form on
M
is a 1-form
\theta
such that
\theta\wedge d\theta^{n}\neq 0
.
If
\theta
is a contact 1-form on
M
, we say that
(M,\theta)
is a contact manifold.

There is a wider notion of contact manifolds:
some authors define a contact structure on a manifold as a maximally non-integrable distribution of hyperplanes
[
24
,
52
,
53
,
49
]
.
Locally, every contact structure is given by the kernel of a contact 1-form.
However, not every contact structure admits a global contact 1-form.
When a contact structure admits a global contact 1-form, the contact manifold is called co-oriented.
In this paper we restrict ourselves to co-oriented contact manifolds and we refer to them simply as contact manifolds (see
[
49
,
50
]
for the more general perspective on contact manifolds and on the related
Hamiltonian systems).

Let
(M,\theta)
be a contact manifold of dimension
2n+1
.
Locally, around any point
p\in M
, there exist local coordinates
(q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},z)
, called canonical or Darboux coordinates, such that

\theta=dz-p_{i}dq^{i}.

(139)

Furthermore, there exists a distinguished vector field
R
on
M
, called the Reeb vector field, defined by

R\lrcorner\theta=1\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptR\lrcorner d\theta=0.

(140)

In canonical coordinates we simply have
R=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
.

To each
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
there corresponds a vector field
X_{f}
on
M
, called the Hamiltonian vector field of
f
, defined through:

X_{f}\lrcorner\theta=-f\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptX_{f}\lrcorner d\theta=df-(Rf)\theta.

(141)

In canonical coordinates we have

X_{f}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}+\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}-f\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

(142)

It can be checked that the assignment
f\longmapsto X_{f}
is linear, that is

X_{f+\alpha g}=X_{f}+\alpha X_{g},

(143)

\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

While symplectic and cosymplectic manifolds are Poisson manifolds (the symplectic and cosymplectic structures define corresponding Poisson brackets),
a contact manifold is in general a Jacobi manifold,
i.e., the contact 1-form defines a Jacobi bracket
[
13
]
.
Given
f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
the Jacobi bracket of
f
and
g
is defined by

\{f,g\}=X_{g}f+fRg=\theta([X_{f},X_{g}]).

(144)

In canonical coordinates we have

\{f,g\}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q^{i}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_{i}}-g\right)-\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}-f\right).

(145)

Hamiltonian systems on contact manifolds are called contact Hamiltonian systems.
Given
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
, the dynamics of a system on
(M,\theta)
with Hamiltonian
H
is defined by the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
.
In canonical coordinates we have

X_{H}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial z}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}+\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}-H\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

(146)

The trajectories
\psi(t)=(q^{1}(t),\cdots,q^{n}(t),p_{1}(t),\cdots,p_{n}(t),z(t))
of the system are the integral curves of
X_{H}
,
which satisfy the contact version of Hamilton’s equations, namely

\dot{q^{i}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}},\hskip 28.45274pt\dot{p_{i}}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial z}\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\dot{z}=p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}-H.

(147)

The evolution of a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
(an observable) along the trajectories of the system reads

\dot{f}=L_{X_{H}}f=X_{H}f=\{f,H\}-fRH.

(148)

We say that a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
is a constant of motion of the system if it is constant along the trajectories of the system, that is,
f
is a constant of motion if
L_{X_{H}}f=0
(equivalently,
\{f,H\}-fRH=0
).

A.4
Cocontact Hamiltonian systems

This subsection, together with the previous ones, completes the notation and the language employed in this work.
Here we briefly review the formalism of time-dependent contact Hamiltonian systems through the so-called cocontact geometry recently introduced in
[
16
]
.

Definition 31
.

Let
M
be a
(2n+2)
-dimensional smooth manifold.
A cocontact structure on
M
is a couple
(\theta,\eta)
of 1-forms on
M
such that

\eta
is closed and
\eta\wedge\theta\wedge(d\theta)^{n}\neq 0
.
If
(\theta,\eta)
is a cocontact structure on
M
, we say that
(M,\theta,\eta)
is a cocontact manifold.

Let
(M,\theta,\eta)
be a cocontact manifold of dimension
2n+2
.
Locally, around any point
p\in M
,
there exist local coordinates
(t,q^{1},\cdots,q^{n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n},z)
, called canonical coordinates or Darboux coordinates, such that

\theta=dz-p_{i}dq^{i}\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\eta=dt.

(149)

Furthermore, there exist two distinguished vector fields
R_{z}
and
R_{t}
on
M
,
called the contact Reeb vector field and the time Reeb vector field respectively,
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}R_{z}\lrcorner\eta=0\\
R_{z}\lrcorner\theta=1\\
R_{z}\lrcorner d\theta=0\end{array}\right.

(150)

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}R_{t}\lrcorner\eta=1\\
R_{t}\lrcorner\theta=0\\
R_{t}\lrcorner d\theta=0\,.\end{array}\right.

(151)

In canonical coordinates, we have
R_{z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
and
R_{t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
.

To each
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
there corresponds a vector field
X_{f}
on
M
, called the contact Hamiltonian vector field of
f
,
defined through:

X_{f}\lrcorner\theta=-f,\hskip 28.45274ptX_{f}\lrcorner d\theta=df-(R_{z}f)\theta-(R_{t}f)\eta\hskip 28.45274pt\textit{and}\hskip 28.45274ptX_{f}\lrcorner\eta=0.

(152)

In canonical coordinates, we have

X_{f}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}+\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}-f\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

(153)

We observe that the assignment
f\longmapsto X_{f}
is linear, that is

X_{f+\alpha g}=X_{f}+\alpha X_{g},

(154)

\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, as for the case of contact manifolds, cocontact manifolds are Jacobi manifolds.
Indeed, given
f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)
the Jacobi bracket of
f
and
g
is defined by

\{f,g\}=X_{g}f+fR_{z}g.

(155)

In canonical coordinates, we have

\{f,g\}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q^{i}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_{i}}-g\right)-\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}-f\right).

(156)

Time-dependent contact Hamiltonian systems can be described within the framework of cocontact geometry.
Given
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
, the dynamics of a system on
(M,\theta,\eta)

with Hamiltonian
H
is given by the evolution vector field
E_{H}=X_{H}+R_{t}
.
In canonical coordinates

E_{H}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial z}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}+\left(p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}-H\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}.

(157)

The trajectories
\psi(s)=(t(s),q^{1}(s),\cdots,q^{n}(s),p_{1}(s),\cdots,p_{n}(s),z(s))
of the system are the integral curves of
E_{H}
,
and thus they satisfy the equations

\dot{q^{i}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}},\hskip 28.45274pt\dot{p_{i}}=-\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^{i}}+p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial z}\right),\hskip 28.45274pt\dot{z}=p_{i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}-H,\hskip 28.45274pt\dot{t}=1\,.

(158)

The evolution of a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
(an observable) along the trajectories of the system is given by

\dot{f}=L_{E_{H}}f=E_{H}f=X_{H}f+R_{t}f=\{f,H\}-fR_{z}H+R_{t}f.

(159)

We say that a function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
is a constant of motion of the system if it is constant along the trajectories of the system, that is,
f
is a constant of motion if
L_{E_{H}}f=0
(equivalently,
\{f,H\}-fR_{z}H+R_{t}f=0
).
