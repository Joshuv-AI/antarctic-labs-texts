Multiple-scattering model for the effective refractive index of dense suspensions of forward-scattering particles Journal: osajournal Publication type: Research Article
Alexander Nahmad-Rohen \authormark 1,* and Augusto García-Valenzuela \authormark 1

Abstract
We present a multiple-scattering model for the effective refractive index of an arbitrarily dense suspension of forward-scattering particles. The model provides a very simple formula for the effective refractive index of such a suspension and reproduces with high accuracy available experimental results. Furthermore, the derivation we present herein is mathematically transparent and enables us to obtain information on the underlying physical processes rather than obscuring them. We also provide insight into the extent of the model’s validity and a simple way to determine whether or not it will be valid for an arbitrary suspension. Due to its simplicity, analytical closedness and wide range of applicability, we believe the model can be used as a diagnostic tool for complex materials of vastly different natures.

Introduction

The study of optically complex materials —that is, heterogeneous mixtures of multiple components, each with its own optical properties, such as colloids— is inherently interesting and has applications in a wide variety of fields, including atmospheric physics
[
1
,
2
]
, astronomy
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, communications
[
4
]
, materials science
[
5
,
6
]
, biology
[
4
,
7
]
and medicine
[
4
,
7
,
8
]
. However, it is also, by its very nature, complicated. Effective-medium theory provides one way to study them; it involves the calculation of an effective electromagnetic response (including an effective refractive index) which encodes the optical and geometric properties of the material’s components. Within effective-medium theory, multiple models have been proposed throughout the years, starting with the Maxwell-Garnett model
[
9
]
, which neglects scattering by the different components, and culminating in van de Hulst’s well-known formula
[
1
]
, which is consistent to first order in the number density of the scattering components of the material and is a benchmark against which other models are very frequently compared. Most of these models feature the scattering amplitude of the particles that make up a complex material, typically in the forward direction, because they are fundamentally scattering models. Therefore, to properly study complex materials it is strictly necessary to understand how their various components scatter light.

It is important to emphasise the limitations of effective-medium models. For a material consisting of particles of the order of the wavelength or larger, they are appropriate for describing the refraction of light at the material’s edge and the propagation of light through the material (with an effective propagation constant, from which the aforementioned effective refractive index can be obtained), but not reflection at the material’s edge
[
10
,
11
]
. This limitation is due to the inherent non-locality of such systems
[
12
]
.

Nevertheless, within its limits of applicability, the effective refractive index is a useful tool to optically characterise complex materials, which we shall here take to consist of a number of particles suspended in a homogeneous medium called the matrix. It depends on predictable and often measurable ways on the amount of particles per unit volume (or, equivalently, the volume fraction, which is the volumetric concentration of particles or, in other words, the fraction of the suspension’s volume taken up by the particles; this quantity is often much more useful than the particle number density), the refractive indices of the particles and the matrix, the size of the particles and the wavelength of light.

The applicability of existing models to dense suspensions (volume fractions higher than about 0.1) has been widely debated
[
6
,
13
,
14
]
. The principal reason for this is the existence of dependent scattering — that is, the field scattered by any one particle in the suspension depends on the fields scattered by all other particles in the suspension, leading to a highly coupled system which is not always easy to treat mathematically. “Dependent scattering” is presently an ambiguous term for which no precise definition exists, as different definitions have been given by different authors
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
; here we will take the side of those who define it as a non-linear dependence of the effective refractive index on the particle number density
[
15
,
16
,
13
]
.

Despite van de Hulst’s formula having been shown to be widely applicable and sometimes even be accurate for dense suspensions of particles
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
, its validity for such suspensions remained controversial until recently
[
7
]
, when it was rigorously shown that for suspensions of large tenuous particles it can be valid up to volume fractions of about 0.45 with relatively small errors. While said proof will hopefully end such controversy, it was too limited by making strong assumptions about the particle and matrix properties. Furthermore, while the mathematics of said proof was unambiguous, the physics of light-matter interaction in dense suspensions was somewhat unclear.

A very recently proposed model
[
21
]
taking into account dependent scattering is applicable to dense suspensions of arbitrary particles. Once again, however, the complicated mathematics involved in the derivation of the relatively simple formula obscures the physical processes involved. Furthermore, while the model is applicable to a very wide range of possible suspensions of randomly positioned particles, the formula is computationally costly. Other works are even more mathematically complicated and thus hide the underlying physics further
[
22
]
, are applicable only to thin films rather than bulk matter
[
23
]
or require extremely tenuous particles
[
24
,
25
]
.

Thus, most effective-medium models remain unsatisfactory for the description of the optical properties of dense suspensions; either they are restricted to small particles and/or dilute suspensions, they involve complicated mathematical expressions which hide the underlying physics and cannot be solved analytically, or both. Thus, an analytical formula for the effective refractive index of a dense suspension remains elusive.

In this work, we present a detailed derivation of a formula for the effective refractive index of suspensions of particles which scatter light mainly in the forward direction, which we call “highly-forward-scattering particles” here. The formula takes into account dependent scattering and will be seen to be valid for surprisingly dense suspensions (up to, and possibly exceeding, volume fractions of 0.4 in some cases; note that 0.4 is already close to the maximum possible density for identical spherical particles, which is about 0.74). Our model is based, initially, on the quasi-crystalline approximation
[
26
]
. In the process, we provide insight into the theory of the effective refractive index in general, into when dependent scattering arises and how it affects our treatment of the problem, and into the physics of the interaction of highly-forward-scattering particles with light. For example, it shall be seen that particle correlation functions cease to become important as the particles become increasingly forward-scattering. Our final formula is essentially as simple as van de Hulst’s, and we have aimed to make both the mathematics and the physics as transparent as possible. This is in contrast to the traditional approach to the quasi-crystalline approximation, which is to obtain numerical solutions to a self-consistent integral equation, which is computationally costly and provides no physical insight into the relationship between the effective refractive index and the parameters of the suspension.

We then show that our model reproduces experimental results available in the scientific literature for suspensions of forward-scattering particles with very high accuracy even at high volume fractions. We further discuss when our formula is valid and when it is not, and we provide examples of real systems to which it is applicable, contributing to optical diagnosis of such systems. An in-depth analysis of the application of our model to some such systems, however, remains a topic for future articles.

2
General multiple-scattering theory

Let us consider a plane wave
\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}_{0}\,e^{ik_{\text{m}}z}
travelling in the
z
direction through a homogeneous medium (which we call the matrix) with refractive index
n_{\text{m}}
and entering at
z=0
a region consisting of a suspension of
N
identical homogeneous particles suspended in the matrix. Here
k_{\text{m}}=n_{\text{m}}k
, where
k
is the wave number in vacuum.

The total field at an arbitrary point
\vec{r}
in the matrix is

\displaystyle\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}(\vec{r}),

(1)

where
\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}
is the total scattered field, equal to the sum of the fields scattered by the individual particles of the suspension.

The field scattered by any given particle, which we identify with the index
j
and whose centre is located at
\vec{r}_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})
, is a function of the field
\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}
that excites that particle. We may write

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}(\vec{r})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{Q}_{j}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}](\vec{r}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{j}
is a linear operator which tells us how the
j
th particle scatters light.
\mathcal{Q}_{j}
is, strictly speaking, an integral operator where the integration occurs over the whole volume available to the particle (i.e. the whole volume of the suspension of particles)
[
26
,
27
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{j}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}](\vec{r})=\displaystyle\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{\mathcal{G}(\vec{r},\vec{r}\,^{\prime})\mathcal{T}_{j}(\vec{r}\,^{\prime}-\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}\,^{\prime\prime}-\vec{r}_{j})\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}(\vec{r}\,^{\prime\prime})}\,\text{d}{x^{\prime\prime}}}\,\text{d}{y^{\prime\prime}}}\,\text{d}{z^{\prime\prime}}}\,\text{d}{x^{\prime}}}\,\text{d}{y^{\prime}}}\,\text{d}{z^{\prime}},

(3)

where ,
\mathcal{T}_{j}
is the
j
th particle’s dyadic transition operator and
\mathcal{G}
is the dyadic Green’s function (throughout this work, an integral without integration limits is understood to be over the entirety of the suspension’s volume – or, in the case of Fourier integrals, from
-\infty
to
\infty
).
\mathcal{T}_{j}
is such that the integrand is zero outside the particle’s volume.

In turn, within the
j
th particle’s volume,
\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}](\vec{r}),

(4)

where
\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}
is the field exciting the
\ell
th particle within the
\ell
th particle’s volume, yielding a strongly coupled system of equations for the
N
exciting fields. Combining equations
1
,
2
and
4
, we obtain an expression for the total field in the matrix:

\displaystyle\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{Q}_{j}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}](\vec{r})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\mathcal{Q}_{j}\!\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}]\right]\!(\vec{r}).

(5)

We could continue iterating on the above, writing

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{q\neq\ell}\mathcal{Q}_{q}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{q}](\vec{r})

(6)

and so on, yielding

\displaystyle\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{Q}_{j}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}](\vec{r})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\mathcal{Q}_{j}\!\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}]\right]\!(\vec{r})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\sum_{q\neq\ell}\mathcal{Q}_{j}\!\left[\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}\!\left[\mathcal{Q}_{q}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}]\right]\vphantom{\frac{b}{b}}\!\right]\!(\vec{r})+\ldots.

(7)

The beauty of this representation of the total field is that each scattering order is presented as a separate term; beginning with the terms with sums in them, the first term is the incidentfield scattered once (by the
j
th particle), the second term is the incident field scattered twice (first by the
\ell
th particle and then by the
j
th particle) and so on. Because the term corresponding to
s
th-order scattering (for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
) has
s
nested sums, it is proportional to
N(N-1)^{s-1}
, which, for large
N
, is approximately equal to
N^{s}
. This is because the outermost sum in each term (with index
j
) is over the entire collection of particles and every other sum (with indices other than
j
) is over all particles except one.

Since equation
7
is an infinite series, we truncate it at some value of
s
(this is usually done at the first-order-scattering term). All terms on the right side of the truncated series are then dependent on
\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}
except for the last one, which is dependent on the field exciting the particle with the index corresponding to the final (
s
th) nested sum in that term. No approximations have been made thus far, but calculating the aforementioned exciting field in the final term of the truncated series can sometimes be difficult.

The usual approach at this stage is to take the configurational average of
\vec{E}
— that is, the average of
\vec{E}
over all possible configurations (positions and orientations) of the particles. This is appropriate because we do not know the exact positions and orientations of the particles. This average is the coherent component of the light transmitted through the suspension and will be called the coherent field throughout this work; we denote it by
\vec{E}^{\text{\,c}}\equiv\langle\vec{E}\rangle
.

The averages are calculated by integrating over the entire volume of the suspension (we neglect writing the integrals over particle orientations for the sake of clarity):

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,c}}(\vec{r})

\displaystyle=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{g(\vec{r}_{j})\mathcal{Q}_{j}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}](\vec{r})}\,\text{d}{x_{j}}}\,\text{d}{y_{j}}}\,\text{d}{z_{j}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=\,\,}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{g(\vec{r}_{j})\mathcal{Q}_{j}\!\left[\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}](\vec{r})}\,\text{d}{x_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{y_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{z_{\ell}}\right]\!(\vec{r})}\,\text{d}{x_{j}}}\,\text{d}{y_{j}}}\,\text{d}{z_{j}}+\ldots.

(8)

In the above,
g(\vec{r}_{j})
is the probability density that the
j
th particle is located within a volume differential
\text{d}{x_{j}}\,\text{d}{y_{j}}\,\text{d}{z_{j}}
around
\vec{r}_{j}
.
g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})=c(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}_{\ell})g(\vec{r}_{\ell})
is the conditional probability density that the
\ell
th particle is located within a volume differential
\text{d}{x_{\ell}}\,\text{d}{y_{\ell}}\,\text{d}{z_{\ell}}
around
\vec{r}_{\ell}
given that the
j
th particle is located at
\vec{r}_{j}
;
c(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}_{\ell})
is the two-particle correlation function. Subsequent terms will include
s
-particle correlation functions with
s>2
.

It is here implicit that, when we write
g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})
, integration over all particles other than the
j
th and
\ell
th ones has already been performed; this is not shown for clarity. The analogous is true for higher-order terms.

The calculation thus becomes increasingly complicated and impractical the more terms of the series are taken. It is usual to truncate the series (as stated, at the first-order term) and replace the
\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}
in the last remaining term with
\vec{E}^{\text{\,c}}
, which is called the effective-field approximation. This yields a model which is self-consistent to first order in the particle volume density. However, this is not the only approach.

If all particle orientations occur with equal probability and the particles are homogeneously distributed over the volume
V
of the suspension, then, by rotational and translational symmetry,
\vec{E}^{\text{\,c}}
must be a plane wave propagating in the
z
direction, just like
\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}
, but with an effective propagation constant
k_{\text{e}}=n_{\text{e}}k
, where
n_{\text{e}}
is the effective refractive index of the suspension and is what we are after. We write
\vec{E}^{\text{\,c}}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,c}}_{0}e^{ik_{\text{e}}z}
.

3
The model

Here we will truncate the series at the 2nd-order-scattering term and focus on the average field exciting the
j
th particle. This is called the quasi-crystalline approximation
[
26
]
. This will turn equation
4
into an expression correct to first order in the number density
\rho
of the particles (or, equivalently, in the volume fraction
f
), which we can then insert into equation
2
to yield an expression for
\vec{E}^{\text{\,c}}
correct to second order in
\rho
. We will not explicitly calculate this expression for the coherent field, since we are only interested in finding
n_{\text{e}}
, but this shows that our model is self-consistent to second order in
\rho
.

Once our self-consistent expression for the average field exciting the
j
th particle is established, we will solve it for
k_{\text{e}}
in order to obtain a simple formula for the effective refractive index
n_{\text{e}}
of the suspension. We will first assume the particles are spherical and then generalise the model to suspensions of arbitrarily shaped particles.

3.1
The average field exciting the
j
th particle

The term on which
\mathcal{Q}_{j}
operates is the average field exciting the
j
th particle,
\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}j}
(the subindex
j
outside the average brackets indicates that this configurational average is over all particles other than the
j
th one; the
j
th particle’s position remains fixed). This is given by taking the configurational average on both sides of equation
4
:

\displaystyle\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}j}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}[\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}](\vec{r})}\,\text{d}{x_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{y_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{z_{\ell}}.

(9)

Instead of iterating as in the preceding section in order to find an expression for
\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}j}
in powers of
\rho
, we will henceforth assume that the field exciting the
\ell
th particle can be replaced by its configurational average (fixing the
\ell
th particle’s position), which, by rotational and translational symmetry, is a plane wave, just like the coherent field:

\displaystyle\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}j}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\int{\!\!\!\int{\!\!\!\int{g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}[\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}\ell}](\vec{r})}\,\text{d}{x_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{y_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{z_{\ell}}.

(10)

This is the self-consistency equation in the quasi-crystalline approximation
[
26
]
. The field scattered by the
\ell
th particle, denoted by
\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}
, will thus, from now on, be assumed to be the field scattered by a particle on which the plane wave
\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}\ell}
is incident:

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(\vec{r})=\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}[\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}\ell}](\vec{r}).

(11)

If the particles scatter mostly in the forward direction, then the field scattered by the
\ell
th particle only contributes to the field at the
j
th particle’s position,
\vec{r}_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})
, if
z_{\ell}+a\leqslant z_{j}-a
(where
a
is a measure of the size of a particle measured from the particle’s centroid; for example, for spherical particles
a
is their radius), so we insert a Heaviside function
\Theta(z_{j}-z_{\ell}-2a)
in the integral in equation
10
, which is equivalent to changing the upper integration limit in the integral over
z_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}j}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\int_{0}^{z_{j}-2a}{\int{\!\!\!\int{g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(\vec{r})}\,\text{d}{x_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{y_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{z_{\ell}}.

(12)

By the same logic, we write
g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})\approx 1/V
. Since, a priori, there is no constraint on the position of any given particle (ignoring the presence of all other particles), we have
g(\vec{r}_{\ell})=1/V
. Thus, we have the approximation
c(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}_{\ell})\approx 1
in equation
10
. We justify this form of
g(\vec{r}_{\ell}|\vec{r}_{j})
as follows: In the simplest case,
c(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}_{\ell})
would be the hole correlation function, where the probability that the
\ell
th particle is at
\vec{r}_{\ell}
is zero in a vicinity of the
j
th particle (because particles cannot overlap) and uniform elsewhere; the simplification of this to
c(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}_{\ell})=1
occurs because we are only considering particles centred at
z_{\ell}\leqslant z_{j}-2a
, where there is no restriction because the
j
th particle is absent in that region. Even if
c(\vec{r}_{j},\vec{r}_{\ell})
were some other function, such as the Percus-Yevick correlation function
[
13
]
, this would only introduce a significant correction in a small (of the order of the particle size) region close to
\vec{r}_{j}
, which is negligible compared to the size of the space
z_{\ell}+a\leqslant z_{j}-a
. We therefore have

\displaystyle\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}j}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\frac{1}{V}\int_{0}^{z_{j}-2a}{\int{\!\!\!\int{\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(\vec{r})}\,\text{d}{x_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{y_{\ell}}}\,\text{d}{z_{\ell}}.

(13)

3.2
The average field scattered by the
\ell
th particle

We now turn our attention to
\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}
. At the
z=z_{\ell}+a
plane, called the expansion plane of the
\ell
th particle, the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the field scattered by the
\ell
th particle,
\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}
, is

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(k_{x},k_{y},z_{\ell}+a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\!\!\!\int{\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(x,y,z_{\ell}+a)\,e^{-i(k_{x}x+k_{y}y)}}\,\text{d}{x}}\,\text{d}{y},

(14)

where we have taken the convention (usual in mathematics) that the direct and inverse Fourier transforms both have a factor
1/\sqrt{2\pi}
per variable. The inverse Fourier transform of
\vec{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}
at the expansion plane is, of course,

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(x,y,z_{\ell}+a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\!\!\!\int{\vec{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(k_{x},k_{y},z_{\ell}+a)\,e^{i(k_{x}x+k_{y}y)}}\,\text{d}{k_{x}}}\,\text{d}{k_{y}}.

(15)

\displaystyle\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}\ell}(x,y,z\geqslant z_{\ell}-a)=\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell,0}\,e^{i\left(k_{\text{e}}(z_{\ell}-a)+k_{\text{m}}(z-z_{\ell}+a)\vphantom{b^{2}}\right)}

\ell
th particle is embedded in the matrix, so we must take the matrix propagation constant
k_{\text{m}}
starting from

. Since the expansion plane is located after the

We can write
\vec{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}
in terms of the field scattered by a hypothetical particle centred at

with the expansion plane passing through the origin of coordinates, which will make further calculations simpler. To do so, we employ the coordinate change
\xi:=x-x_{\ell}
,
\eta:=y-y_{\ell}
,
\zeta:=z-(z_{\ell}+a)

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell}(k_{x},k_{y},0)=\frac{e^{i\left(-k_{x}x_{\ell}-k_{y}y_{\ell}+k_{\text{e}}(z_{\ell}-a)+2k_{\text{m}}a\vphantom{b^{2}}\right)}}{2\pi}\int{\!\!\!\int{\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell,0}(x,y,z_{\ell}+a)\,e^{-i(k_{x}\xi+k_{y}\eta)}}\,\text{d}{\xi}}\,\text{d}{\eta}.

By defining

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell,0}(k_{x},k_{y}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\!\!\!\int{\vec{E}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell,0}(x,y,z_{\ell}+a)\,e^{-i(k_{x}\xi+k_{y}\eta)}}\,\text{d}{\xi}}\,\text{d}{\eta}

(20)

(i.e.
\vec{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{\,s}}_{\ell,0}
is the Fourier transform of

The effective refractive index

Substituting equation

\displaystyle\langle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j}\rangle_{{\textstyle\mathstrut}j}(\vec{r})=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}(\vec{r})+\sum_{\ell\neq j}\frac{2\pi i\left(e^{i\left(k_{\text{m}}z+(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})(z_{j}-3a)\vphantom{b^{2}}\right)}-e^{i\left(k_{\text{m}}z-(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})a\vphantom{b^{2}}\right)}\right)}{(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{2}V}\,S_{\ell}(0)\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{\ell,0}.

\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0}
in place of
\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{j,0}

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0}\,e^{i\left(k_{\text{e}}(z_{j}-a)+k_{\text{m}}(z-z_{j}+a)\vphantom{b^{2}}\right)}=\left(\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}_{0}+\frac{2\pi i\rho\left(e^{i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})(z_{j}-3a)}-e^{-i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})a}\right)}{(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{2}}\,S(0)\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0}\right)e^{ik_{\text{m}}z},

(35)

where

is the particle number density. Here we see that our treatment of the problem is indeed consistent to second order in

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0}\,e^{i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})(z_{j}-a)}=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}_{0}+\frac{2\pi i\rho\left(e^{i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})(z_{j}-3a)}-e^{-i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})a}\right)}{(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{2}}\,S(0)\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0},

(36)

which can be split into two even simpler expressions by noting that the equality with only the terms with

\displaystyle\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0}\,e^{-i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})a}\,e^{i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})z_{j}}=\frac{2\pi i\rho\,e^{-3i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})a}\,e^{i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})z_{j}}}{(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{2}}\,S(0)\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0}

\displaystyle 0=\vec{E}^{\text{\,i}}_{0}-\frac{2\pi i\rho\,e^{-2i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})a}}{(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{2}}\,S(0)\vec{E}^{\text{\,e}}_{0}.

\displaystyle k_{\text{e}}=k_{\text{m}}\left(1+\frac{2\pi i\rho S(0)\,e^{-2i(k_{\text{e}}-k_{\text{m}})a}}{k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{3}}\right)\!,

]

\displaystyle k_{\text{e}}=k_{\text{m}}\left(1+\frac{2\pi i\rho S(0)}{k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{3}}\right)\!.

(40)

As a correction to this, let us iterate once and insert van de Hulst’s formula in place of
k_{\text{e}}
on the right side of equation
39

k
to obtain the effective refractive index of the suspension,

\displaystyle n_{\text{e}}=n_{\text{m}}\left(1+\frac{2\pi i\rho S(0)\,e^{\frac{4\pi\rho S(0)a}{k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{2}}}}{k_{\text{m}}\,\!^{3}}\right).

(41)

One might consider the possibility of iterating further and replacing
k_{\text{e}}
on the right side of equation
39
with the expression that comes from equation
41
. However, as we have stated, our model is self-consistent to second order. By doing this and performing the corresponding algebra, it is easy to see that successive iterations will not affect the term which is of second order in
\rho
and
S(0)
, only higher-order terms. We therefore deem further iterations unnecessary. In fact, for values of
f=\rho v
(where
v
is the volume of one particle) very close to dense packing, it could be detrimental to continue iterating, as it could introduce spurious corrections to high-order terms. We believe that such high densities would require a higher-order treatment of the problem from the beginning (equation
7
) and that in this case
N
-particle correlations (with
N

4
that this first iteration is enough to reproduce experimental results accurately.

For non-spherical particles, the forward scattering amplitude of a particle depends on its orientation. For random systems where particle orientation and position are uncorrelated, one must thus average
S(0)
over all the possible particle orientations and use that in place of
S(0)
in equation
41

\displaystyle\langle S(0)\rangle=\int{P(\Omega)S(0)}\,\text{d}{\Omega},

(42)

where
\Omega
represents the orientation of a particle and

is the probability density function for particle orientation.

For small values of
\rho S(0)
, equation
41
reduces to van de Hulst’s formula. Note that this does not necessarily imply small
\rho

6
Conclusions

We have derived a model for the effective refractive index
n_{\text{e}}
of a suspension of identical particles. The model was derived unambiguously from fundamental scattering theory assuming only that the particles in question scatter mostly in the forward direction; it has the advantage of providing an analytical expression for
n_{\text{e}}
rather than being a conceptually difficult and computationally costly numerical simulation. It is self-consistent up to terms of second order in the particle number density
\rho
(or, equivalently, in the volume fraction
f
). Though the formula is the same one that appeared in a previous work
[
7
]
, the result is much more general, as it does not require that the particles be large and tenuous, merely that they scatter mostly in the forward direction, and the derivation presented here is much more transparent both mathematically and physically. For instance, although the two-particle correlation function is present in our derivation, we have shown that the forward-scattering assumption makes the form this function takes unimportant. Our model’s consistency with experimental data confirms this.

We have shown that our model reproduces remarkably well the (admittedly scarce) experimental data on
\text{Im}\!\left({n_{\text{e}}}\right)
for suspensions of medium and large forward-scattering particles available in the scientific literature; this is true even for dense suspensions where almost 40% of the volume is taken up by the particles. Whether the model can also accurately calculate
\text{Re}\!\left({n_{\text{e}}}\right)
and whether a similar model can properly calculate the effective refractive index of semi-ordered and ordered suspensions are things that remain to be seen; this will require appropriate experimental data to become available. Presently, however, this proves that
\text{Im}\!\left({n_{\text{e}}}\right)
can be used as a tool for the diagnosis of dense suspensions of forward-scattering particles.

Because our model is applicable to any suspension of forward-scattering particles, it is important to know exactly what “forward-scattering particles” means in this context. Previous works
[
8
,
7
,
25
,
44
,
45
]
have restricted this to particles which are simultaneously large with respect to the wavelength (
ka\gg 1
) and tenuous (
\Delta_{n}\ll 1
), for which the anomalous-diffraction approximation is valid (here, however, we do not require this approximation). Our comparison with experiment and subsequent analysis of the angular scattering of light by the types of particles involved in said experiment have shown that a less restrictive, but still appropriate, definition is “any particle for which at least 75% of its scattered light is scattered at angles below
\pi/6
”. We have shown a map of a region in
(\Delta_{n},ka)
space which is of interest in various fields of science, engineering and medicine and marked not only which portion of this region corresponds to forward-scattering particles, but also where certain highly important types of suspension fall on the map. The model we have presented is therefore applicable to a wide range of suspensions, including those with high volume fractions, such as artificial materials of particular industrial, chemical and pharmaceutical interest
[
ref-tadros-E
]
to most kinds of biological tissue
[
33
]
.

There is no reason our model should not be applicable to suspensions with absorbing matrix and/or particles; nowhere in our derivation have we assumed
n_{\text{m}}
and
n_{\text{p}}
are real. Determining the region of validity of the model, however, requires experimental data on such suspensions to become available.

We strongly believe this work can contribute to all-optical, non-destructive, rapid diagnosis of some blood diseases, determination of food quality and structural analysis of artificial materials, to name just a few applications.
