Local resetting with geometric confinement
Asaf Miron

Abstract
“Local resetting” was recently introduced to describe stochastic resetting in interacting systems where particles independently try to reset to a common “origin”. Our understanding of such systems, where the resetting process is itself affected by interactions, is still very limited. One ubiquitous constraint that is often imposed on the dynamics of interacting particles is geometric confinement, e.g. restricting rigid spherical particles to a channel so narrow that overtaking becomes difficult. We here explore the interplay between local resetting and geometric confinement in a system consisting of two species of diffusive particles: “bath” particles, and “tracers” which undergo local resetting. Mean-field analysis and numerical simulations show that the resetting tracers, whose stationary density profile exhibits a typical “tent-like” shape, imprint this shape onto the bath density profile. Upon varying the ratio of the degree of geometric confinement over particle diffusivity, the system is found to transition between two states. In one tracers expel bath particles away from the origin, while in the other they ensnare them instead. Between these two states, we find a special case where the mean field approximation becomes exact.

Following the recent introduction of local resetting, where interactions

Here we study the interplay between stochastic resetting and the geometrically
constrained dynamics of interacting diffusive particles. Envision
a narrow channel with rigid walls occupied by two species of diffusive
spherical particles that interact via short-ranged repulsion. Particles
of both species are completely identical except for two properties:
1) each species may have a different mass, and 2) the particles of
one of the species undergo local resetting, which here means that
an attempt to reset to a target location in the channel is successful
only if some fixed volume surrounding the target is vacant of any
other particle. Consider particles whose radius is not much smaller
than one quarter of the channel’s width, where interactions and geometric
confinement conspire to reduce the rate at which neighboring particles
overtake one another. Analytically tackling this interacting non-equilibrium
system is a very challenging task with current methods and tools.
Yet its main ingredients, the interplay between local resetting and
geometric constraints, can be studied using a toy model that is more
susceptible to analytical treatment. Extending the efforts of
[

26

we model the narrow channel setup via the stochastic dynamics of hopping
particles on a
1D

L

of the species undergoing local resetting as “tracer” particles,
while particles of the other species are called “bath” particles.
Hard-core exclusion interactions

by which particles are excluded from entering an occupied site, replace
the channel’s short-ranged repulsive interactions. The different diffusive
dynamics of each particle species in the channel, which follow from
their mass difference, are modeled by the different rates with which
particles attempt to hop into adjacent neighboring lattice sites,
setting the tracer hopping rate to

the geometric confinement imposed by the narrow channel’s walls is
modeled by an “overtaking rate”

, at which a lattice particle
attempts to exchange places with a neighboring particle at an adjacent

After formulating equations for the evolution of the mean bath and
tracer particle density profiles, we apply the MF approximation and
compute the profiles in the stationary limit

\frac{\varepsilon}{D}<1

2
The model

We aim to construct a lattice model to describe the dynamics of two
species of diffusive spherical particles, locally resetting tracers
of mass
m_{t}
and bath particles of mass
m_{b}
, that are confined
to a narrow periodic channel and subject to short-ranged repulsive
interactions (right panel of Fig.
1
). To this end,
consider a
1D
ring lattice of sites labeled
\ell=0,...,L-1
occupied
by
N
bath particles and
M
tracer particles, whose respective
mean densities
\overline{\rho}=N/L
and
\bar{\tau}=M/L
satisfy
\overline{\rho}+\overline{\tau}\leq 1
.
The system evolves in continuous time with the following stochastic
dynamics: a bath particle at site
\ell
attempts hopping to adjacent
neighboring sites
\ell+1
and
\ell-1
with rate
D
to each side.
Tracer particles similarly attempt hopping to neighboring sites, but
with rate
1
to each side. In addition to hopping, each tracer also
tries to reset its position to the “origin” site
\ell=0
with
rate
r
, independently of all other tracers, a process termed “local
resetting” in
[
25
]
. Two mechanisms mediate interactions
between the particles. The first is hard-core “exclusion”
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
,
by which hopping and resetting attempts that would lead a particle
into an occupied site are rejected. Correspondingly, each lattice
site can be occupied by one particle at most. The second mechanisms
is “exchange”, where a particle at site
\ell
attempts with rate

\varepsilon
to exchange sites with a neighboring particle at sites
\ell+1

and
\ell-1
. The attempt is rejected if the target site is vacant.
This mechanism has been previously used to model geometric constraints
in similar settings, including the narrow channel setup discussed
above
[
41
,
50
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
.
Note that since particles of the same species are indistinguishable,
considering exchanges between two particles of the same species is
immaterial. Figure
1
provides schematic illustrations
of the lattice dynamics (left panel) and the narrow channel setup
(right panel).

The different states of
\rho\left(x\right)
are most easily understood
upon fixing the value of the exchange rate
\varepsilon
to unity, thus setting

to escape the dense region via exchanges with tracers. Once it succeeds
in doing so, reaching a region where there is a high density on one
side and a low density on the other, the bath particle is more likely
to distance itself by hopping away from the origin, rather than to
remain stuck therein. The opposite happens in the trapped state
\varepsilon/D>1

has a higher chance to remain trapped in the dense region near the
origin. For
\varepsilon/D=1

D=1

a completely homogeneous environment, since hopping into a vacant
site and exchanging sites with a tracer occur with the same rate.

4
Evolution equations for the mean density profiles

We proceed by formulating the equations governing the evolution of
the mean bath and tracer density profiles. Let
\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\left(t\right)

denote the instantaneous occupation of site
\ell
by a bath particle
at time
t
, assuming the value
1
if a bath particle is present
and
0
otherwise, and let
\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\left(t\right)
denote
the same for tracers. Considering the configurations affecting the
occupation of a bath particle at site
\ell
and averaging over the
system’s stochastic dynamics, we obtain an evolution equation for
the mean bath density profile
\left\langle\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\left(t\right)\right\rangle

\partial_{t}\left\langle\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\right\rangle=D\left\langle\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}-\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\right)\left(\tilde{\rho}_{\ell+1}+\tilde{\rho}_{\ell-1}\right)\right\rangle-D\left\langle\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\left[\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{\ell+1}-\tilde{\tau}_{\ell+1}\right)+\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{\ell-1}-\tilde{\tau}_{\ell-1}\right)\right]\right\rangle

+\varepsilon\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{\ell+1}+\tilde{\rho}_{\ell-1}\right)\right\rangle-\varepsilon\left\langle\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{\ell+1}+\tilde{\tau}_{\ell-1}\right)\right\rangle.

(1)

The first term on the right-hand side describes the bath density gain
at site
\ell
due to configurations where a bath particle at site

\ell\pm 1
attempts hopping to site
\ell
with rate
D
. Notice
the “exclusion factor”
\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\left(t\right)-\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\left(t\right)\right)

weighing this term, which vanishes if site
\ell
is already occupied
by a bath or tracer particle, and is
1
otherwise. The second term
describes the loss of bath density due to the bath particle attempting
to leave site
\ell
by hopping into an adjacent site. The third and
fourth terms analogously describe gain and loss terms associated with
exchanges between bath and tracer particles, which are attempted with
rate
\varepsilon
. The equation for the mean tracer density is

\partial_{t}\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(\tilde{\tau}_{\ell+1}+\tilde{\tau}_{\ell-1}\right)\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}-\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\right)\right\rangle-\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\left[\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{\ell+1}-\tilde{\tau}_{\ell+1}\right)+\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{\ell-1}-\tilde{\tau}_{\ell-1}\right)\right]\right\rangle

+\varepsilon\left\langle\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{\ell+1}+\tilde{\tau}_{\ell-1}\right)\right\rangle-\varepsilon\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{\ell+1}+\tilde{\rho}_{\ell-1}\right)\right\rangle+\tilde{\chi}_{\ell},

(2)

and its construction is analogous to that of Eq. (
1
).
The two equations have nearly the same structure, with the exception
of the last term
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
on the right-hand side of Eq.
(
2
). This term, which describes the local resetting
of tracers

\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}r\left\langle\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{0}-\tilde{\tau}_{0}\right)\tilde{\tau}_{n}\right\rangle&\ell=0\\
-r\left\langle\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{0}-\tilde{\tau}_{0}\right)\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\right\rangle&\ell\neq 0\end{cases},

(3)

behaves differently at site
\ell=0
versus sites
\ell\neq 0
:
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell=0}

is a gain term at site
\ell=0
that accounts for the attempts made
by tracers, located anywhere besides the origin, to reset their position
to the origin with rate
r
, whereas
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell\neq 0}
describes
the loss of tracer density at sites
\ell\neq 0
due to resetting attempts
with rate
r
. The exclusion factor
\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{0}-\tilde{\tau}_{0}\right)

in
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
ensures that resetting attempts are rejected
if the origin is occupied.

\tau_{\ell}=\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\right\rangle
simplifies
Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) to

\frac{1}{D}\partial_{t}\rho_{\ell}=\nabla_{\ell}^{2}\rho_{\ell}+\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\nabla_{\ell}\left(\left\langle\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\tilde{\tau}_{\ell+1}\right\rangle-\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\tilde{\rho}_{\ell+1}\right\rangle\right),

(4)

and

\partial_{t}\tau_{\ell}=\nabla_{\ell}^{2}\tau_{\ell}-\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\nabla_{\ell}\left(\left\langle\tilde{\rho}_{\ell}\tilde{\tau}_{\ell+1}\right\rangle-\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\tilde{\rho}_{\ell+1}\right\rangle\right)+\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}.

(5)

It is now apparent that the mean density profiles
\rho_{\ell}
and

\tau_{\ell}
are affected by correlations that separate into two contributions:
“exchange correlations”, which appear in the second term on the
right-hand sides of both equations and are generated by the exchange
interactions, and “local resetting” correlations, which only enter
Eq. (
5
) via
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
and are
generated by the local resetting process. Particle conservation places
an additional constraint on
\rho_{\ell}
and
\tau_{\ell}
, requiring
that

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\rho_{\ell}\left(t\right)=N\text{ and }\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\tau_{\ell}\left(t\right)=M.

(6)

Notice that the exchange correlations vanish from both equations for

\varepsilon=D=1
. In this case at sites
\ell\neq 0
(in the “bulk” of
the system) Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
)
reduce to

\partial_{t}\rho_{\ell}=\nabla_{\ell}^{2}\rho_{\ell},

(7)

and

\partial_{t}\tau_{\ell}=\nabla_{\ell}^{2}\tau_{\ell}-r\left\langle\left(1-\tilde{\rho}_{0}-\tilde{\tau}_{0}\right)\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\right\rangle,

(8)

where the last term in the
\tau_{\ell}
equation comes from Eq. (
3
)
for
\ell\neq 0
. With the effect of correlations eliminated from Eq.
(
7
) for
\rho_{\ell}
, we can now solve it
independently of Eq. (
8
) for
\tau_{\ell}
.
Imposing periodic boundary conditions and bath particle number conservation

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\rho_{\ell}=N
yields the stationary solution
\overline{\rho}
.
Equation (
8
) for
\tau_{\ell}
is nearly identical
to the one considered in
[
25
]
, which studied identical
particles undergoing diffusion and local resetting. For a finite particle
density and large
L
, the stationary density profile obtained there
using the MF approximation was found to be remarkably similar to the
empirical profile obtained via numerical simulations of the model’s
dynamics, a result which was later validated in
[
26
]
.
Together with the support provided by the numerical evidence presented
in Sec.
3
, when local resetting is the only
source for correlations, as in Eqs. (
8
)
and (
7
), the MF approximation seems to become
exact in the thermodynamic limit. Applying the MF approximation to
the “full” Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
),
which contain general overtaking rates and general bath and tracer
diffusion rates, is correspondingly expected to provide an increasingly
accurate description of the model as
\varepsilon,D\rightarrow 1
. We comment that for
\varepsilon=1
but a general
D\neq 1
, Eq. (
5
) still contains correlations between the tracer occupation at site
\ell
and the bath occupation at the origin.

\rho_{\ell}

N

One may apply the procedure used in deriving Eqs. (

and
\left\langle\tilde{\tau}_{\ell}\right\rangle

Applying the MF approximation to Eqs. (

\frac{1}{D}\partial_{t}\rho_{\ell}=\nabla_{\ell}^{2}\rho_{\ell}+\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\nabla_{\ell}\left(\rho_{\ell}\tau_{\ell+1}-\tau_{\ell}\rho_{\ell+1}\right),

(9)

and

\partial_{t}\tau_{\ell}=\nabla_{\ell}^{2}\tau_{\ell}-\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\nabla_{\ell}\left(\rho_{\ell}\tau_{\ell+1}-\tau_{\ell}\rho_{\ell+1}\right)+\chi_{\ell},

(10)

where
\tilde{\chi}_{\ell}
in Eq. (

) is
replaced by

\chi_{\ell}=\begin{cases}\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}r\left(1-\rho_{0}-\tau_{0}\right)\tau_{n}&\ell=0\\
-r\left(1-\rho_{0}-\tau_{0}\right)\tau_{\ell}&\ell\neq 0\end{cases}.

(11)

Despite the apparent crudeness of uncontrollably factorizing correlations,
as shown in Sec.
3
, comparing the MF solutions
to numerical simulation results suggest that MF effectively captures
the salient features arising from the interplay between geometrically
constrained diffusion and local resetting (at least) in the stationary
limit
t\rightarrow\infty
. That said, we shall henceforth focus our attention
on the model’s
stationary
density profiles.

We seek solutions for the stationary density profiles of the form

\rho_{\ell}\cong\rho\left(x\right)
and
\tau_{\ell}\cong\tau\left(x\right)
,
where
x=\ell/L\in\left[0,1\right)
denotes the macroscopic distance and
\cong

is used to denoted asymptotic equality in the thermodynamic limit

L\rightarrow\infty
. If such solutions exist for all values of the problem’s
parameters and satisfy the boundary conditions, they are the unique
solutions of the problem. We shall thus self-consistently assume this
scaling. Expanding out terms of the form

u_{\ell\pm 1}=u\left(x\right)+\frac{1}{L}\partial_{x}u\left(x\right)+\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\partial_{x}^{2}u\left(x\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(L^{-3}\right),

(12)

while keeping the leading order behavior in large
L
, yields the
stationary bath density equation

0=\partial_{x}^{2}\rho+\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\left(\rho\partial_{x}^{2}\tau-\tau\partial_{x}^{2}\rho\right),

(13)

and the stationary tracer density equation

0=\partial_{x}^{2}\tau-\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\left(\rho\partial_{x}^{2}\tau-\tau\partial_{x}^{2}\rho\right)-rL^{2}\left(1-\rho_{0}-\tau_{0}\right)\tau.

(14)

It is interesting to notice that Eq. (
14
)
can only be affected by local resetting, diffusion, and exchange processes
if
\left(1-\rho_{0}-\tau_{0}\right)\propto L^{-2}
as
L\rightarrow\infty
, as
was previously noted and discussed in
[
25
]
. Otherwise,
the only contribution to
\tau
would come from local resetting. For
now we keep the densities at the origin
\rho_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
as
parameters whose value will ultimately be set by demanding self-consistency
with the stationary MF profiles at
x=0
. This approach allows us
to restrict our analysis to the “bulk” of the system, i.e. sites

\ell\neq 0
, explaining why the more general
\chi_{\ell}
is replaced
by
\chi_{\ell\neq 0}
in the last term of Eq. (
14
).

5.1
Relating
\rho
and
\tau

Our first step towards the solution of Eqs. (
13
)
and (
14
) involves relating the stationary
density profile
\rho\left(x\right)
to the stationary tracer density
profile
\tau\left(x\right)
. Rewriting the bath density Eq. (
13
)
as

-\rho\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\partial_{x}^{2}\tau=\left[1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau\right]\partial_{x}^{2}\rho,

(15)

and performing integration-by-parts yields

-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\left[\rho\left(y\right)\partial_{y}\tau\right]\biggl|_{y=0}^{x}+\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\int_{0}^{x}dy\partial_{y}\rho\partial_{y}\tau

=\left[\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau\left(y\right)\right)\partial_{y}\rho\right]\biggl|_{y=0}^{x}+\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\int_{0}^{x}dy\partial_{y}\tau\partial_{y}\rho.

(16)

The integrals in Eq. (
16
) cancel out, leaving

C_{0}-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\rho\partial_{x}\tau=\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau\right)\partial_{x}\rho,

(17)

where
C_{0}
collects the boundary terms and is given by

C_{0}=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\rho\left(0\right)\partial_{y}\tau\biggl|_{y=0}+\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau\left(0\right)\right)\partial_{y}\rho\biggl|_{y=0}.

(18)

We proceed by guessing that
C_{0}=0
and will show a-posteriori
that this guess is correct. If
C_{0}=0
Eq. (
17
)
becomes

-\frac{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\partial_{x}\tau}{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau}=\frac{\partial_{x}\rho}{\rho},

(19)

whose solution is

\rho\left(x\right)=C_{1}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau\left(x\right)\right),

(20)

where
C_{1}
is an integration constant. To set its value we use
the conservation of the number of bath and tracer particles in Eq.
(
6
), transforming the discrete conservation
laws to integral form as

\int_{0}^{1}dx\rho\left(x\right)=\overline{\rho}\text{ and }\int_{0}^{1}dx\tau\left(x\right)=\overline{\tau}.

(21)

Integrating Eq. (
20
) over the interval
\left[0,1\right]

and using Eqs. (
21
) sets the integration constant
to

C_{1}=\frac{\overline{\rho}}{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}}.

(22)

We have thus derived the relation between the bath density profile

\rho\left(x\right)
and the tracer density profile
\tau\left(x\right)

\rho\left(x\right)=\overline{\rho}\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau\left(x\right)}{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}}.

(23)

Verifying self-consistency of the assumption
C_{0}=0
, for
C_{0}

given in Eq. (
18
), is now immediate. Note that the denominator
in Eq. (
23
) only vanishes if
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}=1-\frac{1}{\overline{\tau}}
,
which cannot happen since
0<\overline{\tau}<1
so the right-hand side
is always negative while both
\varepsilon
and
D
are positive. Another remark is that it is important to recall that Eq. (
23
) was derived under the assumption that the two stationary profiles are scaling functions of
x=\ell/L
. This assumption will next be shown to be self-consistent for local resetting, since
(1-\rho_{0}-\tau_{0})
evidently scales as
\propto 1/L^{2}
.

5.2
Tracer density profile

We next use the relation between
\rho\left(x\right)
and
\tau\left(x\right)

in Eq. (
23
) to solve Eq. (
14
)
for
\tau\left(x\right)
which becomes

0=\partial_{x}^{2}\tau-\alpha^{2}\tau,

(24)

where the parameter
\alpha^{2}
is defined as

\alpha^{2}\coloneqq L^{2}r\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}}{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}-\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\overline{\rho}}\left(1-\tau_{0}-\rho_{0}\right).

(25)

The solution to Eq. (
24
) is simply

\tau\left(x\right)=A_{-}e^{-\alpha x}+A_{+}e^{\alpha x},

(26)

with the constants
A_{\pm}
set using symmetry about the origin,
which dictates
\tau\left(x\right)=\tau\left(1-x\right)
, and tracer
number conservation
\int_{0}^{1}dx\tau\left(x\right)=\overline{\tau}

of Eq. (
21
). The former yields
A_{-}=A_{+}e^{\alpha}

and the latter
A_{+}=\frac{\alpha\overline{\tau}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}{4\sinh\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}\right]}
,
so that we arrive at

\tau\left(x\right)=\frac{\alpha\overline{\tau}}{2}\frac{\cosh\left[\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)\right]}{\sinh\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}\right]}.

(27)

The stationary tracer density profile
\tau\left(x\right)
has the
same form as that found in
[
25
]
, which studied resetting
particles with exclusion. The only difference will appear when determining
the value of
\alpha
, which here also depends on the bath density at
the origin
\rho_{0}
. The tracer density profile
\tau\left(x\right)

exhibits the characteristic tent-like shape, that was first obtained
for the stationary distribution of a single resetting Brownian particle

[
1
]
.

Since the bath density profile
\rho\left(x\right)
is related to the
tracer density profile
\tau\left(x\right)
via Eq. (
23
),
it may appear that our solution is complete. However, at this point,

\alpha
still remains an unknown parameter. An equation for
\alpha
is
derived by demanding that
\tau\left(x\right)
at
x=0
be consistent
with the tracer density at the origin

\tau_{0}=1-\frac{\overline{\rho}}{\frac{D}{\varepsilon}+\left(1-\frac{D}{\varepsilon}\right)\left(\overline{\rho}+\overline{\tau}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(L^{-2}\right).

(28)

Equation (
28
) is obtained by first using Eq. (
23
)
to get
\rho_{0}=\overline{\rho}\frac{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau_{0}}{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}}
,
then solving Eq. (
25
) for
\tau_{0}
to leading order
in
L
, and finally demanding agreement with
\tau\left(x\right)

of Eq. (
27
) at
x=0
. The result is a transcendental
equation for
\alpha

\frac{\alpha\overline{\tau}}{2\tanh\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}\right]}=1-\frac{\overline{\rho}}{\frac{D}{\varepsilon}+\left(1-\frac{D}{\varepsilon}\right)\left(\overline{\rho}+\overline{\tau}\right)},

(29)

which we must solve numerically to compute the profiles. The denominator
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
29
) vanishes if
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}=1-\frac{1}{\overline{\rho}+\overline{\tau}}

but, since
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}>0
and
0<\overline{\rho}+\overline{\tau}<1
,
this equality cannot be satisfied as
1-\frac{1}{\overline{\rho}+\overline{\tau}}

is negative.

The set of Eqs. (
23
), (
27
),
and (
29
) constitute the stationary MF solutions to
the bath and tracer density profiles. An inspection of Eq. (
23
)
reveals that the ratio
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}
, which describes the competition
between the degree of geometric constraints and the bath diffusivity,
plays a crucial role in shaping the bath density profile
\rho\left(x\right)
.
Specifically, it determines the sign of
\rho\left(x\right)
’s curvature.
Taking a second derivative of the relation between
\rho\left(x\right)

and
\tau\left(x\right)
in Eq. (
23
) gives

\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\rho}{\partial_{x}^{2}\tau}=\frac{\overline{\rho}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{D}-1\right)}{1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}}.

(30)

Local resetting causes the tracer density to be highest at the origin,
while the tracers’ exchange and diffusive dynamics ensure that it
falls off rapidly (faster than linear) with distance from the origin.
This leads to a positive curvature for
\tau\left(x\right)
. As such,
the curvature of
\rho\left(x\right)
is set by the right-hand side
of Eq. (
30
). We immediately notice the two limits

\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\rightarrow 0
and
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\rightarrow\infty
, respectively corresponding
to
\partial_{x}^{2}\rho=-\frac{\overline{\rho}}{1-\overline{\tau}}\partial_{x}^{2}\tau<0

and
\partial_{x}^{2}\rho=\frac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{\tau}}\partial_{x}^{2}\tau>0
.
A closer look shows that the right-hand side of Eq. (
30
)
is negative for
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}<1
and positive for
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}>1
,
with
\rho\left(x\right)
’s curvature changing sign at
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}=1
.
This result can be understood as follows: When
D>\varepsilon
the bath particles
manage to diffuse away from the high tracer-density region surrounding
the origin. Since the high-density region decays strongly, once outside
its reach bath particles quickly spread out and inhabit the remaining
regions, implying a negative curvature for
\rho\left(x\right)
. But
when
D<\varepsilon
the bath particles are too slow and become trapped inside
the high-density region. The bath density thus remains slightly higher
near the origin and slowly falls-off as the tracer density diminishes,
indicative of a positive curvature for
\rho\left(x\right)
. The transition
in
\rho\left(x\right)
’s curvature is also reflected in the effective
MF potential
V\left(x\right)
that the bath particles experience
due to the tracer profile (see Fig.
5
). To find
V\left(x\right)

we compare
\rho\left(x\right)
to the equilibrium Boltzmann distribution

\overline{\rho}\frac{e^{-\beta V\left(x\right)}}{Z}
, where
Z=\int_{0}^{1}dxe^{-\beta V\left(x\right)}

is the partition function. We find

Z=\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}\right)^{-1},

(31)

and the potential
V\left(x\right)

V\left(x\right)=-\frac{\log\left[1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\tau\left(x\right)\right]}{\beta}.

(32)

Notice that the bath particles feel no external potential when
\varepsilon/D=1
,
which is consistent with their homogeneous profile. For
\varepsilon/D\neq 1

we can find the shape of
V\left(x\right)
near the origin by substituting

\tau\left(x\right)
of Eq. (
27
) into
V\left(x\right)

and expanding around
x=0
. This gives

V\left(x\right)=V_{0}-\frac{\alpha^{2}\overline{\tau}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)}{\alpha\overline{\tau}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\coth\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}\right]-2}\left|x\right|+\mathcal{O}\left(x^{2}\right),

(33)

with
V_{0}=\log\left[1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\coth\left[\frac{\alpha}{2}\right]\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{D}\right)\overline{\tau}\right]
,
which is a linear potential whose slope can be shown to change sign
at
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}=1
.

We conclude this section with two additional points that are worth
mentioning. As found in
[
25
]
, here too the resetting
rate
r
never enters the profiles in the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
. This
can be heuristically understood from the fact that, in this limit,
there is an infinite number of tracers in the system. Correspondingly,
there is an infinite number of tracers attempting to reset their positions
to the origin at any given moment. As long as the resetting attempt
rate
r
is finite (and independent of
L
), its precise value is
irrelevant. Another interesting point is that, although Eqs. (
13
)
and (
14
) depend on
\varepsilon
and
D
separately,
the MF profiles depend only on the ratio
\frac{\varepsilon}{D}

r
,
\varepsilon
, and

do individually
enter the higher order, finite-size corrections to the asymptotic
profiles.

6
Conclusion and discussion

This work studies the interplay of geometric constraints and “local
resetting”, where particles attempt stochastic resetting independently
of one-another, thus introducing interactions into the resetting process.
The dynamics associated with the classical setup of diffusive spherical
particles with short-ranged repulsive interactions confined to a narrow
channel is extended to include local resetting, and then its main
ingredients are encoded into the dynamics of hopping particles on
a
1D
lattice ring. The lattice contains two particles species,
locally resetting “tracers” of mean density
\overline{\tau}
and
non-resetting “bath” particles of mean density
\overline{\rho}
.
Their continuous time evolution dynamics mimic those of the narrow
channel setup: hard-core exclusion replaces the short-ranged interactions,
the different masses are modeled by setting the bath and tracer diffusion
rates to
1
and
D
respectively, and the channel’s width is replaced
by a finite exchange rate
\varepsilon
. Evolution equations for the mean
bath and tracer density profiles are derived, and then solved in the
stationary limit using the mean-field (MF) approximation in the thermodynamic
limit
L\rightarrow\infty
. The MF tracer density profile exhibits the typical
tent-like shape with a cusp at the origin, as found for a single resetting
diffusive particle
[
1
]
. Yet the interplay of local
resetting and geometric confinement manifests most dramatically in
the MF bath density profile
\rho\left(x\right)
, which transitions
between “repelled” and “trapped” states as the value of
\varepsilon/D

crosses
1
. For
\varepsilon/D<1
bath particles are strongly repelled from
the origin, with
\rho\left(x\right)
taking the shape of an inverted
tent with a negative curvature
\rho^{\prime\prime}\left(x\right)<0
. For
\varepsilon/D>1

the bath particle are too slow to escape the dense origin and remain
trapped. The shape of
\rho\left(x\right)
is then similar to that
of
\tau\left(x\right)
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}\left(x\right)>0
. For
\varepsilon/D=1

the bath particles experience a homogeneous environment, since hopping
into a vacant site and exchanging with a tracer happen with the same
rate, and we obtain
\rho\left(x\right)=\overline{\rho}
. While the
MF approximation successfully predicts the existence of both states
and the transition between them, there is no a-priori reason for it
to be exact in any regime. However, numerical investigations of the
model provide strong evidence to suggest that the MF approximation
does in fact become exact for
\varepsilon=D=1
as
L\rightarrow\infty
. This result,
whose underlying origins remain unclear, joins the observations of

[
25
,
26
]
where the MF approximation was found
to stand in unexpectedly good agreement with numerical simulation
results of models exhibiting local resetting.

Looking forward, many interesting open questions still remain unsolved
and demand attention. First and foremost, why does the MF approximation
seem to work well in models featuring local resetting? Is there any
way to adapt exact methods like the matrix product ansatz
[
51
,
52
]
,
which have been used to derive exact solutions for various lattice
models with exclusion interactions, to treat local resetting? Another
exciting direction is to explore the temporal evolution in the presence
of local resetting. While this is interesting for local resetting
in general, as dynamical phase transitions were found in the temporal
evolution of the density profile of a single resetting Brownian particle

[
9
]
, it is even more interesting in the context
of geometric confinement and tracer sub-diffusion
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
.
In addition, both experimental and theoretical research following
this line of work would of greatly benefit from pursuing a quantitative
relation between the parameters describing the narrow channel setup,
and the corresponding parameters of the lattice model.
