Intractability results for integration in tensor product spaces
Erich Novak, Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
In this paper we study lower bounds on the worst-case error of numerical integration in tensor product spaces. Thereby integrands are assumed to belong to d d -fold tensor products of spaces of univariate functions. As reference we use the N N -th minimal error of linear rules that use N N function values. The information complexity is the minimal number N N of function evaluations that is necessary such that the N N -th minimal error is less than a factor ε \varepsilon times the initial error, i.e., the error for N = 0 N=0 , where ε \varepsilon belongs to ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) . We are interested to which extent the information complexity depends on the number d d of variables of the integrands. If the information complexity grows exponentially fast in d d , then the integration problem is said to suffer from the curse of dimensionality. Under the assumption of the existence of a worst-case function for the uni-variate problem, which is a function from the considered space whose integral attains the initial error, we present two methods for providing good lower bounds on the information complexity. The first method is based on a suitable decomposition of the worst-case function. This method can be seen as a generalization of the method of decomposable reproducing kernels, that is often successfully applied when integration in Hilbert spaces with a reproducing kernel is studied. The second method, although only applicable for positive quadrature rules, has the advantage, that it does not require a suitable decomposition of the worst-case function. Rather, it is based on a spline approximation of the worst-case function and can be used for analytic functions. The methods presented can be applied to problems beyond the Hilbert space setting. For demonstration purposes we apply them to several examples, notably to uniform integration over the unit-cube, weighted integration over the whole space, and integration of infinitely smooth functions over the cube. Some of these results have interesting consequences in discrepancy theory.



Keywords:
Numerical integration, worst-case error, discrepancy, curse of dimensionality, quasi-Monte Carlo

Introduction

In this paper we study numerical integration of
d
-variate functions from spaces which are
d
-fold tensor products of identical normed function spaces of uni-variate functions. This is a typical setting that belongs to general linear tensor product problems, that are studied in a multitude of papers (see, e.g.,
[
13
,
14
]
and the references therein).

Let
(F_{1},\|\cdot\|_{1})
be a normed space of univariate integrable functions over the Borel measurable domain
D_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and let, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
(F_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
be a
d
-fold tensor product space

F_{d}=\underbrace{F_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes F_{1}}_{d-\mbox{{\scriptsize fold}}},

which consists of functions over
the domain
D_{d}=D_{1}\times\ldots\times D_{1}
(
d
-fold),
equipped with a crossnorm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
, i.e., for elementary
tensors
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=f_{1}(x_{1})\cdots f_{d}(x_{d})
with
f_{j}:D_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we have
\|f\|_{d}=\|f_{1}\|_{1}\cdots\|f_{d}\|_{1}
.
We do not not claim that the space
F_{d}
is uniquely defined by the requirements above.

Consider multivariate integration

I_{d}(f):=\int_{D_{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\mbox{for $f\in F_{d}$}.

The so-called initial error of this integration problem is

e(0,d)=\|I_{d}\|=\sup_{f\in F_{d}\atop\|f\|_{d}\leq 1}\left|I_{d}(f)\right|.

(1)

We approximate the integrals
I_{d}(f)
by algorithms based on
N
function evaluations of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\varphi(f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}),f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}),\ldots,f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{N})),

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary function and where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
are points in
D_{d}
, called integration nodes. Typical examples are linear algorithms of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}),

(2)

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
are in
D_{d}
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{N}
are real weights that we call integration weights. If
a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=a_{N}=1/N
, then the linear algorithm (
2
) is a so-called quasi-Monte Carlo algorithm, which is denoted by
A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}}
.

Define the worst-case error of an algorithm by

e(F_{d},A_{d,N})=\sup_{f\in F_{d}\atop\|f\|_{d}\leq 1}\left|I_{d}(f)-A_{d,N}(f)\right|.

(3)

It is well known (see
[
2
]
or
[
13
, Theorem 4.7]
) that in order to minimize the worst-case error it suffices to consider linear algorithms of the form (
2
). So it suffices to restrict the following considerations to linear algorithms.
We define the
N
-th minimal worst-case error as

e(N,d):=\min_{A_{d,N}}e(F_{d},A_{d,N})

where the minimum is extended over all linear algorithms of the form (
2
) based on
N
function evaluations along points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
from
D_{d}
and with real integration weights
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
the information complexity is defined as

N(\varepsilon,d)=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ e(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e(0,d)\}.

The information complexity of a problem (in the present paper we only consider integration problems) is a well studied concept in information based complexity. It is the minimal number of function evaluations (of “information”) that is necessary in order to be able to reduce the initial error by a factor of
\varepsilon
, the so-called error demand.

Usually one is interested in the grow rate of
N(\varepsilon,d)
when the error demand
\varepsilon
tends to 0 and the dimension
d
tends to infinity. A problem is called tractable, if
N(\varepsilon,d)
grows at most sub-exponential in
d
and in
\varepsilon^{-1}
when
d\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
. In literature many notions of tractability are studied in order to classify the sub-exponential grow rates like, e.g., weak tractability, polynomial tractability, strong polynomial tractabilty. See, for example, the trilogy
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. If, however,
N(\varepsilon,d)
grows at least exponentially fast in
d
and/or
\varepsilon^{-1}
for
d\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
, then the problem is said to be intractable. If, in particular,
N(\varepsilon,d)
grows at least exponentially fast in
d
for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
, then it is said that the problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

For a given (integration) problem it is an important question to decide whether it is tractable or not. In order to verify the possible curse of dimensionality, one needs good lower bounds on the
N
-th minimal error in dimension
d
. This, however, is often a very difficult task. There are some methods like the method of constructing suitable “fooling-functions” (also known as “bump functions”)
which are functions in the given space, which are zero along a given set of integration nodes, but which have a large value of the integral (see, e.g., the survey
[
3
, Section 2.7]
).

A strong method for proving lower bounds is the “method of reproducing kernels”
that was invented by Novak and Woźniakowski in
[
12
]
and which is presented in detail also in the book
[
14
, Chapter 11]
. However, this method can only be applied for integration problems in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces whose reproducing kernel
K_{1}
satisfies the (strong) property of being decomposable, which means that there exists a number
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that the sets

D_{(0)}:=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\leq a\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{(1)}:=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\geq a\}

are nonempty and

K_{1}(x,t)=0\quad\mbox{for $(x,t)\in D_{(0)}\times D_{(1)}\cup D_{(1)}\times D_{(0)}$,}

or whose reproducing kernel
K_{1}
has an additive part, which is decomposable in the above sense (see
[
14
, Section 11.5]
).

It is the aim of the present paper to reformulate the method of decomposable kernels in a way such that it can be applied to other settings beyond reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. Our approach is based on a decomposition of a so-called worst-case function, which is a function in the unit ball of the function space under consideration, whose integral attains the initial error. In general, such a worst-case function does not have to exist. Our results rely on the assumption of the existence of a worst-case function. We will assume that the worst-case function can be decomposed in a suitable way, which mimics the “kernel-decomposition” in the method of decomposable kernels. If this is the case, then we can show that the integration problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality. From this point of view our approach might be called the “method of decomposable worst-case functions”. The method will be presented in Section
2
. We discuss the cases of worst-case functions that are fully decomposable (Section
2.1
) as well as worst-case function which only have a decomposable additive part (Section
2.2
), where the latter can be seen as generalization of the method for non-decomposable kernels from
[
14
, Section 11.5]
beyond the Hilbert space setting.

In Section
3
we discuss several applications of the presented method. First we revisit the method of decomposable kernels and show that the new approach indeed comprises the “method of decomposable kernels” as a special case. Then we consider uniform integration of functions of smoothness
r
. The special case
r=1
shows that the
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored in a point
a\in(0,1)
suffers from the curse of dimensionality for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. The same holds true for the
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at
a\in(0,1)
. Finally, we consider weighted integration over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of functions of smoothness
r
.

The assumption of having a
suitably decomposable
worst-case function is rather strong. Furthermore, one of the conditions for the proposed decomposition is rather hard to check. This problem can be overcome when one restricts the consideration to positive linear rules. This will be done in Section
4
. We propose a spline approximation of the worst-case function which has the
advantage that it works completely without any decomposition;
hence it can be used for analytic functions.
Applications of this method are presented in Section
5
,
which concludes the paper.

(x)_{+}=x
if
x>0
and 0 if
x\leq 0
.
Furthermore, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
we use the notation

.

2
General quadrature rules

In the following we use a notation that is very close to the notation in
[
14
, Section 11.4]
with the intention to make it easier for the reader to compare our approach to the “method of decomposable kernels” as presented in
[
14
, Section 11.4]
.

Assume that there exists a worst-case function
h_{1}\in F_{1}
, i.e., a function
h_{1}\in F_{1}
which satisfies

e(0,1)=I_{1}\left(\frac{h_{1}}{\|h_{1}\|_{1}}\right),

where
e(0,1)=\|I_{1}\|
is the initial error for integration in
F_{1}
like in (
1
).
In addition we always assume that the tensor product

h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{1}(x_{j})

is a worst-case function for
d>1
and hence

e(0,d)=e(0,1)^{d}
is the initial error for integration in
F_{d}
.

Remark 1
.

As already mentioned, in general a worst-case function does not have to exist. For example take the anchored space

F_{1}=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ :\ f(0)=0,\,f\,\mbox{ abs. cont. and }\,f^{\prime}\in L_{1}([0,1])\},

where here and later on “abs. cont.” stands for “absolutely continuous”. As norm on
F_{1}
we use

\|f\|_{1,1}=\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime}(t)|\,{\rm d}t.

Then the norm of the integral operator
I_{1}
over
F_{1}
is 1, but, because of the anchor-condition and the continuity of the functions in
F_{1}
, there does not exist a function in the unit ball of
F_{1}
whose integral is 1.

Examples for integration problems with an existing worst-case function will be given throughout the paper.

2.1
Decomposable worst-case functions

Assume that a worst-case function
h_{1}
exists and can be decomposed in the form

h_{1}(x)=h_{1,(0)}(x)+h_{1,(1)}(x)\quad\mbox{for $x\in D_{1}$}

(4)

with the following properties:

D1

h_{1,(0)},h_{1,(1)}\in F_{1}
;

D2

there exists a decomposition point
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

D_{(0)}:=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\leq a\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{(1)}:=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\geq a\}

are non-empty and the support of
h_{1,(0)}
is contained in
D_{(0)}
and the support of
h_{1,(1)}
is contained in
D_{(1)}
;

D3

the integrals
I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})
and
I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})
are positive; and

D4

for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and every family
\mathcal{D}
of subsets of
[d]
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}\leq\left\|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}.

Some remarks are in order:

Remark 2
.

•

Obviously,
D_{(0)}\cup D_{(1)}=D_{1}
and
D_{(0)}\cap D_{(1)}\subseteq\{a\}
.

•

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in D_{d}
we have

\displaystyle h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{1}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}(h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})+h_{1,(1)}(x_{j}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})

and, in particular,

\left\|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}=\|h_{d}\|_{d}=\|h_{1}\|_{1}^{d}.

•

In view of the last item property
D4
can be interpreted as follows: let
g_{d}
be any restriction of
h_{d}
to arbitrary quadrants

\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}E_{j}\quad\mbox{where $E_{j}\in\{D_{(0)},D_{(1)}\}$,}

and zero out off these quadrants, then
\|g_{d}\|_{d}\leq\|h_{d}\|_{d}.
Indeed, later we will use property
D4
in exactly this vein.

Now we state our first theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(F_{1},\|\cdot\|_{1})
be a normed space of univariate functions over
D_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and let for
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
(F_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
be the
d
-fold tensor product space equipped with a cross norm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
. Assume in
F_{1}
exists a worst-case function
h_{1}
which can be decomposed according to (
4
) satisfying properties
D1
-
D4
. Then for the
N
-th minimal integration error in
F_{d}
we have

e(N,d)\geq(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+}\,e(0,d),

(5)

where

\alpha:=\frac{\max(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))}{I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})}\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right).

(6)

Furthermore, for the information complexity we have

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{d}\,(1-\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$.}

In particular, the integration problem over
F_{d}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
be a set of
N
quadrature nodes in
D_{d}
.
Now we use
D2
. For this specific
\mathcal{P}
define the “fooling function”

g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j}),

(7)

where
\mathcal{D}^{\ast}=\mathcal{D}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P})
is the set of all subsets
\mathfrak{u}
of
[d]
with the property that for all
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
it holds that

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\not\in\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}E_{j,\mathfrak{u}},

(8)

where

E_{j,\mathfrak{u}}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}D_{(0)}&\mbox{if $j\in\mathfrak{u}$,}\\
D_{(1)}&\mbox{if $j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}$.}\end{array}\right.

Note that
\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}E_{j,\mathfrak{u}}
contains the support of

\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})

(9)

and hence condition (
8
) means that every point from the set
\mathcal{P}
is outside the support of the function in (
9
). This, however, guarantees that

g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=0\quad\mbox{for all $k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}$.}

Observe that the fooling function
g_{d}
is an element of the
2^{d}
-dimensional
(tensor product) subspace generated, for
d=1
, by the two functions

h_{1,(0)}
and
h_{1,(1)}
. Formally, we prove the lower bound for this
finite-dimensional subspace.

Since the sum
g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})
constitutes a partial sum of
h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})
it follows from property
D4
that

\|g_{d}\|_{d}\leq\|h_{d}\|_{d}.

(10)

Set
\widetilde{g}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\|h_{d}\|_{d}^{-1}g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})
such that
\|\widetilde{g}_{d}\|_{d}\leq 1
.

Since
\widetilde{g}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=0
for all
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
we have for any linear algorithm (
2
) that is based on
\mathcal{P}
that

e(F_{d},A_{d,N})\geq I_{d}(\widetilde{g}_{d})=\frac{1}{\|h_{d}\|_{d}}\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}}(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}.

(11)

Due to the construction of
g_{d}
, at most
N
of the sets

\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}E_{j,\mathfrak{u}}\quad\mbox{with $\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]$}

can contain a point from the node set
\mathcal{P}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}}(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}

(12)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}-N\max_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})\right)^{d}-N\left(\max(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))\right)^{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I_{1}(h_{1}))^{d}(1-N\alpha^{d}),

with
\alpha
from (
6
) (note that
\alpha\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1)
, because of
D3
). Since, obviously, (
12
) is non-negative, we can re-write the lower bound as

\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}}(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}\geq(I_{1}(h_{1}))^{d}(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+}.

Inserting into (
11
) yields

e(N,d)\geq e(F_{d},A_{d,N})\geq\frac{(I_{1}(h_{1}))^{d}}{\|h_{d}\|_{d}}(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+}=e(0,d)(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+},

where we used that
(I_{1}(h_{1}))^{d}\|h_{d}\|_{d}^{-1}=(I_{1}(h_{1})\|h_{1}\|_{1}^{-1})^{d}=(e(0,1))^{d}=e(0,d)
. This proves (
5
).

Now let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and assume that
e(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e(0,d)
. This implies that
(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+}\leq\varepsilon
. If
N<\alpha^{-d}
, then

(1-N\alpha^{d})=(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+}\leq\varepsilon

and hence

N\geq(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{d}.

This lower bound trivially holds true if
N\geq\alpha^{-d}
. For the information complexity this implies

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{d}.

Note that
\alpha\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1)
and hence
1<\frac{1}{\alpha}\leq 2
. This finally implies that the integration problem over
F_{d}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(F_{1},\|\cdot\|_{1})
be a normed space of univariate functions over

and let for
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
(F_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
be the
d
-fold tensor product space equipped with a cross norm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
. Assume that there exists a

such that
\|\cdot\|_{d}
satisfies for all

the

-property. Assume in
F_{1}
exists a worst-case function
h_{1}
which can be decomposed according to (

-

. Then for the
N
-th minimal integration error in
F_{d}
we have

e(N,d)\geq(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+}\,e(0,d),

where

\alpha:=\frac{\max(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))}{I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})}\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right).

Furthermore, for the information complexity we have

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{d}\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$.}

In particular, the integration problem over
F_{d}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Proof.

The result follows directly from the proof of Theorem
1
. Define the fooling function
g_{d}
like in (
7
). Then it is obvious that the terms

\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\quad\mbox{for $\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]$}

have pairwise a disjoint support. From the

-property of the norm we obtain

\displaystyle\|g_{d}\|_{d}^{q}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}}\left\|\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\left\|\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}^{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|h_{d}\|_{d}^{q},

when
q
is finite, and similarly for
q=\infty
. Hence, we have
\|g_{d}\|_{d}\leq\|h_{d}\|_{d}
. The remainder of the proof is the same as the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

2.2
Worst-case functions with a decomposable part

Decomposability of a worst-case function is a rather strong assumption. However, in some cases one has a more relaxed situation in which a worst-case function has at least an additive part, which is decomposable in the previous sense. In more detail, assume now that a worst-case function
h_{1}
exists and can be decomposed in the form

h_{1}(x)=h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(0)}(x)+h_{1,2,(1)}(x)

(15)

with

DP1

h_{1,1},h_{1,2,(0)},h_{1,2,(1)}\in F_{1}
;

DP2

there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that

D_{(0)}=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\leq a\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{(1)}=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\geq a\}

are non-empty and the support of
h_{1,2,(0)}
is contained in
D_{(0)}
and the support of
h_{1,2,(1)}
is contained in
D_{(1)}
;

DP3

the integrals
I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)})
and
I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)})
are positive; and

DP4

for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
, every
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
and every family
\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{u}}
of subsets of
\mathfrak{u}
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\not\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,1}(x_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{u}}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\not\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,1}(x_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}.

Remark 3
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in D_{d}
we have

\displaystyle h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{1}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{d}(h_{1,1}(x_{j})+h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})+h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\not\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,1}(x_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j})

and, in particular,

\left\|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\not\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,1}(x_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}=\|h_{d}\|_{d}=\|h_{1}\|_{1}^{d}.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(F_{1},\|\cdot\|_{1})
be a normed space of univariate functions over
D_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and let for
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
(F_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
be the
d
-fold tensor product space equipped with a crossnorm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
. Assume in
F_{1}
exists a worst-case function
h_{1}
which can be decomposed according to (
15
) satisfying properties
DP1
-
DP4
. Then for the
N
-th minimal integration error in
F_{d}
we have

e(N,d)\geq\frac{\alpha_{1}^{d}}{\|h_{d}\|_{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d}{d\choose k}\alpha_{3}^{k}\left(1-N\alpha^{k}\right)_{+},

(16)

where

\alpha:=\frac{\max(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))}{I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)})}\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right),

(17)

and

\alpha_{1}:=I_{1}(h_{1,1}),\quad\alpha_{2}:=I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\alpha_{3}:=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}}>0.

(18)

Furthermore, we have

\liminf_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e(\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor,d)}{e(0,d)}=1\quad\mbox{for all }C\in(1,\alpha^{-\alpha_{3}/(1+\alpha_{3})})

(19)

and for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a
d_{0}(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor\quad\mbox{for all $d\geq d_{0}(\varepsilon)$}

with
C
like in (
19
). In particular, the integration problem over
F_{d}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
be a set of
N
quadrature nodes in
D_{d}
. For
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
let
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,\mathfrak{u}}
denote the projection of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
to the coordinates
j
in
\mathfrak{u}
. For this specific
\mathcal{P}
define the “fooling function”

g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\prod_{j\not\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,1}(x_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\mathfrak{u}}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j}),

where, for every
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
, the set
\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{\mathfrak{u}}=\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{\mathfrak{u}}(\mathcal{P})
is the set of all subsets
\mathfrak{v}
of
\mathfrak{u}
with the property that for all
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
it holds that

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,\mathfrak{u}}\not\in\bigotimes_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}E_{j,\mathfrak{v}},

(20)

where

E_{j,\mathfrak{v}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}D_{(0)}&\mbox{if $j\in\mathfrak{v}$,}\\
D_{(1)}&\mbox{if $j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}$.}\end{array}\right.

Note that
\bigotimes_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}E_{j,\mathfrak{v}}
contains the support of
\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j}).
Hence, condition (
20
) means that every point from the set
\mathcal{P}
is outside the support of
\prod_{j\not\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,1}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,2,(1)}(x_{j})
and this guarantees that

g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=0\quad\mbox{for all $k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}$.}

Observe that the fooling function
g_{d}
is an element of the
3^{d}
-dimensional
(tensor product) subspace generated, for
d=1
, by the three functions

h_{1,1}
,
h_{1,2,(0)}
and
h_{1,2,(1)}
. Formally, we prove the lower bound for this
finite-dimensional subspace.

Since
g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})
constitutes a partial sum of
h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})
it follows from property
DP4
that

\|g_{d}\|_{d}\leq\|h_{d}\|_{d}.

Set
\widetilde{g}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\|h_{d}\|_{d}^{-1}g_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})
such that
\|\widetilde{g}_{d}\|_{d}\leq 1
.

Since
\widetilde{g}_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=0
for all
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
we have for any linear algorithm (
2
) that is based on
\mathcal{P}
that

\displaystyle e(F_{d},A_{d,N})\geq

\displaystyle I_{d}(\widetilde{g}_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|h_{d}\|_{d}}\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}(I_{1}(h_{1,1}))^{d-|\mathfrak{u}|}

\displaystyle\hskip 51.21504pt\times\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\mathfrak{u}}}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{v}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|-|\mathfrak{v}|}.

(21)

Now, for fixed
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
, we estimate the inner-most sum in (
21
). Due to the construction of
g_{d}
, at most
N
of the
2^{|\mathfrak{u}|}
sets

\bigotimes_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}E_{j,\mathfrak{v}}\quad\mbox{with $\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}$}

can contain a point from the node set
\mathcal{P}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\mathfrak{u}}}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{v}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|-|\mathfrak{v}|}

(22)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{v}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|-|\mathfrak{v}|}-N\max_{\mathfrak{v}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{v}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|-|\mathfrak{v}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)})\right)^{|\mathfrak{u}|}-N\left(\max(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))\right)^{|\mathfrak{u}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{|\mathfrak{u}|}(1-N\alpha^{|\mathfrak{u}|}),

with
\alpha
from (
17
) (note that
\alpha\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1)
because of
DP3
) and
\alpha_{2}
from (
18
). Since, obviously, (
22
) is non-negative, we can re-write the lower bound as

\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}_{\mathfrak{u}}}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}))^{|\mathfrak{v}|}(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))^{|\mathfrak{u}|-|\mathfrak{v}|}\geq\alpha_{2}^{|\mathfrak{u}|}(1-N\alpha^{|\mathfrak{u}|})_{+}.

Inserting into (
21
), thereby using the notation
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{3}
from (
18
) we obtain

e(N,d)\geq e(F_{d},A_{d,N})\geq\frac{\alpha_{1}^{d}}{\|h_{d}\|_{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d}{d\choose k}\alpha_{3}^{k}(1-N\alpha^{k})_{+}

and this finishes the proof of (
16
).

For the normalized
N
-th minimal error we therefore obtain

\frac{e(N,d)}{e(0,d)}\geq\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{I_{1}(h_{1})}\right)^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{d}{d\choose k}\alpha_{3}^{k}(1-N\alpha^{k})_{+},

(23)

where we used that
\|h_{d}\|_{d}\,e(0,d)=\left(\|h_{1}\|_{1}\,e(0,1)\right)^{d}=(I_{1}(h_{1}))^{d}
.

Now we proceed using ideas from
[
14
, p. 185]
. Let
b\in(0,1)
and let
N=\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor
with
C\in(1,\alpha^{-\alpha_{3}/(1+\alpha_{3})})
. This means that
C\alpha^{\alpha_{3}/(1+\alpha_{3})}<1
. Then there exists a positive
c
such that

c<\frac{\alpha_{3}}{1+\alpha_{3}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad C\alpha^{c}<1.

Put
k(d):=\lfloor cd\rfloor
. For sufficiently large
d
we have

N\alpha^{k}\leq C^{d}\alpha^{cd-1}=\alpha^{-1}(C\alpha^{c})^{d}\leq b\quad\mbox{ for all $k\in(k(d),d]$.}

(24)

Put further
C_{d,k}:={d\choose k}\alpha_{3}^{k}
. Then (
23
) and (
24
) imply that

\displaystyle\frac{e(\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor,d)}{e(0,d)}\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{I_{1}(h_{1})}\right)^{d}\sum_{k=k(d)+1}^{d}C_{d,k}(1-N\alpha^{k})_{+}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-b)\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{I_{1}(h_{1})}\right)^{d}\sum_{k=k(d)+1}^{d}C_{d,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-b)\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{I_{1}(h_{1})}\right)^{d}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{d}C_{d,k}-\sum_{k=0}^{k(d)}C_{d,k}\right).

Note that

\sum_{k=0}^{d}C_{d,k}=(1+\alpha_{3})^{d}=\frac{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})^{d}}{\alpha_{1}^{d}}=\left(\frac{I_{1}(h_{1})}{\alpha_{1}}\right)^{d}.

This and
e(0,1)=I_{1}(h_{1})/\|h_{1}\|_{1}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{e(\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor,d)}{e(0,d)}\geq

\displaystyle(1-b)\left(1-\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{k(d)}C_{d,k}}{(1+\alpha_{3})^{d}}\right).

Now put

\alpha(d):=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{k(d)}C_{d,k}}{(1+\alpha_{3})^{d}}.

It is shown in
[
14
, p. 185]
that
\alpha(d)
tends to zero when
d\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, for any positive
\delta
we can find an integer
d(\delta)
such that for all
d\geq d(\delta)
we have

1\geq\frac{e(\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor,d)}{e(0,d)}\geq(1-b)(1-\delta).

Since
b
and
\delta
can be arbitrarily close to zero, it follows that

\liminf_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e(\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor,d)}{e(0,d)}=1,

and this holds true for all
C\in(1,\alpha^{-\alpha_{3}/(1+\alpha_{3})})
. This finishes the proof of (
19
).

Finally, take
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then there exists a
d_{0}(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
d\geq d_{0}(\varepsilon)
it holds that

\varepsilon\leq\frac{e(\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor,d)}{e(0,d)}

and therefore

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq\lfloor C^{d}\rfloor\quad\mbox{for all $d\geq d_{0}(\varepsilon)$}.

Since
C>1
this means the curse of dimensionality.
∎

3
Applications: Part 1

An important application of the method of decomposable worst-case functions can be found in
[
10
]
where we proved that the classical
L_{p}
-star discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality for a sequence of parameters
p
in
(1,2]
. By nature, there the most difficult part is a verification of property
DP4
. In this section we present further concrete applications of the method. In the first application we show that the presented method is essentially a generalization of the “method of decomposable kernels”.

3.1
The method of decomposable kernels revisited

Let
q=2
and assume that
F_{1}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of absolutely integrable functions over
D_{1}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}^{1/2}
. Assume that the kernel
K_{1}
is decomposable, i.e., there exists an element
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that the sets

D_{(0)}:=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\leq a\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{(1)}:=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\geq a\}

are nonempty and

K_{1}(x,t)=0\quad\mbox{for $(x,t)\in D_{(0)}\times D_{(1)}\cup D_{(1)}\times D_{(0)}$.}

Compare with
[
14
, Section 11.4]
.

The
d
-fold tensor product space
F_{d}
is again a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel
K_{d}
, inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{d}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{d}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{d}^{1/2}
. It is well known that
K_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}K_{1}(x_{j},y_{j})
for
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}=(y_{1},,\ldots,y_{d})
in
D_{d}:=D_{1}^{d}
. Furthermore, for elementary tensors
f({\boldsymbol{x}})=f_{1}(x_{1})\cdots f_{d}(x_{d})
and
g({\boldsymbol{x}})=g_{1}(x_{1})\cdots g_{d}(x_{d})
we have

\langle f,g\rangle_{d}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\langle f_{j},g_{j}\rangle_{1}.

(25)

See
[
1
]
for an introduction into the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

It is well known that the worst-case function of the integration problem in
F_{1}
is given by

h_{1}(x)=\frac{\int_{D_{1}}K_{1}(x,t)\,{\rm d}t}{\left\|\int_{D_{1}}K_{1}(\cdot,t)\,{\rm d}t\right\|_{1}}.

Now we use the decomposition

h_{1,(0)}(x)=\frac{\int_{D_{(0)}}K_{1}(x,t)\,{\rm d}t}{\left\|\int_{D_{1}}K_{1}(\cdot,t)\,{\rm d}t\right\|_{1}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad h_{1,(1)}(x)=\frac{\int_{D_{(1)}}K_{1}(x,t)\,{\rm d}t}{\left\|\int_{D_{1}}K_{1}(\cdot,t)\,{\rm d}t\right\|_{1}}.

Then, obviously
h_{1}(x)=h_{1,(0)}(x)+h_{1,(1)}(x)
where
h_{1,(0)},h_{1,(1)}\in F_{1}
and
{\rm supp}\,h_{1,(0)}\subseteq D_{(0)}
and
{\rm supp}\,h_{1,(1)}\subseteq D_{(1)}
. We assume that
I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})>0
and
I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})>0
such that our decomposition satisfies properties
D1
-
D3
.

Finally,
h_{1,(0)}
and
h_{1,(1)}
are orthogonal, because

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{D_{(0)}}K_{1}(\cdot,t)\,{\rm d}t,\int_{D_{(1)}}K_{1}(\cdot,s)\,{\rm d}s\right\rangle_{1}=

\displaystyle\int_{D_{(0)}}\int_{D_{(1)}}\langle K_{1}(\cdot,t),K_{1}(\cdot,s)\rangle_{1}\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D_{(0)}}\int_{D_{(1)}}K_{1}(s,t)\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}s=0.

Then for every family
\mathcal{D}
of subsets of
[d]
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j}),\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{D}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\rangle_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j}),\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{v}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\rangle_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j}),\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\rangle_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\left\|\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(0)}(x_{j})\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus\mathfrak{u}}h_{1,(1)}(x_{j})\right\|_{d}^{2}

where we used the orthogonality of
h_{1,(0)}
and
h_{1,(1)}
and the property (
25
) of the inner product for elementary tensors. This shows that also property
D4
is satisfied.
So we meet all properties
D1
-
D4
and hence, according to Theorem
1
we have

e(N,d)\geq(1-N\alpha^{d})_{+}\,e(0,d),

where

\alpha:=\frac{\max(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))}{I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})}\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right),

and

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{d}\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$,}

such that the integration problem over
F_{d}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

3.2
Uniform integration of functions of smoothness
r

For
q\in(1,\infty]
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and
a\in(0,1)
let

\displaystyle F_{1}=W_{a,q}^{r}([0,1])=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ :\

\displaystyle f^{(j)}(a)=0\,\mbox{ for $0\leq j<r$},

\displaystyle f^{(r-1)}\,\mbox{ abs. cont. and }\,f^{(r)}\in L_{q}([0,1])\}.

As norm on
F_{1}
we use

\|f\|_{1,q}:=\|f^{(r)}\|_{L_{q}}.

We study integration in the
d
-fold tensor product space
F_{d}=W_{a,q}^{(r,r,\ldots,r)}([0,1]^{d})
, equipped with the crossnorm
\|f\|_{d,q}:=\|f^{(r,r,\ldots,r)}\|_{L_{q}}
. For
q=2
and
r=1
it is shown in
[
14
, Sec. 11.4.2, pp. 173ff]
that the integration problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality. With our method we are now able to tackle the general case
q\in(1,\infty]
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

First consider
q\in(1,\infty)
and observe that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1,q}
as well as the crossnorm
\|\cdot\|_{d,q}
satisfy the
q
-property (
13
) with the same parameter
q
like in the definition of the norm. That is, we aim to apply Theorem
2
.

Next we determine the worst-case function. For
f\in F_{1}
and for
x\in[0,1]
we have the representation

f(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f^{(r)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\,{\rm d}t.

Using this representation it is elementary to prove that

\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{1}f^{(r)}(t)g(t)\,{\rm d}t,

where

g(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}(-t)^{r}/r!&\mbox{if $t\in[0,a)$,}\\
(1-t)^{r}/r!&\mbox{if $t\in[a,1]$.}\end{array}\right.

Applying Hölder’s inequality we obtain

\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,{\rm d}x\right|\leq\|f^{(r)}\|_{L_{q}}\|g\|_{L_{p}}=\|f\|_{1,q}\|g\|_{L_{p}},

where
p
is the Hölder conjugate of
q
, i.e.,
1/p+1/q=1
, with equality if
|f^{(r)}(t)|=c|g(t)|^{p-1}
for some
c>0
. This holds for

f(t):=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{(r!)^{p-1}}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\frac{a^{rp}-t^{rp}}{\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(rp-i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(t-a)^{j}a^{rp-j}}{j!\prod_{i=j}^{r-1}(rp-i)}\right)&\mbox{if $t\in[0,a)$,}\\[10.00002pt]
\left(\frac{(1-a)^{rp}-(1-t)^{rp}}{\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(rp-i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(t-a)^{j}(1-a)^{rp-j}}{j!\prod_{i=j}^{r-1}(rp-i)}(-1)^{j}\right)&\mbox{if $t\in[a,1]$.}\end{array}\right.

(26)

This means that
h_{1}:=f
is the worst-case function (note that also
h_{1}^{(j)}(a)=0
for
0\leq j<r
).

We use the decomposition of
h_{1}
with

h_{1,(0)}(x)={\bf 1}_{[0,a]}(x)h_{1}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad h_{1,(1)}(x)={\bf 1}_{[a,1]}(x)h_{1}(x).

In particular, we have
D_{1}=[0,1]
,
D_{(0)}=[0,a]
and
D_{(1)}=[a,1]
. Then

\displaystyle I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}a^{rp+1}}{(r!)^{p-1}\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(rp-i)}\left(\frac{rp}{rp+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(j+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}(rp-i)\right)

and

\displaystyle I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}(1-a)^{rp+1}}{(r!)^{p-1}\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(rp-i)}\left(\frac{rp}{rp+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(j+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}(rp-i)\right).

It can be shown that

\frac{rp}{rp+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(j+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}(rp-i)\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>0&\mbox{if $r$ is odd,}\\
<0&\mbox{if $r$ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Hence, in any case, we have

I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})>0.

So we meet all required properties
D1
-
D3
and

\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})}{\max(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))}=1+\left(\frac{1}{\max(a,1-a)}-1\right)^{rp+1}>1

and Theorem
2
can be applied.

For the case
q=\infty
we can argue similarly. Note that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1,\infty}
as well as the crossnorm
\|\cdot\|_{d,\infty}
satisfy the
\infty
-property (
14
) and hence Theorem
2
still can be applied. The Hölder conjugate of
q=\infty
is
p=1
. A brief consideration shows that the worst-case function for
q=\infty
is still
h_{1}=f
with
f
from(
26
) with
p=1
. The remainder works in the same way as for finite
q
.

Now Theorem
2
implies the following result:

Corollary 1
.

For
q\in(1,\infty]
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in(0,1)
integration in the tensor product space
W_{a,q}^{(r,r,\ldots,r)}([0,1]^{d})
suffers from the curse of dimensionality. In more detail, we have

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p,r}^{d}\,(1-\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$,}

where
p
is the Hölder conjugate of
q
and where

C_{p,r}=1+\left(\frac{1}{\max(a,1-a)}-1\right)^{rp+1}>1.

(27)

That is, we generalized the example in
[
14
, Sec. 11.4.2, pp. 173ff]
from
q=2
and
r=1
to general
q\in(1,\infty]
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

Example 2
.

In view of the next two applications and also in order to facilitate the comparison with the result in
[
14
, Sec. 11.4.2, pp. 173ff]
we briefly specify the case of smoothness
r=1
explicitly. Here we have

h_{1}(x):=\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,a]}(x)\frac{a^{p}-x^{p}}{p}+\boldsymbol{1}_{[a,1]}(x)\frac{(1-a)^{p}-(1-x)^{p}}{p},

with

I_{1}(h_{1})=\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}=\left(\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{1/q},

such that

e(0,1)=\left(\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{1/p}.

A suitable decomposition of
h_{1}
is given
by

h_{1,(0)}(x)={\bf 1}_{[0,a]}(x)\frac{a^{p}-x^{p}}{p}\quad\mbox{and}\quad h_{1,(1)}(x)={\bf 1}_{[a,1]}(x)\frac{(1-a)^{p}-(1-x)^{p}}{p}.

Then

I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})=\frac{a^{p+1}}{p+1}>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})=\frac{(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}>0

and

\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})}{\max(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))}=1+\left(\frac{1}{\max(a,1-a)}-1\right)^{p+1}>1.

For example, for
a=1/2
we have
1/\alpha=2
.

3.3
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a

For a point set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1)^{d}
, for
a\in[0,1]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
is defined as

L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\bf 1}_{J({\boldsymbol{t}})}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{k})-{\rm Vol}(J({\boldsymbol{t}}))\right|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)^{1/p},

where, for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
, the test set
J({\boldsymbol{t}})
is defined as

J({\boldsymbol{t}})=J(t_{1})\times\ldots\times J(t_{d})

with
J(t)=[\min(t,a),\max(t,a))
for
t\in[0,1]
and
{\rm Vol}(\cdot)
denotes the volume in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. See
[
14
, Section 9.5.3]
and
[
8
, Example 1]
.

Consider integration in the tensor product space
W_{a,q}^{(r,r,\ldots,r)}([0,1]^{d})
from the previous section. For
r=1
it can be shown that the worst-case error of a quasi-Monte Carlo rule

A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}}(f)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\quad\mbox{for $f\in W_{a,q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})$,}

(28)

which is based on a point set
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
, is exactly the
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
of the point set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}:={\boldsymbol{a}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\pmod{1}
and where
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a,\ldots,a)
, i.e.,

e(W_{a,q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d}),A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}})=L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}),

where
1/p+1/q=1
. See
[
14
, Section 9.5.3]
and
[
14
, Sec. 11.4.3]
. The initial
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
is

L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}(\emptyset)=\left(\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{d/p}=e(0,d),

where the latter is the initial error of integration in
W_{a,q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
.

From Corollary
1
we therefore obtain:

Corollary 2
.

For
a\in(0,1)
and for
p\in[1,\infty)
the
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
suffers from the curse of dimensionality, i.e.,

\displaystyle N_{L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}}(\varepsilon,d)

\displaystyle:=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \exists\,\mathcal{P}\subseteq[0,1)^{d}\mbox{ s.t. }|\mathcal{P}|=N\mbox{ and }L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}(\mathcal{P})\leq\varepsilon L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}(\emptyset)\},

grows exponentially fast with the dimension
d
. More detailed,

N_{L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p,1}^{d}\,(1-\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$,}

where
C_{p,1}>1
is like in (
27
) with
r=1
.

We point out that in this result even the endpoint
p=1
is included. For
a=0
the
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored in 0 is the same as the usual
L_{p}
-star discrepancy. This case is not covered by Corollary
2
. Proving the curse of dimensionality for the
L_{p}
-star discrepancy for finite
p
beyond
p=2
was a long standing open question which was recently solved in
[
11
]
, however, only for
p\in(1,\infty)
. The quest about the curse of dimensionality for the
L_{1}
-star discrepancy is still open.

3.4
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at
a

For a point set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1)^{d}
, for
a\in[0,1]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at
a
is defined as

L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\bf 1}_{Q({\boldsymbol{t}})}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{k})-{\rm Vol}(Q({\boldsymbol{t}}))\right|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)^{1/p},

where, for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
, the test set
Q({\boldsymbol{t}})
is defined as

Q({\boldsymbol{t}})=Q(t_{1})\times\ldots\times Q(t_{d})

with
Q(t)=[0,t)
if
t<a
, and
Q(t)=[t,1)
if
t\geq a
. For
a=1/2
and
p=2
this discrepancy was studied by Hickernell
[
4
]
under the name
centered discrepancy
. See also
[
14
, Section 9.5.4]
,
[
8
, Example 2]
, and
[
5
]
.

As before, consider integration in the tensor product space
W_{a,q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
. Then the worst-case error of a quasi-Monte Carlo rule (
28
) for
f\in W_{a,q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
which is based on a point set
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
, is exactly the
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at
a
of the same point set, i.e.,

e(W_{a,q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d}),A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}})=L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}(\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}),

where
1/p+1/q=1
. See
[
14
, Sec. 9.5.4]
(for the case
p=2
). The initial
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at
a
is

L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}(\emptyset)=\left(\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{d/p}=e(0,d),

where the latter is the initial error of integration in
W_{a,q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
.

Now we can generalize
[
14
, Corollary 11.11]
. From Corollary
1
we obtain:

Corollary 3
.

For
a\in(0,1)
and for
p\in[1,\infty)
the
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at
a
suffers from the curse of dimensionality, i.e.,

\displaystyle N_{L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}}(\varepsilon,d)

\displaystyle:=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \exists\,\mathcal{P}\subseteq[0,1)^{d}\mbox{ s.t. }|\mathcal{P}|=N\mbox{ and }L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}(\mathcal{P})\leq\varepsilon L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}(\emptyset)\},

grows exponentially fast with the dimension
d
. More detailed,

N_{L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p,1}^{d}\,(1-\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$,}

where
C_{p,1}>1
is like in (
27
) with
r=1
.

Also here we point out that in this result even the endpoint
p=1
is included. For
a=1
the
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at 1 is the same as the usual
L_{p}
-star discrepancy, but, unfortunately, also this case is not covered by Corollary
3
.

3.5
Weighted integration of functions of smoothness
r

For
q\in(1,\infty]
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
let

\displaystyle F_{1}=W_{0,q}^{r}(\mathbb{R})=\{f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ :\

\displaystyle f^{(j)}(0)=0\,\mbox{ for $0\leq j<r$},

\displaystyle f^{(r-1)}\,\mbox{ abs. cont. and }\,f^{(r)}\in L_{q}(\mathbb{R})\}.

As norm on
F_{1}
we use

\|f\|_{1,q}:=\|f^{(r)}\|_{L_{q}}.

We study weighted integration in the
d
-fold tensor product space
F_{d}=W_{0,q}^{(r,r,\ldots,r)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the crossnorm
\|f\|_{d,q}:=\|f^{(r,r,\ldots,r)}\|_{L_{q}}
with respect to a probability density
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
, i.e.,

I_{d}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{t}})\psi({\boldsymbol{t}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\quad\mbox{for $f\in F_{d,q,r}$}

with
\psi({\boldsymbol{t}}):=\psi(t_{1})\cdots\psi(t_{d})
for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
. For
q=2
it is shown in
[
12
]
and in
[
14
, Sec. 11.4.1, pp. 169ff]
that the integration problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality. With our method we are now able to tackle the case
q\in(1,\infty]
.

Again we observe that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1,q}
as well as the crossnorm
\|\cdot\|_{d,q}
satisfy the
q
-property with the same parameter
q
like in the definition of the norm. That is, we aim to apply Theorem
2
.

In the following we only explicate the case
q\in(1,\infty)
. The case
q=\infty
can be treated similarly, incorporating the usual adaptions, like in Section
3.2
.

For the sake of simplicity we assume that
\psi
is symmetric around
0
, i.e.,
\psi(x)=\psi(-x)
. Furthermore, we assume that
\psi
is such that the iterated integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{y_{r}}\ldots\int_{0}^{y_{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}(x-y_{1})^{r-1}{\bf 1}_{[y_{1},\infty)}(x)\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x\right)^{p-1}\,{\rm d}y_{1}\ldots\,{\rm d}y_{r}\psi(t)\,{\rm d}t<\infty,

(29)

where
p
is the Hölder conjugate of
q
. This assumption guarantees that the integrals that appear in the following are well defined.

At first we determine the worst-case function. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have (in the same way as above)
f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f^{(r)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\,{\rm d}t
(where
\int_{0}^{x}f^{(r)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\,{\rm d}t=-\int_{x}^{0}f^{(r)}(t)\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\,{\rm d}t
whenever
x<0
) and hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{0}f^{(r)}(t)\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{(r)}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t.

Let

\Psi_{r}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}{\bf 1}_{(-\infty,t]}(x)\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x\quad\mbox{for $t<0$,}\\[10.00002pt]
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(x-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}{\bf 1}_{[t,\infty)}(x)\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x\quad\mbox{for $t\geq 0$,}\end{array}\right.

(note that
\Psi_{r}(-t)=(-1)^{r}\Psi_{r}(t)
.) Then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{(r)}(t)\Psi_{r}(t)\,{\rm d}t.

Applying Hölder’s inequality, where
p
is the Hölder conjugate of
q
, we obtain

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\psi(x)\,{\rm d}x\right|\leq\|f^{(r)}\|_{L_{q}}\|\Psi_{r}\|_{L_{p}}=\|f\|_{1,q}\|\Psi_{r}\|_{L_{p}}

with equality if
|f^{(r)}(t)|=c|\Psi_{r}|^{p-1}
for some
c>0
. This holds for
f
of the form

f(t)=\int_{0}^{|t|}\int_{0}^{y_{r}}\int_{0}^{y_{r-1}}\ldots\int_{0}^{y_{2}}\left(\Psi_{r}(y_{1})\right)^{p-1}\,{\rm d}y_{1}\ldots\,{\rm d}y_{r-1}\,{\rm d}y_{r}.

(30)

This means that
h_{1}:=f
is the worst-case function. Note that
h_{1}(t)=h_{1}(-t)
.

We use the decomposition of
h_{1}
with

h_{1,(0)}(x)={\bf 1}_{(-\infty,0]}(x)h_{1}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad h_{1,(1)}(x)={\bf 1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)h_{1}(x).

In particular, we have
D_{1}=\mathbb{R}
,
D_{(0)}=(-\infty,0]
and
D_{(1)}=[0,\infty)
.
We assumed that
\psi
is symmetric around 0, in particular,
\psi
does not vanish over
(-\infty,0]
and
[0,\infty)
. Hence

\int_{-\infty}^{0}h_{1}(t)\psi(t)\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{1}(t)\psi(t)\,{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h_{1}(t)\psi(t)\,{\rm d}t>0.

Note that these integrals exist because of assumption (
29
). So we meet all required properties
D1
-
D3
and

\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{I_{1}(h_{1,(0)})+I_{1}(h_{1,(1)})}{\max(I_{1}(h_{1,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,(1)}))}=2.

Thus Theorem
2
implies the following result.

Corollary 4
.

Let
q\in(1,\infty]
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\psi
be symmetric around
0
and assume that
\psi
satisfies (
29
). Then
\psi
-weighted integration in
W_{0,q}^{(r,r,\ldots,r)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
suffers from the curse of dimensionality, in more detail, we have

N(\varepsilon,d)\geq 2^{d}\,(1-\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$.}

That is, we generalized the example in
[
14
, Sec. 11.4.1, pp. 169f]
from
q=2
to general
q\in(1,\infty]
.

4
Positive quadrature rules

The big issue in the application of the methods presented so far is to check whether the fooling function
g_{d}
satisfies
\|g_{d}\|_{d}\leq\|h_{d}\|_{d}
. This is guaranteed by means of the rather general conditions
D4
and
DP4
, respectively, which however are in most cases very difficult to verify. This problem can be overcome for positive linear rules
A_{N,d}^{+}
like in (
2
), but only with non-negative weights
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\geq 0
. We present a method that is based on spline approximations of the worst-case function. In order to state the results we slightly adapt the definitions of the minimal error and the information complexity. Define

e^{+}(N,d):=\min_{A_{d,N}^{+}}e(F_{d},A_{d,N}^{+}),

where here the minimum is extended over all linear algorithms of the form (
2
) based on
N
function evaluations along points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
from
D_{d}
and non-negative
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\geq 0
.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
define

N^{+}(\varepsilon,d)=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ e^{+}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e(0,d)\}.

We stress that
N^{+}(\varepsilon,d)
is a kind of restricted complexity since we only allow positive quadrature formulas.

Now we can state our main theorem for this section:

Theorem 4
.

Let
(F_{1},\|\cdot\|_{1})
be a normed space of univariate functions over
D_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and let for
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
(F_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
be the
d
-fold tensor product space equipped with a crossnorm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
. Assume in
F_{1}
exists a worst-case function
h_{1}
.

Assume that for every
y\in D_{1}
there exists a function
s_{y}\in F_{1}
such that
s_{y}\geq 0
and
s_{y}(y)=h_{1}(y)
and

\alpha:=\max_{y\in D_{1}}\|s_{y}\|_{1}<\|h_{1}\|_{1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta:=\max_{y\in D_{1}}I_{1}(s_{y})<I_{1}(h_{1}).

(31)

Put

\widetilde{C}:=\min\left(\frac{\|h_{1}\|_{1}}{\alpha},\frac{I_{1}(h_{1})}{\beta}\right).

(32)

Then
\widetilde{C}>1
and

N^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq\widetilde{C}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$.}

In particular, the integration problem in
F_{d}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality for positive quadrature formulas.

Proof.

Consider a positive linear algorithm
A_{d,N}^{+}
based in nodes
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
in
D_{d}
and with weights
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\geq 0
. Let
x_{i,j}
be the
j
-th coordinate of the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
j\in[d]
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we define functions

P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=s_{x_{i,1}}(x_{1})s_{x_{i,2}}(x_{2})\cdots s_{x_{i,d}}(x_{d}),\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in D_{d}.

In order to estimate the error of
A_{d,N}^{+}
we consider the two functions
h_{d}
and
f^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}
. Since
A_{d,N}^{+}
is a positive rule we have

A_{d,N}^{+}(f^{*})\geq A_{d,N}^{+}(h_{d}).

Then we have the error estimate

e(F_{d},A_{d,N}^{+})\geq\frac{\left(I_{d}(h_{d})-I_{d}(f^{*})\right)_{+}}{2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d},\|f^{*}\|_{d})},

(33)

which is trivially true if
I_{d}(h_{d})\leq I_{d}(f^{*})
and which is easily shown if
I_{d}(h_{d})>I_{d}(f^{*})
, because then

\displaystyle\left(I_{d}(h_{d})-I_{d}(f^{*})\right)_{+}\leq

\displaystyle I_{d}(h_{d})-A_{d,N}^{+}(h_{d})+A_{d,N}^{+}(f^{*})-I_{d}(f^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h_{d}\|_{d}\,e(F_{d},A_{d,N}^{+})+\|f^{*}\|_{d}\,e(F_{d},A_{d,N}^{+})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d},\|f^{*}\|_{d})\,e(F_{d},A_{d,N}^{+}).

According to (
31
) we have
\|f^{*}\|_{d}\leq N\alpha^{d}
and
I_{d}(f^{*})\leq N\beta^{d}
. Inserting into (
33
) yields

e^{+}(N,d)\geq e(F_{d},A_{d,N}^{+})\geq\frac{(I_{d}(h_{d})-N\beta^{d})_{+}}{2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d},N\alpha^{d})}.

(34)

Now let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and assume that
e^{+}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e(0,d)
. This implies

2\,\varepsilon\,e(0,d)\,\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d},N\alpha^{d})\geq(I_{d}(h_{d})-N\beta^{d})_{+}.

(35)

Consider
\widetilde{C}
from (
32
). According to (
31
) we have
\widetilde{C}>1
.

If
N\leq\widetilde{C}^{d}
, then we obtain
\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d},N\alpha^{d})=\|h_{d}\|_{d}
and
I_{d}(h_{d})\geq N\beta^{d}
and hence (
35
) implies

\displaystyle I_{d}(h_{d})-N\beta^{d}=

\displaystyle(I_{d}(h_{d})-N\beta^{d})_{+}\leq 2\,\varepsilon\,e(0,d)\,\|h_{d}\|_{d}=2\,\varepsilon\,I_{d}(h_{d}).

Hence

N\geq\frac{I_{d}(h_{d})}{\beta^{d}}\,(1-2\varepsilon)=\left(\frac{I_{1}(h_{1})}{\beta}\right)^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)\geq\widetilde{C}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon).

If
N>\widetilde{C}^{d}
, then we trivially have
N\geq\widetilde{C}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon)
.
This yields

N^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq\widetilde{C}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon),

and we are done.
∎

Remark 4
.

Let us compare Theorem 4 with recent lower bounds
from
[
6
,
7
,
9
]

that are based on a new result of
Vybíral
[
16
]

who improved the
Schur product theorem.

Both lower bounds do not need a decomposition of
h_{1}

and therefore can be applied to classes
of analytic functions or even polynomials.
Our result can be applied to more functionals
and more spaces, while the lower bounds
in the papers cited above
only work for particular functionals and
Hilbert spaces of functions.
Our approach has the disadvantage, however,
that it only works for positive quadrature formulas,
such as quasi Monte Carlo methods.

Sometimes one has a decomposition of the worst-case function in the sense of Section
2.1
and
2.2
. I.e., the worst-case function
h_{1}
exists and can be decomposed in the form

h_{1}(x)=h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(0)}(x)+h_{1,2,(1)}(x)

(36)

with the following properties:

DP
\boldsymbol{1}^{+}

h_{1,1},h_{1,2,(0)},h_{1,2,(1)}\in F_{1}
are non-negative;

DP
\boldsymbol{2}^{+}

there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that the sets

D_{(0)}=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\leq a\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{(1)}=\{x\in D_{1}\ :\ x\geq a\}

are non-empty and the support of
h_{1,2,(0)}
is contained in
D_{(0)}
and the support of
h_{1,2,(1)}
is contained in
D_{(1)}
;

DP
\boldsymbol{3}^{+}

the integrals
I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)})
and
I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)})
are positive;

DP
\boldsymbol{4}^{+}

for
\alpha:=\max\left(\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(0)}\|_{1},\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(1)}\|_{1}\right)
we have
\alpha<\|h_{1}\|_{1}
.

Note that
h_{1,1}
might also be 0 on
D_{1}
. Then we have the following corollary to Theorem
4
.

Corollary 5
.

Let
(F_{1},\|\cdot\|_{1})
be a normed space of univariate functions over
D_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and let for
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
(F_{d},\|\cdot\|_{d})
be the
d
-fold tensor product space equipped with a crossnorm
\|\cdot\|_{d}
. Assume in
F_{1}
exists a worst-case function
h_{1}
which can be decomposed according to (
36
) satisfying properties
{\bf DP1^{+}}
-
{\bf DP4^{+}}
. Put

C:=\min\left(\frac{\|h_{1}\|_{1}}{\alpha},\frac{I_{1}(h_{1})}{\beta}\right),

(37)

where
\beta:=I(h_{1,1})+\max(I_{1}(h_{1,2,(0)}),I_{1}(h_{1,2,(1)}))
.

Then
C>1
and

N^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$.}

In particular, the integration problem in
F_{d}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality for positive quadrature formulas.

Proof.

Choose in Theorem
4

s_{y}(x)=h_{1,1}(x)+\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h_{1,2,(0)}(x)&\mbox{if $y\in D_{(0)}$,}\\
h_{1,2,(1)}(x)&\mbox{if $y\in D_{(1)}$.}\end{array}\right.

Then
s_{y}(y)=h_{1}(y)
for every
y\in D_{1}
and (
32
) is satisfied because of
{\bf DP3^{+}}
and
{\bf DP4^{+}}
. Hence, the result follows from Theorem
4
.
∎

5
Applications: Part 2

An important application of the method for positive quadrature rules from Section
4
can be found in
[
11
]
where we proved that the classical
L_{p}
-star discrepancy for every
p\in(1,\infty)
suffers from the curse of dimensionality. Here we provide some further applications.

5.1
Integration of polynomials of degree at most 2

Consider
F_{1}:=P_{2}
as the space of all real polynomials of degree at most 2 as functions over
[0,1]
, equipped with the
q
-norm

\|f\|_{q}:=\left(\|f\|_{L_{q}}^{q}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}+\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}\right)^{1/q}

for some
q\in[1,\infty)
. Consider integration
I_{1}(f)=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,{\rm d}x
for
f\in F_{1}
. This problem is studied in
[
14
, Section 10.5.4]
, but only for the Hilbert-space case
q=2
.

The initial error for this problem is
e(0,1)=1
and the worst-case function is
h_{1}(x)=1
for
x\in[0,1]
. Obviously
\|h_{1}\|_{q}=1
and
\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x=1
.

For
y\in[0,1]
choose
s_{y}(x):=1-c(y-x)^{2}
with some
c\in(0,1/2)
that will be specified later. Then
s_{y}\in F_{1}
and
s_{y}\geq 0
for every
y\in[0,1]
. Furthermore,

I_{1}(s_{y})=1-c\left(y^{2}-y+\frac{1}{3}\right)

and hence
\beta=1-\frac{c}{12}<1
.

We estimate
\|s_{y}\|_{q}
. Since
s_{y}(x)\in[0,1]
and
q\geq 1
we have

\|s_{y}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}=\int_{0}^{1}|s_{y}(x)|^{q}\,{\rm d}x\leq I_{1}(s_{y})\leq\beta=1-\frac{c}{12}.

Furthermore, since
s_{y}^{\prime}(x)=2c(y-x)
and
s_{y}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-2c
we have

\|s_{y}^{\prime}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}=\int_{0}^{1}|2c(y-x)|^{q}\,{\rm d}x=(2c)^{q}\frac{y^{q+1}+(1-y)^{q+1}}{q+1}\leq\frac{(2c)^{q}}{q+1}

and

\|s_{y}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L_{q}}^{q}=\int_{0}^{1}|-2c|^{q}\,{\rm d}x=(2c)^{q}.

Hence

\|s_{y}\|_{q}^{q}\leq 1-\frac{c}{12}+(2c)^{q}\frac{q+2}{q+1}.

For
q\in(1,\infty)
the latter is strictly less than 1, if

c<\left(\frac{1}{12\cdot 2^{q}}\frac{q+1}{q+2}\right)^{1/(q-1)}=:u_{q}.

(38)

Thus for the quantity in (
32
) we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{c\in(0,u_{q})}\min\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{c}{12}+(2c)^{q}\frac{q+2}{q+1}\right)^{1/q}},\frac{1}{1-\frac{c}{12}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{c\in(0,u_{q})}\left(1-\frac{c}{12}+(2c)^{q}\frac{q+2}{q+1}\right)^{-1/q}.

It is easily checked that the maximum is attained for

c=\left(\frac{1}{q}\frac{1}{12\cdot 2^{q}}\frac{q+1}{q+2}\right)^{1/(q-1)}=\frac{u_{q}}{q^{1/(q-1)}}.

Hence, with
u_{q}
taken from (
38
),

\widetilde{C}_{q}=\left(1-\frac{u_{q}}{12q^{1/(q-1)}}+\frac{2^{q}u_{q}^{q}}{q^{q/(q-1)}}\frac{q+2}{q+1}\right)^{-1/q}.

(39)

Now Theorem
4
implies:

Corollary 6
.

For
q\in(1,\infty)
the integration problem in the tensor-product space based on
F_{1}=P_{2}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality for positive quadrature formulas. More detailed, we have
N^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq\widetilde{C}_{q}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon)
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\widetilde{C}_{q}
is from (
38
) and hence
\widetilde{C}_{q}>1
.

Exemplarily, we have the following (rounded) figures for
\widetilde{C}_{q}
:

\begin{array}[]{r||c|c|c|c|c|c|c}q&2&3&4&5&10&100&1000\\
\hline\cr\widetilde{C}_{q}&1.00016&1.00098&1.00161&1.00195&1.00204&1.00039&1.00004\end{array}

and
\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{C}_{q}=1
.

5.2
Integration of
C^{\infty}
-functions

Let
F_{1}
be the space of
C^{\infty}([0,1])
functions such that the norm

\|f\|_{q}:=(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}\|f^{(\alpha)}\|^{q}_{L_{q}})^{1/q}
is finite, where
q\in[1,\infty)
.
The worst-case error of integration in
F_{1}
is
e(0,1)=1
and the worst-case function is again
h_{1}(x)=1
for
x\in[0,1]
. It follows from Corollary
6
that for
q\in(1,\infty)
integration in the corresponding tensor-product space suffers from the curse of dimensionality for positive quadrature formulas.

We do not know whether this is also true for
q=\infty
, i.e., for the norm
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\max_{\alpha}\|f^{(\alpha)}\|_{\infty}
.

5.3
Uniform integration without anchor condition

As yet another application we revisit the problem of uniform integration from Section
3.2
, but now we remove the anchor condition
f(a)=0
. For the sake of simplicity we restrict ourselves to the case
r=1
and
q\in(1,\infty)
. Let

\displaystyle F_{1}=W_{q}^{1}([0,1])=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ :\ f\,\mbox{ abs. cont. and }\,f^{\prime}\in L_{q}([0,1])\}

equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{1,q}=\left(|f(a)|^{q}+\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime}(t)|^{q}\,{\rm d}t\right)^{1/q}

for some fixed
a\in(0,1)
.
We study integration in the
d
-fold tensor product space
F_{d}=W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
equipped with the crossnorm

\|f\|_{d,q}=\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\|f_{\mathfrak{u}}(\cdot_{\mathfrak{u}},a)\|_{L_{q}}^{q}\right)^{1/q},

where
f_{\mathfrak{u}}(\cdot_{\mathfrak{u}},a)
denotes the partial derivative of
f
of order 1 with respect to the coordinates that belong to
\mathfrak{u}
and all other components set to be
a
. For
q=2
it is shown in
[
14
, Sec. 11.5.2, pp. 188f]
that the integration problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality. We tackle the more general case and aim to apply Corollary
5
.

Let again
p
be the Hölder conjugate of
q
. Using methods similar to Section
3.2
it is easy to prove that the worst-case function for the integration problem in
F_{1}
is

h_{1}(x)=1+\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,a]}(x)\frac{a^{p}-x^{p}}{p}+\boldsymbol{1}_{[a,1]}(x)\frac{(1-a)^{p}-(1-x)^{p}}{p}.

(40)

We have

\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x=1+\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}

and

\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}=\left(1+\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{1/q}.

The initial error is

e(0,1)=\left(1+\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{1/p}.

We immediately see the suitable decomposition of
h_{1}
, namely

\displaystyle h_{1,1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,a]}(x)\frac{a^{p}-x^{p}}{p}

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}_{[a,1]}(x)\frac{(1-a)^{p}-(1-x)^{p}}{p}.

Then
{\bf DP1^{+}}
and
{\bf DP2^{+}}
are satisfied, furthermore

\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,2,(0)}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{a^{p+1}}{p+1}>0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,2,(1)}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}>0

such that
{\bf DP3^{+}}
is satisfied. Finally, since
a\in(0,1)
,

\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(0)}\|_{1,q}^{q}=1+\frac{a^{p+1}}{p+1}<\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}^{q}

and

\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(1)}\|_{1,q}^{q}=1+\frac{(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}<\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}^{q},

such that also
{\bf DP4^{+}}
is satisfied. Thus Corollary
5
implies:

Corollary 7
.

For
q\in(1,\infty)
and
a\in(0,1)
integration in the tensor product space
W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
suffers from the curse of dimensionality for positive quadrature formulas. In more detail, we have

N^{+}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$,}

where
p
is the Hölder conjugate of
q
and where

C_{p}=\left(\frac{p+1+a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1+\max(a^{p+1},(1-a)^{p+1})}\right)^{1/q}>1.

(41)

For example for
a=1/2
we have

\begin{array}[]{r||c|c|c|c|c}p&2&3&4&5&10\\
\hline\cr C_{p}&1.0198&1.01023&1.00465&1.00208&1.00004\end{array}

5.4
Generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a

For a point set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
in
[0,1)^{d}
the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
is defined as

L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a),{\rm gen}}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}):=\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\left(L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1,\mathfrak{u}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N,\mathfrak{u}}\})\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p},

where
L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a)}
denotes the
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored in
a
from Section
3.3
and where, for
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k,\mathfrak{u}}
denotes the projection of
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}
to the coordinates
j
in
\mathfrak{u}
.

Consider integration in the tensor product space
W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
. It can be shown that the worst-case error of a quasi-Monte Carlo rule (
28
) for
f\in W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
, which is based on a point set
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
, is exactly the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
of the point set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}:={\boldsymbol{a}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\pmod{1}
and where
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a,\ldots,a)
, i.e.,

e(W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d}),A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}})=L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a),{\rm gen}}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}),

where
1/p+1/q=1
. See
[
14
, Section 9.5.3]
. The initial generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
is

L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a),{\rm gen}}(\emptyset)=\left(1+\frac{a^{p+1}+(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)^{d/p}=e(0,d),

where the latter is the initial error of integration in
W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})
.

From Corollary
7
we therefore obtain:

Corollary 8
.

For
a\in(0,1)
and for
p\in(1,\infty)
the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy anchored at
a
suffers from the curse of dimensionality, i.e.,

\displaystyle N_{L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a),{\rm gen}}}(\varepsilon,d)

\displaystyle:=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \exists\,\mathcal{P}\subseteq[0,1)^{d}\mbox{ s.t. }|\mathcal{P}|=N\mbox{ and }L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a),{\rm gen}}(\mathcal{P})\leq\varepsilon L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a),{\rm gen}}(\emptyset)\},

grows exponentially fast with the dimension
d
. More detailed,

N_{L_{p}^{\pitchfork,(a),{\rm gen}}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{for all $\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)$ and $d\in\mathbb{N}$,}

where
C_{p}>1
is like in (
41
).

Generalized
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at

Likewise, also a generalized
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at

is studied. For a point set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}

-quadrant discrepancy at

L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a),{\rm gen}}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}):=\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]}\left(L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}(\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1,\mathfrak{u}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N,\mathfrak{u}}\})\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p},

where
L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a)}
denotes the
L_{p}
-quadrant discrepancy at

from Section
3.4
and where, for
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq[d]
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k,\mathfrak{u}}
denotes the projection of
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}
to the coordinates
j
in
\mathfrak{u}
.

Again, it can be shown that the worst-case error of a quasi-Monte Carlo rule (
28
) for

\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}

-quadrant discrepancy at

For

\displaystyle N_{L_{p}^{\boxplus,(a),{\rm gen}}}(\varepsilon,d)

grows exponentially fast with the dimension

. More detailed,
