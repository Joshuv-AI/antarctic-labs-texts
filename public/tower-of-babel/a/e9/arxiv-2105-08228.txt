Theory of thermoelectric effect in insulators
Yugo Onishi, Hiroki Isobe, Naoto Nagaosa

Abstract
We propose a thermopolarization effect with a purely electronic origin, where the electric polarization P P is induced by the temperature gradient ∇ T \nabla T . This is observable as a Seebeck effect in a finite-size system where the linear size L L is shorter than the Debye length λ \lambda , which characterizes the screening due to the thermal excitation of free carriers. The dual effect is the ac heat current induced by the time derivative of the electric field E E . The Seebeck coefficient S S diverges as ∼ t as / e ​ T \sim t_{\mathrm{as}}/eT when the temperature T T goes to 0 0 , where t as t_{\mathrm{as}} represents the asymmetry of the conduction band and the valence band and e e is the elementary charge.

In metals with Fermi degeneracy, the typical energy scale of an electron is

On the other hand, in band insulators, the thermally activated electrons carry both charge and heat. For finite thermoelectric responses, an asymmetry between the conduction band and the valence band is necessary. The energy of an electron is measured from the chemical potential and approximately half of the band gap
E_{G}/2

\Pi\sim E_{G}/2e

Therefore, the behavior of

Note that there is no charge current flowing while the heat current is finite in an experimental measurement of the Seebeck effect.
Therefore an important question is if the Seebeck effect can be defined for insulators without assuming the thermally excited electrons.

To observe the Seebeck effect in insulators experimentally, it is also important to consider the effects of carrier density variations at the sample ends
Mahan 2000a
;
Mahan 2015
.
In general, such effects due to the sample ends are screened by carriers with screening length
\lambda
, called the Debye length.
However, in insulators,
\lambda
can be larger than the sample size
L
at low temperatures and therefore we cannot observe the voltage drop across the sample even if the Seebeck coefficient is finite.
We note that the discussion in
Mahan 2000a
;
Mahan 2015
does not take into account the effects of charge at the sample ends due to polarization. As discussed below, polarization plays an important role in the Seebeck effect in insulators. In fact, the polarization is induced by the temperature gradient and the electric field due to the polarization contributes to the Seebeck effect at low temperatures where the screening by carriers in the bulk can be ignored.

The induction of the polarization by the temperature gradient is called thermopolarization effect.
References
Tagantsev 1987
;
Tagantsev 1991
study the thermopolarization effect from ion displacement within quantum theories and charge redistribution within classical theories.
Marvan phenomenologically discusses the relation between the thermopolarization effect and thermoelectric effects
Marvan 1969
. In contrast to the preceding works, we present thermopolarization effect from a purely electronic origin, based on quantum mechanics. We derive the response coefficients for the thermopolarization effect and discuss the relation to the Seebeck effect.

A calculation of polarization is theoretically a subtle problem because it depends on the boundary condition of the sample. This subtlety can be avoided by considering only the change of the polarization or the polarization current

;
Resta 1994
;
King-Smith and Vanderbilt 1993
;
Vanderbilt 2018

For example, one can obtain the polarizability as an electric current response coefficient to an adiabatically applied electric field,
E(t)=Ee^{\eta t}
, where
\eta
is an infinitesimal positive quantity. Similarly, we consider the polarization current due to an application of a temperature gradient and derive the thermopolarization effect in the present work.
These calculations can be done through the linear response theory for an adiabatically applied external field or, more generally, for an external field with a finite frequency
\omega
, as described below.
To discuss polarization, we need to include the interband effect in the discussion. Such effects on thermoelectric effects are rarely discussed, but they are investigated in a discussion on nonlinear thermoelectric effects
Bhalla 2021

The spin Seebeck effect in magnetic insulators, in which the spin voltage due to magnons is induced by the temperature gradient, has also been investigated

;
Xiao et al. 2010

However, the Seebeck effect we discuss below does not involve any magnetism and also does not require the metallic system attached to the insulator such as Pt to convert the spin voltage to the electric voltage drop.
