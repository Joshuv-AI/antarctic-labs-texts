Evaluation of transition rates from nonequilibrium instantons
Eric R. Heller, David T. Limmer

Abstract
Equilibrium rate theories play a crucial role in understanding rare, reactive events. However, they are inapplicable to a range of irreversible processes in systems driven far from thermodynamic equilibrium like active and biological matter. Here, we develop an efficient numerical method to compute the rate constant of rare nonequilibrium events in the weak-noise limit based on an instanton approximation to the stochastic path integral and illustrate its wide range of application. We demonstrate excellent agreement of the instanton rates with numerically exact results for a particle under a non-conservative force. We also study phase transitions in an active field theory. We elucidate how activity alters the stability of the two phases and their rates of interconversion in a manner that can be well described by modifying classical nucleation theory.



I
Introduction

Unlike their equilibrium counterparts, rare nonequilibrium transitions are not governed by the energetics of static transition states.
This renders efficient equilibrium techniques for computing rates and elucidating mechanisms largely invalid.
Instead, reaction rates in systems driven far from thermodynamic equilibrium are dependent on the details of their trajectories. This is formalized by Freidlin–Wentzell theory in the weak-noise limit with the optimal transition path or
instanton

Freidlin and Wentzell 2012
;
Onsager and Machlup 1953
;
Graham 1973
.
Instantons have recently emerged as a powerful computational tool, offering insights into a wide variety of nonequilibrium phenomena across diverse disciplines like fluid dynamics, soft active matter, biology, and chemistry
Weinan et al. 2004
;
Heymann and Vanden-Eijnden 2008a
;
Heymann and Vanden-Eijnden 2008b
;
Kohn et al. 2006
;
Grafke et al. 2015
;
Grafke et al. 2017
;
Fuchs et al. 2022
;
Chaves-O’Flynn et al. 2011
;
de la Cruz et al. 2018
;
Zakine and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
;
Woillez et al. 2019
;
Bouchet et al. 2019
;
Kikuchi et al. 2020
.

In this work, we develop a numerically efficient instanton method to compute the rate constant of rare nonequilibrium transitions, which, in addition to the path action, includes a prefactor encoding fluctuations around the optimal path. This nonequilibrium instanton rate theory (NEQI) is derived through a rigorous asymptotic approximation of the exact path-integral representation of the transition rate and provides a quantitative understanding of transitions in complex driven systems.
We show the instanton rates computed with our method are in excellent agreement with numerically exact results at weak noise for a particle under a non-gradient force and illustrate dramatic changes of transition rates and mechanisms due to nonequilibrium driving. Further, we apply our method to an active field theory relevant to the study of how phase transforms are altered in the presence of active forces, revealing how insights from classical nucleation theory can be recovered even in nonequilibrium phase transitions
Cates and Nardini 2023
;
Richard et al. 2016
;
Redner et al. 2016
.

Derivation of the nonequilibrium instanton rate equation

We consider a system with position vector
{\mathsf{x}=\{x_{1},...,x_{f}\}}
described by overdamped Brownian motion

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}[\mathsf{x}(t)]+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\mathbf{\Lambda}\,\eta(t),

(1)

We examine rate problems, where, in the absence of the nonequilibrium driving, an unstable transition state (TS) at

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\frac{\braket{h_{\text{R}}(0)\dot{h}_{\text{P}}(t_{\text{f}})}}{\braket{h_{\text{R}}}},

The indicator functions

The flux–side correlation function can be written as an integral over the dividing surface parameterized by the

\displaystyle=\int\rho(\mathsf{x}_{\sigma},t_{\text{f}};\mathsf{x}_{\text{R}},0)\,\dot{{x}}_{\sigma}(t_{\text{f}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{s},

(3)

where

\mathsf{x}_{\sigma}
on the dividing surface at time

\dot{x}_{\sigma}=\nabla\sigma(\mathsf{x})\cdot\dot{\mathsf{x}}

\rho(t=0)=\delta[\mathsf{x}(0)-\mathsf{x}_{\text{R}}]

;
Lehmann et al. 2000b
;

The probability density in Eq. (
3

\displaystyle\rho(\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}},t_{\text{f}};\mathsf{x}_{\text{i}},0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{x}(0)=\mathsf{x}_{\text{i}}}^{\mathsf{x}(t_{\text{f}})=\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}}}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\mathsf{x}(t)]/\epsilon}\,\mathcal{D}[\mathsf{x}(t)],

(4)

S[\mathsf{x}]=\int_{0}^{t_{\text{f}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{x},\dot{\mathsf{x}})\,\mathrm{d}t

;
Graham 1973
;

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{x},\dot{\mathsf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}[\dot{\mathsf{x}}-\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{x})]^{\text{T}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\,[\dot{\mathsf{x}}-\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{x})].

(5)

The exact evaluation of Eq. (
4

;

In the weak-noise limit, however, we can use Laplace’s method to devise an efficient approximation about the minimum-action path or instanton,
\bar{\mathsf{x}}(t)
.

The instanton, which can be interpreted as the optimal transition path, constitutes the centerpiece of our rate method. The action of the instanton is determined by its endpoints and the propagation time so that it can be written as
S\equiv S(\mathsf{x}_{\text{i}},\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}};t_{\text{f}})=\int_{0}^{t_{\text{f}}}\bar{\mathsf{p}}^{\text{T}}\!\mathbf{D}\bar{\mathsf{p}}\,\mathrm{d}t
,
where

\bar{\mathsf{p}}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}[\dot{\bar{\mathsf{x}}}-\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\bar{\mathsf{x}})]/2
is the canonical momentum along the instanton.
The Lagrangian in Eq. (
5
) can be Legendre transformed to obtain the corresponding Hamiltonian, whose value
E=\bar{\mathsf{p}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{D}\bar{\mathsf{p}}+\bar{\mathsf{p}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
is conserved along the instanton because we consider forces without explicit time dependence.
The Hamilton–Jacobi formalism naturally yields the well-known relations for the instanton energy and the momentum at the endpoint
Landau and Lifshitz 1976
;
Heller and Richardson 2020a

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}

\displaystyle\equiv\bar{\mathsf{p}}(t_{\text{f}})=\frac{\partial S}{\partial\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}}},\qquad\frac{\partial S}{\partial t_{\text{f}}}=-E.

(6)

For the Laplace approximation of the rate, we require an instanton from reactant to product that minimizes the action not only in coordinate space but also in time
t_{\text{f}}
, which is achieved when
E=0
or equivalently
t_{\text{f}}\rightarrow\infty
.
Consequently, the instanton takes the form of a “double kink” illustrated in Appendix
A
, connecting the three fixed points of the force, even in the presence of a non-gradient force.
In spite of the infinite propagation time, the action remains finite because the dwell phases do not contribute.

From the stationarity conditions
\delta S=0
and
E=0
follows
\dot{\bar{\mathsf{x}}}^{2}=[\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\bar{\mathsf{x}})]^{2}
.
At equilibrium, the action is directly related to the height of the potential-energy barrier because the instanton minimizes the potential along its path, thus aligning with the mobility-scaled conservative force
\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}_{\text{c}}=-\mathbf{\mu}\nabla V
.
Consequently,
\dot{\bar{\mathsf{x}}}=\pm\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}_{\text{c}}
, where the minus sign corresponds to the
activation path
from reactant to TS, and the plus sign corresponds to the
relaxation path
, which follows the force into the product configuration.
Notably, only the activation path contributes to the action.
Unlike the relaxation path, which remains aligned with the force also under nonequilibrium conditions, the activation path generally deviates, leading to changes in transition rate and mechanism.

Since the relaxation path does not contribute to the rate
Luckock and McKane 1990
, the natural choice for the location of the dividing surface is the unstable fixed point of the total force, to which we will refer as a TS. Therefore, we can focus solely on the activation path, which we henceforth call the instanton.
Combining Eqs. (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
) leads to the path-integral representation of the rate constant

\displaystyle k\,Z_{\text{R}}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\int\frac{\dot{x}_{\sigma}(t_{\text{f}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-S_{N}/\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\mathsf{x}_{1}...\mathrm{d}\mathsf{x}_{N-1}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{s}}{(\text{det}[4\pi\epsilon\tau\mathbf{D}])^{N/2}},

(7)

where, in order to evaluate the action numerically, we discretized the paths into
N
equal-time segments of length
\tau=t_{\text{f}}/N

de Pirey et al. 2022
. In the instanton limit, the reactant path partition function

=1.
The discretized action
S_{N}\equiv S_{N}(\mathsf{x}_{0},...,\mathsf{x}_{N};t_{\text{f}})
is a function of the “beads”
\mathsf{x}_{n}
, which constitute copies of the system along the path

S_{N}=\frac{\tau}{4}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left[\frac{\mathsf{x}_{n+1}-\mathsf{x}_{n}}{\tau}-\mathbf{\mu}F(\mathsf{x}_{n})\right]^{\text{T}}\\
\times\mathbf{D}^{-1}\left[\frac{\mathsf{x}_{n+1}-\mathsf{x}_{n}}{\tau}-\mathbf{\mu}F(\mathsf{x}_{n})\right].

(8)

We aim to evaluate the integrals over the beads and the dividing surface with Laplace’s method.
Hence, the instanton with endpoints
\mathsf{x}_{0}=\mathsf{x}_{\text{R}}
and
\mathsf{x}_{N}=\mathsf{x}^{\ddagger}
must satisfy
\nabla_{\text{s}}S_{N}=0
, where
\nabla_{\text{s}}=(\partial/\partial\mathsf{x}_{1},...,\partial/\partial\mathsf{x}_{N-1},\partial/\partial\mathsf{s})
is the gradient with respect to the positions of all intermediate beads and the parameters of the dividing surface.
The optimal path can therefore be located with standard optimization algorithms

;
Weinan et al. 2004

(see Appendix
B
).
From the stationarity condition
\partial S_{N}/\partial s_{i}=\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}\cdot\partial\mathsf{x}_{N}/\partial s_{i}=0

Lehmann et al. 2003
. Given that the mobility-scaled force and the instanton velocity are parallel at the TS, it follows that
\dot{\bar{x}}_{\sigma}(t_{\text{f}})=\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{D}\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}/\bar{p}_{\text{f}}

\bar{p}_{\text{f}}=\lVert\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}\rVert
.

Note that the second-derivative matrix of the action,
\nabla_{\text{s}}^{2}S_{N}

\nabla F
is not symmetric.
Approximating Eq. (
7
) by Laplace’s method
now appears straightforward.
However, the instanton, as an infinite-time trajectory, can dwell at the reactant or the TS for an arbitrary time without changing its action (see Appendix

However, progress can be made by separating the integral over the Goldstone mode from the integral over the other, orthogonal modes

;
Luckock and McKane 1990

IV
Nucleation in an active field theory

To illustrate the generality of our approach, we next consider the phase transformation of a collection of active particles. Rather than consider an explicit particle model, we employ a coarse-grained representation or active field theory known as Active model A dynamics
Cates and Tailleur 2015
;
Caballero and Cates 2020
, which has proven fruitful in understanding the emergent phenomena of these systems on large length scales.
The Langevin equation for a non-conserved scalar field
\phi
in
f
dimensions is

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(\mathsf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\mu F[\phi(\mathsf{x},t)]+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\eta(\mathsf{x},t),

(11)

where the mobility
\mu
is constant, the white noise is defined via
\braket{\eta(\mathsf{x},t)\eta(\mathsf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}=\delta(\mathsf{x}-\mathsf{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

Our nonequilibrium instanton theory directly extends to such field-theoretical descriptions, where the action,

\displaystyle S[\phi(\mathsf{x},t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t_{\text{f}}}\int\left(\dot{\phi}(\mathsf{x},t)-\mu F[\phi(\mathsf{x},t)]\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{x}\,\mathrm{d}t,

Notably, the driving accelerates the rates for all values of
h
, which is accompanied by a decrease in the size of the critical nucleus required for a transition (see Appendix
F
).
The speed-up is most pronounced for processes with high equilibrium barriers.
Despite these nonequilibrium effects, the inset of Fig.
3
(b) reveals that the rate curves for different
\nu
nearly collapse when one accounts for the relative stability of the two phases using the difference in instanton action between forward and backward processes.
This suggests that the activity primarily shifts the coexistence line between the two phases, not alters the effective surface tension. This shift could be incorporated into a modified classical nucleation theory through a renormalized chemical potential
Cho and Jacobs 2023
. Extending this analysis to conservative field theories like active model B would be an interesting future direction
Wittkowski et al. 2014
.

V
Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed an instanton method to compute the rate constant of rare nonequilibrium transitions, which is derived through a rigorous weak-noise approximation of the exact rate expression. The numerical efficiency of the method allows applications to complex high-dimensional systems in either particle or field-theory representations.
NEQI combines quantitative estimates of the nonequilibrium rate with the intuitive insight into the transition mechanism from the optimal path, which complements nonequilibrium sampling approaches also outside of the weak-noise limit
Das et al. 2022
;
Allen et al. 2009
.
Due to its generality, efficiency and conceptual simplicity, we believe the method to find wide application in the description of rare transitions in dissipative systems across scales.

The source code for an instanton calculation and the data that supports the findings of this study are openly available online
Heller and Limmer
.

Appendix A
Time-translation symmetry

As described in Sec.
II
, the instanton is an infinite-time trajectory. The typical “double kink” shape of an optimal transition path between reactant and product is illustrated in Fig.
4
, where the path length is shown as a function of time.

Figure 4:
Plot of the path length as a function of time along an instanton in the long-time limit. The comparatively fast transitions between the fixed points of the force are separated by long dwell phases at the reactant, product and TS configurations, giving rise to the typical double kink shape. The activation path reaches the TS at time
t_{\text{f}}
. The red dashed paths indicate equivalent activation paths related through time translation.

It can be seen that the instanton may dwell for an arbitrary, quasi-infinite time at the reactant, product or TS configurations (the fixed points of the force) without changing its action because force, velocity and canonical momentum go to zero at these points [Eq. (
5
)]. Hence, there exists an infinite number of instantons related through time translation, as indicated in Fig.
4
.

The rate-determining activation path, connecting reactant and TS, corresponds to the first kink in Fig.
4
.
In numerical calculations, the long-time limit is reached when the activation process is fully resolved, which is fast compared to the infinitely long dwell phases.
The time-translation symmetry of the activation path manifests itself as a zero eigenvalue in the second-derivative matrix of the action, rendering a “naïve” Laplace approximation of the rate divergent.
We address this issue in Sec.
II
by transforming the integral over the zero mode to a collective time integral, as encountered in the evaluation of flux–flux correlation functions, which must be solved separately.

Appendix B
Instanton optimization

Locating an instanton consists in the minimization of the discretized Onsager–Machlup action [Eq. (
8
)], where the endpoints are fixed to the reactant and TS configurations. The TS can be located either by optimizing a saddle point of the force directly or by locating the full minimum-action pathway between reactant and product.
A whole host of established minimization routines may be put to use for the instanton optimization. In this work, we employ conjugate-gradient and Newton–Raphson algorithms that minimize the magnitude of the gradient vector of the discretized action with respect to all intermediate bead positions.
For the latter, a small shift must be added to the diagonal of the second-derivative matrix to avoid problems with zero eigenvalues
Richardson 2018
. The banded, symmetric properties of the second-derivative matrix of the action may be leveraged to significantly improve the memory and time efficiency of Newton–Raphson optimizers for large systems. Furthermore, we update the second-derivative matrix during the optimization
Fletcher 1987
, requiring only a single calculation of the second-derivative matrix for the whole minimization.
Alternatively, specialized instanton optimization routines have been suggested
Heymann and Vanden-Eijnden 2008a
;
Kikuchi et al. 2020
;
Zakine and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
.

The instanton rate must be converged with respect to both the number of beads,
N
, and time,
t_{\text{f}}
. For the latter, a plateauing plot of the path length, as in Fig.
4
, indicates that the long-time limit is reached. Additionally, the numerical value of the “zero eigenvalue” from time translation can be monitored because the time-translation symmetry is only approximate for long but finite time. As
t_{\text{f}}
is increased, the ratio between the zero eigenvalue and the remaining eigenvalues decreases. A sufficiently small ratio thus indicates convergence. However, the most reliable metric for convergence remains the comparison between actions and rates at different values of
t_{\text{f}}
and
N
.
We have found that it is often useful to first converge a minimum-action path with a relatively short time based on a simple initial guess, e.g., equidistant beads along a straight line connecting the endpoints. The result can then be used as an initial guess for subsequent optimizations with gradually increasing
t_{\text{f}}
and
N
.

Appendix D
Trajectory formalism

The direct calculation of the instanton rate from a discretized path integral [Eq. (
9
)] offers high numerical stability but requires diagonalizing a
Nf\times Nf
matrix, scaling as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
with the number of beads. Utilizing the banded structure of
\nabla_{\text{s}}^{2}S_{N}
reduces the scaling to
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
. However, for certain large systems requiring many beads for convergence, the rate calculation may still become the bottleneck.
Here, we present an alternative, computationally more efficient approach to calculating the instanton rate within the “trajectory formalism”, building upon the work of Althorpe and Richardson for Euclidean actions
Althorpe 2011
;
Richardson 2015
.

The key quantity in the definition of the rate constant is the joint probability density [Eqs. (3),(4)], which we approximate by Laplace’s method

\displaystyle\rho(\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}},t_{\text{f}};\mathsf{x}_{\text{i}},0)\sim[\det(\mathbf{D})]^{-Nf/2}\sqrt{\frac{[\det(\mathbf{J})]^{-1}}{(4\pi\epsilon\,\tau)^{f}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-S_{N}/\epsilon},

(14)

where all quantities are evaluated at the instanton,
\bar{\mathsf{x}}(t)
, connecting
\mathsf{x}_{\text{i}}
to
\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}}
in time
t_{\text{f}}
. The instanton satisfies
\nabla_{\text{r}}S_{N}=0
, where
\nabla_{\text{r}}=(\partial/\partial\mathsf{x}_{1},...,\partial/\partial\mathsf{x}_{N-1})

defines the derivative vector with respect to all intermediate beads. The fluctuations around the instanton are encoded in
\mathbf{J}=2\tau\nabla_{\text{r}}^{2}S_{N}

Alternatively, the probability density in Eq. (
14
) may be written in analogy to the van-Vleck approximation of the quantum-mechanical propagator
Hunt and Ross 1981

The trajectory formalism developed above can be used to approximate probability densities and related quantities of interest. However, as laid out in Appendix

Getfert and Reimann 2010

.

C=C_{\text{a}}C_{\text{b}}\\
\times\left[\det\left(\frac{\partial^{2}S(\mathsf{x}_{\text{i}},\mathsf{x}_{\text{m}};t_{\text{m}})}{\partial\mathsf{x}_{\text{m}}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}S(\mathsf{x}_{\text{m}},\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}};t_{\text{f}}-t_{\text{m}})}{\partial\mathsf{x}_{\text{m}}^{2}}\right)\right]^{-1},

\displaystyle C_{\text{a}}

(23)

\displaystyle C_{\text{b}}

\displaystyle=\det\left(-\frac{\partial^{2}S(\mathsf{x}_{\text{m}},\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}};t_{\text{f}}-t_{\text{m}})}{\partial\mathsf{x}_{\text{m}}\partial\mathsf{x}_{\text{f}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t_{\text{m}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}[\mathsf{x}(t)]\mathrm{d}t},

\displaystyle k\sim\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{\dot{\bar{q}}_{\text{m}}}{\bar{p}_{\text{f}}}\,\sqrt{\frac{C_{\text{a}}C_{\text{b}}}{\Omega_{\text{m}}\Omega^{\ddagger}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-S/\epsilon},

For a

\displaystyle V[\phi(x)]

\displaystyle Z^{\ddagger}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\text{det}^{\prime}\left(-\kappa\Delta+\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial\phi^{2}}\right)_{\!\!\phi(\mathsf{x})=\phi^{\ddagger}(\mathsf{x})}\right\rvert^{-1/2},

From the stationarity condition at the TS,
\delta V/\delta\phi=0

\displaystyle V[\phi^{\ddagger}(x)]

\displaystyle=\int\left[\frac{\kappa}{2}\,\lvert\nabla\phi^{\ddagger}\rvert^{2}+W(\phi^{\ddagger})+E_{x}(\phi^{\ddagger})-E_{x}\right]\mathrm{d}\mathsf{x}.

\displaystyle=B_{x}+V[\phi_{>}(\mathsf{x})],

(32)

Here, we used that,

In practice, we represent the field on a grid. Using Eq. (
30

\xi\sum_{i=0}^{N_{x}-1}\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\frac{\varphi_{i+1}-\varphi_{i}}{\xi}\right)^{\!\!2}+W(\varphi_{i}),

(33)

where
\varphi_{i}\equiv\phi(x_{i})

\varphi_{0}=\varphi_{N}

The optimization of stationary points and evaluation of the fluctuation terms [Eqs. (
31

\varphi_{i}
and correctly accounting for
\xi

8
)].
The corresponding discretized instanton action in field space [Eq. (
12

\displaystyle S_{N}(\phi_{0},...,\phi_{N};t_{\text{f}})=\frac{\tau\xi}{4}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\phi_{n+1}-\phi_{n}}{\tau}-\mu F(\phi_{n})\right)^{\!\!2},

(34)

where each bead
\phi_{n}=(\varphi_{0},...,\varphi_{N_{x}-1})
corresponds to a field configuration, and

is the sum of all conservative and non-conservative forces.
The instanton connects
\phi_{0}\equiv\phi_{\text{R}}
to
\phi_{N}\equiv\phi^{\ddagger}

34
) with respect to all intermediate beads
(\phi_{1},...,\phi_{N-1})
.

The path-integral representation of the rate is given by

k\,Z_{\text{R}}=\lim_{N,N_{x}\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\xi}{4\pi\epsilon\tau}\right)^{\!\!NN_{x}/2}\\
\times\int\dot{\phi}_{\sigma}(t_{\text{f}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-S_{N}(\phi_{0},...,\phi_{N};t_{\text{f}})/\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\phi_{1}...\mathrm{d}\phi_{N-1}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{s},

(35)

where
\dot{\phi}_{\sigma}(t_{\text{f}})
is the velocity orthogonal to the dividing surface and the generalization to
f
dimensions is straightforward.
Carrying out a Laplace approximation in analogy to Sec.
II
then leads to the instanton rate for active field theories in Eq. (
13
).
The fluctuations around the instantons are contained within the determinant of
\nabla^{2}_{\phi}S_{N}
, where
\nabla_{\phi}=(\partial/\partial\phi_{1},...,\partial/\partial\phi_{N-1},\partial/\partial\mathsf{s})
is the derivative vector with respect to all intermediate beads and the parameters of the field-space dividing surface
\mathsf{s}
.
However, in addition to the time-translation mode, also the modes corresponding to translation of the interface must be projected out via the zero eigenvectors of
\partial F(\phi^{\ddagger})/\partial\phi
.
As discussed in Sec.
II
, the reaction coordinate at the TS can be projected out via
\bar{p}_{\text{f}}=\frac{1}{\xi}\frac{\partial S_{N}}{\partial\phi_{N}}
.

Appendix G
Rate calculation with variable time steps

Beads tend to accumulate around the fixed points of the force, which is due to the kink shape of the instanton in Fig.
4
. Several methods have been devised to distribute the beads in a more uniform way, either by transforming from physical time to an arc length coordinate
Heymann and Vanden-Eijnden 2008a
;
Heymann and Vanden-Eijnden 2008b
;
Zakine and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
or by lifting the requirement of equal time steps
Zhou et al. 2008
;
Rommel and Kästner 2011
;
Cvitaš and Althorpe 2016
, typically choosing longer time steps close to the fixed points and shorter time steps where the system configuration changes fastest.

Especially the latter strategy is easily incorporated into the presented formalism by following Ref.
Rommel and Kästner 2011
. The instanton then corresponds to the minimum of the discretized action

\tilde{S}_{N}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{\mathsf{x}_{n+1}-\mathsf{x}_{n}}{\sqrt{\tau_{n+1}}}-\sqrt{\tau_{n+1}}\,\mathbf{\mu}F(\mathsf{x}_{n})\right)^{\!\!\text{T}}\\
\times\mathbf{D}^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathsf{x}_{n+1}-\mathsf{x}_{n}}{\sqrt{\tau_{n+1}}}-\sqrt{\tau_{n+1}}\,\mathbf{\mu}F(\mathsf{x}_{n})\right),

(36)

with variable time intervals
\tau_{n}>0
.
The rate expression from Eq. (
9
) generalized for flexible time intervals is given by

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2\pi\tilde{p}_{\text{f}}}\,\sqrt{\tilde{B}_{t}}\,\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{Nf}\lambda^{\text{R}}_{n}}{\prod_{n=3}^{Nf}\lambda^{\text{I}}_{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{S}_{N}/\epsilon},

(37)

where
(\lambda_{n}^{\text{R/I}})^{2}
are the eigenvalues of the time-weighted second-derivative matrix of the action with blocks
\mathbf{H}_{mn}=\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{S}_{N}}{\partial\mathsf{x}_{m}\partial\mathsf{x}_{n}}/\sqrt{\tau_{n}\tau_{m}}
evaluated at the reactant and instanton configuration respectively, where
n,m\in[1,N]
. The reactant path is collapsed at
\mathsf{x}_{\text{R}}
, which can be used to simplify the calculation of the eigenvalues
(\lambda_{n}^{\text{R}})^{2}

Rommel and Kästner 2011
. As in Eq. (
9
), the time-translation mode and the reaction coordinate are excluded from the calculation of the fluctuation factor at the instanton.
Further, we defined the time-weighted canonical momentum at the endpoint
\tilde{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{S}_{N}}{\partial\mathsf{x}_{N}}/\sqrt{\tau_{N}}
with norm
\tilde{p}_{\text{f}}=\lVert\tilde{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}\rVert
, and we indicate that
\tilde{B}_{t}
must be evaluated on the chosen (non-uniform) time grid.

In analogy to Ref.
Rommel and Kästner 2011
, a possible choice for the
\tau_{n}
can be derived from the conservation of the energy along the instanton. Consequently,
\lVert\dot{\mathsf{x}}(t)\rVert=\sqrt{4E+[\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{x}(t))]^{2}}
and upon discretization
\tau_{n+1}=\frac{\Delta x}{\sqrt{4E+[\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\mathsf{x}_{n})]^{2}}}
, where
E
can be chosen so as to achieve the desired distribution of beads and the constant
\Delta x
is fixed by the relation
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau_{n}=t_{\text{f}}
.
In the process of converging the rate constant, instantons with different
N
are optimized, as shown in Table
1
. Based on a previously optimized path (e.g., with lower
N
), a good estimate for
E
can be obtained as the average of the instantaneous energy
E_{n}=\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{n}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{D}\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{n}+\bar{\mathsf{p}}_{n}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{\mu}\mathsf{F}(\bar{\mathsf{x}}_{n})
evaluated at each bead along the path. In Fig.
9
(a), we show optimized instantons at different values of
E
initialized from a straight line with equidistant segments connecting reactant minimum and TS. It can be seen that smaller values of
E
lead to a larger number of beads in the transition region because the time steps in the center of the path shorten compared to the time steps close to the endpoints. Note that it is crucial to project out the time-translation mode in the optimization of these instantons with flexible time step.

From Fig.
9
(b), it can be seen that a decent choice for
E
can significantly enhance the rate of convergence of the instanton results with respect to the number of beads.
However, it is worth noting that Eq. (
37
) converges to the same result in the
N\rightarrow\infty
limit irrespective of the choice for the
\tau_{n}
. In the spirit of the geometric minimum action method
Heymann and Vanden-Eijnden 2008a
;
Heymann and Vanden-Eijnden 2008b
;
Zakine and Vanden-Eijnden 2023
, also segments of equal arc length can be obtained by solving the system of equations
\frac{\partial\tilde{S}_{N}}{\partial\mathsf{x}_{n}}=0
for the
\tau_{n}
under the constraint
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau_{n}=t_{\text{f}}
based on an initial path with equidistant segments. However, this typically does not lead to the fastest convergence of the rate constant because very few beads are placed close to the fixed points
Rommel and Kästner 2011
.

Appendix H
State-dependent diffusion

Here, we show that the proposed instanton method extends straightforwardly to the case of state-dependent diffusion, where the dynamics are governed by an overdamped Langevin equation with multiplicative (position-dependent) noise

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{\mu}(\mathsf{x})\mathsf{F}[\mathsf{x}(t)]+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\mathbf{\Lambda}(\mathsf{x})\,\eta(t).

(38)

Both the diffusion matrix,
\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{x})=\mathbf{\Lambda}(\mathsf{x})\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\!\text{T}}(\mathsf{x})
, and mobility matrix,
\mathbf{\mu}(\mathsf{x})=\beta\epsilon\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{x})
, depend on position, and we again assume
\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{x})
to be non-singular. The derivatives of the discretized action [Eq. (
8
)] thus also involve derivatives of the diffusion and mobility matrices. However, the general procedure of deriving Eq. (
9
) as well as its numerical evaluation remains the same. Accounting for the position-dependence of the diffusion matrix in the path measure then leads to the following definition of the fluctuation determinant
\Sigma=\det^{\prime}[2\tau\nabla^{2}_{\text{s}}S_{N}]\prod_{n=1}^{N}\text{det}[\mathbf{D}(\mathsf{x}_{n})]
. Here, we employ the Itô convention for the interpretation of the stochastic differential equation as well as for the corresponding path integral. The instanton method, however, can also be used with other discretization conventions
de Pirey et al. 2022
.

Appendix I
Underdamped dynamics

Figure 11:
Underdamped instanton pathway in phase space for potential

In the main text, we focus on dynamics described by an overdamped Langevin equation, as is common in most condensed-phase environments, where inertial motion is rapidly quenched. However, some systems exhibit degrees of freedom with weak friction, for which the inertial motion becomes significant. In these cases, the underdamped Langevin equation must be employed

\displaystyle m\ddot{\mathsf{x}}(t)+\gamma\dot{\mathsf{x}}(t)=\mathsf{F}[\mathsf{x}(t)]+\sqrt{2\epsilon\gamma}\,\eta(t),

(39)

where for notational simplicity the mass
m
and friction
\gamma
are taken to be the same for all degrees of freedom. Following Ref.
Lehmann et al. 2003
, Eq. (
39
) can be written in the extended space
\vec{\mathsf{x}}=(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{v})

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{\mathsf{x}}}

\displaystyle=\vec{\mathsf{F}}+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\vec{\mathbf{\sigma}}\vec{\mathsf{\eta}},

(40)

where

\displaystyle\vec{\mathsf{F}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{v}\\
\frac{1}{m}\mathsf{F}-\frac{\gamma}{m}\mathsf{v}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\vec{\mathbf{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{m^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\delta\,\mathbb{I}&0\\
0&\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix},

(41)

and
\vec{\mathbf{D}}=\vec{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{\text{T}}\vec{\mathbf{\sigma}}
. Here, an auxiliary noise scaled by
\delta
is introduced to avoid a singular
\vec{\mathbf{D}}
matrix.
The regularization factor
\delta
imposes the constraint
\dot{\mathsf{x}}=\mathsf{v}
and can thus be viewed as a Lagrange multiplier, whose optimal value is zero.
In practice,
\delta
can simply be set to a constant that is small enough to tightly enforce the constraint. The instanton and the rate constant are then independent of the precise value of
\delta
, which is easily tested numerically. Alternatively, a Legendre transform to a Hamiltonian formulation of the instanton dynamics could be employed
Lehmann et al. 2003
.
The action for an underdamped system is given by

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{\text{f}}}\vec{\mathsf{p}}^{\,\text{T}}\!\vec{\mathbf{D}}\vec{\mathsf{p}}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(42)

where
\vec{\mathsf{p}}=\vec{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}[\dot{\vec{\mathsf{x}}}-\vec{\mathsf{F}}]/2
is the canonical momentum along the instanton in the extended space.

In Fig.
11
, we show an underdamped instanton for a one-dimensional double well in phase space. It can be seen that the inertial motion leads to vortex-like curves within the potential wells, corresponding to the particle bouncing back and forth before overcoming the barrier. Like a person on a swing, the underdamped system can thus build up momentum to facilitate a more efficient transition, a mechanism that is lacking in overdamped descriptions due to the absence of inertial motion.

Given the similarity between Eq. (
39
) and Eq. (
1
), the
derivation of the underdamped instanton rate is analogous to the derivation for overdamped systems but in the extended
\vec{\mathsf{x}}
-space. The boundary conditions for the activation path are
\vec{\mathsf{x}}_{\text{R}}=(\mathsf{x}_{\text{R}},0)
and
\vec{\mathsf{x}}^{\ddagger}=(\mathsf{x}^{\ddagger},0)
. The resulting underdamped instanton rate constant is given by

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{4\pi\tau}\sqrt{\frac{B_{t}}{\tau\vec{p}_{\text{f}}^{\,2}\Sigma}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-S_{N}/\epsilon},

(43)

where the fluctuation determinant
\Sigma=\det^{\prime}[2\tau\vec{\nabla}^{2}_{\text{s}}S_{N}](\text{det}[\vec{\mathbf{D}}])^{N}
is obtained from the second-derivative matrix of the action in the extended space. The time-translation mode and the reaction coordinate along
\vec{\mathsf{p}}_{\text{f}}
are again excluded from the determinant, and
B_{t}=\int\dot{\vec{\mathsf{x}}}^{\,2}\,\mathrm{d}t
is the Jacobian for the transformation of the integral over the time-translation mode.

.

For systems at thermodynamic equilibrium, the instanton rate reduces to the underdamped Kramers–Langer rate

\displaystyle k_{\text{KLT}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\text{u}}}{2\pi}\,\sqrt{\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}V_{\text{R}})}{\lvert\det(\nabla^{2}V^{\ddagger})\rvert}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V^{\ddagger}},

(44)

where
\lambda_{\text{u}}
is the sole positive eigenvalue of the Jacobian of the extended force,
\nabla\vec{\mathsf{F}}(\vec{\mathsf{x}}^{\ddagger})
, evaluated at the TS.
