Hoffmann-Jørgensen Inequalities for Random Walks on the Cone of Positive Definite Matrices
Armine Bagyan and Donald Richards

Abstract
We consider random walks on the cone of m × m m\times m positive definite matrices, where the underlying random matrices have orthogonally invariant distributions on the cone and the Riemannian metric is the measure of distance on the cone. By applying results of Khare and Rajaratnam ( Ann. Probab. , 45 (2017), 4101–4111), we obtain inequalities of Hoffmann-Jørgensen type for such random walks on the cone. In the case of the Wishart distribution W m ​ ( a , I m ) W_{m}(a,I_{m}) , with index parameter a a and matrix parameter I m I_{m} , the identity matrix, we derive explicit and computable bounds for each term appearing in the Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities. Key words and phrases . Orthogonal invariance; Riemannian metric; submartingale; symmetric cone; Thompson’s metric; Wishart distribution. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification . Primary: 60E15, 62E15. Secondary: 60B20, 62E17. Running head : Hoffmann-Jørgensen Inequalities for Positive Definite Matrices.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider inequalities of Hoffmann-Jørgensen type for random walks on the cone of positive definite matrices. These inequalities are obtained by adapting results of Khare and Rajaratnam
[
19
]
obtained in the broader setting of metric semigroups. In studying these inequalities on matrix cones, we are motivated by the appearance of random samples of positive definite matrices in numerous fields, including: statistical inference on Riemannian manifolds
[
15
,
21
,
22
]
, microwave engineering
[
31
, p. 156 ff.]
, diffusion tensor imaging
[
11
,
18
]
, financial time series
[
5
]
, wireless communication systems
[
30
]
, polarimetric radar imaging
[
4
]
, factor analysis
[
8
]
, and goodness-of-fit testing
[
14
]
.

The resulting generalized Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities on
\mathcal{P}_{m}
, the cone of
m\times m
positive definite (symmetric) matrices, are derived under the assumption that the underlying random matrices have an orthogonally invariant distribution on the cone. Specializing to the case of the Wishart distribution
W_{m}(a,I_{m})
, with index parameter
a
and identity matrix parameter
I_{m}
, we derive explicit and computable bounds for each term appearing in the Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities. Although we restrict our attention in this paper to
\mathcal{P}_{m}
, our methods apply more generally to the cone of Hermitian positive definite matrices and to abstract symmetric cones
[
10
,
12
]
.

In Section
2
, we provide notation and preliminaries for random walks on
\mathcal{P}_{m}
and for the natural action of
GL(m,\R)
, the group of nonsingular
m\times m
matrices, on the random walks. We provide in this section some necessary properties of the Riemannian metric, which is perhaps the most prominent metric on
\mathcal{P}_{m}
, and we show in Proposition
2.3
that the probability distribution of the distance between orthogonally invariant random matrices is preserved under orthogonally invariant random walks.

In Section
3
we derive from a result of Khare and Rajaratnam
[
19
, Theorem A]
a generalization, to the cone
\mathcal{P}_{m}
, of the classical Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities. This result, which is stated in Theorem
3.1
, provides bounds on the probability distribution of the distance between
X_{n}
, the
n
th “step” in an invariant random walk from the initial matrix
I_{m}
, the
m\times m
identity matrix.

In order to apply an Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequality in a practical setting, it is necessary to calculate or obtain an upper bound for each term appearing in the inequality; this will be seen to be a non-trivial problem for the random walks considered here. In Section
4
, we consider the special case in which the random walk is generated by random samples from the Wishart distribution
W_{m}(a,I_{m})
, with index parameter
a
and matrix parameter
I_{m}
, the identity matrix; we are particularly interested in Wishart-distributed random walks because of their appearance in the literature on diffusion tensor imaging, factor analysis, and financial volatility. Under the Wishart assumption, we obtain explicit, computable bounds for each term in the Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities in Theorem
3.1
.

Finally, in Appendix
A
, we establish the submartingale properties of some random variables appearing in the generalized Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities and show that the corresponding Kolmogorov inequalities reduce to Markov’s inequalities for those variables.

2
Random matrices and random walks on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}_{m}}

We denote by
G
the general linear group
GL(m,\R)
of all real,
m\times m
, nonsingular matrices. The group
G

acts
on
\mathcal{P}_{m}

via
the correspondence,
g\cdot A=gAg^{\prime}
, where
g\in G
,
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, and
g^{\prime}
denotes the transpose of
g
. It is elementary to verify that this correspondence is a
group action
, i.e.,
g_{1}g_{2}\cdot A=g_{1}\cdot(g_{2}\cdot A)
, for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
.

The group action also is transitive: given
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, there exists
g\in G
such that
g\cdot A=B
; simply choose
g=B^{1/2}A^{-1/2}
. Alternatively, since
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, there exist
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
such that
A=g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}
,
B=g_{2}g_{2}^{\prime}
; then
g=g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}
satisfies
g\cdot A=B
. Denote by
I_{m}
the identity matrix in
G
. Under this group action, the isotropy group of
I_{m}
is
O(m)
, the maximal compact subgroup of
m\times m
orthogonal matrices. Thus, the homogeneous space
G/O(m)
can be identified with
\mathcal{P}_{m}
through the correspondence
g\cdot k=(gk)(gk)^{\prime}=gg^{\prime}
for all
k\in O(m)
.

Whenever two random entities
X
and
Y
have the same probability distribution, we will write
X\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}Y
. Then a random matrix
X\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
is said to be
orthogonally invariant
if
X\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}kXk^{\prime}
for any
k\in O(m)
. Examples of orthogonally invariant distributions are the well-known Wishart distribution
W_{m}(\alpha,I_{m})

[
2
, Chapter 7]
and the multivariate beta distribution
[
2
, p. 377]
. Similarly, a function
f:\mathcal{P}_{m}\to\R
is said to be
orthogonally invariant
if
f(X)=f(kXk^{\prime})
for all
k\in O(m)
and
X\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
.

If the random matrix
X=gg^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
is orthogonally invariant, where
g\in G
, then for any
k_{1},k_{2}\in O(m)
,

(k_{1}gk_{2})(k_{1}gk_{2})^{\prime}=k_{1}gk_{2}k_{2}^{\prime}g^{\prime}k_{1}^{\prime}=k_{1}gg^{\prime}k_{1}^{\prime}=k_{1}Xk_{1}^{\prime}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X.

Therefore, for any orthogonally invariant random matrix
X\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, we can identify
X
with a corresponding
g\in G
such that
g
is left- and right-invariant under
O(m)
.

Suppose that the random matrix
X\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
is orthogonally invariant and
\E(X)
is finite. Since
X\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}kXk^{\prime}
for all
k\in O(m)
then
\E(X)=\E(kXk^{\prime})=k\E(X)k^{\prime}
. It follows from Schur’s Lemma
[
29
, p. 315]
that
\E(X)=cI_{m}
for some constant
c>0
.

Given orthogonally invariant random matrices
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, the convolution product,
X_{1}\circ X_{2}
, is defined by convolving the corresponding random matrices
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
and then identifying the outcome in
\mathcal{P}_{m}

via
the natural map from
G/O(m)\to\mathcal{P}_{m}
. Concretely, if
X_{1}=g_{1}g_{1}^{\prime}
and
X_{2}=g_{2}g_{2}^{\prime}
for orthogonally bi-invariant random matrices
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
, then
X_{1}\circ X_{2}=(g_{1}g_{2})(g_{1}g_{2})^{\prime}=g_{1}g_{2}g_{2}^{\prime}g_{1}^{\prime}
.

To express the distribution of
X_{1}\circ X_{2}
in terms of a product of functions of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, we recall that by polar coordinates on matrix space,
g_{1}=kX_{1}^{1/2}
for some
k\in O(m)
. Hence, by the orthogonal invariance of the random matrix
g
, we have
X_{1}\circ X_{2}=g_{1}g_{2}g_{2}^{\prime}g_{1}^{\prime}=g_{1}X_{2}g_{1}^{\prime}=kX_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}(kX_{1}^{1/2})^{\prime}=kX_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}k^{\prime}
. Since
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are orthogonally invariant, then replacing
X_{j}
by
k^{\prime}X_{j}k
,
j=1,2
and using the identity
(kXk^{\prime})^{1/2}=kX^{1/2}k^{\prime}
for any positive definite matrix
X
, we obtain

kX_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}k^{\prime}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}kk^{\prime}X_{1}^{1/2}kk^{\prime}X_{2}kk^{\prime}X_{1}^{1/2}kk^{\prime}=X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}.

Consequently,

X_{1}\circ X_{2}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}.

(2.1)

Although the binary operation
(A,B)\mapsto A^{1/2}BA^{1/2}
,
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, is not associative, the following result shows that associativity holds in distribution for mutually independent, orthogonally invariant random matrices.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that the random matrices
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
are mutually independent and orthogonally invariant. Then
(X_{1}\circ X_{2})\circ X_{3}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{1}\circ(X_{2}\circ X_{3})
.

Proof.
By (
2.1
),

\displaystyle X_{1}\circ(X_{2}\circ X_{3})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{1}^{1/2}(X_{2}\circ X_{3})X_{1}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}^{1/2}X_{3}X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}^{1/2}=\big(X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}^{1/2}\big)^{\prime}X_{3}\big(X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}^{1/2}\big).

Since
X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}=(X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}^{1/2})^{\prime}(X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}^{1/2})
then, by polar coordinates on matrix space, there exist
k\in O(m)
such that
X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}^{1/2}=k(X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2})^{1/2}
. Then,

\displaystyle X_{1}\circ(X_{2}\circ X_{3})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\big(X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}k^{\prime}X_{3}k\big(X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\big(X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}X_{3}\big(X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\big(X_{1}\circ X_{2}\big)^{1/2}X_{3}\big(X_{1}\circ X_{2}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}(X_{1}\circ X_{2})\circ X_{3}.

The proof now is complete.

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that the random matrices
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
are independent and orthogonally invariant. Then
X_{1}\circ X_{2}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{2}\circ X_{1}
.

More generally, if the random matrices
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
are mutually independent and orthogonally invariant then
X_{1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{n}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{\tau(1)}\circ\cdots\circ X_{\tau(n)}
for all permutations
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the symmetric group on
n
symbols.

Proof.
Since
X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}
and
X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}
have the same spectrum then there exists
k\in O(m)
such that
X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}=kX_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}k^{\prime}
; therefore,

X_{1}\circ X_{2}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{1}^{1/2}X_{2}X_{1}^{1/2}=kX_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}k^{\prime}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}k(X_{2}\circ X_{1})k^{\prime}.

Since the distributions of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are orthogonally invariant then it follows that

\displaystyle k(X_{2}\circ X_{1})k^{\prime}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}k(k^{\prime}X_{2}k\circ k^{\prime}X_{1}k)k^{\prime}

\displaystyle=k(k^{\prime}X_{2}k)^{1/2}k^{\prime}X_{1}k(k^{\prime}X_{2}k)^{1/2}k^{\prime}

\displaystyle=kk^{\prime}X_{2}^{1/2}kk^{\prime}X_{1}kk^{\prime}X_{2}^{1/2}kk^{\prime}

\displaystyle=X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{2}\circ X_{1}.

Therefore,
X_{1}\circ X_{2}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{2}\circ X_{1}
.

Finally, the proof that the distribution of
X_{1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{n}
is invariant under permutation of the
X_{i}
is obtained by using the property that each permutation can be expressed as a product of transpositions together with the previously-proved commutativity and associativity properties.

∎

For
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, denote by
\lambda_{1}(A),\ldots,\lambda_{m}(A)
the eigenvalues of
A
. The
Riemannian metric
on
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is defined as

d_{R}(A,B)=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
. The function
d_{R}
is a genuine metric since, for all
A,B,C\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
:
d_{R}(A,B)\geq 0
,
d_{R}(A,B)=d_{R}(B,A)
, and

d_{R}(A,C)\leq d_{R}(A,B)+d_{R}(B,C).

(2.2)

The quantity
d_{R}(A,B)
is the distance along the shortest geodesic path, on the cone, starting at
A
and ending at
B

[
17
,
25
,
28
]
. Since the matrices
A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}
and
A^{-1}B
have the same spectrum, then we also have an alternative formula,

d_{R}(A,B)=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}(A^{-1}B)\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

(2.3)

from which it follows that
d_{R}
is orthogonally bi-invariant:
d_{R}(kAk^{\prime},kBk^{\prime})=d_{R}(A,B)
for all
k\in O(m)
and
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
A,B,X\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
be orthogonally invariant random matrices. Then

d_{R}(X\circ A,X\circ B)\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}d_{R}(A,B)\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}d_{R}(A\circ X,B\circ X).

Proof.
By (
2.1
), there exist
k_{1},k_{2}\in O(m)
such that
X\circ A=k_{1}X^{1/2}AX^{1/2}k_{1}^{\prime}
and
X\circ B=k_{2}X^{1/2}BX^{1/2}k_{2}^{\prime}
. Therefore

\displaystyle d_{R}(X\circ A,X\circ B)

\displaystyle=d_{R}(k_{1}X^{1/2}AX^{1/2}k_{1}^{\prime},k_{2}X^{1/2}BX^{1/2}k_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d_{R}(kX^{1/2}AX^{1/2}k^{\prime},X^{1/2}BX^{1/2}).

where
k=k_{2}^{\prime}k_{1}
. By polar coordinates on matrix space, there exists
k_{3}\in O(m)
such that
kX^{1/2}=X^{1/2}k_{3}
; hence
X^{1/2}k^{\prime}=k_{3}^{\prime}X^{1/2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{R}(kX^{1/2}AX^{1/2}k^{\prime},X^{1/2}BX^{1/2})

\displaystyle=d_{R}(X^{1/2}k_{3}Ak_{3}^{\prime}X^{1/2},X^{1/2}BX^{1/2})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}d_{R}(X^{1/2}AX^{1/2},X^{1/2}BX^{1/2})

\displaystyle=d_{R}(A,B),

where the equality in distribution follows by the orthogonal invariance of
A
and the latter equality follows from (
2.3
).

Again by (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle d_{R}(A\circ X,B\circ X)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}d_{R}(A^{1/2}XA^{1/2},B^{1/2}XB^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}\big((A^{1/2}XA^{1/2})^{-1}B^{1/2}XB^{1/2}\big)\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Since
A^{1/2}XA^{1/2}
and
X^{1/2}AX^{1/2}
have the same spectrum then there exists
k_{1}\in O(m)
such that
A^{1/2}XA^{1/2}=k_{1}X^{1/2}AX^{1/2}k^{\prime}_{1}
; and similarly, there exists
k_{2}\in O(m)
such that
B^{1/2}XB^{1/2}=k_{2}X^{1/2}BX^{1/2}k^{\prime}_{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{R}(A\circ X,B\circ X)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}\big((k_{1}X^{1/2}AX^{1/2}k^{\prime}_{1})^{-1}k_{2}X^{1/2}BX^{1/2}k^{\prime}_{2}\big)\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}(k_{1}X^{-1/2}A^{-1}X^{-1/2}k^{\prime}_{1}k_{2}X^{1/2}BX^{1/2}k^{\prime}_{2})\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}(kX^{-1/2}A^{-1}X^{-1/2}k^{\prime}X^{1/2}BX^{1/2})\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

where
k=k^{\prime}_{2}k_{1}
. By an earlier argument, there exists
k_{3}\in O(m)
such that
kX^{-1/2}=X^{-1/2}k_{3}
, and hence
X^{-1/2}k^{\prime}=k_{3}^{\prime}X^{-1/2}
. Therefore

\displaystyle d_{R}(A\circ X,B\circ X)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}(X^{-1/2}k_{3}A^{-1}k_{3}^{\prime}X^{-1/2}X^{1/2}BX^{1/2})\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}(k_{3}A^{-1}k_{3}^{\prime}B)\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\log\lambda_{j}(A^{-1}B)\big]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=d_{R}(A,B),

where the equality in distribution follows from the orthogonal invariance of
A
.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

Many metrics on the cone
\mathcal{P}_{m}
have been studied in the literature, cf.,
[
24
,
26
]
, and it is possible to extend the results in this section to some of those metrics. We mention, in particular, the
Thompson metric
,

\displaystyle d_{T}(A,B)

\displaystyle:=\log\max\{\lambda_{\max}(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}),\lambda_{\max}(B^{-1/2}AB^{-1/2})\}

\displaystyle=\log\max\{\lambda_{\max}(A^{-1}B),1/\lambda_{\min}(A^{-1}B)\},

A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, where
\lambda_{\max}(A)
is the largest eigenvalue of
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}

[
17
]
. It can be shown that Proposition
2.3
holds for
d_{T}
, also that

d_{T}(A,B)\leq d_{R}(A,B)\leq\sqrt{m}\,d_{T}(A,B),

so that each probabilistic inequality obtained in Section
3
for
d_{R}
is equivalent to a corresponding inequality for
d_{T}
.

3
Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}_{m}}

Let
\{X_{n},n\in\N\}
be a sequence of mutually independent, and orthogonally invariant, random matrices in
\mathcal{P}_{m}
. Define the partial products,
S_{j}=X_{1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{j}
,
j=1,2,3,\ldots
; equivalently,

S_{1}=X_{1},\qquad S_{j+1}=S_{j}\circ X_{j+1}\equiv S_{j}^{1/2}X_{j+1}S_{j}^{1/2},\quad j\geq 1.

(3.1)

As we noted before, the binary relation
A\circ B
is not commutative; however, by Lemma
2.2
,
X_{1}\circ X_{2}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{2}\circ X_{1}
because the random matrices
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent and orthogonally invariant. Therefore, we may apply the results derived by Khare and Rajaratnam
[
19
]
(cf.,
[
20
]
) for commutative semigroups.

We now apply
[
19
, Theorem A]
with
{{\hskip 1.0pt}\mathscr{G}}
as the collection of orthogonally invariant random matrices on
\mathcal{P}_{m}
,
{{\hskip 1.0pt}\mathscr{G}}
being endowed with the binary operation given in (
2.1
), and
d_{{\hskip 1.0pt}\mathscr{G}}
being the Riemannian metric
d_{R}
. For
n=1,2,3,\ldots
define

M_{n}=\max\{d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1}),\ldots,d_{R}(I_{m},X_{n})\}

(3.2)

and

U_{n}=\max\{d_{R}(I_{m},S_{1}),\ldots,d_{R}(I_{m},S_{n})\}

(3.3)

and let
\delta_{ij}
denote Kronecker’s delta. We also use the notation
\chi(\mathscr{S})
to equal
1
if a statement
\mathscr{S}
is valid and
0
if it is not.

Theorem 3.1
.

(
[
19
, Theorem A]
)

Let
\{X_{n},n\in\N\}
be independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.), orthogonally invariant random matrices in
\mathcal{P}_{m}
. For
t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{l}\in[0,\infty)
and integers
l,n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}\in\N
, let
n_{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{l}
, and define

\mathcal{I}_{0}=\Big\{1\leq i\leq l:[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t_{i})]^{n_{i}-\delta_{i1}}\leq\frac{1}{n_{i}!}\Big\}.

(3.4)

If
n_{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}\leq n+1
then

\mathbb{P}\Big(U_{n}>(n_{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}-1)t_{0}+(2n_{1}-1)t_{1}+2\sum_{j=2}^{l}n_{j}t_{j}\Big)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t_{0})+[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t_{1})]^{\chi(1\notin\mathcal{I}_{0})}\cdot\prod_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{0}}[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t_{j})]^{n_{j}}\cdot\prod_{j\notin\mathcal{I}_{0}}\frac{1}{n_{j}!}\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t_{j})}{\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t_{j})}\right)^{n_{j}}.

(3.5)

Further, let
Y_{(1)}<\cdots<Y_{(n)}
be the order statistics of
d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1}),\ldots,d_{R}(I_{m},X_{n})
. Then the inequality (
3.5
) can be strengthened by replacing
\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t_{0})
by

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{j=n-n_{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}+2}^{n}Y_{(j)}>(n_{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}-1)t_{0}\bigg).

Remark 3.2
.

Note that (
3.5
) can also be written as

\mathbb{P}\Big(U_{n}>(n_{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.8}{{\hskip 1.0pt}$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}-1)t_{0}+(2n_{1}-1)t_{1}+2\sum_{j=2}^{l}n_{j}t_{j}\Big)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t_{0})+[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t_{1})]^{\chi(1\notin\mathcal{I}_{0})}\cdot\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{l}[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t_{j})]^{n_{j}}}{\prod_{j\notin\mathcal{I}_{0}}n_{j}!\,[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t_{j})]^{n_{j}}}.

(3.6)

Example 3.3
.

Similar to remarks in
[
19
, p. 4104]
, if in Theorem
3.1
we set
l=2
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=1
, and
t_{1}=t_{2}=t
then by (
3.4
),

\mathcal{I}_{0}=\big\{1\leq i\leq 2:[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)]^{1-\delta_{i1}}\leq 1\big\}=\{1,2\}
, and (
3.5
) reduces to the inequality

\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t_{0}+3t)\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t_{0})+[\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)]^{2},

which is analogous to classical Hoffmann-Jørgensen inequalities
[
19
, p. 4101]
.

We prove in Appendix
A
that if
\E(\|S_{n}\|_{\textrm{sp}})<\infty
for all
n\geq 1
then the sequence
\{S_{n},n\geq 1\}
is a martingale and the sequences
\{M_{n},n\geq 1\}
and
\{U_{n},n\geq 1\}
are submartingales. However, these martingale properties lead only to Markov’s inequalities for
\{M_{n},n\geq 1\}
and
\{U_{n},n\geq 1\}
and in any case a martingale approach cannot lead to explicit results for specific choices of the distribution of
\{X_{n},n\geq 1\}
. Therefore we develop in Section
4
an approach that produces detailed inequalities for
\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t)
and
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)
in the Wishart case.

In the following result we adopt the convention that an empty product of matrices is the identity matrix.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\{X_{n},n\in\N\}
be a sequence of i.i.d. orthogonally invariant random matrices in
\mathcal{P}_{m}
. Then for all
n\geq 1
,

S_{n+1}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{n+1}\circ S_{n}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{n+1}^{1/2}X_{n}^{1/2}\cdots X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}\cdots X_{n}^{1/2}X_{n+1}^{1/2}.

(3.7)

Proof.
First, it follows from Lemma
2.2
and induction on
n
that
S_{n}
is orthogonally invariant.

By Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, the composition of independent, orthogonally invariant random matrices is commutative and associative
in distribution
. Since

\displaystyle S_{n+1}

\displaystyle=S_{n}\circ X_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(S_{n-1}\circ X_{n})\circ X_{n+1}

\displaystyle=((S_{n-2}\circ X_{n-1})\circ X_{n})\circ X_{n+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle=((((X_{1}\circ X_{2})\circ X_{3})\circ\cdots\circ X_{n-1})\circ X_{n})\circ X_{n+1},

then it follows that

S_{n+1}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{1}\circ X_{2}\circ\cdots\circ X_{n+1}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{n+1}\circ X_{n}\circ\cdots\circ X_{1}
. Therefore,

S_{n+1}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{n+1}\circ(X_{n}\circ\cdots\circ X_{1})\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{n+1}\circ S_{n}
.
This proves the first equality (in distribution) in (
3.7
).

To prove the second equality in distribution in (
3.7
), we will use induction on
n
. Since
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are i.i.d. and
S_{1}=X_{1}
then

S_{2}=S_{1}\circ X_{2}=X_{1}\circ X_{2}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{2}\circ X_{1}=X_{2}\circ S_{1}=X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}.

This establishes the second part of (
3.7
) for
n=1
.

For the induction step, suppose that (
3.7
) holds for
n=j-1
, where
j\geq 2
. By (
3.1
) and the first equality (in distribution) in (
3.7
),

\displaystyle S_{j+1}

\displaystyle=S_{j}\circ X_{j+1}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{j+1}\circ S_{j}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{j+1}\circ(X_{j}^{1/2}X_{j-1}^{1/2}\cdots X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}\cdots X_{j-1}^{1/2}X_{j}^{1/2})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}X_{j+1}^{1/2}X_{j}^{1/2}X_{j-1}^{1/2}\cdots X_{2}^{1/2}X_{1}X_{2}^{1/2}\cdots X_{j-1}^{1/2}X_{j}^{1/2}X_{j+1}^{1/2}.

Therefore (
3.7
) holds for
n=j
. This completes the proof by induction.

∎

4
The random walk with Wishart matrices

In order for the inequalities (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) to be applied in practice, it will be necessary to obtain upper bounds for the terms
P(M_{n}>t)
,
P(U_{n}\leq t)
, and
P(U_{n}>t)
appearing on the right-hand sides of those inequalities. In this section we obtain, for the case in which the random matrices
\{X_{n},n\in\N\}
are Wishart-distributed, upper bounds on
P(M_{n}>t)
,
P(U_{n}\leq t)
, and
P(U_{n}>t)
.

For
a>\tfrac{1}{2}(m-1)
, the multivariate gamma function is defined as

\Gamma_{m}(a)=\pi^{m(m-1)/4}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Gamma(a-\tfrac{1}{2}(j-1)).

For
x=(x_{ij})\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, let
\,\textrm{d}x=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\,\textrm{d}x_{ij}
denote Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{m}
. A random matrix
X\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
is said to have the
Wishart distribution
, denoted
X\sim W_{m}(a,I_{m})
, if the probability density function of
X
relative to Lebesgue measure on the cone is

w(x)=\frac{1}{2^{ma}\,\Gamma_{m}(a)}(\det x)^{a-\frac{1}{2}(m+1)}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr x),\qquad x\in\mathcal{P}_{m}.

(4.1)

Throughout this section, we suppose that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is a random sample from the Wishart distribution
W_{m}(a,I_{m})
. Note that once we have obtained an upper bound,
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)\leq b
with
b<1
, then it follows that

\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)}=\frac{1}{1-\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)}\leq\frac{1}{1-b};

so we only need upper bounds for
\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t)
,
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)
, and
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)
.

4.1
A bound on the distribution function of
\boldsymbol{M_{n}}

Define the normalizing constant,

c_{m}=\frac{\pi^{{m^{2}}/2}}{2^{ma}\,m!\,\Gamma_{m}(a)\,\Gamma_{m}(m/2)}.

(4.2)

For
u\leq v
and
i,j=1,\ldots,m
, define

\displaystyle F_{a+j}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{u}^{v}t^{a+j-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)}\,\exp(-t/2)\,\textrm{d}t,

\displaystyle F_{a+i,a+j}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{u}^{v}t^{a+j-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)}\,\exp(-t/2)\,F_{a+i}(u,t)\,\textrm{d}t,

and

r_{ij}(u,v)=F_{a+i-1,a+j-1}(u,v)-F_{a+j-1,a+i-1}(u,v).

Define the
m\times m
matrix
R=\big(r_{ij}(u,v)\big)_{i,j=1,\ldots,m}
; also, for
l=1,\ldots,m
, define the
(m-1)\times(m-1)
principal submatrices

R_{l}=\big(r_{ij}(u,v)\big)_{i,j=1,\ldots,l-1,l+1,\ldots,m},

and the corresponding Pfaffians

H(u,v)=[\det(R)]^{1/2}\ \ \hbox{and}\ \ H_{l}(u,v)=[\det(R_{l})]^{1/2}.

We now have the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t)\leq 1-\big[m!\,c_{m}\,\rho_{m}\big(\exp(-m^{-1/2}t),\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\big)\big]^{n},

(4.3)

where

\rho_{m}(u,v)=\begin{cases}H(u,v),&\hbox{if }m\hbox{ is even}\\
\operatornamewithlimits{\sum}\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{j}\,F_{a+j}(u,v)\,H_{j+1}(u,v),&\hbox{if }m\hbox{ is odd}\end{cases}.

(4.4)

Proof.
Since
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. then

\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq t)=\mathbb{P}\big(\max\big\{d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1}),\ldots,d_{R}(I_{m},X_{n})\big\}\leq t\big)=\big[\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1})\leq t\big)\big]^{n}.

By (
2.3
),

\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1})\leq t\big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}[\log\lambda_{j}(X_{1})]^{2}\leq t^{2}\Big),

and since

\sum_{j=1}^{m}[\log\lambda_{j}(X_{1})]^{2}\leq m\cdot\max\{[\log\lambda_{1}(X_{1})]^{2},\ldots,[\log\lambda_{m}(X_{1})]^{2}\}

then it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1})\leq t\big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\big(m\cdot\max\big\{[\log\lambda_{1}(X_{1})]^{2},\ldots,[\log\lambda_{m}(X_{1})]^{2}\big\}\leq t^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}\{[\log\lambda_{j}(X_{1})]^{2}\leq m^{-1}t^{2}\}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}\{-m^{-1/2}t\leq\log\lambda_{j}(X_{1})\leq m^{-1/2}t\}\bigg).

(4.5)

Let
\lambda_{\min}(X_{1})
and
\lambda_{\max}(X_{1})
denote the smallest and largest eigenvalues, respectively, of
X_{1}
. Solving the inequalities in (
4.1
) for
\lambda_{j}(X_{1})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1})\leq t)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcap_{j=1}^{m}\{\exp(-m^{-1/2}t)\leq\lambda_{j}(X_{1})\leq\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\exp(-m^{-1/2}t)\leq\lambda_{\min}(X_{1})<\lambda_{\max}(X_{1})\leq\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\big),

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M_{n}>t)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\leq t)

\displaystyle=1-\big[\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1})\leq t\big)\big]^{n}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\big[\mathbb{P}\big(\exp(-m^{-1/2}t)\leq\lambda_{\min}(X_{1})<\lambda_{\max}(X_{1})\leq\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\big)\big]^{n}.

(4.6)

From a result of Krishnaiah and Chang
[
23
, Theorem 3.2]
for the joint distribution function of
\{\lambda_{\min}(X_{1}),\lambda_{\max}(X_{1})\}
, we obtain the explicit, computable expression,

\mathbb{P}\big(u\leq\lambda_{\min}(X_{1})<\lambda_{\max}(X_{1})\leq v\big)=m!\,c_{m}\,\rho_{m}(u,v),

where
c_{m}
and
\rho_{m}(u,v)
are defined in (
4.2
) and (
4.4
), respectively. Applying this expression to (
4.1
), we obtain (
4.3
).

∎

4.2
Bounds on the distribution function of
\boldsymbol{U_{n}}

It is more difficult to derive bounds for the cumulative distribution function of
U_{n}
than for
M_{n}
, the reason being that
M_{n}
is defined in terms of the i.i.d. random matrices
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
whereas
U_{n}
is defined in terms of the dependent random matrices
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
.

For
0<\theta<1
, define

I_{m}(\theta)=(1-\theta)^{-m[(2a-m-1)(1-\theta)+m]}\prod_{j=1}^{m}j!\,\Gamma\big((2a-m-1)(1-\theta)+j\big).

(4.7)

Also let

\mathcal{D}=\{(v,\theta):0\leq v<\tfrac{1}{2}((2a-m-1)\theta+1),\,0<\theta<1\},

(4.8)

and define

K(v,\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{(2a-m-1)\theta}\exp(2v|\log\lambda|-\theta\lambda)\,\textrm{d}\lambda,

(4.9)

and

G(v,\theta)=2\log c_{m}+\log I_{m}(\theta)+m\log K(v,\theta),

(4.10)

(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}
, where
c_{m}
is given in (
4.2
). We now establish the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)\leq\ n\exp\Big[\inf_{(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}}\big(-vt+\tfrac{1}{2}nG(v,\theta)\big)\Big].

(4.11)

Proof.
By the exponential Markov (or Chernoff) inequality,

\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)=\mathbb{P}\big(\exp(vU_{n})>\exp(vt)\big)\leq\exp(-vt)\E\big(\exp(vU_{n})\big)

(4.12)

for
t,v>0
. To obtain a bound for
\E\big(\exp(vU_{n})\big)
we apply an approach from the area of concentration inequalities,
[
7
, p. 375]
,
[
9
]
. For
j=1,\ldots,n
, consider
\xi_{j}(v)=\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(I_{m},S_{j})\big)
,
v\geq 0
, the moment-generating function of
d_{R}(I_{m},S_{j})
. For
j\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\xi_{j}(v)

\displaystyle=\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(I_{m},S_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(I_{m},X_{j}^{1/2}S_{j-1}X_{j}^{1/2})\big)=\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(X_{j}^{-1},S_{j-1})\big).

By the triangle inequality (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle d_{R}(X_{j}^{-1},S_{j-1})

\displaystyle\leq d_{R}(X_{j}^{-1},I_{m})+d_{R}(I_{m},S_{j-1})

\displaystyle=d_{R}(I_{m},X_{j})+d_{R}(I_{m},S_{j-1}).

Exponentiating the latter inequality, taking expectations, applying the independence of
X_{j}
and
S_{j-1}
, and the i.i.d. property of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\xi_{j}(v)

\displaystyle\leq\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(I_{m},X_{j})\big)\cdot\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(I_{m},S_{j-1})\big)

\displaystyle=\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(I_{m},X_{1})\big)\cdot\E\exp\big(v\>d_{R}(I_{m},S_{j-1})\big)

\displaystyle=[\xi_{1}(v)]\xi_{j-1}(v).

Iterating this inequality for
j=2,\ldots,n
we obtain

\xi_{j}(v)\leq[\xi_{1}(v)]^{j}

(4.13)

for all
j\geq 1
. Therefore

\displaystyle\E\big(\exp(vU_{n})\big)

\displaystyle=\E\big[\exp\big(v\cdot\max\{d_{R}(I_{m},S_{1}),\ldots,d_{R}(I_{m},S_{n})\}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\E\big[\max\big\{\exp\big(vd_{R}(I_{m},S_{1})\big),\ldots,\exp\big(vd_{R}(I_{m},S_{n})\big)\big\}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\E\big[\exp\big(vd_{R}(I_{m},S_{j})\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}(v).

(4.14)

Since
U_{j}\geq 0
then
\xi_{j}(v)\geq 1
for all
v\geq 0
; hence
\xi_{j}(v)\leq[\xi_{1}(v)]^{j}\leq[\xi_{1}(v)]^{n}
for all
v\geq 0
and
j=1,\ldots,n
. By (
4.2
),
\E\big(\exp(vU_{n})\big)\leq n[\xi_{1}(v)]^{n}
; also, by (
4.12
),

\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)\leq n\exp(-vt)[\xi_{1}(v)]^{n},

(4.15)

a bound which is valid for all
v>0
such that
\xi_{1}(v)
exists. Let
\Dom(\xi_{1})=\{v\geq 0:\xi_{1}(v)<\infty\}
; then we obtain, by minimization with respect to
v
, the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)

\displaystyle\leq n\inf_{v\in\Dom(\xi_{1})}\exp(-vt)[\xi_{1}(v)]^{n}

(4.16)

\displaystyle\equiv n\inf_{v\in\Dom(\xi_{1})}\exp\big(-vt+n\log\xi_{1}(v)\big).

We shall show later that
[0,a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m-1))\subseteq\Dom(\xi_{1})
; since we have assumed that
a>\tfrac{1}{2}(m-1)
then it follows that
\Dom(\xi_{1})
contains a non-empty open set.

Since each
X\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
is symmetric then, by diagonalization,
X=kDk^{\prime}
where
k\in O(m)
and
D
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of
X
. It follows that
f(X)=f(D)
for any orthogonally invariant function
f:\mathcal{P}_{m}\to\C
. Denote by
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
the eigenvalues of
x\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
and denote by
\R_{+}
the positive real line; then by Anderson
[
2
, p. 538, Theorem 13.3.1]
, for any integrable, orthogonally invariant function
f:\mathcal{P}_{m}(\R)\to\C
,

\int_{\mathcal{P}_{m}}f(x)\,\textrm{d}x=\frac{\pi^{m^{2}/2}}{m!\,\Gamma_{m}(m/2)}\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}f\big({\rm{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})\big)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\prod_{j=1}^{m}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}.

(4.17)

Recalling that

\xi_{1}(v)=\E d_{R}(I_{m},X)=\E\exp\bigg(v\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}[\log\lambda_{j}(X)]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\bigg),

(4.18)

then, since
X\sim W_{m}(a,I_{m})
, it follows from (
4.1
), (
4.2
), (
4.17
), and (
4.18
) that

\displaystyle\xi_{1}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{ma}\Gamma_{m}(a)}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{m}}\exp\bigg[v\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}[\log\lambda_{j}(x)]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\bigg](\det x)^{a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr x)\,\textrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c_{m}\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\exp\bigg[v\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\log\lambda_{j})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\bigg]\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)}e^{-\lambda_{j}/2}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}.

(4.19)

Also, it is straightforward to verify that

\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\log\lambda_{j})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\log\lambda_{j}|,

(4.20)

and by substituting this inequality into (
4.2
) we obtain

\xi_{1}(v)\leq c_{m}\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\exp\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{m}(v|\log\lambda_{j}|-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{j})\Big]\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}.

(4.21)

To verify that
\Dom(\xi_{1})\neq\emptyset
, we now obtain
via
(
4.21
) an open interval that is contained in
\Dom(\xi_{1})
.

For each
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
, the group of permutations on
m
symbols, denote by
\mathop{\rm{sgn}}(\tau)
the signature of
\tau
. Recall the well-known Vandermonde determinant formula,

(-1)^{m(m-1)/2}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})=\det(\lambda_{i}^{j-1})=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\mathop{\rm{sgn}}(\tau)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{\tau(j)}^{j-1};

consequently, for
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}>0
,

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\leq\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{\tau(j)}^{j-1}.

(4.22)

Substituting the bound (
4.22
) into (
4.21
), and using the symmetry of the resulting integrand, we find that the integral of each term on the right-hand side of (
4.22
) is the same. Therefore
\xi_{1}(v)<\infty
if

\displaystyle\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\exp\Big(v\sum_{j=1}^{m}

\displaystyle|\log\lambda_{j}|\Big)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)+j-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{j}}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(v|\log\lambda_{j}|-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{j})\lambda_{j}^{(2a-m+2j-3)/2}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}<\infty.

The
j
th integral in this product can be written as a sum of an integral over the interval
(0,1]
and a second integral over
(1,\infty)
, each of which is an incomplete gamma function. Applying well-known convergence properties of the incomplete gamma function we find that the
j
th integral converges if and only if
0\leq v<(2a-m+2j-1)/2
; and from the case in which
j=1
we deduce that
[0,a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m-1))\subseteq\Dom(\xi_{1})
.

For
0<\theta<1
, write

\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)\big)\theta}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\theta\lambda_{j}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{\big(a-\tfrac{1}{2}(m+1)\big)(1-\theta)}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\theta)\lambda_{j}},

and substitute the right-hand side into the integrand in (
4.2
). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle[\xi_{1}(v)]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{m}^{2}\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\exp\bigg[2v\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\log\lambda_{j})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\bigg]\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(2a-m-1)\theta}e^{-\theta\lambda_{j}}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(2a-m-1)(1-\theta)}e^{-(1-\theta)\lambda_{j}}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}.

By Selberg’s integral
[
3
, p. 406]
, the latter integral can be evaluated explicitly:

\displaystyle I_{m}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(2a-m-1)(1-\theta)}e^{-(1-\theta)\lambda_{j}}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}

(4.24)

\displaystyle=(1-\theta)^{-m[(2a-m-1)(1-\theta)+m]}\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(2a-m-1)(1-\theta)}e^{-\lambda_{j}}\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta)^{-m[(2a-m-1)(1-\theta)+m]}\prod_{j=1}^{m}j!\,\Gamma\big((2a-m-1)(1-\theta)+j\big),

as stated in (
4.7
). Applying the log-inequality (
4.20
), we obtain

\displaystyle[\xi_{1}(v)]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{m}^{2}I_{m}(\theta)\int_{\R_{+}^{m}}\exp\bigg[2v\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\log\lambda_{j}|\bigg]\prod_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{(2a-m-1)\theta}\exp(-\theta\lambda_{j})\,\textrm{d}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=c_{m}^{2}I_{m}(\theta)\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(2v|\log\lambda|-\theta\lambda)\lambda^{(2a-m-1)\theta}\,\textrm{d}\lambda\bigg)^{m}.

(4.25)

By the convergence properties of the incomplete gamma function, we deduce that the integral in (
4.2
) converges if and only if
(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}
, the set defined in (
4.8
). Therefore, by (
4.9
) and (
4.2
),

\xi_{1}(v)\leq c_{m}[I_{m}(\theta)]^{1/2}\big(K(v,\theta)\big)^{m/2},

(4.26)

(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}
, and by substituting (
4.26
) into (
4.16
) we obtain

\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)\leq n\inf_{(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}}\exp\big[-vt+n\log c_{m}+\tfrac{1}{2}n\log I_{m}(\theta)+\tfrac{1}{2}mn\log K(v,\theta)\big].

Interchanging the infimum and the exponential function, and using the definition of
G(v,\theta)
from (
4.10
), we obtain (
4.11
).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

(i) Proposition
4.2
implies that, for large
t
,
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)
converges to zero at a rate that is at least
O(e^{-ct})
, where
c>0
.

(ii) A bound on
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)
can also be obtained by using (
4.13
) and the resulting 3inequality,

\displaystyle\E\big(\exp(vU_{n})\big)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}(v)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\xi_{1}(v)]^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}[c_{m}[I_{m}(\theta)]^{1/2}\big(K(v,\theta)\big)^{m/2}]^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(c_{m}[I_{m}(\theta)]^{1/2}(K(v,\theta))^{m/2}\big)^{n+1}-1}{c_{m}[I_{m}(\theta)]^{1/2}(K(v,\theta))^{m/2}-1}-1.

This bound is sharper than the one given in (
4.16
), so it can be expected to lead to sharper inequalities for
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}>t)
. However, the resulting numerical calculations will be more involved.

We now consider the issue of calculating the infimum in (
4.11
) for given values of
m
,
n
, and
a
. We will see that the objective function,
(v,\theta)\mapsto-vt+\tfrac{1}{2}mG(v,\theta)
,
(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}
, is particularly well-behaved as it is strictly convex in
\mathcal{D}
; consequently, the infimum occurs at a unique point in
\mathcal{D}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

(i) For
(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}
, the function
K(v,\theta)
, is strictly log-convex in
(v,\theta)
jointly, strictly log-convex in
v
for fixed
\theta
, and strictly log-convex in
\theta
for fixed
v
.

(ii) The function
I_{m}(\theta)
,
\theta\in(0,1)
, is strictly log-convex.

(iii) For
t\geq 0
, the objective function
(v,\theta)\mapsto-vt+\tfrac{1}{2}mG(v,\theta)
,
(v,\theta)\in\mathcal{D}
, is strictly convex.

Proof.
These properties follow readily from the fact that
K(v,\theta)
and
I_{m}(\theta)
can be viewed as modified Laplace transforms of positive measures. For instance, let us write
K(v,\theta)
in the form

K(v,\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\big(2v\phi_{1}(\lambda)-\theta\lambda+\phi_{2}(\lambda)\big)\,\textrm{d}\lambda,

where
\phi_{1}(\lambda)=|\log\lambda|
and
\phi_{2}(\lambda)=(2a-m-1)\theta\log\lambda
. By proceeding as in the theory of exponential families of distributions (Barndorff-Nielsen
[
6
, p. 103 ff.]
), the standard method of applying Hölder’s inequality leads to the conclusion that
K(v,\theta)
is log-convex in
(v,\theta)
jointly. Moreover, the strict log-convexity of
K(v,\theta)
follows from the fact that the function

\bigg|\begin{matrix}\phi_{1}(\lambda_{1})&\phi_{1}(\lambda_{2})\\
\phi_{2}(\lambda_{1})&\phi_{2}(\lambda_{2})\\
\end{matrix}\bigg|^{2},

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\R_{+}^{2}
, is strictly positive on an open subset of
\R_{+}^{2}
.

The remaining statements in (i) can be proved similarly, (ii) can be proved analogously using the integral representation given in (
4.24
), and (iii) follows from (i) and (ii).

∎

Once the strict convexity property of the objective function has been established, it is numerically straightforward to compute in (
4.11
) both the value of the infimum and its point of location. We remark that it is especially simple to compute numerical examples using, e.g., the
SageMath
software system
[
32
]
.

Next, we obtain an upper bound for the distribution function,
\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)
. Denoting by
\chi^{2}_{\alpha}
a random variable having the chi-squared distribution with degrees-of-freedom
\alpha
, we have the following result.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
be i.i.d.,
\chi^{2}_{(2a-m-1)m}
-distributed random variables. Then,

\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)\leq\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{P}\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{j}R_{i}\leq m^{j}\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\Big),

(4.27)

t>0
. In particular,

\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\chi^{2}_{(2a-m-1)m}\leq m\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\big).

(4.28)

Proof.
By Bonferroni’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(U_{n}\leq t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\max\{d_{R}(I,S_{1}),\ldots,d_{R}(I,S_{n})\}\leq t\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I,S_{1})\leq t,\ldots,d_{R}(I,S_{n})\leq t\big)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I,S_{j})\leq t\big).

(4.29)

For
j=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I,S_{j})\leq t\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big([d_{R}(I,S_{j})]^{2}\leq t^{2}\big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}[\log\lambda_{i}(S_{j})]^{2}\leq t^{2}\Big).

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}[\log\lambda_{i}(S_{j})]^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{m}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\lambda_{i}(S_{j})\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\Big(\log\prod_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(S_{j})\Big)^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\big[\log(\det S_{j})\big]^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I,S_{j})\leq t\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{1}{m}\big[\log(\det S_{j})\big]^{2}\leq t^{2}\Big)=\mathbb{P}\big((\det S_{j})\leq\exp(m^{1/2}t)\big).

By (
3.7
),
\det S_{j}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\prod_{i=1}^{j}(\det X_{i})
, and we proceed to obtain a lower bound on the distribution of this product.

For random variables
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, recall that
Q_{1}
is said to be
stochastically greater than

Q_{2}
, written
Q_{1}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\geq}Q_{2}
, if
\mathbb{P}(Q_{1}\leq t)\leq\mathbb{P}(Q_{2}\leq t)
for all
t\in\R
. It is well-known that if
Q_{ij}
,
i,j=1,2
are positive random variables such that
Q_{11}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\geq}Q_{21}
and
Q_{12}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\geq}Q_{22}
then
Q_{11}Q_{12}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\geq}Q_{21}Q_{22}
.

Since
X_{j}\sim W_{m}(a,I_{m})
then, by
[
13
, Theorem 4]
,

m(\det X_{j})^{1/m}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\geq}\chi^{2}_{(2a-m-1)m}.

Therefore, since
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
are i.i.d. and
\chi^{2}_{(2a-m-1)m}
-distributed then it follows that

m^{j}(\det S_{j})^{1/m}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\prod_{i=1}^{j}m(\det X_{i})^{1/m}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{\geq}\prod_{i=1}^{j}R_{i},

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(d_{R}(I,S_{j})\leq t\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\det S_{j}\leq\exp(m^{1/2}t)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(m^{j}(\det S_{j})^{1/m}\leq m^{j}\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{j}R_{i}\leq m^{j}\exp(m^{-1/2}t)\Big).

Applying (
4.2
), we now obtain (
4.27
). Also, by choosing
j=1
we obtain (
4.28
).

∎

We remark that much is known about the exact distribution of a product of independent chi-square random variables; cf.
[
2
,
13
]
. Such results can be used to obtain sharper, but more complicated, upper bounds on the distribution function of
U_{n}
.
