Improving ATLAS Hadronic Object Performance with ML/AI Algorithms
Benjamin Hodkinson, on behalf of the ATLAS Collaboration

Abstract
Hadronic object reconstruction is one of the most promising settings for cutting-edge machine learning and artificial intelligence algorithms at the LHC. In this contribution, selected highlights of ML/AI applications by ATLAS to particle and boosted-object identification, MET reconstruction and other tasks are presented.



1
Introduction

Hadronic objects are ubiquitous in the proton–proton collision events recorded by the ATLAS detector
[
1
]
at the LHC.
This includes hadronic jets, which are reconstructed from a large number of low-level calorimeter/track-based constituent objects, and missing transverse momentum (
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
), which involves every detector component and final-state object.
The complexity and abundance of jets and
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
in the ATLAS dataset makes their reconstruction a promising setting for machine learning (ML) applications.
This contribution introduces several recent developments in ATLAS which use ML to improve the performance of
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
reconstruction
[
2
]
, pion reconstruction
[
3
]
and jet tagging
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
These applications can broadly be separated into two categories:

1.

Regression of truth-level quantities from detector-level information.

2.

Classification of hadronic objects.

2
METNet: A combined
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
working point

ATLAS employs several working points for
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
reconstruction
[
7
]
, each of which is optimal for different event topologies and pile-up conditions.

METNet

[
2
]
is a neural network (NN) designed to pick and combine the reconstructed
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
for each working point into a single
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
estimate.
This is achieved by regressing particle-level (‘true’)
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
given the detector-level
p_{\text{T}}^{\text{miss}}
predictions for each working point and information characterising pile-up and event topology. The NN is trained on a mixture of
t\bar{t}
and di-boson Monte Carlo (MC) events.
The performance of two iterations of
METNet
is presented here: one trained using the Huber loss
[
8
]
function, and another including an additional Sinkhorn
[
9
]
contribution to the loss to reduce an observed negative bias, denoted METNet (Sk).

3
Pion reconstruction

The ATLAS detector has non-compensating calorimetry, so being able to distinguish charged and neutral pions allows the corresponding hadronic energy depositions to be restored to the correct scale.
Figure
3a
indicates the
\pi^{0}\ \mathrm{vs.}\ \pi^{\pm}
classification performance of several ML models trained in Reference
[
3
]
compared to a non-ML baseline classifier (labelled
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{EM}}_{\mathrm{clus}}
).
All methods outperform the baseline and the Graph Neural Network (GNN) shows the best performance overall.

Additionally, several ML models are trained to calibrate pion energy.
Given that pions are produced in abundance in nearly all hadronic showers, understanding and improving pion reconstruction is central to improving jet reconstruction.
The models in Reference
[
3
]
show significant improvement on current (non-ML) calibration baselines, particularly when combining tracking and calorimeter information.
Figure
3b
shows energy resolution for several ML methods along with the track resolution.
The resolution is quantified as one-half the interquantile range (IQR) divided by the median predicted energy, where the IQR represents the width of the response data (where response is the ratio of the predicted pion energy to particle-level pion energy) from 1
\sigma
to
-1\sigma
of the median.
This captures a measure of the spread of energy predictions.
The ML methods approximate the tracker energy resolution up to around
50\ \text{Ge\kern-1.00006ptV}
before the calorimeter energy resolution dominates, indicating that ML is providing the best of both tracking and calorimetry performance.

Boosted jet tagging

Large-radius jets from massive particles (such as
W
-bosons,
Z
-bosons and top quarks) can be distinguished from light quark/gluon-initiated jets using jet substructure information.
ATLAS employs a variety of taggers for this purpose, and improving their classification accuracy enhances the performance of both searches and precision measurements.
The latest taggers developed in ATLAS use jets reconstructed from Unified Flow Objects
[
11
]
which combine topocluster and tracking information to provide improved pile-up resilience and jet mass resolution.

Figure

This highlights the need to develop taggers in a realistic context.
As a result, the simulated data set used in this study has been made publicly available in Reference
[
13
