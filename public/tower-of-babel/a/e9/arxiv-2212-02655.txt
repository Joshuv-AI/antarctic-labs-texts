Triangular norms on bounded trellises
Lemnaouar Zedam, Bernard De Baets

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce the notion of a t-norm on bounded pseudo-ordered sets and in particular on bounded trellises (also known as weakly associative lattices), and provide some basic examples. The impact of abandoning transitivity is considerable: on a proper bounded trellis, the meet operation is not a t-norm, and there might actually exist no or even multiple maximal t-norms. We provide a first generic construction method that allows to extend a t-norm on an interior range of a given ∧ \wedge -semi-trellis to the entire ∧ \wedge -semi-trellis. Also, we discuss at length an instantiation of this method based on a particular interior range, namely a finite sub-trellis of the set of right-transitive elements of a given trellis. We pay specific attention to bounded pseudo-chains and modular trellises.



1
Introduction

The study of triangular norms (t-norms, for short) has a long history. These mathematical operations were introduced as early as the 1940s by Menger
[
32
]
when generalizing the triangle inequality from classical metric spaces to statistical metric spaces (nowadays called probabilistic metric spaces). In such spaces, distances
are no longer non-negative real numbers, but are described by distribution functions instead.
The axiomatic description of t-norms as used today is due to Schweizer and Sklar (see, e.g.,
[
40
,
41
]
):
a t-norm is an increasing, commutative and associative binary operation on the unit interval with
1
as neutral element. The key property here is associativity: it allows a t-norm to be extended to any number of arguments
in an unambiguous way. From an algebraic point of view, the unit interval
[0,1]
equipped with a t-norm can be seen as a totally ordered commutative monoidal structure. There is deep and extensive knowledge on t-norms, such as the
ordinal sum characterization of continuous t-norms by Ling
[
31
]
.

Soon after it was realized that t-norms can also serve the role of the logical connective ‘and’ in fuzzy set theory
[
3
,
16
]
, indispensable for defining the intersection of fuzzy sets, they witnessed a second boom. Researchers in the field of fuzzy set theory replaced the hitherto used standard minimum operation by a t-norm, and this in virtually any theoretical development (such as Zadeh’s extension principle
[
28
,
47
]
) or practical application (like in fuzzy optimization). Also mathematicians got involved, further expanding the knowledge on these operations, culminating in the seminal book
of Klement, Mesiar and Pap
[
30
]
. Undoubtedly, they also marked the inception of another flourishing subfield of fuzzy set theory — and data science at large — namely the theory of aggregation functions (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
7
,
23
]
). Of similar importance and sharing a long history are the binary operations called copulas introduced by Sklar
[
44
]
. A copula is a bivariate cumulative distribution function for which
the marginal probability distribution of each variable is uniform on the unit interval
[0,1]
. They are not associative, thus requiring appropriate definitions for higher dimensions. The latter problem disappears in the case of associative copulas. Interestingly, associative copulas are nothing else but 1-Lipschitz continuous t-norms
[
33
]
, explaining another wave of cross-fertilization between probability theory (studying copulas) and fuzzy set theory (studying t-norms).

Soon after Zadeh’s seminal paper on fuzzy set
[
46
]
, it was already realized by Goguen
[
25
]
that the lattice-theoretic setting (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
10
]
) is the most natural one for the development of fuzzy set theory. Notwithstanding the many achievements mentioned above, all situated in the comfortable setting of real analysis, it took until the 90s for the study of t-norms on more general structures to take off
[
13
,
14
]
, in particular on partially ordered sets (posets, for short) and lattices, thus being related to the study of partially ordered semigroups
[
22
]
. Not surprisingly, research in this direction has been a lot slower, initially being focused on construction methods
[
1
,
2
,
8
,
26
,
29
]
, and only recently more profound characterizations in terms of ordinal sums
[
17
,
34
,
35
]
.

Note that in a bounded lattice
(L,\leq,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
, the transitivity of the partial order relation
\leq
and/or the associativity of the meet operation
\wedge
and join operation
\vee
play an important role.

Characteristic of the partial order relation of a poset or lattice is indeed its transitivity property, by far the most fascinating relational property. Above all, transitivity is a convenient property, simplifying mathematical reasoning until it becomes an automatism. However, many theoretical and practical developments warrant us to look beyond transitivity. For instance, the study of (absence of) transitivity in the comparison of random variables
[
11
,
12
]
has proven to more than intriguing and resulted in the framework of cycle-transitivity. Indeed, absence of transitivity can manifest itself in two flavors:
the presence of cycles or preference loops (A better than B, B better than C, and C better than A) or simply incomparability (A better than B, B better than C, but A and C being incomparable). Although often inconvenient to mathematicians and computer scientists (see the interest in acyclic directed graphs, for instance), cycles have been shown to be of extreme importance in practical situations, e.g. cycles in species competition structures such as tournaments preventing extinction and supporting biodiversity
[
27
,
39
]
, usually catalogued under the Rock-Paper-Scissors metaphor. Also incomparibility is a well-known phenomenon, such as the intransitivity of indifference
[
18
]
.

Motivated by the interest in non-transitive relations as well as our unrelenting mathematical curiosity, in this paper we set out to introduce and study t-norms on lesser-known, albeit interesting mathematical structures, namely the class of pseudo-ordered sets (psosets, for short), and in particular the subclass of trellises
[
42
]
. The latter are also known as tournament lattices
[
19
]
, non-associative lattices
[
15
]
or weakly associative lattices
[
9
,
20
,
21
]
. However, we prefer the designated technical term ‘trellis’. Psosets generalize posets by replacing the partial order relation by a more general reflexive and antisymmetric relation, while trellises do the same compared to lattices, but preserve the existence of meets and joins,
i.e.
the existence of greatest lower bounds and smallest upper bounds of 2-element subsets
[
9
,
20
,
21
]
). However, the absence of transitivity of the pseudo-order relation is reflected in the absence of the associativity property of the meet and join operation of a trellis. Major contributions to the study of trellises have been made by Skala
[
42
,
43
]
, followed by several other scholars.
For instance, Gladstien
[
24
]
proved that trellises of finite length are complete if and only if every cycle has a smallest upper bound and a greatest lower bound. Later on, this characterization was generalized to psosets in terms of joins of cycles and pseudo-chains
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
.

Our goal is to unravel how abandoning the transitivity property affects the notion of a t-norm on bounded psosets, and in particular on bounded trellises. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In the preliminary Section
2
, we recall the basic notions on psosets and trellises needed in this paper. We identify some potentially interesting subsets of a trellis in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we extend the notion of a t-norm to the setting of bounded psosets and bounded trellises, and provide some examples. Our main contribution is to be found in Sections
5
and
6
, in which we present a first generic construction method based on a t-norm on an interior range of a given
\wedge
-semi-trellis. We illustrate the method for a particular
interior operator with as range an appropriate finite subset of the set of right-transitive elements of a given trellis.
Finally, we present some concluding remarks and future research lines in Section
7
.

2
Basic concepts

This section serves an introductory purpose. First, we recall some definitions and properties related to pseudo-ordered sets and trellises. Second, we present some specific elements of a trellis that will be needed throughout this paper.

2.1
Pseudo-ordered sets and trellises

In this subsection, we recall the notions of pseudo-ordered sets and trellises; more information can be found in
[
19
,
42
,
43
]
.
A
pseudo-order (relation)

\unlhd
on a set
X
is a binary relation on
X
that is reflexive (
i.e.
,
x\unlhd x
,
for any
x\in X
) and antisymmetric (
i.e.
,
x\unlhd y
and
y\unlhd x
implies
x=y
, for any
x,y\in X
). A set
X
equipped
with a pseudo-order
\unlhd
is called a
pseudo-ordered set
(
psoset
, for short) and is denoted by
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)
; a psoset that is not a poset is called a proper psoset.
For any two elements
a,b\in X
, if
a\unlhd b
and
a\neq b
, then we write
a\lhd b
; if
a\unlhd b
does not hold, then we also write
a\ntrianglelefteq b
. For any element
x\in X
, we introduce the following subsets of
X
:

\downarrow x=\{y\in X\mid y\unlhd x\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\uparrow x=\{y\in X\mid x\unlhd y\}\,.

For a given psoset
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)
and
x,y\in X
, we write
x\lesssim y
if there exists a finite sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of elements from
X
such that
x\unlhd x_{1}\unlhd\ldots\unlhd x_{n}\unlhd y
. Note that the relation
\lesssim
is a pre-order relation,
i.e.
, it is reflexive and transitive, but not necessarily antisymmetric.

Consider a subset
\mathcal{C}
of
X
and
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
. We write
x\lesssim_{\mathcal{C}}y
if there exists a finite sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of elements from
\mathcal{C}
such that
x\unlhd x_{1}\unlhd\ldots\unlhd x_{n}\unlhd y
. If for any
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
,
x\lesssim_{\mathcal{C}}y
or
y\lesssim_{\mathcal{C}}x
holds, then
\mathcal{C}
is called a
pseudo-chain
.
If for any
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
, both
x\lesssim_{\mathcal{C}}y
and
y\lesssim_{\mathcal{C}}x
hold,
then
\mathcal{C}
is called a
cycle
.
Note that every singleton
C=\{x\}
is a trivial pseudo-chain as well as a trivial cycle. Due to the antisymmetry of
\unlhd
, any non-trivial cycle contains at least three elements.

Similarly as for partially ordered sets (posets, for short), a finite pseudo-ordered set can be represented by
a
Hasse-type diagram
(or, simply,
Hasse diagram
) with the following difference: if
x
and
y
are not related, while in a poset set this would be implied by transitivity, then
x
and
y
are joined by a dashed edge. If
x\lesssim y
and
y\unlhd x
, then
x
and
y
are joined by a directed edge going from
y
to
x
.

The notions of
minimal/maximal
element,
smallest/greatest
element,
lower/upper bound
,

greatest lower bound
(or
infimum
),

smallest upper bound
(or
supremum
) for psosets are defined in the same way as the corresponding notions for posets. For a subset
A
of a psoset
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)
, the antisymmetry of the pseudo-order implies that if
A
has an infimum (resp. supremum), then it is unique, and is denoted by
\bigwedge A
(resp.
\bigvee A
). If
A=\{a,b\}
,
then we write
a\wedge b
(called

) instead of
\bigwedge\{a,b\}
and
a\vee b
(called
join
) instead of
\bigvee\{a,b\}
. A
bounded psoset
is a psoset that has a smallest element denoted by
0
and a greatest element denoted by
1
,
i.e.
,
0\unlhd x\unlhd 1
, for any
x\in X
. A bounded psoset
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)
is denoted by
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd,0,1)
.

Definition 2.1
.

[
24
]

A
\wedge
-semi-trellis (resp.
\vee
-semi-trellis) is a psoset
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)
such that
x\wedge y

(resp.
x\vee y
) exists for all
x,y\in X
. A
trellis
is a psoset that is both a
\wedge
-semi-trellis and a
\vee
-semi-trellis; it is denoted by
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
; a trellis that is not a lattice is called a proper trellis.

A trellis can also be characterized as an algebra
(X,\wedge,\vee)
, where
X
is a nonempty set and the binary operations
\wedge
and
\vee
satisfy the following properties,
for any
a,b,c\in X

[
42
]
:

(i)

a\vee b=b\vee a
and
a\wedge b=b\wedge a
(
commutativity
) ;

(ii)

a\vee(b\wedge a)=a=a\wedge(b\vee a)
(
absorption
) ;

(iii)

a\vee((a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c))=a=a\wedge((a\vee b)\wedge(a\vee c))
(
part-preservation
) .

Theorem 2.1
.

[
42
]

A set
X
with two commutative, absorptive, and part-preserving operations
\wedge
and
\vee
is a trellis if
the pseudo-order
\unlhd
is defined as follows:
a\unlhd b
if
a\wedge b=a
and/or
a\vee b=b
.
The operations are also idempotent (i.e.,
x\wedge x=x\vee x=x
, for any
x\in X
).

One observes that the difference between the notions of a lattice and a trellis is that the operations
\wedge
and
\vee
are not required to be associative in the case of a trellis.

A trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
is called
bounded
if it is bounded as a psoset. The notation
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
is used for a bounded trellis. Also, a trellis is called
complete
if every
subset has an infimum and a supremum.

Definition 2.2
.

[
24
]

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
be a trellis and
A\subseteq X
. Then

(i)

A
is called a
\wedge
-sub-trellis
of
\mathbb{T}
if
x\wedge y\in A
, for any
x,y\in A
;

(ii)

A
is called a
\vee
-sub-trellis
of
\mathbb{T}
if
x\vee y\in A
, for any
x,y\in A
;

(iii)

A
is called a
sub-trellis
of
\mathbb{T}
if it is both a
\wedge
-sub-trellis and a

\vee
-sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
;

(iv)

A
is called a
sub-lattice
of
\mathbb{T}
if it is a sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
and

\unlhd
is transitive on
A
.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
43
]

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
be a trellis. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\unlhd
is transitive;

(ii)

both
\wedge
and
\vee
are associative;

(iii)

one of
\wedge
and
\vee
is associative.

Definition 2.3
.

[
43
]

A trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
is said to be
modular
, if for any
x,y,z\in X
,
it holds that
x\unlhd z
implies
x\vee(y\wedge z)=(x\vee y)\wedge z
.

Remark 2.1
.

A modular trellis does not include any cycle of three elements. Indeed, suppose that a modular trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
includes a cycle of three distinct elements
\mathcal{C}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
. This implies that
x_{i}\lesssim_{\mathcal{C}}x_{j}
, for any
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
with
i\neq j
. That means that exactly one of the following statements holds:
x_{1}\unlhd x_{2}\unlhd x_{3}\unlhd x_{1}
;
x_{1}\unlhd x_{3}\unlhd x_{2}\unlhd x_{1}
;
x_{2}\unlhd x_{1}\unlhd x_{3}\unlhd x_{2}
;
x_{2}\unlhd x_{3}\unlhd x_{1}\unlhd x_{2}
;
x_{3}\unlhd x_{1}\unlhd x_{2}\unlhd x_{3}
or
x_{3}\unlhd x_{2}\unlhd x_{1}\unlhd x_{3}
.
Any of these statements contradicts the modularity of
\mathbb{T}
. Suppose for instance that
x_{1}\unlhd x_{2}\unlhd x_{3}\unlhd x_{1}
. Since
x_{1}\unlhd x_{2}
, it holds by the modularity of
\mathbb{T}
that
x_{1}\vee(x_{3}\wedge x_{2})=(x_{1}\vee x_{3})\wedge x_{2}
. Hence,
x_{2}=x_{1}
, a contradiction.

2.2
Specific elements of a trellis

In this subsection, we present some specific elements of a trellis that will play an important role in this paper.

Definition 2.4
.

[
43
]

Let
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)
be a psoset. An element
a\in X
is called:

(i)

right-transitive
, if
a\unlhd x\unlhd y
implies
a\unlhd y
, for any
x,y\in X
;

(ii)

left-transitive
, if
x\unlhd y\unlhd a
implies
x\unlhd a
, for any
x,y\in X
;

(iii)

middle-transitive
, if
x\unlhd a\unlhd y
implies
x\unlhd y
, for any
x,y\in X
;

(iv)

transitive
, if it is right-, left- and middle-transitive.

Definition 2.5
.

[
43
]

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
be a trellis.

(i)

A 3-tuple
(x,y,z)\in X^{3}
is called
\wedge
-
associative
(resp.
\vee
-
associative
) if
(x\wedge y)\wedge z=x\wedge(y\wedge z)
(resp.
(x\vee y)\vee z=x\vee(y\vee z)
).

(ii)

An element
a\in X
is called
\wedge
-associative (resp.
\vee
-associative) if any 3-tuple including
a
is
\wedge
-associative (resp.
\vee
-associative).

(iii)

An element
a\in X
is called
associative
if it is both
\wedge
- and
\vee
-associative.

Note that due to the commutativity of
\wedge
and
\vee
, it is sufficient to consider 3-tuples
of the type
(a,x,y)
in Definition
2.5
(ii).

For further use, we recall some results about transitive and associative elements.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
43
]

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
be a trellis. Any
\wedge
-associative or
\vee
-associative element is transitive, but the converse does not hold in general.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
43
]

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
be a trellis. If
\mathbb{T}
is a modular trellis or a pseudo-chain, then any element is associative if and only if it is transitive.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
43
]

Let
(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
be a trellis and
a\in X
.

(i)

If
a
is right-transitive, then

(a)

a\lesssim x
implies
a\unlhd x
, for any
x\in X
;

(b)

a\unlhd x
implies
a\vee y\unlhd x\vee y
, for any
x,y\in X
.

(ii)

If
a
is left-transitive, then

(a)

x\lesssim a
implies
x\unlhd a
, for any
x\in X
;

(b)

x\unlhd a
implies
x\wedge y\unlhd a\wedge y
, for any
x,y\in X
.

(iii)

If
a
is
\wedge
-associative, then
x\unlhd y
implies
a\wedge x\unlhd a\wedge y
, for any
x,y\in X
.

(iv)

If

\vee
-associative, then

.

Proposition 2.3
.

Note that if a cycle
\mathcal{C}
of a psoset
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)

Proof.

Let

and

. Indeed, there exist two finite sequences

From Proposition

(i) and the antisymmetry of

. Inductively, it follows that

∎

.

.

[

Let

It is easy to see that if

4
Triangular norms on bounded psosets and trellises

In this section, we extend the notion of a t-norm to the setting of bounded psosets and trellises and provide some examples. For further use, we recall several notions and list some properties of binary operations on psosets and trellises.

4.1
Binary operations on psosets and trellises

In this subsection, we present some basic definitions and properties of binary operations on a psoset or trellis. Some of them are adopted from the corresponding notions on a poset or lattice (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
20
,
45
]
).
Consider a bounded psoset
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd)
. A binary operation
F
on
\mathbb{P}
is called:

(i)

commutative
, if
F(x,y)=F(y,x)
, for any
x,y\in X
;

(ii)

associative
, if
F(x,F(y,z))=F(F(x,y),z)
, for any
x,y,z\in X
;

(iii)

right-increasing
, if
x\unlhd y\text{ implies }F(z,x)\unlhd F(z,y)
, for any
x,y,z\in X
;

(iv)

left-increasing
, if
x\unlhd y\text{ implies }F(x,z)\unlhd F(y,z)
, for any
x,y,z\in X
;

(v)

increasing
, if
x\unlhd y
and
z\unlhd t\text{ implies }F(x,z)\unlhd F(y,t)
, for any
x,y,z,t\in X
.

If a binary operation
F
on
\mathbb{P}
is increasing, then it is right- and left-increasing. The converse also holds if
\unlhd
is transitive,
i.e.
, if
\mathbb{P}
is a poset.

A binary operation
F
on a trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)

is called:

(i)

conjunctive
, if
F(x,y)\unlhd x\wedge y
, for any
x,y\in X
;

(ii)

disjunctive
, if
x\vee y\unlhd F(x,y)
, for any
x,y\in X
.

Remark 4.1
.

Consider a trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee)
.

(i)

The meet
\wedge
(resp. join
\vee
) is conjunctive (resp. disjunctive).

(ii)

If a binary operation
F
on
X
satisfies

F(x,y)\unlhd x
and
F(x,y)\unlhd y

(resp.
x\unlhd F(x,y)
and
y\unlhd F(x,y)
),
for any
x,y\in X
, then it is conjunctive (resp. disjunctive).
The converse holds if
\unlhd
is transitive (
i.e.
, if
\mathbb{T}
is a lattice).
If the trellis
\mathbb{T}
is bounded, and
F
is right- and left-increasing and
1
(resp.
0
)
is the neutral element of
F
, then
F
being conjunctive (resp. disjunctive) implies that

F(x,y)\unlhd x
and
F(x,y)\unlhd y
(resp.
x\unlhd F(x,y)
and
y\unlhd F(x,y)
), for any
x,y\in X
.

4.2
T-norms on a bounded psoset or trellis

In this subsection, we introduce the notion of a t-norm on a bounded psoset and present some examples.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd,0,1)
be a bounded psoset. A binary operation
T:X^{2}\to X
is called a triangular norm (t-norm, for short) on
\mathbb{P}
if it is increasing, commutative, associative and has
1
as neutral element,
i.e.
,
T(x,1)=x
, for any
x\in X
.

Remark 4.2
.

(i)

The meet operation
\wedge
on a proper bounded trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
is not a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
as it is not necessarily increasing or associative.

(ii)

Any t-norm on a bounded trellis is conjunctive.

The pseudo-order
\unlhd
can be extended pointwisely to a pseudo-order on t-norms,
i.e.
,

T_{1}\unlhd T_{2}
if
T_{1}(x,y)\unlhd T_{2}(x,y)
for any
x,y\in X
.

The following proposition shows that the existence of an idempotent t-norm on a given bounded trellis
\mathbb{T}
implies that
\mathbb{T}
is a bounded lattice. The proof is straightforward.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
be a bounded trellis. If there exists an idempotent t-norm
T
on
\mathbb{T}
, then
T=\wedge
and
\mathbb{T}
is a bounded lattice.

Note that this result can be stated more generally outside the study of t-norms as already any increasing, conjunctive and idempotent binary operation coincides with the meet operation. Hence, there exists no increasing, conjunctive and idempotent binary operation on a proper trellis.

Next, we present some examples. We start with the smallest possible t-norm.

Example 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=(X,\unlhd,0,1)
be a bounded psoset. The binary operation
T_{\mathrm{D}}
on
X
defined by:

T_{\mathrm{D}}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{x}&\text{, if }y=1\\
{y}&\text{, if }x=1\\
{0}&\text{, otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(1)

is the smallest t-norm on
\mathbb{P}
, and will be called the
drastic t-norm
.

Note that on a bounded trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
,
the t-norm
T_{\mathrm{D}}
can also be written as

T_{\mathrm{D}}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{x\wedge y}&\text{, if }x=1\text{ or }y=1\\
{0}&\text{, otherwise}\end{array}\right..

(2)

On a bounded modular lattice, the binary operation
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
,
a slight modification of the t-norm
T_{\mathrm{D}}
, defined by:

T_{\mathrm{Z}}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{x\wedge y}&\text{, if }x\vee y=1\\
{0}&\text{, otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(3)

is also a t-norm
[
13
]
. This result can be extended to the setting of bounded modular trellises under a suitable necessary and sufficient condition. The proof is based on the following proposition that recalls an interesting property of bounded modular trellises. This property will be also exploited in the next section.

Proposition 4.3
.

[
42
]

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
be a bounded modular trellis. For any
x,y,z\in X
,
it holds that
x\unlhd z
and
x\vee y=1
imply
x\wedge y\unlhd z
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
be a bounded modular trellis. Then it holds that
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
if and only if

(\forall(x,y,z,t)\in X^{4})(((x\wedge y\neq 0)\mbox{ and }(x\vee y=1))\Rightarrow(x\vee z)\vee(y\vee t)=1)\,.

(4)

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
satisfies condition (
4
). Obviously,
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is commutative and has
1
as neutral element.

Next, we prove that
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is increasing. Let
x,y,z,t\in X
such that
x\unlhd y
and
z\unlhd t
. We consider the following two cases:

(i)

If
T_{\mathrm{Z}}(x,z)=0
, then it trivially holds that
T_{\mathrm{Z}}(x,z)\unlhd T_{\mathrm{Z}}(y,t)
.

(ii)

If
T_{\mathrm{Z}}(x,z)=x\wedge z\neq 0
, then
x\vee z=1
. Due to
(
4
), it holds that
(x\vee y)\vee(z\vee t)=1
.
Since
x\unlhd y
and
z\unlhd t
, this implies
y\vee t=1
, and hence

T_{\mathrm{Z}}(y,t)=y\wedge t
.
Furthermore, from Proposition
4.3
it follows that
x\wedge z\unlhd y
and
x\wedge z\unlhd t
. Hence,
x\wedge z\unlhd y\wedge t
. Thus,
T_{\mathrm{Z}}(x,z)\unlhd T_{\mathrm{Z}}(y,t)
.

Therefore,
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is increasing.

Now, we prove that
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is associative. Let
x,y,z\in X
, then

T_{\mathrm{Z}}(x,T_{\mathrm{Z}}(y,z))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{x\wedge(y\wedge z)}&\text{, if }y\vee z=1\text{ and }x\vee(y\wedge z)=1\\
{0}&\text{, otherwise}\end{array}\right.

and

T_{\mathrm{Z}}(T_{\mathrm{Z}}(x,y),z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{(x\wedge y)\wedge z}&{\text{, if }x\vee y=1\text{ and }z\vee(x\wedge y)=1}\\
{0}&{\text{, otherwise}}\end{array}\right.\,.

First, we show that the condition (
y\vee z=1
and
x\vee(y\wedge z)=1
) is equivalent to the condition (
x\vee y=1
and
z\vee(x\wedge y)=1
). Suppose that
y\vee z=1
and
x\vee(y\wedge z)=1
. Since
y\unlhd x\vee y
and
y\vee z=1
, Proposition
4.3
implies
y\wedge z\unlhd x\vee y
. Together with
x\unlhd x\vee y
, we get
1=x\vee(y\wedge z)\unlhd x\vee y
.
Thus,
x\vee y=1
. Moreover, since
x\vee(y\wedge z)=1
, it holds that
y=y\wedge(x\vee(y\wedge z))
. The modularity of
X
implies

y=y\wedge(x\vee(y\wedge z))=(x\wedge y)\vee(y\wedge z)=((x\wedge y)\vee z)\wedge y
. Thus,

y\unlhd(x\wedge y)\vee z
. On the other hand, since

z\unlhd(x\wedge y)\vee z
, it follows that
y\vee z\unlhd(x\wedge y)\vee z
. Hence,
(x\wedge y)\vee z=1
. The proof of the converse implication is similar.

Second, it now suffices to prove that
x\wedge(y\wedge z)=(x\wedge y)\wedge z
, for any
x,y,z\in X
satisfying
y\vee z=1
and
x\vee(y\wedge z)=1
. Since
y\wedge z\unlhd z
and
x\vee(y\wedge z)=1
, Proposition
4.3
implies
x\wedge(y\wedge z)\unlhd z
. Similarly,
y\wedge z\unlhd y
and
x\vee(y\wedge z)=1
imply
x\wedge(y\wedge z)\unlhd y
. Together with
x\wedge(y\wedge z)\unlhd x
, we obtain
x\wedge(y\wedge z)\unlhd x\wedge y
, and, hence,
x\wedge(y\wedge z)\unlhd(x\wedge y)\wedge z
. Similarly, we find
(x\wedge y)\wedge z\unlhd x\wedge(y\wedge z)
. Hence,
x\wedge(y\wedge z)=(x\wedge y)\wedge z
. Therefore,
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is associative. Consequently,
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
.

To prove the converse implication, suppose that
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
. Let
x,y,z,t\in X
be such that
x\wedge y\neq 0
and
x\vee y=1
. It then holds that

. Thus,
(x\vee z)\vee(y\vee t)=1
.
∎

5
A generic construction method based on interior operators

As mentioned several times before, on a proper bounded trellis the meet operation is not a t-norm.
In this section, inspired by the t-norms
T_{\mathrm{D}}
and
T_{\mathrm{Z}}
, we investigate whether
we can build a t-norm starting from the meet operation on an interior range of a given
\wedge
-semi-trellis.
First, we extend the notion of an interior operator
[
6
]
to the trellis setting.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge)
be a
\wedge
-semi-trellis. A mapping
I\colon X\to X

is called an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
if it satisfies the following three conditions:

(i)

I(x)\unlhd x
, for any
x\in X
;

(ii)

I
is idempotent,
i.e.
,
I(I(x))=I(x)
, for any
x\in X
;

(iii)

I
is a meet-homomorphism,
i.e.
,
I(x\wedge y)=I(x)\wedge I(y)
, for any
x,y\in X
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge)
be a
\wedge
-semi-trellis. A subset
R
of
X
is called
an interior range of
\mathbb{T}
if there exists an interior operator
I
on
\mathbb{T}
such that

R=I(X)=\{I(x)\mid x\in X\}
.

The following proposition is immediate.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge)
be a
\wedge
-semi-trellis and
I
an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
with range
R_{I}
. It holds that:

(i)

if
x\in R_{I}
, then
I(x)=x
;

(ii)

I
is increasing, i.e., if
x\unlhd y
, then
I(x)\unlhd I(y)
, for any
x,y\in X
.

The following proposition discusses the structure of the range of an interior operator on a given
\wedge
-semi-trellis.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge)
be a
\wedge
-semi-trellis and
I
an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
with range
R_{I}
.
It holds that:

(i)

(R_{I},\unlhd,\wedge)
is a
\wedge
-sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
;

(ii)

if
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,0,1)
is bounded, then
(R_{I},\unlhd,\wedge,0,I(1))
is also bounded.

Proof.

(i)

Let
x,y\in R_{I}
. Proposition
5.1
(i) and Definition
5.1
(iii) guarantee that
x\wedge y=I(x)\wedge I(y)=I(x\wedge y)
. Hence,
x\wedge y\in R_{I}
. Thus,
(R_{I},\unlhd,\wedge)
is a
\wedge
-sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
.

(ii)

Follows from Proposition
5.1
.

∎

In case of a bounded
\wedge
-semi-trellis, some authors require an interior
operator to satisfy
I(1)=1

[
17
]
.

The following result follows from Proposition
5.2
and Corollary
3.2
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,0,1)
be a bounded
\wedge
-semi-trellis and
I
an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
with range
R_{I}
. If
R_{I}\subseteq X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
or
R_{I}\subseteq X^{\mathrm{ltr}}
, then
(R_{I},\unlhd,\wedge,0,I(1))
is a bounded
\wedge
-sub-lattice of
\mathbb{T}
.

For a given t-norm
V
on a bounded
\wedge
-sub-trellis
(R_{I},\unlhd,\wedge,0,I(1))
, we define the following binary operation on
\mathbb{T}
:

T^{I,V}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{y}&\text{, if }x=1\\
{x}&\text{, if }y=1\\
{V(I(x),I(y))}&\text{, otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(6)

In view of Proposition
5.1
(i), we can express

T^{I,V}
as:

T^{I,V}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{y}&\text{, if }x=1\\
{x}&\text{, if }y=1\\
{V(x,y)}&\text{, if }x,y\in R_{I}\\
{V(I(x),I(y))}&\text{, otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(7)

The following theorem shows that
T^{I,V}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
when
R_{I}
is a subset of
X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,0,1)
be a bounded
\wedge
-semi-trellis,
I
an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
with range
R_{I}
. If
R_{I}
is a subset of
X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
and
V
is a t-norm on the bounded
\wedge
-sub-lattice

(R_{I},\unlhd,\wedge,0,I(1))
, then the binary operation
T^{I,V}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Obviously,
T^{I,V}
is commutative and has
1
as neutral element.

Next, we prove that
T^{I,V}
is increasing. Let
x,y,z,t\in X
such that
x\unlhd y
and
z\unlhd t
.
We consider the following two cases:

(i)

First case:
x=1
, and hence
y=1
.
Then
T^{I,V}(x,z)=z
and
T^{I,V}(y,t)=t
. Hence,
T^{I,V}(x,z)\unlhd T^{I,V}(y,t)
.
The same holds when
z=1
.

(ii)

Second case:
x\neq 1
and
z\neq 1
. Two subcases can be distinguished: (
y=1
or
t=1
) or (
y\neq 1
and
t\neq 1
).

(a)

First subcase:
y=1
. Since
T^{I,V}(x,z)=V(I(x),I(z))
, it follows from the increasingness of
I
on
\mathbb{T}
(Proposition
5.1
(ii)) and the increasingness of
V
on
R_{I}
that

T^{I,V}(x,z)=V(I(x),I(z))\unlhd V(I(y),I(t))
. If
y=1
, then
T^{I,V}(x,z)\unlhd V(I(1),I(t))=I(t)\unlhd t=T^{I,V}(1,t)=T^{I,V}(y,t)
. The fact that
T^{I,V}(x,z)=V(I(x),I(z))\in R_{I}\subseteq X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
implies that
T^{I,V}(x,z)\unlhd T^{I,V}(y,t)
. The proof is similar for
t=1
.

(b)

Second subcase:
y\neq 1
and
t\neq 1
. Since
I
is increasing on
\mathbb{T}
and
V
is increasing on
R_{I}
, it follows that
T^{I,V}(x,z)=V(I(x),I(z))\unlhd V(I(y),I(t))=T^{I,V}(y,t)
.

Therefore,
T^{I,V}
is increasing.

Next, we prove that
T^{I,V}
is associative. Let
x,y,z\in X
, then we need to prove that

T^{I,V}(x,T^{I,V}(y,z))=T^{I,V}(T^{I,V}(x,y),z)\,.

We consider the following two cases:

(i)

If
1\in\{x,y,z\}
, then the equality trivially holds.

(ii)

If
1\notin\{x,y,z\}
, then

T^{I,V}(x,T^{I,V}(y,z))=V(I(x),V(I(y),I(z))\,.

The associativity of
V
on
R_{I}
guarantees that

V(I(x),V(I(y),I(z)))=V(V(I(x),I(y)),I(z))\,.

Hence,

T^{I,V}(x,T^{I,V}(y,z))=T^{I,V}(T^{I,V}(x,y),z)\,.

Thus,
T^{I,V}
is associative. Therefore,
T^{I,V}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
.
∎

Considering as t-norm
V
the meet operation restricted to
R_{I}
leads to the following corollary.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,0,1)
be a bounded
\wedge
-semi-trellis and
I
an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
with range
R_{I}
. If
R_{I}
is a subset of
X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
, then the binary operation
T^{I}
on
\mathbb{T}
defined as:

T^{I}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{y}&\text{, if }x=1\\
{x}&\text{, if }y=1\\
{x\wedge y}&\text{, if }x,y\in R_{I}\\
{I(x)\wedge I(y)}&\text{, otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(8)

is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
.

The following proposition shows that
T^{I}
is a meet-preserving t-norm,
i.e.
, its partial mappings are meet-homomorphisms.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,0,1)
be a bounded
\wedge
-semi-trellis
and
I
an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
with range
R_{I}\subseteq X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
. Then the t-norm
T^{I}
is meet-preserving, i.e.,

T^{I}(x,y\wedge z)=T^{I}(x,y)\wedge T^{I}(x,z)\,,

for any
x,y,z\in X
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the fact that
I
is a meet-homomorphism and the associativity of
T^{I}
.
∎

6
A particular family of interior operators

In this section, we consider a particular family of interior operators on a bounded trellis.
For a subset
A
of a given bounded trellis
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
, we define the mapping
\lambda_{A}\colon X\to X
:

\lambda_{A}(x)=\bigvee\{a\in A\mid a\unlhd x\}=\bigvee(A\,\cap\downarrow x)\,.

(9)

In general, this mapping is not well defined since the supremum
\bigvee(A\,\cap\downarrow x)
does not necessarily exist.
However, if
A
is a finite subset of
X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
containing
0
, then Proposition
3.7

guarantees that it is well defined.

The following proposition shows that
\lambda_{A}
is an interior operator.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
be a bounded trellis and
A
a finite subset of
X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
containing
0
. If
A
is a sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
, then
\lambda_{A}
is an interior operator on
\mathbb{T}
and
R_{\lambda_{A}}=A
.

Proof.

(i)

Obviously,
\lambda_{A}(x)\unlhd x
, for any
x\in X
.

(ii)

Let
x\in X
. From Proposition
3.7
, it follows that
\lambda_{A}(x)\in A
. Hence,
\lambda_{A}(\lambda_{A}(x))=\lambda_{A}(x)
. Thus,
\lambda_{A}
is idempotent.

(iii)

Let
x,y\in X
, then
\lambda_{A}(x),\lambda_{A}(y)\in A
and
\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)\in A
. Since
A\subseteq X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
,
\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)\unlhd\lambda_{A}(x)\unlhd x
implies
\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)\unlhd x
. Similarly,
\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)\unlhd y
. Hence,
\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)\unlhd x\wedge y
. The fact that
\lambda_{A}
is idempotent and increasing implies that
\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)=\lambda_{A}(\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y))\unlhd\lambda_{A}(x\wedge y)
. The increasingness of
\lambda_{A}
also implies that
\lambda_{A}(x\wedge y)\unlhd\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)
. Hence,
\lambda_{A}(x\wedge y)=\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)
. Thus,
\lambda_{A}
is a meet-homomorphism.

Obviously,
\lambda_{A}(x)=x
, for any
x\in A
. Hence,
R_{\lambda_{A}}=A
.
∎

Note that due to Proposition
5.3
,
under the conditions of Proposition
6.1
, the sub-trellis
A
is a bounded
\wedge
-sub-lattice.

Combining Theorem
5.1
and Proposition
6.1
, we obtain the following result showing that
T^{\lambda_{A},V}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
when
A
is a sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
. Note that we use the notation
T^{[A,V]}
instead of
T^{\lambda_{A},V}
.

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
be a bounded trellis and
A
a finite subset of
X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
containing
0
. If
A

is a sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
and
V
is a t-norm on the bounded
\wedge
-sub-lattice
(A,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,\lambda_{A}(1))
,
then the binary operation
T^{[A,V]}
on
\mathbb{T}
defined as:

T^{[A,V]}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{y}&\text{, if }x=1\\
{x}&\text{, if }y=1\\
{V(x,y)}&\text{, if }x,y\in A\\
{V(\lambda_{A}(x),\lambda_{A}(y))}&\text{, otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(10)

is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
.

In the same line, combining Corollary
5.1
and Proposition
6.1
, we obtain the following result showing that
T^{\lambda_{A}}
is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
when
A
is a sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
. Note that
we use the notation
T^{[A]}
instead of
T^{\lambda_{A}}
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
be a bounded trellis and
A
a finite subset of
X^{\mathrm{rtr}}
containing
0
. If
A
is a sub-trellis of
\mathbb{T}
, then the binary operation
T^{[A]}
on
\mathbb{T}
defined as:

T^{[A]}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{y}&\text{, if }x=1\\
{x}&\text{, if }y=1\\
{x\wedge y}&\text{, if }x,y\in A\\
{\lambda_{A}(x)\wedge\lambda_{A}(y)}&\text{, otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(11)

is a t-norm on
\mathbb{T}
.

Remark 6.1
.

We list some basic observations:

(i)

If
A=\{0,i\}
, with
i
a co-atom of
\mathbb{T}
, then
T^{[A]}=T_{i}
.

(ii)

If
A=\{0,1\}
, then
T^{[A]}=T_{\mathrm{D}}
.

(iii)

If
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)
is a bounded lattice, then
T^{[X^{\alpha}]}=\wedge
, for any
\alpha\in\{\mathrm{ass},\mathrm{\wedge\text{-}ass},\mathrm{\vee\text{-}ass},\mathrm{tr},\mathrm{rtr}\}
.

Using Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
, and Corollary
6.2
, we obtain the following corollaries.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\mathbb{T}=(X,\unlhd,\wedge,\vee,0,1)

Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced the notion of a t-norm on bounded psosets and trellises and provided some basic examples. As the meet operation of a proper bounded trellis is not a t-norm, we have explored how we can construct a t-norm starting from a t-norm on an appropriate interior range.
We have provided illustrative examples by introducing interior operators whose range is an appropriate finite subset of the set of right-transitive elements of the given trellis. Similar results can be formulated for t-conorms on bounded psosets and trellises by considering the join operation, the set of left-transitive elements and closure operators.
Since the set of right-transitive elements does not include non-trivial cycles, future work will explore how cycles can be involved. We anticipate that this study will open the door to various follow-up studies on t-norms and t-conorms on bounded psosets and trellises, and, more generally, on aggregation functions in the same setting.
