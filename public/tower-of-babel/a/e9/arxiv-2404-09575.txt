Binary quadratic forms with the same value set
Étienne Fouvry, Peter Koymans

Abstract
Given a binary quadratic form F ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ X , Y ] F\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y] , we define its value set F ⁡ ( ℤ 2 ) F(\mathbb{Z}^{2}) to be { F ⁡ ( x , y ) : ( x , y ) ∈ ℤ 2 } \{F(x,y):(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\} . If F F and G G are two binary quadratic forms with integer coefficients, we give necessary and sufficient conditions on F F and G G for F ⁡ ( ℤ 2 ) = G ⁡ ( ℤ 2 ) F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=G(\mathbb{Z}^{2}) .



1
Introduction

Let

F(X,Y)=aX^{2}+bXY+cY^{2}

(1.1)

be a binary quadratic form with integer coefficients
a,b,c
. The group
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
acts on the set of such forms by the following linear change of variables: if
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}
belongs to
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, then
F\circ\gamma
is defined by

\left(F\circ\gamma\right)(X,Y)=F(\alpha X+\beta Y,\gamma X+\delta Y).

The orbit of
F
under this action is denoted by
[F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
and the quadratic form
G
is
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to
F
if and only if it belongs to
[F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
. We then write
G\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
. In §
2
and
3
, we will be interested by the action of the group
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and the corresponding notations will then be obvious.

One fascinating invariant of
F
is its
value set
, defined by

\text{Im}(F)=\{F(x,y):(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}.

This is indeed an invariant, as it is left unchanged by the above action of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
on
F
. We say that a binary quadratic form
F
is
positive definite
if
b^{2}-4ac<0
and
a>0
. These conditions are equivalent to the condition
F(x,y)>0
for all pairs of integers
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
.

Our paper is inspired by the following result.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
4
,
21
,
22
]
)
.

Let
F,G\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
be two positive definite binary quadratic forms such that
\textup{Im}(F)=\textup{Im}(G)
. Then exactly one of the following is true

•

F
and
G
are
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent;

•

F
is
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to
c(X^{2}+XY+Y^{2})
and
G
is
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to
c(X^{2}+3Y^{2})
for some
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
;

•

F
is
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to
c(X^{2}+3Y^{2})
and
G
is
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent to
c(X^{2}+XY+Y^{2})
for some
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

According to the historical information that we have at our disposal, we found it adequate to name the quadratic forms
X^{2}+XY+Y^{2}
and
X^{2}+3Y^{2}

Delone–Watson forms
(see
[
8
, Introduction]
). We respectively denote them by

{\rm dw}(X,Y):=X^{2}+XY+Y^{2}\text{ and }{\rm DW}(X,Y):=X^{2}+3Y^{2},

and they satisfy the important relation

{\rm dw}(2X,Y)={\rm DW}(X+Y,X).

(1.2)

Furthermore, by
[
21
, Theorem]
, the content of Theorem
1.1
remains true when the forms
F
and
G
have real coefficients, not necessarily integral.

In this paper we study the indefinite case, which corresponds to
b^{2}-4ac>0
. To state our results, we introduce some notation. Let
F
and
G
be two quadratic forms. We say that
F\sim_{\text{val}}G
if
\text{Im}(F)=\text{Im}(G)
. We write
[F]_{\text{val}}
for the corresponding equivalence class containing
F
. As remarked before, we have the trivial inclusion

[F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\subseteq[F]_{\text{val}}.

(1.3)

When the inclusion (
1.3
) is strict, we say that
F
is an
extraordinary form
. When (
1.3
) turns out to be an equality, we say that
F
is an
ordinary form
. Theorem
1.1
states that in the set of positive definite quadratic forms, there are only two extraordinary forms, up to a positive homothety and up to the action of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Our purpose is to study the set of extraordinary forms mainly in the case of indefinite quadratic forms, but we shall recover Theorem
1.1
in the process.

We first recall some classical notions and notations in that context. The
discriminant
of
F
, as written in (
1.1
), is defined by

{\rm disc}(F):=b^{2}-4ac,

so we have
{\rm disc}(F)\equiv 0,1\bmod 4
. The form
F
is said to be
primitive
if and only if
\gcd(a,b,c)=1.
The discriminant of a form is invariant under the operation of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and so is the value of
\gcd(a,b,c)
. This remark allows us to speak of a
primitive class

[F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
when one element of the class is primitive, and so all the elements are. Similarly, Lemma
3.3
below allows us to also speak of a
primitive class

[F]_{\rm val}
. Starting from the
F
, we build the forms
F^{\dagger}
and
F^{\ddagger}
defined by

F^{\dagger}(X,Y):=F(2X,Y)\text{ and }F^{\ddagger}(X,Y):=F(X,2Y).

(1.4)

In particular, the equality (
1.2
) implies

{\rm DW}\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}{\rm dw}^{\dagger}.

(1.5)

1.1
Different types of class numbers

Let
d
be an integer (of any sign) different from a square. Then
d
is the discriminant of a quadratic order
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{d}
if and only if
d\equiv 0,1\bmod 4
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{O}
is unique. We denote by
h(d)
the class number of that order, so

h(d)=\left|I(\mathcal{O})/P(\mathcal{O})\right|,

where
I(\mathcal{O})
is the group of proper fractional
\mathcal{O}
–ideals and
P(\mathcal{O})
is the subgroup of principal ideals (see
[
2
, Def. 5.2.7]
or
[
3
, Ch. 2, §7]
for instance). The quotient
I(\mathcal{O})/P(\mathcal{O})
is called the
(ordinary) class group
of
\mathcal{O}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{C\ell}(\mathcal{O})
(or
\mathcal{C\ell}(d)
).

Let
P^{+}(\mathcal{O})
be the subgroup of principal ideals generated by a totally positive element. The quotient
I(\mathcal{O})/P^{+}(\mathcal{O})
is called the
narrow class group
of
\mathcal{O}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{C\ell}^{+}(\mathcal{O})
(or
\mathcal{C\ell}^{+}(d)
). Its cardinality is the
narrow class number
and denoted by
h^{+}(d)
. The ratio
h^{+}(d)/h(d)
has value
1
or
2
. When
d<0
, this ratio is always equal to
1
. When
d>0
the ratio equals
1
if and only if
\mathcal{O}
has a fundamental unit with norm equal to
-1
. Finally,
h^{+}(d)
is also the number of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–classes of primitive quadratic forms with discriminant
d
(see Proposition
3.4
below).

We also introduce
h^{\star}(d)
, which is the number of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–classes of primitive quadratic forms with discriminant
d
. The number
h^{\star}(d)
depends on the group structure of
\mathcal{C\ell}^{+}(d)
. Indeed, if the group law on
\mathcal{C\ell}^{+}(d)
is written multiplicatively, and if we identify
x
with
x^{-1}
, we obtain a set with cardinality
{h^{\star}}(d)
. We have the inequality

h^{\star}(d)\geq\max\{1,h^{+}(d)/2\}.

1.2
The main theorems

Our first result gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a primitive form to be extraordinary.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
F
be a primitive binary quadratic form with integer coefficients such that its discriminant is not a square. Then
[F]_{\textup{val}}
breaks either into one or into two equivalence classes under
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

It breaks into two equivalence classes if and only if there exists
f
such that
f\sim_{\textup{val}}F
and such that the discriminant
d
of
f
satisfies
d\equiv 5\bmod 8
and
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
. In this case we have a decomposition

[F]_{\textup{val}}=[f]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\cup[f^{\dagger}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}.

(1.6)

We thank Andrew Earnest for pointing out that Delang Li
[
15
]
has previously shown a rather similar result to Theorem
1.2
for indefinite forms. More precisely,
[
15
]
proves our result with the condition
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
replaced by an equivalent condition on the fundamental unit, see also our Corollary
1.9
. It is worth pointing out that Li’s main result
[
15
, Theorem 2]
is stated rather imprecisely. Indeed, using Li’s notation, Li seems to forget that both forms
f
and
g
may be replaced by
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent forms. The results in
[
15
]
were also extended to real coefficients in
[
16
]
.

Our later theorems strengthen the results in
[
15
]
in various ways: in particular by showing that there are infinitely many different extraordinary forms and that being extraordinary depends only on the discriminant (this is clear for
d\equiv 5\bmod 8
, but much less clear for discriminants of the form
4d
with
d\equiv 5\bmod 8
). Moreover, unlike previous works, we are able to treat the definite and indefinite forms uniformly, and we also study coprime value sets in Corollary
1.12
.

If
F
is extraordinary, the class
[F]_{\textup{val}}
is the union of exactly two distinct classes
\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
by Theorem
1.2
. One of these classes is
[F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
, by (
1.3
). Any element
G
of the second class is also extraordinary, and we call
(F,G)
a
pair of associated extraordinary forms
. Of course, for any integer
c\neq 0
, for any
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
the pair
(cF\circ\gamma,cG\circ\gamma^{\prime})
is also a pair of associated extraordinary forms. Finally, we deduce from (
1.6
) that for any pair
(F,G)
of associated primitive extraordinary forms, we have

{\rm disc\,}F/{\rm disc\,}G\in\{1/4,\,4\}.

(1.7)

We follow the convention to write a pair of associated extraordinary forms under the form
(F,G)
with
\text{disc }G=4\cdot\text{disc }F
. Then
F
is called a
lower
extraordinary form, and
G
an
upper
extraordinary form. By
([F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},[G]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})})
we denote the pair of
\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
classes respectively containing
F
and
G
and we say that
([F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},[G]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})})
is a
pair of associated extraordinary classes
.

Our second result expands on Theorem
1.2
by giving a rather easy criterion to exhibit extraordinary forms. It also shows a complete difference between definite and indefinite quadratic forms. This difference can be imputed to the dissimilarity of the structure of the set of invertible elements in the corresponding orders.

Theorem 1.3
.

According to the sign of the discriminants of quadratic forms we have:

(1)

There are exactly two pairs of primitive associated extraordinary classes with negative discriminants:
([{\rm dw}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},[{\rm DW}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})})
and

([{\rm-dw}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},[-{\rm DW}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})})
.

(2)

Let
f
be a primitive quadratic form with a non square positive discriminant
d\equiv 1\bmod 4
.

a.

If
d\equiv 5\bmod 8
and if
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
, then
f
is lower extraordinary with associated upper extraordinary form
f^{\dagger}
. In particular, every (primitive) form with discriminant
d
is lower extraordinary.

b.

If
d\equiv 1\bmod 8
or if
h^{+}(d)\neq h^{+}(4d)
, then
f
is ordinary. In particular, every (primitive) form with discriminant
d
is ordinary.

(3)

Let
f
be a primitive quadratic form with a non square positive discriminant
d\equiv 0\bmod 4
.

a.

If
d\equiv 20\bmod 32
and if
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(d/4)
, then
f
is upper extraordinary and there exists
\gamma\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that
(f\circ\gamma)(X/2,Y)
has integer coefficients and such that
((f\circ\gamma)(X/2,Y),f(X,Y))
is a pair of associated primitive extraordinary forms. In particular, every (primitive) form with discriminant
d
is upper extraordinary.

b.

If
d\bmod 32\in\{0,4,8,12,16,24,28\}
or if
h^{+}(d)\neq h^{+}(d/4)
, the form
f
is ordinary. In particular, every (primitive) form with discriminant
d
is ordinary.

In §
1.5
we will study the frequency of these different cases.

1.3
A first list of remarks

The following corollary is a consequence of (
1.7
). However, it will be directly proved in Lemma
3.12
below.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
F
and
G
be two forms such that their non square discriminants satisfy
{\rm disc\,}F={\rm disc\,}G
and such that
F\sim_{\rm val}G
. Then
we have
F\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}G
.

In the following corollary, we generalize Theorem
1.2
to the case where
F
is not necessarily primitive.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
F
be a binary quadratic form with integer coefficients, written as in (
1.1
), such that its discriminant is not a square. Let
\Delta:=\gcd(a,b,c)
. Then
[F]_{\textup{val}}
breaks either into one or into two equivalence classes under
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. It breaks into two equivalence classes if and only if there exists
f
such that
f\sim_{\textup{val}}F
and such that the discriminant
d
of
f
satisfies
d/\Delta^{2}\equiv 5\bmod 8
and
h^{+}(d/\Delta^{2})=h^{+}(4d/\Delta^{2})
. In this case we have a decomposition

[F]_{\textup{val}}=[f]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\cup[f^{\dagger}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}.

Every pair of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–classes of associated extraordinary forms has the shape

\bigl([F]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},[F^{\dagger}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\bigr),

where
{\rm disc}\,F
satisfies the above properties.

Remark 1.6
.

The first item of Theorem
1.3
is only a reformulation of Theorem
1.1
. The items (2) and (3) investigate all the the possible values of the positive discriminant
d\equiv 0
or
1\bmod 4
. Thus the property for a form to be lower or upper extraordinary, or ordinary is completely determined by the value of its discriminant. It is interesting to compare this with the situation of binary forms with degree
d\geq 3
, see
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

Theorem
1.3
allows us to deduce the following corollary by looking at the value sets of the principal form, and observing that two different fundamental discriminants can never differ by a factor of
4
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be two quadratic fields such that the set of norms of the integral elements of
K
and
K^{\prime}
coincide. Then
K=K^{\prime}
.

Remark 1.8
.

We state our results with the help of the quadratic form
f^{\dagger}
. Of course they remain true if one replaces
f^{\dagger}
by
f^{\ddagger}
, defined in (
1.4
). In particular, suppose that
d
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1.3
(2).a, we deduce that

f\sim_{\rm val}f^{\dagger},\quad f\sim_{\rm val}f^{\ddagger},

which implies that
f^{\dagger}\sim_{\rm val}f^{\ddagger}
. Since these forms have the same discriminant, they are
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent by Theorem
1.2
.

1.4
More advanced remarks and consequences

The equality
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
plays a central role in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. The following corollaries give another form to these theorems appealing to the fundamental unit
\varepsilon_{d}
of the order
\mathcal{O}_{d}
or to the ordinary class number
h
.

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
d
be a non square discriminant. Choose a decomposition of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
as
\mathcal{O}_{d}=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\omega_{d}
. Let
\epsilon_{d}
be a fundamental unit of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
, and write it as

\epsilon_{d}=x_{d}+y_{d}\omega_{d}\quad(x_{d},y_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}).

Then Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
remain true if one replaces the condition
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
by the condition
2\nmid y_{d}
.

The proof will be given in Lemma
3.6
. We also have the following variant.

Corollary 1.10
.

Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
remain true if one replaces the condition
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
by the condition
h(d)=h(4d)
.

The proof of Corollary
1.10
will be given in §
6
.

Remark 1.11
.

Theorem
1.3
is very efficient in exhibiting primitive extraordinary forms when one only has at one’s disposal tables in classical books (for instance
[
1
]
,
[
2
, p.505–509]
,
[
12
, p.465–472]
). Usually, these tables give the values of
h^{+}(d)
for fundamental
d
and the fundamental unit, which provides a sufficient amount of information thanks to Corollary
1.9
. Alternatively, one could use Magma or Sage.

To illustrate our results, consider
d=229
. It is a prime
\equiv 5\bmod 8
and by
[
2
, p. 507]
, it satisfies
h^{+}(229)=3
and
\epsilon_{229}=7+\frac{1+\sqrt{229}}{2}
. Lemma
3.6
implies
h^{+}(229)=h^{+}(4\cdot 229)=3
. So there exist exactly three classes of quadratic forms with discriminant
229
modulo the action of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. These are

[X^{2}-XY-57Y^{2}]_{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},\,[3X^{2}+13XY-5Y^{2}]_{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},\,[9X^{2}+7XY-5Y^{2}]_{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}.

(1.8)

We have
\mathcal{C\ell}^{+}(229)\simeq\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
and
h^{\star}(229)=2
. So there are two classes of lower extraordinary forms modulo
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. They are

[X^{2}-XY-57Y^{2}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}\textup{ and }[3X^{2}+13XY-5Y^{2}]_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})},

since the change of variables
(X,Y)\mapsto(-X,-2X+Y)
transforms the second form of the list (
1.8
) into the third.

The following consequence is inspired by
[
21
, p.73]
. It concerns the image of

\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\text{prim}}:=\{(m,n):\gcd(m,n)=1\}.

By construction
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\text{prim}})
is the set of integers which admit a
primitive representation
by
F
. If
F
and
G
are
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–equivalent, then certainly
F(\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\text{prim}})=G(\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\text{prim}})
. Conversely, we have

Corollary 1.12
.

Let
F
and
G
be two primitive quadratic forms, with non square discriminants and with
F\bigl(\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\textup{prim}}\bigr)=G\bigl(\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\textup{prim}}\bigr)
. Then we have
F\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}G
.

Proof.

Let
F
and
G
be as above, with discriminants
d_{F}
and
d_{G}
satisfying
|d_{F}|\leq|d_{G}|
without loss of generality. From the decomposition

\mathbb{Z}^{2}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}k\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\text{prim}},

and from the hypothesis, we deduce that
F\sim_{\rm val}G
by homogeneity. So
d_{F}
and
d_{G}
have the same sign. If
d_{F}\ =d_{G}
, we have
F\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}G
by Corollary
1.4
.

If
|d_{F}|<|d_{G}|
,
(F,G)
is a pair of associated extraordinary forms. By Theorem
1.3
(2), we know that
F(X,Y)=aX^{2}+bXY+cY^{2}
, with
b^{2}-4ac\equiv 5\bmod 8
. So all the coefficients
a
,
b
and
c
are odd and we have

F(X,Y)\equiv X^{2}+XY+Y^{2}\bmod 2.

The form
X^{2}+XY+Y^{2}
never represents primitively an even integer. This implies that
F
never represents primitively an even integer. By Theorem
1.3
(2), we have
G\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F^{\dagger}
. So the form
G
primitively represents the integer
F^{\dagger}(1,2)=F(2,2)\equiv 0\bmod 2
, contradiction.
∎

1.5
Infinitely many classes of extraordinary forms

Consider the two sets of integers

\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}_{5,8}:=\{d>0:d\equiv 5\bmod 8,\,h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)\},\\
\mathcal{D}_{20,32}:=\{d>0:d\equiv 20\bmod 32,\,h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(d/4)\}.\end{cases}

As a consequence of Theorem
1.3
, a form, with positive discriminant, is lower (resp. upper) extraordinary if and only if its discriminant belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{5,8}
(resp.
{\mathcal{D}}_{20,32}
). In the definitions of
\mathcal{D}_{5,8}
and of
\mathcal{D}_{20,32}
we do not require
d
or
d/4
to be squarefree. In order to describe the asymptotic cardinality of
\mathcal{D}_{5,8}
, we consider the following subset

\mathcal{S}_{5,8}:=\{d>0:d\text{ squarefree, }d\equiv 5\bmod 8,\,h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)\}.

(1.9)

All the elements of
\mathcal{S}_{5,8}
are fundamental discriminants. We will put the sets
\mathcal{D}_{5,8}
and
\mathcal{S}_{5,8}
in perspective with the more general set

\mathcal{G}_{5,8}:=\{d>0:d\text{ squarefree},d\equiv 5\bmod 8\}.

(1.10)

For
x\geq 1
, the number of elements
d\leq x
belonging to the sets
\mathcal{D}_{5,8}
,
\mathcal{S}_{5,8}
and
\mathcal{G}_{5,8}
are respectively denoted by
D_{5,8}(x)
,
S_{5,8}(x)
and
G_{5,8}(x)
. With this notation set, we have

Theorem 1.13
.

As
x
tends to infinity one has the inequality

S_{5,8}(x)\geq(1/2-o(1))\cdot G_{5,8}(x).

(1.11)

In particular, we have the inequality

D_{5,8}(x)\geq\left(\frac{1}{2\,\pi^{2}}-o(1)\right)x.

(1.12)

The inequality (
1.11
) means that, for a positive proportion of positive discriminants
d
, every form
F
with discriminant
d
is extraordinary. This implies that the set of pairs of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–classes of primitive extraordinary forms is infinite. This situation is completely different from the case of definite forms.

To go from (
1.11
) to (
1.12
), we use the Möbius function
\mu
to write the equalities

G_{5,8}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leq x\\
d\equiv 5\bmod 8\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\\
2\nmid k\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\leq x/k^{2}\\
\delta k^{2}\equiv 5\bmod 8\end{subarray}}1\sim\prod_{p\geq 3}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{x}{8}\sim\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\cdot x.

So it remains to prove the inequality (
1.11
). This will be done in §
7
.

1.6
Ordinary forms of a special type

Theorem
1.3

2.(b)
or
3.(b)
proves that there exist infinitely many classes of ordinary forms by simply choosing special congruences for
d
. We present a more involved way to exhibit ordinary forms.

Theorem 1.14
.

There exist infinitely many
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
–classes of primitive ordinary forms
f

The proof, based on results in

, will be given in §

.

2
Schering’s result

We now state the rather old result of Schering (1859). After recalling the notations and definitions he used, we give his central result. Then in §
2.2
we will comment on the consequences of Theorem
2.1
and their links with Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. Finally, in §
3
we will give an alternative proof of it, based on rather modern concepts.

2.1
The statement

In this section only, we adopt Schering’s notations inspired by Gauss
[
11
, Section 5]
. They do not fit to those used in §
1
. In particular, the middle coefficients of the quadratic forms are now even. The result of Schering concerns binary forms
f(X,Y)
written as

f(X,Y)=aX^{2}+2bXY+cY^{2},

(2.1)

with coefficients
a
,
b
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}
. The form
f
is
primitive
when

\gcd(a,b,c)=1.

Three integers are attached to
f
. These are

\displaystyle\text{ \it the determinant of }f:

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=b^{2}-ac,

\displaystyle\text{ \it the order of }f:

\displaystyle o

\displaystyle=\gcd(a,b,c),

\displaystyle\text{ \it the species (in french, {\it esp\`{e}ce}) of }f:

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=\frac{\gcd(a,2b,c)}{\gcd(a,b,c)}.

Obviously, the species of a quadratic form is equal to
1
or
2
and its determinant is divisible by the square of its order. The primitive form
f
is
properly primitive
if
e=1
. In that case
\gcd(a,b,c)=\gcd(a,2b,c)=1
and the form
f
is primitive in the sense of §
1
. If
e=2
, we say that the primitive form is
improperly primitive
. In that case the form
f/2
is primitive in the sense of §
1
. See
[
3
, p. 65]
for comments on these notations.

The set of forms of the shape (
2.1
) is stable under the action of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
by change of variables. Thus we obtain the notion of
equivalent forms
and
equivalence classes of quadratic forms
, or
classes modulo
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
for short. The numbers
d
,
e
and
o
are constant in each class. We say that a form
f
is
contained in a form
F
if there exist some integer coefficients
a_{i}
such that
f(X,Y)=F(a_{1}X+a_{2}Y,a_{3}X+a_{4}Y)
. This implies the inclusion of the images
f(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\subseteq F(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.

Let

F(X,Y)=AX^{2}+2BXY+CY^{2}

be another quadratic form with integers
A
,
B
and
C
. We denote by capital letters
D
,
E
and
O
the determinant, the species and the order of
F
.

Motivated by a result stated, without precision nor proof, by Legendre concerning forms contained in another one (see
[
14
, pages 237–238]
), Schering showed a theorem that we reproduce as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
17
, Troisième Théorème]
and
[
5
, p. 26]
)
.

The quadratic forms
f
and
F
have the same image if and only if the following conditions are satisfied

(1)

one of the two forms is contained in the other one,

(2)

oe=OE
,

(3)

e^{2}d=E^{2}D
,

(4)

and if
e\neq E
, the number
D/O^{2}(=d/o^{2})
is congruent to
5
modulo
8
, and for this value of the determinant, there are as many improperly primitive classes as properly primitive classes modulo
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
(which means that the classes with the corresponding
e=2
and
e=1
are equally numerous).

2.2
Comments and interpretation

We now interpret the statement of Theorem
2.1
.

•

Let
F
and
f
be two binary quadratic forms such that
f\sim_{\rm val}F
but
f\not\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
. Trivially, the integers
d
and
D
have the same sign. With the notations of this theorem, we have the equality

\frac{D}{d}=\left(\frac{e}{E}\right)^{2}.

Since the numbers
e
and
E
take only the values
1
or
2
, we deduce that
D/d\in\{1,4\}
, if we suppose that
|D|\geq|d|
. Let
\gamma
be a matrix with integer coefficients such that (see condition (1))

F=f\circ\gamma.

(2.2)

By the relation
D=d\cdot(\det\gamma)^{2}
we deduce that
\det\gamma=\pm 1
or
\det\gamma=\pm 2
. The first case is forbidden since it would give
f\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
by (
2.2
). So
\det\gamma=\pm 2
. Using the Smith normal form, there exist two matrices
A,B\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\gamma=A\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}B.

Inserting this equality into (
2.2
), we deduce that there exists a quadratic form
g\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}f
such that

F(X,Y)\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}g(2X,Y).

•

From the equality
D=d\cdot(\det\gamma)^{2}
, we deduce the following statement:
if the forms
f
and
F
are such that
d=D
and such that
f\sim_{\rm val}F
, then we have
f\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
.

•

The hypothesis
(1)
of Theorem
2.1
is not required in the proofs of our Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
and
4.1
leading however to the same type of conclusions. Another significant advantage of our results is that condition
(4)
of Schering’s result is demystified by reinterpreting it in terms of more modern invariants such as the class group (or alternatively the fundamental unit by Corollary
1.9
). Indeed, a computation shows that the number of improperly primitive forms equals
h^{+}(d)
, while the number of properly primitive forms equals
h^{+}(4d)
. Therefore, the condition
(4)
is equivalent to the condition
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
appearing in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.

3.1
Auxiliary results

We will need the following auxiliary results.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f(X,Y)=aX^{2}+bXY+cY^{2}
be a primitive quadratic form. Then

\gcd\left\{f(m,n):(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\}=1.

Such a lemma is not true for primitive forms with degree
\geq 3
. For example, the cubic primitive form

has image contained in the set of even integers.

Proof.

Let

be a prime, if it exists, such that
p\mid f(m,n)
for all

and

in
\mathbb{Z}

p\mid f(1,0)=a

p\mid f(0,1)=c
and

p\mid\gcd(a,b,c)
, which is contrary to the primitivity of

.
∎

By homogeneity, we deduce

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f(X,Y)=aX^{2}+bXY+cY^{2}
be a quadratic form. Then we have the equality

\gcd(a,b,c)=\gcd\left\{f(m,n):(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\}.

This result directly gives

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

and

be forms such that

. Then we have the equality

In particular, if

is primitive, every form in

is also primitive.

3.4
More on the classes
\sim_{\rm val}

Our next result is the analogue of
[

, Lemma 2.7, Remark 2.8]
, where the proof is given in the context of definite primitive forms representing almost the same primes.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f
and
F
be primitive forms of discriminant
d=D
. Then
f\sim_{\textup{val}}F
if and only if
f\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
. In particular, in any pair of primitive extraordinary forms
(f,F)
with discriminants
d
and
D
we have
d\neq D
and if we impose
|d|<|D|
, we have
d\equiv 5\bmod 8
and
D=4d
. Finally, there is no primitive form which is simultaneously lower and upper extraordinary.

Proof.

It suffices to show that, if
d=D
, then
f\sim_{\textup{val}}F
implies
f\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
. Since
f\sim_{\textup{val}}F
, these forms certainly represent the same primes.

We use the notations introduced in §
3.1
. Suppose that
f
corresponds to the ideal class
[I]
via the correspondence of Theorem
3.4
and suppose that
[I]
corresponds to
\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(R/K)
via the Artin map. Then a prime
p\nmid 2d
is represented by
f
if and only if
\text{Frob}_{p}\in\{\sigma,\sigma^{-1}\}
.

If we assume that
F
corresponds to
[J]
and
\tau
respectively, then the Chebotarev density theorem yields
\{\sigma,\sigma^{-1}\}=\{\tau,\tau^{-1}\}
and therefore
\{I,I^{-1}\}=\{J,J^{-1}\}
. Thus we have that
f\sim_{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
or
f\sim_{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F^{-1}
, where
F^{-1}
is the inverse of
F
. Since a form is always
\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
-equivalent to its inverse, the first part of the lemma follows.

The second part follows from Theorem
3.9
and from the fact that there is no integer

such
4d\equiv 5\bmod 8
.
∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Every equivalence class of
\sim_{\textup{val}}
consists of one or two equivalence classes under
\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}
.

Remark 3.14
.

The fact that the equivalence
[F]_{\rm val}
breaks in one or two

4
An improved version of Schering’s result

In this section, we improve the content of Theorem
3.9
in order to give a characterization of pairs of associated extraordinary forms. Thus we will meet the result of Schering given in Theorem
2.1
, particularly the related comments given in §

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f
and
F
be primitive forms with
|d|\leq|D|
. Then
f\sim_{\textup{val}}F
if and only if one of the following conditions hold

•

f\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}F
or

•

d\equiv 5\bmod 8
,
D=4d
,
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(D)
and
F\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}f^{\dagger}
.

The following corollary characterizes pairs of extraordinary classes when the lower extraordinary form is given.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
d
be an integer such that

and such that
h^{+}(d)=h^{+}(4d)
. Then every primitive quadratic form
f
with discriminant

is lower extraordinary. In particular, every pair
(f,f^{\dagger})
is a pair of associated primitive extraordinary forms.

Proof.

Let
d
satisfy the above hypotheses. Let
f
be a form with discriminant
d
and let
F=f^{\dagger}
be with discriminant
D
. Then, obviously
D=4d
,
F\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}f^{\dagger}
, and
F\not\sim_{\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})}f
by computing the discriminants. By Theorem

we deduce that

∎

\mathcal{C\ell}^{+}(d/4)\cong\mathcal{C\ell}^{+}(d)
. This forces the equality
h^{\star}(d/4)=h^{\star}(d)
.

Now consider the mapping
\Phi
that sends a primitive form
f(X,Y)
of discriminant
d/4
to
f(2X,Y)
. So we have
\Phi(f)=f^{\dagger}
. Since
d/4\equiv 5\bmod 8
, we check that
f(X,Y)=aX^{2}+bXY+cY^{2}
is such that
a
and
c
are odd. Thus
f^{\dagger}
is primitive as well.

We now check that
\Phi
gives a well-defined map from the set of forms (with discriminant
d/4
) up to the action of
\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. So we have to prove that if
f_{1}\sim_{\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}f_{2}
, then
f_{1}^{\dagger}\sim_{\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}f_{2}^{\dagger}
. For this we note that
f_{i}\sim_{\text{val}}f_{i}^{\dagger}
by Theorem
1.2
so
f_{1}^{\dagger}\sim_{\text{val}}f_{2}^{\dagger}
by the assumptions and transitivity. But two forms of the same discriminant that are
\sim_{\text{val}}
–equivalent are
\sim_{\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}
–equivalent by Lemma
3.12
. So
f_{1}^{\dagger}\sim_{\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}f_{2}^{\dagger}
.

Finally, we have to check that
\Phi
is injective (since the sets in consideration have the same cardinality
h^{\star}(d/4)=h^{\star}(d)
, we get the desired bijection). Consider two primitive quadratic forms
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
with discriminant
d/4
such that
f_{1}^{\dagger}\sim_{\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}f_{2}^{\dagger}
. Then we have
f_{1}^{\dagger}\sim_{\text{val}}f_{2}^{\dagger}
. By Theorem
1.3

(2).a
we have
f_{i}\sim_{\rm val}f_{i}^{\dagger}
(
i\in\{1,2\}
). This implies that
f_{1}\sim_{\rm val}f_{2}
. Then
f_{1}\sim_{\text{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}f_{2}
because the forms
f_{i}
have the same discriminant and the same image (Lemma
3.12
). The proof of item
(3).a
is finished.
∎

6
Proof of Corollary
1.10

This corollary is trivial when
d<0
since
h^{+}(d)=h(d)
. For
d\equiv 0
or
1\bmod 4
, let
\epsilon_{d}
be a fundamental unit of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
. Then Corollary
1.10
is a direct consequence of the following proposition.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
d\equiv 1\bmod 4
be a non square integer. Then we have

N(\epsilon_{d})=-1\iff N(\epsilon_{4d})=-1.

Proof.

By the inclusion
\mathcal{O}_{4d}\subset\mathcal{O}_{d}
, we have the implication
N(\epsilon_{4d})=-1\Rightarrow N(\epsilon_{d})=-1
. It remains to prove the converse. For this, one directly checks that
\epsilon_{d}^{3}\in\mathcal{O}_{4d}
by exploiting that
(\mathcal{O}_{d}/2\mathcal{O}_{d})^{\ast}
is a group of order
3
and that
1+2\mathcal{O}_{d}\subset\mathcal{O}_{4d}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.13

In
[
19
]
Stevenhagen investigates some questions posed by Eisenstein
[
7
]
, particularly
concerning the set

that Stevenhagen calls
Eisenstein set
. Since

implies
h^{+}(4d)\in\{h^{+}(d),3h^{+}(d)\}
(see Lemma
3.5
), the set
\mathcal{S}_{5,8}
, that we are interested in, is complementary to the set
\mathcal{E}
in
\mathcal{G}_{5,8}
(see (
1.9
) and (
1.10
) for the notations). Stevenhagen proved the following inequality for the upper density of
\mathcal{E}
:

Lemma 7.1

(
[
19
, Theorem p.261]
)
.

We have

\underset{x\rightarrow\infty}{{\rm lim\sup}}\,\frac{|\{d\in\mathcal{E}:d\leq x\}|}{G_{5,8}(x)}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Considering the complementary set, we obtain

Lemma 7.2
.

As
x
tends to infinity, one has

S_{5,8}(x)\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-o(1)\right)\cdot{G_{5,8}(x)}.

This proves (
1.11
) and the proof of Theorem
1.13
is complete.

In
[
19
, Conjecture 1.4]
Stevenhagen conjectures that the Eisenstein set
\mathcal{E}
has density
1/3
in
\mathcal{G}_{5,8}
. This is a natural hypothesis since for the fundamental unit
\epsilon_{d}
, there are exactly three possible images
(a,b)
modulo
2
(with the notations of Lemma
3.6
), and only one of these images corresponds to
d\in\mathcal{E}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.14

We are now interested in
d
satisfying the congruence
d\equiv 5\bmod 8
, and the condition
h^{+}(d)\neq h^{+}(4d)
. By Lemma
3.5
, this last condition is equivalent to
h^{+}(4d)=3h^{+}(d)
. If we impose the condition that
d
is squarefree, we recognize the Eisenstein set
\mathcal{E}
as defined in (
7.1
). In
[
19
, Theorem 1.6]
Stevenhagen proves that the Eisenstein set
\mathcal{E}
is infinite, under the form of the asymptotic inequality

\left|\{d\leq x:d\in\mathcal{E}\}\right|\gg\sqrt{x}.

This gives Theorem
1.14
.
