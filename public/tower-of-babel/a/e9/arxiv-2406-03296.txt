Multi-relational Network Autoregression Model with Latent Group Structures


Abstract
Multi-relational networks among entities are frequently observed in the era of big data. Quantifying the effects of multiple networks have attracted significant research interest recently. In this work, we model multiple network effects through an autoregressive framework for tensor-valued time series. To characterize the potential heterogeneity of the networks and handle the high dimensionality of the time series data simultaneously, we assume a separate group structure for entities in each network and estimate all group memberships in a data-driven fashion. Specifically, we propose a group tensor network autoregression (GTNAR) model, which assumes that within each network, entities in the same group share the same set of model parameters, and the parameters differ across networks. An iterative algorithm is developed to estimate the model parameters and the latent group memberships simultaneously. Theoretically, we show that the group-wise parameters and group memberships can be consistently estimated when the group numbers are correctly- or possibly over-specified. An information criterion for group number estimation of each network is also provided to consistently select the group numbers. Lastly, we implement the method on a Yelp dataset to illustrate the usefulness of the method. KEY WORDS: Multi-relational networks; Latent group; Tensor-valued time series; Network autoregression.

Introduction

With the world becoming increasingly connected, studying network effects has become an important research topic in various disciplines, including economics, finance and many others.
The primary focus of our study is to analyze the network effects inherent in high dimensional time series observed over multiple networks.
The existing literature has seen notable progress in the study of time series within a single network.
For instance,
Zhu et al. 2017
introduce a network autoregression model for investigating time series within large social networks.

Chen et al. 2023
propose a community network vector autoregression model for the high-dimensional time series.
In addition,
Ma et al. 2023
delve into a sparse spatio-temporal autoregression model and estimated the model by profiling and bagging.

Jiang et al. 2023
present a first-order autoregressive model for dynamic network processes.
In comparison to conventional high-dimensional time series models
(
Walden and Serroukh 2002
;
Leng and Tang 2012
;
Zhou 2014
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Chang et al. 2023
)
, the network autoregressive model distinguishes itself by offering enhanced parameter interpretability, thus offering more insights into the intricacies of network dynamics.

In real-world scenarios, entities within a population commonly establish connections across multiple networks, often referred to as multi-relational or multi-layer networks. Recently, there has been significant interest in investigating these networks collectively, as seen in works like
Lei et al. 2020
,
Zhang et al. 2020
,
Jing et al. 2021
,
MacDonald et al. 2022
,
Ma and Nandy 2023
.
While the majority of these studies focus on identifying community structures within multi-layer networks, there is also a considerable interest in quantifying the impacts of multi-relational network effects on various research objectives.
For example,
Emch et al. 2016
investigate the joint effects of spatial and social networks on disease transmission.

Chen et al. 2017
propose the utilization of network metrics from various social networks to predict the adoption of new products in marketing research.
Besides,
Corradini et al. 2021
investigate the influence of multi-dimensional social networks on negative reviews posted on Yelp.
In the spatial data analysis,
Lee and Yu 2012
consider spatial panel models with time-varying network structures, and
Zhang and Yu 2018
propose a model selection and averaging procedure for multiple weighting matrices in the spatial autoregressive model.
Although these models are valuable in empirical research, a critical
gap remains in
the availability of rigorous statistical models with the capability of providing valid statistical inferences on multiple network effects, and it is our intention to fill this gap.

The objective of our work is to investigate time series indexed across multi-relational networks. To offer a more lucid representation of our methodology, we demonstrate it through a motivating dataset collected from Yelp (https://www.yelp.com/). Yelp serves as a prominent review platform for various businesses, including restaurants, local retailers, entertainment establishments, and more. It also functions as a social platform where users can share information and their personal experiences.
The dataset covers the period from 2010 to 2018 and is collected from five North American cities (i.e., Charlotte, Las Vegas, Phoenix, Scottsdale and Toronto), and comprises four main categories of information: user data (e.g., user registration time on Yelp), user-friend relationships, business information (including spatial location), and user reviews of businesses.
For example, Figure

In this case, the user gave the restaurant a five-star rating, and their review received 18 tags from other users, including 8 “useful”, 3 “funny”, and 7 “cool” tags. Overall, the restaurant has accumulated 1611 reviews.

The first challenge we encounter when analyzing this dataset is that neither users nor districts can be considered isolated units. As a result, it becomes imperative to model the

’s in a collective manner. Specifically, users form a social network, while districts establish a spatial network that fosters substantial interactions among their respective network members. These interactions, in turn, significantly influence the outcome variable

when considered jointly. For example,
Tiwari and Richards 2016
find that peer social networks play a highly effective role in influencing restaurant preferences within social circles of friends. The social network analysis conducted on Yelp data by
Fe 2023
suggests that social network friends are 64% more likely to visit the same restaurant when compared to non-friends.
Sun and Paule 2017
discover significant spatial effects on ratings across various categories of Yelp venues and
Gan et al. 2021
investigate the spatial network effects on the tourism economy. However, these studies are primarily empirical in nature and lack strong theoretical foundations in statistics. Furthermore, they tend to concentrate solely on the impact of a single network, rather than considering multiple network effects jointly. For our motivating example, Figure

Both network effects contribute jointly to the outcome variable. Therefore, the first challenge we intend to address is how to construct a multivariate time series model that can rigorously quantify the impact of multiple network effects for data similar to our Yelp review dataset.

The second challenge we encounter in the Yelp review dataset relates to the heterogeneity among members in both social and spatial networks. For instance, users with diverse posting habits and socioeconomic backgrounds may exhibit varying review patterns and distinct interactive relationships with their friends. Similarly, restaurants located in different spatial regions, such as the central business district or other areas, may experience different levels of popularity, leading to varying degrees of spatial spillover effects
(
Koschinsky 2009
, see, e.g.,)
.
For our motivating example, we conducted a preliminary regression analysis with the aggregated users’ comment volumes (log transformed) as the response variable, denoted as
Y_{it}=\sum_{j}Y_{ij,t}
. Two covariates were included: the lagged response (
Y_{i(t-1)}
) and the user’s duration since registration (i.e., the number of months after joining Yelp by the
(t-1)

. An evident heterogeneous pattern is observed among the users’ coefficients. To tackle this challenge, we adopt the approach proposed in

Let
\mathbf{A}^{(1)}=(a^{(1)}_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{1}}
and
\mathbf{A}^{(2)}=(a^{(2)}_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times N_{2}}
represent the adjacency matrices characterizing the social and spatial networks, respectively.
Specifically,
a_{ij}^{(1)}=1
implies that the
i
th user follows the
j
th user, while
a_{ij}^{(1)}=0
otherwise. Similarly,
a_{ij}^{(2)}=1

th district is a spatial neighbor of the
j
th district, while
a_{ij}^{(2)}=0
otherwise.
We follow the convention by setting
a_{ii}^{(1)}=0
and
a_{jj}^{(2)}=0
for
1\leq i\leq N_{1}
and
1\leq j\leq N_{2}
.
We assume the existence of
G_{1}
groups in the social network and
G_{2}
groups in the spatial network. The group membership for the
i
th node in the social network is denoted as
g_{i}^{(1)}
(
1\leq g_{i}^{(1)}\leq G_{1}
), and the group membership for the
j
th node in the spatial network is denoted as

(
1\leq g_{j}^{(2)}\leq G_{2}
). We propose the following model with a two-way group structure:

\begin{split}Y_{ij,t}&=\underbrace{\lambda_{g_{i}^{(1)}}^{(1)}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}\frac{a^{(1)}_{ik}}{n_{1i}}Y_{kj,(t-1)}}_{\text{\rm Social Network main effect}}+\underbrace{\lambda_{g_{j}^{(2)}}^{(2)}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{2}}\frac{a^{(2)}_{kj}}{n_{2j}}Y_{ik,(t-1)}}_{\text{\rm Spatial Network main effect}}\\
&\hskip 130.0002pt+\underbrace{\alpha_{g_{i}^{(1)}g_{j}^{(2)}}Y_{ij,(t-1)}}_{\text{\rm Self-momentum}}+\underbrace{{\bf x}_{it}^{(1)\top}{\bm{\zeta}}^{(1)}_{g_{i}^{(1)}}+{\bf x}_{jt}^{(2)\top}{\bm{\zeta}}^{(2)}_{g_{j}^{(2)}}}_{\text{\rm Covariate effects}}+\varepsilon_{ij,t},\end{split}

(1.1)

where
n_{1i}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}a^{(1)}_{ik}
,
n_{2j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{2}}a^{(2)}_{kj}
,
{\bf x}^{(1)}_{it}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
{\bf x}^{(2)}_{jt}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
are exogenous covariate vectors of finite dimensions associated with the
i
th user and
j
th district, respectively. Besides,
\varepsilon_{ij,t}
represents independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) white noise with
E(\varepsilon_{ij,t})=0
and
\hbox{var}(\varepsilon_{ij,t})=\sigma^{2}
. For identifiability, it is required that
\sum_{g^{(1)}=1}^{G_{1}}\zeta_{g^{(1)},1}^{(1)}=0
when both intercepts are included in
{\bf x}^{(1)}_{it}
and
{\bf x}^{(2)}_{it}
, where
\zeta^{(1)}_{g_{i}^{(1)},1}
(the first element of
{\bm{\zeta}}^{(1)}_{g_{i}^{(1)}}
) represents the intercept for
{\bf x}^{(1)}_{it}
.

The first term in (
1.1
), i.e.,
\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}(a^{(1)}_{ik}/n_{1i})Y_{kj,(t-1)}
, represents the average number of reviews (
\hbox{log}(1+x)
-transformed)
by user
i
’s following friends on the restaurants in
district
j
in the previous quarter. Consequently,
\lambda_{g_{i}^{(1)}}^{(1)}

quantifies the influence of following friends on user
i
’s attitude
towards district
j
and encapsulates a social network main effect. On
the other hand, the second term in (
1.1
), i.e.,
\sum_{k=1}^{N_{2}}(a^{(2)}_{kj}/n_{2j})Y_{ik,(t-1)}
, calculates the average log
number of reviews by the
i
th user on districts that are connected with the
j
th district in the previous quarter. Thus,
\lambda_{g_{j}^{(2)}}^{(2)}
signifies how user’s review count towards district
j
is influenced by his/her reviews towards the districts connected with district
j
, and can be interpreted as the spatial network main effect.
Additionally,
\alpha_{g_{i}^{(1)}g_{j}^{(2)}}
represents the self-driven time effect
for the
(i,j)
th time series, quantifying the momentum effect of the
review activity by the
i
th user towards the
j
th district in
the previous quarter. A higher value of
\alpha_{g_{i}^{(1)}g_{j}^{(2)}}
suggests a
greater level of loyalty of user
i
to district
j
. Finally,
{\bm{\zeta}}_{g_{i}^{(1)}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
{\bm{\zeta}}_{g_{j}^{(2)}}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
are external covariate effects at the user and district levels, enhancing the model’s capacity to account for user and district heterogeneity in the data. By evaluating the significance of
\lambda_{g^{(1)}}^{(1)}
’s and
\lambda_{g^{(2)}}^{(2)}
’s, one can examine the presence of social and spatial network main effects while controlling for other factors in Model (
1.1
).

Our framework for multiple networks further extends the two-network model (
1.1
) to multi-relational networks given by Model (
2.1
) in Section
2.2
, which we refer to as the Group Tensor Network Autoregression (GTNAR) model. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows.
First, we introduce a highly interpretable network autoregression model for high-dimensional multivariate time series indexed by multiple networks. Second, to account for network heterogeneity, we allow separate group structures on related networks.
Third, we establish estimation consistency for both model parameters and group memberships, not only when the numbers of groups are correct, but also over-specified. Simultaneously estimating group memberships for multiple networks presents significant challenges compared to existing literature, which typically considers at most one network
(
Zhu et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. We develop new theoretical tools to address this challenge.
Lastly, we develop a selection criterion to consistently select the true group numbers and establish asymptotic normality when the group numbers are correctly specified. Our theoretical framework enables rigorous tests for multiple network effects, which are crucial in various research disciplines.

In Section

Section
6

Finally, Section

Suppose there are
q

\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{A}^{(q)}

l

\mathbf{A}^{(l)}=(a_{ij}^{(l)})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}\times N_{l}}

Specifically,
a_{ij}^{(l)}=1
implies that the
i
th node is connected with the
j
th node in the
l
th network, while
a_{ij}^{(l)}=0
otherwise. We follow the convention by setting
a_{i_{l}i_{l}}^{(l)}=0
for
1\leq i_{l}\leq N_{l}
and
1\leq l\leq q
.
We assume that there exists
G_{l}
groups in the
l
th network, and the group membership for the
i
th node in this network is denoted as
g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}
(
1\leq g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}\leq G_{l}
). As a result, each network has its own latent group structure among the

\mathcal{G}_{l}=(g_{i}^{(l)}:1\leq i\leq N_{l})

.
Under such a model framework, the response variables of interest constitute a tensor-valued time series, denoted by
\mathcal{Y}_{t}=(Y_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},t})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times\cdots\times N_{q}}
, with the following model structure

\displaystyle Y_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},t}=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\underbrace{\lambda_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{l}}\frac{a_{i_{l}k}^{(l)}}{n_{li_{l}}}Y_{i_{1}...i_{l-1}ki_{l+1}...i_{q},(t-1)}}_{\text{\rm The $l$th Network main effect}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\alpha_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)}...g_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}Y_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},(t-1)}}_{\text{\rm Self-momentum}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{q}\underbrace{{\bf x}_{i_{l}t}^{(l)\top}{\bm{\zeta}}_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}}_{\text{\rm The $l$th covariate effects}}+\varepsilon_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},t},

(2.1)

where
n_{li_{l}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}a_{i_{l}k}^{(l)}
,
{\bf x}_{i_{l}t}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{l}}
represents exogenous covariates associated with the
i_{l}
th member in the
l
th network, and
\varepsilon_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},t}
denotes the white noise.
For convenience, we define
\mathcal{G}_{l}=(g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}:1\leq i_{l}\leq N_{l})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}
and denote
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{G}_{l}:1\leq l\leq q\}
.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{g}^{(l)}=\{i_{l}:g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}=g\}
and further denote

Denote

as the group membership indices excluding the

th network,
and denote the group index

as a function of

, which takes values from

Define

\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\sum_{l=1}^{q}(\mathcal{Y}_{t-1}\times_{l}\mathbf{W}^{(l)})\times_{l}{\bf L}^{(l)}+\mathcal{A}\odot\mathcal{Y}_{t-1}+\sum_{l=1}^{q}{\bf 1}_{N_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf 1}_{N_{l-1}}\circ\bm{\beta}^{(l)}_{X_{l},t}\circ{\bf 1}_{N_{l+1}}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf 1}_{N_{q}}+\mathcal{E}_{t},

2.3
Comparisons with Existing Literature

The utilization of latent group structures to model heterogeneous data
has a well-established history in panel data analysis. For instance,

Bonhomme and Manresa 2015
and
Bester and Hansen 2016
introduce
grouped linear panel models with time-varying fixed effects and
individual fixed effects, respectively.
Su et al. 2016

introduce a Classifier Lasso (C-Lasso) estimator for panel models,
and more recently,
Liu et al. 2020
explore estimation
and inference in cases of possible over-specification of the group
number. There have also been recent efforts to leverage latent group
structures in modeling time series data within a single network, see, e.g.,

Zhu and Pan 2020
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2023
and
Liu et al. 2024
. However,
these works have predominantly focused on time series observed on
a single network, and to the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to tackle time series indexed by multiple networks with distinct group structures.
This extension provides a novel autoregression framework that dynamically constructs input features based on various network structures, facilitating statistical inferences on different types of network effects.
Moreover, addressing the theoretical challenges involved in extending from a single network to multiple networks is non-trivial due to the interactions between different group structures, necessitating the development of new theoretical tools.
In summary, our proposed framework equips researchers with valuable tools for analyzing data collected in complex network environments, closely aligning with many real-world applications.

Another closely related line of research is the high dimensional
vector autoregression (VAR) model
(
Davis et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Miao et al. 2023
)
. By stacking elements in the tensor

\mathcal{Y}_{t}=(Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{q}}
as a vector, the GTNAR
model (
2.2
) can be re-written as a VAR model with a
dimension of
\prod_{l}N_{l}
.
This allows the application of existing techniques for high-dimensional VAR models.
While this formulation imposes fewer assumptions on the model coefficients, it leads to a much larger number of parameters that need to be estimated, on the order of
\prod_{l}(N_{l}^{2})
, as compared to
\sum_{l}G_{l}(p_{l}+1)+\prod_{l}G_{l}
(when fixing the group memberships) for the GTNAR model.
Even with commonly used sparsity regularization, the estimation variance of model parameters is likely to be much higher than that of the GTNAR model when the data is generated from the GTNAR model.
For instance,
Zhu et al. 2023
demonstrate that the estimation error based on the regularized sparse VAR model is much larger than that of the Network VAR model when the data is generated from the latter.
Additionally, the coefficients estimated in the general VAR model lack clear interpretations and provide limited insights into network effects.
This drawback has also been recognized in existing literature and has motivated the development of matrix-valued or tensor-valued autoregressive models
(
Chen et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
.

A third line of related research is the Spatial Dynamic Panel Data (SDPD) models
(
Yu et al. 2008
;
Lee and Yu 2014
;
Kuersteiner and Prucha 2020
)
. For example,
Lee and Yu 2014
explore a model with the formulation
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\lambda\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}_{t}+\gamma\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\alpha\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\bm{\xi}^{\top}{\bf x}_{it}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
, where
\lambda
and
\gamma
represent network effects,
\alpha
signifies the momentum effect, and
\bm{\xi}
accounts for covariates’ impact. These models primarily address only spatial network effects without considering the heterogeneity of spatial locations. In contrast, our proposed model aims to jointly model multiple network effects while accommodating the heterogeneity of network nodes. It is noteworthy that, unlike SDPD models, our framework does not include the cross-sectional spatial network effect (captured by
\lambda
). This aspect could be a promising avenue for future research. Additionally, extending our framework to incorporate time-varying networks and common shocks, as explored in
Kuersteiner and Prucha 2020
, would also be an interesting research topic.

Lastly, the proposed GTNAR model has strong connections to recent research in matrix-valued and tensor-valued time series models
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Bi et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen and Fan 2023
;
Chang et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
.
For instance, corresponding to our model (
2.1
) with
q=2
,
Chen et al. 2021
introduce a matrix autoregression (MAR) model to model
{\bf Y}_{t}
, which uses a bilinear autoregressive model of the form
\mathbf{Y}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y}_{t-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}+\mathbf{E}_{t}
. Here,
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
represent unknown autoregressive coefficient matrices, and
\mathbf{E}_{t}
is the noise matrix.
In contrast, the proposed GTNAR model can be expressed in an additive form as
\mathbf{Y}_{t}={\bf C}{\bf Y}_{t-1}+{\bf Y}_{t-1}{\bf D}^{\top}+{\bf A}\circ{\bf Y}_{t-1}+\mathbf{E}_{t}
when
{\bf Y}_{t}
is a matrix, where
{\bf C}={\bf L}\mathbf{W}_{1}
,
{\bf D}={\bf G}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{\top}
, and
{\bf A}
is defined in (
2.2
).
This highlights that the GTNAR model differs from the matrix-valued autoregressive model of
Chen et al. 2021
. Importantly, the GTNAR model further explores the (heterogeneous) network structures present in both the rows and columns of the response variable matrix when
q=2
, resulting in a highly interpretable model as illustrated in Model (
2.1
).

For tensor-valued times series,

Chen et al. 2022
introduce a general framework of factor models for tensor-valued time series.

Han et al. 2021
propose a factor model and a high order projection estimator to analyze high-dimensional dynamic tensor time series.

Zhou et al. 2023
propose a tensor regression model, whose response is partially observed dynamic tensors, and they estimate the model by a nonconvex alternating updating algorithm.

Chen and Lam 2024
introduce a pre-averaging procedure, allowing for a spectrum of factor strengths, as well as the weak factors when both cross-sectional and serial correlations are present.
Besides,
Wang et al. 2024
propose a low-rank tensor autoregression (LRTAR) model, which folds the high-dimensional transition matrix into a low-rank one through Tucker decomposition. However, these existing works do not utilize the network structure information in each tensor dimension as the proposed GTNAR model does, and therefore do not offer direct statistical inference on these network effects.

3
Model Estimation

In this section, we discuss the estimation of the GTNAR model (
2.2
). For the network corresponding to the
l
th tensor dimension, there exist
G_{l}
groups for the network nodes, denoted by
g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]
. For each group, the group-level parameters are denoted by
{\bm{\theta}}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}=(\lambda_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)},{\bm{\zeta}}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{l}+1}
. The collection of network-specific parameters is then denoted as
{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}=({\bm{\theta}}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}:g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}])\in\mathbb{R}^{G_{l}(p_{l}+1)}
, leading to the collection of
\bm{\xi}=({\bm{\theta}}^{(1)\top},\cdots,{\bm{\theta}}^{(q)\top},\mathrm{vec}({\bm{\alpha}})^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l}G_{l}(p_{l}+1)+\prod_{l}G_{l}}
for all
q
tensor dimensions. Finally, the collection of self-momentum parameters is denoted as
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}})_{g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]}\in\mathbb{R}^{G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{q}}
.

To simultaneously estimate the model parameters and the group memberships, we aim to minimize the following least square objective function:

\displaystyle Q({\bm{\xi},\mathcal{G}})=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{q}=1}^{N_{q}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(Y_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},t}

\displaystyle-\sum_{l=1}^{q}\lambda_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{l}}\frac{a^{(l)}_{i_{l}k}}{n_{li_{l}}}Y_{i_{1}...i_{l-1}ki_{l+1}...i_{q},(t-1)}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)}...g_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}Y_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},(t-1)}-\sum_{l=1}^{q}{\bf x}_{i_{l}t}^{(l)\top}{\bm{\zeta}}_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}\Big)^{2}.

(3.1)

We first discuss the estimation when the group memberships
\mathcal{G}
are given. In this case, the minimization of (
3
) is equivalent to solving

\frac{\partial Q(\bm{\xi},\mathcal{G})}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}}=\mathbf{0},~~\frac{\partial Q(\bm{\xi},\mathcal{G})}{\partial\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}}=0

for all
g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]
and
l\in[q]
.
Recall that
N_{lg^{(l)}}=|\mathcal{R}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}|

and let

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathrm{vec}\big\{(\mathcal{Y}_{t-1}\times_{l}\mathbf{W}^{(l)})_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}\big\},{\bf 1}_{N_{1g^{(1)}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{(l)})^{(\mathcal{R}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)},\cdot)}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bf 1}_{N_{qg^{(q)}}}\Big),

(3.2)

where
\mathbf{X}_{t}^{(l)}=({\bf x}_{1t}^{(l)},\cdots,{\bf x}_{N_{l}t}^{(l)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}\times p_{l}}
,
and that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Y}_{t-1}\times_{l}\mathbf{W}^{(l)})_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i_{l}=1}^{N_{l}}Y_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{l},\cdots,i_{q},t-1}(a^{(l)}_{s,i_{l}}/n_{ls})\Big)_{i_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{g^{(1)}},\cdots,s\in\mathcal{R}_{g^{(l)}},\cdots,i_{q}\in\mathcal{R}_{g^{(q)}}}.

Then one can verify that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{Q(\bm{\xi},\mathcal{G})}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}}=\Big(\sum_{t,g^{-(l)}}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)\top}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)}\Big){\bm{\theta}}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle-\sum_{t,g^{-(l)}}\Big\{\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)\top}\Big(\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}-\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},(t-1)}\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}-\sum_{m\neq l}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(m)}{\bm{\theta}}_{g^{(m)}}^{(m)}\Big)\Big\},

(3.3)

where
\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}=\mathrm{vec}\Big\{\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\big(\mathcal{R}_{g^{(1)}}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathcal{R}_{g^{(q)}}^{(q)}\big)}\Big\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1g^{(1)}}\cdots N_{qg^{(q)}}}
,
and the summation
\sum_{g^{-(l)}}
is the simplified notation for
\sum_{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(l-1)},g^{(l+1)},\cdots g^{(q)}}
.
Furthermore, it holds that

\hskip-15.00002pt\frac{\partial{Q(\bm{\xi},\mathcal{G})}}{\partial\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}}=\sum_{t}\|\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},(t-1)}\|^{2}\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}-\sum_{t}\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},(t-1)}^{\top}\Big(\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}-\sum_{l=1}^{q}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)}{\bm{\theta}}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}\Big).

(3.4)

Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) define a system of linear equations, whose solutions have the form
{\widehat{\bm{\xi}}={\bf M}^{-1}{\bf b}}
with
{\bf M}\in\mathbb{R}^{\{\sum_{l}G_{l}(p_{l}+1)+\prod_{l}G_{l}\}\times\{\sum_{l}G_{l}(p_{l}+1)+\prod_{l}G_{l}\}}
and
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l}G_{l}(p_{l}+1)+\prod_{l}G_{l}}
, where

\displaystyle{\bf M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}{\bf M}^{(1)}&{\bf M}^{(12)}&{\bf M}^{(13)}&\cdots&{\bf M}^{(1q)}&{\bf M}^{(1\alpha)}\\
{\bf M}^{(21)}&{\bf M}^{(2)}&{\bf M}^{(23)}&\cdots&{\bf M}^{(2q)}&{\bf M}^{(2\alpha)}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
{\bf M}^{(q1)}&{\bf M}^{(q2)}&\cdots&\cdots&{\bf M}^{(q)}&{\bf M}^{(q\alpha)}\\
{\bf M}^{(1\alpha)\top}&{\bf M}^{(2\alpha)\top}&\cdots&\cdots&{\bf M}^{(q\alpha)}&{\bf M}^{(\alpha)}\end{array}\right),~~~{\bf b}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf b}^{(1)}\\
\cdots\\
{\bf b}^{(q)}\\
{\bf b}^{\alpha}\end{array}\right).

Specifically, the terms in (
3
) have the expressions,

\displaystyle{\bf M}^{(l)}_{g^{(l)}}=\sum_{t,g^{-(l)}}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)\top}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)},~~~{\bf M}^{(l)}=\hbox{diag}\big({\bf M}^{(l)}_{g^{(l)}}:g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{G_{l}(p_{l}+1)\times G_{l}(p_{l}+1)},

\displaystyle{\bf M}^{(lm)}_{g^{(l)}g^{(m)}}=\sum_{t,g^{-(l,m)}}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)\top}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(m)},

\displaystyle{\bf M}^{(lm)}=\big({\bf M}^{(lm)}_{g^{(l)}g^{(m)}}:g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}],g^{(m)}\in[G_{m}]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{G_{l}(p_{l}+1)\times G_{m}(p_{m}+1)},

\displaystyle{\bf M}^{(l\alpha)}_{g^{(l)}\mathcal{I}_{g^{(l)^{\prime}}g^{-(l)}}}=\sum_{t}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)\top}\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},(t-1)}I(g^{(l)}=g^{(l)^{\prime}})~\text{with}~\mathcal{I}_{g^{(l)^{\prime}}g^{-(l)}}=g^{(l)^{\prime}}+\sum_{m\neq l}((g^{(m)}-1)G_{l}),

\displaystyle{\bf M}^{(l\alpha)}=\Big({\bf M}^{(l\alpha)}_{g^{(l)}\mathcal{I}_{g^{(l)^{\prime}}g^{-(l)}}}:g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}],\mathcal{I}_{g^{(l)^{\prime}}g^{-(l)}}\in\Big[\prod_{l}G_{l}\Big]\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{G_{l}(p_{l}+1)\times(\prod_{l}G_{l})},

\displaystyle{\bf M}^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)}},\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)^{\prime}},\cdots g^{(q)^{\prime}}}}=\sum_{t}\|\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},(t-1)}\|^{2}I\{(g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)})=(g^{(1)^{\prime}},\cdots,g^{(q)^{\prime}})\},

\displaystyle{\bf M}^{\alpha}=\Big({\bf M}^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)}},\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)^{\prime}},\cdots g^{(q)^{\prime}}}}:\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)}}\in\Big[\prod_{l}G_{l}\Big],\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)^{\prime}},\cdots g^{(q)^{\prime}}}\in\Big[\prod_{l}G_{l}\Big]\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{(\prod_{l}G_{l})\times(\prod_{l}G_{l})},

\displaystyle{\bf b}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}=\sum_{t,g^{-(l)}}\mathbb{X}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t}^{(l)\top}\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t},~~~{\bf b}^{(l)}=\big({\bf b}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)}:g^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{G_{l}(p_{l}+1)},

\displaystyle{\bf b}_{\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)}}}^{\alpha}=\sum_{t}\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},(t-1)}^{\top}\mathbb{Y}_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)},t},~~~{\bf b}^{\alpha}=\Big({\bf b}_{\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)}}}^{\alpha}:\mathcal{I}_{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)}}\in\Big[\prod_{l}G_{l}\Big]\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{l}G_{l}},

where
\sum_{t,g^{-(l,m)}}
is the simplified notation for
\sum_{\{g^{(1)},\cdots,g^{(q)}\}\setminus\{g^{(l)},g^{(m)}\}}
.

Subsequently, given the estimated parameters
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
, we update the
group memberships
\mathcal{G}_{l}
from
l=1
to
q
iteratively.
Specifically, given
\bm{\xi}
and
\mathcal{G}_{-l}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{l}\}
, the
\mathcal{G}_{l}
is updated by

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{i_{l}}^{(l)}\in\arg\min_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]}\sum_{i_{-l}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(Y_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},t}

\displaystyle-\sum_{l=1}^{q}\lambda_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{l}}\frac{a^{(l)}_{i_{l}k}}{n_{li_{l}}}Y_{i_{1}...i_{l-1}ki_{l+1}...i_{q},(t-1)}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}...g_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}Y_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{q},(t-1)}-\sum_{l=1}^{q}{\bf x}_{i_{l}t}^{(l)\top}{\bm{\zeta}}_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}\Big)^{2},

(3.14)

where
\sum_{i_{-l}}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{l-1}=1}^{N_{l-1}}\sum_{i_{l+1}=1}^{N_{l+1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{q}=1}^{N_{q}}
.

We summarize the algorithm in Algorithm
1
, which consists of iterations of two major steps. The first step update the estimated parameters given the group memberships, while the second step updates group memberships given the parameter estimates. Each step can be efficiently computed due to the simple analytical forms.
Moreover, Algorithm
in Appendix
provides reasonable initial estimators for Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Estimation of the GTNAR Model

1:

Input:

\{\mathcal{Y}_{t},\mathbf{X}_{t}^{(l)},\mathbf{W}^{(l)},G_{l}\}
for
1\leq l\leq q
.

2:

Obtain initial group memberships
\mathcal{G}_{l}^{[0]}
according to Algorithm
. Let
\{\bm{\xi}^{[k]},\mathcal{G}_{l}^{[k]}
be the estimators and memberships in the
k
th iteration.

3:

Repeat
Step 1
and
Step 2
for
k=1,2,\cdots

until convergence.

Step 1.
Given
\{\mathcal{G}_{l}^{[k-1]}\}
, calculate

\bm{\xi}^{[k-1]}=({\bf M}^{[k-1]})^{-1}{\bf b}^{[k-1]}
, where
{\bf M}^{[k-1]}
and
{\bf b}^{[k-1]}
are obtained from
(
3
) with
\mathcal{G}_{l}^{[k-1]}
specified.

Step 2.
Given
\bm{\xi}^{[k-1]}
, update the memberships by (
3.14
) as
\mathcal{G}_{l}^{[k]}=(g_{i_{l}}^{(l)[k]}:1\leq i_{l}\leq N_{l})^{\top}
.

4:

Output:
Final estimator and memberships:

\widehat{\bm{\xi}}=\bm{\xi}^{[K]}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}=\{\mathcal{G}_{l}^{[K]}:l\in[q]\}
.
Here
K
is the final number of iteration rounds.

3.1
Selection of Group Numbers

To simplify the notations, denote
\underline{G}=(G_{1},\cdots,G_{q})^{\top}
, and denote the corresponding true group numbers as
\underline{G}_{0}
.
We focus our later theoretical analysis on the case that
G_{l}
is finite for
1\leq l\leq q
.
Write
{\widehat{\bm{\xi}}(\underline{G})},\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\underline{G})
as the estimators when the group numbers are specified as
\underline{G}
. Then, we estimate
\underline{G}_{0}
by utilizing the following information criterion:

\displaystyle\textup{QIC}(\underline{G})=\hbox{log}\{Q({\widehat{\bm{\xi}}(\underline{G})},\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(\underline{G}))\}+\lambda(\underline{G}),

(3.15)

where
Q(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
3
), and
\lambda(\underline{G})
is a penalty function.
Then we estimate the group numbers by

\widehat{\underline{G}}\in\arg\min_{\underline{G}}\textup{QIC}(\underline{G})
.
In practice, we specify
\lambda(\underline{G})=\kappa(\sum_{l}G_{l})
, in which
\kappa
is a tuning parameter.
Our theoretical analysis shows that if

T^{-1/2}(m+\sum_{l}\hbox{log}N_{l})\ll\kappa\ll c_{\textup{gap}}c_{\pi}^{q}/(\prod_{l}G_{l})
, the QIC can consistently select

\underline{G}_{0}
.
Here
c_{\textup{gap}}
and
c_{\pi}
are group structures related values depending on the strength of model signals,
which will be define in our theoretical analysis in Section
4
.
In our numerical study, we specify
\kappa=\{C(\hbox{log}T)T^{1/8}\}^{-1}
with
C=40
, which achieves reliable finite sample performances in all of our numerical studies.

4
Theoretical Properties

4.1
Estimation Consistency

Define
\bm{\Theta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}=({\bm{\theta}}_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)}}^{(1)\top},\cdots,{\bm{\theta}}_{g_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}^{(q)\top},\alpha_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots g_{i_{q}}^{(l)}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l}(p_{l}+1)+1}
and

\bm{\Theta}=(\bm{\Theta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}:i_{l}\in[N_{l}],1\leq l\leq q)

as a tensor of dimension
N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{q}\times\{\sum_{l}(p_{l}+1)+1\}
.
With
\bm{\Theta}
we can rewrite the loss function (
3
) as follows

\displaystyle Q(\bm{\Theta})=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{q}=1}^{N_{q}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}-\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}\big)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{q}=1}^{N_{q}}Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}(\bm{\Theta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}),

(4.1)

where

\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}}w^{(1)}_{i_{1}k}Y_{ki_{2}\cdots i_{q},(t-1)},{\bf x}_{i_{1}t}^{(1)\top},\cdots,\sum_{k=1}^{N_{q}}w^{(q)}_{i_{q}k}Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{(q-1)}k,(t-1)},{\bf x}_{i_{q}t}^{(q)\top},Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},(t-1)}\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l}(p_{l}+1)+1}.

Denote by
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}=\big(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}=(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\widehat{g}_{i_{1}}^{(1)}}^{(1)\top},\cdots,\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\widehat{g}_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}^{(q)\top},\widehat{\alpha}_{\widehat{g}_{i_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots\widehat{g}_{i_{q}}^{(q)}})^{\top}\big)
the global minimizer of
Q(\bm{\Theta})
with the estimated group memberships
\widehat{g}_{i_{l}}^{(l)}~(i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[q])
,
we define
a pseudo distance as follows

\displaystyle d(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}},\bm{\Theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i_{l}=1}^{N_{l}}\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\widehat{g}_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}-{\bm{\theta}}_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\prod_{l}N_{l}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{q}=1}^{N_{q}}|\widehat{\alpha}_{\widehat{g}_{i_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots\widehat{g}_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}-\alpha_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots g_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}|^{2}.

(4.2)

Intuitively,
d(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}},\bm{\Theta})

measures the average distance between
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
and
\bm{\Theta}
.
Next, we establish the consistency of
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
in this pseudo distance, for which we require the following definition and assumptions.

Definition 1
.

(
K
-Convex concentration)
Let
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector.
If for every 1-Lipschitz convex function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

E|\varphi({\bf x})|<\infty
and for every
t>0
, it holds that

P\Big(\Big|\varphi({\bf x})-E\{\varphi({\bf x})\}\Big|\geq t\Big)\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/K^{2}),

then
{\bf x}
is said to have the
K
-convex concentration property.

Assumption 1
.

(Parameter Space)
The parameter satisfies that
\|\bm{\Theta}\|_{\max}<\infty
.

Assumption 2
.

(Convexity)

Let
\bm{\Sigma}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}=E(\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}^{\top})
. We assume that

\tau_{\min}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\min_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{q}}\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}})

is a positive constant.

Assumption 3
.

(Distribution of Noise Term)

Assume
\varepsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}
is i.i.d across
i_{l}\in[N_{l}]
for all
l\in[q]
and
t\in[T]
.
In addition,
\varepsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}
is a zero-mean sub-Gaussian
variable with a scale factor
0<\sigma<\infty
, i.e.,

E\{\exp(u\varepsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t})\}\leq\exp(\sigma^{2}u^{2}/2)
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\varepsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}
be independent of
\{\mathcal{Y}_{s-1},\mathbf{X}_{s}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{X}_{s}^{(q)}:s\leq t\}
, where
\mathbf{X}_{s}^{(l)}=({\bf x}_{i_{l}s}^{(l)}:i_{l}\in[N_{l}])^{\top}
.

Assumption 4
.

(Distribution of Covariates)

Assume
E({\bf x}_{i_{l}t}^{(l)})=\mathbf{0}
for all
i_{l}\in[N_{l}]
and
t\in[T]
.
Let
{\bm{\eta}}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{l}}
be a constant vector satisfying
\|{\bm{\eta}}_{l}\|\leq c
for
l\in[q]
, where
c
is a positive constant.
Define
{\bf x}_{t}^{(l)\eta}=({\bf x}_{i_{l}t}^{(l)\top}{\bm{\eta}}_{l}:i_{l}\in[N_{l}])^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}
.
Assume
{\bf x}^{(l)\eta}=({\bf x}_{t}^{(l)\eta\top}:0\leq t\leq T)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}(T+1)}

satisfies the
K
-convex concentration property for some constant
K

according to Definition
1
.

Assumption 5
.

(Stability)

Denote
\mathbb{Y}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{Y}_{0})=\mathbf{0}
, and assume that
\max_{g^{(1)}\in[G_{1,0}],\cdots,g^{(q)}\in[G_{q,0}]}\\
\big|\sum_{l}\lambda_{g^{(l)}}^{0}+\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}^{0}\big|\leq\kappa_{\max}<1
,
where
G_{l,0}
is the true number of groups in the
l
th dimension, and
\kappa_{\max}
is a positive constant.

Assumption 6
.

(Group Difference)

\min_{g_{1}^{(l)}\neq g_{2}^{(l)}}\{\|{\bm{\theta}}_{g_{1}^{(l)}}^{(l)0}-{\bm{\theta}}_{g_{2}^{(l)}}^{(l)0}\|^{2}+\max_{g^{-(l)}\in[G_{-l}^{0}]}|\alpha_{g_{1}^{(l)}g^{-(l)}}^{0}-\alpha_{g_{2}^{(l)}g^{-(l)}}^{0}|^{2}\}\geq c_{\textup{gap}}
holds for all
l\in[q]
,
where
c_{\textup{gap}}>0
and is allowed to go to zero as
\min_{l}N_{l}\to\infty
.
Here
\{g^{-(l)}\in[G_{-l}^{0}]\}
denotes
\{g^{(1)}\in[G_{1,0}],\cdots,g^{(l-1)}\in[G_{l-1,0}],g^{(l+1)}\in[G_{l+1,0}],\cdots,g^{(q)}\in[G_{q,0}]\}
.

Assumption 7
.

(Group Proportion)

Let
\{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)0}:i_{l}\in[N_{l}]\}
be non-random true membership sequences.
Let
\pi_{g^{(l)},N_{l}}^{(l)}=\sum_{i_{l}}I(g_{i_{l}}^{(l)0}=g^{(l)})/N_{l}
for
g^{(l)}\in[G_{l,0}]
.
Assume that
\min_{l\in[q],g^{(l)}\in[G_{l,0}]}\pi_{g^{(l)},N_{l}}^{(l)}\geq c_{\pi}>0
for sufficiently large
N_{l}
, where
c_{\pi}
is a positive constant.

Assumption
1
requires the parameter space to be bounded.
Assumption
2
ensures the convexity of the element-wise objective function, i.e.,

Q_{i_{1}\cdots i_{1}}(\bm{\Theta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}})
, as a function of
\bm{\Theta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}
for sufficiently large
T
.
This condition is crucial for establishing the consistency result for the metric (
4.2
).

Assumptions
3
–
4
concern about the distributions of the error term and covariates respectively.
Specifically, Assumption
3
requires the error term
\varepsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q},t}
to be
i.i.d.
sub-Gaussian variables, which is widely used in high dimensional time series literature
(
Wang et al. 2013
;
Lugosi and Mendelson 2019
;
Fan et al. 2021
)
.
Subsequently, Assumption
4
allows the covariates
\{{\bf x}_{i_{l}t}^{(l)}\}
to be correlated but satisfying the
K
-convex concentration property according to Definition
1
.
This assumption is employed to establish Hanson-Wright type inequality for dependent variables
(
Adamczak 2015
)
.
Although this is a high level condition, there are a variety of random variables satisfying Definition
1
, as discussed in the following Remark
1
.
We further comment that the Assumptions
3
–
4
together imply that

\mathbf{v}^{\eta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\eta}:0\leq t\leq T)^{\top}

satisfies the
K
-convex concentration property for some constant
K
.
Here
\mathbf{v}_{t}^{\eta}=({\bf x}_{t}^{(1)\eta\top},\cdots,{\bf x}_{t}^{(q)\eta\top},\mathbb{E}_{t}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l}N_{l}+\prod_{l}N_{l}}
, where
\mathbb{E}_{t}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t})
.

Remark 1
.

As discussed by
Adamczak 2015
,
there are a variety of random vectors
{\bf x}
satisfying the
K
-convex concentration property in Definition
1
. For example, (i) Any random vector
{\bf x}
with its elements
x_{i}
s independent for all
i
, and
|x_{i}|\leq 1~a.s.
, satisfies Definition
1

(
Talagrand 1988
)
; (ii) Any random vector
{\bf x}
with its elements in a bounded interval and geometrically strongly mixing satisfies Definition
1

(
Samson 2000
)
.
We refer to
Adamczak 2015
for more detailed discussions.

Next, Assumption
5
ensures the stability of the
tensor-valued time series data as
T
goes to infinity, as
defined in
Lütkepohl 2005
.
Assumptions
6
and
7
are imposed on certain group properties.
Assumption
6
assumes there is a gap between true parameters of two different groups within any network.
The condition is an extension of the same type conditions assumed by the group panel data models with groups assigned on one dimension
(
Su et al. 2016
;
Ando and Bai 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
.
In Assumption
6
, special care is paid to the self-momentum parameter
\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}
, indexed by the tensor dimension-wise group memberships, to ensure the parameter identifiability.
Specifically, we require a min-max type condition for
\alpha_{g^{(1)}\cdots g^{(q)}}^{0}
in Assumption
6
.
Furthermore, instead of assuming
c_{\textup{gap}}>c>0
by a positive constant
c
in existing literature
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
,
we allow
c_{\textup{gap}}\to 0
to study how this signal strength affects the theoretical properties.
Lastly, Assumption
7
assumes a lower bound of group proportions.
In the following we establish the consistency result for the pseudo metric.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
G_{l}\geq G_{l,0}
for all
l\in[q]
, where
G_{l,0}
is the true number of groups.
In addition, assume Assumptions
1
–
5
hold.
Denote by
m=\sum_{l}(p_{l}+1)+1
, then it follows that

\displaystyle d(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}},\bm{\Theta}^{0})=O_{p}\{T^{-1/2}(m+\sum_{l}\textup{\hbox{log}}N_{l})\},

where

\bm{\Theta}^{0}=(\bm{\Theta}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{q}}^{0}=({\bm{\theta}}_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)0}}^{(1)0\top},\cdots,{\bm{\theta}}_{g_{i_{q}}^{(q)0}}^{(q)0\top},\alpha_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)0}\cdots g_{i_{q}}^{(q)0}}^{0})^{\top})

is the true parameter for
\bm{\Theta}
.

Theorem
1
implies that as long as we have

T^{1/2}\gg\sum_{l}\hbox{log}N_{l}+m
,
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
is a consistent estimator
for
\bm{\Theta}^{0}
in the metric when
G_{l}
is possibly over-specified, i.e.,

G_{l}\geq G_{l,0}
.
Subsequently, we show in Theorem
2
that the QIC can
consistently select the true group numbers.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
7
, and let

c_{\textup{gap}}\gg T^{-1/2}(m+\sum_{l}\textup{\hbox{log}}G_{l})(\prod_{l}G_{l})
.
In addition, assume
\kappa=\lambda(\underline{G})/(\sum_{l}G_{l})
satisfies

\displaystyle T^{-1/2}(m+\sum_{l}\textup{\hbox{log}}N_{l})\ll\kappa\ll c_{\textup{gap}}/(\prod_{l}G_{l}).

(4.3)

Then we have
P(\widehat{G}_{1}=G_{1,0},\cdots,\widehat{G}_{q}=G_{q,0})\to 1
as
\min\{N_{1},\cdots,N_{q},T\}\to\infty
.

Theorem
2
implies that if we set
\kappa
to satisfy (
4.3
), then we can consistently estimate the true group numbers.
Denote
G_{-l}=(G_{k}:k\neq l)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{q-1}
, we need
\kappa\gg T^{-1/2}(m+\sum_{l}\hbox{log}N_{l})
to ensure that
\textup{QIC}(\underline{G})>\textup{QIC}(\underline{G}_{0})
for the over-fitting case, i.e.,

G_{l}>G_{l,0}
and
G_{-l}\geq G_{-l,0}
for any
l\in[q]
.
Here
G_{-l}\geq G_{-l,0}
means
G_{k}\geq G_{k,0}
for any
k\neq l
.
Conversely, we need
\kappa\ll c_{\textup{gap}}/(\prod_{l}G_{l})
to guarantee that
\textup{QIC}(\underline{G})>\textup{QIC}(\underline{G}_{0})
for the under-fitting case, i.e., there exists an
l
with
G_{l}<G_{l,0}
.
When both conditions are met, we can obtain
\widehat{G}_{l}=G_{l,0}
for all
l\in[q]
with a probability approaching 1.
In the next subsection, we further discuss the results of node-wise parameter estimation and the consistency of group membership estimation.

4.2
Membership Estimation Consistency and Asymptotic Normality

As we stated before, the metric in (
4.2
)
measures the average estimation error between
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
and
\bm{\Theta}^{0}
.
Therefore, the result in Theorem
1
is not
sufficient to imply the parameter consistency for each node.
To this end, we derive the following node-wise parameter consistency
result, which will be crucial to build the membership estimation
consistency later.

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
5
,
when
G_{l}\geq G_{l,0}
for all
l\in[q]
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{i_{l}}\left\{\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\widehat{g}_{i_{l}}^{(l)}}^{(l)}-{\bm{\theta}}_{g_{i_{l}}^{(l)0}}^{(l)0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\prod_{m\neq l}N_{m}}\sum_{m\neq l}\sum_{i_{m}=1}^{N_{m}}|\widehat{\alpha}_{\widehat{g}_{i_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots\widehat{g}_{i_{q}}^{(q)}}-\alpha_{g_{i_{1}}^{(1)0}\cdots g_{i_{q}}^{(q)0}}^{0}|^{2}\right\}=O_{p}\Big\{T^{-1/2}(m+\sum_{l}\textup{\hbox{log}}N_{l})\Big\}.

(4.4)

Recall that
\mathcal{G}_{l}=(g_{i_{l}}^{(l)}:1\leq i_{l}\leq N_{l})^{\top}
,
and let
\mathcal{G}_{l}^{0}
denote the corresponding true memberships.
Equation (
4.4
) establishes uniform node-wise parameter estimation consistency, which is crucial for achieving the following group membership consistency for
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{l}
when
G_{l}\geq G_{l,0}
for all
l\in[q]
.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
7
, and suppose
G_{l}\geq G_{l,0}
for all
l\in[q]
.
Let
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\widetilde{g}^{(l)}}^{(l)}=\{i_{l}:\widehat{g}_{i_{l}}^{(l)}=\widetilde{g}^{(l)}\}
for
\widetilde{g}^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]
and
\mathcal{R}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)0}=\{i_{l}:g_{i_{l}}^{(l)0}=g^{(l)}\}
for
g^{(l)}\in[G_{l,0}]
,
where
\widehat{g}_{i_{l}}^{(l)}
is obtained by (
3.14
) when
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
is specified.
Let
T^{1/2}c_{\textup{gap}}\gg\sum_{l}\textup{\hbox{log}}N_{l}+m
, then for each
\widetilde{g}^{(l)}\in[G_{l}]
, there exists a
g^{(l)}\in[G_{l,0}]
,
such that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\widetilde{g}^{(l)}}^{(l)}\subset\mathcal{R}_{g^{(l)}}^{(l)0}
with probability
tending to 1.

As implied by the Theorem
3
, the true groups are split into subgroups
instead of joining into new groups when
G_{l}\geq G_{l,0}
for all
l\in[q]
.
Define
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{l}=(\widehat{g}_{i_{l}}^{(l)}:i_{l}\in[N_{l}])^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{l}}
as the estimated membership vectors for all
l\in[q]
.
Particularly, when
G_{l}=G_{l,0}
, we can show that

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{l}=\mathcal{G}_{l}^{0}
holds with probability tending to 1 under certain label permutations.
We would like to remark that establishing the group consistency result is non-trivial due to several challenges under our framework.
As shown in the GTNAR model (
2.2
), group memberships from different dimensions are involved and tangled in an additive form.
In addition, the network dependence structure creates extra difficulty for establishing the node-wise membership consistency result.
To establish the membership consistency,
for instance, in
Zhu et al. 2023
, a refinement procedure is further required for obtaining this property.
In
Su et al. 2023
, a two step estimation procedure is used to achieve group membership estimation consistency when the true group number is known.
Specifically, their two-step procedure establishes node-wise parameter convergence and consistent group membership identification sequentially, while we allow for estimating the group membership and corresponding group-wise parameters simultaneously in a unified framework.
In contrast to their work, a further investigation of our additive model form allows us to deal with these challenges and establish the group membership consistency result for stochastically estimated
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{l}
directly, which is critically important for later statistical inference.
Furthermore, let
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}^{\textup{or}}
be the oracle estimator when
the true group memberships
\mathcal{G}_{l}^{0}
s are known for all
l\in[q]
.
Then the oracle property holds that
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}=\widehat{\bm{\xi}}^{\textup{or}}
with probability tending to 1, where
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
is the estimator for
\bm{\xi}^{0}=({\bm{\theta}}^{(1)0\top},\cdots,{\bm{\theta}}^{(q)0\top},\mathrm{vec}({\bm{\alpha}}^{0})^{\top})^{\top}
.
The results are presented in the following Corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
7
,
and assume
G_{l}=G_{l,0}
for all
l\in[q]
.
Assume
T^{1/2}c_{\textup{gap}}\gg\sum_{l}\hbox{log}N_{l}+m
.
Then under label permutations, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\min\{N_{1},\cdots,N_{q},T\}\to\infty}P\left(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{1}=\mathcal{G}_{1}^{0},\cdots,\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{q}=\mathcal{G}_{q}^{0}\right)\to 1,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{\min\{N_{1},\cdots,N_{q},T\}\to\infty}P\left(\widehat{\bm{\xi}}=\widehat{\bm{\xi}}^{\textup{or}}\right)\to 1.

(4.6)

The results in Corollary
1
imply that
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}^{\textup{or}}
. Therefore, to derive the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
, it is sufficient to investigate the case for
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}^{\textup{or}}
.
We establish the asymptotic normality result in the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Assume Assumptions
1
–
7
,
G_{l}=G_{l,0}
and that there exists
n
, such that
c_{1}n\leq\min_{l}N_{l}\leq\max_{l}N_{l}\leq c_{2}n
for some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
.
Suppose that
T^{1/2}c_{\textup{gap}}\gg\sum_{l}\hbox{log}N_{l}+m
.
Define
{\bf M}_{nT}^{0}=n^{-q}T^{-1}E({\bf M})
and assume
{\bf M}^{0}=\lim_{\min\{n,T\}\to\infty}{\bf M}_{nT}^{0}
exists, where
{\bf M}
is given in (
3
).
Assume
\lambda_{\min}({\bf M}^{0})\geq\tau>0
for a positive constant
\tau
.
Let
k=\sum_{l}\{G_{l}(p_{l}+1)\}+\prod_{l}G_{l}
.
Then for any
{\bm{\eta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
\|{\bm{\eta}}\|=1

we have

n^{q/2}T^{1/2}{\bm{\eta}}^{\top}(\widehat{\bm{\xi}}-\bm{\xi}^{0})\to_{d}N\left(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}{\bm{\eta}}^{\top}({\bf M}^{0})^{-1}{\bm{\eta}}\right).

(4.7)

Theorem
4
establishes the asymptotic normality of the estimator.
Specifically, the convergence rates of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}
is
\sqrt{n^{q}T}
for all
l\in[q]
.
Using (
4.7
), we are able to conduct the statistical inference.

To evaluate the finite sample performance of the proposed method, we conduct several simulation studies in this section for

Three different scenarios for

In each scenario, the node memberships

The dimension of exogenous covariates are set as

For each scenario, the following two network structures are considered.

Example 1.

In the

Clauset et al. 2009
.
For the
i
th node in the
l
th network, its in-degree

assumed to be power-law distributed.
Specifically, we first generate
\widetilde{d}_{i}^{(l)}
with a probability

Then,
d_{i}^{(l)}

As a result, the resulting adjacency matrix is not symmetric, which implies a directed network.

Next, we construct the adjacency matrices among the users (
\mathbf{A}^{(1)}
) and districts (
\mathbf{A}^{(2)}
) respectively as follows.
The user network is built based on the friend list information.
Specifically, if user
j
is on the friend list of user
i
on Yelp,
then we set
a^{(1)}_{ij}=1
. Otherwise we set
a^{(1)}_{ij}=0
.
The spatial network is built based on the geographical adjacent
relationship. Specifically,
we set
a^{(2)}_{ij}=1
if the district
j
is adjacent to district
i
.
We calculate the densities for the above two networks,
i.e.,
d_{\text{user}}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}a^{(1)}_{ij}/\{N_{1}(N_{1}-1)\}
and

d_{\text{dis}}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}a^{(2)}_{ij}/\{N_{2}(N_{2}-1)\}
, which are
shown in Table
1
. One could observe that the user
networks are quite sparse in all cities.

Lastly, to characterize the dynamic patterns of the responses, we
collect a number of covariates for users and districts, respectively.
For user
i
in quarter
t
, we consider the following five
covariates: (1) the number of months after joining Yelp by
the start of the quarter
t
(
x^{(1)}_{it,\text{dur}}
), (2) whether the
user is VIP by the start of the quarter
t
(
x^{(1)}_{it,\text{vip}}
),
(3) average tags (i.e., “useful”, “funny” and “cool”) the user

i_{1}
obtains for his/her reviews during the last quarter (
x^{(1)}_{it,\text{use}},x^{(1)}_{it,\text{fun}},x^{(1)}_{it,\text{cool}}
).
Next,
for the
j
th district in quarter
t
, we consider two covariates: (1) the average “stars” (
x^{(2)}_{jt,\text{star}}
), and (2)
the average review number (
x^{(2)}_{jt,\text{num}}
) obtained by the
j
th
district during the
(t-1)
th quarter.
These two covariates are indicative of the average popularity levels in the preceding time period.
We standardize all continuous covariates to be in the range
[0,1]
for the subsequent analysis.
In Figure
in Appendix
, we visualize the relationship between these user-related covariates and the response variable. The plot reveals that users who receive more tags for their reviews tend to be motivated to contribute more reviews themselves.
Notably, VIP users in Scottsdale and Toronto tend to write more reviews, whereas VIP users in Charlotte exhibit comparatively less activity. Subsequently, we apply the proposed GTNAR model to each of the five cities, enabling us to analyze and understand the distinctive group patterns within each urban area.

6.2
Estimation Results

We employ QIC for the selection of group numbers, and the estimation results are detailed in Table
2
and Table
in Appendix
. The numbers of user groups and district groups vary across the five cities, indicating different levels of heterogeneity among them. For instance, consider the results for Phoenix. Notably, the estimated
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}
values are all positive, indicating a positive self-motivated effect. Additionally, the spatial (column) network effects are consistently positive, suggesting a favorable effect from neighboring districts. Such an observation is consistent with the findings in the literature
(
Sun and Paule 2017
, e.g.)
. Furthermore, we can also observe that within these spatial effects,
\widehat{\lambda}_{2}^{(2)}=0.270
is the largest, signifying a strong neighbor effect.
In other words, if the second group districts’ neighbors obtain more reviews in the last period, then it is likely that these district would receive more reviews in this period.
This implies that the districts in this group are closely
interconnected to their neighboring counterparts.
We further visualize the districts by estimated groups in the left
panel of Figure
5
, where the pink
districts are from the first group and the green ones are from the
second one. We can observe obvious blocks (marked as red) in group 2. To
further investigate the local area of the three main blocks
shown in the figure, we map some of the shops in these districts to the Google Map, and mark some restaurants on it (as shown in the right panel). The first block is concentrated around the central city area, and most shops are located in downtown, where large commercial and government buildings are placed.
The second block is in/near the Camelback East Village. This area has
two main streets, and most of the shops are located in
these two streets.
The third block is in the northern region, spreading from the North Mountain Village to the Deer Valley area, which include some mountains and hills.
The three blocks, as shown in the Wikipedia of Phoenix, have their own features, which may lead to three business patterns, within each of which the connections are quite close due to the inner similarity.

Performing similar investigation on the user dimension, one
can find that the social network effect in the first group
appears to be significantly negative (
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}^{(1)}=-0.02
),
whereas corresponding coefficients in the other two groups are
positive. This implies that user activities in the first group are
influenced oppositely by their friends’ behaviors, whereas in the
other groups, users are still positively influenced by their
friends. Considering that the social network effect is generally found
to be positive on the choice of restaurant

(
Tiwari and Richards 2016
;
Fe 2023
)
, it is of interest to look
closer into this user subgroup and explain why they react negatively
toward actions of friends they follow.

Further,
we discuss how the response relates to user and district level covariates. Firstly, concerning user-related covariates, we observe that users who receive more “useful” tags for their reviews in the last quarter tend to write more reviews in the subsequent quarter. This reflects an encouraging effect on user behavior, indicating that users may become more active when their comments are valued as “valuable” by others. Secondly, regarding district-related covariates, we find that districts with higher star ratings in the last quarter tend to attract more customers on this platform, subsequently leading to an increase in the number of reviews in the next quarter. This underscores the positive impact of high ratings on customer engagement and review generation.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we introduce a novel Group Tensor Network Autoregression (GTNAR) model designed for time series data indexed by multi-relational networks.
By leveraging network structures on each dimension, our model establishes a unique framework for
analyzing tensor-valued time series data.
This model presents a valuable tool for analyzing data collected in complex network environments, shedding light on various network effects.
Furthermore, it is worth exploring models that consider multiple network effects in a multiplicative form, as investigated by
Chen et al. 2021
.
Besides, introducing a hidden factor structure into the GTNAR model can potentially offer more insights into the high-dimensional data, capturing more underlying information within the tensor-valued time series, and thus will be an interesting future research topic.
Lastly, it is also important to further investigate how to model categorical responses in our modelling framework.
