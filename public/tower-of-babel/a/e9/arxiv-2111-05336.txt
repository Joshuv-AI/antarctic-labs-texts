The Jacobi theta distribution
Caleb Deen Bastian, Grzegorz A Rempala, Herschel Rabitz

Abstract
We form the Jacobi theta distribution through discrete integration of exponential random variables over an infinite inverse square law surface. It is continuous, supported on the positive reals, has a single positive parameter, is unimodal, positively skewed, and leptokurtic. Its cumulative distribution and density functions are expressed in terms of the Jacobi theta function. We describe asymptotic and log-normal approximations, inference, and a few applications of such distributions to modeling.



Keywords
: Jacobi theta function, Jacobi theta distribution, Laplace transform, log-normal distribution, inverse-square law

Introduction

We describe a univariate continuous distribution called the Jacobi theta distribution supported on the positive reals that does not appear in the literature to the best of our knowledge (see e.g.,
Johnson et al., 1994
). The distribution is attained from the action of an infinite random measure
N
with random weights
\{W_{x}\}
and fixed atoms
{\symAMSb N}_{\geq 1}
, where the weights are
iid
exponential random variables with common mean
m\in(0,\infty)
, on the test function
f(x)=1/x^{2}
for
x\in{\symAMSb N}_{\geq 1}
, such that
Nf
is a random variable having the Jacobi theta distribution. It is represented as the infinite sum

Nf=\sum_{x\geq 1}W_{x}/x^{2}

Its law is encoded in its Laplace transform

\alpha\mapsto\sqrt{\alpha m}\pi\csch(\sqrt{\alpha m}\pi)

The Jacobi theta distribution is continuous, has a single parameter
m
, is unimodal, positively skewed, and leptokurtic.

This note is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give the mathematical backdrop in terms of random measures. In Section
3
we give the main result of the existence of the distribution and state some of its properties. In Section
4
we show that the distribution may be approximated by asymptotic expansion and by the log-normal distribution. In Section
5
we give three applications to modeling data. In Section
6
we end with discussions and conclusions.

(2)

The

Hence, assuming at least one of the

A product random measure

{\symAMSb E}(Ng)^{n}=\sum_{(x,\dotsb,y)\in D^{n}}{\symAMSb E}(W_{x}\dotsb W_{y})g(x)\dotsb g(y)\for g\in\mathscr{E}_{\geq 0},\quad n\geq 1

3
Distribution

We define the Jacobi theta function.

Definition 1

(Jacobi theta function)
.

The Jacobi theta function is defined as

\theta_{2}(z,q)=2q^{1/4}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{k(k+1)}\cos((2k+1)z)

(9)

Now we give the main result on the existence of the Jacobi theta distribution.

Theorem 1

(Jacobi theta)
.

Consider the random measure
N
(
4
). Let
D={\symAMSb N}_{\geq 1}
with
(E,\mathscr{E})=({\symAMSb R}_{>0},\mathscr{B}_{{\symAMSb R}_{>0}})
and let
\{W_{x}\}
be an independency of exponential random variables with common mean
m\in(0,\infty)
. Then for
f\in\mathscr{E}_{\geq 0}
as
f(x)=1/x^{2}
,
Nf
has Laplace transform

F(\alpha)=\sqrt{\alpha m}\pi\csch(\sqrt{\alpha m}\pi)\for\alpha\in{\symAMSb R}_{\geq 0}

cumulative distribution function

\eta(Nf\leq x)=\sqrt{\frac{m\pi}{x}}\theta_{2}\left(0,e^{-\frac{m\pi^{2}}{x}}\right)

and density

\eta(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{\sqrt{m\pi}}{2x^{5/2}}\left(2m\pi^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{2}\left(0,e^{-\frac{m\pi^{2}}{x}}\right)}{4}+2e^{-\frac{m\pi^{2}}{x}}\sum_{k\geq 1}k(k+1)(e^{-\frac{m\pi^{2}}{x}})^{k(k+1)-3/4}\right)-x\theta_{2}\left(0,e^{-\frac{m\pi^{2}}{x}}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x

Proof.

The Laplace transform is computed as

\displaystyle{\symAMSb E}e^{-\alpha Nf}

\displaystyle={\symAMSb E}e^{-\sum_{x\in D}\alpha W_{x}f(x)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{x\in D}{\symAMSb E}e^{-\alpha W_{x}f(x)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{x\in D}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\frac{e^{-z/m_{x}}}{m_{x}}e^{-\alpha zf(x)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{x\in D}\frac{1}{1+\alpha m_{x}f(x)}

which specialized for
m_{x}=m
gives the result. The inverse Laplace transform follows from noting that

\displaystyle L^{-1}(\frac{\csch{\sqrt{a}}}{\sqrt{a}})(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi x}}\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{-(2k+1)^{2}/(4x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{2}(0,e^{-1/x})}{\sqrt{\pi x}}

so that

L^{-1}(\sqrt{a}\csch(\sqrt{a}))(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}L^{-1}(\frac{\csch{\sqrt{a}}}{\sqrt{a}})(x)

giving the cumulative distribution function and density upon substitution of
x\leftarrow x/(m\pi^{2})
. The derivative follows from the derivative of
\theta_{2}
in the second coordinate.
∎

The frequency spectrum of
Nf
is given by
C(\omega)=F(-i\omega)
for
\omega\in{\symAMSb R}
with magnitude squared

|C(\omega)|^{2}=|\omega|m\pi^{2}\csch(\sqrt{-i\omega m}\pi)\csch(\sqrt{i\omega m}\pi)\for\omega\in{\symAMSb R}

and the phase spectrum is given by
P(\omega)=\arctan(\mathfrak{I}(C(\omega))/\mathfrak{R}(C(\omega)))
. Both are plotted below in Figure

The statistics follow and are expressed in terms of the Riemann zeta function. Both skewness and kurtosis are constant.

Corollary 1

(Statistics)
.

The mean, variance, and second moment are

\displaystyle{\symAMSb E}Nf

\displaystyle=m\zeta(2)=m\pi^{2}/6

\displaystyle{\symAMSb V}arNf

\displaystyle=m^{2}\zeta(4)=m^{2}\pi^{4}/90

\displaystyle{\symAMSb E}(Nf)^{2}

\displaystyle=7m^{2}\pi^{4}/180

with constant signal to noise ratio
{\symAMSb E}Nf/\sqrt{{\symAMSb V}arNf}=\sqrt{5/2}\simeq 1.58
, where
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function. The skewness is

\text{Skewness}(Nf)=4\sqrt{10}/7\simeq 1.81

and the kurtosis is

\text{Kurtosis}(Nf)=57/7\simeq 8.14

Next we show that the moments are finite at all orders and that the Jacobi theta distribution is uniquely defined by its moments.

Proposition 1

(Finite characterizing moments)
.

Nf
has finite moments of all orders

{\symAMSb E}(Nf)^{n}<\infty\for m>0,\quad n\geq 1

with moment generating function

M(t)=F(t)<\infty\for t\in{\symAMSb R}

where
F
is the Laplace transform of
Nf
.

Proof.

The finiteness of all moments follows from (
8

\displaystyle{\symAMSb E}(Nf)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,\dotsb,y)\in D^{n}}{\symAMSb E}(W_{x}\dotsb W_{y})f(x)\dotsb f(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x,\dotsb,y)\in D^{n}}{\symAMSb E}W_{x}^{n}f(x)\dotsb f(y)

\displaystyle=n!(m\zeta(2))^{n}<\infty\for m>0,\quad n\geq 1

For the mgf, we have that
M(t)=F(-t)=F(t)
for
t\in{\symAMSb R}
.
∎

Asymptotic

Proposition 3

Proposition 4

(Estimator)
.

Let

We solve the equation

4.2
Log-normal

The Jacobi theta distribution can be approximated by the log-normal distribution
\text{LogNormal}(\mu,\sigma)
. Matching first and second moments, we have

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{\frac{5}{7}}\pi^{2}m\right)

(10)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{7}{5}\right)}

(11)

which gives approximate density

\widetilde{\eta}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{\frac{5}{7}}\pi^{2}m\right)-\log(x)\right)^{2}}{2\log\left(\frac{7}{5}\right)}\right)}{x\sqrt{2\pi\log\left(\frac{7}{5}\right)}}\mathrm{d}x

and cumulative distribution function

\widetilde{\eta}(t\leq x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\erf\left(\frac{\log x-\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\right))

The skewness is
17\sqrt{2/5}/5\simeq 2.15
and the kurtosis is
7\,631/625\simeq 12.21
, both independent of
m
.

The entropy can be approximated as

H\simeq\log_{2}(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\exp(\mu+1/2))=\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\frac{5e}{7}}\pi^{5/2}m\sqrt{\log\left(\sqrt{\frac{7}{5}}\right)}\right)\sim O(\log_{2}(m))

4.3
Maximum likelihood

Proposition 5

(MLE)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=\{X_{i}:i=1,\dotsb,n\}
be a collection of data. Then the log-normal maximum likelihood estimator with parameters
\mu
(
10
) and
\sigma
(
11
) for
m\in(0,\infty)
of the Jacobi theta distribution is given by

\hat{m}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{7}{5}}\left(\prod_{i}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{1/n}\sim\text{LogNormal}(\mu-\sigma^{2}/(2n)+\log\left(\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{7}{5}}\right),\sigma/\sqrt{n})

with mean and variance

\displaystyle{\symAMSb E}(\hat{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{7}{5}}\exp\mu=m

\displaystyle{\symAMSb V}ar(\hat{m})

\displaystyle=m^{2}\left(\exp(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n})-1\right)\xrightarrow{n\text{ large}}m^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)

Proof.

The parameter
m
can be estimated using the log-normal maximum likelihood estimator of

\hat{\mu}=\frac{\sum_{i}^{n}\log(X_{i})}{n}\xrightarrow{n\text{ large}}\text{Gaussian}(\mu,\sigma^{2}/n)

The log-normal distribution follows from the MLE of the log-normal distribution. ∎

5
Applications

The Jacobi theta distribution can be applied to any problem where the random variable of interest is an infinite superposition of exponential random variables with inverse quadratically scaled means relative to the positive integers. Superpositions at integer points correspond to discrete integration over an inverse square law surface. Inverse square laws are found in many applications, including gravitation, electric fields and forces, intensity of light, radiation from a source, intensity of sound, and so on. Exponential distributions arise as the maximum entropy distribution on the positive reals with fixed mean.

5.3
Economic gravity model

Consider an equispaced grid of locations of countries relative to a country at the origin with spacing
d
. For each location
x

U_{x}
uniformly taking values in
(0,1)

\text{Gamma}(2,\lambda)

.

5.4
Electric field

Consider an equispaced grid of locations of point charges with spacing
d>0
. The point charges are
\{Q_{x}:x=1,2,\dotsb\}
and are distributed
\text{Exponential}(4\pi\varepsilon_{0}d^{2}\lambda)

6
Discussion and conclusions

We describe a continuous univariate distribution supported on the positive reals based on integration of random measures composed of exponential random variables across an inverse-square surface. The Jacobi theta distribution is single-parameter, positively skewed, and leptokurtic. The cumulative distribution and density functions are expressed in terms of the Jacobi theta function, and asymptotic and log-normal approximations enabling tractable calculations and exact maximum likelihood inference.
