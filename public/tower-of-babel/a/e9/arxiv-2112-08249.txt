Abstract

Introduction

In
[
24

[
7

Definition 1.1
.

For
0<\alpha\leq 1

E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

Here and throughout,

\gamma(\alpha)=\inf\{\dim_{H}(E):E\text{ is an }\alpha\text{-Furstenberg set}\},

\max\Big\{2\alpha,\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\Big\}\leq\gamma(\alpha)\leq\frac{3\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{2}.

(1)

Wolff conjectured that the upper bound is sharp. When

In the influential paper
[
14

(\delta,\alpha)_{n}

\mathbb{R}^{n}
that have Hausdorff dimension
\alpha

n,\alpha,\varepsilon,C,\delta

Definition 1.2
.

We say a set

(\delta,\alpha)_{n}
-set

E

\delta

Discretized
(\alpha,\beta)
-Furstenberg sets

To prove Theorem
1.7
, Héra, Shmerkin, and Yavicoli introduced an object they called a discretized
(\alpha,\beta)
-Furstenberg set. Before defining this object, we will introduce some additional notation. If
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the
\delta
-covering number
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X)
to be the minimum number of balls of radius
\delta
required to cover
X
. If
X
is itself a union of balls of radius
\delta
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(X)\sim\delta^{-n}|X|
, where the implicit constant depends on
n
. Abusing notation, we say a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(which need not be a union of balls of radius
\delta
) is a
(\delta,\alpha)_{n}
-set if for each ball
B
of radius
r\geq\delta
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(E\cap B)\leq C\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)^{\alpha}
. In particular, if
X
is a union of balls of radius
\delta
, then it is a
(\delta,\alpha)_{n}
-set in the sense given here if and only if it is a
(\delta,\alpha)_{n}
-set using the previous definition, though the associated constant
C
will differ in these two definitions by a multiplicative factor depending on
n
. Since
n\in\{1,2\}
throughout this paper, this distinction will not be important.

Next, if
L
is a set of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we can identify
L
with a subset of the affine Grassmannian
A(2,1)
. For concreteness, we will parameterize a coordinate patch of
A(2,1)
as follows: if
l
is the line
y=mx+b
with
(m,b)\in[0,1]^{2},
we define
\iota(l)=(m,b)
and
\operatorname{dir}(l)=(1,m)/\sqrt{1+m^{2}}
. In the discussions that follow, we can assume that every line is of the above form. We define the distance between two lines
l,l^{\prime}
to be the Euclidean distance between the points
\iota(l)
and
\iota(l^{\prime}),
and we define
\angle(l,l^{\prime})=\angle(\operatorname{dir}(l),\operatorname{dir}(l^{\prime}))
.

We say a set
L
of lines is
\delta
-separated if
\operatorname{dist}(\iota(l),\iota(l^{\prime}))\geq\delta
for all distinct
l,l^{\prime}\in L
. A set
L
of lines is said to be a
(\delta,\beta)_{2}
-set if
\iota(L)\subset[0,1]^{2}
is a
(\delta,\beta)_{2}
-set. With these definitions, we recall the following definition from
[
12
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
0<\alpha\leq 1,

0<\beta\leq 2
,
\varepsilon>0
,
C>0
, and
\delta>0
, we say that a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a (planar)
discretized
(\alpha,\beta)
-Furstenberg set
if
E\supset\bigcup_{l\in L}P_{l}
, where:

(i)

L
is a
\delta
-separated,
(\delta,\beta)_{2}
-set of lines (with implicit parameters
\varepsilon
and
C
), and
\#L\geq C^{-1}\delta^{\varepsilon-\beta}
.

(ii)

For each
l\in L,

\mathcal{P}_{l}\subset l
is a
\delta
-separated
(\delta,\alpha)_{1}
-set (with implicit parameters
\varepsilon
and
C
), and
\#\mathcal{P}_{l}\geq C^{-1}\delta^{\varepsilon-\alpha}
.

The authors in
[
12
]
proved Theorem
1.7
by showing that every discretized
(\alpha,2\alpha)
-Furstenberg set must have large
\delta
covering number. The relationship between these two statements is as follows.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
12
]
, Lemma 3.3)
.

Let
0<\alpha<1,

0<\beta<2
, and
s\geq 0
. Suppose that there exists
\varepsilon>0
and
C>0
so that for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
, every discretized
(\alpha,\beta)
-Furstenberg set
E
satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(E)\geq\delta^{-s}.
Then every
(\alpha,\beta)
-Furstenberg set has Hausdorff dimension at least
s
.

Thus, in order to prove Theorem
1.8
, it suffices to establish the corresponding statement for discretized Furstenberg sets. Our result in this direction is the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

Define
c(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{6(155+68\alpha)}
. Then for every
0<\alpha<1
and every
c<c(\alpha)
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and
C>0
so that the following holds for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small. Let
E
be a discretized
(\alpha,2\alpha)
-Furstenberg set (with implicit parameters
\varepsilon
and
C
). Then
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(E)\geq\delta^{-2\alpha-c}.

At a qualitative level, the connection between sum-product estimates, point-line incidences, and Furstenberg sets is well known. In
[
4
]
, Bourgain, Katz, and Tao proved a sum-product estimate over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and used this to obtain a point-line incidence bound in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
. The argument used by Héra, Shmerkin, and Yavicoli
[
12
]
is also based on these ideas, but uses Bourgain’s discretized projection theorem
[
2
]
instead of the discretized sum-product theorem. Our main contribution is that our arguments are carefully structured to obtain an effective quantitative relationship between the discretized sum-product theorem and the size of
(\alpha,2\alpha)
-Furstenberg sets, and our result gives an understanding of the quantitative limits of current approaches to the problem. This is in contrast to previous arguments that were mostly optimized for clarify of exposition. While previous proofs do not claim explicit exponents for the size of
(\alpha,2\alpha)
-Furstenberg sets near
\alpha=1/2
, an examination of these arguments shows that the resulting estimates would yield an analogue of Proposition
2.3
with a value of
c(\alpha)
that was several orders of magnitude smaller. Further discussion about the efficiency of our methods can be found in Remark
4.5
.

One major difficulty we encounter when proving Proposition
2.3
is that while both Theorem
1.5
and Proposition
2.3
involve sets that satisfy non-concentration conditions, not all paths between these two non-concentration conditions are equally efficient. To overcome this difficulty, we construct a sequence of point-line configurations that allow us to translate between the two notions of non-concentration. See Figure
1
for an overview of this translation step.

There are two steps in particular that weaken the quantitative dependence between the exponent in Theorem
1.5
and the value of
c(\alpha)
from Proposition
2.3
. The first of these steps is a series of reductions that transforms a (hypothetical) counter-example to Proposition
2.3
into an arrangement of points and lines, where the points are contained in a projective Cartesian product, and then subsequently into an arrangement where the points are contained in a rectilinear Cartesian product. Our estimates worsen as the transformation between these latter two structures becomes more singular, and our arguments are carefully structured to mitigate this. The second step is an application of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem to transform an arrangement of points and lines with many point-line incidences into an arrangement of points and lines where the arrangement of points has small projection in three linearly independent directions. Again, our arguments are structured to minimize the losses coming from the application of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem.

Tools from combinatorics

We will begin by introducing several standard tools from additive combinatorics. See
[
23
]
for an introduction to the topic. We will also state and prove a more technical version of Theorem
1.5
.

We begin with some definitions. If
A,B
are finite subsets of an Abelian group, and
E\subset A\times B
, we define the partial sum and partial difference sets

A\overset{E}{+}B=\{a+b:(a,b)\in E\},\quad A\overset{E}{-}B=\{a-b:(a,b)\in E\}.

If
E=A\times B
, then we will abbreviate this as
A+B
(resp.
A-B
); this is the usual Minkowski sum and Minkowski difference.

We recall the following version of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem. The version stated here follows by making a small modification to Lemma 2.2 from
[
3
]
. The exact formulation given below can be found in
[
13
, Lemma 8]
.

Here and throughout, we write
X\gtrsim Y
or
Y\lesssim X
if there is an absolute constant
C>0
such that
X\geq CY
. If
X\gtrsim Y
and
X\lesssim Y
, we write
X\sim Y
.

Lemma 3.1

(Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers)
.

Let
A,B
be finite subsets of an Abelian group, and let
E\subset A\times B
. Then there is a set
A^{\prime}\subset A
such that

\#A^{\prime}\gtrsim\frac{\#E}{\#B},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\#(A^{\prime}-A^{\prime})\lesssim\frac{(\#A)^{4}(\#B)^{3}(A\overset{E}{-}B)^{4}}{(\#E)^{5}}.

Next, we will introduce some notation to describe the additive and multiplicative energy of discretized sets. Recall that if
A,B
are subsets of an Abelian group (with group operation
+
), we define the additive energy

E(A,B)=\#\{(a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}\colon a+b=a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\}=\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}\#\big((a+B)\cap(b+A)\big).

The following result is a simple consequence of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality; a proof can be found in
[
23
, Corollary 2.10]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A,B
be finite subsets of an Abelian group. Then

E(A,B)\leq E(A,A)^{1/2}E(B,B)^{1/2}.

We use the notation
N_{\delta}(X)
to mean the
\delta
-neighborhood of
X
. That is,

N_{\delta}(X)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\inf_{x\in X}|x-y|\leq\delta\},

where the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
should be clear from context.

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
, we define
A_{\delta^{+}}=N_{\delta}(A)\cap(\delta\mathbb{Z})
and
A_{\delta^{\times}}=N_{2\delta}(A)\cap 2^{\delta\mathbb{Z}}
; note that with this definition, for each

there is a point

with

. Similarly, since

for

has distance less than

from

with

In practice, we will always have
A,B\subset[1,2]
.

A_{\delta^{+}}
(resp.
A_{\delta^{\times}}
) is a subset of the Abelian group
\delta\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
2^{\delta\mathbb{Z}}
). We define

E_{+}^{\delta}(A,B)=E(A_{\delta^{+}},B_{\delta^{+}}),\quad E_{\times}^{\delta}(A,B)=E(A_{\delta^{\times}},B_{\delta^{\times}}).

3.1
Moran-regular trees

In this section,
k
and
T
will be large integers; later, we will fix a small number
\varepsilon
and set
k=\lfloor 1/\varepsilon\rfloor,
and we will select
T
to be an integer so that
2^{-k(T+1)}\leq\delta<2^{-kT}.
With this choice of
k
and
T
, and once
\varepsilon
is a fixed constant, we will have
2^{k}\leq 2^{1/\varepsilon}\sim 1
and
2^{T}\leq\delta^{-2\varepsilon}
.

Definition 3.4
.

A
2^{T}
-adic interval is a subset of
[0,1)
of the form
[s2^{-jT},(s+1)2^{-jT})
for some
j\geq 0
and
0\leq s\leq 2^{T}
. A
2^{T}
-adic square is a Cartesian product of
2^{T}
-adic intervals. In what follows, we will simply use “square” to refer to a
2^{T}
-adic square.

Note that two distinct squares are either disjoint, or one contains the other. If
S
is a square of side-length
2^{-jT}
that contains a square
S^{\prime}
of side-length
2^{-(j+1)T}
, then we say
S^{\prime}
is a child of
S
.

Definition 3.5
.

If
A\subset[0,1)^{d}
and if
S
is a square, we say
S
meets
A
if
S\cap A\neq\emptyset
. We say
A
is discretized at scale
2^{-kT}
if every square of side-length
2^{-kT}
intersects at most one point from
A
.

Definition 3.6
.

If
A\subset[0,1)^{d}
is discretized at scale
2^{-kT}
, and if
N_{1},\ldots,N_{k}
are integers, we say that
A
is Moran-regular with branching
(N_{0},\ldots,N_{k-1})
if for each
j=0,\ldots,k-1
, every square
S
of side-length
2^{-jT}
that meets
A
has exactly
N_{j}
children
S^{\prime}\subset S
that meet
A
.

The next lemma says that every discretized set has a large subset that is Moran-regular. See
[
15
, Lemma 3.4]
or
[
19
, Lemma 2.2]
for a detailed proof.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
A\subset[0,1)^{d}
be discretized at scale
2^{-kT}
. Then there exists a set
A^{\prime}\subset A
and integers
N_{0},\ldots,N_{k-1}
so that
\#A^{\prime}\geq(2dT)^{-k}(\#A)
, and
A^{\prime}
is Moran-regular with branching
(N_{0},\ldots,N_{k-1})
. Furthermore, we can ensure that each of the integers
N_{0},\ldots,N_{k-1}
are of the form
2^{z}
for some non-negative integer
z
.

We can also refine a collection of discretized sets, so that they are all Moran regular with the same branching.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a collection of subsets of
[0,1)^{d}
, each of which is discretized at scale
2^{-kT}
. Then there exist integers
N_{0},\ldots,N_{k-1}
; a set
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{A}
; and for each
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, a set
A^{\prime}\subset A
so that

\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\#A^{\prime}\geq(2dT)^{-2k}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\#A,

and for each
A\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,
A^{\prime}
is Moran-regular with branching
(N_{0},\ldots,N_{k-1})
.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
3.7
to each
A\in\mathcal{A}
, and let
A^{\prime}\subset A
and

∎

Finally, we observe that if

and Moran-regular with branching

or

Remark 4.2

Remark 4.5
.

When
c=1/2
, the discretized sum-product theorem (Proposition
3.3
) gives a gain of roughly
\delta^{-1/100}
. The final gain Proposition
4.1
is roughly 50 times, or two orders of magnitude, worse than this. Roughly speaking, one order of magnitude comes from repeated pigeonholing and use of Cauchy-Schwarz, while the other order of magnitude comes from the use of Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers. While some further optimizations are likely possible, it appears that such optimizations would yield rather modest gains. In particular, Proposition
3.3
requires that the difference set
A-A
be small (rather than merely requiring that
E_{+}(A,A)
be large), so it appears difficult to avoid using the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem). While a more efficient sequence of pigeonholing and Cauchy-Schwarz arguments might improve the final bound, since the current argument only loses (roughly) one order of magnitude at this step, any improvement here would at best yield a modest improvement in the final bound.

5
From Proposition
4.1
to discretized Furstenberg

Our goal in this section is to prove that Proposition
4.1
implies Proposition
2.3
, and hence Theorem
1.8
. First, we will briefly describe the differences between Propositions
4.1
and
2.3
. Both are incidence theorems about collections of points and lines, but Proposition
4.1
requires two additional non-concentration conditions.

Property (
E
) is sometimes called “robust transversality,” and it asserts that for each
p\in P
, a typical pair of lines incident to
p
point in well-separated directions. With this condition, Proposition
4.1
can also be viewed as a bilinear incidence theorem. Thus our reduction from Proposition
4.1
to
2.3
will employ “bilinear to linear” type arguments, which were first used by Tao, Vargas, and Vega in the context of the Kakeya and Restriction problems
[
22
]
. A prototype for the specific arguments used here can be found in
[
9
, §2.3]
. See also
[
21
]
for a similar argument in a slightly different setting.

Property (
F
) is also an assertion about the distribution of directions
\{\operatorname{dir}(l)\colon(p,l)\in I\}
. This property imposes stronger non-concentration conditions at small scales (specifically at length scales between
\delta
and
(\delta^{-2\alpha}/\#P)^{1/\alpha}
), but it says nothing about non-concentration at larger scales. To the best of our knowledge, the arguments used to obtain the assertion in Property (
F
) are novel, and we hope that similar arguments might be helpful for related problems.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Fix
0<\alpha<1
and
c<c(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{6(155+68\alpha)}
. We will show that if the parameters
\varepsilon>0
and
C
are chosen appropriately, then for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small, if
E\supset\bigcup_{l\in L_{0}}P_{l}
is a discretized
(\alpha,2\alpha)
-Furstenberg set (with implicit parameters
\varepsilon
and
C
), then

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(E)\geq\delta^{-2\alpha-c}.

(33)

In particular, (
33
) will follow from the estimate

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}\Big(\bigcup_{l\in L_{0}}P_{l}\Big)\gtrapprox\delta^{-2\alpha-c(\alpha)}.

(34)

Constructing the incidence arrangement.

Our goal is to construct a
\delta
-separated set
P\subset\bigcup_{l\in L_{0}}P_{l}
, and an incidence relation
I\subset P\times L
that satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
4.1
. While it is tempting to simply choose
P
to be a maximal
\delta
-separated subset of
\bigcup_{l\in L_{0}}P_{l}
, it is possible that a set constructed in this fashion will have the following undesirable property: let
R
be a rectangle of dimensions
r\times\delta
, so that many lines
l\in L_{0}
intersect
R
in a line-segment of length roughly
r
; while Item (

) from Definition
2.1
guarantees that each set
R\cap P_{l}
has cardinality
\lessapprox(r/\delta)^{\alpha}
, the union
R\cap\bigcup P_{l}
might be much larger. For technical reasons that will become apparent later in the proof, we would like to avoid this type of incidence arrangement.
