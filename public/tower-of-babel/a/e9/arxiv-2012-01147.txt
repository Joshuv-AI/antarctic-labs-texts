The Manin–Drinfeld theorem and the rationality of Rademacher symbols
Claire Burrin

Abstract
. For any noncocompact Fuchsian group Γ \Gamma , we show that periods of the canonical differential of the third kind associated to residue divisors of cusps are expressed in terms of Rademacher symbols for Γ \Gamma — generalizations of periods appearing in the classical theory of modular forms. This result provides a relation between Rademacher symbols and the famous theorem of Manin and Drinfeld. More precisely, Fuchsian groups whose Rademacher symbols are rational-valued verify the statement of Manin–Drinfeld. We then establish the rationality of Rademacher symbols for various families of Fuchsian groups.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Gamma
be a noncocompact Fuchsian group. Then
\Gamma\backslash\mathbf{H}
is an open Riemann surface, and its compactification
X
can be seen as a smooth algebraic curve. Fixing a base point
x\in X
, we have an embedding
X\hookrightarrow J(X)
into its Jacobian
J(X)
. Manin and Mumford conjectured that for any such embedding, there are only finitely many torsion points in the image. The conjecture was first confirmed by Raynaud
[
Ray83
]
.

If
\Gamma
is a congruence subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, Drinfeld
[
Dri73
]
, generalizing a result of Manin
[
Man72
]
, provided significant examples of these finitely many torsion points on modular curves; the subgroup
C(\Gamma)\subset J(X)
spanned by the images of the cusps of
\Gamma
is contained in
J(X)^{\rm tor}
. Their proofs used Hecke operators, limiting the reach of the statement to modular curves. In this note, we observe a new connection between the Manin–Drinfeld theorem and Rademacher symbols, which are ubiquitous invariants appearing in connection to Dedekind sums
[
RG72
]
, class numbers of real quadratic fields
[
Mey57
,
Zag75
]
, aspects of Atiyah–Bott–Singer index theory
[
Ati87
]
, and linking numbers of knots
[
Ghy07
,
DIT17
]
. On the basis of this relation, we give a new proof of the Manin–Drinfeld theorem and examine for which other families of curves it might hold.

As is customary, we reframe this discussion in terms of divisors. Recall that a divisor is a formal sum

D=\sum a_{i}x_{i},

where
x_{i}\in X
, and
a_{i}
are integers, only finitely many of which are non-zero. The degree of the divisor
D
is the sum
\sum a_{i}
, and divisors of degree zero form a group. Let
D
be a divisor of degree zero. By the Abel–Jacobi theorem, there is an isomorphism between divisor classes (with respect to linear equivalence) of degree zero and the Jacobian
J(X)
modulo periods. By Riemann–Roch, there is a differential
\omega_{D}
of the third kind (i.e., a meromorphic differential) whose residual divisor is
D
, and this differential can be made unique; see, e.g., Theorem 5.3 in
[
Lan82
]
. Then
D
is torsion if and only if the periods of
\omega_{D}
are in
2\pi i\mathbf{Q}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a noncocompact Fuchsian group with
h
inequivalent cusps

\mathfrak{a}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{a}_{h}
. Let
D=\sum m_{i}(\mathfrak{a}_{i})
be a divisor of degree zero, and let
\omega_{D}
be the canonical differential of the third kind associated to the residue divisor
D
. Then for every hyperbolic element
\gamma\in\Gamma
of positive trace,

\int_{\gamma}\omega_{D}=2\pi i\left(m_{1}\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}(\gamma)+\dots+m_{h}\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}_{h}}(\gamma)\right),

where
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}
is the Rademacher symbol on
\Gamma
for the cusp
\mathfrak{a}
. Moreover, if all Rademacher symbols on
\Gamma
are rational-valued, then
C(\Gamma)\subset J(X)^{\rm tor}
.

We first describe Rademacher symbols as they appear in the classical theory of modular forms and elliptic functions. Dedekind showed that the transformation-theory of the
\eta
-function

(1.1)

\displaystyle\eta(z)=q^{\tfrac{1}{24}}\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n}),

(
q=e^{2\pi iz}
), under the linear fractional action of
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
on the upper half-plane
\mathbf{H}
is encoded by the Dedekind symbol

\Phi(\gamma)\ =\ \begin{cases}\frac{b}{d}&{\rm if}\ c=0,\\
\frac{a+d}{c}-12\operatorname{sign}(c)\cdot s(a,c)&{\rm if}\ c\neq 0.\end{cases}

where
s(a,c)
are Dedekind sums — arithmetic sums depending only on
a/c
, and obeying a reciprocity law that makes them easy to compute via the Euclidean algorithm. The same reciprocity law also allows to show that
\Phi(\gamma)
is always an integer. These classical facts are exposed in the monograph of Rademacher’s lecture notes on the subject; see
[
RG72
]
. In his study of Dedekind sums, Rademacher introduced the associated function

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Psi(\gamma)=\Phi(\gamma)-3\operatorname{sign}(c(a+d)),

which has the remarkable feature of being conjugacy class invariant. In fact, on hyperbolic elements, it can be realized as the period

\int_{\gamma}E_{2}(z)dz=\Psi(\gamma),

where
E_{2}
is Hecke’s modified Eisenstein series of weight 2 (see
§
3
and
[
DIT18
]
for further historical references).

This is for the classical theory. Over the last 50 years, analogues of these various functions have been constructed for other noncocompact Fuchsian groups. A standard construction builds on extending the celebrated first limit formula of Kronecker, which classically can be used to deduce the transformation-theory of
\eta
and determine the Dedekind sums. The classical first limit formula of Kronecker expresses the constant term in the Laurent expansion of the non-holomorphic Eisenstein series
E(z,s)
for
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
at its pole
s=1
. More generally, to each cusp
\mathfrak{a}
of a general noncocompact Fuchsian group, one has an associated non-holomorphic Eisenstein series
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z,s)
. On the basis of Selberg’s study of their analytic properties, one obtains a Kronecker limit formula in this extended setting, and this limit formula can be used to define a generalized Dedekind symbol
\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}
. Such constructions have been extensively studied in recent years
[
Gol73
,
Bur17
,
JOS20
]
. Much less attention has been given the associated Rademacher symbol
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}:\Gamma\to\mathbf{R}
, which we define in
§
2
. In
Lemma
3.1
, we show that
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}
can again be realized as the hyperbolic period

\int_{\gamma}E_{2,\mathfrak{a}}(z)dz=\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma).

The Fuchsian Rademacher symbols
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}
are a priori real-valued. In the face of
Theorem
1
, we do not expect Fuchsian Rademacher symbols to be rational-valued in general. In fact, Rohrlich observed that for some non-congruence subgroups of
\Gamma(2)
appearing in connection to the Fermat curve,
C(\Gamma)
must be infinite (see p. 198 in
[
KL81
]
) and thus the associated Rademacher symbols can not all be rational.
Nevertheless, for certain groups, it is possible to show that the image is in fact rational through rigorous arithmetic computations. This is the case for the Rademacher symbol
\Psi_{\infty}^{\Gamma}
when
\Gamma
is any principal congruence group
\Gamma(N)
or Hecke congruence group
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, following the works of
[
Dri83
]
,
[
Tak86
]
, and
[
Vas96
]
. By a theorem of Helling
[
Hel66
]
, every noncocompact arithmetic subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
is conjugate to a subgroup of
\Gamma_{0}(N)^{+}
, the group obtained by adding all Atkin–Lehner involutions to
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, for some squarefree
N
. (See
§
4
for an explicit parametrization.) Moreover, each maximal noncocompact arithmetic group (under inclusion) is a conjugate of
\Gamma_{0}(N)^{+}
for some squarefree
N
. (The converse does not hold; see
[
Bai87
]
.) As noted by Helling, the groups
\Gamma_{0}(N)^{+}
have a unique cusp, which can be taken at the point at
\infty
. The 44 groups
\Gamma_{0}(N)^{+}
of genus 0 famously appear in connection to monstruous moonshine.

Theorem 2
.

Rademacher symbols are rational for all genus 0 noncomcompact Fuchsian groups, for all Helling groups
\Gamma_{0}(N)^{+}
, and for all congruence subgroups of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
.

In particular, we recover a new proof of the Manin–Drinfeld theorem. By a theorem of Takeuchi
[
Tak77
]
, we know that among triangle groups, only finitely many (up to conjugation) are arithmetic; it is interesting to note that the phenomenon described by Manin–Drinfeld does not restrict to arithmetic groups. The proof of
Theorem
2
builds on certain relations between Rademacher symbols for
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma
, when
\Gamma_{1}<\Gamma
is a normal subgroup of finite index; see
Proposition
4.2
.

2.
Rademacher symbols for cusps

The classical monographs of Shimura
[
Shi71
]
and Kubota
[
Kub73
]
and the more recent book of Iwaniec
[
Iwa02
]
serve as references for facts cited in this section on Fuchsian groups and Eisenstein series.

2.1.
Cusps, Eisenstein series

Let
\mathfrak{a}
be a cusp for
\Gamma
, and let
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}
be the stabilizer subgroup of
\mathfrak{a}
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}\}
. The group
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}
is infinite cyclic. We will denote by
\gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}
the cyclic generator of
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}
. We say that two cusps
\mathfrak{a}
,
\mathfrak{b}
are equivalent if
\mathfrak{b}=\gamma\mathfrak{a}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
. If
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are equivalent, then
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\Gamma_{\mathfrak{b}}
.

We may choose a scaling transformation
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbf{R})
such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}(\infty)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{Z}\\
&1\end{pmatrix}.

Such a choice of
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}
is only unique up to right multiplication by an element of
\pm\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\mathbf{R}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Conjugating
\Gamma
by
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}
provides a group with a cusp at infinity of width 1, and facilitates computation.

The group
\Gamma
acts discontinuously on the upper half-plane
\mathbf{H}
by fractional linear transformation
\pm\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right):z\mapsto\tfrac{az+b}{cz+d}
. For each
z\in\mathbf{H}
, the Eisenstein series for the cusp
\mathfrak{a}
is defined by

E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z,s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}\backslash\Gamma}{\mathfrak{Im}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\gamma z)^{s},

where
s
is a complex parameter controlling the convergence of the infinite series; the series
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z,s)
converges absolutely and uniformly on compact subsets when
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)>1
. Beyond this half-plane, Eisenstein series admit a meromorphic continuation to the whole complex plane, as famously proved by Selberg. The definition of
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z,s)
does not depend on the particular choice of scaling
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}
. Moreover, equivalent cusps yield identical Eisenstein series.

To understand the behavior of the Eisenstein series
E_{\mathfrak{a}}
at the (not necessarily distinct) cusp
\mathfrak{b}
of
\Gamma
, one considers the series
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}z,s)
, for which we have the relation

E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}z,s)=E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma_{\mathfrak{b}}\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}z,s)=E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}(z+1),s)

and the resulting Fourier expansion is explicitly given by

E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}z,s)=\delta_{\mathfrak{ab}}y^{s}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(s-1/2)}{\Gamma(s)}\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(s)y^{1-s}+\frac{2\pi^{s}\sqrt{y}}{\Gamma(s)}\sum_{n\neq 0}|n|^{s-1/2}K_{s-1/2}(2\pi|n|y)\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,s)e(nx),

where
\delta_{\mathfrak{ab}}=1
if the cusps
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are in the same
\Gamma
-orbit, and 0 otherwise, where
\Gamma(s)
denotes the classical
\Gamma
-function, where
K_{s}(z)
is the
K
-Bessel function (or, modified Bessel function of the second kind), where
e(z)=e^{2\pi iz}
, and where
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(s)
and
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,s)
are the Dirichlet series

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c>0}c^{-2s}\#\{d\in[0,c):\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\Gamma\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}\},

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c>0}c^{-2s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq d<c\\
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\Gamma\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}\end{subarray}}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right).

These Dirichlet series do not depend on a particular representative for the cusps
\mathfrak{a}
,
\mathfrak{b}
, but
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,s)
depends on the particular choice of the scaling transformation
\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}
up to a unitary multiplicative factor. Indeed, if we replace
\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}
by
\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}n_{x}
,
n_{x}=\pm\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, then
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,s)
is replaced by
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,s)e(nx)
. Both Dirichlet series are absolutely convergent on
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)>1
.

On the vertical line
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)=1
, the Eisenstein series are holomorphic, save for a simple pole at
s=1
. Examining the local behavior of its Fourier coefficients, we note that the
K
-Bessel function is entire as a function of
s
— in fact, at
s=1
, we have
2(|n|y)^{1/2}K_{1/2}(2\pi|n|y)e(nx)=e^{-2\pi|n|y}e(nx)
, see
[
Iwa02
, p. 227]
—, and that the Dirichlet series
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,s)
are holomorphic along the vertical line
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)=1
. At
s=1
,
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,1)
is real valued, hence
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,1)=\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(-n,1)
, and satisfies the estimate
|\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,1)|\ll n^{1+\epsilon}
, for any
\epsilon>0
; see
[
JO05
, Theorem 1.1]
. Finally, the Dirichlet series defining
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(s)
has a simple pole at
s=1
of residue
(\pi V)^{-1}
, where
V
denotes the hyperbolic volume of any fundamental domain for
\Gamma
. Thus
\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(s)
has a Laurent expansion at
s=1
of the form

\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(s)=\frac{(\pi V)^{-1}}{s-1}+\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}(s-1)^{n}.

2.2.
Kronecker’s first limit formula

An extension (see
[
Gol73
, Theorem 3-1]
) of Kronecker’s classical first limit formula gives the constant coefficient in the Laurent series for
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}z,s)
at the simple pole at
s=1
. Using the Fourier expansion of the Eisenstein series and letting
s\to 1^{+}
, we immediately obtain the corresponding formal Laurent series at
s=1
, given by

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 1^{+}}\left(E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}z,s)-\frac{V^{-1}}{s-1}\right)\

\displaystyle=\ c_{0}-V^{-1}\log y+\delta_{\mathfrak{ab}}y+\sum_{n>0}\pi\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,1)e(nz)+\sum_{n<0}\pi\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,1)e(n\overline{z})

\displaystyle=\ c_{0}-V^{-1}\log y-{\mathfrak{Re}}\left(\delta_{\mathfrak{ab}}iz-2\pi\sum_{n>0}\varphi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(n,1)e(nz)\right).

We denote the term in parenthesis by
f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z)
and record that
f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}n_{x}}(z)=f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(n_{x}z)-\delta_{\mathfrak{ab}}ix
. As a function of
z
,
f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}
is holomorphic; this establishes the validity of the limit formula.

Specializing to
\Gamma=\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, we recover the classical first limit formula of Kronecker as follows. We have

\displaystyle E(z,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c,d\in\mathbf{Z}\\
(c,d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{y^{s}}{|cz+d|^{2s}},\qquad\varphi(s)=\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{\phi(c)}{c^{2s}},

\displaystyle\varphi(n,s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\geq 1}c^{-2s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1\\
(c,d)=1\end{subarray}}^{c}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right)=\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{1}{c^{2s}}\sum_{\delta|(c,n)}\delta\mu\left(\frac{c}{\delta}\right)=\frac{1}{\zeta(2s)}\sum_{\delta|n}\frac{1}{\delta},

where
\phi
is Euler’s totient function, and we have used Kluyven’s identity for Ramanujan sums in the last line. Using that
\zeta(2)=\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{6}
,

f(z)=\frac{12}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi iz}{12}-\sum_{m,n>0}\frac{q^{mn}}{m}\right),

and the term in parenthesis is a branch of the logarithm of Dedekind’s
\eta
-function, defined by (
1.1
).

2.3.
Dedekind and Rademacher symbols

We introduce two auxiliary functions on
\Gamma
; the inner automorphism
\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma)=\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\gamma\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}
, and the automorphic cocycle
j(\gamma,z)=cz+d
. The latter is a multiplicative cocycle: for any pair
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
, we have
j(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2},z)=j(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}z)j(\gamma_{2},z)
. The limit formula derived above implies the relation

{\mathfrak{Re}}f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\tau_{\mathfrak{b}}(\gamma)z)=V^{-1}\ln|j(\tau_{\mathfrak{b}}(\gamma),z)|^{2}+{\mathfrak{Re}}f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z)

for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
. We choose
\log z
to be the principal branch of logarithm, i.e.,
\log z=\ln|z|+i\arg(z)
with
\arg(z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
. Then
\log(-j(\gamma,z)^{2})
is well defined, and
{\mathfrak{Re}}\left(\operatorname{sign}(c)^{2}\log(-j(\gamma,z)^{2})\right)=\ln|j(\gamma,z)|^{2}
. Therefore, the holomorphic function

(2.1)

\displaystyle F_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma;z):=f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma)z)-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z)-V^{-1}\operatorname{sign}(c_{\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma)})^{2}\log\left(-j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma),z)^{2}\right)

has trivial real part, and thus by the Open Mapping Theorem, it must be a constant function of
z
, i.e.,
F_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma;z)=F_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma)
. As a result, it is also independent of the particular choice of the scaling transformation
\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}
, since

F_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}n_{x}}(\gamma)=f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{b}}(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma)n_{x}z)-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(n_{x}z)-V^{-1}\operatorname{sign}(c_{\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma)})^{2}\log\left(-j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma),n_{x}z)^{2}\right)=F_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(\gamma).

We henceforth denote this function
F_{\mathfrak{ab}}
. We can now define the Dedekind and Rademacher symbols attached to the cusp
\mathfrak{a}
. Set

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-iF_{\mathfrak{ab}}(\gamma),

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Phi_{\mathfrak{aa}}(\gamma),

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)-\pi V^{-1}\operatorname{sign}(c(a+d)),

with
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma)
.
When
\Gamma=\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbf{Z})
, we recover the classical Dedekind and Rademacher symbols
\Phi
and
\Psi
; see Chapters 4A-C in
[
RG72
]
. A detailed study of the properties of (a slight modification of) the Dedekind symbols
\Phi_{\mathfrak{ab}}
appears in
[
JOS20
]
. We only record here that, following the analysis in pp. 52–53 of
[
RG72
]
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})=\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma_{1})+\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma_{2})-\pi V^{-1}\operatorname{sign}(c_{1}c_{2}c_{3})

for any
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
, with
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
c_{1}&*\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma_{1})
,
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
c_{2}&*\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma_{2})
, and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
c_{3}&*\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})
. The following relations follow immediately:

\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(I)=\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(I)=0
,
\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(-\gamma)=\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)
,
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(-\gamma)=\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)
,

\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma^{-1})=-\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)
,
\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma^{-1})=-\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma).

Proposition 2.1
.

Rademacher symbols are rational on elliptic and parabolic motions.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a parabolic element. Recall that
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{b}}
is an infinite cyclic group and denote its generator by
\gamma_{\mathfrak{b}}
. Then
\gamma=\gamma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbf{N}
. We first observe that
\Phi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(\gamma)=-i\left(f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z+m)-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z)\right)=\delta_{\mathfrak{ab}}\cdot m
. Hence
\Phi_{\mathfrak{b}}
is an integer on
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{b}}
. Suppose now that
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are inequivalent cusps. Since
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}z,s)=E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}z^{\prime},s)
with
z^{\prime}=\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}z
, the Kronecker limit formula implies the relation

f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z^{\prime})-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(z)=V^{-1}\log\left(-j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}},z)^{2}\right)+\text{constant}.

Then

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=-i\left(f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma)z)-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(z)-V^{-1}\log\left(-j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma),z)^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-i\left(f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z^{\prime}+m)-V^{-1}\log(-j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}},\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma_{\mathfrak{b}})z)^{2}\right)-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad+V^{-1}\log\left(-j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}},z)^{2}\right)-V^{-1}\log\left(-j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma),z)^{2}\right))

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathfrak{ab}}(\gamma)+2iV^{-1}\left(\log j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}},\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma)z)+\log j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma),z)-\log j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}},z)+i\frac{\pi}{2}A_{\mathfrak{ab}}(\gamma)\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathfrak{ab}}\cdot m+2iV^{-1}\left(2\pi iB_{\mathfrak{ab}}(\gamma)+i\frac{\pi}{2}A_{\mathfrak{ab}}(\gamma)\right),

where
A_{\mathfrak{ab}}
and
B_{\mathfrak{ab}}
are integer-valued. Looking at the Gauss–Bonnet formula for
V
(see, e.g.,
[
Iwa02
, p. 46]
), we have
\pi V^{-1}\in\mathbf{Q}
and this concludes. Let now
\gamma
be an elliptic element of order
m
. Applying (
2.2
) recursively, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle 0=\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma^{m})

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma

\int_{\gamma}E_{2,\mathfrak{a}}(z)dz=\Psi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma).

Proof.

\gamma

\displaystyle\int_{z_{0}}^{\gamma z_{0}}E_{2,\mathfrak{a}}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\sigma^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}z_{0}}^{\sigma^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}\gamma z_{0}}E_{2,\mathfrak{a}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}z)\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{\prime}(z)dz

\displaystyle=-V^{-1}\int_{\sigma^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}z_{0}}^{\sigma^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}\gamma z_{0}}\frac{dz}{y}+\frac{1}{i}\left(f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma)\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0})-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0})\right).

\frac{dz}{y}\circ g-\frac{dz}{y}=\frac{|cz+d|^{2}-(cz+d)^{2}}{(cz+d)^{2}y}dz=-\frac{2ic}{cz+d}dz=-2i\ d\log j(g,z)

\int_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0}}^{\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma)\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0}}\frac{dz}{y}=-2i\log j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma),\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0}).

\displaystyle f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma)\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0})-f_{\mathfrak{a},\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0})=i\Phi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\gamma)+V^{-1}\operatorname{sign}(c_{\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma)})^{2}\log\left(-j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma),\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\log\left(-j(\tau_{\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}}(\gamma),\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}z_{0})^{2}\right)

Writing

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}\backslash\Gamma}j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\gamma,z)^{-2}=\sum_{\tau}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1,\mathfrak{a}}\backslash\Gamma_{1}}j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\gamma\tau,z)^{-2}=\sum_{\tau}j(\tau,z)^{-2}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1,\mathfrak{a}}\backslash\Gamma_{1}}j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\gamma,\tau z)^{-2}.

Notice that this equality does not depend on the particular choice of coset representatives. Hence for each hyperbolic element

Suppose that
\tau\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{b}

\delta=\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}^{-1}\tau\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}.
Since
