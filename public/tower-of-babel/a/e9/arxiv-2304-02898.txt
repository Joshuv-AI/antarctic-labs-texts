Fluctuations in the logarithmic energy for zeros of random polynomials on the sphere
Marcus Michelen, Oren Yakir

Abstract
. Smale’s Seventh Problem asks for an efficient algorithm to generate a configuration of n n points on the sphere that nearly minimizes the logarithmic energy. As a candidate starting configuration for this problem, Armentano, Beltrán and Shub considered the set of points given by the stereographic projection of the roots of the random elliptic polynomial of degree n n and computed the expected logarithmic energy. We study the fluctuations of the logarithmic energy associated to this random configuration and prove a central limit theorem. Our approach shows that all cumulants of the logarithmic energy are asymptotically linear in n n , and hence the energy is well-concentrated on the scale of n \sqrt{n} .



1.
Introduction

Consider the random polynomial of degree
n\geqslant 1
defined by

(1)

f_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\,\sqrt{\binom{n}{j}}\,z^{j}\,,\qquad{z\in\mathbb{C}}

where
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian random variables. This model for random polynomials, usually referred to as elliptic polynomials, Kostlan polynomials or the
SU(2)
model, was introduced in the physics literature by works of Bogomolny, Bohigas, Leboeuf
[
10
,
11
]
and Hannay
[
18
]
, and from the mathematical point of view by Kostlan
[
20
]
and Shub, Smale
[
31
]
. A remarkable feature of this model is that the random point process on the sphere

\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,\|x\|=1\}

given by the stereographic projection of the zero set of
f_{n}
has a distribution which is invariant under isometries of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, see Figure
1
. In fact, it is the unique Gaussian analytic function with this invariance property, see
[
33
]
or
[
7
, Theorem 2.5.2]
.

Figure 1.
Realization of the zero set of the random elliptic polynomial of degree
n=300
(left) and its stereographic projection (right).

1.1.
Logarithmic energy

Given a collection of points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
the discrete logarithmic energy is given by

\mathcal{E}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i\not=j}\log\frac{1}{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}\,.

The asymptotic behavior of the minimum of this energy has been extensively studied, see for example the survey paper
[
13
]
. Smale’s Seventh Problem
[
32
]
asks whether one can find an efficient algorithm which generates configurations of points on the sphere whose logarithmic energy is close to the minimum. By relating the discrete minimization problem to a continuous variational problem in the
n=\infty
limit, Bétermin and Sandier
[
8
]
showed that there exists a constant
C_{{\sf{min}}}
so that

(2)

\min_{x_{1}\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathcal{E}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\log n+C_{\sf{min}}n+o(n)\,.

as
n\to\infty
. The exact value of
C_{{\sf{min}}}
is not known, with the best-known current bounds giving

-0.0569...\leqslant C_{\sf{min}}\leqslant 2\log 2+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{2}{3}+3\log\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{{\Gamma}(1/3)}=-0.0556...

see
[
22
,
36
]
. It is conjectured that the upper bound on
C_{\sf{min}}
is in fact its true value, see
[
8
,
13
]
. Points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
which achieve the minimum in (
2
) are called elliptic Fekete points.

Motivated by constructing near minima to the minimization problem (
2
), it is natural to study random configurations of points on the sphere which are expected to have small logarithmic energy. If we put
n
independent, uniformly distributed points on the sphere then it is easy to check that the expected logarithmic energy is

\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n

which agrees with the minimal value (
2
) up to a
\Theta(n\log n)
term. One downside to using uniformly distributed points on the sphere is that there will be many pairs of points that are quite close to each other; this has the effect of introducing an extra term of order
n\log n
over the minimum possible energy. In order to obtain a random configuration of points with smaller expected logarithmic energy, one route is to look at a distribution of points that experiences repulsion between points.

An idea introduced by Armentano, Beltrán and Shub
[
3
]
is to consider the stereographic projection of the zeros of the random elliptic polynomial given by (
1
). As the zeros of random functions tend to repel each other, the points are unlikely to clump and hence the energy is expected to be small. Indeed, Armentano, Beltrán and Shub
[
3
]
proved that the expected logarithmic energy for this model is given by

\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\log n-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n\,.

That is, the expected logarithmic energy for the elliptic zeros agrees with the minimal value (
2
) up to a
\Theta(n)
term.

1.2.
Main result

Throughout this paper, we identify the Riemann sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with the extended complex plane
\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
via the stereographic projection. The spherical distance on
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is the three-dimensional Euclidean distance after stereographic projection and is given explicitly by

(3)

d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z,w)=\frac{2|z-w|}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}}\,,\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{C}

and

d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z,\infty)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}}\,.

Denote by
\mathcal{Z}=\{\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}\}
the zero set of the random polynomial
f_{n}
given by (
1
), and consider the corresponding logarithmic energy

(4)

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\mathcal{E}_{n}(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n})=-\sum_{i\not=j}\log d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\zeta_{i},\zeta_{j})\,.

The main result of
[
3
]
(see also Lemma
2.6
below) states that

(5)

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{E}_{n}\big]=\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\log n-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n\,.

In this paper, we prove that the logarithmic energy is well concentrated around its mean by proving a central limit theorem for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
at the scale
\sqrt{n}
. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the logarithmic energy (
4
) of the roots of the random polynomial
f_{n}
given by (
1
). There exists a constant
c_{\ast}>0
such that

{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{E}_{n}\big]=c_{\ast}n\big(1+o(1)\big)

as
n\to\infty
.
Furthermore, the sequence of random variables

\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}_{n}]}{\sqrt{c_{\ast}\,n}}

converges in distribution as
n\to\infty
to the standard (real) Gaussian law.

The constant
c_{\ast}
is somewhat explicit and is given as a sum of several integrals, see Section
6
(and, in particular, see (
76
)) for more details. Numerical computations of these integrals show that

c_{\ast}\approx 0.0907056\,.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
will follow the method of moments, and in fact we will prove that for all
k\geqslant 1
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}_{n}])^{2k}\right]}{n^{k}}=\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}c_{\ast}^{k}\,.

By Chebyshev’s inequality, this proves concentration of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

For each
A\geqslant 0
there exists a constant
C_{A}
so that for all
T>0
and
n\geqslant 1
we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}_{n}]\big|\geqslant T\sqrt{n}\,\Big)\leqslant C_{A}T^{-A}\,.

Conversely, the central limit theorem of Theorem
1.1
shows a limiting probability for fluctuations on the scale
\sqrt{n}
, namely that for each fixed
T
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{E}_{n}\big]<-T\sqrt{n}\Big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi c_{\ast}}}\int_{-\infty}^{{-T}}e^{-t^{2}/(2c_{\ast})}\,{\rm d}t\,.

In the context of Smale’s Seventh Problem, Armentano, Beltrán and Shub
[
3
]
introduced the idea of using random polynomials to construct near-minimizers for the logarithmic energy; they outline the strategy of starting with a configuration generated by the roots of
f_{n}
and subsequently performing gradient descent in order to further minimize the energy. The recent book of Borodachov, Hardin and Saff
[
12
]
contains descriptions of many other point configurations on the sphere and numerically compares their logarithmic energy, all of which are deterministic aside from uniformly random points. Notably,
all
of the point configurations they consider appear to have logarithmic energy varying by
\Omega(n)
from the suspected minimizer (see
[
12
, Figure 7.16]
), as is the case for the configuration considered here. One potential benefit of starting with a random configuration of points, such as the configuration generated by random polynomials, is that the function
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}:(\mathbb{S}^{2})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
appears to have exponentially many local minima in
n
(see the discussion in
[
5
]
). One possibility is that a random configuration such as one provided by random polynomials may provide a good starting point for a subsequent algorithm such as gradient descent.

1.3.
Related Works

As mentioned above, the expectation of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
was already computed by Armentano, Beltrán and Shub in
[
3
]
. Recently, de la Torre and Marzo
[
15
]
gave a different approach for computing the expectation which allowed them, among other things, to study the expected Riesz energies for the random elliptic zeros; this amounts to replacing the logarithm in the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
with a function of the form
z\mapsto z^{s}
. Another possible generalization is to consider the analogous expected energies for random sections of any positive line bundle over compact Kähler manifolds, see Feng and Zelditch
[
16
]
.

A natural question is to study the logarithmic energy for other point processes on the sphere which exhibits repulsion, with the hope of finding an example with energy closer to the minimum in (
2
). A natural candidate is the spherical ensemble, where one lets
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
be independent random matrices with i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian entries and stereographically projects the eigenvalues of
A_{n}^{-1}B_{n}
onto the sphere. Krishnapur
[
21
]
showed that this ensemble is in fact a determinantal point process on the sphere, and so many computations become tractable. Indeed, Alishahi and Zamani
[
1
]
computed the asymptotic expected energies (both logarithmic and Riesz) as the number of points in the process tends to infinity. Interestingly, the expected logarithmic energy for the spherical ensemble is asymptotically larger than that of the elliptic zeros, which hints that the random zeros are closer to being elliptic Fekete points. An advantage for working with the spherical ensemble is that it generalizes to any dimension, and indeed Beltrán, Marzo and Ortega-Cerdà
[
6
]
computed the asymptotic for the expected logarithmic energy in arbitrary dimension.

In terms of computing the variance or proving central limit theorems for these types of energies, not much seems to be known. In the context of independent uniform points on the sphere, the logarithmic (or Riesz) energy is a special case of a more general framework of studying U-statistics, see
[
19
, Chap. 6]
. Indeed, a central limit theorem for U-statistics of a Poisson point process (in a general measure space) was proved by Reitzner and Schulte in
[
28
]
. Another work in this direction is by Błaszczyszyn, Yogeshwaran and Yukich
[
9
]
which proved, among other things, a central limit theorem for local score functions for general stationary point processes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As the logarithmic energy is not a local quantity (the work
[
9
]
requires the interaction to decay exponentially with distance), their result do not apply in our case. Perhaps more relevant to our work is the Wiener chaos expansion (sometimes referred to as the Hermite-Itô expansion); an orthogonal decomposition of non-linear functionals of some underlying Gaussian processes, see
[
19
, Chap. 2]
. In the context of Gaussian analytic functions the chaos technique was used, either implicitly or explicitly, in
[
14
,
26
,
34
]
to prove normal fluctuations for linear statistics of the zeros. The main difference between the logarithmic energy (studied in this paper) and the previous works is that the logarithmic energy is
not
a linear statistic of the roots but rather a singular function of the distinct pairs. Nevertheless, we will use the Wiener chaos framework to obtain a simpler form for the limiting variance, see Section
6
.

1.4.
Outline of the proof

The proof of Theorem
1.1
begins by finding an expression for the logarithmic energy
\mathcal{E}_{n}
that reflects the spherical symmetry inherent in the problem. This takes the form of Lemma
2.1
, in which we write

(6)

\mathcal{E}_{n}-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n^{2}+\frac{n\log n}{2}-(\log 2)n=n\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(z)|\,{\rm d}\mu(z)-\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{n}}\log|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\zeta)|

where
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is the zero set of
f_{n}
and
\widehat{f}_{n},D\widehat{f}_{n}
are given as

\widehat{f}_{n}(z)=\frac{f_{n}(z)}{(1+|z|^{2})^{n/2}}\,,\qquad D\widehat{f}_{n}(z)=\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{\sqrt{n}(1+|z|^{2})^{n/2-1}}\,.

Here and everywhere,
m
is the Lebesgue measure and

(7)

{\rm d}\mu(z)=\frac{{\rm d}m(z)}{\pi(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}

is the uniform (probability) measure on
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
.
From here, we see that the two terms on the right-hand side of (
6
) both have distribution which is invariant under spherical isometries of the Riemann sphere. To get a feel for why a central limit should hold, note that a simple calculation shows that the Gaussian process
f_{n}
decorrelates on the scale of
1/\sqrt{n}
with respect to the spherical metric. This means that for points
w,z
with
d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(w,z)\gg n^{-1/2}
, we expect
f_{n}
to behave roughly independently when evaluated at
z
and
w
. The two terms on the right-hand side (
6
) may each be understood as similar to sums of
n
nearly independent random variables. Another key observation, is that the contribution to the right-hand side of (
6
) coming from spherical caps of radius
\sim n^{-1/2}
contribute a random quantity with fluctuations of constant order. The heuristics above suggests that the total fluctuations should be of order
\sqrt{n}
.

1.4.1.
The Gaussian Entire Function

One way to understand the scaling factor
1/\sqrt{n}
is to see that the rescaled function

(8)

f_{n}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\,\sqrt{n^{-j}\binom{n}{j}}\,z^{j}

has a non-degenerate limit as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, recall that the Gaussian Entire Function (G.E.F.) is defined by the random Taylor series

(9)

g(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\frac{z^{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\,,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}

where, as before,
\{a_{j}\}
is an i.i.d. sequence of standard complex Gaussian random variables. A remarkable feature of the random zeros of the G.E.F. is its distribution invariance with respect to isometries of the plane
\mathbb{C}
, see
[
27
,
34
]
. It is not difficult to check (see Claim
3.1
below), that the sequence of Gaussian processes
f_{n}(\cdot/\sqrt{n})
converges as
n\to\infty
to the G.E.F. in some appropriate sense. Using this observation, we will show that as
n
becomes large the local contributions to the right-hand side of (
6
) is close to a similar contribution coming from the zeros of the G.E.F., with the later contribution having typical fluctuations of constant order. We note that our analysis could be made to work without making use of the G.E.F. as the scaling limit, and that the use of this scaling limit is for convenience and motivation for certain calculations.

1.4.2.
Intensity functions

The
k
-point functions for
k\geqslant 1
express correlations within
k
points from the zero set
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
. It is the symmetric function

\rho=\rho_{k}:\big\{Z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}:\,z_{i}\not=z_{j}\ \text{for }i\not=j\big\}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}

defined via the relation

\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\#(\mathcal{Z}_{n}\cap B_{j})\Big]=\int_{B_{1}\times\ldots\times B_{k}}\rho(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\,{\rm d}m(z_{1})\cdots{\rm d}m(z_{k})\,,

where
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}
is any family of disjoint Borel sets. For each fixed
k\geqslant 1
, the
k
-point function for
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
exists for all
n
large enough, see for example
[
7
, Corrolary 3.4.2]
. To justify the almost independence argument suggested in Section
1.4
, we will prove a (strong) clustering property for the intensity functions of the random zero set
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}_{n}
, which may be of independent interest. The clustering property is a quantitative manifestation of near independence for points from the process at large distances, see
[
29
, §4.4]
, in the sense that the correlations decay with the distance.

1.4.3.
Clustering of the random zeros

Let
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}
be a non-trivial subset, set
J=\{1,\ldots,k\}\setminus I
and let
Z_{I}=\{z_{i_{1}},\ldots,z_{i_{\ell}}\}
and define
Z_{J}
analogously. We denote

d(Z_{I},Z_{J})=\inf_{i\in I,j\in J}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{i},z_{j})\,.

Theorem 1.3
.

For all
k\geqslant 2
there exists constants
c_{k},C_{k},D_{k}>0
such that the following holds. For any non-trivial partition
\{1,\ldots,k\}=I\sqcup J
such that
d(Z_{I},Z_{J})\geqslant D_{k}/\sqrt{n}
we have

\big|\rho(Z)-\rho(Z_{I})\rho(Z_{J})\big|\leqslant C_{k}\,n^{k}e^{-c_{k}nd(Z_{I},Z_{J})^{2}}

for all
n\geqslant 1
large enough.

In fact, due to the non-linear nature of the logarithmic energy
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we will need to prove this clustering property for more complicated densities (like the ones defined in Definition
2.4
below), and the clustering property for the
k
-point function is a special case of the more general result we will prove, see remark after Theorem
2.9
. In
[
25
]
Nazarov and Sodin proved, among other things, the clustering property holds for the zero set of the G.E.F. Our proof for the clustering property for
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
follows their ideas at certain steps, modulo some technicalities which come from the different nature of the densities we consider, see Section
4
for more details. Additionally, a necessary feature of our approach shows that the clustering property holds quantitatively as
n
tends to infinity, and not only in the limiting case of the G.E.F.

1.4.4.
Degeneracy near the diagonal

When working with the
k
-point functions of
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
, we will need to understand their behavior near the diagonal. To illustrate the technical problem, we will focus here just on the case
k=2
. The classical
Kac-Rice formula
(sometimes also attributed to Hammersley) reads that for
z\not=w,

(10)

\rho_{2}(z,w)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[|f_{n}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\,|f_{n}^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\mid f_{n}(z)=f_{n}(w)=0\big]}{\pi^{2}\det\big[\operatorname{Cov}(f_{n}(z),f_{n}(w))\big]}

see
[
7
, Corollary 3.4.2]
or
[
4
, Theorem 6.3]
. The issue now becomes apparent: if
z
and
w
are close then the Gaussian vector
\big(f_{n}(z),f_{n}(w)\big)
becomes nearly-degenerate and both the numerator and the denominator in (
10
) tend to zero. To handle this issue of degeneracy, we will consider divided differences of our polynomial
f_{n}
. For instance, in our example, if
z
and
w
are very close to each other, rather than considering the nearly-degenerate Gaussian vector
\big(f_{n}(z),f_{n}(w)\big)
, we will consider instead

\left(f_{n}(z),\frac{f_{n}(w)-f_{n}(z)}{w-z}\right)

which we will show is uniformly non-degenerate provided
z
and
w
are not too far apart. Bounds on the Kac-Rice density will follow from rewriting the density entirely in terms of these non-degenerate divided differences, and showing that the singularities in the denominator are canceled out by a similar term in the numerator.

The use of divided differences to study random polynomials has been developed in recent years, pioneered in the works by Ancona-Letendre
[
2
]
and then further developed by Gass
[
17
]
(see also Michelen-Sahasrabudhe
[
23
]
for another instance). One benefit of this more combinatorial approach is that it allows one to control all moments of the logarithmic energy, providing concentration of the form of Corollary
1.2
in addition to a limit theorem. It is also worth mentioning that in
[
25
]
, Nazarov and Sodin also dealt with a similar degeneracy of the intensity functions (of the random zeros of Gaussian analytic functions) near the diagonal using simple tools from complex analysis. As we will work with a representation of the divided differences using Cauchy integrals of rational functions (see (
34
) below), our approach can be thought of as a unification of the approaches considered above.

Organization of the paper

Section
2
contains some preliminary results and the breakdown of the proof of Theorem
1.1
as described in the outline. To deal with the joint moments (or rather, the joint cumulants) of the two terms of the right-hand side of (
6
) we will introduce Kac-Rice type densities, similar to the one described in (
10
) but adapted to the logarithmic nature of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. The bulk of the argument is to show that these densities satisfy an upper bound (Theorem
2.7
) and approximately factor when evaluated on two collections of points of spherical distance
\gg n^{-1/2}
apart (Theorem
2.9
). Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.7
and in Section
4
we prove Theorem
2.9
. With these two statements in mind, a combinatorial argument which is carried out in Section
5
shows that each cumulant of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is asymptotically linear in
n
. From here, to conclude the desired central limit theorem we just need to show that the variance is not
o(n)
, and this is indeed shown in Section
6
.

2.
Preliminaries

Recall that
f_{n}
is the random polynomial given by (
1
) with zero set
\mathcal{Z}
. In fact,
f_{n}
is the mean-zero (complex) Gaussian process on
\mathbb{C}
, with the covariance kernel

(11)

K(z,w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\big[f_{n}(z)\overline{f_{n}(w)}\big]=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}(z\overline{w})^{j}=\big(1+z\overline{w}\big)^{n}\,.

In view of (
11
), it is natural to consider the following normalization

(12)

\widehat{f}_{n}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\frac{f_{n}(z)}{(1+|z|^{2})^{n/2}}\,.

We will also normalize the corresponding derivative as

(13)

D\widehat{f}_{n}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\frac{f_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{\sqrt{n}(1+|z|^{2})^{n/2-1}}\,.

We will see in Section
2.2
that this scaling allows us to see the spherical symmetry underlying the function
f_{n}
.

2.1.
Rewriting the logarithmic energy

The first key step towards proving Theorem
1.1
will be rewriting the logarithmic energy (
4
) in a way that reveals the spherical symmetry. Denote by

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}n\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(z)|\,{\rm d}\mu(z)

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}}\log|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\zeta)|\,.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the logarithmic energy (
4
) and let
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be given by (
14
) and (
15
), respectively. Then

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n^{2}-\frac{n\log n}{2}+\mathcal{I}_{n}-\mathcal{S}_{n}+(\log 2)n\,.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{Z}=\{\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}\}
and expand

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\not=j}\log\frac{2|\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{j}|}{\sqrt{1+|\zeta_{i}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|\zeta_{j}|^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2(n-1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\sqrt{1+|\zeta_{j}|^{2}}-\sum_{i\not=j}\log|\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{j}|-\log(2)\,n(n-1)\,.

Noting that for each fixed
j
we have
|f^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})|=|a_{n}|\prod_{i\neq j}|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{i}|
yields

(16)

\mathcal{E}_{n}=2(n-1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\sqrt{1+|\zeta_{j}|^{2}}-(\log 2)\,n(n-1)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log|f^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})|+n\log|a_{n}|\,.

This equality appears as
[
3
, Proposition 1]
, with an extra factor
\log 2
due to different normalization of the spherical metric. The lemma follows from the elementary calculation

(17)

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|f_{n}(z)|\,{\rm d}\mu(z)=\log|a_{n}|+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\sqrt{1+|\zeta_{j}|^{2}}\,.

Indeed, by plugging (
17
) into (
16
) we get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}+

\displaystyle\log(2)\,n(n-1)

\displaystyle=\left(n-2\right)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\sqrt{1+|\zeta_{j}|^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log|f^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})|+n\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|f(z)|\,{\rm d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle=n\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left(\frac{|f(z)|}{(1+|z|^{2})^{n/2}}\right)\,{\rm d}\mu(z)+\frac{n^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left(1+|z|^{2}\right)\,{\rm d}\mu(z)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\left(\frac{|f^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})|}{(1+|\zeta_{j}|^{2})^{n/2-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{2}-\frac{n\log n}{2}+\mathcal{I}_{n}-\mathcal{S}_{n}\,,

where in the last equality we used

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\big(1+|z|^{2}\big)\,{\rm d}\mu(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}2t\,\frac{\log(1+t^{2})}{(1+t^{2})^{2}}\,{\rm d}t=1\,.

To conclude the proof of the lemma, it remains to prove (
17
). Since
f_{n}
is a polynomial, it is enough if we prove that for all
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|z-\zeta|\,{\rm d}\mu(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\big(1+|\zeta|^{2}\big)\,.

Recalling Jensen’s formula

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|te^{i\theta}-\zeta|\,{\rm d}\theta=\begin{cases}\log|\zeta|&t<|\zeta|\,,\\
\log t&t\geqslant|\zeta|\,;\end{cases}

we can compute in polar coordinates and get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|z-\zeta|\,{\rm d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{(1+t^{2})^{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|te^{i\theta}-\zeta|\,{\rm d}\theta\bigg)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\log|\zeta|\int_{0}^{|\zeta|}\frac{2t}{(1+t^{2})^{2}}\,{\rm d}t+\int_{|\zeta|}^{\infty}\log(t)\,\frac{2t}{(1+t^{2})^{2}}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\log|\zeta|\frac{|\zeta|^{2}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\log(1+|\zeta|^{2})-\log|\zeta|+\frac{\log|\zeta|}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\log(1+|\zeta|^{2})\,.

This proves (
17
) and we are done.
∎

In view of Lemma
2.1
, the central limit theorem for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
will follow if we can prove that
\big(\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{S}_{n}\big)
, after proper normalization, converges in distribution as

to a non-trivial Gaussian vector in

. We prove the latter statement by computing the joint cumulants of

and

Theorem 2.2
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sf Var\left[\mathcal{I}_{n}\right]}{n}=c_{1}\,,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sf Var\left[\mathcal{S}_{n}\right]}{n}=c_{2}\,,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sf Cov\left[\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{S}_{n}\right]}{n}=c_{3}\,.

Furthermore, we have the relation
c_{\ast}=c_{1}+c_{2}-2c_{3}>0
, where

is the limiting constant from Theorem

The proof of Theorem

is given in Section

. The fact that the limiting constant is positive is then proved in Appendix

), see Lemma

2.2.
Spherical symmetry

Recall that the law of the random zero set
\mathcal{Z}
is invariant under rotations of the Riemann sphere. We wish to establish the same property also for the joint law of the random variables (
14
) and (
15
). Every isometry of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is of the form

(18)

\tau(z)=\frac{\alpha z+\beta}{\overline{\alpha}-\overline{\beta}z}

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
. Given such an isometry
\tau
, we set

u_{\tau}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\bigg(\frac{\overline{\alpha}-\overline{\beta}z}{|\overline{\alpha}-\overline{\beta}z|}\bigg)^{n}\,.

Recalling that
f_{n}
is the random polynomial given by (
1
), it is straightforward to check that the random function
f_{n}^{\tau}
defined by

(19)

f_{n}^{\tau}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}u_{\tau}(z)^{-1}\bigg(\frac{1+|z|^{2}}{1+|\tau(z)|^{2}}\bigg)^{n/2}f_{n}(\tau(z))\,,

has the same law as
f_{n}
, (see
[
7
, §2.3]
). In particular,
f_{n}^{\tau}
is a polynomial.

Suppose that
Z=(Z_{1},Z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{a+b}
is a mean-zero complex Gaussian random vector with
Z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{a}
and
Z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{b}
,
a,b\geqslant 1
. The covariance matrix can be partitioned in the obvious way

\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\Sigma_{12}\\
\Sigma_{12}^{\ast}&\Sigma_{22}\end{bmatrix}\,.

We will frequently use the basic fact that on the event
\{Z_{1}=\mathbf{0}\}
, where
\mathbf{0}=(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{C}^{a}
, the conditional law of
Z_{2}
is also complex Gaussian in
\mathbb{C}^{b}
with mean-zero and covariance matrix

\Sigma_{22}-\Sigma_{12}^{\ast}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\Sigma_{12}\,,

(see for instance
[
7
, Ex. 2.1.3]
or
[
30
, §2.13, Theorem 2]
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and suppose
w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then the law of the random vector

\big(|\widehat{f}_{n}(w_{1})|,\ldots,|\widehat{f}_{n}(w_{\ell})|,|D\widehat{f}_{n}(z_{1})|,\ldots,|D\widehat{f}_{n}(z_{m})|\big)

conditioned on the event
\big\{f(z_{1})=\ldots=f(z_{m})=0\big\}
is rotation invariant. That is, for any isometry
\tau
of the form (
18
), the above law does not change if
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
are replaced by
\tau(z_{1}),\ldots,\tau(z_{m})
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}
are replaced by
\tau(w_{1}),\ldots\tau(w_{\ell})
.

Proof.

Let
\tau
be an isometry of the form (
18
). Recall that our random polynomial
f_{n}
has the same law as
f_{n}^{\tau}
which is given by (
19
). Therefore, conditioned on the event
\big\{f(z_{1})=\ldots=f(z_{m})=0\big\}
, the Gaussian random vector

\big(f(w_{1}),\ldots,f(w_{\ell}),f^{\prime}(z_{1}),\ldots,f^{\prime}(z_{m})\big)

has the same law as the Gaussian vector

\big(f_{n}^{\tau}(w_{1}),\ldots,f_{n}^{\tau}(w_{\ell}),(f_{n}^{\tau})^{\prime}(z_{1}),\ldots,(f_{n}^{\tau})^{\prime}(z_{m})\big)

conditioned on the event
\big\{f_{n}(\tau(z_{1}))=\ldots=f_{n}(\tau(z_{m}))=0\big\}
. If
f_{n}(\zeta)=0
for some point
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
, then

(f_{n}^{\tau})^{\prime}(\zeta)=u_{\tau}(\zeta)^{-1}\bigg(\frac{1+|\zeta|^{2}}{1+|\tau(\zeta)|^{2}}\bigg)^{n/2}f_{n}^{\prime}(\tau(\zeta))\,\tau^{\prime}(\zeta)\,.

Direct computation shows that

\tau^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{\big(\overline{\alpha}-\overline{\beta}z\big)^{2}}\,,\qquad|\tau^{\prime}(z)|=\frac{1+|\tau(z)|^{2}}{1+|z|^{2}}\,.

Since
|u_{\tau}(z)|=1
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we conclude that the random vector

\big(|f(w_{1})|,\ldots,|f(w_{\ell})|,|f^{\prime}(z_{1})|,\ldots,|f^{\prime}(z_{m})|\big)

conditioned on the event
\big\{f(z_{1})=\ldots=f(z_{m})=0\big\}
have the same law as the random vector

\displaystyle\left(|f_{n}^{\tau}(w_{1})|,\ldots,|f_{n}^{\tau}(w_{\ell})|,\left(\frac{1+|z_{1}|^{2}}{1+|\tau(z_{1})|^{2}}\right)^{n/2-1}|f_{n}^{\prime}(\tau(z_{1}))|,\ldots,\left(\frac{1+|z_{m}|^{2}}{1+|\tau(z_{m})|^{2}}\right)^{n/2-1}|f_{n}^{\prime}(\tau(z_{m}))|\right)

conditioned on the event
\left\{f_{n}(\tau(z_{1}))=\ldots=f_{n}(\tau(z_{m}))=0\right\}
. Recalling the definitions of
\widehat{f}_{n}
and
D\widehat{f}_{n}
concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

2.3.
Kac-Rice type densities

Recall that
\mathcal{Z}
is the zero set of our random polynomial
f_{n}
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{Z}^{m}
the set of distinct
m
-tuples of zeros from
\mathcal{Z}
, with the agreement that
\mathcal{Z}^{1}=\mathcal{Z}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{P}(k,m)
the collection of all unordered partitions of the set
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
into
m
non-empty blocks, and by
\mathcal{P}(k)
the collection of all partitions of
[k]
. For
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k,m)
, we denote the blocks by
\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m}\}
and let
|\pi_{\ell}|
be the size of the
\ell
’th block. For
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k,m)
, we set

(20)

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\pi)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})\in\mathcal{Z}^{|\pi|}}\log^{|\pi_{1}|}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\zeta_{1})|\cdots\log^{|\pi_{m}|}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\zeta_{m})|\,.

The sums of the form (
20
) appear when considering the moments of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, since

(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{k}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\pi)}\,.

It is evident that
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\pi)}
, as a function of
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k,m)
, depends only on the numbers
\{|\pi_{1}|,\ldots,|\pi_{m}|\}
, which leads us to consider densities of the following form.

Definition 2.4
.

For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{m}
, we consider the function
\rho_{\ell,m,p}:\mathbb{C}_{\not=}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{C}_{\not=}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(21)

\rho_{\ell,m,p}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}n^{\ell+m}\frac{{\Lambda}_{\ell,m,p}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})}{\det\Big[\operatorname{Cov}\big(\widehat{f}_{n}(z_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{m}\Big]}\,,

where
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell})
,
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})
and

{\Lambda}_{\ell,m,p}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(w_{t})|\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\log^{p_{j}}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(z_{j})|\cdot|D\widehat{f}_{n}(z_{j})|^{2}\Big)\,\Big|\,f_{n}(z_{j})=0,\,\forall j\in[m]\bigg]\,.

The family of functions given by (
21
) will allow us to compute the joint moments of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We note that by plugging
\ell=0
and
p_{1}=\ldots=p_{m}=0
in the above definition we recover the usual
m
-point function for the point process
\mathcal{Z}
on the sphere
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
described in the introduction, see
[
7
, §3.3]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

For all
\ell,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k,m)
we have

\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathcal{I}_{n})^{\ell}\,\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\pi)}\big]=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{\ell}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}\rho_{\ell,m,p}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\,{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes m}(\mathbf{z})\,{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes\ell}(\mathbf{w})

where
p_{j}=|\pi_{j}|
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant m
, and
\rho_{\ell,m,p}
is given by Definition
2.4
.

The proof of Proposition
2.5
is quite standard and follows the same scheme as in the usual Kac-Rice type results. As such, we defer the proof to Appendix
A
. With Proposition
2.5
at our disposal, we can already recover the main result from
[
3
]
, that is, compute the expectation of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Although it will not play a role in what follows, we provide this computation for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the logarithmic energy (
4
). We have

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{E}_{n}\big]=\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\log n-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\log 2\Big)n\,.

Proof.

Let
Z
be a standard complex Gaussian. We have

\mathbb{E}\big[\log|Z|\big]=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|z|\,e^{-|z|^{2}}\frac{{\rm d}m(z)}{\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\log t)e^{-t}\,{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{2}{\Gamma}^{\prime}(1)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}

where
{\Gamma}
is the Gamma function and
\gamma
is Euler’s constant. Furthermore, as
\mathbb{E}\big|\log|Z|\big|<\infty
, we can apply Fubini’s theorem and get

(22)

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{I}_{n}\big]=n\int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{E}\big[\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(z)|\big]\,{\rm d}\mu(z)=-n\frac{\gamma}{2}\,.

To compute
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{n}]
we will use Proposition
2.5
with
\ell=0
and
k=1
. For all
z\in\mathbb{C}
the Gaussian vector
\big(\widehat{f}_{n}(z),D\widehat{f}_{n}(z)\big)
has mean zero and covariance matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&z\sqrt{n}\\
\overline{z}\sqrt{n}&1+n|z|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

Therefore, the distribution of
D\widehat{f}_{n}(z)
conditioned on the event that
\{f_{n}(z)=0\}
is that of a standard complex Gaussian. For
{\Lambda}
which is given by (
21
), we conclude that

\displaystyle{\Lambda}_{0,1,1}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\log|D\widehat{f}(z)|\cdot|D\widehat{f}(z)|^{2}\ \big|\,f(z)=0\Big]=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}(\log|z|)\,|z|^{2}\,e^{-|z|^{2}}{\rm d}m(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(t)\,t\,e^{-t}\,\rm dt=\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\,.

Proposition
2.5
now gives

(23)

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{S}_{n}\big]=n\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}}{\rm d}\mu(z)=n\,\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\,.

By linearity of the expectation, we combine (
22
) and (
23
) together with Lemma
2.1
and get the desired formula for the expected energy. ∎

To give effective bounds on the cumulants of
\mathcal{E}_{n}

, as described in the introduction.

2.4.

Throughout this section, we fix

and

as

Let

.

.

1

2.2
and get that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{E}_{n}\big]}{n}=c_{\ast}>0\,.

For all
k\geqslant 2
, (
27
) implies a binomial identity for cumulants

s_{k}\big(\mathcal{E}_{n}\big)=s_{k}\big(\mathcal{I}_{n}-\mathcal{S}_{n}\big)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{j}\gamma_{n}\big(j,k-j\big)

where
\gamma_{n}
is given by (
29
). Theorem
2.10
implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s_{k}\big(\mathcal{E}_{n}\big)}{n}

exists and is finite for all
k\geqslant 2
. By the scaling relation
s_{k}\big(n^{-1/2}\mathcal{E}_{n}\big)=n^{-k/2}s_{k}\big(\mathcal{E}_{n}\big)
, we see that for all
k\geqslant 3

\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{k}\big(n^{-1/2}\mathcal{E}_{n}\big)=0\,.

By the method of moments, we conclude that the sequence
\big(\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{E}_{n}\big]\big)/\sqrt{n}\,
converges in distribution to the Gaussian law of mean
0
and variance
c_{\ast}
as
n\to\infty
and the proof is complete.
∎

3.
Local bounds on the density. Proof of Theorem
2.7

3.1.
Local limit: Understanding the G.E.F

Recall that the Gaussian Entire Function (abbreviated G.E.F.) is the random Taylor series given by

(30)

g(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\frac{a_{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}z^{j}\,,

where
\{a_{j}\}
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussians. First, we show the standard fact that
g(\cdot)
is the limit of
f(\cdot/\sqrt{n})
in a suitable sense.

Claim 3.1
.

For each
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-(k+\ell)/2}\mathbb{E}\bigg[f_{n}^{(k)}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\overline{f_{n}^{(\ell)}\Big(\frac{w}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\Big[g^{(k)}(z)\overline{g^{(\ell)}(w)}\Big]\,.

Furthermore, the above convergence is uniform for
z,w
in a compact set.

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{E}\bigg[f_{n}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\overline{f_{n}\Big(\frac{w}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)}\bigg]=K_{n}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{w}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:covariance_kernel_for_f}}}{{=}}\Big(1+\frac{z\overline{w}}{n}\Big)^{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}e^{z\overline{w}}

uniformly for
z,w
in a compact set. It remains to note that
\mathbb{E}\big[g(z)\overline{g(w)}\big]=\exp(z\overline{w})
, which is evident from (
30
), to establish the case
k=\ell=0
of the claim. Note that both
K_{n}(z,w)
and
\exp(z\overline{w})
are holomorphic in
z
and anti-holomorphic in
w
, and thus a simple application of Cauchy’s theorem gives the desired statement for
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
.
∎

In view of Claim
3.1
, to prove the local bounds on the density
\rho=\rho_{\ell,m,p}
, it will be sufficient to prove the analogous statement for the G.E.F. replacing our original polynomial. We denote by
\widehat{g}(z)=e^{-|z|^{2}/2}g(z)
and
D\widehat{g}(z)=e^{-|z|^{2}/2}g^{\prime}(z)
.

Definition 3.2
.

For
m,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, let

(31)

\rho_{\ell,m,p}^{G}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})=\frac{{\Lambda}_{\ell,m,p}^{G}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})}{\det\big[\operatorname{Cov}\big(\widehat{g}(z_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{m}\big]}\,,

where
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell})
,
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})
and

{\Lambda}_{\ell,m,p}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|\widehat{g}(w_{t})|\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\log^{p_{j}}|D\widehat{g}(z_{j})|\,|D\widehat{g}(z_{j})|^{2}\Big)\,\Big|\,g(z_{j})=0,\,\forall j\in[m]\bigg]\,.

The main result of this section, Theorem
2.7
, will follow easily from the analogous statement for the G.E.F.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}
be a compact set. There exist a constant
C=C(m,\ell,p,K)
such that for all configurations of pairwise distinct point
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\in K^{\ell+m}
we have

|\rho_{\ell,m,p}^{G}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})|\leqslant C\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\Delta_{j}\,|\log^{p_{j}}(\Delta_{j})|\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant t\leqslant\ell}|\log(\Delta^{\prime}_{t})|\bigg)\,.

where
\rho_{\ell,m,p}^{G}
is given by (
31
) and
\Delta_{j},\Delta^{\prime}_{t}
are given by (
24
).

3.2.
Divided differences

For an entire function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
and a collection of distinct points
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})
define the divided difference of
f
with respect to
\mathbf{z}
via

(32)

f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{f(z_{j})}{\prod_{j^{\prime}\not=j}(z_{j}-z_{j^{\prime}})}\,.

The divided difference of
f
are in fact the coefficients in Newton’s form for polynomial interpolation, see
[
24
]
for basic facts on the divided difference.

It will be convenient for us later on to write (
32
) in a matrix form. Indeed, for distinct
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
we consider the lower triangular
m\times m
matrix

M(\mathbf{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(z_{j}-z_{i})\bigg)_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant m}

with the agreement that the empty product is equal to 1. It is not hard to check that (
32
) can be written as the matrix product

(33)

\begin{pmatrix}f(z_{1})\\
f(z_{2})\\
\vdots\\
f(z_{m})\end{pmatrix}=M(\mathbf{z})\begin{pmatrix}f[z_{1}]\\
f[z_{1},z_{2}]\\
\vdots\\
f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}]\end{pmatrix}

see for instance
[
17
, Lemma 2.13]
. Since
M(\mathbf{z})
is lower triangular, we have

\det M(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{i<j}(z_{j}-z_{i})\,.

Suppose now that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in K
where
K\subset\mathbb{C}
is a compact set and let
\gamma
be a simple closed contour that bounds a simply connected domain containing
K
. A simple residue computation shows that

(34)

f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}]=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{1})\cdots(z-z_{m})}\,{\rm d}z\,.

Using (
34
), we extend by continuity the definition (
32
) of
f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}]
to non-distinct tuples. Namely, for a tuple of points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in K
with
z_{j}
appearing
n_{j}
times for
j=1,\ldots,m
, we have

f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}]=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_{1})^{n_{1}}\cdots(z-z_{m})^{n_{m}}}\,{\rm d}z\,.

Note that the mapping
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\mapsto f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}]
is smooth and that

f[\underbrace{z,\dots,z}_{m\,\text{times}}]=\frac{f^{(m-1)}(z)}{(m-1)!}\,.

Claim 3.4
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}
be a compact set. For any
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}\in K
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-(m+\ell-2)/2}\,\mathbb{E}\bigg[f_{n}\Big[\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\ldots,\frac{z_{m}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big]\overline{f_{n}\Big[\frac{w_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\ldots,\frac{w_{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big]}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\left[g[z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}]\overline{g[w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}]}\right]

where the convergence is uniform in
K
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a simple closed contour whose interior contain
K
. We have

f_{n}\Big[\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\ldots,\frac{z_{m}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big]=\frac{n^{(m-1)/2}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\gamma}f_{n}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\frac{{\rm d}z}{\prod_{j}(z-z_{j})}\,.

By writing a similar expression for the divided difference along
(w_{j}/\sqrt{n})
and applying Claim
3.1
we get the desired claim
∎

To rewrite the densities in terms of the divided differences, we need to understand the effect of the conditioning on the divided differences. This understanding is made clear by the following simple Lemma
3.5
. We note that a similar statement also appears in Gass
[
17
, Lemma 2.11]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be an entire function and let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},y\in\mathbb{C}
. Suppose that
f(z_{1})=f(z_{2})=\ldots=f(z_{m})=0
, then

f(y)=f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},y]\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}(y-z_{\ell})\quad\text{and}\quad f^{\prime}(z_{1})=f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},z_{1}]\prod_{\ell=2}^{m}(z_{1}-z_{\ell})\,.

Proof.

When the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},y
are distinct, the first equality follows immediately from (
32
). When the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
are not distinct, then the equality follows from continuity of
f[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},y]
as seen from (
34
). To see the second equality, simply apply the first with
y=z_{1}+h
for some
h\not=0
, divide both sides by
h
and then take
h\to 0
.
∎

Our next observation, which is the key step in proving Theorem
3.3
, is that the Gaussian vector of divided differences for the G.E.F. is uniformly non-singular and of bounded variance.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}
be a compact set and let
g
given by (
30
). There exist constants
c=c(K,m)>0
and
C=C(K,m)
such that for all points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in K
we have

(35)

\det\bigg[\operatorname{Cov}\Big(g[z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}]\Big)_{j=1}^{m}\bigg]\geqslant c\,,\qquad\mathbb{E}|g[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}]|^{2}\leqslant C

and for all
n
we have

(36)

\det\bigg[\operatorname{Cov}\left(f_{n}\left[\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\ldots,\frac{z_{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]\right)_{j=1}^{m}\bigg]\geqslant cn^{m(m-1)/2}\,,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left|f_{n}\left[\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\ldots,\frac{z_{m}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]\right|^{2}\leqslant Cn^{{m-1}}

Proof.

Note that assuming the bounds in (
35
) hold for some constant
c
the bounds in (
36
) hold for some smaller constant
c
by Claim
3.4
. Thus we focus our attention on (
35
).

For the latter bound in (
35
), choose the contour
\gamma
in (
34
) to be of distance, say, at least
1
from
K
and apply the triangle inequality. To prove the former bound,
seeking a contradiction assume there exist
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in K
such that

\det\bigg[\operatorname{Cov}\Big(g[z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}]\Big)_{j=1}^{m}\bigg]=0\,.

Thus, there exist complex numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
, not all of which are zero, such that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\alpha_{\ell}\,g[z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}]=0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

That is,
\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}g(z)\,r(z)\,{\rm d}z=0
, where

r(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{(z-z_{1})\cdots(z-z_{j})}\,.

The definition (
30
) of
g
implies that

(37)

\int_{\gamma}z^{j}\,r(z)\,{\rm d}z=0\qquad\text{for all }j\geqslant 0\,,

but since
r(z)
is a non-trivial rational function that vanishes at infinity we get a contradiction. The fact that we can choose a uniform lower bound throughout
K
follows from the fact that

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\mapsto\det\bigg[\operatorname{Cov}\Big(g[z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}]\Big)_{j=1}^{m}\bigg]

is continuous.
∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
2.7

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.7
. The idea is to rewrite all terms in
\rho
in terms of appropriately normalized divided differences of
f_{n}
and then use Lemma
3.6
to handle the resulting quantities.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

Throughout this proof, for a complex number
z
we write
\widehat{z}
for
z/\sqrt{n}
, and extend this notation to vectors. We also note that for
z\in K
we have
(1+|\widehat{z}|^{2})^{n}=\Theta(1)
, and so we will safely ignore these corrective multiplicative terms and only lose a constant depending on
K
. Starting with the denominator, the multiplicativity of the determinant together with (
33
) implies that

\displaystyle\det\left[\operatorname{Cov}\left({f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})\right)_{j=1}^{m}\right]

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left|\det\left[M(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})\right]\right|^{2}\det\left[\operatorname{Cov}\left(f_{n}[\widehat{z}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{z}_{j}]\right)_{j=1}^{m}\right]\,.

We also note that

(38)

|\det[M(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})]|=n^{-m(m-1)/4}|\det[M(\mathbf{z})]|

and so, Lemma
3.6
gives the bound

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\rho_{\ell,m,p}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}},\widehat{\mathbf{z}}\right)\right|}{n^{m+\ell}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\big|\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{w}_{t})|\big|\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\big|\log^{p_{j}}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|\big||D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|^{2}\right)\,\big|\,f_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})=0\right]}{\left|\det[M(\mathbf{z})]\right|^{2}}

By Lemma
3.5
, conditioned on the event that
\big\{f_{n}(z_{j})=0,\,\forall j\in[m]\big\}
, we have that

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-m}\prod_{j=1}^{m}|f_{n}^{\prime}(\widehat{z}_{j})|^{2}=n^{-m}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\left|f_{n}[\widehat{z}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{z}_{m},\widehat{z}_{j}]\right|^{2}\prod_{j^{\prime}\not=j}|\widehat{z}_{j}-\widehat{z}_{j^{\prime}}|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=n^{-m}\left|\det[M(\widehat{\mathbf{z}})]\right|^{4}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left|f_{n}[\widehat{z}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{z}_{m},\widehat{z}_{j}]\right|^{2}

(40)

\displaystyle=\left|\det[M(\mathbf{z})]\right|^{4}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left|\frac{f_{n}[\widehat{z}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{z}_{m},\widehat{z}_{j}]}{n^{m/2}}\right|^{2}.

Furthermore, on the event that
\big\{f_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})=0,\,\forall j\in[m]\big\}
, Lemma
3.5
also gives that

\displaystyle\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{w}_{t})|

\displaystyle=\log\left|\frac{f_{n}[\widehat{z}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{z}_{m},\widehat{w}_{t}]}{n^{m/2}}\right|+\log\prod_{j=1}^{m}|w_{t}-z_{j}|+\Theta(1)

\displaystyle\log^{p_{j}}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|

\displaystyle=\bigg(\log\left|\frac{f_{n}[\widehat{z}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{z}_{m},\widehat{z}_{j}]}{n^{m/2}}\right|+\log\prod_{j^{\prime}\not=j}|z_{j}-z_{j^{\prime}}|+\Theta(1)\bigg)^{p_{j}}\,.

Combining the above with (
3.3
) with the elementary inequality
(|\alpha|+|\beta|)^{p}\leqslant 2^{p-1}(|\alpha|^{p}+|\beta|^{p})
for all
p\geqslant 1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[

\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\left|\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{w}_{t})|\right|\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\big|\log^{p_{j}}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|\big|\,|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|^{2}\right)\,\big|\,f_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})=0\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{p_{1}+\ldots+p_{m}}\left|\det[M(\mathbf{z})]\right|^{4}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\left(\Big|\log\Big|\frac{f_{n}[\mathbf{z},w_{t}]}{n^{m/2}}\Big|\Big|+|\log(\Delta_{t}^{\prime})|+\Theta(1)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\left(\Big|\log^{p_{j}}\Big|\frac{f_{n}[\mathbf{z},z_{j}]}{n^{m/2}}\Big|\Big|+\left|\log^{p_{j}}(\Delta_{j})\right|+\Theta(1)\right)\left|\frac{f_{n}[\mathbf{z},z_{j}]}{n^{m/2}}\right|^{2}\right)\,\Big|\,f_{n}(z_{j})=0,\,\forall j\Bigg]\,,

where we used the notation
f_{n}[\mathbf{z},y]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}f_{n}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},y]
for
y\in\mathbb{C}
. Finally, by Lemma
3.6
, we know that the Gaussian vector of divided differences is uniformly non-degenerate in a compact set; we can thus expand out the product and bound each term by Hölder’s inequality. This yields the existence of a constant
C=C(K,\ell,m,p)>0
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\big|\log|\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{w}_{t})|\big|

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\big|\log^{p_{j}}|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|\big|\,|D\widehat{f}_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})|^{2}\right)\,\Big|\,f_{n}(\widehat{z}_{j})=0\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left|\det[M(\mathbf{z})]\right|^{4}\,\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}|\log^{p_{j}}(\Delta_{j})|\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant t\leqslant\ell}|\log(\Delta^{\prime}_{t})|\bigg)\,.

Plugging the above inequality into (
39
), we get that

\displaystyle|\rho_{\ell,m,p}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\left|\det[M(\mathbf{z})]\right|^{2}\,\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}|\log^{p_{j}}(\Delta_{j})|\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant t\leqslant\ell}|\log(\Delta^{\prime}_{t})|\bigg)

\displaystyle=C\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\Delta_{j}\,|\log^{p_{j}}(\Delta_{j})|\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant t\leqslant\ell}|\log(\Delta^{\prime}_{t})|\bigg)

as desired.
∎

4.
Clustering of the densities. Proof of Theorem
2.9

The starting point is to write the density (
21
) in a slightly more convenient form. Denote by
\varphi_{n}:\mathbb{C}^{2m+\ell}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
the joint density for the complex Gaussian random variables

(41)

\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),\ldots,\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{m}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),\,D\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),\ldots,D\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{m}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),\,\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{w_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),\ldots,\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{w_{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),

that is

(42)

\varphi_{n}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\pi^{2m+\ell}\det{\Gamma}_{n}}\exp\Big(-\left\langle{{\Gamma}_{n}^{-1}\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{\eta}}\right\rangle\Big)

where
\widetilde{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta^{\prime})^{{\sf T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m+\ell}
with
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
,
\eta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
and
{\Gamma}_{n}
is the covariance matrix of the (complex) Gaussian variables (
41
). The density (
21
) is then given by

(43)

\rho_{\ell,m,p}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathbf{z}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)=n^{\ell+m}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m+\ell}}\varphi_{n}(\mathbf{0},\eta,\eta^{\prime})\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|\eta_{t}^{\prime}|\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\,\log^{p_{j}}|\eta_{j}|\Big)\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\,,

where
\mathbf{0}=(0,\ldots,0)
and
m
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}^{m+\ell}
. The goal of this section is to prove that whenever the points
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}
can be split into two separated sets, then the Gaussian density (
42
) approximately factors, in the precise technical sense as stated in Theorem
2.9
. To achieve this, we start by proving that the covariance matrix
{\Gamma}_{n}={\Gamma}_{n}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})
factors in the operator sense.

4.1.
Preliminaries

Fix for the moment a configuration of points
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}_{\not=}^{m}
and
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{C}_{\not=}^{\ell}
. For
\alpha=(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2},\alpha^{\prime})^{\sf T}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m+\ell}
we consider the random variable

(44)

L_{n}=L_{n}(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big[\alpha_{j}^{1}\,\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)+\alpha_{j}^{2}\,D\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big]+\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{t}^{\prime}\,\widehat{f}_{n}\Big(\frac{w_{t}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\,.

The relevance of these random variables becomes clear by the following simple claim.

Claim 4.1
.

For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{2m+\ell}
we have
\left\langle{{\Gamma}_{n}\,\alpha,\alpha}\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}|L_{n}(\alpha)|^{2}
.

Proof.

This follows simply from the fact that
{\Gamma}_{n}
is the covariance matrix of (
41
) and a direct inspection of
\mathbb{E}|L_{n}(\alpha)|^{2}
.
∎

Since
\widehat{f}(\cdot/\sqrt{n})
converges to the normalized G.E.F. as
n\to\infty
, we are led to consider the limiting random variable

(45)

L_{\infty}(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big[\alpha_{j}^{1}\,\widehat{g}(z_{j})+\alpha_{j}^{2}\,D\widehat{g}(z_{j})\big]+\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{t}^{\prime}\,\widehat{g}(w_{t})

where, as before,
\widehat{g}=e^{-|z|^{2}/2}g(z)
and
D\widehat{g}(z)=e^{-|z|^{2}/2}g^{\prime}(z)
with
g
given by (
30
).

Claim 4.2
.

We have
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}|L_{n}(\alpha)|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|L_{\infty}(\alpha)|^{2}
, with uniform convergence for
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})
inside a compact set.

Proof.

By Claim
4.1
it suffices to prove that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle{{\Gamma}_{n}\,\alpha,\alpha}\right\rangle=\left\langle{{\Gamma}_{\infty}\,\alpha,\alpha}\right\rangle\,,

where
{\Gamma}_{\infty}
is the covariance matrix for the Gaussian variables

(46)

\widehat{g}(z_{1}),\ldots,\widehat{g}(z_{m}),\,D\widehat{g}(z_{1}),\ldots,D\widehat{g}(z_{m}),\,\widehat{g}(w_{1}),\ldots,\widehat{g}(w_{\ell}).

(That is, the limit for the random vector (
41
) as
n\to\infty
.) The later fact follows easily from Claim
3.1
.
∎

For
r>0
we denote by
r\mathbb{D}=\{|z|\leqslant r\}
and
r\mathbb{T}=\{|z|=r\}
. Suppose that the vector
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})
which participates in the definition of (
44
) has all its coordinates inside
r\mathbb{D}
. Then, by Cauchy’s theorem, we have that

(47)

L_{n}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{2r\mathbb{T}}f_{n}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)R_{n}^{\alpha}(z)\,{\rm d}z

where

(48)

R_{n}^{\alpha}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big[\frac{\alpha_{j}^{1}}{(1+|z_{j}|^{2}/n)^{n/2}}\,\frac{1}{z-z_{j}}+\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{(1+|z_{j}|^{2}/n)^{n/2-1}}\,\frac{1}{(z-z_{j})^{2}}\Big]\\
+\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{(1+|w_{t}|^{2}/n)^{n/2}}\,\frac{1}{z-w_{t}}\,.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exist a positive constant
C=C(m,\ell,r)
such that for large enough

and for all

we have

\max_{|z|=2r}|R_{n}^{\alpha}(z)|\leqslant C\,\mathbb{E}|L_{n}(\alpha)|^{2}\,.

Remark
.

In fact, it is easy to see from (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|L_{n}|^{2}\lesssim\max_{|z|=2r}|R_{n}^{\alpha}(z)|
also holds, but we will not use this fact in what follows.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

We start with the observation that, as

, the rational function

converges to

(49)

R^{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{-|z_{j}|^{2}/2}\Big[\frac{\alpha_{j}^{1}}{z-z_{j}}+\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{(z-z_{j})^{2}}\Big]+\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}e^{-|w_{t}|^{2}/2}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{z-w_{t}}\,,

where this convergence occurs uniformly inside a compact set which contains
2r\mathbb{T}
. By Claim
4.2
, the desired inequality will follow once we prove that

(50)

\max_{|z|=2r}|R^{\alpha}(z)|^{2}\leqslant C\,\mathbb{E}|L_{\infty}(\alpha)|^{2}

where
L_{\infty}(\alpha)
is given by (
45
). We also note the simple relation

(51)

L_{\infty}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{2r\mathbb{T}}g(z)R^{\alpha}(z)\,{\rm d}z\,.

The upper bound (
50
) follows from
[
25
, Claim 2.1]
, we sketch the argument here for the sake of completeness. Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the set of all rational functions of degree at most
(2m+\ell)
vanishing at infinity and having all of their poles in
r\mathbb{D}
, and note that the function
R^{\alpha}
given by (
49
) belongs to this class. Then it is easy to check (see
[
25
, Claim 2.2]
) that any sequence
\{R_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{R}
with
\max_{|z|=2r}|R_{k}(z)|\leqslant 1
has a subsequence that converges uniformly on
\{|z|=2r\}
to
R\in\mathcal{R}
.

Assume by contradiction that (
50

\{L_{\infty}^{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
all of the form (
45
), along with the corresponding sequence of rational functions
\{R_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
of the form (
49

\max_{|z|=2r}|R_{k}(z)|=1\,,\qquad\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}|L_{\infty}^{k}|^{2}=0\,.

By the previous paragraph, we can choose a subsequence such that

converge uniformly on

to a non-zero rational function

. Setting

L=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{2r\mathbb{T}}g(z)R(z)\,{\rm d}z\,,

we see from (

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big|L-L_{\infty}^{k}\big|^{2}\leqslant(2\pi r)^{2}\cdot\int_{2r\mathbb{T}}\mathbb{E}|g(z)|^{2}\,|{\rm d}z|\cdot\lim_{k\to\infty}\big(\max_{|z|=2r}|R(z)-R_{k}(z)|^{2}\big)=0\,.

That is,

almost surely. In view of (

∎

4.2.
Identifying the scale

For the random polynomial

as defined by (

\mathbb{E}\Big[T_{\xi}\,f_{n}(z)\overline{T_{\xi}\,f_{n}(w)}\Big]=K_{n}(z,w)\,,\qquad\forall z,w\in\mathbb{C}

is given by (

.

For the vector
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\in(r\mathbb{D})^{\ell+m}
, recall the random variable
L_{n}=L_{n}(\alpha)
of the form (
44
). For
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
we set

(52)

T_{\xi}(L_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{2r\mathbb{T}}T_{\xi}\,f_{n}\Big(\frac{z}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)R_{n}^{\alpha}(z)\,{\rm d}z

where

is the rational function given by (

47

L_{n}
and

have the same distribution. Let
L_{n}^{1}
and

be random variables of the form (

when the spherical distance between

is large. We note that this comes down to upper bounding

for

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
m,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
,
r>0
and suppose that
d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\geqslant 100r/\sqrt{n}
. Suppose that
L_{n}^{1}(\alpha)
and
L_{n}^{2}(\beta)
are random variables of the form (
44
), and suppose that the corresponding rational functions
R_{n,1}^{\alpha}
and
R_{n,2}^{\beta}
given by (
48
) have all their poles inside
r\mathbb{D}
. Then

(53)

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[T_{\xi_{1}}(L_{n}^{1})\,\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}(L_{n}^{2})}\Big]\Big|\leqslant C(r,m,\ell)\,\exp\Big(-\frac{n}{16}\,d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{2}\Big)\Big(\mathbb{E}\big|L_{n}^{1}\big|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\big|L_{n}^{2}\big|^{2}\Big)\,,

for all
n\geqslant 2
large enough.

Proof.

Using the representation (
52
), we see that

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[T_{\xi_{1}}(L_{n}^{1})\,\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}(L_{n}^{2})}\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\iint_{2r\mathbb{T}\times 2r\mathbb{T}}R_{n,1}^{\alpha}(z_{1})\overline{R_{n,2}^{\beta}(z_{2})}\,\mathbb{E}\bigg[T_{\xi_{1}}\,f_{n}\left(\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}\,f_{n}\left(\frac{z_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)}\,\bigg]\,{\rm d}z_{1}{\rm d}z_{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{|z_{1}|=|z_{2}|=2r}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[T_{\xi_{1}}\,f_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\,\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}\,f_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)}\bigg]\bigg|\Big(\max_{|z|=2r}|R_{n,1}^{\alpha}(z)|^{2}+\max_{|z|=2r}|R_{n,2}^{\beta}(z)|^{2}\Big)\,,

and by Lemma
4.3
we get that

(54)

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[T_{\xi_{1}}(L_{n}^{1})\,\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}(L_{n}^{2})}\Big]\Big|\lesssim\max_{z_{1},z_{2}\in 2r\mathbb{T}}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[T_{\xi_{1}}\,f_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\,\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}\,f_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)}\bigg]\bigg|\,\Big(\mathbb{E}\big|L_{n}^{1}(\alpha)\big|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\big|L_{n}^{2}(\beta)\big|^{2}\Big)\,.

A computation shows that for all
{\lambda}_{1},{\lambda}_{2},\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[T_{\xi_{1}}\,f_{n}({\lambda}_{1})\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}\,f_{n}({\lambda}_{2})}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{\big(1-{\lambda}_{1}\overline{\xi}_{1}\big)\big(1-\overline{{\lambda}}_{2}\xi_{2}\big)}{\sqrt{1+|\xi_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|\xi_{2}|^{2}}}\bigg(1+\Big(\frac{{\lambda}_{1}+\xi_{1}}{1-{\lambda}_{1}\overline{\xi}_{1}}\Big)\Big(\frac{\overline{{\lambda}}_{2}+\overline{\xi}_{2}}{1-\overline{{\lambda}}_{2}\xi_{2}}\Big)\bigg)\Bigg)^{n}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{\big(1-{\lambda}_{1}\overline{\xi}_{1}\big)\big(1-\overline{{\lambda}}_{2}\xi_{2}\big)+\big({\lambda}_{1}+\xi_{1}\big)\big(\overline{{\lambda}}_{2}+\overline{\xi}_{2}\big)}{\sqrt{1+|\xi_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|\xi_{2}|^{2}}}\Bigg)^{n}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{1+\xi_{1}\overline{\xi}_{2}+{\lambda}_{1}\overline{{\lambda}}_{2}\big(1+\overline{\xi}_{1}\xi_{2}\big)-{\lambda}_{1}\big(\overline{\xi}_{1}-\overline{\xi}_{2}\big)+\overline{{\lambda}}_{2}\big(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\big)}{\sqrt{1+|\xi_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|\xi_{2}|^{2}}}\Bigg)^{n}\,.

We also note that

\frac{|1+\xi_{1}\overline{\xi}_{2}|}{\sqrt{1+|\xi_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|\xi_{2}|^{2}}}=\bigg(1-\frac{d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{2}}{4}\bigg)^{1/2}\,.

Therefore, by the triangle inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\max_{|z_{1}|=|z_{2}|=2r}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\xi_{1}}\,f_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\,\overline{T_{\xi_{2}}\,f_{n}\Big(\frac{z_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\bigg(1-\frac{d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{2}}{4}\bigg)^{1/2}\Big(1+\frac{4r^{2}}{n}\Big)+\frac{2r}{\sqrt{n}}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\bigg)^{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{n}{16}\,d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{2}\Big)

provided that
d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\geqslant 100r/\sqrt{n}
. Plugging into (
54
), we get the lemma.
∎

Claim 4.5

Suppose we are given

We start the procedure by taking

and

y_{i}
and
y_{i^{\prime}}

d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\widetilde{y},y_{i})^{2}=d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\widetilde{y},y_{i^{\prime}})^{2}=2-\sqrt{4-d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(y_{i},y_{i^{\prime}})^{2}}\,.

(The point
\widetilde{y}
is the midpoint of the geodesic

2

\widetilde{y}
to be an arbitrary midpoint.
which connects
y_{i}
and

Lemma 4.6

For all

We apply the construction of Claim

\mathbb{D}^{+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{D}

\mathbb{D}^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}
, which make the following identity obvious

\mathbb{C}^{\ell+m}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Upsilon_{m+\ell}}\mathbb{D}^{\sigma(1)}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{D}^{\sigma(m+\ell)}\,.

Fix for a moment some
\sigma\in\Upsilon_{m+\ell}
and let
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}=\mathbb{D}^{\sigma(1)}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{D}^{\sigma(m+\ell)}
. On each domain
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
the function

(\eta^{\prime},\eta)\mapsto\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|\eta_{t}^{\prime}|\Big)\,\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\,\log^{p_{j}}|\eta_{j}|\Big)

does not change sign. In view of (
56
), there exist a sign
\tau=\tau(\sigma)\in\{-1,+1\}
such that, if we let
\widetilde{\eta}=(\mathbf{0},\eta,\eta^{\prime})
,

(59)

\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|\eta_{t}^{\prime}|\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\,\log^{p_{j}}|\eta_{j}|\Big)e^{-\left\langle{{\Gamma}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{\eta}}\right\rangle}\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\\
\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|\eta_{t}^{\prime}|\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\,\log^{p_{j}}|\eta_{j}|\Big)e^{-(1+\tau\varepsilon)^{-1}\left\langle{({\Gamma}^{I,J})^{-1}\,\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{\eta}}\right\rangle}\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\\
=(1+\tau\varepsilon)^{2m+\ell}\int_{(1+\tau\varepsilon)\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|(1+\tau\varepsilon)\eta_{t}^{\prime}|\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\log^{p_{j}}|(1+\tau\varepsilon)\eta_{j}|\Big)\\
\times\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\Big)\,e^{-\left\langle{({\Gamma}^{I,J})^{-1}\,\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{\eta}}\right\rangle}\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\,.

We make a few simple observations. For any
\tau\in\{-1,1\}
we have

\big|\log\big((1+\tau\varepsilon)x\big)-\log x\big|\lesssim\varepsilon

uniformly in
x>0
. We also have the relation

\Big|1-\big(1+\varepsilon\big)^{2m+\ell}\Big|\lesssim\varepsilon\,.

Finally, as
|\log(1+x)|\lesssim\varepsilon
uniformly in
x\in[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]
we can also deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{(1+\tau\varepsilon)\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}\triangle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}|\log|\eta_{t}^{\prime}||\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\log^{p_{j}}|\eta_{j}||\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\Big)\,e^{-\left\langle{({\Gamma}^{I,J})^{-1}\,\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{\eta}}\right\rangle}\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{m+\ell}\int_{(1+\tau\varepsilon)\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}\triangle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\Big)\,e^{-\left\langle{({\Gamma}^{I,J})^{-1}\,\widetilde{\eta},\widetilde{\eta}}\right\rangle}\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\,.

Combining this observations with (
58
), we can sum the inequality (
59
) over
\sigma\in\Upsilon_{m+\ell}
and obtain that

\rho_{\ell,m,p}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathbf{z}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)-\rho_{|I^{\prime}|,|I|,p_{I}}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{w}_{I^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathbf{z}_{I}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\rho_{|J^{\prime}|,|J|,p_{J}}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{w}_{J^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathbf{z}_{J}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\\
=n^{\ell+m}\sum_{\sigma\in\Upsilon_{m+\ell}}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}\Big(\varphi_{n}(\widetilde{\eta})-\varphi_{n}^{I,J}(\widetilde{\eta})\Big)\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\log|\eta_{t}^{\prime}|\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\,\log^{p_{j}}|\eta_{j}|\Big)\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\\
\lesssim\varepsilon n^{\ell+m}\max_{\vec{\mathrm{p}}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m+\ell}}\varphi_{n}^{I,J}(\widetilde{\eta})\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\big|\log|\eta_{t}^{\prime}|\big|\Big)\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{m}|\eta_{j}|^{2}\,\big|\log^{\mathrm{p}_{j}}|\eta_{j}|\big|\Big)\,{\rm d}m(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\,,

where the maximum runs over all integer vectors
\vec{\mathrm{p}}=(\mathrm{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{m})
with
\mathrm{p}_{j}\leqslant p_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,m
. In view of Theorem
2.7
(and Remark
2.8
), the above maximum is bounded by

\bigg(\prod_{1\leqslant t\leqslant\ell}|\log(\Delta^{\prime}_{t})|\bigg)

and this gives the desired upper bound. The lower bound follows in a symmetric way by repeating the same argument with
\tau(\sigma)
replaced by
-\tau(\sigma)
for all
\sigma\in\Upsilon_{m+\ell}
. Combining both lower and upper bounds proves Theorem
2.9
.
∎

5.
Cumulants densities and bounds. Proof of Theorem
2.10

In this section we will use the clustering property for the Kac-Rice densities
\rho_{\ell,m,p}
to establish the existence of limits for the cumulants of the logarithmic energy
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. We refer the reader to
[
30
, Chapter II, §12]
for basic properties of the cumulants of random variables and their relation to the moments. For a finite set
A
, let
\mathcal{P}(A)
denote all partitions of
A
into non-trivial blocks, and recall that
\mathcal{P}(k)=\mathcal{P}([k])
. We denote by
|\pi|
the number of blocks in the partition
\pi
. We start this section by recording two simple claims on the relation between the moments and the cumulants that follow from applying the Möbius inversion formula on the lattice of set partitions (see
[
35
, §3]
).

Claim 5.1

(
[
35
]
)
.

For
k\geqslant 1
, let
(\mathfrak{m}_{J})_{J\subset[k]}
and
(\mathfrak{s}_{J})_{J\subset[k]}
be two collections of real numbers indexed by subsets of
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Then the following two identities are equivalent:

\displaystyle\forall J\subset[k],\quad\mathfrak{m}_{J}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(J)}\prod_{I\in\pi}\mathfrak{s}_{I}

(60)

\displaystyle\forall J\subset[k],\quad\mathfrak{s}_{J}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(J)}(-1)^{|\pi|-1}\big(|\pi|-1\big)!\prod_{I\in\pi}\mathfrak{m}_{I}\,.

Furthermore, if there exists a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)
with
|\pi|\geqslant 2
so that

\forall J\subset[k]\,,\quad\mathfrak{m}_{J}=\prod_{I\in\pi}\mathfrak{m}_{I\cap J}

then (
5.1
) implies that
\mathfrak{s}_{[k]}=0
.

If we assume that the “moments”
(\mathfrak{m}_{J})_{J\subset[k]}
approximately factor, then we will get a quantitative bound for the “cumulant”
\mathfrak{s}_{[k]}
.

Claim 5.2
.

For each
k\geqslant 1
there exist a constant
C=C(k)>0
so that the following holds. For
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1\leqslant M
, let
(\mathfrak{m}_{J})_{J\subset[k]}
be a collection of real numbers, all in the interval
[-M,M]
. Suppose there exists a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)
with
|\pi|\geqslant 2
so that for all
J\subset[k]
we have

\Big|\mathfrak{m}_{J}-\prod_{I\in\pi}\mathfrak{m}_{I\cap J}\Big|\leqslant\varepsilon\,,

then

\bigg|\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(k)}(-1)^{|{\lambda}|-1}\big(|{\lambda}|-1\big)!\prod_{J\in{\lambda}}\mathfrak{m}_{J}\bigg|\leqslant CM^{k-1}\varepsilon\,.

Proof.

Put
\widetilde{\mathfrak{m}}_{J}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\prod_{I\in\pi}\mathfrak{m}_{J\cap I}
for
J\subset[k]
. By Claim
5.1
we have that

\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(k)}(-1)^{|{\lambda}|-1}(|{\lambda}|-1)!\prod_{J\in{\lambda}}\widetilde{\mathfrak{m}}_{J}=0\,.

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(k)}(-1)^{|{\lambda}|-1}\big(|{\lambda}|-1\big)!\prod_{J\in{\lambda}}\mathfrak{m}_{J}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(k)}\big(|{\lambda}|-1\big)!\Big|\prod_{J\in{\lambda}}\mathfrak{m}_{J}-\prod_{J\in{\lambda}}\widetilde{\mathfrak{m}}_{J}\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(k)}\big(|{\lambda}|-1\big)!(2M)^{|{\lambda}|-1}\varepsilon

\displaystyle\lesssim M^{k-1}\varepsilon\,.

∎

5.1.
Cumulant densities

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
be fixed throughout. Recall that
\gamma_{n}(a,b)
given by (
29
), is the joint cumulant for
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of order
(a,b)
. The goal of this section is to express this joint cumulant in term of the moments given by Proposition
2.5
. To do so, we will need to introduce some more combinatorial notation. Recall that
\mathcal{P}(a,m)
is the set of all partitions
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(a)
with
|\pi|=m
. We always think of
\mathcal{P}(0)
as the empty set. We shall construct partitions of
\mathcal{P}(a+b)
by first partitioning
[a]
, and then completing the partition adding elements of
[b]
, either to existing blocks or by constructing new blocks. The following definition becomes relevant.

Definition 5.3
.

For integers
m,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{b}(m)
as the set of all partitions of
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{1},\ldots,y_{b}\}
such that the
x_{j}
’s are in distinct blocks. For
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(m)
, let
|\pi|
be the total number of blocks, and note that
|\pi|\geqslant m
. We further denote by
\mathcal{P}_{b}(m,t)
to be the set of all partitions in
\mathcal{P}_{b}(m)
with
|\pi|=t
. Finally, we denote by
\ell_{j}(\pi)
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant t
, as the number of
y
-terms in the
j
’th block for
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(m,t)
. We will always enumerate the blocks of
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(m,t)
so that the first block contains
x_{1}
, the second block contains
x_{2}
and so forth, while the rest of the
t-m
blocks are enumerated arbitrarily.

With this definition in mind, we can write the following formula for bivariate cumulants.

Claim 5.4
.

Let
X,Y
be random variables with all moments being finite. Then

\displaystyle s\big(\underbrace{X,\ldots,X}_{a\,\text{times}},\underbrace{Y,\ldots,Y}_{b\,\text{times}}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{a}\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a,m)}\sum_{t=m}^{m+b}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(m,t)}(-1)^{t-1}(t-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\big[X^{|{\lambda}_{j}|}Y^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\big]

where we interpret
\lambda_{j}=\varnothing
for
j\geqslant|{\lambda}|
.

Proof.

By (
26
), we have

(61)

s(\underbrace{X,\ldots,X}_{a\,\text{times}}\,,\,\underbrace{Y,\ldots,Y}_{b\,\text{times}})=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a+b)}(|\sigma|-1)!\,(-1)^{|\sigma|-1}\left(\prod_{I\in\sigma}\mathbb{E}\Big[X^{x_{I}}Y^{|I|-x_{I}}\Big]\right)

where
x_{I}
is the number of indices from
[a]
which appear in the block
I\in\sigma
. To construct a partition
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a+b)
, we start with a partition
{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a,m)
and label its blocks as
\{{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,{\lambda}_{m}\}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
. We now need to add this partition the elements
\{a+1,\ldots,a+b\}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{b}\}
, which amounts to considering a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(m)
. Every partition
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a+b)
can be uniquely decomposed in this way, with
|\sigma|=|\pi|
and with
|{\lambda}_{j}|
being the number of indices from
[a]
appearing in the
j
’th block. Partitioning the sum in (
61
) and summing over all possible lengths of
{\lambda}
and
\pi
yields the desired claim.
∎

For partitions
\sigma,{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a)
, we say that
\sigma
is a
refinement
of
{\lambda}
and denote by
\sigma\preceq{\lambda}
, if all blocks of
\sigma
are contained in blocks of
{\lambda}
. Whenever
\sigma\preceq{\lambda}=\{{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,{\lambda}_{m}\}
, we will denote by
{\lambda}_{j}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{P}(|{\lambda}_{j}|)
the unique partition on
[|{\lambda}_{j}|]
induced by
\sigma
, simply by collecting the corresponding blocks of
\sigma
inside the block
{\lambda}_{j}
.

Recall that for all
k\geqslant 0

(62)

(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{k}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\pi)}\,.

where
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\pi)}
is given by (
20
).

Lemma 5.5
.

For
\gamma_{n}(a,b)
given by (
29
) we have

\gamma_{n}(a,b)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a)\\
\sigma\preceq{\lambda}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(|{\lambda}|)}(|\pi|-1)!(-1)^{|\pi|-1}\prod_{j=1}^{|\pi|}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathcal{S}_{n}^{({\lambda}_{j}(\sigma))}\,\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\Big]

where, as before, we interpret
\lambda_{j}=\varnothing
for
j\geqslant|{\lambda}|
and
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\varnothing)}=1
.

Proof.

Applying Claim
5.4
with
X=\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
Y=\mathcal{I}_{n}
yields the formula

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{a}\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a,m)}\sum_{t=m}^{m+b}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(m,t)}(-1)^{t-1}(t-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{S}_{n}^{|{\lambda}_{j}|}\,\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\big]

(63)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{a}\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a,m)}\sum_{t=m}^{m+b}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(m,t)}(-1)^{t-1}(t-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{t}\Big(\sum_{\sigma^{j}\in\mathcal{P}(|{\lambda}_{j}|)}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\sigma^{j})}\,\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\big]\Big)\,,

where the last equality is (
62
) together with the linearity of the expectation. Taken together, the collection
\{\sigma^{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}
is a partition
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a)
such that
\sigma\preceq{\lambda}
. Equivalently,
{\lambda}
can be thought of a partition of
[|\sigma|]
, and under this new description we have
\sigma^{j}={\lambda}_{j}(\sigma)
. Expanding the brackets gives

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{t}\Big(\sum_{\sigma^{j}\in\mathcal{P}(|{\lambda}_{j}|)}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\sigma^{j})}\,\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma^{1}\in\mathcal{P}(|{\lambda}_{1}|)}\cdots\sum_{\sigma^{m}\in\mathcal{P}(|{\lambda}_{m}|)}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{S}_{n}^{(\sigma^{j})}\,\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a)\\
\sigma\preceq{\lambda}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{S}_{n}^{({\lambda}^{j}(\sigma))}\,\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\big]\,.

Plugging this into (

), we can change the order of summation and get

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a)}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(|{\lambda}|)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a)\\

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a)\\
\sigma\preceq{\lambda}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(|{\lambda}|)}(|\pi|-1)!(-1)^{|\pi|-1}\prod_{j=1}^{|\pi|}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathcal{S}_{n}^{({\lambda}_{j}(\sigma))}\,\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\ell_{j}(\pi)}\Big]

which finishes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to define our Kac-Rice cumulants density. For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and a partition

, we define the

F_{b,\sigma}:\mathbb{C}^{b+|\sigma|}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(64)

F_{b,\sigma}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(a)\\
\sigma\preceq{\lambda}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(|{\lambda}|)}(|\pi|-1)!(-1)^{|\pi|-1}\prod_{j=1}^{|\pi|}\rho_{\ell_{j}(\pi),|{\lambda}_{j}|,{\lambda}_{j}(\sigma)}(\mathbf{w}_{\ell_{j}(\pi)},\mathbf{z}_{|{\lambda}_{j}|})

where
\rho_{\ell_{j}(\pi),|{\lambda}_{j}|,{\lambda}_{j}(\sigma)}
is given by (
21
).
Combining Proposition
2.5
, Lemma
5.5
we see that

(65)

\gamma_{n}(a,b)=\sum_{m=1}^{a}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a,m)}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{b}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}F_{b,\sigma}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\,{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes m}(\mathbf{z})\,{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes b}(\mathbf{w})\,,

where
F_{b,\sigma}
is given by (
64
).

5.2.
Convergence of cumulant densities

The goal of this section is to give the proof of Theorem
2.10
. In view of (
65
), the desired limit would follow if we can show that

(66)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{b}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}F_{b,\sigma}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\,{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes m}(\mathbf{z})\,{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes b}(\mathbf{w})

exists and is finite, for all
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a)
fixed. To establish this limit, we record two simple claims that will help us to justify an application of the dominated convergence theorem.

Claim 5.6
.

Let
a\geqslant 1
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(a,m)
. There exist a constants
C,c>0
, which depend only on
a
and
b
, such that for all
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{C}^{b+m}
we have

\displaystyle\big|F_{b,\sigma}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\big|

\displaystyle\leqslant Cn^{m+b}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\log(n|w_{i}-z_{j}|^{2})|^{C}\Big)e^{-cn\sum_{j=2}^{m}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},z_{j})^{2}-cn\sum_{i=1}^{b}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},w_{i})^{2}}\,.

Furthermore, for
a=0
, we have the similar upper bound

\big|F_{b,\varnothing}(\mathbf{w})\big|\leqslant Cn^{b}e^{-cn\sum_{i=2}^{b}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(w_{1},w_{i})^{2}}.

Proof.

We will focus on the case where
a\geqslant 1
, as the other case is easier and follows from the same argument. We first argue there exist partitions of
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{w}_{I^{\prime}},\mathbf{w}_{J^{\prime}})
and
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{z}_{I},\mathbf{z}_{J})
such that

(67)

{\sf dist}\,\Big\{\big(\mathbf{w}_{I}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}_{I}\big),\big(\mathbf{w}_{J}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}_{J}\big)\Big\}^{2}\geqslant c\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{m}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},z_{j})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{b}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},w_{i})^{2}\bigg)

where
{\sf dist}
is defined by (
25
) and
c=c(m,b)>0
is some constant. Indeed, let
X
be the set of all coordinates in
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})
(so the cardinality of
X
is
m+b
) and let
\zeta\in X
be such that

d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},\zeta)=\max_{x\in X\setminus\{z_{1}\}}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},x)\,.

By the pigeonhole principle, we can partition the set
X=X_{I}\sqcup X_{J}
so that

{\sf dist}\big\{X_{I},X_{J}\big\}\geqslant\frac{1}{m+b}\,d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},\zeta)\,.

This, together with the bound

d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},\zeta)^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{m+b}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{m}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},z_{j})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{b}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},w_{i})^{2}\bigg)

yields (
67
). Furthermore, we note that the sum
\sum_{\sigma\preceq{\lambda}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{b}(|{\lambda}|)}
in the definition (
64
) of
F_{b,\sigma}
runs in fact over all partitions
\widetilde{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(m+b)
, by first partitioning the
m
blocks of
\sigma
(this partition is dictated by
{\lambda}
) and then adding to each block the elements from
\{m+1,\ldots,m+b\}
. With this identification we have
|\widetilde{\pi}|=|\pi|
, and so we can apply Claim
5.2
together with
M
being the bound in Theorem
2.7
and
\varepsilon
the bound from Theorem
2.9
, applied with the partition (
67

\big|F_{b,\sigma}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})\big|\lesssim n^{m+b}\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant b}|\log(n\Delta^{\prime}_{i})|^{m+b}\exp\bigg(-cn\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{m}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},z_{j})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{b}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(z_{1},w_{i})^{2}\Big)\bigg)\,,

where
\Delta_{i}^{\prime}
is given by (
24

|\log|n\Delta_{i}^{\prime}||\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\log(n|w_{i}-z_{j}|^{2})|

and the proof is complete.
∎

Claim 5.7
.

For any

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

The desired result will follow once we establish the limit (
66
). By applying the obvious scaling, it is equivalent to showing that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{m+b+1}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{b}}F_{b,\sigma}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathbf{z}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{{\rm d}m(z_{j})}{\pi(1+|z_{j}|^{2}/n)^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{{\rm d}m(w_{i})}{\pi(1+|w_{i}|^{2}/n)^{2}}

exists and is finite. Assuming that
m\geqslant 1
, we use the rotation invariance of
F_{b,\sigma}
, guaranteed by Lemma
2.3
, and rotate the vector
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z})
so that
z_{1}=0
. If
m=0
, we rotate
\mathbf{w}
so that
w_{1}=0
and argue in the same way. Since

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{{\rm d}m(z_{1})}{\pi(1+|z_{1}|^{2}/n)^{2}}=n\,,

we see that existence of the limit (
66
) is equivalent to existence of the limit

(69)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{m+b}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m-1}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{b}}F_{b,\sigma}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\prod_{j=2}^{m}\frac{{\rm d}m(z_{j})}{\pi(1+|z_{j}|^{2}/n)^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{{\rm d}m(w_{i})}{\pi(1+|w_{i}|^{2}/n)^{2}}\,,

where
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}(0,z_{2},\ldots,z_{m})
. The convergence claimed in (
69
) will follow from an application of the dominated convergence theorem which we now justify. Let

g_{n,\sigma,b}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}n^{-b-m}F_{b,\sigma}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\prod_{j=2}^{m}\frac{1}{\pi(1+|z_{j}|^{2}/n)^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{1}{\pi(1+|w_{i}|^{2}/n)^{2}}\,.

As we have the pointwise convergence of
g_{n,\sigma,b}
(Claim
5.7
) it is enough to find an integrable majorant. By Claim
5.6
, we have the upper bound

(70)

\displaystyle|g_{n,\sigma,b}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})|\lesssim\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\log|w_{i}-z_{j}||^{C}\Big)\prod_{j=2}^{m}\frac{e^{-c|z_{j}|^{2}/(1+|z_{j}|^{2}/n)}}{(1+|z_{j}|^{2}/n)^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{e^{-c|w_{i}|^{2}/(1+|w_{i}|^{2}/n)}}{(1+|w_{i}|^{2}/n)^{2}}\,.

It is evident that
\exp(-cx)\lesssim(1+x)^{-2}

for all
x\geqslant 0
.
Applying this simple observation with
x=\frac{t}{1+t/n}
, we see that

\frac{e^{-ct/(1+t/n)}}{(1+t/n)^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\big((1+t/n)(1+\frac{t}{1+t/n})\big)^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{(1+t)^{2}}

uniformly in
n\geqslant 1
. Plugging
t=|z_{j}|^{2}
and
t=|w_{i}|^{2}
in the above inequality and applying it to the bound (
70
), we see that

|g_{n,\sigma,b}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})|\lesssim\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\log|w_{i}-z_{j}||^{C}\Big)\prod_{j=2}^{m}\frac{1}{(1+|z_{j}|^{2})^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{b}\frac{1}{(1+|w_{i}|^{2})^{2}}\,.

As the right hand side of the above is integrable on
\mathbb{C}^{m-1}\times\mathbb{C}^{b}
(with respect to the usual Lebesgue measure), we can apply the dominated convergence theorem and see that the limit (
69
) exists and is equal to

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{m-1}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{b}}F_{b,\sigma}^{G}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\prod_{j=2}^{m}{\rm d}m(z_{j})\prod_{i=1}^{b}{\rm d}m(w_{i})

which is finite. As a consequence, we proved that the limit (
66
) exists and, in view of (
65
), this proves Theorem
2.10
.
∎

6.1.
Wiener chaos decomposition

The basic tool which allows us to compute the limits above is an orthogonal decomposition scheme, which is valid in general Gaussian Hilbert spaces and is commonly known as
Wiener chaos
, see
[
19
, Chap. 2]
. We record below the basic facts that we will use. First, we find the coefficients in the orthogonal expansion of the functions
\log x
and
x\log x
in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},e^{-x}\,{\rm d}x)
. The Laguerre polynomials

{\sf L}_{j}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\frac{e^{x}}{j!}\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial x^{j}}\big(x^{j}e^{-x}\big)\,,

for
m\geqslant 0
, form an orthonormal basis in this Hilbert space. The relevance of these polynomials becomes evident from the following simple observation: if
(Z_{1},Z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
a mean-zero random complex Gaussian vector with
\mathbb{E}|Z_{1}|^{2}=\mathbb{E}|Z_{2}|^{2}=1
and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}\overline{Z_{2}}]=\theta
, then

(77)

\mathbb{E}\big[{\sf L}_{m}(|Z_{1}|^{2})\,{\sf L}_{k}(|Z_{2}|^{2})\big]=\begin{cases}|\theta|^{2m}&m=k,\\
0&m\not=k,\end{cases}

see for instance
[
7
, Lemma 3.5.2]
or
[
19
, Theorem 3.9]
. Throughout this section, we use
\gamma
for the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

Claim 6.1
.

Let
\alpha_{0}=-\gamma
and
\alpha_{j}=-\frac{1}{j}
for
j\geqslant 1
, then

(78)

\log x=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\alpha_{j}{\sf L}_{j}(x)

in the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},e^{-x}\,{\rm d}x)
sense. Similarly, if we set
\beta_{0}=1-\gamma
,
\beta_{1}=\gamma-2
and
\beta_{j}=\frac{1}{j(j-1)}
for
j\geqslant 2
, then

(79)

x\log x=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\beta_{j}{\sf L}_{j}(x)

in the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},e^{-x}\,{\rm d}x)
sense.

Proof.

We will only compute the expansion (
79
), as the expansion (
78
) follows in a similar way (it also exists in the proof of
[
7
, Lemma 3.5.2]
). For
j\geqslant 2
we integrate by parts and see that

\displaystyle\beta_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\log x)\,{\sf L}_{j}(x)e^{-x}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{j!}\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\log x)\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial x^{j}}\big(x^{j}e^{-x}\big)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial^{j}(x\log x)}{\partial x^{j}}\,x^{j}e^{-x}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{(j-2)!}{j!}\int_{0}^{\infty}xe^{-x}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{j(j-1)}\,.

The values of
\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{1}
follow from direct integration and the claim follows.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2.5

The starting point is a sufficiently general version for the Kac-Rice formula, given by the following theorem from
[
4
]
.

Theorem A.1
.

[
4
, Theorem 6.4]

Let
Z:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Gaussian random field with
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
open. Assume that:

•

the function
t\mapsto Z(t)
is almost surely smooth;

•

for each
t\in U
we have
\det\operatorname{Cov}(Z(t))\neq 0
;

•

\mathbb{P}\big(\exists t\in U,Z(t)=0,\det(Z^{\prime}(t))=0\big)=0
.

Suppose further that one has another random field
Y^{t}:U\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying the following conditions:

•

Y^{t}
is measurable and almost surely
(t,w)\mapsto Y^{t}(w)
is continuous;

•

for each
t
the random process
(s,w)\mapsto(Z(s),Y^{t}(w))
is (real) Gaussian.

Let
H:U\times\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{\ell},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded and continuous function, where the topology on
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{\ell},\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets. Then, for each compact
I\subset U
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{t\in I,Z(t)=0}H(t,Y^{t})\right)=\int_{I}\mathbb{E}\left(|\det(Z^{\prime}(t))|\,H(t,Y^{t})\mid Z(t)=0\right)p_{Z(t)}(0)\,dt

where
p_{Z(t)}(0)
is the density of the random vector
Z(t)
evaluated at
0
.

With Theorem
A.1
at our disposal, we can prove the following version of it for Gaussian analytic functions, which will take us most of the way towards Proposition
2.5
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f
be a Gaussian analytic function so that for any
k
distinct points
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
we have
\det\big[\operatorname{Cov}(f(w_{1}),\ldots,f(w_{k}))\big]\neq 0
. For a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathcal{Z}_{k}(K)
denote the set of distinct
k
-tuples of zeros of
f
in
K
. Then for bounded and continuous functions
h_{j}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant k+\ell
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int_{K^{\ell}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}h_{k+i}(f(z_{i}),z_{i})\,{\rm d}m(\mathbf{z})\bigg)

\displaystyle\sum_{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}(K)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}(f^{\prime}(y_{j}),y_{j})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int_{K^{\ell}}\int_{K^{k}}\frac{{\Lambda}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z};h,f)}{\pi^{k}\det\big[\operatorname{Cov}\big((f(u_{j}))_{j=1}^{k}\big)\big]}\,{\rm d}m(\mathbf{u})\,{\rm d}m(\mathbf{z})\,,

where

{\Lambda}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z};h,f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{k}|f^{\prime}(u_{j})|^{2}h_{j}(f^{\prime}(u_{j}),u_{j})\Big)\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}h_{k+i}(f(z_{i}),z_{i})\Big)\,\Big|\,f(u_{j})=0,\ \forall j\in[k]\bigg]\,.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that the
h_{j}
’s are non-negative. Let
\delta>0
and consider the set

U_{\delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\big\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in K^{k}\,:\,|y_{i}-y_{j}|\geqslant\delta,\ \forall i\not=j\big\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{k}\,.

We define
Z:U_{\delta}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
by

Z(\mathbf{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\big(f(u_{1}),\ldots,f(u_{k})\big)

and note that
Z
is a smooth Gaussian process. It is straightforward to check that

|\det\big(Z^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\big)|=\prod_{j=1}^{k}|f^{\prime}(u_{k})|\,,

and so, if
t\in U_{\delta}
is such that
Z(t)=\det Z^{\prime}(t)=0
we must have a
z\in K
with
f(z)=f^{\prime}(z)=0
. By
[
7
, Lemma 2.4.2]
, this event has probability zero.

Define
Y^{\mathbf{u}}:U_{\delta}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{C}^{\ell+k}
by

Y^{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\big(f^{\prime}(u_{1}),\ldots f^{\prime}(u_{k}),f(z_{1}),\ldots,f(z_{\ell})\big)

and note that
Y^{\mathbf{u}}
is measurable, continuous and Gaussian. Finally, we set

H(\mathbf{u},Y^{\mathbf{u}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}(f^{\prime}(u_{j}),u_{j})\bigg)\bigg(\int_{K^{\ell}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}h_{k+i}(f(z_{i}),z_{i})\,{\rm d}m(\mathbf{z})\bigg)

and note that
H
is bounded and continuous. By the above, we may apply Theorem
A.1
and see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\int_{K^{\ell}}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}h_{k+i}(f(z_{i}),z_{i})\,{\rm d}m(\mathbf{z})\right)\sum_{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}(K)\cap U_{\delta}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}(f^{\prime}(y_{j}),y_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{U_{\delta}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\,|\det[Z^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})]|\,H(\mathbf{u},Y^{\mathbf{u}})\mid Z(\mathbf{z})=0\Big]\,p_{Z(\mathbf{z})}(0)\,{\rm d}m(\mathbf{u})\,.

where
p_{Z(\mathbf{z})}
is the density function of the Gaussian vector
\big(f(u_{1}),\ldots,f(u_{k})\big)
, and hence

p_{Z(\mathbf{z})}(0)^{-1}=\pi^{k}\det\big[\operatorname{Cov}\big((f(u_{j}))_{j=1}^{k}\big)\big]\,.

Since we assumed that the
h_{j}
’s are non-negative, we can send
\delta\downarrow 0
and apply the monotone convergence theorem, which implies the lemma.
∎
