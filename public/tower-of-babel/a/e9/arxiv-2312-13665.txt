Coherency properties for monoids of transformations and partitions Thanks: Supported by the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council [EP/V002953/1, EP/V003224/1]
Matthew Brookes, Victoria Gould, Nik Ruškuc

Abstract
. A monoid S S is right coherent if every finitely generated subact of every finitely presented right S S -act itself has a finite presentation; it is weakly right coherent if every finitely generated right ideal of S S has a finite presentation. We show that full and partial transformation monoids, symmetric inverse monoids and partition monoids over an infinite set are all weakly right coherent, but that none of them is right coherent. Left coherency and weak left coherency are defined dually, and the corresponding results hold for these properties. In order to prove the non-coherency results, we give a presentation of an inverse semigroup which does not embed into any left or right coherent monoid.

1.
Introduction

A monoid
S
is
right coherent
if every finitely generated
subact of every finitely presented right
S
-act itself has a finite presentation. The concept has several natural sources, including the corresponding notion for rings
[
2
]
,
the model theoretic notion of model companion
[
17
]
and questions of axiomatisability of classes of existentially closed or algebraically closed
S
-acts
[
9
]
. It is also intimately connected with notions of injectivity and purity for
S
-acts
[
4
]
.

A monoid
S
is
weakly right coherent
if every finitely generated
right ideal of
S
has a finite presentation. For rings the corresponding notions of right coherency and weak right coherency coincide, essentially because every cyclic right module is determined by a right ideal. This is not the situation for monoids. Not every cyclic right
S
-act is determined by a right ideal: one must consider right congruences per se.

We remark that other notions of coherency exist in the literature. For example, a group is coherent if every finitely generated subgroup has a finite presentation
[
18
]
. More broadly, one can say that a (universal) algebra is coherent if every finitely generated subalgebra has a finite presentation. Thus, a monoid
S
is right coherent in our sense if every finitely presented right
S
-act is coherent in this latter sense.

Left coherency and weak left coherency are defined dually to the right-handed concepts.
Left and right coherency, and hence left and right weak coherency, are finitary conditions for monoids in the sense that any finite monoid satisfies them. These notions were introduced in

[
9
]
and developed in a number of articles. Groups, right noetherian monoids, free monoids and regular monoids in which all right ideals are finitely generated are right coherent
[
10
,
12
,
5
]
. On the other hand, free inverse monoids of rank
\geq 2
are weakly left and right coherent but neither left nor right coherent
[
11
]
. The pattern of these results does not guide us in drawing immediate conjectures with respect to some very natural infinite monoids of transformations and partitions.
The aim of this paper is to remedy this situation. In particular, we resolve the question of (weak) left and right coherency for a number of monoids of major interest. The following is an aggregate of Theorems
3.4
,
3.5
and
3.7
(for weak coherency), and Theorem
5.1
(for coherency).

Main theorem
.

Let
X
be an infinite set. The full transformation monoid

\mathcal{T}_{X}
, the partial transformation monoid
\mathcal{PT}_{X}
, the symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}_{X}
and the partition monoid
\mathcal{P}_{X}
on
X
, are all weakly left and right coherent but neither left nor right coherent.

In order to prove the non-coherency results, we develop a new piece of methodology. Namely, we give a presentation of a monoid
P(g,h,e)

such that if
P(g,h,e)
occurs as a subsemigroup of any monoid
M
, it prevents
M
from being right or left coherent.

We summarise the concepts required for this paper in Section
2
. In
Section
3
we prove the positive results for weak left and right coherency. In
Section
4
we introduce the monoid
P(g,h,e)
and show that it cannot be a subsemigroup of any left or right coherent monoid.
We observe that
P(g,h,e)
is isomorphic to a submonoid of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Having done the brunt of the work, in Section
5

we are rapidly able to prove our non-coherency results.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Monoids

Throughout this section, unless otherwise stated,
S
will stand for a monoid, and its identity will be denoted by
1_{S}
.
We use
E(S)
to denote the set of idempotents of
S
. A subset
T\subseteq S
is a
subsemigroup
of
S
if it is closed under the multiplication, and a
submonoid
if additionally
1_{S}\in T
.
A
right ideal
of
S
is a subset
I\subseteq S
such that
IS:=\{xs\mid x\in I,s\in S\}\subseteq I
.

2.2.
Right congruences

For a relation
\rho\subseteq X\times X
on a set
X
, we will use both
(x,y)\in\rho
and
x\,\rho\,y
to mean that
x
and
y
are
\rho
-related. We also write
\rho^{-1}
for the relation
\{(x,y)\mid(y,x)\in\rho\}
.

A
right congruence
on a monoid
S
is an equivalence relation
\rho\subseteq S\times S
which is
right compatible
, i.e. satisfies
a\,\rho\,b\Rightarrow ac\,\rho\,bc
for all
a,b,c\in S
. We denote by
\Delta
the equality relation on
S
, which is the smallest right congruence on
S
.
For a set
Y\subseteq S\times S
we denote by
\langle Y\rangle
the right congruence on
S

generated
by
Y
,
which is the smallest right congruence that contains
Y
.
The following criterion for a pair to belong to
\langle Y\rangle
will be used throughout the paper: for
a,b\in S
we have

(a,b)\in\langle Y\rangle
if and only if there exists a
Y
-sequence from
a
to
b
, i.e. a sequence of the form

a=c_{1}t_{1},\,d_{1}t_{1}=c_{2}t_{2},\cdots,d_{m}t_{m}=b,

where
m\geq 0
,
(c_{i},d_{i})\in Y\cup Y^{-1}
,
t_{i}\in S
,
i=1,\dots,m
.
The number
m
is called the
length
of the sequence. The case
m=0
is interpreted as
a=b
.
For details see
[
14
, Lemma 1.4.37]
.
A right congruence
\rho
is
finitely generated
if there is a finite set
Y
such that
\rho=\langle Y\rangle
. Equivalently,
\rho
is finitely generated precisely when there is no infinite strictly ascending chain of right congruences
\rho_{1}\subset\rho_{2}\subset\,\dots
such that
\rho=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{n}
.

2.3.
Right
S
-acts

A
right
S
-act
is a set
A
together with a function
A\times S\rightarrow S
,
(a,s)\mapsto as
, such that
(as)t=a(st)
and
a1_{S}=a
for all
a\in A
and all
s,t\in S
.
For a set
X\subseteq A
we write
XS:=\{xs\mid x\in X,s\in S\}
.
A subset
B\subseteq A
is an
subact
of
A
if
BS\subseteq B
.
The
S
-act
A
is said to be
generated
by a set
X\subseteq A
if
XS=A
,

finitely generated
if it is generated by a finite set, and
monogenic
if it is generated by a single element.
For a more detailed introduction to actions see
[
14
, Section 1.4]
.

The right ideals of
S
are right
S
-acts via right multiplication by the elements from
S
.
They are precisely the subacts of the (monogenic) right
S
-act
S
.
If
\rho
is a right congruence on
S
then the
quotient act

S/\rho
is the set of equivalence classes together with the action
(a\rho)s=(as)\rho
. It is well known that there is a natural bijective identification between monogenic
S
-acts and right congruences; see
[
3
, Theorem 11.6]
.

2.4.
Right coherence

We saw in the Introduction how the notions of right coherence and weak right coherence both naturally arise from the notion of right coherence for rings. In addition to the definitions given in the Introduction, in terms of finite generation and presentability of
S
-acts, both these notions have a number of additional equivalent formulations. Here we present those that are most convenient for our purposes.
For this we need the notion of the
right annihilator
congruence of an element
a\in S
with respect to a right congruence
\rho
:

r(a\rho):=\{(u,v)\in S\times S\mid au\ \rho\ av\}.

It is straightforward to check that
r(a\rho)
is indeed a right congruence.

Theorem

(
[
10
, Corollaries 3.3, 3.4]
)
.

Let
S
be a monoid.

(2.4.1)

S
is right coherent if and only if for all
a,b\in S
and for all finitely generated right congruences
\rho
the subact
(a\rho)S\cap(b\rho)S
of the right
S
-act
S/\rho
is finitely generated and the right annihilator
r(a\rho)
is finitely generated.

(2.4.2)

S
is weakly right coherent if and only if for all
a,b\in S
the right ideal
aS\cap bS
of
S
is finitely generated (as a subact) and the right annihilator
r(a\Delta)
is finitely generated.

2.5.
Green’s relations

For a monoid
S
,

Green’s preorder

\leq_{\mathcal{R}}
is defined by
a\leq_{\mathcal{R}}b
if there is an
s\in S
such that
bs=a.

Green’s equivalence

\mathcal{R}
is the equivalence relation induced by the preorder
\leq_{\mathcal{R}}
,
i.e.
a\ \mathcal{R}\ b
if there are
s,t\in S
such that
as=b
and
bt=a
.
For details see
[
13
, Section 2.1]
.

2.6.
Left-right duality

All the notions that have been prefixed by the word ‘right’ have obvious left-right duals.
Thus we have left congruences, left
S
-acts, weak left coherence, left coherence, left annihilators, Green’s preorder
\leq_{\mathcal{L}}
and Green’s equivalence
\mathcal{L}
.

2.7.
Idempotents

Green’s preorder
\leq_{\mathcal{R}}
is related to the natural partial order on the set of idempotents
E(S)
, which is defined by
e\leq f
if and only if
fe=ef=e
.
Clearly
e\leq f
implies
e\leq_{\mathcal{R}}f
. Since
\leq
is defined purely in terms of the two idempotents involved, for a subsemigroup
T\leq S
we have
e\leq f
in
T
if and only if
e\leq f
in
S
.
The analogue is not true in general for the
\leq_{\mathcal{R}}
preorder.

2.8.
Regular and inverse monoids

An element
a\in S
is said to be
regular
if there is
y\in S
such that
aya=a
and
S
is called
regular
if every
a\in S
is regular.
In this case
ay
and
ya
are idempotents and we have

ay\,\mathcal{R}\,a\,\mathcal{L}\,ya
.
In fact,
S
is regular precisely when every
\mathcal{R}
-class contains an idempotent.
Furthermore, a regular
a\in S
has a
generalised inverse

x
, which satisfies
axa=a
and
xax=x
.
If every element has a
unique
generalised inverse,
S
is said to be an
inverse monoid
; in this case we denote the unique generalised inverse of
a
by
a^{-1}
.
Equivalently, inverse monoids are precisely the regular monoids in which the idempotents form a commutative subsemigroup. For details see
[
13
, Sections 2.3, 2.4, 5.1]
.
The link between the partial order
\leq
on idempotents, and the Green’s preorder
\leq_{\mathcal{R}}
becomes tight for inverse semigroups, in the sense that
a\leq_{\mathcal{R}}b\Leftrightarrow aa^{-1}\leq bb^{-1}
; see
[
13
, Proposition 2.4.1]
and its proof.
If
T
is a regular subsemigroup of a monoid
S
then the Green’s preorders
\leq_{\mathcal{R}}
and
\leq_{\mathcal{L}}
on
T
are
equal to the restrictions to
T
of the preorders
\leq_{\mathcal{R}}
and
\leq_{\mathcal{L}}
on
S
; see
[
13
, Proposition 2.4.2]
.

2.9.
Transformation monoids

Let
X
be a set.
The
partial transformation monoid

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
is the set of partial mappings from
X
to itself under composition of
mappings.
For
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
, we define its
domain
,
image
and
kernels
as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}\alpha

\displaystyle:=\{x\in X\mid x\alpha\text{ is defined}\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{im}\alpha

\displaystyle:=\{x\alpha\mid x\in X\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{ker}\alpha

\displaystyle:=\{(x,y)\in\operatorname{dom}\alpha\times\operatorname{dom}\alpha\mid x\alpha=y\alpha\},

\displaystyle\kerr\alpha

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{ker}\alpha\cup\{(x,y)\>:\>x,y\in X\setminus\operatorname{dom}\alpha\}.

The
full transformation monoid

\mathcal{T}_{X}
and the
symmetric inverse monoid

\mathcal{I}_{X}
on
X
consist respectively of full and injective mappings from
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
:

\mathcal{T}_{X}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}\mid\operatorname{dom}\alpha=X\},\quad\mathcal{I}_{X}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}\mid\operatorname{ker}\alpha=\Delta_{\operatorname{dom}\alpha}\}.

It is elementary that each of
\mathcal{T}_{X},\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{I}_{X}
is regular, and indeed
\mathcal{I}_{X}
is inverse.
Therefore to describe Green’s relations on all three it suffices to state them for
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
and then appeal to
2.8
.
Here is such a description:

\alpha\leq_{\mathcal{L}}\beta\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{im}\alpha\subseteq\operatorname{im}\beta,\quad\alpha\leq_{\mathcal{R}}\beta\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{dom}\alpha\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\beta\text{ and }\kerr\beta\subseteq\kerr\alpha.

For more details on transformation semigroups we refer the reader to
[
8
]
, though do note that while stated for finite
X
this assumption is not used. Also, the reader should note that in
[
8
]
mappings are written to the left of arguments, and the relation
\kerr\alpha
is denoted by
\pi_{\alpha}
.

2.10.
Partition monoids

Let
X
be a set, and let
X^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}\mid x\in X\}
be a disjoint copy of
X
. The
partition monoid
on
X
, denoted
\mathcal{P}_{X}
, consists of all set partitions of
X\cup X^{\prime}
under the composition defined as follows. Each partition in
\mathcal{P}_{X}
is identified with any graph on
X\cup X^{\prime}
whose connected components are the blocks of the partition. These graphs are drawn with the vertices in two rows, the vertices from
X
in the top row and those from
X^{\prime}
in the bottom row. Given
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
let
\alpha_{\downarrow}
and
\beta^{\uparrow}
be the graph obtained by relabelling every lower vertex
x^{\prime}
in
\alpha
and every upper vertex
x
in
\beta
by
x^{\prime\prime}
. Then let
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
be the graph on
\{x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\mid x\in X\}
with edge set the union of the edge sets of
\alpha_{\downarrow}
and
\beta^{\uparrow}
. The product
\alpha\beta\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
is then the partition such that the blocks are the subsets of
X\cup X^{\prime}
which are in the same connected component of
\Pi(\alpha,\beta).
The mapping
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\ast}
, where
\alpha^{\ast}
is obtained by swapping the top and bottom rows of
\alpha
, is an anti-isomorphism. Furthermore,
\alpha^{\ast}
is a generalised inverse of
\alpha
, and hence
\mathcal{P}_{X}
is regular.
The product in
\mathcal{P}_{X}
and the anti-isomorphism
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\ast}
may be nicely visualised in terms of graphs
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
, which may help a reader with subsequent arguments, for example see
[
7
]
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
. A block of
\alpha
is said to be
upper
if it is contained in
X
;
lower
if it is contained in
X^{\prime}
; or
transversal
if it contains elements from both
X
and
X^{\prime}
.
Green’s preorders on
\mathcal{P}_{X}
can be described in terms of the following parameters:

•

\operatorname{dom}\alpha
(resp.
\operatorname{codom}\alpha
) consisting of all
x\in X
(resp.
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime})
that belong to a transversal of
\alpha
;

•

\operatorname{ker}\alpha
(resp.
\operatorname{coker}\alpha
), the partition on
X
(resp.
X^{\prime}
) induced by
\alpha
;

•

N_{U}(\alpha)
(resp.
N_{L}(\alpha)
) the set of all upper (resp. lower) blocks of
\alpha
.

With this notation, for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
we have (
[
6
, Theorem 4.4]
):

\displaystyle\alpha\leq_{\mathcal{R}}\beta\Leftrightarrow\operatorname{ker}\beta\subseteq\operatorname{ker}\alpha\text{ and }N_{U}(\beta)\subseteq N_{U}(\alpha),

\displaystyle\alpha\leq_{\mathcal{L}}\beta\Leftrightarrow\operatorname{coker}\beta\subseteq\operatorname{coker}\alpha\text{ and }N_{L}(\beta)\subseteq N_{L}(\alpha).

3.
Weak coherency for transformation and partition monoids

In this section we prove that each of the monoids
\mathcal{I}_{X}
,
\mathcal{T}_{X}
,
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}_{X}
is weakly right and left coherent.
We begin with some general observations, which reduce the work needed to check the conditions from

(2.4.2)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S
be a monoid and let
s\in S
. The right congruence
\langle(1,s)\rangle
is given by

\kappa=\{(u,v)\in S\times S\mid s^{m}u=s^{n}v\text{ for some }m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
\kappa
is reflexive, symmetric and right compatible. Further, if
s^{n}u=s^{m}v
and
s^{k}v=s^{l}w
where
m,n,k,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
then
s^{n+k}u=s^{m+l}w
so
\kappa
is a right congruence, which evidently contains
(1,s)
. Conversely, note that
(1,s^{k})\in\langle(1,s)\rangle
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It then easily follows that if
s^{n}u=s^{m}v
as above, then
(u,v)\in\langle(1,s)\rangle
.
∎

Note that in Lemma
3.1
if
s
is idempotent then
\kappa
is equal to
r(e\Delta)
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
S
be a monoid and let
e\in E(S)
. Then
r(e\Delta)=\langle(1,e)\rangle
.

Corollary 3.3
.

A regular monoid
S
is weakly right (resp. left) coherent if and only if
aS\cap bS
(resp.
Sa\cap Sb
) is finitely generated for all
a,b\in S
.

Proof.

We prove the first assertion; the second is dual.
By
(2.4.2)
, it is sufficient to show that, when
S
is regular, each right annihilator
r(a\Delta)
is finitely generated.
Let
b\in S
be such that
aba=a
, and let
e=ba\in E(S)
.
Note that for any
x,y\in S
we have

ax=ay\Rightarrow bax=bay\Rightarrow abax=abay\Leftrightarrow ax=ay,

from which
r(a\Delta)=r(e\Delta)
. The result now follows from Corollary
3.2
.
∎

The condition that
aS\cap bS
is finitely generated for all
a,b\in S
is by virtue of distributivity equivalent to the condition that the intersection of finitely generated right ideals is finitely generated. Monoids satifying this condition are the focus of
[
1
]
where they are called
right ideal Howson
; under the name of
finitely (right) aligned
they play a role in the theory of
C^{*}
-algebras
[
16
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

The monoids
\mathcal{I}_{X}
,
\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
are all weakly right coherent.

Proof.

As each of
\mathcal{I}_{X}
,
\mathcal{T}_{X}
or
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
is regular, by Corollary
3.3
it suffices to check that the intersection,
\alpha S\cap\beta S
for
S=\mathcal{I}_{X},\mathcal{T}_{X}
or
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
, is finitely generated. We give a detailed proof for
S=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
, and then outline how to adapt it for
S=\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
S=\mathcal{I}_{X}
.

Recall from
2.8
and
2.9
that for
\mu\in S
we have

(1)

\mu S=\{\nu\in S\mid\operatorname{dom}\nu\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\mu,\ \kerr\mu\subseteq\kerr\nu\}.

Also, for any
\mu,\nu\in S
with
\operatorname{dom}\nu\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\mu
we have

(2)

\kerr\mu\subseteq\kerr\nu\ \Leftrightarrow\text{ every }\operatorname{ker}\nu\text{-class is a union of }\operatorname{ker}\mu\text{-classes}.

Now consider arbitrary
\alpha,\beta\in S
.
Let
Y
be the union of the equivalence classes of
\operatorname{ker}\alpha\vee\operatorname{ker}\beta
contained in
\operatorname{dom}\alpha\cap\operatorname{dom}\beta
. Note that
Y
may be empty.
Define
\epsilon\in S
to be any element with
\operatorname{dom}\epsilon=Y
and
\operatorname{ker}\epsilon=(\operatorname{ker}\alpha\vee\operatorname{ker}\beta)|_{Y}
.

We claim that

\alpha S\cap\beta S=\epsilon S
.
The reverse inclusion (
\supseteq
) follows by observing that
\operatorname{dom}\epsilon=Y\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\alpha\cap\operatorname{dom}\beta
, and that

\operatorname{ker}\epsilon
classes are unions of both
\operatorname{ker}\alpha
-classes and
\operatorname{ker}\beta
-classes, and then appealing
to (
2
) and then (
1
) to deduce
\epsilon\in\alpha S\cap\beta S
.
For the direct inclusion (
\subseteq
), suppose
\delta\in\alpha S\cap\beta S
.
By (
1
) we have

\operatorname{dom}\delta\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\alpha,\ \operatorname{dom}\delta\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\beta,\ \kerr\alpha\subseteq\kerr\delta,\ \kerr\beta\subseteq\kerr\beta.

From this it immediately follows that
\operatorname{dom}\delta\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\alpha\cap\operatorname{dom}\beta
, and that every
\operatorname{ker}\delta
-class is a union of
(\operatorname{ker}\alpha\vee\operatorname{ker}\beta)
-classes.
Since
Y
is by definition the largest subset of
\operatorname{dom}\alpha\cap\operatorname{dom}\beta
that is a union of
(\operatorname{ker}\alpha\vee\operatorname{ker}\beta)
-classes, we deduce
\operatorname{dom}\delta\subseteq Y=\operatorname{dom}\epsilon
.
Now, using (
2
) we have that
\kerr\delta\subseteq\kerr\epsilon
, and therefore
\delta\in\epsilon S
by (
1
), proving that
\alpha S\cap\beta S=\epsilon S
as claimed, and hence the theorem for
S=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
.

For
S=\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
S=\mathcal{I}_{X}
the same proof works, but with some conditions concerning domains and kernels omitted. Specifically, as
\operatorname{dom}\alpha=X
for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}_{X}
, all requirements concerning domains become vacuous in this case, and furthermore
\kerr\alpha=\operatorname{ker}\alpha
.
Likewise, for
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
we have
\operatorname{ker}\alpha=\Delta_{\operatorname{dom}\alpha}
, so that the conditions regarding kernels are equivalent to those for domains, and only one set needs to be kept.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

The monoids
\mathcal{I}_{X}
,
\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
are all weakly left coherent.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to, and easier than, that of Theorem
3.4
.
This time, given
\alpha,\beta\in S=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
, we need to establish existence of
\epsilon\in S
such that

S\alpha\cap S\beta=S\epsilon
. This is equivalent to
\operatorname{im}\epsilon=\operatorname{im}\alpha\cap\operatorname{im}\beta
, and it is clear that such
\epsilon
exists.
The modification to
S=\mathcal{T}_{X}
is equally straightforward, but it should be noted that the possibility
S\alpha\cap S\beta=\varnothing
arises when
\operatorname{im}\alpha\cap\operatorname{im}\beta=\varnothing
.
No modification is needed for
\mathcal{I}_{X}
.
∎

Remark 3.6

One can prove the assertions for

Theorem 3.7
.

The partition monoid
\mathcal{P}_{X}
is weakly right coherent and weakly left coherent.

Proof.

Yet again, by Corollary
3.3
, it is sufficient to show for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
that
\alpha\mathcal{P}_{X}\cap\beta\mathcal{P}_{X}
is finitely generated.

If
\alpha\mathcal{P}_{X}\cap\beta\mathcal{P}_{X}=\varnothing
we are done. Suppose therefore that there is some
\gamma\in\alpha\mathcal{P}_{X}\cap\beta\mathcal{P}_{X}
, so that
\gamma\mathcal{P}_{X}\subseteq\alpha\mathcal{P}_{X}
and
\gamma\mathcal{P}_{X}\subseteq\beta\mathcal{P}_{X}
. From
2.10
,
\operatorname{ker}\alpha\subseteq\operatorname{ker}\gamma
,
N_{U}(\alpha)\subseteq N_{U}(\gamma)
,
\operatorname{ker}\beta\subseteq\operatorname{ker}\gamma
and
N_{U}(\beta)\subseteq N_{U}(\gamma)
. Let
Y
be the union of the vertices in
N_{U}(\alpha)\cup N_{U}(\beta)
. Note that as
N_{U}(\alpha)\cup N_{U}(\beta)\subseteq N_{U}(\gamma)
any block of
N_{U}(\alpha)
either coincides with a block of
N_{U}(\beta)
or is disjoint from all blocks of
N_{U}(\beta)
, and vice versa. Therefore
N_{U}(\alpha)\cup N_{U}(\beta)
is a partition of

. Also, since
\gamma
exists, there are no edges in
\alpha
or
\beta
between
X\backslash Y
and
Y.

Consider the partition
\Gamma
of
X\backslash Y
induced by
(\operatorname{ker}\alpha\cup\operatorname{ker}\beta)|_{X\backslash Y}
. For each part
Z
in
\Gamma
choose some
z\in Z.
Then consider the element
\delta\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
which has

and the other non-trivial blocks are

for each

Weak left coherence of

4.
Annihilator congruences

In this section we prove a number of technical results in the context where we have several elements in a monoid
S
satisfying some prescribed constraints. We then define a particular right annihilator congruence and show that, under a few assumptions about the chosen elements, it is not finitely generated. The results of this section will enable us to prove our non-coherency results in the next section.
Throughout the section,
\mathbb{N}
stands for the set
\{1,2,\dots\}
of natural numbers, and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The semigroup
P=P(g,h,e)
defined by the presentation

\begin{array}[]{ccl}P(g,h,e)=\langle g,h,e&|&hg=gh,\ ghg=g,\ hgh=h,\ ghe=egh=e,\ e=ee,\\
&&eg^{k}eh^{k}=g^{k}eh^{k}e,\ eh^{k}eg^{k}=h^{k}eg^{k}e,\ k\in\mathbb{N}\ \rangle.\end{array}

is isomorphic to the inverse submonoid of the symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}
generated by the elements

\gamma:x\mapsto x+1\ (x\in\mathbb{Z}),\ \epsilon:x\mapsto x\ (x\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})

via
g\mapsto\gamma
,
h\mapsto\gamma^{-1}
,
e\mapsto\epsilon
.

Proof.

Let
P^{\prime}
denote the inverse submonoid of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}
generated by
\gamma
and
\epsilon
.
A routine computation verifies that
\gamma
,
\gamma^{-1}
and
\epsilon
satisfy the defining relations for
P
.
Hence the above mapping induces an onto homomorphism
\phi:P\rightarrow P^{\prime}
. We will show that
\phi
is also injective.

Working in
P
,
it is immediate from the presentation that
gh=hg
is the identity element. In the remainder of the proof we will write this element as
1
.
The element
h
is the group inverse of
g
, so we will write
g^{-1}
instead of
h
.

Since
g^{m}eg^{-m}g^{m}eg^{-m}=g^{m}e1eg^{-m}=g^{m}eg^{-m}

it follows that all
g^{m}eg^{-m}
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}
are idempotents.
These idempotents commute:

(g^{n}eg^{-n})(g^{m}eg^{-m})=g^{m}(g^{n-m}eg^{m-n})eg^{-m}=g^{m}e(g^{n-m}eg^{m-n})g^{-m}=(g^{m}eg^{-m})(g^{n}eg^{-n}).

Every element
u
of
P
is equal to a product of the form

u=g^{i_{1}}eg^{i_{2}}eg^{i_{3}}e\dots eg^{i_{n-1}}eg^{i_{n}}

with
n\geq 1
,
i_{1},i_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
i_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
for
2\leq j\leq n-1
, which in turn can be written as:

u=(g^{i_{1}}eg^{-i_{1}})(g^{i_{1}+i_{2}}eg^{-(i_{1}+i_{2})})\dots(g^{i_{1}+\dots+i_{n-1}}eg^{-(i_{1}+\dots+i_{n-1})})g^{i_{1}+\dots+i_{n}}.

Using idempotency and commutativity, it follows that
u
can be written as

(3)

u=(g^{k_{1}}eg^{-k_{1}})\dots(g^{k_{p}}eg^{-k_{p}})g^{k},

where
p\geq 0
, and where
k_{1},\dots,k_{p}
and
k
are integers with
k_{1}<\dots<k_{p}
.

To show that
\phi
is injective, let
u,v\in P
be such that
u\phi=v\phi
.
Let
u
be written as in (
3
), and write
v
analogously as

v=(g^{l_{1}}eg^{-l_{1}})\dots(g^{l_{q}}eg^{-l_{q}})g^{l},

with
q\geq 0
, and where
l_{1},\dots,l_{q}
and
l
are integers with
l_{1}<\dots<l_{q}
.
Now,
u\phi
is the element
\mu
of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}
with domain
\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-k_{1},\dots,-k_{p}\}
and
x\mu=x+k
for all
x
in that domain.
Likewise,
v\phi
is the element
\nu
of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}
with domain
\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-l_{1},\dots,-l_{q}\}
and
x\nu=x+l
for all
x
in that domain.
From
\mu=\nu
we obtain
k=l
, and recalling that
k_{1}<\dots<k_{p}
and
l_{1}<\dots<l_{q}
, also that
p=q
and
k_{t}=l_{t}
for

t=1,\dots,p
. It then follows that
u=v
in

, and the proof is complete.
∎

From the concrete representation of
P
given in Proposition
4.1
it is easy to see that
the group of units of

is the infinite cyclic subgroup

generated by

.
The decomposition (
3
) from the proof of Proposition
4.1
implies that

is generated by

and the single idempotent

.
Such semigroups were considered by McAlister in
[

]
.
It is possible to deduce that

is inverse via this general approach, but the above argument seems more straightforward in this instance.
In fact,

is a semidirect product of

, and the semilattice of idempotents

,
with

acting on

by conjugation; this can be derived from the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Motivated by

, we now list some properties of elements

in a monoid
S
, where
g
is regular,
h
is a generalised inverse of
g
with
gh=hg
, and

is an idempotent. These properties will transpire to be a barrier to coherence.
Recall from
2.7
that the set of idempotents

of a monoid

is equipped with the partial order
\leq
defined via

e\leq f\Leftrightarrow ef=fe=e
.
The properties are:

(NC1)

hge=e=ehg
;

(NC2)

e(g^{n}eh^{n})=(g^{n}eh^{n})e
and
e(h^{n}eg^{n})=(h^{n}eg^{n})e
for all

;

(NC3)

for

we have
g^{m}eh^{m}\nleq h^{n}eg^{n}
and
h^{n}eg^{n}\nleq g^{m}eh^{m}
; furthermore, when
m\neq n
, we also have
g^{m}eh^{m}\nleq g^{n}eh^{n}
and
h^{m}eg^{m}\nleq h^{n}eg^{n}
;

(NC4)

for all

we have that
eg^{n}eh^{n}\not\leq g^{k}eh^{k}
for any

and
eg^{n}eh^{n}\not\leq h^{k}eg^{k}
for any

.

Note that, as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, conditions
(NC1)
,
(NC2)
imply that all the elements appearing in
(NC3)
,
(NC4)
are idempotents.

Informally, condition
(NC3)
asserts that the conjugates of

by the integer powers of

are all distinct and form an infinite antichain in
E(S)
.
Condition
(NC4)
then gives information about the position of an element of the form
eg^{n}eh^{n}

with respect to a finite portion
\{g^{k}eh^{k},h^{k}eg^{k}\>:\>k=1,\dots,n\}
of this antichain: it (obviously) lies below

and nothing else.

Proposition 4.2
.

The elements
g,h,e
of the monoid

from Proposition
4.1
satisfy all the properties
(NC1)
–
(NC4)
.

Proof.

The necessary pre-conditions for
g,h,e
, as well as properties
(NC1)
and
(NC2)
, follow from the defining presentation for
P
.
To prove that
(NC3)
and
(NC4)
are satisfied we work in the isomorphic copy
P^{\prime}
of
P
given in Proposition
4.1
.
From
2.8
and
2.9
we have that the relation
\eta\leq\zeta
on
E(\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}})
is equivalent to
\operatorname{dom}(\eta)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(\zeta)
. Since

\operatorname{dom}(\epsilon\gamma^{k}\epsilon\gamma^{-k})=\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0,-k\}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{dom}(\gamma^{k}\epsilon\gamma^{-k})=\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{-k\}

it follows that
\{\gamma^{n}\epsilon\gamma^{-n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is an antichain with respect to the natural partial order on

and that
\epsilon\gamma^{n}\epsilon\gamma^{-n}\leq\gamma^{k}\epsilon\gamma^{-k}
only when

or

. Thus conditions
(NC3)
and
(NC4)
are satisfied, and the proof is complete.
∎

Our main objective in this section is to prove the following result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
S
be a monoid, let
g\in S
be regular with
h\in S
a generalised inverse of
g
such that
hg=gh
, and let
e\in E(S)
.
If these elements satisfy
(NC1)
–
(NC4)

then

is not right or left coherent.

Corollary 4.4
.

If a monoid
S
contains the monoid
P(g,h,e)
from Proposition
4.1
as a subsemigroup, then
S
is not left or right coherent.

The remainder of this section comprises the proof of Theorem
4.3
. We shall prove that the conditions imply that
S
is not right coherent; as the conditions are left-right dual we deduce that
S
is also not left coherent.

The first stage of our proof for Theorem
4.3
is the following technical lemma in which we give an explicit generating set for a right annihilator congruence.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
g\in S
be regular, let
h\in S
be a generalised inverse of
g
such that
hg=gh
, and let
e\in E(S)
be such that
(NC1)
and
(NC2)
hold. Let
\rho:=\langle(1,g)\rangle
. Then the right annihilator
r(e\rho)
is given by

(4)

\displaystyle r(e\rho)

\displaystyle=\{(u,v)\in S\times S\mid\textup{for some }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ g^{n}eu=ev\ \text{or}\ h^{n}eu=ev\}

(5)

\displaystyle=\langle\{(1,e)\}\cup\{(g^{n}e,h^{n}eg^{n})\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{(h^{n}e,g^{n}eh^{n})\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\rangle.

Proof.

Note that
(NC1)
implies
(hg)^{n}e=e
for all
n.
We remark, under the assumptions on
g,h,e
, that
g^{n}eu=ev
is equivalent to
eu=h^{n}ev.
Indeed, suppose
g^{n}eu=ev,
then

h^{n}(ev)=h^{n}(g^{n}eu)=(hg)^{n}(eu)=eu,

and the converse is analogous.

From the definition of the right annihilator and Lemma
3.1
we have that

r(e\rho)=\{(u,v)\in S\times S\mid\exists m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ g^{m}eu=g^{n}ev\}.

The fact that
g
and
h
are inverses of each other in the subsemigroup of
S
generated by
g,h,e

establishes (
4
).

For (
5
), let
\sigma
denote the set on the right hand side. As
r(e\Delta)\subseteq r(e\rho)
, by Corollary
3.2
, we have
(1,e)\in r(e\rho)
. Also, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

h^{n}e(g^{n}e)=e(h^{n}eg^{n}),

which implies that
(g^{n}e,h^{n}eg^{n})\in r(e\rho)
. By the same argument,
(h^{n}e,g^{n}eh^{n})\in r(e\rho)
, hence
\sigma\subseteq r(e\rho)
.

For the reverse inclusion suppose that
g^{n}eu=ev,
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

v=1v\ \sigma\ ev=g^{n}eu=(g^{n}e)eu\ \sigma\ h^{n}eg^{n}eu=eh^{n}eg^{n}eu=eh^{n}ev=eu\ \sigma\ 1u=u.

Analogously,
h^{n}eu=ev
implies
(u,v)\in\sigma
, and thus
r(e\rho)=\sigma,
as required.
∎

The following provides the main step in proving Theorem
4.3
, it is again technical and carries forward several assumptions from the previous lemma.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
g\in S
be regular, let
h\in S
be a generalised inverse of
g
such that
hg=gh
, and let
e\in E(S)
be such that

(NC1)
–
(NC4)
hold. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let

Y_{n}=\{(1,e)\}\cup\{(g^{k}e,h^{k}eg^{k})\mid 0<k\leq n\}\cup\{(h^{k}e,g^{k}eh^{k})\mid 0<k\leq n\}.

Then

\langle Y_{n}\rangle\neq\langle Y_{n+1}\rangle

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
(g^{n}e,h^{n}eg^{n})\notin\langle Y_{n-1}\rangle
for any
n>1
. Aiming for a contradiction we suppose that there is an
n
for which this is untrue. We note that
g^{n}e\neq h^{n}eg^{n}
, since
g^{n}e\ \mathcal{R}\ g^{n}eh^{n}
and
the elements
g^{n}eh^{n}
and
h^{n}eg^{n}
are incomparable by
(NC3)
. Thus there is a
Y_{n-1}
-sequence of length
m>0
from
g^{n}e
to
h^{n}eg^{n}.
Consider such a sequence of minimum length, explicitly:

g^{n}e=c_{1}t_{1},\,d_{1}t_{1}=c_{2}t_{2},\,\dots,\ d_{m}t_{m}=h^{n}eg^{n}.

where
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
t_{i}\in S
and
(c_{i},d_{i})\in Y_{n-1}\cup Y_{n-1}^{-1}
for
i=1,\dots,m
.

We shall show by induction that each
(c_{i},d_{i})
is equal to
(1,e)
or
(e,1)
and that
eg^{n}e=et_{i}
for each
i.
This contradicts the fact that
d_{m}t_{m}=h^{n}eg^{n}
as we would then have

eg^{n}eh^{n}\ \mathcal{R}\ eg^{n}e=et_{m}=ed_{m}t_{m}=e(h^{n}eg^{n})\leq h^{n}eg^{n}

which contradicts assumption
(NC4)
.

We proceed with the inductive argument. We have
g^{n}e=c_{1}t_{1},
so
g^{n}e\leq_{\mathcal{R}}c_{1}
. By assumption
(NC3)
, we have
g^{n}eh^{n}\not\leq g^{k}eh^{k}
or
h^{k}eg^{k}
for any
0<k<n
, which implies that
c_{1}\neq g^{k}e,h^{k}eg^{k},h^{k}e,g^{k}eh^{k}
. Therefore
(c_{1},d_{1})=(1,e)
or
(e,1).
It then also follows that
et_{1}=ec_{1}t_{1}=e(g^{n}e)
as required.

For the inductive step we assume that
(c_{i},d_{i})
is
(1,e)
or
(e,1)
and
et_{i}=eg^{n}e
for each
1\leq i<l.
Then we have that
d_{l-1}t_{l-1}=c_{l}t_{l}
and we know

is equal to
1
or
e
; we consider these cases separately. First suppose
d_{l-1}=e
so
eg^{n}e=et_{l-1}=c_{l}t_{l}
, which implies that
eg^{n}e\leq_{\mathcal{R}}c_{l}.
Therefore, by assumption
(NC4)
,
c_{l}
is not of the form
g^{k}e,\ h^{k}eg^{k},\ h^{k}e
or
g^{k}eh^{k}
for any
0<k<n
, so that
c_{l}
is
1
or
e.
Now suppose that
d_{l-1}=1
, so that
t_{l-1}=c_{l}t_{l}.
In this case, if
c_{l}=g^{k}e
then using assumption
(NC2)
we have

eg^{n}eh^{n}\ \mathcal{R}\ eg^{n}e=et_{l-1}=ec_{l}t_{l}=e(g^{k}e)t_{l}=(g^{k}h^{k})eg^{k}et_{l}=(g^{k}e)(h^{k}eg^{k}t_{l})\leq_{\mathcal{R}}g^{k}eh^{k}.

By
2.8
this implies
eg^{n}eh^{n}\leq g^{k}eh^{k}
, which contradicts assumption
(NC4)
. Very similar arguments, using the condition that
eg^{n}eh^{n}\not\leq h^{k}eg^{k},g^{k}eh^{k}
, demonstrate that
c_{l}
is also not
h^{k}eg^{k},\ h^{k}e
or
g^{k}eh^{k}
. Therefore we have
(c_{l},d_{l})=(1,e)
or
(e,1).
Furthermore, as
d_{l-1}=1
or

we obtain that

et_{l}=ec_{l}t_{l}=ed_{l-1}t_{l-1}=et_{l-1}=eg^{n}e.

This completes the inductive step, which completes the proof.

4.3
.

Let

Remark 4.7
.

We have seen in Corollary
4.4
that the monoid
P(g,h,e)
is a bar to left or right coherency of over-monoids.
We note that in this regard, the monoid
P(g,h,e)
is somewhat special, in that it cannot be replaced by an arbitrary non-coherent monoid.
For example, if
F_{3}
is the free monoid on 3 generators, then from
[
5
, Example 6.1]
the monoid
F_{3}\times F_{3}
is not right coherent, but it embeds into a group, and all groups are right coherent
[
10
]
.

Remark 4.8
.

We remark that whereas the conditions in Proposition
4.6
are sufficient to imply that the monoid is not left or right coherent, they are not necessary. For example, the non-right coherent monoid
F_{3}\times F_{3}
mentioned above has no regular elements, other than the identity. Consequently, there are no suitable elements
e,g,h
to witness the conditions of Proposition
4.6
.
An alternative example, this time with non-trivial group of units and a non-unit regular element, may be found in Example 3.1 of
[
10
]
.
In the monoid of this example the two idempotents are comparable, violating the conditions of Proposition
4.6
.

5.
Transformation and partition monoids are not right or left coherent

In Section
3
we saw that the monoids
\mathcal{I}_{X}
,
\mathcal{T}_{X}
,
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{P}_{X}
are all weakly left and right coherent. We proceed to show that none of these monoids are right or left coherent.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any infinite set
X
, the symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}_{X}
, the full transformation monoid
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, the partial transformation monoid
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
, and the partition monoid
\mathcal{P}_{X}
are not right or left coherent.

Proof.

All the assertions follow from Corollary
4.4
and Proposition
4.1
via a number of embeddings.
First,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}
naturally embeds into
\mathcal{I}_{X}
, since
X
, being infinite, contains a countable subset.
Next,
\mathcal{I}_{X}
is a submonoid of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
by definition. It is also isomorphic to a submonoid of
\mathcal{P}_{X}
, if we view partial bijections as partitions with trivial kernels and cokernels. Finally,
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
embeds into
\mathcal{T}_{Y}
, where
Y:=X\cup\{-\}
, via the standard trick of interpreting a partial mapping
\alpha
on
X
as a full mapping on
Y
, which sends to
-
all
x\in X
such that
x\alpha
is undefined, as well as
-
itself. But,
X
is infinite, so
|Y|=|X|
, and thus
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{X}
embeds into
\mathcal{T}_{X}
.
∎

We conclude by remarking that the method of demonstrating that a given monoid is not left or right
coherent by virtue of the existence of a subsemigroup with particular properties is a novel one. The subsemigroup
P(g,h,e)
we devise here for this purpose is inverse
with a non-trivial group of units and infinitely many idempotents.
As already mentioned in Remark
4.8
, the method does not apply
with this

P(g,h,e)
,
to some monoids that are known by ad hoc
methods not to be left or right coherent. Furthermore, there are whole classes of monoids, whose coherency properties
are unknown, to which the method, as is, cannot be applied, since all the monoids in questions have only trivial subgroups or only one idempotent. We therefore pose the question of whether it would be possible to
find other configurations of elements within monoids that would prevent them from
being left or right coherent, and such that our method could then be applied to these classes.
A particular case of interest would be that of cancellative monoids, including cancellative monoids of transformations.
