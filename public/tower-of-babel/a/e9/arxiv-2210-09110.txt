Symmetry of positive solutions for Lane-Emden systems involving the Logarithmic Laplacian
Rong Zhang, Vishvesh Kumar and Michael Ruzhansky

Abstract
. We study the Lane-Emden system involving the logarithmic Laplacian: { ℒ Δ ​ u ​ ( x ) = v p ​ ( x ) , x ∈ ℝ n , ℒ Δ ​ v ​ ( x ) = u q ​ ( x ) , x ∈ ℝ n , \begin{cases}\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}u(x)=v^{p}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\ \ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}v(x)=u^{q}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases} where p , q > 1 , n ≥ 2 p,q>1,n\geq 2 and ℒ Δ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta} denotes the logarithmic Laplacian arising as a formal derivative ∂ s | s = 0 ( − Δ ) s \partial_{s}|_{s=0}(-\Delta)^{s} of fractional Laplacians at s = 0 . s=0. We recall that, the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s},\,s\in(0,1)
, on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a nonlocal pseudo-differential operator defined on
u\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=C_{n,s}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy=C_{n,s}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash B_{\epsilon}(x)}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy,

(1.1)

where
P.V.
stands for the Cauchy principal value,
B_{\epsilon}(x)
is the open ball with centre
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and radius

\epsilon>0
, and

C_{n,s}=2^{2s}\pi^{-\frac{n}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+2s}{2})}{-\Gamma(-s)}}=2^{2s}\pi^{-\frac{n}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+2s}{2})}{\Gamma(1-s)}}

is a normalized positive constant depending on
n
and
s
(see
[
32
]
) so that we have the following definition of the operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
via the Euclidean Fourier transform:

\widehat{(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)}=|\xi|^{2s}\hat{u}(\xi),\ u\in S,

where
\hat{u}
is the Euclidean Fourier transform of
u
.

The non-locality of the fractional Laplacian makes it difficult to investigate. In order to overcome this difficulty, the extension method was firstly introduced by Caffarelli and Silvestre in
[
11
]
. The main idea of the extension method is to reduce the nonlocal problem to a local one in higher dimensions. Namely, for a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we consider the extension
U:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that satisfies

\begin{cases}\ \text{div}(y^{1-2s}\nabla U)=0,&(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty),\\
\ U(x,0)=u(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

Then we have

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=-C_{n,s}\lim_{y\rightarrow 0^{+}}y^{1-2s}\frac{\partial U}{\partial y},\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1.2)

When

\frac{1}{p+1}+\frac{1}{q+1}>\frac{n-2}{n}

For non-radial solutions, and

[

It is known that if
(p,q)
in critical or supercritical case (i.e.
\frac{1}{p+1}+\frac{1}{q+1}\leq\frac{n-2}{n}

1.2
) admits some positive classical solutions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
[
38

When
0<s<1

[
9

obtained that in the subcritical case (i.e.
1<p,q<\frac{n+2s}{n-2s}

1.2

p=q=\frac{n+2s}{n-2s}
), the solutions are radial symmetric about some point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the special case
p=q
,
u=v
, system (
1.2
) will become

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=u^{p}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1.3)

Chen, Li and Li
[
14

developed a direct method of moving planes for the fractional Laplacian. By using the method of moving planes, they established symmetry and monotonicity for positive solutions of (
1.3
). Namely, in the critical case
p=\frac{n+2s}{n-2s}
, the solution is radially symmetric and monotone decreasing about some point, and in the subcritical case
1<p<\frac{n+2s}{n-2s}
, the equation (
1.3
) does not have any positive solutions.

The main aim of this paper to begin an investigation about the qualitative properties of solutions to the nonlinear equations and systems involving the logarithmic Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n},
defined as a formal derivative
\partial_{s}|_{s=0}(-\Delta)^{s}
of fractional Laplacians at
s=0.

The logarithmic Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}
was introduced by Chen and Weth

[
10
]
. On compactly supported Dini continuous functions, the logarithmic Laplacian
\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}
is defined by the following pointwise evaluation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}[(-\Delta)^{s}u](x)

(1.4)

\displaystyle=C_{n}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)1_{B_{1}(x)}(y)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n}}dy+\rho_{n}u(x),

where

C_{n}:=\pi^{-\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}),\ \rho_{n}:=2\log 2+\psi(\frac{n}{2})-\gamma.

Here
\Gamma
is the Gamma function,
\psi=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}
is the Digamma function,
\gamma=-\Gamma^{\prime}(1)
is the Euler Mascheroni constant,
B_{1}(x)
is an open ball with
x
as its center and
1
as its radius and
1_{B_{1}(x)}
is the characteristic function of
B_{1}(x)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We note that (see
[
10
, Remark 1.2]
)
\rho_{n}=-2\gamma+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{(n-1)/2}\frac{2}{2k-1}
for
n
odd and
\rho_{n}=2(\log 2-\gamma)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{(n-2)/2}\frac{1}{k}
for
n
even. It is easy to see that
\rho_{n}\geq 0
for every
n\geq 2.

Throughout the paper, we will assume that
n\geq 2
so that
\rho_{n}\geq 0.
If there is no confusion, we will not write P.V. in the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}
from this point.

Using the expression (
1.4
), one can define
\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}
for a quite large class of functions
u
. To illustrate this, we define, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the space
L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as the space of locally integrable functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\|u\|_{L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|}{(1+|x|)^{n+2s}}dx<+\infty.

It was proved by
[
10
, Proposition 1.3]
that for
u\in L_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which is also Dini continuous at some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the quantity
[\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}u](x)
is well defined by the formula (
1.4
). Furthermore, as it was shown in
[
10
]
,
\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}
is a weakly singular operator having an intrinsic scaling property of the logarithmic order with the Fourier symbol
2\log|\xi|.
They also establish that if
u\in C_{c}^{\vartheta}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
\vartheta>0
, then

\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}u(x)=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{d}{ds}(-\Delta)^{s}u.

(1.5)

The strong maximum principle for pointwise solution on bounded Lipschitz domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
was established by Chen and Weth
[
10
, Proposition 4.1]
.
Among other things, they (
[
10
]

least-energy solution of a limiting problem in terms of the logarithmic Laplacian by using variational methods, uniform energy-derived estimates,
and a new logarithmic-type Sobolev inequality.
Very recently, a direct method of moving plane for the logarithmic Laplacian on

\begin{cases}\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}u(x)=v^{p}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\

Theorem 1.1

Let

u(x)=O\bigg(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}\bigg),\ v(x)=O\bigg(\frac{1}{|x|^{\beta}}\bigg),\quad|x|\rightarrow\infty,

(1.7)

\ \qquad\qquad\cdot\\
\ \qquad\qquad\cdot\\
\ \qquad\qquad\cdot\\

Theorem 1.2

Let

2.
Key ingredients for the method of moving planes

This section is devoted to developing basic and key results needed to apply the method of moving planes for establishing the proof of our main result in the next section.

We first present some basic notation and nomenclatures which will be beneficial for the rest of the paper.

Select any direction as the
x_{1}
-direction, consider the
x_{1}
-direction, for
x=(x_{1},\cdot\cdot\cdot,x_{n})=(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Denote the moving planes

T_{\lambda}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{1}=\lambda,\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}

and the region to the left by

\Sigma_{\lambda}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{1}<\lambda\}.

Let

x^{\lambda}=(2\lambda-x_{1},x^{\prime})

is the reflection of the point
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})
about the plane
T_{\lambda}
.

Define

u_{\lambda}(x):=u(x^{\lambda}),\quad v_{\lambda}(x):=v(x^{\lambda}),

and denote

U_{\lambda}(x)=u_{\lambda}(x)-u(x),\quad V_{\lambda}(x)=v_{\lambda}(x)-v(x).

It is easy to see that
U_{\lambda}
and
V_{\lambda}
are anti-symmetric functions, that is,
U_{\lambda}(x)=-U_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda})
and
V_{\lambda}(x)=-V_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda}).

Now, we will prove the maximum principles for the logarithmic Laplacian.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Maximum principle for anti-symmetric functions) Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\Sigma_{\lambda}.
Suppose that
U_{\lambda}\in L_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
V_{\lambda}\in L_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
are Dini continuous functions in
\Omega
and continuous functions in
\bar{\Omega}
such that the pair
(U_{\lambda},V_{\lambda})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Omega,\\
\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Omega,\\
\ U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Sigma_{\lambda}\backslash\Omega,\\
\ U_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda})=-U(x),&in\ \Sigma_{\lambda},\\
\ V_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda})=-V(x),&in\ \Sigma_{\lambda}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Then we have

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad in\ \Omega.

(2.2)

If
U_{\lambda}(x)
and
V_{\lambda}(x)
attain
0
somewhere in

\Omega
, then

U_{\lambda}(x)=V_{\lambda}(x)\equiv 0,\quad a.e.\ \text{for}\ x\in\ \mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

Suppose, if possible, that (
2.2
) does not hold, so that there exists a
x^{o}\in\bar{\Omega}
such that

U_{\lambda}(x^{o})=\min\limits_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}U_{\lambda}(x)<0\,\,\text{or}\,\,V_{\lambda}(x^{o})=\min\limits_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}V_{\lambda}(x)<0.

We can further deduce from (
2.1
) that
x^{o}
is in the interior of
\Omega
. If
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0,
it follows from (
1.4
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})

\displaystyle=C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)-U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)-U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy+\rho_{n}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})

(2.3)

\displaystyle=C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)-U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y^{\lambda})-U_{\lambda}(y^{\lambda})}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy+\rho_{n}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)-U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y^{\lambda})+U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy+\rho_{n}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})(1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)+1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y^{\lambda}))}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy+\rho_{n}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0,

which contradicts the inequality (
2.1
).

Similarly, for
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
, we also reach at contradiction with (
2.1
). This verifies (
2.2
).

Now, we have proved that
U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0
, and
V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. If there exists some point
x^{o}\in\Omega
, such that
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})=0
and
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})=0
, then from (
2
) we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})=C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{-U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy-C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy=C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{-U_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy,

(2.4)

and

0\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}V_{\lambda}(x^{o})=C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{-V_{\lambda}(y)}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy.

(2.5)

We derive immediately using
U_{\lambda}\geq 0
and
V_{\lambda}\geq 0
that

U_{\lambda}(x)=V_{\lambda}(x)\equiv 0,\quad a.e.\ in\ \mathbb{R}^{n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

The following narrow region principle will also be necessary for proving subsequent results.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Narrow region principle)
Let
\Omega
be a bounded (narrow) Lipschitz domain in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
such that it is contained in
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\lambda-l<x_{1}<\lambda\}
with small
l.
Suppose that
c_{i}(x),i=1,2
, are both bounded from below in
\Omega
with
c_{2}(x)\leq 0
. Suppose that
U_{\lambda}\in L_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
V_{\lambda}\in L_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
are Dini continuous functions in
\Omega
and continuous functions in
\bar{\Omega}
such that they satisfy

\begin{cases}\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x)+c_{1}(x)V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Omega,\\
\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}V_{\lambda}(x)+c_{2}(x)U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Omega,\\
\ U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Sigma_{\lambda}\backslash\Omega,\\
\ U_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda})=-U(x),V_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda})=-V(x),&in\ \Sigma_{\lambda},\end{cases}

(2.6)

then, for sufficiently small
l
, we have

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad in\ \Omega.

(2.7)

Furthermore, if
U_{\lambda}(x)
and
V_{\lambda}(x)
attain
0
somewhere in

\Omega
, then

U_{\lambda}(x)=V_{\lambda}(x)\equiv 0,\quad a.e.\ in\ \mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.8)

Proof.

If (
2.7
) does not hold then there exists a point
x^{o}\in\bar{\Omega}
such that

U_{\lambda}(x^{o})=\min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}U_{\lambda}(x)<0\,\,\text{or}\,\,V_{\lambda}(x^{o})=\min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}V_{\lambda}(x)<0,

and one can further deduce using the assumption
U_{\lambda}\geq 0,V_{\lambda}\geq 0
on
\Sigma_{\lambda}\backslash\Omega
from (
2.1
) that
x^{o}
is in the interior of
\Omega
.

Without loss of generality, we suppose that
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
. Let us first consider the case when
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})\geq 0
. By performing similar calculations as in (
2
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})\leq C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})(1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)+1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y^{\lambda}))}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy+\rho_{n}U_{\lambda}(x^{o}).

(2.9)

Let
H=\{y\mid l<y_{1}-x_{1}^{o}<1,|y^{\prime}-(x^{o})^{\prime}|<1\}
,
s=y_{1}-x_{1}^{o}
,
\tau=|y^{\prime}-(x^{o})^{\prime}|
and
\omega_{n-2}=|B_{1}(0)|
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
. Now we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{H}\frac{1}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy=\int_{l}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega_{n-2}\tau^{n-2}}{(s^{2}+\tau^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}d\tau ds

(2.10)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega_{n-2}t^{n-2}}{(1+t^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}dtds\geq C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds\rightarrow\infty,\,\,\,\text{as}\,\,l\rightarrow 0,

where we have used the substitution
\tau=st
.

Since
c_{1}(x)
is lower bounded in
\Omega
and
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
, we get, using (
2.10
), that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o})

\displaystyle\leq CU_{\lambda}(x^{o})\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o})

(2.11)

\displaystyle=U_{\lambda}(x^{o})\Bigg(C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{1}(x^{o})\frac{V_{\lambda}(x^{o})}{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})}\Bigg)<0,

when
l
sufficiently small. This is contradiction for a condition in (
2.6
). Therefore, (
2.7
) must be true.

Now, let us consider the case when
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
, by (
2.6
) and (
2.10
), we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o})\leq CU_{\lambda}(x^{o})\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o}).

(2.12)

Since
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
, there exists
\hat{x}\in\Omega
such that

V_{\lambda}(\hat{x})=\min_{\bar{\Omega}}V_{\lambda}(x)<0.

Similarly to (
2.12
), by (

) and

, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}V_{\lambda}(\hat{x})+c_{2}(\hat{x})U_{\lambda}(\hat{x})

\displaystyle\leq CV_{\lambda}(\hat{x})\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{2}(\hat{x})U_{\lambda}(\hat{x})

(2.13)

\displaystyle\leq CV_{\lambda}(\hat{x})\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{2}(\hat{x})U_{\lambda}(x^{o}).

Adding (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), we get

\displaystyle\Bigg(C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{2}(\hat{x})\Bigg)U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+\Bigg(C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{1}(x^{o})\Bigg)V_{\lambda}(\hat{x})\geq 0.

(2.14)

Since
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
and
V_{\lambda}(\hat{x})<0
, then at least one of the following holds:

C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{2}(\hat{x})\leq 0,\ or\ C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{1}(x^{o})\leq 0.

(2.15)

However, when
l
sufficiently small, from the fact that
c_{i}(x),i=1,2
are both bounded from below in
\Omega
, we obtain

C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{2}(\hat{x})>0,\ and\ C\int_{l}^{1}\frac{1}{s}ds+c_{1}(x^{o})>0,

(2.16)

which is a contradiction with (
2.15
).
Therefore, (
2.7
) must be true.

This completes the proof.
∎

The following decay at infinity is also indispensable for further use in the main result.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Decay at infinity)
Assume that
\Omega
is an unbounded Lipschitz domain in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. Suppose that
U_{\lambda}\in L_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
V_{\lambda}\in L_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
are Dini continuous functions in
\Omega
and continuous functions in
\bar{\Omega}
such that the pair
(U_{\lambda},V_{\lambda})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x)+c_{1}(x)V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Omega,\\
\ \mathcal{L}_{\Delta}V_{\lambda}(x)+c_{2}(x)U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Omega,\\
\ U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,&in\ \Sigma_{\lambda}\backslash\Omega,\\
\ U_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda})=-U(x),V_{\lambda}(x^{\lambda})=-V(x),&in\ \Sigma_{\lambda},\end{cases}

(2.17)

with

c_{i}(x)\leq 0\,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,x\in\Omega,

and

c_{i}(x)=O\bigg(\frac{1}{|x|^{\kappa_{i}}}\bigg),\,\,\text{for some}\,\,k_{i}>0,\,\,\text{as}\,\,|x|\rightarrow\infty,\,\,\ \text{for}\,\ i=1,2.

(2.18)

Then, there exists a constant
R_{o}>0
such that if

U_{\lambda}(x^{o})=\min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}U_{\lambda}(x)<0,\ or\ V_{\lambda}(x^{o})=\min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}V_{\lambda}(x)<0,

then

|x^{o}|\leq R_{o}.

(2.19)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we suppose that
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
. If
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})\geq 0
, similarly to (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})\leq C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{U_{\lambda}(x^{o})(1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)+1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y^{\lambda}))}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy+\rho_{n}U_{\lambda}(x^{o}).

(2.20)

Suppose that (
2.19
) does not hold. Then, for each fixed
\lambda
, when
|x^{o}|\geq\lambda
, we have
B_{|x^{o}|}(x^{1})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Sigma_{\lambda}
with
x^{1}=(3|x^{o}|+x_{1}^{o},(x^{o})^{\prime})
, and it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\frac{(1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y)+1_{B_{1}(x^{o})}(y^{\lambda}))}{|x^{o}-y^{\lambda}|^{n}}dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{|x^{o}|}(x^{1})}\frac{1}{|x^{o}-y|^{n}}dy\geq\int_{B_{|x^{o}|}(x^{1})}\frac{1}{4^{n}|x^{o}|^{n}}dy=\frac{\omega_{n}}{4^{n}},

(2.21)

where (
2.21
) follows from
|x^{o}-y|\leq|x^{o}-x_{1}|+|x^{o}|=4|x^{o}|
for all
y\in B_{|x^{o}|}(x^{1})
.

Then we have from (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) using the fact that
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o})

(2.22)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}\right)U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o}).

Since
c_{1}(x^{o})\leq 0
from (
2.18
), we have

\left(\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}\right)U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0,

(2.23)

which contradicts (
2.22
).

If
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o})

(2.24)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}\right)U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{1}(x^{o})V_{\lambda}(x^{o}),

and

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}V_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{2}(x^{o})U_{\lambda}(x^{o})

(2.25)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}\right)V_{\lambda}(x^{o})+c_{2}(x^{o})U_{\lambda}(x^{o}).

Adding (
2.24
) and (
2.25
), we get

\displaystyle\left(\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}+c_{2}(x^{o})\right)U_{\lambda}(x^{o})+\left(\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}+c_{1}(x^{o})\right)V_{\lambda}(x^{o})\geq 0.

(2.26)

Since
U_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
and
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})<0
, if (
2.26
) holds, at least one of

\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}+c_{2}(x^{o})\leq 0,\ or\ \frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}+c_{1}(x^{o})\leq 0,

(2.27)

holds. However, if
|x^{o}|
is sufficiently large, it follows from (
2.18
) that

\frac{C_{n}\omega_{n}}{4^{n}}+\rho_{n}+c_{i}(x^{o})>0,\ i=1,2,

which is a contradiction with (
2.27
).

Thus, we get a contradiction in the case when
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})\geq 0
as well as when
V_{\lambda}(x^{o})\leq 0.
Therefore, we conclude that (
2.19
) holds.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.
The proof of the main results

In this section, we will present the proof of the main results.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let

U_{\lambda}(x)=u_{\lambda}(x)-u(x),\quad V_{\lambda}(x)=v_{\lambda}(x)-v(x).

\mathbf{Step\ 1.}
We show that, for
\lambda
sufficiently negative,

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}.

(3.1)

First, notice that, by the definition of
U_{\lambda}
and
V_{\lambda}
, we have

\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}U_{\lambda}(x)=0,\quad\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}V_{\lambda}(x)=0.

(3.2)

Thus, if
U_{\lambda}
or
V_{\lambda}
is negative somewhere in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, then the negative minima of
U_{\lambda}
or
V_{\lambda}
would be attained in the interior of
\Sigma_{\lambda}
.

From (
1.6
), at points where
V_{\lambda}(x)
is negative, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=v_{\lambda}^{p}(x)-v^{p}(x)

(3.3)

\displaystyle\geq pv^{p-1}(x)V_{\lambda}(x),

where (
3.3
) follows from the mean value theorem. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda}(x)+c_{1}(x)V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,

(3.4)

with

c_{1}(x)=-pv^{p-1}(x).

(3.5)

Likewise, when
U_{\lambda}(x)
is negative at some points, we similarly have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}V_{\lambda}(x)+c_{2}(x)U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,

(3.6)

with

c_{2}(x)=-qu^{q-1}(x).

(3.7)

It is easy to verify using (
1.7
), (
3.1
) and (
3.5
) that, for
|x|
sufficiently large,

c_{1}(x)=O\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\beta(p-1)}}\right).

(3.8)

In the same way, we obtain, for
|x|
sufficiently large, that

c_{2}(x)=O\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha(q-1)}}\right).

(3.9)

Moreover, it follows from (
3.4
) and (
3.6
) that
c_{i}(x)\leq 0,i=1,2.
Hence,
c_{i}(x)
satisfy (
2.18
) in Theorem
2.3
.
Therefore, we conclude using Theorem
2.3
that, there exists an
R_{o}>0
(independent of
\lambda
), such that if
\bar{x}
is a negative minimum of
U_{\lambda}
or
V_{\lambda}
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, then

|\bar{x}|\leq R_{o}.

(3.10)

Now, for
\lambda\leq-R_{o}
, we must have

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}.

\mathbf{Step\ 2.}

Step 1 provides a starting point, from which we can now move the plane
T_{\lambda}
to the right as long as (
3.1
) holds to its limiting position.

Let us define the following

\lambda_{0}=\sup\{\lambda<x_{1}^{o}\,\mid U_{\mu}(x)\geq 0,\ V_{\mu}(x)\geq 0,\ \forall x\in\Sigma_{\mu},\ \mu\leq\lambda\}.

In this part, our aim is to show that

\lambda_{0}=x_{1}^{o},

and

U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\equiv 0,\ and\ \ V_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\equiv 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}.

(3.11)

Suppose that
\lambda_{0}<x_{1}^{o}
, we show that the plane
T_{\lambda}
can be moved further right, that is, there exists some
\epsilon>0
, such that for any
\lambda\in(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}+\epsilon)
, we have

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}.

(3.12)

This would be a contradiction with the definition of
\lambda_{0}
. Therefore, we must have

\lambda_{0}=x_{1}^{o}.

(3.13)

Now we prove (
3.11
) by combining the use of the narrow region principle (Theorem
2.2
) and decay at infinity (Theorem
2.3
).

By (
3.10
), the negative minimum of
U_{\lambda}(x)
cannot be attained outside of
B_{R_{o}}(0)
. Now, we will show that it can not be attained inside of
B_{R_{o}}(0)
as well. That is, we will show that for
\lambda
sufficiently close to
\lambda_{0}
,

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R_{o}(0)}.

(3.14)

According to Theorem
2.2
, there is a small
\delta>0
, such that for
\lambda\in[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}+\delta)
, if

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}-\delta},

(3.15)

then

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\backslash\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}-\delta}.

(3.16)

Then what is left is to show (
3.15
), and actually, we only need

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}-\delta}\cap B_{R_{o}(0)}.

(3.17)

In fact, when
\lambda_{0}<x_{1}^{o}
, we have

U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)>0,\ and\ V_{\lambda_{0}}(x)>0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}.

(3.18)

If not, there exists some
\tilde{x}
such that

U_{\lambda_{0}}(\tilde{x})=\min_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}}U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)=0,\,\,\text{or}\,\,\ V_{\lambda_{0}}(\tilde{x})=\min_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}}V_{\lambda_{0}}(x)=0.

Without loss of generality, we suppose that

U_{\lambda_{0}}(\tilde{x})=\min_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}}U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)=0.

For
V_{\lambda_{0}}(\tilde{x})\geq 0
, since
|\tilde{x}-y|>|\tilde{x}-y^{\lambda_{0}}|
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda_{0}}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}}\frac{-U_{\lambda_{0}}(y)}{|\tilde{x}-y|^{n}}dy-C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}}\frac{U_{\lambda_{0}}(y)}{|\tilde{x}-y|^{n}}dy

(3.19)

\displaystyle=C_{n}\int_{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\tilde{x}-y^{\lambda_{0}}|^{n}}-\frac{1}{|\tilde{x}-y|^{n}}\Big)U_{\lambda_{0}}(y)dy

\displaystyle<0.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}U_{\lambda_{0}}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=v_{\lambda_{0}}^{p}(\tilde{x})-v^{p}(\tilde{x})

(3.20)

\displaystyle\geq pv^{p-1}(\tilde{x})V_{\lambda_{0}}(\tilde{x})

\displaystyle\geq 0,

which is a contradiction with (
3.19
).

Therefore, we get (
3.18
). It follows from (
3.18
) and Theorem
2.1
that there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
, such that

U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\geq c_{0},\ and\ V_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\geq c_{0},\quad x\in\overline{\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}-\delta}\cap B_{R_{o}(0)}}.

Since
U_{\lambda}
and
V_{\lambda}
both depend on
\lambda
continuously, there exists
0<\epsilon<\delta
, such that for all
\lambda\in(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}+\epsilon)
, we have

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq c_{0},\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq c_{0},\quad x\in\overline{\Sigma_{\lambda-\delta}\cap B_{R_{o}(0)}}.

(3.21)

Combining (
3.9
), (
3.16
) and (
3.21
), we conclude that for all
\lambda\in(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}+\epsilon)
,

U_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}.

This contradicts the definition of
\lambda_{0}
, Therefore, we must have

\lambda_{0}=x_{1}^{o},\ \ and\ \ U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\geq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}.

Similarly, one can move the plane
T_{\lambda}
from
+\infty
to the left and show that

U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\leq 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\leq 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}}.

Thus, we have shown that

\lambda_{0}=x_{1}^{o},\ \ and\ \ U_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\equiv 0,\ and\ V_{\lambda_{0}}(x)\equiv 0,\quad x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{0}},

completing the proof of
Step 2
.

Since the
x_{1}
-direction can be chosen arbitrarily, we have actually shown that
(u,v)

are radially symmetric about
x^{o}
. The monotonicity of
(u,v)
follows from the process of moving the planes.

This is complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

For the convenience of the reader, we present a concise proof of Theorem
1.2
as it proceeds similar to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The proof will again be based on the moving planes method. For this, purpose,
let us define

W_{i,\lambda}(x):=u_{i,\lambda}(x)-u_{i}(x),

where
u_{i,\lambda}(x)=u_{i}(x^{\lambda})
, and
i=1,2,\ldots,m
.

\mathbf{Step\ 1.}
We show that, for
\lambda
sufficiently negative,

W_{i,\lambda}(x)\geq 0,\quad\forall x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\quad\forall\,i=1,2,\ldots,m.

By the definition of
W_{i,\lambda}(x),\,i=1,2,\ldots,m
, we conclude

\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}W_{i,\lambda}(x)=0.

(3.22)

From (
1.8
), at points where
W_{j+1,\lambda}(x)
is negative, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}W_{j,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=u_{j+1,\lambda}^{p_{j}}(x)-u_{j+1}^{p_{j}}(x)

\displaystyle\geq p_{j}u_{j+1}^{p_{j}-1}(x)W_{j+1,\lambda}(x),

for
j=1,\cdot\cdot\cdot,m-1
, and

\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}W_{m,\lambda}(x)\geq p_{m}u_{1}^{p_{m}-1}(x)W_{1,\lambda}(x).

(3.23)

Therefore, for
j=1,\cdot\cdot\cdot,m-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}W_{j,\lambda}(x)+c_{j}(x)W_{j+1,\lambda}(x)\geq 0,

with

c_{j}(x)=-p_{j}u_{j+1}^{p_{j}-1}(x),

(3.24)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}W_{m,\lambda}(x)+c_{m}(x)W_{1,\lambda}(x)\geq 0,

with

c_{m}(x)=-p_{m}u_{1}^{p_{m}-1}(x).

(3.25)

It is easy to verify that, for
|x|
sufficiently large, we have

c_{j}(x)=O\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha_{j+1}(p_{j}-1)}}\right),\quad\text{for}\,\,\,\,j=1,\ldots,m-1,

and

c_{m}(x)=O\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha_{1}(p_{m}-1)}}\right).

Moreover, it follows from (
3.24
) and (
3.25
) that
c_{i}(x)\leq 0
.
Therefore, we conclude using Theorem
2.3
that, there exists an
R_{o}>0
(independent of
\lambda
), such that, if
\bar{x}
is a negative minimum of
W_{i,\lambda}(x)
in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, then

|\bar{x}|\leq R_{o}.

(3.26)

\mathbf{Step\ 2.}

Step 1 provides a starting point, from which we can now move the plane
T_{\lambda}
to the right as long as (
3
) holds to its limiting position.

Let us define the following

\lambda^{\prime}_{0}=\sup\{\lambda<x_{1}^{o}\,\mid W_{i,\mu}(x)\geq 0,\,\text{for}\,\,\,i=1,\ldots,m,\ \forall x\in\Sigma_{\mu},\ \mu\leq\lambda\}.

Similar to the proof of Step 2 in Theorem
1.1
, we can get

\lambda^{\prime}_{0}=x_{1}^{o},

and

W_{i,\lambda^{\prime}_{0}}(x)\equiv 0,\ x\in\Sigma_{\lambda^{\prime}_{0}},

(3.27)

completing the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
∎
