Landscape modification meets spin systems: from torpid to rapid mixing, tunneling and annealing in the low-temperature regime
Michael C.H. Choi

Abstract
. Given a target Gibbs distribution π β 0 ∝ e − β ​ ℋ \pi^{0}_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}} to sample from in the low-temperature regime on Σ N := { − 1 , + 1 } N \Sigma_{N}:=\{-1,+1\}^{N} , in this paper we propose and analyze Metropolis dynamics that instead target an alternative distribution π α , c , 1 / β f ∝ e − ℋ α , c , 1 / β f \pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}} , where ℋ α , c , 1 / β f \mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta} is a transformed Hamiltonian whose landscape is suitably modified and controlled by the parameters f , α , c f,\alpha,c and β \beta and shares the same set of stationary points as ℋ \mathcal{H} . With appropriate tuning of these parameters, the major advantage of the proposed Metropolis dynamics on the modified landscape is that it enjoys an 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) critical height while its stationary distribution π α , c , 1 / β f \pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta} maintains close proximity with the original target π β 0 \pi^{0}_{\beta} in the low-temperature. We prove rapid mixing and tunneling on the modified landscape with polynomial dependence on the system size N N and the inverse temperature β \beta , while the original Metropolis dynamics mixes torpidly with exponential dependence on the critical height and β \beta . In the setting of simulated annealing, we prove its long-time convergence under a power-law cooling schedule that is faster than the typical logarithmic cooling in the classical setup. We illustrate our results on a host of models including the Ising model on various deterministic and random graphs as well as Derrida’s Random Energy Model. In these applications, (with high probability) the original Metropolis dynamics mixes torpidly while the proposed dynamics on the modified landscape mixes rapidly with polynomial dependence on both β \beta and N N and find the approximate ground state provably in 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 4 ) \mathcal{O}(N^{4}) time. This paper highlights a novel use of the geometry and structure of the landscape, in particular the global minimum value of ℋ \mathcal{H} or its estimate, to the design of accelerated samplers or optimizers. AMS 2010 subject classifications : 60J27, 60J28, 65C40 Keywords : Metropolis-Hastings; simulated annealing; spectral gap; Ising model; random energy model; metastability; landscape modification; energy transformation



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider spin systems on the discrete cube
\Sigma_{N}:=\{-1,+1\}^{N}
with a given Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. The primary objective is to approximately sample from the Gibbs distribution
\pi^{0}_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}}
when the temperature
T=1/\beta
is small using the classical Metropolis dynamics and its variants. The problem of sampling in the low-temperature regime appears in a variety of application domains ranging from molecular dynamics
Lelièvre et al. 2010
, Bayesian inference
Roberts and Rosenthal 2004
;
Robert and Casella 2004
to computational physics
Del Moral 2013
. In addition, this setup is also utilized in stochastic optimization
Catoni 2004
as the Gibbs distribution
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
in the low-temperature regime concentrates around the set of global minima of
\mathcal{H}
. We are interested in characterizing the time complexity, or the so-called mixing time of the classical Metropolis dynamics, as it gives practical guidance in the time it takes to simulate the chain until one approximately samples from
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
or obtains an approximate minimizer of
\mathcal{H}
. Throughout this paper, we say that a mixing or tunneling time parameter
t
is a
torpid
parameter if
t
is at least exponential in
N^{\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. On the other hand, we say that
t
is a
rapid
parameter if
t
is at most polynomial in the system size
N
. Note that the notion of torpid versus rapid mixing has been studied intensively in the literature in a wide range of sophisticated statistical physics models (such as the Potts model, the Blume-Emery-Griffiths model or the Generalized Random Energy Model) and advanced Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms (such as parallel tempering or the swapping algorithm, simulated tempering, Swendsen-Wang dynamics and the equi-energy sampler), see for example
Ebbers et al. 2014
;
Madras and Zheng 2003
;
Woodard et al. 2009a
;
Woodard et al. 2009b
;
Borgs et al. 1999
;
Ebbers and Löwe 2009
;
Gore and Jerrum 1999
;
Bhatnagar and Randall 2016
and the references therein.

While the classical Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is known to mix efficiently on unimodal target distribution in the Euclidean space
Johndrow and Smith 2018
, one of the culprits that leads to torpid mixing of the Metropolis dynamics on a possibly high-dimensional and non-convex landscape specified by the target Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is that its relaxation time grows at least in the order of
e^{\beta m}
in the low-temperature regime
Holley and Stroock 1988
, where
\beta
is the inverse temperature and
m
is the so-called critical or saddle height of the landscape (see (
2.4
) below for the definition of
m
). In many spin models of interest,
m
grows at least in the order of the system size
N
. Coupled with an exponential dependence on
\beta
, this consequently leads to torpid total variation mixing and relaxation time in the low-temperature setting. For example, in the ferromagnetic Ising model defined on an expander graph,
m
grows at least in the order of
N

Dommers et al. 2017
. As another example, with high probability
m
is shown to grow at least in the order of
N
in Derrida’s Random Energy Model in Section
5.4
.

To possibly overcome this bottleneck of exponential dependence on both
\beta
and
m
in these mixing and tunneling time parameters, one natural approach is to transform the landscape suitably to hopefully reduce the magnitude of
m
. Originated from the study of overdamped Langevin diffusion with state-dependent noise
Fang et al. 1997
, it is shown in
Choi 2021a
that the effect of state-dependent noise is de-facto equivalent to altering the target Hamiltonian from
\mathcal{H}
to

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma):=\int^{\mathcal{H}(\sigma)}_{\mathcal{H}_{*}}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f((u-c)_{+})+1/\beta}\,du,

where
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}
,
\mathcal{H}_{*}:=\min\mathcal{H}
and for any real number
a
, we use
a_{+}:=\max\{a,0\}
to denote the non-negative part. We assume that the parameters
f,\alpha,c
are chosen according to Assumption
2.1
below. With appropriate tuning of these parameters, one significant advantage of targeting
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
is that the relaxation time of the Metropolis dynamics on the modified landscape is at most of the order of
N^{3}
(see Theorem
3.1
item (
2
) below) for any
\mathcal{H}
on
\Sigma_{N}
that consequently gives rise to rapid mixing. The crux to this proof is that upon choosing
f(x)=f_{2}(x):=x^{2}
,
\alpha=\beta
, the modified Hamiltonian is given by

\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)=\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)\wedge c-\mathcal{H}_{*})+\arctan(\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-c)_{+}),

where we write
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. If the saddle height is attained at
\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}(\eta)
and
c
is chosen to be close enough to
\mathcal{H}_{*}
, the linear term in
\beta
is cancelled and only the
\arctan
term remains which can be upper bounded by
\pi/2
. The rest of the work is to ensure that the prefactor in front of the exponential term in the relaxation time upper bound is at most polynomial in both
N
and
\beta
.

While there is perhaps the benefit of reduced critical height by running algorithms on the modified landscape, we are nonetheless introducing bias to the dynamics as we are afterall targeting
\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
instead of the original Gibbs distribution
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
in the Metropolis dynamics. However, when the temperature is low enough, the total variation distance between
\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
and
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
is small as both distributions can be shown to concentrate on the set of global minima of
\mathcal{H}
, and hence
\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
is a reasonably accurate proxy or surrogate of
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
.

1.1.
Notations

In this subsection, we recall a few standard asymptotic notations that are used throughout the paper. Suppose that we have two real-valued functions
g,h
. We write
g(N)=\mathcal{O}(h(N))
if there exists a positive constant
M
such that for all large enough
N
we have
|g(N)|\leqslant Mh(N)
. We also write
g(N)=\Omega(h(N))
if there exists a positive constant
M
such that for all large enough
N
we have
|g(N)|\geqslant Mh(N)
. If both
g(N)=\mathcal{O}(h(N))
and
g(N)=\Omega(h(N))
hold, then we write
g(N)=\Theta(h(N))
. Finally, we write
g(N)\overset{N\to\infty}{\sim}h(N)

.

2.
Spin systems with landscape modification

In this paper, the primary objective is to design accelerated samplers (i.e. provably rapidly mixing) to approximately sample from the Gibbs distribution
\pi^{0}_{\beta}\propto e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}}
in the low-temperature regime when the temperature
T=1/\beta
is small. While there are various advanced stochastic sampling algorithms to perform such task (as mentioned in the Introduction in Section
1
), we are particularly interested in the classical Metropolis dynamics. Consider a simple random walk proposal with transition matrix
P^{SRW}=(P^{SRW}(\eta,\sigma))_{\eta,\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}
given by

\displaystyle P^{SRW}(\eta,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{N}\mathds{1}_{\{\textrm{there exists }i\textrm{ such that }\eta(i)=-\sigma(i)\textrm{ and }\eta(j)=\sigma(j)\textrm{ for all }j\neq i\}}.

The stationary distribution of
P^{SRW}
is the uniform distribution on
\Sigma_{N}
given by
\mu^{SRW}(\sigma):=1/2^{N}
. For the Metropolis dynamics, we instead target the Gibbs distribution with a landscape-modified Hamiltonian function given by, for any spin configuration
\sigma=(\sigma(i))_{i=1}^{N}\in\Sigma_{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)=\int^{\mathcal{H}(\sigma)}_{\mathcal{H}_{*}}\dfrac{1}{\alpha f((u-c)_{+})+1/\beta}\,du,

where we recall from the Introduction in Section
1
that
\mathcal{H}_{*}=\min\mathcal{H}
and
a_{+}=\max\{a,0\}
is the non-negative part of a real number
a
. We also write
\mathcal{H}_{max}:=\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)
throughout this paper. The function
f
describes the effect of the transformation above the threshold parameter
c
, while the parameter
\alpha
controls the magnitude of the transformation. These parameters are chosen to satisfy the following assumption:

Assumption 2.1
.

f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is non-negative, non-decreasing and satisfies
f(0)=0
.
c
is chosen such that
\mathcal{H}_{max}\geqslant c\geqslant\mathcal{H}_{*}
.
\alpha
is assumed to be non-negative, i.e.
\alpha\geqslant 0
.

In our main results in Section
3
below we will primarily be interested in
f(x)=f_{2}(x):=x^{2}
, the quadratic function, and some other common choices of
f
include the linear function
f(x)=x
and the square root function
f(x)=\sqrt{x}
. We note that
\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
can be written as

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)=\beta(c\wedge\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}_{*})+\sqrt{\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}}\arctan(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-c)_{+}).

For the explicit expressions of
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,\beta,1/\beta}
for other choices of
f
, we refer readers to the calculation in
Choi 2021a
. We will explain the benefits of
\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}
over other similar transformations or modifications in Section
3
.

With these notations in mind, the continuized Metropolis dynamics is a Markov chain that we denote by
X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}=(X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
with infinitesimal generator
L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}=(L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\eta,\sigma))_{\eta,\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}
defined to be

(2.2)

\displaystyle L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\eta,\sigma):=\begin{cases}P^{SRW}(\eta,\sigma)e^{-(\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma_{1}))_{+}},&\mbox{if }\eta\neq\sigma;\\
-\sum_{\xi:\xi\neq\eta}L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\eta,\xi),&\mbox{if }\eta=\sigma,\end{cases}

and stationary distribution given by a generalized Gibbs distribution:

\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)=\dfrac{e^{-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)}\mu^{SRW}(\sigma)}{\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}e^{-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)}\mu^{SRW}(\sigma)}=:\dfrac{e^{-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)}}{Z^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}}\propto e^{-\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)}.

Note that in the special case when we take
f\equiv 0
or
\alpha\equiv 0
, we recover the classical Gibbs distribution at inverse temperature
\beta
and Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
since
\pi^{0}_{\beta}=\pi^{0}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}=\pi^{f}_{0,c,1/\beta}
. As such without overburdening our notations we write
X^{0}_{\beta}\equiv X^{0}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
,
L^{0}_{\beta}\equiv L^{0}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
and
Z^{0}_{\beta}\equiv Z^{0}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
.

Before we introduce the list of mixing and tunneling time parameters of both
X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
and
X^{0}_{\beta}
, we shall fix and recall some classical notions in Markov chain mixing. For any two probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\Sigma_{N}
, the total variation distance is given by
\left\lVert\mu-\nu\right\rVert_{TV}:=1/2\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}|\mu(\sigma)-\nu(\sigma)|
. For
A\subset\Sigma_{N}
, we also write
\tau_{A}^{f}:=\inf\left\{t\geqslant 0;~X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(t)\in A\right\}
to be the first hitting time of
A
by
X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
, and we denote the hitting time to a single state by
\tau^{f}_{\sigma}=\tau^{f}_{\{\sigma\}}
. The usual convention of
\inf\emptyset=\infty
applies. We say that
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}
is a local minimum of
\mathcal{H}
if for all
i\in\llbracket N\rrbracket:=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
, we have
\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{(i)})\geqslant\mathcal{H}(\sigma)
, where
\sigma^{(i)}_{j}=\sigma_{j}
for all
j\neq i
and
\sigma^{(i)}_{i}=-\sigma_{i}
. Intuitively,
\sigma
is a local minimum if flipping the sign of any of the
N
coordinates does not decrease the value of the Hamiltonian. We also endow the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta})
with the usual inner product
\langle u,v\rangle_{\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}u(\sigma)v(\sigma)\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)
, where
u,v\in\ell^{2}(\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta})
are real-valued functions defined on
\Sigma_{N}
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, we introduce the following list of parameters of interest. We emphasize that the dependency on
\alpha,c,\beta
maybe suppressed in the notations when there is no ambiguity.

•

(Total variation mixing time to
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
by
X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
(resp.
X^{0}_{\beta}
) on the modified (resp. original) landscape)

\displaystyle t^{f}_{mix}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\inf\bigg\{t\geqslant 0;~\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\left\lVert(P^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta})^{t}(\sigma,\cdot)-\pi^{0}_{\beta}\right\rVert_{TV}\leqslant\varepsilon\bigg\}.

\displaystyle t^{0}_{mix}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\inf\bigg\{t\geqslant 0;~\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\left\lVert(P^{0}_{\beta})^{t}(\sigma,\cdot)-\pi^{0}_{\beta}\right\rVert_{TV}\leqslant\varepsilon\bigg\}.

Note that we are
not
measuring
\left\lVert(P^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta})^{t}(\sigma,\cdot)-\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}\right\rVert_{TV}
in the definition of

. By convention in the literature we write
t^{f}_{mix}=t^{f}_{mix}(1/4)
and
t^{0}_{mix}=t^{0}_{mix}(1/4)
.

•

(Relaxation time of
X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
(resp.
X^{0}_{\beta}
) on the modified (resp. original) landscape)
First, we recall that the second smallest eigenvalue of
-L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
and
-L^{0}_{\beta}
are defined respectively to be

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(-L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}):

\displaystyle=\inf_{g;~\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(g)=0}\dfrac{\langle-L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}g,g\rangle_{\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}}}{\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(-L^{0}_{\beta}):

\displaystyle=\inf_{g;~\pi^{0}_{\beta}(g)=0}\dfrac{\langle-L^{0}_{\beta}g,g\rangle_{\pi^{0}_{\beta}}}{\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi^{0}_{\beta}}}.

Their respective relaxation times are then

\displaystyle t^{f}_{rel}:

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{2}(-L^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta})},\quad t^{0}_{rel}:=\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{2}(-L^{0}_{\beta})}.

•

(Largest mean tunneling time from a local minimum to a global minimum)
Let
\mathcal{LM}:=\{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N};~\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{(i)})\geqslant\mathcal{H}(\sigma)\quad\mathrm{for}\,\,i=1,\ldots,N\}
be the set of local minima of
\mathcal{H}
and suppose that
\sigma_{*}\in\mathcal{GM}:=\{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N};~\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\mathcal{H}_{*}\}
is a global minimum. We would be interested in estimating both

\displaystyle\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{LM}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(\tau^{f}_{\sigma_{*}}),\quad\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{LM}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(\tau^{0}_{\sigma_{*}}).

•

(First time reaching
\mathcal{H}_{*}
with high probability) We shall be comparing

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{f}(\varepsilon)=\inf\bigg\{t\geqslant 0;~\inf_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{H}(X^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(t))=\mathcal{H}_{*})

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{0}(\varepsilon)=\inf\bigg\{t\geqslant 0;~\inf_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{H}(X^{0}_{\beta}(t))=\mathcal{H}_{*})

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\bigg\}.

We proceed to introduce two critical heights:
m^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
and
m
. These constants play an important role in understanding our main results in Section
3
, and in particular in bounding the spectral gap of the dynamics. A path from
\eta
to
\sigma
is any sequence of spin configurations starting from
\sigma_{1}=\eta,\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{n}=\sigma
such that
P^{SRW}(\sigma_{i-1},\sigma_{i})>0
for
i=2,\ldots,n
. For any
\eta\neq\sigma
, such path exists as the proposal chain
P^{SRW}
is irreducible. We write
\Gamma^{\eta,\sigma}
to be the set of paths from
\eta
to
\sigma
, and elements of
\Gamma^{\eta,\sigma}
are denoted by
\gamma=(\gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. By considering
\mathcal{H}(\sigma)
as the elevation at
\sigma
, the highest elevation along a path
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\eta,\sigma}
is defined as

\mathrm{Elev}(\gamma):=\max\{\mathcal{H}(\gamma_{i});~\gamma_{i}\in\gamma\},

and the least highest elevation from
\eta
to
\sigma
is

(2.3)

\displaystyle H(\eta,\sigma):=\min\{\mathrm{Elev}(\gamma);~\gamma\in\Gamma^{\eta,\sigma}\}.

The classical critical height
m
is then defined to be

(2.4)

\displaystyle m=m(P^{SRW},\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle:=\max_{\eta,\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\{H(\eta,\sigma)-\mathcal{H}(\eta)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma)\}+\mathcal{H}_{*}.

We also consider
\Lambda^{\eta,\sigma}
to be the set of paths from
\eta
to
\sigma
by flipping the coordinates in
\{i;~\eta_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}\}
one at a time and from left to right, and the element of
\Lambda^{\eta,\sigma}
is denoted by
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. Note that

3.
Main results

In this section, we state the main results in Theorem

and Theorem
3.2
. In Theorem
3.1
, we consider the setting of fixed but small enough temperature. Thus the results are particularly useful in the context of approximately sampling from the Gibbs distribution
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
in the low-temperature or stochastic optimization of the Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
. On the other hand in Theorem

we investigate the setting of simulated annealing which the temperature cools down according to an annealing schedule. With suitable choices of the landscape-modification parameters such as

and

with

\Sigma_{N}
that satisfy certain regularity conditions. These conditions are readily checked in all models to be presented in Section
5
.

The central intuition to the proofs of these two main results is that we choose
f=f_{2}
,
\alpha=\beta
,
c\in(\mathcal{H}_{*},\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{LM}\cap\mathcal{GM}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)]
while
\mathcal{H}
is assumed to have a unique global minimum. In this setup, the modified Hamiltonian is then

\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)=\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)\wedge c-\mathcal{H}_{*})+\arctan(\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-c)_{+}),

and since

Remark 3.1
.

In the following remarks, we assume the same setting as in Theorem
3.1
. The aim of this remark is to show other transformations of
\mathcal{H}
may not enjoy the same benefits as the proposed transformation
\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}
.

(1)

(Slower or lack of concentration of the associated Gibbs distribution as
\beta\to\infty
) Suppose we consider transforming the target Hamiltonian function to

\mathcal{H}\mapsto T\mathcal{H}

or

\mathcal{H}\mapsto T\ln(\beta)\mathcal{H}.

The benefit of these transformations is to remove the exponential dependency on
\beta
in the relaxation time: according to
Holley and Stroock 1988
the relaxation time scales at least in the order of
\Omega(e^{m})
and
\Omega(e^{(\ln\beta)m})
respectively. The disadvantages are twofold. First, if
m=\Omega(N)
, then the chain still mixes torpidly with exponential dependency on
N
. Second, the Gibbs distributions associated with these transformations concentrate at a slower rate on the set of global minima of
\mathcal{H}
than
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
as
\beta\to\infty
.
Writing
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
to be respectively the Gibbs distribution with Hamiltonian
T\mathcal{H}
and
T(\ln\beta)\mathcal{H}
, then, for fixed

,

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Sigma_{N}\backslash\{\sigma_{*}\})

\displaystyle\geqslant\dfrac{(2^{N}-1)e^{-(\mathcal{H}_{max}-\mathcal{H}_{*})}}{1+(2^{N}-1)e^{-\Delta}}=\Omega(1),

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(\Sigma_{N}\backslash\{\sigma_{*}\})

\displaystyle\geqslant\dfrac{(2^{N}-1)e^{-(\ln\beta)(\mathcal{H}_{max}-\mathcal{H}_{*})}}{1+(2^{N}-1)e^{-(\ln\beta)\Delta}}=\Omega(\beta^{\Delta-(\mathcal{H}_{max}-\mathcal{H}_{*})}),

\displaystyle\pi^{0}_{\beta}(\Sigma_{N}\backslash\{\sigma_{*}\})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\beta\Delta}\right),

where the right hand side of the first inequality above does not depend on
\beta
, which does not converge to

as

. As such there are non-negligible biases if we approximately sample from

or
\mu_{2}

\pi^{0}_{\beta}
. On the other hand, the proposed target

can be arbitrarily close to
\pi^{0}_{\beta}
provided that
\beta
is large enough.

(2)

(Exponential dependency on
\beta
) Suppose we consider changing the target Hamiltonian function to

\mathcal{H}\mapsto\dfrac{\mathcal{H}}{\mathcal{H}_{max}-\mathcal{H}_{*}},

or

\mathcal{H}\mapsto\arctan(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{*}).

The advantage of these transformations is a reduced critical height: the new critical height associated with these transformations is denoted by

, which is independent of the system size

. On the other hand, there are two possible drawbacks of considering these transformations. First, in practice it is perhaps difficult to estimate the oscillation of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.
\mathcal{H}_{max}-\mathcal{H}_{*}
. Second, in the low-temperature regime as
\beta\to\infty
, according to
Holley and Stroock 1988
the relaxation time scales at least in the order of

which exhibits an exponential dependency on
\beta
. However, in the proposed landscape modification the relaxation time can be bounded above independently of
\beta
as in Theorem
3.1
item (
2
).

(3)

(Landscape modification enjoys the "best of both worlds") The proposed transformation
\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}
in Theorem

enjoys the advantages while addressing the drawbacks mentioned in both item

and
2

. Precisely, recall that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)=\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)\wedge c-\mathcal{H}_{*})+\arctan(\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-c)_{+}).

On the part of the landscape at or below the threshold parameter

\mathcal{H}^{f_{2}}_{\beta,c,1/\beta}
effectively exploits and concentrates on the original landscape, while on the part of the landscape above

\beta
and the system size

, thus giving rise to rapid mixing and tunneling.

(4)

(On other choices of
f
such as
f_{1}(x)=x
)
Suppose we instead consider

\mathcal{H}^{f_{1}}_{1,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)=\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)\wedge c-\mathcal{H}_{*})+\ln(1+\beta(\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-c)_{+}).

The advantage of

\arctan
transformation, while for

which can still be large depending on
\mathcal{H}
.

(5)

(On tuning of the threshold parameter

) Given a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
which we know about its

where
\delta
is small enough. In this way the landscape is appropriately modified with a modified critical height that is independent of

. For details we refer interested readers to Section

where we consider a comprehensive suite of Ising model on graphs and the random energy model.

In our second main result, we consider the setting of simulated annealing, that is, the temperature decreases according to a cooling schedule, instead of the fixed temperature case as in Theorem
3.1
. The crucial observation is similar to that of Theorem
3.1
: recall that the modified critical height can be bounded above by

m^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}\leqslant\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma)-\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{LM}\cap\mathcal{GM}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}^{f}_{\alpha,c,1/\beta}(\sigma),

the linear term in the difference of (

gets canceled out, so that the relaxation time can be bounded above independently of the inverse temperature. This observation is crucial as this allows us to show that we can operate a power-law cooling schedule, namely

With a slight abuse of notations, we write

and

to be the inverse temperature schedule and temperature schedule respectively. We also let

to be the annealing chain with transition semigroup defined to be, for any

,

\left(P^{f_{2}}_{\bm{\beta},c,\mathbf{T}}\right)^{t}(\sigma,\eta):=\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}L^{f_{2}}_{\beta_{s},c,1/\beta_{s}}\,ds\bigg\}(\sigma,\eta),

where we recall the instantaneous infinitesimal generator

at time

is first introduced in (

As a result of the power-law cooling schedule, the simulated annealing chain can approximately reach
\mathcal{H}_{*}
in polynomial time in

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{H}
has a unique global minimum, and we choose
f(x)=f_{2}(x)=x^{2}
,
\alpha_{t}=\beta_{t}=t^{a}
with
a\in(0,1)
and
c\in(\mathcal{H}_{*},\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{LM}\cap\mathcal{GM}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)]
. For any
\delta
small enough such that
\delta<c-\mathcal{H}_{*}
, we then have, for
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(\mathcal{H}\left(X^{f_{2}}_{\bm{\beta},c,\mathbf{T}}(t)\right)\geqslant\mathcal{H}_{*}+\delta\right)\leqslant\varepsilon

\displaystyle t_{0}=t_{0}(\mathcal{H},N,a,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\tau_{2}=\tau_{2}(\mathcal{H},N,a,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\tau_{3}=\tau_{3}(\mathcal{H},N,a,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{N^{3}e^{\pi/2}}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{3}{\varepsilon^{2}}e^{4t_{0}}t_{0}^{a}(\mathcal{H}_{max}-\mathcal{H}_{*})\right).

is at most polynomial in
N
and
\varepsilon=\delta=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{N})
then the simulated annealing chain takes at most polynomial time in
N
to reach an approximate global minimum with high probability.

Remark 3.2
.

In the following remarks, we assume the same setting as in Theorem
3.2
.

(1)

The assumption that
\mathcal{H}_{max}-\mathcal{H}_{*}
is at most polynomial in

(2)

(Improving the convergence rate of the instantaneous Gibbs distribution from

In classical simulated annealing
Miclo 1992
or kinetic simulated annealing

on

5.

In this section, we specialize our model-independent results in Theorem
3.1
into four different models.

In the first example, we investigate the Ising model on the complete graph

. We demonstrate rapid mixing in the low-temperature regime for the landscape-modified sampler while the critical height grows at least in

on the original landscape that consequently leads to torpid mixing using the original Metropolis dynamics. This also serves as our warm-up example before the subsequent models with random energy landscape.

Next, we look into three models with random landscape and geometry, namely the Ising model on the random

In these model-dependent results, we incorporate information or estimates of the original critical height

3.1
.

5.4.
Derrida’s Random Energy Model (REM)

In this subsection, we consider Derrida’s Random Energy Model (REM). Let
(X_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}
be a family of i.i.d. standard normal random variables. At a spin configuration
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}
, the value of the random Hamiltonian function at
\sigma
is

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=-\sqrt{N}X_{\sigma}.

It is known, see for instance
Bovier and Kurkova 2004
, that the maximum of
X_{\sigma}
over
\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}
, when normalized by
\sqrt{N}
, converges in probability to
\sqrt{2\ln 2}
, that is, for any
\epsilon>0
we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}X_{\sigma}-\sqrt{2\ln 2}\right|>\epsilon\right)=0.

We first present two auxiliary lemmata. In the first lemma below, we offer practical guidance on the tuning of the threshold parameter
c
in landscape modification in Derrida’s REM that leverages on (
5.3
):

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider Derrida’s REM with Hamiltonian given by (
5.2
). For any deterministic sequence
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)>-N\sqrt{2\ln 2}-\epsilon_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=1,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)<-N\sqrt{2\ln 2}+\ln N\right)

\displaystyle=1,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{N};~\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}}|\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{\prime})|\geqslant N^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle=1.

In particular, we take
\epsilon_{N}\equiv 0
and set

c=-N\sqrt{2\ln 2}+\frac{N^{1/4}}{4}

such that w.h.p. we have

0<c-\mathcal{H}_{*}\leqslant\left(\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{LM}\cap\mathcal{GM}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}_{*}\right)\wedge\Delta\wedge m/4,

where we recall
m
is the original critical height of
X^{0}_{\beta}
in Derrida’s REM, and
\Delta
is introduced in Theorem
3.1
.

The proof of Lemma
5.3
is deferred to Section
5.4.1
. In the second auxiliary lemma, we extract bounds on the original critical height
m
in Derrida’s REM from the relaxation time estimates in
(
Fontes et al. 1998
, Theorem
1
)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Consider Derrida’s REM with Hamiltonian given by (
5.2
). Then there exist universal constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}<\infty
so that, w.h.p.,

N\sqrt{2\ln 2}-C_{1}\sqrt{N\ln N}\leqslant m\leqslant N\sqrt{2\ln 2}+C_{2}\sqrt{N\ln N}+C_{3}\ln N.

The proof of Lemma
5.4
is postponed to Section
5.4.2
. With the above lemma that gives an estimate of
m
, we sharpen the bounds presented in Theorem
3.1
to arrive at the following corollary:

Corollary 5.4
.

Consider Derrida’s REM with Hamiltonian given by (
5.2
). Suppose that the parameter
c
is chosen in the same manner as Lemma
5.3
, where we set
\delta=-N\sqrt{2\ln 2}+\frac{N^{1/4}}{4}-\mathcal{H}_{*}
. Note that w.h.p.
\delta=\Omega(N^{1/4}-\ln N)

3.1

\varepsilon=e^{-N}
and for low enough temperature such that

\beta\geqslant\dfrac{3N}{\delta},

then for large enough
N

(1)

t^{0}_{rel}=\Omega\left(e^{\beta\left(N\sqrt{2\ln 2}-C_{1}\sqrt{N\ln N}\right)-(\ln 4)N}\right),

(2)

(From torpid to rapid total variation mixing)

t^{0}_{mix}(e^{-N})=\Omega\left(e^{\beta\left(N\sqrt{2\ln 2}-C_{1}\sqrt{N\ln N}\right)-(\ln 4)N}N\right),

(3)

\beta=\dfrac{1}{\delta}\Theta(N),

then

\mathcal{T}^{f_{2}}(e^{-N})=\mathcal{O}(N^{4}).

5.4.1.
Proof of Lemma
5.3

First, we write
\Phi
(resp.
\phi
) to be the cumulative distribution function (resp. probability density function) of standard normal. For any sequence
(x_{N})
with
x_{N}\to\infty
, we recall the following asymptotics
(
Leadbetter et al. 1983
, equation
(1.5.4)
)
:

\mathbb{P}(X_{\sigma}>x_{N})=1-\Phi(x_{N})\overset{N\to\infty}{\sim}\dfrac{\phi(x_{N})}{x_{N}}.

Now, we take
x_{N}=\sqrt{2N\ln 2}+\dfrac{\epsilon_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}
and calculate that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\phi(x_{N})}{x_{N}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2N\ln 2}+\dfrac{\epsilon_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\bigg\{-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{2N\ln 2}+\dfrac{\epsilon_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=2^{-N}\exp\bigg\{-\sqrt{2\ln 2}\epsilon_{N}+\dfrac{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}{2N}-\ln\left(\sqrt{2N\ln 2}+\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\ln\sqrt{2\pi}\bigg\}.

We proceed to prove (
5.4
). Consider

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)>-\sqrt{N}x_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}X_{\sigma}<x_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-(1-\mathbb{P}(X_{\sigma}>x_{N})))^{2^{N}}

\displaystyle\overset{N\to\infty}{\sim}\left(1-2^{-N}\exp\bigg\{-\sqrt{2\ln 2}\epsilon_{N}+\dfrac{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}{2N}-\ln\left(\sqrt{2N\ln 2}+\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\ln\sqrt{2\pi}\bigg\}\right)^{2^{N}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\exp\bigg\{-\exp\bigg\{-\sqrt{2\ln 2}\epsilon_{N}+\dfrac{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}{2N}-\ln\left(\sqrt{2N\ln 2}+\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\ln\sqrt{2\pi}\bigg\}\bigg\},

where the last equality utilizes
(
Königsberger 2004
, Chapter 8 Lemma 1)
. Note that up to this point we have not invoked any specific properties of
\epsilon_{N}
. If
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, then the limit above equals to
1
.

Next, we prove (
5.5
). By taking
\epsilon_{N}=-\ln N
in the proof of (
5.4
) above, we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)>-N\sqrt{2\ln 2}+\ln N\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{N\to\infty}\exp\bigg\{-\exp\bigg\{\sqrt{2\ln 2}(\ln N)+\dfrac{(\ln N)^{2}}{2N}-\ln\left(\sqrt{2N\ln 2}+\frac{-\ln N}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\ln\sqrt{2\pi}\bigg\}\bigg\}=0.

We proceed to prove (
5.6
). Writing
Y
to be a normal random variable with mean
0
and variance
2
, we then have, for any
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{\prime})|>N^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(|Y|>\dfrac{1}{N^{1/4}}\right)

\displaystyle=2-2\Phi\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}N^{1/4}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{N\to\infty}{\sim}1.

Now, for any
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
and
\eta\neq\eta^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{\prime})|>N^{1/4},|\mathcal{H}(\eta)-\mathcal{H}(\eta^{\prime})|>N^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{\prime})|>N^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{H}(\eta)-\mathcal{H}(\eta^{\prime})|>N^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{\prime})|>N^{1/4}\,\mathrm{or}\,|\mathcal{H}(\eta)-\mathcal{H}(\eta^{\prime})|>N^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{N\to\infty}{\sim}1.

Iterating the above process for
2^{N}(2^{N}-1)/2
times give the desired result

\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{N};~\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}}|\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma^{\prime})|>N^{1/4}\right)\overset{N\to\infty}{\sim}1.

In particular, using (
5.5
) we see that for large enough
N
w.h.p. we obtain

\mathcal{H}_{*}<-\sqrt{2\ln 2}N+\ln N<c,

and hence
c-\mathcal{H}_{*}>0
. On the other hand, it is straightforward from the definition of (
5.6
) that

c-\mathcal{H}_{*}\leqslant\left(\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{LM}\cap\mathcal{GM}^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}_{*}\right)\wedge\Delta\wedge m/4.

5.4.2.
Proof of Lemma
5.4

As in
(
Holley and Stroock 1988
, Theorem
2.1
)
, for low enough temperature we have

t^{0}_{rel}=\Theta(e^{\beta m}).

The desired result follows by combining the above result with
(
Fontes et al. 1998
, Theorem
1
)
, that is, w.h.p. for any inverse temperature
\beta>0
we have

\beta\left(N\sqrt{2\ln 2}-C_{1}\sqrt{N\ln N}\right)\leqslant\ln t^{0}_{rel}\leqslant\beta\left(N\sqrt{2\ln 2}+C_{2}\sqrt{N\ln N}+C_{3}\ln N\right).
