On the radiation field of a linearly accelerated charged particle in Born-Infeld theory
Miguel L. Peñafiel, Santiago Esteban Perez Bergliaffa

Abstract
The electric potential and the electromagnetic field for a linearly accelerated Born-Infeld charged particle are obtained in an inertial frame by a method that can, in principle, be applied to any electromagnetic theory. The method is based on (i) the fact that the metric near the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole is equivalent to that of Rindler spacetime, and (ii) a theorem that guarantees that the electrostatic potential for a given nonlinear theory in a static, spherically symmetric spacetime is entirely specified by the Maxwellian electrostatic potential in the same background. Using analytical and numerical methods, the features of the radiation field and the radiation-reaction for such an accelerated particle are discussed in detail.



I
Introduction

The study of the radiation emitted by an accelerated charged particle is usually performed in Maxwell Electrodynamics (ME) through the electromagnetic potential for the particle, known as the Liénard-Wiechert (LW) potential, the derivation of which relies on the well-known Green’s function method. While
the properties of the radiation field are obtained in a rather straightforward way, the description of the action of the field on the particle within ME has been theoretically challenging.
Divergences arise, along with runaway solutions
Dirac 1938
. Although most of these theoretical inconsistencies were overcome with the advent of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED), the study of such classical problems remains of interest today
Gralla et al. 2009
;
Kiessling 2019
;
Ruhlandt et al. 2020
.

The situation changes drastically when
the same problem is considered in nonlinear electrodynamics (NLED). In particular, the Green’s function method is no longer applicable

. The radiation field has not been studied, to our knowledge, in NLED with the exception of ModMax theory
Lechner et al. 2022
.
As we shall see, the well-known equivalence between the geometry
near the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole and that of Rindler’s spacetime paves the way to the study of such problems

II
Nonlinear electrodynamics for point particles

Theories of nonlinear electrodynamics (NLED) are
generalizations of Maxwell’s theory that aim at solving
theoretical inconsistencies of the latter,
as well as describing fundamental interactions at the effective level
Plebaǹski 1970
;
Boillat 1970
;
de Oliveira Costa and Bergliaffa 2009
;
Ejlli et al. 2020
;
Sorokin 2022
. Maxwell’s equations are linear in the fields and hence follow from a variational principle applied to quadratic combinations of the fields,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{M}}=-F/8\pi
, where
F=\frac{1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
is the invariant constructed from the Faraday tensor
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
. From a theoretical standpoint, Lagrangians involving higher-order derivatives of the fields
Podolsky 1942
or even nonlinear functions on the two linearly independent invariants
F
and
G
can be considered, where
G=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}
,

\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}
, and
\eta^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
is the totally antisymmetric Levi-Civita tensor. The field equations that follow from an arbitrary Lagrangian will depend on its specific functional form, which can be constrained by imposing physical requirements, such as duality and Maxwell’s weak-field limit
Plebaǹski 1970
;
Salazar I. et al. 1987
;
Salazar Ibarguen et al. 1989
;
García D. et al. 1984
;
de Oliveira Costa and Bergliaffa 2009
.

Adopting the usual coupling between fields and spinless particles, the action is given by

S=-\int{\rm d}s\left({m\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}}+eA_{\mu}\dot{x}^{\mu}}\right)+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int{{\rm d}^{4}x\mathcal{L}\left({F,G}\right)}

(1)

where
\dot{x}^{\mu}
stands for the four-velocity of the particle,
A_{\mu}
is the four-potential associated with the electromagnetic field,
\mathcal{L}\left({F,G}\right)
is an arbitrary Lagrangian density,
m
stands for the bare mass of the particle, and
e
denotes its electric charge. The equations of motion that follow from Eq. (
1
) are given by

\displaystyle f^{\mu}=ma^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle eF^{\mu}_{\ \nu}\frac{{\rm d}x^{\nu}}{{\rm d}s}\ ,

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}E^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-4\pi j^{\nu}\ ,

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\ ,

(4)

\displaystyle j^{\mu}\left({x}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}sv^{\mu}(s)\delta^{4}\left[x-z(s)\right]\ .

(5)

Here, Eq. (
2
) is the usual expression for the Lorentz force, and the electromagnetic field equation in Eq. (
3
) is expressed in terms of the excitation tensor
E_{\mu\nu}
, defined as

E_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial F^{\mu\nu}}=2\left({\mathcal{L}_{F}F_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{L}_{G}\widetilde{F}_{\mu\nu}}\right)\ ,

(6)

where
\mathcal{L}_{X}\equiv\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial X
. Note that, by construction,
E^{\mu\nu}
is antisymmetric, and Eq. (
3
) closely resembles the inhomogeneous Maxwell’s equations. The key difference is that
E^{\mu\nu}
is, in general, a nonlinear function of
F^{\mu\nu}
, which can be interpreted as a constituitive relation for the vacuum, leading to effects such as birrefringence
Plebaǹski 1970
;
Boillat 1970
;
Boillat 1966
. Additionally, Eq. (
4
) is the Bianchi identity and Eq. (
5
) gives the definition of the four-current of a point particle moving along and arbitrary worldline
z^{\mu}\left({s}\right)
.

In this work, we consider a well-known example of NLED proposed by Born and Infeld (BI)
Born and Infeld 1934
and
defined by the Lagrangian density

\mathcal{L}_{\text{BI}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\beta^{2}\left({1-\sqrt{U}}\right)\ ,

(7)

where
U\equiv 1+F/\beta^{2}-G^{2}/4\beta^{4}
and
\beta
is the maximum field parameter.

The field equations can be written in the usual vector notation as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{D}=4\pi\rho\ ,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{J}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\ ,

(8a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\ ,\ \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{B}=0\ ,

(8b)

where the
spacelike vector fields

D^{\mu}\equiv-E^{\mu\nu}v_{\nu}
and
H^{\mu}\equiv-\widetilde{E}^{\mu\nu}v_{\nu}
were introduced, with
v^{\mu}
being the normalized 4-velocity of the observer’s worldline. Both fields are nonlinearly dependent of the electric and magnetic induction fields, constructed as the irreducible parts of the Faraday tensor
F_{\mu\nu}
.

Additionally, BI electrodynamics possesses the so-called
F-P
duality
Bronnikov 2001

which ensures that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between two different possible representations of the Lagrangian density via a Legendre transform. For a generic NLED, it is possible to define a Hamiltonian-like density,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(P,S)
from which the field equations can be derived. Both Hamiltonian-like and Lagragian pictures are connected via a Legendre transform:

\mathcal{H}=E^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}-\mathcal{L}\ .

(9)

In order to complete the Legendre transformation, it must be possible to invert the excitation tensor such that
E^{\mu\nu}\left({F^{\mu\nu}}\right)\to F^{\mu\nu}\left({E^{\mu\nu}}\right)
, and the Hamilton equations of motion are completed with

F^{\mu\nu}=2\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial E_{\mu\nu}}=2\left({\mathcal{H}_{P}E^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{H}_{S}\widetilde{E}^{\mu\nu}}\right)\ ,

(10)

where
\mathcal{H}_{X}\equiv\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial X
. Therefore the quantity
\mathcal{H}
can be expressed as a function of only the two invariants constructed from
E^{\mu\nu}
and its dual,
i.e.,

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}\left({P,S}\right)
with

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{2}E^{\mu\nu}E_{\mu\nu}\ ,

(11)

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{E}^{\mu\nu}E_{\mu\nu}\ .

(12)

For the case of BI electrodynamics, the following relations are valid:

\displaystyle E^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{U}}\left({F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{G}{2\beta^{2}}\widetilde{F}^{\mu\nu}}\right)\ ,

(13a)

\displaystyle F^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\left({E^{\mu\nu}+\frac{S}{2\beta^{2}}\widetilde{E}^{\mu\nu}}\right)\ ,

(13b)

where
V\equiv 1-P/\beta^{2}-S^{2}/4\beta^{4}

.
Using Eq. (
13b

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\left({\mathbf{D}+\frac{\left({\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{H}}\right)}{\beta^{2}}\mathbf{H}}\right)\ ,

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\left({\mathbf{H}-\frac{\left({\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{H}}\right)}{\beta^{2}}\mathbf{D}}\right)\ .

In the electrostatic case,

\mathbf{E}=\frac{\mathbf{D}}{\sqrt{1+|\mathbf{D}|^{2}/\beta^{2}}}\ .

(15)

The field equations in Eq. (
8
) indicate that the sources generate the fields
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{H}
, and these fields yield regular solutions for the fields
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{B}
via the constitutive relations given by Eq. (
14

e
, where
\mathbf{D}=e/r^{2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}

\lambda_{\text{BI}}\equiv\sqrt{e/\beta}

15

\mathbf{E}=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{1+r^{4}/\lambda_{\text{BI}}^{4}}}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\ ,

(16)

which, when evaluated at the origin, gives the finite value

. The electrostatic potential for a point particle in flat spacetime follows from the integration of the electric field along a
trajectory,
and is finite at the position of the particle, namely

(17)

Finally, the energy-momentum tensor for Born-Infeld electrodynamics is obtained through the definition
T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta\sqrt{-g}\mathcal{L}}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}
, resulting in

T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{U}}\left(F_{\nu}^{\ \lambda}F_{\lambda\mu}+\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}G^{2}g_{\mu\nu}\right)+g_{\mu\nu}\beta^{2}\left(\sqrt{U}-1\right)\right]\ .

(18)

II.1
Born-Infeld particle in the vicinity of a Schwarzschild black hole

In this subsection, we summarize the results of

II.2
Black hole polarization and approximated expression for the potential

The electrostatic potential
\phi

that the nonlinearity regions will remain confined both outside and inside the horizon for the real and image particle correspondingly. This is illustrated in Figure

(33)

III
BI particle near the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole as a BI particle in Rindler spacetime

We are interested in the field of an accelerated Born-Infeld source in flat spacetime. As previously stated, the usual approach to obtain a solution for the potential of the accelerated source is not feasible in this case via the Green’s function method. Nonetheless, we can exploit the equivalence between a static observer close to the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole and an accelerated observer in flat spacetime: both are characterized by Rindler coordinates.

The starting point is the expansion of the Schwarzschild metric near the horizon as (see
A
for a detailed derivation)

{\rm d}s^{2}=2\kappa_{s}\lambda{\rm d}t^{2}-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{s}\lambda}{\rm d}\lambda^{2}-{\rm d}\rho^{2}-\rho^{2}{\rm d}\varphi^{2}\ ,

(34)

where
\kappa_{s}
is the
surface gravity of Schwarzschild’s black hole . This metric reduces to Minkowski spacetime when
\lambda=\lambda_{0}=1/2\kappa_{s}=r_{s}
. Then, in this coordinate system the particle is at rest at
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
. By performing the corresponding expansions, Copson’s potential, Eq. (
23
), for the near-horizon approximation reads

\phi_{M(C)}\left({\lambda}\right)=\frac{e}{2r_{s}}\frac{\lambda+\lambda_{0}+\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2}{\sqrt{\left[\lambda-\lambda_{0}+\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2\right]^{2}+2\kappa_{s}\lambda_{0}\rho^{2}}}\ .

(35)

It is important to point out that this expression is the exact electric potential of a uniformly accelerated charged particle in Maxwell’s theory in a comoving frame
Gupta and Padmanabhan 1998
.

The solution for the electrostatic potential in a region arbitrarily close to the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole is given by Eq. (
30
), where
\bar{\phi}
is to be identified with
\phi_{M(C)}\left({\lambda}\right)
in Eq. (
35
). Now, it is important to stress that when approximating Schwarzschild to Rindler spacetime, a certain amount of information is lost due to neglecting the spacetime curvature effects on the particle’s field acting over itself
Smith and Will 1980
;
Poisson et al. 2011
, which can alternatively be interpreted as losing the contribution on the potential coming from the effect of black hole polarization,
i.e.
,
\phi_{M(p)}
will no longer be present when considering Rindler spacetime
MacDonald and Suen 1985
. Hence, since we are solely interested in the Rindler’s limit for Schwarzschild spacetime, the electric field is given exactly by the relation

\mathbf{E}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{M(C)}}{\sqrt{1+|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{M(C)}|^{2}\beta^{-2}}}\ ,

(36)

and, in order to obtain the electrostatic potential, it suffices to integrate

\phi=-\int^{r}_{\infty}\mathbf{E}\cdot{\rm d}\mathbf{l}\ .

(37)

Let us use the expressions obtained above to calculate some relevant quantities. Figure

In order to obtain an exact expression for

IV
Transformation to the inertial coordinate system

The calculations
presented in the previous section
are valid for a BI particle arbitrarily close to the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole. At this limit, the static particle outside the horizon behaves exactly like a linearly accelerated particle in flat spacetime and is described in terms of Rindler coordinates adapted to the particle.

Gupta and Padmanabhan
Gupta and Padmanabhan 1998
considered an accelerated charged particle in Maxwell’s theory in comoving (Rindler) coordinates, and showed that the corresponding field components expressed in inertial coordinates are those of the Liénard-Wiechert field of a linearly accelerated charge. In this section we shall follow Ref.
Gupta and Padmanabhan 1998
in order to obtain the relevant electromagnetic field components in the laboratory frame for a linearly accelerated BI source, expressed in Rindler (comoving) coordinates in Eqs. (
46
)-(
47
).

IV.1
Coordinate transformation

In brief, let us consider the motion of the accelerated particle in two different systems. In what follows, the system
S
is an inertial system
with respect to which the particle is accelerated; while the system

is a non-inertial system adapted to the accelerated particle.

Let
\{x^{\mu}\}=\left({t,z,\rho,\phi}\right)
be the coordinates corresponding to the inertial system

\left({t,z,\rho,\phi}\right)

and

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2g\lambda}}{g}\cosh\left({g\tau}\right)\ ,

(53)

VI
Conclusions

Physically meaningful solutions of nonlinear electromagnetic theories are hard to obtain, even in static cases with a high degree of symmetry. The addition of charges in motion makes matters worse. In BI electrodynamics, the dynamics of a charged particle is usually analyzed using a perturbative approach
Chruscinski 1998
;
Chruściński 1998
. In the present work, we have analyzed the behavior of a linearly accelerating charged BI particle using the electrostatic potential of a BI charged particle in the vicinity of a Schwarzschild black hole
Falciano et al. 2019
. By exploiting the fact that the spacetime arbitrarily close to the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole is reduced to Rindler’s spacetime, and using the theorem presented in Sec.
II.1
, we calculated the electrostatic potential and fields in the comoving system. These quantities were then transformed to the inertial frame. The electromagnetic field components for such a particle were also calculated.

Armed with these results, we have discussed the influence of the nonlinearities on the radiation. In doing so, we have explored the theoretical situation of
\beta
being a constant whose value determines only the region where the nonlinearities of the theory become important (
i.e.
, where Maxwell’s results do not hold) and, as such, has not a fixed value. In this context, we have shown that both the radiation field and the radiation-reaction force are influenced by
\beta
.
In fact, for small values of this parameter, both radiation and radiation-reaction for the accelerated particle are less important than those in Maxwell’s case, while
the nonlinearities are still relevant for physically viable values for the
\beta
parameter (
i.e.
, when
\beta\gg 1
).
The effects of the influence of nonlinearity on radiation and radiation-reaction are discussed in Sec.
V
, and are synthesized in Figures
5
,
6
and
9
. These features can be traced back to the existence of the nonlinearity region defined by
\lambda_{\text{BI}}=\sqrt{e/\beta}
, which offers a screening mechanism for the electromagnetic field. It becomes increasingly easier to saturate the electric field within this region. Therefore, in the case where the nonlinear regime dominates in a very large region, the accelerated BI particle emits less radiation than in the opposite case, which in turn results in the weaker response of the particle to this radiation. We also showed how the
\beta
parameter of Born-Infeld electrodynamics influences both the angle of maximum radiation intensity
\theta_{\text{max}}
and the total radiated power for the uniformly accelerated charge. In particular, the more extended the departure from Maxwell’s theory is in space, the larger the value of
\theta_{\rm{max}}
.

Let us remark that an important part of the present work resides in showing that, despite the general impossibility of having a well-defined electrostatic potential for NLED, it is still possible to derive approximated results for this potential, which lead to the electric field of an accelerating particle at a fair level of approximation, specially in the neighbourhood of the particle. Also, we must stress that, contrary to what was previously obtained in the literature for ME, our result for BI furnishes only regular contributions to the particle’s force, as expected, and the Maxwellian radiation-reaction force is recovered in the proper limit. This last statement is a consequence of the fact that the expression of the Lorentz force was used in the calculations. Additional research using alternative, well-motivated definitions of force (c.f.
Rohrlich 2008
) may is also of interest.

Let us also mention that we presented evidence that indicates that the electric field of a Born-Infeld particle vanishes on the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole (a result that was known to be valid for Maxwell’s theory, in which the horizon acts like a conducting surface). An immediate consequence of this fact is that the black hole polarization phenomenon is also present within BI electrodynamics. Whether this is also true for other nonlinear electromagnetic theories remains to be seen.

Finally, let us stress that the method introduced in this work can, in principle, be applied to any electromagnetic theory, thus paving the way to the study of the radiation field and radiation-reaction in other nonlinear theories of interest. We hope to return to this problem, as well as to that of the alternative definitions of force, in future publications.

(110)

Considering

r\approx r_{s},\theta\approx 0

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{s}\left({1+\epsilon\frac{\tilde{r}}{r_{s}}}\right)\sin\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{s}\theta+\mathcal{O}\left({\theta^{2},\epsilon^{2},\epsilon\theta}\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{\rm d}\rho

\displaystyle=

{\rm d}s^{2}=\epsilon\frac{\tilde{r}}{r_{s}}{\rm d}t^{2}-\epsilon\frac{r_{s}}{\tilde{r}}{\rm d}\tilde{r}^{2}-{\rm d}\rho^{2}-\rho^{2}{\rm d}\varphi^{2}\ .

\lambda=\kappa_{s}Z^{2}/2
, thus obtaining

{\rm d}s^{2}=\kappa_{s}^{2}Z^{2}{\rm d}t^{2}-{\rm d}Z^{2}-{\rm d}\rho^{2}-\rho^{2}{\rm d}\varphi^{2}\ .

(113)

where, since
\kappa_{s}
has dimensions of 1/length, the quantity
\kappa_{s}^{2}Z^{2}
is dimensionless and in these coordinates the particle is located at
Z_{0}=2r_{s}
. By shifting the
Z
coordinate as
\tilde{Z}=Z-1/\kappa_{s}
,
it follows that

ds^{2}=\left({1+\kappa_{s}\tilde{Z}}\right)^{2}{\rm d}t^{2}-{\rm d}\tilde{Z}^{2}-{\rm d}\rho^{2}-\rho^{2}{\rm d}\varphi^{2}\ .

(114)

It is particularly convenient to use either Eq. (
113
) or Eq. (
114
) because the spatial part of the line element can be mapped directly into usual Cartesian coordinates.

We now proceed to calculate Copson and Linet’s potential in near-horizon coordinates. In standard coordinates,
Copson’s potential for a particle located at a distance
a
from the origin is given by

\phi_{M(C)}=\frac{e}{2ar}\frac{\left({2r-r_{s}}\right)\left({2a-r_{s}}\right)-r_{s}^{2}\cos\theta}{\left[\left({2r-r_{s}}\right)^{2}+\left({2a-r_{s}}\right)^{2}-2\left({2a-r_{s}}\right)\left({2r-r_{s}}\right)\cos\theta-r_{s}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right]^{1/2}}\ .

(115)

By performing the coordinate transformation displayed in Eq. (
110
) we obtain

\phi_{M(C)}\left({\tilde{r}}\right)=\frac{e}{2r_{s}}\frac{\epsilon\tilde{\alpha}+\epsilon\tilde{r}+\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2}{\sqrt{\left({\epsilon\left[\tilde{r}+\tilde{\alpha}\right]+\kappa_{s}\rho^{2}/2}\right)^{2}-2\epsilon\kappa_{s}\rho^{2}\tilde{\alpha}}}\ .

(116)

Let us now transform the potential in Eq. (
116
) to the other coordinate systems presented in this section. In the
(t,\lambda,\rho,\varphi)
coordinate system we have

\phi_{M(C)}\left({\lambda}\right)=\frac{e}{2r_{s}}\frac{\lambda+\lambda_{0}+\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2}{\sqrt{\left[\lambda-\lambda_{0}+\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2\right]^{2}+2\kappa_{s}\lambda_{0}\rho^{2}}}

(117)

Transforming to the
(t,Z,\rho,\varphi)
coordinate system,

\phi_{M(C)}\left({Z}\right)=\frac{e}{2r_{s}}\frac{Z^{2}+\rho^{2}+\left({1/\kappa_{s}}\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\left({Z^{2}+\rho^{2}-\left({1/\kappa_{s}}\right)^{2}}\right)^{2}+4\rho^{2}\left({1/\kappa_{s}}\right)^{2}}}\ .

(118)

Defining
\omega\equiv\sqrt{\left[\lambda-\lambda_{0}+\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2\right]^{2}+2\kappa_{s}\lambda_{0}\rho^{2}}
,
the derivatives of the potential defined in Eq. (
117
) read

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\phi_{M(C)}\left({\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e\lambda_{0}}{r_{s}\omega^{3}}\left[\lambda-\lambda_{0}-\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2\right]\ ,

(119)

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}\phi_{M(C)}\left({\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\frac{e}{r_{s}}\frac{\lambda\lambda_{0}\rho\kappa_{s}}{\omega^{3}}\ .

(120)

Hence, the norm of the electric field is

|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{M(C)}\left({\lambda}\right)|^{2}=\frac{e^{2}}{\omega^{6}}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{0}}\left\{\left[\lambda-\lambda_{0}-\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}/2\right]^{2}+2\lambda\rho^{2}\kappa_{s}\right\}\ .

(121)

Correspondingly, in the
(t,Z,\rho,\varphi)
coordinate system,

\displaystyle\partial_{Z}\phi_{M(C)}\left({Z}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\frac{eZ\left({1/\kappa_{s}}\right)^{2}}{r_{s}\omega^{3}}\left({Z^{2}-\rho^{2}-\left({1/\kappa_{s}}\right)^{2}}\right)\ ,

(122)

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}\phi_{M(C)}\left({Z}\right)

\displaystyle=-4\frac{e}{r_{s}}\frac{\rho Z^{2}}{\omega^{3}}\left({1/\kappa_{s}}\right)^{2}\ ,

(123)

and the
components of the
electric field in this coordinate system read

\displaystyle E^{Z}

\displaystyle=4e\left({\frac{1}{g}}\right)^{2}\frac{Z^{2}-\rho^{2}-\left({1/\kappa_{s}}\right)^{2}}{\omega^{3}}\ ,

(124)

\displaystyle E^{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{8e\rho Z}{\omega^{3}}\left({\frac{1}{g}}\right)^{2}\ .

(125)

Appendix B
Electrostatic potential of a particle near the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole

In the case in which the BI particle is arbitrarily close to the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole, the metric reduces to the form displayed in Eq. (
112
) and the electrostatic potential, given by Eq. (
117
), is a function of the coordinates
(\lambda,\rho)
. Expanding
\phi_{M(C)}

about the position of the particle,
i.e.
, taking
\lambda\to\lambda_{0}(1+\varepsilon)
and
\rho\to\delta
, with
\varepsilon\ll 1
and
\delta/\rho\ll 1
, we obtain, at leading order

\phi_{M(C)}\approx\frac{e}{\lambda_{0}\varepsilon}+\mathcal{O}\left({\varepsilon^{2},\delta^{2}}\right)\ ,

(126)

while the derivatives take the form

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\phi_{M(C)}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{e}{\lambda_{0}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left({\epsilon^{2},\delta^{2}}\right)\ ,

(127)

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}\phi_{M(C)}

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}(\delta)\ ,

(128)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{M(C)}|

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e}{\lambda_{0}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left({\epsilon^{2},\delta^{2}}\right)\ .

(129)

Note that the potential
\phi_{M(C)}
in Eq. (
126
) represents the electrostatic potential for a charged particle without accounting for the effects of the presence of the black hole which
would be represented by black hole polarization. In other words, if we were to take
\phi_{M(C)}
as the complete solution for the potential then the physical situation represented will be that of a uniformly accelerated particle in Rindler spacetime, which is precisely the focus of the present work. Therefore, the electric field can be expressed in terms of the gradient of
\phi_{M(C)}
as

\mathbf{E}\left({\lambda,\rho}\right)=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{M(C)}}{\sqrt{1+|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{M(C)}|^{2}\beta^{-2}}}\ .

(130)

It remains to integrate Eq. (
130
) in order to obtain the electrostatic potential
\phi
. Using Eq. (
129
), we can approximate

|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi_{M(C)}|^{2}\approx\beta^{2}\left({\frac{\phi_{M(C)}}{\Delta}}\right)^{4}\ ,

(131)

where
\Delta\equiv\sqrt{e\beta}
. Then, Eq. (
130
) reads

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi\approx\Delta\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left({\phi_{M(C)}/\Delta}\right)}{\sqrt{1+\left({\phi_{M(C)}/\Delta}\right)^{4}}}\ ,

(132)

which can be directly integrated to obtain

\phi=-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\phi_{M(C)}}\ _{2}F_{1}\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4},\frac{5}{4};-\left({\frac{\Delta}{\phi_{M(C)}}}\right)^{4}\right]+C_{\infty}\ .

(133)

The integration constant
C_{\infty}
can be obtained by demanding that far from the particle, the potential
\phi
coincides with Copson’s potential
\phi_{M(C)}
. By taking the limit of Eq. (
133
) for
\lambda\to\infty
we obtain

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\phi=C_{\infty}-\frac{\Delta\Gamma\left({\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{2}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}+\phi_{M(C)}+\mathcal{O}\left({\phi_{M(C)}^{5}}\right)\ .

(134)

Hence,
C_{\infty}=\Delta\Gamma\left({\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{2}/4\sqrt{\pi}
and the approximated electrostatic potential reads

\phi=\Delta\left({\frac{\Gamma\left({\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{2}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}-\frac{\Delta}{\phi_{M(C)}}\ _{2}F_{1}\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4},\frac{5}{4};-\left({\frac{\Delta}{\phi_{M(C)}}}\right)^{4}\right]}\right)\ .

(135)

Appendix C
Liénard-Wiechert potential and field

In order to set up our notation, let us review the derivation of the Liénard-Wiechert (LW) potential. This topic is often addressed in standard textbooks (c.f.
Lechner 2018
;
Kosyakov 2007
). The LW potential for a particle with charge
e
is given by

A^{\mu}=e\frac{u^{\mu}}{(R^{\lambda}u_{\lambda})}\ ,

(136)

where
R^{\mu}=x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(s)
is the difference between the observation point
x^{\mu}
and the point where the particle is located along the worldline
z^{\mu}(s)
, and
u^{\mu}
is the particle’s four-velocity.
Note that the relation
R_{\lambda}R^{\lambda}=0
holds, and that the requirement of considering the retarded Green’s function can be recast as
R^{0}>0
. The Faraday tensor for the LW potential (often referred to as the LW field) is given by

F^{\mu\nu}=\frac{e}{\rho^{3}}\left[\left(R^{\mu}u^{\nu}-R^{\nu}u^{\mu}\right)\left(1-a^{\lambda}R_{\lambda}\right)+\rho\left(R^{\mu}a^{\nu}-R^{\nu}a^{\mu}\right)\right]\ ,

(137)

where
\rho\equiv R_{\lambda}u^{\lambda}
, and
a^{\mu}
is the particle’s four-acceleration. From
Eq. (
137
)
the invariants
F
and
G

can be directly calculated, yielding

F=-\frac{e^{2}}{\rho^{4}}\ ;\qquad G=0\ .

(138)

The expression for the LW field depends of the particle’s velocity and acceleration. Hence,

F^{\mu\nu}
can be rewritten as

F^{\mu\nu}=F^{\mu\nu}_{v}+F^{\mu\nu}_{\mathrm{ac}}\ ,

(139)

where

\displaystyle F^{\mu\nu}_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{\rho^{3}}\left(R^{\mu}u^{\nu}-R^{\nu}u^{\mu}\right)\ ,

(140)

\displaystyle F^{\mu\nu}_{\mathrm{ac}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{\rho^{3}}\left[\rho\left(R^{\mu}a^{\nu}-R^{\nu}a^{\mu}\right)-R^{\lambda}a_{\lambda}\left(R^{\mu}u^{\nu}-R^{\nu}u^{\mu}\right)\right]

(141)

respectively. It is straightforward to show that the terms involving the acceleration of the particle do not contribute to the invariant
F
. The fact that the acceleration of the particle – despite being nontrivial – does not enter in the expression for the invariant
F
is closely related to the fact that
F
is a Lorentz invariant and, since inertial observers must agree in the value of such an invariant, it should not depend on the acceleration of the particle. Otherwise, there may exist inertial observers detecting (or failing to detect) phenomena such as radiation from the accelerated particle which, in fact, leads to a series of apparent paradoxes that have attracted attention over the years
Boulware 1980
;
Léauté and Linet 1983
.

Appendix D
Gupta & Padmanabhan calculation of the radiation-reaction force.

We reproduce here some important results of Gupta & Padmanabhan’s paper regarding the calculation of the radiation reaction force
in Maxwell’s theory
Gupta and Padmanabhan 1998
. First, in the limit over the particle’s position the electric field components behave as (here we are using the
\left({t,Z,\rho,\phi}\right)
coordinate system)

\displaystyle E^{Z}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4e}{g^{2}}\frac{1}{\left({Z^{2}-Z_{0}^{2}}\right)^{2}}\ ,

(142)

\displaystyle E^{\rho}

\displaystyle=0\ .

(143)

Clearly, as we approach the particle’s position the
E^{Z}
component diverges. Moreover, we can express the latter result as

E^{Z}=\frac{e}{\left({R_{\mu}v^{\mu}_{\mathrm{ret}}}\right)^{2}}\ .

(144)

Since we are interested in the case of a uniformly accelerated charge, we need to take into account two events, namely
O
and
O^{\prime}
, where the particle is instantaneously at rest (i.e., both the velocity and acceleration at this point are zero) and where the particle is accelerated . Furthermore, we take an observation point
P
which is simultaneous with
O
. The light cone connecting
O^{\prime}
with
P

is not necessarily a straight line, however since we need to consider points very close to the event
O
, the actual shape of this line is not relevant.

As
O^{\prime}\to O
the behavior of the field will be mainly dominated by the Couloumbian part. At
O
the field is static and given by Eq. (
144
). Since we are considering not points at
O
but rather very close to it, we need to consider a modification to the denominator in Eq. (
144
) due to the velocity
u^{Z}
of the particle as

R_{\mu}v_{\mathrm{ret}}^{\mu}\approx\frac{g}{2}\left(Z^{2}-Z_{0}^{2}(-\tau_{0})\right)-\left(Z-Z_{0}(-\tau_{0})\right)u^{Z}(-\tau_{0})\ .

At the event
O
, the particle has zero velocity and acceleration, but the derivative of the proper acceleration
(denoted by
\dot{\alpha}
)
is nonvanishing. Then, the relevant quantities in
R_{\mu}v_{\mathrm{ret}}^{\mu}

can be Taylor-expanded around
\tau_{0}
(where the event
O^{\prime}
occurs) and then expand the position as
Z=Z_{0}+\delta
with
\delta/Z\ll 1
such that, at this limit, the electric field component
E^{Z}
is given by

E^{Z}=\frac{4e}{g^{2}}\frac{1}{\left[\delta^{2}+2\delta Z_{0}+\tau_{0}^{3}Z_{0}\dot{\alpha}/3-\delta\tau_{0}^{2}Z_{0}\dot{\alpha}\right]^{2}}\ .

(145)

In order to find a relation between both perturbative parameters
(\tau_{0},\delta)
we can use the relation for
R_{\mu}v_{\mathrm{ret}}^{\mu}
and evaluate it at
\tau=0
and
\rho=0
, finally obtaining that
\delta\sim\tau_{0}
. Then, the force over the particle exerted by its own field is simply
F^{Z}=eE^{Z}
and expanded in powers of
\delta
reads

F^{Z}\approx\frac{e^{2}}{\delta^{2}}-\frac{e^{2}g}{\delta}+\frac{3}{4}g^{2}e^{2}+\frac{2}{3}e^{2}\dot{\alpha}+\mathcal{O}\left({\delta}\right)\ .

(146)

The first two terms in Eq. (
146
) are clearly divergent and are further absorbed through mass renormalization. The third term is constant and can be neglected due to the singular character of
some terms in Eq. (
146
). Hence, the only relevant contribution from Eq. (
146
) is the term
linear in
\dot{\alpha}
. This term can be directly transformed to the inertial system by transforming the force into the inertial coordinates, resulting directly in

f^{z}=\frac{2e^{2}}{3}\left(\dot{a}^{z}-v^{z}\left(\dot{a}^{\mu}v_{\mu}\right)\right)\ ,

(147)

which is the radiation reaction term originally derived by Dirac.
