py-oopsi: the python implementation of the fast-oopsi algorithm
Benyuan Liu

Abstract
Fast-oopsi was developed by joshua vogelstein in 2009, which is now widely used to extract neuron spike activities from calcium fluorescence signals. Here, we propose detailed implementation of the fast-oopsi algorithm in python programming language. Some corrections are also made to the original fast-oopsi paper.

II
Calcium fluorescence model

Let
\mathbf{{F}}
be a one-dimensional fluorescence trace. At time
t
, the fluorescence measurement
F_{t}
is a linear Gaussian function of the intracellular calcium concentration
[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{t}
at that time:

F_{t}=\alpha[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{t}+\beta+e_{t},\qquad e_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

(1)

\alpha
determines the scale of the signal,
\beta
absorbs the offset.
\alpha
and
\beta
may be learned independently per neuron. The noise
e_{t}
is assumed to be i.i.d distributed.

The calcium concentration jumps
A

\mu
M after each spike and decays back down to baseline
C_{b}

\mu
M, with time constant
\tau
,

[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{t+1}=(1-\Delta/\tau)[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{t}+(\Delta/\tau)C_{b}+An_{t},

(2)

where
\Delta
is the frame interval. The scale
A
and
\alpha
, baseline
C_{b}
and
\beta
are not identifiable, therefore, we may let
A=1
and
C_{b}=0
without loss of generality.
n_{t}
indicates the number of times the neuron spiked in time
t
, we may also write it as a delta function
\delta_{t}
.

Finally, letting
\gamma=(1-\Delta/\tau)
, we have

C_{t}=\gamma C_{t-1}+n_{t}

(3)

and (the filtering model)

C[z]=\frac{1}{1-\gamma z^{-1}}N[z]

(4)

Note that
C_{t}
does not refer to the absolute intracellular concentration, but rather, a relative measure
[
2
]
. The simulated calcium trace can be generated if we synthetically generate
n_{t}
from a probability distribution. To complete the generative model, we assume spikes are sampled according to a Poisson distribution,

n_{t}\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda\Delta)

(5)

where
\lambda\Delta
is the expected firing rate per bin,
\Delta
is included to ensure that the expected firing rate is independent of the frame rate
[
2
]
.

III
Bayes Model

We aim to find the most likely spike trains
\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}
given the fluorescence
\mathbf{{F}}
,

\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}=\argmax_{n_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{0},\forall t}p(\mathbf{{n}}|\mathbf{{F}})

(6)

Using Bayes’ rule,

p(\mathbf{{n}}|\mathbf{{F}})=\frac{1}{p(\mathbf{{F}})}\cdot p(\mathbf{{F}}|\mathbf{{n}})p(\mathbf{{n}})

(7)

given that
p(\mathbf{{F}})
merely scales the results, we rewrite (
6
) as,

\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}=\argmax_{n_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{0},\forall t}p(\mathbf{{F}}|\mathbf{{n}})p(\mathbf{{n}})

(8)

and we already have,

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{{F}}|\mathbf{{n}})

\displaystyle=p(\mathbf{{F}}|\mathbf{{C}})=\prod p(F_{t}|C_{t}),

(9)

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{{n}})

\displaystyle=\prod p(n_{t}),

(10)

where,

\displaystyle p(F_{t}|C_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\alpha C_{t}+\beta,\sigma^{2}),

(11)

\displaystyle p(n_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda\Delta)

(12)

The Poisson distribution penalize sparsity (a sparse prior).

Finally, we have the cost function,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{n_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(F_{t}-\alpha C_{t}-\beta)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\}\frac{\exp\left\{-\lambda\Delta\right\}(\lambda\Delta)^{n_{t}}}{n_{t}!}

(13)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{n_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(F_{t}-\alpha C_{t}-\beta)^{2}+n_{t}\ln\lambda\Delta-\ln n_{t}!

(14)

However, solving for this discretized optimization problem is computational intractable.

IV
Approximate Bayes Filter

We can approximate the Poisson distribution with an exponential distribution of the same mean,

\frac{\exp\{-\lambda\Delta\}(\lambda\Delta)^{n_{t}}}{n_{t}!}\rightarrow(\lambda\Delta)\exp\{-n_{t}\lambda\Delta\}

(15)

and consequently,

\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}=\argmax_{n_{t}>0}\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(F_{t}-\alpha C_{t}-\beta)^{2}-n_{t}\lambda\Delta

(16)

note that
n_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
has been replaced by
n_{t}>0
, since exponential distribution can yield any nonnegative number
[
2
]
. The exponential approximation imposes a sparsening effect, and also, it makes the optimization problem concave in
\mathbf{{C}}
, meaning that any gradient descent algorithm guarantees achieving
the global maxima
(because there are no local minima).

We may further drop the constraint (nonnegative) by adopting
interior point
method,

\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}=\argmax_{n_{t}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(F_{t}-\alpha C_{t}-\beta)^{2}-n_{t}\lambda\Delta+z\ln n_{t}

(17)

where we add a weighted barrier term that approaches
-\infty
as
n_{t}
approaches zero, by solving for a series of
z
going down to nearly zero. The goal is to efficiently solve,

\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}=\argmax_{C}\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(F_{t}-\alpha C_{t}-\beta)^{2}-(C_{t}-\gamma C_{t-1})\lambda\Delta+z\ln(C_{t}-\gamma C_{t-1})

(18)

this cost function is twice differentiable, one can use the Newton-Raphson technique to ascend the surface.

V
Matrix Notation and the Newton-Raphson solver

To proceed, we have

\mathbf{{M}}\mathbf{{C}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\lambda&1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-\lambda&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&-\lambda&1&0\\
0&\cdots&0&0&-\lambda&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}\\
C_{2}\\
\vdots\\
C_{T-1}\\
C_{T}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}\\
n_{2}\\
\vdots\\
n_{T-1}\end{bmatrix}

(19)

\mathbf{{M}}
is a
(T-1)\times T
matrix. Now letting
\mathbf{{1}}
be a
(T-1)\times 1
column vector,
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda\Delta)\mathbf{{1}}
,
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
a
T
-dimensional vector,
\odot
to indicate element-wise operations, then
1
1

1

contrary to
[
2
]
, but alike
fast-oopsi.m
, we choose
\mathbf{{M}}
as a sparse
T\times T
matrix, and
\mathbf{{1}}
as
T\times 1
column vector. Therefore we have
n_{0}=C_{0}
, we will correct
n_{0}=\epsilon
after convergence.

\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}=\argmax_{\mathbf{{MC}}\geq_{\odot}\mathbf{{0}}}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mathbf{{F}}-\bm{\alpha}\mathbf{{C}}-\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}-(\mathbf{{MC}})^{T}\bm{\lambda}+z\ln_{\odot}(\mathbf{{MC}})^{T}\mathbf{{1}}

(20)

We instead iteratively minimize the cost function
\mathcal{L}
(called
post
in our python implementation) where,

\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}_{z}=\argmin_{\mathbf{{C}}}\mathcal{L},\quad\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mathbf{{F}}-\bm{\alpha}\mathbf{{C}}-\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+(\mathbf{{MC}})^{T}\bm{\lambda}-z\ln_{\odot}(\mathbf{{MC}})^{T}\mathbf{{1}}

(21)

\mathcal{L}
is
convex
, when using Newton-Raphson method to
descend
a surface, one iteratively computes the gradient
\mathbf{{g}}=\nabla\mathcal{L}
(first derivative) and Hessian
\mathbf{{H}}=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}
(second derivative) of the argument to be optimized. Then,
\mathbf{{C}}=\mathbf{{C}}-s\mathbf{{d}}
, where
s
is the step size and
\mathbf{{d}}
is the step direction by solving
\mathbf{{H}}\mathbf{{d}}=\mathbf{{g}}
. The gradient and Hessian, with respect to
\mathbf{{C}}
, are

\displaystyle\mathbf{{g}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\bm{\alpha}}{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{{F}}-\bm{\alpha}\mathbf{{C}}-\bm{\beta})+\mathbf{{M}}^{T}\bm{\lambda}-z\mathbf{{M}}^{T}(\mathbf{{MC}})_{\odot}^{-1}

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{{I}}+z\mathbf{{M}}^{T}(\mathbf{{MC}})_{\odot}^{-2}\mathbf{{M}}

(23)

s
is found via
backtracking linesearches
.
\mathbf{{M}}
is bidiagonal, so
\mathbf{{H}}
is tridiagonal,
\mathbf{{d}}=\mathbf{{H}}^{-1}\mathbf{{g}}
can be efficiently implemented in matlab by assuming
\mathbf{{H}}
is a sparse matrix. In python, we may use sparse linsolvers (linsolve.spsolve) to efficiently find
\mathbf{{d}}
. Once
\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}
is obtained, it is a simple linear transform to obtain
\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}
, via
\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}=\mathbf{{M}}\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}
. We will normalize
\mathbf{{n}}
by
\mathbf{{n}}=\mathbf{{n}}/\mathrm{max}(\mathbf{{n}})
after convergence.

VI
Parameters initialize and update

The parameters
\bm{\theta}=\{\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\gamma,\lambda\}
are unknown. We may use pseudo expectation-maximization method,
(1), initialize the parameters, (2) recursively computes
\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}
and updating
\bm{\theta}
given the new
\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}
until the convergence is met.

The scale of
\mathbf{{F}}
relative to
\mathbf{{n}}
is arbitrary, therefore,
\mathbf{{F}}
is firstly
detrended
, and then
linearly mapped
between
0
and
1
.

\mathbf{{F}}=\mathrm{detrend}(\mathbf{{F}}),\quad\mathbf{{F}}=(\mathbf{{F}}-F_{min})/(F_{max}-F_{min}),

(24)

Next, because spiking is sparse in many experimental settings,
\mathbf{{F}}
tends to be around baseline,
\beta
is set to the median of
\mathbf{{F}}
. We use median absolute deviation (MAD) and correction factor
K
, as a robust normal scale estimator of
\mathbf{{F}}
where
K=1.4826
. Previous works showed that the results
\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}_{z}
are robust to minor variations in the time constant, we let
\gamma=1-\Delta
. Finally,
\lambda
is set to
1
Hz, which is between baseline and evoked spike rate for data of interest
2
2

2

corrections to
[
2
]
: 1), add detrend to
\mathbf{{F}}
, 2),
K=1.4826
and it is multiplied (not divided by)
\mathrm{MAD}(\mathbf{{F}})
.
.

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=1,

(25)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\mathrm{median}(\mathbf{{F}}),

(26)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MAD}(\mathbf{{F}})\cdot K=\mathrm{median}(|\mathbf{{F}}-\beta|)\cdot K,\quad K=1.4826

(27)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=1-\Delta/(1\mathrm{sec}),

(28)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=1\mathrm{Hz}

(29)

Then, given
\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}
, we may (approximately) update
\bm{\theta}
by,

\hat{\bm{\theta}}\approx\argmax_{\bm{\theta}}p(\mathbf{{F}},\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}|\bm{\theta})=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}}\ln p(\mathbf{{F}}|\hat{\mathbf{{C}}};\{\alpha,\beta,\sigma\})+\ln p(\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}|\lambda)

(30)

where,

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\lambda>0}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left[\ln(\lambda\Delta)+\hat{n_{t}}\lambda\Delta\right]

(31)

\displaystyle\{\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta},\hat{\sigma}\}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\alpha,\beta,\sigma>0}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{F_{t}-\alpha C_{t}-\beta}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right]

(32)

We have (by taking the derivatives and letting them equal zero),

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{\Delta\sum_{t}n_{t}},

(33)

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=1,

(34)

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t}(F_{t}-C_{t})}{T},

(35)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t}(F_{t}-C_{t}-\beta)^{2}}{T}=\frac{\|\mathbf{{F}}-\mathbf{{C}}-\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}}{T}

(36)

where
\hat{\lambda}
is the inverse of the inferred firing rate,
\hat{\alpha}
can be set to
1.0
because the scale of
\mathbf{{C}}
is arbitrary,
\hat{\beta}
is the mean bias,
\hat{\sigma}
is the root-mean-square of the residual error.

VIII
Wiener filter (linear regression, simple convex optimization)

In the wiener filter, we approximate the Poisson distribution with a Gaussian distribution,

p(n_{t})\sim\mathcal{N}(\lambda\Delta,\lambda\Delta)

(37)

then, the MAP estimator yields,

\hat{\mathbf{{n}}}=\argmax_{n_{t}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(F_{t}-\alpha C_{t}-\beta)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\lambda\Delta}(n_{t}-\lambda\Delta)^{2}\right]

(38)

and its matrix notation,

\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}=\argmax_{\mathbf{{C}}}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mathbf{{F}}-\alpha\mathbf{{C}}-\beta\mathbf{{1}}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\lambda\Delta}\|\mathbf{{MC}}-\lambda\Delta\mathbf{{1}}\|_{2}^{2}

(39)

which is
quadratic
,
concave
in
\mathbf{{C}}
.

Finally, we aim to optimize (minimize,
quadratic
,
convex
optimization),

\hat{\mathbf{{C}}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{{C}}}\mathcal{L},\quad\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mathbf{{F}}-\alpha\mathbf{{C}}-\beta\mathbf{{1}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda\Delta}\|\mathbf{{MC}}-\lambda\Delta\mathbf{{1}}\|_{2}^{2}

(40)

where
\mathcal{L}
is
convex
in
\mathbf{{C}}
. Using Newton-Raphson update, we find
\mathbf{{C}}=\mathbf{{C}}-\mathbf{{d}}
,
\mathbf{{H}}\mathbf{{d}}=\mathbf{{g}}
and
\mathbf{{g}}=\nabla\mathcal{L}
,
\mathbf{{H}}=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}
. The gradient
\mathbf{{g}}
and Hessian
\mathbf{{H}}
are,

\displaystyle\mathbf{{g}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{{F}}-\alpha\mathbf{{C}}-\beta\mathbf{{1}})+\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta}\left[\mathbf{{M}}^{T}(\mathbf{{MC}})+\lambda\Delta\mathbf{{M}}^{T}\mathbf{{1}}\right]

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{{I}}+\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta}\mathbf{{M}}^{T}\mathbf{{M}}

(42)

In the python implementation, we let
