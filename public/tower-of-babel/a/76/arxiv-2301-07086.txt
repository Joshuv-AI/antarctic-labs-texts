A continuum limit for dense spatial networks Preprint: APS/123-QED
Sidney Holden 1,2, Geoffrey Vasil 2

Abstract
Many physical systems—such as optical waveguide lattices and dense neuronal or vascular networks—can be modeled by metric graphs , where slender “wires” (edges) support wave or diffusion equations subject to Kirchhoff boundary conditions at the nodes. This work proposes a continuum-limit framework that replaces discrete vertex-based equations with a coarse-grained partial differential equation (PDE) defined on the continuous space occupied by the network. The derivation naturally introduces an edge-conductivity tensor , an edge-capacity function , and a vertex number density to encode how each microscopic patch of the graph contributes to the macroscopic phenomena. The results have interesting similarities and differences with the Riemannian Laplace-Beltrami operator. We calculate all macroscopic parameters from first principles via a systematic discrete-to-continuous local homogenization, finding an anomalous effective embedding dimension resulting from a homogenized diffusivity. Numerical examples—including an axisymmetric “spiderweb”, several periodic lattices, random Delaunay triangulations, nearest-neighbor geometric graphs, and aperiodic monotiles—demonstrate that each finite model converges to its corresponding PDE (posed on different manifolds like tori, disks, and spheres) in the limit of increasing vertex density.

I
Mathematical setup

A
metric graph

G=(V,\,E)
is a collection of vertices,
V
, and edges,
E
, equipped with local Euclidean coordinates
x\in[0_{e},\ell_{e}]\simeq e\in E
. We denote two adjacent vertices with the homogeneous relation
v\sim w
(equivalently
w\sim v
)
Berkolaiko and Kuchment 2013
—not to be confused with the standard use of ‘asymptotic to’, which we intentionally avoid throughout this work to avert potential confusion.
We denote the corresponding edge
e_{vw}
and drop the subscript when the correspondence is evident. In the initial construction, we avoid loops and multiple edges between two given vertices. Later in our analysis, multiple edges enter the picture when “periodizing” local partitions. Nevertheless, multiple edges are (in some ways) equivalent to a graph with reweighted edge lengths; see Theorem 1 in SM
sup 2025
.

The parameterized edges of a metric graph within a manifold need not be straight; a balled-up fishing net is the same as its laid-flat counterpart.
A real-valued scalar function,
f_{e}(x)
for
x\in{e}
, is globally continuous if it is continuous on all individual edges and single-valued on all vertices. However, the derivatives of arbitrarily smooth functions along each edge generically disagree at the vertices. The Kirchhoff condition (below) quantifies the balance of multiple derivative values from incident edges.

We derive a system of equations via an action principle,
\delta\mathcal{S}=0
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(f)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in E}\int_{0_{e}}^{\ell_{e}}\!\mathcal{L}_{e}(x,f_{e},f_{e}^{\prime})\,\text{d}{x},

(1)

with edgewise Lagrangian function,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}(x,f_{e},f_{e}^{\prime})\,=\,K_{e}(x)\left|f_{e}^{\prime}(x)\right|^{2}-\lambda\,C_{e}(x)\,|f_{e}(x)|^{2}.

(2)

Eq. ​(
1
) could also include additional direct pointwise terms at the vertices, but these require no special treatment in the continuum limit and simply add to the eventual final result.
We initially assume general smooth edge “conductivities”,
K_{e}(x)
and “capacities”,
C_{e}(x)
. The generality is useful in applications like biological transport networks
Katifori et al. 2010
.
However, the dense limit only requires leading-order constant mean values of
K_{e}
and
C_{e}
along each edge, but still depending on the given edges.

For any continuous and differentiable
\delta\!f_{e}(x)
with support on a single edge,
e
, with
\delta\!f_{e}(0_{e})=\delta\!f_{e}(\ell_{e})=0
, the stationarity condition
\delta\mathcal{S}=0
with respect to
\delta\!f_{e}
gives the edge equations,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{C_{e}(x)}\frac{d}{dx}\left(K_{e}(x)f_{e}^{\prime}(x)\right)\,=\,\lambda\,f_{e}(x).

(3)

Alternatively, if
\delta\!f_{e}(x)
also has support on a single vertex, then the
Kirchhoff condition
at
x=v
is

\displaystyle\sum_{e\sim v}\hat{B}_{ve}\,K_{e}(v)f_{e}^{\prime}(v)\,=\,0.

(4)

where, the “incidence matrix”,
\hat{B}_{ve}=\pm{1}
, gives the unit normal outward from edge
e
at vertex
v
.
Here, the sum is over edges
e
connected to
v
(denoted
e\sim v
).
The Kirchhoff condition, eq. ​(
4
), shows why relevant functions usually have multi-valued derivatives at vertices.
The manifestly self-adjoint nature of the problem implies that the spectrum
\sigma(\lambda)
is real-valued and discrete with
\lambda>0

Berkolaiko and Kuchment 2013
. The system could represent (e.g.) the spatial component of diffusion (
\lambda\to-\partial_{t}
), wave dynamics (
\lambda\to-\partial_{t}^{2}
), or Schrödinger evolution (
\lambda\to i\partial_{t}
).

At this point, we simplify to edgewise constant
C_{e}
,
K_{e}
, which applies in the eventual short-edge length limit.
For a given edge
e
with endpoints
v\simeq 0_{e}
,
w\simeq\ell_{e}
and setting
k_{e}=\sqrt{\lambda\,C_{e}/K_{e}}
, the local solution to eq. ​(
3
) is

\displaystyle f_{e}(x)\,=\,f(v)\,\frac{\sin\!\left(k_{e}(\ell_{e}-x)\right)}{\sin\!\left(k_{e}\ell_{e}\right)}+f(w)\,\frac{\sin\!\left(k_{e}x\right)}{\sin\!\left(k_{e}\ell_{e}\right)},

(5)

which manifestly expresses continuity at the vertices
f_{e}(0_{e})=f(v)
,
f_{e}(\ell_{e})=f(w)
.
Substituting into the vertex Kirchhoff condition eq. ​(
4
) at vertex
v
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{e\sim v}k_{e}K_{e}\left(\cot\!\left(k_{e}\ell_{e}\right)f(v)-\csc\!\left(k_{e}\ell_{e}\right)f(w)\right)\,=\,0.

(6)

The system is now a finite nonlinear eigenvalue problem (NEP)
Güttel and Tisseur 2017
;
Colbrook and Townsend 2023
with spectral parameter
\lambda
and eigenvectors
f(V)
over the set of vertices,
V
; see SM
sup 2025

Finally, eq. ​(
6
) derives from the 1st-order stationarity of the finite quadratic action functional,

\displaystyle S(f)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\sim v}

\displaystyle k_{e}K_{e}\Big\{\!\cot\!\left(k_{e}\ell_{e}\right)\left(f(v)-f(w)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-2\tan\!\left(\tfrac{k_{e}\ell_{e}}{2}\right)f(v)f(w)\Big\},

(7)

which assumes
K_{e}
and
C_{e}
are constant along the edges. A similar functional still exists in the more general case, albeit in terms of generalized Green’s functions in the place of
\sin\!\left(k_{e}x\right)/\sin\!\left(k_{e}\ell_{e}\right)
.

II
Continuum limit

We embed our metric-graph solution in a general Riemannian manifold,
M\supset G
.
The vertices are simply points,
v\in{M}
.
The edges are either fully embedded (
e\subset{M}
) or could be straight chords embedded within a higher-dimensional Euclidean space.
In the continuum limit, both options will give the same result: for any two adjacent vertices,
w\sim v
, we take the limit as
\ell_{e}\to 0
but allow the number of vertices to fill in densely in
M
.

We assume
M
has a Riemannian structure with a natural volume measure.
However, our eventual results are independent of a particular underlying Riemannian metric.
The graph requires a
vertex number density
,
\mu(x)
, such that for all sufficiently coarse-grained subsets,
S\subseteq{G}
,

\displaystyle\int_{S}\mu(x)\,\text{d}{x}\,=\,\text{number of vertices}\,\in\,S.

(8)

We can use the empirical measure on the vertices,
\mu_{V}(x)=\sum_{v\in V}\delta(x-x_{v})
, which Portmanteau’s theorem guarantees converges weakly to a continuum limit as the graph becomes dense. For an orthonormal basis of square-integrable functions,
\psi_{k}(x)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(G)
,

\displaystyle\mu_{N}(x)\,=\,\sum_{k\leq N}\psi_{k}(x)\sum_{v\in V}\psi_{k}(x_{v})\,\approx\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{v}|},

(9)

for
x\in\mathcal{A}_{v}
, the dual cell area (volume in general) surrounding a given vertex (see figure
1
b).
The spectral representation approximates the empirical measure for functions of bounded variation, which is also sufficient to define the weak derivative
Evans and Garzepy 2018
.

We derive a formal continuum limit
\ell_{e}\to 0
in eq. ​(
7
). First,
\cot\!\left(z\right)=z^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(z)
and
2\tan\!\left(z/2\right)=z+\mathcal{O}(z^{3})
, as
z\to 0
. Then,

\displaystyle f(w)-f(v)\,=\,r_{e}\cdot\nabla{f(v)}+\mathcal{O}(\ell_{e}^{2}),

(10)

with
\hat{r}_{e}=r_{e}/\ell_{e}
(
\ell_{e}=\|r_{e}\|
by definition) the unit tangent pointing from
v
to
w
along their common edge,
e
. On a general manifold,
r_{e}
is formally in the tangent vector along a parameterized edge from
v
to
w
embedded within the manifold, and
\nabla f(v)
is in the cotangent space over
v
. We impose a local Riemannian structure when stating
\hat{r}_{e}\cdot\hat{r}_{e}=1
and supposing that
\ell_{e}
is the geodesic distance along
e
. Finding a globally smooth extension of the metric for a general graph is beyond our current scope
Nash 1956
;
Nash 1966
. We assume the deviation between edge curves and geodesics becomes negligible as
\ell_{e}\to 0
.

Now comes the crux of the derivation. We define the following graph “edge” tensors at each vertex (see figure
1
b)

\displaystyle R_{K}(v)

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{e\sim v}\|r_{e}\|\,K_{e}\,\hat{r}_{e}\otimes\hat{r}_{e},

(11)

\displaystyle R_{C}(v)

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{e\sim v}\|r_{e}\|\,C_{e}\,\hat{r}_{e}\otimes\hat{r}_{e},

(12)

where
\hat{r}_{e}\cdot\hat{r}_{e}=1
. For the capacity,

\displaystyle\text{tr}\!\left({R_{C}(v)}\right)

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{e\sim v}\|r_{e}\|\,C_{e},

(13)

Therefore, to
\mathcal{O}(\ell_{e}^{2})
,

\displaystyle S(f)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\int_{G}\left[\nabla f(x)\cdot R_{K}(x)\cdot\nabla f(x)\right.-\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\left.\lambda\,\text{tr}\!\left({R_{C}(x)}\right)f(x)^{2}\right]\mu(x)\,\text{d}{x}.

(14)

Computing the variational derivative of the continuum functional produces the eigenvalue problem in the dense limit:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mu\,\text{tr}\!\left({R_{C}}\right)}\nabla\cdot\left(\mu\,R_{K}\cdot\nabla f\right)\,=\,\lambda f,\quad x\in G.

(15)

The associated “natural” boundary conditions are

\displaystyle\hat{n}\cdot R_{K}\cdot\nabla f\,=\,0,\quad x\in\partial G,

(16)

where
\hat{n}
is the unit normal to the boundary. Alternatively,
f|_{\partial G}=0
for pinned boundary vertices.

For the examples, we simplify to the special case where
K_{e}=C_{e}=1
for all edges,
e
, and then
\lambda=k^{2}
. We define

\displaystyle R(v)\,=\,\sum_{e\sim v}\|r_{e}\|\,\hat{r}_{e}\otimes\hat{r}_{e},\quad\text{tr}\!\left({R(v)}\right)\,=\,\sum_{e\sim v}\|r_{e}\|.

(17)

III
Tensor comparisons

The edge tensors,
R_{K}
and
R_{C}
, serve a similar purpose to other well-known tensors in continuum modeling but with some notable differences.

Nematic Comparison

The edge tensor is analogous to other examples in various applications but with a different inverse length scaling.
Generalizing the summand,
Q_{e}^{(p)}=\|r_{e}\|^{p}\,r_{e}\!\otimes r_{e}
, with
R(v)
corresponding to
p=-1
. For
p=0
, the conformation (a.k.a neumatic-order) tensor,
Q
, gives the degree of alignment in liquid crystals
De Gennes 1971
,

\displaystyle Q(v)\,\propto\,\sum_{e\sim v}\left(Q_{e}^{(0)}-\frac{1}{d}\,\text{tr}\!\left({Q_{e}^{(0)}}\right)I\right),

(18)

so that
\text{tr}\!\left({Q}\right)=0
by definition. An isotropic material implies
Q=0
, and
Q
is a diagonal matrix for a perfect nematic.

Riemannian Comparison

Eq. ​(
15
) gives genuinely distinct behavior from a traditional Laplace-Beltrami operator, with the main differences resulting from the trace,
\text{tr}\!\left({R}\right)
.
For example, for a completely homogeneous and isotropic graph, the only symmetric rank-2 tensor is proportional to the
d
-dimensional identity,

\displaystyle R\,=\,\frac{\text{tr}\!\left({R}\right)}{d}\,I_{d}\quad\implies\quad-\frac{1}{d}\,\Delta f\,=\,\lambda f.

(19)

Uniform edge lengths imply
\text{tr}\!\left({R}\right)=\ell\,\deg(v)
for every vertex. For this particular case, eq. ​(
15
) reduces to the standard Laplace-Beltrami operator,
but weighted by the spatial dimension of the graph embedding
.
Overall, the continuum “graph material” can resemble many other materials but is (roughly speaking) less stiff by the dimension of the embedding space. Including globally constant conductivity and capacity, satisfying
K/C=d
, would cancel the anomalous diffusivity factor in eq. ​(
19
).
On perfectly square lattices, previous work
Exner et al. 2006b
;
Pankrashkin 2012
;
Nakamura and Tadano 2021
;
Exner et al. 2022b
rigorously proves formal operator convergence to eq. ​(
19
), commenting on the dimension factor.

The inverse Riemannian metric tensor roughly corresponds to
p=-2
above.
The reduced system resembles the standard Laplace-Beltrami operator on a manifold with an augmented local metric tensor,
g(x)
.
However, there is no clear way (without adjusting
K_{e}
and
C_{e}
) to put eq. ​(
15
) into one-to-one correspondence with a Laplace-Beltrami operator, even if we impose conditions on the type of Riemannian metric.
That is, the edge tensor is defined similarly to a discrete approximation of a Riemannian metric, but with a different distance scaling,
i.e.
, with closest correspondence (see SM
sup 2025
)

\displaystyle g(v)^{-1}\ \longleftrightarrow\ \frac{\text{dim}(M)}{\text{deg}(v)}\sum_{e\sim v}\hat{r}_{e}\otimes\hat{r}_{e}.

(20)

However, for Riemannian metrics,
\text{tr}\!\left({g}\right)=\text{dim}(M)
by definition.

Axisymmetric spiderweb

The first preliminary example, an axisymmetric “spiderweb” (figure
1

R

The spiderweb has small but finite local vertex spacings
dr
,
d\theta
in each polar-coordinate direction
r
,
\theta
. Counting the central vertex at the origin, boundary vertices on the unit circle, and interior vertices gives a total of
|V|=(N_{r}-1)N_{\theta}+1
where
N_{r},N_{\theta}
are the numbers of vertices in the radial and angular directions.
At interior vertices,

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle\,=\,R_{rr}\,\hat{r}\otimes\hat{r}+R_{\theta\theta}\,\hat{\theta}\otimes\hat{\theta},

(21)

\displaystyle R_{rr}

\displaystyle\,=\,2\,dr+4\,r\sin^{3}(d\theta/2)=2\,dr+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}),

(22)

\displaystyle R_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{r\sin^{2}(d\theta)}{\sin\!\left(d\theta/2\right)}=2\,r\,d\theta+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}).

(23)

The vertex density is
\mu=1/\mathcal{A}_{v}
with vertex-cell area

.
The angular spacing is

.
The radial spacing depends smoothly on

Angular symmetry allows Fourier solutions,

Putting everything together,

\displaystyle r\rho(r)f_{m}^{\prime\prime}(r)-\left[r(r+\rho(r))k^{2}+m^{2}\right]f_{m}(r)\,=\,0.

(24)

We use pinned outer boundary conditions
f_{m}(r=1)=0
.
For center-point conditions,
f_{m}^{\prime}(r=0)=0
if

and
f_{m}(r=0)=0
otherwise.
No choice of

can render the above operator into a Riemannian metric-based Laplacian.
The original graph problem eq. ​(
6

The specific form

, with

\displaystyle\Lambda\,=\,\left\{\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\,a_{i}\ ;\ n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

(27)

The full periodic graph is
G=G_{0}+\Lambda
.
The 2D square graph has
V_{0}=\{(0,0)\}
and
a_{1}=(\ell,0)
,
a_{2}=(0,\ell)
(see figure
3
(a1)).
The 2D hexagonal graph has
V_{0}=\{(0,0),(\ell,0)\}
and
a_{1}=(3/2,\sqrt{3}/2)\,\ell
,
a_{2}=(3/2,-\sqrt{3}/2)\,\ell
.
This construction is equivalent to a multi-lattice (
i.e.
, a Bravais lattice) when
G_{0}
is a single vertex.

For periodic graphs, solving the full-graph NEP requires information only from
G_{0}
. The vertex condition at
v\in V_{0}
with neighbors
w\in V_{0}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\Lambda}\sum_{w+r\sim v}\big[

\displaystyle\cot\!\left(k\,\ell_{v,w+r}\right)\,f(v)

\displaystyle-\csc\!\left(k\,\ell_{v,w+r}\right)\,f(w+r)\big]\,=\,0,

(28)

where
\ell_{v,w+r}=\ell_{w,v-r}
is the length of the edge connecting
v
and
w+r
(equivalently
w
to
v-r
). Eq. ​(
28
) assumes only a single edge exists between any pair
(v,w+r)
, but multiple edges can connect
v,w
with a different lattice vector
r
. Extending this assumption to the multi-edge case is straightforward by including an additional sum for each edge between
v
and
w+r
.
We assume plane-wave solutions,

\displaystyle f(x)\,=\,\hat{f}(\kappa)\,e^{i\kappa\cdot x}+\text{c.c.},

(29)

hence the translation acts via
f(w+r)=e^{i\kappa\cdot r}f(w)
.
The wave vectors lie in the dual lattice,
\kappa\in\Lambda^{\!*}
. For convenience, we adopt the short-hand notation throughout the following sections, defining the phase angle,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{i}\,=\,\kappa\cdot a_{i}.

(30)

Given
\vartheta_{i}
, we can reconstitute
\kappa=\sum_{i}\vartheta_{i}\,a^{*}_{i}
with the dual lattice basis,
a^{*}_{i}\in\Lambda^{\!*}
where
a^{*}_{i}\cdot a_{j}=\delta_{i,j}
. In each given periodic graph, we use
\ell
to denote some measure of the edge lengths, which differs from graph to graph. We also define the shorthand,

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}\,=\,\frac{\vartheta_{i}}{\ell}\,=\,\mathcal{O}(1)\quad\text{as}\quad\ell\to 0.

(31)

In all cases with uniform
\ell
, we define the spectral parameter,

\displaystyle\gamma\,=\,\cos(k\ell),

(32)

where
\lambda=k^{2}
for the original metric graph problem.

The embedding of
\Lambda
into the
d
-dimensional torus,
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, happens by taking
\kappa
to satisfy various quantisation conditions. The solutions are periodic

\displaystyle f(x+r_{i})\,=\,f(x),

(33)

with respect to a set of
d
linearly independent,
r_{i}\in\Lambda
. Therefore, in terms of the wave vector,

\displaystyle\kappa\cdot r_{i}\,=\,2\pi\,n_{i}\qquad\text{for}\qquad n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(34)

V.2
Square Lattice

Figure
3
(a1) shows a periodic square graph. The fundamental lattice vectors are
a_{1}=(\ell,0)
and
a_{2}=(0,\ell)
with uniform edge length
\ell>0
. The unit graph
G_{0}
comprises a single vertex in
V_{0}=\{v\}
, and two orthogonal edges. Figure
3
(a2) shows
v
has four neighbors:
\{v\pm a_{1},v\pm a_{2}\}
. In terms of Fourier parameters, eq. ​(
28
) becomes

\displaystyle\cos\!\left(k\ell\right)\,=\,\frac{\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{1}\right)+\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{2}\right)}{2},

(35)

which is for cardinal (NSEW) connectivity.
Eq. ​(
35
) is particularly intriguing: it is the Pythagorean Theorem for a right triangle on the
surface of a sphere
with spherical side angles
|\vartheta_{1}\pm\vartheta_{2}|/2
and hypotenuse angle
k\ell
. We note that nontrivial geometric structure in spectral space can lead to interesting phenomena (e.g. topological protection
Berry 1984
).
To leading order as
\ell\to 0
,

\displaystyle k^{2}\,=\,\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+\kappa_{2}^{2}}{2}\,=\,\frac{\kappa\cdot\kappa}{2}.

(36)

The limit follows analytically from eq. ​(
35
) as
\ell\to 0
. However, it also follows automatically from the local Euclidean nature of the 2-sphere in
\kappa
space.

Figure
3
(b1) shows additional ordinal (NE-SW-SE-NW) connectivity by diagonal edges to the unit graph, obtaining the periodic 8-connected graph with,

\displaystyle\cos\!\left(k\ell_{1}\right)+\cos\!\left(k\ell_{2}\right)\,=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{1}\right)+\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{2}\right)}{2}

\displaystyle+\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{1}\right)\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{2}\right),

(37)

for
\ell_{1}=\ell
and
\ell_{2}=\sqrt{2}\ell
.
Eq. ​(
37
) is simply the sum of eq. ​(
35
), and an additional right spherical triangle angles
\vartheta_{1}
,
\vartheta_{1}
and
\sqrt{2}k\ell
.

For both cardinal and ordinal cases, the square lattice has constant vertex density,
\mu(v)=\ell^{-2}
, with

\displaystyle R(v)\,=\,2\left(a_{1}\otimes a_{1}+a_{2}\otimes a_{2}\right)\,=\,2\ell\,I.

(38)

The proportionality constants of the identity scale out of the limiting PDE, and we have the
\text{tr}\!\left({I}\right)=2
version of eq. ​(
19
).

V.3
Rectangular Lattice

An anisotropic rectangular generalization has respective horizontal and vertical edge vectors
a_{1}=(\ell_{1},0)
and
a_{2}=(0,\ell_{2})
where
\ell_{1}=\alpha\ell
and
\ell_{2}=\ell
. The dispersion relation, in this case,

\displaystyle\cot\!\left(k\ell_{1}\right)+\cot\!\left(k\ell_{2}\right)\,=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{1}\right)}{\sin\!\left(k\ell_{1}\right)}+\frac{\cos\!\left(\vartheta_{2}\right)}{\sin\!\left(k\ell_{2}\right)}.

(39)

In this case, as
\ell\to 0
,

\displaystyle k^{2}\,=\,\frac{\kappa\cdot R\cdot\kappa}{\text{tr}\!\left({R}\right)}.

(40)

As before the density is constant,
\mu(x)=\alpha^{-1}\ell^{-2}
. However,

\displaystyle R(v)

\displaystyle\,=\,2\left(a_{1}\otimes a_{1}+a_{2}\otimes a_{2}\right)\,=\,2\ell\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

(41)

The corresponding PDE agrees with the limiting NEP,

-\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\partial_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\,\partial_{y}^{2}\right)f\,=\,\lambda f,

(42)

which matches
Tzella and Vanneste 2016
, who model advection-diffusion on a rectangular grid.
By approximating the concentration in the long-time limit with a Gaussian diffusion kernel, they find an effective zero-advection diffusivity tensor,
D
, near the source (
Tzella and Vanneste 2016
eqs. (12-14))

\displaystyle D\,=\,\frac{R}{\text{tr}\!\left({R}\right)}\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

(43)

They, furthermore, find a medium-range diffusion kernel based on the squared Manhattan distance,
\|x\|_{1}
, rather than the rotationally invariant Euclidean metric,
\|x\|_{2}
. However, the general bound
\|x\|_{1}^{2}\leq{d}\,\|x\|_{2}^{2}
implies the results agree in the full continuum limit.

V.4
Hexagonal Lattice

Figure
3
(d1) shows the first case with more than one distinct base vertex,
V_{0}=\{v_{E},v_{W}\}
with displacement vector
r_{WE}=(\ell,0)
. Figure
3
(d2) shows the adjacency structure of
G_{0}
.
The fundamental lattice vectors are

\displaystyle a_{1},\,a_{2}\,=\,\frac{\ell}{2}\left(3,\,\pm\sqrt{3}\right).

(44)

In this case, eq. ​(
28
) is a
2\times 2
system over
V_{0}
,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-3\gamma&1+e^{i\vartheta_{1}}+e^{i\vartheta_{2}}\\
1+e^{-i\vartheta_{1}}+e^{-i\vartheta_{2}}&-3\gamma\end{bmatrix}\!\!\begin{bmatrix}f_{E}\\
f_{W}\end{bmatrix}\,=\,0.

(45)

The dispersion relation is,

\displaystyle\gamma\,=\,\pm\frac{\left|1+e^{i\vartheta_{1}}+e^{i\vartheta_{2}}\right|}{3}.

(46)

In this case,
\ell\to 0
leads to an initially confusing result,

\displaystyle k^{2}\,=\,\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+\left(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}\right)^{2}+\kappa_{2}^{2}}{9}=\frac{\kappa\cdot\kappa}{2}.

(47)

However, by definition,
\kappa_{i}=\kappa\cdot a_{i}/\ell
, meaning the middle term in eq. ​(
47
) is represented in the
a_{i}
basis. The right-hand side follows because,

\displaystyle a_{1}\otimes a_{1}-\frac{a_{1}\otimes a_{2}+a_{2}\otimes a_{1}}{2}+a_{2}\otimes a_{2}=\frac{9\ell^{2}}{4}I.

(48)

For the direct continuum model, the neighbor vectors,
r_{j}=\ell(\cos\!\left(j\pi/3\right),\sin\!\left(j\pi/3\right))
. A “West” vertex,
v_{W}
, links to three “East” vertices at
v_{W}+r_{j}
with
j\in\{0,2,4\}
. Likewise, an East vertex,
v_{E}
, links to three West vertices at
v_{E}+r_{j}
for
j\in\{1,3,5\}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle R(v_{W})\,=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=\text{even}}r_{j}\otimes r_{j}\,=\,\frac{3\ell}{2}I,

(49)

\displaystyle R(v_{E})\,=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{j=\text{odd }}r_{j}\otimes r_{j}\,=\,\frac{3\ell}{2}I.

(50)

Because
\mu(v)=2\ell^{-2}/\sqrt{27}
and
R_{E}=R_{W}\propto I
, we again have eq. ​(
19
).

V.5
Truncated Square Lattice

By chance, the hexagonal graph has a constant
R
tensor despite having multiple types of vertex neighborhood.
Here, we present a slightly more complicated example with a well-defined continuum limit but without (initially) a well-behaved
R
tensor. Fixing this complication leads to a more general strategy that works for more general random graphs.

Figure
4
(a) shows truncated square graph with
V_{0}=\{v_{N},v_{S},v_{E},v_{W}\}
and figure
4
(b) shows the adjacency structure. In this case, eq. ​(
28
) is a
4\times 4
system over
V_{0}
,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-3\gamma&e^{i\vartheta_{2}}&1&1\\
e^{-i\vartheta_{2}}&-3\gamma&1&1\\
1&1&-3\gamma&e^{i\vartheta_{1}}\\
1&1&e^{-i\vartheta_{1}}&-3\gamma\end{bmatrix}\!\!\begin{bmatrix}f_{N}\\
f_{S}\\
f_{E}\\
f_{W}\end{bmatrix}\,=\,0,

(51)

with lattice basis vectors

\displaystyle a_{1}\,=\,(\alpha\,\ell,0),\qquad a_{2}\,=\,(0,\alpha\,\ell),

(52)

where
\ell
is the edge length and
\alpha=1+\sqrt{2}
; see figure ​
4
. The dispersion relation is,

\displaystyle 81\gamma^{4}-54\gamma^{2}-24\,\gamma_{\text{C}}\,\gamma-4\gamma_{\text{O}}+1=0.

(53)

where we define the respective cardinal and ordinal spectral parameters from §
V.2
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{C}}=\frac{\cos(\vartheta_{1})+\cos(\vartheta_{2})}{2},\quad\gamma_{\text{O}}=\cos(\vartheta_{1})\cos(\vartheta_{2}).

(54)

Notably, the truncated square lattice reduces to the cardinal square lattice as the diagonal edges shrink to zero, and all the NSEW vertices become a single vertex. Likewise, the lattice reduces to the ordinal case as the vertical and horizontal edges shrink to zero.

This particular lattice is the first example of behavior that does not fit directly within the homogeneous
R
-tensor framework. In particular, as
\ell\to 0
,

\displaystyle k^{2}\,=\,\frac{\alpha^{2}}{6}\frac{\kappa\cdot\kappa}{2},

(55)

The limiting dispersion relation suggests a different scaling of the standard Laplace operator than anything up to this point.
We cannot obtain an extra factor like
\alpha^{2}/6\approx 0.97
from the continuum PDE in its current form.

Figure 4:
Construction and NEP setup for the truncated square graph. (a) The orthogonal length
(1+\sqrt{2})\ell
lattice vectors
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
translate the diamond-shaped unit graph
G_{0}
. (b) The base vertices in
V_{0}=\{v_{N},v_{S},v_{E},v_{W}\}
each have three neighbors: two base vertices and one translated base vertex.

To obtain a simple rescaled Laplace operator, we require
R\propto I
, which gives
d^{-1}\Delta
after dividing out by the trace.
However, the
R
tensor has no apparent well-defined pointwise limit in this case.
For the edge vectors,
r_{j}=(\cos\!\left(j\pi/4\right),\sin\!\left(j\pi/4\right))
, North vertices have
j\in\{2,5,7\}
; South has
j\in\{1,3,6\}
; East has
j\in\{0,3,5\}
; and West has
j\in\{1,4,7\}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle R(v_{N})\,=\,R(v_{S})

\displaystyle\,=\,\ell\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&2\end{bmatrix},

(56)

\displaystyle R(v_{E})\,=\,R(v_{W})

\displaystyle\,=\,\ell\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

(57)

These represent an
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\ell\right)
oscillation in
R
at the scale of a vertex neighborhood, with no obvious well-defined limit as
\ell\to 0
. However, we reiterate the overall system has a well-defined continuum behavior seen in eq. ​(
55
).
In the following section, we resolve the issue using a mixed discrete-continuum
homogenization
technique to generate a well-defined renormalized local
R
tensor.

VI
Homogenization

We express
f(v)
as an unknown function
F(x)
of the macroscale,
x
(defined below), displaced by an also unknown microscale “corrector” vector
\xi_{v}

Berlyand and Rybalko 2018
,

\displaystyle f(v)\,=\,F(x)+\xi_{v}\cdot\nabla_{\!x}F(x),

(58)

where
\nabla_{\!x}
is the macroscale gradient. To first order,

\displaystyle f(w)-f(v)\,=\,\left(r_{xy}+\xi_{vw}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\!x}F(x)+\mathcal{O}(r^{2},\xi^{2}),

(59)

where,
v\in x
,
w\in y
,
r_{xy}
is the tangent vector pointing from
x
to
y
, and
\xi_{vw}=\xi_{w}-\xi_{v}
.

VI.1
Partitioning and Periodization

Figure 5:
Illustration of the patching and periodization procedures: (a) For a graph
G
within an embedding space
M
, we extract a subgraph patch (red) and boundary vertices and edges (green).
We periodize the subgraph within the patch, producing the top-right periodic graph. The periodic graph comprises a base graph,
G_{x}
, translated by the lattice,
\Lambda_{x}
, analogous to (e.g.) figure ​
4
(a). In the top-right, we show
3\times 3
repeated copies of
G_{x}
(blue).
(b) After partitioning into patches, the periodization procedure identifies boundary vertices with bulk (internal) vertices. Each boundary vertex (green) maps to the closest bulk vertex (red) after wrapping around the patch, with the mapping in eq. ​(
63
).
Penultimately, the periodic edges have twice the density of the bulk edges. We, therefore, prune each periodic edge with 50% probability.

To specify macro and micro scales, we partition the vertex set,
V
, into a collection of disjoint connected vertex sets,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(V)=\{x_{i}\subseteq V\ ;\ x_{i}\cap x_{j}=\emptyset\ \ \text{for}\ \ i\neq j\}.

(60)

Each
x
corresponds to a given subgraph, where
|\mathcal{P}(V)|\leq|V|
is the number of subgraphs.
Altogether,

\displaystyle V=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{P}(V)}x.

(61)

For
x,y\in\mathcal{P}(V)
, we say
y\sim x
if there exist
v\in x
and
w\in y
with
w\sim v
.
We require that each
x
is connected.
The resulting
partition
graph with vertex set
\mathcal{P}(x)
is automatically connected because
G
is connected.
In general, partitioning is notoriously difficult with numerous applications and practical algorithms
Buluç et al. 2016
. We assume the partition is evident from the context.

With these definitions, the double summation in the bilinear form becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{(w\sim v)\in V}\,=\,\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}(V)}\sum_{(y\sim x)\in\mathcal{P}(V)}\sum_{v\in x}\sum_{(w\in y)\sim v}.

(62)

Eq. ​(
62
) sums over the full set of graph edges,
E
; both versions exactly count the number of edges twice. The homogenization procedure requires a set of independent decoupled sums at each local
x\in\mathcal{P}(V)
. However, we need suitable boundary conditions. Periodicity gives the most reliable results in this regard
Davies 2002
.

There is no unique way to
“periodize”
a given patch,
x\in\mathcal{P}(V)
.
We outline a procedure as follows (illustrated in figure
5
).
We impose a local lattice structure,
\Lambda_{x}
, which can vary across partition patches. We require the
r_{xy}\in\Lambda_{x}
. We can choose the
a_{x,i}
basis vectors to coincide with some linearly independent set of these, but this is optional. On a curved manifold (e.g. a sphere), the
r_{xy}\in\Lambda_{x}
condition requires that the lattice varies from patch to patch.

We take
x
to be the “base” vertices of the unit graph,
G_{0}
from §
V.1
,
i.e.
,
x\simeq V_{0}
. The
periodization
transports
x
and its edges to all cells within the lattice, including the nodes within
y\sim x
at the end of the boundary edges.

The next complication comes from needing to connect the boundary vertices
w\in y
to vertices
v\in x
. We identify each
w
with its nearest neighbor after periodically wrapping it around the fundamental base cell. That is, for each
(w\in y)\sim x
, we replace

\displaystyle w\,\to\,\text{argmin}\,\|x-(w\text{ mod }\Lambda_{x})\|,

(63)

which leaves
x\in\mathcal{P}(V)
closed with all edges connected.

The last complication is that the number of boundary edges is, statistically, twice as large as the original boundary distribution. The periodization works well if the resulting local graphs are statistically similar enough to the original and the local correlation length is smaller than the patch size. To remedy the double counting issue, we remove every boundary edge probability
1/2
. Alternatively, we regain the original for a periodic graph by only removing doubled vertices.

The boundary-edge reattachment and pruning procedure advantageously removes large-scale imbalances in the number of edges. Large-scale linear gradients are the biggest distinction between periodic and nonperiodic systems. Again, the periodization procedure works well if there is a bound on the degree of large-scale imbalance in the edge statistics.

VI.2
Discrete Homogenization

With the periodization, the exact sum correspondence in eq. ​(
62
) maps to a sum over locally periodic patches,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{(w\sim v)\in V}\to\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}(V)}\sum_{r\in\Lambda_{x}}\sum_{v\in x}\sum_{w+r\sim v}.

(64)

That is, the vertices in neighboring patches,
(w\in y)\sim x
, are replaced with images
w+r\in x
for
r\in\Lambda_{x}
.
Moreover, the tangent vectors pointing from
x
to
y
also map to lattice vectors,
r_{xy}\in\Lambda_{x}
.

We define the conductivity- and capacity-weighted edge lengths on each edge,
e
,

\displaystyle\ell^{(K)}_{e}=\frac{\ell_{e}}{K_{e}},\qquad\ell^{(C)}_{e}=C_{e}\,\ell_{e}.

(65)

Altogether, the two-scale bilinear form becomes

\displaystyle S(F,\xi)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}(V)}\!\!\left(\,\|\nabla_{\!x}F(x)\|_{\xi,K}^{2}-\lambda\,\mathcal{C}(x)\,F(x)^{2}\,\right),

(66)

where,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\!x}F\|_{\xi,K}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r\in\Lambda_{x}}\sum_{v\in x}\sum_{w+r\sim v}\!\!\frac{|(r+\xi_{vw})\cdot\nabla_{\!x}F|^{2}}{\ell^{(K)}_{v,w+r}},

(67)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(x)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r\in\Lambda_{x}}\sum_{v\in x}\sum_{w+r\sim v}\ell^{(C)}_{v,w+r},

(68)

which represents the sum over all edges with at least one endpoint in
x
.

Extremizing eq. ​(
67
) with respect to
\xi_{v}
for arbitrary
\nabla_{\!x}F
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\Lambda_{x}}\sum_{w+r\sim v}\frac{r+\xi_{w}-\xi_{v}}{\ell^{(K)}_{v,w+r}}\,=\,0.

(69)

We reorganize eq. ​(
69
) into a compact form using Hilbert spaces over the vertex and edge states within
x\in\mathcal{P}(V)
.

First, define the
\xi
state vector,

\displaystyle\left|\xi_{x}\right>=\sum_{v\in x}\xi_{v}\otimes\left|v\right>,

(70)

with the orthonormal basis

\displaystyle\left<v|v^{\prime}\right>\,=\,\delta_{v,v^{\prime}}.

(71)

We use the tensor product for eq. ​(
70
) because the
\xi_{v}\in T_{x}M
are themselves vectors in the local tangent space.
We define the basis for edges in terms of vertex pairs,
v,w\in x
, and a lattice vector
r\in\Lambda_{x}\subset T_{x}M
. The basis elements are defined provided
w+r\sim v
or
v-r\sim w
, with the anti-symmetry

\displaystyle\left|v,w,r\right>\,=\,-\left|w,v,-r\right>.

(72)

We define the edge Hilbert space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{x}=\text{span}\!\left\{\left|v,w,r\right>\right\},

(73)

where

\displaystyle\left<v,w,r|v^{\prime},w^{\prime},r^{\prime}\right>\,=\,\delta_{v,v^{\prime}}\,\delta_{w,w^{\prime}}\,\delta_{r,r^{\prime}}.

(74)

We define the discrete derivative operator,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r\in\Lambda_{x}}\sum_{v,w\in x}\left|v,w,r\right>\left(\left<w\right|-\left<v\right|\right),

(75)

which takes the
\xi_{w}-\xi_{v}
difference and places the value on every
v\to w+r
edge.
We include the factors of
1/2
to account for the redundancy resulting in eq. ​(
72
).
Eq. ​(
69
) also has inverse length factors, hence we define the proper transposes,

\displaystyle\left|v\right>^{*}\,=\,\left<v\right|,\quad\left|v,w,r\right>^{*}\,=\,\frac{1}{\ell^{(K)}_{v,w+r}}\left<v,w,r\right|.

(76)

That is, the vertex basis behaves in the simple way one might expect. But the edge-basis transpose is weighted such that, for any state
\left|t_{x}\right>\in\mathcal{H}_{x}
,

\displaystyle\left|t_{x}\right>^{\!*}\,=\,\left<t_{x}\right|\text{K}_{x},

(77)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}_{x}\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r\in\Lambda_{x}}\sum_{v,w\in x}\frac{1}{\ell^{(K)}_{v,w+r}}\left|v,w,r\right>\left<v,w,r\right|.

(78)

We define the right-hand side edge vector

\displaystyle\left|r_{x}\right>\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r\in\Lambda_{x}}\sum_{v,w\in x}r\otimes\left|v,w,r\right>.

(79)

Again, we use the tensor product because the lattice is a subset of the tangent space
r\in T_{x}M
.
Both
\mathrm{d}
and
\left|r_{x}\right>
are invariant under the action
(v,w,r)\to(w,v,-r)
.
Finally, eq. ​(
69
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{*}\mathrm{d}\left|\xi_{x}\right>\,=\,-\mathrm{d}^{*}\left|r_{x}\right>,

(80)

The difference operator,
\mathrm{d}
, has the constant null-vector,

\displaystyle\left|x\right>\,=\,\sum_{v\in x}\left|v\right>.

(81)

Eq. ​(
80
) is solvable (non-uniquely) because
\left<x\right|\mathrm{d}^{*}=0
. We impose the gauge-fixing condition,

\displaystyle\left<x|\xi_{x}\right>\,=\,0.

(82)

Eq. ​(
80
) is an exact discrete analogue of asymptotic homogenization for PDEs
Allaire 1992
;
Nguetseng 2003
;
Nguetseng 2004
. In particular, Eq. ​(
80
) is called the “cell problem” or “corrector equation” for regularizing continuous elliptic operators with rapidly varying non-constant coefficients. Recent work also exists on discrete versions of the homogenization problem
Biskup 2011
;
Gloria 2011
. To our knowledge, no work on homogenization yet exists on the continuous limit for metric graphs.

Eq. ​(
80
) also takes the simpler form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{*}\left|t_{x}\right>=0,

(83)

where

\displaystyle\left|t_{x}\right>\,=\,\left|r_{x}\right>+\mathrm{d}\left|\xi_{x}\right>,

(84)

which implies,
\left|t_{x}\right>=(1-\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{d}^{*}\mathrm{d})^{+}\mathrm{d}^{*})\left|r_{x}\right>
where
\mathrm{d}^{+}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.
However,
\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{d}^{*}\mathrm{d})^{+}\mathrm{d}^{*}=\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{d}^{+}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|t_{x}\right>\,=\,(1-\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{d}^{+})\left|r_{x}\right>

(85)

Note the vectors
r_{x}\in\Lambda_{x}\subset T_{x}M
. But in general,
t_{x}\in T_{x}M
, but not necessarily the lattice.
Eq. ​(
83
) is also equivalent to the Hodge decomposition,

\displaystyle\left|r_{x}\right>=-\mathrm{d}\left|\xi_{x}\right>+\delta\left|\phi_{x}\right>,

(86)

where
\delta^{*}
is the “cycle-space” operator
Gross et al. 2018
such that

\displaystyle\delta^{*}\mathrm{d}=0.

(87)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\delta^{*}\delta\left|\phi_{x}\right>=\delta^{*}\left|r_{x}\right>,

(88)

however,
\delta
has linearly independent columns (
\left|\phi_{x}\right>
has no gauge freedom), therefore
\delta^{*}\delta
is directly invertible and,

\displaystyle\left|t_{x}\right>=\delta(\delta^{*}\delta)^{-1}\delta^{*}\left|r_{x}\right>=\delta\,\delta^{+}\left|r_{x}\right>,

(89)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{d}^{+}+\delta\,\delta^{+}=1

(90)

Recall
r,\xi_{v}\in T_{x}M
and therefore
\left|t_{x}\right>\in T_{x}M\otimes\mathcal{H}_{x}
. Therefore, the regularized edge conductivity tensor,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(x)

\displaystyle\,=\,\mathrm{tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{x}}\!\!\left(\left|t_{x}\right>\otimes\left|t_{x}\right>\right),

(91)

where
\mathrm{tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{x}}
represents the partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{x}
with respect to the metric,
\mathrm{K}_{x}
. That is, for any
\left|e\right>,\left|f\right>\in\mathcal{H}_{x}
, and
t,s\in T_{x}M
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{x}}\!\!\left((t\otimes\left|e\right>)\otimes(s\otimes\left|f\right>)\right)\,=\,\left<e\right|\mathrm{K}_{x}\!\left|f\right>\,t\otimes s.

(92)

Unlike in the original case, we lose the direct connection between
\text{tr}\!\left({\mathcal{K}}\right)
and
\mathcal{C}
. In all of our examples, we observe
\text{tr}\!\left({\mathcal{K}}\right)\leq\mathcal{C}/d
(for
d
the dimension of
M
); we don’t know if this holds in general.

With all definitions in place for
x\in\mathcal{P}(V)
, we define the regularized action functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(F)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\big[\nabla_{\!x}F(x)\cdot\mathcal{K}(x)\cdot\nabla_{\!x}F(x)-

\displaystyle\lambda\,\mathcal{C}(x)\,F(x)^{2}\big]\,\mu(x)\,\text{d}{x},

(93)

where
\mu(x)=|x|/\mathrm{vol}(x)
is local vertex number density,
|x|=\left<x|x\right>
is the number of vertices in
x
and
\mathrm{vol}(x)
is the volume that
x
occupies within the manifold,
M
. Taking the variational derivative once more,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mu\,\mathcal{C}}\nabla_{x}\cdot(\mu\,\mathcal{K}\cdot\nabla_{x}F)=\lambda\,F.

(94)

Finally, we define an overall scalar
diffusivity
as a measure of the homogenization

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\,=\,\frac{\text{tr}\!\left({\mathcal{K}}\right)}{\mathcal{C}},

(95)

which we use as a standard diagnostic in the examples. In the perfectly homogenous examples that do not require homogenization,
\mathcal{D}=1/d
, where
d
is the dimension of space. Therefore, we define the
effective dimension
,

\displaystyle d_{\text{eff.}}\,=\,\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}},

(96)

which is analogous to a fractal dimension for the underlying graph.
In SM
sup 2025
, we rederive the anomalous diffusivity from eq. ​(
55
) for the truncated square graph.

VIII
Conclusions

We have introduced and studied a continuum limit for metric graphs, capturing the mesoscopic behavior of networks whose edge lengths become small and whose vertices fill an embedding space at high density. By starting from the standard Laplace-type differential equations on each edge and implementing Kirchhoff conditions at each vertex, we showed that, under suitable dense-graph conditions, the resulting macroscopic system can be described by a continuous-space PDE along with boundary conditions inherited from the network structure.

Several examples illustrate how these “graph materials” behave and converge to continuum models. The axisymmetric spiderweb shows excellent agreement with PDE solutions despite anisotropy. Periodic lattices (square, rectangular, hexagonal, and more complex truncated square networks) confirm that the leading-order continuum operator is consistent with the expected Laplacian, albeit with dimension-dependent prefactors. We then introduced a discrete homogenization procedure, inspired by continuum homogenization theory, to handle non-uniform local neighborhoods — most prominently demonstrated by the truncated square lattice example, in which naive local tensors would otherwise appear inconsistent.

Random graphs (Delaunay triangulations, geometric graphs, and aperiodic monotilings) further verify that moderately dense irregular embeddings produce meaningful PDE-like solutions with clear convergence of graph modes to their continuum counterparts. An interesting “effective dimension” emerged in these comparisons, reflecting the interplay of local edge geometry and global connectivity. Such results bolster confidence in using macroscopic PDEs to model network transport, wave propagation, or diffusion in intricate media — ranging from synthetic materials to biological tissues, such as vascular or neuronal networks.

In parallel to established “constitutive equation” frameworks in fields like porous-media theory (Darcy–Brinkman
Brinkman 1949
;
Weinbaum et al. 2003
;
Holter et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2022
), vascular networks (Murray’s law
Murray 1926
), and nematic liquid crystals (Q-tensors
De Gennes 1971
;
Schopohl and Sluckin 1987
;
Wensink et al. 2012
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Mackay et al. 2020
), the present metric-graph continuum limit supplies a way to derive effective PDE parameters (edge conductivity tensor,
\mathcal{K}
; capacity,
\mathcal{C}
; and vertex density,
\mu
) directly from microscopic network properties. Future applications may combine this PDE-based viewpoint with empirically informed conductivity tensors and capacities in modeling (e.g.) transport through complex neuron tissues or constructing “edge-thickness” laws that respond to evolving network morphologies.

Otherwise, nonlinearity stands out as the next significant frontier. The possibility that local graph parameters, such as edge capacities, might change in response to global fields suggests a range of nonlinear PDEs with dynamically updated coefficients, determined from first principles from the underlying local structure. Beyond networks in the brain or circulatory systems, one might envision analogous “graph-material” models in urban infrastructure or geophysical fluid flows, where fine-scale connectivity transitions lead to rich emergent behavior on larger scales.
