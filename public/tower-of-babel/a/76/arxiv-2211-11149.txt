Higher modularity of elliptic curves over function fields
Adam Logan, Jared Weinstein, with an appendix by Masato Kuwata

Abstract
We investigate a notion of “higher modularity” for elliptic curves over function fields. Given such an elliptic curve E E and an integer r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 , we say that E E is r r -modular when there is an algebraic correspondence between a stack of r r -legged shtukas, and the r r -fold product of E E considered as an elliptic surface. The (known) case r = 1 r=1 is analogous to the notion of modularity for elliptic curves over 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} . Our main theorem is that if E / 𝐅 q ​ ( t ) E/\mathbf{F}_{q}(t) is a nonisotrivial elliptic curve whose conductor has degree 4, then E E is 2-modular. Ultimately, the proof uses properties of K3 surfaces. Along the way we prove a result of independent interest: A K3 surface admits a finite morphism to a Kummer surface attached to a product of elliptic curves if and only if its Picard lattice is rationally isometric to the Picard lattice of such a Kummer surface.

Introduction

Let
K
be a function field over a finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, and let
E/K
be an elliptic curve. In this article we investigate a notion of “higher modularity” for
E/K
.

1.1
Analytic modularity, geometric modularity

Let us briefly recall the meaning of modularity for an elliptic curve
E
over the rational numbers
\mathbf{Q}
. Let
N
be the conductor of
E
. Following
[
Ulm03
]
, we might distinguish between two notions of modularity for
E/\mathbf{Q}
:

1.

(Analytic modularity) There exists a cuspidal holomorphic form
f
of weight
2
and level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
such that
L(f,\chi,s)=L(E,\chi,s)
for all Dirichlet characters
\chi
.

2.

(Geometric/motivic modularity) There exists a non-constant morphism
X_{0}(N)\rightarrow E
defined over
\mathbf{Q}
, where
X_{0}(N)
is the modular curve of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

The two notions are equivalent. If
E
is geometrically modular, the required cusp form
f
may be found by pulling back a nonzero holomorphic differential form on
E
through
X_{0}(N)\rightarrow E
. Conversely, if
E
is analytically modular, there exists an elliptic curve quotient
\Jac X_{0}(N)\rightarrow E^{\prime}
corresponding to its cusp form
f
. The equality of
L
-functions implies that the representations of
\Gal(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}/\mathbf{Q})
on the rational
\ell
-adic Tate modules of
E
and
E^{\prime}
are isomorphic. A theorem of Faltings
[
Fal83
]
implies that
E
and
E^{\prime}
are isogenous, from which we deduce that
E
is geometrically modular.

In any case, the conjecture of Shimura-Tanayama is a theorem
[
BCDT01
]
; every elliptic curve
E/\mathbf{Q}
is analytically modular, hence geometrically modular. An important consequence is that the Heegner point on
X_{0}(N)
for an imaginary quadratic field
F
satisfying the Heegner hypothesis relative to
N
, gives rise to a Heegner point
P_{F}\in E(F)
. The celebrated theorem of Gross-Zagier
[
GZ86
]
relates the height of
P_{F}
to the derivative
L^{\prime}(E,F,1)
. The theorem of Kolyvagin
[
Kol90
]
shows that if
P_{F}
is not torsion, then it generates a finite-index subgroup of
E(F)
, and furthermore one gets finiteness of the Shafarevich-Tate group.

Let us now return to the setting of an elliptic curve
E
over a function field
K
with field of scalars
\mathbf{F}_{q}
. We assume that
E
is nonisotrivial, meaning that
j(E)\not\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
. Then
L(E,\chi,s)
is entire for every Hecke character
\chi
. (In fact
L(E,\chi,s)
is a polynomial in
q^{-s}
.) The analogue of analytic modularity for
E
is the condition that there exists a cuspidal automorphic representation
\pi
of
\GL_{2}
over
K
, such that
L(\pi,\chi,s)=L(E,\chi,s)
for all
\chi
. The analytic modularity of
E
follows from a theorem of Deligne together with Weil’s converse theorem (see
[
Ulm03
]
for a discussion).

The analogue of geometric modularity for
E
is the subject of this paper. The interest here hinges on the correct analogue for the modular curve. Let
X/\mathbf{F}_{q}
be the nonsingular projective curve with function field
K
.
Let
N
be the conductor of
E
, with support
\Sigma\subset\left\lvert X\right\rvert
. As a natural starting point, we may consider the Drinfeld modular curve
\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)
. Here
\infty\in\left\lvert X\right\rvert
is a place of split multiplicative reduction for
E
; thus
N=N_{f}+(\infty)
for a divisor
N_{f}
which is prime to
\infty
. If
A
is the ring of functions on
X
regular away from
\infty
, then
\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)
parametrizes Drinfeld
A
-modules of rank 2 endowed with
\Gamma_{0}(N_{f})
-structure. There is a morphism
\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)\rightarrow X\smallsetminus\Sigma
(sending a Drinfeld module to its “characteristic”), whose fibers are smooth curves. Let
\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)_{K}
be the generic fiber of this morphism, and let
\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)_{\overline{K}}
be its base change to an algebraic closure
\overline{K}/K
.

Drinfeld’s computation
[
Dri74
]
of the cohomology of Drinfeld modular curves shows that
H^{1}(E_{\overline{K}})
appears as a summand of
H^{1}(\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)_{\overline{K}})
. (We mean étale cohomology with
\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
-coefficients, considered as a representation of the Galois group of
K
.) We may once again apply Faltings’ theorem to conclude that there is a nonconstant morphism
\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)_{K}\rightarrow E
. If
K^{\prime}/K
is a separable quadratic extension satisfying the Heegner hypothesis relative to
E
, there is a Heegner point
P_{K^{\prime}}\in E(K^{\prime})
, whose height is related to the derivative
L^{\prime}(E/K^{\prime},1)
, see
[
YZ19
, Remark 1.5]
. If
L^{\prime}(E/K^{\prime},1)\neq 0
, then
P_{K^{\prime}}
is not a torsion point. But also we have that the rank of
E(K^{\prime})
is
\leq 1
, since the “easy inequality” of the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture is a theorem for function fields. We conclude immediately that the rank is exactly 1, and so the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer holds for
E^{\prime}/K
.

1.2
Stacks of shtukas, and the definition of higher modularity

The notion of geometric modularity in the function field setting may be generalized far beyond the Drinfeld modular curve. Namely, for
r=1,2,\dots
we have the moduli stack of
r
-legged shtukas of rank 2, which lies over the
r
-fold product
X^{r}
over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
. These were introduced by Drinfeld
[
Dri80
]
in the case
r=2
to establish the Langlands correspondence for
\GL_{2}
. For general
r
, these are examples of the spaces used by V. Lafforgue
[
Laf18
]
to prove the automorphic-to-Galois direction of the Langlands correspondence for general reductive groups.

The spaces of shtukas relevant to us are relative to the group
G=\PGL_{2}
. To define them, we need an effective divisor
N
of
X
, with support
\Sigma
. We also need a subset
\Sigma_{\infty}\subset\Sigma
, such that each
v\in\Sigma_{\infty}
appears with multiplicity 1 in
N
.
Finally, we need an integer
r\geq 1
satisfying the parity condition
\#\Sigma_{\infty}\equiv r\pmod{2}
. Following
[
YZ19
]

we let

\lambda_{r}\colon\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})\rightarrow(X\smallsetminus\Sigma)^{r}

denote the moduli stack of
G
-shtukas with
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-level structure, with
r
“moving legs” in
X\smallsetminus\Sigma
, and “fixed legs” at the places in
\Sigma_{\infty}
(these are analogous to the archimedean places in the number field setting). These legs are “minuscule”; i.e., they are the type considered by Drinfeld in his original definition. We save the precise definition of
\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
for §
2
. It is a Deligne-Mumford stack, generically smooth of relative dimension
r
over
(X\smallsetminus\Sigma)^{r}
.

For example, when
r=0
, the set
\Sigma_{\infty}
is the ramification locus of a projective quaternionic group
G^{\prime}
, and then
\Sht^{0}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
is the discrete set
G^{\prime}(K)\backslash G^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}_{K})/\Gamma^{\prime}
, for an appropriate level subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset G^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}_{K})
. When
r=1
and
\Sigma_{\infty}=\left\{\infty\right\}
is a singleton, one of the connected components of
\Sht^{1}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\left\{\infty\right\})
is isomorphic over
X\smallsetminus\Sigma
to a quotient of
\DrMod(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)
; this is the shtuka correspondence
[
Mum78
]
.

We can now start to describe our notion of “higher modularity” for our elliptic curve
E/K
. Let
U=X\smallsetminus\Sigma
, and let
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow U
be the family of elliptic curves with generic fiber
E
. The idea goes like this: Consider the family
h^{1}(\mathcal{E})
of motives over
U
. Let
h^{1}(\mathcal{E})^{\boxtimes r}
be its
r
th external tensor power, meaning the family of motives over
U^{r}
whose fiber over
(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})
is
h^{1}(\mathcal{E}_{s_{1}})\otimes\cdots\otimes h^{1}(\mathcal{E}_{s_{r}})
. For
E
to be
r
-modular should mean that
h^{1}(\mathcal{E})^{\boxtimes r}
is a quotient of the (compactly supported) cohomology of a stack of
r
-legged shtukas.

The precise condition involves the existence of a degree 0 algebraic correspondence between
\Sht^{r}:=\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
and
\mathcal{E}^{r}
. By a degree 0 algebraic correspondence, we mean a
\mathbf{Q}
-linear combination of irreducible closed substacks

Z\subset\Sht^{r}\times_{U^{r}}\mathcal{E}^{r}

such that each geometric fiber of
Z\rightarrow U^{r}
has dimension
r
.

Let
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})
be a geometric point of
U^{r}
, and let
\ell
be a prime not dividing
q
. The
\ell
-adic cycle class of
Z_{s}
gives rise to a map

p_{Z}(s)\colon H^{r}_{c}(\Sht^{r}_{s},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})\rightarrow\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}H^{1}(\mathcal{E}_{s_{i}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})

(1.2.1)

(Explanation: the
\ell
-adic cycle class of
Z_{s}
belongs to
H^{2r}(\Sht^{r}_{s}\times\mathcal{E}^{r}_{s},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})(r)
, which maps via the Künneth isomorphism to
H^{r}(\Sht^{r}_{s},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})(r)\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}H^{1}(\mathcal{E}_{s_{i}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
. Poincaré duality identifies the latter with the space of maps as in (
1.2.1
).) For
Z=\sum_{j}a_{j}Z_{j}
a formal
\mathbf{Q}
-linear combination of correspondences
Z_{j}
as above, we define
p_{Z}(s)=\sum_{j}a_{j}p_{Z_{j}}(s)
.

Definition 1.2.1
.

Let
K/\mathbf{F}_{q}
be a function field, and let
E/K
be a nonisotrivial elliptic curve whose conductor
N

has support
\Sigma
. Let
U=X\smallsetminus\Sigma
, and let
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow U
be the family of elliptic curves with generic fiber
E
. Let
\Sigma_{\infty}\subset\Sigma
be a subset of places appearing with multiplicity 1 in
N
, satisfying
\#\Sigma_{\infty}\equiv r\pmod{2}
. We say that
E/K
is
(r,\Sigma_{\infty})
-modular
if there exists a degree 0 algebraic correspondence
Z
between
\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
and
\mathcal{E}^{r}
such that for all geometric points
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})
of
U^{r}
, the map
p_{Z}(s)
of (
1.2.1
) is surjective.
We say that
E/K
is
r
-modular
if it is
(r,\Sigma_{\infty})
-modular for all
\Sigma_{\infty}
satisfying the hypotheses above.

Remark 1.2.2
.

When
r
is odd, we must assume that
E/K
has at least one place of multiplicative reduction in order to have an interesting notion of
r
-modularity.

Conjecture 1.2.3
.

A nonisotrivial elliptic curve
E/K
is
r
-modular for all
r\geq 1
.

The main goal of this article is to give the first examples of 2-modular and 3-modular elliptic curves. Our results apply to what is arguably the simplest class of elliptic fibrations, namely the
extremal rational elliptic fibrations

\mathcal{E}\rightarrow X
.
Here “rational” means that
\mathcal{E}
is birational to
\mathbf{P}^{2}
(and consequently
X\cong\mathbf{P}^{1}
), and “extremal” means that
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow X
has Mordell-Weil rank 0. (The generic rational elliptic fibration has Mordell-Weil rank 8.) For such a fibration we necessarily have
\deg N=4
and
L(E,s)=1
identically. Conversely, if an elliptic fibration
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
has degree 4 conductor, then it is extremal rational.

The extremal rational elliptic fibrations
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow X
are classified in
[
MP86
]
. The number of singular geometric fibers of a extremal rational elliptic fibration is either two, three, or four, with two occurring only if
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow X
is isotrivial or if it has wild fibers (which happens only in characteristics 2 and 3). A nonisotrivial extremal rational elliptic fibration always has at least one multiplicative fiber, and so there is always a nontrivial notion of higher modularity for
\mathcal{E}
.

Theorem 1.2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow X
be a nonisotrivial extremal rational elliptic fibration with generic fiber
E
. Then
E
is 2-modular.

In the next two subsections we will (a) explain why one should believe Conjecture
1.2.3
, and (b) sketch the proof of Theorem
1.2.4
.

1.3
Relation to the Tate conjecture

We attempt to motivate the definition of higher modularity. Using analytic modularity (which is a theorem) combined with prior results on the cohomology of stacks of shtukas, we will reduce Conjecture
1.2.3
to a sufficiently strong version of the Tate conjecture. Let
\eta=\Spec K
be the generic point of
X
, and let
\overline{\eta}=\Spec\overline{K}
be a geometric generic point.

We use the normalization of the global Langlands correspondence found in
[
Dri80
]
, adapted to the group
G=\PGL_{2}
. This is a bijection between isomorphism classes:

•

Cuspidal automorphic representations
\pi
of
G
with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}
, and

•

Irreducible representations
\sigma\colon\Gal(\overline{K}/K)\rightarrow\GL_{2}\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}
, such that
\det\sigma=\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}(-1)
.

Suppose that
\pi
and
\sigma
correspond. Let
N
be the conductor of
\pi
, in the sense that
\dim\pi^{\Gamma_{0}(N)}=1
, and let
\Sigma
be the support of
N
. Let
U=X\smallsetminus\Sigma
. Then
\sigma
factors through a representation of
\pi_{1}(U,\overline{\eta})
of conductor
N
. The correspondence is characterized by the property that for all
v\in U
with residue field
\mathbf{F}_{q_{v}}
and Frobenius
\Frob_{v}
, we have
L(s-1/2,\pi_{v})=\det(1-\sigma(\Frob_{v})q_{v}^{-s})^{-1}
.

Remark 1.3.1
.

We have chosen this normalization of the global Langlands correspondence so that the
H^{1}
of a nonisotrivial elliptic curve over
K
corresponds to a cuspidal automorphic representation of
G
. Under the “usual” normalization of the correspondence, the Langlands parameter of an automorphic representation of
G
is a homomorphism
\Gal(\overline{K}/K)\rightarrow\SL_{2}\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}

G

\GL_{2}
saves us certain notational headaches having to do with the center of
\GL_{2}
.

We now reference some facts about the relation between the cohomology of stacks of shtukas and the global Langlands correspondence. Let

, write
\eta^{r}
for the

\eta
over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, and write
\eta_{r}
for the generic point of

. Let
\overline{\eta}_{r}\rightarrow\eta_{r}
be an algebraic closure lying over
\overline{\eta}^{r}\rightarrow\eta^{r}
. Then there is a homomorphism

\pi_{1}(U^{r},\overline{\eta}_{r})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(U,\overline{\eta})^{r}

(1.3.1)

The cohomology

admits commuting actions of

and the Hecke algebra

for

. The following proposition is along the lines of the Kottwitz conjecture for Shimura varieties. It appears later as Proposition

Let

Now suppose

\sigma

1.4
Strategy of proof of 2-modularity

Let
N
be an effective divisor of
X=\mathbf{P}^{1}_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}
of degree 4 with support
\Sigma
. As before, let
U=X\smallsetminus\Sigma
. Let
\Sigma_{\infty}\subset\Sigma
be a set of places appearing with multiplicity 1 in
N
, such that
\#\Sigma_{\infty}
is even. The stack of shtukas in this situation can be described with simple equations, at least up to birational equivalence. More precisely, each fiber of

\Sht^{2}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})\rightarrow U^{2}

is birational to an elliptic surface (Theorem
2.5.1
).
For

We prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.4.1
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface over an algebraically closed field. Assume there is an isometry
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong\Pic K\otimes\mathbf{Q}

K

\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
, where
E_{1},E_{2}
are nonisogenous elliptic curves. Then there exists a finite morphism from
S
to a Kummer surface of this form.

Remark 1.4.2
.

Theorem
1.4.1

[

which states that if
S
and
S^{\prime}
are two complex K3 surfaces, then a Hodge isometry
H^{2}(S,\mathbf{Q})\cong H^{2}(S^{\prime},\mathbf{Q})

S

S^{\prime}
.

Recall our family of K3 surfaces
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow U^{2}

\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\rightarrow U

Theorem 1.4.3

Assume

be the Legendre elliptic curve, with Weierstrass equation

This

1.5
Application: Heegner-Drinfeld cycles on
\mathcal{E}^{r}

This article was inspired by the theorem of Yun-Zhang
[
YZ19
]
relating
Heegner-Drinfeld cycles
on stacks of shtukas, to the Taylor expansion of
L
-functions of automorphic forms. We explain here the contact between higher modularity and the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer for elliptic curves over function fields.

As before, we let
X
be a curve over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
with function field
K
. Let
f\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow X
be an elliptic fibration with generic fiber
E/K
. Let
\ell
be a prime not dividing
q
. Recall
[
Tat95
]
that the inequality
\ord_{s=1}L(E/K,s)\geq\rank E(K)
is known unconditionally, and that the following are equivalent:

1.

The conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) holds for
E/K
; i.e.,
\ord_{s=1}L(E/K,s)=\rank E(K)
.

2.

The Tate conjecture holds for the surface
\mathcal{E}/\mathbf{F}_{q}
. By this, we mean that
H^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))^{\Fr_{q}=1}
is spanned by classes of divisors.

3.

The Tate-Shafarevich group
\Sha(E/K)
is finite.

We briefly review the connection between these statements. Let
\NS(\mathcal{E})
be the Néron-Severi group of
\mathcal{E}
(divisors modulo algebraic equivalence). Define a decreasing two-step filtration
\Fil^{i}\NS(\mathcal{E})
, with
\Fil^{1}\NS(\mathcal{E})
the subgroup of divisors whose intersection pairing with a fiber of
f
is zero, and with
\Fil^{2}\NS(\mathcal{E})
the subgroup generated by irreducible components of fibers. Then
\NS(\mathcal{E})/\Fil^{1}\NS(\mathcal{E})\cong\mathbf{Z}
and
\Fil^{1}\NS(\mathcal{E})/\Fil^{2}\NS(\mathcal{E})\cong E(K)
, the Mordell-Weil group. The identity section splits off the first filtrant:
\NS(\mathcal{E})\cong\Fil^{1}\NS(\mathcal{E})\oplus\mathbf{Z}
.

Meanwhile, the Leray spectral sequence for the composition
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow X\rightarrow\Spec\mathbf{F}_{q}
degenerates on the second page. As a result there is a decreasing two-step filtration
\Fil^{i}H^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))
with graded pieces
H^{2}(X_{{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}}},f_{*}\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))\cong\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
,
H^{1}(X_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},R^{1}f_{*}\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))
,
H^{0}(X_{{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}}},R^{2}f_{*}\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))
. This filtration is preserved by the action of Frobenius
\Fr_{q}
. Once again, the identity section splits off the first filtrant:
H^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))\cong\Fil^{1}H^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))\oplus\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
.

The cycle class map
\NS(\mathcal{E})\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}\rightarrow H^{2}(\mathcal{E}_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))
respects the filtrations on either side, and induces an isomorphism on the first and third graded pieces. On the second graded pieces, we get an injective map

E(K)\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}\rightarrow V:=H^{1}(X_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},R^{1}f_{*}\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}(1))

which lands in the Frobenius-fixed part
V^{\Fr_{q}=1}
. The Tate conjecture for
\mathcal{E}/\mathbf{F}_{q}
reduces to the statement that
E(K)\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}\rightarrow V^{\Fr_{q}=1}
is an isomorphism. Note that
V^{\Fr_{q}=1}
is the Selmer group for
E/K
(with
\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}
-coefficients), and the statement that
E(K)\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}\rightarrow V^{\Fr_{q}=1}
is an isomorphism is equivalent to the statement that the
\ell
-primary part of
\Sha(E/K)
is finite.

The vector space
V
is related to the
L
-function via:

L(E/K,s)=\det(1-q^{1-s}\Fr_{q}|V).

Thus
\ord_{s=1}L(E/K,s)
is the dimension of the generalized
1
-eigenspace for
\Fr_{q}
acting on
V
, which a priori may be larger than
V^{\Fr_{q}=1}
. Thus we have inequalities:

\rank E(K)\leq\dim V^{\Fr_{q}=1}\leq\ord_{s=1}L(E/K,s)

If BSD holds for
E/K
, then the leftmost and rightmost quantities are equal, and consequently the Tate conjecture holds for
\mathcal{E}
, and as a bonus we also find that the generalized
1
-eigenspace for
\Fr_{q}
on
V
coincides with the eigenspace. Conversely, if the Tate conjecture holds for
\mathcal{E}
, Tate shows that the generalized
1
-eigenspace for
\Fr_{q}
on
V
coincides with the eigenspace, and therefore BSD holds for
E/K
. Note that the statement that the generalized
1
-eigenspace for
\Fr_{q}
on
V
coincides with the eigenspace is a consequence of the general conjecture that
\Fr_{q}
acts semisimply on all
H^{i}(\mathcal{E}_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
; this statement is sometimes packaged along with the Tate conjecture.

Let
N
be the conductor of
E
, and let
\Sigma
be the support of
N
. Assume that
N
is multiplicity-free. Let
\Sigma_{\infty}\subset\Sigma
be a subset. For each

with the same parity as
\#\Sigma_{\infty}
, we have a space of

, without removing

[

]
. In particular

is a smooth Deligne-Mumford stack.)

Let

be a quadratic extension corresponding to a (possibly branched) double cover

is geometrically connected over

, and that the branch points of

Let

On the other hand, the main result

away from
\bigcup_{i}^{r}\gamma_{i}
is bounded by
W_{\mu}
if it factors into
f_{r}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
, where
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
are as above. Comparing degrees of vector bundles, we find that such an isomorphism can exist only if
\sum_{i}\mu_{i}=\deg{\mathcal{G}}_{r}-\deg{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
.

From now on we fix

G=\PGL_{2},

so that
\hat{G}=\SL_{2}
. Note that
\mathrm{std}
is self-dual when considered as a representation of
\hat{G}
. Let us simply write

\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})

for the moduli stack of
G
-shtukas with
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-structure and archimedean places
\Sigma_{\infty}
which are bounded by

W_{r}:=\mathrm{std}^{\boxtimes r}.

We have an isomorphism

\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})\cong\Sht_{\GL_{2}}^{W_{\mu}}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})/\Pic_{X}(\mathbf{F}_{q}),

where
\mu
is any vector of signs satisfying
\sum_{i}\mu_{i}=\#\Sigma_{\infty}
. Therefore a necessary condition for
\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
to be nonempty is the congruence

r\equiv\#\Sigma_{\infty}\pmod{2}.

The stack
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
is a Deligne-Mumford stack, and the morphism

\lambda^{r}\colon\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})\rightarrow(X\smallsetminus\Sigma)^{r}

sending a shtuka to its legs
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}
is generically smooth of relative dimension
r
.

Remark 2.2.2
.

Keeping the intermediate
{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
as above, one obtains the notion of an iterated shtuka. The moduli space of iterated
\GL_{2}
-shtukas bounded by
\mu=(1,-1)
was originally considered by Drinfeld
[
Dri80
]
. If we had worked with iterated shtukas, the morphism
\lambda^{r}
would be smooth over
(X\smallsetminus\Sigma)^{r}
. The spaces of iterated and non-iterated shtukas coincide over the locus in
X^{r}
where the legs are pairwise distinct.

Example 2.2.3

(Relation to Drinfeld modular curves)
.

In the special case that
r=1
and
\Sigma_{\infty}=\left\{\infty\right\}
is a singleton, the stack
\Sht^{1}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\infty)
is related to a Drinfeld modular curve. Namely, decompose
N
as
N=N_{f}+(\infty)
, and let
\mathrm{DrMod}(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)
classify Drinfeld
A
-modules, where
A
is the ring of functions on
X
regular away from
\infty
, equipped with a
\Gamma_{0}(N_{f})
-structure. The precise relationship is that there is an isomorphism

\Sht^{1}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\infty)\cong\mathrm{DrMod}(\Gamma_{0}(N_{f});\infty)/\Pic^{\circ}_{X}(\mathbf{F}_{q}).

For
r>1
the stack
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
is generally not of finite type. To produce finite-type stacks, we need to pass to truncations. There is a map

\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})\rightarrow\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))

sending a shtuka as in Definition
2.2.1
to its
G
-torsor
{\mathcal{F}}
. For each
i\geq 0
, let
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\leq i}
be the pullback under this map of
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\leq i}
. Then
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\leq i}
is of finite type.

2.3
Cohomology of stacks of shtukas

Here we prove Proposition
1.3.2
, which states that irreducible Langlands parameters for
G
appear as direct summands in the cohomology of stacks of shtukas. We will need the main results of
[
Xue20a
]
and
[
Xue20b
]
, which we now review.

Recall our conventions on generic points:
\eta
(resp.,
\eta_{r}
) is the generic point of
X
(resp,
X^{r}
), and
\overline{\eta}_{r}\rightarrow\eta_{r}
is an algebraic closure lying over
\overline{\eta}^{r}\rightarrow\eta^{r}
. Let
\Sigma
be the support of
N
, and let
U=X\smallsetminus\Sigma
.

The truncated stack of shtukas
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\leq i}
is of finite type over
U^{r}
, so it makes sense to define the cohomology with compact supports

H^{r}_{c}(\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\leq i}_{\overline{\eta}_{r}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),

a finite-dimensional
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}
-vector space admitting an action of
\pi_{1}(U^{r},\overline{\eta}_{r})
. Define

H^{r}_{c}(\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})_{\overline{\eta}_{r}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}):=\varinjlim_{i\geq 0}H^{r}_{c}(\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\leq i}_{\overline{\eta}_{r}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),

and abbreviate

H^{r}_{c}:=H^{r}_{c}(\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})_{\overline{\eta}_{r}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}).

Then
H^{r}_{c}
admits an action of the Hecke algebra
T_{0}(N)
which commutes with the action of
\pi_{1}(U^{r},\overline{\eta}_{r})
. Let
(H^{r}_{c})^{\text{cusp}}\subset H^{r}_{c}
be the cuspidal part. Assuming that
\Sigma_{\infty}=\emptyset
, a theorem of Xue
[
Xue20a
, Theorem 5.0.1]
shows that
(H^{r}_{c})^{\text{cusp}}
is finite-dimensional, and then Drinfeld’s lemma extends the action of
\pi_{1}(U^{r},\overline{\eta}_{r})
on
(H^{r}_{c})^{\text{cusp}}
to an action of
\pi_{1}(U,\overline{\eta})^{r}
.

Proposition 2.3.1
.

Assume that Xue’s theorem
[
Xue20a
, Theorem 5.0.1]
extends to the case of arbitrary
(r,\Sigma_{\infty})
. As a representation of
T_{0}(N)\times\pi_{1}(U,\overline{\eta})^{r}
, there is a decomposition

(H^{r}_{c})^{\mathrm{cusp}}=\bigoplus_{\pi}\pi^{\Gamma_{0}(N)}\otimes\sigma^{\boxtimes r}_{\pi},

where
\pi
runs over cuspidal representations of
G
containing a
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-fixed vector, and
\sigma_{\pi}
is the 2-dimensional representation of
\pi_{1}(U,\overline{\eta})
corresponding to
\pi
under the global Langlands correspondence.

Remark 2.3.2
.

When

and
\Sigma_{\infty}=\emptyset
, (

) is a special case of a theorem of Drinfeld
[
Dri80
]
.

Proof.

The formalism of excursion operators developed in
[
Laf18
]
furnishes a decomposition of

-modules

indexed by isomorphism classes of Langlands parameters
\sigma\colon\pi_{1}(U,\overline{\eta})\rightarrow\GL_{2}\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}
.

Let
\sigma
be an irreducible Langlands parameter for
G
. In the case that
\Sigma_{\infty}=\emptyset
, Corollaire 2.1 to
[
LZ18
, Proposition 1.2]
identifies

with
\pi^{\Gamma_{0}(N)}\otimes\sigma^{\boxtimes r}
, where
\pi
corresponds to
\sigma
under the Langlands correspondence. (We remark here that our

is the space denoted

is the external tensor product of

For
r
even and
\Sigma_{\infty}
nonempty, let

be the form of

corresponding to the quaternion algebra over

which is ramified exactly at
\Sigma_{\infty}

as a group scheme over

and reductive elsewhere. Then our stack of shtukas

for the group

(with no archimedean places). Here we have written

for the part of

away from
\Sigma_{\infty}
. Under the Langlands correspondence,

corresponds to a cuspidal representation

of

.
We now apply
[

, Proposition 1.2]
:
there exists a finite-dimensional

-module

such that for all even

-modules:

(Note that the group

in

Setting

is nonzero. Thus for any even

we find a nonzero
\sigma
-isotypic component in

.

For
r
odd, let
\infty\in\Sigma_{\infty}
, and let
G^{\prime}
be the form of

corresponding to the quaternion algebra
D/K
ramified exactly at
\Sigma_{\infty}\smallsetminus\left\{\infty\right\}
.
Then
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})
is isomorphic to a stack of shtukas

\pi^{\prime}
be the automorphic representation of

corresponding to

The same technique as in
[

]
applies (conditionally on the extension of Xue’s theorem to this case) to give a finite-dimensional

such that for all odd

Setting

is the cohomology of either a Drinfeld modular curve (if

∎

In the situation of Proposition
1.3.2
, suppose
\sigma
is an irreducible Langlands parameter of conductor

2.3.1
shows that

appears as a summand of

To proceed, we require the result
[

, Theorem 2]
. We must assume that this result applies to arbitrary pairs

. The result is that

is finitely generated over the local spherical Hecke algebra at any place of

on

to an action of

Furthermore one gets once again a decomposition over Langlands parameters, as explained in
[

, Proposition 3.7.10]
. Let

be the local spherical Hecke algebra at a place

show as before that as a

Now suppose that

is an irreducible representation of

for

,

2.4
Vector bundles with level structures on
\mathbf{P}^{1}

At this point we specialize our study of shtuka spaces to the case that the base curve
X
is the projective line. We make heavy use of the fact that the degree
d
component
\Bun_{2}^{d}
admits a dense open substack containing only one isomorphism class, namely
\mathcal{O}(d/2)^{\oplus 2}
or
\mathcal{O}((d-1)/2)\oplus\mathcal{O}((d+1)/2)
as
d
is even or odd, respectively; these are exactly the objects with instability index
\leq 1
. As a result, it becomes possible to give explicit equations for dense open substacks of the moduli stacks of shtukas.

The following lemmas, concerning
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
for divisors
N
of degree 3 and 4, exemplify the simplifications that will occur.

Lemma 2.4.1
.

Let
F
be a field, and let
X=\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}
. Let
N
be an effective divisor of degree 3. There is a dense substack
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
of
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
, such that each connected component
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{d,\omega}
(
d=0,1
) is isomorphic to a single point
\Spec F
.

Proof.

We given an explicit description of
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
in the case that
N=P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3}
where each
P_{i}
is
F
-rational, the other cases being similar. It is the locus of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}
where the instability of each fractional twist
{\mathcal{F}}(\textstyle\frac{1}{2}D)
is
\leq 1
, where
D
runs over divisors supported on
N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}
. One sees that each of its components
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{d,\omega}
is a single point. In degree 0, for instance, let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}\in\Bun_{G}^{0}(\Gamma_{0}(N))(F)
be the object with
{\mathcal{F}}
the trivial
G
-torsor. The level structure, represented by a triple of pairwise distinct elements of
\mathbf{P}^{1}(F)
, determines an object of
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
if and only if the three points in the triple are pairwise distinct. (Translated into vector bundles, this is the observation that elementary modifications of
\mathcal{O}^{\oplus 2}
at points
P_{1},P_{2}
along the lines
L_{1},L_{2}\subset F^{2}
produce
\mathcal{O}(1)\oplus\mathcal{O}(1)
generically, but will produce
\mathcal{O}(2)\oplus\mathcal{O}
if
L_{1}=L_{2}
.) As
G
acts simply transitively on such triples, we find an isomorphism
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{0,\omega}\cong\Spec F
.

∎

Lemma 2.4.2
.

Let
F
be a field, and let
X=\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}
.

1.

Let
N=P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3}+P_{4}
be a multiplicity-free divisor of degree 4. There exists a dense open substack
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
, stable under the Atkin-Lehner operators, each of whose connected components is isomorphic to
\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}\smallsetminus N
. With respect to these isomorphisms, the Atkin-Lehner involution
\AL(\textstyle\frac{1}{2}(P_{1}+P_{2})))
(here
i\neq j
are distinct indices) is the unique involution which exchanges
P_{1}
with
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
with
P_{4}
.

2.

Let
N=P_{1}+P_{2}+2P_{3}
, with
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
pairwise distinct. There exists a dense open substack
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
, stable under the Atkin-Lehner operators (supported only on
P_{1},P_{2}
), each of whose connected components is isomorphic to
\mathbf{A}^{1}_{F}
.

Proof.

(1) Let
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
be the locus of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}
where (once again) all Atkin-Lehner twists
{\mathcal{F}}(\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Sigma)
have instability index
\leq 1
. This time, the
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-level structures
{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}
on the trivial
G
-torsor correspond to quadruples of points of
\mathbf{P}^{1}
. One computes that
{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}
lies in
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
if and only if the quadruple is pairwise distinct and is not in the
G
-orbit of
(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4})
. The set of
G
-orbits of such quadruples is
P^{1}_{F}\smallsetminus N
. (The claim about Atkin-Lehner involutions is not used in the sequel and we omit its proof.)

(2) Let
\Bun_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
be the preimage of the substack described in Lemma
2.4.1
. The parameter space for
\Gamma_{0}(2P_{3})
-structures lying over a given
\Gamma_{0}(P_{3})
-structure is described by the fibers of
\Res^{2P_{3}}_{P_{3}}\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}
, which are all
\mathbf{A}^{1}_{F}
.

∎

2.5
Explicit presentations of shtuka spaces with
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-structure

The following theorem and its proof give an explicit presentation of the space of shtukas with
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-structure, where
N
is a divisor of degree 3 or 4.
It is a special case of the main results of
[
dFF20
]
. Define
\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\omega}
to be the dense open substack where the underlying
G
-bundle of the shtuka lies in the substack
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{\omega}
described by Lemmas
2.4.1
and
2.4.2
.

Theorem 2.5.1
.

Let
X=\mathbf{P}^{1}_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}
, let
r\geq 1
be an integer, and let
\Sigma_{\infty}\subset\left\lvert X\right\rvert
be a finite set of degree 1 points satisfying
\#\Sigma_{\infty}\equiv r\pmod{2}
. Let
\eta_{r}=\Spec\mathbf{F}_{q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})
be the generic point of
X^{r}
.

1.

Let
N
be an effective divisor of degree 3, such that each point of
\Sigma_{\infty}
has multiplicity 1 in
N
. The stack
\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\omega}_{\eta_{r}}
is a quasiprojective variety, each of whose connected components is rational; more precisely, there is a birational morphism from each component to
(\mathbf{P}^{1}_{\eta_{r}})^{r}
.

2.

Let
N
be a multiplicity-free effective divisor of degree 4 whose support contains
\Sigma_{\infty}
. The stack
\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\omega}_{\eta_{r}}
is a quasiprojective variety. Each of its connected components is birational to a hypersurface in
(\mathbf{P}^{1}_{\eta})^{r+1}
of degree
(2,2,\dots,2,q+1)
.

3.

Let
N=P_{1}+P_{2}+2P_{3}
be an effective divisor of degree 4, such that
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
are pairwise distinct and
\Sigma_{\infty}\subset\left\{P_{1},P_{2}\right\}
. The stack
\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{\omega}_{\eta_{r}}
is a quasiprojective variety. Each of its connected components is birational to a hypersurface in
(\mathbf{P}^{1}_{\eta})^{r+1}
of degree
(2,2,\dots,2,q)
.

Remark 2.5.2
.

There are no cusp forms on
\PGL_{2}
of conductor
N
if
\deg N=3
, whereas if
\deg N=4
and
N
is multiplicity-free, then the dimension of the space of cusp forms of conductor
N
is exactly
q
. (See
[
DF13
, §7]
for these results and related formulas.) So it is not surprising that non-rational varieties appear when passing from degree 3 to degree 4.

Remark 2.5.3
.

It is possible to extend Theorem
2.5.1
to include the cases
N=2P_{1}+2P_{2}
,
N=P_{1}+3P_{2}
, and
N=4P
. We refer the reader to
[
dFF20
]
for details.

Proof.

(1) By Lemma
2.4.1
,
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))^{d,\omega}
is a point, corresponding to an object
{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}
of
\Bun_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N))(\mathbf{F}_{q})
. Thus for a test scheme
S
, the shtukas in
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{d,\omega}(S)
classify isomorphisms of
G
-torsors with
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-structure:

f\colon{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}|_{(X\times_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}S)\backslash\cup_{i}\gamma_{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\mathrel{\smash{\meno}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}\rule{0.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}{\mathcal{F}}^{\dagger}(\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{\infty})|_{(X\times_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}S)\backslash\cup_{i}\gamma_{i}}

which are bounded by
W_{r}
.

With Frobenius out of the picture, all that remains is linear algebra. Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}^{\dagger}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{\dagger}
be any two lifts of
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}(\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{\infty})
to
\Bun_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{q})
, such that
\deg{\mathcal{F}}_{2}=\deg{\mathcal{F}}_{1}+r
. (This is possible due to the parity condition on
r
.) Note that the dimension of
\Hom({\mathcal{F}}_{1}^{\dagger},{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{\dagger})
(meaning, morphisms of vector bundles, not necessarily injective, which preserve level structures) is
2r+1
.
The stack of shtukas
\Sht_{G}^{r}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{d,\omega}
is isomorphic to the quotient by
\mathbf{G}_{m}
of the moduli stack of morphisms of vector bundles with
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-structure
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}^{\dagger}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{\dagger}
which are bounded by
\mathrm{std}^{\boxtimes r}
at the legs. This is a quasi-projective variety; indeed it is the locally closed subvariety of

(\mathbf{P}^{1})^{r}\times\mathbf{P}\Hom({\mathcal{F}}_{1}^{\dagger},{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{\dagger})\cong(\mathbf{P}^{1})^{r}\times\mathbf{P}^{2r},

consisting of data
(x_{1},\dots,x_{r},f)
, where the divisor of
\det f
is
\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{r}
.

For each
i=1,\dots,r
, the local behavior of
f
at
\gamma_{i}
gives an
S
-point of the affine Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{G}
: this is the period morphism at the
i
th leg. If the legs are pairwise disjoint, all the period morphisms land in the minuscule Schubert cell
\mathrm{Gr}_{G}^{\mathrm{std}}=\mathbf{P}^{1}
. We claim that over the generic point
\eta_{r}
, the product of the period morphisms

\Pi\colon\Sht^{r}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})^{d,\omega}_{\eta_{r}}\rightarrow(\mathbf{P}^{1}_{\eta_{r}})^{r}

is a birational map. Indeed, let
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}
be generic legs, and consider the fiber of
\Pi

Proof.

We assume

On the other hand, the data

We can only speculate on the geometry of

3
Isogenies between K3 surfaces

3.1
Motivation:
2
-modularity of extremal rational elliptic fibrations

Let

be a nonisotrivial elliptic curve with degree 4 conductor

\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
. Let

be the support of

. Let

be a subset of even cardinality consisting of places appearing in

(2,\Sigma_{\infty})
-modular. The first phase of the plan was to find a rational map

\Sht^{2}_{G}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})\dashrightarrow{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E}),

where

is defined as a certain cartesian product:

\textstyle{{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{E}\times_{\mathbf{F}_{q}}U^{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathbf{P}^{1}\times U^{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{2:1}
\textstyle{\mathbf{P}^{1}\times U^{2}}

(3.1.1)

The lower horizontal arrow is a certain family of nonsplit double covers of

parametrized by

, derived from the moduli space of 3-legged coincidences. Now, the base change of a rational elliptic surface
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
by a nonsplit double cover

is a K3 surface. Thus
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow U^{2}
is a family of K3 surfaces. As explained in the introduction, in order to prove that

is 2-modular, it suffices to find a finite morphism
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\Km(\mathcal{E}^{2})
commuting with the maps to

. This is what we accomplish in this section:

Theorem 3.1.1
.

There exists a finite morphism
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\Km(\mathcal{E}^{2})
commuting with the maps to

.

Remark 3.1.2
.

Theorem
3.1.1
can be stated in any characteristic, since the definition of the space of coincidences is independent of characteristic. In the cases where
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
is unstable our formulas confirm Theorem

in any characteristic.

Generally, it is an interesting problem to give sufficient conditions for two K3 surfaces to be isogenous, see
[
Muk87
]
,
[
Bus19
]
,
[
Yan22
]
. A necessary condition is some relation between the Picard lattices of the two surfaces. Naturally one wants to know if this is sufficient:

Question 3.1.3
.

Let

be a K3 surface over an algebraically closed field. Let

be a lattice, such that

and

are isometric. Does there exist a K3 surface

in correspondence with

such that

?

For many values of

, operations on elliptic fibrations can be used to construct the correspondence. The main theorem of this section answers a special case of Question

.

Theorem 3.1.4
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface over an algebraically closed field. Suppose there is an isometry

, where

for two non-isogenous elliptic curves

and

. Then there is a morphism of finite degree from

to a Kummer surface of this form.

3.2
Elliptic fibrations on K3 surfaces

We begin with a few remarks on elliptic fibrations and especially elliptic K3 surfaces. Some standard references are
[
SS10
]
for elliptic fibrations and
[
Huy16
]
for K3 surfaces. Unless stated otherwise, by “surface” we mean a smooth projective surface over an algebraically closed field.

3.2.1
Generalities on elliptic surfaces

Let
S
be a surface. A
genus 1 fibration
on
S
is a morphism
S\rightarrow X
to a curve whose geometric generic fiber is a smooth integral curve of genus 1. If
S\rightarrow X
admits a section, we call it an
elliptic fibration
. In any case it always has a
multisection
, meaning a curve
C\subset S
such that
C\rightarrow X
is finite; the degree of the multisection is the degree of
C\rightarrow X
. An
elliptic surface
is a surface admitting an elliptic fibration, such that no fiber contains an exceptional curve (meaning a smooth rational curve of self-intersection
-1
).

For a surface
S
, the
Néron-Severi group

\NS(S)
is the group of divisors modulo algebraic equivalence. It is a finitely generated torsion-free abelian group endowed with an intersection pairing, which we write as
(v,w)
. This pairing is nondegenerate on the torsion-free part of
\NS(S)
. We write
\rho(S)
for the rank of the torsion-free part of
\NS(S)
. If
S\rightarrow X
is an elliptic fibration with general fiber
F
and section
O
, the
trivial lattice

T\subset\NS(S)
is generated by
O
and all irreducible components of fibers of
S\rightarrow X
. Suppose the reducible fibers occur at
R\subset X
. For
v\in R
, let
x_{0},\dots,x_{m_{v}-1}\in\NS(S)
be the irreducible components of the fiber
S_{v}
, where
x_{0}
is the unique such component which meets
O
. As all fibers are equivalent to
F
, the trivial lattice
T
is the orthogonal direct sum of
\left<O,F\right>
and
\left<x_{1},\dots,x_{m_{v}-1}\right>
.

The isomorphism classes of singular fibers of an elliptic surface have a well-known classification by Kodaira symbol. For each such fiber, the corresponding summand of
T
is a root lattice of type
A_{n}
,
D_{n}

(n\geq 4)
,
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
, or
E_{8}
. Here our convention is that our root lattices are
negative definite
. We will make use of the standard facts about each Kodaira symbol without comment, e.g., the corresponding Dynkin diagram, multiplicity of components, discriminant, etc.

There are two useful formulas relating the geometry of an elliptic surface
S
to the singular fibers of an elliptic fibration on
S
. One is the Shioda-Tate formula, and the other is a formula for the Euler number of
S
.

The Mordell-Weil group of an elliptic fibration
S\rightarrow X
is the group of sections
S(X)
. Equivalently it is the set of rational points of the generic fiber of
S\rightarrow X
, which is an elliptic curve. There is an isomorphism

NS(X)/T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\mathrel{\smash{\meno}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}\rule{0.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}S(X)

(3.2.1)

sending a divisor to its generic fiber. In particular we have the Shioda-Tate formula
[
SS10
, Proposition 6.6]
:

\rho(S)=2+\sum_{v\in R}(m_{v}-1)+\rank S(X)/(\text{torsion})

(3.2.2)

The smooth locus in each singular fiber
S_{v}
of an elliptic fibration has a group structure, with neutral component either
\mathbf{G}_{m}
(type
I_{n}
) or
\mathbf{G}_{a}
(all other types). We call
S_{v}
a multiplicative or additive fiber, respectively.
For additive fiber
v
, let
\delta_{v}
be the index of wild ramification, defined by the formula:

\delta_{v}=v(\Delta)-1-m_{v}

where
\Delta
is the discriminant of a Weierstrass equation for
S
over the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{X,v}
. Then
\delta_{v}\neq 0
only if the ground field has characteristic 2 or 3. If
\delta_{v}=0
for all
v
, we say that
S\rightarrow X
is tame.

For a projective variety
Y
, write
e(Y)
for the Euler number (= topological Euler characteristic = alternating sum of Betti numbers of
Y
). For a singular fiber
S_{v}
of an elliptic fibration
S\rightarrow X
, we have

e(S_{v})=\begin{cases}m_{v},&\text{if $S_{v}$ is multiplicative}\\
m_{v}+1,&\text{if $S_{v}$ is additive.}\end{cases}

(3.2.3)

We have the following relations:

\chi(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})=\frac{1}{12}e(S)

(3.2.4)

(where
\chi
= Euler characteristic), and

e(S)=\sum_{v}(e(S_{v})+\delta_{v}),

(3.2.5)

where the sum is over the singular fibers of
S\rightarrow X
.

3.2.2
K3 elliptic surfaces

A smooth projective surface
S
is
K3
if it has trivial canonical bundle and
H^{1}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})=0
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface. Then on
S
, the notions of linear, algebraic, and numerical equivalence of divisors agree, and
\Pic S\cong\NS(S)
is torsion-free. We refer to this group as the Picard lattice. It has signature
(1,\rho(S)-1)
. By Riemann-Roch, an irreducible curve
C\subset S
of arithmetic genus
g
has
(C,C)=2g-2
. This implies that
\Pic S
is an even lattice.

In the case that
k=\mathbf{C}
is the field of complex numbers, then
\Pic S
is a primitive sublattice of
H^{2}(S,\mathbf{Z})
. For its part,
H^{2}(S,\mathbf{Z})
is isomorphic to the
K3 lattice

E_{8}^{2}\oplus U^{3}
, where
U
is the hyperbolic plane. By the Lefschetz principle, this fact about
\Pic S
extends to any field of characteristic 0. In positive characteristic, we can use the following lemma.

Proposition 3.2.1

(
[
Huy16
, Proposition 5.8]
)
.

Let
X
be a K3 surface of finite height
over a perfect field
k
of characteristic
p
. Then
X
can be lifted
to
W(k)
in such a way that the specialization map from the Néron-Severi
group of the general to the special fibre is an isomorphism.

We are interested in those K3 surfaces which admit genus 1 fibrations. If
S
is K3 and
S\rightarrow X
is a genus 1 fibration, then necessarily
X\cong\mathbf{P}^{1}
. An interesting fact about elliptic K3 surfaces
S
is, if their Picard rank is not too small, they typically admit several genus 1 fibrations which are distinct modulo the action of
\Aut S
. It will be important for us to recognize the genus 1 fibrations on
S
purely by examining the lattice
\Pic S
.

Proposition 3.2.2

(
[
Pvv71
, §3, Theorem 1]
)
.

Assume that the characteristic of
k
is not 2 or 3. Let
S
be a K3 surface.

1.

Suppose
F
is a primitive class in
\Pic S
with
(F,F)=0
. Then there exists a genus 1 fibration
S\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
whose fiber class is
\O(\Pic S)
-equivalent to
F
.

2.

Continuing, let
d
be the greatest common divisor of
(F,D)
for all
D\in\Pic S
. Then
S\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
admits a multisection of degree
d
.

3.

Continuing further, suppose
d=1
, so that
S\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
is an elliptic fibration with fiber
F
and section
O
. Then
\left<F,O\right>
is the hyperbolic plane. The reducible fibers of
S\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
correspond exactly to the root lattice summands of the root sublattice of
\left<F,O\right>^{\perp}
. (The root sublattice is the sublattice spanned by vectors

with
(v,v)=-2
.)

Proof.

(1) One can apply standard results on
\Pic S

[
Huy16
, Corollary 8.2.9]
to find a sequence of smooth rational curves
C_{1},\dots,C_{r}
on
S
such that
F^{\prime}=\pm\rho_{C_{r}}\circ\cdots\circ\rho_{C_{1}}(F)
is numerically effective (nef), where
\rho_{C}(v)=D-(2(v,C)/(C,C))C
is the reflection about the line spanned by a curve
C\subset S
. Since each
C_{i}
is a smooth rational curve, we have
(C_{i},C_{i})=-2
and consequently
(F^{\prime},F^{\prime})=0
. By
[

, the linear system
\left\lvert F^{\prime}\right\rvert
has no base points, and the associated morphism to projective space factors through a genus 1 fibration

, with fiber equivalent to
F
.

(2) It is clear that
F^{\prime}\equiv F\pmod{d}
in
\Pic S
, so that there exists
D\in\Pic S
with
(F^{\prime},D)=d
. After replacing
D
with
D+nF^{\prime}
for
n\gg 0
, we may assume that
D
is effective. Then

is a multisection of the fibration of degree
d
.

(3)
Let
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\left<F,O\right>
be the set of
C
that satisfy

(C,C)=-2
. By Riemann-Roch either
C
or
-C
is effective, so let

{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
be the set of effective elements. We claim that every
element of
{\mathcal{C}}^{+}
is a sum of classes of curves contained in fibers.

Indeed, write
C\in{\mathcal{C}}^{+}=\sum a_{i}[C_{i}]
, where the
a_{i}
are
nonnegative integers and the
C_{i}
are classes of irreducible curves.
Since
[F]
moves with empty base locus, it has nonnegative intersection
with all curves, and positive intersection with all curves not contained in
any member of the linear series
|F|
. In particular
O
is not among the

C_{i}
, because
[F]\cdot[O]=1
.
Therefore
O\cdot C_{i}\geq 0
for all

, and so

is not one of
the
C_{i}
either. On the other hand it is clear that if
C_{i}
is a
curve in a fibre that does not meet

, then
[C_{i}]\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

Let
R
be the root sublattice of
\left<F,O\right>
. From the above we see
that

is freely generated by the classes of curves in fibers that do not
meet
O
. For each reducible fibre we obtain a root lattice as in

[

, and distinct fibers give orthogonal

.
∎

Corollary 3.2.3
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface. If the Picard rank

is at least 5, then

admits a genus 1 fibration.

Proof.

The quadratic form of
\Pic S
is indefinite and represents zero over
\mathbf{Q}_{p}
for all primes

. Therefore by Hasse-Minkowski it represents zero rationally; i.e., there exists
F\in\Pic S
with
(F,F)=0
. By Proposition

, the surface

admits a genus 1 fibration.
∎

Remark 3.2.4
.

If the characteristic of

is

or

In the situation of Proposition

(

Lemma 3.2.6
.

Let

be a principal ideal domain, let

be a free

be a quadratic form on

be a submodule such that the discriminant of

is a unit in

.

Proof.

First note that

would

Let
F
be the fraction field of

, and let

. Then

Lemma 3.2.7
.

Let

Suppose

If

is divisible by
p
. Indeed, if
x\in N
satisfies
Q(x)\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}^{\times}
, then
M\oplus\left<x\right>
would be a larger submodule with unit discriminant. In fact we claim that the intersection form on
N
is divisible by
p
, i.e., that
N\subset pN^{\vee}
. This follows automatically if
p
is odd. If
p=2
, we also need to exclude the possibility of
x,x^{\prime}\in N
with
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\times}
. But since we already know that
(x,x),(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\times}
, we have
(x,x)(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})-(x,x^{\prime})^{2}\in\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\times}
, and again we have constructed a larger submodule
M\oplus\left<x,x^{\prime}\right>
with unit discriminant.

Recall that the discriminant group of a lattice
\Lambda
(over whatever PID base) is
D(\Lambda)=\Lambda^{\vee}/\Lambda
. Then
D(L)\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{p}\cong D(L\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{p})
. On the other hand
L\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{p}=M\oplus N
as above, and we have
D(M)=0
, whereas

D(N)\otimes\mathbf{F}_{p}=N^{\vee}/(N,pN^{\vee})=N^{\vee}/pN^{\vee}=N^{\vee}\otimes\mathbf{F}_{p}

since
N\subset pN^{\vee}
as we observed above. We conclude from this that
\dim_{\mathbf{F}_{p}}D(L)\otimes\mathbf{F}_{p}=\rank N
.

Now we use that fact that
L=\Pic S=\NS(S)
embeds primitively into the K3 lattice
H=H^{2}(X,\mathbf{Z})
. (This is because for any complex projective surface
S
, we have
\NS(S)\cong H^{1,1}(S,\mathbf{C})\cap H^{2}(S,\mathbf{Z})
.) Crucially,
H
is unimodular. Let
L^{\perp}
be the orthogonal complement of
L
in
H
. The intersection pairing on
H
induces a pairing
D(L)\otimes H/(L\oplus L^{\perp})\rightarrow\mathbf{Q}/\mathbf{Z}
.

This pairing puts
D(H)
and
H/(L\oplus L^{\perp})
into Pontrjagin duality.
Proof: if
h\in H
satisfies
(L^{\vee},h)\subset\mathbf{Z}
, write
h=\ell+\ell^{\perp}
, where
\ell\in L\otimes\mathbf{Q}
,
\ell^{\perp}\in L^{\perp}\otimes\mathbf{Q}
. Then
(L^{\vee},\ell)\subset\mathbf{Z}
implies
\ell\in L^{\vee\vee}=L
and therefore
\ell^{\perp}\in H\cap(L^{\perp}\otimes\mathbf{Q})=L^{\perp}
and
h\in L\oplus L^{\perp}
. Conversely if
\ell^{\vee}\in L^{\vee}
satisfies
(\ell^{\vee},H)\in\mathbf{Z}
, then
\ell^{\vee}\in H^{\vee}\cap L^{\vee}=H\cap L^{\vee}\in H\cap(L\otimes\mathbf{Q})=L
because
L
is embedded primitively in
H
.

In particular
\dim_{\mathbf{F}_{p}}D(L)\otimes\mathbf{F}_{p}=\dim(H/(L\oplus L^{\perp})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{p}\leq\rank L^{\perp}=22-\rank L
, as one can see by extending a
\mathbf{Z}
-basis of
L
to
H
. Thus
\rank N\leq 22-\rank L
.

We have assumed
\rho(S)=\rank L\geq 13
, and so

\rank M=\rank L-\rank N\geq 2\rank L-22\geq 4.

Our divisor
D
was assumed to satisfy
p|(D,x)
for all
x\in L
. If we decompose
D
as
D=m+n
with
m\in M
,
n\in N=M^{\perp}
, then
p|(m,x)
for all
x\in M
, which is to say,
m\in pM^{\vee}
. But since
M
is unimodular, we find that
m\in pM
. We find that
n\equiv D\pmod{L\otimes p\mathbf{Z}_{p}}
. We have also assumed that
L[D/p]
is a lattice, which implies that
p^{2}|(D,D)
; if
p=2
we have assumed
8|(D,D)
. The same statements are true when
D
is replaced with
n
. By Lemma
3.2.7
, we can find a vector
x\in M
such that
(x,x)=-(n,n)/p^{2}
. Then
D_{0}=n+px\in L\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{p}
satisfies
D_{0}\equiv D\pmod{p}
and
(D_{0},D_{0})=0
.

By weak approximation on quadric hypersurfaces, there exists
D^{\prime}\in L\otimes\mathbf{Q}
satisfying
(D^{\prime},D^{\prime})=0
which is
p
-adically close to
D_{0}
. After clearing denominators, we may assume
D^{\prime}\in L
. Now we may apply Proposition

3.2.2
to obtain the required genus 1 fibration.

∎

Corollary 3.2.9
.

Let
S
be an ordinary K3 surface of Picard number
\geq 13
, and let

p
be an odd prime. Let
d=\disc\Pic S
, and let
p^{v}
be the largest power of
p
dividing
d
. Suppose we have an isometry

\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{p}\cong L\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{p}
, where
L
is a unimodular
\mathbf{Z}_{p}
-lattice. Then there is a map of finite degree from
S

to a K3 surface
S^{\prime}
for which
\disc\Pic S^{\prime}=d/p^{v}
.

Proof.

The hypothesis on
\Pic S
implies that
v
is even, as the discriminant of a quadratic form is well-defined up to a square. It also implies that the Hasse invariant of
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{p}
is trivial, as this is the case for
L\otimes\mathbf{Q}_{p}
. Indeed, we may diagonalize the quadratic form on
L
as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}
, with
a_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}^{\times}
, and then the Hasse invariant is
\prod_{i<j}(a_{i},a_{j})
(Hilbert symbol). Each factor in the product is trivial (note that
p
is odd).

We proceed by induction on
v
, the case
v=0
being obvious. First suppose that
D(\Pic S)
contains an element of order
p^{2}
, represented by
D^{\vee}\in(\Pic S)^{\vee}
. Then
D=p^{2}D^{\vee}\in\Pic S
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
3.2.8
, in which case there is a degree
p^{2}
map
S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
with
\disc\Pic S^{\prime}=d/p^{2}
.

Therefore assume that
D(\Pic S)\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{p}
is
p
-torsion, in which case its
\mathbf{F}_{p}
-dimension is
v
. The quadratic form on
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{p}
is equivalent to the diagonal form

pa_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+pa_{v}x_{v}^{2}+a_{v+1}x_{v+1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}^{2},

(3.2.6)

with each
a_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}^{\times}
. If
v>2
, there exist
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}
such that
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}+a_{3}x_{3}^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
since every nonsingular conic over
\mathbf{F}_{p}
has a rational point. Let
D\in\Pic S
be
p
-adically close to
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0,\dots)\in\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Z}_{p}
. Then
D
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
3.2.8
, and again we can remove a factor of
p^{2}
from
\disc\Pic S
.

We are now reduced to the case
v=2
. Standard formulas for the Hilbert symbol reveal that
(pa_{1},a_{i})=(pa_{2},a_{i})
for all
i\geq 3
, so that the Hasse invariant of (
3.2.6
) is
(pa_{1},pa_{2})=(-a_{1}a_{2}/p)
(Legendre symbol). But by the observation in the first paragraph, the Hasse invariant is trivial; i.e.,
-a_{1}a_{2}
is a square modulo
p
. Thus
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+a_{2}x_{2}^{2}
represents 0 modulo
p
, and we proceed as in the previous paragraph.
∎

3.3
Kummer surfaces
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
of Picard rank 18

Here we recall the basic constructions and properties of the Kummer surface
\Km(A)
attached to an abelian surface
A
. For the moment let us suppose we are in characteristic not 2. Denoting by
\iota
the involution
x\mapstochar\rightarrow-x
on
A
, the quotient
A/\iota
has rational double point singularities at each of the 16 fixed points of
\iota
(namely, the 2-torsion points of
A
). Let
\Km(A)
be the minimal resolution of
A/\iota
. Then
\Km(A)
is a K3 surface.

The Picard lattice
\Pic\Km(A)=\NS\Km(A)
contains both
\NS(A)
and the classes of the 16 exceptional divisors. In fact these generate
\Pic\Km(A)
and we have:

\rho(\Km(A))=16+\rho(A)

(3.3.1)

We are mostly interested in the case that
A=E_{1}\times E_{2}
is a product of non-isogenous elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
. In this case, (
3.3.1
) gives
\rho(\Km(A))=18
. There is an elliptic fibration
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
given by projecting onto
E_{2}/\iota\cong\mathbf{P}^{1}
; the geometric fibers are all isomorphic to
E_{1}
. In fact this gives an alternate construction of
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
: it is the quadratic twist of the constant elliptic fibration
E_{1}\times\mathbf{P}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
by the double cover
E_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
. (Of course, we could have reversed the roles of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
in this construction.)

The bad fibers of
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
occur at the four branch points of
E_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
, and they are all of type
I_{0}^{\ast}=\tilde{D}_{4}
. Therefore the trivial lattice of the fibration is
U\oplus D_{4}^{\oplus 4}
. We note here that
\disc D_{4}=4
. On the other hand the Mordell-Weil group of this fibration has order 4 (coming from the 2-torsion in
E_{1}
). We conclude from (
3.2.1
) that
\Pic\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
has discriminant
-16
. The lattice
\Pic\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
does not depend on the elliptic curves
E_{1}
or
E_{2}
, so long as they are nonisogenous. In the complex setting, the transcendental lattice of
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
is
T_{E_{1}\times E_{2}}(2)\cong(H^{1}(E_{1},\mathbf{Z})\otimes H^{1}(E_{2},\mathbf{Z}))(2)\cong U^{\oplus 2}(2)
.

We are interested in the question of whether a given K3 surface
S
of rank 18 is isogenous to a Kummer surface of the form
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
, and if so, how to find the elliptic curves
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
. The following result relies on a deep theorem of Mukai.

Proposition 3.3.1
.

Let
S
be a complex K3 surface such that
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}
is isometric to
\Pic\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})\otimes\mathbf{Q}
for some nonisogenous elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
. Then there exists an isogeny between
S
and a Kummer surface of that form. (An isogeny between K3 surfaces
S
and
S^{\prime}
is an algebraic cycle on
S\times S^{\prime}
inducing an isometry
H^{2}(S,\mathbf{Q})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\mathrel{\smash{\meno}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}\rule{0.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}H^{2}(S^{\prime},\mathbf{Q})
.)

Proof.

(Sketch.) Let
T_{S}
be the transcendental lattice of
S
. Then
T_{S}
has signature
(2,-2)
, and is the complement of
\Pic\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
in
H^{2}(S,\mathbf{Z})
. A calculation involving Hasse-Minkowski invariants shows that
T_{S}\otimes\mathbf{Q}
is isometric to
U^{\oplus 2}\otimes\mathbf{Q}
. Via this isometry, the Hodge structure on
T_{S}
now determines a Hodge structure on
U^{\oplus 2}
, which is to say, a morphism of real groups
h\colon\mathbf{S}\rightarrow O(2,2)
, where
\mathbf{S}
is the Deligne torus. Now observe that there is an isomorphism
(g_{1},g_{2})\mapstochar\rightarrow g_{1}\otimes g_{2}
from
(\Sp(2)\times\Sp(2))/\left\{\pm 1\right\}
onto the neutral component of
O(2,2)
. Therefore
h
factors through a morphism
\mathbf{S}\rightarrow(\Sp(2)\times\Sp(2))/\left\{\pm 1\right\}
. For
i=1,2
, let
h_{i}
be the projection of this morphism onto the
i
th copy of
\Sp(2)/\left\{\pm 1\right\}\cong\PGL_{2}
; then
h_{i}
determines an elliptic curve
E_{i}
. Thus we have an isometry of rational Hodge structures:

T_{S}\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong H^{1}(E_{1},\mathbf{Q})\otimes H^{1}(E_{2},\mathbf{Q})\cong T_{\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})}\otimes\mathbf{Q}

We now invoke a theorem of Mukai
[
Muk87
, Corollary 1.10]
: an isometry between rational Hodge structures of two K3 surfaces of rank
\geq 11
is always induced from an isogeny.
∎

In this section we prove an effective version of Proposition
3.3.1
, whereby the elliptic curves
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
can in theory be computed from
S
, and where the isogeny is simply a finite rational map (which can also be computed). The idea is to leverage operations on elliptic fibrations. We build up the result in stages.

Proposition 3.3.2
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface whose Picard lattice is isometric to
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
for some nonisogenous elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
. Then
S
is isomorphic to a Kummer surface of that form.

Proof.

Proposition
3.2.2
produces an elliptic fibration
S\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
with
I_{0}^{\ast}
fibers at four points of
\mathbf{P}^{1}
. Let
E_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
be the elliptic curve branched at exactly these four points. Then the quadratic twist of
S\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
by
E_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
has
I_{0}
reduction at these points; i.e., it has good reduction everywhere and therefore must be a constant elliptic curve
E_{1}\times\mathbf{P}^{1}
. Thus
S
is a quadratic twist of
E_{1}\times\mathbf{P}^{1}
by
E_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
, and this is exactly
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
.
∎

Remark 3.3.3
.

Recall that a
Shioda-Inose structure

(
[
Mor84
, Definition 6.1]
) on a complex K3 surface

is a rational map

S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
of degree
2
such that
S^{\prime}
is a Kummer surface and

T_{S^{\prime}}\cong 2T_{S}
, meaning that that the Gram
matrix of
T_{S^{\prime}}
is twice that of
T_{S}
. Morrison proves
[
Mor84
, Theorem 6.3]
that
S
has
a Shioda-Inose structure if and only if
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}
is isometric
to
\Pic K\otimes\mathbf{Q}
for some Kummer surface
K=\Km(A)
, if and only if

T_{A}
embeds primitively in
U^{3}
.

Proposition 3.3.4
.

Assume that
\car k\neq 2
. Let
S
be a K3 surface whose Picard lattice has rank
18
and
discriminant
-1
. Then there is a finite map from
S
to a K3 surface
of the form
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
, where

E_{1},E_{2}
are nonisogenous elliptic curves.

Proof.

The transcendental lattice of
S
is an even unimodular lattice
of rank
4
and signature
(2,2)
, so it is isometric to
U^{2}
. By
Remark
3.3.3
, there is a Shioda-Inose structure on

S
. The codomain of this isogeny has transcendental lattice
U^{2}(2)
,
which as we have seen is that of the Picard lattice of
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})

for nonisogenous
E_{1},E_{2}
. The result now follows from
Proposition
3.3.2
.
∎

We need a further lattice-theoretic result.

Proposition 3.3.5
.

Every even lattice
L
of discriminant
-4^{i}
and signature
(1,17)
is
contained in a unimodular lattice, necessarily
II_{1,17}=U\oplus E_{8}^{\oplus 2}
.

Proof.

We will show that if
i\geq 1
then
L
is contained in a lattice of discriminant
-4^{i-1}
, and then the result follows by induction. The idea is that if
x\in D(L)=L^{\vee}/L
is an element of order 2 satisfying
(x,x)\in 2\mathbf{Z}
(ie,
x
is isotropic in
D(L)[2]
), then
L^{\prime}=L+\left<x\right>
is again an even lattice, and
\disc L^{\prime}=(\disc L)/4
.

We need the fact that (for general lattices
L
) the
\mathbf{F}_{2}
-dimension of
D(L)[2]
has the same parity as the rank of
L
, which in our case is even. (Proof: Use the classification of lattices over
\mathbf{Z}_{2}
to reduce to the case that the quadratic form is either
x^{2}
or
2^{r}(ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2})
, with
a,c\in\mathbf{Z}_{2}
and
b\in\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\times}
.) As a result the following four cases are exhaustive.

•

Case 1:
D(L)
contains an element
a
of order
2^{i}
, where
i>2
. We have
(a,2^{i}a)\in\mathbf{Z}
, and therefore
(a,a)\in 2^{-i}\mathbf{Z}
. Now let
x=2^{i-1}a
; then
x
has order
2
in
D(L)
and
(x,x)=2^{2i-2}(a,a)\in 2^{i-2}\mathbf{Z}\subset 2\mathbf{Z}
as desired.

•

Case 2:
D(L)
contains two independent elements
a,b
of
order
4
. Let these be
a,b
. We then have
4(a,a),4(b,b),4(a,b)\in\mathbf{Z}
.
If
4(a,a)
and
4(b,b)
are both odd, then

(2a+2b,2a+2b)=4(a,a)+8(a,b)+4(b,b)
is even.
Thus at least one of
2a,2a+2b,2b
is isotropic in
D(L)[2]
.

•

Case 3:
D(L)[2]
contains
(\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})^{\oplus 4}
. Let
S
be the subset of
D(L)[2]

consisting of
x
with
(x,x)\in\mathbf{Z}
; it is easy to see this is a subgroup. If
x,y\in D(L)[2]
do not lie in
S
, each of
(x,x),(y,y)\in\mathbf{Z}+\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
, and then
(x+y,x+y)=(x,x)+2(x,y)+(y,y)\in\mathbf{Z}
. This shows that
S
has index at most 2 in

, so that
S
contains
(\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})^{\oplus 3}
. Let
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\in S
be independent elements.
If any
s_{i}
satisfies
(s_{i},s_{i})=0
, it is isotropic.
If
(s_{i},s_{j})\in\mathbf{Z}
for any

, then
(s_{i}+s_{j},s_{i}+s_{j})\in 4\mathbf{Z}
.
If not, then
(s_{i},s_{i})\in 1+2\mathbf{Z}
for all

and
(s_{i},s_{j})\in 1/2+\mathbf{Z}

for all

, so
(s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3},s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3})\in 2\mathbf{Z}
.

•

Case 4:
D(L)\cong(\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})^{2}
. Let
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
be the nonzero elements of
D(L)
. Assume that none of these are isotropic. The possible values of
2(a_{i},a_{i})
modulo 4 are
(1,1,2),(3,3,2),(1,3,2)
.
By
[
Mor84
, Theorem 2.8]
we can embed
L
into the K3 lattice

U^{3}+E_{8}^{2}
, and the complement is a lattice of signature
(2,2)
and
discriminant
4
. By
[
Mor84
, Theorem 2.8]
again we can embed
this into
U^{3}
, with complement of signature
(1,1)
and discriminant

-4
. This lattice has the same discriminant form as
L
, by
applying
[
Mor84
, Lemma 2.4]
twice. On the other hand, there are
only two such lattices

, with Gram matrices

M_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
2&2\end{pmatrix}
and

M_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
2&0\end{pmatrix}

(proof: let
x,y
be a basis.
If
(x,x)(y,y)<0
then we must have
(x,x)=2,(y,y)=-2,(x,y)=0
,
or the same with

switched, and that is the first case. If not,
we can take

and

So

[

for

. In both cases there is a vector in

, namely

∎

We have reached the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.3.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be the Picard lattice of (any) Kummer surface of the form
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
, where
E_{1},E_{2}
are nonisogenous elliptic curves. Let
S
be a K3 surface. Assume there is an isometry
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong\Lambda\otimes\mathbf{Q}
. Then there exists a finite morphism
S\rightarrow\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
for nonisogenous elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\Lambda
has discriminant
-16
, the hypothesis implies that
\left\lvert\disc\Pic S\right\rvert
is a square. We apply
Corollary
3.2.9
to all odd primes dividing
\disc\Pic S
to obtain a finite morphism from
S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
, where
S^{\prime}
is a K3 surface with
\disc\Pic S^{\prime}=-4^{n}
. By Proposition
3.3.5
, there is an embedding of
L^{\prime}=\Pic S^{\prime}
into
L^{\prime\prime}
, where
L^{\prime\prime}=II_{1,17}
is the even unimodular lattice of signature
(1,17)
. We can factor this embedding as
L^{\prime}=L_{0}\subset L_{1}\subset\cdots\subset L_{m}=L^{\prime\prime}
, with
L_{i}/L_{i+1}\cong\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}
for each
i
. Successive applications of
3.2.8
give a finite morphism
S^{\prime}\rightarrow S^{\prime\prime}
, where
\Pic S^{\prime\prime}\cong L^{\prime\prime}
. Finally, by Proposition
3.3.4
there is a finite morphism from
S^{\prime\prime}
to a Kummer surface of the form
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
.
∎

We conclude this section with some remarks on potential extensions of Theorem
3.3.6
to the case of more general Kummer surfaces. Recall that if
A
is an abelian surface, the Picard rank of the Kummer surface
\Km(A)
equals

. In characteristic 0, the rank of
\Km(A)
takes one of the values 17,18,19,20. The cases of rank 19,20 are easy to deal with.

Remark 3.3.7
.

If
\rank\Pic S>18
, then a Shioda-Inose structure on
S
always exists

[
Mor84
, Theorem 6.3, Corollary 6.4]
,
so there is a map of degree
2
from
S
to a Kummer surface.
Thus the analogue of Theorem
3.3.6
is true for such
S
.

We now turn to the case of rank 18. We pose the question of whether a K3 surface
S
of rank 18 should admit a finite morphism to a Kummer surface
\Km(A)
. Here,
A
would have to be an abelian surface admitting endomorphisms by an order
\mathcal{O}
in a real (and possibly split) quadratic extension of
\mathbf{Q}
. Then
\disc\Pic\Km(A)=-16\disc\mathcal{O}
. We have already treated the split case
\mathcal{O}=\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{Z}
, which corresponds to the case that
A
is a product of elliptic curves. Our methods can also treat the case of
\mathcal{O}=\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
.

Proposition 3.3.8
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface. Let
\Lambda
be the Picard lattice of (any) Kummer surface of the form
\Km(A)
, where
A
is an abelian surface with
\End A=\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
. Assume there is an isometry
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong\Lambda\otimes\mathbf{Q}
. Then there exists a finite morphism
S\rightarrow\Km(A)
for an abelian surface
A
of this sort.

Proof.

(Sketch.) As in Corollary
3.2.9
, we may remove all odd primes from the discriminant of
\Pic S
. Therefore assume that
\disc\Pic S=-2^{2i+1}
for some
i\geq 0
. An even lattice of rank
>1
may not have discriminant
\pm 2
, so in fact we may assume
i\geq 1
.

We claim that
\Pic S
is contained in a lattice of discriminant
-8
. We argue as in the proof of Proposition
3.3.5
: referring to that proof, if
i\geq 2
then one of the first three cases always holds and we can always find an overlattice of
\Pic S
of index 2. Therefore let us assume that
i=1
, so that
\disc\Pic S=-8
. Using the results of
[
CS99
, Chapter 15]
, we can confirm that there is only one isomorphism class of even lattices with discriminant
-8
and signature
(1,17)
, namely
D_{9}\oplus E_{7}\oplus U
. We can now assume that
\Pic S
is isomorphic to this lattice.

Invoking a computation with Hasse-Minkowski invariants, this forces the transcendental lattice of
S
to be isomorphic to
U\oplus\langle-2\rangle\oplus\langle 4\rangle
. (This is also a consequence of
[
EK14
, Proposition 7]
.) Thus by
[
Mor84
, Corollary 6.2]
there is a Shioda-Inose structure
S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
, where
S^{\prime}=\Km(A)
is the Kummer surface of an
A
satisfying
T_{A}\cong U\oplus\langle-2\rangle\oplus\langle 4\rangle
. The Néron-Severi lattice
\NS(A)
of
A
, being the complement of
T_{A}
in
H^{2}(A,\mathbf{Z})\cong U^{\oplus 3}
, is isomorphic to
\langle 2\rangle\oplus\langle-4\rangle
. The vector of length 2 in
\NS(A)
is a principal polarization on
A
, and
\End A=\NS(A)
is the quadratic order of discriminant
-8
, namely
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-2}]
.

∎

Remark 3.3.9
.

It is easy to embed
D_{9}\oplus E_{7}\oplus U
into the K3 lattice

E_{8}^{\oplus 2}\oplus U^{\oplus 3}
with complement either

U\oplus\langle-2\rangle\oplus\langle 4\rangle
or

U\oplus\langle 2\rangle\oplus\langle-4\rangle
. However,
these need not be distinguished. Indeed,
let
L
be the lattice
\langle 2\rangle\oplus\langle-4\rangle

and let
x,y
be the given basis. Then
L\cong-L
, as one sees by
changing to the basis
(x+y,2x+y)
. (This is a manifestation of the
fact that
\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{2}]
has a unit of norm
-1
.)

Finally we turn to the case of Picard rank 17.

Proposition 3.3.10
.

Let
S
be a K3 surface. Let
\Lambda
be the Picard lattice of (any) Kummer surface of the form
\Km(A)
, where
A
is an abelian surface with
\End A=\mathbf{Z}
. Assume there is an isometry
n\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong\Lambda\otimes\mathbf{Q}
, where
n\in\{1,2\}
. Then there exists a finite morphism
S\rightarrow\Km(A)
for an abelian surface
A
of this sort.

Proof.

(Sketch.) Again we apply the techniques of Corollary
3.2.9
and
3.3.5
to assume that
\disc\Pic S
is either
-2
or
-4
. In these two cases we must have
n=1
or
2
respectively.
In both cases, the even lattice
\Pic S
is uniquely determined by its discriminant and signature.

In the case of discriminant
-2
, we have
\Pic S\cong E_{8}\oplus E_{7}\oplus U
. This lattice is rationally isometric to
\Lambda
. The orthogonal complement of
\Pic S
in
H^{2}(S,\mathbf{Z})
is isomorphic to
A_{1}\oplus U^{\oplus 2}
. The existence of a Shioda-Inose structure
now follows from
[
Mor84
, Corollary 6.4 (iii)]
.

In the case of discriminant
-4
,
\Pic S\cong L\oplus U
, where
L
is a lattice containing its root sublattice
L_{0}
with index 2, and
L_{0}\cong A_{3}\oplus D_{12}
. Therefore by Proposition
3.2.2
, there exists an elliptic fibration on
S
with 2-torsion section. Let
S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
be the quotient by the 2-torsion section. The induced map
T_{S^{\prime}}\rightarrow T_{S}
is not generally a rational isometry, but rather
T_{S^{\prime}}
is rationally isometric to
2T_{S}
: see
[
BSV17
, §2.4]
. Since the rank of
T_{S}
is odd,
the discriminants of
T_{S}
and
T_{S^{\prime}}
differ by twice the square of a rational number. Since
\Pic S
is the complement of
T_{S}
in a unimodular lattice, and similarly for
\Pic S^{\prime}
, we find that
\disc\Pic S^{\prime}=-2n^{2}
for some integer
n\geq 1
. Also, we now have a rational
isometry
\Pic S^{\prime}\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong\Lambda\otimes\mathbf{Q}
. After once again removing squares from the discriminant we are in the case of the previous paragraph.
∎

3.4
Extremal rational elliptic fibrations, and associated K3 surfaces

In this section we address Theorem
3.1.1
concerning the base change of a nonisotrivial extremal rational fibration
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
by a double cover
\mathbf{P}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
.

First, some preparations. A rational elliptic surface
\mathcal{E}
over an algebraically closed field
F
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{P}^{2}
blown up at 9 points (possibly infinitely near), so that
\rho(\mathcal{E})=10
and
e(\mathcal{E})=1
. If in addition
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
is extremal, and if we also assume that the singular fibers are tame, then by the Shioda-Tate formula (
3.2.2

\sum_{v}(m_{v}-1)=8
, where
m_{v}
is the number of irreducible components in the fiber
\mathcal{E}_{v}

3.2.3
) we have
\sum_{v}e(\mathcal{E}_{v})=1
, where
e(E_{v})
is
m_{v}
or
m_{v}+1
as
v
is multiplicative or additive. Therefore the singular fibers of
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
fall into one of the following three possibilities:

1.

Four multiplicative fibers,

2.

Two multiplicative fibers and one additive fiber,

3.

Two additive fibers.

The case of two additive fibers can only occur if
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
is isotrivial (i.e., has constant
j
-invariant). We discard this case, and refer to the (1) as the semistable case and (2) as the unstable case.

Remark 3.4.1
.

There do exist nonconstant extremal rational elliptic fibrations with two singular fibers, for example the curve with Weierstrass equation

Remark 3.4.4
.

In characteristic 2, the
\mathrm{IV},\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{6}
fibration is the specialization of the
\mathrm{I}_{1},\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{3},\mathrm{I}_{6}

Fix a nonisotrivial extremal rational elliptic fibration
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}
.
Let
C\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}
be a separable double cover, with
C
a rational curve; thus
C\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}
is ramified at two points. Assume these points are disjoint from the singular locus of
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{F}
. Consider the base change

S=C\times_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}\mathcal{E}.

Then
S
is a K3 surface. Indeed,
S
is a double cover of the rational surface
\mathcal{E}
branched along the sextic described by the union of two cubics (namely, the fibers of
\mathcal{E}
over the branch points of
C\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
).

Under our hypothesis that
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
is rational and extremal, we must have
\rho(\mathcal{E})=10=2+8
, where the 2 is from the identity
O
and fiber
F
, and the 8 is from irreducible components of fibers which do not cross
O
. Considering the elliptic fibration
S\rightarrow C
, the contribution to
\rho(S)
from irreducible components of fibers is
2\cdot 8=16
, and so
\rho(S)\geq 2+16=18
.

We now have a rational map of moduli spaces: (double covers of
\mathbf{P}^{1}
branched at 2 points)
\rightarrow
(K3 surfaces of Picard rank
\geq 18
). Both spaces are 2-dimensional, so we expect the generic double cover
C\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
to produce a K3 surface
S
of rank 18, with Picard lattice not depending on
C
.

Proposition 3.4.5
.

Assume that
\Pic S
has rank 18.
There exists a rational isometry
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong\Lambda\otimes\mathbf{Q}
, where
\Lambda
is the Picard lattice of the Kummer surface associated to the product of two nonisogenous elliptic curves.

Proof.

We have
\Pic\mathcal{E}\cong E_{8}\oplus U
. Let
L\subset\Pic\mathcal{E}
be the sublattice generated by reducible fibers not meeting the identity section, so that
L\oplus U
embeds into
E_{8}\oplus U
with finite cokernel. Observe therefore that we have embeddings of lattices:
L^{\oplus 2}\oplus U\subset E_{8}\oplus L\oplus U\subset E_{8}^{\oplus 2}\oplus U
.

Now
\Pic S
contains a finite-index sublattice isomorphic to
L^{\oplus 2}\oplus U
, generated by reducible fibers and the identity section. By the observation above,
\Pic S\otimes\mathbf{Q}\cong(E_{8}^{\oplus 2}\oplus U)\otimes\mathbf{Q}
. A calculation involving Hasse-Minkowski invariants shows that the latter is isomorphic to
\Lambda
.
∎

With this background, we now turn to Theorem
3.1.1
. We have a family of K3 surfaces
\mathbf{Z}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow U^{2}
, all obtained via base change from
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
by a family of double covers
\mathbf{P}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
parametrized by
U^{2}
. Theorem
1.4.1
now predicts that each geometric fiber of
\mathbf{Z}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow U^{2}
admits a finite morphism to the Kummer surface of a product of elliptic curves. Theorem
3.1.1
is the statement that
\mathbf{Z}^{2}(\mathcal{E})
itself admits a finite morphism to the Kummer surface of a product of elliptic curves over
U^{2}
, and that this product is none other than
\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}
.

We prove Theorem
3.1.1
in cases.

3.4.1
The
\mathrm{I}_{2}^{\ast}\mathrm{I}_{2}\mathrm{I}_{2}
fibration, with Mordell-Weil group
(\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})^{2}

The simplest case of Theorem
3.1.1
concerns the Legendre fibration
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{t}
, with equation

y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-t)

(3.4.1)

over a field
k
of characteristic
\neq 2
. Then
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{t}
has singular fibers
\mathrm{I}_{2}^{*},\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{2}
at
t=\infty,0,1
, with split multiplicative reduction at
t=1
. The conductor is
N=(0)+(1)+2(\infty)
, and the Mordell-Weil group is
(\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})^{2}
. Let
U=\mathbf{P}^{1}\smallsetminus\left\{0,1,\infty\right\}
. We write
t_{1},t_{2}
for the coordinates on
U^{2}
.

Let
\Sigma_{\infty}=\left\{1\right\}
(this choice is unimportant); we computed in Example
2.7.1
that the space of coincidences
\Coinc^{3}(\Gamma_{0}(N);\Sigma_{\infty})\cong\mathbf{P}^{1}_{s}\times U^{2}
is a double cover of
\mathbf{P}^{1}_{t}\times U^{2}
, with equation

t=\frac{s(s-t_{1}-t_{2}+1)}{s-t_{1}t_{2}}

(3.4.2)

(obtained by solving for
t=t_{3}
in (
2.7.1
)).

The elliptic fibration
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}_{s}\times U^{2}
may be defined by substituting (
3.4.2
) into (
3.4.1
). For each pair
(t_{1},t_{2})\in U^{2}
, it has singular fibers of type
\mathrm{I}_{2}^{*},\mathrm{I}_{2}^{*},\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{2},\mathrm{I}_{2}
at
s=\infty,t_{1}t_{2},0,t_{1},t_{2}
, and
\delta:=t_{1}+t_{2}-1
, respectively. Generically, the Mordell-Weil group is again
(\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})^{2}
. From this we conclude that the Picard lattice of the generic fiber of
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow U^{2}
has rank 18 and determinant
-16
, which agrees with the corresponding data for the lattice of
\Km(A)
, where
A
is the product of two nonisogenous elliptic curves. In fact the two lattices are isomorphic, suggesting that we can find an isomorphism between
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})
and such a Kummer surface. This is in fact the case: there is an
isomorphism
(not just a finite rational map) of K3 surfaces over
U^{2}
:

{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\mathrel{\smash{\meno}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow}\rule{0.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}\Km(\mathcal{E}^{2})

To find the isomorphism, we should look for an elliptic fibration on
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})
with four
\mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}
fibers. One
\mathrm{I}_{0}^{*}
configuration can be found within the union of the identity section
\sigma_{0}
, together with two of the
\mathrm{I}_{2}^{*}
s and two of the
\mathrm{I}_{2}
s, shown here as
2\sigma_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}+b_{1}+b_{2}
:

By Proposition
3.2.2
(3), there exists an elliptic fibration
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
containing this
I_{0}^{*}
as a fiber.
We write it down explicitly: Introduce a function
w\colon{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
by the substitutions (all are equivalent):

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{(s-\delta)(s-t_{1}w)}{(s-t_{1}t_{2})(w-1)}

\displaystyle x-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{(s-t_{1})(s-t_{2}-(t_{1}-1)w)}{(s-t_{1}t_{2})(w-1)}

\displaystyle x-t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{(s-\delta)(s-t_{1})w}{(s-t_{1}t_{2})(w-1)}

Substituting the above into
y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-t)
yields (after absorbing square factors into

y^{2}=-w(w-1)(s-t_{1}t_{2})(s-t_{1}w)(s-t_{2}-(t_{1}-1)w),

and then

brings this into the form

y^{2}=w(w-1)(w-t_{2})u(u-1)(u-t_{1}),

which is the Kummer surface
\Km(\mathcal{E}^{2})
.

3.5
Appendix by Masato Kuwata: Kummer surfaces and Inose surfaces

Throughout this note, the base field
k
is a field of characteristic different from

.

Let

and

.
Let

\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})
corresponding to the projections

(

\displaystyle E_{1}:y^{2}=x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{4}x+a_{6},

Then an affine model of

is given by

(3.5.1)

This equation can be viewed as a cubic curve in

,

over the function field

.
The map
\Km(E_{1}\times E_{2})\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
given by

defines a genus 1 fibration. This is Inose’s pencil. It does not admit a section unless

or

has a

Let

be the Jacobian of Inose’s pencil (

+\bigl(\Delta_{E_{1}}t^{2}-(c_{6}a^{\prime}_{6}+32a_{2}a_{4}a^{\prime}_{2}a^{\prime}_{4}-864a_{6}a^{\prime}_{6}+c^{\prime}_{6}a_{6})+\Delta_{E_{2}}t^{-2}\bigr),

\displaystyle\Delta_{E_{1}}=-16(4a_{2}^{3}a_{6}-a_{2}^{2}a_{4}^{2}-18a_{2}a_{4}a_{6}+4a_{4}^{3}+27a_{6}^{2}),

\displaystyle\Delta_{E_{2}}=-16(4a^{\prime}_{2}{}^{3}a^{\prime}_{6}-a^{\prime}_{2}{}^{2}a^{\prime}_{4}{}^{2}-18a^{\prime}_{2}a^{\prime}_{4}a^{\prime}_{6}+4a^{\prime}_{4}{}^{3}+27a^{\prime}_{6}{}^{2}),

3.6
The semistable fibrations

The verification of Theorem
3.1.1
for the semistable extremal rational elliptic fibrations is more difficult, since now one must keep track of the locations of the singular fibers of
\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
, and also there are fundamentally different choices for
\Sigma_{\infty}
. Each time, we found a genus 1 fibration on
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})
with two
\mathrm{II}^{\ast}
fibers, which means we can apply the techniques of §
3.5
, and conclude that there is a product of elliptic curves
\mathcal{E}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}_{2}\rightarrow U^{2}
and a finite morphism
{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\Km(\mathcal{E}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}_{2})
commuting with the maps to
U^{2}
. We demonstrate this fact with the figures that follow.

However, we were not able to compute
\mathcal{E}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}_{2}
in all cases.
The trouble is that in two of the cases, the genus 1 fibration on

{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}(\mathcal{E})
with two
\mathrm{II}^{\ast}
fibers has a multisection of degree 5
(resp., 6). There is currently no explicit formula for the Weierstrass
equation of the Jacobian of a genus 1 curve with such a multisection.
(Compare with
[
ARVT05
]
, which has formulas
for the Jacobian of a plane cubic, and with
[
AKM+01
]
, which shows
how to proceed for the intersection of two quadrics in
\mathbf{P}^{3}
.)
Fisher
[
Fis18
]
gives a method for finding the equation in all degrees,
and it has
been implemented in Magma for degree
5
. However, the implementation
did not conclude
within a reasonable time in the example that arose. For degree
6
, even
this resource is not available.

Despite this, we can still prove that
\mathcal{E}
is 2-modular via the following argument. Since the cohomology
H^{1}((\mathcal{E}_{1})_{\overline{\eta}_{2}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})\otimes H^{1}((\mathcal{E}_{2})_{\overline{\eta}_{2}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
appears as an irreducible subquotient of

2.3.1
forces

to be isogenous to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
for some elliptic fibration
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
with conductor
N
, which is then 2-modular. The classification of semistable fibrations in Proposition
3.4.2
shows that (at least for large enough characteristic)
N
determines the isogeny class of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, so that in fact
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is isogenous to
\mathcal{E}
.

Remark 3.6.1
.

In all four cases, there is an elliptic parameter for the

fibration
whose divisor is of the form

is
the torsion order of the semistable fibration and

generates the torsion group, while

is a fibral divisor for this

were also to consider the curves

4.1
Fiber products of two elliptic fibrations

The fiber product of two rational elliptic fibrations
S_{1},S_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
over a common base was studied in
[
Sch88
]
, as a means of constructing interesting Calabi-Yau threefolds. To see why such a threefold might result, let us model
S_{i}
as an equation of bidegree
(3,1)
in
\mathbf{P}^{2}\times\mathbf{P}^{1}
. Then the fiber product
S=S_{1}\times_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}S_{2}
is defined by equations of tridegree
(3,0,1),(0,3,1)
in
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}^{2}\times\mathbf{P}^{2}\times\mathbf{P}^{1}
, and so by the adjunction formula, its canonical divisor is
K_{S}=(K_{\mathbf{P}})|_{S}\otimes\mathcal{O}(3,3,2)=\mathcal{O}_{S}
.

The fiber product
S
generally has singular points; to construct (nonsingular) Calabi-Yau threefolds, we must determine whether the canonical divisor is trivial on a desingularization of
S
. Recall that a
crepant resolution

f\colon\tilde{S}\rightarrow S
is a desingularization which does not affect the canonical class:
f^{*}K_{S}=K_{\tilde{S}}
. Thus if our fiber product
S=S_{1}\times_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}S_{2}
admits a crepant resolution, then its desingularization is a Calabi-Yau threefold.

It is not hard to see that the only possible singularities of
S
occur over those points

where both
S_{v}
and
S^{\prime}_{v}
are singular. It is shown in
[
Sch88
]
that as long as
S_{v}
and
S_{v}^{\prime}
are multiplicative, then all singular points of
S
are ordinary double points, and consequently
S
admits a crepant resolution.

However, in the fiber product defining
{\mathcal{Z}}^{3}(\mathcal{E})
, the fibers of the two fibrations
{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1}
at
\infty
are of multiplicative and additive type, respectively, and so we are not in the situation considered in
[
Sch88
]

Remark 4.1.2
.

Similar considerations show a stronger result: The fiber product

The fiber product

4.2
The Kummer threefold

On the other hand, there is a separate Calabi-Yau threefold that we can associate to three elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
; this is a generalization of the Kummer surface associated to the product of two elliptic curves.

Definition 4.2.1
.

Let
Q=(E_{1}\times E_{2}\times E_{3})/V
, where nontrivial elements of the group
V=(\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})^{\oplus 2}
act by negating two of the factors
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}

Proposition 4.2.2
.

The threefold
Q
admits a crepant resolution

Proof.

(Assuming
\car k\neq 2

The product

∎

Now consider

Remark 4.3.2
.

In contrast to the theory of elliptic fibrations on a K3 surface, which
is well developed and draws on the very rich theory of lattice genera,
K3 surface fibrations on Calabi-Yau varieties are not well understood.
Such fibrations are described by certain extremal rays of the ample
cone. However, there is no general theory that allows one to construct a
set of orbit representatives for such extremal rays with respect to the
action of the automorphism group. In any case, this would not be sufficient,
because we might need to consider not only K3 fibrations on one particular
Calabi-Yau model but on all equivalent ones. Though there are finitely
many Calabi-Yau varieties birational to a given one up to isomorphism,
it is not clear how to determine them all in practice or approximately
how many there should be for a variety such as
Q
.
It is known (the relevant facts are nicely summarized in

[
Fry01
, Introduction]
) that the ample cones of different Calabi-Yau
models exhaust the movable cone, but one expects that for a Calabi-Yau
variety of Picard number
51
such as
Q
the geometry of this partition
would be exceedingly complicated. For example, we can blow up the
48

curves of singularities in any order (though blowups in two disjoint curves
commute). The group of automorphisms
acting on this set of Calabi-Yau models is small,
and presumably there are many other ways to obtain models as well.

These problems have discouraged us from attempting to extend the result of
