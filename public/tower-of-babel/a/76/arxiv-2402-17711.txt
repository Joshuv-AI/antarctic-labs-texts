Abstract
. This paper studies the family of interior penalty discontinuous Galerkin methods for solving the Herrmann formulation of the linear elasticity eigenvalue problem in heterogeneous media. By employing a weighted Lamé coefficient norm within the framework of non-compact operators theory, we prove convergence of both continuous and discrete eigenvalue problems as the mesh size approaches zero, independently of the Lamé constants. Additionally, we conduct an a posteriori analysis and propose a reliable and efficient estimator. The theoretical findings are supported by numerical experiments.

1.
Introduction

Recently in
[
15
]
the nearly incompressible elasticity eigenvalue problem has been analyzed. In this reference the authors have proved that the elasticity spectrum converges to the Stokes eigenvalues when the Poisson ratio tends to
1/2
. From this fundamental observation, and with the aid of inf-sup stable families of finite elements for Stokes, the authors approximated the spectrum accurately. Motivated by these findings and in alignment with our research trajectory, we now turn our attention to the application of the interior penalty discontinuous Galerkin method.

Interior Penalty Discontinuous Galerkin Finite Element Method (IPDG) represents a sophisticated approach to tackling eigenvalue problems, leveraging the advantages of both discontinuous Galerkin methods and interior penalty techniques. This method excels in handling problems associated with singularities, discontinuities, or complex geometries by incorporating penalty terms within the weak formulation. The inclusion of these penalty terms facilitates the imposition of continuity constraints across element boundaries, effectively minimizing numerical oscillations and ensuring stability. IPDG proves particularly adept at capturing eigenvalues in problems with variable coefficients or irregular domains. By introducing penalty terms to penalize jumps in the solution and its gradient across element interfaces, this method strikes a balance between accuracy and stability, making it a reliable choice for eigenvalue problems characterized by challenging features and intricate geometries.

The application of the IPDG methods for eigenvalue problems is a current subject of study and we refer to
[
2
,
4
,
17
,
19
,
18
,
20
]
as our main references which explore applications encompassing the Laplace operator, the Maxwell’s equations, Stokes, elasticity and acoustics. These studies collectively demonstrate the broad applicability and efficacy of IPDG techniques in diverse scientific domains. Here the main advantage lies, in one hand, on the flexibility of the method where hanging nodes are allowed, and on the other, the easy computational implementation where with no major difficulties is possible to consider high order approximations for the method. This inherent adaptability not only enables accurate approximation of the spectrum but also highlights the method’s suitability for tackling complex eigenvalue problems. However, there is a price to pay when using this method and is related to the stabilization parameter that precisely penalizes the jumps between elements. While this parameter is crucial for ensuring stability and convergence, its determination can pose significant computational hurdles. Theoretical investigations
[
17
,
19
,
18
,
20
]
have underscored the significance of the stabilization parameter in determining the stability of IPDG methods. The appropriate selection of this parameter depends on several factors, such as boundary conditions, geometrical assumptions and physical parameters, among others. The correct choice of the stabilization parameter is fundamental to achieve an accurate approximation to the physical spectrum, without spurious eigenvalues. The literature suggests that the stabilization parameters leading to accurate results are proportional to the square of the polynomial degree, although the proportionality constant varies depending on the specific physical parameters involved. This empirical observation highlights the intricate interplay between numerical methods and physical phenomena, and emphasizes the need for judicious parameter selection to ensure the fidelity of the computational results.

1.2.
Model problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
be an open and bounded
domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let us assume that
the boundary is divided into two parts
\partial\Omega:=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
,
where
|\Gamma_{D}|>0
. In particular, we assume that the structure is fixed on
\Gamma_{D}
and free of stress on
\Gamma_{N}
.

The eigenvalue problem of interest is given by: Find
(\widehat{\kappa},\boldsymbol{u})
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left[2\widehat{\mu}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\widehat{\lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\boldsymbol{I}\right]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\kappa}\rho\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\left[2\widehat{\mu}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\widehat{\lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\boldsymbol{I}\right]\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{N},

where
\boldsymbol{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
represents the identity matrix,
\sqrt{\widehat{\kappa}}
is the natural frequency,
\boldsymbol{u}
is the displacement and
\rho
is the material density. The Lamé parameters
\widehat{\lambda}
and
\widehat{\mu}
are defined by

\widehat{\lambda}:=\frac{E(x)\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mu}:=\frac{E(x)}{2(1+\nu)},

where
E(x)
represents the space-variable Young’s modulus,
\nu
represents the Poisson ratio which we assume as a constant. The strain tensor is represented by
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
and defined as
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{v})^{\texttt{t}})
, where
t
represents the transpose operator. Here and thereafter we assume the existence of two positive constants

E_{\min}
and
E_{\max}
such that

E_{\min}\leq E(x)\leq E_{\max}.

For the analysis of (
1.1
) we scale the system with the quantity
(1+\nu)
leading to the following problem. Find
(\kappa,\boldsymbol{u})
with
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left[2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\boldsymbol{I}\right]

\displaystyle=\kappa\rho\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\left[2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\boldsymbol{I}\right]\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{N},

where the scaled eigenvalue and the Lamé coefficients are given by

(1.3)

\kappa:=(1+\nu)\widehat{\kappa},\qquad\lambda:=\frac{E(x)\nu}{1-2\nu},\qquad\mu:=\frac{E(x)}{2}.

Note that for a given
E(x)
, the eigenvalue problem (
1.2
) has the advantage of having
\mu
independent of
\nu
. The incompressibility of the material is explicitly given only by the behavior of the constant
\nu
through the Lamé parameter
\lambda
.

With the scaled parameters specified in (
1.3
) we rewrite system (
1.1
) as the following system:
We seek the eigenvalue
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a vector field
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, and a scalar field
p\neq 0
such that

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\kappa\rho\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{\lambda}p

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\left[2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})-p\boldsymbol{I}\right]\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{N}.

We note from (
1.3
) that if the Poisson ratio approaches
1/2
,
then
\lambda\uparrow\infty
.
In such case, we recover the Stokes system.
This is an important matter in view of deriving robust stability and error bounds. From now and on, and for simplicity,
in the analysis below we consider
\rho=1
.

1.3.
Main contribution

To the best of our knowledge, the realm of IPDG methods remains uncharted territory in the context of the model problem (
1.2
). This paper marks the inaugural exploration in this direction. The primary focus is on the family of IPDG methods employed for solving the eigenvalue problem in nearly incompressible elasticity within heterogeneous media. A cornerstone of this research lies in the comprehensive examination of both a priori and a posteriori error analyses, shedding light on the method’s performance. Notably, our proposed estimator exhibits remarkable robustness with respect to the Poisson ratio, underpinning its suitability for addressing complex, real-world scenarios. Furthermore, it is established that the spectrum of the nearly incompressible elasticity eigenvalue problem within heterogeneous media converges to the spectrum of the Stokes problems as the parameter
\lambda
tends towards infinity

2.
Variational formulation and preliminary results

Let us begin by defining the spaces
\mathrm{H}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\;:\;\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\right\}
and
\mathrm{Q}:=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
where we will seek the displacement and the pressure, respectively. Now, by testing
system (
1.2
) with adequate
functions and imposing the boundary conditions,
we end up with the following saddle point variational formulation:
Find
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
with
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\neq(\boldsymbol{0},0)
such that

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,dx-\int_{\Omega}p\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,dx

\displaystyle=\kappa\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}q\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\lambda(x)}pq\,dx

\displaystyle=\,0

\displaystyle\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.

This variational problem can be rewritten as follows:

Find
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)&=&\kappa d(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u},q)-c(p,q)&=&\;0&\forall q\in\mathrm{Q},\end{array}\right.

where the bilinear forms

a:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{H}\to\mathbb{R}
,

b:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
,

c:\mathrm{Q}\times\mathrm{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
,
and

d:\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{H}\to\mathbb{R}

are defined by

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=2\int_{\Omega}\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,dx,\quad b(\boldsymbol{v},q):=-\int_{\Omega}q\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,dx,

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,dx,\quad c(p,q):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\lambda(x)}p\,q\,dx,

for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}
, and
p,q\in\mathrm{Q}
.

To perform the analysis, we need a suitable
norm which in particular depends on the parameters of the problem.
With this in mind, and for all
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times Q
,
we define the following weighted norm,

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}\times Q}^{2}:=\|\mu(x)^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mu(x)^{-1/2}q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\lambda(x)^{-1/2}q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Moreover, in what follows, we assume that
\mu(x),1/\lambda(x)\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Let us introduce and define the kernel of
b(\cdot,\cdot)
by

\mathcal{K}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}\,:\,b(\boldsymbol{v},q)=0,\quad\forall\,q\in Q\}\,=\,\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}\,:\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,=\,0\,\,\,\,{\rm in}\,\,\Omega\},

and let us recall that the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the inf-sup condition:

\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}}}{{\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0}}}\frac{|b(\boldsymbol{v},q)|}{\|\mu(x)^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}}\geq\beta_{2}\|\mu(x)^{-1/2}q\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{Q},

with an inf-sup constant
\beta_{2}>0
only depending on
\Omega
; see
[
16
]
, for instance.

Now, we are in a position to introduce the solution operator which we denote by

\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
and is such that
\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
, where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
is the solution of the following well posed source problem: Given
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
find
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{p})&=&d(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H},\\
b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},q)-c(\widehat{p},q)&=&\;0&\forall q\in\mathrm{Q}.\end{array}\right.

Thanks to the Babuška-Brezzi theory
we have that
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
is well defined and the following estimate holds
\|\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}\boldsymbol{f},\widehat{p})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}

with a constant depending on the inverse of
\mu
and the Poincaré constant.
It is easy to check that
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
is a selfadjoint
operator with respect to the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner product. Also, let
\chi
be a real number such that
\chi\neq 0
. Notice that
(\chi,\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}
is an eigenpair of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
if and only if there exists
p\in\mathrm{Q}
such that,
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))
solves problem (
2.1
) with
\chi:=1/\kappa
.

The following result states an additional regularity
result for problem (
2.2
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})
be the unique solution
of (
2.2
), then there exists

s\in(0,1]
such that
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega)

and the following estimate holds (see for instance
[
11
]
or
[
21
]
)

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+\|\widehat{p}\|_{s,\Omega}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where the hidden constant depends on
\Omega
and the Lamé coefficients.

Let us remark that Lemma
2.1
holds true
even for
\lambda\uparrow\infty
(Further comments on this fact can be seen in
[
18
]
).

As a consequence of this result, we conclude the compactness of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
.
In fact, since
\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega)^{d}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}

we have that
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
is a compact operator. Hence, we are in position to establish the spectral characterization of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

The spectrum of
\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu}
satisfies
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}_{\nu})=\{0\}\cup\{\chi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\{\chi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive eigenvalues.

On the other hand, from
[
8
]
we have the following regularity result for the eigenfunctions of the eigenproblem (
2.1
).

Theorem 2.3
.

If
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
solves (
2.1
), then, there exists
r>0
such that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{H}^{1+r}(\Omega)^{d}
and
p\in\mathrm{H}^{r}(\Omega)
. Moreover, the following estimate holds

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1+r,\Omega}+\|p\|_{r,\Omega}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Now,
Let us end this section introducing the bilinear form

\mathcal{A}:(\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q})\times(\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q)):=a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)+b(\boldsymbol{u},q)+c(p,q),

which allows us to rewrite the eigenvalue problem as follows. Find
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

(2.3)

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q))=\kappa d(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times Q.

Let us remark that
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a bounded bilinear form, in the sense that there exists a positive constant
\widehat{C}:=\max\{2,\mu_{min}^{-1/2}\mu_{max}^{1/2}\}

such that

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q))\leq\widehat{C}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}\times Q}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}\times Q}\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}.

Next we state the stability result for the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
, there exists
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
with
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\lesssim\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q)).

3.
The DG method

Now our aim is to introduce the DG methods. In order to do this, we need to set some notations and definitions, inherent for these type of methods, as DG spaces, jumps, averages, and discrete bilinear forms. For the sake of completeness, in this section we will rigorously monitor the constants in order to provide bounds that explicitly reflect the importance of the physical parameters in the stability of the method.

3.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape regular family of meshes which subdivide the domain
\bar{\Omega}
into
triangles/tetrahedra that we denote by
K
. Let us denote by
h_{K}

the diameter of any element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and let
h
be the maximum of the diameters of all the
elements of the mesh, i.e.
h:=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\{h_{K}\}
.

Let
F
be a closed set. We say that
F\subset\overline{\Omega}

is an interior edge/face if
F
has a positive
(n-1)
-dimensional
measure and if there are distinct elements
K
and
K^{\prime}

such that
F=\bar{K}\cap\bar{K}^{\prime}
. A closed
subset
F\subset\overline{\Omega}
is a boundary edge/face if
there exists
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
such that
F
is an edge/face
of
K
and
F=\bar{K}\cap\Gamma
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
be the sets of interior edges/faces
and boundary edges/faces, respectively.
We assume that the boundary mesh
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
is
compatible with the partition
\Gamma=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
, namely,

\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D}}F=\Gamma_{D}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}}F=\Gamma_{N},

where
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D}:=\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial};\quad F\subset\Gamma_{D}\}
and

\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}:=\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}:\quad F\subset\Gamma_{N}\}
.
Also we denote
\mathcal{F}_{h}:=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
and
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}:=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D}
.
Also, for any element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we introduce the set

\mathcal{F}(K):=\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}:\,\,F\subset\partial K\}
of edges/faces composing the boundary of
K
.

For any
t\geq 0
, we define the following broken Sobolev space

\mathrm{H}^{t}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{n}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{n}:\quad\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[\mathrm{H}^{t}(K)]^{n}\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

Also, the space of the skeletons of the triangulations

\mathcal{T}_{h}
is defined by
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h}):=\prod_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\mathrm{L}^{2}(F).

In the forthcoming analysis,
h_{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h})

will represent the piecewise constant function
defined by
h_{\mathcal{F}}|_{F}:=h_{F}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
,
where
h_{F}
denotes the diameter of an edge/face
F
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
be the space of piecewise polynomials respect with to

\mathcal{T}_{h}
of degree at most
m\geq 0
;
namely,

\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{d}:\,v|_{K}\in[\mathcal{P}_{m}(K)]^{d},\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}.

Let
k\geq 1
. To approximate the displacement we introduce the space

\mathrm{H}_{h}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{d}\,:\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{K}\in[\mathcal{P}_{k}(K)]^{d},\,\,\,\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\},

and for the pressure, the finite element space is
\mathrm{Q}_{h}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{k-1}(K),\,\,\,\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\}
.

Let us assume
\mathcal{F}(K)\cap\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. For a scalar field
q
, we define the average
\{q\}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
and the jump
\llbracket q\rrbracket\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
by
\{q\}:=(q_{K}+q_{K^{\prime}})/2
and

Symmetric and nonsymmetric DG schemes

With the discrete spaces defined previously at hand, we introduce the discrete counterpart of (

where

and
\texttt{a}_{S}>0
is a positive constant which we will refer as the stabilization parameter and

.
On the other hand, we define the bounded bilinear form

.

We have that

is bounded on the broken weighted norm. Indeed, for
\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}
and applying (

\leq 2C\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
+\texttt{a}_{S}\|\mu^{1/2}h_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\|h_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}^{-1/2}h_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\\
+2\|h_{\mathcal{F}}^{1/2}\{\mu(x)\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\}\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\|h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}+2\epsilon\|h_{\mathcal{F}}^{1/2}\{\mu(x)\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\}\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\|h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\\
\leq 2C\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
+\texttt{a}_{S}C_{E_{\min}}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)h_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)h_{\mathcal{F}}\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\\
+2C_{\texttt{tr}}C_{E_{\min}}^{2}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\\
+2\epsilon C_{\texttt{tr}}C_{E_{\min}}^{2}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\\

where
C_{1}:=2\max\{C,C_{E_{\min}}\texttt{a}_{S},C_{\texttt{tr}}C_{E_{\min}}^{2},\epsilon C_{\texttt{tr}}C_{E_{\min}}^{2},1\}
.

For

|b_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})|\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{h}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}q_{h}\right|+\left|\int_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\{q_{h}\}\llbracket\underline{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}}\rrbracket\right|\\

+C_{\texttt{tr}}C_{E_{\max}}C_{E_{\min}}\|\mu^{-1/2}(x)q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\mu^{1/2}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\underline{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\\

Remark 3.1
.

Observe that the parameter

dictates if the IPDG method is symmetric or non-symmetric. More precisely, if

we obtain
the classic symmetric interior penalty method (SIP),
if

and if
\epsilon=0
the incomplete interior penalty method (IIP). Clearly when non-symmetric methods are considered, complex computed eigenvalues are expected when
the spectrum is approximated. Moreover, the SIP attains optimal order of convergence for the approximation of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions, whereas
the NIP and IIP methods deliver suboptimal orders.

Finally, the IPDG discretization of (

Lemma 3.2

(ellipticity of

)
.

For any

, there exists a positive parameter
\texttt{a}^{*}
such that for all

where

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathcal{K}_{h}
. Hence, applying Cauchy-Schwarz and Korn’s inequalities and the the well known identity
\displaystyle ab\leq\frac{a^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{\eta b^{2}}{2}
for

and

a_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})=\int_{\Omega}2\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\int_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\frac{\texttt{a}_{S}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}2\mu(x)\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\\
-2(1+\epsilon)\int_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\{\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\}\cdot\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\\
\geq 2C_{\texttt{Korn}}E_{\min}\|\widetilde{\mu}^{1/2}\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+2\texttt{a}_{S}\|\mu^{1/2}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}^{2}\\
+2(1+\epsilon)E_{\max}\left(-\frac{\|h_{\mathcal{F}}^{1/2}\widetilde{\mu}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}^{2}}{2\eta}-\eta\frac{\|h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\widetilde{\mu}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}^{2}}{2}\right)\\
\geq\frac{1}{E_{\max}}\underbrace{\left(2C_{\texttt{Korn}}E_{\min}-\frac{2(1+\epsilon)C_{E_{\max}}}{2\eta}\right)}_{:=\widehat{C}_{1}}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\\
+\underbrace{\left(\texttt{a}_{S}-\frac{E_{\max}(1+\epsilon)\eta}{2E_{\min}}\right)}_{:=\widehat{C}_{2}}\|\mu^{1/2}(x)h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}^{2}\geq\min\{\widehat{C}_{1},\widehat{C}_{2}\}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}^{2}.

We observe that to conclude the proof, the parameter

must be chosen as

\eta>\frac{(1+\epsilon)C_{E_{\max}}}{2C_{\texttt{Korn}}E_{\min}}\quad\text{and hence}\quad\texttt{a}_{S}>\texttt{a}^{*}:=\frac{(1+\epsilon)^{2}C_{E_{\max}}E_{\max}}{4E_{\min}^{2}C_{\texttt{Korn}}}.

This leads to the positiveness of
\widehat{C}_{1}
and
\widehat{C}_{2}
and hence, the proof.
∎

4.
Convergence and error estimates

The aim of this section is to derive convergence results and error estimates for our DG methods. Despite to the fact that
\boldsymbol{T}
is compact, the classic theory of compact operators is not enough to conclude the convergence in norm between the continuous and discrete solution operators, since the numerical method of our interest is non conforming. Is this reason, and following the spirit of
[
2
]
, why we resort to the theory of non compact operators of
[
6
,
7
]
.

In what follows, we will denote by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{H}(h),\mathrm{H}(h))}

the corresponding norm acting from
\mathrm{H}(h)
into the same space.
In addition, we will denote by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{H}_{h},\mathrm{H}(h))}

the norm of an operator restricted to the discrete subspace
\mathrm{H}_{h}
;
namely, if
\boldsymbol{L}:\mathrm{H}(h)\to\mathrm{H}(h)
, then

\lVert\boldsymbol{L}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{H}_{h},\mathrm{H}(h))}:=\sup_{\mathbf{0}\neq\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\rVert_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\rVert_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}.

Our first task is to prove the following properties

•

P1.
\lVert\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{H}_{h},\mathrm{H}(h))}\to 0
as
h\to 0
.

•

P2.
\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathrm{H}
, there holds
\inf_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\tau}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\rVert_{\mathrm{H}(h)}\,\,\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad h\to 0
.

We need this properties in order to establish spectral correctness (see
[
6
]
)
for all the discrete methods (symmetric or nonsymmetric). Property P2 is immediate as a consequence of the density of continuous piecewise degree
k
polynomial functions in
\mathrm{H}_{h}
. The task now is to prove P1.

The following convergence result holds for the continuous and discrete solution operators.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{H}
, the following estimate holds

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}\lesssim h^{s}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where
s>0
and the hidden constant is independent of
h
.

Proof.

For
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{H}
we have
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}:=\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\boldsymbol{f}
. Then, there exists a constant
\widetilde{C}>0
such that the following Céa estimate holds

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widehat{p}-\widehat{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}}\\
\leq\widetilde{C}\inf_{(\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\widehat{q}_{h})\in\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q_{h}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\widehat{p}-\widehat{q}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}}\\
=\widetilde{C}\left(\inf_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}(h)}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}+\inf_{\widehat{q}_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}}\|\widehat{p}-\widehat{q}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{Q}_{h}}\right).

For the first infimum on the identity above, if
\Pi_{h}
represents the Lagrange interpolation operator, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}(h)}

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}\leq\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\Pi_{h}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}

\displaystyle=\|\mu^{1/2}(x)\nabla(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\Pi_{h}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\mu^{1/2}(x)h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1/2}\llbracket\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\Pi_{h}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}

(4.1)

\displaystyle\leq C_{E_{\max}}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\Pi_{h}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C_{E_{\max}}C\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s}\leq C_{E_{\max}}C\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

For the second infimum, if
\textrm{P}_{h}
represents the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-projection operator, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{q}_{h}\in\mathrm{Q}_{h}}\|\widehat{p}-\widehat{q}_{h}\|_{\mathrm{Q}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{p}-\textrm{P}_{h}\widehat{p}\|_{\mathrm{Q}}=\|\mu^{-1/2}(x)(\widehat{p}-\textrm{P}_{h}\widehat{p})\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\lambda^{-1/2}(x)(\widehat{p}-\textrm{P}_{h}\widehat{p})\|_{0,\Omega}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\leq(E_{\min}+E_{\min})\|\widehat{p}-\textrm{P}_{h}\widehat{p}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C(2E_{\min})\|\widehat{p}\|_{s,\Omega}\leq CE_{\min}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Then, combining (
4
) and (
4
) we conclude the proof.
∎

The previous lemma states that if we consider a compact subset
E

of the complex plane such that
E\cap\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})=\emptyset
for

small enough and for all

, operator

is invertible. Moreover, there exists a positive constant

independent of

such that
\lVert(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})^{-1}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{H}(h),\mathrm{H}(h))}\leq C

for all

. This fact is important since it
determines that the numerical method is spurious free for
h
small enough.
This is summarized in the following result proved in
[

]
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{C}
be a compact subset not intersecting
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})
.
Then, there exists
h_{0}>0
such that, if

, then
E\cap\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}_{h})=\emptyset.

We recall the definition of the resolvent operator of
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
respectively:

\displaystyle(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T})^{-1}\,:\,\mathrm{H}\to\mathrm{H}\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}),

\displaystyle(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})^{-1}\,:\,\mathrm{H}_{h}\to\mathrm{H}_{h}\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}_{h}).

We introduce the definition of the
gap

\widehat{\delta}
between two closed

\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
:

\widehat{\delta}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\max\big\{\delta(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}),\delta(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X})\big\},

where

\delta(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ \left\|x\right\|_{0,\Omega}=1}\left(\inf_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\|x-y\right\|_{0,\Omega}\right).

Let
\kappa
be an isolated eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{T}

and let

\kappa

\boldsymbol{T}
lying in

We introduce the spectral projector corresponding to the continuous

\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\left(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}\right)^{-1}\,dz:\mathrm{H}(h)\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}(h),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{h}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\left(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\right)^{-1}\,dz:\mathrm{H}(h)\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}(h).

The following approximation result for the spectral projections holds.

Lemma 4.8
.

There holds

\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\|\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}-\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{h}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{H}_{h},\mathrm{H}(h))}=0.

Proof.

See
[
2
, Theorem 5.1]
.
∎

The next result provides, for the proposed IPDG methods, an error estimate for the eigenfunctions and a double order of convergence for the eigenvalues. More precisely, the result presents
estimates for symmetric and nonsymmetric methods, where optimal and suboptimal order of convergence are attained, respectively.

Theorem 4.9
.

There exists a strictly positive constant
h_{0}
such that, for
\varepsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}
and
h<h_{0}
there holds

\widehat{\delta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}(\mathrm{H},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{h}(\mathrm{H}_{h}))\lesssim h^{\min\{r,k\}},\quad\text{ and }\quad\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|\kappa-\kappa_{i,h}|\lesssim\,h^{\sigma\min\{r,k\}},

where

and the hidden constants are independent of

.

Proof.

The proof for the eigenfunction estimate follows the same arguments of
[
17
, Lemma 7]
. Let
(\kappa_{i,h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathrm{H}_{h}\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}
be the solution of (
3.2
) with
i=1,\ldots,m
and let

(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}

Let us consider the following well known algebraic identity

(4.3)

\mathcal{A}_{h}((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}),(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}))-\kappa(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})=\left(\kappa_{i,h}-\kappa\right)(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}).

Hence, taking modulus to
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}_{h}((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}),(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}))|

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|^{2}+\|p-p_{h}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\delta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}(\mathrm{H},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{h}(\mathrm{H}_{h}))\lesssim h^{2\min\{r,k\}}.

On the other hand

(4.5)

\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq\widehat{\delta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}(\mathrm{H},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{h}(\mathrm{H}_{h}))\lesssim h^{2\min\{r,k\}}.

Also, invoking (

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{h}((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}),(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}))}{|\kappa_{i,h}|}\geq\frac{\widehat{\alpha}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}}}{|\kappa_{i,h}|}\geq\widehat{C}>0.

Hence, plugin (
4
), (
4.5
), and (
4.6
) in (

) we conclude the proof.

5.
A posteriori error analysis

This section aims to present a viable residual-based error estimator for the Stokes/elasticity eigenvalue problem. The primary objective is to demonstrate the equivalence between the proposed estimator and the actual error. Furthermore, in the subsequent analysis, our focus will be exclusively on eigenvalues with a multiplicity of 1. Having established the a priori error estimates accurately in terms of the stabilization constant, the bounding constants will not be followed up during this section.

5.1.
The local and global indicators

In this section, we introduce an a posteriori error estimator based on residuals for the elasticity eigenvalue problem. The proposed estimator also works with the limit case
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. For non-symmetric IPDG methods, the eigenvalue can be complex. However, from
[
19
]
it is observed that the imaginary part is negligible. Hence, we provide the analysis for the real part of the spectrum, which works for symmetric and non-symmetric IPDG.

Consider an eigenpair approximation
(\kappa_{h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathrm{H}_{h}\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}
. For every element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the interior residual estimator
\eta_{R_{K}}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\eta_{R_{K}}^{2}:=

\displaystyle\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}h_{K}(\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nabla\cdot(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))-\nabla p_{h})\|_{0,K}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|((2\mu_{h})^{-1}+\lambda^{-1})^{-1/2}[\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+(1/\lambda)\,p_{h}]\|_{0,K}^{2},

and the facet residual estimator
\eta_{\mathcal{F}_{K}}
by

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathcal{F}_{K}}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\partial K\cap\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}}\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{1/2}[\![(p_{h}\boldsymbol{I}-2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))\textbf{n}]\!]\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\partial K\cap\mathcal{F}_{h}^{N}}\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{1/2}(p_{h}\boldsymbol{I}-2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))\textbf{n}\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}},

where
\boldsymbol{I}
represents the identity matrix. Moving forward, we present the estimator
\eta_{J_{K}}
, designed to quantify the jump in the approximate solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
.

\eta_{J_{K}}^{2}:=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\partial K\cap\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}}\|(2\mu_{h})^{1/2}\gamma_{h}^{1/2}[\![\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]\!]\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}}+\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\partial K\cap\mathcal{F}_{h}^{D}}\|(2\mu_{h})^{1/2}\gamma_{h}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\otimes\boldsymbol{n}\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}},

with
\gamma_{h}=\texttt{a}_{S}/h_{\mathcal{F}}
, where
\texttt{a}_{S}
denotes the penalty parameter as discussed in the section
3
. The local error indicator, obtained by summing the three aforementioned terms, is defined as:

\eta_{K}^{2}:=\eta_{R_{K}}^{2}+\eta_{\mathcal{F}_{K}}^{2}+\eta_{J_{K}}^{2}.

Lastly, we present the (global) a posteriori error estimator

(5.1)

\eta:=\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{K}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

and the data oscillation term is given by

\Theta:=\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Theta_{K}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\Theta_{K}:=\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}(\mu-\mu_{h})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0,K}
.

5.2.
Reliability

Following the approach in
[
13
,
14
]
, we define
\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}=\mathrm{H}_{h}\cap\mathrm{H}
.
The orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}
in
\mathrm{H}_{h}
with respect to the norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}
is denoted as
\mathrm{H}_{h}^{\perp}
.
Consequently, we have the decomposition
\mathrm{H}_{h}=\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}\oplus\mathrm{H}_{h}^{\perp}
, allowing us to uniquely decompose the DG velocity approximation into
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c}+\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{r}
,
where
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{r}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}^{\perp}
.
Applying the triangle inequality, we can express:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{r}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}.

Referring to
[
13
, Proposition 4.1]
, we derive the upper bound for the second term.

(5.2)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{r}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}\lesssim\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{J_{K}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

It is important to remark that the DG form
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
lacks a well-defined definition for functions
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
within
\mathrm{H}
. This challenge can be addressed by employing an appropriate lifting operator, as outlined in
[
5
,
14
]
. However, we explore an alternative approach here, wherein the DG form
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is decomposed into multiple components.

a_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=(2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}))+C_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+J_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),

where
C_{h}
and
J_{h}
are defined by

\displaystyle C_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}\{2\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u})\}\cdot\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\rrbracket-\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}\{2\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v})\}\cdot\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}\rrbracket,

\displaystyle J_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}\frac{\texttt{a}_{S}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}{2\mu(x)}\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\rrbracket.

The following result provides an estimate for the term
C_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
in terms of the indicator
\eta_{J_{K}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}
; then, the following relation holds:

C_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})\lesssim|\epsilon|\texttt{a}_{S}^{-1/2}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{J_{K}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}^{c}_{h}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}.

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}
, it follows that

C_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})=-\epsilon\int_{\mathcal{F}^{*}_{h}}\{2\mu(x)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v})\}\cdot\llbracket\boldsymbol{u}\rrbracket.

Utilizing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields:

\displaystyle C_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})

\displaystyle\lesssim|\epsilon|\left(\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\gamma_{h}^{-1}\|[\![(2\mu)^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}]\!]\|_{0,\mathcal{F}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}\gamma_{h}\|(2\mu)^{1/2}[\![\boldsymbol{u}_{h}]\!]\|_{0,\mathcal{F}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\hskip-9.95863pt.

Applying a trace estimate in conjunction with a discrete inverse inequality for an edge/face
\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}
, where
\mathcal{F}=K_{1}\cap K_{2}
if
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}=K_{1}
,
K_{2}=\emptyset
if
\mathcal{F}\subset\partial\Omega
, results in:

\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}\|_{0,E}\lesssim h_{K}^{-1/2}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}\|_{0,K_{1}\cup K_{2}}.

Thus we have

C_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})\lesssim|\epsilon|\texttt{a}_{S}^{-1/2}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|(2\mu)^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}\|^{2}_{0,K}\right)^{1/2}\,\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{J_{K}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Let us denote by
{\Pi}_{h}:\mathrm{H}\to\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}
the Scott-Zhang interpolation operator
[
22
]
(see also
[
14
, eq. (4.5) and eq. (4.6)]
), which is stable

\|\nabla({\Pi}_{h}\boldsymbol{v})\|_{0}\lesssim\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}

and satisfies the following
interpolation property

(5.3)

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-2}\|\boldsymbol{v}-{\Pi}_{h}\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{0,K}+\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{*}}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{0,E}\lesssim\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}^{2},

for any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}=\Pi_{h}\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}^{c}
, where
\Pi_{h}
denotes the Scott-Zhang interpolation of
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}
. For any
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\mathrm{H}_{h}
,
p_{h}\in Q_{h}
, and
\kappa_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the following estimate holds:

\kappa_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})-(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}))+(p_{h},\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}))\lesssim\eta{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}.

Proof.

Employing integration by parts on each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we obtain:

\kappa_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})-(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}))+(p_{h},\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}))\\
\qquad=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{K}(\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nabla\cdot(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}))-\nabla p_{h})(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})d\boldsymbol{x}\\
\qquad\qquad+\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{\partial K}(p_{h}\boldsymbol{I}-2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})\textbf{n}_{K}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})d\boldsymbol{s}=T_{1}+T_{2}.

Now the task is to estimate the terms
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. Let us begin with
T_{1}
. By applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
5.3
), we arrive at:

T_{1}\lesssim\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|h_{K}(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}(\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nabla\cdot 2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-\nabla p_{h})\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
\times\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{K}^{-2}\|(2\mu_{h})^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})\|^{2}_{0,K}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{R_{K}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}.

Given that
(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})|_{\partial\Omega}=\boldsymbol{0}
, we can express
T_{2}
in terms of a sum over interior facets:

T_{2}=\sum_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}}\int_{E}[\![(p_{h}\boldsymbol{I}-2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\boldsymbol{n}]\!](\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})d\boldsymbol{s}.

Once more, the application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
5.3

T_{2}\lesssim\left(\sum_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}}h_{\mathcal{F}}\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}[\![(p_{h}\boldsymbol{I}-2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\boldsymbol{n}]\!]\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}}\right)^{1/2}\\
\times\left(\sum_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}}h_{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\|(2\mu_{h})^{1/2}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\eta_{\mathcal{F}_{K}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}.

Consolidating the aforementioned estimates establishes the desired result.
∎∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
be the solution of the continuous eigenvalue problem (

)
and let
(\kappa_{h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathrm{H}_{h}\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}
be the solution of the IPDG discretization given by (

for the conforming velocity and pressure errors

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}\lesssim\eta+\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}(\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0}+\Theta.

Proof.

Using
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
, there exists a pair
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathrm{H}\setminus\{0\}\times\mathrm{Q}
such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}^{2}\lesssim{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h};\boldsymbol{v},q),

and
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}
.

, we have

{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h};\boldsymbol{v},q)=(2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}))-(p-p_{h},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\\
-(q,\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c}))-(q,1/\lambda(p-p_{h})).

Based on (
2.3

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h};\boldsymbol{v},q)=\kappa(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-(2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}))+(p_{h},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v})+(q,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c})+(q,1/\lambda p_{h})\\

as the Scott-Zhang interpolation of

. The utilization of (

Then we have
\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h};\boldsymbol{v},q)=T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}
,
where the terms

,

are defined by

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle:=\kappa_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})-(2\mu_{h}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c}))+(p_{h},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h}^{c})

\displaystyle+(q,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h})+(q,1/\lambda p_{h}),

\displaystyle:=(q,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{r})+(2\mu\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{r}),\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})),

By employing Lemma

T_{1}\lesssim\eta{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}.

T_{2}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{r}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}\lesssim\eta{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}.

.
The application of Cauchy-Schwarz and the Poincaré inequality results in:

T_{4}\lesssim\left(\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}(\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0}+\Theta\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{v}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|_{\mathrm{H}(h)}}

Finally, with the combination of the aforementioned information with the estimate
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}\lesssim{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}
,
we attain the desired outcome.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
(\kappa,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathrm{H}\times\mathrm{Q}
be the solution of the continuous eigenvalue problem (
2.1
)
and let
(\kappa_{h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathrm{H}_{h}\times\mathrm{Q}_{h}
be the solution of the IPDG discretization given by (
3.2
). Then the following a posteriori error bound is derived:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{c},p-p_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\mathrm{H}(h)\times Q}\lesssim\eta+\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}(\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0}+\Theta,

where the hidden constant in the expression is independent
of the penalty parameter
\texttt{a}_{S}
, provided that
\texttt{a}_{S}
is sufficiently large and satisfies
\texttt{a}_{S}\geq 1
.

Proof.

The assertion is readily established by jointly applying Lemma
5.3
and (
5.2
).
∎

Applying the algebraic identity in conjunction with Theorem
5.4
results in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.5
.

If
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
and
p\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, then the eigenvalue error satisfies

|\kappa-\kappa_{h}|\lesssim\eta^{2}+\|(2\mu_{h})^{-1/2}(\kappa\boldsymbol{u}-\kappa_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0}^{2}+\Theta^{2}.

6.
Numerical experiments

In this section we report some numerical tests in order to assess the performance of the proposed numerical method in the computation of the eigenvalues of problem (
3.2
). These results have been obtained using the FEniCSx project
[
3
,
23
]
, while the meshes are constructed using GMSH
[
10
]
.The convergence rates of the eigenvalues have been obtained with a standard least square fitting and highly refined meshes.

We denote by
N
the mesh refinement level, whereas
dof
denotes the number of degrees of freedom. We denote by
\kappa_{h_{i}}
the i-th discrete eigenfrequency, and by
\widehat{\kappa}_{h_{i}}:=\kappa_{h_{i}}/(1+\nu)
the unscaled version of
\kappa_{h_{i}}
.

Hence, we denote the error on the
i
-th eigenvalue by
\texttt{err}(\widehat{\kappa}_{i})
with

\texttt{err}(\widehat{\kappa}_{i}):=|\widehat{\kappa}_{h_{i}}-\widehat{\kappa}_{i}|.

We remark that computing
\sqrt{\widehat{\kappa}_{h_{i}}}
or
\sqrt{\widehat{\kappa}_{i}}
gives the corresponding discrete and exact eigenfrequency.

With the aim of assessing the performance of our estimator, we consider domains with singularities in two and three dimensions in order to observe the improvement of the convergence rate. On each adaptive iteration, we use the blue-green marking strategy to refine each
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
whose indicator
\eta_{T^{\prime}}
satisfies

\eta_{T^{\prime}}\geq 0.5\max\{\eta_{T}\,:\,T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\}.

We define the effectivity indexes with respect to
\eta
and the eigenvalue
\widehat{\kappa_{i}}
by

\texttt{eff}(\widehat{\kappa_{i}}):=\texttt{err}(\widehat{\kappa_{i}})/\eta^{2}
.

We note that other spectral analysis using DG methods introduce spurious eigenvalues when the stabilization parameter is not correctly chosen (see for instance
[
18
,
19
]
). In the first part of this section, we study the safe range where the methods is spurious-free by comparing with references values from the literature. We will analyze the influence stabilization parameter
\texttt{a}_{S}
. More precisely, the stabilization parameter
\texttt{a}_{S}
in the bilinear form
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
(cf. (
3.3
)) will be chosen proportionally to the square of the polynomial degree
k
as
\texttt{a}_{S}=\texttt{a}k^{2}
with
\texttt{a}>0
. Also, taking into account the theoretical convergence rates presented Theorem
4.9
, the results in Table
2
below and the results from the a posteriori analysis, the experiments will be focused on the SIP method in order to observe the recovery of the optimal double convergence rate.
