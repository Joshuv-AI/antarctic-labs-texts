Sample Recycling for Nested Simulation with Application in Portfolio Risk Measurement
Kun Zhang, Ben M. Feng, Guangwu Liu, Shiyu Wang

Abstract
Nested simulation is a natural approach to tackle nested estimation problems in operations research and financial engineering. The outer-level simulation generates outer scenarios and the inner-level simulations are ran in each outer scenario to estimate the corresponding conditional expectation. The resulting sample of conditional expectations is then used to estimate different risk measures of interest. Despite its flexibility, nested simulation is notorious for its heavy computational burden. We introduce a novel simulation procedure that reuses inner simulation outputs to improve the efficiency and accuracy in solving nested estimation problems. We analyze the convergence rates of the bias, variance, and MSE of the resulting estimator. In addition, central limit theorems and variance estimators are presented, which lead to asymptotically valid confidence intervals for the nested risk measure of interest. We conduct numerical studies on two financial risk measurement problems. Our numerical studies show consistent results with the asymptotic analysis and show that the proposed approach outperforms the standard nested simulation and a state-of-art regression approach for nested estimation problems. Key words : nested simulation, risk management, likelihood ratio method, central limit theorem, confidence interval

In theory, the risk estimator in a nested simulation procedure converges to the true risk measure as the numbers of outer and inner simulations grow.
However, depending on the complexity of the risk factor models and the derivative payoffs, every inner replication can be quite time-consuming to compute.
So, in practice, the simulation budget
\Gamma
can be an excessive computational burden and unbearably large computations may be required to achieve satisfactory accuracy.

Alleviating the computational burden, by different means and in different ways, has attracted much research attention in the simulation literature.
Firstly, some studies focus on intelligent allocations of a fixed simulation budget
\Gamma
so that the resulting risk measure
\rho
is accurately estimated.

Lee 1998
,
Lee and Glynn 2003
, and
Gordy and Juneja 2010
analyze the nested simulation estimator and demonstrate that, under some assumptions, the asymptotic mean squared error (MSE) of the standard nested risk estimator diminishes at an optimal rate of
\Gamma^{-2/3}
;
Gordy and Juneja 2010
shows that this optimal convergence rate is achieved when
m^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{1/3})
and
n=\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{2/3})
as
\Gamma\rightarrow\infty
.

Broadie et al. 2011
proposes a sequential allocation scheme where different outer scenarios have different number of inner replications when estimating the probabilities of large portfolio losses.
The MSE of the resulting risk estimator is shown to have a rate of convergence of
\Gamma^{-4/5+\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Liu et al. 2010
and
Lan et al. 2010
use ranking-and-selection techniques to adaptively allocate the simulation budget to estimate CVaR and its confidence interval, respectively.

A second line of research aims to reduce the standard nested simulation’s computational burden by estimating
\mathbb{E}[H|X]
via regression or metamodeling techniques.
For example, least-square Monte Carlo (LSMC)
(
Longstaff and Schwartz 2001
;
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 2001
)
is a quintessential parametric approach for pricing American options, where a regression model is used to approximate the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[H|X]
.
See also
Carriere 1996
for a general discussion of nonparametric regression techniques in Monte Carlo simulation.

Broadie et al. 2015
applies this LSMC approach in nested estimation of financial risk and shows that the MSE of the resulting risk estimator converges at the order of
\Gamma^{-1+\delta}
for any
\delta>0
.
Despite fast convergence rate, the MSE generally converges to a nonzero asymptotic squared bias that depends on the selection of basis functions.

Liu and Staum 2010
considers a metamodeling approach that estimates
\mathbb{E}[H|X]
by a stochastic kriging model
(
Ankenman et al. 2010
)
.
Besides selecting appropriate basis functions and covariance functions, the implementation of stochastic kriging is not trivial and may be prone to numerical instability
(
Staum 2009
)
.

Hong et al. 2017
proposes a kernel smoothing approach, which estimates
\mathbb{E}[H|X]
by the well-known Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator
(
Nadaraya 1964
;
Watson 1964
)
.
The MSE of the resulting risk estimator achieves a convergence rate of
\Gamma^{-\min\{1,4/(d+2)\}}
, where
d
is the problem dimension.
These approaches use simulation outputs from different scenarios, sometimes from a pilot experiment, to calibrate the regression model or metamodel that approximates or predicts
\mathbb{E}[H|X]
for different scenarios.
While the pooling of simulation outputs improves simulation efficiency, these approaches suffer from modeling errors that depend on selection of basis functions, covariance functions, or kernel bandwidth.
As a result, these approaches lead to biased estimators; sometimes this bias vanishes asymptotically, sometimes the bias persists.

In this article we study a novel simulation procedure, called the green nested simulation (GNS) procedure, that pools inner simulation outputs from different outer scenarios but avoids the difficulties in the regression- and metamodeling-based techniques.
The contributions of our study include:

1.

We propose an efficient simulation procedure that is non-nested in nature and recycles the same set of inner simulation outputs via the likelihood ratio method to estimate
\mathbb{E}[H|X]
in different scenarios.
The proposed procedure does not require any model selection or calibration.

2.

We establish that the asymptotic bias, variance, and MSE of the risk estimator all converge to zero at rate
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{-1})
.
This convergence rate is faster than that of nested stimulation with optimal allocation and that of the kernel-based approach.
Most importantly,
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{-1})
is the same fast convergence rate as a non-nested Monte Carlo simulation.

3.

We establish central limit theorem (CLT) and valid variance estimates for the nested simulation estimators for different forms of
\rho
.
These results enable users to construct valid confidence intervals for nested simulation estimators without running macro replications.
The analysis is non-trivial considering that all conditional expectations are estimated using the same set of inner simulation outputs thus are all correlated.

In essence, the GNS procedure recycles the same set of simulation outputs, via the likelihood ratio method
(
Beckman and McKay 1987
;
L’Ecuyer 1990
)
, to estimate the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[H|X]
for different scenarios
X
.
The GNS procedure is inspired by green simulation
(
Feng and Staum 2017
)
and likelihood ratio metamodeling
(
Dong et al. 2018
)
, which improve simulation efficiency by reusing simulation outputs.
Stochastic mesh for American option pricing
(
Broadie et al. 2000
;
Broadie and Glasserman 2004
;
Avramidis and Hyden 1999
;
Avramidis and Matzinger 2004
)
is also an application of the likelihood ratio method.
The GNS procedure and the stochastic mesh are mathematically similar but the two approaches tackle different problems, serve different purposes, and are applied in different contexts.
The former aims to solve nested estimation problems (risk measurement) while the latter solves a dynamic programming problem (American option pricing).

A Sample Recycling Approach

2.1
Standard Nested Simulation

Standard nested simulation (SNS), as illustrated in Figure
1
, is a common approach for estimating the quantity in Equation (
1.1
).

1.

(Outer simulation) Simulate
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) outer scenarios, denoted by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

2.

(Inner simulation) For each scenario
X_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, simulate
m^{\prime}
i.i.d. inner replications, e.g.,
Y_{i1},\ldots,Y_{im^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}f(y|X=x_{i})
then estimate
L(X_{i})
by
L^{SNS}_{m^{\prime}}(X_{i})=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}H(X_{i},Y_{ij})
.

3.

(Risk estimation) Estimate the risk measure
\rho
in (
1.1
) by
\rho^{SNS}_{m^{\prime}n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(L^{SNS}_{m^{\prime}}(X_{i}))
.

In general, the risk estimation step treats
L^{SNS}_{m^{\prime}}(X_{1}),\ldots,L^{SNS}_{m^{\prime}}(X_{n})
as i.i.d. samples of
L(X)
to estimate different risk measures.
In this study, we focus on risk measures of the form (
1.1
) with different risk functions
g:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.

For illustrative purpose, we present a financial risk measurement example.
Let
S_{t}
be a vector of risk factors, which may be the values of equities, bonds, interest rates, exchange rates, etc., at any time
t\geq 0
.
Consider a portfolio of financial instruments, which may include stocks, bonds, and derivatives whose values are affected by the risk factors.
Let
t=0
be the current time when the initial risk factor values
S_{0}
are known and
T>0
be the maximum maturity of all the instruments in the portfolio.
The portfolio manager is interested in estimating some risk measures of the portfolio’s profit and loss at a fixed future time
\tau\in(0,T)
.
Specifically, let
V_{\tau}
be the portfolio value at time
\tau
so the time
\tau

L_{\tau}=V_{0}-V_{\tau}
, which is a random variable at time
0
.
Nested simulation can be used to estimate risk measures of
L_{\tau}
: The risk factors
up to
\tau
are denoted by
X=\{S_{t}:t\in[0,\tau]\}
, which are the outer-level scenarios.
The risk factors
exceeding
\tau
are denoted by
Y=\{S_{t}:t\in(\tau,T]\}
, which are the inner-level sample paths.
The inner simulation model
H(X,Y)
is the discounted portfolio payoff for the simulated path
(X,Y)
and the risk function
g(\cdot)
depends on the risk measure of interest.

As alluded in the introduction, important risk measures such as exceedance probability, Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)

Standard nested simulation is computationally burdensome due to its nested nature, which requires a simulation budget of
\Gamma=m^{\prime}n
inner replications.
Moreover, this nested structure leads to a wasteful use of the simulation budget because each estimator

In the next section, we propose an efficient simulation procedure that circumvents the nested structure between the outer and inner simulation by recycling all inner simulation outputs in estimating
L(X_{i})
for every scenario
X_{i}
.
This recycling saves computations and improves efficiency.

2.2
Sample Recycling via Likelihood Ratios

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the scenario space and
X\in\mathcal{X}
be a given scenario.
For example,
\mathcal{X}
may be the support of the random scenario
X
.
Also, let
f(y|X)
be the conditional density of the inner random variable
Y
given the scenario
X
.
In other words, the distribution of the inner random variable
Y
is characterized by the outer scenario
X
.
This is a mild limitation of our method, as majority of risk measurement problems and many nested estimation problems in operations research satisfy this condition.

Suppose there exists a
sampling density

\widetilde{f}(y)
.
We assume that one can generate samples from
\widetilde{f}(y)
and can calculate values for both
\widetilde{f}(y)
and
f(y|x)
.
Moreover, the sampling density
\widetilde{f}
satisfies the condition that
H(x,y)f(y|x)=0
whenever
\widetilde{f}(y)=0
.
Then
L(X)=\mathbb{E}[H(X,Y)|X]
can be written as

L(X)=\mathbb{E}[H(X,Y)|X]=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{f}}\left[H(X,Y)\frac{f(Y|X)}{\widetilde{f}(Y)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{f}}\left[\widehat{H}(X,Y)\right],

(2.1)

where shorthand notation
\widehat{H}(x,y):=H(x,y)\frac{f(y|x)}{\widetilde{f}(y)}
denotes the likelihood-ratio-weighted simulation output and the subscript in the expectations indicates that
Y\sim\widetilde{f}
.
The identity (
2.1
) is mathematically identical to importance sampling, but we do not select the sampling density for variance reduction.
We assume that the sampling density
\widetilde{f}
is given and we only use the likelihood ratio as a way to recycle simulation outputs for different outer scenarios.
As we see in the numerical experiments, in practical applications usually there is a natural choice of sampling distribution
\widetilde{f}
.

In light of (
2.1
), we propose the following green nested simulation (GNS) procedure:

1.

(Outer simulation) Simulate
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) outer scenarios, denoted by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

2.

(Inner simulation) Simulate
m

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\widetilde{f}(y)
then estimate
L(X_{i})
by

L_{m}(X_{i})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}H(X_{i},Y_{j})\frac{f(Y_{j}|X_{i})}{\widetilde{f}(Y_{j})}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{H}(X_{i},Y_{j}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(2.2)

3.

(Risk estimation) Estimate the risk measure
\rho

1.1

\rho_{mn}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(L_{m}(X_{i})).

One advantage of the GNS procedure over the standard nested simulation is the computational saving because of sample recycling.
Specifically, when
m=m^{\prime}
, the GNS procedure and the standard nested simulation use the same number of inner simulation outputs, likelihood-ratio-weighted or not, to estimate each
L(X_{i})
.
Then, the computational saving is significant:

•

The GNS procedure generates
n
times less inner samples compared to the standard nested simulation.
In particular, the former simulates
\{Y_{j}\sim\widetilde{f}(y),j=1,\ldots,m\}
while the latter simulates
\{Y_{ij}\sim f(y|X_{i}),j=1,\ldots,m^{\prime},i=1,\ldots,n\}
.

•

In many applications, the inner simulation model can be decomposed into two components, one depends on the scenario
X
and the other depends on the inner replication
Y
, i.e.,
H(X,Y)=H^{\prime}(h_{1}(X),h_{2}(Y))
for some functions
H^{\prime}
,
h_{1}
, and
h_{2}
. For example, for Asian option payoffs,
h_{1}(X)
and
h_{2}(Y)
may be the averages of
X
and
Y
, respectively.
In these cases, the standard nested simulation requires
m^{\prime}n
calculations of the second component
h_{2}(Y_{ij})
while the GNS procedure only requires
m
such computations.
This is an
n
-fold saving on the second component of the inner simulation model.

•

In some applications, e.g., non-path-dependent payoffs, the inner simulation model depends sole on the inner replication, i.e.,
H(X,Y)=H(Y)
.
Then, the GNS procedure only calculates
m
inner simulation outputs, i.e.,
\{H(Y_{j}),j=1,\ldots,m\}
, then recycle and reuse them in estimating
L(X_{i})
for all
n
outer scenarios.
The standard nested simulation, in contracts, calculates
m^{\prime}
inner simulation outputs, i.e.,
\{H(Y_{ij}),j=1,\ldots,m^{\prime}\}
, for each of the
n
outer scenarios.
This is an
n
-fold saving on the entire simulation output computation.

•

Moreover, if the user chooses to increase the number of outer scenarios after an experiment, the GNS procedure can continue reusing the same set of inner simulation outputs while more inner replications are required for standard nested simulation.

•

Admittedly, the GNS procedure requires likelihood ratio calculations to reuse the inner simulation outputs, but in most applications computational efforts of the likelihood ratio
{f(Y|X)/\widetilde{f}(X)}
is small or even negligible compared to the inner simulation model
H(X,Y)
.
For example, as we see in Section
5
, in risk management applications where the underlying asset model is Markovian, the likelihood ratio calculation can be simplified.
Thus this additional cost is worth paying for the savings in generating new inner replications and calculating additional simulation outputs.

A second advantage of the GNS procedure is its high accuracy.
When
m=m^{\prime}n
so the GNS procedure matches the same simulation budget as standard nested simulation, each
L(X_{i})
is estimated by
m=m^{\prime}n
inner simulation outputs in the former versus
m^{\prime}
in the latter.
Despite the likelihood ratio weight, since the GNS procedure estimates each
L(X_{i})
with
n
times more inner simulation outputs than standard nested simulation so the former is expected to be much more accurate than the latter.
Also, as indicated in Equation (
2.1
), the likelihood ratio estimator (
2.2
) is unbiased.
Compared to the LSMC
(
Longstaff and Schwartz 2001
)
and to the kernel smoothing approach
(
Hong et al. 2017
)
for nested simulation, the GNS procedure does not have model error because it does not require the user to select any basis function, kernel function, or kernel bandwidth.

A third advantage of the GNS procedure is the strong convergence of the estimator
L_{m}(X)
to
L(X)
and the fast convergence of the risk estimator
\rho_{mn}
to
\rho
as
\min\{m,n\}\to\infty
, which are shown by the asymptotic analyses in Sections
3
and
4
.

We first state a useful result for later discussions.

Lemma 3.1

If Assumption

(I)

∎

Lemma

directly implies
\mathbb{E}[|L|^{p}]<\infty
so the latter does not need to be explicitly stated provided the former holds.
This simplifies the statements of our propositions and theorems, e.g., Proposition
3.2
, whose proof is in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 3.2
.

If Assumption
3.1

(I)
holds, then
L_{m}(x)
is an unbiased estimator for
L(x)
for any fixed scenario
x
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[L_{m}(x)\right]=L(x).

In addition, if Assumption
3.1

(II)
also holds and
\mathbb{E}\left[|\widehat{H}|\right]<\infty
, then
L_{m}(X)
is a strongly consistent estimator for
L(X)
, i.e.,

L_{m}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}L(X)\mbox{ as }m\rightarrow\infty.

The first part of Proposition
3.2
is the well-known unbiasedness of the importance sampling estimator.
The second part shows the almost sure convergence of
L_{m}(X)
to
L(X)
in light of the stochastic of
X
.
This almost sure convergence is useful for establishing asymptotic properties of the GNS risk estimator
\rho_{mn}
.

To facilitate further analysis in Section
4
, we establish two more useful lemmas below.
Even though we attribute Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
to
Avramidis and Matzinger 2004
, our lemmas are extensions of theirs to accommodate the general analysis in this article.
For completeness, we provide their detailed proofs in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 3.3

(Lemma 1 in
Avramidis and Matzinger 2004
)
.

Suppose
R
is a random variable with
\mathbb{E}[R^{2p}]<\infty
for some positive integer
p
. Then, for any arbitrary
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{G}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[(R-\mathbb{E}\left[R|\mathcal{G}\right])^{2p}\right]\leq 4^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[R^{2p}\right].

Lemma 3.4

(Lemma 2 in
Avramidis and Matzinger 2004
)
.

Consider identically distributed random variables
\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
such that
\mathbb{E}[R_{1}^{2p}]<\infty
for some positive integer
p
.
In addition, conditional on an arbitrary
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{G}
,
\{R_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are mutually independent and
\mathbb{E}\left[R_{j}|\mathcal{G}\right]=0
for all
1\leq j\leq m
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}R_{j}\right)^{2p}\right]=\frac{c_{1}}{m^{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}^{2p}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{m^{p+1}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(m^{-p}),\mbox{ as }m\rightarrow\infty,

where
c_{1}=\binom{2p}{2}\binom{2p-2}{2}\cdots\binom{2}{2}/{p!}
. In particular, for
p=1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}R_{j}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}^{2}\right]}{m}.

(3.1)

Theorem 3.5
.

If Assumption
3.1
holds and
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{H}^{2p}\right]<\infty
for some positive integer
p
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(L_{m}-L\right)^{2p}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(m^{-p}\right)\mbox{ as }m\rightarrow\infty.

Theorem
3.5
demonstrates the
\mathcal{L}^{2p}
convergence of
L_{m}
to
L
at rate
\mathcal{O}(m^{-p})
.
This is also an important result to establish asymptotic properties, such as bias, variance, MSE, and CLT, of the GNS risk estimator
\rho_{mn}
.

4
Asymptotic Analysis for Risk Estimator
\rho_{mn}

While sample recycling in the GNS procedure leads to computational savings and higher accuracy, as discussed in Section
2
, it also introduces dependency among the estimators
L_{m,i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Despite this intricate dependency, we analyze the asymptotic properties for the GNS estimators in (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).

The asymptotic analysis for
\rho_{mn}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(L_{m,i})
is different for different risk functions
g
.
For linear functions, i.e.,
g(x)=ax+b
for some constants
a
and
b
,
\rho=\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{E}\left[H|X\right])\right]=a\mathbb{E}\left[H\right]+b
can be estimated without nested simulation.
To make our study meaningful, we analyze three classes of nonlinear risk functions:

1.

Smooth function
:
g:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is twice differentiable with a bounded second derivative, i.e., both
g^{\prime}(x)
and
g^{\prime\prime}(x)
exist for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and there exists a nonnegative constant
C_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
|g^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq C_{g}<\infty
.
Analysis for this class of risk functions mainly based on the Taylor approximation

g\left(L_{m}\right)=g\left(L\right)+g^{\prime}\left(L\right)(L_{m}-L)+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\Lambda_{m})}{2}(L_{m}-L)^{2},

(4.1)

where
\Lambda_{m}
is a random variable that lies between
L_{m}
and
L
.

2.

Hockey-stick function
:
g(x):=\max\{x,0\}
.
The hockey-stick function has a kink hence is not differentiable at
x=0
, but it is Lipschitz continuous because
|g(x)-g(y)|\leq|x-y|
.
Moreover, it is clear that
g(x)=x\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
so we can define its derivative
g^{\prime}(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
, which is valid everywhere except at
x=0
; this derivative suffices in our analysis.
The valid bounds
g(x)\leq|x|
and
g^{\prime}(x)\leq 1
are also useful in our analysis.

3.

Indicator function
:
g(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
is neither continuous nor differentiable at
x=0
, which leads to a more complicated analysis than the other two cases.
When needed, we make additional assumptions and employ a smooth approximation to circumvent this difficulty.

Though different assumptions and mathematical tools are required to analyze the three classes of risk functions, we present a concise and coherent analysis that sheds lights on their similarities and common properties.
We also note that the kink and discontinuity at
x=0
in our analysis is only for simplification purpose, which can be generalized to any constant threshold
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
with a change of variable.

Let
L_{m}-L=Z_{m}/\sqrt{m}
and suppose that
Z_{m}
has a nontrivial limiting distribution as
m\rightarrow\infty
.
Assumption
4.1
states some assumptions on the joint density function
p_{m}(\ell,z)
for
(L,Z_{m})
that aid later analysis.

Assumption 4.1
.

(I)

The joint density
p_{m}(\ell,z)
of
(L,Z_{m})
and its partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}p_{m}(\ell,z)
exists for every positive integer
m\geq 1
and for all
(\ell,z)
.

(II)

For every positive integer
m\geq 1
, there exist nonnegative functions
\bar{p}_{0,m}(\cdot)
and
\bar{p}_{1,m}(\cdot)
such that

p_{m}(\ell,z)\leq\bar{p}_{0,m}(z)\mbox{ and }\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}p_{m}(\ell,z)\right|\leq\bar{p}_{1,m}(z),\quad\forall(\ell,z).

(III)

For
i=0,1
and
0\leq r\leq 2

\sup_{m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|z|^{r}\bar{p}_{i,m}(z)dz<\infty.

Assumption
4.1
is difficult to verify in general, but as argued in
Gordy and Juneja 2010
, it can be expected to be true if some of the instruments in the portfolio have sufficiently smooth payoffs.
Mathematically, Assumption
4.1
imposes smoothness and boundedness assumptions on the joint densities
p_{m}(\ell,z)
, which are needed in our analysis to compensate for the lack of differentiability or continuity in the hockey-stick and indicator risk function
g
.
Moreover, Assumption
4.1
implies that the marginal density function of
L
, i.e.,
\widetilde{p}(\ell)=\int p_{m}(\ell,z)dz
exists.

Using Assumption
4.1
, we can show the two identities in Lemma
4.1
that are useful for later analysis.
Detailed proof for Lemma
4.1
is provided in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
and
4.1
hold. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m}\geq 0\}-\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}),\mbox{ and}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|L_{m}\cdot(\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m}\geq 0\}-\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\})|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}).

(4.3)

4.1
Asymptotic Bias

For any given risk function
g
, the bias of the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
can be decomposed as

\mathrm{Bias}[\rho_{mn}]=\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L_{m}\right)-g\left(L\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}\left(L\right)(L_{m}-L)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[r_{m}\right].

(4.4)

for appropriately defined derivative
g^{\prime}
where the remainder term is

r_{m}=g\left(L_{m}\right)-g\left(L\right)-g^{\prime}\left(L\right)(L_{m}-L).

(4.5)

The first expectation in the RHS of (
4.4
) contributes to the bias due to the linear approximation of
g(\cdot)
.
For well defined derivative
g^{\prime}
such as the case for smooth and hockey-stick risk functions, this contribution is zero because

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}\left(L\right)(L_{m}-L)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}(L(X))(L_{m}(X)-L(X))|X\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}(L(X))(\mathbb{E}\left[L_{m}(X)|X\right]-L(X))\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}(L(X))(L(X)-L(X))\right]=0,

where
(*)
holds because
\mathbb{E}\left[L_{m}(X)|X\right]=L(X)
by Proposition
3.2
.

We then show that the bias (
4.4
) converges to zero at the rate
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
for all three classes of risk functions.
Specifically,
|\mathbb{E}[r_{m}]|\leq\mathbb{E}[|r_{m}|]=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
for the smooth and hockey-stick risk functions, where the inequality holds by Jensen’s inequality for the convex function
|x|
.
Equation (
4.2
) in Lemma
4.1
directly indicates that the
\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L_{m}\right)-g\left(L\right)\right]=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
for indicator risk function
g(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
.

•

For a smooth risk function
g
, using the Taylor approximation (
4.1
) and Theorem
3.5
with
p=1
, we have

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[r_{m}\right]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|r_{m}|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|g^{\prime\prime}(\Lambda_{m})|}{2}(L_{m}-L)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C_{g}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(L_{m}-L)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}).

(4.6)

•

For the hockey-stick risk function
g(x)=\max\{x,0\}=x\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
, we define
g^{\prime}(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
so

\displaystyle r_{m}=L_{m}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m}\geq 0\}-L\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\}-\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\}(L_{m}-L)=L_{m}\cdot(\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m}\geq 0\}-\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\})

Then, using Equation (
4.3
) in Lemma
4.1
, we have

\left|\mathbb{E}[r_{m}]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}[|r_{m}|]=\mathbb{E}\left[|L_{m}\cdot(\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m}\geq 0\}-\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\})|\right]=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}).

(4.7)

•

For the indicator risk function
g(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
, we consider the bias directly, i.e.,
\mathrm{Bias}[\rho_{mn}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m}\geq 0\}-\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\}\right]
, which, based on Equation (
4.2
) in Lemma
4.1
, converges at the rate
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
.

Proposition
4.2
summarizes the above discussions about asymptotic biases.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.1
and one of the following sets of assumptions hold:

1.

The risk function
g(\cdot)
is twice differentiable with a bounded second derivative and
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{H}^{2}\right]<\infty
, or

2.

The risk function
g(\cdot)
is a hockey-stick function and Assumption
4.1
holds, or

3.

The risk function
g(\cdot)
is an indicator function and Assumption
4.1
holds.

Then,

\mathrm{Bias}[\rho_{mn}]=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1}).

We can see the advantage of our GNS estimator
L_{m}
by comparing Proposition
4.2
to analogous bias results for other nested estimators.
In the GNS procedure, the total number of inner samples is
m
.
The total number of inner samples for the standard nested simulation is
\Gamma=m^{\prime}n
, where
n
is the number of outer scenarios and
m^{\prime}
is the number of inner samples per outer scenario.
So we consider
m=\Gamma
a fair comparison, i.e., the same simulation budget, between these two procedures.
Proposition
4.2
shows that the bias of the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
converges to zero at the rate of
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})=\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{-1})
, which is fast and depends only on the simulation budget.
In contrast, the bias of the standard nested simulation estimator using the optimal budget allocation scheme in
Gordy and Juneja 2010
is
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{-1/3})
.
The bias of the regression-based procedure in
Broadie et al. 2015
depends on the selection of the basis functions and is generally non-zero regardless of the simulation budget.
The bias of the kernel-based procedure in
Hong et al. 2017
depends not only on the simulation budget but also on the kernel bandwidth.

4.2
Asymptotic Variance

To analyze the variance of the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
, we first note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\rho_{mn}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(L_{m,i}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L_{m}\right)\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g\left(L_{m,i}\right)-g\left(L_{i}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g\left(L_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]\right)+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L_{m}\right)\right]\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 3\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g\left(L_{m,i}\right)-g\left(L_{i}\right)\right)\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(L_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]\right)^{2}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L\right)-g\left(L_{m}\right)\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(**)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 3\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g\left(L_{m,i}\right)-g\left(L_{i}\right)\right)\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{3}{n}\mathrm{Var}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]+3\left(\mathrm{Bias}[\rho_{mn}]\right)^{2},

(4.8)

where
(*)
holds by inequality (
B.1
) in Appendix
B
and
(**)
holds by applying Equation (
3.1
) in Lemma
3.4
to the second term.

We then analyze the three terms in Equation (
4.8
) separately: The last term converges at the rate of
\mathcal{O}(m^{-2})
by Proposition
4.2
. For the second term, we assume that
\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]<\infty
so
\mathrm{Var}[g(L)]<\infty
.
As a result, the second term in Equation (
4.8
) converges at the rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
.
Note that
\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]<\infty
is a standard assumption, which dictates that the Monte Carlo estimator for
\rho
has a finite variance.
For the hockey-stick function
g(x)=\max\{0,x\}\leq|x|
,
\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]<\infty
is satisfied if
\mathbb{E}\left[L^{2}\right]<\infty
, which, by Lemma
3.1
, holds if
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{2}]<\infty
. For the indicator function
g(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}\leq 1
, this assumption is implicitly satisfied because
\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]\leq 1
.

It remains to analyze the first term in Equation (
4.8

Using the inequality (
B.2
) in Appendix

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g\left(L_{m,i}\right)-g\left(L_{i}\right)\right)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(g\left(L_{m}\right)-g\left(L\right))^{2}\right].

•

For smooth risk functions, using the Taylor approximation (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(g\left(L_{m}\right)-g\left(L\right))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g^{\prime}\left(L\right)(L_{m}-L)+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\Lambda_{m})}{2}(L_{m}-L)^{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\leq}}2\mathbb{E}\left[(g^{\prime}\left(L\right)(L_{m}-L))^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\Lambda_{m})}{2}(L_{m}-L)^{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(**)}}{{\leq}}2\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(g^{\prime}\left(L\right))^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(L_{m}-L)^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}+\frac{C_{g}^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(L_{m}-L)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(***)}}{{=}}\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})+\mathcal{O}(m^{-2})=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})

(4.9)

where
(*)
,
(**)
, and
(***)
hold by (

), and Theorem

.

Suppose that Assumption

4.3
Asymptotic Mean Square Error

Combining Propositions
4.2
and
4.3
, we immediately establish the asymptotic MSE of

, as summarized in Theorem

.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose the conditions in Proposition
4.3
hold. Then,

\mathrm{MSE}(\rho_{mn})=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})=\mathcal{O}(\max\{m^{-1},n^{-1}\}).

Theorem
4.4
shows that

and

should grow at the same rate for the MSE of the GNS estimator to converge quickly.
Then, matching the total number of inner samples in the GNS procedure and the standard nested simulation, i.e.,
m=\Gamma
, the GNS estimator’s MSE converges at
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})=\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{-1})
but the MSE of the nested simulation with optimal simulation budget allocation in
Gordy and Juneja 2010
converges at
\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{-2/3})
.
Clearly the GNS estimator’s MSE converges faster.
Also, the GNS procedure is arguably easier to implement compared to the regression-based approach
(
Broadie et al. 2015
)
and the kernel-based approach
(
Hong et al. 2017
)
because the GNS procedure does not require basis functions, kernel function, or kernel bandwidth.

4.4
Central Limit Theorem and Variance Estimators

In this section we establish a Central Limit Theorem (CLT) of the GNS risk estimator
\rho_{mn}
and prove a valid variance estimator for
\rho_{mn}
.
Constructing confidence intervals is a common use of CLT, but the variance of nested estimators are usually difficult to estimate, e.g., by running macro replications to get multiple estimates of
\rho
then estimate the sample variance.
We propose a variance estimator for
\rho_{mn}
that requires only one run of the GNS procedure and that converges to the asymptotic variance.
Simply put, the CLT result and variance estimator in this section lead to asymptotically valid confidence intervals of the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
.

The analysis for the smooth and hockey-stick risk functions are similar, but are different from the analysis for the indicator risk function.
So, for clarity, we provide separate presentations in Sections
4.4.1
and
4.4.2
.

4.4.1
Analysis for Smooth and Hockey-Stick Functions

The CLT for the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
with smooth and hockey-stick risk functions are based on two-sample U-statistics
(see Chapter 5 in
Serfling 2009
, for example)
, whose definition and asymptotic normality are stated below.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\{X_{i},i=1,\ldots,n\}
and
\{Y_{j},j=1,\ldots,m\}
be i.i.d. samples of two independent random variables
X
and
Y
, respectively.
For a given mapping
U(x,y)
, the average
\mathcal{U}_{mn}=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}U(X_{i},Y_{j})
is called a two-sample U-statistic.

Lemma 4.6

(Asymptotic Normality of a Two-Sample U-Statistic)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{mn}
be a two-sample U-statistic in Definition
4.5
.
If
\mathbb{E}\left[(U(X,Y))^{2}\right]<\infty
, then
\mathcal{U}_{mn}
is asymptotically normally distributed, as
\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty
, with mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[U(X,Y)\right]
and variance
\sigma_{mn}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{m}
where
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[U(X,Y)|X\right]\right]
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[U(X,Y)|Y\right]\right]
.
Mathematically,

\frac{\mathcal{U}_{mn}-\mu}{\sigma_{mn}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,1),\mbox{ as }\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty.

In the following, we will show that the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
can be decomposed into two terms: One term is a two-sample U-statistic and the other term vanishes so quickly that it does not affect the asymptotic distribution of
\rho_{mn}
.
Recall that
L_{m,i}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{H}_{ij}
, so
\rho_{mn}
can be decomposed as

\rho_{mn}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(L_{m,i}\right)=\mathcal{U}_{mn}+r_{mn},

(4.11)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{mn}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left[g\left(L_{i}\right)+g^{\prime}\left(L_{i}\right)\left(\widehat{H}_{ij}-L_{i}\right)\right],\mbox{ and }

(4.12)

\displaystyle r_{mn}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[g\left(L_{m,i}\right)-g\left(L_{i}\right)-g^{\prime}\left(L_{i}\right)\left(L_{m,i}-L_{i}\right)\right].

(4.13)

By Assumption
3.1

(II)
, the outer scenarios and the inner samples are i.i.d. samples of two independent random variables.
Then
\mathcal{U}_{mn}
in (
4.12
) is a two-sample U-statistic by Definition
4.5
with the mapping

U\left(X,Y\right)=g\left(L(X)\right)+g^{\prime}\left(L\left(X\right)\right)\left(\widehat{H}\left(X,Y\right)-L\left(X\right)\right).

(4.14)

Next, we validate the conditions of Lemma
4.6
and restate its conclusion for the mapping (
4.14
).
Firstly, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(U(X,Y))^{2}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\leq}}3\left(\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L)\widehat{H})^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L)L)^{2}]\right)

(4.15)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(**)}}{{\leq}}3\left(\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]+(\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L))^{4}])^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{4}])^{1/2}+(\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L))^{4}])^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}[L^{4}])^{1/2}\right),

(4.16)

where
(*)
and
(**)
hold by inequalities (
B.1
) and (
B.3
) in Appendix
B
, respectively.
Then, the moment condition in Lemma
4.6
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[(U(X,Y))^{2}\right]<\infty
can be satisfied by the following:

•

For the smooth risk functions, in light of (
4.16
), sufficient conditions are
\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L))^{4}]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{4}]<\infty
, and
\mathbb{E}[L^{4}]<\infty
.
Also, by Lemma
3.1
,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{4}]<\infty
implies
\mathbb{E}[L^{4}]<\infty
so only the first three conditions need to be explicitly stated.

•

For the hockey-stick function, in light of (
4.15
), sufficient conditions are
\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L)\widehat{H})^{2}]<\infty
, and
\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L)L)^{2}]<\infty
.
Because
g(x)=\max\{x,0\}\leq|x|
we have
\mathbb{E}[(g(L))^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[L^{2}]
.
Also, because
g^{\prime}(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}\leq 1
, we have
\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L)\widehat{H})^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[(g^{\prime}(L)L)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[L^{2}]
.
So the sufficient conditions are simplified to
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[L^{2}]<\infty
.
Lastly, by Lemma
3.1
, these conditions are further simplified to
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{2}]<\infty
.

Note that these moment conditions also ensure the existence of asymptotic variances
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
.

Next, consider the mean
\mu
and the two variances
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
in Lemma
4.6
for the mapping (
4.14
).
Note that
\mathbb{E}[U(X,Y)|X]=g\left(L(X)\right)+g^{\prime}\left(L\left(X\right)\right)\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{H}\left(X,Y\right)|X\right]-L\left(X\right)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}g(L(X)),

where
(*)
holds because
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{H}\left(X,Y\right)|X\right]=L(X)
by (
2.1
).
Also, by the independence of
X
and
Y
we have
\mathbb{E}\left[U(X,Y)|Y\right]=\mathbb{E}[g(L)]+\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}(L)\widehat{H}|Y\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}(L)L\right]
, where the first and the last expectations are constants.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[U(X,Y)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[U(X,Y)|X\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]=\rho,\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[U(X,Y)|X\right]\right]=\mathrm{Var}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}[g(L)^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[g(L)])^{2},\mbox{ and }

(4.17)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}\left(L\right)\widehat{H}|Y\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}\left(L\right)\widehat{H}|Y\right]\right)^{2}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}\left(L\right)\widehat{H}\right]\right)^{2}.

(4.18)

Then Lemma
4.6
implies that
\sigma_{mn}^{-1}(\mathcal{U}_{mn}-\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
as
\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty
.
But, to make a conclusion about the asymptotic distribution of the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
, we also need to consider the remainder term
r_{mn}
in (
4.13
).

Note that
r_{mn}
in (
4.13
) is an average of
n
identically distributed samples of
r_{m}
as defined in (
4.5
).
By (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) we have
\mathbb{E}[|r_{mn}|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|r_{m}|]=\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
and so

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{r_{mn}}{\sigma_{mn}}\right|\right]=\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{m}+\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{n}\right)^{-1/2}\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})=\mathcal{O}\left(\left[m\left(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\cdot\frac{m}{n}\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\rightarrow 0,\mbox{ as }\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty.

This means that
\frac{r_{mn}}{\sigma_{mn}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}^{1}}}{{\rightarrow}}0
and hence
\frac{r_{mn}}{\sigma_{mn}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}0
as
\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty
.
Finally, applying the Slutsky’s theorem to (
4.11
) we arrive at the desired CLT result for the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
, as stated in Theorem
4.7
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.1
and one of the following sets of assumptions hold:

1.

The risk function
g(\cdot)
is twice differentiable with a bounded second derivative,
\mathbb{E}\left[(g\left(L\right))^{2}\right]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}\left[(g^{\prime}\left(L\right))^{4}\right]<\infty
, and
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{H}^{4}\right]<\infty
, or

2.

The risk function
g(\cdot)
is a hockey-stick function, Assumption
4.1
holds, and
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{H}^{2}\right]<\infty
.

Then,

\frac{\rho_{mn}-\rho}{\sigma_{mn}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mbox{ as }\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty,

where
\sigma_{mn}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{m}
,
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]
, and
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}\left(L\right)\widehat{H}|Y\right]\right]
.

Theorem
4.7
demonstrates the asymptotic normality of
\rho_{mn}
and the asymptotic variance decomposition due to the stochasticities of
X
and
Y
separately.
The asymptotic variance has two parts: The first part,
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\left[g\left(L\right)\right]
, is due to the stochasticity of the outer scenario
X
, and
\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{n}
would have been the asymptotic variance in a classical CLT for the sample average of
n
i.i.d. samples of
g(L)
.
The second part,
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[g^{\prime}\left(L\right)\widehat{H}|Y\right]\right]
, is due to the stochasticity of the inner sample
Y
that affects all outer scenarios due to sample recycling.
Moreover, the derivative
g^{\prime}
in the inner conditional expectation indicates that
\sigma_{2}^{2}
is also affected by the nonlinearity of the risk function
g
.

A CLT result like Theorem
4.7
is useful for constructing confidence intervals, typically by replacing unknown population mean and variance by the corresponding sample estimates.
However, the variance of nested simulation estimators are typically difficult or costly to estimate.
One way is by running
macro replications
, i.e., independent repetitions of the entire simulation procedure, then estimate the sample variance of i.i.d. samples of nested simulation estimators.
However, standard nested simulation procedure is costly to run even once, so running macro replications is prohibitively burdensome.

In contrast, we propose a variance estimator for our GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
that only requires running the GNS procedure once.
Specifically,
\sigma_{mn}^{2}
is estimated by
\widehat{\sigma}_{mn}^{2}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{1,mn}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{2,mn}^{2}}{m}
, where the estimators for
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
are

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{1,mn}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g\left(L_{m,i}\right)\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(L_{m,i}\right)\right)^{2},\mbox{ and }

(4.19)

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{2,mn}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{\prime}\left(L_{m,i}\right)\widehat{H}_{ij}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{\prime}\left(L_{m,i}\right)L_{m,i}\right)^{2},\mbox{ respectively.}

(4.20)

Theorem
4.8
shows that the proposed variance estimators are valid as they converge to the corresponding population variances.
The proof for Theorem
4.8
is provided in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem
4.7
hold. Then,

\widehat{\sigma}_{1,mn}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\sigma_{1}^{2}\quad\mbox{, }\quad\widehat{\sigma}_{2,mn}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\sigma_{2}^{2},\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\widehat{\sigma}_{mn}^{2}/\sigma_{mn}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}1,\quad\mbox{ as }\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty.

A direct result of Theorems
4.7
and
4.8
is a valid confidence interval for
\rho
with one run of the GNS procedure, as summarized in Corollary
4.9
.

Corollary 4.9
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem
4.7
hold. Then, the following is an asymptotically valid confidence interval for the nested estimator
\rho
with a confidence level of
1-\alpha
:

(\rho_{mn}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\widehat{\sigma}_{mn},\ \rho_{mn}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\widehat{\sigma}_{mn}),

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{mn}^{2}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{1,mn}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{2,mn}^{2}}{m}
and
z_{1-\alpha/2}
is the
1-\alpha/2
quantile of the standard normal distribution.

4.4.2
Analysis for the Indicator Function

The discontinuity of the indicator risk function
g(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
is a major difficulty in establishing CLT for the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}
in this case.
To circumvent this difficulty, we consider a sequence of smooth approximations of
g(x)
:
Let
\phi(u)=\frac{1}{4\pi}(1-\cos(u))\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{|u|\leq 2\pi\}
, and for any
\epsilon>0
we define a function

g_{\epsilon}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x/\epsilon}\phi(u)du=\begin{cases}1,&x\geq 2\pi\epsilon,\\
\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\left[\dfrac{x}{\epsilon}-\sin\left(\dfrac{x}{\epsilon}\right)\right]+\dfrac{1}{2},&|x|<2\pi\epsilon,\\
0,&x\leq-2\pi\epsilon.\end{cases}

(4.21)

One can show that
g_{\epsilon}(x)
is twice differentiable for any
\epsilon>0
.
Also,
g_{\epsilon}(x)
converges pointwisely to
\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
as
\epsilon\to 0
everywhere except at
x=0
.
To establish the desired CLT in this case, we use a sequence of
\epsilon_{m}
that depends on the number of inner samples
m
. We carefully construct such a sequence in the proof of the CLT, although this sequence is not part of the theorem statement.

As
g_{\epsilon_{m}}(x)
is twice differentiable for any
\epsilon_{m}>0
, we use the Taylor’s theorem for
g_{\epsilon_{m}}
to decompose the GNS estimator
\rho_{mn}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(L_{m,i})
as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{mn}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g\left(L_{m,i}\right)=\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}+r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{a}+r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{b}+r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{c}+r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{d},

(4.22)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[g(L_{i})+g_{\epsilon_{m}}^{\prime}(L_{i})(L_{m,i}-L_{i})\right],

\displaystyle r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{a}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\epsilon_{m}}^{\prime\prime}(L_{i})(L_{m,i}-L_{i})^{2},

\displaystyle r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{b}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[g_{\epsilon_{m}}(L_{i})-g(L_{i})\right],

\displaystyle r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{c}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[g(L_{m,i})-g_{\epsilon_{m}}(L_{m,i})\right],

and
r_{\epsilon_{m},mn}^{d}
is the higher-order remainder term in the Taylor’s expansion of
g_{\epsilon_{m}}
.
The decomposition (
4.22
) is more complicated than (
4.11
) due to using
g_{\epsilon_{m}}
and its Taylor expansion.
Nonetheless, the general strategy to analyze
\rho_{mn}
in this case is similar to that in Section
4.4.1
: First show that
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}
converges to an asymptotically normal distribution then show that the other remainder terms quickly vanishes.
We provide some insights in this section and defer the detailed proofs to Appendix
D
.

We assume that the joint density
\psi(x,\ell)
of
(X,L(X))
exists and define
\psi_{0}(x)=\psi(x,0)
for notational convenience.
Assumption
4.3
is useful for establishing asymptotic results for
\rho_{mn}
with the indicator risk function.

Assumption 4.3
.

(I)

The partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\psi(x,\ell)
exists for all
x
and
\ell
and there exists a nonnegative function
\psi_{1}(x)
such that
|\frac{\partial}{\partial\ell}\psi(x,\ell)|\leq\psi_{1}(x)
in any open neighborhood of
(x,0)
for all
x
.

(II)

For
i=0,1
, the following quantities are finite,

\int\psi_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x<\infty,\,\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int\left(\widehat{H}(x,Y)\right)^{2}\psi_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right]<\infty,\,\mbox{ and }\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int\left|\widehat{H}(x,Y)\right|\psi_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\right]<\infty.

Assumption
4.3

(I)
is similar to Assumption
4.1
, which is useful for applying Taylor theorem to the joint density function
\psi(x,\ell)
of
(X,L(X))
.
Assumption
4.3

(II)
may seem intricate, but it is a moment condition in disguise: Similar to the moment conditions in Theorem
4.7
for the smooth and hockey-stick risk functions, Assumption
4.3

(II)
guarantees the existence of the asymptotic variance (
4.24
) for the indicator risk function.
We note that the first two conditions in Assumption
4.3

(II)
are sufficient for the third one, but we state the latter explicitly nonetheless for ease of reference.

Define the mapping
U_{\epsilon_{m}}(X,Y)=g(L(X))+g_{\epsilon_{m}}^{\prime}(L(X))(\widehat{H}(X,Y)-L(X))
. Then we can write
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}U_{\epsilon_{m}}(X_{i},Y_{j})
.
Despite the similarity,
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}
is not a two-sample U-statistic as in Definition
4.5
because the mapping
U_{\epsilon_{m}}(X,Y)
depends on the number of scenarios
m
, so Lemma
4.6
does not apply.
Nonetheless, we show in Appendix
D
that
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}
has similar asymptotic properties as
\mathcal{U}_{mn}
in Lemma
4.6
, i.e.,
\frac{\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}-\rho}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{mn}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
where
\widetilde{\sigma}_{mn}^{2}=\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}}{m}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}[g(L)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\}]-(\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}\{L\geq 0\}])^{2},\mbox{ and }

(4.23)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int\widehat{H}(x,Y)\psi(x,0)\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\right].

(4.24)

We also show in Appendix
D
that the remainder terms in (
4.22
) vanish quickly so that
\rho_{mn}
has the same asymptotic distribution as
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon_{m},mn}
.
Then we can establish the CLT for
\rho_{mn}
with the indicator risk function, as stated in Theorem
4.10
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Consider the indicator risk function
g(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x\geq 0\}
. Suppose that Assumptions
3.1
,
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
hold.
Then,

\frac{\rho_{mn}-\rho}{\sigma_{mn}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mbox{ as }\min\{m,n\}\rightarrow\infty,

where
\widetilde{\sigma}_{mn}^{2}=\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}}{m}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
are defined as (
4.23
) and (
4.24
), respectively.

Next, we propose variance estimators that require only one run of the GNS procedure.
Specifically,
\widetilde{\sigma}_{mn}^{2}
is estimated by
\widehat{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{mn}^{2}=\frac{\widehat{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{1}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\widehat{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{2}^{2}}{m}
, where the estimators for
\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
are

\displaystyle\widehat{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{1,mn}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m,i}\geq 0\}-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{L_{m,i}\geq 0\}\right)^{2},\mbox{ and }

(4.25)

\displaystyle\widehat{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{2,mn}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\left(L_{m,i}\right)\widehat{H}_{ij}\right)^{2},

(4.26)

respectively.

4.11
; the proof is provided in Appendix

.

Theorem 4.11
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem
4.10
hold. If, in addition,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{H}^{4}]<\infty
and the sequence
\epsilon

,

, and

.

A direct result of Theorems
4.10

.

Corollary 4.12
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem

In summary, for all three classes of risk functions, we establish CLTs for our GNS estimator

5
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we consider two risk management examples to examine the performance of the proposed GNS procedure compared to the standard nested simulation procedure and a state-of-art regression-based procedure.
The first example shows that the GNS estimator’s accuracy increases with the simulation budget and the convergence rate matches the asymptotic analysis in Section
4
.
The second example is a larger example with 240 options, which demonstrates the applicability and performance of the GNS procedure in practical problems.
In the examples, we set
m=n
as this setting leads to the fastest convergence of MSE according to the asymptotic analysis in Section
4
.

In the examples, we consider option portfolios written on one or multiple, e.g.,
d
, underlying assets, whose prices follow the Black-Scholes model.
For simplicity, we assume the same expected return
\mu
for all underlying assets and a constant risk-free rate
r
.
That is, the price dynamics of the underlying assets
\bm{S}_{t}=(S_{t}^{1},...,S_{t}^{d})^{\top}\in{\cal R}^{d}
follows the follow stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}S_{t}^{i}=\mu^{\prime}S_{t}^{i}\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{ij}S_{t}^{i}\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{i},\quad i=1,...,d,

where
\bm{B}_{t}=(B_{t}^{1},...,B_{t}^{d})
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion and, without loss of generality,
\Sigma=[\sigma_{kk^{\prime}}]
is a
d\times d
sub-triangular volatility matrix that specifies the volatility and correlations of the underlying assets.
Then the asset prices at any time
t>0
are

\displaystyle S_{t}^{i}=S_{0}^{i}\exp\left\{\left(\mu^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\sigma_{ij}^{2}\right)t+\sum_{j=1}^{i}\sigma_{ij}B_{t}^{i}\right\},\quad i=1,...,d.

(5.1)

We note that drift
\mu^{\prime}
equals the
k
-th asset’s expected return under the real-world probability measure and equals the risk-free rate
r
under the risk-neutral measure.

We define an option portfolio’s maturity as the longest maturity among all options in the portfolio, which is denoted by
T
.
In our simulation experiments, the current time is
t=0
and asset values are simulated at discrete times
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
.
We are interested in measuring the portfolio risk at a future time
t_{k^{*}}=\tau\in(0,T)
, or
k^{*}\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
.
This is a nested estimation problem:
In a standard nested simulation procedure, one first simulates outer scenarios
X=\{\bm{S}_{t_{k}},k=1,...,k^{*}\}
under the real-world measure then, given
X
, simulates inner sample paths
Y=\{\bm{S}_{t_{k}},k=k^{*}+1,...,N\}
under the risk-neutral measure.
Denote the portfolio’s current value and the payoff (discounted to time
0
) by
V_{0}
and
V_{T}(X,Y)
, respectively.
The portfolio’s loss at time
\tau
given
X
is

L(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.V_{0}-V_{T}(X,Y)\right|X\right],

which is a random variable at time
0
.
We want to measure the portfolio risk
\rho=\mathbb{E}\left[g(L(X))\right]
, where three risk functions
g
are considered: a quadratic function
g(x)=(x-x_{0})^{2}
, a hockey-stick function
g(x)=(x-x_{0})^{+}
, and an indicator function
g(x)=\mathbbm{1}\{x>x_{0}\}
, all with a pre-specified threshold
x_{0}
.

As the Black-Scholes asset model is Markovian, the likelihood ratio calculation is simplified.
Specifically, the outer scenarios
X=\{\bm{S}_{t_{k}},k=1,...,k^{*}\}
are simulated using the Black-Scholes model under the real-world measure.
Independent to the outer scenarios, we simulate
\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}}\sim\widetilde{f}_{k^{*}+1}(s)
where
\widetilde{f}_{k^{*}+1}
is the marginal log-normal distribution of
\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}}
according to (
5.1
) (
k^{*}
steps under the real-world measure and 1 step under the risk neutral measure).
Conditional on
\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}}
, we simulate later values
\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+2}},\ldots,\bm{S}_{t_{N}}
under the risk-neutral measure.
Then the likelihood ratio can be calculated very efficiently, e.g.,

\displaystyle\frac{f(Y|X)}{\widetilde{f}(Y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}},\ldots,\bm{S}_{t_{N}}|\bm{S}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}}})}{\widetilde{f}(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}},\ldots,\bm{S}_{t_{N}})}=\frac{f(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}},\ldots,\bm{S}_{t_{N}}|\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}}})}{\widetilde{f}(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}},\ldots,\bm{S}_{t_{N}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}}|\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}}})f(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+2}}|\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}})\ldots f(\bm{S}_{t_{N}}|\bm{S}_{t_{N-1}})}{\widetilde{f}_{k^{*}+1}(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}})f(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+2}}|\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}})\ldots f(\bm{S}_{t_{N}}|\bm{S}_{t_{N-1}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}}|\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}}})}{\widetilde{f}_{k^{*}+1}(\bm{S}_{t_{k^{*}+1}})}.

Also, calculating the likelihood ratio as a whole is faster than calculating two densities then taking the ratio.

6
Conclusions

We have proposed a green nested simulation (GNS) procedure, that pools inner simulation outputs from different outer scenarios, for solving nested estimation problems. Inner simulation outputs are weighted by likelihood ratios to ensure the unbiasedness of the conditional expectation estimates, helping to produce a convergent GNS estimator. The MSE of the GNS estimator is shown to converge at a rate of
\Gamma^{-1}
, the fastest rate that can be achieved by a typical simulation estimator, where
\Gamma
is the simulation budget. This rate is achieved by simply recycling the inner simulation outputs weighted by likelihood ratios, without introducing modeling errors that appear to be common in existing regression-based and metamodeling-based methods when selecting basis functions, covariance functions or kernel bandwidth. CLT and variance estimates of the GNS procedure have been established, enabling the construction of asymptotically valid confidence intervals. Numerical examples on the portfolio risk measurement application have shown that the proposed GNS procedure works quite well.

Lemma B.1
.

For all vectors
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
of an inner product space, Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality asserts that
|\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\right\rangle|^{2}\leq\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{x}\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle\bm{y},\bm{y}\right\rangle
, where
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
is the inner product.
In particular, if
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\bm{y}
is a vector of ones with compatible dimension, then

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}\leq n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}.

(B.1)

Also, if
X,X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are identically distributed random variables, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{2}\right]\right)=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}].

(B.2)

Moreover, define the inner product of two arbitrary random variables
X
and

\mathbb{E}\left[|XY|\right]\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|Y|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

(B.3)
