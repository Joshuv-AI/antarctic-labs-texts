Improved tetrahedron method for the Brillouin-zone integration applicable to response functions
Mitsuaki Kawamura, Yoshihiro Gohda, Shinji Tsuneyuki 1,

Abstract
We improve the linear tetrahedron method to overcome systematic errors due to overestimations (underestimations) in integrals for convex (concave) functions, respectively. Our method is applicable to various types of calculations such as the total energy, the charge (spin) density, response functions, and the phonon frequency, in contrast with the Blöchl correction, which is applicable to only the first two. We demonstrate the ability of our method by calculating phonons in MgB 2 and fcc lithium.



I
Introduction

In calculations of periodic systems on the basis of density functional
theory (DFT)
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
, integrals of matrix elements over
the Brillouin zone (BZ) are evaluated to obtain various physical quantities
of solids including the total energy, the electron (spin)
density, the density of states, response functions, and the phonon
frequency. Since this integral with respect to the Bloch wave vector

\bm{k}
is replaced with a summation over a range of points described by a discrete variable
\bm{k}
,
approximation schemes employed for this summation can significantly
affect the accuracy and computational costs.
Accurate integration using a modest number of
\bm{k}
points is even more important for
hybrid-DFT
Perdew et al. 1996
and

GW
approximation
Hedin 1965
,
because in these cases the computational cost is proportional to the square of the number of
\bm{k}
points,
whereas standard semi-local approximations have a linear dependence.

There are two kinds of schemes to perform such an integration over the
\bm{k}
points,
namely,
the broadening method
Methfessel and Paxton 1989
and
the tetrahedron method
Jepsen and Andersen 1971
.
In the broadening method,
we replace the delta function with a smeared function
which has a finite broadening width;
we have to check the convergences about both the broadening width and
the number of
\bm{k}
points to obtain accurate results.
In the tetrahedron method,
we perform analytical integration in tetrahedral regions covering the BZ
with the piecewise linear interpolation of a matrix element.
Unlike the broadening method,
we have to check the convergence only about the number of
\bm{k}
points.
The tetrahedron method is applied to calculations of
susceptibility
Rath and Freeman 1975
,
phonon frequency
Savrasov 1992
,
phonon line width
Savrasov and Savrasov 1996
, and
the local Green’s function as part of the dynamical mean field theory
in the Hubbard model
Fujiwara et al. 2003
.

However, the tetrahedron method has a drawback;
if a matrix element
A_{\bm{k}}
is a convex (concave) function of
\bm{k}
,
this method systematically overestimates (underestimates) its contribution to the integral
due to the linear interpolation involved.
Although this can be avoided by using the quadratic interpolation,
we cannot perform analytical integration straightforwardly in such a case.
The Blöchl correction
Blöchl et al. 1994
was invented to overcome this issue
by utilizing the following two facts:
(i)
the difference between the linear interpolation integration
and that using the quadratic interpolation
is approximately proportional to
the second derivative of
A_{\bm{k}}
integrated over the occupied region;
(ii) although
\partial^{2}A_{\bm{k}}/\partial k^{2}
cannot be evaluated
within the framework of linear interpolation,
we can perform the volume integral by replacing it with
the Fermi surface integration of the first derivative of
A_{\bm{k}}

(which can be evaluated by linear interpolation)
using the Gauß theorem.
Using this method we can reduce the number of
\bm{k}
points to obtain converged results for
total energies and charge densities.
However,
in the calculation of response functions or phonon frequencies,
the integral
\int_{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}<\epsilon_{\rm F}}d^{3}kA_{\bm{k}}/(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}})

appears, where
A_{\bm{k}}
is an arbitrary function of
\bm{k}
.
In this case,
the Blöchl correction is inapplicable
because we cannot perform the Fermi surface integration
when
\partial(A_{\bm{k}}/(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}))/\partial k
is singular.

In this work,
we develop a newly improved tetrahedron method
that is applicable to calculations involving integrations of functions
with singularities on the Fermi surfaces.
It is constructed by means of a higher-order interpolation and the least square method.
We apply our method to the BZ integration in calculations of
phonon frequencies based on density functional perturbation theory (DFPT)

Baroni et al. 2001
.
Following that we successfully calculate the frequency of phonons in MgB
2
and fcc Li.
In contrast, it is difficult to achieve convergence in this calculation using conventional methods
because the phonons in these materials couple strongly with electrons
in the vicinity of Fermi surfaces

Calandra et al. 2010
;
Bazhirov et al. 2010
.
In Sec. II,
we describe our new tetrahedron method in detail after summarizing the conventional
linear tetrahedron method and
the Blöchl correction.
Section III shows how our method improves the convergence about the number of
\bm{k}
points
in the calculation of phonons, followed by the conclusion in Sec. IV.

II
Method

In this section, we introduce our new tetrahedron method;
we begin with the standard linear tetrahedron method and the Blöchl correction
to explain why these methods are not necessarily efficient in calculating response
functions such as phonon frequencies.

II.1
The linear tetrahedron method and its drawbacks

Figure 1:
Sub-cell division into six tetrahedra
and numbering of the tetrahedron corners;
the red lines in the rightmost tetrahedron are the edges of the sub-cell.

We overview the general procedure of the tetrahedron method and its drawbacks.
We calculate the integral

\displaystyle\int d^{3}kA_{\bm{k}}B(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}),

(1)

on the basis of the linear tetrahedron method,
where
B(\epsilon_{\bm{k}})
is a function of the orbital energy such as

\theta(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}})
,

\delta(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}})
,
or
\theta(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}})/(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}})
.
Here,
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function.
First, we divide a sub-cell into six tetrahedra (Fig.
1
);
this sub-cell is partitioned with the uniform
\bm{k}
-point mesh;
for convenience, we number the corners of each tetrahedron
from 1 to 4 along specific edges of the sub-cell (see Fig.
1
).
The contribution of this tetrahedron (
T
) to the integral (
1
) is

\displaystyle 6V_{T}\int_{0}^{1}dx\int_{0}^{1-x}dy\int_{0}^{1-x-y}dzA_{T}({\bm{s}})B[\epsilon_{T}({\bm{s}})],

(2)

where
{\bm{s}}=(x,y,z)
, and

\displaystyle A_{T}({\bm{s}})\equiv A_{\bm{k}_{1}^{T}(1-x-y-z)+\bm{k}_{2}^{T}x+\bm{k}_{3}^{T}y+\bm{k}_{4}^{T}z},

(3)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{T}({\bm{s}})\equiv\epsilon_{\bm{k}_{1}^{T}(1-x-y-z)+\bm{k}_{2}^{T}x+\bm{k}_{3}^{T}y+\bm{k}_{4}^{T}z},

(4)

where
\bm{k}_{i}^{T}
is the
\bm{k}
point of the
i
th corner of
T
.
In the linear tetrahedron method, we approximate
A_{T}
and
\epsilon_{T}

with linear functions:

\displaystyle A_{T}^{1}({\bm{s}})

\displaystyle=A_{1}(1-x-y-z)+A_{2}x+A_{3}y+A_{4}z,

(5)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{T}^{1}({\bm{s}})

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{1}(1-x-y-z)+\epsilon_{2}x+\epsilon_{3}y+\epsilon_{4}z,

(6)

where
A_{i}
and
\epsilon_{i}
are the matrix element and the orbital energy at the
i
th corner, respectively.
The integration (
2
)
with formulae (
5
) and (
6
) is performed
analytically
.

However, linear interpolation has a drawback;
if the matrix element
A_{T}({\bm{s}})

is a convex function within the tetrahedron
T
,
the interpolated function
A_{T}^{1}({\bm{s}})
becomes

A_{T}^{1}({\bm{s}})\geq A_{T}({\bm{s}})

in
T
;
hence, the integral is systematically overestimated.
If
A_{T}({\bm{s}})
is a concave function,
the sign of the inequality is reversed (see Fig.
2
(a)).

Figure 2:

Two kinds of approximations of the matrix element.
True and approximated matrix elements
A_{T}
are depicted as black dash-doted lines
and red solid lines, respectively; the black points indicate the matrix elements
A_{\bm{k}}

for a given value of
\bm{k}
; the dashed lines indicate the boundaries of the tetrahedra.
(a) The liner interpolated function
A_{T}^{1}
is always smaller (larger) than
the true function
A_{T}
in the case of a convex (concave) function.
(b) The leveled linear function is a better approximation of the true function.

II.2
The Blöchl correction and its limitation

In the special case that the integral (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle\int d^{3}kA_{\bm{k}}\theta(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}),

(7)

we can overcome the drawback of the linear tetrahedron method
by considering the curvature of
A_{\bm{k}}

within the framework of the linear interpolation
Blöchl et al. 1994
;
this type of integration appears in the calculations of
total energies or charge (spin) densities.
In this case,
we can evaluate the difference between the integral (
7
) with the linear interpolation
of
A_{\bm{k}}
(
A^{\rm lin}
) and that with the quadratic interpolation (
A^{\rm quad}
)
as follows.
First, we write this difference as

\displaystyle\Delta A\equiv A^{\rm quad}-A^{\rm lin}=\sum_{T}^{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}\leq\epsilon_{\rm F}}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{T}\Braket{\frac{\partial^{2}A_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}}_{T},

(8)

where
C_{ij}^{T}
is the form factor
describing the shape and the orientation of the tetrahedron as follows

\displaystyle\hskip-1.00006ptC_{ij}^{T}=\frac{1}{40}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{4}(\bm{k}_{l}^{T})_{i}\sum_{m=1}^{4}(\bm{k}_{m}^{T})_{j}-4\sum_{l=1}^{4}(\bm{k}_{l}^{T})_{i}(\bm{k}_{l}^{T})_{j}.\right],

(9)

and
\Braket{\cdots}_{T}
indicates an integration in the tetrahedron
T
.
Now, we replace
\partial^{2}A_{\bm{k}}/\partial k^{2}
with
\partial A_{\bm{k}}/\partial k

because the former cannot be evaluated in the framework of the linear interpolation,
but the latter can be.
We assume the form factor is a constant over the entire BZ (
C_{ij}^{T}\approx C_{ij}
),
and then we apply the Gauß theorem:

\displaystyle\Delta A

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{ij}C_{ij}\int_{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}<\epsilon_{\rm F}}d^{3}k\frac{\partial^{2}A_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}C_{ij}\int_{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\rm F}}d^{2}k\frac{(\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}})_{i}}{|\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}}|}\frac{\partial A_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{j}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{T}^{{\rm Fermisurface}}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{T}\left\langle\frac{(\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}})_{i}}{|\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}}|}\frac{\partial A_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{j}}\right\rangle_{T}

(10)

However, when we calculate an integral such as

\displaystyle\int d^{3}kA_{\bm{k}}\frac{\theta(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}})}{\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}},

(11)

(this kind of integration appears in the calculations of response functions
and phonon frequencies),
the difference associated with the two kinds of interpolation
becomes

\displaystyle\Delta A=\sum_{T}^{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}<\epsilon_{\rm F}}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{T}\Braket{\frac{\partial^{2}A_{\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{i}\partial k_{j}}}_{T}G(\epsilon_{\bm{k}},\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}}),

(12)

where
G(\epsilon_{\bm{k}},\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}})
is a complicated function of

\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
and
\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
;
therefore, we cannot apply the Blöchl correction
because we cannot replace
\partial^{2}A_{\bm{k}}/\partial k^{2}
with
\partial A_{\bm{k}}/\partial k

as before.
This is due to the presence of the energy denominator;
hence, we have to start with another concept
to overcome this issue.

II.3
A newly improved tetrahedron method applicable to response functions

The systematic error of the tetrahedron method is a result of the
linear interpolation
.
Although we can avoid this problem if we use higher order interpolation, the integral (
2
)
becomes unsolvable analytically.
The real question is: how can we improve the
linear
approximation of the matrix elements?
The answer is to employ
leveling
rather than interpolating
(see fig.
2
b).
The procedure is explained below.

1.

We construct the
N
th polynomial
A_{T}^{N}({\bm{s}})

from
A_{\bm{k}}
and
\bm{k}
using the corners of a tetrahedron
T

and some additional surrounding points for sampling.

2.

We fit a linear function

\displaystyle\hskip-3.00003ptA_{T}^{LSM}({\bm{s}})={\bar{A}}_{1}(1-x-y-z)+{\bar{A}}_{2}x+{\bar{A}}_{3}y+{\bar{A}}_{4}z

(13)

into
A_{T}^{N}({\bm{s}})
through the least square method (LSM);
that is to say, we solve

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bar{A}}_{i}}\int_{0}^{1}dx

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1-x}dy\int_{0}^{1-x-y}dz

\displaystyle\times|A_{T}^{N}({\bm{s}})-A_{T}^{LSM}({\bm{s}})|^{2}=0.

(14)

3.

We apply the same procedure to
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
,
and obtain
\epsilon_{T}^{LSM}({\bm{s}})
.

4.

We evaluate integral (
2
) replacing

A_{T}({\bm{s}})
and
\epsilon_{T}({\bm{s}})
with

A_{T}^{LSM}({\bm{s}})
and
\epsilon_{T}^{LSM}({\bm{s}})
, respectively.

5.

We repeat the above steps for all tetrahedra.

Although the approximated matrix element
A_{T}^{LSM}({\bm{s}})
is discontinuous
at boundaries of tetrahedra (see Fig.
2
(b)),
it is of no concern
because we are interested only in the integrated value.

III
Comparison with other integration schemes for actual calculations

We implement our method in
an
ab initio
electronic structure calculation code

Quantum ESPRESSO
Giannozzi et al. 2009

which uses plane waves to represent Kohn-Sham (KS) orbitals.
Then, we test the effectiveness of the method
through calculations of phonons in two systems,
MgB
2

Nagamatsu et al. 2001
and
fcc lithium at a high pressure (20 GPa),
based on DFPT

Baroni et al. 2001
(Appendix
A
).

Magnesium diboride has the highest
T_{C}
(about 40 K)
out of the known phonon-type superconductors.
Many

studies have been performed since it was discovered

;
Kong et al. 2001
;
Bohnen et al. 2001
;
Choi et al. 2002
;
Eiguren and Ambrosch-Draxl 2008
,
revealing that the high

is a result of the strong interaction between
intra-layer vibrations of B atoms
and their covalent bonding orbitals (

4

We have to evaluate these responses accurately to determine the phonon frequencies precisely.
Lithium exhibits a monatomic fcc structure at pressures between 7.5 and 39 GPa

In this phase it becomes a superconductor.
Its

increases with pressure up to 30 GPa

;
Struzhkin et al. 2002
;
Shimizu et al. 2002

The lower transverse acoustic mode at

In this test, we consider the phonons of fcc Li at a pressure of 20 GPa.

We use norm-conserving pseudopotentials
Hamann et al. 1979

in calculations of MgB
2
;

In the calculations of fcc lithium, we use an ultrasoft pseudopotential

We treat the electrons in the 1s orbitals as valence electrons

In both of these applications,
we use the GGA-PBE functional

IV
Conclusion

We introduced an improvement to the tetrahedron method based on
the third order interpolation and the least square method
that reduces the number of
\bm{k}
points required to obtain converged results of
the BZ integrations.
Our method is applicable to various kinds of
\bm{k}
-integration;
in particular, it is efficient for calculations of phonons and response functions
because the associated computational costs are large
and the Blöchl correction is not applicable to these calculations.
We demonstrated this effectiveness
through calculations of phonon frequencies in MgB
2
and fcc Li.

The integration weights for the DFPT calculations of phonon frequencies are
different from those of the total energy,

\displaystyle=\theta(\epsilon_{\rm F}-\epsilon_{nk})\theta(\epsilon_{nk}-\epsilon_{n^{\prime}k+q}).
