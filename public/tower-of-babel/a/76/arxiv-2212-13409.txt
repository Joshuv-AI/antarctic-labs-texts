A factorization of metric spaces
Yoshito Ishiki

Abstract
. We first prove that for every metrizable space X X , for every closed subset F F whose complement is zero-dimensional, the space X X can be embedded into a product space of the closed subset F F and a metrizable zero-dimensional space as a closed subset. Using this theorem, we next show the existence of extensors of metrics and ultrametrics, which preserve properties of metrics such as the completeness, the properness, being an ultrametrics, its fractal dimensions, and large scale structures. This result contains some of the author’s extension theorems of ultrametrics.

1.
Introduction

Under certain assumptions,
for a topological space
X
, and for a retract
E
of
X
,
Michael
[
19
]

established the existence of a perfect map

f
(or a closed map)
from

X
into the
product space
E\times\mathbb{R}
such that

f(a)=(a,0)
for all
a\in E
.
Michael also
characterized the property
that a retract
E
becomes
a
perfect (or closed) retract of
X
using
the behavior of
E\times\{0\}
in a
product space
E\times\mathbb{R}
.

As an improvement of Michael’s results,
in this paper,
we shall prove that
for every metrizable space
X
, for every
closed
subset
F
whose complement is zero-dimensional
(i.e., it has covering dimension
0
),
there exists a topological embedding

\Phi
of the
whole space
X
into the product space

F\times\Lambda

of the closed subset
F
and
a metrizable zero-dimensional space
\Lambda

(see Theorem
3.1
), where the factor

\Lambda
is a metric quotient space identifying

F
as a single point.
Moreover,
the embedding

\Phi
is a closed map and
there exists
\theta\in\Lambda
such that

\Phi(a)=(a,\theta)
for all
a\in F
.
Based on this theorem, we can roughly
consider that
the space
X
is factorized into a product of
F
and some

0
-dimensional metrizable space, and
we can take a retraction from
X
into
F
as
a projection in a product space in some sense.

To prove Theorem
3.1
,
we utilize the
Engelking’s theorem
[
8
]
on the existence of retraction, stating that
if
X
is a metrizable space and

F
is a non-empty closed subset
F
for which

X\setminus F
is
0
-dimensional,
then there exists a retraction

f\colon X\to F
, which is a closed map.
The construction of this retraction
includes stronger properties than being a closed map,
which we will use in the present paper.

Before explaining applications of our embedding theorem,
we introduce some notions on spaces of metrics and
ultrametrics.
A metric
d
on a set
X
is said to be an
ultrametric
if
it satisfies the so-called strong triangle inequality

d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)\lor d(z,y)
for all
x,y,z\in X
,
where the symbol “
\lor
” stands for the maximum operator on

\mathbb{R}
.
Let
S
be a subset of

[0,\infty)
with
0\in S
,
and
X
be a metrizable space.
We denote by
\met(X;S)

(resp.
\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)
) the set of all
metrics (resp. ultrametrics) on
X
generating the same topology of
X

and
taking values in
S
.
We simply write
\met(X):=\met(X;[0,\infty))
.
Let
\metdis_{X}
be the supremum distance on

\met(X)
. Namely,

\metdis_{X}(d,e)=\sup_{x,y\in X}|d(x,y)-e(x,y)|
.
Then
\metdis_{X}
is a metric on
\met(X)

taking values in
[0,\infty]
.
Using open balls, as is the case of ordinary metric spaces, we can introduce a topology
on
\met(X)
with
\metdis_{X}
.
In what follows, we always consider that

\met(X)
is equipped with the
topology generated by
\metdis_{X}
.
Similarly, for
d,e\in\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)
,
we define
\umetdis_{X}^{S}(d,s)
by the
infimum
\epsilon\in S\setminus\{0\}
such that

d(x,y)\leq e(x,y)\lor\epsilon
and

e(x,y)\leq d(x,y)\lor\epsilon
for all

x,y\in X
.
Then
\umetdis_{X}^{S}
is an ultrametric on

\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)

taking values in
\cl(S)\sqcup\{\infty\}
. We consider that
\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)
is equipped with the topology generated by
\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)
.

In
[
14
]
,
the author showed that for
an ultrametrizable space
X
,
for a closed subset
F
,
for a subset
S
of
[0,\infty)
with
0\in S
,
and
for every
d\in\mathrm{UMet}(F;S)
,
there exists
D\in\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)
such that

D|_{F^{2}}=d
.
This result is an ultrametric analogue of
Hausdorff’s metric extension theorem

[
11
]
.
Some authors have investigated
variants of the Hausdorff’s metric extension
theorem
(see
[
18
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
5
]
).

In
[
16
]
,
the author generalized the results
[
18
]

on the existence of simultaneous extensions of metrics
to the theory of metrics
taking values in general linearly ordered Abelian groups.
This theorem also contains the existence of
simultaneous extensions of ultrametrics.
Namely, for every ultrametrizable space

X
, a closed subset
F
, and a subset
S
of
[0,\infty)
with
0\in S
,
the author obtained an
isometric extensor

\Upsilon\colon\mathrm{UMet}(F;S)\to\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)
.
For extensors of (ultra)metrics,
we refer the readers to
[
23
]
,

[
21
]
and

[
18
]
.

A metric
d
on a set
X

(or a metric space
(X,d)
) is said to be
proper
if
all closed balls of
(X,d)
are compact.
In
[
12
]
,
the author proved that a proper metric

d\in\met(F)
can be extended into
a proper metric
D\in\met(X)
,
and also proved an analogue of this extension theorem for proper ultrametrics.

In the present paper,
using our theorem on embedding
a metrizable space into a product space
(Theorem
3.1
),
we will unify
the author’s extension theorems on
ultrametrics explained above.
Namely, using a structure of a product space,
under the same assumptions to Theorem
3.1
,
we obtain extensors

\Xi,\Sigma\colon\met(F)\to\met(X)
which preserve properties of

d\in\met(F)
such as the completeness, the properness,
being an ultrametrics,
its fractal dimensions,
and large scale structures
(see Theorems
4.6
and

4.7
).

The organization of this paper is as follows:
In Section

,

.

In Section

,

.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we prepare some notions and notations.

2.1.
Generalities

For a metric space
(X,d)
,
and for
x\in X
and

\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
we denote by
B(x,\epsilon;d)

(resp.
U(x,\epsilon;d)
)
the closed (resp. open) ball of
(X,d)
centered
at
x
with radius
r
.
We simply represent it as

B(x,\epsilon)

(resp.
U(x,\epsilon)
) when no confusion can arise.
For a subset
A
of
X
, we define

\varrho_{d,A}\colon X\to[0,\infty)
by

\varrho_{d,A}(x)=\inf_{y\in A}d(x,y)
.
Notice that
\varrho_{d,A}
is continuous.
Moreover, it is
1
-Lipschitz.

A map
f\colon X\to Y
between topological spaces

X
and
Y
is said to be

open
(resp.
closed
) if
for every open (resp. closed) subset
A
of
X
,
the image
f(A)
is open (resp. closed).

Let
(X,d)
and
(Y,e)

be metric spaces.
We define product metrics

d\times_{1}e
and

d\times_{\infty}e

by

(d\times_{1}e)((x,y),(u,v))=d(x,u)+e(y,v)

and

d\times_{\infty}e((x,y),(u,v))=d(x,u)\lor e(y,v)
.
Notice that
d\times_{1}e
and

d\times_{\infty}e
generate the same topology
of
X\times Y
.
Remark that if
e
and
d
are ultrametrics,
then so is
d\times_{\infty}e
.
We can also define the
\ell^{p}
-product metric

d\times_{p}e
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
.
However, we omit it since we only consider the

\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{\infty}
-product metrics in
this paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
and
(Y,e)
be metric spaces.
If both
d
and
e
are proper,
then so are
d\times_{1}e
and
d\times_{\infty}e
.

Proof.

For all

\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
and for every point

p=(a,b)\in X\times Y
,
the sets

B(p,\epsilon;d\times_{1}e)
and

B(p,\epsilon;d\times_{\infty}e)
are
contained in

B(a,\epsilon;d)\times B(b,\epsilon;e)
.
Since
d
and
e
are proper,
the sets
B(a,\epsilon;d)\times B(b,\epsilon;e)
is
compact.
Then

B(p,\epsilon;d\times_{1}e)
and

B(p,\epsilon;d\times_{\infty}e)
are compact.
Hence

d\times_{1}e
and
d\times_{\infty}e
are
proper.
∎

2.2.
Topological dimension

A subset of a topological space is said to be

clopen
if it is closed and open in the space.
For a topological space
X
,
we write
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X)=0
if

X\neq\emptyset
and
every finite covering of
X
has
a refinement covering consisting of
finitely many mutually disjoint open subsets of
X
.
A topological space

X
is said to be
ultranormal
if
for all disjoint closed subsets
A
and
B
of

X
, there exists a clopen subset
C
of
X
such that

A\subset C
and
B\cap C=\emptyset
.
Remark that a topological space is ultranormal
if and only if it has large inductive dimension
0
.
Of cause, we can define higher dimensional spaces,
however, we omit it since, in this paper,
we only consider the

0
-dimensionality.
For more discussions on topological dimensions,
we refer the readers to
[
3
]

and

[
20
]
.

The proof of the next proposition is
presented in
[
3
, Proposition 2.9]

and
[
20
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

A
non-empty topological space
X
is
ultranormal if and only if
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X)=0
.

Remark 2.1
.

In what follows,
without referring to Proposition
2.2
,
we use the equivalence between

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X)=0
and the ultranormality.

A subset
S
of
[0,\infty)
is
said to be

characteristic
if

0\in S
and for all
t\in(0,\infty)
,
there exists
s\in S\setminus\{0\}
such that

s\leq t
.
The next proposition is deduced from

[
14
, Proposition 2.14]
and

[
4
, Theorem II]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For every characteristic subset
S
of
[0,\infty)
,
a metrizable space
X
satisfies

\mathrm{UMet}(X;S)\neq\emptyset
if and only if

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X)=0
.

For a topological space
T
,
and for a subset
E
of
T
,
we denote by
\cl(E)
(resp.
\partial E
)
the closure of
E
(resp. the boundary of
E
) in

T
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and
A
be a
non-empty closed subset of
X
such that

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus A)=0
.
If
U
is an open subset of
X
satisfying that

A\subset U
,
then
there exists a clopen subset
V
of
X

such that
A\subset V
and
V\subset U
.

Proof.

Since
X
is normal,
there exists an open subset
G
of
X
with

A\subset G
and
\cl(G)\subset U
.
Since
\partial G\subset X\setminus A

and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus A)=0
,
we can find a clopen subset

Q
of
X\setminus A
such that

\partial G\subset Q
and

Q\subset U\cap(X\setminus A)
.
Put
V=\cl(G)\cup Q
.
Then
V
is a clopen set as required.
∎

We say that a topological space is a
Cantor space
if it is homeomorphic to the (middle-third)
Cantor set. Note that all Cantor spaces are
homeomorphic to each other.

Proposition 2.5
.

If
X
is a separable metrizable space with

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X)=0
,
then
X
can be topologically embedded into
a Cantor space.

Proof.

The proposition is well-known (see for example

[
3
, Exercise 9.14]
).
We briefly review a construction of an embedding.
Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
be a base of
X

consisting of clopen subsets of
X
. Let
f_{i}\colon X\to\{0,1\}
be the characteristic map of
U_{i}
, i.e.,

f_{i}(x)=1
if and only if
x\in U_{i}
.
We define
g\colon X\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
by

g(x)=(f_{i}(x))_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
.
Then
g
is a topological embedding, and

\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
is a Cantor space.
∎

2.3.
Metric quotient spaces

In this section, we construct a factor of
a target product space in our first main theorem
(Theorem
3.1
).
That factor is a metric quotient space
identifying a given closed subset as a single point.
To consider metric quotient space, we use
Hausdorff’s construction

[
11
]
of a metric vanishing on a given
closed subset.
Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and

F
be a non-empty closed subset of
X
.
Take a point
\omega_{F}
satisfying that

\omega_{F}\not\in X\setminus F
.
We put
X_{/F}=(X\setminus F)\sqcup\{\omega_{F}\}
.
For the sake of convenience,
we choose
\omega_{F}
as a point of
F
.
For a metric
d\in\met(X)
,
we define a symmetric function

\Theta(d)\colon(X_{/F})^{2}\to[0,\infty)
by

\Theta(d)(x,y)=\min\{d(x,y),\varrho_{d,F}(x)+\varrho_{d,F}(y)\}
.
Notice that
\Theta(d)(x,\omega_{F})=\varrho_{d,F}(x)

for all
x\in X_{/F}

since
\omega_{F}\in F
.
In Proposition

2.6
,
we will prove that

\Theta(d)
is actually a metric on

X_{/F}
.
We define a map

\pi\colon X\to X_{/F}
by

\pi(x)=x
if

x\in X\setminus F
; otherwise,

\pi(x)=\omega_{F}
.
Remark that the metric structure of
(X_{/F},\Theta(d))
does
not depend on the choice of
\omega_{F}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space, and

F
be a
non-empty closed subset of
X
.
If
d\in\met(X)
,
then the following statements are true:

(1)

The function
\Theta(d)
is a metric on
X_{/F}
.

(2)

The map
\pi\colon X\to X_{/F}
is

1
-Lipschitz. In particular, it is
continuous.

(3)

The restricted map

\pi|_{X\setminus F}\colon(X\setminus F)\to(X_{/F}\setminus\{\omega_{F}\})
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

The proof of
(1)
is
essentially the same to
[
11
]
.
To prove that
\Theta(d)
is a metric,
it suffices to show that
\Theta(d)
satisfies the
triangle inequality.
Recalling the definition of
\varrho_{d,F}(x)
,
we notice that for all
x,y,z\in X_{/F}
,
all of the following inequalities are true:

(i)

d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)
;

(ii)

\varrho_{d,F}(x)+\varrho_{d,F}(y)\leq(\varrho_{d,F}(x)+\varrho_{d,F}(z))+d(z,y)
;

(iii)

\varrho_{d,F}(x)+\varrho_{d,F}(y)\leq d(x,z)+(\varrho_{d,F}(z)+\varrho_{d,F}(y))
;

(iv)

\varrho_{d,F}(x)+\varrho_{d,F}(y)\leq(\varrho_{d,F}(x)+\varrho_{d,F}(z))+(\varrho_{d,F}(z)+\varrho_{d,F}(y))
.

Then we conclude that

\Theta(d)
satisfies the triangle inequality.

We now verify
(2)
.
For all
x,y\in X
,
we have
\Theta(d)(\pi(x),\pi(y))=\min\{d(x,y),\varrho_{d,F}(x)+\varrho_{d,F}(y)\}
.
Thus we obtain
\Theta(d)(\pi(x),\pi(y))\leq d(x,y)
.
This means that
\pi
is
1
-Lipschitz.

We next show that

\pi|_{X\setminus F}\colon(X\setminus F)\to(X_{/F}\setminus\{\omega_{F}\})
is a homeomorphism.
Remark that

X_{/F}\setminus\{\omega_{F}\}=X\setminus F

as a set.
For all
x\in X\setminus F
,
every number
\epsilon\in(0,\varrho_{d,F}(x))
satisfies
that

U(x,\epsilon;d)=U(\pi(x),\epsilon;\Theta(d))
.
Thus, the restricted map

\pi|_{X\setminus F}
is a homeomorphism.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
and

F
be a closed subset of
X
.
It
is not always true that

\pi\colon X\to X_{/F}
is
open or closed.
We give an example.
Put
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

F=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}
.
Define a metric on
d
on
X

d(x,y)=|x_{1}-y_{1}|+|x_{2}-y_{2}|
, where

x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2})
.
Let
A=\{\,(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\mid x_{2}=(|x_{1}|+1)^{-1}\,\}
.
Then
A
is closed in
X
, and

\pi(A)
is not closed in
X_{/F}

since
\omega_{F}\not\in\pi(A)
and

U(\omega_{F},\epsilon)\cap\pi(A)\neq\emptyset
for all
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
.
Similarly, the set

O=\{\,(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\mid x_{2}<(|x_{1}|+1)^{-1}\,\}
is
open in
X
and
\pi(O)
is not open in

X_{/F}
.
Namely, in this case, the map
\pi
is
neither open nor closed.

The next lemma follows
from the fact that a continuous image of
a separable (resp. compact) space is
separable (resp. compact).

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and
F
be a non-empty closed subset of
X
.
If
d\in\met(X)
,
then the following statements are true:

(1)

If
(X,d)
is separable, then so is
(X_{/F},\Theta(d))
.

(2)

If
(X,d)
is compact, then so is
(X_{/F},\Theta(d))
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
For a subset
A
of
X
,
and for
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
we define

\widetilde{B}(A,\epsilon;d)=\{\,x\in X\mid\varrho_{d,A}(x)\leq\epsilon\,\}

and

\widetilde{U}(A,r;d)=\{\,x\in X\mid\varrho_{d,A}(x)<\epsilon\,\}
.
Remark that

\widetilde{B}(A,\epsilon;d)

is closed and

\widetilde{U}(A,\epsilon;d)
is open in
(X,d)

since
\varrho_{d,A}
is continuous on
(X,d)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and

F
be a non-empty closed subset of
X
.
If
d\in\met(X)
and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus F)=0
, then

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X_{/F})=0
with respect to
the topology generated by
\Theta(d)
.

Proof.

Notice that for all
\eta\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\pi(\widetilde{B}(F,\eta;d))=B(\omega_{F},\eta)
and
\pi(\widetilde{U}(F,\eta;d))=U(\omega_{F},\eta)
.
First we show that the point
\omega_{F}

has a neighborhood system consisting of
clopen subsets of
X_{/F}
.
Take an arbitrary number

\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
.
Put
G=U(\omega_{F},\epsilon)
.
In this case, we have

\pi^{-1}(G)=\widetilde{U}(F,\epsilon;d)
.
Applying Lemma
2.4

to
\widetilde{B}(F,\epsilon/2;d)
and

\pi^{-1}(G)(=\widetilde{U}(F;\epsilon;d))
,
we can find a
clopen subset
H
of
X
such that

\widetilde{B}(F,\epsilon/2;d)\subset H
and

H\subset\pi^{-1}(G)
.
Put
P=\pi(H)
.
Then we obtain

B(\omega_{F},\epsilon/2)\subset P
and

P\subset G
.
We now prove
P
is a clopen neighborhood of

\omega_{F}
in
X_{/F}
with
P\subset G
.
Due to
B(\omega_{F},\epsilon/2)\subset P
,
the set
P
is a
neighborhood of
\omega_{F}
.
Since
\pi|_{X\setminus F}\colon X\setminus F\to X_{/F}\setminus\{\omega_{F}\}
is a homeomorphism
(see
(3)
in Proposition
2.6
)
and

X_{/F}\setminus\{\omega_{F}\}
is
open in
X_{/F}
,
we notice that

\pi(X\setminus H)

and
\pi(H\setminus\widetilde{B}(F,\epsilon/4;d))

are open in

X_{/F}
.
Remark that we obtain the equalities

(2.1)

\displaystyle\pi(X\setminus H)=X_{/F}\setminus P,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\pi(H\setminus\widetilde{B}(F,\epsilon/4;d))=P\setminus B(\omega_{F},\epsilon/4).

Then
the sets

X_{/F}\setminus P

and
P\setminus B(\omega_{F},\epsilon/4)

are open in
X_{/F}
.
In particular,
the set
P
is closed in
X_{/F}
.
We also obtain

P=U(\omega_{F},\epsilon/2)\cup(P\setminus B(\omega_{F},\epsilon/4))
,
and hence

P
is open in
X_{/F}
.
Therefore
the set
P
is clopen in
X_{/F}
such that

\omega_{F}\in P
and
P\subset G
.
This implies that
\omega_{F}
has a clopen neighborhood system.

To prove
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X_{/F})=0
,
we verify that
X_{/F}
is ultranormal.
Let
A
and
B
be disjoint closed subsets of
X_{/F}
.
We may assume that
\omega_{F}\not\in B
and

B\neq\emptyset
.
Due to the argument discussed above,
there exists a clopen neighborhood
V
of

\omega_{F}
with
V\cap B=\emptyset
.
Since
X_{/F}\setminus V=(X\setminus F)\setminus(V\setminus\{\omega_{F}\})
as a set,
the statement
(3)
in
Proposition
2.6

yields

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X_{/F}\setminus V)=0
.
Thus
we can take a clopen subset
Q
of
X\setminus V

such that
A\setminus V\subset Q
and
Q\cap B=\emptyset

(it can happen that
A\setminus V=A
).
Put
O=Q\cup V
. Then
O
is clopen in

X_{/F}
and
A\subset O
and
O\cap B=\emptyset
.
This means that

X_{/F}
is ultranormal, and hence

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X_{/F})=0
.
∎

For a set
S
, let
\card(S)
denote the
cardinality of
S
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and

F
be a non-empty
closed subset of
X
.
If
d\in\met(X)
is complete,
then so is
\Theta(d)
.

Proof.

Take a Cauchy sequence
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
of

(X_{/F},\Theta(d))
.
We only need to show that there exists a convergent subsequence of

\{p_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
.
Put
p_{i}=\pi(x_{i})
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
we define

W_{n}=\{\,i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid\varrho_{d,F}(x_{i})\leq 2^{-n}\,\}
.
We divide the proof into two parts.

Case 1. (For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\card(W_{n})=\aleph_{0}
):
In this case,
we can take a strictly increasing function

\phi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\phi(n)\in W_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Then
\varrho_{d,F}(x_{\phi(n)})\leq 2^{-n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Thus we also obtain

\lim_{i\to\infty}\Theta(d)(p_{\phi(i)},\omega_{F})=\lim_{i\to\infty}\varrho_{d,F}(x_{\phi(i)})\to 0
,
and hence

p_{\phi(i)}\to\omega_{F}
as
i\to\infty
.
Therefore
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
has a convergent subsequence
\{p_{\phi(i)}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
.

Case 2. (There exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\card(W_{k})<\aleph_{0}
):
In this case, we have

\card(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\setminus W_{k})=\aleph_{0}
.
Thus, we can take a strictly increasing function

\phi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\phi(i)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\setminus W_{k}
for all

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Then we have
2^{-k}<\varrho_{d,F}(x_{\phi(i)})
for all

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Since
\{p_{\phi(i)}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
is Cauchy,
we notice that

\Theta(d)(p_{\phi(n)},p_{\phi(m)})\to 0
as

n,m\to\infty
.
From
2^{-k}<\varrho_{d,F}(x_{\phi(i)})
for all

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and from
the definition of
\Theta(d)
, it follows that
for all sufficiently large numbers
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
we have

\Theta(d)(p_{\phi(n)},p_{\phi(m)})=d(x_{\phi(n)},x_{\phi(m)})
and

d(x_{\phi(n)},x_{\phi(m)})\to 0
as
m,n\to\infty
.
Namely, the sequence
\{x_{\phi(i)}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
is
Cauchy in
(X,d)
.
Since
d
is complete, there exists a
limit of
\{x_{\phi(i)}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
,
say
l\in X
.
Then
\{p_{\phi(i)}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
converges to

\pi(l)\in X_{/F}
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

For a non-compact locally compact
Hausdorff space

X
, we denote by
\alpha X
the
one-point compactification of
X
.
Note that
\alpha X=X\sqcup\{\infty\}
as a
set.
Remark that for

F
of

,
the space
\alpha F
can be
regarded as a closed subset of

in a canonical way.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
X
be a non-compact
\sigma
-compact
locally compact metrizable space,
and

be a non-compact closed subset of

.
If

satisfy that

,
then

(\alpha X_{/\alpha F},\Theta(D))

is
isometric
to

(X_{/F},\Theta(d))
.
In particular, the space

We can choose
\omega_{\alpha F}\in\alpha X_{/\alpha F}
so that

\omega_{\alpha F}=\omega_{F}
.
Then we obtain

\alpha X_{/\alpha F}=X_{/F}

Remark 2.3
.

There exist a metrizable space

, and metrics

2.4.
Retractions

For a topological space
X
,
a subset
F
of
X
is said to be
a
retract
if there exists a
continuous map
r\colon X\to F
such that

r(a)=a
for all
a\in F
.
In this case, the map
r
is called a

retraction
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
For a subset
A
of
X
, and for

\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
we define

\widetilde{E}(A,\epsilon)=X\setminus\widetilde{U}(A,\epsilon)
.
Notice that
\widetilde{E}(A,\epsilon)=\{\,x\in X\mid\epsilon\leq\varrho_{d,A}(x)\,\}
and

\widetilde{E}(A,\epsilon)
is closed in
X
.
For a subset
A
of
X
, we denote by
\yodiam(A)

the diameter of
A
.
For
h\in(0,\infty)
, we say that
a subset
E
of
X
is
h
-separated
if
all distinct
x,y\in E
satisfy

h\leq d(x,y)
.

The next theorem is essentially
proven by
Engelking
[
8
, Lemma]
.
Since the conditions
(D)

and
(SR)
in the next theorem does not directly appear in
the statement of
[
8
, Lemma]
,
for the sake of self-containedness,
we give a proof.
The condition
(SR)

plays an important role of the proof of
Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and
F
be a
non-empty closed subset of
X
with

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus F)=0
.
Fix
d\in\met(X)
.
Then there exists a retraction

r\colon X\to F

satisfying the following conditions:

(D)

For all
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
the image set
r\left(\widetilde{E}(F,\epsilon)\right)
is
closed and
discrete in
F
.

(SR)

If
a sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
in
X
satisfies

\lim_{i\to\infty}\varrho_{d,F}(x_{i})=0
,
then we have
\lim_{i\to\infty}d(x_{i},r(x_{i}))=0
.

Proof.

The idea of the construction in this proof roughly coincides
with that of
[
8
, Lemma]
, however,
some details are different from it.

Using Lemma
2.4
and induction,
we can take a nested sequence

\{V_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
of clopen subsets of

X
satisfying that

\widetilde{B}(F,2^{-i-1};d)\subset V_{i}

and

V_{i}\subset\widetilde{U}(F,2^{-i};d)\cap V_{i-1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where we put
V_{-1}=X

(in the
i
-th step, apply Lemma
2.4
to

A=\widetilde{B}(F,2^{-i-1};d)

and

U=\widetilde{U}(F,2^{-i};d)\cap V_{i-1}
).

Note that for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we obtain
the next inclusions.

(a)

\widetilde{E}(F,2^{-i})\subset X\setminus V_{i}
,

(b)

X\setminus V_{i}\subset\widetilde{E}(F,2^{-i-1})
.

Put
U_{0}=X\setminus V_{0}
and

U_{i}=V_{i}\setminus V_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
According to
[
7
, Corollary 1.4]
and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus F)=0
,
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we can find
a mutually disjoint family
\{O_{s}\}_{s\in S(i)}
of
clopen subsets of
X

such that
\coprod_{s\in S(i)}O_{s}=U_{i}
and

\yodiam O_{s}\leq 2^{-i}
.
We may assume that
S(i)\cap S(j)=\emptyset

if
i\neq j
.
Put
S=\coprod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}S(i)
.
Notice that

\coprod_{s\in S}O_{s}=X
.
For every
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
let
P_{i}
be a maximal
(2^{-i})
-separated
subset of
F
with respect to
d
such that

P_{i}\subset P_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Zorn’s lemma guarantees the existence of

\{P_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
.

For
every
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
for every
s\in S(i)
,
put

R_{s}=\inf\{\,d(o,a)\mid\text{$o\in O_{s}$ and
$a\in F$}\,\}
.
We can take points

o_{s}\in O_{s}
and

a_{s}\in F
,
with

d(o_{s},a_{s})<R_{s}+2^{-i}
.
Since
P_{i}
is a maximal
(2^{-i})
-separable subset of
F
,
we can find a point

p_{s}\in P_{i}
with

d(a_{s},p_{s})<2^{-i}
.
Then for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
s\in S(i)
, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle d(o_{s},p_{s})<R_{s}+2^{-i+1}.

We define a map

r\colon X\to F
by

\displaystyle r(x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{$x\in F$,}\\
p_{s}&\text{if $x\in O_{s}$ for some $s\in S$.}\end{cases}

By this definition,
the map
r
satisfies
r(a)=a
for all
a\in F
.

We now show the condition
(D)
.
For every
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
take
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with

2^{-i}\leq\epsilon
.
Then, due to the property
(a)
, we have

r\left(\widetilde{E}(F,\epsilon)\right)\subset r\left(\widetilde{E}(F,2^{-i})\right)\subset r(X\setminus V_{i})=\bigcup_{k=0}^{i-1}r(U_{k})\subset P_{i}.

Since
P_{i}
is closed and discrete in
F
,
so is

r\left(\widetilde{E}(A,\epsilon)\right)
.
This means that
the condition
(D)
is satisfied.

We next show the condition
(SR)
.
Take a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varrho_{d,F}(x_{n})=0
.
If
x_{n}\in F
, then
d(x_{n},r(x_{n}))=0
.
Thus,
we only need to consider the case where
each
x_{n}
belongs to
X\setminus F
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
take
M(n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and

s(n)\in S(M(n))

satisfying that
x_{n}\in O_{s(n)}
.
Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varrho_{d,F}(x_{n})=0
,
the inequality

R_{s(n)}\leq\varrho_{d,F}(x_{n})
implies

R_{s(n)}\to 0
as

n\to\infty
.
To prove
M(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
for the sake of contradiction, suppose that

M(n)\not\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Then
we can take an integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and a strictly increasing sequence

\phi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

M(\phi(n))\leq k
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Notice that

x_{\phi(n)}\in X\setminus V_{k+1}

for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
The inclusion

X\setminus V_{k+1}\subset\widetilde{E}(F,2^{-k-2})
(see
(b)
)
implies that

2^{-k-2}\leq\varrho_{d,F}(x_{\phi(n)})
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
This contradicts the equality that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\varrho_{d,F}(x_{i})=0
.
Thus
M(n)\to\infty

as
n\to\infty
.
From
\yodiam O_{s(n)}\leq 2^{-M(n)}
,

x_{n}\in O_{s(n)}
, and (
2.3
), it follows that

\displaystyle d(x_{n},r(x_{n}))=d(x_{n},p_{s(n)})\leq d(x_{n},o_{s(n)})+d(o_{s(n)},p_{s(n)})

\displaystyle<\yodiam(O_{s(n)})+R_{s(n)}+2^{-M(n)+1}\leq R_{s(n)}+2^{-M(n)+2}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.
This proves the condition
(SR)
.

It remains to show that
r
is continuous.
Take sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
in
X

and
a\in X
such that

x_{i}\to a
.
If
a\in X\setminus F
, then the point

a
belongs to some

O_{s}
. Thus
x_{i}\in O_{s}
for all sufficiently large

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Hence

r(x_{i})\to p_{s}(=r(a))
.
If
a\in F
, then

\lim_{i\to\infty}\varrho_{d,F}(x_{i})=0
.
The condition
(SR)
implies that

\lim_{i\to\infty}d(x_{i},r(x_{i}))=0
.
Namely,

r(x_{i})\to a(=r(a))
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The name of the condition
(SR)
in Theorem
2.11
stands for
“Self-Reference.”

To show that
Theorem
2.11
certainly
covers
[
8
, Lemma]
, we prove the following lemma,
which is not
used to prove our main results
in this paper.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and
F
be a
non-empty closed subset of
X
with

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus F)=0
.
If a retraction

r\colon X\to F

and
d\in\met(X)
satisfy the conditions

(D)
and
(SR)
in
Theorem
2.11
,
then
r
is a closed map.

Proof.

Take an arbitrary closed subset of

A
of

X
and
take an arbitrary point

y\in\cl(r(A))
.
We shall show that

y\in r(A)
.
If

y
is a isolated point of
\cl(A)
,
then

y\in r(A)
.
We only need to consider the case where

y
is not isolated in
\cl(A)
.
In this case,
there exists a sequence

\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

in

A

such that
r(x_{i})\to y

as
i\to\infty

and

r(x_{i})\neq r(x_{j})

for all distinct

i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

For the sake of contradiction,
suppose that
there exists

\epsilon\in(0,\infty)

such that

x_{i}\in\widetilde{E}(F,\epsilon)
for all

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
From the condition
(D)
,
it follow that
the set

\{\,r(x_{i})\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,\}

is closed and discrete in
F
.
This is a contradiction to the assumptions
that

r(x_{i})\to y

as

i\to\infty

and

r(x_{i})\neq r(x_{j})

for all distinct

i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Thus we can take subsequence

\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

of

\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

such that

\lim_{i\to 0}\varrho_{d,F}(z_{i})=0
.
The condition
(SR)

guarantees

\lim_{i\to\infty}d(z_{i},r(z_{i}))=0
.
Hence
z_{i}\to y
as
i\to\infty
.
This yields
y\in A\cap F
, and hence

r(y)=y
. Thus
y\in r(A)
.
Therefore we conclude that

r
is a closed map.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

In
[
2
, Theorem 2.9]
,
for every ultrametric space
(X,d)
, and for
every
closed subset
F
,
Brodskiy–Dydak–Higes–Mitra constructed a
Lipschitz retraction from
X
to
F
.
To remark that their retraction satisfies the
conditions
(D)
and
(SR)
,
we briefly review the construction.
Let
(X,d)
be an ultrametric space,
and
A
be a closed subset of
X
.
Fix
\tau\in(1,\infty)
.
Take an order
\preceq
on
X
such that
it is well-ordered on every bounded subset of
X
.
Such a order can be obtained by
gluing well-orderings on
\{\,x\in X\mid k\leq d(x,y)<k+1\,\}
together, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
For
x\in X
, we put

A[x]=\{\,a\in A\mid d(x,a)\leq\tau\cdot\varrho_{d,A}(x)\,\}
.
We define a map
r\colon X\to A
by

r(x)=\min_{\preceq}A[x]
; namely

r(x)
is the least element of
A[x]
with respect to

\preceq
.
Then
r
is a
(\tau^{2})
-Lipschitz retraction
(see
[
2
, Theorem 2.9]
).
By the construction
(especially,
A[x]
), the map
r
satisfies the condition

(SR)
.
We now prove the condition
(D)
.
We shall show that
for all
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
if
x,y\in\widetilde{E}(A,\epsilon)
satisfies

d(r(x),r(y))<\epsilon
,
then
r(x)=r(y)
.
In this setting, since
d
satisfies the strong triangle inequality and
d(r(x),r(y))<d(x,r(x))
,
we have
d(x,r(x))=d(x,r(y))
, and
hence
r(y)\in A[x]
.
By the definition of
r
, we obtain
r(x)\preceq r(y)
.
Similarly, we also obtain

r(y)\preceq r(x)
.
Hence
r(x)=r(y)
.
Therefore
r(\widetilde{E}(A,\epsilon))
is

\epsilon
-separated in
(X,d)
, and hence
the condition
(D)
is satisfied.
Note that
due to Lemma
2.12
,
the retraction
r
is a closed map.
Based on the fact that
r
satisfies the
condition
(SR)
,
as far as we consider ultrametrizable spaces,
in the arguments discussed below,
we can use Brodskiy–Dydak–Higes–Mitra’s retraction instead of
Engelking’s one.

3.
Embedding into a product space

This section is devoted to proving
our first main result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and

F
be a non-empty closed subset of
X
with

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus F)=0
.
Fix
d\in\met(X)
, and
we consider that
X_{/F}
is equipped with
the topology generated by
\Theta(d)
.
Let
r\colon X\to F
be a retraction
stated in Theorem
2.11

associated with
d
.
Define a map
\Phi\colon X\to F\times X_{/F}
by

\Phi(x)=(r(x),\pi(x))
.
Then the map

\Phi

satisfies the following conditions:

(P1)

We have

\Phi(a)=(a,\omega_{F})
for all
a\in F
.

(P2)

The map
\Phi
is a topological embedding and

\Phi(X)
is a closed subset of

F\times X_{/F}
.

Proof.

First note that the map
\Phi
is continuous and
injective.
The condition
(P1)
is deduced
from the definition of
\Phi
.
To prove the condition
(P2)
,
it suffices to show that

\Phi
is a closed map.
Take an arbitrary closed subset
A
of
X
.
To verify that
\Phi(A)
is
closed in
F\times X_{/F}
,
take
y\in\cl(\Phi(A))
.
If

y
is isolated in
\cl(\Phi(A))
,
then
y\in\Phi(X)
.
We may assume that

y
is non-isolated in

\cl(\Phi(A))
.
Then we can find a sequence

\{x_{i}\}_{\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
in
A

such that

\Phi(x_{i})\to y

as

i\to\infty
.
Put
y=(z,s)
, where
z\in F
and
s\in X_{/F}
.
Remark that in this situation,
we have
r(x_{i})\to z
and

\pi(x_{i})\to s
as

i\to\infty
.
We divide the proof into two cases.

Case 1. (
s=\pi(w)
for some
w\in X\setminus F
):
Since

\pi|_{X\setminus F}\colon X\setminus F\to X_{/F}\setminus\{\omega_{F}\}
is a homeomorphism (see

(3)
in
Proposition

2.6
),
the convergence

\pi(x_{i})\to\pi(w)(=s)
implies
that
x_{i}\to w
as

i\to\infty
. Thus
w\in A
.
Using the continuity of
\Phi
,
we obtain

y=\Phi(w)
, and hence

y\in\Phi(A)
.

Case 2. (

):
In this case,
we have

,
and hence
\varrho_{d,F}(x_{i})\to 0
as

.
The condition

in Theorem

implies that

as

.
Since

as
i\to\infty
, we have

x_{i}\to z
as
i\to\infty
.
Thus

.
Using the continuity of

\Phi
, we have

y=\Phi(z)
, and hence

y\in\Phi(A)
.
Therefore

\Phi(A)
is closed in

.
This finishes the proof.

4.1.
Fractal dimensions

We first review the fractal dimensions.
Most of definitions and
statements on the fractal dimensions in this subsection
are shared
with the author’s preprint

[
15
]
.

In this paper, we mainly deal with
the Hausdorff dimension

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,d)
,
the packing dimension

\dim_{\mathrm{P}}(X,d)
,
the upper box dimension

\overline{\dim}_{\mathrm{B}}(X,d)
,
and
the Assouad dimension

\dim_{\mathrm{A}}(X,d)
.

4.1.1.
The Hausdorff dimension

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
For
\delta\in(0,\infty)
,
we denote by
\mathbf{F}_{\delta}(X,d)
the set of all subsets of
X
with diameter smaller than
\delta
.
For
s\in[0,\infty)
, and
\delta\in(0,\infty)
,
we define the measure
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}

on
X
as

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(A)=\inf\left\{\,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\yodiam(A_{i})^{s}\ \middle|\ A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i},\ A_{i}\in\mathbf{F}_{\delta}(X,d)\,\right\}.

For
s\in(0,\infty)

we define
the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure

\mathcal{H}^{s}

on
(X,d)
as

\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)=\sup_{\delta\in(0,\infty)}\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(A)
.
We denote by
\hdim(X,d)

the
Hausdorff dimension of
(X,d)
defined as

\hdim(X,d)=\sup\{\,s\in[0,\infty)\mid\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)=\infty\,\}=\inf\{\,s\in[0,\infty)\mid\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)=0\,\}
.

4.1.2.
The packing dimension

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
For a subset
A
of
X
, and for
\delta\in(0,\infty)
,
we denote by
\mathbf{Pa}_{\delta}(A)
the set of all
finite or countable sequence
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}(N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\cup\{\infty\})
in
(0,\delta)

for which
there exists a sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in
A
such
that if
i\neq j
, then we have

B(x_{i},r_{i})\cap B(x_{j},r_{j})=\emptyset
.
For
s\in[0,\infty)
,
\delta\in(0,\infty)
,
and a subset
A
of
X
,
we define the quantity
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\delta}^{s}(A)
by

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\delta}^{s}(A)=\sup\left\{\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s}\ \middle|\ \{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbf{Pa}_{\delta}(A)\,\right\}.

We then define the
s
-dimensional pre-packing measure

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{s}
on
(X,d)
as

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{s}(A)=\inf_{\delta\in(0,\infty)}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\delta}^{s}
,
and
we define the

s
-dimensional packing measure

\mathcal{P}^{s}
on

(X,d)

as

\mathcal{P}^{s}(A)=\inf\left\{\,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{s}(S_{i})\ \middle|\ A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}S_{i}\,\right\}.

We denote by
\pdim(X,d)

the
packing dimension of
(X,d)
defined as

\pdim(X,d)=\sup\{\,s\in[0,\infty)\mid\mathcal{P}^{s}(X)=\infty\,\}=\inf\{\,s\in[0,\infty)\mid\mathcal{P}^{s}(X)=0\,\}
.

4.1.3.
Box dimensions

For a metric space
(X,d)
, and for
r\in(0,\infty)
,
a subset
A
of
X
is said to be an

r
-net
if

X=\bigcup_{a\in A}B(a,r)
.
We denote by
\mathrm{N}_{d}(X,r)
the least cardinality of
r
-nets of
X
.
We define
the
upper box dimension
\ubdim(X,d)

by

\displaystyle\ubdim(X,d)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\mathrm{N}_{d}(X,r)}{-\log r}.

4.1.4.
The Assouad dimension

For a metric space
(X,d)
,
we denote by
\adim(X,d)

the
Assouad dimension
of
(X,d)

defined by the infimum of all

\lambda\in(0,\infty)
for which
there exists
C\in(0,\infty)
such that
for all
R,r\in(0,\infty)
with
r<R
and for all
x\in X

we have

\mathrm{N}_{d}(B(x,R),r)\leq C\left(R/r\right)^{\lambda}
.
If such
\lambda
does not exist, we define

\adim(X,d)=\infty
.
We say that a metric space is
doubling
if
its Assouad dimension is finite.

The next theorem is deduced from

[
22
]
,
[
10
, Lemma 2.4.3]
, and

[
9
, Chapters 2 and 3]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The following statements hold true:

(1)

If
(X,d)
is a bounded metric space,
then we have

\hdim(X,d)\leq\pdim(X,d)\leq\ubdim(X,d)\leq\adim(X,d).

(2)

If
(X,d)
is a metric space, then we have

\hdim(X,d)\leq\pdim(X,d)\leq\adim(X,d).

Remark 4.1
.

If
(X,d)
is unbounded, then
the inequality
\ubdim(X,d)\leq\adim(X,d)
is
not always true
since in this setting,
\ubdim(X,d)=\infty
.
For example, the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R},d_{\mathbb{R}})

satisfies that
\ubdim(\mathbb{R},d_{\mathbb{R}})=\infty
and

\adim(\mathbb{R},d_{\mathbb{R}})=1
.

4.2.
Extensors

For a metric space
(X,d)
,
we denote by
\mathrm{Cm}(X)

the set of all complete metrics in
\met(X)
.
We also
denote by
\mathrm{Pr}(X)

the set of all proper metrics in
\met(X)
.
For
\eta\in(0,\infty)
,
a subset
A
of a metric space
(X,d)
is

\eta
-dense
if for all
x\in X
,
there exists
a\in A
with
d(x,a)\leq\eta
.
Let
X
,
Y
, and
Z
be sets.
Let

f\colon X\to Y
and

d\colon Y\times Y\to Z
be maps.
Then, we define a map

f^{*}d\colon X\times X\to Z
by

f^{*}d(x,y)=d(f(x),f(y))
.
For a metrizable space
X
, and
for metrics
d,e\in\met(X)
,
we also define
d\lor e\in\met(X)

by
(d\lor e)(x,y)=d(x,y)\lor e(x,y)
.

The next is our second main result:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
X
be a metrizable space,
and
F
be a non-empty
closed subset of
X
with

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{T}}(X\setminus F)=0
.
Let
\lambda\in\met(X)
and
we consider that
X_{/F}
is
equipped with the topology generated by

\Theta(\lambda)
.
Let
\Phi\colon X\to F\times X_{/F}

be a topological embedding
stated in Theorem
3.1

associated with
\lambda
.
Let
v\in\met(X_{/F})
.
Define a map

\Xi\colon\met(F)\to\met(X)

by
\Xi(d)=\Phi^{*}(d\times_{1}v)
.
Then the map
\Xi

satisfies that

(1)

for all
d\in\met(F)
, we have

\Xi(d)|_{F^{2}}=d
;

(2)

for all

,
we have

\metdis_{X}(\Xi(d),\Xi(e))=\metdis_{F}(d,e)
(i.e.,
\Xi
is

(3)

if

satisfies

for all

\Xi(d)(x,y)\leq\Xi(e)(x,y)
for all

;

(4)

for all

\Xi(d\lor e)=\Xi(d)\lor\Xi(e)
.

In addition,
the following statements are true:

(A1)

If

(A2)

-compact locally compact,

\eta
-dense in
