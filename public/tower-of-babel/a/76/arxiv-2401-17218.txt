On Sequential Versions of Distributional Topological Complexity
Ekansh Jauhari

Abstract
. We define a (non-decreasing) sequence { 𝖽𝖳𝖢 m ⁡ ( X ) } m ≥ 2 \{\dTC_{m}(X)\}_{m\geq 2} of sequential versions of distributional topological complexity ( 𝖽𝖳𝖢 \dTC ) of a space X X introduced by Dranishnikov and Jauhari [ DJ ] . This sequence generalizes 𝖽𝖳𝖢 ⁡ ( X ) \dTC(X) in the sense that 𝖽𝖳𝖢 2 ⁡ ( X ) = 𝖽𝖳𝖢 ⁡ ( X ) \dTC_{2}(X)=\dTC(X) , and is a direct analog to the well-known sequence { 𝖳𝖢 m ⁡ ( X ) } m ≥ 2 \{\TC_{m}(X)\}_{m\geq 2} . We show that like 𝖳𝖢 m \TC_{m} and 𝖽𝖳𝖢 \dTC , the sequential versions 𝖽𝖳𝖢 m \dTC_{m} are also homotopy invariants. Furthermore, 𝖽𝖳𝖢 m ⁡ ( X ) \dTC_{m}(X) relates with the distributional LS-category ( 𝖽𝖼𝖺𝗍 \dcat ) of products of X X in the same way as 𝖳𝖢 m ⁡ ( X ) \TC_{m}(X) relates with the classical LS-category ( 𝖼𝖺𝗍 \cat ) of products of X X . On the other hand, by finding sharp cohomological lower bounds to 𝖽𝖳𝖢 m ⁡ ( X ) \dTC_{m}(X) , we provide various examples of closed manifolds X X for which the sequences { 𝖳𝖢 m ⁡ ( X ) } m ≥ 2 \{\TC_{m}(X)\}_{m\geq 2} and { 𝖽𝖳𝖢 m ⁡ ( X ) } m ≥ 2 \{\dTC_{m}(X)\}_{m\geq 2} coincide.

1.A.
Planning a sequential motion

Consider an advanced autonomous system that is required to reach a position
x_{i}\in X
at a time
t_{i}\in[0,1]
for each
1\leq i\leq m
, where
t_{i}<t_{i+1}
. The idea for the sequential motion of the system is as follows.

(1)

At time
t_{1}
, the system breaks into finitely many weighted pieces at the initial position
x_{1}
. All the pieces travel independently for time
t_{2}-t_{1}
to reach position
x_{2}
at time
t_{2}
.

(2)

At time
t_{2}
and position
x_{2}
, all the pieces reassemble back into the system. Then, the system breaks again at
x_{2}
into the same number of pieces having the same weights. These pieces travel to reach
x_{3}
at time
t_{3}
.

(3)

The process continues like this, where the system breaks into the same number of pieces having the same weights at time
t_{i}
and position
x_{i}
, the pieces travel independently to reach
x_{i+1}
at
t_{i+1}
, where they reassemble into the system, and then the system breaks again in the same manner.

(4)

Finally, the pieces reach the final position
x_{m}
at time
t_{m}
where they reassemble into the system at last.

We assume that our system above has weight
1
, i.e., the sum of the non-negative weights of the pieces involved is
1
.

The “weights of the pieces” represent the percentage of the portion of the original system in the corresponding pieces. For example, if the weights are
0.23,0.16,
and
0.61
, then the system breaks into pieces whose capacities/weights are
23\%,16\%
, and
61\%
of its original capacity/weight. In another sense, the “weight of a piece” represents the
probability
that at a given instance, the system is moving/traveling through that particular piece. More precisely, if we regard each piece
x_{i}
of the system as a “state” of its motion, then the weight
\lambda_{i}
is the probability that the system is in state
x_{i}
. For a related probabilistic interpreptation in the case of
\TC
, we refer to the reader to
[
Far2
, Section 12]
.

The input for our system is an ordered
m
-tuple of positions. Note that we require our system to first analyze the
m
positions and then decide the weighted pieces it needs for a continuous motion between those
m
positions in the given order by the process of repetitive breaking and reassembling. This is different, less chaotic, and more convenient than letting the system decide its weighted pieces at each individual
t_{i}
to travel between
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
for each
1\leq i\leq m-1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be the maximum number of pieces that the system can break into while traveling between all possible ordered
m
-tuples of positions in
X
. If no such
n
exists, we conclude that the system is extremely complicated for practical purposes and we say that its corresponding sequential distributional topological complexity is infinite. Such a case is hardly found in real-world situations and is therefore less interesting, both from a physical as well as a theoretical point of view.

1.B.
Continuous motion planning algorithm

Let
\overline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in X^{m}
be an ordered
m
-tuple. We define an

-distributed

-sequence
of

to be an unordered collection of
n

in

, with respective non-negative weights

, such that

(1)

the sum of the weights is

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}=1
, and

(2)

for each

for all

Hence, the desired sequential motion planning algorithm is a continuous assignment of each

to an
n
-distributed

-sequence of
\overline{x}
, which is an
unordered probability distribution
. For the case

, our above algorithm is in contrast to Farber’s algorithm
[

, Section 12]
corresponding to a probabilistic interpretation of

probability distribution. We note that as shown in
[

, Section 13]

from
[

]
coincides with the original definition of
\TC
from
[
Far1
]
for simplicial complexes.

The continuous algorithm we seek for the above problem is a natural generalization of the one for

from
[
DJ
]
in the following sense. For a metric space

, let
\mathcal{B}(Z)
denote the set of probability measures on

and

\mathcal{B}_{n}(Z)=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{B}(Z)\mid|\supp(\mu)|\leq n\right\}

denote the space of probability measures on
Z
supported by at most
n
points, equipped with the Lévy–Prokhorov metric
[
Pr
]
(see also
[
DJ
, Section 3.1]
). The Lévy–Prokhorov metric on
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}(Z)
is defined by setting the distance between any two given probability measures

as

\inf\left\{\epsilon>0\hskip 2.84526pt\middle|\hskip 2.84526pt\mu(A)<\mu(A^{\epsilon})+\epsilon\text{ and }\nu(A)<\nu(A^{\epsilon})+\epsilon\text{ for all }A\in\mathscr{B}(Z)\right\},

where

is the

\mu=\sum_{z\in F\subset Z,\ |F|\leq n}\lambda_{z}z,

where
\lambda_{z}\geq 0
and

, and
\supp(\mu)=\{z\in Z\mid\lambda_{z}>0\}
. Let

-distributed

We note that the breaking of the system into

-th

\phi_{j}(t_{i})=x_{i}

3.1 Definition
.

A

-navigation algorithm

3.2 Definition

For a given

-th higher distributional topological complexity

Each of the following results generalizes the respective statements for

3.3 Proposition
.

For each
m\geq 2
,
\dTC_{m}
is a homotopy invariant.

Proof.

Let us fix some
m\geq 2
. Let

be a homotopy domination with a continuous right homotopy inverse

. We only need to prove that
\dTC_{m}(Y)\leq\dTC_{m}(X)
. Since

, there exists a homotopy

defined as
h(y)=h_{y}
for each
y\in Y
, where
h_{y}(0)=y
and
h_{y}(1)=fg(y)
. Let
\dTC_{m}(X)=n
and

s_{m}:X^{m}\to\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(P(X))

be an
(n+1)
-distributed
m
-navigation algorithm on
X
. Let

induce a continuous map

due to the functoriality of
\mathcal{B}_{n+1}
. Then the composition

f_{*}s_{m}\hskip 2.84526ptg^{m}:Y^{m}\to\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(P(Y))

maps each
\overline{y}\in Y^{m}
to an
(n+1)
-distributed
m
-sequence of the ordered
m
-tuple
f^{m}g^{m}(\overline{y})=\{fg(y_{1}),\ldots,fg(y_{m})\}
. Let
s_{m}g^{m}(\overline{y})=\sum\lambda_{\phi}\phi
. For each such path
\phi\in\supp(s_{m}g^{m}(\overline{y}))
, let us consider the collection
\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m-1}\}
, where

\phi_{i}(s)=\phi\left(\frac{s+i-1}{m-1}\right)

for each

. So,
\phi_{i}
is a path in

from

to

\widehat{\phi}=\left(h_{y_{1}}\cdot f\phi_{1}\cdot\overline{h}_{y_{2}}\right)\star\left(h_{y_{2}}\cdot f\phi_{2}\cdot\overline{h}_{y_{3}}\right)\star\cdots\star\left(h_{y_{m-1}}\cdot f\phi_{m-1}\cdot\overline{h}_{y_{m}}\right),

where

denotes concatenation done using the map

from Lemma

with

for all

\sigma_{m}:Y^{m}\to\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(P(Y))

\sigma_{m}(\overline{y})=\sum_{\phi\hskip 2.84526pt\in\hskip 2.84526pt\supp(s_{m}g^{m}(\overline{y}))}\hskip 2.84526pt\lambda_{\phi}\hskip 1.42262pt\widehat{\phi}

-distributed

.
∎

The following proposition is straightforward (see
[

for a proof in the special case

3.4 Proposition
.

For each

,
\dTC_{m}(X)\leq\TC_{m}(X)

3.5 Proposition
.

For each
m\geq 2
,
\dcat(X^{m-1})\leq\dTC_{m}(X)

Proof.

Let
\dTC_{m}(X)=n
. Then there exists an
(n+1)
-distributed
m
-navigation algorithm on
X
, say

s_{m}:X^{m}\to\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(P(X)).

For a fixed basepoint

be the map

Let

\phi\in\supp(s_{m}J(\widetilde{x}))

-contraction of

3.6 Proposition
.

For each

,
\dTC_{m}(X)\leq\dcat(X^{m})

Proof.

Let

and let us have an
(n+1)
-contraction

to its fixed basepoint

-distributed

3.8 Proposition

-distributed

For any

and

For

3.11 Remark
.

Let us fix some
m\geq 3
. Due to Corollary
3.10
, we obtain for all

that

\TC_{m}({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})-\dTC_{m}({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})\geq\cat(({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})^{m-1})-\dTC_{m}({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})

\geq n(m-1)-(2^{m-1}-1)\geq n.

For

and any

, we get

Hence, the gap between

and

can be arbitrarily large for each

is a different notion than

for each

and the inequality in Proposition

can be strict for all

.

Since

is a homotopy invariant, for a discrete group

is a classifying space of the universal cover of spaces having

.

3.12 Remark
.

We note that an analog of

does not hold in the case of

for any

4.A.
Symmetric products

For a space

and

, its

-th symmetric product

is defined as the orbit space of the action of the symmetric group

on the product space

by permutation of coordinates. In this section, we regard each

and

denotes the number of times

appears in the unordered

-tuple

The following result in singular cohomology will be very useful in Section

.

(
[

)

For a finite simplicial complex

and any

In this section, we regard

as the subspace of

under the diagonal inclusion
\delta_{k}
and use the term

to refer to the map

5.
Distributional Sectional Category

Let

be a Hurewicz fibration. For each

, define a space

E_{n}(p)=\bigcup_{x\in B}\mathcal{B}_{n}(p^{-1}(x))=\left\{\mu\in B_{n}(E)\hskip 2.84526pt\middle|\hskip 2.84526pt\supp(\mu)\subset p^{-1}(x),\hskip 2.84526ptx\in B\right\}

and a map
\mathcal{B}_{n}(p):E_{n}(p)\to B
such that

\mathcal{B}_{n}(p)(\mu)=x\hskip 11.38109pt\text{whenever}\hskip 11.38109pt\mu\in\mathcal{B}_{n}(p^{-1}(x)).

5.1 Proposition

(
[
DJ
, Proposition 5.1]
)
.

The mapping

is a Hurewicz fibration.

5.2 Definition
.

Given a fibration

, or alternatively, its
distributional sectional category
, denoted
\dsecat(p)
, is the minimal number

such that
\mathcal{B}_{n+1}(p):E_{n+1}(p)\to B
admits a section.

Note that this definition is a distributional analog of the equivalent definition of
\secat
as it follows from Proposition
2.2
. From Proposition
2.2
, it is also to see that
\dsecat(p)\leq\secat(p)
for each fibration

.

We also note that the notion of analog sectional category was introduced and studied in
[

]
which is closely related with the notion of
\dsecat
.
