On the Connection Between Riemann Hypothesis and a Special Class of Neural Networks
Soufiane Hayou

Abstract
The Riemann hypothesis ( ℛ ​ ℋ \mathcal{RH} ) is a long-standing open problem in mathematics. It conjectures that non-trivial zeros of the zeta function all lie on the line Re ​ ( z ) = 1 / 2 \textup{Re}(z)=1/2 . The extent of the consequences of ℛ ​ ℋ \mathcal{RH} is far-reaching and touches a wide spectrum of topics including the distribution of prime numbers, the growth of arithmetic functions, the growth of Euler’s totient, etc. In this note, we revisit and extend an old analytic criterion of the ℛ ​ ℋ \mathcal{RH} known as the Nyman-Beurling criterion which connects the ℛ ​ ℋ \mathcal{RH} to a minimization problem that involves a special class of neural networks. This note is intended for an audience unfamiliar with ℛ ​ ℋ \mathcal{RH} . A gentle introduction to ℛ ​ ℋ \mathcal{RH} is provided.

4
Discussion on the Implications and Limitations

In this section, we discuss some empirical implications of Lemma
1
and Lemma
2

Probabilistic zero-free regions.

Notice that Lemmas
1
and
2
require access to the distance
\|1-f\|_{2}
which is generally intractable. However, we can approximate this quantity using Monte Carlo samples and obtain high probability bounds for this norm. Hence, the best we can do with such criterion is to verify the non-existence of zeroes of
\zeta
in some region
with high probability
. Indeed, using Hoefdding’s inequality, we have the following result.

Lemma 4
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
N\geq 1
and
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{N}
be iid uniform random variables on
I_{d}
. Let
f\in\mathcal{N}_{d}
(where for
d=1
, we denote
\mathcal{N}_{d}=\mathcal{N}
) such that
f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i,j}\rho\left(\frac{\beta_{i,j}}{x_{j}}\right)
for all
x\in I_{d}
, for some
m\geq 1,\beta\in I_{m\times d},c\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
. Then, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have with probability at least
1-\alpha
, the region
R_{N}\overset{def}{=}\{\textup{Re}(z)>\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\Delta_{N}(f)^{1/d}|z|^{2}\right)\}
is free of the zeroes of
\zeta
, where
\Delta_{N}(f)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-f(x_{i}))^{2}\,+(1+\|c\|_{1}^{2})\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)}{N}}
, with
\|c\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|c_{i}|
.

Proof.

The proof follows from a simple application of Hoeffding’s concentration inequality to control the deviations of the empirical risk
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-f(x_{i}))^{2}
. Hoeffding’s lemma requires that the random variables
(1-f(x_{i}))^{2}
are bounded, which is straightforward since
(1-f(x_{i}))^{2}\leq 2(1+f(x_{i})^{2})\leq 2(1+\|c\|^{2}_{1})
almost surely.
∎

The result of
Lemma

4
has an important implication on the choice of the sample size. Indeed, to have the coefficient
\delta_{N}^{1/d}
of order
\epsilon
with high probability, a necessary condition is that
N=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2d})
.

When is the multi-dimensional variant better than the one-dimensional criterion?

For some
d\geq 2
, it is straightforward that the multi-dimensional criterion given in Lemma
2
is better than the one given in Lemma
1
only if
\inf_{f\in\mathcal{N}_{d}}\|1-f\|_{2}^{1/d}<\inf_{f\in\mathcal{N}}\|1-f\|_{2}
. Under this condition, the zero-free region is larger with

. For empirical verification of the

and for same probability threshold

, Lemma

implies that the multi-dimensional setting is better than the one-dimensional counterpart whenever

. We discuss the feasibility of such conditions in the next paragraph.

What does it take to improve upon existing numerical verifications of
\mathcal{RH}
?

The high probability zero-free regions from Lemma
4
are of the form
\{\textup{Re}(z)>\frac{1}{2}(1+\Delta|z|^{2})\}
for some constant
\Delta>0
.

Using a different analytical criterion of the
\mathcal{RH}
,
Platt and Trudgian 2021
showed that the region
\{a+ib:a\in(0,1),b\in(0,\gamma],\gamma\approx 3\cdot 10^{12}\}
is free of the zeroes of
\zeta
. Hence, using Lemma
4
to improve this result requires that the region
R_{N}\cap\{a+ib,a\in(0,1),b\in(0,\gamma]\}
contains complex numbers
z
with imaginary part larger than order
10^{12}
. Let
z=a+ib\in\mathbb{C}
. Having

implies that
b^{2}<-a^{2}+\Delta_{N}(f)^{-1/d}(2a-1)
. For the region of interest where

(

(at least with some high probability certificate), we need to have

\eta(z)=2\frac{2^{z-1}-1}{2^{z}-1}\pi^{z-1}z\,\sin{\frac{\pi z}{2}}\Gamma(-z)\eta(1-z),

(5)

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. Using
Eq.

5

4

\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
for all complex numbers
z\in\mathbb{C}
(
\Gamma
is the analytic continuation of the original Gamma function defined on
\{z\in\mathbb{C},\textup{Re}(z)>0\}
by
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt
), we obtain that for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\textup{Re}(z)\in(0,1)
,

\zeta(z)=2^{z}\pi^{z-1}\sin\left(\frac{\pi z}{2}\right)\Gamma(1-z)\zeta(1-z).

(6)

Using the functional equation

2
, we can extend the definition of
\zeta
to the all the remaining complex numbers

\textup{Re}(z)\leq 0

\zeta

.

Zeros of the
\zeta
function.

From
Eq.

2
, the zeta function satisfies
\zeta(-2k)=0
for any integer
k\geq 1
. The negative even integers
\{-2k\}_{k\geq 1}
are thus called
trivial zeros
of the Riemann zeta function since the result follows from the simple fact that
\sin\left(-\pi k\right)=0
for all integers
k\geq 1
. The other zeros of
\zeta
are called non-trivial zeros, and their properties remain largely misunderstood. The
\mathcal{RH}
conjectures that they all lie on a the line
\textup{Re}(z)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Riemann Hypothesis (
\mathcal{RH}
).

All non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta
have a real part equal to
\frac{1}{2}
.

Whether
\mathcal{RH}
holds is still an open question. However, there have been a number of attempts to prove or disprove
\mathcal{RH}
in the literature. In the next section, we re-visit an old result that provides an analytic point of view of
\mathcal{RH}
.

Appendix B
The Nyman-Beurling Criterion

Theorem
1

(
Nyman 1950
).

The
\mathcal{RH}
is true if and only if
\mathcal{N}
is dense in
L_{2}(I_{1})
.

Beurling 1955
later extended this result by showing that for any
p>1
, the
\zeta
function has no zeroes in the set
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Re}(z)>1/p\}
if and only if the set
\mathcal{N}
is dense in
L_{p}(I_{1})
.

Theorem
2
(
Beurling 1955
)

The Riemann zeta function is free from zeros in the half plane
Re(z)>\frac{1}{p},1<p<\infty
, if and only if
\mathcal{N}
is dense in
L_{p}(I_{1})
.

The following is a sketch of the proof by
Beurling 1955
. It helps understand the machinery of the proofs of
1
and
2
for the sufficient condition.

Sketch of the proof.

The connection between
\mathcal{RH}
and the class
\mathcal{N}
is due to the following identity that relates the zeta function
\zeta
to the fractional part function
\rho
3
3

3

After multiple attempts, we could not find the original paper where this identity has first appeared. However, it has been mentioned in different works, e.g.
(
Nyman 1950
;
Beurling 1955
)
.

\int_{0}^{1}\rho\left(\frac{\theta}{x}\right)x^{z-1}dx=\frac{\theta}{z-1}-\frac{\theta^{z}\zeta(z)}{z},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\textup{Re}(z)>0,\theta\in I.

(7)

Therefore, given a function
f\in\mathcal{N}
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}f(x)x^{z-1}dx=-\frac{\zeta(z)\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\beta_{i}^{z-1}}{z},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\textup{Re}(z)>0.

(8)

Now fix
p>1
and assume that the class
\mathcal{N}
is dense in
L_{p}(I)
. Therefore, given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a function
f\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\|\mathbf{1}-f\|<\varepsilon
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the constant function on
I
equal to
1
everywhere. Using
Eq.

8
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}(1-f(x))x^{z-1}dx=\frac{1}{z}\left(1+\zeta(z)\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\beta_{i}^{z-1}\right),\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C},\textup{Re}(z)>0.

Using Hölder’s inequality, we have for any
z
such that
\textup{Re}(z)>1/p

\|\mathbf{1}-f\|_{p}\|x^{z-1}\|_{q}<\varepsilon\frac{1}{(q(\textup{Re}(z)-1/p))^{1/q}},

where
q>1
satisfies
1/p+1/q=1
. This yields,

|1-\zeta(z)\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\beta_{i}^{z}|^{q}<\frac{\varepsilon^{q}|z|^{q}}{q(\textup{Re}(z)-1/p)}.

Hence, in the region
\{z\in\mathbb{C},\textup{Re}(z)>1/p+\varepsilon^{q}|z|^{q}/q\}
,
\zeta(z)
cannot be equal to zero. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrarily chosen, we conclude that
\zeta(z)\neq 0
if
\textup{Re}(z)>1/p
.

For the necessary condition, We invite the reader to check
Beurling 1955
.

The identity (
7
) is the nub of the proof above. It provides an integral representation of the zeta function
\zeta
in terms of the fractional part function
\rho
. Hence, one would expect that some properties of zeta function should in-principle be reflected on some function classes involving the function
\rho
. This is precisely the idea behind Theorems
1
and
2
.
More importantly, from the analysis above, we have the following analytic criterion for zero-free regions of the zeta function.

Lemma
1

(Nyman-Beurling zero-free regions)

Let
f\in\mathcal{N}
and
\delta=\|1-f\|_{2}
be the distance between the constant function
1
on
I
and
f
. Then, the region
\{z\in\mathbb{C},\textup{Re}(z)>\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\delta^{2}|z|^{2}\right)\}
is free of zeroes of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta
.

Existing empirical verification studies of the
\mathcal{RH}
use different analytic criteria to locate the zeroes. To the best of our knowledge, the most recent verification study was conducted by
Platt and Trudgian 2021
where the authors have found that in the region
\{a+ib:a\in(0,1),b\in(0,\gamma],\gamma\approx 3\cdot 10^{12}\}
, the
\mathcal{RH}
is satisfied, meaning that all the zeroes of
\zeta
are on the line
\textup{Re}(z)=1/2
. Is it possible to use Lemma
1
to beat this record? first, notice that unless the function
f
is simple, the norm
\|1-f\|_{2}
is generally intractable, an can only be approximated with Monte-Carlo sampling. See
Section

4
for more details.

The result of Lemma
1
(and that of
2
) is stated for the function class
\mathcal{N}
which consists of a special neural architecture with one-dimensional inputs.
Can we generalize this to multi-dimensional inputs?
In the next section, we answer this question positively by introducing a generalized class of neural networks with multi-dimensional inputs.

Appendix C
A sufficient condition in the multi-dimensional case

Let
d\geq 1
and consider the following class of neural networks with inputs in
I_{d}
,

\mathcal{N}_{d}=\{f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i,j}\rho\left(\frac{\beta_{i,j}}{x_{j}}\right),x\in I^{d}:m\geq 1,c\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m},\beta\in I_{d\times m},c^{T}\beta=0\},

where
c=(c_{1,1},c_{2,1},\dots,c_{m,1},\dots,c_{m,d})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
is the flattened vector of
(c_{.,j})_{1\leq j\leq d}
. Notice that we recover the Nyman-Beurling class when
d=1
. Using this class, we can generalize the zero-free region result given by
Lemma

1
to a multi-dimensional setting.

Lemma
2

[zero-free regions for general
d\geq 1
]

Let
d\geq 1
and
f\in\mathcal{N}_{d}
. Let
\delta=\|1-f\|_{2}
be the distance between the constant function
1
on
I_{d}
and
f
. Then, the region
\{z\in\mathbb{C},\textup{Re}(z)>\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\delta^{\frac{2}{d}}|z|^{2}\right)\}
is free of zeroes of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta
.

Proof.

Let
d\geq 1
and
f\in\mathcal{N}_{d}
. Using
Eq.

7
, namely
\int_{0}^{1}\rho\left(\frac{\alpha}{x}\right)x^{z-1}dx=\frac{\alpha}{z-1}-\frac{\alpha^{z}\zeta(z)}{z}
for
\alpha\in(0,1),\textup{Re}(z)>1/2
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{I_{d}}f(x)\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{z-1}dx

\displaystyle=z^{-d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i,j}\left(\frac{\beta_{i,j}}{z-1}-\frac{\beta_{i,j}^{z}\zeta(z)}{z}\right)

\displaystyle=-z^{-d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i,j}\beta_{i,j}^{z}\zeta(z),

where we have used the fact that
c^{T}\beta=0
.

Therefore, we have

\int_{I_{d}}(1-f(x))\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{z-1}dx=z^{-d}(1+\zeta(z)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i,j}\beta_{i,j}^{z}).

(9)

Using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we obtain

\left|1+\zeta(z)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i,j}\beta_{i,j}^{z}\right|^{2}\leq\|1-f\|^{2}\frac{|z|^{2d}}{(2\textup{Re}(z)-1)^{d}},

where we have used the fact that
\left\|\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{z-1}\right\|^{2}_{2}=(2\textup{Re}(z)-1)^{-d}
. Therefore, for all complex numbers
z
satisfying
\textup{Re}(z)>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta^{2/d}|z|^{2}}{2}
, we have
\zeta(z)\neq 0
. This is true since we can choose
f
such that
\|1-f\|
is arbitrarily close to
\delta
.
∎

Notice that if
\delta
can be chosen arbitrarily small, then the zero-free region in
Lemma

2
can be extended to the whole half-plane
\{\textup{Re}(z)>1/2\}
. This is a generalization of the sufficient condition of
2
in the multi-dimensional case.
