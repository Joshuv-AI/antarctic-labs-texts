ON VARIATIONS OF YAMA NIM AND TRIANGULAR NIM
Shun-ichi Kimura

Abstract




Abstract

Yama Nim is a two heaps Nim game introduced in the second author’s Master Thesis, where the player takes more than
2
tokens from one heap, and return
1
token to the other heap. Triangular Nim is a generalization, where the player takes several tokens from one heap, and return some tokens (at least one token) to the other heap, so that the total number of the tokens in the heaps decrease strictly. In this paper, we investigate their winning strategies, Grundy numbers, and their variations and generalizations. Particularly interesting is the Wythoff variations, where in addition to the Yama/Triangular Nim moves, the player is allowed to take tokens from both heaps, say
i
tokens from the first heap and
j
tokens from the other, under some restrictions for
i
and
j
. For example when we force
i=j>0
for the Triangular Nim, then
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
with
x\leq y
is in the
{\mathcal{P}}
-position if and only if
(x,y)\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,3),(3,6),(6,10),(10,15),\ldots\}
, namely the winning strategy is described by triangular numbers. In other rulesets, we also found examples where the square numbers, pentagonal numbers, geometric progressions, and so on.

Preliminary

In this paper, we treat a zero-sum combinatorial game played by two players, say Left and Right.
Unless otherwise stated, we consider impartial short games
[
Siegel13
, pp 34-36]
as follows:

Definition 1
.

The ruleset
\Gamma
with the set of positions
X
is called impartial short game if it satisfies the following three conditions:

(1)

(Finite) Each
G\in X
has just finitely many distinct subpositions.

(2)

(Loopfree) Every run of
G\in X
has finite length.

(3)

(Impartial) Both players have exactly the same moves available from each
G\in X
.

For the most part, we work with Nim games with two heaps, and then our set of positions
X
will be written as

X={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\,|\,x\geq 0,y\geq 0\}

where
x
is the number of tokens in the first heap and
y
in the second. Our ruleset can be described by the option function
f:X\to{\mathrm{P}ow}(X)
, where
{\mathrm{P}ow}(X)
the set of subsets of
X
, so that from the position
m\in X
, the player can choose her/his move from
f(m)
. We also assume normal play for the most part of this paper, namely the last player wins. A position
m\in X
is a
{\mathcal{P}}
-position if the previous player has the winning strategy, and
m
is in
{\mathcal{N}}
-position if the next player has the winning strategy. We can also define Grundy numbers to give more information
[
Siegel13
, Chapter IV]
, so that
m\in X
is in
{\mathcal{P}}
-position if and only if its Grundy number is
0
.

It is well-known that a subset
S\in X
coincides with the set of
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions in the normal play if and only if

(1)

For any
m\in S
and for any its option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
, we have
m^{\prime}\not\in S
.

(2)

For any
m\in X\backslash S
, there is some option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
such that
m^{\prime}\in S
.

We start by generalizing the statement above to Grundy numbers.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Gamma
be the ruleset and
X
its set of positions. Assume that
X
is divided into disjoint subsets
X=S_{0}\sqcup S_{1}\sqcup\cdots
, indexed by non-negative integers, in such a way that for any
m\in S_{i}
, its option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
is not
S_{i}
, and for any
m\in S_{i}
with
j<i
, there is some option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
such that
m^{\prime}\in S_{j}
. Then
S_{i}
is exactly the set of positions with its Grundy numbers equal to
i
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
be the
1
-heap Nim (and its positions by abuse of notation) and consider the disjunctive sum
\Gamma+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. It is enough to show that
S=\{(m,i)\in X\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\,|\,m\in S_{i}\}
is the set of
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions. When
(m,i)\in S
, then the options are
(m^{\prime},i)
with
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
, or
(m,j)
with
j<i
, and both of them are clearly out of
S
. On the other hand, if
(m,k)\in X\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
with
m\in S_{i}
, then either
i>k
or
i<k
. When
i>k
, the player has the option
m^{\prime}\in m
with
m^{\prime}\in S_{k}
, and when
i<k
, the player can choose the option
(m,k)\to(m,i)
.
∎

The following is a subset of the results by Yohei Yamasaki
[
Ya80
]
and
[
Ya81
]
, stated in a way convenient for our purpose.

Definition 2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a ruleset with
X
the set of positions. For each
m\in X
with its Grundy number for the normal play
g(m)
, we determine either it is
in the final phase
or
normal phase
, inductively as follows:

(1)

If
g(m)=0
and
m
has no options, namely
f(m)=\emptyset
, then
m
is in the final phase.

(2)

If
g(m)=1
and for all
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
with
g(m^{\prime})=0
,
m^{\prime}
is in the final phase, then
m
is in the final phase. Otherwise,
m
is in the normal phase.

(3)

If
g(m)=0
with
f(m)\not=\emptyset
,
m
is in the final phase if and only if there exists an option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
with
g(m^{\prime})=1
in the final phase.

(4)

If
g(m)>1
, then
m
is in the normal phase.

We say
\Gamma
is
admissible
if for each
m\in X
with
g(m)>1
, either there is an option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
with
g(m^{\prime})=0
in the normal phase, or
m"\in f(m)
with
g(m")=1
in the final phase.

Moreover, we say that
\Gamma
is
universally admissible
if it satisfies the following
3
conditions:

(i)

If
m\in X
has Grundy number
1
and in the normal phase, for any option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
with
g(m)=0
,
m
is in the normal phase.

(ii)

If
m\in X
has Grundy number
0
and in the final phase, for any option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
with
g(m)=1
,
m
is in the final phase.

(iii)

If
m\in X
has Grundy number
g(m)>1
, then it has options
m^{\prime}_{0}
and
m^{\prime}_{1}
so that
g(m^{\prime}_{0})=0,g(m^{\prime}_{1})=1
, and either both of them are in the normal phase, or both of them are in the final phase.

Remark 1
.

Universally admissible rulesets are admissible.

Remark 2
.

Assume that for any position
m\in X
with
g(m)=0
, there exists some option
m^{\prime}\in f(m)
with
g(m^{\prime})=1
. Then Conway induction implies that for any
m\in X
with
g(m)\leq 1
,
m
is in the final phase. If moreover the ruleset is admissible, it is universally admissible.

Proposition 2
.

When the ruleset
\Gamma
is admissible, its winning strategy for the misère play can be described as follows: The
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions for the misère play for
\Gamma
is

\{m\in X\,|\,\hbox{$g(m)=0$ in the normal phase}\}\cup\{m\in X\,|\,\hbox{$g(m)=1$ in the final phase}\}

The disjoint sum of universally admissible rulesets
\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}
is again universally admissible, with
(m_{1},m_{2})
is in the final phase if and only if both
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are in the final phase.

2
Grundy numbers for Yama Nim and Triangular Nim

In this section, we study the Grundy numbers for Yama Nim and Triangular Nim.

First, we start by the ruleset of Yama Nim, which was introduced in the Master Thesis of the second author
[
Y23
]
, where also its Grundy numbers and winning strategies both for the normal play and the misère play are given (see Theorem
1
and Corollay
1
below).

Definition 3
.

Let
X={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
be the set of positions of Yama Nim. The options are given by

f((x,y))=\{(x-i,y+1)\,|\,2\leq i\leq x\}\cup\{(x+1,y-i)\,|\,2\leq i\leq y\}.

Theorem 1
.

The Grundy number for the normal play of Yama Nim is given by

g((x,y))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&(|x-y|\leq 1)\\
{\mathrm{Min}}(x,y)+1&(|x-y|>1)\end{array}.\right.

Proof.

We define
S_{0}=\{(x,y)\,|\,|x-y|\leq 1\}
and for
i>0
,
S_{i}=\{(x,y)\not\in S_{0}\,|\,{\mathrm{Min}}(x,y)+1=i\}
. We check the conditions for Proposition
1
. First, we start showing that if
(x,y)\in S_{i}
, then its option
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is not in
S_{i}
.

When
(x,y)\in S_{0}
, by symmetry, it is enough to show that
(x-i,y+1)\not\in S_{0}
for
i\geq 2
, which follows from

|(x-i)-(y+1)|=|(x-y)-(i+1)|\geq|i+1|-|x-y|\geq 2.

When
(x,y)\in S_{i}
with
i>0
, to show that its options are not in
S_{i}
, we may assume that
x<y
with
y-x>1
. If
(x,y)\to(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
then either
x^{\prime}<x
(in which case
{\mathrm{Min}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})<{\mathrm{Min}}(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
cannot be in
S_{i}
), or
x^{\prime}=x+1
and
y^{\prime}\leq y-2
, in which case if
{\mathrm{Min}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})={\mathrm{Min}}(x,y)
, then we need to have
y^{\prime}=x
, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S_{0}
, not in
S_{i}
.

We also need to show that if
(x,y)\in S_{i}
with
i>0
and
j<i
, then there is some option
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S_{j}
of
(x,y)
. We may assume
x<y
with
y-x\geq 2
. When
j=0
, we can move to
(x+1,x)\in S_{0}
. When
j>0
, we can move to
(x+1,j-1)
because
j\leq i-1\leq x\leq y-2
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

The position
(x,y)
has no option (therefore its Grundy number is
0
), if and only if
x\leq 1
and
y\leq 1
. When
(x,y)
has some option with its Grundy number
0
, then there is an option
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
with the Grundy number
1
, so in the misère play, the
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions are
(x,y)
with
{\mathrm{Min}}(x,y)=0
and
{\mathrm{Max}}(x,y)\geq 2
.

Proof.

When
g(x,y)=0
with non-empty options (therefore
{\mathrm{Max}}(x,y)\geq 2
), say
x\leq y
with
y-x\leq 1
and
y\geq 2
, then the player has the option
(x+1,0)
, which has Grundy number
1
. We can apply Remark
2
. ∎

The
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions of Triangular Nim is exactly same as Yama Nim (hence the same outcome), but Grundy numbers behave in a more interesting way. In particular, the winning strategy for the misère play, Triangular Nim is different from the misère play of Yama Nim. This ruleset was born when the second author talked with Professor Urban Larsson, Professor Indrajit Saha, Professor Koki Suetsugu, Professor Tomoaki Abuku, and Professor Hironori Kiya about Yama Nim on the occasion of a conference at Kyushu University.

Definition 4
.

Let
X={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
be the set of positions of Triangular Nim. The options are given by

f((x,y))=\{(x-i,y+j)\,|\,2\leq i\leq x,1\leq j<i\}\cup\{(x+j,y-i)\,|\,2\leq i\leq y,1\leq j<i\}.

.

The Grundy number for the normal play of Triangular Nim is given as follows:

g((x,y))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&(|x-y|\leq 1)\\

x+y-1&\hbox{($|x-y|=:d>1$ and $x+y\leq d^{2}+1$)}\end{array}\right..

Definition 5
.

For each
g\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, take
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
as the natural number which satisfies the condition

\dfrac{d(d-1)}{2}\leq g<\dfrac{d(d+1)}{2},

namely the
d
-th triangular number
d(d+1)/2
is the first triangular number larger than
g
. Take the arithmetic progression
a_{g,k}=1+g+kd
, whose first term
a_{g,0}=1+g
with the common difference
d
. For example for
g=0
, the arithmetic progression is

\{a_{0,0},a_{0,1},a_{0,2},a_{0,3},\ldots\}=\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\},

and for
g=1
,

\{a_{1,0},a_{1,1},a_{1,2}a_{1,3},\ldots\}=\{2,4,6,8,\ldots\},

and for
g=2
, we have

\{a_{2,0},a_{2,1},a_{2,2},a_{2,3},\ldots\}=\{3,5,7,9,\ldots\},

and for
g=3
, we have

\{a_{3,0},a_{3,1},a_{3,2},a_{3,3},\ldots\}=\{4,7,10,13,\ldots\}.

Theorem 3
.

In Triangular Nim, if
x=y
, the Grundy number for
(x,y)
is
0
. When
(x,y)
is a position with
x<y
,
the Grundy number
g(x,y)
for
(x,y)
is given by

g(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}g&\hbox{(when $(x,y)=(a_{g,k},a_{g,k+1})$ for some $g\geq 0$ and $k\geq 0$)}\\
x+y-1&\hbox{(otherwise)}\end{array}\right..

For example, for
(x,y)
with
x<y
, we have

\displaystyle g(x,y)=0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(x,y)=(0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,4),(4,5),\ldots,

\displaystyle g(x,y)=1

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(x,y)=(0,2),(2,4),(4,6),(6,8),(8,10),\ldots,

\displaystyle g(x,y)=2

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(x,y)=(0,3),(3,5),(5,7),(7,9),(9,11),\ldots,

\displaystyle g(x,y)=3

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(x,y)=(1,3),(0,4),(4,7),(7,10),(10,13),(13,16),\ldots,

\displaystyle g(x,y)=4

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(x,y)=(1,4),(0,5),(5,8),(8,11),(11,14),(14,17),\ldots.

Notice that except for the first few exceptions (which corresponds to the “otherwise” cases), the arithmetic progressions appear to describe the pattern.

Proof.

The equivalence of Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
is easy to check and left to the reader.

We define

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\,|\,|x-y|\leq 1\}

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(0,2),(2,4),(4,6),(6,8),\ldots\}\cup\{\ldots,(8,6),(6,4),(4,2),(2,0)\}

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(0,3),(3,0)\}\cup\{(3,5),(5,7),(7,9),(9,11),\ldots\}\cup\{\ldots,(11,9),(9,7),(7,5),(5,3)\}

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(1,3),(0,4)\}\cup\{(4,7),(7,10),(10,13),(13,16),\ldots\}\cup\{\ldots,(11,9),(9,7),(7,5),(5,3)\}

and for general
k>0
, we define
S_{g}
to be the union of the three sets
T_{g}\cup U_{g}\cup V_{g}
where

\displaystyle T_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\,|\,\hbox{$x+y=g+1$ and $(x+y)\leq(x-y)^{2}+1$}\}

\displaystyle U_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(1+g,1+g+d),(1+g+d,1+g+2d),(1+g+2d,1+g+3d),\ldots\}

\displaystyle V_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\ldots,(1+g+3d,1+g+2d),(1+g+2d,1+g+d),(1+g+d,1+g)\}.

We will check the conditions for Proposition
1
. First, we show that when
(x,y)\in S_{0}
with its option
(x,y)\to(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, then
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\not\in S_{0}
. This is similar to the Yama-Nim case. We have
|x-y|\leq 1
, and we may assume that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(x+iy-j)
where
i\geq 2
and
i>j\geq 1
. Then

|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|=|(x-y)+(i+j)|\geq|i+j|-|x-y|\geq 3-1=2

hence
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\not\in S_{0}
. When
(x,y)\in S_{g}
, there are two cases, either
(x,y)\in T_{g}
or
(x,y)\in U_{g}\cup V_{g}
. When
(x,y)\in T_{g}
, then we have
x\leq g+1
and
y\leq g+1
. When
(x,y)\in U_{g}\cup V_{g}
, we have
x\geq g+1
and
y\geq g+1
. When
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is an option of
(x,y)
, then one component strictly increases, and the other decreases, so it is impossible to move between

and
U_{g}\cup V_{g}
. If both
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are in

, but we have
x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}=g+1=x+y
, so it is impossible. Finally we consider the case
(x,y)\in U_{g}\cup V_{g}
. By symmetry, we may assume that
x<y
. Let us write
a_{k}=1+g+kd,(k\in\{0,1,2,3,\ldots)
with
d(d-1)/2\leq g<d(d+1)/2
, then we can write
x=a_{k}
and

for some
k
. As
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in U_{g}\cup V_{g}
, we need to have
x^{\prime}=a_{\ell}
and
y^{\prime}=a_{m}
for some
\ell

, with

. If
x^{\prime}<x
and

, then we have

(x,y)\in S_{g}

.

Now assume that
(x,y)\in S_{g}

x<y

(x,y)\not\in S_{0}

x\leq h

(h+1,0)\in T_{h}\subset S_{h}

(x,y)\in U_{g}

3
ab
-Yama Nim and
ab
-Triangular Nim

In Yama Nim, the player takes at least
2
tokens from one heap, and return exactly
1
token to the other. In Triangular Nim, the player takes at least
2
tokens from one heap, and return at least
1
token to the other so that the number of the tokens in the heaps decreases. By changing
2
to
a
and
1
to
b
, we get generalizations of these Nim games as follows:

Definition 6
.

Let
a
be a positive integer and
b
an integer with
a>b
. Let
X={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
be the set of positions of
ab
-Yama Nim and
ab
-Triangular Nim. The options for the
ab
-Yama Nim are given by

f((x,y))=\{(x-i,y+b)\,|\,a\leq i\leq x\}\cup\{(x+b,y-i)\,|\,a\leq i\leq y\}.

Also the options for the
ab
-Triangular Nim are given by

f((x,y))=\{(x-i,y+j)\,|\,a\leq i\leq x,b\leq j<i\}\cup\{(x+b,y-i)\,|\,a\leq i\leq y,b\leq j<i\}.

To describe their
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions, we need the following notation.

Definition 7
.

Let
a
be a positive integer and
b
an integer with
a>b
. For a non-negative integer
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, we define
R_{k}(a,b)
to be

R_{k}(a,b)=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}\,|\,k(a-b)\leq x,y<k(a-b)+a\}.

When confusion is unlikely, we simply write
R_{k}
for
R_{k}(a,b)
.

Theorem 4
.

The set of
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions of
ab
-Triangular Nim is
\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(a,b)
. When
b\geq 0
, the set of
{\mathcal{P}}
-positions of
ab
-Yama Nim is the same:
\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(a,b)
.

Proof.

Take a position
(x,y)\not\in\bigcup R_{k}
with
x\leq y
. We would like to show that
(x,y)
has some option
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
in some
R_{k}
. First, we assume that
b\geq 0
, and let
k=\left[\dfrac{x}{a-b}\right]
, then as
k(a-b)\leq x<(k+1)(a-b)=k(a-b)+a-b\leq k(a-b)+a
, in order that
(x,y)\not\in R_{k}
, we need
k(a-b)+a\leq y
. Then
(x,y)
can be sent to
(x+b,k(a-b))
both in
ab
-Yama Nim and
ab
-Triangular Nim, and
(x+b,k(a-b))
is in
R_{k}
because
k(a-b)\leq x\leq x+b<(k+1)(a-b)+b=k(a-b)+a
.

When
b<0
with
ab
-Triangular Nim, again we let
k=\left[\dfrac{x}{a-b}\right]
. When
x<k(a-b)+a
, then in order that
y\not\in R_{k}
,
y
must satisfy the inequality
k(a-b)+a\leq y
. Then because
b<0
, we can move
(x,y)
to
(x,k(a-b))\in R_{k}
in
ab
-Triangular Nim. On the other hand, if
k(a-b)+a\leq x
, then because
x\leq y
, we have
k(a-b)+a\leq y
, and also by the choice of
k
, we have
x<(k+1)(a-b)=k(a-b)+a-b
, hence
x+b<k(a-b)+a
. We can send
(x,y)
to
(x+b,k(a-b))
, which lies in
R_{k}
.

Now we assume that
(x,y)\in R_{k}
, and we need to show that
(x,y)
cannot be sent to
\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(a,b)
. Assume not, and
(x,y)
is sent to
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R_{\ell}
. As the options of
ab
-Yama Nim is a subset of
ab
-Triangular Nim, it is enough to treat
ab
-Triangular Nim case. By symmetry, we may assume that
x+b\leq x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}\leq y+a
. By our assumption
(x,y)\in R_{k}
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R_{\ell}
, we have

k(a-b)\leq x,y<k(a-b)+a,

and

\ell(a-b)\leq x^{\prime},y^{\prime}<\ell(a-b)+a.

Then we have

k(a-b)+a>y\geq y^{\prime}+a\geq\ell(a-b)+a,

\ell(a-b)+a>x^{\prime}\geq x+b\geq k(a-b)+b.

Combining, we obtain

k(a-b)+a>\ell(a-b)+a>k(a-b)+b,

and subtracting
b
from all the terms, we have

(k+1)(a-b)>(\ell+1)(a-b)>k(a-b),

and as
a-b>0
, dividing by
a-b
, we can conclude
k+1>\ell+1>k
, which is impossible for integers
k
and
\ell
.
∎

In classical Wythoff Nim, in addition to the usual Nim move, namely taking some tokens from
1
heap, the player is allowed to take tokens from
2
heaps, as far as she/he takes the same number of tokens from both heaps. In this section, we consider similar twists for Yama Nim and Triangular Nim.

Definition 8

\displaystyle\{(x+1,y-i)\,|\,2\leq i\leq y,\}

\displaystyle\{(x-i,y-j)\,|\,1\leq i\leq x,1\leq j\leq y,|i-j|\leq c\}

7
Transfinite Nim

Fix some infinite set
Y
, and consider all the ordinal numbers whose cardinality is less than or equal to
|Y|
, and we work in the set of these ordinal numbers, which we will write as
{\mathbb{O}}
. Cantor normal form allows us to write an ordinal
\alpha\in{\mathbb{O}}
as

\alpha=A_{1}\omega^{a_{1}}+A_{2}\omega^{a_{2}}+\cdots+A_{r}\omega^{a_{r}}

uniquely, where
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
is a non-negative integer,
A_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0},(i=1,2,\ldots,r)
are positive integers,
\omega=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
is the smallest infinite ordinal number,
a_{1}>a_{2}>\cdots>a_{r}
are strictly decreasing ordinal numbers
[
Sierpinski58
, Thm 2, page 320]
.

Ordinal numbers can be regarded as positions in Conway’s surreal numbers
[
Conway01
]
, and hence we can freely add them as if
\omega
is a free variable, and this semi-group is embedded in the group of surreal numbers. This sum of ordinal numbers agree with natural sums
[
Sierpinski58
, p363]
defined by Hessenberg. Transfinite Nim games are treated in
[
BCG02
, p330]
. Using this notion of natural sum, we can play transfinite Wythoff Nim, transfinite Yama Nim, and transfinite Triangular Nim.

Definition 12
.

Let
Y={\mathbb{O}}\times{\mathbb{O}}
be the set of positions.
In transfinite Wythoff Nim, the player can move from
(\alpha,\beta)
to either
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta)
with
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
,
(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})
with
\beta^{\prime}<\beta
or to
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
with
\alpha+\beta^{\prime}=\beta+\alpha^{\prime}
.

In transfinite Yama Nim, the player can move from
(\alpha,\beta)
to either
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta+1)
with
\alpha^{\prime}+1<\alpha
or
(\alpha+1,\beta^{\prime})
with
\beta^{\prime}+1<\beta
.

In transfinite Triangular Nim, the player can move from
(\alpha,\beta)
to
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
, either

(i)

\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
and
\beta<\beta^{\prime}
, or

(ii)

\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha
and
\beta>\beta^{\prime}

in such a way that
\alpha+\beta>\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}
.

As
{\mathbb{O}}
is a well-ordered set, any strictly decreasing sequence terminates in finitely many steps, and in all definitions above, the total (in the sense of natural sum) number of tokens in the heaps decreases strictly, so even though they may have infinitely many tokens, the games always end in finitely many steps, and we can decide the winner.
