Activation degree thresholds and expressiveness of polynomial neural networks
Bella Finkel, Jose Israel Rodriguez, Chenxi Wu, Thomas Yahl

Abstract
We study the expressive power of deep polynomial neural networks through the geometry of their neurovariety. We introduce the notion of the activation degree threshold of a network architecture to express when the dimension of the neurovariety achieves its theoretical maximum. We prove the existence of the activation degree threshold for all polynomial neural networks without width-one bottlenecks and demonstrate a universal upper bound that is quadratic in the width of largest size. In doing so, we prove the high activation degree conjecture of Kileel, Trager, and Bruna. Certain structured architectures have exceptional activation degree thresholds, making them especially expressive in the sense of their neurovariety dimension. In this direction, we prove that polynomial neural networks with equi-width architectures are maximally expressive by showing their activation degree threshold is one.



Introduction

Characterizing the functional space of artificial neural networks is a fundamental project in deep learning theory. When the functional space is semialgebraic, its geometric properties offer insight and mathematical intuition into the learning dynamics. Such a characterization is a two-way street: it is foundational to explaining empirical phenomena and offers the possibility of designing innovative architectures based on desirable theoretical and practical properties encoded in the space. Semialgebraic machine learning models form a rich class that encompasses, for example, Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) and polynomial neural networks.

The study of deep polynomial neural networks offers a distinct advantage in theoretical machine learning because the functional spaces of such networks are described by algebraic varieties. In particular, a polynomial neural network of fixed architecture and activation degree gives an algebraic map from the network’s weights to a set of polynomials. The image of this map is the space of functions that can be represented by the network. Its closure is an affine variety known as a neurovariety. The algebro-geometric invariants of a neurovariety, such as its dimension, degree, and singularities, encode information about the network’s training dynamics and expressiveness. For a detailed discussion on neuroalgebraic geometry detailing the relationship between invariants of a neurovariety and certain fundamental aspects of machine learning, see
[
29
]
.

The most basic instantiation of a polynomial neural network occurs when the activation function is linear. Linear networks have received wide attention as simplified models for understanding the learning behavior of deep neural networks
[
22
,
6
]
. Even in the case of linear networks, when the neuromanifold is a determinantal variety, there is interesting geometry to explore
[
43
,
25
,
26
]
. New developments in the general polynomial neural network setting have demonstrated the cross-disciplinary connections between algebraic geometry and machine learning and offered results on the viability of polynomial neural networks for applications
[
24
,
40
]
. Results on the choice of the activation degree and the dimension of the neurovariety have improved our understanding of the optimization process of these neural networks and the ability of shallow and deep neural networks to replicate target functions
[

,

,
5

.

While polynomial neural networks with fixed activation degree are not universal approximators
[
20
]
, they become so when the activation degree is allowed to vary. An alternative measure of a polynomial neural network’s expressiveness is the dimension of its neurovariety, which can be defined as the maximum number of independent vectors in the tangent space at a generic point. From the perspective of algebraic geometry, the dimension of an algebraic variety is perhaps its most basic invariant. Characterizations of the neurovariety associated with a neural network represent a distinct description of a network’s expressiveness from universal approximation theorems. The dimension of the neurovariety is a simple and precise measure of the degrees of freedom of the model that admits exact computation.
In contrast to universal approximation theorems, the dimension of the neurovariety is a measure of the expressiveness of a given polynomial neural network. Thus, studying polynomial network expressiveness through the dimension of the neurovariety complements universal approximation results. For an explicit toy example illustrating neurovariety dimension, see
[
29
, Section A]
.

1
Polynomial neural networks and neurovarieties

1.1
Background

An
L
-layer
feedforward neural network

F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
is a composition of affine-linear maps
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
and non-linear maps
\sigma_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
,

F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)=(f_{L}\circ\sigma_{L-1}\circ f_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{2}\circ\sigma_{1}\circ f_{1})(x).

The
architecture
of the neural network
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the sequence
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dotsc,d_{L})
.
Here, the affine-linear maps are given by

f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}},\quad x\mapsto W_{i}x+b_{i},

where the
weights
of the neural network are the matrices
W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{L}
and the
biases
are the vectors
b_{1},\dotsc,b_{L}
. The parameter set
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
consists of these weight matrices and biases.
The
activation map

\sigma_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
is given coordinate-wise by the
activation function
.

Deep learning consists of approximating a target function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
by a neural network
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
of a chosen architecture
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dotsc,d_{L})
and activation function. That is, deep learning concerns recovering parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for which
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
most closely resembles
F
.
If
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the ambient space of the parameter set, the map
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is associated to the
N
-tuple of parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
by the
parameter map

\Psi_{\mathbf{d},\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\Fun(\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}},\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}),\quad{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mapsto F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

(1)

In this article, we are interested in the setting in which the activation function is polynomial. The most important cases are when the activation function is a pure power, and this leads to the following definition.

Definition 1

(Polynomial neural network)
.

A
polynomial neural network

p_{\textbf{w}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
with fixed
activation degree

r
and architecture
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{L})
is a feedforward neural network of the form

p_{\mathbf{w}}(x)=(W_{L}\circ\sigma_{L-1}\circ W_{L-1}\circ\sigma_{L-2}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{1}\circ W_{1})(x)

(2)

where
W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{i-1}}
and the
activation maps

\sigma_{i}(x):\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
are given by coordinate-wise exponentiation to the
r
-th power,

\sigma_{i}(x):=(x_{1}^{r},\dots,x_{d_{i}}^{r}).

The parameters
\mathbf{w}
are the entries in the matrices
W_{i}
, so that

\mathbf{w}=(W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{L}).

Remark 2
.

In the definition of a polynomial neural network, the biases are taken to be zero. The affine-linear map
f_{i}
in the
i
-th layer is then a linear map that we identify with the matrix
W_{i}
.

An
L
-layer polynomial neural network
p_{\mathbf{w}}
with architecture
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dotsc,d_{L})
and activation degree
r
is represented by a tuple of homogeneous polynomials.
Precisely, the parameter map now takes the matrix tuple
(W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{L})
to a tuple of degree
r^{L-1}
homogeneous polynomials in
d_{0}
variables of length
d_{L}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{0}}\times&\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times d_{L-1}}\to(\text{Sym}_{r^{L-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}))^{d_{L}},\\
\mathbf{w}&\mapsto p_{\mathbf{w}}.\end{split}

(3)

To specify an element in the image of
\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}
, it suffices to identify its vector of coefficients in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\binom{r^{L-1}+d_{0}-1}{d_{0}-1}}\simeq\left(\text{Sym}_{r^{L-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})\right)^{d_{L}}
.

Remark 3

(Special case)
.

Note that if
d_{L}=1
, the codomain of the parameter map (
3
) is the space of degree
r^{L-1}
homogeneous polynomials.

The
neuromanifold
or
functional space
associated to
p_{\mathbf{w}}
is the family of all polynomial neural networks with fixed activation degree
r
and architecture
\mathbf{d}

where the weights are allowed to vary. The image of
\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}
is the
neuromanifold

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{d},r}
, which is a semialgebraic set in
(\text{Sym}_{r^{L-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}))^{d_{L}}
. (In spite of the terminology, the neuromanifold is not in general a smooth manifold, and its singularities can affect the behavior of the corresponding model, see
[
4
,
29
,
40
]
.) The
neurovariety

\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
is the Zariski closure of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{d},r}
, and is a real affine variety in
(\text{Sym}_{r^{L-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}))^{d_{L}}
.

The dimension of the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
provides a measure of the expressiveness of a neural network because it quantifies the degrees of freedom of the space of functions the network produces
[
24
]
. In practice, this is the dimension of the neuromanifold
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{d},r}
; as
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{d},r}
is semialgebraic, its dimension is the same as that of
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
.

The neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
is a real variety parameterized by the polynomial map

\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}

where
N=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}d_{i}d_{i+1}
and
M=d_{L}\binom{r^{L-1}+d_{0}-1}{d_{0}-1}
.
Extending the domain of
\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}
to
\mathbb{C}^{N}

results in the map

\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}:\mathbb{C}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{M}.

(4)

Denote the Zariski closure of the image of
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}
by
Y
.
By
[
41
, Theorem 12.2]
,
the dimension of the complex variety
Y
and the dimension of the real variety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
agree if
Y
contains a smooth real point.
Since
Y
is parameterized by polynomials with real coefficients, it follows that
Y
contains a smooth real point and

\operatorname{dim}{Y}=\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}.

(5)

Equation (
5
) puts complex algebraic geometry at the disposal of statements concerning the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
. We exploit this in
Theorems

18
and
22
.
Our approach relies on
understanding the dimension of the
fiber
of
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}
over

y\in\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(\mathbb{C}^{N})
:

\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}^{-1}(y):=\{w\in\mathbb{C}^{N}:\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(w)=y\}.

For all network architectures there exists a symmetry in the weight matrices, known as
multi-homogeneity
.

Lemma 4

(
[
24
]
, Multi-homogeneity)
.

For all invertible diagonal matrices
D_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{i}}
and permutation matrices
P_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{i}\times d_{i}}
(
i=1,\dots,L-1
), the parameter map
\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}
returns the same neural network under the replacement

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow P_{1}D_{1}W_{1}

\displaystyle W_{2}

\displaystyle\leftarrow P_{2}D_{2}W_{2}D_{1}^{-r}P_{1}^{T}

\displaystyle W_{L}

\displaystyle\leftarrow W_{L}D_{L-1}^{-r}P_{L-1}^{T},

where
T
denotes the matrix transpose.

By letting the diagonal matrices
D_{i}
have complex entries,
the multi-homogeneity lemma has known consequences for the fibers of
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}
.
Namely, the fiber of

\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}

over
y=\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(W_{1},\dots,W_{L})
satisfies this containment:

\begin{split}\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}^{-1}(y)\supseteq\Big\{(P_{1}D_{1}W_{1},\,P_{2}D_{2}W_{2}D_{1}^{-r}P_{1}^{T},\,\dots,\,W_{L}D_{L-1}^{-r}P_{L-1}^{T}):\,\phantom{xxxxxxxxxxxx}&\\
P_{i}\text{ is a permutation matrix and }D_{i}\text{ is an invertible diagonal matrix}\Big\},\end{split}

(6)

and the dimension of a general fiber is at least
\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}d_{i}

[
24
, Lemma 13]
. For clarity, we remark that when
L=1
, the right-hand side of (
6
) simplifies to
\{W_{1}\}
, and
the containment becomes an equality.
We are careful to state when a polynomial map is over a real or complex domain to avoid ambiguity when discussing the dimension of preimages that are not general.

Considering the difference of the size of the weight matrices and the dimension of the space of multi-homogeneities leads to the notion of the expected dimension of a neurovariety.

Definition 5

(Expected dimension)
.

The
expected dimension
of the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}:=\min

\displaystyle\Bigg\{d_{L}+\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}(d_{i}d_{i+1}-d_{i+1}),\quad d_{L}\binom{r^{L-1}+d_{0}-1}{d_{0}-1}\Bigg\}.

(7)

This definition was introduced in
[
27
]
and in
[
24
]

it was shown that
\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}
is an upper bound on the dimension of the neurovariety.

Example 6
.

In the case when
L=1
, the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
has the expected dimension
d_{0}d_{1}
, as the map
\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}
is an isomorphism.
The function space is parameterized by the
d_{1}\times d_{0}
matrix
W_{1}
.

The expected dimension is not always equal to the dimension, as the following example demonstrates.

Example 7
.

The dimension of a neurovariety is subtle even in simple settings.
For instance, when the architecture is
(d_{0},d_{1},1)
, a reinterpretation
[
24
, Theorem 9]
of the
Alexander–Hirschowitz Theorem
[
2
]
determines which neurovarieties have the expected dimension.
In
[
27
]
,
the authors coin the term
defect
for the difference between the expected dimension of a neurovariety and its dimension:

\defect(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}):=\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}-\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}.

Examples of neurovarieties
with positive defect are found in

[
27
, Table 1]
and in

[
24
]
as well.

Remark 8
.

The notion of neurovariety dimension as a measure of network expressiveness has been utilized for designing neural networks. For example,
[
21
]
employs a hybrid network with monomial and LeakyReLU activation functions to determine the entanglement type of quantum states. Because their architecture is shallow, they apply the Alexander–Hirschowitz Theorem when choosing the number of neurons in each layer to guarantee that the associated neurovariety achieves the expected dimension.
Such applications motivate the project of describing the neurovariety dimension for broader classes of architectures. Our
Theorem

18
and
Theorem

22
are examples of this kind of result.

1.2
Main result and example

One of our main results proves a lower bound on the dimension of a neurovariety for sufficiently high activation degree.
We introduce the notion of the
activation degree threshold
, whose existence will follow from our
Theorem

18
.

Problem 9
.

Given an architecture
\mathbf{d}
,
does there exist a nonnegative integer
\tilde{r}

such that the following holds:

\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}\quad\text{for all}\quad r>\tilde{r}\,?

(8)

When such a nonnegative integer
\tilde{r}
exists for (
8
) we have the following definition.

Definition 10
.

The
activation degree threshold
of an architecture
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{L})
with
L>1

is

\ActThr(\mathbf{d}):=\min\{\tilde{r}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}:\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}\text{ for all }r>\tilde{r}\}.

In other words,

\ActThr(\mathbf{d})
is
the smallest nonnegative integer
\tilde{r}
such that (
8
) holds.

With this terminology in hand, we state our main result, which shows that the activation degree threshold is well-defined when
\mathbf{d}
has no entries equal to one.

Theorem
18
.

For fixed
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dotsc,d_{L})
satisfying
d_{i}>1
(
i=0,\dotsc,L-1
),
the activation degree threshold
\ActThr(\mathbf{d})
exists.
In other words, there exists an integer
\tilde{r}(\mathbf{d})
such that whenever
r>\tilde{r}(\mathbf{d})
, the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
of the polynomial neural network has the expected dimension,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=d_{L}+\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}(d_{i}-1)d_{i+1}.

Moreover,
for
L>1
,
we have

\ActThr(\mathbf{d})\leq 6{m}^{2}-6m+1,\quad m:=-1+2\max\{d_{1},\dots,d_{L-1}\}.

(9)

Example 11
.

This example aims to illustrate the activation degree threshold by mapping the function space of neural networks with architecture
\mathbf{d}=(3,2,1)
and activation function
x^{r}
for

. To ensure these functions are well-defined, we restrict
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
to the nonnegative orthant and assume that each weight matrix has nonnegative entries.

Denote the corresponding parameter space as
\Theta^{+}
and
the neuromanifold as
\Fun^{+}_{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0},\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right)
.
The neuromanifold

\Fun^{+}_{r}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0},\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right)

has dimension at most six for all

because any element in it is of the form

(ax_{1}+bx_{2}+cx_{3})^{r}+(a^{\prime}x_{1}+b^{\prime}x_{2}+c^{\prime}x_{3})^{r}.

Note that each point
\mathbf{z}
in

induces an evaluation map

Therefore, consider six points

and define the function

Precomposing with the parameter map (

24
, Theorem 14]
.

2.1
A prior number theoretic result by Newman–Slater

In this section, we recover a number-theoretic result stated by
Newman–Slater in
[
33
]
.
The proof here slightly improves their bound (which is
n\leq 8k^{2}
in our notation).

Theorem 13
.

Let
n>1
and

R_{1}^{n}(x)+R_{2}^{n}(x)+\cdots+R_{k}^{n}(x)=1

where

R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{k}
are
non-constant rational functions
in the variable
x
.
Then
n\leq 6{k}^{2}-6k+1
.

Proof.

A proof of this statement follows from modifications of the proof of
[
33
, Section 4, Theorem]
. We include it here for the benefit of the reader.

Consider the
k\times k
Wronskian matrix
W:=W(R_{1}^{n},\dots,R_{k}^{n})
where the
(i,j)
-th entry is
the
(i-1)
-st derivative of
R_{j}^{n}
, which we denote by
\derivD^{i-1}R_{j}^{n}
.
With this setup, we have the following vector equation, with the first standard basis vector on the right-hand side and
\mathbf{1}
denoting the vector of ones:

W\cdot\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{e}_{1}.

By letting
D
be the
k\times k
diagonal matrix with
(j,j)
entry

1/{R_{j}^{n+1-k}}
we have

\left(W\cdot D\right)\left(D^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)=\mathbf{e}_{1}

where

W\cdot D=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{1}{R_{1}^{1-k}}&\dots&\dfrac{1}{R_{k}^{1-k}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\dfrac{\derivD^{k-1}R_{1}^{n}}{R_{1}^{n+1-k}}&\dots&\dfrac{\derivD^{k-1}R_{k}^{n}}{R_{k}^{n+1-k}}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

The benefit of this formulation is that cancellation of common factors in numerators and denominators occurs with the multiplication
W\cdot D
.

If the rational functions
R_{1}^{n},\dotsc,R_{k}^{n}
are linearly independent, then
\det(W)\neq 0
and hence
\det(W\cdot D)\neq 0
.
Using Cramer’s rule, we solve for the first entry of

\left(D^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{1}\right)
:

R_{1}^{n+1-k}=\det(B)/\det(W\cdot D),

(13)

where
B
is obtained by replacing the first column of
W\cdot D
with
\mathbf{e}_{1}
.

Assume each
R_{j}
is written in lowest terms. After reordering the
R_{j}
’s as necessary, suppose
R_{1}
has the largest degree numerator or denominator and denote this degree by
A
. Hence, the sum of the degrees of the numerator and denominator on the left-hand side of (
13
) is between

A\cdot|n-k+1|
and
2A\cdot|n-k+1|
.

Now note that if
f
,
g
are polynomials, then

(f/g^{r})^{\prime}=(f^{\prime}g^{r}-frg^{\prime}g^{r-1})/g^{2r}=(f^{\prime}g-frg^{\prime})/g^{r+1}.

In other words, the sum of the degrees of the numerator and denominator, when taking the derivative once, increases by no more than
2\deg(g)-1
.
As a consequence,
the
(i,j)
-th entry of the matrix
W\cdot D
when written in lowest terms is a rational function for which the sum of the degrees of the numerator and denominator is no more than
2A(k-1)+(2A-1)(i-1)
.
By the Leibniz formula for the determinant, the sum of the degrees of the numerator and denominator for
\det(B)
is no more than

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{k}\big(2A\cdot(k-1)+(2A-1)(i-1)\big)=

\displaystyle 2A\cdot(k-1)^{2}\,+\,(2A-1)k(k-1)/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\cdot(k-1)(3k-2)\,-\,k(k-1)/2

while the sum of the degrees of the numerator and denominator for
\det(W\cdot D)
is no more than

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(2A\cdot(k-1)+(2A-1)(i-1)\big)=

\displaystyle 2A\cdot k(k-1)+(2A-1)k(k-1)/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\cdot(k-1)(3k)-k(k-1)/2.\

Using (
13
),
we have

\displaystyle A\cdot(n-k{+1})

\displaystyle\hskip-36.135pt\leq A\cdot|n-k+1|

\displaystyle\hskip-36.135pt\leq\Big(A\cdot(k-1)(3k-2)-k(k-1)/2\Big)+\Big(A\cdot(k-1)(3k)-k(k-1)/2\Big).

This implies the inequality

\displaystyle n\leq\left(6-\frac{1}{A}\right)k^{2}+\left(-7+\frac{1}{A}\right)k+1.

As
A\geq 1
, we arrive at

n\leq 6{k}^{2}-6k+1.

(14)

We have shown the inequality (
14
)
holds when
R_{1}^{n},\dots,R_{k}^{n}
are linearly independent. If these functions are linearly dependent, then we may find a subset
S
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
with cardinality
\ell
less than
k
such that

\sum_{j\in S}R_{j}^{n}=1
(after potentially rescaling the
R_{j}^{n}
)
and
\{R_{j}^{n}:j\in S\}
is linearly independent.
Applying the Wronskian argument in this setting proves

n\leq 6{\ell}^{2}-6\ell+1.

Since
1<\ell<k
implies

6{\ell}^{2}-6\ell+1<6{k}^{2}-6k+1
, the theorem holds.
∎

Corollary 14
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
and
R
be polynomials in the variable
x
such that
R
is not the zero polynomial and no
P_{i}
is proportional to
R
.
If
P_{1}^{n}+P_{2}^{n}+\cdots+P_{k}^{n}=R^{n}
, then

n\leq 6{k}^{2}-6k+1
.

Proof.

Since
R
is not the zero polynomial,
the equation
P_{1}^{n}+P_{2}^{n}+\cdots+P_{k}^{n}=R^{n}

holds if and only if

\left(\frac{P_{1}}{R}\right)^{n}+\left(\frac{P_{2}}{R}\right)^{n}+\cdots+\left(\frac{P_{k}}{R}\right)^{n}=1.

The result follows from
Theorem

13
since each
P_{i}/R
is a non-constant rational function.
∎

2.2
Powers of non-proportional multivariate polynomials

The statements in the previous subsection are for univariate polynomial functions. In this subsection, we extend those results to the multivariate polynomial setting to apply them to polynomial neural networks in
Section

2.3
.

To that end, first we generalize
Corollary

14

to multivariate polynomials.

Lemma 15
.

Let
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{k}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d}]
denote multivariate polynomials that are pairwise non-proportional (for any
i\not=j
, there is no
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
such that
p_{i}=\alpha p_{j}
).
If

\displaystyle p_{1}^{r}+\dotsb+p_{k}^{r}=0,

then

r\leq 6{(k-1)}^{2}-6(k-1)+1
.

Proof.

Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
be pairwise non-proportional polynomials on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

p_{1}^{r}+\cdots+p_{k}^{r}=0

holds identically.
Consider the line
\ell(t)=tx+(1-t)y
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
that passes through the points
x
and
y
and is parameterized by
t
.
Restricting each
p_{i}
to this line yields the univariate polynomials

p_{1}(\ell(t)),\dots,p_{k}(\ell(t))
in
t
.
Then

\displaystyle\big(p_{1}(\ell(t))\big)^{r}+\dotsb+\big(p_{k}(\ell(t))\big)^{r}=0

holds identically in
t
.

If
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
are general points,
one has

\displaystyle p_{i}(x)/p_{j}(x)\,\neq\,p_{i}(y)/p_{j}(y),\quad{i\neq j},

which implies

\displaystyle p_{i}(\ell(1))/p_{j}(\ell(1))\,\neq\,p_{i}(\ell(0))/p_{j}(\ell(0)),\quad i\neq j.

Therefore the univariate polynomials

p_{1}(\ell(t)),\dots,p_{k}(\ell(t))
are pairwise non-proportional, as any quotient of a pair is non-constant.
By
Corollary

14
,
we conclude that

r\leq 6{(k-1)}^{2}-6(k-1)+1.

∎

Next we demonstrate that given pairwise non-proportional multivariate polynomials
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{k}
, the polynomials
p_{1}^{r},\dotsc,p_{k}^{r}
are linearly independent for all sufficiently large
r
.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a subfield of
\mathbb{C}
.
Given integers
d,k
, there exists an integer
\tilde{r}=\tilde{r}(k)
with the following property.
If
r>\tilde{r}(k)
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]
are pairwise non-proportional, then
p_{1}^{r},\dots,p_{k}^{r}
are linearly independent (over
\mathbb{K}
).
Moreover,

\tilde{r}(k)=6{(k-1)}^{2}-6(k-1)+1
has the desired property.

Proof.

We remark that it suffices to consider the case that
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
. Indeed, if
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
are not linearly dependent over
\mathbb{K}
, then they are not linearly dependent over
\mathbb{C}
. For if there were a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
such that
p_{i}-\alpha p_{j}=0
, then one finds that
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}
by considering the coefficients of this difference. Further, linear independence of
\{p_{1}^{r},\dotsc,p_{k}^{r}\}
over
\mathbb{C}
implies linear independence over any subfield.

We prove the contrapositive statement when
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
. Given integers
d,k
, let

\tilde{r}(k)=6{(k-1)}^{2}-6(k-1)+1
and
r>\tilde{r}(k)
be an integer. Fix pairwise non-proportional polynomials
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{k}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d}]
with the property that the set
\{p_{1}^{r},\dotsc,p_{k}^{r}\}
is linearly dependent over
\mathbb{C}
. Then there exist
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}p_{1}^{r}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}p_{k}^{r}=0.

Let
\beta_{i}
be an
r
th root of
\alpha_{i}
, so that

\displaystyle(\beta_{1}p_{1})^{r}+\cdots+(\beta_{k}p_{k})^{r}=0.

By Lemma
15
, it follows that for some
i\neq j
,
\beta_{i}p_{i}
and
\beta_{j}p_{j}
are proportional. Thus, some
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
are proportional.
∎

Corollary 17

(Conjecture 16,
[
24
]
)
.

There exists
\tilde{r}:\mathbb{N}^{2}_{>0}\to\mathbb{Z}
,
(d,k)\mapsto\tilde{r}(d,k)
with the following property. Whenever
p_{1},...,p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},...,x_{d}]
are
k
homogeneous polynomials of the same degree in
d
variables, no two of which are linearly dependent,
p^{r}_{1},\dots,p^{r}_{k}
are linearly independent if
r>\tilde{r}({d,k})
.

Proof.

Note that
p_{i},p_{j}
being linearly independent implies
p_{i},p_{j}
are non-proportional.
By
Proposition

16
it follows that

\tilde{r}(d,k)=6{(k-1)}^{2}-6(k-1)+1

suffices.
∎

2.3
Deep networks and high activation degree

Next, we show when activation degree thresholds exist for polynomial neural networks. The proof of the following theorem follows the arguments for
[
24
, Theorem 14]
, except where we leverage our
Proposition

16
.

Theorem 18
.

For fixed
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dotsc,d_{L})
satisfying
d_{i}>1
(
i=0,\dotsc,L-1
),
the activation degree threshold
\ActThr(\mathbf{d})
exists.
That is, there exists an integer
\tilde{r}(\mathbf{d})
such that whenever
r>\tilde{r}(\mathbf{d})
, the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
of the polynomial neural network has the expected dimension,

\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=d_{L}+\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}(d_{i}d_{i+1}-d_{i+1})

(15)

Moreover,
for
L>1
,
we have

\ActThr(\mathbf{d})\leq 6{m}^{2}-6m+1,\quad m:=-1+2\max\{d_{1},\dots,d_{L-1}\}.

(16)

Proof.

Fix
r>\tilde{r}(\mathbf{d})=6{m}^{2}-6m+1
.

Since the expected dimension of the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
is the minimum of the two numbers in (
7
), it suffices to prove
\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=d_{L}+\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}(d_{i}d_{i+1}-d_{i+1})
for
r>\tilde{r}(\mathbf{d})
.
We show this
by inducting on the number of layers
L

to prove a general fiber of

\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}
in (
4
)
is equal to the set of multi-homogeneities of any point of the fiber. That is, we show the containment of sets in (
6
) is an equality.

Recall
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}
from (
4
).
When
L=1
, the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
has the expected dimension as the map

\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}

is an isomorphism
and
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}^{-1}(\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(W_{1}))=\{W_{1}\}
.

Now assume
L>1

and suppose that a general fiber for depth
L-1
is completely described by the multi-homogeneity in (
6
).
Fix general weights
(W_{1},\dots,W_{L})\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{0}d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{C}^{d_{L-1}d_{L}}

and consider given weights
(\tilde{W}_{1},\dots,\tilde{W}_{L})\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{0}d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{C}^{d_{L-1}d_{L}}

such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(W_{L}\sigma_{L-1}W_{L-1}\dots\sigma_{1}W_{1})(x)\\
&=(\tilde{W}_{L}\sigma_{L-1}\tilde{W}_{L-1}\dots\sigma_{1}\tilde{W}_{1})(x).\end{split}

(17)

Denote the output of

(W_{L-1}\sigma_{L-2}W_{L-2}\dotsb W_{2}\sigma_{1}W_{1})(x)

by

\left[Q_{({1})}(x),\dots,Q_{({d_{L-1}})}(x)\right]^{T}

and the output of

(\tilde{W}_{L-1}\sigma_{L-2}\tilde{W}_{L-2}\dotsb\tilde{W}_{2}\sigma_{1}\tilde{W}_{1})(x)

by

\left[\tilde{Q}_{({1})}(x),\dots,\tilde{Q}_{({d_{L-1}})}(x)\right]^{T}
.
Since the weights
W_{i}
are general and
d_{i}>1
, the homogeneous polynomials

Q_{({i})}

are pairwise non-proportional
(there is a non-empty Zariski open set of the space of weight matrices where the
polynomials

Q_{({1})},\dots,Q_{({d_{L-1}})}

are pairwise non-proportional).

By examining the first coordinate of the outputs of (
17
) we obtain the decomposition

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{a_{({1})}Q_{({1})}^{r}+a_{({2})}Q_{({2})}^{r}+\dots+a_{({d_{L-1}})}Q_{({d_{L-1}})}^{r}}\\
&={\tilde{a}_{({1})}\tilde{Q}_{({1})}^{r}+\tilde{a}_{({2})}\tilde{Q}_{({2})}^{r}+\dots+\tilde{a}_{({d_{L-1}})}\tilde{Q}_{({d_{L-1}})}^{r}}\end{split}

(18)

where

a_{({j})}
and
\tilde{a}_{({j})}
denote the
(1,j)
entry of the weight matrices
W_{L}
and
\tilde{W}_{L}
respectively.
Because we have taken
r>\tilde{r}(\mathbf{d})
,
Proposition

16
guarantees that (
18
) has two proportional summands.
As
Q_{({i})}

are pairwise non-proportional,
no two summands on the left-hand side may be proportional.
The equality implies that neither may two summands on the right-hand side be proportional.

By permuting terms as necessary, we may assume these proportional terms
occur as the first term on both sides.
We may then rescale so that

Q_{({1})}=\tilde{Q}_{({1})}

and subtract
\tilde{a}_{({1})}\tilde{Q}_{({1})}^{r}

to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(a_{({1})}-\tilde{a}_{({1})}\right)Q_{({1})}^{r}+a_{({2})}Q_{({2})}^{r}+\dots+a_{({d_{L-1}})}Q_{({d_{L-1}})}^{r}\\
=\tilde{a}_{({2})}\tilde{Q}_{({2})}^{r}+\dots+\tilde{a}_{({d_{L-1}})}\tilde{Q}_{({d_{L-1}})}^{r}\end{split}

(19)

We repeatedly apply
Proposition

16
to iteratively reduce the number of terms on the right side, until the right side of (
19
) is zero. Then, the pairwise linear independence of the
Q_{({i})}

and
Proposition

16
imply that each coefficient is also zero. Hence, up to scaling and permutation,

\left[Q_{({1})},\dots,Q_{({d_{L-1}})}\right]^{T}=\left[\tilde{Q}_{({1})},\dots,\tilde{Q}_{({d_{L-1}})}\right]^{T}

and the entries of
W_{L}
and
\tilde{W}_{L}
agree in the first row. This process is repeated for each row of
W_{L}
and
\tilde{W}_{L}
to show that they are equal (up to scaling and permutation).
Therefore, by the inductive hypothesis the fiber
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}^{-1}(\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{L}))

is completely described by multi-homogeneity.
As a consequence, the fiber
has dimension
(d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{L-2})+d_{L-1}.

By the
Fiber-Dimension Theorem
[
39
, Theorem 1.25, Part (ii)]
and Equation (
5
),
the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
satisfies

d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{L-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}d_{i}d_{i+1}-\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}},

and the result follows.
∎

(Conjecture 5.2,
[
27
]
)
.

For any fixed widths
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},\dots,d_{L})

with

In the proof of

3
Equi-width setting

The advantage of depth for neural networks is well-established through both empirical observations and theoretical results
[
31
,
42
,
30
,
37
]
. Width also plays a significant role in network expressiveness and improves the behavior of the loss surface by smoothing the optimization landscape of the loss function
[
19
,
34
,
35
]
. The study of networks with bounded width appears in the context of universal approximation theorems
[
18
,
23
,
28
]
as well.

For sufficiently high activation degree, a neurovariety has the expected dimension by
Theorem

18
. For specific architectures, better bounds on the activation degree threshold may be computed.
In this section, we focus on determining the expressiveness of polynomial neural networks with
equi-width
architecture, meaning the layers have equal
widths, that is,

d_{0}=d_{1}=\dots=d_{L}

in Definition
1
.

We begin with a proposition which will be used in the main result of this section.

Proposition 21
.

Let

{\sigma_{i}}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}

be the map given by coordinate-wise exponentiation to the
r
-th power.
Suppose
W_{1},\dots,W_{L}
are in
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
.
The map

p_{\mathbf{w}}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
given by

p_{\mathbf{w}}(x)=(W_{L}{\sigma_{L-1}}W_{L-1}\dotsb W_{2}{\sigma_{1}}W_{1})(x)

has only the trivial zero if and only if all
W_{i}
are invertible.

Proof.

If each
W_{i}
is invertible, then the preimage of the zero vector under each
W_{i}
and
{\sigma_{i}}
consists of only the zero vector.
Thus, the map

p_{\mathbf{w}}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}

has only the trivial zero.

Conversely, if some
W_{i}
is singular, then let
i_{0}
be the minimal index such that
W_{i_{0}}

is singular and let
v\in\ker W_{i_{0}}
be a non-zero vector.
For each
j<i_{0}
, the linear map
W_{j}
is surjective, and each

{\sigma_{j}}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}

is surjective as well.
Thus, there exists non-zero

x_{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle({\sigma_{i_{0}-1}}W_{i_{0}-1}\dotsb W_{2}{\sigma_{1}}W_{1})(x_{\ast})=v.

As
v\in\ker W_{i_{0}}
, it follows that
p_{\mathbf{w}}(x_{\ast})=(W_{L}{\sigma_{L-1}}W_{L-1}\dotsb W_{2}{\sigma_{1}}W_{1})(x_{\ast})=0
and
x_{\ast}
is a non-trivial zero.
∎

We now provide the main result of this section: the activation degree threshold of an equi-width architecture is one.

Theorem 22
.

If
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dotsc,d_{L})
is an equi-width architecture with
d=d_{0}=d_{1}\dotsb=d_{L}
,
L>1
, and
d>1
, then the activation degree threshold is
\ActThr(\mathbf{d})=1
. That is, if
\mathbf{d}
is equi-width with
L>1
and
d>1
, then for all
r>1
the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
has the expected dimension

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}=Ld^{2}-(L-1)d.

Proof.

If
r=1
, then
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},1}
is the space of linear functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore
\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},1}}=d^{2}
.
Since

\displaystyle\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},1}}=Ld^{2}-(L-1)d,

we have
\defect(\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},1})=(L-1)(d^{2}-d)
. Therefore for all
L>1
,
\ActThr(\mathbf{d})>0
.

Recall that the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
is the Zariski closure of the image of the polynomial map

\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}

where
N=Ld^{2}
and
M={d\binom{r^{L-1}+d-1}{d-1}}
.
Extending the domain of
\Psi_{\mathbf{d},r}
to
\mathbb{C}^{N}

results in the map (
4
)
with the Zariski closure of the image denoted by
Y
.
By Equation (
5
),
to complete the proof,
it suffices to show
\operatorname{dim}{Y}=N-(L-1)d
, which we will do using the Fiber-Dimension Theorem
[
39
, Theorem 1.25]
for (
24
).

For notational convenience, we let

S_{L,d,r}
denote the set of parameters that are mapped to
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(I_{d},\dots,I_{d})
:

S_{L,d,r}:=\left\{(W_{1},\dots,W_{L})\in\left(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}\right)^{L}:\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(I_{d},\dots,I_{d})=\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(W_{1},\dots,W_{L})\right\}.

(20)

We want to show for
L>1
and
d>1
that

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{L,d,r}=\Big\{(W_{1},\dots,\,PDW_{L-1},D^{-r}P^{T})\in\left(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}\right)^{L}:(W_{1},\dots,W_{L-1})\in S_{L-1,d,r},&\\
\,P\text{ is a permutation matrix and }D\text{ is an invertible diagonal matrix}\Big\}.\end{split}

(21)

Note that if
(W_{1},W_{2},\dotsc,W_{L})\in S_{L,d,r}
then

\displaystyle(W_{L}\sigma_{L-1}W_{L-1}\dotsb W_{2}\sigma_{1}W_{1})(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{r^{L-1}}\\
\vdots\\
x_{d}^{r^{L-1}}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, there is the equality

\displaystyle W_{L}\begin{pmatrix}Q_{({1})}^{r}(x)\\
\vdots\\
Q_{({d})}^{r}(x)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{r^{L-1}}\\
\vdots\\
x_{d}^{r^{L-1}}\end{pmatrix}

(22)

where

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}Q_{({1})}(x)\\
\vdots\\
Q_{({d})}(x)\end{pmatrix}:=(W_{L-1}\sigma_{L-2}W_{L-2}\dotsb W_{2}\sigma_{1}W_{1})(x).

Since the polynomial neural network
(x_{1}^{r^{L-1}},\dotsc,x_{d}^{r^{L-1}})
only has the trivial zero,
Proposition

21
implies that
W_{L}
is invertible. Multiplying both sides of (
22
) by
W_{L}^{-1}
, each
Q_{({i})}^{r}

is a linear combination of
x_{1}^{r^{L-1}},\dotsc,x_{d}^{r^{L-1}}
. For
r>1
, this is only possible if each

Q_{({i})}

is a monomial.
Indeed, no linear combination of
x_{1}^{r^{L-1}},\dotsc,x_{d}^{r^{L-1}}
is a pure
r
-th power except for scalar multiples of these individual monomials. Thus each

Q_{({i})}^{r}

is a scalar multiple of some
x_{j}^{r^{L-1}}
and each

Q_{({i})}^{r}

is a scalar multiple of some
x_{j}^{r^{L-2}}
.
It follows that
W_{L}
is a scaled permutation matrix—it is an invertible matrix that sends a set of monomials to a set of scalar multiples of those monomials.
Thus, we have shown (
21
).

By induction, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{L,d,r}=\Big\{(P_{1}D_{1},P_{2}D_{2}D_{1}^{-r}P_{1}^{T},\dots,\,D_{L-1}^{-r}P_{L-1}^{T})\in\left(\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}\right)^{L}:\,\phantom{\text{ is a permutation matrix}}&\\
P_{i}\text{ is a permutation matrix and }D_{i}\text{ is an invertible diagonal matrix}\Big\}.\end{split}

We have shown that
the fiber
\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}^{-1}(\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(I_{d},\dotsc,I_{d}))

is
S_{L,d,r}
and therefore

\operatorname{dim}{\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}^{-1}(\Psi_{{\mathbb{C},\mathbf{d},r}}(I_{d},\dotsc,I_{d}))}\,=\,\operatorname{dim}{S_{L,d,r}}\,=\,(L-1)d.

It follows from the
Fiber-Dimension Theorem

[
39
, Theorem 1.25, Part (i)]
that

N-\operatorname{dim}{Y}\,\leq\,(L-1)d.

(23)

Using these facts on the expected dimension:

\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}\,=\,N-(L-1)d\text{ \quad and \quad}\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}\geq\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}},

along with (
23
),
we have

\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}\,\leq\,\operatorname{dim}{Y}\,=\,\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}\,\leq\,\operatorname{edim}{\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}}.

(24)

Therefore
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
has the expected dimension for all
r>1
and
\ActThr(\mathbf{d})=1
.
∎

4
Outlook

Universal optimal bound for the activation degree threshold

Our bound in
Theorem

18
on the activation degree threshold for polynomial neural networks is not universally optimal.
We proved this to be the case for equi-width networks in
Theorem

22
.
Improving the bound established in
Lemma

15
leads to better bounds on the activation degree threshold in
Theorem

18
.
One potential way to improve the universal bound of activation degree thresholds is to leverage other algebro-geometric and number theoretic results.
Specifically, if the polynomials
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{k}
in
Lemma

15
satisfy the additional hypothesis of having no common zeros, then the result in
[
16
, page 71]
implies the improved bound
r\leq k^{2}-1
. In the language of algebraic geometry,
[
16
, page 71]
establishes a Picard theorem for holomorphic maps into Fermat hypersurfaces.
We are hopeful
that other results in this spirit will be applicable to the study of the dimension of neurovarieties.

Theorem

22
suggests that we should be looking for architectures where the activation degree threshold significantly improves upon the universal upper bound. A direct generalization of
Theorem

22
in this direction is the extension to non-increasing architectures.

Pyramidal polynomial neural networks (Non-increasing architectures)

One future direction in this research area is to determine the activation degree thresholds for
non-increasing
architectures
\mathbf{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\dotsc,d_{L})
— those architectures satisfying
d_{0}\geq d_{1}\geq\dotsb\geq d_{L}
.
By generalizing the proof of Theorem
22
, one might hope to prove
[
27
, Conjecture 5.7]
that for a non-increasing architecture
\mathbf{d}
with
d_{L}>1
, the neurovariety
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{d},r}
has the expected dimension for all
r>1
. That is, prove that a non-increasing architecture
\mathbf{d}
has activation degree threshold less than or equal to one.

Other networks

Another research direction is to introduce the notion of activation degree thresholds for other neural networks. Permitting negative exponents in a polynomial activation map leads to the notion of rational neural networks
[
8
]
. There is experimental evidence that it is beneficial to choose rational activation maps that are of very low degree. Defining activation degree thresholds for rational networks would provide a quantitative measure to make these observations rigorous. Rational neural networks are currently being investigated by Alexandros Grosdos et al. Certain piecewise linear networks may also admit the definition of an activation degree threshold as a means to study expressiveness. Tropical rational neural networks provide a lens to study networks with piecewise linear activation maps
[
3
,
10
,
11
,
46
]
. Such networks encompass ReLU networks; a ReLU network of fixed architecture is described by a semialgebraic set inside the parameter space of tropical rational functions. In this context, tropical rational functions provide means to define activation degree thresholds to frame and obtain results on expressiveness. Moreover, because the dimension of the neurovariety is preserved under tropicalization, activation degree thresholds may aid in translating expressiveness results for tropical neural networks to polynomial networks.
