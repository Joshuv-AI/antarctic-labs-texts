Emergence of vortices at the edges of planet-driven gaps in protoplanetary discs 2020 Emergence of vortices at the edges of planet-driven gaps in protoplanetary discs – E
Nicolas P. Cimerman, Roman R. Rafikov

Abstract
Young planets embedded in protoplanetary discs (PPDs) excite spiral density waves, which propagate, shock and deposit angular momentum in the disc. This results in gap opening around the planetary orbit, even for low (sub-thermal) mass planets, provided that the effective viscosity in the disc is low. The edges of these planet-induced gaps are known to be prone to emergence of observable vortices via the Rossby Wave Instability (RWI). We study timescales for the development of vortices driven by low mass planets in inviscid discs. We employ a recently developed semi-analytical theory of vortensity production by the planet-driven shock to predict vortensity evolution near the planet, from which we derive the radial profile of the planet-induced gap as a function of time (this procedure can have multiple other uses, e.g. to study dust trapping, suppression of pebble accretion, etc.). We then analyze the linear stability of the gap edges against the RWI, obtaining the timescales for the first appearance of unstable modes and (later) fully developed vortices at gap edges. We present useful formulae for these timescales as functions of planetary and disc parameters and provide their physical justification. We also thoroughly test our semi-analytical framework against high resolution 2D hydrodynamic simulations, confirming the accuracy of our theoretical predictions. We discuss ways in which our semi-analytical framework can be extended to incorporate additional physics, e.g. planetary accretion, migration, and non-zero disc viscosity. Our results can be used to interpret observations of PPDs and to predict emergence of vortices in simulations.

Introduction

Recent observations of protoplanetary discs (hereafter PPDs) have shown a plethora of substructures in the millimetre continuum emission that probes the spacial distribution of large dust grains

Indeed, young planets that are still embedded in their natal PPDs are known to gravitationally excite spiral density waves that carry energy and angular momentum across the disc. Wave angular momentum can be transferred to the background disc material via the linear or non-linear wave damping, driving disc evolution and resulting in gap formation around the planetary orbit

;

;

Non-linear damping of planet-driven density waves proceeds via their inevitable evolution into shocks. Planetary shock fronts are known

The timescale on which this pathway for vortex formation operates is an important diagnostic, which can help us constrain the properties of putative planets triggering vortices. Indeed, higher mass planets launch stronger density waves, which drive faster vortensity evolution and cause vortices to form earlier. Thus, if a PPD is observed to have a gap suggestive of a planet opening it and features a vortex at the gap edge, this would imply that either (i) planetary mass is high or (ii) the planet has been present for a long time. Both constraints are useful for interpreting observations. However, so far the problem of determining the vortex emergence timescale has received little attention

The main goal of our present study is to determine the critical time for the appearance of planet-driven vortices in inviscid discs and to explore the dependence of this timescale on the planetary and disc parameters, which are fixed in time. Problem setup

Our setup for studying planet-disc interaction is the same as in
10
. Here, we recite its most important aspects.

We consider a planet of mass

orbiting a star of mass

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathrm{i}}(R)=\Sigma_{\mathrm{i}}(R_{\mathrm{p}})\left(\frac{R}{R_{\mathrm{p}}}\right)^{-p}=\Sigma_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\frac{R}{R_{\mathrm{p}}}\right)^{-p},

(2)

where

2
2

2

We have changed the subscript for the initial conditions from ’0’ to ’i’ as compared to
10

We assume that the mass of the disc is small,
M_{\mathrm{d}}\ll M_{\star}
, such that its self-gravity can be neglected.

We use a

equation of state (EoS),
P=c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\Sigma
, with the spacially constant speed of sound

, which is barotropic (i.e.

7.2.1

Very importantly, in this study

;

In general, the disc maintains a radial centrifugal balance accounting for the radial pressure gradient:

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}(R)=\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{2}(R)+\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{R\Sigma(R)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma(R)}{\mathrm{d}R},

where

Our fiducial disc model is the same as in

3.1
Setup and overview of simulations

To study vortex formation we perform global, non-linear hydrodynamic simulations of planet-disc interaction using Athena++
(
Stone et al. 2020
)
3
3

3

Athena++ is publicly available on
GitHub
.
. The code uses a Godunov scheme to solve the hydrodynamic equations in a conservative form:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=0,

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\rho\mathbf{u})}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}+P\mathbf{I})

\displaystyle=-\rho\nabla\Phi,

(5)

4
Methods: linear analysis of RWI development

In addition to numerical simulations capturing the development of the RWI directly, we also studied the instability using semi-analytical means. Below we present our setup for linear stability analysis of an axisymmetric, 2D barotropic and inviscid flow.

4.1
Linear RWI stability analysis

Consider a barotropic flow in an axisymmetric 2D disc in radial equilibrium with
u_{0,R}=0
everywhere, with prescribed radial profiles of surface density and azimuthal velocity,
\Sigma_{0}(R)
and
u_{0,\phi}(R)=R\Omega_{0}(R)

4
4

4

We note that this axisymmetric state is in general different from the initial conditions we use for disc-planet interactions, which are denoted
\Sigma_{0}
,
\Omega_{0}
in

10
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{i}}
,
\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}
in this work.
.

Introducing small, non-axisymmetric perturbations to
{X\in[\Sigma,u_{R},u_{\phi}]}
of the form

5
5

5

The perturbations
\delta X=X-X_{0}
are perturbations to an axisymmetric state taken at some time
t
after introduction of the planet and are different from the perturbations with respect to the initial state (i.e.
X-X_{\mathrm{i}})
.

\displaystyle\delta X(R,\phi,\omega,m,t)=\delta X_{m}(R)\exp\left[\mathrm{i}(m\phi-\omega t)\right],

(7)

(
\left|{\delta X}\right|\ll\left|{X_{0}}\right|
) where
\delta X_{m}(R)
is a complex amplitude,
m
is the azimuthal mode number and

\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{\mathrm{R}}+\mathrm{i}\gamma

(8)

is the complex mode frequency, with real part
\omega_{\mathrm{R}}
and imaginary part
\gamma
(
unstable

\gamma>0

As shown in
Lovelace et al. 1999

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}^{\prime\prime}+B(R,\omega,m)\Psi_{m}^{\prime}+C(R,\omega,m)\Psi_{m}=0,

(9)

where primes stand for radial derivatives and

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}\equiv\frac{\delta P_{m}}{\Sigma_{0}},

(10)

is the enthalpy perturbation (
\Psi_{m}
is its Fourier component), equal to

in the barotropic case considered here. The coefficients in equation (

\displaystyle B(R,\omega,m)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{R}+\frac{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{F}}-\frac{\Omega_{0}^{\prime}}{\Omega_{0}},

(11)

\displaystyle C(R,\omega,m)

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{m^{2}}{R^{2}}-\frac{\kappa_{0}^{2}-\Delta\omega^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}}-2\frac{m}{R}\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\Delta\omega}\frac{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{F}},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(R,\omega,m)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\Sigma_{0}\Omega_{0}}{\kappa_{0}^{2}-\Delta\omega^{2}}.

(13)

Here
\Delta\omega\equiv\omega-m\Omega_{0}
is the Doppler-shifted mode frequency and

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}^{2}=\frac{2\Omega_{0}}{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}(R^{2}\Omega_{0})}{\mathrm{d}R},

(14)

is the square of the epicyclic frequency. Note that in an axisymmetric 2D disc in radial force equilibrium (with

everywhere) vortensity can be written as

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}(R)=\frac{1}{R\Sigma_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}(R^{2}\Omega_{0})}{\mathrm{d}R}=\frac{\kappa^{2}_{0}}{2\Sigma_{0}\Omega_{0}}.

(15)

9

Lovelace et al. 1999

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}^{2}-\Delta\omega^{2}}{\Sigma_{0}\Omega_{0}}=2\zeta_{0}-\frac{\Delta\omega^{2}}{\Sigma_{0}\Omega_{0}}.

(16)

For corotating modes with

\displaystyle\Xi(R)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{R\mathcal{F}}{\Omega_{0}}}\Psi,

\displaystyle\Xi_{m}^{\prime\prime}-D(R,\omega,m)\Xi_{m}=0

Knowing the vortensity jump at the planet-driven shock

S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\zeta(R,t)}{\partial t}\right|_{\mathrm{sh}}\equiv S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}=\Delta\zeta(R)\times\frac{|\Omega(R)-\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}|}{2\pi}.

(20)

Assuming
\Omega(R)\simeq\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}
, approximating
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
as constant in time
(see
10
, regarding the validity of this assumption)
, and integrating over time we obtain the vortensity profile at
time
t
after the introduction of the planetary potential as

\displaystyle\zeta(R,t)=\zeta(R,t=0)+tS_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}(R).

(21)

Substituting
\Omega
from the relation (
3
) into Equation (
15
) and assuming a barotropic EoS one obtains

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{2}+\frac{3c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}R}+c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\ln\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}R^{2}}}{2\Sigma\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{2}+\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}R}\right)^{1/2}}.

(22)

Following
Lin &
Papaloizou 2010
, but using our
globally
isothermal EoS,
c_{\mathrm{s}}=
const, instead of their
locally
isothermal EoS, we can rearrange this into a second order (non-linear) differential equation for
\Sigma
similar to their equation (18):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R^{3}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}R}\left(R^{3}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}R}\right)+\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{2}}{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}=\frac{2\Sigma}{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}\zeta\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{2}+\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}R}\right)^{1/2},

(23)

where vortensity enters only as a source term on the right-hand side.

This differential equation can be solved numerically in
R
, with the input vortensity profile
\zeta(R,t)
given by equation (
21
) at any
t
. To this end, we use an iterative relaxation scheme and impose the unperturbed surface density (
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mathrm{i}}
) as a boundary condition far from the planet (see Appendix
B
for more details).

Having found
\Sigma(R)
, we then retrieve the rotation profile
\Omega(R)
using equation (
3
), thus fully determining the axisymmetric disc state (i.e.
\zeta(R)\rightarrow[\Sigma(R),\Omega(R)])
. According to
10
, our recipe for constructing
\zeta
is accurate for
0.05\leq M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}<1
, implying that the method for constructing time-dependent
\Sigma(R),\Omega(R)
profiles for discs with gaps should work well for intermediate-mass planets.

In this work we use a fixed planet orbital radius
R_{\mathrm{p}}
and planet mass
M_{\mathrm{p}}
(resulting in time independent
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
), but we note that our 1D method can easily include planetary growth and a prescribed planet migration by making these parameters (and
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
) functions of time (see also Section
7.1
), in which case the second term in (
21
) will no longer be linear in

.

Determination of the timescale for the emergence of vortices at the edges of planet-induced gaps is the main goal of this paper. To that end, we now define a number of important timescales that characterize different stages of the vortex development via the RWI. Given the variety of methods that we employ to study this problem, some of the timescales also depend on the particular method used in defining them, see Fig.
7
for illustration. In particular, we use the superscript ’SA’ for the timescales based on the semi-analytical disc reconstruction technique of Section
4.2

5.1
Timescales based on linear RWI stability analysis

As the planet opens a deeper and deeper gap, the gap edge will at some point become unstable, which results in RWI modes computed by our linear analysis attaining
\gamma_{m}>0
. This allows us to define several characteristic timescales, as follows.

t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
: onset of instability with semi-analytic reconstruction

We define
t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
as the time after which linear stability analysis first predicts unstable RWI modes with a growth rate greater than a critical value
\gamma_{m}>\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}=10^{-2}/2\pi
(corresponding to exponential growth with an e-folding time of 100 planet orbits). This particular definition assumes that the radial structure of the disc used in our linear stability analysis has been obtained through the semi-analytical reconstruction described in Section
4.2
.

t^{\mathrm{sim}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
: onset of instability with simulation input

The time
t^{\mathrm{sim}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
is defined analogous to
t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
but using azimuthal averages of 2D simulation results as
\Sigma(R),\Omega(R)
inputs for the RWI linear stability analysis.

While the above procedure predicts when linear instability

Results of Section

\displaystyle\ln\frac{\left|{\delta\Sigma_{m}(R,t_{\mathrm{f}})}\right|}{\left|{\delta\Sigma_{m}(R,t_{\mathrm{i}})}\right|}=\int_{t_{\mathrm{i}}}^{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\gamma_{m}\,\mathrm{d}t\equiv\Gamma_{m}.

(26)

In other words,

\displaystyle\left|{\delta\Sigma_{m}(R,t_{\mathrm{f}})}\right|=\exp\left(\Gamma_{m}\right)\left|{\delta\Sigma_{m}(R,t_{\mathrm{i}})}\right|=\mathcal{A}_{m}\left|{\delta\Sigma_{m}(R,t_{\mathrm{i}})}\right|,

(27)

where we have defined the amplification factor
\mathcal{A}_{m}\equiv\exp\left(\Gamma_{m}\right)
.

Using this logic, we can define a timescale
t_{\mathrm{nl}}^{\mathrm{SA}}
as the time over which an initial perturbation of order
|\delta\Sigma|/\Sigma\simeq\mathcal{A}_{m,\mathrm{crit}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}(t_{\mathrm{nl}})\geq\mathcal{A}_{m,\mathrm{crit}},

(28)

where
\mathcal{A}_{m,\mathrm{crit}}
is some critical value (see next) and the growth rates in (
26

t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{lin}}<t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{nl}}

By comparing
t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{nl}}

\mathcal{A}_{m,\mathrm{crit}}=10^{4}

\delta\Sigma/\Sigma\simeq 10^{-4}
). Due to the exponential growth of the instability, the timescale is not very sensitive to this choice. Here we assume that the initial noise is of similar amplitude for all mode numbers. t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{lin}}

t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{nl}}

6.3
Theoretical estimate

From our previous discussion (Section
4.1
) and the results of
10
it is natural to expect that the timescale
\tau
on which the disc structure changes and the RWI sets in should depend on the vortensity production rate by the planetary shocks
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
. According to equation (
20
),
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
is given by the product of the vortensity jump at the shock
\Delta\zeta
and the synodic frequency of a shocked gas parcel w.r.t. the planet. To lowest order, we can estimate the synodic frequency using the local approximation, i.e. expanding it to linear order in
R-R_{\mathrm{p}}
:

\displaystyle|\Omega(R)-\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}|\simeq\frac{3\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{2}\left|\frac{R-R_{\mathrm{p}}}{R_{\mathrm{p}}}\right|.

(37)

Figure
11
illustrates the performance of this approximation, with the local expansion (
37
) shown in orange being compared to
|\Omega-\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}|
(solid blue). Vertical coloured lines correspond to
R=R_{\mathrm{p}}\pm 3l_{\mathrm{sh}}
, computed for different values of
h_{\mathrm{p}}
and
M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}
: we know from
10
that
\Delta\zeta
is significant only at radii satisfying
l_{\mathrm{sh}}\lesssim|R-R_{\mathrm{p}}|\lesssim 3l_{\mathrm{sh}}
(for
M_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim M_{\mathrm{th}}
). One can see that within this range the local approximation (
37
) provides a decent fit for small
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
, with order unity deviations becoming particularly noticeable for highest
h_{\mathrm{p}}
and lowest
M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}
, mainly in the inner disc. This has important implications (see Section
6.4
), but for the sake of our simple argument we will adopt the approximation (
37
) in what follows.

Figure 11:
Relative orbital frequency of gas parcels w.r.t. the planet-driven shock. We show the full expression (blue) and its linearised form (equation (
37
), orange). Vertical lines indicate a radial separation
|R-R_{\mathrm{p}}|=3l_{\mathrm{sh}}
from the planet for different
M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}
(colours) and
h_{\mathrm{p}}=0.05
and
h_{\mathrm{p}}=0.1
(dashed and dash-dotted lines, respectively).

Moreover, we have shown in
10
that the vortensity jump at the shock
\Delta\zeta\propto(M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}})^{3}
, but is a weak function of the disc aspect ratio
h_{\mathrm{p}}
. With this in mind, using
|R-R_{\mathrm{p}}|\sim l_{\mathrm{sh}}
and recalling that
l_{\mathrm{sh}}\approx R_{\mathrm{p}}h_{\mathrm{p}}(M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}})^{-2/5}

(
Goodman &
Rafikov 2001
)
, equations (
20
) and (
37
) yield

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}=S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}\propto\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{p}}}{M_{\mathrm{th}}}\right)^{3}\times\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}\frac{l_{\mathrm{sh}}}{R_{\mathrm{p}}}\propto\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}h_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{p}}}{M_{\mathrm{th}}}\right)^{2.6}.

(38)

Note that
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
depends on
h_{\mathrm{p}}
both explicitly and through
M_{\mathrm{th}}
, see equation (
1
).

Let us now make a simple assumption that RWI sets in and vortices appear at the time
\tau
corresponding to the moment when
\delta\zeta
(e.g. its peak or trough value, ignoring the radial structure for simplicity) reaches some threshold value
\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
; we discuss the validity of this assumption next in Section
6.4
. In other words, in this approach
\tau
should be determined from the condition

\displaystyle\int^{\tau}_{0}S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\approx\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}.

(39)

With
M_{\mathrm{p}}
,
h_{\mathrm{p}}
and other parameters not changing in time, as we assume in this work (but see Section
7.1
),
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
remains constant. Then the integral in (
39
) is trivial, and the time for vortices to appear is simply
\tau\approx S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}^{-1}\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
. Given the expression (
38
), it then follows that the characteristic timescale
\tau
for the emergence of vortices at the edges of a planetary gap should scale as

\displaystyle\tau\propto P_{\mathrm{p}}h_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{p}}}{M_{\mathrm{th}}}\right)^{-2.6}.

(40)

Given the simplicity of our argument, this estimate may apply to both
t_{\mathrm{lin}}
and
t_{\mathrm{nl}}
. Regarding the former, equation (
33
) implies stronger
t_{\mathrm{lin}}
dependence on planet mass,
t_{\mathrm{lin}}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$#$\cr\propto\cr\kern 2.0pt\cr\sim\cr\kern-2.0pt\cr}}}\left(M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}\right)^{-3.2}
, than in (
40
). On the other hand, the value of
\alpha
in the
t_{\mathrm{nl}}
fit (
34
) is close to
-2.6
in equation (
40
). This improved agreement for
t_{\mathrm{nl}}
is likely a coincidence, since calculation of
t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{nl}}
(leading to
t_{\mathrm{nl}}
) takes into account the evolution of
\gamma_{m}(t)
during the linear stage of instability, while the estimate (
40
) ignores such details.

Note also that our estimate (
40
) reproduces reasonably well the explicit dependence of the instability timescale on
h_{\mathrm{p}}
. Indeed,
t_{\mathrm{lin}}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$#$\cr\propto\cr\kern 2.0pt\cr\sim\cr\kern-2.0pt\cr}}}h_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1.1}
and
t_{\mathrm{nl}}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$#$\cr\propto\cr\kern 2.0pt\cr\sim\cr\kern-2.0pt\cr}}}h_{\mathrm{p}}^{-0.86}
according to the fits (
33
) & (
34
), which are reasonably close to
\tau\propto h_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}
in terms of the slope of scaling.

6.4
Vortensity threshold for RWI

Whether the onset of planet-induced RWI can be directly associated with
\delta\zeta
exceeding some threshold value
\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
, as assumed in the derivation of the scaling (
40
), is an important question. Existence of a well-defined
\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
would be extremely useful since then one could predict the emergence of vortices without running the time-consuming linear RWI analysis (it would still be needed if one were interested in e.g. the RWI growth rate); instead one would simply follow the evolution of the vortensity profile which can be easily done using the methods of
10
. And it was shown in
Ono
et al. 2016
that for simple models of localized vortensity perturbations in discs (e.g. due to a Gaussian bump, or a sharp increase of
\Sigma
) some statements regarding the RWI triggering in terms of the amplitude and radial scale of
\delta\Sigma(R)
features can indeed be made.

On the other hand, emergence of growing modes in equation (
18
) is determined entirely by the behaviour of the potential
D
, given by equation (
19
). Even for simple models considered in
Ono
et al. 2016

D
has a very complicated radial structure, see Figs. 5 & 9 in that work. In the planetary case
\zeta(R)
is considerably more complex, see Fig.
3
, which gets reflected in even more sophisticated radial profile of
D
. Moreover, RWI triggering depends not only on the amplitude but also on the width of vortensity features, which scales with
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
and changes as
h_{\mathrm{p}}
or
M_{\mathrm{p}}
are varied
(
Li
et al. 2000
, also higher temperatures tend to promote RWI growth, see)
.

Given these complications, to provide a direct test of our
\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
assumption, we measured the extremal values of
\delta\zeta
(deviation from
\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}
) at time
t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
(onset of RWI) in our semi-analytic
13
13

13

We verified that extrema of
\delta\zeta
measured in simulations agree with these values typically to
\lesssim 10\%
, which should be obvious from Figs.
3
&
14
.
calculations for different disc and planet parameters. The results are shown as a function of
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
in Fig.
12
, where we display both the peak (
\delta\zeta_{\mathrm{max}}
, top) and trough (
\delta\zeta_{\mathrm{min}}
, bottom) values of
\delta\zeta
on the side of the gap where the RWI sets in first (inner for
p=0
, outer for
p=3/2
). In Fig.

, when the RWI becomes non-linear and vortices should appear.

There are several observations that we can make based on these plots. First, extremal values of
\delta\zeta
do depend on
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
— the scale of vortensity features; at any given
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
there is a large scatter in
\delta\zeta
(a factor of several) as different parameters are varied, but the envelope clearly increases with
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
. Second, extremal values of
\delta\zeta
follow well-defined branches when only one parameter of the model is varied. In particular, we show in Appendix
E
that the data shown in Fig.
12
can be matched using a simple formula (
48
) with three parameters depending on
p
. Thus, despite the complexity of the radial behaviour of
D
, the extrema of
\delta\zeta
change in a systematic (and not chaotic) fashion. Third, as one decreases
M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}
while keeping
h_{\mathrm{p}}
fixed (varying colour of symbols), the extremal
|\delta\zeta|
increase, but much faster for
p=0
than for
p=3/2
. Fourth, as one increases
h_{\mathrm{p}}
while keeping
M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}
fixed, the extrema of
|\delta\zeta|

increase
for
p=0
, while slightly
decreasing
for
p=3/2
. Fifth, the extremal values of
\delta\zeta
typically vary much less for
p=3/2
(a factor of
\sim 2
) than for
p=0
(which vary by up to
\sim 6
).

Given these trends, it appears that the stability of the gap edge to RWI is determined not only by the overall amplitude of the vortensity perturbation but also by other factors, e.g. the radial scale and overall shape of
\delta\zeta
. For that reason, it is also not surprising that the timescale fit parameters (
33
) and (
34
) show some deviations from the predictions of our simple estimate (
40
). What is remarkable, is that these deviations are rather small and are almost independent of the surface density slope
p
, especially given the clearly
p
-dependent variation of the critical
\delta\zeta
values (by a factor of several) and the diverse trends with
h_{\mathrm{p}}
and
M_{\mathrm{p}}
in Figs.
12
&
13
.

The larger amplitude of extremal
\delta\zeta
for
p=0
can be explained by the fact that in this case vortices first appear at the inner edge of the planetary gap. The rapid variation of
|\Omega-\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}|
in the inner disc (see Fig.
11
) tends to broaden the
\delta\zeta(R)
distribution, requiring higher values of
\delta\zeta
for RWI to set in when
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
is large. But this also explains why the instability timescale stays roughly the same regardless of
p
: for large
l_{\mathrm{sh}}
the rate at which the fluid passes through the shock in the inner disc can be considerably higher than predicted by the local approximation (
37
), see Fig.
11
. Our results imply that this effect almost fully compensates for the increased
|\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}|
in
p=0
discs, resulting in
t_{\mathrm{lin}}
and
t_{\mathrm{nl}}
being essentially independent of
p
.

To summarize, we do not find that, in general,
\zeta_{\mathrm{sh}}
is confined within a well-defined narrow range, as assumed in Section
6.3
. This is approximately true for
p=3/2
, where we observe variations of
\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
by a factor of only
\sim 2
, but for
p=0
critical
\zeta_{\mathrm{sh}}
may vary much more. This is likely good news for interpreting observations, since we expect higher
p
(i.e. radially decreasing
\Sigma
) to be more typical for PPDs. On the other hand, for any
p
we do observe that the characteristic values of
|\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}|
are of order unity, which is reassuring and may explain the insensitivity of both
t_{\mathrm{lin}}
and
t_{\mathrm{nl}}
to
p
, and their rough agreement with the scaling (
40
), which we found in Sections
6.1
&
6.2
. We leave detailed exploration of the trends observed in Figs.
12
&
13
and
17
to future work.

Discussion

Semi-analytic calculation of the timescales
t^{\mathrm{SA}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
and
t_{\mathrm{nl}}^{\mathrm{SA}}
(see Section
5.1
) is a multi-step procedure: we first need to compute the evolution of the vortensity profile, then reconstruct
\Sigma,\Omega
profiles (Section
4.2
), after which we perform the RWI stability analysis (Section
4.1
) and integrate growth rates to obtain
t_{\mathrm{nl}}^{\mathrm{SA}}
(Section
5.1
). Despite this complexity,
t_{\mathrm{nl}}^{\mathrm{SA}}
agrees remarkably well with the simulation-based
\hat{t}^{\mathrm{sim}}_{\mathrm{nl}}
, providing strong support for the robustness of our semi-analytical framework. We now discuss some ways in which one can capitalize on this agreement, and put our results in context with existing studies.

7.1
Applications of our results

Our results (
34
)-(
36
) for the planet-induced vortex development timescales can be used for interpreting observations of protoplanetary discs showing evidence of non-axisymmetric features (arcs, clumps, etc.), which may be interpreted as vortices. Without going into too many details here, we note that equation (
36
) can be used to constrain either the mass or the age of a planet responsible for the appearance of an observed vortex in a protoplanetary disc. We provide more details on this in Rafikov & Cimerman (in prep.).

Our timescale estimates (
31
)-(
34
) are also useful for planning future numerical studies of RWI and vortices in protoplanetary discs. They allow one to estimate the run time of the simulations producing vortices in advance, facilitating making informed decisions about allocation of computing resources. Our timescale fits (
31
)-(
34
) can also be used for benchmarking numerical codes.

Our
\Sigma
,
\Omega
reconstruction technique (see Section
4.2
) based on
Lin &
Papaloizou 2011
allows one to construct radial profile of the planet-induced gap given the knowledge of the radial vortensity profile. Once combined with our recipe for vortensity production (valid for
M_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim M_{\mathrm{th}}
) it provides a fully self-contained semi-analytical method for predicting the radial profiles of shallow gaps at arbitrary moments of time, which we thoroughly test against simulations (see Section
4.2.2
and Appendix
C
). This is a powerful tool, complementary to the existing methods for construction of profiles of deep gaps carved out by massive (
M_{\mathrm{p}}\gtrsim M_{\mathrm{th}}
) planets
(
Crida
et al. 2006
;
Kanagawa et al. 2017
;
Ginzburg &
Sari 2018
;
Duffell 2020
)
, that can find multiple uses.

In particular, radial profiles of
\Sigma
, which can be easily computed as a function of time using our method, could be used to study dust trapping at pressure bumps
(
Stammler
et al. 2019
, e.g. )
. Especially at the outer gap edge, where our semi-analytical model matches simulations well, we expect modelling of dust accumulation to be quite accurate (in the inner disc multiple spirals may lead to some discrepancies with simulations). This provides an efficient and accurate way of studying e.g. the dependence of pebble isolation mass
(
Paardekooper &
Mellema 2004
;
Lambrechts et al. 2014
)
on disc properties such as
h_{\mathrm{p}}
and
p
(in the inviscid limit).

Numerical studies of RWI development, dust accumulation, etc. near the low mass (sub-thermal) planets typically need to be run for thousands of orbits to properly capture the disc structure at late times. Our semi-analytical method provides a useful shortcut: one can use it to construct the approximate disc state (including the forming gap) around the planet at any moment of time after the planet has been introduced in the simulation. This state would provide the initial condition for running the simulation, avoiding the initial ‘burn-in’ stage needed to reach this situation in the simulation.

We should also note that our semi-analytical method can naturally account for various processes that have been neglected in this study — planet migration, history of its mass accumulation
(
Hammer
et al. 2017
;
Hallam &
Paardekooper 2020
)
, evolution of the disc state on long timescales, etc. To enable this, one simply needs to generalize equation (
20
) by allowing
\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\Delta\zeta(R)
to be functions of time set by the evolution of the physical characteristics of the problem at hand, e.g. planetary semi-major axis
R_{\mathrm{p}}
, mass
M_{\mathrm{p}}
, etc. This equation can then be easily integrated, obtaining a more general
\zeta(R,t)
than the solution (
21
) used in this work
14
14

14

This implicitly assumes that the time-variation of
\Delta\zeta
is slow compared to the dynamical time (which is typically satisfied, e.g. since our
t_{\mathrm{lin}}\gg P_{\mathrm{p}}
, see Figure
9
), such that the radial force balance, i.e. equation (
3
), holds.
. Using this
\zeta(R,t)
to reconstruct the evolving
\Sigma
,
\Omega
profiles (see Section
4.2
) and running the RWI linear stability analysis (see Sections
4.1
&
4.3
) with these inputs, one can determine the onset of RWI in these more general situations.

For illustration, let us sketch how our heuristic determination of the vortex emergence timescale
\tau
(see Section
6.3
) would change if
M_{\mathrm{p}}
were not constant but was actually growing in time due to accretion
(
Hallam &
Paardekooper 2020
)
. With time-dependent
M_{\mathrm{p}}(t)
the condition (
39
) would become
\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}\approx\int^{\tau}_{0}S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\propto\int^{\tau}_{0}M_{\mathrm{p}}^{2.6}(t)\mathrm{d}t
, see equation (
38
), providing a new relation for determining
\tau
instead of equation (
40
). Because of the steep dependence on
M_{\mathrm{p}}(t)
in this integral we expect that the determination of
\tau
would be most sensitive to the details of the
M_{\mathrm{p}}(t)
behaviour at late time, when the planet is close to reaching its final mass.

7.2
Potential limitations of this work

In this work, we made several simplifying assumptions to highlight the most important physical processes leading to vortex formation. In particular, we neglected the presence of dust in the disc and its back-reaction on the fluid motions and ignored the disc self-gravity
(
Lin 2012b
)
. Unlike
Hammer
et al. 2017
and
Hallam &
Paardekooper 2020
, we keep the planetary mass fixed in time since accounting for the planetary accretion history would necessarily introduce some ad hoc assumptions about the
M_{\mathrm{p}}(t)
dependence. Similarly, we fix the semi-major axis of the planetary orbit not allowing it to migrate and set planetary eccentricity to zero. We also allow only one planet to be present in the disc. As mentioned in Section
7.1
, our semi-analytical approach outlined in Section
4.2
can be easily generalized to account for the history of planetary mass accretion and migration, multiple planets
(
Garrido-Deutelmoser et al. 2022
)
, and so on. We restrict the disc to be strictly two-dimensional. However, in their study of linear RWI in a 3D disc,
Lin 2012a
;
Lin 2012b
found that its growth rates can be accurately predicted from the 2D problem alone. Thus, introducing the third dimension is unlikely to change our main conclusions.

7.2.1
Non-barotropic thermodynamics

In line with our previous work

(
10
)
, all our calculations assume a globally isothermal EoS, which is a special case of a barotropic EoS (
P=P(\Sigma)
only). In real discs there are various heating and cooling processes that would cause departures from the barotropic setup. Adoption of more sophisticated thermodynamic assumptions, even as simple as the introduction of
\beta
-cooling on a timescale
\beta\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{-1}
, would have several consequences.

First, the excitation of planetary density waves would change, see
Miranda &
Rafikov 2020a
who explored the dependence of planetary torques on
\beta
. Second, (linear) thermal relaxation can dramatically modify damping of the spiral waves as shown in
Miranda &
Rafikov 2020a
;
Miranda &
Rafikov 2020b
. This must affect radial distribution of the vortensity production around the planet and the resulting gap profiles. Third, baroclinic effects may start affecting vortensity evolution. Fourth, irreversible heating of the disc by planet-driven spiral shocks
(
Rafikov 2016
;
Ziampras et al. 2020
, e.g.)
can modify the local temperature profile near the planet, further impacting density wave propagation and potentially driving additional baroclinic effects. Fifth, a specific form of the EoS affects the vortensity generation at the shock front, e.g. compare
Lin &
Papaloizou 2010
and
10
. Finally, even for identical surface density perturbations (i.e. neglecting the aforementioned effects), the linear growth of the RWI is affected by thermal relaxation as shown by e.g.
Les &
Lin 2015
and
Huang &
Yu 2022
.

Thermal relaxation also affects the long-term survival of vortices
(
Fung &
Ono 2021
;
Rometsch et al. 2021
)
, but this issue is beyond the scope of our study, which focuses on vortex
generation
.

7.2.2
The effect of shear viscosity

In line with
10
, we assumed the disc to be inviscid. This assumption is supported by many observations suggesting that viscosity is likely low in most PPDs
(
Pinte
et al. 2016
;
Rafikov 2017
;
Flaherty
et al. 2020
)
. Nevertheless, it is still important to assess the consequences of disc viscosity being non-zero.

Miranda &
Rafikov 2020a
have shown that, unless the disc is very viscous (with effective
\alpha\gtrsim 0.01
), viscous stresses do not affect density wave dissipation, which is determined by the combination of non-linear and radiative damping. However, another effect of viscosity is to diffusively smooth out any features in the radial vortensity distribution. In particular, it introduces the diffusive term in the vortensity evolution equation, which will tend to smear out the sharp peaks and troughs of
\zeta
produced in the vicinity of the planet, see Figs.
3
&
14
. As a result, viscosity would slow down the growth of
\zeta
and could delay the onset of RWI as has been shown in e.g.
Hallam &
Paardekooper 2020
. Thus, our inviscid calculations provide us with the
lower limit
for the time to reach instability, and
\tau
will be longer in sufficiently viscous discs.

High viscosity may suppress the RWI entirely
(
Hammer
et al. 2017
)
, by preventing
\zeta
from reaching the threshold value
\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
necessary for instability to set in. We can provide a simple heuristic estimate of when this might happen. Without invoking the explicit form of the viscous term in the vortensity evolution equation, we can model it as a diffusion process
15
15

15

It should be remembered that the real stress in the disc, for example caused by the MRI, may respond to planetary torques differently from a simple shear viscosity model
(
Zhu
et al. 2013
, e.g.)
.
with a characteristic diffusion coefficient equal to the kinematic viscosity
\nu
(similar to the momentum equation), i.e.
\partial_{t}\zeta|_{\nu}\sim\nu\nabla^{2}\zeta
. Since
\zeta
varies on scales
\sim l_{\mathrm{sh}}
, this term should be of order
\nu\zeta/l_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}
. Viscous diffusion would stop the planet-driven growth of vortensity at some equilibrium value
\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}
at which
\partial_{t}\zeta|_{\nu}
would match
S_{\zeta,\mathrm{sh}}
given by equation (
38
). In other words,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}h_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{p}}}{M_{\mathrm{th}}}\right)^{2.6}\propto\nu\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}}{l_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}},

(41)

which, using equation (
6
) and
\nu=\alpha h_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}R_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}
, can be written as

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\propto\alpha^{-1}h_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{p}}}{M_{\mathrm{th}}}\right)^{1.8}.

(42)

RWI would be suppressed altogether and vortices would not emerge if
\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}<\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}
. In particular, equation (
42
) implies that for this to be the case planetary mass must satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\mathrm{p}}}{M_{\mathrm{th}}}<B\left(\frac{\alpha}{h_{\mathrm{p}}}\right)^{5/9},

(43)

where
B
is a (dimensionless) constant which can be calibrated using simulations.
This relation is necessarily approximate, with true power law exponents possibly deviating somewhat from our predictions, which again can be checked using simulations. We note that
McNally et al. 2019
found a different relation between
M_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\alpha
(with the opposite sign of the exponent), which separates evolution with and without vortices. The reason for this difference is that (unlike us) they also considered planet migration, which can lead to non-trivial feedback effects in the presence of viscosity, see
Rafikov 2002b
. We also cannot directly compare our relation (
43
) with the results of
Hallam &
Paardekooper 2020
, since planet mass was varied in their study. In light of our results, formation of vortices in viscous discs clearly warrants further investigation.

7.3
Comparison with previous works

A number of past studies explored the emergence of vortices at the edges of planetary gaps, and some of their approaches share similarities with our work. For example, in their study of high mass planets (their lowest mass is
M_{\mathrm{p}}=0.8M_{\mathrm{th}}
),
de Val-Borro et al. 2007
investigated linear stability to RWI of a simulated disc perturbed by a planet in a time-dependent manner, like we do when determining
t^{\mathrm{sim}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
and
t^{\mathrm{sim}}_{\mathrm{nl}}
. They found an increase in the growth rate of unstable modes with time (see their Fig. 5) as the planet opens a deeper and deeper gap, similar to what we show in our Fig.
6
. Adopting a constant
\Sigma_{\mathrm{i}}
(i.e.
p=0
) disc, they found higher growth rates at the inner gap edge, in line with our findings. While they speculated that this is caused by the proximity to domain boundaries, our analysis shows that this is a real effect due to the accelerated steepening of the inner spiral arm for
p=0

(
10
)
.

Lin &
Papaloizou 2010
carried out a similar analysis, also focusing on higher mass planets (
M_{\mathrm{p}}\geq M_{\mathrm{th}}
). Their work was the first to introduce
\Sigma,\Omega
reconstruction from vortensity
\zeta
, and they found good performance of this technique using
\zeta
profiles from simulations. We extend this method even further by employing a semi-analytic prescription for
\zeta
evolution from
10
, see Section
4.2.1
, and confirm its accuracy for a range of relevant parameters, see Section
4.2.2
and Appendix
C
.

A number of studies
(
Li
et al. 2009
;
Yu
et al. 2010
;
McNally et al. 2019
, e.g.)
numerically studied emergence of vortices in viscous discs with migrating planets. Because of these additional physical ingredients, which introduce new effects
(
Rafikov 2002a
, e.g. gas redistribution associated with migration,)
we cannot provide a direct comparison of our results with their findings. This also precludes us from comparing our calculations of the vortex emergence timescale
\tau
with the works of
Hammer
et al. 2017
and
Hallam &
Paardekooper 2020
, since they considered time-varying planet mass and viscous discs (although we discuss ways in which the impact of these additional physical ingredients might be understood in Sections
7.1
and
7.2.2
, respectively).

Finally, we note the similarity of planet-driven vortex production in PPDs with the origin of vortices found by
Coleman et al. 2022
in their inviscid 2D simulations of accretion disc boundary layers. The only key difference is that in their case density waves are driven not by planets but by the acoustic instability in the boundary layer
(
Belyaev &
Rafikov 2012
;
Belyaev
et al. 2012
;
Belyaev
et al. 2013
)
, while all subsequent processes — production of vortensity at the shock fronts, triggering of RWI, mergers of the resultant vortices — are essentially identical.

8
Summary

We studied the stability of gaps carved by (sub-thermal mass, fixed in time) planets to RWI in globally isothermal, inviscid, 2D protoplanetary discs. Our primary goals were to study the disc evolution leading to instability and to determine the timescale on which RWI sets in and vortices form at the edges of planet-driven gaps. We used a two-stage approach to reach these goals. First, we developed a closed-form semi-analytical approach for computing the radial profile of a gap induced by a sub-thermal mass planet at any moment of time given a set of disc and planetary parameters (Section
4.2
). This was achieved by coupling the semi-analytical calculation of the vortensity production at the planetary shock
(
10
)
with the
\Sigma,\Omega
-reconstruction technique
(
Lin &
Papaloizou 2010
)
. Second, we carried out a linear RWI stability analysis (Section
4.1
) on these gap profiles to determine the onset of instability. Along the way, our results have been verified at all levels against direct hydro simulations. Below we briefly summarize our main findings.

•

We showed (Section
4.2.2
and Appendix
C
) that our semi-analytical method reliably reproduces gap profiles when compared to expensive 2D simulations for a mass range
M_{\mathrm{p}}=(0.1-0.5)M_{\mathrm{th}}
(with somewhat reduced agreement in the inner disc caused by the emergence and dissipation of the secondary spiral arm). This allowed us to study the RWI stability of gap edges in a fast and efficient manner.

•

We calculated the timescales for the onset of linear RWI and for the development of its non-linear phase. We provided useful fitting formulae for these timescales (Sections
6.1
,
6.2
) valid in the
M_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim M_{\mathrm{th}}
regime and proposed an approximate, heuristic explanation of these scalings (Section
6.3
).

•

Consistent with previous studies, we found that hotter discs (keeping
M_{\mathrm{p}}/M_{\mathrm{th}}
constant)
and higher mass planets accelerate the development of RWI.

•

In agreement with
10
, we found that the disc surface density slope
p
controls, which side of the gap develops RWI first: inner gap edge for constant-
\Sigma
discs (
p=0
), but outer edge for discs with radially decreasing
\Sigma
(
p=3/2
).

•

We found that at the point when RWI starts developing, the planet-induced vortensity deviation
\delta\zeta
shows considerable variation as disc and planetary parameters are changed (Section
6.4
). While we observe certain trends in the behaviour of this characteristic
\delta\zeta
, its high sensitivity to the value of the surface density slope
p
precludes us from drawing simple and universal conclusions.

•

We have shown how our semi-analytical approach can be extended beyond our simple setup, for example, to account for planetary accretion and migration (Section
7.1
), and assessed the impact of non-zero disc viscosity on our results (Section
7.2.2
).

•

Our findings can be used for interpreting observations of PPDs with vortex-like structures that may be caused by planets, as well as for setting up and testing simulations of disc-planet interaction.

In Rafikov & Cimerman (in prep.) we apply the results of this study to constrain masses and ages of putative planets in protoplanetary discs with vortex-like structures.

Appendix A
Search for RWI modes

We discretize the differential operators in equation (
9
) using
N_{R}
cells in radius, which allows us to write this equation as

\displaystyle W(R,\omega,m)\Psi_{m}=0,

(44)

where
W
is a complex, tri-diagonal square matrix of size
N_{R}\times N_{R}
. Existence of non-trivial solutions
\Psi_{m}
thus requires
\det W=0
or, in other words, that
W
has at least one eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}=0
. The problem is then reduced to finding the values of
\omega
giving
\det W=0
. For most parameter sets, we solve this problem on a subdomain of the same logarithmic radial grid that is used in the hydrodynamical models, ranging from
R_{\mathrm{min}}=0.3R_{\mathrm{p}}
to
R_{\mathrm{max}}=3.25R_{\mathrm{p}}
. For some parameter sets (i.e. small
M_{\mathrm{p}}
and large
h_{\mathrm{p}}
), this radial domain is increased to ensure that boundaries are far enough from the gap edges.

In order to allow for wave propagation across boundaries, we use outgoing wave boundary conditions under the WKB approximation
(
Ono
et al. 2016
)
. While low-
m
modes (
m

\lesssim 6
) are very localized and insensitive to boundary conditions, modes of higher
m
(
\gtrsim 6
) are more global, such that BCs become important
(
Lin &
Papaloizou 2010
, e.g.)
.

We use an eigenvalue search strategy similar to
Li
et al. 2000
,
de Val-Borro et al. 2007
and
Ono
et al. 2016
, which we have implemented using
Python3
. Knowing the radial location of the vortensity minimum
R_{0}
, we use the result of previous studies that trapped, unstable modes are corotation modes, i.e. we start our search around
\omega_{\mathrm{R}}\simeq m\Omega(R_{0})
(and
|\Delta\omega|^{2}\ll\Omega^{2}
). We thus restrict our search to a region around corotation, typically
0.9<\omega_{\mathrm{R}}/(m\Omega(R_{0}))<1.1
and begin by looking for modes with low growth
rates
0.01<2\pi\gamma_{m}<0.4
. Poles and roots of
\det W
are found via contour integration in the complex
\omega_{\mathrm{R}}-\gamma
plane as described in
de Val-Borro et al. 2007
. When a contour contains a root, we use Muller’s method
(
Muller 1956
)
for locating it more precisely. Once a mode is found,
\Psi_{m}
is obtained by solving the eigenvalue problem (
44
) using the SciPy routine
scipy.sparse.linalg.eigs
for sparse matrices (since
W
is tri-diagonal). Especially for modes with low
\gamma_{m}
, the solver sometimes finds spurious unphysical modes, that do not represent trapped modes. Thus, our method requires some human interaction, but could be extended to deal with such spurious solutions. Having found an unstable mode for the disc structure at a time
t
, we can use the complex frequency as an initial guess in Muller’s method for the next search at time
t+\Delta t
, as the disc evolves slowly
(
Li
et al. 2000
)
. This saves the more expensive contour integration time.

We have verified our method by confirming that we find the same modes and growth rates as
Ono
et al. 2016
for their fiducial ‘Gaussian Bump’ setup. As another test, we have run several simulations in which we removed planet after the disc reached an unstable state. Re-initializing the disc with the azimuthally averaged
\langle\Sigma(R)\rangle_{\phi}
,
\langle\Omega(R)\rangle_{\phi}
from the last frame with the planet, we monitored the development of RWI with a well-defined (constant in time) growth rate. In parallel, we also carried out linear RWI analysis on the same background disc state. We found very good agreement (at the level of a few percent) of both the growth rates and the radial structure of the mode measured in simulations with linear theory. This experiment not only verifies our linear RWI analysis but also provides a very useful test of the code, in particular, of the orbital advection module, now implemented in Athena++, which has not been used by
Ono
et al. 2018
.

Appendix B
\Sigma,\Omega
reconstruction

To solve their version of equation (
23
),
Lin &
Papaloizou 2010
adopted a shooting method.
However, we found that it fails if the radial region of integration is too large, as only exponentially growing or decaying solutions are found.

For this reason, we developed a more robust iterative method for solving this non-linear differential equation: we used a discretized version of equation (
23
),

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma_{i+1}-2\Sigma_{i}+\Sigma_{i-1}}{w^{2}}=F_{i}(\Sigma,\zeta,R),

(45)

where
w\equiv R_{i+1}-R_{i}
is a constant grid spacing and subscript
i
indicates evaluation at
R_{i}
and

\displaystyle F_{i}(\Sigma,\zeta,R)=

\displaystyle\frac{2\zeta\Sigma_{i}^{2}}{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{2}+\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\left(\Sigma_{i+1}-\Sigma_{i-1}\right)}{2wR\Sigma_{i}}\right)^{1/2}

(46)

\displaystyle-\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{K}}^{2}}{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}\Sigma_{i}+\frac{\left(\Sigma_{i+1}-\Sigma_{i-1}\right){}^{2}}{4w^{2}\Sigma_{i}}-\frac{3\left(\Sigma_{i+1}-\Sigma_{i-1}\right)}{2wR}.

This can be rearranged to give an iterative scheme for
\Sigma_{i}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i}^{(n+1)}=(1-C)\Sigma_{i}^{(n)}+\frac{C}{2}\left(-w^{2}F_{i}+\Sigma_{i+1}+\Sigma_{i-1}\right)^{(n)},

(47)

where the superscript indicates the number of iteration. We typically chose
C=2/3
. If required, adopting a multi-grid method and making
C
a function of iteration number for successive over-relaxation (SOR) could accelerate convergence.

Appendix C
Further tests of the semi-analytical reconstruction

Here we describe further tests of our semi-analytical gap reconstruction technique (Section
4.2

C.1
Variation of the surface density slope
p

First, we test reconstruction in a constant surface density disc (
p=0
). In column A of Fig.
14
,
we show the disc structure at
t=650P_{\mathrm{p}}
for our standard
M_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25M_{\mathrm{th}}

\Sigma_{\mathrm{i}}

10
, this disc model has a non-zero background vortensity gradient (compared to the fiducial
p=3/2

\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}

p

(
60

10
, e.g.)
. Accordingly, the inner gap edge experiences faster vortensity evolution (and becomes unstable earlier) than the outer one in a
p=0
disc, opposite to the
p=3/2
case covered in Section
4.2.1
. This is what we see in Fig.

\Sigma
at the inner gap edge well, but over-predicts it at the outer gap edge. This is due to a consistently stronger perturbation in
\zeta^{\mathrm{SA}}
in this region. Both methods show a stronger asymmetry in the
\Sigma
peaks at the inner and outer gap edges as compared to the fiducial case, with the inner peak dominating. The radial width of the gap is similar to the fiducial case, as expected, since
l_{\mathrm{sh}}

Overall, the level of accuracy of our semi-analytical reconstruction for
p=0
disc is similar to that in Fig.
3
. We note, however, that for the lowest planet mass case,
M_{\mathrm{p}}=0.1M_{\mathrm{th}}
, with
h_{\mathrm{p}}=0.1

p=0
, we find poorer agreement between simulations and semi-analytical method in the inner disc. We attribute this to the fact that in our semi-analytical model, we neglect the advection term in the conservation equation for vortensity, as we found it to be negligible in
10
. However, for this parameter set, the shocking distance

is large, such that gap opening in the inner disc occurs in a region with initially large vortensity gradient. As a result, the vortensity advection term (

C.2
Variation of the disc scale-height
h_{\mathrm{p}}

We next change the disc scale-height at the planet location
h_{\mathrm{p}}

10
