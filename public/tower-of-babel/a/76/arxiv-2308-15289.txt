Energy Space Newton Differentiability for Solution Maps of Unilateral and Bilateral Obstacle Problems
Constantin Christof, Gerd Wachsmuth

Abstract
We prove that the solution operator of the classical unilateral obstacle problem on a nonempty open bounded set Ω ⊂ ℝ d \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} , d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} , is Newton differentiable as a function from L p ​ ( Ω ) L^{p}(\Omega) to H 0 1 ​ ( Ω ) H_{0}^{1}(\Omega) whenever max ⁡ ( 1 , 2 ​ d / ( d + 2 ) ) < p ≤ ∞ \max(1,2d/(d+2))<p\leq\infty . By exploiting this Newton differentiability property, results on angled subspaces in H − 1 ​ ( Ω ) H^{-1}(\Omega) , and a formula for orthogonal projections onto direct sums, we further show that the solution map of the classical bilateral obstacle problem is Newton differentiable as a function from L p ​ ( Ω ) L^{p}(\Omega) to H 0 1 ​ ( Ω ) ∩ L q ​ ( Ω ) H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega) whenever max ⁡ ( 1 , d / 2 ) < p ≤ ∞ \max(1,d/2)<p\leq\infty and 1 ≤ q < ∞ 1\leq q<\infty . For both the unilateral and the bilateral case, we provide explicit formulas for the Newton derivative. As a concrete application example for our results, we consider the numerical solution of an optimal control problem with H 0 1 ​ ( Ω ) H_{0}^{1}(\Omega) -controls and box-constraints by means of a semismooth Newton method.

Introduction and summary of results

This paper is concerned with Newton differentiability properties of
solution maps

S\colon H^{-1}(\Omega)\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
u\mapsto y
,

of obstacle-type variational inequalities (VIs) of the form

y\in K,\qquad\left\langle-\Delta y-u,v-y\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\geq 0\qquad\forall v\in K.

(VI)

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, is a
nonempty open bounded set;
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
H^{-1}(\Omega)
,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
, and

-\Delta
are defined as usual
(see
section

2
); and
K
is a nonempty set of the
type

\displaystyle K:=\{v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\mid\psi\leq v\leq\phi\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega\}

(1)

involving measurable functions
\psi,\phi\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty]
.
The main contributions of this work, that can be seen as a continuation of
our previous study
[
11
]
, are as follows:
First, we prove that the
Newton differentiability result in
[
11
, Corollary 3.9]
,
which yields that the solution map
S\colon u\mapsto y

of (
VI
)
is Newton differentiable as a function from

L^{p}(\Omega)
to
L^{q}(\Omega)

for certain
p
and
q
when
\phi\equiv\infty
holds,
can be improved
to obtain that

S
is even Newton differentiable
as a function from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

whenever
\max(1,2d/(d+2))<p\leq\infty
and
\phi\equiv\infty
.
Second, we show that this energy space Newton differentiability result for the unilateral
case
makes it possible to prove that the solution map of the bilateral obstacle problem, i.e.,
the problem (
VI
) with
\psi\not\equiv-\infty
and
\phi\not\equiv\infty
,
is Newton differentiable as a function from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega)

whenever
\max(1,d/2)<p\leq\infty
and
1\leq q<\infty
.
For the precise statements of these two main results (involving
all assumptions and also the definitions of the respective Newton derivatives),
we refer the reader to
sections

4
and
6
.

Before we begin with our analysis,
let us briefly put our work into perspective.
For the sake of brevity, we focus on
infinite-dimensional problems. For additional information on the finite-dimensional setting,
we refer the reader to

[
14
,
19
,
22
,
23
,
25
]
and the references therein.

In optimization and optimal control,
the concept of Newton differentiability (a.k.a. semismoothness)
plays a crucial role because it characterizes
nonsmooth functions that
are well behaved to such a degree that
classical algorithms for the numerical solution of
smooth minimization problems and operator equations become applicable.
See, e.g.,
[
2
,
6
,
17
,
22
,
32
,
36
]

for various contributions on this topic. Most prominently,
in the presence of Newton differentiability,
it is possible to generalize the classical Newton algorithm
to the so-called
semismooth Newton method

which, under suitable invertibility assumptions
on the involved generalized derivatives,
makes it possible
to solve nonsmooth operator equations
with superlinear convergence speed.
In practice, the main difficulty in applying such a semismooth Newton method
is proving that the nonsmooth operators under consideration
indeed possess a Newton derivative.
In fact, establishing Newton differentiability results for infinite-dimensional functions
is so hard that there are only a handful of results available on this topic.
The one that is most commonly used in applications is
the fact that nonsmooth superposition operators

F\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{q}(\Omega)
,

v\mapsto f(v)
,
that are
induced by a globally Lipschitz continuous and Newton differentiable
function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, are Newton differentiable
when
p
and
q
satisfy
1\leq q<p\leq\infty
(a so-called norm gap)
and the Newton derivative of
F
is constructed from that of
f
by means
of a pointwise-a.e. selection.
We refer to, e.g.,

[
12
,
18
,
30
,
31
,
36
]
,
where this Newton differentiability result
is used for the numerical solution of optimal control problems with
L^{2}
-box-constraints,
and also the recent
[
5
]
.
For functions that are not of superposition type (or, more precisely,
for which superposition operators are not the sole source of nonsmoothness),
Newton differentiability results are very scarce. To the best of our knowledge,
there are only two papers containing genuine contributions on this topic. The first one,

[
4
]
, establishes the Newton differentiability of the solution mapping
of a prototypical rate-independent evolution variational inequality—the so-called scalar stop operator—by means of a semi-explicit solution formula;
see
[
4
, Theorem 7.15]
. The second one is our previous
contribution
[
11
]
,
which
demonstrates by means of pointwise convexity properties that
solution maps of unilateral obstacle problems are Newton differentiable as
functions between suitable Lebesgue spaces when the
strong-weak Bouligand differential is used as a generalized derivative;
see
[
11
, Theorem 2.12, Corollary 3.9]
.
Compare also again with
[
5
]
in this context.
Note that all of these existing contributions have in common that they rely
on pointwise properties of the considered nonsmooth operators—either arising
from a superposition structure or a certain curvature behavior—and
thus only yield Newton differentiability results in Lebesgue spaces.

In the present paper, we demonstrate how this limitation can be overcome.
More precisely, we show how the fact that the set
K
in (
1
) is polyhedric
(in the sense of
[
15
]
)
can be exploited to prove that the solution operator of the unilateral obstacle problem
is Newton differentiable as a function with values in the energy space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
;
see
theorem

13
. We moreover demonstrate that this
refined Newton differentiability result for the unilateral case
and properties of projections onto angled subspaces in
H^{-1}(\Omega)

make it possible to establish
the Newton differentiability of the solution map of the bilateral obstacle problem
as a function from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{q}(\Omega)

for all
\max(1,d/2)<p\leq\infty
and
1\leq q<\infty
;
see
theorem

40
. We would like to point out that the latter of these two main results
is completely beyond the scope of the aforementioned pointwise approaches for proving
Newton differentiability properties as the solution map of the bilateral obstacle problem
neither has a superposition structure nor possesses pointwise-a.e. curvature properties
that could be used to resort to the scalar situation.
At least to the best of our knowledge,

theorem

13
,
theorem

40
,
and the arguments that we employ for the proofs of these main results
are new and
have no analogue in the literature.
We remark that we restrict our attention to the classical obstacle problem
(
VI
) in our analysis mainly for simplicity and to avoid obscuring the basic ideas of our approach with technicalities.
We expect that the techniques that we develop in the following
can be extended rather straightforwardly to, e.g., Signorini-type VIs and thin-obstacle problems.
Compare also with the general functional-analytic setting considered in
[
11
]
in this regard.

the remainder of the paper.

Sections

Section

.
The results in this section

.

Section

is concerned
with the proof of our second main result—the Newton differentiability of the solution map

to

;
see

.
In

Notation and basic concepts

Throughout this paper,
we use the standard symbols

\|\cdot\|_{X}
,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X}
,
and

(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}

to denote
norms,
dual pairings, and
inner products defined on a (real) vector space
X
,
respectively.
The space of linear and continuous functions
between two normed spaces
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})

and
(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
is denoted by
\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
.
For the topological dual space, we write
X^{\star}:=\mathscr{L}(X,\mathbb{R})
,
and for the orthogonal complement of a closed
subspace
W
in a Hilbert space
H
, we write
W^{\perp}
.
The kernel and the range of a function
G\in\mathscr{L}(X,Y)

are denoted by
\mathrm{ker}(G)
and
\mathrm{range}(G)
, respectively.
The identity map is denoted by
\mathrm{Id}
.
If a space

(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
is continuously embedded into a space
(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
,
then we write
X\hookrightarrow Y
.
If the embedding is compact,
then this is denoted by
X\mathrel{\mathop{\hookrightarrow}\limits^{\vbox to0.0pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\textup{c}$}\vss}}}Y
.
Weak and strong convergences are denoted by

\rightharpoonup
and
\to
, respectively. Given a sequence

\{G_{n}\}\subset\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
, we say that

G_{n}
converges in the weak operator topology (WOT) to
G\in\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
,
in symbols
G_{n}\smash{\overset{\textsc{wot}}{\to}}G
,
if
G_{n}h\rightharpoonup Gh
holds in
Y
for all
h\in X
.

A mapping
F
from
X
to the power set of
Y

is denoted by
F\colon X\rightrightarrows Y
.
Recall that a function

F\colon X\to Y
between two normed spaces
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})

and
(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})

is called directionally differentiable if,
for all
x,h\in X
, there exists
F^{\prime}(x;h)\in Y
(the directional derivative)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{0<t\to 0}\frac{F(x+th)-F(x)}{t}=F^{\prime}(x;h).

If
F^{\prime}(x;\cdot)
is an element of
\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
, then

F
is called Gâteaux differentiable at
x
,
F^{\prime}(x):=F^{\prime}(x;\cdot)\in\mathscr{L}(X,Y)

is called the Gâteaux derivative of
F
at
x
,
and
x
is called a Gâteaux point of
F
.
The set of all Gâteaux points of a function
F\colon X\to Y

is denoted by

\mathcal{D}_{F}\subset X
.
A directionally differentiable function
F\colon X\to Y
satisfying

\{h_{n}\}\subset X,\{t_{n}\}\subset(0,\infty),h_{n}\to h,t_{n}\to 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{F(x+t_{n}h_{n})-F(x)}{t_{n}}\to F^{\prime}(x;h)

for all
x,h\in X
is called Hadamard directionally differentiable.
Following
[
8
,
28
]
,
we
define the strong-weak Bouligand differential
\partial_{B}^{sw}F\colon X\rightrightarrows\mathscr{L}(X,Y)

of a function
F\colon X\to Y
by

\displaystyle\partial_{B}^{sw}F(x):=\big\{G\in\mathscr{L}(X,Y)\nonscript\;\big|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\exists\{x_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{F}\colon x_{n}\to x\text{ in }X,~F^{\prime}(x_{n})\smash{\overset{\textsc{wot}}{\to}}G\text{ in }\mathscr{L}(X,Y)\big\}.

A function
F\colon X\to Y

equipped with a mapping

DF\colon X\rightrightarrows\mathscr{L}(X,Y)

satisfying
DF(x)\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in X

is called Newton differentiable if

\sup_{G\in DF(x+h)}\frac{\|F(x+h)-F(x)-Gh\|_{Y}}{\|h\|_{X}}\to 0\quad\text{ for }0<\|h\|_{X}\to 0

(2)

for all
x\in X
;
cf.
[
6
,
17
,
36
]
. In this case,
DF
is called a
Newton derivative of
F
.

Given a closed convex nonempty subset
L
of a space
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})

and
x\in L
,
we denote by
\mathcal{R}(x;L):=\mathbb{R}_{+}(L-x)
the radial cone of
L
at
x

and by
\mathcal{T}(x;L):=\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{R}(x;L))
the tangent cone of
L

at
x
; cf.
[
3
, section 2.2.4]
.
Here,
\mathrm{cl}(\cdot)
denotes a closure in
X
.

Given a nonempty open bounded set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
we denote
by
L^{p}(\Omega)
,
H^{k}(\Omega)
, and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
,

k\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in[1,\infty]
,
the standard real Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces on
\Omega
, respectively.
The norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
,

\|\cdot\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}

are defined as usual; see
[
1
, chapter 5]
.
Analogously to
[
1
, Definition 5.1.4]
,
we define
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

to be the closure of the set
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
of smooth functions
with compact support on
\Omega
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
We equip the space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

with the energy norm of (
VI
), i.e.,

\lVert v\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\qquad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(3)

Here,
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm and
\nabla
is the weak gradient.
The associated inner product is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
.
Due to Poincaré’s inequality,
the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
is
equivalent to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

on
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. For the topological dual space of

(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\|\cdot\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)})
, we use the symbol
H^{-1}(\Omega)
.
The Laplacian

-\Delta\colon H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\Omega)

is defined by

\langle-\Delta v,w\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}:=(v,w)_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\qquad\forall v,w\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(4)

Due to (
3
) and (
4
),

(-\Delta)^{-1}
is precisely the Riesz map
of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
In particular,
-\Delta
is an isometric isomorphism
between
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
H^{-1}(\Omega)
.
This also implies that the inner product on
H^{-1}(\Omega)

associated with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}
is given by

(z_{1},z_{2})_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}=((-\Delta)^{-1}z_{1},(-\Delta)^{-1}z_{2})_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall z_{1},z_{2}\in H^{-1}(\Omega).

(5)

For the spaces of continuous functions
on
\Omega
and
\bar{\Omega}:=\mathrm{cl}(\Omega)
, we use
the symbols
C(\Omega)
and
C(\bar{\Omega})
, respectively. We further define

C_{0}(\Omega):=\{v\in C(\bar{\Omega})\mid v=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}
, where
\partial\Omega
denotes the
boundary of
\Omega
.
Recall that
C(\bar{\Omega})
and
C_{0}(\Omega)
are Banach when endowed
with the maximum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
Given an element
v
of
C(\Omega)
,
we denote by
\{v=c\}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the level set
\{x\in\Omega\mid v(x)=c\}
.
Analogous abbreviations are also used for sublevel, superlevel, and coincidence sets.
For the indicator function of a set
D\subset\Omega
,
we use the notation
\mathds{1}_{D}\colon\Omega\to\{0,1\}
,
and for the Euclidean distance to

,
we write
\dist(\cdot,D)
.
Note that
further symbols etc. are introduced in the following sections wherever necessary.
This additional notation is clarified on its first appearance.

3
Preliminaries on the obstacle problem

In this section, we collect basic results on
the obstacle problem (
VI
).
Throughout
section

3
,
we utilize the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
(standing assumptions for
section

3
).

•

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, is a nonempty, open, and bounded set;

•

\psi,\phi\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty]
are measurable functions such that
K\neq\emptyset
.

We begin with
a basic solution result for (
VI
).

Proposition 2
(solvability and Lipschitz continuity).

For all
u\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
,
the variational inequality (
VI
) possesses a unique solution
S(u):=y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
The solution map
S\colon H^{-1}(\Omega)\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
u\mapsto y
,
of (
VI
) satisfies

\left\lVert S(u_{1})-S(u_{2})\right\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall u_{1},u_{2}\in H^{-1}(\Omega).

(6)

Proof.

See
[
29
, Theorem 4-3.1]
or
[
20
, Theorem II-2.1]
.

Note that
S
is—modulo the Riesz isomorphism—precisely the metric projection
in
(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\|\cdot\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)})
onto the set
K
.
For
L^{p}
-inputs, we also
have the following Lipschitz continuity result for the mapping
S
.

Proposition 3
(
L^{p}
-Lipschitz estimate).

Let
p>\max(1,2d/(d+2))
and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{p}:=\begin{cases}[1,\infty]&\text{ if }p>\frac{d}{2},\\
\left[1,\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{2}{d}\right)^{-1}\right)&\text{ if }p\leq\frac{d}{2},\end{cases}

(7)

with the convention
0^{-1}:=\infty
in the case
p=d/2
.
Then, for every
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{p}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert S(u_{1})-S(u_{2})\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall u_{1},u_{2}\in L^{p}(\Omega).

(8)

Proof.

This result follows from the Sobolev embeddings and
classical truncation arguments.
See, e.g.,
[
11
, Lemma 3.7]

where the assertion of the proposition is proved in the unilateral case.
The proof for the bilateral case is completely analogous.

Recall that
p>\max(1,2d/(d+2))
implies
L^{p}(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\hookrightarrow}\limits^{\vbox to0.0pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\textup{c}$}\vss}}}H^{-1}(\Omega)

by the Sobolev embeddings.
Thus,
S(u_{i})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
in
proposition

3
is well defined for
u_{i}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.
Note further that the functions
S(u_{i})
in (
8
)
might not belong to
L^{q}(\Omega)
due to the low regularity of

\phi
and
\psi
. Only the difference
S(u_{1})-S(u_{2})
is known to be in
L^{q}(\Omega)
;
cf.
[
11
, Lemma 3.7]
and the comments after
[
11
, Corollary 4.2]
.
Next, we state a classical directional differentiability result for (
VI
)
that goes back to
[
15
,
24
]
.

Proposition 4
(Hadamard directional differentiability).

The solution operator

S\colon H^{-1}(\Omega)\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
u\mapsto y
,
of (
VI
) is Hadamard directionally differentiable.
The directional derivative
\delta:=S^{\prime}(u;h)
of
S
at
a point
u
with state
y:=S(u)

in direction

is uniquely characterized by the variational inequality

\delta\in\mathcal{K}(u),\qquad\left\langle-\Delta\delta-h,z-\delta\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\geq 0\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{K}(u).

(9)

Here,

\mathcal{K}(u):=\mathcal{T}(y;K)\cap\mathrm{ker}(\Delta y+u)

is the so-called

associated with

.

Proof.

See
[
7
, Corollary 3.4.3]

and also the classical works
[
15
,
24
]
.

Remark 5
.

The proof of
proposition

4

relies on the so-called
polyhedricity
of the set

,
i.e., the fact that, for all
u\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
with
state
y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, one has

\mathrm{cl}_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\left(\mathcal{R}(y;K)\cap\mathrm{ker}(\Delta y+u)\right)=\mathcal{T}(y;K)\cap\mathrm{ker}(\Delta y+u).

(10)

A tangible characterization of the critical cone

appearing on the right-hand side of (

can be obtained by using the notion of quasi-everywhere

.
For details on this topic, we refer to
[

, section 2.2]
,

[
15
,
24
,
38
]
,
and the references therein.

Using (

one can check that

of Gâteaux points of

if and only if the critical cone

From the Lipschitz estimate (

Theorem 6
(Rademacher’s theorem).

The set

.

Proof.

See
[

In tandem with (

in

\partial_{B}^{sw}S\colon H^{-1}(\Omega)\rightrightarrows\mathscr{L}(H^{-1}(\Omega),H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))

of the solution operator
S\colon H^{-1}(\Omega)\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

of (

).

Lemma 7
(boundedness and nonemptyness of
\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)
).

The set
\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)
is nonempty for all
u\in H^{-1}(\Omega)

and it holds

\lVert G\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{-1}(\Omega),H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))}\leq 1\quad\forall G\in\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)\quad\forall u\in H^{-1}(\Omega).

(11)

Proof.

From (
6
), it follows that

\lVert S^{\prime}(u)\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{-1}(\Omega),H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))}\leq 1

holds for all
u\in\mathcal{D}_{S}
. In combination with the Banach–Alaoglu theorem
for the WOT,
theorem

6
, and the definition of
\partial_{B}^{sw}S
,
this yields
\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)\neq\emptyset
for all
u\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
;
see
[
11
, Theorem 2.5, Corollary 2.6]
.
To establish (
11
),
it suffices to note that,
for all
u,h\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
and all

G\in\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)

with associated Gâteaux points
\{u_{n}\}
as in the definition of
\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert Gh\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lVert S^{\prime}(u_{n})h\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert h\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}

by the weak sequential lower semicontinuity of the norm.
This completes the proof.

The nonemptyness of
\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)
makes
the strong-weak Bouligand differential a suitable candidate
for a Newton derivative of
S
; cf.
theorems

11
,
13
, and
40
below.
As a final preparation for our analysis,
we note the following.

Lemma 8
(norm of derivatives).

Let
u,h\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
be given.

(i)

The derivative
\delta:=S^{\prime}(u;h)
of
S
at
u
in
direction
h
satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert\delta\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}=\langle h,\delta\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}.

(12)

(ii)

For all
G\in\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)
, the function
\delta:=Gh
satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert\delta\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\left\langle h,\delta\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{K}(u)
is a cone,
we may choose
z=0
and
z=2\delta
in (
9
). This yields

\left\langle-\Delta\delta-h,\delta\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=0
and, thus, (
12
).
Suppose now that an operator

G\in\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)
is given and that
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{S}
is a sequence of
Gâteaux points for
G
as in the definition of
\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u)
. In this situation,
we have
S^{\prime}(u_{n})h\rightharpoonup Gh
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and,
by the weak sequential lower semicontinuity of the norm
and
(i)
,

\displaystyle\lVert Gh\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lVert S^{\prime}(u_{n})h\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\langle h,S^{\prime}(u_{n})h\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=\left\langle h,Gh\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}.

This establishes the assertion of
(ii)
and completes the proof.

Remark 9
.

Lemma

8
relies crucially
on the polyhedricity of the set
K
.
Indeed, for nonpolyhedric sets, the variational inequality (
9
)
typically contains an additional curvature term that causes
(
12
) to be invalid and whose behavior for varying
u

is hard to control; see
[
7
, Theorem 1.4.1(ii)]

and
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
.

4
Energy space Newton differentiability for the unilateral case

With the preliminaries in place,
we are in the position to turn our attention
to the proof of the first main result of this paper—the
Newton differentiability of the solution operator of the unilateral
obstacle problem with values in the energy space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Assumption 10
(standing assumptions for
section

4
).

•

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, is a nonempty, open, and bounded set;

•

\psi\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty]
is measurable,
\phi\equiv\infty
,
and
K\neq\emptyset
.

The point of departure for our analysis is the following result
that has recently been obtained in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 11
(
L^{2}
-Newton differentiability for the unilateral obstacle problem).

The solution map
S

of (
VI
)
is Newton differentiable
as a function

S\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)

for all
\max(1,2d/(d+2))<p\leq\infty

with Newton derivative
DS:=\partial_{B}^{sw}S
.

Proof.

See
[
11
, Corollaries 3.9, 4.2]
.

Remark 12
.

The function

S
is even Newton differentiable with values in
L^{q}(\Omega)

for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{p}\setminus\{\infty\}
where
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
is the set in (
7
).
Stating this generalized Newton differentiability result requires some care though
since
S
might not map into
L^{q}(\Omega)

due to the (possibly) low regularity of
\psi
.
The interested reader is referred to

[
11
]
.

To establish the Newton differentiability of
S
as a function with
values in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, we combine

theorem

11

with
lemma

8
.

Theorem 13
(
H_{0}^{1}
-Newton differentiability for the unilateral obstacle problem).

The solution map
S

of (
VI
)
is Newton differentiable
as a function

S\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

for all
\max(1,2d/(d+2))<p\leq\infty

with Newton derivative
DS:=\partial_{B}^{sw}S
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
p<\infty
.
(If the claim is proved for such
p
, then the assertion
for
p=\infty
follows immediately by Hölder’s inequality.)
In order to prove the theorem,
it suffices to show
that, for all
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
and all
sequences
\{t_{n}\}\subset(0,\infty)
,
\{h_{n}\}\subset L^{p}(\Omega)
, and

\{G_{n}\}\subset\mathscr{L}(H^{-1}(\Omega),H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))

satisfying
t_{n}\to 0
,

\lVert h_{n}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=1
, and
G_{n}\in\partial_{B}^{sw}S(u+t_{n}h_{n})
,
we have

\frac{\lVert S(u+t_{n}h_{n})-S(u)-G_{n}t_{n}h_{n}\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}}{\lVert t_{n}h_{n}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}\to 0;

(13)

cf. the definition of Newton differentiability in (
2
).
Due to the boundedness of
\{h_{n}\}
in
L^{p}(\Omega)

and the compact embedding
L^{p}(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\hookrightarrow}\limits^{\vbox to0.0pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\textup{c}$}\vss}}}H^{-1}(\Omega)
,
every subsequence of
\{h_{n}\}

possesses a further subsequence (not relabeled)
such that

h_{n}\rightharpoonup h
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
and

h_{n}\to h
in
H^{-1}(\Omega)

for some
h\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.
From
theorem

11
,
we further get that

\displaystyle\frac{\lVert S(u+t_{n}h_{n})-S(u)-G_{n}t_{n}h_{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\|t_{n}h_{n}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}\to 0,

and from (
6
) and (
11
), it follows that there exists a constant
C>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{S(u+t_{n}h_{n})-S(u)}{t_{n}}-G_{n}h_{n}\right\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C.

Combining these two observations yields

\frac{S(u+t_{n}h_{n})-S(u)}{t_{n}}-G_{n}h_{n}\rightharpoonup 0\text{ in }H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(14)

Due to the convergence
h_{n}\to h
in
H^{-1}(\Omega)

and
proposition

4
,
we know that the first term on the left-hand side of (
14
)
converges strongly in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
to
S^{\prime}(u;h)
.
Thus,

G_{n}h_{n}\rightharpoonup S^{\prime}(u;h)\text{ in }H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(15)

Define
\delta_{n}:=G_{n}h_{n}
,
\delta:=S^{\prime}(u;h)
.
From
lemma

8
,
we obtain that

\lVert\delta_{n}\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\left\langle h_{n},\delta_{n}\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}~\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert\delta\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}=\left\langle h,\delta\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}.

Due to
the convergence
\delta_{n}\rightharpoonup\delta
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

in (
15
) and
h_{n}\to h
in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
,
it follows

\displaystyle\lVert\delta-\delta_{n}\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\delta_{n}\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}-\lVert\delta\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+2(\delta,\delta-\delta_{n})_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle h_{n},\delta_{n}\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}-\left\langle h,\delta\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}+2(\delta,\delta-\delta_{n})_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\to 0.

Thus,

\delta_{n}\to\delta
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. In summary, we have now shown that

\frac{S(u+t_{n}h_{n})-S(u)}{t_{n}}-G_{n}h_{n}=\frac{S(u+t_{n}h_{n})-S(u)}{t_{n}}-S^{\prime}(u;h)+\delta-\delta_{n}\to 0

holds in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

for the subsequence
\{h_{n}\}
selected after (
13
).
A standard subsequence-subsequence argument
now shows that (
13
) holds. This completes the proof.

Remark 14
.

Explicit formulas for certain elements of the strong-weak Bouligand differential

\partial_{B}^{sw}S
of
the solution map
S
of the unilateral obstacle problem
can be found in
[
28
, Proposition 2.11(i), Theorem 4.3]
.
Under mild additional assumptions,
a precise characterization of the entire differential
\partial_{B}^{sw}S
has been
obtained in
[
28
, Theorem 5.6]
. For a tangible corollary of the results of
[
28
]

that is suitable for practical applications, we refer the reader to

definitions

26
and
27
in
section

6
.

5
Auxiliary results on angled subspaces and orthogonal projections

The purpose of this section is to establish
several auxiliary results on angled subspaces and orthogonal projections
that are needed for the analysis of the bilateral obstacle problem in
section

6
.
For the sake of reusability, we consider a general setting.

Assumption 15
(standing assumptions for
section

5
).

•

H
is a real Hilbert space with
closed unit ball
B_{H}:=\{x\in H\mid\|x\|_{H}\leq 1\}
;

•

W_{1},W_{2}\subset H
are closed subspaces of
H
.

We start by defining
the minimal angle between
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
;
see
[
13
]
and the references therein for more information on this concept.

Definition 16
(minimal angle).

The minimal angle

\alpha_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})

between
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
in
H

is defined to be
the angle in
[0,\pi/2]

whose cosine is given by

c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2}):=\sup\big\{(x_{1},x_{2})_{H}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}x_{1}\in W_{1}\cap B_{H},x_{2}\in W_{2}\cap B_{H}\big\}\in[0,1].

It is clear that

\alpha_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)

and

c_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)

are symmetric.
Via a simple scaling argument, it is immediate that

c_{0}=c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})

is the smallest constant in
[0,1]
for which
the following sharpened Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality holds true:

\displaystyle\lvert(x_{1},x_{2})_{H}\rvert\leq c_{0}\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}\qquad\forall x_{1}\in W_{1},x_{2}\in W_{2}.

Next, we recall
[
13
, Lemma 10 (3)]
.
For convenience, we also include its short proof.

Lemma 17
.

Denote by
P_{i}
the orthogonal projection onto
W_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Then,

\displaystyle\lVert P_{1}P_{2}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H,H)}=\lVert P_{2}P_{1}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H,H)}=c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2}).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup\big\{(x_{1},x_{2})_{H}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}x_{1}\in W_{1}\cap B_{H},x_{2}\in W_{2}\cap B_{H}\big\}

\displaystyle=\sup\big\{(P_{1}x_{1},P_{2}x_{2})_{H}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}x_{1}\in B_{H},x_{2}\in B_{H}\big\}

\displaystyle=\sup\big\{(x_{1},P_{1}P_{2}x_{2})_{H}\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}x_{1}\in B_{H},x_{2}\in B_{H}\big\}=\lVert P_{1}P_{2}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H,H)}.

The symmetry of
c_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)

provides the assertion for
P_{2}P_{1}
.

The next lemma shows
that the sine of the minimal angle
has a geometric meaning too.
It is a quantitative version of

[
13
, Theorem 12 (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(3)]
.

Lemma 18
.

Denote by
s_{0}=s_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})
the largest number in
[0,1]

satisfying

\displaystyle\lVert x_{1}+x_{2}\rVert_{H}\geq s_{0}\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}\qquad\forall x_{1}\in W_{1},x_{2}\in W_{2}.

Then it holds that

s_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})^{2}+c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})^{2}=1.

(16)

In particular,
s_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})
is the sine of the minimal angle

\alpha_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})
.

Proof.

We set
c_{0}:=c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})
.
For all
x_{1}\in W_{1}
,
x_{2}\in W_{2}
,
we have

\displaystyle\lVert x_{1}+x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}^{2}+2(x_{1},x_{2})_{H}+\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}\geq\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}^{2}-2c_{0}\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}+\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}^{2}-\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}=(1-c_{0}^{2})\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2},

hence
s_{0}\geq\sqrt{1-c_{0}^{2}}
.
Similarly,
for
x_{1}\in W_{1}\cap B_{H}
,

x_{2}\in W_{2}\cap B_{H}
,
and
t>0
,
we find

\displaystyle 2(x_{1},x_{2})_{H}

\displaystyle=2(t^{-1/2}x_{1},t^{1/2}x_{2})_{H}=\lVert t^{-1/2}x_{1}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert t^{1/2}x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}-\lVert t^{-1/2}x_{1}-t^{1/2}x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq t^{-1}\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}^{2}+t\lVert x_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}-s_{0}^{2}t^{-1}\lVert x_{1}\rVert_{H}^{2}\leq(1-s_{0}^{2})t^{-1}+t.

Taking
the supremum over all
x_{i}\in W_{i}\cap B_{H}
,
i=1,2
,
and
the infimum over all
t>0

shows that
c_{0}\leq\sqrt{1-s_{0}^{2}}
.
This establishes the claim.

Note that the symmetry
of

s_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)

is not obvious from the definition,
but it holds due to the symmetry of
c_{0}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Finally,
we show the invertibility of a certain
operator
that appears in
section

6
.

Theorem 19
.

Let
P_{i}

denote the orthogonal projection onto
W_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Suppose that
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
enclose a positive angle, i.e.,

c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})<1
.
Then the operator

R_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&P_{1}\\
P_{2}&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathscr{L}(H^{2},H^{2})

(17)

is continuously invertible with

\lVert R_{1}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{2},H^{2})}\leq 4/(1-c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2}))
,
the subspace
W_{1}+W_{2}
is closed, and the
orthogonal projection
P
onto
W_{1}+W_{2}
is given by

P=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}R_{1}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}\\
P_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

Proof.

By a standard Schur complement approach, we
find the factorization

\displaystyle R_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&P_{1}\\
P_{2}&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&0\\
P_{2}&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&0\\
0&\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&P_{1}\\
0&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\lVert P_{2}P_{1}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H,H)}=c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})<1
holds by
lemma

17
,
it follows from the Neumann series that the operator

\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1}

is invertible with

\lVert(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H,H)}\leq 1/(1-c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2}))
.
This implies that
R_{1}
is continuously invertible too with

R_{1}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&-P_{1}\\
0&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&0\\
0&(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&0\\
-P_{2}&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix}

(19)

and

\displaystyle\lVert R_{1}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H^{2},H^{2})}\leq 2\cdot\lVert(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}\rVert_{\mathscr{L}(H,H)}\cdot 2\leq\frac{4}{1-c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})}.

It remains to check that
W_{1}+W_{2}
is closed and that
the map
P\in\mathscr{L}(H,H)
in (
18
) is the orthogonal projection
onto
W_{1}+W_{2}
.
By combining (
18
) with (
19
),
we get that

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&\mathrm{Id}-P_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&0\\
0&(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P_{1}\\
P_{2}-P_{2}P_{1}\end{pmatrix}

(20)

\displaystyle=P_{1}+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1}).

Since
P_{i}=P_{i}^{2}
and
P_{i}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{i})=(\mathrm{Id}-P_{i})P_{i}=0
for
i=1,2
, (
20
) yields that

\displaystyle P^{2}

\displaystyle=P_{1}+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})

\displaystyle=P_{1}+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})

\displaystyle=P.

Hence,
P
is idempotent.
Next, we note that

(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(P_{2}P_{1})^{k}=\mathrm{Id}+P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1}P_{2})^{-1}P_{1}.

(21)

Plugging this series expansion into (
20
) gives

P=P_{1}+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(P_{2}P_{1})^{k}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1}).

(22)

As the orthogonal projections
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are self-adjoint,
(
22
) implies that
P
is self-adjoint too.
Thus,
P
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{range}(P)
by
[
13
, Lemma 4]
.
In particular,
\mathrm{range}(P)
is closed.
It remains to show that
\mathrm{range}(P)=W_{1}+W_{2}
.
From (
20
), we obtain that
PP_{1}=P_{1}
holds
and that

\displaystyle PP_{2}

\displaystyle=P_{1}P_{2}+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})P_{2}

\displaystyle=P_{1}P_{2}+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})P_{2}=P_{2}.

Hence,
W_{1}+W_{2}\subset\mathrm{range}(P)
.
From (
20
) and (
21
), we further obtain that

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=P_{1}-P_{1}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})

\displaystyle=P_{1}-P_{1}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})+\left(\mathrm{Id}+P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1}P_{2})^{-1}P_{1}\right)P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})

\displaystyle=P_{1}-P_{1}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{2}P_{1})^{-1}P_{2}(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1})+P_{2}\left(\mathrm{Id}+(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1}P_{2})^{-1}P_{1}P_{2}\right)(\mathrm{Id}-P_{1}).

This shows that
\mathrm{range}(P)\subset W_{1}+W_{2}
.
Thus,
\mathrm{range}(P)=W_{1}+W_{2}
,
the operator
P
is the orthogonal projection onto
W_{1}+W_{2}
,
and
W_{1}+W_{2}
is a closed subspace.

Remark 20
.

•

The closedness of the set
W_{1}+W_{2}
in
theorem

19

can also be derived directly from the condition
c_{0}(W_{1},W_{2})<1
;
cf.
[
13
, Theorem 12]
.
It is interesting to see
that it is also obtained as a byproduct when analyzing the formula (
18
).

•

From (
22
), we obtain the
series representation

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(P_{1}(P_{2}P_{1})^{k}+(P_{2}P_{1})^{k}P_{2}-(P_{1}P_{2})^{k+1}-(P_{2}P_{1})^{k+1}\Big)

\displaystyle=(P_{1}+P_{2})-(P_{1}P_{2}+P_{2}P_{1})+(P_{1}P_{2}P_{1}+P_{2}P_{1}P_{2})-\ldots

for the orthogonal
projection
P
onto the sum
W_{1}+W_{2}
of two
closed angled subspaces
W_{i}
,
i=1,2
, in a Hilbert space
H

in terms of the orthogonal projections
P_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
onto the spaces
W_{i}
.
Note that,
in contrast to the formulas in (
20
) and (
22
),
this representation is symmetric in
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
.

Energy space Newton differentiability for the bilateral case

We are now in the position to prove the

)
in the case
\psi\not\equiv-\infty
and
\phi\not\equiv\infty
.
For the analysis of this bilateral case, we require slightly more restrictive assumptions
than in
section

.

Assumption 21
(standing assumptions for
section

6
).

•

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, is a nonempty, open, and bounded set;

•

p>\max(1,d/2)
is a given, fixed exponent;

•

\Omega

•

For later reference,
we provide a small lemma.

Lemma 23
.

Let
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
be given.
Then,
\min(v,\phi),\max(v,\psi)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We only prove the claim for

.
From Stampacchia’s lemma,
see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 5.8.2]
,
we get that
\min(v,\phi)\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.
By definition, there exists a sequence
\{v_{n}\}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

with
v_{n}\to v
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
By using Stampacchia’s lemma again,
we get
\min(v_{n},\phi)\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap C_{c}(\Omega)
.
A standard mollification argument provides us with

\min(v_{n},\phi)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
It is easy to check that

\min(v_{n},\phi)\to\min(v,\phi)
in

.
Further, this sequence is bounded in

which yields

\min(v_{n},\phi)\rightharpoonup\min(v,\phi)
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
Since

is a closed subspace,
we infer
\min(v,\phi)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Note that

23
implies in particular that
\min(\phi,\max(0,\psi))\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
holds.
Since
\psi\leq\min(\phi,\max(0,\psi))\leq\phi
,
this shows that the admissible set
K

is nonempty in the situation of

and that the results of

sections

3
and
4
are applicable.

and an argumentation similar

To pursue this approach, we require some additional notation.
Henceforth, we denote by

the solution operator of the obstacle problem (

)
with lower obstacle

\infty

by

the solution operator of the obstacle problem (

)

,

the solution operator of the bilateral obstacle problem

\psi

to

see

. We further introduce the

, and

to denote the multiplier maps

, and

From (

Lemma 24
(higher regularity of multipliers and states).

For all

, we have

M_{\psi}(u),M^{\phi}(u),M_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)\in L^{p}(\Omega)

and

.
Moreover,
the maps
S_{\psi}
,
S^{\phi}
, and
S_{\psi}^{\phi}

are globally Lipschitz as functions from

to
C_{0}(\Omega)
,
and the maps
M_{\psi}
,
M^{\phi}
, and

are globally Lipschitz as functions from

to

Proof.

The assertions of the lemma follow
from the so-called Lewy–Stampacchia inequalities; see
[
35
, Theorem 4.35]
.
We include a proof for the convenience of the reader.
We begin with the bilateral case.
Given a function
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with state
y:=S_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)
, we consider
the solution
\tilde{y}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
of the variational inequality

\tilde{y}\geq y,\qquad\left\langle-\Delta\tilde{y}-\min(-\Delta\phi,u),\tilde{v}-\tilde{y}\right\rangle_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\geq 0\quad\forall\tilde{v}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\tilde{v}\geq y.

(25)

Here, the inequalities
\tilde{y}\geq y
and
\tilde{v}\geq y
are to be understood in the a.e.-sense.
In order to show
\tilde{y}\leq\phi
,
we note that

y\leq\min(\tilde{y},\phi)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
holds;
see

23
.
Choosing

as the test function in (

)
yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{y}\cdot\nabla(\min(\tilde{y},\phi)-\tilde{y})-\min(-\Delta\phi,u)(\min(\tilde{y},\phi)-\tilde{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{y}\cdot\nabla(\min(\tilde{y},\phi)-\tilde{y})-(-\Delta\phi)(\min(\tilde{y},\phi)-\tilde{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\tilde{y}-\phi)\cdot\nabla(\min(\tilde{y},\phi)-\tilde{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\lVert\min(0,\phi-\tilde{y})\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Here, we have again used
[

.
The above shows that
\min(0,\phi-\tilde{y})=0
.
Thus,
\psi\leq y\leq\tilde{y}\leq\phi
a.e. in
\Omega
.
By choosing

as the test function in the VI for

\displaystyle\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}(u-\min(-\Delta\phi,u))(y-\tilde{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 0.

This proves

.
From (

\displaystyle-\Delta y-\min(-\Delta\phi,u)=-\Delta\tilde{y}-\min(-\Delta\phi,u)\geq 0,

[

Hence,

.
Analogously, one also shows that

Thus,

Due to (

as a

proposition

3
.

and

We begin with the following observation.

Proposition 30
.

Suppose that
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)

and
y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
are given.
Then it holds
y=S_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)
if and only if there
exist
\lambda_{\psi},\lambda^{\phi}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
satisfying

\begin{gathered}-\Delta y=u-\lambda_{\psi}-\lambda^{\phi},\qquad\lambda_{\psi}=M_{\psi}(u-\lambda^{\phi}),\qquad\lambda^{\phi}=M^{\phi}(u-\lambda_{\psi}).\end{gathered}

(26)

If
y=S_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)
holds, then the functions

\lambda_{\psi}
and
\lambda^{\phi}
are
unique and one has

\lambda_{\psi}=\min(0,M_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)),\quad\lambda^{\phi}=\max(0,M_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)),\quad\text{and}\quad y=S_{\psi}(u-\lambda^{\phi})=S^{\phi}(u-\lambda_{\psi}).

Proof.

Suppose that

y=S_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)
holds. Then
-\Delta y=u-M_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)
is in
L^{p}(\Omega)

by
lemma

24
and the VI for
y
reads

\int_{\Omega}(-\Delta y-u)(v-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 0\qquad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\text{ with }\psi\leq v\leq\phi\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega.

(27)

By exploiting our assumptions on
\psi
and
\phi
and the continuity of
y
,
and by choosing functions of the form
v=y+z
,
z\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
in (
27
),
one easily checks that

-M_{\psi}^{\phi}(u)=-\Delta y-u\begin{cases}=0&\text{ a.e.\ in }\{\psi<y<\phi\},\\
\geq 0&\text{ a.e.\ in }\{y=\psi\},\\
\leq 0&\text{ a.e.\ in }\{y=\phi\},\end{cases}

(28)

where the sets on the right are defined w.r.t. the
C(\Omega)
-representatives of

y
,
\psi
, and
\phi
.
If we set

\lambda_{\psi}:=\mathds{1}_{\{y=\psi\}}(u-(-\Delta)y)\in L^{p}(\Omega)

and

\lambda^{\phi}:=\mathds{1}_{\{y=\phi\}}(u-(-\Delta)y)\in L^{p}(\Omega)
,
then (
28
) yields that

\begin{gathered}-\Delta y=u-\lambda_{\psi}-\lambda^{\phi},\\
\lambda_{\psi}\leq 0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\qquad y-\psi\geq 0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\qquad\lambda_{\psi}(y-\psi)=0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\\
\lambda^{\phi}\geq 0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\qquad y-\phi\leq 0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\qquad\lambda^{\phi}(y-\phi)=0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega.\end{gathered}

(29)

This implies

y=S_{\psi}(u-\lambda^{\phi})
,

y=S^{\phi}(u-\lambda_{\psi})
,

\lambda_{\psi}=M_{\psi}(u-\lambda^{\phi})
,
and

\lambda^{\phi}=M^{\phi}(u-\lambda_{\psi})

and proves the implication “
\Rightarrow
”
as well as the last sentence of the assertion.

To see that “
\Leftarrow
” also holds,
let us assume that

and

satisfying (

) are given. Then

,

\lambda^{\phi}=M^{\phi}(u-\lambda_{\psi})
,

holds.
By the definitions of
S_{\psi}
,
M_{\psi}
,
S^{\phi}
,
and

, this entails that

,
\lambda_{\psi}
,

, and

satisfy (

This complementarity system implies (

which yields

as desired.

Consider now the Hilbert space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:=H^{-1}(\Omega)\times H^{-1}(\Omega)

(endowed with its canonically induced norm)
and the functions defined by

\begin{gathered}F\colon\mathcal{H}\times L^{p}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{H},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\psi}\\
\lambda^{\phi}\\
u\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\psi}-M_{\psi}(u-\lambda^{\phi})\\
\lambda^{\phi}-M^{\phi}(u-\lambda_{\psi})\end{pmatrix},\end{gathered}

\max(0,M_{\psi}^{\phi}(u))\end{pmatrix}=:\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{\psi}(u)\\
\Phi^{\phi}(u)\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

Then we have the following.

Lemma 31
.

It holds
F(\Phi(u),u)=0\in\mathcal{H}
for all

.

Proof.

See (
30
) and
proposition

.

In what follows, our plan is to
argue as in the proof
of the implicit function theorem to establish the Newton differentiability of

\Phi
as a function from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
\mathcal{H}
.
To pursue this approach, we require a generalized derivative for

.

Definition 32
(Newton derivative of
F
).

We denote by

DF
the set-valued map
from
L^{p}(\Omega)^{3}

to

\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})\times\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(\Omega),\mathcal{H})

given by

\displaystyle DF(\lambda_{\psi},\lambda^{\phi},u):=\left\{\mathcal{G}=\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}&G_{\psi}\\
G^{\phi}&\mathrm{Id}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-G_{\psi}\\
-G^{\phi}\end{pmatrix}\right)\nonscript\;\middle|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\begin{aligned} G_{\psi}&\in DM_{\psi}(u-\lambda^{\phi}),\\
G^{\phi}&\in DM^{\phi}(u-\lambda_{\psi})\end{aligned}\right\}.

The two components of
\mathcal{G}
will be denoted by

\mathcal{G}_{1}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})

and

\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathscr{L}(L^{p}(\Omega),\mathcal{H})
.

Now,
one is tempted to use a standard estimate
to establish that the implicit function

\Phi

is Newton differentiable from
L^{p}(\Omega)

to
\mathcal{H}
with Newton derivative

D\Phi(u):=\{-\mathcal{G}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{2}\mid\mathcal{G}\in DF(\Phi(u),u)\}
.
Given
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
,
h\in L^{p}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
, and

G:=-\mathcal{G}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{2}\in D\Phi(u+h)
with

\mathcal{G}\in DF(\Phi(u+h),u+h)
,
one would try

\displaystyle\frac{\lVert\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)-Gh\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}}{\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}=\frac{\lVert\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)+\mathcal{G}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{G}_{2}h\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}}{\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\lVert\mathcal{G}_{1}\left(\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)\right)+\mathcal{G}_{2}h\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}}{\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}=C\frac{\left\|(\mathcal{G}_{1}~~\mathcal{G}_{2})\begin{pmatrix}\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)\\
h\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}}{\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=C\frac{\left\|F(\Phi(u+h),u+h)-F(\Phi(u),u)-\mathcal{G}\begin{pmatrix}\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)\\
h\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}}{\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq CL_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{\left\|F(\Phi(u+h),u+h)-F(\Phi(u),u)-\mathcal{G}\begin{pmatrix}\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)\\
h\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}}{\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)\\
h\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}.

Here,
C>0
denotes a bound on the
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
-norm
of the inverse
\mathcal{G}_{1}^{-1}
that is
(at least for all small enough

) independent of

and
\mathcal{G}_{1}
(and whose existence,
of course, has to be proved);

\mathcal{X}
is a suitable normed space;
and

is a (local) Lipschitz constant
of the function
(\Phi,\mathrm{Id})^{\top}\colon L^{p}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{X}
; cf.
[
21
]

and the references therein.
Ideally, the last term in (

) would converge to zero for

.

Unfortunately, these standard arguments fail
for all

as they require a space
\mathcal{X}

such that
F
is Newton differentiable as a map from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{H}

and such that
(\Phi,\mathrm{Id})^{\top}
is locally Lipschitz as a function from

to
\mathcal{X}
.
To the best of our knowledge,
such a space
\mathcal{X}
is not available for

as the maps
M_{\psi}
and
M^{\phi}

are Newton differentiable only
from

to

and since the map
M_{\psi}^{\phi}
is
Lipschitz only from

to
H^{-1}(\Omega)\cap L^{1}(\Omega)

for all

and

. (For

in (

) can be shown to work with
\mathcal{X}=L^{1}(\Omega)^{3}
.)
We will therefore argue differently and
exploit the structure of
F
and
\Phi

to show directly that

\hskip-4.26773pt\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in DF(\Phi(u+h),u+h)}\hskip-4.26773pt\frac{\lVert\mathcal{G}_{1}(\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u))+\mathcal{G}_{2}h\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}}{\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}\to 0~~\text{for }0<\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\to 0.

Lemma 37
.

Let
Z\subset\Omega
be nonempty and
compact and let
\zeta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

\zeta\equiv 0

Identify

Proof.

We assume that
\zeta\not\equiv 0

\zeta\equiv 0

Let
f\in L^{p}(Z)
be given
and define

By a duality argument,
we get

\displaystyle\lVert y\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert z\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}yz\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\lVert z\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}f(-\Delta)^{-1}z\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\lVert z\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert(-\Delta)^{-1}z\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

where, in the last step, we used
the estimate

that is a special case of
proposition

3
.
As the support
\mathrm{supp}(\zeta)
of
\zeta
and
the set
Z
are nonempty,
disjoint, and compact, we can further find

such that the set

\displaystyle U:=\{x\in\Omega\nonscript\;|\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\dist(x,\mathrm{supp}(\zeta))<\varepsilon\}

does not intersect

.
Now, the solution

satisfies

\displaystyle(y,-\Delta v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(y,v)_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=(f,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\qquad\forall v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U).

By Weyl’s lemma, see
[

From the mean value property of harmonic maps, it now follows
that the convolution

of

satisfies

on
\mathrm{supp}(\zeta)
and that

Owing to Young’s inequality for convolutions, this gives

\displaystyle\lVert\partial^{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathrm{supp}(\zeta))}\leq\lVert y\rVert_{L^{1}(U)}\lVert\partial^{\alpha}\eta\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=C_{\alpha}\lVert y\rVert_{L^{1}(U)}.

Together with the product rule and (

this finishes the proof.

\displaystyle\sup_{G_{\psi}\in DM_{\psi}(u+h-\Phi^{\phi}(u+h))}\left\|\Phi_{\psi}(u+h)-\Phi_{\psi}(u)+G_{\psi}\left(\Phi^{\phi}(u+h)-\Phi^{\phi}(u)-h\right)\right\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}

(43)

\displaystyle=\sup_{G_{\psi}\in DM_{\psi}(\tilde{u}+\tilde{g}(h))}\left\|M_{\psi}(\tilde{u}+\tilde{g}(h))-M_{\psi}(\tilde{u})-G_{\psi}\tilde{g}(h)\right\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}.

Note that, due to the Lipschitz continuity of

\Phi^{\phi}
from

to

(see

24
and (
30
)),
the fact that

is supported only on

,
and

we know that there exist constants

such that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{g}(h)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\|(-\Delta)\zeta^{\phi}(-\Delta)^{-1}(\lambda^{\phi}(0)-\lambda^{\phi}(h))\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+C_{1}\|\lambda^{\phi}(0)-\lambda^{\phi}(h)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{1}C_{2})\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

In combination with the Newton differentiability of

M_{\psi}
as a function from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
H^{-1}(\Omega)
,
(
43
), and the fact that

was arbitrary,
this allows us to conclude that

\displaystyle\sup_{G_{\psi}\in DM_{\psi}(u+h-\Phi^{\phi}(u+h))}\frac{\left\|\Phi_{\psi}(u+h)-\Phi_{\psi}(u)+G_{\psi}\left(\Phi^{\phi}(u+h)-\Phi^{\phi}(u)-h\right)\right\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}}{\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{1}C_{2})\sup_{G_{\psi}\in DM_{\psi}(\tilde{u}+\tilde{g}(h))}\frac{\left\|M_{\psi}(\tilde{u}+\tilde{g}(h))-M_{\psi}(\tilde{u})-G_{\psi}\tilde{g}(h)\right\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}}{\|\tilde{g}(h)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}\to 0

holds for
(0,r]\ni\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\to 0
, where, in the case
\tilde{g}(h)=0
,
the second fraction is to be understood as zero.
By proceeding analogously with the second component in (

)
and by plugging everything in, we now obtain that

\sup_{G\in D\Phi(u+h)}\frac{\lVert\Phi(u+h)-\Phi(u)-Gh\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}}{\lVert h\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}\to 0\quad\text{ for }0<\|h\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\to 0

holds as desired. This completes the proof.

Lemma 44

Let

\displaystyle DQ(y):=\mathrm{Id}+\nu^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-1}DS_{\psi}^{\phi}\left[\nu^{-1}(-\Delta)^{-1}(y_{D}-y)\right](-\Delta)^{-1}\subset\mathscr{L}(L^{2}(\Omega),L^{2}(\Omega)).

exists and

Proof.

The Newton differentiability of

to
