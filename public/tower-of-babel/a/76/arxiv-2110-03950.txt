Nonconvex-Nonconcave Min-Max Optimization with a Small Maximization Domain
Dmitrii M. Ostrovskii, Babak Barazandeh, Meisam Razaviyayn

Abstract
We study the problem of finding approximate first-order stationary points in optimization problems of the form min x ∈ X ⁡ max y ∈ Y ⁡ f ⁡ ( x , y ) \min_{x\in X}\max_{y\in Y}f(x,y) , where the sets X , Y X,Y are convex and Y Y is compact. The objective function f f is smooth, but assumed neither convex in x x nor concave in y y . Our approach relies upon replacing the function f ⁡ ( x , ⋅ ) f(x,\cdot) with its k k -th order Taylor approximation (in y y ) and finding a near-stationary point in the resulting surrogate problem. To guarantee its success, we establish the following result: let the Euclidean diameter of Y Y be small in terms of the target accuracy ε \varepsilon , namely O ⁡ ( ε 2 k + 1 ) O(\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{k+1}}) for k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} and O ⁡ ( ε ) O(\varepsilon) for k = 0 k=0 , with the constant factors controlled by certain regularity parameters of f f ; then any ε \varepsilon -stationary point in the surrogate problem remains O ⁡ ( ε ) O(\varepsilon) -stationary for the initial problem. Moreover, we show that these upper bounds are nearly optimal: the aforementioned reduction provably fails when the diameter of Y Y is larger. For 0 ⩽ k ⩽ 2 0\leqslant k\leqslant 2 the surrogate function can be efficiently maximized in y y ; our general approximation result then leads to efficient algorithms for finding a near-stationary point in nonconvex-nonconcave min-max problems, for which we also provide convergence guarantees.

Moreau envelope and the notion of FOSP.

In this work we use a stationarity criterion based on the Moreau envelope of the primal function.
To define it formally, we first have to remind some standard definitions; readers familiar with the notion of Moreau envelope for weakly convex functions can safely skip this part, while those looking for more details may refer to
[

,

]

[

Recall that

for

P

Typically

(In order to streamline the presentation while keeping the paper self-contained, we provide necessary background on weakly convex functions and weak subdifferentials in

This allows to define the

Definition 1

(Moreau envelope)
.

Let

be

-weakly convex. Given an

\mathaccentV{bar}016\lambda
-Moreau envelope of

to

8

(corresponding to

).

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[

, Lemma 2.2]
) that

is

-smooth when
\mathaccentV{bar}016\lambda>\lambda
, and moreover it has

-Lipschitz gradient whenever
\mathaccentV{bar}016\lambda=(1+c)\lambda
for

.
The standard practice (see, e.g.,
[

]
) is to simply choose
\mathaccentV{bar}016\lambda=2\lambda
and use the gradient norm

as the accuracy measure. Indeed, from the first-order optimality conditions for

one can easily conclude (cf., e.g.,
[

) that

for arbitrary

within

has a subgradient at

Definition 2

(Approximate first-order stationary points)

Taylor expansion and the surrogate problem.

Let us formally define the Taylor expansion of

to be used from now on. Recall that our approach relies on replacing

In particular, in the most practically important cases

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=f(x,\hat{y})+\left\langle\nabla_{\textsf{{y}}}f(x,\hat{y}),y-\hat{y}\right\rangle,\quad\text{and}

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\hat{f}_{1}(x,y)+\tfrac{1}{2}\langle y-\hat{y},\nabla^{2}_{{\textsf{{y}}}{}^{2}}f(x,\hat{y})(y-\hat{y})\rangle.

From now on, we focus on the

Appendix D
Background on weak convexity and the Moreau envelope notion

D.1
Characterization of weakly convex functions

Let
E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}
be a finite-dimensional Euclidean space with inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and norm
\|x\|=\sqrt{\left\langle x,x\right\rangle}
. Moreover, we identify with
E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}
with its dual space
E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}^{*}
(in particular
\|\cdot\|^{*}=\|\cdot\|
). Also we let
\mathaccentV{bar}016\mathds{R}=(\infty,+\infty]
and tacitly assume that all arising convex functions are lsc and proper (not identically
+\infty
).
Recall that any such function
\phi:E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}\to\mathaccentV{bar}016\mathds{R}
admits a variational representation as the pointwise supremum over a non-empty family of affine functions, and this is in fact a criterion (see, e.g.,
[
99
, Thm. 8.13]
).
The set of all affine minorants of
\phi
at given
x\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}
generates the corresponding set of linear functionals,
called the
subdifferential of
\phi
at
x
and denoted
\partial\phi(x)
—i.e.,

\partial\phi(x):=\{\xi\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}:\;\phi(x^{\prime})\geqslant\phi(x)+\langle\xi,x^{\prime}-x\rangle\;\;\forall x^{\prime}\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}\}.

(87)

This set is non-empty at any interior point of
effective domain

{\rm dom}(\phi):=\{x\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}:\phi(x)<+\infty\}
, and its elements are called
subgradients of
\phi
at
x
.
(For simplicity, we assume
{\rm int}({\rm dom}(\phi))
is non-empty.)

Definition 3
.

\phi:E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}\to\mathaccentV{bar}016\mathds{R}
is called
\lambda
-weakly convex (for
\lambda\geqslant 0
) if the function

\phi(\cdot)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}

is convex.

From the above characterization of (proper, lsc) convex functions in terms of subdifferentials it immediately follows that
\phi
is
\lambda
-weakly convex if and only if the function

\phi_{\lambda,x}(\cdot):=\phi(\cdot)+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot-x\|^{2}

is convex for any
x\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}
.
Indeed, by
definition

3

\phi_{\lambda,0}
is convex; meanwhile, for any
x\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}
we have

\phi_{\lambda,x}(u)=\phi_{\lambda,0}(u)+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\left(\|u-x\|^{2}-\|u\|^{2}\right).

By applying
eq.

87
to
\phi_{\lambda,0}
we see that
\phi_{\lambda,x}
has subdifferential

\partial\phi_{\lambda,x}(u)=\partial\phi_{\lambda,0}(u)-\lambda x

(the difference being in the Minkowski sense), and thus is convex with
{\rm dom}(\phi_{\lambda,x})={\rm dom}(\phi)
.
It is then natural to define the
weak subdifferential
of
\phi
at
x
by

\partial\phi(x)=\partial\phi_{\lambda,x}(x).

Equivalently,
\partial\phi(u)=\partial\phi_{\lambda,x}(u)+\lambda(x-u)
for any
u,x\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}
—or, in other words,

\partial\phi(x)=\{\xi\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}:\;\phi(x^{\prime})\geqslant\phi(x)+\langle\xi,x^{\prime}-x\rangle-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2},\;\;\forall x,x^{\prime}\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}\}.

(88)

Thus,
\partial\phi(x)
belongs to the Fréchet (local) subdifferential at
x
, defined as the set of
\xi\in E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}
satisfying

\phi(x^{\prime})\geqslant\phi(x)+\langle\xi,x^{\prime}-x\rangle+o(\|x^{\prime}-x\|)\quad\text{as}\;\;x^{\prime}\to x.

(89)

cf.
[
99
, Def. 8.3]
. Generally, the property in
eq.

89
is much weaker than that in
eq.

88
;
however, for weakly convex functions the weak and Fréchet subdifferentials coincide (see
[
99
, Theorem 12.17]
).

Hessian-based criterion.

Recall Alexandrov’s theorem (
[
100
]
, see also
[
101
, Thm. 2.1]
): a convex
\phi:E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}\to\mathaccentV{bar}016\mathds{R}
is twice differentiable almost everywhere
(and hence
\nabla^{2}\phi\succcurlyeq 0
) in the interior of its domain.
Under
C^{1}
regularity it is a criterion: if
\phi:E_{{\textsf{{x}}}}\to\mathaccentV{bar}016\mathds{R}
is continuously differentiable on
{\rm int}({\rm dom}(\phi))
, and
\nabla^{2}\phi\succcurlyeq 0
almost everywhere on
{\rm int}({\rm dom}(\phi))
, then
\phi
is convex.
From Definition
3
we see that this criterion extends to
\lambda
-weakly convex functions if we replace
\nabla^{2}\phi\succcurlyeq 0
with
\nabla^{2}\phi\succcurlyeq-\lambda I
.

D.2
Upper envelope as a weakly convex function

Let

\phi\to X

\lambda

\nabla\phi

\|\nabla\phi(x^{\prime})-\nabla\phi(x)\|\leqslant\lambda\|x^{\prime}-x\|\quad\forall x,x^{\prime}\in X,

\phi

\lambda

\nabla\phi(x)

More generally, the upper envelope of a family of such functions is
\lambda
-weakly convex. That is, given

eq.

P

) satisfying
1

\lambda<\infty

\lambda
-weakly convex.
Indeed, note that

definition

3

\varphi(x)+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}=\max_{y\in Y}f_{\lambda,x^{\prime}}(x,y)

\varphi

\lambda
-weakly convex.
Moreover,
\partial\varphi(x)

\nabla_{\textsf{{x}}}f(x,y)

Lemma D.1
.

Let

be continuous and satisfy Assumption

with
\lambda<\infty
,

be convex, and

Then

is the closed convex hull of the set of active gradients:

\partial\varphi(x)=\overline{\textup{conv}}\{\nabla_{\textsf{{x}}}f(x,y^{*}),\forall y^{*}\in\textstyle\Argmax_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\}.

We apply Danskin’s theorem (
[
102

) to the function

\displaystyle\partial\varphi_{\lambda,x^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{\textup{conv}}\{\nabla_{\textsf{{x}}}f(x,y^{*})+\lambda(x-x^{\prime}),y^{*}\in\textstyle\Argmax_{y\in Y}f(x,y)\}.

This holds because each

.
It remains to note that

This procedure is given in pseudocode in

It consists of two steps which we are now about to outline and discuss.

Finding the right basis for
\mathcal{K}_{2m}
.

In the first stage of the algorithm (up to
21
) we construct an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{K}_{2m}
in which the associated linear operator (corresponding to
H
in the initial basis), when restricted to
\mathcal{K}_{2m}

with blocks of size

(which pertains to the two vectors

In other words, we construct
\widetilde{H}\in\mathds{R}^{2m\times 2m}
and

such that

\widetilde{H}=Q^{\top}HQ,

where
Q

Q^{\top}Q=I_{2m}
) and

\widetilde{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}^{\vphantom{\top}}&\beta_{2}^{\top}&&\\
\beta_{2}&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\beta_{m}^{\top}\\
&&\beta_{m}&\alpha_{m}\end{bmatrix},

where each
\alpha_{i}\in\mathds{R}^{2\times 2}
is symmetric and each
\beta_{i}\in\mathds{R}^{2\times 2}
is upper-triangular (so
\widetilde{H}
is pentadiagonal).
In the large-scale context, where
H
is accessed through the
y\mapsto Hy
oracle, the one can construct such a decomposition in a computationally feasible manner via the block Lanczos process
[
103
,
104
]
:

1.

Form
q_{1}\in\mathds{R}^{d\times 2}
as the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of
\{g,\xi\}
.

2.

Compute
\alpha_{1}=q_{1}^{\top}Hq_{1}
and put
\beta_{1}=0_{2\times 2}
. (We let
0_{a\times b}
be the
a\times b

3.

For
t\in\{1,m-1\}

\displaystyle q_{t+1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=Hq_{t}^{\vphantom{\top}}-q_{t}^{\vphantom{\top}}\alpha_{t}^{\vphantom{\top}}-q_{t-1}^{\vphantom{\top}}\beta_{t}^{\top},

\displaystyle(q_{t+1},\beta_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\text{QR}(q_{t+1}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}^{\vphantom{\top}}

\displaystyle=q_{t+1}^{\top}Hq_{t+1}^{\vphantom{\top}}.

Here

is the QR decomposition of a squared matrix

—i.e., the ordered pair

of square matrices such that

,

is orthogonal, and
R

that satisfy the matrix relations

\beta_{2}&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\beta_{t}^{\top}\\
&&\beta_{t}&\alpha_{t}\\
\hline\cr&&&\beta_{t+1}\end{bmatrix}\;\;\text{for}\;\;t\in\{1,...,m-1\},\quad\text{and}\quad Hq_{m}=[q_{1}\;\cdots\;q_{m}]\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
\beta_{m}^{\top}\\
\alpha_{m}\end{bmatrix}

while ensuring that

with

The first part of
algorithm

4

Passing to the equivalent problem and solving it.

Once
\widetilde{H}
and

and the top eigenvalue

of
\widetilde{H}
(cf.
24

The first of these two steps amounts to
O(m)
inner products, and the second one can be done in
O(m)
arithmetic operations since
\widetilde{H}
is pentadiagonal.
Using these data, we recast the initial maximization problem (cf.
eq.

40
) by changing the variable

to

—i.e., we pass to

\max_{z\in B_{2m}(2\mathsf{R})}\tfrac{1}{2}z^{\top}\widetilde{H}z+\widetilde{g}^{\top}z.

(91)

The new problem is equivalent to

40

\delta

91

is
\delta

The final part of
algorithm

4
(after

eq.

91

1.

We first test if

is negative-semidefinite (by inspecting

a.o.; thus, we find a high-accuracy maximizer—exact one in floating point arithmetic—in
