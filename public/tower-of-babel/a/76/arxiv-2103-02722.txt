Introduction

A phase-type distribution corresponds to the law of
Y:=\inf\{t\geq 0:J_{t}=\star\}
where
\{J_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov jump process with state space
\{1,\dots,p\}\cup\{\star\}

\{J_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
has a block-partitioned initial distribution

\left[\begin{matrix}A&\bm{b}\\

(1.1)

is of the form

g(x)=\bm{\pi}e^{Ax}\bm{b},\quad x\geq 0.

(1.2)

Indeed, the vector
\bm{\pi}e^{Ax}
yields the probabilities of

being in some state

at time

corresponds to the intensity vector of an absorption happening immediately after. Phase-type distributions were first introduced in
[
12

with the aim of constructing a robust and tractable class of distributions on

to be used in econometric problems. A more comprehensive study of phase-type distributions was carried on by Neuts
[
13
,
14
]
, whose work popularized their use in more general stochastic models.

On the other hand, a
matrix-exponential distribution
of dimension
p\geq 1
is an absolutely continuous distribution on
(0,\infty)
whose density function can be written as

f(x)=\bm{\alpha}e^{Tx}\bm{s},\quad x\geq 0,

(1.3)

where
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{p})
is a
p
-dimensional row vector,
T=\{t_{ij}\}_{i,j\in\{1,\dots,p\}}
is a
(p\times p)
-dimensional square matrix, and
\bm{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{p})^{\intercal}
is a
p
-dimensional column vector, all with complex entries. If the dimension need not be specified, we refer to such a distribution simply as
matrix-exponential
. It follows from (
1.2
) and (

) that the class of phase-type distributions is a subset of those that are matrix-exponential, with the inclusion being strict (see
[
15
]
for details on the latter).

Matrix-exponential distributions were first studied in
[
7
,
8
]
through the concept of complex-valued transition probabilities. More precisely, it was shown that certain systems with complex-valued elements can be formally studied by analytical means without assigning a specific physical interpretation to their components. While their method provided mathematical rigour to systems “driven” by complex-valued intensity matrices, it failed to provide a physical meaning to each individual component, as opposed to the case of Markov jump processes with genuine intensity matrices. Later on, it was proved in
[
15
,
5
]
that matrix-exponential distributions have an interpretation in terms of a Markov process with continuous state space, as opposed to the finite-state-space one that phase-type distributions enjoy. Even after the previous physical interpretations of matrix-exponential distributions were discovered, properties of this class of distributions are still not as well understood as they are for its phase-type counterpart. One of the main reasons for this is that continuous state space processes are more difficult to handle, so that studying matrix-exponential distributions by physical means requires a more sophisticated framework. For example, this is the case in
[
2
,
4
,
3
]
where the theory of piecewise-deterministic Markov processes is used to study models with matrix-exponential components. Thus, having a finite-state system interpretation for matrix-exponential distributions available may potentially lead to the discovery of new properties, as it has traditionally been the case for phase-type distributions.

In this paper we give a physical interpretation to
each element
of the parameters
(\bm{\alpha},T,\bm{s})
satisfying:

A1
.

The elements of
\bm{\alpha}
,
T
and
\bm{s}
are real,

A2
.

The dominant eigenvalue of
T
, denoted
\sigma_{0}
, is real and strictly negative.

Since it can be shown that for a given matrix-exponential density of the form (
1.3
) the parameters
(\bm{\alpha},T,\bm{s})
can be chosen is such a way that

[

), the interpretation that we develop essentially completes the picture laid out in
[
7
,
8
]
. Our method, inspired by the recent work in
[
16

, provides a transparent interpretation of

in terms of a finite-state Markov jump process. To do so, we employ the technique known as
exponential tilting
, which means that we focus on the density proportional to
e^{-\lambda\cdot}f(\cdot)
for large enough
\lambda>0
. After we perform this transformation, we construct a Markov jump process on a finite state space formed by two groups: the

and the

, the latter being a copy of the former. Heuristically, jumps within the set of original states or within the set of anti-states occur according to the off-diagonal nonnegative “jump intensities” of

Our main contribution is showing that this mechanism yields the exponentially-tilted matrix-exponential distribution, and by reverting the exponential tilting, providing some probabilistic insight of the original matrix-exponential distribution as well.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2

2
Preliminaries

Exponential tilting, also known as the
Escher transform
, is a technique which transforms any probability density function
f
with support on
[0,\infty)
into a new probability density function
f_{\lambda}
defined by

f_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda x}f(x)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda r}f(r)\mathrm{d}r},\quad x\geq 0,

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the
tilting rate
. The use of exponential tilting goes back at least to
[
10
]
, where it was used to build upon Crámer’s classical actuarial models
[
9
]
. Later on, the exponential tilting method played a prominent role in the theory of option pricing
[
11
]
.

The exponentially-tilted version of a matrix-exponential distribution has a simple form which happens to be matrix-exponential itself. To see this, notice that if
f
is of the form (
1.3
), then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda r}f(r)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda r}(\bm{\alpha}e^{Tr}\bm{s})\mathrm{d}r=\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s},

where we used the fact that
T-\lambda I
has eigenvalues with strictly negative real parts and thus
e^{(T-\lambda I)r}
vanishes as
r\rightarrow\infty
. Thus,

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda x}(\bm{\alpha}e^{Tx}\bm{s})}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}=\left(\frac{\bm{\alpha}}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}\right)e^{(T-\lambda I)x}\bm{s},\quad x\geq 0,

(2.1)

implying that
f_{\lambda}
corresponds to the density function of a matrix-exponential distribution of parameters
\left(\tfrac{\bm{\alpha}}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}},T-\lambda I,\bm{s}\right)
.

Recall that the parameters
(\bm{\alpha},T,\bm{s})
need not have a probabilistic meaning in terms of a finite state space Markov chain, as opposed to the parameters associated to phase-type distributions. For instance, the parameters

\bm{\alpha}=\left(1,0,0\right),\quad T=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}-1&-1&2/3\\
1&-1&-2/3\\
0&0&-1\end{array}\right],\quad\bm{s}=\left[\begin{array}[]{r}4/3\\
2/3\\
1\end{array}\right]

(2.2)

yield a valid matrix-exponential distribution whose density function is given by
f(x)=\tfrac{2}{3}e^{-x}(1+\cos(x))
, and where the dominant eigenvalue of
T
is
-1
(see
[
6
, Example 4.5.21]
for details). In the following section we show how to assign a probabilistic meaning to the exponentially-tilted version of (
2.2
), and more generally to those attaining properties
A1
and
A2
, in terms of a finite-state Markov jump process.

,
x\geq 0
, be the density of the exponentially-tilted matrix-exponential distribution of parameters
(\bm{\alpha},T,\bm{s})
. Define the vectors

\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{+}:=\tfrac{w^{+}}{w^{+}+w^{-}}\bm{\alpha}^{+}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{-}:=\tfrac{w^{-}}{w^{+}+w^{-}}\bm{\alpha}^{-},

and suppose
\varphi_{0}^{\lambda}\sim(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{+},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{-})
. Then,

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{w^{+}+w^{-}}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{\tau\in[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\}\beta(\varphi_{\tau}^{\lambda})\right]

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{w^{+}+w^{-}}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}\left((\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{+},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{-})\exp\left(\left[\begin{matrix}T^{+}-\lambda I&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}-\lambda I\end{matrix}\right]x\right)\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{s}\\
-\bm{s}\end{matrix}\right]\right)\mathrm{d}x,

(3.13)

where
\tau
and
\beta(\cdot)
are defined as in Theorem
3.1
.

Proof.

Equation (
3.11
) implies that

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}w^{+}\alpha^{+}_{i}\left(\bm{e}_{i}^{\intercal}e^{(T-\lambda I)x}\bm{s}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{p}w^{-}\alpha^{-}_{i}\left(-\bm{e}_{i}^{\intercal}e^{(T-\lambda I)x}\bm{s}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{w^{+}+w^{-}}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\tfrac{w^{+}}{w^{+}+w^{-}}\alpha^{+}_{i}\left(\bm{e}_{i}^{\intercal}e^{(T-\lambda I)x}\bm{s}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\tfrac{w^{-}}{w^{+}+w^{-}}\alpha^{-}_{i}\left(-\bm{e}_{i}^{\intercal}e^{(T-\lambda I)x}\bm{s}\right)\right).

(3.14)

Equality (
3.12
) follows from (
3.14
), (
3.6
) and (
3.8
). Equality (
3.13
) follows from (
3.14
), (
3.7
) and (
3.9
).
∎

Example 3.3
.

Let
(\bm{\alpha},T,\bm{s})
be the matrix-exponential parameters corresponding to (
2.2
). As noted previously, these parameters by themselves lack a probabilistic interpretation, so we apply Theorem
3.1
to construct one. For such parameters we take the tilting parameter
\lambda:=\lambda_{0}=2
, leading to the block-partitioned matrices

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}T^{+}-\lambda I&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}-\lambda I\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr|rrr}-3&0&2/3&0&1&0\\
1&-3&0&0&0&2/3\\
0&0&-3&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&1&0&-3&0&2/3\\
0&0&2/3&1&-3&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&-3\end{array}\right],

(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{+},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{-})=(1,0,0,0,0,0),\qquad\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{s}\\
-\bm{s}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{r}4/3\\
2/3\\
1\\
\hline\cr-4/3\\
-2/3\\
-1\end{array}\right],

and
w^{+}=1
,
w^{-}=0
and
\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}=4
. We can then verify that

\displaystyle\frac{w^{+}+w^{-}}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}\left((\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{+},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{-})\exp\left(\left[\begin{matrix}T^{+}-\lambda I&T^{-}\\

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{6}e^{-3x}(1+\cos(x))=\frac{e^{-2x}}{4}\left(\frac{2}{3}e^{-x}(1+\cos(x))\right),

the latter corresponding to the exponentially-tilted matrix-exponential density function
f(x)=\tfrac{2}{3}e^{-x}(1+\cos(x))
.∎

A probabilistic interpretation of
f_{\lambda}
alternative to that of (
3.12
) is the following.

Corollary 3.4
.

Define
\bm{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{p})^{\intercal}:=-(T^{+}-\lambda I)\bm{1}-T^{-}\bm{1}
to be the termination intensities vector from
\mathcal{E}^{o}
or
\mathcal{E}^{a}
, and define
\bm{q}^{\pm}=(q_{1}^{\pm},\dots,q_{p}^{\pm})^{\intercal}
by

q^{\pm}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{s^{\pm}_{i}}{d_{i}}&\mbox{if}&d_{i}>0,\\
0&\mbox{if}&d_{i}=0.\end{array}\right.

Let
\bar{q}:\mathcal{E}^{o}\cup\mathcal{E}^{a}\mapsto\mathds{R}
be defined by

\displaystyle\bar{q}(i^{o})=q^{+}_{i}-q^{-}_{i}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\bar{q}(i^{a})=q^{-}_{i}-q^{+}_{i}\quad\mbox{ for }\quad i\in\{1,\dots,p\}.

Then,

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{w^{+}+w^{-}}{\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)^{-1}\bm{s}}\right)\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{\tau\in[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\}\bar{q}\big(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}\big)\right]

(3.21)

where
\{\varphi^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\tau
are defined as in Theorem
3.2
.

Proof.

First, notice that the jump mechanism of
\{\varphi^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
described in (
3
) imply that for
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau}=\Delta^{o}\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}=i^{o})

\displaystyle=q_{i}^{+},

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau}=\Delta^{a}\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}=i^{o})

\displaystyle=q_{i}^{-},

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau}=\Delta^{o}\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}=i^{a})

\displaystyle=q_{i}^{-},

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau}=\Delta^{a}\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}=i^{a})

\displaystyle=q_{i}^{+},

which in turn implies that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\beta(\varphi_{\tau}^{\lambda})\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}\right]=\mathds{P}\left(\varphi_{\tau}^{\lambda}=\Delta^{o}\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}\right)-\mathds{P}\left(\varphi_{\tau}^{\lambda}=\Delta^{a}\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}\right)=\bar{q}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{\tau\in[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\}\beta(\varphi_{\tau}^{\lambda})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{\tau\in[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\}\beta(\varphi_{\tau}^{\lambda})\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{\tau\in[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\}\mathds{E}\left[\beta(\varphi_{\tau}^{\lambda})\mid\tau,\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{\tau\in[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]\}\bar{q}\big(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}}\big)\right]

and the result follows from (
3.12
).
∎

Though closely related, the interpretation provided by Corollary
3.4
is more suitable than that of Theorem
3.2
for Monte Carlo applications. Indeed, a realization of
\{\varphi^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
may get absorbed in
\Delta^{o}
,
\Delta^{a}
or terminated. If termination is the case, such realization contributes nothing to the term in the r.h.s. of (
3.12
). In contrast, by observing the process until its exit time of
\mathcal{E}^{o}\cup\mathcal{E}^{a}
and disregard its landing point as in Corollary
3.4
, we make sure that each realization contributes towards the mass in the r.h.s. of (
3.21
).

4
Recovering the untilted distribution

Once the exponentially-tilted density
f_{\lambda}
of a matrix-exponential distribution of parameters
(\bm{\alpha},T,\bm{s})
has a tractable known form, say as in (
3.13
), in principle it is straightforward to recover the original untilted density
f
by taking

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)\bm{s})e^{\lambda x}f_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=(w^{+}+w^{-})(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{+},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}^{-})\exp\left(\left[\begin{matrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{matrix}\right]x\right)\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{s}\\
-\bm{s}\end{matrix}\right],\qquad x\geq 0.

(4.1)

While (
4.1
) is a legitimate matrix-exponential representation of
f
, it has two drawbacks:

1.

The matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
may no longer be a subintensity matrix.

2.

The dominant eigenvalue of
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
may be nonnegative.

The first item may impact the probabilistic interpretation of
f
, while the second one may make integration of certain functions (with respect to the density
f
) more difficult to handle. For instance, in the context of Example
3.3
, the matrix

\left[\begin{matrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr|rrr}-1&0&2/3&0&1&0\\
1&-1&0&0&0&2/3\\
0&0&-1&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&1&0&-1&0&2/3\\
0&0&2/3&1&-1&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&-1\end{array}\right]

is not a subintensity matrix since some row sums are strictly positive, and it has
0
as its dominant eigenvalue. Having
0
as an eigenvalue implies that some entries of
\exp\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right)x\right)
can potentially be of order
e^{0\cdot x}=1
, meaning that the matrix-integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)\exp\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right]x\right)\mathrm{d}x

(4.2)

may only be well-defined for functions
h:\mathds{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathds{R}_{+}
that decrease to
0
fast enough. In comparison,
\exp(Tx)
with
T
as in (
2.2
) has entries of at most
e^{\sigma_{0}x}=e^{-x}
, so that

\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)\exp\left(Tx\right)\mathrm{d}x

(4.3)

is well-defined and finite for every function
h:\mathds{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathds{R}_{+}
of the order
e^{\sigma x}
for any
\sigma<1
. This apparent disagreement between the applicability of (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) vanishes when we multiply
\exp\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right]x\right)
with
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{s}\\
-\bm{s}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Indeed, in the context of Example
3.3
it can be verified that the elements of the vector
\exp\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right]x\right)\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{s}\\
-\bm{s}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
are at most of order
e^{-x}
, with the higher order terms of
\exp\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}T^{+}&T^{-}\\
T^{-}&T^{+}\end{smallmatrix}\right]x\right)
cancelling each other when we multiply the matrix-function by
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\bm{s}\\
-\bm{s}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. In the general case, this “cancellation” of higher order terms than those of
e^{\sigma_{0}x}
occuring
after
the matrix multiplication can be directly deduced from Theorem
3.1
.

In terms of expectations, (
3.12
) and (
3.21
) provide alternative ways to recover properties of any matrix-exponential density
f
of parameters
(\bm{\alpha},T,\bm{s})
in terms of the exponentially-tilted density
f_{\lambda}
. Indeed, for any function
h:\mathds{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathds{R}_{+}
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)f(x)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)f(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(\bm{\alpha}(\lambda I-T)\bm{s})\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x)e^{\lambda x}f(x)\mathrm{d}x

(4.4)

\displaystyle=(w^{+}+w^{-})\mathds{E}\left[h(\tau)e^{\lambda\tau}\beta(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau})\right]

(4.5)

\displaystyle=(w^{+}+w^{-})\mathds{E}\left[h(\tau)e^{\lambda\tau}\bar{q}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{\tau^{-}})\right],

(4.6)

where
\{\varphi^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\tau
are as in Theorem
3.2
. Notice that, as oppossed to the formula in (
4.1
), representations (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) still have probabilistic interpretations in terms of the Markov jump process
\{\varphi^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
