Harnessing The Collective Wisdom: Fusion Learning Using Decision Sequences From Diverse Sources
Trambak Banerjee, Bowen Gang, Jianliang He, [1ex] University of Kansas, Fudan University

Abstract
Learning from the collective wisdom of crowds is related to the statistical notion of fusion learning from multiple data sources or studies. However, fusing inferences from diverse sources is challenging since cross-source heterogeneity and potential data-sharing complicate statistical inference. Moreover, studies may rely on disparate designs, employ myriad modeling techniques, and prevailing data privacy norms may forbid sharing even summary statistics across the studies for an overall analysis. We propose an Integrative Ranking and Thresholding ( IRT ) framework for fusion learning in multiple testing. IRT operates under the setting where from each study a triplet is available: the vector of binary accept-reject decisions on the tested hypotheses, its False Discovery Rate (FDR) level and the hypotheses tested by it. Under this setting, IRT constructs an aggregated and nonparametric measure of evidence against each null hypotheses, which facilitates ranking the hypotheses in the order of their likelihood of being rejected. We show that IRT guarantees an overall FDR control if the studies control their respective FDR at the desired levels. IRT is extremely flexible, and a comprehensive numerical study demonstrates its practical relevance for pooling inferences. A real data illustration and extensions to alternative forms of Type I error control are discussed.



Keywords:
Crowdsourcing, E-values, False Discovery Rate, Integrative inference, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Learning from the wisdom of crowds is the process of synthesizing the collective wisdom of disparate participants for performing related tasks, and has evolved into an indispensable tool for modern scientific inquiry. This process is also known as ‘Crowdsourcing’, which has generated tremendous societal benefits through novel drug discovery

Synthesizing the collective wisdom of crowds is related to the statistical notion of fusion learning from multiple data sources or studies
(
Liu
et al. 2022
;

et al. 2023
)
. Over the past decade, techniques for such fusion learning have found widespread use for incorporating heterogeneity into the underlying analysis and increasing the power of statistical inference. For instance, in recent years the accrescent volume of gene expression data available in public data repositories, such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)
(

, a hybrid federated tool, allows conducting privacy-aware genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on distributed datasets.
In distributed multisensor systems, such as wireless sensor networks, each node in the network evaluates its designated region in space by conducting simultaneous test of several hypotheses. The decision sequence is then transmitted to a fusion center for processing. Sensor fusion techniques are used to synthesize the data from multiple sensors, which provides improved accuracy and statistically powerful inference than that achieved by the use of a single sensor alone
(

;

However, fusing inferences from diverse sources

First

For instance, in microarray meta-analysis, an integrative false discovery rate (FDR) analysis of the multiple hypotheses often requires making additional assumptions such as study independence and strong modeling assumptions to capture between-study heterogeneity.

Besides introducing algorithmic randomness into the individual inferences, it is also unclear how such inferences can be integrated and subsequently interpreted.
Fourth
, often significant statistical and computational experience is required for integrating the individual inferences. This may preclude investigators without substantial statistical training to perform such analyses.

In this paper, we develop a framework for fusion learning in multiple testing that seeks to overcome the aforementioned challenges of integrative inference across multiple data sources. Our framework, which we call

The proposed fusion learning framework has several distinct advantages.
First

Second
,

1.2
Related literature and main methodological contributions

The
IRT
framework is related to multiple strands of literature, which includes FDR control using
e-
values, integrative multiple testing under data-privacy and algorithmic derandomization. We discuss how
IRT
differs from these existing body of works.

Multiple testing with
e-
values -
Recently, there has been a proliferation of interest in developing methods for simultaneous hypotheses testing using
e
-values. See, for instance,
Wang and
Ramdas 2022
;
Ignatiadis
et al. 2024
;
Chi
et al. 2022
;
Vovk and
Wang 2023
;
Xu and
Ramdas 2023
and the references therein. In these works, the
e-
values are typically constructed from either
p-
values or likelihood ratios. In contrast, the generalized
e-
values
(
Wang and
Ramdas 2022
;
Ren and
Barber 2024
)
in our setting arise from the accept / reject decisions of the corresponding null hypotheses and are constructed in a nonparametric fashion.

Integrative multiple testing under data-privacy -
The literature on the evolving field of integrative multiple testing under data-privacy is also related to our work. For instance, under the restricted data-sharing mechanism of
Wolfson
et al. 2010
,
Liu
et al. 2021
propose an integrative high-dimensional multiple testing framework with asymptotic FDR control. Our work differs from
Liu
et al. 2021
in two main aspects. First, we consider a relatively more stringent data-privacy regime where the studies are only allowed to share their decision sequences, the FDR level and the hypotheses tested by them. Second, the integrative FDR control offered by
IRT
is non-asymptotic if the individual studies guarantee a non-asymptotic control of their respective FDR levels (see Section
4.2
). This aspect is particularly appealing when the underlying multiple testing problem is small-scale.

Algorithmic stability -
Numerous methods have been proposed for mitigating algorithmic randomness in recent years. For instance,
Bashari
et al. 2023
propose a method to aggregate conformal tests for outliers obtained with repeated splits of the same
data set, while controlling the FDR. In the context of high dimensional variable selection,
Ren and
Barber 2024
develop a methodology for derandomizing model-X knockoffs
(
Barber and
Candès 2015
;
Candes
et al. 2018
)
with provable FDR control while

et al. 2023a
;
Dai
et al. 2023b
develop a multiple data-splitting method with asymptotic FDR control to stabilize variable selection results. The
IRT
framework can be similarly viewed as a method for mitigating algorithmic randomness that may potentially arise from the use of myriad multiples testing procedures for FDR control by the
d
studies. Crucially, however, our framework does not rely on hard-to-verify assumptions for FDR control, such as

et al. 2023a

The ensuing discussion summarizes our main methodological contributions.

1.

Construction of evidence indices from binary decisions -
at an intuitive level, a multiple testing rule essentially translates evidence indices, such as
p-
values,
e-
values,
z-
scores, into binary decisions. This paper presents a comprehensive solution to the “reverse testing problem”, which involves converting binary decisions into evidence indices. We choose
e-
value as an evidence index because of its many desirable theoretical properties as pointed out in
Wang and
Ramdas 2022
;
Vovk and
Wang 2021
.
While the conversion of binary decisions to
e-
values has been explored in
Ren and
Barber 2024
;
Li and
Zhang 2023
, these studies are limited to specific testing processes, such as knockoffs and BH, and focus solely on testing rules that guarantee FDR control.
In contrast, we show how
e-
values can be constructed from any testing procedure that guarantees some kind of type I error control, such as FDR,
k-
FWER, and PFER (see Section
7
).

2.

Evidence aggregation via multiplication -
for each hypothesis,
IRT
fuses the evidence indices via their arithmetic mean. However, when inferences are pooled with the goal of higher reliability then such an aggregation scheme often renders
IRT
extremely conservative. In Section
3.2
, we provide precise conditions under which the evidence indices can be fused using a product rule. These conditions are related to the notion of partial exchangeablilty on the set of nulls, commonly used in the conformal inference literature
(
Bates et al. 2023
;
Liang
et al. 2024
)
, and symmetric decision rules, which arise naturally in conventional settings where all hypotheses undergo simultaneous testing without the inclusion of auxiliary side information. The underlying inference pooling framework that relies on this product aggregation scheme is called
IRT
∗
and is, typically, more powerful than
IRT
.

3.

A flexible framework for pooling inferences -

while the proposed framework is extremely simple, it enjoys substantial flexibility with which it can be used in myriad applications. For instance,
IRT
(
IRT
∗
) is applicable when only some of the studies share
p-
values, while for the others only the binary decisions are available. In such a setting,
IRT
(
IRT
∗
) can be used in conjunction with a p2e calibrator, such as those proposed in
Vovk and
Wang 2021
, for pooling inferences (see Remark
3
). Furthermore,
IRT
(
IRT
∗
) can pool inferences across studies for an overall FDR control even when studies employ different type I error control metrics, such as family-wise error rate or per-family error rate control (see Remark
4
). Finally, in many practical settings prior knowledge may dictate that some studies share data amongst themselves or rely on auxiliary side information for multiple testing. In this scenario, a hybrid scheme
IRT H
, that combines
IRT
and
IRT
∗
, can synthesize inferences across all the studies and ensure valid FDR control (see Remark
1
).

4.

Robustness to misspecified null distribution -
when the summary statistics, such as
p-
values, are available from all studies then neither
IRT
nor
IRT
∗
are natural candidates for pooling inferences as they may lead to power loss. This is indeed true as long as the null distribution of the test statistic is correctly specified. The numerical experiments in Section
6
reveal that when the null distribution is misspecified, methods that rely directly on the magnitude of the
p-
values, may fail to control FDR at the desired level. In contrast
IRT, IRT
∗
and
IRT H
are relatively robust to such misspecification. This is an important practical setting since typically the null distribution specification relies on large sample approximations, and such large samples may not be available for some studies.

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\dfrac{m_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\delta_{ij}}{\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0}\vee 1}\right]\cdot\mathbb{I}(i\in\mathcal{M}_{j})\leq\dfrac{m}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\dfrac{m_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}\cdot{\rm FDR}(\bm{\delta}_{j})\leq m,

which completes the proof.
∎

While the

\alpha\geq 2\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}/(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})
. Furthermore, noting that the quantity on the right side of the inequality is the harmonic mean of

, but the combined evidence from the two studies is not sufficient to reject
H_{0i}
unless
\alpha>\min(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
? The reason behind this seemingly counterintuitive situation is that

makes no assumption on the dependence between the two studies. For instance, the two studies could be employing identical data and testing methods to reach the same conclusion. Then the collective evidence should not necessarily be interpreted as stronger than the individual evidence from each study, given that the latter study does not introduce any fresh information. For the evidence to truly accumulate, we often presume a certain level of difference in the data or testing procedures applied in these studies. However, such an assumption may be unwarranted when only binary decisions and specified FDR levels are available. Absent any dependence assumptions, the most logical approach would be to remain conservative in our interpretations. The
IRT
aggregation step described in Section
2.2
follows this principle and aggregates the evidence indices via their arithmetic mean. In fact, it is demonstrated in

e-

3.2
Alternative evidence aggregation scheme

If inferences are pooled with the goal of achieving higher reliability then an important implicit assumption is that, informally, the studies are “different” in some sense. In this section we make this idea precise and present
IRT
∗
, which relies on an alternative evidence aggregation scheme.
We will need the following definitions.

Definition 2

(Partial exchangeability)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}}
be a set of random variables and
\mathcal{I}_{0}
a subset of
\mathcal{M}
.
We say
\mathbf{X}=\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}}
is partially exchangeable on
\mathcal{I}_{0}
if

f(\mathbf{X})=f(\Psi_{i,i^{\prime}}\{\mathbf{X}\})
for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
, where
\Psi_{i,j}
is the permutation that swaps the
i
-th and the
j
-th positions, and
f
is the joint density function of
\mathbf{X}
.

Definition 3

(Symmetric decision rule,
Copas 1974
)
.

A decision rule
\bm{\delta}
is symmetric if

\bm{\delta}(\Psi\{{\mathbf{X}}\})=\Psi\{\bm{\delta}(\mathbf{X})\}

for all permutation operators
\Psi
.

Note that the notion of partial exchangeablilty on the set of nulls is commonly used in the conformal inference literature
(
Bates et al. 2023
;
Liang
et al. 2024
)
while symmetric decision rules arise naturally in conventional settings where all hypotheses undergo simultaneous testing without the inclusion of auxiliary side information. Lemma
1
guarantees that if for each study the summary statistics are partially exchangeable and the testing procedure is symmetric then a scaled version of the evidence indices in Equation (
1
) are actually bonafide
e-
values.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose for each study
j
the following holds: (i) the summary statistics
\{X_{ij}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}

are partially exchangeable on
\mathcal{H}_{0j}
, (ii) the testing procedure is symmetric, and (iii) controls FDR at level
\alpha_{j}
. Then for all
i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}]\leq m_{j}/|\mathcal{H}_{0j}|
with
e_{ij}
defined in Equation (
1
). In particular, if
\pi_{j}\in(0,1)
is a lower bound for
|\mathcal{H}_{0j}|/m_{j}
, then
\pi_{j}e_{ij}
is an
e-
value, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\pi_{j}e_{ij}]\leq 1
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Denote
\theta_{i,i^{\prime}}=(\theta_{i},\theta_{i^{\prime}})
and
{\theta}_{-i,i^{\prime}}^{j}=\{\theta_{k}\}_{k\neq i,i^{\prime},k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}
. For all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}|\theta_{i,i^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0},\theta_{-i,i^{\prime}}^{j}=\zeta]

\displaystyle={\int\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}|\mathbf{X}_{j}]\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{j}|\theta_{i,i^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0},\theta_{-i}^{j}=\zeta)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}\mid\boldsymbol{\delta}_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{j})]\cdot{\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{j}|\theta_{i,i^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0},\theta_{-i}^{j}=\zeta)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{j}}.

By symmetry of the decision rule we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}|\bm{\delta}_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{j})]=\mathbb{E}[e_{i^{\prime}j}|\bm{\delta}_{j}(\Psi_{i,i^{\prime}}\{\mathbf{X}_{j}\})].

(5)

Furthermore, by partial exchangeability we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{j}|\theta_{i,i^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0},\theta_{-i,i^{\prime}}^{j}=\zeta)=\mathbb{P}(\Psi_{i,i^{\prime}}\{\mathbf{X}_{j}\}|\theta_{i,i^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0},\theta_{-i,i^{\prime}}^{j}=\zeta).

(6)

Thus, from equations (
5
) and (
6
) we have
\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}]=\mathbb{E}[e_{i^{\prime}j}]
for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}
. Since

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}]\leq m_{j}
as shown in Theorem
1
, we have
\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}]\leq m_{j}/|\mathcal{H}_{0j}|
for all
i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}
.
∎

Bonafide
e-
values are substantially more flexible than generalized
e-
values. For instance,
e-
values facilitate reliable inferences for individual hypotheses while generalized
e-
values are limited to simultaneous inference across a set of null hypotheses. Furthermore, the only aggregation scheme for generalized
e-
values discussed in the literature is (weighted) arithmetic mean
(
Ren and
Barber 2024
;
Li and
Zhang 2023
)
. In contrast, under independence bonafide
e-
values admit aggregation via multiplication, which allows evidence to “accumulate”.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{j:\mathbb{I}(i\in\mathcal{M}_{j})=1\}
denote the set of studies that test hypothesis
H_{0i}
with
|\mathcal{N}_{i}|=n_{i}
. Given a pre-determined
k\in\{1,\ldots,n_{i}\}
, denote
\mathcal{S}_{ki}
as any
k
element subset of
\mathcal{N}_{i}
,
i\in\mathcal{M}
. Let

be a lower bound of
\min_{j\in[d]}\{|\mathcal{H}_{0j}|/m_{j}\}
and define
A_{i,\mathcal{S}_{ki}}=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{ki}}e_{ij}
. The next theorem shows that under certain conditions
e_{i}=\pi^{k}A_{i,\mathcal{S}_{ki}}
is an

Theorem 3
.

Suppose (i) the conditions in Lemma
1
hold and (ii) the summary statistics
for the
i
-th testing problem,

\{X_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}
are independent
conditional on
\theta_{i}
for all

Let
e_{i}=\pi^{k}A_{i,\mathcal{S}_{ki}}
. Then
\mathbb{E}[e_{i}]\leq 1
for
i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i,\mathcal{S}_{ki}}\right]=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{ki}}\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}]\leq\prod_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{ki}}\dfrac{m_{j}}{|\mathcal{H}_{0j}|}

and thus
\mathbb{E}[\pi^{k}A_{i,\mathcal{S}_{ki}}]\leq 1
.
∎

Theorem
3
facilitates evidence accumulation via a product rule as we can multiply the

∗
.

4.1
Connections to existing aggregation and derandomization procedures

Leveraging e-values for aggregation and derandomization for specific FDR methods has been explored in literature recently
(
Ren and
Barber 2024
;
Li and
Zhang 2023
;
Bashari
et al. 2023
;
Zhao and
Sun 2024
)
. In this subsection, we show that these seemly distinct e-values constructions can be viewed as special cases of our construction in Equation (
1
)
when examined from an asymptotic perspective.

A generic FDR procedure can be described abstractly as follows

1.

(Ranking)
Construct a suitable summary statistics
T_{i}
for for each
H_{0,i}
and rank the null hypotheses according to
T_{i}
.

2.

(FDP Estimation)
For any given
t
estimate the FDP of the decision rule
\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)=\{\delta_{1}(t),\ldots,\delta_{m}(t)\}
, where
\delta_{i}(t)=\mathds{1}(T_{i}\leq t)
. Denote the estimate as
\widehat{\rm FDP}(t)
.

3.

(Thresholding)
For a given target FDR level
\alpha
, define

t_{\alpha}=\sup\{t:\widehat{\rm FDP}(t)\leq\alpha\}
. Reject
H_{0,i}
if and only if
T_{i}\leq t_{\alpha}
.

Denote the above FDR procedure as
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m})
, where
\delta_{i}=\mathbb{I}(T_{i}\leq t_{\alpha})
, then
e-values constructed in
Ren and
Barber 2024
;
Li and
Zhang 2023
;
Bashari
et al. 2023
;
Zhao and
Sun 2024
can be written in the form of

e_{i}=\dfrac{m\delta_{i}}{\widehat{\rm FDP}(t_{\alpha})\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{0}}.

(8)

Note that the denominator in Equation (
8
) can be viewed as the estimated number of false discoveries. In what follows we give two explicit examples showing that the construction in (
8
) and (
1
) are equivalent as
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{0}\rightarrow\infty
.

1.

Asymptotic equivalence to
Ren and
Barber 2024
-
The knockoff filter
(
Barber and
Candès 2015
;
Barber and
Candès 2019
)
is a framework for selecting a set of covariates that are relevant for predicting a response variable
Y
with guaranteed control of the FDR. For each covariate
X_{i}
, it constructs a statistic
W_{i}
that is likely to be large if
X_{i}
is relevant for predicting
Y
conditional on
\{X_{j}\}_{j\neq i}
and has a symmetric distribution around 0, otherwise. The knockoff filter selects
X_{i}
if and only if
W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha}
where

t_{\alpha}=\inf\left\{t>0:\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-t)}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t)}\leq\alpha\right\}.

(9)

Ren and
Barber 2024
show that the following is a set of generalized
e-
values.

e_{i}=\frac{m\cdot\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-t_{\alpha})},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{M},

(10)

We now explain how Equation (
10
) is related to Equation (
1
). Denote
\delta_{i}=\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})
. Then
t_{\alpha}
can be written as

t_{\alpha}=\inf\left\{t>0:1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-t)\leq\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}\right\}.

Let
0<\breve{t}_{\alpha}<t_{\alpha}
be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-\breve{t}_{\alpha})=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-t_{\alpha})
. We then have

\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})\leq\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq\breve{t}_{\alpha})<1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-\breve{t}_{\alpha})=2+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-t_{\alpha}),

where the second inequality follows form the definition of
t_{\alpha}
and
\breve{t}_{\alpha}
. For the decision rule
\delta_{i}=\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})
the evidence index defined in Equation (
1
) becomes
{m\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})}/{(\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0})}
and

\displaystyle\frac{m\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})}{\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\leq-t_{\alpha})}\leq\frac{m\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})}{\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}-1}=\frac{m\mathbb{I}(W_{i}\geq t_{\alpha})}{\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}}\cdot\frac{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}}{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}-\alpha^{-1}},

where the first inequality again follows from the definition of
t_{\alpha}
in Equation (
9
).
Hence, in the context of large-scale inference where
\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}/(\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}-\alpha^{-1})\overset{p}{\rightarrow}1
, Equation (
1
) and Equation (
10
) are asymptotically equivalent.

2.

Asymptotic equivalence to
Li and
Zhang 2023
-

and

, the BH procedure with target FDR level

\delta_{i}=\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t_{\alpha}),\text{~where~}t_{\alpha}=\sup\left\{t\in(0,1]:\frac{mt}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t)}\leq\alpha\right\},

(11)

Li and

show that the following is a set of generalized

e_{i}=t_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t_{\alpha}),\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{M}.

(12)

Define

\breve{t}_{\alpha}=t_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{m}.

(13)

We then have

\alpha\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{0}=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t_{\alpha})\leq\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq\breve{t}_{\alpha})<m\breve{t}_{\alpha}=mt_{\alpha}+1,

(14)

where the first and second inequality follows from the definition of

and

For the BH procedure, the evidence index in Equation (

\displaystyle\frac{m\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t_{\alpha})}{\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}}\leq\frac{m}{mt_{\alpha}}\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t_{\alpha})\leq\frac{m\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t_{\alpha})}{\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}-1}=\frac{m\mathbb{I}(p_{i}\leq t_{\alpha})}{\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}}\cdot\frac{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}}{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}-\alpha^{-1}},

4.2
Asymptotic FDR control

A key requirement for the validity of the
IRT
procedure is that the study-specific multiple testing procedure controls FDR at their pre-specified level
\alpha_{j}
. Theorems
2
and
3
implicitly assume that such an FDR control holds for finite samples, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\delta_{ij}/\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0}\vee 1]\leq\alpha_{j}
for all
j\in[d]
. In reality, however, for some studies their FDR control may be asymptotic in
m_{j}
.
In such a scenario,
the
IRT
procedure in Algorithm
1
guarantees FDR control at level
\alpha
as
m_{j}\to\infty
. We summarize the above discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose study
j
controls FDR at level
\alpha_{j}
asymptotically, i.e.,
{\rm FDR}(\bm{\delta}_{j})\leq\alpha_{j}+o_{p}(1)
. Then, Algorithm
1
ensures that FDR is controlled at level
\alpha
asymptotically.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

We first establish that
\mathbf{e}^{\tt agg}
in Equation (
3
) is a set of generalized
e-
values asymptotically. Let
e_{i}^{\tt agg}
be as defined in Equation (
3
).
Akin to the proof of Theorem
2
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\mathbb{E}[e_{i}^{\texttt{agg}}]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m}{\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\dfrac{m_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\delta_{ij}}{\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0}\vee 1}\right]\cdot\mathbb{I}(i\in\mathcal{M}_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{m}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}m_{j}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{m_{j}}{\alpha_{j}}\cdot(\alpha_{j}+o_{p}(1))\leq m(1+o_{p}(1)).

Next, we consider
e_{i}^{\tt agg*}
from Equation (
7
). Similarly, following the same arguments in Lemma
1
, we can show that
\mathbb{E}[e_{ij}]\leq\pi_{j}(1+o_{p}(1))
. Thus, using the same notation as in Theorem
3
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\pi^{k}A_{i,\mathcal{S}_{ki}}]\leq 1+o_{p}(1)
. It follows that
\mathbf{e}^{\tt agg*}=\{e_{i}^{\tt agg*}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}}
is also a set of asymptotic generalized
e-
values. Let
\bm{\delta}=\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m}\}
be the decision of
e-
BH applied on a set of asymptotic generalized
e-
values
\mathbf{e}^{\tt agg}
. Note that
\delta_{i}=1
indicates that
e_{i}^{\texttt{agg}}\geq m/(\alpha\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}\vee 1)
based on the decision rule of
e-
BH procedure. Thus, it holds that

\displaystyle\text{FDR}(\bm{\delta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\dfrac{\mathbb{I}(\delta_{i}=1,\theta_{i}=0)}{\|\bm{\delta}\|_{0}\vee 1}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\dfrac{\alpha}{m}\cdot e_{i}^{\texttt{agg}}\mathbb{I}(\delta_{i}=1,\theta_{i}=0)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}e_{i}^{\tt agg}\mathbb{I}(\theta_{i}=0)\right]=\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot m(1+o_{p}(1))=\alpha+o_{p}(1),

where the first inequality results from
\mathbb{I}(X>t)\leq X/t
holds for all non-negative random variable
X
.

4.3
When original
p-
values are available

While the
IRT
framework takes
\mathcal{D}_{j}
as an input from each study
j
, it is important to consider the setting where study-specific
p-
values, denoted by
\{p_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i},i\in\mathcal{M}}
, are available. The goal under this setting is to aggregate the
p-
values pertaining to each hypothesis
i
and then determine an appropriate threshold for FDR control at level
\alpha
using the aggregated
p-
values. However, choosing an aggregation function for combining multiple
p-
values is challenging without making additional assumptions regarding their dependence structure
(
Vovk and
Wang 2020
)
. Furthermore, if the underlying model is misspecified the validity of the corresponding
p-
values may be affected. In contrast,
e-
values are relatively more robust to such model misspecification
(
Wang and
Ramdas 2022
)
and particularly to dependence between the
p-
values
(
Vovk and
Wang 2021
)
.

In this section, we consider the following calibrator from Equation (B.1) in
Vovk and
Wang 2021
for transforming
p_{ij}
into corresponding
e-
values:

e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}(\kappa)=\begin{cases}\infty~&\text{if~}p_{ij}=0\\
\dfrac{\kappa(1+\kappa)^{\kappa}}{p_{ij}(-\log p_{ij})^{1+\kappa}}~&\text{if~}p_{ij}\in(0,e^{-\kappa-1}]\\
0~~&\text{if~}p_{ij}\in(e^{-\kappa-1},1]\end{cases},

(15)

where we choose
\kappa=1
throughout the paper following the recommendation, and write
e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}:=e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}(1)
.
Note that
e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}
as defined in Equation (
15
) are bonafide
e-
values. Therefore, to aggregate
e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}
we can simply take the average

{e}_{i}^{\texttt{{P2E},agg}}=\dfrac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}~\mathbb{I}(i\in\mathcal{M}_{j}).

(16)

Furthermore, if the
p-
values
\{p_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}
are independent given
\theta_{i}=0
then, in the spirit of Equation (
7
), we can aggregate
e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}
through multiplication as follows:

{e}_{i}^{\texttt{{P2E},agg*}}=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{i}}\binom{n_{i}}{k}^{-1}\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{ki}\in\mathcal{B}_{ki}}\Big[\prod_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{ki}}e_{ij}^{\texttt{P2E}}\Big].

We denote the method that applies the
e-
BH procedure on
{e}_{i}^{\texttt{{P2E},agg}}
and
{e}_{i}^{\texttt{{P2E},agg*}}
as
P2E
and
P2E
∗
, respectively. In Sections
5
and
6
we evaluate the numerical performance of
P2E
and
P2E
∗
vis-a-vis
IRT
and
IRT
∗
.

IRT for alternative forms of Type I error control

Sections
2
and
3
present
IRT
for integrative FDR control. Here, we extend
IRT
to alternative forms of Type I error control.

7.1
IRT
for
k
-Family-wise error rate (
k
-FWER) control

We consider a setting where study
j
controls the
k_{j}-
FWER at level
\alpha_{j}
, i.e.

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\delta_{ij}\geq k_{j}\right)\leq\alpha_{j},\quad\forall j\in[d].

(17)

Under this setting, the three steps of the
IRT
procedure are as follows.

1.

Evidence construction.
Suppose
k_{j}>1
. We consider the following evidence index,

e_{ij}=\dfrac{m_{j}\delta_{ij}}{\max\{c_{1}\alpha_{j}\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0},c_{2}(k_{j}-1),\alpha_{j}\}},\ \text{where}~c_{1},c_{2}>0~\text{and}~\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}=1,\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{M}_{j}.

(18)

If study
j
satisfies Equation (
17
) then the evidence indices in Equation (
18
) are generalized
e-
values. To see this, let
V_{j}
denote the number of false rejections made by study
j
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\dfrac{m_{j}\delta_{ij}}{\max\{c_{1}\alpha_{j}\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0},c_{2}(k_{j}-1),\alpha_{j}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\dfrac{m_{j}\delta_{ij}}{\max\{c_{1}\alpha_{j}\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0},c_{2}(k_{j}-1),\alpha_{j}\}}\bigg|V_{j}>k_{j}\right]\mathbb{P}(V_{j}\geq k_{j})

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\dfrac{m_{j}\delta_{ij}}{\max\{c_{1}\alpha_{j}\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0},c_{2}(k_{j}-1),\alpha_{j}\}}\bigg|V_{j}<k_{j}\right]\mathbb{P}(V_{j}<k_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{m_{j}}{c_{1}\alpha_{j}}\alpha_{j}+\frac{m_{j}}{c_{2}}\cdot 1=m_{j}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}\right)=m_{j}

We recommend choosing
c_{1}=(2k_{j}-1)/k_{j},c_{2}=(2k_{j}-1)/(k_{j}-1)
. The rationale is that when
\alpha_{j}\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0}=k_{j}
(as both are estimates of the number of false positives),
c_{1}=(2k_{j}-1)/k_{j},c_{2}=(2k_{j}-1)/(k_{j}-1)
is the solution to the following optimization problem

\text{minimize}\ \ \ \\
\max\{c_{1}\alpha_{j}\|\bm{\delta}_{j}\|_{0},c_{2}(k_{j}-1)\}\quad\text{subject to}\quad c_{1},c_{2}>0~\text{and}~\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}=1.

An important special case is
k_{j}=1
. For this setting, we fix
c_{1}=1
in Equation (
18
) and recover the evidence index proposed in Equation (
1
) for integrative FDR control. This is not surprising since any method that controls FWER at level
\alpha_{j}
also controls FDR at level
\alpha_{j}
.

We note that if additional information, such as what procedure study
j
used to control
k_{j}
-FWER, is available then it becomes feasible to devise more powerful generalized
e-
values. For example, if Bonferroni procedure is used (i.e.
H_{0i}
is rejected by study
j
if and only if its
p-
value is
\leq k_{j}\alpha_{j}/m_{j}
), then we can verify that

e_{ij}=\dfrac{m_{j}\delta_{ij}}{\alpha_{j}k_{j}},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{M}_{j},

is also a generalized
e-
value. To see this, observe that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}e_{ij}\right]\leq\frac{m_{j}}{k_{j}\alpha_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\delta_{ij}\right]=\dfrac{m_{j}}{k_{j}\alpha_{j}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\mathbb{P}(\delta_{ij}=1)\leq\dfrac{m_{j}}{k_{j}\alpha_{j}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\dfrac{k_{j}\alpha_{j}}{m_{j}}\leq m_{j}.

2.

Evidence aggregation.
Since the evidence indices in Equation (
18
) are generalized
e-
values, Equation (
3
) provides the evidence aggregation scheme in this setting and Theorem
2
guarantees that these aggregated evidences continue to be generalized
e-
values associated with
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Furthermore, if the conditions of Theorem
3
hold then Equation (
7
) represents the aggregated evidence indices.

3.

k
-FWER control.
Given the generalized
e-
values
\mathbf{e}^{\tt agg}=\{e_{1}^{\tt agg},\ldots,e_{m}^{\tt agg}\}
from Step 2 above,
IRT
rejects
H_{0i}
if and only if
e_{i}^{\tt agg}\geq m/(\alpha k)
. This procedure controls the
k
-FWER at level
\alpha
since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\mathbb{I}\Big(e_{i}^{\tt agg}\geq\frac{m}{\alpha k}\Big)\geq k\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\mathbb{I}\Big(e_{i}^{\tt agg}\geq\dfrac{m}{\alpha k}\Big)\right]\leq\dfrac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\dfrac{e_{i}^{\tt agg}\alpha k}{m}\right]\leq\alpha.

7.2
IRT
for Per-family error rate (PFER) control

Suppose study
j
’s testing procedure controls PFER at level
k_{j}
, that is
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0j}}\delta_{ij}\right]\leq k_{j}
for

j\in[d]
.

1.

Evidence construction.
We consider the evidence index

e_{ij}=\dfrac{m_{j}\delta_{ij}}{k_{j}},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{M}_{j}.

(19)

It is then straightforward to check that
\mathbf{e}_{j}=\{e_{ij}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}
are a set of generalized
e-
value associated with
\mathcal{H}_{0j}
.

2.

Evidence aggregation.
The evidence indices in Equation (
19
) are generalized
e-
values. Therefore, Equation (
3
) continues to provide the evidence aggregation scheme in this setting and Theorem
2
guarantees that these aggregated evidences are generalized
e-
values associated with
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Furthermore, if the conditions of Theorem
3
hold then Equation (
7
) represents the aggregated evidence indices.

3.

k
-PFER control.
Given the Genralized
e-
values
\mathbf{e}^{\tt agg}=\{e_{1}^{\tt agg},\ldots,e_{m}^{\tt agg}\}
from Step 2 above,
IRT
rejects
H_{0i}
if and only if
e_{i}^{\tt agg}\geq m/k
. This procedure controls the PFER at level
k
since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\mathbb{I}\Big(e_{i}\geq m/k\Big)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\mathbb{I}\Big(e_{i}k/m\geq 1\Big)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}(e_{i}k/m)\right]\leq\frac{k}{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}e_{i}\right]\leq k.

Remark 4
.

The
IRT
framework can be used for integrative inference even when studies employ different type I error control metrics. Suppose, for instance, that
d_{1}
studies control
k-
FWER,
d_{2}
control PFER and the remaining
d_{3}
studies control FDR at desired levels, where
d_{i}\subset[d],i=1,2,3
,
\cap_{i=1}^{3}d_{i}=\emptyset
and
\cup_{i=1}^{3}d_{i}=[d]
. Denote
e_{ij}^{d_{1}},j\in d_{1},
as the evidence indices for the
d_{1}
studies from Equation (
18
). Similarly,
e_{ij}^{d_{2}}
and
e_{ij}^{d_{3}}
denote, respectively, the evidence indices for studies
d_{2}
and
d_{3}
from equations (
19
) and (
1
). Then
e_{i}=m\left\{\sum_{r=1}^{3}(\sum_{j\in d_{r}}e_{ij}^{d_{r}}/\sum_{j\in d_{r}}m_{j})\right\},~i\in\mathcal{M}
, are a set of generalized
e-
values under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, and the
e-
BH procedure can be applied to
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}}
if, for example, FDR control is the goal. Furthermore, both
IRT
∗
and
IRT H
are also applicable in this setting if prior knowledge regarding data-sharing or the use of auxiliary side information for multiple testing is available for the
d
studies.

8
Conclusion

IRT
is a general framework for fusion learning in multiple testing that operates on the binary decision sequences available from diverse studies and conducts integrative inference on the common parameter of interest. It guarantees an overall FDR control under arbitrary dependence between the aggregated evidence indices as long as the studies control their FDR at the desired levels. Under additional assumptions on the data generating process for each study (see Theorem
3
),
IRT
∗
and
IRT H
provide more powerful procedures for pooling inferences than
IRT
, while controlling the FDR at the same level. Furthermore, the numerical experiments in Section
6
suggest that when the
p-
values are misspecified, procedures that rely directly on the magnitude of these
p-
values for pooling inferences may fail to control FDR at the desired level. In contrast,
IRT, IRT H
and
IRT
∗
are relatively robust to such misspecification and also retain power. The proposed framework can be extended to other popular forms of Type I error control, such as FWER control, as demonstrated in Section
7
. We conclude this article with a discussion on several avenues for further investigation.

Multiple testing of partial conjunction hypotheses -
A potential extension of our framework lies in multiple testing of partial conjunction (PC) hypotheses (see
Benjamini and
Heller 2008
;
Wang
et al. 2022
;
Bogomolov 2023
for an incomplete list of references) where the goal is to test if at least
u\geq 1
out of the
d
studies reject the null hypothesis
H_{0i},~i=1,\ldots,m
. This can be formulated as testing the following null hypotheses
H_{0i}^{u/d}:\text{fewer than $u$ out of $d$ studies are non-null}
. Such problems arise in the study of mediation effects
(
Huang 2019
;
Dai
et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
, finding evidence factors in causal inference
(
Karmakar
et al. 2021
)
, and replicability analysis
(
Heller
et al. 2014
;
Heller and
Yekutieli 2014
)
. Given the triplet
\mathcal{D}_{j}
from each study, a key challenge in this setting is to construct an aggregation scheme such that the aggregated evidence indices provide an effective ranking of the
m
PC hypotheses,
H_{01}^{u/d},\ldots,H_{0m}^{u/d}
and a cutoff along this ranking can be determined for FDR control. Our future research will be directed towards developing such an evidence aggregation scheme.

Accumulation bias in meta-analysis -
In the context of meta-analysis for
m=1
hypothesis,
Schure and
Grünwald 2019
recently demonstrate that due to the accumulating nature of scientific evidence dependencies arise between study size
d
or
meta-analysis timing and the results of the meta-analysis. This dependence introduces bias in the estimates, termed accumulation bias, and invalidates the sampling distribution of the test statistic, which is often assumed to be standard Normal. Consequently, Type I errors are inflated as the underlying
p-
values are sub-optimal. The authors recommend using a framework involving likelihood ratios for constructing valid tests as no valid
p-
valued based procedure is feasible in this setting. Note that likelihood ratios are
e-
values and so are the aggregated evidence indices underlying
IRT
∗
. Therefore, an interesting extension of our framework will be to evaluate the performance of
IRT
∗
for meta-analysis of
m\geq 1
hypotheses in the presence of accumulation bias.
