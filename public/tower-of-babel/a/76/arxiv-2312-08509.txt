Approximating APS Under Submodular and XOS Valuations with Binary Marginals
Pooja Kulkarni, Rucha Kulkarni, Ruta Mehta

Abstract
We study the problem of fairly dividing indivisible goods among a set of agents under the fairness notion of Any Price Share ( APS ). APS is known to dominate the widely studied Maximin share ( MMS ). Since an exact APS allocation may not exist, the focus has traditionally been on the computation of approximate APS allocations. [ Babaioff et al.(2021) ] studied the problem under additive valuations, and asked ( i ) (i) how large can the 𝖠𝖯𝖲 {\sf{APS}} value be compared to the 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} value? and ( i ​ i ) (ii) what guarantees can one achieve beyond additive functions. We partly answer these questions by considering valuations beyond additive, namely submodular and XOS functions, with binary marginals. For the submodular functions with binary marginals, also known as matroid rank functions ( MRFs ), we show that APS is exactly equal to MMS . Consequently, following [ Barman et al.(2020) ] we show that an exact 𝖠𝖯𝖲 {\sf{APS}} allocation exists and can be computed efficiently while maximizing the social welfare. Complementing this result, we show that it is 𝖭𝖯 {\sf NP} -hard to compute the APS value within a factor of 5 / 6 5/6 for submodular valuations with three distinct marginals of { 0 , 1 2 , 1 } \{0,\frac{1}{2},1\} . We then consider binary XOS functions, which are immediate generalizations of binary submodular functions in the complement free hierarchy. In contrast to the MRFs setting, MMS and APS values are not equal under this case. Nevertheless, we can show that they are only a constant factor apart. In particular, we show that under binary XOS valuations, 𝖬𝖬𝖲 ≤ 𝖠𝖯𝖲 ≤ 2 ⋅ 𝖬𝖬𝖲 + 1 {\sf{MMS}}\leq{\sf{APS}}\leq 2\cdot{\sf{MMS}}+1 . Further, we show that this is almost the tightest bound we can get using MMS , by giving an instance where 𝖠𝖯𝖲 ≥ 2 ⋅ 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{APS}}\geq 2\cdot{\sf{MMS}} . The upper bound on APS , combined with [ Li and Vetta(2021) ] , implies a 0.1222 \penalty\ 0.1222 -approximation for 𝖠𝖯𝖲 {\sf{APS}} under binary 𝖷𝖮𝖲 {\sf{XOS}} valuations. And the lower bound implies the non-existence of better than 0.5 0.5 - APS even when agents have identical valuations, which is in sharp contrast to the guaranteed existence of exact MMS allocation when agent valuations are identical.

Finding fair allocations of indivisible resources is a central problem within economics, game theory, social choice theory, and computer science. Given a set
[n]
of agents and a set
[m]
of indivisible items, the problem asks to partition the items among the agents in a

An allocation where every agent gets at least her

. An attractive feature of

The problem of finding approximate

Numerous real world applications of fair division, like public housing to ethnic minorities, assigning kindergarten slots, course seat assignments for classes with capacity constraints and where students can specify preferences for a fixed maximum number of classes, require the valuation functions to be beyond additive and capture a

Question 1: How far apart can the

Question 2: What guarantees for

Notation and Preliminaries

Notation.
[k]
denotes the set
\{1,2,\cdots,k-1,k\}.

Model.
We study the problem of fairly dividing a set of
m
indivisible goods, among
n
agents. Preferences of an agent
i\in[n]
is defined by a valuation function
v_{i}:2^{[m]}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
over the set of goods. We represent a fair division problem instance by
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]})
.

Allocations.
An allocation,
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
is a partition of all the goods among the
n
agents, i.e. for all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
,
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
and
\cup_{i\in[n]}A_{i}=[m]
. We denote the set of all allocations by
\Pi_{[n]}([m])
. We also define a
partial allocation,
denoted by
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}),
as a partition of any subset of goods, that is, where
P_{i}\cap P_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
and
\cup_{i\in[n]}P_{i}\subseteq[m]
. Finally, we use the notion of
non-wasteful allocations
also defined in
[
Barman and Verma(2020)
]
. These are allocations where the marginal utility of all the goods in every bundle is non-zero, that is, for such an allocation
\mathcal{A},

v(g|A_{i}\backslash\{g\})>0
for all
i\in[n]
and any
g\in A_{i}
.

We now define the fairness notions we use in this work.

2.1
Fairness Notions

Any Price Share (
APS
)

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the simplex of price vectors over the set of goods
[m]
, formally,
\mathcal{P}=\{(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})\geq 0\ |\ \sum_{i}p_{i}=1\}
. Informally, Any Price Share is the value that an agent can guarantee themselves at
any
price with the budget of
\frac{1}{n}
. Formally, for an instance
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]}),
the
APS
value of agent
i
is defined as,

{\sf{APS}}_{i}^{n}([m])\coloneqq\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\max_{S\subseteq[m],p(S)\leq\frac{1}{n}}v_{i}(S)

(1)

where
p(S)
is the sum of prices of goods in
S
. We will refer
{\sf{APS}}_{i}^{[n]}([m])
by
{\sf{APS}}_{i}
when the qualifiers
n
and
m
are clear.

An alternate definition without using prices is as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Any Price Share)
.

The
APS
value of an agent
i
for an instance
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]})
is the solution of the following program.

\displaystyle{\sf{APS}}_{i}=\max z

subject to:

\displaystyle\sum_{T\subseteq[m]}\lambda_{T}=1

\displaystyle\lambda_{T}=0

\displaystyle\forall T\text{ such that }v_{i}(T)<z

\displaystyle\sum_{T\subseteq[m]:j\in T}\lambda_{T}\leq\frac{1}{n}

\displaystyle\forall j\in[m]

\displaystyle\lambda_{T}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall{T\subseteq[m]}

Essentially, an agent must decide the maximum value
z
that satisfies the following. They associate non-zero weights
\lambda_{T}
to all the sets
T\subseteq[m]
such that any set with a value less than
z
has weight zero, the sum of the weights on all the sets is
1,
and the total weight on any good, defined as the sum of weights of the sets containing the good, is at most
\frac{1}{n}
. This maximum value of

is their
APS

Both of these definitions and their equivalence is stated in
[
Babaioff et al.(2021)
]

Maximin Share (
MMS
)

The Maximin share (
MMS
) value of an agent
i

([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]})
is defined as the minimum value they can guarantee while partitioning all the goods into
n

{\sf{MMS}}^{n}_{i}([m])=\displaystyle\max_{(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\Pi_{[n]}([m])}\displaystyle\min_{k\in[n]}v_{i}(A_{k}).

We refer to
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}^{n}([m])
by
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}
when the qualifiers
n
and
m
are clear. We will refer to the allocation that defines the

value of any agent
i
, that is,
\argmax_{\Pi_{[n]}([m])}\min_{k\in[n]}v_{i}(A_{k}),
as the
MMS
defining allocation
of agent
i.

Note that both the
APS
and
MMS
values of an agent do not depend on the valuation functions of the other agents, and depend only on the number of agents in the fair allocation instance.

The following relation between the
APS
and
MMS
values of any agent is known.

Claim 2.1
.

[
Babaioff et al.(2021)
]

For any monotone valuation function
v_{i}
of agent
i
, we have
{\sf{APS}}_{i}\geq{\sf{MMS}}_{i}
.

At times we abuse notation, and refer as
the
APS
(or

,
which essentially is a value of an agent whose valuation function is
v(\cdot)
.

2.2
Valuation Functions

Binary marginals.

For a valuation function
v(\cdot),
the
marginal utility
of a good
g\in[m]
over a set
S\subseteq[m]
, denoted by
v(g|S),
is defined as the increase in the total value of the bundle
S\cup\{g\}
over the set
S,
that is,
v(g|S)=v(S\cup\{g\})-v(S)
. We consider valuation functions with
binary
marginals, defined as those for whom the marginal utility of any good over any set is either
0
or
1,
that is,
v(g|S)\in\{0,1\}
for every
g\in[m]
and
S\subseteq[m].

Submodular functions.
A function is called
submodular
if it satisfies the property of
diminishing marginal returns
, which specifies that the marginal utility of any good
g
over any subset of goods
S
must not be larger than its marginal utility over any subset of
S.
Formally, a function
v:2^{[m]}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is called submodular if and only if,

v(g|S)\leq v(g|S^{\prime}),\ \forall g\in[m],\ S^{\prime}\subseteq S\subseteq[m].

XOS
functions.
A function
v:2^{[m]}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is called
additive
, if the value of a set of goods is equal to the sum of values of the goods in the set, that is,
v(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v(\{g\}).
A function
v:2^{[m]}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is said to be
XOS
, or
fractionally subadditive,
if and only if there exists a family of additive set functions
\mathcal{F},
such that the value of each subset
S\subseteq[m]
is the maximum function value of
S
from the functions in
\mathcal{F},
that is,
v(S)=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}f(S).
Note that the cardinality of the family
\mathcal{F}
can be exponentially high in
m.

We focus on submodular and
XOS
functions with binary marginals. In case of the submodular functions, these are equivalent to what are called
matroid rank functions (
MRFs
)
(See Section
2.3
for a matroid based definition of these functions).

For the submodular functions, we also study the case of
ternary marginals,
where all the marginal values
v(g|S)\in\{0,1/2,1\}
for all goods
g\in[m]
and
S\subseteq[m].

2.3
Matroid Preliminaries

Matroid.

A matroid, denoted by
{\mathcal{M}},
is a tuple
(E,{\mathcal{I}})
where
E
is a set of elements, called the
ground set
, and
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq 2^{E}
is a collection of subsets of
E
called the

of the matroid, that satisfies the following properties.

1.

If
S\in{\mathcal{I}}
then
\overline{S}\in{\mathcal{I}}
for all
\overline{S}\subseteq S
.

2.

If
I,J\in{\mathcal{I}}
and
|I|>|J|
then there exists
i\in\{I\setminus J\}
such that
J\cup\{i\}\in{\mathcal{I}}
.

Bases of a matroid.

Any independent set of the largest cardinality, that is any set
B\in\argmax_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}}|I|,
is called a
base
of the corresponding matroid.

Rank of a matroid.

Every matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,{\mathcal{I}})
has what is called a
rank function
associated with it, that maps any subset
S
of the ground set
E
to a non-negative integer, equal to the size of the largest independent set that is a subset of
S
. We denote this function by
r_{{\mathcal{M}}}:2^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
Formally,

r_{{\mathcal{M}}}(S):=\max_{I\subseteq S,I\in{\mathcal{I}}}|I|

(2)

The
rank
of a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
is the value
r_{{\mathcal{M}}}(E).

Matroid rank functions and submodularity.

It is well known that the rank function of a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,{\mathcal{I}})
is equivalent to a submodular function on a set of
|E|
items with binary marginals
[
Schrijver et al.(2003)
]
. That is, any submodular function on a set of
m
items with binary marginals corresponds to a matroid with a ground set of
m
elements, one corresponding to each item, referred as the
underlying matroid
hence forth. The submodular function’s value for any subset of the items is equal to the value of the rank function of the underlying matroid for the set of elements corresponding to the items.

Matroid Union.

The union function applied to a collection of matroids generates a matroid known as the
union matroid
. Let
{\mathcal{M}}^{\cup k}=(E^{\cup k},{\mathcal{I}}^{\cup k})
denote the union of a collection of matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}=(E_{1},{\mathcal{I}}_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{M}}_{k}=(E_{k},{\mathcal{I}}_{k}).

{\mathcal{M}}^{\cup k}
is defined as,

E^{\cup k}=\cup_{i\in[k]}E_{i},\ \ \ \ {\mathcal{I}}^{\cup k}=\{\cup_{i\in[k]}I_{i}|I_{i}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{i}\}.

Essentially, for the union matroid, the ground set is the union of the ground sets of the matroids in the collection, and the independent sets are all possible sets formed by taking the union of one independent set from each underlying matroid.

Rank function of a Union matroid.

The rank function of a union matroid
{\mathcal{M}}^{\cup k}
, denoted by
r_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\cup k}}(\cdot)
or simply
r_{{\mathcal{M}}}(\cdot)
when the underlying matroids are clear, has the following well known formula.

r_{{\mathcal{M}}}(S)=\min_{T\subseteq S}\left[|S\setminus T|+\sum_{i\in[k]}r_{{\mathcal{M}}_{i}}(T\cap E_{i})\right]

(3)

Here for each
i\in[k],

r_{{\mathcal{M}}_{i}}(\cdot)
is the rank function of the underlying matroid
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
.

Union of copies of a matroid.

Given a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,{\mathcal{I}}),
let
{\mathcal{M}}^{n}
denote the union of
n
copies of
{\mathcal{M}}.
Let
r(\cdot),r({\mathcal{M}})
and
r^{n}({\mathcal{M}}^{n})
respectively denote the rank function of
{\mathcal{M}},
the rank of
{\mathcal{M}},
and the rank of
{\mathcal{M}}^{n}.
The following properties relating these quantities are well known

]

Lemma 2.1
.

r^{n}({\mathcal{M}}^{n})=n\cdot r({\mathcal{M}})
if and only if for all subsets
T\subseteq E,

|E\setminus T|\geq n\cdot[r(E)-r(T)].

(4)

.

If

has at least

. We have,

\displaystyle\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)

\displaystyle=\widehat{v}_{i}(g\cup S)-\widehat{v}_{i}(S)

\displaystyle=\min\{v_{i}(g\cup S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}-\min\{v_{i}(S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}.

Now if
\min\{v_{i}(S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}={\sf{APS}}_{i},
then by the monotonicity of
v_{i}(\cdot)
,
\min\{v_{i}(g\cup S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}={\sf{APS}}_{i},
implying their difference is zero, and
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)=0
.

Otherwise, if
v_{i}(S)<{\sf{APS}}_{i},
then
v_{i}(g\cup S)
is at most
1
more than
v_{i}(S),
as
v_{i}(\cdot)
has binary marginals. Therefore,
\min\{v_{i}(g\cup S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}
is also at most
1
more than
v_{i}(S)=\min\{v_{i}(S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\},
their difference is at most one, hence
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)\leq 1.
This shows that
\widehat{v}_{i}(\cdot)
has binary marginals.

It is left to show that
\widehat{v}_{i}(\cdot)
is submodular. We consider any set
S^{\prime}
that is a superset of
S,
and show
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)\geq\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S^{\prime}).

If
g\in S,
then both of these values are zero. Also when
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)=1,
then as
\widehat{v}_{i}(\cdot)
has binary marginals, the inequality follows easily. Finally, suppose
g\notin S,
and
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)=0.
Similarly as
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S),
we have,

\displaystyle\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S^{\prime})=\min\{v_{i}(g\cup S^{\prime}),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}-\min\{v_{i}(S^{\prime}),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}

Here if
v_{i}(S)\geq{\sf{APS}}_{i},
then from the monotonicity of
v_{i},
all the terms
v_{i}(S^{\prime}\cup\{g\}),v_{i}(S^{\prime}),v_{i}(S\cup\{g\})\geq{\sf{APS}}_{i},
and both the marginal utilities
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)
and
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S^{\prime})
are zero.

Otherwise, when
v_{i}(S)<{\sf{APS}}_{i},
then as
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)=0,
we have
\min\{v_{i}(g\cup S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}-v_{i}(S)=0.
Again as
v_{i}(S)<{\sf{APS}}_{i},

\min\{v_{i}(g\cup S),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}=v_{i}(g\cup S).
Therefore,
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S)=v_{i}(g|S),
and
v_{i}(g|S)
is also zero. As
v_{i}(\cdot)
is submodular,
v_{i}(g|S)\geq v_{i}(g|S^{\prime}).
As
v_{i}(\cdot)
has binary marginals,
v_{i}(g|S^{\prime})=0.
We have,

\displaystyle v_{i}(g|S^{\prime})

\displaystyle=v_{i}(g\cup S^{\prime})-v_{i}(S^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{v_{i}(g\cup S^{\prime}),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}-\min\{v_{i}(S^{\prime}),{\sf{APS}}_{i}\}

\displaystyle=\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S^{\prime}).

Thus
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S^{\prime})=0,
and therefore
\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S^{\prime})=\widehat{v}_{i}(g|S).

∎

In this section, we consider

Theorem 4.1
.

If the valuation function of an agent
i
in an instance
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]})
is an
{\sf{XOS}}
function with binary marginals, then their
{\sf{APS}}
and
{\sf{MMS}}
values satisfy,
{\sf{APS}}_{i}\leq 2\cdot{\sf{MMS}}_{i}+1.

Proof Idea.
Recall the notions of partial and wasteful allocations from Section
2
. The crucial step in the proof is Algorithm
\ref{alg:alloc-mms-price},
which takes as input a fair allocation instance
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]}),
and yields a non-wasteful, partial allocation where each of the allocated bundles has value at most
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}+1,
and the set of unallocated goods has a value of at most
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}
. Using this allocation, we fix prices on the goods such that for agent
i
the highest value of any affordable bundle of goods, that is one with total price at most
1/n,
is at most
2{\sf{MMS}}_{i}+1.
The price based definition shown in equation (

) of
{\sf{APS}},
then implies the theorem.

Let us now discuss the details of the proof. Hence forth, we call an allocation
balanced
for an agent, if the difference in the values of the smallest and largest bundles according to the agent’s valuation function is at most
1.

Input :

([n],[m],v_{i}(\cdot))
where
v_{i}
is a binary
{\sf{XOS}}
valuation function

Output :
A non-wasteful, balanced
MMS
allocation according to
v_{i},
where the leftover goods also have value at most
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}+1.

Initialize
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n})
to be any
{\sf{MMS}}
-defining allocation for

1

Initialize
\mathcal{R}\leftarrow\emptyset

2

3

then

4

Let
G\in\{A\subseteq A_{j}|v_{i}(A)=|A|={\sf{MMS}}_{i}
}

5

Set
A_{j}\leftarrow G

6

\mathcal{R}\leftarrow\mathcal{R}\cup\{A_{j}\setminus G\}

7

\mathcal{R}\leftarrow[m]\setminus\cup_{j\in[n]}A_{j}

8

v_{i}(\mathcal{R})\geq{\sf{MMS}}_{i}+1

9

Let

Algorithm 1

Non-wasteful balanced
MMS
allocation

Algorithm.
The algorithm starts by computing any
{\sf{MMS}}
-defining allocation
\mathcal{A}
for agent
i,
and performs two phases. First, while any bundle
A_{j}
’s value in
\mathcal{A}
is more than
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}+1,
it considers any subset
G
of

that has both size and value exactly

leaves

with agent

and removes the remaining goods. All goods removed in this way are added to a bundle called

In the second phase, while the value of
\mathcal{R}
is higher than
{\sf{MMS}}_{1}+1,
it considers any subset
A^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{R}
of both size and value exactly
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}+1.
The algorithm takes away the bundle of the smallest valued agent, adds this to
\mathcal{R},
and gives

to this agent instead.

We use two results that will be useful in establishing that this algorithm converges in the special kind of allocation desired. Claim

is a technical property of
{\sf{XOS}}
valuations, followed by Lemma

which shows a key property of Algorithm

.

Claim 4.1
.

Given an
{\sf{XOS}}
valuation function
v:2^{[m]}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with binary marginals, and any set
S\subseteq[m]
, we can find a subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
such that
v(S^{\prime})=v(S)=|S^{\prime}|
.

Proof.

Since
v
is an
{\sf{XOS}}
function, there is a family
\mathcal{F}
of additive functions such that for all
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[m]
, there is an additive function
f\in\mathcal{F}
with
v(\mathcal{S})=f(\mathcal{S})
. Given the set
S
consider any such function
f
. As
f
is additive,
f(S)=\sum_{g\in S}f(g).
Further, as
v
has binary marginals,
f(g)\in\{0,1\}
for all
g.
We define the set
S^{\prime}\coloneqq\{g\in S|f(g)=1\}
. Thus we have,
v(S)=f(S)=f(S^{\prime})=|S^{\prime}|
.

Finally, as
f(S^{\prime})=|S^{\prime}|,

v(S^{\prime})\geq|S^{\prime}|,
but as
v
has binary marginals,
v(S^{\prime})\leq|S^{\prime}|
, thus
v(S^{\prime})=|S^{\prime}|.

∎

Remark 4.1
.

We note here that while Claim

seems obvious, it is not true for binary subadditive valuations which are the immediate generalisation of binary

valuations. To see this, consider a function on

and any strict subset of the three goods has a value

does not hold for this function.

Lemma 4.1
.

Algorithm

. Furthermore,

Consider the first For loop (Steps

Let us now look at the While loop (Steps

.

Consider the partial allocation

2
. Therefore, this is a feasible solution to the Linear program in the
{\sf{APS}}
definition
2.1
for
z=2
. Thus,
{\sf{APS}}\geq 2
. Therefore, in this instance,
{\sf{APS}}\geq 2\cdot{\sf{MMS}}
.
∎

Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, along with the known computational results for
{\sf{MMS}},
yield the following results for
APS
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Given a fair allocation instance
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]})
where every agent has a binary
XOS
valuation function,

1.

A
0.1222
-
APS
allocation, meaning one that gives every agent a bundle of value at least
0.1222
times their
{\sf{APS}},
can be computed in polynomial time.

2.

Even when agents have identical valuations, no better than
0.5
-
APS
allocation may exist.

To prove this theorem, we first prove the following lemmas to separate the agents with
{\sf{APS}}=0
and
{\sf{MMS}}=0.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given an instance
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]})
with
XOS
binary marginal valuations, one can check in polynomial time if any agent has
{\sf{MMS}}=0.

Proof.

To check if
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}=0
for some agent
i
, we form a complete weighted bipartite graph where one side has
n
vertices, and the goods correspond to vertices on the other side. The weight of each edge is the value of the agent
i
for the good adjacent to the edge. We compute one maximum weight matching of the goods. If each of the
n
vertices in the left part gets assigned a good of value
1,
then one can form an allocation with the matched goods in separate bundles. The remaining goods can be allocated arbitrarily. By subadditivity, each bundle has value at least
1,
hence
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}\geq 0.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For an agent with a binary
XOS
valuation function in a fair allocation instance,
{\sf{APS}}=0
if and only if
{\sf{MMS}}=0.

Proof.

If
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}>0,
then from Claim
2.1
,
{\sf{APS}}_{i}>0.
Otherwise when
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}=0,
the allocation returned by Algorithm
1
has the following properties according to Lemma
4.1
. The value of
\mathcal{R}=0,
and the value of each bundle in
\mathcal{A}
is either
0
or
1.
Further, there are at most
(n-1)
bundles with value
1,
else
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}\geq 1.
Also, each of the
1
valued bundles have exactly
1
good in them, and the
0
valued bundles have no goods in them. We assign prices to the goods as follows. Assign a price of
1/(n-1)
to each good in the highest valued
n-1
bundles of
\mathcal{A},
assign a price of
0
to all the remaining goods (in
\mathcal{R}
).

At a budget of
1/n,
the agent cannot afford any
1
valued bundle. As the remaining goods together have a value
0,

{\sf{APS}}=0.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

From Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
, we remove all the agents with
{\sf{APS}}=0
by giving them no goods in polynomial time. For the remaining agents, we know
{\sf{APS}}\geq{\sf{MMS}}\geq 1.
Combined with
{\sf{APS}}\leq 2\cdot{\sf{MMS}}+1
from Theorem
4.1
, we have
{\sf{APS}}\leq 3\cdot{\sf{MMS}}
for all the remaining agents.

[
Li and Vetta(2021)
]
show that there exists an efficient algorithm to compute a
0.3666
-
MMS
allocation, that is, an allocation where every agent receives a bundle of value at least
0.3666
times their
{\sf{MMS}}
value. As
{\sf{APS}}\leq 3\cdot{\sf{MMS}},
this implies that an allocation that gives every agent a bundle of value at least
\penalty\ 0.1222
times their
{\sf{APS}}
can be computed in polynomial time.

Finally, Theorem
4.2
shows an instance where the agents have identical valuation functions, and their
{\sf{APS}}
is at least twice their
{\sf{MMS}}.
By definition of
{\sf{MMS}},
no allocation can have the smallest bundle’s value more than
{\sf{MMS}}.
Therefore, in every allocation, at least the smallest bundle’s agent receives a bundle of value at most half their
{\sf{APS}},
and a better than
0.5
-
APS
allocation does not exist.
∎

5
Submodular Valuations with Ternary Marginals

In this section, we show the following hardness result.

Theorem 5.1
.

In an instance
([n],[m],(v_{i})_{i\in[n]}),
it is
NP
-hard to compute the
MMS
value of an agent approximately up to a factor better than
5/6,
when the agent has a submodular valuation function, even when all the marginal utilities are in
\{0,1/2,1\}.

An immediate corollary, using Claim
2.1
, is that computing the
{\sf{APS}}
value approximately up to a factor better than
5/6
is also
NP
-hard for such an agent.

The proof of the theorem has three parts. First, we show a reduction from the known
NP
-complete problem
3
-
D
-
MATCHING
to a fair allocation instance with agents with identical valuations. We then show that this valuation function in the reduced instance is submodular with all the marginal values in
\{0,1/2,1\}.
Finally, we show the correctness of the reduction, establishing the factor of the hardness of approximation.

Reduction.
The
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}
problem is as follows. Given are three disjoint sets
X,Y,Z,
having
m
elements each, and a set
\mathcal{T}
of triples
(a,b,c)
, where
a\in X,

b\in Y
and
c\in Z.
The problem is to answer if there is a subset of
m
triples in
\mathcal{T}
called a
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}
of
X,Y,Z,
that
cover
all of
X,Y
and
Z,
meaning for all
s\in X\cup Y\cup Z,

s\in{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}.

Given an instance of
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}},
we form a fair allocation instance as follows. There are
m
agents, and a set
{\mathcal{M}}
of
3m
goods, one good corresponding to each element of
X,Y
and
Z.
All the agents have the following identical valuation function
v
for the goods. For any subset
\mathcal{S}
of
{\mathcal{M}},

v(\mathcal{S})
is defined as follows.

\displaystyle v(\mathcal{S})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=1\\
2,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=2\\
2.5,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=3\text{ and }\mathcal{S}\notin\mathcal{T}\\
3,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=3\text{ and }\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{T}\\
3,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|\geq 4\\
\end{array}\right.

Function
v
is submodular with ternary marginals.
First, let us compute the marginal utility of a good
g
over sets of different sizes. From the definition of
v(\mathcal{S}),
one can verify that,

\displaystyle v(g|\mathcal{S})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|\leq 1\\
0.5,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=2\text{ and }\mathcal{S}\cup\{g\}\notin\mathcal{T}\\
1,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=2\text{ and }\mathcal{S}\cup\{g\}\in\mathcal{T}\\
0.5,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=3\text{ and }\mathcal{S}\notin\mathcal{T}\\
0,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|=3\text{ and }\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{T}\\
0,&\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}|\geq 4.\end{array}\right.

Therefore,
v(g|\mathcal{S})\in\{0,1/2,1\}
for all
g
and
\mathcal{S}.

Also
v(g|\mathcal{S})\leq v(g|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}),
for any two sets
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with
|\mathcal{S}|\geq|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|,
hence also when
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{S}.
This establishes submodularity.

Correctness.
Finally, we prove that the
MMS
value of any agent is
3
if and only if the
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}
instance has a solution, and is at most
2.5
otherwise.

Suppose a
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}
exists. Then one can divide the
3
goods from each triple in the solution to every agent. Each agent receives a bundle of value
3.
As the highest value under
v
of any set of goods is
3,

{\sf{MMS}}\leq 3.
Therefore, in this case,
{\sf{MMS}}=3.

Alternatively, suppose the
{\sf{MMS}}
value of the reduced instance is
3.
Then note that every agent must receive exactly
3
goods. Otherwise, some agent will receive at most
2
goods, and have value at most
2.
A bundle of
3
goods has value
3
only when the corresponding elements form a triple in the
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}
instance. Also, the bundles of goods in the
{\sf{MMS}}
-defining allocation are disjoint, hence the triples allocated to all the agents are disjoint. Therefore, the allocation consists of goods corresponding to
m
disjoint triples, that cover all the elements, hence form a solution of
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}.

Now, if there was an algorithm that computed the
MMS
value within a factor better than
5/6
for such instances with submodular functions and ternary marginals, then given the reduced instance, the algorithm would output a value higher than
2.5
if and only if a
{{\sf 3\text{-}D\text{-}MATCHING}}
existed. This proves Theorem
5.1
.

6
Conclusion

We analyzed the fairness notion of
APS
for indivisible goods under submodular and
XOS
functions with binary marginals, a rich and expressive class of valuation functions
[
Benabbou et al.(2021)
,
Barman and Verma(2020)
,
Barman and Verma(2021a)
,
Li and Vetta(2018)
]
. Under binary submodular valuations, we give a rather surprising result that
APS
=
MMS
. This is not true for even additive valuations (with non-binary marginals.) On the other hand for fractionally subadditive functions with binary marginals, we show a gap of 2 between
APS
and
MMS
and show that is almost tight.

It would be interesting to study the relations between
APS
and
MMS
for other valuation functions, and in particular for binary subadditive valuations. Subadditive functions generalizes both fractionally subadditive and submodular functions, and remains relatively less explored. We note that, most of Section
4
can be extended to work for binary subadditive functions, except Claim
4.1
. A generalization of this claim for subadditive valuations will be helpful in determining the gap between
MMS
and
APS
under these valuations.

Finally, a study of
APS
for the case when agents are heterogeneous, i.e. each agent has a weight or endowment, under valuations beyond-additive with binary marginals is the next natural question.
