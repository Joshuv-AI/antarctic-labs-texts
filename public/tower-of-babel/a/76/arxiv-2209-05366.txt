A framework for a generalisation analysis of machine-learned interatomic potentials
Christoph Ortner, Yangshuai Wang

Abstract
. Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) and force fields (i.e. interaction laws for atoms and molecules) are typically trained on limited data-sets that cover only a very small section of the full space of possible input structures. MLIPs are nevertheless capable of making accurate predictions of forces and energies in simulations involving (seemingly) much more complex structures. In this article we propose a framework within which this kind of generalisation can be rigorously understood. As a prototypical example, we apply the framework to the case of simulating point defects in a crystalline solid. Here, we demonstrate how the accuracy of the simulation depends explicitly on the size of the training structures, on the kind of observations (e.g., energies, forces, force constants, virials) to which the model has been fitted, and on the fit accuracy. The new theoretical insights we gain partially justify current best practices in the MLIP literature and in addition suggest a new approach to the collection of training data and the design of loss functions.



1.
Introduction

A key question in machine learning tasks is to understand how well a trained model generalizes to inputs outside of the training data. This is particularly challenging in scientific machine learning where one oftentimes requires generalisation to inputs
very far from
training data. The present work is concerned with sketching out a multiscale numerical analysis framework suitable to study this scenario.

We will focus in particular on atomistic mechanics simulations using machine learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs)
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
16
,
36
]
. The success of molecular simulation relies on the accuracy and efficiency of the interatomic force models. The two main approaches to computing interatomic forces are ab initio electronic structure models
[
21
,
23
,
24
,
35
]
and purely mechanistic models
[
15
,
20
,
25
,
38
]
. The former are computationally prohibitive while the latter often provide insufficient accuracy. The achievement of MLIPs is to provide a classes of models with tunable accuracy/efficiency ratio which promises to bridge the significant gap in accuracy and capability between ab initio electronic structure models and classical mechanistic models (empirical potentials).

In this work we will study by analytical (as opposed to statistical) methods how the choice of training data and the accuracy of the fit to that training data affect the accuracy of predictions. The key challenge we hope to better understand is the following: training data for MLIPs is obtained from ab initio electronic structure simulations. Due to the high computational cost of these models, only small computational domains (structures) containing at most hundreds of atoms are used. However, predictions during simulations are performed on much larger domains often containing hundreds of thousand or even millions of atoms.

As a prototype application we consider classes of structures containing crystalline defects, and to keep the notational and technical burden to a minimum we further restrict the present work to point defects only. In this setting, training domains would typically be small cells containing a single defect, while simulations would be performed on much larger domains containing potentially many copies of the defects trained on. In this situation, the basic intution is clear: the trained MLIP has already seen local snapshots of the structure on which it is predicting energies or interatomic forces, and due to its functional form that is indeed restricted to only local interactions it is therefore able to to make accurate predictions.

Yet, the details are subtle and warrant a deeper look: How does the prediction error depend on the size of the training domain? How should the various observations we make (energies, forces, virials) be weighted? Which of these observations provide the dominant contribution to the prediction error? Thus, we see that even this highly simplified setting leads to interesting questions that can significantly inform the design of parameter estimation schemes.

1.1.
Outline

We focus on multiple point defects embedded in a periodic homogeneous host crystal, where a rigorous numerical analysis approach is feasible. The atomistic equilibration problem for a single crystalline defect in this context is a well-defined variational problem
[
9
,
17
]
. We review the framework and adapt it to the case of multiple point defects considered in this work along the line of
[
22
]
in Section
2
. This requires in particular a new existence and stability result (Theorem

) for general configurations of multiple point defects. Our generalisation analysis heavily relies on this result since it characterises the structure of the equilibrium structures.

To propose a framework within which the generalisation can be rigorously understood, we investigate the error propagation from fitting MLIPs on a small training domain to predicting the material properties (e.g., defect geometry and formation energy) on a large simulation domain in Section
3
. We demonstrate how the accuracy of the material properties in the simulation depends explicitly on the size of the training structures, on the kind of observations (e.g., energies, forces, force constants) to which the model has been fitted, and on the fit accuracy.
Explicit theoretical convergence rates are summarized in Theorem
3.1
and Table

We then propose a concrete implementation of MLIPs inspired by our generalisation analysis to confirm the analytical error estimates on several model problems in Section

Finally, we will discuss further consequences and limitations our work in Section

In more complex scenarios, such as curved dislocations or extrapolating on grain boundary structures with distinct coordination environment there are additional challenges that our analysis does not cover even heuristically and requires significant additional ideas.

Background: Equilibration of crystalline defects

A rigorous framework for modelling the geometric equilibrium of crystalline defects has been developed in
[

9
,

]

These works formulate the equilibration of a single crystalline defect as a variational problem in a discrete energy space and establish qualitatively sharp far-field decay estimates for the corresponding equilibrium configuration.
We will review the framework and adapt it to the case of multiple point defects considered in this work along the lines of
[

For the sake of simplicity of presentation, we will skip over some technical details but fill these gaps in Section

Let

3.
Main results

3.1.
Error estimates

The computational cost of solving (
2.3
) on a large simulation domain is prohibitive when an electronic structure model is taken into consideration. Surrogate models are therefore introduced, fitted to an electronic structure model, to give reasonable approximations.
If we denote the surrogate site potential by
\widetilde{V}_{\ell}
(resp.
\widetilde{V}^{\rm h}
), then the corresponding energy-difference functionals are given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda_{N}}\widetilde{V}_{\ell}\big(Du(\ell)\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm h}(u):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm h}}\widetilde{V}^{\rm h}\big(Du(\ell)\big).

The resulting variational problem for the equilibration reads

(3.2)

\tilde{u}\in\arg\min\big\{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u),u\in\mathscr{A}\big\}.

The surrogate model enables large-scale simulation but obtaining ab initio training data on a large simulation domain remains intractable. Instead, one normally fits the parameters in the surrogate model to ab initio simulations on very small training domains containing at most a few hundred atoms. These should include all possible local snapshots one expects to encounter in the simulation; in our case, regions of homogeneous crystal or single defects. In the following we will give a rigorous framework within which this intuition is made precise and all the resulting errors are quantified.

To that end, given
L_{0}\leq L\leq L_{\mathcal{D}}
, we call
\Omega_{L}:=\mathsf{B}(-L/2,L/2]^{d}
the
training domain
while
\Omega_{N}
is called the
simulation domain
. Let
\Lambda_{L}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega_{L}
contain a single defect core located at the origin. Similarly as in the previous section, let
\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L}
and
\mathscr{A}_{L}
be the corresponding space of periodic displacements and admissible set. We equip
\mathscr{U}_{L}^{\rm per}
with the norm
\|u\|_{\mathscr{U}_{L}^{\rm per}}:=\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{L})}
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{L}(u)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u)
be the energy functionals defined on
\Lambda_{L}
, and the equilibrium of the corresponding variational problem with
\mathcal{E}_{L}(u)
is denoted as
\bar{u}_{L}
.

Next, we introduce the
matching conditions
between the reference and the
approximated
models, in terms of the observations (energies, forces and force constants) of the possible configurations near
\bar{u}_{L}
. To be more precise, let
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}(\bar{u}_{L})\subset\mathscr{A}_{L}
representing atomic displacements near
\bar{u}_{L}
, then the
matching condition
for the energy is defined by

(3.3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm E}:=\max_{u_{L}\in B_{\delta}(\bar{u}_{L})}\big|\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{L})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u_{L})\big|.

To measure the force error, we introduce

(3.4)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm F}:=\max_{u_{L}\in B_{\delta}(\bar{u}_{L})}\big\|-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{L})+\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u_{L})\big\|_{(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*}}
is the dual norm of
\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L}
.
Furthermore, for the stability analysis, we require the force constant error

(3.5)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm FC}:=\big\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{L}(\bar{u}_{L})-\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(\bar{u}_{L})\big\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L},(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*})}.

In particular, the force constant error on the homogeneous lattice is given by

(3.6)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}:=\big\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\rm h}({\bm{0}})-\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm h}({\bm{0}})\big\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L},(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*})}.

The following result provides a rigorous
a priori
error estimate of the geometry error
\|D\bar{u}-D\tilde{u}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}
and of the formation energy error
|\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u})|
in terms of the size of the training domains and in terms of the fit accuracy of the surrogate model.
The proof is given in Section
6.3
.

Theorem 3.1.

Suppose that
(S)
is satisfied and that
\bar{u}
is a strongly stable equilibrium of (
2.3
). Then, for
L,L_{\mathcal{D}}
sufficiently large, satisfying

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|D\bar{u}-D\tilde{u}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq~C^{\rm G}\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot\big(\varepsilon^{\rm F}+L^{-d/2}\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}+L^{-3d/2}\big),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u})\big|

There are several insights we can gain from our results. First, they clearly tease out an issue that is — to the best of our knowledge — never discussed in the MLIPs literature: the size of training domains significantly affects the quality of the fitted model. Intuitively this happens because one cannot in practise obtain “perfect” snapshots. Secondly, we see the importance of fitting force constants in order to significantly reduce the effect that size of the simulation domain has. Finally, our estimates provide a clear guidance on how energy, force and force-constant observations should be weighted in the least squares loss function, in particular suggesting the optimal balance
\varepsilon^{\rm E}\approx(\varepsilon^{\rm F})^{2}
suggesting to put signifantly higher emphasis on the energy fit.

4.
Numerical results

We propose a concrete implementation of MLIPs inspired by our generalisation analysis of Theorem
3.1
to confirm the analytical error estimates on model problems.

4.1.
Constructions of MLIPs

4.1.1.
Parameterisation

First, we need to choose a parameterisation of the surrogate potential
\widetilde{V}
. Although a wide variety of choices is available nowadays, we have opted for the
linear
atomic cluster expansion (ACE)
[
1
,
27
,
39
]
which has the advantage of achieving close to state of the art accuracy despite being a linear model
[
27
]
. However, there is no reason to believe that the specific choice of MLIP is essential in our tests.
Briefly, in the ACE model, the surrogate potential
\tilde{V}
is written as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{{V}}(\boldsymbol{g}):=V^{\rm ACE}(\boldsymbol{g};\{c_{B}\}_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}})=\sum_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}}c_{B}B(\boldsymbol{g}),

where
B
are the ACE basis functions and
\{c_{B}\}_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}}
are the parameters that we will estimate by minimizing a least squares loss. The basis functions
B
are invariant under rotations, reflections and permutations of an atomic environment.

A more detailed review of the ACE model and in particular its approximation parameters is provided in the Appendix
C
.

4.1.2.
Training sets and loss

Following our generalisation analysis (Theorem
3.1
) we require that the ACE model matches the reference model in the sense of making
\varepsilon^{\rm E},\varepsilon^{\rm F},\varepsilon^{\rm FC}
and
\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}
small. These measures of fit accuracy are specified in terms of max-norms over an infinite set of displacements, which is clearly computationally not tractable. At this point we make several departures from our rigorous analysis.

We first introduce the training set,
\mathfrak{R}
: The complete neighbourhood
B_{\delta}(\bar{u}_{L})
used in the analysis is replaced with a finite number of random samples taken from
B_{\delta}(\bar{u}_{L})
. We fix the perturbed parameter to be
0.01
and denote the number of the configurations in
\mathfrak{R}
as
N_{\rm train}:=\#\mathfrak{R}
. Analogously we also produce a test set. The number of configurations in training and test sets will be specified for each individual example.

Next, we consider the construction of a loss function inspired by our theory. We cannot optimize

\varepsilon^{\rm E}+\varepsilon^{\rm F}+\varepsilon^{\rm FC}+\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}

directly but we propose three
ad hoc
approximations of the
matching conditions
:

•

We replace the max-norm with an
\ell^{2}
-norm to obtain a linear least squares problem.

•

The force error
\varepsilon^{\rm F}
is defined in a dual norm in (
3.4
), but for sufficiently small training domains it is almost indistinguishable from a standard
\ell^{2}
-norm, namely
\big\|-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{L})+\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u_{L})\big\|_{\ell^{2}}
.

•

Finally, we drop the the force constant errors
\varepsilon^{\rm FC}
and
\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}
entirely from the loss function. In fact, we have found that constructing the training set
\mathfrak{R}
as described above and only fitting forces and energies already results in a sufficiently good accuracy of
\varepsilon^{\rm FC}
. We will give a numerical verification of this statement in Section
4.2
, Table
2
.

Given the foregoing approximations, the training set
\mathfrak{R}
constructed above and the parameterisation defined by (
4.1
), we determine the parameters
\{c_{B}\}
by minimising the following loss function

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\big(\{c_{B}\}\big):=\sum_{u_{R}\in\mathfrak{R}}\Big(W_{\rm E}\big|\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{R})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u_{R};\{c_{B}\})\big|^{2}+W_{\rm F}\big|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{R})-\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(u_{R};\{c_{B}\})\big|^{2}\Big),

where
W_{\rm E}
and
W_{\rm F}
are additional weights that might depend on the configurations and observations. According to (
3.8
) (or Table
1
), we choose
W_{E}\gg W_{F}
in practice such that the balance
\varepsilon^{\rm E}\approx(\varepsilon^{\rm F})^{2}
can be achieved. The details will be provided for different model problems in the next section.

The loss function (
4.2
) is quadratic in the parameters
\{c_{B}\}
and can therefore be minimised by using a QR factorisation. In our implementation we use a rank-revealing QR (rr-QR) factorisation
[
7
]
which provides a mechanism analogous to Tychonov-regularisation
[
39
]
. The regularisation parameter for rr-QR factorisation is set to be
10^{-6}
(we tested various values and it gave the best performance for all numerical experiments) throughout this work.

4.2.
Numerical results

We present numerical tests for two examples:

(1)

Toy model:
We consider various configurations of point defects (vacancies and interstitials) in a two dimensional triangular lattice. As the reference model we will use an embedded atom model (EAM)
[
15
]

5.
Conclusion

We proposed a framework for a generalisation analysis in a multi-scale setting, and used it to investigate the error propagation from fitting MLIPs on a small training domain to making predictions on a large simulation domain.
As a prototypical example, we apply the framework to the case of simulating multiple (weakly interacting) point defects in a crystalline solid. Our analysis identifies what observations one should acquire from the reference model to obtain accurate predictions in this case.
Our theoretical results partially justify existing best practices in the MLIP literature, but also provide more fine-grained qualitative information about how prediction accuracy depends on the choice of training data.
This approach also suggests a new perspective on how to approach the collection of training data and the design of loss functions.

Our presentation here is restricted to simple-species Bravais lattices and point defects. Generalisations do require additional technical difficulties to be overcome, but there appears to be no fundamental limitation to extend the method and the results to multi-lattices and a range of other defects in some form. To conclude, we briefly discuss some of these possibilities as well as limitations which deserve further mathematical analysis and algorithmic developments.

•

More complex crystalline structures:
As mentioned above, the extension to multi-lattices is conceptually straightforward and the necessary technical details should be addressed in depth.

•

Straight dislocations:
The extension to straight dislocations appears straightforward applying the techniques of
[
13
,
17
,
22
]
. We expect that the error estimates for straight dislocations depend not only on the force error but also the
matching condition
on the linear elasticity, even the nonlinear elasticity (virials) due to the long-range elastic field.

•

Grain boundaries or curved dislocation lines:
These more complex crystalline defects require a much more significant degree of extrapolation than point defects or straight dislocations.
Both simulations and rigorous analysis appear to be both conceptually and technically much more challenging. While our overarching strategies should still apply, it is far less clear whether our methodologies in this paper can be applied directly.

•

Uncertainty estimation:
It is common knowledge that MLIPs have a fundamental limitation in that they lack a physical model for the phenomenon being predicted and thus have unknown accuracy when extrapolating beyond their training set. The uncertainty quantification (UQ) capabilities would be included to address this problem. Analyzing the propagation of uncertainty in the training procedure to predicted properties could be understood from a Bayesian statistics perspective, where some recent works
[
2
,
29
]
should provide appropriate references.

6.1.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the concepts needed in the proofs of the main results. We review the framework of
[
9
,
17
]
to formulate the equilibration of a single point defect (
n_{\mathcal{D}}=1
, vacancy or interstitial) as a variational problem in a discrete energy space and then give the strong stability assumption
(S)
, which assumes the existence of a single stable core in the infinite lattice
\Lambda
.

The deformed configuration of the infinite lattice
\Lambda
is a map
y:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We can decompose the configuration

into

(6.1)

\displaystyle y(\ell)=x(\ell)+u(\ell)=\ell+u(\ell)\qquad\forall~\ell\in\Lambda,

where
x(\ell)=\ell
is a linear map representing a crystalline reference configuration.
The set of possible atomic configurations is

(6.2)

\displaystyle{\rm Adm}_{0}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{\mathfrak{m}>0}{\rm Adm}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\Lambda)\qquad\text{with}

\displaystyle{\rm Adm}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle:=\left\{y:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},~|y(\ell)-y(m)|>\mathfrak{m}|\ell-m|\quad\forall~\ell,m\in\Lambda\right\},

where the parameter
\mathfrak{m}>0
qualifies the accumulation of atoms.

For site
\ell\in\Lambda

\mathcal{N}(\ell)
as

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\ell):=

\displaystyle\left\{\,m\in\Lambda\setminus\ell~\Big|~\exists\,a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ s.t. }|a-\ell|=|a-m|\leq|a-k|\quad\forall\,k\in\Lambda\,\right\},

which is applied in the definition of the energy norm
\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}
(cf. (
2.1
)).
We introduce the discrete energy space for infinite lattice

(6.4)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda):=\big\{u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}~\big\lvert~\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda)}<\infty\big\},

with the associated semi-norm
\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}
.
We also define the following subspace of compact displacements

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}^{\rm c}(\Lambda):=\big\{u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}~\big\lvert~\exists~R>0~{\rm s.t.}~u={\rm const}~{\rm in}~\Lambda\setminus B_{R}\big\}.

The associated class of admissible displacements is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(\Lambda):=\big\{u\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)~:~x+u\in{\rm Adm}_{0}(\Lambda)\big\}.

In this paper, we make the following assumptions on the regularity and locality of the site potentials, which has been justified for some basic quantum mechanic models
[
8
,
10
,
12
,
31
]
.
We refer to
[
9

(RL)

Regularity and locality:

For all
\ell\in\Lambda
,
V_{\ell}\big(Du(\ell)\big)
possesses partial derivatives up to
\mathfrak{n}
-th order with
\mathfrak{n}\geq 3
. For
j=1,\ldots,\mathfrak{n}
, there exist constants
C_{j}
and
\eta_{j}
such that

(6.6)

\displaystyle\big|V_{\ell,{\bm{\rho}}}\big(Du(\ell)\big)\big|\leq C_{j}\exp\Big(-\eta_{j}\sum^{j}_{l=1}|{\bm{\rho}}_{l}|\Big)

for all
\ell\in\Lambda
and
{\bm{\rho}}\in(\Lambda-\ell)^{j}
.

Although we defined the site potentials on infinite stencils
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\Lambda-\ell}

It is only necessary to assume in this case that the potential

outside the interaction range.
In particular, we will denote by

Following the results in

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\Lambda}(u):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}V_{\ell}\big(Du(\ell)\big).

The corresponding variational problem for the equilibrium state is

\bar{u}^{\rm core}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\Lambda}(u),~u\in\mathscr{A}(\Lambda)\big\},

where “

An auxiliary energy-functional needed in the following analysis is the energy of the homogeneous (defect-free) lattice

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm h}(u):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda^{\rm h}}V^{\rm h}\big(Du(\ell)\big).

We are ready to give the assumption on the existence of a single strongly stable core in infinite lattice.
Let
\bar{u}^{\rm core}
be the corresponding local minimizer of (

\displaystyle\exists~\bar{c}_{\Lambda}>0~\textrm{s.t.}~\big\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\Lambda}(\bar{u}^{\rm core})v,v\big\rangle\geq\bar{c}_{\Lambda}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}\qquad\forall~v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda).

To map the displacements defined on
\Lambda

Then we briefly establish two auxiliary stability results based on

We include them here for the sake of completeness and their proofs can be found in

Proposition 6.1 (Phonon stability).

Suppose that
(S)
holds, then there exists a constant

satisfying

such that

\displaystyle=:S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3},

where we have ensured that supp
\{w^{i}_{k}\}
for
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
only overlaps with supp
\{w^{0}_{k}\}
by choosing
r_{k}\leq L_{k}/3
, and hence all other cross-terms vanish.

For term
S_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z_{k})w_{k}^{0},w_{k}^{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z_{k})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}({\bf 0})\big)w_{k}^{0},w_{k}^{0}\big\rangle+\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}({\bf 0})w_{k}^{0},w_{k}^{0}\rangle

(6.23)

\displaystyle\geq\big(\bar{c}_{\rm hom}-M_{2}\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot r^{-d/2}_{k}\big)\|Dw_{k}^{0}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})},

where the last inequality follows from the Proposition
6.1
.

The term
S_{2}
can be estimated similarly from Lemma
A

(6.24)

\displaystyle\big|S_{2}\big|\geq\big(\bar{c}_{R}-M_{2}\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot r^{-d/2}_{k}\big)\|Dv_{k}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

For the cross-terms
S_{3}
, by assuming
r_{k}\leq L_{k}/3
, we deduce that

\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z_{k})w_{k}^{0},w_{k}^{i}\rangle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z_{k})(v_{k}-w_{k}^{i}),w_{k}^{i}\rangle.

Using the techniques in the proof of
[
22
, Lemma 5.2]
, we can obtain that, for each
i
,

(6.25)

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z_{k})w_{k}^{0},w_{k}^{i}\rangle\rightarrow 0,\qquad\textrm{as}~k\rightarrow\infty.

Combining the estimates from (
6.2
) to (
6.25
), we have

\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z_{k})v_{k},v_{k}\rangle\geq(\bar{c}_{R}-\epsilon_{k})\|Dv_{k}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}+\epsilon_{k},

where
\epsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Furthermore, Lemma
A
implies that for sufficiently large
R
we have
\bar{c}_{R}\geq\bar{c}/2>0
. Hence, together with (
6.21
), we can obtain

\bar{c}_{k}+k^{-1}\geq\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z_{k})v_{k},v_{k}\rangle\geq\bar{c}_{R}\geq\bar{c}/2,

so for
k
sufficiently large, it contradicts the assumption that
\bar{c}_{k}<\bar{c}/2
for all
k
.
∎

We are ready to prove the Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Applying Lemma
A
with the results in Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
6.2
, we can state that there exist
L_{0}>0
, where
\mathcal{D}
satisfies
L_{\mathcal{D}}\geq L_{0}
, and
z
is an approximated solution defined by (
6.12
) corresponding to
\mathcal{D}
. It follows that for any
v\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{{\rm per}}
, there exists
\omega\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{{\rm per}}
with
\|D\omega\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot L_{\mathcal{D}}^{-d/2}
such that

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z+\omega),v\rangle=0,\quad\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z+\omega)v,v\rangle\geq\frac{\bar{c}}{4}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}.

Writing

∎

6.3.
Proof of the generalisation analysis

In this section, we give the detailed proof of the generalisation analysis (Theorem
3.1
), which is the main result in this paper.

Proof.

Applying the framework of the
a priori
error estimates in
[
11
,
30
,
32
,
33
]
, we mainly divide the proof into several steps in order to apply the inverse function theorem (cf. Lemma
A
).

1. Stability:
For any
v\in\mathscr{U}^{{\rm per}}_{N}
, we consider the stability of

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})v,v\rangle+\big(\langle\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})v,v\rangle-\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})v,v\rangle\big)

(6.26)

\displaystyle=:S_{1}+S_{2}.

From the results in Theorem
2
, we can obtain that
\bar{u}
is strongly stable, namely there exists
\bar{c}>0
such that

S_{1}:=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})v,v\rangle\geq\frac{\bar{c}}{4}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}.

Similarly as shown in (
6.14
), we split the test function
v
. For
L\leq L_{\mathcal{D}}
, let
r:=L/3+1
, we define

(6.27)

\displaystyle v_{i}:=\Pi^{\ell_{i}}_{r}v\quad\textrm{for}~i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}},\quad\textrm{and}\quad v_{0}:=v-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}v_{i},

Lemma
A
implies that
\|Dv_{i}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}
for
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
.

The term
S_{2}
can be further split into three parts

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})\big)v,v\big\rangle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i,j=0}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})\big)v_{i},v_{j}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})\big)v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})\big)v_{0},v_{i}\big\rangle

(6.28)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle~S_{21}+S_{22}+S_{23},

where we have ensured that supp
\{v_{i}\}
for
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
only overlaps with supp
\{v_{0}\}
by the choice of
r
and therefore all other cross-terms vanish.

For the term
S_{21}
,
for
L
sufficiently large and
t\in[0,1]
, we can Taylor expand the
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}
at the reference configuration

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\omega)\big)v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}({\bf 0})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}({\bf 0})\big)v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle+\big\langle\big(\delta^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(t\omega)-\delta^{3}\mathcal{E}(t\omega)\big)\omega v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle

(6.29)

\displaystyle=:S^{\rm(a)}_{21}+S^{\rm(b)}_{21}.

We first estimate
S^{\rm(a)}_{21}
by

(6.30)

\displaystyle|S^{\rm(a)}_{21}|\leq C\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

For the term
S^{\rm(b)}_{21}
, similarly we can obtain

(6.31)

\displaystyle|S^{\rm(b)}_{21}|\leq CL^{-d/2}\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

To estimate
S_{22}
, recall the definition of the predictor (
6.12
) and the construction of
v_{i}
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
and
L
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))-\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u}_{L}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u}_{L}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u}_{L}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u}_{L}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}(\cdot-\ell_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~C(\varepsilon^{\rm FC}+\|D\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}-D\bar{u}_{L}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{L})})\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}

(6.32)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~C(\varepsilon^{\rm FC}+L^{-d/2})\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})},

where the last inequality follows from
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Noting that
\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(u)v_{0},v_{i}\rangle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(u)(v-v_{i}),v_{i}\rangle
, the term
S_{23}
can be estimated similarly by

(6.33)

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})\big)v_{0},v_{i}\big\rangle\leq C(\varepsilon^{\rm FC}+L^{-d/2})\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

Hence, combining the estimates from (
6.3
) to (
6.33
), for
L
sufficiently large and the matching conditions
\varepsilon^{\rm FC},\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}
sufficiently small, we have

(6.34)

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})v,v\rangle\geq\frac{\bar{c}}{8}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

2. Consistency:

We estimate the consistency error, for any
v\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{\rm per}
, by

\displaystyle\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u}),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{u}),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}_{i=1}\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{u}),v_{i}\rangle+\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{u}),v_{0}\rangle

(6.35)

\displaystyle=:T_{1}+T_{2},

where
v_{i},i=0,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
, are constructed by (
6.27
).

To estimate
T_{1}
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
, we denote

(6.36)

\displaystyle T^{\rm(i)}_{1}:=\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{u}),v_{i}\rangle=\big\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\big(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}(\cdot-\ell_{i})\big)-\delta\mathcal{E}\big(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}(\cdot-\ell_{i})\big),v_{i}\big\rangle.

Applying
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
, we have
\|D\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}-D\bar{u}_{L}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{L})}\leq CL^{-d/2}
. Hence, given
\delta>0
, for
L
sufficiently large,
\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}\in B_{\delta}(\bar{u}_{L})
.
Recalling the definition of
\varepsilon^{\rm F}
, we can obtain

(6.37)

\displaystyle|T_{1}|\leq\sum^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}_{i=1}|T^{\rm(i)}_{1}|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot\varepsilon^{\rm F}\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

To estimate
T_{2}
, we note that
\bar{u}
is smooth in supp
\{v_{0}\}
for
L
sufficiently large according to Theorem
2
. Hence, we can Taylor expand
\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
and
\delta\mathcal{E}
at the reference configuration

\displaystyle\big\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)-\delta\mathcal{E}(\omega),v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}({\bf 0})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}({\bf 0})\big)\omega,v_{0}\big\rangle+\big\langle\big(\delta^{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(t\omega)-\delta^{3}\mathcal{E}(t\omega)\big)(\omega)^{2},v_{0}\big\rangle

(6.38)

\displaystyle=:T_{21}+T_{22},

where
t\in[0,1]
. The term
T_{21}
can be bounded by

(6.39)

\displaystyle|T_{21}|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot L^{-d/2}\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

For
T_{22}
, it can be estimated similarly

(6.40)

\displaystyle|T_{22}|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot L^{-3d/2}\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

Hence, combining from (
6.3
) to (
6.40
), we can obtain

(6.41)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{u}),v\rangle\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot\big(\varepsilon^{\rm F}+L^{-d/2}\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}+L^{-3d/2}\big)\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}.

3. Application of inverse function theorem:
With the stability (
6.34
) and consistency (
6.41
), we can apply the inverse function theorem (Lemma
A
) to obtain, for
L
sufficiently large and the matching conditions defined in (
3.3
) and (
3.6
) sufficiently small, the existence of a solution
\tilde{u}
to (
3.2
), and the estimate

\|D\bar{u}-D\tilde{u}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C^{\rm G}\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot\big(\varepsilon^{\rm F}+L^{-d/2}\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}+L^{-3d/2}\big),

where
C^{\rm G}
is independent of
N,n_{\mathcal{D}},L
. This completes the proof of (
3.7
).

4: Error in energy:
Next, we estimate the error in the energy. Recall the definition of the predictor
z
by (
6.12
), we first spilt the error in energy into two parts

(6.42)

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u})\big|\leq\big|\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})-\mathcal{E}(z)\big|+\big|\mathcal{E}(z)-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u})\big|=:E_{1}+E_{2}

The term
E_{1}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})-\mathcal{E}(z)\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{0}^{1}\big\langle\delta\mathcal{E}\big((1-s)\bar{u}+sz\big),\bar{u}-z\big\rangle\,{\rm d}s\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{0}^{1}\big\langle\delta\mathcal{E}\big((1-s)\bar{u}+sz\big)-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{u}),\bar{u}-z\big\rangle\,{\rm d}s\Big|

(6.43)

\displaystyle\leq CM_{1}\cdot\|D\bar{u}-Dz\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq Cn_{\mathcal{D}}\cdot L^{-d},

where
M_{1}
is the uniform Lipschitz constant of
\delta\mathcal{E}
.

To estimate
E_{2}
, by applying the technique used in (
6.3
), similarly we can obtain

\displaystyle\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u})-\mathcal{E}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(z)\big|+\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(z)-\mathcal{E}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\widetilde{M}_{1}\cdot\|D\tilde{u}-Dz\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}+\big|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(z)-\mathcal{E}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq Cn_{\mathcal{D}}\cdot\big(\|D\tilde{u}-D\bar{u}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}+\|D\bar{u}-Dz\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}+\varepsilon^{\rm E}\big)

(6.44)

\displaystyle\leq Cn_{D}\cdot\big(\|D\tilde{u}-D\bar{u}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}+L^{-d}+\varepsilon^{\rm E}\big),

where
\widetilde{M}_{1}
is the uniform Lipschitz constant of
\delta\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
.

Combining (
6.42
), (
6.3
) and (
6.3
), we obtain

\big|\mathcal{E}(\bar{u})-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{u})\big|\leq C^{\rm E}n_{\mathcal{D}}\cdot\Big(\big(\varepsilon^{\rm F}+L^{-d/2}\varepsilon^{\rm FC}_{\rm hom}+L^{-3d/2}\big)^{2}+L^{-d}+\varepsilon^{\rm E}\Big),

which completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

We first review a quantitative version of the inverse function theorem, adapted from
[
26
, Lemma B.1]
.

Lemma A.1.

Let
X,Y
be Hilbert spaces,
w\in X
,
F\in C^{2}(B^{X}_{R}(w);Y)
with Lipschitz continuous Hessian,
\|\delta^{2}F(x)-\delta^{2}F(y)\|_{L(X,Y)}\leq M\|x-y\|_{X}
for any
x,y\in B^{X}_{R}(w)
. Furthermore, suppose that there exist constants
\mu,r>0
such that

\langle\delta^{2}F(w)v,v\rangle\geq\mu\|v\|^{2}_{X},\quad\|\delta F(w)\|_{Y}\leq r,\quad\textrm{and}~\frac{2Mr}{\mu^{2}}<1,

then there exists a locally unique
\bar{w}\in B^{X}_{R}(w)
such that
\delta F(\bar{w})=0,\|w-\bar{w}\|_{X}\leq\frac{2r}{\mu}
and

\langle\delta^{2}F(\bar{w})v,v\rangle\geq\big(1-\frac{2Mr}{\mu^{2}}\big)\mu\|v\|^{2}_{X}.

Following
[
17
]
we define a family of truncation operators
\{\Pi_{R}^{\ell_{i}}\}_{\ell_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}
, which we will apply to the single strongly stable core
\bar{u}^{\rm core}
. Let
\eta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,1])
be a cut-off function satisfying
\eta(x)=1
for
|x|\leq 4/6
and
\eta(x)=0
for
|x|\geq 5/6
.

We denote
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
as the
canonical triangulation
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose nodes are the reference sites
\Lambda
(cf.
[
17
, Section 2.1]
). Let
Iu
be the piecewise affine interpolant of
u
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
, and
A_{R}:=B_{5R/6}\setminus B_{4R/6}
be an annulus, then we can define the truncation operator by

(A.1)

\displaystyle\Pi^{\ell_{i}}_{R}u(\ell):=\eta\Big(\frac{\ell-\ell_{i}}{R}\Big)\big(u(\ell)-a^{\ell_{i}}_{R}\big),\quad\textrm{where}\quad a^{\ell_{i}}_{R}:={\mathchoice{{-\mkern-19.0mu\int}}{{-\mkern-16.0mu\int}}{{-\mkern-16.0mu\int}}{{-\mkern-16.0mu\int}}}_{\ell_{i}+A_{R}}Iu(x)\,{\rm d}x.

In addition, we define
\Pi_{R}:=\Pi^{0}_{R}
.

In this paper, we consider multiple point defects in a finite domain
\Lambda_{N}
with periodic boundary condition. Hence, for
R\ll N
, we extend
\Pi_{R}
periodically with respect to
\Lambda_{N}
.
We state the following result concerning the approximation property of the truncation operator
\Pi_{R}
, which follows from results in
[
17
]
.

Lemma A.2.

Let
v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)
, there exists
C>0
such that, for
R
sufficiently large,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\|D\Pi_{R}v-Dv\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda\setminus B_{R/2})}.

In particular, if
\bar{u}^{\rm core}
is the core corrector satisfying
(S)
,
we have

(A.3)

\displaystyle\|D\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}-D\bar{u}^{\rm core}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}}\leq CR^{-d/2},

where
C
is independent of
R
and
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

The estimate (
A.2
) is simply a restatement of
[
22
, Lemma 4.5]
, while the second estimate (
A.3
) then follows immediately from (
6.11
).
∎

Next, we show that the assumption
(S)
implies that
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core})
is also positive for sufficiently large
R
, which follows directly from Proposition
6.1
.

Lemma A.3.

Suppose
\bar{u}^{\rm core}
satisfies
(S)
, then for sufficiently large
R
, there exist a constant
\bar{c}_{R}>0
such that

(A.4)

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core})v,v\rangle\geq\bar{c}_{R}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})},

and
\bar{c}_{R}\rightarrow\bar{c}
given in Proposition
6.1
, as
R\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

For any
v\in\mathscr{U}^{{\rm per}}_{N}
,

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core})v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(T^{\rm per}_{N}\bar{u}^{\rm core})\big)v,v\big\rangle+\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(T_{N}^{\rm per}\bar{u}^{\rm core})v,v\big\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\big(\bar{c}-M_{2}\|D\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\rm core}-D\bar{u}^{\rm core}\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}\big)\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})}

\displaystyle\geq(\bar{c}-M_{2}R^{-d/2})\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda_{N})},

where the mapping
T_{N}^{\rm per}:\mathscr{U}^{1,2}(\Lambda)\rightarrow\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{N}(\Lambda_{N})
is introduced in Section
6.1
and
M_{2}
is the uniform Lipschitz constant of
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}
since
\mathcal{E}
is
(\mathfrak{n}-1)
-times continuously differentiable. As

∎

Appendix C
The Atomic Cluster Expansion

Following
[
1
]
, we briefly introduce the construction of the ACE potential.
Given
\mathcal{N}\in\mathbb{N}
, we first write the ACE site potential in the form of an
atomic body-order expansion
,
\displaystyle V^{\rm ACE}\big(\{\boldsymbol{g_{j}}\}\big)=\sum_{N=0}^{\mathcal{N}}\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{j_{1}\neq\cdots\neq j_{N}}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j_{1}},\cdots,\boldsymbol{g}_{j_{N}})
, where the
N
-body potential
V_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
can be approximated by using a tensor product basis
[
1
, Proposition 1]
,

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{k\ell m}}\big(\{\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\big):=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{k_{j}\ell_{j}m_{j}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j})\quad

\displaystyle{\rm with}\quad\phi_{k\ell m}(\boldsymbol{r}):=P_{k}(r)Y^{m}_{\ell}(\hat{r}),~~\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~r=|\boldsymbol{r}|,~\hat{r}=\boldsymbol{r}/r,

where
P_{k},~k=0,1,2,\cdots
are radial basis functions (for example, Jacobic polynomials), and
Y_{\ell}^{m},~\ell=0,1,2,\cdots,~m=-\ell,\cdots,\ell
are the complex spherical harmonics.
The basis functions are further symmetrised to a permutation invariant form,

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{N}=\sum_{(\boldsymbol{k,\ell,m})~{\rm ordered}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k\ell m}}\circ\sigma,

where
S_{N}
is the collection of all permutations, and by
\sum_{(\boldsymbol{k,\ell,m})~{\rm ordered}}
we mean that the sum is over all lexicographically ordered tuples
\big((k_{j},\ell_{j},m_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{N}
.
The next step is to incorporate the invariance under point reflections and rotations

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{m}i}^{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{k\ell m}}\circ\sigma\quad{\rm with}\quad\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\big\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}~|~-\ell_{\alpha}\leq\mu_{\alpha}\leq\ell_{\alpha}\big\},

where the coefficients
\mathcal{U}_{mi}^{k\ell}
are given in
[
1
, Lemma 2 and Eq. (3.12)]
.
It was shown in
[
1
]
that the basis defined above is explicit but computational inefficient. The so-called “density trick” technique used in
[
3
,
16
,
36
]
can transform this basis into one that is computational efficient. The alternative basis is

\displaystyle B_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{m}i}^{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}A_{\boldsymbol{nlm}}\quad{\rm with~the~correlations}\quad A_{\boldsymbol{nlm}}:=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{n_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j}),

which avoids both the
N!
cost for symmetrising the basis as well as the
C_{J}^{N}
cost of summation over all order
N
clusters within an atomic neighbourhood.
The resulting basis set is then defined by

(C.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{N}:=\big\{B_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}i}~|~(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{2N}~{\rm ordered},~\sum_{\alpha}\ell_{\alpha}~{\rm even},~i=1,\cdots,\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}\big\},

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{k\ell}}
is the rank of body-orders (see
[
1
, Proposition 7 and Eq. (3.12)]
).

Once the finite symmetric polynomial basis set
\boldsymbol{B}\subset\bigcup^{\mathcal{N}}_{N=1}\boldsymbol{B}_{N}
is constructed, the ACE site potential can be expressed as

(C.2)

\displaystyle V^{\rm ACE}(\boldsymbol{g};\{c_{B}\}_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}})=\sum_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}}c_{B}B(\boldsymbol{g})

with the coefficients
c_{B}
. The corresponding force of this potential is denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}
.

The family of potentials are systematically improvable (see
[
1
, §6.2]
): by increasing the body-order, cutoff radius and polynomial degree they are in principle capable of representing an arbitrary many-body potential energy surface to within arbitrary accuracy.

Appendix D
A semi-empirical QM model: NRL tight binding

The NRL tight binding model is first developed in
[
14
]
. The energy levels are determined by the generalised eigenvalue problem

(D.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(y)\psi_{s}=\lambda_{s}\mathcal{M}(y)\psi_{s}\qquad\text{with}\quad\psi_{s}^{\rm T}\mathcal{M}(y)\psi_{s}=1,

where
\mathcal{H}
is the hamiltonian matrix and
\mathcal{M}(y)
is the overlap matrix. The NRL hamiltonian and overlap matrices are construct both from hopping elements as well as on-site matrix elements as a function of the local environment. For carbon and silicon they are parameterised as follows (for other elements the parameterisation is similar):

To define the on-site terms, each atom
\ell
is assigned a pseudo-atomic density

\displaystyle\rho_{\ell}:=\sum_{k}e^{-\lambda^{2}r_{\ell k}}f_{\rm c}(r_{\ell k}),

where the sum is over all of the atoms
k
within the cutoff
R_{\rm c}
of atom
\ell
,
\lambda
is a fitting parameter,
f_{\rm c}
is a cutoff function

\displaystyle f_{\rm c}(r)=\frac{\theta(R_{\rm c}-r)}{1+\exp\big((r-R_{\rm c})/l_{c}+L_{c}\big)},

with
\theta
the step function, and the parameters
l_{c}=0.5
,
L_{c}=5.0
for most elements.
Although, in principle, the on-site terms should have off-diagonal elements, but this would lead to additional computational challenges that we wished to avoid. The NRL model follows traditional practice and only include the diagonal terms.
Then, the on-site terms for each atomic site
\ell
are given by

(D.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(y)_{\ell\ell}^{\upsilon\upsilon}:=a_{\upsilon}+b_{\upsilon}\rho_{\ell}^{2/3}+c_{\upsilon}\rho_{\ell}^{4/3}+d_{\upsilon}\rho_{\ell}^{2},

where
\upsilon=s,p
, or
d
is the index for angular-momentum-dependent atomic orbitals and
(a_{\upsilon})
,
(b_{\upsilon})
,
(c_{\upsilon})
,
(d_{\upsilon})
are fitting parameters. The on-site elements for the overlap matrix are simply taken to be the identity matrix.

The off-diagonal NRL Hamiltonian entries follow the formalism of Slater and Koster who showed in
[
37
]
that all two-centre (spd) hopping integrals can be constructed from ten independent “bond integral” parameters
h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}
, where

\displaystyle(\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu)=ss\sigma,~sp\sigma,~pp\sigma,~pp\pi,~sd\sigma,~pd\sigma,~pd\pi,~dd\sigma,~dd\pi,~{\rm and}~dd\delta.

The NRL bond integrals are given by

(D.3)

\displaystyle h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}(r):=\big(e_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}+f_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r+g_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r^{2}\big)e^{-h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r}f_{\rm c}(r)

with fitting parameters
e_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu},f_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu},g_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu},h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}
. The matrix elements
\mathcal{H}(y)_{\ell k}^{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}}
are constructed from the
h_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}(r)
by a standard procedure
[
37
]
.

The analogous bond integral parameterisation of the overlap matrix
is given by

(D.4)

\displaystyle m_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}(r):=\big(\delta_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}}+p_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r+q_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r^{2}+r_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r^{3}\big)e^{-s_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}r}f_{\rm c}(r)

with the fitting parameters
(p_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}),(q_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}),(r_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu}),(s_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}\mu})
and
\delta_{\upsilon\upsilon^{\prime}}
the Kronecker delta function.

The fitting parameters in the foregoing expressions are determined by fitting to some high-symmetry first-principle calculations: In the NRL method, a database of eigenvalues (band structures) and total energies were constructed for several crystal structures at several volumes. Then the parameters are chosen such that the eigenvalues and energies in the database are reproduced.
For practical simulations, the parameters for different elements can be found in
[
34
]
.
