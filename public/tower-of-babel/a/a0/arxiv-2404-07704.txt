Evaluating matrix power series with the Cayley-Hamilton theorem
Tobias Rindlisbacher

Abstract
The Cayley-Hamilton theorem is used to implement an iterative process for the efficient numerical computation of matrix power series and their differentials. In addition to straight-forward applications in lattice gauge theory simulations e.g. to reduce the computational cost of smearing, the method can also be used to simplify the evaluation of OPEN SU ⁡ ( N CLOSE ) \operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right) one-link integrals or the computation of OPEN SU ⁡ ( N CLOSE ) \operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right) matrix logarithms.



I
Introduction

In lattice field theory, the Cayley-Hamilton theorem is most well known from studies of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
gauge-fermion theories that make use of some sort of gauge smearing to reduce UV cutoff effects. In the stout
[
1
]
and HEX
[
2
]
smearing schemes, for example, the theorem can be used to perform the required matrix exponential computations, and in the nHYP smearing scheme
[
3
]
to evaluate (inverse) matrix square roots
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Beyond applications in smearing, the theorem has been used to compute matrix logarithms in gauge fixing procedures
[
7
]
, and more recnetly, to perform simulations with an improved lattice Dirac operator that involves matrix exponentials
[
8
]
.

In most of these studies the Cayley-Hamilton theorem has, however, been applied ”manually”, in the sense that it has been used to derive algorithms which efficiently perform a given operation, e.g. matrix exponentiation, for a given matrix size. If the matrix size changes, e.g. because one intends to study a theory with
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(5}}\right)
instead of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(3}}\right)
gauge group, the algorithm needs to be adjusted. An exception to this is the application of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem as described in
[
6
]
. There it is used that the theorem can be applied by solving numerically a matrix equation that involves the Vandermonde matrix of eigenvalues of the input matrix. This latter approach is in principle applicable for any matrix size, but requires some care to deal with cases where the Vandermonde matrix becomes singular or nearly singular.

In the present work we are going to discuss a different approach which will not require the computation of matrix eigenvalues. Instead, the Cayley-Hamilton theorem is used to derive an iterative process for the efficient numerical evaluation of matrix power series of arbitrary square matrices
U
. If needed, the iterative process allows also for a simultaneous computation of the differential of the matrix power series of
U
, respectively, of its derivatives with respect to the components of the matrix
U
.

The basic idea behind our method has briefly been discussed already in a review of computational methods for coumputing matrix expnentials about 20 years ago
[
9
]
. There it was pointed out that the algorithm is not particularly favorable because of its computational complexity being
\mathcal{O}\big(N^{4}\big)
in matrix size
N
, and it potential instability. In this work we discuss a regularized version of the algorithm and present results showing that up to
N=10
it outperforms other algorithms for computing matrix exponentials with respect to speed and accuracy.

The paper is organized as follows. Sec.
II
provides a derivation of the iterative Cayley-Hamilton method for matrix power series and discusses some possible extensions. Sec.
III
describes a possible implementation of the method and how it can be extended to simultaneously compute also the differentials of a matrix power series. In Sec.
IV
the method is applied to the problem of computing matrix exponentials and to simplify the numerical computation of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrix logarithms and
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
one link integrals. In Sec.
V
we compare performance and numerical accuracy of our method for computing matrix exponentials with those of other methods and present some results. Sec.
VI
provides a summary.

II
Iterative Cayley-Hamilton

Consider a matrix
U\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
on which we would like to evaluate the matrix power series that is obtained by replacing in the Taylor series of some scalar function
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
,

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\,x^{n}\ ,

(1)

the powers of the scalar variable
x
by the corresponding matrix powers of
U
. In the following, we will use the notation
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
to refer to this procedure.

A naive evaluation of
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
is computationally expensive, since it involves a large number of matrix multiplications and summations. A more efficient way of evaluating
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
would be to perform an eigen-decomposition of
U
and apply
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
to the eigenvalues in the diagonal factor of the decomposition. However, if also the derivatives of
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
with respect to the components of
U
are required, this approach can become tedious since the factors of numerically performed eigen-decompositions are in general not smooth functions of the components of
U
. The latter problem can be overcome by the already mentioned approach descried in
[
6
]
, at the cost of having to solve Vandermonde matrix equations.

In the following we describe an alternative approach in the form of an iterative method derived from the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, which allows for an efficient computation of
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
and also of its derivatives with respect to the components of
U
.

II.1
Basic method

The Cayley-Hamilton theorem states that if
U
is an
N\times N
matrix, then any power
U^{n}
with
n\geq N
can be written as a finite superposition of powers
U^{i}
with
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
, i.e.

U^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\,a_{n,i}\,U^{i}\ .

(2)

In particular, if
n=N
, then the coefficients
a_{N,i}
,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
are given by
a_{N,i}=-c_{i}

\forall\,i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.c_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
being the set of coefficients of the characteristic polynomial
p
of
U
:

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbbm{1}\,\lambda-U}}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\,c_{i}\,\lambda^{i}\ .

(3)

We now observe, that if for some
n
the coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
are known, then we can compute
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n+1,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
by noting that

U^{n+1}=U^{n}\,U

(4)

which after applying (
2
) to
U^{n+1}
and
U^{n}
reads:

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\,a_{n+1,i}\,U^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\,a_{n,i}\,U^{i+1}\ .

(5)

In equation (
5
) the last term in the sum on the right-hand side of the equality sign (for which
i+1=N
) can be replaced by

U^{N}=-\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\,c_{i}\,U^{i}\ ,

(6)

after which a comparison of coefficients between terms of equal powers in
U
on the left- and right-hand side of the equality sign in (
5
) leads to the following recurrence relations in
n
:

\displaystyle a_{n,0}

\displaystyle=-a_{n-1,N-1}\,c_{0}\ ,

(7a)

\displaystyle a_{n,k}

\displaystyle=a_{n-1,k-1}-a_{n-1,N-1}\,c_{k}\ ,

(7b)

for
n\geq N
,
0<k\leq N-1
, and the trivial low order coefficients for
0\leq n<N
,
0\leq k\leq N-1
, given by

a_{n,k}=\delta_{n,k}\ ,

(7c)

with
\delta_{n,k}
being the Kronecker delta, serve as initial conditions. Note that each iteration step requires only
\mathcal{O}\big(N\big)
operations.

By viewing the Cayley-Hamilton coefficients
a_{n,i}
,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
at each order
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as
N
-component column vectors,

v_{n}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{n,i}}}\right)_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\ ,

(8)

the iteration rule (
7
) can be written as

v_{n}=A\,v_{n-1}\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\ ,

(9)

where the
N\times N
matrix
A
is the so-called companion matrix for the characteristic polynomial, Eq. (
3
), given by

A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}&&&&&-c_{0}\\
1&&&&&-c_{1}\\
&1&&&&-c_{2}\\
\phantom{\ddots}&\phantom{\ddots}&\ddots&\phantom{\ddots}&\phantom{\ddots}&\vdots\\[6.0pt]
&&&1&&-c_{N-2}\\
&&&&1&-c_{N-1}\\
\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)\ .

(10)

Equipped with the set of Cayley-Hamilton coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
for the matrix
U
, the function
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
from (
1
) applied to
U
can be written as:

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\,U^{n}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\,\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,a_{n,i}\,U^{i}\\
=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\biggl(\bigg.\underbrace{\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\,a_{n,i}}_{\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.53587pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.53587pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}}\bigg.\biggr)\,U^{i}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\,U^{i}\ .

(11)

II.2
Extensions

After having discussed the basic working principle of the iterative Cayley-Hamilton method for evaluating matrix power series, it is worth point out some possible extensions to the basic use case.

II.2.1
Simultaneous evaluation of multiple scalar functions

Since the coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
in (
11
) are mere functions of the characteristic polynomial coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.c_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
of
U
, and do not depend on the function
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
, the same
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
can be used to evaluate multiple functions
f_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{\alpha,n}\,x^{n}
,
\alpha=1,2,\ldots
simultaneously on
U
; one simply needs to plug the corresponding polynomial coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.r_{\alpha,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
in (
11
).

This can for example be used to efficiently evaluate
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\,U}}\right)
for a sequence of different
s\in\mathbb{C}
, as one has:

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\,U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\,U}}\right)^{n}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,\underbrace{r_{n}\,s^{n}}_{r_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)}\,U^{n}\\
=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\biggl(\bigg.\underbrace{\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\,a_{n,i}}_{\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.53587pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.53587pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)}\bigg.\biggr)\,U^{i}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\,U^{i}\ .

(12)

For given
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
, the computational cost of evaluating the coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
for a given
s\in\mathbb{C}
is about
N
times the cost of evaluating
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)
, and the cost for composing the final matrix
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\,U}}\right)
from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
and the pre-computed
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.U^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
corresponds to
\sim N^{3}
multiplication and addition operations.

II.2.2
Derivatives and differentials

A simple derivative formula follows directly from (
12
), namely that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\,U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}^{\prime}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\,U^{i}\ ,

(13)

where the prime refers to differentiation with respect to
s
, and one has,

\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}^{\prime}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}r^{\prime}_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)\,a_{n,i}\quad\forall i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}\ .

(14)

The iterative Cayley-Hamilton method allows, however, also for the computation of general differentials

\mathrm{d}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\frac{\partial f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}{\partial U^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{b}}}}\,\mathrm{d}U^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{b}}}\ ,

(15)

along with the evaluation of
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
itself. This will be discussed in detail in Sec.
III.3
.

II.2.3
Negative powers

In the case where the matrix
U
is invertible, the coefficient
c_{0}
of the characteristic polynomial of
U
is non-zero and (
7
) can be reversed to compute the coefficients for (
2
) with
n<0
, i.e. to compute negative powers of the matrix
U
. The recurrence relations then read for
n<-1
and
0\leq k<N-1
:

\displaystyle a_{n,N-1}

\displaystyle=-a_{n+1,0}/c_{0}\ ,

(16a)

\displaystyle a_{n,k}

\displaystyle=a_{n+1,k+1}-a_{n+1,0}\,c_{k+1}/c_{0}\ ,

(16b)

with the initial conditions for
n=-1
given by

a_{-1,k}=-c_{k+1}/c_{0}\ ,\ 0\leq k<N\ .

(16c)

The latter are obtained by multiplying both sides of (
6
) by
U^{-1}
and then solving for
U^{-1}
, or by simply inverting (
9
).

II.2.4
Product rule

Assume we have computed the Cayley-Hamilton decomposition coefficients for a family of matrix functions
f_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
,
k=1,2,\ldots
applied to a
N\times N
matrix
U
, so that we can write:

f_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{k,i}\,U^{i}\ .

(17)

We can then compute the Cayley-Hamilton decomposition of the product of any pair
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right),f_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}}\right)
directly from the sets of coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{k,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
and the Cayley-Hamilton coefficients
a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
for
n\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,2\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N-1}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
. We start by writing

f_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k,l}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=f_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)\,f_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{k,i}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j}\,\underbrace{U^{i+j}}_{\mathclap{\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N-1}\,a_{i+j,m}\,U^{m}}}\\
=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N-1}\biggl(\bigg.\underbrace{\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{k,i}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j}\,a_{i+j,m}}_{\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.53587pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.53587pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k,l}}\right),m}}\bigg.\biggr)\,U^{m}\ ,

(18)

and note that
a_{i+j,m}
can for
i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
be written in terms of the companion matrix (
10
) as:

a_{i+j,m}=(A^{i})^{{{m}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.71356pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.71356pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.29834pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.64166pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[4.30226pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[3.07304pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{j}}}=(A^{j})^{{{m}\mathchoice{\makebox[2.82928pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[2.82928pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.68811pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.2058pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[4.30226pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[3.07304pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{i}}}.

(19)

It follows that the Cayley-Hamilton decomposition coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k,l}}\right),m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{m\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
of the product
f_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k,l}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=f_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)\,f_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
can be computed using either of the equalities

\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k,l}}\right),m}=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{k,i}\,(A^{i})^{{{m}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.71356pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.71356pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.29834pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.64166pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[4.30226pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[3.07304pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{j}}}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j}\\
=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j}\,(A^{j})^{{{m}\mathchoice{\makebox[2.82928pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[2.82928pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.68811pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.2058pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[4.30226pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[3.07304pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{i}}}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{k,i}\ ,

(20)

with
\mathcal{O}\big(N^{2}\big)
operations, thanks to the sparseness of the companion matrix
A
.

Eq. (
20
) also holds in the case where the family of matrix functions is simply given by
f_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=U^{k}
,

, i.e. by the set of different matrix powers. We then find,

a_{k+l,m}=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\,a_{k,i}\,(A^{i})^{{{m}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.71356pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.71356pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.29834pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.64166pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[4.30226pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[3.07304pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{j}}}\,a_{l,j}\\
=\sum\limits_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\,a_{l,j}\,(A^{j})^{{{m}\mathchoice{\makebox[2.82928pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[2.82928pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[1.68811pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.2058pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[7.09612pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[4.30226pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[3.07304pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{i}}}\,a_{k,i}\ ,

(21)

which reduces to the iteration formula (
7
) when either
k=1
(using first equality) or
l=1
(using second equality), due to (
7c
).

II.2.5
Shifted power series

There are cases where the function
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
from (
1
) cannot be expanded in a power series around
x=0
. The procedure could then be carried out with a shifted function,

\tilde{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+x_{0}}}\right)\ ,

(22)

and a corresponding matrix
\tilde{U}
with shifted eigenvalues:

\tilde{U}=U-\mathbbm{1}\,x_{0}\ ,

(23)

where
x_{0}
should be chosen so that the expansion coefficients in

\tilde{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,\tilde{r}_{n}\,x^{n}

(24)

do not diverge. One then finds
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
from

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\tilde{f}\bigl(\big.\tilde{U}\big.\bigr)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 0.27777pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle\tilde{r}$}}}_{i}\,\tilde{U}^{i}\ .

(25)

Of course, this procedure is applicable only if all eigenvalues of
\tilde{U}
lay within the domain of convergence of (
24
).

III.2
Coping with round-off errors

Since the computation of the characteristic polynomial coefficients according to (
29
) involves summation of terms of potentially opposite signs, there is a reasonable possibility that the resulting coefficient values get affected by round-off errors. This can in particular happen if the summed floating point numbers are very different in magnitude. And of course, this problem might also occur in the iterative computation of the coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
according to (
7
).

In the following, we discuss two counter measures that can be taken to avoid running into problems due to round-off errors.

III.2.1
Re-scaling of input matrix

We note that (
2
) implies that the Cayley-Hamilton coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
for a matrix
U
can be related as follows to the corresponding coefficients for a scaled matrix
\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}}=s\,U
:

a_{n,i}(\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}})=a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)\,s^{n-i}\ .

(31)

And since
a_{N,i}=-c_{i}
for
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
, one has for the characteristic polynomial coefficients:

c_{i}(\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}})=c_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)\,s^{N-i}\ .

(32)

This means, that we can always perform the computation of the characteristic polynomial coefficients and of the
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
by using an appropriately scaled matrix
\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}}
. The so obtained Cayley-Hamilton coefficients,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}(\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
, can then be re-scaled when computing the coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
, using

\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\,a_{n,i}(\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}})\,s^{i-n}\ ,

(33)

instead of (
30
). Note that the
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}
obtained from (
33
) are the same as those obtained via (
30
) from the original, unscaled matrix
U
. One therefore still has

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\,P_{i}\ ,

(34)

with the
P_{i}=U^{i}

i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
being the matrix powers of the original matrix
U
.
When working instead with the powers
\tilde{P}_{i}=\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}}^{i}
,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
of the scaled matrix
\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}}=s\,U
, the factor
s^{i}
in (
33
) can be dropped, i.e. the coefficients then read

\tilde{\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}}_{i}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\,r_{n}\,a_{n,i}(\ThisStyle{\hbox to0.0pt{\hskip 1.459pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}\hss}{\hbox{$\SavedStyle U$}}})\,s^{-n}\ ,

(35)

and
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
is computed as

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\,\tilde{\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}}_{i}\,\tilde{P}_{i}\ .

(36)

III.2.2
Stabilizing the Cayley-Hamilton iteration

Regardless of whether the input matrix
U
has been re-scaled or not, the iterative computation of the Cayley-Hamilton coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
can be stabilized by normalizing the coefficients
a_{n-1,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
before they are used to compute the coefficients
a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
.

To justify this a bit better, we return to the representation of the Cayley-Hamilton iteration from Eq. (
9
), and note that the companion matrix (
10
) has the same eigenvalues as the matrix
U
. Hence, the norms of the vectors
v_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, in (
9
) will for large
n
scale like the
n
-th power of the magnitude of the largest eigenvalue of
U
. Normalizing the vector
v_{n-1}
before applying
A
to it to compute
v_{n}
prevents problems arising from the magnitudes of the
v_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
growing too large.

To be more precise, we can write each vector
v_{n}
as

v_{n}=\alpha_{n}\,\widehat{v}_{n}\ ,

(37)

with
\alpha_{n}=|v_{n}|
and
\widehat{v}_{n}=v_{n}/\alpha_{n}
. In terms of the pairs
(\alpha_{n},\widehat{v}_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the iteration rule (
9
) would then take the following form:

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{n}

\displaystyle=A\,\widehat{v}_{n-1}\ ,

(38a)

\displaystyle(\alpha_{n},\widehat{v}_{n})

\displaystyle=(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{v}_{n}}}\right|\,\alpha_{n-1},\tilde{v}_{n}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{v}_{n}}}\right|)\ ,

(38b)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The normalization of
\tilde{v}_{n}
should, however, be performed only if
|\tilde{v}_{n}|>1
, in order to avoid amplifying round-off errors. We therefore modify (
38
) slightly, and use instead:

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{n}

\displaystyle=A\,\widehat{v}_{n-1}\ ,

(39a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{n}

\displaystyle=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,|\tilde{v}_{n}|}}\right)\ ,

(39b)

\displaystyle(\alpha_{n},\widehat{v}_{n})

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\alpha}_{n}\,\alpha_{n-1},\tilde{v}_{n}/\tilde{\alpha}_{n})\ .

(39c)

In Alg.
1
the pieces of code required to implement the just described procedure are displayed in gray.

It is also worth noting, that the stabilization procedure described in this section is in principle only necessary if the magnitude of the largest eigenvalue of the input matrix
U
(and therefore of
A
) is larger than
1
; if it is smaller than
1
one has

A^{n}\ {\scriptstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\to\infty}}\right)}}{{\longrightarrow}}}\ 0\ ,

(40)

and therefore

v_{n}=A\,v_{n-1}=A^{n}\,v_{0}\ {\scriptstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\to\infty}}\right)}}{{\longrightarrow}}}\ 0\ .

(41)

The step (
39b
) ensures that (
41
) is in this case also true for the sequence of vectors
\widehat{v}_{n}
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, produced by the iteration procedure (
39
).

III.3
Computing derivative terms

In practical applications it is often necessary to compute not only
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
but also the corresponding matrix valued differentials,

\mathrm{d}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\frac{\partial f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}{\partial U^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{b}}}}\,\mathrm{d}U^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[3.51666pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.1029pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.50208pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{b}}}\ .

(42)

There are two different approaches to compute these quantities with the Cayley-Hamilton method, which differ in whether the Cayley-Hamilton expansion or the differentiation operation is first applied to
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
.

III.3.1
Differentail of Cayley-Hamilton expansion

Plugging in the Cayley-Hamilton expansion of
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
from (
11
), the differential (

) can be written as

\mathrm{d}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}+\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}P_{i}}}\right)\ ,

(43)

with

i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
being the matrix powers of
U
given in (

).
To evaluate (

and
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}
for

, where for the latter, we will in general also need the differential of

.

The differentials of the
P_{i}
,

are easily computed by iteration, using that for

\mathrm{d}P_{i}=U\,\mathrm{d}P_{i-1}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{d}P_{i-1}}}\right)\,U-U\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{d}P_{i-2}}}\right)\,U\ ,

(44a)

with

\mathrm{d}P_{0}=0\quad,\quad\mathrm{d}P_{1}=\mathrm{d}U\quad.

(44b)

Correspondingly, the differentials of the traced powers of
U
,

,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
, are given by

\mathrm{d}p_{i}=\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{d}P_{i}}}\right)\quad,\quad i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}\ .

(45)

Note that in certain cases one might only be interested in the
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\mathrm{d}\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
in (
43
) (cf. Sec.
IV.3
), in which case the
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\mathrm{d}P_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
themselves are not required and the expensive computation in (

IV.1
Computation of matrix exponentials and their differentials

The power series representation of the exponential function has infinite radius of convergence, implying that it should in principle be straightforward to evaluate matrix exponentials with the method described in the previous section. When working with finite precision floating point arithmetic, one can, however, nevertheless run into difficulties. This can already happen in the scalar case, e.g. when computing the exponential of large negative numbers with the power-series approach: the terms initially grow rapidly in magnitude while their alternating signs lead to large cancellations and corresponding round-off errors.

To avoid this kind of issue when evaluating the matrix exponential of some
U\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, one can make us of the fact that the exponential function satisfies,

\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U/n}}\right)}}\right)^{n}\ .

(63)

This leads to the so-called ”scaling and squaring” method to improving the convergence of power-series-based matrix exponential compuations
[
9
]
. The method works as follows: (a) determine the smallest
n=2^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for which
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|U}}\right|/n<c\leq 1
with respect to some matrix norm
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\cdot}}\right|
, (b) compute

V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U/n}}\right)\ ,

(64)

and (c) determine
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)}
by iterative squaring:

V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}=\bigl(\big.V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i-1}}\right)}\big.\bigr)^{2}\ ,\quad i=1,\ldots,k\ .

(65)

Since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|U}}\right|/n<c
with
0<c\leq 1
the computation of the exponential in (
64
) as power series does not suffer from rapidly growing terms and converges quickly.

In terms of the
N\times N
matrices

k

65

(66)

IV.2
Computation of logarithms of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrices

In this section we discuss a method to determine the logarithm of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrices, i.e.

\log:\quad\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)\to\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)\quad,\quad U\mapsto\omega=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)\ ,

(73)

with the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues of
\omega=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
laying in the interval
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\pi,\pi}}\right)
.

The method discussed in Sec.
II
could in principle (in its shifted form (
25
)) be used to compute the logarithm of a matrix
U
from the Mercator series representation of the logarithm,

\tilde{f}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+x}}\right)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-x}}\right)^{n}}{n}\ ,

(74)

setting
\tilde{U}=U-\mathbbm{1}
and
\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\tilde{f}(\tilde{U})
.
However, as is well known, (
74
) holds only if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x}}\right|<1
, implying that the corresponding Cayley-Hamilton expansion for a
U\in\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
would work only for a
U
that is sufficiently close to
\mathbbm{1}
so that all eigenvalues of
\tilde{U}
have modulus smaller than one.

An alternative approach to compute
\omega=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
for
U\in\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
can be reached by noting that the Lie algebra projection
A
of
U
, given by

A=P_{\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=P_{\mathrm{ah}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)-\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{N}\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P_{\mathrm{ah}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}}\right)\ ,

(75)

with

P_{\mathrm{ah}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U-U^{\dagger}}}\right)\

(76)

being the anti-hermitian projection of
U
, can serve as an estimate for
\omega=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
, which gets the better the closer
U
gets to
\mathbbm{1}
.

One can then determine
\omega=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
iteratively. Starting from
B_{0}=U
,
A_{0}=0
, one performs for subsequent
k=1,2,\ldots
the operations

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{k-1}+P_{\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{k-1}}}\right)\ ,

(77a)

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=U\,\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-A_{k}}}\right)\ ,

(77b)

till for some
k=k_{\mathrm{max}}
, one has
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|P_{\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{k-1}}}\right)}}\right|_{1}<\epsilon\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A_{k}}}\right|_{1}
, with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\cdot}}\right|_{1}
being the element-wise matrix 1-norm and
\epsilon>0
specifying the desired precision. The matrix exponential
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-A_{k}}}\right)
can be computed with the method described in Sec.
III
, using the power series coefficients
r_{n}=1/n!
for the exponential function. The number
k_{\mathrm{max}}
of iterations over (
77
) needed to obtain
\omega=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=A_{k_{\mathrm{max}}}+\mathcal{O}\big(\epsilon\big)
is typically
\sim N
or lower (depending on how far
U
is from
\mathbbm{1}
) when working with double precision floating point arithmetcis (DPFPA), and
\epsilon
set to
\epsilon=10\,N^{2}\,\epsilon_{\text{double}}
. Here
\epsilon_{\text{double}}
is the ”machine epsilon”, i.e. the difference between 1.0 and the next larger representable number in DPFPA, and the factor of
10\,N^{2}
is accounting for round-off errors in the components of
P_{\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{k-1}}}\right)
, which accumulate when evaluating the 1-norm. A C++ implementations of this method for determining the matrix logarithm of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrices is included in
[
10
]
and also available in the ”HILA” parallel programming framework
[
13
]
.

IV.3
Computation of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
one-link integrals using Cayley-Hamilton

The Cayley-Hamilton theorem can be used to evaluate
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
one-link integrals of the form

Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S,S^{\dagger}}}\right)=\int\limits_{\mathclap{\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}}\mathrm{d}U\,\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(US+U^{\dagger}S^{\dagger}}}\right)}\ ,

(78)

where
S\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and
\mathrm{d}U
is the
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
Haar measure. Our starting point is the formula by Brower et al.
[
14
]
,

Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S,S^{\dagger}}}\right)=C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,l\,\theta}Z_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)\\
=C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)+2\,\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(l\,\theta}}\right)\,Z_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)}}\right)\ ,

(79)

with

C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N-1}\,k!

(80)

and

Z_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.z_{i}^{j}I_{j+l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\,z_{i}}}\right)\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.x_{i}^{j}\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}\ ,

(81)

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
is the set of
N
eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrix
M=S^{\dagger}S
and we defined
z_{i}=\sqrt{x_{i}}

\forall\,i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
and
\theta=\arg\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right)
. The notation
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{ij}}}\right)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
refers to a matrix with elements
A_{ij}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{ij}}}\right)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
to the corresponding matrix determinant. The function
I_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
\nu
, and

V\bigl(\big.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}\big.\bigr)=\bigl(\big.x_{i}^{j}\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\\
=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&x_{0}^{1}&\cdots&x_{0}^{N-1}\\
1&x_{1}^{1}&\cdots&x_{1}^{N-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
1&x_{N-1}^{1}&\cdots&x_{N-1}^{N-1}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)

(82)

is the Vandermonde matrix with respect to the support points
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
.

While Eq. (
79
) in combination with (
81
) is more appropriate for the numerical evaluation of the one link integral (
78
) than the derivative formula given in
[
15
]
, or the expansions in terms of Weingarten or Schur functions presented in
[
16
]
, the computation of the
Z_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)
via Eq. (
79
) is only possible if
S^{\dagger}S
has full rank and no coinciding eigenvalues, as otherwise the Vandermonde determinant in the denominator of Eq. (
79
) would become zero. The latter case can be dealt with by modifying Eq. (
81
) to be

\tilde{Z}_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{i}^{j}I_{j+l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\,z_{i}}}\right)}}\right)}{\mathrm{d}z_{i}^{m_{i}}}\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m_{i}}z_{i}^{2\,j}}{\mathrm{d}z_{i}^{m_{i}}}\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}\ ,

(83)

where the integer
m_{i}
states how many times the value of
z_{i}
appears among the
z_{j}
with
j<i
. If all
z_{i}
,
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
are distinct one has
m_{i}=0

\forall i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
and Eq. (
83
) redues to Eq. (
81
). On the other hand, if e.g.
z_{0}=z_{1}
, one has
m_{i}=0
for
i\neq 1
and
m_{1}=1
, if
Z_{0}=z_{1}=z_{2}
, one has
m_{i}=0
for
i\neq 1,2
and
m_{1}=1
,
m_{2}=2
, and if e.g.
Z_{0}=z_{1}
and
z_{2}=z_{3}
, one has
m_{i}=0
for
i\neq 1,3
, and
m_{1}=1
,
m_{3}=1
, and so on.

In the remainder of this section, we will show how the Cayley-Hamilton theorem can be used to write Eq. (
81
) in terms of
S
resp.
M
, without reference to the set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
of eigenvalues of
M
or the corresponding square roots,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
. Furthermore, the method we are about to describe for computing the
Z_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
from Eq. (
81
), can be applied regardless of whether
M
is degenerate or has repeated eigenvalues.

To apply the Cayley-Hamilton method to the computation of Eq. (
81
), we recall the power series representation of the modified Bessel functions of the first kind,

I_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\,z}}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{\nu+2\,m}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu+m}}\right)!\,m!}\ .

(84)

From Eq. (
84
) it follows that if we define

B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{l!\,x^{m+j}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(l+m+j}}\right)!\,m!}=\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}\,r_{l,j,n}\,x^{n}\ ,

(85)

with

r_{l,j,n}=\frac{l!}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(l+n}}\right)!\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-j}}\right)!}\ ,

(86)

then

z^{\nu}I_{l+\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\,z}}\right)=\frac{z^{l}}{l!}B_{l,\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z^{2}}}\right)\ ,

(87)

and we can write Eq. (
81
) in the form

Z_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right|^{l}}{l!^{N}}\,\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i}}}\right)\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.x_{i}^{j}\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}\ .

(88)

Since
B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
has infinite radius of convergence, we can replace the scalar argument
x
by the matrix
M=S^{\dagger}S
and apply the Cayley-Hamilton theorem to write

B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\,\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\,M^{k}\ .

(89)

In terms of the eigenvalues
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
of
M=S^{\dagger}S
, Eq. (
89
) can also be written as:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{0}}}\right)\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1}}}\right)\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
\vdots\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{N-1}}}\right)\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)=\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&x_{0}^{1}&\cdots&x_{0}^{N-1}\\
1&x_{1}^{1}&\cdots&x_{1}^{N-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
1&x_{N-1}^{1}&\cdots&x_{N-1}^{N-1}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)}_{V(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.53587pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.53587pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.53587pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.53587pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
\vdots\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\\
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.53587pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.53587pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,N-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\vphantom{x_{0}^{N-1}}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)\ ,

(90)

from which it follows that

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i}}}\right)\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.x_{i}^{j}\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}}\\
=\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.V^{-1}\bigl(\big.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}\big.\bigr)\bigl(\big.B_{l,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i}}}\right)\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\big.\bigr)\\
=\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\ ,

(91)

and therefore:

Z_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right|^{l}}{l!^{N}}\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\\
=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right|^{l}}{l!^{N}}\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.R_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\big.\bigr)\ ,

(92)

where on the last line we set

R_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)=\bigl(\big.\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\big.\bigr)_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\ .

(93)

The components
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)
of
R_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)
are obtained as

\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)=\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}\,r_{l,j,n}\,a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\ ,

(94)

with the
r_{l,j,n}
defined in (
86
),
and the coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}
being determined with the method described in Sec.
III
. Since it is desirable to compute all
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)
simultaneously so that each coefficient
a_{n,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)
is computed only once, the stopping criterion for the summation over
n
in Alg.
1
is modified so that the summation stops as soon as all relevant
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}
remain unchanged within machine precision for at least
\mathrm{nhl\_max}
consecutive iterations. The number of relevant
l
-values,
l\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,1,\ldots,l_{\mathrm{max}}-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
for which the coefficients (
94
) need to be computed is determined in advance by finding the smallest integer
l_{\mathrm{max}}>0
for which within machine precision:

\sum_{l=0}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}+1}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right|^{l}}{l!^{N}}=\sum_{l=0}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)}}\right|^{l}}{l!^{N}}\ .

(95)

By plugging Eq. (
92
) into Eq. (
79
) we arrives at the following expression for
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
one-link integrals:

Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S,S^{\dagger}}}\right)=C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)\biggl(\bigg.\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.R_{0}(S^{\dagger}S)\big.\bigr)\,+\,\\
\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits^{l}(S)+\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits^{l}(S^{\dagger})}{l!^{N}}\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\bigl(\big.R_{l}(S^{\dagger}S)\big.\bigr)\bigg.\biggr)\ ,

(96)

from which it is not too difficult to determine the additional steps that would be required in the just described procedure to compute not only
Z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S,S^{\dagger}}}\right)
, but also its derivatives with respect to the components of
S
or
S^{\dagger}
. The derivatives of
R_{l}(S^{\dagger}S)
can be determined along the lines discussed in Sec.
III.3
.

The determinant of the matrix
S
and those of the matrices
R_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\dagger}S}}\right)
are best computed via LU decomposition. It is also advisable to rescale the matrix
M=S^{\dagger}S
for computing the
R_{l}(M)
matrices, as discussed in Sec.
III.2.1
, and to use the stabilization procedure for the Cayley-Hamilton iteration, described in Sec.
III.2.2
.

A C++ implementation of the here discussed method for computing
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
one-link integrals can be found under
[
17
]
. The latter implementation is intended for applications in
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
lattice gauge theory in
d
dimensions, so that
S
is assumed to be a sum of
n_{\text{stap.}}=2\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)

\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
staple matrices, multiplied by an inverse gauge coupling factor,
\beta/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\,N}}\right)
. The rescaling factor
s
for the matrix
M
is then chosen to be
s=b^{-2}
with

b=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)\beta/N\ .

(97)

This choice of scaling factor results in a rescaled matrix
M
whose largest eigenvalue has magnitude smaller than
1
, in which case one might want to modify the iteration stabilization procedure, discussed in Sec.
III.2.2
, so that it prevents the coefficients not just from growing too big, but also from becoming too small.

The factor
b
form (
97
) can furthermore be used to improve the condition numbers for the determinant computations of the matrices
R_{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)
,
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, namely by multiplying each element
\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)
of (
93
) by a corresponding factor
b^{i-j}
, using that:

\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right))_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\\
=\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(b^{i-j}\ThisStyle{\hskip 0.10532pt\hbox to0.0pt{\hbox{$\SavedStyle r$}\hss}{\hskip-0.10532pt\hbox{$\overline{\hbox{}}$}}}_{l,j,i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right))_{i,j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.0,\ldots,N-1\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}}\ .

(98)

Unfortunately, also after this balancing procedure the condition numbers of these matrices can still be large.

V
Numerical tests

V.1
Matrix exponentiation

We compare two implementations of the iterative Cayley-Hamilton method for computing matrix exponentials with two other methods. One of the two Cayley-Hamilton implementations uses
direct
input matrix re-scaling (CH+dSc), discussed in Sec.
III.2.1
. This approach is equally applicable to the computation of other, more general matrix power series. The other Cayley-Hamilton implementation uses the
scaling and squaring
approach (CH+Sc/Sq), discussed in in Sec.
IV.1
. For better comparability, both methods use the same re-scaling factor, computed according to the scaling and squaring recipe, where for an input matrix
U
, the re-scaling factor
s
is set to
s=2^{-k}
, with
k\in\mathbb{N}
being the smallest integer for which
2^{-k}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|U}}\right|\leq 1
, with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\cdot}}\right|
being the Frobenius norm.

The methods to compare with are the adaptive Taylor series expansion with scaling and squaring (TS+Sc/Sq) and the 6th-order diagonal Padé approximation with scaling and squaring (Pd6+Sc/Sq) from
[
9
]
, which will serve as reference. For the Taylor series method, the scaling and squaring re-scaling factor is computed in the same way as for the Cayley-Hamilton methods. For the Pd6+Sc/Sq method, it is determined with the maximum absolute column sum norm,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\cdot}}\right|_{\infty}
, instead of the Frobenius norm.

We use
\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrices to test and compare the different methods. For each
N\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.2,3,\ldots,9,10,15,20\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
we prepare three sets,
S_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
, of
10^{4}
random
\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrices, sampled as Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}-1}
(spanned by the generators of
\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
) and scaled to have fixed Frobenius norm
r
with
r=\pi
,
3\,\pi
, and
r=4\,\pi
. The motivation for using
\pi
as reference unit for the matrix norm is, that since the matrix norm is an upper bound for the magnitude of the largest eigenvalue of a matrix, it is for
r\leq\pi
guaranteed that the matrices are located within the injectivity domain
I_{\exp}
of the exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)\to\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
(cf.
[
18
]
,
[
19
, Sec. II.B]
), and as long as
s\,U\in I_{\exp}
, with
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
U\in\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
, the matrix exponential
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s\,U}}\right)\in\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
does not show oscillatory behavior as function of
s
, which would give rise to cancellations and numerical loss of accuracy under
direct
re-scaling in the CH+dSc method.

For the tests we pre-compute for every matrix
U\in S_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
its ”exact” matrix exponential
\exp_{\mathrm{ex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
to 50 digits precision, using the MatrixExp routine of Wolfram Mathematica (variable-order Padé approximation). The sets of matrix pairs
\tilde{S}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U,\exp_{\mathrm{ex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}}\right)|U\in S_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
are then converted to double precision and stored as binary data for later use by the test programs (see
[
10
]
for test data and programs).

The test programs then measure for each method
m\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.\text{CH+Sc/Sq},\text{CH+dSc},\text{TS+Sc/Sq},\text{Pd6+Sc/Sq}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}
and set of matrices
\tilde{S}_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
the time
t_{m}
required to compute 100 times the matrix exponential of all
U\in S_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
, as well as the relative errors

\epsilon_{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\exp_{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)-\exp_{\mathrm{ex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}}\right|}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\exp_{\mathrm{ex}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)}}\right|}\ .

(99)

matrices for

As can be seen, up to

of the exponential map

, but more slowly than for the CH+dSc method.

Since the accuracies of the two iterative Cayley-Hamilton methods appear to drop below the accuracy of the Pd6+Sc/Sq method only for

and which are typically of moderate Frobenius norm due to small pre-factors like small smearing coefficients or small time steps required by the used HMC or gradient flow integration schemes.

To test the iterative Cayley-Hamilton method for computing matrix exponentials in a use case where also the differentials of the exponentials are required, we set up HMC simulations of pure
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)

-step stout smeared Wilson gauge action
S_{G}[V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n_{s}}}\right)}[U]]

U=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{}}\right.U_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.}}\right\}_{x,\mu}

U_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\in\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)

\forall x,\mu

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\mu}}\right)

x

\mu=1,\ldots,d

The Wilson gauge
S_{G}

S_{G}\bigl[\big.U\big.\bigr]=\frac{\beta}{N}\sum\limits_{x,\mu<\nu}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\big.\mathbbm{1}-U_{\mu\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\big.\bigr)\ ,

where,
\beta=2\,N/g_{0}^{2}

U_{\mu\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=U_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\,U_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\widehat{\mu}}}\right)\,U_{\mu}^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\widehat{\nu}}}\right)\,U_{\nu}^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\ .

(101)

VI
Summary

The Cayley-Hamilton theorem has been used in Sec.
II
to derive an iterative process that allows for the efficient computation of matrix power series
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
on arbitrary square matrices
U
. For moderately sized matrices the method is significantly faster than a naive evaluation of
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
and, if necessary, also allows for the simultaneous computation of the derivatives of
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
with respect to the components of
U
.

A possible implementation of the basic algorithm was discussed in Sec.
III
.

The algorithm is well suited for use in HMC simulations
[
11
]
of lattice gauge theories. For example, to efficiently perform the matrix exponentials required for stout
[
1
]
and HEX
[
2
]
smearing, and the computation of the HMC force for smeared actions. When applied to the computation of matrix exponentials and their derivatives, the method can efficiently be combined with scaling and squaring
[
9
]
.

In Sec.
IV
we proposed two further applications of the iterative Cayley-Hamilton method, namely to ease the numerical computation of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
one link integrals and to compute matrix logarithms of
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrices.

In Sec.
V
we applied two versions of the iterative Cayley-Hammilton method for computing matrix exponentials (with and without scaling and squaring) to the problem of mapping
\mathfrak{su}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
matrices to
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
, and compared their performances to those of two other matrix exponentiation methods: a 6th-order diagonal Padé method with scaling and squaring
[
9
]
and an adaptive Taylor series method with scaling and squaring. As expected the Taylor series method was computationally the most expensive one, requiring about 1.4-1.8 times the computer time of the Padé method. However, the Taylor series method turned out to produce slightly more accurate results. The iterative Cayley-Hamilton methods clearly outperformed the Padé method for
N\leq 10
. For
N<7
the scaling and squaring version of the Cayley-Hamilton method required only about half the computer time of the Padé method while being equally accurate as long as the Frobenius norms of the input matrices did not significantly exceed the value
\sim 3\,\pi
(cf. Fig.
1
). For input matrices of larger norms, the accuracies of the Cayley-Hamilton methods started to drop faster than those of the other methods. For applications in lattice gauge theory simulations, the latter is of little concern, since the matrices whose exponentials need to be computed in such applications have typically relatively small norms due to small pre-factors like small smearing coefficients or small HMC or gradient flow integration time steps. We performed HMC simulations with two-step stout smeared Wilson gauge actions for
\operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
,
N=2,3,\ldots,10
. With isotropic stout smearing coefficient
c=0.15
, the largest observed Frobenius norms of the matrices, of which the exponentials (and their differentials) had to be computed in these simulations, were between 1.0 (
N=3
) and 1.4 (
N=10
), far from the limit where the Cayley-Hamilton methods would start to loose accuracy.
