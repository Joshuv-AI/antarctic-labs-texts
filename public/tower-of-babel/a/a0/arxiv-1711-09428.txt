Sparse juntas on the biased hypercube


Abstract
We give a structure theorem for Boolean functions on the p p -biased hypercube which are ε \varepsilon -close to degree d d in L 2 L_{2} , showing that they are close to sparse juntas . Our structure theorem implies that such functions are O ⁡ ( ε C d + p ) O(\varepsilon^{C_{d}}+p) -close to constant functions. We pinpoint the exact value of the constant C d C_{d} . We also give an analogous result for monotone Boolean functions on the biased hypercube which are ε \varepsilon -close to degree d d in L 2 L_{2} , showing that they are close to sparse DNFs . Our structure theorems are optimal in the following sense: for every d , ε , p d,\varepsilon,p , we identify a class ℱ d , ε , p \mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p} of degree d d sparse juntas which are O ⁡ ( ε ) O(\varepsilon) -close to Boolean (in the monotone case, width d d sparse DNFs) such that a Boolean function on the p p -biased hypercube is O ⁡ ( ε ) O(\varepsilon) -close to degree d d in L 2 L_{2} if and only if it is O ⁡ ( ε ) O(\varepsilon) -close to a function in ℱ d , ε , p \mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p} .

Introduction

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function on the hypercube, where
\{0,1\}^{n}
is endowed with the uniform measure. If
f
has degree
1
then it is a dictator, and if it has degree
d
then it is a junta
[
13
]
. Friedgut, Kalai, and Naor (FKN)
[
7
]
showed that if
f
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
1
(that is,
\|f^{>1}\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon
) then
f
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to a dictator (that is,
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\varepsilon)
for some dictator
g
). Kindler and Safra
[
12
,
11
]
(see also
[
9
]
) showed that if
f
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
then
f
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to a
2^{O(d)}
-junta.

Kindler and Safra proved their result in the more general setting of the biased hypercube, which is
\{0,1\}^{n}
endowed with the biased measure
\mu_{p}
. They showed that if
f
is a Boolean function on the
p
-biased hypercube which is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
then
f
is
O(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{5/4}/p^{4d})
-close to an
O(1/p^{4d})
-junta, assuming
p\leq 1/2
. When
p
is constant, this gives the same statement as before, but when
p
is small, the result is no longer tight.

The biased hypercube in the small
p
regime is interesting for at least two reasons. First, it is the regime that is most relevant for understanding threshold phenomena in the Erdős–Rényi random graph model
G(n,p)
. Second, it is a good model for other domains with sparse inputs, such as the symmetric group and the Grassmann scheme, the latter of which was recently used to prove the 2-to-2 games conjecture
[
10
]
.

In this paper, we extend the FKN and Kindler–Safra theorems to functions on the biased hypercube, proving a statement which does not deteriorate as
p
gets small. For every
d,\varepsilon
and
p\leq 1/2
we find an “explicit” set of Boolean functions
\mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
(consisting of degree
d
“sparse juntas”, a concept we define below) such that

1.

If
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
then
f
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to a function in
\mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
.

2.

Every function in
\mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to degree
d
with respect to
\mu_{p}
.

Let us demonstrate what we mean by
explicit
using the case
d=1
, which appears in earlier work of Filmus
[
5
]
. Filmus showed that we can take

\mathcal{F}_{1,\varepsilon,p}=\left\{\bigvee_{i\in S}y_{i},\lnot\bigvee_{i\in S}y_{i}:|S|\leq\max(1,C\sqrt{\varepsilon}/p)\right\},

for some constant
C
, where
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is the input point.

The functions in
\mathcal{F}_{1,\varepsilon,p}
are
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
-close to dictators and so
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+p)
-close to some constant function, and the same holds for every Boolean function which is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
1
. This shows that if we consider Boolean functions which are
\varepsilon
-close to degree
1
up to an error of magnitude
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, then all we see is dictators. If we look closer, narrowing the magnitude of the error to
\varepsilon
, then a more nuanced picture emerges. We will be interested in both points of view. We state our results formally in
Section

1.1
.

When
d>1
, one cannot hope for a set
\mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
which is as explicit as in the case
d=1
. Indeed, even a complete description of all Boolean degree
d
functions (corresponding to the case
\varepsilon=0
) is not available. The set
\mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
that we give is as explicit as we were able to make it; perhaps the reader can improve it. Moreover, it allows us to show that Boolean functions which are
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
are
O(\varepsilon^{C})
-close to Boolean degree
d
functions, for an appropriate constant
C
, demonstrating the applicability of our main structure theorem.

The proof of Kindler and Safra deteriorates with
p
due to the use of hypercontractivity. In contrast, our proof works by reduction to the unbiased setting, and so does not suffer from this deterioration. In more detail, we express the biased hypercube as a weighted average of unbiased hypercubes of smaller dimension:

(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\approx(\{0,1\}^{\mathbf{S}},\mu_{1/2}),\text{ where }\mathbf{S}\sim\mu_{2p}.

We apply the
unbiased
Kindler–Safra theorem on each unbiased hypercube
\{0,1\}^{S}
, and combine the results using an
agreement theorem
. We describe our methods in more detail in
Section

1.2
.

1.1
Main results

Here is the generalization of the result of Filmus
[
5
]
to arbitrary
d
. The statement uses various terms which we explain after it. The statement is heavily inspired by the FKN theorem for functions on the symmetric group due to Filmus
[
4
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Main)
.

Suppose that
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
, where
p\leq 1/2
. Then
f
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to
\round(g,\{0,1\})
, where
g
is a degree
d
polynomial satisfying the following properties, for some constant
C
depending only on
d
:

1.

If
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
has (Hamming) weight at most
d
then
g(y)\in\{0,1\}
.

2.

For every
T
and
e
, the monomial support of
g
contains at most
C/p^{e}
sets
U\supseteq T
of size
|T|+e
.

3.

For all
e
, the number of inputs
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
of (Hamming) weight
e
such that
g(y)\notin\{0,1\}
but
g(z)\in\{0,1\}
for all
z<y
is at most
C\varepsilon/p^{e}
.

Conversely, if
g
is a degree
d
polynomial satisfying these properties then
\round(g,\{0,1\})
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to degree
d
.

Rather than specifying the list
\mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
directly, we instead specify a list
\mathcal{G}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
of degree
d
functions. The functions in
\mathcal{F}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
are obtained by rounding the functions in
\mathcal{G}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
to Boolean. The corresponding list for
d=1
is

\mathcal{G}_{1,\varepsilon,p}=\left\{\sum_{i\in S}y_{i},1-\sum_{i\in S}y_{i}:|S|\leq\max(1,C\sqrt{\varepsilon}/p)\right\}.

A degree
d
polynomial
g
is in
\mathcal{G}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
if it satisfies the three properties listed in the theorem. To explain these properties, let us introduce the
monomial expansion
of
g
, which is its unique expansion as a linear combination of monomials
y_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}y_{i}
. We denote the coefficient of
y_{S}
by
\tilde{g}(S)
; these are the
monomial coefficients
of
g
.

The monomial expansion differs from the more usual Fourier expansion, in which instead of the monomials
y_{S}
we have the Fourier characters
\omega_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}\frac{y_{i}-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}
. Using the monomial expansion guarantees that the functions in
\mathcal{G}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
have the same form regardless of
p
(cf.
\sum_{i\in S}y_{i}
to
\sum_{i\in S}\sqrt{p(1-p)}\frac{y_{i}-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}+p|S|
). Concretely, the monomial coefficients appearing in the functions in
\mathcal{G}_{d,\varepsilon,p}
depend only on
d
, a property which fails for the Fourier expansion.

Item

1

states that
g(y)\in\{0,1\}
whenever
y
has (Hamming) weight at most
d
. Equivalently, if
|S|\leq d
then

\sum_{T\subseteq S}\tilde{g}(T)\in\{0,1\}.

This implies that the monomial coefficients of
g
are bounded integers (implying that
g
is integer-valued, as a function on
\{0,1\}^{n}
). As an example, when
d=1
, the constant coefficient is either
0
or
1
; in the former case, all other coefficients are
0
or
1
; and in the latter case, all other coefficients are
0
or
-1
.

Item

2

concerns the
monomial support
of
g
, which is the set

\supp(g)=\{S\subseteq[n]:\tilde{g}(S)\neq 0\}.

Choosing
T=\emptyset
, the property states that the expected number of monomials in
\supp(g)
which evaluate to
1
under a random input is
O(1)
. Considering other
T
, the same holds even under the condition
y_{T}=1
. For these reasons, we call a polynomial satisfying this property a
sparse junta
.

This item fails for the Fourier expansion. For example, the Fourier support of the function
\sum_{i=1}^{C/p^{2}}y_{2i-1}y_{2i}
contains
2C/p^{2}
degree-1 monomials rather than just
O(1/p)
.

Item

3

concerns
minimal non-Boolean inputs
. An input
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is a minimal non-Boolean input of
g
if
g(y)\notin\{0,1\}
but
g(z)\in\{0,1\}
for all inputs
z
strictly below
y
, that is, obtained from
y
by switching one or more
1
s to
0
s.
Equivalently, if
y=\mathbbm{1}_{S}
then the restriction
g|_{S}
obtained by zeroing out all coordinates outside of
S
is Boolean except for the input
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
. Due to property
Item

1
, every minimal non-Boolean input occurring in
g
has (Hamming) weight at least
d+1
.

We can think of a minimal non-Boolean input as a forbidden configuration, which we can describe by specifying the function
g|_{S}
. When
d=1
, there are two minimal non-Boolean inputs, corresponding to the functions

y_{1}+y_{2}\text{ and }1-y_{1}-y_{2}.

If
\tilde{g}(\emptyset)=0
then only the first one
y_{1}+y_{2}
is possible. Defining
m=|\supp(g)|
, the number of minimal non-Boolean inputs is
\binom{m}{2}
, and so
Item

3
states that
\binom{m}{2}=O(\varepsilon/p^{2})
. This implies that either
m\leq 1
or
m=O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}/p)
. The case
\tilde{g}(\emptyset)=1
is similar.

We list all minimal non-Boolean inputs for
d=2
in
Section

2.2
, where we also show that the number of such inputs in independent of
n
.

Monotone version

When the function
f
is monotone, we can guarantee that the function
g
is monotone as well, in fact a
monotone width
d
DNF
, which is a disjunction of
minterms

y_{i_{1}}\land\cdots\land y_{i_{e}}
with
e\leq d
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\{0,1\}
is a monotone function which is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
, where
p\leq 1/2
. Then
f
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to a monotone width
d
DNF
g
, where
g
satisfies the following properties, for some constant
C
depending only on
g
:

1.

For every
T
and
e
, the DNF
g
contains at most
C/p^{e}
minterms of
U\supseteq T
of size
|U|+e
.

2.

For all
e
, the number of sets
S
of size
e
such that
\deg(g|_{S})>d
but
\deg(g|_{T})\leq d
for all
T\subsetneq S
is at most
C\varepsilon/p^{e}
.

Conversely, if
g
is a monotone width
d
DNF satisfying these properties then it is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to degree
d
.

When
d=1
, the unique forbidden configuration (corresponding to
Item

2
) is
y_{1}\lor y_{2}
. We list all forbidden configurations for
d=2
in
Section

2.2
, where we also show that there is a finite number of them for every
d
.

Junta approximation

The FKN theorem of Filmus
[
5
]
implies that if
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
1
then it is
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
-close to a dictator and
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+p)
-close to a constant.
Theorem

1.1
implies a similar result for arbitrary
d
, where
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is replaced by an appropriate power of
\varepsilon
.

Theorem 1.3

(Junta approximation)
.

Suppose that
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
, where
p\leq 1/2
. There are parameters
m,M
, depending only on
d
, such that:

1.

f
is
O(\varepsilon^{1/M})
-close to a Boolean degree
d
function.

2.

f
is
O(\varepsilon^{1/m}+p)
-close to a Boolean constant function.

When
d=1
, the optimal value of both
m
and
M
is
2
, which is tight when
f
is obtained by rounding the following function to Boolean:

g=y_{1}+\cdots+y_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}/p}.

We can generalize this construction to arbitrary
d
. Let
P
be a degree
d
polynomial such that
P(0),\ldots,\allowbreak{P(m-1)}\in\{0,1\}
and
P(0)\neq P(1)
(von zur Gathen and Roche
[
14
]
studied such non-constant polynomials without the restriction
P(0)\neq P(1)
), and take the degree
d
polynomial

g=P(y_{1}+\cdots+y_{\varepsilon^{1/m}/p}).

The input to
P
behaves roughly like a Poisson random variable with expectation
\lambda=\varepsilon^{1/m}
, and so
\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]=O(\lambda^{m})=O(\varepsilon)
. This implies that
\round(g,\{0,1\})
is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to degree
d
.

Since
P(1)\neq P(0)
, a similar calculation shows that
\Pr[g=P(0)]\approx 1-\Theta(\lambda)
and that
\Pr[g=P(1)]\approx\Theta(\lambda)
, and so
g
is
\Theta(\lambda)
-far from all constant functions.

Using a Ramsey-theoretic argument, we show that this kind of construction is the best possible for
m
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Given
d
, the optimal value of
m
in
Theorem

1.1
is the maximum
m
such that there exists a degree
d
polynomial
P
satisfying
P(0),\ldots,P(m-1)\in\{0,1\}
and
P(0)\neq P(1)
.

As an example, when
d=2
we get
m=4
, corresponding to the polynomial
P(z)=\frac{z(3-z)}{2}
.

It is an intriguing open question to find the optimal value of
M
. Another interesting exponent is the best
\nu
such that
f
is
O(\varepsilon^{1/\nu})
-close to some Boolean junta or to some degree
d
junta (both exponents are the same).

1.2
Proof sketch

We prove
Theorem

1.1
by reduction to the Kindler–Safra theorem, in the following form.

Theorem 1.5

(Kindler–Safra)
.

If
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
then there is a Boolean degree
d
function
g
which is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to
f
.

(While Kindler and Safra did not state their theorem in this way, this formulation easily follows from their result.)

Suppose we are given a function
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\{0,1\}
which is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
. We look at restrictions
f|_{S}
obtained by zeroing out the coordinates outside of
S
. If
S\sim\mu_{2p}
then on average,
f|_{S}
is close to degree
d
with respect to the uniform measure on
\{0,1\}^{S}
:

\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}\left[\EE_{\mu_{1/2}}[(f|_{S}-f^{\leq d}|_{S})^{2}]\right]=\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-f^{\leq d})^{2}]\leq\varepsilon.

Indeed, if
S\sim\mu_{2p}
and
y\sim\mu_{1/2}(\{0,1\}^{S})
then the marginal distribution of
y
is
\mu_{p}
.

Applying the Kindler–Safra theorem for every
f|_{S}
, we obtain a Boolean degree
d
function
g_{S}
such that

\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}\left[\EE_{\mu_{1/2}}[(f|_{S}-g_{S})^{2}]\right]=O(\varepsilon).

(1)

We would like to construct the function
g
by pasting together the various functions
g_{S}
, and for that we need to know that the
g_{S}
agree with each other, on average. This sort of pasting is achieved using
agreement theorems
, and in this case we use the following theorem, with
K
being the maximal size of the monomial support of a Boolean degree
d
function,
q=2p
, and
c=\sqrt{1/2}
.

Theorem 1.6

(Junta agreement theorem)
.

Fix the following parameters: integer
K
(junta size),
q\in(0,1)
(bias),
c\in(0,1)
(fractional intersection size).

For each
S\subseteq[n]
, let
g_{S}
be a degree
d
polynomial whose monomial support contains at most
K
monomials.

Suppose that the following agreement condition holds:

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\sim\mu_{cq}\\
T_{1},T_{2}\sim\mu_{r}\end{subarray}}[g_{T\cup T_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{T\cup T_{2}}|_{T}]\leq\varepsilon,\text{ where }r=\frac{(1-c)q}{1-cq}.

(The value of
r
is chosen so that the marginal distribution of
S_{1}:=T\cup T_{1}
and
S_{2}:=T\cup T_{2}
is
\mu_{q}
.)

Then there exists a degree
d
polynomial
g
such that

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]=O_{K,c}(\varepsilon).

(The error bound depends on
K
and
c
but is independent of
q
.)

Moreover, for each
A
, the monomial coefficient
\tilde{g}(A)
is chosen by majority decoding: it maximizes

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\mu_{2p}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S}(A)=\tilde{g}(A)].

The junta agreement theorem follows from the more general agreement theorem in our earlier work
[
2
]
, which does not require a bound on the size of the monomial support. However, since the proof of the junta agreement theorem is much easier than the proof of the general agreement theorem, we include it in this paper.

In order to apply
Theorem

1.6
, we need to show that the condition in the theorem holds. Since
f|_{S_{1}\cap T}=f|_{S_{2}\cap T}
,
Equation

1
implies that

\EE_{S_{1},S_{2}}[(g_{S_{1}}|_{T}-g_{S_{2}}|_{T})^{2}]=O(\varepsilon).

Since
g_{S_{1}},g_{S_{2}}
are both juntas, either
g_{S_{1}}|_{T}=g_{S_{2}}|_{T}
or
\Pr[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]=\Omega(1)
, and this implies the agreement condition of
Theorem

1.6
.

Applying
Theorem

1.6
, we obtain a function
g
such that

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{p}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]=O(\varepsilon).

A short argument now shows that
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\varepsilon)
.

Since
g
is obtained by majority decoding and each
g_{S}
is a junta, we can show that
g
is a sparse junta, proving
Item

2
. This allows us to show that
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
, a step which we highlight below.

Next, let us show why
Item

3
holds. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
be a minimal non-Boolean input of
g
. Since
g
is a sparse junta, the probability that
y_{S}=1
and
y_{T}=0
for all other minimal non-Boolean inputs
\mathbbm{1}_{T}
is
\Omega(p^{|S|})
. These events are disjoint, and so

\Pr[f\neq g]\geq\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]\geq\sum_{S}\Omega(p^{|S|}),

where the sum goes over all minimal non-Boolean inputs of
g
. This implies
Item

3
.

Finally, let us address
Item

1
. The original function
g
doesn’t necessarily satisfy it. However, an argument along the lines of the preceding paragraph shows that the number of offending inputs is small, allowing us to slightly perturb
g
so that it satisfies
Item

1
.

From
L_{0}
to
L_{2}
using the reverse union bound

In the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, we deduce the
L_{2}
guarantee
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
from the
L_{0}
guarantee
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\varepsilon)
using the fact that
g
is a sparse junta. The argument uses a technical tool, the
reverse union bound
, which we would like to highlight here.

Since
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\varepsilon)
and
f
is Boolean, it follows that
\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]=O(\varepsilon)
, and so
\Pr[g(g-1)\neq 0]=O(\varepsilon)
. We will deduce that
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[g^{2}(g-1)^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
, which implies
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
via a short argument.

Since
g
is a sparse junta whose monomial coefficients are quantized, the same holds for
h=g(g-1)
, and also for
h^{2}
. We will show that
\Pr[h\neq 0]=O(\varepsilon)
implies that
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[h^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
.

Since the monomial coefficients of
h^{2}
are quantized, it suffices to show that the monomial support of
h^{2}
is sparse, in the sense that it contains
O(\varepsilon/p^{e})
sets of size
e
for all
e\leq 2d
. Since
h
is a sparse junta, in order to show that the monomial support of
h^{2}
is sparse, it suffices to show that the monomial support of
h
itself is sparse.

The union bound shows that

\Pr[h\neq 0]\leq\sum_{S\in\supp(h)}p^{|S|}.

If the inequality were in the other direction then it would follow that the monomial support of
h
is sparse, since
\Pr[h\neq 0]=O(\varepsilon)
.
In general, we cannot reverse the inequality (consider for example
h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}
, where the left-hand side tends to
1
while the right-hand side tends to infinity for constant
p
).
However, when
h
is a sparse junta, the
reverse union bound
states that we can indeed reverse the direction of the inequality:

\Pr[h\neq 0]=\Omega\left(\sum_{S\in\supp(h)}p^{|S|}\right).

The proof of the reverse union bound is not difficult. It uses the Harris–FKG inequality.

2
Preliminaries

We make constant use of the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
.

We sometimes use the notation
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
.

We use
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
for the characteristic vector of the set
S
or for the indicator of the event
S
. We also use the notation
\mathbbm{1}[S]
for the latter to improve legibility.

The
weight
of
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is the number of coordinates equal to
1
.

A function on
\{0,1\}^{n}
is an
M
-junta
if it depends on at most
M
coordinates.

Monotone functions

If
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
then
x\leq y
if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. We write
x<y
if
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
.

A function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
monotone
if
f(x)\leq f(y)
whenever
x\leq y
.

If
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is monotone then a
minterm
of
f
is an input
x
such that
f(x)=1
and
f(y)=0
for all
y<x
. The set of minterms of
f
constitutes its
minterm support
.

A monotone function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a
width
d
monotone DNF
if all its minterms have weight at most
d
.

Biased measure

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in\{0,1\}
, the measure
\mu_{p}
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
is given by

\mu_{p}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=p^{\sum_{i}y_{i}}(1-p)^{\sum_{i}(1-y_{i})}.

Equivalently, if we identify an element in
\{0,1\}^{n}
with the corresponding subset, then a subset
S\sim\mu_{p}
is sampled by including each element independently with probability
p
.

When the domain
\{0,1\}^{n}
is not clear from context, we write it explicitly, for example
\mu_{p}(\{0,1\}^{S})
or
\mu_{p}(S)
.

Given a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we often think of the domain as endowed with the measure
\mu_{p}
for some
p
. In this case, we write
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\mathbb{R}
. If we then write
\EE[f]
or
\Pr[f\in\{0,1\}]
, then the underlying measure is
\mu_{p}
.

Closeness

Our basic notion of closeness is
L_{2}
.
Two functions
f,g
are
\varepsilon
-close with respect to
\mu_{p}
if
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-g)^{2}]\leq\varepsilon
. If
\mu_{p}
is clear from context then we omit its mention.

Monomial expansion

Every function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
has a unique representation as a multilinear polynomial:

f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\tilde{f}(S)y_{S},\text{ where }y_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}y_{i},

where
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\{0,1\}
.
We call this representation the
monomial expansion
. We stress that it differs from the Fourier expansion.

The functions
y_{S}
are called
monomials
, and the coefficients
\tilde{f}(S)
are called
monomial coefficients
. The
monomial support
of
f
is

\supp(f)=\{S\subseteq[n]:\tilde{f}(S)\neq 0\}.

The
degree
of
f
, denoted
\deg f
, is the maximal size of a set in
\supp(f)
(if
\supp(f)=\emptyset
then
\deg f=0
). This notion of degree coincides with the Fourier-theoretic notion of degree.

Low degree functions

A function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
has
degree
d
if
\deg f\leq d
. A function is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
(with respect to
\mu_{p}
)
if
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-g)^{2}]\leq\varepsilon
for some degree
d
function
g
.

We can also define these concepts via the
p
-biased Fourier expansion. Let
f=\sum_{S}\hat{f}(S)\omega_{S}
, where
\{\omega_{S}\}
is the
p
-biased Fourier basis, described in
Section

4.1
. Let
f^{\leq d}=\sum_{|S|\leq d}\hat{f}(S)\omega_{S}
and
f^{>d}=\sum_{|S|>d}\hat{f}(S)\omega_{S}
. The function
f
has degree
d
if
f^{>d}=0
. The degree
d
function closest to
f
(with respect to
\mu_{p}
) is
f^{\leq d}
, and so
f
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
if
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f^{>d})^{2}]=\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-f^{\leq d})^{2}]\leq\varepsilon
.

Function restriction

We denote the restriction of
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to the coordinates in a subset
S\subseteq[n]
by
y|_{S}\in\{0,1\}^{S}
.

Given a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, the restriction
f|_{S}\colon\{0,1\}^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is obtained by substituting zero for all coordinates outside of
S
:

f|_{S}(y)=\sum_{T\subseteq S}\tilde{f}(T)y_{T}.

Function substitution

Given a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and a subset
S\subseteq[n]
, the substitution
f|_{y_{S}\leftarrow 1}\colon\{0,1\}^{\overline{S}}\to\mathbb{R}
is obtained by substituting
y_{i}=1
for all
i\in S
:

f|_{y_{S}\leftarrow 1}=\sum_{T}\tilde{f}(T)y_{T\setminus S}.

If
S=\{i\}
then we use the notation
f|_{y_{i}\leftarrow 1}
.

Boolean functions

A function is
Boolean
if it is
\{0,1\}
-valued. If
f,g
are Boolean then they are
\varepsilon
-close if and only if
\Pr[f\neq g]\leq\varepsilon
. In other words, for Boolean functions the
L_{2}
and
L_{0}
notions of distance coincide.

If
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
then
\round(f,\{0,1\})
is the Boolean function obtained by rounding each
f(y)
to the nearest value among
\{0,1\}
. The original function
f
is
\varepsilon
-close to Boolean
if

\EE_{y}[\dist(f(y),\{0,1\})^{2}]\leq\varepsilon,

where
\dist(f(y),\{0,1\})=\min(|f(y)-0|,|f(y)-1|)
.

Equivalently,
\EE[(f-\round(f,\{0,1\}))^{2}]\leq\varepsilon
.

2.1
Unbiased structure theorems

Granularity

The monomial coefficients of Boolean functions are bounded integers.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
f
is Boolean and
S\neq\emptyset
then
\tilde{f}(S)
is an integer whose magnitude is at most
2^{|S|-1}
.

Proof 2.2
.

Let
g
be the function obtained by substituting zero in all coordinates outside
S
. Then
\tilde{f}(S)=\tilde{g}(S)
and

g(y)=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{S}}f(z)\prod_{z_{i}=0}(1-y_{i})\prod_{z_{i}=1}y_{i}.

Considering the coefficient of
y_{S}
, we see that

\tilde{f}(S)=\tilde{g}(S)=\sum_{z\colon z|_{\overline{S}}=0}(-1)^{|S|-|z|}f(z).

The claim follows since
2^{|S|-1}
of the summands are
+f(z)
and
2^{|S|-1}
are
-f(z)
.

When
S=\emptyset
, clearly
\tilde{f}(\emptyset)=f(0)\in\{0,1\}
.

Nisan–Szegedy

Nisan and Szegedy
[
13
]
proved the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3

(Nisan–Szegedy)
.

Every Boolean degree
d
function on
\{0,1\}^{n}
is an
M_{d}
-junta, where
M_{d}=d2^{d-1}
.

The number of coordinates in the junta was improved to
O(2^{d})
in
[
1
,
15
]
.

Kindler–Safra

In unpublished work, Kindler and Safra
[
12
,
11
]
proved the following result, which we state in its formulation due to Keller and Klein
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Kindler–Safra)
.

There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that the following holds for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
, where
\varepsilon<c^{d}
, then
f
is
2\varepsilon
-close to a Boolean degree
d
function.

This formulation follows by combining Theorem 1.4 and Proposition 5.6 in
[
9
]
. (Keller and Klein also prove a stronger version in which the closeness is improved to the optimal expression,
\varepsilon+\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
.)

For our purposes, we need a version of the Kindler–Safra theorem which holds for all
\varepsilon
.

Theorem 2.5
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
then
f
is
C^{d}\varepsilon
-close to a Boolean degree
d
function.

Proof 2.6
.

We take
C=\max(1/c,2)
, where
c
is the constant in
Theorem

2.4
.

Suppose that
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
. If
\varepsilon<c^{d}
then the result follows directly from
Theorem

2.4
. Otherwise,
C^{d}\varepsilon\geq 1
, and so
f
is
C^{d}\varepsilon
-close to the constant zero function.

Monotone Kindler–Safra

When the function
f
is monotone, we can guarantee that the approximating function in
Theorem

2.5
is monotone as well.

We start with a warm-up.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\mathbb{R}
is a monotone function and
g\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\mathbb{R}
is an
M
-junta.

Either
g
is monotone or
\Pr[f\neq g]\geq 2^{-M}
.

Proof 2.8
.

Suppose without loss of generality that
g
depends on the first
M
coordinates. We separate accordingly the inputs to
f
and
g
into two parts: a point in
\{0,1\}^{M}
, and a point in
\{0,1\}^{n-M}
.

If
g
is not monotone then
g(x,0)>g(y,0)
for some
x<y
. For each
z\in\{0,1\}^{n-M}
we have
g(x,z)>g(y,z)
while
f(x,z)\leq f(y,z)
, and so either
f(x,z)\neq g(x,z)
or
f(y,z)\neq g(y,z)
. Since there are
2^{n-M}
choices for
z
,

\Pr[f\neq g]\geq\frac{2^{n-M}}{2^{n}}=2^{-M}.

Using a similar argument, we can prove a monotone version of the Kindler–Safra theorem. We are indebted to an anonymous reviewer for suggesting the following proof, which improves the bound on
K_{d}
from doubly exponential to singly exponential.

Theorem 2.9
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
there is a constant
K_{d}>0
such that the following holds.

If
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\{0,1\}
is monotone and
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
then
f
is
K_{d}\varepsilon
-close to a monotone Boolean degree
d
function.

Proof 2.10
.

Suppose that
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})\to\{0,1\}
is monotone and
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
. Applying
Theorem

2.5
,
f
is
C^{d}\varepsilon
-close to a Boolean degree
d
function
g
.

If
g
is monotone then we are done. Otherwise, there is an index
i
and two inputs
x<y
differing only in the
i
’th coordinate such that
g(x)>g(y)
. We will show that there are in fact many such pairs. To this end, define

g_{i}(y)=g(y|_{i\leftarrow 1})-g(y|_{i\leftarrow 0})\text{ and }G_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}g_{i}(g_{i}-1).

Here
y|_{i\leftarrow b}
is obtained from
y
by setting
y_{i}=b
.

The function
g_{i}
, which is just the derivative of
g
with respect to
y_{i}
, attains the values
\{-1,0,1\}
, where
g_{i}(y)=-1
if
(y_{i\leftarrow 0},y_{i\leftarrow 1})
is a pair of inputs violating monotonicity. The function
G_{i}
is the indicator function of this event.

By construction,
G_{i}
has degree
2d-2
, and so
\EE[G_{i}]
is an integer multiple of
2^{-2d-2}
. (One way to see this is to observe that the monomial coefficients of
G_{i}
are integers, and that the expectation of a degree
e
monomial is
2^{-e}
.) By construction,
\EE[G_{i}]>0
, and so

\Pr[g(y_{i\leftarrow 0})=1\text{ and }g(y_{i\leftarrow 1})=0]\geq\frac{1}{2^{2d-2}}.

Since
f
is monotone, this implies that

\Pr[f(y)\neq g(y)]\geq\frac{1}{2}\Pr[f(y_{i\leftarrow 0})\neq g(y_{i\leftarrow 0})\text{ or }f(y_{i\leftarrow 1})\neq g(y_{i\leftarrow 1})]\geq\frac{1}{2^{2d-1}}.

On the other hand, this probability is at most
C^{d}\varepsilon
, and so
\varepsilon\geq C^{-d}/2^{2d-1}
. It follows that
f
is
2^{2d-1}C^{d}\varepsilon
-close to the zero function.

Curiously, the proof of the biased version of
Theorem

2.9
only uses
Lemma

2.7
.

2.2
Forbidden configurations

Our work involves three different types of
forbidden configurations
. These are configurations of coefficients which cannot occur in Boolean functions, but do arise in our context — but only sparingly. For example, the biased FKN theorem
[
5
]
involves functions of the form
f=\sum_{i}c_{i}y_{i}
. If
f
is Boolean then we cannot have
c_{i}=c_{j}=1
, but if
f
is
\varepsilon
-close to Boolean then there could be up to
O(\varepsilon/p^{2})
copies of this configuration.

Minimal non-Boolean inputs

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. An input
x
is a
minimal non-Boolean input
of
f
if
f(x)\notin\{0,1\}
but
f(y)\in\{0,1\}
for all
y<x
.

Suppose that
f
has degree
1
, say

f=c_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}y_{i}.

If
c_{0}\notin\{0,1\}
then
0
is the only minimal non-Boolean input. Otherwise, suppose without loss of generality that
c_{0}=0
. If
c_{i}\notin\{0,1\}
then
\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\}}
is a minimal non-Boolean input. If
c_{i}=c_{j}=1
then
\mathbbm{1}_{\{i,j\}}
is a minimal non-Boolean input. There are no other types of minimal non-Boolean inputs.

In addition, if
x
is a minimal non-Boolean input then this is due to some non-zero monomial coefficients: if
\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\}}
is a minimal non-Boolean input then
c_{i}\neq 0
, and if
\mathbbm{1}_{\{i,j\}}
is a minimal non-Boolean input of
f
then
c_{i},c_{j}\neq 0
. In both cases, the minimal non-Boolean input
x=\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is such that
S
is the union of sets in
\supp(f)
:
\{i\}
in the former case, and
\{i\},\{j\}
in the latter case.

The following lemma shows that a similar picture holds for all
d
. We thank an anonymous reviewer for suggesting the following proof, which improves the bound on
L_{d}
from exponential to linear.

Lemma 2.11
.

For every
d
there is a constant
L_{d}
such that every minimal non-Boolean input of a degree
d
function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
has weight at most
L_{d}
.

Furthermore, if
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal non-Boolean input of
f
then
S
can be written as the union of at most
L_{d}
sets in
\supp(f)
.

Proof 2.12
.

Let
g=f(f-1)
, and observe that
x
is a minimal non-Boolean input of
f
if and only if it is a minimal non-zero input of
g
, that is,
g(x)\neq 0
but
g(y)=0
for all
y<x
. If
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal non-zero input of
g
then
\tilde{g}(S)\neq 0
, and so
|S|\leq\deg g\leq 2d
. Moreover,
S
can be written as the union of at most two sets in
\supp(f)
.

Looking ahead, we will be interested in minimal non-Boolean inputs of degree
d
functions whose weight is larger than
d
.
If
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal non-Boolean input of
f
then
f|_{S}
is a function which is Boolean except for the input
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
. Listing such
minimal non-Boolean functions
is a convenient way to describe all minimal non-Boolean inputs. For example, when
d=1
, the minimal non-Boolean functions are

y_{1}+y_{2},1-y_{1}-y_{2}.

In general, the minimal non-Boolean inputs come in pairs
f,1-f
, and it suffices to list those which satisfy
f(0)=0
.

Here are the minimal non-Boolean functions satisfying
f(0)=0
for
d=2
:

\displaystyle y_{1}(y_{2}+y_{3})

\displaystyle y_{1}y_{2}+y_{1}y_{3}+y_{2}y_{3}

\displaystyle y_{1}(1-y_{2}-y_{3})

\displaystyle y_{1}+y_{2}y_{3}

\displaystyle(y_{1}+y_{2})(1-y_{3})-2y_{1}y_{2}

\displaystyle y_{1}(1-y_{3})+y_{2}-2y_{1}y_{2}

\displaystyle(y_{1}+y_{2})(1-y_{3})-y_{1}y_{2}

\displaystyle y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}-2y_{1}y_{2}-2y_{1}y_{3}-2y_{2}y_{3}

\displaystyle y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}-2y_{1}y_{2}-2y_{1}y_{3}-y_{2}y_{3}

\displaystyle y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}-2y_{1}y_{2}-y_{1}y_{3}-y_{2}y_{3}

\displaystyle y_{1}y_{2}+y_{3}y_{4}

\displaystyle y_{1}(1-y_{3})+y_{2}(1-y_{4})-y_{1}y_{2}

\displaystyle y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}-y_{1}y_{2}-y_{1}y_{3}-y_{2}y_{3}-y_{1}y_{4}

\displaystyle y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}+y_{4}-y_{1}y_{2}-y_{1}y_{3}-y_{1}y_{4}-y_{2}y_{3}-y_{2}y_{4}-y_{3}y_{4}

Minimal non-monotone inputs

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
. An input
x
is
minimal non-monotone
if
f(x)<f(y)
for some
y<x
, and
f(y)\leq f(z)
whenever
y\leq z<x
.

Theorem

2.3
implies that each such input has weight at most
M_{d}
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Every minimal non-monotone input of a degree
d
function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
has weight at most
M_{d}
, where
M_{d}
is the constant from
Theorem

2.3
.

Furthermore, if
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal non-monotone input of
f
then
S
can be written as the union of at most
M_{d}
sets in
\supp(f)
.

Proof 2.14
.

By minimality, if
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal non-monotone input of
f
then
f|_{S}
depends on all inputs, and so
|S|\leq M_{d}
by
Theorem

2.3
. Moreover,
S
is the union of all sets in
\supp(f|_{S})
, and so it is the union of at most
|S|
sets in
\supp(f)
.

We extend the definition on minimal non-monotone inputs to non-Boolean functions as follows.
If
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
then an input
x=\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is
minimal non-monotone Boolean
if
f|_{S}
is Boolean and
x
is a minimal non-monotone input of
f|_{S}
.

Lemma

2.13
holds also for minimal non-monotone Boolean inputs of a degree
d
function
g\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, since if
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal non-monotone Boolean input of
g
then it is also a minimal non-monotone input of the Boolean function
g|_{S}
.

As in the case of minimal non-Boolean inputs, we will be interested in minimal non-monotone inputs of degree
d
functions whose weight is larger than
d
, which we can describe using
minimal non-monotone functions
. When
d=1
, there are no minimal non-monotone functions. When
d=2
, the unique minimal non-monotone function is

y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}-y_{1}y_{2}-y_{1}y_{3}-y_{2}y_{3}.

Minimal high-degree inputs

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, and let
d
be a parameter. An input
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is
minimal high-degree
if
\deg(f|_{S})>d
but
\deg(f|_{T})\leq d
for all
T\subsetneq S
.

Lemma 2.15
.

Every minimal high-degree input of a monotone width
d
DNF
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
has weight at most
L_{d}
, where
L_{d}
is the constant from
Lemma

2.11
.

Furthermore, if
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal high-degree input of
f
then
S
can be written as the union of at most
L_{d}
minterms of
f
.

Proof 2.16
.

By minimality, if
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
is a minimal high-degree input of
f
then
f|_{S}
depends on all coordinates, and so
S
is the union of at most
|S|
minterms in
\supp(f|_{S})
, say
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
.

If
|S|\leq M_{d}
then we are done. Otherwise, let
m
be the minimal index such that
|B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{m}|>M_{d}
. By minimality,
\deg(f|_{B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{m-1}})\leq d
, and so
|B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{m-1}|\leq M_{d}
by
Theorem

2.3
. Since
f|_{B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{m}}
depends on all inputs and
|B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{m}|>M_{d}
,
Theorem

2.3
shows that it cannot have degree
d
, and so
B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{m}=S
. Therefore
|S|\leq M_{d}+d
.

As in the case of minimal non-Boolean inputs and minimal non-monotone inputs, we can describe minimal high-degree inputs as minimal high-degree monotone DNFs. When
d=1
, the unique minimal high-degree monotone DNF is
y_{1}\lor y_{2}
, and when
d=2
, the minimal high-degree monotone DNFs are

\displaystyle y_{1}\land(y_{2}\lor y_{3})

\displaystyle(y_{1}\land y_{2})\lor(y_{1}\land y_{3})\lor(y_{2}\land y_{3})

\displaystyle y_{1}\lor(y_{2}\land y_{3})

\displaystyle y_{1}\lor y_{2}\lor y_{3}

\displaystyle(y_{1}\land y_{2})\lor(y_{3}\land y_{4})

3
Sparsity

The approximating function
g
in
Theorem

1.1
has sparse monomial support, and its monotone counterpart in
Theorem

1.2
has sparse minterm support. This crucial property drives much of the proofs of these theorems.

Here is the notion of sparsity that comes up in both theorems.

Definition 3.1

(Sparse)
.

A set system over a set
V
is a collection of subsets of
V
.

A set system
\mathcal{F}
is
(d,C,\varepsilon)
-sparse
, where
d\geq 0
is an integer,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and
C\geq 1
, if the following three properties hold:

1.

All sets in
\mathcal{F}
have size at most
d
.

2.

For all
T\subseteq[n]
and integer
e\geq 0
, the set system
\mathcal{F}
contains at most
C^{e}
sets
U\supseteq T
of size
|U|+e
.

3.

For all integer
e\geq 0
, the set system
\mathcal{F}
contains at most
C^{e}\varepsilon
sets
U
of size
e
.

A set system is
(d,C)
-sparse if it is
(d,C,1)
-sparse; this corresponds to omitting the final property.

Definition 3.2

(Sparse junta)
.

A function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
(d,C)
-sparse junta
if
\supp(f)
is
(d,C)
-sparse.

A monotone function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a
(d,C)
-sparse DNF
if its minterm support is
(d,C)
-sparse.

We describe some elementary properties of sparse set systems in
Section

3.1
. Sparse set systems satisfy a crucial property we call the
reverse union bound
, which we explain in
Section

3.2
. Finally, we discuss
quantized
sparse juntas, which are sparse juntas in which the monomial coefficients belong to a fixed finite set. We show in
Section

3.3
that if a quantized sparse junta
f
is
L_{0}
-close to Boolean (that is,
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\leq\varepsilon
) then it is also
L_{2}
-close to Boolean (that is,
\EE[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
).

3.1
Elementary properties

We discuss the effect of three natural operations on the sparsity of set systems: union, sum and substitution.

The union of two set systems is their set-theoretic union, and it corresponds to the sum of functions:

\supp(f+g)\subseteq\supp(f)\cup\supp(g),

where the inclusion is tight generically.

Definition 3.3

(Sum of set systems)
.

If
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
are set systems then their
sum
is

\mathcal{F}\sqcup\mathcal{G}=\{A\cup B:A\in\mathcal{F},B\in\mathcal{G}\}.

The
k^{\prime}
th iterated sum
of a set system
\mathcal{F}
is the sum of
k
copies of
\mathcal{F}
:

\mathcal{F}^{\sqcup k}=\{A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{k}:A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Since
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
are not necessarily distinct,
\mathcal{F}^{\sqcup k}
includes
\mathcal{F}^{\sqcup\ell}
for all non-zero
\ell<k
.

The sum of two set systems corresponds to the product of functions:

\supp(fg)\subseteq\supp(f)\sqcup\supp(g),

where the inclusion is tight generically.

Definition 3.4

(Contraction)
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a set system over
V
and
S\subseteq V
then

\mathcal{F}|_{S\leftarrow 1}=\{T\setminus S:T\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Contraction corresponds to substituting the value of variables:

\supp(f|_{y_{S}\leftarrow 1})\subseteq\supp(f)|_{S\leftarrow 1},

and the inclusion is tight generically.

Union, sum and contraction all preserve sparsity, after adapting the parameters.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be
(d,C)
-sparse set systems.

1.

\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}
is
(d,2C)
-sparse.

2.

\mathcal{F}\sqcup\mathcal{G}
is
(2d,O_{d}(C))
-sparse.

3.

\mathcal{F}^{\sqcup k}
are
(kd,O_{k,d}(C))
-sparse.

4.

\mathcal{F}|_{J\leftarrow 1}
is
(d,O_{d,|J|}(C))
-sparse.

Proof 3.6
.

Every set system satisfies the definition of
(d,C)
-sparse for
e=0
, and so it suffices to prove it for
e\geq 1
.

Item

1
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be
(d,C)
-sparse set systems. Given
R
and
e\geq 1
, there are at most
C^{e}
sets in each of
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
of size
|R|+e
which contain
R
. Therefore there are at most
2C^{e}\leq(2C)^{e}
sets in
\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}
of size
|R|+e
which contain
R
.

Item

2
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be
(d,C)
-sparse set systems. Given
R
and
e
, we need to bound the number of sets
S=A\cup B
such that
A\in\mathcal{F}
,
B\in\mathcal{G}
,
A\cup B\supseteq R
, and
|A\cup B|=|R|+e
. We can assume that
|R|\leq 2d
.

We will show that for every
a,a_{r},a_{b}\leq|R|
there are at most
16^{d}C^{e}
pairs
(A,B)
such that:

1.

A\in\mathcal{F}
,
B\in\mathcal{G}
,
A\cup B\supseteq R
,
|A\cup B|=|R|+e
.

2.

|A|=a
,
|A\cap R|=a_{r}
, and
|A\cap B|=a_{b}
.

Since there are at most
(2d+1)^{3}
many options for
a,a_{r},a_{b}
, this will complete the proof.

There are
\binom{|R|}{a_{r}}\leq 4^{d}
choices for
A\cap R
such that
|A\cap R|=a_{r}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is
(d,C)
-sparse, there are at most
C^{a-a_{r}}
sets
A\in\mathcal{F}
extending
A\cap R
of size
a
. There are
\binom{|A|}{a_{b}}\leq 2^{a}\leq 4^{d}
choices for
A\cap B
.

Notice that
B
needs to contain both
A\cap B
and
R\setminus A
, two sets which are disjoint. Also, since
|A\cup B|=|R|+e
, we have
|B|=|A\cup B|+|A\cap B|-|A|=|R|+e+a_{b}-a
.
Since
\mathcal{G}
is
(d,C)
-sparse, the number of sets
B\in\mathcal{G}
of size
b
extending
(A\cap B)\cup(R\setminus A)
is at most
C^{(|R|+e+a_{b}-a)-a_{b}-(|R|-a_{r})}=C^{e-a+a_{r}}
.

Altogether, given
a,a_{r},a_{b}
, the number of pairs
A,B
satisfying the properties listed above is bounded by

\underbrace{4^{d}}_{A\cap R}\cdot\underbrace{C^{a-a_{r}}}_{A\text{ given }A\cap R}\cdot\underbrace{4^{d}}_{A\cap B\text{ given }A}\cdot\underbrace{C^{e-a+a_{r}}}_{B\text{ given }A\cap R,A\cap B}=16^{d}C^{e},

as required.

Item

3
.

This item follows from
Item

2
by induction on
k
.

Item

4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
(d,C)
-sparse set system, and let
J
be a set. Given
R
and
e
, we need to bound the number of sets
T\in\mathcal{F}
such that
T\setminus J\supseteq R
and
|T\setminus J|=|R|+e
. We can assume that
R
is disjoint from
J
.

We will bound the number of such sets
T
given
I=T\cap J
. Any such set
T
contains both
I
and
R
, and
|T|=|T\setminus J|+|T\cap J|=|R|+e+|I|
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is
(d,C)
-sparse, there are at most
C^{(|R|+e+|I|)-(|I|+|R|)}=C^{e}
sets
T\in\mathcal{F}
containing
I\cup R
and of size
|R|+e+|I|
. We conclude that the number of sets
T\in\mathcal{F}
such that
T\setminus J\supseteq R
and

is at most

\sum_{I\subseteq J}C^{e}\leq 2^{|J|}C^{e}\leq(2^{|J|}C)^{e}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be
(d,C,\varepsilon)
-sparse.

1.

\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}
is
(d,2C,\varepsilon)
-sparse.

2.

\mathcal{F}\sqcup\mathcal{G}
is

3.

\mathcal{F}^{\sqcup k}
are

Proof 3.8
.

Item

1
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be
(d,C,\varepsilon)
-sparse set systems.

shows that
\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}
is
(d,2C)
-sparse. Given

, there are at most

sets of size

in each of
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
. Therefore there are at most

in
\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}
.

The case

requires special treatment. If

do not contain

, and so
\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}
also doesn’t contain
\emptyset
. If

then clearly
\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}
contains at most
\varepsilon

.

Item

2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a

-sparse set system and let
\mathcal{G}
be a

-sparse set system.

shows that

is

Given

and

. For this, we show that for each

Item

3
.

This item follows from

.

3.2
Reverse union bound

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a set system. The union bound shows that

\Pr_{Y\sim\mu_{p}}[Y\supseteq S\text{ for some }S\in\mathcal{F}]\leq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}.

In general, the union bound is not tight. For example, suppose that
\mathcal{F}=\{\{1\},\ldots,\{n\}\}
. As
n\to\infty
, the left-hand side tends to
1
while the right-hand side tends to infinity. More generally, if
\mathcal{F}=\{[m]\cup\{m+1\},\ldots,[m]\cup\{n\}\}
then the left-hand side tends to
p^{m}
while the right-hand side tends to infinity.

In this section, we show that the union bound is tight up to a constant factor provided that
\mathcal{F}
is sparse.

Lemma 3.9

(Reverse union bound)
.

Let
p\leq 1/2
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a
(d,C/p)
-sparse set system, and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
.

For
S\in\mathcal{F}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{S}
denote the event that
y_{S}=1
and
y_{R}=0
for all
R\in\mathcal{G}
such that
R\not\subseteq S
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the union of the events
\mathcal{E}_{S}
for all
S\in\mathcal{F}
. Then

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\Theta_{d,C}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}\right).

Proof 3.10
.

If
\mathcal{E}
holds then
y_{S}=1
for some
S\in\mathcal{F}
, and so

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]\leq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}.

In the other direction, observe first that the events
\mathcal{E}_{S}
are disjoint. Indeed, suppose that
S,T\in\mathcal{F}
are distinct. If
S\subseteq T
and
T\subseteq S
then
S=T
, so assume without loss of generality that
S\not\subseteq T
. If
\mathcal{E}_{S}
holds then
y_{S}=1
and so
\mathcal{E}_{T}
cannot hold. This implies that

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{S}].

We now bound each of the summands:

\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{S}]=p^{|S|}\Pr[y_{T}=0\text{ for all }\emptyset\neq T\in\mathcal{G}|_{S\leftarrow 1}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\geq}}p^{|S|}\prod_{\emptyset\neq T\in\mathcal{G}|_{S\leftarrow 1}}\Pr[y_{T}=0],

where
(*)
follows from the Harris–FKG inequality, since the events
y_{T}=0
are anti-monotone.

Lemma

3.5
shows that
\mathcal{G}|_{S\leftarrow 1}
is
(d,K/p)
-sparse, where
K
depends only on
d,C
. Therefore

\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{S}]\geq p^{|S|}\prod_{e=1}^{d}(1-p^{e})^{K^{e}/p^{e}}\geq p^{|S|}\prod_{e=1}^{d}(1-2^{-e})^{(2K)^{e}}=\Omega_{d,C}(p^{|S|}),

since
(1-x)^{1/x}
is decreasing in
[0,1]
.

We conclude that

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{S}]=\Omega_{d,C}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}\right).

When
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G}
, we recover the statement that the union bound is tight up to a constant factor for sparse set systems. Indeed, if
\mathcal{E}
happens then in particular
Y\supseteq S
for some
S\in\mathcal{F}
, and so the
Lemma
shows that

\Pr_{Y\sim\mu_{p}}[Y\supseteq S\text{ for some }S\in\mathcal{F}]\geq\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\Omega_{d,C}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}\right).

3.3
Quantized sparse juntas

The sparse juntas that we consider later on will arise from applying the junta agreement theorem,
Theorem

1.6
. Such functions are
quantized
in the following sense.

Definition 3.11

(Quantized functions)
.

A function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
B
-quantized
, where
B
is a finite set, if all monomial coefficients of

belong to
B
.

Quantization is preserved under basic operations.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f,g\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be
B
-quantized functions of degree
d
, where
B
is a finite set.

1.

For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

is
cB
-quantized, where
cB=\{cb:b\in B\}
.

2.

The function
f+g
is
(B+B)
-quantized, where
B+B=\{b_{1}+b_{2}:b_{1},b_{2}\in B\}
.

3.

The function
fg
is
C
-quantized, where
C
is a finite set depending only on

.

Proof 3.13
.

Items

1
and
2
are clear. For
Item

3
, we use the formula

\widetilde{fg}(U)=\sum_{S\cup T=U}\tilde{f}(S)\tilde{g}(T),

which shows that
\widetilde{fg}(U)
is the sum of
3^{|U|}\leq 3^{2d}
products of two elements from
B
.

The junta agreement theorem will result in a quantized sparse junta

which is close to Boolean in an
L_{0}
sense:
\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]=O(\varepsilon)
. In this section, we show how to conclude closeness in an

sense:
\Pr[\dist(g,\{0,1\})^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
.

We start with a similar result which compares the
L_{0}
and
L_{2}
norms.

Lemma 3.14
.

If
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\mathbb{R}
is a

-quantized
(d,C/p)
-sparse junta, where

\Pr[f\neq 0]=\Theta_{d,C,B}(\EE[f^{2}]).

Proof 3.15
.

If
f\neq 0
then clearly

for some

, and so the union bound shows that

\Pr[f\neq 0]\leq\sum_{S\in\supp(f)}p^{|S|}.

We can get a matching lower bound using

. Let
\mathcal{F}
be all inclusion-minimal sets in

\mathcal{G}=\supp(f)

\mathcal{E}_{S}
in the

then

\Pr[f\neq 0]\geq\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\Omega_{d,C}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}\right).

Since

is a
(d,C/p)
-sparse junta,

\sum_{S\in\supp(f)}p^{|S|}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}\sum_{e=0}^{d-|S|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in\supp(f)\\
T\supseteq S,|T|=|S|+e\end{subarray}}p^{e}\leq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}\sum_{e=0}^{d-|S|}(C/p)^{e}p^{e}=O_{d,C}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}\right),

and we conclude that

\Pr[f\neq 0]\geq\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\Omega_{d,C}\left(\sum_{S\in\supp(f)}p^{|S|}\right).

(2)

Similarly,

\EE[f^{2}]\geq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in B\\
b\neq 0\end{subarray}}b^{2}\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\Omega_{d,C,B}\left(\sum_{S\in\supp(f)}p^{|S|}\right)=\Omega_{d,C,B}(\Pr[f\neq 0]),

showing that
\Pr[f\neq 0]=O_{d,C,B}(\EE[f^{2}])
.

For the other direction,
Equation

2
implies that
\supp(f)
is
(d,C/p,\varepsilon)
-sparse, where

.

Lemma

3.7
shows that
\supp(f^{2})\subseteq\supp(f)^{\sqcup 2}

(2d,K/p,\varepsilon)
-sparse, where

, and

\EE[f^{2}]\leq\max_{b\in B^{\prime}}b\sum_{e=0}^{2d}(K/p)^{e}\varepsilon\cdot p^{e}=O_{d,C,B}(\Pr[f\neq 0]).

Using this, we derive the main result of this section.

Lemma 3.16
.

If
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\mathbb{R}
is a

(d,C/p)
-sparse junta, where

\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]=\Theta_{d,C,B}(\EE[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]).

Proof 3.17
.

Let
g=f(f-1)
. Since
\supp(g)\subseteq\supp(f)^{\sqcup 2}
,

and
3.12
show that

-quantized
(2d,K/p)
-sparse junta, where

\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]=\Pr[g\neq 0]=\Theta_{d,C,B}(\EE[g^{2}]).

We show that
\EE[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=O_{d,C,B}(\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}])
by relating
\EE[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]
to
\EE[g^{2}]
.
Consider any input
y
. If
\round(f(y),\{0,1\})=a
then
|f(y)-(1-a)|\geq 1/2
and so
(f(y)-(1-a))^{2}\geq 1/4
. Consequently,

\dist(f(y),\{0,1\})^{2}=(f(y)-a)^{2}\leq 4(f(y)-a)^{2}(f(y)-(1-a))^{2}=4g(y)^{2}.

It follows that

\EE[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]\leq 4\EE[g^{2}]=O_{d,C,B}(\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]).

The proof of the other direction,
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]=O_{d,C,B}(\EE[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}])
, uses the notion of minimal non-Boolean input, defined in
Section

2.2
.
If
f(y)\notin\{0,1\}
then
y\geq\mathbbm{1}_{S}
for some minimal non-Boolean input
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
of
f
, implying that
y_{S}=1
. Denoting the set of minimal non-Boolean inputs of
f
by
\mathcal{F}
, this shows that

\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\leq\sum_{\mathbbm{1}_{S}\in\mathcal{F}}\Pr[y_{S}=1]=\sum_{\mathbbm{1}_{S}\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}.

We would like to bound the right-hand side using
Lemma

3.9
, but it is not necessarily the case that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\supp(f)
. However,
Lemma

2.11
shows that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\supp(f)^{\sqcup L_{d}}
.
Lemma

3.5
shows that
\supp(f)^{\sqcup L_{d}}
is
(L_{d}d,K^{\prime}/p)
-sparse, where
K^{\prime}
depends on
d,C
. Therefore,
Lemma

3.9
, applied with
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}=\supp(f)^{\sqcup L_{d}}
, shows that

\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\leq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}p^{|S|}=\Theta_{d,C}(\Pr[\mathcal{E}]),

where
\mathcal{E}
is the event that
y_{S}=1
for some
S\in\mathcal{F}
, and
y_{R}=0
for all
R\in\supp(f)^{\sqcup L_{d}}
such that
R\not\subseteq S
. If the event
\mathcal{E}
happens then
f(y)=f(\mathbbm{1}_{S})\notin\{0,1\}
. Moreover, since
|S|\leq L_{d}
by
Lemma

2.11
and
f
is
B
-quantized,
\dist(f(y),\{0,1\})^{2}=\Omega_{d,B}(1)
. Therefore,

\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]=O_{d,C,B}(\EE[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]).

4
Junta agreement theorem

We prove our main structure theorems,
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
, by reduction to the Kindler–Safra theorem on
(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{1/2})
. As we explain in
Section

1.2
, this involves applying the Kindler–Safra theorem to
f|_{S}
for
S\sim\mu_{2p}
, obtaining approximating juntas
g_{S}
, and pasting the juntas together to a global function
g
.

In order to show that the juntas
g_{S}
can be pasted together, we need to assume that they agree with each other, in the sense that

\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\nu}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]\leq\varepsilon

for an appropriate distribution
\nu
supported on triplets
(S_{1},S_{2},T)
such that
S_{1},S_{2}\supseteq T
.

Since we are interested in the behavior of
g_{S}
for
S\sim\mu_{2p}
, we need the marginals of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
to be
\mu_{2p}
. Moreover, we want
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
to be independent given
T
. This naturally leads to the product distribution
\mu_{q,r}
(where
q=2p
and
r\leq q
), which is defined as follows:

1.

T
includes each
i\in[n]
with probability
r
.

2.

For
\ell\in\{1,2\}
,
S_{\ell}
includes each
i\in T
with probability
1
, and each
i\notin T
with probability
\frac{q-r}{1-r}
.

It is easy to check that the marginal distributions of
S_{1},S_{2},T
are
\mu_{q},\mu_{q},\mu_{r}
, respectively.

We can now state the junta agreement theorem, which is a formal statement of
Theorem

1.6
.

Theorem 4.1

(Junta agreement theorem)
.

Fix an integer
K
and
c\in(0,1)
. The following holds for all
q\in(0,1)
.

Suppose that for each
S\subseteq[n]
we are given a degree
d
function
g_{S}\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
whose monomial support contains at most
K
monomials (the
junta assumption
). Assume that the functions
g_{S}
satisfy the following
agreement condition
:

\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]\leq\varepsilon.

Define a degree
d
function
g\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows: for each
|A|\leq d
, let
\tilde{g}(A)
be any value
a
maximizing

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\mu_{q}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S}(A)=a].

The function
g
is a
(d,O_{d,K,c}(1/q))
-sparse junta satisfying

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]=O_{d,K,c}(\varepsilon).

In the statement of the
Theorem
and below, we use the convention that in
\Pr
and
\EE
, we take the distribution on the first line of the subscript conditioned on the constraints in the following lines. For example, in the second display,
S
is chosen by including all elements in
A
with probability
1
and all elements outside of
A
with probability
q
.

Our previous work
[
2
]
proves a stronger version of
Theorem

4.1
, which does not require the
g_{S}
to be juntas but has the same conclusion (without the guarantee that
g
is a sparse junta, which follows from the
g_{S}
being juntas). The proof of this stronger version is somewhat non-intuitive, using the notion of good and excellent sets which is borrowed from
[
8
]
. It turns out that the proof simplifies dramatically when the
g_{S}
are juntas, and this is the proof that we present here.

The proof of
Theorem

4.1
relies on the following expansion lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume the setup of
Theorem

4.1
. There exists a constant
R_{c}
, depending only on
c
, such that for every
|A|\leq d
,

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\sim\mu_{q}\\
S_{1},S_{2}\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S_{1}}(A)\neq\tilde{g}_{S_{2}}(A)]\leq R_{c}\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}\\
T\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S_{1}}(A)\neq\tilde{g}_{S_{2}}(A)].

We first derive
Theorem

4.1
assuming
Lemma

4.2
, and then prove
Lemma

4.2
using elementary Fourier analysis.

Proof 4.3

(Proof of Theorem
4.1
)
.

Agreement

Our first goal is to show that
\Pr[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]=O_{K,c}(\varepsilon)
. We start by applying the union bound to relate the probability to disagreement on a particular monomial coefficient:

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]\leq\sum_{|A|\leq d}\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[S\supseteq A\text{ and }\tilde{g}(A)\neq\tilde{g}_{S}(A)]=\sum_{|A|\leq d}q^{|A|}\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\mu_{q}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}(A)\neq\tilde{g}_{S}(A)].

We chose
\tilde{g}(A)
as a value
a
minimizing
\Pr[\tilde{g}_{S}(A)\neq a\mid S\supseteq A]
, and so

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]\leq\sum_{|A|\leq d}q^{|A|}\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\sim\mu_{q}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S_{1}}(A)\neq\tilde{g}_{S_{2}}(A)].

We can relate this to the agreement condition using
Lemma

4.2
:

\displaystyle\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]

\displaystyle\leq R_{c}\sum_{|A|\leq d}q^{|A|}\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}\\
T\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S_{1}}(A)\neq\tilde{g}_{S_{2}}(A)]

\displaystyle=R_{c}\sum_{|A|\leq d}c^{-|A|}\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[T\supseteq A\text{ and }\tilde{g}_{S_{1}}(A)\neq\tilde{g}_{S_{2}}(A)].

In order to use the junta assumption, we move the sum over
A
inside:

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]\leq R_{c}c^{-d}\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[\operatorname{disagreements}(g_{S_{1}}|_{T},g_{S_{2}}|_{T})],

where
\operatorname{disagreements}
counts the number of different monomial coefficients. The junta assumption implies that there are at most
2K
disagreements, and so

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[g|_{S}\neq g_{S}]\leq R_{c}c^{-d}2K\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]=O_{d,K,c}(\varepsilon).

Sparse junta

It remains to show that
g
is a
(d,O_{K,c}(1/q))
-sparse junta. The main observation is that if
\tilde{g}(A)\neq 0
then

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\mu_{q}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S}(A)=0]\leq\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\mu_{q}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S}(A)=\tilde{g}(A)]\leq\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\mu_{q}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S}(A)\neq 0],

implying that

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\sim\mu_{q}\\
S\supseteq A\end{subarray}}[\tilde{g}_{S}(A)\neq 0]\geq\frac{1}{2},

and so

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[S\supseteq A\text{ and }\tilde{g}_{S}(A)\neq 0]\geq\frac{1}{2}q^{|A|}.

Given
|R|\leq d
, we deduce that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\supp(g)\\
A\supseteq R\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}q^{|A|}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\supp(g)\\
A\supseteq R\end{subarray}}\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[S\supseteq A\text{ and }\tilde{g}_{S}(A)\neq 0]\leq\EE_{S\sim\mu_{q}}[|\supp(g_{S})|\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{S\supseteq R}]\leq q^{|R|}K.

We conclude that the number of sets
A\in\supp(g)
containing
R
of size
|R|+e
is at most
2Kq^{-e}\leq(2K/q)^{e}
, as needed.

4.1
Proof of
Lemma

4.2

It remains to prove
Lemma

4.2
. We first observe that we can sample
S_{1},S_{2}
on the left by sampling
S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2}\sim\mu_{q}(\{0,1\}^{\overline{A}})
and then taking
S_{b}=S^{\prime}_{b}\cup A
. We can similarly sample
S_{1},S_{2},T
on the right via
(S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2},T^{\prime})\sim\mu_{q,cq}(\{0,1\}^{\overline{A}})
. Henceforth we will omit the domain
\{0,1\}^{\overline{A}}
in such expressions to avoid clutter.

For every
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
we define the indicator function
h_{\sigma}\colon\{0,1\}^{\overline{A}}\to\{0,1\}
by

h_{\sigma}(S^{\prime})=\mathbbm{1}[\tilde{g}_{S^{\prime}\cup A}(A)=\sigma].

We can rephrase
Lemma

4.2
in these terms as follows:

\sum_{\sigma}\EE_{S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2}\sim\mu_{q}}[h_{\sigma}(S^{\prime}_{1})(1-h_{\sigma}(S^{\prime}_{2}))]\leq R_{c}\sum_{\sigma}\EE_{(S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[h_{\sigma}(S^{\prime}_{1})(1-h_{\sigma}(S^{\prime}_{2}))].

Lemma

4.2
thus reduces to the following statement.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
h\colon\{0,1\}^{n^{\prime}}\to\{0,1\}
be an indicator function. There exists a constant
R_{c}
, depending only on
c
, such that

\EE_{S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2}\sim\mu_{q}}[h(S^{\prime}_{1})(1-h(S^{\prime}_{2}))]\leq R_{c}\EE_{(S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[h(S^{\prime}_{1})(1-h(S^{\prime}_{2}))].

At this point, we invoke Fourier analysis on
(\{0,1\}^{n^{\prime}},\mu_{q})
. The Fourier basis for this domain is given by the functions

\omega_{U}(y)=\prod_{i\in U}\omega_{i}(y),\text{ where }\omega_{i}(y)=\frac{y_{i}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}.

This basis is orthonormal with respect to
\mu_{q}
, and the corresponding Fourier expansion of
h
is

h(y)=\sum_{U}\hat{h}(U)\omega_{U}(y),

where
\hat{h}(\emptyset)=\EE[h]
. Since
h
is Boolean, we furthermore have

\EE[h]=\EE[h^{2}]=\sum_{U}\hat{h}(U)^{2}.

This allows us to express the left-hand side of
Lemma

4.4
as

\EE_{S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2}\sim\mu_{q}}[h(S^{\prime}_{1})(1-h(S^{\prime}_{2}))]=\EE[h]-\EE[h]^{2}=\sum_{U\neq\emptyset}\hat{h}(U)^{2}.

In order to express the right-hand side in a similar form, it suffices to compute

\rho_{c}=\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[\omega_{i}(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}})\omega_{i}(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{2}})].

Indeed, since the marginal distributions of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are
\mu_{q}
, then

\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[\omega_{i}(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}})]=\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[\omega_{i}(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{2}})]=0.

The independence of coordinates implies that

\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[\omega_{U_{1}}(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}})\omega_{U_{2}}(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{2}})]=\begin{cases}\rho_{c}^{|U_{1}|}&\text{if }U_{1}=U_{2},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore the expectation on the right-hand side of
Lemma

4.4
is

\EE_{(S^{\prime}_{1},S^{\prime}_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}[h(S^{\prime}_{1})(1-h(S^{\prime}_{2}))]=\EE[h]-\sum_{U}\rho_{c}^{|U|}\hat{h}(U)^{2}=\sum_{U}(1-\rho_{c}^{|U|})\hat{h}(U)^{2}\geq(1-|\rho_{c}|)\sum_{U\neq\emptyset}\hat{h}(U)^{2},

since
|\rho_{c}|\leq 1
by Cauchy–Schwarz.
It follows that
Lemma

4.4
holds as long as
R_{c}\geq 1/(1-\rho_{c})
.

In order to complete the proof of
Lemma

4.4
, we compute
\rho_{c}
. If
(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}
then
\Pr[(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}})_{i}=1]=\Pr[(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{2}})_{i}=1]=q
and

\Pr[(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}})_{i}=(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{2}})_{i}=1]=cq+(1-cq)\frac{(q-cq)^{2}}{(1-cq)^{2}}=\frac{q^{2}-2cq^{2}+cq}{1-cq}.

Therefore

\rho_{c}=\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{q,cq}}\left[\frac{((\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}})_{i}-q)((\mathbbm{1}_{S_{2}})_{i}-q)}{q(1-q)}\right]=\frac{1}{q(1-q)}\left[\frac{q^{2}-2cq^{2}+cq}{1-cq}-q^{2}\right]=\frac{cq(1-q)}{(1-cq)q}\leq c,

implying that we can take
R_{c}=1/(1-c)
.

5
Structure theorem

In this section we prove our main structure theorem,
Theorem

1.1
, which we reformulate using terminology defined in
Section

2
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
f\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\{0,1\}
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
, where
p\leq 1/2
. Then
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\varepsilon)
and
\EE[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
for some function
g\colon(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following properties:

1.

If
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
has weight at most
d
then
g(y)\in\{0,1\}
.

2.

g
is a
(d,O_{d}(1/p))
-sparse junta.

3.

For every
e
, the number of minimal non-Boolean inputs of
g
of weight
e
is
O_{d}(\varepsilon/p^{e})
.

Conversely, if
g
satisfies these properties, then it is
O_{d}(\varepsilon)
-close to Boolean.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
f
be as in the theorem. Then
f
is
O_{d}(\varepsilon)
-close to
\round(g,\{0,1\})
, where
g
is the function in the theorem.

Conversely, if
g
satisfies the conditions in the theorem, then
\round(g,\{0,1\})
is
O_{d}(\varepsilon)
-close to degree
d
.

Proof 5.3

(Proof of Corollary
5.2
)
.

Let
f
be as in the theorem. Then

\Pr[f\neq\round(g,\{0,1\})]\leq\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\varepsilon).

Conversely, suppose that
g
satisfies the conditions in the theorem. Then
g
has degree
d
and

\EE[(\round(g,\{0,1\})-g)^{2}]=O_{d}(\varepsilon)

since
g
is
O_{d}(\varepsilon)
-close to Boolean.

Henceforth, all big O constants will depend on
d
, and we do not mention this explicitly.

Most of the effort will be concentrated on proving the direct part of the theorem, which we will do in two steps:

1.

We first construct a sparse junta
h
which is
O(\varepsilon)
-close to
f
.

2.

We then modify
h
to a sparse junta
g
which satisfies the first two properties in the theorem, and show that it satisfies the third property as well.

We then prove the easier converse part of the theorem.

5.1
Step 1: Agreement

Since
f
is
\varepsilon
-close to degree
d
, there is a degree
d
function
f^{\leq d}
such that
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-f^{\leq d})^{2}]\leq\varepsilon
.

For
S\subseteq[n]
, define

\varepsilon_{S}=\EE_{\mu_{1/2}}[(f|_{S}-f^{\leq d}|_{S})^{2}],

where
\mu_{1/2}
refers to the distribution
\mu_{1/2}(\{0,1\}^{S})
; below we usually omit the domain.
Since sampling
S\sim\mu_{2p}
and
y\sim\mu_{1/2}(\{0,1\}^{S})
is the same as sampling
y\sim\mu_{p}
,

\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}[\varepsilon_{S}]=\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-f^{\leq d})^{2}]\leq\varepsilon.

Applying the Kindler–Safra theorem (
Theorem

2.5
), there exist Boolean degree
d
functions
g_{S}
such that
\EE_{\mu_{1/2}}[(f|_{S}-g_{S})^{2}]=O(\varepsilon_{S})
,
and so

\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}\left[\EE_{\mu_{1/2}}[(f|_{S}-g_{S})^{2}]\right]=O\left(\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}[\varepsilon_{S}]\right)=O(\varepsilon).

We would like to paste the functions
g_{S}
to a global function
h
using the junta agreement theorem (
Theorem

4.1
), which we will apply with
K=M_{d}
(the value from
Theorem

2.3
),
c=\sqrt{1/2}
, and
q=2p
.
Theorem

2.3
shows that each
g_{S}
is a
K
-junta. In order to apply the junta agreement theorem, we need to verify the agreement condition:

\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{2p,\sqrt{2}p}}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]=O(\varepsilon).

To see this, first notice that since
g_{S_{1}}
and
g_{S_{2}}
are both
K
-juntas, if
g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}
then
\Pr_{y\sim\mu_{\sqrt{1/2}}}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}(y)\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}(y)]\geq\sqrt{1/2}^{2K}=2^{-K}
. Since
g_{S_{1}}
and
g_{S_{2}}
are Boolean, this shows that

\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{2p,\sqrt{2}p}}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]\leq 2^{K}\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{2p,\sqrt{2}p}}\left[\EE_{\mu_{\sqrt{1/2}}}[(g_{S_{1}}|_{T}-g_{S_{2}}|_{T})^{2}]\right].

Applying the inequality
(g_{S_{1}}|_{T}-g_{S_{2}}|_{T})^{2}\leq 2(g_{S_{1}}|_{T}-f|_{T})^{2}+2(g_{S_{2}}|_{T}-f|_{T})^{2}
, we obtain

\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{2p,\sqrt{2}p}}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]\leq 2^{K+1}\EE_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{2p,\sqrt{2}p}}\left[\EE_{\mu_{\sqrt{1/2}}}[(g_{S_{1}}|_{T}-f|_{T})^{2}]\right].

We can choose
(S_{1},T)
in the following way: choose
S_{1}\sim\mu_{2p}
, and choose
T\sim\mu_{\sqrt{1/2}}(S_{1})
. If we then choose
y\sim\mu_{\sqrt{1/2}}(T)
then it is equivalent to directly sampling
y\sim\mu_{1/2}(S_{1})
.
This shows that

\Pr_{(S_{1},S_{2},T)\sim\mu_{2p,\sqrt{2}p}}[g_{S_{1}}|_{T}\neq g_{S_{2}}|_{T}]\leq 2^{K+1}\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}\left[\EE_{\mu_{1/2}}[(g_{S}-f|_{S})^{2}]\right]=O(\varepsilon),

and so the agreement condition is satisfied.

Applying the junta agreement theorem, we obtain a
(d,O(1/p))
-sparse junta
h
such that

\Pr_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}[h|_{S}\neq g_{S}]=O(\varepsilon).

Furthermore, each monomial coefficient of
h
is a monomial coefficient of some Boolean degree
d
function, and so belongs to
\{-2^{d-1},\dots,2^{d-1}\}
by
Lemma

2.1
.

To conclude this step of the proof, we need to show that
\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-h)^{2}]=O(\varepsilon)
. We first show that
\Pr_{\mu_{p}}[f\neq h]=O(\varepsilon)
. Indeed,

\Pr_{\mu_{p}}[f\neq h]=\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}\left[\Pr_{\mu_{1/2}}[f|_{S}\neq h|_{S}]\right]\leq\EE_{S\sim\mu_{2p}}\left[\Pr_{\mu_{1/2}}[f|_{S}\neq g_{S}]+\mathbbm{1}[g_{S}\neq h|_{S}]\right]=O(\varepsilon).

(The term
\mathbbm{1}[g_{S}\neq h|_{S}]
is an indicator.)

In order to convert this
L_{0}
guarantee to an
L_{2}
guarantee, we use
Lemma

3.16
, which implies that

\EE_{\mu_{p}}[\dist(h,\{0,1\})^{2}]=O(\Pr_{\mu_{p}}[h\notin\{0,1\}])=O(\Pr_{\mu_{p}}[h\neq f])=O(\varepsilon).

This implies that

\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-h)^{2}]\leq 2\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(f-\round(h,\{0,1\}))^{2}]+2\EE_{\mu_{p}}[(\round(h,\{0,1\})-h)^{2}]=2\Pr_{\mu_{p}}[f\neq h]+O(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon).

Concluding, in this step we have constructed a function
h
satisfying the following properties:

1.

h
is a
(d,C/p)
-sparse junta.

2.

h
is
B

In this section we prove

Definition 7.1
.

Given

, the parameter
m_{d}
is the maximum
m
such that there exists a degree
d

P
satisfying
P(0),\ldots,P(m-1)\in\{0,1\}
and
P(0)\neq P(1)
.

It might not be clear from the definition that
m_{d}<\infty
. In fact,
m_{d}\leq 2d
. To see this, suppose that
P
is a degree
d
polynomial
P
such that
P(0),\ldots,P(m-1)\in\{0,1\}
and
P(0)\neq P(1)
. Since
P(0)\neq P(1)
, the polynomial
P
is not constant, and so there can be at most
d

x
such that
P(x)=0
and at most
d
values

such that
P(y)=1
. Consequently
m\leq 2d
. In contrast, the polynomial
P(x)=(1-x)\cdots(d-x)/d!
shows that
m_{d}\geq d+1
.

Von zur Gathen and Roche
[
14
]
considered a similar parameter

in which the condition

The constant

.

We do not know the optimal value of

.
If we consider

Proof 7.3

(Proof of converse part)

If

e_{2}
elements

from
A_{2}

Lemma 7.11

Let

.

The proof is by induction on

Suppose first that if we choose
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\in\mathcal{S}
