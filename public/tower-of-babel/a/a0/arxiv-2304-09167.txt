Optimal PAC Bounds Without Uniform Convergence
Ishaq Aden-Ali, Yeshwanth Cherapanamjeri, Abhishek Shetty, Nikita Zhivotovskiy

Abstract
In statistical learning theory, determining the sample complexity of realizable binary classification for VC classes was a long-standing open problem. The results of Simon [ 49 ] and Hanneke [ 22 ] established sharp upper bounds in this setting. However, the reliance of their argument on the uniform convergence principle limits its applicability to more general learning settings such as multiclass classification. In this paper, we address this issue by providing optimal high probability risk bounds through a framework that surpasses the limitations of uniform convergence arguments. Our framework converts the leave-one-out error of permutation invariant predictors into high probability risk bounds. As an application, by adapting the one-inclusion graph algorithm of Haussler, Littlestone, and Warmuth [ 29 ] , we propose an algorithm that achieves an optimal PAC bound for binary classification. Specifically, our result shows that certain aggregations of one-inclusion graph algorithms are optimal, addressing a variant of a classic question posed by Warmuth [ 61 ] . We further instantiate our framework in three settings where uniform convergence is provably suboptimal. For multiclass classification, we prove an optimal risk bound that scales with the one-inclusion hypergraph density of the class, addressing the suboptimality of the analysis of Daniely and Shalev-Shwartz [ 18 ] . For partial hypothesis classification, we determine the optimal sample complexity bound, resolving a question posed by Alon, Hanneke, Holzman, and Moran [ 5 ] . For realizable bounded regression with absolute loss, we derive an optimal risk bound that relies on a modified version of the scale-sensitive dimension, refining the results of Bartlett and Long [ 14 ] . Our rates surpass standard uniform convergence-based results due to the smaller complexity measure in our risk bound.

2.2
Assumptions

Our main result is applicable to a broad class of predictors satisfying two natural assumptions.
The main assumption pertains to the

As described in

1
, the predictor is first evaluated on each point in the training sample with the predictor trained on the training sample with that point removed. The sum of these errors over all points in the training sample yields the leave-one-out error.
We assume that the predictor has bounded leave-one-out error.

Assumption 2.1
.

The predictor
\widehat{f}
has bounded leave-one-out error for any realizable sample. Formally, for any realizable

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i};S^{-i}{}),y_{i})\leqslant M_{n}.

Further, we will assume that

is monotone in the sample size

for all

.
3
3

3

This is largely without loss of generality since in most natural settings, we can replace

with

Remark 2.2

Note that by averaging over a sample

\displaystyle\E_{S\sim P^{n}}\operatorname{R}_{P}(\widehat{f}\left(\cdot;S\right))\leqslant\frac{M_{n+1}}{n+1}.

(1)

We formally state it as the following assumption.

2.3
Main result

We present our main result which concerns the performance of a sequence of hypotheses generated by training a predictor,
\widehat{f}
, on prefixes of the training data,
(S_{\leqslant t})_{t=1}^{n}
.
When
\widehat{f}
is symmetric and has bounded leave-one-out error, our result asserts that the
average
risk of the predictors in the
suffix
of the sequence
(\widehat{f}(\cdot;S_{\leqslant t}))_{t=1}^{n}
is bounded by
M_{n}
, with high probability.
For the sake of simplicity, we assume for the rest of the paper that
n/4
is an integer.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
, loss function
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
, and a predictor
\widehat{f}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{Y}
satisfying
2.3
and
2.1
.
Then, for any realizable distribution
P
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
,
given a training sample
S\sim P^{n}
we have

\frac{4}{3n}\cdot\sum_{t=n/4}^{n-1}\operatorname{R}_{P}(\widehat{f}(\cdot;S_{\leqslant t}{}))\leqslant 4.82\left(\frac{M_{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right),

with probability at least
1-\delta
over the randomness of
S
.

Our proof technique is based on
online-to-batch conversion
, which transforms any online learning algorithm — making
M
mistakes on a finite sample — into a batch algorithm utilizing the entire sample in a statistical setting.
A key technical aspect of our work is demonstrating that this technique remains applicable even if
M
is a random variable dependent on the specific realization of the training sample.

Another technical element of our proof is the use of suffix averaging.
To illustrate this, consider an algorithm that, upon processing an i.i.d. realizable sample of size
t
, makes an error on a newly sampled point with probability at most
d/t
, where
d
is, for example, the VC dimension. The expected total number of mistakes for a sample of size
n
is then bounded by
\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{n}d/t=O(d\log n)
, which cannot lead to the desired accuracy.
Interestingly, when utilizing only the suffix of the sample, we have
\sum\nolimits_{t=n/2}^{n}d/t=O(d)
, effectively eliminating the logarithmic factor. Using martingale arguments, we essentially convert the last computation into a high probability risk bound that scales as

O\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

Remarkably, the two observations discussed above emerged almost simultaneously in the late 1980s. Haussler, Littlestone, and Warmuth
[
28
]
discovered that suffix averaging does not introduce logarithmic factors in sequential errors, while Littlestone established optimal online-to-batch conversions for algorithms with a deterministic number of errors
[
37
]
.
2.1
connects these two insights.

Let

2.5.1
Relations to uniform convergence results

Characterization of uniform convergence for real valued sets of functions has been a central question in empirical processes theory motivated by problems in statistical learning theory and convex geometry.
The work of Vapnik and Chervonenkis
[
57

initiated the study of uniform convergence for real valued functions in terms of a scale-insensitive version of the complexity measure
\mathrm{V_{\mathcal{H}}}(\gamma)

The work of Alon et al.

studied the complexity measure

Similar conclusions appeared in

See also the related results in

The main problem with the complexity measure

\mathcal{H}
be a class of

-valued functions on some domain set

satisfying: For every

and, for every

f_{A}(x)\geqslant s(x)+\gamma

This complexity measure is typically referred to as the

3
Related work

The optimal sample complexity of binary classification.

Determining the optimal sample complexity, or equivalently the optimal risk bound, of realizable (noiseless) binary classification is a classic problem in statistical learning theory. The early work of Vapnik and Chervonenkis
[
54
]
introduced what we call the PAC model in this paper (a terminology due to Valiant
[
53
]
, who also studied these questions from a computational perspective), providing matching upper and lower risk bounds for finite hypothesis classes
\mathcal{H}
. Subsequently, Vapnik and Chervonenkis presented the first upper bounds on the error rate for an infinite class, specifically the class of half-spaces in
\mathbb{R}^{p}

[
55
]
. Their next groundbreaking work
[
56
]
generalized the shattering properties of half-spaces to general classes and introduced the complexity measure now known as the VC dimension. The monograph
[
58
]
and the paper
[
11
]
provide the canonical risk bound applicable to any classifier consistent with the training sample:

O\left(\frac{d}{n}\log\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

(2)

This convergence rate is known to be unimprovable for certain sample consistent classification rules and hypothesis classes
[
29
,
6
,
49
,
23
]
.
However, the existing lower bound
[
20
]
that applies to any predictor

\Omega\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)

(3)

did not match the risk bound (
2
) by a multiplicative logarithmic factor.
The pivotal work of Haussler, Littlestone, and Warmuth
[
29
]
(originally presented as
[
28
]
) took a significant step towards proving an upper bound that matches the rate of (
3
). They introduced the one-inclusion graph algorithm and presented a simple confidence boosting strategy that occasionally provides a better risk bound

O\left(\frac{d}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right),

(4)

raising the question of what the optimal sample complexity of PAC learning is, and whether the one-inclusion graph algorithm is itself optimal
[
61
]
. Since then, numerous authors have provided bounds matching the rate of the lower bound (
3
) for specific classes
[
13
,
37
,
8
,
6
,
17
,
23
]
and/or under additional assumptions on the distribution of the data
[
40
,
16
,
15
,
23
]
. The problem of obtaining a rate matching (
3
) up to constant factors was ultimately resolved by Hanneke
[
22
]
, who improved upon an earlier result by Simon
[
49
]
which contained a slowly growing additional multiplicative term.

Leave-one-out/exchangeability arguments.

The leave-one-out analysis is a standard approach to control the
expected
risk of classification rules. Some early applications of this method can be traced back to the monograph of Vapnik and Chervonenkis
[
58
]
, where they utilized it to derive optimal risk bounds for both the Perceptron algorithm and the hard margin SVM. However, these results were valid only in-expectation, prompting Vapnik and Chervonenkis
[
58
, Chapter VI, Section 7]
to ask whether the same could be achieved with high probability for some practically relevant algorithms. Lunts and Brailovsky
[
35
]
showed that this is not possible in general by presenting an example where the leave-one-out argument failed to yield a low variance risk bound, thus excluding the possibility of attaining high-probability upper bounds without additional assumptions. The leave-one-out argument is also central to the analysis of the one-inclusion graph algorithm introduced in
[
29
]
. Warmuth
[
61
]
conjectured that this algorithm consistently provides optimal PAC upper bounds. However, it was recently demonstrated in
[
4
]
that, without additional assumptions, the one-inclusion graph algorithm is unable to achieve the optimal PAC bound. Lastly, we draw attention to the recent analysis of
stable compression schemes
in
[
64
,
12
,
25
]
, which reveals that a leave-one-out argument can indeed result in sharp high probability bounds when stability assumptions are incorporated.

Confidence boosting and online to batch conversions.

We first discuss the
confidence boosting
approach, a natural method for deriving high-probability risk bounds from in-expectation ones. This technique involves running the in-expectation optimal algorithm on
O\left(\log(1/\delta)\right)
independent partitions of the original sample and selecting the best performer based on an independent hold-out sample. Confidence boosting was employed by Haussler, Littlestone, and Warmuth
[
29
]
, however this approach only leads to a bound with a multiplicative
\log(1/\delta)
as in (
4
).

A more pertinent approach is the online-to-batch conversion, with its earliest relevant applications tracing back to the works
[
3
,
58
]
. Littlestone
[
37
]
demonstrated that if a conservative online algorithm (an algorithm that only changes its state when a point is misclassified) makes a finite number
M
of mistakes on any sample, it can be converted into a classifier in a statistical setting with a risk bound

O\left(\frac{M}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

(5)

Despite providing a correct additive term containing
\log(1/\delta)
, Littlestone’s bound has two major limitations, both addressed in this paper. The first limitation is the requirement of a finite number of mistakes, characterized by the so-called
Littlestone dimension

[
36
]
, which is infinite for most practically relevant hypothesis classes. Haussler, Littlestone, and Warmuth
[
29
]
attempted to address this issue by considering the
expected
number of mistakes made by one-inclusion graph algorithms run on the sample in an online manner, resulting in an expected number of mistakes scaling as
O(d\log n)
. The second limitation of the bound (
5
) is the necessity for the algorithm to be conservative. Although any algorithm achieving a deterministic mistake bound can be converted to a conservative one
[
21
]
, this is not known to hold for algorithms achieving a mistake bound
in-expectation
as is the case in our work.

A pertinent reference is the work of Wu, Heidari, Grama, and Szpankowski
[
62
]
.
They proved, via a reverse martingale argument, that
high-probability online bounds
analogous to the in-expectation bound of Haussler et al.
[
29
]
can be attained, showing that
O(d\log n+\log(1/\delta))
mistakes are made with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Another observation stems from
[
29
]
, who noted that when the one-inclusion graph algorithm is executed in an online fashion, and its error is measured solely on the second half of the sample — referred to as the
suffix
— the expected number of mistakes diminishes to
O(d)
.
This idea of utilizing the suffix of the sample is quite general and has led to recent sharp high-probability bounds for stochastic gradient descent
[
27
]
.

Multiclass classification and partial hypotheses.

Multiclass classification is a natural extension of the binary case. The works
[
45
,
44
]
extended the PAC model to multiclass problems and introduced an analog of the VC dimension, now commonly referred to as the Natarajan dimension. Latter work asked whether this dimension characterizes learnability and highlighted the insufficiency of standard uniform convergence techniques for answering this question. Subsequent works exploring the Natarajan dimension include
[
26
,
10
]
.
While the Natarajan dimension enables the uniform convergence property with a finite number of classes, ERMs may fail to learn the class when the number of classes is unbounded
[
19
]
.
The authors of
[
47
]
adapted the one-inclusion-graph algorithm to multiclass classification, while Daniely and Shalev-Shwartz
[
18
]
characterized the expected optimal risk of multiclass problems by the one-inclusion hypergraph density. Recent results and a detailed survey on complexity measures in multiclass classification can be found in
[
9
]
.

A partial hypothesis class consists of
\{0,1,\star\}
-valued hypotheses. A key distinction from multiclass classification is the unique treatment of
\star
-values, which do not affect the notion of shattering or corresponding complexity measures. This idea traces back to the work of Haussler and Long
[
26
]
and is further explored in the work of Bartlett and Long
[
14
]
.
The authors in
[
14
]
recognized that uniform convergence should not be relied upon for partial hypotheses and adapted the one-inclusion graph algorithm accordingly. Subsequent work by Long
[
39
]
extended the analysis of partial hypotheses to the agnostic setting and derived corresponding risk bounds. The recent resurgence of interest in this topic has been sparked by the work of Alon, Hanneke, Holzman, and Moran
[
5
]
. Their contributions include determining sharper bounds for both the realizable and agnostic cases, while also extending these ideas to online learning.

We begin with some preliminary definitions for hypergraphs.
We define an

to be a function

such that for any

\sigma_{G}(e)\in e
.
Given a hypergraph

and orientation

of a vertex

\mathrm{out}(v;\sigma_{G})=|\{e\in E:v\in e,\sigma_{G}(e)\not=v\}|.

For a hypergraph

, the

of

\mathrm{out}(\sigma_{G})=\max_{v\in V}\mathrm{out}(v;\sigma_{G})
.

We are now ready to describe the one-inclusion hypergraph algorithm as pseudocode in

Algorithm 1

Inputs:
Training sample

.

Output:

\widehat{f}_{\operatorname{OIG}}(\cdot;{S}):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}

For any point

1:

If there is a unique label

The one-inclusion (hyper)graph algorithm
[

.
The algorithm as presented above is the standard extension that defines a predictor for the entire domain

When predicting on a test point

).
Before we explore the leave-one-out error of this algorithm, we connect
\mathrm{out}(G(\mathcal{H}|_{{\mathcal{X}}_{S}\cup\{x\}}))

.

Every hypergraph

has an orientation

for which the max out-degree

.
This is a standard fact for graphs (e.g., see

[

We expect the generalized statement for hypergraphs to also be known, but since we could not find an explicit reference, we include a proof based on a maximum flow argument generalizing a known proof for the graph case

Lemma A.1
.

For any hypergraph
G=(V,E)
and any integer

, there is an orientation
\sigma_{G}
such that the max out-degree satisfies
\mathrm{out}(\sigma_{G})\leqslant d
.

Proof.

We build an auxilary graph that contains a source
s
, a sink
t
, and two vertex sets
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
between
s
and
t
.

V_{1}
contains a vertex for each edge in

, i.e.,
V_{1}=E
.

V_{2}
is equal to the vertex set of
G
, i.e.,
V_{2}=V
.
There is a weighted and directed edge from
s
to every
e\in V_{1}
.
For each
e\in V_{1}
, the weight of the edge to
e
from
s
is equal to
|e|-1
.
For every
e\in V_{1}
, there is a weighted directed edge from
e
to every

that participates in the hyperedge
e
in
G
.
Each such edge has weight
1
.
Finally, there is an edge from every
v\in V_{2}
to
t
, each with weight
d
.
We will prove that the maximum flow of this graph from
s
to
t
is equal to
m\coloneqq\sum_{e\in V_{1}}|e|-1
.

Since all the weights are integers, there exists an integral maximum flow.
Consider such a flow.
There is a simple way to obtain the desired orientation of
G
from this flow.
Fix an
e\in V_{1}
.
Since
e
gets
|e|-1
units of flow,
|e|-1
of its
|e|
neighbours gets a unit of flow.
Thus, there is a single neighbour

of

that does not get a unit of flow, and we orient the edge
e
in the original hypergraph

towards this

.
Repeat this for every

.
Every unit of flow some

recieves corresponds to an edge in

that it is not selected to be the head of. Since the capacity of the edge out of every

to

is
d
, we can conclude that the orientation satisfies that the out-degree of every

is at most
d
.

We now prove the maximum flow is
m
.
It is easy to see the flow is at most
m
since the sum of the edge weights from
s
to
V_{1}
is equal to
m
.
It remains to prove the maximum flow is at least
m
.
Recall that the maximum flow is equal to the minimum cut (set of edges) that separates
s
from
t
, so it suffices to prove a lower bound for the minimum cut.

The following is a useful observation we will make use of.
Notice that every path from
s
to
t
consists of 3 edges passing through some

and
v\in V_{2}

Fix one such path.
Since we consider a minimum cut, if the first edge (connecting
s
to
e

and
v
since this would only increase the cut value.
Similarly, if the third edge is cut (connecting
v
to
t

e
and
v

Let
U\subseteq V_{1}
be the set of vertices whose edge from
s

are not
cut.
Let

be the set of vertices whose edges to

are

Fix an

There are

Let

to

be the set of vertices for which the paths from

to

Since we consider the minimum cut, we can conclude that

We now upper bound the sum of the weight of the edges from

Write this sum as

Let
