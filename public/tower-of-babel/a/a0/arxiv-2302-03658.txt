Planted Bipartite Graph Detection
Asaf Rotenberg Wasim Huleihel Ofer Shayevitz

Abstract
We consider the task of detecting a hidden bipartite subgraph in a given random graph. This is formulated as a hypothesis testing problem, under the null hypothesis, the graph is a realization of an Erdős-Rényi random graph over n n vertices with edge density q q . Under the alternative, there exists a planted k 𝖱 × k 𝖫 k_{\mathsf{R}}\times k_{\mathsf{L}} bipartite subgraph with edge density p > q p>q . We characterize the statistical and computational barriers for this problem. Specifically, we derive information-theoretic lower bounds, and design and analyze optimal algorithms matching those bounds, in both the dense regime, where p , q = Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) p,q=\Theta\left(1\right) , and the sparse regime where p , q = Θ ⁡ ( n − α ) , α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ] p,q=\Theta\left(n^{-\alpha}\right),\alpha\in\left(0,2\right] . We also consider the problem of testing in polynomial-time. As is customary in similar structured high-dimensional problems, our model undergoes an “easy-hard-impossible” phase transition and computational constraints penalize the statistical performance. To provide an evidence for this statistical computational gap, we prove computational lower bounds based on the low-degree conjecture, and show that the class of low-degree polynomials algorithms fail in the conjecturally hard region.

Problem Formulation

Let us describe the general setting of planted bipartite graph detection. We have a total of
n
vertices. We pick
k_{\mathsf{R}}
out of
n
vertices, uniformly at random, and denote this set by
{\cal R}\subset\left[n\right]
. From the remaining
n-k_{\mathsf{R}}
vertices, we again pick another set of
k_{\mathsf{L}}
vertices, uniformly at random, and denote the obtained set by
{\cal L}\subseteq\left[n\right]\backslash{\cal R}
. We refer to
{\cal R}
and
{\cal L}
as the
right
and
left
planted sets, respectively. Let
\mathscr{E}\left({\cal R},{\cal L}\right)
be the set of edges
\left\{\left(i,j\right):i<j,i\in{\cal R},j\in{\cal L}\text{ or }i\in{\cal L},j\in{\cal R}\right\}
, and let
{\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
be an empty graph on
n
vertices, except for all the elements in
\mathscr{E}\left({\cal R},{\cal L}\right)
. Let
V\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)\triangleq{\cal R}\cup{\cal L}
and
E\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)\triangleq\mathscr{E}\left({\cal R},{\cal L}\right)
denote the set of vertices and edges of
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
, respectively. Note that
\left|V\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)\right|=k_{\mathsf{R}}+k_{\mathsf{L}}
and
\left|E\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)\right|=k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}
. We refer to
{\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
as the planted structure. Finally, let
{\mathsf{K}}^{p}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
be the random graph obtained by keeping each edge in
E\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)
, with probability
p
. Consequently, our detection problem can be phrased as the following simple hypothesis testing problem: under the null hypothesis
{\cal H}_{0}
, the observed graph
\mathsf{G}
is an Erdős-Rényi random graph
{\cal G}(n,q)
with edge density
0<q<1
, which might be a function of
n
. Under the alternative hypothesis
{\cal H}_{1}
, we first draw a random bipartite graph
{\mathsf{K}}^{p}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
on
n
vertices, as described above, and then join every other two vertices, such that at least one of the vertices is outside
{\cal L}\cup{\cal R}
, with probability
q
; the resulted graph is
\mathsf{G}
. Thus, the probability of a planted edge is
p
, and the probability of a non-planted edge is
q
. Without loss of generality, we assume throughout that
p>q
.

Problem 1

(Detection problem)
.

The planted dense bipartite subgraph (PDBS) detection problem, denoted by
\mathsf{PDBS}{\left(n,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}},p,q\right)}
, refers to the problem of distinguishing between the hypotheses:

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{0}:\mathsf{G}\sim{\cal G}(n,q)\quad\mathsf{vs}.\quad{\cal H}_{1}:\mathsf{G}\sim{\cal G}(n,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}},p,q).

(3)

We study the above framework in the asymptotic regime where
n\to\infty
, and
(k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}},p,q)
may also change as a function of
n
. Throughout the paper we assume that
k_{\mathsf{R}}+k_{\mathsf{L}}=o\left(n\right)
. Also, “dense regime” and “sparse regime” correspond to the cases where
p,q=\Theta(1)
and
p,q=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
, for
\alpha\in(0,2]
, respectively. Observing
\mathsf{G}
, the goal is to design a test
\phi:\left\{0,1\right\}^{\binom{n}{2}}\to\{0,1\}
that distinguishes between
{\cal H}_{0}
and
{\cal H}_{1}
. Specifically, the average
\mathsf{Type}

\mathsf{I}
+
\mathsf{II}
risk of a test
\phi
is defined as

\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi)=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\phi(\mathsf{G})=1)+\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\phi(\mathsf{G})=0)
. A sequence of tests
\phi_{n}
indexed by
n
succeeds if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{n})=0
, while if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{n})>0
, it fails. Finally, we denote the optimal risk by
\mathsf{R}_{n}^{\star}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{R}_{n}^{\star}\triangleq\inf_{\phi_{n}}\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{n})
. The above is summarized in the following definition.

Definition 2

(Strong detection)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
be the distributions of
\mathsf{G}
under the uniform and planted hypotheses, respectively. A possibly randomized algorithm
\phi_{n}(\mathsf{G})\in\{0,1\}
achieves strong detection, if its
\mathsf{Type}

\mathsf{I}
+
\mathsf{II}
risk satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{n})=0
. Conversely, if for all sequences of possibly randomized algorithms
\phi_{n}(\mathsf{G})\in\{0,1\}
, the
\mathsf{Type}

\mathsf{I}
+
\mathsf{II}
risk satisfies
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{n})>0
, then strong detection is impossible.

3
Main Results

In this section, we determine the statistical and computational limits of the planted bipartite subgraph detection problem. We start by presenting our information-theoretic lower bounds, followed by our algorithmic upper bounds. Then, we state our computational lower bounds, and finally, present several phase diagrams in order to illustrate our results as a function of the various parameters in our model.

Statistical lower bounds.

The following result give conditions under which strong detection is statistically (or, information-theoretically) impossible. For simplicity of notation, we define

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(x,y)\triangleq\log\left(1+\frac{n}{xy}\frac{\log 2}{2}\right),

(4)

for any
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
. Also, recall that the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between two Bernoulli random variables
\mathsf{Bern}(p)
and
\mathsf{Bern}(q)
is given by
\chi^{2}(p||q)=\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{q(1-q)}
.

Theorem 1

(Statistical lower bounds)
.

Consider the
\mathsf{PDBS}{\left(n,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}},p,q\right)}
detection problem in Problem
1
. Then, strong detection is impossible with
\mathsf{R}_{n}^{\star}>1/2
, if:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\frac{n\cdot\gamma_{n}(k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}})}{8k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}},

(5a)

and at least one of the following two conditions hold:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\frac{\gamma_{n}(k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{R}})}{2k_{\mathsf{L}}}\vee\frac{\gamma_{n}(k_{\mathsf{L}},k_{\mathsf{L}})}{2k_{\mathsf{R}}},

(5b)

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\left(\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n\cdot\gamma_{n}(k_{\mathsf{L}},k_{\mathsf{L}})}{8k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\right)\vee\left(\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}\wedge\frac{n\cdot\gamma_{n}(k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{R}})}{8k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\right).

(5c)

A few remarks are in order. First, it should be emphasized that while the theorem above hold for any values of
p
and
q
, we will see later on that when specified to the dense and sparse regimes, some of the conditions in (
5
) become redundant. Second, we will see that in the polynomial-scale, the thresholds in (
5
) become much simpler analytically, and easy to interpret. Finally, we would like to mention here that the various constants in Theorem
1
are not optimal; they were obtained with the goal of making the proof transparent, and because we focus mainly on the asymptotic behaviour of the thresholds for detection.

Upper bounds.

We now describe our upper bounds. To that end, we propose three algorithms and analyze their performance. Define the statistics:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Scan}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{({\cal R}^{\prime},{\cal L}^{\prime})\in{\cal S}_{k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in{\cal R}^{\prime},j\in{\cal L}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{A}_{ij}},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Count}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{i<j}\mathsf{A}_{ij},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathsf{MaxDeg}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathsf{A}_{ij},

(8)

where
{\cal S}_{k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}}\triangleq\{({\cal R}^{\prime},{\cal L}^{\prime})\subset\left[n\right]\times\left[n\right]:|{\cal R}^{\prime}|=k_{\mathsf{R}},|{\cal L}^{\prime}|=k_{\mathsf{L}},{\cal R}^{\prime}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}=\emptyset\}
. Then, our proposed tests are
\phi_{\mathsf{Scan}}\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\mathsf{Scan}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{Scan}}\right\}
,
\phi_{\mathsf{Count}}\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\mathsf{Count}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{Count}}\right\}
, and
\phi_{\mathsf{Deg}}\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\mathsf{MaxDeg}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{Deg}}\right\}
. As mentioned in the introduction, the tests above are rather classic and were proposed in many other related problems, e.g.,
[
61
,
62
,
30
,
20
,
10
,
8
,
19
,
63
]
. Also, note that the count and maximum degree tests exhibit polynomial computational complexity of
O(n^{2})
operations, and hence efficient. This is not the case for the scan test, which exhibits an exponential computational complexity, and thus inefficient. Indeed, the search space in (
6
) is of cardinality
|{\cal S}_{k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}}|=\binom{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\binom{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}
, which is at least quasi-polynomial already when
k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}=\omega(1)
. The following result provides sufficient conditions under which the risk of each of the tests above is small.

Theorem 2

(Algorithmic upper bounds)
.

Fix
\delta\in\left(0,1\right).
Consider the
\mathsf{PDBS}{\left(n,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}},p,q\right)}
detection problem in Problem
1
, and suppose that
p
and
q
are such that
|p-q|=O{\left(q\right)}
and
q<c<1
, for some constant
0<c<1
. Then,

1.

For
\tau_{\mathsf{Scan}}=k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\frac{p+q}{2}
, we have
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Scan}})\leq\delta
, provided that,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Omega{\left(\frac{\log{\left(\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}+\frac{\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}.

(9)

2.

For
\tau_{\mathsf{Count}}=\binom{n}{2}q+k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\frac{p-q}{2}
, we have
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Count}})\leq\delta
, provided that,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Omega{\left(\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\cdot\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)}.

(10)

3.

For
\tau_{\mathsf{Deg}}=\left(n-1\right)q+k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\frac{p-q}{2}
, we have
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Deg}})\leq\delta
, provided that,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Omega{\left(\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\cdot\left(\log n+\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)\right)}.

(11)

At this point, we would like to reduce the bounds in Theorems
1
and
2
to the dense and sparse regimes, starting with the former. In the dense case, we have
p,q=\Theta(1)
, and therefore
\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Theta(1)
as well. Due to the fact that
k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}=o(n)
, it is clear that the condition in (
5a
) is looser than the condition in (
5b
), and thus, among the two only the later prevail. Accordingly, strong detection is impossible if (
5b
) or (
5c
) hold; as we show next (
5b
) can be complemented by nearly matching upper bounds, and thus (
5c
) is not needed. This is most easily seen when some of the poly-log factors are neglected. Specifically, note that when
k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}\gg n
then (
5b
) can never hold because
\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Theta(1)
. Therefore, in this case it must be that
k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}\ll n
, in which case, (
5b
) boils down to the condition that
k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}=O(\log n)
. To conclude, in the dense regime, strong detection is statistically impossible if,

\displaystyle k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}=O(\log n)\quad\mathsf{and}\quad k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}\ll n.

(12)

This mimics the asymptotic performance of the scan and degree tests. Indeed, using Theorem
2
, we see from (
9
) and (
11
) that strong detection is possible if

\displaystyle k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}=\Omega(\log n)\quad\mathsf{or}\quad k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}\gg n.

(13)

Specifically, the condition in the left-hand-side of (
13
) guarantees that
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Scan}})\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
, while the condition in the right-hand-side of (
13
) guarantees that
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Deg}})\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.
Note that the above results imply that in the dense regime, the statistical barrier depends on the bipartite planted graph through the quantities
k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}
and
k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}
, which essentially are, up to a constant factor, the maximal subgraph density and the maximal vertex degree of the planted structure, respectively. Also, we would like to mention here that while in the above discussion we focused on the polynomial scale, especially, with regard to the conditions at the right-hand-side of (
12
) and (
13
), Theorems
1
and
2
give logarithmic factors as well. Finally, it is interesting to note that in the dense regime, the count test is redundant. This is not the case in the sparse regime as we explain next.

We move forward to the sparse regime, where
p,q=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
, for some
\alpha\in(0,2]
, implying that
\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
. In this case, we need both conditions (
5a
) and (
5c
) in Theorem
1
. Let us simplify those conditions in the polynomial scale, as we for dense case. To that end, we use the fact that
\gamma_{n}(x,y)=\Omega(1\wedge\frac{n}{xy})
. Then, (
5a
) can be written as
\chi^{2}(p||q)=O\left(\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\right)
. Similarly, (
5c
) can be written as the union between
\chi^{2}(p||q)=O\left(\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}\wedge\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\right)
and
\chi^{2}(p||q)=O\left(\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\right)
. Intersecting these conditions we get that strong detection is impossible if
\chi^{2}(p||q)=O\left(\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)
or
\chi^{2}(p||q)=O\left(\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\wedge\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)
, where we have used the fact that asymptotically
\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}}
can never survive the minimum as
\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{R}}},\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}=o\left(\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)
, due to the assumption that
k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}=o(n)
. Finally, it is not difficult to check that the union of the last two conditions give,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=O\left(\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\right).

(14)

This barrier mimics the asymptotic performance of the scan, count, and degree tests. Indeed, using Theorem
2
, we see from (
9
), (
10
), and (
11
) that strong detection is possible if

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Omega\left(\frac{\log n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n^{2}\log n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\wedge\frac{n\log n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\right).

(15)

Indeed, each of the terms from left to right inside the
\Omega(\cdot)
expression at the right-hand-side of (
15
), guarantee that
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Scan}})\to 0
,
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Count}})\to 0
, and
\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Deg}})\to 0
, respectively, as
n\to\infty
. Later on, we illustrate the above bounds using phase diagrams that illustrate the tradeoff between the various parameters as a function of
n
. One evident and important observation here is that both the efficient count and degree tests, and the exhaustive scan test, are needed to attain the information-theoretic lower bounds (up to poly-log factors). As discussed above, however, the scan test is not efficient. Accordingly, from the computational point of view, one may wonder whether the inefficient scan test can be replaced by an efficient test with the same performance guarantees, i.e., one that achieves the same statistical barrier as that of the scan test. We next give evidence that, based on the
low-degree polynomial conjecture
, efficient algorithms that run in polynomial-time do not exist in the regime where the scan test succeeds and the count and degree tests fail.

Computational lower bounds.

Inspecting our lower and upper bounds above, it can be seen that there is a gap in terms of what can be achieved statistically and efficiently. In particular, in the region where
k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\ll n
, for the dense regime, or the region where
\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}}\ll\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}
, for the sparse regime, while the detection problem can be solved by an exhaustive search, we do not have a polynomial-time algorithm. As mentioned above, we conjecture that, in fact, polynomial-time algorithms do not exist in this regions of parameters, and we next provide an evidence for this claim. To that end, we start with a brief introduction to the method of
low-degree polynomials
.

The basic premise of this method is that all polynomial-time algorithms for solving detection problems are captured by polynomials of low-degree. By now, there is growing strong evidence in support of this conjecture. The ideas below were first developed in a sequence of works in the sum-of-squares optimization literature
[
42
,
15
,
14
,
49
]
. We follow the notations and definition in
[
15
,
64
]
. Any distribution
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
on
\Omega_{n}=\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}
induces an inner product of measurable functions
f,g:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[f(\mathsf{G})g(\mathsf{G})]
, and norm
\left\|f\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}=\left\langle f,f\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{1/2}
. We Let
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}})
denote the Hilbert space consisting of functions
f
for which
\left\|f\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}<\infty
, endowed with the above inner product and norm.
The underlying idea is to find the low-degree polynomial that best distinguishes
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
from
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
in the
L^{2}
sense. Let
{\cal L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}})
denote the linear subspace of polynomials
\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree at most
\mathsf{D}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the
\mathsf{D}
-low-degree likelihood ratio

\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}
is the projection of a function
\mathsf{L}_{n}
to the span of coordinate-degree-
\mathsf{D}
functions, where the projection is orthogonal with respect to the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}
. As discussed above, the likelihood ratio optimally distinguishes
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
from
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
in the
L^{2}
sense. The next lemma shows that over the set of low-degree polynomials, the
\mathsf{D}
-low-degree likelihood ratio exhibits the same property.

Lemma 1

(Optimally of
\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}

[
14
,
49
,
64
]
)
.

Consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathrm{max}\;\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}}f(\mathsf{G})\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}f^{2}(\mathsf{G})=1,\;f\in{\cal L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}},

(16)

Then, the unique solution
f^{\star}
for (
16
) is the
\mathsf{D}
-low degree likelihood ratio
f^{\star}=\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}/\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}
, and the value of the optimization problem is
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}
.

As was mentioned above, in the computationally-unbounded regime, an important property of the likelihood ratio is that if
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}
is bounded then
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
are statistically indistinguishable. The following conjecture states that a computational analogue of this property holds, with
\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}
playing the role of the likelihood ratio. In fact it also postulates that polynomials of degree
\approx\log n
are a proxy for polynomial-time algorithms. The conjecture below is based on
[
15
,
14
,
49
]
, and
[
15
, Conj. 2.2.4]
. We give an informal statement of this conjecture which appears in
[
64
, Conj. 1.16]
. For a precise statement, we refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
15
, Conj. 2.2.4]
and
[
64
, Sec. 4]
.

Conjecture 1

(Low-degree conj., informal)
.

Given a sequence of probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
, if there exists
\epsilon>0
and
\mathsf{D}=\mathsf{D}(n)\geq(\log n)^{1+\epsilon}
, such that
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}
remains bounded as
n\to\infty
, then there is no polynomial-time algorithm that distinguishes
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
.

In the sequel, we will rely on Conjecture
1
to give an evidence for the statistical-computational gap observed in our problem. At this point we would like to mention
[
15
, Hypothesis 2.1.5]
, which states a more general form of Conjecture
1
in the sense that it postulates that degree-
\mathsf{D}
polynomials are a proxy for
n^{O(D)}
-time algorithms.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the
\mathsf{PDBS}{\left(n,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}},p,q\right)}
detection problem in Problem
1
.

1.

In the regime where
p,q=\Theta(1)
, if
(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})\ll n
, then
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}\leq O(1)
, for any
\mathsf{D}\leq C\log n
and
C>0
. Conversely, there exists a positive integer
\mathsf{D}
such that if
(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})\gg n
, then
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}\geq\omega(1)
.

2.

In the regime where
p,q=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
, for some
\alpha\in(0,2]
, if
\frac{1}{(k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}})}\ll\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n}{(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})}
, then
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}\leq O(1)
, for any
\mathsf{D}=n^{o(1)}
. Conversely, there exists a positive integer
\mathsf{D}
such that if
\chi^{2}(p||q)\gg\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n}{(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})}
, then
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}\geq\omega(1)
.

Together with Conjecture
1
, Theorem
3
implies that if we take degree-
\log n
polynomials as a proxy for all efficient algorithms, our calculations predict that an
n^{O(\log n)}
algorithm does not exist when
(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})\ll n
in the dense regime, and
\frac{1}{(k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}})}\ll\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n}{(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})}
in the sparse regime. This is summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Computational lower bound)
.

Consider the
\mathsf{PDBS}{\left(n,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}},p,q\right)}
detection problem in Problem
1
, and assume that Conjecture
1
holds. A polynomial-time algorithm that achieves strong detection does not exist in the following situations:

1.

If
p,q=\Theta(1)
and
(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})\ll n
.

2.

If
p,q=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
, for some
\alpha\in(0,2]
, and
\frac{1}{(k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}})}\ll\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n}{(k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}})}
.

These predictions agree precisely with the previously established statistical-computational tradeoffs in the previous subsections. A more explicit formula for the computational barrier which exhibits dependency on
\mathsf{D}
can be deduced from the proof of Theorem
3
; to keep the exposition simple we opted to present the refined result above.

Remark 1
.

We would like to mention that while the focus in second part of Theorem
3
is on the specific scaling of
p,q=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
1
, by finding conditions under which the risk of any algorithm strictly positive. To that end, we will use the following well-known fact that the optimal test, denoted by
\phi^{\star}_{n}
, minimizing the average risk, is given by,

\displaystyle\phi^{\star}_{n}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)\triangleq\begin{cases}1,\ &\text{if }\mathsf{L}_{n}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)\geq 1\\
0,\ &\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(17)

where
\mathsf{L}_{n}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)\triangleq\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)}
.
The optimal risk
\mathsf{R}_{n}^{\star}
associated with the above optimal test, can be lower bounded using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality as follows,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}^{\star}

\displaystyle=1-d_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}|\mathsf{L}_{n}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)-1|

(19)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)]-1}.

(20)

Thus, for finding conditions under which the optimal risk is positive we can find conditions under which
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)]
lies in the interval
(1,5)
.

We start by finding a formula for the second moment of the likelihood under
{\cal H}_{0}
. Note that the likelihood ratio is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{n}(\mathsf{G})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{G}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{G})}\right],

(21)

where the expectation is taken w.r.t.
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
that is uniformly distributed over
{\cal K}_{n,k_{\mathsf{R},k_{\mathsf{L}}}}
, and
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{G}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})
denote the conditional distribution of
\mathsf{G}
given that
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
is the planted bipartite graph under
{\cal H}_{1}
. Introducing an independent copy
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}^{\prime}
of
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
, the squared likelihood ratio can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{2}_{n}(\mathsf{G})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}^{\prime}}\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{G}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{G}|\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}(\mathsf{G})}\right],

(22)

where
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}^{\prime}
denotes that
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
and
\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
are independent. Interchanging the expectations yields,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}^{\prime}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{G}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{G}|\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}(\mathsf{G})}\right)\right].

(23)

Let
\mathsf{A}
denote the adjacency matrix of
\mathsf{G}
, and for any pair
(i,j)\in\binom{\left[n\right]}{2}
, we define
f(\mathsf{A}_{ij})\triangleq\left(p/q\right)^{\mathsf{A}_{ij}}\left[(1-p)/(1-q)\right]^{\mathsf{A}_{ij}}
. Then, note that
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[f(\mathsf{A}_{ij})]=1
and
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[f^{2}(\mathsf{A}_{ij})]=1+\chi^{2}(p||q)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}\left(\mathsf{G}\right)]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}^{\prime}}\left[\prod_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\cap\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[f^{2}\left(\mathsf{A}_{ij}\right)\right]\cdot\prod_{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\triangle\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[f\left(\mathsf{A}_{ij}\right)\right]\right]

(24)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}^{\prime}}\left[\left(1+\chi^{2}(p||q)\right)^{|E\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\cap\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)|}\right]

(25)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}^{\prime}}{\left[\exp\left(\chi^{2}(p||q)\cdot{|E\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\cap\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)|}\right)\right]},

(26)

where in the inequality follows form the fact that
1+x\leq\exp(x)
, for
x\geq 0
. Note that (
26
) is simply the moment generating function of the intersection between two random copies of
{\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
. Our main observation here is the fact that

\displaystyle|E\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\cap\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)|=|{\cal R}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|+|{\cal R}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|,

(27)

which follows directly from the structure of bipartite graphs. Then, by Cauchy–Schwartz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{2}{\left[e^{\chi^{2}(p||q)|E\left({\mathsf{K}}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\cap\mathsf{K}^{\prime}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right)|}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{2}{\left[e^{\chi^{2}(p||q)\left(|{\cal R}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|+|{\cal R}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|\right)}\right]}

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)|{\cal R}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|}\right]}\cdot\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)|{\cal R}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|}\right]}.

(29)

We next bound the first term at the right-hand-side of (
29
). Since
{\cal R}
and
{\cal R}^{\prime}
are drawn uniformly at random, given
H\triangleq|{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|
, we have,

\displaystyle|{\cal R}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|\preceq\mathsf{Hypergeometric}\left(n-H,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{R}}\right),

(30)

where
X\preceq Y
if the random variable
X
is stochastically dominated by
Y
. Now it is well-known that (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Lemma 5]
)
\mathsf{Hypergeometric}\left(n-H,k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{R}}\right)\preceq\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n-H}\right)
. Since we assume that
k_{\mathsf{R}}+k_{\mathsf{L}}=o\left(n\right)
, and as so
k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}\leq n/2
, which in turn implies that
H\leq k_{\mathsf{L}}\leq n/2
, we further have that
\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n-H}\right)\preceq\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)
. Therefore, by the law of total probability we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)|{\cal R}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}{\left[\mathbb{E}{\left.\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)|{\cal R}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|}\right||{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|=H\right]}\right]}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|}\right]}

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)\cdot\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{L}},\frac{2k_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)}\right]}

(33)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}\cdot\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right]},

(34)

where
\mathsf{B}\triangleq\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)
and
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\triangleq\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{L}},\cdot\frac{2k_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)
. In the same way, the second term at the right-hand-side of (
29
) can be bounded as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)|{\cal R}\cap{\cal L}^{\prime}|\cdot|{\cal L}\cap{\cal R}^{\prime}|}\right]}\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\bar{\mathsf{B}}\cdot\bar{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}}\right]},

(35)

where
\bar{\mathsf{B}}\triangleq\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{R}},\frac{2k_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)
and
\bar{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\triangleq\mathsf{Binomial}\left(k_{\mathsf{L}},\frac{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)
. Our goal is now to find conditions under which each one of the expectation terms in (
29
) is upper bounded by, for example, 2. To that end, we will use the upper bounds we derived in (
34
) and (
35
). These in turn will imply that (
26
), and as so, the second moment, is upper bounded by 2 as well, which due to (
20
) entail the impossibility of strong detection.

We start by upper bounding (
34
) using two different approaches. The first approach will lead to the condition in (
5b
), while the second approach will lead to the condition in (
5c
). Accordingly, under
either
one of those conditions (
34
) will be bounded by 2. Let us start with the first approach. Using the fact that
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\leq k_{\mathsf{L}}
with probability one, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}\cdot\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}\cdot k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right]}

(36)

\displaystyle={\left[1+2\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\left(e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)k_{\mathsf{L}}}-1\right)\right]}^{k_{\mathsf{R}}},

(37)

where we have used the moment generating function of the binomial random variable
\mathsf{B}
. Now, since
2^{\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}}-1\geq\frac{\log{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}
, the right-hand-side of (
37
) is upper bounded by 2 if

\displaystyle 2\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\left(e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)k_{\mathsf{L}}}-1\right)\leq\frac{\log{2}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}},

(38)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{L}}}\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}}\frac{\log{2}}{2}\right)}.

(39)

If we repeat the same arguments as above but use the bound
\mathsf{B}\leq k_{\mathsf{R}}
in (
36
) instead, we will get that if

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}}\frac{\log{2}}{2}\right)},

(40)

then the right-hand-side of (
34
) is upper bounded by 2. Combining both conditions we get,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{L}}}\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}}\frac{\log{2}}{2}\right)},\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{R}}}\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}}\frac{\log{2}}{2}\right)}\right\},

(41)

which is exactly the condition in (
5b
).

Next, we propose another approach for bounding (
34
). Using the moment generating function of the binomial random variable
\mathsf{B}
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}\cdot\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}{\left[\left(1+2\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\left(e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}-1\right)\right)^{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right]}.

(42)

We assume that
2\chi^{2}(p||q)k_{\mathsf{L}}\leq 1
, and thus, combined with the fact that
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\leq k_{\mathsf{L}}
with probability one, we have
2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\leq 1
with probability one. Therefore, using the fact that
\exp(x)-1\leq x+x^{2}
, for
x<1.79
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[\left(1+2\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\left(e^{2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}-1\right)\right)^{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[\left(1+2\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\left(2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}+\left(2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right)\right)^{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right]}

(43)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[\left(1+8\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\right)^{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right]}

(44)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\leq\mathbb{E}{\left[\exp\left(8\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}{n}\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\right)\right]}

(45)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt=\left[1+2\frac{k_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\left(e^{8\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}{n}\chi^{2}(p||q)}-1\right)\right]^{k_{\mathsf{L}}},

(46)

where the second inequality is because
2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\leq 1
, and so
\left(2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\leq 2\chi^{2}(p||q)\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
, with probability one, the third inequality is due to the fact that
1+x\leq\exp(x)
, for any
x\geq 0
, and in the last equality we have used the moment generating function of the binomial random variable
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
. As in the previous subsection, using the inequality
2^{\frac{1}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}}-1\geq\frac{\log{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}
, it can be seen that (
46
), as as so (
34
) as well, is upper bounded by 2, if

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\frac{n}{8k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\cdot{\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\frac{\log 2}{2}\right)}}.

(47)

Combined with our assumption that
2\chi^{2}(p||q)k_{\mathsf{L}}\leq 1
, we obtain that (
34
) is upper bounded by 2 if,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{L}}},\frac{n}{8k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\cdot{\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\frac{\log 2}{2}\right)}}\right\}.

(48)

If we repeat the same arguments as above, but replace the roles of
\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
, and assume that
2\chi^{2}(p||q)k_{\mathsf{R}}\leq 1
instead, we will get that (
34
) is upper bounded by 2, if

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{2k_{\mathsf{R}}},\frac{n}{8k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}\cdot{\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\frac{\log 2}{2}\right)}}\right\},

(49)

and so when combined, (
34
) is upper bounded by 2 if either (
48
) or (
49
) hold, which is exactly the condition stated in (
5c
).

Finally, we upper bound (
35
). Repeating the exact same arguments as in the later approach above, it can be shown that the right-hand-side of (
35
) is upper bounded by 2 if,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)\leq\frac{n}{8k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}}\cdot{\log{\left(1+\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}}\frac{\log 2}{2}\right)}},

(50)

which is the condition we stated in (
5a
). This concludes the proof.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
2

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
2
by upper bounding the risk associated with each of the three tests proposed in Section
3
, starting with the scan test.

4.2.1
Scan test

Under the null hypothesis, for any
({\cal R}^{\prime},{\cal L}^{\prime})\in{\cal S}_{k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}}
we have that
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in{\cal R}^{\prime},j\in{\cal L}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{A}_{ij}\sim\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}},q\right)}
. Recall the definition of
\mathsf{Scan}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}
in (
6
), and note that the maximum in (
6
) taken over a set of size
|{\cal S}_{k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}}|=\binom{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\binom{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}
, which can be upper bounded as follows,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\binom{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}\leq\binom{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\binom{n}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}\leq\left(\frac{en}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)^{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\left(\frac{en}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)^{k_{\mathsf{L}}}\leq\left(\frac{en}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)^{2k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}},

(51)

where the second inequality follows from
(y/x)^{x}\leq\binom{y}{x}\leq(ey/x)^{x}
, and the last inequality is because
(n/a)^{a}
is monotonically non-decreasing as a function of
a
if
a=o\left(n\right)
. Thus, by the union bound and Bernstein’s inequality we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left(\phi_{\mathsf{Scan}}(\mathsf{A})=1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}{\left(\mathsf{Scan}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{Scan}}\right)}

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{({\cal R}^{\prime},{\cal L}^{\prime})\in{\cal S}_{k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}}}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}{\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in{\cal R}^{\prime},j\in{\cal L}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{A}_{ij}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{Scan}}\right]}}

(53)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{en}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)^{2k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\cdot\exp{\left(-\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}/4}{2k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}q+k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}/3}\right)}

(54)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot\log{\left(\frac{en}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}-C_{1}\cdot k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(55)

where
C_{1}>0
, and the last inequality follows from the assumptions that
|p-q|=O(q)
and
q<c<1
, for some
0<c<1
. Under the alternative hypothesis, if
{\mathsf{K}}^{\star}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
denotes the underlying planted bipartite subgraph, then there exist right and left sets of vertices
({\cal R}^{\ast},{\cal L}^{\ast})
such that
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in{\cal R}^{\ast},j\in{\cal L}^{\ast}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{A}_{ij}\sim\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}},p\right)}
. Therefore, by the multiplicative Chernoff’s bound, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left(\phi_{\mathsf{Scan}}(\mathsf{A})=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}{\left(\mathsf{Scan}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\leq\tau_{\mathsf{Scan}}\right)}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}{\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in{\cal R}^{\ast},j\in{\cal L}^{\ast}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{A}_{ij}\leq\tau_{\mathsf{Scan}}\right]}

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}/4}{2k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}p}\right)}

(58)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-C_{2}\cdot k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(59)

for some constant
C_{2}>0
, and again the last inequality follows from the assumptions that
|p-q|=O(q)
and
q<c<1
. Thus, from (
55
) and (
59
), it is clear that there exists a constant
C_{3}>0
such that,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Scan}})\leq 2\cdot\exp{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot\log{\left(\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}-C_{3}\cdot k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(60)

which is less than
\delta
provided that,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}

\displaystyle\geq C{\left[\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}}\cdot\log{\left(\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}+\frac{\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right]}

(61)

\displaystyle=C{\left[\frac{\log{\left(\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right)}+\frac{\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}}{k_{\mathsf{R}}\wedge k_{\mathsf{L}}}\right]},

(62)

for some
C>0
, which completes the proof.

4.2.2
Count test

Recall the count statistics in (
7
). Under the null hypothesis, it is clear that
\mathsf{Count}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\sim\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(\binom{n}{2},q\right)}
. Therefore, by Bernstein’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left(\phi_{\mathsf{Count}}(\mathsf{A})=1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}{\left(\mathsf{Count}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{Count}}\right)}

(63)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}/4}{2{\binom{n}{2}}q+k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}/3}\right)}

(64)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-C_{1}\cdot\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(65)

where
C_{1}>0
, and the last inequality follows from the assumptions that
|p-q|=O(q)
and
q<c<1
, for some
0<c<1
. Under the alternative hypothesis, the random variable
\mathsf{Count}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}
is distributed as the independent sum of
\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}},p\right)}
and
\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(\binom{n}{2}-k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}},q\right)}
. Therefore, by the multiplicative Chernoff’s bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left(\phi_{\mathsf{Count}}(\mathsf{A})=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}{\left(\mathsf{Count}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\leq\tau_{\mathsf{Count}}\right)}

(66)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}/4}{2{\binom{n}{2}}q+2\cdot k_{\mathsf{R}}k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}}\right)}

(67)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-C_{2}\cdot\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(68)

where
C_{2}>0
. Thus, combining (
65
) and (
68
), we obtain that there exists a constant
C_{3}>0
, such that,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Count}})\leq 2\cdot\exp{\left(-C_{3}\cdot\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)}.

(69)

This is less than
\delta
provided that,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{C_{3}\cdot k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\cdot\log{\frac{2}{\delta}},

(70)

which completes the proof.

4.2.3
Degree test

Recall the degree statistics in (
8
). Under the null hypothesis, we clearly have that
\sum_{j}\mathsf{A}_{ij}\sim\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(n-1,q\right)}
. Therefore, by the union bound and Bernstein’s inequality, we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left(\phi_{\mathsf{Deg}}(\mathsf{A})=1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}{\left(\mathsf{MaxDeg}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{Deg}}\right)}

(71)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\exp{\left(-\frac{{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\right)}^{2}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}/4}{2\left(n-1\right)q+k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}/3}\right)}

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(\log{n}-C_{1}\cdot\frac{{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\right)}^{2}}{n}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(73)

where
C_{1}>0
, and the last inequality follows from the assumptions that
|p-q|=O(q)
and
q<c<1
, for some
0<c<1
. Under the alternative hypothesis, there is at least one row
i^{\ast}
such that
\sum_{j}\mathsf{A}_{i^{\ast}j}
can be represented as the independent sum of
\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}},p\right)}
and
\mathsf{Binomial}{\left(n-1-k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}},q\right)}
. Therefore, by the multiplicative Chernoff’s bound, we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left(\phi_{\mathsf{Deg}}(\mathsf{A})=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}{\left(\mathsf{MaxDeg}{\left(\mathsf{A}\right)}\leq\tau_{\mathsf{Deg}}\right)}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\right)}^{2}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}/4}{2\left(n-1\right)q+2\cdot k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\cdot{\left(p-q\right)}}\right)}

(75)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(-C_{2}\cdot\frac{{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\right)}^{2}}{n}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(76)

for some constant
C_{2}>0
. Thus, combining (
73
) and (
76
), we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}(\phi_{\mathsf{Deg}})\leq 2\cdot\exp{\left(\log{n}-C\cdot\frac{{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\right)}^{2}}{n}\cdot\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\right)},

(77)

for some constant
C>0
. This is less than
\delta
provided that,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(p||q)=\frac{{\left(p-q\right)}^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\geq\frac{n}{C\cdot{\left(k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\right)}^{2}}\cdot\left(\log n+\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}\right),

(78)

which completes the proof.

4.3
Proof of Theorem
3

Let us expand the likelihood ratio
\mathsf{L}_{n}
in a basis of orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
. Specifically, suppose that
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}:\Omega^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is an orthonormal basis for the coordinate-degree
\mathsf{D}
functions (with respect to
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}
), and that
f_{0}
is the unit constant function. Therefore,
\left\langle f_{i},f_{j}\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}=\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}=0
if
i\neq j
, and
\delta_{ii}=1
. Then, measuring the norm of
\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}
in this basis, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\left\langle f_{i},\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\rangle_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

(79)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\mathsf{L}_{n}(\mathsf{G})f_{i}(\mathsf{G})\right]\right]^{2}

(80)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}}f_{i}(\mathsf{G})\right]^{2},

(81)

where we have used the fact that
(\mathsf{L}_{n}-\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}})
is orthogonal to
\{f_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}
. Therefore, we need to compute
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}}f_{i}(\mathsf{G})
for some orthonormal basis functions
f_{i}
. In our setting, for
\alpha\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
, define the Fourier character

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}(\mathsf{G})=\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\alpha}\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}},

(82)

for each
\mathsf{G}\in\{0,1\}^{\binom{n}{2}}
. Note that any subset
\alpha\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
can be identified with the graph on vertex set
[n]
induced by edges in
\alpha
. Therefore, we can say “graph
\alpha
” without ambiguity. Then,
|\alpha|
denotes the number of edges in the graph
\alpha
. Also, with some abuse of notation, we let
V(\alpha)
denote the vertex set of
\alpha
, i.e., the set of vertices
v\in[n]
that are non-isolated by the edges of
\alpha
, and finally we let
v(\alpha)
denote its size, namely,
v(\alpha)=|V(\alpha)|
. It is known
[
65
]
that
\{\chi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2},|\alpha|\leq\mathsf{D}}
form an orthonormal basis for the degree-
\mathsf{D}
functions with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
. In light of (
81
), we next compute
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\chi_{\alpha}(\mathsf{G})
for each such
\alpha
. We have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\chi_{\alpha}(\mathsf{G})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\chi_{\alpha}(\mathsf{G})

(83)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left(\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\alpha}\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}\right)

(84)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}\right),

(85)

where we have used the fact that conditioned on the planted structure
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
, the edges of
\mathsf{G}
become independent. Depending on the edge
\{i,j\}
and its relation to
\alpha
and
\mathsf{G}
, there are two possible cases:

•

If
\{i,j\}\in\alpha
is such that
i,j\not\in V(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}\right|i,j\not\in V(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})\right]=0,

(86)

since if
i
or
j
is not in
\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}
, then the edge
\{i,j\}
is included in
\mathsf{G}
with probability
q
.

•

If
\{i,j\}\in\alpha
is such that
i,j\in V(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})
and
\{i,j\}\in E(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}\right|i,j\in V(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}),\{i,j\}\in E(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})\right]

\displaystyle=p\sqrt{\frac{1-q}{q}}-(1-p)\sqrt{\frac{q}{1-q}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\chi^{2}(p||q)}.

(87)

•

If
\{i,j\}\in\alpha
is such that
i,j\in V(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})
and
\{i,j\}\not\in E(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}\right|i,j\in V(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}),\{i,j\}\not\in E(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})\right]=0.

(88)

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}\right)=\sqrt{\chi^{2}(p||q)}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{\{i,j\}\in\alpha\cap E(\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}})\right\}.

(89)

This in turn implies that

\displaystyle\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{G}_{ij}-q}{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}\right)=(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha\cap\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}|}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right\}.

(90)

Combining the above we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\chi_{\alpha}(\mathsf{G})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left[(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha\cap{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}|}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right\}\right]

(91)

\displaystyle=[\chi^{2}(p||q)]^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left[\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right].

(92)

Therefore, from (
81
) we get that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}=1+\sum_{0<|\alpha|\leq\mathsf{D}}[\chi^{2}(p||q)]^{|\alpha|}\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left[\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right],

(93)

where the summand
1
correspond to the empty graph
\alpha=\emptyset
. Now, the main observation here is that if
\alpha
is
not
a bipartite graph itself, then
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left[\alpha\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right]=0
. This follows from the fact that any subgraph of a bipartite graph is, itself, bipartite. This can be easily seen as follows: it is well-known that a graph is bipartite if and only if it does not contain a cycle of odd length. Since the subgraph of a bipartite graph could not gain a cycle of odd length, it must also be bipartite. Therefore, the summation in (
93
) over
\alpha
can be reduced to bipartite graphs only. Accordingly, let
{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}
denote the set of all possible bipartite subgraphs in the complete graph over
n
vertices, with at most
\mathsf{D}
edges, and at least a single edge (because
|\alpha|>0
). For clarity, we will use the notation
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}
in place of
\alpha
, and we let
v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})
and
v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})
denote the number of left and right vertices of
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}=1+\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}}[\chi^{2}(p||q)]^{|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|}\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left[\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right].

(94)

We next evaluate the summation in (
94
), separately for the dense and sparse regimes, starting with the former. We would like to mention here that, in fact, our analysis for the sparse regime can be generalized so that it will include the dense regime as a special case. Nonetheless, we opted to present the analysis of both cases, as the bounding techniques we use for the dense case are simpler, and might be of use in other related problems.

4.3.1
Dense regime

Consider the case where
p,q=\Theta(1)
, for which
\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Theta(1)
. Our goal is to prove that if
k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\ll\sqrt{n}
then
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}
is bounded. For simplicity of notation, we denote
\lambda\triangleq\chi^{2}(p||q)
, and without loss of generality we assume that
k_{\mathsf{R}}\geq k_{\mathsf{L}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\subseteq{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{n-v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}{k_{\mathsf{R}}-v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\binom{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}-v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}{k_{\mathsf{L}}-v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}}{\binom{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}}\binom{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}}}

(95)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\binom{k_{\mathsf{L}}}{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}}{\binom{n}{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\binom{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}}

(96)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{L}}}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}

(97)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}

(98)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})+v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\left(\frac{n}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}

(99)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{v(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\exp\left(v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})\cdot\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right),

(100)

where the first inequality follows from
(y/x)^{x}\leq\binom{y}{x}\leq(ey/x)^{x}
, the second inequality is because
k_{\mathsf{R}}\geq k_{\mathsf{L}}
, and in the last inequality we use
1+x\leq\exp(x)
, for
x\geq 0
, and
v(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})=|V(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})|
is the total number of non-isolated vertices in
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}
. Now, notice that for any
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}
, we must have
v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})\leq\mathsf{D}
. Thus, in the regime where
k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\ll\sqrt{n}
, since
\mathsf{D}=n^{o(1)}
, we have
\exp\left(v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})\cdot\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)=1+o(1)
, for sufficiently large
n
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\subseteq{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\right)\leq(1+o(1))\cdot\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{v(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\triangleq g(|V(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})|).

(101)

Thus, using (
94
) we get

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}}\lambda^{|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|}g^{2}(|V(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})|).

(102)

It is convenient to analyze the cases
\lambda>1
and
\lambda\leq 1
separately, and we start with the former. For any set
{\cal V}\subseteq[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}:V(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})={\cal V}}\lambda^{|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|}g^{2}(|V(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})|)

\displaystyle=g^{2}(|{\cal V}|)\cdot\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}:\;v(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})={\cal V}}\lambda^{|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|}

(103)

\displaystyle\leq g^{2}(|{\cal V}|)\cdot\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}:\;\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\subseteq\binom{{\cal V}}{2}}\lambda^{|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|}

(104)

\displaystyle=g^{2}(|{\cal V}|)\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{\min\left(\mathsf{D},\binom{|{\cal V}|}{2}\right)}\binom{\binom{|{\cal V}|}{2}}{\ell}\lambda^{\ell}

(105)

\displaystyle\leq g^{2}(|{\cal V}|)\cdot\sum_{\ell=1}^{\min\left(\mathsf{D},\binom{|{\cal V}|}{2}\right)}\left[\binom{|{\cal V}|}{2}\lambda\right]^{\ell}

(106)

\displaystyle\leq g^{2}(|{\cal V}|)\left(1+\binom{|{\cal V}|}{2}\lambda\right)^{\min\left(\mathsf{D},\binom{|{\cal V}|}{2}\right)},

(107)

where in the last inequality we have used the fact that
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{n}{i}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}n^{i}1^{k-i}\leq(1+n)^{k}
. Next, every
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}
with
|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|\leq\mathsf{D}
has
|v(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})|\leq 2\mathsf{D}
. Also, there are
\binom{n}{t}\leq n^{t}
sets
{\cal V}\subseteq[n]
sets
{\cal V}
such that
|{\cal V}|=t
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+(1+o(1))\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{2\mathsf{D}}(1+t)^{2}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{2t}n^{t}(1+t^{2}\lambda/2)^{\min\left(\mathsf{D},t^{2}/2\right)}

(108)

\displaystyle\leq 1+(1+o(1))\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{2\mathsf{D}}(1+t)^{2}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{t}\left[t\sqrt{\lambda}\right]^{2\min\left(\mathsf{D},t^{2}/2\right)}

(109)

\displaystyle=1+(1+o(1))\cdot\sum_{t\leq\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}(1+t)^{2}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{t}\left[t\sqrt{\lambda}\right]^{t^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+(1+o(1))\cdot\sum_{\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}<t<2\mathsf{D}}(1+t)^{2}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{t}\left[t\sqrt{\lambda}\right]^{2\mathsf{D}},

(110)

where the second inequality follows from the fact that
\lambda>1
and thus
t^{2}\lambda>2
. Denote the summand in the first series at the right-hand-side of (
110
) by
\mathsf{T}_{t}
. Then, applying the ratio test on the first term we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{T}_{t+1}}{\mathsf{T}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2+t)^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}}\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\frac{(t+1)^{(t+1)^{2}}}{t^{t^{2}}}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+t}

(111)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2+t)^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}}\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}e^{t}(1+t)^{1+2t}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+t}

(112)

\displaystyle\leq(2+t)^{1+2t}\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}e^{t}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+t}

(113)

\displaystyle\leq(2+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}})^{1+2\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}e^{\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}},

(114)

where in the first inequality we have used the fact that
(1+x)\leq\exp(x)
, for
x\geq 0
, the second inequality is because
t\geq 2
, and finally, the last inequality is because
t\leq\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}
and the fact that (
113
) is monotonically increasing in
t
. Thus, if,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}{n}e^{\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}(2+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}})^{2\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}+1}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}<c<1,

(115)

for some
0<c<1
, then the series at the right-hand-side of (
110
) is upper bounded by a constant. Note that for any
\mathsf{D}\leq C\log n
, and any
C>0
, we have
e^{\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}(2+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}})^{2\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}+1}\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}=n^{o(1)}
, and thus this factor in (
115
) do not contribute to the polynomial scale of the term in the left-hand-side of (
115
). Accordingly, it is easy to check that (
115
) holds if
k_{\mathsf{R}}\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}
, for any
\epsilon>0
. Next, in the same way, we denote the summand in the second term at the right-hand-side of (
110
) by
\mathsf{T}_{t}^{\prime}
. Then, applying the ratio test on the second term we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{T}^{\prime}_{t+1}}{\mathsf{T}^{\prime}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2+t)^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}}\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\frac{(1+t)^{2\mathsf{D}}}{t^{2\mathsf{D}}}

(116)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2+t)^{2}}{(1+t)^{2}}\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}e^{\frac{2\mathsf{D}}{t}}

(117)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}\frac{(2+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}})^{2}}{(1+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}})^{2}}\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}.

(118)

Again, it is easy to check that since
\mathsf{D}=O(\log n)
then
e^{\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}}\frac{(2+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}})^{2}}{(1+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}})^{2}}=n^{o(1)}
, and thus if
k_{\mathsf{R}}\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}
, for any
\epsilon>0
, then (
118
) is strictly less than one, which implies that the second series at the right-hand-side of (
110
) is upper bounded by a constant. Therefore, for (
110
) to be bounded as
n\to\infty
we need
k_{\mathsf{R}}\ll\sqrt{n}
, as claimed. Recall the fact that we have assumed that
k_{\mathsf{R}}\geq k_{\mathsf{L}}
. If reversed, then we will get by symmetry that (
110
) is bounded as
n\to\infty
if
k_{\mathsf{L}}\ll\sqrt{n}
; therefore, in general
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}
is bounded if
k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\ll\sqrt{n}
. Finally, we discuss the case where
\lambda\leq 1
. In this case, we further bound
\lambda^{|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|}\leq 1
, for any
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}
, and then (
102
) becomes,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}}g^{2}(|V(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})|)

(119)

\displaystyle\leq 1+(1+o(1))\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{2\mathsf{D}}(1+t)^{2}\left(\frac{e\cdot k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)^{2t}n^{t}.

(120)

Using the same arguments as above, we get from the ratio test that
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}
is bounded under the same condition, namely,
k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}\ll\sqrt{n}
.

4.3.2
Sparse regime

Next, we analyze the sparse regime where
p,q=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
, for
\alpha\in(0,2]
. Without loss of generality we assume that
k_{\mathsf{R}}\geq k_{\mathsf{L}}
. Also, recall that
k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}=o(n)
, and thus
k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}\leq n/2
. Finally, we may assume that
\chi^{2}(p||q)<\frac{1}{2\mathsf{D}^{2}}
; indeed, in the sparse regime we have
\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Theta(n^{-\alpha})
, and thus clearly
2\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q)<1
for
n
sufficiently large, and any
\mathsf{D}=n^{o(1)}
.

Using (
94
) and (
97
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}\leq 1+\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}}[\chi^{2}(p||q)]^{|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{2v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{L}}}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)^{2v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}.

(121)

Let
{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}
denote the set of all edge-induced bipartite subgraphs of the complete graph
\mathsf{K}_{n}
on
n
vertices, having
\ell_{1}
right vertices,
\ell_{2}
left vertices, and
m
edges. Formally,

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}\triangleq\left\{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{n}:v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})=\ell_{1},\;v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})=\ell_{2},\;|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|=m\right\}.

(122)

Note that as the graphs in
{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}
are edge induced and we only consider
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}
such that
|\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}|\leq\mathsf{D}
, we have

\displaystyle 1\leq\ell_{1}\leq\mathsf{D},\;1\leq\ell_{2}\leq\mathsf{D},\;\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}\leq m\leq\mathsf{D}.

(123)

Now, using the fact that

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}=\bigcup_{1\leq\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\leq\mathsf{D}}\bigcup_{\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}\leq m\leq\mathsf{D}}{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m},

(124)

we get

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\sum_{m=\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}^{\mathsf{D}}\sum_{\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{m}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{2\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{L}}}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)^{2\ell_{2}}

(125)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\sum_{m=\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}^{\mathsf{D}}|{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}|\cdot(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{m}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{2\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{ek_{\mathsf{L}}}{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}\right)^{2\ell_{2}}.

(126)

Let us upper bound
|{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}|
. To that end, we first enumerate the possible choices for the right and left vertices among the
n
possible vertices, and then the
m
edges among the
\ell_{1}\cdot\ell_{2}
possible edges. Specifically,

\displaystyle|{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}|

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{\ell_{1}}\binom{n-\ell_{1}}{\ell_{2}}\binom{\ell_{1}\cdot\ell_{2}}{m}

(127)

\displaystyle\leq n^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}}(\ell_{1}\cdot\ell_{2})^{m}

(128)

\displaystyle\leq n^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}}\mathsf{D}^{2m}.

(129)

Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\sum_{m=\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}^{\mathsf{D}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{m}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}n}{(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}

(130)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{2}}\sum_{m=\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}^{\mathsf{D}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{m}

(131)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{2}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}

(132)

\displaystyle\triangleq 1+2\cdot({\cal W}_{1}+{\cal W}_{2}+{\cal W}_{3}),

(133)

where in the second inequality we have used the fact that
k_{\mathsf{R}}\leq n/2
, and the third inequality follows from the inequality
\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\beta^{i}=\frac{\beta^{j}}{1-\beta}\leq 2\beta^{j}
, for
\beta<1/2
, along with the fact that
\chi^{2}(p||q)<\frac{1}{2\mathsf{D}^{2}}
, and finally in (
133
) we defined,

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{4e^{4}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n^{2}}\right)^{\ell}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell},

(134)

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{2}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}},

(135)

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{3}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{\ell_{2}>\ell_{1}}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{2}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{2}}.

(136)

Next, we find the conditions under which
{\cal W}_{1}
,
{\cal W}_{2}
, and
{\cal W}_{3}
are all bounded simultaneously, which by using (
133
) will imply that
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}
is bounded as well under the same conditions. Starting with
{\cal W}_{1}
, the geometric series in (
134
) is bounded provided that
\frac{4\mathsf{D}^{2}e^{4}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n^{2}}\chi^{2}(p||q)<c<1
, for some constant
c
. This is clearly the case when
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}
. Next, we analyze
{\cal W}_{2}
. We have,

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{2}=\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{2}}.

(137)

We consider two cases. If
\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}<1
, then we use the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{j}\beta^{i}\leq j\cdot\beta
, for
\beta<1
, and get,

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}\ell_{1}\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}

(138)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{D}\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}.

(139)

Accordingly, the geometric series in (
139
) is bounded provided that
\frac{\mathsf{D}^{2}e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\chi^{2}(p||q)<c<1
, for some constant
c
. This holds when
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}
. Conversely, if
\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\geq 1
, then we use the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{j}\beta^{i}\leq j\cdot\beta^{j}
, for
\beta\geq 1
, and get,

\displaystyle{\cal W}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}\ell_{1}\left(\frac{4e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\right)^{\ell_{1}}

(140)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{D}\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}\left(\frac{4e^{4}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n^{2}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}(\mathsf{D}^{2}\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}.

(141)

As for
{\cal W}_{1}
, the series in (
141
) is bounded if
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}
. Finally, we analyze
{\cal W}_{3}
exactly as we analyzed
{\cal W}_{2}
. We get that if
\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}<1
then
{\cal W}_{3}
is bounded provided that
\frac{4\mathsf{D}^{2}e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}\chi^{2}(p||q)<c<1
, for some constant
c
. This is clearly the case when
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}
; since we assume that
k_{\mathsf{R}}\geq k_{\mathsf{L}}
then this condition is redundant because
\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}<1
. Conversely, if
\frac{e^{2}k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{n}\geq 1
, we get that
{\cal W}_{3}
is bounded provided that
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}
. Again, since
k_{\mathsf{R}}\geq k_{\mathsf{L}}
then this condition is less stringent compared to the conditions we got for
{\cal W}_{2}
. Combining the above, we get that
{\cal W}_{1}
,
{\cal W}_{2}
, and
{\cal W}_{3}
, are all bounded when:

1.

If
k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\ll n
, then we need
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}
and
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}
, and since the later dominates, the intersection reduces to
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}
.

2.

If
k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\gg n
, then we need
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}
.

Notice that when the above two cases are combined we get the condition
\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}
, and if we remove the assumption that
k_{\mathsf{R}}\geq k_{\mathsf{L}}
, we obtain

\chi^{2}(p||q)\ll\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}
. This concludes the proof.

4.4
Converse

In this subsection, we prove the converse part of Theorem
3
, namely, that in the dense and sparse regimes if
k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\gg n
and
\chi^{2}(p||q)\gg\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}
then
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}\geq\omega(1)
, respectively. Recall that we assume that
k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}=o(n)
, and thus
k_{\mathsf{R}},k_{\mathsf{L}}\leq n/2
. Next, we lower bound
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}
in (
94
). Using (
96
) and the inequality
(y/x)^{x}\leq\binom{y}{x}\leq(ey/x)^{x}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}}\left[\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\subseteq\mathsf{K}_{{\cal R},{\cal L}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\binom{k_{\mathsf{L}}}{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}}{\binom{n}{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\binom{n-k_{\mathsf{R}}}{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}}

(142)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{en}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{R}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})}\left(\frac{k_{\mathsf{L}}}{e(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})}\right)^{v_{\mathsf{L}}(\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}})},

(143)

for any
\alpha_{\mathsf{Bip}}\in{\cal B}_{\mathsf{D}}
. Then, using similar arguments that lead to (
133
), we get

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}|{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}|\cdot(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{m}\left(\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{en}\right)^{2\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k_{\mathsf{L}}}{e(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})}\right)^{2\ell_{2}}

(144)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}\left(\frac{n}{e\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{n-\ell_{1}}{e\ell_{2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{m}\left(\frac{k_{\mathsf{R}}}{en}\right)^{2\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k_{\mathsf{L}}}{e(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})}\right)^{2\ell_{2}}

(145)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\geq 1}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}(n-\ell_{1})}{e^{3}(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}\ell_{2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}

(146)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\cal W}_{1}+\tilde{\cal W}_{2}+\tilde{\cal W}_{3}

(147)

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{\cal W}_{1}\vee\tilde{\cal W}_{2}\vee\tilde{\cal W}_{3},

(148)

where in the second inequality have used the fact that
|{\cal B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m}|\geq\binom{n}{\ell_{1}}\binom{n-\ell_{1}}{\ell_{2}}\geq\left(\frac{n}{e\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{n-\ell_{1}}{e\ell_{2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}
, for any
m
, and we defined,

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal W}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathsf{D}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n\ell}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}(n-\ell)}{e^{3}(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}\ell}\right)^{\ell},

(149)

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal W}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{1}>\ell_{2}}^{\mathsf{D}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}(n-\ell_{1})}{e^{3}(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}\ell_{2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}},

(150)

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal W}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{2}>\ell_{1}}^{\mathsf{D}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{2}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}(n-\ell_{1})}{e^{3}(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}\ell_{2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}.

(151)

To prove the converse, it turns out that it is suffice to find conditions under which either
\tilde{\cal W}_{1}
or
\tilde{\cal W}_{2}
at the right-hand-side of (
148
) diverge, which in turn imply that
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}
diverge as well under the same conditions. Starting with
\tilde{\cal W}_{1}
, by lower bounding
\tilde{\cal W}_{1}
by the first term in the sum (i.e., the one that corresponds to the index
\ell=1)
, we get
\tilde{\cal W}_{1}\geq\chi^{2}(p||q)\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n}\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}(n-1)}{e^{3}(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}}\geq\chi^{2}(p||q)\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n}\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{e^{3}n}
, where the second inequality follows from the fact that
\frac{n-\ell_{1}}{(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{n}
, because
\ell_{1}<n
. Thus, if
\chi^{2}(p||q)\gg\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}
, then
\tilde{\cal W}_{1}=\omega(1)
, which implies
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}=\omega(1)
. Next, we prove that
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}=\omega(1)
also if
\chi^{2}(p||q)\gg\frac{n}{k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}\vee k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}}
, by analyzing
\tilde{\cal W}_{2}
. We have,

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal W}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}(n-\ell_{1})}{e^{3}(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}\ell_{2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}

(152)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}(n-\ell_{1})}{e^{3}(n-k_{\mathsf{R}})^{2}}\right)

(153)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{e^{3}n}\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{\mathsf{D}}(\chi^{2}(p||q))^{\ell_{1}}\left(\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{e^{3}n\ell_{1}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}

(154)

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{e^{3}n}\frac{\zeta\cdot(\zeta^{\mathsf{D}}-1)}{\zeta-1},

(155)

where
\zeta\triangleq\chi^{2}(p||q)\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}{\mathsf{D}e^{3}n}
. We claim that there exist an integer
\mathsf{D}
such that if
\zeta\gg 1
(which is equivalent to
\chi^{2}(p||q)\gg\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}}
) then
\tilde{\cal W}_{2}=\omega(1)
. First, for
\zeta>2^{1/\mathsf{D}}
note that (
155
) is further lower bounded by
\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{2e^{3}n}\zeta^{\mathsf{D}}
. Now, if
k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\gg n
, then clearly (
155
) diverges when
\zeta\gg 1
. If
k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\ll n
, then since the ratio
\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{n}
decreases at most polynomially with
n
, while
\zeta
increases polynomially with
n
, then there must be an integer
\mathsf{D}
such that the product
\frac{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}{2e^{3}n}\zeta^{\mathsf{D}}
diverges. Let
\kappa_{n}
be any sequence such that
\kappa_{n}=\omega(1)
. Then, any
\mathsf{D}
such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}\geq\frac{\log\kappa_{n}}{\log\xi}+\frac{\log\frac{2e^{3}n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}}{\log\xi},

(156)

implies that
\tilde{\cal W}_{2}=\omega(1)
. If
\xi\gg 1
, we have
\xi=\Theta(n^{\epsilon})
, for some
\epsilon>0
. Also, since
k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}\ll n
, then
\frac{2e^{3}n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}=\Theta(n^{\eta})
, from some
\eta>0
. Thus, for any
\kappa_{n}=o(\log n)
, if we take any integer
\mathsf{D}
such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}\geq\frac{\eta}{\epsilon}+o(1),

(157)

then
\tilde{\cal W}_{2}=\omega(1)
. Finally, by replacing the roles of
k_{\mathsf{R}}
and
k_{\mathsf{L}}
we obtain the condition
\chi^{2}(p||q)\gg\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}
, and thus when combined with the sufficient condition we obtained for
\tilde{\cal W}_{1}
, we conclude that
\left\|\mathsf{L}_{n,\leq\mathsf{D}}\right\|_{{\cal H}_{0}}^{2}=\omega(1)
if
\chi^{2}(p||q)\gg\frac{n^{2}}{k_{\mathsf{L}}^{2}k_{\mathsf{R}}^{2}}\wedge\frac{n}{k^{2}_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k^{2}_{\mathsf{L}}}
, as stated.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the problem of detecting a planted bipartite structure in a large random graph. We derived asymptotically tight lower and upper bounds in both the dense and sparse regimes. It turns out that the, seemingly simple, bipartite structure has essentially a rich geometry, which produces a surprising phase diagram for the detection problem, as can be seen in, for example, Figure
2
. Finally, we provided an evidence that in the region of parameters where the inefficient scan test is successful, but the count and degree tests fail, the problem is conjecturally computationally hard, by proving that the class of low-degree polynomials fail in this region. An interesting direction for future research is to consider the more general case where
p=\Theta(n^{-\beta})
and
q=\Theta(n^{-\gamma})
, for
\beta<\gamma
. It seems that while some of our results and techniques apply for this case as well, in order to obtain tight statistical and computational barriers, other techniques will be needed, especially in the regime where
k_{\mathsf{R}}\vee k_{\mathsf{R}}\ll\sqrt{n}
and
\gamma>2\beta
, for which
\chi^{2}(p||q)=\Theta(n^{\gamma-2\beta})\gg 1
. Finally, while in this paper we analyzed the detection problem, it is quite interesting and important to consider the recovery problem as well. Our detection algorithms can be easily converted to a respective recovery algorithm (e.g., the scan test is motivated by the maximum likelihood estimator). For lower bounds, other techniques are needed. For example, for computational lower bounds it will be interesting to use the recent framework of low-degree polynomials for recovery
[
66
]
.
