Before stating the main results of the paper, we need to describe the structure of lattice isometries of spaces of continuous functions.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
T:C_{0}(X)\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)
be a lattice isomorphism, with
X
and

locally compact Hausdorff. Then there exists a homeomorphism
\phi:Y\rightarrow X
and a continuous map

with

, such that for all

and

,

[Tf](y)=a(y)\cdot f(\phi(y)).

Moreover,

and
\phi

Proof.

By
[
10

, for any lattice homomorphism
T:C_{0}(X)\rightarrow C_{0}(Y)
there exist a continuous map
\phi:Y\supset Y^{\prime}\rightarrow X
and a function
a:Y\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, continuous on
Y^{\prime}
and vanishing outside of it, so that
[Tf](y)=a(y)\cdot f(\phi(y))
holds for any
f\in C_{0}(X)
and
y\in Y
.
As
T
is bijective,

coincides with

, and
\phi:Y\to X
is bijective. Further,
T^{*}\delta_{y}=a(y)\delta_{\phi(y)}
for any

. As

is bounded above and below, so must be

.

In a similar fashion, we can write
[T^{-1}g](x)=b(x)g(\psi(x))
, where
b:X\to(0,\infty)
is continuous with
0<\inf b\leq\sup b<\infty
, and
\psi:X\to Y
is a continuous surjection.
It is easy to observe that
T^{*}\delta_{t}=a(t)\delta_{\phi(t)}
for any
t\in Y
, and likewise,
T^{-1*}\delta_{s}=b(s)\delta_{\psi(s)}
for any
s\in X
.
The equality
T^{-1*}T^{*}=I
implies

. Consequently,
\phi^{-1}
is continuous.

Finally, the uniqueness of the representation of

follows from the fact (noted above) that
T^{*}\delta_{y}=a(y)\delta_{\phi(y)}

∎

Topological basics

In this section we analyze families of (locally) equicontinuous mappings on a topological space

From now on, for any lattice isomorphism

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a locally compact metric space. Suppose
g_{n}:C_{0}(X)\to C_{0}(X)
(

) are lattice isomorphisms, with
\sup_{n}\|g_{n}\|<\infty
.
Then
g_{n}\rightarrow g
in the SOT if and only if the following conditions hold:

(1)

\phi_{g_{n}}|_{K}\rightarrow\phi_{g}|_{K}
uniformly, for any compact
K\subset X
.

(2)

a_{g_{n}}|_{K}\rightarrow a_{g}|_{K}
uniformly, for any compact
K\subset X
.

(3)

For any compact
K\subset X
, and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
so that
d\big(\phi_{g_{n}}^{-1}(t),\phi_{g}^{-1}(K)\big)\leq\varepsilon
for any
t\in K
and
n\geq N
.

Moreover, if the family
(\phi^{-1}_{g_{n}})
is locally equicontinuous for any compact
K
, then
(1)
implies
(3)
. Consequently, if
(\phi_{g_{n}}^{-1})
is locally equicontinuous, then
g_{n}\to g
in SOT if and only if
\phi_{g_{n}}\to\phi_{g}
uniformly on compact sets.

For
K\subset X
and
\delta<0
, we use the notation
K_{\delta}
for
\{t\in X:d(t,K)\leq\delta\}
. The following result may be known.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
(X,d)
is locally compact, and
K\subset X
is compact, then
K_{\delta}
is compact for
\delta
small enough.

Proof.

For
t\in X
and
c>0
, we let
U(t,c)=\{s\in X:d(t,s)<c\}
.

For each
t\in K
, find
c_{t}>0
so that
\overline{U(t,c_{t})}
is compact. The sets
U(t,c_{t})
(
t\in K
) form an open cover of
K
, which has a finite subcover
(U(t_{i},c_{t_{i}}))_{i=1}^{N}
. Then
S=\cup_{i=1}^{N}\overline{U(t_{i},c_{t_{i}})}
is compact. We claim that there exists
\delta>0
so that
K_{\delta}\subset S
. Indeed, otherwise for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
u_{n}\in K
and
v_{n}\in X\backslash S
so that
d(u_{n},v_{n})<1/n
. By passing to a subsequence, we can assume that
u_{n}\to u\in K
; consequently,
v_{n}\to u
as well.

Find
i
so that
u\in U(t_{i},c_{t_{i}})
. As the latter set is open, we conclude that
v_{n}\in U(t_{i},c_{t_{i}})
, for

v_{n}\notin S\supset U(t_{i},c_{t_{i}})
, providing us with the desired contradiction.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Suppose

in SOT.
First show that (1) holds. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that for some compact
K
, there is
\varepsilon>0
and a sequence
(k_{n})\subseteq K

d(\phi_{g_{n}}(k_{n}),\phi_{g}(k_{n}))>2\varepsilon

.
Now

d(\phi_{g_{n}}(k_{n}),\phi_{g}(k_{n}))\leq d(\phi_{g_{n}}(k_{n}),\phi_{g}(k))+d(\phi_{g}(k),\phi_{g}(k_{n})),

so for large enough

, we have

Pick

such that
\|gf\|=1=[gf](k)=f(\phi_{g}(k))

.
Observe that for large enough

\|g_{n}f-gf\|<1/2
, and

is such that
[gf](k_{n})>\frac{1}{2}

|[g_{n}f](k_{n})-[gf](k_{n})|<\frac{1}{2}

. Since, therefore,

To establish (2), pick a compact
K\subset X
and
\delta>0
. We shall show that, for

|a_{g_{n}}(t)-a_{g}(t)|\leq\delta
holds for any

Begin by finding
\varepsilon>0
so that
\phi_{g}(K)_{\varepsilon}
is compact.
Pick

, and

\phi_{g}(K)_{\varepsilon}
. Select

so that

.

Above, we have established that

For

To handle (3), suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that for some compact

Indeed, otherwise

Remark 2.3
.

In general, items (1) and (2) of Proposition
2.1
alone do not imply that
g_{n}\to g
in SOT. Below we construct an example of a locally compact metrizable space
X
, and a sequence of continuous maps
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots:X\to X
, with continuous inverses, so that
\phi_{n}\to id
uniformly on compact sets, but there exists
x\in C_{0}(X)
so that
x\circ\phi_{n}\not\to x
.

Let
X
consist of pairs
(0,x)
(
x\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
) and
(i,j)
, with
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Define the metric
d
by setting
d\big((0,x),(0,y)\big)=2^{-\min\{x,y\}}
, and
d\big((a,b),(c,d)\big)=1
if
\max\{a,c\}\geq 1
.
Then
K\subset X
is compact iff it is a union of a finite set and an interval
\{(0,x):n\leq x\leq\infty\}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
).

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define
\phi_{n}:X\to X
as follows:

•

\phi_{n}(0,i)=(0,i+1)
for
n\leq i<\infty
,
\phi_{n}(0,\infty)=(0,\infty)
.

•

\phi_{n}(n,n)=(0,n)
.

•

\phi_{n}(n,i)=(n,i-1)
for
n<i<\infty
.

•

\phi_{n}(a,b)=(a,b)
if either
a\notin\{0,n\}
, or
a\in\{0,n\}
and
b<n
.

Clearly, for each
n
,
\phi_{n}
is continuous, bijective, and has a continuous inverse. Further,
\phi_{n}\to id
uniformly on compact sets. However, the family
(\phi_{n}^{-1})
is not equicontinuous on
\{(0,i):i\geq m\}
, for any
m
.

To show that the lattice isomorphisms
g_{n}:x\mapsto x\circ\phi_{n}
do not converge to the identity in SOT, define
x\in C_{0}(X)
by setting
x(a,b)=1
if
a=0
,
x(a,b)=0
otherwise. Then
x\circ\phi_{n}(n,n)=x(0,n)=1
, and so,
x\circ\phi_{n}\not\to x
in norm.

From this point on, and up to the end of Section
5
,
G
denotes a group of lattice isometries of
C_{0}(X)
.
The above discussion shows that for any
g\in G
there exists a (necessarily unique) homeomorphism
\phi_{g}:X\to X
so that
gx=x\circ\phi_{g}
(in other words,
a_{g}=1
).
For ease of notation, we sometimes use
g(t)
for
t\in X
to refer to
\phi_{g}(t)
.

Finally, the following description of SOT convergence will be used in the sequel.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

be a locally compact Polish space, and suppose

By Proposition

Suppose now that

for all

, we need only to show that
\phi_{g_{n}}\to\phi_{g}
uniformly on compact sets. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and a compact

We have to show that, for

g,h\in{\mathrm{ISO}}^{\approx}(C_{0}(X))
, we have
(hg)(f)=(a_{h}\cdot a_{g}\circ\phi_{h})\cdot f\circ(\phi_{g}\phi_{h})
, so
\phi_{hg}=\phi_{g}\circ\phi_{h}
, and
a_{hg}=a_{h}\cdot a_{g}\circ\phi_{h}
.
In particular
\phi_{g^{-1}}=\phi_{g}^{-1}
, and
a_{g^{-1}}=1/a_{g}\circ\phi_{g}^{-1}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
be a bounded group of isomorphisms on
C_{0}(X)
. Then there exists a (not necessarily continuous) function
m_{G}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\|x\|_{G}=\|m_{G}\cdot x\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
m(t)=\inf_{g\in G}a_{g}(t)
. We now show that
\|x\|_{G}\leq 1
iff
|x|\leq m
. Given
x\in C_{0}(X)
, we have:

\displaystyle\|x\|_{G}\leq 1\iff

\displaystyle\forall g\forall t\ |(gx)(t)|\leq 1

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall g\forall t\ |a_{g}(t)\cdot x(\phi_{g}(t))|\leq 1

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall g\forall t\ |x(\phi_{g}(t))|\leq a_{g^{-1}}(\phi_{g}(t))

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall t\forall g\ |x(t)|\leq a_{g^{-1}}(t)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\forall t\ \ \ \ |x(t)|\leq m(t)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle|x|\leq m.

Since
G
is bounded,
m
is bounded below, so let
m_{G}=1/m
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Suppose the hypotheses of Theorem
6.1
are valid and, in addition, the family
\{a_{g}:g\in G\}
is locally equicontinuous on
X
. Then
m_{G}
is continuous.

Proof.

We will show that
m=1/m_{G}
, defined in the proof for Theorem
6.1
, is continuous. Since
m
is bounded away from
0
,
m_{G}
is also continuous.

Let
t\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, and pick
\delta
such that for all
s\in X
,
d(s,t)<\delta
implies for all
g\in G,\ |a_{g}(s)-a_{g}(t)|<\varepsilon
. Then

m(s)=\inf_{g\in G}|a_{g}(s)|\leq\sup_{g\in G}|a_{g}(s)-a_{g}(t)|+\inf_{g\in G}|a_{g}(t)|\leq\varepsilon+m(t).

Similarly, we have
m(t)\leq\varepsilon+m(s)
, so
|m(t)-m(s)|\leq\varepsilon
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

The local equicontinuity of the family
(a_{g})_{g\in G}
cannot be omitted from the preceding proofs. For instance, let
X
be the one-point compactification of
\{0,1\}\times\mathbb{N}
; that is,
X
consists of points
(i,k)
(
i\in\{0,1\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
), as well as
\infty
.
Equip
X
with the following metric:
d\big((i,k),(j,m)\big)=2^{-\min\{k,m\}}
,
d\big((i,k),\infty\big)=2^{-k}
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define
a_{n}(t)=2
if
t=(0,n)
,
a_{n}(t)=1/2
if
t=(1,n)
, and
a_{n}(t)=1
otherwise. Further, let
\phi_{n}(i,n)=(1-i,n)
, and
\phi_{n}(t)=t
for
t\notin\{(0,n),(1,n)\}
.
For
x\in C(X)
let
g_{n}(x)=a_{n}\cdot x\circ\phi_{n}
. Note that
g_{n}
’s commute, and
g_{n}^{2}=I
. One can show that
\{g_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded group of lattice isomorphisms on
C(X)
.

However, it is easily seen that the family
(a_{n})
is not equicontinuous (look at the vicinity of
\infty
). The discontinuity of
m
is witnessed by
m(\infty)=1
, while
m((1,n))=1/2
.
Also, the map
\Phi^{-1}
is not SOT-continuous:
(g_{n})
is SOT-divergent (as can be seen by applying these maps to the constant one function), while
(\Phi g_{n})
converges in SOT to
I_{C(X)}
.

For
\phi\in C_{b}(X)
(the space of bounded continuous functions on
X
), define the multiplication operator
\mathbf{M}_{\phi}:C_{0}(X)\to C_{0}(X):f\mapsto\phi f
.
Define the map
\Phi
on
B(C_{0}(X))
: for an operator
T
on
C_{0}(X)
, let
\Phi T=\mathbf{M}_{m_{G}}T\mathbf{M}_{m_{G}^{-1}}
. Clearly
\Phi
is a surjective algebraic isomorphism; also, both
\Phi
and its inverse are SOT-continuous.

Further, for any
x\in C_{0}(X)
and
g\in G
, we have
\|m_{G}\cdot gx\|_{\infty}=\|m_{G}\cdot x\|_{\infty}
. Taking
y=m_{G}\cdot x
, we conclude that
\|m_{G}g(y/M_{G})\|_{\infty}=\|y\|_{\infty}
. Thus,
\Phi(g)
is an isometry on
C_{0}(X)
.

Summarizing the two preceding paragraphs, we obtain:

Corollary 6.4
.

Suppose
X
is a locally compact metrizable space, and suppose
G
is a bounded group of lattice isomorphisms of
C_{0}(X)
for which the family
\{a_{g}:g\in G\}
is locally equicontinuous on
X
.
Then there exists a group homomorphism
\Phi
, taking
G
to a group
G^{\prime}
of lattice isometries of
C_{0}(X)
. Moreover,
\Phi
is both norm and SOT continuous.

In light of Theorem
1.2
, this immediately implies:

Corollary 6.5
.

Suppose
X
is a locally compact Polish space, and suppose
G
is a bounded relatively SOT-closed group of lattice isomorphisms of
C_{0}(X)
for which the families
\{a_{g}:g\in G\}
and
\{\phi_{g}:g\in G\}
are locally equicontinuous on
X
. Suppose also that orbits in
X
induced by
\{\phi_{g}:g\in G\}
are nowhere dense. Then
G
can be displayed on
C_{0}(X)
.

One may also ask what groups can be presented on
C_{b}(X)
(the space of bounded continuous functions on the Hausdorff space
X
, equipped with the
\sup
norm).
The following result shows that, at least for locally compact
X
, no extra groups arise.

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose
X
is a metric space.
Then any lattice isomorphism
T
on
C_{b}(X)
is of the form
[Tx](t)=a(t)x(\phi(t))
, where
a\in C_{b}(X)
with
\inf a>0
, and
\phi:X\to X
is a homeomorphism. Moreover,
\|a\|_{\infty}=\|T\|
.

In particular, if
X
is a locally compact metric space, then any
T
as above maps
C_{0}(X)
onto itself, and moreover, is completely determined by its action on
C_{0}(X)
.
Consequently, if
X
is a locally compact Polish space, then, by Corollary
6.5
, any relatively SOT-closed bounded group of lattice isomorphisms of
C_{b}(X)
satisfying the equicontinuity and density conditions mentioned in Corollary
6.5
can be displayed on
C_{0}(X)
.

Proof.

We identify
C_{b}(X)
with
C(\beta X)
, where
\beta X
is the Stone-Čech compactification of
X
. For
x\in C_{b}(X)
, we denote by
x^{\beta}
its unique extension to
\beta X
.
Likewise, for
f\in C(\beta X)
, we shall use the notation
f_{\beta}
for
f\circ\beta\in C_{b}(X)
.
Then there exist
a\in C(\beta X)
with
\inf a>0
, and a homeomorphism
\phi:\beta X\to\beta X
so that, for any
x\in C(\beta X)
and
\omega\in\beta X
, we have
[Tx](\omega)=a(\omega)x(\phi(\omega))
. We have to show that
\phi^{-1}
(and likewise,
\phi
) maps
X
to itself.

By
[
5
, Lemma 7.12]
,
\alpha\in\beta X
belongs to
X
iff there exists
x\in C(\beta X)
so that
x
vanishes at
\alpha
, and nowhere else.
For such
\alpha
and
x
,
[Tx](\omega)=0
iff
\phi(\omega)=\alpha
, or in other words,
\omega=\phi^{-1}(\alpha)
. Consequently, the function
Tx\in C(\beta X)
vanishes at
\phi^{-1}(\alpha)
, and only there. Thus,
\phi^{-1}(\alpha)\in X
.
∎

7.
Questions for further research

Question 7.1
.

Can Theorem
1.2
be generalized to
AM
-spaces?
[
11
]
provides a partial answer: any separable AM-space can be renormed to have trivial lattice isometries only.
In
[
11
]
, a key step is to switch to a special kind of an AM-space called a regular AM space. Thus, one approach would consist of displaying groups on regular AM spaces.

Question 7.2
.

Can Theorem
1.2
be extended to the spaces
C_{0}(X)
, where
X
has isolated points? In particular, what happens when
X=\mathbb{N}
(and
C_{0}(X)
is simply
c_{0}
)?
Note that, by
[
6
]
, if
G
is a closed subgroup of the infinite permutation group
S_{\infty}
, then
G\times\{-1,1\}
can be displayed on
S_{\infty}
, in the Banach space sense. However, the new norm on
c_{0}
producing this display is not a lattice norm.

Question 7.3
.

Is every Polish group displayable in a separable lattice for some lattice
X
?

To motivate this question, recall that, by
[
6
, Theorem 31]
, for any Polish group
G
there exists a separable real Banach space
X
so that
G\times\{-1,1\}
is displayable on
X
. The
\{-1,1\}
multiple is important here, as any group displayable on a real Banach space must include a central involution.
