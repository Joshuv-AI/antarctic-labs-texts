Optimal parameter estimation for linear SPDEs from multiple measurements
Randolf Altmeyer, Anton Tiepner, Martin Wahl

Abstract
The coefficients in a second order parabolic linear stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) are estimated from multiple spatially localised measurements. Assuming that the spatial resolution tends to zero and the number of measurements is non-decreasing, the rate of convergence for each coefficient depends on its differential order and is faster for higher order coefficients. Based on an explicit analysis of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space of a general stochastic evolution equation, a Gaussian lower bound scheme is introduced. As a result, minimax optimality of the rates as well as sufficient and necessary conditions for consistent estimation are established.

Introduction

Stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) form a flexible class of models for space-time data. They combine phenomena such as diffusion and transport that occur naturally in many processes, but also include random forcing terms, which may arise from microscopic scaling limits or account for model uncertainty. Quantifying the size of these different effects is an important step in model validation.

Suppose that
X=(X(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
solves the linear parabolic SPDE

dX(t)=A_{\vartheta}X(t)dt+dW(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(1.1)

on an open, bounded and smooth domain
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with some initial value
X_{0}
, a space-time white noise
dW
and a second order elliptic operator

\displaystyle A_{\vartheta}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\vartheta_{i}A_{i}+A_{0}

. A prototypical example is

\displaystyle A_{\vartheta}=\vartheta_{1}\Delta+\vartheta_{2}(\nabla\cdot b)+\vartheta_{3},\qquad\vartheta\in(0,\infty)\times\R\times(-\infty,0],

(1.3)

with diffusivity, transport and reaction coefficients

,
\vartheta_{2}
,
\vartheta_{3}

While the estimation of a scalar parameter in front of the highest order operator

Let us briefly describe our main contributions. Our first result extends the augmented MLE

and the CLT of

4
]
to

). We compute the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of the Gaussian measures induced by the laws of

Combined with our CLT we deduce for general point spread functions

with non-intersecting supports that consistent estimation is possible if and only if

Conceptually, spectral measurements can be obtained approximately from local measurements on a dense grid over the entire domain by a discrete Fourier transform and we recover the rates of convergence of

The information geometry underlying local measurements is complex due to the non-linear dependence of the solution

[
35

non-applicable in this context. Instead, we introduce a novel lower bound scheme for Gaussian measures, which exploits that the Hellinger distance of their laws can be related to properties of their RKHS. This is different from the lower bound approach of
[
4
]
for

and paves the way to rigorous lower bounds for each coefficient and an arbitrary number of measurements. One of our key results states that the RKHS of the Gaussian measure induced by

with

and its squared RKHS norm equals

\displaystyle\|\Delta h\|_{L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\Lambda))}^{2}+\|h^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\Lambda))}^{2}+\|(-\Delta)^{1/2}h(0)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}+\|(-\Delta)^{1/2}h(T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}.

This surprisingly simple formula generalises the finite-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck case
[
38

X
. To the best of our knowledge the RKHS of

[
58

2.3
A central limit theorem

3
The RKHS

In Section
4
, we show optimality of the rates of convergence appearing in Theorem
2.3
. A crucial ingredient for these lower bound considerations is a good understanding of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of the Gaussian measure induced by the law of the observations when
A_{\vartheta}=\Delta
.

We first derive the RKHS of the stochastic convolution (
2.1
) in a more general setting. Suppose that
A
is an (unbounded) negative self-adjoint closed operator on a Hilbert space
(\mathcal{H},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{H}})
with domain
\mathcal{D}(A)\subset\mathcal{H}
such that
Ae_{j}=-\lambda_{j}e_{j}
for a non-decreasing sequence
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of positive real numbers with
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
and an orthonormal basis
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of
\mathcal{H}
, and such that
A
generates a strongly continuous semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{H}

[
18
]
. With a cylindrical Wiener process
W
, consider the stationary stochastic convolution

\displaystyle X(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}S(t-t^{\prime})dW(t^{\prime}),\quad t\geq 0.

(3.1)

As discussed after (
2.1
) the process
X=(X(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
is understood as a random element with values in
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}
almost surely for some larger Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

In what follows, we use the convention that a RKHS is denoted by the letter
H
. Moreover, we add a subscript to indicate the process which is under consideration. For instance,
H_{X}
denotes the RKHS of
X
considered as a Gaussian random variable taking values in the Hilbert space
L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}_{1})
. Since the RKHS of
X
depends only on its distribution, the RKHS, as well as its norm, in the next theorem are independent of the embedding space
\mathcal{H}_{1}
(see, e.g., Exercise 2.6.5 in
[
22
]
). For the proof and some background on the RKHS of a Gaussian measure see Section
6.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(H_{X},\|\cdot\|_{X})
be the RKHS of the process
X
in (
3.1
). Let
T\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle H_{X}

\displaystyle=\{h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}):h\text{ absolutely continuous, }Ah,h^{\prime}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})\}

and for
h\in H_{X}

\displaystyle\|h\|_{X}^{2}=\|Ah\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})}^{2}+\|h^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})}^{2}+\|(-A)^{1/2}h(0)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+\|(-A)^{1/2}h(T)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}},

as well as

\displaystyle\|h\|_{X}^{2}\leq 3\|Ah\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})}^{2}+\|h\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})}^{2}+2\|h^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})}^{2}.

Note that
h,h^{\prime},Ah\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})
implies that the map
t\mapsto\langle Ah(t),h(t)\rangle
is absolutely continuous (cf. the proof of
[
19
, Theorem 5.9.3]
and the proof of Theorem
3.1
), so that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
is indeed well-defined.
Theorem
3.1
generalises the result for scalar Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes to the infinite dimensional process
X
, cf. Lemma
6.8
below.

Next, as in (
2.3
), consider the Gaussian process
(\langle X(t),z\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})_{t\geq 0,z\in\mathcal{H}}
, where the ‘inner product’ here corresponds to

\displaystyle\langle X(t),z\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\langle S(t-t^{\prime})z,dW(t^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

satisfying (
2.3
) for
z\in\mathcal{D}(A^{*})=\mathcal{D}(A)
by analogous arguments. We study the RKHS of
(\langle X(t),z\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})_{0\leq t\leq T}
for finitely many
z
. A first proof considers
z
from the dual space of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. In that case, we can realise
\langle X,z\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
as a linear transformation of
X
by a bounded linear map
L
from
L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}_{1})
to
L^{2}([0,T])^{M}
, and this allows for relating the RKHS of
X
and
\langle X,z\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
using Theorem
3.1
. Another proof is presented in Section
A.7
, which circumvents this by an approximation argument.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
K_{1},\dots,K_{M}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
and with
X
in (
3.1
) consider the process
X_{K}
with
X_{K}(t)=(\langle X(t),K_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})_{k=1}^{M}
. Suppose that the Gram matrix
G=(\langle K_{k},K_{l}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})_{1\leq k,l\leq M}
is non-singular, and let
G_{A}=(\langle AK_{k},AK_{l}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})_{1\leq k,l\leq M}
. Let
T\geq 1
. Then the RKHS
(H_{X_{K}},\|\cdot\|_{X_{K}})
of
X_{K}
satisfies
H_{X_{K}}=H^{M}
, where

\displaystyle H=\{h\in L^{2}([0,T]):h\text{ absolutely continuous},h^{\prime}\in L^{2}([0,T])\}

and for
h=(h_{k})_{k=1}^{M}\in H_{X_{K}}

\displaystyle\lVert h\rVert_{X_{K}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(3\|G^{-1}\|_{\operatorname{op}}^{2}\|G_{A}\|_{\operatorname{op}}+\|G^{-1}\|_{\operatorname{op}})\sum_{k=1}^{M}\lVert h_{k}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T])}+2\|G^{-1}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\lVert h_{k}^{\prime}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T])}.

Theorem
3.2
(and its slight generalisation in (
6.16
)) can be used to compute the RKHS of quite general observation schemes. In the specific case
A=\Delta
and local measurements with
K_{k}=K_{\delta,x_{k}}
we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
(H_{X_{\delta}},\|\cdot\|_{X_{\delta}})
be the RKHS of
X_{\delta}
with respect to

,

with

and

and for

\displaystyle\|h\|_{X_{\delta}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{\lVert\Delta K\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{d})}}{\delta^{4}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\|h_{k}\|_{L^{2}([0,T])}^{2}+2\sum_{k=1}^{M}\|h_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}([0,T])}^{2}.

Similar results hold for the RKHS of

, see Corollary

4
Optimality

In this section, we show that the rates of convergence
M^{1/2}\delta^{1-n_{i}}
achieved by the augmented MLE for parameters
\vartheta_{i}
with respect to operators
A_{i}
of order

are indeed optimal and cannot be improved in our general setup. The proof strategy (presented in Section

) relies on a novel lower bound scheme for Gaussian measures by relating the Hellinger distance of their laws to properties of their RKHS. The Gaussian lower bound is then applied to one-dimensional submodels
(\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta})_{\vartheta\in\Theta_{i}}
with
A_{\vartheta}
from (
1.3
) assuming a sufficiently regular kernel function

and a stationary initial condition.

Assumption L
.

Suppose that
\P_{\vartheta}
corresponds to the law of the stationary solution
X
to the SPDE (
1.1
) and assume that the following conditions hold:

(i)

The kernel function satisfies
K=\Delta^{2}\tilde{K}
with
\tilde{K}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\R^{d})
.

(ii)

The models are
A_{\vartheta}=\vartheta_{1}\Delta+\vartheta_{2}(\nabla\cdot b)+\vartheta_{3}
for
\vartheta\in\R^{3}
, a fixed unit vector
b\in\R^{d}
, and where
\vartheta
lies in one of the parameter classes

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\vartheta=(\vartheta_{1},0,0):\vartheta_{1}\geq 1\},

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\vartheta=(1,\vartheta_{2},0):\vartheta_{2}\in[0,1]\},

\displaystyle\Theta_{3}

\displaystyle=\{\vartheta=(1,0,\vartheta_{3}):\vartheta_{3}\leq 0\}.

(iii)

Let

be

\Lambda
, that is,

for all

The parameter classes

correspond to the cases of estimating the diffusivity

and reaction coefficient

in front of operators

with differential orders

,

,

.

Grant Assumption

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}_{i}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vartheta\in\Theta_{i}\\

5.3
Practical aspects

In this section, we outline a precise situation where local measurements arise, and how the augmented MLE can be applied, even if the additional measurements
X^{A_{i}}_{\delta,k}
are not available.

Optical measurements of physical or chemical concentrations
X(t)
at a focal point
x_{k}\in\Lambda
are obtained as (normalised) counts of certain markers, e.g., photons
[
17
]
. According to classical microscopy
[
34
]
, diffraction leads to a blurred image of
X(t)
, and the blur pattern can be described by convolution with a specific point spread function, which can be written as

\displaystyle X_{\delta,k}(t)=\langle X(t),K_{\delta,x_{k}}\rangle=(X(t)*\bar{K}_{\delta})(x_{k}),\qquad\bar{K}_{\delta}(y)=\delta^{d/2}K(-\delta^{-1}y).

(5.1)

It is reasonable to assume that the additional measurement noise due to photon counting is negligible and that measuring happens on faster time scales than the dynamics of
X
.

The resolution
\delta
is specific to the measurement device and determines how far focal points can be apart to distinguish them
[
34
, Definition 2.5]
. The point spread function depends inversely on
\delta
, and is often approximated by a normal density with standard deviation
\delta

[
6
]
. This phenomenon is the source for the large number of statistical works on Gaussian deconvolution. In applications, both the point spread function and the resolution
\delta
are usually known, and can even be engineered to meet desired specifications
[
7
]
. Note that multiplicative constants such as the scaling of
K_{\delta,x_{k}}
cancel out in the augmented MLE and therefore play no role for parameter estimation.

If we have (time discrete) local measurements (
5.1
) at our disposal, then exchanging differentiation and convolution gives

X^{A_{i}}_{\delta,k}=(X(t)*A_{i}^{*}\bar{K}_{\delta})(x_{k})=A_{i}^{*}(X(t)*\bar{K}_{\delta})(x_{k}).

This can be approximated by finite differences. For example, if
A_{i}=\Delta
and
x_{k-1}=x_{k}-\delta e_{i}
,
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\delta e_{i}
are ‘neighbours’ of
x_{k}
in the
i
-th coordinate with the unit vector
e_{i}
, separated by a distance
\delta
, then
X^{A_{i}}_{\delta,k}(t)
can be approximated by
\delta^{-2}(X_{\delta,k+1}(t)-2X_{\delta,k}(t)+X_{\delta,k-1}(t))
. Using suitable Riemann sum approximations for Lebesgue and stochastic integrals, we thus obtain a discretised version of the augmented MLE
\hat{\vartheta}_{\delta}
.

While a full analysis of such discretisation schemes is beyond the scope of this paper, we shortly report on a recent case study for cell motility, using the augmented MLE for real and simulated data
[
2
, Sections 5 and 6]
. There, the first component of a coupled stochastic activator-inhibitor system
(X_{1},X_{2})
follows a semi-linear SPDE with diffusity
\vartheta
, reaction function
f
and noise level
\sigma>0
,

\displaystyle dX_{1}(t)=(\vartheta\Delta X_{1}(t)+f(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t)))dt+\sigma dW(t).

The equation models the change in actine concentration along the cell cortex during cell repolarisation. In
[
43
]
, on a time grid of up to 256 seconds
M=100
measurements for 18 different cells, expected to have about the same diffusivities, were used to fit parameters in the deterministic PDE with
\sigma=0
. In
[
2
]
, the same data were taken as local measurements from
X_{1}(t)
, and
\vartheta
was estimated by the discretised augmented MLE as discussed above, providing a biologically reasonable magnitude for
\vartheta
, which can be used to distinguish the mechanisms contributing to diffusion. The resolution
\delta
was found as an upper bound on the spatial mesh size. The estimates are stable across the cell populations as opposed to
[
43
]
, which averaged the different estimates across cells to reduce ‘noise’, and obtained in this way a much inflated average diffusivity.

6.1.1

6.2
RKHS computations

The proofs of the RKHS results from Section
3
are achieved by basic operations on RKHS, in particular under linear transformation (see, e.g.,

[
38
, Chapter 4]
or
[
59
, Chapter 12]
).

Recall the stationary process
X
in (
3.1
) and that
Ae_{j}=-\lambda_{j}e_{j}
with eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
and an orthonormal basis
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of
\mathcal{H}
. The cylindrical Wiener process can be realised as
W=\sum_{j\geq 1}e_{j}\beta_{j}
for independent scalar Brownian motions
\beta_{j}
and we obtain

\displaystyle X(t)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\lambda_{j}(t-t^{\prime})}d\beta_{j}(t^{\prime})e_{j}=\sum_{j\geq 1}Y_{j}(t)e_{j},

(6.6)

with independent stationary Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes
Y_{j}
satisfying

\displaystyle dY_{j}(t)=-\lambda_{j}Y_{j}(t)dt+d\beta_{j}(t).

For a sequence
(\mu_{j})
of non-decreasing, positive real numbers, take
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to be the closure of
\mathcal{H}
under the norm

\displaystyle\lVert z\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}^{2}}\langle z,e_{j}\rangle^{2}_{\mathcal{H}},

such that
\mathcal{H}
is continuously embedded in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. If for

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\lVert S(t^{\prime})\rVert^{2}_{\operatorname{HS}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}_{1})}dt^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\lVert S(t^{\prime})e_{j}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}dt^{\prime}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-2\lambda_{j}t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}<\infty,

(6.7)

then we conclude by
[
15
, Theorem 5.2]
that the law of
X
induces a Gaussian measure on the Hilbert space
L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}_{1})
. A first universal choice is given by
\mu_{j}=j
for all
j\geq 1
. Moreover, if
A
is a second order elliptic differential operator, then Weyl’s law
[
52
, Lemma 2.3]
says that the
\lambda_{j}
are positive real numbers of the order

, meaning that the choice
\mu_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{s/2}
is possible whenever

and

. In this case,
\mathcal{H}_{1}
corresponds to a Sobolev space of negative order
-s
induced by the eigensequence
(\lambda_{j},e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
.

Let us introduce some background on the RKHS of a centred Gaussian random variable
Z
, defined on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{Z}
. Its covariance operator
C_{Z}
is necessarily positive self-adjoint and trace-class. This means, by the spectral theorem, there exist strictly positive eigenvalues
(\sigma_{j}^{2})_{j\geq 1}
and an associated orthonormal system of eigenvectors
(u_{j})_{j\geq 1}
such that
C_{Z}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\sigma_{j}^{2}(u_{j}\otimes u_{j})
. Associate with
Z
(or rather with the induced centred Gaussian measure) the so-called kernel or RKHS
(H_{Z},\|\cdot\|_{Z})
, where

\displaystyle H_{Z}=\{h\in\mathcal{Z}:\|h\|_{Z}<\infty\},\qquad\|h\|_{Z}^{2}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\langle u_{j},h\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}

(6.8)

(see, e.g.,
[
38
, Example 4.2]
and also
[
38
, Chapters 4.1 and 4.3]
and
[
22
, Chapter 3.6]
for other characterizations of the RKHS of a Gaussian measure or process).
Alternatively, we have
H_{Z}=C_{Z}^{1/2}\mathcal{Z}
and
\|h\|_{Z}=\lVert C_{Z}^{-1/2}h\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}}
for
h\in H_{Z}
. A useful tool to compute the RKHS is the fact that the RKHS behaves well under linear transformation. More precisely, if
L:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
is a bounded linear operator between Hilbert spaces, then the image
L(Z)
is a centred Gaussian random variable with RKHS
L(H_{Z})
and norm
\lVert h\rVert_{L(Z)}=\inf\{\lVert f\rVert_{Z}:f\in L^{-1}h\}
(see Proposition 4.1 in
[
38
]
and also Chapter 3.6 in
[
22
]
).

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(\lambda_{j}h(t)+h^{\prime}(t))^{2}\,dt+2\lambda_{j}h^{2}(0)

6.3
Proof of the lower bounds

In this section, we give the main steps of the proof of Theorem

, which follows a Gaussian route. First, we combine a classical Gaussian lower bound (based on two hypotheses) with arguments from the Feldman-Hajek theorem to formulate a lower bound scheme that is expressed in terms of covariance operators and RKHS norms. Second, we invoke the RKHS computations from Section
3
to further reduce our analysis to
L^{2}
-distances of the involved (cross-)covariance kernels and their first and second derivatives. Finally, we use semigroup perturbation arguments to compute these distances in the setting of Assumption
Assumption L
. The proofs of three key lemmas are deferred to the appendix.

6.3.1
Gaussian minimax lower bounds

Let
(\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta})_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
be a family of probability measures defined on the same measurable space with a parameter set
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}

[
56

\displaystyle\ H^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta^{0}},\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta^{1}})\leq 1,

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}}\max_{\vartheta\in\{\vartheta^{0},\vartheta^{1}\}}\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta}\Big(|\hat{\vartheta}-\vartheta|\geq\frac{|\vartheta^{0}-\vartheta^{1}|}{2}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{2-\sqrt{3}}{4}=:c_{3},

[

Next, let

and

be two Gaussian measures defined on a separable Hilbert space

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{J}}\lVert S^{*}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda_{\delta,x})}\lesssim 1\wedge t^{-1/2},\quad t\geq 0,\delta\geq 0.

(A.3)

Plugging this into the last display and using
M\delta^{2}\lesssim 1
provides us with

\displaystyle\E[\mathcal{I}_{\delta}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t\delta^{-2}}(1\wedge s^{-1})dsdt\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\log(t\delta^{-2})dt\lesssim\log(\delta^{-1}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{I}_{\delta})

\displaystyle\lesssim M^{2}\delta^{6}\left(\int_{0}^{T\delta^{-2}}(1\wedge(t^{\prime})^{-1})dt^{\prime}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T\delta^{-2}}(1\wedge t^{-1/2})dt\right)^{2}\lesssim\log(\delta^{-1}).

We are thus left with showing
\E[\mathcal{I}_{\delta}]\gtrsim\log(\delta^{-1})
. First, note that
\lVert S^{*}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K\rVert_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}\geq e^{-T|\vartheta|}\lVert\tilde{S}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K\rVert_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}
and decompose

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{S}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}=\langle\tilde{S}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K,\tilde{S}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K\rangle_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}

\displaystyle\quad=\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}+\langle e^{t\Delta_{0}}K+\tilde{S}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K,\tilde{S}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K-e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rangle_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}.

Recalling
K\geq 0
, the inner product here is uniformly in
x\in\mathcal{J}
up to a universal constant upper bounded by

\displaystyle\langle e^{t\Delta_{0}}K,(e^{t\Delta_{0}}K^{2})^{1/2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}(\delta t^{1/2}e^{-\delta^{-2}t^{-1}})^{1/2}\leq\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rVert_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K^{2}\rVert_{L^{1}(\R^{2})}\delta t^{1/2},

concluding by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
\sup_{x\geq 0}xe^{-x}\lesssim 1
in the last inequality. Since
\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K^{2}\rVert_{L^{1}(\R^{2})}=\lVert K\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}
and using (
A.3
), it thus follows for some

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{S}_{\vartheta,\delta,x}(t)K\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}\geq\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}-C(1\wedge t^{-1/2})\delta t^{1/2}.

Hence, using
M\delta^{2}\gtrsim 1

\displaystyle\E[\mathcal{I}_{\delta}]

\displaystyle\quad\geq\int_{0}^{\delta^{-1}}\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rVert_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}^{2}dt-\int_{0}^{\delta^{-1}}C\delta dt=\int_{0}^{\delta^{-1}}\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rVert_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}^{2}dt-C.

Suppose without loss of generality that the support of
K
is contained in the unit ball

as convolution with the heat kernel

we have

\displaystyle\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rVert_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}^{2}=\int_{\R^{2}}\left(\int_{B_{1}(0)}q_{t}(y-x)K(x)dx\right)^{2}dy.

The heat kernel

is decreasing as

we bound

for any

\displaystyle\lVert e^{t\Delta_{0}}K\rVert_{L^{2}(\R^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\R^{2}}\left(\int_{B_{1}(0)}q_{t}(y+y/|y|)K(x)dx\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\lVert K\rVert_{L^{1}(\R^{2})}^{2}\int_{\R^{2}}q_{t}(y+y/|y|)^{2}dy\gtrsim t^{-1}.

In all, we conclude that
\E[\mathcal{I}_{\delta}]\gtrsim C^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\delta^{-1}}t^{-1}dt-C
for
C^{\prime}>0
, implying the wanted lower bound in (
A.2
).

Lemma A.3
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}
be
\delta
-separated points in
\R^{d}
, and let
p>d
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{M}\frac{1}{|x_{1}-x_{k}|^{p}}\leq C\delta^{-p},

where
C
is a constant depending only on
d
and
p
.

Since
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}
are
\delta
-separated, the Euclidean balls
B(x_{k},\delta/2)=\{y\in\R^{d}:|y-x_{k}|\leq\delta/2\}
around the
x_{k}
of radius
\delta/2
are disjoint. Moreover, for
y\in B(x_{k},\delta/2)
and
k>1
, we have

\displaystyle|y-x_{1}|\leq|y-x_{k}|+|x_{k}-x_{1}|\leq\frac{\delta}{2}+|x_{k}-x_{1}|\leq\frac{3}{2}|x_{k}-x_{1}|,

implying that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|x_{k}-x_{1}|}\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{1}{|y-x_{1}|}.

We conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{M}\frac{1}{|x_{1}-x_{k}|^{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{M}\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(B(x_{k},\delta/2))}\int_{B(x_{k},\delta/2)}\limits\frac{1}{|x_{1}-x_{k}|^{p}}dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(3/2)^{p}}{(\delta/2)^{d}\operatorname{vol}(B(0,1))}\sum_{k=2}^{M}\int_{B(x_{k},\delta/2)}\limits\frac{1}{|y-x_{1}|^{p}}dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(3/2)^{p}}{(\delta/2)^{d}\operatorname{vol}(B(0,1))}\int_{B(x_{1},\delta/2)^{c}}\limits\frac{1}{|y-x_{1}|^{p}}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{(3/2)^{p}}{(\delta/2)^{d}\operatorname{vol}(B(0,1))}\int_{B(0,\delta/2)^{c}}\limits\frac{1}{|y|^{p}}dy.

Changing to polar coordinates, we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{M}\frac{1}{|x_{1}-x_{k}|^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d(3/2)^{p}}{(\delta/2)^{d}}\int_{\delta/2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{p}}t^{d-1}dt=\frac{d(3/2)^{p}}{(\delta/2)^{p}}\int_{1}^{\infty}s^{d-p-1}ds.

Since
p>d
, the latter integral is finite, and the claim follows.
∎

Argue as in the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle(A_{i}^{*}K)_{i=1}^{p}\text{ are linearly independent in }L^{2}(\Lambda).

(A.21)

Define

\displaystyle H=\Big(\Big\langle\frac{A_{i}^{*}K}{\lVert A_{i}^{*}K\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}},\frac{A_{j}^{*}K}{\lVert A_{j}^{*}K\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}}\Big\rangle\Big)_{i,j=1}^{p},

.

Let

Let
