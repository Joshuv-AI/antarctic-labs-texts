Abstract
In our previous work [arXiv:2202.10042], the complexity of Sinkhorn iteration is reduced from O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) to the optimal O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) by leveraging the special structure of the kernel matrix. In this paper, we explore the special structure of kernel matrices by defining and utilizing the properties of the L ower- Co l L inear T riangular Matrix (L-CoLT matrix) and U pper- Co l L inear T riangular Matrix (U-CoLT matrix). We prove that (1) L/U-CoLT matrix-vector multiplications can be carried out in O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) operations; (2) both families of matrices are closed under the Hadamard product and matrix scaling. These properties help to alleviate two key difficulties for reducing the complexity of the Inexact Proximal point method (IPOT), and allow us to significantly reduce the number of iterations to O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) . This yields the Fast Sinkhorn II (FS-2) algorithm for accurate computation of optimal transport with low algorithm complexity and fast convergence. Numerical experiments are presented to show the effectiveness and efficiency of our approach.

The Wasserstein-1 metric and the IPOT method

Given two unit discrete distributions
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
,

\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots u_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
u_{i}\geq 0
,
v_{j}\geq 0
, and
\sum_{i}u_{i}=\sum_{j}v_{j}=1
.
The Wasserstein-1 distance between them is defined as
[
31
]

W_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right)=\min_{\Gamma\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{u},\Gamma^{T}\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{v}}\left\langle C,\;\Gamma\right\rangle,

(1)

where
C=[c_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the cost matrix. The element
c_{ij}=\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\|_{1}
represents the cost of transporting the unit mass from position
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
to position
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
and the variable
\Gamma=[\gamma_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
to be optimized is the transport plan. Here, the Frobenius inner product
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}b_{ij}
, where
A=[a_{ij}],B=[b_{ij}]
are real-valued matrices.

The Sinkhorn algorithm
[
10
,
34
]
solves an entropy regularized OT problem to obtain an approximate result of (
1
). However, the small regular parameter required by good approximation leads to a slow convergence rate and numerical instability. To avoid this problem, the proximal point iteration (
2
) is developed to solve (
1
) accurately
[
35
]
. It begins with a transport map
\Gamma^{(0)}
and iteratively solves the following minimization problem

\Gamma^{\left(t+1\right)}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\Gamma\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{u},\Gamma^{T}\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{v}}\left\langle C,\;\Gamma\right\rangle+\delta^{\left(t\right)}D_{h}\left(\Gamma,\;\Gamma^{\left(t\right)}\right),

(2)

where
D_{h}
is Bregman divergence, taken in the form of the KL divergence in
[
35
]
,

D_{h}\left(A,\;B\right)=\sum_{i,j}\left(a_{ij}\ln\frac{a_{ij}}{b_{ij}}-a_{ij}+b_{ij}\right)

and
\delta^{\left(t\right)}
is the regular parameter. The Lagrangian of the above equation writes

L(\Gamma,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\left\langle C,\;\Gamma\right\rangle+\delta^{\left(t\right)}D_{h}\left(\Gamma,\;\Gamma^{\left(t\right)}\right)+\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{T}\left(\Gamma\mathbf{1}-\boldsymbol{u}\right)+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\left(\Gamma^{T}\mathbf{1}-\boldsymbol{v}\right).

Taking derivative of the Lagrangian with respect of
\gamma_{ij}
directly leads to

\gamma_{ij}=e^{-\alpha_{i}/\delta^{\left(t\right)}}Q_{ij}^{(t)}e^{-\beta_{j}/\delta^{\left(t\right)}},\;\;\textrm{where}\;\;Q_{ij}^{(t)}=\gamma^{\left(t\right)}_{ij}e^{-c_{ij}/\delta^{\left(t\right)}}>0.

Denoting
\odot
as the Hadamard product,
Q^{(t)}=K\odot\Gamma^{(t)}
and
K=[e^{-c_{ij}/\delta^{(t)}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the kernel matrix.
Letting
\phi_{i}=e^{-\alpha_{i}/\delta^{\left(t\right)}}
,

\psi_{j}=e^{-\beta_{j}/\delta^{(t)}}
, and vectors
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{i})
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(\psi_{j})
, one obtains

{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\phi})Q^{(t)}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{u},\quad{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\psi})Q^{(t)\top}{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{v}.

(3)

By iteratively updating vectors
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(t,\ell+1)}=\boldsymbol{v}\oslash(Q^{(t)\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t,\ell)}),\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(t,\ell+1)}=\boldsymbol{u}\oslash(Q^{(t)}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(t,\ell+1)}),

(4)

one can obtain an accurate solution for the original OT problem (
1
). Here
\oslash
represents pointwise division,
t
is the proximal iteration step (outer iteration) and
\ell
it the Sinkhorn-type iteration step (inner iteration). The pseudo-code of IPOT is shown in
algorithm

1
.

The Collinear Triangular Matrix

3.1
Definition and Fast Matrix-Vector Multiplication

Definition 1
(Lower/Upper-Collinear Triangular Matrix).

A lower triangular matrix is called a
Lower-Collinear Triangular Matrix
(L-CoLT matrix) if its corresponding entries on any two rows (columns) have the same–column (row) independent– ratio except those dividing by
0
. Specifically, the N-dimensional L-CoLT matrix set is defined as follows:

\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}\!=\!\left\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\;|\;m_{\!i+1,j\!}/m_{\!i,j\!}=r_{i},\;j\leq i;\;m_{\!i,j\!}=0,\;i<j,\;\boldsymbol{r}\in\left(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}\right)^{N-1}\right\}.

(5)

Similarly, we define
Upper-Collinear Triangular Matrix
(U-CoLT matrix), which is a strictly upper triangular matrix:

\mathcal{C}_{U}^{N}\!=\!\left\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\;|\;m_{\!i-1,j\!}/m_{\!i,j\!}=r^{\prime}_{i-1},\;i<j;\;m_{\!i,j\!}=0,\;i\geq j,\;\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\in\left(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}\right)^{N-2}\right\}.

(6)

We call the vectors
r
and
r^{\prime}
in (
5
)-(
6
) the
ratio vectors
of the collinear triangular matrix.

The matrices introduced in
definition

1
are termed as collinear triangular matrices (CoLT), due to the following collinearity between columns:

m_{i,j}/m_{i,j+1}=m_{k,j}/m_{k,j+1}\iff m_{i,j}/m_{k,j}=m_{i,j+1}/m_{k,j+1}.

Theorem 2
(Vector Representation of Collinear Triangular Matrix).

Any L-CoLT matrix
M_{L}
can be represented by its diagonal elements
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and the ratio vector
\boldsymbol{r}
in Equation (
5
). Any U-CoLT matrix
M_{U}
can be represented by its superdiagonal elements
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}
and the ratio vector
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
in (
6
).

Proof.

For any L-CoLT matrix
M_{L}\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}
, if its corresponding
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
are given, then
m_{i,j}=\gamma_{j}\prod\limits_{k=i}^{N-1}r_{k}
. The proof of U-CoLT is similar.

In the following, we use
\textsc{L-CoLT}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\;\boldsymbol{r})
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\;\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
and
\textsc{U-CoLT}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime},\;\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1},\;\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-2}
to represent a L-CoLT matrix and a U-CoLT matrix, respectively. A
specific correspondence of L-CoLT and U-CoLT is shown as follow:

For the L-CoLT matrix
M_{L}

M_{L}=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\;\boldsymbol{r})=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}&&&&\\
\gamma_{1}r_{1}&\gamma_{2}&&&\\
\gamma_{1}r_{1}r_{2}&\gamma_{2}r_{2}&\gamma_{3}&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\\
\gamma_{1}\!\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!r_{i}&\gamma_{2}\!\prod\limits_{i=2}^{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!r_{i}&\gamma_{3}\!\prod\limits_{i=3}^{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!r_{i}&\cdots&\;\;\gamma_{N}\end{pmatrix}

Similarly, for the U-CoLT matrix
M_{U}

M_{U}=\textsc{U-CoLT}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime},\;\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}0\quad&\;\gamma^{\prime}_{1}&\;\gamma^{\prime}_{2}r^{\prime}_{1}&\;\cdots&\;\gamma^{\prime}_{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\!N\!-\!2\!}\!r^{\prime}_{i}\\

\;&\;&\;&\;&0\;\\
\end{pmatrix}

The special nature of the L-CoLT and U-CoLT matrices allows us to compute matrix-vector multiplications in

For any

\gamma_{1}r_{1}r_{2}y_{1}&+&\gamma_{2}r_{2}y_{2}&+&\gamma_{3}y_{3}&\cdots&+&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\gamma_{1}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{N-1}r_{i}y_{1}&+&\gamma_{2}\prod\limits_{i=2}^{N-1}r_{i}y_{2}&+&\gamma_{3}\prod\limits_{i=3}^{N-1}r_{i}y_{3}&\cdots&+&\gamma_{N}y_{N}\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

3.2
Some Basic Properties

In this subsection, we justify some basic properties of those matrices involved, which will be used in our algorithm.

Theorem 4
.

\left(\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N},\;\odot\right)
and
\left(\mathcal{C}_{U}^{N},\;\odot\right)
are Abelian groups, where
\odot
is the Hadamard product.

Proof.

We only prove the theorem for
\left(\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N},\;\odot\right)
. It suffices to show that
\left(\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N},\;\odot\right)
has the following properties:

Closure: For any two matrices
A=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\;\hat{\boldsymbol{r}})
and
B=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
, we set
D=A\odot B
. Since
d_{i,j}=a_{i,j}b_{i,j}
, one has

\displaystyle d_{i,j}/d_{i+1,j}=\left(a_{i,j}b_{i,j}\right)/\left(a_{i+1,j}b_{i+1,j}\right)=\hat{r}_{i}\tilde{r}_{i},

\displaystyle\text{ }j=1,2,\cdots,i,

(9)

and the strictly upper triangle part of
D
is obviously 0, which means
D=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\odot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\;\hat{r}\odot\tilde{r})\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}
.

Identity and Inverses: Let

E=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&&\\
1&\quad 1&&&\\
1&\quad 1&\quad 1&&\\
\vdots&\quad\vdots&\quad\vdots&\quad\ddots&\\
1&\quad 1&\quad 1&\quad\cdots&\quad 1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N},

(10)

then for any
A=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\;\boldsymbol{r})
,
A\odot E=E\odot A=A
, which means
E
is the identity element. Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}1/a_{11}&&&&\\
1/a_{21}&\quad 1/a_{22}&&&\\
1/a_{31}&\quad 1/a_{32}&\quad 1/a_{33}&&\\
\vdots&\quad\vdots&\quad\vdots&\quad\ddots&\\
1/a_{n1}&\quad 1/a_{n2}&\quad 1/a_{n3}&\quad\cdots&\quad 1/a_{nn}\end{pmatrix}.

Since

b_{i,j}/b_{i+1,j}=a_{i+1,j}/a_{i,j}=1/r_{i},\;j=1,2,\cdots,i,

then
B\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}
. And obviously,
A\odot B=B\odot A=E
, which means
B
is the inverse of
A
.

Commutativity and Associativity: The commutativity and associativity can be derived from the commutative and associative law of real number multiplication.

Based on the above theorem, we can deduce directly

Corollary 5
.

\left(\mathcal{C}^{N},\;\odot\right)
is an abelian group with identity element
\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{T}
.

Theorem 6
.

For any vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\left(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}\right)^{N}
,
f_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(M\right)=\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)M
and
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(M\right)=M\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)
are permutations in
\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}
and
\mathcal{C}_{U}^{N}
.

Proof.

We only prove the theorem for
\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}
.

Closure: For any vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\left(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}\right)^{N}
, and
M_{L}=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\;\boldsymbol{r})
, let
E
be the one defined in Equation (
10
). Since

E_{1}=\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)E=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&&&&\\
x_{2}&\;x_{2}&&&\\
x_{3}&\;x_{3}&\;x_{3}&&\\
\vdots&\;\vdots&\;\vdots&\;\ddots&\\
x_{n}&\;x_{n}&\;x_{n}&\;\cdots&\;x_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N},

E_{2}=E\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&&&&\\
x_{1}&\;x_{2}&&&\\
x_{1}&\;x_{2}&\;x_{3}&&\\
\vdots&\;\vdots&\;\vdots&\;\ddots&\\
x_{1}&\;x_{2}&\;x_{3}&\;\cdots&\;x_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N},

we have

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(M\right)=\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)M=\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)E\odot M=E_{1}\odot M\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N};

(11)

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(M\right)=M\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)=M\odot E\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)=M\odot E_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}.

The last set membership can be derived by the closure of
\left(\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N},\;\odot\right)
proved in
theorem

4
. Hence,
f_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}
are maps from
\mathcal{C}_{L}^{N}
to itself.

Injectiveness: For any two matrices
A=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\;\hat{r})
and
B=\textsc{L-CoLT}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}})
, let
D_{1}
be the inverse of
E_{1}
and

be the inverse of

. If

which means
f_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\cdot\right)
and

Surjectiveness: For any

, let
Q_{1}=D_{1}\odot M
and
Q_{2}=M\odot D_{2}
, then

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(Q_{1}\right)=E_{1}\odot D_{1}\odot M=E\odot M=M;

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(Q_{2}\right)=M\odot D_{2}\odot E_{2}=M\odot E=M,

which means
f_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\cdot\right)
and
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\cdot\right)
are surjective functions.

Corollary 7
.

f_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\cdot\right)
and
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\cdot\right)
are permutations in
\mathcal{C}^{N}
.

4
The Fast Sinkhorn II

In this section, we will discuss the implementation details to accelerate IPOT. In
algorithm

1
, three parts lead to
O(N^{2})
algorithm complexity, i.e., the matrix Hadamard product (line 4), the matrix-vector multiplication (lines 6-7), and the matrix scaling (line 8). They all rely on the representation and manipulation of L/U CoLT matrices.

For two discrete distributions on a 1D uniform mesh grid with a grid spacing of
h
, by introducing the notation
\lambda=e^{-h/\delta}
, the kernel matrix
K
is written as

K=\begin{pmatrix}1&\lambda&\lambda^{2}&\cdots&\lambda^{N-1}\\
\lambda&1&\lambda&\cdots&\lambda^{N-2}\\
\lambda^{2}&\lambda&1&\cdots&\lambda^{N-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda^{N-1}&\lambda^{N-2}&\lambda^{N-3}&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}^{N}.

(12)

Below we discuss step by step of IPOT (
algorithm

1
) to reduce the complexity:

•

line 2:
\Gamma=\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}^{T}_{N}\in\mathcal{C}^{N}
, we only need four vectors
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
to represent
\Gamma
according to
theorem

2
.

•

line 4: the matrix Hadamard product
Q=K\odot\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{N}
according to
theorem

4
and
corollary

5
. By updating the four representation vectors
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
, we can obtain
Q
with
O(N)
cost.

•

lines 6-7: the matrix-vector multiplication
Q^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
Q\boldsymbol{\psi}
can be implemented with
O(N)
cost according to
algorithm

4
.

•

line 8: the matrix scaling
\Gamma=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\phi})Q\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\mathcal{C}^{N}
according to
theorem

6
and
corollary

7
. By updating the four representation vectors
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
, we can obtain
\Gamma
with
O(N)
cost.

Based on the above discussions, we proposed the FS-2 algorithm with
O(N)
complexity. The pseudo-code is presented in

.

There is a minor flaw in the above algorithm. The computational cost of
W_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
is still
O(N^{2})
in the last step. This was also ignored in our previous paper
[
24
]
. Now, we would like to discuss this issue. The computation of
W_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right)=\left\langle C,\;\Gamma\right\rangle
can be regarded as the summation of all elements of the following matrix.

\small C\odot\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}0&h\gamma^{\prime}_{1}&2h\gamma^{\prime}_{2}r^{\prime}_{1}&\cdots&(\!N\!-\!1\!)h\gamma^{\prime}_{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\!N\!-\!2\!}\!r^{\prime}_{i}\\
h\gamma_{1}r_{1}&0&h\gamma^{\prime}_{2}&\cdots&(\!N\!-\!2\!)h\gamma^{\prime}_{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!\prod\limits_{i=2}^{\!N\!-\!2\!}\!r^{\prime}_{i}\\
2h\gamma_{1}r_{1}r_{2}&h\gamma_{2}r_{2}&0&\cdots&(\!N\!-\!3\!)h\gamma^{\prime}_{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!\prod\limits_{i=3}^{\!N\!-\!2\!}\!r^{\prime}_{i}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\left(\!N\!-\!1\!\right)h\gamma_{1}\!\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!r_{i}&\;\;\left(\!N\!-\!2\!\right)h\gamma_{2}\!\prod\limits_{i=2}^{\!N\!-\!1\!}\!r_{i}&\;\;\left(\!N\!-\!3\!\right)h\gamma_{3}\!\prod\limits_{i=3}^{N\!-\!1}\!r_{i}&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

We separate the summation of the matrix to the lower and strictly upper triangular parts. Thus, the
k
-th line summation of two parts can be written as

p_{k}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{ki},\quad q_{k}=\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{N}\omega_{ki}.

We can consider the following recursive computation

\displaystyle p_{1}=0,\quad p_{2}=h\gamma_{1}r_{1},\quad p^{\prime}_{2}=h\gamma_{1}r_{1}+h\gamma_{2},

(14)

\displaystyle p_{i}=r_{i-1}\left(p_{i-1}+p^{\prime}_{i-1}\right),\quad p^{\prime}_{i}=r_{i-1}p^{\prime}_{i-1}+h\gamma_{i},\quad i=3,\;4,\cdots,\;N.

\displaystyle q_{N}=0,\quad q_{N-1}=h\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1},\quad q^{\prime}_{N-1}=h\gamma^{\prime}_{N-1}+h,

\displaystyle q_{j}=r^{\prime}_{j}\left(q_{j+1}+q^{\prime}_{j+1}\right),\quad q^{\prime}_{j}=r^{\prime}_{j}q^{\prime}_{j+1}+h,\quad j=1,\;2,\cdots,\;N-2.

Thus, the Wasserstein-1 metric can be finally obtained with
O(N)
cost

W_{1}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\left\langle C,\;\Gamma\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(p_{i}+q_{i}\right).

5
Extension to high dimension

In this section, we illustrate how the FS-2 algorithm generalizes to higher dimensions using the two-dimensional case as an example.

5.1
Block Collinear Triangular Matrix

Hereinafter, for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{MN\times MN}
, we break it into
M^{2}
uniform blocks with size
N\times N
:

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c}A_{1,1}&A_{1,2}&A_{1,3}&\cdots&A_{1,M}\\
\hline\cr A_{2,1}&A_{2,2}&A_{2,3}&\cdots&A_{2,M}\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\hline\cr A_{M,1}&A_{M,2}&A_{M,3}&\cdots&A_{M,M}\end{array}\right).

And for vectors
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{kN}
, we break it into
k
uniform blocks as
\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\right)^{T}
, in which

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\left(x_{1+(i-1)N},\;x_{2+(i-1)N},\;\cdots,\;x_{iN}\right)^{T},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,k.

To carry out the fast implementation of the matrix-vector multiplication of the block matrix above, we generalize the definition of
\mathcal{C}^{N}
in (
8
) to the block case as

Definition 8
.

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{C}^{N,M}=\{A\in&\mathbb{R}^{MN\times MN}|A_{k,k}\in\mathcal{C}^{N};\;\boldsymbol{r}^{L},\boldsymbol{r}^{U}\in\left(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}\right)^{\left(M-1\right)N};\\
&A_{i+1,j}=\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{L}_{i}\right)\right)A_{i,j},\;j\leq i;\;A_{i-1,j}=\left(\text{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{r}^{U}_{i-1}\right)\right)A_{i,j},\;i\leq j\}\end{aligned}.

Since
\mathcal{C}^{N,M}
is a generalization of
\mathcal{C}^{N}
, we can also use the strategy of
algorithm

4
in blocks to reduce the computational cost of matrix-vector multiplications. For a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{NM}
, the matrix-vector multiplication
A\boldsymbol{x}
is written as

A\boldsymbol{x}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1,1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}&+&A_{1,2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}&+&A_{1,3}\boldsymbol{x}_{3}&\cdots&+&A_{1,M}\boldsymbol{x}_{M}\\
A_{2,1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}&+&A_{2,2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}&+&A_{2,3}\boldsymbol{x}_{3}&\cdots&+&A_{2,M}\boldsymbol{x}_{M}\\
A_{3,1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}&+&A_{3,2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}&+&A_{3,3}\boldsymbol{x}_{3}&\cdots&+&A_{3,M}\boldsymbol{x}_{M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{M,1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}&+&A_{M,2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}&+&A_{M,3}\boldsymbol{x}_{3}&\cdots&+&A_{M,M}\boldsymbol{x}_{M}\end{pmatrix}.

(15)

We separate the summation of row
k
to the lower triangular part
\boldsymbol{p}_{k}
and the strictly upper triangular part
\boldsymbol{q}_{k}
. Then computing
A\boldsymbol{x}
is formulated as

A\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{p}+\boldsymbol{q},\quad\boldsymbol{p}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{k,i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\quad\boldsymbol{q}_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{M}A_{k,i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\quad k=1,\cdots,M.

If
A
is in
\mathcal{C}^{N,M}
with
\boldsymbol{r}^{L}
and
\boldsymbol{r}^{U}
, instead of directly calculating
\boldsymbol{p}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{q}_{k}
, a successive computation is used

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{1}=A_{1,1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{p}_{k}=\boldsymbol{r}^{L}_{k-1}\odot\boldsymbol{p}_{k-1}+A_{k,k}\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\quad k=2,\cdots,M,

(16)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{M}=\mathbf{0}_{N},\quad\boldsymbol{q}_{k-1}=\boldsymbol{r}^{U}_{k-1}\odot(\boldsymbol{q}_{k}+A_{k,k}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}),\quad k=M,M-1,\cdots,2.

Since the computation of
A_{k,k}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
can be carried out with
O(N)
complexity by using
algorithm

4
, the whole computation is of
O(NM)
complexity.

Similar to
theorem

4
and
theorem

6
,
\mathcal{C}^{N,M}
is closed under the Hadamard product and matrix scaling.

Theorem 9
.

\left(\mathcal{C}^{N,M},\odot\right)
is an Abelian group; Matrix scaling operations are permutations in
\mathcal{C}^{N,M}
.

Proof.

For any
A\in\mathcal{C}^{N,M}
, since the diagonal blocks of
A
are in
\mathcal{C}^{N}
, by
corollary

7
, all blocks in
A
are in
\mathcal{C}^{N}
. Then the two properties can be proved in a similar way as in
section

3.2
.

5.2
The 2D FS-2 Algorithm

Consider two discretized probabilistic distributions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\left(u_{11},u_{21},\cdots,u_{N1},u_{12},\cdots,u_{i_{1}j_{1}},\cdots,u_{NM}\right),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\left(v_{11},v_{21},\cdots,v_{N1},v_{12},\cdots,v_{i_{2}j_{2}},\cdots,v_{NM}\right),

on a uniform 2D mesh of size
N\times M
with a vertical spacing of
h_{1}
and a horizontal spacing of
h_{2}
. The corresponding kernel matrix is written as

K=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c}K_{0}&\lambda_{2}K_{0}&\lambda_{2}^{2}K_{0}&\cdots&\lambda_{2}^{M-1}K_{0}\\
\hline\cr\lambda_{2}K_{0}&K_{0}&\lambda_{2}K_{0}&\cdots&\lambda_{2}^{M-2}K_{0}\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\hline\cr\lambda_{2}^{M-1}K_{0}&\lambda_{2}^{M-2}K_{0}&\lambda_{2}^{M-3}K_{0}&\cdots&K_{0}\end{array}\right),

where the sub-matrix

K_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{1}^{N-1}\\
\lambda_{1}&1&\cdots&\lambda_{1}^{N-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{1}^{N-1}&\lambda_{1}^{N-2}&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix},

and

\lambda_{1}=e^{-h_{1}/\delta},\quad\lambda_{2}=e^{-h_{2}/\delta}.

Obviously, the original 2D kernel
K
contains blocks which are multiples of the 1D kernel, hence belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{N,M}
. By an analysis similar to that in
section

4
and using
theorem

9
, the matrices
Q
and
\Gamma
are in
\mathcal{C}^{N,M}
throughout the course of the iteration, which means that all the matrix-vector multiplications can be carried out by using recursion (
16
). Thus, the total cost of matrix-vector multiplication of our FS-2 algorithm for 2D Wasserstein-1 metric is reduced to
O(NM)
.

In the 2D FS-2 algorithm, we use ‘
\widehat{}
’ to distinguish whether it is a coefficient of the block or the inner sub-matrix, and update them simultaneously after an inner loop. The pseudo-code is presented in
algorithm

6
. Updating the coefficients of inner sub-matrices and computation of
W_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right)
are omitted in the pseudo-code since they are similar to the 1D case, which we have described in detail.

Conclusion

As the follow-up of the FS-1 paper, we generalize the result of matrix-vector multiplication at
O(N)
costs for the special matrix to the more general L/U-CoLT matrix. We illustrate that only two vectors are required to represent any L/U-CoLT matrix. Moreover, we also prove the closure of families of L/U-CoLT matrices to matrix Hadamard product and matrix scaling. Therefore, the above matrix operations are essentially updating the representation vectors, which reduce both time and space complexity to
O(N)
. These results can be directly applied to the Inexact Proximal point method for Optimal Transport problem and reduce the overall computational complexity to O(N). From this, we develop the Fast Sinkhorn II algorithm. It does not seem to be an overstatement that for the computation of the Wasserstein-1 metric, we have probably obtained the most competitive method, both in terms of convergence speed and computational complexity.
