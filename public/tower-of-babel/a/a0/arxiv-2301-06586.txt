A New Formula for the Determinant and Bounds on Its Tensor and Waring Ranks
Robin Houston, Adam P. Goucher, Nathaniel Johnston

Abstract
We present a new explicit formula for the determinant that contains superexponentially fewer terms than the usual Leibniz formula. As an immediate corollary of our formula, we show that the tensor rank of the n × n n\times n determinant tensor is no larger than the n n -th Bell number, which is much smaller than the previously best known upper bounds when n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 . Over fields of non-zero characteristic we obtain even tighter upper bounds, and we also slightly improve the known lower bounds. In particular, we show that the 4 × 4 4\times 4 determinant over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} has tensor rank exactly equal to 12 12 . Our results also improve upon the best known upper bound for the Waring rank of the determinant when n ≥ 17 n\geq 17 , and lead to a new family of axis-aligned polytopes that tile ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} .

Introduction

The determinant and permanent of an
n\times n
matrix
A
are defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{per}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma(i)}\right),

(1)

respectively, where
S_{n}
is the symmetric group over the set
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. There are numerous other explicit formulas for the permanent of a matrix, such as Ryser’s formula
[
BR91
]

\displaystyle\operatorname{per}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\left(\operatorname{sgn}(S)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in S}a_{i,j}\right),

(2)

where
\operatorname{sgn}(S)=(-1)^{|S|+n}
, as well as Glynn’s formula
[
Gly10
]

\displaystyle\operatorname{per}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{\delta}\left(\operatorname{sgn}(\delta)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i}a_{i,j}\right),

(3)

where
\operatorname{sgn}(\delta)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{k}
and the outer sum ranges over all vectors
\delta\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
with
\delta_{1}=1
.

These alternative formulas for the permanent, despite looking more complicated than the defining formula of Equation (
1

n
factors, with each factor in the product being a linear combination of entries from a single row of
A
. One of the advantages of the formulae in Equations (
2
) and (

2^{n-1}
, respectively, instead of the
n!

On the other hand, while there are numerous known methods of computing the determinant of a matrix, most of them do not provide an explicit formula with fewer terms being summed than the defining formula of Equation (
1
). For example, cofactor expansions are just factored forms of Equation (
1
) that still consist of a sum of
n!
terms, and most numerical methods (e.g., those based on Gaussian elimination or matrix decompositions) are iterative (see
[
Rot01
]
, for example) and/or require division by entries of the matrix. The only progress in the direction of finding more efficient explicit formulas for the determinant that we are aware of comes from the fact that, when
n=3
, there are several ways that are known to write the determinant as a sum of just
5

3!=6
(see
[
Der16
,
IT16
,
KM21
]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{det}(A)&=(a_{1,2}+a_{1,3})(a_{2,1}+a_{2,3})(a_{3,1}+a_{3,2})\\
&\quad-a_{1,2}a_{2,1}(a_{3,1}+a_{3,2}+a_{3,3})\\
&\quad-a_{1,3}(a_{2,1}+a_{2,2}+a_{2,3})a_{3,1}\\
&\quad-(a_{1,1}+a_{1,2}+a_{1,3})a_{2,3}a_{3,2}\\
&\quad+a_{1,1}a_{2,2}a_{3,3}.\end{split}

(4)

Derksen noticed that, when combined with cofactor expansions, formulas like Equation (

determinant that consist of a sum of

[
Der16
]
. This formula has the fewest terms in the sum known until now, except over fields of characteristic

, where
\operatorname{det}(A)=\operatorname{per}(A)
(since
-1=1
) and Ryser’s formula of Equation (
2
) is a sum of just
2^{n}-1
terms.

Glynn’s

-term formula of Equation (

Our main contribution is to present a new explicit formula for the determinant (Theorem

1.1
Tensor Rank

We can regard the
n\times n
determinant over a field
\mathbb{F}
as a tensor living in
(\mathbb{F}^{n})^{\otimes n}
, and we can then ask questions of it like we ask of any tensor. In particular, we can ask what its
tensor rank
is
[
Lan12
]
. That is, if we use
\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}\in(\mathbb{F}^{n})^{\otimes n}
to denote the
n\times n
determinant tensor over the field
\mathbb{F}
, what is the least integer
r
for which there exist
\{\mathbf{v_{j,k}}\}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n}
with

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathbf{v_{1,k}}\otimes\mathbf{v_{2,k}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{v_{n,k}}?

(5)

We denote tensor rank (i.e., minimal
r
) by
\operatorname{Trank}
, so the tensor rank of the determinant is denoted by
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})
.

Our interest in the tensor rank comes from the fact that every determinant formula present in this paper corresponds to a tensor decomposition of the form of Equation (
5
) by replacing each occurrence of
a_{i,j}
in the formula by
\mathbf{e_{j}}
(the
j
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
) in the
i
-th tensor factor of the tensor decomposition. For example, the defining formula (
1
) corresponds to the tensor decomposition

\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbf{e}_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\sigma(2)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{e}_{\sigma(n)},

which shows that
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq|S_{n}|=n!
. Similarly, the formula for the
3\times 3
determinant from Equation (
4
) immediately gives us the following tensor decomposition of
\operatorname{det}^{3}_{\mathbb{F}}
, which demonstrates that
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{3}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 5
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}^{3}_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{3}})\otimes(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{3}})\otimes(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{2}})

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbf{e_{2}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{1}}\otimes(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{3}})

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbf{e_{3}}\otimes(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{3}})\otimes\mathbf{e_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad-(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{3}})\otimes\mathbf{e_{3}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbf{e_{1}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{2}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{3}}.

In fact, it is known that
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{3}_{\mathbb{F}})=5
over every field
[
KM21
]
, so this decomposition is optimal. When
n\geq 4
, the exact value of
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})
is not known, and until now the best known upper bounds on it were exactly the bounds that we discussed earlier:
2

2

It was mentioned in
[
KM21
]
that
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{5}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 20
and
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{7}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 100
, but these are typographical errors; the authors meant
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{4}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 20
and
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{5}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 100

.

\displaystyle\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})

\displaystyle\leq(5/6)^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}n!

for all fields
\mathbb{F}
, and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})

has characteristic

Our formula improves upon these bounds by showing that

1.2
Waring Rank

Another notion of the rank of the determinant comes from thinking of it as a homogeneous polynomial in the
n^{2}
entries of the matrix on which it acts. That is, we can think of the determinant as the following degree-
n
polynomial in the
n^{2}
variables
\{x_{i,j}\}
(we abuse notation slightly and use
\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}
to refer to both this polynomial, as well as the tensor from Equation (
5
), but which one we mean will always be clear from context):

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}(x_{1,1},x_{1,2},\ldots,x_{n,n})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\left(\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,\sigma(i)}\right).

(6)

We are interested in the
Waring rank
of this polynomial. That is, what is the least integer
r
for which there exist linear forms
\{\ell_{k}\}\subset\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{F}^{n\times n},\mathbb{F})
and scalars
\{c_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{F}
with

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}c_{k}\ell_{k}^{n}?

(7)

We denote the Waring rank of this determinant polynomial by
\operatorname{Wrank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})
.

For example, if
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic not equal to
2
then the Waring rank of the degree-
2
polynomial
xy
is
2
, since

xy=\frac{1}{4}\left((x+y)^{2}-(x-y)^{2}\right),

is a linear combination of
2
squares of linear forms, and it is not possible to write
xy
as a linear combination (i.e., scalar multiple) of the square of just a single linear form. Similarly, it is well-known that if the characteristic of
\mathbb{F}
is
0
or strictly larger than
n
then the Waring rank of the
n
-variable polynomial
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
is exactly
2^{n-1}
as shown in
[
RS11
]
.
3
3

3

If the characteristic is between
2
and
n
(inclusive) then the Waring rank of
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
is infinite: it cannot be written as a linear combination of
any
number of
n
-th powers of linear forms.
By replacing each term in a formula for the determinant with a linear combination of
2^{n-1}
different
n
-th powers of linear forms, we immediately get the following (also well-known) simple relationship between the tensor and Waring ranks of the determinant:

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field with characteristic
p
. If
p=0
or
p>n
then

\operatorname{Wrank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 2^{n-1}\cdot\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}}).

When combined with the bounds on
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})
from Section
1.1
, this lemma tells us that if
\mathbb{F}
is a field with characteristic
0
or strictly larger than
n
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Wrank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n-1}(5/6)^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}n!.

(8)

This upper bound was improved in
[
JT22
]
, in the case when
\mathbb{F}
also contains a primitive root of unity (e.g., if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
), to

\displaystyle\operatorname{Wrank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot n!.

(9)

Our formula improves upon these bounds by showing that
\operatorname{Wrank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 2^{n-1}\cdot B_{n}
, even without the primitive root of unity assumption (Corollary
3
). Our bound is strictly better (i.e., smaller) than the one provided by Inequality (
8
) for all
n\geq 4
, and is better than the one provided by Inequality (
9
) for all
n\geq 17
.

1.3
Arrangement of the Paper

In Section
2
, we present our main contribution, which is a new formula for the determinant of a matrix (Theorem
2
). As an immediate corollary, we obtain our new field-independent upper bounds on the tensor and Waring ranks of the determinant (Corollary

We present two independent proofs of our formula. First, we present a combinatorial proof in Section

, thereby proving the result in full generality over arbitrary commutative rings. This proof also demonstrates some new axis-aligned polytope tilings of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the 1-skeleta of the polytopes can be naturally identified with flip graphs for ordered partial partitions.

In Section
5
, we investigate what our formula says about the tensor rank of the determinant over fields with non-zero characteristic, and we obtain tighter upper bounds than the field-independent one (Corollaries
11
,
12
, and
13
). Finally, in Section
6
we (very slightly) improve upon the best known

(Theorem

), demonstrating optimality of our formula in this case.

The Formula

Theorem 2

Let

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}(A)

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{P\in\operatorname{PP}(n)}\ \ \operatorname{sgn}(P)|P|!\prod_{i=1}^{n}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\ \ \sum_{j\underset{P}{\sim}i,j\neq i}a_{i,j}&\textrm{ if $i\underset{P}{\sim}i$;}\\
\displaystyle a_{i,i}+\sum_{j\underset{P}{\sim}j}a_{i,j}&\textrm{ if $i\underset{P}{\not\sim}i$,}\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\operatorname{sgn}(P)=\prod_{S\in P}(-1)^{|S|+1}

[n]

. We can compute

Combinatorial Proof

We now present a combinatorial proof of Theorem
2
. This proof works by just brute-force showing that the formula (
16
), when expanded as a linear combination of monomials, gives the exact same quantity as the defining formula (
1
). More precisely, let
f:[n]\rightarrow[n]
be a function (not necessarily a permutation). Our goal is to show that the coefficient of
a_{1,f(1)}a_{2,f(2)}\cdots a_{n,f(n)}
is the same in Equation (
16
) as it is in the defining formula for the determinant (
1
).

To this end we say that a partial partition
P\in\operatorname{PP}(n)
is
algebraically compatible
with
f
if, for all
i\in[n]
, we have the following two properties:

(
\alpha
)

If
i\underset{P}{\sim}i
then
f(i)\neq i
and
f(i)\underset{P}{\sim}i
, and

(
\beta
)

If
i\underset{P}{\not\sim}i
then
f(i)=i
or
f(i)\underset{P}{\sim}f(i)
.

If we let
\operatorname{ACPP}(f)
denote the set of partial partitions that are algebraically compatible with
f
, then Equation (
16

c_{f}
of the coefficient of
a_{1,f(1)}a_{2,f(2)}\cdots a_{n,f(n)}
in an expansion of the determinant is equal to

\displaystyle c_{f}=\ \ \sum_{P\in\operatorname{ACPP}(f)}\ \ \operatorname{sgn}(P)|P|!.

(18)

Lemma 4
.

Let
c_{f}
be the coefficient of
a_{1,f(1)}a_{2,f(2)}\cdots a_{n,f(n)}
after expanding the polynomial in the right-hand side of Equation (

c_{f}=\operatorname{sgn}(f)

To prove Lemma

Case 1:

As

Note that

j
(otherwise), then
P^{\prime}\in\operatorname{ACPP}(f)
and has the
opposite sign to
P
. This defines an involution on
\operatorname{ACPP}(f)
mapping each
algebraically compatible partial partition to one of opposite sign, so

c_{f}=0
.

Case 2:

f
is a permutation.

We can write
f
as a product of disjoint cycles of length at least
2
:
f=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{k}
. Each cycle
\sigma=(i_{1}\ i_{2}\ \ldots\ i_{\ell})
corresponds naturally to a subset
S_{\sigma}:=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}\subseteq[n]
(though this correspondence is many-to-one since the order of the entries
\sigma
matters, whereas it does not matter in
S_{\sigma}
). Similarly, from
f
we can build the partial partition
P_{f}:=\{S_{\sigma_{1}},S_{\sigma_{2}},\ldots,S_{\sigma_{k}}\}
.

If
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
are in the same cycle of
f
then, for any
P\in\operatorname{ACPP}(f)
we have
i_{1}\underset{P}{\sim}i_{2}
. It follows that
P\in\operatorname{ACPP}(f)
if and only if
P=P_{f}
or
P
can be obtained from
P_{f}
by unioning together some of its parts. In other words, there exists a (non-partial) partition
K=\{K_{1},\ldots,K_{m}\}
of
[k]
such that

\displaystyle P=\left\{\bigcup_{i\in K_{j}}S_{\sigma_{i}}:j\in[m]\right\}.

(19)

If
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{m}
denotes the
(k,m)
-th Stirling number of the 2nd kind, then there are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{m}
partitions of
[k]
with exactly
m
parts, so there are
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{m}
partial partitions
P
of the form (
19
) with exactly
m
parts. Each one has
|P|=m
and
\operatorname{sgn}(P)=(-1)^{k+m}\operatorname{sgn}(f)
, so Equation (
18
) can be written more explicitly as

\displaystyle\begin{split}c_{f}&=\ \ \sum_{P\in\operatorname{ACPP}(f)}\ \ \operatorname{sgn}(P)|P|!\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{k}(-1)^{k+m}\operatorname{sgn}(f)\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{m}m!\\
&=(-1)^{k}\operatorname{sgn}(f)\left(\sum_{m=1}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{m}(-1)^{m}m!\right).\end{split}

(20)

We now plug
x=-1
into the well-known formula

\sum_{m=1}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{m}x(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-m+1)=x^{k}

to see that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{m}(-1)^{m}m!=(-1)^{k}.

(21)

Substituting Equation (
21
) into the bottom line of Equation (

) shows that
c_{f}=(-1)^{2k}\operatorname{sgn}(f)=\operatorname{sgn}(f)
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 7

4.3
The Polytope
\mathbf{F_{A}}

For an
n\times n
matrix
A
satisfying the conditions (a)–(c), we defined
F_{A}
in Equation (
22
) by subtracting translated copies of the cuboid
C_{A}
from the original cuboid
C_{A}
. In this subsection, we study the polytope
F_{A}
and its 1-skeleton (i.e., its vertices and
1
-dimensional edges between them; see Figure
5
), obtaining a description of it as a flip graph associated with a family of combinatorial objects.

To start, we describe the combinatorial objects that will be the vertices of the graph. An
ordered partial partition
(
OPP
) on
[n]
is defined to be a relation
\preccurlyeq
with the following properties:

(i)

Transitive: if
x\preccurlyeq y
and
y\preccurlyeq z
, then
x\preccurlyeq z
.

(ii)

Weakly reflexive: if
x\preccurlyeq y
, then
x\preccurlyeq x
and
y\preccurlyeq y
.

(iii)

Weakly connected: if
x\preccurlyeq x
and
y\preccurlyeq y
, then either
x\preccurlyeq y
or
y\preccurlyeq x
or both.

The set of all ordered partial partitions on
[n]
is denoted by
\operatorname{OPP}(n)
and the number of them is enumerated in
[
SKS96
]
.

The name “ordered partial partition” refers to the fact that these relations on
[n]
correspond bijectively with (ordered, possibly empty) tuples of pairwise disjoint non-empty subsets of
[n]
(i.e., partial partitions of
[n]
in which we care about the order of the parts):

(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k}),

where
X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset
whenever
i\neq j
. This tuple of sets corresponds to the original relation in the following way:
x\preccurlyeq y
if and only if there exist integers
1\leq i\leq j
such that
x\in X_{i}
and
y\in X_{j}
.

For each
z\in[n]
, we define a function
f_{z}:\operatorname{OPP}(n)\rightarrow\operatorname{OPP}(n)
as follows:

(1)

If
X_{1}=\{z\}
then discard
X_{1}
, obtaining the tuple
(X_{2},\ldots,X_{k})
.

(2)

If
X_{j}=\{z\}
for some
j\geq 2
, then merge it into the immediately preceding part, obtaining the tuple

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{j-1}\cup\{z\},X_{j+1},\ldots,X_{k}).

(3)

If
z\in X_{j}\neq\{z\}
for some
j\geq 1
, then split it off into its own immediately next part, obtaining the tuple

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{j}\setminus\{z\},\{z\},X_{j+1},\ldots,X_{k}).

(4)

Otherwise, if
z\notin\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}X_{j}
, then make it into its own first part, obtaining the tuple

(\{z\},X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}).

For each
z\in[n]
, this function
f_{z}
is an involution on
\operatorname{OPP}(n)
with no fixed points: if
f_{z}(\preccurlyeq)=\preccurlyeq^{\prime}
, then
f_{z}(\preccurlyeq^{\prime})=\preccurlyeq
. Moreover, if
x,y\in[n]
with
x\neq z
, then
x\preccurlyeq y
if and only if
x\preccurlyeq^{\prime}y
.

We form a graph (called a

and its edges be between ordered partial partitions that are exchanged by an involution

for some

(see Figure

Proposition 8
.

Suppose that

are in bijective correspondence with the elements of

Proof.

Recall that we originally defined

by subtracting translated copies of the cuboid

:

\displaystyle F_{A}:=\overline{C_{A}\setminus\bigcup_{\emptyset\neq S\subseteq[n]}\left\{x+\sum_{i\in S}A\mathbf{e_{i}}:x\in C_{A}\right\}}.

However,

C_{A}=\overline{P\setminus\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\big\{x+A\mathbf{e_{i}}:x\in P\big\}},

28

still works even if we replace each instance of

Given a vertex

of

\mathbf{e_{i}}

must be contained in a facet of one of the translates of

is then the vertex of the intersection of these

Each vertex of

that arises therefore corresponds to the vertex of an intersection of translated positive orthants, which is the lowest (in terms of each coordinate) vertex of the corresponding intersection of translated copies of
C_{A}
. We already characterised these intersections in the proof of Lemma
7
when we performed the inclusion-exclusion calculation of the volume of

, identifying them with chains of subsets of
[n]
, which are in turn naturally identified with ordered partial partitions.
7

In the proof of Lemma

Proposition 9
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix satisfying conditions (a)–(c) and let

. Then two vertices
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
of
F_{A}
are connected by an edge parallel to the standard basis vector
\mathbf{e_{i}}
if and only if the corresponding (in the sense of Proposition
8
) elements of
\operatorname{OPP}(n)
are swapped by the involution
f_{i}
.

Proof.

Combinatorially, the polytope
F_{A}
does not depend on the choice of matrix
A
(besides the fact that
A
satisfies conditions (a)–(c)), so suppose without loss of generality that the entries are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Then we have that
v_{k}=w_{k}
if and only if
\sum_{j:k\preccurlyeq j}a_{k,j}=\sum_{j:k\preccurlyeq^{\prime}j}a_{k,j}
if and only if

\displaystyle\{j:k\preccurlyeq j\}=\{j:k\preccurlyeq^{\prime}j\}.

(29)

Consequently, two vertices lie on a line parallel to the basis vector
\mathbf{e_{i}}
if and only if Equation (
29

.

This implies that the ordered partial partitions induced on
[n]\setminus\{i\}
are equal, so the original ordered partial partitions can only differ in terms of where
i
is located. It also constrains the position of
i
relative to the other parts, namely

\displaystyle\{k\neq i:k\preccurlyeq i\}=\{k\neq i:k\preccurlyeq^{\prime}i\}.

(30)

If we remove and reinsert
i
, there are two possibilities: it is either introduced as its own singleton part, or it is not (either by being introduced into an existing part, or being deleted completely). In each of these two cases, the position of
i
is determined by Equation (
30

\preccurlyeq^{\prime}=\preccurlyeq
, while the other corresponds to
\preccurlyeq^{\prime}=f_{i}(\preccurlyeq)
.

Given that
F_{A}
is an orthogonal polytope, it follows that there are at most
n

\mathbf{v}
by an edge, namely the vertices obtained by applying
f_{i}
to the corresponding element of
\operatorname{OPP}(n)
. Moreover, every vertex in an orthogonal polytope must have degree

∎

We can label the edges

of the flip graph, as in Figure

This description of

5
Fields of Non-Zero Characteristic

For fields
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
p>0
, many of the terms in the formula (
16
) are multiples of
p
and thus equal to zero. As a result, the formula simplifies and we get an even tighter upper bound on the tensor rank of
\operatorname{det}_{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
. In particular, we have the following variant of Theorem
2
:

Theorem 10
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix over a field
\mathbb{F}
with characteristic
p>0
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}(A)

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\operatorname{PP}(n)\\
|P|\leq p-1\end{subarray}}\ \ \operatorname{sgn}(P)|P|!\prod_{i=1}^{n}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\ \ \sum_{j\underset{P}{\sim}i,j\neq i}a_{i,j}&\textrm{ if $i\underset{P}{\sim}i$;}\\
\displaystyle a_{i,i}+\sum_{j\underset{P}{\sim}j}a_{i,j}&\textrm{ if $i\underset{P}{\not\sim}i$,}\end{cases}

(31)

where
\displaystyle\operatorname{sgn}(P)=\prod_{S\in P}(-1)^{|S|+1}
.

Proof.

This formula is identical to the one provided by Theorem
2
, except the partial partitions
P
that we sum over here are restricted to have
|P|\leq p-1
. Since any term with
|P|\geq p
has a coefficient of
|P|!
, which is a multiple of
p
, which equals
0
in
\mathbb{F}
, this restriction on
|P|
does not change the value of the sum.
∎

In order to count the number of non-zero terms in the sum (
31
), and thus obtain a tighter an upper bound on
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})
when
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic
p>0
, we need to introduce another combinatorial quantity. Let
B_{n,k}
be the number of partial partitions of
[n]
that contain exactly
k
parts and no singletons, or equivalently the number of partitions of
[n]
that contain exactly
k
parts all of which have size strictly greater than
1
. The sum in Equation (
31
) contains exactly
\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}B_{n,k}
(potentially) non-zero terms, so we immediately get the following corollary:

Corollary 11
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field with characteristic
p>0
. Then
\displaystyle\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}B_{n,k}
.

By making use of Lemma
1
, we could also say that if
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic
p>n
then
\operatorname{Wrank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq 2^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}B_{n,k}
. However, this provides no improvement over Corollary
3
, since
\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}B_{n,k}=B_{n}
whenever
p>n
(in fact, whenever
p>\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
, since no partial partition can contain more than
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
parts unless it has some singletons).

Numerous properties and formulas for
B_{n,k}
are known (see
[
Deu06
,
NRB20
]
and the references therein). We summarize those that are most relevant for us here:

(i)

B_{n,0}=1
.

(ii)

B_{n,1}=\binom{n}{2}+\binom{n}{3}+\cdots+\binom{n}{n}=2^{n}-n-1
, since
\binom{n}{k}
counts the number of partial partitions of
[n]
with one part of size
k
. It follows that if
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic
2
then
\operatorname{Trank}(\operatorname{det}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}})\leq B_{n,0}+B_{n,1}=2^{n}-n
(we return to this special case in more detail in Section
5.1
).

(iii)

The recurrence relation
B_{n,k}=(k+1)B_{n-1,k}+(n-1)B_{n-2,k-1}
holds whenever
n\geq 2k\geq 2
. When combined with the facts that
B_{n,0}=1
for all
n\geq 0
and
B_{n,k}=0
whenever
n<2k
, this recurrence relation can be used to compute
B_{n,k}
for all
n
and
k
.

(iv)

In particular, for small values of
k
we have the following additional explicit formulas:

\displaystyle B_{n,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(3^{n}-(n+2)2^{n}+(n^{2}+n+1)\big),

\displaystyle B_{n,3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\big(4^{n}-(n+3)3^{n}+3(n^{2}+3n+4)2^{n-2}-(n^{3}+2n+1)\big),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle B_{n,4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}\big(5^{n}-(n+4)4^{n}+2(n^{2}+5n+9)3^{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-(n^{3}+3n^{2}+8n+8)2^{n-1}+(n^{4}-2n^{3}+5n^{2}+1)\big).

(v)

B_{n,k}\leq(k+1)^{n}/k!
(furthermore, for fixed
k
we have
B_{n,k}\sim(k+1)^{n}/k!
, so this inequality is not too lossy). To verify this inequality, consider the following function
f
from the set of
P\in\operatorname{OPP}(n)
8
8

8

Recall that
\operatorname{OPP}(n)
is the set of ordered partial partitions, defined in Section
4.3
.
in which there are exactly
k
parts and no singletons to the set

:

\displaystyle f(P):=\;

\displaystyle(f_{1}(P),f_{2}(P),\ldots,f_{n}(P)),\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle f_{i}(P)\;=\;

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\text{if $i\underset{P}{\not\sim}i$},\\
j&\text{if $i$ is in the $j$-th part of $P$.}\end{cases}

Since

completely specifies the ordered partial partition

,

members of

with exactly

In summary, all of the above observations and formulas, when combined with Corollary

, but much easier to evaluate and work with) corollary:

Corollary 12

Glynn’s formula (

Before we can prove this, we begin by establishing a useful lemma about the tensor rank of members of the antisymmetric subspace:

Lemma 16
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{F}
is a field,
n,s
are positive integers, and
T
is a nonzero element of the antisymmetric subspace of
(\mathbb{F}^{n})^{\otimes s}
. Then
\operatorname{Trank}(T)\geq s

Proof.

If
s=1
, the result is immediate, because every nonzero tensor has positive tensor rank. We shall henceforth assume that
s\geq 2
.

We view the tensor
T
as a multilinear form from
(\mathbb{F}^{n})^{s}
to
\mathbb{F}
. By assumption that it is nonzero, there must exist vectors
\mathbf{x_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{x_{s}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
T(\mathbf{x_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{x_{s}})
is nonzero. By multiplying one of these vectors by an appropriate scalar, we can assume without loss of generality that
T(\mathbf{x_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{x_{s}})=1
.

Note that
\{\mathbf{x_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{x_{s}}\}
must be linearly independent. Otherwise, we could write one of the vectors as a linear combination of the others, which we will assume without loss of generality is
\mathbf{x_{1}}
. That is,
\mathbf{x_{1}}=\alpha_{2}\mathbf{x_{2}}+\cdots+\alpha_{s}\mathbf{x_{s}}
, and then we would have the following by linearity in the first argument:

T(\mathbf{x_{1}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{x_{s}})=\sum_{i=2}^{s}\alpha_{i}T(\mathbf{x_{i}},\mathbf{x_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{x_{s}}).

By antisymmetry, each term on the right-hand side vanishes, and the left-hand side equals 1, so we obtain a contradiction. As such, it follows that
\{\mathbf{x_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{x_{s}}\}
is indeed linearly independent.

For a fixed
1\leq i\leq s
, we define a covector
\mathbf{y_{i}}:\mathbb{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
by contracting
T
on its first
s-1
arguments with the elements of
\{\mathbf{x_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{x_{s}}\}\setminus\{\mathbf{x_{i}}\}
and applying an appropriate sign change:

\mathbf{y_{i}}(\mathbf{v}):=(-1)^{s-i}T(\mathbf{x_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{x_{i-1}},\mathbf{x_{i+1}},\dots,\mathbf{x_{s}},\mathbf{v})

Observe that the antisymmetry properties of
T
imply the following:

\mathbf{y_{i}}(\mathbf{x_{j}})=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{ if $i=j$;}\\
0&\textrm{ if $i\neq j$.}\end{cases}

Equivalently, if we consider the space
V
spanned by
\mathbf{x_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{x_{s}}
, then the covectors
\mathbf{y_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{y_{s}}
form a basis for
V^{\star}
, specifically the dual basis of
\mathbf{x_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{x_{s}}
.

Consequently, the matrix obtained from
T
by the flattening
(\mathbb{F}^{n})^{\otimes s}=(\mathbb{F}^{n})^{\otimes(s-1)}\otimes(\mathbb{F}^{n})
has tensor rank at least
s
, and thus
\operatorname{Trank}(T)\geq s
.
∎

We showed in the proof of Theorem

\mathbf{e_{2}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{3}}
, we get
B_{5}+B_{11}=\mathbf{e_{1}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{4}}+\mathbf{e_{4}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{1}}
.

There are only 6 different ways to write each of these rank-2 matrices as the sum of two rank-1 matrices. In particular, we must have

B_{8},B_{11}\in\{\mathbf{e_{2}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{4}},\mathbf{e_{4}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{2}},\mathbf{e_{2}}\otimes(\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{4}}),(\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{4}})\otimes\mathbf{e_{2}},(\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{4}})\otimes\mathbf{e_{4}},\mathbf{e_{4}}\otimes(\mathbf{e_{2}}+\mathbf{e_{4}})\}

and

B_{5},B_{11}\in\{\mathbf{e_{1}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{4}},\mathbf{e_{4}}\otimes\mathbf{e_{1}},\mathbf{e_{1}}\otimes(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{4}}),(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{4}})\otimes\mathbf{e_{1}},(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{4}})\otimes\mathbf{e_{4}},\mathbf{e_{4}}\otimes(\mathbf{e_{1}}+\mathbf{e_{4}})\}.

Observe that these two sets are disjoint, so
B_{11}
cannot be in both of them. This is a contradiction that eliminates this single candidate solution for
r=11
, completing the proof of Theorem
17
.
