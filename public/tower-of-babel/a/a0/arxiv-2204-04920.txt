A graphical construction of free Markov categories Thanks: The author would like to thank Tobias Fritz for very helpful discussions.
Yimu Yin

Abstract
. We describe how to perform surgeries on Joyal-Street style diagrams and thereby construct free Markov categories. We also show that the construction is functorial over ordered directed acyclic graphs.

To formulate the notion of a free Markov category, as in
[
7
]
, we first need to fix a suitable class of signatures (called “tensor schemes” in
[
5
]
), as follows.

A
graph monoid
is a directed graph
G=(V(G),A(G),s,t)
whose set of vertices come with the additional structure of a monoid. A
graph monoid homomorphism
is a graph homomorphism that is also a homomorphism between the monoids in question.

For any set

, let

denote the free monoid of words over (the alphabet)

. It is a graph monoid, with the trivial directed graph structure (no arrows). More generally, a
free graph monoid
is a triple
(G,S,g)
, where
G
is a graph monoid and

is an injection, such that the monoid
V(G)
is isomorphic to the free monoid

over

. We usually just say

is free graph monoid and leave
S
,
g
implicit in notation when they are not needed in the discussion. Also it is harmless to treat
S
as if it is a subset of

.

A
free
DD
-graph monoid
is a free graph monoid
(G,S,g)
such that, for each
a\in S
, there are distinguished arrows
a\longrightarrow aa
, called the
duplicate
on
a
, and
a\longrightarrow\emptyset
, called the
discard
on
a
.

A Markov category
C
is
free
over a free

-graph monoid

if there is a graph monoid homomorphism
i:G\longrightarrow\textup{{C}}
matching duplicates and discards such that, for any Markov category
D
, any graph monoid homomorphism
f:G\longrightarrow\textup{{D}}
matching duplicates and discards factors through
i
, that is, there is a Markov functor
F:\textup{{C}}\longrightarrow\textup{{D}}
such that

, and

is unique up to a unique monoidal natural isomorphism. The objects and morphisms in

are referred to as
generators
.

Since Markov categories are symmetric monoidal categories, the same graphical language can be used, but with the additional syntax depicted in (
1.1
), (
1.2
), and (
1.3
). This does not give the free Markov category over a free graph monoid, though, at least not for string diagrams up to isomorphisms. We need a coarser equivalence relation to accommodate the equations in (

), and (

).

Remark 1.1
.

The surgery method described in this paper works rather well in some applications where free Markov categories play an essential role, see
[
8
]
. For other monoidal categories with more complex conditions, it is not that the method does not work, it is just that the equivalence relation so constructed may be hard to parse visually, and consequently working with a graphical language does not seem to offer advantages over a symbolic one, which in a sense defeats the purpose of graphical languages.

For the construction of free Markov categories, there is a more sophisticated combinatorial approach, see
[
3
]
.

2.
Preliminaries on Joyal-Street style diagrams

The discussion below assumes familiarity with the first two chapters of
[
5
]
. We begin by recalling some definitions therein.

A
generalized topological graph

\Gamma=(\Gamma,\Gamma_{0})
consists of a Hausdorff space
\Gamma
and a finite subset
\Gamma_{0}\subseteq\Gamma
such that the complement
\Gamma\smallsetminus\Gamma_{0}
is a one-dimensional manifold without boundary and with finitely many connected components. It is
acyclic
if it has no circuits, that is, there is no embedding from the unit circle in
\mathds{R}^{2}
into
\Gamma
, in particular, each of the connected components in
\Gamma\smallsetminus\Gamma_{0}
is homeomorphic to the open unit interval
(0,1)\subseteq\mathds{R}
. We shall only consider acyclic graphs and hence may omit the qualifier for brevity.

An element of
\Gamma_{0}
is called a
node
. A connected component of
\Gamma\smallsetminus\Gamma_{0}
is called an
edge
. The set of edges of
\Gamma
is denoted by
\Gamma_{1}
, and the set
\overline{\Gamma}\smallsetminus\Gamma
by
\partial\Gamma
, where
\overline{\Gamma}
is the topological closure (compactification) of
\Gamma
. The points in
\partial\Gamma
are referred to as the
outer nodes
of
\Gamma
(nodes in
\Gamma_{0}
are also called
inner nodes
for clarity). Note that
\partial\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{0}
together form the boundary of
\Gamma
.

The compactification
\hat{e}
of an edge
e
is homeomorphic to the closed unit interval
[0,1]\subseteq\mathds{R}
. An edge
e
is called
pinned
if the inclusion
e\subseteq\Gamma
can be extended to an embedding
\hat{e}\longrightarrow\Gamma
, and
half-loose
if it can only be extended to
\hat{e}
minus one endpoint, and
loose
if neither of the previous two cases holds.

An
orientation
of an edge
e
is just a total ordering on the pair of endpoints of
\hat{e}
, where the
source

e(0)
is the image of the first element under the embedding
\hat{e}\longrightarrow\overline{\Gamma}
and the

target

e(1)
that of the last element. We say that
\Gamma
is
progressive
if it carries a choice of orientation for
each of its edges. In that case, the
input

\inn(x)
of a node
x\in\Gamma_{0}
is the set of the oriented edges with target
x
and the
output

\out(x)
the set of those with source
x
. An inner node
x\in\Gamma_{0}
is
initial
if
\inn(x)=\emptyset
and
terminal
if
\out(x)=\emptyset
. If, in addition,
\Gamma
is equipped with a choice of total orderings on
\inn(x)
and
\out(x)
for each
x\in\Gamma_{0}
then it is
polarized
. We shall only consider polarized graphs and hence may omit the qualifier for brevity.

The
domain

\dom(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
consists of the edges whose sources are outer nodes and the
codomain

\cod(\Gamma)
those whose targets are outer nodes. The edges in
\dom(\Gamma)
,
\cod(\Gamma)
are often identified with the corresponding outer nodes for convenience. We say that
\Gamma
is
anchored
if both
\dom(\Gamma)
and
\cod(\Gamma)
are equipped with a total ordering.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
C
be a symmetric strict monoidal category. A
valuation

v:\Gamma\longrightarrow\textup{{C}}
is a pair of functions

v_{1}:\Gamma_{0}\longrightarrow\textup{{C}}_{1},\quad v_{0}:\Gamma_{1}\longrightarrow\textup{{C}}_{0}

such that, for every node
x\in\Gamma_{0}
with
\inn(x)=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
and
\out(x)=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
,
v_{1}(x)
is of the form
\bigotimes_{i}v_{0}(a_{i})\longrightarrow\bigotimes_{i}v_{0}(b_{i})
; here if
n=0
or
m=0
then the monoidal product in question is just the monoidal unit
\mathbf{I}
of
C
. The pair
(\Gamma,v)
is called a
diagram
in
C
, and is denoted simply by
\Gamma
when the context is clear. If
\Gamma
is anchored then the domain
\dom(\Gamma,v)
of
(\Gamma,v)
is the object
\bigotimes_{e_{i}\in\dom(\Gamma)}v_{0}(e_{i})
, where the monoidal product is taken with respect to the ordering on
\dom(\Gamma)
, similarly for the codmain
\cod(\Gamma,v)
of
(\Gamma,v)
.

An
isomorphism of diagrams

\Gamma\longrightarrow\Omega
is an isomorphism of the graphs that is compatible with the valuations in the obvious way. If the graphs are anchored then the isomorphism is required to preserve the anchoring too.

A diagram in a free graph monoid
(G,S,g)
is formulated in the same way, with the valuation
v:\Gamma\longrightarrow(G,S,g)
mapping the edges and nodes into the alphabet
S
and the set
A(G)
of arrows, respectively.

Remark 2.2
.

Isomorphisms of graphs are homeomorphisms of one-dimensional topological spaces and, insofar as diagrams in symmetric monoidal categories are concerned, they can be equivalently replaced by ambient isotopies of graphs embedded in
\mathds{R}^{4}
, but not in lower dimensions. The reason is simply that, in the graphical language of symmetric monoidal categories, we represent a symmetry as a braid, which is then forced by the axioms to be trivial, and indeed all braids in
\mathds{R}^{4}
are trivial. For instance, the crossing in the third diagram of (
1.1
) is a projection in
\mathds{R}^{2}
of the symmetry braid in
\mathds{R}^{4}
, and there is no need to depict, between the two strands, which one lies over which one, precisely because all the cases represent the same braid, that is, the trivial one. Thus, the intersection point therein is not a node of the graph, it is merely the (intentional) overlap of the shadows in
\mathds{R}^{2}
of the two edges, which do not intersect in
\mathds{R}^{4}
at all.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
)
.

The isomorphism classes of anchored diagrams
(\Gamma,v)
in the free graph monoid
G
, denoted by
[\Gamma,v]
, form the free symmetric monoidal category
\textup{{F}}_{s}(G)
over
G
.

From here on we assume that
\Gamma
is anchored.

Recall that
\Gamma_{0}
may be ordered as follows:
x<y
if and only if there is a directed path
p
in
\Gamma
with
p(0)=x
to
p(1)=y
; the thus formed partially ordered set is denoted by
\dot{\Gamma}
.

An initial segment of
\dot{\Gamma}
is referred to as a
level
of
\Gamma
, with
\emptyset
the smallest one and
\dot{\Gamma}
the largest one.

An edge
e
is said to be
cut
by a level
L
when
e(0)\in L\cup\dom(\Gamma)
and
e(1)\in(\dot{\Gamma}\smallsetminus L)\cup\cod(\Gamma)
.

Let
\cut(L)
denote the set of edges cut by
L
; we have
\cut(\emptyset)=\dom(\Gamma)
and
\cut(\dot{\Gamma})=\cod(\Gamma)
. For a pair of levels
L\subseteq M
, the
layer

\Gamma[L,M]
in
\Gamma
is the subgraph of
\Gamma
such that

•

its inner nodes are those in
M\smallsetminus L
,

•

its pinned edges are those
e\in\Gamma_{1}
with
e(0),e(1)\in M\smallsetminus L
,

•

its loose and half-loose edges are those in
\cut(L)\cup\cut(M)
.

So
\cut(L)=\dom(\Gamma[L,M])
and
\cut(M)=\cod(\Gamma[L,M])
, and the loose edges are exactly those in
\cut(L)\cap\cut(M)
, in particular,
\dom(\Gamma)=\dom(\Gamma[\emptyset,L])
and
\cod(\Gamma)=\cod(\Gamma[L,\dot{\Gamma}])
.

Any subgraph
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
inherits the orientations of the edges and the total orderings of
\inn_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(x)\subseteq\inn_{\Gamma}(x)
,
\out_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(x)\subseteq\out_{\Gamma}(x)
for each
x\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
, and hence is polarized. On the other hand, there is not a natural way to order
\dom(\Gamma^{\prime})\smallsetminus\dom(\Gamma)
or
\cod(\Gamma^{\prime})\smallsetminus\cod(\Gamma)
, so
\Gamma^{\prime}
is not naturally anchored unless these two sets are empty.

Remark 2.4
.

Since a layer
\Gamma[L,M]
is not naturally anchored, it cannot be used as is to produce a morphism in
\textup{{F}}_{s}(G)
. If we choose total orderings on the domain and codomain then of course
\Gamma[L,M]
may be turned into an anchored graph, although different choices in general bring about nonisomorphic results. In some situations, what orderings we choose is immaterial, as long as they are compatible. For instance, if
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
, and
\Gamma_{3}
are only equipped with partial orderings on the domains and codomains then by writing

[\Gamma,v]=[\Gamma_{3},v_{3}]\circ[\Gamma_{2},v_{2}]\circ[\Gamma_{1},v_{1}]

we mean that the partial orderings may be extended to compatible total orderings so to make the equality hold. In particular, this shall be what is meant when we write

[\Gamma,v]=[\Gamma[M,\dot{\Gamma}],v_{3}]\circ[\Gamma[L,M],v_{2}]\circ[\Gamma[\emptyset,L],v_{1}],

where
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
, and
v_{3}
are the induced valuations.

3.
Surgeries on diagrams

We continue to work with an anchored graph
\Gamma
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a subgraph
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
is
normal
if

•

no directed path in
\Gamma
contains two distinct edges in
\Gamma^{\prime}
if one of them is loose in
\Gamma^{\prime}
,

•

the degree of every
x\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
is the same as in
\Gamma
, in other words, all the edges at
x
in
\Gamma
are included in
\Gamma^{\prime}
,

•

every directed path
p
in
\Gamma
with
p(0),p(1)\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
belongs to
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

For a subgraph
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
, let
\Gamma^{\prime\flat}
denote the subgraph obtained from
\Gamma^{\prime}
by deleting its loose edges.

Observe that if two subgraphs
\Gamma^{\prime}
,
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
have the same set of nodes and both satisfy the second condition above then
\Gamma^{\prime\flat}
,
\Gamma^{\prime\prime\flat}
must be equal. Also, in a normal subgraph
\Gamma^{\prime}
of
\Gamma
, if
x\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
is initial in
\Gamma^{\prime}
then it is initial in
\Gamma
, similarly if it is terminal.

Lemma 3.2
.

A subgraph
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
is normal if and only if there is a layer
\Gamma[L,M]
in
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma[L,M]
and
\Gamma[L,M]^{\flat}=\Gamma^{\prime\flat}
.

Proof.

For the “if” direction, it is enough to show that
\Gamma[L,M]
is normal. First, let
p
be a directed path in
\Gamma
. Suppose for contradiction that
p
contains two distinct edges
e
,
e^{\prime}
in
\Gamma[L,M]
, where
e
is loose in
\Gamma[L,M]
. We have, in
\dot{\Gamma}
, either
e(1)\leq e^{\prime}(0)
or
e^{\prime}(1)\leq e(0)
. For the first case, since
e
is cut by
M
, we have
e(1)\in\dot{\Gamma}\smallsetminus M
and hence
e^{\prime}(0)\in\dot{\Gamma}\smallsetminus M
, which entails that
e^{\prime}
cannot be in
\Gamma[L,M]
, contradiction. Similarly, for the second case, since
e
is cut by
L
as well, we have
e(0)\in L
and hence
e^{\prime}(1)\in L
, so
e^{\prime}
cannot be in
\Gamma[L,M]
, contradiction again. Next, if
p(0),p(1)\in\Gamma[L,M]_{0}=M\smallsetminus L
then clearly
p
belongs to
\Gamma[L,M]
. Lastly, for any
x\in\Gamma[L,M]_{0}
, if
e\in\inn_{\Gamma}(x)
is cut by
L
then it belongs to
\Gamma[L,M]
by definition, otherwise
e(0)\in M\smallsetminus L
and hence
e
still belongs to
\Gamma[L,M]
by definition, similarly for
e\in\out_{\Gamma}(x)
. So
\Gamma[L,M]
is normal.

For the “only if” direction, let
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
be normal. Let
L
be the set of the nodes
x\in\dot{\Gamma}
such that either
x\notin\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
and
x<y
for some
y\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
or
x\leq e(0)\in\dot{\Gamma}
for some loose edge
e
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
. If the first case holds and

then
z\notin\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
, for otherwise

and hence
x\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
too by the third condition in Definition

, contradiction. So
L
is a level. Actually, if
x\leq e(0)\in\dot{\Gamma}
for some loose edge
e
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
then
x\notin\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
, for otherwise
x<e(0)
and there would be a directed path in
\Gamma
that contains
e
and an edge
e^{\prime}
at
x
, and since
e^{\prime}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
by the second condition in Definition
3.1
, this contradicts the first condition therein. So
L\cap\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}=\emptyset
. Now, let
M
be the set of the nodes
x\in\dot{\Gamma}
such that
x\leq y
for some
y\in\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
or
x\leq e(0)\in\dot{\Gamma}
for some loose edge
e
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Clearly

is a level and contains

. We have

.

Since
\Gamma[L,M]^{\flat}
is normal too, we have
\Gamma[L,M]^{\flat}=\Gamma^{\prime\flat}
. For any loose edge

in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, if
e(1)\in M
then there would be a directed path in
\Gamma
that contains two distinct edges in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, one of which is

, contradicting the first condition in Definition

, so

is cut by both

and

, in other words,

is a loose edge in
\Gamma[L,M]
. So
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma[L,M]
.
∎

Denote by
\Gamma^{\prime\sharp}
the layer

constructed from

in the latter half of the proof above.

So normal subgraphs are almost the same as layers. The point, of course, is that they are more intuitive to work with.

Suppose that
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
is a normal subgraph. Let
\phi:\Omega\longrightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}
be an isomorphism of graphs. Let

\alpha:\dom(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\dom(\Omega),\quad\beta:\cod(\Lambda)\longrightarrow\cod(\Omega)

by identifying the edges in
\dom(\Lambda)
,

with the corresponding edges in
\Gamma^{\prime}
— for this to work, the loose edges in

need to be duplicated, with one copy each in

and
\cod(\Gamma^{\prime})
— and then cut out the rest of

from

-surgery

for the resulting graph, where

\alpha

is referred to as the

Clearly there are induced bijections

and hence

is indeed naturally anchored; in particular, if

is isomorphic to
\Omega
(as polarized graphs) then
\Gamma^{\Omega}_{\Lambda}
is isomorphic to
\Gamma
(as anchored polarized graphs). If the template is empty, that is, if
\Lambda=\Omega=\emptyset
, then
\Gamma^{\Omega}_{\Lambda}=\Gamma
.

Let
G
be a free graph monoid. Let
v:\Gamma\longrightarrow G
,
w:\Lambda\longrightarrow G
, and
u:\Omega\longrightarrow G
be valuations. Let
\phi:(\Omega,u)\longrightarrow(\Gamma^{\prime},v^{\prime})
be an isomorphism of diagrams, where

is the restriction of

to
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Suppose that

,

are compatible with respect to
\alpha
and
\beta
, that is,

for

, and similarly for

. Since

is normal, there is an induced valuation

fusing

for

, and similarly for

\dom(\Gamma^{\Omega}_{\Lambda},v_{w})=\dom(\Gamma,v)\quad\text{and}\quad\cod(\Gamma^{\Omega}_{\Lambda},v_{w})=\cod(\Gamma,v).

The transition from the diagram
(\Gamma,v)
to the diagram
(\Gamma^{\Omega}_{\Lambda},v_{w})
is called the

-surgery
on
(\Gamma,v)
with the

; again, we usually only show

Observe that a surgery can be reversed (by another surgery) in the sense that the resulting diagram is isomorphic to the one we started with.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Omega

\Lambda

\Gamma^{\prime}

Generalizing the construction of a causal conditional in

Remark 5.3

Suppose that

A

We have a more flexible notion of morphisms in

For instance, under the standard definition, there can be no morphisms from any directed graph with a nonempty set of arrows to the directed graph with exactly one vertex; on the other hand, the one-vertex graph is the terminal object in
