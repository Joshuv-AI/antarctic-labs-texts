Proof of the Bessenrodt–Ono inequality by Induction
Bernhard Heim, Markus Neuhauser

Abstract
. In 2016 Bessenrodt–Ono discovered an inequality addressing additive and multiplicative properties of the partition function. Generalization by several authors have been given; on partitions with rank in a given residue class by Hou–Jagadeesan and Males, on k k -regular partitions by Beckwith–Bessenrodt, on k k -colored partitions by Chern, Fu, Tang, and Heim–Neuhauser on their polynomization, and Dawsey–Masri on the Andrews 𝑠𝑝𝑡 {\it spt} -function. The proofs depend on non-trivial asymptotic formulas related to the circle method on one side, or a sophisticated combinatorial proof invented by Alanazi–Gagola–Munagi. We offer in this paper a new proof of the Bessenrodt–Ono inequality, which is built on a well-known recursion formula for partition numbers. We extend the proof to the result of Chern–Fu–Tang and its polynomization. Finally, we also obtain a new result.

(Bessenrodt–Ono 2016)

Let

]

The partition numbers are considered as the coefficients of a weakly modular form,

[

]

The modularity condition is very strong, and it would be desirable to also have other proofs.

Alanazi, Gagola, and Munagi

Chern, Fu, and Tang
[

]

except for
(a,b,k)\in\left\{(1,1,2),(1,2,2),(2,1,2),(1,3,2),(3,1,2),(1,1,3)\right\}

Two proofs are given. One depends on the Bessenrodt–Ono inequality and some new ideas, the second
is combinatorial and motived by the work of Alanazi, Gagola, and Munagi
[
AGM17
]
.

Bessenrodt–Ono type inequalities appeared also in works by Beckwith and Bessenrodt

]

Let

This includes work of Nicolas

]

and De Salvo and Pak

2.
New Proof of the Bessenrodt–Ono inequality

We estimate the divisor sum function
\sigma(n)
and the partition numbers
p(n)

with the following upper and lower bounds:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sigma(n)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n\,\big(1+\mathop{\rm ln}(n)\big)

(2.2)

\displaystyle p(n)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k!}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\text{ for all }m\in\mathbb{N}.

The upper bound for
\sigma(n)
follows easily by integral comparison.
We have a strict upper bound for
n>1
.
There are exactly
\binom{n-1}{k-1}

ways to represent
n
as a sum of exactly
k
positive integers.
Thus, we have at most
k!
compositions representing the same partition.
For a generalization we refer to Section
3
and the relation to
associated Laguerre polynomials.

Proof.

Let
A:=2
and
B:=10
. Let
n\geq B
. We say the statement
S(n)
is true if for all partitions

n=a+b
with
a,b\geq A
:

(2.3)

p_{a,b}(n):=p(a)\,p(b)-p(a+b)>0.

By symmetry the claim can be reduced to all pairs
(a,b)
with
A\leq b\leq a
. We assume that

n>N_{0}>1
and
S(m)
are true for all
B\leq m\leq n-1
. For
B\leq\ell\leq N_{0}

we show
S(\ell)
by a direct computer calculation with PARI/GP.

Note, it is sufficient to prove
S(n)
for
fixed
A\leq b\leq a
with
a+b=n
.
We have introduced the constants
A
,
B
and
N_{0}
to make the generalization of the
given proof in our applications
transparent. It will turn out that we can chose
N_{0}=2184
.

We utilize the recurrence (
1.8
)
and obtain for
p_{a,b}\left(n\right)=L+R
the
expressions:

(2.4)

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{b}\frac{\sigma\left(k+a\right)}{a+b}p\left(b-k\right)

(2.5)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{a}\Big(\frac{\sigma(k)}{a}\,p(a-k)\,p(b)-\frac{\sigma(k)}{a+b}\,p(a+b-k)\Big).

We show that
p_{a,b}\left(n\right)>0
. Further, we will refine the right sum
R
into

R=R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}.

2.1.
Left sum
L

We have

(2.6)

L=-\sum_{k=1}^{b}\frac{\sigma\left(k+a\right)}{a+b}p\left(b-k\right)>-b\,p\left(b\right)\,\big(1+\ln(a+b)\big).

2.2.
Right sum
R

The dominant term is related to
k=1
appearing in the right sum
R
.
Note that the induction
hypothesis cannot be applied in general to all terms.
Therefore, we decompose the right sum
R

into three parts. Let

(2.7)

k_{0}:=a-\max\left\{B-b,A\right\}+1.

Thus,
k_{0}=a-\max\left\{B-b-1,A-1\right\}=a-\max\left\{9-b,1\right\}
for
A=2
and
B=10
. Let

(2.8)

R_{1}:=\sum_{k=1}^{1}\,f_{k}(a,b),\,\,R_{2}:=\sum_{k=2}^{k_{0}-1}\,f_{k}(a,b),\,\,R_{3}:=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{a}\,f_{k}(a,b).

(2.9)

f_{k}(a,b):=\frac{\sigma(k)}{a}\,p(a-k)\,p(b)-\frac{\sigma(k)}{a+b}\,p(a+b-k).

2.2.1.
The sum
R_{1}

The first sum related to
k=1
is simplified by the induction hypothesis:

-p(a+b-1)>-p(a-1)\,p(b).

Thus, we obtain
the lower bound:

(2.10)

R_{1}=\frac{p(a-1)\,p(b)}{a}-\frac{p(a+b-1)}{a+b}>\frac{b}{2a^{2}}\,p\left(a-1\right)p\left(b\right).

2.2.2.
The sum
R_{2}

The second sum, using again the induction hypothesis, can be
estimated from
below with
0
. This will be sufficient for our purpose:

R_{2}>0
.

2.2.3.
The sum
R_{3}

We split the third sum again into three parts:
R_{3}=R_{31}+R_{32}+R_{33}
, where

(2.11)

\displaystyle R_{31}:=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{a-A}\,f_{k}(a,b)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{a-A-1}\sigma(k)\left(\frac{p(2)\,p(b)}{a}-\frac{p(a+b-k)}{a+b}\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 5\,\big(4-p(8)\big)\,\big(1+\ln\left(a\right)\big),

(2.12)

\displaystyle R_{32}:=\sum_{k=a-A+1}^{a-1}\,f_{k}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(a-1)\left(\frac{p(1)\,p(b)}{a}-\frac{p(b+1)}{a+b}\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma(a-1)p(b)}{a}>-p\left(b\right)\,\big(1+\ln\left(a\right)\big),

(2.13)

\displaystyle R_{33}:=\sum_{k=a}^{a}\,f_{k}(a,b)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0.

We first use that

p\left(9-b\right)p\left(b\right)\geq p\left(9\right)
.
This leads to at most
5
summands.
The next estimation in (
2.11
) follows from
p(a-k)\geq p(2)
.
For the second estimation we use that

p\left(b\right)\geq p\left(2\right)=2,\,\,\frac{1}{a+b}<\frac{1}{a},\text{ and }p(a+b-k)\leq p(8).

We also refer to (
2.1
).
The estimation in (
2.11
) follows from the obvious inequality
\frac{1}{a+b}<\frac{1}{a}
and

p(b+1)<2\,p(b)
.
Thus,

(2.14)

R_{3}>-\left(1+\ln\left(a-1\right)\right)\frac{\left(a-1\right)\,p(b)}{a+b}-90\left(1+\ln\left(a\right)\right).

Since
b\geq 2
,
90\leq\frac{45}{2}\,b\,p\left(b\right)
,

\frac{1}{a+b}\geq\frac{1}{2a}
, and

a-1<a<a+b

we obtain

(2.15)

R_{3}>-b\,p\left(b\right)\left(1+\ln\left(a+b\right)\right)\frac{1+45}{2}.

2.3.
Final step

Putting everything together leads to

(2.16)

\displaystyle p_{a,b}\left(n\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{b\,p\left(b\right)}{2\,a^{2}}\left(-48\,a^{2}\,\left(1+\ln\left(a+b\right)\right)+p\left(a-1)\right)\right)

(2.17)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{b\,p\left(b\right)}{2\,a^{2}}\left(-48\,a^{2}\,\left(1+\ln\left(2a\right)\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{5}\frac{1}{\ell!}\binom{a-2}{\ell-1}\right).

In the last step we used the property (
2.2
). For
a\rightarrow\infty

we can immediately observe that this is positive
since the sum is a polynomial of
degree
4
in
a
which grows faster than
a^{2}\left(1+\ln\left(2a\right)\right)
.
In fact the expression (
2.17
) is positive for all
a\geq 1093
. Note that if
a<1093
then
a+b\leq 2a\leq 2184=N_{0}
.
Therefore,
we have shown that
p_{a,b}\left(n\right)>0
, which proves the Theorem.
∎

3.
Applications

We extend the proof method presented in Section
2

to prove the Bessenrodt–Ono inequality
for
k
-colored partitions
and its extension to D’Arcais polynomials.
Let

. Then

is equal to the

Let

-associated

. We refer to
[

]
.
This implies

(3.2)

P_{n}(x)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\text{ for all }m\in\mathbb{N}.

Bessenrodt–Ono for

and

.

Putting everything together leads to

\displaystyle P_{a,b}\left(x\right)

3.2.
The

Chern–Fu–Tang (
[

]

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
a
and
b
be positive integers with
a,b\geq 1
and

.
Then
P_{a,b}(2)>0
.

Proof.

We have
A=1
,
B=5
, and
k_{0}=a-\max\left\{5-b,1\right\}+1
.
Let
n\geq 5
and
S(n)
be the statement:
P_{a,b}(n)>0
for all
a,b\geq 1
with

.
A numerical calculation with PARI/GP shows that

is true for all

.
We prove

by induction on

. Let
n=a+b>N_{0}
and
1\leq b\leq a
. Let
P_{a,b}(2)=L+R
.
Then

L>-b\,P_{b}(2)\,\big(1+\ln(a+b)\big).

Further,

R_{1}>\frac{b}{2\,a^{2}}\,P_{a-1}(2)\,P_{b}(2).

We have
R_{2}\geq 0
by the induction hypothesis and
R_{3}\geq 0
for
b>3
.
Moreover

\frac{P_{a-k}\left(2\right)P_{b}\left(2\right)}{a}-\frac{P_{a+b-k}\left(2\right)}{a+b}>\frac{P_{a-k}\left(2\right)P_{b}\left(2\right)-P_{a+b-k}\left(2\right)}{a}.

From the induction hypothesis and

we see that this is

or

. For

we obtain

\left(P_{1}\left(2\right)\right)^{2}-P_{2}\left(2\right)=-1
.
This leads to

(3.5)

\displaystyle P_{a,b}\left(2\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{b\,P_{b}\left(2\right)}{2\,a^{2}}\left(-3\,a^{2}\,\left(1+\ln\left(a+b\right)\right)+P_{a-1}\left(2\right)\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{b\,P_{b}\left(2\right)}{2\,a^{2}}\left(-3\,a^{2}\,\left(1+\ln\left(2a\right)\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{5}\frac{2^{\ell}}{\ell!}\binom{a-2}{\ell-1}\right).

In the last step we used the property (
3.2
). For
a\rightarrow\infty

we can immediately observe that this is positive
since the sum is a polynomial of
degree
4
in
a
, which grows faster than
a^{2}\left(1+\ln\left(2a\right)\right)
.
In fact, the expression (
3.6
) is positive for all
a\geq 15
.
For the remaining
