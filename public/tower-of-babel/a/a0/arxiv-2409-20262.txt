Bootstrap-Based Goodness-of-Fit Test for Parametric Families of Conditional Distributions
Gitte Kremling, Gerhard Dikta

Abstract
A consistent goodness-of-fit test for distributional regression is introduced. The test statistic is based on a process that traces the difference between a nonparametric and a semi-parametric estimate of the marginal distribution function of Y Y . As its asymptotic null distribution is not distribution-free, a parametric bootstrap method is used to determine critical values. Empirical results suggest that, in certain scenarios, the test outperforms existing specification tests by achieving a higher power and thereby offering greater sensitivity to deviations from the assumed parametric distribution family. Notably, the proposed test does not involve any hyperparameters and can easily be applied to individual datasets using the gofreg-package in R.

Introduction

In many scientific applications, a response variable together with a number of features that may influence the outcome is observed. It is of high interest to figure out in which way the response (random) variable
Y\in\mathbb{R}
depends on the vector of input (random) variables
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. In this paper, we will propose a test to check whether the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
fits into a given parametric family. According to
Andrews (1997)
, many models in micro-econometric and biometric applications are of this type, and
Maddala (1983)
and
McCullagh and Nelder (1983)
list numerous examples.
A common class of parametric regression models used in practice is the generalized linear model (GLM). It was first introduced in
Nelder and Wedderburn (1972)
and later on thoroughly discussed in
Fahrmeir et al. (2013)
;
Fox and Weisberg (2018)
;
Dikta and Scheer (2021)
.

The test problem we want to consider is defined as follows. Let
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an i.i.d. sample of covariates and response variables with distribution function
H
for
X
and conditional density function
f(\,\cdot\,|x)
for
Y
given
X=x
with respect to a
\sigma
-finite measure
\nu
.
We want to create a test for

\displaystyle H_{0}:f\in\mathcal{F}=\{(x,y)\mapsto f(y|\vartheta,x)~|~\vartheta\in\Theta\}\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:f\notin\mathcal{F},

(1)

where

\Theta\coloneqq\left\{\vartheta\,\Big|\,\int\int f(y|\vartheta,x)\nu(dy)H(dx)<\infty\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}

defines the set of admissible parameters. If
H_{0}
holds, we denote the true distribution parameter by
\vartheta_{0}
.
Note that, as opposed to classical regression, which merely assumes a model for the conditional mean
m(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[Y|X=x\right]
, we consider a model for the whole distribution of
Y
given
X
. This enables us not only to predict the value of
Y
for a new feature vector
X
but also, for example, to provide a confidence band for the estimate.
Kneib et al. (2023)
offers a thorough investigation of distributional regression models, advocating for their superiority compared to mean regression and discussing the four currently most prominent model classes.

The literature on goodness-of-fit tests for conditional distributions and related model checks for parametric families of regression functions is very extensive. The methods can be categorized into two general approaches, namely those that make use of nonparametric kernel estimators and those that do not. Representatives of the former class are, for example, given in
Rodríguez-Campos et al. (1998)
;
Zheng (2000)
;
Fan et al. (2006)
;
Pardo-Fernández et al. (2007)
;
Cao and González-Manteiga (2008)
;
Ducharme and Ferrigno (2012)
. Since methods of this type suffer from the problem of choosing an appropriate smoothing parameter, we suggest a model check that falls into the second category and compare it to other representatives of this class.

In
Andrews (1997)
, a so-called conditional Kolmogorov (CK) test is proposed for the test problem defined in equation (
1
). It is based on a process tracing the difference between a nonparametric and semi-parametric estimate of the joint distribution of
X
and
Y
defined by

\nu_{n}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\quantity(\ind{Y_i \le t} - F\big(t|\vtn, X_i\big)){{I_{\left\{X_{i}\leq x\right\}}}},\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{p},

(2)

where
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
denotes an estimator for the distribution parameter
\vartheta
. The corresponding CK test statistic is given by
\sup_{j=1,\dots,n}\absolutevalue{\nu_n(Y_j, X_j)}
. A practical problem of this test may occur for high-dimensional covariate vectors as, even for large sample sizes, the inequality
X_{i}\leq X_{j}
may never be satisfied for
i\neq j
. In this case, the test statistic collapses to

\sup_{j=1,\dots,n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(1-F(Y_{j}|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},X_{j}))\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

which means that, regardless of the true underlying conditional distribution, the test asymptotically never rejects the null hypothesis! An example application with
X\in\mathbb{R}^{16}
where this issue is encountered can be found in
Bierens and Wang (2012, Sec. 4)
.

Another approach, first introduced in
Stute and Zhu (2002)
and later on extended by a bootstrap method in
Dikta and Scheer (2021)
, relies on the estimated marked empirical process in estimated direction, which is given by

\bar{R}_{n}^{1}(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\quantity(Y_i - m(X_i, \betan)){{I_{\left\{\hat{\beta}_{n}^{T}X_{i}\leq u\right\}}}},\quad u\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}.

(3)

It is applicable to parametric and semi-parametric GLMs in which the regression function
m(\cdot,\cdot)
is assumed to belong to a parametric family
\{x\mapsto m(x,\beta)=g^{-1}(\beta^{T}x)\,|\,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
with
g
denoting the invertible link function. Technically, this constitutes a test for the conditional mean – the assumed conditional distribution function is solely used to calculate an estimator for
\hat{\beta}_{n}
.

A different technique is used in the test procedure proposed in
Delgado and Stute (2008)
. Here, the test statistic is derived from an empirical process of the Rosenblatt transformations
(U_{i},V_{i})=(\hat{H}_{n}(X_{i}),F(Y_{i}|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},X_{i}))
which follow a uniform distribution in the limit. To obtain an asymptotically distribution-free test statistic, the authors suggest performing a martingale transform. Although the paper is very extensive, also describing the possibility of performing directional tests, this approach is fairly involved, model-dependent and difficult to automate using software.

Yet another possible way to test for
H_{0}
, described in
Bierens and Wang (2012)
, exploits the idea of comparing the empirical conditional characteristic function with the one implied by the model. The underlying process is defined as

Z_{n}(\tau,\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\quantity(\exp\qty(i \tau Y_j) - \int\exp\qty(i \tau y) dF(y|\vtn, X_j))\exp\quantity(i \xi^T X_j).

(4)

An adequate choice of the index set for this process, as well as a technique to avoid integration, is thoroughly discussed in the paper, ultimately resulting in the so-called simulated integrated conditional moment test. A drawback of this approach is the requirement to select an appropriate hyperparameter, denoted as
c
in their paper, which defines the integration domain and can impact the small sample power of the test.

Finally, in
Veazie and Ye (2020)
, a modified Pearson Chi-square test using the uniformly distributed transformations
F(Y_{i}|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},X_{i})
as inputs is suggested. This method also involves the choice of a hyperparameter, specifically the number of intervals
K
. Furthermore, its theoretical foundation is limited, as, for instance, consistency has not been proven.
Most of the stated methods involve the approximation of the critical value by a bootstrap method and many of them were shown to be consistent. Some of them were even extended to time series data or function-valued parameters (see
Bai (2003)
;
Rothe and Wied (2013)
;
Troster and Wied (2021)
).

In this paper, we derive a new test statistic for the problem at hand. Its advantages over the described methods proposed in the literature are manifold:

1.

It is model-independent and thus easy to automate.

2.

It does not involve any hyperparameters, making it more robust for practical usage.

3.

It has a rigorous theoretical foundation.

4.

It is applicable for high-dimensional input vectors
X
.

Note that, as explained above, all other methods lack at least one of these qualities.

Section
2
comprises a detailed derivation of the proposed test statistic. A result on the limit distribution of the underlying process is established in section
3
which, in theory, can be used to approximate the
p
-value for large sample sizes
n
. However, it turns out that the limit distribution is dependent on the true distributions of
X
and
Y
, which are unknown. To circumvent this problem and still be able to approximate the
p
-value, we suggest a parametric bootstrap method and establish its asymptotic justification in section
4
. The consistency of the resulting goodness-of-fit test is verified in section
5
. In section
6
, the finite sample behavior of our method is studied, applying the method to both simulated and real datasets and comparing the results to methods from the literature. Importantly, it can be seen that our new test seems to be more sensitive to deviations from the distribution family. Beyond that, we developed an R package called gofreg that streamlines the application of bootstrap-based goodness-of-fit tests for parametric regression and thereby enhances their usability. The appendix finally provides proofs of the theoretical results stated in the text.

2
Definition of the test statistic

Our test statistic will be based on the difference between a non-parametric and a semi-parametric estimate of the marginal distribution function
F_{Y}
of
Y
. A natural choice for the non-parametric estimator is the empirical distribution function (ecdf)
\hat{F}_{Y,n}
of
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
. For the derivation of the estimator of
F_{Y}
taking the assumed parametric family
\mathcal{F}
for the conditional density function
f
into account, we write

\displaystyle F_{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{{I_{\left\{Y\leq t\right\}}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[{{I_{\left\{Y\leq t\right\}}}}\,|\,X\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{E}\left[{{I_{\left\{Y\leq t\right\}}}}\,|\,X=x\right]H(dx)

\displaystyle=\int F(t|x)H(dx),

where
F(\,\cdot\,|x)
is the true conditional distribution function of
Y
given
X=x
. The semi-parametric estimate of
F_{Y}
now follows in two approximation steps. First, we substitute the true conditional distribution with a parametric estimate, yielding

\displaystyle F_{Y,\hat{\vartheta}_{n}}(t)\coloneqq\int F\big(t|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},x\big)H(dx).

A classical choice for
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
is the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE). Our analysis is, however, worded in general terms and only requires the estimator to meet certain assumptions. As in most applications the distribution of the covariates is unknown, we further approximate
H
by the ecdf
\hat{H}_{n}
of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, resulting in

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{Y,\hat{\vartheta}_{n}}(t)\coloneqq\int F(t|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},x)\hat{H}_{n}(dx)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F\big(t|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},X_{i}\big).

Now, we define the conditional empirical process with estimated parameters as

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{F}_{Y,n}(t)-\hat{F}_{Y,\hat{\vartheta}_{n}}(t)\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\quantity(\textcolor{black}{\ind{Y_i \le t} - F\big(t|\vtn, X_i\big)}),\quad t\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}.

As a test statistic, we can use some continuous functional of the process
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
. The supremum norm
\norm{\tnproc}=\sup_{t\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}}\absolutevalue{\tnproc(t)}
, for example, yields a Kolmogorov-Smirnov type statistic, whereas the integral
\int\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{2}(t)F\big(t|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},dx\big)
represents a Cramér-von-Mises type statistic. In the following, we will consider
\norm{\tnproc}
in particular, but the results can be easily transferred to other statistics. The
p
-value corresponding to an observed test statistic value
A
is given by

p\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\norm{\tnproc}\geq A).

As usual, the null hypothesis is rejected if the
p
-value is below some level of significance. In order to be able to compute the
p
-value (or equivalently the critical value), we need to know the distribution of
\norm{\tnproc}
under the null hypothesis.

3
Asymptotic null distribution

To investigate the asymptotic null distribution of our test statistic, we need to impose some conditions on the parametric model
\mathcal{F}
and the estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
. We first state a set of assumptions that is closely aligned with the one in
Andrews (1997)
. Later, we show that the result remains valid under an alternative set of conditions that is easier to verify in practice.

Assumption M1
.

Define
w(t,\vartheta,x)\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}F(t|\vartheta,x)
and
W(t,\vartheta)=\mathbb{E}\left[w(t,\vartheta,X)\right]
.

(i)

There exists a neighborhood
V\subseteq\Theta
of
\vartheta_{0}
such that
\vartheta\mapsto F(t|\vartheta,x)
is differentiable on
V
for all
t
and
H-
a.e.
x
.

(ii)

For all non-negative sequences
\{r_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, it holds

\sup_{t\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}}\sup_{\norm{\vt- \vt_0}\leq r_{n}}\norm{\frac{1}{n} \sumin w(t, \vt, X_i) - W(t, \vt_0)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\quad\text{in pr.}

(iii)

W(t,\vartheta_{0})
is uniformly continuous in
t
.

Assumption E1
.

(i)

There exists a function
L
such that

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\vartheta}_{n}-\vartheta_{0})=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(X_{i},Y_{i};\vartheta_{0})+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

(ii)

\mathbb{E}\left[L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})(L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0}))^{T}\right]<\infty
.

The estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
admitting an asymptotic linear representation as assumed in
E1
is a classic condition for convergence theorems of parametric test statistics. Usually, it is fulfilled for least squares or maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs). For the MLE, a corresponding result was established in
Dikta and Scheer (2021, Corollary 5.56)
. Technically, their setting was a little different in that they were considering parametric GLMs in particular, but the results can be easily extended to general conditional distribution families.

The following theorem establishes a convergence result for the conditional empirical process with estimated parameters
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
. Specifically, we consider weak convergence in the space of uniformly bounded functions on the extended real line
\ell^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{R}})
as defined in
Kosorok (2008, Chap. 7)
. The asymptotic distribution of the test statistic
\norm{\tnproc}
can be derived subsequently using the Continuous Mapping Theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Under
H_{0}
and Assumptions
M1
and
E1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{n}\Rightarrow\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}\quad\text{in }\ell^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}),

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}
is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function

\displaystyle K(s,t)=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left(Y\leq\min(s,t)\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[F(s|\vartheta_{0},X)F(t|\vartheta_{0},X)\right]

\displaystyle+W^{T}(t,\vartheta_{0})\mathbb{E}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left[\quantity(F(s|\vt_0,X) - \ind{Y \le s})L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})\right]

\displaystyle+W^{T}(s,\vartheta_{0})\mathbb{E}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left[\quantity(F(t|\vt_0,X) - \ind{Y \le t})L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})\right]

\displaystyle+W^{T}(s,\vartheta_{0})\text{COV}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left(L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})\right)W(t,\vartheta_{0}).

In fact, a similar result can be directly obtained from
(
Andrews, 1997
, Theorem 1)
using the Continuous Mapping Theorem. However, we want to emphasize that our Assumption
M1
is weaker than its analogue in Andrews’ work. While the two sets of assumptions are largely comparable, the key distinction lies in the additional indicator function
{{I_{\left\{X\leq x\right\}}}}
appearing in Andrews’ formulation of
w(t,\vartheta,x)
. In particular, his assumptions analogous to
M1
(
ii
) and (
iii
) have to hold uniformly for H-a.e.
x
, whereas we do not impose that requirement. It should further be noted that, as opposed to Andrews, we only need the convergence in Assumption
M1
(
ii
) to hold in pr. to prove Theorem
1
. The stronger assumption of convergence wp1 is needed to derive the asymptotic distribution of the bootstrap process in Theorem
3
. The same applies to the uniform boundedness of
t\mapsto W(t,\vartheta_{0})
.

Next, we introduce a different set of assumptions under which the conclusion of Theorem
1
remains valid. Although they are more restrictive, it is instructive to mention them as they are easier to verify and better comparable to the assumptions in
Dikta and Scheer (2021)
.

Assumption M1’
.

Define
v(t,\vartheta,x)\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}f(t|\vartheta,x)
.

(i)

There exists a neighborhood
V\subseteq\Theta
of
\vartheta_{0}
in which
v(t,\vartheta,x)
is defined and there is a function
g(t,x)
such that
\norm{v(t,\vt,x)}\leq g(t,x)
and
\vartheta\in V
with
\int g(t,x)\nu(dt)<\infty
for
H
-a.e.
x
.

(ii)

For
V
defined in (i), it holds

\int\sup_{\vartheta\in V}\int\norm{v(t,\vt,x)}\nu(dt)H(dx)<\infty.

(iii)

The family of functions
\{\vartheta\mapsto w(t,\vartheta,x)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is equicontinuous at
\vartheta_{0}
for
H
-a.e.
x
, meaning that for
H
-a.e.
x
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta\equiv\delta(x,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\norm{w(t, \vt, x) - w(t, \vt_0, x)}<\varepsilon\quad\text{if}\quad\norm{\vt- \vt_0}<\delta.

(iv)

W(t,\vartheta)
is uniformly continuous in
t
at
\vartheta_{0}
.

Corollary 2
.

Theorem
1
also applies under Assumptions
M1’
and
E1
, since
M1’
implies
M1
.

Assumptions
M1’
(
i
) and (
ii
) are analogous to
Dikta and Scheer (2021, 6.4.3(iv) and (v))
. In their book, the parametric regression function
m(\cdot,\cdot)
plays the role of the conditional density
f(\cdot\,|\,\cdot,\cdot)
in our analysis. A sufficient condition for Assumption
M1’
(
ii
) is
\int\int g(t,x)\nu(dt)H(dx)
to be finite with
g
defined in Assumption
M1’
(
i
).
Assumption
M1’
(
iv
) is the analogue of
Dikta and Scheer (2021, 6.5.2(xi))
.

As the asymptotic null distribution in Theorem
1
involves
\vartheta_{0}
as well as the distribution of
X
, it is case dependent and cannot be tabulated. For that reason, we suggest a bootstrap method to approximate the limit distribution.

4
Parametric bootstrap method

The goal of bootstrap methods, in general, is to estimate the distribution of a given test statistic under the null hypothesis by generating new samples, introducing some type of randomness while sticking as close as possible to the original sample. In our case, this means that we want to estimate the distribution of
\norm{\tnproc}
by generating new samples
\{(X_{i,n}^{*},Y_{i,n}^{*})\}
whose conditional distribution is guaranteed to belong to the conditional distribution family
\mathcal{F}
while at the same time keeping the distributions as similar as possible to the original sample. These considerations lead to the following resampling scheme:

(1)

Keep the covariates the same:
X_{i,n}^{*}=X_{i}
.

(2)

Generate new response variables
Y_{i,n}^{*}
according to the estimated conditional distribution function
F\big(\cdot|\hat{\vartheta}_{n},X_{i,n}^{*}\big)
.

(3)

Based on this new bootstrap sample, find an estimate
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}^{*}
for the distribution parameter.

(4)

Determine the bootstrap conditional empirical process with estimated parameters

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{*}(t)\coloneqq\sqrt{n}\left(F^{*}_{Y,n}(t)-\hat{F}_{Y,\hat{\vartheta}_{n}^{*}}(t)\right),

where
F^{*}_{Y,n}(t)
denotes the ecdf of
\{Y_{i,n}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

The
p
-value
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\quantity(\norm{\tnproc} \ge A)
is then approximated by
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\quantity(\norm{\tsnproc} \ge A)
with
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}
indicating the probability measure corresponding to the bootstrap random variables based on
n
original observations. This approach is justified if the bootstrap process
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{*}
converges to the same limit distribution as
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
. To prove a corresponding result, some additional assumptions are needed. Again, we first consider assumptions similar to those in
Andrews (1997)
and in a second step provide stronger ones that are easier to verify in practice.

Assumption M2
.

Let
w
and
W
be the functions defined in Assumption
M1
.

(i)

For all non-negative sequences
\{r_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, it holds

\sup_{t\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}}\sup_{\norm{\vt- \vt_0}\leq r_{n}}\norm{\frac{1}{n} \sumin w(t, \vt, X_i) - W(t, \vt_0)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\quad\text{wp1}.

(ii)

The function
t\mapsto W(t,\vartheta_{0})
is uniformly bounded, i.e.
\sup_{t\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}}\norm{W(t, \vt_0)}<\infty.

Assumption ME2
.

Let
L
be the function defined in Assumption
E1
. The convergence

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{n}^{*}\left[\ell_{k}(X_{i},Y_{i,n}^{*},\hat{\vartheta}_{n})\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left[\ell_{k}(X,Y,\vartheta_{0})\right]

holds wp1 for the following functions (for all values of
s,t\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}
):

(i)

\ell_{1}(x,y,\vartheta)=\absolutevalue{\ind{y \le s} - \ind{y \le t}}
,

(ii)

\ell_{2}(x,y,\vartheta)=\quantity(\ind{y \le s} - F(s|\vt, x))\quantity(\ind{y \le t} - F(t|\vt, x))
,

(iii)

\ell_{3}(x,y,\vartheta)=L(x,y,\vartheta)\quantity(L(x,y,\vt)^T)
, and

(iv)

\ell_{4}(x,y,\vartheta)=\quantity(\ind{y \le s} - F(s|\vt, x))L(x,y,\vartheta)
.

Assumption E2
.

Let
L
be the function defined in Assumption
E1
.

(i)

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\vartheta}_{n}^{*}-\hat{\vartheta}_{n})=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(X_{i},Y_{i,n}^{*};\hat{\vartheta}_{n})+o_{\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}}(1).

(ii)

\mathbb{E}_{n}^{*}\left[L(X,Y^{*};\hat{\vartheta}_{n})\right]=0.

(iii)

For the neighborhood
V
defined in Assumption
M1
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that

\int\sup_{\vartheta\in V}\int\norm{L(x,y,\vt)}^{2+\delta}f(y|\vartheta,x)\nu(dy)H(dx)<\infty.

Assumptions
E2
(
i
),
E2
(
ii
) and
ME2
(
iii
) are usually fulfilled for appropriate estimators
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
. Their validity for the MLE can be proven similarly to
Dikta and Scheer (2021, Corollary 5.62)
.

The following theorem establishes a weak convergence result for the bootstrap process
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{*}
and thereby justifies the bootstrap approximation.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
M1
,
M2
,
ME2
,
E1
,
E2
and if
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}\to\vartheta_{0}
wp1, it holds wp1 that

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{n}^{*}\Rightarrow\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}\quad\text{in }\ell^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}),

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}
is the same process as in Theorem
1
.

Just as for Theorem
1
, a similar result could be directly obtained from
Andrews (1997, Theorem 2)
, but, importantly, this would entail stronger assumptions involving the indicator
{{I_{\left\{X\leq x\right\}}}}
. Note that Assumption
M2
is already included in
Andrews (1997, Assumption M1)
, and Assumptions
ME2
and
E2
are the analogues of
Andrews (1997, Assumptions M2(i) and E2)
, respectively.

In a next step, we want to consider sufficient conditions that are easier to verify in practice.

Assumption ME2’
.

Let
L
be the function defined in Assumption
E1
and
V
be the neighborhood defined in Assumption
M1’
.

(i)

It holds that

\int\int\sup_{\vartheta\in V}f(y|\vartheta,x)\nu(dy)H(dx)<\infty.

(ii)

L
is continuous in
\vartheta
at
\vartheta_{0}
and there exist neighborhoods
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
of
\vartheta_{0}
such that

\int\int\sup_{\vartheta_{1}\in V_{1}}\norm{L(x,y,\vt_1)}\sup_{\vartheta_{2}\in V_{2}}f(y|\vartheta_{2},x)\nu(dy)H(dx)<\infty.

Corollary 4
.

Theorem
3
also applies under Assumptions
M1’
,
ME2’
,
ME2
(
iii
),
E1
and
E2
.

In practice, the bootstrap
p
-value
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\quantity(\norm{\tsnproc} \ge A)
is, in turn, approximated by a Monte Carlo simulation repeating steps (1)-(4)
m
times yielding
\{\tilde{\alpha}_{n,k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{m}
and finally computing

\tilde{p}\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}{{I_{\left\{\norm{\tilde{\alpha}_{n,k}^*}\geq A\right\}}}}\approx\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\quantity(\norm{\tsnproc} \ge A)\approx\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\quantity(\norm{\tnproc} \ge A)=p.

(5)

This additional approximation is justified by the strong law of large numbers (SLLN).

Note that Theorem
3
is also valid under
H_{1}
as long as
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
converges to some
\vartheta_{0}\in\Theta
and Assumptions
M1
,
M2
,
ME2
,

5
Consistency of the test

In the following, we show that our proposed test is consistent against any conditional density
f_{1}
in the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
, as long as the corresponding conditional distribution function
F_{1}
is distinguishable from all members of the parametric family.

Assumption H1
.

For every
\vartheta\in\Theta
, there exists a
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[F_{1}(t|X)\right]\neq\mathbb{E}\left[F(t|\vartheta,X)\right].

Further, we need to impose the following assumption on the parameter estimator
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
.

Assumption E3
.

There exists a
\vartheta_{1}\in\Theta
such that
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}\to\vartheta_{1}
in pr.

The limit value
\vartheta_{1}
is sometimes called the pseudo-true parameter. In case
\hat{\vartheta}_{n}
is the MLE,
\vartheta_{1}
minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the true distribution of the data and the parametric model.

Theorem 5
.

Let
c_{n}
denote the (random) sequence of bootstrap critical values for a given significance level
0<\alpha<1
, as defined by
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\quantity(\norm{\tsnproc} > c_n)\leq\alpha
.
Under the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
, Assumptions
H1
and
E3
as well as
M1
,
M2
,
ME2
,
E1
,
E2
with
\vartheta_{0}
being replaced by the pseudo-true value
\vartheta_{1}
, it holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\norm{\tnproc}>c_{n}\right)=1.

Simulations and examples

In this section, we will investigate the finite sample behavior of the proposed goodness-of-fit test for parametric families of conditional distributions. For that, we will apply it to both artificially created data (for which the true distribution is known) and real datasets (for which the true distribution is unknown). The results will be compared to other established bootstrap-based tests used for the same purpose. Specifically, we will also evaluate the methods proposed by
Bierens and Wang (2012)

Andrews (1997)
and
Dikta and Scheer (2021)

To investigate the power of the different methods in more detail and thereby shed light on the cases in which each is most sensitive, a theoretical comparison of the variances of the respective limit processes would be informative. However, as such an analysis is a more involved endeavor, we leave it for future research. Here, we merely want to give some first insights into the comparison between our method and that of Andrews. The variance of the process
\tilde{\alpha}_{\infty}
is given in Theorem
1
as

\displaystyle\text{Var}\quantity(\tinfproc(t))

\displaystyle=K(t,t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left(Y\leq t\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\quantity(F(t|\vt_0, X))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2W^{T}(t,\vartheta_{0})\mathbb{E}\left[\quantity(F(t|\vt_0,X) - \ind{Y \le t})L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+W^{T}(t,\vartheta_{0})\text{COV}\left(L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})\right)W(t,\vartheta_{0}).

(6)

In contrast, the process
\nu_{n}(t,x)
defined in equation (
2
) and analyzed in
Andrews (1997)
converges to a mean zero Gaussian process
\nu_{\infty}(t,x)
with variance

\displaystyle\text{Var}\quantity(\nu_\infty(t,x))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left(X\leq x,Y\leq t\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\quantity(F(t|\vt_0, X))^{2}{{I_{\left\{X\leq x\right\}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2W_{\nu}^{T}(t,x,\vartheta_{0})\mathbb{E}\left[\quantity(F(t|\vt_0,X) - \ind{Y \le t})L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0}){{I_{\left\{X\leq x\right\}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+W_{\nu}^{T}(t,x,\vartheta_{0})\text{COV}\left(L(X,Y;\vartheta_{0})\right)W_{\nu}(t,x,\vartheta_{0})

(7)

where
W_{\nu}(t,x,\vartheta_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[w(t,\vartheta_{0},X){{I_{\left\{X\leq x\right\}}}}\right]
. A general comparison of these variances is not particularly meaningful, as for example

\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left(X\leq x,Y\leq t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta_{0}}\left(Y\leq t\right)

while

-\mathbb{E}\left[\quantity(F(t|\vt_0, X))^{2}{{I_{\left\{X\leq x\right\}}}}\right]\geq-\mathbb{E}\left[\quantity(F(t|\vt_0, X))^{2}\right].

Moreover, to compare the third and fourth summands of the variances in (
6
) and (
7
), the relationship between
W(t,\vartheta_{0})
and
W_{\nu}(t,x,\vartheta_{0})
has to be investigated. Since

W(t,\vartheta_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[w(t,\vartheta_{0},X)\quantity(\ind{X \le x} + \ind{X > x})\right]=W_{\nu}(t,x,\vartheta_{0})+\mathbb{E}\left[w(t,X,\vartheta_{0}){{I_{\left\{X>x\right\}}}}\right],

the dominance of one term or the other depends on the sign of
w(t,x,\vartheta_{0})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}F(t|\vartheta_{0},x)
, which can vary with
x
. Thus, a more informative comparison would likely require focusing on specific values of
x
.

Appendix A
Proofs

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The proof will be based on a Durbin-like splitting of the process (see
Durbin (1973)
) given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\quantity(\textcolor{black}{\ind{Y_i \le t} - F(t|\vt_0,X_i)})}_{\alpha_{n}^{(1)}(t)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\quantity(\textcolor{black}{F(t|\vt_0,X_i) - F(t|\vtn,X_i)})}_{\alpha_{n}^{(2)}(t)}.

To prove the convergence of
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
, we will first verify that the two processes are asymptotically tight in
\ell^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{R}})
and find asymptotic iid representations for them to analyze the covariance structure of the finite-dimensional distributions (fidis). Then we use the multivariate central limit theorem (CLT) for the convergence of the fidis and finally apply
