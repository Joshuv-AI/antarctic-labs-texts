1
Introduction

Volatility trading has become remarkably important in finance in recent years and plays a crucial role in risk management, portfolio diversification, asset pricing and econometrics. The Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) established the first volatility index, the
VXO
, in January 1993. According to
[
35
]
, the
VXO
index represents the implied volatility of a hypothetical at-the-money option on the S&P 100 (OEX) expiring in 30 days. The CBOE introduced in September 2003 a new volatility index, the
VIX
, in a model-free procedure. The
VIX
is computed as a weighted sum of out-of-the money S&P 500 (SPX) calls and puts expiring in 30 days across all available strikes. A convenient property of the
VIX
index is that its square approximates the 30-day conditional risk neutral expectation of the return variance, that is, the 30-day variance swap, see
[
12
]
. Many further volatility indices have been introduced like the
VXN
, the
VXD
, the
VCAC
and others
[
29
]
.

[
9
]
and
[
10
]
described in 1989 and 1993, respectively, the need of volatility derivatives to allow traders to better manage their portfolio and hedge against volatility risk. In that regard, the CBOE Futures Exchange (CFE) introduced
VIX
futures contracts in March 2004 followed by the launch of VIX options in February 2006. Thereafter, many new trading opportunities became available for traders giving them more control on volatility risk. The trading popularity of volatility derivatives has consistently increased through the years. This increase is, to some extent, because
VIX
movements are negatively correlated with movements in SPX returns.

Due to the importance of volatility trading in finance, a considerable amount of research has been conducted to develop and expand stochastic volatility models for pricing VIX derivatives. In particular, including jumps in the stock and/or the volatility is a common practice. For instance,
[
29
]
shows that a mean reverting logarithmic diffusion with Poisson jumps approximates the behaviour of the VIX index and an analytical pricing formula for European VIX options is derived.
[
31
]
uses a Heston model with Poisson jumps in the volatility to derive a pricing formula for volatility derivatives and
[
32
]
for the Heston model with Poisson jumps both in returns and volatility. Also,
[
39
,
24
]
obtain an exact solution for the pricing of VIX futures in the Heston model with simultaneous Poisson jumps in the asset price and the volatility.

An important reason to go beyond the Poisson jump diffusion models is that the volatility clustering feature and the high-volatility effect
[
3
,
5
]
are not included due to the constant intensity of the Poisson process. In that regard,
[
25
,
13
,
36
,
29
]
obtain pricing formulas for VIX derivatives under a stochastic volatility model with jumps in the asset and/or volatility with state-dependent intensity. Additionally,
[
20
]
proposes a Heston model with Hawkes jumps in the stock price and
[
36
]
a Heston model with Hawkes jumps both in the asset and the volatility, respectively. The main purpose of these models is to include the jump clustering effect in the stock and/or the volatility, that is, the occurrence of a jump increases the probability of future jumps. Other fundamental works about the VIX and volatility derivatives are
[
21
,
37
,
38
,
27
,
11
,
14
]
.

The objective of our work is to obtain a semi-analytical pricing formula for European VIX call options under the Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model introduced in
[
7
]
. Essentially, the Heston-Hawkes model is a Heston model with a compound Hawkes process in the volatility. In reference to that, the SVCIJ-H model in
[
36
]
and their approach present some similarities but also some important differences with our paper that are thoroughly described in Section
3
. Fundamentally, the SVCIJ-H model in
[
36
]
has simultaneous and independent Hawkes jumps in the stock and volatility and the size of the jumps is exponentially distributed. In contrast, the self-exciting jumps are only present in the volatility of the Heston-Hawkes model but their size has a general distribution, being the exponential distribution a particular case. This leads to technical challenges in qualitative ODE theory which are properly tackled in Section
5
.

First and foremost, the existence of risk neutral probability measures is crucial to make sense of the pricing problem. To the best of our knowledge, the usual procedure in the aforementioned literature is to present the model directly under
\mathbb{Q}
after assuming that the model is already arbitrage-free. Nevertheless,
[
8
,
30
]
present some stochastic volatility models where no equivalent local martingale measure exist. Moreover, in terms of risk management practices,
[
34
,
33
]
show that exposures computed under the risk neutral measure are essentially arbitrary and the passage from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{Q}
is required. Therefore, a proper study about the existence of risk neutral probability measures is fundamental for the pricing of VIX derivatives. In that respect,
[
7
]
proves that the Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model is arbitrage-free and an explicit family of equivalent martingale measures is given.

Our approach to derive a semi-analytical pricing formula is based on the use of the following equality

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max\{\sqrt{X}-K,0\}\right]=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{1-\text{erf}\left(K\sqrt{\phi}\right)}{\phi^{3/2}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\phi X}\right]\right]d\phi_{I},

where
erf
is the error function and some technical conditions described in Section
3
must hold. In order to use the previous formula, an explicit expression of
\text{VIX}^{2}
is required. Using the Markov property of the exponential Hawkes, it is possible to derive such an explicit expression as a linear combination of the variance and the Hawkes intensity. Another requirement for using the equality is a closed expression of the joint characteristic function of the variance and the Hawkes intensity. Thanks to the exponential affine structure of the model, the characteristic function can be computed after solving some generalized Riccati ODEs. In addition, to apply (
1.1
) it is crucial to check that the domain of the characteristic function intersects the complex numbers with strictly positive real part.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we outline the Heston-Hakwes stochastic volatility model introduced in
[
7

. In Section
3
we present the problem of pricing European
VIX
call options and the technical results that are required to derive a semi-analytical pricing formula. In Section
4
we summarize the arbitrage-free property of the stochastic volatility model proven in
[
7
]
and some results on the compensators of the Hawkes process under the risk neutral probability measures given in
[
6
]
. In Section
5
we compute the joint characteristic function of the variance and the Hawkes intensity using the exponential affine structure of the model. Before, we study the solution of some generalized Riccati ODEs that appear in the expression of the characteristic function. In Section
6
we derive an explicit expression of the
VIX
index using the Markov property of the exponential Hawkes. Finally, in Section
7
we present the semi-analytical pricing formula for European VIX call options under the Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model. In the appendix we give all the technical proofs.

2
Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model

We outline the Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model introduced in
[
7
]
. Essentially, this model is an extension of the well-known Heston model that incorporates the volatility clustering effect by adding an independent compound Hawkes process to the variance.

Let
T\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
T>0
be a fixed time horizon. On a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, we consider a two-dimensional standard Brownian motion
(B,W)=\left\{\left(B_{t},W_{t}\right),t\in[0,T]\right\}
and its minimally augmented filtration
\mathcal{F}^{(B,W)}=\{\mathcal{F}^{(B,W)}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
. On
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, we also consider a Hawkes process
N=\{N_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
with stochastic intensity given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}=\lambda_{0}+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\beta(t-s)}dN_{s},

or, equivalently,

(2.1)

\displaystyle d\lambda_{t}=-\beta(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{0})dt+\alpha dN_{t},

where
\lambda_{0}>0
is the initial intensity,
\beta>0
is the speed of mean reversion and
\alpha\in(0,\beta)
is the self-exciting factor. Note that the stability condition
\alpha<\beta
holds. See
[
4
, Section 2]
and
[
19
, Section 3.1.1]
for further details on Hawkes processes.
Then, we consider a sequence of i.i.d., strictly positive and integrable random variables
\{J_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 1}
and the compound Hawkes process
L=\{L_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
given by

\displaystyle L_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}J_{i}.

We make the following assumption on the distribution of the jumps size.

Assumption 1
.

There exists
\epsilon_{J}>0
such that the moment generating function
M_{J}(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tJ_{1}}]
of
J_{1}
is well defined in
(-\infty,\epsilon_{J})
. Moreover,
(-\infty,\epsilon_{J})
is the maximal domain in the sense that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\epsilon_{J}^{-}}M_{J}(t)=\infty.

Since
\epsilon_{J}>0
, all positive moments of
J_{1}
are finite. Note that it is a rather mild assumption, and that the exponential distribution belongs to the class of distributions satisfying the condition.

We assume that
(B,W),N
and
\{J_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 1}
are independent of each other. We write
\mathcal{F}^{L}=\left\{\mathcal{F}^{L}_{t},t\in[0,T]\right\}
for the minimally augmented filtration generated by
L
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(B,W)}\vee\mathcal{F}_{t}^{L},t\in[0,T]\},

for the joint filtration. We assume that
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{F}_{T}
and we will work with
\mathcal{F}
. Since
(B,W)
and
L
are independent processes,
(B,W)
is also a two-dimensional
(\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
-Brownian motion.

Finally, with all these ingredients, we introduce the Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model. We assume that the interest rate is deterministic and constant equal to
r
. The stock price
S=\{S_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
and its variance
v=\{v_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
are given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}dt+\sqrt{v_{t}}\left(\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}dB_{t}+\rho dW_{t}\right),

(2.3)

\displaystyle dv_{t}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\left(v_{t}-\bar{v}\right)dt+\sigma\sqrt{v_{t}}dW_{t}+\eta dL_{t},

where
S_{0}>0
is the initial price of the stock,
\mu:[0,T]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a measurable and bounded function,
\rho\in(-1,1)
is the correlation factor,
v_{0}>0
is the initial value of the variance,
\kappa>0
is the variance’s mean reversion speed,
\bar{v}>0
is the long-term variance,
\sigma>0
is the volatility of the variance and
\eta>0
is a scaling factor. We assume that the Feller condition
2\kappa\bar{v}\geqslant\sigma^{2}
is satisfied, see
[
1
, Proposition 1.2.15]
. For more details and further results on the model see
[
7
]
.

3
Pricing VIX options

The objective of this paper is to derive a semi-analytical pricing formula for European
VIX
call options under the Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility model. Namely, let
\mathbb{Q}
be a risk neutral probability measure, the price
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbb{Q}}(t,T,K)
at time
t\in[0,T]
of an European
VIX
call option with maturity time
T
and strike
K
is given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\mathbb{Q}}(t,T,K)=e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\max\{\text{VIX}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{T}-K,0\}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],

where the VIX index can be defined (with mathematical simplification) in the following way

\displaystyle\left(\text{VIX}_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}}\right)^{2}=-\frac{2}{\Delta}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\log\left(e^{-r\Delta}\frac{S_{t+\Delta}}{S_{t}}\right)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\cdot 100^{2},

where
\Delta=\frac{30}{365}
and
S
follows the dynamics in (
2.2
). See
[
23
,
25
,
16
,
39
]
for further details on the definition of the VIX index. Our approach to obtain the pricing formula involves the use of Fourier transform techniques. Precisely, we will employ the following convenient equality

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max\{\sqrt{X}-K,0\}\right]=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{\text{erfc}\left(K\sqrt{\phi}\right)}{\phi^{3/2}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\phi X}\right]\right]d\phi_{I},

where
\phi=\phi_{R}+i\phi_{I}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\phi_{R}>0
,
X
is a positive random variable such that
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\phi_{R}X}\right]<\infty
and
erfc
is the complementary error function defined by
\text{erfc}(z)=1-\text{erf}(z)
. For a reference of this formula see
[
36
,
22
,
28
]
. The usefulness of this formula relies on the fact that we can derive an explicit expression of
\text{VIX}^{2}
and its characteristic function. Several technical issues arise if the formula given in (
3.2
) is going to be applied to derive a pricing formula for (
3.1
):

1.

The existence of risk neutral probability measures is required to make sense of (
3.1
). To the best of our knowledge, the common procedure in the literature regarding pricing of VIX options under stochastic volatility models with jumps is to give the model directly under
\mathbb{Q}
after assuming that the model is arbitrage-free. It is discussed in
[
8
,
30
]
that this property is not obvious in general and a proper study is required.
[
7
]
proves that the Heston-Hawkes stochastic volatility is arbitrage-free and an explicit family of equivalent martingale measures is given. This is crucial to make sense of this problem and we summarize the results of
[
7
]
in Section
4
. Moreover,
[
34
,
33
]
show that exposures computed under the risk neutral measure are essentially arbitrary and the passage from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{Q}
is crucial for risk management practices.

2.

An explicit expression of
\text{VIX}^{2}
is necessary in order to use (
3.2
). In Section
6
we obtain such expression by exploiting the Markov property of the exponential Hawkes
[
26
, Remark 1.22]
.

3.

There must exist
\phi=\phi_{R}+i\phi_{I}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\phi_{R}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\phi_{R}X}\right]<\infty
to use the formula in (
3.2
). In our setting, this condition is reduced to the existence of
\phi,\psi\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}(\phi)>0
and
\operatorname{Re}(\psi)>0
such that
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{\phi v_{T}+\psi\lambda_{T}}\right]<\infty
where
\mathbb{Q}
is a risk neutral probability measure. This study is conducted in Section
5
.

4.

A closed expression of the characteristic function
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\phi X}\right]
is needed to obtain a tractable pricing formula. In Section
5
we derive an (almost) explicit expression of the conditional characteristic function
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{\phi v_{T}+\psi\lambda_{T}}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]
for
t\in[0,T]
using the exponential affine structure of the model
[
17
,
36
]
.

After presenting the model, the problem and the approach, it is pertinent to thoroughly explain the connection of our paper with
[
36
]
.

Remark 3.1
.

It is important to mention the connection of our paper with
[
36
]
. While the objective is the same and the stochastic volatility models are similar, there is a significant amount of differences that are worth to highlight.

The model SVCIJ-H presented in
[
36
]
has simultaneous and independent self-exciting jumps in the stock and the variance and their size is exponentially distributed. In our model, the self-exciting jumps occur only in the variance and their size has a general distribution, being the exponential distribution a particular case.

The stochastic volatility models presented in
[
36
]
are given directly under
\mathbb{Q}
while we justify the existence of a family of equivalent martingale measures for our model. As commented before, the assumption that the model is arbitrage-free may not be obvious
[
8
,
30
]
and the passage from
\mathbb{P}
to
\mathbb{Q}
is necessary for risk management procedures like computing exposures
[
34
,
33
]
.

Since the jumps in our setting can have an arbitrary distribution, the ODEs that appear when computing the characteristic function take a more general form than the ones in
[
36
, Proposition 2]
. In addition, in Section
5
we prove that the domain of the characteristic function intersects the complex numbers with strictly positive real part, which is crucial to use the formula in (
3.2
). However, we miss this study in
[
36
, Proposition 2]
for the SVCIJ-H model.

Joint characteristic function

The following result implies that
\mathbb{E}[f(J_{1})]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}(a)}[f(J_{1})]

Definition 5.2
.

Define the constant
L_{J}:=\frac{1}{\eta}M_{J}^{-1}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-1\right)\right)

In the following result we apply qualitative ODE theory to study the existence of some generalized Riccati ODEs. The solutions of such equations appear in the joint characteristic function of

Lemma A.6
.
