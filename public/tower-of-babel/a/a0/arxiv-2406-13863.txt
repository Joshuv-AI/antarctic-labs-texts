Revealing initial state properties through ultra-central symmetric heavy-ion collisions
S. M. A. Tabatabaee Mehr, S. F. Taghavi

Abstract
Heavy-ion experiments provide a new opportunity to gain a deeper understanding of the structure of nuclei. To achieve this, it is crucial to identify observables under circumstances that are minimally affected by the process that leads to the initial state of heavy-ion collisions from nuclear wavefunction. In this study, we demonstrate that when assuming scale-invariance, the effect of this stage on the initial energy or entropy density moments in ultra-central symmetric collisions is negligible for nucleon sizes of approximately 0.7 fm or larger for large nuclei. By borrowing cluster expansion method from statistical physics and using scale-invariance assumption, we calculate the average ellipticity of initial density at the presence of short-range correlation. We compare our calculations to Monte Carlo studies and assess the accuracy of various methods of short-range correlation sampling. Additionally, we find that the isobar ratio can constrain the initial state parameters, in addition to deformation. Our study indicates that the isobar ratios in ultra-central collisions are especially sensitive to the fluctuation in the weight of the nuclei constituents and the two-body correlation among nucleons. This insight is crucial for drawing conclusions about nuclear deformations based on isobar ratios.

II
Ultra-central symmetric collisions and the impact of scale-invariance

In this section, we provide evidence to support the claim that the form of the reduced thickness function has a small impact on the geometry of the initial entropy density in UCSC, by assuming the scale-invariance.

The scale-invariance assumption in T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
model relies on elliptic flow observations in ultra-central U-U collisions
[
14
]
. Uranium nuclei have a large quadruple deformation, which means that during a collision, the two nuclei may collide on their tips, bodies, or somewhere in between. It can be argued that in tip-tip collisions, there are more binary collisions compared to body-body collisions. This leads to an increase in entropy and, consequently, more particles. This concept is implemented in MC-Glauber, where the reduced thickness function is defined as

T_{R}(T_{A},T_{B})\propto\frac{1}{2}\left(T_{A}+T_{B}\right)+\alpha T_{A}T_{B},

(3)

where
\alpha
controls the contribution of binary collisions.
In the presence of binary collisions, events with the highest multiplicities should be populated by tip-tip collisions with smaller ellipticity, which goes against the STAR data from the RHIC experiment
[
18
,
19
]
. It is concluded in Ref.
[
14
]
that U-U collisions do not favor the entropy production with binary collisions. On the other hand, by using the combination
(N_{A}+N_{B})/2+\alpha N_{col}
, it is possible to link the collision geometry to the final particle production. This approach has been studied in Refs.
[
27
,
28
]
. The conclusion drawn is that the centrality dependence of the charged-particle multiplicity in Pb-Pb collisions can only be described when
\alpha
is nonvanishing. This implies that one need to replace
(N_{A}+N_{B})/2
with an unspecified function
f(N_{A},N_{B})
to open room for a function compatible with both U–U collision measurements and centrality dependence of the charged-particle multiplicity in Pb–Pb.

Since tip-tip and body-body U–U collisions lead to same multiplicity, it is possible to assume that all the collision configurations are equivalent to the collision of
N_{A}
one-to-one nucleon. The latter configuration suggests that the number of produced particles should be directly proportional to the number of nucleons, i.e.,
N_{\text{ch}}\propto N_{A}
, where the constant of proportionality depends on the one-to-one nucleon interactions. Based on this argument, one can suggest that the scaling property holds true for the collision of any configuration of
N_{A}
and
N_{B}
nucleons:

N_{\text{ch}}\propto f(N_{A},N_{B})

(4)

where
f
has a scale-invariant property,
f(cN_{A},cN_{B})=cf(N_{A},N_{B})
, leading to linear scaling
N_{\text{ch}}\propto N_{A}
when
N_{A}=N_{B}
. It is important to note that the reverse logic is not valid, and we can have functions
f(x,y)
that are not scale-invariant but collapse into a linear function at
f(x,x)
. Although microscopic theories based on CGC suggest reduced thickness function with scale-invariance property (which we will discuss shortly), there is still room for exploration regarding the scale-invariance in non-central collisions and the case where
N_{A}\neq N_{B}
from an experimental point of view.

It is a known approximation that the final multiplicity is proportional to the initial entropy,
N_{\text{ch}}\propto S
. Also, by definition, the participant thickness functions,

T_{A(B)}(\mathbf{x})=\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{N_{A(B)}}\int dz\,\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{i}),

(5)

scales similar to the number of participant
N_{A(B)}
where
\mathbf{x}
is the coordinate in the transverse space. Here, we have assumed
\int d^{3}x\rho_{\text{nucleon}}
is dimensionless and normalized to unity, and
\kappa
is a dimensionless constant for overall normalization. Therefore, by defining the reduced thickness function as the entropy density in the transverse direction,

T_{R}(\mathbf{x})=\int dz\,s(\vec{x})
,
one suggests a differential form of Eq. (
4
) as
T_{R}(T_{A},T_{B})\propto f(T_{A},T_{B})
. In Eq. (
1
), an specific form of
f(T_{A},T_{B})
is chosen.

It is important to note that the argument mentioned above assumes that the system has reached its local thermal equilibrium from its initial state. In order to extend the argument to the out-of-equilibrium period of the evolution, it is necessary to establish a connection between the entropy density,
s_{\text{hyd}}
, at the start of local thermal equilibrium,
\tau_{\text{hyd}}
, and the energy density,
e_{0}
, at an earlier time,
\tau_{0}\gtrsim 0^{+}
.
In Ref.
[
29
]
, the concept of hydrodynamic attractors was used to obtain,
s_{\text{hyd}}\tau_{\text{hyd}}\propto(e_{0}\tau_{0})^{2/3}
where the right-hand side is in the out-of-equilibrium period. This formula can be used to calculate the final multiplicity, which is given by

\frac{dN_{\text{ch}}}{d\eta}\propto\frac{dS}{d\eta}=A_{\perp}(s_{\text{hyd}}\tau_{\text{hyd}})\propto A_{\perp}(e_{0}\tau_{0})^{2/3},

(6)

where
A_{\perp}
is the transverse size of the system at the time
\tau_{\text{hyd}}

A_{\perp}
. To achieve scale-invariance in Eq. (

T_{R}^{\text{energy}}(T_{A},T_{B})=\mathcal{N}\frac{\tau_{\text{hyd}}^{3/2}}{\tau_{0}}f^{3/2}(T_{A},T_{B}).

(7)

Assuming
T_{A(B)}
as the energy thickness function, the constant
\kappa
in Eq. (
5
) has a dimension of energy too.

In the context of the CGC framework, Ref.
[
30

obtains the initial energy density using the saturation scale

\begin{split}T_{R}^{\text{energy}}&(T_{A},T_{B})\propto\\
&\frac{T_{A}T_{B}}{(T_{A}+T_{B})^{5/2}}\left[2T_{A}^{2}+7T_{A}T_{B}+2T_{B}^{2}\right].\end{split}

(8)

The scaling of

In Ref.

The global Bayesian analysis in Ref.

-0.18\end{subarray}

In summary, scale-invariance refers to the case that the initial state scaling results a same amount of scaling in the final multiplicity. If the initial entropy density is used, the reduced thickness function need to be a homogeneous function of degree one. If the energy density is used, the degree of homogeneity should be 3/2. For this study, we assume that the initial state is modeled using the entropy density.

III.1
Two-body correlations from a statistical mechanics analogy

The position of the sources is determined by the wavefunction of the colliding nuclei. Specifically, the one-body density, denoted as
p_{A}(\vec{x})
, and the two-body correlation, denoted as
c_{2}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})
, have a significant impact on the measurement of anisotropic flow. To establish a relationship between the anisotropic flow observables and
p_{A}(\vec{x})
and
c_{2}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})
at UCSC, we make use of the approximation discussed in Section
II
. In this scenario, the reduced thickness function is assumed to be the sum of
T_{A}(\mathbf{x})
and
T_{B}(\mathbf{x})
. It can be demonstrated that in this case, the moments of
T_{R}
can be divided into two parts: one part is influenced by the location of the sources, and the other by the distribution of the constituents, such as nucleons or quarks (appendix
C.1
). This can be observed in Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
), where the contribution of
2w^{2}
is separated from the average source location. In UCSC and assuming a sufficiently large nucleon-nucleon cross-section, it is assumed that all nucleons participate in the collision. This implies that the location of the initial state sources must be similar to those in each nucleus at the time of the collision. Consequently, the fluctuation of sources in a single nucleus can be translated into fluctuations of the entire initial state (appendix
C.2
).

The process of sampling the nucleon sources becomes more complicated when considering correlations, especially in the context of deformed nuclei. In subsection
III.2
, we will explore this further. The approximation introduced in UCSC allows us to take a more direct approach to understanding the effect of short-range correlations by using classical techniques from statistical mechanics. We will explore this concept in the following.

The cornerstone concept of classical statistical mechanics is that to obtain thermodynamic quantities in an equilibrated system, the time average over physical quantities can be replaced by averaging over an ensemble of systems. The state of each system is distributed based on statistical distributions, mostly depending on variables such as temperature or chemical potential. In the study of fluctuation in heavy-ion physics, the ensemble of systems has a clear counterpart, which is the collection of independent events. The concept of an equilibrated system is translated to the fact that all events in heavy-ion collisions are equivalent to each other and are achieved under the same experimental conditions, such as the center of mass energy or the same species collisions. The “state” of an event is the position of the sources, which are distributed based on the nucleus wavefunction. To make the analogy clearer, assume
P_{A}(\vec{x}_{1},\ldots,\vec{x}_{A})
as the distribution of sources in a nucleus,

P_{A}(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{A}}F_{A}(\vec{x}),

(13)

where
\mathcal{Z}_{A}
is
P_{A}
normalization factor. If sources are distributed based on a Gaussian profile with width
\sigma
, one needs to define
F_{A}(\mathbf{x})=e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{A}|\mathbf{x}_{i}|^{2}}
, analogous to a free non-relativistic gas with
F_{N}(\vec{p})=e^{-\beta E}=e^{-\frac{1}{2mkT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\vec{p}_{i}|^{2}}
.

This analogy becomes particularly useful when attempting to account for short-range correlations. The cluster expansion method is utilized to determine thermodynamic quantities in the presence of short-range correlation (see Ref.
[
31
]
). To apply this method to our problem, we define

F_{A}(\vec{x})=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{A}p_{A}(\vec{x}_{i})e^{\frac{1}{A}\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}f_{a}(\vec{x}_{i})}\right]\left[\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq A}c_{2}(\vec{x}_{j},\vec{x}_{j})\right].

(14)

In statistical mechanics, the partition function typically serves as the generating function. The parameter
\beta
is used to extract the thermodynamic variables and their fluctuations. In our approach, we introduce an auxiliary term
e^{\frac{1}{A}\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}f_{a}(\vec{x}_{i})}
, where
\lambda_{a}
plays a similar role to
\beta
, but it needs to be set to zero once the desired average is obtained by differentiating the partition function. In order to find the desired moment, it is necessary to select an appropriate function
f_{a}(\vec{x})
.

The cluster expansion provides a closed form for the grand-partition function (details in appendix
C.3
),

\begin{split}\log\mathscr{Q}&(\langle A\rangle,\lambda_{a})=\\
b_{1}(\lambda_{a})&\;\langle A\rangle+\left[b_{2}(\lambda_{a})-2b_{2}(0)b_{1}(\lambda_{a})\right]\;\langle A\rangle^{2}+\cdots.\end{split}

(15)

The
\ell
-cluster integrals,
b_{\ell}(\lambda_{a})
, connect partition function to
p_{A}(\vec{x})
and
c_{2}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})
,

\displaystyle b_{1}(\lambda_{a})

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}x\;p_{A}(\vec{x})\;e^{\frac{1}{A}\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}f_{a}(\vec{x})},

(16a)

\displaystyle b_{2}(\lambda_{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x_{1}d^{3}x_{2}\;p_{A}(\vec{x}_{1})p_{A}(\vec{x}_{2})\;

(16b)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pte^{\frac{1}{A}\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}(f_{a}(\vec{x}_{1})+f_{a}(\vec{x}_{2}))}\;f_{12}.

where the so-called Mayer function is defined as
f_{12}=c_{2}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})-1
. Here, we assume that
\langle A\rangle\equiv A
is large enough that we can disregard the fluctuation of
A
and approximate
\mathscr{Q}(\langle A\rangle,\lambda_{a})\propto Q_{A}(\lambda_{a})
, where
Q_{A}(\lambda_{a})
is the canonical partition function at a fixed value of
A
. It’s important to note that the calculated observables from canonical and grand-canonical partition functions might have differences in subleading orders in the
1/A
expansion, which are ignored in the present study.

To find moments such as
\langle\{f_{1}\}^{m_{1}}\{f_{2}\}^{m_{2}}\rangle
, one should calculate
\partial_{\lambda_{1}}^{m_{1}}\partial_{\lambda_{2}}^{m_{2}}\mathscr{Q}(\langle A\rangle,\lambda_{a})/\mathscr{Q}(\langle A\rangle,\lambda_{a})
and set
\lambda_{a}=0
. To obtain
\langle\{r^{2}\}\rangle
, we choose
f_{1}(\vec{x})=|\mathbf{x}|^{2}
,
m_{1}=1
, and
m_{2}=0
. The functions
f_{1}(\vec{x})=f_{2}^{*}(\vec{x})=|\mathbf{x}|^{2}e^{2i\varphi}
with
m_{1}=m_{2}=1
corresponds to the moment average
\left\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{2i\varphi}\}\right|^{2}\right\rangle
.

The method described above is general. For a specific choice of
p_{A}(\mathbf{x})
as
\rho_{\text{WS}}(\mathbf{x})
with radius
\tilde{R}_{0}
and skin thickness
\tilde{a}_{0}
, and
c_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\Theta(|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}|-d_{\text{min}})
, which corresponds to nucleons’ hard inner core with a radius of
d_{\text{min}}/2
, we obtain an analytical estimate for the radius as

\begin{split}\left\langle\{r^{2}\}_{s,A}\right\rangle=&\frac{2\tilde{R}_{0}^{2}}{5}\left[1+\frac{7\pi^{2}}{3}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}_{0}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{2}\right.\\
&\left.\quad+\frac{7\pi^{2}}{3}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}_{0}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{d_{\text{min}}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{3}+\cdots\right].\end{split}

(17)

Here,
\langle\{\cdots\}_{s,A}\rangle
stands for the average over the position of nucleons in a single nucleus
A
. The elliptic shape of the initial density can also be found as

\begin{split}&\left\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{2i\varphi}\}_{s,A}\right|^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{A}\frac{8\tilde{R}_{0}^{2}}{35}\left[1+6\pi^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}_{0}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{2}\right.\\
&\left.\hskip 28.45274pt+\frac{31\pi^{4}}{3}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}_{0}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{4}+d^{\prime}_{\varphi}\left(\frac{d_{\text{min}}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{3}+\cdots\right],\end{split}

(18)

where:

d^{\prime}_{\varphi}=-A\left[1-6\pi^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}_{0}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{2}-\frac{31\pi^{4}}{3}\left(\frac{\tilde{a}_{0}}{\tilde{R}_{0}}\right)^{4}\right].

(19)

When calculating the expression
d^{\prime}_{\varphi}
, we replaced WS distribution with a step function
\Theta(\tilde{R_{0}}-|\vec{x}|)
to facilitate the analytical integration. We compared our analytical estimate for
d^{\prime}_{\varphi}
with the numerical result and found that the numerical value of
d^{\prime}_{\varphi}
is approximately half of the value obtained using the step function, indicating the significance of the skin thickness in determining this quantity. We confirmed our calculation using Monte Carlo sampling in sections
III.2
and
IV
.

III.2
Note on the one-body density and two-body correlation and Monte Carlo event sampling

Short-range correlation changes the one-body distribution. For example, nucleons with a hard central core will be pushed into the outer regions, altering the one-body density assumed by the WS distribution. The two-body density is given by

\begin{split}&p_{A}^{(2)}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})=\\
&\frac{\rho_{\text{WS}}(\vec{x}_{1})\rho_{\text{WS}}(\vec{x}_{2})\Theta(|\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2}|-d_{\text{min}})}{\int d\vec{x}^{\prime}_{1}d\vec{x}^{\prime}_{2}\rho_{\text{WS}}(\vec{x}^{\prime}_{1})\rho_{\text{WS}}(\vec{x}^{\prime}_{2})\Theta(|\vec{x}^{\prime}_{1}-\vec{x}^{\prime}_{2}|-d_{\text{min}})},\end{split}

(20)

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step-function. It is important to note that
p_{A}(\vec{x}_{1})=\int d\vec{x}_{2}p_{A}^{(2)}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})
is not coincident with the WS distribution, although we might approximate it with the WS distribution with modified
R_{0}
and
a_{0}
at small values of
d_{\text{min}}
(see section.
III.3
). The two-body correlation also deviates from the step-function as discussed in Ref.
[
32
]
.

To overcome the problem of one-body density modification, in T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
event generator, the radial direction of the sources is sampled first based on WS distribution to keep the radial size of the nucleus fixed. Afterward, the spherical angles of the sources are sampled iteratively. Sources are sorted in the radial direction and moving from smaller radii, the
i
th source is placed at
\vec{x}_{i}
if its distance from all other
i-1
sources is greater than
d_{\text{min}}
, otherwise the
i
th source angel are rejected and resample the angles until it is placed somewhere in the space. Although the pre-sampling of the radial direction is performed to keep the WS structure under control, it systematically leads to inaccuracy in placing the nucleons with minimum distance. Considering two nucleons are sampled at
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
radial direction, the maximum distance of these two nucleons is
|r_{1}-r_{2}|
, meaning if this distance is smaller than
d_{\text{min}}
, it is impossible to place the second source at any given spherical angles. In T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
event generator, the angles are sampled 1000 times and if it fails, the source leaves it at its last iteration. The failure percentage is negligible for small values of
d_{\text{min}}
, but this systematic inconsistency should be considered as a hint that this method may lead to an inaccurate two-body correlation. More importantly, for deformed nuclei, the nucleus radius depends on the orientation, and the method employed by T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
event generator is no longer applicable.

There is also a caveat in placing the sources iteratively.
One may assume that at each iteration, all three directions are generated based on the WS distribution, and the minimum distances with former generated sources are examined iteratively. However, in the iterative method starting from WS with no information from short-range behavior, the
i
th source generated in the
i
th iteration is only correlated with all
(i-1)
sources generated in the previous iterations. The first source, in fact, contains no information from short-range correlation. On the other hand, if we start from
p_{A}(\vec{x})
and
c_{2}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})
, the information of the correlation is already encoded in the sampling from the first iteration. We have examined this claim in a 1D toy model with only two sources, where each source is generated based on a Gaussian distribution, and the correlation is imposed using minimum distance. We can compare our toy Monte Carlo with analytical calculations using this simplified toy model. The study mentioned in the appendix
E
suggests that the iterative method works correctly only if we use the true one-body density that contains the short-range effect information. This indicates that in more realistic cases, the iterative method works well if we use the one-body density and two-body correlation extracted from Eq. (
20
). Short-range correlations can affect the radius of spherically symmetric nuclei. In the T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
model, the radius is kept fixed, so one considers that the short-range correlation is accounted for in this method, although pre-generating the radius can cause issues with source placement and not applicable for deformed nuclei.

An alternative way of generating sources using WS distribution and minimum distance among nucleons is to generate
A
random sources based on WS and then compare all the mutual distances. If there is any pair with condition
|\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2}|<d_{\text{min}}
, the whole set of
A
sources should be discarded, and a new set is generated. We call this method as direct method. In this way, we find consistent one-body density (which is different from the original WS) and two-body correlation. There is no problem with the position of the source when using this method, and it can be used for any deformed nuclei. However, it should be noted that this method takes longer to execute than the iterative method, especially for larger values of
d_{\text{min}}
. In Ref.
[
33
]
, the sources with appropriate two-body correlation are generated in a box using Metropolis random search, and then the WS probability distribution is imposed to choose those sources relevant to the one-body density. This method has been employed by SMASH initial state model in Ref.
[
34
]
. We have examined a similar strategy in our 1D toy model by generating sources with some minimum distances and then imposing the Gaussian constraint. The result is coincident with direct method and leads to correct one-body density and two-body correlation. There is also a recent method (we call it as shifting method) in imposing the two-body correlations by generating independent sources first based on WS distribution and then shifting away the sources using a carefully constructed function based on the two-body correlation
[
32
]
.

In Fig.
2
, we compare three different methods: direct method, iterative method, and the shifting method, to appreciate their differences. The iterative method generates all three coordinates of the sources, not just the angular part, which makes it different from the method implemented in T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
. To eliminate the complications arising from entropy production and nucleon participation, we consider only one Au nucleus to generate point-like sources and impose a two-body correlation using a step-function. We calculate
\langle\{r^{2}\}_{s,A}\rangle
and
\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{i2\varphi}\}_{s,A}\right|^{2}\rangle
for different values of
d_{\text{min}}
. In the direct method, we did not go beyond
d_{\text{min}}=0.4\,
[fm] due to the inefficiency of the direct method. The figure shows that all three sets of events give the same values when there are zero short-range correlations. However, for
d_{\text{min}}>0
, the main difference appears in
\langle\{r^{2}\}_{s,A}\rangle
, which quantifies the size of the one-body density. Short-range correlation modifies the one-body density, but the modification from the three methods is different. The dashed (solid) lines are analytical (numerical) estimations of
d^{\prime}_{\varphi}
. The cluster expansion calculation is compatible with direct method of source sampling. The difference in
\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{i2\varphi}\}_{s,A}\right|^{2}\rangle
is less pronounced. The direct method and cluster expansion calculation rely on using consistent one-body density and two-body correlation. Iteratively sampling sources without considering the short-range correlation into the one-body density can lead to inconsistency. The authors still need to investigate in greater detail the observed difference between the direct method and the shifting method.

III.3
Radius and skin thickness redefinition

As seen from Eq. (
17
), the short-range correlation modifies the size of the nucleus as expected. The physical values of the radius and skin thickness contain the effect of the short-range correlation. For that reason, we redefine “bare” values of
\tilde{R}_{0}
and
\tilde{a}_{0}
to absorb the term corresponding to the correlation in
\left\langle\{r^{2}\}_{s,A}\right\rangle
and write the final result in terms of “physical” values
R_{0}
and
a_{0}
. Using Eq. (
17
), we cannot fix both radius and skin thickness. We assume two cases: first, the skin thickness is fixed,
\tilde{a}_{0}=a_{0}
, but the radius is changed, and second, the radius is fixed,
\tilde{R}_{0}=R_{0}
, but the skin thickness is modified. For the former case, we find the value of
\tilde{R}_{0}
in terms of physical quantities
R_{0}
as follows:

\begin{split}\tilde{R}_{0}^{2}&=R_{0}^{2}\left[1-A\frac{7\pi^{2}}{3}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{d_{\text{min}}}{R_{0}}\right)^{3}+\cdots\right],\end{split}

(21)

while
\tilde{a}_{0}
for the latter reads as

\begin{split}\tilde{a}_{0}&=a_{0}\left[1-\frac{A}{2}\left(\frac{d_{\text{min}}}{R_{0}}\right)^{3}+\cdots\right].\end{split}

(22)

III.4
Constituent weight fluctuation

In addition to the fluctuation in the position of the sources, the contribution of each source to the initial entropy density can fluctuate. Ref.
[
20
]
discusses that the final multiplicity fluctuation in p–Pb collision can be described by assuming that the participant contribution to the initial entropy density fluctuation follows a Gamma distribution,

p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma)=\frac{k^{k}}{\Gamma(k)}\gamma^{k-1}e^{-k\gamma},\qquad\sigma_{\text{fluc}}=1/\sqrt{k}.

(23)

To take the effect of fluctuation into account, we replace
p_{A}(\vec{x}_{i})
with
p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma_{i})p_{A}(\vec{x}_{i})
and modify the auxiliary function to
e^{\frac{1}{A}\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}\gamma_{i}f_{a}(\vec{x}_{i})}
in Eq. (
14
). We have examined the contribution of
\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}(d_{\text{min}}/R_{0})^{3}
in moment
\left\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{2i\varphi}\}_{s,A}\right|^{2}\right\rangle
. This contribution is zero for the case WS distribution is replaced by step-function. Simulation based on T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
model also confirms that this contribution is small. Therefore, we calculate the contribution of
\sigma_{\text{fluc}}
and
d_{\text{min}}
separately. By ignoring the effect of two-body correlation, we find that the canonical partition function is written in terms of 1-cluster integral,
Q_{A}(\lambda)\propto b_{1}^{A}(\lambda)
. Employing
\partial_{\lambda}Q_{A}(\lambda)/Q_{A}(\lambda)|_{\lambda=0}
leading to

\langle\{f\}_{s,A}\rangle=\int d^{3}x\,f(\vec{x})\,p_{A}(\vec{x}).

(24)

In the case of calculating second-order moments average, from
\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\partial_{\lambda_{2}}Q_{A}(\lambda)/Q_{A}(\lambda)|_{\lambda_{i}=0}
, we find

\begin{split}\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s,A}\{f_{2}\}_{s,A}\rangle&=\frac{1+\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}}{A}\int d^{3}x\,f_{1}(\vec{x})f_{2}(\vec{x})\,p_{A}(\vec{x})\\
&+\frac{A-1}{A}\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s,A}\rangle\langle\{f_{2}\}_{s,A}\rangle,\end{split}

(25)

where we have used the fact that
\int d\gamma\,\gamma\,p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma)=1
and
\int d\gamma\,\gamma^{2}\,p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma)=1+\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}
. Approximating
Q_{A}(\lambda)
by its grand-canonical counterpart, as discussed in section
III.1
, would have led to replacing
A-1
with
A
in the second term of Eq. (
25
). This modification is negligible for large
A
and ignored here.

To find the values of
\langle\{r^{2}\}\rangle
and
\langle\{r^{2}\}^{2}\rangle
, we need to substitute
f(\vec{x})=|\mathbf{x}|^{2}
and
f_{1}(\vec{x})=f_{2}(\vec{x})=|\mathbf{x}|^{2}
into Equations (
24
) and (
25
) respectively. It is evident from Equation (
25
) that the second term contributes the most when
A
is large, which implies that
\langle\{r^{2}\}^{2}\rangle\approx\langle\{r^{2}\}\rangle^{2}
. This shows that the value of
\langle\{r^{2}\}^{2}\rangle
is mainly influenced by the size and has a small contribution from fluctuation. On the other hand, to determine
\left\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{2i\varphi}\}_{s,A}\right|^{2}\right\rangle
, we choose
f_{1}(\vec{x})=f_{2}^{*}(\vec{x})=|\mathbf{x}|^{2}e^{2i\varphi}
. In this case, the second term in Equation (
25
) is zero, which means that the primary contribution is proportional to
(1+\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2})/A
, indicating that it solely originates from the fluctuations.

In Equation (
25
), the presence of
\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}
arises from the calculation of second order moments. Therefore, the dependence on
\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}
can be accurate for values greater than unity. When there is fluctuation present, we use the expression
\{f\}_{s}=\sum_{i}f(\vec{x}_{i})/N
instead of
\{f\}_{s}=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}f(\vec{x}_{i})/\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}
to calculate many event averages. The approximation we make is that the numerator and denominator fluctuate independently event by event. We have provided a more detailed discussion in Appendix
C
. We validated this approximation for
\sigma_{\text{fluc}}
up to 2 using a toy Monte Carlo.

Ellipticity fluctuation.
In the previous section, we explicitly estimated two moments of the initial state entropy density. In this section, we will use these moments to derive an analytical estimate for the average ellipticity. Additionally, we will validate this estimation, along with the moments stated in Eqs. (

VI
Summary

In this paper, we demonstrated that the functionality of the reduced thickness function has minimal effect on the moments of initial state entropy density in UCSC when the nucleon width is large enough, and the initial state has scale-invariance property. We validated this argument by studying a few initial state moments using the T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
Monte Carlo event generator (see Figs.
1
and
7
).

In our study on UCSC, we utilized the cluster expansion method to determine the impact of the one-body density and two-body correlation of the nucleus on moments
\left\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{2i\varphi}\}\right|^{2}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle\{r^{2}\}^{2}\right\rangle
, and
\epsilon_{2}\{2\}
. To carry out these calculations, we employed the WS distribution for the distribution of nucleons and hard inner core for nucleons to enforce correlation among them. Our findings indicate that even though the minimum distance between nucleons modifies the one-body density, it is still possible to describe this density with WS, with a larger radius up to at least
d_{\text{min}}=0.4\,
[fm]. We also compared different methods of Monte Carlo sampling of sources in the presence of short-range correlations and demonstrated that our approach can be used to assess the accuracy of short-range correlations sampling. This revealed that incorporating information on short-range correlations in the one-body density is essential for obtaining accurate results. We calculated the effect of cross-section and nucleon substructure. To validate, we compared results with T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
model outcome.

We investigated the impact of initial state parameters on the elliptic flow ratio for Ru–Ru and Zr–Zr in ultra-central collisions. Our findings confirmed that the quadrupole deformation of Ru and Zr has the most significant contribution. We also found that the effect of constituent weight fluctuation and two-body correlation must be taken into account when inferring the nuclei deformation.

Although we compared our calculation with the T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
model in this study, our result should be valid for any other initial state models that satisfy the scale-invariance.

Appendix C
Event-by-event fluctuation and cluster-expansion

In this section, we discuss the details of calculations based on the cluster expansion.

C.1
Decomposition of constituents contribution and source position

We start with the collision of two nuclei, each with mass number A. Assuming nucleons as structureless spherical objects, the reduced thickness function is given by

T_{R}(\mathbf{x})\approx\frac{\mathcal{N}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{part}}}\gamma_{i}\int dz\,\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{i}),

(65)

where we have used Eq. (1) with
p=1
, which is similar to any initial state with the scale-invariance property at large nucleon size.
Here, the location of the nucleon is represented by
\vec{x}_{i}
, and the number of participants is denoted by
N_{\text{part}}
. Following Refs.
[
14
,
20
]
, the parameter
\gamma_{i}
is randomly selected from a gamma distribution that has a mean value of unity and a variance of
\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}
. The shape of
T_{R}(\mathbf{x})
can be analyzed by calculating its characteristic function,

e^{W_{R}(\bm{k})}=\frac{\int d\mathbf{x}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}T_{R}(\mathbf{x})}{\int d\mathbf{x}T_{R}(\mathbf{x})}.

(66)

The expansion coefficient of
e^{W_{R}(\bm{k})}
(
W_{R}(\bm{k})
) in terms of
\bm{k}
leads to the moments (cumulants) of
T_{R}(\mathbf{x})

[
38
]
. We label the averages with superscript
{(c)}
if we refer to cumulants.
Substituting
T_{R}
from Eq. (
65
) into the above, we find that the characteristic function can be written as
e^{W_{\text{nucleon}}(\bm{k})}e^{W_{\text{source}}(\bm{k})}
. For Gaussian nucleon with width
w
, one finds
W_{\text{nucleon}}(\bm{k})=-|\bm{k}|^{2}w^{2}/2
. Therefore, the cumulants of reduced thickness function depends on the nucleons width trivially only in
\{r^{2}\}
. The non-trivial information, therefore, encoded in the position of the sources,

e^{W_{\text{source}}(\bm{k})}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{part}}}\gamma_{i}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{part}}}\gamma_{i}}.

(67)

C.2
Source fluctuation distribution and moments average

Decomposing the sources into target and projectile participants.
The initial state event-by-event fluctuation comes from the fluctuation of the sources (nucleons, constituent). Suppose we have a distribution of the location of sources given by
P_{s}(\gamma_{1},\vec{x}_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N_{\text{tot}}},\vec{x}_{N_{\text{tot}}})
, where
N_{\text{tot}}=n_{c}N_{\text{part}}
, and assume large
\sigma_{\text{inel}}^{\text{NN}}
such that all nucleons participate in the collision. In this case, no matter how complex the distribution is, the scale-invariance in UCSC allows us to break it down into two separate distributions of target and projectile.

\displaystyle P_{s}(\gamma,\vec{x})=P_{A}(\gamma,\vec{x})P_{B}(\gamma,\vec{x}),

(68)

where
P_{A(B)}
is the distribution of participant sources from the target (projectile). The correction to this decomposition is discussed in appendix.
F.2
. The parameters
\gamma
and
\vec{x}
are used as collective coordinates that run through the number of sources in the target (projectile) in
P_{A(B)}
, or their combination in
P_{s}
. The average over position of sources is given as follows (see Eq. (
67
)),

\{f\}_{s}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}f(\mathbf{x}_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}},

(69)

where
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})
is a given function, defining our moment. The effect of nucleon width appears only in the cumulants
\{r^{2}\}^{(c)}
. In the present study, we are particularly interested in averages
\{r^{2}\}^{(c)}
and
\{r^{2}e^{i2\varphi}\}^{(c)}
where due to the rotational invariance of WS at the leading order, these cumulants are equivalent with the moments
\{r^{2}\}
and
\{r^{2}e^{i2\varphi}\}
.

Assuming that the
\gamma
variables fluctuate independently of the source location, we can calculate the average of the moments using

\langle\{f\}_{s}\rangle=\int d^{N}\gamma\,d^{2N}x\,d^{N}z\,\{f\}_{s}\,P_{s}(\gamma,\vec{x}).

(70)

In particular, one finds the following relation between moments and the nucleons position averages:

\displaystyle\langle\{r^{2}\}\rangle=\langle\{r^{2}\}_{s}\rangle+2w^{2},

(71a)

\displaystyle\langle\{r^{2}\}^{2}\rangle=\left\langle\left(\{r^{2}\}_{s}+2w^{2}\right)^{2}\right\rangle,

(71b)

\displaystyle\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{i2\varphi}\}\right|^{2}\rangle=\langle\left|\{r^{2}e^{i2\varphi}\}_{s}\right|^{2}\rangle.

(71c)

For nucleons with substructure,
w
should be replaced by
v
.
For symmetric collisions
A=B
, one can assume
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{A}}\gamma_{i}\approx\sum_{i=1+N_{A}}^{N_{A}+N_{B}}\gamma_{i}
, and decompose Eq. (
69
) into two parts,
\{f\}_{s}\approx\frac{1}{2}\left[\{f\}_{s,A}+\{f\}_{s,B}\right]
. Since
P_{A}
and
P_{B}
are same distributions in Eq. (
68
), one immediately deduce that

\displaystyle\langle\{f\}_{s}\rangle=\langle\{f\}_{s,A}\rangle,

(72a)

\displaystyle\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s}\{f_{2}\}_{s}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s,A}\{f_{2}\}_{s,A}\rangle+\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s,A}\rangle\langle\{f_{2}\}_{s,A}\rangle\right],

(72b)

The average on the left-hand side is taken over
P_{s}
, while on the right-hand side, it is taken over
P_{A}
, given
\mathcal{O}_{A}
. Therefore, we only need to calculate observables for one nucleus participant. The correction due to the finite nucleon-nucleon inelastic cross-section will be discussed in appendix
F.2
.

The effect of the constituent weight fluctuation on observables.
As mentioned, the constituent weight fluctuation is followed by the gamma distribution in Eq. (
23
). Given that fluctuations are assumed independent, referring to Eq. (
69
), the the averages such as
\langle\{f\}_{s}\rangle
or
\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s}\{f_{2}\}_{s}\rangle
can be written as the average of numerator divided by denominator. Using
p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma)
to find the denominator average, one estimates that

\displaystyle\langle\{f\}_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right\rangle,

(73a)

\displaystyle\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s}\{f_{2}\}_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\left\langle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})f_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right\rangle.

(73b)

There is an extra term proportional to
\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}/N^{3}
in Eq. (
73b
). This is ignored in this study as it is a subleading contribution in the
1/N
expansion.

C.3
Initial state fluctuation from cluster expansion method

For independent nucleons without structure, the distribution of A sources is given by:

P_{A}(\gamma,\vec{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{A}p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma_{i})p_{A}(\vec{x}_{i}),

(74)

which allows us to decompose the average on the right-hand side of equation (
73a
) and perform the integration over a single source distribution. This leads to Eq. (
24
).
In the case of calculating second-order moments average,
\langle\{f_{1}\}_{s,A}\{f_{2}\}_{s,A}\rangle
, we find Eq. (
25
)
from equation (
73b
) where we have used
\int d\gamma\,\gamma^{2}\;p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma)=1+\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}
. To include the effect of short-range correlation, we employ the statistical mechanics analogy as discussed in Sec.
III
where the distribution is defined as
P_{A}(\vec{x})=F_{A}(\vec{x})/\mathcal{Z}_{A}
.

Although we usually assume a fixed number of sources, when studying fluctuation in cluster expansion, closed-form results can be obtained in grand-canonical systems. It should be noted that the calculated observables from canonical and grand-canonical systems may differ with terms in the order of
O(1/A)
. With this caveat in mind, we can define grand-canonical partition functions as follows:

\mathscr{Q}(z,V,\lambda_{a})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}\mathcal{Q}_{n}(V,\lambda_{a}),

(75)

where the canonical partition function is given as
\mathcal{Q}_{A}(V,\lambda_{a})=\mathcal{Z}_{A}(V,\lambda_{a})/h^{3A}A!
. The dimensionful parameter
h
is included to make the partition functions dimensionless. The Mayer function
f_{ij}
is defined as
c_{2}(\vec{x}_{i},\vec{x}_{j})=1+f_{ij}
, which leads to

\mathcal{Q}_{A}(V,\lambda_{a})=\frac{1}{h^{3A}A!}\int\mathcal{D}^{3A}x\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq A}(1+f_{ij}),

(76)

where the measure is defined as

\mathcal{D}^{3}x(\lambda_{a})=d^{3}x\;p_{A}(\vec{x})\;e^{\frac{1}{A}\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}f_{a}(\vec{x}_{i})}.

(77)

In the limit of no correlation, we have
f_{ij}=0
, and

\mathcal{Q}_{A}(V,\lambda_{a})=\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{A}(V,\lambda_{a})/A!=\left[\int\mathcal{D}^{3}x(\lambda_{a})\right]^{A}/h^{3A}A!.

(78)

The averages
\langle\{f_{1}\}\rangle
and
\langle\{f_{1}\}\{f_{2}\}\rangle
corresponds to
f_{1}(\mathbf{x})
and
f_{2}(\mathbf{x})
in Eq. (
24
) and (
25
) can be obtained by appropriately differentiating
\mathcal{Q}_{A}(V,\lambda_{a})
with respect to
\lambda_{a}
and setting
\lambda_{a}
to zero in the end.

To calculate the canonical partition function in the presence of correlation, one needs to evaluate integrals of the form
\int\mathcal{D}^{3}x_{1}\cdots\mathcal{D}^{3}x_{N}f_{\alpha_{1}}f_{\alpha_{2}}\cdots f_{\alpha_{k}}
, where
\alpha_{i}
represents a pair of distinct numbers between 1 and
A
. Depending on the values of the pairs
\alpha_{i}
, the integral may or may not be decomposed into lower-dimensional integrals or clusters. In the presence of correlations, finding the grand-canonical partition function is an easier task, and it is given by:

\log\mathscr{Q}(z,\lambda_{a})=\sum_{\ell=1}b_{\ell}(\lambda_{a})z^{\ell}.

(79)

The first term in the above expansion corresponds to the grand-canonical partition function between sources with no correlations. The coefficients
b_{\ell}(\lambda_{a})
are Mayer cluster integrals. The 1-cluster and 2-cluster integrals read as:

\displaystyle b_{1}(\lambda_{a})

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}^{3}x(\lambda_{a}),

(80a)

\displaystyle b_{2}(\lambda_{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathcal{D}^{3}x_{1}(\lambda_{a})\mathcal{D}^{3}x_{2}(\lambda_{a})\;f_{12}.

(80b)

Now, we are able to connect the cluster integrals to the desired moments.
The average value of
A
can be obtained as follows,

\langle A\rangle=\left.z\frac{\partial\log\mathscr{Q}}{\partial z}\right|_{\lambda_{a}\to 0}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell b_{\ell}(0)z^{\ell}.

(81)

In the absence of correlation, only 1-cluster integral is non-vanishing, meaning
\langle A\rangle
is equal to
b_{1}(0)z
. From the definition in Eq. (
77
), we know that
b_{1}(0)=1
. Therefore, we obtain
z=\langle A\rangle
. Also, assuming
A\equiv\langle A\rangle
, we can conclude from Eqs. (
75
) and (
78
) that grand-canonical partition function is written in terms of canonical partition function as
\mathscr{Q}=e^{A\mathcal{Q}_{1}}
. To account for the effect of correlation, we need to find the correction to
z=\langle A\rangle
. We do this by substituting
z=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}\langle A\rangle^{\ell}
into Eq. (
81
) and finding
a_{\ell}
order by order. Afterward, we substitute the expansion of
z
into Eq. (
79
) to obtain the partition function, which is written as

\begin{split}\log\mathscr{Q}&(\langle A\rangle,\lambda_{a})=\\
b_{1}(\lambda_{a})&\;\langle A\rangle+\left[b_{2}(\lambda_{a})-2b_{2}(0)b_{1}(\lambda_{a})\right]\;\langle A\rangle^{2}+\cdots.\end{split}

(82)

From Eq. (
75
), we note that the dominant term in
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{n}(\lambda_{a})/n!
is around
n\approx z
for large
z\approx\langle A\rangle
. Therefore, we can approximately assume
\mathscr{Q}(\langle A\rangle,\lambda_{a})\propto\mathcal{Q}_{A}(\lambda_{a})
. The moments average can be obtained by appropriately differentiating the grand partition function with respect to
\lambda_{a}
as discussed in the main text.

The introduced method is generic, and a realistic one-body density and two-body correlation can be used to perform the cluster integrals. For particular choice of one-body density and two-body correlation, we can perform the integrals analytically. In particular, we assume
p_{A}(\vec{x})
keeps the WS distribution structure. Considering that the value of
R_{0}
and
a_{0}
in WS is fixed from experimental measurements, we start with unknown parameters
\tilde{R}_{0}
and
\tilde{a}_{0}
, and then adjust it so that
\{r^{2}\}
leads to a correct nucleus radius. This is a reasonable approximation for small values of
d_{\text{min}}
(see subsection.
III.3
). To impose two-body correlation, we assume a hard central core for each nucleon,

c_{2}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})=1+f_{12}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2})=\begin{cases}0&|\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2}|\leq d_{\text{min}}\\
1&|\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2}|>d_{\text{min}}\end{cases}.

(83)

The details of the moments calculation can be found in appendix
D
. The results are presented in Eqs. (
17
) and (
19
).

The one-body density is given by

p_{A}(\vec{x})\approx\frac{1}{\rho_{0}\,}\frac{\rho_{\text{WS}}(\vec{x})}{-8a_{0}^{3}\text{Li}_{3}(e^{R_{0}/a_{0}})}.

(87)

The moments of WS distribution can be obtained analytically as follows:

\begin{split}I_{m}(R_{0},a_{0})&=\frac{4^{m}a_{0}^{2m}\,\Gamma(m+2)\Gamma(m+1)\;\text{Li}_{3+2m}\left(-e^{R_{0}/a_{0}}\right)}{\text{Li}_{3}\left(-e^{R_{0}/a_{0}}\right)}\\
&=\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(m+1)}{\Gamma(m+5/2)}\right)R_{0}^{2m}\left[1+\frac{2m(2m+5)\pi^{2}}{6}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}+\cdots\right].\end{split}

(88)

In particular, following moments are relevant for us in this study,

\displaystyle I_{1}(R_{0},a_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{2R_{0}^{2}}{5}\left[1+\frac{7\pi^{2}}{3}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}+\cdots\right],

(89a)

\displaystyle I_{2}(R_{0},a_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{8R_{0}^{4}}{35}\left[1+6\pi^{2}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}+\frac{31}{3}\pi^{4}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)^{4}+\cdots\right].

In the following, we suggest an analytical estimation for
J_{m,n,\ell}
integral when a minimum distance between nucleons is imposed. To calculate
J_{m,n,\ell}
analytically, we still need to approximate WS distribution. It is important to note that any approximation to WS will result in a slight error to our estimation since the integral

is the correction to the observable. We replace WS with a step-function
\Theta(\tilde{R}_{0}^{2}-|\vec{x}|^{2})
and calculate the integral in Eq. (

) analytically by using the integral representation of the step-function.

In cases where there is no correlation between sources, the value of
f_{ij}
becomes zero. In such cases, the absence of correlation causes the moments

\rho_{\text{WS}}(|\vec{x}|)\propto\theta(R_{0}^{2}-|\vec{x}_{1}|^{2})

J(R_{0},d_{\text{min}})=\frac{1}{(4/3\pi R_{0}^{3})^{2}}\int d^{3}x_{1}d^{3}x_{2}\;f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\;\theta(R_{0}^{2}-|\vec{x}_{1}|^{2})\theta(R_{0}^{2}-|\vec{x}_{2}|^{2})f_{12}(\vec{x}_{1},\vec{x}_{2}),

(90)

we employ the integral representation of the step-function

\Theta(x)=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon\to 0^{+}\\
\Lambda\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\frac{e^{i\tau x}}{\tau-i\epsilon}d\tau.

(91)

Performing the change of variable
\vec{x}_{1}=(\vec{x}^{\prime}_{2}-\vec{x}^{\prime}_{1})/2
and
\vec{x}_{2}=(\vec{x}^{\prime}_{2}+\vec{x}^{\prime}_{1})/2

J(R_{0},d_{\text{min}})=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon\to 0^{+}\\

\Lambda^{\prime}\to\infty\end{subarray}}\tilde{J}(R_{0},d_{\text{min}},\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}),

(92)

where

\tilde{J}=\frac{1}{8}\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\int d\tau d\tau^{\prime}\,d^{3}x_{1}^{\prime}d^{3}x_{2}^{\prime}\;\frac{1}{\tau-i\epsilon}\frac{1}{\tau^{\prime}-i\epsilon^{\prime}}\;f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{2})\;e^{iR_{0}^{2}(\tau+\tau^{\prime})+\frac{i(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}{2}r_{1}r_{2}\cos\Omega_{12}-\frac{i(\tau+\tau^{\prime})}{4}(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2})}f_{12}(r_{1}).

(93)

Here, we have used the

, and

\begin{split}|\vec{x}_{1}|^{2}&=\frac{1}{4}\left[r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}-2r_{1}r_{2}\cos\Omega_{12}\right],\qquad|\vec{x}_{2}|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left[r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+2r_{1}r_{2}\cos\Omega_{12}\right].\end{split}

(94)

In present study, the function

\int_{0}^{d}dx\,x^{\alpha}e^{-ax^{2}-bx}=\frac{d^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}+\cdots,

To calculate the integral

\begin{split}\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon^{\prime}\to 0^{+}\\
\Lambda^{\prime}\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathcal{I}_{n}(1,1,\eta)&=\int d\tau^{\prime}\,\frac{e^{iR_{0}^{2}\tau^{\prime}}}{\tau^{\prime}-i\epsilon^{\prime}}\left(\frac{2\pi i}{\tau^{\prime n}}-2\pi i\frac{e^{-iR_{0}^{2}\tau^{\prime}}f_{n-1}(\tau^{\prime})}{\tau^{\prime n}}\right).\end{split}

(100)

(101)

For the second part of the integral in Eq. (

\int_{-\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}d\tau^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{\tau^{\prime}-i\epsilon^{\prime}}\frac{f_{n-1}(\tau^{\prime})}{(\tau^{\prime}-i\epsilon^{\prime\prime})^{n}}=-\frac{1}{\Lambda^{\prime}}\left(-2\frac{(iR_{0}^{2})^{n-1}}{(n-2)!}\right),

(102)

which vanishes in the limit
\Lambda^{\prime}
to infinity.
Therefore

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon^{\prime}\to 0^{+}\\
\Lambda^{\prime}\to\infty\end{subarray}}\mathcal{I}_{n}=-\frac{4i^{n}\pi^{2}R_{0}^{2n}}{\Gamma(n+1)}.

(103)

Appendix E
Two-body correlation in a 1D toy model

We aim to determine the fluctuation of a pair of sources on a one-dimensional line distributed via a one-dimensional Gaussian distribution. The distribution is given by the equation
p(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}
. We also assume that a minimum distance is imposed among these sources. The two-body density is defined as follows:

\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{p(x_{1})p(x_{2})\Theta(|x_{1}-x_{2}|-d_{\text{min}})}{\int dx_{1}dx_{2}p(x_{1})p(x_{2})\Theta(|x_{1}-x_{2}|-d_{\text{min}})},

(113)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function. We can redefine the two-body density as
\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\rho_{1}(x_{1})\rho_{1}(x_{2})c_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
,
where the one-body density is given by:

\rho_{1}(x_{1})=\int dx_{2}\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=p(x_{1})\left[1+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}-2\sigma p(x_{1})\right)\left(\frac{d_{\text{min}}}{\sigma}\right)+\cdots\right],

(114)

and the two-body correlation reads as:

c_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\Theta(|x_{1}-x_{2}|-d_{\text{min}})\left[1-\left(\frac{d_{\text{min}}}{\sigma}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}-2\sigma p(x_{1})-2\sigma p(x_{2})\right)+\cdots\right].

(115)

Figure 9
:
This figure illustrates the implementation of two-body correlation on a 1D toy model. The blue dots represent the results obtained using a toy Monte Carlo based on the direct method, while the red dots correspond to the iterative method with the step-function as a two-body correlation. The dashed curves depict the analytical calculation.

In the following, we test three scenarios of generating two sources at positions
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. We analytically calculate
\langle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\rangle
and
\langle(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}\rangle
and compare our calculation with toy Monte Carlo simulations.

Method 1: Direct method.
We first generate two random numbers and calculate their distance. If the distance is smaller than
d_{\text{min}}
, we discard the pair; otherwise, we accept it. This method matches with the following calculation,

\displaystyle\langle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int dx_{1}dx_{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=2\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma d_{\text{min}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\cdots,

(116a)

\displaystyle\langle(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int dx_{1}dx_{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}\rho_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=8\sigma^{4}+\frac{5\sigma^{3}d_{\text{min}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\cdots.

(116b)

Method 2: Iterative method with
p(x)
as one-body density and step-function as two-body correlation.
First, we generate a random source. Then, we generate a second source and calculate its distance from the first one. If the distance is less than the value of
d_{\text{min}}
, we discard the second source and generate a new one. If the distance is greater than or equal to
d_{\text{min}}
, we accept the second source. The analytical calculation for this method is as follows: The second source’s position depends on the first source’s position. Therefore, we first calculate
\langle x_{2}^{2}\rangle(x_{1})\propto\int dx_{2}x_{2}^{2}p(x_{2})\Theta(|x_{1}-x_{2}|-d_{\text{min}})
. Then, we calculate
\langle x_{1}^{2}+\langle x_{2}^{2}\rangle(x_{1})\rangle=\int dx_{1}(x_{1}^{2}+\langle x_{2}^{2}\rangle(x_{1}))p(x_{1})
using the result from the previous step. We follow the same process for
(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}
,

\displaystyle\langle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma d_{\text{min}}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}+\cdots,

(117a)

\displaystyle\langle(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=8\sigma^{4}+\frac{7\sigma^{3}d_{\text{min}}}{4\sqrt{\pi}}+\cdots.

(117b)

Method 3: Iterative method with
\rho_{1}(x)
as one-body density and and
c_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
as two-body correlation.
This method is similar to the previous one while we replace
p(x)
with
\rho_{1}(a)
(Eq. (
114
)) and step-function with
c_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
(Eq. (
115
)). The result is given by

\displaystyle\langle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma d_{\text{min}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\cdots,

(118a)

\displaystyle\langle(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=8\sigma^{4}+\frac{5\sigma^{3}d_{\text{min}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\cdots.

(118b)

The above analysis shows that method 2 produces inconsistent outcomes compared to methods 1 and 3. It can be concluded that using an iterative method with true
\rho_{1}(x)
and
c_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
leads to a result similar to that obtained by starting directly from the two-body density. The difference between methods can be explained as follows: In the direct method (method 1), the most likely position for uncorrelated sources is around the origin. However, when correlation is present, finding sources very close to the origin becomes unlikely, and the probability of finding their position is symmetric for both sources. In method 2, the first source is generated independently and is usually located around the center. On the other hand, the second source cannot be too close to the center and needs to be placed further away from the origin. In method 3, the one-body density modifies, resulting in a distribution that differs from the Gaussian distribution. This distribution is already more populated symmetrically in points on both sides of the origin, making it similar to method 1.

In Fig.
9
, we have compared the toy Monte Carlo of methods 1 and 2 with our analytical calculation, where we define
\delta r
as follows:

\delta r=\frac{\langle(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}\rangle-2\langle(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\rangle^{2}}{\langle(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})\rangle^{2}}.

(119)

In equations (
116
) to (
118
), we presented the leading terms, while in the figure, we showed the full calculation. The figure makes it clear that there is a more drastic effect on
\langle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\rangle
, which corresponds to the size of the one-body density, compared to
\delta r
, which captures the two-body correlation.

Appendix F
Effect of substructure, finite cross-section, and deformation

F.1
Estimation of the nucleon substructure contribution

It is possible to assume substructure constituents for the nucleons. The reduced thickness function can then be written as follows:

T_{R}(\mathbf{x})\approx\frac{\mathcal{N}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{part}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\gamma_{i,j}\int dz\,\rho_{\text{c}}(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{i}-\vec{\xi}_{i,j}),

(120)

where
\vec{\xi}_{i,j}
indicates the location of
j
th constituent of the
i
th nucleon, and
n_{c}
is the number of constituents. Here, we assume the constituents are distributed inside the nucleons via a normal distribution with width
\sigma_{w}
, and constituents have a round Gaussian profile with width
v
.
We have similar decomposition as that in appendix
C.1
,
e^{W_{R}}=e^{W_{c}}e^{W_{\text{source}}}
.

Considering that the substructure of nucleons has a small contribution, we will exclude the two-body correlation from our calculations. Additionally, we will assume that the density of the nucleus is Gaussian in shape with a width of
\sigma_{G}\approx R_{0}/\sqrt{5}
. It is important to note that the contribution of the nucleon substructure is local, and the overall shape of the nucleus should not have a significant impact. These assumptions will allow us to use Eqs (
24
) and (
25
).

Suppose a nucleon with a density distribution of
\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{x})
and width of
\sigma_{w}
is located at position
\vec{x}_{i}
. Hence, the constituents are distributed based on this distribution at locations
\vec{\xi}_{i,1},\ldots,\vec{\xi}_{i,n_{c}}
. The fluctuation of these constituents is given by
\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{\xi}_{i,1}-\vec{x}_{i})\cdots\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{\xi}_{i,{n_{c}}}-\vec{x}_{i})
. However, we impose a constraint that the average location of
n_{c}
constituents must coincide with the location of the nucleon at
\vec{x}_{i}
. This constraint is given by

\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{\xi}_{i,1}-\vec{x}_{i})\cdots\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{\xi}_{i,{n_{c}}}-\vec{x}_{i})\;\delta([\vec{x}]_{i}-\vec{x}_{i}),

where
[\vec{x}]_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\vec{\xi}_{i,j}
is the location of the nucleon’s constituent center. Hence, we obtain the following expression for the source fluctuation distribution,

\begin{split}P_{A}=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{A}g([\vec{x}]_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{\xi}_{i,j}-[\vec{x}]_{i})p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma_{i,j})\right],\end{split}

(121)

which is the generalized version of Eq. (
74
) at the presence of nucleon substructure. Here,
g(\vec{x})
is a Gaussian distribution with width
\sigma_{G}
. Evidently, the Gaussian distribution for nucleus density is a poor approximation. For that reason, we only single out the contribution of the substructure to the observables and incorporate it with those obtained in the previous parts based on WS distribution.

To isolate the effect of the substructure, it is useful to define the following measures:

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}\xi_{i}&=\left(d^{3}\xi_{i,1}\cdots d^{3}\xi_{i,n_{c}}g([\vec{x}]_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\rho_{\text{nucleon}}(\vec{\xi}_{i,j}-[\vec{x}]_{i})\right),\\
\mathcal{D}\gamma&=d\gamma_{i,1}p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma_{i,1})\cdots d\gamma_{i,n_{c}}p_{\text{fluc}}(\gamma_{i,n_{c}}).\end{split}

(122)

We also define the following function:

F(\gamma_{i,j},\vec{\xi}_{i,j})=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\gamma_{ij}f(\vec{\xi}_{i,j}).

(123)

Using these definitions, the moments read as

\mathcal{O}=\frac{1}{N_{A}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{A}}F_{i}(\gamma_{i,j},\vec{\xi}_{i,j}),

(124)

and one can straightforwardly find

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}F(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})}{\int\mathcal{D}\xi},

(125a)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{A}}\frac{\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}F_{1}(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})F_{2}(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})}{\int\mathcal{D}\xi}+\frac{N_{A}-1}{N_{A}}\left(\frac{\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}F_{1}(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})}{\int\mathcal{D}\xi}\right)\left(\frac{\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}F_{2}(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})}{\int\mathcal{D}\xi}\right).

(125b)

Therefore, we need to calculate the integrals over substructures. These integrals are
n_{c}
dimensions and cannot be decomposed into smaller dimensions due to the constraint
[\vec{x}]_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\vec{\xi}_{i,j}
. We still, however, relate the integration over
F(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})
to the integration over
f(\vec{\xi}_{i,j})
,

\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}F(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})=\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\gamma_{1,j}f(\vec{\xi}_{1,j})=\int\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}f(\vec{\xi}_{1,1}),

(126)

where we used the fact that partons are indistinguishable in the second equality.
Similarly,

\begin{split}\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}F_{1}(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})F_{2}(\gamma_{1,j},\vec{\xi}_{1,j})&=\frac{1}{n_{c}^{2}}\int\mathcal{D}\gamma_{1}\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n_{c}}\gamma_{1,j}^{2}f_{1}(\vec{\xi}_{1,j})f_{2}(\vec{\xi}_{1,j})+\sum_{j\neq k}^{n_{c}}\gamma_{1,j}\gamma_{1,k}f_{1}(\vec{\xi}_{1,j})f_{2}(\vec{\xi}_{1,k})\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\left(1+\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{2}\right)\int\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}\;f_{1}(\vec{\xi}_{1,1})f_{2}(\vec{\xi}_{1,1})+\frac{n_{c}-1}{n_{c}}\int\mathcal{D}\xi_{1}\;f_{1}(\vec{\xi}_{1,1})f_{2}(\vec{\xi}_{1,2}).\end{split}

(127)

Now, we calculate the integration over

F.2
Effect of finite cross-section

Previously, we considered that all nucleons in nuclei were involved in the collision, which means
N_{\text{part}}=2A
. However, this assumption does not take into account the mutual correlation between the sources in the target and the sources in the projectile. When the inelastic cross-section is finite, the correlation between the nucleons in the target and the projectile is induced, which means that the decompression in Eq. (
68
) requires a correction, and Eq. (
72
) must be modified.

T
\mathrel{\raisebox{-2.1pt}{R}}
ENT
\mathrel{\raisebox{0.0pt}{o}}
model calculates the probability of colliding sources forming the target and the projectile. This probability is given by a distribution
P_{\text{coll}}(\bm{d})
, which depends on the size of each nucleon and the inelastic cross-section between two nucleons. In the black-disk approximation,
P_{\text{coll}}(\bm{d})
is assumed to be a step-function, and collisions occur when the distance between two nucleons is less than
\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{\text{NN}}/\pi}
. After the position of the sources is sampled, the distance between pairs from the target and the projectile is calculated, and they are accepted as participants based on
P_{\text{coll}}(\bm{d})
.

It is a challenging task to include the correlation induced by collisions directly because a nucleon in the target may be correlated with more than one nucleon in the projectile. To estimate this correlation analytically, we adopt the optical Glauber model approach (see Ref.
[
10
]
). Instead of assuming nucleons that collide with some other nucleons, we begin with
2A
sources and then deduct the effect of those which did not collide. The probability of a nucleon from nucleus
A
passing through a nucleus with
B
nucleons and not experiencing any collision is given by:

\begin{split}T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})&\left(1-\frac{1}{B}T_{B}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{\text{inel}}^{\text{NN}}\right)^{B}\\
&\approx T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})e^{-T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{\text{inel}}^{\text{NN}}},\end{split}

(136)

Here,
T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})
is defined as
T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})=A\int dz\rho_{\text{WS}}(\vec{x})
.
We have used the approximation that
T_{A}(\mathbf{x})\approx T_{B}(\mathbf{x})
and the large
A
and
B
limit. The number of sources in a nucleus that do not participate in the collision can be obtained via the following relation,

\overline{N}=\int d\mathbf{x}\;T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})e^{-T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{\text{inel}}^{\text{NN}}}.

(137)

To estimate the impact of finite cross-section, we use an approximation for the WS distribution. The effect of the cross-section is expected to be small, so any error associated with the approximated WS will be negligible. To do this, we substitute the WS distribution with the following function:

\rho_{G}(|\vec{x}|)=\frac{\rho_{0}}{1+\exp\left[(|\vec{x}|^{2}-R_{0}^{2})/(2a_{0}R_{0})\right]}.

(138)

For small
a_{0}
, the above distribution is similar to the WS distribution. We will also keep the leading term in
a_{0}
, where the distribution becomes
\rho_{0}
for
|\vec{x}|^{2}<R_{0}^{2}
, and
\rho_{0}e^{-(|\vec{x}|^{2}-R_{0}^{2})/(2a_{0}R_{0})}
for
|\vec{x}|^{2}>R_{0}^{2}
. The approximation of distribution in Eq. (
138
) in the
a_{0}\to 0
limit yields the following approximation for the thickness function,

T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})\approx\frac{3A}{4\pi R_{0}^{3}}\left[2\Theta(R_{0}-|\mathbf{x}|)\sqrt{R_{0}^{2}-|\mathbf{x}|^{2}}+\Theta(|\mathbf{x}|-R_{0})\sqrt{2\pi a_{0}R_{0}}\,e^{-\frac{|\mathbf{x}|^{2}-R_{0}^{2}}{2a_{0}R_{0}}}\right].

(139)

We employ the above to calculate the following moment,

\begin{split}&\int d\mathbf{x}\;|\mathbf{x}|^{2m}\,T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})e^{-T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}=\mathcal{I}_{1,m}+\mathcal{I}_{2,m},\end{split}

(140)

where (
s_{\perp}=\pi R_{0}^{2}/A
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi A}{4\pi R_{0}^{3}/3}\int_{0}^{R_{0}}rdr\,r^{2m}\,2\sqrt{R_{0}^{2}-r^{2}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}{s_{\perp}}\right)\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{r}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}}\right],

(141a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{A(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{a_{0}R_{0}}}{4\pi R_{0}^{3}/3}\,\int_{R_{0}}^{\infty}rdr\,r^{2m}\,\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)\left[\left(\frac{r}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}-1\right]\right.

(141b)

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\left.-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}{s_{\perp}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)^{1/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)\left[\left(\frac{r}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}-1\right]}\right].

To perform the first integral, we change the variable as
\tilde{r}=\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{r}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}}
. The result reads as

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1,m}&=3AR_{0}^{2m}\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{r}^{2}d\tilde{r}\,\left(1-\tilde{r}^{2}\right)^{m}\,\exp\left[-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}{s_{\perp}}\right)\tilde{r}\right]\\

(142)

To calculate the second integral, we change the variable as
\tilde{r}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{r}{R_{0}}\right)^{2}-1}
which leads to

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{2,m}&=3A\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}}\;R_{0}^{2m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{r}d\tilde{r}\left(1+\tilde{r}^{2}\right)^{m}\,\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)\tilde{r}^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}{s_{\perp}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)^{1/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)\tilde{r}^{2}}\right]\\
&=2A\,R^{2m}\,\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{s_{\perp}}{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}\right)+\cdots.\end{split}

(143)

The combination of these two integrals leads to,

\begin{split}&\int d\mathbf{x}\,|\mathbf{x}|^{2m}\,T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})e^{-T_{A}^{\text{opt}}(\mathbf{x})\sigma_{\text{fluc}}^{\text{NN}}}=AR_{0}^{2m}\left[2\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{\pi R_{0}^{2}/A}{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}\right)+\frac{16}{9}\left(\frac{\pi R_{0}^{2}/A}{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}\right)^{3}+\cdots\right].\end{split}

(144)

In the above, the first term represents the contribution from the correction of skin thickness, while the second term represents the contribution when the WS distribution is approximated with a step-function. Usually, the first term has a greater contribution than the second term, except when

The probability of finding sources in the skin part of the distribution is lower compared to the other parts. Hence, the sources from this region are more likely to miss the collision. Using Eq. (
144

\begin{split}\overline{N}&=2A\left(\frac{a_{0}}{R_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{\pi R_{0}^{2}/A}{\sigma^{\text{NN}}_{\text{inel}}}\right)+\cdots,\end{split}

(146)

In the presence of interaction, we can write the moments as

F.3
Effect of nucleus deformation

The one-body density of a deformed nucleus, denoted as
p_{A}(\vec{x})
, is not rotationally invariant. For nuclei with quadrupole deformation, an elliptical shape is expected in the initial entropy density in the transverse space, even in the infinite number of nucleons at zero impact parameter. In the collision of such nuclei, we expect the maximum ellipticity value in the body-body collision and the minimum in the tip-tip collision. For randomly rotating nuclei and an infinite number of nucleons, the ellipticity of the overlapping region fluctuates event-by-event between these two values.

The scale-invariance approximation in UCSC events is valid when two deformed nuclei have maximum overlap, i.e., the target and projectile have the same orientation in space, or they must be mirror reflections compared to the transverse plane. However, selecting such events is not an easy task. At finite values of the cross-section, it is always possible that a nucleon from the projectile passes through the target without any interaction. On the other hand, at very large values of the cross-section, all the nucleons participate in the collision, irrespective of the orientation of the target and projectile. As a result, events with the same number of participants (and the same multiplicity) might correspond to event topologies when the geometrical overlapping region of the target and projectile is not the same. Ignoring such complications in selecting the most accurate, maximally overlapping events and following Ref.
[
6
]
, we assume that the target and projectile rotate randomly and study the mean deformation and its fluctuation from the independent random orientation of colliding nuclei.

The rotated one-body density is given by
p_{A}(\mathcal{R}\cdot\vec{x})
, where
\mathcal{R}(\alpha_{e},\beta_{e},\gamma_{e})
is the rotation matrix written in terms of the Euler anglers. The moment of the overlapping region in a single event is obtained as follows,

\begin{split}&\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\left(p_{A}(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cdot\vec{x})+p_{A}(\mathcal{R}_{2}\cdot\vec{x})\right)f(\mathbf{x}),\end{split}

(153)

and the many event average of the above moment is given by

\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\cdots\rangle=\int\,\mathcal{O}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{2}\cdots\,d\Omega_{1}d\Omega_{2},\end{split}

(154)

where
d\Omega=\sin\beta_{e}d\alpha_{e}d\beta_{e}d\gamma_{e}
. One can estimate the effect of random nuclei orientation for a given deformed one-body density by performing the integral numerically.
