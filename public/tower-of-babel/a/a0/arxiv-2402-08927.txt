Critical speeding-up in dynamical percolation
Eren Metin Elçi, Timothy M. Garoni

Abstract
We study the autocorrelation time of the size of the cluster at the origin in discrete-time dynamical percolation. We focus on binary trees and high-dimensional tori, and show in both cases that this autocorrelation time is linear in the volume in the subcritical regime, but strictly sublinear in the volume at criticality. This establishes rigorously that the cluster size at the origin in these models exhibits critical speeding-up. The proofs involve controlling relevant Fourier coefficients. In the case of binary trees, these Fourier coefficients are studied explicitly, while for high-dimensional tori we employ a randomised algorithm argument introduced by Schramm and Steif in the context of noise sensitivity.



Keywords
: Critical speeding-up, percolation, noise sensitivity

1
Introduction

Just as models in statistical mechanics typically exhibit divergent spatial scales near a critical point, stochastic dynamics associated
with such models also tend to display divergent time scales
[
1
]
. For example, Markov chains employed in Monte Carlo
algorithms at critical points typically exhibit relaxation times and stationary autocorrelation times which scale superlinearly with
volume
[
2
]
; this well-known phenomenon is referred to as
critical slowing-down
. By contrast, a less familiar possibility
is that time scales associated with certain observables scale sublinearly with volume at criticality. This phenomenon of
critical
speeding-up
has been observed experimentally (see
e.g.
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
),
and also in Monte Carlo simulations
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

In particular, in
[
7
]
the heat-bath dynamics of the critical Fortuin-Kasteleyn random-cluster model was simulated, on
2-dimensional and 3-dimensional tori, at a variety of cluster fugacities. The autocorrelations of certain observables were then studied. The
sum of squared cluster sizes, which is a natural estimator for the susceptibility, was studied in detail. It was observed generically that
its stationary autocorrelation function decays on a time scale strictly sublinear in the volume. Moreover, for a range of cluster
fugacities, sublinear scaling was also observed for its autocorrelation time, which quantifies the time scale between “effectively
independent” samples. In particular, these behaviours were observed for the case of unit cluster fugacity, for which the stationary
distribution reduces to that of independent bond percolation. We will refer to this special case of the random-cluster model heat-bath
dynamics as discrete-time dynamical percolation; see section
2.1
for a precise
definition.

Independently, closely-related questions were being addressed in the context of noise sensitivity of Boolean functions defined on the
discrete hypercube; see
[
10
]
for an overview. The notion of noise sensitivity was introduced
in
[
11
]
, and in essence it amounts to the fast decay of autocorrelation functions in dynamical percolation; for
precise definitions see section
3
. In particular, it was shown in
[
11
]
that
the indicator for a left-right crossing of a rectangle in critical bond percolation on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is noise sensitive. A quantitative version of
this result, bounding the rate of decay of these autocorrelations, was then presented in
[
12
]
. A key tool employed in
these results was Fourier analysis on the hypercube.

Our main aim in the current work is to study critical speeding-up using the same framework introduced for noise sensitivity. Whilst the
existing noise sensitivity literature focuses largely on Boolean functions, our focus will instead be on non-Boolean functions.
Specifically, we consider the size of the cluster containing the origin/root in dynamical percolation on binary trees and high-dimensional
tori. We show in both cases that this autocorrelation time is linear in the volume in the subcritical regime, but strictly sublinear in the
volume at criticality. In the case of high-dimensional tori, a key tool in our proof is a theorem due to Schramm and
Steif
[
12
]
which bounds Fourier coefficients by considering randomised algorithms.

Definitions and results

2.1
Dynamical percolation and autocorrelations

Let
S:=\{-1,1\}
, and let
B
be any finite set. For a given
p\in(0,1)
, let

\pi_{p}(s):=p\,\mathbf{1}(s=+1)+(1-p)\,\mathbf{1}(s=-1),\qquad s\in S\;,

(1)

denote the Bernoulli distribution on
S
, and let
\pi_{p,B}
be the corresponding product measure on
S^{B}
, so that

\pi_{p,B}(x):=\prod_{i\in B}\pi_{p}(x_{i})\;,\qquad\text{ for all }x\in S^{B}\;.

(2)

We refer to the elements of
B
as bits, and with respect to
x\in S^{B}
we say
i\in B
is open if
x_{i}=+1
and closed if
x_{i}=-1
.

Now consider the stochastic matrix on state space
S^{B}
with entries

P_{p,B}(x,y):=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}\pi_{p}(y_{i})\,\prod_{j\in B\setminus i}\mathbf{1}(y_{j}=x_{j})\;.

(3)

This is the special case of the discrete-time heat-bath chain for the Fortuin-Kasteleyn model (see e.g.
[
13
]
) on a graph with
edge set
B
, when the cluster fugacity is
q=1
. In the special case
p=1/2
, it reduces to lazy simple random walk on the hypercube

S^{B}
. The latter also corresponds to the infinite temperature limit of the discrete-time single-spin heat-bath process for the Ising
model, on a graph with vertex set
B
. For a given choice of
B
and
p
, let
Z^{p,B}=(Z_{s}^{p,B})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
be a
stationary Markov chain with transition matrix
P_{p,B}
. We refer to the process
Z^{p,B}
as discrete-time dynamical percolation
on
B
with parameter
p
. We emphasise that for each
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the state
Z^{p,B}_{s}
has distribution
\pi_{p,B}
.

Discrete-time dynamical percolation has an intimate relationship to the dynamical percolation model first studied
in
[
14
]
. The latter corresponds to a countably infinite family of independent continuous-time Markov chains on

S
, each with generator
\Pi_{p}-I
, where
\Pi_{p}
is the matrix on
S
with entries
\Pi_{p}(s,s^{\prime})=\pi_{p}(s^{\prime})
. It is easily seen that the
subset of chains corresponding to a particular set of bits
B
is a continuous time Markov chain on
S^{B}
with generator

Q_{p,B}=|B|(P_{p,B}-I)
; in particular,
Q_{p,B}
is the rate-
|B|
continuization (see e.g.
[
15
, section
20.1]
) of
P_{p,B}
.

We are interested in the autocorrelations of the real-valued processes
f(Z_{s}^{p,B})
induced by functions
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
. The
corresponding
autocorrelation function
is

\rho_{p,f}(s):=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(f(Z_{0}^{p,B}),f(Z_{s}^{p,B}))}{\operatorname{var}(f(Z_{0}^{p,B}))},\qquad s\in\mathbb{N}\;,

(4)

and
autocorrelation time
1
1

1

This is often referred to as the
integrated
autocorrelation time
[
2
]
.
is

\tau_{p,f}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\rho_{p,f}(|s|)\;.

(5)

Analogous quantities can also be defined for continuous-time dynamical percolation. If
Y^{p,B}=(Y_{t}^{p,B})_{\geq 0}
is a stationary
continuous-time Markov chain with generator
Q_{p,B}
, then we set

\tilde{\rho}_{p,f}(t):=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(f(Y_{0}^{p,B}),f(Y_{t}^{p,B}))}{\operatorname{var}(f(Y_{0}^{p,B}))},\qquad t\in[0,\infty)\;,

(6)

and

\tilde{\tau}_{p,f}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{\rho}_{p,f_{B}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\;.

(7)

One concrete motivation for interest in
\tau_{p,f}
is that (see e.g.
[
16
, equation (9.2.11)]
)

\operatorname{var}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}f(Z_{s}^{p,B})\right)\sim\frac{2\tau_{p,f}}{T}\operatorname{var}(f(Z_{0}^{p,B})),\qquad T\to\infty\;.

(8)

Therefore, if one wishes to estimate
\mathbb{E}(f(Z_{0}^{p,B}))
by simulating
Z^{p,B}
, the squared error is proportional to

\tau_{p,f}
. By contrast, the variance of the sample mean of
m
independent realisations of
f(Z_{0}^{p,B})
is simply

\operatorname{var}(f(Z_{0}^{p,B}))/m
. Since each such independent realisation consists of
|B|
bit updates, in order to meaningfully compare
estimates from independent samples with those constructed using the Markov chain
Z^{p,B}
, one should set
T=m|B|
.

Now fix
p
, and consider an increasing sequence of bit sets,
B_{n}
, and a sequence of functions
f_{n}:S^{B_{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
. It follows
that Markov-chain sampling asymptotically outperforms independent sampling in estimation of
\mathbb{E}(f_{n}(Z_{0}^{p,B_{n}}))
iff
\tau_{p,f_{n}}=o(|B_{n}|)
as
n\to\infty
. When a sequence of functions displays such behaviour, for a given value of
p
, we say it exhibits

speeding up
. In section
2.2
we discuss the autocorrelation time for the cluster size at the origin in discrete-time
dynamical percolation on trees and high-dimensional tori. We find that while speeding up does not occur in the subcritical regime, it does occur at
criticality. This phenomenon is precisely what we shall mean by
critical speeding-up
.

The autocorrelations of discrete-time and continuous-time dynamical percolation are closely related. In fact, it follows immediately
from lemma
3.2
below that

\tau_{p,f}=|B|\,\tilde{\tau}_{p,f}-1/2\;.

(9)

From equation (
9
), it follows that a sequence of functions
f_{n}
displays speeding up, at
parameter
p
, iff the corresponding sequence
\tilde{\tau}_{p,f_{n}}=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
. Moreover, the following result, whose proof is
deferred to section
3
, implies that the continuous-time and discrete-time autocorrelation functions have
closely related asymptotics, on non-trivial timescales.

Lemma 2.1
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
. Let
B_{n}
be an increasing sequence of finite sets, and let
f_{n}:S^{B_{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a sequence of non-constant
functions. Then as
n\to\infty
, for any positive integer sequence
s_{n}
satisfying
s_{n}=\omega(1)
we have

\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n})=\tilde{\rho}_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n}/|B_{n}|)+o(1)\;.

There is a direct relationship between the decay of the continuous-time autocorrelation function, and the notion of noise sensitivity. Fix

p\in(0,1)
, and let
X^{p,B}
be a random element of
S^{B}
with distribution
\pi_{p,B}
. For given
\epsilon>0
, let

X^{p,B}_{\epsilon}
denote the following perturbation of
X^{p,B}
: independently for each bit
i
, with probability
1-\epsilon

the
i
th bit of
X^{p,B}_{\epsilon}
agrees with the
i
th bit of
X^{p,B}
, and with probability
\epsilon
it is set to a new
random value chosen according to
\pi_{p}
, independent of its previous value, and independent of the values of all other bits. Now consider,
again, an increasing sequence of finite sets
B_{n}
. A sequence of Boolean functions
f_{n}:S^{B_{n}}\to S
is called
noise
sensitive

[
10
]
if the covariance of
f_{n}(X^{p,B_{n}})
and
f_{n}(X^{p,B_{n}}_{\epsilon})
goes to zero as

n\to\infty
for all
\epsilon>0
. However, it is straightforward to verify directly that if
\epsilon=1-\rme^{-t}
then

(X^{p,B},X^{p,B}_{\epsilon})
and
(Y^{p,B}_{0},Y^{p,B}_{t})
have the same joint distribution. Therefore, the covariance of

f_{n}(X^{p,B_{n}})
and
f_{n}(X^{p,B_{n}}_{\epsilon})
is precisely the covariance of
f_{n}(Y_{0}^{p,B})
and
f_{n}(Y_{t}^{p,B})
, and the
correlation of
f_{n}(X^{p,B_{n}})
and
f_{n}(X^{p,B_{n}}_{\epsilon})
is precisely
\tilde{\rho}_{p,f_{n}}(t)
. Provided that

\operatorname{var}(f_{n}(X^{p,B_{n}}))
is bounded away from zero, it then follows that
f_{n}
is noise sensitive iff
\tilde{\rho}_{p,f_{n}}(t)\to 0
as

n\to\infty
for all fixed
t>0
. We remark that although noise sensitivity is typically only considered in the specific case
p=1/2
, the
above discussion makes sense for any fixed
p\in(0,1)
.

In the case of non-Boolean functions, where the variance is no longer bounded from above by 1, it is arguably more natural to consider the
correlation from the outset, rather than the covariance. In addition to the question of speeding up, we will be interested in the question of
sublinear autocorrelation decay, in the sense that there exists
c_{n}=o(|B_{n}|)
such that
\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n})=o(1)
for all
s_{n}=\omega(c_{n})
.
We emphasise that speeding up and autocorrelation decay can occur at strictly different time scales. A simple example illustrating this is
presented below in equation (
15
).

2.2
Results

Our main results concern autocorrelations of the size of the cluster containing a fixed vertex, in dynamical percolation on certain trees
and high-dimensional tori. The set of bits,
B
, then corresponds to the edge set of the tree or torus under consideration, and for
x\in S^{B}
drawn from
\pi_{p,B}
, we consider the connected components of the spanning subgraph defined by the open edges. The size of a such a
connected component, or cluster, is then simply the number of vertices which it contains.

Our result for trees is as follows. By a
binary tree
, we simply mean a rooted tree in which each vertex has at most 2 children. We
say a binary tree is
perfect
if each non-leaf vertex has precisely two children, and all leaves have the same depth. Recall (see
e.g.
[
17
, theorem 1.8]
) that the critical threshold for bond percolation on the infinite-depth perfect binary tree is

p_{\mathrm{c}}=1/2
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, let
(V_{n},B_{n})
denote the perfect binary tree of depth
n
. For
p\in(0,1)
, consider discrete-time dynamical
percolation on
(V_{n},B_{n})
, and let
f_{n}
denote the size of the cluster containing the root.

(i)

Let
s_{n}
be a positive integer sequence. If
p\in(0,1)
and
p\neq 1/2
then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }s_{n}=o(|B_{n}|),\\
0,&\text{ if }s_{n}=\omega(|B_{n}|).\\
\end{cases}

(ii)

Let
s_{n}
be a positive integer sequence. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{1/2,f_{n}}(s_{n})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }s_{n}=o(|B_{n}|/n),\\
0,&\text{ if }s_{n}=\omega(|B_{n}|/n).\\
\end{cases}

(iii)

As
n\to\infty

\dfrac{\tau_{p,f_{n}}}{|B_{n}|}\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-2p}{2p(1-p)}\log\left(\dfrac{1-2p^{2}}{1-2p}\right),&p\in(0,1/2),\\
6\dfrac{\log(n)}{n},&p=1/2,\\
\dfrac{2p-1}{(1-p)}\log\left(\dfrac{p}{2p-1}\right),&p\in(1/2,1).\end{cases}

Part
(iii)
of proposition
2.2
clearly illustrates the phenomenon of critical speeding up; the
autocorrelation time for the cluster at the root is linear in the volume for all off-critical
p
, but is strictly sublinear at
criticality.

Our other example concerns dynamical percolation on high-dimensional tori. For integer
d\geq 2
, let
p_{\mathrm{c}}(d)
denote the critical
threshold of bond percolation on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For integer
L>2
, we denote by
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{L}
the
d
-dimensional discrete torus of side length
L
,
whose vertex set we take to be
[-L/2,L/2)^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
d,L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with
L>2
. Consider discrete-time dynamical percolation on
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{L}
, and let
f_{d,L}
denote the size of the cluster
connected to the origin. For any fixed but sufficiently large
d
, we have as
L\to\infty
that

\tau_{p,f_{d,L}}=\begin{cases}\Theta(L^{d}),&0<p<p_{\mathrm{c}}(d)\;,\\
O(L^{d-1/13}),&p=p_{\mathrm{c}}(d)\;,\end{cases}

and for a positive sequence
t_{L}
that

\rho_{p,f_{d,L}}(\left\lceil t_{L}L^{d}\right\rceil)=\begin{cases}\Theta(1),&t_{L}=o(1),\,p<p_{\mathrm{c}}(d)\;,\\
o(1),&t_{L}=\omega(L^{-1/13}),\,p=p_{\mathrm{c}}(d)\;.\end{cases}

We note that the implied constants in the asymptotic statements in proposition
2.3
may in general depend on
p

and
d
.

3
Spectral representations

3.1
Autocorrelations

Given
p\in(0,1)
and a finite set
B
, let

l^{2}(\pi_{p,B})
denote the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{S^{B}}
endowed with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{p,B}:=\sum_{x\in S^{B}}f(x)g(x)\pi_{p,B}(x),\qquad\text{ for all }f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{S^{B}}\;.

(10)

Let

\nu_{p}:=\sqrt{(1-p)/p}\;,

(11)

and for each
A\subseteq B
, define
\Psi_{A}^{p,B}:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
via

\Psi_{A}^{p,B}(x)=\prod_{b\in A}\,x_{i}\,\nu_{p}^{x_{i}},\qquad\text{ for all }x\in S^{B}\;.

(12)

It can be easily verified that
\{\Psi_{A}^{p,B}\}_{A\subseteq B}
is an orthonormal basis for
l^{2}(\pi_{p,B})
, and that, for
each
A\subseteq B
,
\Psi_{A}^{p,B}
is an eigenvector of
P_{p,B}
with eigenvalue

\lambda_{A}^{B}:=1-\frac{|A|}{|B|}\;.

(13)

Remark 3.1
.

Combining the explicit expression for the eigenvalues from equation (
13
) with general spectral results on
autocorrelations in
[
16
, equations (9.2.26) and (9.2.27)]
immediately implies that for any
p\in(0,1)
and any function

f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
we have
\tau_{f,p}=O(|B|)
for large
|B|
. In particular, dynamical percolation does not suffer from critical
slowing down.

Given a non-constant function
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, it is convenient to introduce the random variable
\mathcal{W}_{p,f}
, taking values in
[B]
,
with probability mass function

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}=k)=\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\frac{\langle f,\Psi_{A}^{p,B}\rangle_{p,B}^{2}}{\operatorname{var}(f(Z_{0}^{p,B}))}\;.

(14)

Autocorrelation functions and autocorrelation times for both the discrete time process
(Z_{s}^{p,B})_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
and continuous time
process
(Y_{t}^{p,B})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
can be conveniently expressed in terms of
\mathcal{W}_{p,f}
. The following lemma, whose proof we omit,
is a simple consequence of the spectral decomposition of
P_{p,B}
, using the explicit expressions from equations (
12
) and (
13
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
, let
B
be a finite set, and let
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-constant function. Then:

(i)

\rho_{p,f}(s)=\mathbb{E}\left(1-\dfrac{\mathcal{W}_{p,f}}{|B|}\right)^{s}
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}

(ii)

\tau_{p,f}=|B|\,\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}^{-1})-1/2

(iii)

\tilde{\rho}_{p,f}(t)=\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t\mathcal{W}_{p,f}})
for all
t\in[0,\infty)

(iv)

\tilde{\tau}_{p,f}=\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}^{-1})

As a simple example, fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
and consider
f:S^{[n]}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f:=n^{-\gamma/2}\,\Psi_{\{1\}}^{p,[n]}+\sqrt{1-n^{-\gamma}}\,\Psi_{[n]}^{p,[n]}\;.

(15)

If
p=1/2
, then
\Psi_{\{1\}}^{p,[n]}
is referred to as a dictator function, and
\Psi_{[n]}^{p,[n]}
is the parity function; see
e.g.
[
18
]
. For any
p\in(0,1)
, however, equation (
14
) yields

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}=k)=n^{-\gamma}\,\mathbf{1}(k=1)+(1-n^{-\gamma})\,\mathbf{1}(k=n)\;.

(16)

Lemma
3.2
then implies that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{p,f}(1)=0
and
\tau_{p,f}\sim n^{1-\gamma}
as

n\to\infty
. In particular, we see in this example that while the timescale at which
\rho_{p,f}
decays to zero is of constant order in

n
, the autocorrelation time diverges as a power law. This provides a simple example, as mooted at the end of section
2.1
, that the timescales characterising autocorrelation decay and speeding up are not related in general.

Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
will be proved by establishing appropriate results for the distribution
of
\mathcal{W}_{p,f}
, and then applying lemma
3.2
. Lemma
3.2
also
provides a straightforward proof of lemma
2.1
.

Proof of lemma
2.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
s\in\mathbb{N}
. From lemma
3.2
we have

\displaystyle\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[B_{n}]}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}=k)\left(1-\frac{k}{|B_{n}|}\right)^{s}

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|B_{n}|-1}\rme^{-ks/|B_{n}|}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f_{n}}=k)\rme^{-s\left(k/|B_{n}|\right)^{2}h\left(k/|B_{n}|\right)}

(18)

where
h:[0,1)\to[0,\infty)
is defined via

h(x):=\frac{-x-\log(1-x)}{x^{2}}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{l}}{l+2}

(19)

and the simple identity
\rme^{x}(1-x)=\rme^{-x^{2}h(x)}
has been employed. This immediately yields the upper bound

\displaystyle\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{|B_{n}|}\rme^{-ks_{n}/|B_{n}|}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f_{n}}=k)

(20)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n}/|B_{n}|)\;.

(21)

Now let
c_{n}
be a positive sequence satisfying
c_{n}=\omega(1)
and
c_{n}=o(\sqrt{s_{n}})
, and set

a_{n}:=\lfloor|B_{n}|c_{n}/s_{n}\rfloor
. Then

\displaystyle\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{a_{n}}\rme^{-ks_{n}/|B_{n}|}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f_{n}}=k)\rme^{-s_{n}\left(k/|B_{n}|\right)^{2}h\left(k/|B_{n}|\right)}

(22)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{a_{n}}\rme^{-ks_{n}/|B_{n}|}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f_{n}}=k)\rme^{-(c_{n}/\sqrt{s_{n}})^{2}h\left(c_{n}/s_{n}\right)}

(23)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n}/|B_{n}|)+o(1)

(24)

where the second inequality utilised the monotonicity of
h
. The stated result now follows.
∎

3.2
Randomised bit-query algorithms

In this section we discuss a slight variant of
[
12
, theorem 1.8]
, which provides an upper bound for
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}=k)
in
terms of certain properties of a randomised algorithm.

By a “bit querying algorithm”, we picture a procedure which takes as input a bit string,
x\in S^{B}
, and outputs a subset of bits,

J\subseteq B
, in such a way that bits are added one-by-one to
J
, and the choice of the next bit to add can depend on the states in

x
of the bits already examined. To make this more precise, we offer the following concrete model.

We define a
query tree
on
B
to be a binary tree of depth at least 1, with its non-leaf vertices labelled from
B
and its
edges labelled from
S
, such that the following two conditions hold:

(i)

on each root-to-leaf path, each
i\in B
occurs at most once

(ii)

no vertex has two descendent edges (i.e. edges connecting to descendants) with the same label

We denote by
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
the set of all query trees on
B
. By construction,
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
is finite. Now let
x\in S^{B}
and
t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}
. If
v\in V(t)
has label
b_{v}\in B
, then we abuse notation by writing
x(v):=x(b_{v})
. We define the “query path for
x
in

t
” to be the unique path
v_{0},\ldots v_{k}
in
t
satisfying:

(i)

v_{0}
is the root

(ii)

for each
0\leq i\leq k-1
, the edge
v_{i}v_{i+1}
has label
x(v_{i})

(iii)

v_{k}
has no descendent edge with label
x(v_{k})

In words, given
x
, at each step, one looks at the bit in
x
corresponding to the current vertex in
t
; if there is an outgoing edge with
this label, traverse it; if not, terminate. Finally, we define
J:\mathcal{Q}_{B}\times S^{B}\to\mathcal{P}(B)
so that for each
(t,x)\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}\times S^{B}
, the image
J(t,x)
is the set of vertex labels appearing on the query path for
x
in
t
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that the above definition of a query tree is very closely related to the notion of a decision tree (see
e.g.
[
18
]
). However, we emphasise that, unlike a decision tree, the leaves of a query tree play no special role; in
particular, they are not labelled by the possible output values of any real-valued function.

We now wish to consider
J
acting on random elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
and
S^{B}
. We begin with some helpful notation. For any

I\subseteq B
and
x\in S^{B}
, we denote by
x_{I}
the restriction of
x
to
I
; i.e.
x_{I}:I\to S
is defined by
x_{I}(i)=x(i)
for all

i\in I
. Let
\bar{I}:=B\setminus I
. For any
y\in S^{I}
and
z\in S^{\bar{I}}
, we define
y\oplus z
to be the unique element of

S^{B}
satisfying

(y\oplus z)(i)=\begin{cases}y(i),&i\in I,\\
z(i),&i\in\bar{I}.\end{cases}

(25)

Finally, if
g:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
then for any
I\subseteq B
and
y\in S^{I}
we define the map
g_{I|y}:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
via

g_{I|y}(x)=g(y\oplus x_{\bar{I}}),\qquad\text{ for all }x\in S^{B}\;.

(26)

We are now ready to define the two key quantities that will be used to bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}=k)
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
B
be a finite set, let
\pi
be a probability measure on
S^{B}
, and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
. Let

X\sim\pi
and
T\sim\mu
be independent. The
revealment
of
\mu
(with respect to
\pi
) is

\delta_{\mu,\pi}:=\max_{i\in B}\,\mathbb{P}[J(T,X)\ni i]

(27)

and for any non-constant
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
the
predictability
of
f
(with respect to
\mu
and
\pi
) is

\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})]}{\operatorname{var}(f(X))}

(28)

where
f_{J|X_{J}}
abbreviates
f_{J(T,X)|X_{J(T,X)}}
, and the notation
\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})
means that for each fixed realisation of

(T,X)
, one computes the variance with respect to
\pi
of the map
f_{J|X_{J}}:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.5
.

We emphasise that since
f_{J|X_{J}}
is a random map,
\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})
is a random variable on the space

(\mathcal{Q}_{B}\times S^{B},\mu\times\pi)
. We also note the bounds
\delta_{\mu,\pi}>0
, which follows from the requirement that
query trees have depth at least one, and
\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}\leq 1
, which is established in corollary
6.3
.

We are now ready to state the following slight variant of the revealment theorem of
[
12
]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
B
be a finite set. Let
\pi:=\pi_{p,B}
, and let
\mu
be any distribution on
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
. For any
non-constant function
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}=k)\leq 2\,\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}+2\,\delta_{\mu,\pi}\,k.

We note that the statement of
[
12
, theorem 1.8]
refers specifically to the case of randomised algorithms which exactly
determine
f
, meaning that the predictability term is identically zero. A generalisation to randomised algorithms which instead merely
approximate
f
is, however, presented in
[
12
, equation (2.5)]
. Our use of query trees is intended simply to provide a
concrete model for these randomised approximation schemes, where the focus is shifted away from approximating
f
, to simply constructing a
rule determining the set of queried bits. We also note that
[
12
, theorem 1.8]
and
[
12
, equation
(2.5)]
only refer to the special case that
p=1/2
, however the generalisation to arbitrary
p\in(0,1)
is
straightforward. For completeness, we present a full proof of theorem
3.6
in
section
6
.

Combining theorem
3.6
with lemma
3.2
provides the following
proposition, which is the main tool applied in section
5
. In short, it reduces
the problem of bounding
\tau_{p,f}
and
\rho_{p,f}(s)
to the problem of constructing a distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
for which the
revealment and predictability both vanish sufficiently fast as
|B|
increases.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
B
be a finite set with
n:=|B|>1
. Let
\pi:=\pi_{p,B}
, and let
\mu
be any distribution on

\mathcal{Q}_{B}
. For any non-constant function
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

\tau_{p,f}\leq 5\,n\,\log(n)\,\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}+3\,n\sqrt{\delta_{\mu,\pi}}\;,

(29)

and

\rho_{p,f}(\left\lceil n\,t\right\rceil)\leq 2\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}}{t}+\frac{\delta_{\mu,\pi}}{t^{2}}\right)\;,\qquad\text{ for all }t\in(0,\infty)\;.

(30)

Proof.

For any
a_{n}\geq 1
, we have from theorem
3.6
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{W}_{p,f}^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor a_{n}\wedge n\rfloor}\frac{1}{k}+2\delta_{\mu,\pi}(\lfloor a_{n}\rfloor\wedge n)+\frac{1}{a_{n}}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq 5\,\log(n)\,\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}+2\delta_{\mu,\pi}\,a_{n}+a_{n}^{-1}

(32)

where in obtaining the second inequality we used the fact (see e.g.
[
19
, equation (6.60)]
) that for all
n\geq 2

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}\leq\frac{5}{2}\log(n)\;.

(33)

The first stated result then follows by choosing
a_{n}=1/\sqrt{\delta_{\mu,\pi}}
and applying lemma
3.2
.

Moreover, theorem
3.6
implies that for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t\,\mathcal{W}_{p,f}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\rme^{-t\,k}+2\delta_{\mu,\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\,\rme^{-t\,k}

(34)

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}\,\frac{1}{\rme^{t}-1}+2\delta_{\mu,\pi}\,\frac{\rme^{t}}{(\rme^{t}-1)^{2}}

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}}{t}+\frac{2\delta_{\mu,\pi}}{t^{2}}\;.

(36)

But from lemma
3.2
, and the fact that
1-x\leq\rme^{-x}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{p,f}(\left\lceil nt\right\rceil)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{W}_{p,f}}{n}\right)^{nt}

(37)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t\mathcal{W}_{p,f}})

(38)

which establishes the second stated result.
∎

4
Binary Trees

We now prove proposition
2.2
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, let
(V_{n},B_{n})
denote the perfect binary tree of depth
n
, and let
o

denote its root. For
p\in(0,1)
, we consider discrete-time dynamical percolation on
(V_{n},B_{n})
, and let
f_{n}
denote the size of the
cluster containing
o
. To simplify notation, we denote variance with respect to
\pi_{p,B_{n}}
by
\operatorname{var}_{p}(\cdot)
, and set

\mathcal{W}_{p,n}:=\mathcal{W}_{p,f_{n}}
. Lemma
4.1
presents a simple exact expression for the distribution of
\mathcal{W}_{p,n}
,
valid for all
n
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
p\in(0,1)
. For all
k\in[B_{n}]
we have

\operatorname{var}_{p}(f_{n})\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{W}_{p,n}=k)=\nu_{p}^{2k}\,\sum_{d=1}^{n}\binom{d-1}{k-1}\,2^{d}\,g_{p,n}(d)

where
g_{p,n}:[n]\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

g_{p,n}(d):=\begin{cases}p^{2d}\left[\dfrac{1-(2p)^{n+1-d}}{1-2p}\right]^{2},&\text{ if }p\neq 1/2,\\
2^{-2d}\,(n+1-d)^{2},&\text{ if }p=1/2.\end{cases}

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
p\in(0,1)
, and let
A\subseteq B_{n}
be non-empty. To simplify notation, we abbreviate
\langle.,.\rangle_{p,B_{n}}
by

\langle.,.\rangle
and set
\Psi_{A}:=\Psi_{A}^{p,B_{n}}
. We begin by finding an expression for the coefficient
\langle f_{n},\Psi_{A}\rangle
.
For
v\in V_{n}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{v}
denote the edge set of the unique path in
(V_{n},B_{n})
between
o
and
v
, and let
\delta_{v}
be the
depth of
v
. Then
v\leftrightarrow o
iff every
e\in\mathcal{P}_{v}
is open. Therefore, for all
x\in S^{B_{n}}
we have

f_{n}(x)=\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\prod_{e\in\mathcal{P}_{v}}\left(\frac{1+x_{e}}{2}\right)\;.

(39)

It follows that, if
X
denotes a random element of
B_{n}
with distribution
\pi_{p,B_{n}}
, then

\begin{split}\langle f_{n},\Psi_{A}\rangle&=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\prod_{e\in\mathcal{P}_{v}}\left(\frac{1+X_{e}}{2}\right)\prod_{g\in A}X_{g}\,\nu_{p}^{X_{g}}\right)\\
&=\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\prod_{e\in\mathcal{P}_{v}\setminus A}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1+X_{e}}{2}\right)\prod_{g\in A\setminus\mathcal{P}_{v}}\mathbb{E}(X_{g}\,\nu_{p}^{X_{g}})\\
&\qquad\times\prod_{h\in A\cap\mathcal{P}_{v}}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}(X_{h}\,\nu_{p}^{X_{h}})}{2}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(X_{h}^{2}\,\nu_{p}^{X_{h}})}{2}\right)\\
&=\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\,p^{|\mathcal{P}_{v}\setminus A|}\,(\sqrt{p(1-p)})^{|A\cap\mathcal{P}_{v}|}\,\mathbf{1}(A\setminus\mathcal{P}_{v}=\emptyset)\\
&=\nu_{p}^{|A|}\,\sum_{v\in V_{n}}\mathbf{1}(\mathcal{P}_{v}\supseteq A)\,p^{\delta_{v}}\;,\end{split}

(40)

where we used the fact that for any
e\in B_{n}
we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{p}(X_{e}\,\nu_{p}^{X_{e}})&=0\;,\\
\mathbb{E}_{p}(X_{e}^{2}\,\nu_{p}^{X_{e}})&=2\sqrt{p(1-p)}\;.\end{split}

(41)

Now let

\mathcal{S}_{n}:=\{A^{\prime}\subseteq B_{n}\,:\,\exists v\in V_{n}\text{ with }\mathcal{P}_{v}\supseteq A^{\prime}\}\;,

(42)

and

d_{A}:=\max\{\delta_{v}\,:\,v\in V_{n}\text{ and }\exists u\in V_{n}\text{ with }A\ni uv\}\;.

(43)

If
A\not\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, then equation (
40
)
implies
\langle f_{n},\Psi_{A}\rangle=0
. Assume instead that
A\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, and let
v_{A}\in V_{n}
be the (unique) deepest endpoint of the edges in

A
. Then
\mathcal{P}_{w}\supseteq A
for
w\in V_{n}
iff
\mathcal{P}_{w}\supseteq\mathcal{P}_{v_{A}}
. For each
d_{A}+1\leq k\leq n
, there are
2^{k-d_{A}}

descendants of
v_{A}
at depth
k
. Therefore, equation (
40
) implies

\begin{split}\langle f_{n},\Psi_{A}\rangle&=\mathbf{1}(A\in\mathcal{S}_{n})\,\nu_{p}^{|A|}\,p^{d_{A}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-d_{A}}(2p)^{k}\\
&=\mathbf{1}(A\in\mathcal{S}_{n})\,\begin{cases}\nu_{p}^{|A|}\,p^{d_{A}}\dfrac{1-(2p)^{n-d_{A}+1}}{1-2p},&p\neq 1/2\;,\\
2^{-d_{A}}(n-d_{A}+1),&p=1/2\;.\end{cases}\end{split}

(44)

It then follows from equation (
44
) that

\langle f_{n},\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}=\nu_{p}^{2|A|}\,g_{p,n}(d_{A})\,\mathbf{1}(A\in\mathcal{S}_{n})\;.

(45)

Finally, from equation (
45
) we conclude that for any
k\in[B_{n}]

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\binom{B_{n}}{k}}\,\langle f_{n},\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{n}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V_{n}\\
\delta_{v}=d\end{subarray}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\\
|A|=k,\,v_{A}=v\end{subarray}}\,\nu_{p}^{2k}\,g_{p,n}(d)

(46)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}^{2k}\,\sum_{d=1}^{n}\,2^{d}\,\binom{d-1}{k-1}\,g_{p,n}(d)\;.

(47)

The stated result now follows from equation (
14
).
∎

Armed with lemma
4.1
, we can now prove proposition
2.2
.

Proof of proposition
2.2
.

Asymptotic statements here refer to the limit
n\to\infty
with
p
fixed. We begin by proving part
(i)
. To that end, let
z\in[0,1]
. From lemma
4.1
we
have for any
p\in(0,1)
that

\begin{split}\operatorname{var}_{p}(f_{n})\,\mathbb{E}(z^{\mathcal{W}_{p,n}})&=\sum_{k\in[B_{n}]}\,z^{k}\,\nu_{p}^{2k}\,\sum_{d=1}^{n}\,2^{d}\,\binom{d-1}{k-1}\,g_{p,n}(d)\\
&=\frac{z\,\nu_{p}^{2}}{1+z\,\nu_{p}^{2}}\,\sum_{d=1}^{n}\,2^{d}\,(1+z\,\nu_{p}^{2})^{d}\,g_{p,n}(d)\;.\end{split}

(48)

For
p\neq 1/2
it then follows that

\operatorname{var}_{p}(f_{n})\,\mathbb{E}(z^{\mathcal{W}})=\frac{z\,\nu_{p}^{2}}{(1+z\,\nu_{p}^{2})(1-2p)^{2}}\,\sum_{d=1}^{n}\,[2p^{2}(1+z\,\nu_{p}^{2})]^{d}\,[1-(2p)^{n+1-d}]^{2}\;.

(49)

Now suppose
z_{n}\in[0,1]
is a convergent sequence, and set
z_{\infty}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}z_{n}
. If
p\in(0,1/2)
,
so that
(2p)^{n}
is exponentially decaying for large
n
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\operatorname{var}_{p}(f_{n})\,\mathbb{E}(z_{n}^{\mathcal{W}_{p,n}})=\frac{z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2}}{(1+z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2})(1-2p)^{2}}\frac{2p^{2}(1+z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2})}{[1-2p^{2}(1+z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2})]}\;,

(50)

while if
p\in(1/2,1)
, so that
(2p)^{n}
is exponentially increasing in
n
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,(2p)^{-2n}\,\operatorname{var}_{p}(f_{n})\,\mathbb{E}(z_{n}^{\mathcal{W}_{p,n}})=\frac{z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2}}{(1+z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2})(2p-1)^{2}}\frac{4p^{2}(1+z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2})}{[1-z_{\infty}\,\nu_{p}^{2}]}\;.

(51)

In particular, taking
z_{n}=1
and employing the identity
1+\nu_{p}^{2}=1/p
yields

\operatorname{var}_{p}(f_{n})\sim\begin{cases}\dfrac{2p(1-p)}{(1-2p)^{3}},&p\in(0,1/2),\\
\dfrac{4p^{2}(1-p)}{(2p-1)^{3}}\,(2p)^{2n},&p\in(1/2,1).\end{cases}

(52)

Taking instead
z_{n}=\rme^{-t_{n}}
implies
z_{\infty}=1
when
t_{n}=o(1)
, and
z_{\infty}=0
when
t_{n}=\omega(1)
, and so equations (
50
), (
51
) and (
52
) imply that for
p\neq 1/2
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t_{n}\mathcal{W}_{p,n}})=\begin{cases}1,&t_{n}=o(1),\\
0,&t_{n}=\omega(1).\end{cases}

(53)

Now let
s_{n}
be a positive integer sequence, and set
t_{n}=s_{n}/|B_{n}|
. If
s_{n}=\omega(|B_{n}|)
then combining equation (
53
) with lemmas
2.1
and
3.2

implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n})=0\;.

(54)

Suppose instead that
s_{n}=o(|B_{n}|)
. If
s_{n}=\omega(1)
,
then combining equation (
53
) with lemmas
2.1
and
3.2

implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s_{n})=1\;.

(55)

If
s_{n}
is instead bounded, then we can bound it above by
s_{n}^{\prime}
satisfying
s_{n}^{\prime}=\omega(1)
and
s_{n}^{\prime}=o(|B_{n}|)
. By then applying
equation (
55
) with
s_{n}
replaced by
s_{n}^{\prime}
, together with the fact that
\rho_{p,f_{n}}(s)
is
decreasing in
s
, and bounded above by 1, we see that in fact equation (
55
) also holds for bounded

s_{n}
. We have therefore established part
(i)
.

We now consider part
(ii)
. From equation (
48
) we have for
z\in[0,1]
that

\operatorname{var}_{1/2}(f_{n})\,\mathbb{E}(z^{\mathcal{W}_{1/2,n}})=\frac{z}{2}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,j^{2}\,\left(\frac{1+z}{2}\right)^{n-j}\;.

(56)

Taking
z=1
in equation (
56
) immediately yields

\operatorname{var}_{1/2}(f_{n})=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{12}\;,

(57)

and it then follows that for
z\in[0,1)

\mathbb{E}(z^{\mathcal{W}_{1/2,n}})=\frac{12}{(1+n^{-1})(2+n^{-1})}\left(\frac{z}{n(1-z)}-2\frac{z(1+z)}{n^{2}(1-z)^{2}}\right.\\
\left.+\frac{z(1+z)(3+z)}{n^{3}(1-z)^{3}}\left[1-\left(\frac{1+z}{2}\right)^{n}\right]\right)\;.

(58)

In particular, if
z=\rme^{-t_{n}}
with
t_{n}>0
and
t_{n}\to c\in(0,+\infty]
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t_{n}\mathcal{W}_{1/2,n}})=0\;.

(59)

Suppose instead that
t:=t_{n}=o(1)
. It then follows from equation (
58
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t\mathcal{W}_{1/2,n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{12}{(1+n^{-1})(2+n^{-1})}\left[\frac{t}{(\rme^{t}-1)}\frac{1}{nt}-2\frac{t^{2}(\rme^{t}+1)}{(\rme^{t}-1)^{2}}\frac{1}{(nt)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\frac{t^{3}(\rme^{t}+1)(3\rme^{t}+1)}{(\rme^{t}-1)^{3}}\frac{1}{(nt)^{3}}\left[1-\left(\frac{1+\rme^{-t}}{2}\right)^{n}\right]\right]

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{12}{(1+n^{-1})(2+n^{-1})}\left(\frac{1}{nt}[1+O(t)]-2\frac{1}{(nt)^{2}}[2-t+O(t^{2})]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\frac{1}{(nt)^{3}}[8-2t+O(t^{2})]\left[1-\left(\frac{1+\rme^{-t}}{2}\right)^{n}\right]\right)\;,

(61)

where in performing the above Taylor approximation it is helpful to recall that
t/(\rme^{t}-1)
is the exponential generating function of
the Bernoulli numbers (see e.g.
[
19
]
). If
t=\omega(n^{-1})
it now follows immediately
from equation (
61
) that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t_{n}\,\mathcal{W}_{1/2,n}})=0\;.

(62)

Conversely, now suppose that
t=o(n^{-1})
. Then

1-\left(\frac{1+\rme^{-t}}{2}\right)^{n}=\frac{nt}{2}-\frac{(nt)^{2}}{8}-\frac{nt^{2}}{8}+\frac{(nt)^{3}}{48}+O[n^{2}t^{3}\vee(nt)^{4}]\;,

(63)

and so the last term in equation (
61
) satisfies, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{(nt)^{3}}[8-2t+O(t^{2})]\left[1-\left(\frac{1+\rme^{-t}}{2}\right)^{n}\right]=\frac{4}{(nt)^{2}}-\frac{1}{nt}-\frac{2}{n^{2}t}+\frac{1}{6}+o(1)\;.

(64)

Combining equation (
64
) with equation (
61
) then yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t\mathcal{W}_{1/2,n}})=1\;,

(65)

We have therefore established that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}(\rme^{-t_{n}\mathcal{W}})=\begin{cases}1,&t_{n}=o(n^{-1})\\
0,&t_{n}=\omega(n^{-1}).\end{cases}

(66)

Just as part
(i)
was shown above to follow from (
53
), a
precisely analogous argument shows that part
(ii)

g,h:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, then
\langle g_{J|X_{J}},h_{J|X_{J}}\rangle
is a random variable
on the space
(\mathcal{Q}_{B}\times S^{B},\mu\times\pi)
. We have the following useful lemma, whose proof we defer until the end of this section.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any
g,h:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
we have
\mathbb{E}\,[\langle g_{J|X_{J}},h_{J|X_{J}}\rangle]=\langle g,h\rangle
.

Now let
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
. To simplify notation, we set
\delta:=\delta_{\mu,\pi}
and
\epsilon:=\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}
. Our central task is
to establish the following inequality

\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\langle f,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})\right]+2\,k\,\operatorname{var}(f(X))\,\delta\;.

(123)

First note that both sides of the above inequality are invariant upon replacing
f
by
f-\langle f,\Phi_{\emptyset}\rangle
. It therefore suffices to
establish equation (
123
) under the assumption that
\langle f,\Phi_{\emptyset}\rangle=0
. Under this assumption we have

\|f\|^{2}=\operatorname{var}(f(X))
.

Fix
k\in[B]
, and define
g:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
via

g:=\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\langle f,\Psi_{A}\rangle\,\Psi_{A}\;,

(124)

and note that

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\langle f,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}=\|g\|^{2}\;.

(125)

Equation (
123
) holds trivially if
g=0
, so assume
g\neq 0
.

From lemma
6.1
we have

\displaystyle\|g\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\langle g_{J|X_{J}},f_{J|X_{J}}\rangle

(126)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle+\mathbb{E}\langle g_{J|X_{J}},f_{J|X_{J}}-\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle

(127)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle+\mathbb{E}\,\|g_{J|X_{J}}\|\,\|f_{J|X_{J}}-\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle\Psi_{\emptyset}\|

(128)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}]\,\mathbb{E}[\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}]}

(129)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\,[\|g_{J|X_{J}}\|^{2}]\,\mathbb{E}[\|f_{J|X_{J}}-\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle\Psi_{\emptyset}\|^{2}]}

(130)

where the penultimate step follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, and the final step follows from the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality applied to
\mathbb{E}(\cdot)
.

For any
h:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, one has
\|h-\langle h,\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle\Psi_{\emptyset}\|^{2}=\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(h)
,
and lemma
6.1
implies

\mathbb{E}\|h_{J|X_{J}}\|^{2}=\|h\|^{2}\;.

(131)

Applying these observations to equation (
130
) yields

\|g\|^{2}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}]\,\mathbb{E}[\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}]}+\|g\|\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})]}\;.

(132)

Now observe that for any
h:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}

\langle h,\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}\leq\|h\|^{2}-\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\langle h,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}\leq\|h\|^{2}\;.

(133)

Applying equations (
131
) and (
133
) with
h=f_{J|X_{J}}
, and recalling

\|f\|^{2}=\operatorname{var}(f(X))
, then results in

\|g\|^{2}\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(f(X))\,\mathbb{E}[\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}]}+\|g\|\,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})]}\;,

(134)

and dividing by
\|g\|
and squaring
2
2

2

Making use of
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
yields

\|g\|^{2}\leq 2\operatorname{var}(f(X))\,\frac{\mathbb{E}[\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}]}{\|g\|^{2}}+2\,\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})]\;.

(135)

It remains only to bound
\mathbb{E}[\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}]
. Applying equations (
131
)
and (
133
) now to
h=g_{J|X_{J}}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|^{2}-\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\mathbb{E}\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}

(136)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\mathbb{E}[\langle g,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}-\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}]\;.

(137)

One can easily verify that for any
A,I\subseteq B
and
y\in S^{B}

we have

(\Psi_{A})_{I|y_{I}}=\Psi_{A\cap I}(y)\Psi_{A\setminus I}\;,

(138)

and it follows, for any
h:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, that

h_{I|y_{I}}=\sum_{C\subseteq B}\langle h,\Psi_{C}\rangle\Psi_{C\cap I}(y)\Psi_{C\setminus I}\;.

(139)

Consequently, for any
A\subseteq B

\langle g_{I|y_{I}},\Psi_{A}\rangle=\sum_{C\in\binom{B}{k}}\langle g,\Psi_{C}\rangle\Psi_{C\cap I}(y)\langle\Psi_{C\setminus I},\Psi_{A}\rangle\;.

(140)

In particular, if
|A|=k
we obtain

\langle g_{I|y_{I}},\Psi_{A}\rangle=\langle g,\Psi_{A}\rangle\mathbf{1}(A\cap I=\emptyset)\;,

(141)

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle g,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}-\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}]

\displaystyle=\langle g,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}\,\mathbb{P}(A\cap J\neq\emptyset)

(142)

\displaystyle\leq\langle g,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}\,\sum_{i\in A}\mathbb{P}(J\ni i)

(143)

\displaystyle\leq\langle g,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}\,k\,\delta\;.

(144)

Combining equations (
144
) and (
137
) yields

\mathbb{E}\langle g_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{\emptyset}\rangle^{2}\leq k\,\delta\,\sum_{A\in\binom{B}{k}}\langle g,\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}=k\,\delta\,\|g\|^{2}\;,

(145)

and equation (
123
) then follows upon combining equation (
145
) with
equations (
135
) and (
125
). If
f
is non-constant, one can then divide
both sides of equation (
123
) by
\operatorname{var}(f(X))
, and specialising to the case
\pi=\pi_{p,B}
for some

p\in(0,1)
then yields the statement of theorem
3.6
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

A comparison of equation (
123
) with
[
12
, equation (2.5)]
shows that where
the latter contains a factor
\|f\|^{2}
, the former contains the slightly improved factor
\operatorname{var}(f(X))
. This minor difference is a
simple consequence of the argument applied immediately below (
123
).

It remains now only to prove lemma
6.1
.

Proof of lemma
6.1
.

Let
t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}
and
I\subseteq B
. From the definition of query paths, if for some
y\in S^{I}
and
z\in S^{\bar{I}}
we have

J(t,y\oplus z)=I
, then in fact
J(t,y\oplus z^{\prime})=I
for all
z^{\prime}\in S^{\bar{I}}
. It follows that if

\Sigma_{t}^{I}:=\{y\in S^{I}\,:\,\exists x\in S^{B}\text{ with }x_{I}=y,J(t,x)=I\}\;,

(146)

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,[\langle g_{J|X_{J}},h_{J|X_{J}}\rangle]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}}\sum_{I\subseteq B}\sum_{y\in\Sigma_{t}^{I}}\sum_{z\in S^{\bar{I}}}\mathbb{P}(T=t,X_{I}=y,X_{\bar{I}}=z)\,\langle g_{I|y},h_{I|y}\rangle

(147)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}}\sum_{I\subseteq B}\sum_{y\in\Sigma_{t}^{I}}\mathbb{P}(T=t,X_{I}=y)\,\langle g_{I|y},h_{I|y}\rangle

(148)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}}\sum_{I\subseteq B}\sum_{y\in\Sigma_{t}^{I}}\mathbb{P}(T=t,X_{I}=y)\,\sum_{x^{\prime}\in S^{B}}\pi(x^{\prime})g_{I|y}(x^{\prime})h_{I|y}(x^{\prime})

(149)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}}\sum_{I\subseteq B}\sum_{y\in\Sigma_{t}^{I}}\mathbb{P}(T=t,X_{I}=y)\,

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{y^{\prime}\in S^{I}}\sum_{z^{\prime}\in S^{\bar{I}}}\mathbb{P}(X_{I}=y^{\prime},X_{\bar{I}}=z^{\prime})g_{I|y}(y^{\prime}\oplus z^{\prime})h_{I|y}(y^{\prime}\oplus z^{\prime})

(150)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}}\sum_{I\subseteq B}\sum_{y\in\Sigma_{t}^{I}}\mathbb{P}(T=t,X_{I}=y)\,

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{y^{\prime}\in S^{I}}\sum_{z^{\prime}\in S^{\bar{I}}}\mathbb{P}(X_{I}=y^{\prime},X_{\bar{I}}=z^{\prime})g(y\oplus z^{\prime})h(y\oplus z^{\prime})

(151)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}}\sum_{I\subseteq B}\sum_{y\in\Sigma_{t}^{I}}\sum_{z\in S^{\bar{I}}}\mathbb{P}(T=t,X_{I}=y)\,\mathbb{P}(X_{\bar{I}}=z)g(y\oplus z)h(y\oplus z)

(152)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{Q}_{B}}\sum_{x\in S^{B}}\mathbb{P}(T=t,X=x)\,g(x)h(x)

(153)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in S^{B}}\pi(x)\,g(x)h(x)

(154)

\displaystyle=\langle g,h\rangle\;.

(155)

∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
B
be a finite set. Let
\pi:=\pi_{p,B}
, and let
\mu
be any distribution on
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
. For any
non-constant function
f:S^{B}\to\mathbb{R}
we have
\epsilon_{\mu,\pi,f}\leq 1
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbb{E}(f)=0
, so that
\langle f,f\rangle=\operatorname{var}(f(X))
. Since

\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[n]\\
A\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\,\langle f_{J|X_{J}},\Psi_{A}\rangle^{2}\leq\langle f_{J|X_{J}},f_{J|X_{J}}\rangle\;,

(156)

it follows from lemma
6.1
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}_{\pi}(f_{J|X_{J}})]\leq\operatorname{var}(f(X))\;.

(157)

Since both sides of equation (
157
) are unchanged upon adding a constant to
f
, the assumption of mean zero
can be relaxed, and the stated result follows.
