Quantum differential and difference equations for Hilb n ​ ( ℂ 2 ) \mathrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}) .
Andrey Smirnov, asmirnov@email.unc.edu

Abstract
We consider the quantum difference equation of the Hilbert scheme of points in ℂ 2 {\mathbb{C}}^{2} . This equation is the K-theoretic generalization of the quantum differential equation discovered by A. Okounkov and R. Pandharipande in [ 27 ] . We obtain two explicit descriptions for the monodromy of these equations - representation-theoretic and algebro-geometric. In the representation-theoretic description, the monodromy acts via certain explicit elements in the quantum toroidal algebra 𝒰 ℏ ​ ( 𝔤 ​ 𝔩 ^ ^ 1 ) {\mathscr{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}}}_{1})} . In the algebro-geometric description, the monodromy features as transition matrices between the stable envelope bases in equivariant K-theory and elliptic cohomology. Using the second approach we identify the monodromy matrices for the differential equation with the K-theoretic R R -matrices of cyclic quiver varieties, which appear as subvarieties in the 3 ​ D 3D -mirror Hilbert scheme. Most of the results in the paper are illustrated by explicit examples for cases n = 2 n=2 and n = 3 n=3 in the Appendix.

Introduction

1.1
Quantum differential equation for
\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})

In the study of Gromov-Witten/Donaldson-Thomas theories of threefolds and representation theory of affine Yangians a certain first order differential equation with remarkable properties emerges naturally. This equation features as the quantum differential equation (qde) in the equivariant quantum cohomology of the Hilbert scheme of points in the plane
\mathsf{X}=\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
and was first discovered and investigated by A. Okounkov and R. Pandharipande in
[
26
,
27
]
.

The quantum differential equation describes a connection in a trivial bundle over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with fiber given by the equivariant cohomology
H^{\bullet}_{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{X})
. The singularities of this connection are located at:

{\mathsf{Sing}}=\{0,\infty,\sqrt[k]{1},k=2,...,n\}\setminus\{1\}\,\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}

where
\sqrt[k]{1}
denotes the set of all complex
k
-th roots of
1
. The most important global properties of the qde are encoded in the homomorphism

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus{\mathsf{Sing}},z)\to\textrm{End}(H_{\mathsf{T}}^{\bullet}(\mathsf{X}))

(1)

provided by the monodromy of the corresponding connection.
One of the goals of this paper is to give a complete description of this homomorphism for special choices of the based point
z\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

We recall the explicit form of the qde and its main properties in Section
2
.

1.2

\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})

In order to understand the monodromy of qde, we first consider its

\displaystyle\Psi(zq)=\mathbf{M}(z)\Psi(z),\ \ \ \Psi(z)\in K_{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{X}),\ \ \ |q|<1

The operator

{\mathscr{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}}}_{1})}

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(z)=\mathscr{O}(1)\!\!\prod^{\longrightarrow}\limits_{{w\in{\mathbb{Q}}}\atop{-1\ll w<0}}\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)

In the cohomological limit, which corresponds to sending

1.3
Monodromy of the quantum difference equation

It is well known that the
q
-difference equations are easier to work with than their differential limits. In particular, the multiplicative nature of
q
-difference equations allows one to describe their fundamental solutions in terms of infinite products.
In Section

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}(z)\sim\prod^{\longrightarrow}\limits_{{w\in{\mathbb{Q}}}}\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q).

(4)

We explain our notation for the product over rational numbers later in Section
3.5

In cohomological limit

\mathbf{Mon}(z)
we obtain description of the monodromy of the differentiation equation.
In particular, we find that the operators

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{w}:=\mathbf{B}_{w}(\infty,1)=:\!\exp\Big(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}r^{w}_{k}\alpha^{w}_{-k}\alpha^{w}_{k}\Big)\!:

(5)

describe the monodromy of the qde along the loop based at
z=0
which goes around the singularity located at the root of unity

, see Fig.
4
in Section
8

17

1

1.5

Using (
7

The monodromy of the Casimir connection for the classical symplectic resolutions

Important structures emerge the from the algebra

As we will see below, with this choice of normalization, the transports of the qde from
z=0
to
z=\infty
have a natural Gauss decomposition.

2.7
Transport of qde

Assume that we have analytic continuations of the fundamental solutions
\psi^{0}_{\mathsf{DT}}(z)
and
\psi^{\infty}_{\mathsf{DT}}(z)
to some larger domains. In this case we can compare them at the points of

\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|=1,z\not\in{\mathsf{Sing}}\}.

Explicitly, this is the set of points
z=e^{2\pi is}
for some
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let us consider the transition matrix between two fundamental solutions:

\displaystyle\textrm{Tran}_{\mathsf{DT}}(s)=\psi^{0}_{\mathsf{DT}}(e^{2\pi is})^{-1}\psi^{\infty}_{\mathsf{DT}}(e^{2\pi is}).

(33)

This operator describes the transport of the qde from
z=0
to
z=\infty
along the line which intersects
|z|=1
at
e^{2\pi is}
. The transport depends only on homotopy equivalence class of the path. This means that
\textrm{Tran}_{\mathsf{DT}}(s)
is a piecewise constant function of
s
, which changes value only when
e^{2\pi is}
hits a singularity.

Theorem 4

(Section 4.6
[
27
]
)
.

The transport of qde along the positive part of the real axis
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
equals:

\mathrm{Tran}_{\mathsf{DT}}(0)=\mathsf{P}^{-1}\,\mathsf{P}^{*}.

where

\mathsf{P}^{*}=(-1)^{l}\,\mathsf{P}\,\mathbf{S}.

Proof.

Let
\psi^{\infty}_{\mathsf{DT}}(z)
be the solution (
30
).
Theorem
3
together with (
16
) gives

\psi^{\infty}_{\mathsf{DT}}(1)=\Gamma_{\mathsf{GW}}(-1)^{l}\,\mathsf{P}\,\mathbf{S}

Thus,

\textrm{Tran}_{\mathsf{DT}}(0)=\psi^{0}_{\mathsf{DT}}(1)^{-1}\psi^{\infty}_{\mathsf{DT}}(1)=\mathsf{P}^{-1}\,(-1)^{l}\,\mathsf{P}\,\mathbf{S}.

∎

In Section
8
we generalize this result and describe

\textrm{Tran}_{\mathsf{DT}}(s)
for an arbitrary
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
e^{-2\pi is}\not\in{\mathsf{Sing}}
.

3
K-theoretic
q
-difference equation

The quantum difference equation (QDE) is the K-theoretic generalization of the quantum differential equation (qde) in cohomology. For the Nakajima quiver varieties these equations were computed in
[
28
]
. In particular, the case
of the Hilbert scheme
\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
is considered in detail in Section 8 of
[
28
]
. In this section we briefly review this construction.

3.1
Fock representation of quantum toroidal
\mathfrak{gl}_{1}

In this section we denote

\displaystyle\textsf{Fock}={\mathbb{Q}}[p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\dots]\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1},t_{2}).

(34)

with
t_{1},t_{2}
given by (
26
). There is an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1},t_{2})
-vector spaces

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\,K_{\mathsf{T}}(\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}))_{loc}=\textsf{Fock}

(35)

Under this isomorphism the K-theory classes
\mathscr{O}_{\lambda}\in K_{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{X})
of fixed points
\lambda\in\mathsf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}
are mapped to
Macdonald polynomials

P_{\lambda}\in\textsf{Fock}
in Haiman’s normalization
[
13
]
.
These polynomials form a basis of the Fock space, see Appendix
B
for examples of
P_{\lambda}
.

Similarly to the qde in cohomology, the K-theoretic QDE for
\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
is described via an action of certain algebra on
Fock
. The K-theoretic structure is, however, much richer. The Fock space (
34
) is a natural representation of a quantum group
{\mathscr{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}}}_{1})}
, called
quantum toroidal

{\mathfrak{gl}}_{1}
. The representation theory of this algebra and its role in mathematical physics is an exceptionally rich subject: we refer to
[
18
,
4
,
21
,
32
,
19
,
9
,
10
]
for a very incomplete list of research by different groups.

The algebra
{\mathscr{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}}}_{1})}
, has an explicit presentation in terms of generators and relations
[
32
]
:

{\mathscr{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}}}_{1})}=\Big\langle e_{(n,m)}:(n,m)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\setminus(0,0)\Big\rangle/\textrm{relations}

Given a rational number

w=\frac{a}{b}
with
\textrm{gcd}(a,b)=1
one can consider the generators with “slope”
w
, see Fig.
1
:

\alpha^{w}_{k}=e_{bk,ak},\ \ k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}.

For each
w\in{\mathbb{Q}}\cup\{\infty\}
the elements
\alpha^{w}_{k}
generate the slope
w
Heisenberg subalgebra
H_{w}\subset{\mathscr{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}}}_{1})}

subject to the following relations:

[\alpha^{w}_{i},\alpha^{w}_{j}]=\delta_{i+j}r^{(w)}_{i},\ \ \ r^{(w)}_{i}=\dfrac{i\,(\hbar^{\frac{kb}{2}}-\hbar^{-\frac{kb}{2}})}{(t_{1}^{\frac{k}{2}}-t_{1}^{-\frac{k}{2}})(t_{2}^{\frac{k}{2}}-t_{2}^{-\frac{k}{2}})(\hbar^{\frac{k}{2}}-\hbar^{-\frac{k}{2}})}

In particular, the slope
w=0
Heisenberg subalgebra
\alpha^{0}_{k}=e(k,0)
acts on the Fock space by

\alpha^{0}_{m}(f)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rr}\dfrac{-p_{-m}\cdot f}{(t_{1}^{m/2}-t_{1}^{-m/2})(t_{2}^{m/2}-t_{2}^{-m/2})}&m<0\\
-m\dfrac{df}{dp_{m}}&m>0\end{array}\right.

which is a natural deformation of (
2.1
). Note that the slope
w=\infty
-subalgebra is commutative (in this case
b=0
). The elements
\alpha^{\infty}_{k}=e(0,k)
act diagonally in the basis of fixed points
P_{\lambda}
and can be identified with operators of multiplication by tautological bundles in the equivariant K-theory
K_{\mathsf{T}}(\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}))
, see Section 8.1.5 of
[
28
]
for more details. The operators
\alpha^{\infty}_{k}
can also be identified with so called Macdonald operators for
\mathfrak{gl}_{\infty}
.

For a general slope
w=\frac{a}{b}
the operators
\alpha^{w}_{k}
act in (
35
) changing the weight by
kb
units

\displaystyle\alpha^{w}_{k}:K_{\mathsf{T}}(\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}))\longrightarrow K_{\mathsf{T}}(\textrm{Hilb}^{n-kb}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})).

(36)

In particular,
\alpha^{w}_{k}(f)=0
for any
f\in K_{\mathsf{T}}(\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}))
with
n<bk
.
For this reason
\alpha^{w}_{-k}
with
k>0
are usually referred to as
creation operators
and
\alpha^{w}_{k}
as
annihilation operators
. The action of the operators
\alpha^{w}_{k}
on the Fock space for general
w
and
k
is quite complicated. Still, it can be described explicitly in the fixed point basis
[
20
]
.

w=1/2

3.2
Wall-crossing operators

For any
w\in{\mathbb{Q}}\cup\{\infty\}
we define the
wall-crossing operator

\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)
acting on the Fock space by

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)=:\!\exp\Big(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{r^{w}_{k}}{1-z^{-bk}{q^{ak}}}\alpha^{w}_{-k}\alpha^{w}_{k}\Big)\!:

(37)

where
a
and
b
denote the numerator and the denominator of
w
and
q
is a formal complex parameter. The symbol
::
denotes the normally ordered operator. This means that in the Taylor expansion of the exponent (
37
) the
annihilation operators

\alpha^{w}_{k}
with
k>0
act before the
creation operators

\alpha^{w}_{-k}
. By (
36
) only finitely many terms in this expansion act non-trivially. Thus the action of
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)
for
w\in{\mathbb{Q}}
is well defined.

It is clear from (
37
) that the wall-crossing operators preserve the summands in (
35
). By the same reason as above the operators
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)
act non-trivially (i.e.,
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)\neq 1
) on
K_{\mathsf{T}}(\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}))
only for
w
corresponding to the Farey sequence of order
b
:

\mathsf{Walls}_{n}=\Big\{w=\frac{a}{b}\in{\mathbb{Q}}:1\leq|b|\leq n\Big\}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}

which we will call
walls
. From the explicit formulas for the action of the
\infty
-slope Heisenberg algebra one finds that the action of
\mathbf{B}_{\infty}(z,q)
is also well defined, but we will not need it in this paper.

Finally, we note that the matrix elements of
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)
are rational functions in
z
and
q
. Using the action of
{\mathscr{U}_{\hbar}(\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}}}_{1})}
on the Fock space the matrices
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)
can be computed very explicitly, see examples in Sections 8.3.7-8.3.8 of
[
28
]
.

3.3
Monodromy operators

Note that
\mathbf{B}_{w}(0,q)=1
. We denote

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{w}=\mathbf{B}_{w}(\infty,q)=:\!\exp\Big(\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}r^{w}_{k}\alpha^{w}_{-k}\alpha^{w}_{k}\Big)\!:

(38)

We call
\mathbf{B}_{w}
the
monodromy operators
. The matrix elements of
\mathbf{B}_{w}
do not depend on
z
or
q
.
From the explicit formulas above one computes

\mathbf{B}_{0}=(t_{1}t_{2})^{n}
. It follows from Proposition
2
below that

\mathbf{B}_{w}=(t_{1}t_{2})^{n},\ \ w\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

In contrast, the monodromy operators
\mathbf{B}_{w}
for non-integral values of
w
are quite nontrivial, see the examples in Appendix
G
.

We denote
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z):=\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,1)
.
The following Lemma is immediate from definition (
37
).

Lemma 1
.

For
s\in{\mathbb{Q}}
we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}(zq^{s},q)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{B}_{w}&w>s\\
1&w<s\\
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)&s=w\end{array}\right.

In Section
8.4
we prove that the operator
\mathbf{B}_{w}
describes the monodromy of
\nabla_{\textsf{DT}}
along the loop around the singularity of
\nabla_{\textsf{DT}}
at
z=e^{2\pi iw}
, which explains our choice of its name.

3.4
Basic properties of wall-crossing operators

Let us denote by
\mathscr{L}_{0}
the operator of tensor multiplication by the line bundle
\mathscr{O}(1)
in equivariant K-theory. In the basis of fixed points it is characterized by the following eigenvalues

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{0}\,(P_{\lambda})=(\prod\limits_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\,t_{1}^{i-1}t_{2}^{j-1})\,P_{\lambda}.

(43)

Proposition 2
.

\mathscr{L}_{0}
intertwines the action of the Heisenberg algebras
H_{w}
and
H_{w-1}
on the Fock space:

\mathscr{L}_{0}^{-1}\alpha^{w}_{k}=\alpha^{w-1}_{k}\mathscr{L}_{0}^{-1}.

In particular, it intertwines the wall crossing operators

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{-1}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{w}(zq,q)\mathscr{L}_{0}=\mathbf{B}_{w-1}(z,q)

(44)

and the monodromy operators

\mathscr{L}^{-1}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)\mathscr{L}_{0}=\mathbf{B}_{w-1}(z),\ \ \ \mathscr{L}^{-1}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{w}\mathscr{L}_{0}=\mathbf{B}_{w-1}.

Proof.

See
[
28
]
, Section 8.
∎

Proposition 3
.

For arbitrary complex parameters
a
and
b
the wall-crossing operators commute

\mathbf{B}_{w}(a,q)\mathbf{B}_{w}(b,q)=\mathbf{B}_{w}(b,q)\mathbf{B}_{w}(a,q),

i.e., the coefficients of the Taylor expansion
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{B}_{w,k}z^{k}
commute

\mathbf{B}_{w,i}\mathbf{B}_{w,j}=\mathbf{B}_{w,j}\mathbf{B}_{w,i}

Proof.

Follows directly from (
37
) and relations in
H_{w}
.
∎

The following result describes the transformation of the wall-crossing operators and the quantum difference equation under
z\to z^{-1}
.

Proposition 4
.

\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)\mathbf{B}_{w}^{-1}=\mathbf{B}_{w}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)=\mathbf{B}_{w}(z^{-1},q^{-1})^{-1}

in particular, at
q=1

\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)\mathbf{B}_{w}^{-1}=\mathbf{B}_{w}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)=\mathbf{B}_{w}(z^{-1})^{-1}.

Proof.

Follows directly from (
37
) and relations in
H_{w}
.
∎

3.5
Quantum difference equation for
\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})

The
quantum difference equation
for
\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
(QDE) has the following form

\displaystyle\Psi(zq)=\mathbf{M}(z)\Psi(z),\ \ \Psi(z)\in\textsf{Fock}

(45)

where
\mathbf{M}(z)\in\textrm{End}(\textsf{Fock})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(z)=\mathscr{L}_{0}\prod\limits_{w\in[-1,0)}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q).

(46)

In the “classical limit”
z=0
the operator
\mathbf{M}(z)

coincides with
\mathscr{L}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(0)=\mathscr{L}_{0}.

(47)

In (
46
) and throughout this paper we use the following conventions:

\prod\limits_{w\in I}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)

denotes the product over the rational numbers in an interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
ordered so that
w
increases from the left to the right. Similarly,

\prod\limits_{w\in I}^{\longleftarrow}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}(z,q)

denotes the ordered product of operators with
w
increasing from right to left. If
I\cap\mathsf{Walls}_{n}
is finite, as for instance in (
46
), then all but finitely many terms in these products act on the Fock space as identity operators. Thus, for fixed
n
and bounded
I
these products are finite and well defined.

Example 1
.

Let us assume that
n=3
then, for instance

\prod\limits_{w\in[0,1)}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)=\mathbf{B}_{0}(z)\mathbf{B}_{\frac{1}{3}}(z)\mathbf{B}_{\frac{1}{2}}(z)\mathbf{B}_{\frac{2}{3}}(z)

or

\prod\limits_{w\in(-1,1)}^{\longleftarrow}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}=\mathbf{B}_{\frac{2}{3}}\mathbf{B}_{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{B}_{\frac{1}{3}}\mathbf{B}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{-\frac{1}{3}}\mathbf{B}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{B}_{-\frac{2}{3}}.

4
Solutions of QDE and monodromy

The multiplicative nature of
q
-difference equations allows us to construct fundamental solutions and monodromy matrices as infinite products of the wall crossing operators. For instance, in the case of zero-dimensional
A_{\infty}
quiver varieties we are dealing with the first order
scalar

q
-difference equations and the analysis of monodromy is elementary, see Section 5 of
[
7
]
. In this section apply the same logic for the Hilbert scheme
\mathrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
.

4.1
Fundamental solution of QDE near
z=0

By (
47
) the operator
\mathbf{M}(0)
is diagonal in the basis of fixed points, which in the Fock space corresponds to the basis of Macdonald polynomials
P_{\lambda}
. We denote by
P
the matrix with columns given by eigenvectors
P_{\lambda}
. We denote by
{\mathbf{E}_{0}}
the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues, so that

\mathbf{M}(0)\,\textsf{P}=\textsf{P}\,{\mathbf{E}_{0}}.

The eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{E}_{0}}
are monomials in
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
given by (
43
).

From the basic theory of
q
-difference equations there exists a unique fundamental solution of the QDE of the form

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(z)=\mathsf{P}\,\Psi^{reg}_{0}(z)\,e^{\frac{\ln({\mathbf{E}}_{0})\ln(z)}{\ln(q)}},\ \ \ \Psi^{reg}_{0}(z)=1+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\,\Psi^{reg}_{0,k}\,z^{k}

(48)

The matrix
\Psi^{reg}_{0}(z)
solves

\displaystyle\Psi^{reg}_{0}(zq){\mathbf{E}}_{0}=\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}(z)\Psi^{reg}_{0}(z).

(49)

where
\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}(z)=\mathsf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{M}(z)\mathsf{P}
is the matrix for the operator
\mathbf{M}(z)
in the basis of the fixed points
\mathsf{P}
. Here, again, we assume implicitly that
\mathbf{M}(z)
is the “matrix” of the corresponding operator in the basis
p_{\mu}
and
\mathsf{P}
is the transition matrix from
p_{\mu}
to the basis of Macdonald polynomials
P_{\mu}
, see Remark
1
above and Appendix
B
for examples of
\mathsf{P}
.

Let us define

\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{0}(z):={\mathbf{E}}^{-1}_{0}\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}(z),\ \ \ \mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{k}(z):={\mathbf{E}}^{-k}_{0}\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{0}(zq^{k}){\mathbf{E}}^{k}_{0}.

Then the infinite product

\displaystyle\Psi^{reg}_{0}(z)=\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{0}(z)^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{1}(z)^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{2}(z)^{-1}\dots

(50)

solves (
49
). We denote by

\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}(z):=\mathsf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)\mathsf{P},\ \ \ \mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}:=\mathsf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{w}\mathsf{P}

the matrices of operators
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)
and
\mathbf{B}_{w}
in the basis of fixed points
\mathsf{P}
.

Proposition 5
.

For
s\in{\mathbb{Q}}
the fundamental solution (
50
) has the following limits:

\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\Psi^{reg}_{0}(zq^{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&s\geq 0\\
\Big(\prod\limits^{\longleftarrow}_{w\in(s,0)}\,(\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w})^{-1}\Big)\cdot\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{s}(z)^{-1}&s<0\end{array}\right.

Proof.

In the basis of fixed points
\mathsf{P}
the matrix of the operator
\mathscr{L}_{0}
is given by
{\mathbf{E}}_{0}
, thus

\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{0}(z)=\prod\limits_{w\in[-1,0)}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}(z)

and from (
44
) we find

\mathbf{M}^{\bullet}_{k}(z)=\prod\limits_{w\in[-1-k,-k)}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}(z)

which gives

\Psi^{reg}_{0}(z)=\prod\limits^{\longleftarrow}_{w\in(-\infty,0)}\,\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}(z)^{-1}.

The proposition follows from (
1
).
∎

4.2
Fundamental solution of QDE near
z=\infty

By the general theory of quantum difference equations for Nakajima quiver varieties
[
28
]
the operator
\mathbf{M}(z)
near
z=\infty
defines the QDE for the Nakajima variety with opposite stability condition. In particular, the matrix
\mathbf{M}(\infty)
is diagonalizable over
{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1},t_{2})
with eigenvalues given by monomials in
t_{1},t_{2}
.

Let
\textsf{H}^{\ast}
be the matrix with columns given by the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{M}(\infty)
. Let
{\mathbf{E}_{\infty}}
be the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues so that:

\mathbf{M}(\infty)\,\textsf{H}^{\ast}=\textsf{H}^{\ast}\,{\mathbf{E}_{\infty}}

In Section
8.3
we will show that

\textsf{H}^{\ast}=\textsf{P}^{\ast}
with
\textsf{P}^{\ast}
as in Theorem
4
, but at the moment we only need the fact that the eigenbasis
\textsf{H}^{\ast}
exists.
The QDE has unique fundamental solution of the form

\displaystyle\Psi_{\infty}(z)=\textsf{H}^{\ast}\,\Psi^{reg}_{\infty}(z)\,e^{\frac{\ln({\mathbf{E}}_{\infty})\ln(z)}{\ln(q)}},\ \ \ \Psi^{reg}_{\infty}(z)=1+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\Psi^{reg}_{\infty,k}\,z^{-k}.

(51)

The matrix
\Psi^{reg}_{\infty}(z)
solves the equation:

\displaystyle\Psi^{reg}_{\infty}(z)\,{\mathbf{E}}_{\infty}=\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z)\Psi^{reg}_{\infty}(z)

(52)

where
\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z)=(\textsf{P}^{\ast})^{-1}\,\mathbf{M}(z)\,\textsf{P}^{\ast}
denotes the matrix of
\mathbf{M}(z)
in the basis of eigenvectors
\textsf{P}^{\ast}
. We denote

\mathbf{M}_{0}^{\ast}(z)={\mathbf{E}}^{-1}_{\infty}\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z),\ \ \ \mathbf{M}^{\ast}_{k}(z)={\mathbf{E}}^{k}_{\infty}\,\mathbf{M}^{\ast}_{0}(zq^{-k})\,{\mathbf{E}}^{-k}_{\infty}

then the infinite product

\displaystyle\Psi^{reg}_{\infty}(z)=\mathbf{M}_{1}^{\ast}(z)\mathbf{M}_{2}^{\ast}(z)\mathbf{M}_{2}^{\ast}(z)\cdots

(53)

solves (
52
). Let us denote

\mathbf{B}^{\ast}_{w}(z):=(\textsf{P}^{\ast})^{-1}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)\,\textsf{P}^{\ast},\ \ \mathbf{B}^{\ast}_{w}:=(\textsf{P}^{\ast})^{-1}\,\mathbf{B}_{w}\,\textsf{P}^{\ast}

the matrices of the operators
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)
and
\mathbf{B}_{w}
in the basis
\textsf{P}^{\ast}
.

Proposition 6
.

The solution (
53
) has the following limits:

\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\,\Psi^{reg}_{\infty}(zq^{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{B}^{\ast}_{s}(z^{-1})^{-1}\prod\limits_{w\in[0,s)}^{\longleftarrow}\,(\mathbf{B}^{\ast}_{w})^{-1}&s\geq 0\\
&\\
1,&s<0\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Let us consider a finite approximation of infinite product (
53
):

\Psi^{reg}_{N}(z)=\mathbf{M}_{1}^{\ast}(z)\mathbf{M}_{2}^{\ast}(z)\cdots\mathbf{M}_{N}^{\ast}(z)=\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z/q)\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z/q^{2})\cdots\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z/q^{N}){\mathbf{E}}^{-N}_{\infty}

Using (
44
) we rewrite the last expression in the form

\Psi^{reg}_{N}(z)=\Big(\prod\limits_{[0,N)}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}^{*}_{w}(z,q)\Big)\,(\mathbf{M}(0)^{*})^{N}\,{\mathbf{E}}^{-N}_{\infty}.

From (
1
) we find:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\,\Psi^{reg}_{N}(zq^{s})=\mathbf{B}^{*}_{s}(z)\Big(\prod\limits_{(s,N)}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}^{*}_{w}\Big)\,(\mathbf{M}(0)^{*})^{N}\,{\mathbf{E}}^{-N}_{\infty}

(54)

Let us note that

\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z)\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z/q)\cdots\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(z/q^{N-1})=\Big(\prod\limits_{w\in[0,N)}\,\mathbf{B}^{\ast}_{w}(z,q)\Big)\,\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(0)^{N}

where the last equality is by (
44
). At
z=\infty
we obtain

{\mathbf{E}}^{N}_{\infty}=\Big(\prod\limits_{w\in[0,N)}\,\mathbf{B}^{\ast}_{w}\Big)\,\mathbf{M}^{\ast}(0)^{N}

which is the same as

\displaystyle\Big(\prod\limits_{[0,N)}^{\longrightarrow}\,\mathbf{B}^{*}_{w}\Big)\,(\mathbf{M}(0)^{*})^{N}\,{\mathbf{E}}^{-N}_{\infty}=1.

(55)

Dividing (
54
) by (
55
) and assuming that
N>s
we obtain

\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\,\Psi^{reg}_{N}(zq^{s})=\mathbf{B}^{*}_{s}(z)\prod\limits_{[0,s]}^{\longleftarrow}\,(\mathbf{B}^{*}_{w})^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{*}_{s}(z^{-1})^{-1}\prod\limits_{[0,s)}^{\longleftarrow}\,(\mathbf{B}^{*}_{w})^{-1}

where the last equality is by Proposition
4
.
We see that for large
N
, the limit does not depend on
N
and the proposition follows.
∎

4.3
Monodromy of QDE

The monodromy of QDE is defined as the transition matrix between the two fundamental solutions constructed above:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}(z,t_{1},t_{2}):=\Psi_{0}(z)^{-1}\Psi_{\infty}(z).

(56)

Clearly,
\mathbf{Mon}(zq,t_{1},t_{2})=\mathbf{Mon}(z,t_{1},t_{2})
.

Remark 4
.

If we change a basis of the Fock space by
A\in\textrm{End}(\textsf{Fock})
then the fundamental solutions transforms as

\Psi_{0}(z)\to\textsf{A}\Psi_{0}(z),\ \ \Psi_{\infty}(z)\to\textsf{A}\Psi_{\infty}(z)

Thus,
\mathbf{Mon}(z,t_{1},t_{2})
does not depend on the basis in which the QDE is considered.

It will be convenient to work with
the regular part of monodromy
which is defined like (
56
) but without exponential factors in (
48
) and (
51
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}^{reg}(z,t_{1},t_{2}):=e^{\frac{\ln({\mathbf{E}}_{0})\ln(z)}{\ln(q)}}\,\mathbf{Mon}(z,t_{1},t_{2})\,e^{-\frac{\ln({\mathbf{E}}_{\infty})\ln(z)}{\ln(q)}}.

(57)

The regular part is not
q
-periodic

\displaystyle\mathbf{Mon}^{reg}(zq,t_{1},t_{2})={\mathbf{E}}_{0}\,\mathbf{Mon}^{reg}(z,t_{1},t_{2})\,{\mathbf{E}}^{-1}_{\infty}.

(58)

Combining Propositions
5
and
6
we obtain:

Theorem 5
.

For
s\in{\mathbb{Q}}
the monodromy of qde has the following asymptotic at
q\to 0
:

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to 0}\mathbf{Mon}^{reg}(zq^{s},t_{1},t_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{s}(z^{-1})^{-1}\,\prod\limits_{w\in[0,s)}^{\longleftarrow}\,(\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w})^{-1}\cdot\,\mathbf{T}&s\geq 0,\\
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{s}(z)\,\prod\limits^{\longrightarrow}_{w\in(s,0)}\,\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}\,\cdot\mathbf{T},&s<0.\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}(z)
,
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{w}
denote the matrices of the operators
\mathbf{B}_{w}(z)
and
\mathbf{B}_{w}
in the basis of fixed points
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathbf{T}
is the matrix of transition matrix between the bases
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{H}^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}:=\mathsf{P}^{-1}\mathsf{H}^{\ast}.

(62)

Remark 5
.

The above theorem says that the limit
\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\mathbf{Mon}^{reg}(zq^{s},t_{1},t_{2})
is a piecewise constant function of
s\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, which changes only when
s
crosses a “wall” from
\mathsf{Walls}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}
. Moreover, if
s\not\in\mathsf{Walls}_{n}
then
\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{s}(z)=1
and the limit is independent on the Kähler variable

, see example below.

Example 2
.

Let us consider the case
n=3
and
s=-2/3
then

\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\mathbf{Mon}^{reg}(zq^{-2/3},t_{1},t_{2})=\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{-2/3}(z)\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{-1/2}\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{-1/3}\,\mathbf{T},

but for
n=2
we obtain

\lim\limits_{q\to 0}\mathbf{Mon}^{reg}(zq^{-2/3})=\mathbf{B}^{\bullet}_{-1/2}\,\mathbf{T},

in particular, the last limit does not depend on

5
Elliptic stable envelope

A new approach to studying the monodromy was recently suggested in
[
2
,
24
,
25
]
.
In this approach, the monodromy of the QDE for a variety
\mathsf{X}
is identified with the transition matrix between the
elliptic stable bases
in the equivariant elliptic cohomology of
\mathsf{X}
.
In this section we apply this idea to
\mathsf{X}=\textrm{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
.
In this and next sections we will also use equivariant parameters
a
and
\hbar
defined by

t_{1}=a\hbar^{1/2},\ \ t_{2}=a^{-1}\hbar^{1/2}.

5.1
Multiplicative notations

Let us introduce the following functions:

\hat{s}(x):=x^{1/2}-x^{-1/2},\ \ \varphi(x):=\prod\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(1-xq^{i})

the last product converges for
|q|<1
which we assume throughout. Let

\displaystyle\theta(x):=\varphi(qx)\hat{s}(x)\varphi(qx^{-1}).

(63)

denote the
q
-theta function. We will denote by
\hat{S}
,
\Phi
,
\Theta
multiplicative extensions of these functions to Laurent polynomials via the rules:

\hat{S}(a+b)=\hat{S}(a)\hat{S}(b),\ \ \Phi(a+b)=\Phi(a)\Phi(b),\ \ \Theta(a+b)=\Theta(a)\Theta(b)

Given a Laurent polynomial
P
we defile by
\det(P)
via

\det(a+b)=ab.

Example 3
.

Let
P=a-2b+3c
then

\Phi(P)=\dfrac{\varphi(a)\varphi(c)^{3}}{\varphi(b)^{2}},\ \ \det(P)=\dfrac{ac^{3}}{b^{2}}.

If
P^{*}=a^{-1}-2b^{-1}+3c^{-1}
denote the dualization in K-theory then

\Theta(P)=\Phi(qP)\hat{S}(P)\Phi(qP^{*}).

The last formula, clearly, holds for any K-theory class
P
.

Let
{\cal{V}}\in K_{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{X})
be the class of the tautological bundle over the Hilbert scheme
\mathsf{X}
. The class

P={\cal{V}}+{\cal{V}}\otimes{\cal{V}}^{\ast}t_{1}-{\cal{V}}\otimes{\cal{V}}^{\ast}

is a polarization of
\mathsf{X}
, in other words, it is half of the class of the tangent bundle:

T\mathsf{X}=P+\hbar^{-1}P^{*}\in K_{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{X}).

For the computations below, the following Lemma is convenient.

Lemma 2
.

\dfrac{1}{\hat{s}(T\mathsf{X})}\,\dfrac{\Theta(P)}{\Phi((q-\hbar)P)}=\Phi(qT\mathsf{X})\,\mathscr{O}(1)^{1/2}\,\hbar^{-n/2}\,t_{1}^{n^{2}/2}

Proof.

Using the multiplicative notation we write:

\dfrac{1}{\hat{s}(T\mathsf{X})}\,\dfrac{\Theta(P)}{\Phi((q-\hbar)P)}=\dfrac{1}{\hat{s}(T\mathsf{X})}\,\dfrac{\Phi(qP)\hat{s}(P)\Phi(qP^{\ast})}{\Phi((q-\hbar)P)}=\dfrac{\det(P)^{1/2}\Phi(P^{\ast}+\hbar P)}{\hat{s}(T\mathsf{X})}.

Since
P^{\ast}+\hbar^{-1}P=T\mathsf{X}
we have

\dfrac{1}{\hat{s}(T\mathsf{X})}\,\dfrac{\Theta(P)}{\Phi((q-\hbar)P)}=\det(P)^{1/2}\det(T\mathsf{X})^{1/2}\Phi(qT\mathsf{X}).

Using the explicit form of the polarization and
\det({\cal{V}})=\mathscr{O}(1)
we compute

\det(P)^{1/2}=\det({\cal{V}})^{1/2}t_{1}^{n^{2}/2}=\mathscr{O}(1)^{1/2}t_{1}^{n^{2}/2}.

As
\mathsf{X}
is symplectic, with the
\mathsf{T}
-weight of the symplectic form given by
\hbar
, for each
w\in\textrm{weight}_{\mathsf{T}}(T_{\lambda}\mathsf{X})
we have the symplectic dual dual weight
w^{-1}\hbar^{-1}\in\textrm{weight}_{\mathsf{T}}(T_{\lambda}\mathsf{X})
. Thus

\det(T\mathsf{X})=\hbar^{-n}.

Lemma follows.
∎

5.2
Fixed point components of elliptic stable envelopes

For a cocharacter
\sigma\in\mathrm{Lie}_{{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathsf{A})
and a fixed point
\lambda\in\mathsf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}
the construction of
[
2
,
24
]
provides a class
\mathrm{Stab}^{Ell}_{\sigma}(\lambda)
in the equivariant elliptic cohomology of
\mathsf{X}
, called the elliptic stable envelope of
\lambda
. In this paper we assume that
\sigma
corresponds to the cocharacter
a\to 0
.

The classes
\mathrm{Stab}^{Ell}_{\sigma}(\lambda)
can be characterized by their fixed point components

\displaystyle U_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z,\hbar):=\left.\mathrm{Stab}^{Ell}_{\sigma}(\lambda)\right|_{\mu}

(64)

By their definition, the components
U_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z,\hbar)
are sections of a certain line bundle over the abelian variety
E^{\,3}=E_{z}\times E_{a}\times E_{\hbar}
where
E={\mathbb{C}}^{\times}/q^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
denotes the elliptic curve with modulus
q
. The parameters
a,z,\hbar
are viewed as coordinates on the factors.
For the Hilbert scheme
\mathsf{X}
the explicit formulas for
U_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z,\hbar)
in terms of the theta functions were computed in
[
33
]
.

Let us denote by
\succ
the standard dominance order on partitions. From the support condition, in the definition of the elliptic stable envelope classes
U_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z,\hbar)=0
if
\lambda\succ\mu
. In other words, the matrix
U_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z,\hbar)
is
upper triangular
if the fixed points are ordered by
\succ
.

The diagonal elements of this matrix are given explicitly by

U_{\lambda,\lambda}(a,z,\hbar)=\Theta(N^{-}_{\lambda}):=\prod\limits_{{w\in\textrm{char}_{\mathsf{T}}(T_{\lambda}\mathsf{X})}\atop{\langle\sigma,w\rangle<0}}\,\theta(w).

where
T_{\lambda}\mathsf{X}=N^{+}_{\lambda}\oplus N^{-}_{\lambda}
is the decomposition into repelling and attracting subspaces for
\sigma
, and the product is over the
\mathsf{T}
-weights of
N^{+}_{\lambda}
. Similarly, the elliptic stable envelopes for the opposite cocharacter are characterized by the
lower triangular
matrix

\displaystyle L_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z,\hbar):=\left.\mathrm{Stab}^{Ell}_{-\sigma}(\lambda)\right|_{\mu}

(65)

with
L_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z,\hbar)=0
if
\mu\succ\lambda
and

L_{\lambda,\lambda}(a,z,\hbar)=\Theta(N^{+}_{\lambda}):=\prod\limits_{{w\in\textrm{char}_{\mathsf{T}}(T_{\lambda}\mathsf{X})}\atop{\langle\sigma,w\rangle>0}}\,\theta(w).

5.3
Properties of the elliptic stable envelope

Let us consider the automorphism of the torus

\iota:\mathsf{T}\to\mathsf{T},\ \ \ \iota(t_{1},t_{2})=(t_{2},t_{1}).

and let
\iota^{*}
be the corresponding induced maps in equivariant K-theory or elliptic cohomology. On the classes of fixed points it acts by
\lambda\to\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}
(
\lambda^{\prime}
denotes the transposed partition). Note that
\iota(a)=a^{-1}
and thus the corresponding map of the Lie algebras maps the cocharacter
\sigma
to
-\sigma
. The uniqueness of the elliptic stable envelope classes thus implies that:

\iota^{*}(\mathrm{Stab}^{Ell}_{\sigma}(\lambda))=\mathrm{Stab}^{Ell}_{-\sigma}(\lambda^{\prime}).

In the fixed point components this gives:

Lemma 3
.

The matrices of stable envelopes (
64
) and (
65
) are related by

\displaystyle L(a,z,\hbar)=\mathbf{S}\ U(a^{-1},z,\hbar)\,\mathbf{S}

(66)

where
\mathbf{S}
is the antidiagonal matrix describing the transformation
\lambda\to\lambda^{\prime}
. In the basis of partitions ordered by
\succ
it has the form:

\mathbf{S}_{\lambda,\mu}:=\delta_{\lambda,\mu^{{}^{\prime}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\cdots&1\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
1&\cdots&0\end{array}\right).

Comparing the diagonal elements of (
66
) we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}\left.\Theta(N^{-})\right|_{a=1/a}\mathbf{S}=\Theta(N^{+})

(67)

The following property is also convenient for explicit computations:

Lemma 4
.

There are matrix identities

L^{t}(a,z^{-1}\hbar^{-1},\hbar)U(a,z,\hbar)=\Theta(T\mathsf{X})

where
\Theta(T\mathsf{X})
denotes the diagonal matrix with

\Theta(T\mathsf{X})_{\lambda,\lambda}=\Theta(T_{\lambda}\mathsf{X}).

Proof.

This identity is Proposition 3.4 in
[
2
]
applied to
\mathsf{X}
.
∎

In this and following sections we use
\,\tilde{\,}
to denote the normalized matrices of stable envelopes, defined by

\tilde{\bf{U}}_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z):=\dfrac{{{U}}_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z)}{{{U}}_{\mu,\mu}(a,z)},\ \ \ \tilde{{L}}_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z):=\dfrac{{{L}}_{\lambda,\mu}(a,z)}{{{L}}_{\mu,\mu}(a,z)},

so that
\tilde{\bf{U}}_{\lambda,\lambda}(a,z)=\tilde{\bf{L}}_{\lambda,\lambda}(a,z)=1

Note that the previous lemma implies that

\displaystyle\tilde{\bf{L}}^{t}(a,z^{-1}\hbar^{-1})\tilde{\bf{U}}(a,z)=Id.

5.6
Monodromy from the elliptic stable envelope

The one-leg vertex functions with a descendent

The vertex functions are certain generalizations of hypergeometric functions. In particular, they provide a basis to a certain system of

The functions

Let us consider the vertex functions of

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bf V}}_{\infty}(z)={\bf\Lambda}(z)\tilde{{\bf V}}_{0}(z)

\displaystyle{\bf\Lambda}(z)=(-1)^{n}\,{\bf{L}}(a^{-1},z^{-1}\hbar^{-1})^{-1}\,{\bf{U}}(a,z).

(75)

This is Corollary 3.2 in
[

]
applied to Hilbert scheme

. We only need to explain the sign

: in
[
25
]
the normalization of stable envelope is fixed by formula (45) in
[

]
.
It differs from the one accepted in this paper by

where

denotes the index of the fixed point
\lambda
corresponding to the chamber

The fundamental solutions

Let us normalize solutions of QDE by
