Reduced-Rank Regression with Operator Norm Error
Praneeth Kacham, David P. Woodruff

Abstract
A common data analysis task is the reduced-rank regression problem: min rank- ​ k ​ X ⁡ ‖ A ​ X − B ‖ , \min_{\textrm{rank-}k\ X}\|AX-B\|, where A ∈ ℝ n × c A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c} and B ∈ ℝ n × d B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d} are given large matrices and ∥ ⋅ ∥ \|\cdot\| is some norm. Here the unknown matrix X ∈ ℝ c × d X\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times d} is constrained to be of rank k k as it results in a significant parameter reduction of the solution when c c and d d are large. In the case of Frobenius norm error, there is a standard closed form solution to this problem and a fast algorithm to find a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximate solution. However, for the important case of operator norm error, no closed form solution is known and the fastest known algorithms take singular value decomposition time. We give the first randomized algorithms for this problem running in time ( nnz ​ ( A ) + nnz ​ ( B ) + c 2 ) ⋅ k / ε 1.5 + ( n + d ) ​ k 2 / ε + c ω , (\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+\textnormal{{nnz}}(B)+c^{2})\cdot k/\varepsilon^{1.5}+(n+d)k^{2}/\varepsilon+c^{\omega}, up to a polylogarithmic factor involving condition numbers, matrix dimensions, and dependence on 1 / ε 1/\varepsilon . Here nnz ​ ( M ) \textnormal{{nnz}}(M) denotes the number of nonzero entries of a matrix M M , and ω \omega is the exponent of matrix multiplication. As both (1) spectral low rank approximation ( A = B A=B ) and (2) linear system solving ( n = c n=c and d = 1 d=1 ) are special cases, our time cannot be improved by more than a 1 / ε 1/\varepsilon factor (up to polylogarithmic factors) without a major breakthrough in linear algebra. Interestingly, known techniques for low rank approximation, such as alternating minimization or sketch-and-solve, provably fail for this problem. Instead, our algorithm uses an existential characterization of a solution, together with Krylov methods, low degree polynomial approximation, and sketching-based preconditioning.

Introduction

Given an
n\times c
matrix
A
, an
n\times d
matrix
B
, and an integer parameter
k
, the reduced-rank regression problem asks to solve for a rank at most
k
matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times d}
for which

\|AX-B\|
is minimized in some norm. A standard motivation is that by
constraining
X
to have rank at most
k
, the solution
X
can be
represented using only
(c+d)k
parameters rather than
c\cdot d
parameters. Another important motivation is
that the rank constraint provides regularization on the solution, which often leads to better generalization.
Yet another motivation is that the solution
X
can be explained by at most
k
latent factors, and one can
try to interpret the latent factors, plot them
[
Braak and Looman 1994
]
, and so on. This is commonly done in ecology, where reduced-rank regression
is known as redundancy analysis
[
Legendre and Anderson 1999
]
, and is a type of ordination method
[
Kobak et al. 2019
]
. For a survey, we refer the reader to the textbook by
Velu and Reinsel 2013
devoted to reduced-rank regression.

The
\min_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\|AX-B\|
problem is only known to have a closed form solution when the error
measure is the Frobenius norm. In this case, the solution is given by

X=A^{+}[AA^{+}B]_{k}
(see, e.g.,
Friedland and Torokhti 2007
). Here for a matrix
M
,
[M]_{k}
denotes the best rank
k
approximation for
M
in Frobenius norm and
M^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse. This has a natural geometric interpretation - project each of the columns of
B
onto the column span of
A

and find the best rank-
k
approximation to the projected matrix. By the Pythagorean
theorem, one can show there is no loss in this approach, as the optimal cost decomposes into the sum of squared
distances of columns of
B
to the column span of
A
followed by the best rank-
k
approximation to the projected
matrix inside of the column span of
A
.

In a number of applications, the Frobenius norm is not the right measure. For example, in cancer genetics more robust versions are desired, and versions based on the sum of Euclidean lengths instead of the sum of squared
Euclidean lengths are sometimes used
[
She and Chen 2017
]
. Still, in other applications, the operator norm error solution may give a solution of much better quality. Indeed,
if
B
has a heavy tail of singular values, as is common for data analysis and
learning applications, then it has no good rank-
k
approximation,
much less one in the column span of
A
, and consequently, outputting an
X^{\prime}

with
\|AX^{\prime}-B\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|AX_{\mathsf{F}}-B\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
, where
X_{\mathsf{F}}
is the optimal
Frobenius norm solution, may be meaningless as one could just set
X^{\prime}=0
. Indeed,
this is sometimes a motivation (see, e.g.,
Musco and Musco 2015
) for the low rank approximation problem with operator norm error, which is a special case of our problem when
A=B
, and a number of works
[
Halko et al. 2011
;
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Karnin and Liberty 2015
;
Szlam et al. 2014
]
suggest considering operator norm error in certain contexts.

It is tempting to think that the Frobenius norm solution
holds also for other unitarily invariant norms, such as the operator
norm. However, one can show this is not the case.
Indeed, let
X_{\mathsf{F}}
be the solution to
\min_{\textrm{rank-}k\ X}\lVert AX-B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
. It was shown by
Boutsidis 2011
that this is a
\sqrt{2}
-approximation, namely, that
\|{AX_{\mathsf{F}}-B}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot\textnormal{Opt}
where
\textnormal{Opt}=\min_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\|{AX-B}\|_{2}
. Unfortunately, the
\sqrt{2}
factor is tight and there are instances where the Frobenius norm solution really does give at best a
\sqrt{2}
-approximation. Suppose, for example
1
1

1

We thank Ankur Moitra for pointing out this example to us.

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\text{ and }B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\\
0&1+\gamma\end{bmatrix}.

For the problem
\min_{\text{rank-}1\ X}\|AX-B\|_{\mathsf{F}}
, the optimum solution is

X_{\mathsf{F}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1+\gamma\end{bmatrix}\text{, with }AX_{\mathsf{F}}-B=-\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

and thus,
\|AX_{\mathsf{F}}-B\|_{2}=\sqrt{2}
. On the other hand, for

X=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\ AX-B=-\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\\
0&1+\gamma\end{bmatrix},

and so
\|AX-B\|_{2}=(1+\gamma)
. As
\gamma\rightarrow 0
, the approximation factor becomes arbitrarily close to
\sqrt{2}
.

We note that the reduced-rank regression problem in operator norm is non-convex in
X
due to the rank constraint, and it is not even clear this problem can be solved in polynomial time. Of the few techniques that are known for rank-constrained optimization, they do not apply here. One common method is alternating minimization, writing the problem above as
\min_{U,V}\|AUV-B\|_{2}
, where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
. The idea is to fix
U
, then solve for
V
, then fix
V
and solve for
U
, and repeat. When
U
is fixed, then
V=(AU)^{+}B
is the optimum, and when
V
is fixed, the solution turns out to be
U=A^{+}BV^{+}
, though this is not as obvious, see (1.3) in
Maher 2007
, taking
p\rightarrow\infty
, for a proof. It turns out if one initializes with the Frobenius norm solution
U,V
, then each of these operations does not change
U
or
V
, and so by the example above, alternating minimization gives at best a
\sqrt{2}
-approximation. Other techniques include sketching to a small problem,
and solving the small problem in the sketch space; sketches are well-known not to
apply to operator norm low rank approximation problems, motivating the first open
question in
Woodruff 2014
.

This issue of polynomial time solvability was raised in the control theory literature by
Sou and Rantzer 2012
, where a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation was obtained, but the the time required to find the solution was at least the time to perform a singular value decomposition (SVD) on matrices
A
and
B
, which is prohibitive for large
n,c,
and
d
. This is a common setting of parameters and indeed, one of the motivations for constraining
X
to have rank at most
k
in the first place. This motivates the question:

“
Are there fast algorithms for reduced-rank regression with operator norm error?
”

1.1
Main Result

We answer the question above by designing a new randomized algorithm running
in time

O\left(\left(\frac{\textnormal{{nnz}}(B)\cdot k}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)\cdot k}{\varepsilon^{1.5}}+\frac{c^{2}k}{\varepsilon^{1.5}}+\frac{(n+d)k^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\textnormal{polylog}(\kappa(B),n,d,k,1/\varepsilon)+c^{\omega}\right).

Here,
\kappa(B)
denotes
\sigma_{1}(B)/\sigma_{k+1}(B)
.
This significantly
improves over Sou and Rantzer’s polynomial time result, which takes
\Omega(nd^{2}+nc^{2})
time.

We note that spectral
low rank approximation is a special case in which
A=B
, and the best known upper
bound is
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)\cdot k/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
for this problem, up to logarithmic factors

Musco and Musco 2015
. A major open question in randomized numerical linear algebra
is to improve this bound (see, e.g., Open Question 1 of
Woodruff 2014
), or show
that it is not possible. We note that for
k=1
, in the matrix-vector query model,

\Omega(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
queries is known to be required if a slightly stronger guarantee
than spectral low rank approximation is desired, even for adaptive
algorithms
Braverman et al. 2020
;
Simchowitz et al. 2018
.
Another important point is that when
n=c
and
d=1
, this
is just the time to solve an arbitrary linear system, for which the best known
time is
c^{\omega}
. Improving either spectral low rank approximation or linear
system solving is a major open question, and barring that, our algorithm is optimal
up to a
1/\varepsilon
factor and polylogarithmic factors involving
matrix dimensions and condition numbers.

1.2
Our Techniques

Throughout the paper, let
\textnormal{Opt}:=\inf_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\|{AX-B}\|_{2}
,
\beta
be such that
(1+\varepsilon)\textnormal{Opt}\leq\beta\leq(1+2\varepsilon)\textnormal{Opt}
, and let
\Delta:=B^{\mathsf{T}}(I-AA^{+})B
. The work of
Sou and Rantzer 2012
shows that
X_{\beta}=A^{+}[AA^{+}B(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}]_{k}(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{1/2}
satisfies
\|{AX-B}\|_{2}<\beta
. For completeness, we give a short proof of this
fact in this paper. It is not a priori clear how to extract a solution from this expression,
while multiplying out all of the matrices, computing an inverse square root, and taking an SVD would take a prohibitive amount of time. This is essentially the algorithm
of
Sou and Rantzer 2012
.

We instead show that not only the best rank
k
approximation of the matrix
AA^{+}B(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
, but even a
1+\varepsilon
approximation in spectral norm yields an overall solution of cost at most
\beta(1+O(\varepsilon))
. To obtain such a
1+\varepsilon
approximation, we next try to apply the iterative method of

Musco and Musco 2015
which computes the Krylov matrix

K=[C\cdot G,(CC^{\mathsf{T}})\cdot C\cdot G,(CC^{\mathsf{T}})^{2}\cdot C\cdot G,\ldots,(CC^{\mathsf{T}})^{(q-1)/2}\cdot C\cdot G]

where
G
is a Gaussian matrix with
k
columns,
q=O(\log(d/\varepsilon)\sqrt{1/\varepsilon})
is an odd integer, and
C=AA^{+}B(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
.
The first problem with this approach is that we have to compute the matrix vector product
CG
and to do this, in each
iteration we need to (1) multiply by the square root of an inverse (multiplication by
(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2})
, and then (2) project onto the column span of
A
(multiplication by
AA^{+}
).

Computing exact matrix-vector products with the matrices
AA^{+}
and
(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
, is slow when
c,d
are large, and finding the matrices
AA^{+}
and
(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
takes at least
\Omega(nc^{2}+\textnormal{{nnz}}(B)\cdot c+d^{\omega})
time. To avoid such a running time, we show that the Block Krylov Iteration algorithm of
Musco and Musco 2015
works even with approximate matrix-vector products i.e., we only need algorithms to compute vectors
C\circ v
and
C^{\mathsf{T}}\circ v^{\prime}
for arbitrary vectors
v,v^{\prime}
such that
\|{C\circ v-Cv}\|_{2}
and
\|{C^{\mathsf{T}}\circ v^{\prime}-C^{\mathsf{T}}v^{\prime}}\|_{2}
are small. Here and throughout the paper, we use the notation
M\circ v
to denote an approximation to the matrix-vector product
Mv
.

An important idea of
Musco and Musco 2015
is that the Krylov matrix
K
spans a rank
k
matrix
p(C)G=\sum_{\text{odd\ }i\leq q}p_{i}(CC^{\mathsf{T}})^{(i-1)/2}G
, where
p
is a polynomial, such that projecting the columns of the matrix
C
onto the column span of
p(C)G
gives a good rank
k
approximation. To prove that the algorithm works even with approximate matrix-vector products, we first show that the approximations computed to matrices
(CC^{\mathsf{T}})^{(i-1)/2}CG
for
i=1,\ldots,q
are good enough to imply that the approximate Krylov matrix
K^{\prime}
spans a matrix
\mathit{Apx}
that is close to the matrix
p(C)G
in Frobenius norm. To then conclude that the column space of
\mathit{Apx}
is also a good subspace to project the matrix
C
onto, we need to show that
(\mathit{Apx})(\mathit{Apx})^{+}\approx(p(C)G)(p(C)G)^{+}
. We prove a simple lemma that shows if
\lVert p(C)G-\mathit{Apx}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
is small, and
p(C)G
has a good condition number, and so then
\|{(p(C)G)(p(C)G)^{+}-(\mathit{Apx})(\mathit{Apx})^{+}}\|_{2}
is small. Crucially, as
G
is a Gaussian matrix that has, with good probability a good condition number, we only have to bound
\sigma_{1}(p(C))/\sigma_{k}(p(C))
to obtain a bound on the condition number of
p(C)G
. Using several properties of Chebyshev polynomials used to define the polynomial
p(x)
, we show that
\sigma_{1}(p(C))/\sigma_{k}(p(C))
can be bounded in terms of
\kappa=\sigma_{1}(C)/\sigma_{k+1}(C)
, which finally shows that the
k
-dimensional column span of
\mathit{Apx}
is also a good subspace to project the columns of
C
.

As the parameters of the polynomial
p(x)
are unknown, we cannot actually compute the matrix
\mathit{Apx}
and then project
C
onto the column span. But using the fact that
K^{\prime}
spans
\mathit{Apx}
, we can conclude, similarly to the arguments of
Musco and Musco 2015
, that the best rank
k
Frobenius norm approximation of
C
in the span of
K^{\prime}
is a good rank
k
approximation to
C
. Using the oracle to compute approximate matrix-vector products with the matrix
C
, we recover a
1+\varepsilon
approximation to the best rank
k
Frobenius norm approximation of
C
inside the span of
K^{\prime}
, which we then show is a
1+\varepsilon
approximation to a spectral norm low rank approximation of matrix
C
. Our analysis that the Block Krylov Iteration algorithm works with approximate matrix-vector products could help justify why the Block Krylov Iteration algorithm works well when using finite precision arithmetic rather than exact arithmetic. Our results address the comments of
Musco et al. 2018
about the stability of block Lanczos based methods for problems such as low rank approximation. Though several analyses of the noisy power method have been done previously
[
Balcan et al. 2016
;
Hardt and Price 2014
;
Hardt and Roth 2013
]
, where each intermediate computation is corrupted by Gaussian noise, we are not aware of an analysis that works for worst case corruption. Also, previous work bounds the amount of Gaussian noise that can be added in terms of a gap between
\sigma_{k}
and
\sigma_{k+1}
, which can be
0
, and would not work for our analysis.

We return to the task at hand, i.e., of computing a low rank approximation of
AA^{+}B(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
. We show that we can replace the matrix
(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
with the matrix
(1/\beta){r}(\Delta/\beta^{2})
, where
{r}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, using polynomial approximation techniques based on Chebyshev polynomials (see, e.g.,
Sachdeva and Vishnoi 2014
and the references therein). Here we crucially use the fact that
(1+2\varepsilon)\textnormal{Opt}\geq\beta\geq(1+\varepsilon)\textnormal{Opt}\geq(1+\varepsilon)\|{(I-AA^{+})B}\|_{2}
to lower bound the minimum singular value of the matrix
(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)
, thereby obtaining an upper bound on the number of terms required to approximate
(I-(\Delta/\beta^{2}))^{-1/2}
with a Taylor series. Then we replace each monomial in the Taylor series with a low degree polynomial approximation to construct a polynomial
{r}(x)
. The replacement of
(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
with the matrix
{r}(\Delta/\beta^{2})
is done as we can give very fast algorithms to approximately multiply a vector with the matrix
{r}(\Delta/\beta^{2})
, as discussed below.

Let
\mathcal{M^{\prime}}=AA^{+}B\cdot{r}(\Delta/\beta^{2})
. Recall
\Delta=B^{\mathsf{T}}(I-AA^{+})B
. To approximate the matrix-vector product
\Delta u
for an arbitrary vector
u
, we need only approximate
B^{\mathsf{T}}AA^{+}Bu
, since
B^{\mathsf{T}}Bu
can be computed exactly in
\textnormal{{nnz}}(B)
time. For computing an approximation to
AA^{+}(Bu)
, we use fast sketching-based preconditioning methods for linear regression, which show given an arbitrary vector
b
and accuracy parameter
\varepsilon_{\textnormal{reg}}
how to find an
x
for which
\|{Ax-AA^{+}b}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\textnormal{reg}}\|{(I-AA^{+})b}\|_{2}
in time
O((\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+c^{2})\log(1/\varepsilon_{\textnormal{reg}})+c^{\omega})
, where
\omega\approx 2.376
is the exponent of matrix multiplication
[
Clarkson and Woodruff 2013
;
Meng and Mahoney 2013
;
Nelson and Nguyen 2013
]
. We note that we only need to pay the
c^{\omega}
time once
to compute a preconditioner, after which each regression problem takes
O((\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+c^{2})\log(1/\varepsilon_{\textnormal{reg}}))
time. This algorithm to approximately compute
\Delta u
for an arbitrary vector
u
is extended to approximate
{r}(\Delta/\beta^{2})\cdot v
for an arbitrary
v
. After approximating the product
{r}(\Delta/\beta^{2})\cdot v
with a vector
y
, we approximate the vector
AA^{+}By
again using the sketching-based preconditioning methods for linear regression.

Similarly we also give an algorithm to approximate
\mathcal{M^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{T}}v^{\prime}
for an arbitrary vector
v^{\prime}
. Thus, as discussed above, we can obtain using a Block Krylov algorithm, a matrix
Z
with orthonormal columns for which

\|{ZZ^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{M^{\prime}}-\mathcal{M^{\prime}}}\|_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sigma_{k+1}(\mathcal{M^{\prime}})

and then conclude that

\|{AA^{+}Z(AA^{+}Z)^{+}B-B}\|_{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\beta=(1+O(\varepsilon))\textnormal{Opt}

and that the rank
k
matrix
X=A^{+}Z(AA^{+}Z)^{+}B
is a
1+O(\varepsilon)
approximation for the problem
\min_{\text{rank-}k}\|{AX-B}\|_{2}
.

The time complexity of our algorithm depends logarithmically on
\kappa(B)=\sigma_{1}(B)/\sigma_{k+1}(B)
and
\kappa(AA^{+}B)=\sigma_{1}(AA^{+}B)/\sigma_{k+1}(AA^{+}B)
. We show that if
\widetilde{B}=B+\alpha GF^{\mathsf{T}}
where
G
is an
n\times(k+1)
random Gaussian matrix and
F^{\mathsf{T}}
has
k+1
orthonormal rows, then for a suitable value of
\alpha
, the condition number
\kappa(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})\leq(Cn/\varepsilon)\kappa(B)
for a constant
C
. We also show that a
1+\varepsilon
approximation for reduced rank regression computed using the matrix
\widetilde{B}
is a
1+O(\varepsilon)
approximation for reduced rank regression on matrix
B
, thus removing the dependence on
\kappa(AA^{+}B)
. Note that matrix-vector products with
\widetilde{B}
can be computed in
\textnormal{{nnz}}(B)+(n+d)k
time.

Our final dependence on
\varepsilon
in the running time is
1/\varepsilon^{3/2}
, ignoring polylogarithmic factors, where a factor of
1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is from the number of iterations in the Block Krylov Iteration algorithm of
Musco and Musco 2015
, a factor of
1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is from the degree of the polynomial
{r}(x)
, which is used as a proxy for the matrix
(\beta^{2}I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
with a matrix
{r}(\Delta/\beta^{2})
, and a factor of
1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is due to the running time of high-precision regression methods based on the accuracy with which the approximate matrix products need to be computed.

Notation and Preliminaries

For a matrix
M
,
\textnormal{{nnz}}(M)
denotes the number of nonzero entries in
M
. We refer to the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) with only nonzero singular values as the “thin” SVD. Given an arbitrary matrix
M
,
\text{colpsan}(M)
denotes the subspace spanned by the columns of
M
, and the matrix
M^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of matrix
M
. Given a subspace
V
, the matrix
\mathbb{P}_{V}
denotes the projection onto the subspace
V
. Therefore
\mathbb{P}_{V}u=\argmin_{v\in V}\|{u-v}\|_{2}
for all vectors
u
. Given a matrix
M
, we use
\mathbb{P}_{M}
to denote
\mathbb{P}_{\text{colspan}(M)}
.

For a matrix
M
, the Frobenius norm
(\sum_{i,j}M_{i,j}^{2})^{1/2}
is denoted by
\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
and the operator norm (or spectral norm)
\sup_{x}\|{Mx}\|_{2}/\|{x}\|_{2}
is denoted by
\|{M}\|_{2}
. For a square matrix
M
,
tr
(
M
) denotes the sum of diagonal entries. For matrices
M
and
M^{\prime}
of the same dimensions,
\langle M,M^{\prime}\rangle
denotes
\text{tr}(M^{\mathsf{T}}M^{\prime})=\sum_{i,j}M_{i,j}M^{\prime}_{i,j}
. We use the following standard facts repeatedly throughout the paper: for any matrix
M
, (1)
\|{M}\|_{2}\leq\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
, (2)
\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\sqrt{\text{rank}(M)}\|{M}\|_{2}
and (3)
\mathbb{P}_{M}=MM^{+}
. For any matrices
A,B
and
C
, (i)
\text{tr}(ABC)=\text{tr}(BCA)
, (ii)
\lVert ABC\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\|{A}\|_{2}\lVert B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\|{C}\|_{2}
and (iii)
\langle A,B\rangle\leq\lVert A\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\lVert B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
.

For a symmetric matrix
M
, define
\text{psd}(M)
to be the closest positive semi-definite matrix to
M
in Frobenius norm. It can be shown that if
M=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
, then
\text{psd}(M)=\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}\geq 0}\lambda_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Weyl’s Inequality.
For matrices
A
and
B
, Weyl’s inequality gives that

\sigma_{i+j-1}(A+B)\leq\sigma_{i}(A)+\sigma_{j}(B)

for all
i
and
j
. In particular, if
\|{A-B}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
,
|\sigma_{i}(A)-\sigma_{i}(B)|\leq\varepsilon
for all
i
.

Polynomials and Matrices.
Let
{p}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}p_{i}x^{i}
be a degree
d
polynomial. We define
\|{p}\|_{1}:=\sum_{i}|p_{i}|
to be the sum of absolute values of the coefficients of the polynomial
p(x)
. Given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, let
A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}
be the singular value decomposition of
A
with
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Define
{p}(A):=Up(\Sigma)V^{\mathsf{T}}
where
{p}(\Sigma)
is the matrix with main diagonal entries
{p}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,{p}(\sigma_{d})
. It is easy to check that the singular values of
{p}(A)
are equal to
|{p}(\sigma_{1})|,\ldots,|{p}(\sigma_{d})|
.

Singular Value Excess.
Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
n\geq d
be an arbitrary matrix. Let
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}\geq 0
be the singular values of matrix
A
. The Singular Value Excess of matrix
A
, denoted by
\text{sve}(A)
, is defined as the number of singular values of matrix
A
that are greater than or equal to
1
i.e.,

\text{sve}(A)=|\{i\in[d]\,|\,\sigma_{i}\geq 1\}|.

As eigenvalues of matrix
I-A^{\mathsf{T}}A
are
1-\sigma_{1}^{2}\leq\cdots\leq 1-\sigma_{d}^{2}
,
\text{sve}(A)

Sketching Based Preconditioning for High-Precision Regression.
Given a matrix

and a vector

, we use fast sketching based preconditioning methods given by the following theorem to obtain a

approximation to the problem

. See

Theorem 1

(High Precision Regression/Approximate Projections)
.

Given a matrix

and a vector

, we can compute a vector

in time

By the Pythagorean theorem, the vector

We have to pay

only once to compute a preconditioner. Thereafter, every regression problem can be solved in time

. Throughout the paper, we use

Low Rank Approximation(LRA).
Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
and

be its “thin” Singular Value Decomposition, where
U^{\mathsf{T}}U=I
,

with

where

denotes the

and

denotes the

-th row of matrix

. The matrix

optimally solves the problems

and

. As computing

exactly is expensive, we use the Block Krylov Iteration algorithm of

to obtain a matrix

for which

is a good solution to the Frobenius norm and spectral norm low rank approximation problems.

Theorem 2

(

)
.

Given a matrix

such that the products

and

can be computed in time

and returns a matrix

Frobenius Norm Reduced-Rank Regression.
As discussed in the introduction, there is a closed form solution to the reduced-rank Frobenius norm regression problem.

Lemma 3

(Lemma 4.1 of
Woodruff 2014
, Lemma 2 of
Musco and Musco 2015
)
.

Given matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
,

, and a rank parameter

, let matrix

denote an orthonormal basis for the column span of

\min_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\lVert AX-B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}=\lVert Q[Q^{\mathsf{T}}B]_{k}-B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}=\lVert[AA^{+}B]_{k}-B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}.

If
\bar{U}\bar{\Sigma}^{2}\bar{U}^{\mathsf{T}}
is the SVD of

, and

denotes the first

[Q^{\mathsf{T}}B]_{k}=\bar{U}_{k}\bar{U}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}Q^{\mathsf{T}}B

\min_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\lVert AX-B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}=\lVert Q[Q^{\mathsf{T}}B]_{k}-B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}=\lVert(Q\bar{U}_{k})(Q\bar{U}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}B-B\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}.

Chebyshev Polynomials.
The Chebyshev polynomials are defined as

\displaystyle T_{0}(x)=1,\ T_{1}(x)=x\ \text{and}\ T_{i}(x)=2xT_{i-1}(x)-T_{i-2}(x)

for all

. Thus

is a polynomial of degree

. It can be shown that if

has only odd degree monomials. Chebyshev polynomial

has the property that

for all

. See

for more properties of Chebyshev polynomials.

Previous work

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
be a matrix and
U\Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}
be the “thin” SVD of
A
, where
U
is an orthonormal basis for the column space of
A
. Note that the projection matrix onto the column space of

is given by

.
The first algorithm to solve
\min_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\|{AX-B}\|_{2}

was by
Sou and Rantzer 2012
. They consider the following problem:

minimize

\displaystyle\textnormal{rank}(X)

such that

\displaystyle\|{AX-B}\|_{2}<1.

(1)

As multiplying a matrix with a projection matrix does not increase the operator norm, we have that
\|{AX-B}\|_{2}\geq\|{(I-AA^{+})(AX-B)}\|_{2}=\|{(I-AA^{+})B}\|_{2}
. Thus the problem is feasible only when
\|{(I-AA^{+})B}\|_{2}=\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2}<1
. The following theorem characterizes the solution for (
1
).

Theorem 4

(
Sou and Rantzer 2012
)
.

Given matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
and a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, if there is a matrix
Y
such that
\|{AY-B}\|_{2}<1
, then the optimum value of (
1
) is sve(
B
) where sve(
B
) denotes the number of singular values of
B
that are greater than or equal to
1
.

For an arbitrary
s>0
, consider the problem (
1
) with matrices
A/s
and
B/s
. The problem is feasible if and only if
\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})(B/s)}\|_{2}<1
, i.e., if and only if
\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2}<s
. Suppose
s
is such that
s>\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2}
. Then Theorem
4
implies that there is a rank
k
matrix
X
such that
\|{(A/s)X-(B/s)}\|_{2}<1
if and only if

, i.e.,
\sigma_{k+1}(B/s)<1
. This argument shows that for any

, there is a rank
k
matrix
X
such that
\|{AX-B}\|_{2}<s
. Thus
\textnormal{Opt}=\max(\sigma_{k+1}(B),\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2})
.

It is interesting and perhaps surprising that the above theorem implies we can obtain a solution that has a value
\max(\sigma_{k+1}(B),\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2})
, which is a simple lower bound on the optimum. This shows that if
\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2}\leq\sigma_{k+1}(B)
, there is a rank
k
matrix in the column span of matrix
A
that is as good of an approximation to
B
in spectral norm as
[B]_{k}
. Also, if
\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{k+1}(B)
, then there is a rank-
k
matrix in the column space of
A
that is as good of an approximation to
B
in spectral norm as
AA^{+}B=UU^{\mathsf{T}}B
, the projection of

onto the column span of

.

We thus have the following corollary summarizing the discussion above. The corollary was also observed in
[see

in terms of a different parameter they call the critical rank.

Corollary 5
.

Given matrices

\inf_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\|{AX-B}\|_{2}=\max(\|{(I-AA^{+})B}\|_{2},\sigma_{k+1}(B)).

We give a proof of Theorem

for completeness in Appendix

A.1
Proof of Theorem
4

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we prove the theorem assuming
A
has orthonormal columns. Thus
U=A
. Let
X
be an arbitrary matrix such that
\|{UX-B}\|_{2}<1
. We will give a series of statements equivalent to
\|{UX-B}\|_{2}<1
that prove the theorem. Using the fact that for any matrix
A
,
\|{A}\|_{2}<1
if and only if
A^{\mathsf{T}}A\prec I
, we obtain the equivalent statement

(UX-B)^{\mathsf{T}}(UX-B)\prec I.

Writing
B
as
UU^{\mathsf{T}}B+(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B
, we get another equivalent statement

(UX-UU^{\mathsf{T}}B)^{\mathsf{T}}(UX-UU^{\mathsf{T}}B)\prec I-B^{\mathsf{T}}(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B=I-\Delta.

As the LHS of the above relation is a positive semi-definite matrix, we obtain that
I-\Delta\succ 0
and hence is invertible. Thus, the above condition can be equivalently written as

(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}(UX-UU^{\mathsf{T}}B)^{\mathsf{T}}(UX-UU^{\mathsf{T}}B)(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}\prec I.

Using the fact that
\Delta
is symmetric and
U^{\mathsf{T}}U=I
, we get that the above condition is the same as

\|{X(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}-U^{\mathsf{T}}B(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}}\|_{2}<1.

Thus, we obtain that in the case that
\|{(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})B}\|_{2}<1
, for an arbitrary matrix
X
, the condition that
\|{UX-B}\|_{2}<1
is equivalent to
\|{X(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}-U^{\mathsf{T}}B(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}}\|_{2}<1
. Let
\hat{B}:=U^{\mathsf{T}}B(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}
. It is easy to see that
X=[\hat{B}]_{\text{sve}(\hat{B})}(I-\Delta)^{1/2}
satisfies
\|{UX-B}\|_{2}<1
and that any matrix
X
that satisfies
\|{UX-B}\|_{2}<1
must have rank at least
\text{sve}(\hat{B})
. All that remains to show is that
\text{sve}(\hat{B})=\text{sve}(B)
. We will show that
k^{-}(I-\hat{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{B})=k^{-}(I-B^{\mathsf{T}}B)
, which completes the proof:

\displaystyle I-\hat{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{B}

\displaystyle=I-(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}B^{\mathsf{T}}UU^{\mathsf{T}}B(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=I-(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}(B^{\mathsf{T}}B-\Delta)(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=I-(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}(B^{\mathsf{T}}B-I+I-\Delta)(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=I-I+(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}(I-B^{\mathsf{T}}B)(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}(I-B^{\mathsf{T}}B)(I-\Delta)^{-1/2}.

Thus
k^{-}(I-\hat{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{B})=k^{-}((I-\Delta)^{-1/2}(I-B^{\mathsf{T}}B)(I-\Delta)^{-1/2})
. By Sylvester’s law of inertia
[
Carrell 2017
, p313]
,
k^{-}((I-\Delta)^{-1/2}(I-B^{\mathsf{T}}B)(I-\Delta)^{-1/2})=k^{-}(I-B^{\mathsf{T}}B)
. Therefore

\text{sve}(\hat{B})=k^{-}(I-\hat{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{B})=k^{-}(I-B^{\mathsf{T}}B)=\text{sve}(B).

Thus
\text{sve}(B)
is the optimum value for
(
1
)
if it is feasible.
∎

\displaystyle\leq\|{A}\|_{2}\|{A^{+}-B^{+}}\|_{2}+\frac{\|{\Delta}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}.

\displaystyle=\|{(A^{\mathsf{T}}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf{T}}-(B^{\mathsf{T}}B)^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{(A^{\mathsf{T}}A)^{-1}-(B^{\mathsf{T}}B)^{-1}}\|_{2}\|{A}\|_{2}+\frac{\|{\Delta}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\min}(B)^{2}}.

Given an arbitrary matrix

, if an orthonormal basis

to an
m
-dimensional subspace, where

is the largest integer such that

As

\displaystyle\|{M-Y^{m}(Y^{m})^{\mathsf{T}}M}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{M-M_{m}}\|_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\sigma_{k+1}(M)^{2}\leq\sigma_{m+1}(M)^{2}+\varepsilon\sigma_{k+1}(M)^{2}

\|{M-\hat{Y}^{m}(\hat{Y}^{m})^{\mathsf{T}}M}\|_{2}\leq(1+2\varepsilon)\sigma_{k+1}(M)+\sqrt{\delta}
.
∎

The above lemma shows that we need only compute a matrix

Polynomial Approximation of

We want to obtain a polynomial
p(x)
such that
|p(x)-(1-x)^{-1/2}|\leq\delta
in the interval

(1-x)^{-1/2}

(1-x)^{-1/2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2j)!}{2^{2j}j!^{2}}x^{j}.

The above series converges for all
|x|<1

q(x)
be the Taylor series up to
T

1>x\geq 0

0\leq q(x)\leq(1-x)^{-1/2}
and for
0\leq x\leq 1/(1+\varepsilon)

(1-x)^{-1/2}-q(x)=\sum_{j=T}^{\infty}\frac{(2j)!}{2^{2j}j!^{2}}x^{j}\leq\sum_{j=T}^{\infty}x^{j}=\frac{x^{T}}{1-x}\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)^{T}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)^{T-1}}.

Thus, if
T-1\geq 4\log(1/(\varepsilon\delta))/\varepsilon\geq\log(1/\varepsilon\delta)/\log(1+\varepsilon)

(1+\varepsilon)^{T-1}\geq 1/\varepsilon\delta
which implies that

0\leq(1-x)^{-1/2}-q(x)\leq\delta

for all
0\leq x\leq 1/(1+\varepsilon)

t=O(\log(1/\varepsilon\delta)/\varepsilon)

(1-x)^{-1/2}
up to an error
\delta

[0,1/(1+\varepsilon)]

t

First we have the following theorem.

Theorem 27

For any positive integers

\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|p_{s,d}(x)-x^{s}|\leq 2e^{-d^{2}/2s}.

Further, this polynomial

p_{s,d}(x)=\E_{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{s}}[T_{|D|}(x)\mathbb{I}[|D|\leq d]]

where

\mathbb{I}

Clearly the polynomial

\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|p_{s,d}(x)-x^{s}|\leq 2e^{{}^{-\log(1/\delta)}}\leq 2\delta.

Thus, given an arbitrary degree

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|\sum_{i=0}^{t}q_{i}x^{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{t}q_{i}p_{i,d}(x)|

\|{AX_{1}Y_{1}-B}\|_{2}=\|{ZZ^{\mathsf{T}}B-B}\|_{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\textnormal{Opt}.

Thus
X_{1}\cdot Y_{1}
is a
(1+O(\varepsilon))
-approximation to the regression problem. By appropriately scaling
\varepsilon
, we obtain the proof.
∎

D.5
Proof of Lemma
13

Proof.

Let
G\sim N(0,1)^{n\times(k+1)}
and
F^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times d}
be a matrix with
k+1
orthonormal rows. Let
\alpha
be a parameter to be chosen later and
\widetilde{B}:=B+\alpha GF^{\mathsf{T}}
. For all matrices
X
, by the triangle inequality,

\|{AX-\widetilde{B}}\|_{2}\in\|{AX-B}\|_{2}\pm\alpha\|{GF^{\mathsf{T}}}\|_{2}.

With probability
\geq 9/10
,
\|{G}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{n}
. Thus

\|{AX-\widetilde{B}}\|_{2}\in\|{AX-B}\|_{2}\pm 2\alpha\sqrt{n}.

Therefore, if
\widetilde{X}
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation to
\min_{\text{rank-}k\ X}\|{AX-\widetilde{B}}\|_{2}
, then
\|{A\widetilde{X}-B}\|_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textnormal{Opt}+6\alpha\sqrt{n}
.

We now have
\sigma_{1}(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})\leq\|{\widetilde{B}}\|_{2}\leq\|{B}\|_{2}+2\alpha\sqrt{n}
from the above discussion. We now lower bound
\sigma_{k+1}(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})
. Let
U
be an orthonormal basis for the columns of
A
. Therefore
AA^{+}=UU^{\mathsf{T}}
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k+1}(UU^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{B})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k+1}(U^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{B})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k+1}(U^{\mathsf{T}}B+\alpha U^{\mathsf{T}}GF^{\mathsf{T}})

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{k+1}(U^{\mathsf{T}}BFF^{\mathsf{T}}+\alpha U^{\mathsf{T}}GF^{\mathsf{T}})

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{k+1}(U^{\mathsf{T}}BF+\alpha U^{\mathsf{T}}G).

As the rows of
U^{\mathsf{T}}
are orthonormal, the matrix
G^{\prime}=U^{\mathsf{T}}G
is a matrix of i.i.d. normal random variables. Assuming
A
is of full rank,
G^{\prime}
is a
c\times(k+1)
matrix. Assuming
c\geq k+1
, let
E
be the top
(k+1)\times(k+1)
submatrix of
U^{\mathsf{T}}BF+\alpha G^{\prime}
. Then
E
can be seen as a fixed
(k+1)\times(k+1)
matrix where each entry is perturbed by a Gaussian random variable of variance
\alpha^{2}
. From Theorem 2.2 of
Vu and Tao 2007
, we obtain that
\sigma_{\min}(E)\geq\alpha/(C\sqrt{k})
for a constant
C
with probability
\geq 9/10
. Thus
\sigma_{k+1}(U^{\mathsf{T}}BF+\alpha U^{\mathsf{T}}G)\geq\sigma_{\min}(E)\geq\alpha/(C\sqrt{k})
.

Thus,
\sigma_{1}(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})/\sigma_{k+1}(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})\leq(\|{B}\|_{2}+2\alpha\sqrt{n})/(\alpha/(C\sqrt{k}))
. For
\alpha=\frac{\varepsilon\sigma_{k+1}(B)}{(6\sqrt{n})}
, we obtain that

\sigma_{1}(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})/\sigma_{k+1}(AA^{+}\widetilde{B})\leq\frac{Cn}{\varepsilon}\kappa

for a constant
C
with probability
\geq 4/5
. Also, if
\widetilde{X}
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation as mentioned above,
\|{A\widetilde{X}-B}\|_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textnormal{Opt}+\varepsilon\sigma_{k+1}(B)\leq(1+2\varepsilon)\textnormal{Opt}
. We obtain the proof by scaling
\varepsilon
appropriately.
∎
