Continuously tracked, stable, large excursion trajectories of dipolar coupled nuclear spins
Ozgur Sahin, Hawraa Al Asadi, Paul M. Schindler, Arjun Pillai, Erica Sanchez, Matthew Markham, Mark Elo, Maxwell McAllister, Emanuel Druga, Christoph Fleckenstein, Marin Bukov, Ashok Ajoy

Abstract
We report an experimental approach to excite, stabilize, and continuously track Bloch sphere orbits of dipolar-coupled nuclear spins in a solid. We demonstrate these results on a model system of hyperpolarized C 13 {}^{13}\textrm{C} nuclear spins in diamond. Without quantum control, inter-spin coupling leads to rapid spin decay in T 2 ∗ ≈ 1.5 T_{2}^{\ast}{\approx}1.5 ms. We elucidate a method to preserve trajectories for over T 2 ′ > 27 T_{2}^{\prime}{>}27 s at excursion solid angles up to 16 ∘ 16^{\circ} , even in the presence of strong inter-spin coupling. This exploits a novel spin driving strategy that thermalizes the spins to a long-lived dipolar many-body state, while driving them in highly stable orbits. We show that motion of the spins can be quasi-continuously tracked for over 35s in three dimensions on the Bloch sphere. In this time the spins complete > {>} 68,000 closed precession orbits, demonstrating high stability and robustness against error. We experimentally probe the transient approach to such rigid motion, and thereby show the ability to engineer highly stable “designer” spin trajectories. Our results suggest new ways to stabilize and interrogate strongly-coupled quantum systems through periodic driving and portend powerful applications of rigid spin orbits in quantum sensing.

I
Introduction

Periodically driven quantum systems have attracted enormous interest for their ability to host novel far-from-equilibrium phases of matter
Goldman and Dalibard 2014
;
Bukov et al. 2015
;
Eckardt 2017
;
Khemani et al. 2019
;
Else et al. 2020
, and for sustaining long-lived, highly stable states wherein absorption of energy can be controllably suppressed
Singh et al. 2019
;
Rubio-Abadal et al. 2020
;
Peng et al. 2021
. Consider, for instance, a network of dipolar coupled solid-state spins; their interaction drives rapid free induction decay of prepared transverse states in a very short time
T_{2}^{\ast}
. However, via Floquet prethermalization
Abanin et al. 2015
;
Mori et al. 2016
;
Beatrez et al. 2021
;
Peng et al. 2021
, periodically driving the spins can greatly extend these state lifetimes from
T_{2}^{\ast}
to
T_{2}^{\prime}
in a manner that parametrically increases with the driving frequency.

However, this approach remains restricted to specific initial states, typically those aligned parallel to the driving field. More generally applicable schemes for stabilization, especially along
arbitrary
axes, remain elusive. In this paper, we propose and demonstrate a strategy to excite and stabilize
closed spin orbits
on the Bloch sphere, including those that span large angular excursions away from the driving axis. Our approach exploits novel applications of the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH) and quantum thermalization
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
, combined with the engineering of a family of effective Hamiltonians
Beatrez et al. 2022
under the simultaneous action of two orthogonal and frequency-separated driving fields. Stable orbits are then excited within the
micromotion
dynamics between these Hamiltonians
Desbuquois et al. 2017
. In a model system of strongly interacting
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclear spins in diamond with an intrinsic
T_{2}^{\ast}{\approx}1.5
ms
Ajoy et al. 2019a
, we demonstrate the ability to stabilize highly tunable orbits with excursion angles
{>}16^{\circ}
for a lifetime
T_{2}^{\prime}{>}27
s. This corresponds to an increase in the spin lifetimes of over
18,000
-fold, even in the presence of strong inter-spin dipolar couplings.

This same method allows us to quasi-continuously track the resulting spin motion in three-dimensions on a Bloch sphere over long periods; here we demonstrate the quasi-continuous tracking of spins as they traverse
{>}
68,000 cycles of the engineered stabilized orbits. While such state tracking is difficult in many experimental scenarios
Murch et al. 2013
;
Zeiher et al. 2021
, our method makes it easily attainable by (1) exploiting weak coupling of the nuclear spins to a readout cavity in a manner that imposes no back-action upon them
Schuster et al. 2005
, (2) employing “hyperpolarization”, yielding considerable gains in measured signal to noise
Ajoy et al. 2018a
;
Ajoy et al. 2018b
, and (3) arranging a hierarchical,
{>}10^{5}
-fold separation of time scales between the rates of cavity-induced spin interrogation (signal sampling) and that of the spin orbiting. Ultimately, this rapid spin tracking unravels the emergence of stable spin orbits, including insights into the underlying dynamical thermalization processes that are key to their rigidity. Leveraging these special features, we demonstrate the ability to excite and track
“designer”
spin trajectories created via dynamically controlled Hamiltonians
Freeman 1998
.

Overall, this work greatly expands the toolbox for engineering and stabilizing long-range strongly interacting quantum systems, and via their continuous tracking, portends diverse applications in quantum sensing
Degen et al. 2017
and simulation
Weitenberg and Simonet 2021
. In particular, the rigidity and symmetry of motion induced on the spins suggests new ways to detect weak external fields in the spin environment.

II
Exciting and Stabilizing Spin trajectories

Our experimental system consists of hyperpolarized
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclear spins dipolar-coupled with the interaction Hamiltonian
Duer 2004
,

\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}{=}\sum_{n<m}b_{nm}(3I_{nz}I_{mz}-\textbf{I}_{n}\cdot\textbf{I}_{m})
, where
I_{n\alpha}
refer to spin-1/2 Pauli matrices of nuclear spin
n
, with
\alpha{=}\{x,y,z\}
. The coupling strength
b_{nm}{=}c_{\mathrm{exp}}[3\cos^{2}(\beta_{nm})-1]/r_{nm}^{3}
, with the constant
c_{\mathrm{exp}}{=}\mu_{0}\hbar\gamma_{n}^{2}/4\pi
, and angle
\beta_{nm}{=}\cos^{-1}(\bm{r}_{nm}\cdot\bm{B_{0}}/r_{nm}B_{0})
of the internuclear vector
\bm{r}_{nm}
in a magnetic field
B_{0}
. At natural abundance
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
concentration,
J{=}\left\langle b_{nm}\right\rangle{\approx}0.66
kHz
Ajoy et al. 2019a
;
Beatrez et al. 2021
, where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the median over internuclear vector positions. When prepared in a transverse state
\rho_{I}{=}I_{\nu}{=}\sum_{j}I_{j,\nu}
, where
\nu{=}\{x,y\}
, evolution under
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}
causes the spins to exhibit rapid decay in
T_{2}^{\ast}{\approx}1.5
ms
Beatrez et al. 2021
;
Sahin et al. 2022
. In addition to
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}
, the spins are also subject to time fluctuating local fields,
\mathcal{H}_{z}{=}\sum_{j}c_{j}(t)I_{jz}
, arising primarily from lattice paramagnetic impurities (P1 centers)
Ajoy et al. 2020a
.

One strategy to preserve spin polarization in such transverse states to long times is by engineering an effective Hamiltonian that conserves the magnetization along a
\hat{\bm{\nu}}
-axis to leading order
Ajoy et al. 2020a
. Such state protection can be understood within the framework of Floquet theory where the spin state is described by a prethermal ensemble under this effective Hamiltonian. In Ref.
Beatrez et al. 2021
, we demonstrated that the resulting transverse lifetimes can exceed
T_{2}^{\prime}{=}90.9
s (a ¿60,000-fold extension).

This approach, however, only preserves spins along a
single
transverse axis, henceforth labeled
\hat{\textbf{x}}
for convenience. Instead, as shown in Fig.
1
C, our goal here is to preserve spins in a closed trajectory of arbitrary size, quantified by excursion angle
\Phi
on the Bloch sphere away from
\hat{\textbf{x}}
. The protocol we employ is described in Fig.
1
B. Spins are subject to a
\tau
-periodic train of spin-locking (SL)
\vartheta
pulses
Rhim et al. 1973
;
Rhim et al. 1976
(of length
t_{\mathrm{pulse}}
and interpulse spacing
\tau
), while simultaneously being driven by a lower-frequency oscillating (AC) magnetic field along
\hat{\textbf{z}}
. Larmor precession of the spins is continuously monitored in windows (
t_{\textrm{acq}}{\sim}\tau/2
) between the pulses; the induction signal reports on the precession amplitude and phase in each window. Hyperpolarization, carried out by transferring spin polarization from optically pumped Nitrogen Vacancy (NV) centers
Fischer et al. 2013
;
Ajoy et al. 2018a
;
Ajoy et al. 2020b
, allows high signal-to-noise readout (SNR
{>}10^{3}
per window).

The resulting spin dynamics are most conveniently described by the rotating (dressed) frame Hamiltonian [cf. App.
G
],

\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{rot}}(t)=\mathcal{H}_{z}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}+\delta I_{z}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{SL}}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}(t)\,,

(1)

where the Hamiltonians corresponding to the orthogonal spin-locking (SL) and the continuous (AC) periodic drives are given by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{SL}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega\;\Theta(t)I_{x},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{\textrm{AC}}\cos(\omega_{\textrm{AC}}t+\phi_{\textrm{AC}})I_{z}\,.

(2)

Here,
\Omega
is the Rabi frequency. Time dependence of SL sequence is modeled by a
\tau
-periodic step-function
\Theta(t)
;
B_{\textrm{AC}}
and
\phi_{\textrm{AC}}
refer to the amplitude and phase of the AC field, respectively.

On-site random fields
\mathcal{H}_{z}
play an insignificant role in dictating the dynamics, and will be ignored for simplicity
Ajoy et al. 2020c
. Moreover,
\Omega{\gg}\{B_{\textrm{AC}},J\}
and hence, during the SL pulses, we can also ignore the AC-field-driven evolution and the dipolar dynamics induced by
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}
to a good approximation (see App.
G
). These assumptions allow us to capture the nonequilibrium dynamics in the simplified Hamiltonian,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rot}}(t)\approx\begin{cases}\delta I_{z}+\Omega I_{x},&t_{n}\leq t<t_{n}+t_{\mathrm{pulse}}\\
\delta I_{z}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}},&t_{n}+t_{\mathrm{pulse}}\leq t<t_{n+1}\end{cases}\,,

(3)

where
t_{n}{=}n\tau
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
(see Fig.
1
B).

In the following, we assume both drives to be commensurate, i.e., such that
N
-SL pulses are applied in the time the AC drive completes one period (see Fig.
3
A). This results in a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{rot}}(t)
, periodic with frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{AC}}
, and allows the use of Floquet theory
Shirley 1965
to analyze the evolution of the system over one AC period
T_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{AC}}
. In particular, we define the unitaries:

U_{F;k}=\exp\left(-iT_{\mathrm{AC}}\mathcal{H}_{F;k}\right),

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}_{F;k}
is a discrete family of stroboscopic Floquet Hamiltonians (
k{=}1,\cdots,N
), obtained by setting the beginning of the stroboscopic frame to be given by the
k{\textrm{th}}
SL pulse
Bukov et al. 2015
.

While theoretical analysis is made complex due to the simultaneous application of continuous and kicked drives, it is analytically feasible when
\vartheta
is close to
2\pi/N
,
i.e.

\vartheta{=}2\pi/N+\delta\vartheta
with
\delta\vartheta{\ll}1
(here
\delta
refers to the detuning of the SL pulses from resonance). We employ a Floquet-Magnus expansion
Magnus 1954
in the SL toggling frame over one AC drive period, to expand
\mathcal{H}_{F;k}
in the inverse frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{AC}}^{-1}
. In the high-frequency regime
\omega_{\mathrm{AC}}{\gg}\{J,\Omega,B_{\mathrm{AC}},\delta\}
, the dynamics are governed by the time-independent effective Hamiltonian (see App.
H
)

\frac{t_{\mathrm{acq}}}{\tau}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;k}^{(0)}=\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}(\alpha,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}})\cdot\bm{I}+(1-3\sin^{2}\alpha)\sum_{n<m}b_{nm}\left(\frac{3}{2}\tilde{\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{ff}}}-\bm{I}_{n}\cdot\bm{I}_{m}\right),

(5)

with the flip-flop term
\tilde{\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{ff}}}{=}I_{jz}I_{kz}+I_{jy}I_{ky}
, and
\alpha(\vartheta){=}\arctan(\delta/\Omega)
(here the tildes denote operators transformed under the change of basis mapping
\left(\cos(\alpha)I_{x}+\sin(\alpha)I_{z}\right)\to I_{x}
, see App.
H
). There is an emergence of a new effective static field

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}(\alpha,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\delta\sin\alpha+\delta\vartheta/\tau\right)\;\hat{\textbf{x}}

(6)

\displaystyle+B_{\mathrm{AC}}\cos\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(-\sin n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{y}}+\cos n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{z}}\right)f_{n+k}(\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}),

which changes direction conditioned on
k
, since
f_{n+k}(\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}){=}\int_{t_{n}+t_{\mathrm{pulse}}}^{t_{n+1}}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T_{\mathrm{AC}}}+\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t_{\textrm{acq}}}
. Importantly, however, the interaction term in Eq. (
5
) remains independent of the stroboscopic frame index
k
.

Eq. (
5
) is a key result of this work.
\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}
elucidates
micromotion
of the spins as they are successively kicked by the SL pulses. Within the duration of the parametrically long-lived prethermal plateau, the spins thermalize under the non-integrable Hamiltonian
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;k}^{(0)}
.
Hence, we anticipate formation of a prethermal state effectively captured by density matrix
\tilde{\rho}_{F}{\propto}\exp\left(-\beta\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;k}^{(0)}\right)
with the inverse temperature
\beta
set by the energy density of the initial state, in a similar manner to ETH
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
. It is straightforward to see that whenever the detuning
\delta{\neq}0
(
\delta\vartheta{\neq}0
), the initial state
\tilde{\rho}_{I}{\propto}I_{x}
has a small but non-vanishing energy density,
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{I}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;k}^{(0)}\right){\propto}\left(\delta+\delta\vartheta/\tau\right)
, which leads to a finite temperature
\beta{>}0
in the prethermal plateau. Hence, for
{\beta J{\ll}1}
, we have

From Eq. (

III
Continuous spin tracking on the Bloch sphere

An important practical goal of this paper is to be able to continuously
track
the rotating-frame spin trajectories in three dimensions on the Bloch sphere. The principle we employ is elucidated in App.
A
. Typically, spin trajectory tracking is challenging because it requires: (1) the ability to interrogate the spins without perturbing them, and (2) that state interrogation can occur simultaneously with the control field
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}
. To make such tracking viable, we employ a series of special features in our system:

(i)
weak spin-cavity coupling that permits non-destructive measurements via RF induction,

(ii)
a hierarchical
{>}10^{5}
-fold time-scale separation between the rate at which the induction signal is sampled and cyclic orbital motion (see Table
1
), and
(iii)
hyperpolarization, which yields high single-shot readout SNR.

App.
B

Since the spins undergo very little decay during successive cavity interrogation windows,
(\omega^{s}T_{2}^{\prime})^{-1}{\ll}1

VII
Conclusions and Outlook

This work has great potential to be extended in diverse directions, both fundamental and applied. The ability to stabilize and continuously track spin orbits in interacting spin ensembles opens avenues to use them in quantum sensing, such as in magnetometry
Boss et al. 2017
;
Schmitt et al. 2017
, gyroscopes
Ajoy and Cappellaro 2012
;
Ledbetter et al. 2012
;
Jaskula et al. 2019
;
Jarmola et al. 2021

Sahin et al. 2021
, and dark matter searches
Budker et al. 2014
. Our method may make

Barry et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2020
. Beyond high-field magnetometry with
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclear spins
Sahin et al. 2021
, we anticipate that this approach can be extended to NV center ensembles
Wolf et al. 2015
and polar molecules
Micheli et al. 2006
where analogous driving schemes may be implemented. Quantum sensing may benefit even further from this control scheme because the symmetry imposed on the spin orbits (Fig.
3

frequencies of interest over noise and other frequencies, which do not produce any symmetry of the spin motion. Finally, spin orbits under a gradient may serve as an encoding strategy for hyperpolarized MR imaging
Lv et al. 2021

While our experiments have focused on hyperpolarized

nuclear spins in diamond, organic molecules which host triplet photoexcitable electrons

Tateishi et al. 2014

Negoro et al. 2018

More fundamentally, the experiments here provide a new way to interrogate thermalization dynamics in interacting quantum systems

Finally, the principle for long-time quasi-continuous spin tracking developed here is generally applicable to wide classes of control fields

App. A
Principle of Continuous Spin Trajectory Tracking

In this section we describe the principle of spin tracking employed in this paper; schematically shown in Fig.
10
A. Indeed, considering the protocol in Fig.
10
B, the pulsed SL pulses
dress
the spins such that the energy gap
\omega{=}\eta\Omega
, where
\Omega
is the Rabi frequency, and
\eta{=}t_{\textrm{pulse}}/\tau
is the pulsing duty cycle. Micromotion under the action of the control field
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}(t)
results in spin evolution in a rotating (dressed frame) trajectory. In our experiments, this rotating-frame spin trajectory is “sampled” quasi-continuously at a rate
\omega^{s}/{2\pi}{=}\tau^{-1}
(green points). Practically, this is accomplished by interrogating the spins via an RF cavity by allowing them to precess in the lab frame (orange circle) at Larmor frequency
\omega_{L}
in
t_{\textrm{acq}}({\sim}\tau/2)
windows between the SL pulses. Crucially, cavity interrogation is arranged to be very rapid: if the rotating-frame trajectory is sampled at rate
\omega^{s}
, then for each such sample the cavity readout is carried out multiple times, sampled at a rate
\omega_{L}^{s}
(orange points) such that
\omega_{L}^{s}/\omega^{s}{>}10^{4}
. Our strategy therefore revolves around ensuring a hierarchal time-scale separation (see Table
1
) such that,
\omega_{L}^{s}{\gg}\omega_{L}{\gg}\omega^{s}{>}\omega{>}B_{\textrm{AC}}
. This leverages the relatively low
\omega_{L}
Larmor frequencies of the nuclear spins, and their ability to be driven for long periods (
T_{2}^{\prime}
), but interrogated rapidly (at
\omega_{L}^{s}
). Table
1
shows typical values as employed in experiment; the orders of magnitude overall time-scale separation proves the key to
quasi-continuous
state tracking.

To see this more clearly, consider some comparisons in Table
1
. First, the wide separation
\omega_{L}/\omega{\gg}1
, allows the applied AC fields at
\omega_{\textrm{AC}}({\approx}\omega
) to be filtered from the cavity response (
\omega_{L}
). This permits RF spin interrogation to proceed simultaneously with the application of the AC field
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}(t)
. Second, the hierarchy
\omega^{s}{\ll}\omega_{L}
means that cavity interrogation can be carried out for multiple Larmor cycles for each sampled point in the rotating-frame spin trajectory (Fig.
10
A). This yields a high readout signal-to-noise (SNR) per sampled point on the trajectory, and
\omega^{s}{\gg}(T_{2}^{\prime})^{-1}
means that the tracking is for most practical purposes, quasi-continuous. Finally, the large ratio
\omega_{L}^{s}/\omega_{L}
means it is possible to carry out exquisite filtration of the cavity response, suppressing all components except those exactly at
\omega_{L}
, also yielding SNR gains.

App. D
Robustness of trajectories to amplitude distortion

A remarkable feature of the spin orbits is that they are robust to the exact functional form of the AC field. Indeed, it is possible to obtain orbits similar to Fig.
3
C even when the AC field is significantly distorted from a sinusoid. An immediate consequence of this resilience is that amplitude fluctuations (noise) in the AC field have very little effect to the obtained spin orbits.

To illustrate this, in Fig.
13
we carry out experiments with an exemplary AC field that is constructed by combining two half-period sinusoids with a five-fold amplitude ratio (schematically shown in Fig.
13
A).
\vartheta{=}\pi/2
SL pulses are employed here, and the overall period of the AC field is resonant: one full AC field period matches that of four SL pulses (
\omega_{\textrm{AC}}{=}2\pi/(4\tau)
). We then carry out spin tracking on the resulting orbits; as the projections in Fig.
13
B indicate, the obtained trajectories continue to closely resemble a “square”, as expected for a pure undistorted sinusoid as in Fig.
3
C(i). The obtained trajectory also remains equivalently rigid, as evident in tracked data in Fig.
13
C.

The origin of this robustness can be seen from Eq. (
6
) where the vector family
\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}
is constructed out of functions
f_{n}(\phi_{\textrm{AC}})
) that are evaluated as an integral over the
entire
period
T_{\textrm{period}}
of the AC field, and not on the instantaneous value of the amplitude
B_{\textrm{AC}}(t)
in-between the pulses (see also App.
H
). Hence, a distortion in
B_{\textrm{AC}}(t)
only affects the area enclosed, but not the orbital shape itself (see also App.
E
).

App. E
Robustness of trajectories to SL flip angle error

In this Appendix, we demonstrate that the “resonance condition” as employed in the main paper can be very relaxed, offering a high degree of robustness with respect to the choice of the SL flip angle

Sahin et al. 2022

\vartheta{=}\{107^{\circ},124^{\circ},130^{\circ}\}

\omega_{\textrm{AC}}{=}\omega_{\textrm{res}}{=}2\pi/(N\tau)

App. G
Theoretical model and rotating frame Hamiltonian

In this section, we present a derivation of the rotating frame Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
). Consider first that the lab frame Hamiltonian of the interacting system of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclear spins is given by,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{lab}}\left(t\right)=\omega_{L}I_{z}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}+\mathcal{H}_{z}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{AC}}\left(t\right)+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{SL},\,\mathrm{lab}}\left(t\right)\,

(7)

with the Larmor frequency
\omega_{L}{=}\gamma_{n}B_{0}
, where
\gamma_{n}
is the magnetogyric ratio and
B_{0}{=}7
T is the bias magnetic field. Eq. (
7
) also includes the dipole-dipole interaction
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}
, the time-fluctuating on-site fields
\mathcal{H}_{z}
, and the AC drive
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{AC}}
, as defined in Eq. (
2
). Now, in the lab frame, the pulsed spin-locking (SL) drive can be described by the Hamiltonian,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{SL},\,\mathrm{lab}}\left(t\right)=\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{SL}}t)\Omega\Theta\left(t\right)I_{x}\,,

(8)

where we assume the carrier frequency is applied close to the Lamor frequency resonance,
\omega_{\mathrm{SL}}=\omega_{L}+\delta
, with the small detuning given by
\delta
(with
\delta/\omega_{L}{\ll}1
).

As is typical at high field, the Larmor frequency
\omega_{L}
exceeds all other energy scales of the Hamiltonian Eq. (
7
) by at least three orders of magnitude (see Table
1
). We can remove this large energy scale by changing to a rotating frame with respect to
W\left(t\right)=\exp(-i(\omega_{\mathrm{SL}}t+\phi)I_{z})
.
In principle, a generic transformation to the rotating frame can also have a static (i.e., change-of-basis) contribution, which introduces an offset in the experimentally observed phase
\phi
[for more details see SI
F
]; for simplicity, we will choose
\phi{=}0
hereinafter.
Note that the transformation
W(t)
also affects
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{SL},\,\mathrm{lab}}
which is transformed to
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{SL}}=\Omega\Theta\left(t\right)I_{x}
upon performing a rotating wave approximation.
Taking all these considerations into account, it is straightforward to arrive at the rotating frame Hamiltonian as specified in Eq. (
1
),

\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{rot}}(t)=\mathcal{H}_{z}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}+\delta I_{z}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{SL}}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}(t)\,.

We now specify the assumptions we will employ to aid the theoretical analysis. First, the on-site random fields
\mathcal{H}_{z}
play an insignificant role for the dynamics of the system, and as such we will ignore them. Second, while the detuning
\delta
is small compared to the Larmor frequency
\omega_{L}
, we emphasize that it can be comparable to, or larger than, the remaining energy scales in the Hamiltonian, i.e., our experiments can be carried out even in the regime where
\delta{\gtrsim}J
. In fact, a finite detuning is crucial to theoretically capture the experimentally observed tilt of the spin orbits away from the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
axis and towards the
\hat{\textbf{y}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{z}}
plane, see App.
H
and Ref.
Sahin et al. 2022
. Finally, an important hierarchy of energy scales in the rotating frame Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
) is that the SL Rabi frequency
\Omega
exceeds all other scales by at least an order of magnitude:
\Omega{\gg}J{\approx}\left|\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}\right|
. Therefore, it is sufficient to keep only the contribution of the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-kicks and detuning
\delta
during the SL pulses. These approximations lead directly to the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
3
).

App. H
Derivation of the leading-order Floquet Hamiltonian

In the following, we derive the family of Floquet Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}
, given in Eq. (
5
) of the main text, starting from the simplified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{rot}}(t)
in Eq. (
3
) and employing the assumptions elucidated above (see App.
G
).

Our discussion will be based on the sequence in Fig.
16
A for clarity. In general, there exist
N
distinct unitaries
U_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+j}
(labelled by index
k
,
k{=}1,\,{\cdots},\,N
) corresponding to the different instants
t_{k}{=}T_{\mathrm{AC}}k/N
that we consider to represent the start of a period. The Floquet unitary describing the time evolution over one AC period
T_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{AC}}
can be written as

\displaystyle U_{F;\,k}

\displaystyle=U_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+N}U_{\mathrm{SL}}\dots U_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+2}U_{\mathrm{SL}}U_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+1}U_{\mathrm{SL}}

(9)

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{N}U_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+j}U_{\mathrm{SL}},

where
U_{\mathrm{acq},\,j}\!=\!\exp(-it_{\mathrm{acq}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{acq}})
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{acq}}\!=\!\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{dd}}+\delta\,I_{z}+f_{j}(\phi_{\mathrm{AC}})I_{z}
;
f_{n}(\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}){=}\int_{t_{n}+t_{\mathrm{pulse}}}^{t_{n+1}}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T_{\mathrm{AC}}}+\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right)\mathrm{d}t/{t_{\mathrm{acq}}}
is the effective phase developed due to evolution between two successive SL pulses. This is highlighted in Fig.
16
. The unitary operator modeling the action of the SL pulses can be written as,

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{SL}}

\displaystyle=\exp(-it_{\mathrm{pulse}}\left[\Omega I_{x}+\delta I_{z}\right])

(10)

\displaystyle=\exp(-i\vartheta\left[\cos(\alpha)I_{x}+\sin(\alpha)I_{z}\right])\,,

where we identify
\vartheta{=}t_{\mathrm{pulse}}\sqrt{\Omega^{2}+\delta^{2}}
as the total angle acquired during a single pulse, and
\alpha{=}\arctan(\delta/\Omega)
represents the deviation of the pulse direction from the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
axis.

In order to simplify the analysis, we consider a static change of basis which maps
\left(\cos(\alpha)I_{x}+\sin(\alpha)I_{z}\right)\to I_{x}
. In effect, this allows us to ignore the trivial tilt of the spin orbits away from the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
axis due to the SL pulse detuning, and introduce it back later. The basis change here amounts to a global static rotation about the
\hat{\textbf{y}}
-axis by an angle
\alpha{=}\arctan(\delta/\Omega)
, and can be described by the unitary
V{=}\exp(-i\alpha I_{y})
. In what follows, we will denote operators transformed in this basis by a tilde.

We will also consider the experimentally relevant scenario (see Fig.
3
) where each of the SL pulses accumulates a flip angle
\vartheta={}2\pi/N+\delta\vartheta
, with
\delta\vartheta\ll 1
(see also Ref.
Sahin et al. 2022

G
), such that their combined rotation adds up to (almost) unity over one full AC period. To account for the deviation away from
2\pi/N
, we split up the action of the SL pulse in two parts,
\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}{=}\exp(-i\delta\vartheta I_{x})\exp(-i2\pi/NI_{x})
. We include the first part into
\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k}
, i.e.,
\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k}\to\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k}^{\prime}{=}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k}\exp(-i\delta\vartheta I_{x})
, and isolate the commensurate kicks
\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}\to\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{\prime}{=}\exp(-i2\pi/NI_{x})
.
For readability we will drop the prime superscript on the unitaries.

In order to describe the time evolution over stroboscopic times
nT_{\mathrm{AC}}+t_{k}
, where
t_{k}
is fixed, we make use of Floquet’s theorem; it states that the stroboscopic evolution is generated by a time-independent Floquet Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{F;\,k}
defined via,

U_{F;\,k}=\exp(-iT_{\mathrm{AC}}\mathcal{H}_{F;\,k}),

(11)

where the index
k
explicitly indicates that the Floquet Hamiltonian depends on the time instant that defines the start of the sequence,
t_{k}{=}k/NT_{\mathrm{AC}}
.
However, as our model in Eq. (
3
) is both non-integrable and long-range interacting, solving for the exact Floquet Hamiltonian is intractable. Instead, we make use of the high-frequency expansion
\mathcal{H}_{F;\,k}{=}\sum_{n}\omega_{\mathrm{AC}}^{-n}\mathcal{H}_{F;\,k}^{(n)}
.
Using the Floquet-Magnus Expansion, or Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula
Eckardt and Anisimovas 2015
, we can combine the unitaries in Eq. (
9
) into one unitary
\exp(-i\sum_{j}t_{\mathrm{acq}}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+j}+2\pi/NI_{x})
, up to an error of order
\mathcal{O}(t_{\mathrm{acq}}J,\,2\pi/N)
, where
J
is the energy scale of the Hamiltonian.

Since the kick angle
2\pi/N
is large (
\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
), also terms higher order in
2\pi/N
(i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(t_{\mathrm{acq}J,\,j}|,\,(2\pi/N)^{n})
), contribute to the leading order expression for
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}
in
T_{\mathrm{AC}}
.
Instead, we can account for contributions of the SL pulse to the Floquet Hamiltonian exactly, by writing Eq. (
9
) in the toggling frame as,

\tilde{U}_{F;\,k}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+j}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}=\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{-j}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+j}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{j}\,,

(12)

which is obtained by introducing
N
identities,
\mathbf{1}{=}U_{\mathrm{SL}}^{-j}U_{\mathrm{SL}}^{j}
.

This leads to an average Hamiltonian, which is valid up to
\mathcal{O}(T_{\mathrm{AC}})
-corrections, and reads as

\displaystyle\frac{t_{\mathrm{acq}}}{\tau}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{-j}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{acq},\,k+j}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{j}

(13)

\displaystyle=\!\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}(\alpha,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}})\cdot\bm{I}+(1\!-\!3\sin^{2}\alpha)\!\sum_{n<m}\!b_{nm}\left(\frac{3}{2}\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{ff}}-\bm{I}_{n}\cdot\bm{I}_{m}\right)\,,

with the flip-flop term
\tilde{\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{ff}}}{=}I_{jz}I_{kz}+I_{jy}I_{ky}
, and where,

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}\left(\alpha,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sin(\alpha)\delta+\frac{\delta\vartheta}{\tau}\right)\hat{\textbf{x}}+\tilde{u}_{k}\left(\alpha,\,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right).

(14)

with,

\tilde{\bm{u}}_{k}(\alpha,\,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}})=B_{\mathrm{AC}}\cos\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(-\sin n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{y}}+\cos n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{z}}\right)f_{n+k}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right).

(15)

A priori, it is unclear whether the sum in Eq. (
15
) would yield a nonzero number, since both terms under the sum vanish individually, i.e.
\sum_{n}f_{n}{=}0
and
\sum_{n=1}\left(-\sin n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{y}}+\cos n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{z}}\right){=}0
.
However, for the special case
\vartheta\approx 2\pi/N
they interfere constructively to yield a finite contribution.

To describe this more clearly, let us consider the exemplary case of
N{=}4
, sketched in Fig.
16
, for
\vartheta{\approx}\pi/2
and
\vartheta{\approx}\pi
. Both cases are sketched in Fig.
16
C (i), with the colored and black vectors respectively. First, we note that by symmetry, and regardless of
\vartheta
,
f_{1}{=}-f_{3}
and
f_{2}{=}-f_{4}
, due to the periodicity of the sine function (see Fig.
16
A). Let us first consider the case of
\vartheta{\approx}\pi/2
. Defining the vectors
\bm{\nu}_{n}\left(\vartheta\right){=}\left(-\sin n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{y}}+\cos n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{z}}\right)
, we then have the quantities,

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{1,\,3}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nu}_{1,\,3}\left(\pi/2\right)=\pm\hat{\textbf{y}}

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{2,\,4}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nu}_{2,\,4}\left(\pi/2\right)=\mp\hat{\textbf{z}}\>,

and following Eq. (
15
), the emergent field is then given by

\tilde{\bm{u}}_{k}(\alpha,\,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}){=}B_{\mathrm{AC}}\cos\alpha\sum_{n}f_{n+k}(\phi_{\textrm{AC}})\bm{a}_{n}
. Now, the two pairs of weights cancel each other out, i.e.,
f_{1}{=}-f_{3}
and
f_{2}{=}-f_{4}
, and are associated with anti-parallel vectors, see Fig.
16
B. This leads to an enhancement instead of a cancellation, and hence
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{k=0}(\vartheta=\pi/2)=2f_{1}\hat{\textbf{y}}-2f_{2}\hat{\textbf{z}}
(see Fig.
16
C (i)). The other vectors
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{k}
(
k=1,2,3
) are simply related to the first one by micromotion-induced rotation about the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-axis.

On the other hand however, for the case of
\vartheta{\approx}\pi
, we have,

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{1,\,3}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nu}_{1,\,3}\left(\pi\right)=\hat{\textbf{z}}

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{2,\,4}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nu}_{2,\,4}\left(\pi\right)=-\hat{\textbf{z}},

In this case, the interference is destructive yielding
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{k=0}\left(\vartheta=\pi\right){=}(f_{1}+f_{3}-f_{2}-f_{4})\hat{\textbf{z}}=\bm{0}
(see black vector in Fig.
16
C (i)).

From Eq. (
13
) it follows directly that the
N
different stroboscopic Floquet Hamiltonians are related by a rotation about the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-axis:
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k^{\prime}}^{(0)}{=}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{k^{\prime}-k}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{SL}}^{k-k^{\prime}}
.
Notice that the leading order Floquet Hamiltonians
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}
only differ in their single particle terms
\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}(\alpha,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}})\cdot\bm{I}
.
Moreover, in the absence of an AC drive, the Hamiltonians
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}\left(B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0\right)
become
k
-independent and are invariant under global rotations about the
x
-axis, i.e.,
\left[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}\left(B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0\right),\,I_{x}\right]{=}0
.
To sum up, introducing a finite AC drive leads to non-trivial emergent fields, but it does not affect the dipole-dipole interaction term, to lowest order in
T_{\mathrm{AC}}
.
We emphasize that these arguments still hold true after undoing the basis transformation
V
; however, the conserved axis (for
B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0
) is rotated into
\hat{\textbf{n}}{=}\cos(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{x}}{+}\sin(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{z}}
.

These expressions are derived in the simplified tilde basis that does not account for the tilt of the trajectories away from the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
axis on account of SL pulse detuning. In order now to write down the average Hamiltonian in the original rotating frame, we carry out the transformation
\mathcal{H}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}=V^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}V
, resulting in,

\frac{t_{\mathrm{acq}}}{\tau}{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}=\bm{w}_{k}(\alpha,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}})\cdot\bm{I}+(1-3\sin^{2}\alpha)\sum_{n<m}b_{nm}\left(\frac{3}{2}H_{\mathrm{ff}}-\bm{I}_{n}\cdot\bm{I}_{m}\right)\,,

(16)

with the transformed flip-flop Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{ff}}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}(\alpha)I_{jz}I_{kz}+\sin^{2}(\alpha)I_{jx}I_{kx}+I_{jy}I_{ky}

(17)

\displaystyle-\cos(\alpha)\sin(\alpha)\left(I_{jx}I_{kz}+I_{jz}I_{kx}\right),

and the single particle contributions

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{k}\left(\alpha,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}\left(\alpha\right)+\bm{u}_{k}\left(\alpha,\,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right)\,

(18)

where,

\displaystyle\bm{v}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sin(\alpha)\delta+\frac{\delta\vartheta}{\tau}\right)\left(\cos(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{x}}+\sin(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{z}}\right),

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{k}\left(\alpha,\,\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right)

\displaystyle=B_{\mathrm{AC}}\cos\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[-\sin n\vartheta\;\hat{\textbf{y}}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\cos n\vartheta\;\left(\cos(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{z}}-\sin(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{x}}\right)\right]f_{n+k}\left(\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}\right)

to leading order in the AC period
T_{\mathrm{AC}}
.

Since undoing the basis transformation
V
hides the simplicity of the result and undoing the transformation is straightforward, we will hereinafter carry out most of the analysis in the transformed frame and only undo the transformation for the final expressions.

We emphasize that, due to the finite detuning (
\delta{\neq}0
), the vectors
\bm{w}_{k}
are not centered around the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-axis (see SI
Sahin et al. 2022

Instead, the points measured on the Bloch sphere are centered around
\hat{\textbf{n}}{=}\cos(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{x}}+\sin(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{z}}

\hat{\textbf{z}}
-tilt that moves the midpoint of the polygon into the lower or upper half of the Bloch-sphere as sketched in Fig.

\hat{\textbf{z}}

16

App. I
Properties of the state in the prethermal plateau

The leading order Floquet Hamiltonian
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}
describes the steady-state of the system in the prethermal plateau
Abanin et al. 2017
.
When measured at times
nT_{\mathrm{AC}}+k\tau
for some integers
n,k<N
, we thus anticipate the system to prethermalize to a state that is well-described by a prethermal density matrix,

\tilde{\rho}_{F;\,k}=\exp(-\beta\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)})/Z\,,

(20)

with
Z{=}\mathrm{Tr}\exp(-\beta\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)})
.
Due to the unitarity of the time evolution, the temperature
\beta_{k}^{-1}
is determined by the initial energy density
\varepsilon_{k}[\tilde{\rho}_{0}]{=}\textrm{Tr}\left\{{\tilde{\rho}_{0}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)})}\right\}/L{\overset{!}{=}}\textrm{Tr}\left\{{\tilde{\rho}_{F;\,k}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}}\right\}/L
which matches the energy density of the state evolved in the prethermal plateau.
For an initial state with magnetization
\mu
along the
x
-direction
\tilde{\rho}_{0}{\propto}(\bm{1}+\mu I_{x})
, using
\sin(\alpha){=}\delta/\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\Omega^{2}}
, we find that the initial energy density

\varepsilon_{k}=\textrm{Tr}\left\{{\tilde{\rho}_{0}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}}\right\}/L=\mu{\left|\left(\delta/\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\Omega^{2}}\right)+\left(\delta\vartheta/\tau\right)\right|}\,,

(21)

in general does not vanish, as long as either
\delta\vartheta
or
\delta
are finite.
Equation (
21
) is a direct result of the tracelessness of the spin operators
\mathrm{Tr}\left(I_{a}\right){=}\mathrm{Tr}\left(I_{na}I_{mb}\right){=}0
, for
a,\,b{=}x,\,y,\,z
and
n{\neq}m
, and
\mathrm{Tr}\left(I_{a}I_{a}\right)/Z{=}L
.

Notice that the initial magnetization, and with it the inverse temperature, is relatively small; this justifies the high-temperature approximation
\tilde{\rho}_{F;\,k}{\approx}(\mathbf{1}-\beta\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,k}^{(0)})/Z
.
Within the high-temperature approximation, using the relation

|\bm{w}_{k}|^{2}{=}|\tilde{\bm{w}}_{k}|^{2}
,
for the inverse temperature we find

\beta_{k}=-\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}+{|\bm{w}}_{k}|^{2}}\,.

(22)

Note that the (inverse) temperature at which the system prethermalizes is set by the relative phase
\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}
between the AC and the SL pulse drives; e.g., for
\phi_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0
, we have
\beta{=}\beta_{k=0}
, etc. We stress that, once the system has reached the prethermal plateau, its state has a single well-defined temperature throughout the entire micromotion dynamics.

In Eq. (
22
),

h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}=2\left(1-3\sin^{2}\alpha\right)\sum_{n<m}b_{nm}^{2}/L\propto J^{2}

(23)

is an energy scale associated with the dipole-dipole coupling. In general,
h_{\mathrm{dd}}
is finite also in the thermodynamic limit since
b_{nm}^{2}{\propto}1/|r_{nm}|^{6}
is sufficiently short-ranged in three dimensional space for the sum above to converge. Here,
r_{nm}
is the internuclear position vector.
However, the precise value of
h_{\mathrm{dd}}
depends on microscopic details and cannot be accessed easily.

To summarize, as stated in the main text, it follows that the magnetization in the prethermal plateaus is determined by

\bm{M}_{k}=\textrm{Tr}\left\{{\bm{I}\rho_{F;\,k}}\right\}/L=\frac{-\varepsilon_{k}}{h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}+|\bm{w}_{k}|^{2}}\bm{w}_{k}\,,

(24)

which scales linearly with the AC amplitude,
\bm{M}_{k}{\propto}B_{\mathrm{AC}}
, for small amplitudes compared to the dipole-dipole coupling,
{B_{\mathrm{AC}}/h_{\mathrm{dd}}}{\ll 1}
.

In order to characterize the measured prethermal polygon shapes, let us introduce two metrics: the linear size
\ell
and the excursion angle
\Phi
away from the spin-lock axis.
To quantify the size of the shapes independent of the number of measured points
N
, we note that all points lie on a circle in
3\mathrm{D}
, and hence we can use the radius of this circle to define the linear size
\ell
of the polygons:

\ell=|\bm{w}_{k;\,\parallel}|=|\bm{w}_{k}-\hat{\textbf{n}}\left(\hat{\textbf{n}}\cdot\bm{w}_{k}\right)|\,.

(25)

As the corners of the polygon,
\bm{w}_{k}
, all lie in one plane, we can extract the in-plane components
{\bm{w}_{k;\,\parallel}{=}\bm{w}_{k}-\bm{c}}
by subtracting the center point
\bm{c}{=}\hat{\textbf{n}}\left(\hat{\textbf{n}}\cdot\bm{w}_{k}\right)
of the polygon, where
\hat{\textbf{n}}{=}\cos(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{x}}+\sin(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{z}}
is the unit-vector perpendicular to the plane.

In addition, let us define the excursion angle

\Phi=2\arctan\left(\frac{\ell}{{|\bm{c}|}}\right)\,.

(26)

The excursion angle is a measure for the size of the deviation of the polygon points from the axis
\hat{\textbf{x}}
which is conserved in the absence of the AC drive.
We note that, for regular shapes, these two measures are single valued, i.e.,
\Phi
and
\ell
should be independent of the index
k
.

Using Eq. (
24
) it is straightforward to show that

\ell\propto\frac{\varepsilon_{k}B_{\mathrm{AC}}}{h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}+{\left|\bm{w}_{k}\right|^{2}}}

(27)

and

\tan\left(\frac{\Phi}{2}\right)=\varepsilon_{k}\frac{{\left|\bm{u}_{k}\right|}}{{\left|\bm{v}(\alpha)\right|}}\propto B_{\mathrm{AC}}\,.

(28)

App. J
Theory of the quench dynamics

The transient dynamics when turning on and off the AC field are highly model dependent.
Nevertheless, we can make qualitative predictions about the behavior of the system by considering the thermal properties of the density matrices before and after the quench.
We will, in particular, consider three scenarios,
(i) initial opening of the shapes,
(ii) closing of the shapes, and
(iii) the combination of closing and reopening, corresponding to the scenarios depicted in Fig.
6
and Fig.
8
.
We will restrict the following analysis to ”square” shapes, for simplicity.

In the following analysis we will analyze the states
\rho_{f}
the system is expected to thermalize to after each quench, given the properties of the state
\rho_{i}
before each quench.
Following the discussion in the main text, we will further denote states for which the AC field is on (off) by a
\mathrm{on}
(off) subscript.

The initial opening
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{init}}\to\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{on},f}
has already been described in detail in App.
I
.
For the closing of the shapes,
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{on},i}\to\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{off},f}
, we point out the special role of the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-magnetization conservation, such that the thermal state is characterized by both a temperature and a magnetization. To account for the conservation law we introduce an additional Lagrange multiplier
\gamma
in the prethermal ensemble

\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{off},f}=\exp\left(-\beta_{\mathrm{off},f}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\left(B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0\right)-\gamma I^{x}\right]\right)/Z\,.

(29)

The inverse temperature
\beta_{\mathrm{off},f}
and the new parameter
\gamma
are determined by the initial energy
\textrm{Tr}\left\{{\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{on},i}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}}\right\}{\overset{!}{=}}\textrm{Tr}\left\{{\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{off},f}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}}\right\}
and magnetization
\textrm{Tr}\left\{{I_{x}\tilde{\rho_{0}}}\right\}{\overset{!}{=}}\textrm{Tr}\left\{{I_{x}\tilde{\rho}_{F}\left(B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0\right)}\right\}
, respectively.

The initial state
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{on},i}
is given by the thermal state before the quench, i.e.,
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{on},i}{=}\tilde{\rho}_{F;\,0}\left(\phi_{c}\right){=}\exp(-\beta\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,0}^{(0)}\left(\phi_{c}\right))/Z
, introduced in App.
I
, where the phase at closing is determined by the time
t_{\mathrm{c}}
at which the closing happens via
\phi_{c}{=}\phi_{\mathrm{AB}}+\left(\omega_{\mathrm{AC}}t_{\mathrm{c}}\right)\mathrm{mod}\left(2\pi\right)
.

Combining the high temperature approximation
{\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{on},i}{\approx}\left(\mathbf{1}-\beta_{\mathrm{on},i}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F;\,0}^{(0)}\left(\phi_{c}\right))\right)/Z}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{off},f}{\approx}\left(\mathbf{1}-\beta_{\mathrm{off},f}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\left(B_{\mathrm{AC}}=0\right)-\gamma I_{x}\right]\right)/Z
, the temperature and magnetization after the quench are immediately given by

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{off},f}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\mathrm{on},i}

\displaystyle\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=0\,.

(30)

We emphasize that this does not imply the equality of the two states, since
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\left(B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0\right){\neq}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F}^{(0)}\left(B_{\mathrm{AC}}{\neq}0\right)
. However, our result suggests that the closing quench merely leads to a decay of all components orthogonal to the preserved axis
\hat{\textbf{x}}
.
In this respect, note also that the final state, after closing the shape, has a finite dipolar order. In contrast, the initial state
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{init}}{\propto}I_{x}
has vanishing dipolar order.

For these reasons, reopening the shapes,
\rho_{\mathrm{on},i}{\to}\rho_{\mathrm{on},f}
is different from the initial opening of the shapes.
In particular, the temperature after reopening is given by,

\beta_{\mathrm{on},f}=\beta_{\mathrm{on},i}\frac{h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}+{\bm{v}\left(\alpha\right)}\cdot{\bm{w}_{k}}}{h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}+{|\bm{w}}_{k}|^{2}}\,,

(31)

where
\beta_{\mathrm{on},i}
is the temperature before the quench. The fraction on the right hand side of Eq. (
31
) is always smaller than
1
, as
{|\bm{w}}_{k}|\geq{|\bm{v}\left(\alpha\right)|}
, with equality only at
{B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0}
.

Therefore, combining Eqs. (
24
), (
30
), and (
31
), we find that the total magnetization
|\bm{M}_{\mathrm{on},f}|
, after closing and reopening, is given by

|\bm{M}_{\mathrm{on},f}|/|\bm{M}_{\mathrm{on},i}|=\beta_{\mathrm{on},f}/\beta_{\mathrm{on},i}=1-\sigma.

(32)

Here
|\bm{M}_{\mathrm{on},i}|
is the total magnetization prior to closing, and
{\sigma{=}\left(h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}+{\bm{v}\left(\alpha\right)}\cdot{\bm{w}_{k}}\right)/\left(h_{\mathrm{dd}}^{2}+{|\bm{w}}_{k}|^{2}\right)}
.
Hence, with each closing and reopening, the amplitude of the magnetization is reduced by a factor
\left(1-\sigma\right)
.
However, each closing and reopening process results only in a tiny loss, since
{\sigma{\ll}1}
in the experimentally relevant regime
{B_{\mathrm{AC}}{\ll}h_{\mathrm{dd}}}
.

App. B
Experimental setup

We now provide more details of the apparatus employed for
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
spin control and interrogation in our experiments. In reality, the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei are hyperpolarized at room temperature at low-fields (
\sim
38mT), and subsequently “shuttled” to high-field (7T) where they are controlled and interrogated. We refer the reader to Ref.
Sarkar et al. 2021
for details on the hyperpolarization apparatus and mechanism, and Ref.
Ajoy et al. 2019b
for details on the shuttling apparatus.

Specifically, the detection of the spins proceeds via inductive coupling to an RF cavity and measurement of corresponding
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) signals at high-field (7T), where the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
resonance frequency is 75.38MHz. This is accomplished via a homemade NMR probe that was detailed in Ref.

\sim

{\sim}

\sim
0.15. The probe also has a z-coil that is employed to apply the time-varying AC field to the sample. This is a loop of a 2-turn coaxial cable that is wound outside the RF coil (see Ref.
Sahin et al. 2021
).

The AC field itself is produced with a Tabor TE5201 arbitrary waveform generator, and is amplified by an AE Techron 7224 amplifier into the z-coil. The spin-lock RF pulses on the other hand are produced by a Varian NMR spectrometer. High signal-to-noise detection of the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclear precession is carried out with a quarter wave line and bandpass filter combination following a transmit/receive switch, and the signal is digitized by a high-speed arbitrary waveform generator (Tabor Proteus). The high data acquisition rate of the device (1GS/s) allows for high-fidelity sampling of the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
induction signal between the pulses
Sahin et al. 2021
. The entire experiment is synchronized via a PulseBlaster digital pulse generator (100MHz).

App. E
Numerical Simulations

Our experimental and analytical results are complemented with exact numerical simulations performed on a system of
L
spins interacting
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclear quantum spins positioned on a pseudo-random graph. For the design of pseudo-random graphs we respect the following condition
Beatrez et al. 2022
: each spin of the graph is required to have at least one nearest neighbor within a distance
r_{\mathrm{max}}
while no other spin is closer than
r_{\mathrm{min}}
.
This ensures a certain average spin density and, importantly, avoids weakly coupled outliers. While such weakly coupled spins are in principle expected to emerge to some extent in the experimental system, their influence on the many-body dynamics is negligible. In particular, from a numerical perspective where only a relatively small number of interacting spins can be simulated, outliers are wastefully problematic as each of them reduces the effective interacting Hilbert space by a factor of
2
.

The pseudo-random graphs are generated iteratively: first, we randomly propose a new spin position
j
and check if (i) the inter-spin vectors with all other spins
k
on the graph satisfy
|\vec{r}_{jk}|>r_{\mathrm{min}}
, while (ii) at least one inter-spin vector satisfies
|\vec{r}_{jk}|<r_{\mathrm{max}}
. If (i) and (ii) are satisfied, we accept the proposal and update the random graph with the spins at position
j
. Otherwise, we discard the proposed spin. We repeat this procedure until a graph of
L
spins is constructed. The simulations are performed with
L{=}14
,
r_{\mathrm{min}}{=}0.9
and
r_{\mathrm{max}}{=}1.1
; here
r_{\mathrm{min}}
and
r_{\mathrm{max}}
are given in units of
\sqrt[3]{\mu_{0}\hbar\gamma_{n}^{2}}
.

For numerical simulations, unless stated explicitly otherwise, we initialize the system in a
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-polarized pure product state
|\psi_{0}\rangle\!=\!\bigotimes_{j=1}^{L}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\!\uparrow_{j}\rangle\!+\!|\downarrow_{j}\rangle\right)
and perform numerically exact time evolution using the protocol of Eq. (
3
). Due to the critical long-range interaction strength, the characteristic energy scales of the model are not immediately obvious (the energy density integral diverges in three spacial dimensions, which indicates the necessity to impose a lattice cutoff scale). However, they can be estimated from the dynamics of a free-induction decay: to this end, the initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is evolved under
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{dd}}
which induces a decay of the initial
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-magnetization. Eventually, this provides an energy scale
J=1/\tau_{d}
where
\tau_{d}
is the timescale on which the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-magnetization decays to
1/e
of its initial value as the system approaches equilibrium. We note that the value of
J
obtained in this way can only serve as a rough estimate of energy scales, as different initial states might lead to different values of
J
.

To minimize detrimental finite size effects and increase the ergodic properties of the drive we add a small uniformly distributed random noise
\delta t_{\textrm{acq}}\in[-0.05t_{\textrm{acq}},0.05t_{\textrm{acq}}]
to the time duration
t_{\textrm{acq}}
, i.e.
t_{\textrm{acq}}\rightarrow t_{\textrm{acq}}+\delta t_{\textrm{acq}}

Fleckenstein and Bukov 2021a
;
Fleckenstein and Bukov 2021b
which results in a slightly different unitary for each driving cycle. Such a ”noisy” driving protocol can remove left-over synchronization effects emerging from the discreteness of the many-body spectrum. However, since the noise term
\delta t_{\textrm{acq}}
breaks the periodicity of the drive, one needs to carefully examine that no additional physics, such as an extra contribution to heating, is induced due to finite
\delta t_{\textrm{acq}}
. For the system under investigation, this has been demonstrated in Ref.
Beatrez et al. 2022
.

Finally, let us present some additions to the data presented in the main text, i.e. in Fig.
2
and Fig.
4
. In Fig.
S3
, we present the full numerical data for the simulated time evolution partially depicted in Fig.
2
B. In particular, the simulated data also leads to the square shapes when projected to the
\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
or
\hat{\textbf{y}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{z}}
plane.
In addition to the excursion angle
\Phi
and linear scale
\ell
shown in Fig.
4
C-D, we show the corresponding raw data and decay times as functions of the AC amplitude in Fig.
S4
.
Let us in particular point out the increase in the lifetime with increasing AC Amplitude by a factor of up to
2
, suggested by the numerical simulation.
This is not observed in the experiment and obtaining analytical expressions for the decay time are beyond reach for our non-integrable long-range interacting systems.
Notice however that the lifetime is only affected by a factor of
2
over a large range of AC amplitudes and that the impact of finite size effects on the various quantities is not well-understood.

App. F
Role of the Phase in the Transformation to the Rotating Frame

In App.
G
we introduced the rotation to the dressed rotating frame,
W(t,\chi){=}\exp\left[-i\left(\omega_{\mathrm{SL}}t+\chi\right)I_{z}\right]
which features the phase
\chi
.
A finite phase,
\chi{\neq}0
, corresponds to a constant shift in the experimentally observed dressed frame phase
\varphi{=}\arctan\left(I_{x}/I_{y}\right)-\chi
.
While the derivations in App.
G
and following are done for
\chi{=}0
, one can simply account for the additional phase
\chi
by performing a subsequent static unitary transformation:
W_{\mathrm{static}}=\exp(-i\chi I_{z})
on the final results.

Note that the phase
\chi
naturally enters the expression for the experimentally measured phase
\varphi
.
However, due to the subtraction of the ramp-like profile of the phase
\varphi
from the raw experimental data, see Fig.
11
and App.
B
, the actual observable becomes
\varphi_{0}{=}\varphi(B_{\mathrm{AC}})-\varphi(B_{\mathrm{AC}}\!=\!0)
.
Therefore, this subtraction procedure rather can be naturally viewed as fixing the gauge freedom associated with the phase
\chi
, so that
\varphi{=}0
at
B_{\mathrm{AC}}{=}0
.

App. G
Role of detuning and imperfect pulses on observed shapes

In the main text we discussed two imperfections of the SL pulse drive: the detuning
\delta
from the Lamor frequency
\omega_{L}
, and the deviation in the pulse angle
\delta\vartheta{=}\vartheta-2\pi k/N
. Here, we show that they can lead to an enhancement of the signal. In fact, they are essential for reproducing the experimental observations. Eventually, we also investigate the related question of having noise in the kick sequence.

We start with the detuning
\delta
. Let us stress again that, due to the large scale separation
\omega_{L}/J\leq 10^{-5}
, even a small deviation (
0.01\%
) can lead to a comparably large detuning
\delta\leq J
.
Note that the detuning affects the direction of the kicks
\hat{\textbf{x}}\to\hat{\textbf{n}}=\cos(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{x}}+\sin(\alpha)\hat{\textbf{z}}
, cf. Eq. (
10
). Therefore, the obtained shapes lie on circles which are centered around the
\hat{\textbf{n}}
axis.
Note that
\hat{\textbf{n}}
has a finite
\hat{\textbf{z}}
contribution,
\hat{\textbf{n}}_{z}\propto\delta
, which leads to a finite
\hat{\textbf{z}}
-tilt, as can also be observed in the simulations in Fig.
S5
.
Therefore, the detuning is crucial for explaining the experimentally observed
\hat{\textbf{z}}
-tilt.

Moreover, the detuning, together with the imperfection in the pulse strength
\delta\vartheta
, leads to a finite
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-magnetization,
M_{x}\propto\sin(\alpha)+\delta\vartheta/\tau
, in the leading-order Floquet Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{F;\,k}^{(0)}
, cf. Eq. (
5
).
This leads to an increase in the
\hat{\textbf{x}}
-polarization strength, as can also be observed in Fig.
S5
A(i) and Fig.
S6
A(i).
In addition, as mentioned in App.
I
is it vital that
\delta
or
\delta\vartheta
are finite, in order for the initial state to have a finite energy density
\epsilon
, and thus also a finite temperature
|\beta|\propto|\sin(\alpha)+\delta\vartheta/\tau|>0
.
Such an increase in temperature is correlated with the increase in
\hat{\textbf{y}}
- and
\hat{\textbf{z}}
-magnetizations in Fig.
S5
and Fig.
S6
, cf. Eq. (
24
).

Note that the robustness of the shapes with respect to imperfection in the kicks, i.e. for
\delta\vartheta\neq 0
, also demonstrates that the shapes are not simply a single-particle effect but are stabilized by the many-body interactions.

Finally, let us emphasize that we numerically do not observe a significant change in the lifetime when varying the detuning
\delta
or the angle
\delta\theta
.

To explore this behavior, we simulate noisy pulses
\vartheta{=}{(1+\zeta)\times 2\pi/4}
for the special case of
N{=}4
, where
\zeta
are random numbers that differ for every pulse uniformly distributed in the interval
[-\eta,\,\eta]
(with
\eta
being the noise strength). We observe a strong decrease in the lifetime of the prethermal plateau with increasing
\eta
, see Fig.
S7
.
This result is not surprising as the periodic nature of the drive is lost with increasing randomness.
