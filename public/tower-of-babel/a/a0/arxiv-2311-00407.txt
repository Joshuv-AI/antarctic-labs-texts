Abstract
We measure the CO-to-H 2 conversion factor ( α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} ) in 37 galaxies at 2 kpc resolution, using dust surface density inferred from far-infrared emission as a tracer of the gas surface density and assuming a constant dust-to-metals ratio. In total, we have ∼ 790 \sim 790 and ∼ 610 \sim 610 independent measurements of α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} for CO (2-1) and (1-0), respectively. The mean values for α CO ⁡ ( 2 − 1 ) \alpha_{\mathrm{CO~(2-1)}} and α CO ⁡ ( 1 − 0 ) \alpha_{\mathrm{CO~(1-0)}} are 9.3 − 5.4 + 4.6 9.3^{+4.6}_{-5.4} and 4.2 − 2.0 + 1.9 ​ M ⊙ ​ p ​ c − 2 ​ ( K ​ k ​ m ​ s − 1 ) − 1 4.2^{+1.9}_{-2.0}~M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}~(K~km~s^{-1})^{-1} , respectively. The CO-intensity-weighted mean for α CO ⁡ ( 2 − 1 ) \alpha_{\mathrm{CO~(2-1)}} is 5.69, and 3.33 for α CO ⁡ ( 1 − 0 ) \alpha_{\mathrm{CO~(1-0)}} . We examine how α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} scales with several physical quantities, e.g. star-formation rate (SFR), stellar mass, and dust-mass-weighted average interstellar radiation field strength ( U ¯ \overline{U} ). Among them, U ¯ \overline{U} , Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} , and integrated CO intensity ( W CO W_{\mathrm{CO}} ) have the strongest anti-correlation with spatially resolved α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} . We provide linear regression results to α CO \alpha_{\rm CO} for all quantities tested. At galaxy integrated scales, we observe significant correlations between α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} and W CO W_{\mathrm{CO}} , metallicity, U ¯ \overline{U} , and Σ SFR \Sigma_{\rm SFR} . We also find that the normalized α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} decreases with stellar mass surface density ( Σ ⋆ \Sigma_{\star} ) in the high surface density regions ( Σ ⋆ ≥ 100 ​ M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 \Sigma_{\star}\geq 100~{\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}} ), following the power-law relations α CO ⁡ ( 2 − 1 ) ∝ Σ ⋆ − 0.5 \alpha_{\mathrm{CO~(2-1)}}\propto\Sigma_{\star}^{-0.5} and α CO ⁡ ( 1 − 0 ) ∝ Σ ⋆ − 0.2 \alpha_{\mathrm{CO~(1-0)}}\propto\Sigma_{\star}^{-0.2} . The power-law index is insensitive to the assumed dust-to-metals ratio. We interpret the decrease in α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} with increasing Σ ⋆ \Sigma_{\star} as a result of higher velocity dispersion compared to isolated, self-gravitating clouds due to the additional gravitational force from stellar sources, which leads to the reduction of α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} . The decrease in α CO \alpha_{\mathrm{CO}} at high Σ ⋆ \Sigma_{\star} is important for accurately assessing molecular gas content and star-formation efficiency in the centers of galaxies, which bridge “MW-like” to ”starburst-like” conversion factors.

Introduction

Star formation is fueled by the molecular gas in the interstellar medium (ISM). Thus, observing the molecular ISM is essential for studies of star formation and galaxy evolution. Unfortunately, the most abundant molecule in the ISM, H
2
, is not directly observable in many cases in the the cold molecular ISM due to its high transition energies (
h\nu/k_{\mathrm{B}}\sim 510~\mathrm{K}
) for the lowest rotational levels.
As a result, low-
J
CO emission lines are the most widely used tracer for the molecular ISM, as the second most abundant molecule with strong millimeter rotational lines that can be excited at typical temperatures in molecular clouds.
The standard practice is to use a CO-to-H
2
conversion factor
\alpha_{\rm CO}

\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)}W_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)},~\mathrm{for}~\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)\\
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)}W_{\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)},~\mathrm{for}~\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)\end{array}\right.\,.

(1)

where

[

With the precise measurements of CO emission from modern instruments, our understanding of

2

;
Accurso et al. 2017

Gong et al. 2020

Hirashita 2023

The second trend is that

This phenomena is often referred to as the “starburst conversion factor.” Because galaxy centers and U/LIRGs tend to be bright in CO and thus easily observed, understanding this starburst conversion factor is important to making best use of a wide range of extragalactic observations in characterizing the star formation efficiency, gas dynamics and
H i

2

Bolatto et al. 2013
proposed a formula for conversion factor treating the CO-dark gas and the starburst trend independently and simultaneously. This formula aimed to predict both the spatially resolved measurements from
Sandstrom et al. 2013
and the (U)LIRGs measurements in
Downes & Solomon 1998

\frac{\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)}}{1~{\rm M_{\odot}\,pc^{-2}\,(K\,km\,s^{-1})^{-1}}}=\\
\rm 2.9\times\exp\left(\frac{0.4}{Z^{\prime}}\right)\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\Sigma_{\rm Total}^{100}\right)^{-0.5}&,\,\Sigma_{\rm Total}^{100}\geq 1\\
1&,\,\Sigma_{\rm Total}^{100}<1\end{array}\right.\,.

(2)

Here
\rm Z^{\prime}

\Sigma_{\rm Total}^{100}

\Sigma_{\mathrm{Total}}=\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}+\Sigma_{\star}
) in unit of
100~\mathrm{M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}}

\alpha_{\rm CO}
occurs, i.e. galaxy centers and (U)LIRGs. The authors found that with a threshold at
\Sigma_{\rm Total}^{100}\geq 1
(a threshold related to self-gravitating giant molecular clouds), the relation
\alpha_{\rm CO}\propto\left(\Sigma_{\rm Total}^{100}\right)^{-0.5}
reproduces the trend found in galaxy centers and ULIRG samples. A similar formula was also suggested by
Ostriker & Shetty 2011

\alpha_{\rm CO}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}
to describe the decrease in
\alpha_{\rm CO}
needed for their simulations to match observations. They showed that a relation of
\alpha_{\rm CO}\propto\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}^{-0.5}
over the surface density range
10^{2}
to
10^{3}

\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}
brings the observations and simulations into agreement.

Calculating
\alpha_{\rm CO}

To calculate
\alpha_{\rm CO}
, we first estimate
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
without using CO emission and an adopted conversion factor. In this study, we use a dust-based strategy to estimate
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
by assuming a value for the fraction of metals locked in solid phase, i.e. the dust-to-metals ratio (D/M). The D/M is defined as:

{\rm D/M}=\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{dust}}}{Z\times\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}},

(3)

where
\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}=\Sigma_{\mathrm{atom}}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
is the total neutral gas surface density,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
is the molecular gas surface density, and the metallicity
Z
converts
\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}
to a “metal mass surface density”. By replacing
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
in the above equation with the definition of
\alpha_{\rm CO}
in
Eq. 1

\alpha_{\rm CO}=\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}~W_{\mathrm{CO}}^{-1}=\Big(\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{dust}}}{Z\times\mathrm{(D/M)}}-\Sigma_{\mathrm{atom}}\Big){W_{\mathrm{CO}}^{-1}},

(4)

where
\Sigma_{\mathrm{dust}}
, Z,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{atom}}
, and
W_{\mathrm{CO}}
are measurable quantities, thus, by assigning a value of D/M, we can then calculate
\alpha_{\rm CO}
with our data set. The uncertainty of
\alpha_{\rm CO}
is propagated from the uncertainties of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{dust}}
, metallicity,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{atom}}
, and
W_{\mathrm{CO}}
. The typical uncertainty of the pixel-by-pixel
\alpha_{\rm CO}
measurements in this work is in the range of 0.2--0.5 dex

R_{21}=0.62\left(\frac{I_{\mathrm{WISE4}}}{1\,{\rm MJy\,sr\textsuperscript{-1}}}\right)^{0.22}.

\sim 0.15

III.1
Physical Quantities and Data Sets

Dust properties.

We obtain dust properties by fitting the dust emission spectral energy distribution (SED) to the
Draine & Li 2007
physical dust model. The details of the IR data processing and dust SED fitting are reported in
Chastenet et al. 2021
and J. Chastenet et al. (in preparation). We briefly summarize the process below.

We obtain the dust emission SED in the IR observed by two space telescopes: the 3.4, 4.6, 12 and 22 µm bands with the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer
(
Wright et al. 2010
, WISE,)
, and the 70, 100, 160 and 250 µm bands with the
Herschel Space Observatory

(
Pilbratt et al. 2010
)
.
The
Herschel
and WISE maps are first convolved to SPIRE 250 PSF and then to a 21″ Gaussian PSF using the SPIRE 250-to-Gauss-21″ kernel from
Aniano et al. 2011
. The 21″ PSF is the “moderate” Gaussian from
Aniano et al. 2011
that provides relatively high angular resolution without amplifying image artifacts. Finally, these maps are convolved to the desired resolution: a Gaussian PSF with spatial resolution corresponding to FWHM of 2 kpc.

After convolving the IR maps, we fit the dust SED with the
Draine & Li 2007
physical dust model with the dust opacity calibration derived in
Chastenet et al. 2021
. This calibration is based on metallicity measured with “direct” electron-temperature-based methods, which is consistent with the strong lien calibration adopted in this work
(
Pilyugin & Grebel 2016
, S-calibration in)
, and yields reasonable D/M. Thus, the calibration ties dust opacity, D/M and metallicity into one framework.
The complete set of data products from the fitting includes the maps of dust mass surface density (
\Sigma_{\mathrm{dust}}
), interstellar radiation field (the minimum radiation field
U_{\mathrm{min}}
and the fraction of dust heated by the power-law radiation field
\gamma
) and the fractional dust mass in the form of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (
\rm q_{PAH}
). The maximum radiation field is fixed at
U_{\rm max}=10^{7}
and the power-law index for radiation field distribution is fixed at
\alpha=2
. From the fitted
U_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\gamma
, we can derive the dust-mass-averaged radiation field
\overline{U}
, which is the fiducial tracer for radiation field in this work.

We also note that we assume fixed dust properties in our dust SED fitting throughout this study, which is the most frequently adopted strategy in the literature. The accuracy of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{dust}}
estimates, derived by fitting the IR SED with dust emission models, can be affected by variations in the dust opacity. Interstellar dust grains are not uniform in their chemical composition, size distribution, and shape, leading to variations in their opacity
(
Draine & Li 2007
;
Hirashita & Voshchinnikov 2014
;
Draine & Hensley 2021
, e.g.)
.
In the MW,
Stepnik et al. 2003
found that the dust opacity increases by
\sim 3
times from the diffuse ISM to the dense clouds. The authors argued that the increase in dust opacity is resulted from the deficit of small grains due to grain-grain coagulation.
It is challenging to measure the variation in opacity of interstellar dust since it is degenerate with the environmental dependence of
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and D/M.
Moreover, many of the mechanisms that affects dust opacity, e.g. grain-grain coagulation and ice mantles, are smoothed out in kpc-scale extra-galactic studies
(
Galliano et al. 2018
)
, meaning that its variation is likely less observable than the other degenerate factors like
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and D/M.
We note that there are extra-galactic studies that attribute all the variations in dust and gas properties to dust opacity to evaluate its variation, e.g.
Clark et al. 2019
found that dust opacity change by a factor
\sim 2
within M74 and
\sim 5
within M83.

Atomic gas surface density.
We trace the atomic gas surface density (
\Sigma_{\mathrm{atom}}
) with the
H i
21 cm integrated intensity (
I_{\textsc{H\,i}}
), assuming the opacity is negligible
(
Walter et al. 2008
, e.g.,)
:

\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{atom}}}{1~\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}}=1.36\times(1.46\times 10^{-2})\times\frac{W_{\textsc{H\,i}}}{1~{\rm K\,km\,s\textsuperscript{-1}}}~,

(7)

where the
1.36
factor accounts for the mass of helium.

We obtain
W_{\textsc{H\,i}}
from both literature and new data, as listed in
. The two new
H i
surveys are the EveryTHINGS survey (P.I. K. M. Sandstrom; I. Chiang et al. in preparation) and the PHANGS-VLA survey (P.I. D. Utomo). The EveryTHINGS survey targets nearby galaxies with
Herschel
photometric data but without high-resolution
H i
observations, while the PHANGS-VLA survey focuses on galaxies in the Physics at High Angular resolution in Nearby Galaxies (PHANGS) project
5
5

5

http://phangs.org/
.
Both surveys have their data observed with the C- and D-configurations of
Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA)
6
6

6

The VLA is operated by the National Radio Astronomy Observatory (NRAO), which is a facility of the National Science Foundation operated under cooperative agreement by Associated Universities, Inc.
, which yield an angular resolutions in the range of
20\arcsec
to
30\arcsec
. Both surveys provide new high-sensitivity 21 cm
H i
observations in tens of nearby galaxies. We did not include WHISP
(
Swaters et al. 2002
)
data because the galaxies that only have WHISP data have resolution coarser than 2 kpc after convolving to a circular PSF.

CO low-
J
rotational lines.
The integrated intensity of CO low-

Eq. 1
), and is key to this study. We use the compilation of CO mapping assembled by
Leroy et al. 2022
;
Leroy et al. 2023
from several publicly available CO
(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)
and CO
(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)
data:

•

CO
(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)
data from the COMING survey
(
Sorai et al. 2019
)
and the CO Atlas
(
Kuno et al. 2007
)
.

•

CO
(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)
data from HERACLES
(
Leroy et al. 2009a
)
, the PHANGS-ALMA survey
(
Leroy et al. 2021
)
, and a new survey observed by the IRAM-30m focused on the Virgo Cluster
(
Leroy et al. 2022
, P.I. A. Schruba; processed in)
.

The source of CO data for each galaxy is listed in
, where CO
(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)
and CO
(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)
are listed separately. All these literature measurements focus on the
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}^{16}\mathrm{O}
isotopologue, hereafter CO for simplicity.

Surface densities of stellar mass and SFR.
We trace the surface densities of stellar mass and SFR (
\Sigma_{\star}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
, respectively) using the data and conversion formulae presented in the
z
0MGS survey
(
Leroy et al. 2019
)
. We utilize the
z
0MGS compilation of the background-subtracted WISE
(
Wright et al. 2010
)

\lambda\sim 3.4
and 22 µm (hereafter WISE1 and WISE4, respectively) data and the Galaxy Evolution Explorer
(
Martin et al. 2005
, GALEX,)

\lambda\sim 154~\rm nm
(hereafter FUV) data.

We use WISE1 data to trace stellar mass surface density (
\Sigma_{\star}
) with:

\frac{\Sigma_{\star}}{1~\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}}=3.3\times 10^{2}\left(\frac{\Upsilon^{3.4}_{\star}}{0.5~\mathrm{M_{\odot}~L_{\odot}^{-1}}}\right)\frac{I_{\rm WISE1}}{1~{\rm MJy\,sr\textsuperscript{-1}}}~,

(8)

where
\Upsilon^{3.4}_{\star}
is the
\Sigma_{\star}
-to-WISE1 mass-to-light ratio. The value of
\Upsilon^{3.4}_{\star}
is calculated from the galaxy-by-galaxy SFR-to-WISE1 ratio, a “specific SFR-like” quantity, with the prescription calibrated in Appendix A of
Leroy et al. 2019
.

We use FUV and WISE4 data to trace the SFR surface density (
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
) also with the conversion formula suggested by
z
0MGS
(
Leroy et al. 2019
;
Belfiore et al. 2023
)
. For galaxies with both FUV and WISE4 available, we use:

\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}}{1~{\rm M_{\odot}\,yr}^{-1}\,{\rm kpc}^{-2}}=\\
8.85\times 10^{-2}\frac{I_{\rm FUV}}{1~{\rm MJy\,sr\textsuperscript{-1}}}+3.02\times 10^{-3}\frac{I_{\rm WISE4}}{1~{\rm MJy\,sr\textsuperscript{-1}}}~.

(9)

For NGC3953 and NGC4689, where only WISE4 is available, we use:

\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}}{1~{\rm M_{\odot}\,yr}^{-1}\,{\rm kpc}^{-2}}=3.81\times 10^{-3}\frac{I_{\rm W4}}{1~{\rm MJy\,sr\textsuperscript{-1}}}~.

(10)

For both WISE and GALEX maps, we blank the regions with foreground stars identified in the
z
0MGS database. We interpolate the intensities in the blanked region with a Gaussian kernel FWHM = 22.5″(the adopted WISE and GALEX maps have FWHM = 15″) with the function
interpolate_replace_nans
in
astropy.convolution
. This interpolation is done on the maps before any convolution, reprojection, or unit conversion. Regarding the WISE maps, this treatment is only done for the maps used for calculating
\Sigma_{\star}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
. For the WISE maps used in dust SED fitting, we refer the readers to J. Chastenet et al. (in preparation).

Specific SFR.
With the measurements of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
and
\Sigma_{\star}
, we calculate the specific SFR (sSFR) as:

\frac{\rm sSFR}{\rm 1~yr^{-1}}=1\times 10^{-6}\times\big(\bm{\sum}_{i}\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}}{1~{\rm M_{\odot}\,yr}^{-1}\,{\rm kpc}^{-2}}\big)\big(\bm{\sum}_{i}\frac{\Sigma_{\star}}{1~\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}}\big)^{-1},

(11)

where
\bm{\sum}_{i}
is the summation over pixels in a galaxy. Meanwhile, we calculate the spatially resolved sSFR (rsSFR) as:

\frac{\rm rsSFR}{\rm 1~yr^{-1}}=1\times 10^{-6}\times\frac{\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}}{1~{\rm M_{\odot}\,yr}^{-1}\,{\rm kpc}^{-2}}\big(\frac{\Sigma_{\star}}{1~\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}}\big)^{-1}.

(12)

Metallicity.
We use the abundance of oxygen,
12+\log({\rm O/H})
, to trace the metallicity (
Z
). We assume a fixed abundance pattern, i.e. a constant oxygen-to-total-metal mass ratio. The conversion from
12+\log({\rm O/H})
to
Z
then becomes:

Z=0.0134\times 10^{12+\log({\rm O/H})-8.69},

(13)

where 0.0134 and 8.69 are the adopted
Z_{\odot}
and
12+\log({\rm O/H})_{\odot}
, respectively
(
Asplund et al. 2009
)
.

We calculate
12+\log({\rm O/H})
for each pixel as a function of the galactocentric distance by adopting the radial gradient of
12+\log({\rm O/H})
derived from measurements in
H ii
regions. We use data from two surveys: the PHANGS-MUSE survey
(
Emsellem et al. 2022
;
Groves et al. 2023
)
and the
Zurita et al. 2021
compilation. We use the
Pilyugin & Grebel 2016
S-calibration
7
7

7

Pilyugin & Grebel 2016
utilizes the
S_{2}=I_{\rm[S~II]}\lambda 6717+\lambda 6731/I_{\rm H\beta}
,
N_{2}=I_{\rm[NII]}\lambda 6548+\lambda 6584/I_{\rm H\beta}
, and
R_{3}=I_{\rm[OIII]}\lambda 4959+\lambda 5007/I_{\rm H\beta}
line ratios to measure gas-phase
12+\log({\rm O/H})
.
(hereafter PG16S) as the fiducial calibration for
12+\log({\rm O/H})
. PG16S is a calibration method that shows good agreement with direct metallicity measurements
(
Croxall et al. 2016
;
Kreckel et al. 2019
)
. Since PG16S only relies on lines covered by MUSE, the
12+\log({\rm O/H})
measurement can be expanded to the full PHANGS-MUSE dataset in our future works.

For galaxies in the PHANGS-MUSE survey, we adopt radial
12+\log({\rm O/H})
gradients presented in
Santoro et al. 2022
, which are calculated with the PG16S calibration. For galaxies that only appear in the
Zurita et al. 2021
emission data compilation, we use the
Zurita et al. 2021
data to calculate the PG16S
12+\log({\rm O/H})
and then fit the radial
12+\log({\rm O/H})
gradient in these galaxies. We only consider galaxies that have at least 5 measurements spanning at least
0.5R_{25}
in the
Zurita et al. 2021
data table. The uncertainties of the
12+\log({\rm O/H})
gradient is not explicitly provided in either work. We will assume a 0.1 dex scatter for
12+\log({\rm O/H})
derived from gradients as suggested in
Berg et al. 2020
.

For galaxies without measurements of
12+\log({\rm O/H})
in either
Zurita et al. 2021
or
Santoro et al. 2022
, we use the two-step strategy proposed in
Sun et al. 2020
to estimate their
12+\log({\rm O/H})
. First, we use a mass-metallicity relation to predict
12+\log({\rm O/H})
at one effective radius (
R_{\mathrm{e}}
) in a given galaxy. Second, we extend the prediction with a radial gradient of
-0.1~\mathrm{dex}/R_{\mathrm{e}}
suggested by
Sánchez et al. 2014
. We characterize the mass-metallicity with a function of the form:

12+\log({\rm O/H})=a+bxe^{-x},

(14)

where
x=\log(M_{\star}/\mathrm{M}_{\odot})-11.5

(see
Sánchez et al. 2019
, and references therein)
.
a
and
b
are free parameters. We fit the function with
12+\log({\rm O/H})
at one
\rm R_{e}
from galaxies with the available measurements listed in
. The best-fit parameters are
a=8.56\pm 0.02
and
b=0.010\pm 0.002
, which are robust under resampling. Meanwhile, the typical statistical uncertainty in the
12+\log({\rm O/H})
data used for fitting is
\sim 0.013
dex. However, the root mean square error (
\Delta_{\mathrm{rms}}
) between the best fit and data is 0.13 dex, meaning that there is still intrinsic scatter in
12+\log({\rm O/H})
that is not explained by the mass-metallicity relation and the adopted radial gradients, e.g. the azimuthal variations
(
Williams et al. 2022
)
.
We use this fitted relation to derive the radial metallicity gradient of galaxies without metallicity measurements with the
M_{\star}
and
R_{\mathrm{e}}
listed in
. We will assume an uncertainty of 0.15 dex (rounding up
0.013+0.13
dex) for galaxies with this type of
12+\log({\rm O/H})
measurements.

Studies have reported that the PG16S calibration could result in
12+\log({\rm O/H})
value lower than other calibrations
(
De Vis et al. 2019
, e.g.)
. Aligning with that, there are also studies reporting that the
12+\log({\rm O/H})
calibrated with PG16S in Orion Nebula and other
H ii
regions in the solar neighborhood have values
\sim 0.2
dex lower than the solar reference value
(
Esteban et al. 2022
, e.g.)
. This effect could lead to an underestimate of Z’ and thus an overestimate in
\alpha_{\rm CO}
with our methodology. For consistency with the direct metallicity calibration used in
Chastenet et al. 2021
for calibrating dust opacity, we will use PG16S in this work.

III.2
Uniform Data Processing

The analyses in this work are done at a common resolution of 2 kpc for all data.
For
H i
, CO,
\Sigma_{\star}
, and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
, we convolve them to a circular Gaussian PSF with a FWHM corresponding to 2 kpc, using the
astropy.convolution
package
(
Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013
;
Astropy Collaboration et al. 2018
;
Astropy Collaboration et al. 2022
)
. The images are then reprojected onto a grid with a pixel size of one third of the FWHM (that is, we oversample at roughly the Nyquist sampling rate) with the
astropy
affiliated package
reproject
.
The convolution and reprojection of the dust maps are done in J. Chastenet et al. (in preparation). They convolve the IR maps into the final resolution using kernels from
Aniano et al. 2011
. Note that the convolution is done before SED fitting for dust properties.
The galactocentric radius and metallicity are directly calculated on the final pixel grids.
All the surface density and surface brightness quantities presented in this work have been corrected for inclination, listed in
.

III.3
S/N Mask and Completeness

S/N Mask.
For statistical quantities that only involve
\alpha_{\rm CO}
, e.g. mean values and percentiles, we masked out the low signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) pixels. Specifically, we blank pixels with
\mathrm{S/N}<1
in
W_{\mathrm{CO}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
. Note that
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
here is derived from
\Sigma_{\mathrm{dust}}
(with IR photometry), metallicity and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{atom}}
(
Eq. 4
), thus the uncertainty of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
is propagated from the uncertainties of these three quantities and the IR photometry.

Completeness.
For statistical quantities that involve
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and another quantity (
X
), e.g. correlations and linear regression, in addition to the S/N mask, we only calculate with data with high (
>50\%
) completeness in
X
as the trend confidence criteria. The completeness for data with
X_{i}\leq X<X_{f}
, or
[X_{i},~X_{f})
, is defined as:

Completeness\equiv\frac{{\rm num~of~S/N~>~1}~pixels~with~[X_{i},~X_{f})}{{\rm num~of~all~pixels~with}~[X_{i},~X_{f})},

(15)

where the definition of “S/N ¿ 1” is the same as the S/N mask earlier this subsection. We show the completeness and the
50\%
threshold in our data set in
Figure 1
. For most quantities, the CO
(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)
data has a better completeness coverage than the CO
(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)
data. Note that at the high-
\overline{U}
end, the completeness fluctuates around 50%. We treat all data with
\log\overline{U}>0.75
as incomplete for simplicity.

VI
Discussion

VI.1
General suggestions for
\alpha_{\rm CO}
prescriptions

In
Section IV.1
, we present how the measured
\alpha_{\rm CO}
correlates with local physical quantities and provide linear regression for each quantity at 2 kpc scale in
Table 1
. These measurements consider the statistical behavior of the overall sample. Among the quantities,
W_{\mathrm{CO}}
,
\overline{U}
, and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
usually have the strongest correlations with
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and smallest
\Delta_{\mathrm{rms}}
from linear regression. We would suggest the readers go with these parameters if the parameter space of their sample overlaps with this study (see
Figure 1
for the completeness of each quantity).

Meanwhile, we explicitly explore the relation between
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and
\Sigma_{\star}
in
Section V
as a possible tracer for starburst
\alpha_{\rm CO}
at 2 kpc scale, and find strong correlation between
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and
\Sigma_{\star}
after normalization at some
\Sigma_{\star}
threshold. There are two ways to adopt these results. The first one is a stand-alone prescription combining the indices from
Section V.1
and normalization from
Table 3
, using galaxy-averaged
\overline{U}_{\rm gal}
as an example:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)}=10^{-0.4\log(\overline{U}_{\rm gal})+1.0}\Sigma_{\star}^{-0.48}\\
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)}=10^{-0.7\log(\overline{U}_{\rm gal})+0.8}\Sigma_{\star}^{-0.22}\end{array},~\Sigma_{\star}\geq 100~\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2},\right.

(18)

where
\alpha_{\rm CO}
is in unit of
{\rm M_{\odot}\,pc^{-2}\,(K\,km\,s^{-1})^{-1}}
, and the
\overline{U}_{\rm gal}
-dependent normalization could be replaced with other quantities listed in
Table 3
, e.g. ¡
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
¿. Please refer to
Section V
for relevant uncertainties. We note that the possible variation of the power-law index could be up to
\sim 0.2
due to internal variations of D/M (
Appendix A
).

On the other hand, one key mechanism that sets
\alpha_{\rm CO}
, the CO-dark gas, is likely not parameterized by our formula (see
Section VI.2
). This is because the CO-dark gas effect is relatively weak in the metallicity span of our sample. However, both the CO-dark gas and the “starburst
\alpha_{\rm CO}
” effects should be considered for an
\alpha_{\rm CO}
prescription to be applied through all environments. Thus, another suggestion we have is to make a
Bolatto et al. 2013
-style combination (also see E. Schinnerer & A. K. Leroy ARA&A submitted) of our
\Sigma_{\star}
power-law term with existing
\alpha_{\rm CO}
prescriptions tracing the CO-dark gas effect, e.g.
Wolfire et al. 2010
,
Narayanan et al. 2012
,
Schruba et al. 2012
,
Hunt et al. 2015
,
Accurso et al. 2017
, or
Sun et al. 2020
. That is, assuming the adopted existing CO-dark prescription is
\alpha_{\rm CO}^{\mathrm{CO\text{-}dark}}
, we suggest:

\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{\rm CO}^{\mathrm{CO\text{-}dark}}&,~\Sigma_{\star}<60~\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}\\
\alpha_{\rm CO}^{\mathrm{CO\text{-}dark}}\Sigma_{\star}^{-0.48}&,~\Sigma_{\star}\geq 60~\rm M_{\odot}~pc^{-2}\end{array}\right..

(19)

Under this functional form, we expect the normalization (
\alpha_{\rm CO,gal,T}
) to be taken into account by the
\alpha_{\rm CO}^{\mathrm{CO\text{-}dark}}
term. Since this formula does not include our own normalization, the
\Sigma_{\star}
power-law can be extended to lower
\Sigma_{\star}
threshold. For
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)}
case, one can simply replace the power-law index with
-0.22
.

One of the future directions we will take is to study how
\alpha_{\rm CO}
correlates with physical quantities at cloud scales instead of kpc scales and build
\alpha_{\rm CO}
prescriptions accordingly.
The advantage in this direction is that the physical quantities at cloud scale are more strongly linked to fundamental physics of CO emission and dynamics of molecular clouds.
For instance, based on the
\alpha_{\rm CO}
measurements in this work,
Teng et al. 2023a
have reported
\alpha_{\rm CO}
dependence with cloud-scale velocity dispersion which likely traces CO opacity change. We also refer the readers to
Teng et al. 2022
;
Teng et al. 2023b
for more details.

The other possible future direction for dust-based
\alpha_{\rm CO}
is a new strategy that simultaneously allows variations in
\alpha_{\rm CO}
, dust properties (e.g. D/M in
Appendix A
and dust opacity in
Section III.1
) and metallicity at best-possible resolution. The
Leroy et al. 2011
and
Sandstrom et al. 2013
strategy is a good demonstration of the concept for most items on the list except the resolution. A more sophisticated strategy would help identify the next step forward on
\alpha_{\rm CO}
prescriptions. We are also interested in investigating whether the
\Sigma_{\star}
-dependence still applies to
\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}
-dominated environments.

VI.2
Interpreting of the environmental dependence of
\alpha_{\rm CO}

In this section, we will discuss the physical interpretations of the correlations between
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and the physical quantities we present in the previous sections. As we mentioned in
Section I
, we expect two main trends in the variation of
\alpha_{\rm CO}
: (1) the “CO-dark gas” trend, where
\alpha_{\rm CO}
increases toward lower metallicity as shielding for CO weakens; (2) the “starburst conversion factor” trend, where
\alpha_{\rm CO}
decreases toward galaxy centers and U/LIRGs with the decrease in CO optical depth or increase in CO excitation.

Regarding the CO-dark gas trend, we observe moderate to weak anti-correlation between
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and
12+\log({\rm O/H})
at kpc scale (
Section IV.1
), and moderate anti-correlation at galaxy scale (
Section IV.2
). One possible explanation for the weak correlation is that the statistical significance becomes weaker with the small dynamic range of our
12+\log({\rm O/H})
data: 80% of the
12+\log({\rm O/H})
measurements fall within a 0.2 dex range from 8.4 to 8.6. The
\alpha_{\rm CO}
-
\Sigma_{\star}
relation we present in
Section V
is unlikely caused by this CO-dark gas effect since the dynamic range in
12+\log({\rm O/H})
is even smaller for data above the
\Sigma_{\rm\star,T}
threshold. Another explanation is that the CO-dark gas effect is weaker at nearly solar metallicity
(
Wolfire et al. 2010
;
Glover & Mac Low 2011
;
Hunt et al. 2015
, e.g.)
. However, we note that recent simulations show that there is significant fraction of CO-dark gas (
f_{\mathrm{dark}}
) up to solar metallicity, e.g.
Gong et al. 2018
found
f_{\mathrm{dark}}
ranges from 26%–79%. These studies found that
f_{\mathrm{dark}}
correlates with extinction and/or
W_{\mathrm{CO}}

(
Smith et al. 2014
;
Gong et al. 2018
;
Gong et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2022
)
.

We interpret
\overline{U}
and (r)sSFR as empirical tracers for regions with high SFR, where the “starburst conversion factor” trend matters and lowers down
\alpha_{\rm CO}
. Some studies have also argued that
\alpha_{\rm CO}
could decrease with increased radiation field due to CO dissociation
(
Israel 1997b
;
Wolfire et al. 2010
;
Accurso et al. 2017
)
. However, we did not observe this trend, and one possible explanation is that the CO dissociation effect should be weak as long as the CO emission is optically thick
(
Wolfire et al. 2010
;
Bolatto et al. 2013
)
.
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
, which simultaneously traces the UV radiation and starburst regions, also have moderate anti-correlation with
\alpha_{\rm CO}
across all cases. In general, we observe a stronger correlation between
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
and
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)}
than
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)}
. We also observe moderate anti-correlation between
W_{\mathrm{CO}}
and
\alpha_{\rm CO}
. This is consistent with the theoretical assumption under optically thick assumption
Bolatto et al. 2013
and recent observations
(
Hunt et al. 2023
)
.

We interpret the
\alpha_{\rm CO}
-to-
\Sigma_{\star}
anti-correlation as the increase of velocity dispersion of molecular gas from additional gravity.
Bolatto et al. 2013
suggested that in high-
\Sigma_{\star}
environments,
the molecular gas experiences gravitational potential from stellar sources, ending up with a total pressure larger than isolated, virialized clouds. This larger pressure results in gas line width larger than one would expect from self-gravitating clouds. This increase in gas line width scales with total mass (stars and gas) in the system, or
\alpha_{\rm CO}\propto\big(M_{\mathrm{mol}}/(M_{\mathrm{mol}}+M_{\star})\big)^{0.5}
. The above functional form approximates a
\alpha_{\rm CO}
-to-
\Sigma_{\star}
power law in
M_{\star}
-dominated regions. For the argument to hold, the CO emission must be optically thick.
Bolatto et al. 2013
mentioned that the only possible structure for molecular gas that satisfies this scenario is an extended molecular medium.

VI.3
Comparing to previous
\alpha_{\rm CO}
surveys

In this section, we compare our measurements to
\alpha_{\rm CO}
values obtained in previous dust-based
\alpha_{\rm CO}
surveys. Since we will cover studies with both
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)}
and
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)}
measurements, we will convert
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)}
values in literature and this work to
\alpha_{\textup{CO}\,(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)}
with
R_{21}=0.65
for simplicity and uniformity.
First, we compare our
\alpha_{\rm CO}
maps with
Sandstrom et al. 2013
. They measured spatially resolved
\alpha_{\rm CO}
in 26 nearby, star-forming galaxies using a dust-based methodology
(
Leroy et al. 2011
;
den Brok et al. 2023
;
Yasuda et al. 2023
, also see)
. They assume that the variation of D/G is minimal in a few-kpc-scale “solution pixel” consisting of 37 samples in a hexagonal region, and fit D/G and
\alpha_{\rm CO}
simultaneously from data by minimizing the variation in D/G.
Sandstrom et al. 2013
used CO
(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)
data from HERACLES
(
Leroy et al. 2009a
)
and
R_{21}=0.7
. We rescale their results with
R_{21}=0.65
for uniformity. Compared to our work, the
Sandstrom et al. 2013
metholodogy has larger degrees of freedom for the spatial variation of D/G and D/M. Meanwhile, it is more difficult to push their methodology to a larger sample size at fixed physical resolution.

There are 13 galaxies that are studied in both
Sandstrom et al. 2013
and this work.
We show the
\alpha_{\rm CO}
measurements from both works as a function of galacto-centric radius (
R_{\mathrm{g}}
in terms of
R_{25}
.) in
Figure 12
. We adopt
R_{25}
values in this work instead of the
Sandstrom et al. 2013
values. The simple mean
\alpha_{\rm CO}
of all measurements in
Sandstrom et al. 2013

\sim 2.3~{\rm M_{\odot}\,pc^{-2}\,(K\,km\,s^{-1})^{-1}}
. Similar with
Sandstrom et al. 2013
, we find a weak to moderate (but significant) positive correlation between
\alpha_{\rm CO}
and
R_{\mathrm{g}}
.
When we normalize the
\alpha_{\rm CO}
in each galaxy by the mean
\alpha_{\rm CO}
in each galaxy (¡
\alpha_{\mathrm{CO,~gal}}
¿), both works show a flat trend with radius in mid- to outer-disk.
Sandstrom et al. 2013
data shows a more significant decrease in
\alpha_{\rm CO}
in the galaxy center. Several factors could contribute to the difference in the inner most radial bins. If we calculate the
W_{\mathrm{CO}}
-weighted mean instead of the median in each bin, the difference in the bin with smallest radii will decrease by 0.1 dex, which partially explains the discrepancy. Another possible explanation is that some of the measurements with small
\Sigma_{\mathrm{mol}}
(and possibly small
\alpha_{\rm CO}
) are removed due to small S/N; however, they are taken into account in
Sandstrom et al. 2013
. It is not clear whether the difference in resolution is a cause. When we calculate
\alpha_{\rm CO}
at 1 kpc resolution with the galaxies with distance within 10 Mpc, there is no clear trend of the resulting
\alpha_{\rm CO}
with resolution. The fixed D/M is unlikely to be a major cause since the

Summary

In this work, we measure the spatially resolved CO-to-H

within D/M=0.4–0.7, the D/M values for inner disk inferred from literature. The power-law index is insensitive to the assumed D/M, and it is roughly constant at

When fitting the power-law relation within individual galaxies, we find significant dependence of the normalization of
\alpha_{\rm CO}

This decrease of
\alpha_{\rm CO}
in the high-
\Sigma_{\star}
region is likely due to the increased CO brightness with increased line width. The line width is larger than self-gravitating clouds due to the additional gravity from stellar sources, and the structure satisfying this scenario is likely an extended molecular medium. Understanding the decrease in
\alpha_{\rm CO}
at high
\Sigma_{\star}
is important for accurately assessing molecular gas content and star-formation efficiency in the centers of galaxies and bridges the “MW-like” to ”starburst” conversion factor.

Appendix A
Internal variation of D/M

It is questionable whether D/M is a constant within galaxies, even in galaxy centers. Observations have found internal variations of D/M within galaxies
(
Jenkins 2009
;
Roman-Duval et al. 2014
;
Roman-Duval et al. 2017
;
Chiang et al. 2018
;
Vílchez et al. 2019
)
. In these studies, people found a higher D/M toward higher metallicity or gas surface densities. A varying D/M within galaxies is also expected by several models
(
Hou et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Aoyama et al. 2020
)
. However, how to characterize the variation of D/M with local condition is a topic remained unsolved and it is outside the main scope of this work.

To demonstrate how a varying D/M might affect our results, we define a toy model with D/M increasing toward galaxy centers. For the galaxy disks (
R_{\mathrm{g}}>R_{\mathrm{e}}
), we assume
\mathrm{D/M}=0.4
. This value is inspired by several recent studies, e.g.
\sim 0.5
in
Draine et al. 2014
,
0.5\pm 0.1
in
Clark et al. 2016
,
0.4\pm 0.2
in
Clark et al. 2019
, and
0.46^{+0.12}_{-0.06}
in
Chiang et al. 2021
. For the very center of the galaxies (
R_{\mathrm{g}}=0
), we assume an efficient dust growth, i.e. all refractory elements are completely depleted and gaseous elements (e.g. oxygen and nitrogen) are partially depleted, and adopted
\mathrm{D/M}=0.7
from
Feldmann 2015
. In
R_{\mathrm{e}}\geq R_{\mathrm{g}}\geq 0
, we assume a smooth transition, that is:

\mathrm{D/M}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0.7-0.3\times R_{\mathrm{g}}/R_{\mathrm{e}}&,~R_{\mathrm{g}}\leq R_{\mathrm{e}}\\
0.4&,~R_{\mathrm{g}}>R_{\mathrm{e}}\end{array}\right..

(A1)

With this toy model, we find
a=-0.74^{+0.06}_{-0.08}
and
-0.47^{+0.04}_{-0.05}
for CO
(2{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)
and CO
(1{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}0)
, respectively. These indices are steeper than our fiducial case, i.e. constant D/M, indicating that the
\alpha_{\rm CO}
-to-
\Sigma_{\star}
relation observed in
Section V
is not caused by the variation in dust properties. Although we do expect an internal variation of D/M, the variation in the galaxy center predicted in simulations is more gentle than our toy model
(
Romano et al. 2022
;
Choban et al. 2022
)
. Thus the calculation with this toy model should be interpreted as an extreme case. The constant D/M case and the toy model case should sandwich the actual indices.
