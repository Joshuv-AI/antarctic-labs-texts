Synchrony and Oscillatory Dynamics for a 2-D PDE-ODE Model of Diffusion-Sensing with Small Signaling Compartments
Sarafa A. Iyaniwura, Michael J. Ward

Abstract
We analyze a class of cell-bulk coupled PDE-ODE models, motivated by quorum and diffusion sensing phenomena in microbial systems, that characterize communication between localized spatially segregated dynamically active signaling compartments or “cells” that have a permeable boundary. In this model, the cells are disks of a common radius ε ≪ 1 \varepsilon\ll 1 and they are spatially coupled through a passive extracellular bulk diffusion field with diffusivity D D in a bounded 2-D domain. Each cell secretes a signaling chemical into the bulk region at a constant rate and receives a feedback of the bulk chemical from the entire collection of cells. This global feedback, which activates signaling pathways within the cells, modifies the intracellular dynamics according to the external environment. The cell secretion and global feedback are regulated by permeability parameters across the cell membrane. For arbitrary reaction-kinetics within each cell, the method of matched asymptotic expansions is used in the limit ε ≪ 1 \varepsilon\ll 1 of small cell radius to construct steady-state solutions of the PDE-ODE model, and to derive a globally coupled nonlinear matrix eigenvalue problem (GCEP) that characterizes the linear stability properties of the steady-states. The analysis and computation of the nullspace of the GCEP as parameters are varied is central to the linear stability analysis. In the limit of large bulk diffusivity D = D 0 / ν ≫ 1 D={D_{0}/\nu}\gg 1 , where ν ≡ − 1 / log ε \nu\equiv{-1/\log\varepsilon} , an asymptotic analysis of the PDE-ODE model leads to a limiting ODE system for the spatial average of the concentration in the bulk region that is coupled to the intracellular dynamics within the cells. Results from the linear stability theory and ODE dynamics are illustrated for Sel’kov reaction-kinetics, where the kinetic parameters are chosen so that each cell is quiescent when uncoupled from the bulk medium. For various specific spatial configurations of cells, the linear stability theory is used to construct phase diagrams in parameter space characterizing where a switch-like emergence of intracellular oscillations can occur through a Hopf bifurcation. The effect of the membrane permeability parameters, the reaction-kinetic parameters, the bulk diffusivity, and the spatial configuration of cells on both the emergence and synchronization of the oscillatory intracellular dynamics, as mediated by the bulk diffusion field, is analyzed in detail. The linear stability theory is validated from full numerical simulations of the PDE-ODE system, and from the reduced ODE model when D D is large.



Key Words:
Green’s function, bulk diffusion, globally coupled
eigenvalue problem (GCEP), Hopf bifurcation, cell-bulk coupling,
synchronous oscillations, diffusion-sensing.

The coupled PDE-ODE model of
[

]
is formulated as follows:
Within
\Omega

m

circular signalling compartments or “cells” of a common radius

R_{0}
, denoted by
\Omega_{j}

\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\in\Omega

\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{X},T)

confined within

diffusion equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{T}=

\displaystyle D_{B}\,\Delta\,\mathcal{U}-k_{B}\,\mathcal{U}\,,\quad T>0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{X}\in\Omega\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{m}\,\Omega_{j}\,;

(1.0.1a)

\displaystyle\,\partial_{n_{\boldsymbol{X}}}\,\mathcal{U}=\,0,\quad\boldsymbol{X}\in\partial\Omega\,;

\displaystyle\qquad D_{B}\,\partial_{n_{\boldsymbol{X}}}\,\mathcal{U}=\beta_{1j}\,\mathcal{U}-\beta_{2j}\,\mu_{j}^{1}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{X}\in\partial\Omega_{j}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.

(1.0.1b)

Here
D_{B}>0
and

are the dimensional bulk diffusivity and
rate of degradation of the bulk signal, respectively. In the Robin
boundary condition (
1.0.1b
) on the cell membrane,

\beta_{1j}>0
and
\beta_{2j}>0
are dimensional parameters modeling
the influx and efflux of chemical into and out of the
j^{\text{th}}

cell, while
\partial_{n_{\boldsymbol{X}}}
denotes the outer normal
derivative on the cell boundary that points into the bulk region.
Within each cell we assume that there are
n
interacting species
represented by the vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}=(\mu_{j}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{j}^{n})^{T}
.
Assuming that the cells are sufficiently small so that there are no
spatial chemical gradients within them, the intracellular
reaction-kinetics
\boldsymbol{F}_{j}
for the
j^{\text{th}}
cell is coupled
to the bulk medium via the integration of the flux across the cell
membrane. In this way, the intracellular dynamics within the

j^{\text{th}}
cell is coupled to the bulk signal (
1.0.1
) by

\begin{split}\frac{d\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}{dT}&=k_{R}\,\mu_{c}\,\boldsymbol{F}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}/\mu_{c}\right)+\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\int_{\partial\Omega_{j}}\,\left(\beta_{1j}\,\mathcal{U}-\beta_{2j}\,\mu_{j}^{1}\right)\,\text{d}S_{\boldsymbol{X}}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.\end{split}

(1.0.1c)

Here
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
,
k_{R}>0
is the dimensional
reaction rate for the intracellular kinetics, and
\mu_{c}>0
is a
typical value for
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}
. In this model, one signaling
chemical, labeled by
\mu_{j}^{1}
can permeate the cell membrane with an
efflux parameter
\beta_{2j}
and, by diffusion through the bulk
medium, can communicate with spatially distant cells. The influx
permeability parameter
\beta_{1j}
controls the global feedback into
the
j^{\mbox{th}}
cell from the bulk diffusion field, which is
determined by the entire collection of cells. In
Fig.
1
we schematically illustrate the cell-bulk
coupling for the case of
n=2
intracellular species.

We assume that the radius
R_{0}
of the signaling compartments is small
relative to the domain length-scale
L
, and so we introduce a small
parameter
\varepsilon\equiv R_{0}/L\ll 1
. Then, by non-dimensionalizing
the coupled PDE-ODE model (
1.0.1
) using the approach of

[
20
]
, we obtain that the dimensionless concentration of
chemical
U(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\,D\,\Delta U-\,U\,,\quad t>0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{m}\,\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\,;

(1.0.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\,U=\,0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;

\displaystyle\qquad\varepsilon D\,\partial_{n}U=d_{1j}\,U-d_{2j}\,u_{j}^{1}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,

(1.0.2b)

which is coupled to the dimensionless dynamics within the
j^{\text{th}}
cell by

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle=\,\boldsymbol{F}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\right)+\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}{\varepsilon\tau}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}}\,(d_{1j}\,U-d_{2j}\,u_{j}^{1})\,\,\text{d}s\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,

(1.0.2c)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}=(u_{j}^{1},\ldots,u_{j}^{n})^{T}
is the dimensionless vector
representing the
n
chemical species in the
j^{\text{th}}
cell, labeled by

\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\equiv\{{\boldsymbol{x}\,|\,\,|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|\leq\varepsilon\}}
. We assume that the
centers of the cells are well-separated in the sense that

\mbox{dist}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})={\mathcal{O}}(1)
for

and

\mbox{dist}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\partial\Omega)={\mathcal{O}}(1)
as

\varepsilon\to 0
. In this dimensionless formulation

1.0.2
), the key dimensionless parameters are

D\equiv\frac{D_{B}}{k_{B}L^{2}}\,,\qquad d_{1j}\equiv\varepsilon\frac{\beta_{1j}}{k_{B}L}={\mathcal{O}}(1)\,,\qquad d_{2j}\equiv\varepsilon\frac{\beta_{2j}L}{k_{B}}={\mathcal{O}}(1)\,,\qquad\tau\equiv\frac{k_{R}}{k_{B}}\,.

(1.0.3)

We refer to

and

reaction-time parameter, the bulk diffusivity, a Sel’kov kinetic

2
Asymptotic analysis of the dimensionless coupled model

In this section, strong localized perturbation theory in the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
is applied to the dimensionless PDE-ODE model
(
1.0.2
) for the regime
D=\mathcal{O}(1)
. This theory
is used to asymptotically approximate the steady-state solution of the
coupled model, and also to formulate a globally coupled eigenvalue
problem (GCEP) for studying the linear stability properties of the
derived steady-state solution.

2.1
Asymptotic construction of the steady-state
solution

We construct the steady-state solution for (
1.0.2
) using
matched asymptotics. In the
j^{\text{th}}
inner region,
defined within an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
neighborhood of the
boundary of the
j^{\text{th}}
cell, we introduce the local variables

\boldsymbol{y}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
and

U_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=U_{j}(\varepsilon\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{x_{j}})
, where

\rho\equiv|\boldsymbol{y}|
. Upon writing (
1.0.2
) in terms
of the inner variables, for
\varepsilon\to 0
the steady-state
problem in the
j^{\text{th}}
inner region is

\Delta U_{j}=0
for
\rho\geq 1
, subject to

D\,\partial_{\rho}U_{j}=d_{1j}U_{j}-d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}
on

\rho=1
. The radially symmetric solution to this problem is

\begin{split}U_{j}(\rho)&=A_{j}\log\rho+\frac{1}{d_{1j}}\left(D\,A_{j}+d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}\right)\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,\end{split}

(2.1.1)

where
A_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
are constants to be determined. Upon
substituting (
2.1.1
) into the steady-state problem of
(
1.0.2c
), we obtain that the steady-state intracellular
dynamics
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
of the
j^{\text{th}}
cell satisfies

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{F}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\right)+\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\,A_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\,=\boldsymbol{0}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.\end{split}

(2.1.2)

This determines
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
in terms of the unknown constant

A_{j}
. To proceed, we must derive another algebraic system for the
constants
A_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, which is then coupled to
(
2.1.2
).

By matching the far-field behaviour of the inner solution
(
2.1.1
) to an outer steady-state solution for
(
1.0.2a
), we obtain that the outer solution must satisfy
a specific singularity behaviour as
\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
. In this
way, the steady-state outer approximation for the bulk solution satisfies

\begin{split}\Delta U-\varphi^{2}\,U&=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\setminus\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m}\}\,;\qquad\partial_{n}U=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;\\
U\sim A_{j}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|&+\frac{A_{j}}{\nu}+\frac{1}{d_{1j}}(DA_{j}+d_{2j}u_{j}^{1})\,,\quad\text{as}\quad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,\end{split}

(2.1.3)

where
\nu\equiv{-1/\log\varepsilon}
, with
\varepsilon\ll 1
,
and
\varphi\equiv\sqrt{1/D}
. The pre-specification of the regular
part of each singularity structure in (
2.1.3
) yields a
constraint. Overall these constraints provide an algebraic system for

A_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
.

To determine this algebraic system, we next represent the solution to
(
2.1.3
) as

\begin{split}U=-2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}G(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,,\end{split}

(2.1.4)

where the reduced-wave Green’s function
G(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
satisfies

\begin{split}\Delta G\,-\,&\varphi^{2}G=-\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\,;\qquad\partial_{n}G=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;\\
G(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})&\sim-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|+R(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})+o(1)\,,\quad\text{as}\quad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,.\end{split}

(2.1.5)

Here
R_{j}\equiv R(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the regular part of

G(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
. By expanding
(
2.1.4
) as
\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
, we simply require that the
non-singular terms of the resulting expression agree with that
specified in (
2.1.3
) for each
j=1,\ldots,m
. This leads to an
algebraic system for the vector

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\equiv(A_{1},\ldots,A_{m})^{T}
, which is given in
matrix form as

\begin{split}\Big(I+2\pi\nu\mathcal{G}+\nu DP_{1}\Big)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=-\nu\,P_{2}\,\boldsymbol{u}^{1}\,,\end{split}

(2.1.6)

where
\boldsymbol{u}^{1}\equiv(u_{1}^{1},u_{2}^{1},\ldots,u_{m}^{1})^{T}
denotes the vector of
chemicals that is secreted into the bulk region by the cells. In
(
2.1.6
),
\mathcal{G}
is the symmetric reduced-wave
Green’s interaction matrix, while
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are
m\times m

diagonal matrices, defined by

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}R_{1}&G_{12}&\dots&G_{1m}\\
G_{21}&R_{2}&\dots&G_{2m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
G_{m1}&G_{m2}&\dots&R_{m}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad P_{1}\equiv\text{diag}\Big(\frac{1}{d_{11}},\ldots,\frac{1}{d_{1m}}\Big)\,,\quad P_{2}\equiv\text{diag}\Big(\frac{d_{21}}{d_{11}},\ldots,\frac{d_{2m}}{d_{1m}}\Big)\,.\end{split}

(2.1.7)

Here
G_{ji}=G_{ij}\equiv G(\boldsymbol{x}_{j};\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
for
i\neq j
,
and
R_{j}\equiv R(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, are obtained
from the solution to (
2.1.5
).

Overall, the asymptotic steady-state solution is determined in terms
of the solution
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
for

j=1,\ldots,m
, to the
n\times m
dimensional nonlinear algebraic
system (NAS) given by (
2.1.2
) and (
2.1.6
). This system
applies to arbitrary local reaction kinetics
\boldsymbol{F}_{j}
and
permeabilities parameters
d_{1j}>0
and
d_{2j}>0
for

j=1,\ldots,m
. When the kinetics and permeability parameters are
identical for all the cells, the NAS reduces to the system given in
equations
(2.4)
and
(2.9)
of
[
20
]
.

Depending on the specific reaction kinetics assumed, the solution
structure to (
2.1.2
) and (
2.1.6
) as parameters are
varied can involve solution multiplicity, saddle-node points, and
other bifurcations. However, to illustrate our asymptotic theory we
will focus on the two-component Sel’kov reaction kinetics for which
(
2.1.2
) and (
2.1.6
) has a unique solution.

2.2
Linear stability analysis

In the previous subsection, we characterized steady-state solutions of
the coupled model (
1.0.2
) using strong perturbation
theory. Suppose that the NAS (
2.1.2
) and (
2.1.6
)
has a solution for a given set of parameters. This then yields an
approximation to the steady-state solution solution
U_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})
and

\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}
, for
j=1,\ldots,m
, to (
1.0.2
). To
determine the linear stability of this steady-state we begin by
introducing the perturbation

\begin{split}U(\boldsymbol{x},t)=U_{e}(\boldsymbol{x})+e^{\lambda t}\xi(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(t)&=\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}+e^{\lambda t}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,m\,.\end{split}

(2.2.1)

where
\lambda
is the eigenvalue of the linearization, and

\xi(\boldsymbol{x})
and

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\equiv(\phi_{j}^{1},\ldots,\phi_{j}^{n})^{T}
are the
corresponding eigenfunctions in the bulk region and in the

j^{\text{th}}
cell, respectively. Upon substituting this
perturbation into the PDE-ODE model (
1.0.2
), in the bulk
region we obtain the linearized problem

\displaystyle\tau\lambda\xi=

\displaystyle\,D\,\Delta\xi-\,\xi\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{m}\,\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\,;

(2.2.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\,\xi=\,0,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;

\displaystyle\qquad\varepsilon D\,\partial_{nj}\xi=d_{1j}\,\xi-d_{2j}\,\phi_{j}^{1}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,

(2.2.2b)

which is coupled to the linearized intracellular dynamics of the

j^{\text{th}}
cell given in terms of

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}\equiv(\phi_{j}^{1},\ldots,\phi_{j}^{n})^{T}
, by

\displaystyle\lambda\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}

\displaystyle=J_{j}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}+\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}{\varepsilon\tau}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}}(d_{1j}\,\xi-d_{2j}\,\phi_{j}^{1})\,\text{d}s\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.

(2.2.2c)

Here
J_{j}\equiv J(\boldsymbol{u}_{ej})
is the Jacobian matrix of the local
kinetics
\boldsymbol{F}_{j}
evaluated at the steady-state
\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}
.

Next, we use strong localized perturbation theory to analyze the
eigenvalue problem (
2.2.2
) in the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
. This analysis leads to a limiting globally
coupled eigenvalue problem (GCEP) for
\lambda
in the form of a
nonlinear matrix eigenvalue problem. This GCEP will be used to
investigate instabilities of the steady-state solution for the PDE-ODE
system (
1.0.2
).

To derive this GCEP, we first construct an inner region in an

\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
neighborhood of the
j^{\text{th}}
cell by
introducing the local variables

\boldsymbol{y}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
and

\xi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\xi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{y})
with

\rho=|\boldsymbol{y}|
. From (
2.2.2
), we obtain for

\varepsilon\to 0
that

\displaystyle\Delta\,\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=0,\quad 1<\rho<\infty;\qquad D\,\partial_{\rho}\,\xi_{j}=d_{1j}\xi_{j}-d_{2j}\phi_{j}^{1},\quad\text{on}\quad\rho=1,

(2.2.3)

in the
j^{\text{th}}
inner region. The radially symmetric solution to
(
2.2.3
) is

\begin{split}\xi_{j}&=c_{j}\log\rho+\frac{1}{d_{1j}}\left(D\,c_{j}+d_{2j}\phi_{j}^{1}\right)\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,\end{split}

(2.2.4)

where
\rho=|\boldsymbol{y}|
and
c_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
are constants to
be determined. Upon substituting (
2.2.4
) into the
linearized intracellular dynamics of the
j^{\text{th}}
cell
(
2.2.2c
), we obtain a linear relation between

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}
and
c_{j}
given by

\begin{split}(J_{j}-\lambda I)\boldsymbol{\phi}_{j}=-\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\,c_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\,.\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.\end{split}

(2.2.5)

Next, by analyzing the outer solution in the bulk region, we will
derive another linear system, which will be coupled to
(
2.2.5
). These two systems will provide the GCEP that is
needed to study the linear stability of the steady-state solution.

To determine this additional linear system, we first match the far-field
behaviour of the inner solution (
2.2.4
) to the outer
solution in order to obtain the singularity behaviour of the outer
solution as
\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
. This yields in the bulk region that

\begin{split}\Delta\xi-\varphi_{\lambda}^{2}\,\xi&=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\setminus\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m}\}\,;\qquad\partial_{n}\xi=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;\\
\xi\sim c_{j}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|&+\frac{c_{j}}{\nu}+\frac{1}{d_{1j}}(Dc_{j}+d_{2j}\phi_{j}^{1})\,,\quad\text{as}\quad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,\end{split}

(2.2.6)

where
\nu\equiv{-1/\log\varepsilon}
and

\varphi_{\lambda}=\sqrt{(1+\tau\lambda)/D}
. The solution to
(
2.2.6
) is represented as

\begin{split}\xi(\boldsymbol{x})=-2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\,G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,,\end{split}

(2.2.7)

where the eigenvalue-dependent reduced-wave Green’s function

G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta G_{\lambda}-

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}^{2}G_{\lambda}=-\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\,;\qquad\partial_{n}G_{\lambda}=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;

(2.2.8a)

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|+R_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})+o(1)\,,\quad\text{as}\quad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,.

(2.2.8b)

Here
R_{\lambda j}\equiv R_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the regular part
of
G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
. In
(
2.2.7
) we have specified the principal branch of

\varphi_{\lambda}
to ensure that
G_{\lambda}
is analytic in

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
and that
G_{\lambda}
decays far away
from the cells.

By expanding (
2.2.7
) as
\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
, we
equate the non-singular terms of the resulting expression with those
specified in (
2.2.6
) for each
j=1,\ldots,m
. This
yields a linear system for
\boldsymbol{c}\equiv(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})^{T}
given in
matrix form by

\begin{split}\big(I+2\pi\nu\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}+\nu\,DP_{1}\big)\,\boldsymbol{c}=-\nu\,P_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}^{1}\,,\end{split}

(2.2.9)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{1}\equiv(\phi_{1}^{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}^{1})^{T}
and

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is the eigenvalue-dependent reduced-wave
Green’s matrix whose entries are defined by

(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})_{ij}=(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})_{ji}\equiv G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\quad\text{for}\quad i\neq j\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\ (\mathcal{G}_{\lambda})_{jj}=R_{\lambda j}\equiv R_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,.

(2.2.10)

In (
2.2.9
), the diagonal matrices
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are
defined in (
2.1.7
).

Next, we will combine the algebraic system (
2.2.9
) with
(
2.2.5
) in order to derive the GCEP for
\lambda
and

\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})^{T}
. We first use (
2.2.5
) to determine

\phi_{j}^{1}
in terms of the constant
c_{j}
as

\phi_{j}^{1}=2\pi D\tau^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{T}(\lambda I-J_{j})^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\,c_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, provided that
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of
J_{j}

for any
j=1,\ldots,m
. In matrix form this yields

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{1}=\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\mathcal{K}\boldsymbol{c}\,,\end{split}

(2.2.11)

where
\mathcal{K}\equiv\mathcal{K}(\lambda)
is an
m\times m

diagonal matrix
\mathcal{K}\equiv\mbox{diag}\left(\mathit{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{K}_{m}\right)
, whose entries are given by

\begin{split}\mathit{K}_{j}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{T}(\lambda I-J_{j})^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{T}N_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}{\det(\lambda I-J_{j})}=\frac{(N_{j})_{11}}{\det(\lambda I-J_{j})}\,.\end{split}

(2.2.12a)

Here
N_{j}
is the
n\times n
matrix of cofactors of

(\lambda I-J_{j})
, while
(N_{j})_{11}
is its entry in the first
row and first column given by

(N_{j})_{11}\equiv(N_{j}(\lambda))_{11}=\det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-\left.\frac{\partial F_{j}^{2}}{\partial u_{2}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{e,j}}&\dots&\left.-\frac{\partial F_{j}^{2}}{\partial u_{n}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{e,j}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\left.-\frac{\partial F_{j}^{n}}{\partial u_{2}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{e,j}}&\dots&\lambda-\left.\frac{\partial F_{j}^{n}}{\partial u_{n}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{e,j}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(2.2.12b)

The functions
F_{j}^{2},\ldots,F_{j}^{n}
are the components of the local reaction
kinetics
\boldsymbol{F_{j}}\equiv(F_{j}^{1},\ldots,F_{j}^{n})^{T}
of the
j^{\text{th}}
cell.

Upon substituting (
2.2.11
) into (
2.2.9
), we obtain the

m
-dimensional homogeneous algebraic system

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}(\lambda)\boldsymbol{c}&=\boldsymbol{0}\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\mathcal{M}(\lambda)\equiv I+2\pi\nu\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}+\nu\,D\,P_{1}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}P_{2}\mathcal{K}\,,\end{split}

(2.2.13a)

where
\nu\equiv{-1/\log\varepsilon}
. The homogeneous system
(
2.2.13a
), where
{\mathcal{M}}
is a symmetric but
non-Hermitian matrix, is referred to as the globally coupled
eigenvalue problem (GCEP). The GCEP is a nonlinear matrix eigenvalue
problem for
\lambda
, and it has a nontrivial solution

\boldsymbol{c}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
if and only
\lambda
satisfies

\det\mathcal{M}(\lambda)=0
. We label the set

\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
as the union of all such roots, i.e.

\begin{split}\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})\equiv\{{\lambda\,|\,\det\mathcal{M}(\lambda)=0\}}\,.\end{split}

(2.2.13b)

The parameters in the GCEP (
2.2.13a
) are the bulk
diffusivity
D
, the reaction-timescale
\tau
and the permeabilities

d_{1j}
and
d_{2j}
, for
j=1,\ldots,m
, which are encoded in the
matrices
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
given in (
2.1.7
). Moreover, in
(
2.2.13a
), the effect of the spatial configuration of the
centers
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m}\}\in\Omega
of the cells
and the domain shape
\Omega
arises from both the
eigenvalue-dependent reduced wave Green’s interaction matrix

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
and the steady-state solution, which
determines
{\mathcal{K}}
in (
2.2.12
).

A recent survey of nonlinear matrix eigenvalue problems and available
solution strategies for certain classes of matrices is given in

[
23
]
and
[
4
]
. A range of applications of such
problems, but in simpler contexts where
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
is
either a polynomial or rational function of
\lambda
, are discussed
in
[
3
]
.

Any element
\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
satisfying

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
provides an approximation, valid as

\varepsilon\to 0
, for an unstable discrete eigenvalue of the
linearized system (
2.2.2
). This leads to the following
criterion regarding the linear stability of the steady-state solution.

Proposition 1
.

For
\varepsilon\to 0
, a steady-state
solution to (
1.0.2
) is linearly stable when there are
no roots to
\mbox{det}{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)=0
in

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
, i.e. for all

\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
we have

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)<0
. Moreover, if
{\mathcal{A}}_{e}
and

\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
is a non-degenerate solution to
the NAS (
2.1.2
) and (
2.1.6
), for which
J_{j}
is
non-singular, then
\lambda=0
is not a root of

\mbox{det}{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)=0
.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon\to 0
, any discrete eigenvalue

\lambda
of the linearization (
2.2.1
) corresponds to a
non-trivial solution to (
2.2.2
). Since, for

\varepsilon\to 0
, these discrete eigenvalues comprise the set

\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
in (
2.2.13b
), the steady-state is
linearly stable if all
\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
satisfy

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)<0
. Next, suppose that
{\mathcal{A}}_{e}
and

\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
is a non-degenerate solution to
the NAS (
2.1.2
) and (
2.1.6
). Introducing the
perturbation
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}_{e}+\boldsymbol{\psi}
and

\boldsymbol{u}_{j}=\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}+\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
, where
|\boldsymbol{v}_{j}|\ll 1
and

|\boldsymbol{\psi}|\ll 1
, we linearize (
2.1.2
) and
(
2.1.6
) to obtain

\big(I+2\pi\nu\mathcal{G}+\nu\,DP_{1}\big)\,\boldsymbol{\psi}=-\nu\,P_{2}\,\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\,,\qquad J_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=-\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\,\psi_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\,.\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,

(2.2.14)

where
\boldsymbol{v}^{1}\equiv(v_{1}^{1},\ldots,v_{m}^{1})^{T}
,

\boldsymbol{\psi}=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m})^{T}
and

\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=(v_{j}^{1},\ldots,v_{j}^{n})^{T}
. Assuming that
J_{j}
is invertible,
(
2.2.14
) yields that

\mathcal{J}\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{0}\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathcal{J}\equiv I+2\pi\nu\mathcal{G}+\nu\,D\,P_{1}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}P_{2}\mathcal{K}_{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{K}}_{0}\equiv-\mbox{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{T}J_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{T}J_{m}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\right)\,.

(2.2.15)

We conclude that
\det{\mathcal{J}}\neq 0
owing to the fact that

{\mathcal{A}}_{e}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
is assumed to
be a non-degenerate solution of the NAS (
2.1.2
) and
(
2.1.6
). Finally, since it is readily verified that

\mathcal{J}={\mathcal{M}}(0)
, where
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
is the
GCEP matrix in (
2.2.13a
), we conclude that
\lambda=0
is
not a root of (
2.2.13b
).
∎

Proposition
1
implies that branches of non-degenerate
solutions to the NAS (
2.1.2
) and (
2.1.6
) obtained
as a parameter is varied cannot lose stability through a
zero-eigenvalue crossing of the GCEP (
2.2.13a
). This
simple observation is the motivation for analzying whether stability
can be lost through Hopf bifurcations associated with the
linearization.

In terms of the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{c}
and eigenvalues

\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
of the GCEP (
2.2.13
), we
obtain from (
2.2.1
), (
2.2.7
) and
(
2.2.5
) that the linearization around the bulk and
intracellular steady-state solutions is, for
\varepsilon\to 0
, given
by the superposition

U(\boldsymbol{x},t)\sim U_{e}-\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})}e^{\lambda t}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\right)\,;\qquad\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(t)\sim\boldsymbol{u}_{ej}+\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})}e^{\lambda t}c_{j}\left(\lambda I-J_{j}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,m\,,

(2.2.16)

where each
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})^{T}
depends on the particular
eigenvalue
\lambda
. To relate the diffusive flux into the
j
-th
cell to the components of
\boldsymbol{c}
we use the the inner solutions
(
2.1.1
) and (
2.2.4
). To determine the
effect on the intracellular component that can be transported across
the membrane we calculate
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\equiv u_{j}^{1}
in
(
2.2.16
). This yields that

D\partial_{\rho}U|_{\rho=1}\sim D\left(A_{j}+\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})}c_{j}e^{\lambda t}\right)\,,\qquad u_{j}^{1}\sim u_{ej}^{1}+\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})}\left({\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{c}\right)_{j}e^{\lambda t}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,

(2.2.17)

where

{\mathcal{K}}\equiv\mbox{diag}\left(\mathit{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{K}_{m}\right)
, with
\mathit{K}_{j}
defined in (
2.2.12
).
As evident from (
2.2.17
), and discussed for various
examples in §
4.2
and §
5.1
, the modulus
|({\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{c})_{j}|

and argument
\mbox{arg}({\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{c})_{j}
of the components of
a complex-valued
{\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{c}
, resulting from
pure imaginary eigenvalues with
\mbox{Re}(\lambda)=0
and

\mbox{Im}(\lambda)\neq 0
, determines the relative amplitudes and
phase differences of small scale intracellular oscillations near a
Hopf bifurcation of the steady-state solution.

Our linear stability theory for steady-state solutions of PDE-ODE
model (
1.0.2
) can be applied to any configuration of
cells in an arbitrary 2-D bounded domain and for arbitrary local
reaction kinetics. However, in our illustrations of the theory below
in
section

4
and
section

5
, we will
consider the two-component Sel’kov model, which is used in simple
models of glycolysis (cf.
[
37
]
,
[
44
]
). From
(
1.0.2c
), for an isolated cell with no influx from the
bulk, the intracellular dynamics within the
j^{\text{th}}
cell that
accounts for efflux from the cell boundary is given by

{\text{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}/\text{d}t}=\boldsymbol{F}_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\right)-{2\pi d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}/\tau}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}=(u_{j}^{1},u_{j}^{2})^{T}

and the Sel’kov kinetics
\boldsymbol{F}_{j}(v,w)=(F_{j1}(v,w),F_{j2}(v,w))^{T}
are
defined by

\begin{split}F_{j1}(v,w)=\alpha_{j}w+wv^{2}-v\,,\qquad F_{j2}(v,w)=\zeta_{j}\left[\mu_{j}-\left(\alpha_{j}w+wv^{2}\right)\right]\,.\end{split}

(2.2.18)

The steady-state for this isolated cell is given by

u_{j}^{1}={\mu_{j}/\chi_{j}}
and

u_{j}^{2}={\mu_{j}/\left(\alpha_{j}+(u_{j}^{1})^{2}\right)}
, where

\chi_{j}\equiv 1+{2\pi d_{2j}/\tau}
. The
determinant and trace of the Jacobian
J_{ej}
for this isolated cell
with boundary efflux is

\mbox{det}(J_{ej})=\zeta_{j}\chi_{j}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\chi_{j}^{2}}\right)\,,\qquad\mbox{tr}(J_{ej})=\frac{2\mu_{j}^{2}}{\chi_{j}}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\chi_{j}^{2}}\right)^{-1}-\chi_{j}-\zeta_{j}\left(\alpha_{j}+\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\chi_{j}^{2}}\right)\,.

(2.2.19)

Since
\mbox{det}(J_{ej})>0
, the steady-state for this isolated cell
is linearly stable only if
\mbox{tr}(J_{ej})<0
. We will choose
Sel’kov kinetic parameters
\alpha_{j}
,
\mu_{j}
and
\zeta_{j}
so that
an isolated cell with zero boundary efflux (i.e.
d_{2j}=0
) is
linearly stable, but with parameters rather close to the stability
threshold. A set of such parameters is shown in
Fig.
2(a)
where we verify that

\mbox{tr}(J_{ej})<0
on
0.7<\alpha_{j}<1.0
when
\mu_{j}=2
and

\zeta_{j}=0.15
. The Hopf bifurcation (HB) boundary in the
\alpha_{j}

versus
\mu_{j}
parameter plane, as obtained by setting

\text{tr}(J_{ej})=0
is

\alpha_{j}=-\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\chi_{j}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2\zeta_{j}}\left[-\chi_{j}+\sqrt{\chi_{j}^{2}+\frac{8\zeta_{j}\mu_{j}^{2}}{\chi_{j}}}\right]\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\quad\chi_{j}\equiv 1+\frac{2\pi d_{2j}}{\tau}\,.

(2.2.20)

For an isolated cell, a simple application of the Poincare-Bendixson
theorem shows that when the steady-state is unstable the cell will
have limit cycle oscillations. When there is no boundary efflux,
i.e.
d_{2j}=0
, this parameter range of periodic solutions is given
by the green-shaded region in Fig.
2(b)
. However,
no time-periodic oscillations with Sel’kov kinetics are possible when
the steady-state is linearly stable. In Fig.
2(c)

we show how the HB boundary for an isolated cell depends on the
boundary efflux parameter
d_{2j}
. As expected, for the fixed value

3
A limiting ODE system with global coupling:

D=\mathcal{O}(\nu^{-1})

In this section, we use a singular perturbation approach to reduce the
dimensionless coupled PDE-ODE model (
1.0.2
) to an ODE
system that is valid for the limiting regime

D=\mathcal{O}(\nu^{-1})\gg\mathcal{O}(1)
, where

\nu\equiv{-1/\log\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon\ll 1
. This ODE
system depends weakly on the spatial configuration of the cells and on
the scaled diffusivity
D_{0}={\mathcal{O}}(1)
, defined by

D={D_{0}/\nu}
.

Consider a collection of
m
small cells centered at the points

\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m}
in a 2-D bounded domain
\Omega
. Define

\Omega_{p}\equiv\cup_{j=1}^{m}\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}
as the region
formed by the union of the cells, and the average bulk concentration

\overline{U}=\overline{U}(t)
by

\overline{U}=\frac{1}{|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}|}\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}}U\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,.

(3.0.1)

Our goal is to derive an ODE for

\overline{U}\equiv\overline{U}(t;\nu)
, accurate to

{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)
, which is coupled to the intracellular dynamics of
the cells, as given in (
1.0.2b
). Upon multiplying
(
1.0.2a
) by
1/|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}|
and using the
divergence theorem, we obtain

\tau\overline{U}_{t}+\overline{U}=\frac{2\pi}{|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}|}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}-\frac{d_{1j}}{2\pi\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}}U\,\text{d}s\right)\,,

(3.0.2)

where we used
|\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}|=2\pi\varepsilon

for the perimeter of each cell. Since

|\Omega_{p}|=m\pi\varepsilon^{2}
, we estimate

|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}|=|\Omega|-\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
, so that

|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}|\to|\Omega|
as
\varepsilon\to 0

in (
3.0.2
). Next, by evaluating the integral in
(
1.0.2b
), and using

|\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}|=2\pi\varepsilon
, we obtain

\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}}{\text{d}t}=\boldsymbol{F}_{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{j})-\frac{2\pi\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}{\,\tau}\left(d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}-\frac{d_{1j}}{2\pi\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}}U\,\text{d}s\right)\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.

(3.0.3)

In (
3.0.2
) and (
3.0.3
), we must
estimate the bulk concentration
U\equiv U(\boldsymbol{x},t)
on the

j^{\text{th}}
cell boundary
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}
.

For
D={\mathcal{O}}(\nu^{-1})
we introduce the scaling

D=\frac{D_{0}}{\nu},\quad\text{where}\quad\nu\equiv\frac{-1}{\log\varepsilon}\,,\quad\varepsilon\ll 1\quad\text{and}\quad D_{0}=\mathcal{O}(1)\,.

(3.0.4)

Upon substituting (
3.0.4
) into (
1.0.2
), we
obtain in the bulk region that

\begin{split}\tau U_{t}&=\frac{D_{0}}{\nu}\,\Delta U-\,U\,,\qquad t>0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{m}\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\,;\\
\partial_{n}\,U=\,0,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\,;&\qquad\varepsilon\frac{D_{0}}{\nu}\,\partial_{n}U=d_{1j}\,U-d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.\end{split}

(3.0.5)

In the inner region at an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
neighborhood of
the
j^{\text{th}}
cell, we introduce the inner variables

\boldsymbol{y}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
and

U(\boldsymbol{x})=V_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{y};\nu)
, with

\rho=|\boldsymbol{y}|
. Writing (
3.0.5
) in terms of
these inner variables, we obtain for
\varepsilon\to 0
that

\begin{split}\Delta V_{j}=0,\quad\rho\geq 1\,;\qquad\,\partial_{\rho}\,V_{j}=\frac{\nu}{D_{0}}\Big(\,d_{1j}\,V_{j}-d_{2j}\,u_{j}^{1}\Big)\,,\quad\text{on}\quad\rho=1\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,\end{split}

(3.0.6)

which has the radially symmetric solution

\begin{split}V_{j}=\nu\,b_{j}\log\rho+U^{0}_{j}\,,\qquad\text{where}\qquad b_{j}\equiv\frac{1}{D_{0}}\Big(d_{1j}\,U^{0}_{j}-d_{2j}\,u_{j}^{1}\Big)\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,\end{split}

(3.0.7)

where
V_{j}|_{\rho=1}=U_{j}^{0}
is to be determined. By writing
(
3.0.7
) in the outer
\boldsymbol{x}
variable, and using

|\boldsymbol{y}|=\varepsilon^{-1}|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|
, we obtain the
following asymptotic matching condition for the outer solution in the bulk
region:

\begin{split}U\sim\nu\,b_{j}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|+\Big(\frac{d_{1j}}{D_{0}}+1\Big)\,U^{0}_{j}-\frac{d_{2j}}{D_{0}}\,u_{j}^{1}\,,\qquad\text{as}\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,.\end{split}

(3.0.8)

Since
V_{j}(\rho)=U_{j}^{0}
on
\rho=1
from (
3.0.7
), we
have
\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon j}}U\,\text{d}s=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{2\pi}V_{j}|_{\rho=1}\,\text{d}\theta=2\pi\varepsilon\,U_{j}^{0}
. Then, from (
3.0.2
) and
(
3.0.3
), and recalling (
3.0.7
) for

b_{j}
, we obtain for
\varepsilon\to 0
that

\begin{split}\tau\overline{U}_{t}+\overline{U}&=\frac{2\pi}{|\Omega|}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}-d_{1j}U_{j}^{0}\right)=-\frac{2\pi D_{0}}{|\Omega|}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}\,,\\
\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}}{\text{d}t}-\boldsymbol{F}_{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{j})&=-\frac{2\pi\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}{\,\tau}\left(d_{2j}u_{j}^{1}-d_{1j}U_{j}^{0}\right)=\frac{2\pi D_{0}}{\tau}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}b_{j}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,.\end{split}

(3.0.9)

To complete the derivation of the ODE system we must obtain an
algebraic system for
b_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
from the analysis of the
outer solution. From (
3.0.5
) and
(
3.0.8
), and relating
U_{j}^{0}
to
b_{j}
using
(
3.0.7
), the outer problem for
U(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is

\begin{split}\tau U_{t}&=\frac{D_{0}}{\nu}\,\Delta U-\,U\,,\quad t>0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{m}\Omega_{\varepsilon_{j}}\,;\qquad\partial_{n}\,U=\,0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\,;\\
\qquad U&\sim\nu b_{j}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|+b_{j}\left(1+\frac{D_{0}}{d_{1j}}\right)+\frac{d_{2j}}{d_{1j}}u_{j}^{1}\,,\qquad\text{as}\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,.\end{split}

(3.0.10)

We then expand
U(\boldsymbol{x},t)
as

U(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\overline{U}+\frac{\nu}{D_{0}}\,U_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+\ldots\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\quad\int_{\Omega}U_{1}\,d\boldsymbol{x}=0\,.

(3.0.11)

The zero average constraint on
U_{1}
ensures that
\overline{U}
is
the spatial average of
U
to terms of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)
. Upon
substituting (
3.0.11
) into (
3.0.10
), we
obtain in the sense of distributions that
U_{1}
satisfies

\begin{split}\Delta U_{1}&=\tau\,\overline{U}_{t}+\overline{U}+2\pi D_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\,;\qquad\partial_{n}U_{1}=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;\\
U_{1}&\sim b_{j}D_{0}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|-\frac{D_{0}}{\nu}\overline{U}+\frac{D_{0}}{\nu}\left[b_{j}\Big(1+\frac{D_{0}}{d_{1j}}\Big)+\frac{d_{2j}}{d_{1j}}\,u_{j}^{1}\right]\,,\qquad\text{as}\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,.\end{split}

(3.0.12)

The divergence theorem applied to (
3.0.12
) yields the
ODE given in (
3.0.9
) while the linear system for
b_{j}
,
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, is obtained from the constraints involved with
specifying the form of the regular part of the singularity behavior in
(
3.0.12
).

The solution to (
3.0.12
), with
\int_{\Omega}U_{1}\,d\boldsymbol{x}=0

is written as

U_{1}=-2\pi D_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\,G_{0}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,,

(3.0.13)

where
G_{0}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the unique Neumann Green’s function
satisfying

\displaystyle\Delta G_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}-\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\,;\qquad\partial_{n}G_{0}=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega\,;

(3.0.14a)

\displaystyle G_{0}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{x}_{j})

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|+R_{0j}+o(1)\,,\quad\text{as}\quad\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}\,G_{0}\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{x}=0\,.

(3.0.14b)

Here
R_{0j}
is the regular part of
G_{0}
at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
.
By expanding (
3.0.13
) as
\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
, we enforce
that the nonsingular part of the resulting expression agrees with that
in (
3.0.12
). This yields that

b_{j}\left(1+\frac{D_{0}}{d_{1j}}\right)+2\pi\nu\left(b_{j}R_{0j}+\sum_{i\neq j}^{m}b_{i}G_{0ji}\right)=\overline{U}-\frac{d_{2j}}{d_{1j}}u_{j}^{1}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m\,,

(3.0.15)

where
G_{0ji}=G_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j};\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
. This linear system for

b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
is then coupled to the ODEs given in
(
3.0.9
). Upon writing this ODE system in matrix form we
summarize the result as follows:

Proposition 2
.

Let
\varepsilon\to 0
and assume that

),

is

(3.0.17)

For

terms
to

(3.0.18a)

where the matrix

Analysis of the GCEP

Consider a ring and center cell pattern of

where

. For this pattern, the reduced-wave Green’s matrix

\mathcal{G}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc:c}&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&g_{m}\\

Here

is the
(m-1)\times(m-1)

{\mathcal{G}}_{m-1}\boldsymbol{e}=\omega_{1}\boldsymbol{e}\,,\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\boldsymbol{e}=(1,\dots,1)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m-1}\,,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\omega_{1}\equiv R_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}G(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,.

(4.1.3)

) there is a common interaction, represented

, between each ring cell and the center cell owing to the

\mu_{1}=\mu_{j}
,

\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{j}
and
\zeta_{1}=\zeta_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m-1
. Since
the unique steady-state solution to (

) has the form

{\mathcal{A}}=(A_{c},\ldots,A_{c},A_{m})^{T}
, we readily find that
A_{c}
and

A_{m}
satisfies the
2\times 2
linear system

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\nu D}{d_{1}}+2\pi\nu\omega_{1}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle A_{c}+2\pi\nu g_{m}\,A_{m}=-\nu\mu_{1}\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}\,;

(4.1.4a)

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\nu D}{d_{1m}}+2\pi\nu R_{m}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}\frac{d_{2m}}{d_{1m}}\right)

\displaystyle A_{m}+2\pi\nu\,g_{m}\,(m-1)A_{c}=-\nu\mu_{m}\frac{d_{2m}}{d_{1m}}\,,

(4.1.4b)

where
\omega_{1}
is the eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{G}}_{m-1}
in
(
4.1.3
). In terms of
{\mathcal{A}}=(A_{c},\ldots,A_{c},A_{m})^{T}
, the
steady-state for the intracellular species as obtained from
(
2.2.21a
) is

\begin{split}u_{ej}^{1}=\begin{cases}u_{e1}^{1}\equiv\mu_{1}+\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\,A_{c}\,,&j=1\,,\ldots,m-1\,,\\
u_{em}^{1}\equiv\mu_{m}+\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}\,A_{m}\,,&j=m\,,\end{cases}\,;\qquad u_{ej}^{2}=\begin{cases}u_{e1}^{2}\equiv\frac{\mu_{1}}{\alpha_{1}\,\,+\,\,(u_{e1}^{1})^{2}}\,,&j=1\,,\ldots,m-1\,,\\
u_{em}^{2}\equiv\frac{\mu_{m}}{\alpha_{m}\,\,+\,\,(u_{em}^{1})^{2}}\,,&j=m\,.\end{cases}\end{split}

(4.1.5)

Next, we determine the GCEP for the ring and center cell pattern using
(
2.2.13a
). For this pattern, the GCEP matrix

\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
in (
2.2.13a
) is written as

\mathcal{M}(\lambda)=2\pi\nu\,\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}+\mathcal{M}_{0}\,,

(4.1.6a)

where
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is the eigenvalue-dependent Green’s
matrix, as defined in (
2.2.10
), and where the diagonal

\mathcal{M}_{0}
is defined by

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{0}&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&\\
&&&M_{0}&\\
&&&&M_{m}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\begin{cases}M_{0}\equiv 1+\frac{\nu D}{d_{1}}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}\mathit{K}_{c}\,,\\
\\
M_{m}\equiv 1+\frac{\nu D}{d_{1m}}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}\frac{d_{2m}}{d_{1m}}\mathit{K}_{m}\,.\end{cases}\end{split}

(4.1.6b)

Here
\mathit{K}_{c}=\mathit{K}_{c}(\lambda)
and

\mathit{K}_{m}=\mathit{K}_{m}(\lambda)
are the entries of the

m\times m
diagonal matrix

\mathcal{K}=\text{diag}(\mathit{K}_{c},\dots,\mathit{K}_{c},\mathit{K}_{m})
defined in
(
2.2.12a
). For the Sel’kov kinetics given in
(
2.2.18
), (
2.2.12a
) yields

\mathit{K}_{c}\equiv\frac{\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{1})}{\lambda^{2}-\mbox{tr}(J_{1})\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{1})}\,,\qquad\mathit{K}_{m}\equiv\frac{\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{m})}{\lambda^{2}-\mbox{tr}(J_{m})\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{m})}\,,

(4.1.7a)

where the trace and determinant of the Jacobians of the intracellular
dynamics for the identical ring cells and the center cell are given in
terms of the steady-state values in (
4.1.5
) by

\begin{split}\mbox{det}(J_{1})=\zeta_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}+(u_{e1}^{1})^{2}\right)\,,\qquad\mbox{det}(J_{m})&=\zeta_{m}\left(\alpha_{m}+(u_{em}^{1})^{2}\right)\,,\\
\mbox{tr}(J_{1})=\frac{\left[2(u_{e1}^{1})\mu_{1}-\left(\alpha_{1}+(u_{e1}^{1})^{2}\right)-\zeta_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}+(u_{e1}^{1})^{2}\right)^{2}\right]}{\alpha_{1}+(u_{e1}^{1})^{2}}\,,\quad\mbox{tr}(J_{m})&=\frac{\left[2(u_{em}^{1})\mu_{m}-\left(\alpha_{m}+(u_{em}^{1})^{2}\right)-\zeta_{m}\left(\alpha_{m}+(u_{em}^{1})^{2}\right)^{2}\right]}{\alpha_{m}+(u_{em}^{1})^{2}}\,.\end{split}

(4.1.7b)

From Proposition
1
, discrete eigenvalues
\lambda

associated with the ring and center cell pattern are roots of

\mbox{det}{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)=0
. A convenient way to implement this
determinant root-finding problem numerically is to use the special
structure of
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
in order to determine
explicit formulae for its matrix spectrum

{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)\boldsymbol{c}_{j}=\sigma_{j}\boldsymbol{c}_{j}
, for
j=1,\ldots,m
, where

\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}(\lambda)
. Then, we need only numerically solve
the scalar root-finding problems
\sigma_{j}(\lambda)=0
for
\lambda

for each
j=1,\ldots,m
.

To do so, we use the convenient fact that
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}
can be
partitioned, similar to that in (
4.1.2
), as

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc:c}&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&g_{\lambda m}\\
&\mathcal{G}_{\lambda(m-1)}&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\vdots\\
&&\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&g_{\lambda m}\\
\hline\cr[2pt/2pt]g_{\lambda m}&\dots&g_{\lambda m}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&R_{\lambda m}\end{array}\right)\,;\qquad g_{\lambda m}\equiv G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}_{m})=G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{m},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,,\quad j=1\,\ldots,m-1\,,\quad R_{\lambda m}\equiv R_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{m})\,,

(4.1.8)

where
G_{\lambda}
is the eigenvalue-dependent reduced-wave Green’s
function with regular part
R_{\lambda}
satisfying
(
2.2.8
). In (
4.1.8
), the
(m-1)\times(m-1)

matrix block
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda(m-1)}
, representing cell
interactions on the ring, is symmetric and cyclic. As a result, it has
the well-defined eigenspace

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda(m-1)}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=\omega_{\lambda j}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{j},\quad j=1,\dots,m-1\,;\qquad\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=0\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,m-2\,,\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{m-1}=\boldsymbol{e}\equiv(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m-1}\,.

(4.1.9)

By using this special matrix structure, it readily follows that the
GCEP matrix
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
in (
4.1.6
) admits
m-2

anti-phase modes
, characterized by eigenvectors of the form

\boldsymbol{c}_{j}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{j},0)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
where
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m-1}

are those eigenvectors of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda(m-1)}
in
(
4.1.9
), which satisfy
\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=0
for

j=1,\ldots,m-2
. With this choice, (
4.1.6
) becomes

\begin{pmatrix}2\pi\nu\mathcal{G}_{\lambda(m-1)}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}+M_{0}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\\
\\
2\pi\nu g_{\lambda m}\,\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\\
\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,m-2\,.

(4.1.10)

Since
\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,m-2
, we obtain from
(
4.1.10
), (
4.1.9
) and (
4.1.6b
)
that
m-2
eigenpairs of
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
are

\sigma_{j}=2\pi\nu\omega_{\lambda j}+M_{0}=2\pi\nu\omega_{\lambda j}+1+\frac{\nu D}{d_{1}}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}\mathit{K}_{c}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{c}_{j}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{j},0)^{T}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\quad j=1,\dots,m-2\,,

(4.1.11)

where
\mathit{K}_{c}=\mathit{K}_{c}(\lambda)
is defined in
(
4.1.7a
). We remark that since
\mathit{K}_{c}
depends on
the steady-state values
A_{c}
and
A_{m}
, as obtained from the linear
system (
4.1.4
), this term depends on the
permeabilities and local kinetics of the center cell. Discrete
eigenvalues
\lambda
for the anti-phase modes are union of the zeroes
of
\sigma_{j}(\lambda)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,m-2
.

For the remaining two eigenpairs of the GCEP matrix

{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
the associated eigenvector
\boldsymbol{c}
has the
form
\boldsymbol{c}=(\boldsymbol{e},\gamma)^{T}
for some scalar
\gamma
to be
determined and
\boldsymbol{e}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m-1}
. This eigenvector
is referred to as the
in-phase mode
since any instability
associated with this mode has the same phase for the cells on the ring.
With this choice, (
4.1.6
) reduces to

\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}+{M_{0}/(2\pi\nu)}&g_{\lambda m}\\
(m-1)g_{\lambda m}&R_{\lambda m}+{M_{m}/(2\pi\nu)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\gamma\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\sigma}{2\pi\nu}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\gamma\end{pmatrix}\,,

(4.1.12)

where

{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda(m-1)}\boldsymbol{e}=\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}\boldsymbol{e}
from
(
4.1.9
).

Upon eliminating
\sigma
from the
2\times 2
matrix problem
(
4.1.12
) we obtain that
\gamma_{\pm}
are the roots of
the quadratic equation

\gamma^{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi\nu g_{\lambda m}}\Big[(M_{0}-M_{m})+2\pi\nu(\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}-R_{\lambda m})\Big]\gamma-(m-1)=0\,,

(4.1.13)

given by

\begin{split}\gamma_{\pm}=-\frac{\beta_{\lambda}}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{\lambda}+4(m-1)}\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\beta_{\lambda}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi\nu g_{\lambda m}}\Big[(M_{0}-M_{m})+2\pi\nu(\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}-R_{\lambda m})\Big]\,.\end{split}

(4.1.14)

Since
\gamma_{+}\gamma_{-}=-(m-1)>0
, but with
\gamma_{\pm}

possibly complex-valued, we confirm that the two possible in-phase
modes
\boldsymbol{c}_{\pm}=(1,\dots,1,\gamma_{\pm})^{T}
are orthogonal. The
two eigenvalues
\sigma=\sigma_{\pm}(\lambda)
, given by

\sigma_{\pm}\equiv 2\pi\nu\big(\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}+\gamma_{\pm}\,g_{\lambda m}\big)+M_{0}
, can be written as

\sigma_{\pm}(\lambda)=\frac{(h_{1}+h_{2})}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(h_{1}-h_{2}\right)^{2}+16\pi^{2}\nu^{2}(m-1)g_{\lambda m}^{2}}\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad h_{1}\equiv 2\pi\nu\,\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}+M_{0}\,,\quad h_{2}\equiv 2\pi\nu\,R_{\lambda m}+M_{m}\,.

(4.1.15)

Here
M_{0}=M_{0}(\lambda)
and
M_{m}=M_{m}(\lambda)
are given in
(
4.1.6b
) and
\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}
is defined by

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda(m-1)}\boldsymbol{e}=\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}\boldsymbol{e}

from (
4.1.9
).

Alternatively, rather than solving (
4.1.12
) for

\sigma_{\pm}
, and then setting
\sigma_{\pm}(\lambda)=0
by using a
root-finder for
\lambda
, we can more directly conclude that

{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)\boldsymbol{c}=\bf{0}
for
\boldsymbol{c}=(\boldsymbol{e},\gamma)^{T}

if and only if the determinant of the
2\times 2
matrix in
(
4.1.12
) vanishes. In this way, a discrete eigenvalue

\lambda
of the GCEP (
2.2.13
) for the in-phase mode,
which satisfies
\mbox{det}({\mathcal{M}}(\lambda))=0
, is a root of

{\mathcal{H}}_{c}(\lambda)=0
defined by

{\mathcal{H}}_{c}(\lambda)=\left(\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}+\frac{M_{0}}{2\pi\nu}\right)\left(R_{\lambda m}+\frac{M_{m}}{2\pi\nu}\right)-(m-1)g_{\lambda m}^{2}\,.

(4.1.16)

For any root of (
4.1.16
), the
corresponding eigenvector
\boldsymbol{c}
of
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
is

\boldsymbol{c}=(1,\ldots,1,\gamma)^{T}\,,\qquad\gamma=-\frac{1}{g_{\lambda m}}\left(\omega_{\lambda(m-1)}+\frac{M_{0}}{2\pi\nu}\right)\,.

(4.1.17)

It is readily verified using (
4.1.15
) that if

\lambda^{\star}
satisfies either
\sigma_{+}(\lambda^{\star})=0
or

\sigma_{-}(\lambda^{\star})=0
, then we must have

{\mathcal{H}}_{c}(\lambda^{\star})=0
. In contrast, if
\lambda^{\star}

satisfies
{\mathcal{H}}_{c}(\lambda^{\star})=0
, then we can only
conclude that
either

\sigma_{+}(\lambda^{\star})=0
or

\sigma_{-}(\lambda^{\star})=0
. Therefore, in implementing a
root-finding strategy based on the single scalar equation
(
4.1.16
) instead of the two scalar equations
(
4.1.15
) care must be taken to identify all possible
roots of
{\mathcal{H}}_{c}(\lambda)=0
for the same parameter set.

We summarize our result for eigenvalues
\lambda
of the GCEP
(
2.2.13
) for a ring and center cell pattern as follows:

Proposition 3
.

Consider a ring and center hole pattern
of
m\geq 3
cells in the unit disk with cell centers at
(
4.1.1
). The set
\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
as obtained from
the GCEP (
2.2.13
), and which approximates as
\varepsilon\to 0

all the discrete eigenvalues of the linearization of the PDE-ODE
system (
1.0.2
) around the steady-state solution, is

\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})\equiv\Big\{\lambda\,\,\big|\,\,\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{m-2}\{{\,\sigma_{j}(\lambda)=0\,\}}\,,\,\,\bigcup\,\{{\sigma_{\pm}(\lambda)=0\}}\Big\}\,.

(4.1.18)

Here
\sigma_{j}(\lambda)
, for
j=1,\ldots,m-2
, for the anti-phase
modes are defined in (
4.1.11
), while

\sigma_{\pm}(\lambda)
for the in-phase modes are defined in
(
4.1.15
). As shown in Remark
1
of Appendix

A
, due to mode degeneracy of

{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda(m-1)}
, there are
{(m-1)/2}
distinct
anti-phase modes if
m
is odd and
{(m-2)/2}
distinct anti-phase
modes if
m
is even.

For the unit disk, where an infinite series representation of the
solution to (
2.2.8
) is available, explicit formulae for the
eigenvalues
\omega_{\lambda j}
of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda(m-1)}
, as
needed in (
4.1.11
) and (
4.1.15
), are given in
Appendix
A
. The result in Remark
1
of
Appendix
A
regarding mode degeneracy results from the
fact that
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda(m-1)}
is both symmetric and
cyclic. In Appendix
A
we also show how to readily
calculate the quantities in (
4.1.2
) and
(
4.1.3
), which are needed in (
4.1.4
) for
determining the steady-state.

As indicated by Proposition
1
, stability boundaries in
the
\tau
versus
D
parameter space for the steady-state under
Sel’kov kinetics are determined by HB boundaries where

\lambda=i\lambda_{I}\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
. To determine Hopf
bifurcation boundaries for the anti-phase modes we set

\mbox{Re}(\sigma_{j}(i\lambda_{I}))=0
and

\mbox{Im}(\sigma_{j}(i\lambda_{I}))=0
for
j=1,\ldots,m-2
in
(
4.1.11
). Upon separating real and imaginary parts in
(
4.1.7a
) we obtain the following nonlinear algebraic
system for each
j=1,\ldots,m-2
:

2\pi\nu\,\mbox{Re}(\omega_{\lambda j})+\left(1+\nu\frac{D}{d_{1}}\right)+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}\,\mbox{Re}(\mathit{K}_{c}(i\lambda_{I}))=0\,,\qquad\mbox{Im}(\omega_{\lambda j})+\frac{D}{\tau}\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}\,\mbox{Im}(\mathit{K}_{c}(i\lambda_{I}))=0\,,

(4.1.19a)

where

\mbox{Im}(\mathit{K}_{c}(i\lambda_{I}))=\frac{\lambda_{I}(\mbox{det}(J_{1})-\lambda_{I}^{2})\,+\lambda_{I}\mbox{det}(J_{1})\mbox{tr}(J_{1})}{(\mbox{det}(J_{1})-\lambda_{I}^{2})^{2}+(\lambda_{I}\,\mbox{tr}(J_{1}))^{2}}\,,\quad\mbox{Re}(\mathit{K}_{c}(i\lambda_{I}))=\frac{\mbox{det}(J_{1})(\mbox{det}(J_{1})-\lambda_{I}^{2})-\lambda_{I}^{2}\,\mbox{tr}(J_{1})}{(\mbox{det}(J_{1})-\lambda_{I}^{2})^{2}+(\lambda_{I}\,\mbox{tr}(J_{1}))^{2}}\,.

(4.1.19b)

Here
J_{1}
is the Jacobian of the Sel’kov kinetics for the ring cells with
determinant and trace given in (
4.1.7b
). Similarly, the Hopf
bifurcation boundaries for the in-phase modes are obtained by
setting
\sigma_{\pm}(i\lambda_{I})=0
in (
4.1.15
), which yields
the nonlinear algebraic system

\mbox{Re}(\sigma_{\pm}(i\lambda_{I}))=0\,,\qquad\mbox{Im}(\sigma_{\pm}(i\lambda_{I}))=0\,,

(4.1.20)

or equivalently
{\mathcal{H}}_{c}(i\lambda_{I})=0
from (
4.1.16
).

4.2
Example: Two cells on a ring with a center cell

We now apply the theory developed in
section

4.1
to a
population of
m=3
cells, where two of the cells are equally spaced
on a concentric ring of radius
r_{0}
within the unit disk, with the
remaining one centered at the origin (see Fig.
3
). For this
configuration, the eigenvalues of the
3\times 3
GCEP matrix

\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
are given in (
4.1.11
) and
(
4.1.15
) for a single anti-phase mode and the two in-phase
modes, respectively. To compute the HB boundaries in the
\tau
versus

D
parameter plane for these modes, we solve (
4.1.19
) and
(
4.1.20
) numerically by implementing the psuedo-arclength
continuation algorithm TEST_CON (cf.
[
1
]
) with respect to

D
, while using Newton’s method to compute
\tau
and
\lambda_{I}

at each point on the solution path. Such a continuation scheme in
D

is needed owing to the possibility of fold points along the HB boundary.

To determine regions of instability in open sets of the
\tau
versus

D
parameter plane we use (
4.1.18
) of Proposition

3
, together with the winding number criterion of
complex analysis, to identify the number
{\mathcal{N}}
of eigenvalues

\lambda\in\Lambda({\mathcal{M}})
with
\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
. To do
so, we first define

\mathcal{F}(\lambda)\equiv\det(\mathcal{M}(\lambda))
, where

{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
is the GCEP matrix in (
4.1.6
). Provided
that there are no zeroes or poles on the imaginary axis,
\mathcal{N}

is the number of zeroes of
\mathcal{F}(\lambda)=0
in

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
, which from the argument principle is

\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\big[\text{arg}\,\mathcal{F}(\lambda)\big]_{\Gamma}+{\mathcal{P}}\,.

(4.2.1)

Here
{\mathcal{P}}
is the number of poles of
\mathcal{F}(\lambda)

in
\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
, while

\big[\text{arg}\,\mathcal{F}(\lambda)\big]_{\Gamma}
denotes the
change in the argument of
\mathcal{F}(\lambda)
over the closed,
counter-clockwise oriented contour
\Gamma
. This contour
\Gamma
is
the limit as
\mathcal{R}\to\infty
of the union of the imaginary
axis
\Gamma_{I}=i\lambda_{I}
, for
|\lambda_{I}|\leq\mathcal{R}
, and
the semi-circle
\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}
, defined by

|\lambda|=\mathcal{R}
with
|\text{arg}(\lambda)|\leq\pi/2
. To
count the number of poles of
\mathcal{F}(\lambda)
in

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
, we must examine the analyticity properties
of the GCEP matrix
\mathcal{M}(\lambda)
in (
4.1.6
). Since the
entries of the Green’s matrix
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
are analytic in

\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
, any singularity of
\mathcal{F}(\lambda)

must arise from the diagonal matrix

{\mathcal{K}}(\lambda)\equiv\mbox{diag}(\mathit{K}_{c},\mathit{K}_{c},\mathit{K}_{m})
of
(
4.1.6b
), which is given explicitly in (
4.1.7
) in
terms of the Jacobians
J_{1}
and
J_{m}
of the Sel’kov kinetics for the
identical ring cells and the center cell, respectively. Since

\mbox{det}(J_{1})>0
, (
4.1.7a
) yields that
\mathit{K}_{c}
has a
complex conjugate pair of poles in
\mbox{Re}(\lambda)>0
only if

\mbox{tr}(J_{1})>0
. Since this term involves two rows of

{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
,
{\mathcal{P}}
must be incremented by four
whenever
\mbox{tr}(J_{1})>0
. Similarly, since
\mbox{det}(J_{m})>0
,

{\mathcal{P}}
is increased by two when
\mbox{tr}(J_{m})>0
for the
center cell.

With
{\mathcal{P}}
determined in this way, we numerically compute the
number of unstable eigenvalues
\mathcal{N}
of the linearization of
the steady-state by evaluating (
4.2.1
) for each
point
(D,\tau)
in the
\tau
versus
D
plane. For each such point,
we numerically construct the closed contour
\Gamma
for some value

\mathcal{R}
(chosen so that
\Gamma
encloses all the poles of

\mathcal{F}
) in the complex
\lambda
-plane. As the closed curve

\Gamma
is traversed in a counter-clockwise direction, the closed
image curve
\mathcal{F}(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}_{R}+i\mathcal{F}_{I}

is evaluated numerically in the complex
\mathcal{F}
-plane. The
winding number, denoting the number of times
\mathcal{F}

encloses/winds around the origin, is computed numerically from the
algorithm of
[
2
]
, and this is used to
calculate
\big[\text{arg}\,\mathcal{F}(\lambda)\big]_{\Gamma}
.
If the orientation of

{\mathcal{F}}(\lambda)={\mathcal{F}}_{R}+i{\mathcal{F}}_{I}
around the
origin is in the counter-clockwise direction, then

\big[\text{arg}\,\mathcal{F}(\lambda)\big]_{\Gamma}
is positive;
otherwise, it is negative. In our computations, we chose

\mathcal{R}=1.5
(since any pole of
\mathcal{F}
is close to the
imaginary axis of the
\lambda
-plane), and we discretized the closed
contour
\Gamma=\Gamma_{I}\cup\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}
into
subintervals, with
\Gamma_{I}
having 800 subintervals while the
semi-circle
\Gamma_{{\mathcal{R}}}
had
50
subintervals. In the
function evaluation, the identity

{\mathcal{F}}(\overline{\lambda})=\overline{{\mathcal{F}}(\lambda)}

was used to halve the computational effort.

5
Asymptotic analysis for a large population of cells

As the number of cells increases it becomes increasingly more
challenging numerically to determine the stability boundaries in
parameter space from the root-finding condition

\mbox{det}{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)=0
given an arbitrary cell
configuration
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
with arbitrary permeability parameters

d_{1j}
and
d_{2j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. In this section we provide a
simpler approach to determine the stability boundaries from the GCEP
(
2.2.13
) for the large bulk diffusion parameter regime where

D={\mathcal{O}}(\nu^{-1})
. To isolate only the effect of different
cell configurations, such as shown in Fig.
16
, as well
as the effect of different cell permeabilities, in our analysis below
we will assume that the Sel’kov kinetic parameters are all
cell-independent, i.e. that
\alpha_{j}=\alpha
,
\mu_{j}=\mu
and

\zeta_{j}=\zeta
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Our analysis is easily extended to
remove this assumption.

(b)

We first recall from (
2.2.21b
) of

section

2.2
that, for
\varepsilon\to 0
, the
steady-state solution with Sel’kov kinetics is determined in terms of
the solution
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})^{T}
to the linear
system

\left(I+2\pi\nu\mathcal{G}+\nu\,D\,P_{1}+\frac{2\pi\nu D}{\tau}P_{2}\right){\mathcal{A}}=-\mu\nu P_{2}\,\boldsymbol{e}\,;\qquad P_{1}=\mbox{diag}\Big(\frac{1}{d_{11}},\ldots,\frac{1}{d_{1m}}\Big)\quad P_{2}=\mbox{diag}\Big(\frac{d_{21}}{d_{11}},\ldots,\frac{d_{2m}}{d_{1m}}\Big)\,,

(5.0.1a)

where
\boldsymbol{e}=(1,\dots,1)^{T}
and
\mathcal{G}
is the Green’s
interaction matrix defined in (
2.1.7
). In terms of the
solution
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
to (
5.0.1a
), we obtain from
(
2.2.21a
) that the steady-state for the intracellular species

\boldsymbol{u}_{e}=(u^{1}_{ej},u^{2}_{ej})^{T}
in each cell is

u^{1}_{ej}=\mu+\frac{2\pi D}{\tau}A_{j}\,,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad u^{2}_{ej}=\frac{\mu}{\alpha+(u^{1}_{ej})^{2}}\,.

(5.0.1b)

Since this simple steady-state construction is accurate to all orders
in
\nu
for any
D>0
, we will not seek an approximation to it
valid for the regime
D={\mathcal{O}}(\nu^{-1})
. Therefore, in the
GCEP matrix
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
given in (
2.2.13a
),
the diagonal matrix
{\mathcal{K}}(\lambda)
, as defined in
(
2.2.12a
), will be evaluated at the solution to
(
5.0.1
). With Sel’kov kinetics, this yields

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(\lambda)\equiv\mbox{diag}\Big(\mathit{K}_{1},\dots,\mathit{K}_{m}\Big)\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\mathit{K}_{j}=\frac{\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{j})}{\lambda^{2}-\lambda\,\mbox{tr}(J_{j})+\mbox{det}(J_{j})}\,,

(5.0.2a)

where
J_{j}
is the Jacobian matrix for the local Sel-kov kinetics
at the
j^{\text{th}}
cell, for which

\mbox{det}(J_{j})=\zeta\left(\alpha+(u_{ej}^{1})^{2}\right)\,,\qquad\mbox{tr}(J_{j})=\frac{\left[2\mu u_{ej}^{1}-\left(\alpha+(u_{ej}^{1})^{2}\right)-\zeta\left(\alpha+(u_{ej}^{1})^{2}\right)^{2}\right]}{\alpha+(u_{ej}^{1})^{2}}\,.

(5.0.2b)

Our goal in this subsection of determining a more tractable formula
for determining the roots of
\det\mathcal{M}(\lambda)=0
when
m
is
large and in the limit
D={D_{0}/\nu}
, where

\nu={-1/\log\varepsilon}
and
D_{0}={\mathcal{O}}(1)
, is based on
approximating the eigenvalue-dependent Green’s matrix

{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}
in (
2.2.13a
), while retaining the
full
\mathcal{K}
matrix, as defined in (
5.0.2
).

To do so, we readily calculate from (
2.2.8
) that when

D={D_{0}/\nu}\gg 1
we have (see
[
20
]
)

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}=\frac{D_{0}}{\nu|\Omega|(1+\tau\lambda)}+\mathcal{G}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,.

(5.0.3)

Here
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is the Neumann Green’s matrix of
(
3.0.17
) given in terms of the Neumann Green’s
function
G_{0}
of (
3.0.14
), which is known analytically for
the the unit disk in (
3.0.21
). By using
(
5.0.3
) in (
2.2.13a
), we find that the GCEP
matrix reduces to

\mathcal{M}(\lambda)={\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda)+\mathcal{O}(\nu^{2})\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}\equiv{\mathcal{B}}+\gamma E+2\pi\nu{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\,.

(5.0.4a)

In (
5.0.4a
) the scalar
\gamma
, the rank-one matrix
E
and the
diagonal matrix
{\mathcal{B}}
are defined by

\gamma=\gamma(\lambda)\equiv\frac{2\pi mD_{0}}{(1+\tau\lambda)|\Omega|}\,,\qquad E\equiv\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{e}\,\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)\equiv I+D_{0}P_{1}+\frac{2\pi D_{0}}{\tau}P_{2}\,\mathcal{K}(\lambda)\,.

(5.0.4b)

Neglecting terms of order
{\mathcal{O}}(\nu^{2})
, in the limit

D={D_{0}/\nu}\gg 1
we conclude that the GCEP (
2.2.13
) reduces
to finding values of
\lambda
for which there is a nontrivial solution

\boldsymbol{c}
to

\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(\lambda)\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{0}\,,\qquad\mbox{which occurs iff}\qquad\det\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(\lambda)=0\,.

(5.0.4c)

We remark that in our asymptotic reduction (
5.0.4
) of the
GCEP (
2.2.13
), the cell configuration enters through the
diagonal matrix
{\mathcal{K}}
, as defined in (
5.0.2
),
and at order
\nu
from the Neumann Green’s matrix
{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
in
(
5.0.4a
).

To analyze (
5.0.4
) we first observe from
(
5.0.4b
) and (
5.0.2a
) that

{\mathcal{B}}=\mbox{diag}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad b_{j}=\frac{(d_{1j}+D_{0})}{d_{1j}}\left[1+\frac{\eta_{j}}{\tau}\mathit{K}_{j}\right]\,,\qquad\eta_{j}\equiv\frac{2\pi d_{2j}D_{0}}{d_{1j}+D_{0}}\,,\quad j=1,\ldots,m\,.

(5.0.5)

The key advantage of our asymptotic reduction is that by using the
matrix structure in (
5.0.4
) we can readily calculate a
tractable analytical expression for

\mbox{det}{{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda)}
, which can be used in
implementing the root-finding condition in (
5.0.4c
)
numerically. Our first result for (
5.0.4
) in this direction
is as follows:

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that
b_{j}\neq 0
for
j=1\,,\ldots,m
so that
{\mathcal{B}}
is invertible. Then,

\mbox{det}{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda)=\mbox{det}\left({\mathcal{B}}+\gamma E+2\pi\nu{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\right)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\right)\kappa_{1\varepsilon}\left(1+{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)\right)\,,

(5.0.6)

where

\kappa_{1\varepsilon}\equiv 1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+2\pi\nu\,\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{T}_{1}\,\mathcal{G}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\nu^{2})\,,\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{T}\equiv\left({1/b_{1}},\ldots,{1/b_{m}}\right)\,.

(5.0.7)

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{B}}
is a diagonal matrix and invertible by assumption, we have

\mbox{det}\left({\mathcal{B}}+\gamma E+2\pi\nu{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\right)=\mbox{det}({\mathcal{B}})\det\left(I+\gamma{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E+2\pi\nu{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\right)\,,

(5.0.8)

where
\mbox{det}{\mathcal{B}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}
. The second term in
(
5.0.8
) is calculated by multiplying all the eigenvalues

\kappa_{\varepsilon}
of

\left(I+\gamma{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E+2\pi\nu{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\right)\,\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}=\kappa_{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}\,.

(5.0.9)

To determine
{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)
accurate expressions for these eigenvalues
we expand an eigenpair
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}
as

\kappa_{\varepsilon}=\kappa_{1}+\nu\,\tilde{\kappa}+\cdots\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\nu\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}+\cdots\,.

(5.0.10)

Upon substituting (
5.0.10
) into (
5.0.9
) we
equate powers of
\nu
to obtain the leading-order problem

(I+\gamma{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E)\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=\kappa_{1}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\,,

(5.0.11)

and the following problem at
\mathcal{O}(\nu)
:

(I+\gamma{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E-\kappa_{1}I)\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=\tilde{\kappa}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-2\pi{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\,.

(5.0.12)

For the leading order problem (
5.0.11
), we first observe that

E\,\boldsymbol{q}_{j}=\boldsymbol{0}
for
j=2,\dots,m
, where

\mbox{span}\{{\boldsymbol{q}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_{m}\}}
is the
m-1

dimensional subspace orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{e}
. We readily conclude that
(
5.0.11
) has the eigenpairs

\kappa_{1j}=1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{1j}=\boldsymbol{q}_{j}\,,\qquad\mbox{for}\quad j=2,\ldots,m\,.

(5.0.13)

To determine the
{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)
correction to these eigenvalues,
we substitute (
5.0.13
) into (
5.0.12
)
to obtain

\gamma{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=\tilde{\kappa}\boldsymbol{q}_{j}-2\pi{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{j}.

(5.0.14)

Since
{\mathcal{B}}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{j}
, with
{\mathcal{B}}^{T}={\mathcal{B}}
,
is a left null-vector of
{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E
, the solvability
condition for (
5.0.14
) is

\boldsymbol{q}_{j}^{T}{\mathcal{B}}\left(\tilde{\kappa}\boldsymbol{q}_{j}-2\pi{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{j}\right)=0
, which
determines
\tilde{\kappa}
for each eigenpair. In this way, we obtain
that
m-1
eigenvalues of (
5.0.9
) are

\kappa_{j\varepsilon}=1+2\pi\nu\,\frac{\boldsymbol{q}_{j}^{T}\,\mathcal{G}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{j}}{\boldsymbol{q}_{j}^{T}\,{\mathcal{B}}\,\boldsymbol{q}_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(\nu^{2})\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\quad\boldsymbol{q}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}=0\,,\quad\mbox{for}\quad j=2,\dots,m\,.

(5.0.15)

To find the remaining eigenpair of the leading order problem we
write (
5.0.11
) as

\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(1-\kappa_{1})=-\frac{\gamma}{m}\left(\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right)\left({1/b_{1}},\ldots,{1/b_{m}}\right)^{T}
. This has the (unnormalized) nontrivial solution

\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=\left({1/b_{1}},\ldots,{1/b_{m}}\right)^{T}\,,\qquad\mbox{iff}\qquad\kappa_{1}=1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{b_{i}}\,.

(5.0.16)

To determine the
\mathcal{O}(\nu)
correction to this eigenvalue we
observe, for this eigenpair, that
\boldsymbol{e}
is a left null-vector of
the matrix in (
5.0.12
) since, by
using (
5.0.16
) for
\kappa_{1}
, we calculate

\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\left(I+\gamma{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E-\kappa_{1}I\right)=\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{b_{i}}\right)-\kappa_{1}\right)=\boldsymbol{0}\,.

(5.0.17)

Therefore, upon left-multiplying (
5.0.12
) by

\boldsymbol{e}^{T}
, the solvability condition for (
5.0.12
) is

\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\left(\tilde{\kappa}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-2\pi{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right)=0
, which yields

\tilde{\kappa}=2\pi{\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{T}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}/\left(\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right)}
where we used

\boldsymbol{e}^{T}{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{T}
. We conclude that a two-term
expansion
\kappa_{1\varepsilon}=\kappa_{1}+\tilde{\kappa}\nu
for
this remaining eigenvalue of (
5.0.9
) is as given in
(
5.0.7
). Finally, by multiplying

\kappa_{1\varepsilon}
with the other eigenvalues given in
(
5.0.15
) we obtain

\det\left(I+\gamma{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}E+2\pi\nu{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\right)=\kappa_{1\varepsilon}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(\nu))
.
In view of (
5.0.8
) this completes the derivation of
(
5.0.6
).

∎

The key assumption in Proposition
4
is that
b_{j}\neq 0
for
any
j=1,\ldots,m
. By using (
5.0.5
) and (
5.0.2a
)
for
b_{j}
and
\mathit{K}_{j}
, respectively, we conclude that
b_{j}=0
if
and only if
\lambda
is a root of the quadratic equation

{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(\lambda)=0
, where

{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(\lambda)\equiv\lambda^{2}-\left(\mbox{tr}(J_{j})-\frac{\eta_{j}}{\tau}\right)\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{j})\left[1+\frac{\eta_{j}}{\tau}\right]\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\quad\eta_{j}\equiv\frac{2\pi d_{2j}D_{0}}{d_{1j}+D_{0}}\,,

(5.0.18)

with
\mbox{det}(J_{j})
and
\mbox{tr}(J_{j})
as given in
(
5.0.2b
). With this criterion and together with
Proposition
4
we readily formulate a simple scalar
root-finding problem to identify values of
\lambda
for which the
reduced GCEP (
5.0.4
) has a nontrivial solution.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose that
\lambda=\lambda^{\star}

is a root of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}(\lambda)=0
, where

{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}(\lambda)\equiv 1+\frac{\gamma}{m}\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+2\pi\nu\,\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{T}_{1}\,\mathcal{G}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}}{\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{1}}\right)\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{1}^{T}\equiv\left({1/b_{1}},\ldots,{1/b_{m}}\right)\,.

(5.0.19)

Here
\gamma=\gamma(\lambda)
and
b_{j}=b_{j}(\lambda)
for
j=1,\ldots,m
are
given in (
5.0.4b
) and (
5.0.5
), respectively. Suppose
that
\lambda^{\star}
satisfies

\lambda^{\star}\notin\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{m}\{{\lambda_{j\pm}\}}\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\quad{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(\lambda_{j\pm})=0\,,

(5.0.20)

and
{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(\lambda)
is the quadratic defined in
(
5.0.18
). Then,

\mbox{det}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda^{\star})=0
and the
corresponding (unnormalized) nontrivial solution
\boldsymbol{c}
to the
reduced GCEP (
5.0.4c
) is

\boldsymbol{c}=\left(\frac{1}{b_{1}(\lambda^{\star})},\ldots,\frac{1}{b_{m}(\lambda^{\star})}\right)^{T}+{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)\,

(5.0.21)

In this way, we can use Proposition
5
to determine Hopf
bifurcation boundaries in the
\tau
versus
D_{0}
parameter plane by simply
letting
\lambda=i\lambda_{I}
, with
\lambda_{I}>0
, and setting

\mbox{Re}\left[{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}(i\lambda_{I})\right]=0\,,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mbox{Im}\left[{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}(i\lambda_{I})\right]=0\,,

(5.0.22)

while ensuring that the condition (
5.0.20
) holds with

\lambda^{\star}=i\lambda_{I}
. A sufficient condition for
(
5.0.20
) to hold along solutions of (
5.0.22
)
as parameters are varied is that
\mbox{tr}(J_{j})\neq{\eta_{j}/\tau}
for
all
j=1,\ldots,m
.

A simple analytically tractable special case of Proposition

5
is when the permeabilities are all identical,
i.e.
d_{1j}=d_{1c}
,
d_{2j}=d_{2c}
, and when the cell configuration

\{{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{m}\}}
is such that
\boldsymbol{e}
is
an eigenvector of the reduced-wave Green’s matrix
{\mathcal{G}}
, and
consequently the Neumann Green’s matrix
{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
. Therefore,

{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\boldsymbol{e}=\beta\boldsymbol{e}
for some eigenvalue
\beta
. A
ring patterns of cells concentric within the unit disk with common
cell permeabilities and Sel’kov parameters is an example of such a
cell pattern. For this case, (
5.0.1
) admits the
solution
{\mathcal{A}}=A_{c}\boldsymbol{e}
and so
J_{j}=J_{c}
for

j=1,\ldots,m
. Since
b_{j}=b_{c}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, we can take

\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=b_{c}^{-1}(1,\ldots,1)^{T}
and readily obtain that the
root-finding condition
{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}(\lambda)=0
in
(
5.0.19
) reduces to

1+\frac{\gamma}{b_{c}}+\frac{2\pi\nu}{mb_{c}}\boldsymbol{e}^{T}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\boldsymbol{e}=0\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\boldsymbol{e}=\beta\boldsymbol{e}\,,

(5.0.23)

while from (
5.0.18
) we have

{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}(\lambda)={\mathcal{Q}}_{c}(\lambda)
for
j=1,\ldots,m
,
where

{\mathcal{Q}}_{c}(\lambda)\equiv\lambda^{2}-\left(\mbox{tr}(J_{c})-\frac{\eta_{c}}{\tau}\right)\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{c})\left[1+\frac{\eta_{c}}{\tau}\right]\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\quad\eta_{c}\equiv\frac{2\pi d_{2c}D_{0}}{d_{1c}+D_{0}}\,,

(5.0.24)

By using (
5.0.5
) and (
5.0.2a
), we obtain
after a little algebra that (
5.0.23
) reduces to

\frac{\lambda+\mbox{det}(J_{c})}{\lambda^{2}-\lambda\,\mbox{tr}(J_{c})+\mbox{det}{J_{c}}}=-\frac{\tau}{2\pi d_{2c}}\left[1+\frac{d_{1c}}{D_{0}}\left(1+2\pi\nu\beta\right)+\frac{2\pi md_{1c}}{|\Omega|(1+\tau\lambda)}\right]\,,

(5.0.25)

which is a cubic equation in
\lambda
. For this special cell pattern,
we conclude that if
\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
is a root of
(
5.0.25
) for which
Q_{c}(\lambda^{\star})\neq 0
,
then
\mbox{det}\,{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda^{\star})=0
. The
corresponding eigenvector of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda^{\star})

is the in-phase mode
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{e}
.

Next, we will show how to determine roots of the reduced GCEP
(
5.0.4c
) in the case where

{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)=\mbox{diag}\left(b_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,b_{m}(\lambda)\right)^{T}
in (
5.0.4b
) is not invertible. We first observe
that a nontrivial
\boldsymbol{c}
to the leading order problem in
(
5.0.4c
) (with
\nu=0
), exists if and only if there is a

\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
at which
at least two

b_{j}
cross through zero
simultaneously. After relabelling the indices as necessary, this
occurs without loss of generality when there is a
\lambda^{\star}

and an integer
J
with
2\leq J\leq m
for which

b_{1}(\lambda^{\star})=\ldots=b_{J}(\lambda^{\star})=0\,,\quad\mbox{with}\quad b_{j}(\lambda^{\star})\neq 0\quad\mbox{for}\quad j=J+1,\ldots,m\quad\mbox{if}\quad J<m\,.

(5.0.26)

Then, for the leading-order problem in (
5.0.4c
) at

\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
the nontrivial solutions
\boldsymbol{c}_{0}
are

\left({\mathcal{B}}(\lambda^{\star})+\gamma(\lambda^{\star})E\right)\boldsymbol{c}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}\,,\quad\implies\quad\boldsymbol{c}_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}\equiv\Big\{{}\boldsymbol{c}_{0}\,\Big|\,\,\boldsymbol{e}^{T}\boldsymbol{c}_{0}=0\,,\,\,\boldsymbol{c}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{c}_{J}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix}\,,\,\,\boldsymbol{c}_{J}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J}\,,\,\,\boldsymbol{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m-J}\Big\}\,.

(5.0.27)

Next, we introduce an orthonormal basis for the subspace

{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
of dimension
J-1
and we decompose
\boldsymbol{c}_{0}

as

\boldsymbol{c}_{0}=\omega_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}+\ldots+\omega_{J-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{J-1}\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}\equiv\mbox{span}\{{\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{J-1}\}}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\delta_{ij}\,,

(5.0.28)

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker symbol, and
\omega_{j}
for

j=1,\ldots,J-1
are scalar coefficients to be found.

To determine the
{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)
correction to the leading-order
eigenvalue
\lambda^{\star}
of the GCEP and identify the constants

\omega_{j}
, we look for a nontrivial solution to

{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda)\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{0}
, as defined in
(
5.0.4
), of the form

\lambda=\lambda^{\star}+{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)
. We substitute the
expansion

\lambda=\lambda^{\star}+\nu\tilde{\lambda}+\cdots\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{c}_{0}+\nu\boldsymbol{c}_{1}+\cdots\,,

(5.0.29)

into (
5.0.4
) and then equate
{\mathcal{O}}(\nu)
terms to
obtain

\left({\mathcal{B}}(\lambda^{\star})+\gamma(\lambda^{\star})E\right)\boldsymbol{c}_{1}=-\tilde{\lambda}\gamma^{\prime}(\lambda^{\star})E\boldsymbol{c}_{0}-\tilde{\lambda}{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(\lambda^{\star})\boldsymbol{c}_{0}-2\pi{\mathcal{G}}_{0}\boldsymbol{c}_{0}\,,

(5.0.30)

where we observe that
E\boldsymbol{c}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
. The solvability
condition for (
5.0.30
) is that the right-hand side of
(
5.0.30
) is orthogonal to each
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
for

j=1,\ldots,J-1
. In this way, we readily obtain that

\tilde{\lambda}
and

\boldsymbol{\omega}\equiv(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{J-1})^{T}
are eigenpairs
of the
J-1
dimensional symmetric generalized matrix eigenvalue
problem

{\mathcal{V}}^{T}{\mathcal{G}}_{0}{\mathcal{V}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}=-\frac{\tilde{\lambda}}{2\pi}{\mathcal{V}}^{T}{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(\lambda^{\star}){\mathcal{V}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{V}}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\,,&\ldots&\,,\boldsymbol{v}_{J-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(5.0.31)

Here
{\mathcal{V}}
is the
m\times J-1
matrix whose columns provide
an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
. In summary, for any
such
\tilde{\lambda}
satisfying (
5.0.31
) a two-term
expansion for a root of the reduced GCEP

\mbox{det}{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(\lambda)=0
is

\lambda=\lambda^{\star}+\nu\tilde{\lambda}
where
\lambda^{\star}

satisfies (
5.0.26
).

We now illustrate this theory for the special case where
J=2
. This
analysis, given below, will be shown in §
5.1.1
to be
relevant for analyzing anti-phase instabilities associated with the
cell configuration of Fig.
23(b)
where a pair of isolated
identical cells is spatially segregated from two symmetric ring
clusters. Suppose that cells 1 and 2 have common permeabilities

d_{1c}\equiv d_{11}=d_{12}
,
d_{2c}\equiv d_{21}=d_{22}
and that
they have the same intracellular steady-states. Then, from
(
5.0.2
) we obtain

\mbox{tr}{J_{c}}\equiv\mbox{tr}{J_{1}}=\mbox{tr}{J_{2}}
,

\mbox{det}{J_{c}}\equiv\mbox{det}{J_{1}}=\mbox{det}{J_{2}}
, and so
(
5.0.5
) and (
5.0.2a
) yields that

b_{c}(\lambda)\equiv b_{1}(\lambda)=b_{2}(\lambda)
. Then, in
(
5.0.31
) we can take

{\mathcal{V}}=\left({1/\sqrt{2}},-{1/\sqrt{2}}\right)^{T}
, and readily
calculate that

\tilde{\lambda}=-2\pi{R_{c}/b_{c}^{\prime}(\lambda^{\star})}
, where

b_{c}(\lambda^{\star})=0
. Here

R_{c}\equiv R_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=R_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
is the common value of the
regular part of the Neumann Green’s function at the centers

\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
of the two cells. For
\nu\ll 1
, a simple
perturbation argument shows that

\lambda\sim\lambda^{\star}+\nu\tilde{\lambda}
can be identified as
the root to
b_{c}(\lambda)+2\pi\nu R_{c}=0
. Finally, by using
(
5.0.5
) for
b_{c}
, together with (
5.0.2
), we
conclude for
\nu\ll 1
that
\lambda
must be a root of the quadratic

\lambda^{2}-\lambda\left(\mbox{tr}{J_{c}}-\frac{\eta_{c}}{\tau f}\right)+\left(1+\frac{\eta_{c}}{\tau f}\right)\mbox{det}J_{c}=0\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\eta_{c}\equiv\frac{2\pi d_{2c}D_{0}}{d_{1c}+D_{0}}\,,\quad f\equiv 1+\frac{2\pi\nu d_{1c}}{d_{1c}+D_{0}}R_{c}\,.

(5.0.32)

Since
\mbox{det}J_{c}>0
, an anti-phase instability occurs for cells 1
and 2, with the other cells remaining quiescent, only when

\mbox{tr}(J_{c})-\frac{\eta_{c}}{\tau f}>0\,.

(5.0.33)

Although this criterion gives a region in the
(D_{0},\tau)
parameter
plane, in §
5.1.1
we will only implement it for the cell
configuration in Fig.
23(b)
at a fixed value of
\tau
in
order to determine a threshold in
D_{0}
.

Finally, to obtain some analytical insight into the possibility of an
unstable mode consisting of anti-phase oscillations in the isolated
cells together with quiescent ring cells, we implement the degenerate
perturbation theory on the reduced GCEP matrix (
5.0.4
) from
the
D={D_{0}/\nu}
regime. By using the simple criterion in
(
5.0.33
), based on the quadratic equation
(
5.0.32
), we calculate for permeability set I that this
anti-phase mode for the isolated cells is unstable when
\tau=0.35

only when
D_{0}<0.0817
. This corresponds to
D<0.245
using

D={D_{0}/\nu}
with
\nu={-1/\log\varepsilon}
and

\varepsilon=0.05
. Although this simple criterion
(
5.0.33
) predicts the existence of an unstable
anti-phase mode for the isolated cells, the threshold value is not so
accurate (see Fig.
24(a)
) owing to the fact that
D

is not asymptotically large. In contrast, for permeability set II, we
calculate from the criterion (
5.0.33
) that the
anti-phase mode for the isolated cells is always stable, as is
consistent with Fig.
24(b)
.

6
Discussion and Outlook

We have analyzed diffusion-sensing behavior for the coupled cell-bulk
PDE-ODE system (
1.0.2
) that was used to model the
switch-like onset of intracellular oscillations for a collection of
heterogeneous cells, as mediated through a passive bulk diffusion
field with finite bulk diffusivity
D
. For the case of Sel’kov
reaction kinetics, we have studied how the onset of intracellular
oscillations depends on the spatial configuration of cells, on the
membrane permeability parameters, and on the triggering effect of a
single “defective” cell with a different kinetic parameter. In
contrast, in
[
26
]
QS behavior resulting from
increase in cell density were studied using only the ODE system
(
3.0.19
) with global coupling, which pertains to the
well-mixed regime of infinite bulk diffusivity.

There are several open theoretical and computational challenges that
should be explored for (
1.0.2
). One key numerical issue
concerns developing efficient and well-conditioned numerical
techniques to implement the full linear stability theory based on the
root-finding condition
\mbox{det}({\mathcal{M}}(\lambda))=0
for the
GCEP given in (
2.2.13
) for a large number, i.e.
m\geq 100
,
of randomly located cells with arbitrary permeabilities. The solution
strategies for such nonlinear matrix eigenvalue problems are typically
restricted to matrices wih special structure, such as Hermitian
matrices, matrices with low-rank dependence on
\lambda
, or
matrices that are quadratic or rational in the eigenvalue parameter

\lambda
(cf.
[
23
]
and
[
4
]
). In contrast, the GCEP
matrix in (
2.2.13
) is not Hermitian when
\lambda
is
complex-valued, and is not of low-rank owing to its dependence on the
full eigenvalue-dependent Green’s matrix
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda}
.

From a mathematical viewpoint, a second open direction would be to
develop a weakly nonlinear theory for the Hopf bifurcations of
steady-state solutions of (
1.0.2
). The numerical results
shown in §
4
and
5
have
suggested that the intracellular oscillations are supercritical for
the Sel’kov kinetics, and that their relative amplitude and phases
within the cells are well-predicted by the eigenvector of the GCEP
matrix
{\mathcal{M}}(\lambda)
in (
2.2.13
). Also of interest
would be to explore whether ODE phase-reduction techniques, such as
surveyed in
[
41
]
and
[
36
]
, can be extended to the
PDE-ODE setting of (
1.0.2
).

From a modeling perspective, the PDE-ODE system (
1.0.1
) is
well-suited for investigating triggered intracellular oscillations and
collective dynamics associated with specific microbial systems for
which detailed models of the signaling pathways are available, the
autoinducer is known, and where membrane permeabilities can be
estimated from biological data. Such calibrated biological models are
readily incorporated into the theoretical framework (
1.0.1
).
In particular, it would be worthwhile to analyze (
1.0.1
) with
the detailed model of
[
56
]
and
[
25
]
for the glycolytic
pathway in yeast cells and the model of
[
33
]
for bacterial
communication of
Escherichia coli
cells. For non-oscillatory QS
systems, it would be interesting to use the intracellular
Lux-signaling pathways of
[
32
]
into our PDE-ODE model
(
1.0.1
) to analyze sudden jumps between small and large
amplitude steady-states for bistable QS systems as the cell density
increases past a saddle-node bifurcation value. Moreover,
(
1.0.1
) can be used to study the effect of spatial diffusion
on the parallel QS signaling pathways associated with the marine
bacterium
V. harveyi
, which were recently modeled in

[
5
]
through an ODE well-mixed limit. Our analysis in §
3
has shown that the dimensionless PDE-ODE system
(
1.0.2
) can be reduced to a limiting ODE system only when
the bulk diffusivity satisfies
D={\mathcal{O}}(-\log\varepsilon)
,
where
\varepsilon
is the dimensionless ratio of the cell radius to
the length scale of the domain. The classic ODE system for the
well-mixed limit corresponds to the regime

D\gg{\mathcal{O}}(-\log\varepsilon)
.

Finally, it would be worthwhile to extend the 2-D model
(
1.0.1
) to allow for two passive bulk diffusion fields, and
to consider 3-D domains, where the cell-cell interaction is weaker
than in 2-D owing to the rapid decay of the 3-D free-space Green’s
function. By including two bulk species, and with a re-formulation of
the class of lattice-based models introduced in
[
42
]
into the
framework of (
1.0.1
), it should be possible to show
analytically that changes in the permeability parameters of a
collection of cells can induce a Turing or transcritical bifurcation
to a patterned steady-state for an activator-inhibitor system even
when the two bulk diffusing species have very similar diffusivities.
In a spatially homogeneous medium without cells, Turing bifurcations
only occur for activator inhibitor systems when there is a
sufficiently large diffusivity ratio, which is not typical for most
biological systems. The PDE-ODE framework of (
1.0.1
), where
the small signaling compartments are modeled explicitly, also provides
an alternative approach for studying large-scale self-organized
structures that have previously been modeled through PDE-ODE lattice
dynamics in which the dynamically active “cells” are treated as
point sources restricted to lattice sites and where a discrete
Laplacian averaging over nearby lattice points replaces the Laplace
operator (cf.
[
6
]
,
[
43
]
,

[
29
]
). Such lattice PDE-ODE systems have been used to
model traveling waves of yeast activation due to substrate addition of
glucose at a localized source (cf.
[
43
]
), and the emergence of
spiral waves resulting from the coupling of Fitzhugh-Nagumo or Rossler
cell kinetics to discrete lattice-based diffusion
(cf.
[
29
]
,
[
6
]
).

Appendix A
The reduced-wave Green’s function for the unit disk

In this appendix we provide explicit expressions for the matrix
spectrum of (
4.1.9
) corresponding to the matrix block of
the eigenvalue-dependent reduced-wave Green’s matrix in
(
4.1.8
) representing the interactions of
m-1\geq 2

cells on a ring of radius
r_{0}
, with
0<r_{0}<1
, concentric within the
unit disk. For notational convenience we define
N
by
N=m-1
. The

N
cells on the ring of radius
r_{0}
are centered at

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=r_{0}\left(\cos\psi_{k},\sin\psi_{k}\right)^{T}
, where

\psi_{k}\equiv{2\pi(k-1)/N}
for
k=1,\ldots,N
. In the unit disk, the
reduced-wave Green’s function
G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\xi})
and its
regular part, satisfying (
2.2.8
), can be calculated using
separation of variables as (see equations (6.10) and (6.11) of

[
20
]
)

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{\,1}{\,2\pi}K_{0}\left(\varphi_{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}|\right)-\frac{\,1}{\,2\pi}\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\beta_{n}\cos\left(n(\psi-\psi_{0})\right)\frac{K^{\prime}_{n}(\varphi_{\lambda})}{I^{\prime}_{n}(\varphi_{\lambda})}I_{n}\left(\varphi_{\lambda}r\right)I_{n}\left(\varphi_{\lambda}\rho\right)\,,

(A.1a)

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\log\left(2\sqrt{D}\right)-\gamma_{e}-\frac{1}{2}\log{\left(1+\tau\lambda\right)}\right]-\frac{\,1}{\,2\pi}\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\beta_{n}\frac{K^{\prime}_{n}(\varphi_{\lambda})}{I^{\prime}_{n}(\varphi_{\lambda})}\left[I_{n}\left(\varphi_{\lambda}\rho\right)\right]^{2}\,,

(A.1b)

where
\varphi_{\lambda}\equiv\sqrt{(1+\tau\lambda)/D}
is the principal
branch of
\varphi_{\lambda}
,
\gamma_{e}=0.5772
is Euler’s constant, and

I_{n}(z)
and
K_{n}(z)
are the modified Bessel functions of the
first and second kind of order
n
, respectively. In (
A.1
),

\beta_{0}\equiv 1
,
\beta_{n}\equiv 2
for
n\geq 1
, while

\boldsymbol{x}\equiv r(\cos\psi,\sin\psi)^{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\equiv\rho(\cos\psi_{0},\sin\psi_{0})^{T}
.

For a ring pattern, the
N\times N
symmetric Green’s matrix

{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda N}
is also cyclic and can be generated by a cyclic permutation of
its first row
\boldsymbol{a}_{\lambda}\equiv(a_{\lambda,1},\ldots,a_{\lambda,N})^{T}
, which is defined term-wise by

a_{\lambda,1}\equiv R_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\,;\qquad a_{\lambda,k}=G_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\,,\qquad k=2,\ldots,N\,.

(A.2)

The matrix spectrum

{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda N}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=\omega_{\lambda j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
in (
4.1.9
) with

N=m-1
is calculated as in §6 of
[
20
]
. The
in-phase eigenpair is

\omega_{\lambda N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{\lambda,k}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{N}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\,,

(A.3a)

while the other eigenvalues, corresponding to the anti-phase modes
for which
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{N}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,N-1
, are

\omega_{\lambda j}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi jk}{N}\right)a_{\lambda,k+1}\,,\qquad j=1,\ldots,N-1\,.

(A.3b)

Since
\omega_{\lambda j}=\omega_{\lambda,N-j}
for
j=1,\ldots,\lceil{N/2}\rceil-1
, there are
\lceil{N/2}\rceil-1
pairs of
degenerate eigenvalues for
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda N}
. Here the ceiling
function
\lceil x\rceil
is defined as the smallest integer not less
than
x
. When
N
is even, there is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
one given by
\omega_{\lambda,\frac{N}{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{k}a_{\lambda,k+1}
. The other eigenvectors for
j=1,\ldots,\lceil{N/2}-1\rceil

are

\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=\left(1,\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right),\ldots,\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j(N-1)}{N}\right)\,\right)^{T}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{N-j}=\left(0,\sin\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right),\ldots,\sin\left(\frac{2\pi j(N-1)}{N}\right)\,\right)^{T}\,.

(A.3c)

Finally, when
N
is even, there is an additional eigenvector

\boldsymbol{v}_{\frac{N}{2}}=(1,-1,\ldots,-1)^{T}
. By using the explicit
formulae for
G_{\lambda}
and its regular part from (
A.1
),
the eigenvalues
\omega_{\lambda j}
of the Green’s matrix

{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda N}
are then easily computed from
(
A.3
).

Remark 1
.

The symmetric and cyclic matrix
{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda N}
has

{N/2}
distinct anti-phase modes if
N
is even and
{(N-1)/2}
distinct
anti-phase modes if
N
is odd.

Finally, in order to calculate the quantities in (
4.1.2
) and
(
4.1.3
), which are needed in (
4.1.4
) for
determining the steady-state solution, we need only set
\lambda=0

in (
A.1
) and (
A.3a
).
