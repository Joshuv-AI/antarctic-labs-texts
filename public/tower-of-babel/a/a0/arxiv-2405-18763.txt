STATIONARY DISTRIBUTION APPROXIMATIONS OF TWO-ISLAND WRIGHT-FISHER AND SEED-BANK MODELS USING STEIN’S METHOD
Han L. Gan, Maite Wilke–Berenguer

Abstract
We consider two finite population Markov chain models, the two-island Wright-Fisher model with mutation, and the seed-bank model with mutation. Despite the relatively simple descriptions of the two processes, the the exact form of their stationary distributions is in general intractable. For each of the two models we provide two approximation theorems with explicit upper bounds on the distance between the stationary distributions of the finite population Markov chains, and either the stationary distribution of a two-island diffusion model, or the beta distribution. We show that the order of the bounds, and correspondingly the appropriate choice of approximation, depends upon the relative sizes of mutation and migration. In the case where migration and mutation are of the same order, the suitable approximation is the two-island diffusion model, and if migration dominates mutation, then the weighted average of both islands is well approximated by a beta random variable. Our results are derived from a new development of Stein’s method for the stationary distribution of the two-island diffusion model for the weak migration results, and utilising the existing framework for Stein’s method for the Dirichlet distribution.

Introduction

The Wright-Fisher model, originating in
Fisher, 1930
;
Wright, 1931
is one of the canonical probabilistic models used in mathematical population genetics. In this work, we study the two-island model, which allows for the population to be subdivided into two islands, with migration between them. Our goal is to study the approximation of stationary distributions for finite population two-island Wright-Fisher Markov chains with the stationary distributions of scaling diffusion limits. We begin by carefully defining the two models.

The finite population two-island Wright-Fisher Markov chain generalises the Wright-Fisher model by having two islands with their individual Wright-Fisher dynamics, and allowing migration between the two. The model is a Markov chain used to study genetic type frequencies from generation to generation for two interacting populations of fixed sizes. The formal mathematical definition of the model is as follows.

Given
(X,Y)
let
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
be one step ahead in Markov chain. For
N,M,c\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}\in(0,1)
, and

\displaystyle A|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Bin}(N-c,p_{1}+(1-p_{1}-p_{2})X),

\displaystyle B|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Bin}(c,p_{1}+(1-p_{1}-p_{2})X),

\displaystyle C|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Bin}(c,q_{1}+(1-q_{1}-q_{2})Y),

\displaystyle D|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Bin}(M-c,q_{1}+(1-q_{1}-q_{2})Y).

Then
(NX^{\prime},MY^{\prime})|(X,Y)=(A+C,B+D)|(X,Y)
.

We briefly describe a standard interpretation of the mathematical formulation. Given two islands of fixed size

or
2

as the Markov chain that tracks the proportion of type
1

Given the parent generation, we think of the process that generates the next offspring generation in a two step manner, first the offspring choose a parent, and then their type is decided by the type of their parents and a potential mutation step.

For the first island, independently and uniformly at random,
N-c
offspring chose a parent from the first island, and
c

M-c

c

1

(2)

2

(1)

p_{2}

(p_{1})

The seed-bank model is similar in motivation to the two-island Wright-Fisher model in that it is used to model genetic type frequencies from generation to generation for two interacting populations of fixed size, but it differs from the two-island Wright-Fisher model, by disabling reproduction and mutation in the second island.

Given
(X,Y)
let
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
be one step ahead in Markov chain. For
N,M,c\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
p_{1},p_{2}\in(0,1)

\displaystyle A|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Bin}(N-c,p_{1}+(1-p_{1}-p_{2})X),

\displaystyle B|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Bin}(c,p_{1}+(1-p_{1}-p_{2})X),

\displaystyle C|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Hg}(M,MY,c),

\displaystyle D|(X,Y)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Hg}(M,MY,M-c).

Importantly, the above random variables are pairwise conditionally independent except
D|(X,Y)=M-C|(X,Y)

We again briefly describe a standard interpretation of the mathematical formulation. Let the populations be of sizes
N
and
M
, and we will call them the active population and seed-bank population respectively. We again consider a haploid population with types
1
or
2
, and set
(X_{t},Y_{t})
as the Markov chain that tracks the proportion of type
1
on the two respective populations from one generation to the next. Given the parent generation, the dynamics are described as follows.

As per the two-island model, let
c
represent the migration rate. For the active population,
N-c
offspring choose a parent from the first island independently and uniformly at random with replacement.
c
individuals from the seed-bank parent generation are chosen uniformly
without
replacement to migrate to the active population. For the seed-bank, the remaining
M-c
individuals who did not migrate remain ‘dormant’ and stay over into the next generation, and
c
offspring are sampled uniformly at random with replacement from the active population. Mutation occurs, but only for offspring of the active population. As in the two-island model, if the chosen parent is of type
1

(2)
, then the child mutates to type
2

(1)
with probability
p_{2}

(p_{1})
or otherwise has the same type as its parent. Individuals that migrate from the seed bank do not change their types.

Seed banks
arise in populations who exhibit
dormancy
, which describes de capability of an individual to enter and exist a state of reduced metabolic activity during which it cannot reproduce, but is at the same time better protected from death, e.g. through difficult environmental conditions. The set of dormant individuals is then referred to as a seed bank. This mechanism is particularly prevalent among microbial populations, but has independently developed several times across the tree of life (cf.
Lennon and Jones, 2011
;
Shoemaker and Lennon, 2018
). It has in recent years received increased attention and become an active field of research in population genetics and beyond, see
Lennon et al., 2021
and references therein. One key parameter is the time-scale of dormancy compared to that of reproduction (or the population size). The model for dormancy presented above (and introduced in
Blath et al., 2016
) describes a so-called
strong
seed bank effect, where dormancy occurs at the same scale as reproduction. In this regime, the frequency process described above converges to the so-called seed-bank diffusion, while the ancestry converges to the seed bank coalescent
Blath et al., 2016
.

Closed form solutions for the stationary distributions for both of the two-island and seed-bank models is known to be a difficult problem with no nice solutions
Blath et al., 2019
, and hence good approximations are of particular value. In this paper we provide two approximations each for both models, namely the stationary distribution of the two-island Wright-Fisher diffusion model, and the beta distribution. All of the bounds for the approximations are explicit, including the constants, and are general for any possible values of the parameters
N,M,p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2},c
.

In this paper we investigate approximations for the stationary distributions of these processes, in particular as the population sizes grow. We restrict the scope of this paper to two allele type models with mutation and only two islands, but note that generalising to multiple types and multiple islands would not require any additional theoretical mathematical complexity, but significant additional tedious bookkeeping notation and calculations. The two main driving forces in the models are mutation and migration. As a result there are three main scenarios to consider. Namely if mutation dominates migration, if migration dominates mutation, or if mutation and migration are of the same order. In this paper, we focus on the latter two scenarios, as the first case is relatively uninteresting as the islands effectively operate independently in this scenario. More precisely, to assess the accuracy and applicability of the approximations we consider the following asymptotic regimes for the mutation and migration rates. We will follow common population genetics terminology and call mutation
weak
if mutation is rare in the sense that the mutation probabilities
p_{1},p_{2}
are of order
\mathrm{O}(1/N)
and similarly for
q_{1},q_{2}
. Throughout the rest of this paper we will be assuming that mutation is weak. In contrast, we will vary the level of migration. We will consider migration to be of the order
\mathrm{O}(N^{\epsilon})
where
\epsilon\in[0,1]
. If
\epsilon=0
, this corresponds to a constant order of migration, which we will also therefore call weak. If
\epsilon>0
, then we will call migration strong.

Assuming weak mutation, the appropriate scaling diffusion limit depends upon whether migration is strong or weak. We show that under strong migration, the two islands are ‘well-mixed’, and the appropriate approximation is to approximate the stationary distribution of the weighted average of the two islands with a beta distribution. In contrast, under weak migration, each island maintains its individual characteristics and the appropriate approximating distribution is the stationary distribution of the two-island diffusion model. One approach to understand this dichotomy is to consider the coalescent process under weak or strong migration, see for example
Nordborg and Krone, 2002
. If migration occurs on the same scaling as mutation, then lineages will usually coalesce without migration. On the other hand with strong migration, migration occurs a larger number of times before coalescence, and in the limit the standard one-island coalescent is achieved.

Before we present the main results, we first define the two distributions we use as reference measures. We then describe the general order the bounds for the approximations for both models and both distributions.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x,y)

Full results & additional discussion

The main results are derived using Stein’s method, in particular the exchangeable pairs approach. The final bounds, while initially may appear to be intimidatingly long, have a relatively straightforward interpretation. One interpretation for Theorems
2.1

2.3

Barbour, 1990

Each term of the final bounds quantify the differences between the continuous generator and a Taylor expansion of the discrete generator. The first order terms
A_{x},A_{y}
measure the differences in the “drift” components, the second order terms
A_{xx},A_{yy},A_{xy}
measure the differences in the “diffusion” components, the remaining terms bound higher order error terms. An analogous interpretation applies for Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
and their beta approximations which are simpler due to the projection to the one dimensional approximating distribution.

3
Proofs of main results

The primary tool used in this paper to prove the main results is Stein’s method. Stein’s method is a powerful tool in probability theory that is used to derive an
explicit bound
for the difference between two probability distributions. Typically one aims to use it to find an upper bound on the errors incurred when approximating an intractable
target
distribution with a commonly used simple
reference
distribution. It was first developed for the Normal distribution in
Stein, 1972
to bound the approximation errors when applying the central limit theorem, and it has since been developed numerous distributions, such as Poisson
Chen, 1975
;
Barbour et al., 1992
, beta
Goldstein and Reinert, 2013
;
Döbler, 2015
, Dirichlet
Gan et al., 2017
, negative binomial
Brown and Phillips, 1999
, exponential
Fulman and Ross, 2013
to just name a few. For many more examples and applications, see for example the surveys or monographs
Ross, 2011
;
Chatterjee, 2014
;
Barbour and Chen, 2005
;
Ley et al., 2017
. We first briefly introduce Stein’s method and outline the main steps for the proofs of the main theorems.

3.1
Introduction to Stein’s method

To successfully apply Stein’s method, there are typically three main steps which we outline and give a brief description of our particular approach below. Our goal is to bound the difference between the typically unknown or intractable law of our target random variable
W
with the law of a well understood and simple reference random variable
Z
.

1.

Identify a characterising operator
\mathcal{A}
or identity that is satisfied by only the reference distribution
\pi
. In this paper the characterising operators are the Wright-Fisher diffusion generator and the two-island diffusion generator. The generator characterises its associated stationary distribution through the identity that
\mathbbm{E}\mathcal{A}f(Z)=0
for all suitable functions
f
when
Z
follows the stationary distribution.

2.

For any arbitrary function
h
, solve for the function
f_{h}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f_{h}(x)=h(x)-\mathbbm{E}h(Z).

Then by setting
x=W
and taking expectations,

(3.1)

\displaystyle|\mathbbm{E}\mathcal{A}f_{h}(W)|=|\mathbbm{E}h(W)-\mathbbm{E}h(Z)|.

Properties of the function
f_{h}
turn out to be crucial to derive a good bound with Stein’s method. Typically one will require good bounds on
f_{h}
and its derivatives. Much of this paper is dedicated to this process.

3.

The final step is to bound (
3.1
) for all
h
from a rich enough family of test functions
\mathcal{H}
, for example
\mathcal{H}
could be the family of all polynomials, in which case would yield a bound on the differences in all moments for the two distributions. Rather than directly bounding
|\mathbbm{E}h(W)-\mathbbm{E}h(Z)|
, using (
3.1
) we instead derive the bound for
|\mathbbm{E}\mathcal{A}f_{h}(W)|
which is more tractable. Our technique of choice in this paper is to use what is known in the Stein’s method literature as the ‘exchangeable pairs’ approach.

For Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
, steps 1 and 2 have been established in
Gan et al., 2017
and we only need to complete the third step. However for the TI distribution, steps 1 and 2 have not been established in the literature, so we first derive its general Stein’s method framework in this paper.

3.2
Stein’s method for the TI distribution

In this section, we focus on the first two of the three steps outlined in Section
3.1
, and leave the final step for the appendix. The first step for Stein’s method for the stationary moments of the two-island diffusion is to define our characterising operator. We follow the generator approach of
Barbour, 1988
;
Barbour, 1990
;
Götze, 1991
and our operator is therefore the generator of the two-island diffusion (
1.1

Given non-negative constants

Recall the next step in Stein’s method is for any arbitrary function

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f_{h}(x,y)=h(x,y)-\mathbbm{E}h(Z_{1},Z_{2}).

3.2
, we describe a key component of the proofs, moment duality of the two-island diffusion model. For a more detailed discussion of this duality see
Blath et al., 2019
. For
f(x,y)=x^{n}y^{m}
, where

, then (

) takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}x(1-x)n(n-1)x^{n-2}y^{m}+[a_{1}-(a_{1}+a_{2})x+c_{1}(y-x)]nx^{n-1}y^{m}

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{\beta}{2}y(1-y)m(m-1)x^{n}y^{m-2}+[b_{1}-(b_{1}+b_{2})y+c_{2}(x-y)]mx^{n}y^{m-1}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}n(n-1)+na_{1}\Big)\Big[x^{n-1}y^{m}-x^{n}y^{m}\Big]+na_{2}\Big[0-x^{n}y^{m}\Big]+nc_{1}\Big[x^{n-1}y^{m+1}-x^{n}y^{m}\Big]

\displaystyle\ \ +\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}m(m-1)+mb_{1}\Big)\Big[x^{n}y^{m-1}-x^{n}y^{m}\Big]+mb_{2}\Big[0-x^{n}y^{m}\Big]+mc_{2}\Big[x^{n+1}y^{m-1}-x^{n}y^{m}\Big].

We define our dual process on the space of polynomial functions. We could equivalently define the process on tuples
(n,m)
as in
[

, but in our case it is more natural to work in the space of polynomial functions. Given the function is in ‘state’

it transitions to the following with rates as described:

•

x^{n-1}y^{m}
with rate
\frac{\alpha}{2}n(n-1)+na_{1}
,

•

x^{n-1}y^{m+1}
with rate
c_{1}n
,

•

x^{n}y^{m-1}
with rate
\frac{\beta}{2}m(m-1)+mb_{1}
,

•

x^{n+1}y^{m-1}
with rate
c_{2}m
,

•

0

na_{2}+mb_{2}
.

Q^{x,y}_{n,m}(t)
be the process governed by the rates as above and with initial value

Lemma 3.3
.

For functions
h

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{x,y}(t))=\mathbbm{E}Q^{x,y}_{n,m}(t).

This follows from the dual process construction and
[

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Using Lemma
3.3
,

\displaystyle f(x,y)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}]\mathbbm{E}Q^{x,y}_{n,m}(t)-\mathbbm{E}h(Z_{1},Z_{2})]dt.

The process

will reach an absorbing state in finite time, and this is straightforward by considering process of the sum of the exponents and noting that it is a non-increasing process. Furthermore
[
Blath et al., 2019
, Propositions 2.4, 2.6]
show that the expected value of the process at absorption is exactly
\mathbbm{E}h(Z_{1},Z_{2})
. Hence
f(x,y)
is well-defined.

Again using Lemma
3.3
, it is straightforward to see that the semi-group generated by
\mathcal{A}
is strongly continuous. We then use
[
Ethier and Kurtz, 1986
, Proposition 1.5(a)]
, which implies
f^{(u)}(x,y):=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{u}[\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{(x,y)}(t))-\mathbbm{E}h(Z_{1},Z_{2})]dt
is in the domain of
\mathcal{A}
and is the solution to

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f^{(u)}(x,y)=h(x,y)-\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{(x,y)}(u)).

Furthermore,
[
Ethier and Kurtz, 1986
, Corollary 1.6]
implies that
\mathcal{A}
is a closed operator, and as
u\to\infty
, again using
[
Blath et al., 2019
, Propositions 2.4, 2.6]
,

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}}|\mathcal{A}f^{(u)}(x,y)-\mathcal{A}f(x,y)|=\sup_{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}}|\mathbbm{E}h(Z_{1},Z_{2})-\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{x,y}(u))|\to 0.

Hence (
3.2
) is satisfied by

.

For the first derivative,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[f(x+\epsilon,y)-f(x,y)\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{(x+\epsilon,y)}(t))-\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{(x,y)}(t))\right]dt.

Using the duality of the semi-group,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x,y)\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\left|\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{(x+\epsilon,y)}(t))-\mathbbm{E}h((X,Y)_{(x,y)}(t))\right]dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left|Q^{x+\epsilon,y}_{n,m}(t)-Q^{x,y}_{n,m}(t)\right|dt.

We can couple the transition times of the two processes
Q^{x+\epsilon,y}_{n,m}(t)
,
Q^{x,y}_{n,m}(t)
exactly in the obvious manner, and the two processes will become identical when they reach one of the two absorbing states. We first note that

\displaystyle|(x+\epsilon)^{k_{1}}y^{k_{2}}-x^{k_{1}}y^{k_{2}}|

\displaystyle=\epsilon k_{1}x^{k_{1}-1}y^{k_{2}}+\mathrm{o}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon k_{1}+\mathrm{o}(\epsilon).

It is now straightforward to see that
|Q^{x+\epsilon,y}_{n,m}(t)-Q^{x,y}_{n,m}(t)|\leq(n+m)\epsilon+\mathrm{o}(\epsilon)
for all

where
\tau_{n,m}
is the absorption time of the process. Therefore

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}\left|Q^{x+\epsilon,y}_{n,m}(t)-Q^{x,y}_{n,m}(t)\right|dt=\epsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}N^{*}_{n,m}(t)dt+\mathrm{o}(\epsilon),

where
N^{*}_{n,m}(t)
is the exponent of
x
at time

and the starting exponents of
x
and

are
n
and
m
respectively. To derive a bound for the above integral, we define the following auxiliary urn process. Consider two urns, and suppose there are
n
balls in the first urn and
m
balls in the second urn.

•

At rate
(a_{1}+a_{2})n
, remove a ball from the first urn.

•

At rate
(b_{1}+b_{2})m
, remove a ball from the second urn.

•

At rate
c_{1}n
, move a ball from the first to second urn.

•

At rate
c_{2}m
, move a ball from the second to first urn.

Let
N_{n,m}(t)
(and

) denote the number of balls in the first (second) urn at time

given we start with
n
(
m
) balls in the first (second) urn. Given the rates at which balls are removed are slower than the rates of the dual process, it is clear we can construct a coupling such that

is stochastically dominated by
N_{n,m}(t)
. We begin by focusing on the case

and
m=0
and the partial derivative with respect to
x
. We also simultaneously solve the case
n=0
and
m=1
. Let
P_{n,m,t}(z_{1},z_{2})=\mathbbm{E}\big(z_{1}^{N_{n,m}(t)}z_{2}^{M_{n,m}(t)}\big)
denote the joint probability generating function for
(N_{n,m}(t),M_{n,m}(t))
and set
a=a_{1}+a_{2}
and
b=b_{1}+b_{2}
. Then routine calculations (similar to the derivation of Kolmogorov forwards/backwards equations) yield,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{1,0,t}(z_{1},z_{2})=a+c_{1}P_{0,1,t}(z_{1},z_{2})-(a+c_{1})P_{1,0,t}(z_{1},z_{2}).

In an analogous calculation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{0,1,t}(z_{1},z_{2})=b+c_{2}P_{1,0,t}(z_{1},z_{2})-(b+c_{2})P_{0,1,t}(z_{1},z_{2}).

The explicit solutions to the above pair of differential equations are elementary to compute, however they are very long so we omit them. We note that one could equivalently solve the standard Kolmogorov forwards or backwards equations to calculate the transition probabilities and expectations without any difficulty. Both approaches yield the same final result, but we chose this approach since the joint nature of the probability generating function results in fewer equations. Remark 3.5
.

In the previous theorem we have chosen to derive bounds that are simple, explicit, and easy to use. The true bounds are certainly sharper, and we outline below an approach that would give sharper bounds, at the cost of significant complexity. This approach combines moment duality and the coupling ideas of
Brown and Xia, 2001
. This will ultimately yield a recursive set of equations that will solve for all the Stein solutions.
We begin with a base case of
h(x,y)=x
. As before

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{h(x,y)=x}(x,y)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}[Q_{1,0}^{x+\epsilon,y}(t)-Q_{1,0}^{x,y}(t)]dt.

Let

be the waiting time until the first transition of the processes, then using the strong Markov property,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{h(x,y)=x}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigg[\mathbbm{E}\int_{0}^{\tau}\epsilon dt+\frac{c_{1}}{a+c_{1}}\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\mathbbm{E}[Q_{1,0}^{x+\epsilon,y}(t)-Q_{1,0}^{x,y}(t)|Q_{1,0}^{x+\epsilon,y}(\tau)=Q_{1,0}^{x,y}(\tau)=y]dt\Bigg]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{a+c_{1}}+\frac{c_{1}}{a+c_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{h(x,y)=y}(x,y).

Similarly one can show,

Solving the two equations yields
