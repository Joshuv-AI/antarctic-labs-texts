Abstract
. In this paper, we establish the following two results: (1) a skew translation generalized quadrangle of even order is a translation generalized quadrangle, (2) a generalized quadrangle of even order does not admit a point regular group of automorphisms. The first result confirms a conjecture of Payne (1975) based on earlier work of Ott (2021), and the second result confirms a conjecture of Ghinelli (1992).



1.
Introduction

A generalized quadrangle
{\mathcal{S}}
of order
(s,t)
is a finite point-line incidence structure such that each line has
s+1
points, each point is on
t+1
lines, and for each point-line pair
(P,\ell)
that is not incident there is a unique point collinear with
P
on
\ell
. If
s=t
, we say that it has order
s
. The quadrangle
{\mathcal{S}}
is said to be thick if
\min\{s,t\}\geq 2
. An automorphism of
{\mathcal{S}}
consists of a permutation of the points and a permutation of lines that preserve the incidence. For a given point
P
, an elation about
P
is an automorphism
\theta
that is either an identity, or fixes each line through
P
and no point not collinear with it. If further
\theta
fixes each point collinear with
P
, then it is a symmetry about
P
. We refer the reader to
[
23
]
for basics on generalized quadrangles and
[
31
]
for a comprehensive study from the viewpoint of symmetries.

Suppose that
G
is a group of automorphisms of a generalized quadrangle
{\mathcal{S}}
. If
G
consists of elations about a point
P
and acts regularly on the points not collinear with
P
, then
{\mathcal{S}}
is an elation generalized quadrangle with base point
P
and elation group
G
. If the elation group
G
contains a subgroup consisting of
t
symmetries about
P
, then
{\mathcal{S}}
is a skew translation generalized quadrangle. If an elation quadrangle
{\mathcal{S}}
has an abelian elation group, then it is a translation generalized quadrangle. We refer to
[
17
]
for a description of elation generalized quadrangles using coset geometries, and refer to
[
29
,
28
]
for more on skew-translation and translation quadrangles.

The classification of finite generalized quadrangles that satisfy certain transitivity assumptions has a long history. In recent years, there has been significant progress towards Kantor’s 1991 conjecture
[
18
]
that a finite flag-transitive generalized quadrangle is either classical or has order
(3,5)
or
(15,17)
up to duality, cf.
[
3
,
4
]
. The generalized quadrangles with a group of automorphisms acting primitively on points have also attracted much attention, cf.
[
5
]
and the references therein. In the O’Nan-Scott Theorem as stated in
[
24
, Section 6]
, there are several types of primitive permutation group actions that have a regular subgroup.

In this paper, we are concerned with skew-translation generalized quadrangles and quadrangles with a point regular group of automorphisms. The symplectic quadrangle
W(q)
whose points and lines are the totally singular points and lines respectively of the rank
2
symplectic polar space
W(3,q)
is a skew translation generalized quadrangle, and it is a translation generalized quadrangle when
q
is even. Conversely, a skew-translation generalized quadrangle of odd order
q
must be the symplectic quadrangle
W(q)
. This follows by combining the results in Yoshiara’s 2007 work
[
32
]
and Ghinelli’s 2012 work
[
13
]
, and is first observed by Bamberg, Glasby and Swartz
[
1
]
who also corrected an error in
[
13
]
. K. Thas
[
29
]
also has an independent proof of this fundamental characterization. In the even order case, Payne
[
22
]
made in 1975 the conjecture that a skew translation generalized quadrangle of even order
s
must be a translation generalized quadrangle. The order
s
of a skew translation generalized quadrangle is a power of
2
and the elation group
G
has order
s^{3}
, cf.
[
8
,
15
]
. In 2021, Ott
[
21
]
made major progress and confirmed the conjecture in the case where
s
is an odd power of
2
. In the preprint
[
30
]
, K. Thas gave a short geometrical proof of Ott’s result.

The study of finite generalized quadrangles with a regular group of automorphisms was initiated by Ghinelli
[
12
]
in 1992, and we refer to
[
2
,
10
,
11
,
27
,
32
]
for research on this topic. By determining all the point regular groups of automorphisms of the Payne derived quadrangle of the symplectic quadrangle
W(q)
,
q
odd, we
[
11
]
showed that the finite groups that act regularly on the points of a finite generalized quadrangle can have unbounded nilpotency classes. This is an indication of difficulty in the study of such quadrangles. In Ghinelli’s original work
[
12
]
, she made the conjecture that a generalized quadrangle of even order does not admit a regular group of automorphisms. In
[
27
]
, Swartz made the milder conjecture that a thick generalized quadrangle of order
s
can not have a group of automorphisms acting regularly on both the point set and the line set. By Theorem 1.3 of
[
27
]
, a counterexample would have even order. There has been little progress on both conjectures.

In this paper, we confirm both Payne’s 1975 conjecture and Ghinelli’s 1992 conjecture by introducing new techniques from character theory and extremal graph theory. Swartz’s conjecture in
[
27
]
then follows as a corollary. To be specific, we prove the following results.

Theorem 1.1
.

A skew translation generalized quadrangles of even order is a translation generalized quadrangle.

Theorem 1.2
.

A generalized quadrangle of even order does not admit a point regular group of automorphisms.

Here is an outline of the paper. For an elation generalized quadrangle
{\mathcal{S}}
of order
(s,t)
with elation group
G
, we define a
4
-dimensional algebra from the associated
4
-gonal family. We use it to define two class functions
\chi_{S},\chi_{T}
on
G
that bear geometric information about
{\mathcal{S}}
, and show that they are characters of
G
in Section 2.1. In the case
{\mathcal{S}}
is a skew translation quadrangle of even order
s
, we apply the characters to provide an alternative proof for the part of Ott’s proof in
[
21
]
that requires
s
to be an odd power of
2
. The new argument works for all powers of
2
, so that we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section 2.2 based on the work of Ott. In the case of finite quadrangles with a point regular group
G
of automorphisms, we use the Expander Crossing Lemma for bipartite graphs as found in
[
9
]
to obtain a bound on
|{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(g)|
(see (
3.2
) for definition) for

in Section 3.1. By using this bound and the theory of solvable linear groups
[
26
]
, we present the proof of Theorem
1.2
in Section 3.2.

We use standard notation on group theory as is found in
[
14
]
. Let

be elements in a group

, and let

be subgroups of

, and set

. We use

for the conjugacy class of

that contains

. We write

and

for the derived subgroup of

.

2.
Skew translation generalized quadrangles

Suppose that
{\mathcal{S}}
is an elation generalized quadrangle of order
(s,t)
with base point
p
and elation group
G
, and fix a point
y
not collinear with
P
. Let
M_{0},\ldots,M_{t}
be the lines through
y
. By the generalized quadrangle property, there is exactly one point
z_{i}
on each
M_{i}
that is collinear with
P
. Set
A_{i}
,
A_{i}^{*}
to be the stabilizers of
M_{i}
and
z_{i}
respectively for each
i
. Then each
A_{i}
has order
s
, each
A_{i}^{*}
has order
st
, and
A_{i}^{*}
contains
A_{i}
. Moreover, we have

(K1)

A_{i}A_{j}\cap A_{k}=1
for distinct
i,j,k
,

(K2)

A_{i}^{*}\cap A_{j}=1
for distinct
i,j
.

We call
(G,\{A_{i}\},\{A_{i}^{*}\})
the
4
-gonal family associated with
{\mathcal{S}}
. It is first noted in
[
17
]
that we can reconstruct
{\mathcal{S}}
from the associated
4
-gonal family, see also
[
23
, p. 106]
.

We now define a
4
-dimensional algebra from the
4
-gonal family
(G,\{A_{i}\},\{A_{i}^{*}\})
associated with the elation quadrangle
{\mathcal{S}}
of order
(s,t)
. For a group
H
, we write
H^{\#}:=H\setminus\{1\}
. Let
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
be the group ring of
G
over
{\mathbb{R}}
, cf.
[
20
]
. For a subset
X
of
G
, we also write
X
for the sum in
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
of all the elements in
X
. We define two elements of
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle:=A_{0}^{\#}+A_{1}^{\#}+\cdots+A_{t}^{\#},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Delta^{*}

\displaystyle:=(A_{0}^{*})^{\#}+(A_{1}^{*})^{\#}+\cdots+(A_{t}^{*})^{\#}.

(2.2)

Lemma 2.1
.

Take notation as above. Then
\Delta
and
\Delta^{*}
commute, and we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=(s-2)(t+1)+(t+1)G+(s-t-2)\Delta-\Delta^{*},

\displaystyle\Delta\Delta^{*}

\displaystyle=(s-t-1)(t+1)+t(t+1)G-(t+1)\Delta+(s-t-1)\Delta^{*},

\displaystyle(\Delta^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle=(st-t-1)(t+1)+t^{2}(t+1)G+(st-2t-2)\Delta^{*}.

In particular,
\langle 1,\Delta,\Delta^{*},G\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}}
is a
4
-dimensional subalgebra of
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
.

Proof.

For each
i
, the sets in
\{A_{i}^{*}\}\cup\{A_{j}^{\#}A_{i}:\,j\in\{0,\ldots,t\}\setminus\{i\}\}

are pairwise disjoint by (K1) and (K2). They form a partition of
G
, since the sum of their sizes is
st+t(s-1)s=|G|
. We reformulate this fact as
(\Delta-A_{i}^{\#})\cdot A_{i}+A_{i}^{*}=G
in
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
, i.e.,
\Delta\cdot A_{i}^{\#}=G-A_{i}^{*}+(s-1)A_{i}-\Delta
. Taking summation over
i
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=(t+1)G-(\Delta^{*}+t+1)+(s-1)(\Delta+t+1)-(t+1)\Delta

\displaystyle=(s-2)(t+1)+(s-t-2)\Delta-\Delta^{*}+(t+1)G.

This is the first equation in the lemma.

We have
A_{j}A_{i}^{*}=G
for
j\neq i
by (K2). Taking summation over
j\neq i
, we obtain
(\Delta+t-A_{i}^{\#})A_{i}^{*}=tG
, i.e.,
\Delta(A_{i}^{*})^{\#}=(s-1)A_{i}^{*}+t(G-A_{i}^{*})-\Delta=tG+(s-t-1)A_{i}^{*}-\Delta
. Taking summation over
i
, we obtain the second equation in the lemma. Starting with the fact
A_{j}^{*}A_{i}=G
for
j\neq i
, we derive the same expression for
\Delta^{*}\Delta
, so
\Delta
and
\Delta^{*}
commute in
{\mathbb{R}}[G]
. The third equation is derived in a similar way, and we omit the details. This completes the proof.
∎

2.1.
The characters
\chi_{S},\chi_{T}
of the elation group
G

For each element
g\in G
, we define two functions on
G
as follows:

\displaystyle\chi_{S}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s(s+t)}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\left(|C_{G}(g)|\cdot(s|A_{i}\cap g^{G}|+|A_{i}^{*}\cap g^{G}|)-|G/G^{\prime}|\cdot(s|A_{i}\cap gG^{\prime}|+|A_{i}^{*}\cap gG^{\prime}|)\right),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\chi_{T}(g)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gcd(s,t)}{st(s+t)}\sum_{i=0}^{t}\left(|C_{G}(g)|\cdot(t|A_{i}\cap g^{G}|-|A_{i}^{*}\cap g^{G}|)-|G/G^{\prime}|\cdot(t|A_{i}\cap gG^{\prime}|-|A_{i}^{*}\cap gG^{\prime}|)\right).

(2.4)

The right hand sides of (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) only involve
|C_{G}(g)|
,
g^{G}
and
gG^{\prime}
, so
\chi_{S}
and
\chi_{T}
are constant on the conjugacy classes, i.e., they are class functions of
G
. We establish the following result in this section.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{S}}
is an elation generalized quadrangle of order
(s,t)
with elation group
G
and associated
4
-gonal family
(G,\{A_{i}\},\{A_{i}^{*}\})
. The class functions
\chi_{S}
,
\chi_{T}
defined by (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) respectively are characters of
G
.

The remaining part of this subsection is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.2
. For this purpose, we consider the character values of
\Delta,\Delta^{*}
by examining the
4
-dimensional algebra in Lemma
2.1
. For a complex character
\chi
of
G
, its kernel is
\ker(\chi):=\{g\in G:\,\chi(g)=\chi(1)\}
. If
\chi
is linear, then by
[
20
, Remark 4.2.11]
we extend it to a ring homomorphism

\chi:\,{\mathbb{R}}[G]\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\quad\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g\mapsto\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}\chi(g)\quad\textup{ for $a_{g}$'s in ${\mathbb{R}}$}.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\chi
is a linear character of a finite group
G
and
H
is a subgroup, then
\chi(H)=|H|
or
0
according as
H
is in
\ker(\chi)
or not.

Proof.

The claim is clear if
H
is contained in
\ker(\chi)
, so assume that
H
contains an element
g
such that
\chi(g)\neq 1
. We deduce from
H=gH
that
\chi(H)=\chi(g)\chi(H)
, i.e.,
\chi(H)(1-\chi(g))=0
. It follows that
\chi(H)=0
as desired. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
\chi
is a nonprincipal linear character of
G
, and let
u
and
u^{\prime}
be the numbers of
A_{i}
and
A_{i}^{*}
that are contained in
\ker(\chi)
respectively. Then we have

\chi(\Delta)=su-t-1,\quad\chi(\Delta^{*})=stu^{\prime}-t-1,

(2.5)

where
(u,u^{\prime})
is one of
(1,1)
,
(0,0)
and
(\frac{t}{s}+1,0)
.

Proof.

Since
\chi
is a nonprincipal linear character, we deduce that
\chi(G)=0
by Lemma
2.3
. For each
i
we have
\chi(A_{i})=s
or
0
according as
A_{i}
is contained in
\ker(\chi)
or not by the same lemma, and there is a similar result for
A_{i}^{*}
. Taking summation over
i
, we obtain the expressions of
\chi(\Delta)
,
\chi(\Delta^{*})
as in (
2.5
), where
u,u^{\prime}
are as in the statement of the proposition.

Recall that
A_{i}A_{j}^{*}=G
for distinct
i,j
by (K2) and
A_{i}<A_{i}^{*}
for each
i
. It follows that
A_{i}^{*}A_{j}^{*}=G
for
i\neq j
. We deduce that
\ker(\chi)
contains at most one
A_{i}^{*}
, and if
A_{i}^{*}
is in
\ker(\chi)
then the only
A_{j}
contained in
\ker(\chi)
is
A_{i}
. In other words, we have
u^{\prime}\leq 1
, and
u^{\prime}=1
implies that
u=1
. We apply
\chi
to both sides of the first equation in Lemma
2.1
to obtain

\displaystyle(su-t-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=(s-2)(t+1)+(s-t-2)(su-t-1)-(stu^{\prime}-t-1),

which simplifies to
u^{2}s-ut-us+u^{\prime}t=0
. When
u^{\prime}=0
, we deduce that
u=0
or
u=\frac{t}{s}+1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose that
s
does not divide

. Then
A_{i}^{*}G^{\prime}=A_{i}G^{\prime}
for each

.

Proof.

The claim is clear if
G=G^{\prime}
, so assume that
G^{\prime}<G
. We set

, and let
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}[G]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}[H]
be the ring homomorphism that linearly extends the natural epimorphism from

X_{1}:=\phi(\Delta)+t+1

\chi
be a linear character of

\chi

\chi(X_{1})=s(t+1)
,
\chi(X_{2})=st(t+1)
. If
\chi
is nonprincipal, then
(\chi(X_{1}),\chi(X_{2}))
is one of
(0,0)
,
(s,st)
by Proposition

\chi(X_{2})=t\chi(X_{1})
for each character

of

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\chi
be a nonlinear irreducible character of
G
. Then

\chi(S)=s(s+t)\omega_{\chi},\quad\chi(T)=\frac{(s+t)st}{\gcd(s,t)}z_{\chi}

.

Let
\psi
be a matrix representation for

, and write

. Let

be the identity matrix of order

, so that
\psi(1)=I_{m}
. For each

we deduce that
\psi(A_{i})^{2}=s\psi(A_{i})

. Similarly,
\chi(\Delta^{*})=stv_{\chi}-(t+1)m
with

. It follows that

.

Since
\chi
is irreducible, we have
\psi(G)=0
by Schur’s Lemma. Applying
\psi
to the three equations in Lemma

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Take notation as above. Let
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{d}
be all the irreducible characters of
G
, where the first
r
are linear and the last
d-r
are nonlinear. By the column orthogonality relation
[
14
, Theorem 2.8]
, for each
g\in G
it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\chi_{i}(g)\overline{\chi_{i}(S)}

Suppose that

.

We have

\displaystyle=(t+1)s+(s-1)\Delta+(t+1)(G-1-\Delta)

[

. It divides

can be diagonalized over

, we deduce that
1+s
divides
|H^{\prime}|=[G^{\prime}:\,N]
. Also,
|H^{\prime}|

p^{d}-1

3.17
(3). Write
p^{d}-1=\alpha(1+s)

\alpha

\displaystyle 0\equiv\alpha^{2}(1+s^{2})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}+(p^{d}-1-\alpha)^{2}\equiv 2\alpha^{2}+2\alpha+1\pmod{p^{d}}.

In particular,
2\alpha^{2}+2\alpha+1\geq p^{d}
. It follows that
\alpha\geq\frac{-1+\sqrt{2p^{d}-1}}{2}
, and so
s=\frac{p^{d}-1}{\alpha}-1\leq\sqrt{2p^{d}-1}
.
It is equivalent to
p^{d}\geq\frac{s^{2}+1}{2}
. Since
s^{2}+1
is odd and
p^{d}

, we deduce that
p^{d}=s^{2}+1
. Then
\alpha=\frac{p^{d}-1}{s+1}=\frac{s^{2}}{s+1}
which is not an integer: a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Recall that
|G|=(1+s)(1+s^{2})
,
|N|=q=q_{1}^{e}
,
H=G/C_{G}(N)
,
H=\langle\rho^{k},\rho^{s_{0}}\sigma\rangle
,
H^{\prime}=\langle\rho^{k(q_{1}-1)}\rangle
, where
e
is an odd integer. Set
\beta:=\frac{q_{1}^{e}-1}{q_{1}-1}
. By Lemma
3.11
,
e>1
, and
|H^{\prime}|
divides
\beta
. By Lemmas
3.24
and
3.25
,
C_{G}(N)=G^{\prime\prime}=N
. It follows that
H^{\prime}=G^{\prime}/N
and
[G:\,G^{\prime}]=[H:\,H^{\prime}]
,
|H^{\prime}|=[G^{\prime}:\,N]
. Set
u:=[G:\,G^{\prime}]
and
u_{0}:=\frac{1}{e}u

(3). By Lemmas
3.10
and
3.26

p^{d}
divides
1+s^{2}
, and
u

1+s
. Since
|G|=u\cdot|H^{\prime}|\cdot q=(1+s)(1+s^{2})
, there are odd divisors

|H^{\prime}|

1+s=n_{1}u,\quad 1+s^{2}=qn_{2},\quad n_{1}n_{2}=|H^{\prime}|,\quad\beta\equiv 0\pmod{n_{1}n_{2}}.

We deduce from the first two equations that
1+(en_{1}u_{0}-1)^{2}=qn_{2}
. Taking modulo
n_{1}u_{0}

n_{2}-2\equiv 0\pmod{n_{1}u_{0}}

n_{1}u_{0}

q-1

n_{1}u_{0}

n_{2}\geq n_{1}u_{0}+2
. Therefore,

e^{2}n_{1}^{2}u_{0}^{2}>qu_{0}n_{1}

n_{1}>r

\displaystyle r^{2}u_{0}-\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2}}{e^{4}u_{0}}-\frac{q-1}{q_{1}-1}>\frac{4q^{2}}{e^{4}(q_{1}-1)}-\frac{q}{q_{1}-1}.

Since

odd, the last term is always positive. This contradiction completes the proof of Theorem
