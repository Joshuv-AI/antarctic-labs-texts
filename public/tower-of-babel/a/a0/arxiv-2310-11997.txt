On the Upper Bound of Non-Thermal Fusion Reactivity with Fixed Total Energy
Huasheng Xie, Xueyun Wang

Abstract
Fusion reactivity represents the integration of fusion cross-sections and the velocity distributions of two reactants. In this study, we investigate the upper bound of fusion reactivity for a non-thermal reactant coexisting with a thermal Maxwellian background reactant while maintaining a constant total energy. Our optimization approach involves fine-tuning the velocity distribution of the non-thermal reactant. We employ both Lagrange multiplier and Monte Carlo methods to analyze Deuterium-Tritium (D-T) and Proton-Boron11 (p-B11) fusion scenarios. Our findings demonstrate that, within the relevant range of fusion energy, the maximum fusion reactivity can often surpass that of the conventional Maxwellian-Maxwellian reactants case by a substantial margin, ranging from 50% to 300%. These enhancements are accompanied by distinctive distribution functions for the non-thermal reactant, characterized by one or multiple beams. These results not only establish an upper limit for fusion reactivity but also provide valuable insights into augmenting fusion reactivity through non-thermal fusion, which holds particular significance in the realm of fusion energy research.

Introduction

The counting of fusion reactions per unit volume and per unit time is given by
[
1
,
2
]
:

R_{12}=\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{1+\delta_{12}}\langle\sigma v\rangle,

(1)

where
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
represent the number densities of the two reactants, respectively. The term
\delta_{12}
is equal to 0 for different reactants and equal to 1 for the same reactants, in order to prevent double counting of the reaction.

Here,
\sigma=\sigma(E)
or
\sigma=\sigma(v)
represents the fusion cross section, with
E
being the energy in the center-of-mass frame, defined as

E=\frac{1}{2}m_{r}v^{2},~~v=|{\bm{v}}|=|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|,~~m_{r}=\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},

(2)

where
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
denote the mass of the two reactants, and
m_{r}
represents the reduced mass of the system. The fusion reactivity
\langle\sigma v\rangle
is calculated as the integral of the fusion cross section and the velocity distribution functions of the reactants:

\langle\sigma v\rangle=\int\int d{\bm{v}}_{1}d{\bm{v}}_{2}\sigma(|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|)|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|f_{1}({\bm{v}}_{1})f_{2}({\bm{v}}_{2}),

(3)

where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are the normalized velocity distribution functions of the two ions, i.e.,
\int f_{j}({\bm{v}}_{j})d{\bm{v}}_{j}=1
with
j=1,2
, and
d{\bm{v}}_{j}=dv_{xj}dv_{yj}dv_{zj}
. We assume
m_{1}\leq m_{2}
, i.e., the mass of the first reactant is lighter than the second one.

It is interesting to study non-thermal fusion reactivity, as it is common in fusion experiments (cf.,
[
3
]
), and it offers a potentially attractive solution to enhance realistic fusion energy production with the challenges posed by advanced fuels (cf.,
[

,

[

[
12

, and velocity-space
anisotropy distribution
[

]
in fusion plasmas have revealed potential enhancement ranges for fusion reactivity when compared to thermal Maxwellian plasmas. It has also been demonstrated that even a modest increase in proton-Boron fusion reactivity, such as 20%, can significantly impact the feasibility of proton-Boron fusion energy production (cf.,
[
11
,
14
]
). The growing interest in studying advanced fuel for fusion energy
[

requires the exploration of potential approaches to increase fusion reactivity and reduce radiation loss. Therefore, enhancing fusion reactivity becomes a crucial topic that needs resolution.

In our previous studies (cf.,
[
11

11

[

), it did not provide information about which distribution could yield the maximum fusion reactivity or what the upper limit of the enhancement factor might be. This question has motivated the present work. Resolving this problem can provide us with insights into selecting specific distributions to maximize fusion yields.

In fusion energy research, the most crucial parameter is the ratio of output fusion yield energy to input heating energy. The input heating energy is associated with the total energy of the plasma. The total energy for a given distribution is
E_{j}=\frac{1}{2}m_{j}\int v_{j}^{2}f_{j}({\bm{v}}_{j})d{\bm{v}}_{j}
. In this work, we are interested in determining the maximum fusion reactivity
\langle\sigma v\rangle
when
E_{j}
is fixed, along with the corresponding distribution functions
f_{j}
. To make the problem more analytically tractable, we limit
f_{2}
to be a Maxwellian (thermal) distribution and focus on optimizing only

2.1
Reducing the Dimensions of the Problem

Initially, considering that
f_{2}
is an isotropic Maxwellian distribution background given by

f_{2}({\bm{v}})=\Big(\frac{m_{2}}{2\pi k_{B}T_{2}}\Big)^{3/2}\exp\Big[-\frac{m_{2}{\bm{v}}^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{2}}\Big],

(5)

we observe that each beam injected into the Maxwellian background results in fusion reactivity that is solely dependent on the beam’s energy, independent of its direction. This can also be inferred from Eq.(
23
) in
A
. Thus, for the study of fusion reactivity, we can limit our consideration to the one-dimensional distribution of
f_{1}
, specifically:

f_{1}(v)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(v-v_{n}),

(6)

where
f_{1}(v)=\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi v^{2}\sin\theta f_{1}({\bm{v}})
, and
v=|{\bm{v}}|
,
v_{n}=|{\bm{v}}_{n}|\geq 0
. Notice that the fusion reactivity of two drift Maxwellian reactants forms the basis for the present work, we provide the details in
A
.

Hence, we have proven that
f_{1}
can be expressed as
f_{1}(v)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(v-v_{n})
. The problem then becomes the optimization of
v_{n}
to maximize:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn},

(7)

where
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn}
can be calculated from Eq.(
23
). The constraint is:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}m_{1}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}v_{n}^{2}=E_{1}.

(8)

Solving the above problem in energy space is much simpler than in velocity space. We set
f_{1}(E)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(E-E_{1n})
, and the problem becomes the optimization of
E_{1n}=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}v_{n}^{2}
to maximize:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn},

(9)

with the constraint:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}E_{1n}=E_{1}.

(10)

The fusion reactivity enhancement factor is defined as:

\displaystyle f_{\langle\sigma v\rangle}=\frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle}{\langle\sigma v\rangle_{M}},

(11)

where
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{M}
is the fusion reactivity of the corresponding Maxwellian case with the same kinetic energy. Note that for the drift Maxwellian distribution in Eq.(
19
), the total kinetic energy of species 1 is
[
11
]
:
E_{k1}=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}\int v_{1}^{2}f_{1}({\bm{v}}_{1})d{\bm{v}}_{1}=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}T_{1}+E_{d1}
. Hence, to maintain the total kinetic energy the same, the corresponding Maxwellian temperature for the beam energy is
k_{B}T_{1}=\frac{2}{3}E_{1}
.

2.3
Theoretical maximum via Lagrange multipler method

We can utilize the Lagrange multiplier method to determine the maximum of Eq.(
9
) while adhering to the constraint given in Eq.(
10
). We define the Lagrangian as follows:

\displaystyle L(E_{11},E_{12},\cdots,E_{1N},\lambda)

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn}-\lambda\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}E_{1n}-E_{1}\Big],

which leads to the requirement:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(E_{11},E_{12},\cdots,E_{1N},\lambda)}{\partial E_{1n}}=\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn}}{\partial E_{1n}}-\lambda\frac{1}{N}=0.

(13)

Eq.(
13
) has solutions where either
E_{1n}=0
or

\displaystyle g(E_{1n},T_{2})\equiv\frac{\partial\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn}}{\partial E_{1n}}=\lambda,

(14)

holds for all
n=1,2,\cdots,N
, with
\lambda
being a constant. For a given
T_{2}
, we observe in Figures
3
and
4
that the function
g(E_{1n},T_{2})={constant}
can only be satisfied for a few specific
E_{1n}
values. For example, in the case of D-T fusion, there are only 1-2 solutions when
T_{2}=0
. This implies that the maximum of Eq.(
9
) occurs when
E_{1n}
takes on 1-3 specific values, indicating that the distribution of
f_{1}(E)
should consist of 1-3 beams, regardless of the value of
N
. The actual values of the beam energies depend on Eq.(
10
).

The model described above remains challenging to solve for
N\to\infty
. Nevertheless, armed with the insights gained from the previous results, we can make further progress.
Since only a limited number of solutions exist from the Lagrange multiplier approach, i.e., even as
N\to\infty
, the energies of these solutions are confined to several specific values, such as
N_{1}
for energy
E_{1}
,
N_{2}
for energy
E_{2}
, and so on, where
N=N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{3}
(take 3 solutions as an example). Therefore, it is unnecessary to solve for
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
and
N_{3}
individually as
N\to\infty
. Instead, we further assume
x_{1}=N_{1}/N
,
x_{2}=N_{2}/N
,
x_{3}=N_{3}/N
, and solve for
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
, which can yield rigorous solutions.

Now, we present the new procedure. Let us assume

f_{1}(E)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\delta(E-E_{1n}),

(15)

where
N
represents the number of solutions to Eq.(
13
), and
x_{n}
denotes the weight of the corresponding beam. This assumption is subject to the constraints:

\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}=1,~~0\leq x_{n}\leq 1,

(16)

and

\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}E_{1n}=E_{1}.

(17)

We aim to maximize

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn}.

(18)

Given a value of
\lambda
, we can determine all
E_{1n}
. Subsequently, we can solve for
x_{n}
as a linear programming problem, using Eqs.(
16
)-(
18
). By exploring the maximum of
\langle\sigma v\rangle
for all
\lambda
within the range
[\min(g),\max(g)]
, we can establish the upper limit of fusion reactivity, along with the corresponding values of
x_{n}
and
E_{1n}
. Further justification for the assumption of
f_{1}(v)
comprising several beams is provided in
B
.

3
Results

We can begin by gaining some analytical insights. It is evident from Eq. (
18
) that for any
E_{1}
, we must have
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{\rm max}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn,\max}=\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn,\max}
.

For
E_{1}>E_{1c}
, where
E_{1c}
represents energy with the maximum
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn}
, we can set
x_{1}\to 1
and
x_{2}\to 0
with
E_{11}=E_{1c}
and
E_{12}\to\infty
to satisfy Eq. (
17

x_{1}E_{11}+x_{2}E_{12}=E_{1}

\langle\sigma v\rangle=x_{1}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{G1}+x_{2}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{G2}=\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn,\max}
, indicating that the maximum reactivity approaches a constant, i.e.,
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{\rm max}=\langle\sigma v\rangle_{Gn,\max}
for

We now numerically calculate the theoretical upper limit versus

for different

using the method outlined in subsection
2.3

4
Summary and Conclusion

In this study, we have determined the maximum fusion reactivity for a non-thermal reactant, specifically deuterium (D) for D-T fusion and proton (p) for p-B11 fusion, in the presence of a thermal Maxwellian background reactant, which is tritium (T) for D-T and boron (B) for p-B11 fusion. Our analysis focused on these two fusion scenarios. We optimized the velocity distribution of the first reactant while keeping its total energy constant, with the goal of achieving the maximum fusion reactivity. The results indicate that the optimized velocity distribution typically comprises one or two beams.

In the context of fusion energy research, we found that the maximum fusion reactivity can often exceed that of the conventional Maxwellian-Maxwellian reactants case by a significant margin, ranging from 50% to 300%, within the fusion energy-relevant energy range. The corresponding distribution functions of the first reactant exhibit one or several beam-like features. These findings establish an upper limit for fusion reactivity and provide valuable insights into enhancing fusion reactivity through non-thermal fusion, which holds particular significance in the field of fusion energy research.

It’s important to note that this work primarily addresses the mathematical aspects of the problem, and further studies are required to explore the underlying physical implications. For instance, while the cold background of the distribution in Case 1 (Fig.
9
) may not significantly enhance fusion reactivity, it serves to reduce the average kinetic energy of the system. Additionally, considering the effects of collisions and plasma transport, the actual velocity distributions are subject to change and tend to relax toward thermal distributions. As future research directions, it would be essential to investigate the relaxation of distributions in realistic plasma scenarios in the time scale of confinement time of the plasmas, and explore the implications of having a non-thermal
f_{2}({\bm{v}})
.
In theoretical terms, while we assert that the results presented in this work represent an upper limit, it’s important to note that the demonstration provided here is more akin to a physicist’s proof rather than a rigorously formal mathematical proof. A rigorous mathematical proof is beyond the scope of the present work.

Appendix A
Fusion Reactivity for Drift Maxwellian Reactants

The fusion reactivity of two drift Maxwellian reactants forms the basis for the present work and can be regarded as a type of Green function. Here, we provide details in this appendix. The velocity distribution function is given by:

f_{j}({\bm{v}})=\Big(\frac{m_{j}}{2\pi k_{B}T_{j}}\Big)^{3/2}\exp\Big[-\frac{m_{j}({\bm{v}}-{\bm{v}}_{dj})^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{j}}\Big],

(19)

where
j=1,2
, and
k_{B}
represents the Boltzmann constant. The fusion reactivity is given by
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{DM}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}v_{tr}v_{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(v)v^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{v^{2}+v_{d}^{2}}{v_{tr}^{2}}\Big)\cdot\sinh\Big(2\frac{vv_{d}}{v_{tr}^{2}}\Big)dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi m_{r}k_{B}^{2}T_{r}T_{d}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(E)\sqrt{E}\cdot

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\frac{E+E_{d}}{k_{B}T_{r}}\Big)\sinh\Big(\frac{2\sqrt{EE_{d}}}{k_{B}T_{r}}\Big)dE,

Here,
\sinh(x)=(e^{x}-e^{-x})/2\simeq x+x^{3}/6+\cdots
. The effective temperature
T_{r}
, thermal velocity
v_{tr}
, drift velocity
v_{d}
, and drift energy
E_{d}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle T_{r}=\frac{m_{1}T_{2}+m_{2}T_{1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},~~v_{tr}=\sqrt{\frac{2k_{B}T_{r}}{m_{r}}},

\displaystyle v_{d}=|{\bm{v}}_{d2}-{\bm{v}}_{d1}|,~~E_{d}\equiv k_{B}T_{d}=\frac{m_{r}v_{d}^{2}}{2}.

(21)

It’s important to note that the drift velocity
{\bm{v}}_{dj}
can be in arbitrary directions, meaning
{\bm{v}}_{d1}
and
{\bm{v}}_{d2}
are not required to be in the same direction.

When
{\bm{v}}_{dj}=0
, Eq.(
20
) reduces to the well-known reactivity for two Maxwellian reactants
[
19
]
:

\langle\sigma v\rangle_{M}=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi m_{r}}}\frac{1}{(k_{B}T_{r})^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(E)E\exp\Big(-\frac{E}{k_{B}T_{r}}\Big)dE.

(22)

For
T_{1}\to 0
, Eq.(
20
) reduces to:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{G}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}v_{t2}v_{d1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(v)v^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{v^{2}+v_{d1}^{2}}{v_{t2}^{2}}\Big)\cdot\sinh\Big(2\frac{vv_{d1}}{v_{t2}^{2}}\Big)dv,

where

If further

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{G}=\sigma(v_{d1})v_{d1},

(24)

representing the beam-target case.

Appendix B
When
f_{1}(v)
is a Continuous Function

We demonstrate that when
f_{1}(v)
is a continuous function, a solution does not exist. Therefore, we assume that
f_{1}(v)
as a series of delta functions is a reasonable choice. For simplification, we will consider the case when
T_{2}=0
, which implies that
f_{2}({\bm{v}}_{2})=\delta({\bm{v}}_{2})
. Consequently, we have
\langle\sigma v\rangle=\int d{\bm{v}}\sigma(v)vf_{1}({\bm{v}})
.

We start with the normalization condition:

\displaystyle\int d{\bm{v}}f_{1}({\bm{v}})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi v^{2}\sin\theta f_{1}(v,\theta,\phi)=1.

(25)

We redefine:

\displaystyle f_{1}(v)=\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi v^{2}\sin\theta f_{1}(v,\theta,\phi),

(26)

which yields:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dvf_{1}(v)=1,

(27)

\displaystyle E_{1}=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dvv^{2}f_{1}(v)=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}v_{t1}^{2},

(28)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\sigma(v)vf_{1}(v).

(29)

Now, we need to find
f_{1}(v)
to maximize
\langle\sigma v\rangle
. Assuming
f_{1}(v)
is a continuous function, we can expand it using orthogonal basis functions:

\displaystyle f_{1}(v)=\sum a_{n}h_{n}(v),

(30)

with:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{n}(v)h_{m}(v)dv=\delta_{mn}.

(31)

Hence:

\displaystyle\sum_{n}a_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{n}(v)dv=1,

(32)

\displaystyle\sum_{n}a_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dvv^{2}h_{n}(v)=v_{t1}^{2},

(33)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle=\sum_{n}a_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\sigma(v)vh_{n}(v).

(34)

We define the Lagrangian:

\displaystyle L(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle-\lambda_{1}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}dvf_{1}(v)-1\Big]-\lambda_{2}\Big[\int_{0}^{\infty}dvv^{2}f_{1}(v)-v_{t1}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}a_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\sigma(v)vh_{n}(v)-\lambda_{1}\Big[\sum_{n}a_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{n}(v)dv-1\Big]

\displaystyle-\lambda_{2}\Big[\sum_{n}a_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dvv^{2}h_{n}(v)-v_{t1}^{2}\Big].

This requires:

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial L(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}{\partial a_{n}}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dv\sigma(v)vh_{n}(v)-\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{n}(v)dv-\lambda_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dvv^{2}h_{n}(v).

It turns out that there are no values of
a_{n}
that can satisfy the above equation, which implies that the assumption in Eq.(
30
) is unreasonable. In other words,
f_{1}(v)
should not be a continuous function.
