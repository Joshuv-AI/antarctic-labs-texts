Quantitative and stable limits of high-frequency statistics of Lévy processes: a Stein’s method approach Thanks: The authors gratefully acknowledge financial support of ERC Consolidator Grant 815703 “STAMFORD: Statistical Methods for High Dimensional Diffusions”.
Chiara Amorino, Arturo Jaramillo, Mark Podolskij

Abstract
We establish inequalities for assessing the distance between the distribution of errors of partially observed high-frequency statistics of multidimensional Lévy processes and that of a mixed Gaussian random variable. Furthermore, we provide a general result guaranteeing stable convergence. Our arguments rely on a suitable adaptation of the Stein’s method perspective to the context of mixed Gaussian distributions, specifically tailored to the framework of high-frequency statistics. Keywords: High frequency data, Lévy process, mixed normal distribution, Stein’s method AMS 2010 subject classifications: Primary 62E17, 60F05 secondary 60G51

Introduction

The aim of this paper is to establish a collection of mathematical techniques, allowing one to describe mixed Gaussian convergences and tightly assess the associated marginal distance, within the context of high-frequency statistics of Lévy processes. More precisely, given a
d
-dimensional Lévy process
\bm{X}=(\bm{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the form
\bm{X}_{t}=(X_{t}^{(1)},\dots,X_{t}^{(d)})
, defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, we consider the high-frequency statistics

\displaystyle G_{t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}g\left(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{{I}}_{i,n}\right),

(1.1)

for
t\geq 0
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d(m+2)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{i,n}:=\left(a_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i}{n}}-\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}}),a_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i+1}{n}}-\bm{X}_{\frac{i}{n}}),\dots,a_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i+m}{n}}-\bm{X}_{\frac{i+m-1}{n}})\right)

(1.2)

for
m\geq 0
and some suitable normalization constant
a_{n}
. The monitoring of any given time-evolving system is typically composed of an entirely observable component which is progressively revealed and a random perturbation. Such high-frequency statistics are very popular in e.g. mathematical finance, where
\bm{X}
represents a price process. For more specific examples of functionals of type (
1.1
), we refer to estimation of local times of
\alpha
-stable Lévy processes
[
1
]
, (weighted) characteristic functions
[
8
]
and (weighted) empirical distributed functions
[
25
]
.
We recommend the monograph
[
7
]
for a comprehensive study of high-frequency statistics of Itô semimartingales.
From the martingale theory perspective the natural centring
of
g(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})
is given by conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{(i-1)/n}]
. Thus, the quantity of interest becomes the statistic

\displaystyle E_{t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\left(g(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})-\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{\frac{i-1}{n}}]\right).

The description of the asymptotic behavior of
E_{t}^{(n)}
is the main object of our study, with emphasis on determining bounds for rate of convergence of a suitable probabilistic distance between the underlying marginal distributions and their limit.

In order to tackle this problem, we extend the ideas of Stein’s method, developed by Charles Stein in his fundamental work
[
24
]
, into a framework designed ad-hoc to the problem described above, which will allow us to both determine asymptotic mixed normality and assess the associated rate of convergence. It is worth observing that a universal Stein’s method for mixed Gaussian limits constitutes a virtually unexplored topic in the current literature. This is due to the flexibility of choosing a random variance in a mixed Gaussian limit approximation, which shatters the simplicity of its distribution and demands very precise knowledge of the sequence of distributions under consideration, information which is not available in most cases. Besides this technical challenge, a handful of special instances have been treated in the literature, among which we highlight the work on additive functionals of the Brownian motion by Papanicolau-Stroock-Varadhan Theorem (see e.g.
[
22
, Theorem XIII-(2.6) and Proposition XIII-(2.8)]
), the subsequent extensions to the fractional Brownian motion case by Hu, Jaramillo, Nualart, Nourdin, Peccati and Xu in
[
4
,
9
,
16
]
, and the work of Nourdin et.al.
[
12
,
13
]
, where mixed Gaussian limits are studied in the context of variables in the Wiener chaos. In the continuous semimartingale setting Edgeworth expansions associated with mixed Gaussian limits and their applications have been investigated in
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
26
]
, with Malliavin calculus and martingale techniques being the main mathematical tools. Later the theoretical results have been extended to the framework of Skorohod integrals
in
[
17
]
.

Despite the aforementioned studies,
a complete understanding of the mixed Gaussian phenomenology is far from being complete, as the entirety of the available results are valid only in settings where the underlying randomness emerges from Gaussian processes. The goal of this paper is to
contribute to the completion of this theory. In particular, we provide a general
criterion

for stable convergence towards a mixed normal limit in Theorem
3.1
, which relies on an adaptation of Stein’s lemma and does not require the random variables to be
embedded in a Gaussian space. The reach of the technique is illustrated by some remarks and example of applications, such as its implementation in the study of
E_{t}^{(n)}
. Our second main statement is Theorem
3.7
, which studies the rates of convergence associated with
asymptotic mixed normality of the statistic
E_{t}^{(n)}
. This result builds upon the classical ideas from the Lindeberg method
[
11
]
and the
K
-function approach
[
3
]
, although its implementation requires the incorporation of new ideas that could deal with the stochastic variance of the limit.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the basic setting under which we state our results and present some particular instances that illustrate (and motivate) the reach of the theory developed in the manuscript. In Section
3
, we present our main results, which include stable limit theorems and a quantitative evaluation of the marginal distribution of the processes under consideration. In Section
4
, we prove a general criterion for asymptotic mixed normality and the previously mentioned quantitative assessment. Additional technical results, along with their proofs, can be found in the Supplementary Material.

The setting and overview

Consider a univariate Lévy process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and let
\psi
be its Lévy exponent defined through

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\bm{i}\beta X_{t})]=\exp(t\psi(\beta)),\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{R}.

By the Lévy-Khintchine formula,
\psi
has the form

\psi(\beta)=\bm{i}\gamma\beta-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\exp(\bm{i}\beta x)-1-\bm{i}\beta x\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|<1\}}\right)\Pi(dx),

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\geq 0
and
\Pi(dx)
is a Radon measure on
[-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(x^{2}\land 1)\Pi(dx)<\infty
.
As characteristic functions uniquely determine their underlying probability distributions, each Lévy process is uniquely determined by the Lévy-Khintchine triplet
(\gamma,\sigma,\Pi)
. Standard textbooks on the topic include
[
2
,
10
,
23
]
.

Throughout this work we consider a
d
-dimensional Lévy process whose components
X_{t}^{(1)},...,X_{t}^{(d)}
are iid and have Lévy triplet
(\gamma,\sigma,\Pi)
. Our results will be stated in terms of a suitable smooth distance, which we define next. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the collection of functions defined by

\mathcal{H}:=\left\{\psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\mbox{ test function such that }\left\|\psi^{(l)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1\mbox{ for }l=0,1,\dots,5\right\}.

The family of functions gives raise to the smooth metric

\displaystyle d(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{\psi\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\mu(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)\nu(dx)\right|,

(2.1)

on the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. Observe that if
\mu_{n}
is a sequence of probability measures satisfying
d(\mu_{n},\nu)\rightarrow 0
as
n
tends to infinity, then the characteristic function of
\mu_{n}
converges pointwise towards the characteristic function of
\nu
. In particular, the metric
d
induces a topology on the class of probability measures over
\mathbb{R}
which is stronger than the topology of convergence in distribution.

Theorem
3.7
below reveals that the proper scaling of
E_{t}^{(n)}
required for yielding an asymptotic non-trivial limit is
{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, reason for which the forthcoming results will be stated in terms of the following rescaling of
E_{t}^{(n)}
:

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\left(g(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})-\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{\frac{i-1}{n}}]\right).

(2.2)

The choice of
a_{n}
, which has been introduced in (
1.2
), will be made in such a way that for every
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
i\leq n
, the sequence
\text{Var}[g\left(\bm{x},\mathcal{I}_{i,n}\right)]
converges as
n
tends to infinity. Naturally, under suitable conditions over
g
, this convergence will automatically hold whenever
a_{n}
is such that
a_{n}\textbf{X}_{1/n}
converges in law.

In addition to this, a number of adequate extra hypotheses will be needed, but before discussing them in detail we present some particular instances of our results in order to motivate the reader.

A representative statement

One of the main motivations of the present paper is to explore in full generality the asymptotic behavior of
Z_{t}^{(n)}
for the case where
X
is an
\alpha
-stable process with i.i.d. components and
\alpha\in(0,2)
. The case where
X
is a Brownian motion is of interest as well, although it has been studied thoroughly by means of martingale techniques (see e.g.
[
7
]
) in the case
m=0
. The martingale techniques in the case
m>0
are not completely obvious and their level of complexity seems to be comparable to the Stein’s approach presented in Theorem
3.1
.

As mentioned earlier, one of the methodological breakthroughs of our work is the successful implementation of Stein’s method for high-frequency statistics converging towards a mixed Gaussian limit. Our main findings are stated in full generality in Theorem
3.7
and Theorem
3.4

below, and the following results are immediate consequences of such general statements. For this purpose, for a general Lévy
process
\bm{X}
, we introduce
3m+1
independent copies of
\bm{X}
denoted
\tilde{\bm{X}}^{(1-m)},...,\tilde{\bm{X}}^{(2m)}
and the limit as
n
tends to infinity of the covariance of
g
. In particular, we introduce the functions
\mathfrak{g}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{n}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=-m}^{m}Cov\left(g(\bm{x},a_{n}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\frac{1}{n}}^{(1)},...,a_{n}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\frac{1}{n}}^{(m+1)}),g(\bm{x},a_{n}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\frac{1}{n}}^{(j+1)},...,a_{n}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\frac{1}{n}}^{(j+m+1)})\right).

(2.3)

In the sequel, we will assume the pointwise existence of the limit
\lim_{n}\mathfrak{g}_{n}
, which is denoted by
\mathfrak{g}
; namely,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\lim_{n}\mathfrak{g}_{n}(\bm{x}).

(2.4)

Observe that in the particular case where
\bm{X}
is
an
\alpha
-stable Lévy process, one naturally chose
a_{n}=n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
. Then,
the sequence
\mathfrak{g}_{n}(\bm{x})
is a constant sequence over
n
, so in particular
\mathfrak{g}(\bm{x})=\mathfrak{g}_{n}(\bm{x})
for all
n
.

Theorem 2.1

(Special case of Theorem
3.7
)
.

Let
\bm{X}
be a symmetric
\alpha
-stable process with
\alpha\in(0,2)
and assume that

is such that
\|g\|_{\infty},\|\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}\|_{\infty},\|\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\|_{\infty}<\infty,
for all
i,j=1,\dots,d
. Then, the function

\displaystyle A_{s}:=\mathfrak{g}(\bm{X}_{s})

(2.5)

is well defined. Under these conditions, for all

, there exists a standard Brownian motion

, defined on an extended probability space, such that

1.

When

\displaystyle d\left(Z_{t}^{(n)},\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{A_{s}}W(ds)\right)\leq Cn^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Remark 2.2
.

We remark that the upper bound (

In order to prove the asymptotic mixed normality of

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{i}^{n})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{(i-1)/n}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{0}^{t}A_{s}ds,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{n}(M_{i/n}-M_{(i-1)/n})|\mathcal{F}_{(i-1)/n}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{i}^{n})^{2}1_{\{|\xi_{i}^{n}|>\epsilon\}}|\mathcal{F}_{(i-1)/n}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,\qquad\forall\epsilon>0,

(2.13)

where condition (

3
Main results

In this section, we describe our main findings, whose presentation we split into sections addressing the functional behavior and quantitative estimates respectively.

Asymptotic functional description of
Z^{(n)}

In the sequel, we will require the notion of finite dimensional
stable convergence
, which is originally due to Renyi
[
21
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}=\sigma(X_{t}:~t\geq 0)
where
X
is a Lévy process with characteristic exponent as described in Section
2
. Let
r\geq 1
be a given positive integer. For
\mathbb{R}^{r}
-valued
random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and

Y
we say that
Y_{n}
converges
\mathcal{G}
-stably in law to
Y
, where
Y
is defined on an extension

(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathbb{P}^{\prime})
of the original probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, if and only if for any continuous bounded function
f:\mathbb{R}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
and any bounded

\mathcal{G}
-measurable random variable
F
it holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Ff(Y_{n})]=\mathbb{E}^{\prime}[Ff(Y)].

(3.1)

In this case we write
Y^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{G}-\textup{\tiny st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y
.

The next result not only gives a sufficient Stein type condition for asymptotic mixed normality, but also guarantees stable convergence. We demonstrate it in a rather general setting as it may have interest in its own right. Its proof is given in Section
4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{F}
. We consider
\mathbb{R}^{r}
-valued random variables
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and a random diagonal matrix

\Sigma=\text{\rm diag}(V_{1}^{2},\ldots,V_{r}^{2})
, which is assumed to be
\mathcal{G}
-measurable. Furthermore, we assume that
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[\|S_{n}\|^{2}]<\infty
,

\mathbb{E}[V_{j}^{2}]<\infty
for all
j=1,\ldots,r
, and all
V_{j}^{2}
’s have no positive mass at
0
. If the convergence

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[FS_{n}^{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}h(S_{n})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[FV_{k}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}^{2}}h(S_{n})\right]\right)\to 0

(3.2)

holds for all bounded
\mathcal{G}
-measurable random variables
F
and all functions
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{r};\mathbb{R})
with
\|\nabla h\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla^{2}h\|_{\infty}<\infty
,
then we obtain the stable convergence

S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{G}-\textup{\tiny st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}S=\Sigma^{1/2}N,

(3.3)

where
N\sim\mathcal{N}_{r}(0,\text{id})
is a standard
r
-dimensional normal variable defined on an extended space and independent of
\mathcal{G}
. In particular,
S
has a mixed normal distribution with mean
0
and conditional variance
\Sigma
.

We remark that condition (
3.2
) does not provide the rate of convergence towards the limit
S
(in contrast to classical Stein’s method). This is not surprising as stable convergence is not metrizable in general.

Remark 3.2
.

The paper
[
15
]
discusses another technique to show stable convergence of weighted functionals of fractional Brownian motion towards a mixed normal distribution, which is somewhat similar in spirit to the statement Theorem
3.1
. The authors consider a conditional characteristic function of their statistic and prove via Malliavin calculus that it asymptotically satisfies the differential equation determined by the conditional characteristic function of the limit.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Theorem
3.1
has multiple applications, as (
3.2
) provides a convenient condition for obtaining stable convergence in several settings. We briefly demonstrate an example
of application of Theorem
3.1
in the Gaussian setting. The interested reader is referred to
[

Consider a univariate isonormal Gaussian process

associated with a real separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
and a sequence of Skorohod integrals

sufficient conditions that guarantee asymptotic mixed normality for the sequence

. More specifically, they show that conditions

\displaystyle\langle u_{n},h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\xrightarrow{L^{1}}0\qquad\text{for all }h\in\mathbb{H},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\langle u_{n},DS_{n}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\xrightarrow{L^{1}}\Sigma,

(3.5)

where

denotes the Malliavin derivative,
imply the stable convergence
S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{G}-\textup{\tiny st}}}{{\longrightarrow}}S=\Sigma^{1/2}N
, where
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is defined on an extended space and is independent of
\mathcal{G}
. This result easily follows
from our Theorem
3.1
via checking condition (
3.2
).
Indeed, the integration by parts formula gives the identity

For

(3.7)

By an approximation argument the above convergence holds for any bounded random variable

On the other hand, via condition (

(3.8)

Hence, Theorem

. ∎

7.3
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Recall that
h_{\gamma}
is the
unique bounded
solution of the Stein equation

\displaystyle xh_{\gamma}^{\prime}(x)-\gamma h_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi(x)-\mathbb{E}[\psi(\sqrt{\gamma}N)],

for a positive real
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. By
[
14
, Proposition 4.3.2]
,

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\psi(\sqrt{\gamma}N)]-{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(e^{-\theta}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2\theta}}\sqrt{\gamma}N)]\Big)d\theta.

From here it follows that

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\theta}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\psi^{\prime}(e^{-\theta}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2\theta}}\sqrt{\gamma}N)\right]d\theta,

and consequently, if
\tilde{\gamma}>0
is another positive real,

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}^{\prime}(x)-h_{\tilde{\gamma}}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\theta}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Psi_{x,\theta}(\sqrt{\gamma}N)-\Psi_{x,\theta}(\sqrt{\tilde{\gamma}}N)\right]d\theta,

(7.25)

where
\Psi_{x,\theta}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the function

\displaystyle\Psi_{x,\theta}(y)

\displaystyle:=\psi^{\prime}\left(e^{-\theta}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2\theta}}y\right).

One can easily check that
\Psi_{x,\theta}\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and

\|\Psi_{x,\theta}^{(4)}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\psi^{(5)}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

Thus, using Equation (
7.25
) and Lemma
8.1
, as well as the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi_{x,\theta}}{\partial y^{2}}(y)

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-2\theta})\psi^{(3)}\left(e^{-\theta}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2\theta}}y\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{4}\Psi_{x,\theta}}{\partial y^{4}}(y)

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-2\theta})^{2}\psi^{(5)}\left(e^{-\theta}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2\theta}}y\right),

we obtain

\displaystyle\left|h_{\gamma}^{\prime}(x)-h_{\tilde{\gamma}}^{\prime}(x)-(\gamma-\tilde{\gamma})\mathcal{V}[\psi](x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\theta}\|\psi^{(5)}\|_{\infty}|\gamma-\tilde{\gamma}|^{2}d\theta,

which proves the statement of Lemma
5.1
.

Then, we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[F\,{(Z_{t_{q}}^{(n)}-Z_{t_{q-1}}^{(n)})}{\frac{\partial h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}}}({\mathcal{Z}_{n}})\right]\\
\begin{aligned} &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i={\lfloor nt_{q-1}\rfloor+1}}^{{\lfloor nt_{q}\rfloor}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})(F-\dot{F}[i]){\frac{\partial h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}}}({\mathcal{Z}_{n}})\right]\\
&+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i={\lfloor nt_{q-1}\rfloor+1}}^{{\lfloor nt_{q}\rfloor}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})\dot{F}[i]({\frac{\partial h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}}}({\mathcal{Z}_{n}})-{\frac{\partial h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}}}({\dot{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}[i]})\right]\\
&+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i={\lfloor nt_{q-1}\rfloor+1}}^{{\lfloor nt_{q}\rfloor}}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})\dot{F}[i]{\frac{\partial h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}}}({\dot{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}[i]})\right]\\
&=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.\end{aligned}

We start dealing with
I_{3}
. We observe that
\dot{F}[i]{\frac{\partial h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}}}({\dot{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}[i]})
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}
. Then,

\displaystyle I_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i={\lfloor nt_{q-1}\rfloor+1}}^{{\lfloor nt_{q}\rfloor}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[f_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})|\mathcal{F}^{(i)}]\dot{F}[i]{\frac{\partial h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}}}({\dot{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}[i]})\right]=0.

Regarding
I_{1}
, from the boundedness of
f_{n}
and
{\nabla h_{V_{q}}}
we conclude that

|I_{1}|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[|F-\dot{F}[i]|\right],

where
t
is defined by
t:=t_{1}\vee\cdots\vee t_{r}
. Thus, from the definition of
F
and
\dot{F}[i]
it is straightforward to see that,
thanks to the fact that
\bm{X}_{s}-\dot{\bm{X}}_{s}[i]
is as in (
5.5
),

\displaystyle{}\mathbb{E}\left[|F-\dot{F}[i]|\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}+Cn^{-1}\left(1+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha=1\}}\log n\right),

(7.29)

where we have also used Lemma
8.2
. It follows that

|I_{1}|\leq Cn^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}}+Cn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha=1\}}\log n\right),

which clearly converges to
0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
.

To conclude the proof we deal with
I_{2}
.
We underline that it is the same as the main term appearing in (
5.2
) in the previous proof, with an extra
\dot{F}[i]
, before the application of Taylor approximation. It is easy to check that, as
\dot{F}[i]
is independent from
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}
, the proof of Theorem
3.7
starting from Taylor development as in (
5.13
) still applies.

Define

\displaystyle I_{2,0}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{p=1}^{r}\mathbbm{1}_{\{p\neq q\}}\sum_{i=\lfloor nt_{q-1}+1\rfloor}^{\lfloor nt_{q}\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})\dot{F}[i]\frac{\partial^{2}h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}x_{p}}(\mathcal{Z}_{n})(Z_{t_{p}}^{(n)}-\dot{Z}_{t_{p}}^{(n)}[i])\right]

\displaystyle I_{2,1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=\lfloor nt_{q-1}+1\rfloor}^{\lfloor nt_{q}\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(\bm{X}_{\frac{i-1}{n}},\mathcal{I}_{i,n})\dot{F}[i]\frac{\partial^{2}h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}^{2}}({\mathcal{Z}_{n}})(Z_{t_{q}}^{(n)}-\dot{Z}_{t_{q}}^{(n)}[i])\right],

and
I_{2,2}:=I_{2}-(I_{2,0}+I_{2,1})
. Observe that
I_{2,2}
plays a similar role to
T_{3,n}
in the proof of Theorem
3.7
. However, on
I_{2,0}+I_{2,1}
, we have not yet applied the decomposition derived from (
5.14
) and (
5.15
). Once applied, it will become evident that both
I_{2,0}
and
I_{2,1}
can be viewed as
T_{1,n}+T_{2,n}+T_{4,n}
, where
h^{\prime\prime}_{V}(Z_{t}^{(n)})
is replaced by
\frac{\partial^{2}h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}x_{p}}(\mathcal{Z}n)
in the first case and
\frac{\partial^{2}h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}^{2}}({\mathcal{Z}_{n}})
in the second. Then, as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
, we have that
I_{2,2}
goes to
0
due to the
L^{2}
bound on the increments
(Z_{t}^{(n)}-\dot{Z}_{t}^{(n)}[i])
gathered in Lemma
5.2
, while
I_{2,0}+I_{2,1}
is the main contribution. Applying the same decomposition and the same approach as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
, and using that
\beta_{n}
defined as in (
3.11
) converges to
0
because of (
3.9
), one obtains that
|I_{2,1}-\mathbb{E}[\dot{F}[i]V_{q}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}^{2}}(\mathcal{Z}_{n})]|\rightarrow 0
and
I_{2,0}\rightarrow 0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The proof is complete once one notices that, according with the arguing above,

I_{2}-\mathbb{E}[FV_{q}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}^{2}}(\mathcal{Z}_{n})]=\mathbb{E}[(F-\dot{F}[i])V_{q}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{V_{q}}}{\partial x_{q}^{2}}(\mathcal{Z}_{n})]+o(1)

and that the first term in the right hand side above converges towards
0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
thanks to (
7.29
).

∎

8
Technical lemmas

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})
. Then we have that for every function
\psi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
with
\|\psi^{(4)}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)-\psi(Y)-(\sigma^{2}-s^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(Y)]|

\displaystyle\leq 4\|\psi^{(4)}\|_{\infty}|s^{2}-\sigma^{2}|^{2}.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\sigma^{2}>s^{2}
.
Remark that in the left hand side above we can replace
X
and
Y
with any coupling of
X
and
Y
. In particular, we can choose
X
and
Y
such that
,
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{=}}Y+\varepsilon Z
, where
\varepsilon:=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}-s^{2}}
and
Z
is a standard Gaussian random variable independent of
Y
. By the independence of
Y
and
Z
,
\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(Y)Z]=\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(Y)Z^{3}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(Y)Z^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\psi^{\prime}(Y)]
. Consequently,

|\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)-\psi(Y)-(\sigma^{2}-s^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(Y)]|\\
\begin{aligned} &=|\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y+\varepsilon Z)-\psi(Y)-\psi^{\prime}(Y)(\varepsilon Z)-\psi^{\prime\prime}(Y)(\varepsilon^{2}Z^{2})-\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(Y)(\varepsilon^{3}Z^{3})]]|\\
&\leq\|\psi^{(4)}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}[|\varepsilon Z|^{4}]|=3\|\psi^{(4)}\|_{\infty}\varepsilon^{4}.\end{aligned}

In the case
\sigma^{2}<s^{2}
, we have that
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Law}}{{=}}X+\delta Z
, where
\delta:=\sqrt{s^{2}-\sigma^{2}}
. Proceeding as before, we get

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y)-\psi(X)-(s^{2}-\sigma^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(X)]|

\displaystyle\leq 3\|\psi^{(4)}\|_{\infty}\delta^{4}.

Combining this inequality with the relation

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[(s^{2}-\sigma^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(X)]-\mathbb{E}[(s^{2}-\sigma^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(Y)]|

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{4}\|\psi^{(4)}\|_{\infty},

we get

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}[\psi(Y)-\psi(X)-(s^{2}-\sigma^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}(Y)]|

\displaystyle\leq 4\|\psi^{(4)}\|_{\infty}\delta^{4}.

The result easily follows from here.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\bm{X}
be a process satisfying (
2.14
) for some
\alpha\in(0,2]
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-1}\left(1+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha=2\}}\log(n)\right).

(8.30)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}+n^{-1}\left(1+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha=1\}}\log(n)\right)\right).

(8.31)

In the case
\alpha>1
, the above inequalities generalize to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-1}\left(1+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha=2\}}\log(n)\right).

(8.32)

If
\alpha=2
and
\textbf{H}_{3}
holds true for all
t\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{-1}.

(8.33)

Proof.

We first consider the case
\alpha\in(0,2)
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\geq 1]+\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

(8.34)

so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa n^{-1}+\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\kappa n^{-1}+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}s\mathbbm{1}_{\{s\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}ds\right]=\kappa n^{-1}+2\int_{0}^{1}s\mathbb{P}[s\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|]ds

\displaystyle\leq\kappa n^{-1}+2\int_{0}^{1}s(1\wedge(\kappa n^{-1}s^{-\alpha}))ds.

We get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s(1\wedge(\kappa n^{-1}s^{-\alpha}))ds

\displaystyle\leq\kappa n^{-1}\int_{\kappa^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}^{1}s^{1-\alpha}ds+\int_{0}^{\kappa^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}sds

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leq C\left(n^{-1}+n^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}\right)\leq Cn^{-1},}

(8.35)

yielding the desired result. For handling the case
\alpha=2
, the above analysis gives

\displaystyle{}\int_{0}^{1}s(1\wedge(\kappa n^{-1}s^{-2}))ds

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}\log(\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{1}{2}})+\frac{1}{2}\kappa n^{-1},

(8.36)

giving the desired bound. The proof of (
8.31
) follows by a similar reasoning. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\geq 1]+\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa n^{-1}+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}ds\right]\leq\kappa n^{-1}+\int_{0}^{1}(1\wedge(\kappa n^{-1}s^{-\alpha}))ds.

As before we obtain for
\alpha\neq 1
:

\int_{0}^{1}\left(1\wedge(\kappa n^{-1}s^{-\alpha})\right)ds\leq\kappa n^{-1}+\kappa^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}n^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}},

while for
\alpha=1
we have

\int_{0}^{1}\left(1\wedge(\kappa n^{-1}s^{-1})\right)ds\leq\kappa n^{-1}\log(n)+\kappa^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}n^{-1}.

Hence, (
8.31
) holds true.

For proving inequality (
8.32
), we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\geq 1\}}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|]+\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{1\vee s\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\}}ds\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}s\mathbbm{1}_{\{s\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}ds\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\kappa n^{-1}(1\vee s)^{-\alpha}ds+2\int_{0}^{1}s(1\wedge(\kappa n^{-1}s^{-\alpha}))ds.

The first integral is bounded by
C/n
due to the condition
\alpha>1
, while the second one can be bounded by means of inequality (
8
),
while the case
\alpha=2
is studied in (
8.36
)
. Inequality (
8.32
) follows from here.

For the case where
\alpha=2
and in addition
\textbf{H}_{3}
holds for some
\gamma>1
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\geq 1\}}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|].

Now, using that

\int_{0}^{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|}sds=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2},\qquad\int_{0}^{\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|}ds=\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\wedge\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}s\mathbbm{1}_{\{s\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\leq 1\}}ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s\lor 1\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\}}ds\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}s\mathbb{P}[s\leq\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|]ds+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\|\bm{X}_{1/n}\|\geq 1\lor s]ds

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}+2\int_{0}^{1}s(1\wedge(\kappa(n^{-1}s^{-2})^{\gamma}))ds+{\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(n^{-1}(1\lor s)^{-2}\Big)^{\gamma}ds.

The first integral on the right can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s(1\wedge(\kappa(n^{-1}s^{-2})^{\gamma}))ds

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}u(1\wedge(\kappa u^{-2\gamma}))du,

while the second is bounded by
n^{-\gamma}(\int_{0}^{1}1ds+\int_{1}^{\infty}s^{-2\gamma}ds)
.
The result follows from the fact that
\gamma>1
.

∎
