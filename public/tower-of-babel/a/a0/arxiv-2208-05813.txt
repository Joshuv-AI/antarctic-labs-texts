Stiefel-Whitney Classes of representations of SL ⁡ ( 2 , q ) \SL(2,q)
Neha Malik, Steven Spallone

Abstract
. We describe the Stiefel-Whitney classes (SWCs) of orthogonal representations π \pi of the finite special linear groups G = SL ⁡ ( 2 , 𝔽 q ) G=\SL(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}) , in terms of character values of π \pi . From this calculation, we can answer interesting questions about SWCs of π \pi . For instance, we determine the subalgebra of H ∗ ​ ( G , ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ ) H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}) generated by the SWCs of orthogonal π \pi , and we also determine which π \pi have nontrivial mod 2 2 Euler class.

1.
Introduction

The theory of Stiefel-Whitney classes (SWCs) of vector bundles is an old unifying concept in geometry. Real representations, or equivalently, orthogonal complex representations of a group
G
give rise to
flat
vector bundles over the classifying space
BG
. Via this construction one associates SWCs
w_{i}(\pi)\in H^{i}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
to an orthogonal representation
\pi
of
G
. Their sum
w(\pi)
, living in the group cohomology
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
, is known as the
total Stiefel-Whitney class
of
\pi
.

There do not seem to be many explicit calculations of such SWCs, notably when
G
is nonabelian. (However see
[
8
]
.) This paper contains formulas for SWCs in terms of character values for
\SL(2,q)
, when
q
is a prime power, achieved via
detection results.
Formulas for SWCs of cyclic groups are well-known; we review this for the group of order
2
in Section
2.5
. The second SWCs of representations of
S_{n}
and related groups were found in
[
12
]
.

The case of
\GL(n,q)
with
q
odd was completed recently by Ganguly-Joshi in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
. To explain how their work relates to ours, we need to introduce “detection”.

Let
G
be a finite group, and write
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
for the mod
2
group cohomology ring of
G
. Let
H^{*}_{\SW}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
be the subalgebra of
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
generated by SWCs
w_{i}(\pi)
of orthogonal representations
\pi
of
G
. We say a subgroup
K

detects
the mod
2
cohomology of
G
when the restriction map

i^{*}:H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\to H^{*}(K,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})

is an injection, and we say
K

detects SWCs
of
G
, when this map is injective on
H^{*}_{\SW}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
.
Suppose one of these is true, and that
\pi
is an orthogonal representation of
G
. Then
w(\pi)
is specified by its image in
H^{*}(K,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
, which is actually the SWC of the restriction of
\pi
to
K
. When there is a detecting subgroup, one simply writes
w(\pi)
for this image.

An essential tool in Ganguly-Joshi’s papers is a theorem of Quillen
[
20
]
, asserting that the diagonal subgroup of
\GL(n,q)
detects its mod
2
cohomology. However, such a result is not available to us. Instead, most of this paper is devoted to establishing the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G=\SL(2,q)
with
q
odd. Then the center
Z
detects SWCs of
G
.

From this we obtain:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G=\SL(2,q)
with
q
odd. Let
\pi
be an orthogonal representation of
G
. Then the total SWC of
\pi
is

w(\pi)=(1+\mathfrak{e})^{r_{\pi}},

where
\mathfrak{e}
is the non-zero element in
H^{4}(\SL(2,q),\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
and
r_{\pi}=\frac{1}{8}(\chi_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-\chi_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1})).

(
Here
\mathbbm{1}
is the identity matrix.
)
If
\pi
is also irreducible, then
w(\pi)=1
.

In future work, these two theorems will be applied to determine SWCs of other finite groups of Lie type.

The case of
q
even is quite different. Here the upper unitriangular subgroup
N\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}
detects the mod
2
cohomology of the special linear group, and we use this to prove:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\pi,V)
be an orthogonal representation of
G=\SL(2,q)
, with
q
even. Then the total SWC of
\pi
is

w(\pi)=(1+\mathcal{D})^{m_{\pi}},

where
m_{\pi}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(V/V^{G}\right)
, where
V^{G}
is the
G
-fixed vectors in
V
, and
\mathcal{D}
is the sum of the Dickson invariants of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, viewed as a
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-vector space.

(Dickson invariants are reviewed in Section
5
.)

There are several corollaries of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. Let
G=\SL(2,q)
. Firstly, we describe the subgroup of the complete cohomology ring
H^{\bullet}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
(see Section
2.6
) generated by
w(\pi)
, as
\pi
varies over orthogonal representations.

Corollary 1.4
.

This subgroup equals:

(i)

\{(1+\mathfrak{e})^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, for
q
odd,

(ii)

\{(1+\mathcal{D})^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, for
q
even.

Secondly, we compute
H^{*}_{\SW}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
.

Corollary 1.5
.

The subalgebra
H^{*}_{\SW}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is:

(i)

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[\mathfrak{e}]
, for
q
odd,

(ii)

generated by the Dickson invariants of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, for
q
even.

Another characteristic class of importance is the Euler class
e(\pi)\in H^{d}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, where
d=\deg\pi
. We define
w_{\Top}(\pi)=w_{\deg\pi}(\pi)
; it is the “top” Stiefel-Whitney class of
\pi
. By Property 9.5 of
[
19
]
, we know that
w_{\Top}(\pi)
is the reduction of
e(\pi)
mod
2
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
(\pi,V)
be an orthogonal representation of
G
. Then
w_{\Top}(\pi)\neq 0
precisely when:

(i)

\pi(-\mathbbm{1})=-1
, meaning
\pi(-\mathbbm{1})
acts on
V
by the scalar
-1
, for
q
odd,

(ii)

V^{N}=\{0\}
, for
q
even.
(
Equivalently,
\pi
is cuspidal.
)

Finally, we determine the
obstruction degree
of an orthogonal
\pi
, meaning the least
k>0
with
w_{k}(\pi)\neq 0
. (If
w(\pi)
is trivial, then this degree is infinite.) Write
\ord_{2}(n)
for the highest power of
2
which divides an integer
n
.

Corollary 1.7
.

The obstruction degree of
\pi
is:

(i)

2^{t+2}
, where
t=\ord_{2}(r_{\pi})
, for
q
odd,

(ii)

2^{r+s-1}
, where
s=\ord_{2}(m_{\pi})
and
q=2^{r}
.

The layout of the paper is as follows: Section
2
recalls some fundamental notions used throughout the paper. Section
3
is dedicated to the generalized quaternions, particularly the Quaternion group
Q
of order 8. These groups are featured in the detection of
\SL(2,q)
when
q
is odd. Here we also determine
w_{4i}(\pi)
for orthogonal representations
\pi
of
Q
. In Section
4
, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, the corollaries for
q
odd, and finally compare our result to the
\GL(2,q)
case. In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.3
and the corollaries for
q
even.

2.
Notations & Preliminaries

2.1.
Representations

Let
G
be a finite group. All our representations will be finite-dimensional. Let
\pi
be a representation of
G
on a complex vector space
V
.
Write
\chi_{\pi}(g)
for the character of
\pi
at an element
g\in G
.
If
H
is a subgroup of
G
, write
\res^{G}_{H}\pi
for the restriction of
\pi
to
H
.
Write
\reg(G)
for the regular representation of
G
.

We say
\pi
is
orthogonal
, provided there exists a non-degenerate
G
-invariant symmetric bilinear form
B:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}
.
We say it is
symplectic
, provided there exists a non-degenerate
G
-invariant antisymmetric bilinear form
B:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}
.

When
\pi
is self-dual and irreducible, it is either orthogonal or symplectic. In this case, the Frobenius-Schur Indicator
\varepsilon(\pi)
is a sign defined as
1
when
\pi
is orthogonal, and
-1
when
\pi
is symplectic.

2.2.
Orthogonal Representations

We are especially interested in orthogonal representations.

From
[
4
, Chapter II, Section 6]
, we note:

Proposition 2.1
.

If
(\pi,V)
is orthogonal, then there is a
(\pi_{0},V_{0})
, with
V_{0}
a real vector space, so that
\pi_{0}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\cong\pi
. Moreover,
\pi_{0}
is unique up to isomorphism.

Given a representation
(\pi,V)
with dual
(\pi^{\vee},V^{\vee})
, we can symmetrize it to get an orthogonal representation of
G
by defining

S(\pi):=\pi\oplus\pi^{\vee}

on the vector space
V\oplus V^{\vee}
. We give a symmetric
G
-invariant bilinear map
B
on
V\oplus V^{\vee}
as,

B((v,\alpha),(w,\beta))=\langle\alpha,w\rangle+\langle\beta,v\rangle.

We call
S(\pi)
the
symmetrization
of
\pi.

Every orthogonal complex representation
\Pi
of
G
can be decomposed as

(1)

\Pi\cong\bigoplus_{i}\pi_{i}\oplus\bigoplus_{j}S(\varphi_{j}),

such that each
\pi_{i}
is irreducible orthogonal and
\varphi_{j}
are irreducible non-orthogonal representations of
G
.

Definition 2.2
.

A complex representation
\pi
of
G
is said to be an
orthogonally irreducible representation
(OIR), provided
\pi
is orthogonal, and can not be decomposed into a direct sum of orthogonal representations.

An irreducible representation
\pi
is
orthogonally irreducible
if and only if
\pi
is orthogonal. Moreover, for
\varphi
irreducible and non-orthogonal, its symmetrization
S(\varphi)
is an OIR.
In fact, the decomposition in (
1
) establishes that all the OIRs of
G
are either irreducible orthogonal
\pi
, or of the form
S(\varphi)
with
\varphi
irreducible and non-orthogonal.

2.3.
Stiefel-Whitney Classes

In this section we define Stiefel-Whitney classes for orthogonal complex representations of finite groups. This is equivalent to the definition in
[
3
]
or
[
15
]
, given for real representations.

Recall from
[
19
]
that a (
d
-dimensional real) vector bundle
\mathcal{V}
over a paracompact space
B
is assigned a sequence

w_{1}(\mathcal{V}),\ldots,w_{d}(\mathcal{V})

of cohomology classes, with each
w_{i}(\mathcal{V})\in H^{*}(B,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
.

For every finite group
G
there is a classifying space
BG
with a principal
G
-bundle
EG
, unique up to homotopy. From a real representation
(\rho,V)
one can form the associated vector bundle
EG[V]
over
BG
. Then one puts

w_{i}^{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)=w_{i}(EG[V]);

see for instance
[
3
]
or
[
15
]
.
Moreover the singular cohomology
H^{*}(BG,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to the group cohomology
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
. At this point we simply write
H^{*}(G)=H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
.

Hence, to a real representation
\rho
, we can associate cohomology classes
w_{i}^{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)\in H^{i}(G)
, called Stiefel-Whitney classes (SWCs).
We prefer to work with an orthogonal complex representation
\pi
, which by Proposition
2.1
comes from a unique real representation
\pi_{0}
.
We thus define

w_{i}(\pi):=w_{i}^{\mathbb{R}}(\pi_{0}),

for
0\leq i\leq\deg\pi
.

A group homomorphism
\varphi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
induces a map

\varphi^{*}:H^{*}(G_{2})\to H^{*}(G_{1}).

The SWCs are
functorial
in the following sense: If
\pi
is an orthogonal representation of
G_{2}
, then

(2)

\varphi^{*}(w(\pi))=w(\pi\circ\varphi).

The SWCs are also
multiplicative.
This means if
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are both orthogonal, then

(3)

w(\pi_{1}\oplus\pi_{2})=w(\pi_{1})\cup w(\pi_{2}).

The first few
w_{i}(\pi)
have nice interpretations. Firstly,
w_{0}(\pi)=1\in H^{0}(G)
and the first SWC, applied to linear characters
G\to\{\pm 1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, is the well-known isomorphism

w_{1}:\Hom(G,\pm 1)\overset{\cong}{\to}H^{1}(G).

More generally, if
\pi
is an orthogonal representation, then
w_{1}(\pi)=w_{1}(\det\pi)
, where
\det\pi
is simply the composition of
\pi
with the determinant map.
When
\det\pi=1
, then
w_{2}(\pi)
vanishes iff
\pi
lifts to the corresponding spin group. (See
[
12
]
for details.)

To complex representations
\pi
of
G
, are associated cohomology classes
c_{i}(\pi)\in H^{2i}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, called
Chern classes
. As with SWCs,
c_{0}(\pi)=1
and the first Chern class, applied to linear characters, gives the well-known isomorphism

c_{1}:\Hom(G,S^{1})\xrightarrow{\cong}H^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}).

More generally,
c_{1}(\pi)=c_{1}(\det\pi)
for a complex representation
\pi
.

The SWC of a symmetrization
S(\pi)
can be obtained from the Chern class of
\pi
via the formula

(4)

w(S(\pi))=\kappa(c(\pi)).

Here
\kappa:H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is the
coefficient homomorphism
of cohomology.
(See
[
10
, Lemma 1]
, based on
[
19
, Problem 14-B]
.)

From this we make an observation for later use:

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\det\pi=1
, then
w_{i}(S(\pi))=0\text{ for }i=1,2,3
.

Proof.

Since Chern classes live in even degrees, all the odd SWCs of
S(\pi)
vanish. Moreover
c_{1}(\pi)=0
by the determinant hypothesis.
∎

2.4.
Steenrod Squares

For
n,i\geq 0
, there are operations on cohomology, called
Steenrod Squares
,

\Sq^{i}:H^{n}(G)\rightarrow H^{n+i}(G).

These are additive homomorphisms and
\Sq^{0}
is the identity. Steenrod operations are
functorial
, meaning for a group homomorphism
\varphi:G\to G^{\prime}
, we have

\varphi^{*}(\Sq^{i}y)=\Sq^{i}(\varphi^{*}(y))\text{ for all }y\in H^{n}(G^{\prime}).

We now state the well-known
Wu formula
:

Proposition 2.4

(
[
18
]
, Chapter 23, Section 6)
.

Let
\pi
be an orthogonal representation of
G
. Then, the cohomology class
\Sq^{i}(w_{j}(\pi))
can be expressed as a polynomial in
w_{1}(\pi),\ldots,w_{i+j}(\pi)
:

\Sq^{i}(w_{j}(\pi))=\sum_{t=0}^{i}{j+t-i-1\choose t}w_{i-t}(\pi)w_{j+t}(\pi).

With
i=1
,
j=2
for instance, we can express
w_{3}(\pi)
in terms of
w_{1},w_{2}
as follows:

w_{3}(\pi)=w_{1}(\pi)\cup w_{2}(\pi)+\Sq^{1}(w_{2}(\pi)).

2.5.
Elementary Abelian
2
-groups

Let
C_{2}=\{\pm 1\},
the cyclic group of order
2
. Here we review explicit descriptions of group cohomology for elementary abelian 2-groups
C_{2}^{n}
and describe SWCs in terms of these descriptions.

We write ‘sgn’ for the order
2
linear character of
C_{2}
. It is known
[
16
]
that

(5)

H^{*}(C_{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v],

where
v=w_{1}(\sgn)
. Since this group is so simple, we can give our first example of expressing SWCs in terms of character values. (Note that every representation of
C_{2}
is orthogonal.)

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\pi
be a representation of
C_{2}
, and
b_{\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\deg\pi-\chi_{\pi}(-1)\right)
. Then, the total SWC of
\pi
is

w(\pi)=(1+v)^{b_{\pi}}.

Proof.

We can write

\pi=a1\oplus b(\sgn)

for non-negative integers
a,b
. From the multiplicativity of SWCs, we obtain

w(\pi)=(1+v)^{b}.

To express
b
in character values, consider the equations:

\displaystyle\chi_{\pi}(1)

\displaystyle=a+b

\displaystyle\chi_{\pi}(-1)

\displaystyle=a-b

giving
b=\cfrac{1}{2}(\chi_{\pi}(1)-\chi_{\pi}(-1))=b_{\pi}.

∎

Let
C_{2}^{n}
be the
n
-fold product of
C_{2}
, with projection maps
\pr_{i}:C_{2}^{n}\to C_{2}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. By Künneth, we have

H^{*}(C_{2}^{n})=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]

where we put
v_{i}=w_{1}(\sgn\circ\pr_{i})
.

2.6.
SWCs for Virtual Representations

A virtual representation of
G
can be thought as a difference
\pi=\pi_{1}\ominus\pi_{2}
, for representations
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
. When the
\pi_{i}
are orthogonal, one may define the SWC of
\pi
as

w(\pi)=w(\pi_{1})\cup w(\pi_{2})^{-1},

but we must “complete” the cohomology ring so that the inversion makes sense.

More formally, let
G
be a finite group, and let
\RO(G)
be the free abelian group generated by the isomorphism classes of OIRs (following
[
4
, Chapter II, Section 7]
). Members of
\RO(G)
are called
virtual orthogonal representations
of
G
.

Write

H^{\bullet}(G)=\prod_{i}H^{i}(G),

for the complete mod
2
cohomology ring of
G
, consisting of all formal infinite series
\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\cdots
, with
\alpha_{i}\in H^{i}(G)
. (Please see
[
22
, page 44]
.) Each
w(\pi)
is invertible in this ring.

It is clear now that we may “extend”
w
to a group homomorphism, taking values in the unit group of
H^{\bullet}(G)
, i.e.,

(6)

w:\RO(G)\to H^{\bullet}(G)^{\times}.

Later in this paper we shall determine the image of
w
for
G=\SL(2,q)
.

3.
Quaternion Groups

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
involves subgroups of
\SL(2,q)
isomorphic to certain quaternion groups. In this section we review the group cohomology of the quaternion group of order
8
, and the so-called “generalized quaternions”
Q_{2^{n}}
. This will play a vital role in our detection theorem.

3.1.
Orthogonal Representations of
Q

Let
Q
be the Quaternion group of order 8. If we write
\mathbb{H}
for the familiar real division algebra of quaternions, then
Q
can be quickly described as the subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{\times}
generated by
i
and
j
.

There are 4 one-dimensional representations of
Q
, which we denote by 1,
\chi_{1},\chi_{2},\chi_{3}=\chi_{1}\otimes\chi_{2}
. Each one is orthogonal.
The group
Q
also possesses a unique 2-dimensional irreducible representation

\rho:Q\rightarrow\SL(2,\mathbb{C})

defined by

\rho(i)=\begin{pmatrix}i&&0\\
0&&-i\end{pmatrix}\text{ , }\;\rho(j)=\begin{pmatrix}0&&1\\
-1&&0\end{pmatrix}.

Of course,
\mathbb{H}
can be described as the algebra of complex matrices of the form

\begin{pmatrix}s+ti&u+vi\\
-u+vi&s-ti\end{pmatrix}\text{ where }s,t,u,v\in\mathbb{R}.

Since both
\rho(i)
,
\rho(j)
are of this form,
\rho
is an injection of
Q
into
\mathbb{H}^{1}
, the subgroup of norm
1
quaternions. In particular,
\rho
is symplectic.
Thus,
S(\rho)=\rho\oplus\rho
is the only OIR of
Q
, which is not irreducible.

3.2.
The Cohomology ring
H^{*}(Q)

The SWCs of orthogonal representations of
Q
are certain elements in
H^{*}(Q)
. Here we give a description of the cohomology ring
H^{*}(Q)
with
[
1
, Chapter IV]
as our reference.

Recall that
w_{1}
gives an isomorphism between
\Hom(Q,\pm 1)=\{1,\chi_{1},\chi_{2},\chi_{3}\}
and
H^{1}(Q)
. We define

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle:=w_{1}(\chi_{1}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle:=w_{1}(\chi_{2}).

The cohomology group
H^{4}(Q)
is one-dimensional over
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, and we write ‘
e
’ for its nonzero element.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
1
]
, Section V.1)
.

The cohomology ring of
Q
is

H^{*}(Q)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[x,y,e]/{(xy+x^{2}+y^{2},x^{2}y+xy^{2})}.

The first few cohomology groups of
Q
are as follows:

\displaystyle H^{0}(Q)

\displaystyle=\{0,1\}

\displaystyle H^{1}(Q)

\displaystyle=\{0,x,y,x+y\}

\displaystyle H^{2}(Q)

\displaystyle=\{0,x^{2},y^{2},x^{2}+y^{2}\}

\displaystyle H^{3}(Q)

\displaystyle=\{0,x^{2}y\}

\displaystyle H^{4}(Q)

\displaystyle=\{0,e\}.

Note that
x^{3}=y^{3}=0
. Moreover, one can obtain the higher cohomology groups via the cup product with
e
due to Proposition
3.1
. This means
for
i\geq 0,
the map

\displaystyle H^{i}(Q)

\displaystyle\to H^{i+4}(Q)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto z\cup e

is an isomorphism of groups.

3.3.
SWCs of Representations of
Q

We first compute the total SWCs of OIRs of
Q
.
For the linear orthogonal representations of
Q
, it is clear that

w(1)=1,

w(\chi_{1})=1+x
,
w(\chi_{2})=1+y
, and
w(\chi_{1}\otimes\chi_{2})=1+x+y
with
x,y\in H^{1}(Q)
from above.

Lemma 3.2
.

The total SWC of
S(\rho)
is

w(S(\rho))=1+e,

where
e
is the non-trivial element of
H^{4}(Q)
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.3
, we have

w_{i}(S(\rho))=0\text{ for }i=1,2,3.

This gives
w(S(\rho))=1+w_{4}(S(\rho))
.
We now prove that
w_{4}(S(\rho))=e\in H^{4}(Q).

Let
Z
be the center of
Q
, which is
\{\pm 1\}
. Since

\displaystyle\rho(-1)=\small\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

we have the restriction
\res_{Z}^{Q}\rho=\sgn\oplus\sgn,

where
\sgn:Z\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
is the linear character of order
2
.

From (
5
), we have
H^{*}(Z)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v]
where
v=w_{1}(\sgn)
. Then, the multiplicativity of SWCs gives

\displaystyle w(\res_{Z}^{Q}S(\rho))=w(\sgn)^{4}=(1+v)^{4}=1+v^{4},

which implies
w_{4}(S(\rho))\neq 0
. Therefore,
w_{4}(S(\rho))=e
, since this is the only nonzero element in
H^{4}(Q)
.
∎

We also see from the above proof that
e
maps to
v^{4}
under the restriction
H^{4}(Q)\to H^{4}(Z)

(1+v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{2})^{r_{\pi}}(1+v_{1}+v_{2})^{s_{\pi}},&q\equiv 3\;(\Mod\>4),\end{cases}

for a certain integer
s_{\pi}
, and
r_{\pi}=\dfrac{1}{8}(\chi_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-\chi_{\pi}(-\mathbbm{1}))
as above.

Our two papers are compatible via the following diagram, formed from the restriction maps on cohomology:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(\widetilde{G})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(\widetilde{T})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{*}(Z)}

The RHS of (
12
) lies in
H^{*}(\widetilde{T})
. The left map is injective by Quillen’s work, and the right map is injective on SWCs by Theorem
1.1
. The lower map
H^{*}(\widetilde{T})\to H^{*}(Z)
maps
v_{i}
to
v
for
q\equiv 3\>(\Mod\>4)
, and when
q\equiv 1\>(\Mod\>4)
, it maps
s_{i}
to
0
and
t_{i}
to
v^{2}
.
This gives:

w(\pi|_{Z})=(1+v^{4})^{r_{\pi}}

in all cases. Thus, for orthogonal representations of
G
which extend to orthogonal representations of
\widetilde{G}
, our Theorem
1.2
agrees with the main result of
[
10
]
.

Remark 4.11
.

Not every orthogonal representation of
G
extends to
\widetilde{G}
. For instance when
q\equiv 1\>(\Mod\>4)
, the representation
\pi=\Ind_{\widetilde{B}}^{\widetilde{G}}(\sgn\boxtimes 1)
(with
\widetilde{B}
the upper triangular subgroup of
\widetilde{G}
), when restricted to
G
is the sum of two irreducible orthogonal representations, neither of which extend to
\widetilde{G}
.

5.
\SL(2,q)
with
q
even

Let
G=\SL(2,q)
with
q=2^{r}
throughout this section. Recall that we write
N
for the subgroup of upper unitriangular matrices. It is well-known that this is a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
G
, and therefore
N
detects the mod
2
cohomology of
G
by Lemma
4.2
. All representations of
G
are orthogonal by the main result in
[
21
]
.

5.1.
Formulas for SWCs

The subgroup
N
is an elementary abelian
2
-group of rank
r
, so as in Section
2.5
, the mod
2
cohomology ring of
N\cong C_{2}^{r}
may be written as

H^{*}(N)\cong H^{*}(C^{r}_{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}].

Let
T
be the subgroup of diagonal matrices in
G
. That is

T=\Bigg\{\small\begin{pmatrix}a&&0\\
0&&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}:a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\Bigg\}.

The conjugation action of
T
on
N
is equivalent to the action of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
through multiplication by squares. Since
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
has odd order, this action is transitive on
\mathbb{F}_{q}-\{0\}
. Therefore:

Lemma 5.1
.

T
acts transitively on the non-trivial linear characters of
N
.

Let
\widehat{N}=\Hom(N,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
, the character group of
N
. From the above lemma, the
T
-orbits of
\widehat{N}
are:

\{1\}\;,\;\{\chi:\chi\neq 1\}.

Let us define

\reg(N)^{\prime}=\reg(N)\ominus 1=\bigoplus_{\chi\neq 1}\chi.

Let
\pi
be a representation of
G
.
Since
\res^{G}_{N}\pi
is
T
-invariant, it is of the form

(13)

\begin{split}\res^{G}_{N}\pi&\cong\ell_{\pi}1\oplus m_{\pi}\reg(N)^{\prime},\end{split}

where
\ell_{\pi},m_{\pi}
are non-negative integers.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\pi
be a non-trivial irreducible representation of
G
. Then,
m_{\pi}=1
.

Proof.

For
r\geq 2
, it is known that
G
has no non-trivial normal subgroups. For non-trivial
\pi
, we must have
m_{\pi}\geq 1
because ker
(\pi)=\mathbbm{1}
and
N\not\leqslant\ker(\pi)
. Now from (
13
), we have

\deg\pi=\ell_{\pi}+m_{\pi}(q-1).

If
m_{\pi}>1
, then the sum
\ell_{\pi}+m_{\pi}(q-1)
would be greater than the highest possible degree for an irreducible representation of
G
, which is
(q+1)
. So actually
m_{\pi}=1
.

For
r=1
, we have
\SL(2,2)\cong S_{3}
with only two non-trivial irreducible representations. That is
\pi
is either the ‘sgn’ representation or the 2-dimensional standard representation of
S_{3}.
Here we can see from direct calculations that
m_{\pi}=1
.

∎

It follows that
m_{\pi}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(V/V^{G})
, where
V
denotes the representation space of
\pi
. It is also the number of non-trivial irreducible constituents of
\pi
. We can get a character formula for
m_{\pi}
by taking character values at
n_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&&1\\
0&&1\end{pmatrix}\in N.
In fact,

\displaystyle\chi_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})

\displaystyle=\ell_{\pi}+(q-1)m_{\pi}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\chi_{\pi}(n_{0})

\displaystyle=\ell_{\pi}-m_{\pi},

so that
m_{\pi}=\frac{1}{q}(\chi_{\pi}(\mathbbm{1})-\chi_{\pi}(n_{0}))
.

We are now ready to describe the SWCs of representations of
G
. Since
N
is a detecting subgroup, we may and will identify
w(\pi)
with its image in
H^{*}(N)
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
q=2^{r}.
Let
\pi
be a representation of
\SL(2,q)
. Then, the total SWC of
\pi
is

w(\pi)=\big(\prod_{v\in H^{1}(N)}(1+v)\big)^{m_{\pi}}.

Proof.

The restriction
\res^{G}_{N}\pi
, from (
13
), is of the form

\res^{G}_{N}\pi\cong(\ell_{\pi}-m_{\pi})1\oplus m_{\pi}\reg(N).

Since
\reg(N)=\sum\limits_{\chi\in\widehat{N}}\chi
, we use the isomorphism between
\widehat{N}
and
H^{1}(N)
and the multiplicativity of SWCs to obtain

\displaystyle w(\res^{G}_{N}\pi)

\displaystyle=w(\reg(N))^{m_{\pi}}

\displaystyle=\big(\prod_{v\in H^{1}(N)}(1+v)\big)^{m_{\pi}}.

∎

Remark 5.4
.

The formula in Theorem
5.3
above is also valid for
\widetilde{G}=\GL(2,q)
with
q
even, since
N
is also a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
\widetilde{G}
. Thus this result in some sense completes
[
10
]
.

5.2.
Dickson Invariants

The expansion of the product above is well-known. We have

(14)

\prod_{v\in H^{1}(N)}(1+v)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}\in H^{*}(N),\\

where
d_{i}
are
Dickson invariants
in the algebra
H^{*}(N)
.

We digress for a moment to recount the theory of Dickson invariants for mod
2
spaces. Let
E
be an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space. The ring of polynomials from
E
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}
can be identified with the symmetric algebra
S[E^{\vee}]
on the dual space
E^{\vee}
. The linear group
\GL(E)
acts on
S[E^{\vee}]
via the contragradient map. It is natural to look for the invariants under this action.

Theorem 5.5

(
[
23
]
)
.

Suppose
\dim(E)=r
. Then the ring of invariants
S[E^{\vee}]^{\GL(E)}
is a polynomial algebra generated by elements
d_{i}
called Dickson invariants, for
1\leq i\leq r
. The polynomial
d_{i}
has degree
2^{r}-2^{r-i}
. Moreover,

\prod_{v\in E^{\vee}}(1+v)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{r}{d_{i}}\in S[E^{\vee}].

These invariants are described explicitly in terms of certain determinants. (See
[
23
]
, or
[
1
, Chapter III]
for instance.)

Let us illustrate with some examples. If
E=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, then
S[E^{\vee}]
is a polynomial algebra with only one generator
v
, that is
S[E^{\vee}]\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v]
. Here
d_{1}=v
giving the ring of invariants

S[E^{\vee}]^{\GL(V)}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v]^{\GL(1,2)}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v].

Also Theorem
5.3
for
G=\SL(2,2)
and
\pi\neq 1
irreducible says that

w(\pi)=1+v=1+d_{1}.

Next, suppose
\dim E=2
. Then
S[E^{\vee}]
is a polynomial algebra with two generators, say
v_{1},v_{2}
such that
S[E^{\vee}]\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v_{1},v_{2}]
. Here the ring of invariants
S[E^{\vee}]^{\GL(E)}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v_{1},v_{2}]^{\GL(2,2)}
is generated by

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+v_{1}v_{2},

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=v_{1}v_{2}(v_{1}+v_{2}).

Theorem
5.3
gives:
For
G=\SL(2,4)
, the total SWC of a non-trivial irreducible representation
\pi
of
G
is

w(\pi)=1+d_{1}+d_{2}.

Now let
\mathcal{D}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}
be the sum of the Dickson invariants, so that we can succinctly write

w(\pi)=(1+\mathcal{D})^{m_{\pi}},

which is Theorem
1.3
from the Introduction.

Example 5.6
.

We have

w(\reg(G))=(1+\mathcal{D})^{q^{2}-1}.

5.3.
Corollaries

Now we prove the corollaries mentioned in the Introduction.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
G=\SL(2,q)
with even
q
. Then the image of
w
in (
6
) is

\{(1+\mathcal{D})^{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be a non-trivial irreducible representation of
G
. Then, its total SWC is

w(\pi)=1+\mathcal{D},

since
m_{\pi}=1
by Lemma
5.2
.
With the virtual representation
n\pi
, we obtain

(1+\mathcal{D})^{n}
in the image of
w
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

The above proof also gives:

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
G=\SL(2,q)
with
q=2^{r}
. Then,

H^{*}_{\SW}(G)=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}].

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
G=\SL(2,q)
with
q
even. Let
(\pi,V)
be a representation of
G
. Then,
w_{\Top}(\pi)\neq 0
if and only if
V^{N}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

From the preceding, we have

w_{\Top}(\reg(N)^{\prime})=d_{r},

since this invariant has degree
q-1
. The highest degree term of
w(\pi)
is therefore

d_{r}^{m_{\pi}},

which has degree
m_{\pi}(q-1)
; this is the degree of
\pi
iff
\ell_{\pi}=0
iff
V^{N}=\{0\}
.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

A representation
\pi
of
\SL(2,q)
is cuspidal iff
V^{N}=\{0\}
. (See
[
2
, page 410]
.)

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
G=\SL(2,q)
with
q=2^{r}
. Let
\pi
be a representation of
G
. Put
s=\ord_{2}(m_{\pi})
. Then, the obstruction class of
\pi
is equal to
w_{2^{r+s-1}}(\pi)=d_{1}^{2^{s}}.

Proof.

From Theorem
1.3
, we have

w(\pi)=\sum_{i=0}^{m_{\pi}}{m_{\pi}\choose i}\mathcal{D}^{i}.

As in Corollary
4.10
, we can say that
m_{\pi}\choose 2^{s}
is the first odd binomial coefficient appearing in the above sum. By expanding the term

{m_{\pi}\choose 2^{s}}\mathcal{D}^{2^{s}}={m_{\pi}\choose 2^{s}}(d_{1}^{2^{s}}+\ldots+d_{r-1}^{2^{s}}+d_{r}^{2^{s}}),

we can deduce that
{m_{\pi}\choose 2^{s}}d_{1}^{2^{s}}
has the least degree, which is
(2^{r-1}\cdot 2^{s})
. Therefore, the least
k>0
such that
w_{k}(\pi)\neq 0
is
2^{r+s-1}
as claimed.
∎
