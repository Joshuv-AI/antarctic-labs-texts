Limit theorems for Quantum Trajectories
Tristan Benoist, Jan-Luka Fatras, Clément Pellegrini

Abstract
. Quantum trajectories are Markov processes modeling the evolution of a quantum system subjected to repeated independent measurements. Under purification and irreducibility assumptions, these Markov processes admit a unique invariant measure – see Benoist et al. Probab. Theory Relat. Fields 2019. In this article we prove finer limit theorems such as Law of Large Numbers (LLN), Functional Central Limit Theorem, Law of Iterated Logarithm and Moderate Deviation Principle. The proof of the LLN is based on Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem and an analysis of harmonic functions. The other theorems are proved using martingale approximation of empirical sums.



1.
Introduction

Quantum trajectories are Markov processes modeling the evolution of a quantum system subjected to repeated independent measurements. A quantum system interacts with a series of independent identical probes. After each interaction a measurement is performed on the probe that just interacted. Following the postulates of quantum mechanics, the measurement outcome and the resulting system state are random. Since the probes are independent and identical, the evolution of the system state is described by a homogeneous Markov chain. A typical experiment modeled by quantum trajectories is that of Serge Haroche’s group (see
[
GBD+07
]
for example). In this experiment, the system is a monochromatic electromagnetic field similar to a harmonic oscillator and the probes are atoms with two energy levels relevant for the experiment. The light field is measured and manipulated using the atoms and the measurements performed on them. Numerous quantum optics experiments are modeled by quantum trajectories – see
[
Car93
,
HR06
,
WM10
]
.

From a mathematical point of view, these Markov chains are relatively singular. They are not generically
\varphi
-irreducible
1

1

The definition of
\varphi
-irreducibility is a standard generalization of the irreducibility criteria for countable state space Markov chains – see
[
MT09
, §1.3.1]
.
as we show in Section
8
. This is not a complete surprise since they can be seen as place dependent iterated function systems (IFS) – see the definition in
[
BDEG88
]
. However, they do not satisfy the standard criteria of contractivity for IFS (see Section
8
). Hence, standard results on uniqueness of invariant measures such as the one of
[
BDEG88
]
cannot be applied. In
[
BFPP19
]
an approach based on the convergence of an estimator of the system state given the measurement outcomes was developed. Under common assumptions for quantum trajectories, the authors showed the Markov chain accepts a unique invariant measure and the convergence is exponentially fast towards this measure. The first assumption is the purification one, first introduced in
[
MK06
]
. In this seminal reference the authors showed that quantum trajectories tend to get closer to the set of extreme points (pure states) of the convex set they live in. They moreover showed that under the purification assumption, in large time limit, the Markov chain lives in this set. The second assumption concerns the average evolution of the system state. It is similar to the irreducibility assumption for finite state space Markov chains and leads to the ergodicity of the dynamical system formed by the sequence of measurement results (see
[
KM03
]
). As mentioned in
[
Szc21
]
, the probability measures describing the law of the measurement outcomes, are also known as Kusuoka measures as defined in
[
Kus89
]
.

Since the pioneering works of Kümmerer and Maassen
[

,
KM04
,
MK06
]
a large body of literature has been dedicated to limit theorems for the measurement outcomes.
Notably, Law of Large Numbers
[

, Central Limit Theorems

,
CP15
]
and Large Deviation Principles

have been derived. Recently, extensions to non ergodic repeated quantum measurements have been made, see

In the present article, we prove some limit theorems, not for the measurement outcomes, but for the system state,
i.e.
the quantum trajectory
(\hat{x}_{n})_{n}
. We study the time asymptotics of the empirical sum

S_{n}(g):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{x}_{k})

for some function
g
.

We show a strong Law of Large Numbers (LLN), a Central Limit Theorem (CLT) and its functional version (FCLT), a Law of Iterated Logarithm (LIL) and a Moderate Deviation Principle (MDP). The proof of the LLN is a consequence of Birkhoff’s stationary process ergodic theorem and the approximation of
\hat{x}_{n}
by a random variable depending only on measurement outcomes established in
[
BFPP19
]
. Note that quantum trajectories are generally not positive Harris as required in
[
MT09
, Theorem 17.1.7]
. Hence, not all bounded harmonic functions are constant. Only continuous harmonic functions are constant. We circumvent this issue by proving the probability the LLN holds is continuous with respect to the initial state of the system. The proofs of the other theorems are all based on a martingale approximation of
S_{n}(g)
for
g
Hölder continuous. The FCLT is derived using its standard version for martingales (see
[
HH80
, Theorem 4.1]
for the version we use), the LIL proof follows from the original article
[
Sto70
]
and the MDP one is a consequence of
[
Gao96
, Theorem 1.1]
. The martingale approximation is a versatile tool. For example, a concentration bound similar to the one of
[
GGG22
, Theorem 5]
could also be derived. However, to our knowledge, it cannot be used to derive a large deviation principle. This question is still open for quantum trajectories.

We work in the same mathematical context as in
[
BFPP19
]
and use similar notations and definitions. The Hilbert space we consider is the canonical complex
d
dimensional space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and especially its projective space
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
.
For a non zero vector
x\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we denote
\hat{x}
the equivalence class of
x
in
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. For
\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
,
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is an arbitrary norm one representative of
\hat{x}
and
\pi_{\hat{x}}
is the orthogonal projector onto
{\mathbb{C}}x
.
For a linear map
v\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
we denote
v\cdot\hat{x}
the element of the projective space represented by
v\,x
whenever
v\,x\neq 0
. We equip
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. We consider a measure
\mu
on
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
such that
v\mapsto\|v\|
is square integrable,

\int_{M_{d}(\mathbb{C})}\|v\|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mu(v)<\infty,

and the following stochasticity condition is fulfilled,

(1)

\int_{M_{d}(\mathbb{C})}v^{*}v\,\mathrm{d}\mu(v)={\rm Id}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}.

A quantum trajectory is a realization of a Markov chain
(\hat{x}_{n})
on
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, defined by

\hat{x}_{n+1}=V_{n}\cdot\hat{x}_{n},

where
V_{n}
is a
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued random variable with law
||vx_{n}||^{2}{\rm d}\mu(v).
More precisely, we study Markov chains associated with the transition kernel given for a set
S\in\mathcal{B}
and
\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
by

(2)

\Pi(\hat{x},S)=\int_{M_{d}({\mathbb{C}})}\mathbf{1}_{S}(v\cdot\hat{x})\|vx\|^{2}{\rm d}\mu(v).

Every
\hat{x}_{n}
can be written using a random product of matrices

\hat{x}_{n}=V_{n}\ldots V_{1}\cdot\hat{x}_{0},

with
(V_{n})_{n}
non i.i.d. but its law given by
\|v_{n}\dotsb v_{1}x_{0}\|^{2}{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes n}(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n})
. Since the matrices are not i.i.d. the results on products of i.i.d. matrices (see
[
BL85
]
) cannot be applied. Though, since
V_{1}x_{0}\neq 0
almost surely,
V_{1}\cdot\hat{x}_{0}
is almost surely well defined and we do not require the matrices be invertible. In
[
BFPP19
]
the authors showed that, assuming purification and matrix irreducibility, these Markov chains admit unique invariant measures
\nu_{inv}
. The matrix irreducibility assumption is weaker than the strong irreducibility one used for products of i.i.d. matrices. It only requires that there is a unique non trivial minimal common invariant subspace to the matrices charged by
\mu
. In the same reference, the convergence towards the invariant measure is shown to be geometric in Wasserstein distance uniformly with respect to the initial measure. This is our starting point. Indeed, such strong convergence result often leads to finer limit theorems for the chain. However, since
(\hat{x}_{n})
is in general not
\nu_{inv}
-irreducible (see Section
8
), the usual proofs require adaptations.

First, in Section
3
, we show a LLN for any continuous function
g
on
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{x}_{k})\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g),\quad\text{a.s.}

whatever is the distribution
\nu
of the initial state
\hat{x}_{0}
. This result cannot be extended to
L^{1}
functions since, following
[
MT09
, Theorem 17.1.7]
, it would imply quantum trajectories are positive Harris and therefore
\nu_{inv}
-irreduccible (see
[
MT09
, §9]
) and, as already mentioned, we provide a counter example to that property in Section
8
. This result is a generalization of
[
KM04
, Theorem 5]
which applies only to affine functions in
\pi_{\hat{x}}
.

Second, in Section
4
, we show that for any Hölder continuous function
g
, the Poisson equation admits a continuous solution
\tilde{g}
, that is

(3)

(I-\Pi)\tilde{g}=g-{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)=:\overline{g}.

The continuity of the solution is important since the LLN holds only for continuous functions. Then, as usual, the solution
\tilde{g}
allows for the approximation of

S_{n}(g):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{x}_{k})

by a martingale
M_{n}(g)
with uniformly bounded increments. This leads to several limit theorems inherited from limit theorems for martingales. In particular, in Section
5
we show a CLT:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{S_{n}(\overline{g})}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_{g}^{2}),\quad\mbox{in law, with }\gamma_{g}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(\tilde{g}^{2}-(\Pi\tilde{g})^{2})

and its functional version. In Section
6
we show a LIL:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\pm S_{n}(\overline{g})}{\sqrt{2n\log\log(n)}}=\gamma_{g},\quad\mbox{a.s.}

In Section
7
, we show a MDP: for any function
n\mapsto a(n)
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{a(n)^{2}}=0
and
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\nu}\left|\frac{n}{a(n)^{2}}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}\left(\exp\left(\tfrac{a(n)}{n}zS_{n}(\overline{g})\right)\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}z^{2}\gamma_{g}^{2}\right|=0,

with the supremum taken over the distribution of
\hat{x}_{0}
. Moreover, if
\gamma_{g}^{2}>0
, for any Borel set
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\nu}\frac{n}{a(n)^{2}}\log{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}(\tfrac{1}{a(n)}S_{n}(\overline{g})\in B)=-\inf_{y\in B}\frac{y^{2}}{2\gamma_{g}^{2}}.

If
\gamma_{g}^{2}=0
, if
0
is not in the closure of
B
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\nu}\frac{n}{a(n)^{2}}\log{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}(\tfrac{1}{a(n)}S_{n}(\overline{g})\in B)=-\infty,

if
0
is in the interior of
B
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\nu}\frac{n}{a(n)^{2}}\log{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}(\tfrac{1}{a(n)}S_{n}(\overline{g})\in B)=0.

Notice that since
M_{n}(g)
has uniformly bounded increments, the LLN could be proved using the martingale LLN and the density of Hölder continuous functions in the Banach space of continuous functions equipped with the supremum norm. However, since in general the existence of the solution to Poisson equation is not needed for the proof of the LLN, we preferred the proof presented here. Indeed, it may turnout useful in situations where the existence of a solution to Poisson equation cannot be proved easily, like when
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is replaced by an infinite dimensional Hilbert space.

There exists a continuous time version of quantum trajectories (see
[
BG09
,
BVHJ07
]
). As shown in
[
Pel08
,
Pel10
,
BBB13
]
, they are approximations of the discrete time version we study here. Adapting our proofs, especially using the methods and results of
[
BFPP21
]
instead of
[
BFPP19
]
and up to possible standard technical adaptations, all our results translate to continuous time quantum trajectories.

Before we state and prove the main results, in Section
2
, we make precise the notation we use and recall some useful properties of quantum trajectories. At the end, in Section
8
, we present an example of quantum trajectory to which our results apply yet the Markov chain does not verify the usual
\varphi
-irreducibility or contractivity assumptions.

2.
Notations and preliminaries

2.1.
Measurement process and Quantum trajectory

In this article, we mostly follow the notations and definitions of
[
BFPP19
]
. We refer the reader to it for more details and comments.

Let
\Omega:=M_{d}({\mathbb{C}})^{\mathbb{N}}
. We equip it with its cylinder set
\sigma
-algebra. More precisely, let
\mathcal{M}
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
M_{d}({\mathbb{C}})
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\mathcal{O}_{n}
be the smallest
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
making the sets
\{(v_{1},v_{2},\dotsc)\in\Omega:v_{1}\in A_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}\in A_{n}\}
measurable for any
A_{1},\dotsc,A_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then the smallest
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{O}
containing
\mathcal{O}_{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
makes
\Omega
measurable. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, and denote

\mathcal{J}_{n}=\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{n},\qquad\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{O}.

This makes
\big({\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\times\Omega,\mathcal{J}\big)
a measurable space. We identify sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\{\emptyset,{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\}\times\mathcal{O}
with
\mathcal{O}
, and equivalently identify any
\mathcal{O}
-measurable function
f
with the
\mathcal{J}
-measurable function
f
satisfying
f(\hat{x},\omega)=f(\omega)
.

For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the random variables
V_{i}:\Omega\to M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
,

(4)

V_{i}(\omega)=v_{i}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\omega=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots),

and we introduce
\mathcal{O}_{n}
-mesurable random variables
(W_{n})
defined for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
as

W_{n}=V_{n}\ldots V_{1}.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure over
({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})},\mathcal{B})
. We extend it to a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
over
({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\times\Omega,\mathcal{J})
by setting, for any
S\in\mathcal{B}
and any set
O_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
,

(5)

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(S\times O_{n}):=\int_{S\times O_{n}}\|W_{n}(\omega)x\|^{2}{\rm d}\nu(\hat{x}){\rm d}\mu^{\otimes n}(\omega).

Equation (
1
) ensures that (
5
) defines a consistent family of probability measures and, by Kolmogorov’s theorem, this defines a unique probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
on
{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\times\Omega
. In addition, the restriction of
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
to
\mathcal{B}\otimes\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
is, by construction,
\nu
. In the sequel we shall refer systematically to
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
even if the involved r.v. does not imply
\mathcal{O}
(this allows a unification of the notations all along the paper).

We now define the random process
(\hat{x}_{n})
. For
(\hat{x},\omega)\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\times\Omega
let
\hat{x}_{0}(\hat{x},\omega)=\hat{x}
. Note that for any
n
, the definition (
5
) of
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
imposes

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}(W_{n}x_{0}=0)=0.

This allows us to define a sequence
(\hat{x}_{n})
of
(\mathcal{J}_{n})
-adapted random variables on the probability space
({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\times\Omega,\mathcal{J},{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu})
by letting

(6)

\hat{x}_{n}:=W_{n}\cdot\hat{x},

whenever the expression makes sense, i.e. for any
\omega
such that
W_{n}(\omega)x\neq 0
, and extending it arbitrarily to the whole of
\Omega
. The process
(\hat{x}_{n})
on
(\Omega\times{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d}),\mathcal{J},\mathbb{P}_{\nu})
has the same distribution as the Markov chain defined by
\Pi
and initial probability measure
\nu
. We always work with this construction of the Markov chain. Namely, for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\hat{x}_{n}
is a
\mathcal{J}_{n}
-measurable random variable.

From a physical point of view, a sequence
\omega=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)
is interpreted as the results of measurements that a physical apparatus records. Conditionally on these results, the Markov chain
(\hat{x}_{n})
is updated. Like hidden Markov chain models,
\omega=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)
represents the observed process whereas
(\hat{x}_{n})
is not directly measured. Note that if
\mu
is a sum of Dirac measures, that is for example

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{A_{i}},

the quantum trajectory is defined by

\hat{x}_{n+1}=A_{i}\cdot\hat{x}_{n}=\widehat{\frac{A_{i}x_{n}}{\|A_{i}x_{n}\|}}\,\,\textrm{with probability}\,\,\|A_{i}x_{n}\|^{2}.

These quantum trajectories are the most commonly encountered in the physics literature. A measurement apparatus produces a number
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
as an outcome and the system state is updated conditionally to this information.

We focused our definitions on pure states, namely system states that can be represented by elements of
{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
. Equivalently, these states are represented by rank one orthogonal projectors on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
. It is often useful in quantum mechanics to consider a more general set of states, density matrices. They are elements of the convex hull of rank one projectors:

\mathcal{D}_{d}:=\operatorname{convex}\{\pi_{\hat{x}}:\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})\}=\{\rho\in M_{d}({\mathbb{C}}):\rho\geq 0,\operatorname{tr}\rho=1\}.

Quantum trajectory definition can be extended consistently to density matrices introducing the process
(\rho_{n})
defined by

\rho_{n+1}=\frac{V_{n+1}\rho_{n}V_{n+1}^{*}}{\operatorname{tr}(V_{n+1}\rho_{n}V_{n+1}^{*})}\quad\mbox{with}\quad V_{n+1}\sim\operatorname{tr}(v^{*}v\rho_{n}){\rm d}\mu(v)

conditioned on the value of
\rho_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
. However, since the set of pure states is stable under this random dynamics and our standing assumption
(Pur)
implies
\rho_{n}
converges almost surely to the set of pure states as
n
grows according to
[
MK06
]
, we limit ourselves to the pure state formulation of quantum trajectories.

Density matrices are useful to describe average states and restrictions of the measures
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
to the outcome
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{O}
. To that end, for any probability measure
\nu
over
{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
, let

(7)

\rho_{\nu}:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(\pi_{\hat{x}}).

By definition,
\rho_{\nu}\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
.

We define probability measures over
\Omega
that depend on a density matrix. For
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
and any set
O_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
, let

(8)

{\mathbb{P}}^{\rho}(O_{n}):=\int_{O_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\big(W_{n}(\omega)\rho\ W_{n}^{*}(\omega)\big)\mathrm{d}\mu^{\otimes n}(\omega).

Again using (
1
), this defines a probability measure over
(\Omega,\mathcal{O})
through Kolmogorov extension theorem.

The measures
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
can be expressed using the newly defined ones. For any
S\in\mathcal{B}
and
A\in\mathcal{O}
,

(9)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}(S\times A)=\int_{S}{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\hat{x}}}(A)\,{\rm d}\nu(\hat{x}).

As proved in
[
BFPP19
, Proposition 2.1]
, the marginal of
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
on
\mathcal{O}
is the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\rho_{\nu}}
.

We will manipulate some metric-related notion such as Hölder continuity. We thus equip
{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
with the metric

d(\hat{x},\hat{y})=\sqrt{1-|\langle x,y\rangle|^{2}}=\|\pi_{\hat{x}}-\pi_{\hat{y}}\|_{\infty}.

2.2.
Some preliminary results on
\Pi

The proof of geometric convergence towards the invariant measure in
[
BFPP19
]
relies on two assumptions:

Assumptions

(Pur)
Any orthogonal projector
\pi
such that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\pi v_{1}^{*}\ldots v_{n}^{*}v_{n}\ldots v_{1}\pi\propto\pi
for
\mu^{\otimes n}
-almost all
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
, is of rank one.

(Erg)
There exists a unique minimal subspace
E\neq\{0\}
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
vE\subset E
for all
v\in\operatorname{supp}\mu
.

As mentioned in
[
BFPP19
]
, while formulated in terms of the measure
\mu
, assumption
(Erg)
depends only on the quantum channel

\phi:\rho\mapsto\int_{M_{d}(\mathbb{C})}v\rho v^{*}\ {\rm d}\mu(v).

The assumption
(Erg)
is equivalent to
\phi
accepting a unique fixed point
\rho_{inv}
in
\mathcal{D}_{d}
. Then
E=(\ker\rho_{inv})^{\perp}
. Especially, assumption
(Erg)
with
E={\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is equivalent to assuming
\phi
is irreducible (see
[
Wol12
, Section 6.2]
for formulations of this notion of irreducibility).

Unless stated otherwise, all the results of the present article assume the same two assumptions hold. We do not mention them explicitly each time.

We refer to
[
BFPP19
]
for a complete discussion regarding the link with the usual assumptions in the context of i.i.d product of random matrices (see
[
BL85
]
for a reference on this subject). Let us mention that, as proved in
[
BFPP19
, Appendix A]
,
(Pur)
is equivalent to the contractivity assumption. Concerning
(Erg)
, as already mentioned in Introduction, it is weaker than the strong irreducibility condition.

The convergence obtained in
[
BFPP19
]
is expressed in terms of Wasserstein metric. A convergence in total variation is not accessible as is proved by the example of Section
8
. Wasserstein metric of order
1
between two probability measures
\sigma
and
\tau
on a metric space
(X,d)
is defined by

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(\sigma,\tau)=\inf_{P}\int_{X\times X}d(x,y){\rm d}P(x,y)

where the infimum is taken over all probability measures
P
over
X\times X
such that
P({\rm d}x,X)=\sigma({\rm d}x)
and
P(X,{\rm d}y)=\tau({\rm d}y)
.
For
X
compact, using Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality theorem it can be expressed as

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\sigma,\tau)=\sup_{f\in{\rm Lip}_{1}(X)}\left|\int_{X}f\,{\rm d}\sigma-\int_{X}f\,{\rm d}\tau\right|,

where
{\rm Lip}_{1}(X)=\{f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ |f(x)-f(y)|\leq d(x,y)\}
is the set of Lipschitz continuous functions with constant one.

Then, the main result of
[
BFPP19
]
is the following theorem, numbered Theorem 1.1 in the original reference.

Theorem 2.1
.

The Markov kernel
\Pi
accepts a unique invariant probability measure
\nu_{inv}
.

Moreover, there exists
m\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
C>0
and
0<\lambda<1
such that for any probability measure
\nu
over
\big({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})},\mathcal{B}\big)
,

(11)

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\nu\Pi^{mn+r},\nu_{inv}\right)\leq C\lambda^{n}.

We finish these preliminaries with a result that was overlooked in
[
BFPP19
]
. Indeed no proof of the existence of an invariant measure was provided. An invariant measure exists if
\Pi
maps continuous functions to continuous function. In other words if
\Pi
is Feller. As we will need this property, we provide a short proof. Note that the conclusion of the proposition holds even if
(Erg)
and
(Pur)
do not hold.

Proposition 2.2
.

The operator
\Pi
is Feller, that is for every continuous function
f
,
\Pi f
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
f
be a continuous function on
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, then for all
\hat{x}
, we have

(12)

\Pi f(\hat{x})=\int_{M_{d}(\mathbb{C})}f(v\cdot\hat{x})\|vx\|^{2}{\rm d}\mu(v).

We need to show that
\hat{x}\mapsto\Pi f(\hat{x})
is continuous.
Since
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is compact,
f
is bounded and

\psi:\hat{x}\mapsto f(v\cdot\hat{x})\|vx\|^{2}

is continuous or can be extended to a continuous function whenever

\psi
is bounded by

which is independent of
\hat{x}
and
\mu
integrable since

is assumed square integrable with respect to
\mu

∎

3.1.
Harmonic functions

We recall that, in the context of Markov chains, harmonic functions are measurable functions
f
such that
\Pi f=f
. They are deeply related to ergodic properties of Markov chains – see
[
MT09
, §17.1]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

If the assumptions
(Erg)
and
(Pur)
hold, all continuous harmonic functions for
\Pi
are constant.

Proof.

Let
\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
, introduce for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\overline{\nu}_{n}(\hat{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta_{\hat{x}}\Pi^{k}.

Since
{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
is compact, the sequence of measures
\overline{\nu}_{n}(\hat{x})
is tight and by Prokhorov’s theorem, there exists a weak limit
\overline{\nu}_{\infty}(\hat{x})
along a subsequence
(n_{l})_{l}
. Since
\Pi f=f
by assumption and
f
is continuous,
\Pi f
is continuous. The weak convergence implies
\lim_{l\to\infty}\overline{\nu}_{n_{l}}(\hat{x})\Pi f=\overline{\nu}_{\infty}(\hat{x})\Pi f
. Since
\overline{\nu}_{n}(\hat{x})\Pi=\overline{\nu}_{n}(\hat{x})-\frac{1}{n}(\delta_{\hat{x}}-\delta_{\hat{x}}\Pi^{n})
, it follows
\overline{\nu}_{\infty}(\hat{x})\Pi=\overline{\nu}_{\infty}(\hat{x})
. Theorem
2.1
implies
\overline{\nu}_{\infty}(\hat{x})=\nu_{inv}
. If
f
is harmonic,
f(\hat{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\nu}_{n}(\hat{x})}(f)
for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If moreover
f
is continuous, the weak convergence of
\overline{\nu}_{n}(\hat{x})
implies
f(\hat{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\nu}_{\infty}(\hat{x})}(f)={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(f)
. Hence
f
is constant and the proposition is proved.
∎

For any continuous function
g:{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, let us introduce the function
g_{\infty}
defined by

g_{\infty}(\hat{x})=\mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}\left(\left\{\tfrac{1}{n}S_{n}(g)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right\}\right),

for all
\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d}).

We prove that this function is constant by applying Proposition
3.2
. To this end, we need the two following lemmas which imply the continuity. Before expressing the lemmas let us introduce the maximum likelihood estimator of the initial state

(13)

\hat{z}_{n}(\omega)=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}}\,\|W_{n}x\|^{2}

and its evolved version

(14)

\hat{y}_{n}=W_{n}\cdot\hat{z}_{n}.

Proposition 3.5 in
[
BFPP19
]
(or Lemma 2.3 in the same reference) proves that
\hat{y}_{n}
is a good estimator of
\hat{x}_{n}
. We use this property to replace
(\hat{x}_{n})
by
(\hat{y}_{n})
in

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
g
be a continuous function on
{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
. For any
\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
,

(15)

\displaystyle g_{\infty}(\hat{x})=\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\hat{x}}}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{y}_{k})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right\}\right).

Proof.

Proposition 3.5 in
[
BFPP19
]
implies

d(\hat{x}_{n},\hat{y}_{n})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0,\quad\mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Since
g
is a continuous function and
{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
is compact, uniform continuity implies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(g(\hat{x}_{k})-g(\hat{y}_{k})\right)\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0,\quad\mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

This way it is clear that
g_{\infty}
satisfies for all
\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}

g_{\infty}(\hat{x})=\mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{y}_{k})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right\}\right).

The set
\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{y}_{k})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right\}
is
\mathcal{O}
mesurable then Equation (
9
) yields the lemma by taking
\nu=\delta_{\hat{x}}
and
S={{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
.
∎

We will use the next lemma to prove the continuity of
g_{\infty}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, we have the following estimation in total variation distance

\|\mathbb{P}^{\rho}-\mathbb{P}^{\sigma}\|_{TV}:=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{O}}|\mathbb{P}^{\rho}(A)-\mathbb{P}^{\sigma}(A)|\leq\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1},

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the trace norm defined by
\|A\|_{1}=\operatorname{tr}(\sqrt{A^{*}A})
.

Proof.

Since sets
O
such that there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
O\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
form a generating family for
\mathcal{O}
and since the upper bound is independent of
O
, following
[
Wal00
, Theorem 0.7]
, it is sufficient to prove the inequality for such sets. Let thus
O_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
for an arbitrary
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}^{\rho}(O_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\sigma}(O_{n})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{O_{n}}\text{tr}\big(W_{n}\rho W_{n}^{*}\big)d\mu^{\otimes n}-\int_{O_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\big(W_{n}\sigma W_{n}^{*}\big){\rm d}\mu^{\otimes n}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\operatorname{tr}\big((\rho-\sigma)\int_{O_{n}}W_{n}^{*}W_{n}{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes n}\big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\Big(|\rho-\sigma|\int_{O_{n}}W_{n}^{*}W_{n}{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1},

where for the first inequality, we used that
|\operatorname{tr}(AB)|\leq\operatorname{tr}(|A|B)
for any
B
positive semidefinite and
A
self adjoint with
|A|=\sqrt{A^{*}A}
, and for the second one, we used that
\int_{M_{d}({\mathbb{C}})^{n}}W_{n}^{*}W_{n}{\rm d}\mu^{\otimes n}={\rm Id}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

The function
g_{\infty}
is continuous and harmonic, hence constant.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.2
, it is sufficient to prove
g_{\infty}
is continuous and harmonic. Let
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
be elements of
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, using Lemma
3.3
and the fact that
\hat{y}_{n}
is
\mathcal{O}
-measurable,

\displaystyle|g_{\infty}(\hat{x})

\displaystyle-g_{\infty}(\hat{y})|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\hat{x}}}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{y}_{k})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right\}\right)-\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\hat{y}}}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{y}_{k})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right\}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\hat{x}}}-\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\hat{y}}}\|_{TV}

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi_{\hat{x}}-\pi_{\hat{y}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,d(\hat{x},\hat{y}).

We used that for a rank
r
matrix
A
,
\|A\|_{1}\leq r\|A\|_{\infty}
.
Hence
g_{\infty}
is 2-Lipschitz, therefore continuous.

The harmonicity of
g_{\infty}
is a classical result and one can follow the proof of
[
MT09
, Proposition 17.1.6]
. For sake of completeness, we reproduce it here in our context. Let
\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

\displaystyle\Pi g(\hat{x})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}_{1}}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}S_{n}(g)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left(\left.{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{x}_{k+1})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right|\mathcal{J}_{1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\left[\frac{n+1}{n}\frac{1}{n+1}S_{n+1}(g)-\frac{1}{n}g(\hat{x}_{0})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\hat{x}).

The passage from line 1 to line 2 uses the Markov property and the remainder uses the fact that
g
is bounded as a continuous function on a compact space.
∎

We turn to the proof of the Law of Large Numbers.

3.2.
Proof of the Law of Large Numbers, Theorem
3.1

The proof is split in two parts. First Theorem
3.1
is proved with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
using Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem and
[
BFPP19
, Proposition 3.5]
. Then, the result is extended to any initial probability measure
\nu
using
g_{\infty}
as is done for positive Harris Markov chains in
[
MT09
, Proposition 17.1.6]
.

Before we prove Theorem
3.1
with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
we prove the invariance of this measure with respect to the shift

\displaystyle\Theta:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\times\Omega

\displaystyle\to{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\times\Omega

\displaystyle(\hat{x},v_{1},v_{2},\dotsc)

\displaystyle\mapsto(v_{1}\cdot\hat{x},v_{2},v_{3},\dotsc).

Lemma 3.6
.

The probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
is
\Theta
-invariant.

Proof.

Let
f:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
be continuous and bounded. Then,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(f\circ\Theta)=\int f(v_{1}\cdot\hat{x},v_{2},\dotsc){\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\hat{x}}}(v_{1},v_{2},\dotsc){\rm d}\nu_{inv}(\hat{x}).

By definition
{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\hat{x}}}(v_{1},v_{2},\dotsc)=\|v_{1}x\|^{2}{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{v_{1}\cdot\hat{x}}}(v_{2},v_{3},\dotsc){\rm d}\mu(v_{1})
, thus,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(f\circ\Theta)=\int f(v_{1}\cdot\hat{x},v_{2},\dotsc){\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{v_{1}\cdot\hat{x}}}(v_{2},v_{3},\dotsc)\|v_{1}x\|^{2}{\rm d}\mu(v_{1}){\rm d}\nu_{inv}(\hat{x}).

By invariance of
\nu_{inv}
with respect to
\Pi
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(f\circ\Theta)=\int f(\hat{x},v_{2},\dotsc){\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\hat{x}}}(v_{2},\dotsc){\rm d}\nu_{inv}(\hat{x}).

A change of variables yields
{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(f\circ\Theta)={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(f)
.
∎

We can now prove the LLN with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
g
be a continuous function on
{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. The LLN is satisfied for the invariant measure
\nu_{inv}
, that is

\tfrac{1}{n}S_{n}(g)\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g),\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Proof.

This proof is an adaptation of
[
MT09
, Section 17.1]
.
By definition,
S_{n}(g)
can be written

S_{n}(g)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g\circ\zeta\circ\Theta^{k}(\hat{x},v_{1},v_{2}\dotsc)

with for all
\hat{x}
and all
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)

\zeta(\hat{x},v_{1},v_{2},\dotsc)=\hat{x}.

Following Lemma
3.6
, the probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
is
\Theta
-invariant. Hence, Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem for stationary processes implies,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}S_{n}(g)=X_{g}\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv.}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

with
X_{g}
a
\Theta
invariant random variable.

Since
g
is continuous (and therefore uniformly continuous), the convergence
d(\hat{x}_{n},\hat{y}_{n})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
-almost surely implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{y}_{k})=X_{g}\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Since the sequence
(\hat{y}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{O}
-measurable, so is
X_{g}
. For
\mathcal{O}
-measurable functions,
\Theta
is the left shift
(v_{1},v_{2},\dotsc)\mapsto(v_{2},v_{3},\dotsc)
. Since
{\mathbb{P}}^{\rho_{\nu_{inv}}}
is left shift ergodic as a consequence of
[
BFPP19
, Proposition 3.4]
(see also
[
KM03
, Section 5]
),
X_{g}
is
{\mathbb{P}}^{\rho_{\nu_{inv}}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}
constant. Then, from the definition of
{\mathbb{P}}^{\rho_{\nu_{inv}}}
,

X_{g}=\mathbb{E}^{\rho_{\nu_{inv}}}(X_{g})={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv.}}(X_{g})={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv.}}(g),\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv.}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

∎

Now, we have all the ingredients to prove Theorem
3.1

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition
3.7
shows that

\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{inv}}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\hat{x}_{k})\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)\right\}\right)=1,

which can be written as

\int_{{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})}g_{\infty}(\hat{x})d\nu_{inv}(\hat{x})=1.

Since the integrand is no greater than one, it means that it is equal to one. For
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{inv}}
-almost every
\hat{x}
,
g_{\infty}(\hat{x})=1
and since Proposition
3.5
implies

is constant,
g_{\infty}(\hat{x})=1
for all
\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
and the theorem is proved.
∎

4.
Poisson equation and martingale approximation

The remaining limit theorems are proved using the existence of a continuous solution
\tilde{g}
to the Poisson equation

\tilde{g}-\Pi\tilde{g}=\overline{g}=g-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)

We prove such a solution exists as long as
g
is
\alpha
-Hölder for some
\alpha\in\,]0,1]
where
1
-Hölder means Lipschitz.

Once a continuous solution
\tilde{g}
is found,
S_{n}(\overline{g})
can be approximated by the
(\mathcal{J}_{n})
-martingale

(16)

M_{n}(g):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{k})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{k-1}).

Indeed,

S_{n}(\overline{g})=\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{0})-\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n})+M_{n}(g).

Since
\tilde{g}
is continuous and therefore uniformly bounded,
(M_{n}(g))_{n}
has bounded increments and

(17)

|S_{n}(\overline{g})-M_{n}(g)|\leq\operatorname{osc}(\tilde{g})\leq 2\|\tilde{g}\|_{\infty},

almost surely, where
\operatorname{osc}
is the oscillation defined by
\operatorname{osc}(f)=\sup_{\hat{x},\hat{y}}|f(\hat{x})-f(\hat{y})|
.

First we prove a geometric convergence for Hölder functions which will be a key ingredient in the sequel.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists
0<\lambda<1
such that for any
g:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\to{\mathbb{C}}
Hölder continuous, there exists
C>0
such that for any probability measure
\nu
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(18)

\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[g(\hat{x}_{mn+r})]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}[g(\hat{x})]\right|\leq C\lambda^{\alpha n}

where
\alpha
is the Hölder exponent of
g
.

Proof.

For a coupling
P_{n}
between
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\nu\Pi^{mn+r}
and
\nu_{inv}
,

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[g(\hat{x}_{mn+r})]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}[g(\hat{x})]={\mathbb{E}}_{P_{n}}(g(\hat{x})-g(\hat{y})).

Since
g
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, there exists
C
such that

|{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{n}}(g(\hat{x})-g(\hat{y}))|\leq C{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{n}}(d(\hat{x},\hat{y})^{\alpha}).

Using that
x\mapsto x^{\alpha}
is concave, Jensen inequality implies,

|{\mathbb{E}}_{P_{n}}(g(\hat{x})-g(\hat{y}))|\leq C\left({\mathbb{E}}_{P_{n}}(d(\hat{x},\hat{y}))\right)^{\alpha}.

The coupling
P_{n}
being arbitrary we can take an infimum over it, so that

\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[g(\hat{x}_{mn+r})]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}[g(\hat{x})]\right|\leq CW_{1}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\nu\Pi^{mn+r},\nu_{inv}\right).

Then Theorem
2.1
yields the lemma.
∎

A major upside in the above lemma is the uniformity of the bound with respect to the initial measure. We often exploit this property in the sequel and in particular in the following proof of existence of a continuous solution to the Poisson equation (
3
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
g:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be Hölder continuous. If the assumptions
(Erg)
and
(Pur)
hold, then the Poisson equation

(19)

({\rm Id}-\Pi)\tilde{g}=\bar{g},

admits a continuous solution
\tilde{g}
. Two continuous solutions of the Poisson equation (
19
) are equal up to an additive constant.

As a consequence the martingale approximation of Equation (
17
) holds. That is,

(20)

\sup_{n}|S_{n}(\overline{g})-M_{n}(g)|\leq\operatorname{osc}(\tilde{g})\leq 2\|\tilde{g}\|_{\infty}

almost surely.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
g=\bar{g}
. Let

f_{n}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=0}^{nm+r-1}\Pi^{k}g,

for
r\in\{0,...,m-1\}
. Proposition
2.2
implies
\Pi
preserves the continuity, hence
f_{n}^{(r)}
is continuous for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
r\in\{0,...,m-1\}
.

For all
\hat{x}
and
k\geq 0
, denote
\nu_{k,\hat{x}}=\delta_{\hat{x}}\Pi^{k}
, then for all
p\leq q
and all
\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

(21)

\begin{split}\left|f_{p}^{(r)}(\hat{x})-f_{q}^{(r)}(\hat{x})\right|&=\Big|\sum_{k=pm+r}^{qm+r-1}\Pi^{k}g(\hat{x})\Big|=\Big|\sum_{k=pm}^{qm-1}\Pi^{k}\Big(\Pi^{r}g(\hat{x})\Big)\Big|\\
&\leq m\left|\sum_{k=p}^{q-1}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\Pi^{r}\Pi^{km+l}g(\hat{x})\right|=m\left|\sum_{k=p}^{q-1}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\nu_{r,\hat{x}}\Pi^{km+l}g\right|\\
&\leq m\sum_{k=p}^{q-1}C\lambda^{\alpha k}\leq\frac{mC}{1-\lambda^{\alpha}}\lambda^{\alpha p},\end{split}

where the constants
C
and
\lambda
are the ones from Lemma
4.1
and are thus uniform in
\nu_{r,\hat{x}}

It follows the sequence
\{f_{p}^{(r)}\}_{p\geq 0}
is uniformly Cauchy and converges therefore uniformly towards a continuous function
f_{\infty}^{(r)}
. The equality
({\rm Id}-\Pi)f_{n}^{(r)}=g-\Pi^{nm+r}g
and Lemma
4.1
imply the uniform convergence

(22)

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}({\rm Id}-\Pi)\Big(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}f_{n}^{(r)}\Big)&=g-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\Pi^{nm+r}g=g.\end{split}

Since
({\rm Id}-\Pi)
is a bounded operator with respect to the uniform norm,

({\rm Id}-\Pi)\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}f_{\infty}^{(r)}\right)=g.

Hence,
\tilde{g}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}f_{\infty}^{(r)}
is a solution to Poisson equation (
19
). As a finite sum of continuous functions, it is also continuous.

In order to show uniqueness up to an additive constant, let
\tilde{g}_{1}
and
\tilde{g}_{2}
be two continuous solutions of Equation (
3
), then
\tilde{g}_{1}-\tilde{g}_{2}
satisfies
\Pi(\tilde{g}_{1}-\tilde{g}_{2})=\tilde{g}_{1}-\tilde{g}_{2}
. Since
\tilde{g}_{1}-\tilde{g}_{2}
is continuous and harmonic, it is constant by Proposition
3.2
.
∎

).

5.

This section is devoted to the proof of a FCLT. We define a version of
S_{n}
as a continuous function interpolating between the partial sums
S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}
for

. For any Hölder continuous function

let

s_{n}(t):=S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(g)+(nt-\lfloor nt\rfloor)(S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}(g)-S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(g)).

We recall that
\gamma_{g}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(\tilde{g}^{2}-(\Pi\tilde{g})^{2})
with
\tilde{g}
a solution to the Poisson equation (
3
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)=0
. If the assumptions
(Erg)
and
(Pur)
hold,

(1)

If
\gamma_{g}^{2}>0
, for any initial measure
\nu

\left(\frac{s_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n\gamma_{g}^{2}}}\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{\mathcal{L}}B\equiv(B_{t})_{t\in[0,1]},

where

is a unidimensional Brownian motion and the convergence is meant for the process.

(2)

If
\gamma_{g}^{2}=0
, then

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|s_{n}(t)|\leq 6\operatorname{osc}(\tilde{g}),\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

and for any measure
\nu
, for any

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\frac{s_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n}}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{P}}_{\nu})}=0.

Proof.

First, assume
\gamma_{g}^{2}>0
. We apply the Central Limit Theorem for martingales. For
t\in[0,1]
and any
n
, let

m_{n}(t):=M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(g)+(nt-\lfloor nt\rfloor)(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}(g)-M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(g)).

We first show
(m_{n}(t))_{t}
converges to a Brownian motion. We prove the following two properties:

a)

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
almost surely

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(M_{k}(g)-M_{k-1}(g))^{2}|\mathcal{J}_{k-1}]\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}\gamma^{2}_{g}.

b)

For all
\varepsilon>0
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
almost surely

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(M_{k}(g)-M_{k-1}(g)\Big)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{(M_{k}(g)-M_{k-1}(g))^{2}\geq\varepsilon n\}}|\mathcal{J}_{k-1}\Big]\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0.

Item
b)
follows directly from
\sup_{k}\|M_{k}(g)-M_{k-1}(g)\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|\tilde{g}\|_{\infty}
. Indeed that implies the terms in the sum on the left hand side vanish for
n
large enough. Hence the left hand side is null for
n\geq 4\|\tilde{g}\|_{\infty}^{2}/\varepsilon
.

Item
a)
follows from the LLN. For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(24)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[(M_{k}(g)-M_{k-1}(g))^{2}|\mathcal{J}_{k-1}]&=\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{k})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{k-1}))^{2}|\mathcal{J}_{k-1}]\\
&=\Big(\Pi{\tilde{g}}^{2}(\hat{x}_{k-1})-(\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{k-1}))^{2}\Big)\\
&=h(\hat{x}_{k-1}),\end{split}

where
h(\cdot)=\Pi\tilde{g}^{2}(\cdot)-(\Pi\tilde{g}(\cdot))^{2}
. Since
\Pi
is Feller (see Proposition
2.2
) and
\tilde{g}
is continuous,
h
is a continuous function. Then, Theorem
3.1
and
\gamma_{g}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(h)
yield Item
a)
.

Theorem 4.1 in
[
HH80
]
implies

\left(\frac{m_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n\gamma_{g}^{2}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathcal{L}}B.

We turn to
(s_{n}(t))_{t}
. Proposition
4.2
implies

(25)

\sup_{t\in[0,1],n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|m_{n}(t)-s_{n}(t)|\leq 6\operatorname{osc}(\tilde{g}).

Thus,

(26)

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\frac{s_{n}(t)-m_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n\gamma_{g}^{2}}}\right|\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0,\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Then Slutsky’s lemma implies Item
(1)
.

Second, assume
\gamma_{g}^{2}=0
. By the martingale property,
{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2}(g))=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}((\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{k})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{k-1}))^{2})
. Taking a conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{J}_{k-1}
inside the expectation leads to

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2}(g))=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}\left(h(\hat{x}_{k-1})\right).

Invariance of
\nu_{inv}
implies,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2}(g))=\lfloor nt\rfloor\gamma_{g}^{2}=0.

Hence,
M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(g)=0

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
-almost surely. Similarly,
M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}(g)=0

{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{inv}}
-almost surely. Then, Proposition
4.2
yields the first part of Item
(2)
.

For an arbitrary measure
\nu
over
{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
, the martingale property implies

{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2}(g))=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}\nu(\Pi^{k-1}h).

Then, Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
imply
\frac{1}{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}^{2}(g))\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\gamma_{g}^{2}=0
. Similarly,
\frac{1}{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}(M_{\lfloor nt\rfloor+1}^{2}(g))\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\gamma_{g}^{2}=0
. Then Proposition
4.2
yields the
L^{2}
-norm convergence of the second part of Item
(2)
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The convergence in
L^{2}
-norm when
\gamma_{g}^{2}=0
should also hold
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
almost surely. However, such a result requires a finer analysis of the function
h
. In
[

]
,

is shown to be Hölder continuous. That implies

by Lemma

. Then the almost sure convergence follows from Doob’s martingale convergence theorem as in the proof of
[
MT09
, Theorem 17.1.7]
.

Remark 5.3
.

The case
\gamma_{g}^{2}=0
is somewhat trivial. We therefore concentrate on the situation where
\gamma_{g}^{2}>0
.

6.
Law of Iterated Logarithm

This section is devoted to the the Law of Iterated Logarithm. It shows that at only a slightly faster speed (
\log\log n
) the weak convergence of the CLT is lost.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
g:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be Hölder continuous and such that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)=0
. Assume
(Erg)
and
(Pur)
hold. Let
\tilde{g}
be a continuous solution to the Poisson equation (
3
) and
\gamma_{g}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(\tilde{g}^{2}-(\Pi\tilde{g})^{2})
. Assume
\gamma_{g}^{2}>0
. Then for any initial measure
\nu
,

\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\pm S_{n}(g)}{\sqrt{2n\gamma_{g}^{2}\log\log(n)}}=1\;\;\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Proof.

As for the FCLT, Proposition
4.2
allows us to prove the statement only for
M_{n}(g)
instead of
S_{n}(g)
. We apply the theorem of
[
Sto70
]
.

Let

s_{n}^{2}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(M_{k}(g)-M_{k-1}(g))^{2}|\mathcal{J}_{k-1}]

and

u_{n}=\sqrt{2\log\log(s_{n}^{2})}.

Following (
24
), since
\Pi
is Feller according to Proposition
2.2
,
h
is continuous and Theorem
3.1
implies
\frac{1}{n}s_{n}^{2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\gamma_{g}^{2},\,\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}
. Hence,

s_{n}^{2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}+\infty,\,\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Then, since almost surely,

|M_{n}(g)-M_{n-1}(g)|\leq 2\|\tilde{g}\|_{\infty},

following the remark at the end of
[
Sto70
, Section 3]
, the theorem in the introduction of the same reference applies and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\pm M_{n}(g)}{s_{n}u_{n}}=1,\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Since
s_{n}^{2}/n\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\gamma_{g}^{2},\,\,{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}
, we have the almost sure equivalences
s_{n}\sim\sqrt{n\gamma_{g}^{2}}
and
u_{n}\sim\sqrt{2\log\log n}
as
n
grows. These equivalences yield the theorem.
∎

7.
Moderate Deviation Principle

Our final limit theorem concerns the probability of deviation from the law of large numbers for rates in
n
faster that the
\sqrt{n}
of the CLT and slower than the linear one of the LLN. The Moderate Deviation Principle shows that at any of these rates the probability of deviation is essentially gaussian with variance
\gamma_{g}^{2}
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
g:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be Hölder continuous and such that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)=0
. Let
t\mapsto a(t),t>0
be a non negative function such that

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t}{a(t)}=\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{a(t)}{\sqrt{t}}=+\infty.

Then, if the assumptions
(Erg)
and
(Pur)
hold,

(1)

For all
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\Big|\frac{n}{a^{2}(n)}\log\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\Big[\exp{\frac{a(n)}{n}zS_{n}(g)}\Big]\Big)-\frac{z^{2}\gamma_{g}^{2}}{2}\Big|=0.

(2)

For any Borel subset
B
of
{\mathbb{R}}
:

\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n}{a^{2}(n)}\log\Big(\sup\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{a(n)}S_{n}(g)\in B\Big)\Big)\leq-\inf_{y\in\bar{B}}J(y)

\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n}{a^{2}(n)}\log\Big(\inf\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{a(n)}S_{n}(g)\in B\Big)\Big)\geq-\inf_{y\in B^{\circ}}J(y)

where
\bar{B}
denotes the closure of
B
and
B^{\circ}
its interior and

J(y)=\sup\limits_{v\in\mathbb{R}}\big(yv-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma_{g}^{2}v^{2}\big)=\begin{cases}\frac{y^{2}}{2\gamma_{g}^{2}}&\text{if }\gamma_{g}^{2}>0\\
0&\text{if }\gamma_{g}^{2}=0\text{ and }y=0\\
+\infty&\text{if }\gamma_{g}^{2}=0\text{ and }y\neq 0\end{cases}.

Remark 7.2
.

If
\gamma_{g}^{2}>0
,
y\mapsto J(y)
is continuous, then, for
B
with non empty interior, the inequalities turn into equalities. Hence,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{a^{2}(n)}\log\left(\sup_{\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}}{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}}\left(\tfrac{1}{a(n)}S_{n}(g)\in B\right)\right)=-\inf_{y\in B}\frac{y^{2}}{2\gamma_{g}^{2}}.

We prove Theorem
7.1
through an application of
[
Gao96
, Theorem 1.1]
to
(M_{n}(g))_{n}
. In our context the two assumptions of this theorem are:

(A.1) there exists
\delta>0
such that,

\sup_{\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}}\sup_{n\geq 0}\|\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}(\exp(\delta|M_{n+1}(g)-M_{n}(g)|)|\mathcal{F}_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}})}<+\infty,

(A.2) the following limit inferior holds:

\liminf_{n\to\infty,\frac{n}{m}\to 0}\sup_{\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})}\sup_{j\geq 0}\Big|\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+j+i})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+j+i-1})\big)^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{j}\Big]-\gamma_{g}^{2}\Big|=0.

Assumption (A.1) is trivially satisfied with an arbitrary
\delta>0
since
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|M_{n+1}(g)-M_{n}(g)|\leq 2\|\tilde{g}\|_{\infty}

{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}}
-almost surely with
\tilde{g}
a continuous solution to the Poisson equation (
3
). Assumption (A.2) requires more work and cannot be derived from
[
Gao96
]
since in that article the Markov chain is assumed Harris recurrent. The next lemma shows a uniform convergence of the Cesàro mean of
{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{x}}(h(\hat{x}_{n}))
with
h
defined in Equation (
24
) to
\gamma_{g}^{2}
. It is the key to the proof of Assumption (A.2).

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
g:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\to{\mathbb{C}}
and
\tilde{g}
a continuous solution to the Poisson equation (
3
). Then,

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\sup\limits_{j\geq 0}\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{j+i})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{j+i-1})\big)^{2}\right]-\gamma_{g}^{2}\right|=0.

Proof.

Taking the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{J}_{j+i-1}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{x}}(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{j+i})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{i+j-1}))^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{x}}(h(\hat{x}_{i+j-1}))

with
h:\hat{x}\mapsto\Pi\tilde{g}^{2}(\hat{x})-(\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}))^{2}
the continuous function defined in Equation (
24
).

Since Lipschitz continuous functions are dense in the set of continuous functions with respect to the sup norm, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
h_{\epsilon}
Lipschitz continuous such that
\|h-h_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
. It follows that

|\gamma_{g}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(h_{\epsilon})|\leq\epsilon

and

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sup\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\sup\limits_{j\geq 0}\left|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\hat{x}_{i+j-1})\right]-\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{i+j-1})\right]\right|\leq\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon
is arbitrary and the second bound is uniform in
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it remains only to prove

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\sup\limits_{j\geq 0}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left[h_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{j+i-1})\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(h_{\epsilon})\right|=0

for any
\epsilon>0
.

First, by the Markov property,

\sup\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\sup\limits_{j\geq 0}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left[h_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{j+i-1})\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(h_{\epsilon})\right|\leq\sup\limits_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})})}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[h_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{i-1})\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(h_{\epsilon})\right|,

where
\mathcal{P}({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})})
is the set of probability measures over
{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
. By definition of the Wasserstein distance of order
1
, Theorem
2.1
implies there exists
C>0
such that

\sup\limits_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})})}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[h_{\epsilon}(\hat{x}_{i-1})\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(h_{\epsilon})\right|\leq C\tfrac{1}{n}.

Taking
n
to infinity yields the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

As remarked in
[
Gao96
]
, it is sufficient to prove Item
(1)
since then Gärtner–Ellis theorem (see
[
DZ09
, Theorem 2.3.6]
) implies the remainder of the theorem. We adapt the proof of Theorem 1.3 in
[
Gao96
]
.

As mentioned before Lemma
7.3
, it only remains to prove Assumption (A.2) of Theorem 1.1 in
[
Gao96
]
.
For
m>0
,

\begin{split}\sup_{\hat{x}}\sup_{n\geq 0}\sup_{j\geq 0}\Big|\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+j+i})&-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+j+i-1})\big)^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{j}\Big]-\gamma_{g}^{2}\Big|\\
&=\sup_{\hat{x}}\sup_{n\geq 0}\sup_{j\geq 0}\Big|\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}_{j}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+i})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+i-1})\big)^{2}\Big]-\gamma_{g}^{2}\Big|\\
&\leq\sup\limits_{\hat{y}}\sup\limits_{n\geq 0}\Big|\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+i})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+i-1})\big)^{2}\Big]-\gamma_{g}^{2}\Big|\\
\end{split}

Lemma
7.3
implies the convergence to
0
of the first line. This convergence being uniform in
n
, it implies

\liminf_{n\to\infty,\frac{n}{m}\to 0}\sup_{\hat{x}\in{\mathrm{P}}(\mathbb{C}^{d})}\sup_{j\geq 0}\Big|\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+j+i})-\Pi\tilde{g}(\hat{x}_{n+j+i-1})\big)^{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{j}\Big]-\gamma_{g}^{2}\Big|=0,

which is Assumption (A.2) of Theorem 1.1 in
[
Gao96
]
. It follows that for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sup\limits_{\hat{x}\in P({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\Big|\frac{n}{a^{2}(n)}\log\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\Big[\exp{\frac{a(n)}{n}zM_{n}(g)}\Big]\Big)-\frac{z^{2}\gamma_{g}^{2}}{2}\Big|=0.

Since
|S_{n}(g)-M_{n}(g)|\leq 2\operatorname{osc}(\tilde{g})
almost surely, Item
(1)
holds and the theorem is proved.
∎

8.
Keep-Switch example

To illustrate our results and demonstrate quantum trajectories can be relatively singular, we briefly discuss the “Keep-Switch” model presented in
[
BCJP21
, Section 2.1.2]
. Let
\mu=\delta_{A_{1}}+\delta_{A_{2}}
be a measure on
M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
, where

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p}&0\\
0&\sqrt{q}\end{pmatrix}\,\,\textrm{and}\,\,A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{p}\\
\sqrt{q}&0\end{pmatrix}

with
p\in]0,\frac{1}{2}[
and
q=1-p
. Direct computation shows that
A_{1}^{*}A_{1}+A_{2}^{*}A_{2}={\rm Id}
. Since
A_{1}^{*}A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}p&0\\
0&q\end{pmatrix}
is not proportional to the identity, the purification assumption
(Pur)
holds. The matrices
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
have a common non trivial invariant subspace only if they have a common eigenvector. The eigenvectors of
A_{1}
are multiples of
e_{a}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
and
e_{b}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}
. They are not eigenvectors of
A_{2}
. Hence,
\mu
verifies the ergodicity assumption
(Erg)
with
E={\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.
Direct computation shows that the invariant measure of Theorem
2.1
is
\nu_{inv}=p\delta_{\hat{e}_{a}}+q\delta_{\hat{e}_{b}}
.

This relatively simple example already exhibits some unusual properties. For example, it is not
\varphi
-irreducible as defined in
[
MT09
]
. It cannot therefore be Harris recurrent.

Proposition 8.1
.

There does not exist a non trivial positive measure
\varphi
over
{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}
such that the Markov chain associated to
\mu
is
\varphi
-irreducible.

Proof.

Assume such a measure
\varphi
exists. Then
\Pi{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\}}(\hat{e}_{a})=1
implies
\varphi({{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\setminus\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\})=0
. Since
\varphi
is non trivial,
\varphi(\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\})>0
. However, setting
e_{+}=e_{a}+e_{b}
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{e}_{+}}(\cup_{n}\{\hat{x}_{n}\in\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\}\})=0
as a consequence of
{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{e}_{+}}(\cup_{n}\{\langle e_{a},x_{n}\rangle=0\vee\langle e_{b},x_{n}\rangle=0\})=0
. Hence, there does not exists
\varphi
such that the Markov chain is
\varphi
-irreducible.
∎

As mentioned at the beginning of Section
3
, since the Markov chain is not Harris recurrent, following
[
MT09
, Theorem 17.1.7]
, there must exist a non continuous
L^{1}(\nu_{inv})
function such that the LLN does not hold.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
g={\mathbf{1}}_{\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\}}
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{inv}}(g)=1

but for any
\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\setminus\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\}
, any
n\geq 0
,

S_{n}(g)=0,\quad{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}

Proof.

The fact that
\nu_{inv}(g)=1
follows from the expression of the invariant measure.

For the proof that
S_{n}(g)=0
, as in the proof of previous proposition, for any
\hat{x}\in{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\setminus\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\}
,
{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}}(\cup_{n}\{\hat{x}_{n}\in\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\}\})=0
. Hence
g(\hat{x}_{k})=0
for all
n

{\mathbb{P}}_{\hat{x}}\operatorname{-}\mathrm{a.s.}
and the proposition holds.
∎

The place dependent IFS defined by
p_{i}:\hat{x}\mapsto\|A_{i}x\|^{2}
and
f_{i}:\hat{x}\mapsto A_{i}\cdot\hat{x}
is not contracting as defined in
[
BDEG88
]
either. Note that since
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are invertible,
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are well defined on
\mathrm{P}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
.

Proposition 8.3
.

The place dependent IFS defined by the Markov kernel
\Pi
is not contracting. Namely,

\sup_{\hat{x},\hat{y}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{x}}(d(V_{1}\cdot\hat{x},V_{1}\cdot\hat{y}))}{d(\hat{x},\hat{y})}\geq 1.

Proof.

Take
\hat{x}=\hat{e}_{a}
and
\hat{y}=\hat{e}_{b}
, then
d(\hat{x},\hat{y})=1
and
A_{1}\cdot\hat{x}=\hat{x}
,
A_{1}\cdot\hat{y}=\hat{y}
,
A_{2}\cdot\hat{x}=\hat{y}
and
A_{2}\cdot\hat{y}=\hat{x}
. Hence,
\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{x}}(d(V_{1}\cdot\hat{x},V_{1}\cdot\hat{y}))}{d(\hat{x},\hat{y})}=1
and the proposition is proved.
∎

For this example, the variance
\gamma_{g}^{2}
takes a simple form.

Proposition 8.4
.

For any
g:{{\mathrm{P}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})}\to{\mathbb{C}}
Hölder continuous,

\gamma_{g}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}\left(\left(g(\hat{x})-{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g(\hat{x}))\right)^{2}\right)=pg^{2}(\hat{e}_{a})+qg^{2}(\hat{e}_{b})-(pg(\hat{e}_{a})+qg(\hat{e}_{b}))^{2}.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{supp}\nu_{inv}=\{\hat{e}_{a},\hat{e}_{b}\}
, it is sufficient to compute
\tilde{g}(\hat{e}_{a})
and
\tilde{g}(\hat{e}_{b})
. Direct computation shows there exists
\tilde{g}
solution of the Poisson equation (
3
) such that
\tilde{g}(\hat{e}_{a})=g(\hat{e}_{a})-\nu_{inv}(g)
and
\tilde{g}(\hat{e}_{b})=g(\hat{e}_{b})-\nu_{inv}(g)
. Indeed,
\Pi g(\hat{e}_{a})=\Pi g(\hat{e}_{b})=\nu_{inv}(g)
. Moreover,
\nu_{inv}((\Pi\tilde{g})^{2})=0
. Hence,
\gamma_{g}^{2}=\nu_{inv}(\tilde{g}^{2})={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu_{inv}}(g(\hat{x}))
.
∎
