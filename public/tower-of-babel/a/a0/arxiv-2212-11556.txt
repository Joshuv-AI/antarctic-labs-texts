Abstract
. This is the first contribution of a sequence of papers introducing the notions of s s -weak order and s s -permutahedra, certain discrete objects that are indexed by a sequence of non-negative integers s s . In this first paper, we concentrate purely on the combinatorics and lattice structure of the s s -weak order, a partial order on certain decreasing trees which generalizes the classical weak order on permutations. In particular, we show that the s s -weak order is a semidistributive and congruence uniform lattice, generalizing known results for the classical weak order on permutations. Restricting the s s -weak order to certain trees gives rise to the s s -Tamari lattice, a sublattice which generalizes the classical Tamari lattice. We show that the s s -Tamari lattice can be obtained as a quotient lattice of the s s -weak order when s s has no zeros, and show that the s s -Tamari lattices (for arbitrary s s ) are isomorphic to the ν \nu -Tamari lattices of Préville-Ratelle and Viennot. The underlying geometric structure of the s s -weak order will be studied in a sequel of this paper, where we introduce the notions of s s -permutahedra and s s -associahedra.

Introduction

The weak order is a partial order on the set of permutations of
[n]
, which is defined as the inclusion order of their corresponding inversion sets.
This partial order turns out to be a lattice, whose Hasse diagram can be realized geometrically as the edge graph of a polytope called the permutahedron.
Restricting the weak order to the set of
231
-avoiding permutations gives rise to the Tamari lattice, a well-studied lattice whose
Hasse diagram can be realized as the edge graph of another polytope called the associahedron
[
MHPS12
]
.

s

.
Their Hasse diagrams are the edge graphs of the
s
-permutahedron and the
s
-associahedron.

The Tamari lattice and the associahedron have been central objects in the combinatorial and discrete geometry communities for the past decades. See for example the collection of papers gathered in
[
MHPS12
]
or the recent survey on Loday’s associahedron
[
PSZ23
]
. Many interesting connections have been made with the weak order, related in particular to Hopf algebraic structures
[
LR02
,
LR98
,
AS06
,
HNT05
]
and geometric realizations
[
HL07
,
HLT11
]
. The Tamari lattice is a lattice quotient of the weak order that can be realized as a generalized permutahedron, and it was proven in
[
PS19
]

that every lattice quotient of the weak order satisfies this property.
The Tamari lattice also inspired the study of many more general objects such as Cambrian lattices
[
Rea06
]
and the Permutree lattices
[
PP18
]
, which in turn connect to other subjects such as cluster algebras
[
FZ02
,
FZ03
]
.

New specific interests in the Tamari lattice arose in connection to representation theory and generalizations of the theory of diagonal harmonics. For instance, the number of intervals in the Tamari lattice
[
Cha07
]
is conjectured to be equal to the dimension of the alternating component of an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module in the study of trivariate diagonal harmonics
[
Hai94
,
BP12
]
.
This motivated the introduction of the
m
-Tamari lattice in
[
BP12
]
, whose number of intervals was conjectured to be the dimension of the alternating component of an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module in higher trivariate diagonal harmonics. A formula for their enumeration and connections to representation theory can be found in
[
BMCPR13
,
BMFPR11
]
.
Later on, the more general concept of the
\nu
-Tamari lattice was introduced in
[
PRV17
]
(which includes the
m
-Tamari and classical Tamari lattices). The connection to diagonal harmonics and representation theory is still unclear at this time, but several further investigations have been made, see for instance
[
BCP22
]
.
Other examples include the connections with the enumeration of non-separable planar maps in
[
FPR17
]
, and the tropical geometric realizations of
\nu
-Tamari lattices as the edge graph of a polytopal complex called the
\nu
-associahedron in
[
CPS20
]
. The interest for these new combinatorial and geometrical structures along with the numerous relations between the weak order and the Tamari lattice lead to a natural question:
what is the equivalent of the weak order for the
\nu
-Tamari lattices?
In the case of
m
-Tamari, the question was briefly approached in an extended abstract
[
Pon15
]
. The Hopf algebraic aspects were later studied in
[
NT20
]
.

This article is the first contribution of a sequence of papers which aims to answer this question by generalizing and completing the work started in
[
Pon15
]
, while connecting it to interesting new geometrical structures in relation to the
\nu
-Tamari lattice and the
\nu
-associahedron. We define the
s
-weak order
which is indexed by a weak composition
s=(s(1),s(2),\dots,s(n))
. The case were
s=(1,1,\dots,1)
is the classical weak order. In general, the
s
-weak order is a partial order on a set of certain trees which we call
s
-decreasing trees
,
and is defined as the inclusion order of their
s
-tree inversions
.
We show that the
s
-weak order has the structure of a lattice (Theorem
1.21
), and give a complete characterization of
its cover relations in terms of a simple combinatorial rule on the trees (Theorem
1.32
).
We also study its lattice properties and show that it is semidistributive and congruence
uniform (Theorem
1.40
)
.

Restricting the
s
-weak order to certain trees, which play the role of
231
-avoiding permutations,
gives rise to a sublattice which we call the
s
-Tamari lattice
(Theorem
2.2
).
We show that if
s
contains no zeros, then the
s
-Tamari lattice can also be obtained as a quotient lattice of the
s
-weak order (Theorem
2.20
).
We also give a simple characterization of the cover relations of the
s
-Tamari lattice (Theorem
2.25
),
and show that it is isomorphic to a well chosen

-Tamari lattice
(Theorem

).

Some of the connections between the Tamari lattice and the theory of diagonal harmonics mentioned above were originally discovered in connection to the study of Garcia–Haiman

,

On the other hand, the number of elements of the

For instance, if

It would be interesting to investigate whether these

We leave this as an open problem for the interested reader.

1.
The
s
-weak order

We denote by
\mathbb{P}
the set of positive integers, by
\mathbb{N}
the set of nonnegative integers and for any

n\in\mathbb{P}
let
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. A
weak composition
is a finite sequence

\mu=(\mu(1),\mu(2),\dots,\mu(n))
of numbers
\mu(i)\in\mathbb{N}
. For a weak composition
\mu
, we
define its
weight

|\mu|:=\sum_{i}\mu(i)
and its
length

\ell(\mu):=n
. For example
for
\mu=(2,0,3,4,0,1)
we have that
\ell(\mu)=6
and
|\mu|=10
.
A
composition
is a weak composition
\mu
such that
\mu(i)\neq 0
for all
i\in[\ell]
, in
other words, a composition is a finite sequence of entries in
\mathbb{P}
.

1.1.
s
-permutations

Let
s=(s(1),s(2),\dots,s(n))
be a composition (
without zero entries
).
an
s
-permutation is any permutation of the word
1^{s(1)}2^{s(2)}\dots n^{s(n)}
. We say that an
s
-permutation is
121
-avoiding (respectively
132
-avoiding) if it does not contain any subsequence of the form
a\dots b\dots a
for
a<b
(respectivelty
a\dots c\dots b
for
a<b<c
).
We will often refer to
s
as the
s
ignature
. This is the reason why we use the letter
s
.

The
s
-permutations appear in the work on Hopf algebras of Novelli and Thibon
[
NT20
]
, who study generalizations of the Hopf algebra of permutations for “
m
-permutations” of
1^{m}2^{m}\dots n^{m}
, and of the Hopf algebra on planar binary trees for
(m+1)
-ary trees. The authors introduce a notion of metasylvester congruence on permutations that allows them to obtain Hopf algebras based on decreasing trees. The element representatives of the congruence classes are
121
-avoiding
s
-permutations for
s=(m,m,\dots,m)
, see
[
NT20
, Proposition 3.10]
. These 121-avoiding
m
-permutations and associated lattices called metasylvester lattices were also discovered and studied by Pons in
[
Pon15
]
. One interesting property of this lattice is that the
m
-Tamari lattice can be obtained both as a sublattice and as a quotient lattice of it
[
Pon15
, Theorem 4.7]
.

In this section we generalize the results in
[
Pon15
]
for general signatures
s
.
This gives a natural notion of “rational” metasylvester lattices in the case where the entries of
s+\bf 1
count the numbers of boxes in each of the rows of an
a\times b
rectangle that are crossed by its main diagonal,
when
a\geq b
are relatively prime.
It turns out that the number of
132
-avoiding permutations in this case, see e.g
[
CGD19
]
, coincides with the rational Catalan number

\Cat(a,b)=\frac{1}{a+b}{a+b\choose a}.

The rational Catalan elements also have a natural lattice structure described by Préville-Ratelle and Viennot in
[
PRV17
]
, which we refer to as the
rational Tamari lattice
. More generally, they introduce a
v
-Tamari lattice
for arbitrary lattice paths
v
consisting of east and north steps in the plane.
As we will see in Section
2.5
,
our signature
s
can be thought as the composition that counts the number of east steps of
v
in each of the rows starting from top to bottom.

In this set up, it is natural to study the case where the signature
s
has some zero entries, corresponding for example to the case when
v
has two consecutive north steps. In this case, there is a row with zero number of east steps that will contribute a zero to the signature.
If the signature
s
has zero entries,
it is difficult to define well behaved notions of 121- and 132-avoiding
s
-permutations.
In this case, these notions
can be described more naturally in the context of
s
-decreasing labeled trees.

Let

The goal of this section is to describe the cover relations of the

Definition 1.24

Let

(ii)

(iii)

if

(iv)

if it exists, the strict right child of

The above definition can be visually interpreted from the tree:
basically,

Proposition 1.27
.

A couple

is a tree-ascent if and only it satisfies the following statements

(i)

for all

\card(d,c)=\card(d,a)

(ii)

\card(c,a)<s(c)
;

(iii)

\card(c,b)=\card(c,a)
implies that
\card(b,a)=s(b)

(iv)

\card(a,a^{\prime})=s(a)

\card(c,a^{\prime})>\card(c,a)

Proof.

Each condition of the proposition is a translation of the corresponding condition in Definition
1.24
. First, suppose that
(a,c)
is a tree-ascent in the sense of Definition
1.24

(i)

a

, so in particular, for any

and

(ii)

a
is not weakly right of

and cannot be right of

.

(iii)

Let us suppose that there is

. If

Definition 1.36

A lattice is said to be

if for all elements

(5)

\displaystyle z\wedge x=z\wedge y\Rightarrow z\wedge\left(x\vee y\right)=z\wedge x=z\wedge y.

\displaystyle z\vee x=z\vee y\Rightarrow z\vee\left(x\wedge y\right)=z\vee x=z\vee y.

Theorem 2.2
.

Proof.

We need to show that the collection of

Let

\card_{T\vee R}(c,a)\leq\card_{T\vee R}(c,b)
for any

It suffices to show this in the case where

For this, we will use the following alternative description of the inversions of

Let us call a

-transitive path between

The inversion

is the highest possible value of
\max\{\card_{T}(b_{1},b_{2}),\card_{R}(b_{1},b_{2})\}
over all such

If

we need to show

Without loss of generality we can assume that

In the other case,

\card_{T}(b_{k-1},a+1)\geq\card_{T}(b_{k-1},a)\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\card_{R}(b_{k-1},a+1)\geq\card_{R}(b_{k-1},a).

. Thus,

Definition 2.7
.

Let

s

The following proposition shows that the

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
s
be a composition (without zeros) and
T\preccurlyeq T^{\prime}
be a cover relation of the
s
-weak order.
Then,
T\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}T^{\prime}
if and only if
T^{\prime}
is obtained by applying an
s
-congruence rotation on
T
. Moreover, if the cover relation is not an
s
-congruence rotation, then
\pi_{\downarrow}(T)\prec\pi_{\downarrow}(T^{\prime})
.

You can check on Figure
14
that each cover relation satisfies this condition.

Proof.

Let
T
be an
s
-decreasing tree with a tree-ascent
(a,c)
, and
T^{\prime}
the result of the corresponding
s
-tree-rotation. We write
Q=\pi_{\downarrow}(T)
and
Q^{\prime}=\pi_{\downarrow}(T^{\prime})
. Let
A
be the set containing the node
a
and all its middle descendants. In particular
a^{\prime}\leq a
for all
a^{\prime}\in A
. Remember by Lemma
1.29
that the only increased cardinalities in
T^{\prime}
are those of
(c,a^{\prime})
where
a^{\prime}\in A
.

Assume that
(a,c)
is an
s
-congruence tree-ascent. This means that there exists
b
with
a<b<c
and
\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)
. Because
s(b)>0
, by transitivity this gives
\card_{T}(c,a^{\prime})\geq\card_{T}(c,b)
for all
a^{\prime}\in A
(all elements of
A
are right of
b
). Thus, the minimal cardinality computed by
\pi_{\downarrow}
is not obtained through the elements of
A
,
i.e.
for all
a^{\prime\prime}\leq a
(whether they are in
A
or not),
\card_{Q}(c,a^{\prime\prime})=\min(\card_{T}(c,b^{\prime});b^{\prime}\notin A;a^{\prime\prime}\leq b^{\prime}\leq c)
. This is also true in
T^{\prime}
and we get
\card_{Q^{\prime}}(c,a^{\prime\prime})=\min(\card_{T^{\prime}}(c,b^{\prime});b^{\prime}\notin A;a^{\prime\prime}\leq b^{\prime}\leq c)
. These two expressions are equal because for all
b^{\prime}\notin A
, we have
\card_{T}(c,b^{\prime})=\card_{T^{\prime}}(c,b^{\prime})
. As these are the only changes between
T
and
T^{\prime}
, we obtain that
Q=Q^{\prime}
.

Now suppose on the other hand that
(a,c)
is not an
s
-congruence tree-ascent. This means that there is no
b
with
a<b<c
such that
\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)
. In other words, for all
b
with
a<b<c
,
a
is left of
b
and we have
\card_{T}(c,a)\leq\card_{T}(c,b)
(this is true only because
s(b)>0)
. This implies that
\card_{Q}(c,a)=\card_{T}(c,a)
. In
T^{\prime}
, we have
\card_{T^{\prime}}(c,a^{\prime})=\card_{T}(c,a^{\prime})+1
for all
a^{\prime}\in A
. This includes
a
and only elements smaller than
a
. In particular,
\card_{T^{\prime}}(c,b)=\card_{T}(c,b)
and
\card_{T^{\prime}}(b,a)=\card_{T}(b,a)
for all
b
with
a<b<c
. We get that
\card_{Q^{\prime}}(c,a)=\card_{T^{\prime}}(c,a)=\card_{Q}(c,a)+1
. So
Q\neq Q^{\prime}
. Furthermore, the cardinality of
(c,a)
has strictly increased, and for all
a^{\prime\prime}\leq a
, we have

\card_{Q^{\prime}}(c,a^{\prime\prime})=\min(\card_{T^{\prime}}(c,b^{\prime});a^{\prime\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<c)\geq\min(\card_{T}(c,b^{\prime});a^{\prime\prime}\leq b^{\prime}<c)=\card_{Q}(c,a^{\prime\prime}).

Moreover, all other cardinalities are unchanged, and so
Q\prec Q^{\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
s
be a composition (without zeros), then
\pi_{\downarrow}
is order preserving.

Proof.

Proposition
2.8
states that if
T\preccurlyeq T^{\prime}
is a cover relation of the
s
-weak order, then
\pi_{\downarrow}(T)\preccurlyeq\pi_{\downarrow}(T^{\prime})
(the equality depending on whether or not the rotation is an
s
-congruence rotation). As this is true on cover relations, it can be extended to any
T\preccurlyeq T^{\prime}
.
∎

We say that a subset
X
of the
s
-weak order is

if
T,T^{\prime\prime}\in X
implies that
T^{\prime}\in X
for every
T\preccurlyeq T^{\prime}\preccurlyeq T^{\prime\prime}
.

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
s
be a composition (without zeros), the
s
-Tamari classes are closed by interval.

Proof.

Suppose that we have three
s
-decreasing trees
T\preccurlyeq T^{\prime}\preccurlyeq T^{\prime\prime}
such that
\pi_{\downarrow}(T)=\pi_{\downarrow}(T^{\prime\prime})=Q
. Since
\pi_{\downarrow}
is order preserving, we get
Q\preccurlyeq\pi_{\downarrow}(T^{\prime})\preccurlyeq Q
, and so
\pi_{\downarrow}(T^{\prime})=Q
.
∎

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
s
be a composition (without zeros), and
\equiv
be the equivalence relation on
s
-decreasing trees defined by:
T\equiv T^{\prime}
if and only if
T
and
T^{\prime}
can be connected by a sequence of
s
-congruence rotations. Then
\equiv
is the same equivalence relation than
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.8
,
s
-congruence rotations preserve the classes of
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
.
As a consequence, the equivalence
T\equiv T^{\prime}
implies
T\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}T^{\prime}
.
Now assume that
T\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}T^{\prime}
, we need to show that
T\equiv T^{\prime}
.
Consider the
s
-Tamari tree
Q=\pi_{\downarrow}(T)=\pi_{\downarrow}(T^{\prime})
obtained by projecting down
T
and
T^{\prime}
. This is the minimal element in the class of
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
that contains
T
and
T^{\prime}
.
By Corollary
2.11
, the intervals
[Q,T]
and
[Q,T^{\prime}]
also contained in this class,
and each covering relation inside one of these intervals corresponds to an
s
-congruence rotation by Proposition
2.8
.
Thus,
Q
is connected to both
T
and
T^{\prime}
by a sequence of
s
-congruence rotations, and so
T\equiv T^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Note that in the case of permutations (
s=(1,1,\dots)
), the equivalence relation
\equiv
is a slight deformation of the
sylvester congruence
defined in
[

, Definition 8]
. More precisely, it is the transitive closure of the relation
\dots b\dots ac\dots\equiv\dots b\dots ca\dots
with

. This small transformation corresponds to an

-congruence rotation in our setting.

We have almost all the ingredients in order to prove that the equivalence relation
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
(or equivalently
\equiv
) is a lattice congruence. We still need to prove that the induced
s
-Tamari classes have a unique maximal element, and that the projection
\pi_{\uparrow}
is order preserving.
We start by describing the maximal elements of the classes.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
s
be a composition (without zeros) and
T
be an
s
-decreasing tree such that if
\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)
then
\card_{T}(c,a)=s(c)
for all
c>b
. Then,
T
is maximal in its
s
-Tamari class and we call it an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree
.

In the case of permutations,
s
-maximal-Tamari trees correspond to
213
avoiding permutations. You can check on Figure
14
that the trees satisfying this condition are exactly the maximal elements of the classes.

Proof.

This is also a consequence of Proposition
2.8
. Suppose that we have an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree
T
with a tree ascent
(a,c)
. Then, by definition of a tree-ascent,
\card_{T}(c,a)<s(c)
. As
T
is an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree, this means that there is no
b
with
a<b<c
such that
\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)
, and so
(a,c)
is not an
s
-congruence rotation.
∎

Proposition 2.15
.

For an
s
-decreasing tree
T
, the multi inversion set defined by

\card_{R}(c,a)=\begin{cases}s(c),&\text{if there exists }b\text{ with }a<b<c\text{ and }\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)\\
\card_{T}(c,a)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for
c>a
, is an
s
-tree-inversion set.
Abusing of notation, we define
\pi_{\uparrow}(T)=R
to be the corresponding
s
-decreasing tree.
Then,
R
is an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree and
T\preccurlyeq R
in the
s
-weak order .

Proof.

First we show that the multi inversion set is an
s
-tree-inversion set as defined in Definition
1.5
.
Again, we need to verify the transitivity and planarity conditions.

Let us start with transitivity. Suppose that we have
a<b<c
with
\card_{R}(c,b)=i
and
\card_{R}(b,a)>0
. We need to show that
\card_{R}(c,a)\geq i
. We consider two possible cases.

Case 1: Assume that
\card_{T}(b,a)=0
. Then, there exists
b^{\prime}
with
a<b^{\prime}<b
and
\card_{T}(b^{\prime},a)=s(b^{\prime})
. By definition, this also implies that
\card_{R}(c,a)=s(c)\geq\card_{R}(c,b)=i
.

Case 2: Assume that
\card_{T}(b,a)>0
. If
\card_{T}(c,b)=i
, then transitivity in
T
would imply
\card_{T}(c,a)\geq i
, which itself implies
\card_{R}(c,a)\geq i
as desired.
On the other hand, if
\card_{T}(c,b)\neq i
, then there exists
b^{\prime}
with
b<b^{\prime}<c
and
\card_{T}(b^{\prime},b)=s(b^{\prime})
. As we have assumed
\card_{T}(b,a)>0
, this gives by transitivity that
\card_{T}(b^{\prime},a)=s(b^{\prime})
, and so
\card_{R}(c,a)=s(c)\geq i
as desired.

Now let us prove planarity. Let
a<b<c
with
\card_{R}(c,a)=i
.
We need to show that
\card_{R}(b,a)=s(b)
or
\card_{R}(c,b)\geq i
. We consider again two cases.

Case 1: If
\card_{T}(c,a)=i
, then by planarity in
T
, either
\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)
or
\card_{T}(c,b)\geq i
. Both properties remain true for
R
and so, planarity holds in this case.

Case 2: If
\card_{T}(c,a)\neq i
, then there exists
b^{\prime}
with
a<b^{\prime}<c
and
\card_{T}(b^{\prime},a)=s(b^{\prime})
(and
\card_{R}(c,a)=s(c)
).
If
b^{\prime}\leq b
, this implies
\card_{R}(b,a)=s(b)
.
If
b^{\prime}>b
, this implies by construction
\card_{R}(c,b)=s(c)
. In both cases, the planarity condition holds.

We have shown that the multi inversion set
R
satisfies the transitivity and planarity conditions, and therefore it is an
s
-tree inversion set. By Proposition
1.6
, it is the tree inversion set of an
s
-decreasing tree, which we also denote by
R
for simplicity.

By definition of
R
, it is straight forward to check that if
\card_{R}(b,a)=s(b)
then
\card_{R}(c,a)=s(c)
for all
c>b
. Therefore,
R
is an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree. The fact that
T\preccurlyeq R
is also clear from the definition, as we only increase cardinalitites.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
T
be an
s
-decreasing tree, then
\pi_{\uparrow}(\pi_{\uparrow}(T))=\pi_{\uparrow}(T)
. In other words,
\pi_{\uparrow}
is indeed a projection. In particular, if
T
is an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree,
\pi_{\uparrow}(T)=T
.

Proof.

We proved in Proposition
2.15
that the image of
\pi_{\uparrow}
is an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree.
Therefore, it suffices to show that
\pi_{\uparrow}(T)=T
when
T
is an
s
-maximal-Tamari tree.
This follows immediately from the definition.
∎

Like
\pi_{\downarrow}
,
\pi_{\uparrow}
defines a
\pi_{\uparrow}
-equivalence relation
given by
T\equiv_{\pi_{\uparrow}}T^{\prime}
if and only if
\pi_{\uparrow}(T)=\pi_{\uparrow}(T^{\prime})
.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
T\preccurlyeq T^{\prime}
be a cover relation of the
s
-weak order. Then
T\equiv_{\pi_{\uparrow}}T^{\prime}
if and only if
T^{\prime}
is obtained by applying an
s
-congruence rotation on
T
. Moreover, if the cover relation is not an
s
-congruence rotation, then
\pi_{\uparrow}(T)\prec\pi_{\uparrow}(T^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Let
T
be an
s
-decreasing tree with a tree-ascent
(a,c)
and
T^{\prime}
the result of the corresponding
s
-tree-rotation. We write
R=\pi_{\uparrow}(T)
and
R^{\prime}=\pi_{\uparrow}(T^{\prime})
.
As in the proof of Proposition
2.8
,
we denote by
A
the set containing the node
a
and all its middle descendants, and recall that the only increased cardinalities in
T^{\prime}
are those of
(c,a^{\prime})
where
a^{\prime}\in A
.

Assume that
(a,c)
is an
s
-congruence tree-ascent. This means that there exists
b
with
a<b<c
such that
\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)
. Therefore, for all
a^{\prime}\in A
, we also have
\card_{T}(b,a^{\prime})=s(b)
. This implies that for all
a^{\prime}\in A
, and all
c^{\prime}\geq c
, we have
\card_{R}(c^{\prime},a^{\prime})=s(c)
. This is also true in
T^{\prime}
, and all the other cardinalities stay unchanged, which gives
R^{\prime}=R
.

Now if
(a,c)
is not an

-congruence tree-ascent, there exists no

with
a<b<c
and
\card_{T}(b,a)=s(b)
. This gives
\card_{R}(c,a)=\card_{T}(c,a)
. For the same reason and because no
(b,a)
cardinality is modified by the rotation, we obtain
\card_{R^{\prime}}(c,a)=\card_{T^{\prime}}(c,a)=\card_{R}(c,a)+1
. For all other

, we have
\card_{R}(c^{\prime},a^{\prime})\leq\card_{R^{\prime}}(c^{\prime},a^{\prime})
because the cardinality can only increase. We then obtain that

.
∎

Corollary 2.18
.

Let
s
be a composition (without zeros), then:

(1)

\pi_{\uparrow}
is order preserving.

(2)

The classes of the
\equiv_{\pi_{\uparrow}}
equivalence are closed by interval.

(3)

The three equivalence relations
\equiv_{\pi_{\uparrow}}
,
\equiv
, and
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
are the same.

Proof.

The proofs of the three items in this corollary are exactly the same as the proofs of the three Corollaries

,
2.11
, and
2.12
, respectively, using Proposition
2.17
instead of Proposition
2.8
.
∎

.

Note that the proofs of Propositions

. Indeed, even when

is order preserving and

. Nevertheless, it is not the same as

Theorem 2.20
.

For
s
a composition (without zeros),
the equivalence relation
\equiv_{\pi_{\uparrow}}
(or equivalently any of
\equiv
or
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
) is a lattice congruence.
Moreover, the
s
-Tamari lattice is the quotient lattice of the
s
-weak order determined by
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
.

Proof.

We have defined the
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
equivalence as the fibers of the projection
\pi_{\downarrow}
onto
s
-Tamari trees. As
\pi_{\downarrow}(T)\preccurlyeq T
, the classes have a unique minimal element. Similarly, the classes of
\equiv_{\pi_{\uparrow}}
have a unique maximal element. By Corollaries
2.11
and
2.18
, they are the same classes and are closed by interval. So, the classes are intervals themselves. Moreover, Corollaries
2.10
and
2.18
prove that
\pi_{\downarrow}
and
\pi_{\uparrow}
are order preserving. As a consequence
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
is a lattice congruence as in Definition

.

The quotient lattice determined by
\equiv_{\pi_{\downarrow}}
is then isomorphic to the subposet of either the minimal class elements (the

-Tamari trees) or the maximal ones (the
s
-maximal-Tamari trees). The first is by definition the
s
-Tamari lattice.
∎

.

As a consequence, we get that the

. But note that Theorem

: the fact that

and, besides, Theorem

is true even when

Definition 2.22

Let

.

Note that

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
T
be an
s
-Tamari tree for some weak composition
s
, and
(a,c)
a Tamari-ascent of
T
.
Then
\left(\inv(T)+(c,a)\right)^{\mathsf{tc}}
is an

-Tamari inversion set. The inversions of its corresponding

-Tamari tree

are

(9)

\card_{T^{\prime}}(c^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\card_{T}(c^{\prime},a^{\prime})+1&\mbox{if }c^{\prime}=c\text{ and }a^{\prime}\text{ is equal to }a\text{ or a non-left descendant of }a,\\
\card_{T}(c^{\prime},a^{\prime})&\mbox{otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

In other words, a node moves to the right with its right children and middle children.

Proof.

Let
(c,a)
be a pair of nodes in
T
such that
a
is a non-right child of
c
, and denote
K=\inv(T)+(c,a)
for simplicity. The proof follows the same steps as in the proof of Lemma
1.29

is an

-Tamari tree.

Part 1

(

is a tree-inversion set)
.

By Lemma
1.16
and Lemma
1.19
, it suffices to show that
K
satisfies the planarity condition.
For this we need to show that for any

,
\card_{K}(z,x)=i
implies
\card_{K}(y,x)=s(y)
or
\card_{K}(z,y)\geq i
. We consider two possible cases.

If
(z,x)\neq(c,a)
then
\card_{T}(z,x)=\card_{K}(z,x)=i
, which implies
\card_{T}(y,x)=s(y)
or
\card_{T}(z,y)\geq i
. Whichever condition holds is also true for
K
.

Now consider the case
a<b<c
. Here we have
\card_{K}(c,a)=\card_{T}(c,a)+1=i
, which implies
\card_{T}(b,a)=\card_{K}(b,a)=s(b)
or
\card_{T}(c,b)=\card_{K}(c,b)\geq i-1
.
Note that
\card_{T}(c,b)
can not be equal to

, otherwise

would both belong to the same subtree

of
T
. But this contradicts the fact that
T
is a decreasing tree because
a
is a child of
c
and therefore the root of

by assumption.

Part 2

(Equation (
9
) defines

, the smallest transitive multi inversion set containing

)
.

We start by proving that the multi inversion set defined by Equation (
9
) is transitive.

Let
x<y<z
and assume that
\card_{T^{\prime}}(z,y)=i
. We need to show that

\card_{T^{\prime}}(y,x)=0\quad\text{or}\quad\card_{T^{\prime}}(z,x)\geq i.

Case 1

\mathbf{(z,y)=(c,a^{\prime})}
for some

a non-left descendant of

in

.

In this case
\card_{T^{\prime}}(z,y)=\card_{T}(z,y)+1=i

\card_{T}(y,x)=0

by transitivity of

.
If
\card_{T}(y,x)=0
then
\card_{T^{\prime}}(y,x)=0

and we are done.
If
\card_{T}(z,x)>i-1
