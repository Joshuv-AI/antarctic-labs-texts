Accelerated Gradient and Skew-Symmetric Splitting Methods for a Class of Monotone Operator Equations Thanks: L. Chen and J. Wei are supported by National Science Foundation DMS-2012465 and DMS-2309785.
Long Chen, Jingrong Wei

Abstract
. A class of monotone operator equations, which can be decomposed into sum of the gradient of a strongly convex function and a linear and skew-symmetric operator, is considered in this work. Based on discretization of the generalized gradient flow, gradient and skew-symmetric splitting (GSS) methods are proposed and proved to converge in linear rates. To further accelerate the convergence, an accelerated gradient flow is proposed and accelerated gradient and skew-symmetric splitting (AGSS) methods are developed, which extends the acceleration among the existing works on the convex minimization to a more general class of monotone operator equations. In particular, when applied to smooth saddle point systems with bilinear coupling, a linear convergent method with optimal lower iteration complexity is proposed. The robustness and efficiency of GSS and AGSS methods are verified via extensive numerical experiments.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider iterative methods for solving a class of monotone operator equations

(1.1)

\mathcal{A}(x)=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\mathcal{A}
is strongly monotone: there exists
\mu>0
, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.2)

(\mathcal{A}(x)-\mathcal{A}(y),x-y)\geq\mu\|x-y\|^{2}.

We further assume
\mathcal{A}(x)
can be decomposed into

(1.3)

\mathcal{A}(x)=\nabla F(x)+\mathcal{N}x,

with a strongly convex
C^{1}
function
F
, and a linear and skew-symmetric operator
\mathcal{N}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}^{\intercal}=-\mathcal{N}
. Then
\mathcal{A}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{\mathcal{A}}\leq L_{F}+\|\mathcal{N}\|
, where
L_{F}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla F
. Therefore
\mathcal{A}
is monotone and Lipschitz continuous which is also known as inverse-strongly monotonicity
[
5
,
26
]
or co-coercitivity
[
40
]
.
Consequently (
1.1
) has a unique solution
x^{\star}

[
33
]
.

1.1.
Contribution

The goal of this paper is to design iterative methods for computing the solution
x^{\star}
of (
1.1
) based on the splitting (
1.3
), which will be called Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting (GSS) methods and its accelerated version Accelerated Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting (AGSS) method. Our contribution is summarized as below.

•

Acceleration techniques in optimization have traditionally been limited to convex optimization problems
[
31
,
32
]
. This paper significantly broadens the scope of acceleration beyond its conventional boundaries by extending to a more general class of monotone operator equations.

•

We leverage ordinary differential equation (ODE) models as a foundation for crafting discrete optimization schemes. Our framework encompasses various numerical methods tailored for solving (
1.1
). Notably, in comparison to the traditional proximal point methods, our novel GSS method and AGSS method offer explicit and practical schemes.

•

For solving non-Hermitian positive definite linear systems
\mathcal{A}x=b
, our new AGSS method achieves the same accelerated rate as Hermitian/skew-Hermitian splitting method (HSS)
[
2
]
without treating the symmetric part implicitly, and more importantly can handle non-linear problems.

•

When applied to smooth strongly-convex-strongly-concave saddle point systems with bilinear coupling, our new AGSS methods are few first-order algorithms achieving optimal lower iteration complexity.

The robustness and efficiency of GSS and AGSS methods are verified via extensive numerical experiments: quadratic problems, convection-diffusion partial differential equations, and empirical risk minimization.

1.2.
Gradient and Skew-symmetric Splitting Methods

We shall develop our iterative methods based on discretization of ODE flows. Namely we treat
x(t)
as a continuous function of an artificial time variable
t
and design ODE systems
x^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{G}(x(t))
so that the
x^{\star}
is a stable equilibrium point of the corresponding dynamic system, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}(x^{*})=0
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)=x^{*}
. Then we apply ODE solvers to obtain various iterative methods. By doing this way, we can borrow the stability analysis of dynamic systems and convergence theory of ODE solvers.

The most popular choice is the generalized gradient flow:

(1.4)

x^{\prime}(t)=-\mathcal{A}(x(t))=-\nabla F(x(t))-\mathcal{N}(x(t)).

One can easily show the exponential stability of
x^{\star}
using the monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
. Although
\mathcal{A}
is not the gradient of some scalar function when
\mathcal{N}\neq 0
, we still use the name convention for the convex case
\mathcal{N}=0
.

The computation complexity is measured by the iteration step to reach an

We briefly go through methods in Table

. The implicit Euler scheme with step size
\alpha_{k}
is equivalent to the proximal method

(1.5)

(I+\alpha_{k}\mathcal{A})(x_{k+1})=x_{k},

which is unconditionally stable for any
\alpha_{k}>0
. For increasing step size
\alpha_{k}
, the implicit Euler scheme can even achieve super-linear convergence. The explicit Euler scheme gives the generalized gradient descent method:

(1.6)

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{A}(x_{k}).

For
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\mu/L_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}
, (
1.6
) achieves the linear rate

\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(1-1/\kappa^{2}(\mathcal{A})\right)\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

But this linear rate is pretty slow when
\kappa(\mathcal{A})\gg 1
since the dependence is
\kappa^{2}(\mathcal{A})
.
In contrast, for convex optimization, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}=0
, the rate for the gradient descent method is
1-c/\kappa(F)
for step size
\alpha_{k}=1/L_{F}
and
1-c/\sqrt{\kappa(F)}
for accelerated gradient methods
[
30
]
.

Based on the splitting
\mathcal{N}=B^{\sym}-2B
or
\mathcal{N}=2B^{\intercal}-B^{\sym}
, we propose a new method called Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting (GSS) method:

(1.7)

\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}=-\left(\nabla F(x_{k})+B^{\sym}x_{k}-2Bx_{k+1}\right),

Notice that
B
is lower triangular,
x_{k+1}
in (
1.7
) can be computed via forward substitution. We prove that GSS method (
1.7
) achieves the linear convergence rate, for
\alpha=\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{1}{4\|B^{\sym}\|},\frac{1}{4L_{F}}\right\}
,

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+4/\max\{\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym}),\kappa(F)\}}\right)^{k}6\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

In summary, in GSS, AOR is used to accelerate the skew-symmetric part and improve the dependence of condition number from
\kappa^{2}
to
\kappa
. The convergence rate is validated through a numerical example on quadratic problems in Section
6.1
.

[
2
]

We also allow an inexact solver for approximating
(\beta I+\mathcal{N})^{-1}
using a perturbation argument to control the accumulation of the inner solve error. Formal results are presented in Section
4.4
. In practice, we showcase the efficiency of the IMEX scheme through computations on a convection-diffusion model in Section
6.2
.

To fully avoid computing
(\beta I+\mathcal{N})^{-1}
, we provide an explicit AGSS scheme combining acceleration of
F
and AOR technique for
\mathcal{N}
:

(1.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{x}_{k+1},

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\mu^{-1}\left(\nabla F(\hat{x}_{k+1})+B^{\sym}y_{k}-2By_{k+1}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{2}(x_{k+1}+\hat{x}_{k+1}).

For the step size
\displaystyle\alpha=\min\left\{\frac{\mu}{2L_{B^{\sym}}},\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{2L}}\right\}

1.10

\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+1/\max\{4\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym}),\sqrt{8\kappa(F)}\}}\right)^{k}\frac{2\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\alpha B}}{\mu}

where
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{0}=D_{F}(x_{0},x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mu I-2\alpha B^{\sym}}
is nonnegative according to our choice of the step size.

In summary, AGSS based on the accelerated gradient flow (
1.8

\kappa(F)
to
\sqrt{\kappa(F)}

1.4.

(1.11)

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}(u,p):=f(u)-g(p)+(Bu,p),

f,g

\nabla\mathcal{L}(u,p)=0

1.5.
Related Work

More specific class of monotone operator equation is particularly of interest, since it is closely related to convex optimization and saddle point problems. For instance, when
\mathcal{N}=0
, (
1.1
) becomes the convex minimization problem

(1.16)

\min_{x}F(x).

We refer to the book
[
30
]
on the first-order methods for solving (
1.16
) especially the accelerated gradient method which achieved the linear rate
1-1/\sqrt{\kappa(F)}
with optimal lower complexity bound. Most acceleration methods are restricted to convex optimization and extension to general monotone operators is rare. We significantly broaden the scope of acceleration.

Another important application is the strongly-convex-strongly-concave saddle point problem with bilinear coupling (
1.11
).
In
[
39
]
, it is shown that if the duality gap
\Delta(u,p):=\max_{q}\mathcal{L}(u,q)-\min_{v}\mathcal{L}(v,p)

is required to be bounded by
\epsilon
, the number of iterations for the first-order methods is at least

(1.17)

\Omega\left(\sqrt{\kappa(f)+\tilde{\kappa}^{2}(\mathcal{N})+\kappa(g)}|\ln\epsilon|\right)

where
\kappa(f),\kappa(g)
are condition numbers of
f,g
respectively,
\tilde{\kappa}(\mathcal{N})=\|B\|/\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}}
measuring the coupling, and
\Omega
means the iteration complexity bounded below asymptotically, up to a constant. Only few optimal first-order algorithms have been developed recently
[
28
,
34
]
. If further the proximal oracles for
f,g
are allowed, the lower complexity bound of the class of first-order algorithms is
\displaystyle\Omega\left(\tilde{\kappa}(\mathcal{N})|\ln\epsilon|\right)
and proximal methods achieving this lower bound can be found in
[
6
,
7
]
. Our new method AGSS applied to the saddle point problems becomes first-order algorithms achieving optimal lower iteration complexity and achieve robust performance; see Section
6.3
for the comparison with other optimal complexity methods
[
23
,
25
,
29
,
34
]
.

When
\mu_{g}=0
, transformed primal-dual (TPD) methods
[
12
,
10
]
can be combined with the accelerated gradient flow. Here, no transformation is included in the AGSS algorithms.

One more important class is for linear operator
\mathcal{A}
which has the following decomposition:

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{\rm s}+\mathcal{N},

where
\mathcal{A}^{\rm s}=(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}^{\intercal})/2
is the symmetric (Hermitian for complex matrices) part and
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{A}^{\intercal})/2
is the skew-symmetric part. The condition
\mathcal{A}
is monotone is equivalent to
\mathcal{A}^{\rm s}
is symmetric and positive definite (SPD) and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})\geq\mu
. Bai, Golub, and Ng in
[
2
]
proposed the Hermitian/skew-Hermitian splitting method (HSS) for solving general non-Hermitian positive definite linear systems
\mathcal{A}x=b
:

(1.18)

\displaystyle(\alpha I+\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})x_{k+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=(\alpha I-\mathcal{N})x_{k}+b

\displaystyle(\alpha I+\mathcal{N})x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\alpha I-\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})x_{k+\frac{1}{2}}+b.

The iterative method (
1.18
) solves the equations for the symmetric (Hermitian) part and skew-symmetric (skew-Hermitian) part alternatively. For the HSS method (
1.18
), efficient solvers for linear operators
(\alpha I+\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})^{-1}
and
(\alpha I+\mathcal{N})^{-1}
are needed. A linear convergence rate of
\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})}+1}
can be achieved for an optimal choice of parameter
\alpha
. Several variants of HSS are derived and analyzed in
[
1
,
3
]
.

For linear systems, comparing with HSS, AGSS achieves the same accelerated rate without treating the symmetric part implicitly, i.e., no need to compute
(\alpha I+\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})^{-1}
, and consequently significantly improve the efficiency. More importantly, AGSS can handle non-linear problems while HSS is restricted to linear algebraic systems only.

For the rest of the paper, we first review convex function theory and Lyapunov analysis theory in Section
2
, as basic preliminaries for our proofs. The generalized gradient flow and convergence of GSS methods for the considered monotone equation are proposed in Section
3
. Then in Section
4
, we extend the preconditioned accelerated gradient flow in convex minimization and derive AGSS methods with accelerated linear rates. As applications, we give optimal algorithms for strongly-convex-strongly-concave saddle point system with bilinear coupling in Section
5
. In Section
6
, we validate the algorithms with numerical examples. Concluding remarks are addressed in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminary

In this section, we follow
[
30
]
to introduce notation and preliminaries in convex function theory. We also briefly review a unified convergence analysis of first order convex optimization methods via strong Lyapunov functions established in
[
11
]
.

2.1.
Convex Functions

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Hilbert space with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
and norm
\|\cdot\|
.
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is the linear space of all linear and continuous mappings, which is called the dual space of
\mathcal{X}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the duality pair between
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{X}
. For any proper closed convex and
C^{1}
function
F:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the Bregman divergence of
F
is defined as

D_{F}(x,y):=F(x)-F(y)-\langle\nabla F(y),x-y\rangle.

We say
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
or
\mu
-strongly convex with
\mu>0
if
F
is differentiable and

D_{F}(x,y)\geqslant\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

In addition, denote by
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
if
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
and there exists
L>0
such that

D_{F}(x,y)\leqslant\frac{L}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

For fixed
y\in\mathcal{X},D_{F}(\cdot,y)
is convex as
F
is convex and

\nabla D_{F}(\cdot,y)=\nabla F(\cdot)-\nabla F(y).

In general
D_{F}(x,y)
is non-symmetric in terms of
x
and
y
.
A symmetrized Bregman divergence is defined as

\langle\nabla F(y)-\nabla F(x),y-x\rangle=D_{F}(y,x)+D_{F}(x,y).

By direct calculation, we have the following three-terms identity.

Lemma 2.1

(Bregman divergence identity
[
8
]
)
.

If function
F:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is differentiable, then for any
x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that

(2.1)

\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(y),y-z\rangle=D_{F}(z,x)-D_{F}(z,y)-D_{F}(y,x).

When
F(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, identity (
2.1
) becomes

(x-y,y-z)=\frac{1}{2}\|z-x\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|z-y\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

For a non-smooth function
F
, the subgradient is a set-valued function defined as

\partial F(x):=\left\{\xi\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}:F(y)-F(x)\geqslant\langle\xi,y-x\rangle,\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

For a proper, closed and convex function
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\partial F(x)
is a nonempty bounded set for
x\in\mathcal{X}

[
30
]
. It is evident that the subgradient for smooth
F
is a single-valued function reduced to the gradient, that is
\partial F(x)=\{\nabla F(x)\}
. We extend the set
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
using the notion of subgradient:
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
if for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,

F(y)\geqslant F(x)+\langle\xi,y-x\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall\xi\in\partial F(x).

Given a convex function
F
, define the proximal operator of
F
as

(2.2)

\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma f,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(y):=\underset{x}{\operatorname{argmin}}~F(x)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x-y\|^{2},

for some
\gamma>0
. The proximal operator is well-defined since the function
F(\cdot)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\cdot-y\|^{2}
is
1/\gamma
-strongly convex.

2.2.
Inner Products and Preconditioners

The standard
l^{2}
dot product of Euclidean space
(\cdot,\cdot)
is usually chosen as the inner product and the norm induced is the Euclidean norm. We now introduce inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}
induced by a given SPD operator
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
defined as follows

(x,y)_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}:=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}x,y)=(x,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}y),\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}

and associated norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}
, given by

\|x\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}=(x,x)^{1/2}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}.

The dual norm w.r.t the
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
-norm is defined as: for
\ell\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}

\|\ell\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}=\sup_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\langle\ell,x\rangle}{\|x\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}}=\left(\ell,\ell\right)_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}}^{1/2}=\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}\ell,\ell\right)^{1/2}.

We shall generalize the convexity and Lipschitz continuity with respect to
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
-norm: we say
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}}
with
\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}\geqslant 0
if
F
is differentiable and

D_{F}(x,y)\geqslant\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

In addition, denote
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}},L_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}}
if
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}}
and there exists
L_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}>0
such that

D_{F}(x,y)\leq\frac{L_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

The gradient method in the inner product
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
reads as:

(2.3)

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}\nabla F(x_{k}),

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}
can be understood as a preconditioner. The convergence rate of the preconditioned gradient descent iteration (
2.3
) is
1-1/\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(F)
with condition number
\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(F)=L_{F,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}/\mu_{F,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}
.
To simplify notation, we skip
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
in the constants
\mu
and
L
, e.g., write
\mu_{F,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}
as
\mu_{F}
, but keep in the condition number, e.g.
\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(F)
, to emphasize that the condition number is measured in the
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
inner product.

For two symmetric operators
A,B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, we say
A\geq(>)B
if
A-B
is positive semidefinite (definite). Therefore
A>0
means
A
is SPD. One can easily verify that
A\geq B
is equivalent to
\lambda_{\min}(B^{-1}A)\geq 1
or
\lambda_{\max}(A^{-1}B)\leq 1
.

For a general symmetric matrix
A
, we define

\|x\|_{A}^{2}:=(x,x)_{A}:=x^{\intercal}Ax.

In general,
\|\cdot\|_{A}
may not be a norm if
A
is not SPD. However the identity for squares still holds:

(2.4)

2(a,b)_{A}=\|a\|_{A}^{2}+\|b\|_{A}^{2}-\|a-b\|_{A}^{2}.

2.3.
Lyapunov Analysis

In order to study the stability of an equilibrium
x^{*}
of a dynamical system defined by an autonomous system

(2.5)

x^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}(x(t)),

Lyapunov introduced the so-called Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(x)

[
22
,
14
]
, which is nonnegative and the equilibrium point
x^{*}
satisfies
\mathcal{E}\left(x^{*}\right)=0
and the Lyapunov condition:

-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x)\cdot\mathcal{G}(x)>0
for
x
near the equilibrium point
x^{*}
.
Then the (local) decay property of
\mathcal{E}(x)
along the trajectory
x(t)
of the autonomous system (
2.5
) can be derived immediately:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(x(t))=\nabla\mathcal{E}(x)\cdot x^{\prime}(t)=\nabla\mathcal{E}(x)\cdot\mathcal{G}(x)<0.

To further establish the convergence rate of
\mathcal{E}(x(t))
, Chen and Luo
[
11
]
introduced the strong Lyapunov condition:
\mathcal{E}(x)
is a Lyapunov function and there exist constant
q\geqslant 1
, strictly positive function
c(x)
and function
p(x)
such that

-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x)\cdot\mathcal{G}(x)\geq c(x)\mathcal{E}^{q}(x)+p^{2}(x)

holds true near
x^{*}
. From this, one can derive the exponential decay
\mathcal{E}(x(t))=O\left(e^{-ct}\right)
for
q=1
and the algebraic decay
\mathcal{E}(x(t))=O\left(t^{-1/(q-1)}\right)
for
q>1
. Furthermore if
\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq C\mathcal{E}(x)
, then we can derive the exponential stability of
x^{*}
from the exponential decay of Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(x)
.

Note that for an optimization problem, we have freedom to design the flow, i.e., the vector field
\mathcal{G}(x)
, and Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(x)
.

3.
Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting Methods

In this section, we consider the generalized gradient flow

(3.1)

x^{\prime}(t)=-\mathcal{A}(x(t)),

and derive several iterative methods for solving
\mathcal{A}(x)=0
by applying ODE solvers to (
3.1
). Based on the gradient and skew-symmetric splitting (
1.3
), we apply Accelerated OverRelaxation (AOR)
[
16
,
15
]
technique to the non-symmetric part to obtain explicit schemes with linear convergence rate
(1+c/\kappa(\mathcal{A}))^{-1}
.

3.1.
Stability

Define the quadratic Lyapunov function:

(3.2)

\mathcal{E}_{q}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

For
x(t)
solving the generalized gradient flow (
3.1
):

\displaystyle\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\mathcal{E}_{q}(x(t))

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}_{q}(x),x^{\prime}\rangle=-\langle x-x^{\star},\mathcal{A}(x)-\mathcal{A}(x^{\star})\rangle\leq-2\mu\mathcal{E}_{q}(x),

which leads to the exponential decay

\mathcal{E}_{q}(x(t))\leq e^{-2\mu t}\mathcal{E}_{q}(x(0))

and consequently the solution
x^{*}
is an exponentially stable equilibrium point of the dynamic system defined by (
3.1
).

3.2.
Implicit Euler Schemes

The implicit Euler scheme for the generalized gradient flow (
3.1
) with step size
\alpha_{k}>0
is

(3.3)

x_{k+1}-x_{k}=-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1}),

which is equivalent to the proximal iteration

(3.4)

(I+\alpha_{k}\mathcal{A})(x_{k+1})=x_{k}.

As
\mathcal{A}
is strongly monotone and Liptschitz continuous, the operator
(I+\alpha_{k}\mathcal{A})^{-1}
, which is called the resolvent of
\mathcal{A}
, is nonexpansive
[
5
]
and has a unique fixed point
[
33
]
.

Using the identity of squares and the strong monotonicity, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k})={}

\displaystyle(x_{k+1}-x_{k},x_{k+1}-x^{\star})-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k}\left\langle{\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{A}(x^{\star}),x_{k+1}-x^{\star}}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle-2\alpha_{k}\mu\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k+1}),

from which the linear convergence follows naturally

\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{1+2\alpha_{k}\mu}\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

There is no restriction on the step size
\alpha_{k}
which is known as the unconditional stability of the implicit Euler method. Choosing
\alpha_{k}\gg 1
will accelerate the convergence with the price of solving a regularized nonlinear problem (
3.4
), which is in general not practical. The implicit Euler method is presented here as a reference to measure the difference of other and more practical methods.

3.3.
Explicit Euler Schemes

The explicit Euler scheme for the generalized gradient flow (
3.1
) leads to the gradient decent method:

(3.6)

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{A}(x_{k}).

We write (
3.6
) as a correction of the implicit Euler scheme (
3.3
):

x_{k+1}-x_{k}=-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1})+\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{A}(x_{k})),

and follow (
3.5
) to obtain

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k})=

\displaystyle(x_{k+1}-x_{k},x_{k+1}-x^{\star})-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-2\alpha_{k}\mu\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k+1})-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k}\left\langle{\mathcal{A}(x_{k})-\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-x^{\star}}\right\rangle.

Here we use the implicit Euler scheme as the reference scheme and call the term
\alpha_{k}(\mathcal{A}(x_{k})-\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-x^{\star})
in (
3.7
) a mis-matching term. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the Lipschitz continuity of
\mathcal{A}
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}|\left\langle{\mathcal{A}(x_{k})-\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-x^{\star}}\right\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2\mu}\|\mathcal{A}(x_{k})-\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1})\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{k}\mu}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{k}L_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}}{2\mu}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}\mu\,\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k+1})

Substitute back to (
3.7
) and let

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\mu/L_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}
, we obtain

\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{1+1/\kappa^{2}(\mathcal{A})}\mathcal{E}_{q}(x_{k}).

This linear rate is pretty slow when
\kappa(\mathcal{A})\gg 1
. We will discuss techniques to improve the dependence
\kappa^{2}(\mathcal{A})
.

3.4.
Accelerated Overrelaxation Methods for Shifted skew-Symmetric Problems

In this subsection, we consider the shifted skew-symmetric linear system

(3.9)

(\mu I+\mathcal{N})x=b,

which is a special case of (
1.1
) with
F(x)=\frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^{2}-(b,x)
and
\mathcal{A}(x)=(\mu I+\mathcal{N})x-b
. An efficient solver for (
3.9
) is an ingredient of AGSS methods for the nonlinear equation (
1.1
) we shall develop later on.

The system (
3.9
) can be solved with Krylov subspace methods. For example, minimal residual methods
[
18
,
20
]
based on the Lanczos algorithm require short recurrences of vector storage. The convergence theorem using Chebyshev polynomial
[
20
]
shows that the residual converges with a linear rate of
O((1+\mu/\|\mathcal{N}\|)^{-1})
. Compared with Krylov subspace methods for solving (
3.9
), our method enjoys a similar linear convergence rate but with a much simpler form and in-space vector storage.

Recall that, for the matrix equation

(D-L-U)x=b,

where
D
a diagonal matrix, and
L
and
U
are strictly lower and upper triangular matrix, respectively, the Accelerated OverRelaxation (AOR) method
[
16
,
15
]
with the acceleration parameter
r
and relaxation factor
\omega>1
is in the form

(D-rL)x_{k+1}=[(1-\omega)D+(\omega-r)L+\omega U]x_{k}+\omega b,

which is a two-parameter generalization of the well known successive overrelaxation (SOR) method
[
38
]
. We shall apply AOR to (
3.9
) with well-designed
r
and
\omega
.

As
\mathcal{N}=-\mathcal{N}^{\intercal}
, all diagonal entries of
\mathcal{N}
are zero. Let
B=-{\rm lower}(\mathcal{N})
be the lower triangular part of
\mathcal{N}
. Then
\mathcal{N}=B^{\intercal}-B
. Let
B^{\sym}=B+B^{\intercal}
be a symmetrization of
B
. We have the splitting

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=B^{\sym}-2B,\quad\text{and}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=2B^{\intercal}-B^{\sym}.

As a symmetric matrix, all eigenvalues of
B^{\sym}
are real. Let
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
B^{\sym}
. Since
{\rm trace}(B^{\sym})=0
, we know
\lambda_{1}<0
and
\lambda_{n}>0
. That is
B^{\sym}
is symmetric but indefinite. Define
L_{B^{\sym}}=\|B^{\sym}\|=\max\{|\lambda_{1}|,\lambda_{n}\}
and
\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym})=L_{B^{\sym}}/\mu
.

We discretize the gradient flow (
3.1
) by the matrix splitting (
3.10
) and treat
B^{\sym}
as the explicit term:

(3.12)

\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}=-\left(\mu x_{k+1}+B^{\sym}x_{k}-2Bx_{k+1}-b\right).

The update of
x_{k+1}
is equivalent to solve

(3.13)

\left[(1+\alpha\mu)I-2\alpha B\right]x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha(B^{\sym}x_{k}-b),

which is AOR for solving

\displaystyle(\mu I-B+B^{\intercal})x=b

with
r=\frac{2\alpha\mu}{1+\alpha\mu}
and
\omega=\frac{\alpha\mu}{1+\alpha\mu}
. The left hand side of (
3.13
) is an invertible lower-triangular matrix and
x_{k+1}
can be efficiently computed by a forward substitution.
We can also use the splitting (
3.11
) to get another AOR scheme

(3.14)

\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}=-\left(\mu x_{k+1}-B^{\sym}x_{k}+2B^{\intercal}x_{k+1}-b\right),

for which
x_{k+1}
can be efficiently computed by a backward substitution.

Without loss of generality, we focus on the convergence analysis of scheme (
3.12
). Notice that when
0<\alpha<1/L_{B^{\sym}}
,
I\pm\alpha B^{\sym}
is SPD. For
0<\alpha<1/L_{B^{\sym}}
, consider the Lyapunov function

(3.15)

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{B^{\sym}}=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{I-\alpha B^{\sym}},

which is nonnegative and
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x)=0
if and only if
x=x^{\star}
. Noted that
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}
might be negative without control on the step size
\alpha
as
B^{\sym}
is indefinite.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the sequence generated by (
3.12
) with arbitrary initial guess
x_{0}
and step size
0<\alpha<1/L_{B^{\sym}}
. Then for the Lyapunov function (
3.15
), we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha\mu}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k}).

In particular, for
\alpha=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L_{B^{\sym}}}
, we have

(3.17)

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+1/\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym})}\right)^{k}3\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Proof.

We use the identity for squares (
2.4
):

(3.18)

\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}=(x_{k+1}-x^{\star},x_{k+1}-x_{k})-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

Write GSS scheme (
3.12
) as a correction of the implicit Euler scheme

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-x_{k}

\displaystyle=-\alpha(\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{A}(x^{\star}))+\alpha B^{\sym}(x_{k+1}-x_{k}).

For the first term, we have

-\alpha\langle x_{k+1}-x^{\star},\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{A}(x^{\star})\rangle=-\alpha\mu\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

We use the identity (
2.4
) to expand the cross term as

\displaystyle(x_{k+1}-x^{\star},x_{k+1}-x_{k})_{\alpha B^{\sym}}={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|_{\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|_{\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}.

Substitute back to (
3.18
) and rearrange the terms, we obtain the identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\alpha\mu\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{I-\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\alpha\mu\,\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k+1})-\frac{\alpha\mu}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|_{I+\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{I-\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}.

As
0<\alpha<\displaystyle 1/L_{B^{\sym}}
,
I\pm\alpha B^{\sym}
is SPD and the last two terms are non-positive. Dropping them, we obtain the inequality

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k})\leq-\alpha\mu\,\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k+1})

and (
3.16
) follows by arrangement.

When
\alpha=1/(2L_{B^{\sym}})
, we have the bound

(3.19)

\frac{1}{4}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x)\leq\frac{3}{4}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}

which implies (
3.17
).
∎

For SOR type iterative methods, usually spectral analysis
[
3
,
4
,
15
]
is applied to the error matrix which is in general non-symmetric and harder to estimate. The Lyapunov analysis provides a new and relatively simple approach and more importantly enables us to study nonlinear systems.

3.5.
Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting Methods for Nonlinear Problems

For non-linear equation (
1.1
), we treat
\nabla F
explicitly and propose the following Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting (GSS) scheme

(3.20)

\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}=-\left(\nabla F(x_{k})+B^{\sym}x_{k}-2Bx_{k+1}\right).

Similarly, modification on (
3.14
) gives another GSS scheme

(3.21)

\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}=-\left(\nabla F(x_{k})-B^{\sym}x_{k}+2B^{\intercal}x_{k+1}\right).

Both schemes are explicit as
\nabla F(x_{k})
is known and
x_{k+1}
can be computed by solving a triangular matrix equation.

We focus on the GSS method (
3.20
). The proof for (
3.21
) follows in line with a sign change in the following Lyapunov function

(3.22)

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k}):=\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{I-\alpha B^{\sym}}-\alpha D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k}).

Lemma 3.2
.

For
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L_{F}}
and
\alpha<1/\max\{2L_{B^{\sym}},2L_{F}\}
, the Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x)\geq 0
and
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x)=0
if and only if
x=x^{\star}
.

Proof.

Since
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
, the Bregman divergence is non-negative and

D_{F}(x^{\star},x)\leq\frac{L_{F}}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Then for
\alpha<1/\max\{2L_{B^{\sym}},2L_{F}\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|_{B^{\sym}}-\frac{\alpha L_{F}}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4L_{B^{\sym}}}\|x-x^{\star}\|_{B^{\sym}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}\geq 0.

If
x\neq x^{\star}
, then the second
\geq
becomes
>
. So
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x)=0
if and only if
x=x^{\star}
.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the sequence generated by the AOR method (
3.20
) with arbitrary initial guess
x_{0}
and step size
\alpha<1/\max\{2L_{B^{\sym}},2L_{F}\}
.
Then for the discrete Lyapunov function (
3.22
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha\mu}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k}).

In particular, for
\alpha=\displaystyle 1/(4\max\left\{L_{B^{\sym}},L_{F}\right\})
, we have

(3.23)

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(1+1/\max\left\{4\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym}),4\kappa(F)\right\}\right)^{-k}6\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Proof.

We write the scheme (
3.20
) as a correction of the implicit Euler scheme

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-x_{k}=

\displaystyle-\alpha(\mathcal{A}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{A}(x^{\star}))+\alpha B^{\sym}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})

\displaystyle+\alpha(\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})).

The first two terms are treated as before; see the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
The last cross term is expanded using the identity (
2.1
) for the Bregman divergence:

\displaystyle\langle x_{k+1}-x^{\star},\nabla F(x_{x+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle=D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k+1})+D_{F}(x_{k+1},x_{k})-D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k}).

Substituting back to (
3.18
) and rearranging the terms, we obtain the identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k})=

\displaystyle~-\alpha\mu\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k+1})-\frac{\alpha\mu}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|_{I+\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\mu D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k+1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{I-\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}+\alpha D_{F}(x_{k+1},x_{k}).

When
\alpha<1/\max\{2L_{B^{\sym}},2L_{F}\}
,
I\pm\alpha B^{\sym}
is SPD and

\displaystyle\alpha D_{F}(x_{k+1},x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|_{I-\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}.

Thus the last two terms are non-positive. Dropping all non-positive terms, we obtain the inequality

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k})\leq-\alpha\mu\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k+1})

and the linear reduction follows by arrangement.

When
\alpha=\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{1}{4L_{B^{\sym}}},\frac{1}{4L}\right\}
, we have the bound

(3.24)

\frac{1}{8}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x)\leq\frac{3}{4}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}

which implies (
3.23
).
∎

Notice the rate in Theorem
3.3
is
(1+c/\kappa)
for
\kappa=\max\{\kappa(F),\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym})\}
which matches the rate of the gradient descent method for convex optimization problems. We expect a combination of the accelerated gradient flow and AOR for the skew-symmetric part will give an accelerated explicit scheme.

3.6.
Implicit-Explicit Euler Schemes

If we treated the skew-symmetric part implicitly, we have the implicit-explicit scheme:

(3.25)

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla F(x_{k})-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{N}(x_{k+1}).

Define the Lyapunov function

(3.26)

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha D}(x_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|-\alpha D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k}).

Following Lemma
3.2
,
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha D}\geq 0
if
\alpha<1/L_{F}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha D}=0
iff
x_{k}=x^{\star}
.

Combining the proof of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
, we obtain the following linear convergence.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the sequence generated by the scheme (
3.25
) with arbitrary initial guess
x_{0}
and step size
\alpha<1/L_{F}
.
Then for the discrete Lyapunov function (
3.26
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha D}(x_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha\mu}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha D}(x_{k}).

In particular, for
\alpha=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L_{F}}
, we have

(3.27)

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(1+1/(2\kappa(F)\right)^{-k}2\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

With the explicit part as the gradient of a convex function, we can accommodate acceleration techniques
[
27
]
for convex optimization to achieve accelerated rates; see the proposed AGSS scheme in Section
4.3
.

4.
Accelerated Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting Methods

In this section, we shall develop the accelerated gradient flow and propose Accelerated Gradient and Skew-symmetric Splitting (AGSS) methods with accelerated linear rates. For the implicit-explicit scheme, we can relax to inexact inner solvers with computable error tolerance.

4.1.
Accelerated Gradient Flow

We introduce an auxiliary variable
y
and an accelerated gradient flow

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y-x,\\
y^{\prime}&=x-y-\mu^{-1}(\nabla F(x)+\mathcal{N}y).\end{aligned}\right.

Comparing with the accelerated flow in
[
27
]
for convex optimization, the difference is to split
\mathcal{A}(x)\rightarrow\nabla F(x)+\mathcal{N}y
. Denote the vector field on the right hand side of (
4.1
) by
\mathcal{G}(x,y)
. Then
\mathcal{G}(x^{\star},x^{\star})=0
and thus
(x^{\star},x^{\star})
is an equilibrium point of (
4.1
).

We first show
(x^{\star},x^{\star})
is exponentially stable. Consider the Lyapunov function:

(4.2)

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

For
\mu
-strongly convex
F
, function
D_{F}(\cdot,x^{\star})\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
. Then
\mathcal{E}(x,y)\geq 0
and
\mathcal{E}(x,y)=0
iff
x=y=x^{\star}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
, we have

(4.3)

\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\rangle\geq D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2},

Proof.

By direct computation,
\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\rangle=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+D_{F}(x^{\star},x)
and thus (
4.3
) follows from the bound

\min\{D_{F}(x,x^{\star}),D_{F}(x^{\star},x)\}\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2},

for a convex function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
.
∎

We then verify the strong Lyapunov property.

Lemma 4.2

(Strong Lyapunov Property)
.

Assume function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
. Then for the Lyapunov function (
4.2
) and the accelerated gradient flow vector field
\mathcal{G}
, the following strong Lyapunov property holds

(4.4)

-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y)\cdot\mathcal{G}(x,y)\geq\mathcal{E}(x,y)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

Proof.

First of all, as
\mathcal{G}(x^{\star},x^{\star})=0
,

-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y)\cdot\mathcal{G}(x,y)=-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y)\cdot(\mathcal{G}(x,y)-\mathcal{G}(x^{\star},x^{\star})).

Direct computation gives

\displaystyle-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y)\cdot\mathcal{G}(x,y)=

\displaystyle~\langle\nabla D_{F}(x;x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}-(y-x^{\star})\rangle-\mu(y-x^{\star},x-x^{\star})

\displaystyle+\mu\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle+(y-x^{\star},\mathcal{N}(y-x^{\star}))

\displaystyle=~\langle\nabla F(x)

\displaystyle-\nabla F(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\rangle+\mu\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\mu(y-x^{\star},x-x^{\star})

where we have used
\nabla D_{F}(x;x^{\star})=\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star})
and
(y-x^{\star},\mathcal{N}(y-x^{\star}))=0
since
\mathcal{N}
is skew-symmetric.

Using the identity for squares (
2.4
), we expand

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}-(y-x^{\star},x-x^{\star})=\frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Under the bound (
4.3
), we obtain (
4.4
).
∎

The calculation is more clear when
\nabla F(x)=Ax
is linear with
A=\nabla^{2}F\geq\mu I
. Then
-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y)\cdot(\mathcal{G}(x,y)-\mathcal{G}(x^{\star},y^{\star}))
is a quadratic form
v^{\intercal}Mv
with
v=(x-x^{\star},y-x^{\star})^{\intercal}
. We calculate the matrix
M
as

\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\mu I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\
-I+\mu^{-1}A&\quad I+\mu^{-1}\mathcal{N}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A&-A\\
-\mu I+A&\;\mu I+\mathcal{N}\end{pmatrix}.

As
v^{\intercal}Mv=v^{\intercal}\sym(M)v
, we compute the symmetric part

\displaystyle\sym\begin{pmatrix}A&-A\\
-\mu I+A&\;\mu I+\mathcal{N}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A&-\mu I/2\\
-\mu I/2&\mu I\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\geq\begin{pmatrix}A/2&0\\
0&\;\mu I/2\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mu I&-\mu I\\
-\mu I&\;\mu I\end{pmatrix},

where in the last step we use the convexity
A\geq\mu I
. Then (
4.4
) follows.

To see how the condition number changes using the accelerated gradient flow, we consider the following
2\times 2
matrix

(4.5)

G=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
1-a&\;-1+b{\rm i}\end{pmatrix}

with
a\geq 1
representing the eigenvalue of
\nabla^{2}F/\mu
and
b{\rm i}
for
\mathcal{N}/\mu
as the eigenvalue of skew-symmetric matrix is pure imaginary. Then the eigenvalue of
G
is

\lambda(G)=-1+\frac{b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+4(a-1)}}{2}{\rm i}.

The real part is always
-1
which implies the decay property of ODE
x^{\prime}=Gx
. The spectral radius is

|\lambda|=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{a})+\mathcal{O}(|b|),\quad\text{as }a\gg 1,|b|\gg 1.

As a comparison,
|a+b{\rm i}|=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(a)+\mathcal{O}(|b|)
. We improve the dependence from
\mathcal{O}(a)
to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{a})
.

4.2.
Implicit Euler Schemes

If we apply the implicit Euler method for the accelerated gradient system (
4.1
), the linear convergence is a direct consequence of the strong Lyapunov property (
4.4
). Consider

(4.6a)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{x}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1}):=y_{k+1}-x_{k+1},

(4.6b)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{y}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1}):=x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\mu^{-1}(\nabla F(x_{k+1})+\mathcal{N}y_{k+1}).

As we have shown before, the implicit Euler method is unconditionally stable with the price of solving a nonlinear equation system where
x_{k+1}
and
y_{k+1}
are coupled together, which is usually not practical. Again we present the convergence analysis here as a reference.

Denote
\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k})
and
z_{k}=(x_{k},y_{k})
. Using the convexity of the Lyapunov function and under the same assumption of Lemma
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq{}

\displaystyle(\nabla\mathcal{E}_{k+1},z_{k+1}-z_{k})-\frac{\mu}{2}\|z_{k+1}-z_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\left\langle{\mathcal{\nabla}\mathcal{E}_{k+1},\mathcal{G}(z_{k+1})}\right\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\|z_{k+1}-z_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k+1},

from which the linear convergence follows naturally

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}\mathcal{E}_{k}

for arbitrary step size
\alpha_{k}>0
.

4.3.
Implicit-Explicit Scheme

If we treat the skew symmetric part implicit, we can achieve the acceleration like the convex optimization problem. Consider the following implicit-explicit (IMEX) scheme of the accelerated gradient flow:

(4.7a)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{x}_{k+1},

(4.7b)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\mu^{-1}\left(\nabla F(\hat{x}_{k+1})+\mathcal{N}y_{k+1}\right),

(4.7c)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}.

We first treat
y
known as
y_{k}
and solve the first equation to get
\hat{x}_{k+1}
and then with known
\hat{x}_{k+1}
to solve the following linear equation

(4.8)

\left[(1+\alpha_{k})I+\alpha_{k}\mu^{-1}\mathcal{N}\right]y_{k+1}=b(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k}),

with known right hand side
b(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k})=\alpha_{k}\hat{x}_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}\mu^{-1}\nabla F(\hat{x}_{k+1})+y_{k}
. The linear equation is associated to a shifted skew-symmetric system
(\beta I+\mathcal{N})
with
\beta=1+\mu/\alpha_{k}
, which can be solved as discussion in Section
3.4
.
Then with computed
y_{k+1}
, we can solve for
x_{k+1}
again using an implicit discretization of
x^{\prime}=y-x
. In terms of the ODE solvers, (
4.7
) is known as the predictor-corrector method. The intermediate approximation
\hat{x}_{k+1}
is a predictor and
x_{k+1}
is a corrector.

As the skew-symmetric part is treated implicitly, the scheme is expected to achieve the accelerated linear rate as the accelerated gradient method for minimizing the convex function
F
.
For simplicity, we denote

(4.9)

\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k}),\quad\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})

for the Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}
defined in (
4.2
).

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
. Let
(\hat{x}_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by the accelerated gradient method (
4.7
). Then for
k\geq 0
,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha_{k}\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}-D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x_{k}).

Proof.

Direct computation using (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) gives

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}={}

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{x}\mathcal{E}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}),\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+\langle\partial_{y}\mathcal{E}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle-D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x_{k})-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}.

Then substitute

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\mathcal{G}^{x}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})+\alpha_{k}(y_{k}-y_{k+1})

\displaystyle y_{k+1}-y_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\mathcal{G}^{y}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}),

and use the strong Lyapunov property (
4.4
) at
(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})
to get the desired result.
∎

The term
-\alpha_{k}\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1};x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle
on the right hand side of (
4.10
) accounts for
-\alpha_{k}\langle\partial_{x}\mathcal{E}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle
for using explicit
y_{k}
in (
4.7a
), which is again a mis-match term compare with
\mathcal{G}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})
used in the implicit Euler scheme (
4.6a
).
The correction step (
4.7c
) will be used to bound the mis-match term. We restate and generalize the result in
[
27
]
in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume
\nabla F
is
L_{F}
-Lipschitz continuous and
(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})
is generated from
(x_{k},y_{k})
such that: for the Lyapunov function (
4.2
), there exists
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
satisfying

(4.11)

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq~-\alpha_{k}c_{1}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}c_{2}\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{c_{3}}{2}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}.

Then set
x_{k+1}
by the relation

(4.12)

(1+\alpha_{k}c_{1})(x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1})=\alpha_{k}c_{2}(y_{k+1}-y_{k}).

and choose
\alpha_{k}>0
satisfying

(4.13)

\alpha_{k}^{2}L_{F}c_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\alpha_{k}c_{1})c_{3},

we have the linear convergence

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}c_{1}}\mathcal{E}_{k}.

Proof.

Using the relation (
4.12
), we rewrite (
4.11
) into

(4.14)

\displaystyle(1+\alpha_{k}c_{1})\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-(1+c_{1}\alpha_{k})\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\right\rangle-\frac{(1+\alpha_{k}c_{1})^{2}c_{3}}{2\alpha_{k}^{2}c_{2}^{2}}\left\|x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\right\|^{2}.

As
D_{F}(\cdot,x^{\star})\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L_{F}}
, we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=D_{F}(x_{k+1},x^{\star})-D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\right\rangle+\frac{L_{F}}{2}\|x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}.

Summing
(1+c_{1}\alpha_{k})\times
(
4.15
) and (
4.14
) we get

\displaystyle(1+c_{1}\alpha_{k})\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq-\left(\frac{(1+\alpha_{k}c_{1})^{2}c_{3}}{2\alpha_{k}^{2}c_{2}^{2}}-\frac{(1+\alpha_{k}c_{1})L_{F}}{2}\right)\left\|x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\right\|^{2}\leq 0

according to our choice of
\alpha_{k}
. Rearrange the terms to get the desired inequality.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The largest step size
\alpha_{k}
satisfying (
4.13
) is given by

\alpha_{k}=\frac{c_{1}c_{3}+\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}c_{3}^{2}+4L_{F}c_{2}^{2}c_{3}}}{2L_{F}c_{2}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{c_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{c_{3}}{L_{F}}},

where the last one is a simplified formula for the step size satisfying (
4.13
).

We showed the decay of Lyapunov function in Lemma
4.3
satisfying the assumption of Lemma
4.4
with
c_{1}=c_{2}=1,c_{3}=\mu
and the correction step (
4.7c
) matches the relation (
4.12
). As a result, we state the following accelerated linear convergence rate of the IMEX scheme (
4.7
).

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L_{F}}
. Let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by the accelerated gradient method (
4.7
) with arbitrary initial value and step size satisfying

\alpha_{k}^{2}L_{F}\leq(1+\alpha_{k})\mu,

then for the Lyapunov function (
4.2
),

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}\mathcal{E}_{k}.

For
\alpha_{k}=1/\sqrt{\kappa(F)}
, we achieve the accelerated rate

\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+1/\sqrt{\kappa(F)}}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}_{0},

which implies

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\|y_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+1/\sqrt{\kappa(F)}}\right)^{k}2\mathcal{E}_{0}/\mu.

For linear problems, in comparison with the HSS scheme (
1.18
), we achieve the same accelerated rate for both linear and nonlinear systems without the need to compute the inverse of the symmetric part
(\beta I+\nabla^{2}F)^{-1}
. The efficiency of our algorithm is confirmed through computations on a convection-diffusion model in Section
6.2
. When relaxing to an inexact inner solver for the skew-symmetric part, our algorithm surpasses HSS methods. We provide an analysis using perturbation arguments to control the accumulation of the inner solve error in the following sub-section.

4.4.
Inexact Solvers for the Shifted Skew-symmetric System

In practice, we can relax the inner solver to be an inexact approximation. That is we solve equation (
4.8
) up to a residual
\varepsilon_{\rm in}=b-\left[(1+\alpha_{k})I+\alpha_{k}\mu^{-1}\mathcal{N}\right]y_{k+1}
. The scheme can be modified to

(4.16a)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{x}_{k+1},

(4.16b)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\mu^{-1}\left(\nabla F(\hat{x}_{k+1})+\mathcal{N}y_{k+1}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon_{\rm in}}{\alpha_{k}},

(4.16c)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{2}(x_{k+1}+\hat{x}_{k+1}).

Notice that in the third step (
4.16c
), we use
\frac{1}{2}(x_{k+1}+\hat{x}_{k+1})
instead of
x_{k+1}
for discretization variable
x
at
k+1
. The perturbation
\varepsilon_{\rm in}
is the residual of the linear equation (
4.8
).

Corollary 4.7
.

Assume function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L_{F}}
. If we compute
y_{k+1}
such that the residual of (
4.16b
) satisfies

(4.17)

\|\varepsilon_{\rm in}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\left(\|\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}\|y_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\right),

then for
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by the inexact accelerated gradient method (
4.16
) with arbitrary initial value and step size satisfying

\alpha_{k}^{2}L_{F}\leq(1+\alpha_{k}/2)\mu,

we have the linear convergence with repect to the Lyapunov function (
4.2
):

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}/2}\mathcal{E}_{k}.

For
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{1/\kappa(F)}
, we achieve the accelerated rate

\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1/\kappa(F)}}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}_{0}.

Proof.

We write (
4.16b
) as

y_{k+1}-y_{k}=\alpha_{k}\mathcal{G}^{y}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})+\varepsilon_{\rm in}.

Compared with Lemma
4.3
, the inexactness introduces a mis-match term
\mu(y_{k+1}-x^{\star},\epsilon_{\rm out})
in
\langle\partial_{y}\mathcal{E}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle
which can be bounded by

\displaystyle|(y_{k+1}-x^{\star},\epsilon_{\rm out})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{k}\mu}{4}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{\alpha_{k}}\|\varepsilon_{\rm in}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{k}\mu}{4}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\left(\|\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}\|y_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}\right).

Use
-\alpha_{k}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}/2
to cancel the first term and the additional quadratic term in (
4.10
) to cancel the second. Then we have

(4.18)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq-\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2},

which with the correction step (
4.16c
) satisfying assumptions in Lemma
4.4
with
c_{1}=1/2,c_{2}=1
and
c_{3}=\mu
.
∎

When we choose
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{1/\kappa(F)}
, (
4.8
) forms a shifted skew-symmetric equation
(\beta I+\mathcal{N})y=b(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k})
with
\beta=(1+\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}})\sqrt{\mu L_{F}}
. Solvers for the shifted skew-symmetric equation is discussed in Section
3
. In particular, the inner iteration steps are roughly

C_{\rm in}\sqrt{\kappa(F)}L_{B^{\sym}}/L_{F}.

The outer iteration is
C_{\rm out}\sqrt{\kappa(F)}
. Constants
C_{\rm in}=\mathcal{O}(|\ln\varepsilon_{\rm in}|)
and
C_{\rm out}=\mathcal{O}(|\ln\epsilon_{\rm out}|)
depend on the tolerance
\varepsilon_{\rm in}
for the inner iteration and
\epsilon_{\rm out}
for the outer iteration. Therefore (
4.16
) requires

•

\nabla F(x_{k})
gradient evaluation:
C_{\rm out}\sqrt{\kappa(F)}
;

•

(\beta I+\mathcal{N})x
matrix-vector multiplication:
C_{\rm in}C_{\rm out}\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym})
,

where we use the relation
\kappa(F)L_{B^{\sym}}/L_{F}=\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym})
.

4.5.
Accelerated Gradient and Skew-symmetric Splitting Methods

Combining the IMEX scheme in Section
4.3
and AOR in Section
3.5
, we propose the following explicit discretization of the accelerated gradient flow:

(4.19a)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{x}_{k+1},

(4.19b)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\nabla F(\hat{x}_{k+1})+B^{\sym}y_{k}-2By_{k+1}\right),

(4.19c)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{2}(x_{k+1}+\hat{x}_{k+1}).

The update of
y_{k+1}
is equivalent to solve a lower triangular linear algebraic system

\left((1+\alpha)I-\frac{2\alpha}{\mu}B\right)y_{k+1}=b(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k})

with
b(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k})=y_{k}+\alpha\hat{x}_{k+1}-\frac{\alpha}{\mu}\nabla F(\hat{x}_{k+1})-\frac{\alpha}{\mu}(B^{\intercal}+B)y_{k}
which can be computed efficiently by a forward substitution and thus no inexact inner solver is needed. Subtracting (
4.19c
) from (
4.19a
) implies the relation (
4.12
) with
c_{1}=1/2,c_{2}=1
.

Consider the modified Lyapunov function:

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x,y)

\displaystyle=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mu I-\alpha B^{\sym}}.

For
\displaystyle 0<\alpha<\frac{\mu}{L_{B^{\sym}}}
, we have
\mu I-\alpha B^{\sym}
is positive definite. Then
\mathcal{E}\geq 0
and
\mathcal{E}=0
if and only if
x=y=x^{\star}
. We denote

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{k}=\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k},y_{k}),\text{ and }\quad\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha B}_{k}=\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(\hat{x}_{k},y_{k}).

Lemma 4.8
.

Assume function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
. Let
(\hat{x}_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by the accelerated gradient method (
4.19
) with
0<\alpha\leq\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2L_{B^{\sym}}}
. Then, for
k\geq 0
, the modified Lyapunov function (
4.20
) satisfies

(4.21)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha B}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{k}\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{2}\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha B}_{k+1}-\alpha\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\mu}{4}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}.

Proof.

We write the difference of the modified Lyapunov function into

(4.22)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha B}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{k}=

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\alpha B^{\sym}}+\frac{1}{2}\|y_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\alpha B^{\sym}}.

where
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1},\mathcal{E}_{k}
are defined in (
4.9
) and
\mathcal{E}
refers to the Lyapunov function (
4.2
).
Since
\mathcal{E}
is
\mu
-convex,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq{}

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{x}\mathcal{E}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}),\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle~\langle\partial_{y}\mathcal{E}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}.

Then write the scheme as a correction of the implicit Euler scheme

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathcal{G}^{x}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})+\alpha(y_{k}-y_{k+1}),

\displaystyle y_{k+1}-y_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha\mathcal{G}^{y}(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})+\frac{\alpha}{\mu}B^{\sym}(y_{k+1}-y_{k}).

According to the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we get

(4.23)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq

\displaystyle-\alpha\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\alpha\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+(y_{k+1}-x^{\star},y_{k+1}-y_{k})_{\alpha B^{\sym}}.

We use the identity (
2.4
) to expand the last cross term in (
4.23
) as

\displaystyle(y_{k+1}-x^{\star},y_{k+1}-y_{k})_{\alpha B^{\sym}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|_{\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|_{\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|y_{k}-x^{\star}\|_{\alpha B^{\sym}}^{2}.

Substitute back to (
4.23
) and rearrange terms using (
4.22
),

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha B}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{k}\leq

\displaystyle-\alpha\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}-\alpha\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\mu I-\alpha B^{\sym}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{2}\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha B}_{k+1}-\frac{\alpha}{2}D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star})-\frac{\alpha}{4}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|_{\mu I+\alpha B^{\sym}}

\displaystyle-\alpha\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\mu I-\alpha B^{\sym}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{2}\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{\alpha B}_{k+1}-\alpha\left\langle\nabla D_{F}(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\mu}{4}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{2}D_{F}

\displaystyle(\hat{x}_{k+1},x^{\star})-\frac{\alpha}{4}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|_{\mu I+\alpha B^{\sym}}-\frac{1}{4}\left\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\mu I-2\alpha B^{\sym}}.

According to our choice
\alpha\leq\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2L_{B^{\sym}}}
, both
\mu I-2\alpha B^{\sym}
and
\mu I+\alpha B^{\sym}
are SPD. Dropping the last three non-positive terms we have the desired result.
∎

The decay of the modified Lyapunov function (
4.21
) and the appropriate relation (
4.12
) satisfies assumptions of Lemma
4.4
with
c_{1}=1/2,c_{2}=1
, and
c_{3}=\mu/2
. Although the Lyapunov function is slightly different, (
4.15
) still holds for
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}
. We conclude with the following linear convergence rate.

Theorem 4.9
.

Assume function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L_{F}}
. Let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by the accelerated gradient method (
4.19
) with arbitrary initial value and step size satisfying

0<\alpha\leq\min\left\{\frac{\mu}{2L_{B^{\sym}}},\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{2L_{F}}}\right\},

then for the modified Lyapunov function (
4.2
),

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha/2}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{k}.

In particular,
\displaystyle\alpha=\min\left\{\frac{\mu}{2L_{B^{\sym}}},\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{2L_{F}}}\right\}
, we achieve the accelerated rate

\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\mu}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}_{k}\leq\left(1+1/\max\left\{4\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym}),\sqrt{8\kappa(F)}\right\}\right)^{-k}\frac{2\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\alpha B}}{\mu}.

As expected, we have developed an explicit scheme (
4.19
) which achieves the accelerated rate.
Therefore the cost of gradient
\nabla F(x_{k})
evaluation and matrix-vector
(\beta I+B)^{-1}b
multiplication are both
C_{\rm out}\max\{\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym}),\sqrt{\kappa(F)}\}
. Compare with the inexact IMEX scheme (
4.7
), we may need more gradient evaluation but less matrix-vector multiplication. If
\tilde{\kappa}(B^{\sym})\gg\sqrt{\kappa(F)}
and
\nabla F(x_{k})
is computationally expensive to evaluate, then the IMEX scheme (

) or its inexact version (
4.16
) is favorable; otherwise if the error tolerance for the inexact inner solve is small, i.e.,

5.
Nonlinear Saddle Point Systems

In this section, we derive algorithms for strongly-convex-strongly-concave saddle point system which achieve optimal lower complexity. Moreover, by taking advantage of the block structure of the saddle point problems, we introduce the preconditioned version of GSS and AGSS methods.

5.1.
Problem Setting

Consider the nonlinear smooth saddle point system with bilinear coupling:

(5.1)

\min_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\max_{p\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{L}(u,p)=f(u)-g(p)+(Bu-b,p)

where
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{R}^{n},m\geq n
are finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces with inner product induced by SPD operators
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, respectively. Functions
f(u)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{f},L_{f}},
and
g(p)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{g},L_{g}}
, are measured in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
norm and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
norm, respectively. The operator
B
is an
n\times m
matrix of full rank.

The point
(u^{\star},p^{\star})
solves the min-max problem (
5.1
) is said to be a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}(u,p)
, that is

\mathcal{L}(u^{\star},p)\leq\mathcal{L}(u^{\star},p^{\star})\leq\mathcal{L}(u,p^{\star})\quad\forall\ (u,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The saddle point
(u^{*},p^{*})
satisfies the first order necessary condition for being the critical point of
\mathcal{L}(u,p)
:

(5.2a)

\displaystyle\nabla f(u^{\star})+B^{\intercal}p^{\star}=0,

(5.2b)

\displaystyle-Bu^{\star}+\nabla g(p^{\star})=0.

The first equation (
5.2a
) is
\partial_{u}\mathcal{L}(u^{\star},p^{\star})=0
but the second one (
5.2b
) is
-\partial_{p}\mathcal{L}(u^{\star},p^{\star})=0
. The negative sign is introduced so that (
5.2
) is in the form

\mathcal{A}(x^{\star})=0,

where
x=(u,p)\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{Q}
,
\nabla F=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f&0\\
0&\nabla g\end{pmatrix}
, and
\mathcal{N}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\;B^{\intercal}\\
-B&\;0\end{pmatrix}
. To avoid confusion, we use the notation
\mathcal{B}^{\sym}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\;B^{\intercal}\\
B&\;0\end{pmatrix}
in this section. The splitting (
3.10
) becomes

\mathcal{N}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\;B^{\intercal}\\
B&\;0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&\;0\\
2B&\;0\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{N}=\begin{pmatrix}I&\;0\\
0&\;-I\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{B}^{\sym}.

Therefore
\|\mathcal{N}\|=\|\mathcal{B}^{\sym}\|
for any operator norm.

Given two SPD operators
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, we denote by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}&0\\
0&\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix},

and

\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}&0\\
0&\mu_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix}.

Then for any
x=(u,p),y=(v,q)\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{Q},

(\mathcal{A}(x)-\mathcal{A}(y),x-y)\geq\|x-y\|_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}.

The accelerated gradient flow and the discrete schemes follows from discussion in Section
4
. The results are slightly sharp by including the preconditioner
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
as a block diagonal matrix.

5.2.
Accelerated Gradient Flow for Saddle Point Problems

The component form of (
4.1
) becomes the preconditioned accelerated gradient flow for the saddle point system:

(5.3)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=v-u,

\displaystyle p^{\prime}

\displaystyle=q-p,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=u-v-\mu_{f}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}(\nabla f(u)+B^{\intercal}q),

\displaystyle q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p-q-\mu_{g}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}(\nabla g(p)-Bv).

Recall that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
are SPD operators considered as preconditioner or change of metric. We employ the inverse notation based on the intuition that a preconditioner can serve as a linear solver to approximate the matrix inverse, and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
will become a novel metric for the variable spaces. Our analysis is designed to provide a general framework that offers the flexibility to incorporate various metrics tailored to specific problems. In particular, when
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
are identities, then (
5.3
) reduced to the accelerated gradient flow (
4.1
) under typical Euclidean metric.

Consider the Lyapunov function:

(5.4)

\mathcal{E}(x,y):=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}^{2},

where
D_{F}(x,x^{\star}):=D_{f}(u,u^{\star})+D_{g}(p,p^{\star})
. As
f,g
are strongly convex,
\mathcal{E}(x,y)\geq 0
and
\mathcal{E}(x,y)=0
iff
x=y=x^{\star}
.
Denote the vector field on the right hand side of (
5.3
) by
\mathcal{G}(x,y)
. It is straightforward to verify the strong Lyapunov property following Lemma
4.2
:

(5.5)

\displaystyle-\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y)\cdot\mathcal{G}(x,y)\geq\mathcal{E}(x,y)+\frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}.

5.3.
GSS and AGSS Methods for Saddle Point Problems

5.3.1.
GSS method

For saddle point problems, the preconditioned GSS method (
3.20
) is written as

(5.6)

\displaystyle\frac{u_{k+1}-u_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{f}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\left(\nabla f(x_{k})+B^{\intercal}p_{k}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{p_{k+1}-p_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{g}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}\left(\nabla g(x_{k})+Bu_{k}-2Bu_{k+1}\right).

Consider the modified Lyapunov function

(5.7)

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k}):=\frac{1}{2}\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\alpha\mathcal{B}^{\sym}}-\alpha D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k})

We first show
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}
is non-negative if
\alpha
is sufficiently small.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{f}}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{g}}
. When the step size
\alpha\leq\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}/(4L_{S})}
with
L_{S}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal})
, the matrix

\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-2\alpha\mathcal{B}^{\sym}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}&-2\alpha B^{\intercal}\\
-2\alpha B&\mu_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix}

is symmetric and positive semidefinite.

Proof.

We have the block matrix factorization

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}&-2\alpha B^{\intercal}\\
-2\alpha B&\mu_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&0\\
-2\alpha\mu_{f}^{-1}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}&0\\
0&\mu_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}-4\alpha^{2}\mu_{f}^{-1}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&-2\alpha\mu_{f}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Then if
\alpha\leq\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}/(4L_{S})}
, we have
\mu_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}-4\alpha^{2}\mu_{f}^{-1}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}\geq 0
and the results follows.
∎

As a result, one can easily show that
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}\geq 0
if

\alpha<\min\{\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}/(4L_{S})},\mu_{g}/(2L_{f}),\mu_{g}/(2L_{g})\}

and
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x)=0
if and only if
x=x^{\star}
. We skip the proof since it is similar to that of Lemma
3.2
.

The convergence analysis will depend on the condition number of the Schur complement
S=B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}
in the
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
inner product.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\mathcal{B}^{\sym}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\;B^{\intercal}\\
B&\;0\end{pmatrix}
, we have

L_{\mathcal{B}^{\sym}}:=\|\mathcal{B}^{\sym}\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}=\sqrt{L_{S}}=\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{B}^{\sym}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+n)^{2}}
is symmetric,
\mathcal{B}^{\sym}
has
(m+n)
real eigenvalues. Let
\lambda
be the largest absolute value eigenvalue with respect to
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
x=(u,p)
be the corresponding eigenvector, that is
\|\mathcal{B}^{\sym}\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}=\lambda
and

\mathcal{B}^{\sym}x=\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}x.

The component form follows as

\displaystyle Bu=\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}p,\quad B^{\intercal}p=\lambda\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}u.

Substitute
u
in the first equation side using the other equation, we get

\displaystyle B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}p=\lambda^{2}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}p.

Since
B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}
is positive definite, we have
|\lambda|=\sqrt{L_{S}}
,
L_{S}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal})
.
∎

Define
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\alpha BD}:=\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k})
and
\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}):=\sqrt{L_{S}/(\mu_{f}\mu_{g})}
. With
\kappa(F)
refined to
\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)
and
\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)
,
we state the linear convergence of the GSS method for saddle point problems as a direct corollary of Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\{u_{k},p_{k}\}
be the sequence generated by the GSS method (
5.6
) with arbitrary initial guess
(u_{0},p_{0})
and step size

0<\alpha<\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{1}{2\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})},\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)},\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}\right\}.

Then for the discrete Lyapunov function (
5.7
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha BD}(x_{k}).

In particular, for
\alpha=\displaystyle 1/\max\left\{4\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}),4\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f),4\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)\right\}
, we have

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}\leq\left(1+1/\max\left\{4\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}),4\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f),4\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)\right\}\right)^{-k}6\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}^{2}.

5.3.2.
Implicit in the gradient part

We can use the generalized gradient flow (
3.1
) and treat
\nabla F
implicitly and
\mathcal{N}
explicitly with AOR

(5.8)

\displaystyle u_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{prox}_{\alpha/\mu_{f}f,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(u_{k}-\alpha\mu_{f}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}p_{k}),

\displaystyle p_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{prox}_{\alpha/\mu_{g}g,\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(p_{k}-\alpha\mu_{g}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}B(u_{k}-2u_{k+1})).

When
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
are identities, that matched the typical proximal operators defined in (
2.2
). The parameters
\mu_{f}
and
\mu_{g}
are proper scaling for later analysis. Notice that the proximal operation works for non-smooth functions.

Consider the Lyapunov function for AOR methods:

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(u,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{f}}{2}\|u-u^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}\|p-p^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}^{2}-\alpha(B(u-u^{\star}),p-p^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\alpha\mathcal{B}^{\sym}}.

Similar to Lemma
5.1
, on can show
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x)\geq 0
for
0\leq\alpha<1/\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})
and
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x)=0
if and only if
x=x^{\star}
. As
\nabla F
is treated implicitly, the following result can be proved similar to Theorem
3.1
. Recall that

\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}):=\sqrt{L_{S}/(\mu_{f}\mu_{g})}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal})/(\mu_{f}\mu_{g})}.

Theorem 5.4
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{f}}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{g}}
. Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the sequence generated by (
5.8
) with arbitrary initial guess
x_{0}
and step size
0<\alpha<1/\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})
. Then for the Lyapunov function (
5.9
),

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k}).

In particular, for
\alpha=1/(2\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}))
, we have

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}\leq\left(1+1/(2\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})\right)^{-k}3\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}.

As a special case when
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
are identity matrices,
\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})=\|B\|/\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}}
is indeed the optimal lower bound shown in
[
39
]
. Proximal methods combining
overrelaxation parameters are considered and yielding optimal rates; see
[
6
,
7
,
19
]
for variants of schemes and applications. Here we extend the framework to proximal operators under general metric other than Euclidean norm, which has wider application for example in
[
13
,
17
]
.

5.3.3.
AGSS method

Recall that
x=(u,p),y=(v,q)
. The accelerated gradient and skew-symmetric splitting method is:

(5.11a)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{x}_{k+1},

(5.11b)

\displaystyle\frac{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{k+1}-v_{k+1}-\mu_{f}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\left(\nabla f(\hat{u}_{k+1})+B^{\intercal}q_{k}\right),

(5.11c)

\displaystyle\frac{q_{k+1}-q_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{k+1}-q_{k+1}-\mu_{g}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}\left(\nabla g(\hat{p}_{k+1})-2Bv_{k+1}+Bv_{k}\right),

(5.11d)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}+\frac{1}{2}(x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k+1}).

Each iteration requires
2
matrix-vector products if we store
Bv_{k}
,
2
gradient evaluations and the computation of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
.
Notice that the scheme is explicit as
v_{k+1}
can be first updated by (
5.11b
) and then used to generate
q_{k+1}
in (
5.11c
).

Consider the tailored discrete Lyapunov function:

(5.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x,y):={}

\displaystyle D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|_{\mu\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}-\alpha\mathcal{B}^{\sym}}^{2}.

As a result of Lemma
5.1
,
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}\geq 0
if
\alpha\leq\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}/(4L_{S})}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x,y)=0
only if
x=x^{\star}
. Using the first order necessary conditions (
5.2
), the Bregman divergence part can be related to the duality gap
\Delta(u,p):=\max_{q}\mathcal{L}(u,q)-\min_{v}\mathcal{L}(v,p)
:

\displaystyle D_{F}(x,x^{\star})=

\displaystyle D_{f}(u;u^{\star})+D_{g}(p;p^{\star})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(u)-f(u^{\star})-\langle\nabla f(u^{\star}),u-u^{\star}\rangle+g(p)-g(p^{\star})-\langle\nabla g(p^{\star}),p-p^{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(u)-f(u^{\star})+\langle B^{\intercal}p^{\star},u-u^{\star}\rangle+g(p)-g(p^{\star})-\langle Bu^{\star},p-p^{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,p^{\star})-\mathcal{L}(u^{\star},p)\leq\Delta(u,p).

Define
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\alpha B}:=\mathcal{E}^{\alpha B}(x_{k},y_{k})
and recall that
\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})=\sqrt{L_{S}/(\mu_{f}\mu_{g})}
. With
\kappa(F)
refined to
\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)
and
\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)
,
we have the following linear convergence as a direct corollary of Theorem
4.9
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{f},L_{f}}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{g},L_{g}}
. Let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by the accelerated gradient and skew-symmetric splitting method (
5.11
) with arbitrary initial value and step size satisfying

0<\alpha\leq\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{1}{2\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})},\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)}},\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}}\right\}.

Then for the discrete Lyapunov function (
5.12
),

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}^{\alpha B}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha/2}\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\alpha B}.

In particular, for
\alpha=1/\max\left\{2\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}),\sqrt{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)},\sqrt{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}\right\}
, we achieve the accelerated rate

\displaystyle\mu_{f}\|u_{k}-u^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}^{2}+\mu_{g}\|p_{k}-p^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+1/\max\left\{4\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}),2\sqrt{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)},2\sqrt{2\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}\right\}\right)^{-k}2\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\alpha B}.

For strongly-convex-strongly-concave saddle point systems, set
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
to identity matrices, scheme (
5.11
) is an explicit scheme achieving the lower complexity bound:
\Omega\left(\sqrt{\kappa(f)+\kappa^{2}(\mathcal{N})+\kappa(g)}|\ln\epsilon|\right)

established in
[
39
]
. Notice that in
[
39
]
, only the theoretical lower bound is proved and no algorithms are developed to match the lower bound. Ours are among the first few explicit schemes
[
21
,
23
,
34
]
achieving this lower bound. The optimality of our algorithm is confirmed through the solution of empirical risk minimization in Section
6.3
.

The condition numbers can be improved using appropriate SPD preconditioners
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
with the price of computing
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
. The term
\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})
might be the leading term compare with
\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)}
and
\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}
.
Preconditioner
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
can be chosen so that
L_{S}
is small. Namely
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
is a preconditioner for the Schur complement
B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal}
. For example, if
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}=I_{m}
, then the ideal choice is
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}=(BB^{\intercal})^{-1}
so that
L_{S}=1
. Such choice of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
may increase the condition number
\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)
as
(BB^{\intercal})^{-1}
may not be a good preconditioner of
g
. Transformed primal dual methods developed in
[
12
]
will provide a better approach but cannot fit into our approach.

5.3.4.
Implicit in the skew-symmetric part

The term
\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})
is due to the bilinear coupling or equivalently from the explicit treatment for the skew-symmetric component
\mathcal{N}
. We can treat
\mathcal{N}
implicitly and obtain the following IMEX scheme:

(5.13a)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-\hat{x}_{k+1},

(5.13b)

\displaystyle\frac{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{k+1}-v_{k+1}-\mu_{f}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}\left(\nabla f(\hat{u}_{k+1})+B^{\intercal}q_{k+1}\right),

(5.13c)

\displaystyle\frac{q_{k+1}-q_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{k+1}-q_{k+1}-\mu_{g}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}\left(\nabla g(\hat{p}_{k+1})-Bv_{k+1}\right),

(5.13d)

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}.

As the skew-symmetric part is treat implicitly, the restriction
\alpha\leq 1/(2\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N}))
can be removed. We state the convergence result directly as the proofs are similar to discussion in Section
4.3
for IMEX schemes.

Theorem 5.6
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{f},L_{f}}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{g},L_{g}}
. Let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by the preconditioned accelerated gradient method (
5.13
) with arbitrary initial value and step size
\alpha_{k}
satisfying

\alpha_{k}^{2}L_{f}\leq(1+\alpha_{k})\mu_{f},\quad\alpha_{k}^{2}L_{g}\leq(1+\alpha_{k})\mu_{g},

then for the Lyapunov function (
5.4
),

(5.14)

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}\mathcal{E}_{k}.

In particular for
\alpha_{k}=1/\max\{\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)},\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}\}
, we achieve the accelerated rate

\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq\left(1+1/\max\{\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)},\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}\}\right)^{-k}\mathcal{E}_{0}.

We discuss the inner solve on (
5.13b
)-(
5.13c
) which is equivalent to the following linear algebraic equation

(5.15)

\begin{pmatrix}(1+\alpha_{k})\mu_{f}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}&\alpha_{k}B^{\intercal}\\
-\alpha_{k}B&(1+\alpha_{k})\mu_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{k+1}\\
q_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=b(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k}),

with
b(\hat{x}_{k+1},y_{k})=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}(y_{k}+\alpha_{k}\hat{x}_{k+1})-\alpha_{k}\nabla F(\hat{x}_{k+1})
.
Solving equation (
5.15
) basically costs the effort of solving

((1+\alpha_{k})^{2}\mu_{f}\mu_{g}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}+\alpha_{k}^{2}B\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}B^{\intercal})x=b,

which can be solved by preconditioned conjugate gradient methods.

As a direct application of Section
4.4
, the inner solver can be inexact. We denote the tolerance by
|\ln\epsilon_{\rm in}|
. With the restriction on the step size
\alpha_{k}
, the outer iteration complexity is
O(\alpha_{k}^{-1}|\ln\epsilon_{\rm out}|)
.
Therefore we conclude that IMEX scheme (
5.13
) requires

•

gradient evaluation:
\max\{\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}}(f)},\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}}(g)}\}|\ln\epsilon_{\rm out}|
,

•

matrix-vector multiplication:
\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})|\ln\epsilon_{\rm out}||\ln\epsilon_{\rm in}|
,

•

preconditioners
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
:
\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathcal{N})|\ln\epsilon_{\rm out}||\ln\epsilon_{\rm in}|
,

which matches the optimal lower bound in
[
39
]
.
The preconditioners
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}^{-1}
or
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{-1}
are designed to balance the cost of gradient evaluation, matrix-vector multiplication and preconditioning solver computation.

6.3.
Empirical Risk Minimization

Consider the regularized empirical risk minimization (ERM) with linear predictors, which is a classical supervised learning problem. Given a data matrix
B=[b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{n}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
where
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

is the feature vector of the
i
-th data entry, the ERM problem aims to solve

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g(Bu)+f(u),

where
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is some convex loss function,
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex regularizer and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the linear predictor. Equivalently, we can solve the dual problem
\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{-g^{*}(p)-f^{*}\left(-B^{\intercal}p\right)\right\}
or the saddle point problem

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{p^{\intercal}Bu-g^{*}(p)+f(u)\right\}.

The saddle point formulation is favorable in many scenarios, e.g., when such formulation admits a finite-sum structure
[
36
]
, reduces communication complexity in the distributed setting
[
37
]
or exploits sparsity structure
[
24
]
.

We define the loss function as the mean square error, given by
g^{*}(p)=\frac{1}{2}\|p\|_{G}^{2}
, induced by the
G
-norm associated with an SPD matrix
G
. Additionally, we incorporate an
\ell_{2}
regularizer, where
f(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}
. The ERM model is then formulated as a linear saddle point problem. In this formulation, we have
\nabla^{2}F=\begin{pmatrix}I&~0\\
0&~G\end{pmatrix}
, and
\mathcal{B}^{\sym}=\begin{pmatrix}0&~B^{\intercal}\\
B&~0\end{pmatrix}
.

For our numerical experiments, we consider two cases:

•

Case 1:
\kappa(\mathcal{B}^{\sym})\gg\kappa(G)
;

•

Case 2:
\kappa(\mathcal{B}^{\sym})\ll\kappa(G)
.

•

Case 3:
\kappa(\mathcal{B}^{\sym})=\sqrt{\kappa(G)}
.

We implement the GSS method (
5.6
) and AGSS method (
5.11
) with
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
set as identities. Additionally, we provide a list of other first-order primal-dual algorithms for comparison:

•

Lifted primal-dual (LPD) method for bilinearly coupled smooth minimax optimization proposed in
[
34
]
.

•

AcceleratedGradient-OptimisticGradient (AG-OG) method
[
25
]
with restarting regime.

•

Accelerated primal-dual gradient (APDG) method
[
23
]
for smooth and convex-concave saddle-point problems with bilinear coupling.

•

Optimistic gradient descent ascent (OGDA) method
[
29
]
in smooth convex-concave saddle point problem.

Among all the tests, we set the parameters as specified. AGSS, LPD, AG-OG, and APDG are first-order algorithms achieving optimal convergence rates, while OGDA obtains a sublinear rate for general saddle point problems.

In Table
7
, we can observe that the performance of GSS and AGSS is comparable as the non-symmetric part dominate, i.e.
\kappa(\mathcal{B}^{\sym})\gg\kappa(G)
, the acceleration is not helpful. However, when
\kappa(G)
are relatively large, as shown in Table
8
and Table
9
, AGSS is much faster than GSS due to the acceleration technique.

Overall, AGSS is more stable and efficient concerning iteration number and CPU time, especially when the condition number
\kappa(G)
is large.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose GSS methods and AGSS methods for solving a class of strongly monotone operator equation
\mathcal{A}(x)=0
based on the splitting
\mathcal{A}(x)=\nabla F(x)+\mathcal{N}x
. The proof of strong Lyapunov property bridges the design of dynamical system on the continuous level, the choice of Lyapunov function and the linear convergence of the numerical schemes (as discretization of the continuous flow). As direct applications, we derive optimal algorithms for strongly-convex-strongly-concave saddle point systems with bilinear coupling.

As it is well-known in the convex minimization, the optimal mixed type convergence rates is
\mathcal{O}(\min\{1/k^{2},(1-1/\sqrt{\kappa(f)})^{-k})\}

[
11
]
, which enjoys a sub-linear rate as long as
f
is convex only, i.e.,
\mu=0
. With more careful design of parameters, our AGSS may also achieve accelerated sub-linear convergence rates. Such method can be used as a smoother for deriving nonlinear multigrid methods, yielding iteration complexity free of problem size and condition number.
