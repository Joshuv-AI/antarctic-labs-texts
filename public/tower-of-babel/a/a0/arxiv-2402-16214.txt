Products and Powers of Principal Symmetric Ideals
Eric Dannetun, Bruce Fang, Riccardo Formenti, Bo Y. Gao, Juliann Geraci, Ross Kogel, Yuelin Li, Shreya Mandal, Vinuge Rupasinghe, Alexandra Seceleanu, Duc Van Khanh Tran, Noah Walker

Abstract
. Principal symmetric ideals were recently introduced by Harada, Seceleanu, and Şega in [ HSS ] , where their homological properties are elucidated. They are ideals generated by the orbit of a single polynomial under permutations of variables in a polynomial ring. In this paper we determine when a product of two principal symmetric ideals is principal symmetric and when the powers of a principal symmetric ideal are again principal symmetric ideals. We characterize the ideals that have the latter property as being generated by polynomials invariant up to a scalar multiple under permutation of variables. Recognizing principal symmetric ideals is an open question for the purpose of which we produce certain obstructions. We also demonstrate that the Hilbert functions of symmetric monomial ideals are not all given by symmetric monomial ideals, in contrast to the non-symmetric case.

Primary: 13A50, 13C13, Secondary: 13D40, 13F20.

1.
Introduction

Principal symmetric ideals, abbreviated in this paper as psi’s, were introduced in
[
HSS
]
. We recall this notion. Given an element
f
of the polynomial ring
R={\sf k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, the principal symmetric ideal
(f)_{S_{d}}
is the ideal generated by the orbit of
f
under the natural action of the symmetric group
S_{d}
on
R
. In detail, the definition states

(f)_{S_{d}}=(f(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(d)}):\sigma\in S_{d}).

Throughout this paper we make the assumption that the generator
f
of a psi is a
homogeneous
polynomial. In this case the psi
(f)_{S_{d}}
is a homogeneous ideal and we may consider its invariants such as the minimal number of generators and the Hilbert function.

In this paper we address a fundamental question.

Question 1.1
.

How can one determine whether a given ideal is a psi or not?

Whereas an ideal may be a psi despite not being written given in the form displayed above, this is a difficult question. An important ingredient in the remainder of our results consists of developing obstructions for an ideal to be a psi based on its number of generators and other characteristics. We investigate this paradigm in more detail in
Section

3
. A highlight of our analysis is
3.6
, whose statement is too technical to state here.

Next we consider powers and products of psi’s. Simple examples show that powers, and hence products of psi’s need not be psi’s; see
5.2
. We investigate the questions:

Question 1.2
.

When is a product of psi’s a psi?
When are the powers of a psi psi’s?

We settle the question regarding products completely in the case of monomial psi’s and in the case of psi’s in two dimensional polynomial rings in
5.4
and
5.9
, respectively. This characterization is in terms of
{\sf k}
-symetric polynomials, which are those only changed by a multiplication by a constant upon the action of any permutation of the variables. Regarding powers, we characterize psi’s whose sufficiently large powers are also pis’s as follows:

Theorem

(abstracted from
5.5
,
5.7
, and
5.10
)
.

Let
I
be a homogeneous principal symmetric ideal. Consider the statements

(1)

I^{n}
is a principal symmetric ideal for all
n\geq 1

(2)

I^{n}
is a principal symmetric ideal for all sufficiently large integers
n

(3)

I^{2}
is a principal symmetric ideal

(4)

I
is generated by a
{\sf k}
-symmetric polynomial

Statements (1), (2), and (4) are equivalent for all
I
. Statement (3) is equivalent to the rest when
I
is a monomial ideal and when
I
is an ideal of
{\sf k}[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

F.S. Macaulay’s classical work
[
Mac
]
yields that for every homogeneous ideal
I
there exists a monomial ideal having the same Hilbert function as
I
. We ask whether an analogous phenomenon takes place in the category of symmetric homogeneous ideals:

Question 1.3
.

Given a symmetric ideal
I
, does there exist a symmetric monomial ideal with the same Hilbert function as
I
?

In
6.2
we show that the answer to the question above is negative when
I
ranges over a large class of psi’s. Once again the key to this observation is an analysis of the number of generators of general psi’s performed in
[
HSS
]
versus that of monomial psi’s in a large number of variables. We study monomial psi’s in more detail in
Section

4
, where we show the following useful characterization:

Theorem

(
4.4
)
.

A principal symmetric ideal
I
is a monomial ideal if and only if
I=(m)_{S_{d}}
for some monomial
m
.

Our work opens up avenues of investigation into a series of related questions which we list in
Section

7
.

2.
Terminology

Throughout the paper
{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the set of natural numbers (including zero) and
R
denotes the polynomial ring
{\sf k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
. All facts stated in this paper are true for
d=1
, but to avoid trivialities we assume henceforth that the number of variables is
d\geq 2
. The symmetric group
S_{d}
acts on
R
by means of setting for
\sigma\in S_{d}
and
f\in R

\sigma\cdot f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)}).

Working with the symmetric group
S_{d}
leads naturally to partitions with
d
parts.

Definition 2.1
.

A
partition
is a tuple
{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
such that
a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{d}
. Its number of parts is
d
and the sum of its parts is
|{\mathbf{a}}|=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}
.

We denote by
P(n)
the number of partitions of
n
into arbitrary number of parts, that is,

P(n)=\#\{{\mathbf{a}}:|{\mathbf{a}}|=n\}.

Partitions are in bijection with equivalence classes of monomials under the action of the symmetric group.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})
be a partition with
d
parts. A monomial
m\in{\sf k}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]
is said to be of
order type

{\mathbf{a}}
if

m=x_{1}^{a_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots x_{d}^{a_{\sigma(d)}}\text{ for some }\sigma\in S_{d}.

We denote by
R_{\mathbf{a}}
the
{\sf k}
-linear span of all monomials of order type
{\mathbf{a}}
in
R={\sf k}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]
and by
(R_{\mathbf{a}})
the ideal generated by this set.

Let
k
denote the number of distinct values in the tuple
{\mathbf{a}}
and let
\{n_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be the number of occurrences of each value in
{\mathbf{a}}
. Then we have:

(2.1)

\dim_{\sf k}R_{\mathbf{a}}=\frac{d!}{n_{1}!n_{2}!\cdots n_{k}!}.

We single out a class of polynomials which generate psi’s having well-controlled generators.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
f
be a homogeneous polynomial.
We say that
f
is
strongly homogeneous
if all monomials in
f
have the same order type.
If
f
is strongly homogeneous, then the order type of
f
is the order type of a monomial in
f
.

Note that the property of strong homogeneity and the order type of a polynomial are preserved under permutation of variables.

Definition 2.4
.

An ideal
I
is
strongly homogeneous
if
I
is generated by strongly homogeneous polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
such that all
f_{i}
’s have the same order type.
A
strongly homogeneous psi
is a principal symmetric ideal generated by a strongly homogeneous polynomial.

For example, principal symmetric ideals generated by a monomial are strongly homogeneous psi’s. Note that a strongly homogeneous psi is also a strongly homogeneous ideal in the sense of
2.4
. Whether a homogeneous ideal is strongly homogeneous can be checked using any minimal generating set.

Definition 2.5
.

If
I
is a homogeneous ideal of a graded
{\sf k}
-algebra, it admits a decomposition
I=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}I_{n}
, where
I_{n}
is the
{\sf k}
-vector space of elements of degree
n
in
I
.
The
Hilbert function
of
I
is the function

H_{I}:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}},\,H_{I}(n)=\dim_{\sf k}I_{n}.

3.
Recognizing principal symmetric ideals

In this section we are concerned with the problem of identifying whether a given ideal is a psi. This is highly non trivial as the ideal may have a different set of generators than the one expected in the definition of a psi. Thus the difficulty of the problem consists in the non-uniqueness of minimal generating sets for homogeneous ideals.

In this section we focus on obstructions preventing a homogeneous ideal from being a psi. For a homogeneous ideal
I
we denote by
\mu(I)
its minimal number of generators. Since a psi
I
has all its generators in a single degree, say degree
n
, one can compute the minimal number of generators of
I
as
\mu(I)=\dim_{\sf k}(I_{n})
. The number of generators, when too large, can be construed as an obstruction to being a psi by means of the following observation.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
I
is homogeneous psi of
{\sf k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, then
\mu(I)\leq d!
.

Proof.

By definition, a set of (not necessarily minimal) generators of
I
is in bijection with the group
S_{d}
.
∎

For a polynomial
f
we denote by
f({\bf 1})
the element of
{\sf k}
obtained by evaluating
f
at
x_{i}=1
for
1\leq i\leq d
. Note that every element in the
S_{d}
orbit of
f
has the same value when evaluated at
{\bf 1}
. More can be said regarding obstructions to being a psi in terms of this invariant.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f
be a strongly homogeneous polynomial in
{\sf k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
with order type
{\mathbf{a}}
. If
f({\bf 1})=0
then
(f)_{S_{d}}\subsetneq(R_{\mathbf{a}})
. In particular
\mu((f)_{S_{d}})\leq\dim_{\sf k}R_{\mathbf{a}}-1
.

Proof.

The containment
(f)_{S_{d}}\subsetneq(R_{\mathbf{a}})
follows because
f\in R_{\mathbf{a}}
implies
\sigma\cdot f\in R_{\mathbf{a}}
for any
\sigma\in S_{d}
. The condition
f({\bf 1})=0
implies
(\sigma\cdot f)({\bf 1})=0
for all
\sigma\in S_{d}
and moreover
g({\bf 1})=0
for any polynomial
g
in the degree
|{\mathbf{a}}|
component of
I
. Therefore there are elements of
R_{\mathbf{a}}
not in
I
, for example, no monomials of degree
{\mathbf{a}}
are in
I
. This gives the strict containment and hence the claimed inequality.
∎

The following technical lemmas are the main ingredients in the proof of
3.6
as well as
4.3
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f
be a homogeneous polynomial and write
f=g_{1}+\cdots+g_{r}
, where the monomials in each of the
g_{i}
’s share no order types. Suppose there exists a homogeneous polynomial
g\in(f)_{S_{d}}
such that
\deg(g)=\deg(f)
,
g
shares order types only with
g_{1}
among all
g_{i}
’s, and
g({\bf 1})\not=0
. Then for all
i\not=1
,
g_{i}({\bf 1})=0
. Thus,
f({\bf 1})=g_{1}({\bf 1})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
g\in(f)_{S_{d}}
with
g({\bf 1})\not=0
.
Since
\deg(g)=\deg(f)
, there exist constants
c_{\sigma}\in{\sf k}
such that

g=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{r})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot g_{1}+\cdots+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot g_{r}.

Since
g
shares order types only with
g_{1}
and the
g_{i}
’s share no order types, we conclude from the identity displayed above that
g=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot g_{1}
and
\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot g_{i}=0
for
i>1
.
Hence,

\displaystyle g({\bf 1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}(\sigma\cdot g_{1})({\bf 1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}g_{1}({\bf 1})

\displaystyle=g_{1}({\bf 1})\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}.

Since
g({\bf 1})\not=0
, it follows that
\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\neq 0
.
For
i>1
we deduce from
\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot g_{i}=0
that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}(\sigma\cdot g_{i})({\bf 1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}g_{i}({\bf 1})

\displaystyle=g_{i}({\bf 1})\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}

Since
\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\neq 0
, this yields
g_{i}({\bf 1})=0
for
i>1
, as desired, and thus
f({\bf 1})=g_{1}({\bf 1})
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f
be homogeneous and
g\in(f)_{S_{d}}
be a homogeneous polynomial such that
\deg(g)=\deg(f)
.
Then
f=g^{\prime}+h
for some
g^{\prime}
which contains precisely the same order types as
g
and for some
h
which shares no order types with
g
.
Also, if
g({\bf 1})\not=0
, then
g^{\prime}({\bf 1})\not=0
.

Proof.

Since
g\in(f)_{S_{d}}
and
\deg(g)=\deg(f)
, there exist
c_{\sigma}\in K
such that

g=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot f.

Since order types are invariant under permutation,
f
must contain a nonzero term for every order type present in
g
.
Let
g^{\prime}
contain precisely those terms of
f
whose order type is present in
g
.
Let
h=f-g^{\prime}
, so that
f=g^{\prime}+h
and
h
shares no order types with
g
.

Now, suppose that
g({\bf 1})\not=0
.
Note that

g=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot(g^{\prime}+h)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot g^{\prime}+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot h.

Since
h
shares no order types with
g
,
\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot g^{\prime}=g
and
\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}\sigma\cdot h=0
.
Thus,

g({\bf 1})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma}(\sigma\cdot g^{\prime})({\bf 1})=g^{\prime}({\bf 1})\sum_{\sigma\in S_{d}}c_{\sigma},

so
g^{\prime}({\bf 1})\not=0
.
∎

We illustrate the previous lemma with an example.

Example 3.5
.

Let
f=x_{1}^{3}-x_{2}^{3}+x_{1}^{2}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}]
. One can verify, for example by using Macaulay2
[
M2
]
, that
g=x_{1}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}\in(f)_{S_{4}}
. Among the terms of
f
, those which share order types with
g
are
x_{1}^{2}x_{3}
and
x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}
, while
x_{1}^{3}
and
-x_{2}^{3}
do not. So the proof of
3.4
tells us to set
g^{\prime}=x_{1}^{2}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}
and
h^{\prime}=x_{1}^{3}-x_{2}^{3}
. Observe that
g({\bf 1})\not=0
and also
g^{\prime}({\bf 1})\not=0
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
I
be a homogeneous ideal generated in degree
n
.
Let
g_{1},g_{2}
be homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
that share no order types such that
g_{1}({\bf 1})\not=0
and
g_{2}({\bf 1})\not=0
.
If
g_{1},g_{2}\in I
then
I
is not a principal symmetric ideal.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that
I
is a principal symmetric ideal generated by some
f
.
Then by
3.4
,
f=g_{1}^{\prime}+h_{1}
and
f=g_{2}^{\prime}+h_{2}
where
g_{i}^{\prime}
and
h_{i}
share no order types and moreover
g_{i}^{\prime}
contains precisely the same order types as
g_{i}
.
Thus, the identity
g_{1}^{\prime}+h_{1}=g_{2}^{\prime}+h_{2}
combined with the fact that
g_{1},g_{2}
share no order types shows that the terms of
g_{1}^{\prime}
are a subset of the terms of
h_{2}
and the terms of
g_{2}^{\prime}
are a subset of the terms of
h_{1}
.

Let
h^{\prime}=h_{2}-g_{1}^{\prime}
and note that
h^{\prime}
shares no order types with
g_{1}^{\prime},g_{2}^{\prime}
. Indeed,
h^{\prime}
shares no order types with
g_{1}^{\prime}
since all terms of
g_{1}^{\prime}
cancel in
h_{2}-g_{1}^{\prime}
and these are exactly the terms of
h_{2}
of the same order type with terms in
g_{1}^{\prime}
. Moreover,
h^{\prime}
shares no order types with
g_{2}^{\prime}
since the terms of
h^{\prime}
are a subset of the terms of
h_{2}
and
h_{2}
shares no order types with
g_{2}^{\prime}
.

Then,
f=g_{1}^{\prime}+g_{2}^{\prime}+h^{\prime}
.
Since
g_{1}({\bf 1}),g_{2}({\bf 1})\not=0
, we may deduce
g_{1}^{\prime}({\bf 1}),g_{2}^{\prime}({\bf 1})\not=0
by
3.4
.
We now apply
3.3
for
g=g_{1}\in(f)_{S_{d}}
based on the decomposition
f=g_{1}^{\prime}+g_{2}^{\prime}+h^{\prime}
. To verify the hypothesis of this lemma, note that
g_{1}
shares order types only with
g_{1}^{\prime}
, but not with
g_{2}^{\prime}
or
h^{\prime}
. This is true since the order types of
g_{2}^{\prime}
are precisely the order types of
g_{2}
and
g_{1}
shares no order types with
g_{2}
, while
h^{\prime}
shares no order types with
g_{1}^{\prime}
, as established above, but
g_{1}^{\prime}
has exactly the same order types as
g_{1}
. In view of this,
3.3
yields that
g_{2}^{\prime}({\bf 1})=0
and
h^{\prime}({\bf 1})=0
. The fomer identity provides the desired contradiction.
∎

4.
Monomial principal symmetric ideals

As a matter of terminology, a
monomial principal symmetric ideal
may be interpreted to be a psi that is also a monomial ideal or a psi generated by a monomial, that is, an ideal of the form
(m)_{S_{d}}
, where
m
is a monomial. We prove in
4.4
that these two potential interpretations describe the same class of ideals.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume
(f)_{S_{d}}
is a monomial ideal.
If
f
contains a term of order type
{\mathbf{a}}
, then

R_{\mathbf{a}}\subseteq(f)_{S_{d}}
.
If, in addition,
f
is strongly homogeneous, then
(R_{\mathbf{a}})=(f)_{S_{d}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f
contains a term which is a scalar multiple of a monomial
m
of order type
{\mathbf{a}}
and let
I=(f)_{S_{d}}
. Since
f\in I
and
I
is assumed to be a monomial ideal, we deduce
m\in I
. Since the ideal
I
is symmetric,
(m)_{S_{d}}\subseteq(f)_{S_{d}}
. Since the monomials of order type
{\mathbf{a}}
are a basis for
R_{\mathbf{a}}
and any monomial of order type
{\mathbf{a}}
can be expressed as a permutation of
m
, we conclude that
(R_{\mathbf{a}})=(m)_{S_{d}}\subseteq(f)_{S_{d}}
.

If
f
is strongly homogeneous order type
{\mathbf{a}}
, then all terms of
f
are in
R_{\mathbf{a}}
, therefore
f\in R_{\mathbf{a}}
and
(f)_{S_{d}}\subseteq(R_{\mathbf{a}})
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(f)_{S_{d}}
be a monomial ideal, where
f
is homogeneous.
Decompose
f
into a sum of strongly homogeneous components of pairwise distinct order types,
f=g_{1}+\cdots+g_{t}
.
Then, for each
i
,
(g_{i})_{S_{d}}
is a monomial ideal and
(g_{i})_{S_{d}}=(R_{\mathbf{a}})
for some vector
{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
that depends on
i
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{a}}
be the order type of
g_{i}
.
By
4.1
,

R_{\mathbf{a}}\subseteq(f)_{S_{d}}=(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{t})_{S_{d}}\subseteq(g_{1})_{S_{d}}+\cdots+(g_{t})_{S_{d}}.

Since
g_{i}
has a different order type than the other
g_{j}
’s,
R_{\mathbf{a}}\subseteq(g_{i})_{S_{d}}
.
Also, the minimal generators of
(g_{i})_{S_{d}}
form a subset of
R_{\mathbf{a}}
, so
(g_{i})_{S_{d}}=(R_{\mathbf{a}})
. Thus
(g_{i})_{S_{d}}
is a monomial ideal.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume
(f)_{S_{d}}
is a monomial ideal.
If
f
is homogeneous, then
f
is strongly homogeneous and
(f)_{S_{d}}=(m)_{S_{d}}
for some monomial
m
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that
f
contains monomial terms of different order types,
{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{a}}_{t}
say.
Then we can write
f=g_{1}+\cdots+g_{t}
, where each
g_{i}
is the sum of the terms of order type
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}
.

We show that

(4.1)

(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{t})_{S_{d}}=(f)_{S_{d}}=(g_{1})_{S_{d}}+\cdots+(g_{t})_{S_{d}}.

Clearly, one containment holds

(f)_{S_{d}}=(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{t})_{S_{d}}\subseteq(g_{1})_{S_{d}}+\cdots+(g_{t})_{S_{d}}.

By
4.2
we have
(g_{i})_{S_{d}}=(R_{{\mathbf{a}}_{i}})
and by
4.1
there are containments
R_{{\mathbf{a}}_{i}}\subseteq(f)_{S_{d}}
.
Hence, there is a containment

(R_{{\mathbf{a}}_{i}})+\cdots+(R_{{\mathbf{a}}_{t}})=(g_{1})_{S_{d}}+\cdots+(g_{t})_{S_{d}}\subseteq(f)_{S_{d}}=(g_{1}+\cdots+g_{t})_{S_{d}}.

Since every term of
f
is in
(R_{{\mathbf{a}}_{i}})+\cdots+(R_{{\mathbf{a}}_{t}})
, the claimed equality follows. In particular we see that
g_{i}\in(f)_{S_{d}}
for each
i
.

Since
g_{i}
is strongly homogeneous and
(g_{i})_{S_{d}}=(R_{{\mathbf{a}}_{i}})
,
3.2
yields that
g_{i}({\bf 1})\not=0
for all
1\leq i\leq t
. If
t>1
, then
3.6
contradicts the fact that
I
is a psi. Hence
t=1
and therefore
f
is strongly homogeneous.

∎

Corollary 4.4
.

A principal symmetric ideal
I
is a monomial ideal if and only if
I=(m)_{S_{d}}
for some monomial
m
.

Proof.

The forward direction follows from
4.3
. For the backward direction, note that all ideals
(m)_{S_{d}}
, where
m
is any monomial, are both psi’s and monomial ideals.
∎

5.
Products and powers of principal symmetric ideals

Simple examples show that powers, and hence products, of psi’s need not be psi’s.

Example 5.1
.

Consider the ideal
(x_{1})_{S_{2}}=(x_{1},x_{2})
. Its square
I^{2}=(x_{1}^{2},x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}^{2})
cannot be a psi in
{\sf k}[x_{1},x_{2}]
since it has three minimal generators whereas a psi with respect to the action of the symmetric group
S_{2}
has at most two generators by
3.1
.

A contrasting behavior is offered by the following family of examples.

Example 5.2
.

Suppose that
f
is a
{\sf k}
-symmetric polynomial and
g
is arbitrary. Then
(f)_{S_{d}}(g)_{S_{d}}=(fg)_{S_{d}}
and
\left((f)_{S_{d}}\right)^{n}=(f^{n})_{S_{d}}
are principal symmetric ideals

In view of
5.2
one may wonder whether this class of examples characterizes the affirmative answer to
1.2
. In
5.4
,
5.5
,
5.7
, and
5.9
we confirm that this is indeed the case.

We start with a result on the number of generators of a product of psi’s of importance to the future developments in this section.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
I=(f)_{S_{d}}
be a principal symmetric ideal and let
J=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
be a homogeneous ideal generated in a single degree. Assume that
f
is not
{\sf k}
-symmetric.
Then

\mu(IJ)\geq\mu(J)+1.

In particular, if
I,J
are principal symmetric ideals neither of which is generated by a
{\sf k}
-symmetric polynomial, then
\mu(IJ)\geq\max\{\mu(I),\mu(J)\}+1
.

Proof.

We may assume that
f
has no
{\sf k}
-symmetric divisor and that the polynomials
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
have no non-constant common divisor since dividing by such divisors does not change any of the quantities involved. Then there is an irreducible factor of

, call it
p
, that does not divide all polynomials
\sigma\cdot f
. Indeed, if
p\mid\sigma\cdot f
for all
\sigma\in S_{d}
then
\sigma^{-1}\cdot p\mid f
and therefore
\left(\prod_{\sigma\in S_{d}}\sigma\cdot p\right)\mid f
. Since the product
\prod_{\sigma\in S_{d}}\sigma\cdot p
is
{\sf k}
-invariant we have obtained a contradiction.

Pick
\sigma\in S_{d}
so that
p\nmid\sigma\cdot f
. Let
U
be the span of the minimal generators of
IJ
and let
V
be the span of the minimal generators of
J
. Let
h=\sigma\cdot f
. Then
U
contains
fV+hV
and consequently we have

\displaystyle\mu(IJ)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\sf k}U

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{\sf k}(fV+hV)

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{\sf k}(fV)+\dim_{\sf k}(hV)-\dim_{\sf k}(fV\cap hV)

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{\sf k}(fV)+1=\dim_{\sf k}(V)+1

\displaystyle=\mu(J)+1.

The only inequality that requires explanation is
\dim_{\sf k}(hV)-\dim_{\sf k}(fV\cap hV)\geq 1
, equivalently
\dim_{\sf k}(hV)>\dim_{\sf k}(fV\cap hV)
or

. Assume towards a contradiction that

. Then each element of

is in
fV
. For example,
hg_{i}\in fV
for each

, which yields that
f
, and therefore also
p
, divides
hg_{i}
for each

. However,
p
is coprime to

by assumption, so
p
divides each

. This contradicts the assumption that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
have no non-constant common divisor, thus proving the claim.
∎

The following theorem answers

Theorem 5.4
.

Let

be monomial principal symmetric ideals.
Then

Proof.

\Leftarrow:
follows from

\Rightarrow:

Suppose that

Let

By assumption,

Without loss of generality assume that

We can write

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\underbrace{a_{1},\dots,a_{1}}_{\mbox{\small$i-1$ times}},a_{i},\dots)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\underbrace{b_{1},\dots,b_{1}}_{\mbox{\small$j-1$ times}},b_{j},\dots)

\displaystyle(1\,j)\cdot{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b_{j},\underbrace{b_{1},\dots,b_{1}}_{\mbox{\small$j-1$ times}},\dots).

Definition 5.11
.

For

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
I=(f)_{S_{d}}
and
J=(g)_{S_{d}}
.
If

Proof.

By definition one has
IJ=((\sigma\cdot f)(\tau\cdot g):\sigma,\tau\in S_{d})

For each
\sigma\cdot(fg)
in the generating set of

.
Thus,
\sigma\cdot(fg)\in IJ

(fg)_{S_{d}}\subseteq IJ
.

Given

\sigma^{-1}\tau\in\operatorname{Stab}_{f}\operatorname{Stab}_{g}
, so
\sigma^{-1}\tau=\gamma^{-1}\delta^{-1}
for some

and

.
Hence,
\iota=\gamma\sigma^{-1}\tau\delta
.
By definition of
\operatorname{Stab}_{f}
and
\operatorname{Stab}_{g}
,
c_{1}(\sigma\gamma^{-1})\cdot f=\sigma\cdot f
and

.
Therefore,

\displaystyle(\sigma\cdot f)(\tau\cdot g)

\displaystyle=(c_{1}(\sigma\gamma^{-1})\cdot f)(c_{2}(\tau\delta)\cdot g)

\displaystyle=c_{1}c_{2}(\sigma\gamma^{-1}\cdot f)(\tau\delta\cdot g)

\displaystyle=c_{1}c_{2}(\sigma\gamma^{-1}\gamma\sigma^{-1})\cdot\left((\sigma\gamma^{-1}\cdot f)(\tau\delta\cdot g)\right)

\displaystyle=c_{1}c_{2}(\sigma\gamma^{-1})\cdot\left((\gamma\sigma^{-1}\sigma\gamma^{-1}\cdot f)(\gamma\sigma^{-1}\tau\delta\cdot g)\right)

\displaystyle=c_{1}c_{2}(\sigma\gamma^{-1}\cdot(f(\gamma\sigma^{-1}\tau\delta\cdot g)))

\displaystyle=c_{1}c_{2}(\sigma\gamma^{-1}\cdot(fg))\qquad\text{(since }\gamma\sigma^{-1}\tau\delta=\iota).

.

Corollary 5.13
.

Let
I=(f)_{S_{d}}
and
J=(g)_{S_{d}}
.
If
\operatorname{Stab}_{f}=A_{d}
(the alternating group) and

Proof.

Note that
\operatorname{Stab}_{f}\operatorname{Stab}_{g}=\{\sigma\tau:\sigma\in S_{f},\tau\in S_{g}\}
.
Furthermore, we show that all such products are distinct.
Suppose that
\sigma\tau=\sigma^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}
for some

and
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Stab}_{g}
.
If

6.
Hilbert functions of principal symmetric ideals

A symmetric monomial ideal is a monomial ideal
J
that is closed under the action of the symmetric group, that is,
\sigma\cdot m\in J
for any
m\in J
and
\sigma\in S_{d}
. In this section we consider
1.3
which aims to compare the collection of Hilbert functions of psi’s to that of symmetric monomial ideals (not necessarily monomial psi’s). We are not aware of a characterization of all Hilbert functions attained by psi’s. However, in
[
HSS
]
the Hilbert function of “most” psi’s is determined. We now review the relevant facts.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A parameter space for the set of principal symmetric ideals generated in degree
n
is
{\mathbb{P}}^{N-1}
, where
N=\binom{n+d-1}{n}
.
Indeed, let
M_{n}
denote the set of monomials of degree
n
in
R={\sf k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
listed as
M_{n}=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N}\}
in an arbitrary order.
Points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N-1}
parametrize principal symmetric ideals generated in degree
d
via the assignment

\displaystyle\Phi:{\mathbb{P}}^{N-1}\to\text{ principal symmetric ideals}

\displaystyle\Phi(c_{1}:\cdots:c_{N}):=(f_{c})_{S_{n}}\,,\qquad\text{where}\quad f_{c}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}m_{i}\,.

The map
\Phi
above is onto, but not one-to-one. It allows us to formulate a notion of a general principal symmetric ideal, which we now formalize.
We will say that
a general principal symmetric ideal generated in degree
d
satisfies property
\mathcal{P}
if there exists a non-empty Zariski-open set
U
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
so that for each
c\in U
, the principal symmetric ideal
\Phi(c)=(f_{c})_{S_{n}}
satisfies the property
\mathcal{P}
. The following was shown in
[
HSS
]
:

Theorem 6.1

(Partial statement of
[
HSS
, Theorem 8.4]
)
.

Suppose
{\rm char}({\sf k})=0
and fix an integer
n\geq 2
.
1
1

1

Note that the meanings of
n
and
d
in this reference are interchanged compared to our notation here.
For sufficiently large

, a general principal symmetric ideal

of

generated in degree

H_{R/I}(i):=\dim_{\sf k}(R/I)_{i}=\begin{cases}\dim_{\sf k}R_{i}&\text{if $i\leq d-1$}\\
P(n)-1&\text{if $i=d$}\\

In the following we will refer to a psi

such that

as general provided that it satisfies the conclusion of

where
C=\left(\text{sRed}(f),\sigma\cdot\text{sRed}(f)\right)
is generated by a regular sequence. The isomorphism provides the equality
H_{I}(n)=H_{C}(n-k+\ell)
and the Hilbert function of
C
is obtained from the Koszul complex

0\to R(-2\ell)\to R^{2}(-\ell)\to C\to 0

which yields
H_{C}(n)=2H_{R}(n-\ell)-H_{R}(n-2\ell)
. This establishes the claimed formula in the case
\ell>0
.

Assume now that
f
is
{\sf k}
-symmetric, therefore
\ell=0
. Then, as
(f)_{S_{2}}=(f)
is a principal ideal in the usual sense we have
H_{(f)_{S_{2}}}(n)=H_{R}(n-k)
. This agrees with the claim.
∎

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
f\in{\sf k}[x_{1},x_{2}]
be homogeneous, let
\ell=\deg(\text{\rm sRed}(f))
and
k=\deg(f)
. There exists a monomial symmetric ideal
J
, such that
H_{(f)_{S_{2}}}(n)=H_{J}(n)
if and only if
k+\ell
is even.

Proof.

Set
I=(f)_{S_{2}}
.
From
6.4
applied in degree
n>k+\ell
with
n
odd we obtain

H_{I}(n)=2H_{R}(n-k)-(n-k-\ell+1)\equiv n-k-\ell+1\pmod{2}.

Suppose that
J
is a symmetric monomial ideal such that
H_{(f)_{S_{2}}}(n)=H_{J}(n)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Fix
n
to be an odd integer. Then since
d=2
and there are no partitions
{\mathbf{a}}
of an odd integer
n
with two equal parts we see by
Equation

2.1
that
\dim_{\sf k}R_{\mathbf{a}}=2
for all
{\mathbf{a}}
with
|{\mathbf{a}}|=n
. Consequently by
Equation

6.1
,
H_{J}(n)
is even. Since
H_{J}(n)=H_{I}(n)\equiv k+\ell\pmod{2}
we conclude that
k+\ell
must be even.

Conversely, assume
k+\ell
is even, set
a=\frac{k-\ell}{2}
and
g=x_{1}^{a+\ell}x_{2}^{a}
. By
6.4
the Hilbert function of
J=(g)_{S_{2}}
is uniquely determined by
\deg(\text{sRed}(g))
and
\deg(g)
. Since
\text{sRed}(g)=x_{1}^{\ell}
, which has degree
\ell
and since
\deg(g)=k=\deg(f)
we obtain
H_{(g)_{S_{2}}}(n)=H_{I}(n)
.

∎

7.
Open problems

Satisfactory partial answers to
1.1
and
1.2
of the introduction have been provided in this manuscript, however these questions are still open in full generality. We single out a few additional questions which result from our work.

In view of
5.8
, where the polynomial
x_{1}-x_{2}
symmetrically generating the relevant ideal is
{\sf k}
-invariant under the action of the subgroup
S_{2}
of
S_{d}
we ask:

Question 7.1
.

Is it true that if
I
and
I^{2}
are principal symmetric ideals, then
I=(f)_{S_{d}}
for some polynomial
f
which is
{\sf k}
-invariant under the action of a symmetric subgroup of
S_{d}
?

We remark that
[
HSS
, Lemma 5.1]
and
6.1
give an upper bound on the minimal number of generators of any principal symmetric ideal
I
generated by a polynomial of degree
n
in the ring
{\sf k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
with
d
sufficiently large, namely

\mu(I)\leq\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}-P(n)+1.

On the other hand several of our results proceed by way of estimating
lower bounds
on the number of minimal generators of various ideals as well as their products and powers; see for example
5.3
. It is therefore of interest to continue the search for such bounds.

Problem 7.2
.

Find (nontrivial) upper and lower bounds for the number of minimal generators of the product of two homogeneous principal symmetric ideals.

Find (nontrivial) upper and lower bounds for the number of minimal generators of the powers of a homogeneous principal symmetric ideal.

Finally,
6.2
opens up the search for a non-monomial ideal analogue of Macaulay’s theorem in the category of homogeneous symmetric ideals. Evidence from
[
HSS
]
suggests that binomial ideals could replace monomial ideals in the desired result.

Question 7.3
.

If
{\sf k}
is a field of characteristic zero and
I
is a homogeneous symmetric ideal of
{\sf k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
that does not have
{\sf k}
-symmetric polynomials in any set of minimal generators, does there exists a symmetric ideal
J
, generated by monomials and binomials, so that
H_{I}(n)=H_{J}(n)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
?

The answer to
7.3
is affirmative if
I
is a general psi by
[
HSS
, Proposition 6.12]
and
6.1
. The answer is also affirmative in two-dimensional polynomial rings without any restriction on the generators of
I
, as we show next.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
{\sf k}
be a field of characteristic different from two and let
f\in{\sf k}[x_{1},x_{2}]
be a homogeneous polynomial. Then there exists a symmetric ideal
J
generated by binomials or monomials such that
H_{J}(n)=H_{(f)_{S_{2}}}(n)

Proof.

Let
\ell=\deg(\text{sRed}(f))
,
k=\deg f\geq\ell
. Consider the binomial

g=x_{1}^{\ell}x_{2}^{k-\ell}+x_{1}^{k}=x_{1}^{\ell}(x_{2}^{k-\ell}+x_{1}^{k-\ell}).

Thus
\deg(g)=k
and since
\deg(\text{sRed}(g))=\ell
,
6.4
gives that
H_{(f)_{S_{2}}}(n)=H_{(g)_{S_{2}}}(n)
.
∎
