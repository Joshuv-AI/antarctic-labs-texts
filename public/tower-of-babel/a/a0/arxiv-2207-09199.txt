Abstract
. We investigate a variety of cut and choose games, their relationship with (generic) large cardinals, and show that they can be used to characterize a number of properties of ideals and of partial orders: certain notions of distributivity, strategic closure, and precipitousness.

Fundamental games related to large cardinals are cut and choose games that were first introduced and studied by Fred Galvin, Jan Mycielski and Stanisław Ulam in the 1960s (see

In this paper, we survey and extend results on these games and some of their variants, their connections with generic large cardinals and properties of ideals, in particular with precipitousness and distributivity.

We start by introducing what seems to be perhaps the most standard notion of asymmetric cut and choose game.

Definition 1.1
.

Let

Remark 1.3

If there is some cardinal

Various other interesting classes of games can be obtained from the above cut and choose games by the following adjustments,
several of which have been studied in the set theoretic literature before.

(1)

Winning conditions:

(a)

Final requirements.

Instead of the requirement that the final intersection cannot have size at most
1
, this should hold in each round while the final intersection is only required to be nonempty.
This is the weakest possible cut and choose game in the sense that it is easiest for
Choose
to win.
We study this variant in Section
2

(b)

Notions of smallness.

The family of subsets of
\kappa
of size at most
1
is replaced by an arbitrary monotone family, i.e. a family of subsets of
\kappa
that is closed under subsets.
A canonical choice is the bounded ideal
\bd_{\kappa}
on
\kappa

{<}\kappa
-closed ideals on
\kappa
that extend
\bd_{\kappa}
. We study such generalizations in Section

(2)

Types of moves:

(a)

Partitions.

Each move of
Cut
is a partition of
\kappa
into a number of pieces which are disjoint only modulo a
{<}\kappa
-closed ideal
I
in Section
5

This leads to characterizations of various notions of distributivity of
I
in Section
6
and precipitousness of
I
in Section
7

(b)

Poset games.

The moves of

are maximal antichains in a poset, of which

picks one element. In order for

to win, their choices need to have lower bounds in the poset.
This is used in Section

to characterize notions of distributivity.

2.
The weakest cut and choose game

Regarding Definition
1.1
, it may seem somewhat odd to require
two
elements in the final intersection of choices in order for
Choose
to win games of the form
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,\gamma)
. But note that if we required only
one
element in this intersection, then
Choose
easily wins any of these games by fixing some ordinal
\alpha<\kappa
in advance, and then simply picking the set that contains
\alpha
as an element in each of their moves, for this
\alpha
will then clearly be contained as an element in the final intersection of their choices as well. By requiring
two
elements in the final intersection of their choices, this strategy is not applicable as soon as
Cut
plays a partition of the form
\langle\{\alpha\},Y\rangle
.

In this section, we will be considering canonical variants of the games
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,\gamma)
. Among the cut and choose games of length
\gamma
that we consider in this paper, these are the easiest for
Choose
to win. They have (or rather, an equivalent form of them has) already been considered in unpublished work of Galvin (see
[
20
, Section 3, A game of Galvin]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\kappa
be a regular uncountable cardinal, and let
\gamma\leq\kappa
be a limit ordinal. Let
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,{\leq}\gamma)
denote the following game of length (at most)
\gamma
on the cardinal
\kappa
. As in the game
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,\gamma)
, starting with all of
\kappa
, players
Cut
and
Choose
take turns, with
Cut
dividing a given subset of
\kappa
in two, and
Choose
picking one of the pieces and returning it to
Cut
for their next move.
Cut
wins and the game immediately ends if
Choose
ever picks a singleton. At limit stages, intersections are taken. If the game lasts for
\gamma
-many stages,
Choose
wins in case the final intersection of their choices is nonempty. Otherwise,
Cut
wins.

Note that unlike in the games
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,\gamma)
, fixing one element
\alpha\in\kappa
at the beginning of the game, and picking the set which contains
\alpha
as an element in each of their moves is not a winning strategy in the games
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,{\leq}\gamma)
, since
Cut
can play a partition of the form
\langle\{\alpha\},X\rangle
at some point, so that
Choose
would pick
\{\alpha\}
, and would thus immediately lose such a run.

The games
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,{\leq}\gamma)
behave somewhat differently with respect to the existence of winning strategies for
Cut
. At least the forward direction in the following observation is attributed to unpublished work of Galvin, and independently to Hechler in
[
20
]
. We do not know of any published proof of this result. For arbitrary ordinals
\gamma
, we let
2^{<\gamma}=\sup\{2^{\delta}\mid\delta<\gamma
is a cardinal
\}
.

Observation 2.2
.

If
\kappa
is a regular uncountable cardinal and
\gamma<\kappa
is a limit ordinal, then
Cut
has a winning strategy in the game
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,{\leq}\gamma)
if and only if
\kappa\leq 2^{<\gamma}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
Cut
has a winning strategy
\sigma
in the game
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,{\leq}\gamma)
. The strategy
\sigma
can be identified with a binary tree
T
of height
\gamma
, where the root of the tree is labelled with
\kappa
, and if a node of the tree is labelled with a set
X
which is not a singleton, then its immediate successor nodes are labelled with the sets from the partition that is the response of
\sigma
to the sequence of cuts and choices leading up to the choice of
X
, and limit nodes are labelled with the intersection of the labels of their predecessors.
\sigma
being a winning strategy means that the intersection of labels along any branch of
T
of length
\gamma
is empty. Thus, for each ordinal
\alpha<\kappa
there has to be a node labelled with
\{\alpha\}
, for this is the only reason why

would not appear in an intersection of choices along some branch of

2^{<\gamma}
-many nodes in this tree, hence
\kappa\leq 2^{<\gamma}
.

Now assume that
\kappa\leq 2^{<\gamma}
. Let
X\subseteq{}^{\gamma}2
be such that for

, we have
y\in X
if and only if there is
\alpha<\gamma
such that for all
\beta<\gamma
,

•

y(2\cdot\beta)=1\,\leftrightarrow\,\beta\geq\alpha
and

•

\beta\geq\alpha\,\to\,y(2\cdot\beta+1)=1
.

By our assumption on
\kappa
, we may identify
\kappa
with a subset
Y
of the space

. The winning strategy for
Cut
is to increasingly partition the space

in
\gamma
-many steps using sets of the form

for functions

with increasingly large

. That is, during a run of
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,{\leq}\gamma)
,

and

work towards constructing a function

, the only possible element of the intersection of all choices of

, fixing one digit in each round of the game. Now in any even round,

cannot possibly pick the digit

, for this would correspond to picking a set
[f]\cap Y
that is only a singleton, by the definition of the set

. But this means that either

already wins at some stage less than
\gamma
, or that
F\colon\gamma\to 2
is not an element of

. But this again means that

wins, for it implies that the intersection of all choices of

is in fact empty.
∎

Choose
having a winning strategy in
\mathcal{U}(\kappa,{\leq}\omega)
for some cardinal

has the consistency strength of a measurable cardinal. A slightly weaker version of this result for the game

with essentially the same proof, that is due to Silver and Solovay, appears in
