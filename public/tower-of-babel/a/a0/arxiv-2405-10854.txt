Strong log-convexity of genus sequences
Bojan Mohar

Abstract
For a graph G G , and a nonnegative integer g g , let a g ​ ( G ) a_{g}(G) be the number of 2 2 -cell embeddings of G G in an orientable surface of genus g g (counted up to the combinatorial homeomorphism equivalence). In 1989, Gross, Robbins, and Tucker [ 21 ] proposed a conjecture that the sequence a 0 ​ ( G ) , a 1 ​ ( G ) , a 2 ​ ( G ) , … a_{0}(G),a_{1}(G),a_{2}(G),\dots is log-concave for every graph G G . This conjecture is reminiscent to the Heron–Rota–Welsh Log Concavity Conjecture that was recently resolved in the affirmative by June Huh et al. [ 24 , 1 ] , except that it is closer to the notion of Δ \Delta -matroids than to the usual matroids. In this short paper, we disprove the Log Concavity Conjecture of Gross, Robbins, and Tucker by providing examples that show strong deviation from log-concavity at multiple terms of their genus sequences.

2
Multiple log-convexity of genus sequences is viable

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of non-negative numbers. The
k
th term
a_{k}
(
k\geq 1
) of this sequence is said to be
log-concave
if
a_{k}^{2}\geq a_{k-1}a_{k+1}
, and it is
log-convex
if
a_{k}^{2}\leq a_{k-1}a_{k+1}
. If the corresponding inequality is strict, we say that the term is
strictly log-concave
or
strictly log-convex
, respectively. The sequence is
log-concave
if all of its terms are log-concave. These definitions extend to any finite sequence, which can be viewed as an infinite sequence with zeros appended at the end to make it infinite.

The afore-mentioned LCGD Conjecture
1
of Gross, Robbins, and Tucker
[
21
]
states that the genus sequence of every graph is log-concave. In this section we refute this long-standing open problem by proving that there are graphs whose genus sequence has many strictly log-convex terms.

Theorem 3
.

For every integer
g\geq 0
and every

there is a
4
-connected graph
G_{g,k}
of genus
g
such that the terms
a_{g+1},a_{g+3},a_{g+5},\dots,a_{g+2k-1}
of its genus sequence are all strictly log-convex.

In the proof of this result we will need several properties about combinatorics of 2-cell embeddings.

An embedding of a graph in a surface
S
is a
2
-cell embedding
if each face of the embedding is homeomorphic to an open disk.

Suppose that a graph
G
is 2-cell embedded in a closed (orientable) surface
S
of genus
g
.
A closed walk or a cycle in
G
that bounds a face in this embedding is said to be a
facial walk
or

. A cycle
C
of

is
contractible
if it is contractible viewed as a curve on the surface. This is the same as saying that
C
splits the surface into two components, one of which is a disk. More generally,
C
is
surface separating
if it splits the surface into two components.

Combinatorially,

is a
non-separating cycle
of the graph
G
if the subgraph
G-V(C)
is connected; the cycle

is an
induced cycle
of
G
if it is induced as the subgraph of

, i.e. it has no chords in
G
. Realizing that facial cycles of 3-connected plane graphs (i.e. graphs of 3-dimensional convex polyhedra) are induced and non-separating, Tutte
[
44
]
called such cycles
peripheral
. Due to this relationship, we say that a 2-cell embedding of a graph in a surface is a
polyhedral embedding
if every face is bounded by a peripheral cycle.

If a peripheral cycle in a 2-cell embedded graph is surface separating, then it must be facial, see
[
35
]
for more details. For further use we state this simple fact as a lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
C
is a peripheral cycle of a graph
G
that is
2
-cell embedded in a surface
S
. If
C
is not facial, then it is surface non-separating.

More generally, we say that a family
\mathcal{C}
of peripheral cycles in
G
is
peripheral
if any two cycles in the family are disjoint and nonadjacent (i.e., no two vertices on distinct cycles are adjacent) and
G-\cup\{V(C)\mid C\in\mathcal{C}\}
is connected.

The following result is a simple generalization of Lemma
4
.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is a peripheral family of cycles of a graph
G
that is
2
-cell embedded in a surface
S
of genus
g
. If none of the cycles in
\mathcal{C}
is facial, then
|\mathcal{C}|\leq g
.

Proof.

Each cycle in
\mathcal{C}
is surface nonseparating and has a neighbor on each side (otherwise it would be facial). Those neighbors are not on any of the other cycles in the family. This implies that the union of all cycles in
\mathcal{C}
is surface non-separating. Consequently, repeatedly cutting the surface along any cycle in
\mathcal{C}
decreases the genus by
1
(easily seen by using Euler characteristic computation), and hence the number of cycles is at most
g
.
∎

Two facial walks
W_{1},W_{2}
are said to be

if there is a facial walk
W
that contains a vertex in
W_{1}
and a vertex in
W_{2}
. In particular, two facial walks that intersect are cofacial. The facial walk

is said to be a
cofacial connector
for
W_{1}
(and at the same time also for
W_{2}
). Given a set
\mathcal{C}
of facial cycles, and

, we denote by

the number of different cofacial connectors for

is

for each

.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
G
is a 3-connected graph with a polyhedral embedding in some surface and
\mathcal{C}
is a sparse collection of its facial cycles. Suppose that
G
has another embedding
\Pi
in which none of the cycles in
\mathcal{C}
is facial. Then the genus of the embedding

is at least
|\mathcal{C}|
.

Proof.

Let us first observe that the cycles in
\mathcal{C}
are peripheral since they are faces of a polyhedral embedding. Moreover, sparsity implies that cycles in
\mathcal{C}
are pairwise disjoint and pairwise nonadjacent.
The basic property used in the proof will be the following. For
C\in\mathcal{C}
, let

be the set of vertices that are not on

but have a neighbor on
C
, and let
W(C)
be the set of vertices that are cofacial with a vertex on
C
but do not belong to
\cup\mathcal{C}
. The vertices in

are ordered clockwise as
u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{p}
, and each

is connected with

via part

of a facial walk that does not intersect

. Since

is sparse, we may assume that the facial walks

(except possibly for

) are disjoint from

. This shows that for every

connecting them.

Each cycle in
\mathcal{C}
is surface nonseparating in

. Moreover, the family
\mathcal{C}
is peripheral. To see this, it is clear that

is induced. To show that it is nonseparating, take vertices

and consider a path from

but other vertices on

, then we can replace

connecting them (as proved above). By repeating this for all such subpaths

The proof is now easily concluded by applying Lemma

∎

edges connecting
W_{i}
and
W_{i}^{\prime}
can stay where it is or it can be flipped and reembedded into
F
, giving
2^{2d}
different choices. By doing this for
i=1,2,3
and for each of the
q
chosen connectors, we get
2^{6dq}
different embeddings, all of genus
g+2q
. This proves the claim when
r
is even.

If
r
is odd, we select one of the remaining connecting triples and in that case flip the first edge of just one of the connectors. This will further increase the genus by one. In the same way as above we see that we can independently reembed each of the
2d
edges between
W_{1}
and
W_{1}^{\prime}
into the new big face
F
which has
W_{1}
and
W_{1}^{\prime}
on its boundary traversed in the opposite directions. This gives additional
2^{2d}
possible choices to each of the previously described embeddings of genus
g+r-1
.
∎

The next claim gives an upper bound on
a_{h}(G_{g,k})
, where we will not try to optimize the bound.

Claim 2
.

Suppose that
1\leq r\leq 2k
. Let
q:=\lfloor r/2\rfloor
and
\nu=\nu(g,k)
. Then we have:

a_{g+r}(G_{g,k})\leq\left((5!)^{31}\nu\right)^{g+2k}\cdot\left(108d\right)^{36k^{2}}\cdot 2^{2d(q+r)}.

Proof.

We split the vertices of
G=G_{g,k}
into four sets,
V_{A},V_{B},V_{C},V_{D}
, which are defined as follows. First,
V_{D}
contains for each of the
3k
connectors the six vertices in the two triangles containing the first and the last edge of the connector (respectively) and the vertex adjacent to that edge but outside of the connector; the set
V_{C}
contains the remaining
2d-2
vertices on each of the connectors; the set
V_{B}
contains all vertices that are incident to any of the nontriangular faces and are not in
V_{C}\cup V_{D}
; lastly,
V_{A}
contains all remaining vertices.
Note that
|V_{D}|=18k
,
|V_{C}|=6k(d-1)
,
|V_{B}|=2k(m-6)
, and
|V_{A}|=\nu(g,k)-2k(m+3)
.

Suppose that
\Pi
is a 2-cell embedding of
G
whose genus is (at most)
g+r
. We will compare which facial triangles under
\Pi_{0}
are no longer facial under
\Pi
. We say that a
\Pi_{0}
-facial triangle is
unstable
if it is not
\Pi
-facial; otherwise it is
stable
. We say that a vertex
v\in V(G)
is
unstable
if it is contained in an unstable triangle. In the first part of the proof, we will show that there are not too many unstable vertices in
V_{A}\cup V_{B}\cup V_{D}
. By “guessing” which vertices in
V_{A}\cup V_{B}\cup V_{D}
are unstable, we get a bound on the number of possible embeddings
\Pi
having the guessed unstable set. On the other hand, the number of unstable vertices in
V_{C}
can be much larger. For the number of rotation systems on the connectors we will need more specific homotopy arguments.

Let us first consider the set
U
of all unstable vertices in
V_{A}\cup V_{B}
. Let
U^{\prime}
be the largest set of unstable triangles, each of which containing at least one vertex in
V_{A}
and such that any two triangles in
U^{\prime}
are at distance at least 2 in
G
. It is easy to see that the only possible cofacial connector of any two triangles in
U^{\prime}
is one of the long faces and such a long face is unique for each triangle in
U^{\prime}
. (Note that the possible exceptions to this property would be the triangles with their vertices in
V_{C}\cup V_{D}
. This was actually the reason to define
V_{D}
.) Consequently, the set
U^{\prime}
is sparse, and Lemma
6
shows that

|U^{\prime}|\leq g+r.

By maximality of
U^{\prime}
, each vertex in
U
is at distance at most 2 from
U^{\prime}
. Since
G
has maximum degree 7 and vertices of degree 7 are at distance at least 4 from each other, it is easy to see that

|U|\leq 30|U^{\prime}|.

Among the 30 or fewer vertices at distance at most 2 from a triangle in
U^{\prime}
, there is at most one of degree 7 (by property (d)), and thus they give fewer than
6!(5!)^{29}
different choices for rotations around these vertices. This “overcount” also counts possibilities that stable triangles can have two orientations as the faces in
\Pi
.
This yields that there are less than

\binom{\nu(g,k)}{|U^{\prime}|}\cdot\left(6\cdot 6!\cdot(5!)^{29}\right)^{|U^{\prime}|}

ways to select rotations around vertices in
V_{A}\cup V_{B}
. Similarly, vertices in
V_{D}
have at most
(5!)^{18k}
ways to choose local rotations around them.

To count the number of ways to change the local rotations around vertices in
V_{C}
and still have genus at most
g+r
, we first note that the above bounds and properties (e) and (f) show that the genus of the subgraph at distance
f(g,k)
from the long faces of
G
is at least
g
. Thus we can afford to increase the genus at most by
r
when changing the rotation around the vertices on the connectors.

Let us now consider one of the connectors. We will use the notation shown on Fig.
4
, where the connector is drawn horizontally. Starting on the left with triangle
T_{1}
, we find the first unstable triangle
T_{i_{1}}
. Suppose that there is another unstable triangle
T_{j}
that is homotopic to
T_{i_{1}}
under the embedding
\Pi
. (Here the homotopy is free homotopy among some orientation of two triangles.) We let
T_{i_{2}}
be the homotopic triangle with largest possible
i_{2}
, possibly
i_{2}=i_{1}
. Since the two homotopic noncontractible triangles bound a cylinder, any triangle
T_{j}
with
i_{1}\leq j\leq i_{2}
is either contractible or homotopic to
T_{i_{1}}
. Then we look further and find the smallest
i_{3}\geq i_{2}+3
such that
T_{i_{3}}
is unstable. That unstable triangle is disjoint from
T_{i_{1}}
and
T_{i_{2}}
. If
i_{3}
exists, then we define
i_{4}
and
T_{i_{4}}
in the same way as we did to obtain
i_{2}
after having fixed
T_{i_{1}}
. Proceeding, we obtain pairs
(i_{1},i_{2}),(i_{3},i_{4}),\dots,(i_{2t-1},i_{2t})
. The triangles
T_{i_{1}},T_{i_{3}},\dots,T_{i_{2t-1}}
are pairwise disjoint and nonhomotopic, and they increase the genus at most by
r
. Thus, by Lemma
7
,
t\leq 3r-2
.

The above paragraph shows that we can determine the number of possibilities of different local rotations on one of the connectors by first finding
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{2t-1},i_{2t}
(less than
(2d)^{6r-4}

T_{i_{2j-1}}

has two choices for joining the two paths on the connector, which amounts all together to at most

T_{i_{2j}}
and
T_{i_{2j+1}}

stated above.

Without trying to optimize and use the same bounds on other connectors, we take it from the proof of Claim

that at most

All over, we obtain the upper bound of

\displaystyle\binom{\nu}{|U^{\prime}|}\cdot\left(6\cdot 6!\cdot(5!)^{29}\right)^{|U^{\prime}|}

\displaystyle\cdot(5!)^{18k}\cdot 2^{3k}\Bigl(2^{2d}\bigl(2d\cdot 3^{3}\bigr)^{6r-4}\Bigl)^{q+r}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left((5!)^{31}\nu\right)^{g+2k}\cdot\left(108d\right)^{36k^{2}}\cdot 2^{2d(q+r)},

3
Many consecutive log-convex terms

One obvious question is whether having only every second term being strongly log-convex is necessary. Our examples can be generalized to obtain the following result.

Theorem 9
.

For every
g\geq 0
and
k\geq 1
, there exists a
4
-connected graph of genus
g
, whose genus distribution sequence has
k
consecutive terms that are strongly log-convex.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as that of Theorem
3
and we do not give all details here. However, we provide the basic idea.

The graphs for this theorem are essentially the same as those in the proof of Theorem
3
. The graphs
G_{g,k}
have
k
connecting parts, in each of which three connectors join one stacked antiprism with another one. We replace the
j
th triple of connectors with
k+j
connectors (
1\leq j\leq k
). We also make their lengths slightly different, the lengths increasing with
j
(this will make the proof work). For some very large
d
, we let the length of all these
q=k+j
connectors be
c_{q}d
, where

c_{q}=\frac{q-1}{q}+\frac{q}{4k^{2}},\qquad k\leq q\leq 2k.

Note that
c_{k}=1-\tfrac{3}{4k}<c_{k+1}<\cdots<c_{2k}=1
and that the function
r(q)=q.c_{q}
is strictly convex:

2qc_{q}<(q-1)c_{q-1}+(q+1)c_{q+1}.

(4)

Details are left to the reader.

The genus coefficients
a_{g},a_{g+1},\dots,a_{g+k-1}
grow “roughly” with the factor of
2^{(1+o_{d}(1))d}
, i.e.

a_{g+t}=2^{(1+o_{d}(1))dt}

for
1\leq t<k
. This growth is realized by joining
t+1
large faces of the last connectors where we have
2k+1
connectors of height
c_{2k}d=d
.
However,
a_{g+k},a_{g+k+1},\dots,a_{g+2k}
grow exponentially faster. We claim that

a_{g+k+t}=2^{c_{k+t+1}d(k+t+1)(1+o_{d}(1))}

(5)

for
0\leq t\leq k
. To see that
a_{g+k+t}\geq 2^{c_{k+t+1}d(k+t+1)}
, we use a similar proof as for Claim
1
. With
k+t+1
connectors of height
c_{k+t+1}d
we increase genus
g
to
g+k+t
to obtain one face where the sides of these
k+t+1
connectors appear as in the mentioned proof, yielding
2^{c_{k+t+1}d(k+t+1)}
different embeddings of the same genus.

To prove that the right hand side of (
5
) is an upper bound, we have to prove that using the increase of the genus by
k+t
as in the lower bound construction above is best possible. To show this we have to argue that merging
k+t+1
big faces with the highest connectors of height
c_{k+t+1}d
gives much smaller numbers, and that using the gain where we merge
k+s
faces of height
c_{k+s}d
and adding the rest of the genus by merging
t-s+1
faces with the connectors of height
c_{2k}d
also gives lower numbers. This comparison reduces to the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle c_{k+t+1}(k+t+1)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle c_{2k}(k+t)\quad\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle c_{k+t+1}(k+t+1)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle c_{k+s+1}(k+s)+c_{2k}(t-s),\quad 0\leq s<t.

Using the fact that
c_{2k}=1
, they are both easy to verify and details are left to the reader.

Finally, (
5
) implies that for every
0\leq t<k
the following holds

\displaystyle 2\log_{2}a_{g+k+t}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2c_{k+t+1}d(k+t+1)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle c_{k+t}d(k+t)+c_{k+t+2}d(k+t+2)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\log_{2}a_{g+k+t-1}(1+o_{d}(1))+\log_{2}a_{g+k+t+1}(1+o_{d}(1)),

where
\approx
means equality up to a factor
1+o(1)
, and
a\ll b
means that
a/b<1-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. This harsh inequality follows from (
4
). This completes the proof.
∎

Our counterexamples to the LCGD Conjecture are still unimodal. As far as the author can tell, the unimodal weakening of the LCGD Conjecture could (and actually should) still be true.

Conjecture 10
.

The genus distribution of every graph
G
is unimodal.

Conjecture 11
