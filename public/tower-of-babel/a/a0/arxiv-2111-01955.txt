Probing to Minimize
Weina Wang ⓡ Anupam Gupta ⓡ Jalani Williams

Abstract
We develop approximation algorithms for set-selection problems with deterministic constraints, but random objective values, i.e., stochastic probing problems. When the goal is to maximize the objective, approximation algorithms for probing problems are well-studied. On the other hand, few techniques are known for minimizing the objective, especially in the adaptive setting, where information about the random objective is revealed during the set-selection process and allowed to influence it. For minimization problems in particular, incorporating adaptivity can have a considerable effect on performance. In this work, we seek approximation algorithms that compare well to the optimal adaptive policy. We develop new techniques for adaptive minimization, applying them to a few problems of interest. The core technique we develop here is an approximate reduction from an adaptive expectation minimization problem to a set of adaptive probability minimization problems which we call threshold problems . By providing near-optimal solutions to these threshold problems, we obtain bicriteria adaptive policies. We apply this method to obtain an adaptive approximation algorithm for the Min-Element problem, where the goal is to adaptively pick random variables to minimize the expected minimum value seen among them, subject to a knapsack constraint. This partially resolves an open problem raised in Goel et al. 2010 . We further consider three extensions on the Min-Element problem, where our objective is the sum of the smallest k k element-weights, or the weight of the min-weight basis of a given matroid, or where the constraint is not given by a knapsack but by a matroid constraint. For all three of the variations we explore, we develop adaptive approximation algorithms for their corresponding threshold problems, and prove their near-optimality via coupling arguments.

Introduction

Consider the following stochastic optimization problem: given a
collection
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
of non-negative random variables,
with each r.v.
X_{e}
having an associated cost
c_{e}
and a known probability
distribution, pick a subset
S\subseteq[n]
(from a collection of feasible sets

\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
) that minimizes

f_{\min}(S)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\min_{e\in S}X_{e}}}\right];

more generally, given a “well-behaved” function
f
, pick a set
S\in\mathcal{F}
to minimize

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(S)}}\right].

Such a
constrained stochastic minimization

problem may arise in many contexts.
For example, the government might have
\$100
billion to disburse for vaccine research, and seek to produce a viable vaccine as quickly as possible. Given that each company
e
requests a funding amount
c_{e}
and, upon receiving funding, produces a viable vaccine in time
X_{e}
, the government might seek to fund companies in a way that minimizes the time it takes for at least one company to produce a viable vaccine.
Alternatively, consider a home-repair setting, where among a large pool of possible repairmen, a homeowner might be willing to ask for at most
k
price estimates for a home-repair, and
seek to minimize her final price paid; or a collection setting, where among a pool of
4000
possible sellers, a collector might purchase multiple copies of the same collectible from various distributors, and seek to minimize the number of defects in the best copy.
In all of these settings, there is a natural
tradeoff between quality assurance and constraint impact which makes
these problems hard: often, riskier prospects are “cheaper” but
could pay off handsomely, while more stable prospects are either more
expensive or have a smaller possible upside.

While there has been considerable work on stochastic problems in the

maximization
setting (see, e.g.,
Guha and Munagala 2007
;
Golovin and Krause 2011
;
Gupta et al. 2016
), few algorithms and techniques are known
for
minimization
problems. Indeed, the minimization problems
appear much harder.
One exception is the work of
Goel et al. 2010
who consider the
Min-Element
problem, in which we choose a set
S
to minimize
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\min_{e\in S}{X_{e}}}}\right]
,
subject to
S
satisfying a knapsack constraint

\sum_{e\in S}c_{e}\leq B
.
Goel et al. 2010
consider the

non-adaptive
version of this
Min-Element
problem, where one commits to a set
S
of
variables before seeing any of the outcomes
\{X_{e}\}_{e\in S}
. They give a non-adaptive
bicriteria
approximation to the
optimal non-adaptive policy, i.e., a set
S
whose cost exceeds the budget

B
by an
O(\log\log m)
factor (where the r.v.s take on values in
the set
\{0,\ldots,m\}
), such that
\mathbb{E}[\min_{e\in S}X_{e}]
is at
most
(1+\varepsilon)
times the optimum.

Such non-adaptive solutions are particularly interesting when they
have a small
adaptivity gap
, i.e., when non-adaptive solutions have
a performance close to the best adaptive solutions. An
adaptive

solution builds its set
S

element-by-element: the outcome of an r.v.’s weight is revealed
immediately after it is added to the selection set
S
, and the
selection policy can then
adapt
and make future selections
based on all the outcomes seen so far. Unfortunately, simple examples
show that the
Min-Element
problem can have a large adaptivity gap:
see §
A
for a case with three random
variables such that the best non-adaptive solution is arbitrarily
worse than the best adaptive one.
Given such a large gap, it becomes
interesting to
find adaptive solutions which compare well to the
optimal adaptive policy
, so that we do not pay the price of the
huge adaptivity gap.
Goel et al. 2010
mention getting good
adaptive solutions as a (potentially challenging) open problem; our
work does exactly this.

1.1
Our results

We focus on providing efficiently computable
bicriteria guarantees.
We call an algorithm

(\alpha,\beta)
-approximation if the selection set

is the
union of at most
\beta

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right]
is at most
\alpha
times the optimal expected objective value.
Henceforth,
[m]_{\geq 0}\triangleq\{0,1,2,\dots,m\}
.

Our first main result is for the

Min-Element
problem in the adaptive setting; to differentiate it from the extensions we
consider
next, we call it the

Min-Element
-
Knapsack

Theorem 1
.

For the
Min-Element
-
Knapsack
problem where the random variables take values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
, there exists an adaptive policy that obtains a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4,O(\log\log m)}}\right)
-approximation.

Theorem

Interestingly, our

Goel et al. 2010

is a basic building block in our approach.

The

-
Knapsack

richer function classes
(and not just the minimum function),

richer constraint sets
(and not just the

outcomes in

. We call this

\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack

Theorem 2
.

For the
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8,O\bigl(\log\log m\cdot\log^{2}{k}\bigr)}}\right)
-approximation.

To prove Theorem

, we use the same adaptive binary search idea from
Theorem
1
,
and relate the resultant probability minimization problem to a different probability maximization problem
that we call the

-heads problem
, which is an interesting problem in its own right.
We then obtain a better-than-optimal solution for this problem by extending

, albeit overspending by an

factor. A detailed discussion appears

Next we consider
the
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Matroid
problem, where the objective function

is
again the sum of the smallest
k
outcomes in the selection set

,
but the constraint is now a matroid constraint.

2

A

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I})
is specified by a ground set

.

Our solution for this setting has an even better approximation than

Theorem 3
.

For the
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Matroid
problem where the random variables take values in

, there exists an adaptive policy that obtains a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8,O(\log\log m\cdot\log k)}}\right)

Theorem

takes the techniques developed for the

\textrm{{Min}-}k
-

problem, and shows the core reason why those

.

Finally, we investigate the

MinBasis
-
Cardinality
problem, where the constraint is a cardinality

B

generalize
the objective function from being the sum of the

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I})
of rank

, and we
consider the minimum-weight basis in this matroid.
One should note that taking a

-

Theorem 4
.

For the
MinBasis
-
Cardinality
problem where the random variables takes value in

, there exists an adaptive policy that obtains a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8,O(\log\log m\cdot\log k)}}\right)
-approximation.

While the result of Theorem

shows the importance of interchangeability
in the constraint, Theorem

shows the importance of
interchangeability in the objective. When the matroid constraint moves
into the objective, the previously optimal non-adaptive matroid greedy
algorithm now needs to be made

in order to maintain
optimality. We note a peculiarity here: while solving the non-adaptive
probability minimization problem is non-trivial, the
adaptive

version of this problem has a simpler optimal solution.

1.2
Techniques

We now give some more details about the main techniques behind our results.

1.2.1
Reduction to threshold problems

The primary technique we use is a reduction to threshold problems. The
idea is a clean one, provided we observe that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right]\leq\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>0}}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\log m\rfloor}\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>2^{j}}}\right)2^{j}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right].

(1)

This suggests that a good policy for minimizing
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right]
should somehow simultaneously minimize
all of these
\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>t}}\right)
terms
for values of
t
that are powers of
2
. Now if we have an algorithm, referred to as
Threshold
, which can
(\alpha,\beta)
-approximate
these threshold problems for every power-of-
2
threshold, we obtain a

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\alpha,O(\log{m})\beta}}\right)
-approximation to the optimal
adaptive policy. But this is incredibly wasteful, and does not use the
adaptivity. To avoid this loss, we use the property that
f(\cdot)
is non-increasing; if we
obtain
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\leq t
, we no longer need to worry about solving threshold
problems with thresholds larger than
t
. To make use of this
observation, we instead perform an
adaptive binary search
to
determine the next threshold to call
Threshold
on.

Adaptive binary search.
Starting with the median threshold
t
,
Threshold
probes
a set
S_{t}
that aims to minimize the probability of
f(S_{t})>t
. If
Threshold
obtains
f(S_{t})\leq t
, we say it succeeds and we recurse on the thresholds smaller than
t
; if

Threshold
fails to obtain
f(S_{t})\leq t
, we recurse on the set
of thresholds larger than
t
. While this seems simple, one should
note the asymmetry between success and failure here: success at a
threshold
t
guarantees that our objective value is at most
t
from then on, but failure
at
t

does not guarantee that the objective value is greater than
t
from then on
.
Nevertheless, we construct an upper bound
\mathrm{UB}
on our algorithm’s objective value, which allows us to
analyze it as if failure at
t

does
imply that the objective is at least
t
from then on. See §
2
for details. This
adaptive binary search now reduces the number of calls to

O(\log\log m)
.

1.2.2
The
Min-Element
-
Knapsack
problem

Armed with this reduction to a threshold problems,
the proof of Theorem
1
follows from an
approximation to the
Min-Element
-
Knapsack
threshold problem

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min_{e\in S^{(\pi)}}{X_{e}}>t}}\right)}.

Since any policy
\pi
succeeds at obtaining
\min_{e\in S}{X_{e}}\leq t
as soon as it finds a single element for which
X_{e}\leq t
, adaptivity adds nothing to solving this problem. Hence we focus on the non-adaptive problem

\min_{S\in\mathcal{F}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min_{e\in S}{X_{e}}>t}}\right)}.

Taking the logarithm of the objective, we reduce to a knapsack instance where each element has
reward
equal to
-\log{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}>t}}\right)}
.
Now a greedy procedure gives a
(1,2)
-approximation to the threshold
problem. Combining all these together gives the

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4,O(\log\log m)}}\right)
-approximation from Theorem
1
.

1.2.3
The
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack
problem

We turn next to a strict extension of the
Min-Element
-
Knapsack
problem: the

\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack
problem. The first step, like in the

Min-Element
-
Knapsack
problem, is to apply a threshold-based reduction. An
idea similar to that from §
1.2.1
allows us to
reduce a slightly richer set of objective functions
to threshold minimization problems.
Indeed, if
y_{i}(S)
is the weight of the
i
-th smallest weight
element in
S
, then our objective
f(S)
is
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{y_{i}(S)}
,
the sum of the
k
smallest weights in
S
. Writing the expectation as
a sum of thresholds like in (
1
), we get that it suffices to
find approximations to the threshold problem

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>t}}\right)}

for different values of
t
. This problem can be thought of in the following way.

Problem

(
The
i
-heads problem
)
.

We are given a set of coins
\mathcal{U}
, each having cost
c_{e}
and
bias
p_{e}
. We can flip a coin
e
at most once, at a cost of

c_{e}
. Given a budget
B
, the
i
-heads problem seeks to adaptively
flip coins to maximize
\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{see at least $i$ heads}}}\right)
.

We extend the greedy knapsack strategy from §
1.2.2

used to solve the case
i=1
to the case of a general
i
: the idea
is again to pick items in increasing order of cost-per-reward, except
that the budget is extended from
B
to
B+i\delta
, where
\delta

is the maximum cost of any element. In order to control this
\delta

term, we bucket the coins and choose the best coins at each of the top

O(\log i)
levels until the cost of each coin becomes smaller than

B/i
.

The proof that this greedy policy

G
outperforms any admissible policy
\pi
requires some new ideas. The
heart of the argument is to first construct a surrogate policy

\tilde{\pi}
which strictly outperforms any policy
\pi
, and then to
show an ordering of the coins of
G
such that

•

The policies
G
and
\tilde{\pi}
probe the same set after obtaining their first heads.

•

For any amount of remaining budget
B^{\prime}
, the probability that
G
obtains its first heads with
B^{\prime}
budget remaining is greater than the probability that
\pi
obtains its first heads with
B^{\prime}
budget remaining.

In the end, our argument shows that
G
stochastically dominates every
admissible policy
\pi
, which completes the proof.

1.2.4
The
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Matroid
problem

We now discuss our first matroid-based variation on the

\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack
problem, which we call the
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Matroid
problem. The same reduction used in the
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack
problem implies that
proving Theorem
3
reduces to providing a
(1,O(1))
-approximation to threshold problem for matroids. We show that the matroid greedy algorithm
MGreedy
, described in Algorithm
4
, is an optimal policy for this problem. In fact, the matroid greedy algorithm stochastically dominates any other policy. We argue this via induction on the matroid rank. The main observation one must make here is that, after probing a single element, we find ourselves in a problem state nearly exactly like the starting state, but with a possibly reduced number of heads, and a resulting constraint family which is a matroid with a strictly smaller rank.

1.2.5
The
MinBasis
-
Cardinality
problem

We now discuss the treatment of our final problem, the

MinBasis
-
Cardinality
problem. The proof of
Theorem
4
is again through the reduction to threshold problems for an objective
f(S)
that is in the form of
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{g_{i}(S)}
.
However, unlike the case of a
\textrm{{Min}-}k
objective, now it is not obvious how we can
write the
MinBasis
objective
f(S)
as
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{g_{i}(S)}
for a monotonic
function sequence
(g_{i}(\cdot))
where each
g_{i}(\cdot)
is a
non-decreasing function taking values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
. In our proof,
we first show that we can specify
g_{i}(S)
to be the
i
-th smallest
element in the minimum-weight basis contained in
S
generated by the standard
matroid greedy algorithm. Then we show inductively that

AdapMGreedy
, a simple adaptive matroid greedy algorithm described in
Algorithm
5
, is optimal for the resulting
threshold problem. The key idea here is again
to use our inductive assumption to reason about the future behavior of
an optimal policy.

1.3
Related Work

The work closest to ours in content and aim is
Goel et al. 2010
. That paper allows a more general model, where element weights can take on non-negative discrete values
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{L}
, but restricts it focus to the
Min-Element
objective
f(S)\triangleq\min_{e\in S}{X_{e}}
.
Goel et al. 2010
give a bicriteria
approximation scheme, showing for every
\varepsilon>0
how to obtain a set
T
such that

\mathtt{cost}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T}}\right)\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log(L)+\log{\frac{1}{\log{(1+\varepsilon)}}}}}\right)

and meanwhile
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(T)}}\right]\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(S^{*})}}\right]
for
S^{*}\triangleq\argmin_{S\in\mathcal{F}}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(S)}}\right]}
. Moreover, they
show that finding a
\textrm{poly}(L)

approximation to the non-adaptive
problem is NP-hard, by establishing an approximation-preserving
reduction to a special class of covering integer programs. Our results differ in several ways. First,
Goel et al. 2010
consider non-adaptive approximations
to the optimal non-adaptive policy, whereas
we provide adaptive approximations to the optimal adaptive policy,
partially closing an open question posed by them.
Second, they obtain a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation instead of a
constant-factor approximation.
Third, their
techniques are quite different from ours: they use the submodularity
of (a transformation of) the expected objective
\mathbb{E}[f(S)]
, while we adaptively explore elements through an adaptive binary search.
Moreover, we consider several generalizations of the
Min-Element
problem with more general objectives and constraints.

Stochastic
maximization
problems have been studied much more
broadly: the results for these problems very often show small
adaptivity gaps and focus on particular classes of objective functions
(
Adamczyk et al. 2013
;
Gupta et al. 2017
;
Bradac et al. 2019
;
Golovin and Krause 2011
)
. In contrast, we show that one of our minimization problems
has a large adaptivity gap, and our main technical result, the reduction to threshold problems, applies to a general class of objective functions. Moreover, the approaches used for these
problems and for ours are quite different.

With respect to solving threshold problems, to the authors’ knowledge, generally little is known. However, the threshold problem associated with the
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack
problem (see §
4
) has been heuristically treated before. In particular, it is a Bernoulli version of a more general adaptive knapsack problem, where rewards are independently random but the cost of each item is fixed, and the goal is to achieve a cumulative reward above some threshold with optimal probability. Previous work (
Carraway et al. 1993
,
Ilhan et al. 2011
) on this problem has focused on the development of heuristics and on optimizing the dynamic programming solution, in the setting where rewards are normally distributed. This work is instead concerned with the development of bicriteria approximation algorithms in the setting where rewards are Bernoulli distributed, though it may be possible to extend our methods to the more general case.

2
General Framework with Threshold Problems

In this section, we present the full details of the reduction from the stochastic minimization problem to threshold problems, first alluded to in Section
1.2
.
Formally, the
f
-
\mathcal{F}
threshold problem with threshold
t
is an adaptive probability minimization problem, wherein the policy
\pi
tries to avoid
f(S^{(\pi)})>t
with as high a probability as possible, while abiding by the constraint that
S^{(\pi)}\in\mathcal{F}
. Lemma
2.1
says that, given an
(\alpha,\beta)
-approximation for the
f
-
\mathcal{F}
threshold problem, one can construct a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4\alpha,O(\log\log{m})\beta}}\right)
-approximation for the adaptive expectation minimization problem
\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(S^{(\pi)})}}\right]}
.
Our results are summarized in Lemma
2.1
and Corollary
2.1
, which we state now.

Lemma 2.1

(
Reduction to threshold problems
)
.

Let
f(S)
be a non-increasing objective function taking values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
for a positive integer
m
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the constraint family and
\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)
be the set of feasible policies under
\mathcal{F}
. Suppose that for any
t\in[m]_{\geq 0}
, the
f
-
\mathcal{F}
threshold problem

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>t}}\right)}

admits an
(\alpha,\beta)
-approximation. Then the stochastic minimization problem

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right]}

admits a
(4\alpha,O(\log\log m)\cdot\beta)
-approximation.

Corollary 2.1

(
Sum-of-
k
reduction
)
.

Let
g_{i}(S)
,
i\in[k]
, be a non-increasing function taking values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
, and assume that the function sequence
(g_{i})
is monotonic in
i
. Let
f(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{g_{i}(S)}
. Suppose that for any
t\in[m]_{\geq 0}
, the
g_{i}
-
\mathcal{F}
threshold problem

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>t}}\right)}

admits an
(\alpha,\beta)
-approximation. Then the stochastic minimization problem

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right]}

admits an
(8\alpha,O(\log\log m\cdot\log k)\cdot\beta)
approximation.

As described in Section
1.2
, to build the intuition for the reduction, we start with the following decomposition for any policy
\pi
:

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right]\leq\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>0}}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\log m\rfloor}\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)>2^{j}}}\right)2^{j}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right].

In this section, we focus on the

3.1
No adaptivity gap

Lemma 3.1
.

The

-
Knapsack

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary

\mathcal{U}
. Clearly, the adaptivity gap is

\mathcal{U}
consists of one element.

Assume that the adaptivity gap is

n

6

n

\mathcal{U}

be an optimal adaptive policy for it. We will construct a non-adaptive policy, represented by a set

Without loss of generality, we can assume that

is the first element probed by the optimal adaptive policy

since the first probing decision does not depend on realizations of the random variables. After
X_{1}
is probed, we consider the threshold problem whose input consists of a universe

and a budget

We claim that

satisfies (

be the subsequent policy of
\pi^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=t+1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}=x\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{e\in S^{(\pi^{*})}}X_{e}>t\;\middle|\;X_{1}=x\right)

3.2
Reduction to knapsack

By virtue of Lemma
3.1
, to solve the threshold problem (
6
), it suffices to solve its non-adaptive counterpart:

\min_{S\in\mathcal{F}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min_{e\in S}{X_{e}}>t}}\right)}

Note that

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min_{e\in S}X_{e}>t}}\right)=\prod_{e\in S}\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}>t}}\right).

Taking a

\max_{S\in\mathcal{F}}{\sum_{e\in S}\bigl(-\log\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}>t}}\right)\bigr)}

This is a knapsack problem where the reward of each element

until the first time the total cost
exceeds the budget. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

Having given a construction for

and

Our argument here centers on the

\mathtt{cost}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G-T^{*}_{j}}}\right)\geq\mathtt{cost}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L^{(\pi)}_{j}}}\right).

Theorem

.

For the
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Matroid

, there exists an adaptive policy that obtains a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8,O(\log\log m\cdot\log k)}}\right)
-approximation.

Recall the definition of

as “the
i
-th smallest weight in
S
”.
Following the derivation in §
4
, we can utilize the fact that
f(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{y_{i}(S)}
and apply Corollary

to reduce Theorem

to finding an optimal policy for the

-
\mathcal{F}

Recalling further the definition of
\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)=\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e\in S:X_{e}\leq t}}\right\}\rvert
and the equivalence of the two statements

and

, we recover the

-th rank problem first treated in §

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}.

We show that the standard matroid greedy algorithm

is optimal for this problem, and in doing so prove Theorem

. A full description of

Algorithm 4

\text{{{MGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I},t)

1:

Initialization:
S\leftarrow\emptyset
;
\textsc{pool}\leftarrow\mathcal{U}

2:

while

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e\in\mathcal{U}-S\colon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup S\in\mathcal{I}}}\right\}\neq\emptyset

3:

\ell\leftarrow\argmax_{e\in\textsc{pool}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}\leq t}}\right)}

\triangleright

Perform greedy selection

4:

S\leftarrow S\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\ell}}\right\}

5:

\textsc{pool}\leftarrow\textsc{pool}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\ell}}\right\}

6:

return

S

Lemma 5.1
.

Given a matroid
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I})
, let the set
G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})=\text{{{MGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I},t)
. Then
G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})
is optimal for any rank problem with constraint set
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{I}
, i.e., for any

,

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)\geq\max_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}.

5.1
Proof of Lemma
5.1

We prove this via induction on the matroid rank of the outer matroid
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}
, i.e., the number of elements in a basis for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}
.
In the base case, the matroid rank of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}
is

Taking the element with the highest probability of being below-threshold is clearly optimal; this is precisely

’s selection.

Definition 5.1
.

Define the contraction of

\mathcal{M}/S=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{T\subseteq\mathcal{U}-S:T\cup S\in\mathcal{I}}}\right\}.

In other words, the collection
\mathcal{M}/S
is the collection of all sets which can be added to

while maintaining independence.

We now treat the inductive case.
Assume that, whenever the matroid rank of a given matroid
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
is smaller than the matroid rank of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}
, then the set
G(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})=\text{{{MGreedy}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime},t}}\right)
is an optimal policy for any rank problem with constraint
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, i.e., that, for any
i
,

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}^{\prime})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)\geq\max_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}.

(32)

We show that
G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})
is optimal for any rank problem with constraint
\mathcal{I}
, i.e., that, for any
i
,

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)\geq\max_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{I}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}.

(33)

Consider any
i
and let
\pi^{*}_{i}
be an optimal policy for the maximization problem on the right-hand-side of (
33
).
Assume without loss of generality that the element
e
is the first element probed by
\pi^{*}_{i}
and let
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
be the independent sets of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\{e\}
.
Then after seeing the realization of
X_{e}
, the rest of
\pi^{*}_{i}
probes sets from
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
.
We use
\pi^{*}_{i}(x)
to denote the rest of the policy
\pi^{*}_{i}
after seeing
X_{e}=x
for some

.
Then
\pi^{*}_{i}(x)
itself is a policy in
\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}}\right)
.
Note that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\{e\}
has strictly smaller matroid rank than
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{*}_{i}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in[m]_{\geq 0}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{*}_{i}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i\;\middle|\;X_{e}=x}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in[m]_{\geq 0}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{*}_{i}(x)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-\mathbf{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\leq t}}\right\}}}}\right)}

(34)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in[m]_{\geq 0}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})}}\right)\geq i-\mathbf{1}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\leq t}}\right\}}}}\right)}

(35)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})}}\right)\geq i}}\right),

(36)

where (
34
) follows from the independence of the elements and (
35
) follows from the inductive hypothesis. Thus, we have shown that the non-adaptive set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})
is optimal.

Now we specifically show that
G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})=\text{{{MGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I},t)
is optimal. Without loss of generality, let element
1
be the element in
\mathcal{U}
with the largest probability of being below-threshold. Then by the design of the algorithm
\text{{{MGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{I},t)
, we have
G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})=\{1\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\})
. Note that

\displaystyle\mspace{17mu}\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{1\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)+\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\})}}\right)=i-1}}\right)\cdot\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}\leq t}}\right).

(37)

We next construct an independent set
S
that contains element
1
and elements from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

. Then as long as

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})\setminus S

\mathcal{I}
. We augment

. We continue this procedure until

.
By this construction, only one element in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})

. We denote this element as element

.
Clearly,

; let us call this set

We next lower bound (
37

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}\leq t}}\right)\geq\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{y}\leq t}}\right)

\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\})}}\right)

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)\geq\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L}}\right)\geq i}}\right).

\displaystyle\mspace{17mu}\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}})}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})}}\right)\geq i}}\right).

Since we have already shown that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\cup G(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{O}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\})

\mathcal{F}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\subseteq\mathcal{U}\colon\lvert S\rvert\leq B}}\right\}

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subseteq S^{(\pi)}\\

(38)

Our goal is proving Theorem

, which we restate now.

Theorem
4
.

For the
MinBasis
-
Cardinality
problem where the random variables takes values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
, there exists an adaptive policy that obtains a
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(8,O(\log\log m\cdot\log k)}}\right)
-approximation.

The proof of Theorem
4
is again through Corollary
2.1
.
However, it is not obvious how to write
f(S)
as
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{g_{i}(S)}
for a monotonic function sequence
(g_{i})
where each
g_{i}
is a non-decreasing function taking values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
.
In our proof, we first show that we can specify
g_{i}(S)
to be the
i
-th smallest element in the minimum-weight basis contained in
S
generated by the matroid greedy algorithm.
Then we show inductively that
AdapMGreedy
, a simple adaptive matroid greedy algorithm described in Algorithm
5
, is optimal for a rank problem that is equivalent to the resulting
g_{i}
-
Cardinality
threshold problem.

6.1
Reduction to rank problems

Decomposition of the objective function.

We first describe the decomposition
f(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{g_{i}(S)}
. Given a set
S
and a weighted matroid
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}
, it is well known that the matroid greedy algorithm will find a basis for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}
of minimum weight, where the matroid greedy algorithm at each step adds a minimum-weight element among the elements whose addition would preserve the independence. Let
G(S,\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}})
be the set generated by this process. It follows that for the selection set
S^{(\pi)}
of any policy
\pi
,

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subseteq S^{(\pi)}\\
\text{$T$ is a basis for $\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}$}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{e\in T}{X_{e}}}=\sum_{e\in G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}}}\right)}{X_{e}}.

(39)

In other words, to minimize our original objective, it suffices to minimize the cumulative weight of the post-selection greedy set
G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}}}\right)
. Note also that, by ordering the summation of elements within this greedy set accordingly, we have

\sum_{e\in G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}}}\right)}{X_{e}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{y_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}}}\right)}}\right)},

where one must recall that
y_{i}(T)
is the weight of the
i
-th smallest element in the set
T
.
Then we specify the function
g_{i}
in the decomposition
f(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{g_{i}(S)}
as
g_{i}(S)=y_{i}(G(S,\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}))
. To complete our reduction, we show that these
g_{i}
’s satisfy the conditions of Corollary
2.1
, i.e., the function sequence
(g_{i})
is monotonic, and each function
g_{i}
is a non-decreasing function taking values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
.

First, we show that
g_{i}(S)\leq g_{i+1}(S)
. By the nature of greedy selection, elements are chosen for
G(S,\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}})
in order of increasing weight. It follows that the
i
-th smallest element in
G(S,\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}})
must be smaller than the
(i+1)
-th smallest element in
G(S,\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}})
.

Second, we show that for each function
g_{i}
, given a subset
T\subseteq S
,
g_{i}(S)\leq g_{i}(T)
. It suffices to show that for all
t\in[m]_{\geq 0}
, if
g_{i}(T)\leq t
, then
g_{i}(S)\leq t
. To see this, note that if
g_{i}(T)\leq t
, then there must exist an independent set of size
i
within
T
within which all elements are of weight at most
t
. We again argue via the greedy selection process. Note that
S
must also contain the size
i
independent set previously mentioned. It follows from the matroid exchange axiom that, in the
\ell
-th round of greedy selection for
\ell\leq i
, there must always exist an element of weight
\leq t
that we can add to the greedy set we are building. As such, the the first
i
elements in our greedy set must have weight
\leq t
, and thus
g_{i}(S)\leq t
.

Third, since every weight
X_{e}
takes values
[m]_{\geq 0}
, it follows that
g_{i}(S)
takes values in
[m]_{\geq 0}
. Thus, the conditions of Corollary
2.1
are satisfied, and to prove Theorem
4
, it suffices to exhibit an optimal algorithm for the
g_{i}
-
\mathcal{F}
threshold problem. Before that, we introduce a helpful reinterpretation of this problem by defining a rank problem.

From threshold problems to rank problems.

Definition 6.1

(The matroid threshold rank
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
)
.

Given a threshold
t
and a matroid
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}
, for any set
S
, let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
denote the size of the largest independent set contained in
S
within which all elements are below the threshold
t
.
Using our previous definition
\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{e\in T\colon X_{e}\leq t\}}}\right|
, we can define
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
as

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)=\max_{T\subseteq S,T\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}}{\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T}}\right)}.

We call this the matroid
threshold
rank to distinguish it from the matroid rank of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}
, which is simply the size of a basis for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}
.

Following the exposition of
\textrm{{Min}-}k
-
Knapsack
problem, we also have the following equivalence between the threshold condition and a rank condition:
g_{i}(S)\leq t
if and only if
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)\geq i
. To see this, if
g_{i}(S)\leq t
, then
S
must contain an independent set of size
i
wherein all elements are of weight
\leq t
. For the other direction, if
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)\geq i
, then there exists an independent set
T\subseteq S
with
\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T}}\right)\geq i
. Since
T
is an independent set itself, we have
G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T,\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}}}\right)=T
and thus
g_{i}(T)\leq t
. Therefore,
g_{i}(S)\leq g_{i}(T)\leq t
by the property that
g_{i}(\cdot)
is a non-increasing function.

By this equivalence, it follows that the
g_{i}
-
\mathcal{F}
threshold problem satisfies

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{i}(S)\leq t}}\right)}=\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}.

(40)

We use the above characterization in the remainder of this section, which we devote to proving the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

The adaptive greedy algorithm

, described in Algorithm

) for all

.

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)\geq i

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)\geq i

T^{\prime}=\bigcup_{r=1}^{i-1}{v_{r}}
is in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}
, and that, since
T^{\prime}
is a subset of the below-threshold set
T
, the augmenting set
T^{\prime}
itself must also be below-threshold. Thus,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T^{\prime}}}\right)=i-1.

The other direction is easy. If
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1
, then there must exist a size
(i-1)
below-threshold set
T^{\prime}
contained in
S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}
, which lies in
\mathcal{M}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}
. It follows that
T^{\prime}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}
, and, since
X_{e}\leq t
, the combined set
T^{\prime}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}
is also below-threshold. Thus, we have

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)\geq\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T^{\prime}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)=i,

as desired.

∎

Continuing from the claim and recalling that
\pi_{x}
represents the continuing behavior of the policy
\pi
after it probes the element
e
and obtains
X_{e}=x
, we see that

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\leq t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}=x}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\leq t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\leq t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}\leq t}}\right)\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)},

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\subseteq\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\colon\lvert S\rvert\leq B-1}}\right\}
. Continuing the same procedure (in downwards induction), we find that, when
B=i
, any optimal adaptive policy has the same success probability as a non-adaptive policy, i.e., that

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\max_{S\in\mathcal{F}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\max_{S\in\mathcal{F},S\in\mathcal{M}}{\prod_{\ell\in S}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{\ell}\leq t}}\right)}}

which after some manipulations becomes

\displaystyle=\exp{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max_{S\in\mathcal{F},S\in\mathcal{M}}{\sum_{\ell\in S}{\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{\ell}\leq t}}\right)}}\right]}}}}}\right)}.

But
\max_{S\in\mathcal{F},S\in\mathcal{M}}{\sum_{\ell\in S}{\log{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{\ell}\leq t}}\right)}}\right]}}}
is a max-weight basis problem for the matroid
(\mathcal{U},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\in\mathcal{I}:\lvert S\rvert\leq B}}\right\})
with element weights
\log{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}\leq t}}\right)}
. It follows that the matroid greedy algorithm is optimal for this problem, and thus that the adaptive greedy algorithm is optimal (since, if every element probed is below-threshold, then the adaptive greedy algorithm is equivalent to the matroid greedy algorithm). This completes the base case.

Inductive Case.

Now, assume inductively that, for all inner matroids
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
and budgets
B^{\prime}<B
, the adaptive greedy algorithm
\textrm{{{AdapMGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime},B^{\prime},t)
is optimal for all targets
i^{\prime}
. We split the proof of the inductive case into two claims. First, we show that, under our inductive assumption, it is always optimal to follow the adaptive greedy policy after probing the one’s first element. Second, we show that the element first probed by the adaptive greedy strategy is optimal. It follows directly from these two claims that the adaptive greedy strategy is optimal. Thus, we now state and prove the claims.

Claim 6.2
.

Assume that, for all inner matroids
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
, budgets
B^{\prime}<B
, and targets
i^{\prime}
, the adaptive greedy algorithm
\textrm{{{AdapMGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime},B^{\prime},t)
is optimal for the problem

\min_{\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}}\right)}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\prime}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right)\geq i^{\prime}}.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\subseteq\mathcal{U}\middle|\lvert S\rvert\leq B}}\right\}
. For any policy
\pi\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)
, let
e
be the first element
\pi
probes. If
\pi^{\prime}
is the policy that probes
e
then follows the adaptive greedy strategy, then

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)\leq\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right).

Proof.

Let
\textrm{AG}(-)=\textrm{{{AdapMGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\},\mathcal{I},B-1,t)
and
\textrm{AG}(+)=\textrm{{{AdapMGreedy}}}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}},B-1,t)
, be the adaptive greedy procedure which would be optimal follow after probing element
e
, given that either
X_{e}>t
or
X_{e}\leq t
, respectively. Then, using Claim
6.1
,

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in[m]_{\geq 0}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}=x}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\leq t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}=x}}\right)}

\displaystyle\;\;+\sum_{x>t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}=x}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\leq t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)}

\displaystyle\;\;+\sum_{x>t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{x}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\leq t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(-)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)}

\displaystyle\;\;+\sum_{x>t}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}=x}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(+)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}\leq t}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(+)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\;\;+\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}>t}}\right)\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(-)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right),

where the last line is precisely the probability of success if one probes
e
then follows the adaptive greedy strategy.
∎

We now show that the adaptive greedy algorithm does not choose a sub-optimal first element.

Claim 6.3
.

Let element
1
be the element that would be picked first by the adaptive greedy policy. Then, for the threshold problem (
40
), there exists an optimal policy which probes
1
first.

Proof.

Assume there exists a policy
\pi
whose first selection is an element
e
which is not
1
. We will show how to construct a policy
\pi^{\prime}
which first probes
1
, and succeeds at least as often as
\pi
. There are two cases, depending on whether or not
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\}\in\mathcal{I}
.

In the first case,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\}\in\mathcal{I}
. From the above, we know that it is optimal to
follow
AG
after probing
e
. Regardless of the value of
X_{e}
, the next element to be probed would then be
1
, since

1=\argmax_{\ell\in\mathcal{U}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{\ell}\leq t}}\right)}=\argmax_{\ell\in\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,\ell}}\right\}\in\mathcal{I}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{\ell}\leq t}}\right)}.

From Claim
6.2
, we know that, after probing element
1
and element
e
, it is optimal to follow the adaptive greedy strategy. Since we always probe both element
e
and element
1
and follow the same strategy, we can switch the order that we probe them in without changing the probability of success. In other words, there must exist an optimal policy which probes element
1
first (then element
e
, then follows
AG
), as desired.

In the second case,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\}\not\in\mathcal{I}
. Assume (without loss of success probability, by Claim
6.1
) that
\pi
follows the adaptive greedy algorithm. We note three facts. First, note that, if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\}\not\in\mathcal{I}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}
, since if there exists
T
such that
T\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\in\mathcal{I}
, then, after augmenting
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}
with
T\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}
, we must obtain
T\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}
and vice versa (since independence is subset-closed). Second, note that, if
X_{e}>t
, then the second element probed would be element
1
since
1=\argmax_{\ell\in\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}}{\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{\ell}\leq t}}\right)}
. For brevity, we write
AG
as an abbreviation of
AdapMGreedy
. Third, note that we can always eliminate non-independent elements from the universe of the greedy argument, i.e., if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\ell}}\right\}\not\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, then
\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime},B^{\prime},t)=\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}^{\prime}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\ell}}\right\},\mathcal{I}^{\prime},B^{\prime},t)
. From this third fact along with the first, it follows that

\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}},B^{\prime},t)=\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}},B^{\prime},t)=\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}},B^{\prime},t),

where one should recall that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v}}\right\}}
is the contraction of
\mathcal{I}
by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v}}\right\}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v}}\right\}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\in\mathcal{I}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v}}\right\}:S\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v}}\right\}\in\mathcal{I}}}\right\}

We consider the following policy
\pi^{\prime}
: First, probe element
1
. If
X_{1}\leq t
, then do what
\pi
does when
X_{e}\leq t
(follow
\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}},t)
). If
X_{1}>t
, then probe element
e
and, if
X_{e}\leq t
, do what
\pi
does when
X_{e}>t
and
X_{1}\leq t
; otherwise, do what
\pi
does when both
X_{e}>t
and
X_{1}>t
.

It suffices to show that
\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)-\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)\geq 0
. By construction, we see that

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}\leq t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}},B-1,t)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}},B-1,t)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{1}\leq t}}\right);

we assign this value a shorthand
R_{a}
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}>t,X_{1}\leq t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}},B-2,t)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}},B-2,t)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{1}\leq t,X_{e}>t}}\right),

which we call
R_{b}
. And, likewise, we have

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}>t,X_{1}>t}}\right)=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i\middle|X_{e}>t,X_{1}>t}}\right),

which we call
R_{c}
.
Putting these observations together, we find that, abbreviating
\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}\leq t}}\right)
as
p_{e}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}\leq t}}\right)R_{a}+\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{e}>t}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}\leq t}}\right)R_{b}+\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{1}>t}}\right)R_{c}}}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{e}R_{a}+(1-p_{e})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{1}R_{b}+(1-p_{1})R_{c}}}\right),

and analogously that

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)=p_{1}R_{a}+(1-p_{1})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{e}R_{b}+(1-p_{e})R_{c}}}\right).

Comparing the two, we find

\displaystyle\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)-\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)

\displaystyle=(p_{1}-p_{e})(R_{a}-R_{b}).

Since
p_{1}\geq p_{e}
by definition, it suffices to show that
R_{a}\geq R_{b}
, i.e., that

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}},B-1,t)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right)\geq\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}},B-2,t)}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i-1}}\right),

but, from a simple coupling argument, one sees that having an additional element available for probing and having a larger budget to probe can only increase the probability of the adaptive greedy algorithm’s success. In other words,
\textrm{{{AdapMGreedy}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}},B-1,t}}\right)
will succeed more often than
\textrm{AG}(\mathcal{U}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\},\mathcal{I}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}},B-2,t)
. Thus,
R_{a}\geq R_{b}
, and

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi^{\prime}}}\right)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)-\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{I}}\text{-}\mathtt{rank}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)\geq i}}\right)\geq 0,

as desired. Since
\pi
is optimal and
\pi^{\prime}\in\textbf{Adm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F}}}\right)
, it follows that
\pi^{\prime}
is optimal as well, meaning that, in the case where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e,1}}\right\}\not\in\mathcal{I}
, there exists an optimal policy which probes element
1
first. This completes the proof of Claim
6.3
, and thus our proof of Lemma
6.1
and, accordingly, our proof of Theorem
4
.
∎

Appendix A
Adaptivity Gap Example

Example 1
.

Consider the following probing problem. We are given a universe of three elements,
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
, and
X_{3}
to choose from, independently distributed from each other, with distributions we describe later. We are allowed to choose 2 of these random variables for our set
S
, and the end objective
f(S)
is the minimum weight element in
S
. The distributions are as follows:
X_{1}
is
N^{2}
with probability (w.p.)
\frac{1}{N^{2}}
and
1
otherwise,
X_{2}
is
N^{2}
w.p.
\frac{1}{N}
and
0
otherwise, and
X_{3}
is
N
w.p.
1
.

Computing the expected value of each fixed two-element set, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(\{1,3\})}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\min(X_{1},N)}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{N^{2}}}}\right)\cdot 1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}}}\right)\cdot N\geq 1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(\{1,2\})}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{N}}}\right)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{1}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{N}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{N^{2}}}}\right)\cdot 1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}}}\right)\cdot N^{2}}}\right]\geq 1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(\{2,3\})}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\min(X_{2},N)}}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\cdot N=1.

Now consider the following adaptive policy, which we call
\pi
: First, probe element
1
. If
X_{1}=1
, then probe element
2
; otherwise probe element
3
. Intuitively, this policy probes the ‘risky’ element 2 only if it has already secured an objective value of
1
. Computing the expected objective value (taking
S^{(\pi)}
to be the set chosen by policy
\pi
), we find

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{(\pi)}}}\right)}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{N^{2}}}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{N}\cdot 1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}}}\right)\cdot N\leq\frac{2}{N}.

Thus, the adaptivity gap of this instance is at least
\frac{N}{2}
, which can be made arbitrarily large.
