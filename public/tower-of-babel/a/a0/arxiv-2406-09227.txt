Well-posedness of aggregation-diffusion systems with irregular kernels
José A. Carrillo, Yurij Salmaniw, Jakub Skrzeczkowski

Abstract
. We consider aggregation-diffusion equations with merely bounded nonlocal interaction potential K K . We are interested in establishing their well-posedness theory when the nonlocal interaction potential K K is neither differentiable nor positive (semi-)definite, thus preventing application of classical arguments. We prove the existence of weak solutions in two cases: if the mass of the initial data is sufficiently small, or if the interaction potential is symmetric and of bounded variation without any smallness assumption. The latter allows one to exploit the dissipation of the free energy in an optimal way, which is an entirely new approach. Remarkably, in both cases, under the additional condition that ∇ K ∗ K \nabla K\ast K is in L 2 L^{2} , we can prove that the strong solution is unique. When K K is a characteristic function of a ball, we construct the classical unique solution. Under additional structural conditions we extend these results to the n n -species system.

The primary purpose of this work is to establish a robust well-posedness theory (global existence & uniqueness) for irregular interaction potentials, with the motivating prototypical form being the top-hat kernel in one dimension:

Let us now discuss the sharpness of our results. First, regarding the existence of weak solutions, classical blow-up results for the Keller-Segel system
[

,

,

where the nonexistence arises from a lack of derivatives rather than from a singularity of

which proves that for appropriate initial conditions, the solution to

\alpha\in(0,1)
), even for fairly regular kernels such as
K(x)=e^{-|x|}

Regarding the uniqueness of solutions, there have recently been several counterexamples for the related advection–diffusion equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot(u\,V)+\Delta u.

(1.5)

In
[
61
]
, the Authors, using the stochastic Lagrangian method, constructed an example of

with
p<2
for which solutions to (
1.5
) are not unique in the natural class
L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
. Another construction was obtained by the convex integration method
[
59
,
60
]
. For instance, in
[
60
]
, the Authors constructed
V\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\cap L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for suitable

and

such that uniqueness fails in the class

, where

is the Hölder conjugate of

. Let us emphasize two points. First, it does not seem immediate to adapt these constructions to the case

, and to the best of our knowledge, no nonuniqueness results are currently known for (
1.1
). Second, in the above counterexamples, uniqueness fails in broader function classes compared to our setting, where we assume

together with
\Delta u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In particular, we do not exclude the possibility of other solutions with lower regularity. Finally, we mention
[
38
,
72
]
where the authors derive formulas for two different solutions to the Keller-Segel system in the space of measures (after the blow-up), suggesting nonuniqueness in the class of measure solutions.

Finally, we want to comment that the small-mass case (Theorem
2.1
) can be combined with approaches of
[

]
and
[

to allow to consider even more general kernels. Namely, we can consider

Last but not least, it is natural to ask whether our results can be extended to the more general, nonlinear equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(D\nabla u^{m}+u\nabla(K*u)\right)

Main Results

2.1.
Hypotheses

In the present work, we consider the well-posedness of system (
1.2
) on the whole space for any dimension
d\geq 1
for merely bounded kernels, noting that these results hold true for the
d
-dimensional torus with no essential change to the proofs.

Given a function
g(\cdot)
, we denote by
\tilde{g}(\cdot):=g(-\cdot)
its reflection. Our hypotheses on the kernels
K_{ij}
are as follows.

(H1)

K_{ij}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
;

(H2)

there exists
C_{K_{ij}}>0
such that for all
\phi(\cdot)
smooth there holds
\left\|\nabla K_{ij}*\phi\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq C_{K_{ij}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}
or equivalently, as proven below,
K_{ij}\in\BV({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
with the total variation
\|\nabla K_{ij}\|_{\text{TV}}<\infty
for each
i,j=1,\ldots,n
;

(H3)

K_{ij}(\cdot)
is symmetric about the origin for each
i,j=1,\ldots,n
;

(H4)

\nabla(\tilde{K}_{ij}*K_{ij})\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
;

(H5)

K_{ij}
are in detailed balance, that is, there exists constants
\pi_{i}>0
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, such that
\pi_{i}K_{ij}(\cdot)=\pi_{j}K_{ji}(\cdot)
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
;

(H6)

K_{ij}
is compactly supported for each
i,j=1,\ldots,n
.

We briefly comment on assumptions that are not standard.

…

Hypothesis
(H2)
.
We recall from
[
3
]
that
K\in\BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})
means that its distributional gradient
\nabla K
is a bounded measure, i.e.

\|\nabla K\|_{TV}=\sup_{\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K\,\DIV\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

(2.1)

For example, when
K=\mathds{1}_{U}
and
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded set with smooth boundary, the condition is satisfied because

\|\nabla K\|_{TV}:=\sup_{\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\int_{\partial U}\langle\varphi,{\bf{n}}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S\leq|\partial U|<\infty.

Let us check the equivalence between the statements in hypothesis
(H2)
. Indeed, let
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 1
and assume (
2.1
) holds. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla K*\phi\,\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K\,\DIV\phi*\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\|\nabla K\|_{\text{TV}}\,\|\phi*\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|\nabla K\|_{\text{TV}}\,\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Taking supremum over all
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 1
we get

\left\|\nabla K*\phi\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq\|\nabla K\|_{TV}\,\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}.

(2.2)

Conversely, assume this condition holds. Let
\{\omega_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
be a standard mollifier. We have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\DIV\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\,\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{K}\,\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

so passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
and taking supremum over all
\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 1
we deduce (
2.1
).

Hypothesis
(H4)
can be better viewed in the Fourier space. Indeed, with
K=K_{ij}
and
\widehat{K}
its Fourier transform, it is sufficient by Plancherel’s theorem to check that there holds

\int_{|\xi|>1}|\xi|^{2}\,|\widehat{K}(\xi)|^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi<\infty,

(2.3)

where we ignore the set
\{|\xi|\leq 1\}
as the Fourier transform of an integrable function is bounded. Condition (
2.3
) is satisfied for
K(x)=\mathds{1}_{Q}
where
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded cube since one can easily compute
|\widehat{K}(\xi)|\approx{C}\,{|\xi|^{-d}}
for large
\xi
. Condition (
2.3
) is also satisfied by
K(x)=\mathds{1}_{B_{r}}
where
B_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a ball of radius
r
since
\widehat{K}(\xi)=r^{d/2}\,|\xi|^{-d/2}\,J_{d/2}(r\,|\xi|)
where
J_{d/2}
is a Bessel function of the first kind (see, e.g.,
[
75
, Lemma 12.2]
) and it is known that for large arguments
\xi
we have
|J_{d/2}(\xi)|\approx C\,|\xi|^{-1/2}
(see, e.g.,
[
75
, Prop. 5.6]
).

We also comment that hypothesis
(H4)
is implied by
(H1)
and
(H2)
. Indeed, as above, we consider function
K\ast K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}
which is clearly differentiable. Let
\psi
be such that
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 1
. Then,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla K\ast K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\,\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K\,\nabla K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|K\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\|\nabla K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\|\nabla K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|K\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\|\nabla K\|_{\text{TV}}

by (

). It follows that
\|\nabla K\ast K\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|K\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\|\nabla K\|_{\text{TV}}
so that passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
we deduce that
\nabla K\ast K
exists as a function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Hypothesis
(H5)
: in the scalar equation, this condition is superfluous. For

interacting species, it is a moderate generalization of a simpler symmetry condition

which ensures that the system has a gradient flow structure

We can roughly divide our results into the following:

Let us briefly explain the proofs of Theorems
2.1
–
2.3
. The existence results are obtained via compactness, departing from regularized equations. We now discuss the main apriori estimates leading to the proof of Theorem
2.1
. We first analyse the evolution of the entropy functional

\displaystyle H[u]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\log u\,{\rm d}x.

(2.5)

More precisely, differentiating
H[u(t)]
along solutions of (
1.1
), one can show that

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}H[u(t)]+D\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq m\,\|K\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Classical arguments using the evolution of the second moment allow us to estimate the entropy from below and control the entropy in bounded time intervals using the small mass condition.

Theorem
2.2
is a more subtle consequence of the gradient flow structure and so we can only prove it for symmetric kernels (see
(H3)
). More precisely, (
1.1
) is the Wasserstein gradient flow of the free energy functional, i.e. the weighted sum of the entropy and the interaction energy
\mathcal{K}[u]
of the system, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[u]:=D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\log u\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(K*u)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=:DH[u]+\mathcal{K}[u].

(2.6)

The dissipation of
\mathcal{F}[u(t)]
is given by

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\mathcal{F}[u(t)]=-\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f:=\sqrt{u}\,\nabla(D\log u+K*u).

Note that this allows one to estimate
\nabla u
in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
since
D\nabla u=f\,\sqrt{u}-u\,\nabla K\ast u
. Indeed, the first term can be bounded using the dissipation of the free energy and the conservation of mass. The second term
u\nabla K\ast u
can be split into small values of
u
, controlled by the bounded variation of
K
in
(H2)
, and large values of
u
, controlled by the uniform equi-integrability estimate from the entropy. We conclude that
\nabla\sqrt{u}\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
, which is precisely the information obtained in the small-mass case.

Improved regularity is first based on the novel condition
\nabla K\ast K\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, cf.
(H4)
. This allows one to obtain better estimates on the vector field
\nabla K\ast u
by testing (
1.1
) with
K\ast K\ast\Delta u
. The main point here is that while
K
itself may not be differentiable,
K\ast K
enjoys regularization by convolution and may be differentiable. Once we obtain bounds on
\nabla K\ast u
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\Delta K\ast u
in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
, we can show
u
is bounded (see Lemma
3.8
) and uniqueness can be established. The uniqueness result is obtained by usual energy methods with a modification that we study the evolution of the mixed local-nonlocal energy

\mathcal{E}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|K\ast u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

for some constant
C>0
. Combining two quantities allows us to prove that if
u
and
v
are regular solutions to (
1.1
) then
\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(u-v)\leq C(t)\,\mathcal{E}(u-v)
which implies uniqueness. Under some additional technical requirements on the initial data, we can improve the integrability of
\nabla u
and ultimately apply Schauder estimates to obtain the classical differentiability of the solution.

Finally, the methods discussed above are flexible enough to be extended to the case of the
n
-species systems, see Theorems
4.1
-
4.3
in Section
4
.

3.
The Scalar Equation

In this section, we will prove well-posedness in the scalar case, Theorems
2.1
-
2.3
. This will simplify the exposition of the general
n
-species case. We start by introducing some notations needed and the notion of weak solution we will work with directly in the general
n
-species case.

3.1.
Preliminaries

We consider our problem over
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
for
d\geq 1
, and denote by
Q_{T}:={\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times(0,T)
for some
T>0
fixed. For
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
denotes the usual Lebesgue space over the spatial domain
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, with
\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}
the essential supremum of
u
over
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. For
1\leq p,q<\infty
,
L^{q}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
then denotes the typical Lebesgue space over
Q_{T}
with norm

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{L^{q}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}:=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left\|v(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{q}{\rm d}t\right)^{1/q},

(3.1)

and when
1\leq p\leq q=\infty

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}:=\esssup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|v(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}.

(3.2)

In cases where
p=q
, for notational brevity we simply write
L^{p}(Q_{T}):=L^{p}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
. We denote by
C^{1+\sigma/2,2+\sigma}_{loc}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
the class a functions which are (locally) twice differentiable in space and once differentiable in time (in the classical sense), with its second order spatial derivatives and first order time derivative being Hölder continuous for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
. We understand a
weak solution
to problems (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) in the following sense.

Definition 3.1

(weak solution)
.

We call
\mathbf{u}:=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})
a
weak solution
to problem (
1.2
) corresponding to the initial data
\mathbf{u_{0}}:=(u_{10},\ldots,u_{n0})
if

{u_{i}}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),\quad\nabla\sqrt{u_{i}}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\tfrac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial t}\in L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;W^{-1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\mbox{ with }q=\begin{cases}\frac{2d}{2d-1}&\mbox{ for }d\neq 2,\\
\frac{6}{5}&\mbox{ for }d=2,\end{cases}

(3.3)

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, and for all test functions
\phi_{i}\in L^{4}(0,T;W^{1,q^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left<(u_{i})_{t},\phi_{i}\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+D_{i}\iint_{Q_{T}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\phi_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\iint_{Q_{T}}u_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(K_{ij}*\nabla u_{j})\cdot\nabla\phi_{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(3.4)

were
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
denotes the dual pairing between
W^{-1,q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
W^{1,q^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
with
1/q+1/q^{\prime}=1
, and the initial data is satisfied in the sense of
W^{-1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We call
\mathbf{u}
a
global weak solution
if (
3.4
) holds for all
T>0
.

The following lemma shows where the exponent
q
comes from.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})})
,
\nabla\sqrt{u}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then,
u\,\nabla K\ast u
and
\nabla u
belong to the same space
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with
q
given by (
3.3
) and with the norm being controlled only by the norm of
K
in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and by the norms of
u
and
\nabla\sqrt{u}
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})})
and
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, respectively.

Proof.

First, assume
d\neq 2
. We analyse two terms separately.

Term
u\,\nabla K\ast u
.
By the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality,
\sqrt{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, and so
u
is in
L^{1}(0,T;L^{\frac{d}{d-2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Interpolating with
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
we obtain
u\in L^{\frac{1}{\theta}}(0,T;L^{\frac{d}{d-2\theta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
. Since
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\nabla u=2\,\nabla\sqrt{u}\,\sqrt{u}
is in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we deduce that
\nabla K\ast u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Finally, by Hölder’s inequality we obtain that
u\,\nabla K\ast u\in L^{\frac{2}{2\theta+1}}(0,T;L^{\frac{d}{d-2\theta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. The last step requires
\theta\leq\frac{1}{2}
so that
\frac{2}{2\theta+1}\geq 1
.

Term
\nabla u
.
From the computations above,
\sqrt{u}\in L^{\frac{2}{\theta^{\prime}}}(0,T;L^{\frac{2d}{d-2\theta^{\prime}}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for any
\theta^{\prime}\in[0,1]
. By writing
\nabla u=2\,\nabla\sqrt{u}\,\sqrt{u}
and using Hölder’s inequality we obtain
\nabla u\in L^{\frac{2}{\theta^{\prime}+1}}(0,T;L^{\frac{d}{d-\theta^{\prime}}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for any
\theta^{\prime}\in[0,1]
.

We now choose
\theta=\frac{1}{4}
and
\theta^{\prime}=2\theta
so that
\nabla u
and
u\nabla K\ast u
belong to the same space
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{\frac{2d}{2d-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. For
d=2
, the regularity
\sqrt{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
is not attained and we only know
\sqrt{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\frac{2}{\delta}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
for all
\delta\in(0,1]
. Repeating the calculations above we obtain that
\nabla u
and
u\nabla K\ast u
belong to the same space
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{\frac{4}{3+\delta}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
. Choosing
\delta=\frac{1}{3}
we obtain
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
as desired.
∎

In fact, for many kernels we obtain a
strong
solution to problems (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) in the following sense.

(strong solution)
.

. We call the strong solution

Finally, we refer to a

solution to problems (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) in the following sense.

.

We call
\mathbf{u}:=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})

) corresponding to the initial data

We begin with the proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Consider the entropy functional
H[u(t)]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\log u\,{\rm d}x
. Taking the derivative with respect to time, using the conservation of mass, and integrating by parts we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}H[u(t)]

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\nabla u}{u}\left(D\,\nabla u+u\nabla(K*u)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla u\,\nabla(K*u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.15)

We first control the second term of (
3.15
) using Hölder’s inequality and Young’s convolution inequality:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla u\nabla(K*u){\rm d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|K*\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}.

(3.16)

Furthermore, we can estimate
\nabla u
via Hölder’s inequality and the conservation of mass

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{u}\,\frac{\left|\nabla u\right|}{\sqrt{u}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\leq\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=m\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.17)

Combining estimates (
3.16
)-(
3.17
), (
3.15
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}H[u(t)]+4c_{1,1}\left\|\nabla\sqrt{u}(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\leq 0.

(3.18)

Integrating both sides from
0
to
t
yields

\displaystyle H[u(t)]+4c_{1,1}\left\|\nabla\sqrt{u}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{t})}^{2}\leq H[u_{0}].

(3.19)

We also notice that by Hölder’s inequality, Young’s convolution inequality and (
3.17
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{u}\,\nabla(K*u)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\left|K*\nabla u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq m\left\|K*\nabla u\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq m\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4m^{2}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\nabla\sqrt{u}(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

We seek to combine these estimates to obtain an estimate on

by considering the set
\underline{\Omega}:=\{(t,x):u\leq 1\}
and splitting it for two subsets

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi_{\underline{\Omega}}u\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in(0,1)}\left|\sqrt{\xi}\log\xi\right|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\left|x\right|^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\{e^{-\left|x\right|^{2}/2}\leq u\leq 1\}}u\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

where

where

\displaystyle I[u(t)]\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}I[u(s)]{\rm d}s+8\,\varepsilon(D^{2}+m^{2}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2})\left\|\nabla\sqrt{u}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}+I[u_{0}].

\ell>1
to be chosen sufficiently large. We then estimate as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|u\nabla K*u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}=\left\|\chi_{\underline{\Omega}}u\nabla K*u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}+\left\|\chi_{\overline{\Omega}}u\nabla K*u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

\displaystyle\leq\ell\left\|\nabla K*u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}+\left\|\frac{u\left|\log u\right|}{\log\ell}\nabla K*u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\ell\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}+\frac{1}{\log\ell}\left\|u\left|\log u\right|\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}\left\|\nabla K*u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\ell\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}+\frac{1}{\log\ell}\left\|u\left|\log u\right|\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\ell m\sqrt{T}+\frac{C_{2}}{\log\ell}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}.

(3.31)

The first inequality uses the properties of the sets
\underline{\Omega}
,
\overline{\Omega}
; the second inequality uses hypothesis
(H2)
and Hölder’s inequality; the third inequality uses Young’s convolution inequality; the fourth inequality uses hypothesis
(H1)
and the previously obtained bounds on
u
and
u\left|\log u\right|
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
. Combining (
3.30
)-(
3.31
) we have shown that

\displaystyle D\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}\leq\sqrt{m}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}+C_{K}\ell m\sqrt{T}+\frac{C_{2}}{\log\ell}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))},

(3.32)

whence choosing
\ell
sufficiently large yields a uniform estimate on
\nabla u
in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Consequently, Hölder’s inequality, Young’s convolution inequality and hypothesis
(H1)
yields

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{u}\nabla K*u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sqrt{u}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}^{1/2}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{m}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

(3.33)

and so (
3.33
) paired with the uniform estimate obtained from (
3.32
) yields estimate (
3.13
).

Finally, as
2D\nabla\sqrt{u}=f-\sqrt{u}\nabla K*u
, estimate (
3.33
) and the bound on the dissipation
f
in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
shows that
\nabla\sqrt{u}
is bounded in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
and estimate (
3.14
) follows.
∎

3.3.
Improved estimates

In this section we improve the estimates obtained in Lemmas
3.5
-
3.6
. As the the following result uses only the estimates on
\nabla\sqrt{u}
and
\sqrt{u}\nabla K*u
, the same argument applies assuming the conditions of either Lemma
3.5
or Lemma
3.6
are met.

First, we obtain estimates to
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
for any
2\leq p<\infty
and on
\nabla u
in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
with no additional assumptions required.

Lemma 3.7

(Improved estimates with no further assumptions)
.

Assume the conditions of Lemma
3.5
(Lemma
3.6
) are satisfied.
Then, for any
p\in(1,\infty)
, if
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then

\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq e^{C\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}p/D}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})},

(3.34)

where
C
is given in Lemma
3.5
(Lemma
3.6
). Moreover, if
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then

\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\leq D^{-1}\,\tilde{C}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})},

(3.35)

where
\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}(C)
.

Proof.

We consider the quantity
\tfrac{1}{p}\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{p}
for
p\in(1,\infty)
. Taking the derivative with respect to time and integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-D(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}u^{p-2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p-1}\nabla u\cdot K*\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{4D(p-1)}{p^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u^{p/2}\right|^{2}{\rm d}x-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p-1}\nabla u\cdot K*\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.36)

We use the first term on the right hand side of (
3.36
) to control the second term using Cauchy’s inequality with
\varepsilon
and Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p-1}\nabla uK*\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2(p-1)}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla u^{p/2}u^{p/2}K*\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon(p-1)}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u^{p/2}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\tfrac{(p-1)}{\varepsilon p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p}\left|K*\nabla u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon(p-1)}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u^{p/2}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\tfrac{(p-1)}{\varepsilon p}\left\|(K*\nabla u)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.37)

Choosing
\varepsilon=2D/p
we combine estimate (
3.37
) with (
3.36
) to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\tfrac{2D(p-1)}{p^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u^{p/2}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{(p-1)}{2D}\left\|(K*\nabla u)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.38)

Grönwall’s lemma implies that

\displaystyle\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{3}(T)p/D}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})},

(3.39)

where
C_{3}(T)=\left\|K*\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{2}\leq C\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}
by estimate (
3.9
) (or (
3.12
)). Taking the supremum over
t\in(0,T)
yields estimate (
3.34
). Note carefully that this estimate does not allow one to take
p\to\infty
as the coefficient on the right hand side depends critically on
p
.

Returning to estimate (
3.38
), we fix
p=2
and integrate both sides from
0
to
T
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D\iint_{Q_{T}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq D^{-1}C\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}.

(3.40)

Using estimate (
3.39
) with
p=2
and rearranging the result yields estimate (
3.35
).
∎

3.4.
Existence of weak solutions (proof of Theorems
2.1
-
2.2
)

We are now prepared to prove Theorems
2.1
-
2.2
.

Proof of Theorems
2.1
-
2.2
.

We present the details for Theorem
2.1
only; the conclusion of Theorem
2.2
follows in an identical fashion by using the estimates obtained in Lemma
3.6
, which are the same as those obtained in Lemma
3.5
for the small mass regime.

To start, we consider approximate solutions for any
\varepsilon>0
given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}=D\Delta u^{\varepsilon}+\nabla\cdot(u^{\varepsilon}\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}),

(3.41)

satisfying the initial data
u^{\varepsilon}(0,x)=u_{0}(x)
for each
\varepsilon>0
, where
{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
denotes the standard mollifier. We seek to pass the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. First note that a unique, positive classical solution
u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)
solving (
3.41
) with
u(\cdot,x)=u_{0}(x)
exists for each
\varepsilon>0
by the methods used in the proof of, e.g.,
[
28
, Theorem 2.2]
. Moreover, by Young’s convolution inequality we have that

\left\|K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}=\left\|K\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})},

and so if the small mass condition (
2.4
) is satisfied, it is also satisfied for the regularized problem for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Finally, for each
t>0
, the solution
u^{\varepsilon}
is strictly positive by the strong maximum principle as in (
3.81
).

By Lemma
3.5
, we have the following uniform bounds on the approximate solutions
u^{\varepsilon}
:

(A)

\{\nabla\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
;

(B)

\{\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
;

(C)

\{\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{\varepsilon}\left|x\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded.

(A) implies that
2\nabla u^{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}\nabla\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}
belongs to
L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
uniformly in
\varepsilon
. Moreover,
\{\frac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;W^{-1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for
q=\begin{cases}\frac{2d}{2d-1}&\mbox{ for }d\neq 2,\\
\frac{6}{5}&\mbox{ for }d=2,\end{cases}
. Indeed, given any
\phi\in L^{4}(0,T;W^{1,q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
Hölder’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq D\left\|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\left\|\nabla\phi\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T;L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}

\displaystyle+\left\|u^{\varepsilon}\,\nabla K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\left\|\nabla\phi\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T;L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\,(1+D)\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{4}(0,T;W^{1,q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))},

where
C_{5}
is a constant given by Lemma
3.2
which depends only on
\|K\ast\omega^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
and the norms of
u^{\varepsilon}
and
\nabla\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})})
and
L^{2}(Q_{T})
, respectively.

Consequently, (A) paired with the conservation of mass implies that
u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
) uniformly in
\varepsilon
. We then conclude by the Aubin-Lions lemma with

W:=\left\{v\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})):\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial t}\in L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;W^{-1,q}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\right\}

that there exists a subsequence (still denoted by
\varepsilon
) such that

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}\to u\quad\text{ strongly in }\quad L^{1}_{loc}(Q_{T})

(3.42)

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. This implies that, perhaps for another subsequence,

\displaystyle\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}\to\sqrt{u}\quad\text{ strongly in }\quad L^{2}_{loc}(Q_{T})

(3.43)

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
by the Vitali convergence theorem. By (C) it is easy to see that given any
\delta>0
, for
R
chosen sufficiently large there holds
\left\|u^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}))}<\delta
, independent of
\varepsilon
, and so
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
has compact closure in
L^{1}(Q_{T})
. In particular, the strong convergence obtained in (
3.42
)-(
3.43
) holds globally.

Next, (B) implies that there exists
\xi\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}\to\xi\quad\text{ weakly in }\quad L^{2}(Q_{T})

(3.44)

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. We claim that
\xi=\sqrt{u}\nabla K*u
. To identify the limit, we may identify it in
L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
. Notice first that
\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
since
\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, and so has a weak
∗
limit in the same space. Hence, there holds

\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}\to\nabla K*u\quad\text{ weak* in }\quad L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Furthermore, the limit can be identified as
\nabla K*u=K*\nabla u
because of the
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
estimate on
\nabla u^{\varepsilon}
. Then, as
\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}
converges strongly in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
, given any
\phi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
there holds

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}-\sqrt{u}K*\nabla u\right)\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left([\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{u}\,]\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}]\right)\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{u}\phi\left(\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}-K*\nabla u\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\left\|\sqrt{u^{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{u}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\left\|\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{u}\phi(\nabla K*{\omega}^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}-K*\nabla u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right|\to 0

(3.45)

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Notice that the second term converges to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
since
\sqrt{u}\phi\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
, the predual of
L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
. Hence, (
3.43
)-(
3.44
) hold and
\xi=\sqrt{u}\nabla(K*u)
as asserted.

These convergence results allow us to conclude that in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
there holds

\int_{0}^{T}\left<\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial t},\phi\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+D\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}^{d}u\nabla(K*u)\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

for any
\phi
smooth and compactly supported. By (A)-(B), the
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
-regularity of
\nabla u
and
u\,\nabla(K\ast u)
, the
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;W^{-1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
-regularity of
\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}
and a density argument, this weak formulation holds for any
\phi\in L^{4}(0,T;W^{1,q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Finally, by writing
u(t,\cdot)-u_{0}=\int_{0}^{t}\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
, we see that
u(t,\cdot)\to u_{0}(\cdot)
in
W^{-1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and indeed the initial data is satisfied in the sense of
W^{-1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As
T>0
was arbitrary, the solution obtained is in fact global.

To conclude, we recall that by Lemma
3.7
we have that
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
for all
1\leq p<\infty
whenever
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\nabla u\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
if
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

3.5.
Higher order estimates

Under the additional hypothesis
(H4)
and further conditions on the initial data we can obtain better estimates on
\nabla u
,
\Delta u
, and a uniform estimate on
u
over
Q_{T}
.

Lemma 3.8

(Higher order estimates)
.

Assume that
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\nabla u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
K*u_{0}\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
p>2
. Suppose hypothesis
(H4)
holds in addition to the conditions of Lemma
3.5
(resp. Lemma
3.6
). Then, there exists a constant
\tilde{C}
so that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C};

(3.46)

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta u\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C};

(3.47)

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C},

(3.48)

where
\tilde{C}=\tilde{C}(C,\left\|\nabla(\tilde{K}*K)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})})
, and
C
is given in Lemma
3.5
(resp. Lemma
3.6
).

Proof.

Before estimating
u
and its derivatives, we first obtain improved estimates on
K*\nabla u
and
K*\Delta u
. To this end, recall that
\tilde{K}(\cdot):=K(-\cdot)
and multiply (
1.1
) by
\tilde{K}*K*\Delta u
, integrate over
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, and integrate by parts to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla(K*u)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta(K*u)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{K}*K*\Delta u\nabla\cdot(u\nabla(K*u))

\displaystyle=-D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta(K*u)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta(K*u)K*(\nabla u\cdot\nabla(K*u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,\Delta(K*u)(\tilde{K}*\Delta(K*u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.49)

We first estimate the second term on the right hand side of (
3.49
) via Cauchy’s inequality with
\varepsilon=D/2
, followed by Young’s convolution inequality and Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta(K*u)

\displaystyle\,K*(\nabla u\cdot K*\nabla u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{D}{4}\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+D^{-1}\left\|K*(\nabla u\cdot\nabla(K*u))\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{D}{4}\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+D^{-1}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\nabla u\cdot\nabla(K*u)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{D}{4}\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+D^{-1}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\nabla(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle=:\tfrac{D}{4}\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+D^{-1}a^{2}(t)\left\|\nabla(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2},

(3.50)

where
a(t):=\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}
. Notice that
a^{2}(t)\in L^{1}(0,T)
for any fixed
T>0
by Lemma
3.7
.

Next, we estimate the third term appearing in (
3.49
) via two applications of Hölder’s inequality followed by Cauchy’s inequality with
\varepsilon=D/2
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,\Delta(K*u)(\tilde{K}*\Delta(K*u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\Delta(K*u)(\tilde{K}*\Delta(K*u))\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\tilde{K}*(\Delta(K*u))\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+D^{-1}\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\tilde{K}*(\Delta(K*u))\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2},

(3.51)

We must ensure that the second product belongs to
L^{1}(0,T)
. By Lemma
3.7
, we know that
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
. For the remaining term, we use the fact that

\displaystyle\tilde{K}*\Delta(K*u)=\nabla(\tilde{K}*K)*\nabla u.

Hence, we may estimate via Young’s convolution inequality

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{K}*(\Delta(K*u))\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla(\tilde{K}*K)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2},

(3.52)

from which we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,\Delta(K*u)(\tilde{K}*\Delta(K*u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle+D^{-1}b^{2}(t)

(3.53)

where

\displaystyle b(t):=\left\|\nabla(\tilde{K}*K)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})},

and
b^{2}(t)\in L^{1}(0,T)
by hypothesis
(H4)
and by estimate (
3.35
) of Lemma
3.7
.

Combining estimates (
3.50
) and (
3.53
) with (
3.49
), we finally obtain

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla(K*u)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta(K*u)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq D^{-1}\left(a^{2}(t)\left\|\nabla(K*u))\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+b^{2}(t)\right).

(3.54)

By Gronwall’s lemma we conclude that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|\nabla(K*u)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq D^{-1}A(T)\left(\left\|\nabla(K*u_{0})\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}b^{2}(t){\rm d}t\right),

(3.55)

where
A(t):=e^{\int_{0}^{t}a^{2}(s){\rm d}s}
. Hence,
\nabla(K*u)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Returning to estimate (
3.54
), we extract the second term on the left hand side and use estimate (
3.55
) to conclude that in fact

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta(K*u)\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq 2D^{-1}\left(\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|\nabla(K*u)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|a(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}+\left\|b(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\right),

(3.56)

and so
\Delta(K*u)\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
.

We now improve the estimate on
\Delta(K*u)
from
L^{2}(Q_{T})
to
L^{2}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
for some
p>2
. To this end, notice first that if
u
solves the heat equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-D\Delta u=g\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})),

for some function
g(t,x)
, then by the linearity of the convolution we have also that
K*u
solves

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(K*u)-D\Delta(K*u)=K*g.

In our case, using the improved estimates on
K*\nabla u
,
K*\Delta u
, we have

g=\nabla u\,K*\nabla u+u\,K*\Delta u\,\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})),

so that
K*g\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
for any
q\in[1,\infty]
since
K\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Hence, by the maximal regularity of the heat equation (see, e.g.,
[
49
, Theorem 3.1]
), we find that
\Delta(K*u)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
where
p
is an exponent such that
K*u_{0}\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We are now ready to obtain estimates (
3.46
)-(
3.47
). Multiplying (
1.1
) by
\Delta u
and integrating over
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|u\,\Delta u(K*\Delta u)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\,\Delta u(K*\nabla u)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.57)

We estimate the right hand side via Cauchy’s inequality. For the first term we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|u\,\Delta u(K*\Delta u)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|u\right|^{2}\left|K*\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D^{-1}\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|K*\Delta u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D^{-1}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}^{2}\left\|K*\Delta u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D^{-1}e^{8C(T)/D}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\left\|K*\Delta u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2},

(3.58)

where we have used Hölder’s inequality and that
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
for any exponent
q:=2p/(p-2)\in(1,\infty)
. From our improved estimates on
\Delta(K*u)
we have already shown that
\left\|K*\Delta u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T)
for some
p>2
.

For the second term, we again use Cauchy’s inequality

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\Delta u(K*\nabla u)\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|K*\nabla u\right|^{2}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D^{-1}\left\|K*\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}{\rm d}x,

where
\left\|K*\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\in L^{1}(0,T)
since
\nabla u\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
and
K\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Hence, (
3.57
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{D}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{1}(t)+C_{2}(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(3.59)

for functions
C_{1}(t)
,
C_{2}(t)\in L^{1}(0,T)
. Grönwall’s lemma implies
\Delta u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
and
\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
from which estimates (
3.46
)-(
3.47
) follow.

We now obtain a uniform bound on the solution
u(t,x)
via an Alikakos type argument
[
1
]
, which arises as a consequence of
\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))\Rightarrow K*\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
. Set
C_{0}:=\left\|K*\nabla u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}
. To begin, we return to the right hand side of estimate (
3.36
) and apply Cauchy’s inequality

\displaystyle-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p-1}\nabla uK*\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{2(p-1)}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p/2}\nabla(u^{p/2})K*\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{2(p-1)}{p}C_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p/2}\left|\nabla u^{p/2}\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\varepsilon\left|\nabla u^{p/2}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}u^{p}\right){\rm d}x,

where
\varepsilon>0
is to be chosen. We then recall the Nash’s inequality: there exists
C_{N}>0
such that for
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there holds

\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{N}\,\|g\|^{4/(d+2)}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\|\nabla g\|^{2d/(d+2)}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\delta\,C_{N}^{(d+2)/d}\,\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\delta^{-d/2}\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

where the second step is a consequence of the Young’s inequality with conjugate exponents
\frac{d+2}{2}
and
\frac{d+2}{d}
, and
\delta>0
is to be chosen. We apply it with
g=u^{p/2}
and we combine it with estimate (
3.36
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p}{\rm d}x\leq

\displaystyle-\tfrac{4D(p-1)}{p^{2}}\|\nabla u^{p/2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\varepsilon\,C_{0}\|\nabla u^{p/2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}C_{0}\left(\delta\,C_{N}^{(d+2)/d}\,\|\nabla u^{p/2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\delta^{-d/2}\|u^{p/2}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\right).

(3.60)

We now set
\delta\,C_{N}^{(d+2)/d}=\varepsilon^{2}
and choose
\varepsilon=\tfrac{2D(p-1)}{C_{0}p^{2}}
so that

2C_{0}\varepsilon=\frac{4D(p-1)}{p^{2}}\Rightarrow\varepsilon=\frac{2D(p-1)}{C_{0}p^{2}}.

It follows that the gradient term vanishes and (
3.60
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{6}\,\varepsilon^{-d-1}\left\|u^{p/2}(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\leq C_{7}\,p^{d+1}\left\|u^{p/2}(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2},

(3.61)

where
C_{6}
,
C_{7}
depend on
C_{N}
,
C_{0}
,
D
but do not depend on
p
. Integrating both sides from
0\to t
yields

\displaystyle\left\|u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{p}\leq\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{p}+C_{7}\,p^{d+2}T\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p/2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{p}.

(3.62)

Without loss of generality, for
T
fixed we may assume that

\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{p}\leq C_{7}\,p^{d+2}\,T\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p/2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}^{p},

for all
p\geq p^{*}
for some
p^{*}\geq 1
. Otherwise, there exists a sequence
\{p_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that
p_{k}\to+\infty
as
k\to+\infty
so that

\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p_{k}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{p_{k}}\geq C_{7}\,{p_{k}}^{d+2}\,T\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p_{k}/2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}^{p_{k}}

from which the boundedness of
u
follows. Set
C_{8}=(2C_{7}T)^{1/(d+2)}
and
M(p):=\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}.

We then have that

M(p)\leq(C_{8}\,p)^{(d+2)/p}M(p/2),\quad\forall p\geq p^{*}.

Take
i\geq 1
so that
2^{i}\geq p^{*}
. For any fixed
k
we proceed iteratively to obtain

\displaystyle M(2^{k})\,

\displaystyle\leq\,(C_{8}2^{k})^{(d+2)/{2^{k}}}M(2^{k-1})\,\leq\,(C_{8}2^{k})^{(d+2)/{2^{k}}}(C_{8}2^{(k-1)})^{(d+2)/{2^{k-1}}}M(2^{k-2})

\displaystyle\ldots\leq M(2^{i-1})\prod_{j=i}^{k}(C_{8}2^{j})^{(d+2)/2^{j}}.

(3.63)

It follows that
M(2^{k})
is bounded independent of
k
so long as

\lim_{k\to\infty}\prod_{j=i}^{k}(C_{8}2^{j})^{(d+2)/2^{j}}<\infty\iff\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}\frac{(d+2)\log(C_{8}2^{j})}{2^{j}}<\infty,

which readily follows from the fact that
2^{j}\gg(d+2)\,j
. Hence,
u
is uniformly bounded in
Q_{T}
for any
T>0
fixed and estimate (
3.48
) follows.
∎

Lemma 3.9

(Improved integrability of gradient)
.

Suppose
\nabla u_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
p\in[2,\infty)
. Under the conditions of Lemma
3.8
there exists a constant
\tilde{C}_{0}
such that there holds

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\leq\tilde{C}_{0},

(3.64)

where
\tilde{C}_{0}=\tilde{C}_{0}(\tilde{C},p,\left\|\nabla u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})})
, and
\tilde{C}
is given in Lemma
3.8
.

Proof.

Under the conditions of Lemma
3.8
, we have that

u\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T});\quad\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega));\quad\Delta u\in L^{2}(Q_{T}).

Assume that
u
is a smooth solution to problem (
1.1
). For any
p\in[2,\infty)
, we multiply the equation for
u
by

-\mbox{div}\left(\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)=-(p-1)\,\Delta u\,|\nabla u|^{p-2}

and integrate over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\nabla u\,\nabla u_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\,\Delta u\,(D\Delta u+\nabla uK*\nabla u+uK*\Delta u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.65)

Clearly, the left hand side of (
3.65
) equals
\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
and so rearrangement of (
3.65
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p\,(p-1)}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}{\rm d}x\leq

\displaystyle-D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}{\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-1}\left|\Delta u\right|\left|K*\nabla u\right|{\rm d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}u\left|\Delta u\right|\left|K*\Delta u\right|

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle-D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}{\rm d}x+I_{1}+I_{2}.

(3.66)

We estimate
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
separately. For
I_{1}
, Cauchy’s inequality yields

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{D}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}{\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2D}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}\left|K*\nabla u\right|^{2}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{D}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}{\rm d}x+C_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}{\rm d}x,

(3.67)

where

C_{1}:=\frac{\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R^{d}})}^{2}\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}}{2D}<\infty,

since
\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. For
I_{2}
, we first estimate via Cauchy’s inequality as

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\frac{D}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}\left|\Delta u\right|^{2}{\rm d}x+C_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}{\rm d}x,

(3.68)

where

C_{2}(t):=\frac{\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}^{2}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\left\|\Delta u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}}{2D}\in L^{1}(0,T),

since
u\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
and
\Delta u\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
.

To estimate
\int\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}{\rm d}x
, we first suppose that
p>4
(the case
p=4
is direct) and apply Young’s inequality for products with
p^{\prime}=(p-2)/(p-4)
,
q^{\prime}=(p-2)/2
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p-2}{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p(p-4)/(p-2)}\left|\nabla u\right|^{4/(p-2)}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}+\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}){\rm d}x.

(3.69)

Combining estimates (
3.67
)-(
3.69
) with estimate (
3.65
) leaves

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p\,(p-1)}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}{\rm d}x\leq

\displaystyle\left(C_{1}+C_{2}(t)\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla u\right|^{p}{\rm d}x+C_{2}(t)\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

(3.70)

Since
C_{2}(t)\in L^{1}(0,T)
, and since

\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\Rightarrow C_{2}(t)\left\|\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T),

we obtain the estimate (
3.64
) via Grönwall’s lemma. The case when
p\in(2,4)
is obtained via interpolation between the cases
p=2
and
p=4
.
∎

3.6.
Uniqueness

The higher regularity results from the previous subsection allow us to show the following uniqueness result.

Theorem 3.10
.

Suppose hypothesis
(H4)
holds. Then any nonnegative solution
u
solving problem (
1.1
) belonging to the class

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}));

\displaystyle\nabla u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}));

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(Q_{T})

is unique.

Proof.

Assume there are two nonnegative solutions
u
and
v
. We write

\displaystyle(u-v)_{t}-D\Delta(u-v)=\nabla\cdot((u-v)\nabla K*u)+\nabla\cdot(v\nabla K*(u-v)).

(3.71)

Set
w=u-v
. Testing (
3.71
) against
w
, integrating over
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w\nabla(K*u)\nabla w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v\nabla(K*w)\nabla w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla w^{2}\nabla(K*u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v\nabla(K*w)\nabla w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.72)

We estimate each term separately. For the first term, we integrate by parts and use that
\Delta u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))\Rightarrow K*\Delta u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
to obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla w^{2}\nabla(K*u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w^{2}K*\Delta u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|K*\Delta u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

For the second term, we apply Cauchy’s inequality with
\varepsilon=D\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}^{-1}
to obtain

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v\nabla(K*w)\nabla w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(D\left|\nabla w\right|^{2}+\frac{\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}^{2}}{D}\left|K*\nabla w\right|^{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Combining these estimates, (
3.72
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{D}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|K*\nabla w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(3.73)

where
C_{1}(t)=\left\|K*\Delta u(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
C_{2}=D^{-1}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}^{2}
.

Next, we test (
3.71
) against
\tilde{K}*K*w
, integrate over
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and integrate by parts to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|K*w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla K*w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w\nabla(K*u)\nabla(\tilde{K}*K*w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v\nabla(K*w)\nabla(\tilde{K}*K*w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.74)

Since
\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
, there holds
\nabla(K*u)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
so long as
K\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Moreover,

\left\|\nabla(\tilde{K}*K*w)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq\left\|\nabla(\tilde{K}*K)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}=C_{3}\left\|w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})},

where
C_{3}=\left\|\nabla(\tilde{K}*K)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}<\infty
under hypothesis
(H4)
. Thus, the first term of (
3.74
) can be estimated as

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w\nabla(K*u)\nabla(\tilde{K}*K*w){\rm d}x\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla(K*u)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla(\tilde{K}*K*w)(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{3}\left\|\nabla(K*u)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}\left\|w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}.

(3.75)

For the second term of (
3.74
), we use that
v\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))\Rightarrow v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))
and apply Cauchy’s inequality:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v\nabla(K*w)\nabla(\tilde{K}*K*w){\rm d}x\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|v(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla K*w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left\|\nabla\tilde{K}*K*w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{3}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}\left(\varepsilon\left\|\nabla K*w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left\|w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\right).

(3.76)

Choosing
\varepsilon=\frac{D}{2C_{3}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}))}}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|K*w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{D}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla K*w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\left\|w(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2},

(3.77)

where
C_{4}<\infty
.

We now combine estimates (
3.73
) and (
3.77
). To this end, we multiply estimate (
3.73
) by
\frac{D}{4C_{2}}
and add the result to (
3.77
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left(\frac{D}{4C_{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|K*w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)+\frac{D^{2}}{8C_{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla K*w\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq(C_{1}(t)+C_{4})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq(C_{1}(t)+C_{4})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(w^{2}+\left|K*w\right|^{2}\right){\rm d}x.

(3.78)

Grönwall’s lemma implies that
w\equiv 0
a.e. in
Q_{T}
, and uniqueness follows.
∎

3.7.
Existence of unique strong and classical solutions (proof of Theorems
2.3
-
2.4
)

We are now ready to prove Theorems
2.3
-
2.4
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

By Lemma
3.8
, the global weak solution obtained in either Theorem
2.1
(under the addition of hypothesis
(H4)
) or Theorem
2.2
belongs to the class

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}));

\displaystyle\nabla u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}));

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(Q_{T}).

By Theorem
3.10
the solution is unique. Directly from (
1.1
), the spatial regularity of
u
implies that
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
. Consequently,
u\in C(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and so we conclude that
\left\|u(t,\cdot)-u_{0}(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Working with the approximate solutions
u^{\varepsilon}
as in (
3.41
), we expand the right hand side

\frac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-D\Delta u^{\varepsilon}=\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon})+u^{\varepsilon}\Delta(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon}).

By Lemma
3.8
,
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
; moreover, by Lemma
3.9
,
\{\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for some
p>d+2
. Therefore,
\{\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Similarly, writing
\Delta(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon})=\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon})*\nabla u^{\varepsilon}
, Hypothesis
(H2)
allows one to conclude that
\{u^{\varepsilon}\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon})*\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is also bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

Hence,
L^{p}
-estimates for parabolic equations (see, e.g.,
[
74
, Theorem 6]
) imply that for any smooth, compact set
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
W^{1,2}_{p}((0,T)\times\Omega^{\prime})
, the space of functions twice weakly differentiable in space, once weakly differentiable in time, with derivatives belonging to
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega^{\prime})
. Therefore, as
p>d+2
, the Sobolev embedding (see, e.g.,
[
76
, Theorem 1.4.1]
) implies that in fact
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
C^{\sigma,1+\sigma}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Since
\Omega^{\prime}
is arbitrary, we have that
u^{\varepsilon}\in C^{\sigma,1+\sigma}_{loc}(Q_{T})
uniformly in
\varepsilon
.

We now set
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}:=\tfrac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial x_{i}}
for each
i=1,\ldots,d
. Working with each component, we again expand the right hand side of the equation solved by
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w^{\varepsilon}_{i}}{\partial t}-D\Delta w^{\varepsilon}_{i}=

\displaystyle\,\nabla w^{\varepsilon}_{i}\cdot\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon})+\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*w_{i}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\,+w^{\varepsilon}_{i}\Delta(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon})+u^{\varepsilon}\Delta(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*w^{\varepsilon}_{i}).

(3.79)

Since
u^{\varepsilon}
is bounded in
W^{1,2}_{p,loc}(Q_{T})
, we have that
\nabla w_{i}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{p}_{loc}(Q_{T})
component-wise, for each
i=1,\ldots,d
. Moreover, by the classical differentiability obtained,
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}
is locally uniformly bounded over
Q_{T}
for each
i=1,\ldots,d
.
Therefore, the first and third terms on the right hand side of (
3.79
) are uniformly bounded in
L^{p}_{loc}(Q_{T})
. Furthermore, by hypotheses
(H2)
,
(H6)
, and the boundedness of
u^{\varepsilon}
, the second and fourth terms on the right hand side of (
3.79
) are uniformly bounded in
L^{p}_{loc}(Q_{T})
. To understand why we use the compact support of
K
, we look closely at the second term of (
3.79
) in
L^{p}(B_{R}(0))
, the ball of radius
R>0
centred at
0
. Since
\nabla u^{\varepsilon}=(w_{1}^{\varepsilon},\ldots,w_{d}^{\varepsilon})
is locally uniformly bounded, we need only control for
x\in B_{R}(0)

\displaystyle\left|(\nabla K*\omega^{\varepsilon})*w_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon})(y)\right|\left|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x+y)\right|{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\supp{K}+B_{1}(0)}\left|\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon})(y)\right|\left|w_{i}^{\varepsilon}(x+y)\right|{\rm d}y,

(3.80)

and since
x\in B_{R}(0)
, we have
x+y\in B_{R+1}(0)+\supp{K}
. We may then use the local uniform boundedness of
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}
to control
(\nabla K*\omega^{\varepsilon})*w_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)
in
L^{p}((0,T)\times B_{R}(0))
for any
R>0
. The fourth term can be treated analogously.

Using once more
L^{p}
-estimates for parabolic equations and the Sobolev embedding, we conclude that
w_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\tfrac{\partial u^{\varepsilon}}{\partial x_{i}}\in C^{\sigma,1+\sigma}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega^{\prime}})
for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
, for any smooth, compact set
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consequently, we conclude that
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
C^{\sigma,2+\sigma}_{loc}(Q_{T})
for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
. In particular,
\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla(K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*u^{\varepsilon})+u^{\varepsilon}K*\omega^{\varepsilon}*\Delta u^{\varepsilon}\in C^{\sigma}_{loc}(Q_{T})
for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
.

Returning once more to the right hand side of the equation satisfied by
u^{\varepsilon}
, we have shown that
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}-D\Delta u^{\varepsilon}\in C^{\sigma}_{loc}(Q_{T})
. By Schauder estimates for parabolic equations (see, e.g.,
[
58
, Theorem 5.1.8]
),
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in
C^{1+\sigma/2,2+\sigma}_{loc}(Q_{T})
for some
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Consequently, there exists a subsequence (still labelled by
\varepsilon
) such that
u^{\varepsilon}\to u\in C^{1+\sigma^{\prime}/2,2+\sigma^{\prime}}_{loc}(Q_{T})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
for any
\sigma^{\prime}<\sigma
, and
u
is the classical solution to problem (
1.1
). By the assumed regularity of the initial data, we conclude that
u(t,x)\to u_{0}(x)
in
C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

We conclude by showing that the solution is strictly positive over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
t>0
. The approach is standard once we have the boundedness of
\Delta(K*u)
. Consider the auxiliary function
v:=e^{\lambda t}u
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
to be chosen later. Then
v
is nonnegative, nonconstant, and satisfies

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}-D\Delta v-\nabla v\cdot\nabla(K*u)-v[\Delta(K*u)+\lambda]=0.

(3.81)

By the smoothness of
u
and Hypothesis
(H6)
, we have that
K*\Delta u\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega^{\prime})
for any
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact, and any
T>0
fixed. Suppose
\Omega^{\prime}
is connected and that there exists a point
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T)\times\Omega^{\prime}
such that
v(t_{0},x_{0})=0
. Choosing
\lambda
large enough so that
\lambda-\left|K*\Delta u\right|\geq 0
over
(0,T)\times\Omega^{\prime}
, we find that
v_{t}-D\Delta v-\nabla(K*u)\cdot\nabla v\geq 0
over
(0,T)\times\Omega^{\prime}
. By the strong maximum principle, we conclude that
v
is constant over
(0,t_{0})\times\Omega^{\prime}
, a contradiction.

Since
\Omega^{\prime}
,
T
were arbitrary, we conclude that
v
cannot attain a minimum (of zero) in any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and must be positive everywhere in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

4.1.
Apriori estimates

As in the scalar case, we first establish some a priori estimates under the assumption that we have a positive smooth solution
\mathbf{u}:=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
solving problem (
1.2
). To this end, fix
n\geq 2
and define the following
n
-population counterparts to the entropy, total interaction energy, and free energy of the scalar system.

We introduce the entropy (sometimes referred to as the weighted
Shannon entropy

[
52
]
):

{\bf H}[\mathbf{u}(t)]:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\pi_{i}H[u_{i}(t)].

Then the total interaction energy is given by

{\mathbfcal{K}}[\mathbf{u}(t)]:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi_{i}u_{i}(K_{ij}*u_{j})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

and so the free energy for a system in detailed balance (hypothesis

) is given as

{\mathbfcal{F}}[\mathbf{u}(t)]:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}D_{i}\pi_{i}u_{i}\log u_{i}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi_{i}u_{i}(K_{ij}*u_{j})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x={\bf H}[\mathbf{u}(t)]+{\mathbfcal{K}}[\mathbf{u}(t)]

In the small mass regime, we use the following auxiliary functional, the sum of the individual entropies of the species:

{\bf H}_{S}[\mathbf{u}(t)]:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}H[u_{i}(t)].

We first prove the following, an
n
-species analogue to Lemma
3.5
.

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\sqrt{u_{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C;

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{u_{i}}\nabla K_{ij}*u_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}

(4.4)

for each

We first consider the quantity

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}{\bf H}_{S}[\mathbf{u}(t)]

where the control of the negative part of

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

As in the scalar case, the main idea is to write the dissipation for the

u_{i}\in C^{1+\sigma^{\prime}/2,2+\sigma^{\prime}}_{loc}(Q_{T})
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, for any
\sigma^{\prime}<\sigma
. This additional regularity paired with
(H6)
and the strong maximum principle yields the strict positivity of each component
u_{i}
.
∎

5.
Numerical Simulations

In this section, we present some illustrative numerical examples in one spatial dimension and discuss how they relate to the theory developed in the previous sections. A primary motivation of this exploration is that the long-time asymptotics for general kernels is not entirely clear. In particular, whether nontrivial stationary states solving the scalar equation (
1.1
) exist on the whole space remains a challenging question. Consider the following: in
[
27
, Theorem 1.3]
, the authors establish some minimal requirements on the kernel
K
such that solutions to (
1.1
) satisfy
\left\|u(t,\cdot)-G(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0
in the long-time limit, where
G(t,x)
is the heat kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. More precisely, in addition to some other technical requirements, they assume that
K\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\nabla W\in L^{d-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
\varepsilon>0
. On the other hand, as noted in
[
27
]
, when the kernel
K
is sufficiently singular (e.g.,
K(x)=-\alpha\ln\left|x\right|
,
\alpha>0
, as in the case of Keller-Segel, see
[
15
]
), nontrivial solution profiles may exist in the long-time limit. This leaves a gap in the regularity assumptions on
K
that is occupied by those kernels of bounded variation: they do not have a well-defined element
\nabla K
belonging to any
L^{p}
-space, nor are they singular. Relatedly, in
[
25
, Theorem 6.1]
, it is shown that for linear diffusion, global minimisers do not exist for kernels satisfying
\limsup_{\left|x\right|\to\infty}\nabla K\cdot x<2dD
; when
K
is only of bounded variation, it is not immediately clear whether those techniques used could produce the same result for lower regularity kernels.

We therefore demonstrate differences in dynamical behaviour for the prototypical top-hat kernel defined in (
1.4
), which satisfies
(H2)
but otherwise falls outside of the regularity regimes of currently known results. We consider several aspects: a. the magnitude of the strength of attraction to the potential; b. whether the potential is attractive/repulsive; c. differences between the single- and multi-species cases.

In our numerical simulations, it is difficult to determine whether a true (numerical) steady state has been reached, or whether
metastability
is present for this particular kernel
[
24
]
. In some cases (e.g., Figure
1
), solutions appear to decay as in the smooth-kernel cases; in other cases (e.g., Figures
2
-
3
), it is less clear that solutions will continue to decay. This leaves the following possibilities for general kernels:

(1)

Case I: there are no nontrivial stationary states for problem (
1.1
) on the whole space; if so, the observed “stationary” profiles may be metastable states, or otherwise some nontrivial state that appears due to an approximation of the whole space via a bounded domain with no-flux boundary conditions. This suggests that, as the numerical domain length
L
increases, the observed state would decrease.

(2)

Case II: there exist nontrivial stationary states for problem (
1.1
) on the whole space; if so, we may then ask several further questions, including how many exist, and their stability.

In either case, it is an interesting and challenging question to ask: what are the minimal regularity requirements on the kernel
K
so that nontrivial stationary states solving problem (
1.1
) exist on the whole space, or even on a bounded domain with zero-flux boundary conditions?

Once such a criterion has been established, we may then ask: are these same conditions shared by the
n
-species system (
1.2
)? In addition to the implications that kernel regularity may have on the existence/non-existence of stationary states to (
1.2
) on the whole space, there are other features not covered by our previous analysis. In particular, it is unclear whether solutions exist in the arbitrary mass regime when the detailed balance condition
(H5)
is false; this has several implications, such as the lack of a free-energy functional, which is essential to proving the non-existence of local/global minimisers as achieved in
[
25
]
. This lack of energy structure may yield long-term dynamics that are significantly different from the scalar cases. We hope that these simulations, as complements to the rigorous analysis, provide some possibilities for future study of cases falling outside our technical criteria used.

Using a finite-volume method proposed in
[
22
,
4
]
, we solve the problem in a fixed one-dimensional domain with no-flux boundary conditions for
1
- and
2
-species cases as an approximation to the problem on the whole space. We describe the method briefly here, directing readers to
[
23
]
for further details and discussion. The approximate domain
(-L,L)
is divided into uniform finite-volume cells
C_{j}=[x_{j-\tfrac{1}{2}},x_{j+\tfrac{1}{2}}]
of size
\Delta x
, where
x_{j}=j\Delta x
, for a fixed number of cells
j\in\{-M,\ldots,M\}
. We approximate the cell averages of the solution
u
,
\overline{u}_{j}(t):=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{C_{j}}u(t,x){\rm d}x
, via the system of ODEs for
\overline{u}_{j}(t)
:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\overline{u}_{j}}{{\rm d}t}=-\left(\frac{F_{j+1/2}-F_{j-1/2}}{\Delta x}\right),

which is obtained by integrating (
1.1
) each cell
C_{j}
. The numerical flux
F_{j+1/2}(t)
is an approximation of the continuous flux

\displaystyle\xi(t,x):=D\log(u)+K*u

(5.1)

at the cell interface
x_{j+1/2}
at time
t
. To compute the flux, we use a standard upwind scheme with a generalised minmod limiter, see
[
23
, Eq. (2.2)-(2.7)]
. The scheme is second order accurate in space, and we use 100 cells per spatial unit, i.e., given a domain of length
2L
,
200^{*}L
cells are used to solve the problem over
(-L,L)
. Time stepping is performed using a third order strong stability preserving Runge-Kutta method
[
48
]
.

5.1.
The Scalar Equation

Several models studied in mathematical ecology feature a spatial convolution with a tophat kernel directing movement towards or away from some stimulus (e.g., a resource gradient
[
41
]
, scent marks on a landscape
[
70
]
, remembered locations of previous interactions
[
68
,
69
]
, or detection of the population density itself
[
70
,
73
]
). We consider the following prototypical scenario with tophat kernel:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+u\frac{\partial K_{\text{tophat}}*u}{\partial x}\right)\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=\chi_{(-\ell,\ell)}(x),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(5.2)

where
D>0
and
\chi_{(-\ell,\ell)}(x)
is the characteristic function of the symmetric interval of radius
\ell>0
, normalized such that
\left\|\chi_{(-\ell,\ell)}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=1
. Here,
K_{\text{tophat}}
denotes the top-hat kernel with a strength parameter
\alpha
and perceptual radius
R
introduced in (
1.4
). Members of population
u
are able to detect a nonlocal average density
R
units about the location of detection, biasing the movement towards/away from areas of high/low density regions depending on the sign and magnitude of the parameter
\alpha
. The convention in (
1.4
) is such that it is an
attractive
kernel, hence
\alpha>0
corresponds to attraction to areas of high density, whereas
\alpha<0
corresponds to repulsion from high density areas.

As previously noted, the top-hat kernel satisfies hypotheses
(H1)
-
(H3)
and
(H6)
, but does not have a gradient in any
L^{p}
space. In this example, it is informative to note that in one dimension the problem becomes
local
in the sense that the nonlocal interaction term can be written as the difference of Diracs:

\frac{\partial K_{\text{tophat}}*u}{\partial x}(t,x)=-\alpha\left(\frac{u(t,x+R)-u(t,x-R)}{2R}\right).

Therefore, for any given smooth
\phi
there holds

\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(K_{\text{tophat}}*\phi)\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\frac{\left|\alpha\right|}{R}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})},

and so
(H2)
is satisfied with
C_{K}=\left|\alpha\right|/R
. By the comments made in the introduction, this is sufficient to conclude that hypothesis
(H4)
is also satisfied. Hypothesis
(H3)
and
(H6)
are obviously satisfied.

By Theorem
2.4
, there exists a unique, global classical solution solving problem (
5.2
). Strictly speaking, the initial data does not satisfy the hypotheses of the Theorem; in this sense, the solution is only classical on the open time interval
(0,T)
and the initial data is satisfied in the sense of a weak solution.

The size of the simulated domain
L
is chosen large enough to approximate the problem on the whole
\mathbb{R}
if the long-time behaviour leads to a fast-decaying at infinity steady state. If the diffusion is dominating, then the no-flux boundary condition will change the long-time asymptotic behaviour of the equation. Our numerical experiments indicate that if there are steady solutions of (
5.2
), they are supported on the whole domain as one expects from the effect of the linear diffusion term.

In Figures
1
-
4
, we present simulations for four exemplary cases of the dynamical behaviour. In all cases, we fix

\displaystyle D=0.25,\quad R=1.0,\quad\ell=4.0,

(5.3)

and
L
is indicated in each of the figure captions. In each figure, we change only the strength parameter
\alpha
. In the upper panel (A), we display a contour plot of the solution profile and plot the free energy with respect to time; in the lower panel (B), we display cross-sections of the solution profile at select times. The solution profile is the solid black line, while the quantity
\left|\xi\right|
is the dashed black line, where
\xi
is given by (
5.1
) with
K=K_{\textup{tophat}}
, which should be constant on the support of the solution
u
when a (numerical) steady-state is achieved. Studying this quantity provides evidence that we are indeed at a (numerical) steady-state. Of course, by Theorem
2.4
we know that the solution is strictly positive everywhere; therefore, the grey columns indicate regions for which the solution
u
is smaller than
10^{-4}
.

In Figure
1
, we have a small amount of self-attraction (
\alpha=2
). In this case, the diffusive forces clearly dominate and the solution settles down to the constant state (i.e.,
u\equiv 1/2L
). The free energy decreases very slowly. On a
\log
-scale (not depicted here), the free energy is still decreasing. We observe similar behaviour for small amounts of self-repulsion.

In Figure
2
, we have a large amount of self-attraction (
\alpha=30
). In contrast to Figure
1
, the attractive forces appear to dominate and the solution concentrates a majority of its mass within a single region about
x=0
. Within the grey column, the “numerical support” of
u
, we observe at time
t=100
that
\xi
is constant. For longer times,
\xi
appears to approach a constant value over the entire domain (as in, e.g., Figure
3
). On a
\log
-scale, different from the weak attraction case, the free energy does not appear to be changing. By the numerical method used here, it remains difficult to determine whether this is an effect of the no-flux boundary condition alone.

In contrast to the single peak observed for strong attraction, in Figure
3
we observe two distinct peaks when the amount of self-attraction is intermediate between the strengths used for Figure
1
and Figure
2
(
\alpha=20
). In very short times, we see that
\xi
is constant over the regions of concentration; running until time
t=200
, we observe that
\xi
is constant over the entire domain. The solution, during the time spanned from
t=2.7
to
t=200
, is slowly concentrating around the two peaks dissolving the mass that is still located at
x=0
and
t=2.7
. We have checked this behavior by plotting the densities in log-scale. We again remark that it is difficult to determine whether this behaviour is due to the no-flux boundary condition used.

Finally, in Figure
4
we have a large amount of self-repulsion (
\alpha=-20
). In this case, the repulsive forces produce a patterned state, at least for small times; however, for larger times we begin to observe a very slow decay to a constant steady state. Similar to Figure
1
, we observe the (numerical) solution to approach
1/2L
. In such cases, we do not believe a no-flux boundary condition will appropriately approximate the solution on the whole space for fixed numerical domain lengths.

These examples, modifying only the strength of nonlocal interaction, highlight some intuitive behaviour. First, when the interaction strength is too small, patterned states cannot form; this is known for smooth potentials in a bounded domain, see
[
28
]
. On the other hand, patterned states can form for either attractive or repulsive kernels on a bounded domain; it may be the case that without confinement by the domain itself, repulsive kernels cannot produce a stationary state on the whole space. As discussed at the beginning of this section, it is unclear which attractive kernels of sufficient irregularity can produce a nontrivial patterned state on the whole space. We leave these questions for future investigation.

5.2.
The
n
-Species System

Different from the scalar equation case, the
n
-species system leaves more room to explore the differences between Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
due to the increased complexity of the detailed balance hypothesis
(H5)
for several interacting populations. For simplicity, we first consider the following general
2
-species aggregation-diffusion system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+u\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(K_{11}*u+K_{12}*v)\right),\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(K_{21}*u+K_{22}*v)\right),\quad\text{ in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=v(0,x)=\chi_{(-\ell,\ell)}(x),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(5.4)

In these simulations we again fix
D
,
R
and
\ell
as in (
5.3
) with the initial data normalized so that
m_{i}=1
for
i=1,2
. We again use no-flux boundary conditions on a domain of length
2L>0
specified in each figure caption.

Under hypothesis
(H1)
, Theorem
4.1
guarantees that there exists a global weak solution
(u,v)
solving problem (
5.4
) so long as there holds

\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|K_{12}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\left\|K_{21}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right)<\min_{i=1,2}\left\{D-\left\|K_{ii}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right\}.

Notice that if the small mass condition (
2.4
) from the scalar case is violated for either population
u
or
v
, we cannot guarantee the existence of a solution from Theorem
4.1
; when the small mass condition is satisfied, a global weak solution exists for the
2
-species system so long as the cross-interaction between the two populations (described by
K_{12}
and
K_{21}
) is not too strong. It is interesting to note that this requires no relation between the kernels of different populations: the kernel
K_{12}
may be a completely different form than
K_{21}
, such as featuring different detection radii
R
between populations. We do not explore such possibilities here, however. We instead consider as a concrete example the typical case where

\displaystyle K_{ij}(\cdot):=\alpha_{ij}K_{\text{tophat}}(\cdot),

(5.5)

for some coefficients
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i,j=1,2
. In this way, both populations have identical detection abilities, differing only in their relative rates of attraction/repulsion. With our chosen parameters, a necessary condition for the existence of a weak solution via Theorem
4.1
is

\left|\alpha_{ii}\right|<2DR=\frac{1}{2},\quad i=1,2.

Then, if the cross interaction is such that
\left|\alpha_{ij}\right|\ll 1
,
i\neq j
, Theorem
4.1
ensures the existence of a global weak solution; since
(H4)
is also satisfied, Theorem
4.3
ensures that this solution is in fact the unique, global classical solution for a rather large class of interaction kernels with no symmetry requirements of any kind. In such cases, numerical simulation reveals behaviour similar to that of a single equation: the cross-interaction cannot be too strong, and so the dynamics are primarily governed by diffusion and self-interaction (i.e.,
\alpha_{ii}
).

On the other hand, while Theorem
4.2
allows for strong cross-interactions, the addition of hypothesis
(H5)
requires some additional structure on the coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
. We explore this briefly below.

For
n=2
interacting populations, it is not difficult to verify that the condition

\pi_{i}\alpha_{ij}=\pi_{j}\alpha_{ji}

results in a consistent system so long as the cross-interaction terms
\alpha_{ij}
,
i\neq j
, are of the same sign. Therefore, Theorem
4.2
ensures the existence of a global weak solution to problem (
5.4
) for any initial mass
m_{i}>0
, any
D_{i}>0
fixed, and any kernels of the form given in (
5.5
) so long as there holds

\sign\{\alpha_{ij}\}=\sign\{\alpha_{ji}\},

for each
i,j=1,\ldots,n
. Moreover, hypothesis
(H4)
is again satisfied by the top-hat detection function and so the solution obtained is in fact the unique, classical solution.

In Figure
5
we present the solution profiles for the two-species system with repulsive symmetric cross-interaction, i.e.
\alpha_{12}=\alpha_{21}<0
. We choose
\alpha_{11}=20
so that the expected solution profile for
u
is that of Figure
3
in the absence of
v
; we then choose
\alpha_{22}=2
(an order of magnitude less than
\alpha_{11}
), so that the expected solution profile of
v
is that of Figure
1
in the absence of
u
. It is interesting to note that the solution behaviour is roughly as expected for each single equation with the no-flux boundary conditions, where the steady-state for
u
concentrates into two peaks as indicated by the flatness of
\xi_{u}
on the essential support of
u
and the solution profile for
v
stabilizing towards a steady state as much as
u
by noticing the constant behavior of
\xi_{v}
on its essential support. We observe the segregation of the mass of both species as well as the decay of the free energy functional in time. We remark that we expect the long time behavior of the solutions in the whole space completely different from the no-flux boundary conditions. Clarifying this point would need further numerical exploration.

In Figure
6
, we run a simulation for a scenario that is not covered by the theory presented here. We fix
\alpha_{ii}=20
,
i=1,2
, so that a patterned state of the form in Figure
3
for each population in the absence of the other is expected. We then choose
\alpha_{12}=-10<\alpha_{21}=5
so that the detailed balance condition of Hypothesis
(H5)
is violated. Over short times scales some complex rearrangement of the two population densities occurs, but eventually the solution profile settles down to a form found in Figures
2
-
3
. The flatness of
\xi_{u}
,
\xi_{v}
in the grey regions suggests this is indeed the steady-state, and this continues to flatten over a longer simulation time. Of course, this case no longer features a gradient flow structure, and so we observe non-monotone behaviour of the “free energy” of this simulation. It is interesting to note that the behaviour of the solution does not appear fundamentally different than that of cases falling within the theory of well-posedness obtained in the present work. Therefore, we believe that the detailed balance condition is a technical requirement for the techniques of the scalar equation to be applied to the general
n
-species system, and the system should remain well-posed with no further condition on the sign or magnitude of the coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
.

When
n\geq 3
, even cross-interactions of the same sign do not guarantee the existence of a solution via Theorem
4.2
. To see this, we note that generically the condition
\pi_{i}\alpha_{ij}=\pi_{j}\alpha_{ji}
gives at most
n(n-1)/2
equations with
n(n-1)
unknowns (disregarding the diagonal elements). Hence the detailed balance condition hypothesis
(H5)
becomes significantly under-determined as the number of interacting species grows. 4
interacting populations.

Pairing our analytical insights with our numerical observations, we propose the following questions for future interest.

(1)

For the scalar equation (
1.1
), when the kernel
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies
(H2)
, but otherwise features no additional regularity, do solutions decay to
0
in the long-time limit? More generally, what are minimal regularity requirements on
K
, which do not require
\nabla K
to belong to some
L^{p}
space, that yield no nontrivial stationary states to the scalar equation on the whole space?

(2)

For the
n
-species system (
1.2
), when all kernels satisfy
(H2)
but the detailed balance condition
(H5)
is violated, does a unique strong solution exist? Our numerical simulations suggest that the answer is
yes
, but it is not immediately clear how to extend the current approach to such cases.

(3)

Relating the scalar and system cases, given minimal conditions on
K
such that the solution to the scalar equation tends to
0
in the long-time limit, do solutions to the
n
-species system enjoy the same property? It appears that for cases with an energy functional (i.e., detailed balance is satisfied), this should be the case; however, it is not at all obvious whether this remains true when the energy structure is removed.

Appendix A
Extension of Theorem
2.1
to the positive-definite and Keller-Segel cases

We present here a result which shows that the crucial estimate in Lemma
3.5
can be combined with the positive-definite case in
[
52
]
and the Keller-Segel case in
[
37
]
(the latter only in dimension
d=2
) to consider even more general kernels
K
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
u
be the smooth and strictly positive solution of (
1.1
) with
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
u_{0}>0
, and with the kernel
K=K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3}
where
K_{1}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\Delta K_{2}=\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Radon measure with bounded positive part
\mu^{+}
satisfying
\|\mu^{+}\|_{\text{TV}}<\infty
while
K_{3}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is positive-definite in the sense that for all
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\xi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\xi(x)\,K_{3}\ast\xi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
. Moreover, if
\mu^{+}\neq 0
, we assume
d=2
and we let
C_{GN}
to be the best constant in the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality
\|f\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{GN}\|\nabla f\|^{1/2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,\|f\|^{1/2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
for all
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Let
m=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{0}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. Then, if

D-m\,\|K_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}-\frac{C_{\text{GN}}^{4}\,m\,\|\mu^{+}\|_{\text{TV}}}{4}>0,

we have the estimate

\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\left(D-m\,\|K_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}-\frac{C_{\text{GN}}^{4}\,m\,\|\mu^{+}\|_{\text{TV}}}{4}\right)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 0.

Proof.

We multiply (
1.1
) by
\log u
and we integrate by parts to obtain

\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\log u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+D\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\\
=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{1}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{2}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{3}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The first term is controlled as in Lemma
3.5
to obtain

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{1}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\|K_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq m\,\|K_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(A.1)

The second term is controlled as in
[
37
]

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{2}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta K_{2}\ast u\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu^{+}\ast u\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\|\mu^{+}\|_{\text{TV}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

where we used the Hölder inequality and
\|\mu^{+}\ast u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|\mu^{+}\|_{\text{TV}}\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. Then, we use the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality from the statement with
f=\sqrt{u}
to obtain

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{2}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{C_{\text{GN}}^{4}\,m\,\|\mu^{+}\|_{\text{TV}}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(A.2)

We stress that the manipulation is valid only in dimension
d=2
if
\mu^{+}\neq 0
(see Remark
A.2
below). Finally,
-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{3}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 0
and we conclude the proof.
∎

Remark A.1
.

The regularity of
K_{3}
is mainly needed to make the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{3}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
well-defined and to pass to the limit in this term in the approximating scheme when proving the existence of solution. The basic estimate on
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
implies that
\nabla u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
so
K_{3}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is sufficient. On the other hand, one could potentially bootstrap the estimates on
u
and
\nabla u
in the spirit of Lemma
3.7
to weaken the required assumption on
K_{3}
.

Remark A.2
.

If
\mu^{+}\neq 0
, we have to restrict to
d=2
. First, the inequality fails in dimensions
d>4
since the Sobolev exponent satisfies
\frac{2d}{d-2}<4
for
d>4
. Second, even for
d\in\{3,4\}
, the inequality reads
\|f\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{GN}\|\nabla f\|^{d/4}_{L^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,\|f\|^{(4-d)/4}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
so the computation in (
A.2
) yields a bound on
-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{2}\ast\nabla u\,\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
in terms of
\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{d}
which cannot be compensated by the (LHS). Another approach would be to study
\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{d/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
, which is an important quantity for the Keller-Segel system in an arbitrary dimension
d

[
34
]
; however, mimicking (
A.1
), one has to estimate
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla u^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\nabla K_{1}\ast u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
in terms of
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u^{\frac{d}{4}}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
which does not seem easy.

Remark A.3
.

It is not clear if we can extend in the similar way Lemma
3.6
which is based on the fact that
f:=\sqrt{u}\,\nabla(D\log u+K*u)
is bounded in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
. Decomposing
K=K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3}
, it is not clear how to use the information on
\Delta K_{2}
to control
\sqrt{u}\,\nabla K_{2}\ast u
or the positive-definiteness of
K_{3}
to control
\sqrt{u}\,\nabla K_{3}\ast u
.
