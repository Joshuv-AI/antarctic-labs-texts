Abstract.

The main goal of this paper is to establish the nonlocal-to-local convergence of strong solutions to a Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard model with singular potential describing immiscible, viscous two-phase flows with matched densities, which is referred to as the Model H. This means that we show that the strong solutions to the nonlocal Model H converge to the strong solution to the local Model H as the weight function in the nonlocal interaction kernel approaches the delta distribution. Compared to previous results in the literature, our main novelty is to further establish corresponding convergence rates. Before investigating the nonlocal-to-local convergence, we first need to ensure the strong
well-posedness of the nonlocal Model H. In two dimensions, this result can already be found in the literature, whereas in three dimensions, it will be shown in the present paper. Moreover, in both two and three dimensions, we establish suitable uniform bounds on the strong solutions of the nonlocal Model H, which are essential to prove the nonlocal-to-local convergence results.

Keywords:
Nonlocal-to-local convergence, Navier–Stokes equation, nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard equation, two-phase flows, singular potential, strong solutions, convergence rates.

1  Introduction

The mathematical description of two-phase flows is an important but very challenging topic of modern fluid dynamics with various applications in biology, chemistry and engineering. The motion of a mixture of two immiscible fluids, both having a constant individual density, can essentially be captured by describing the motion of the interface separating the fluids. Therefore, two fundamental mathematical approaches have been developed:
sharp-interface models
and the
diffuse-interface models
.
In sharp-interface models, the interface between the fluids is represented as an evolving hypersurface, which leads to a free boundary problem.
In diffuse-interface models (also referred to as phase-field models), the interface is approximated by a thin tubular neighborhood. The concentrations (or volume fractions) of the two fluids are represented by an order parameter, the so-called phase-field. Except at the diffuse interface, this phase-field will attain values close to
-1
or
1
as these values respresent the two fluids, respectively. At the diffuse interface, we expect the phase-field to exhibit a continuous transition between
-1
and
1
.
The main advantage of this method is that the time evolution of the phase-field can be described by a PDE system in Eulerian coordinates. This avoids directly tracking the interface as it needs to be done in free boundary problems. In many cases, diffuse-interface models can be related to a corresponding sharp-interface model by the so-called sharp-interface limit, where the interfacial width is sent to zero. For more details on the two approaches, especially in the context of two-phase flows, we refer to
[
4
]
and the references therein.

In this work, we investigate a Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system known as the
Model H
(both its local and its nonlocal version), which is a diffuse-interface model describing the time evolution of two immiscible, viscous fluids with
matched densities
. This means that the individual densities of the two fluids can be approximately considered as equal.

Depending on a parameter
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the local and the nonlocal version of the Model H can be formulated simultaneously as follows.
For
n\in\{2,3\}
, let
\Omega
either be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whose boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{3}
, or let
\Omega
be the
n
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}:=\big[\mathbb{R}/\big((2\mathbb{Z}+1)\pi\big)\big]^{n}
.
For any final time
T>0
, we write

\Omega_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
and if
\Omega
is a bounded domain, we further use the notation
\Gamma_{T}:=\Gamma\times(0,T)
.
Then, the following Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system is referred to as the
Model H
:

\displaystyle\rho\big(\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\big)-\nu\Delta\mathbf{v}+\nabla p=\mu\nabla c,\hskip 10.00002pt\operatorname{div}(\mathbf{v})=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega_{T},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla c=m\Delta\mu\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega_{T},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mu=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+f^{\prime}(c)\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega_{T},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}|_{t=0}=\mathbf{v}_{0},\hskip 10.00002ptc|_{t=0}=c_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega.

(1.1d)

In case
\Omega
is a bounded domain, we further impose the standard boundary conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma_{T},

(1.1e)

and if
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, we assume periodic boundary conditions.
Here,
\mathbf{v}:\Omega_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the

associated with the mixture of two fluids,
p:\Omega_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
represents the corresponding

,

is the

and

denotes the

.
The quantities
\rho
,

and

represent the

of the mixture, the

, and the

, respectively, which are all assumed to be positive constants.
The function

is the derivative of a potential

, which is usually double-well shaped. A physically relevant choice is the logarithmic potential

f_{\mathrm{log}}(s):=\frac{\theta}{2}\big[(1+s)\,\ln(1+s)+(1-s)\,\ln(1-s)\big]-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}(1-s^{2})

(1.2)

for all
s\in(-1,1)
,
which is also referred to as the

. It is classified as a singular potential as its derivative tends to
\pm\infty
as its argument approaches
\pm 1
. In our mathematical analysis, we will even be able to handle a more general class of singular potentials that will be specified by the assumptions
(S1)
–
(S3)
.

For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
and a sufficiently regular function

, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
appearing in (

) is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u(x):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(|x-y|)\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\;\mathrm{d}y&&\text{if $\varepsilon>0$},\\[4.30554pt]
&-\Delta u(x)&&\text{if $\varepsilon=0$},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.3)

for all
x\in\Omega
. Here,
J_{\varepsilon}
is a suitable nonnegative interaction kernel, whose exact properties will be specified in
(A3)
.

In the case
\varepsilon=0
,
\mathcal{L}_{0}=-\Delta
is a local differential operator, where
\Delta
denotes the Laplace operator subject to the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=0
on
\partial\Omega
if
\Omega
is a bounded domain, and the Laplace operator with periodic boundary conditions if

is the torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}
.
Therefore, system (
1.1
) with
\varepsilon=0
will be called the

.
It has already been proposed in
[
38
]
, and a mathematical derivation was provided later in
[
37
]
. For the analysis of the local Model H, we refer to
[

,
11
,
26
,
36
]
and references therein.
Variants of the local Model H with dynamic boundary conditions, which allow for a better description of short-range interactions between the fluids on the boundary of the domain, have been proposed an analyzed, for instance, in
[

,
29
,
35
,
28
]
.
We further point out that a generalization of the local Model H that also covers the situation of
unmatched densities
(i.e., both fluids may have different individual densities) has been derived in
[

]
and is known as the

. It has been analyzed, for instance, in
[

,
2
,
3
,
10
,
32
,

]
. A variant of the same model allowing to treat multi-phase fluids is also studied in

In the case
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is a nonlocal operator since for any
x\in\Omega
,
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u(x)
depends on all values

with

.
Therefore, system (
1.1
) with
\varepsilon>0
is referred to as the

.
In contrast to the local Model H, where only short-range interactions between the fluids are taken into account by the differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, the nonlocal Model H also describes long-range interactions between the materials, which are weighted by the interaction kernel
J_{\varepsilon}
.
To the best of our knowledge, the nonlocal Model H has first been investigated in
[
12
]
. Afterwards, it has further been analyzed, for instance, in
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,

,

,

]
.

The system (
1.1
) is associated with the energy functional

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v},c)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{2}|\mathbf{v}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(c)+\int_{\Omega}f\big(c(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x

(1.4)

where, depending on the choice of
\varepsilon
, the contribution
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(c)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(c):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big|c(x)-c(y)\big|^{2}\>\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x&&\text{if $\varepsilon>0$},\\[4.30554pt]
&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\big|\nabla c(x)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x&&\text{if $\varepsilon=0$}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

The first summand on the right-hand side of (
1.4
) represents the kinetic energy, whereas the last two summands in (
1.4
) represent the free energy of the mixture, which is either of Ginzburg–Landau type (
\varepsilon=0
) or of Helmholtz type (
\varepsilon>0
).

For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
, sufficiently regular solutions of the Model H (
1.1
) satisfy the
mass conservation law

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c(t)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}c_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$}

(1.6)

as well as the
energy dissipation law

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\varepsilon}\big(\mathbf{v}(t),c(t)\big)=-\nu\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{v}(t)\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-m\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

(1.7)

In the case
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, we further have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}(t)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$.}

(1.8)

As shown in
[
45
,
44
]
, the nonlocal energies
E_{\varepsilon}
with
\varepsilon>0
and the local energy
E_{0}
can be related via the nonlocal-to-local convergence

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(c)\to\mathcal{E}_{0}(c)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{as $\varepsilon\searrow 0$,}

(1.9)

provided that
c\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Based on this result, the nonlocal-to-local convergence

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(c)\to\mathcal{L}_{0}(c)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{as $\varepsilon\searrow 0$}

(1.10)

as well as corresponding nonlocal-to-local convergence results (without rates) for the Cahn–Hilliard equation were established in
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
39
]
. The nonlocal-to-local convergence of the Model H (without rates) has already been established in
[
9
]
. In fact, even the more general case of unmatched densities was covered there.

Recently, in
[
8
]
, stronger nonlocal-to-local convergence results for the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
(which will be recalled in Proposition
2.3
) were obtained and applied to the Allen–Cahn equation and the Cahn–Hilliard equation. The most substantial improvement of these new results is that concrete rates for the convergence (
1.10
) could be shown. However, compared to previous results, higher regularity of the function
c
is required.

Outline of this paper.

In the present contribution, we intend to prove the nonlocal-to-local convergence of the Model H (
1.1
) along with corresponding convergence rates. Therefore, in order to apply the convergence results established in
[
8
]
, we have to consider strong solutions of both the local and the nonlocal Model H. In this regard, the coupling with the Navier–Stokes equation leads to additional difficulties compared to the results for nonlocal-to-local convergence of the Cahn–Hilliard equation. For example, in three dimensions, we can merely expect local-in-time existence of strong solutions to the Model H as the global existence of strong solutions of the Navier–Stokes equation is a well-known open problem.

Concerning the existence and uniqueness of weak and strong solutions, the local Model H is already very well understood. It is also known that strong solutions satisfy the so-called
strict separation property
. This means that the phase-field attains its values only in a strict subinterval of
(-1,1)
and is thus separated from the pure phases that are associated with
\pm 1
(see (
2.14
) and (
2.16
)). For more details about separation properties of the local Cahn–Hilliard equation, we refer to
[
41
,
34
,
24
,
31
,
30
]
.
All the aforementioned results will be recalled in Proposition
2.4
.
We point out that the strict separation property of strong solutions to the local Model H will be a crucial ingredient in the proof of nonlocal-to-local convergence.

For the nonlocal Model H, the existence of a weak solution has already been proven in
[
17
]
. Moreover, in two dimensions, the strong well-posedness has been established in
[
23
]
(see also
[
25
]
). However, apparently, the strong well-posedness in three dimensions has not yet been addressed in the literature. Therefore, in Theorem
3.1
, we collect the existence and uniqueness results in two dimensions and we prove the local-in-time strong well-posedness in three dimensions. Moreover, we establish certain bounds on weak and strong solutions, which are independent of the parameter
\varepsilon
. These uniform bounds will be essential in the proof of nonlocal-to-local convergence for strong solutions. We further show that for any
\varepsilon>0
, the strong solution satisfies a strict separation property, which holds as long as the solution exists. However, we are not able to exploit this strict separation result to prove the nonlocal-to-local convergence, since the confinement interval is not uniform in
\varepsilon
. We will thus resort to a different technique.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
is presented in Section
4
.
For more details about separation properties for the nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard equation, we refer to
[
23
,
42
]
, in which the first results in 2D and 3D, respectively, are shown (see also, for instance,
[
24
,
30
]
and references therein).

Eventually, in Theorem
3.3
, we establish the nonlocal-to-local convergence of strong solutions to the Model H (
1.1
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
along with associated convergence rates. The proof of this result is presented in Section
5
.

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{|F^{\prime}(-1+2\delta)|}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\dfrac{1}{F^{\prime\prime}(-1+2\delta)}

\displaystyle=O\left(\delta\right).

(2.4)

as
\delta\to 0^{+}
.
Moreover, there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
such that
F^{\prime\prime}
is monotonously increasing on
(-1,-1+\gamma_{0}]
and on
[1-\gamma_{0},1)
.

Remark 2.1
.

We point out that the logarithmic potential (also known as the Flory–Huggins potential), which is given by

f_{\mathrm{log}}(s)=F_{\mathrm{log}}(s)-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}s^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in[-1,1]$}

(2.5)

with
F_{\mathrm{log}}(\pm 1)=\theta\ln(2)
and

F_{\mathrm{log}}(s)=\frac{\theta}{2}((1+s)\text{ln}(1+s)+(1-s)\text{ln}(1-s))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in(-1,1)$},

–
(S3)

–
(S3)
allow for a much more general class of potentials (see, e.g.,
[

]
for a discussion).

2.3  Preliminaries

(P1)

The Laplace operator and its inverse.

It is well-known that the operator

\mathcal{A}:H_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow H_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002pt\left\langle\mathcal{A}u,v\right\rangle_{H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega)}=(\nabla u,\nabla v)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $v\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)$}

is a continuous linear isomorphism. If
\Omega
is a bounded domain,
\mathcal{A}
can be interpreted as the negative Laplace operator with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, and if
\Omega
is the torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}
,
\mathcal{A}
represents the Laplace operator with periodic boundary conditions.
We denote the inverse of
\mathcal{A}
, which is a bounded linear operator, by

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{A}^{-1}:H_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)\rightarrow H_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega).

For any
g,h\in H_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)
, we set

(g,h)_{\ast}:=\big(\nabla\mathcal{N}g,\nabla\mathcal{N}h\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\|g\|_{\ast}:=\|\nabla\mathcal{N}g\|.

This defines a bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\ast}
which is an inner product on the Hilbert space
H_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)
. Its induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{\ast}
is equivalent to the standard operator norm on this space.

Moreover, due to elliptic regularity theory, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
g\in L_{(0)}^{2}(\Omega)
,

\|\mathcal{N}g\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|g\|.

(2.7)

We further point out that the mapping

g\mapsto\big(\|g-\overline{g}\|_{\ast}^{2}+|\overline{g}|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}
defines a norm
H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}
that is equivalent to the standard operator norm on this space.

(P2)

The Stokes operator and its inverse.

The
Stokes operator
, which is defined as

A_{S}:\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{u}\mapsto(\nabla\mathbf{u},\nabla\mathbf{v})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)$}

is a continuous linear isomorphism.
For any
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, we set

(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})_{\sigma}:=\big(\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\mathbf{v},\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\mathbf{w}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\sigma}:=\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\|.

This defines a bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\sigma}
, which is an inner product on the Hilbert space
\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime}
.
Its induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{\sigma}
is equivalent to the standard operator norm on this space. In particular, due to Poincaré’s inequality, there exists a constant
C_{S,1}>0
such that for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime}
, it holds

\displaystyle\|A_{S}^{-1}\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{S,1}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\sigma}.

(2.8)

Moreover, due to regularity theory for the Stokes operator, there exists a constant
C_{S,2}>0
such that for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, it holds

\|A_{S}^{-1}\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{S,2}\|\mathbf{u}\|.

(2.9)

In particular, using the
Leray–Helmholtz projector

\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}:\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
, we obtain the representation

A_{S}\big|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\cap\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}=-\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\Delta,

with
\Delta
being the standard Laplace operator.

.

(a)

To obtain the strict separation properties (

is already strictly separated (i.e.,
\|c_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1-\delta_{0}
for some
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
). In the case
n=2
, this already follows from the assumption
\mu_{0}=-\Delta c_{0}+f^{\prime}(c_{0})\in H^{1}(\Omega)
by means of De Giorgi iterations as employed in
[
30
, Theorem 4.3]
.

In the case
n=3
, at least up to now, the separation property (

) can merely be obtained on a local neighborhood of the initial time.
For this result, it is sufficient to assume that the potential

satisfies
(S1)
.
If

, assuming both
(S1)
and
(S2)
, even a strict separation property on the entire interval

can be established.
The question, whether this property can also be proven for

is a challenging open problem.
As shown in
[
30
, Section 6.1.1]
, a strict separation property on the entire right-maximal interval

can also be obtained in the case

if slightly more singular potentials

than the Flory–Huggins potential (see Remark

) are used.

The strict separation properties (
2.14
) and (
2.16
) will be an essential ingredient in the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

(b)

As pointed out in Proposition
2.4
, the unique strong solution exists globally in time (i.e,
T_{\star}=\infty
) if
n=2
. In the case
n=3
, due to the involved Navier–Stokes equation, only local existence of the strong solution (i.e,

) for general initial data is known so far.
However, if the initial data are sufficiently close to a stationary point (i.e., a minimizer of the total energy), the global existence of the strong solution can still be ensured.
If we additionally assume
c_{0}
to be strictly separated, up to reducing the size of some norms of the initial data, the strict separation property (
2.16
) can also be established globally in time (see
[
30
, Theorem 6.4]
).

(c)

We point out that most of the results in the literature concerning the local version of the Model H consider the case of bounded domains. However, it is clear that these results can usually be transferred to the case
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
by slightly adapting the arguments.

Note that, if
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the assumption
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
already includes the condition

\overline{\mathbf{v}_{0}}=\mathbf{0}
. This then implies
\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}(t)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x=0
(cf. (
1.8
)) and therefore, we may apply the inverse Stokes operator
A_{S}^{-1}
(see
(P2)
) directly on

for every

for which the solution exists.

However, the assumption
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
really does not mean any loss of generality as we could simply consider the difference
\mathbf{v}-\overline{\mathbf{v}}
instead of
\mathbf{v}
, which would not have a major impact on our mathematical analysis.

If
\Omega
is a bounded domain, the existence of a weak solution was established in
[
1
, Theorem 1]
. In the case
n=2
, the uniqueness of the weak solution was shown, e.g., in
[
36
]
.
Concerning the assertions on strong well-posedness we refer to
[
36
, Sections 4-5]
.
Note that the compatibility condition
\mu_{0}=-\Delta c_{0}+f^{\prime}(c_{0})\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is crucial for obtaining strong solutions.

If
\Omega
is the torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the same results can be obtained by adapting the arguments in the aforementioned literature to the periodic setting. For instance, in the case
n=2
, the existence of a unique global strong solution was established in
[
32
]
.

In the case
n=2
, the strict separation property (
2.14
) can be established by following the line of argument in
[
30
, Theorem 3.3]
, which is based on De Giorgi iterations. A crucial ingredient in this proof is the estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\|F^{\prime}(c(t))\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\sqrt{p},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $p\in[2,\infty)$}.

(2.18)

It can be derived by means of a Gagliardo–Nirenberg type estimate, which can be found, e.g., in
[
48
, p. 479]
. For more details, we refer to the derivation of (

) below, which is obtained by similar computations.
Once (

) is established, one can proceed as in
[
30
, Theorem 3.3]
to deduce the strict separation property (

.
In this context, we recall that, as pointed out in Remark
2.5

, the strict separation property (

\displaystyle\|c(t)-c(s)\|_{\ast}\leq\left|\int_{s}^{t}\|\partial_{t}c\|_{\ast}\,\text{d}\tau\right|\leq C|t-s|

\displaystyle\leq\|J_{\varepsilon}\|_{W^{1,1}(X)}\,\|c\|\,\|\nabla c_{2}\|\|A_{S}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\|\leq\frac{\theta_{0}}{16}\|c\|^{2}+C\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\sigma}^{2}.

In summary, we thus have

\big|\big(\mu_{1}\nabla c_{1}-\mu_{2}\nabla c_{2},A_{S}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\big)\big|\leq\frac{\theta_{0}}{4}\|c\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}+C\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\sigma}^{2}.

(4.9)

Combining (
4.3
)–(
4.9
), we conclude

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\|c\|_{\ast}^{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\|c\|\\
&\quad\leq C\Big(1+\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}^{4}+\|\mathbf{v}_{2}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}^{4}\Big)\Big(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\|c\|_{\ast}^{2}\Big).\end{split}

Applying Gronwall’s lemma, and recalling that
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in L^{4}(0,T;\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega))
,
i=1,2
, we eventually obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}(t)\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\|c(t)\|_{\ast}^{2}\leq\big(\|\mathbf{v}_{0}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\|c_{0}\|_{\ast}^{2}\big)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Big(1+\|\mathbf{v}_{1}(s)\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}^{4}+\|\mathbf{v}_{2}(s)\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}^{4}\Big)\;\mathrm{d}s\right)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. As the right-hand side vanishes if
\mathbf{v}_{0,1}=\mathbf{v}_{0,2}
and
c_{0,1}=c_{0,2}
a.e. in
\Omega
, this proves the uniqueness of the corresponding strong solution.

4.3  Uniform estimates for weak solutions

We now want to verify item
(iii)
of Theorem
3.1
.
To this end, let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary, and let
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})
be a corresponding weak solution to (
1.1
) that can be constructed by a semi-Galerkin scheme explained in Subsection
4.1
. We point out that all the following computations can be carried out rigorously within this semi-Galerkin scheme as the associated approximate solutions are sufficiently regular. Eventually, by passing to the limit in the approximation parameter, the obtained uniform bounds hold true for the considered weak solution
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})
.

From now on, the letter
C
denotes generic positive constants that may depend only on the choice of
\Omega
, the initial data and the system parameters, but not on
\varepsilon
.
The exact value of
C
may vary throughout this proof.

Testing the equations (
1.1a
) by
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
, (
1.1b
) by
\mu_{\varepsilon}
and (
1.1c
) by
\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}
, and using integration by parts, we derive the energy inequality

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\big(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}(t),c_{\varepsilon}(t)\big)+\int_{0}^{t}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}(s)\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq E_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0},c_{\varepsilon,0})\leq C_{0}

(4.10)

for all
t\geq 0
.
We point out that, in a rigorous semi-Galerkin scheme, we initially merely obtain the local-in-time existence of an approximate solution. However, as this approximate solution fulfills a discrete version of (
4.10
) as long as it exists, we can use this estimate to conclude that the approximate solution can actually be extended onto
[0,\infty)
.

Let now
T>0
be arbitrary and let
C(T)
denote generic positive constants that may depend only on
\Omega
, the initial data and the system parameters, but not on
\varepsilon
. The exact value of
C(T)
may vary in the subsequent line of argument.

In view of
(S1)
, the boundedness of the energy resulting from (
4.10
) already entails

\displaystyle|c_{\varepsilon}|<1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $\Omega_{T}$.}

(4.11)

Using this bound as well as Korn’s inequality, we further conclude from (
4.10
) that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|c_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\\
&\quad+\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(T).\end{aligned}

(4.12)

We now recall the inequality

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})|\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})(c_{\varepsilon}-\overline{c}_{\varepsilon})\;\mathrm{d}x+C\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $[0,T]$,}

(4.13)

which can be found, e.g., in
[
40
, Proposition 4.3.]
.
Testing (
1.1c
) by
c_{\varepsilon}-\overline{c}_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}(c_{\varepsilon}-\overline{c}_{\varepsilon})\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}(c_{\varepsilon}-\overline{c}_{\varepsilon})\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\big(F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-\theta_{0}\big)(c_{\varepsilon}-\overline{c}_{\varepsilon})\;\mathrm{d}x.

(4.14)

By the definition of the mean, the left-hand side can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}(c_{\varepsilon}-\overline{c}_{\varepsilon})\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\mu}_{\varepsilon})c_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x.

In view of the properties of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
, the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.14
) is nonnegative.
Due to (
4.12
), the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})(c_{\varepsilon}-\overline{c}_{\varepsilon})\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C\big(1+\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|\big).

(4.15)

Hence, by means of (
4.13
), we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})|\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C\big(1+\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|\big).

(4.16)

Consequently, it holds

\displaystyle|\overline{\mu}_{\varepsilon}|=\big|\overline{F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})}-\theta_{0}\overline{c}_{\varepsilon}\big|\leq C\big(1+\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|\big).

(4.17)

Recalling (
4.12
) and applying Poincaré’s inequality, we thus conclude

\displaystyle\|\mu_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(T).

(4.18)

By comparison in (
1.1b
), we further have

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime})}\leq C(T)

(4.19)

with the help of (
4.12
) and (
4.18
).
Furthermore, using again (
4.12
) and (
4.18
), and recalling the definition of
\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}
(see
(P2)
), we deduce

\big\|\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\big[\mu_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\big]\big\|_{\sigma}\leq\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|.

Performing the usual estimates for the remaining terms in the Navier–Stokes equation (
1.1a
), we conclude

\big\|\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\big[(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}+2\operatorname{div}(\nu D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon})\big]\big\|_{L^{\frac{4}{n}}(0,T;\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime})}\leq C(T),

which directly yields

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{n}}(0,T;\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime})}\leq C(T)

(4.20)

by a further comparison argument. Combining (
4.12
), (
4.18
), (
4.19
) and (
4.20
), we have thus verified (
3.3
).

If
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, we further test (
1.1c
) by
-\Delta c_{\varepsilon}
. After integrating by parts, we use the identity

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\nabla\big(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}\big)\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y\end{aligned}

(4.21)

to deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x+\theta_{0}\|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}.\end{aligned}

(4.22)

We point out that (
4.21
) follows from the relation
\nabla(J_{\varepsilon}\ast c_{\varepsilon})=J_{\varepsilon}\ast\nabla c_{\varepsilon}
, which holds if
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, but is (in general) not valid if
\Omega
is a bounded domain.
Exploiting Lemma
2.2
(a)
with
\gamma=\frac{1}{4\theta_{0}}
, we find
\varepsilon_{w}=\varepsilon_{w}(\theta_{0})>0
such that

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y+C\|c_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}

(4.23)

if
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{w}]
.
Combining (
4.22
) and (
4.23
), we infer

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y\\
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x+C\|c_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}.\end{aligned}

(4.24)

provided that
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{w}]
.
Recalling (
4.12
) and that
F^{\prime\prime}\geq\theta
, we use Young’s inequality to infer from (
4.24
) that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \theta\|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}&\leq\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}J_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla c_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y\\[4.30554pt]
&\leq C(1+\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|^{2})+\frac{\theta}{2}\|\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\|^{2},\end{aligned}

(4.25)

if
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{w}]
.
In combination with (
4.12
), we thus conclude

\displaystyle\|c_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if $\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}$ and $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{w}]$.}

(4.26)

This means that the uniform estimate (
3.4
) is established and thus, property
(iii)
is verified.

4.4  Uniform estimates for strong solutions

Next, we intend to verify item
(vi)
of Theorem
3.1
.
Therefore, we fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{w}]
, and we consider the corresponding right-maximal strong solution solution
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})
to (
1.1
).
Again, all the following computations can be carried out rigorously within the semi-Galerkin scheme mentioned in Subsection
4.1
as the associated approximate solutions are sufficiently regular. Eventually, by passing to the limit in the approximation parameter, the obtained uniform bounds hold true for the considered strong solution
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon})
.

Step 1: Uniform estimates for
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
and
p_{\varepsilon}
.

Our first step is to derive higher order bounds on the velocity field
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
, which are uniform with respect to
\varepsilon
. In the case
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, it is well known that testing the momentum equation (
1.1a
) by
-\Delta\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
(instead of
A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
) is sufficient to bound
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
in the
\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)
-norm (see, e.g.,
[
47
]
). However, as we also want to cover the case of
\Omega
being a bounded domain, we use a more general approach and test the momentum equation by
A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}=-\mathbf{P}_{\sigma}\Delta\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
.
Performing this testing procedure and employing the well-known identity

\frac{1}{2}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s

for almost all
t\in[0,T_{\varepsilon,\ast})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}&=\frac{1}{2}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\cdot A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\\
&\qquad-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s.\end{aligned}

(4.27)

for almost all
t\in[0,T_{\varepsilon,\ast})
.

Performing an integration by parts, and applying Hölder’s inequality, Young’s inequality and (
4.12
), we get

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\cdot A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}\cdot A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|\leq C\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}.

(4.28)

If
n=2
, we use Hölder’s inequality, Young’s inequality, the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality as well as (
4.12
) to deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Big|\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|\leq C\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|\\
&\quad\leq C\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|+C\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{4}.\end{aligned}

(4.29)

In fact, if
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the above integral even vanishes (see, e.g.,
[
47
, Lemma 3.1]
).
In the case
n=3
, we proceed similarly to derive the estimate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Big|\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|\leq C\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{3}(\Omega)}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|\\
&\quad\leq C\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+C\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{6}.\end{aligned}

(4.30)

Furthermore, testing the momentum equation by
\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
, we derive the identity

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s&=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Delta\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s.\end{aligned}

(4.31)

for almost all
t\in[0,T_{\varepsilon,\ast})
.
Using Young’s inequality as well as integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2},

(4.32)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}c_{\varepsilon}\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}.

(4.33)

for some positive constant
C_{0}
depending on the same quantities as the constants denoted by
C
.
Now, proceeding similarly as in the derivation of (
4.29
) and (
4.30
), we deduce

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+C_{0}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+C\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{\gamma},

(4.34)

where
\gamma=4
if
n=2
and
\gamma=6
if
n=3
.
We now add inequality (
4.27
) and inequality (
4.31
) multiplied by
\frac{1}{8C_{0}}
. By means of (
4.18
), (
4.29
)–(
4.28
) and (
4.32
)–(
4.33
), we infer

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{16C_{0}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{16}\int_{0}^{t}\|A_{S}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C+\frac{1}{2}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0}\|^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}\|D\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|^{\gamma}\;\mathrm{d}s,

for almost all
t\in[0,T_{\varepsilon,\ast})
with
\gamma
as introduced above.
We now recall (
4.12
), the uniform estimates stated in
(iii)
as well as assumption (
3.12
).
Applying Bihari’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Lemma II.4.12]
), we conclude that the estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega))}+\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega))}\leq C(T)

(4.35)

holds for all
T\in(0,T_{\varepsilon,\ast})
. In the case
n=3
, due to the uniform bound assumed in
(
3.12
), Bihari’s inequality (as stated in
[
11
, Lemma II.4.12]
) implies the existence of a time
T_{\ast}\in(0,T_{\varepsilon,\ast})
, which is independent of
\varepsilon
, such that (
4.35
) holds true for all
T\in(0,T_{\ast}]
. By a comparison argument, we eventually obtain

\displaystyle\|p_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C(T)

(4.36)

for any
T\in(0,\infty)
if
n=2
(since then
T_{\varepsilon,*}=\infty
) and any
T\in(0,T_{*}]
if
n=3
.

Step 2: Uniform estimates for
c_{\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}
.

In the following, let
T\in(0,\infty)
if
n=2
and
T\in(0,T_{*}]
if
n=3
be arbitrary.
The next goal is to derive further uniform bounds on
c_{\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}
.
Therefore, in order to obtain the desired estimates, we need to truncate the initial datum
c_{\varepsilon,0}
as it was done in
[
43
]
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the Lipschitz continuous truncation

\sigma_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002pts\mapsto\begin{cases}-1+\tfrac{1}{k}&\text{if $s<-1+\tfrac{1}{k}$},\\
s&\text{if $-1+\tfrac{1}{k}<s<1-\tfrac{1}{k}$},\\
1-\tfrac{1}{k}&\text{if $s>1-\tfrac{1}{k}$},\end{cases}

and we set
c_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}:=\sigma_{k}\circ c_{\varepsilon,0}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}:=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}+f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon,0}^{k})
.
The strong solution corresponding to initial datum
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0},c_{\varepsilon,0}^{k})
will be denoted as
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}^{k},p_{\varepsilon}^{k},c_{\varepsilon}^{k},\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k})
.
We point out that the estimates (
4.10
), (
4.12
), (
4.18
), (
4.19
), (
4.20
), (
4.35
) and (
4.36
) remain valid for the solution
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}^{k},p_{\varepsilon}^{k},c_{\varepsilon}^{k},\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k})
and are uniform with respect to
k
as long as
k
is sufficiently large.
Indeed, for the
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{s}]
chosen above, there exists
k_{0}=k_{0}(\varepsilon)
such that we have
{E}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0},c_{\varepsilon,0}^{k})\leq{E}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0},c_{\varepsilon,0})+C
for all
k\geq k_{0}
, and clearly the initial datum
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0}
does not depend on
k
. In the following, we thus consider
k\geq k_{0}(\varepsilon)
.

Due to the assumptions in Theorem
3.1
, we clearly have
\mu_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, as shown in
[
43
, Formula (3.9)]
, the function
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}
has the additional regularity

\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}\in C([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega)).

(4.37)

In view of the regularities stated in (
3.6
), we further have

c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\in C_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega))

(4.38)

thanks to an embedding result, which can be found, e.g., in
[
46
, Corollary 2.1]
.

Arguing as in
[
25
, Proof of Theorem 4.1]
(
n=2
) or
[
43
, Proof of Theorem 2.2]
(
n=3
), we derive the identity

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}(t)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\,\partial_{t}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\,\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime\prime}(c_{\varepsilon}^{k})|\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\theta_{0}|\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\end{aligned}

(4.39)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]
.
Formally, (
4.39
) can be obtained as follows:
We differentiate (
1.1c
) with respect to time, which yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}+f^{\prime\prime}(c_{\varepsilon}^{k})\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}

(4.40)

Now, we test (
1.1b
) by
-\partial_{t}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}
and (
4.40
) by
\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}
. Adding and integrating the resulting equations with respect to time from
0
to
t
, and using the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}\|^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}\,\Delta\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}\|^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\,\partial_{t}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s,

we arrive at (
4.39
).

By a straightforward computation, the second summand on the left-hand side of (
4.39
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\partial_{t}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\\

(4.41)

for almost all

Due to the properties of

\displaystyle H_{\varepsilon}^{k}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}\|^{2}-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon,0}\cdot\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}\,c_{\varepsilon,0}^{k}\;\mathrm{d}x.

4.41

4.42

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &H_{\varepsilon}^{k}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\theta|\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s+2\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k})\;\mathrm{d}s\\[4.30554pt]
&\quad\leq H_{\varepsilon}^{k}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\mu_{\varepsilon}^{k}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}s\\

(4.44)

for almost all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\leq H_{\varepsilon}^{k}(0)+C\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}c_{\varepsilon}^{k}\|_{*}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s

Step 3: A uniform estimate for

In the following, let

Therefore, we test equation (

) by

is strictly increasing, so is

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}\leq C\sqrt{p}.

(4.57)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\|f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\|_{L^{2}(A_{\delta})}|A_{\delta}|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus A_{\delta}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime\prime}(sc_{\varepsilon}+(1-s)c)(c_{\varepsilon}-c)\ ds\right|\ \;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\|f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\|\|c_{\varepsilon}-c\|+C_{\delta}\|c_{\varepsilon}-c\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\big(\|f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})\|+\|f^{\prime}(c)\|\big)\|c_{\varepsilon}-c\|+C_{\delta}\|c_{\varepsilon}-c\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{C}{\delta}+\left|\Omega\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}C_{\delta}\right)\|c_{\varepsilon}-c\|=:K_{\delta}\|c_{\varepsilon}-c\|.\end{aligned}

(5.8)

Here, we used that
f^{\prime}(c)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
(see (
2.13
)) and that
f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon
(see (
3.6
)).

Step 2: Estimates for the Navier–Stokes equation.

From now on, the letter
C
denotes generic positive constants that may depend only on the choice of
\Omega
, the number
\delta
from (
5.2
), the initial data and the system parameters, but not on
\varepsilon
.
The exact value of
C
may vary throughout this proof.

Testing (
5.1a
) by
A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
and invoking the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\big\|^{2}=(\partial_{t}\tilde{\mathbf{v}},A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}),

we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\big[(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}-(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\big]\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{v}}:\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}-\mu\nabla c)\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x.\end{aligned}

(5.9)

Recalling that
\mathbf{v}
,
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
are divergence-free, the second term on the left-hand side of (
5.9
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\big[(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}-(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\big]\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{v}}):\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\otimes\mathbf{v}):\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x.\end{aligned}

(5.10)

Using the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality, Young’s inequality and the uniform bound (
3.7
), the first term can be estimated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{v}}):\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\,\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{3}(\Omega)}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|\\
&\quad\leq C\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{16}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.11)

Proceeding similarly, we deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\int_{\Omega}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{3}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\quad\leq C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{16}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.12)

Combining (
5.10
)–(
5.12
), we conclude

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\big[(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}-(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\big]\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{1}{8}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}.

(5.13)

Using integration by parts and recalling once more that
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
is divergence-free, we further obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{\mathbf{v}}:\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x=\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}.

(5.14)

Via integration by parts, the right-hand side of (
5.9
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\mu_{\varepsilon}\nabla c_{\varepsilon}-\mu\nabla c)\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mu}\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x.

(5.15)

Employing the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality, Young’s inequality and the uniform bound (
3.7
), the first term can be estimated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|\|\tilde{c}\|\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq C\|\nabla\mu_{\varepsilon}\|\|\tilde{c}\|\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\|\tilde{c}\|\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{\theta_{0}}{16}\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{\theta_{0}}{8}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.16)

We point out that this estimate is one of the main reasons for which the solution
(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon},c_{\varepsilon})
has to be strong since otherwise we would only have
\mu_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.
By means of (
5.1c
), the second term in (
5.15
) can be expanded as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mu}\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\big)\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}\>\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad+\int_{\Omega}\big(f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\big)\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x.\end{aligned}

(5.17)

Using Young’s inequality, Agmon’s inequality and the uniform estimate (
3.7
), the first summand on the right-hand side can be estimated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\big)\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla c\|^{2}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+C\|\nabla c\|^{2}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|\\
&\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2},\end{aligned}

(5.18)

where we exploited (
2.13
) for the
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
-regularity of
\mu
.
To estimate the second summand on the right-hand side of (
5.17
),
we use the folowing Poincaré type inequality, which can be found in
[
45
, Theorem 1]
: there exists
C>0
, such that for all
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(f)\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(5.19)

We further recall (
2.15
), (
2.17
) and (
3.7
), the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
, and the continuous embeddings

H^{3}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{2,4}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,4}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)
. With the help of these results, we derive the estimate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}\>\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\leq 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})}\,\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}})}\leq C\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})}\,\|\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq C\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})}\,\Big(\|\nabla c\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)}+\|D^{2}c\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\|\nabla c\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|\Big)\\
&\quad\leq C\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})}\,\|c\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}={\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\big(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\big)+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2},\end{aligned}

(5.20)

recalling
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})=\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\big(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\big)
.
Here, the first inequality follows by exploiting the properties of the interaction kernel
J_{\varepsilon}
(cf.
[
15
, p. 128]
).
Invoking (
5.8
), Agmon’s inequality and Young’s inequality, the third summand on the right-hand side of (
5.17
) can be bounded via the estimate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\big(f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\big)\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\leq C\|f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|\nabla c\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\quad\leq CK_{\delta}\|c_{\varepsilon}-c\|\,\|\nabla A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{1}{8}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{16}\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+CK_{\delta}^{4}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{8}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.21)

In view of (
5.17
),(
5.18
), (
5.20
) and (
5.21
), we thus have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mu}\nabla c\cdot A_{S}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\big(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\big)+\frac{1}{4}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}.

(5.22)

Eventually, combining (
5.9
) with (
5.13
), (
5.14
) and (
5.22
), we conclude

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{3}{4}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}\\
&\quad\leq C\big(\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\big(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\big)+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.23)

Step 3: Estimates for the convective Cahn–Hilliard system.

Testing (
5.1b
) with
\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})
and using the identity

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\nabla\mathcal{N}\big(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\big)\|^{2}=\big(\partial_{t}\tilde{c},\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\big),

we derive the equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}=-\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla c\big)\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x,

(5.24)

Expressing
\tilde{\mu}
via (
5.1c
), the second term on the right-hand side can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x&=-\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}+\Delta c\big)(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad-\int_{\Omega}\big(f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\big)(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x.\end{aligned}

(5.25)

Recalling the definition of
f
and the condition
F^{\prime\prime}\geq\theta

(see
(S1)
), we use Young’s inequality along with (
5.8
) to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &-\int_{\Omega}\big(f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\big)(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}\big(F^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-F^{\prime}(c)\big)\tilde{c}\;\mathrm{d}x+\theta_{0}\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}-\int_{\Omega}\big(f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\big)\overline{\tilde{c}}\;\mathrm{d}x\\[4.30554pt]
&\leq-\theta\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+\theta_{0}\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+|\overline{\tilde{c}}|\;\|f^{\prime}(c_{\varepsilon})-f^{\prime}(c)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq-\theta\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+\frac{9}{8}\theta_{0}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.26)

We next use the identity

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}\,(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x=2\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}),

(5.27)

which follows by a straightforward computation exploiting the symmetry of the interaction kernel
J_{\varepsilon}
.
Using this result, we deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}+\Delta c\big)(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x&=-\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\big)(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+\frac{\theta_{0}}{8}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}-2\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}).\end{aligned}

(5.28)

Combining (
5.25
), (
5.25
) and (
5.28
), we have

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{5}{4}\theta_{0}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}-2\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})-\theta\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}.

(5.29)

Recalling that
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{v}
are divergence-free and using integration by parts, the second summand on the right-hand side of (
5.24
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla c\big)\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}c\>\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x.\end{aligned}

(5.30)

Invoking Hölder’s inequality, the Gagliardo–Nirengberg inequality, the embedding
\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)
and the uniform bound
(
3.7
), the first term on the right-hand side can be estimated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left|\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\tilde{c}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{6}(\Omega)}\|\tilde{c}\|\|\nabla\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\|_{\mathbf{L}^{3}(\Omega)}\\
&\quad\leq C\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{\frac{1}{2}}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|\,\|\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}\|\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{\theta_{0}}{4}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.31)

Moreover, employing (
3.7
) and Hölder’s inequality, we show that the second summand on the right-hand side of (
5.30
) fulfills the estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}c\>\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|\|c\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\|\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}.

(5.32)

Using (
5.31
) and (
5.32
) to estimate the right-hand side of (
5.30
), we infer

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla c_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla c\big)\mathcal{N}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{\theta_{0}}{4}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}+C\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.33)

Eventually, using (
5.29
) and (
5.33
) to bound the right-hand side of (
5.24
), we conclude

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}+\theta\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+2\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\theta_{0}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}+C\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.34)

Step 4: Completion of the proof.

Adding (
5.22
) and (
5.34
) we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+\theta\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\big(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\big)\\
&\quad\leq C\Big(\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}\Big)+\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+2\theta_{0}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}}|^{2}\end{aligned}

(5.35)

in
[0,T]
.
Recalling the definition of
\varepsilon_{s}
in (
4.47
),
applying Lemma
2.2
(b)
with
\gamma=\frac{1}{2\theta_{0}}
yields

\displaystyle 2\theta_{0}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}})+C\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}

as we consider
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{s}]
. Plugging this estimate into (
5.35
) and recalling

\overline{\tilde{c}}(t)=\overline{\tilde{c}_{0}}\;\;\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})=\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\big(\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\big),

we infer

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}+\theta\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})\\
&\quad\leq C\Big(\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{\ast}^{2}\Big)+\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}+C|\overline{\tilde{c}_{0}}|^{2}\end{aligned}

(5.36)

in
[0,T]
.
Thus, Gronwall’s lemma implies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(t)\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{c}(t)-\overline{\tilde{c}}(t)\|_{\ast}^{2}\\
&+\theta\int_{0}^{T}\|\tilde{c}\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})\;\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{c}_{0}\|_{\ast}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{T}|\overline{\tilde{c}_{0}}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\right)e^{CT}.\end{aligned}

(5.37)

Now, since
c\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))
(see (
2.15
) and (
2.17
)), Proposition
2.3
yields

\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}c+\Delta c\|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2\alpha}\|c\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2\alpha},

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
in case
\Omega
is a bounded domain and
\alpha=1
if
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Together with assumption (
3.13
), we thus have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(t)\|_{\sigma}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{c}(t)-\overline{\tilde{c}}(t)\|_{\ast}^{2}

\displaystyle+\theta\|\tilde{c}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{c})\;\mathrm{d}t\leq C\varepsilon^{2\alpha}.

(5.38)

As the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\sigma}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime}}
on
\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime}
are equivalent, this also yields

\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbf{H}^{1}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{\prime})}\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(t)\|_{\sigma}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha}.

(5.39)

Moreover, since the norms
\|\cdot\|_{*}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}}
on
H_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)
are equivalent, we further have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{c}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{c}-\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime})}+\|\overline{\tilde{c}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}

(5.40)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{c}(t)-\overline{\tilde{c}}(t)\|_{\ast}^{2}+\left|\overline{\tilde{c}_{0}}\right|\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha}.

Combining (
5
)–(
5.40
), we have thus verified estimate (
3.14
). Hence, the proof is complete.
