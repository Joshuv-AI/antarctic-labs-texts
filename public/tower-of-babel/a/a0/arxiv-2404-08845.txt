Enumerating 6D supergravities with 𝑻 ≤ 𝟏 T\leq 1
Yuta Hamada, and Gregory J. Loges

Abstract
The space of 6D supergravities with minimal supersymmetry is greatly constrained by anomaly cancellation. Nevertheless, a large number of models satisfy all low-energy consistency conditions and in this work we make progress towards exhaustively enumerating all anomaly-free models with at most one tensor multiplet. Generalizing previous techniques, we describe a general algorithm using multi-hypergraphs and simplicial complexes to systematically enumerate anomaly-free models with gauge groups of any number of simple factors and with hypermultiplets falling into any representations. Using these new ideas, we obtain a complete list of anomaly-free models for T ≤ 1 T\leq 1 , the only simplifying assumption being that the gauge group contains no U ⁡ ( 1 ) \operatorname{U}(1) , SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \operatorname{SU}(2) , SU ⁡ ( 3 ) \operatorname{SU}(3) or Sp ⁡ ( 2 ) \operatorname{Sp}(2) factors. We also study how many/which models in this ensemble satisfy several UV and swampland bounds which have been proposed and previously utilized to great effect, finding that none are ruled out for T = 0 T=0 and ≈ 50 % \approx\!50\% are inconsistent with quantum gravity for T = 1 T=1 .

From the structure of

1.

Due to their small hypermultiplet multiplicities, no vertices with

S

b_{i}\cdot b_{i}<-4

E_{7}

\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{\underline{3}},\mathbf{\underline{56}})

\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{\underline{3}},\mathbf{\underline{56}})

\SU(3)

4.2
Branch and prune

After having built the multi-hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
, admissible models can be constructed recursively using the ideas discussed in section 4.5 of
[
19
]
. Here we only point out some improvements that can be made for
T\leq 1
in particular (leveraging the structure discussed in section
4.1
). First of all, since trivial edges are so rare it is unnecessary to distinguish models which are
irreducible
, as described in section 4.4 of
[
19
]
. Second, because there can be at most one type-
S
vertex in an admissible model it is useful for this to the be
first
vertex as admissible models of ever-larger size are built iteratively, if at all.

We first present some useful ideas which can be used to bound the values of
\Delta
for admissible models. These will serve two purposes: (i) they can be used during the pruning step to abandon models for which no future branching will bring
\Delta
down in accordance with the bound
\Delta\leq 273-29T
, and (ii) they can be used to limit the size of the multi-hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
by providing bounds on
\Delta
of vertices which can ever appear in an anomaly-free model. In contrast to
[
18
]
where universal bounds were derived in the more limited setting where the hypermultiplet representations are restricted to a small handful of options, here the bounds are determined empirically.

Given an admissible model, represented by a labelled simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, for which the charged hypermultiplets are
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K})=\bigoplus_{R}n_{R}(\mathcal{K})\times R
where
n_{R}(\mathcal{K})>0
and
R
runs over representations of
G(\mathcal{K})
, we define

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K};\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{R\;:\;(H_{R},n_{R}(\mathcal{K}))\in\mathcal{R}}\hskip-20.0ptn_{R}(\mathcal{K})H_{R}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathcal{K};z,\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\Delta(\mathcal{K})-z\,H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K};\mathcal{R})\,,

(16)

where
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a region in the
(H,n)
-plane. Clearly
H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K};\emptyset)=0
and
\Delta(\mathcal{K})=\Delta(\mathcal{K};z,\emptyset)=\Delta(\mathcal{K};0,\mathcal{R})
. These two quantities satisfy some obvious monotonicity properties:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K};\mathcal{R}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K};\mathcal{R})\,,

(17)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}

\displaystyle<z

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathcal{K};z^{\prime},\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle\geq\Delta(\mathcal{K};z,\mathcal{R})\,,

\displaystyle z\geq 0\,,\;\;\mathcal{R}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathcal{K};z,\mathcal{R}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\Delta(\mathcal{K};z,\mathcal{R})\,.

It will useful to define the following semi-infinite rectangular regions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}[a,b]\vcentcolon=[0,a]\times[b,\infty)\,,\qquad\mathcal{R}[\infty,b]\vcentcolon=[0,\infty)\times[b,\infty)\,.

(18)

Then we have
H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K})=H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K};\mathcal{R}[\infty,0])
. The motivation for defining these regions in particular is that hypermultiplets
n_{R}\times R
can potentially merge with
n_{S}\times S
only when
(H_{R},n_{R})\in\mathcal{R}[n_{S},H_{S}]
, i.e. when
H_{R}\leq n_{S}
and
n_{R}\geq H_{S}
.

We will write
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
when there exists some subset of vertices of
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
such that the simplicial subcomplex induced by these vertices is
\mathcal{K}
(and similarly
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
for proper simplicial subcomplexes). We then write
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{K}
for the “complement” simplicial subcomplex, i.e. the simplicial subcomplex induced by the other vertices. Physically, decomposing a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
representing an admissible model into
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{K}
corresponds to restricting the gauge group
G(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})=\prod_{i\in I^{\prime}}G_{i}
to the subgroups
\prod_{i\in I\subset I^{\prime}}G_{i}
and
\prod_{i\notin I}G_{i}
and restricting the hypermultiplets appropriately.

The utility of the quantities
H^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K};\mathcal{R})
and
\Delta(\mathcal{K};z,\mathcal{R})
comes from the following inequality which relates a simplicial complex to its simplicial subcomplexes. For every
\mathcal{K}_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}
, if we write
\mathcal{K}_{2}=\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{1}
then

\Delta(\mathcal{K})\geq\Delta(\mathcal{K}_{1};z,\mathcal{R}_{\ast}^{(1)})+\Delta(\mathcal{K}_{2};1-z,\mathcal{R}_{\ast}^{(2)})\,,\quad\forall\;z\in[0,1]\,,

(19)

where the pair
(\mathcal{R}_{\ast}^{(1)},\mathcal{R}_{\ast}^{(2)})
is the fixed point of the following iteration:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}^{(1)}=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathcal{R}[\infty,0]\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\ell+1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{R\;:\;(H_{R},n_{R}(\mathcal{K}_{2}))\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}^{(2)}}\hskip-20.0pt\mathcal{R}[n_{R}(\mathcal{K}_{2}),H_{R}]\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\ell+1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{R\;:\;(H_{R},n_{R}(\mathcal{K}_{1}))\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}^{(1)}}\hskip-20.0pt\mathcal{R}[n_{R}(\mathcal{K}_{1}),H_{R}]\,.

Notice that
\mathcal{R}_{\ell+1}^{(i)}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\ell}^{(i)}
and there are only a finite number of possible values for
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}^{(i)}
, so
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}^{(i)}
are eventually constant and
\mathcal{R}_{\ast}^{(i)}
exist. We have the following inclusions which are often sufficient in practice:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\ast}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{1}^{(2)}=\bigcup_{R}\mathcal{R}[n_{R}(\mathcal{K}_{1}),H_{R}]\subseteq\mathcal{R}[n_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{K}_{1}),H_{\text{min}}^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K}_{1})]

(21)

\displaystyle n_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sup_{R}n_{R}(\mathcal{K})\qquad H_{\text{min}}^{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathcal{K})\vcentcolon=\inf_{R\;:\;n_{R}(\mathcal{K})>1}H_{R}

We will use this bound on
\mathcal{R}_{\ast}^{(2)}
, which importantly only depends on
\mathcal{K}_{1}
, momentarily.

During the pruning step one needs to discard all admissible models with
\Delta>273-29T
for which no amount of future branching will produce an anomaly-free model. Let
\mathcal{K}_{1}
be the simplicial complex under consideration with
\Delta(\mathcal{K}_{1})>273-29T
, and let
\mathcal{K}
denote some (larger, yet-to-be-constructed) model such that
\mathcal{K}_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}
. Then, using equations (
19
) and (
21
) along with
H_{R}\geq 3
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathcal{K})\geq\max_{z\in[0,1]}\Big[\Delta\big(\mathcal{K}_{1};1-z,\mathcal{R}[\infty,0]\big)+\hskip-10.0pt\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{K}_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3164,0.3477,0.7227}{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.8789,0.4063,0.2109}{{\mathrm{T}}}}}\\
\text{admissible}\end{subarray}}\hskip-10.0pt\Delta\big(\mathcal{K}_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3164,0.3477,0.7227}{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.8789,0.4063,0.2109}{{\mathrm{T}}}}};z,\mathcal{R}[n_{\text{max}}(\mathcal{K}_{1}),3]\big)\Big]\,.

(22)

Models for which the right-hand side above is larger than
273-29T
should be pruned. The superscript in
\mathcal{K}_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3164,0.3477,0.7227}{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.8789,0.4063,0.2109}{{\mathrm{T}}}}}=\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{1}
emphasizes that
\mathcal{K}
will not contain any additional type-
S
vertices other than those in
\mathcal{K}_{1}
, based on the choice made above to have type-
S
vertices included first, if at all. The admissible models which minimize
\Delta(\mathcal{K}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3164,0.3477,0.7227}{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.8789,0.4063,0.2109}{{\mathrm{T}}}}};z,\mathcal{R}[a,3]\big)
for fixed
a
turn out to be very simple and the infimum above is piece-wise linear in
z
, allowing for a straightforward maximization over
z
. For example, for
a=5
we find

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{K}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3164,0.3477,0.7227}{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.8789,0.4063,0.2109}{{\mathrm{T}}}}}\\
\text{admissible}\end{subarray}}\Delta\big(\mathcal{K}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3164,0.3477,0.7227}{{\mathrm{N}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.8789,0.4063,0.2109}{{\mathrm{T}}}}};z,\mathcal{R}[5,3]\big)=\begin{cases}\min\{56,\,61-64z,\,91-105z\}&z\in[0,\tfrac{13}{15}]\,,\\
-\infty&z\in(\tfrac{13}{15},1]\,,\end{cases}

(23)

In appendix
C
we discuss the determination of this function for all
a
in more detail. In doing so we provide strong empirical evidence that the claimed bounds are correct and that the above argument is not subject to any logical inconsistencies; at face value we are using properties of the full ensemble of admissible models in order to prune models, meaning that some of the larger admissible models are never constructed.

Discussion

In this work we have performed an exhaustive enumeration of anomaly-free models for 6D,
\mathcal{N}=(1,0)
supergravity with
T\leq 1
, the only simplifying assumption being that the gauge group contains no
\U(1)
,
\SU(2)
,
\SU(3)
or
\Sp(2)
factors. In particular, we saw how a natural generalization of previous graph-theoretic techniques to multi-hypergraphs and simplicial complexes allows for general hypermultiplet representations. Implementing these ideas for
T=0
, we find exactly
20
models with hypermultiplets charged under three or more gauge factors, most of which can be related via Higgsing. For
T=1
there are
608,\!355
anomaly-free models, with the number of simple gauge factors reaching as high as ten. We have made the data for this ensemble available at
[
35
]
, here highlighting some choices, extreme examples of models with large gauge groups (both dimension and rank), large values of
\Delta(\mathcal{v})
for individual vertices or notable hypermultiplet representations. As for
T=0
, very few models in this ensemble make use of hypermultiplets charged under three or more gauge factors, but we exemplify a general strategy which produces anomaly-free models with hypermultiplets charged under four or more gauge factors, necessarily including
\SU(2)
,
\SU(3)
or
\Sp(2)
.

Our findings suggest that extending this exhaustive enumeration of anomaly-free models to include
\SU(2)
,
\SU(3)
or
\Sp(2)
or to
T\geq 2
is currently infeasible. These three low-rank groups lead to a multi-hypergraph of unwieldy size due to their large number of low-dimension representations. Similarly, even for
T=2
vertices are less constrained because the lattice
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{1,2}
is so much larger, and (extrapolating from figure
2
) we should expect to have to go out to
\Delta(\mathcal{v})\gtrsim 900
in order to capture all relevant vertices.

We also considered the effects of imposing three additional UV bounds, one bound which arises from the consistency of string probes and two proposed bounds which restrict the allowed hypermultiplet representations. Somewhat curiously, we find that anomaly cancellation is strong enough for
T=0
so that
all
models are consistent with the UV bounds we impose, suggesting that the field theory and quantum gravity landscapes may coincide for
T=0
. For
T=1
they definitely do not coincide: all told we were able to rule out
\mathcal{O}(50\%)
of anomaly-free models as inconsistent, with the string probe bound disproportionately killing models with odd charge lattice. Using the other two bounds, we are also able to provide an independent argument why a certain family of anomaly-free models with particular type-
S
vertices, which had previously been shown to have no F-theory realization, is inconsistent with quantum gravity.

It is an interesting question to determine precisely how many/which of the models we have not been able to rule out as inconsistent actually have a realization in string theory. As mentioned above, some of the “maximal” examples of section
5.2
can be realized non-geometrically in the heterotic string using asymmetric orbifolds. It has also been shown that a large class of models have a geometric F-theory description
[
16
]
. Whether these two construction methods encompass all consistent models remains to be seen.

Unimodular lattices of signature
(1,1)

There are two unimodular lattices of signature
(1,1)
, both consisting of points
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
but with different inner product
\Omega
: the odd lattice
\Gamma_{1,1}
has
\Omega=\diag(1,-1)
and the even lattice
U
has
\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. For
\Gamma_{1,1}
,
b_{0}\cdot b_{0}=8
uniquely determines
b_{0}=(3,1)
up to discrete symmetries of the lattice. For
U
, there are two inequivalent choices:
b_{0}=(2,2)
and
b_{0}=(1,4)
. However, the latter is superfluous for the following reason: since
b_{0}\cdot b_{i}\equiv b_{i}\cdot b_{i}\mod{2}
for all
b_{i}
, we know that each vector takes the form
b_{i}=(u_{i},2v_{i})
, but the sub-lattice of vectors of this shape in
U
is isomorphic to a different sub-lattice of index two for which
b_{0}=(2,2)
through
\varphi\big((u,2v)\big)=(2u,v)
. Therefore if a choice for the
b_{I}\in U
has
b_{0}=(1,4)
there is always a different choice for which
b_{0}=(2,2)
is a characteristic vector of the lattice. In
[
37
,
38
]
it was shown that
b_{0}=(1,4)
can be ruled out from a bottom-up perspective by requiring that the model be well-defined on any spin manifold. By the above argument we see that this does not actually rule out any models since one can always pick
b_{0}=(2,2)
instead.

For both of the two lattices we can determine each vector
b_{i}
from
b_{i}\cdot b_{i}
and
b_{0}\cdot b_{i}
alone. First of all, it must be that
8b_{i}\cdot b_{i}-(b_{0}\cdot b_{i})^{2}=-d^{2}
for some
d\in\mathbb{Z}
since
b_{0}\mathbb{Z}\oplus b_{i}\mathbb{Z}
is a sublattice of a unimodular lattice for each
i
. A straightforward calculation yields the following conditions and solutions:

•

\Gamma_{1,1}
with
b_{0}=(3,1)
:
b_{i}
exists iff
b_{0}\cdot b_{i}\equiv\pm 3d\mod{8}
, in which case

\displaystyle b_{0}\cdot b_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv+3d\mod{8}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\;\;b_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{3b_{0}\cdot b_{i}-d}{8},\tfrac{b_{0}\cdot b_{i}-3d}{8}\big)\,,

(54)

\displaystyle b_{0}\cdot b_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv-3d\mod{8}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\;\;b_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{3b_{0}\cdot b_{i}+d}{8},\tfrac{b_{0}\cdot b_{i}+3d}{8}\big)\,.

There are cases where both conditions are met and
b_{i}
is not uniquely determined. For example,
b_{i}\cdot b_{i}=24
,
b_{0}\cdot b_{i}=16

Appendix B
Multi-hypergraph construction

In our analysis one of our goals is be comprehensive when it comes to the allowed hypermultiplet representations. However, for small-rank simple groups this poses a computational problem because the number of irreducible representations with dimension less than
x
grows very quickly with
x
, leading to a proliferation of vertices even with only moderate
\Delta(\mathcal{v})
and a multi-hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
of unwieldy size. In practice, we find that
\mathcal{G}
can be constructed for
T\leq 1
for the groups
\SU(N\geq 5)
,
\SO(N\geq 7)
,
\Sp(N\geq 4)
,
E_{N}
,
F_{4}
and
G_{2}
without any special considerations, while
\SU(4)
and
\Sp(3)
come within reach with some additional ideas which we discuss below.
\SU(2)
,
\SU(3)
and
\Sp(2)
appear to be well out of reach without limiting the allowed hypermultiplet representations by hand. In addition to contributing a large number of vertices, these groups allow for many more non-trivial edges because of their low-dimension fundamental representations.

For
\SU(4)
and
\Sp(3)
, generating all vertices up to
\Delta(\mathcal{v})\lesssim 700
for

is possible. We find that together these contribute

, compared to

for
all
higher-rank groups combined. This would lead to

hyperedges since
\mathcal{G}
is not sparse, which is well out of our reach to manage. Instead, the approach we take is the following. After having generated vertices for
\mathcal{G}
out to sufficiently large
\Delta(\mathcal{v})

for those between type-

vertices. Since the number of type-

and type-

vertices is quite small, the number of such edges is only

is the number of simple groups being considered. Then for each type-

vertex

Appendix D
Automated Higgsing

As a non-trivial check of our results (both the code and the bounds discussed in appendix
C

T=0
and
T=1
are closed under Higgsing. By this we just mean the group-theoretic process of picking a maximal subgroup and computing the resulting hypermultiplet spectrum; we make no attempt at a detailed analysis of the scalar potential for any of the models. In this section we outline some of the details of this procedure.

Given a set of hypermultiplets
\mathcal{H}
falling into representations of the gauge group
G
, the hypermultiplets
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
which result upon breaking to a subgroup
G^{\prime}\subset G
are determined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\varphi(\mathcal{H})\oplus(-1)\varphi(\mathrm{Adj}_{G})\oplus\mathrm{Adj}_{G^{\prime}}

where
\varphi
gives the branching rules. For example,
\Sp(7)
has a maximal subgroup
\Sp(3)\times\Sp(4)
for which the branching rules include

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{\underline{1}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{\underline{1}},\mathbf{\underline{1}})\,,

(63)

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{\underline{14}})

Generically, anomaly-free models have gauge groups containing more than one simple factor and the procedure is more involved than simply checking maximal subgroups of each gauge factor independently. There are models for which more than one simple factor must break to a maximal subgroup simultaneously, corresponding, e.g., to giving a bi-fundamental hypermultiplet a VEV: see figure
6
for many such examples. For a model with gauge group
G=\prod_{i=1}^{\kappa}G_{i}
and hypermultiplets
\mathcal{H}
, introduce the following notation. First, let
L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{k})
denote a choice of subgroups for each
G_{i}
, where
L_{i}
is either
G_{i}
itself or one of its maximal subgroups, and let
|L|
be the number of
L_{i}
for which
L_{i}\neq G_{i}
is a
proper
subgroup. Write
G_{L}
and
\mathcal{H}_{L}
to denote the model which results upon Higgsing to the maximal subgroup of
G
as specified by
L
and write
G_{L}|
and
\mathcal{H}_{L}|
for the restriction to those gauge factors for which
L_{i}\neq G_{i}
(i.e. ignoring gauge factors
G_{i}
which were unaltered). Then we apply the following procedure:

1.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\{(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})\}
. That is,
\mathcal{L}_{0}
consists only of the trivial Higgsing which leaves the model unaltered.

2.

For each
\ell=1,2,\ldots,\kappa
in turn…

(a)

Construct the set of candidates
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
consisting of
L
with
|L|=\ell
which have the property that reverting any single
L_{i}\neq G_{i}
in
L
back to
G_{i}
produces an element of
\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}
.

(b)

For each
L\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
, apply the Higgsing described by
L
to the full model, removing by hand all
\U(1)
,
\SU(2)
,
\SU(3)
and
\Sp(2)
factors other than any
\SU(3)
NHCs. If the resulting model is anomaly-free (i.e. all
n_{R}\geq 0
in
\mathcal{H}_{L}
), check if it appears in the ensemble. Otherwise, if
\mathcal{H}_{L}|
has
n_{R}<0
for any non-trivial

, remove
L
from
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
.

Following the above procedure finds all breaking patterns
G\to G^{\prime}
which are minimal, in the sense that if Higgsing
G
to the maximal subgroup
G^{\prime}\subset G
produces an anomaly-free model then there is no intermediate subgroup

which also produces an anomaly-free model.
