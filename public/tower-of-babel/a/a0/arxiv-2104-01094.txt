High pressure study of low-Z superconductor Be 22 ​ Re \text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re} Preprint: APS/123-QED
J. Lim, A. C. Hire, Y. Quan, J. Kim, L. Fanfarillo, S. R. Xie, R. S. Kumar, C. Park, R. J. Hemley, Y. K. Vohra, R. G. Hennig, P. J. Hirschfeld, G. R. Stewart, J. J. Hamlin

Abstract
With T c ∼ 9.6 K T_{c}\sim$9.6\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$ , Be 22 ​ Re \text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re} exhibits one of the highest critical temperatures among Be-rich compounds. We have carried out a series of high-pressure electrical resistivity measurements on this compound to 30 GPa 30\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a} . The data show that the critical temperature T c T_{c} is suppressed gradually at a rate of d ​ T c / d ​ P = − 0.05 K / GPa dT_{c}/dP=$-0.05\text{\,}\mathrm{K}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}$ . Using density functional theory (DFT) calculations of the electronic and phonon density of states (DOS) and the measured critical temperature, we estimate that the rapid increase in lattice stiffening in Be 22 Re overwhelms a moderate increase in the electron-ion interaction with pressure, resulting in the decrease in T c T_{c} . High pressure x-ray diffraction measurements show that the ambient pressure crystal structure of Be 22 ​ Re \text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re} persists to at least 154 GPa 154\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a} . We discuss the relationship between low-Z Be-rich superconductors and the high- T c T_{c} superhydrides.



I
Introduction

In 1968, Ashcroft proposed that a metallic form of dense hydrogen would become a high-temperature superconductor (HTSC) within the framework of Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) theory
Ashcroft 1968
.
The high transition temperature derives in part from the uniquely high vibrational phonon frequencies associated with the lightest of all elements, hydrogen.
The experimental realization of metallic hydrogen, however, has been extremely challenging due to the required ultra high pressures - theoretical predictions suggest pressures over
\sim$450\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}$
may be required
McMinis et al. 2015
.
Some forty years after the prediction of monatomic metallic hydrogen, Ashcroft
Ashcroft 2004
proposed that hydrogen-rich metallic compounds are also candidates for HTSC.
It was suggested that by subjecting hydrogen to “chemical precompression” through the incorporation of heavier elements, the pressures required to reach the metallic state could be lowered substantially.

Recently, these ideas have been borne out in dramatic fashion, with the discovery of several hydrogen-rich compounds that become superconducting at record-breaking temperatures
Drozdov et al. 2015
;
Somayazulu et al. 2019
;
Drozdov et al. 2019
;
Snider et al. 2020
.
A key feature of these superconducting phases is that they appear to contain higher ratios of hydrogen than are stable at ambient pressure.
For example, at ambient pressure, the most hydrogen-rich compound formed by hydrogen and sulfur is
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
, but under pressure this compound can decompose into S and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{S}
(the later being a high temperature superconductor
Drozdov et al. 2015
).
These become stable only at high pressures (often well above
\sim$100\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}$
) and most form following treatment at high temperatures (over
\sim$1000\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
by laser heating)
Somayazulu et al. 2019
;
Drozdov et al. 2019
;
Kong et al. 2019
.
Compositions of hydrogen:non-hydrogen higher than 6:1 are referred to as “superhydrides”
Geballe et al. 2018
and several such materials have been found to become superconducting at high temperatures, even approaching room temperature (
e.g.

\text{LaH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}

Somayazulu et al. 2019
;
Drozdov et al. 2019
and
\text{YH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}

Kong et al. 2019
).

Though hydrogen-rich compounds offer a route to extraordinarily high superconducting critical temperatures, the presence of this element produces unique challenges.
The experiments typically start with host materials (lower hydrides) loaded into a diamond cell with dense hydrogen gas.
In addition to the normal challenges of ultra high experiments, the presence of large amounts of highly compressible hydrogen can lead to
a sample that is extremely small (
\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
in diameter),
difficulties establishing good contacts for electrical resistivity measurements,
and an increased likelihood of failure of the diamond anvils.
These challenges make it worthwhile to investigate whether other low-Z (
i.e.
, light element) superconductors may also exhibit high superconducting critical temperatures.

Among systems at ambient pressure,
\text{MgB}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(
T_{c}=$39\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
) provides the best known example of a low-Z superconductor with a high critical temperature
Nagamatsu et al. 2001
.
The lightest elemental metal, Li (
Z=3
), exhibits a critical temperature of only
0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{K}
at ambient pressure
Tuoriniemi et al. 2007
, but this increases to
15-$20\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
under pressures of
\sim$30\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}$

Shimizu et al. 2002
;
Struzhkin et al. 2002
;
Deemyad and Schilling 2003
.
A number of studies have focused on the potential for high-
T_{c}
superconductivity in novel lithium based compounds at high pressure (
e.g.
, Ref.
Rosner et al. 2002
).
Substantially less work has been done on compounds of the second lightest elemental metal, Be (
Z=4
).
This may be due, in part, to the dangers associated with the inhalation of Be, though alloys and compounds of Be such as Be-Cu are safe to handle and find widespread use.
Elemental Be is a poor superconductor with
T_{c}=$26\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{K}$

Falge 1967
, but the potential for high
T_{c}
values in Be compounds has been appreciated for some time
Klein et al. 1980
.
Beryllium tends to form compounds that are very Be-rich (
e.g.
,
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}
), but unlike the case of the superhydrides, these low-Z rich compounds can often be synthesized at ambient pressure.
Several Be-rich compounds are found to be superconductors at ambient pressure (
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{U}

Ott et al. 1983
,
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{Th}

Uhlířová et al. 2018
,
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{Lu}

Uhlířová et al. 2018
, and
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}
), while several others have yet to be reported to be superconducting, (
e.g.
,
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{La}
,
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{Y}
,
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{Re}
).
It is interesting to consider whether higher
T_{c}
values can be induced in Be rich compounds through the application of high pressure.

Among Be rich compounds,
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}
displays one of the highest critical temperatures, with
T_{c}=$9.6\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
(nearly 400 times higher than the
T_{c}
of elemental Be
Falge 1967
).
Superconductivity was reported in the
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{X}
(
X
= Mo, W, Tc, or Re) family of compounds by Bucher and Palmy in 1967
Bucher and Palmy 1967
.
The crystal structure is a cubic,
\text{ZrZn}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}
-type, with space group
{Fd}\overline{3}m
(No. 227) and
Z=8
formula units per conventional unit cell.
The structure is reminiscent of the clathrate-like structures found in certain super-hydrides at high pressure
Salke et al. 2019
.
Recent measurements on
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}
indicate isotropic
s
-wave superconductivity
Shang et al. 2019
.

In this work, we report the pressure-dependence of the superconducting transition temperature in
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}
revealed by high-pressure electrical resistivity measurements to
30\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}
.
The superconducting transition temperature,
T_{c}
, decreases monotonically with increasing pressure at a rate of
-0.055(3)\text{\,}\mathrm{K}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}
.
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that pressure decreases the electronic density of states at the Fermi level
N(0)
, and we present arguments why the effect of pressure on the electron-phonon matrix element and the electronic density of states is weak, such that lattice stiffening dominates the reduction of
T_{c}
with pressure.

II
Methods

Polycrystalline
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}
was synthesized by arc-melting.
Be and Re in stoichiometric amounts (with 3% excess Be added to account for mass loss during melting) were arc melted together three times using 99.5% pure Be from Brush Wellman and 99.97% pure Re from Alfa Aesar.

Powder x-ray diffraction measurements were performed using a Panalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer.
Analysis of the diffraction pattern was performed using the software GSAS-II
Toby and Von Dreele 2013
and indicates single phase material after melting (see Fig.
1
).
Magnetic susceptibility measurements performed using a Quantum Design MPMS gave a
T_{c}
onset of
8.6\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
and indicate full shielding (see Fig.
2
).

As the primitive cell contains 46 atoms, a complete calculation of the full phonon dispersion at multiple pressures is computationally prohibitive.
Therefore, we attempt here to develop a qualitative understanding of trends in

\Gamma

The phonon frequencies are calculated using the finite-difference method

To estimate the phonon density of states (PDOS),
F(\omega)
from the
\Gamma
point phonon frequencies, we apply a Gaussian smearing with a width of
\sigma=
1\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
(
4.135\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
),

F(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{D(\omega_{i})}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp{\left(-\frac{(\omega-\omega_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)},

(1)

where the summation is over all the
\Gamma
point phonons,
D(\omega_{i})
is the degeneracy of the phonon with frequency
\omega_{i}
.

Allen
et al.

e.g.

\alpha^{2}F

Assuming that the same holds also for
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}

\alpha^{2}F

\alpha^{2}F=kN(0)F(\omega),

(2)

where

N(0)

\lambda

\omega_{\text{log}}
, and
\left<\omega^{2}\right>

Allen and Dynes 1975
from the phonon density of states as

\lambda=2\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\alpha^{2}F(\omega)}{\omega}=2kN(0)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{F(\omega)}{\omega},

(3)

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{log}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\frac{2}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{\alpha^{2}F(\omega)}{\omega}\ln{\omega}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\frac{2kN(0)}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{F(\omega)}{\omega}\ln{\omega}\right],

(4)

\displaystyle\langle\omega^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega~\alpha^{2}F(\omega)\omega^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2kN(0)}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega~F(\omega)\omega^{n-1}.

(5)

If
k

\omega

E_{f}
and the phonons at frequency
\omega

In this study, we first assume that

\omega

We then fix the value of the constant
k

i.e.
,
f_{1}=f_{2}=1

Allen and Dynes 1975

T_{c}=\frac{\omega_{\text{log}}}{1.20}\exp\left[-\frac{1.04(1+\lambda)}{\lambda-\mu^{*}(1+0.62\lambda)}\right]

The fact that

Adjacent to Be in the periodic table, Li is a prototypical simple metal at ambient pressure, but exhibits a remarkable divergence from simple metal behavior at high pressure.
Under pressure, Li becomes superconducting at temperatures approaching
20\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, exhibits complex crystal structures, and even becomes semiconducting above
75\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}

Neaton and Ashcroft 1999
;
Hanfland et al. 2000
;

;
Struzhkin et al. 2002
;
Deemyad and Schilling 2003
;

;

The anomalous behavior of Li has been attributed to the influence of the ion cores, which approach each other at high pressure, increasingly restrict the valence electrons to low symmetry interstitial regions, and eventually localize them enough to produce semiconducting behavior
Neaton and Ashcroft 1999
;
Lv et al. 2011
;
Matsuoka and Shimizu 2009
.
Similar physics is thought to influence the behavior of certain Li-rich compounds which have either been found
Matsuoka et al. 2008
to exhibit superconductivity (
T_{c}
=
13\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
) under pressure or have been predicted to exhibit higher temperature superconductivity or complex crystal structures under pressure
Neaton and Ashcroft 1999
;
Feng et al. 2007
;
Feng et al. 2008
.
However, the same evolution of complex crystal structures does not appear likely to occur in Be-rich compounds because the ion cores of Be are 25-40% smaller than those of Li

;
Martienssen and Warlimont 2005

The size difference is significant enough that even at
300\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}

;
Matsuoka et al. 2014

Thus, Be and Be-rich compounds may tend towards simple metal behavior even at multi-megabar pressures.

IV
Conclusions

In summary, experiments show that the superconducting critical temperature of
\text{Be}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{Re}
is suppressed by pressure to at least
30\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}
.
Computational estimates based on electronic density of states and phonon calculations suggest that
T_{c}
will continue to be monotonically suppressed at higher pressures. Furthermore, the calculations and measurements indicate that lattice stiffening overcomes electronic effects, leading to the observed decrease in
\lambda
and
T_{c}
with pressure.
High pressure x-ray diffraction shows that the ambient pressure crystal structure is remarkably stable and remains unchanged to at least
150\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}
.
This stability is similar to that observed in elemental Be, which remains in the ambient pressure
hcp
structure to at least
170\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}

McMahon and Nelmes 2006
.
