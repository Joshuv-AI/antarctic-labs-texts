Mathematical modelling of delay discounting with bi-faceted impulsivity
Shanu Shukla, Trambak Bhattacharyya

Abstract
Existing mathematical models of delay discounting (e. g. exponential model, hyperbolic model, and those derived from nonextensive statistics) consider impulsivity as a single entity. However, the present article derives a novel mathematical model of delay discounting considering impulsivity as a multi-faceted quantity. It involves the bi-faceted characteristic of impulsivity, and considers impulsivity as a variable represented by two positive and fluctuating quantities (e.g. these facets may be trait and state impulsivity). To derive the model, the superstatistics method, which has been used to describe fluctuating physical systems like a thermal plasma, has been adapted. According to the standard practice in behavioural science, we first assume that the total impulsivity is a mere addition of the two facets. However, we also explore the possibility beyond an additive model and conclude that facets of impulsivity may also be combined in a non-additive way. We name this group of models the Extended Effective Exponential Model or E 3 M. We find a good agreement between our model and experimental data. Keywords: Delay discounting model, impulsivity, random variables, fluctuation, superstatistics



I
Introduction

Delay discounting, a well-explored phenomenon in various fields including behavioural science, economics, and finance, describes the tendency for the subjective value of a reward to decrease as its delivery is postponed. This can be illustrated by a hypothetical scenario where an individual is presented with a choice between receiving $50 immediately or $70 after a month. Such a decision involves selecting between a smaller-sooner reward and a larger-later reward, influenced by personal factors like impulsivity and situational factors such as trust. Some individuals may opt for the immediate reward, exhibiting impulsive behaviour according to behavioural analysts
Odum 2011
.
This behaviour suggests that a distant reward is perceived as less valuable than an immediate one. Delay discounting has been observed across various species, reward types, and sample populations, as evidenced in studies
Mazur 1987
;
Rachlin and Jones 2008
;
Lempert and Pizzagalli 2010
;
West and Grigolini 2010
;
Cajueiro 2006
.
Researchers have proposed several mathematical models to comprehend this phenomenon, including the exponential, hyperbolic, and

q
-exponential models of delay discounting
Samuelson 1937
;
Rachlin 2006
;
Takahashi 2007
. These models commonly incorporate impulsivity as a parameter. However,
they also often treat impulsivity as a singular entity, while numerous studies acknowledge impulsivity as
a multi-faceted variable
Griffin and Trull 2021
.

To account for this complexity, we introduce a mathematical model that considers impulsivity as having two facets, as examined in Refs.
Wingrove and Bond 1997
;
Antons and Brand 2018
. As per the standard practice, we first assume an additive model in which the total quntity is a mere addition of the two facets. While addition offers utility, the facets may not be inherently additive. Such an indication may be obtained Ref.
Kendler et al. 2004
that concludes that people with high neuroticism were more likely to experience a depressive episode following stressful life events than those with low neuroticism. This conclusion hints at an interplay between trait (inherent) and state (induced by the environment) facets that need to be taken into account while combining them. However, this argument refers to neuroticism and there may be some complex relationships between neuroticism and impulsivity.

Now coming back to impulsivity and delay discounting, even if we assume that the facets of impulsivity are independent, addition is merely only one of the many ways to combine them to get the total impulsivity. Hence, in this article, along with an additive model, we also propose a nonadditive model
that we utilize to analyze existing datasets obtained from previous experiments.

The structure of this paper is as follows: the subsequent section provides a brief overview of existing mathematical models of delay discounting. Section
IV
presents a comprehensive derivation of our proposed model.
In Section
V
, we analyze available datasets and present the findings. Finally, we summarize and conclude our study in Section
VI
.

II
Existing mathematical models of delay discounting

In the experiments studying delay discounting, the subjective values of a reward are established by finding indifference points
Critchfield and Kollins 2001
.
In the case of rational agents, indifference points follow an exponential decay with delay. This model is called the exponential model

Samuelson 1937
(EM) and considers an exponential dependence of the subjective value of a reward on delay given by

V(D)=V(0)\exp({-\kappa_{0}D}),~~~~~~\text{(--EM--)}

(1)

where
V(0)
is the undiscounted value of a reward, and
V(D)<V(0)
is the discounted value of the reward after a delay
D
. The quantity

\kappa_{0}>0
is described as the impulsivity parameter. However, the exponential model describes the situation when the discount rate is a constant,
a characteristic observed for rational agents. Studies in delay discounting recognize inter-temporal consistency as a characterization of rationality.
However, rationality of agents has long been a matter of discussion and debate
Tversky and Kahneman 1986
in behavioural science. For non-rational agents, one needs to look
beyond the exponential model and previous researchers utilized the hyperbolic model
Rachlin 2006
.

However, the hyperbolic model is not able to distinguish between inter-temporal inconsistency and impulsivity, and hence Takahashi
Takahashi 2007
proposed the following model, also utilized in Ref.
Cajueiro 2006
, containing the
q
-exponential function

\displaystyle V(D)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V(0)}{\left(1+(1-q)\kappa_{1}D\right)^{\frac{1}{1-q}}}.

(2)

The
q
-exponential model has been used in many other behavioral studies
Takahashi 2010
;
Takahashi et al. 2012
;
Lempert and Pizzagalli 2010
;
Ishii and Eisen 2018
;
Stegall et al. 2019
;
Oller et al. 2021
.

II.0.1
Revisiting the effective Exponential Model

Models given by Eqs. (
1
)-(
2
) (or the ones equivalent to them) follow a phenomenological approach. However, we are interested in another formulation, called superstatistics, whose application in social systems was proposed recently in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
. This approach considers fluctuations in the scale parameter in an exponential model. For the physical systems, this leads to temperature fluctuation, and for the delay discounting model, this implies impulsivity to be considered as a random variable. Interestingly, superstatistics was first introduced in the study of physical systems
Beck and Cohen 2003
;
Wilk and Włodarczyk 2000
. As shown in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
, superstatistics provides an ab initio derivation of the effective exponential model, that is dual to the Takahashi
q
-exponential model given by Eq. (
2
).

Figure 1:
Fitting of observed data obtained from Ref.
Mansouri et al. 2020
using the EM and EEM. EM:
\kappa_{0}=0.0044\pm 0.0015
.
EEM:
q=9.067\pm 0.116,~\kappa_{1}=0.048\pm 0.013
.

Superstatistics considers that impulsivity, which has long been used in various models of delay
discounting as a scale parameter, is a positive and fluctuating quantity. This comes as no surprise as within a social group impulsivity does vary.
With this observation, the Effective Exponential Model (EEM), which contains a power-law function resembling the one discussed by Constantino Tsallis
Tsallis 1988
, was formulated in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
. It was shown that the
q
-exponential model of delay discounting proposed by Takahashi in Ref.
Takahashi 2007
is dual to the effective exponential model in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
by a
q\leftrightarrow 2-q
transformation. Hence, the effective exponential and the Takahashi model are similar, but not exactly the same in appearance. Notwithstanding, Takahashi’s model was not derived from superstatistics, and hence the approach followed in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
(and to be adapted in the present article) differs.

At this point, it will be worthwhile to briefly recapitulate some key details of the EEM. It was motivated that in a social system, impulsivity as a positive random variable should have a distribution. In the absence of any more knowledge of how the impulsivity distribution may look like, we may choose one of the ‘least informative options’ – the gamma distribution
Hogg et al. 2019
;
Budini 2015
.

By weighting impulsivity
\kappa
in the exponential model (EM) of delay discounting in Eq. (
1
) with the gamma distribution, the effective exponential factor, that looks like the Tsallis-like
q
-exponential power-law function, was obtained. The EEM proposed in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
is given by

\displaystyle V(D)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(0)\exp_{q}(-\kappa_{1}D)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{V(0)}{\left(1+(q-1)\kappa_{1}D\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}}}.~~~~~~\text{(--EEM--)}

In the above equation,
q
may be called the ‘nonextensivity parameter’ and
\kappa_{1}
is impulsivity. In Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
, they were shown to be related to the relative variance and the mean of impulsivity respectively. It is also straightforward to verify the
q\leftrightarrow 2-q
duality between Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
).
Fig. (
1
) shows a comparison of the EM and the EEM while describing experimental data.

Statement of the problem
: This article aims to extend the Effective Exponential Model by incorporating impulsivity as a bi-faceted construct. One important question that may be asked in this context is how to combine the two facets to get a resultant value. For example, if the two facets of impulsivity are trait and state impulsivities, how do they combine to yield the total impulsivity? One popular choice is to add the two facets. We show that such an operation to combine the facets also lead to a model similar to the effective exponential model in Eq. (
3
). However, we also explore the possibility of other operations to combine two facets of impulsivity.
The resulting group of models may be referred to as the Extended Effective Exponential Models or E
3
M. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to derive a mathematical model of delay discounting considering that impulsivity may be a multi-faceted entity. Furthermore, the novelty of this paper lies in the utilization of the superstatistics approach that considers fluctuation in scale parameters (e. g. temperature in a plasma or impulsivity in a social system). Similar to the EEM, the E
3
M identifies a power-law factor that can represent temporal discounting. In the following section, we will delve into the details of the steps to obtain such a factor for both additive and nonadditive cases.

III
Additive model

In this model we consider impulsivity
\kappa
to be described by an addition of two facets
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}
. Hence, the exponential factor in Eq. (
1
) will simply be

\exp\left(-(k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}})D\right)
.
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}
are fluctuating quantities such distributed according to a gamma distribution,

\displaystyle f(k_{i})=\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q_{i}-1}\right)\left\{\frac{1}{(q_{i}-1)k_{i}^{0}}\right\}^{\frac{1}{q_{i}-1}-1}\exp\left(-\frac{k_{i}}{(q_{i}-1)k_{i}^{0}}\right)(i:\text{T},\text{S}).

(4)

Following Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
we can define the effective exponential factor

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{i}~f(k_{i})\exp\left(-k_{i}D\right)=\left(1+(q_{i}-1)k_{i}^{0}D\right)^{-\frac{1}{q_{i}-1}}

(5)

that is nothing but a
q
-exponential function. Hence the additive model would be given by the following equation:

\displaystyle V(D)=V(0)\left(1+(q_{\text{T}}-1)k_{\text{T}}^{0}D\right)^{-\frac{1}{q_{\text{T}}-1}}\left(1+(q_{\text{S}}-1)k_{\text{S}}^{0}D\right)^{-\frac{1}{q_{\text{S}}-1}}.

(6)

However, it is possible to show that addition is not the only operator that can combine two facets of impulsivity. In the next section we explore this aspect.

IV
Nonadditive model

IV.1
Step 1: finding a joint distribution

Following the previous section, we consider impulsivity to be represented by
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}
, two positive, independent random variables. In the absence of any further knowledge of how they are distributed in a (social) system, one of the least informative options,
i.e.
, a joint gamma distribution of
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}
given by
Hogg et al. 2019
;
Budini 2015

\displaystyle f(k_{\text{T}},k_{\text{S}})=\frac{k_{\text{T}}^{\frac{1}{q_{\text{T}}-1}-1}k_{\text{S}}^{\frac{1}{q_{\text{S}}-1}-1}\exp(-\frac{k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}}}{\theta})}{\theta^{\frac{1}{q_{\text{T}}-1}}\theta^{\frac{1}{q_{\text{S}}-1}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q_{\text{T}}-1}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q_{\text{S}}-1}\right)},

(7)

for a single scale parameter
\theta>0
and shape parameters
1<q_{\text{T}}<\infty
and
1<q_{\text{S}}<\infty
, may be considered.

Now, we consider that impulsivity
k_{\text{R}}
is formed by a (nonadditive) combination of
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}
. Hence, we have to find out the distribution of the new positive, random variable
k_{\text{R}}
. Such a ritual is clearly described in textbooks of statistics
Hogg et al. 2019
and involves introducing an auxiliary random variable
k_{\text{A}}=k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}}
. So, the variable transformation we are looking for is
\{k_{\text{T}},k_{\text{S}}\}\rightarrow\{k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}}\}
. The distribution in terms of
k_{\text{R}}
and
k_{\text{A}}
is related to that of
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}
(Eq.
7
) by the following relation:

\displaystyle f(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}})=f[k_{\text{T}}(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}}),k_{\text{S}}(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}})]\left|\text{det}~J\right|,

(8)

where
\left|\text{det}~J\right|
is the absolute value of the determinant of the Jacobian variable transformation matrix defined by

\displaystyle J=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial k_{\text{S}}}{\partial k_{\text{R}}}&\frac{\partial k_{\text{S}}}{\partial k_{\text{A}}}\\
\\
\frac{\partial k_{\text{T}}}{\partial k_{\text{R}}}&\frac{\partial k_{\text{T}}}{\partial k_{\text{A}}}\end{pmatrix}

(9)

It is noteworthy that by choosing the auxiliary variable to be
k_{\text{A}}=k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}}
, we exclude the possibility that
k_{\text{R}}=k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}}
, as in that case the Jacobian would be zero.
It is apparent from Eq. (
9
) that to find the joint distribution we must know what
k_{\text{R}}
is. So far, we have just mentioned that
k_{\text{R}}
is formed
out of a nonadditive combination of
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}
, but did not explore what exactly that combination may be. We discuss this question in the next section.

IV.2
Step 2: combining
k_{\text{T}}
and
k_{\text{S}}

How the two facets of impulsivity combine does not have a straightforward answer. Although to provide utility they are added in general, the real scenario may be different.
To begin with, we introduce examples used in Ref.
Budini 2015
that considers two impulsed moments induced by fluctuations in a medium. In terms of financial markets two random variables may be similar to predicted increase or decrease of stock prices. So, fluctuation in price is given by the difference of the two variables. Such a system’s response will be dependent on these variables and can be represented by some kind of mean (
\mathcal{M}
) between the two variables.
So following the analogy of Ref.
Budini 2015
, one option is to treat
k_{\text{R}}
as the following:

\displaystyle k_{\text{R}}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(k_{\text{T}},k_{\text{S}})(k_{\text{T}}-k_{\text{S}}).

(10)

But adapting such an ansatz for
k_{\text{R}}
in our problem leads to a few issues. The first issue lies in an absence of a direct analogy between two facets of impulsivity and increase/decrease of stock prices. Even if we assume the analogy to be valid,
k_{\text{R}}
can be negative in Eq. (
10
) whereas, according to our consideration, impulsivity is a (a) positive and (b) fluctuating variable (
0<k_{\text{R}}<\infty
). So, although the ansatz in Eq. (
10
) satisfies condition (b), condition (a) is not satisfied. Ref.
Budini 2015
also explores the combination
k_{\text{R}}=k_{\text{T}}(k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}})^{-1}
that may be obtained from Eq. (
10
). But this ansatz for
k_{\text{R}}
, although yields a positive fluctuating variable, makes it bounded between 0 and 1. So,
k_{\text{R}}=k_{\text{T}}(k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}})^{-1}
may be a possible candidate modulo the boundedness restriction. Further exploration brings us to the last option suggested in Ref.
Budini 2015
given by
k_{\text{R}}=k_{\text{T}}k_{\text{S}}^{-1}
. As one notices, the last ansatz yields a positive, random variable that is not bounded, and it fulfils our criteria. Hence, in our analysis we choose

\displaystyle k_{\text{R}}=\frac{k_{\text{T}}}{k_{\text{S}}}.

(11)

IV.3
Step 3: finding a joint distribution in terms of
k_{\text{R}}

Now, with
k_{\text{R}}=k_{\text{T}}k_{\text{S}}^{-1}
and
k_{\text{A}}=k_{\text{T}}+k_{\text{S}}
, we find that

\displaystyle k_{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{\text{A}}k_{\text{R}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-1};~k_{\text{S}}=k_{\text{A}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-1};

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k_{\text{S}}}{\partial k_{\text{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{\text{A}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-2};~\frac{\partial k_{\text{S}}}{\partial k_{\text{A}}}=(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-1};~

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k_{\text{T}}}{\partial k_{\text{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{\text{A}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-1}-k_{\text{A}}k_{\text{R}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-2};~

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k_{\text{T}}}{\partial k_{\text{A}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{\text{R}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-1};~

\displaystyle\text{and~hence,}~\left|\text{det}~J\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{\text{A}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-2}.

(12)

Putting Eqs. (
12
) in Eq. (
8
), we obtain the following joint distribution:

\displaystyle f(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\text{A}}}{(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{2}}\left(\frac{k_{\text{A}}k_{\text{R}}}{k_{\text{R}}+1}\right)^{\alpha_{\text{T}}-1}\left(\frac{k_{\text{A}}}{k_{\text{R}}+1}\right)^{\alpha_{\text{S}}-1}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{k_{\text{A}}}{\theta}\right)}{\theta^{\alpha_{\text{T}}+\alpha_{\text{S}}}\Gamma\left(\alpha_{\text{T}}\right)\Gamma\left(\alpha_{\text{S}}\right)}

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k_{\text{R}}^{\alpha_{\text{T}}-1}k_{\text{A}}^{\alpha_{\text{T}}+\alpha_{\text{S}}-1}}{(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{\alpha_{\text{T}}+\alpha_{\text{S}}}}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{k_{\text{A}}}{\theta}\right)}{\theta^{\alpha_{\text{T}}+\alpha_{\text{S}}}\Gamma\left(\alpha_{\text{T}}\right)\Gamma\left(\alpha_{\text{S}}\right)},

where we have defined two positive quantities
\alpha_{\text{S}}=(q_{\text{S}}-1)^{-1}
and
\alpha_{\text{T}}=(q_{\text{T}}-1)^{-1}
.

IV.4
Step 4: Finding the extended effective exponential factor

We noticed earlier
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
that if we replace the exponential factor in the exponential model in Eq. (
1
) with a weighted average, we get the power-law
effective exponential model given by Eq. (
3
). Similarly, to obtain a nonadditive (i.e. the facets are not added) model we define an ‘extended effective exponential factor’ (essentially an average)
defined by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\text{eff}}^{\text{ext}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}\exp(-k_{\text{R}}D)f(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}})}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}f(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}})}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}\exp(-k_{\text{R}}D)k_{\text{R}}^{\alpha_{\text{T}}-1}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-\alpha_{\text{T}}-\alpha_{\text{S}}}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}k_{\text{R}}^{\alpha_{\text{T}}-1}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-\alpha_{\text{T}}-\alpha_{\text{S}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{\text{S}}+\alpha_{\text{T}})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{\text{S}})}~\mathcal{U}(\alpha_{\text{T}},1-\alpha_{\text{S}},D),

(15)

and replace the exponential factor of the exponential model with
\epsilon_{\text{eff}}^{\text{ext}}
. Hence the E
3
M can be written as

\displaystyle V(D)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(0)\times\epsilon_{\text{eff}}^{\text{ext}}=V(0)~\mathcal{U}(\alpha_{\text{T}},1-\alpha_{\text{S}},D)~\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{\text{S}}+\alpha_{\text{T}})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{\text{S}})}

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(0)~\mathcal{U}\left(\frac{1}{q_{\text{T}}-1},\frac{q_{\text{S}}-2}{q_{\text{S}}-1},D\right)~\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q_{\text{S}}-1}+\frac{1}{q_{\text{T}}-1}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{q_{\text{S}}-1}\right)}.~~~~~~\text{(--E${}^{3}$M--)}

In the above equation,
\Gamma
is the gamma function represented by the integral
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972c

\displaystyle\Gamma(n)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\exp(-x)x^{n-1};~\forall~\mathcal{R}(n)>0,

(17)

and
\mathcal{U}
is the confluent hypergeometric function of the second kind represented by the integral
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972b

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(a,b,z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\exp(-zt)t^{a-1}(1+t)^{b-a-1};~\forall~\mathcal{R}(a),\mathcal{R}(z)>0.

(18)

Hence, the numerator of Eq. (
14
) yields the confluent hypergeometric function
\mathcal{U}
. On the other hand,
the denominator of Eq. (
14
) can be calculated in terms of the Beta function represented by
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972a

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(m+1,n+1)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\hskip-20.0ptu^{m}du}{(1+u)^{m+n+2}}=\frac{\Gamma(m+1)\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(m+n+2)}.

(19)

Eq. (
16
) is the main result of our paper.

To further explore the result, variation of the factor
\epsilon_{\text{eff}}^{\text{ext}}
with delay is shown in Figs. (
3
) and (
3
). We observe that the factor monotonically decreases with delay and hence is suitable to describe the gradual decrease of indifference points in experimental observables. The average value of the variable
k_{\text{R}}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle k_{\text{R}}\rangle=\left\langle\frac{k_{\text{T}}}{k_{\text{S}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}k_{\text{R}}f(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}})}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}f(k_{\text{R}},k_{\text{A}})}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}k_{\text{R}}^{\alpha_{\text{T}}}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-\alpha_{\text{T}}-\alpha_{\text{S}}}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\text{R}}k_{\text{R}}^{\alpha_{\text{T}}-1}(k_{\text{R}}+1)^{-\alpha_{\text{T}}-\alpha_{\text{S}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{\text{T}}}{\alpha_{\text{S}}-1}=\frac{q_{\text{S}}-1}{(q_{\text{T}}-1)(2-q_{\text{S}})}~~~~~\text{(using Eq.~\ref{betafunc})}.

Since
k_{\text{R}}
is a positive quantity, its average should be positive. This imposes a new constraint
q_{\text{S}}<2
for Eq. (
20
) to be satisfied (since
q_{\text{S}},q_{\text{T}}>1
).

V
, we will consider a set of observed data to investigate the applicability of the model.

VI
Summary, conclusion and outlook

In this article, we propose an extension of the effective exponential model forwarded in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
. The present model, named
the extended effective exponential model or E
3
M, considers bi-faceted impulsivity represented by two positive, random variables. This is a reasonable consideration given the fact that
impulsivity does fluctuate in a social system from person to person. We find that with this consideration, the subjective value of a reward follows a power-law decay. However, unlike the EEM proposed in Ref.
Bhattacharyya et al. 2022
, this power-law decay is now dictated by a special mathematical function called the confluent hypergeometric function of the second kind.

The novelty of this article lies (a) in the superstatistics approach it takes and (b) in considering impulsivity as a bi-faceted entity.
To our knowledge, this is the first such attempt to derive a mathematical model of delay discounting involving two distinct facets of impulsivity. We conclude that while the two facets of impulsivity can be added, it is not the only way to combine the two facets. In this article we show that a ratio of the two facets is one possible combination that leads to our proposed model describing experimental data.

As we proceed with the mathematical analysis, an additional constraint like
q_{\text{S}}<2
is obtained. Given the constraint
q_{\text{S}}<2
, we argue that the

\langle k_{\text{R}}\rangle
can be any positive number for observed datasets. However, from the datasets analyzed in this paper, we observe that
\langle k_{\text{R}}\rangle<1
.

In this work, we consider datasets only from three different studies, and more datasets need to be analyzed with the help of the E
3
M. Since we consider the two facets to be independent, it would also be interesting to derive a generalized model considering some kind of correlation between the two facets. It is also worth mentioning that the present article only studies temporal discounting. Similar studies may be carried out when other types
of behaviour in effort discounting
Mitchell 2004
, probability discounting
Rachlin et al. 1991
and social discounting
Białaszek et al. 2019
are taken into account.

Another interesting formal development constitutes extending the E
3
M, considering more facets of impulsivity. There are some behavioural studies relying on a three-factor
Kapitány-Fövény et al. 2020
(cognitive impulsivity, behavioural impulsivity, and impatience/restlessness), or even the four-factor UPPS model
Whiteside et al. 2005
(urgency, premeditation, perseverance, and sensation seeking) of impulsivity. We think that present method adapted in Section
IV
may very well be extended to models accommodating multiple (
>
2) variables, but we reserve that work for the future.

To conclude, the approach adapted in the article is a generalized one involving random variables. It is, by no means, limited only to the studies of impulsivity and delay discounting. In fact, any system represented by random variables has benefited (e.g., physical systems like a thermal plasma) and will benefit from these lines of argument. So, we hope that more and more investigations in this direction will throw light on the applicability of the work in explaining systems represented by multi-faceted random variables.
