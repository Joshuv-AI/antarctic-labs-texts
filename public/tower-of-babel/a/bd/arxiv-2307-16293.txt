Regularity in polar spaces of infinite rank
Antonio Pasini

Abstract
In this paper we propose a definition of regularity suited for polar spaces of infinite rank and we investigate to which extent properties of regular polar spaces of finite rank can be generalized to polar spaces of infinite rank.



1
Introduction

This paper is a continuation of
[
9
]
. In
[
9
]
I mainly stressed on differences between polar spaces of infinite rank and those of finite rank, focusing on properties which hold for all polar spaces of finite rank but fail to hold in many polar spaces of infinite rank, thus unwillingly suggesting that the infinite rank case might be too wild for nice theories can be composed for it. In this paper I support the opposite view. Focusing on regularity and related properties, I shall set down pieces of a theory suited for all polar spaces, including those of infinite rank. As expected, the picture we can get in the infinite rank case is not so neat and simple as for polar spaces of finite rank. Nevertheless, it looks nicer than I dared to hope and more interesting than in the finite rank case.

1.1
Premise

We are not going to recall all basics on polar spaces; we presume the reader knows them. We only fix here some notation and terminology to be used in this introductory section. More information will be offered in Section
2
.

We use the symbol
\perp
to denote collinearity, with the convention that every point is collinear with itself. Given a point
x
of a polar space
{\cal S}
we denote by
x^{\perp}
the set of points of
{\cal S}
collinear with
x
and, for a set of points
X
of
{\cal S}
, we set
X^{\perp}:=\cap_{x\in X}x^{\perp}
. Two singular subspaces
X
and
Y
are said to be
opposite
when
X^{\perp}\cap Y=Y^{\perp}\cap X=\emptyset
. In particular, two points are opposite if and only if they are non-collinear and two maximal singular subspaces, henceforth called
generators
, are opposite if and only if they are disjoint.

The symbol
\langle.\rangle
stands for spans, but we use it for spans in polar spaces as well as in projective and vector spaces. Thus, if
X
is a set of points of a polar space
{\cal S}
then
\langle X\rangle
is the subspace of
{\cal S}
generated by
X
and, if
{\cal S}
admits a projective embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
, then
\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle
is the projective subspace of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
spanned by
\varepsilon(X)
.

In this paper we will often use an informal simplified notation, writing
\{X,Y\}^{\perp}
for
(X\cup Y)^{\perp}
,
\langle X,Y\rangle
for
\langle X\cup Y\rangle
,
\langle X\cap Y,Z^{\perp}\rangle
for
\langle(X\cap Y)\cup Z^{\perp}\rangle
and so on. We trust this notation will not confuse the reader.

We recall that the rank of a non-degenerate polar space
{\cal S}
, henceforth called
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
, is the least upper bound of the set of the ranks of the generators of
{\cal S}
, the rank of a projective space being its dimension augmented by 1. Following a well established custom, we write
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
and
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
as shortenings of the sentences “
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
is an infinite cardinal number” and “
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
is finite” respectively. We warn that, while when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
all generators of
{\cal S}
have the same dimension, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
all generators of
{\cal S}
are infinite dimensional but in general not all of them have the same dimension.

All polar spaces to be considered in the sequel are non-degenerate and thick-lined of rank at least 2, by assumption. All embeddings are full projective embeddings.

1.2
A definition of regularity

Different but equivalent ways exists to define regularity for pairs of opposite points of a generalized quadrangle. For instance, the following is a rephrasing of the special case
m=2
of a definition stated in
[
18
, 6.4.1]
for generalized
m
-gons: a pair
\{a,b\}
of opposite points of a generalized quadrangle is
regular
if, for any point
c
opposite both
a
and
b
, if
|\{a,b,c\}^{\perp}|>1
then
c\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
; equivalently, if
x,y\in\{a,b\}^{\perp}
and
x\neq y
then
\{x,y\}^{\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
. The following is also equivalent to this definition: for every line
\ell
, if
\ell\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp}\neq\emptyset
then
\ell\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\neq\emptyset
. In particular, in the finite case the latter amounts to say that
|\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}|
is equal to the number of lines through a point (compare Payne and Thas
[
13
, 1.3]
).

A generalization of these definitions to polar spaces of arbitrary but finite rank is proposed in
[
12
]
and
[
3
]
: two opposite points of a polar space
{\cal S}
of finite rank are said to form a
regular
pair if

({\mathrm{R}}1)

(X\cup Y)^{\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
for any two opposite generators
X
and
Y
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
; equivalently, if
c
is a point opposite both
a
and
b
and
\{a,b,c\}^{\perp}
contains two opposite generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
, then
c\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
.

It is proved in
[
12
, Lemma 5.5]
(also
[
3
, Proposition 5.1]
) that
({\mathrm{R}}1)
is equivalent to the following:

({\mathrm{R}}2)

if a generator
M
of
{\cal S}
contains a generator of the polar space
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
, then
M\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\neq\emptyset
.

Property
({\mathrm{R}}2)
still makes sense when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
. In fact
({\mathrm{R}}2)
is involved in a characterization of symplectic polar spaces of arbitrary (possibly infinite) rank (see
[
3
]
). Property
({\mathrm{R}}1)
also makes sense when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
, however it might possibly be vacuous in certain cases. Indeed it is still an open problem whether polar spaces of infinite rank exist which admit no pair of opposite generators. If
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
admits no pair of opposite generators then
({\mathrm{R}}1)
is (trivially true but) vacuous for
\{a,b\}
. This is not the unique problem we face with
({\mathrm{R}}1)
when
rk({\cal S})=\infty
; the following is another one. It is easy to see that
({\mathrm{R}}2)
implies
({\mathrm{R}}1)
, even if
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
. On the other hand, if
({\mathrm{R}}1)
holds for a pair
\{a,b\}
of opposite points and every generator of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
admits an opposite in
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
, then
({\mathrm{R}}2)
holds for
\{a,b\}
if and only if
X^{\perp}\cap M\neq\emptyset
for any generator
X
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
and every generator
M
of
{\cal S}
containing a generator of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
(see the proofs of
[
12
, Lemma 5.5]
and
[
3
, Proposition 5.1]
). So, if every generator of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
admits an opposite in
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
and
{\cal S}
satisfies the following property
(\mathrm{GS})
, then we can prove that if
({\mathrm{R}}1)
holds for
\{a,b\}
then
({\mathrm{R}}2)
also holds, otherwise we get stuck.

(\mathrm{GS})

X^{\perp}\cap M\neq\emptyset
for every generator
M
and every non-maximal singular subspace
X
of
{\cal S}
.

It is well known that
(\mathrm{GS})
holds true when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
but in general it fails when
\mathrm{rank}(S)=\infty
. This considered, in
[
3
]
we looked at
({\mathrm{R}}2)
as a possible definition of regularity when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
, discarding
({\mathrm{R}}1)
. In support of this proposal, consider the following sharpening of
({\mathrm{R}}1)
:

({\mathrm{R}}3)

we have
(X\cup Y)^{\perp}=\langle X\cap Y,\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
for any two generators
X
and
Y
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
.

Obviously,
({\mathrm{R}}3)
implies
({\mathrm{R}}1)
. In Section
3.1
(Theorem
3.3
), without assuming that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
, we shall prove the following:

Theorem 1.1

Properties
({\mathrm{R}}2)
and
({\mathrm{R}}3)
are equivalent.

As previously recalled, properties
({\mathrm{R}}1)
and
({\mathrm{R}}2)
are equivalent when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
. In this case
({\mathrm{R}}3)
and
({\mathrm{R}}1)
are equivalent. We believe that when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
property
({\mathrm{R}}3)
is stronger than
({\mathrm{R}}1)
, although we have no example at hand which shows that this is indeed the case.

We are now ready to state our definition of regularity for polar spaces of arbitrary, possibly infinite rank.

Definition 1.2

We say that a pair of opposite points of
{\cal S}
is regular if it satisfies property
({\mathrm{R}}3)
(equivalently,
({\mathrm{R}}2)
). If all pairs of opposite points of
{\cal S}
are regular then
{\cal S}
is said to be regular.

1.3
Tight embeddings

We say that an embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
of a polar space
{\cal S}
is
tight
if
\langle\varepsilon(M\cup M^{\prime})\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(V)
for at least one pair of (necessarily opposite) generators
M
and
M^{\prime}
of
{\cal S}
. When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=n<\infty
an embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
is tight if and only if
\dim(V)=2n
. If this is the case then
\langle\varepsilon(M\cup M^{\prime})\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(V)
for every pair pair of opposite generators
M
and
M^{\prime}
of
{\cal S}
. The following is proved in
[
10
, Theorem 1.2]
:

Proposition 1.3

An embeddable polar space of finite rank is regular if and only if it admits a tight embedding.

In particular, when
{\cal S}
(has finite rank and) admits a unique embedding, as it is the case when
{\cal S}
is defined over a division ring of characteristic different from
2
, then
{\cal S}
is regular if and only if it can be spanned by the union of (any) two opposite generators
[
10
, Corollary 1.3]
.

As we shall prove in Section
3.3
(Theorem
3.8
), Proposition
1.3
admits the following generalization:

Theorem 1.4

An embeddable polar space
{\cal S}
of possibly infinite rank admits a tight embedding if and only if the following holds for at least one (equivalently, every) pair
\{a,b\}
of opposite points of
{\cal S}
:

({\mathrm{R}}4)

the subspace
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
contains two singular subspaces
X
and
Y
such that
X
and
Y
are opposite generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
, we have

(X\cup Y)^{\perp}~=~\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}

(1)

and, if
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
is an embedding of
{\cal S}
, then

\langle\varepsilon(X\cup Y\cup(X\cup Y)^{\perp})\rangle~=~\mathrm{PG}(V).

(2)

When
{\cal S}
is embeddable the group
\mathrm{Aut}({\cal S})
acts transitively on the set of ordered pairs of opposite points. Accordingly, if
({\mathrm{R}}4)
holds for at least one pair
\{a,b\}
of opposite points of
{\cal S}
then it holds for any such pair. If furthermore
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
then
\mathrm{Aut}({\cal S})
is also transitive on the set of pairs of opposite singular subspaces of any given rank. Hence, if condition (
1
) holds for at least one choice of opposite points
a
and
b
of
{\cal S}
and opposite generators
X
and
Y
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
, then it holds for any such choice. In this case
({\mathrm{R}}4)
is equivalent to
{\cal S}
being regular.

In contrast, if
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
is infinite then in general
\mathrm{Aut}({\cal S})
is intransitive on the set of pairs of opposite generators and, similarly, the stabilizer of
\{a,b\}
in
\mathrm{Aut}({\cal S})
acts intransitively on the set of pairs of opposite generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
. So, it can happen that (
1
) holds for a particular choice of opposite generators
X
and
Y
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
but not for all of them. If this is the case then
{\cal S}
is not regular.

Moreover, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
then (
2
) holds for any choice of opposite singular subspaces
X
and
Y
. So, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
condition (
2
) is redundant. On the other hand, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
then in general
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
admits opposite generators which do not satisfy (
2
) (but might possibly satisfy (
1
)). Notably, this also can happen when
{\cal S}
is regular.

Suppose that
{\cal S}
admits a tight embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
and
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
is infinite. Then in general opposite generators
M
and
N
also exist such that
\langle\varepsilon(M\cup N)\rangle\subset\mathrm{PG}(V)
. This fact also explains why, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
, conditions (
1
) and (
2
) of
({\mathrm{R}}4)
might hold for certain pairs of opposite generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
but not for all of them. Indeed, as one can prove, if
X
and
Y
are opposite generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
then
\varepsilon(X\cup Y)
spans
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle
if and only if
X
and
Y
satisfy both properties (
1
) and (
2
).

Summarizing the above discussion, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
is infinite
({\mathrm{R}}4)
is not equivalent to
{\cal S}
being regular. In fact, as we shall see in Section
5.4
, polar spaces of infinite rank exist which admit a tight embedding (hence they satisfy
({\mathrm{R}}4)
) but are not regular.

Conjecture 1.5

Every regular polar space admits a tight embedding.

1.4
The three-generators property

Another characterization of regularity has been obtained in
[
12
]
by means of the so-called three-generators property. Let
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
. When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
is odd we assume that
{\cal S}
is thick. So,
{\cal S}
admits triples of mutually opposite generators and every pair of opposite generators belongs to at least one such triple. Let
M,M_{1},M_{2}
be such a triple. For every subspace
X
of
M
we put

\pi^{M}_{1,2}(X)~:=~((X^{\perp}\cap M_{1})^{\perp}\cap M_{2})^{\perp}\cap M.

(3)

Thus we obtain a duality
\pi^{M}_{1,2}
of
M
. The duality
\pi_{2,1}
is defined in the same way but switching
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Clearly,
\pi_{2,1}^{M}
is the inverse of
\pi_{1,2}^{M}
. Hence
\pi_{1,2}^{M}
is a polarity if and only if
\pi_{1,2}^{M}=\pi_{2,1}^{M}
.

Definition 1.6

We say that a pair
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
of opposite generators of
{\cal S}
satisfies the three-generators property if
\pi_{1,2}^{M}
is a polarity for every generator
M
of
{\cal S}
opposite to both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
.

The following has been proved in
[
12
]
.

Proposition 1.7

A polar space
{\cal S}
of finite rank is regular if and only if every pair of opposite generators of
{\cal S}
satisfies the three-generators property.

Remark 1

Definition
1.6
also makes sense when
{\cal S}
is non-tick of odd rank, but it is vacuous in this case. Indeed in this case no triples of mutually opposite generators exist; consequently, every pair of opposite generators trivially satisfies the 3-generators condition. However
{\cal S}
is regular in this case (by Proposition
1.3
when
{\cal S}
is embeddable and
[
12
, Proposition 5.11]
when it isn’t). Hence, when
{\cal S}
is non-thick of odd rank Proposition
1.7
trivially holds true.

The first obstacle we meet when looking for a generalization of Proposition
1.7
suited for polar spaces of infinite rank is the fact that, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
, the mapping
\pi_{1,2}^{M}
defined as in (
3
) cannot be a duality. Indeed in this case
\dim(M)
is infinite and infinite dimensional projective spaces admit no dualities. Moreover, as property
(\mathrm{GS})
might fail to hold in
{\cal S}
, it can happen that for some point
x\in M
we have
\pi_{1,2}^{M}(x)=M
. We must change our setting, replacing dualities with something weaker. We do as follows.

Given a projective space
\mathbb{P}
, let
\mathbb{P}^{*}
be its dual. Let
P
and
P^{*}
be the point-set of
\mathbb{P}
and the set of hyperplanes of
\mathbb{P}
respectively.

Definition 1.8

A partial duality of
\mathbb{P}
is a mapping
\pi:P\rightarrow P^{*}\cup\{P\}
such that, for every choice of distinct points
x
and
y
of
\mathbb{P}
, if both
\pi(x)
and
\pi(y)
belong to
P^{*}
then
\pi(x)\neq\pi(y)
and
\pi
induces a bijection from the line of
\mathbb{P}
through
x
and
y
to the line of
\mathbb{P}^{*}
through
\pi(x)
and
\pi(y)
.

Let
\pi
be a partial duality of
\mathbb{P}
. The set
\pi^{-1}(P^{*}):=\{x\in P~|~\pi(x)\neq P\}
is a (possibly empty) subspace of
\mathbb{P}
. If
\pi^{-1}(P^{*})=P
then we say that
\pi
is
non-degenerate
. Obviously,
\pi
is non-degenerate if and only if it is injective. In contrast, when
\pi^{-1}(P^{*})=\emptyset
we say that
\pi
is
trivial
. If for every two distinct points
x,y\in P
we have
x\in\pi(y)
if and only if
y\in\pi(x)
, then we say that
\pi
is
reflexive
. If
\pi
is reflexive then
P\setminus\pi^{-1}(P^{*})\subseteq\pi(x)
for every
x\in\pi^{-1}(P^{*})
. However no hyperplane of
\mathbb{P}
contains the complement of a proper subspace of
\mathbb{P}
. Therefore
\pi
is reflexive only if it is either non-degenerate or trivial. A non-trivial reflexive partial duality is called a
polarity
.

When
\dim(\mathbb{P})<\infty
the dualities of
\mathbb{P}
are precisely the (mappings from the poset of subspaces of
\mathbb{P}
to the poset of subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{*}
defined by) non-degenerate partial dualities; a duality is involutory if and olny if it is reflexive.

Turning back to our polar space
{\cal S}
but now allowing
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
to be infinite, let
M,M_{1},M_{2}
be three mutualy opposite generators of
{\cal S}
. Equation (
3
), whith
X
ranging in the set of points of
M
instead of the set of subspaces of
M
, defines a partial duality
\pi_{1,2}^{M}
of
M
(Section
4.4
, Lemma
4.6
). As in the finite rank case,
\pi_{1,2}^{M}
is a polarity if and only if
\pi_{1,2}^{M}=\pi_{2,1}^{M}
(Section
4.4
, Lemma
4.7
). Definition
1.6
still makes sense in the present setting. We are not going to repeat it here. However we need one more definition.

Definition 1.9

A generator
M
of
{\cal S}
is hyperbolic if every hyperplane of
M
is contained in at most one generator other than
M
.

As we shall prove in Section
4.5
(Theorem
4.11
), the following holds:

Theorem 1.10

Let
{\cal S}
be a polar space of infinite rank, defined over a division ring of characteristic different from
2
. Let
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
be opposite generators of
{\cal S}
such that at least one generator of
{\cal S}
exists which is opposite to both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Suppose moreover that neither
M_{1}
nor
M_{2}
are hyperbolic. Under these hypoyheses,
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
satisfies the three-generators property if and only if
\langle M_{1}\cup M_{2}\rangle={\cal S}
.

Conjecture 1.11

The hypothesis that the underlying division ring of
{\cal S}
has characteristic different from
2
can be dropped from Theorem
1.10
, provided that the conclusion of that theorem is rephrased as follows: the pair
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
satisfies the three-generators property if and only if
{\cal S}
admits a (necessarily tight) embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
such that
\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}\cup M_{2})\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(V)
.

2
Preliminaries

As in the Introduction, throughout this section
{\cal S}
is a non-degenerate thick-lined polar space of rank at least 2, possibly
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
.

2.1
A survey of elementary properties

In this subsection we recall a few well known properties of subspaces of
{\cal S}
, to be be freely used in the sequel of this paper.

We have
\langle X\rangle\subseteq X^{\perp\perp}
for every set
X
of points of
{\cal S}
. Also, if
X\subseteq Y
then
X^{\perp}\supseteq Y^{\perp}
. Consequently,
X^{\perp}=X^{\perp\perp\perp}
for every set
X
of points of
{\cal S}
. If moreover
X
is a singular subspace of
{\cal S}
then
X=X^{\perp\perp}
if and only if
X
is the intersection of a family of generators (as it is always the case when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
). Note that, in general, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
non-maximal singular subspaces exist which are contained in a unique generator. If
X
is one of them then
X^{\perp\perp}
is the unique generator containing
X
.

Let
X
be a singular subspace of
{\cal S}
and
M
a generator containing
X
. Let
\mathrm{cod}_{M}(X)
be the codimension of
X
in
M
. If
\mathrm{cod}_{M}(X)<\infty
then
\mathrm{cod}_{N}(X)=\mathrm{cod}_{M}(X)
for every generator
N
containing
X
. Indeed the star of
X
is a (possibly degenerate) polar space of finite rank equal to
\mathrm{cod}_{M}(X)
. If
\mathrm{cod}_{M}(X)=1
for some (hence every) generator
M
containing
X
then we say that
X
is a
sub-generator
of
{\cal S}
.

Let
a
and
b
be two opposite points of
{\cal S}
. Then
\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cong\mathrm{St}(c)
for every
c\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
, where
\mathrm{St}(c)
(the
star
of
c
) is the polar space with the lines and the planes of
{\cal S}
through
c
as points and lines respectively. All generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
are sub-generators of
{\cal S}
. So, if
N
and
N^{\prime}
are generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
then
M=\langle N,a\rangle
and
M^{\prime}=\langle N^{\prime},b\rangle
are generators of
{\cal S}
. Note that
M\cap M^{\prime}=N\cap N^{\prime}
. Indeed, if
c\in M\cap M^{\prime}
then
c\in\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cap N^{\perp}\cap N^{\prime\perp}=N\cap N^{\prime}
. Hence
M
and
M^{\prime}
are opposite if and only if
N
and
N^{\prime}
are opposite. Conversely, if
M
and
M^{\prime}
are opposite generators of
{\cal S}
and
a\in M
,
b\in M^{\prime}
are opposite, then
N:=M\cap b^{\perp}
and
N^{\prime}:=M^{\prime}\cap a^{\perp}
are opposite generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
.

Sets as
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
for
a
and
b
opposite points are called
hyperbolic lines
. Note that the points of a hyperbolic line
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
are mutually opposite (in particular,
\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}=\emptyset
) and if
c
and
d
are distinct points of
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
then
\{c,d\}^{\perp\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
. Note also that
\langle\{a,b\}^{\perp},c\rangle=c^{\perp}
for every point
c\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
. Accordingly,
\langle\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cup\{a,b\}\rangle
contains both
a^{\perp}
and
b^{\perp}
. However
a^{\perp}
and
b^{\perp}
are distinct maximal subspaces of
{\cal S}
. Hence
\langle\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cup\{a,b\}\rangle={\cal S}
.

2.2
Projective embeddings of point-line geometries

In the present subsection we recall some generalities and fix some terminology on projective embeddings of point-line geometries. This will be helpful in the next subsection, where projective embeddings of polar spaces will be discussed.

As in Shult
[
15
]
, a projective embedding of a point-line geometry
{\cal G}=(P,{\cal L})
, henceforth called just
embedding
of
\cal G
for short, is an injective mapping
\varepsilon:P\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
from the point-set
P
of
\cal G
to the point-set of a projective geometry
\mathbb{P}
such that
\varepsilon(P)
spans
\mathbb{P}
and
\varepsilon(\ell):=\{\varepsilon(p)\}_{p\in\ell}
is a line of
\mathbb{P}
for every line
\ell\in{\cal L}
of
\cal G
. We take the dimension of
\mathbb{P}
as the
dimension

\dim(\varepsilon)
of
\varepsilon
. Note that if
\cal G
admits skew lines then necessarily
\dim(\mathbb{P})>2
, hence
\mathbb{P}
is desarguesian. If
\mathbb{P}
is desarguesian and
\mathbb{K}
is the underlying division ring of
\mathbb{P}
then we say that
\varepsilon
is
defined over

\mathbb{K}
. If all embeddings of
\cal G
are defined over the same division ring
\mathbb{K}
we say that
\cal G
is
defined over

\mathbb{K}
.

Given two embeddings
\varepsilon:{\cal G}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}:{\cal G}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
of
\cal G
defined over the same division ring
\mathbb{K}
, a
morphism
from
\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is a morphism of projective geometries
\varphi:\mathbb{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
such that
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varphi\circ\varepsilon
. (See Faure and Frölicher
[
6
, Chaper 6]
) for morphisms of projective geometries.) Note that the condition
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varphi\circ\varepsilon
forces the morphism
\varphi:\mathbb{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
to be surjective (and, when
\cal G
is connected, it uniquely determines
\varphi
). If
\varphi
is also injective then we say that
\varphi
is an
isomorphism
from
\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and we write
\varepsilon\cong\varepsilon^{\prime}
. In general, if a morphism exists from
\varepsilon
to
\varepsilon^{\prime}
we say that
\varepsilon

covers

\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is a
quotient
of
\varepsilon
. A motivation for this terminology is the following: given a morphism
\varphi:\varepsilon\rightarrow\varepsilon^{\prime}
, let
K:=\mathrm{Ker}(\varphi)
be the kernel of
\varphi
(notation and terminology as in
[
6
]
), let
p_{K}
be the projection of
\mathbb{P}
onto the star
\mathbb{P}/K
of
K
in
\mathbb{P}
and put
\varepsilon/K:=p_{K}\circ\varepsilon
. Then
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{P}/K
and
\varepsilon/K\cong\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

Following Shult
[
15
]
, we say that an embedding is
relatively universal
if it admits no proper cover. As proved by Ronan
[
14
]
, every embedding
\varepsilon
of a geometry
\cal G
is covered by a relatively universal embedding
\tilde{\varepsilon}
of
\cal G
, uniquely determined by
\varepsilon
up to isomorphisms and characterized by the following property:
\tilde{\varepsilon}
covers all embeddings which cover
\varepsilon
. We call
\tilde{\varepsilon}
the
hull
of
\varepsilon
. Thus, an embedding is relatively universal if and only if it is its own hull.

An embedding of
\cal G
is said to be
absolutely universal
if it covers all embeddings of
\cal G
. So,
\cal G
admits the absolutely universal embedding if and only if all embeddings of
\cal G
admit the same hull; equivalently, up to isomorphism,
\cal G
admits a unique relatively universal embedding. Clearly, if
\cal G
admits the absolutely universal embedding and at least one of its embeddings is defined over a given division ring
\mathbb{K}
, then
\cal G
itself is defined over
\mathbb{K}
.

Finally, we say that an embedding is
minimal
if it admts no proper quotients. For instance, tight embeddings of polar spaces, as defined in Section
1.3
, are minimal.

2.3
Projective embeddings of polar spaces

As proved by Tits
[
17
, chapters 8 and 9]
(see also Buekenhout and Cohen
[
1
, chapters 7-11]
and Cuypers et al.
[
4
]
) all polar spaces of rank at least 3 are embeddable but for two families of polar spaces of rank 3. The non-embeddable ones are the line-grassmannians of 3-dimensional projective spaces defined over non-commutative division rings and certain polar spaces with Moufang but non-desarguesian planes, described in
[
17
, Chapter 9]
.

Let
\cal S
be an embeddable polar space and, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=2
, assume that
{\cal S}
is neither a grid of order at least
4
nor a generalized quadrangle as in Tits
[
17
, 8.6(II)(a)]
. Then
{\cal S}
admits the absolutely universal embedding as well as a unique minimal embedding (Tits
[
17
, Chapter 8]
; see also Johnson
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
and Cuypers et al.
[
5
]
). In the two excluded cases, all embeddings are 3-dimensional, hence they are both relatively universal and minimal.

In any case, all embeddings of
{\cal S}
have dimension at least
2\cdot\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})-1
(
\geq 3
as
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})\geq 2
by assumption); hence they embed
{\cal S}
in desarguesian projective spaces. When
{\cal S}
is not an infinite grid, these projective spaces are defined over the same division ring (so
{\cal S}
is defined over that ring).

Let
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}=\mathrm{PG}(V)
be an embedding of a polar space
{\cal S}
. The
\varepsilon
-image
\varepsilon({\cal S})
of
{\cal S}
is a full subgeometry of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
and there exists a unique quasi-polarity
\pi_{\varepsilon}
of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
(see Buekenhout and Cohen
[
1
, Definition 7.1.9]
for the definition of quasi-polarities) such that all points of
\varepsilon({\cal S})
are absolute for
\pi_{\varepsilon}
and for any two points
x,y\in{\cal S}
we have
x\perp y
if and only if
\varepsilon(x)\perp_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon(y)
, where
\perp_{\varepsilon}
is the orthogonality relation associated to
\pi_{\varepsilon}
(Buekenhout and Cohen
[
1
, Chapter 9]
).

More explictly, let
{\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
be the (possibly degenerate) polar space defined by
\pi_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathrm{PG}(V)
. Then
\varepsilon({\cal S})
is a subspace of
{\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
, possibly
\varepsilon({\cal S})={\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
. Note that, as
{\cal S}
is non-degenerate by assumption, if
\varepsilon({\cal S})={\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
then
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is a polarity, namely its radical is trivial. (Recall that the
radical
of a quasi-polarity
\pi
of a projective geometry
\mathbb{P}
is the subspace of
\mathbb{P}
formed by the points
p\in\mathbb{P}
such that
\pi(p)=\mathbb{P}
.)

Suppose that
\varepsilon
is relatively universal and let
\mathbb{K}
be its underlying division ring. Then one of the following occurs (Tits
[
17
, Chaper 8]
).

(1)

\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{K})\neq 2
, the polarity
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is defined by a non-degenerate alternating form and
\varepsilon({\cal S})={\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
.

(2)

The quasi-polarity
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is defined by the sesquilinearized of a non-degenerate
\sigma
-quadratic form
q:V\rightarrow\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{K}_{\sigma,1}
as defined by Tits
[
17
, Chapter 8]
for an involutory anti-automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{K}
and
\varepsilon({\cal S})
is the polar space
{\cal S}_{q}
associated to
q
. In this case, if either
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{K})\neq 2
or
\sigma
acts non-trivially on the center
Z(\mathbb{K})
of
\mathbb{K}
, then
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is a polarity and
\varepsilon({\cal S})={\cal S}_{q}={\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
.

Suppose moreover that
\varepsilon
is absolutely universal (as when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})>2
or case (1) occurs). In case (1) and in case (2) with
\pi_{\varepsilon}
a polarity
\varepsilon
is the unique embedding of
{\cal S}
. When
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is not a polarity we can factorize
\varepsilon
over any subspace
K
of the radical
R_{\varepsilon}
of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
, thus obtaining quotients
\varepsilon/K
of
\varepsilon
. In particular,
\varepsilon/R_{\varepsilon}
is the minimum embedding of
{\cal S}
.

2.3.1
Relations between
\perp
and
\perp_{\varepsilon}

Let
X
be a set of points of
{\cal S}
and
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
an embedding of
{\cal S}
. Clearly
\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp})\rangle\subseteq\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
.

Proposition 2.1

If
X^{\perp}\not\subseteq X^{\perp\perp}
then
\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp})\rangle
.

Proof.
Let
\cal X
be a projective subspace of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
and suppose that
{\cal X}\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})\not\subseteq{\cal X}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
. Then
\cal X
is spanned by
{\cal X}\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})
. When
\varepsilon
is relatively universal this claim follows from Tits
[
17
, §§8.1.6, 8.2.7]
. Otherwise,
\varepsilon
is a quotient of the absolutely universal embedding
\tilde{\varepsilon}:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})
of
{\cal S}
, say
\varepsilon=\tilde{\varepsilon}/K
for a subspace
K
of the radical
R_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}
of
\pi_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}
. Let
p_{K}
be the projection of
\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})
onto
\mathrm{PG}(V)=\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})/K
and put
\widetilde{\cal X}:=p_{K}^{-1}({\cal X})
. Then
{\cal X}\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})=p_{K}(\widetilde{\cal X}\cap\tilde{\varepsilon}({\cal S}))
. However
{\cal X}\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})\not\subseteq{\cal X}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
by assumption. Hence
\widetilde{\cal X}\cap\tilde{\varepsilon}({\cal S})\not\subseteq{\cal X}^{\perp_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}
. By Tits
[
17
, §§8.1.6, 8.2.7]
, the set
\widetilde{\cal X}\cap\tilde{\varepsilon}({\cal S})
spans
\widetilde{\cal X}
. Hence
{\cal X}\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})
spans
\cal X
.

Let now
X^{\perp}\not\subseteq X^{\perp\perp}
as in the hypotheses of the lemma and put
{\cal X}=\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
. Then
{\cal X}\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})=\varepsilon(X^{\perp})
, which is not contained in
{\cal X}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
because
X^{\perp}\not\subseteq X^{\perp\perp}
. By the previous paragraph,
{\cal X}\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})
spans
\cal X
, namely
\varepsilon(X^{\perp})
spans
\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
.
\Box

Corollary 2.2

If
X^{\perp}\not\subseteq X^{\perp\perp}\not\subseteq X^{\perp}
then

\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp\perp})\rangle=\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
.

Proof.
Let
X^{\perp}\not\subseteq X^{\perp\perp}
. Then
\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp})\rangle=\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
by Proposition
2.1
. Suppose moreover that
X^{\perp\perp}\not\subseteq X^{\perp}
. Then, since
X^{\perp}=X^{\perp\perp\perp}
, we have that
\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp\perp})\rangle=\varepsilon(X^{\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
by Lemma
2.1
with
X^{\perp}
in place of
X
. However
\varepsilon(X^{\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp})\rangle^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
, since
\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp})\rangle=\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
. Finally
\langle\varepsilon(X^{\perp\perp})\rangle=\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
, as claimed.
\Box

Corollary 2.3

We have
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle=\{\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
and
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp})\rangle=\{\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
for any two opposite points
a
and
b
of
{\cal S}
.

Proof.
We have
\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp\perp}\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}=\emptyset
. The conclusion follows from Proposition
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
.
\Box

2.3.2
Subspaces of an embeddable polar space

Given an embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
, a subspace
X
of
{\cal S}

arises
from
\varepsilon
if
X=\varepsilon^{-1}({\cal X})
for a projective subspace
\cal X
of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
; equivalently,
\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle\cap\varepsilon({\cal S})=\varepsilon(X)
.

Definition 2.4

Given a singular subspace
K
of
{\cal S}
(possibly
K=\emptyset
) let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of singular subspaces such that each of them contains
K
as a hyperplane,
X_{i}^{\perp}\cap X_{j}=K
for any choice of
i,j\in I
with
i\neq j
and
|I|>1
. Then
X:=\cup_{i\in I}X_{i}
is a subspace of
{\cal S}
, henceforth called a rosette. Clearly,
K
is the radical of
X
. In particular, if
K=\emptyset
then
X
is a set of mutually opposite points.

Proposition 2.5

Let
{\cal S}
be embeddable and let
X
be a subspace of
{\cal S}
. Then
X
arises from an embedding of
{\cal S}
, except possibly when
X
a rosette.

Proof.
When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
the above is just the main result of
[
2
]
. Suppose that
{\cal S}
has infinite rank. Then
{\cal S}
admits the universal embedding, say
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
. We shall prove that, if
X
is not a rosette, then
X
arises from
\varepsilon
.

When
X
is a singular subspace there is nothing to prove. So, suppose that
X
is neither a singular subspace nor a rosette. By contradiction, suppose that
X\subset\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle)=:X^{\prime}
. Choose a point
x_{0}\in X^{\prime}\setminus(X\cup X^{\perp})
. As
\varepsilon(x_{0})\in\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle
, there exists a finite subset
A\subseteq X
such that
\varepsilon(x_{0})\in\langle\varepsilon(A)\rangle
. As
X
is not a rosette, we can always choose
A
in such a way that
X_{0}:=\langle A\rangle
contains a pair of mutually opposite lines. Hence
X_{0}
is not a rosette.

Put
X^{\prime}_{0}:=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(X_{0})\rangle)
. Clearly,
X^{\prime}_{0}
has finite rank, since
\langle\varepsilon(X_{0})\rangle
is finite dimensional. If
X^{\prime}_{0}
is non-degenerate, then put
X_{1}=X^{\prime}_{0}
. Otherwise, let
R:=X^{\prime}_{0}\cap X_{0}^{\prime\perp}
be the radical of
X^{\prime}_{0}
. Then
R
has finite rank, since
\mathrm{rank}(X^{\prime}_{0})<\infty
. However
{\cal S}
is non-degenerate of infinite rank. An easy argument exploiting induction on
\mathrm{rank}(R)
shows that we can find a finite subset
B
of
{\cal S}
such that
\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(X_{0}\cup B)\rangle)
is non-degenerate. With
B
chosen in this way, put
X_{1}:=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(X_{0}\cup B)\rangle)
. Again,
X_{1}
has finite rank, since
\langle\varepsilon(X_{0}\cup B)\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(A\cup B)\rangle
has finite dimension (less than
|A\cup B|
). Moreover
X_{1}
is non-degenerate, thanks to the addition of
B
to
A
. By construction,
X_{1}
arises from
\varepsilon
. Therefore
\varepsilon(X_{1})
is the polar space defined on
\langle\varepsilon(X_{0}\cup B)\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(X_{1})\rangle
by the pseudoquadratic (or alternating) form induced on
\langle\varepsilon(X_{1})\rangle
by the pseudoquadratic (respectively, alternating) form which describes
\varepsilon({\cal S})
. This induced form is non-degenerate, since
X_{1}
is non-degenerate. Moreover the embedding of
X_{1}
in
\langle\varepsilon(X_{1})\rangle
, say
\varepsilon_{1}
, is universal by the above and since
\varepsilon
is universal by assumption (which means that in the characteristic 2 case
\varepsilon({\cal S})
cannot be described by an alternating form). Thus we can apply Theorem 1 of
[
2
]
to
X_{0}
as a subspace of
X_{1}
. By that theorem,
X_{0}
arises from
\varepsilon_{1}
, namely
X_{0}=\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon_{1}(X_{0})\rangle)
. However
\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon_{1}(X_{0})\rangle)=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(X_{0}\rangle)
and
x_{0}\in\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(X_{0})\rangle
by definition of
X_{0}
. In the end,
x_{0}\in X_{0}
. However
X_{0}\subseteq X
. Therefore
x_{0}\in X
. We have reached a final contradiction.
\Box

2.3.3
Optimally embeddable subspaces

Suppose that
{\cal S}
is embeddable and let
X
be a subspace of
{\cal S}
. Then
\langle\varepsilon(X),\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle\subseteq\varepsilon(X\cap X^{\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
for every embedding
\varepsilon
of
{\cal S}
. Since both
\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
and
\varepsilon(X\cap X^{\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
contain the radical
R_{\varepsilon}
of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
and all quotients of
\varepsilon
arise by factorizing
\varepsilon
over subspaces of
R_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\langle\varepsilon(X),\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle~=~\varepsilon(X\cap X^{\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}

(4)

if and only if the same holds for a quotient of
\varepsilon
. Consequently, if (
4
) holds for
\varepsilon
then it also holds for all covers and all quotients of
\varepsilon
. If
\varepsilon({\cal S})={\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
then (
4
) holds for every subspace
X
such that
\dim(\varepsilon(X))<\infty
. (This follows from the fact that the reflexive sesquilinear form
f
associated to
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is trace-valued and if
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is a polarity then
f
is non-degenerate.) In particular, if
{\cal S}
is one of the exceptional embeddable generalized quadrangles which do not admit the absolutely universal embedding then (
4
) holds for every subspace
X
and every embedding
\varepsilon
of
{\cal S}
. So, if (
4
) holds for a subspace
X
of
{\cal S}
and at last one embedding of
{\cal S}
then it holds for
X
and all embeddings of
{\cal S}
. We are now ready to state the following definition.

Definition 2.6

If
X
satisfies property (
4
) for some (equivalently, every) embedding of
{\cal S}
then we say that
X
is optimally embeddable.

Clearly, al singular subspaces of
{\cal S}
are optimally embeddable. As previously recalled, if
{\cal S}
admits an embedding
\varepsilon
such that
\varepsilon({\cal S})={\cal S}_{\pi_{\varepsilon}}
then every finitely generated subspace of
{\cal S}
is optimally embeddable. Note also that a non-degenerate subspace
X
of
{\cal S}
is optimally embeddabe if and only if
\varepsilon(X)\cup\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
spans
\mathrm{PG}(V)
for every embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
of
{\cal S}
.

Proposition 2.7

Let
Y
and
Z
be finite-dimensional opposite singular subspaces of
{\cal S}
and put
X:=\langle Y,Z\rangle
. Then
X\cap X^{\perp}=\emptyset
and
\langle\varepsilon(X),\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(V)
for every embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
of
{\cal S}
.

Proof.
Let
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
be an embedding of
{\cal S}
. The projective subspace
\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle
is the union of the lines of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
joining a point of
\varepsilon(Y)
with a point of
\varepsilon(Z)
. Let
\ell
be one of these lines, say
\ell=\langle\varepsilon(y),\varepsilon(z)\rangle
for
y\in Y
and
z\in Z
. Suppose that
\ell
meets
\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
non-trivially. Then at least one of
y
and
z
belongs to
X^{\perp}
. This contradicts the assumption that
Y
and
Z
are opposite. Therefore
\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle\cap\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\emptyset
. Accordingly,
X\cap X^{\perp}=\emptyset
.

By assumption,
\dim\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle<\infty
. We have
\langle{\cal X},{\cal X}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle=({\cal X}\cap{\cal X}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
for every finite-dimensional subspace
\cal X
of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
. Therefore

\langle\varepsilon(X),\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle~=~(\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle\cap\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}.

However
\langle\varepsilon(X)\rangle\cap\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\emptyset
, as shown in the previous paragraph. Therefore
\langle\varepsilon(X),\varepsilon(X)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(V)
, as claimed.
\Box

Lemma 2.8

Let
a
and
b
be opposite points of
{\cal S}
. If
\varepsilon
is minimal then
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cap\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\emptyset
.

Proof.
Let
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
be minimal, namely
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is a polarity. Hence
\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
has codimention 2 in
\mathrm{PG}(V)
and, consequently,
L:=\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
is a line of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
. By way of contradiction, suppose that
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cap L\neq\emptyset
. Then
L\subseteq L^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
, since
L
contains
\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)
and
p
, which are mutually distinct, each of these points is absolute for
\pi_{\varepsilon}
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}(p)
contains
\varepsilon(a)
and
\varepsilon(b)
. Consequently
\varepsilon(a)\perp_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon(b)
, namely
a\perp b
, while
a\not\perp b
by assumption.
\Box

Proposition 2.9

Let
a
and
b
be opposite points of
{\cal S}
. Then both
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
and
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
are optimally embeddable.

Proof.
As
\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp\perp}\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}=\emptyset
, we need to prove that the following holds for at least one embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
of
{\cal S}
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp}),\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle&=&\mathrm{PG}(V),\\
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}),\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle&=&\mathrm{PG}(V).\end{array}

(5)

We have
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle=\{\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
and
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp})\rangle=\{\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
by Corollary
2.3
. Moreover
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
. Hence both equations of (
5
) are equivalent to the following:

\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}},\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle~=~\mathrm{PG}(V).

(6)

We can assume that
\varepsilon
is minimal. Hence
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
is a line, as noticed in the proof of Lemma
2.8
. Therefore

\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}},\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle=(\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cap\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}.

However
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
. Hence the above equality amounts to the following:

\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}},\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle~=~(\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cap\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}.

(7)

We know that
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cap\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\emptyset
by Lemma
2.8
and, obviously,
\emptyset^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\mathrm{PG}(V)
. So, (
7
) is the same as (
6
).
\Box

3
Regularity

Throughout this section
{\cal S}
is a (possibly non-embeddable) non-degenerate thick-lined polar space of rank at least
2
.

3.1
Definitions and a proof of Theorem
1.1

Lat
a
and
b
be two opposite points of
{\cal S}
and
N,N^{\prime}
generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
. Clearly,
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}\supseteq\langle N\cap N^{\prime},\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
.

Definition 3.1

We say that two generators
N,N^{\prime}
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
form a
\perp
-minimal pair if
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\langle N\cap N^{\prime},\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
. A generator
N
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
will be said to be
\perp
-minimal if
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal for every generator
N^{\prime}
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
(in particular,
N^{\perp}=\langle N,\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
). We say that
\{a,b\}
is regular if all generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
are
\perp
-minimal. If all pairs of opposite points of
{\cal S}
are regular, then
{\cal S}
is said to be regular.

Lemma 3.2

We have
\langle X,\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle=\cup_{x\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}}\langle X,x\rangle
for every singular subspace
X
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
.

Proof.
Let
x,y
be distinct points of
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
. Then
x\not\perp y
. Consequently, no point of
\langle X,x\rangle\setminus X
can be collinear with a point of
\langle X,y\rangle\setminus X
. The conclusion follows from this remark.
\Box

In particular, if
N
is a generator of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
then
\langle N,\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
is the union of the generators of
{\cal S}
which contain
N
and meet
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
non-trivally. Accordingly,
\{N,N\}
is
\perp
-minimal if and only if all generators of
{\cal S}
containing
N
meet
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
non-trivially.

Theorem 3.3

Let
N
be a generator of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
. If the pair
\{N,N\}
is
\perp
-minimal then
N
is
\perp
-minimal.

Proof.
Let
\{N,N\}
be
\perp
-minimal, namely all generators of
{\cal S}
containing
N
can be obtained as
\langle N,c\rangle
for a point
c\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
(Lemma
3.2
). Given another generator
N^{\prime}
of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
, let
x\in\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
and suppose that
x\not\in N\cap N^{\prime}
. Therefore
x\not\in N
(otherwise
\langle x,N^{\prime}\rangle
is a singular subspace contained in
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
and properly containing
N^{\prime}
, a contradiction with
N^{\prime}
being a generator of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
). Accordingly,
M:=\langle N,x\rangle
is a generator of
{\cal S}
. As
\{N,N\}
is
\perp
-minimal,
M
contains a point
c\in\{a,b\}^{\perp}
. If
x=c
then
x\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\subseteq\langle N\cap N^{\prime},\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
. Otherwise, the line
\langle x,c\rangle
meets
N
in a point, say
y
. We have
\langle x,c\rangle\subseteq N^{\prime\perp}
, since both
x
and

belong to
N^{\prime\perp}
. However,
N\cap N^{\prime\perp}=N\cap N^{\prime}
. Therefore
y\in N\cap N^{\prime}
. Hence

.

We have proved that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}\subseteq\langle N\cap N^{\prime},\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
. Hence
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\langle N\cap N^{\prime},\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
since
(N\cap N^{\prime})\cup\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\subseteq\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
. So,
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal. As
N^{\prime}
is an arbitrary generator of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
,
N
is
\perp
-minimal.
\Box

Theorem
1.1
immediately follows from Theorem
3.3
. Indeed in property
({\mathrm{R}}3)
it is assumed that every pair of generators of

is
\perp
-minimal while
({\mathrm{R}}2)
is equivalent to
\{N,N\}
being
\perp
-minimal for every generator

of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
. Trivially,

implies
({\mathrm{R}}2)
. Conversely, by Theorem

, property
({\mathrm{R}}2)
implies
({\mathrm{R}}3)
.

Corollary 3.4

Assume that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
and let

be a generator of

. If there exists a generator

of

is
\perp
-minimal.

Proof.
In view of Theorem

, we only need to prove that, if a generator

{\cal S}
which contain

-minimal.
\Box

3.2
An improvement of Definition
3.1

Our definition of

of opposite points, but we have dropped the reference to

in our terminology presuming that this is implicit in choosing

and

among the generators of

Theorem 3.6

Let
N
and

, except possibly when

is non-embeddable of rank

,

and the stabilizer in

Proof.
Suppose firstly that
{\cal S}
is embeddable and let

. We can assume to have chosen

. Moreover, by Lemma

the subpspace

is the union of the subspaces
\langle N\cap N^{\prime},x\rangle
for

, A similar description holds for

Suppose that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal with respect to
\{a,b\}
, namely:

\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}~=~\langle N\cap N^{\prime},\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle.

(8)

By assumption,

and

. Therefore, by (

When

is regular

[

. Then

and

are points, say

When either

with the following.

Definition 3.7

Let
N
and
N^{\prime}
be sub-generators of
{\cal S}
, possibly
N=N^{\prime}
. We say that
N
and
N^{\prime}
form a
\perp
-minimal pair if either

is a singular subspace or
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\langle N\cap N^{\prime},\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
for every (equivalently, at least one) choice of opposite points

. A sub-generator
N
is said to be
\perp
-minimal if
\{N,N\}
is
\perp
-minimal, namely either

is a generator of
{\cal S}
or

of opposite points of

. A pair
\{a,b\}
of opposite points is regular if all generators of

are
\perp
-minimal. The polar space
{\cal S}
is regular if all of its sub-generators are
\perp
-minimal (equivalently, all pairs of opposite points of

when defining

and

are opposite sub-generators, then not two distinct points of

are colliear. Hence

such that

.

Remark 4

When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=2
, if

and

are opposite points and

and

are distinct (hence opposite) points of

Corollary 3.9

is regular if and only if it admits a tight embedding.

Proof.
Let
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
. Then the subspace spanned by two opposite sub-generators is optimally embeddable by Proposition
2.7
. Accordingly, we can drop the condition that
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
is optimally embeddable from
({\mathrm{R}}4)
of Theorem
3.8
. However, if we remove that condition then, in view of Corollary
3.5
, what remains of
({\mathrm{R}}4)
amounts to say that
{\cal S}
is regular.
\Box

Lemma 3.10

Suppose that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})\geq 3
and
{\cal S}
admits a unique embedding. Let
N
and
N^{\prime}
be opposite sub-generators of
{\cal S}
such that the pair
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal and
|\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}|>1
. Then
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
is optimally embeddable if and only if
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle=\{a,b\}^{\perp}
for every choice of distinct (hence opposite) points
a,b\in\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
.

Proof.
The ‘if’ part of the statement immediately follows from the fact that
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
is optimally embeddable for every choice of opposite points
a
and
b
(Proposition
2.9
). Note that the hypotheses that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})>2
and
{\cal S}
admits a unique embedding play no role in this implication. Turning to the ‘only if’ part, let
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
be the (unique) embedding of
{\cal S}
and suppose that
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
is optimally embeddable. Then

\langle\varepsilon(\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle),\varepsilon(N\cup N^{\prime})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle~=~\varepsilon(\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle\cap\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}.

Hence, given two opposite points
a,b\in\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
(which exist because
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
is non-singular by assumption), we have

\langle\varepsilon(\langle N\cup N^{\prime}\rangle),\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}\rangle~=~\mathrm{PG}(V)

(13)

because
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
by assumption,
\{a,b\}^{\perp}\cap\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}=\emptyset
and
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp})\rangle=\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
by Corollary
2.3
.

Recall that
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cup\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
spans
\mathrm{PG}(V)
. The embedding
\varepsilon
is both absolutely universal and minimal, since by assumption
\varepsilon
is the unique embedding of
{\cal S}
. As
\varepsilon
is minimal,

\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
and
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
are disjoint by Lemma
2.8
. So,
\mathrm{PG}(V)
is the direct sum of
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
and
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}
. Moreover
\varepsilon(\{a,b\})^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle
by Corollary
2.3
and
\langle\varepsilon(\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle)\rangle\subseteq\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle
, since
N\cup N^{\prime}\subseteq\{a,b\}^{\perp}
. By comparing all this information with equation (
13
) we see that
\langle\varepsilon(\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle)\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle
.

So far we have made no use of the hypothesis that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})>2
. We shall use it now. Since
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})>2
, the subspace
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
is not a rosette. Therefore it arises from an embedding of
{\cal S}
, by Proposition
2.5
. However
\varepsilon
is the unique embedding of
{\cal S}
. Hence
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
arises from
\varepsilon
, namely
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle)\rangle)
. Similarly,
\{a,b\}^{\perp}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle)
. The equality
\langle\varepsilon(\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle)\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle
now forces
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle=\{a,b\}^{\perp}
.
\Box

Theorem 3.11

Suppose that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})\geq 3
and
{\cal S}
admits a unique embedding. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

the unique embedding of
{\cal S}
is tight;

(2)

{\cal S}
admits admits a pair opposite sub-generators
N
and
N^{\prime}
such that
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle=\{a,b\}^{\perp}
for two opposite points
a
and
b
of
{\cal S}
;

(3)

{\cal S}
admits two opposite generators
M
and
M^{\prime}
such that
\langle M,M^{\prime}\rangle={\cal S}
.

Proof.
Trivially (3) implies (1) while (1) implies (2) by Theorem
3.8
and Lemma
3.10
. It remains to show that (2) implies (3). Given
N,N^{\prime},a
and
b
as in (2), let
M=\langle N,a\rangle
and
M^{\prime}=\langle N^{\prime},b\rangle
. Then
M
and
M^{\prime}
are opposite generators of
{\cal S}
. Moreover
\langle M,M^{\prime}\rangle=\langle N,N^{\prime},a,b\rangle=\langle\{a,b\}^{\perp},a,b\rangle
, since
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle=\{a,b\}^{\perp}
. However
\langle\{a,b\}^{\perp},a,b\rangle={\cal S}
. Hence
\langle M,M^{\prime}\rangle={\cal S}
.
\Box

Remark 5

In Lemma
3.10
and Theorem
3.11
we cannot drop the hypothesis that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})>2
. Indeed the sub-generators of a generalized quadrangle are its points. Hence, with
N,N^{\prime},a
and
b
as in the hypotheses of Lemma
3.10
, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=2
we have
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle=\{a,b\}^{\perp}
only if
{\cal S}
is a grid. When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=2
we should replace the equality
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle=\{a,b\}^{\perp}
with
\langle\varepsilon(\langle N,N\rangle)\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle
, for a minimal embedding
\varepsilon
of
{\cal S}
, but this condition amounts to
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
being optimally embeddable.

The hypothesis that
{\cal S}
admits a unique embedding is also necessary, both in Lemma
3.10
and in Theorem
3.11
. Indeed suppose that
{\cal S}
admits a tight embedding
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
and let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be opposite generators of
{\cal S}
such that
\langle\varepsilon(M),\varepsilon(M^{\prime})\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(V)
. If
\varepsilon
, which is minimal, is not universal, let
\tilde{\varepsilon}:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})
be a proper cover of

. Then
\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}(M),\tilde{\varepsilon}(M^{\prime})\rangle\subset\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})
, hence
\langle M,M^{\prime}\rangle\subset{\cal S}
; similarly,
\langle\ X,Y\rangle\subset\{a,b\}^{\perp}
for any two singular subspaces

, even if
X
and

are such that
\langle\varepsilon(\langle X,Y\rangle)\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp})\rangle
.

Remark 6

When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
, if
{\cal S}=\langle M,M^{\prime}\rangle
for two (necessarily opposite) generators
M
and
M^{\prime}
then
{\cal S}=\langle X,X^{\prime}\rangle
for any two opposite generators
X
and
X^{\prime}
. In general, as we shall see in Section

, when the rank of

is infinite

can admit pairs of generators
\{X,X^{\prime}\}
such that
\langle X,X^{\prime}\rangle={\cal S}
as well as opposite generators
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
such that
\langle Y,Y^{\prime}\rangle\subset{\cal S}
.

3.4
Complementary generators and regularity

In this subsection
{\cal S}
is supposed to admit the absolutely universal embedding. Hence it also admits the minimum embedding. Throughout this subsection
\varepsilon:{\cal S}:\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
is the minimum embedding of
{\cal S}
.

The following definitions, which complete previously stated definitions, will help us to make our exposition more concise. Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be opposite generators of
{\cal S}
. If
\langle\varepsilon(M),\varepsilon(M^{\prime})\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(V)
then we say that
M
and
M^{\prime}
are
complementary
, also that each of them is a
complement
of the other one. Obviously,
{\cal S}
admits a pair of complementary generators if and only if
\varepsilon
is tight.

A pair
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
of sub-generators of
{\cal S}
is said to be
optimally embeddable
if
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
is optimally embeddable (Definition
2.6
); it is said to be
\perp
-
degenerate
if
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
is a singular subspace. Note that, according to Definition
3.7
, all
\perp
-degenerate pairs of opposite sub-generators are
\perp
-minimal. Note also that, if
N
and
N^{\prime}
are opposite sub-generators, then
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-degenerate precisely when
|\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}|\leq 1
.

Lemma 3.12

No optimally embeddable pair of opposite sub-generators is
\perp
-degenerate.

Proof.
Let
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
be an optimally embeddable pair of opposite sub-generators of
{\cal S}
. By way of contradiction, let
|\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}|\leq 1
. Suppose first that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\{c\}
for a point
c
. Let
\tilde{\varepsilon}:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})
be the universal embedding of
{\cal S}
. Since
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is optimally embeddable, we have
\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}(N),\tilde{\varepsilon}(N^{\prime}),\{\tilde{\varepsilon}(N),\tilde{\varepsilon}(N^{\prime})\}^{\perp_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})
. However,
\tilde{\varepsilon}(c)
is the unique singular point of
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}(N),\tilde{\varepsilon}(N^{\prime})\}^{\perp_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}
. Then
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}(N),\tilde{\varepsilon}(N^{\prime})\}^{\perp_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}\subseteq\tilde{\varepsilon}(c)^{\perp_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}
. This forces
\tilde{\varepsilon}(c)^{\perp_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}=PG(\widetilde{V})
, hence
c^{\perp}={\cal S}
. We have reached a contradiction.

Therefore
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\emptyset
. Hence
\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}(N),\tilde{\varepsilon}(N^{\prime})\rangle=\mathrm{PG}(\widetilde{V})
, since
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is optimally embedded by assumption. Consequently, if
M
is a generator of
{\cal S}
contaning
N
, then
\tilde{\varepsilon}(M)
meets
\tilde{\varepsilon}(N^{\prime})
non-trivially. Equivalently
M
meets
N^{\prime}
non-trivially. This contradicts the assumption that
N
and
N^{\prime}
are opposite.
\Box

Lemma 3.13

Let
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
be an optimally embeddable pair of opposite sub-generators of
{\cal S}
and suppose that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal. Then both
N
and
N^{\prime}
are
\perp
-minimal.

Proof.
By Lemma
3.12
,
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}
contains at least two distinct (hence opposite) points
a
and
b
. So,
N
and
N^{\prime}
are opposite generators of
\{a,b\}^{\perp}
and
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
since
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal.

By way of contradiction, suppose that
N
is not
\perp
-minimal. So,
N^{\perp}
contains a point
c\not\in N\cup\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
. However
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
by assumption. Moreover
\{\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\}^{\perp_{\varepsilon}\perp_{\varepsilon}}=\langle\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\rangle
since
\varepsilon
is minimal and
\mathrm{PG}(V)=\langle\varepsilon(N),\varepsilon(N^{\prime}),\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\rangle
, since
\langle N,N^{\prime}\rangle
is optimally embeddable. It follows that the projective plane
P=\langle\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b),\varepsilon(c)\rangle
meets
\langle\varepsilon(N),\varepsilon(N^{\prime})\rangle
in a point, say
p
. However
P\subseteq\varepsilon(N)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}
. Hence
p\in X:=\varepsilon(N)^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cap\langle\varepsilon(N),\varepsilon(N^{\prime})\rangle
. However
p\not\in\varepsilon(N)
. Therefore
X
properly contains
\varepsilon(N)
. Consequently,
X\cap\varepsilon(N^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. So,
\varepsilon(N^{\prime})
meets
X
non-trivially. Equivalently,
N^{\prime}
meets
N^{\perp}
non-trivially. This contradicts the hypothesis that
N
and
N^{\prime}
are opposite.
\Box

In the finite rank case the next lemma is absolutely trivial and its hypotheses are redundant. However
\mathrm{rank}(S)=\infty
is allowed here. We recall that, so far, nobody has been able to prove that in every polar space of infinite rank every generator admits an opposite, although no counterexample is known which refutes this conjecture. So, the hypothesis that
M
admits an opposite cannot be dropped from the next lemma and a proof is required for the conclusions.

Lemma 3.14

Let
M
be a generator of
{\cal S}
and
p
a point exterior to
M
. If
M
is opposite to at least one generators of
{\cal S}
then there exists a generator of
{\cal S}
opposite to
M
and containing
p
. If moreover
M
admits a complement, then
M
also admits a complement which contains
p
.

Proof.
We firstly prove that if
M
admits an opposite generator then a generator
M^{\prime}
also exists which is opposite to
M
and contains
p
.

Let
M_{1}
be a generator of
{\cal S}
opposite to
M
. If
M_{1}
contains
p
then there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, let
M_{2}:=\langle p^{\perp}\cap M_{1},p\rangle
. If
M_{2}\cap M=\emptyset
then again we are done:
M^{\prime}=M_{2}
does the job. Assume that
M_{2}\cap M\neq\emptyset
. As
M_{2}
contains a hyperplane
p^{\perp}\cap M_{1}
which is disjoint from
M
, necessarily
M_{2}\cap M=\{a\}
for a point
a
. Clearly,
a\neq p
. Put
N:=M\cap p^{\perp}
. As both
p
and
a
belong to
N^{\perp}
, the line
\langle p,a\rangle
is contained in
N^{\perp}
. Therefore this line meets
N
in a point, since
N
is a sub-generator. It follows that
a\in N
. Now,
p^{\perp}\not\subseteq a^{\perp}
(indeed
{\cal S}
is non-degenerate and
p^{\perp}\neq a^{\perp}
since
p\neq a
). Hence there exists a point
b\in p^{\perp}\setminus a^{\perp}
. Put
M_{3}:=\langle b,b^{\perp}\cap M_{2}\rangle
. The generator
M_{3}
contains
p
.

We claim that
M_{3}\cap M=\emptyset
. Indeed, suppose the contrary and let
c\in M_{3}\cap M
. Then
c\neq b
, as
b\not\perp a
while
c\perp a
. The line
\langle b,c\rangle
meets
b^{\perp}\cap M_{2}
in a point
d
. If
c\neq d
then
b\in\langle c,d\rangle
. However,
\langle c,d\rangle\subseteq a^{\perp}
, since
c\perp a
(because
c,a\in M
) and
d\perp a
(because
d,a\in M_{2})
. It follows that
b\perp a
. This conclusion contradicts the choice of
b
. Therefore
d=c
. However
d\in b^{\perp}\cap M_{2}
, which is disjont from
M
since
b\not\perp a
and
a
is the unique point of
M_{2}\cap M
, while
c\in M
. Again a contradiction. Hence
M_{3}\cap M=\emptyset
and
M^{\prime}=M_{3}
is the required generator opposite to
M
and containing
p
.

Turning to the second claim of the lemma, suppose that
M_{1}
is a complement of
M
. If
p\in M_{1}
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, let
M_{2}
be constructed as above. If
M_{2}\cap M=\emptyset
then
M_{2}
is the required complement. Otherwise
\varepsilon(M)\cup\varepsilon(M_{2})
spans a hyperplane of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
. In this case the generator
M_{3}
constructed as above is a complement of
M
.
\Box

Theorem 3.15

The following are equivalent:

(1)

{\cal S}
is regular and, for every sub-generator
N
of
{\cal S}
, if
N^{\perp}
is not a generator of
{\cal S}
then a sub-generator
N^{\prime}
of
{\cal S}
exists opposite to
N
and such that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is optimally embeddable.

(2)

Every generator of
{\cal S}
admits a complement.

Proof.
Assume (1). Let
M
be a generator of
{\cal S}
and, given a point
a\in M
, let
b
be a point opposite to
a
. Put
N=b^{\perp}\cap M
. Then
a,b\in N^{\perp}
, hence
N^{\perp}
is not a generator. By (1), there exists a sub-generator
N^{\prime}
such that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is optimally embeddable, hence it is not
\perp
-degenerate by Lemma
3.12
. However,
{\cal S}
is regular by (1). Hence
N^{\perp}=\langle N,\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
and, since
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is not
\perp
-degenerate,
N^{\prime}\subset\{c,d\}^{\perp}
for two opposite points
c,d\in N^{\perp}
. Moreover,
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal, namely
\{N,N^{\prime}\}^{\perp}=\{c,d\}^{\perp\perp}
, because
{\cal S}
is regular. The hyperbolic line
\{c,d\}^{\perp\perp}
meets every generator
\langle N,x\rangle
with
x\in\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
. So, we can assume that
c\in\langle N,a\rangle
and
d\in\langle N,b\rangle
. Accordingly, we can safely replace
a
with
c
and
b
with
d
. In other words, we can assume that
a=c
and
b=d
. Put
M^{\prime}:=\langle N^{\prime},b\rangle
. Then
M
and
M^{\prime}
are opposite. Recalling that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is perp-minimal and optimally embeddable, we obtain that
\varepsilon(M)\cup\varepsilon(M^{\prime})
spans
\mathrm{PG}(V)
as in the proof of Theorem
3.8
. So,
M^{\prime}
is a complement of
M
.

Conversely, assume (2). We firstly prove that every sub-generator of
{\cal S}
is
\perp
-minimal. Let
N
be a sub-generator of
{\cal S}
. If
N^{\perp}
is a generator then
N
is
\perp
-minimal (Definition
3.7
). Suppose that
N
contains at least two distinct points, say
a
and
b
. Clearly,
a\not\perp b
. Put
M:=\langle N,a\rangle
. In view of (2),
M
admits a complement. By Lemma
3.14
, there exists a complement
M^{\prime}
of
M
which contains
b
. Put
N^{\prime}:=a^{\perp}\cap M^{\prime}
. As in the proof of Theorem
3.8
, we see that
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is
\perp
-minimal and optimally embeddable. By Lemma
3.13
, the sub-generator
N
is
\perp
-minimal. So we have proved that every sub-generator of
{\cal S}
is
\perp
-minimal. Namely,
{\cal S}
is regular. Morover,
\{N,N^{\prime}\}
is optimally embeddable, as required in (1).
\Box

All polar spaces constructed in Sections
5.1
,
5.2
and
5.3
are regular and, in view of Proposition
5.3
and its analogues for the hyperbolic and hermitian case, they satisfy condition (2) (hence (1) too) of Theorem
3.15
.

Remark 7

When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
, the existence of a pair of complementary generators (namely the minimal embedding being tight) is not sufficient for
{\cal S}
to be regular and, conversely,
{\cal S}
being regular does not imply that all opposite generators are complementary. For instance, the quadric
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
described in Section

is not regular but it admits a pair of complementary generators, namely

and
[V^{\prime}]
. Conversely, the polar spaces described in Sections

,
5.2
and
5.3
are regular but each of them admits pairs of opposite generators which are not complementary. For instance, in each of these spaces, the generators called

and
[V^{*}]
are complementary. Generators also exist which are opposite to both
[V]
and
[V^{*}]
. Each of them is a complement of
[V^{*}]
but not of
[V]
. (See also Lemma
4.13
.)

4
The three generators game

Throughout this section
{\cal S}
is assumed to be embeddable. For ease of exposition, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=2
we assume that
{\cal S}
is neither a grid of order
s>3
nor a generalized quadrangle as in
[
17
, 8.6(II)(a)]
. So,
{\cal S}
admits both the absolutely universal embedding and a unique minimum embedding.

We shall deal with triples of mutually opposite generators. Recall that a polar space
{\cal S}
of finite rank admits triples of opposite generators if and only if either
{\cal S}
is thick or
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
is even. We cannot hope for such a sharp picture for polar spaces of infinite rank, all the more that we do not even know if every generator of a polar space of infinite rank admits an opposite. However polar spaces of infinite rank exist which admit triples of mutually opposite generators. For instance, in each of the spaces described in Sections
5.1
,
5.2
and
5.3
, the generators
[V]
and
[V^{*}]
are opposite and generators exist which are opposite to both
[V]
and
[V^{*}]
.
So, the theory we are going to develope in this section is not vacuous in the infinite rank case.

4.1
More on opposite generators

Let
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
be the minimum embedding of
{\cal S}
. Given two opposite generators
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
of
{\cal S}
, put
{\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}:=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle
. Clearly,
{\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}\supseteq\langle M_{1},M_{2}\rangle
. We have
{\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}=\langle M_{1},M_{2}\rangle
if and only if
\varepsilon
is the unique embedding of
{\cal S}
.

Lemma 4.1

The following holds for
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
: all hyperplanes of
M_{i}
are
\perp
-minimal as sub-generators of
{\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}
and, if
N
is a hyperplane of
M_{i}
such that
N^{\perp}\cap{\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}\neq M_{i}
, then
N^{\perp}\cap M_{j}=\{x_{j}\}
for a point
x_{j}\in M_{j}
and
N^{\perp}\cap{\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}=\langle M_{i},x_{j}\rangle=\langle N,x_{i},x_{j}\rangle
for any
x_{i}\in M_{i}\setminus N
.

Proof.
Put
{\cal S}^{\prime}:={\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}
for short and, denoted by
V^{\prime}
the subspace of
V
corresponding to
\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle
, let
\varepsilon^{\prime}:{\cal S}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V^{\prime})=\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle
be the embedding of
{\cal S}^{\prime}
induced by
\varepsilon
. The embedding
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is minimal and
\pi_{\varepsilon}
induces
\pi_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
on
\mathrm{PG}(V^{\prime})
. Explicitly,
X^{\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}=X^{\perp_{\varepsilon}}\cap\mathrm{PG}(V^{\prime})
for every subset
X
of
\mathrm{PG}(V^{\prime})
.

Let
N
be a hyperplane of
M_{1}
such that
M_{1}\subset N^{\perp}\cap{\cal S}^{\prime}
. Then
\varepsilon(N)^{\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}
properly contains
\varepsilon(M_{1})
. Hence it meets
\varepsilon(M_{2})
non-trivially. The intersection
\varepsilon(N)^{\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\cap\varepsilon(M_{2})
cannot contain a line, otherwise

contains a line and
\langle N,N^{\perp}\cap M_{2}\rangle
is a singular subspace containg

as a subspace of codimension at least

, contradcting the fact that

is a sub-generator. Therefore
\varepsilon(N)^{\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\cap\varepsilon(M_{2})=\{\varepsilon(x_{2})\}
for a point
x_{2}\in M_{2}
and
\varepsilon(N)
has codimension 2 in
\varepsilon(N)^{\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}
. This implies that
\varepsilon(N)^{\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}=\langle\varepsilon(N),\varepsilon(x_{1}),\varepsilon(x_{2})\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(N),\{\varepsilon(x_{1}),\varepsilon(x_{2})\}^{\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\perp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\rangle
for

. Accordingly,

.
\Box

Corollary 4.2

The generators
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are complementary if and only if the following holds for

:

(\ast)

all hyerplanes of

are
\perp
-minimal as sub-generators of
{\cal S}
and, if

such that

Proof.
The ‘only if’ part is Lemma

(see also the final part of the proof of that lemma). Turning to the ‘if’ part, assume

4.2
Deep and hyperbolic sub-generators

Let
X
be a sub-sub-generator of
{\cal S}
, namely a singular subspace of
{\cal S}
of codimension 2 in at least one (hence all) of the generators of
{\cal S}
which contain it. The star
\mathrm{St}(X)
of
X
is either a line (if
X
is contained in just one generator) or a pencil of lines (if just one of the sub-generators containing
X
is contained in at least two generators) or a non-degenerate generalized quadrangle (if none of the sub-generators containing
X
is contained in just one generator).

Lemma 4.3

If a generator
M
of
{\cal S}
contains a sub-generator which is contained in just two generators, then all sub-generators contained in
M
are contained in at most two generators.

Proof.
By way of contradiction, suppose that
M
contains two sub-generators
N
and
N^{\prime}
with
N
contained in just two generators and
N^{\prime}
in at least three generators. Put
X=N\cap N^{\prime}
. Then
\mathrm{St}(X)
is a non-degenerate thick-lined generalized quadrangle. However, the point of
\mathrm{St}(X)
corresponding to
N
belongs to just two lines of
\mathrm{St}(X)
while the point corresponding to
N^{\prime}
belongs to at least three lines. This is impossible in any thick-lined generalized quadrangle.
\Box

Definition 4.4

Let
N
be a sub-generator of
{\cal S}
. We say that
N
is deep if
N^{\perp}
is a generator, namely
N
is contained in just one generator; in other words,
\{N,N\}
is
\perp
-degenerate. If
N
is contained in exactly two generators then we say that
N
is hyperbolic. So, by Lemma
4.3
, if a generator contains a hyperbolic sub-generator then it is hyperbolic (Definition
1.9
). If all non-deep sub-generators of
{\cal S}
are hyperbolic (equivalently, all of its generators are hyperbolic) then we say that
{\cal S}
is hyperbolic.

Obviously, hyperbolic sub-generators are
\perp
-minimal. If
{\cal S}
admits a hyperbolic sub-generator, then at least one of the hyperbolic lines of
{\cal S}
has size 2. Hence all of them have size 2, since
\mathrm{Aut}({\cal S})
is transitive on the set of pairs of opposite points of
{\cal S}
. So, if
{\cal S}
is hyperbolic then it is regular.

If
M
is a generator and
p
a point exterior to
M
then
p^{\perp}\cap M
is a non-deep sub-generator. So, every generator contains non-deep sub-generators. Accordingly, a generator is non-hyperbolic if and only if all of its non-deep hyperplanes are non-hyperbolic.

We recall that if
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})<\infty
then
{\cal S}
admits no deep sub-generators. In this case either
{\cal S}
is hyperbolic or none of its sub-generators is hyperbolic (hence
{\cal S}
is thick). In contrast, in general, when
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
deep sub-generators exist in
{\cal S}
. So, in general, a generator of
{\cal S}
contains both deep and non-deep sub-generators. Moreover, polar spaces of infinite rank also exist where some but not all of the non-deep sub-generators are hyperbolic. The quadrics discussed in Section
5.4
have this property.

Remark 9

When
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
the stabilizer in
\mathrm{Aut}({\cal S})
of a generator
M
of
{\cal S}
acts intransitively on the set of hyperplanes of
M
. Indeed
M
is an infinite-dimensional projective space and the automorphism group of an infinite-dimensional projective space is never transitive on the set of hyperplanes of that space. In view of this fact, the existence of generators containing both deep and non-deep hyperplanes is not so surprising.

The next theorem is an anlogue of Lemma

{\cal S}
exists which is opposite to both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Suppose moreover that neither
M_{1}
nor
M_{2}
is hyperbolic. Then
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
satisfies the three-generator property if and only if
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are complementary.

Proof.
Lemma
4.9
provides the ‘if’ part of the theorem. Put
{\cal S}^{\prime}:={\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}
. By definition,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are complementary if and only if
{\cal S}^{\prime}={\cal S}
. We shall prove that if
{\cal S}^{\prime}\subset{\cal S}
then
(3\mathrm{G})
fails to hold for
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
, thus proving also the ‘only if’ part.

Suppose that
{\cal S}^{\prime}\subset{\cal S}
. Pick a point
a\in{\cal S}\setminus{\cal S}^{\prime}
and let
N:=a^{\perp}\cap M_{1}
. Suppose that
N^{\perp}\cap M_{2}\neq\emptyset
, say
N^{\perp}\cap M_{2}=\{b\}
. As
{\cal S}
is regular,
N
is
\perp
-minimal. Hence
N^{\perp}=\langle N,\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\rangle
and
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}
meets
M_{1}
in a point
c
. Recall now that the embedding
\varepsilon
of
{\cal S}
which we use to define the subspace
{\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle_{V})
is the minimum one. Therefore
\langle\varepsilon(\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp})\rangle=\langle\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\rangle
. It follows that the line
L:=\langle\varepsilon(a),\varepsilon(b)\rangle
meets
\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle
in two distinct points, namely
\varepsilon(b)
and
\varepsilon(c)
. Hence
L\subseteq\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle
, namely
\{a,b\}^{\perp\perp}\subseteq{\cal S}^{\prime}={\cal S}_{M_{1},M_{2}}
. So,
a\in{\cal S}^{\prime}
. This contradicts the choice of
a
.

Therefore
N^{\perp}\cap M_{2}=\emptyset
. Consequently, if
M
is a generator of
{\cal S}
containing
a
and opposite both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
(which exists by Theorem
4.5
), then
\pi_{1,2}^{M}(a)=M
. Property
(3\mathrm{G})
fails to hold for
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
.
\Box

Theorems
4.11
and
4.12
show that, under suitable hypotheses on
{\cal S}
, the three generators property boils down to forming a complementary pair. We shall see now that, given three mutually opposite generators, if they have not the same dimension (hence
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
) then at least one of the three pairs we can form with them is not a complementary pair.

Lemma 4.13

Let
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
be a complementary pair of generators of
{\cal S}
with
\dim(M_{1})\geq\dim(M_{2})
. Then
\dim(M_{3})\leq\dim(M_{2})
for every generator
M_{3}
of
{\cal S}
opposite to both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. If moreover
M_{3}
is a complemet of
M_{1}
then
\dim(M_{3})=\dim(M_{2})
.

Proof.
Suppose that
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
(otherwise there is nothing to prove). So, the hupothesis that
M_{2}
is a complement of
M_{1}
combined with the hypothesis that
\dim(M_{1})\geq\dim(M_{2})
imply that
\dim(M_{1})=\dim(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
, with
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
the minimum embedding of
{\cal S}
. Hence
\dim(M_{3})\leq\dim(M_{1})
. Therefore, if
\dim(M_{2})=\dim(M_{1})
then
\dim(M_{3})\leq\dim(M_{2})
.

Let now
\dim(M_{2})<\dim(M_{1})
and, for a contradiction, suppose that
\dim(M_{3})>\dim(M_{2})
. Recall
\mathrm{PG}(V)=\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle
since
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are complementary. Let
p^{M_{1}}_{M_{2}}:\mathrm{PG}(V)\rightarrow\varepsilon(M_{2})
be the projection of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
onto
\varepsilon(M_{2})
with
\varepsilon(M_{1})
as the kernel. As
\dim(M_{3})>\dim(M_{2})
,
p^{M_{1}}_{M_{2}}
induces a non-injective mapping on
\varepsilon(M_{3})
. Hence there exist points
z\in M_{2}
and
x,y\in M_{1}
such that
x\neq y
and both lines
\ell_{x}:=\langle\varepsilon(z),\varepsilon(x)\rangle
and
\ell_{y}:=\langle\varepsilon(z),\varepsilon(y)\rangle
meet
\varepsilon(M_{3})
non-trivially, say
\ell_{x}\cap\varepsilon(M_{3})=\{p_{x}\}
and
\ell_{y}\cap\varepsilon(M_{3})=p_{y}
. Clearly
p_{x}\neq p_{y}
, since otherwise
p_{x}=p_{y}=\varepsilon(z)
, which is impossible since
M_{3}\cap M_{2}=\emptyset
. Therefore
\ell:=\langle p_{x},p_{y}\rangle
is a line and
\ell
meets
\langle\varepsilon(x),\varepsilon(y)\rangle
in a point, say
p
. This point belongs to both
\varepsilon(M_{3})
and
\varepsilon(M_{1})
. This conclusion contradict the hypothesis that
M_{3}
is opposite to
M_{1}
. Therefore
\dim(M_{3})\leq\dim(M_{2})
.

When
M_{3}
is also a complement of
M_{1}
we can permute
M_{2}
and
M_{3}
in the previous paragraph, thus obtaining that
\dim(M_{2})\leq\dim(M_{3})
. In this case
\dim(M_{3})=\dim(M_{2})
.
\Box

Corollary 4.14

Let
M_{1},M_{2}
and
M_{3}
be three mutually opposite generators of
{\cal S}
and suppose that they have not the same dimension. Then at least one of
\{M_{1},M_{2}\},\{M_{1},M_{3}\}
or
\{M_{2},M_{3}\}
is not a complementary pair.

Proof.
Since
M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
have not the same dimension, necessarily
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})=\infty
. To fix ideas, suppose that both
M_{2}
and
M_{3}
are complements of
M_{1}
. Suppose firstly that
\dim(M_{1})\geq\dim(M_{2})
. Then
\dim(M_{2})=\dim(M_{3})
by Lemma
4.13
. By assumption,
M_{1},M_{2}
and
M_{3}
have not the same dimension. Hence
\dim(M_{1})>\dim(M_{2})=\dim(M_{3})
. It follows that
\dim(M_{1})=\dim(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{rank}({\cal S})
, where
\varepsilon:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(V)
is the minimum embedding of
{\cal S}
. Hence
\dim\langle\varepsilon(M_{2}),\varepsilon(M_{3})\rangle=\dim(M_{2})=\dim(M_{3})<\dim(M_{1})=\dim(\varepsilon)
. So,
\langle\varepsilon(M_{1}),\varepsilon(M_{2})\rangle\subset\mathrm{PG}(V)
. The generators
M_{3}
is not a compement of
M_{2}
.

On the other hand, let
\dim(M_{1})
be less than
\dim(M_{2})
or
\dim(M_{3})
, say
\dim(M_{1})<\dim(M_{2})
. Then
\dim(\varepsilon)=\dim(M_{2})=\mathrm{max}(\dim(M_{1}),\dim(M_{3}))
, since both pairs
\{M_{1},M_{2}\}
and
\{M_{1},M_{3}\}
are complementary. As
\dim(M_{1})<\dim(M_{2})
, the equality
\dim(M_{2})=\mathrm{max}(\dim(M_{1}),\dim(M_{3}))
implies that
\dim(M_{3})=\dim(M_{2})
. So,
\dim(M_{1})<\dim(M_{2})=\dim(M_{3})
. If
M_{3}
is a complement of
M_{2}
then, by permuting
M_{1}
and
M_{3}
in the statement of Lemma
4.13
, we obtain that
\dim(M_{1})=\dim(M_{2})
, while
\dim(M_{1})<\dim(M_{2})
by assumption. Therefore
\{M_{2},M_{3}\}
is not a complementary pair. (Indeed now we would obtain that
M_{2}\cap M_{3}\neq\emptyset
, thus contradicting the hypothesis that
M_{1},M_{2}
and
M_{3}
are mutually opposite.)
\Box

5
A few examples of infinite rank

Throughout this section
\mathbb{F}
is a given field,
V
is a infinite dimensional
\mathbb{F}
-vector space,
V^{*}
is the dual of
V
and
\overline{V}:=V\oplus V^{*}
. Recall that
\dim(V)<\dim(V^{*})
. So
\dim(\overline{V})=\dim(V^{*})
.

We will adopt the following notation. We denote by
[v]
the point of
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
represented by a non-zero vector
v
of
\overline{V}
and by
[W]
the subspace of
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
corresponding to a subspace
W
of
\overline{V}
.

The kernel of a linear functional
\xi\in V^{*}
is denoted by
\ker(\xi)
and, for a subspace
\Xi
of
V^{*}
, we put
\ker(\Xi)=\cap_{\xi\in\Xi}\ker(\xi)
. Given
x\in V
, we define
\ker^{*}(x):=\{\xi\in V^{*}~|~\xi(x)=0\}
(a subspace of
V^{*}
) and, for a subspace
X
of
V
, we put
\ker^{*}(X):=\cap_{x\in X}\ker^{*}(x)
.

Finally,
p_{V}
and
p_{V^{*}}
are the projections of
\overline{V}
onto
V
and
V^{*}
respectively with respectively
V^{*}
and
V
as the kernels and, for a subspace
W
of
\overline{V}
,
p_{V}^{W}
and
p_{V^{*}}^{W}
are their restrictions to
W
.

All polar spaces to be considered in this section are full subgeometries of
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
. In order to avoid confusions between generation in a polar space
{\cal S}\subseteq\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
and spans in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
, henceforth we renounce to use the symbol
\langle.\rangle
when referring to spans in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
, keeping it for generation in
{\cal S}
and using phrases as “the span of … in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
” or switching to spans in
\overline{V}
when dealing with spans in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
. However we keep the symbol
\langle.\rangle
for spans in
\overline{V}
. No ambiguity arises from doing so, since the vectors of
\overline{V}
are not points of
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
, let alone points of
{\cal S}
. Thus, if
X\subseteq\overline{V}
then
\langle X\rangle
is the span of
X
on
\overline{V}
,
[\langle X\rangle]
is the span of
[X]:=\{[x]\}_{x\in X\setminus\{0\}}
in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
and, if
[X]\subseteq{\cal S}
, then
\langle[X]\rangle
is the subspace of
{\cal S}
generated by
[X]
.

Turning to perps, let
f
be the bilinear or hermitian form which defines the quasi-polarity of
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
associated to
{\cal S}
(or the quasi-polarity of
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V}^{\prime})
associated to
{\cal S}
if
{\cal S}
lives in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V}^{\prime})
for a subspace
\overline{V}^{\prime}
of
\overline{V}
). We use the symbol
\perp_{f}
for orthogonality with respect to
f
both in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
and
\overline{V}
(respectively, in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V}^{\prime})
and
\overline{V}^{\prime}
), keeping the symbol
\perp
with no subscript for collinearity in
{\cal S}
.

5.1
A symplectic polar space

As in
[
9
]
, we define a (non-degenerate) alternating form
f:\overline{V}\times\overline{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
as follows:

f(a\oplus\alpha,b\oplus\beta)~=~\alpha(a)-\beta(b),\hskip 14.22636pt\forall a,b\in V,~\alpha,\beta\in V^{*}.

(14)

Let
{\cal S}_{f}
be the symplectic polar space defined by
f
in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
. Of course, the inclusion maping of
{\cal S}_{f}
into
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
is the minimum embedding of
{\cal S}_{f}
(the unique embedding of
{\cal S}_{f}
when
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.). This embedding is tight. Indeed the subspaces
V=V\oplus\{0\}
and
V^{*}=\{0\}\oplus V^{*}
of
\overline{V}
yield opposite generators
[V]
and
[V^{*}]
of
{\cal S}_{f}
and
V+V^{*}=\overline{V}
(but if
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
then
\langle[V],[V^{*}]\rangle\subset{\cal S}_{f}
). Clearly,
\mathrm{rank}({\cal S}_{f})=\dim(V^{*})
.

As proved in
[
3
]
, all symplectic polar spaces are regular. Hence
{\cal S}_{f}
is regular. So, if
N
is a non-deep sub-generator of
{\cal S}_{f}
then
N^{\perp}
contains a hyperbolic line
h
and
\{\langle N,x\rangle\}_{x\in h}
is the set of generators containing
N
. Deep sub-generators also exist in
{\cal S}_{f}
. For instance,
V^{*}
admits infinitely many hyperplanes
H
such that
H\neq\ker^{*}(x)
for any
x\in V
. If
N=[H]
for such a hyperplane
H
then
N^{\perp}=[V^{*}]
.

5.1.1
Singular subspaces and generators of
{\cal S}_{f}

Let
W
be a subspace of
\overline{V}
. Put
K_{1}:=\ker(p_{V^{*}}^{W})=W\cap V
and
K_{2}:=\ker(p_{V}^{W})=W\cap V^{*}
and let
C
be a complement of
K_{1}\oplus K_{2}
in
W
. Then
p_{V}^{C}
and
p_{V^{*}}^{C}
are injective. Accordingly, if
C_{1}:=p_{V}^{C}(C)
and
C_{2}:=p_{V^{*}}^{C}(C)
, then
C_{1}\cong C\cong C_{2}
and the mappings
p_{1,2}:=p_{V^{*}}^{C}\circ(p_{V}^{C})^{-1}
and
p_{2,1}=p_{V^{*}}^{C}\circ(p_{V}^{C})^{-1}
are isomorphisms from
C_{1}
to
C_{2}
and from
C_{2}
to
C_{1}
respectively. Also,
p_{2,1}=p_{1,2}^{-1}
. So,
C=\{x\oplus p_{1,2}(x)\}_{x\in C_{1}}=\{p_{2,1}(\xi)\oplus\xi\}_{\xi\in C_{2}}
.

Lemma 5.1

With
C_{1},C_{2},K_{1},K_{2}
as above,
C_{i}\cap K_{i}=\{0\}
for
i=1,2
.

Proof.
If
x\in C_{1}\cap K_{1}
then
W
contains
(x\oplus p_{1,2}(x))-x=p_{1,2}(x)
. Therefore
p_{1,2}(x)\in W\cap V^{*}=K_{2}
, namely
x\oplus p_{1,2}(x)\in K_{1}\oplus K_{2}
. Hence
x\oplus p_{1,2}(x)=0
, namely
x=0
.
\Box

Clearly,
[W]
is a singular subspace of
{\cal S}_{f}
if and only if

\left.\begin{array}[]{lll}K_{1}\subseteq\ker(C_{2}+K_{2}),&K_{2}\subseteq\ker^{*}(C_{1}+K_{1})&\mbox{and}\\
p_{1,2}(x)(y)=p_{1,2}(y)(x),&\forall x,y\in C_{1}.&\end{array}\right\}

(15)

where for subspaces
X\subseteq V
and
\Xi\subseteq V^{*}
we put

\ker^{*}(X):=\{\xi\in V^{*}~|~\ker(\xi)\supseteq X\}\mbox{ and }\ker(\Xi):=\cap_{\xi\in\Xi}\ker(\xi).

Lemma 5.2

Assume that
[W]
is a singular subspace of
{\cal S}_{f}
. Then
[W]
is a generator of
{\cal S}_{f}
if and only if

\ker^{*}(C_{1}+K_{1})=K_{2}~\mbox{ and }~\ker(C_{2}+K_{2})=K_{1}.

(16)

Proof.
Suppose that
[W]
is a generator of
{\cal S}_{f}
. Clearly
W^{\perp_{f}}
contains both
\ker(C_{2}+K_{2})
and
\ker^{*}(C_{1}+K_{1})
. However
W
is maximal among the totally
f
-isotropic subspaces of
\overline{V}
. Hence both
\ker(C_{2}+K_{2})
and
\ker^{*}(C_{1}+K_{1})
are contained in
W
, namely
\ker(C_{2}+K_{2})=K_{1}
and
\ker^{*}(C_{1}+K_{1})=K_{2}
, as claimed in (
16
).

Conversely, assume (
16
). Note that, if
X
is a subspace of
V
, then for every vector
x\in V\setminus X
there exists a hyperplane
H
of
V
which contains
X
but does not contain
x
. Consequently,

\ker(\ker^{*}(X))~=~X.

(17)

By (
17
) and the first equality of (
16
) we obtain

C_{1}+K_{1}~=~\ker(K_{2}).

(18)

Let now
a\oplus\alpha\in W^{\perp_{f}}
. Then
0=f(a\oplus\alpha,\kappa)=\kappa(a)
for every
\kappa\in K_{2}
. Therefore
a\in\ker(K_{2})
. Hence
a=c+k
for some
c\in C_{1}
and some
k\in K_{1}
by (
18
). So, we can replace
a\oplus\alpha
with
c\oplus\alpha=(a\oplus\alpha)-(k\oplus 0)
. In other words, we can assume to have chosen
a\oplus\alpha
with
a\in C_{1}
. However
W
contains
a\oplus p_{1,2}(a)
. Hence we can replace
a\oplus\alpha
with
a\oplus\alpha-a\oplus p_{1,2}(a)=0\oplus(\alpha-p_{1,2}(a))=\alpha-p_{1,2}(a)
. So, we can assume to have chosen
a\oplus\alpha
with
a=0
. Thus, we are reduced to the case of
\alpha\in V^{*}
such that
\alpha\perp_{f}W
. This condition forces
\alpha\in\ker^{*}(C_{1}+K_{1})
. Hence
\alpha\in K_{2}
by the first equality of (
16
). We have proved that
W^{\perp_{f}}=W
. Thus
[W
] is a generator of
{\cal S}_{f}
.
\Box

Proposition 5.3

Every generator of
{\cal S}_{f}
admits a complement.

Proof.
Let
[W]
be a generator of
{\cal S}_{f}
. With
C_{1},C_{2},K_{1},K_{2}
defined as in the first paragraph of this subsection, choose a complement
H_{1}
of
C_{1}+K_{1}
in
V
and put
H_{2}:=\ker^{*}(H_{1})
. Let
W^{\prime}:=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
. The two relations of (
16
), referred to
W^{\prime}=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
, amount to the equality
\ker^{*}(H_{1})=H_{2}
, which holds by definition of
H_{2}
, and the equality
\ker(H_{2})=H_{1}
, namely
\ker(\ker^{*}(H_{1}))=H_{1}
, which is a special case of (
17
). So,
W^{\prime}
satisfies both equations of (
16
). Hence
[W^{\prime}]
is a generator.

Let
x\oplus\xi\in W\cap W^{\prime}
. Then
x\in H_{1}
,
\xi\in H_{2}
and
x\oplus\xi=(k+c)\oplus(\kappa+p_{1,2}(c))
for suitable
k\in K_{1}
,
\kappa\in K_{2}
and
c\in C_{1}
. However
H_{1}\cap(K_{1}+C_{1})=0
. Therefore
x=0
. Accordingly,
k+c=0
, namely
c=-k
. Lemma
5.1
now forces
c=k=0
. Hence
p_{1,2}(c)=0
. In the end,
\xi=\kappa
. Recall that
\xi\in H_{2}=\ker^{*}(H_{1})
while
\kappa\in K_{2}=\ker^{*}(K_{1}+C_{1})
. Moreover

\ker^{*}(H_{1})\cap\ker^{*}(K_{1}+C_{1})~=~\ker^{*}(H_{1}+(K_{1}+C_{1})).

However
H_{1}+K_{1}+C_{1}=V
by our choice of
H_{1}
. Hence
H_{2}\cap K_{2}=\ker^{*}(V)=0
. It follows that
\xi=\kappa=0
. Therefore
W\cap W^{\prime}=0
.

We shall now prove that
W+W^{\prime}=\overline{V}
. The subspace
W+W^{\prime}
contains
K_{2}+H_{2}
. Let
\xi\in V^{*}
. If
\ker(\xi)
contains
H_{1}
then
\xi
belongs to
H_{2}
. Suppose that
\ker(\xi)\cap H_{1}
is a hyperplane of
H_{1}
. As
H_{1}
is a complement of
K_{1}+H_{1}
and
K_{2}=\ker^{*}(K_{1}+H_{1})
, there exists
\kappa\in K_{2}
such that
\kappa
and
\xi
induce the same linear functional on
H_{1}
. Put
\chi:=\xi-\kappa
. Then
\chi\in\ker^{*}(H_{1})=H_{2}
. So,
\xi=\kappa+\chi\in K_{2}+H_{2}
.

We have proved that
W+W^{\prime}
contains
V^{*}
. Consequently
(W+W^{\prime})\cap V
contains
(K_{1}+C_{1})+H_{1}=V
. So,
W+W^{\prime}
contains both
V
and
V^{*}
. Hence
W+W^{\prime}=\overline{V}
.
\Box

The space
{\cal S}_{f}
also admits opposite generators which are not complementary, as in the following example.

Example 5.4

Given a basis
(e_{i})_{i\in\mathfrak{I}}
of
V
, for every
i\in\mathfrak{I}
define a linear functional
\eta_{i}\in V^{*}
by the clause that
\eta_{i}(e_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
(Kronecker symbol) for any
j\in\mathfrak{I}
. The linear functionals
\eta_{i}
defined in this way span a proper subspace
V^{\prime}
of
V^{*}
isomorphic to
V
. Note that
\ker(V^{\prime})=0
. Let
W
be the subspace of
\overline{V}
spanned by the vectors
e_{i}\oplus\eta_{i}
for
i\in\mathfrak{I}
. It is easy to see that
[W]
is a generator of
{\cal S}_{f}
. Clearly,
V\cap W=0
. However
\dim(V+W)=\dim(V)<\dim(V^{*})=\dim(\overline{V})
. Hence
V+W\subset\overline{V}
. So,
[W]
is opposite to
[V]
but it is not a complement of
[V]
.

5.1.2
The characteristic two case

The natural embeding of
{\cal S}_{f}
in
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
is universal if and only if
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Accordingly, when
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
the union of any two complementary generators generates
{\cal S}_{f}
. In contrast, when
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
no two generators of
{\cal S}_{f}
generate
{\cal S}_{f}
, even when they are complementary. For instance, when
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
the subspace
{\cal Q}:=\langle[V],[V^{*}]\rangle
of
{\cal S}_{f}
is the set of points of
\mathrm{PG}(\overline{V})
represented by the non-zero vectors
a\oplus\alpha
of
\overline{V}
such that
\alpha(a)=0
. The set
{\cal Q}
is a proper subspace of
{\cal S}_{f}
. In fact
{\cal Q}
is an infinite dimensional analogue of a hyperbolic quadric, defined by the quadratic form which maps every vector
a\oplus\alpha\in\overline{V}
onto the scalar
\alpha(a)\in\mathbb{F}
. Referring to the next subsection (Section
5.2
) for a discussion of these quadrics, we devote the next paragraph to a description the universal embedding of
{\cal S}_{f}
when
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
.

Assuming that
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=2
, let
\mathbb{F}^{2}:=\{t^{2}\}_{t\in\mathbb{F}}
be the square subfield of
\mathbb{F}

\mathrm{St}([U_{0,1}])
is isomorphic to the polar space associated with the form induced by

on a complement
X

U_{0,1}
in

, we can choose

\Box

5.4.3
Stars of sub-sub-generators

Let
X
be a sub-sub-generator of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
. In principle,
\mathrm{St}(X)
could be any of the following:

(1)

a copy of the hyperbolic quadric
{\cal Q}^{+}_{4}(\mathbb{F})
of
\mathrm{PG}(3,\mathbb{F})
;

(2)

a copy of the quadric
{\cal Q}_{4}(\mathbb{F})
of
\mathrm{PG}(4,\mathbb{F})
;

(3)

a non-degenerate quadric of rank 2 in a projective space of dimension at least 5 over
\mathbb{F}
;

(4)

a quadratic cone in a projective space of dimension at least 3, with a non-degenerate quadric of rank
1
as a basis;

(5)

a pair of lines meeting at a point;

(6)

one single line.

In case (1) all sub-generators containing
X
are hyperbolic while in cases (2) and (3) none of them is hyperbolic nor deep. In case (4) the sub-generator corresponding to the vertex of the cone is neither hyperbolic nor deep while all remaining points of
\mathrm{St}(X)
are provided by deep sub-generators. In case (5) the meet-point of the two lines of
\mathrm{St}(X)
corresponds to a hyperbolic sub-generator of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
and the remaining points of
\mathrm{St}(X)
correspond to deep sub-generators. Finally, in case (6) all sub-generators containing
X
are deep.

Case (1), (2), (5) and (6) actually occur in
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
. We have shown in Section
5.4.2
how to define a sub-sub-generator
X
with
\mathrm{St}(X)
as in case (2). In order to obtain (1), choose two hyperplanes
H
and
H^{\prime}
of
V
(or of
V^{\prime}
) such that
\ker^{*}(H)\cap V^{\prime}\neq\{0\}\neq\ker^{*}(H^{\prime})\cap V^{\prime}
(respectivley
\ker(H)\neq\{0\}\neq\ker(H^{\prime})
) and put
X=[H\cap H^{\prime}]
. When
\ker^{*}(H)\cap V^{\prime}=\{0\}\neq\ker^{*}(H^{\prime})\cap V^{\prime}
we get (5) and, if
\ker^{*}(H\cap H^{\prime})\cap V^{\prime}=\{0\}
then we get (6). We do not know if cases (3) and (4) actually occur.

In cases (1), (5) and (6) all generators of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
containing
X
are also generators of
{\cal S}_{f^{\prime}}
while in cases (2) and (5) they are deep sub-generators of
{\cal S}_{f^{\prime}}
. Several possibilities for
\mathrm{St}(X)
are allowed in cases (3) and (4), depending on the dimensions of the projective space
X^{\perp_{f^{\prime}}}/X
which hosts the quadric
\mathrm{St}(X)
and the dimension of the radical of the bilinearization of the quadratic form which defines that quadric. Of course, the range of hypothetical possibilities depends on
\mathbb{F}
. For instance, if
\mathbb{F}
is finite then
\mathrm{St}(X)\cong{\cal Q}_{5}^{-}(\mathbb{F})
is the unique possibility in (3) while two subcases are allowed in (4), according to whether the basis of the cone is a conic of
\mathrm{PG}(2,\mathbb{F})
or an ovoid of
\mathrm{PG}(3,\mathbb{F})
. In contrast, if
\mathbb{F}
is quadratically closed then (3) is impossible and just one subcase survives in (4), where the basis of the cone is a conic.

If, as we believe, cases (3) and (4) never occur then the generators of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
are partioned in two families, namely the family
{\cal M}_{1}
formed by generators of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
which are generators of
{\cal S}_{f^{\prime}}
and the family
{\cal M}_{2}
formed by the generators of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
which are (deep) sub-generators of
{\cal S}_{f^{\prime}}
. The family
{\cal M}_{1}
contains all hyperbolic generators of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
while
{\cal M}_{2}
contains the non-hyperbolic ones. Moreover, a non-hyperbolic generator of
{\cal S}_{q^{\prime}}
contains no deep or hyperbolic sub-generator.

5.4.4
A conjecture

The quadric
{\cal S}_{q}
described in Section
5.2
is regular because it is hyperbolic (Proposition
5.8
). The proof of Proposition
5.8
relies on an analogue of Lemma
5.2
. In the proof of Lemma
5.2
we have exploited the fact that
\ker(\ker^{*}(X))=X
for every subspace
X
of
V
. If we replace
V^{*}
with a subspace
V^{\prime}
of
V^{*}
then, in order to repeat that proof, we need that
\ker(\ker^{*}(X)\cap V^{\prime})=X
for every subspace
X
of
V
. This property holds only if
V^{\prime}=V^{*}
. This remark suggests the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.16

With
q
as in Section
5.2
, let
V^{\prime}
be any proper subspace of
V^{*}
such that
\ker(V^{\prime})=\{0\}
. Then the polar space associated to the form induced by
q
on
V\oplus V^{\prime}
is non-regular.

Proposition
5.11
testifies in favor of this conjecture.
