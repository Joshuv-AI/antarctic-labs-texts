Abstract
In this note we will use Faulhaber’s Formula to explain why the odd Bernoulli numbers are equal to zero.



1
Introduction

For odd numbers greater than or equal to seven, why are the Bernoulli
numbers equal to zero? Because Faulhaber’s Formula tells us that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{2m+1}

is a polynomial in
\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\right)^{2}
, and
\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\right)^{2}=\frac{n^{2}+2n^{3}+n^{4}}{4}
.

2
Faulhaber’s Formula

We might know already

\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{3}=\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\right)^{2}.

Through inductive reasoning like that in
[
6
]
we might discover
further

\displaystyle 2^{1}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\binom{2}{1}\cdot\sum k^{3}

\displaystyle 2^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=\binom{3}{0}\cdot\sum k^{3}+\binom{3}{2}\cdot\sum k^{5}

\displaystyle 2^{3}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle=\binom{4}{1}\cdot\sum k^{5}+\binom{4}{3}\cdot\sum k^{7}

(1)

\displaystyle 2^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{5}

\displaystyle=\binom{5}{0}\cdot\sum k^{5}+\binom{5}{2}\cdot\sum k^{7}+\binom{5}{4}\cdot\sum k^{9}

\displaystyle 2^{5}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{6}

\displaystyle=\binom{6}{1}\cdot\sum k^{7}+\binom{6}{3}\cdot\sum k^{9}+\binom{6}{5}\cdot\sum k^{11}.

(We abbreviate
\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{m}
by
\sum k^{m}
.) The general
case is

\displaystyle 2^{m-1}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{0}\cdot\sum k^{m}+\binom{m}{2}\cdot\sum k^{m+2}

(2)

\displaystyle+\cdots+\binom{m}{m-3}\cdot\sum k^{2m-3}+\binom{m}{m-1}\cdot\sum k^{2m-1}

or

\displaystyle 2^{m-1}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{1}\cdot\sum k^{m+1}+\binom{m}{3}\cdot\sum k^{m+3}

(3)

\displaystyle+\cdots+\binom{m}{m-3}\cdot\sum k^{2m-3}+\binom{m}{m-1}\cdot\sum k^{2m-1}.

If we wish to prove such expressions, by
[
1
,
2
,
3
]

we may proceed using Pascal’s observation of telescoping sums. Consider
the special case of

\displaystyle\left(\frac{3\cdot 4}{2}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1\cdot 2}{2}\right)^{m}-0+\left(\frac{2\cdot 3}{2}\right)^{m}-\left(\frac{1\cdot 2}{2}\right)^{m}+\left(\frac{3\cdot 4}{2}\right)^{m}-\left(\frac{2\cdot 3}{2}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left[\left(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\right)^{m}-\left(\frac{(k-1)k}{2}\right)^{m}\right].

For the general case of

\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\left(\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\right)^{m}-\left(\frac{(k-1)k}{2}\right)^{m}\right],

(4)

the expression in brackets we may rewrite as

\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{m}\left((1+k)^{m}-(-1+k)^{m}\right)

and then use the binomial theorem to arrive at (
2
) or (
3
).

What if we want to rewrite such expressions in terms of a particular

\sum k^{2m+1}
? For example, by looking at (
1
) we may
write

\displaystyle\sum k^{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{3}{2}}\cdot\left[2^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{3}-\binom{3}{0}\cdot\sum k^{3}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\left[4\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-1\right]\cdot\left(\sum k\right)^{2},

which then implies

\displaystyle\sum k^{7}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{4}{3}}\cdot\left[2^{3}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{4}-\binom{4}{1}\cdot\sum k^{5}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\left[2^{3}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{2}-4\cdot\frac{1}{3}\cdot\left(4\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-1\right)\right]\cdot\left(\sum k\right)^{2}.

By a proof by mathematical induction on the
m
of (
2
)
or (
3
) we may arrive at the general result of

\displaystyle\sum k^{2m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m+1}\cdot\left[2^{m}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{m-1}-a_{2}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{m-2}+a_{3}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{m-3}\right.

(5)

\displaystyle\left.\mp\cdots\mp a_{m-2}\cdot\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^{2}\mp a_{m-1}\cdot\frac{1}{3}\cdot\left(4\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-1\right)\right]\cdot\left(\sum k\right)^{2},

where the
a_{i}
are rational numbers and
m\geqq 3
. This relationship
we will call Faulhaber’s Formula. (For some of the history of the
problem, see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.)

3
Bernoulli Numbers

By
[
1
,
2
]
or Chapter 1 of
[
5
]
we may define
the Bernoulli numbers
B_{n}
by

B_{0}=1,\quad\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{k}\cdot B_{k}=0,

where
n\geqq 1
. For example, to find
B_{1}
we write

\sum_{k=0}^{1}\binom{1+1}{k}\cdot B_{k}=\binom{2}{0}\cdot B_{0}+\binom{2}{1}\cdot B_{1}=0,

which implies
B_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}
. It turns out
B_{3}=B_{5}=0
.
Following the section “Back to Faulhaber’s form” of
[
4
]

we may write

\displaystyle\sum k^{2m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2m+2}\cdot\left[\binom{2m+2}{0}\cdot B_{0}\cdot n^{2m+2}-\binom{2m+2}{1}\cdot B_{1}\cdot n^{2m+1}\right.

(6)

\displaystyle\left.\pm\cdots+\binom{2m+2}{2m}\cdot B_{2m}\cdot n^{2}-\binom{2m+2}{2m+1}\cdot B_{2m+1}\cdot n\right],

for which we will assume
m\geqq 3
.

4
Conclusion

With regard to the claim at the start, suppose we set (
5
)
and (
6
) equal to one another. If we multiply out (
5
),
we see it does not contain the term
n
. That means the last term
of (
6
),

-\frac{1}{2m+2}\cdot\binom{2m+2}{2m+1}\cdot B_{2m+1}\cdot n,

must be equal to zero. In other words, for all
m\geqq 3
,
B_{2m+1}=0
.
