Generalized asymptotic formulae for estimating statistical significance in high energy physics analyses
M. J. Basso

Abstract
Within the framework of likelihood-based statistical tests for high energy physics measurements, we derive generalized expressions for estimating the statistical significance of discovery using the asymptotic approximations of Wilks and Wald for a variety of measurement models. These models include arbitrary numbers of signal regions, control regions, and Gaussian constraints. We extend our expressions to use the representative or “Asimov” dataset proposed by Cowan et al. such that they are made data-free. While many of the generalized expressions are complicated and often involve solving systems of coupled, multivariate equations, we show these expressions reduce to closed-form results under simplifying assumptions. We also validate the predicted significance using toy-based data in select cases.

Introduction

1.1
Relevant Theory

In the field of high energy physics (HEP), likelihood-based statistical tests entail the construction of a likelihood function
L
describing a particular measurement model; the likelihood function in turn describes the “likelihood” of measuring parameters defining the model given some observed data
[
1
]
. For counting experiments typical of HEP analyses at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), the likelihood may be written most simply as a product of Poisson probability density functions (PDFs) over
N
“regions” or “bins”:

L(\vec{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P(n_{i}|\nu_{i}(\vec{\theta}))=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\nu_{i}(\vec{\theta})^{n_{i}}\cdot\exp{(-\nu_{i}(\vec{\theta})})}{n_{i}!}\,,

(1)

where
n_{i}
and
\nu_{i}
are the observed and expected yields in region
i
, respectively, and
\vec{\theta}
are the free parameters defining out model. Here, we have assumed a uniform prior
\pi(\vec{\theta})
for our free parameters (i.e., no prior knowledge). The best-fit parameters for a given measurement will be those which
maximize
the likelihood.

Typically, one is interested in measuring some signal
s
(e.g., the number of Higgs boson decay events to
WW^{*}
) given some known or constrained background
b
(e.g., the number of Drell-Yan events). In this case,
s
defines our parameter-of-interest (POI), what we are interested in measuring, while
b
defines a nuisance parameter (NP), a parameter we measure but which may not be physically interesting. We may parametrize the expected yield as
\nu(\mu,b)=\mu s+b
, where
s
is now fixed and our signal strength
\mu
is what tunes the amount of signal, now becoming our POI
1
1

1

N.B.: it is equally valid to let
s
be our POI, but in the spirit of consistency with the literature on this topic, we adopt this reparametrization.
. Absorbing
b
into
\vec{\theta}
, which we now assumes contains only our NPs, and letting
L=L(\mu,\vec{\theta})
, we construct the log-likelihood ratio:

\lambda({\tilde{\mu}})=\frac{L({\tilde{\mu}},\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{\theta}}}})}{L(\hat{\mu},\vec{\hat{\theta}})}\,,

(2)

where
\hat{\mu}
and
\vec{\hat{\theta}}
are the
unconditional
maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) of
L
(i.e., the values of
\mu
and
\vec{\theta}
which set
\partial L/\partial\mu=0
and
\partial L/\partial\theta_{i}=0\,\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N
where
N
is the number of NPs) and
\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{\theta}}}}
is the
conditional
MLE of
L
for fixed
\mu={\tilde{\mu}}
(i.e., the values of
\vec{\theta}
which set
\partial L/\partial\theta_{i}=0\,\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N
where
N
is the number of NPs)
[
1
,
2
]
–
{\tilde{\mu}}
is herein referred to as the
hypothesized
value of
\mu
. Eq.
2
ranges between 0 and 1, where
\lambda({\tilde{\mu}})\sim 1
indicates good agreement between the hypothesized value of
\mu
and its MLE while
\lambda({\tilde{\mu}})\ll 1
indicates disagreement.

Using Eq.
2
, we define our test statistic:

t_{\tilde{\mu}}=-2\ln\lambda({\tilde{\mu}})\,,

(3)

where
t_{\tilde{\mu}}\sim 0
indicates good agreement between the hypothesized
\mu
and its MLE and increasing
t_{\tilde{\mu}}
indicates increasing disagreement
[
1
,
2
]
. We may define a
p
-value,
p_{\tilde{\mu}}
, representing the probability of observering equal or greater disagreement with the hypothesized
\mu
as:

p_{\tilde{\mu}}=\int_{t_{{\tilde{\mu}},\textrm{obs}}}^{\infty}f(t_{{\tilde{\mu}}}|{\tilde{\mu}})\,dt_{{\tilde{\mu}}}\,,

(4)

where
f(t_{{\tilde{\mu}}}|{\tilde{\mu}})
is the PDF of the test statistic assuming hypothesized
\mu={\tilde{\mu}}
and
t_{{\tilde{\mu}},\textrm{obs}}
is the observed value of the test statistic. As is common practice in HEP, one may translate the
p
-value into the number of standard deviations from the mean of a standard Gaussian whose integrated, one-sided tail equals such a probability, i.e.:

Z_{\tilde{\mu}}=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_{\tilde{\mu}})\,,

(5)

where
\Phi^{-1}
is the inverse cumulative function of a standard Gaussian. This quantity is referred to as the
significance
or the
sensitivity
of the measurement.

In “discovery” HEP analyses, one looks to measure the presence of signal (i.e.,
\mu s+b
with
\mu>0
) among background processes, adopting the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that no signal is present (i.e.,
{\tilde{\mu}}=0
) and the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
that signal is present in some fixed amount. An example of a discovery analysis would be a measurement of vector boson fusion production of Higgs bosons decaying to
WW^{*}
over the prevailing top quark pair and single top production, Drell-Yan, and diboson backgrounds.

Within the framework of likelihood-based statistical tests for HEP, we adopt the test statistic for discovery analyses proposed by Cowan
et al.

[
2
]
:

t_{0}=\begin{cases}-2\ln\lambda(0)&\text{if $\hat{\mu}\geq 0$}\\
0&\text{if $\hat{\mu}<0$}\\
\end{cases}\,,

(6)

where, as before,
\hat{\mu}
is our unconditional MLE of
\mu
. As we should not measure a negative signal strength for a signal model predicting an enhancement to our measured yields, we set
t_{0}
equal to 0 as a lower bound on our test statistic (i.e., consistent with the null hypothesis). By Eq.
4
, our
p
-value becomes:

p_{0}=\int_{t_{0,\textrm{obs}}}^{\infty}f(t_{0}|0)\,dt_{0}\,,

(7)

and by Eq.
5
, our significance of discovery is:

Z_{0}=\Phi^{-1}(1-p_{0})\,.

(8)

To claim the discovery of a signal, it is typical to require that the significance exceeds
5\sigma
:
Z_{0}\geq 5
. This corresponds to exluding the null hypothesis at the level of
p_{0}=2.87\cdot 10^{-7}
.

Often, a physicist will want to know the expected significance of a measurement assuming their signal model in MC to be true and correct. In the case of discovery analyses, this will necessitate knowledge of
f(t_{0}|0)
, the PDF of the test statistic assuming no signal. An approximation of
t_{0,\textrm{obs}}
may be made by setting it equal to the median value of
t_{0}
distributed according to
f(t_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}})
, the PDF of the test statistic for discovery assuming a
true
signal strength
{\mu^{\prime}}
. As an equation, the median
p
-value assuming a true signal strength
{\mu^{\prime}}
is given by:

\textrm{med}[p_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}]=\int_{\textrm{med}[t_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}]}^{\infty}f(t_{0}|0)dt_{0}\,.

(9)

Without knowing
f(t_{0}|0)
and
f(t_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}})
, the above expression is difficult to evaluate. Using the approximations of Wilks
[
3
]
and Wald
[
4
]
, Cowan
et al.

[
2
]
show that
p
-value for discovery may be approximated as:

p_{0}=1-F(t_{0}|0)\approx 1-\Phi(\sqrt{t_{0}})\,,

(10)

where
F(t_{0}|0)
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) for
f(t_{0}|0)
. The approximation is valid in the asymptotic limit (i.e.,
1/\sqrt{N}\ll 1
where
N
is the sample size) and assuming the best-fit signal strength
\hat{\mu}
is Gaussian distributed. Inserting Eq
10
into Eq.
8
yields:

Z_{0}\approx\sqrt{t_{0}}\,,

(11)

under the same assumptions. Given that
Z_{0}
is a monotonically decreasing function of
p_{0}
and using Eqs.
8
,
9
, and
11
, we may also write:

\textrm{med}[Z_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}]=\Phi^{-1}\!\left(1-\int_{\textrm{med}[t_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}]}^{\infty}f(t_{0}|0)dt_{0}\right)\approx\sqrt{\textrm{med}[t_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}]}\,.

(12)

To evaluate the above, Cowan
et al.
propose the use of the “Asimov” dataset where the estimators of all parameters yield their true values
[
2
]
. In our above formulae, this is equivalent to setting all parameters equal to their true values given our particular physics model (e.g.,
\hat{\mu}\rightarrow{\mu^{\prime}}
and
n\rightarrow{\mu^{\prime}}s+b
). If
t_{0,\textrm{A}}
is the Asimov value of our test statistic for discovery assuming the true signal strength
{\mu^{\prime}}
, then we can write:

\textrm{med}[t_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}]=t_{0,\textrm{A}}\,,

(13)

and by inserting Eq.
13
into Eq.
12
, we yield:

\textrm{med}[Z_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}]\approx\sqrt{t_{0,\textrm{A}}}\,.

(14)

This is one of the important results shown by Cowan
et al.

[
2
]
. It says we can estimate the median significance of discovery as the square root of the test statistic for discovery evaluted using Asimov data. Using the above, one can produce analytical approximations for a variety of measurement scenarios, giving a physicist a handle on the expected power of their analysis techniques without relying on numerical recourse. As Cowan
el al.
discuss in their paper, the asymptotic approximation is already quite good for
N\sim\mathcal{O}(100)
(see for instance Fig. 7 of Ref.
[
2
]
).

This note proceeds as follows: we will motivate and construct several different measurement scenarios (e.g., multiple control regions, multiple signal regions, etc.) a physicist typically encounters and derive expressions for the median significance of discovery using the asymptotic approximation and assuming Asimov data. In all cases, we generalize to an arbitrary number of regions or constraints
N
and show that the resulting formulae reduce to expected formulae (i.e., derived elsewhere) in the
N=1
case or to agree with numerical simulation in test cases.

Additionally, we will simplify the use of Eq.
14
in the following sections by dropping the approximation (i.e., setting it to an equality) and by assuming
{\mu^{\prime}}=1
, typical of discovery analyses where the signal model’s cross section is normalized to theoretical expectations. We define:

Z_{0}\equiv\textrm{med}[Z_{0}|{\mu^{\prime}}=1]=\sqrt{t_{0,\textrm{A}}}=\sqrt{-2\ln\!\left(\frac{L(0,\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{\theta}}}}_{\textrm{A}})}{L(\hat{\mu}_{\textrm{A}},\vec{\hat{\theta}}_{\textrm{A}})}\right)}\,,

(15)

where we have inserted Eq.
6
followed by Eq.
2
and the best-fit values of
\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{\theta}}}}
,
\hat{\mu}
, and
\vec{\hat{\theta}}
are assumed to be evaluated using Asimov data (hence the subscript “A”).

2.1
1 Signal Region +
N
Control Regions

2.1.1
General Case

Assuming a uniform prior, we can write our likelihood with 1 signal region (SR) and
N
orthogonal auxiliary measurements (read as:
N
control regions (CRs) for backgrounds
b_{i}
,
i=1,{\ldots},N
) as:

L(s,\vec{b})=P\left(n\,\middle|\,s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{N}P\left(m_{i}\,\middle|\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}\right)\,,

(16)

where
P
refers to a Poisson PDF,
n
is the observed yield in our SR,
m_{i}
is the observed yield in CR
i
, and
\tau_{ij}
are the transfer factors which carry background
j
in our SR to CR
i
. Inserting the mathematical form for
P
yields:

L(s,\vec{b})=\frac{\left(s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\right)^{n}\cdot\exp\left(-\left(s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\right)\right)}{n!}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}\right)^{m_{i}}\cdot\exp\left(-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}\right)\right)}{m_{i}!}\,,

(17)

and taking the logarithm yields:

\ln{L(s,\vec{b})}=n\cdot\ln\left(s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\right)-s-\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(m_{i}\cdot\ln\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}\right)\,.

(18)

As we are dealing with a likelihood, constant offsets do not affect our optimization and so we have dropped
-(\ln(n!)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln(m_{i}!))
.

We first consider the most probable value for the backgrounds,
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}
, in the absence of signal,
s=0
. We are interested in maximizing
\ln{L}
. As signal is fixed and constant, we make this explicit in
\ln{L}
by evaluating it at
s=0

prior
to taking any partial derivatives. The result is then differentiated with respect to background
k
and evaluated at
\vec{b}=\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}
to yield:

\frac{\partial\ln L(0,\vec{b})}{\partial b_{k}}\bigg|_{\vec{b}=\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.16736pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}}=\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}-1+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(\frac{m_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{j}}-1\right)\,.

(19)

Setting all of the partial derivatives,
k=1,{\ldots},N
, equal to 0 yields the following system of equations for
\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}
:

\left\{0=\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}-1+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(\frac{m_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{j}}-1\right)\,;\,k=1,{\ldots},N\right\}\,.

(20)

We now consider maximizing the likelihood in the presence of signal
s
. In this situation, we let
\hat{s}
and
\vec{\hat{b}}
be the signal and background yields, respectively, which maximize our likelihood. Signal and background yields are both left floating, and so we take the partial derivative with respect to
s
evaluated at
(s,\vec{b})=(\hat{s},\vec{\hat{b}})
:

\frac{\partial\ln L(s,\vec{b})}{\partial s}\bigg|_{(s,\vec{b})=(\hat{s},\vec{\hat{b}})}=\frac{n}{\hat{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{b}_{i}}-1\,,

(21)

as well as the partial derivative with respect to
b_{k}
:

\frac{\partial\ln L(s,\vec{b})}{\partial b_{k}}\bigg|_{(s,\vec{b})=(\hat{s},\vec{\hat{b}})}=\frac{n}{\hat{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{b}_{i}}-1+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(\frac{m_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{b}_{j}}-1\right)\,.

(22)

Setting Eqs.
21
and
22
equal to 0 and substituting Eq.
21
into Eq.
22
yields:

0=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(\frac{m_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{b}_{j}}-1\right)\,.

(23)

Our system of equations for
\hat{s}
and
\vec{\hat{b}}
is then:

\left\{\hat{s}=n-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{b}_{i}\,,\,0=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(\frac{m_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{b}_{j}}-1\right)\,;\,k=1,{\ldots},N\right\}\,.

(24)

We make the intuitive ansatz that the solutions to Eq.
24
are
\hat{s}=s
and
\vec{\hat{b}}=\vec{b}
when assuming Asimov data (i.e.,
n=s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}
and
m_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}\,\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N
). Indeed, this can be explicitly checked:

\begin{split}&\hat{s}-n+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{b}_{i}=s-\left(s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}=0\,,\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(\frac{m_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{b}_{j}}-1\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}}-1\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{ik}\cdot\left(1-1\right)=0\,.\end{split}

(25)

Using Eq.
15
and taking
\hat{s}=s
and
\vec{\hat{b}}=\vec{b}
, our significance of discovery is:

Z_{0}=\sqrt{-2\cdot\left(n\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}}\right)+s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left((b_{i}-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i})+m_{i}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\cdot(b_{j}-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{j})\right)\right)}\,,

(26)

In this expression and the expressions for
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}
, we set
n=s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}
and
\vec{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}
. We may simplify the above further by using
n=s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}
to yield:

Z_{0}=\sqrt{-2\cdot\left(n\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{n}\right)+n+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}+m_{i}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}b_{j}}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{ij}\cdot(b_{j}-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{j})\right)\right)}\,.

(27)

This is our expression for the median significance of discovery in the asymptotic limit.

&=\sqrt{-2\cdot\ln\!\left(\left(\frac{n+m}{1+\tau}\right)^{n+m}\cdot\frac{\tau^{m}}{n^{n}m^{m}}\right)}\,,\end{split}

(31)

matching what is shown in Eqs. 21 and 22 of Ref.

2.1.3
Assuming Diagonal
\bm{\tau}

Often CRs are defined such that they yield high-stats, pure regions for a specific background. Here, we assume CR
i
targets background
i
by assuming the matrix of transfer factors
\bm{\tau}
is diagonal (i.e., the acceptance of CR
i
is 1 for background
i
and 0 for all other backgrounds). Letting
\tau_{k}\equiv\tau_{kk}
, our equation for
\vec{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}
, Eq.
20
, simplifies as:

0=\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}-1+\frac{m_{k}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{k}}-\tau_{k}\Leftrightarrow\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}+\frac{m_{k}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{k}}=1+\tau_{k}\,,

(32)

for
k=1,\ldots,N
. Our significance of discovery is:

Z_{0}=\sqrt{-2\cdot\left(n\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{n}\right)+n+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}+m_{i}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{b_{i}}\right)+\tau_{i}\cdot(b_{i}-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i})\right)\right)}\,,

(33)

where we used
\tau_{ij}=\tau_{i}\cdot\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta function. Or, given
(-n\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{k})/(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i})=m_{k}-(1+\tau_{k})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{k}=-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{k}+\tau_{k}\cdot(b_{k}-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{k})
by Eq.
32
, we can also write:

\begin{split}Z_{0}&=\sqrt{-2\cdot\left(n\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{n}\right)+n+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(m_{i}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{b_{i}}\right)-\frac{n\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{j}}\right)\right)}\\
&=\sqrt{-2\cdot\ln\!\left(\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{n}\right)^{n}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{b_{i}}\right)^{m_{i}}\right)}\,.\end{split}

(34)

2.1.4
Assuming
N=2
Control Regions and Diagonal
\bm{\tau}

As a special case of the previous section, we consider 2 CRs (
N=2
) and assume each CR to be pure in the background they target, i.e.,
\bm{\tau}
is diagonal. Then by Eq.
32
:

\left\{0=\frac{n}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}+\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}}-1+\left(\frac{m_{1}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}}-\tau_{1}\right)\,,\,0=\frac{n}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}+\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}}-1+\left(\frac{m_{2}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}}-\tau_{2}\right)\right\}\,,

(35)

and subtracting the second from the first yields:

0=\left(\frac{m_{1}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}}-\frac{m_{2}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}}\right)-\left(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}\right)\Leftrightarrow\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}=\frac{m_{1}\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}}{m_{2}+\left(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}\right)\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}}\,.

(36)

Consider the simpler case where
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\tau>0
. Then
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}=\frac{m_{1}}{m_{2}}\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}
and:

\begin{split}0&=n\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}-(1+\tau)\cdot(\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}+\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}+m_{2}\cdot(\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}+\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\\
&=n\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}-(1+\tau)\cdot\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{m_{2}}\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}^{2}+(m_{1}+m_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}\\
&\Rightarrow\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}=\frac{m_{2}\cdot(n+m_{1}+m_{2})}{(1+\tau)\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2})}\,,\end{split}

(37)

(throwing away the
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}=0
solution). By symmetry, we can send subscripted
1\rightarrow 2
and
2\rightarrow 1
to yield our
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}
solution:

\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}=\frac{m_{1}\cdot(n+m_{1}+m_{2})}{(1+\tau)\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2})}\,.

(38)

We now consider the more complex case where we have
\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}
with
\tau_{1}>0
,
\tau_{2}>0
:

\begin{split}0&=n\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}-(1+\tau_{2})\cdot(\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}+\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}+m_{2}\cdot(\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}+\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\\
&=(m_{2}-(1+\tau_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}+((n+m_{2})-(1+\tau_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}\\
&=(m_{2}-(1+\tau_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\cdot m_{1}+((n+m_{2})-(1+\tau_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\cdot(m_{2}+\Delta\tau_{12}\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2})\\
&=m_{2}\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2}+n)+(\Delta\tau_{12}\cdot(n+m_{2})-(1+\tau_{2})\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2}))\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}-\Delta\tau_{12}\cdot(1+\tau_{2})\cdot\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}^{2}\,,\end{split}

(39)

where
\Delta\tau_{12}\equiv\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}
, which can only vary between
\tau_{1}
and
-\tau_{2}
. We have also cancelled an overall factor of
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}
on the third line, to remove the uninteresting solution
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}=0
. Our solution is:

\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}=\frac{-B_{2}\pm\sqrt{B_{2}^{2}-4A_{2}C_{2}}}{2A_{2}}\,,

(40)

where:

\begin{split}A_{2}&=-\Delta\tau_{12}\cdot(1+\tau_{2})\,,\\
B_{2}&=\Delta\tau_{12}\cdot(n+m_{2})-(1+\tau_{2})\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2})\,,\\
C_{2}&=m_{2}\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2}+n)\,.\end{split}

(41)

By symmetry, we have:

\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}=\frac{-B_{1}\pm\sqrt{B_{1}^{2}-4A_{1}C_{1}}}{2A_{1}}\,,

(42)

where:

\begin{split}A_{1}&=\Delta\tau_{12}\cdot(1+\tau_{1})\,,\\
B_{1}&=-\Delta\tau_{12}\cdot(n+m_{1})-(1+\tau_{1})\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2})\,,\\
C_{1}&=m_{1}\cdot(m_{1}+m_{2}+n)\,.\end{split}

(43)

The expressions above are only physically meaningful if
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}>0
and
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}>0
(you don’t expect a
negative
number of events). We suppose, for definiteness,
\tau_{1}>\tau_{2}\Rightarrow\Delta\tau_{12}>0
and
require

\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}>0
and
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}>0
(i.e., we assume to have a physically meaningful solution). Then
A_{2}<0
and
C_{2}>0
so
-4A_{2}C_{2}>0
, which implies
B_{2}^{2}-4A_{2}C_{2}>0
and
\sqrt{B_{2}^{2}-4A_{2}C_{2}}
has a real root. Additionally,
2A_{2}<0
and
B_{2}<\sqrt{B_{2}^{2}-4A_{2}C_{2}}
, so to always pick up a positive solution for
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}
, we choose the negative sign:

\begin{split}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{2}&=\frac{-B_{2}-\sqrt{B_{2}^{2}-4A_{2}C_{2}}}{2A_{2}}\\
&=\frac{B_{2}+\sqrt{B_{2}^{2}+4|A_{2}C_{2}|}}{2|A_{2}|}\,.\end{split}

(44)

For
\Delta\tau_{12}>0
; this solution is real and positive. We turn to
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}
:
A_{1}>0
and
C_{1}>0
so
-4A_{1}C_{1}<0
and
B_{1}>\sqrt{B_{1}^{2}-4A_{1}C_{1}}
. Additionally,
-B_{1}>0
, so our solution is always positive and we may write it as:

\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}=\frac{|B_{1}|\pm\sqrt{B_{1}^{2}-4|A_{1}C_{1}|}}{2|A_{1}|}\,.

(45)

The sign choice is still ambiguous, so we return to Eq.
35
and subsitute in our expressions for each. One can show that the negative sign is required to solve our system of equations, and so our solution is:

\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{1}=\frac{|B_{1}|-\sqrt{B_{1}^{2}-4|A_{1}C_{1}|}}{2|A_{1}|}\,.

(46)

The above is always positive, but the condition for being real requires
B_{1}^{2}-4|A_{1}C_{1}|>0
. It can be shown that
B_{1}^{2}-4A_{1}C_{1}=B_{2}^{2}-4A_{2}C_{2}>0
and so the real requirement is always met.

Our significance of discovery in the asymptotic limit is then Eq.
34
with Eqs.
44
and
46
appropriately substituted in. Assuming Asimov data, we let
n=s+b_{1}+b_{2}
,
m_{1}=\tau_{1}\cdot b_{1}
, and
m_{2}=\tau_{2}\cdot b_{2}
, where
s
and
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
are our theoretical signal and background yields in our SR, respectively.

2.2
N
Signal Regions + 1 Control Region

We now consider the case where we have
N
SRs (read as:
N
signal bins) and 1 shared CR. Then for our
i=1,\ldots,N
SRs, we have have transfer factors
\{\tau_{i}\,;\,i=1,\ldots,N\}
where
\tau_{i}
is the transfer factor carrying the background yield in our CR to SR
i
. We also assume the signal yields
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{N}
among our
N
SRs are correlated and tuned by a single POI, our signal strength
\mu
. Taking our theoretical background yield in our CR to be
b
and our observed value to be
m
, we can write our likelihood as:

L(\mu,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left\{P\left(n_{i}\,\middle|\,\mu s_{i}+\frac{b}{\tau_{i}}\right)\right\}\cdot P\left(m\,\middle|\,b\right)\,,

(47)

where we are dividing by the transfer factors, as in Section
2.1
we took
\tau
to be the factor which multiplies yields in our SR to give yields in our CR. We can immediately write our log-likelihood as:

\ln{L(\mu,b)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(n_{i}\cdot\ln\!\left(\mu s_{i}+\frac{b}{\tau_{i}}\right)-\mu s_{i}-\frac{b}{\tau_{i}}\right)+m\cdot\ln(b)-b\,,

(48)

where we have discarded the constant
-(\ln(m!)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln(n_{i}!))
. Going right ahead with finding our conditional and unconditional MLEs:

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial\ln L(0,b)}{\partial b}\bigg|_{b=\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{4.50977pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{n_{i}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)+\frac{m}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}-1=0\\
&\Leftrightarrow\frac{m+\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{i}}{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}=1+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\\
&\Leftrightarrow\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}=\frac{m+\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{i}}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}}\,.\end{split}

(49)

Also:

\frac{\partial\ln L(\mu,b)}{\partial\mu}\bigg|_{(\mu,b)=(\hat{\mu},\hat{b})}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\cdot\left(\frac{n_{i}}{\hat{\mu}s_{i}+\frac{\hat{b}}{\tau_{i}}}-1\right)=0\,,

(50)

and:

\frac{\partial\ln L(\mu,b)}{\partial b}\bigg|_{(\mu,b)=(\hat{\mu},\hat{b})}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{n_{i}}{\tau_{i}\hat{\mu}s_{i}+\hat{b}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)+\frac{m}{\hat{b}}-1=0\,.

(51)

While these equations are difficult to solve in the general sense, we may propose the ansatz that
\hat{\mu}=1
and
\hat{b}=b
when assuming Asimov data (i.e.,
n_{i}=s_{i}+b/\tau_{i}\,\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N
and
m=b
). While the solution is intuitive, it can be explicitly checked to solve Eq.
50
:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\cdot\left(\frac{n_{i}}{\hat{\mu}s_{i}+\frac{\hat{b}}{\tau_{i}}}-1\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\cdot\left(\frac{s_{i}+\frac{b}{\tau_{i}}}{s_{i}+\frac{b}{\tau_{i}}}-1\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}\cdot\left(1-1\right)=0\,,

(52)

and explicitly checked to solve Eq.
51
:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{n_{i}}{\tau_{i}\hat{\mu}s_{i}+\hat{b}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)+\frac{m}{\hat{b}}-1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{s_{i}+\frac{b}{\tau_{i}}}{\tau_{i}s_{i}+b}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)+\frac{b}{b}-1=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}\right)+1-1=0\,.

(53)

Using Eq.
15
and the above solutions for
\hat{\mu}
and
\hat{b}
, we can write our significance of discovery in the asymptotic limit as:

Z_{0}=\sqrt{-2\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left(s_{i}+\frac{b}{\tau_{i}}\right)\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}{\tau_{i}s_{i}+b}\right)+s_{i}+\frac{b-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}{\tau_{i}}\right)+b\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}}{b}\right)+(b-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}})\right)}\,,

(54)

where
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}
is given by Eq.
49
and Asimov data is assumed.

As in Section
2.1.4
, we have numerically validated our results for the scenario where we have 3 SRs,
N=3
, and 1 CR, sampling Poisson PDFs in each of our 4 bins (with mean values of
s_{1}+b/\tau_{1}
,
s_{2}+b/\tau_{2}
,
s_{3}+b/\tau_{3}
, and
b
) in order to generate our simulated yields. We plotted the asymptotic signficance of discovery, Eq.
54
, continuously alongside these numerical results. This is shown in Fig.
3
. As before, we see excellent agreement between the numerical and asymptotic results over the range of theoretical yields and parameters studied.

We have also plotted alongside our results the “naive” approximation of the significance of discovery where the bin-by-bin significances are summed in quadrature:

Z_{0}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{s_{i}}{\sqrt{s_{i}+b/\tau_{i}}}\right)^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{s_{i}+b/\tau_{i}}}\,,

(55)

and indeed in the low
s/b
regime (where we are in this regime if
all
bins are in this regime), we see good agreement between all three methods. In the
s/b\sim 1
regime (where we are in this regime if
any
bin is in this regime), the naive approximation fails and no longer shows good agreement with the asymptotic and numerical methods, as expected.

2.3
N
Signal Regions +
M
Control Regions

We now consider the case where we have
N
SRs (read as:
N
signal bins) and
M
CRs (one for each background process). We assume the signal yields
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{N}
among our
N
SRs are correlated and tuned by a single POI, our signal strength
\mu
. We also assume the definitions of the CRs are
SR-independent
and
orthogonal
. For
i=1,\ldots,N
SRs, we have have transfer matrices
\{\bm{\tau}^{i}\,;\,i=1,\ldots,N\}
where
\bm{\tau}^{i}
is the transfer matrix carrying the yields in SR
i
to our
M
CRs (e.g.,
\tau_{jk}^{i}
carries the yield for background
k
in SR
i
to CR
j
). Our likelihood is:

L(\mu,\bm{B})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left\{P\left(n_{i}\,\middle|\,\mu s_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{ji}\right)\right\}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left\{P\left(m_{j}\,\middle|\,\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{M}\tau_{jj^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}b_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\right)\right\}\,,

(56)

where
\bm{B}
is our matrix of background yields in our SRs, where each row corresponds to specific background process and each column corresponds to a specific SR (e.g.,
b_{ji}\equiv[\bm{B}]_{ji}
corresponds to the yield for background
j
in SR
i
). Additionally,
i^{\prime}
can be any integer from 1 to
N
, but for consistent background yields in a given CR, regardless of which SR we are extrapolating from, we require
\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{M}\tau_{jj^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}b_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}=S_{j}\,\forall\,i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N
, where
S_{j}
is the expected sum of weights in CR
j
(i.e., a
constant
). For definiteness, we take
i^{\prime}=1
. Then taking the logarithm of Eq.
56
:

where

is the response function of background

\begin{split}R_{ij}(+1)&=1+\sigma_{ij}^{+}\,,\\
R_{ij}(0)&=1\,,\\
R_{ij}(-1)&=1-\sigma_{ij}^{-}\,,\end{split}

where
\sigma_{ij}^{+}
and
\sigma_{ij}^{-}

Our likelihood may be written as:

L(s,\vec{\theta})=P\left(n\,\middle|\,s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{i}(\vec{\theta})\cdot b_{i}\right)\cdot G\left(\vec{0}\,\middle|\,\vec{\theta},\bm{\Sigma}\right)\,,

where we have moved the functional dependence from

(75)

or taking the logarithm:

(76)

where we dropped the constant

. Note that we can also write the matrix multiplication in the last term using sums:

\vec{\theta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\vec{\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}[\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}]_{ij}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=i}^{M}(2-\delta_{ij})[\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}]_{ij}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\,,

(77)

where

\left\{\left(\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}-1\right)\cdot\sigma_{k}-\frac{(\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{k}-b_{k})}{\sigma_{k}}=0\,;\,k=1,\ldots,N\right\}\,,

(87)

which can be solved to yield the best-fit values of the backgrounds in the absence of signal. Finally, with our assumptions and redefinitions, Eq.
83

Z_{0}=\sqrt{-2\cdot\left(n\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}}{n}\right)+n-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}+\frac{(b_{i}-\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right)}\,,

(88)

which is our significance of discovery in the asymptotic limit. Assuming Asimov data, we would let
n=s+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}
.

We have also numerically validated our results for the scenario where we have 2 backgrounds each with 1 Gaussian constraint – we also assume the constraints are decorrelated. To simulate our yields, we sample a Poisson PDF with mean
\mu=s+b_{1}+b_{2}
in our SR. Additionally, to avoid biasing ourselves, we must sample the nominal value of each of the NPs from a Gaussian PDF centered on
b_{i}

\sigma_{i}
for

i=2

G(\tilde{b}_{i}\,|\,b_{i},\sigma_{i})
with
\tilde{b}_{i}
sampled from
G(x\,|\,b_{i},\sigma_{i})
). We have plotted the asymptotic signficance of discovery, Eq.

, continuously alongside these numerical results – this is shown in Figs.

s/b
from
\mathcal{O}(0.01)
to
\mathcal{O}(1)
and uncertainties on the background yields from
\mathcal{O}(0.1\%)
to
\mathcal{O}(100\%)
.

Alongside our results, we have also plotted the “naive” approximation of the significance of discovery:

Z_{0}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{s+b_{1}+b_{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\,,

(89)

(where

and

only responds to

2.4.5
Choice of Reponse Function

In the previous sections, there is freedom in the choice of the response function
R(\theta)
for interpolating between the up/down response of a background to a particular NP, i.e., subject to the constraints in Eq.
73
. For a symmetric up/down response (i.e.,
\sigma_{ij}^{+}=\sigma_{ij}^{-}
), a linear response function is the most intuitive (and possibly the only sensible) choice. For an asymmetric up/down response (i.e.,
\sigma_{ij}^{+}\neq\sigma_{ij}^{-}
), the situation becomes more complicated. The most intuitive response function is a piecewise linear function:

R(\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}R_{\textrm{up}}(\theta)\,,&\theta\geq 0\\
R_{\textrm{down}}(\theta)\,,&\theta<0\end{array}\right.=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+\sigma_{\textrm{up}}\cdot\theta\,,&\theta\geq 0\\
1+\sigma_{\textrm{down}}\cdot\theta\,,&\theta<0\end{array}\right.\,,\\

(93)

where
R_{\textrm{up}}
and
R_{\textrm{down}}
are the up and down responses, respectively, and
\sigma_{\textrm{up}}
and
\sigma_{\textrm{down}}
are the up and down relative yield changes, respectively. However, Eq.
93
is non-differentiable at
\theta=0
– as Eq.
80
requires derivatives of
R(\theta)
, it is desirable to ensure the response is differentiable for all
\theta
.

One way of ensuring differentiability is to smooth the response function in the vicinity of
\theta=0
using a weight function
w(\theta)
:

R(\theta)=w(\theta)\cdot R_{\textrm{down}}(\theta)+(1-w(\theta))\cdot R_{\textrm{up}}(\theta)\,,

(94)

where the weight function is subject to the following constraints:

\begin{split}w(0)&=\frac{1}{2}\,,\\
\lim_{\theta\rightarrow-\infty}w(\theta)&=1\,,\\
\lim_{\theta\rightarrow+\infty}w(\theta)&=0\,.\end{split}

(95)

The above ensures that the behaviour of the response in the up or down directions is faithfully retained:

\begin{split}R(0)&=R_{\textrm{down}}(0)=R_{\textrm{up}}(0)\,,\\
R(-1)&\approx R_{\textrm{down}}(-1)\,,\\
R(+1)&\approx R_{\textrm{up}}(+1)\,,\end{split}

(96)

as expected. If
w(\theta)
is smooth, the reponse function is smooth throughout the entire domain.

Any function satisfying Eq.
95
may be chosen, examples include:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textrm{Heaviside:}&w(\theta)=1-H(\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0\,,&\theta>0\\
1/2\,,&\theta=0\\
1\,,&\theta<0\end{array}\right.\,,\\
\textrm{Arctangent:}&w(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan{(\kappa\pi\theta/2)}\right)\,,\\
\textrm{Hyperbolic tangent:}&w(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh{(\kappa\theta)}\right)\,,\\
\textrm{Error function:}&w(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\textrm{erf}\,(\kappa\theta))=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\kappa\theta}\exp(-t^{2})dt\right)\,,\\
\textrm{Sigmoid function:}&w(\theta)=1-(1+\exp(-\kappa\theta))^{-1}\,.\\
\end{array}

(97)

The parameter
\kappa
tunes how sharply the transition is between the up/down responses at
\theta=0
. N.B.: the Heaviside weighting function is identical to the non-differentiable case, Eq.
93
.

In Fig.
7
, the effect of the choice of weighting function on the response function, Eq.
94
, and its first derivative are plotted. The hyperbolic tangent and error functions result in responses which converge the fastest to that using the Heaviside weighting function, while the arctangent and sigmoid functions result in responses which converge much more slowly. In contrast, the hyperbolic, error, and sigmoid functions result in large overshoots for the first deratives of these response functions, while the arctangent function results in a response whose first derivative converges the fastest to that using the Heaviside weighting function. With these remarks in mind, it may be concluded that the sigmoid weighting function is the poorest choice of weighting function for smoothing the behaviour of an asymmetric response about
\theta=0
. All choices result in a response which is differentiable at
\theta=0
, as desired.

Summary

We have presented a collection of derivations of generalized formulae for estimating the median significance of discovery in the asymptotic limit for various measurement models in HEP. The formulae have been verified to agree with numerical results using toy-based data in select cases; other times, they are shown to reduce to known formulae in simpler cases derived by similar means. Appendix A
Solution to
\frac{A}{\sum_{j}x_{j}}+B_{i}x_{i}+C_{i}=0\,,\,i=1,\ldots,N

We consider the solutions to the system of equations:

\left\{\frac{A}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}}+B_{i}x_{i}+C_{i}=0\,;\,i=1,\ldots,N\right\}\,,

(A1)

where
A
is a constant and
B_{i}
and
C_{i}
are equation-dependent constants, analogous to Eq.
87
. Subtracting the
j
-th equation from the
i
-th equation:

B_{i}x_{i}+C_{i}-B_{j}x_{j}-C_{j}=0\Leftrightarrow x_{j}=\frac{B_{i}x_{i}+(C_{i}-C_{j})}{B_{j}}\,,

(A2)

which can then be inserted into the
i
-th equation of Eq.
A1
to yield an expression entirely in terms of
x_{i}
:

\begin{split}&A+B_{i}x_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}+C_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}=0\\
&\Leftrightarrow A+B_{i}x_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{B_{i}x_{i}+(C_{i}-C_{j})}{B_{j}}+C_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{B_{i}x_{i}+(C_{i}-C_{j})}{B_{j}}=0\\
&\Leftrightarrow\left(B_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{B_{j}}\right)x_{i}^{2}+\left(B_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2C_{i}-C_{j}}{B_{j}}\right)x_{i}+\left(A+C_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{C_{i}-C_{j}}{B_{j}}\right)=0\,,\end{split}

(A3)

which may be solved using the quadratic equation, selecting the real and positive root for
x_{i}
(or the answer which statisfies the system of equations as the physical solution as
x_{i}\leftrightarrow\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{b}}}_{i}
). From Eq.
87
, we identify
A=n
,
B_{i}=-1/\sigma_{i}^{2}
, and
C_{i}=b_{i}/\sigma_{i}^{2}-1
. Thus in Eq.
A3
, we know:

B_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{B_{j}}=\frac{-1}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}<0\,,

(A4)

as
\sigma_{i}>0\,\forall\,i
. From Eq.
A3
, we identify the quadratic equation:

-|\tilde{A}_{i}|x^{2}+\tilde{B}_{i}x+\tilde{C}_{i}=0\,,

(A5)

where:

\begin{split}|\tilde{A}_{i}|&=\left|B_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{B_{j}}\right|=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\,,\\
\tilde{B}_{i}&=B_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2C_{i}-C_{j}}{B_{j}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{2b_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{b_{j}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}-1\right)\,,\\
\tilde{C}_{i}&=A+C_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{C_{i}-C_{j}}{B_{j}}=n+\left(1-\frac{b_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{b_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{b_{j}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}\right)\,,\end{split}

(A6)

which has the solutions:

x_{i}=\frac{\tilde{B}_{i}\pm\sqrt{\tilde{B}_{i}^{2}+4|\tilde{A}_{i}|\tilde{C}_{i}}}{2|\tilde{A}_{i}|}\,.

(A7)

Using Eq.
A6
, it can be shown that:

\tilde{B}_{i}=\frac{\tilde{C}_{i}-n}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i}}-(\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i})\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|\,,

(A8)

and therefore:

\begin{split}\tilde{B}_{i}^{2}+4|\tilde{A}_{i}|\tilde{C}_{i}&=\left(\frac{\tilde{C}_{i}-n}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i}}\right)^{2}+(\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i})^{2}\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|^{2}-2\cdot(\tilde{C}_{i}-n)\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|+4|\tilde{A}_{i}|\tilde{C}_{i}\\
&=\left(\frac{\tilde{C}_{i}-n}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i}}\right)^{2}+(\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i})^{2}\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|^{2}+2\cdot(\tilde{C}_{i}-n)\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|+4n|\tilde{A}_{i}|\\
&=\left(\frac{\tilde{C}_{i}-n}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i}}+(\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i})\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|\right)^{2}+4n|\tilde{A}_{i}|\,,\end{split}

(A9)

which is always positive, so we are
always
guaranteed a real root in Eq.
A7
. We consider the difference:

\left(\frac{\tilde{C}_{i}-n}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i}}+(\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i})\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|\right)-\left(\frac{\tilde{C}_{i}-n}{\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i}}-(\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i})\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|\right)=2\cdot(\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{i})\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|\,.

(A10)

If
\sigma_{i}^{2}>b_{i}
, the above is
positive
: this means the argument of our square root will
always
be larger than
\tilde{B}_{i}
and we
must
select the positive sign solution for the
i
-th equation for a physical solution. By Eq.
A8
, we know:

\tilde{B}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{b_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{b_{j}}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}\right)+(b_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{2})\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|\,.

(A11)

If
b_{i}>\sigma_{i}^{2}
, the second term in the above is positive. And if
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot\left(b_{i}/\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{j}/\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)<0
and
|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot\left(b_{i}/\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{j}/\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)|>(b_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{2})\cdot|\tilde{A}_{i}|
, then we
must
select the positive sign solution for a physical solution. In the case where
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot\left(b_{i}/\sigma_{i}^{2}-b_{j}/\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)>0
, we must test both solutions for the
i
-th equation amongst all other solutions and pick the one which satisfies our system of equations and yields positive solutions
x_{i}>0\,\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N
. In this way, we have not precisely solved our system of equations, but we have set an upper limit of
N\times N
solutions to be explored before the physical solution is found.
