CoBarS: Fast reweighted sampling for polygon spaces in any dimension
Jason Cantarella, Henrik Schumacher

Abstract
We present the first algorithm for sampling random configurations of closed n n -gons with any fixed edgelengths r 1 , … , r n r_{1},\dots,r_{n} in any dimension d d which is proved to sample correctly from standard probability measures on these spaces. We generate open n n -gons as weighted sets of edge vectors on the unit sphere and close them by taking a Möbius transformation of the sphere which moves the center of mass of the edges to the origin. Using previous results of the authors, such a Möbius transformation can be found in O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) time. The resulting closed polygons are distributed according to a pushforward measure. The main contribution of the present paper is the explicit calculation of reweighting factors which transform this pushforward measure to any one of a family of standard measures on closed polygon space, including the symplectic volume for polygons in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} . For fixed dimension, these reweighting factors may be computed in O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) time. Experimental results show that our algorithm is efficient and accurate in practice, and an open-source reference implementation is provided. MSC-2020 classification: 60D05, 65D18, 82D60

Introduction

We consider configurations of
n
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with positions
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}
separated by a length vector
r
of fixed distances
r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n}>0
. We treat indices cyclically, so we may write the displacement vectors
v_{i+1}-v_{i}=r_{i}\,y_{i}
where
y_{i}
is a unit vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, letting
r_{n}\,y_{n}=v_{n}-v_{1}
. The elements of such a space are polygons with edgelengths given by the
r_{i}
. Our goal in this paper is to give an efficient way to randomly sample such polygons.

This sampling problem is of interest in the statistical physics of polymers, where the configuration space of polygons with fixed edgelengths is the state space for the
freely-jointed chain model

[
27
]
of a ring polymer. (See the survey paper
[
33
]
for many applications of these kinds of models in physics and biology.) The same space is studied in robotics
[
15
]
, where it models the kinematic configuration space of a robot arm with spherical revolute joints forming a closed loop.

[

of a kinematic loop is that of a robot in a fixed position manipulating an object which is also constrained, such as a door handle. Here there are implicit constraints connecting the base of the robot to the door’s hinge and the hinge to the door handle. In more complicated situations, multiple kinematic loops may be present at the same time, but this is beyond the scope of the present paper.
It is also of intrinsic interest in differential geometry and topology
[
24
,
16
,

,

]
.

Various algorithms have been proposed to construct random polygons
[
8
,
3
,
9
,
30

35
,
26
,
1
,
31
,
32
,

]
. However, all of them suffer from one or more deficiencies; they are either explicitly restricted to dimension
d=2
or
d=3
, not proved to sample the correct measure, and/or only generate equilateral polygons. Therefore, there is a need for a polygon sampling method which is fast, can be proved to sample the correct measure, and gracefully accommodates arbitrary choices of dimension and edgelengths. In this paper, we present such a method: conformal barycenter sampling (CoBarS).

We start by observing that it is easy to construct configurations of
n+1
points
v_{1},\dots,v_{n+1}
so that
\lvert{v_{i+1}-v_{i}}\rvert=r_{i}
by sampling unit vectors
x_{i}
uniformly from a product of spheres and letting
v_{i}=\sum_{j<i}r_{i}\,x_{i}
.
These configurations have the correct edgelengths, but they usually fail to close because
v_{n+1}=v_{1}
is satisfied if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,x_{i}=0
.
However, we will build a map (Definition
4
) from the space of open polygons to the space of closed polygons using the conformal barycenter (Definition
1
).
This map is only defined implicitly, but can be computed efficiently using the algorithm in
[
7
]
. This gives us a fast sampling algorithm, but the resulting samples are biased. The main theoretical contribution of this paper is a fast and explicit way to compute reweighting factors which eliminate this sampling bias. We then see in experiments that we can compute integrals over the configuration space of polygons quickly and accurately using this reweighting. The resulting method is faster and more general than the Action-Angle method described in
[
8
,
3
]
.

We note that the idea of generating closed polygons from open ones or (more or less equivalently) polygons with given edgelengths from arbitrary closed polygons is definitely not a new one and a variety of polygon closure or resampling algorithms have been proposed
[
2
]
. Any of these could be used to generate (biased) samples from closed polygon space, which could in principle be reweighted to sample the standard measure. The key new feature of this approach is that our closure algorithm is mathematically controlled enough that we can prove that it (almost) always converges, provide time bounds, and explicitly compute reweighting factors.

Proposition 7
.

The restrictions of the maps

We first observe that for any fixed

\displaystyle\mathrm{cl}\big\lparen\mathrm{op}(w,y)\big\rparen=\mathrm{cl}\big\lparen\sigma(-w,y)\big\rparen

Proposition 8
.

Suppose that

where
K(w,y)=\frac{\chi(w)}{J\mathrm{op}(w,y)}
, and
J\mathrm{op}
is the Jacobian determinant with respect to the metric
g_{\varrho}
on
{\mathrm{Arm}}
and the metric
g_{\mathbb{B}}\times g_{\varrho,r}
on
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}
, where
g_{\mathbb{B}}
is the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{B}
.

Proof.

Suppose we have an integrable function
h\colon\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that we want to integrate with respect to the product measure
{\mathrm{vol}}_{\mathbb{B}}\times{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}
. We first observe that it suffices to integrate
h
over
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
as the result will be the same.

Assuming that
\mathrm{cl}\colon{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
is a diffeomorphism (

), we can use the change of variables formula to pull back the integral of

{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}
, writing

\int_{\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}}h(w,y)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\mathbb{B}}(w)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}(y)=\int_{{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}}h(\mathrm{cl}(x))\,J\mathrm{cl}(x)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho}(x).

Here

is the nonzero Jacobian determinant given by

J\mathrm{cl}(x)=\sqrt{\det\big\lparen D\mathrm{cl}(x)^{*}D\mathrm{cl}(x)\big\rparen},

where we need to keep in mind that if

, the differential

is an invertible linear map

is defined relative to these inner products. We cannot compute

\displaystyle J\mathrm{cl}(x)=\frac{1}{J\mathrm{op}(\mathrm{cl}(x))},\quad\text{where}\quad J\mathrm{op}(w,y)=\sqrt{\det\left\lparen D\mathrm{op}(w,y)^{*}\,D\mathrm{op}(w,y)\right\rparen}\neq 0.

As above,

is the Riemannian adjoint. This yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}}h(w,y)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\mathbb{B}}(w)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}}\frac{h(\mathrm{cl}(x))}{J\mathrm{op}(\mathrm{cl}(x))}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho}(x).

We actually want to integrate the integrable function

. So at this point we specialize this formula by assuming that we have chosen some integrable

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}}\chi(w)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\mathbb{B}}(w)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}(y)}{\int_{\mathbb{B}}\chi(w)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\mathbb{B}}(w)}

(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathbb{B}}\chi(w)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\mathbb{B}}(w)}\int_{{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}}\frac{h(\mathrm{cl}(x))}{J\mathrm{op}(\mathrm{cl}(x))}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho}(x).

Now
\mathrm{cl}(x)=(w_{*}(x),y_{*}(x))
, so

\displaystyle\frac{h(\mathrm{cl}(x))}{J\mathrm{op}(\mathrm{cl}(x))}=f(y_{*}(x))\,K(w_{*}(x),y_{*}(x))\quad\text{where}\quad K(w,y)\coloneqq\frac{\chi(w)}{J\mathrm{op}(w,y)}.

Of course, we want to compute integrals with respect to the normalized volume (or probability measure) on
{\mathrm{Pol}}
given by
P_{\varrho,r}=\frac{1}{{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}({\mathrm{Pol}})}\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}
. By the law of large numbers, we may use reweighted sampling to estimate expectations over

as usual:

Corollary 9

If

is integrable and if

is a sequence of independent samples drawn from

on
{\mathrm{Arm}}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(y_{*}(x^{(j)}))\,K(\mathrm{cl}(x^{(j)}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}K(\mathrm{cl}(x^{(j)})}\rightarrow\int_{\mathrm{Pol}}f(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P_{\varrho,r}(y)\quad\text{almost surely as $N\rightarrow\infty$.}

(4)

3
Calculating

Our eventual goal is to compute the weight function

. We start by proving:

Theorem 10
.

For
(w,y)\in\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
we have

\displaystyle J\mathrm{op}(w,y)=\left\lparen\frac{2}{1-\lvert{w}\rvert^{2}}\right\rparen^{d}\,\frac{\det\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,\big\lparen I_{d}-y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\right\rparen}{\sqrt{\det\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}/\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\big\lparen I_{d}-y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\right\rparen}}\,\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-\lvert{w}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert{w+y_{i}}\rvert^{2}}\right\rparen^{d-1}\neq 0.

(5)

For fixed

, this can be computed in
O(n)
time and memory. Since this determinant does not vanish,
\mathrm{op}
is a diffeomorphism from
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
to
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}
and hence its inverse map
\mathrm{cl}
is a diffeomorphism from
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}
to

This theorem is surprising, because the matrix

The proof of this theorem will require us to do some detailed matrix computations. Accordingly, we take a moment to establish a system of coordinates and maps, along with some notation.

Lemma 11

We may extend

is an open neighborhood of

to

is an open neighborhood of
\mathbb{B}\times\bar{\mathbb{B}}

Proof.

Using (

The right hand side is in

1-2\,\langle{w,z}\rangle+\mathopen{}\left\lvert w\right\rvert\mathclose{}^{2}\,\mathopen{}\left\lvert z\right\rvert\mathclose{}^{2}\geq 1-2\,\mathopen{}\left\lvert w\right\rvert\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left\lvert z\right\rvert\mathclose{}+\mathopen{}\left\lvert w\right\rvert\mathclose{}^{2}\,\mathopen{}\left\lvert z\right\rvert\mathclose{}^{2}=(1-\mathopen{}\left\lvert w\right\rvert\mathclose{}\,\mathopen{}\left\lvert z\right\rvert\mathclose{})^{2},

Now we must compute

Lemma 13
.

We have

\displaystyle\det(Z^{*\!}\,Z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lparen\frac{1-\lvert{w}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert{w+y_{i}}\rvert^{2}}\right\rparen^{2\,(d-1)},

which cannot vanish because
\lvert{y_{i}}\rvert=1
and
\lvert{w}\rvert<1

Proof.

The map

\displaystyle Z^{*\!}\,Z\colon T_{y}{\mathrm{Arm}}=T_{y_{1}}\mathbb{S}\times\dotsm\times T_{y_{n}}\mathbb{S}\to T_{y}{\mathrm{Arm}}=T_{y_{1}}\mathbb{S}\times\dotsm\times T_{y_{n}}\mathbb{S}

is the direct product of
b_{i}^{*}b_{i}\colon T_{y_{i}}\mathbb{S}\to T_{y_{i}}\mathbb{S}

b_{i}\colon T_{y_{i}}\mathbb{S}\to T_{x_{i}}\mathbb{S}
is the map induced by
\tilde{b}_{i}
and where the adjoint is with respect to the Riemannian metric
\varrho_{i}^{2}\,g_{\mathbb{S}}
on
\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle\det(Z^{*\!}\,Z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\det(b_{i}^{*}b_{i}).

Note that
\tilde{b}_{i}\colon\big\lparen{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big\rparen\to\big\lparen{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big\rparen

\sigma(-w,-)

\lambda_{i}

can be computed easily and is

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1-\lvert{w}\rvert^{2}}{1+2\,\langle{w,y_{i}}\rangle+\lvert{w}\rvert^{2}}=\frac{1-\lvert{w}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert{w+y_{i}}\rvert^{2}}

using the fact that
\lvert{y_{i}}\rvert=1
. Since
\tilde{b}_{i}
maps

into

b_{i}

\lambda_{i}^{2}
. Since

Lemma 14
.

Consider
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}

and the rescaled inner product

\langle{u,v}\rangle_{\varrho}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\langle{u_{i},v_{i}}\rangle

({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
and let

be the orthogonal projector onto the subspace

. Then

\displaystyle\det(A^{*\!}\,A)=\det(P\,\tilde{A}^{*\!}\,\tilde{A}\,P+I_{(n+1)\,d}-P).

where adjoints are with respect to the inner product.

Proof.

First,
A^{*}A
and
P\,\tilde{A}^{*\!}\,\tilde{A}\,P=P^{*}\tilde{A}^{*}\tilde{A}P
are self-adjoint matrices and may be diagonalized.
Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{(n-1)\,d}\in T_{w}\mathbb{B}\times T_{y}{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
be the orthonormal eigenvectors and

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d\,(n-1)}
be the corresponding eigenvalues of
A^{*}A
.
Because
A
coincides with
\tilde{A}\,P

We complete this basis to an orthonormal basis for
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
by appending some vectors
e_{n\,(d-1)+1},\dotsc,e_{(n+1)\,d}

P\tilde{A}^{*}\tilde{A}P+(I_{(n+1)\,d}-P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n\,(d-1)}\lambda_{i}\,e_{i}\,e_{i}^{T}+\sum_{i=n\,(d-1)+1}^{(n+1)\,d}\!\!e_{i}\,e_{i}^{T},

proving that
\det(P\tilde{A}^{*}\tilde{A}P+I_{(n+1)\,d}-P)=\prod_{i=1}^{n\,(d-1)}\lambda_{i}=\det(A^{*}A)

∎

3.1
The projector
P

Our next task is to find a suitable representation for
P
. Note that
{\mathrm{Pol}}
consists of the points in
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
where the polygon closes (so
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}y_{i}=0
) and the

have unit norm. This means that we can write
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}
as the zero set of the map

\displaystyle\varTheta\colon\mathbb{B}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\quad\varTheta(w,y)=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,y_{i}\\
\frac{1}{2}(\lvert{y_{1}}\rvert^{2}-1)\\
\vdots\\
\frac{1}{2}(\lvert{y_{n}}\rvert^{2}-1)\end{pmatrix}.

The derivative
B\coloneqq D\varTheta(w,y)
is a linear mapping

\displaystyle B\colon T_{w}\mathbb{B}\times T_{y}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\oplus({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Since
y\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,y_{i}
is linear in the
y_{i}
, it is its own derivative; the derivative of
\frac{1}{2}(\lvert{y_{i}}\rvert^{2}-1)
is easily computed to be
y_{i}^{T}
(when
\lvert{y_{i}}\rvert=1
). Therefore, with respect to this decomposition of vector spaces,

can be written as a block matrix

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}0&r_{1}\,I_{d}&\cdots&\cdots&r_{n}\,I_{d}\\
0&y_{1}^{T}&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&y_{n}^{T}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\varOmega\\
0&Y^{T}\end{pmatrix},

where
\varOmega\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}\,I_{d}&\cdots&r_{n}\,I_{d}\end{pmatrix}

and
Y\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
are matrices of size
d\times(d\,n)
and
(n\,d)\times n
, respectively.
We claim that
B
is surjective whenever

. We prove this by establishing the following Lemma.

Lemma 15
.

Let
(w,y)\in\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
and
B\coloneqq D\varTheta(w,y)
. Then the matrix
B\,B^{*\!}
is invertible, where
B^{*\!}
is the adjoint of
B

with respect to the metric

g_{1}\coloneqq\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{\varrho}

on

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{n\,d}

and
with respect to the standard metric

g_{2}\coloneqq\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}

on

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

Proof.

Recall that we defined the metric
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{\varrho}
in
Section

1
. Now the definition of adjoint is that for all
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\oplus({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
and all

we have

g_{2}(B\,u,v)=g_{1}(u,B^{*}\,v)
.
We compute

\displaystyle g_{2}(B\,u,v)

\displaystyle=\langle{\varOmega\,u_{2},v_{1}}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}+\langle{Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,u_{2},v_{2}}\rangle_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}=\langle{u_{2},\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,v_{1}+Y\,v_{2}}\rangle_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}}

\displaystyle=\langle{u_{2},R^{-2}\,\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,v_{1}+R^{-2}\,Y\,v_{2}}\rangle_{\varrho}=g_{1}\Big\lparen(u_{1},u_{2}),(0,R^{-2}\,\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,v_{1}+R^{-2}\,Y\,v_{2})\Big\rparen,

where
R
is the blockdiagonal matrix of size
(n\,d)\times(n\,d)
with blocks
\varrho_{i}\,I_{d}
on the main diagonal.
That means the adjoint
B^{*\!}
has the following block structure:

\displaystyle B^{*\!}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
R^{-2}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}&R^{-2}\,Y\end{pmatrix},

Note that
R^{-2}\,Y=Y\,\mathrm{diag}(\varrho_{1}^{-2},\dotsc,\varrho_{n}^{-2})
and
Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,Y=\mathrm{diag}(\lvert{y_{1}}\rvert^{2},\dotsc,\lvert{y_{n}}\rvert^{2})=I_{n}
, because

. Hence we may write
B\,B^{*\!}
as the following block matrix:

\displaystyle B\,B^{*\!}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\varOmega\\
0&Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
R^{-2}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}&R^{-2}Y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\varOmega\,R^{-2}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}&\varOmega\,R^{-2}Y\\
Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,R^{-2}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}&\mathrm{diag}(\varrho^{-2})\end{pmatrix}.

Any
2\times 2
block matrix whose lower right block is invertible may be written in UDL form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&BD^{-1}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A-BD^{-1}C&0\\
0&D\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
D^{-1}C&I\end{pmatrix},

(9)

where the upper left block of the center matrix is the Schur complement of the lower right block of the original matrix.
For our matrix
B\,B^{*\!}
, this Schur complement is

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varOmega\,R^{-2}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}-\varOmega\,R^{-2}Y\,\big(\mathrm{diag}(\varrho^{-2})\big)^{-1}Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,R^{-2}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}

(10)

\displaystyle=\varOmega\,R^{-2}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}-\varOmega\,R^{-1}Y\,Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,R^{-1}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}

\displaystyle=\varOmega\,R^{-1}\big\lparen I_{n\,d}-Y\,Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\,R^{-1}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}

and we may factorize

as follows:

\displaystyle B\,B^{*\!}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&\varOmega\,Y\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&0\\
0&\mathrm{diag}(\varrho^{-2})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&0\\
Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

The two outer factors are triangular matrices with ones on the main diagonals. Thus they are invertible. The center matrix is block diagonal and the block
\mathrm{diag}(\varrho^{-2})
is invertible by assumption. Hence
B\,B^{*\!}
is invertible if and only if the Schur complement
\gamma
is invertible.

Keeping in mind that
R^{-1}
and
I_{n\,d}-Y\,Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}
are block-diagonal, and that multiplying by
\varOmega
takes a weighted sum over rows or columns, this matrix can be written as a weighted sum of the diagonal blocks of
I_{n\,d}-Y\,Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}
:

\displaystyle\gamma=\varOmega\,R^{-1}\big\lparen I_{n\,d}-Y\,Y^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\,R^{-1}\varOmega^{\mathsf{T\!}}={\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}}r_{i}^{2}/\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\big\lparen I_{d}-y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen.

We see that
\gamma
is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Assume
\gamma
is not positive definite.
Then there is a unit vector
V\in\mathbb{S}
with

\displaystyle 0=\langle{V,\gamma\,V}\rangle={\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}}r_{i}^{2}/\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\lparen 1-\langle{y_{i},V}\rangle^{2}\rparen.

This can only happen if

for all

. Since we require

, this implies that there must be at least one pair of indices

with

. But this contradicts the condition

. We note that this is why we introduced
{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
in the first place.

Hence
\gamma
must be positive definite, showing that
B\,B^{*\!}
is invertible and that
B^{*\!}
is surjective.
∎

One severe problem with this sampling seems to be that

\displaystyle J\mathrm{op}(w,y)=2^{d}\,\left\lparen 1-\lvert{w}\rvert^{2}\right\rparen^{n\,(d-1)-d}\frac{\det\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,\big\lparen I_{d}-y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\right\rparen}{\sqrt{\det\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}/\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\big\lparen I_{d}-y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\right\rparen}}\,\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{n}\,\lvert{w+y_{i}}\rvert^{2}\right\rparen^{1-d}.

The quotient by
\mathrm{SO}(d)

In many contexts, one is interested in shapes of polygons but not their poses in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Therefore, it is desirable to identify configurations which are related by a rigid motion and study the resulting moduli space of equivalence classes of polygons. This is the point of view taken by mathematicians who study polygons in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
via their symplectic structure
[
24
,
20
,
28
,
23
]
, and by the present authors in our previous papers
[

8
]
. We now see how to transfer our work to this moduli space.

The diagonal action of the special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
on
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
is an action by isometries of our metric
\langle{u,v}\rangle_{\varrho}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\langle{u_{i},v_{i}}\rangle
. Since each
\mathbb{S}
is invariant under this action, it restricts to an action by isometries on
({\mathrm{Arm}},g_{{\mathrm{Arm}}})
. Since the closure condition
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,y_{i}=0
is also
\mathrm{SO}(d)
-invariant, the action further restricts to an action by isometries on
(\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}},\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle\times g_{{\mathrm{Pol}}})
.
Since
\mathrm{op}
is Möbius-equivariant and
\mathrm{SO}(d)
is a subgroup of the Möbius group, the diffeomorphism
\mathrm{op}\colon\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}\to{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}
is
\mathrm{SO}(d)
-equivariant. These actions are faithful, but they are not free if the
y_{i}
lie in some lower-dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. So now we slightly restrict our attention to avoid these troublesome configurations.

Definition 20
.

We define
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
to the be subset of
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}
where the points

do not lie in any affine hyperplane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We define

to be the subset of
{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
where the

do not lie in any linear hyperplane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Lemma 21
.

The closure map
\mathrm{cl}
and the opening map
\mathrm{op}
are diffeomorphisms between
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
and
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}
. If

, then
{\mathrm{Arm}}\setminus{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
and

are nullsets.

Proof.

Suppose
(w,y)\in\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}
. We claim that

. Suppose not. Then the
x_{i}
lie in an affine hyperplane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Since the
x_{i}
also lie in the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}
, they lie in some
S^{d-2}
formed by the intersection of the affine hyperplane with
\mathbb{S}
. It follows that the
x_{i}
and their conformal barycenter

lie on some unique
S^{d-1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and that this sphere
S
intersects the unit sphere at right angles.

Now the
y_{i}
(and the origin) are the image of the
x_{i}
(and
w
) under a Möbius transformation. Therefore, they lie in the image of
S
under this Möbius transformation. Since this image is either a sphere or a hyperplane, since it meets the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}
at right angles, and since it contains the origin, it must be a hyperplane. This contradicts our assumption that
(w,y)\in\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}
. Thus
\mathrm{op}
maps
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}
into
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
.

The argument that
\mathrm{cl}
maps
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
into
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}
is quite similar. Suppose
x\in{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
, but
\mathrm{cl}(x)
is not in
{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}
. Then the
y_{i}
all lie in some hyperplane
H
. Now the opening map
\mathrm{op}(w,y)
is a Möbius transformation (of the
y_{i}
), so it maps
H
to some
S^{d-1}
containing
w
. This sphere also contains the
x_{i}
. Since the
x_{i}
are also on the unit sphere, they lie in the intersection of two different

.

Now
\mathrm{cl}
and
\mathrm{op}
are diffeomorphisms between the larger sets
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}
and
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\times}
, so they remain diffeomorphisms on the open subsets
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
and
\mathbb{B}\times{\mathrm{Pol}}
. Now observe that
{\mathrm{Arm}}\setminus{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
is the disjoint union of
{\mathrm{Arm}}\setminus{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}
and
{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\times}\setminus{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}
. We know that

is a nullset (

is a nullset, too: The probability that the points

span an affine hyperplane in

is one, while the probability that

lies in the same affine hyperplane is zero.

Finally, since the diffeomorphism
\mathrm{cl}
maps

Proposition 22
.

Suppose that
\varphi\colon\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is an integrable function. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}}\varphi(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widehat{{\mathrm{vol}}}_{\varrho,r}(y)=\int_{\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}^{\diamond}}\varphi(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widehat{{\mathrm{vol}}}_{\varrho,r}(y)=\int_{{\mathrm{Arm}}^{\diamond}}\varphi\big\lparen\pi(y_{*}(x))\big\rparen\,\hat{K}(\mathrm{cl}(x))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho}(x).

Here, if
\lambda_{1}(y),\dots,\lambda_{d}(y)
are the eigenvalues of
\varSigma(y)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varrho_{i}^{2}\,y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}
, and
K(w,y)
are the sampling weights from (
16
), we define

\displaystyle\hat{K}(w,y)\coloneqq\frac{K(w,y)}{{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(d))}\,\left\lparen\prod_{1\leq k<l<n}\frac{\lambda_{k}(y)+\lambda_{l}(y)}{2}\right\rparen^{-1/2}.

(18)

Proof.

The coarea formula (see
[
10
, p.160]
for a formulation in terms of differential forms or
[
14
, Section 3.4.2]
for one in terms of Hausdorff and Lebesgue measures) tells us that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}}\varphi(\pi(y))\,J\pi(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}(y)=\int_{\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}^{\diamond}}\left\lparen\int_{\pi^{-1}(z)}\varphi(\pi(y))\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}^{\pi^{-1}(z)}(y)\right\rparen\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widehat{{\mathrm{vol}}}_{\varrho,r}(z),

where

J\pi(y)=\sqrt{\det\left\lparen\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(y)^{*\!}\right\rparen}.

Since
\mathrm{SO}(d)
acts freely, each fiber
\pi^{-1}(z)
is parametrized by
\mathrm{SO}(d)
, and the submanifold volume
\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}^{\pi^{-1}(z)}
on the fiber with respect to the ambient metric
g_{\varrho,r}
is also the
\dim\mathrm{SO}(d)=d(d-1)/2
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{d(d-1)/2}
with respect to this metric.

In the quotient metric,
\pi
is a Riemannian submersion, so
J\pi(y)=1
. Observing that
\varphi\circ\pi
is constant on each fiber of
\pi
, we can simplify the above to

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathrm{Pol}}^{\diamond}}\varphi(\pi(y))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}_{\varrho,r}(y)=\int_{\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}^{\diamond}}\varphi(z)\,\mathcal{H}^{d(d-1)/2}(\pi^{-1}(z))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widehat{{\mathrm{vol}}}_{\varrho,r}(z).

The fiber
\pi^{-1}(z)
is an orbit of the
\mathrm{SO}(d)
action. We now set out to calculate the volume of this orbit. There is no reason to expect that all
\mathrm{SO}(d)
orbits will have the same volume, so we start by fixing some
y\in\pi^{-1}(z)
and compute the orbit volume in terms of
y
.

We start by parametrizing the orbit of
y
by the smooth map
f\colon\mathrm{SO}(d)\to{\mathrm{Pol}}
given by
f(Q)=(Q\,y_{1},\dotsc,Q\,y_{n})=\mathrm{diag}(Q)\,y
. The orbit volume is then computed by the integral

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\sqrt{\det\big\lparen Df(Q)^{*\!}\,Df(Q)\big\rparen}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{vol}}(Q).

The map
f
is
\mathrm{SO}(d)
equivariant, i.e., we have
f(Q\,Q^{\prime})=\mathrm{diag}(Q)\,f(Q^{\prime})
for all
Q
,
Q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Applying the derivative with respect to
Q^{\prime}
on both sides, we obtain with the chain rule that
Df(Q\,Q^{\prime})\,Q=\mathrm{diag}(Q)\,Df(Q^{\prime})
. For
Q^{\prime}=I_{d}
this can be rewritten as
Df(Q)=\mathrm{diag}(Q)\,Df(I_{d})\,Q^{*\!}
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle\det\big\lparen Df(Q)^{*\!}\,Df(Q)\big\rparen

\displaystyle=\det\Big\lparen(\mathrm{diag}(Q)\,Df(I_{d})\,Q^{*\!})^{*\!}\,\mathrm{diag}(Q)\,Df(I_{d})\,Q^{*\!}\Big\rparen

\displaystyle=\det\Big\lparen Q^{*\!}\,Q\,Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\,\mathrm{diag}(Q)^{*\!}\,\mathrm{diag}(Q)\,Df(I_{d})\Big\rparen=\det\big\lparen Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\,Df(I_{d})\big\rparen.

Here we exploited that
\mathrm{diag}(Q)
is an isometry with respect to
g_{\varrho,r}
and that
\mathrm{SO}(d)
acts on itself isometrically with respect to the Frobenius metric.
Therefore, the integrand is constant and it suffices to evaluate it at
Q=I_{d}
.

At
I_{d}
, the tangent space to
\mathrm{SO}(d)
consists of the skew-symmetric matrices. Further, if
\xi
and
\eta
are
d\times d
skew-symmetric matrices, then

\displaystyle Df(I_{d})\,\xi=\begin{pmatrix}\xi\,y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\xi\,y_{n}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad Df(I_{d})\,\eta=\begin{pmatrix}\eta\,y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\eta\,y_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

We now observe that (using the cyclic invariance of trace and the skew-symmetry of
\xi
),

\displaystyle\langle{\xi,Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\,Df(I_{d})\,\eta}\rangle_{\text{Frob}}=\langle{Df(I_{d})\,\xi,Df(I_{d})\,\eta}\rangle_{\varrho}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\langle{\xi\,y_{i},\eta\,y_{i}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\tr\Big\lparen y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,\xi^{\mathsf{T\!}}\,\eta\,y_{i}\Big\rparen=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\tr\Big\lparen\eta\,\big\lparen y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\,\xi^{\mathsf{T\!}}\Big\rparen=-\tr\big\lparen\eta\,\varSigma\,\xi\big\rparen.

We now choose
\eta
and
\xi
in order to calculate entries of the
(d(d-1)/2)\times(d(d-1)/2)
matrix
Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\,Df(I_{d})
. We first choose an orthonormal basis for
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which diagonalizes the symmetric
d\times d
matrix
\varSigma
and assume the diagonal entries are
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{d}
. With respect to this basis for
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the
d\times d
skew-symmetric matrices have an orthonormal basis given by matrices

\displaystyle\xi(i,j)_{k,l}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\text{if $(k,l)=(i,j)$,}\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&\text{if $(k,l)=(j,i)$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that multiplying by
\xi(i,j)
on the right swaps the
i
-th and
j
-th columns, multiplying the
j
-th by
-1/\sqrt{2}
and the
i
-th by
+1/\sqrt{2}
. We may then compute

\displaystyle\big\lparen Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\,Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\big\rparen_{(i,j),(k,l)}\coloneqq-\tr\Big\lparen\xi(i,j)\,\varSigma\,\xi(k,k)\Big\rparen=-\tr\Big\lparen\xi(k,l)\,\xi(i,j)\,\varSigma\Big\rparen=\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}{2}\,\updelta_{(i,j),(k,l)}

as the matrix product
\xi(k,l)\,\xi(i,j)
has diagonal elements if and only if
(i,j)=(k,l)
, in which case it has
-\frac{1}{2}
in positions
k
and
l
. Thus
Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\,Df(I_{d})
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
\frac{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}}{2}
.
It follows that the orbit volume is

{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(d)\,y)={\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(d))\,\det\big\lparen Df(I_{d})^{*\!}\,Df(I_{d})\big\rparen={\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(d))\,\left\lparen\prod_{1\leq k<l\leq n}\frac{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}}{2}\right\rparen^{1/2}.\qed

As before, we can immediately write down the formula for Monte Carlo sampling:

Corollary 23
.

If
f\colon\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is integrable and if
x^{(j)}
is a sequence of independent samples drawn from
P_{\varrho}
on
{\mathrm{Arm}}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}f\big\lparen\pi(y_{*}(x^{(j)}))\big\rparen\,\hat{K}(\mathrm{cl}(x^{(j)}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{K}(\mathrm{cl}(x^{(j)})}\rightarrow\int_{\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}}f\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hat{P}_{\varrho,r}\quad\text{almost surely as $N\rightarrow\infty$.}

(19)

We note that as in (
16
), we may ignore constant factors in the definition of the sampling weights
\hat{K}
when using (
19
), as they will cancel in the numerator and denominator. Therefore, we can ignore
{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(d))
and the factors of
2
in the denominator, effectively using

\displaystyle\hat{K}(w,y)=\frac{\sqrt{\det\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}/\varrho_{i}^{2}\,\big\lparen I_{d}-y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\right\rparen}}{\det\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,\big\lparen I_{d}-y_{i}\,y_{i}^{\mathsf{T\!}}\big\rparen\right\rparen}\,\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{n}\,\lvert{w+y_{i}}\rvert^{2}\right\rparen^{d-1}\,\left\lparen\prod_{1\leq k<l\leq n}\big\lparen\lambda_{k}(y)+\lambda_{l}(y)\big\rparen\right\rparen^{-1/2}

(20)

as sampling weights for
\hat{P}_{\varrho,r}
on
\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}
.

We note that if
n<d
, the polygon always lies in a lower-dimensional subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
no longer acts freely. One can apply essentially the same techniques as above, and obtain a similar formula containing only the
\frac{1}{2}\,(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})
for which
\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}>0
. As this case is not of much relevance to Monte-Carlo sampling, we leave the details to the interested reader.

6
Conclusion

We have given a new algorithm for sampling configurations of closed polygons with arbitrary prescribed edgelengths in any dimension. Our method can sample the same symplectic volume on
\widehat{{\mathrm{Pol}}}
for
d=3
as the Progressive Action-Angle method
[
8
]
. However, it is much more general, allowing us to construct samples with arbitrary edgelengths, in other dimensions, and with respect to the measure
P_{\varrho,r}
as well as the measure
\hat{P}_{\varrho,r}
on the quotient. We provide an open-source implementation of our algorithm:
see
[
4
]
for a parallel, header-only implementation in
C++
; and see
[
5
]
for a
Mathematica
interface. Our algorithm runs in
O(n)
time as opposed to the
O(n^{2})
complexity of the Progressive Action Angle method. Though time-per-sample can be misleading for reweighted samplers, our performance comparisons show that the new sampler is faster at estimating means to fixed confidence intervals.

We now suggest some avenues for further investigation. Many authors have studied the homology of the space of planar polygons with fixed edgelengths
[
18
,
22
]
. It would be interesting to combine our sampling algorithm with tools from topological data analysis to find cohomology groups for polygon spaces computationally. This could give new insight into the remaining open questions regarding the cohomology rings of polygon spaces in higher dimensions as well, as in
[
17
]
.

The volumes of polygon spaces in any dimension can (in principle) be computed explicitly by integrating the reweighting factors of (
16
) and (
20
) over
{\mathrm{Arm}}
.
Very little appears to be known about these volumes. In 2d, Kamiyama
[
21
]
has given computational results on the volume of the space of polygons with one long edge for
n=4,5,6
. In 3d, Khoi
[
25
]
used Witten’s formula to compute symplectic volumes for all polygon spaces. Khoi proved that among all
n
-gons with the same total edgelength in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the equilateral
n
-gons are the most flexible in the sense that their configuration space has the largest symplectic volume. These methods are deeply symplectic and so tied to the three dimensional case. But it is very natural to ask: are the equilateral
n
-gons the most flexible in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
? Our algorithm provides a powerful toolkit for this kind of investigation.
