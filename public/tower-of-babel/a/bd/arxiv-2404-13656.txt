Coboundaries and eigenvalues of morphic subshifts
Paul MERCAT

Abstract
We define a morphic subshift as a subshift generated by the image of a substitution subshift by another substitution. In other words, it is the subshift associated with a ultimately periodic directive sequence. We present an efficient algorithm for computing eigenvalues of morphic subshifts using coboundaries. We show that continuous eigenvalues of 𝒮 \mathcal{S} -adic subshifts for primitive directive sequences are always associated with some coboundary. For morphic subshifts, we provide a characterization of the dimension of the ℚ \mathbb{Q} -vector space generated by eigenvalues. Additionally, if the substitutions are unimodular and without coboundaries, we give a necessary and sufficient condition for weak mixing of the subshift.



1
Introduction

Eigenvalues of topological or measure-preserving dynamical systems are important invariants.
For example a rational (additive) eigenvalue indicates that the system is partitioned with a cyclic dynamic on pieces,
while an irrational eigenvalue implies a rotation of the circle as a factor.
Additionally, a minimal system is weakly mixing if and only if the set of its additive eigenvalues is
\mathbb{Z}
.

Coboundaries can naturally occur.
Indeed, it arose when we where studying a problem of Novikov with Pascal Hubert and Olga Paris-Romaskevich.
We found a renormalization of our dynamical system that allows us to conjugate it with a
\mathcal{S}
-adic subshift
with a coboundary (see Example
7.8
).

In 1986, a seminal paper by Host (see
[
Hos86
]
) characterized eigenvalues of a substitution subshift
with coboundaries and showed the surprising result that every eigenvalue is associated with a continuous eigenfunction.

Ten years later, Ferenczi-Mauduit-Nogueira (see
[
FMN96
]
) provided a completely different algebraic characterization of eigenvalues of substitution subshifts,
allowing for explicit computation. However their characterization is complicated and they even made a calculation error
in one of the two examples they provided (see Example 9.1 in
[
Mer23
]
for more details).
More recently, I proposed a simpler algorithm to compute these eigenvalues (see
[
Mer23
]
),
based on Host’s characterization and using a algorithm for proprification due to Durand and Petite (see
[
DP20
]
).
However, this algorithm only works for pseudo-unimodular substitutions, and the proprification algorithm
can sometimes produce very large matrices, that can complicate the computation of eigenvalues.

Much work has been done to extend Host’s result, for example, for linearly recurrent subshifts (see
[
CDHM03
]
),
or for minimal Cantor systems (see
[
BDM05
]
and
[
FD19
]
), or particular examples (see
[
CFM08
]
).
But they always assume that substitutions are left-proper, which avoid coboundaries.
In
[
BBY22
]
, Berthé-Bernales-Yassawi propose a generalization of Host’s result using coboundaries,
but they make very strong assumptions and their definition of coboundary is complex and not very general.

I start in Section
2
by giving notations and definitions used in the article.
Then, in Section
3
, I give a general criterion ensuring the existence of eigenvalues for a subshift.
In Section
4
, I partially extend Host’s result and show that any continuous eigenvalue of a primitive directive sequence is associated with some coboundary.
I use it in Section
5
to propose a new algorithm to compute eigenvalues of morphic subshifts that works without proprification and for more general subshifts.
I give an explicit description of the set of eigenvalues, and algorithms to compute it and to check aperiodicity and recognizability.
In Section
6
, I give a characterization of the dimension of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space spanned by eigenvalues of a morphic subshift.
If moreover the iterated substitution is unimodular without coboundary,
I provide a necessary and sufficient condition for the subshift to be weakly mixing, by looking at the action of some Galois group on pairs of algebraic numbers.
I finish by providing examples and a way to construct examples with non-trivial coboundaries in Section
7
, with some examples coming from geometrical problems.

Definitions and notations

2.1
Words and worms

An
alphabet
is defined as a finite set.
The set of finite words over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is denoted
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
It forms a monoid under concatenation.
We use
\left|w\right|
to represent the length of a word
w
, and
\left|w\right|_{a}
to indicate the number of occurrences of the letter
a
in
w
.
We denote
w_{[i,j)}
the subword of
w
between indices
i
(inclusive) and
j
(exclusive).
The
abelian vector
of a word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is defined as
\operatorname{ab}(w)=(\left|w\right|_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{A}
.
We denote
e_{a}=\operatorname{ab}(a)
, so that
(e_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
forms the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
.

The
worm
of an infinite word
x\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is

W(x)=\{\operatorname{ab}(x_{[0,n)})\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}=\{\operatorname{ab}(p)\mid p\text{ prefix of }x\}
.

2.2
Subshift

Given an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, we equip the sets of infinite words
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
and bi-infinite words
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
with the product toplogy.
A
subshift
is a compact subset
X
of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
or
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
endowed with the
shift map

S:\mathcal{A}^{N}\to\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
or
S:\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. We refer to the subshift as
infinite
or
bi-infinite
.
A subshift
(X,S)
is
minimal
if every orbit is dense in
X
.
The
language
of a subshift
(X,S)
is the set
\mathcal{L}_{X}
of finite factors of elements of
X
.

An (additive)
eigenvalue
of a subshift
(X,S)
is a real number
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that there exists a function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\in L^{2}(\mu)
, where
\mu
is an
S
-invariant measure,
such that
f\circ S=f+t
. If
f
is continuous, we refer to

as a
continuous eigenvalue
.

Let
(X,S)
be a bi-infinite subshift. Then, we have an associated infinite subshift
(\tilde{X},S)

where elements of
\tilde{X}
are the right-infinite part of elements of
X
.
We have the following (see Proposition 2.1 in
[
BBY22
]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift.
Then,
(X,S)
and
(\tilde{X},S)
have the same continuous eigenvalues.

A subshift
(X,S)
is
linearly recurrent
if there exists a constant
K
such that
for every
x\in X
and for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
0<n<Kk
such that
x_{[0,k)}=x_{[n,n+k)}
.

2.3
Return words and coboundaries

A
return word
of a subshift
X
is a word
w
such that
ww_{0}\in\mathcal{L}_{X}
and
\left|w\right|_{w_{0}}=1
, where
w_{0}
denotes the first letter of
w
.
We refer to the subspace
R(X)
(or just
R
) of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
, generated by
\operatorname{ab}(w)
for return words
w
, as the
return space
.
The
return space on letter
a
is denoted
R_{a}(X)
and is the subspace of
R(X)
generated by return words on letter
a
.
A
coboundary
of a subshift
X
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a morphism
c:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

such that
c(w)=0
for every return word
w
.
Note that the data provided by such a morphism is equivalent to that of a map
\tilde{c}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

such that
\tilde{c}\circ\operatorname{ab}=c
. Throughout the following, we use the same notation to represent both maps.
A
real coboundary
of a subshift
X
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a linear form
c:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

c(\operatorname{ab}w)=0
for every return word
w
.
As for coboundaries, we can see a real coboundary as a map
\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift.
Any coboundary
c:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
has a representative
\tilde{c}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}
which is a real coboundary.

Proof.

Given a coboundary
c
, we construct a directed graph with edges labeled by
\mathcal{A}
and vertices in
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
,
as follows:
Start from vertex
0
and choose some letter
a\in\mathcal{L}_{X}
.
Add an edge
0\xrightarrow{a}c(a)
.
Choose a letter
b\in\mathcal{A}
such that
ab\in\mathcal{L}_{X}
, then add the edge
c(a)\xrightarrow{b}c(ab)
.
Choose a letter
c\in\mathcal{A}
such that
abc\in\mathcal{L}_{X}
, then add the edge
c(ab)\xrightarrow{c}c(abc)
.
Continue this process until every letter has been considered.

Note that each letter appears in the graph only once, and every word of
\mathcal{L}_{X}
starting by letter
a
corresponds to a path from
0
in the automaton.
Next, for each vertex of the graph, choose a representative in
\mathbb{R}
.
We define
\tilde{c}(a):=e-s
for each edge
s\xrightarrow{a}e
. Then
\tilde{c}
defines a real coboundary and
c=\tilde{c}
mod
\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

We call
coboundary space
of a subshift
(X,S)
the vector space of real coboundaries.
In other words, it is
R(X)^{\circ}
,
where
E^{\circ}=\{w\in E^{*}\mid\forall x\in E,\ wx=0\}
denotes the annihilator of
E
.
Note that it is completely determined by a graph that we call
coboundaries graph
with
d+1
vertices where
d
is the dimension of the coboundary space.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the periodic shift generated by the periodic word
(ab)^{\omega}
.
Then,
(1/2,1/2)
is a coboundary.
The corresponding graph is
0
1/2
a
b

Next, we obtain the linear form
(1/2,-1/2):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
that annihilates return words
\{ab,ba\}
.
The coboundary space is the one-dimensional subspace generated by this linear form, and the coboundaries graph is this graph.

Coboundaries naturally extend to infinite words:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift, and let
c:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
be a coboundary.
Then we can extend
c:X\cup\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
such that
c(x_{0})=c(x)-c(Sx)

for every
x\in X\cup\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of previous lemma, consider a coboundary graph associated with the coboundary
c
.
We define
c
on
X
by
c(x)=-v
where
v\xrightarrow{x_{0}}\cdot
is the unique arrow
labeled by letter
x_{0}
in the graph. Then we have
c(x_{0})=c(x)-c(Sx)
.
∎

Note that it also works with real coboundaries.
The extension of
c
to
X
is unique if we set the value
c(x)
at a point
x\in X
.

2.4
Matrices

A matrix
M\in M_{d}(\mathbb{N})
is

•

primitive
if there exists
n\geq 1
such that
M^{n}
has strictly positive coefficients,

•

irreducible
if its characteristic polynomial is irreducible,

•

unimodular
if its determinant is
\pm 1
,

•

pseudo-unimodular
if the product of its non-zero eigenvalues is
\pm 1
, counting multiplicities.

•

Pisot
if it has one eigenvalue
>1
and every other eigenvalue has a modulus strictly less than
1
.

We denote
M>0
if every coefficient of
M
is strictly positive.
We define the
pseudo-inverse
of a square matrix
M
as the matrix
M^{-1}
obtained by inversion
of every invertible Jordan block and taking the transpose of the other ones in the Jordan decomposition of the matrix.
When the matrix
M
is invertible, this is the usual inverse of the matrix.
In restriction to the eventual image
I=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M^{n}\mathbb{R}^{A}
, the matrix
\mathchoice{{M\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=1.27501pt}}_{\,I}}{{M\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=1.27501pt}}_{\,I}}{{M\,\smash{\vrule height=3.82668pt,depth=0.85472pt}}_{\,I}}{{M\,\smash{\vrule height=2.73334pt,depth=1.44028pt}}_{\,I}}
is invertible
and its inverse is the pseudo-inverse
\mathchoice{{M^{-1}\,\smash{\vrule height=6.91205pt,depth=1.27501pt}}_{\,I}}{{M^{-1}\,\smash{\vrule height=6.51204pt,depth=1.27501pt}}_{\,I}}{{M^{-1}\,\smash{\vrule height=4.99203pt,depth=0.85472pt}}_{\,I}}{{M^{-1}\,\smash{\vrule height=3.73334pt,depth=1.44028pt}}_{\,I}}
.

A number
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
is
Perron
if it is an algebraic integer
\beta>1
such that every Galois conjugate has modulus strictly less than
\beta
. It is
Pisot
if moreover Galois conjugates have modulus strictly less than
1
.

We have the following well-known theorem.

Theorem 2.5
.

(Perron-Frobenius)
If
M\in M_{d}(\mathbb{N})
is a primitive matrix, then its greatest eigenvalue is simple and is a Perron number.
Moreover, it has an associated eigenvector with strictly positive coordinates.

We call this greatest eigenvalue the
Perron eigenvalue
and an associated eigenvector is a
Perron eigenvector
.

For sequences of invertible matrices, we have the following.

Theorem 2.6

(Osseledet’s Theorem)
.

Let
\mu
be an ergodic invariant measure on the full shift
(\mathcal{M}^{\mathbb{N}},S)
, where
\mathcal{M}\subset GL_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is finite.
For almost every sequence
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
there exists
0<r\leq d
,
\theta_{1}>...>\theta_{r}
and subspaces
\mathbb{R}^{d}=E_{1}\supsetneqq...\supsetneqq E_{r-1}\supsetneqq E_{r}=\{0\}
such that

v\in E_{i}\backslash E_{i+1}\Longleftrightarrow\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left\lVert vM_{[0,n)}\right\rVert=\theta_{i}.

We call
\theta_{i}
a
Lyapunov exponent
of
(M_{n})
and
E_{i}
the corresponding
Lyapunov space
.

2.5
Substitution

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two alphabets.
A
word morphism
is a morphism
\sigma:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
for the concatenation of words.
It is
non erasing
if
\forall a\in\mathcal{A},\ \left|\sigma(a)\right|\geq 1
.
A
substitution
is a non-erasing word morphism.
We call
\mathcal{A}
the
domain alphabet
and
\mathcal{B}
the
codomain alphabet
of
\sigma:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
.
If both alphabets of a substitution
\sigma
are the same, we denote it
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
.

The
matrix
of a substitution
\sigma:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
is the unique matrix
M_{\sigma}
(or
\operatorname{ab}(\sigma)
) satisfying
\operatorname{ab}(\sigma(w))=M_{\sigma}\operatorname{ab}(w)
for every word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

For example, the matrix of the Tribonnacci substitution
a\mapsto ab,\ b\mapsto ac,\ c\mapsto a
is

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}
.

We say that a substitution is primitive, irreducible, Pisot, pseudo-unimodular, etc…
if its matrix has the corresponding property and if domain and codomain alphabets are the same.

The
language of a substitution

\sigma
is the set
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
of factors of
\sigma^{n}(a)
, for
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the domain alphabet of
\sigma
.
The infinite (resp. bi-infinite)
subshift of a substitution

\sigma
is the set of infinite (resp. bi-infinite) words
whose factors are in
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
.
Note that the language of a primitive substitution is the language of its subshift.

A
substitution subshift
is the subshift of a primitive substitution.
A
morphic subshift
is the subshift generated by the image by a substitution of a substitution subshift.

Let
X
be a subshift over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
,
and let
\sigma:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
be a morphism.
Let
Y
be the shift generated by
\sigma(X)
.
Every element of
Y
can be written in the form
S^{k}\sigma(x)
, with
x\in X
and
0\leq k<\left|\sigma(x_{0})\right|
.
We say that
\sigma
is
recognizable
in
X
if such a representation is unique for every element of
Y
.
We have the following (see
[
Mos96
]
Theorem 1).

Theorem 2.7

(Mossé)
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive aperiodic substitution.
Then
\sigma
is recognizable in the bi-infinite subshift
X_{\sigma}
.

Note that a primitive substitution is aperiodic if its Perron eigenvalue is not an integer by Remark
5.10
.

The
prefix-suffix automaton
of a substitution
\sigma:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
is a labeled directed graph
with vertices the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and with edges
a\xrightarrow{p,s}b

if and only if
\sigma(a)=pbs
.
The
prefix automaton
is the same automaton where labels
p,s
are replaced by
p
.
We denote
a_{n+1}\xrightarrow{p_{n}}a_{n}...\xrightarrow{p_{0}}a_{0}
if
a_{k+1}\xrightarrow{p_{k}}a_{k}
for every
0\leq k\leq n
.

Every element
x
of the bi-infinite subshift
X_{\sigma}
has a
prefix-suffix decomposition

...\sigma^{3}(p_{3})\sigma^{2}(p_{2})\sigma(p_{1})p_{0}\cdot a_{0}s_{0}\sigma(s_{1})\sigma^{2}(s_{2})\sigma^{3}(s_{3})...

This decomposition is not always equal to
x
since it may not define a bi-infinite word.
If
\sigma
is recognizable, such a decomposition is unique.
Thus, it permits to define maps
p_{n}:X_{\sigma}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

The following lemma gives a property of real coboundaries of a substitution subshift.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive substitution.
The coboundary space
R(X_{\sigma})^{\circ}
is included in the sum of generalized left-eigenspaces of
M_{\sigma}

for eigenvalues roots of unity and zero.

In particular, if
M_{\sigma}
has no eigenvalue root of unity nor zero, then
X_{\sigma}
has no non-trivial coboundary.

Proof.

We have
R^{\circ}M_{\sigma}\subseteq R^{\circ}
,
since the image by
\sigma
of a return word is a product of return words.
Let
c\in R^{\circ}
. Then
(cM_{\sigma}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.
Indeed, for every letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
\sigma^{n}(a)\in\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
, and
c(\mathcal{L}_{\sigma})
is bounded.
Thus,
c
is orthogonal to every generalized eigenspace of
M_{\sigma}
for an eigenvalue of modulus
>1
.
Moreover,
R
is rational, thus
R^{\circ}
is stable by field morphisms,
so it is orthogonal to every generalized eigenspace of
M_{\sigma}
for an eigenvalue not root of unity nor zero.
∎

2.6
Directive sequences

A
directive sequence
is an infinite sequence
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of substitutions
such that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sigma_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*}
(i.e. domains and codomains are compatible to compose
\sigma_{n}\sigma_{n+1}
).
We use the standard notation
\sigma_{[i,j)}=\sigma_{i}...\sigma_{j-1}
.

A directive sequence is
finitary
if its set of substitutions is finite.
We say that a directive sequence
\bm{\sigma}
is
primitive
if

\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\exists n\in\mathbb{N}
,
M_{[k,k+n)}>0
.
It is
strongly primitive
if
\exists n\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall k\in\mathbb{N},\ M_{[k,k+n)}>0
.

The
language of a directive sequence

\bm{\sigma}
is the set
\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\sigma}}
of factors of
\sigma_{[0,n)}(a)
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
The infinite (resp. bi-infinite)
subshift of a directive sequence

\bm{\sigma}
is the set
X_{\sigma}
of infinite (resp. bi-infinite) words whose factors are in the language of
\bm{\sigma}
.

We denote
S:\mathcal{S}^{N}\to\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{N}}
the shift of directive sequences over substitutions
\mathcal{S}
.
We denote
X_{\bm{\sigma}}^{(n)}
(or just
X^{(n)}
) the subshift
X_{S^{n}\bm{\sigma}}
.
Idem for
\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\sigma}}^{(n)}
.

A directive sequence
\bm{\sigma}
is
recognizable
if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sigma_{n}
is recognizable in
X^{(n+1)}
.
Note that if substitutions of
\bm{\sigma}
are unimodular and
X_{\bm{\sigma}}
is aperiodic,
then
\bm{\sigma}
is recognizable by Theorem 3.1 in
[
BSTY19
]
.

The
prefix-suffix diagram
is a natural generalization of prefix-suffix automaton to directive sequences,
but it is no longer an automaton; instead it is a Bratelli diagram with vertices
\mathcal{A}_{n}
at level
n

(alphabets are considered to be disjoint)
and edges
a_{n+1}\xrightarrow{p_{n},s_{n}}a_{n}
if and only if
\sigma_{n}(a_{n+1})=p_{n}a_{n}s_{n}
.
The
prefix diagram
is identical but with only
p_{n}
on labels of edges.
Every element of
X_{\bm{\sigma}}
admits a
prefix-suffix decomposition

..\sigma_{[0,3)}(p_{3})\sigma_{[0,2)}(p_{2})\sigma_{0}(p_{1})p_{0}\cdot a_{0}s_{0}\sigma_{0}(s_{1})\sigma_{[0,2)}(s_{2})\sigma_{[0,3)}(s_{3})...

where
...\xrightarrow{p_{n},s_{n}}a_{n}...\xrightarrow{p_{0},s_{0}}a_{0}
is an infinite path
in the prefix-suffix diagram.
But this decomposition is not always equal to the original bi-infinite word: if
(p_{n})
or
(s_{n})
are ultimately constant to
\epsilon
, then it does not gives a bi-infinite word. See
[
CS01
]
for more details.
When the directive sequence
\bm{\sigma}
is recognizable, such decomposition is unique so maps
p_{n}:X^{(n)}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}^{*}

and
a_{n}:X^{(n)}\to\mathcal{A}_{n}
are well-defined.

3
A general sufficient condition to be an eigenvalue

We give a general sufficient condition for a number to be an eigenvalue of a subshift.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift over a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\varphi:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
be a morphism such that
\varphi(\mathcal{L}_{X})
is bounded
and such that
\forall a,b\in\mathcal{A}
,
\varphi(a)=\varphi(b)
mod
1
.
Then,
\varphi(a)
is a continuous eigenvalue of
(X,S)
.

Note that this proposition can be reformulated with
\varphi
replaced by a linear form of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
, with the image of
\operatorname{ab}(\mathcal{L}_{X})
bounded.
And the hypothesis on
\varphi
can be replaced by
\varphi(W(x))
is bounded for one
x\in X
.

To prove the proposition, we need the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X
be a minimal shift over a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\varphi\in(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}})^{*}
be a linear form such that
\varphi(\mathcal{L}_{X})
is bounded.
Let
x\in X
.
Then, the map defined by
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f(S^{n}x)=\varphi(\operatorname{ab}x_{[0,n)})

can be extended by continuity to
X
.

Proof.

If
x=w^{\omega}
is periodic, then the hypothesis give
\varphi(\operatorname{ab}w)=0
, so the map
f
is well-defined.
If the shift
X
is periodic, then it is equal to an orbit and the result is obvious.
Otherwise, we follow the proof of Lemma 8.2.5 in
[
MA20
]
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
t=\varphi(a)\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
x\in X
.
We define a map

mod

.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
(X,S)
be a minimal subshift. Let

.
If

is at bounded distance of a line, then coordinates of the direction vector of sum

are
continuous eigenvalues of

.

In other words if the discrepancy is bounded (see

]
for a definition), then frequencies of letters are

Example 3.4
.

Let

or let

Corollary 3.5

(Pisot morphic subshift)
.

Let
\sigma
be a Pisot substitution and
\tau
be any substitution.
Let

be a Perron eigenvector of

\frac{\operatorname{ab}(\tau)v}{\left\lVert\operatorname{ab}(\tau)v\right\rVert_{1}}

are continuous eigenvalues of the subshift
(X_{\tau\sigma^{\omega}},S)
.

In other words, frequencies of letters are eigenvalues for such subshifts.
This result is due to Host (see (6.2) in
[

]
) in the particular case where

Proof.

Let
f_{a}
be the coordinate of
\frac{\operatorname{ab}(\tau)v}{\left\lVert\operatorname{ab}(\tau)v\right\rVert_{1}}
for letter
a
, which is in fact the frequency of
a
.
Let
\phi=f_{a}(1,...,1)-e_{a}
.
Let us show that
\phi(\operatorname{ab}\mathcal{L}_{\tau\sigma^{\omega}})
is bounded.
It is equivalent to show that
\phi(W(u))
is bounded for some

.
We take for example the image by
\tau
of a periodic point of
\sigma
.
Elements of

are of the form

\operatorname{ab}(p_{0})+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}M_{\tau}M_{\sigma}^{n}\operatorname{ab}(p_{n+1})

for a path

.
Thanks to the hypothesis, we have

,
since
\phi M_{\tau}M_{\sigma}^{n}v=0
,
and this convergence is exponentially fast.
Thus,

is uniformly bounded.
∎

Corollary 3.6

(
\mathcal{S}
-adic with coboundary)
.

Let
\bm{\sigma}

4
Eigenvalues of
\mathcal{S}
-adic subshifts

This section aims to characterize continuous eigenvalues of
\mathcal{S}
-adic subshift using coboundaries.

4.1
Main results

The two following theorems partially extends the result of Host for a single substitution (see
[
Hos86
]
Theorem (1.4)).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\bm{\sigma}
be a finitary primitive directive sequence. If
t\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is a continuous eigenvalue of
(X_{\bm{\sigma}},S)
,
then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and a linear form
c:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
c(\operatorname{ab}w)\in\mathbb{Z}
for every return word
w
of
\mathcal{L}^{k}
and
such that

(t(1,...,1)M_{[0,k)}+c)M_{[k,n)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Note that such a map
c
give a coboundary of
X_{S^{k}\bm{\sigma}}
.
If we remove the finitary hypothesis and replace it by
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right|<\infty
, then we still have the conclusion but with a convergence modulo
1
.

If the speed of convergence is fast enough, we have the reciprocal,
which is a generalization of Theorem 4.8 and Theorem 4.12 in
[
BBY22
]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\bm{\sigma}
be a finitary, primitive and recognizable directive sequence.
If there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a linear form
c:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
c(\operatorname{ab}w)\in\mathbb{Z}
for every return word
w
of
\mathcal{L}^{k}
and
such that

\sum_{n\geq k}(t(1,...,1)M_{[0,k)}+c)M_{[k,n)}

converges, then
t
is a continuous eigenvalue of
(X_{\bm{\sigma}},S)
.

Remark 4.3
.

In Theorem
4.2
, if the substitution
\sigma_{[0,k)}
is unimodular,
then the conclusion of the theorem holds without the recognizability hypothesis.
Indeed,
c
can be decomposed as the sum of a real coboundary
\tilde{c}
and an integer row vector
w
by Lemma
2.2
, and
\tilde{c}M_{[0,k)^{-1}}(\mathcal{L}_{X})
is bounded and
wM_{[0,k)}^{-1}
is an integer row vector, thus hypotheses of Corollary
3.6
are satisfied with
\pi=t(1...1)+wM_{[0,k)}^{-1}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Since
\bm{\sigma}
is recognizable, the maps
p_{n}:X_{\bm{\sigma}}\to\mathcal{L}^{(n)}
are well-defined and continuous.
Then, we define a continuous map
f:X_{\bm{\sigma}}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
by

f=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}t(1...1)M_{[0,n)}\operatorname{ab}p_{n}+\sum_{n\geq k}(t(1...1)M_{[0,k)}+c)\operatorname{ab}\sigma_{[k,n)}p_{n}+c(\theta_{k}),

where the last occurrence of
c
is a map
c:X_{S^{k}\bm{\sigma}}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
such that
c(Sx)+c(x_{0})=c(x)
,
and
\theta_{k}:X_{\bm{\sigma}}\to X_{S^{k}\bm{\sigma}}
is the map such that
\sigma_{[0,k)}(\theta_{k}(x))=\sigma_{[0,k-1)}(p_{k-1}(x))...\sigma_{0}(p_{1}(x))p_{0}(x)x
.
In other words,
\theta_{k}(x)
is the word obtained after
k
steps of desubstitution from
x
.
The map
f
is continuous since the series is normally convergent.
Let us show that
f
is an eigenfunction for the eigenvalue
t
.
Let
x\in X_{\bm{\sigma}}
be such that
p_{n}(x)=\epsilon
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
\bm{\sigma}
is primitive,
(X_{\bm{\sigma}},S)
is minimal, and
it is enough to prove the equality
f\circ S=f+t
on the orbit of
x
.
Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists
N\geq k
such that
p_{n}(S^{m}x)=\epsilon
for every
n\geq N
,
thus

\displaystyle f(S^{m}x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k}t(1...1)\operatorname{ab}\sigma_{[0,n)}\operatorname{ab}p_{n}(S^{m}x)+

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{n=k}^{N}(t(1...1)\operatorname{ab}(\sigma_{[0,k)})+c)\operatorname{ab}\sigma_{[k,n)}p_{n}(S^{m}x)+c\theta_{k}(S^{m}x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t(1...1)\operatorname{ab}(\sigma_{[0,N)}(p_{N})...\sigma_{0}(p_{1})p_{0})+

\displaystyle\quad c\operatorname{ab}(\sigma_{[k,N)}(p_{N})...\sigma_{k}(p_{k+1})p_{k})+c\theta_{k}(S^{m}x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle mt+c(\theta_{k}(x))=f(x)+mt.

This before last equality comes from the relations

\sigma_{[0,N)}(p_{N}(S^{m}x))...\sigma_{0}(p_{1}(S^{m}x))p_{0}(S^{m}x)S^{m}x=x,

\sigma_{[k,N)}(p_{N}(S^{m}x))...\sigma_{k}(p_{k+1}(S^{m}x))p_{k}(S^{m}x)\theta_{k}(S^{m}x)=\theta_{k}(x).

∎

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we need to introduce some important notions such as nested sequences.

4.2
Nested sequences

In this subsection, we consider subshifts over infinite words.
Let
\bm{\sigma}
be a directive sequence over alphabets
(\mathcal{A}_{n})
.
In this subsection we consider only first letters in images of letters,
so we can assume that substitutions of
\bm{\sigma}
have constant lengths
1
.
A
nested sequence
is a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Pi_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{A}_{n}
of letters such that

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ \sigma_{n}(a_{n+1})=a_{n}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{\sigma}}
(or just
\mathcal{N}
) the set of nested sequences.

Lemma 4.4
.

If alphabets
\mathcal{A}_{n}
are non-empty, then
\mathcal{N}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We assume that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\sigma_{n}
has length one, up to replace
\sigma_{n}(a)
by its first letter for every
a\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
The intersection
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sigma_{[0,n)}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
is non-empty since
(\sigma_{[0,n)}(\mathcal{A}_{n}))
is a decreasing sequence of non-empty subsets of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. We then construct a nested sequence
(a_{n})
by induction: if
a_{n}\in\bigcap_{k\geq n}\sigma_{[n,k)}(\mathcal{A}_{k})
, then we can find
a_{n+1}\in\bigcap_{k\geq n+1}\sigma_{[n+1,k)}(\mathcal{A}_{k})
such that
\sigma_{n}(a_{n+1})=a_{n}
.
∎

The following tells us that nested sequences are almost pairwise disjoint.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
are two different nested sequences,
then

\exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall n\geq n_{0},\ a_{n}\neq b_{n}.

Proof.

If
a_{n_{0}}=b_{n_{0}}
, then for every
n\leq n_{0}
,
a_{n}=b_{n}
. Thus, if the conclusion was not true, then the two nested sequences would be equal.
∎

We deduce the following.

Lemma 4.6
.

\left|\mathcal{N}\right|\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right|.

We assume in the following that alphabets
\mathcal{A}_{n}
are disjoints.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{B}_{k}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}_{k}\mid\forall(a_{n})\in\mathcal{N},\ \sigma_{[0,k)}(a)\not\in[a_{0}]\}
.
This set corresponds to letters that never reach a nested sequence.
We have the following.

Lemma 4.7
.

\exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall n\geq n_{0},\ \mathcal{B}_{n}=\emptyset.

Proof.

If
a\in\mathcal{B}_{n+1}
, then the first letter of
\sigma_{n}(a)
is in
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
Thus, we can define
\tau_{n}:\mathcal{B}_{n+1}\to\mathcal{B}_{n}
such that
\tau_{n}(a)
is the first letter of
\sigma_{n}(a)
.
By definition of the sets
\mathcal{B}_{n}
,
(\tau_{n})
has no nested sequence.
Thus, by Lemma
4.4
,
\exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{N},\ \mathcal{B}_{n_{0}}=\emptyset
.
Then, for every
n\geq n_{0}
,
\mathcal{B}_{n}=\emptyset
since
\tau_{[n_{0},n)}(\mathcal{B}_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n_{0}}
.
∎

We deduce the following.

Lemma 4.8
.

There exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and a map
\alpha:\bigcup_{n\geq n_{0}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\to\mathcal{N}

such that
\forall n\geq n_{0},\ \forall a\in\mathcal{A}_{n},\ \sigma_{[0,n)}(a)\in[\alpha(a)_{0}]
.

4.3
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Let
\bm{\sigma}
be a primitive directive sequence with
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right|<\infty
.
Thanks to Proposition
2.1
, we can work only with right-infinite subshifts as in previous subsection.

Lemma 4.9
.

For every nested sequence
\bm{a}=(a_{n})\in\mathcal{N}
,
(\sigma_{[0,n)}(a_{n}))
is a nested sequence of words that converges to an infinite word

.

Let
f
be a continuous eigenfunction associated with an eigenvalue

.
Let

\mathcal{F}=\left\{f(w_{\bm{a}})\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\mid\bm{a}\in\mathcal{N}\right\}.

By Lemma
4.6
, the set
\mathcal{F}
is finite.
Let
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon<\min\{\left|x-y\right|\mid x\neq y\in\mathcal{F}\}
.
Let
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

\forall\bm{a}\in\mathcal{N},\ \left|f\left(\left[\sigma_{[0,n_{1})}(a_{n_{1}})\right]\right)-f(w_{\bm{a}})\right|\leq\epsilon/4.

Then, let

and

be given by Lemma
4.8
for the directive sequence

.
We have

\displaystyle\forall n\geq n_{0},\ \forall x\in X_{\bm{\sigma}}^{(n)},\ \left|f(\sigma_{[0,n)}(x))-v_{x_{0}}\right|\leq\epsilon/4,

(1)

where we denote

.

\forall n\geq n_{0},\ \forall ab\in\mathcal{L}^{(n)},\ \left|th_{n}(a)+v_{a}-v_{b}\right|\leq\epsilon/2,

where

Proof.

For every

∎

We define
c_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

.
It is well defined. Indeed, if

.
And if
w

\mathcal{L}_{n}
, then clearly

since we have

. Thus,

.

Lemma 4.11
.

For every
n\geq n_{0}
, we have
c_{n+1}=c_{n}\circ\sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

Let

.
We have
c_{n+1}(a)=v_{a}-v_{b}
.
Let
a_{0}
and

be respectively the first letters of
\sigma_{n}(a)
and
\sigma_{n}(b)
.
We have
\sigma_{n}(a)b_{0}\in\mathcal{L}^{(n)}

.
Now, by definition we have

c_{n+1}(a)=c_{n}(\sigma_{n}(a))
.
∎

Since
\epsilon>0
could be chosen arbitrarily small, Lemma
4.10
gives

t(1...1)M_{[0,n)}+c_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\text{ mod }1.

Thus, by Lemma

we get
(t(1...1)M_{[0,n_{0})}+c_{n_{0}})M_{[n_{0},n)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\text{ mod }1
.

Now we need the following easy generalization of Lemma 1 in
[
Hos86
]
.
It is where we use the finitary hypothesis.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in M_{d_{n},d_{n+1}}(\mathbb{Z})
with bounded coefficients and with
\forall n\in\mathbb{N},d_{n}\geq 1
. If
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
is such that

.
Then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

(vM_{[0,k)}-w)M_{[k,n)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
.

For every

, let

.

∎

Let
\tilde{c}_{n_{0}}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}_{n_{0}}}\to\mathbb{R}

.

Recall that a

.
In other words, it is the subshift generated by

5.1
Characterization of eigenvalues of morphic subshifts

The following result allows us to compute eigenvalues of morphic subshifts.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive aperiodic substitution, and let
\tau
be a word morphism recognizable in

.
Then, the set of eigenvalues of
(X_{\tau\sigma^{\omega}},S)
is

\left(W\cap\Delta_{M_{\sigma}}\right)v

where

•

for an eigenvalue of modulus
\geq 1
, annihilated by coboundaries (for example the Perron eigenvector) and normalized such that
(1...1)M_{\tau}v=1

•

\Delta_{M_{\sigma}}=\{w\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}\mid\exists n\in\mathbb{N},\ wM_{\sigma}^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}\}

5.2
The algorithm

Let
\sigma
and
\tau
be two substitutions with
\sigma
primitive aperiodic and
\tau
recognizable in
X_{\sigma}
.
The algorithm to compute eigenvalues of
(X_{\tau\sigma^{\omega}},S)
is the following.

•

Compute the coboundary space
R^{\circ}
of
X_{\sigma}
. See Subsection
5.3
for more details.

•

Let
C
be the matrix with first row
(1...1)M_{\tau}
and other rows with a basis of the coboundary space
R^{\circ}
.
Let
d
be the number of rows of
C
.

•

Compute a matrix
V
whose columns are bases of generalized eigenspaces of
M_{\sigma}

for eigenvalues of modulus
\geq 1
, with coordinates in the algebraic field
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.

•

Echelon the system
xCV=wV
with unknowns
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
w
is a row vector.
It gives a matrix whose columns are linear combination of columns of
V
and whose left-kernel
intersected with
\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}
gives
W
.
Decompose each columns in a basis of a common number field of coefficients.
It gives a rational matrix whose left-kernel is
W
.
Then we can compute a basis of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
W
.

•

Compute the Perron eigenvector
v
of
M_{\sigma}
such that
(1...1)M_{\tau}v=1
.

•

Then, the set of eigenvalues is

(W\cap\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}M_{\sigma}^{-k})v

where
M_{\sigma}^{-1}
is the pseudo-inverse of
M_{\sigma}
.

Note that we may replace the Perron eigenvector
v
by any generalized eigenvector annihilated by coboundaries and such that

(1...1)M_{\tau}v=1
.
Note that in general, the set
(W\cap\Delta_{M_{\sigma}})v
is not equal to
Wv\cap\Delta_{M_{\sigma}}v
since
W
has no reason to be
M_{\sigma}
-invariant.
However, in many situations, the set of eigenvalues can be made more explicit:

•

If
\sigma
is pseudo-unimodular (i.e. the product of non-zero eigenvalues is
\pm 1
), then the set of eigenvalues is
(W\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}})v
.

Indeed, by Lemma 6.4 in
[
Mer23
]
, if
M_{\sigma}
is pseudo-unimodular then
\Delta_{M_{\sigma}}
is equal to

\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}
modulo the generalized eigenspace for the eigenvalue
0
.

•

If the matrix
CV
is invertible, or more generally if
WM_{\sigma}=W
,
and if
v
is an eigenvector for an eigenvalue
\beta
,
then the set of eigenvalues is

\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{\beta}\right](W\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}})v.

Indeed, we have

\left(W\cap\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}M_{\sigma}^{-n}\right)v=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(WM_{\sigma}^{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}})v/\beta^{n}.

•

If
(1...1)M_{\tau}
is a left-eigenvector of
M_{\sigma}
for an eigenvalue
\beta
, then we fall into the previous case. Indeed, it gives that
C
and
CM_{\sigma}
have same row space
modulo the generalized left-eigenspace of
M_{\sigma}
for the eigenvalue
0
and we have

xCV=wV\Leftrightarrow(xC-w)M_{\sigma}V=0\Leftrightarrow xCM_{\sigma}V=wM_{\sigma}V
, so
WM_{\sigma}=W
.

Note that when
\tau=\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}
, it corresponds to the constant length case.

•

If there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
M_{\sigma}^{n}=0
mod
\det(M_{\sigma})
.
Then, the set of eigenvalues is

\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{\det(M_{\sigma})}\right](W\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}})v.

Indeed, under this hypothesis we have
\Delta_{M}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{\det(M_{\sigma})^{n}}\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}}
.

See Section
7
for examples of computations of eigenvalues.

5.3
Computation of coboundaries

The following lemma permits to reduce the computation of the return space to the one of return space on only one letter,
if we want to compute coboundaries only modulo the generalized left eigenspace
E_{0}
for the eigenvalue
0
.
It is indeed what we need in the computation of eigenvalues of morphic subshifts since
E_{0}V=0
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive substitution over alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and let
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
Let
R
be the return space and
R_{a}
be the return space on letter
a
.
Let
I=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M_{\sigma}^{n}\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{A}}
.
Then

I\cap R=I\cap R_{a}\quad\text{ and }\quad R^{\circ}+E_{0}=R_{a}^{\circ}+E_{0},

where
E_{0}=I^{\circ}
is the generalized left-eigenspace of
M_{\sigma}
for the eigenvalue
0
.

Proof.

The image of a return word by a substitution is a product of return words. Thus
M_{\sigma}R\subseteq R
.
Since
\mathchoice{{M_{\sigma}\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=1.27501pt}}_{\,I}}{{M_{\sigma}\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=1.27501pt}}_{\,I}}{{M_{\sigma}\,\smash{\vrule height=3.82668pt,depth=0.85472pt}}_{\,I}}{{M_{\sigma}\,\smash{\vrule height=2.73334pt,depth=1.44028pt}}_{\,I}}
is invertible, we have
M_{\sigma}(R\cap I)=R\cap I
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
M_{\sigma}^{k}>0
.
Let
(w)_{w\in J}
be a family of return words such that
(\operatorname{ab}w)_{w\in J}
is a basis of
R\cap I
.
Then,
(M_{\sigma}^{k}\operatorname{ab}w)_{w\in J}
is also a basis of
R\cap I
.
Let
w\in J
. Let
p,s\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
such that
\sigma^{k}(w_{0})=pas
.
The word
\sigma^{k}(ww_{0})
is in the language of
\sigma
, so
as\sigma^{k}(Sw)p
is a product of return word on letter
a
.
Moreover,
\operatorname{ab}(as\sigma^{k}(Sw)p)=\operatorname{ab}(\sigma^{k}(w))
.
Thus
R\cap I\subseteq R_{a}\cap I
. The other inclusion is obvious.
Then, we have
R^{\circ}+E_{0}=(R\cap I)^{\circ}=(R_{a}\cap I)^{\circ}=R_{a}^{\circ}+E_{0}
.
∎

The computation of the set
\mathcal{R}_{a}
of return words on letter
a
can be done by computing a substitution
called return substitution and whose alphabet is the set of return words on letter
a
.
This is done in the next subsection.

Remark 5.4
.

The coboundary space can be computed more efficiently by computing the coboundary graph,
that is the largest invariant oriented graph
with edges labeled by letters where each letter appears exactly once.

5.4
Return substitution

The notion of return substitution comes from F. Durand (see
[
Dur98
]
).
We recall the definition and how to compute it even if it is well-know, in order to have a complete algorithm.
Let
\sigma
be a primitive substitution, and let
a\in\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
be a letter such that
\sigma(a)=a
.
Note that such a letter always exists up to replacing
\sigma
with a power of itself.
The
return substitution
on letter
a
is denoted as
\sigma_{a}
or
\partial_{a}\sigma
.
Its alphabet is the set
\mathcal{R}_{a}
of return words on letter
a
, and its satisfies

\sigma_{a}\circ\varphi_{a}=\varphi_{a}\circ\sigma
, where
\varphi_{a}:\mathcal{R}_{a}\to\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}^{*}

maps an element of
\mathcal{R}_{a}
to the corresponding word.
We can also extend this definition in an obvious manner:
if
w
is a prefix of a fixed point of
\sigma
, then we the define the return substitution
\sigma_{w}
accordingly.

We can compute the return substitution with the following algorithm:

•

Compute
\sigma^{n}(a)
from
n=1
and up to find a first return word on letter
a
.

•

Take the image of a return word
r
by
\sigma
: it is a product of return words.
We deduce what is
\sigma_{a}(r)
.

•

Continue the previous step with every new return word found.

When this algorithm stops, then we have found every return word on letter
a
.
Indeed, if
x
is the fixed point of
\sigma
starting by letter
a

and if
A
is such that
\varphi_{a}(A)
is the first return word found,
then
x
is equal to
\varphi_{a}(y)
where
y
is the fixed point of
\sigma_{a}
starting by letter
A
.
Since
\sigma
is primitive, every return word appears in
x
, thus the alphabet of
\sigma_{a}

is the set of return words on letter
a
.

Example 5.5
.

Consider the Thue-Morse substitution

\sigma:a\mapsto ab,\ b\mapsto ba.

If we compute
\sigma^{2}(a)=abba
, we see that
abb
is a return word.
Then, we compute
\sigma(abb)=abbaba
. We found two new return words:
\{ab,a\}
.
We compute
\sigma(ab)=abba
and
\sigma(a)=ab
. There are no more return words.
Thus the return substitution is

\sigma_{a}:A\mapsto ABC,\ B\mapsto AC,\ C\mapsto B

with
\varphi_{a}:A\mapsto abb,\ B\mapsto ab,\ C\mapsto a
.

5.5
Deciding aperiodicity

Let
\sigma
be a primitive substitution.
Using return substitutions, we can provide an algorithm to decide if
(X_{\sigma},S)
is periodic.
The fact that aperiodicity is decidable is already well-know, but we give an algorithm for completeness.

Assume that
\sigma(a)=a
, up to replacing
\sigma
by a power of itself.
We define the
n
-th derivative of
\sigma
for the letter
a
by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
:
if
n=0
,
\partial_{a}^{n}\sigma=\sigma
; and if
n>0
,
\partial_{a}^{n}=\partial\partial_{a}^{n-1}\sigma
,
where the return substitution is taken for the first letter, ensuring that the fixed point with this first letter is mapped
to the fixed point of the original substitution starting with
a
.

Lemma 5.6
.

(X_{\sigma},S)
is periodic if and only if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that the alphabet of
\partial_{a}^{n}\sigma

has only one letter.

Proof.

Since
\sigma
is primitive, the shift is periodic if and only if the fixed point
x
starting by letter
a

is periodic.
And a word is periodic if and only if the sequence of its derivatives (i.e. rewriting the word as product of its return words on first letter) becomes constant to the word with only one letter.
∎

Such criterion can be algorithmically tested, since the sequence
\partial_{a}^{n}\sigma
is ultimately periodic
thank to the following proposition (see Proposition 7.6.7 in
[
DP22
]
), and since
\partial^{n}\sigma
is of the form
\sigma_{p}
for some prefix
p
of a fixed point of
\sigma
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
x
be a fixed point of a primitive substitution
\sigma
.
Then the set
\{\sigma_{w}\mid w\text{ prefix of }x\}
is finite.

Example 5.8
.

Consider the Thue-Morse substitution

\sigma:a\mapsto ab,\ b\mapsto ba.

Its consecutive return substitutions on letter
a
are

\displaystyle\partial_{a}\sigma:

\displaystyle A\mapsto ABC,\ B\to AC,\ C\mapsto B

\displaystyle\partial_{a}^{2}\sigma:

\displaystyle 0\mapsto 01,\ 1\to 23,\ 2\mapsto 013,\ 3\mapsto 2

Then, we have
\partial_{a}^{3}\sigma=\partial_{a}^{2}\sigma
.
We conclude that
(X_{\sigma},S)
is aperiodic.

Example 5.9
.

Consider the substitution
\sigma:a\mapsto ab\ b\mapsto c\ c\mapsto abc
.
Its derivative is
\partial_{a}\sigma:A\mapsto AA
thus
(X_{\sigma},S)
is periodic.

Remark 5.10
.

If
\sigma
is primitive periodic, then its Perron eigenvalue is an integer.
Indeed, if
X_{\sigma}
is of the form
\bigcup_{k=0}^{\left|w\right|-1}S^{k}w^{\omega}
, then
\operatorname{ab}(w)
is a Perron eigenvector of
M_{\sigma}

since it is the limit of
\left|w\right|\operatorname{ab}(\sigma^{n}(w_{0}))/\left|\sigma^{n}(w_{0})\right|
.

5.6
Semi-deciding recognizability

Given a substitution
\sigma
and a morphism
\tau
, the fact that
\tau
is recognizable in
X_{\sigma}

is semi-decidable thank to the following.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive substitution and
\tau
be a word morphism. Let
X_{\sigma}
be the bi-infinite shift associated with
\sigma
.
The substitution
\tau
is recognizable in
X_{\sigma}
if and only if

\begin{split}\exists\epsilon>0,\ \forall x,y\in X_{\sigma},\ \forall 0\leq p<\left|\tau(x_{0})\right|,\ \forall 0\leq q<\left|\tau(y_{0})\right|,\\
d(S^{p}\tau(x),S^{q}\tau(y))\leq\epsilon\ \Longrightarrow\ p=q\text{ and }x_{0}=y_{0}.\end{split}

In other words, we can uniquely desubstitute if we know the bi-infinite word in a large enough window.
It permits to semi-decide the recognizability of
\tau
in
X_{\sigma}
since
for each
\epsilon>0
there are only a finite number of possibilities to test.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be as in the last statement.
Let us prove that
\tau
is recognizable in
X_{\sigma}
.
Let
x
,
y\in X_{\sigma}
,
0\leq p<\left|\tau(x_{0})\right|
and
0\leq q<\left|\tau(y_{0})\right|
be such that

S^{p}\tau(x)=S^{q}\tau(y)
.
We have
d(S^{p}\tau(x),S^{q}\tau(y))=0<\epsilon

so
p=q
and
x_{0}=y_{0}
.
Then, if we shift
x
and
y
,
we have
d(\tau(Sx),\tau(Sy))=0
, thus
x_{1}=y_{1}
.
And we can also shift in the other direction:
we have
d(S^{-1}\tau(x),S^{-1}\tau(y))=0
, thus
x_{-1}=y_{-1}
.
By induction, we get that
x=y
. Thus
\tau
is recognizable in
X_{\sigma}
.

Reciprocally, assume that such
\epsilon>0
does not exists.
For each
\epsilon=\frac{1}{n}>0
it gives
x^{(n)}
and
y^{(n)}\in X_{\sigma}
,
0\leq p_{n}<\left|\tau(x^{(n)}_{0})\right|

and
0\leq q_{n}<\left|\tau(y^{(n)}_{0})\right|
such that

d(S^{p_{n}}\tau(x^{(n)}),S^{q_{n}}\tau(y^{(n)}))<1/n
and (
p_{n}\neq q_{n}
or
x^{(n)}_{0}\neq y^{(n)}_{0}
).
Then, by compacity there exists subsequences
x^{(\phi(n))}
and
y^{(\phi(n))}
that converges and
such that
x_{0}^{(\phi(n))}
,
y_{0}^{(\phi(n))}
,
p_{\phi(n)}
and
q_{\phi(n)}
are constant to
a
,
b
,
p
and
q
respectively, with either
a\neq b
or
p\neq q

x=\lim_{n\to\infty}x^{(\phi(n))}
and
y=\lim_{n\to\infty}y^{(\phi(n))}

We have

since

and (
p\neq q

.
∎

6
A criterion for weak mixing

We use our description of the set of eigenvalues to deduce a characterization of the dimension of the set of eigenvalues.
Moreover, we provide a necessary and sufficient condition ensuring weak mixing,
for primitive unimodular substitutions without coboundary.
First, we need to introduce some notations.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a set of algebraic integers.
We denote
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathcal{B})
the Galois group of
\mathbb{K}_{\mathcal{B}}:\mathbb{Q}
,
where
\mathbb{K}_{\mathcal{B}}
is the splitting field of the product of minimal polynomials of elements of
\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a set of algebraic integers.
We define the
graph
of
\mathcal{B}
by

•

vertices that are elements of
\mathcal{B}
and their conjugates,

•

edges between
\gamma
and
\delta
if and only if there exists
f\in\operatorname{Gal}(\mathcal{B})

such that
f(\gamma)\in\mathcal{B}
and
f(\delta)\in\mathcal{B}
.

We say that
\mathcal{B}
is
connected
if its graph is connected.

Note that if
\beta
has degree
d
and
\operatorname{Gal}(\{\beta\})
contains a
d
-cycle
f
, then
\{\beta,f(\beta)\}
is connected.
For example, if the degree of
\beta
is a prime number and
\gamma
is a conjugate of
\beta
, then
\{\beta,\gamma\}
is connected.

Note that if
\mathcal{B}
contains more than half of the conjugates of an algebraic integer, then
it is connected. For example, if
\beta
is a Salem number, its set of conjugates of modulus
\geq 1
is connected.

Note that if
\mathcal{B}
is connected, then any set of algebraic integers containing
\mathcal{B}
is connected.

Theorem 6.2

(Criterion for weak mixing)
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive aperiodic unimodular substitution with no non-trivial coboundary.
Let
\tau
be a word morphism recognizable in
X_{\sigma}
, and such that
\gcd((1...1)M_{\tau})=1
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of eigenvalues of
M_{\sigma}
of modulus greater than or equal to
1

associated with at least one generalized eigenvector
v
with
(1...1)M_{\tau}v\neq 0
.
Then, the subshift
(X_{\tau\sigma^{\omega}},S)
is weakly mixing if and only if
\mathcal{B}
is connected.

This criterion is a consequence of the following theorem that characterizes the dimension of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space spanned by eigenvalues of the subshift.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive aperiodic substitution, and let
\tau
be a word morphism recognizable in
X_{\sigma}
.
Let
\pi_{0}\pi_{1}...\pi_{n}
be the decomposition of the minimal polynomial of
M_{\sigma}
with

\pi_{0}
the product of all cyclotomic factors, and with
\pi_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
,
the remaining powers of irreducible factors in
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let

\mathcal{B}=\{\beta\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\mid\exists i\in\{0,...,n\},\ (1...1)M_{\tau}G_{i}\neq\{0\},\ \pi_{i}(\beta)=0\text{ and }\left|\beta\right|\geq 1\},

where
G_{0}=R(X_{\sigma})\cap\ker\pi_{0}(M_{\sigma})
, and
\forall i\geq 1
,
G_{i}=\ker\pi_{i}(M_{\sigma})
.

Then, the dimension of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space spanned by the eigenvalues of the subshift
(X_{\tau\sigma^{\omega}},S)

is the number of connected components of the graph of
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Assume that
\mathcal{B}
is connected.
Since
\sigma
is unimodular, the set of eigenvalues of the subshift is of the form
(W\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}})v_{0}
,
where
v_{0}
is the Perron eigenvector normalized such that
(1...1)M_{\tau}v_{0}=1
.
By Theorem
6.3
and Lemma
6.5
, the dimension of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
W
is
1
.
Thanks to the hypothesis
\gcd((1...1)M_{\tau})=1
, we deduce that
W\cap\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{A}}=\mathbb{Z}(1...1)M_{\tau}
.
Thus the set of eigenvalues of the subshift is
\mathbb{Z}
.

Reciprocally, if the subshift is weakly mixing,
then the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space spanned by its eigenvalues has dimension
1
,
thus by Theorem
6.3
,
\mathcal{B}
is connected.
∎

Let

Lemma 6.5

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(Wv)+\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(E_{0}+R^{\circ})=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(W)

We decompose the non-contracting space as

Proof.

∎

If

.
But it also contains every conjugate of

In the following, we assume that

Let

Let

thanks to the following.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\varphi:E\to\mathbb{K}
be a linear form and let

.
Then,

\ker(\varphi)\cap\bigoplus_{i\in I}A_{i}=\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I}\ker(\varphi)\cap A_{i}\right)\oplus\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{K}}\{v_{i}-v_{j}\mid i,j\in I\},

For each

Let

.

Let

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}^{n}\cap E^{\circ})=n-\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(GE)=n-\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}^{n}\cap GE).

It is a consequence of Proposition

∎

Hence, to find the dimension of

We obtain

7
Examples

In this section, we give examples with non-trivial coboundaries and with explicit computation of eigenvalues.

7.1
Constructing examples with non-trivial coboundary

We provide a recipe for constructing examples with non-trivial coboundaries:

Begin by selecting any directed graph where edges are labeled with letters, ensuring that each letter appears only once.
Next, choose any map
f:V\to V
, where
V
is the set of vertices.
Construct a substitution
\sigma
as follow:
For each letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
we choose
\sigma(a)
to be labels of a path from
f(s)
to
f(e)
,
where
s\xrightarrow{a}e
is the edge labeled by
a
in the graph.

For example, consider the graph
\cdot
\cdot
a
b
c

We can construct the following substitutions preserving this graph:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a\mapsto bc\\
b\mapsto ab\\
c\mapsto caa\end{array},\begin{array}[]{l}a\mapsto caab\\
b\mapsto c\\
c\mapsto ab\end{array},\begin{array}[]{l}a\mapsto bc\\
b\mapsto aa\\
c\mapsto bca\end{array}\right\}.

Then, the coboundary
\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-1\end{pmatrix}
associated with the graph is an eigenvector of incidence matrices.
In the first case, it is associated with the eigenvalue
1
because it preserves each vertex.
In the second case, the eigenvalue is
-1
because it exchanges vertices.
In the third case, the eigenvalue is
0
since both vertices are mapped to the same vertex.
Any directive sequence of substitutions constructed using this recipe for a given graph yields a subshift
that have coboundaries determined by the graph.

Example 7.9

(Weakly mixing Interval Translation Maps)
.

In
[
BR23
]
, it is shown that every primitive pre-periodic directive sequence with substitutions

1\mapsto 2,2\mapsto 31^{k},3\mapsto 31^{k-1}
,
k\geq 1
,
gives a weakly mixing subshift. But their proof relies on awful calculations.
Thanks to our criterion, we can give a different proof without calculation.
There are no non-trivial coboundaries since any product of two of these substitutions is left-proper.
They prove that the incidence matrix of the periodic part has two eigenvalues of modulus
\geq 1

(see Proposition 3.1 in
[
BR23
]
).
Moreover, it is unimodular, so it is irreducible, and
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|=2>3/2
, so
\mathcal{B}
is connected.
By Theorem
6.2
, the subshift is weakly mixing.

Example 7.10

(Arnoux-Yoccoz IET)
