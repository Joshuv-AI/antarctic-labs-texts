Discrete Ollivier-Ricci curvature Thanks: * the corresponding author


Abstract
. We analyze both continuous and discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvatures of locally-finite weighted graphs 𝒢 \mathcal{G} equipped with a given distance “ d \mathrm{d} ” (w.r.t. which 𝒢 \mathcal{G} is metrically complete) and for general random walks. We show the continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature is well-defined for a large class of Markovian and non-Markovian random walks and provide a criterion for existence of continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature; the said results generalize the previous rather limited constructions in the literature. In addition, important properties of both discrete-time and continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvatures are obtained including – to name a few – Lipschitz continuity, concavity properties, piece-wise regularity (piece-wise linearity in the case of linear walks) for the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci as well as Lipschitz continuity and limit-free formulation for the continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci. these properties were previously known only for very specific distances and very specific random walks. As an application of Lipschitz continuity, we obtain existence and uniqueness of generalized continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature flows. Along the way, we obtain – by optimizing McMullen’s upper bounds – a sharp upper bound estimate on the number of vertices of a convex polytope in terms of number of its facets and the ambient dimension, which might be of independent interest in convex geometry. The said upper bound allows us to bound the number of polynomial pieces of the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature as a function of time in the time-polynomial random walk. The limit-free formulation we establish allows us to define an operator theoretic Ollivier-Ricci curvature which is a non-linear concave functional on suitable operator spaces.

Introduction

Along with the vivid rise in using data and network analysis, the scientific community has witnessed emergence of many important discrete models for example in life sciences and finance; study of these models – beyond the classical ways – would require new novel techniques.

Also as the research in discrete structures furthers, it further reveals the innate power that lies in the seeming “reduction” that takes place combined with the “adequacy” that is retained when approximating continuous structures by discrete ones; meaning, the theories and computations become programmable (reduction) and yet the recent developments in discrete geometry indicate that one can still successfully apply classical ideas and tools to fruition (adequacy).

Because of the said revelations, and inspired by the already established power of geometric-analytic tools in continuous phase spaces, the interest in applying such methods to discrete structures has surged among both Mathematicians and scientists from other fields alike. Needless to say, a nontrivial geometry entails the introduction of curvature; and the quest of studying various types of curvature of weighted graphs – as epitome of discrete structures – has proven very fruitful; many similarities to the continuous setting has been unearthed and many useful generalizations made.

Unlike what we see in the Riemannian manifolds, when it comes to weighted graphs, there are numerous ways of defining a notion of “Ricci curvature”. Some main stream ones include Ricci curvature bounds using optimal transport theory (the so called Lott-Sturm-Villani curvature bounds)
[
2
]
, discrete Bakry-Émery bounds
[
14
]
, Ollivier-Ricci curvature
[
20
,
15
]
and Forman-Ricci curvature
[
6
]
, each suited for a set of different purposes. From the geometrical point of view, if we look at a network as the 1-skeleton of a CW-complex, the Bakry-Émery curvature-dimension bounds are construed as vertex type curvatures or curvatures on the 0-skeleton. In these notes, we wish to look at the Ollivier’s definition of coarse Ricci curvature on the 1-skeleton.

The aforementioned Ricci curvatures appear in the study of many real life discrete models. These applications – discovered not so long a go – include the use of Ollivier-Ricci and Bakry-Émery Ricci curvature as indicators of robustness and as tools by which to measure the difference of two networks; the applications have thus far been in the fields of social, biological or financial networks and at a growing rate due to successes achieved by using these methods. For more details, we refer to
[
5
]
and the reference therein. The other important application of Ollivier-Ricci curvature and flow is in community detection i.e. finding clusters with high connectivity in weighted networks
[
18
,
23
,
12
]
.

We are considering the original version of Ollivier-Ricci curvature instead of limited modified versions, an endeavor that is long overdue. The already existing constructions and modified versions of Ollivier-Ricci curvature are very useful yet they are not exploring the full potential of Ollivier’s definition of coarse Ricci curvature. It is worth mentioning that almost all of the already established properties for the modified versions of Ollivier-Ricci curvature would follow as spacial cases of our results if we restrict ourselves to
\upbeta
-walks (lazy walks); see §
6.2.2
.

This article is inspired by

A brief setup and notations

Consider a quadruple
\left(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m},\omega,\mathrm{d}\right)
where
\mathcal{G}
is a locally-finite graph,
\mathdutchcal{m}
is a vertex measure,
\omega=\left\{\omega_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
is a finite set of edge weight functions i.e. we can consider
\omega:E\to\mathbb{R}^{I}
as a vector-valued edge weight, and
\mathrm{d}
is a distance on
\mathcal{G}
with the condition that
\left(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{d}\right)
is complete, thus, a Polish metric space. For defining the Laplacian, we will just use the notation
\omega
which can be considered as the zeroth element (
\omega_{0}
) in the collection of the said weight functions. Sometimes, we will also allow the distance
\mathrm{d}
to be induced by a secondary edge weight
\eta
.

A random walk on
\mathcal{G}
is a collection of probability measures
\upmu_{z},z\in\mathcal{G}
;
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
determines – once at
z
– where and with what probability, a walker can go next. In these notes, we will work with a
1
-parameter family of random walks
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. We will be concerned with
1
-parameter walks that are continuous in
\varepsilon
and with
\upmu_{z}^{0}=\delta_{z}
, so this will be an standing assumption throughout.

For a given fixed
\varepsilon
, the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature is given (a la Ollivier) by

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=1-\nicefrac{{\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)}}{{\mathrm{d}(x,y)}},\quad 0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1.

where
\mathcal{W}_{1}
is the
\mathrm{L}^{1}
-Wasserstein distance also known as the Kantorovich-Rubinstein metric.

Both the discrete-time and continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvatures are formulated using this
1
-parameter family of random walks. Of course in the discrete-time version the parameter does not play a role in the definition however it becomes important when one considers the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci as a function of the parameter.

Definition 1.1

(finite-step walks)
.

A
1
-parameter family
\upmu_{z},z\in\mathcal{G}
;
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
of random walks is said to be a
finite-step
walk when for each
z
and each
\varepsilon
, it has bounded support w.r.t. the combinatorial distance which in conjunction with local finiteness means the support is a finite set.

Definition 1.2

(time-affine, time-polynomial or time-analytic walks)
.

A continuous-time random walk
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
is said to be time-affine, time-polynomial or time-analytic whenever for every fixed
x,y
,
\mathdutchcal{p}_{xy}(\varepsilon):=\upmu^{\varepsilon}_{x}(y)
is affine, polynomial or analytic (resp.) in
\varepsilon
and
admits an analytic continuation
over
\left(-\updelta_{xy},1+\updelta_{xy}\right)
for some small
\updelta_{xy}>0
.

Notice the analytic continuation condition is automatically satisfied by time-affine and time-polynomial walks. Also notice we are not assuming the walk has a continuation, so the continuation might not be a nonnegative measure or a probability measure.

We should remark that when working with the continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature, we only need the walk to admit a continuation beyond
\varepsilon=0
.

Definition 1.3
.

A continuous-time random walk
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
is said to be a local walk whenever for each
z
, there exists a finite subgraph
{\mathcal{K}}_{z}
such that
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{z}=\mathrm{supp}\left(\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}\right)
is included in
{\mathcal{K}}_{z}
for all
\varepsilon
.
This means for fixed
x,y
, we have
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}:=\mathrm{supp}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}\right)\cup\mathrm{supp}\left(\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)
is included in a finite set
\mathcal{K}_{xy}
with cardinality
{\mathcal{N}}_{xy}:=|\mathcal{K}_{xy}|
. In particular, a local walk is a finite-step walk but the converse does not hold. We set
{\mathcal{N}}_{xy}:=|\mathcal{K}_{xy}|
.

Special cases of walks that our results apply to, include
\uptheta
-walks; in particular, if one uses the restricted cases of
\upbeta
,
\upzeta
and
\upxi
-walks (these are time-affine and are also called lazy walks in the literature), one retrieves the known constructions in the literature
[
15
,
17
,
13
,
18
]
; see §
6.2.2
for further details and definitions.

Summary of Main results

Here we give a brief mention of the definitions and main results; further details and proofs are to be found in the referenced sections.

Discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature

For more details regarding the following theorem, see §
6.2
. Notice that the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci is well-defined as soon as the walk had finite first moment; for local walks, we get much more.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
is a (time-affine or time-polynomial) time-analytic local random walk. For any fixed pair
x,y
,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
as a function of
\varepsilon
, satisfies the following;

(1)

(piece-wise regularity) it is a piece-wise (affine, polynomial) analytic function of
\varepsilon
;

(2)

(locality) it coincides with the discrete-time curvature computed in the subgraph
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
;

(3)

(concavity for time-affine) it is concave for time-affine walks (also holds without the locality condition);

(4)

(Lipschitz regularity) it is Lipschitz continuous in
\varepsilon
;

(5)

(finite pieces) it admits finitely many regular pieces;

(6)

(upper bound for time-polynomial walks) for time polynomial walks, the number of distinct polynomial pieces is bounded above by

\left(\max\limits_{z,w\in\mathcal{K}_{xy}}\deg{\mathdutchcal{p}}_{zw}(\varepsilon)\right)\cdot\Uplambda\left(\mathcal{N}_{xy},2\,\mathcal{N}_{xy}^{\,{}^{2}}-2\,\mathcal{N}_{xy}+1\right).

where the function
\Uplambda
is an upper bound function for bounded convex polytopes given in Theorem
3.5
.

Continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature

The continuous time Ollivier-Ricci curvature
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)
is defined (a la Ollivier) as the derivative

\text{-}\,\nicefrac{{d}}{{d\varepsilon}}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\varepsilon=0}\;\nicefrac{{\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\mathrm{d}}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)}}{{\mathrm{d}(x,y)}},

whenever it exists
[
20
]
. The original definition is intended for a continuous-time random walk generated by a Markov kernel, however the same definition can be used for more general
1
-parameter walks. We note that for the definition of continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature, we just need to know the germ of the random walk at
\varepsilon=0
however for simplicity we always assume that
\varepsilon
ranges from
0
to
1
but this is indeed of no significance in the theory.

Let us point to an important class of continuous-time random walks that we call pleasant walks; these are walks of the form

\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}=\updelta_{z}+\varepsilon\upmu_{z}+\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{z},

whose
1
-jet is a time-analytic walk with some further second order asymptotic conditions on
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{z}
; see Definition
6.17
. In particular
\upmu_{z}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{z}
are zero-mass signed measures.

Also some important function spaces that frequently appear are the space of
1
-Lipschitz functions which is denoted by
\Lip(1)
and the space of finite support functions which we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})
.

Theorem 1.5
.

The continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)
, is well-defined for continuous-time pleasant walks. Furthermore, for pleasant walks with local
1
-jets, the following hold;

(1)

(limit-free formulation)
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
is given by the limit-free variational formula

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\Lip(1)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}\\
\nabla_{xy}f=1\end{subarray}}\nabla_{yx}\mathcal{L}f,

where the operator
\mathcal{L}
is the initial velocity of the (not necessarily Feller) process corresponding to the given
1
-parameter random walk;

(2)

(Lipschitz continuity 1)
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)
is locally Lipschitz in
\upmu_{z}
and in the distance
\mathrm{d}
(as variables);

(3)

(Lipschitz continuity 2) if
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
as in the previous item is locally Lipschitz in
\omega
, then
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)
is locally Lipschitz continuous in the arguments
\omega
and in the distance
\mathrm{d}
(as a variable);

(4)

(minimizer) the limit-free formulation admits a minimizer;

(5)

(locality) the minimizer can be localized to be supported in
\mathcal{K}_{xy}
;

More details regarding the above theorem are to be found in §
6.3
.

We refer the reader to §
5
for the precise definitions of operator-theoretic notions that we will shortly see.

Theorem 1.6
.

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
is well-defined for Markovian walks
e^{\varepsilon{\mathcal{L}}}\updelta_{z}
when
\mathcal{L}
is a good operator and it satisfies

(1)

e^{\varepsilon\mathcal{L}}
and
\text{-}\mathcal{L}
satisfy rough comparison principles with range
0
;

(2)

\left|\mathcal{L}d(z,\cdot)(z)\right|\leq C(z)
.

Good operators are translation invariant essentially self-adjoint operators that are roughly of divergence form; see Definition
5.8
. We say
\mathcal{G}
is locally
\mathrm{d}
-finite if metric balls are finite.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be locally
\mathrm{d}
-finite. Then,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
is well-defined for Markovian walks
e^{\varepsilon{\mathcal{L}}}\updelta_{z}
when
\mathcal{L}
is a good operator and it satisfies

(1)

e^{\varepsilon\mathcal{L}}
satisfies rough comparison principle with range
0
;

(2)

\mathcal{L}
is a semi-local operator with range
R
;

(3)

\mathcal{L}
satisfies rough comparison principle on
\Lip_{1}(\mathcal{G})
with range
2R
;

(4)

\left|\mathcal{L}\mathrm{d}(z,\cdot)(w)\right|\leq C(z)
holds
\forall w\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}(z)
.

We will refer the reader to §
6.3.4
for the above existence theorems in the spacial setting where the walk is the heat kernel i.e. where
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}=e^{\varepsilon\Delta}\delta_{z}
.

Operator theoretic Ollivier-Ricci curvature

Let

\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{G}\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{L})\to\mathbb{R}^{G}

be an operator with

\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})\subset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{L})
.
We define operator-theoretic Ollivier-Ricci curvature by

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}(x,y):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})\\
f\in\Lip(1)\\
\nabla_{xy}f=1\end{subarray}}\nabla_{yx}\mathcal{L}f.

Theorem 1.8
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the following properties

(1)

\mathrm{d}(z,\cdot)\in\mathsf{Dom}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)
,
\forall z
;

(2)

\mathcal{L}
is a rough differential operator;

(3)

\mathcal{L}
satisfies a two-sided rough comparison principle.

Then,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)
is finite.

See Definition
5.3
for two-sided comparison principle. Roughly speaking, semi-local means
\mathcal{L}f(x)
only depend on the values of
f
on a metric ball around
x
; rough differential operator means the operator is translation invariant; see §
6.3.7
.

Theorem 1.9
.

In locally
\mathrm{d}
-finite
\mathcal{G}
,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)
is finite for bounded semi-local rough differential operators
\mathcal{L}
.

Here, boundedness of the operator is with respect to the sup-norm. Furthermore, notice in above theorems, for any two such operators
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, the concavity relation

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{t\mathcal{L}_{1}+(1-t)\mathcal{L}_{2}}\geq t{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}+(1-t){{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}

should clearly hold.

Continuous-time generalized Ollivier-Ricci flows

The continuous-time generalized Ollivier-Ricci curvature flow equation
{\sf ORF}_{f,g,c}
is the ODE system

{\sf ORF}_{f,g,h}:\begin{cases}\dot{\omega}=f(t,\omega,\mathrm{d},\mathdutchcal{m},{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric})\\
\dot{\mathrm{d}}=g\left(t,\omega,\mathrm{d},\mathdutchcal{m},{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}\right)\\
\dot{\mathdutchcal{m}}=h\left(t,\omega,\mathrm{d},\mathdutchcal{m},{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\scal}\right)\end{cases}

for given functions
f,g
and
h
. See §
7
for more details.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite graph. Suppose a time-analytic random walk
\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}
depends on
\omega
and
\mathdutchcal{m}
in a locally Lipschitz continuous way. Also suppose
f,g,h
are each locally uniformly Lipschitz in the
t
variable and locally Lipschitz in the other variables.
Then, for any initial data in the interior of te phase space, there exists a unique solution to this flow and the maximal solution exists as long as the primary edge weight
\omega
stays non-negative and
m
stay positive and
\mathrm{d}
stays a distance. In particular, the Ollivier-Ricci flow

\dot{\omega}=-{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}\cdot\omega,\quad\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}_{\omega}.

admits unique solutions.

We note that once a local Lipschitz continuity of
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
is at our disposal, we can show existence and uniqueness a wide range of similar ODE systems not necessarily restricted to the form (
1
).

Miscellaneous

In order to get an upper bound on the number of polynomial pieces in discrete-time generalized Ollivier-Ricci curvature for time-polynomial local walks, we needed to prove an upper bound theorem on the number of vertices of a bounded convex polytope in

in terms of the number of its facet; the latter was achieved by using and slightly modifying the famous McMulen’s upper bound theorem

Also we needed to discuss the Lipschitz sensitivity of the optimal value function in linear programming problems, to perturbations of the constraints and objective functions; for that we built upon the perturbation theorem of Renegar

; this also might be of independent interest; see Theorem

.

2.
A quick setup of discrete calculus

2.1.
Weighted graphs

An un-directed locally finite weighted graph
\mathcal{G}
is a non-negative symmetric primary weight function
\omega:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with vertex set

V:=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;\exists y\in\mathbb{Z}\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\omega(x,y)>0\;\;\text{or}\;\;\omega(x,y)>0\},

and edge set

E:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;\omega(x,y)>0\}/\sim,\quad(x,y)\sim(y,x).

in which for every
x
,
\omega(x,\cdot)
has finite support i.e. all vertices have finite combinatorial degrees.

Throughout these notes, all graphs are assumed to be locally finite. For simplicity,
x\sim y
means there is an edge between
x
and
y
. We set
\omega_{xy}:=\omega(x,y)
. The combinatorial degree of a vertex
x
is denoted by
\deg_{x}
while we signify the the weighted degree by

\mathrm{Deg}_{x}:=\nicefrac{{1}}{{{\mathdutchcal{m}}(x)}}\sum_{y\sim x}\omega_{xy}=:{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\\
\mathdutchcal{m}\end{subarray}\underset{y\sim x}{\sum}}1;

here,
{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\\
\mathdutchcal{m}\end{subarray}\underset{y\sim x}{\sum}}
is our adopted notation to represent the doubly (vertex and edge) weighted summation.

Weighted path distance

There is a variety of ways to assign a distance function to a graph. We will only recall the most common one which is the weighted path distance. Let
\eta
be a symmetric edge-weight function. The distance
\mathrm{d}_{\eta}
is defined by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\eta}\left(x,z\right)=\inf_{z_{0}\sim z_{1}\sim\dots\sim z_{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\eta_{z_{i}z_{i+1}}.

For example, for
\eta\equiv 1
, one retrieves the combinatorial distance
\mathrm{d}
. Paths that minimize the
\mathrm{d}_{\eta}
distance are called
\mathrm{d}_{\eta}
-geodesics.

2.2.
Calculus tools

Recall
\mathrm{d}
is a distance on
\mathcal{G}
and we always assume
\left(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{d}\right)
is metrically complete. The differential-geometric objects defined in this section are mostly depending on the distance
\mathrm{d}
.

Tangent space and space of directions

Definition 2.1

(Direction space at a vertex)
.

The direction space at a vertex
x\in\mathcal{G}
is given by

\Dir_{x}(\mathcal{G}):=\left\{\text{all the $\mathrm{d}$-geodesics emanating from $x$}\right\}.

So,
\mathsf{Geod}(\mathcal{G})=\bigcup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{G}}\Dir_{x}(\mathcal{G})=:\Dir\mathcal{G}
is the set of all
\mathrm{d}
-geodesics. We will denote the length of a geodesic
\upgamma
by
\|\upgamma\|
.

Set

\mathbb{L}_{x}:=\mathrm{d}(x,\cdot)^{-1}(\mathcal{G})

which is an ordered subset

\left\{0=\ell_{x,0},\ell_{x,1},\dots,\ell_{x,n_{x}}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

providing all the distances realized by geodesics emanated from
x
;
here,
n_{x}
is the combinatorial length the longest geodesic emanating from
x
. The set
\mathbb{L}_{x}
can be written as the union of
\mathbb{L}_{x\sim y}
(distances set off in the direction
x\sim y
) where

\mathbb{L}_{x\sim y}:=\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{L}_{x}\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;\exists\upgamma\in\Dir_{x}(\mathcal{G})\;\;\text{with}\;\;y\in\upgamma\;\;\text{and}\;\;\|\upgamma\|=\ell\right\}.

Definition 2.2

(Tangent space)
.

We define the tangent space at a vertex
x\in\mathcal{G}
to be

\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{G}):=\bigcup\limits_{y\sim x}\left\{(x,y)\right\}\times\mathbb{L}_{x\sim y}/\sim.

where
(x,y,0)\sim(x,z,0)
. So a tangent vector specifies an “infinitesimal direction”
x\sim y
and a length
\ell
. Set
\Tan\mathcal{G}:=\bigcup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{G}}\Tan_{x}\mathcal{G}
. The length of tangent vectors are obviously defined by

\|(x,y,\ell)\|=\ell.

The tangent sphere,
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{x}\mathcal{G}
, is the set of all unit tangent vectors at
x
. The union of all these, is called the unit sphere bundle
\mathbb{S}^{1}\mathcal{G}
; notice the latter might very well be an empty set.

The tangent space
\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{G})
is manifestly depending on the distance
\mathrm{d}
; a fact which is in contrast to the construction of tangent space in the smooth setting.

Evaluation, exponential and logarithm maps

For any
\upgamma\in\Dir_{x}(\mathcal{G})
, the distances realized by
\upgamma
are

\mathbb{L}_{x,\upgamma}:=\mathrm{d}(x,\cdot)^{-1}\left(\mathrm{image}(\upgamma)\right)=\left\{0=\ell^{0}_{x,\upgamma},\ell^{1}_{x,\upgamma},\dots\ell^{n_{x,\upgamma}}_{x,\upgamma}\right\}\subset\mathbb{L}_{x},

where
n_{x,\upgamma}
is the combinatorial length of
\upgamma
. Define the evaluation maps
{\sf e}:\mathrm{Geod}(\mathcal{G})\times\mathbb{L}\to\mathcal{G}
by

{\sf e}\left(\upgamma,\ell^{m}_{\upgamma(0),\upgamma}\right)=\left\{v\in\mathrm{image}(\upgamma)\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;\mathrm{d}(\upgamma_{0},v)=\ell^{m}_{\upgamma(0),\upgamma}\right\},\quad m\leq n_{\upgamma(0),\upgamma};

So,
\mathsf{e}\left(\upgamma,\ell^{m}_{\upgamma(0),\upgamma}\right)
simply gives the
m
-th vertex along the geodesic
\upgamma
.

Definition 2.3

(exponential and logarithm maps)
.

The exponential map

\bar{\exp}_{x}:\Tan_{x}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{G}

is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\exp}_{x}(x,y,\ell):=

\displaystyle\left\{z\in\mathcal{G}\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;\mathrm{d}(x,z)=\ell\;\;\text{and there exists a geodesic from $x$ to $z$ passes through $y$}\right\}.

It is readily evident that

Differentiation operators

Definition 2.4

(Differentiation operators)
.

The tangent map of a function
f
is the multi-valued map

\nabla_{\centerdot}\;f:\Tan\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}

defined by

\bar{\nabla}_{e}f:=\ell^{-1}\left(f(\exp_{x}(e))-f(x)\right),\quad(x,y,\ell)=e\in\Tan_{x}\mathcal{G},

where, for
n=0
, we set it equal to
0
. The tangent map factors through a single-valued map with domain
\mathsf{Geod}(\mathcal{G})=\Dir\mathcal{G}
which we denote by
\nabla
and call the directional derivative. So with a slight abuse of notations and terminology, for
x
and
y
in
\mathcal{G}
, we write

\nabla_{xy}f:=\nabla_{\log_{x}{y}}f=\mathrm{d}(x,y)^{-1}\left(f(y)-f(x)\right),

which is the derivative of
f
in the multi-valued direction
\log_{x}{y}
. Suppose
\upgamma
is one such direction, then we sometimes also write
\nabla_{\upgamma}f
for brevity.

So with the definition of directional derivative, a function
f
being
\sf L
-Lipschitz is equivalent to
|\nabla f|\leq{\sf L}
; we denote the lowest such
{\sf L}
by
\lip(f)
.

A special case is when
\mathrm{d}
is the combinatorial distance; in this case, we will also denote by
\nabla^{1}_{\bullet}u:\mathbb{S}^{1}\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
, the map that is given by

\nabla^{1}_{xy}u:=\bar{\nabla}_{(x,y,1)}f=f(y)-f(x).

Discrete Laplacian(s)

The most general form of discrete Laplacian is given by

\Delta f(x):=\mathdutchcal{m}(x)^{-1}\sum_{x\sim y}\left(f(y)-f(x)\right)\omega_{xy}=:{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\\
\mathdutchcal{m}\end{subarray}\underset{y\sim x}{\sum}}\left(f(y)-f(x)\right).

The special case of the combinatorial graph Laplacian
\mathbf{\Delta}
is obtained when
\mathdutchcal{m}\equiv 1
and
\omega\equiv 1
, i.e.

\Delta_{\sf c}f(x):=\sum_{x\sim y}\left(f(y)-f(x)\right).

The normalized Laplacian is also a common one and is obtained if we set
{\mathdutchcal{m}}(x)=\omega(x):=\sum_{y\sim x}\omega_{xy}
i.e.
\mathrm{Deg}\equiv 1
;

\Delta_{\sf n}f(x):=\omega(x)^{-1}\sum_{x\sim y}\left(f(y)-f(x)\right)\omega_{xy}.

The normalized Laplacian is particularly important since it is the infinitesimal generator of the weighted random walk; e.g. see
[
1
]
.

3.
An upper bound result for convex polytopes

In the next section, we will be dealing with linear programming problems with feasible sets that are bounded convex polytopes in some
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. One estimate that will be important to us is an upper bound for the number of vertices of a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
that has
m
facets (faces of co-dimension
1
).

3.1.
Upper bound

We will need the following two elementary lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Set

\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r):=\binom{m-r}{r},2r\leq m;

the following hold.

(1)

For fixed
m
,
\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r)
is an increasing function of
r
for
0\leq r\leq\lfloor r_{11}(m)\rfloor
and is a decreasing function of
r
for
r\geq\lceil r_{11}(m)\rceil
where

r_{11}(m)=\nicefrac{{\left(5m-3-\left(5m^{2}+10m+9\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}}{{10}};

(2)

as a result of (1), one has
\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r)\leq\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}\left(m,\lfloor r_{11}(m)\rfloor\right)\vee\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}\left(m,\lceil r_{11}(m)\rceil\right)
.

Proof
.

Set

\displaystyle\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r+1)\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{m-r-1}{r+1}\binom{m-r}{r}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{(m-2r)(m-2r-1)}}{{(m-r)(r+1)}}:=\mathcal{R}_{1}(m,r).

Treating
\mathcal{R}_{1}
as a function of real variables,
\mathcal{R}_{1}(m,r)\geq 1
is equivalent to

q_{1}(r)=5r^{2}+\left(-5m+3\right)r+m^{2}-2m\geq 0.

q_{1}(r)
has real roots
r_{1}(m)<r_{2}(m)
given by

r_{11}(m)=\nicefrac{{\left(5m-3-\left(5m^{2}+10m+9\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}}{{10}},\quad r_{12}=\nicefrac{{\left(5m-3+\left(5m^{2}+10m+9\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}}{{10}}.

It is easy to verify that for
m\geq 2
, both roots are non-negative. This means for fixed
m\geq 2
,
\mathcal{R}(m,r)\geq 1
holds for
0\leq r\leq r_{1}(m)
and
r>r_{2}(m)
, and
\mathcal{R}(m,r)\leq 1
for
r_{1}(m)\leq r\leq r_{2}(m)
; this verifies (1).

We also have

\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}<\nicefrac{{\left(5m-3+\left(5m^{2}+10m+9\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}}{{10}}=r_{12}(m).

So, as
r
increases to
\lfloor r_{1}(m)\rfloor
,
h(m,r)
increases; and as
r
increases from
\lceil r_{1}(m)\rceil
all the way up to
\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}\rfloor
,
\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r)
will decrease. Therefore, its maximum must be attained at either
\lfloor r_{1}(m)\rfloor
or at
\lceil r_{1}(m)\rceil
; this proves (2).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Set

\mathdutchcal{h}_{2}(m,r):=\binom{m-r}{r-1},2r\leq m+1;

then, the items in Lemma
3.1
hold, using
r_{21}(m)
instead where

r_{21}(m)=\nicefrac{{\left(5m+2-\left(5m^{2}+4\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}}{{10}}.

Proof
.

Similar to the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we proceed as follows.

\displaystyle\mathdutchcal{h}_{2}(m,r+1)\mathdutchcal{h}_{2}(m,r)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{m-r-2}{r}\binom{m-r-1}{r-1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{(m-2r)(m-2r+1)}}{{(m-r)(r)}}:=\mathcal{R}_{2}(m,r).

\mathcal{R}_{2}(m,r)\geq 1\iff q_{2}(r)=5r^{2}+(-5m-2)r+m^{2}+m\geq 0.

Roots of
q_{2}
are

r_{21}=\nicefrac{{\left(5m+2-\left(5m^{2}+4\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}}{{10}},\quad r_{22}=\nicefrac{{\left(5m+2+\left(5m^{2}+4\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)}}{{10}};

and both are positive. Furthermore,

\nicefrac{{(m+1)}}{{2}}<r_{22}.

clearly holds; so we can argue in the same fashion as in Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Let us recall that McMullen’s upper bound theorem states that among all
d
-dimensional polytopes with
n
vertices, the cyclic ones maximize the number of
i
-dimensional faces for
i=1,\cdots,d-1
. We refer the reader to the exposition
[
9
]
and the original paper
[
16
]
for more details.

The following proposition is a direct consequence of the upper bound theorem for convex polytopes in combination with the concept of dual polytopes; see
[
16
,
9
,
22
]
. The number of facets in nontrivial cases satisfy
m\geq 3
so the above lemmas apply.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{P}
is a
d
-polytope with
m
number of facets and
n
number of vertices. Then

n\leq{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}(d,m),

where
{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}
is given by

{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}(d,m):=\binom{m-\lceil\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rceil}{\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor}+\binom{m-1-\lceil\nicefrac{{(d-1)}}{{2}}\rceil}{\lfloor\nicefrac{{(d-1)}}{{2}}\rfloor},

which is the number of facets in a cyclic
d
-polytope with
m
vertices.

Proof
.

First notice that going to the dual polytope, we deduce
d<m
so
{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}(d,m)
is well-defined. Now suppose

n>{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}(d,m)

then, the dual polytope
\mathfrak{P}^{*}
has more than
{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}(d,m)
facets; hence, by McMullen’s upper bound theorem, the dual polytope
\mathfrak{P}^{*}
must have more than
m
vertices. This means
\mathfrak{P}
must have more than
m
facets which is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Notice that the function
{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}(d,m)
can be written as

\displaystyle{\rm F}_{\sf cyc}(d,m)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\binom{m-r}{r}+\binom{m-r-1}{r-1}&\text{if}\quad d=2r\\
2\binom{m-r-1}{r}&\text{if}\quad d=2r+1\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r)+\mathdutchcal{h}_{2}(m-1,r)&\text{if}\quad d=2r\\
2\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m-1,r)&\text{if}\quad d=2r+1\end{cases}.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{P}
is a bounded convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
m
number of facets and
n
vertices. Then

\displaystyle n\leq 2\mathdutchcal{h}_{111}(m-1)\vee\Big(\mathdutchcal{h}_{111}(m)+\mathdutchcal{h}_{221}(m-1)\Big):=\Uplambda(N,m),

with the convention

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathdutchcal{h}_{ijk}(m):=\mathdutchcal{h}_{i}\left(m,\lfloor r_{jk}(m)\rfloor\wedge\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor\right)\vee\mathdutchcal{h}_{i}\left(m,\lceil r_{jk}(m)\rceil\wedge\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor\right).

Proof
.

Based on Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, we clearly have

2\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m-1,r)\leq 2\mathdutchcal{h}_{111}(m-1),

and

\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r)+\mathdutchcal{h}_{2}(m-1,r)\leq\mathdutchcal{h}_{111}(m)+\mathdutchcal{h}_{221}(m-1),

which by virtue of Remark
3.4
and
r\leq\lfloor\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\rfloor\leq\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor
, gives the desired conclusion.
∎

Theorem 3.6

(sharpness)
.

The upper bound
\Uplambda
is achieved in some cases. So by definition, it is a sharp bound.

Proof
.

Set
N=3
and
m
large, we get the same bound but this time, by Remark
3.4
, we know
2m-4
is sharp for cyclic 3-polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
m
facets. Since the upper bound
\Uplambda
is achieved in some cases, it is a sharp bound.
∎

Example 1.
(sharpness and non-sharpness)
One can naturally find cases in which the upper bound
\Uplambda
is an overkill. Set
N=2
and consider a regular polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
m>4
vertices and facets. for
m
sufficiently large Then

2\mathdutchcal{h}_{111}(m-1)=2\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m-1,1)=2(m-2)=2m-4,

and

\mathdutchcal{h}_{111}(m)+\mathdutchcal{h}_{221}(m-1)=\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,1)+\mathdutchcal{h}_{2}(m-1,1)=m,

so

\Uplambda(m,2)=2m-4\vee m=\begin{cases}m&m\leq 4\\
2m-4&m>4\end{cases},

which shows the upper bound is not sharp in this cases if
m>4
and is only sharp for triangles.

Corollary 3.7
.

A crude upper bound is given by

\displaystyle\Uplambda(N,m)\leq

\displaystyle\quad\Big(\max\limits_{\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\rfloor-1\leq r\leq\lceil\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}\rceil\wedge\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor}2\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}\left(m-1,r\right)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\vee\left(\max\limits_{\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\rfloor-1\leq r\leq\lceil\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}\rceil\wedge\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor}\mathdutchcal{h}_{1}(m,r)+\max\limits_{\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\rfloor\leq r\leq\lceil\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}\rceil\wedge\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor}\mathdutchcal{h}_{2}(m-1,r)\right).

Proof
.

From the inequalities

2(m+1)\leq\left(5m^{2}+10m+9\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\leq 3(m+1),

we deduce

\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}-\nicefrac{{3}}{{5}}\leq r_{11}(m)\leq\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},

and

\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}-\nicefrac{{4}}{{5}}\leq r_{11}(m-1)\leq\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}-\nicefrac{{4}}{{5}}.

Similarly by

2m\leq\left(5m^{2}+4\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\leq 3m,

we get

\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{5}}\leq r_{21}(m)\leq\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{5}},

and

\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\leq r_{21}(m-1)\leq\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}.

As a result,

\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\rfloor-1\leq\lfloor r_{11}(m)\rfloor\leq\lceil r_{11}(m)\rceil\leq\lceil\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}\rceil,

as well as

\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\rfloor-1\leq\lfloor r_{11}(m-1)\rfloor\leq\lceil r_{11}(m-1)\rceil\leq\lceil\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}\rceil.

Similarly we have

\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\rfloor\leq\lfloor r_{21}(m)\rfloor\leq\lceil r_{21}(m)\rceil\leq\lceil\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}\rceil,

and

\lfloor\nicefrac{{m}}{{5}}\rfloor\leq\lfloor r_{21}(m-1)\rfloor\leq\lceil r_{21}(m-1)\rceil\leq\lceil\nicefrac{{3m}}{{10}}\rceil.

Therefore, using Theorem
3.5
, we get the conclusion.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

For constant

,
\Uplambda(N,m)
is constant for

.

Proof.

When
N>m
, it follows

\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor\geq\lfloor\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}\rfloor>r_{i1}(m)>r_{i1}(m-1),\quad i=1,2,

so the dependence on

in the RHS of (

) disappears.
∎

4.
Sensitivity analysis for LP problems

Consider the linear programming problem

{\sf LP}_{\bf d}:=\begin{cases}\min{\bf c}\cdot\hat{x}\\
{\bf a}\hat{x}\leq{\bf b}\\
\hat{x}\geq 0\end{cases},\quad{\bf d}:=({\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}),

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in which
{\bf c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
{\bf a}_{m\times n}
is a matrix; to this problem, we assign the vector
{\bf d}:=({\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c})
in
\mathbb{R}^{mn+m+n}
. Recall that the dual problem is given by

{\sf LP}^{*}_{\bf a,b,c}:=\begin{cases}\max\hat{y}\cdot{\bf b}\\
y{\bf a}\geq{\bf c}\\
\hat{y}\geq 0\end{cases}.

Let
{\mathcal{PF}}
denote the set of all primary feasible
{\bf d}
and
{\mathcal{PI}}
, the set of all primary infeasible
{\bf d}
; also let
{\mathcal{DI}}
be the set of all
{\bf d}
such that the dual problem is infeasible.

For any such feasible vector
{\bf d}\in\mathcal{PF}
, denote the optimal value by
\mathrm{val}({\bf d})
. Due to finite dimensionality in our case, the following theorem holds with any chosen norm on
\mathbb{R}^{mn+m+n}
.

Proposition 4.1

(Lipschitz continuity of the optimal value
[
21
]
)
.

Consider the linear programming problem
{\sf LP}_{\bf d_{0}}
with
\uprho_{\bf d_{0}}:=\min\{\mathrm{d}\left({\bf d},\mathcal{PI}\right),\mathrm{d}\left({\bf d},\mathcal{DI}\right)\}>0
. There exists
L\left(\uprho_{\bf d_{0}},{\bf d_{0}}\right)>0
such that for any
{\bf d}\in\mathcal{B}_{\uprho_{\bf d_{0}}}\left({\bf d_{0}}\right)
, the local Lipschitz property

\left|\mathrm{val}({\bf d})-\mathrm{val}\left({\bf d_{0}}\right)\right|\leq L\left(\uprho_{\bf d_{0}},{\bf d_{0}}\right)\left\|{\bf d}-{\bf d_{0}}\right\|,

holds true, with the convention
\infty-\infty=0
. i.e.
\mathrm{val}({\bf d})
is locally Lipschitz in the interior of
{\sf PF}
(with induced topology; in our case, this coincides with the so-called relative interior). We will denote this interior by
\mathrm{relint}({\sf PF})
.

4.1.
Perturbation of the objective function

We wish to focus on the perturbation of the objective function. Let us start with the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
be a continuous one parameter vector. Suppose a set of
\varepsilon
-independent constraints are given that give rise to a constant feasible set that is a bounded convex polytope
\mathfrak{Q}
; also assume the linear programming problem
\min{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\hat{x}
with the said constraints is well-posed for all
\varepsilon
.

Then, for each
\varepsilon
, the optimal values can only be attained at the vertices; also the vertices that achieve the optimal value are included in a facet of
{\mathfrak{Q}}
. For any two such vertices
\hat{v}_{\varepsilon}
and
\hat{w}_{\varepsilon}
, we have
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\hat{v}_{\varepsilon}-\hat{w}_{\varepsilon}\right)=0
.

Proof
.

The fact that optimal values are attained at vertices is a standard fact of linear programming; indeed, no interior point can achieve the optimal value since otherwise subtracting a small positive multiple of
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
from the interior point provides a point in
{\mathfrak{Q}}
which violates the optimality. Suppose two vertices achieve the optimal value, then the optimal value will also be achieved along the line segment that joins these vertices; hence, the line segment must be included in a facet of the convex polytope
{\mathfrak{Q}}
. The perpendicularity claim should be clear.
∎

Notation

In the setting of Lemma
4.2
, we denote by
{\mathcal{F}^{op}}\left(\varepsilon\right)
the set of vertices of
{\mathfrak{Q}}
that achieve the optimal value (they are sometimes called
basic feasible solutions
). We will denote the optimal value by
\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)
. We denote the set of all vertices of
{\mathfrak{Q}}
by
\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 4.3

(switching time)
.

An
\varepsilon_{0}
will be called a switching time if there exists
\varepsilon_{i}\to\varepsilon
such that
\mathcal{F}^{op}\left(\varepsilon_{i}\right)\cap\mathcal{F}^{op}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right)=\diameter
. We let
\mathcal{S}
denote the set of switching times.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
{\bf d}_{\varepsilon}=\left({\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}\right)\in\mathrm{relint}\left(\mathcal{PF}\right)\cap\mathrm{relint}\left(\mathcal{PF}\right)
. Then, at a each switching time
\varepsilon_{0}
,
\left|{\mathcal{F}^{op}}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right)\right|\geq 2
holds.

Proof
.

Obviously, since
\mathcal{V}
is finite, there exists an
r_{\varepsilon}>0
, such that for
\hat{v}\in\mathcal{V}\smallsetminus{\mathcal{F}^{op}}(\varepsilon)
,

\left|{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\hat{v}-\hat{w}\right)\right|>r_{\varepsilon},\quad\forall\hat{w}\in{\mathcal{F}^{op}}(\varepsilon).

Now suppose contrary to the claim,
{\mathcal{F}^{op}}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right)=\{\hat{v}_{0}\}
is a singleton. Enumerate the vertices of
{\mathfrak{Q}}
as
\mathcal{V}=\left\{{\hat{v}}_{0},{\hat{v}}_{1},\cdots,{\hat{v}}_{l}\right\}
.
Consider the functions

(4.1)

\displaystyle h_{i}(\varepsilon):={\bf c}_{\varepsilon}\cdot{\hat{v}}_{i}.

Since
\varepsilon_{0}
is a switching time, for the sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to\varepsilon
as in Definition
4.3
, it follows

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left|h_{i}\left(\varepsilon_{k}\right)-h_{0}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right)\right|>r_{\varepsilon},\quad\forall i\geq 1.

For any
k
, let
\mathrm{val}\left(\varepsilon_{k}\right)={\bf c}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\cdot\hat{v}_{i_{k}}=h_{i_{k}}\left(\varepsilon_{k}\right)
; then, we must have
1\leq i_{k}\leq l
. From (
4.2
), we deduce

\left|\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon_{k})-\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon_{0})\right|>r_{\varepsilon_{0}};

letting
k\to\infty
, this contradicts the Lipschitz continuity established by Proposition
4.1
.
∎

Definition 4.5

(trivial perturbation)
.

We say a perturbation
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
is trivial if for all
\varepsilon
,
\mathcal{F}^{op}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{V}
holds i.e. when for all times, all vertices achieve the optimal value.

For the rest of this section, we assume the perturbation considered is non-trivial.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
satisfies the hypotheses of the Lemmas
4.2
and
4.4
and in addition, is analytic in
\varepsilon
on the interval
(0,1)
; then for any
0<\updelta<1
,
{\mathcal{S}}\cap[\updelta,1-\updelta]
is a finite set.

Proof
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\left\{\hat{v}_{0},\cdots,\hat{v}_{l}\right\}
be an enumeration of vertices of
{\mathfrak{Q}}
. Consider the analytic functions
h_{i}
given by (
4.1
); by the hypotheses,
h_{i}
are analytic in
\varepsilon
.

For every
i\neq j
, by analyticity, it follows either

\left\{\varepsilon\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;h_{i}(\varepsilon)=h_{j}(\varepsilon)\right\}\cap[\updelta,1-\updelta],

is a discrete set or
h_{i}\equiv h_{j}
on
(0,1)
. Let
\varepsilon_{0}
be a switching time and let
\varepsilon_{k}
be a sequence as in Definition
4.3
;
without loss of generality, we assume
\hat{v}_{0}\in{\mathcal{F}^{op}}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right)
. Take a sequence
\hat{v}_{i_{k}}\in{\mathcal{F}^{op}}\left(\varepsilon_{k}\right)
. So there exists
k_{0}
and a sequence of natural numbers
m
such that
\hat{v}_{i_{m}}=\hat{v}_{i_{k_{0}}}
for all values of
m
; namely, we have extracted a constant subsequence of vertices. So by (Lipschitz) continuity of optimal values, one gets

\mathrm{val}\left(\varepsilon_{m}\right)=h_{i_{k_{0}}}\left(\varepsilon_{m}\right)\to h_{i_{k_{0}}}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right)=\mathrm{val}\left(\varepsilon_{0})\right)=h_{0}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right).

which means
v_{i_{k_{0}}}\in{\mathcal{F}^{op}}\left(\varepsilon_{0}\right)
and furthermore, we know
h_{i_{k_{0}}}\not\equiv h_{0}
. So at every switching time, we find two distinct analytic functions
h_{i_{k_{0}}}
and
h_{0}
that take the same values.

Let

\mathcal{H}:=\left\{h_{i}\right\}

be the set of all distinct
h_{i}
; then if
\mathcal{H}
has only one element, every
\varepsilon
is a switching time and the optimal value is attained at all vertices at all times which is not possible
since the perturbation is non-trivial
. If
|\mathcal{H}|\neq 1
, by pigeonhole principle, we must have

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\varepsilon\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;h_{i}(\varepsilon)=h_{j}(\varepsilon)\;\text{for $h_{i}\not\equiv h_{j}\in\mathcal{H}$}\right\}\cap[\updelta,1-\updelta],

is also a finite set. Since
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{A}
, the conclusion follows.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

In the setting of the previous Lemmas,
\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)
is a piece-wise analytic and locally Lipschitz function on
(0,1)
.

Proof
.

We only need to argue piece-wise analyticity. By Lemma
4.6
, we know there are finitely many switching times in
[\updelta,1-\updelta]
for
\updelta>0
small. Between any two such switching times, the optimal value coincides with one of the analytic functions
h_{i}
; hence, the piece-wise analyticity holds within
[\updelta,1-\updelta]
. Since
0<\updelta<1
can be arbitrary small, we deuce the optimal value is also piece-wise analytic on
(0,1)
; however, notice that
there might be an infinitely many number of pieces (accumulating at the endpoints
0
and/or
1
)
.
∎

4.2.
Finite number of pieces

A sufficient condition for ensuring a finite number of analytic pieces for the optimal value function is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.8
.

Assume the hypotheses of the previous theorem. In addition, suppose for some small
\updelta>0
,
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
admits an analytic continuation over
(0-\updelta,1+\updelta)
; suppose
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
4.7
, then
\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)
has finitely many pieces in
[0,1]
.

Proof
.

We know
\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)
has finitely many analytic pieces within any
[\updelta,1-\updelta]
. Since
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
admits an analytic continuation, we deduce
h_{i}(\varepsilon)
is defined and is analytic on
(0-\updelta,1+\updelta)
.

Suppose
\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)
has infinitely many pieces on
[0,1]
then,
\mathcal{S}
has at least one of the points
0
or
1
as its accumulation point. This would again imply that the functions
h_{i}(\varepsilon)
– that are now analytic in
(0-\updelta,1+\updelta)
all must coincide which contradicts the non-triviality of the perturbation.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Notice that in Theorem
4.8
, we are not assuming the problem is even well-posed for
\varepsilon>1
or for
\varepsilon<0
.

4.2.1.
Upper bounds on the number of pieces

In general, for an analytic perturbation, we can not bound the number of finite analytic pieces; however, if we assume the perturbation is given by polynomials in
\varepsilon
then, an upper bound can be estimated.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
4.7
and furthermore,

{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}=\left(p_{1}(\varepsilon),\cdots,p_{n}(\varepsilon)\right),\quad 0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1,

where
p_{i}(\varepsilon)
is a polynomial of degree
d_{i}
. Then,
\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)
has finitely many pieces and the number of pieces is bounded above by

d_{\max}\cdot\Uplambda(n,m)+1.

in which
m
is the number of facets of
{\mathfrak{Q}}
.

Proof
.

Since the perturbation is given by polynomials, it – in particular – admits analytic continuation to a larger interval; hence, by Theorem
4.8
, we know
\mathrm{val}(\varepsilon)
has finitely many analytic pieces on
[0,1]
. These pieces are indeed polynomials since
h_{i}={\bf c}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\hat{v_{i}}
is a polynomial for all
i
.

The number of pieces is equal to
|\mathcal{S}\cap(0,1)|+1
. On the other hand, from the proof of Lemma
4.6
, one knows

\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{A}
. Let
d_{\max}:=\max d_{i}
; then,
h_{i}
is a polynomial of degree at most
d_{\max}
. The graphs of distinct two such polynomials can have no more than
d_{\max}
points of intersection; hence, we deduce

\left|\mathcal{A}\right|\leq d_{\max}\cdot\left|\mathcal{V}\right|.

By our Theorem
3.5
,
\left|\mathcal{V}\right|\leq\Uplambda(n,m)
holds where
m
is the number of facets of the constant feasible set
{\mathfrak{Q}}
. Therefore, we get

|\mathcal{S}\cap(0,1)|+1\leq d_{\max}\cdot\Uplambda(n,m)+1.

∎

Corollary 4.11
.

If
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}
is affine in
\varepsilon
, then the number of affine pieces of the optimal value function is bounded above by
\Uplambda(n,m)+1
.

5.
Some operator theoretic tools

Notation

Throughout the rest of these notes,
\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})
denotes the space of compact support (continuous) functions with respect to the combinatorial distance; notice these are functions with finite support.
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is the space of square
\mathdutchcal{m}
-summable functions and
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G})
is the space of square summable functions. In this section,
\mathrm{d}
is an arbitrary distance function on
\mathcal{G}
.

Definition 5.1

(self-adjoint property)
.

An operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})

is self adjoint if

\left<\mathcal{A}f,g\right>=\left<f,\mathcal{A}g\right>,\quad\forall f,g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m}),

where the inner product is

<f,g>:=\int_{\mathcal{G}}fgd\mathdutchcal{m}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{G}}f(z)g(z)\mathdutchcal{m}(z).

Operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G})
is said to be essentially self-adjoint if it has unique self-adjoint extension to
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
.

We say an operator
\mathcal{A}
has the self-adjoint property if
\mathcal{A}
is essentially self-adjoint and
\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})\subset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})
.

Definition 5.2

(rough comparison principle)
.

An operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is said to satisfy a rough comparison principle with range
R\geq 0
on a subspace
\mathbb{W}\subset\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
, if there exists a constant
C_{1}
such that for any
f
in
\mathbb{W}\cap\Lip(1)
, it holds

\text{$f$ has a global minimum at $x$}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{A}f(z)\geq C_{1},\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(x).

Definition 5.3

(two-sided rough comparison)
.

The operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is said to satisfy a two sided rough rough comparison principle on a subspace
\mathbb{W}\subset\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
, if there exists a function
C(x)
such that the following hold for
f\in\mathbb{W}\cap\Lip(1)

\text{$f$ has a global minimum at $x$}\Longrightarrow\left|\mathcal{A}f(x)\right|\leq C(x).

Definition 5.4

(rough differential operator)
.

Suppose
\mathdutchcal{m}\in\ell^{1}(\mathcal{G})
. An operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is said to be a rough differential operator whenever its kernel contains
1
(
\iff
all constants). Notice hypothesis
\mathdutchcal{m}\in\ell^{1}(\mathcal{G})
guarantees
1\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
. So these operators are translation invariant.

Definition 5.5

(weakly of divergence type)
.

An operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is said to be weakly of divergence type whenever
\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})\subset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})
and

\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{L}fd\mathdutchcal{m}=0,\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G}).

Definition 5.6

(semi-local operator)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally
\mathrm{d}
-finite graph. An operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is said to be semi-local with respect to
\mathrm{d}
with range
R
in the subspace
\mathbb{W}
, if there exists
C_{2}>0
such that

f\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)}=0\Longrightarrow{\mathcal{A}}f(x)=0,\quad\forall f\in\mathbb{W}\cap\Lip(1).

and

\left|\mathcal{B}_{2R}(x)\right|\leq C_{2},\quad\forall x.

An operator is said to be semi-local whenever it is semi-local with some range
R>0
.

Proposition 5.7

(two-sided comparison)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally
\mathrm{d}
-finite graph. Suppose
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is an operator with the following properties

(1)

\Lip(1)\subset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})
;

(2)

\mathcal{A}
is semi-local with range
R>0
;

(3)

\mathcal{A}
is a rough differential operator;

(4)

\mathcal{A}
satisfies a rough comparison principle on
\Lip_{1}(\mathcal{G})
with range
2R
;

(5)

there exists
C_{3}>0
such that for all
z
,
\left|\mathcal{A}\mathrm{d}(z,\cdot)(w)\right|\leq C_{3}
holds
\forall w\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}(z)
.

Then, there exists
C>0
depending on
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
,
\sup m\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\mathcal{B}_{2R}\left(x_{0}\right)}
such that

\text{$f$ has a global minimum at $x$}\Longrightarrow-\mathcal{A}f\left(x\right)\geq C\left(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},\sup\mathdutchcal{m}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\mathcal{B}_{2R}\left(x\right)}\right).

Proof
.

Suppose
f\in\Lip(1)
attains a global minimum at
x
; since
\mathcal{A}
is a rough differential operator, we can assume
f\geq 0
and
f(x)=0
.
Take the cut-off function

\upchi(z):=\left[2s-\mathrm{d}(x,z)\right]_{+},\quad s:=\sup\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)}\mathrm{d}(x,\cdot);

set
\bar{f}:=\upchi\wedge f
. It is easy to see that
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}(\upchi\wedge\bar{f})
is a
1
-Lipschitz function. We have

\upchi(z)\geq s\geq f(x,z),\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(x),

so

\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\bar{f}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}f\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\mathcal{B}_{R}(x)},

and notice both
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\bar{f}
and
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}f
are
1
-Lipschitz functions.

By the semi-locality hypothesis, we get

\mathcal{A}\bar{f}\left(x_{0}\right)=\mathcal{A}f\left(x_{0}\right).

For
z\not\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}\left(x_{0}\right)
, the semi-locality gives
A\bar{f}(z)=0
. Now being weakly of divergence form implies

\mathcal{A}\bar{f}\left(x_{0}\right)\mathdutchcal{m}\left(x_{0}\right)+\sum_{z\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}\left(x_{0}\right)}\mathcal{A}\bar{f}(z)\mathdutchcal{m}(z)=0,

hence, we deduce

-\mathcal{A}\bar{f}\left(x_{0}\right)\mathdutchcal{m}\left(x_{0}\right)=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}\left(x_{0}\right)}\mathcal{A}\bar{f}(z)\mathdutchcal{m}(z).

Now clearly
\nicefrac{{1}}{{8}}\left(\bar{f}(z)+4\mathrm{d}(x_{0},z)\right)\geq 0
is
1
-Lipschitz and has a global minimum at
z=x_{0}
. So by the rough comparison principle with range
2R
, one arrives at

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}(x)}\mathcal{A}\bar{f}(z)\mathdutchcal{m}(z)

\displaystyle\geq 8C_{1}-4\sum_{z\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}(x)}\mathdutchcal{m}(z)\left|\mathcal{A}\left(\mathrm{d}(x_{0},\cdot)\right)(z)\right|

\displaystyle\geq 8C_{1}-4C_{2}C_{3}\left(\sup\mathdutchcal{m}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\mathcal{B}_{2R}}\right):=C.

So clearly,

-\mathcal{A}f\left(x_{0}\right)=-\mathcal{A}\bar{f}\left(x_{0}\right)\geq C\left(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},\sup\mathdutchcal{m}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\mathcal{B}_{2R}\left(x_{0}\right)}\right),

which is the desired conclusion.
∎

Definition 5.8

(good operators)
.

The operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m})
is said to be a good operator if it satisfies the following properties

(1)

\mathcal{A}
satisfies self-adjoint property;

(2)

\mathcal{A}
is a rough differential operator;

(3)

\mathcal{A}
is weakly of divergence form.

Remark 5.9
.

Except for the definition of the self-adjoint property and consequently for the definition of good operators, all other definitions also make sense for more general operators
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{G}}\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{G}}
that we will deal with in §
6.3.7
.

6.
Discrete Ollivier-Ricci curvature

6.1.
Discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature

Discrete-time Ollivier coarse Ricci curvature,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)
, of a random walk
\left\{\upmu_{z}\right\}_{z\in X}
(here, a random walk is a measurable point dependent measure
\upmu_{z}
on a polish metric space
X
with finite first moments) along
xy
(
x,y
are two points in the space), is defined as the “
deviance
” of the “
ratio of
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance of the corresponding probabilities and the distance of
x
and
y
” from
1

[
20
]
.

In the setting of Riemannian manifolds with
\Ric\geq K
– and by using
\varepsilon
-step random walks characterized by uniform measures
\upmu^{\varepsilon}_{z}
on
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(z)
– the Ollivier coarse curvature possesses nice precise asymptotics depending on distance and Ricci curvature; indeed, when
d(x,y)\approx\varepsilon
, the Ollivier coarse Ricci curvature satisfies

(6.1)

\displaystyle{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\nicefrac{{\varepsilon^{2}}}{{2(N+2)}}\,\Ric\left(\nicefrac{{\vec{xy}}}{{\|\vec{xy}\|}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right);

here,
\vec{xy}
denotes the vector in the tangent space solving
\exp_{x}{\vec{xy}}=y

[
20
]
.

In the setting of continuous-time Markov processes, one can assume the measures depend on time and represent how the delta measures diffuse in space (according to some differential equation
\nicefrac{{\partial}}{{\partial t}}u=\mathcal{L}u
). Then according to
[
20
]
, taking the derivative in the Ollivier coarse Ricci curvature, one computes the continuous-time Ollivier coarse Ricci curvature to be

\displaystyle{{}^{\mathcal{CTO}}\Ric}(x,y)=\text{-}\,\nicefrac{{d}}{{d\varepsilon}}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\varepsilon=0}\;\nicefrac{{\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)}}{{\mathrm{d}_{X}(x,y)}}=\nicefrac{{d}}{{d\varepsilon}}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\varepsilon=0}{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon};

this indeed computes the first order (rescaled) limit of the discrete-time curvature. In the original paper
[
20
]
, this definition is presented contingent upon the the existence of such a limit. That is an important issue which we wish to address – in the discrete setting – in these notes.

Remark 6.1
.

If we take the derivative at
\varepsilon=0
in (
6.1
), we get zero; meaning infinitesimally speaking, random walking on a manifold or the Euclidean space are the same i.e. Riemannian manifolds are infinitesimally Euclidean to the second order or the infinitesimal walks do not see the curvature.

So in the continuous spaces, Ollivier coarse
\varepsilon
-step curvature is more natural to use than the limit version. Or even better, one can use a suitable second derivative version instead e.g.

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathsf{Riem}}(x,y):=\text{-}\,\nicefrac{{d^{2}}}{{d\varepsilon^{2}}}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\varepsilon=0}\;\nicefrac{{\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)}}{{\mathrm{d}(x,y)}}.

which at least in the Riemannian setting – and up to a dimensional multiple of – is comparable to the Ricci curvature lower bound; see the asymptotics in
[
26
]
. We will shortly see that the second derivative version gives zero when applied in graphs and for time-affine walks. So on graphs the second order geometry is trivial for this particular type of random walks.

On graphs, both the discrete-time and continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvatures can be made sense of, exactly as in the continuous setting. Of course these curvatures depend on the random walks that one chooses and for the continuous-time discrete Ollivier-Ricci curvature, existence of the defining derivative must be proven.

6.2.
Discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature

Given a random walk
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
with finite first moment, and using a given distance
\mathrm{d}
, the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvatures are defined as

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=1-\nicefrac{{\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)}}{{\mathrm{d}(x,y)}},

in which, the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein (or Kantorovich-Rubinstein) distance is

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{1},\upmu_{2}\right):=\inf_{q}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{d}(x,y)q(x,y),

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
q\in{\mathscr{P}}(\mathcal{G}\hskip 1.0pt{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\square$}}}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{G})
of
\upmu_{1}
and
\upmu_{2}
; coupling means
\upmu_{1}
and
\upmu_{2}
are marginals of
q
. The above is well-defined since
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance between measures with finite first moment is well-defined. In the sequel, we always assume finite first moments.

6.2.1.
Localization

Lemma 6.2
.

For every
x
, set
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{x}:=\mathrm{supp}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}\right)
. Then, there exists an optimal coupling
q
of
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{x}
and
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{y}
supported in
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}\times\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
where
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}:=\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{x}\cup\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{y}
.

Proof
.

This is straightforward from the fact that the first and second marginals of the couplings are supported in
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{x}
and
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{y}
resp. Hence, the couplings must be supported in
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
. The standard existence of optimal plans theorem
[
25
, Theorem 4.1]
.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

For finite step walks,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
coincides with the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature on the graph
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
and is the optimal value in a finite LP problem.

Proof
.

This is a direct consequence of Lemma
6.2
.
∎

6.2.2.
A particular form of random walks

A particular yet still rather general class of
1
-parameter random walks are

\uptheta_{z}^{\varepsilon}(w):=\begin{cases}\nicelambda_{1}(\varepsilon)+\nicelambda_{2}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{1}(z)&\text{if}\quad w=z\\
\nicelambda_{3}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{2}(z,w)&\text{if}\quad w\neq z\end{cases},\quad\text{$\nicelambda_{i}$ is continuous for $i=1,2,3$.}

Of course since these are probability measures, we need the normalization

\nicelambda_{1}(\varepsilon)+\nicelambda_{2}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{1}(z)+\sum_{w\neq z}\nicelambda_{3}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{2}(z,w)=1,

and the constraints

\nicelambda_{1}(\varepsilon)+\nicelambda_{2}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{1}(z),\nicelambda_{3}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{2}(z,w)\geq 0;

and we need the convergence to
\updelta_{x}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
so,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\nicelambda_{3}(\varepsilon)\varphi_{2}(z,w)=0,

is also needed. We call these,
\uptheta
-walks. Notice there is no assumption on symmetricity of
\varphi_{2}
.

Example 2.
The
\uptheta
-walks provides an important class of random walks on graphs. Upon setting
\nicelambda_{1}(\varepsilon)\equiv 1
,
\nicelambda_{2}(\varepsilon)\equiv-1
,
\nicelambda_{3}=\varepsilon
,
\varphi_{1}(z)=\mathrm{Deg}(z)
, and

\varphi_{2}(z,w)=\begin{cases}\nicefrac{{\omega_{xy}}}{{\mathdutchcal{m}(z)}}&\text{if}\quad w\sim z\\
0&\text{if}\quad w\not\sim z\end{cases},

one retrieves the random walks

\upbeta_{z}^{\varepsilon}(w):=\updelta_{z}(w)+\varepsilon\Delta\updelta_{z}(w)=\begin{cases}1-\varepsilon\mathrm{Deg}(z)&\text{if}\quad w=z\\
\nicefrac{{\varepsilon\omega_{zw}}}{{\mathdutchcal{m}(z)}}&\text{if}\quad w\sim z\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

which were considered in
[
13
]
. Recall
\mathrm{Deg}(z)
is computed using the edge-weights
\omega
. Furthermore, the spacial case
\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}_{\omega}
is considered in
[
17
]
. We call these
\upbeta
-walks.

6.2.3.
Curvature concavity for time-affine local walks

Theorem 6.5

(concavity)
.

Let
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
be a time-affine walk, then
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is a concave function of
\varepsilon
.

Proof
.

The Kantorovich dual formulation of Wasserstein distance (
6.3
) applied to linear walks, gives the Wasserstein distance as the supremum of a family of lines, so it must be convex since since supremum of a suitable family of convex functions is convex. Now let us provide a rigorous proof.

It is straightforward to see that for all
\varepsilon
, all couplings form a convex polytope
\mathfrak{P}_{\varepsilon}
(not necessarily bounded) in
\mathbb{R}^{\aleph_{0}}
(sometimes called the transportation polytope). Due to the affinity of
\upmu^{\varepsilon}_{z}(w)
in
\varepsilon
, one immediately deduces the geometric concavity property

(1-t)\mathfrak{P}_{\varepsilon_{1}}+t\mathfrak{P}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\subset\mathfrak{P}_{(1-t)\varepsilon_{1}+t\varepsilon_{2}}.

In particular, letting
q^{\varepsilon_{1}}
,
q^{\varepsilon_{2}}
be optimal couplings (for existence of optimal couplings, e.g. see
[
25
, Theorem 4.1]
) for
\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon_{1}},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon_{1}}
and
\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon_{2}},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon_{2}}
resp.; then

\bar{q}:=(1-t)q^{\varepsilon_{1}}+tq^{\varepsilon_{2}},

is a coupling for two measures
\upmu_{x}^{(1-t)\varepsilon_{1}+t\varepsilon_{2}}
and
\upmu_{y}^{(1-t)\varepsilon_{1}+t\varepsilon_{2}}
. Using
{\bar{q}}
as a test coupling, we deduce

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{(1-t)\varepsilon_{1}+t\varepsilon_{2}},\upmu_{y}^{(1-t)\varepsilon_{1}+t\varepsilon_{2}}\right)\leq(1-t)\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon_{1}},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)+t\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon_{2}},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\right),

which is the convexity of
\mathcal{W}_{1}
in
\varepsilon
. This – directly from the definition – implies concavity of
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
in
\varepsilon
; namely,

(6.2)

\displaystyle{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{(1-t)\varepsilon_{1}+t\varepsilon_{2}}(x,y)\geq(1-t){{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x,y)+t{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(x,y).

∎

Remark 6.6
.

This concavity does not necessarily hold for non time-affine random walks. However one expects an infinitesimal version of this concavity (as well as affinity) to hold for all walks (think of a concavity for the linear approximation of random walks); indeed – thanks to a general limit-free formulation – such infinitesimal version of concavity holds for the Ollivier-Ricci curvature in the form of a concavity relation in terms of the generating operators
\mathcal{L}
in the operator theoretic Ollivier-Ricci curvature
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}
; see §
6.37
.

Corollary 6.7

The concavity in
\varepsilon
holds for
\upbeta
,
\upzeta
and
\upxi
-walks as well as for all other time-affine
\uptheta
-walks .

6.2.4.
Localization in the dual problem

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
be a finite-step random walk. then

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}\to\mathbb{R}\\
f\in\Lip(1)\end{subarray}}\sum f(z)\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}(z)-\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right);

recall
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}:=\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{x}\cup\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{y}
.

Proof
.

Consider the numbers

s_{x}:=\max_{z\in\Upomega_{x}}\mathrm{d}(x,z),\quad s_{y}:=\max_{w\in\Upomega_{y}}\mathrm{d}(y,z),

and set

s:=s_{x}\vee s_{y}+\mathrm{d}(x,y).

By Kantorovich’s duality
[
25
, Chapter 5]
, it follows

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)=\sup_{f\in\Lip(1)}\sum f(z)\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}(z)-\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right);

so, we will instead look for a localized minimizer
f
for the dual problem.

Translating
f
by a constant would not affect the optimal value in (
6.3
), hence, we can assume
f(x)=0
. Take the cut-off function

\upchi(z):=\left[s\wedge\left(2s-\mathrm{d}(x,z)\right)\right]_{+},

It is easy to check that
\upchi.
, is
1
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\mathrm{d}_{\eta}
and equals
s
on
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
. Hence, we deduce
\upchi\wedge f
coincides with
f
on
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
; indeed, for
z\in\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{y}
, one easily gets

f(z)\leq f(y)+s_{y}\leq\mathrm{d}(x,y)+s_{y}\leq s.

Now set

\tilde{f}:=\left(-\upchi\vee f\right)\wedge\upchi.

Clearly,
\tilde{f}
coincides with
f
on
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
so in particular,
\tilde{f}
is
1
-Lipschitz within
\Upomega_{xy}
(however it is
4
Lipschitz at best over
\mathcal{G}
); furthermore,
\tilde{f}
is supported in
\mathcal{B}^{\eta}_{2s}(x)
. Since
\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}
and
\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}
are supported in
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
, we deduce

\sum f(z)\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}(z)-\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right)=\sum\tilde{f}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}}(z)\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}(z)-\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right).

Notice
\tilde{f}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}}
is a
1
-Lip function on
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
.

So the problem becomes a finite linear programming problem hence, admits a minimizer
\tilde{f}
and by construction this minimizer is supported in
\mathcal{B}_{2s}(x)
. This means

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\raisebox{-0.57503pt}{$\big|$}_{\Upomega_{xy}}\in\Lip(1)\\
\mathrm{supp}\left(f\right)\subset\mathcal{B}^{\eta}_{2s}(x)\end{subarray}}\sum f(z)\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}(z)-\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right);

To furthermore localize to
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
, suppose
f:\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}\to\mathbb{R}
is
1
-Lipschitz, then the standard construction

\hat{f}(z):=\sup_{w\in\mathcal{B}^{\eta}_{2s}(x)}\left(f(w)-\mathrm{d}(z,w)\right),

produces a
1
-Lipschitz extension of
f
over
\mathcal{B}_{2s}(x)
. This implies we can further localize the minimizer to be a
1
-Lipschitz function with domain
\Upomega^{\varepsilon}_{xy}
.
∎

Theorem 6.9

(regularity)

Suppose
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}

Let us consider the Kantorovich’s dual formulation

6.3.1.
Restriction of Kantorovich duality to finite support functions

At some places, we will need to be able to restrict the maximization in Kantorovich duality to finite support test functions. The following theorem affords us the said restriction.

Theorem 6.13
.

For any two measures
\upmu
and
\upnu
with finite first moments,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu,\upnu\right)=\sup_{f\in\Lip(1)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})}\sum f(z)\left(\upmu(z)-\upnu(z)\right),

Proof
.

By Kanotorvitch duality, we know

\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu,\upnu\right)=\sup_{f\in\Lip(1)}\sum f(z)\left(\upmu(z)-\upnu(z)\right);

hence,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu,\upnu\right)\geq\sup_{f\in\Lip(1)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})}\sum f(z)\left(\upmu(z)-\upnu(z)\right).

Take an exhaustion

Then clearly
\upmu_{i}\rightharpoonup\upmu
and
\upnu_{i}\rightharpoonup\upnu
(weakly) in the space of probability measures with first finite moments.
As a result,

25

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(\upmu,\upnu)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\Lip(1)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})}\sum f(z)\left(\upmu(z)-\upnu(z)\right).

Combining (

∎

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\sup_{f\in\Lip(1)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})}\sum\updelta_{z}(w)\left(\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}f\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\sup_{f\in\Lip(1)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})}\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}f(z).

where the equality before last, holds by the self-adjoint property of
\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}
.

Since
\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}
is a rough differential operator, we can assume the normalization
f(z)=0
. Therefore, we get
f(w)\leq\mathrm{d}(z,w)
. The function

\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left(\mathrm{d}(z,\cdot)-f(\cdot)\right),

is a
1
-Lipschitz nonnegative function attaining a global minimim at
z
; hence, by rough the comparison principle with range
0
, one gets

\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}\left(\mathrm{d}(z,\cdot)-f(\cdot)\right)(z)\geq-C_{\varepsilon},

which means

\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}f(z)\leq\mathcal{J}^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}(z,\cdot)(z)+2C_{\varepsilon}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{-1+\min\alpha_{i}}\right);

this in turn implies

\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{1+\min\alpha_{i}}\right),\quad\forall z.

As a result,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)-\mathcal{W}\left({{}^{1}}\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right|=\left|\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)\pm\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}\right)-\mathcal{W}\left({{}^{1}}\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)-\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right|+\left|\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)-\mathcal{W}\left({{}^{1}}\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)+\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{1+\min\alpha_{i}}\right).

Thus,

\left|\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\Big(1-\mathcal{W}\left(\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)\Big)-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\Big(1-\mathcal{W}\left({{}^{1}}\upmu_{x}^{\varepsilon},{{}^{1}}\upmu_{y}^{\varepsilon}\right)\Big)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{\min\alpha_{i}}\right);

upon taking limit as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we deuce

\Ric(x,y)={{}^{1}}\Ric(x,y);

notice the existence of
{{}^{1}}\Ric(x,y)
(curvature w.r.t. the 1-jet random walk which is time-affine) was previously established in Theorem
6.16
.
∎

6.3.5.

A very useful limit-free formulation of discrete Ollivier-Ricci curvature and for

-walks was first observed in

We start with a continuous-time walk which is not necessarily Markovian. Let
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
be a local walk that is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in

Define the (non-log-linear in

\displaystyle\Uptheta^{\varepsilon}(f)(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Upomega_{z}}f(w)d\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}(w)

\displaystyle=f(z)+\int_{\Upomega_{z}}\Big(f(w)-f(z)\Big)d\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}(w)

\displaystyle=f(z)+\sum_{\Upomega_{z}\smallsetminus\{z\}}\left(f(w)-f(z)\right)\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}(w)

\varepsilon

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\left(\Uptheta^{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{I}\right)f(z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\sum_{\Upomega_{z}\smallsetminus\{z\}}\left(f(w)-f(z)\right)\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}(w)

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{d}}{{d\varepsilon}}\raisebox{-0.82147pt}{$\big|$}_{\varepsilon=0}\Psi^{\varepsilon}f(x)

is not necessarily a semigroup (hence, the term non-linear).
Due to the form it takes, one can think of

\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}_{\eta}
.

Example 7.
Setting
\eta=\omega
, then locally finiteness of
\mathcal{G}
immediately implies the finiteness of all
\mathrm{d}_{\omega}
balls, hence,
\mathcal{G}
is locally
\mathrm{d}_{\omega}
-finite. Hence, all the results thus far apply. In particular, using

.

Remark 6.32
.

In practice, when dealing with complex multi-dimensional networks, there are a set of edge weights each representing a certain communication in the network. The general framework we presented in this article allows the random walks to depend on a primary edge weight
\omega_{0}
and another family of edge weights
\omega_{i}
,
i\geq 1
or even more general functions. Our constructions also allows for the distance function to be induced by a secondary edge weight

6.3.7.
Operator theoretic Ollivier-Ricci curvature

The limit-free formulation enables us to generalize the Ollivier-Ricci curvature to be defined for operators instead of random walks.

Definition 6.33
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an arbitrary operator

\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{G}\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{L})\to\mathbb{R}^{G}.

The corresponding operator-theoretic Ollivier-Ricci curvature is defined by

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}(x,y):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})\\
f\in\Lip(1)\\
\nabla_{xy}f=1\end{subarray}}\nabla_{yx}\mathcal{L}f,

provided that
\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})\subset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{L})
, that is a very weak condition. Notice we might get

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)=-\infty
.

Remark 6.34
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is weakly of divergence form i.e.

\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{L}f=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f,\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G}).

Then,
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}:=e^{\varepsilon\mathcal{L}}\updelta_{z}
is a continuous-time Markovian random walk which is in many cases not a local walk. This means the theory developed in these notes, does not ensure that Ollivier-Ricci curvature is well-defined for
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
; however, the operator-theoretic Ollivier-Ricci curvature is well-defined even though it could be
-\infty
.

Recall
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{G}\supset\mathsf{Dom}(\mathcal{L})\to\mathbb{R}^{G}
is said to be a bounded operator w.r.t. the sup-norm if there exits
B>0
such that

\|\mathcal{L}f\|_{\sf sup}\leq B\|f\|_{\sup},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G}).

Theorem 6.35
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the following properties

(1)

\mathcal{L}
is a rough differential operator;

(2)

\mathcal{L}
satisfies a two-sided rough comparison principle (see Definition
5.3
);

Then,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)
is finite.

Proof
.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is a rough differential operator, we can - with no loss of generality – assume a given test function satisfies
f(x)=0
,
f(y)=\mathrm{d}(x,y)
.

The function

\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left(\mathrm{d}(x,z)-f(z)\right),

is a nonnegative
1
-Lipschitz function attaining a global minimum at
x
so by the rough maximum principle with range zero, one deuces

\mathcal{L}\left(\mathrm{d}(x,\cdot)\right)(x)-C_{1}(x)\leq\mathcal{L}f(x)\leq\mathcal{L}\left(\mathrm{d}(x,\cdot)\right)(x)+C_{1}(x);

similarly

\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left(\mathrm{d}(y,z)-f(z)+\mathrm{d}(x,y)\right),

is nonnegative and has a global minimum at
y
hence, from the hypotheses, we also get

\mathcal{L}\left(\mathrm{d}(x,\cdot)\right)(x)-C_{1}(y)\leq\mathcal{L}f(y)\leq\mathcal{L}\left(\mathrm{d}(y,\cdot)\right)(y)+C_{1}(y).

therefore
\nabla_{yx}\mathcal{L}f

is bounded and the infimum is finite.
∎

Theorem 6.36
.

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}}(x,y)
is finite for all bounded semi-local rough differential operators
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof
.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is a rough differential operator, it is translation invariant on
\mathcal{C}_{\sf fs}(\mathcal{G})
so we can assume our test functions satisfy
f(x)=0
and therefore they must satisfy
f(y)=\mathrm{d}(x,y)
. By semi-locality and by Lipschitz extension construction in the proof of Theorem
6.8
(see the bottom of page 19), we only need to find the infimum over test functions that are supported in

\mathcal{B}_{2s}(x)
for
s=R+\mathrm{d}(x,y)
and
\|f\|_{\sf sup}\leq 2s
.

So for these test functions, by boundedness of
\mathcal{L}
, we deuce
\nabla_{yx}\mathcal{L}f
is bounded hence, its infimum is finite.
∎

Proposition 6.37
.

For two operators
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, as in the above two theorems,

(6.11)

\displaystyle{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{t\mathcal{L}_{1}+(1-t)\mathcal{L}_{2}}\geq t{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}+(1-t){{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}.

Proof
.

This is straightforward from the set-theoretic concavity of infimum.
∎

Remark 6.38
.

The concavity (
6.11
) is the infinitesimal (first order) version of the concavity (
6.2
) and holds for a wider class of operators than the generators of pleasant Markovian walks; e.g.
\mathcal{L}
need not be weakly of divergence type.

6.3.8.
Lipschitz regularity

One important consequence of having a limit-free formulation at our disposal is that we can compute the continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature as the optimal value of an LP problem; hence, we can for example show Lipschitz continuity of
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
in terms of the distance and the
1
-jet of the random walk for pleasant Markovian walks appearing in Corollary
6.29
.

Based on the proof of Theorem
6.18
, the Ollivier-Ricci curvature
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)
for pleasant local walks

\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}=\updelta_{z}+\varepsilon\upmu_{z}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{R}_{z},

is a function of
\upmu_{z}
and of the distance
\mathrm{d}
hence, we will write
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(\mathrm{d},\upmu)
and we wish to establish Lipschitz regularity in the
\mathrm{d}
and
\upmu
arguments. Let
\mathcal{M}_{\sf s}(\mathcal{G})
be the space of zero-mass signed measures on
\mathcal{G}
equipped with the sup-norm; notice
\mathcal{M}_{\sf s}(\mathcal{G})
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}
. Also let
\mathsf{D}_{\sf c}(\mathcal{G})
be the space of distances
\mathrm{d}
for which
\left(\mathcal{G},\mathrm{d}\right)
is metrically complete also equipped with the sup-norm; again a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}
.

Set

\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{K}}_{\sf s}(\mathcal{G}):=\mathcal{M}_{\sf s}(\mathcal{G})\cap\left\{\upmu\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;\mathrm{supp}(\upmu)\subset\mathcal{K}\right\}.

Theorem 6.39

(Lipschitz continuity)
.

Restricted to pleasant walks

\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}=\updelta_{z}+\varepsilon\upmu_{z}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{R}_{z},

with
\upmu_{x},\upmu_{y}\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{K}}_{\sf s}(\mathcal{G})
for fixed
x
and
y
and some finite
\mathcal{K}
, the quantity
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(\mathrm{d},\upmu)
is Lipschitz continuous in the
\mathrm{d}
and
\upmu_{z}
arguments.

Proof
.

By the limit-free formulation established in Corollary
6.28
, the identity

(6.12)

\displaystyle{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}\\
f\in\Lip(1)\\
\nabla_{xy}f=1\end{subarray}}\nabla_{yx}\mathcal{L}(f),

holds with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f(z)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{K}\smallsetminus\{z\}}\left(f(w)-f(z)\right)\upmu_{z}(w).

Suppose
\left|\mathcal{K}\right|=N
, (
6.12
) is the optimal value in a linear programming problem in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. There are
2N(N-1)
constraints. Due to the form of
\mathcal{L}
, this problem is again translation invariant so adding another constraint
f(x)=\diam\mathcal{K}
, would ensure non-negativity of the feasible set.

Based on the hypothesis, we only need to study the perturbation of
\upmu
and
\mathrm{d}
on the set
\mathcal{K}
; so the problem at hand is a problem of the form

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(\mathrm{d},\upmu):{\sf LP}_{\bf d}:=\begin{cases}\min{\bf c}(\mathrm{d},\upmu)\cdot\hat{x}\\
{\bf a}\hat{x}\leq{\bf b}(\mathrm{d})\\
\hat{x}\geq 0\end{cases},\quad{\bf d}(\mathrm{d},\upmu):=({\bf a},{\bf b}(\mathrm{d}),{\bf c}(\mathrm{d},\upmu)),

in which the arguments
\mathrm{d}
and
\upmu
are functions with finite domains so we in particular have
\mathrm{d}
is bounded away from zero.

First notice that both
{\bf c}(\mathrm{d},\upmu)
and
{\bf b}(\mathrm{d})
are Lipschitz functions of their arguments. This means to establish Lipschitz continuity of
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(\mathrm{d},\upmu)
in
\mathrm{d}
and
\upmu
, we need to just establish the Lipschitz continuity of the optimal value function of
{\sf LP}_{\bf d}
in terms of its vector variable
{\bf d}
; namely, we only need to show the optimal value function is Lipschitz in
{\bf a}
,
{\bf b}
and
\bf{c}
.

Now, in order to establish the latter Lipschitz continuity by invoking Proposition
4.1
, we need to ensure that the problem is feasible and is away from the boundary of the ill-posed region of parameters. Indeed, since we have a finite linear programming problem, this would automatically imply feasibility and distance to the ill-posed region for the dual problem as well. For the problem at hand these claims clearly hold since as long as
\mathrm{d}
stays within the space
\mathsf{D}_{\sf c}(\mathcal{G})
, and as long as
\upmu
is in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{K}}_{\sf s}(\mathcal{G})
, Theorem
6.18
ensures the existence of the optimal value. This means
\mathbf{d}
is way from the boundary of ill-posed region for
\mathrm{d}
and
\upmu
parameters in both primary and the dual problem. So, by Proposition
4.1
, the Lipschitz continuity follows.
∎

Suppose a pleasant local walk is a locally Lipschitz function of a primary edge weight

and a set of other parameters

.

Proposition 6.44

For time-affine walks, and under the transformation

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{c\varepsilon}(x,y)={{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon_{i}}+s_{i}\left(c\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{i}\right),\quad\text{for},\quad\nicefrac{{\varepsilon_{i}}}{{c}}\leq\varepsilon\leq\nicefrac{{\varepsilon_{i+1}}}{{c}},

holds true, where

6.3.10.
Switching times and bifurcation of optimal trajectories

The switching times in the function
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}
for time-analytic local random walks indicate singularities in the discrete-time curvature function (as well as in the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
). At these singularities, the maximizer in the Kantorovich dual formulation changes (from one vertex of the feasible convex polytope to another in the same facet).

Based on the Kantorvich-Rubinstein duality (see
[
25
, Theorem 5.10, item 5.4 and item 5.16]
), we know the optimal plans are supported on the
\mathrm{d}
-sub-differential of the maximizer
f
. So as the maximizer suddenly changes, the trajectories along which mass is transported also change. If we compare these trajectories to gradient flow of a potential function
f
in the smooth setting, then the change in the trajectories compares to a change in the qualitative behavior of solutions of an ode. So in a sense, at the switching times, the optimal trajectories bifurcate yet in a sudden discontinuous manner. It is worth mentioning that this is a very heuristic discussion since especially for the
\mathrm{L}^{1}
cost, the geometry of optimal transport trajectories become very irregular compared to the higher
\mathrm{L}^{p}
costs. However, in principle, what was said still makes sense.

For time-analytic local walks, one can capture the infinitesimal effect of the said bifurcation on the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature at the switching times by defining the set-valued bifurcating continuous-time Ollivier-Ricci curvature

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}^{\varepsilon}_{\sf b}(x,y):=\left\{\nicefrac{{d^{-}}}{{d\varepsilon}}{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y),\nicefrac{{d^{+}}}{{d\varepsilon}}{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\right\},

which is defined for all values of
\varepsilon
. Obviously
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)={{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}^{0}_{\sf b}(x,y)
.
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}^{\varepsilon}_{\sf b}(x,y)
is single-valued except possibly at the switching times. Notice for time-analytic local walks,
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}^{\varepsilon}_{\sf b}(x,y)
takes finite values.

Another useful quantity to investigate is

\delta{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}^{\varepsilon}_{\sf b}(x,y):=\left(\nicefrac{{d^{+}}}{{d\varepsilon}}-\nicefrac{{d^{-}}}{{d\varepsilon}}\right){{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y).

For example, for time-affine random walks, the concavity in
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}
yields that
\delta{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}^{\varepsilon}_{\sf b}(x,y)
is everywhere zero except at the switching times where it is negative.

Derivative of
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}
measures the rate at which the walks converge in Wasserstein distance and a sudden change in this quantity indicates how – on average – strong the bifurcation of optimal trajectories is. For time-affine walks interestingly enough, at each bifurcation the said rate increases.

6.3.11.
Ollivier scalar curvature bounds

A natural definition of discrete-time scalar curvature for finite step walks is

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\scal}_{\varepsilon}(x):=\sum_{y\in\Upomega_{x}}{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}_{\varepsilon}(x,y),

and the continuous-time scalar curvature is defied by

{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\scal}(x):=\sum_{y\sim x}{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y).

7.
Continuous-time discrete Ollivier-Ricci curvature flows

The continuous time Ollivier-Ricci flow was proposed in
[
19
]
as a natural generalization of Ricci flow to the discrete setting using the Ollivier curvature; a
1
-parameter family of distances on a space
X
(perhaps a graph) is said to be an Ollivier-Ricci flow whenever the continuous time equation

/

d

dt

\,\mathrm{d}(x,y)=-{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)\mathrm{d}(x,y),

is satisfied where
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
at time
t
is defined using a suitably chosen random walk on graphs and using distance
\mathrm{d}
at time
t
. For example, if the distance is the weighted distance (since the combinatorial one does not evolve), this flow becomes

/

d

dt

\,\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(x,y)=-{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}(x,y)\mathrm{d}_{\omega}(x,y);

the issue with the latter is that in the setting of weighted graphs the solutions will not be unique. To remedy this, one can instead consider the more local flow

\dot{\omega}=-{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}\cdot\omega,

as the Ricci flow in the singly weighted setting. Another approach would be to take a fusion of the last two and define Ricci flow such as (but not exclusively)

\dot{\omega}=-{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\omega}.

In these notes, we have developed a general theory for Olivier-Ricci curvature and have seen many important properties such as the Lipschitz continuity for
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
; so it is natural to also explore the Ricci flow in this generalized context. In an upcoming work, we explore the discrete-time Ollivier-Ricci flows and in these notes, we will only consider the continuous-time version.

In below, we consider general curvature flows corresponding to Ollivier-Ricci curvature however we will only consider finite graphs.

Definition 7.1

(Generalized Ollivier-Ricci curvature flows)
.

Let
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
be a pleasant local walk that is locally Lipschitz in
\omega
. A
1
-parameter family

\mathcal{G}(t):=\left(\mathcal{G},\mathdutchcal{m}(t),\omega(t),\mathrm{d}(t)\right),

of quadruples is called a generalized Ollivier-Ricci flow if it is a solution to the ODE system

(7.1)

\displaystyle{\sf ORF}_{f,g,h}:\begin{cases}\dot{\omega}=f(t,\omega,\mathrm{d},\mathdutchcal{m},{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric})\\
\dot{\mathrm{d}}=g\left(t,\omega,\mathrm{d},\mathdutchcal{m},{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}\right)\\
\dot{\mathdutchcal{m}}=h\left(t,\omega,\mathrm{d},\mathdutchcal{m},{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\scal}\right)\end{cases}

Theorem 7.2

(Short time existence and uniqueness)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be finite. Suppose
f,g
and
h
are locally uniformly Lipschitz in
t
and locally Lipschitz in other arguments. Starting from an initial condition
\left(G,\mathdutchcal{m}(0),\omega(0),\mathrm{d}(0)\right)
with
\mathrm{d}(0)\in\mathrm{int}\left(\mathsf{D}_{\sf c}(\mathcal{G})\right)
and with
\mathrm{supp}(\mathdutchcal{m})=\mathcal{G}
. Then, there exists a unique solution to
{\sf ORF}_{f,g},h
for a short time. The solution is at least
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
in time
t
and is Lipschitz in the other arguments.

Proof
.

Unlike the smooth Ricci flow which is a weakly parabolic pde, the continuous time Ollivier Ricci flow is indeed a (not necessarily autonomous) ODE system with phase space

\mathbb{R}^{|E|}\times\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{G})\times\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\times\mathbb{R}^{|V|(|V|-1)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{|E|+2|V|^{2}-|V|}.

where
\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{G})
is the space of distance functions which is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{|V|(|V|-1)}
.

In the interior of the phase space – by the standard ode theory (Picard - Lindelöf theorem) – the existence of solutions follow from the continuity of the RHS while the uniqueness needs locally uniformly Lipschitz continuity of the RHS in
t
and locally Lipschitz continuity in other variables; e.g. see
[
24
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Local Lipschitz continuity of
{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}
was established in Theorem
6.39
(recall
\mathcal{G}
is finite here so all walks are local) so this readily implies the RHS of (
7.1
) is locally Lipschitz in
\mathrm{d}
and
\omega
. Also from the hypothesis, RHS is uniformly locally Lispchitz in
t
so the conclusion follows.
∎

Theorem 7.3

(long-time solution)
.

Starting from
\mathcal{G}(0)
in the interior of the phase space, the generalized Ollivier-Ricci curvature flow
{\sf ORF}_{f,g,h}
admits a unique maximal solution. Indeed the flow can be continued as long as
\omega,\mathdutchcal{m}>0
and
\mathrm{d}
is in the interior of
\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{G})
.

7.0.1.
Classic Ollivier-Ricci flows

Set
\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}_{\omega}
. Consider the well-posed Ollivier-Ricci flow given by

(7.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{\omega}=-{{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\Ric}\cdot\omega\\
\dot{\mathdutchcal{m}}=-h\left({{}^{\mathcal{O}}\!\scal}\right)\cdot\mathdutchcal{m}\end{cases},

so when
h\equiv 0
, we just get a flow of
\omega
and when
h=\mathrm{id}
, we get an evolution on vertex weights modeled on the smooth case.

Corollary 7.4
.

Set
\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}_{\omega}
and suppose
\upmu_{z}^{\varepsilon}
is a random walk that is locally Lipschitz in
\omega
, then the Ollivier-Ricci flow (
7.2
) starting with

, has a unique solution. The maximal solution exists as long as

.

Proof
.

This is a direct consequence of Corollary
6.40
and Theorem
7.2
.
∎
