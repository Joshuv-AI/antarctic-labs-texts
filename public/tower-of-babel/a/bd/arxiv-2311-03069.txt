Analytical approximations of Lotka-Volterra integrals
Niklas L.P. Lundström, Gunnar J. Söderbacka

Abstract
In this note we derive simple analytical bounds for solutions of x − ln ⁡ x = y − ln ⁡ y x-\ln x=y-\ln y and use them for estimating trajectories following Lotka-Volterra-type integrals. We show how our results imply estimates for the Lambert W W function as well as for trajectories of general predator-prey systems, including e.g. Rosenzweig-MacArthur equations. 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 34D23, 34C05. Keywords: Rosenzweig MacArthur; Predator prey; Size of limit cycle; Lyapunov; Lambert W; LambertW

Introduction

Consider the Lotka-Volterra system

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t}=aS-bSX,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}=cSX-dX,

where the non-negative variable
S=S(t)
represents prey and the non-negative variable
X=X(t)
predator biomass,
and
a
,
b
,
c
and
d
are positive constants.
By introducing the nondimensional quantities

x(\tau)=\frac{b}{a}X(t),\,s(\tau)=\frac{c}{d}S(t),\,\tau=at,\,\alpha=\frac{d}{a}

we obtain the equivalent system

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=(1-x)s,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\alpha x(s-1),

(1.1)

which, by the corresponding phase-plane equation, has its trajectories on level curves of

\displaystyle V(x,s)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(x-\ln x\right)+s-\ln s.

(1.2)

In this note we derive analytical bounds for solutions of the Lotka-Volterra integral (
1.2
).
Indeed, we prove that the solution
x<1
of the equation

\displaystyle x-\ln x=y-\ln y,\quad\text{where}\quad y>1,

(1.3)

satisfies the relation
x=zY
, where
Y=ye^{-y}
and
1<z<e
and

is a decreasing function of
y
.
We also prove that the inequalities

, where the

s are explicit functions of
Y

, see Theorem
2.1
in Section

3
Applications to more general predator-prey systems

Consider a general predator-prey system on the form

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=H(S)-q\varphi(S)X,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=p\varphi(S)X-dX,

(3.1)

where the non-negative variable
S=S(t)
represents the prey biomass,
the non-negative variable
X=X(t)
represents the predator biomass,

\varphi
is non-decreasing,

\varphi(0)=H(0)=0
,
and parameters
p,q,d
are positive.
Systems of type (
3
) have been extensively studied through the last centuries, see e.g.
[
5
,
11
,
12
,
9
,
13
,
14
]
and the references therein.

Often, functions
H
and
\varphi
are defined by

\displaystyle H(S)=rS\left(1-\frac{S}{K}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(S)=\frac{S^{n}}{S^{n}+A},

(3.2)

where most commonly
n=1
or
n=2
.
In case of (
3.2
) system (
3
) is usually referred to as a Rosenzweig-MacArthur predator prey system.
If

\displaystyle H(S)=rS\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(S)=S,

then system (
3
) boils down to the Lotka-Volterra equations (
1
).

We now intend to analyze the general system (
3
) using our estimates in Theorem
2.1
.
Assume, without loss of generality, that
q=1
.
The phase-plane equation yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}X}=\frac{F(S)-X}{X}\cdot\frac{\varphi(S)}{p\varphi(S)-d}

where
F(S)=H(S)/\varphi(S)
.
Let us replace
F(S)
by a constant
\overline{F}
for the moment, and observe that then integrating gives

\displaystyle\int\left(p-\frac{d}{\varphi(S)}\right)dS=\int\left(\frac{{\overline{F}}}{X}-1\right)dX

and thus the system can, under reasonable assumptions on
\varphi
and
H
, be analyzed by the generalized Lotka-Volterra integral

\displaystyle V_{\overline{F}}(X,S)=pS-d\int\frac{dS}{\varphi(S)}+X-{\overline{F}}\ln{X}.

Moreover

\displaystyle\nabla V_{\overline{F}}=\left(1-\frac{\overline{F}}{X},p-\frac{d}{\varphi(S)}\right)

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\left(p-\frac{d}{\varphi(S)}\right)S^{\prime}+\left(1-\frac{\overline{F}}{X}\right)X^{\prime}=\left(p\varphi(S)-d\right)\left(F(S)-{\overline{F}}\right).

(3.4)

To proceed we consider a trajectory
T
of system (
3
) with initial condition
(X_{0},S_{0})
where
X_{0}>F(S)
and
S_{0}
satisfies
\varphi(S_{0})<d/p
.
Since
X=F(S)
gives an isocline with
S^{\prime}=0
and

\varphi(S)=d/p
gives an isocline with
X^{\prime}=0
,
this means that both
S
and
X
are decreasing initially.
Suppose that, until
T
intersects
S=S_{0}
the next time,
T
stays in a part of the state space where there exist positive
\underline{F}
and
\overline{F}
such that

\displaystyle\underline{F}<F(S)<\overline{F}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(S)<d/p.

(3.5)

It then follows from (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) that trajectory
T
starting at
(X_{0},S_{0})
remains trapped between the curves
\underline{S}
and
\overline{S}
, defined through

V_{\underline{F}}(X_{0},S_{0})=V_{\underline{F}}(X,\underline{S}(X))\quad\text{and}\quad V_{\overline{F}}(X_{0},S_{0})=V_{\overline{F}}(X,\overline{S}(X)),

see Figure
5
.

Figure 5:
Geometry in the construction of estimates.

Moreover, the ‘barriers’
\underline{S}
and
\overline{S}
are convex with min at

X=\underline{F}
and
X=\overline{F}
,
and intersect
S=S_{0}
a second time at

\underline{X}_{1}
and
\overline{X}_{1}
, respectively.
For the next intersection of
T
with
S=S_{0}
at
(X_{1},S_{0})
it necessarily holds that

\underline{X}_{1}<X_{1}<\overline{X}_{1},

where

\displaystyle V_{\underline{F}}(X_{0},S_{0})=V_{\underline{F}}(\underline{X}_{1},S_{0})\quad\text{giving}\quad X_{0}-\underline{F}\ln{X_{0}}=\underline{X}_{1}-\underline{F}\ln{\underline{X}_{1}},

and

\displaystyle V_{\overline{F}}(X_{0},S_{0})=V_{\overline{F}}(\overline{X}_{1},S_{0})\quad\text{giving}\quad X_{0}-\overline{F}\ln{X_{0}}=\overline{X}_{1}-\overline{F}\ln{\overline{X}_{1}}.

By setting
x=\frac{\underline{X}_{1}}{\underline{F}}
and
y=\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}
the first equation reads

x-\ln x=y-\ln y,

and since
y=\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}>1
an application of Theorem
2.1
gives

x=z\left(y\right)ye^{-y},

where
z
is a decreasing function of
y
.
As the same argument applies to the upper barrier we conclude

z\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}\right){X_{0}}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}}=\underline{X}_{1}<X_{1}<\overline{X}_{1}=z\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}\right){X_{0}}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}}.

Moreover, since

x=-W(-ye^{-y}),

where
W
is the principal branch of the Lambert
W
-function, we also have

-\underline{F}W\left(-\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}}\right)=\underline{X}_{1}<X_{1}<\overline{X}_{1}=-\overline{F}W\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}}\right).

Finally, applying Theorem
2.1
for estimating
z
yields the following result:

Theorem 3.1

Suppose that
T
is a trajectory of system (
3
) with initial condition
(X_{0},S_{0})
where
X_{0}>F(S)
and
S_{0}
satisfies
\varphi(S_{0})<d/p
.
Suppose that, until
T
intersects
S=S_{0}
the next time,
T
stays in a part of the state space where (
3.5
) is satisfied.
Then, for the next intersection of trajectory
T
with
S=S_{0}
at
(X_{1},S_{0})
it holds that

-\underline{F}W\left(-\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}}\right)<X_{1}<-\overline{F}W\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}}\right),

and

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}}<z_{1}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}}<\frac{X_{1}}{X_{0}}<z_{2}e^{-\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}}<z_{0}e^{\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}}<e^{1+\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}},

where
z_{1}=z_{1}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\underline{F}}\right)
,
z_{2}=z_{2}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}\right)
and
z_{0}=z_{0}\left(\frac{X_{0}}{\overline{F}}\right)
.

As a remark, we note that the accuracy of the estimates in Theorem
3.1
improves when
F(S)=H(S)/\varphi(S)
obeys less variation,
i.e., when one can take a tighter interval in assumption (
3.5
).

As an example, for which we easily find the above imposed assumptions satisfied
and thereby conclude Theorem
3.1
,
we consider the system

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=(h(s)-x)s

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=m\,(s-\lambda)\,x

(3.6)

in
x,s\geq 0
, supposing parameters
m>0
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
and where
h
is given by

h(s)=(1-s)(s+a).

(3.7)

Any standard Rosenzweig-MacArthur predator-prey system can be transformed into a system of type (
3
).
In particular, it is equivalent with system (
3
) when (
3.2
) holds with
n=1
,
which can be seen by introducing the non-dimensional quantities

\displaystyle\tau\,=\,\int\frac{rK}{A+S\left(t\right)}dt,\quad s

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{S}{K},\quad x\,=\,\frac{qX}{rK},\quad a\,=\,\frac{A}{K},

\displaystyle m\,=\,\frac{p-d}{r}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\lambda\,=\,\frac{dA}{(p-d)K}.

(3.8)

Comparing to the general system (
3
) we identify

S=s,X=x,F(S)=h(s),\varphi(S)=s,p=m
, and
d=m\lambda
.
Hence, since
a<h(s)=(1-s)(s+a)<h(\lambda)
when
s<\lambda
,
we obtain (
3.5
) satisfied in the region
s<\lambda
with
\underline{F}=a
and
\overline{F}=h(\lambda)
.
Observing that
s=\lambda
is the isocline
x^{\prime}=0
and
x=h(s)
is the isocline
s^{\prime}=0
we conclude, from Theorem
3.1
,
the following estimate for the minimal predator biomass,
x_{\text{min}}
:

Corollary 3.2

Consider a trajectory of system (
3
) starting at
(x_{max},\lambda)
with
x_{max}>h(\lambda)
.
Suppose that
m>0
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
and that (
3.7
) holds.
Then the minimal predator biomass,
x_{\text{min}}
, satisfies

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{a}}<z_{1}e^{-\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{a}}<\frac{x_{\text{min}}}{x_{\text{max}}}<z_{2}e^{-\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{h(\lambda)}}<z_{0}e^{\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{h(\lambda)}}<e^{1+\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{h(\lambda)}},

where
z_{1}=z_{1}\left(\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{a}\right)
,
z_{2}=z_{2}\left(\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{h(\lambda)}\right)
and
z_{0}=z_{0}\left(\frac{x_{\text{max}}}{h(\lambda)}\right)
.

We remark that by shrinking the region
s<\lambda
into
s<\lambda^{*}
for some
0<\lambda^{*}<\lambda
we have the better estimates

a<h(s)<h(\lambda^{*})
and may, for a trajectory starting at
(x_{0},\lambda^{*})
, estimate its next intersection with the line
s=\lambda^{*}
at point
(x_{1},\lambda^{*})
. In particular, we then get
Corollary
3.2

but with
x_{\text{max}}=x_{0}
,
x_{\text{min}}=x_{1}
and
h(\lambda)=h(\lambda^{*})
.
Furthermore, we observe that the upper estimate is good for small
\lambda^{*}
but becomes less efficient when the value of
\lambda
increases beyond
a
.
The bounds in
Corollary
3.2

will be used by the authors in
[
14
]
for estimating the size of the unique limit cycle to system (
3
) which is the global attractor when
2\lambda+a<1
.

Coexistence of predators

As a final remark we consider the following system similar to (
3
) but allowing for
n\geq 1
predators:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=H(S)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\varphi_{i}(S)X_{i},

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=p_{i}\varphi_{i}(S)X_{i}-d_{i}X_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(3.9)

where the non-negative variable
S
represents the prey biomass,
the non-negative variables
X_{i}
represent the predator biomasses,

\varphi_{i}
is non-decreasing,

\varphi(0)=H(0)=0
,
parameters
p_{i}
,
q_{i}
,
d_{i}
are positive.

Following
[
22
, page 2]
we assume
p_{i}>d_{i}
. If not, the corresponding predator will die.
Using the time change
\tau=rt
,
where
\tau
is the new time, and the variable
changes
s=\frac{S}{K},x_{i}=\frac{q_{i}}{rK}X_{i}
,
we transform, when

\displaystyle H(S)=rS\left(1-\frac{S}{K}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{i}(S)=\frac{S}{S+A_{i}},

system (
3
) to the system

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\left(1-s-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{s+a_{i}}\right)s,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=m_{i}\frac{s-\lambda_{i}}{s+a_{i}}x_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where

a_{i}=\frac{A_{i}}{K},\quad m_{i}=\frac{p_{i}-d_{i}}{r},\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{i}=\frac{d_{i}A_{i}}{K(p_{i}-d_{i})}.

Systems of this type have been studied e.g. in
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
22
]
.
In particular, extinction and coexistence results for predators can be found in
[
17
,
22
]
from which we recall the following statement, giving sufficient condition for extinction:

Statement 3.3

[
22
, Statement 2]
Let
L=\frac{\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{j}(1-\lambda_{i})}
and
\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{j}
. If

a_{j}>\frac{a_{i}}{L+a_{i}(L-1)}
,
then the predator
i
goes extinct.

Anyhow the condition is quite far from necessary and it is possible to use
the results in this work to find sufficient conditions for the opposite, that is
for coexistence of predators. The proof of the statement and similar known proofs for extinction essentially only use the equations for
x_{i}
and the properties of the functions
\varphi_{i}
, the equation for
s

is only used to make the obvious restriction
s<1
.
If we consider the case of two predators we notice that in the two dimensional coordinate planes, where one predator is abscent, there can be cycles like in the standard Rosenzweig-MacArthur system.
The instability of one or two of these cycles can be used to get sufficient conditions for coexistence, and thus we conjecture that nice estimates we have produced in this work could be used for getting important coexistence conditions contradicting the known exclusion principle.

Another interesting problem is arising in connection to the bifurcation for this system examined in
[
2
]
. Here they under certain conditions conclude cyclic coexistence of the predators for parameters near to the case
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
where there is a cycle only in one of the coordinate planes. We conjecture that our estimates for this cycle can be used to prove its instability and thus coexistence for a parameter range after the bifurcation.

We observe that in all these cases we suppose to use the results only far beyond the
bifurcation to cycle of the equilibrium when the cycles are big and parameters
a_{i}
and

\lambda_{i}
are small.

It occurs that the behaviour for small prey biomass on the cycle can play an important role in determining the stability,
see
[
19
]
and references therein,
and thus estimates of the limit cycle for small prey, such as those in Theorem (
3.1
), obtained by Theorem
2.1
, may be useful.
