Joint Dynamic Models and Statistical Inference for Recurrent Competing Risks, Longitudinal Marker, and Health Status
Lili Tong, Piaomu Liu, Edsel A. Peña

Abstract
Consider a subject or unit in a longitudinal biomedical, public health, engineering, economic, or social science study which is being monitored over a possibly random duration. Over time this unit experiences competing recurrent events and a longitudinal marker transitions over a discrete state-space. In addition, its “health or performance” status also transitions over a discrete state-space with some states possibly absorbing states. A vector of covariates will also be associated with this unit. If there are absorbing states, of interest for this unit is its time-to-absorption of its health status process, which could be viewed as the unit’s lifetime. Aside from being affected by its covariate vector, there could be associations among the recurrent competing risks processes, the longitudinal marker process, and the health status process in the sense that the time-evolution of each process is associated with the other processes. To obtain more realistic models and enhance inferential performance, a joint dynamic stochastic model for these components is proposed and statistical inference methods are developed. This joint model, formulated via counting processes and continuous-time Markov chains, has the potential of facilitating ‘personalized’ interventions. This could enhance, for example, the implementation and adoption of precision medicine in medical settings. Semi-parametric and likelihood-based inferential methods for the model parameters are developed when a sample of these units is available. Finite-sample and asymptotic properties of estimators of model parameters, both finite- and infinite-dimensional, are obtained analytically or through simulation studies.

To address these questions, the major goals of this paper are (i) to propose a joint stochastic model for the random observables consisting of the RCR, LM, and HS components for such units; and (ii) to develop appropriate statistical inference methods for the proposed joint model when a sample of units are observed. Achieving these two goals may enable statistical prediction of the (remaining) lifetime of a, possibly new, unit; allow for the examination of the synergistic association among the RCR, LM, and HS components; and provide a vehicle to compare different groups of units and/or study the effects of concomitant variables or factors. More importantly, a joint stochastic model endowed with proper statistical inference methods could potentially enable unit-based interventions which are performed after a recurrent event occurrence or a transition in either the LM or HS processes. As such it could enhance the implementation of precision or personalized decision-making; for instance, precision medicine, though this topic is deferred in a future paper.

A specific situation where such a data accrual occurs is in a medical study. For example, a subject may experience different types of recurring cancer, with the longitudinal marker being the prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level categorized into a finite ordinal set, while the health status is categorized into either a healthy, diseased, or dead state, with the last state absorbing. Another situation pertains to recurrent competing falls of an elderly person, with the LM process pertaining to whether the person is in a hospital or in a nursing home, while the HS process pertains to the state of physical or cognitive fitness (see, for instance,

Several previous works dealing with modeling have either focused in the marginal modeling of each of the three data components, or in the joint modeling of two of the three data components. In this paper we tackle the problem of

A joint full model could also subsume these previous marginal or joint models – in fact, our proposed class of models subsumes as special cases models that have been considered in the literature. In contrast, only by imposing restrictive assumptions, such as the independence of the three model components, could one obtain a joint full model from marginal or pairwise joint models. As such, a joint full model will be less likely to be mis-specified, thereby reducing the potential biases that could accrue from mis-specified models among estimators of model parameters or when predicting residual lifetime.

2
A Concrete Situation in the Medical Arena

To demonstrate potential applicability of the proposed joint model, we describe a concrete situation in the medical field where the data accrual described in Section
1
could arise.
Gout is a form of arthritis characterized by sudden and severe attacks of pain, swelling, redness and tenderness in one or more joints in the toes, ankles, knees, elbows, wrists, and fingers. See, for instance, the website in Mayo Clinic about gout

https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/gout/symptoms-causes/syc-20372897

According to
[
32
]
, gout is the most prevalent inflammatory arthritis in the world. When a gout flare occurs, it renders the person incapacitated – personally attested by the senior author (EP) – and the debilitating condition may last for several days, possibly weeks. Since the location of the gout flare could vary, we may consider gout as competing recurrent events — competing with respect to the location of the flare, and recurrent since it could keep coming back. Gout occurs when urate crystals accumulate in the joints, which in turn is associated with high levels of uric acid in the blood.
The level of uric acid is measured by the Serum Urate Level (SUR), which can be categorized as Hyperuricemia (if SUR
>
7.2 mg/dL for males; if SUR
>
6.0 mg/dL for females), or Normal (if 3.5 mg/dL
\leq
SUR
\leq
7.2 mg/dL for males; if 2.6 mg/dL
\leq
SUR
\leq
6.0 mg/dL for females). The SUR level could be considered a longitudinal marker.

Gout is linked to other comorbidities (see
[
32
]
), and one of these pertains to chronic kidney disease (CKD) since kidneys are associated with the excretion of uric acid in the body. The ordinal stages of CKD, based on the value of the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR), are as follows: Stage 1 (Normal) if GFR
\geq
90 mL/min; Stage 2 (Mild CKD) if 60 mL/min
\leq
GFR
\leq
89 mL/min; Stage 3A (Moderate CKD) if 45 mL/min
\leq
GFR
\leq
59 mL/min; Stage 3B (Moderate CKD) if 30 mL/min
\leq
GFR
\leq
44; Stage 4 (Severe CKD) if 15 mL/min
\leq
GFR
\leq
29 mL/min; and Stage 5 (End Stage CKD) if GRF
\leq
14 mL/min. The state of Stage 5 (End Stage CKD) can be viewed as an absorbing state. The CKD status could be viewed as the “health status” of the person.
Other covariates, both environmental and genetic, such as gender, blood pressure, weight, diet, alcohol consumption, genetic markers, etc., could also impact the occurrence of gout flares, uric acid level, and CKD.

When a gout flare occurs, lifestyle interventions could be performed such as (i) consuming skim milk powder enriched with the two dairy products glycomacropeptide (GMP) and G600 milk fat extract; (ii) consuming standard milk or lactose powder; or (iii) taking prescribed medications such as colchicine, alluporinal, etc. The purpose of such interventions is to lessen gout flare recurrences through control of the SUR level.

In such a study, of major interest is to
jointly
model the competing gout recurrences, the categorized SUR process, and the CKD process. The dynamic joint model proposed in this paper could provide a potentially viable model for this situation.

3
Joint Model of RCR, LM, and HS Processes

3.1
Data Observables for One Unit

Denote by
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbf{P})
the basic filtered probability space with filtration
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{s}:s\geq 0\}
where all random entities under consideration are defined. We begin by describing the joint model for the data observable components for
one unit
.

Let
\tau
, the end of monitoring period, have a distribution function
G(\cdot)
, which may be degenerate. The covariate vector will be
{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})
, assumed to be time-independent, though the extension to time-dependent covariates is possible with additional assumptions.
For the RCR component, let
N^{R}=\{N_{q}^{R}(s)\equiv(N^{R}(s;q),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}):\ s\geq 0\}
, with index set

\mathfrak{I}_{Q}=\{1,\ldots,Q\},

be a
Q
-dimensional multivariate counting (column) vector process such that, for
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
,
N_{q}^{R}(s)
is the number of
observed
occurrences of the recurrent event of type
q
over
[0,s]
, with
N_{q}^{R}(0)=0
. Thus, the sample path
s\mapsto N_{q}^{R}(s)
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{0,+}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, is a non-decreasing step-function, and is right-continuous with left-hand limits. We denote by
dN_{q}^{R}(s)=N_{q}^{R}(s)-N_{q}^{R}(s-)
, the jump at time
s
of
N_{q}^{R}
.

For the LM process, let
W=\{W(s):s\geq 0\}
, where
W(s)
takes values in a finite state space
\mathfrak{W}
with cardinality
|\mathfrak{W}|
.
W(s)
represents the state of the longitudinal marker at time
s
. The sample path
s\mapsto W(s)
is a step-function which is right-continuous with left-hand limits. By introducing the lexicographically-ordered index set

\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}=\{\{(w_{1},w_{2}):w_{2}\in\mathfrak{W},w_{2}\neq w_{1}\},w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}\},

so that
|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|=2{\binom{|\mathfrak{W}|}{2}}
.
We can convert
W
into a (column)
|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|
-dimensional multivariate counting process
\{N^{W}\equiv(N^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2}),(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}):s\geq 0\}
, where
N^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})
is the number of observed transitions of
W
from state
w_{1}
into
w_{2}
over the period
[0,s]
, that is, for
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}
,

N^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})=\sum_{t\leq s}I\{W(t-)=w_{1},W(t)=w_{2}\},

with
I(\cdot)
denoting indicator function.
Thus, for each
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}
, the sample path
s\mapsto N^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{0,+}
, is a non-decreasing step-function, right-continuous with left-hand limits, and with
N^{W}(0;w_{1},w_{2})=0
. Furthermore,
\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})\in\{0,1\}
for every
s\geq 0
.

For the HS process, let
V=\{V(s):s\geq 0\}
, where
V(s)
takes values in the finite state space
\mathfrak{V}=\mathfrak{V}_{0}\bigcup\mathfrak{V}_{1}
, where states in
\mathfrak{V}_{0}
are absorbing states, and with
|\mathfrak{V}_{0}|\geq 0
; while states in
\mathfrak{V}_{1}
are transient states.
V(s)
is the state occupied by the HS process at time
s
, so that if
V(s)\in\mathfrak{V}_{0}
, then
V(s^{\prime})=V(s)
for all
s^{\prime}>s
. Similar to the LM process, define the lexicographically-ordered index set

\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}=\{\{(v_{1},v):v\in\mathfrak{V},v\neq v_{1}\},v_{1}\in\mathfrak{V}_{1}\},

whose cardinality is
|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}|=|\mathfrak{V}_{1}|(|\mathfrak{V}|-|\mathfrak{V}_{1}|)
. We convert
V
into a (column)
|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}|
-dimensional multivariate counting process
\{N^{V}\equiv(N^{V}(s;v_{1},v),(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}):s\geq 0\}
, where
N^{V}(s;v_{1},v)
is the number of observed transitions of
V
from state
v_{1}
into state
v
over the period
[0,s]
, that is, for
(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}
,

N^{V}(s;v_{1},v)=\sum_{t\leq s}I\{V(t-)=v_{1},V(t)=v\}.

For each
(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}
, the sample path
s\mapsto N^{V}(s;v_{1},v)
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{0,+}
, and is a non-decreasing step-function, right-continuous with left-hand limits, and with
N^{V}(0;v_{1},v)=0
. In addition,
\sum_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}}dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)\in\{0,1\}
for every
s\geq 0
.
Next, we combine the multivariate counting processes
N^{R}
,
N^{W}
, and
N^{V}
into one (column) multivariate counting process
N=\{N(s):s\geq 0\}
of dimension
Q+|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|+|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}|
, where, with
T
denoting vector/matrix transpose,

N(s)=\left[(N^{R}(s))^{\tt T},(N^{W}(s))^{\tt T},(N^{V}(s))^{\tt T}\right]^{\tt T}.

Since the components of
N
are non-decreasing, clearly
N
is an
\mathcal{F}
-submartingale.

An important point needs to be stated regarding the observables in the study, which will have an impact in the interpretation of the parameters of the joint model. This pertains to the “competing risks” nature of all the possible events at each time point
s
. The possible
Q
recurrent event types, as well as the potential transitions in the LM and HS processes, are all competing with each other. Thus, suppose that at time
s_{0}
, the event that occurred is a recurrent event of type
q_{0}
, that is,
dN^{R}(s_{0};q_{0})=1
. This means that this event has occurred
in the presence
of the potential recurrent events from the other
Q-1
risks, and the potential transitions from either the LM and HS processes. This will entail the use of
crude
hazards, instead of
net
hazards, in the joint modeling, and this observation will play a critical role in the dynamic joint model since each of the competing event occurrences at a given time point
s
from all the possible event sources (RCR, LM, and HS) will be affected by the history of all these processes just before time
s
. This is the aspect that exemplifies the synergistic association among the three components. See
[
35
,
36
]
for some aspects regarding competing risks modeling.

Another observable process for our joint model is a vector of effective (or virtual) age processes
\mathcal{E}=\{(\mathcal{E}_{1}(s),\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{Q}(s)):s\geq 0\}
, whose components are
\mathcal{F}
-predictable processes with sample paths that are non-negative, left-continuous, piece-wise non-decreasing, and differentiable. These effective age processes will carry the impact of interventions performed after each recurrent event occurrence or a transition in either the LM process or the HS process. For articles dealing with virtual ages, see the philosophically-oriented article
[
12
]
and the review article
[
4
]
. We point out that utilizing this notion of effective or virtual ages makes our model quite general since many current models are just special cases of our model with the proper choice of effective age processes.

Finally, we define the time-to-absorption of the unit to be

\tau_{A}=\inf\{s\geq 0:V(s)\in\mathfrak{V}_{0}\}

with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=\infty
. Using this
\tau_{A}
and
\tau
, we define the unit’s at-risk process to be
Y=\{Y(s):s\geq 0\}
, with

Y(s)=I\{\min(\tau,\tau_{A})\geq s\}.

In addition, we define LM-specific and HS-specific at-risk processes as follows: For
w\in\mathfrak{W}
, define
Y^{W}(s;w)=I\{W(s-)=w\}
; and, for
v\in\mathfrak{V}_{1}
, define
Y^{V}(s;v)=I\{V(s-)=v\}
.
For a unit, we could then concisely summarize the random observables in terms of stochastic processes as:

D=(X,N,\mathcal{E},Y,Y^{W},Y^{V})\equiv\{X,(N(s),\mathcal{E}(s),Y(s),Y^{W}(s),Y^{V}(s):s\geq 0)\}.

(1)

Note that the processes are undefined for
s>\min(\tau_{A},\tau)\equiv\inf\{s\geq 0:Y(s+)=0\}
since monitoring of the unit had by then ceased.

3.2
Joint Model Specification for One Unit

The joint model specification will be through the specification of the compensator process vector and the predictable quadratic variation (PQV) process matrix of the multivariate counting process
N
, which is possible because
N
is a submartingale. The predictable compensator process vector
A=\{A(s):s\geq 0\}
is of dimension
Q+|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|+|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}|
and is such that the vector process
M=N-A=\{M(s)=N(s)-A(s):s\geq 0\}
is a zero-mean square-integrable martingale process with PQV matrix process
\langle M,M\rangle
. The vectors
A
and
M
are actually partitioned into three vector components reflecting the RCR, LM, and HS components, according to

A=\left[(A^{R})^{\tt T},(A^{W})^{\tt T},(A^{V})^{\tt T}\right]^{\tt T}\quad\mbox{and}\quad M=\left[(M^{R})^{\tt T},(M^{W})^{\tt T},(M^{V})^{\tt T}\right],

where, with
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
,
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}
,
(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}
, and
s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle A^{R}=\{(A^{R}(s;q))\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad M^{R}=\{(M^{R}(s;q))\};

\displaystyle A^{W}=\{(A^{W}(s;(w_{1},w_{2})))\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad M^{W}=\{(M^{W}(s;(w_{1},w_{2})))\};

\displaystyle A^{V}=\{(A^{V}(s;(v_{1},v)))\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad M^{V}=\{(M^{V}(s;(v_{1},v)))\},

with
A^{R}
and
M^{R}
of dimensions
Q
;
A^{W}
and
M^{W}
of dimensions
|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|
; and
A^{V}
and
M^{V}
of dimensions
|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}|
. The matrix
\langle M,M\rangle
could then be partitioned similarly to reflect these block components.

We can now proceed with the specification of the compensator process vector and the PQV process matrix.
For this purpose, we introduce the following quantities, functions, and processes.

•

For each
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
there is a baseline hazard rate function
\lambda_{0q}(\cdot)
with associated baseline cumulative hazard function
\Lambda_{0q}(\cdot)=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\lambda_{0q}(v)dv
. We also denote by
\bar{F}_{0q}(\cdot)=\prodi_{v=0}^{\cdot}[1-\Lambda_{0q}(dv)]
the associated baseline survivor function, where
\prodi
is the product-integral symbol.

•

For each
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
there is a mapping
\rho_{q}(\cdot|\cdot):\mathbb{Z}_{0,+}^{Q}\times\Re^{d_{q}}\rightarrow\Re_{0,+}
, where the
d_{q}
’s are known positive integers. There is an associated vector
\alpha_{q}\in\Re^{d_{q}}
.

•

There is a collection of non-negative real numbers

\eta=\{\eta(w_{1},w_{2}):(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}\},

and we define for every
w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}
,
\eta(w_{1},w_{1})=-\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{W};w\neq w_{1}}\eta(w_{1},w).

•

There is a collection of non-negative real numbers

\xi=\{\xi(v_{1},v):(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}\},

and we define for every
v_{1}\in\mathfrak{V}
,
\xi(v_{1},v_{1})=-\sum_{v\in\mathfrak{V};v\neq v_{1}}\xi(v_{1},v),
and with
\xi(v_{0},v)=0
for every
v_{0}\in\mathfrak{V}_{0}
and
v\in\mathfrak{V}
.

•

There are
\Re_{1}
-valued link functions

\{(\psi^{R}(s|\theta^{R}),\psi^{W}(s|\theta^{W}),\psi^{V}(s|\theta^{V})):s\geq 0\}

which are non-negative, bounded, and
\mathfrak{F}
-predictable processes and with
(\theta^{R},\theta^{W},\theta^{V})
being finite-dimensional parameters. These processes are assumed to be twice-differentiable with respect to the elements of the parameters.

We describe the specific form of the
\psi
-processes that we utilize. To do so, define the generic mapping
\iota
given a finite set
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}:

\iota_{A}(a)=(I(a_{2}=a),\ldots,I(a_{m}=a)),

an
(m-1)
row vector with
\{0,1\}
-valued elements. Thus, the mapping
\iota
is a converter to dummy variables. With row vectors

\displaystyle B^{R}(s)=[\iota_{\mathfrak{W}}(W(s)),\iota_{\mathfrak{V}}(V(s)),X];B^{W}(s)=[\log(1+N^{R}(s)),\iota_{\mathfrak{V}}(V(s)),X];

\displaystyle B^{V}(s)=[\log(1+N^{R}(s)),\iota_{\mathfrak{W}}(W(s)),X]

and column vectors

\displaystyle\theta^{R}=[(\theta^{RL})^{\tt T},(\theta^{RH})^{\tt T},(\theta^{RX})^{\tt T}]^{\tt T};\theta^{W}=[(\theta^{LR})^{\tt T},(\theta^{LH})^{\tt T},(\theta^{LX})^{\tt T}]^{\tt T};

\displaystyle\theta^{V}=[(\theta^{HR})^{\tt T},(\theta^{HL})^{\tt T},(\theta^{HX})^{\tt T}]^{\tt T},

we let the link functions to be

\displaystyle\psi^{R}(s|\theta^{R})=\exp\{B^{R}(s-)\theta^{R}\};\psi^{W}(s|\theta^{W})=\exp\{B^{W}(s-)\theta^{W}\};

\displaystyle\psi(s|\theta^{V})=\exp\{B^{V}(s-)\theta^{V}\}.

Note that we used the form
\log(1+N^{R}(s))
for the internal covariate associated with the RCR process, but in general we could some other mapping

for some suitable map

.

In addition to the, possibly infinite-dimensional, parameters
\lambda_{0q}(\cdot)

’s,
\eta
,
\xi

For the proposed model, the compensator process vector

denoting the diagonal matrix formed from vector

\langle M,M\rangle(s)=\mbox{Dg}[A(s)]=\mbox{Dg}[((A^{R}(s))^{\tt T},(A^{W}(s))^{\tt T},(A^{V}(s))^{\tt T})^{\tt T}].

The components of this compensator process vector are given by, for
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
,
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}
, and

\displaystyle A^{R}(s;q)=\int_{0}^{s}Y(t)\lambda_{0q}[\mathcal{E}_{q}(t)]\rho_{q}[N^{R}(t-)|\alpha_{q}]\psi^{R}(s|\theta^{R})dt;

\displaystyle A^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})=\int_{0}^{s}Y(t)Y^{W}(t;w_{1})\eta(w_{1},w_{2})\psi^{W}(s|\theta^{W})dt;

\displaystyle A^{V}(s;v_{1},v)=\int_{0}^{s}Y(t)Y^{V}(t;v_{1})\xi(v_{1},v)\psi^{V}(s|\theta^{V})dt.

In the left-hand side of the equations above, we have suppressed writing the dependence on the model parameters. Note that the argument of
\lambda_{0q}(\cdot)
in

is the (random) effective age
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\cdot)
. In addition, observe the dynamic nature of this model in that an event occurrence at an infinitesimal time interval
[s,s+ds)
depends on the history of the processes before time
s
. According to the theory of counting processes, we have the following probabilistic interpretations (statements are almost surely):

\displaystyle E\{dN^{R}(s;q)|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}=dA^{R}(s;q);

\displaystyle E\{dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}=dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2});

\displaystyle E\{dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}=dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v),

and

\displaystyle Var\{dN^{R}(s;q)|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}=dA^{R}(s;q);

\displaystyle Var\{dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}=dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2});

\displaystyle Var\{dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}=dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v),

together with the conditional covariance, given
\mathcal{F}_{s-}
, between any pair of elements of
dN(s)
being equal to zero, e.g.,
Cov\{dN^{R}(s;q),dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}=0
. However, note that we are
not
assuming that the components of
N^{R}
,
N^{W}
, and
N^{V}
are independent, nor even conditionally independent. A way to view this model is that, given
\mathcal{F}_{s-}
, the history just before time
s
, on the infinitesimal interval
[s,s+ds)
,

has a multinomial distribution with parameters
1
and
dA(s)=(dA^{R}(s)^{\tt T},dA^{W}(s)^{\tt T},dA^{V}(s)^{\tt T})^{\tt T}
. As such, for every
s\geq 0
, the following constraint holds:

dN_{\bullet}(s)=dN_{\bullet}^{R}(s)+dN_{\bullet}^{W}(s)+dN_{\bullet}^{V}(s)\in\{0,1\},

with the notation that a subscript of

means the sum over all the appropriate index set, e.g.,
dN_{\bullet}^{R}(s)=\sum_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}dN^{R}(s;q)
and
dA_{\bullet}(s)=dA_{\bullet}^{R}(s)+dA_{\bullet}^{W}(s)+dA_{\bullet}^{V}(s)
. The multinomial distribution above could actually be approximated by independent Bernoulli distributions. To see this, if we have real numbers
p_{k},k=1,\ldots,K,
with
0<p_{k}\approx 0
for each

and with
\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\approx 0
, then we have the approximation

\left(1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\right)\approx\prod_{k=1}^{K}(1-p_{k}).

Consequently, in the equation below, the multinomial probability on the left-hand side is approximately the product of (independent) Bernoulli probabilities in the right-hand side.

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}[dA_{q}(s)]^{dN_{q}^{R}(s)}\right\}\left\{\prod_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}[dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})]^{dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})}\right\}\times

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}}[dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v)]^{dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)}\right\}\left\{[1-dA_{\bullet}(s)]^{1-dN_{\bullet}(s)}\right\}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}[dA_{q}(s)]^{dN_{q}^{R}(s)}[1-dA_{q}^{R}(s)]^{1-dN_{q}^{R}(s)}\right\}\times

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}[dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})]^{dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})}[1-dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})]^{1-dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})}\right\}\times

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}}[dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v)]^{dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)}[1-dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v)]^{1-dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)}\right\}.

This approximate equivalence informs the manner in which we may generate data from the model later in the sections dealing with an illustrative data set (Section
7
) and the simulation studies (Section

) where we could have used this product of Bernoulli probabilities in generating synthetic or simulated data.

Consider a unit that is still at risk just before time
s
whose LM process is at state

and HS process at state

(a)

What is the distribution of the next event occurrence?

(b)

Given in addition that an event occurred at time

Denote by

the time to the next event occurrence starting from time

. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{T>t|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prodi_{u=s}^{s+t}\left[1-\left(\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\lambda_{0q}[\mathcal{E}_{q}(u)]\rho_{q}[N^{R}(u-)|\alpha_{q}]\exp\{B^{R}(u-)\theta^{R}\}-\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\eta(w_{1},w_{1})\exp\{B^{W}(u-)\theta^{W}\}-\xi(v_{1},v_{1})\exp\{B^{V}(u-)\theta^{V}\}\right)du\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\int_{s}^{s+t}\left(\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\lambda_{0q}[\mathcal{E}_{q}(u)]\rho_{q}[N^{R}(u-)|\alpha_{q}]\exp\{B^{R}(u-)\theta^{R}\}-\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\eta(w_{1},w_{1})\exp\{B^{W}(u-)\theta^{W}\}-\xi(v_{1},v_{1})\exp\{B^{V}(u-)\theta^{V}\}\right)du\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\exp\{B^{R}(s-)\theta^{R}\}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\rho_{q}[N^{R}(s-)|\alpha_{q}]\int_{s}^{s+t}\lambda_{0q}[\mathcal{E}_{q}(u)]du+\right.

\displaystyle\left.\eta(w_{1},w_{1})\exp\{B^{W}(s-)\theta^{W}\}t+\xi(v_{1},v_{1})\exp\{B^{V}(s-)\theta^{V}\}t\right\},

with the second equality obtained by invoking the product-integral identity

\prodi_{s\in I}[1-dA(s)]^{1-dN(s)}=\exp\left\{-\int_{s\in I}dA(s)\right\}

and since no events in
[s,s+t)
means
dN_{\bullet}^{R}(u)+dN_{\bullet}^{W}(u)+dN_{\bullet}^{V}(u)=0
for
u\in[s,s+t)
, and the last equality arising since, prior to the next event, there will be
no changes
in the values of
N^{R}
,
B^{R}
,
B^{W}
, and
B^{V}
from their respective values just before time
s
. Note that in particular, if the
\lambda_{0q}

, then the conditional probability that it was an RCR-type

\exp{\{B^{R}(s-)\theta^{R}\}}\rho_{q}[N^{R}(s-)|\alpha_{q}]\lambda_{0q}[\mathcal{E}_{q}(s+t)]/C(s,t),

with

\displaystyle C(s,t)=\exp{\{B^{R}(s-)\theta^{R}\}}\sum_{q^{\prime}=1}^{Q}\rho_{q^{\prime}}[N^{R}(s-)|\alpha_{q^{\prime}}]\lambda_{0q^{\prime}}[\mathcal{E}_{q^{\prime}}(s+t)]-

\displaystyle\eta(w_{1},w_{1})\exp{\{B^{W}(s-)\theta^{W}\}}-\xi(v_{1},v_{1})\exp{\{B^{V}(s-)\theta^{V}\}}.

Similarly, the conditional probability that it was a transition to state
w_{2}
for the LM process is

\eta(w_{1},w_{2})\exp{\{B^{W}(s-)\theta^{W}\}}/C(s,t),

and the conditional probability that it was a transition to state

\xi(v_{1},v)\exp{\{B^{V}(s-)\theta^{V}\}}/C(s,t).

These probabilities demonstrate the competing risks nature of the different possible events (see, for instance,
[
36

). They also provide a computational approach to iteratively and dynamically generate data from the joint model for use in simulation studies, with the basic idea being to first generate a time to any type of event, then to mark the type of event or update each of the counting processes by using the above conditional probabilities.

Denoting by
\Theta
the set of all parameters of the model, the likelihood function arising from observing

, with

the initial joint probability mass function of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Theta|D)=p_{(W,V)}(W(0),V(0))\prodi_{s=0}^{\infty}\left\{\left[\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}[dA^{R}(s;q)]^{dN^{R}(s;q)}\right]\right.\times

\displaystyle\left.\left[\prod_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}[dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})]^{dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})}\right]\right.\times

\displaystyle\left.\left[\prod_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}}[dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v)]^{dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)}\right]\left[1-dA_{\bullet}(s)\right]^{1-dN_{\bullet}(s)}\right\}.

This likelihood could be rewritten in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Theta|D)=p_{(W,V)}(W(0),V(0))\times

(2)

\displaystyle\prodi_{s=0}^{\infty}\left\{\left[\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}[dA^{R}(s;q)]^{dN^{R}(s;q)}\right]\left[\prod_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}[dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})]^{dN^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left[\prod_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}}[dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v)]^{dN^{V}(s;v_{1},v)}\right]\right\}\exp\{-A_{\bullet}(\infty)\}.

Let us examine the meaning of
A_{\bullet}(\infty)\equiv A_{\bullet}^{R}(\infty)+A_{\bullet}^{W}(\infty)+A_{\bullet}^{V}(\infty)
. Simplifying, we see that this equals

A_{\bullet}(\infty)=\int_{0}^{\infty}Y(s)T(s)ds=\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{A}}T(s)ds
,
where

\displaystyle T(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}\lambda_{0q}[\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)]\rho_{q}[N^{R}(s-)|\alpha_{q}]\exp\{B^{R}(s-)\theta^{R}\}-

\displaystyle\sum_{w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}}Y^{W}(s;w_{1})\eta(w_{1},w_{1})\exp\{B^{W}(s-)\theta^{W}\}-

\displaystyle\sum_{v_{1}\in\mathfrak{V}_{1}}Y^{V}(s;v_{1})\xi(v_{1},v_{1})\exp\{B^{V}(s-)\theta^{V}\}.

Recall that
\eta(w_{1},w_{1})
and
\xi(v_{1},v_{1})
are non-positive real numbers. Thus,

could be interpreted as the total risk of an event, either a recurrent event in the RCR component or a transition in the LM or HS components, occurring from all possible sources (RCR, LM, HS) that the unit is exposed to at the infinitesimal time interval
[s,s+ds)
, given the history
\mathcal{F}_{s-}
just before time
s
.

We provide further explanations of the elements of the joint model. First, there is a tacit assumption that no more than one event of any type could occur at any given time
s
. Second, the event rate at any time point
s
for any type of event is in the presence of the other possible risk events. Thus, consider a specific
q_{0}\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
and assume that the unit is still at-risk at time
s_{0}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{dN_{q_{0}}^{R}(s_{0})=1|\mathcal{F}_{s_{0}-}\}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\lambda_{0q_{0}}[\mathcal{E}_{q_{0}}(s_{0})]\rho_{q_{0}}[N^{R}(s_{0}-)|\alpha_{q_{0}}]e^{B^{R}(s_{0}-)\theta^{R}}d{s_{0}}

(3)

is the conditional probability, given
\mathcal{F}_{s_{0}-}
, that an event occurred at
[s_{0},s_{0}+ds_{0})
and is of RCR type
q_{0}

and
all other event types did not occur, which is the essence of what is called a crude hazard rate, instead of a net hazard rate. Third, the effective (or virtual) age functions
\mathcal{E}_{q}(\cdot)
s, which are assumed to be dynamically determined and not dependent on any unknown parameters, encodes the impact of performed interventions after each event occurrence. Several possible choices of these functions are:

•

\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)=s
for all
s\geq 0
and
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
. This is usually referred to as a minimal repair intervention, corresponding to the situation where an intervention simply puts back the system at the age just before the event occurrence. Note that this is the typical effective age process specified in many existing models, which may be interpreted,
erroneously
, as the model having
no
effective age process.

•

\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)=s-S_{N_{\bullet}(s-)}
where
0=S_{0}<S_{1}<S_{2}<\ldots
are the successive event times. This corresponds to a perfect intervention, which has the effect of re-setting the time to zero for each of the RCRs after each event occurrence. In a reliability setting, this means that all
Q
components (unless having exponential lifetimes) are replaced by corresponding identical, but new, components at each event occurrence.

•

\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)=s-S_{N_{\bullet}^{R}(s-)}^{R}
where
0=S_{0}^{R}<S_{1}^{R}<S_{2}^{R}<\ldots
are the successive event times of the occurrences of the RCR events.

•

\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)=s-S_{qN^{R}(s-;q)}^{R}
where
0=S_{q0}^{R}<S_{q1}^{R}<S_{q2}^{R}<\ldots
are the successive event times of the occurrences of RCR events of type
q
. This corresponds to performing a perfect repair at each risk-specific event occurrence for each of the
Q
risks.
This is the effective age function utilized in generating the simulated data for illustrating the estimation procedures in Section
7
, as well as in the simulation study in Section
8
.

•

Other general forms are possible, such as those in
[
10
]
and
[
14
]
, the latter employing ideas of Kijima (see
[
17
]
). See also the discussion on the ‘reality’ of virtual or effective ages in paper
[
12
]
, as well as the review paper
[
4
]
.

One may think that the use of these effective age processes just adds more complications. However, note that as can be seen from some of the possible forms of these processes, one actually gains generality, since whatever analytical results are obtained for the general model will then apply to these specific situations, which includes many existing models. In addition, it should be pointed out that models that seem to be devoid of an effective age, such as the Cox PHM or the Andersen-Gill model, do in fact have an
intrinsic
effective age, which is either
\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)=s
or
\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)=s-S_{N_{\bullet}^{R}(s-)}
and since we usually have
Q=1
in these earlier models.

Fourth, the impact of accumulating RCR event occurrences, which could be adverse, but could also be beneficial as in software engineering applications, is incorporated in the model through the
\rho_{q}(\cdot|\cdot)
functions. One possible choice is an exponential function, such as

\rho_{q}(N^{R}(s-)|\alpha_{q})=\exp\{[\log(1+N^{R}(s-))]^{\tt T}\alpha_{q}\},\ q=1,\ldots,Q,

the form that was utilized in generating the illustrative simulated data. However, other choices could be made as well. Finally, the modulating exponential link function in the model is for the impact of the covariates as well as the values of the RCR, LM, and HS processes just before the time of interest, with the vector of coefficients quantifying the effects of the covariates. The use of
\log(1+N^{R}(s-))
in the model could be viewed as using them as internal covariates, or that the dynamic model is a self-exciting model.

Similar interpretations hold for the parameters
\{\eta(w_{1},w_{2}):(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}\}
and
\{\xi(v_{1},v):(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}\}
. Thus, if just before time
s_{0}
,
W(s_{0}-)=w_{1}
and
V(s_{0}-)=v_{1}
, indicated by
\mathcal{F}_{s_{0}-}(w_{1},v_{1})
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{W(s_{0}+ds_{0})=w_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{s_{0}}(w_{1},v_{1})\}\approx

(4)

\displaystyle\eta(w_{1},w_{2})\exp\{[\log(1+N^{R}(s_{0}-))]^{\tt T}\theta^{LR}+\theta^{LH}_{j(v_{1})}+X\theta^{LX}\}ds_{0};

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{V(s_{0}+ds_{0})=v|\mathcal{F}_{s_{0}}(w_{1},v_{1})\}\approx

(5)

\displaystyle\xi(v_{1},v)\exp\{[\log(1+N^{R}(s_{0}-))]^{\tt T}\theta^{HR}+\theta^{HL}_{j(w_{1})}+X\theta^{HX}\}ds_{0},

where

is the index associated with
v_{1}
in
\mathfrak{V}_{1}
and

is the index associated with

\mathfrak{W}

3.3
Special Case: Independent Poisson Processes and CTMCs for One Unit

There is a special case arising from this general joint model obtained when we set
\lambda_{0q}(s)=\lambda_{0q}
,
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
;
\rho_{q}=1
;
\theta^{R}=0
;
\theta^{W}=0
; and
\theta^{V}=0
. In this situation, we have

\displaystyle dA^{R}(s;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{0q}Y(s)ds,q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q};

\displaystyle dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(w_{1},w_{2})Y(s)Y^{W}(s;w_{1})ds,(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}};

\displaystyle dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\xi(v_{1},v)Y(s)Y^{W}(s,v_{1})ds,(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}.

It is easy to see that this particular model coincides with the model where we have the following situations:

(i)

N^{R}(\cdot;q),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q},
are independent homogeneous Poisson processes with respective rates
\lambda_{0q},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
;

(ii)

W(\cdot)
is a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) with infinitesimal generator matrix (IGM) consisting of
\{\eta(w_{1},w_{2})\}
;

(iii)

V(\cdot)
is a CTMC with IGM consisting of
\{\xi(v_{1},v_{2})\}
;

(iv)

N^{R}
,
W
, and
V
are independent; and

(v)

the processes are observed over
[0,\min(\tau,\tau_{A})]
, where
\tau
is the end of monitoring period, while
\tau_{A}
is the absorption time of
V
into
\mathfrak{V}_{0}
if
\mathfrak{V}_{0}\neq\emptyset
.

In this special situation, the
\lambda_{0q}
’s are both crude and net hazard rates. Also, due to the memoryless property of the exponential distribution, interventions performed after each event occurrence will have no impact in the succeeding event occurrences. This specific joint model further allows us to provide an operational interpretation of the model parameters. Thus, suppose that at time
s
, the LM process is at state
w_{1}
and the HS process is at state
v_{1}\notin\mathfrak{V}_{0}
. Then, the distribution of the time to the next event occurrence of any type (the holding or sojourn time at the current state configuration) has an exponential distribution with parameter
C=\lambda_{0\bullet}-\eta(w_{1},w_{1})-\xi(v_{1},v_{1})
. When an event occurs, then the (conditional) probability that it is (i) an RCR event of type
q
is
\lambda_{0q}/C
; (ii) a transition to LM state
w_{2}\neq w_{1}
is
\eta(w_{1},w_{2})/C
; or (iii) a transition to an HS state
v\neq v_{1}
is
\xi(v_{1},v)/C
. This is the essence of the competing risks nature of all the possible event types: an RCR event, an LM transition, and an HS transition.
As such, the more general model could be viewed as an extension of this basic model with independent Poisson processes for the RCR component and CTMCs for the LM and HS components.
For this special case, the likelihood function in (
2
) simplifies to the expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Theta|D)=p_{(W,V)}(W(0),V(0))\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{A}}T(s)ds\right\}\left[\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}\lambda_{0q}^{N^{R}(\tau\wedge\tau_{A};q)}\right]\times

(6)

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\eta(w_{1},w_{2})^{N^{W}(\tau\wedge\tau_{A};w_{1},w_{2})}\right]\left[\prod_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\xi(v_{1},v)^{N^{V}(\tau\wedge\tau_{A};v_{1},v)}\right].

with

T(s)=\lambda_{0\bullet}-\sum_{w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}}\eta(w_{1},w_{1})Y^{W}(s;w_{1})-\sum_{v_{1}\in\mathfrak{V}_{1}}\xi(v_{1},v_{1})Y^{V}(s;v_{1}).

Note that
T(s)=T(s;\lambda_{0},\eta,\xi)
is a quantity, since it depends on the parameters, instead of a sample statistic.

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}\lambda_{0q}^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{R}(\tau_{i}^{*};q)}\right]\left[\prod_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\eta(w_{1},w_{2})^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{W}(\tau_{i}^{*};w_{1},w_{2})}\right]\times

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}}\xi(v_{1},v)^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{V}(\tau_{i}^{*};v_{1},v)}\right]\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau_{i}^{*}}T_{i}(s;\lambda_{0},\eta,\xi)ds\right\},

where

T_{i}(s;\lambda_{0},\eta,\xi)=\lambda_{0\bullet}-\sum_{w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}}\eta(w_{1},w_{1})Y_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})-\sum_{v_{1}\in\mathfrak{V}}\xi(v_{1},v_{1})Y_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1}).

The score function
U(\Theta|\mathbf{D})=\nabla_{\Theta}\log\mathcal{L}(\Theta|\mathbf{D})
has elements,
where indices are
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
,
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}
, and
(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}
,

\displaystyle U^{R}(\Theta;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{R}(\tau_{i}^{*};q)}{\lambda_{0q}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}^{*};

\displaystyle U^{W}(\Theta;w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{W}(\tau_{i}^{*};w_{1},w_{2})}{\eta(w_{1},w_{2})}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau_{i}^{*}}Y_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})ds;

\displaystyle U^{V}(\Theta;v_{1},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{V}(\tau_{i}^{*};v_{1},v)}{\xi(v_{1},v)}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau_{i}^{*}}Y_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1})ds.

Equating these equations to zeros yield the ML estimators of the parameters, which are given below.

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{0q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{R}(\tau_{i}^{*};q)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}^{*}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dN_{i}^{R}(s;q)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)ds};

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}(w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{W}(\tau_{i}^{*};w_{1},w_{2})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau_{i}^{*}}Y_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})ds}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dN_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)Y_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})ds};

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}(v_{1},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{V}(\tau_{i}^{*};v_{1},v)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau_{i}^{*}}Y_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1})ds}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}dN_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1},v)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)Y_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1})ds}.

These estimators possess the interpretation of being the observed “occurrence-exposure" rates, since
the numerators are total event counts:

•

\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{R}(\tau_{i}^{*};q)
is the total number of observed RCR type
q
events;

•

\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{W}(\tau_{i}^{*};w_{1},w_{2})
is the total number of observed transitions in the LM process from state
w_{1}
into
w_{2}
;

•

\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{V}(\tau_{i}^{*};v_{1},v)
is the total number of observed transitions in the HS process from state
v_{1}
into
v
;

while the denominators are total observed exposure times:

•

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)ds
is the total time at-risk for all the units;

•

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)Y_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})dw
is the total observed time of all units that they were at-risk for a transition in the LM process from state
w_{1}
;

•

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)Y_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1})ds
is the total observed time of all units that they were at-risk for a transition in the HS process from state
v_{1}
.

An important and crucial point to emphasize is that the exposure times take into account the time after the last observed events in each component process until the end of monitoring, whether it is a censoring (reaching
\tau_{i}
) or an absorption (reaching
\tau_{iA}
). If one ignores these right-censored times, then the estimators could be severely biased. This is a critical aspect we mentioned in the introductory section and re-iterate at this point that this should not be glossed over when dealing with recurrent event models.

The elements of the observed information matrix,
I(\Theta;\mathbf{D})=-\nabla_{\Theta^{\tt T}}U(\Theta|\mathbf{D})
, which is a diagonal matrix, have diagonal elements:

\displaystyle I^{R}(\Theta;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{R}(\tau_{i}^{*};q)}{\lambda_{0q}^{2}},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q};

\displaystyle I^{W}(\Theta;w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{W}(\tau_{i}^{*};w_{1},w_{2})}{\eta(w_{1},w_{2})^{2}},(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}};

\displaystyle I^{V}(\Theta;v_{1},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{V}(\tau_{i}^{*};v_{1},v)}{\xi(v_{1},v)^{2}},(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}.

Abbreviating the estimators into
\hat{\Theta}=(\hat{\lambda}_{0},\hat{\eta},\hat{\xi})
, we obtain the asymptotic result, that as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\hat{\Theta}\sim\mbox{AsyMVN}(\Theta,I(\hat{\Theta};\mathbf{D})^{-1}),

(8)

with
AsyMVN
meaning asymptotically multivariate normal. Thus, this result seems to indicate that the RCR, LM, and HS components or the estimators of their respective parameters do not have anything to do with each other, which appears intuitive since the RCR, LM, and HS processes were assumed to be independent processes to begin with. But, let us examine this issue further. The result in (
8
) is an approximation to the theoretical result that

\hat{\Theta}\sim\mbox{AsyMVN}\left(\Theta,\frac{1}{n}\mathfrak{I}(\Theta)^{-1}\right),

(9)

where
\frac{1}{n}I(\hat{\Theta};\mathbf{D})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle pr}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathfrak{I}(\Theta)
. Evidently,
\mathfrak{I}
is a diagonal matrix, so let us examine its diagonal elements. Let
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
. Then we have, with ‘
pr-lim
’ denoting in-probability limit,

\displaystyle\lambda_{0q}^{2}\mathfrak{I}^{R}(\Theta;q)=\mbox{pr-lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dN_{i}^{R}(s;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{pr-lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dM_{i}^{R}(s;q)+\lambda_{0q}\left[\mbox{pr-lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)ds\right].

The first term on the right-hand side (RHS) converges in probability to zero by the weak law of large numbers and the zero-mean martingale property. The second term in the RHS converges in probability to its expectation, hence

\mathfrak{I}^{R}(\Theta;q)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0q}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}E[Y_{i}(s)]\right\}ds\right].

But, now,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}E[Y_{i}(s)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\mathbf{P}\{\tau_{i}\geq s\}\mathbf{P}\{\tau_{i}^{A}\geq s\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\bar{G}_{i}(s-)\mathbf{P}\{V_{i}(u)\notin\mathfrak{V}_{0},u\leq s\}.

with
\bar{G}_{i}=1-G_{i}
. The last probability term above will depend on the generators
\{\xi(v_{1},v):(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}\}
of the CTMC
\{V(s):s\geq 0\}
, so that the theoretical Fisher information or the asymptotic variance associated with the estimator
\hat{\lambda}_{0q}
depends after all on the HS process, as well as on the
G_{i}
s, contrary to the seemingly intuitive expectation that it should not depend on the LM and HS processes. This result is a subtle one which arises because of the structure of the observation processes. If
G_{i}=G,i=1,\ldots,n
, then we have that

\mathfrak{I}^{R}(\Theta;q)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0q}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{G}(s-)\mathbf{P}\{V(u)\notin\mathfrak{V}_{0},u\leq s\}ds,

since
\mathbf{P}\{V_{i}(u)\notin\mathfrak{V}_{0},u\leq s\}=\mathbf{P}\{V(u)\notin\mathfrak{V}_{0},u\leq s\},i=1,\ldots,n
. If we denote by
\Gamma
the generator matrix of
\{V(s):s\geq 0\}
and let
\Gamma_{1}
be the sub-matrix associated with the
\mathfrak{V}_{1}
states, then if
p_{0}^{V}\equiv(p_{V}(v_{1}),v_{1}\in\mathfrak{V}_{1})^{\tt T}
is the initial probability mass function of
V(0)
, we have

\mathbf{P}\{V(u)\notin\mathfrak{V}_{0},u\leq s\}=\mathbf{P}\{V(s)\in\mathfrak{V}_{1}\}=(p_{0}^{V})^{\tt T}\left[\exp\{s\Gamma_{11}\}\right]1_{|\mathfrak{V}_{1}|}

where the matrix exponential is

\exp\{s\Gamma_{11}\}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{s^{k}\Gamma_{11}^{k}}/{k!}

and
1_{K}
is a column vector of
1
s of dimension
K
. Thus, we obtain

\mathfrak{I}^{R}(\Theta;q)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0q}}(p_{0}^{V})^{\tt T}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{G}(s-)\frac{s^{k}}{k!}ds\right]\Gamma_{11}^{k}1_{|\mathfrak{V}_{1}|}.

For example, if
\bar{G}(s)=\exp(-\nu s)
, that is,
\tau_{i}
’s are exponentially-distributed with mean
1/\nu
, then the above expression simplifies to

\mathfrak{I}^{R}(\Theta;q)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0q}}\frac{1}{\nu}(p_{0}^{V})^{\tt T}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{11}}{\nu}\right)^{k}\right]1_{|\mathfrak{V}_{1}|}.

For computational purposes, one may use an eigenvalue decomposition of
\Gamma_{11}
:
\Gamma_{11}=U\mbox{Dg}(d)U^{-1}
where
d
consists of the eigenvalues of
\Gamma_{11}
and
U
is the matrix of eigenvectors associated with the eigenvalues
d
. The main point of this example though is the demonstration that estimators of the parameters associated with the RCR, LM, or HS process will depend on features of the other processes,
even
when one starts with independent processes.

We remark that the estimators
\hat{\lambda}_{0q}
s,
\hat{\eta}(w_{1},w_{2})
s, and
\hat{\xi}(v_{1},v)
s could also be derived as method-of-moments estimators using the martingale structure. The inverse of the observed Fisher information matrix coincides with an estimator using the optional variation (OV) matrix process, while the inverse of the Fisher information matrix coincides with the limit-in-probability of the predictable quadratic variation matrix. To demonstrate for
\lambda_{0q}
, we have that

\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{R}(s;q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[N_{i}^{R}(s;q)-\int_{0}^{s}Y_{i}(t)\lambda_{0q}dt\right]:s\geq 0\right\}

is a zero-mean square-integrable martingale. Let
s\rightarrow\infty
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{R}(\infty;q)=0
. Then solving for
\lambda_{0q}
we obtain
\hat{\lambda}_{0q}
. Next, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{0q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dN_{i}^{R}(s;q)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)ds}=\lambda_{0q}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dM_{i}^{R}(s;q)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}Y_{i}(s)ds}

so that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}[\hat{\lambda}_{0q}-\lambda_{0q}]=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(s)ds\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{I=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dM_{i}^{R}(s;q).

We have already seen where
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(s)ds
converges in probability, whereas by the Martingale Central Limit Theorem, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dM_{i}^{R}(s;q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\sigma^{2}_{R}(q))

with

\displaystyle\sigma_{R}^{2}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\mbox{pr-lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d\langle M_{i}^{R}(\cdot;q),M_{i}^{R}(\cdot;q)\rangle(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\mbox{pr-lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(s)\lambda_{0q}\right\}ds.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}[\hat{\lambda}_{0q}-\lambda_{0q}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\left(0,\frac{\lambda_{0q}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\mbox{pr-lim}_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(s)\right]ds}\right)

\displaystyle=N\left(0,\frac{\lambda_{0q}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E[Y_{i}(s)]\right]ds}\right),

which is the same result stated above using ML theory. Analogous asymptotic derivations can be done for
\hat{\eta}(w_{1},w_{2})
and
\hat{\xi}(v_{1},v)
, though the resulting limiting variances will involve expected occupation times for their respective states of the
W_{i}
-processes coming from the
Y_{i}^{W}(\cdot;w_{1})
terms and the
V_{i}
-processes from the
Y_{i}^{V}(\cdot;v_{1})
terms. Note that, asymptotically, these estimators are independent,
but
their limiting variances depend on the parameters from the other processes.

Semi-Parametric Estimation of Model Parameters

In this section we consider the estimation of model parameters when the baseline hazard rate functions
\lambda_{0q}(\cdot)
s are specified nonparametrically. Recall that
\Lambda_{0q}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{0q}(u)du,q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
are the associated baseline cumulative hazard functions and
\bar{F}_{0q}(t)=\exp\{-\Lambda_{0q}(t)\},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
are the associated baseline survivor functions.
Recall that, for
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
,
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}
, and
(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}
, we have

\displaystyle dA^{R}(s;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y(s)\lambda_{0q}[\mathcal{E}_{q}(s)]\rho_{q}(N^{R}(s-)|\alpha_{q})\psi^{R}(s|\theta^{R})ds;

\displaystyle dA^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y(s)Y^{W}(s;w_{1})\eta(w_{1},w_{2})\psi^{W}(s|\theta^{W})ds;

\displaystyle dA^{V}(s;v_{1},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y(s)Y^{V}(s;v_{1})\xi(v_{1},v)\psi^{V}(s|\theta^{V})ds.

We abbreviate the vector of model parameters into
\Theta\equiv(\Lambda_{0},\alpha,\eta,\xi,\theta^{R},\theta^{W},\theta^{V})
. Our goal is to obtain estimators for these parameters based on the sample data
\mathbf{D}=(D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{n})
.

The basic approach to obtain estimators is to first assume that
(\alpha,\theta^{R},\theta^{W},\theta^{V})
are known, then obtain ‘estimators’ of
(\Lambda_{0},\eta,\xi)
. Having obtained these ‘estimators’, in quotes since they are not yet estimators when
(\alpha,\theta^{R},\theta^{W},\theta^{V})
are unknown, we plug them into the likelihood function to obtain a profile likelihood function. From the resulting profile likelihood function, which depends on
(\alpha,\theta^{R},\theta^{W},\theta^{V})
, we obtain its maximizers with respect to these finite-dimensional parameters to obtain their estimators. These estimators are then plugged into the ‘estimators’ of
(\Lambda_{0},\eta,\xi)
to obtain their final estimators.

The full likelihood function based on the sample data
\mathbf{D}=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
could be written as a product of three “major” likelihood functions corresponding to the three model components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Theta|\mathbf{D})=\left[\prod_{q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}}\mathcal{L}^{R}(\Lambda_{0q},\alpha,\theta^{R};q|\mathbf{D})\right]\times

(10)

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\mathcal{L}^{W}(\eta,\theta^{V};w_{1},w_{2}|\mathbf{D})\right]\left[\prod_{(v_{1},v)\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{V}}}\mathcal{L}^{V}(\xi,\theta^{W};v_{1},v|\mathbf{D})\right]

where, suppressing writing of the parameters in the functions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{R}(\Lambda_{0q},\alpha,\theta^{R};q|\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prodi_{s=0}^{\infty}\left[dA_{i}^{R}(s;q)\right]^{dN_{i}^{R}(s;q)}\right\}\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dA_{i}^{R}(s;q)\right\};

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{W}(\eta,\theta^{W};w_{1},w_{2}|\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prodi_{s=0}^{\infty}\left[dA_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})\right]^{dN_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})}\right\}\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}dA_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1},w_{2})\right\};

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{V}(\eta,\theta^{V};v_{1},v|\mathbf{D})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prodi_{s=0}^{\infty}\left[dA_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1},v)\right]^{dN_{i}^{V}(s;v_{1},v)}\right\}\times

Let

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}M_{i}^{W}(dv;w_{1},w_{2})+\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}S^{0W}(v;w_{1}|{\theta}_{0}^{W})dv\right\}\times

\displaystyle\left\{\left(\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}S^{0W}(v;w_{1}|{\theta}_{0}^{W})dv\right)^{-1}+o_{p}(1)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})+\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{W}(dv;w_{1},w_{2})\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}S^{0W}(v;w_{1}|{\theta}_{0}^{W})dv\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle+o_{p}(1)

Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}M_{i}^{W}(dv;w_{1},w_{2})=o_{p}(1)
, then
\hat{\eta}(w_{1},w_{2})=\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})+o_{p}(1)
, proving the consistency of
\hat{\eta}(w_{1},w_{2})
for
\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})
.

Next, we prove the asymptotic normality result. Recall that
\hat{\theta}^{W}
satisfies the condition that

0=U_{pl}^{W}(\hat{\theta}^{W})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}[B_{i}^{W}(s-)-E^{W}(w_{1}|\hat{\theta}^{W})]N_{i}^{W}(ds;w_{1},w_{2}).

Taylor expansion yields

E^{W}(w_{1}|\hat{\theta}^{W})=E^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W})+V^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{*}^{W})(\hat{\theta}^{W}-\theta_{0}^{W})

where
\theta_{*}^{W}\in[\hat{\theta}^{W},\theta_{0}^{W}]
. Letting

H_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})=B_{i}^{W}(s-)-E^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W}),i=1,\ldots,n;w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W},

we then have the representation

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}[\hat{\theta}^{W}-\theta_{0}^{W}]=\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}}V^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{*}^{W})N_{\bullet}^{W}(s^{*};w_{1},\bullet)\right]^{-1}\times

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}H_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})N_{i}^{W}(ds;w_{1},w_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Sigma^{W}\right]^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}H_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})M_{i}^{W}(ds;w_{1},w_{2})\right\}+o_{p}(1)

since

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}H_{i}^{W}(s)\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})Y_{i}(s)Y_{i}^{W}(s)\psi_{i}^{W}(s|\theta_{0}^{W})ds=0;

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w_{1}\in\mathfrak{W}}V^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{*}^{W})N_{\bullet}^{W}(s^{*};w_{1},\bullet)=\Sigma^{W}+o_{p}(1),

using the fact that
\theta_{*}^{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\theta_{0}^{W}
. Also, using Taylor expansion, we have
\forall(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}
, that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}[\hat{\eta}(w_{1},w_{2})-\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})]=-\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})[E^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W})]^{\tt T}\sqrt{n}[\hat{\theta}^{W}-\theta_{0}^{W}]+

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}S^{0W}(s;w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W})ds\right]^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{W}(ds;w_{1},w_{2})\right]+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})[e^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W})]^{\tt T}\sqrt{n}[\hat{\theta}^{W}-\theta_{0}^{W}]+

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\mathcal{T}}s^{0W}(s;w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W})ds\right]^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{W}(ds;w_{1},w_{2})\right]+o_{p}(1)

Define

\displaystyle Z_{1}^{W}=\sqrt{n}[\hat{\theta}^{W}-\theta_{0}^{W}];

\displaystyle Z_{2}^{W}=\sqrt{n}[\hat{\eta}-\eta_{0}]+\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})(e_{0}^{W})^{\tt T}\sqrt{n}[\hat{\theta}^{W}-\theta_{0}^{W}].

Note that
Z_{1}
is a
\dim(\theta^{W})
-dimensional vector, whereas
Z_{2}^{W}
is a
|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|
-dimensional vector. From the results above, and if we let

H_{i}^{W}(s)=[B_{i}^{W}(s-)-E^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W}),(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}]

be the
(\dim(\theta^{W})\times|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|)
-dimensional matrix, and
I_{|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|}
be the
(|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|\times|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|)
-dimensional identity matrix, and since
M_{i}^{W}(s)
is the
(|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|\times 1)
-dimensional vector with elements

, we therefore have the asymptotic representation

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{1}^{W}\\
Z_{2}^{W}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\Sigma^{W}\right)^{-1}&0\\
0&\left(\mbox{Dg}(\int_{\mathcal{T}}s^{0W}(s|\theta_{0}^{W})ds)\right)^{-1}\end{array}\right]\times

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}H_{i}^{W}(s)\\
I_{|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|}\end{array}\right]M_{i}(ds)\right]+o_{p}(1).

By the Martingale Central Limit Theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}H_{i}^{W}(s)\\
I_{|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|}\end{array}\right]M_{i}(ds)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\Upsilon^{W})

where

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{pr-lim}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}H_{i}^{W}(s)\\
I_{|\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}|}\end{array}\right]\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0}^{W})\mbox{Dg}(Y_{i}^{W}(s))\left[\begin{array}[]{c}H_{i}^{W}(s)\\

\displaystyle Y_{i}(s)\psi_{i}^{W}(s|\theta_{0}^{W})ds=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma^{W}&0\\
0&Dg(\eta_{0}^{W})\end{array}\right]

where the final equality is obtained by using the facts that

\displaystyle\sum_{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{W}}}\eta_{0}(w_{1},w_{2})\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[B_{i}^{W}(s-)-E^{W}(w_{1}|\theta_{0}^{W})]^{\otimes 2}\times

\displaystyle Y_{i}(s)Y_{i}^{W}(s;w_{1})\psi_{i}^{W}(s|\theta_{0}^{W})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\Sigma^{W};

It follows that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{1}^{W}\\
Z_{2}^{W}\end{array}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\end{array},\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(\Sigma^{W})^{-1}&0\\
0&\Xi^{W}\end{array}\right]\right).

where

\Xi^{W}=\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})\left[\mbox{Dg}\left(\int_{\mathcal{T}}s^{0W}(s|\theta_{0}^{W})ds\right)\right]^{-1}.

Finally, since

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\theta}^{W}-\theta_{0}^{W}\\
\hat{\eta}-\eta_{0}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&0\\
-\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})e_{0}^{W}&I\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{1}^{W}\\
Z_{2}^{W}\end{array}\right]

then result (ii) of the theorem follows since

\displaystyle\Gamma^{W}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&0\\
-\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})e_{0}^{W}&I\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(\Sigma^{W})^{-1}&0\\
0&\Xi^{W}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&0\\
-\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})e_{0}^{W}&I\end{array}\right]^{\tt T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\Sigma^{W}\right)^{-1}&-\left(\Sigma^{W}\right)^{-1}e_{0}^{W}\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})\\
-\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})(e_{0}^{W})^{\tt T}\left(\Sigma^{W}\right)^{-1}&\Xi^{W}+\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})(e_{0}^{W})^{\tt T}\left(\Sigma^{W}\right)^{-1}e_{0}^{W}\mbox{Dg}(\eta_{0})\end{array}\right].

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi_{0}\right)\right]{S}_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw).

However,

\displaystyle E_{q}^{R}\left(s^{*},w|\phi_{0}\right)\equiv\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi_{0}\right)=E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi_{0}\right)\right],

completing the proof of the first result.

To prove the second result, we have

\displaystyle\langle U_{pl}^{R}(\phi_{0}|\cdot,t^{*})\rangle(s^{*})=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi_{0})-\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\otimes 2}\times

\displaystyle I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi_{0})\right]^{\otimes 2}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)

\displaystyle-2\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi_{0})\right]\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\tt T}\times

\displaystyle I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)

\displaystyle+\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\otimes 2}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q).

We consider each of these three terms in the last expression. Applying the techniques in proving Proposition
6.2
, the second term becomes

\displaystyle-2\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left\{E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi_{0}\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\tt T}\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\otimes 2}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw),

and since the third term by Proposition
6.2
equals

\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\otimes 2}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw),

then the last two terms simplify to

-\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\otimes 2}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw).

Finally, the first term, applying Proposition
6.2
, equals

\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})}\left\{\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi_{0}\right)\right]^{\otimes 2}\right\}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw),

so that

\displaystyle\langle U_{pl}^{R}(\phi_{0}|\cdot,t^{*})\rangle(s^{*})=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left\{V_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi_{0}\right)\right]\right\}\times

\displaystyle S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}V_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw).

∎

Next we provide a result relating the observed information matrix and the predictable quadratic variation matrix.

Proposition 6.4
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\frac{1}{n}I_{pl}^{R}(\phi|s^{*},t^{*})=\frac{1}{n}\langle U_{pl}^{R}(\phi|\cdot,t^{*})\rangle(s^{*})+o_{p}(1).

Proof.

Using Proposition
6.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi)-\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi\right)\right]I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left\{E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi)}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi\right)\right]\right\}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi\right)\right]S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw).

But

E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi)}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi\right)\right]=\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},w|\phi\right)

so the difference in the preceding equation equals zero. Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[\left\{\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi)-\left(\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi)\right)^{\otimes 2}\right\}-\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left\{\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{S_{q}^{0R}}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi)-\left(\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{S_{q}^{0R}}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi)\right)^{\otimes 2}\right\}\right]\times

\displaystyle I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q)=O_{p}(1)

by invoking the Martingale Central Limit Theorem, then the claimed result is proved.
∎

Proposition 6.5
.

If
\kappa_{iq}(s|\phi)=\exp\{Z_{i}(s-)\phi\}
, where
Z_{i}(s-)
is
\mathfrak{F}_{s-}
-measurable, then, for each
q=1,2,\ldots,Q
,

\displaystyle V_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi)}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{S_{q}^{0R}}(s^{*},w|\phi)-\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{S_{q}^{0R}}(s^{*},w|\phi)\right]^{\otimes 2}.

Proof.

The result immediately follows from the fact that

E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi)}\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi\right)\right]=E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi)}\left[\left(\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{Iq}}{\kappa_{Iq}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi\right)\right)^{\otimes 2}\right]

because, under the exponential form of
\kappa_{iq}(\cdot|\phi)
, we have

E_{\mathbb{P}_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi)}\left[\nabla_{\phi^{\otimes 2}}\log\kappa_{iq}\left(\mathcal{E}_{IqJ}^{-1}(w)|\phi\right)\right]=0.

∎

Henceforth, we shall assume that
\kappa_{iq}(s|\phi)=\exp\{Z_{i}(s-)\phi\}
, where
Z_{i}(s-)
is a function of
N_{i}^{R}(s-)
. This linear form may exclude, however, some load-sharing models that arise in software reliability engineering (see, for instance, the load-sharing models in
[
20
]
). For the more general model that includes such load-sharing models, the regularity conditions presented below need to be amended to impose conditions on the more general form of the
V_{q}(s^{*},w|\phi)
s. The exponential form above for
\kappa_{iq}
s leads to regularity conditions that mimic conditions contained in previous papers such as
[
2
]
and
[
13
]
dealing with the multiplicative intensity model or the Cox proportional hazards model.

RCR-Component Regularity Conditions

There exists a neighborhood
\Phi_{0}\subset\Phi
of
\phi_{0}
, and for each
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
, a scalar function, a
\dim(\phi)\times 1
-dimensional vector function, and a
\dim(\phi)\times\dim(\phi)
-dimensional matrix function, defined on
\mathcal{T}\times[0,t^{*}]\Phi
, and given, respectively, by

s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi),\quad s_{q}^{1R}(s^{*},w|\phi),\quad\mbox{and}\quad s_{q}^{2R}(s^{*},w|\phi),

which, aside from its dependence on
\phi
, may depend on all the true values of the model parameters, and satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

For
k=0,1,2
, and
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and with
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}=S_{q}^{1R}
and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}=S_{q}^{2R}
, we have

\sup_{w\in[0,t^{*}];\phi\in\Phi_{0}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{kR}(s^{*},w|\phi)-s_{q}^{kR}(s^{*},w|\phi)\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0;

(b)

For
k=0,1,2
, and
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
, and for
w\in[0,t^{*}]
,
s_{q}^{kR}(s^{*},w|\phi)
are continuous in
\phi
uniformly in
w
, and bounded on
[0,t^{*}]\times\Phi_{0}
;

(c)

For each
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
,
s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},t^{*}|\phi_{0})>0
and

\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\frac{\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)}{s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})}<\infty;

(d)

For
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
, and for
w\in[0,t^{*}]
, each
s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi)
is twice-differentiable in
\phi
, and with

s_{q}^{1R}(s^{*},w|\phi)=\nabla_{\phi}s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi)\ \mbox{and}\ s_{q}^{2R}(s^{*},w|\phi)=\nabla_{\phi^{\otimes 2}}s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi);

(e)

For
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
and
w\in[0,t^{*}]
, with

e_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi)=\frac{s_{q}^{1R}(s^{*},w|\phi)}{s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi)};

v_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi)=\frac{s_{q}^{2R}(s^{*},w|\phi)}{s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi)}-\left[e_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi)\right]^{\otimes 2},

then each of the matrices

\Sigma_{q}^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})=\int_{0}^{t^{*}}v_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)

is positive definite, so that the matrix

\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\Sigma_{q}^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})

is also positive definite.

We now present asymptotic properties of the estimator
\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Under the “RCR-Component Regularity Conditions,” as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(i)

\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\phi_{0}
;

(ii)

\sqrt{n}[\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})-\phi_{0}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\left(0,\left[\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})\right]^{-1}\right)
.

Proof.

The consistency result is established using analogous techniques in proving the consistency of the estimator
\hat{\theta}^{W}
in the preceding subsection, which builds on those in
[
2
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
1
]
, and
[
27
]
. Thus, we do not anymore present its details.

To prove the asymptotic normality result, since
U_{pl}^{R}(\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})|s^{*},t^{*})=0
, then by Taylor expansion

U_{pl}^{R}(\phi_{0}|s^{*},t^{*})-I_{pl}^{R}(\phi^{\dagger}|s^{*},t^{*})(\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})-\phi_{0})=0

where
\phi^{\dagger}\in[\phi_{0},\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})]
. Thus,

\left[\frac{1}{n}I_{pl}^{R}(\phi^{\dagger}|s^{*},t^{*})\right]\left[\sqrt{n}(\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})-\phi_{0})\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}U_{pl}^{R}(\phi_{0}|s^{*},t^{*}).

From the regularity conditions, and since
\phi^{\dagger}\rightarrow\phi_{0}
by virtue of
\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\phi_{0}
and Proposition
6.4
, we have

\left[\frac{1}{n}I_{pl}^{R}(\phi^{\dagger}|s^{*},t^{*})\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})

which is assumed positive definite. Also, by the Martingale Central Limit Theorem,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}U_{pl}^{R}(\phi_{0}|s^{*},t^{*})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}H_{iq}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q)

where

H_{iq}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi)=\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi)-\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}}{{S}_{q}^{0R}}\left(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi\right),

converges in distribution to a
N(0,\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*}))
random vector by using Proposition
6.3
. As a consequence, we obtain that

\sqrt{n}[\hat{\phi}(s^{*},t^{*})-\phi_{0}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\left(0,\left[\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})\right]^{-1}\right).

∎

We now establish asymptotic results for the estimators of
\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(\cdot),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
.

Theorem 6.7
.

Under the “RCR-Component Regularity Conditions", as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
we have:

(i)

For each
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
,
\sup_{t\in[0,t^{*}]}\left|\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)-\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(t)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0
;

(ii)

The
Q
-dimensional process

\displaystyle\left\{\sqrt{n}\left[(\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)-\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(t)),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right]:t\in[0,t^{*}]\right\}

converges weakly on Skorokhod’s
\mathfrak{D}^{Q}[0,t^{*}]
-space to a zero-mean
Q
-dimensional multivariate Gaussian process

G^{R}=\left\{(G_{q}^{R}(s^{*},t),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}):t\in[0,t^{*}]\right\}

with covariance kernel given by, for
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
and
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,t^{*}]
,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{R}_{q_{1},q_{2}}(s^{*},t_{1},t_{2})\equiv Cov\left\{G_{q_{1}}^{R}(s^{*},t_{1}),G_{q_{2}}^{R}(s^{*},t_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{I\{q_{1}=q_{2}\}\int_{0}^{\min\{t_{1},t_{2}\}}\frac{\Lambda_{0q_{1}}^{0}(dw)}{s_{q_{1}}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})}\right\}+

\displaystyle\left\{\left[\int_{0}^{t_{1}}e_{q_{1}}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q_{1}}^{0}(dw)\right]^{\tt T}[\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})]^{-1}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left[\int_{0}^{t_{2}}e_{q_{2}}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q_{2}}^{0}(dw)\right]\right\}.

To systematically present the proof of Theorem
6.7
, we first establish some intermediate results. We start with an asymptotic representation of
\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)
. Define, for
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
and
t\in[0,t^{*}]
,

\Lambda_{0q}^{*}(s^{*},t)=\int_{0}^{t}I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\}\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw).

Then, we have the following representations.

Lemma 6.8
.

Under the “RCR-Component Regularity Conditions", for each
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
and
t\in[0,t^{*}]
, and as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)-\Lambda_{0q}^{*}(s^{*},t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q)-

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{t}I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})>0\}E_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)\right]^{\tt T}(\hat{\phi}-\phi_{0})+o_{p}(1).

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\hat{\phi})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\hat{\phi})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{R}(s^{*},dw;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\hat{\phi})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\hat{\phi})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}N_{i}^{R}(ds;q).

By Taylor expansion,

\frac{1}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\hat{\phi})}=\frac{1}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}-\frac{[\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{S}}_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi^{\dagger})]^{\tt T}}{[{S}_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi^{\dagger})]^{2}}(\hat{\phi}-\phi_{0})

where
\phi^{\dagger}\in[\phi_{0},\hat{\phi}]
, so that
\phi^{\dagger}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\phi_{0}
. As such

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\hat{\phi})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}N_{i}^{R}(ds;q)-

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\hat{\phi})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi^{\dagger})}\left[E_{q}^{R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi^{\dagger})\right]^{\tt T}\right.

\displaystyle\left.I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}N_{i}^{R}(ds;q)\right\}(\hat{\phi}-\phi_{0}).

Since
\hat{\phi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\phi_{0}
and
\sup_{t\in[0,t^{*}];\phi\in\Gamma_{0}}\left|\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{kR}(s^{*},t|\phi)-s_{q}^{kR}(s^{*},t|\phi)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\phi_{0}
for
k=0,1
, then

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}N_{i}^{R}(ds;q)-

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}\left[E_{q}^{R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})\right]^{\tt T}\right.

\displaystyle\left.I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}N_{i}^{R}(ds;q)\right\}(\hat{\phi}-\phi_{0})+o_{p}(1).

The first-term above is

\displaystyle\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}N_{i}^{R}(ds;q)-

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q)+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q).

But the last-term above, using Proposition
6.2
, equals
\Lambda_{0q}^{*}(s^{*},t)
. This establishes the lemma.

∎

Lemma 6.9
.

Under the “RCR-Component Regularity Conditions", for each
q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}
and as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sup_{t\in[0,t^{*}]}\left|\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)-\Lambda_{0q}^{*}(s^{*},t)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sup_{t\in[0,t^{*}]}\left|\Lambda_{0q}^{*}(s^{*},t)-\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(t)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

Proof.

The second result is immediate from the condition that

\sup_{t\in[0,t^{*}];\phi\in\Gamma_{0}}\left|\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},t|\phi)-s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},t|\phi)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

To prove the first result, from the representation in Lemma
6.8
and
\hat{\phi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\phi_{0}
, it suffices to show that

(i)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q)=o_{p}(1);

(ii)

\int_{0}^{t^{*}}I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})>0\}E_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)=O_{p}(1).

But (ii) is immediate from the regularity conditions that, for
k=0,1
,

\sup_{t\in[0,t^{*}];\phi\in\Gamma_{0}}\left|\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{kR}(s^{*},t|\phi)-s_{q}^{kR}(s^{*},t|\phi)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0

and
s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},t^{*}|\phi_{0})>0
.
To prove (i), let
\epsilon>0
and
\delta>0
, but arbitrary. We may invoke Lenglart’s Inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,t^{*}]}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q)\right|>\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left\{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q)\right|>\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\epsilon^{2}}+

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left\{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{[\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})]^{2}}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)>\delta\right\}.

However, by Proposition
6.2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\frac{I\{\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{[\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})]^{2}}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t^{*}\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\frac{I\{\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})>0\}}{[\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})]^{2}}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\frac{I\{\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})>0\}}{[\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})]}\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o_{p}(1)

since
\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})=O_{p}(1)
because, by the regularity conditions we have

s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},t^{*}|\phi_{0})>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\frac{\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)}{s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w;\phi_{0})}<\infty.

Thus, for any
\epsilon>0
, the right-hand side in the inequality above could be made as small as desired for
n\geq N
by first choosing an appropriate
\delta>0
, and then an appropriate
N=N(\epsilon,\delta)
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Next, let us define, for
t\in[0,t^{*}]
,

\displaystyle Z^{R}(s^{*},t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{1}^{R}(s^{*},t)\\
Z_{2}^{R}(s^{*},t)\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}H_{i1}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})\\
H_{i2}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})\end{array}\right]\mbox{Dg}(I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q})M_{i}^{R}(ds)

where
M_{i}^{R}(ds)=\left[M_{i}^{R}(ds;q),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right]
and

\displaystyle H_{i1}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bullet}}{{\kappa}}_{iq}}{\kappa_{iq}}(s|\phi_{0})-E_{q}^{R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0}),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right]^{\tt T};

\displaystyle H_{i2}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{Dg}\left\{\frac{I\{\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})>0\}}{\frac{1}{n}S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)|\phi_{0})},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right\}.

Lemma 6.10
.

Under the “RCR-Component Regularity Conditions", the process
\{Z^{R}(s^{*},t):t\in[0,t^{*}]\}
converges weakly on Skorokhod’s
\mathfrak{D}^{p+Q}[0,\tau^{*}]
-space, where
p=\dim(\phi)
, to a zero-mean multivariate Gaussian process

\{Z_{\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t):t\in[0,t^{*}]\}=\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{1\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t)\\
Z_{2\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t)\end{array}\right]:t\in[0,t^{*}]\right\}

whose covariance kernels are, for
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,t^{*}]
,

\displaystyle Cov\{Z_{1\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t_{1}),Z_{1\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t_{2})\}=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{\min(t_{1},t_{2})}v_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw);

\displaystyle Cov\{Z_{1\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t_{1}),Z_{2\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t_{2})\}=0;

\displaystyle Cov\{Z_{2\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t_{1}),Z_{2\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t_{2})\}=\mbox{Dg}\left[\int_{0}^{\min(t_{1},t_{2})}\frac{\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)}{s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right].

In particular, note that

Cov\{Z_{1\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t^{*}),Z_{1\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})\}=\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})

and
Z_{1\infty}^{R}
and
Z_{2\infty}^{R}
are independent.

Proof.

We employ the Wold device. Let
c_{1}\in\Re^{p}
and
c_{2}\in\Re^{Q}
with
(c_{1}^{\tt T},c_{2}^{\tt T})\neq 0
. Let
t\in[0,t^{*}]
. Then

\displaystyle c_{1}^{\tt T}Z_{1}^{R}(s^{*},t)+c_{2}^{\tt T}Z_{2}^{R}(s^{*},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[c_{1}^{\tt T}H_{i1}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})+c_{2}^{\tt T}H_{i2}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})\right]\times

\displaystyle\mbox{Dg}\left[I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right]\left[M_{i}^{R}(ds;q),q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[c_{1}^{\tt T}H_{iq1}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})+c_{2}^{\tt T}H_{iq2}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})\right]\times

\displaystyle I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}M_{i}^{R}(ds;q).

By the Martingale Central Limit Theorem, this converges in distribution to a zero-mean normal variable with variance being the in-probability limit of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[c_{1}^{\tt T}H_{iq1}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})+c_{2}^{\tt T}H_{iq2}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})\right]^{2}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[c_{1}^{\tt T}H_{iq1}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})^{\otimes 2}c_{1}\right]I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)+

\displaystyle 2\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[c_{1}^{\tt T}H_{iq1}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})H_{iq2}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})^{\tt T}c_{2}\right]\times

\displaystyle I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q)+

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s^{*}}\left[c_{2}^{\tt T}H_{iq2}^{R}(s^{*},s|\phi_{0})^{\otimes 2}c_{2}\right]I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t\}A_{i}^{R}(ds;q).

Using earlier results, the first-term converges in probability to
c_{1}^{\tt T}\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t)c_{1}
; the second-term equals 0; and the third-term converges in probability to

c_{2}^{\tt T}\mbox{Dg}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)}{s_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})},q\in\mathfrak{I}_{Q}\right]c_{2}.

As such, for each
t\in[0,t^{*}]
,

c_{1}^{\tt T}Z_{1}^{R}(s^{*},t)+c_{2}^{\tt T}Z_{2}^{R}(s^{*},t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}c_{1}^{\tt T}Z_{1\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t)+c_{2}^{\tt T}Z_{2\infty}^{R}(s^{*},t)

for every
(c_{1}^{\tt T},c_{2}^{\tt T})\neq 0
.

For
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,t^{*}]
, joint convergence in distribution to a zero-mean bivariate normal distribution with the covariance matrix specified in the statement of the lemma is established similarly and by noting that

I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t_{1}\}I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq t_{2}\}=I\{\mathcal{E}_{iq}(s)\leq\min(t_{1},t_{2})\}.

So the finite-dimensional distributions of
\{Z^{R}(s^{*},t):t\in[0,t^{*}]\}
converges in distribution to zero-mean multivariate normal distributions with the covariance matrix specified in the statement of the lemma.

To upgrade convergence to weak convergence on Skorokhod’s
\mathfrak{D}^{p+Q}[0,t^{*}]
-space, tightness needs to be established. This could be done using similar arguments in proving the Master Theorem presented in
[
28
]
, with this theorem used in
[
30
]
.
∎

Establishing the results in Theorem
6.7
now immediately follows from these lemmas.

Proof of Theorem
6.7
.

The consistency result in Theorem
6.7
follows from Lemma
6.9
.
The weak convergence result then follows by noting that

\sqrt{n}[\hat{\phi}-\phi_{0}]=[\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})]^{-1}Z_{1}^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})+o_{p}(1)

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}[\hat{\Lambda}_{0q}(s^{*},t)-\Lambda_{0q}^{*}(s^{*},t)]=Z_{2}^{R}(s^{*},t)+

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{t}I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})>0\}E_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)\right]^{\tt T}

\displaystyle[\Sigma^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})]^{-1}Z_{1}^{R}(s^{*},t^{*})+o_{p}(1)

and the fact that, uniformly in
t\in[0,t^{*}]
,

\int_{0}^{t}I\{S_{q}^{0R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})>0\}E_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\int_{0}^{t}e_{q}^{R}(s^{*},w|\phi_{0})\Lambda_{0q}^{0}(dw).

∎

and

]
to more complex and general situations.

Finite-Sample Properties via Simulation Studies

8.1
Simulation Design

We have provided asymptotic results of the estimators in Section
6
. In this section we present the results of simulation studies to assess the finite-sample properties of the estimators of model parameters. This will provide some evidence whether the semi-parametric estimation procedure, which appears to perform satisfactorily for the single illustrative sample data set in Section
7
, performs satisfactorily over many sample data sets.
These simulation studies were implemented using the
R
and
Fortran
programs we developed. In these simulation studies, as in the preceding section, when we analyze each of the sample data, the baseline hazard rate functions are estimated
semi-parametrically
, even though in the generation of each of the sample data sets, two-parameter Weibull models were used in the RCR components. That is, in the estimation procedure, we totally ignored the knowledge that the baseline hazard functions are of the Weibull-type with two parameters.
Aside from the set of model parameters described in Section
7
, the simulation study have the additional inputs which are the sample size
n
and the number of simulation replications
Mreps
, the latter set to 100. The sample sizes used in the two simulation studies are
n\in\{50,100\}
.
For fixed
n
, for each of the
Mreps
replications, the sample data generation is as described in Section
7
. For each estimator, for each
n
, we obtained the mean of the estimates, as well as the standard deviation of the estimates, which is a simulated estimate of the standard error of the estimator. In each of the summary tables, we also provide the true parameter value, and the mean and standard deviation of the estimates for each
n\in\{50,100\}
. These summary tables are provided in the last four columns labelled ‘In Simulation Study’ in Tables
2

8.2
Some Limitations

In implementing the semi-parametric approach to estimating model parameters, there were also some limitations encountered in the computational implementation. Recall that a Newton-Raphson iteration approach was utilized in maximizing the partial likelihood functions. The perennial limitation of finding appropriate seed values for the iteration were also encountered. In experimenting with the algorithmic implementation, we found that sometimes it helps to start with a gradient descent approach in the iteration, and then to later transition to the Newton-Raphson iteration. In addition, when
n
is not large enough, especially when
Q>1
leading to many parameters, the profile likelihoods could be rather flat, hence convergence may be compromised or not occur. We also note that obtaining estimates of the parameters, both finite- and infinite-dimensional, could be computationally time-consuming, hence we were able to run the simulations with just 100 replications. This is even after some portions were converted to
Fortran
codes. One reason for this computational intensiveness is because in estimating the baseline survivor functions, we required the computation of the whole functional estimate, instead of simply focusing on a pre-specified set of time values. We also mention that in this paper we did not apply the model and the estimation procedure to
real
data sets. We hope to apply the modeling approach and the estimation procedure to some real data sets in a separate paper.

9
Concluding Remarks

For the general class of joint models for recurrent competing risks, longitudinal marker, and health status proposed in this paper, which encompasses many existing models considered previously, there are still numerous aspects that need to be addressed in future studies. Foremost among these aspects is a more refined analytical study of the finite-sample and asymptotic properties of the estimators of model parameters, together with other inferential and prediction procedures. The finite-sample and asymptotic results could be exploited to enable performing tests of hypothesis and construction of confidence regions for model parameters. There is also the interesting aspect of computationally estimating the standard errors of the estimators. How would a bootstrapping approach be implemented in this situation, or would such an approach be prohibitively computationally expensive? Another important problem that needs to be addressed is how to perform goodness-of-fit and model validation for this joint model. Though the class of models is very general, there are still possibilities of model mis-specifications, such as, for example, in specifying the effective age processes, or in the specification of the
\rho_{q}(\cdot|\cdot)
-functions. What are the impacts of such model mis-specifications? Do they lead to serious biases that could potentially result in misleading conclusions? These are some of the problems whose solutions await further studies.

A potential promise of this joint class of models is in precision medicine. Because all three components (RCR, LM, HS) are taken into account simultaneously, in contrast to a marginal modeling approach, the synergy that this joint model allows may improve decision-making – for example, in determining interventions to be performed for individual units. In this context, it is of utmost importance to be able to predict in the future the trajectories of the HS process, given information at a given point in time, about all three processes. Thus, an important problem to be dealt with in future work is the problem of forecasting using this joint model. How should such forecasting be implemented? This further leads to other important questions. One is determining the relative importance of each of the components in this prediction problem. Could one ignore other components and still do as well relative to a joint model-based prediction approach? If there are many covariates, how should the important covariates among these numerous covariates be chosen in order to improve prediction of, say, the time-to-absorption?

Finally, though our class of joint models is a natural general extension of earlier models dealing with either recurrent events, competing recurrent events, longitudinal marker, and terminal events, one may impugn it as perhaps not realistic, but instead view it as more of a futuristic class of models, since existing data sets were not gathered (yet) in the manner for which these joint models apply. For instance, in the example pertaining to gout in Section
2
, the SUR level and CKD status are not continuously monitored. However, with the advent of smart devices, such as smart wrist watches, embedded sensors, black boxes, etc., made possible by miniaturized technology, high-speed computing, almost limitless cloud-based memory capacity, and availability of rich cloud-based databases, the era is, in our opinion, fast approaching when continuous monitoring of longitudinal markers, health status, occurrences of different types of recurrent events, be it on a human being, an experimental animal or plant, a machine such as a car, an airplane, a nuclear power plant, a medical equipment, or a space satellite in engineering settings, a company in a economic or business setting, etc., will become more of a standard rather than an exception. It is our hope that by developing the models and inferential methods for such future complex and advanced data sets,
even
before they become available and real, that this will hasten and prepare us all for their eventual and certain arrival.
