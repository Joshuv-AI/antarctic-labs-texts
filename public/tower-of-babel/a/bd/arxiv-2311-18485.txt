From Euclidean field theory to hyperkähler Floer theory via regularized polysymplectic geometry
Ronen Brilleslijper, Oliver Fabert

Abstract
Hamiltonian Floer theory plays an important role for finding periodic solutions of Hamilton’s equation, which can be seen as a generalization of Newton’s equation. Generalizing Newton’s equation to Laplace’s equation with non-linearity, we show, building on the work of Ginzburg and Hein, that this role is taken over by the hyperkähler Floer theory of Hohloch, Noetzel, and Salamon. Apart from establishing C 0 C^{0} -bounds in order to be able to deal with noncompact hyperkähler manifolds, the core ingredient is a regularization scheme for the polysymplectic formalism due to Bridges, which allows us to link Euclidean field theory with hyperkähler Floer theory. As a concrete result, we prove a cuplength estimate.

Let us start by briefly outlining what we mean with the terms Euclidean field theory and Bridges regularization.

The version of field theory we consider in this paper is
Euclidean field theory
. The simplest way of viewing this is as a version of field theory on Euclidean rather than Minkowski space. In the physics community it is used in both the study of quantum field theory and statistical mechanics. For us, the Euclidean metric makes for better analytical properties, since the field equations become elliptic instead of hyperbolic. While the basic example in (Minkowski) field theory is the wave equation with non-linearity, in the Euclidean theory this would turn into Laplace’s equation with non-linearity. Under suitable conditions, classical field theory can be recovered from the Euclidean version by taking the time-variable to be imaginary, called a Wick rotation. For an introduction to Euclidean field theory we refer to
[
PZ11
]
. In this article we will mainly focus on the simplest equation from Euclidean field theory: the Laplace equation with non-linearity (see
equation

3
below).

The term
Bridges regularization
was introduced in
[
BF23
]
. It was first noticed in
[
BD99
]
that there is a problem with the standard coordinate-symmetric way of formulating the wave equation as a Hamiltonian equation. The operator involved has an infinite-dimensional kernel and lacks a good analytical framework. In that article the equations were upgraded by the introduction of an extra variable and an extra constraint equation. Later, Bridges worked out the general structure behind this regularization procedure that works both for Riemannian and pseudo-Riemannian metrics (
[
Bri06
]
). In
section

1.2
we elaborate more about the issues with the standard coordinate-symmetric Hamiltonian formulation of field theory and outline Bridges’ regularization process for the Laplace equation. In chapter 3 of
[
BF23
]
the analogous construction for the (1+1)-dimensional wave equation can be found.

1.1
Motivation from classical mechanics

We start with
Newton’s equation

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}q(t)=-V_{t}^{\prime}(q(t)),

(1)

where
q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to Q
denotes the position of a particle moving in some potential
V_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Q\to\mathbb{R}
on a manifold
Q
. This equation can be rewritten as a first-order system with the help of a Hamiltonian on the cotangent space
T^{*}Q
of
Q
. Let
u=(q,p)\in T^{*}Q
denote the coordinates on the cotangent bundle and
H_{t}(u)=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}+V_{t}(q)
. Then Newton’s equation is equivalent to
Hamilton’s equation

\displaystyle J\frac{d}{dt}u(t)=\nabla H_{t}(u(t)),

(2)

where
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\textup{Id}\\
\textup{Id}&0\end{pmatrix}
is the standard complex structure on
T^{*}Q
and
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to T^{*}Q
. Floer theory can be used to prove the existence of periodic solutions for these systems. The crucial observation is that periodic solutions of Hamilton’s equation are in one-to-one correspondence with critical points of the action functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{1}}(u^{*}\lambda)-\int_{0}^{1}H_{t}(u(t))\,dt,

where
\lambda=p\,dq
is the tautological one-form on
T^{*}Q
. A classical result in Floer theory states that the number of critical points of this functional is bounded below by the cuplength of the free loop space
\Lambda Q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=C^{\infty}(S^{1},Q)
, yielding a lower bound on the number of periodic solutions to Newton’s equation. For a detailed explanation of Floer theory on the cotangent bundle we refer to
[
Cie94
,
AS06
]
and the fundamentals of Floer theory on closed manifolds can be found in
[
AD14
]
.

Generalizing from classical mechanics to Euclidean field theory, the time manifold becomes multi-dimensional and Newton’s
equation

1
turns into the
Laplace equation

\displaystyle\Delta q(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}q(t)=-V_{t}^{\prime}(q(t)),

(3)

where
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
and
\partial_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{j}}
. The main goal of the present article is to establish a link between cuplength results for periodic solutions of
equation

3
and Hamiltonian methods. While Morse theoretic methods have already been applied to semilinear elliptic equations (e.g.
[
Sma00
]
), it is not known yet what analogue of Hamilton’s
equation

2
can be used to formulate a generalization of Floer’s methods to prove this. Our main
theorem

2.3
states a more general result. The straightforward
corollary

2.4
gives a lower bound on the number of periodic solutions to
equation

3
. Along the way we will see a surprising connection to the established version of Floer theory on hyperkähler manifolds. In order to make this connection, we will focus on the Laplace equation in 3 dimension (i.e.
n=3
in the equation above). Moreover, we will only deal with flat tori as target manifolds here. Thus, in this article we will deal with maps
q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{3}\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
for some positive integer
d
.

1.2
Two Hamiltonian formalisms

First approach

Before we can turn to the Floer theory, first a Hamiltonian formalism for Laplace’s equation needs to be established. A first approach to this would be to introduce momentum variables
p_{j}=\partial_{t_{j}}q
, which turns

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\partial_{1}p_{1}-

\displaystyle\partial_{2}p_{2}-

\displaystyle\partial_{3}p_{3}

\displaystyle=V_{t}^{\prime}(q)

\displaystyle=p_{1}

[

]

The main difference between

J\frac{d}{dt}
from

2

K_{\partial}=K_{1}\partial_{1}+K_{2}\partial_{2}+K_{3}\partial_{3}
. Even though periodic solutions to

4

2

equation

3
, whereas
equation

4
is
not elliptic
anymore. This can be seen by noting that the symbol of
K_{\partial}
always has a kernel. As Floer theory relies heavily on ellipticity this is undesirable. Related to the ellipticity is the fact that

does not square to the negative Laplacian, in contrast with

in the setting of Newton’s equation. Moreover,
K_{\partial}
has an
infinite-dimensional kernel
on the space of periodic maps, in contrast to
J\frac{d}{dt}
which has only the constant functions in the kernel. For example
(0,\partial_{2}\psi,-\partial_{1}\psi,0)
and
(0,\partial_{3}\psi,0,-\partial_{1}\psi)
lie in the kernel of
K_{\partial}
for any periodic function
\psi=\psi(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})
. A similar argument as made in the previous article of the authors
[
BF23
, Section 2.3]
shows that Floer curves of finite energy associated to
equation

do not necessarily converge to periodic solutions. Therefore, this Hamiltonian formalism is not suitable for the goal of this paper.

Note that in

2
The setting

The setting in which we will work is a generalization of the scalar Euclidean field theory described above. We will work with maps
q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
between flat tori. As mentioned above, we will focus on the case
n=3
, but the setting can be introduced for general
n
.

As flat tori arise as quotients of Euclidean space, we will start by defining the covering space of the manifold that we will work with. Let

\displaystyle\bar{B}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\bigwedge(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}\oplus\bigwedge\nolimits^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}\oplus\bigoplus_{k=2}^{n}\bigwedge\nolimits^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}\right).

We denote elements
\bar{Z}\in\bar{B}
as
\bar{Z}=(\bar{q},\bar{p},\bar{o})
, where

\displaystyle\bar{q}

\displaystyle=(\bar{q}^{\alpha})_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\bar{p}

\displaystyle=(\bar{p}^{\alpha}_{i})_{\alpha,i}

\displaystyle\bar{o}

\displaystyle=(\bar{o}^{\alpha}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}})_{k,\alpha,i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}

We always take
\alpha=1,\ldots,d
,

and
k=2,\ldots,n
. Note that
\bar{q}
and
\bar{p}
denote the 0- and 1-forms respectively and

\bar{Z}^{\textup{even}}
and
\bar{Z}^{\textup{odd}}
for the collection of even- and odd-degree forms respectively.

The group
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
acts on the even-degree forms by translation, where

. We let
B
denote the quotient by this action. Thus

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

with elements

. We will still denote the elements of

where these coordinates are given by the projections of the corresponding coordinates on

. Note that

Equip

\bar{J}_{\partial}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\textup{Id}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\otimes(d+d^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\bar{B})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\bar{B}),

[

Remark 2.1
.

(i)

so that the

Finally, for a Hamiltonian

\displaystyle J_{\partial}Z(t)=\nabla H_{t}(Z(t)).

Our main example will be Hamiltonians of the form

3.1
Bounds on periodic orbits

In this section we will prove
lemma

3.1
.
Let
J_{\partial}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3},B)\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3},\bar{B})
be defined as in
section

2
and
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{3}\times B\to\mathbb{R}
given by
H_{t}(Z)=\frac{1}{2}|Z^{\textup{odd}}|^{2}+W_{t}(q,p)
. We denote the set of periodic solutions of
equation

8
by
\mathcal{P}(H)=\{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{T}^{3}\to B\mid J_{\partial}Z(t)=\nabla H_{t}(Z(t))\}
and
\mathcal{P}_{a}(H)=\{Z\in\mathcal{P}(H)\mid a\geq\mathcal{A}_{H}(Z)\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
and suppose
W
has bounded
C^{2}
-norm. Then
\mathcal{P}_{a}(H)
is bounded in
L^{\infty}
.

We will start with the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
\mathcal{P}_{a}(H)
is bounded in
L^{2}
, if
W
has finite
C^{1}
-norm.

Proof.

First, define the vector field

\xi=\sum_{i,\alpha}p_{i}^{\alpha}\partial_{p_{i}^{\alpha}}+\sum_{\alpha}o_{123}^{\alpha}\partial_{o_{123}^{\alpha}}

on
B
. A straightforward calculation shows that
\iota_{\xi}\omega^{J_{i}}=\theta_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
where the explicit formulas for
\omega^{J_{i}}=d\theta_{i}
are given in
equation

9
.

Let
Z\in\mathcal{P}_{a}(H)
. Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\omega^{J_{i}}(\cdot,\partial_{i}Z)=dH_{Z}.

(13)

So

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(Z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\theta_{i}(\partial_{i}Z)\,d\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\omega^{J_{i}}(\xi,\partial_{i}Z)\,d\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}dH_{Z}(\xi)\,d\mathcal{V}.

Now by assumption

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{A}_{H}(Z)=\mathcal{A}(Z)-\int H(Z)\,d\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=\int\left(dH_{Z}(\xi)-H(Z)\right)\,d\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\geq h_{0}||Z^{\textup{odd}}||^{2}_{L^{2}}-h_{1},

for some
h_{0}>0
and
h_{1}\geq 0
(compare lemma 1.10 from
[
AS06
]
). Here the last inequality follows from the fact that both
W
and its derivative are bounded, hence
H
grows quadratically in the
p_{i}^{\alpha}
- and
o_{123}^{\alpha}
-coordinates. We conclude that there is a uniform bound on the
L^{2}
-norm of
Z^{\textup{odd}}
. As
Z^{\textup{even}}
maps into
\mathbb{T}^{m}
, its
L^{2}
-norm is bounded by construction. Thus the proposition follows.
∎

Proof of
lemma

3.1
.

We will prove boundedness in
H^{2}
by a bootstrapping argument. The
L^{\infty}
-bounds follow from Sobolev’s embedding theorem as
2\cdot 2>3
.
Recall that
J_{\partial}Z=\nabla H(Z)
. Taking norms on both sides we get

||J_{\partial}Z||_{L^{2}}=||\nabla H(Z)||_{L^{2}}\leq||Z^{\textup{odd}}||_{L^{2}}+||\nabla W_{t}(q,p)||_{L^{2}}.

As the gradient of
W
is bounded and
\mathcal{P}_{a}(H)
is bounded in
L^{2}
, we get a uniform bound on the right-hand side of this equation. Also, for any
k

\displaystyle\begin{split}||J_{\partial}Z||^{2}_{H^{k}}&=\sum_{j\leq k}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{j}}||\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j}}J_{\partial}Z||^{2}_{L^{2}}\\
&=\sum_{j\leq k}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\langle\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j}}J_{\partial}Z,\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j}}J_{\partial}Z\rangle\,d\mathcal{V}\\
&=-\sum_{j\leq k}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\langle\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j}}Z,\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j}}\Delta Z\rangle\,d\mathcal{V}\\
&=\sum_{j\leq k}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{j},i}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{j}}\partial_{i}Z|^{2}\,d\mathcal{V}\\
&=||\nabla Z||^{2}_{H^{k}},\end{split}

(14)

where we used partial integration, the fact that
J_{\partial}
is self-adjoint with respect to the
L^{2}
-metric and that it squares to the negative Laplacian.
Applying this to
k=0
we see that both
||Z||_{L^{2}}
and
||\nabla Z||_{L^{2}}=||J_{\partial}Z||_{L^{2}}
are uniformly bounded, so that we get uniform bounds in
H^{1}
.

By the same reasoning we also get that

||\nabla Z||_{H^{1}}=||J_{\partial}Z||_{H^{1}}\leq||Z^{\textup{odd}}||_{H^{1}}+||\nabla W(q,p)||_{H^{1}}.

By a chain-rule argument it follows that
||\nabla W(q,p)||_{H^{1}}
is bounded by the
C^{2}
-norm of
W
and the
H^{1}
-norm of
Z
. Since we already have boundedness in
H^{1}
, we get a uniform bound on the
H^{2}
-norm of
Z
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Bounds on Floer curves

Let
\chi_{\rho}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth cut-off function such that
\chi_{\rho}(s)=1
when
s\leq\rho-1
and
\chi_{\rho}(s)=0
for

by

\displaystyle\bar{H}^{\rho}_{t}(Z)=\frac{1}{2}|Z^{\textup{odd}}|^{2}+\chi_{\rho}(|Z^{\textup{odd}}|)W_{t}(q,p).

(15)

Note that whenever

we have
\bar{H}^{\rho}_{t}(Z)=\frac{1}{2}|Z^{\textup{odd}}|^{2}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{0}(Z)
.

The following straightforward corollary of
lemma

3.1
gives the correspondence between Hamiltonian orbits of
H
and
\bar{H}^{\rho}
.

Corollary 3.3
.

For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
there exists
\rho>0
such that
H
and
\bar{H}^{\rho}
have the same Hamiltonian orbits of action less than

, meaning
\mathcal{P}_{a}(H)=\mathcal{P}_{a}(\bar{H}^{\rho})
.∎

In view of the discussion under

2.4
this finishes the proof of

2.3
. Since the aim of this article is not just to prove
theorem

2.3
, but also to establish a connection with hyperkähler Floer theory, we would like to include the
C^{0}
-bounds on Floer curves here as well. A Floer curve is defined as a negative gradient line of
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{H}^{\rho}}
with respect to the

-metric.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\widetilde{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3}\to B
be a Floer curve

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\widetilde{Z}+J_{\partial}\widetilde{Z}=\nabla\bar{H}^{\rho}_{t}(\widetilde{Z})

(16)

such that
\widetilde{Z}
converges to critical points of
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{H}^{\rho}}
for
s\to\pm\infty
. Then
|\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}(s,t)|\leq\rho
for any
(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
.

Proof.

Suppose
|\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}(s_{0},t_{0})|>\rho
for some
(s_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
. Then on a neighbourhood of
(s_{0},t_{0})
we have
\bar{H}^{\rho}_{t}(\widetilde{Z}(s,t))=\frac{1}{2}|\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}(s,t)|^{2}
, so

(\partial_{s}^{2}+\Delta)\widetilde{Z}=(\partial_{s}-J_{\partial})(\partial_{s}+J_{\partial})\widetilde{Z}=(\partial_{s}-J_{\partial})\nabla\bar{H}^{\rho}_{t}(\widetilde{Z})=(\partial_{s}-J_{\partial})\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}.

As
J_{\partial}
maps odd coordinates to even ones and vice versa, around the point
(s_{0},t_{0})
we get

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}^{2}+\Delta)\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}=\partial_{s}\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}.

(17)

Let
\phi=\frac{1}{2}|\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}|^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}^{2}+\Delta)\phi

\displaystyle=\partial_{s}\langle\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}},\partial_{s}\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}\rangle+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\langle\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}},\partial_{i}\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\partial_{s}\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}|^{2}+\sum_{i}|\partial_{i}\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}|^{2}+\langle\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}},(\partial_{s}^{2}+\Delta)\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\langle\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}},\partial_{s}\widetilde{Z}^{\textup{odd}}\rangle=\partial_{s}\phi.

Note that the first two terms on the second line are non-negative, so that the inequality on the next line follows from
equation

17
. We see that
(\partial_{s}^{2}+\Delta)\phi-\partial_{s}\phi\geq 0
, so that by the maximum principle
\phi
cannot attain a maximum on a neighbourhood of
(s_{0},x_{0})
. This contradicts the fact that
\widetilde{Z}
converges for
s\to\pm\infty
.
∎

4
Outlook

The connection between Euclidean field theory, hyperkähler Floer theory and Bridges’ equations outlined in this article is still in its first stages. In this section we want to outline some of the directions for ongoing research on this topic. We will not give proofs, but sketch some of the ideas involved and give references to their classical counterparts.

4.1
Towards an isomorphism with Morse homology

As described in this article, Floer-type methods can be used to provide information about solutions to Laplace’s equation. Morse-type methods are also suitable for constructing such a proof. In ongoing work we plan to define the appropriate generalization of hyperkähler Floer homology and to prove an isomorphism with the Morse homology involved. In this section, two possible methods of achieving this isomorphism will be roughly outlined. Both of them will also give extra motivation for the definition of the space
B
, used in the previous sections.

Adiabatic limits

The first method we want to outline is that of taking
adiabatic limits
. This method has been used in
[
SW06
]
to produce an isomorphism between the Floer homology of the cotangent bundle of some manifold
Q
with the Morse homology of the free loopspace
\Lambda Q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=C^{\infty}(S^{1},Q)

Start by defining the
\mathbb{Z}
-modules
C^{a}
to be freely generated by the elements of
\mathcal{P}_{a}(H)
. There are two boundary operators we can define on

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{s}q^{\alpha}-\Delta q^{\alpha}-\partial_{q^{\alpha}}V(q)&=0\\

where the

and

\Lambda^{3}Q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3},Q)

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\partial_{2}q^{\alpha}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\partial_{3}o_{23}^{\alpha}-p_{2}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\epsilon}\partial_{s}

\displaystyle p_{3}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\partial_{1}o_{31}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle=\nabla H(\widetilde{Z})

\displaystyle=Z

Here

(q,o_{23},o_{31},o_{12})
and

correspond to the same solution of the Laplace equation, in which case there is a unique Floer half-cylinder.
This would then mean that the chain homomorphism between the Morse and Floer complexes defined by counting these maps is lower-triangular and has only
\pm 1

For this proof to work, we need the projection

4.2
Bridges polysymplectic reduction

When studying Hamiltonian systems with symmetries, a standard approach is to quotient out those symmetries and work on the quotient manifold. For symplectic manifolds this procedure is standard: when a Lie group
G
acts on a symplectic manifold
\Sigma
, a
moment map

\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is defined, where
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of
G
. It can then be proven that
\mu^{-1}(0)/G
can be equipped again with a natural choice of symplectic form. Moreover, when the symplectic manifold was in fact Kähler, the quotient is too.

A similar construction can be made for field theories. Since the space
B
comes equipped with three symplectic forms, one can enhance the construction of the quotient for a single symplectic form to get the desired quotient. We define
\mu_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Sigma\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
to be the moment maps for
\omega^{J_{i}}
for
i=1,2,3
and combine them into
\mu=\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2}\times\mu_{3}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. As before, the set
\mu^{-1}(0)/G
inherits the three Kähler structures from
B
. This way we can link Euclidean field theories with symmetries to the compact target manifolds studied by
[
HNS09
,
GH12
,
GH13
]
.

Note that a notion of reduction also exists for more general polysymplectic systems like the one in
equation

4
(see
[
MRRSV15
]
). However, the construction is much more complicated, since the forms
\omega^{K_{i}}
are not symplectic themselves and therefore we can not just combine their moment maps. In
[
MRRSV15
]
it can be seen also that additional assumptions have to be made for the polysymplectic quotient to exist. As can be seen from the discussion above, Bridges’ formalism makes for a much more straightforward connection between the Euclidean field theories and compact quotients of the manifolds involved.
