Enhanced dissipation and Hörmander’s hypoellipticity
Dallas Albritton, Rajendra Beekie, Matthew Novack

Abstract
. We examine the phenomenon of enhanced dissipation from the perspective of Hörmander’s classical theory of second order hypoelliptic operators [ 31 ] . Consider a passive scalar in a shear flow, whose evolution is described by the advection–diffusion equation ∂ t f + b ⁡ ( y ) ​ ∂ x f − ν ​ Δ ​ f = 0 ​ on ​ 𝕋 × ( 0 , 1 ) × ℝ + \partial_{t}f+b(y)\partial_{x}f-\nu\Delta f=0\text{ on }\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}_{+} with periodic, Dirichlet, or Neumann conditions in y y . We demonstrate that decay is enhanced on the timescale T ∼ ν − ( N + 1 ) / ( N + 3 ) T\sim\nu^{-(N+1)/(N+3)} , where N − 1 N-1 is the maximal order of vanishing of the derivative b ′ ​ ( y ) b^{\prime}(y) of the shear profile and N = 0 N=0 for monotone shear flows. In the periodic setting, we recover the known timescale of Bedrossian and Coti Zelati [ 8 ] . Our results are new in the presence of boundaries.



1.
Introduction

The advection–diffusion equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla f-\nu\Delta f

\displaystyle=0

describes the evolution of a passive scalar
f
which is transported by a divergence-free velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}
and simultaneously diffuses. It is well known that the interplay between transport
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla
and weak diffusion
\nu\Delta
(
0<\nu\ll 1
) may cause solutions of (
1.1
) to decay more rapidly than by diffusion alone. This phenomenon is known as
enhanced dissipation
.

Our goal is to clarify the relationship between enhanced dissipation and Hörmander’s classical theory of hypoelliptic second order operators, developed in
[
31
]
. We restrict our attention to the class of
shear flows

\boldsymbol{u}=(b(y),0)
. Enhanced dissipation within this class has already been studied by Bedrossian and Coti Zelati in
[
8
]
. We will sharpen and extend their results, though perhaps the main interest of our work is in the method we employ, which is a new application of the old ideas of Hörmander.

We consider (
1.1
) on the spacetime domain
\Omega\times(0,+\infty)
, where
\Omega=\mathbb{T}\times D
and
D=\mathbb{T}
or
(0,1)
. By convention, we identify functions on the torus
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
with functions satisfying periodic boundary conditions on
(0,1)
. When
D=(0,1)
, we impose homogeneous Dirichlet or Neumann conditions on
\{y=0,1\}
. The shear profile
b\in C^{\infty}(\bar{D})
is assumed to have precisely
M\geq 0
critical points. When
M\geq 1
, we denote these by
y_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq M
. We further require that the maximal order of vanishing
N_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
at
y_{i}
is finite:

(1.2a)

\displaystyle b^{(k)}(y_{i})=0\text{ for all }1\leq k\leq N_{i}

(1.2b)

\displaystyle b^{(N_{i}+1)}(y_{i})\neq 0\,.

Let
N=\max_{1\leq i\leq M}N_{i}
, or
N=0
when
M=0
.

Since
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla=b(y)\partial_{x}
in (
1.1
), the
x
-averages

\langle f\rangle(y,t)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x,y,t)\,dx

satisfy the one-dimensional heat equation

\partial_{t}\langle f\rangle-\nu\partial_{yy}\langle f\rangle=0\,

and in general decay only on the diffusive timescale
\sim\nu^{-1}
. To extract an enhanced dissipation timescale, it is necessary to subtract
\langle f\rangle
. Without loss of generality, we thus require that the initial data
f_{\rm in}
satisfies

\langle f_{\rm in}\rangle(y):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{\rm in}(x,y)\,dx\equiv 0\,.

It is instructive to extract the dissipation timescale in the Couette example
b(y)=y
posed for
(x,y)\in(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\times\mathbb{R}
. In this case, (
1.1
) is explicitly solvable, as was already known to Kelvin in
[
35
]
. Upon taking the Fourier transform, we have

\partial_{t}\widehat{f}-k\partial_{\eta}\widehat{f}+\nu(|k|^{2}+|\eta|^{2})\widehat{f}=0\,.

The solution is

(1.3)

\widehat{f}(k,\eta,t)=e^{-\nu k^{2}t}e^{-\nu t(\eta^{2}+\eta kt+k^{2}t^{2}/3)}\widehat{f_{\rm in}}(k,\eta+kt)\,.

The factor
e^{-\nu|k|^{2}t^{3}/3}
dominates for large times and yields the enhancement timescale
T\sim\nu^{-1/3}
, since
|k|\gtrsim 1
when we disregard the
k=0
modes. The fundamental solution on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
was also famously computed by Kolmogorov
[
37
]
(see also Hörmander’s introduction
[
31
]
).

In
[
8
]
, Bedrossian and Coti Zelati identified the following enhanced dissipation timescales depending on the shear profile:

(1.4)

T\sim\nu^{-\frac{N+1}{N+3}}

in the periodic-in-
y
setting, up to a factor
(1+\log\nu^{-1})^{2}
which was removed by Wei
[
44
]
. The enhancement is measured in terms of the ‘flatness’ of the shear profile at its critical points. Since the Couette flow has no critical points, the above timescale for
N=0
matches the timescale extracted from (
1.3
). These timescales are essentially sharp, as demonstrated by Drivas and Coti Zelati in
[
18
]
. In the Neumann setting, Bedrossian and Coti Zelati obtain the same timescale except in the monotone case
N=0
, in which the timescale
T\sim\nu^{-1/2}
(up to a logarithmic correction) is obtained instead of
T\sim\nu^{-1/3}
. We demonstrate that the enhanced dissipation timescale is, at worst, (
1.4
) in the Dirichlet and Neumann settings for all
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

To better clarify the connections to hypoellipticity, we consider the
hypoelliptic
advection-diffusion equation proposed in
[
8
]
:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+b(y)\partial_{x}f-\nu\partial_{yy}f

\displaystyle=0.

The PDEs (
1.1
) and (
1.5
) are actually
equivalent
in the following sense: If
f
is a solution of (
1.5
) with initial data
f_{\rm in}
, then
g=e^{\nu t\partial_{xx}}f
is a solution of (
1.1
). In the sequel, we focus only on smooth solutions of (
1.5
): For each
f_{\rm in}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
satisfying the periodic, Dirichlet, or Neumann boundary conditions, there exists a unique smooth solution
f\in C([0,+\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega}\times(0,+\infty))
to (
1.5
) satisfying
f(\cdot,0)=f_{\rm in}
and the desired boundary conditions. Smoothness up to
t=0
would require additional compatibility conditions on
f_{\rm in}
.
We assume that
\langle f\rangle=\langle f_{\rm in}\rangle\equiv 0
.

Our main theorems are the following:

Theorem 1.1

(Enhanced dissipation)
.

There exist
d_{0},\nu_{0}>0
and
C>1
, depending only on
b
, such that the solution
f
of (
1.5
) with periodic, Dirichlet, or Neumann conditions satisfies

\left\|f(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f_{\rm in}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\exp\left(-d_{0}\nu^{\frac{N+1}{N+3}}t\right)

for all
t>0
and
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
.

Theorem 1.2

(Gevrey-in-
x
smoothing)
.

There exist
d_{0},\nu_{0}>0
depending only on
b
such that the solution
f
of (
1.5
) is instantly Gevrey-
p
regular for all
p>\frac{N+3}{2}
and
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
:

\left\lVert\exp\left(d_{0}\nu^{\frac{N+1}{N+3}}|\partial_{x}|^{\frac{1}{p}}t\right)f(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{b,p}\left\|f_{\rm in}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Remark 1.3

(Improvements)
.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
improve the main theorems of Bedrossian and Coti Zelati
[
8
]
and Wei
[
44
]
in the following ways:

•

We treat Dirichlet conditions in addition to periodic and Neumann conditions.

•

In the Neumann setting, we improve the timescale from
T\sim\nu^{-1/2}
in
[
8
]
to
T\sim\nu^{-1/3}
when
N=0
, and we remove the additional logarithmic factors when
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

In the periodic-in-
y
setting,
p=\frac{N+3}{2}
is also possible in Theorem
1.2
, as demonstrated by Wei
[
44
]
using resolvent estimates and a Gearhardt–Prüss-type theorem.

Remark 1.4

(Generalizations)
.

Our approach generalizes, with minimal effort, to divergence-form operators
\partial_{y}(a(y)\partial_{y}\cdot)
satisfying
a\in L^{\infty}(D)
and
a\geq c>0
. It is also possible to generalize to operators
\div(A(y)\nabla\cdot)
. The norms in the ‘interpolation inequality’, Proposition
4.5
, would need to be adjusted to incorporate
x
derivatives.
1
1

1

Roughly speaking, one should measure
\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|X_{0}f\|_{L^{2}_{t}\dot{H}^{-1}_{x,y}}
instead of
\|\partial_{y}f\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|X_{0}f\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}
.
Doing so would allow us to treat circular flows
\boldsymbol{u}=r\Omega(r)e_{\theta}
in an annulus
r_{0}<r<r_{1}
, where
\Omega
has finitely many critical points. It is also possible to generalize to shear flows which vary on the enhancement timescale
\nu^{-\frac{N+1}{N+3}}
, though the time translation symmetry is convenient in the proof of Proposition
4.5
. We view these generalizations as an indication that, in a certain sense, Hörmander’s method is robust.

The most naïve approach to Theorem
1.1
is to try to generalize the
L^{2}
energy estimate for the heat equation
\partial_{t}f=\nu\Delta f
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

(1.6)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\nu\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-C_{P}^{-1}\nu\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,,

where
C_{P}=4\pi^{2}
is the Poincaré constant and
f
has zero mean. This differential inequality implies
\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq e^{-\nu t/C_{P}}\|f_{\rm in}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. For the hypoelliptic PDE (
1.5
),
-\nu\|\partial_{y}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
appears on the right-hand side of the energy estimate, and we lack a Poincaré inequality for this quantity. The goal of hypoellipticity is precisely to extract differentiability-in-
x
and, thus, a Poincaré inequality, from the
L^{2}
energy estimate and the PDE. In this way, we salvage the naïve approach.

The desire to reproduce (
1.6
) is also a motivation for hypocoercivity. We compare the two approaches after we sketch the main idea.

In the classical terminology, a partial differential operator
P
with smooth coefficients on an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
hypoelliptic
if distributional solutions of
Pu=f
are smooth in
U
for
f\in C^{\infty}(U)
. Let
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{J}
be smooth vector fields on
U
.
In
[
31
]
, Hörmander characterized hypoellipticity for second order operators

L=\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{j}^{*}X_{j}+X_{0}\,.

L
is hypoelliptic
if and only if
the Lie algebra generated by
\{X_{j}\}_{j=0}^{J}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at every point
x_{0}\in U
. Perhaps the main observation in
[
31
]
is that regularity ‘along’ two vector fields
X
and
Y
implies regularity along their commutator
[X,Y]
and sum
X+Y
, up to certain controllable errors.

Hörmander’s theorem implies that solutions to (
1.5
) on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
are smooth, but it does not evidently imply Theorem
1.1
. Rather, we revisit Hörmander’s scheme (i) in a more quantitative manner, (ii) with boundary, and (iii) in the singular limit as
\nu\to 0^{+}
. On the other hand, our problem admits technical simplifications that, we hope, illuminate the original analysis in
[
31
]
.

Our work is partially inspired by the recent work of Armstrong and Mourrat
[
3
]
, who applied Hörmander’s methods in the context of the kinetic Fokker–Planck equation. Hörmander’s analysis was also also recently revisited by Bedrossian and coauthors in
[
7
,
9
]
in the context of stochastic differential equations.

Sketch

Let us provide an exposition of the main steps in our proof. Consider a smooth solution
f\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
to (
1.5
) with periodic or homogeneous Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions and smooth initial data
f_{\rm in}
satisfying
\langle f_{\rm in}\rangle\equiv 0
. Using the boundary conditions, the basic energy estimate is

\frac{1}{2}\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{y}f|^{2}\,dx\,dy\,ds=\frac{1}{2}\|f_{\rm in}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\forall t\geq 0\,.

This energy estimate furnishes two crucial pieces of information, which, to avoid more notation, we present here only for Dirichlet boundary conditions. Evidently,

(1.7)

\nu\|f\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}(H^{1}_{0})_{y}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\lesssim\|f_{\rm in}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,.

Then (
1.7
) and the PDE itself
X_{0}f:=(\partial_{t}+b(y)\partial_{x})f=\nu\partial_{yy}f
give

(1.8)

\nu^{-1}\|X_{0}f\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}H^{-1}_{y}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\lesssim\|f_{\rm in}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,.

As in
[
3
]
, we could define a norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1,\nu}_{\rm hyp}}
based on the quantities controlled in (
1.7
) and (
1.8
), though it is not strictly necessary.

H^{1,\nu}_{\rm hyp}
is actually equivalent to solving the PDE with RHS in
L^{2}_{t,x}H^{-1}_{y}

Hypoellipticity is subsequently extracted from

for
H^{1,\nu}_{\rm hyp}

(Subelliptic estimate and Poincaré inequality, Dirichlet case)

(1.9)

\nu^{\frac{N+1}{N+3}}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\nu^{\frac{N+1}{N+3}}\|f\|_{Q^{1/(N+3)}_{\partial_{x}}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\lesssim_{b}\nu\|f\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}H^{1}_{0,y}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\nu^{-1}\|X_{0}f\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}H^{-1}_{y}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\,,

where
\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
,
T=T_{0}\nu^{-\frac{N+1}{N+3}}
,
\nu\leq\nu_{0}
, and

The quantity

is a Besov-type norm measuring

-differentiability in the direction
\partial_{x}

With (

\nicefrac{{d}}{{dt}}\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-C_{0}\nu^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\|f(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

indicates that ‘nothing happens’ until the ‘crossover time’, which is proportional to
\nu^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}
. In light of this, the prefactor

in Theorem

and the scaling of

in Proposition

Sample Hörmander inequalities

To convey the main idea of the embedding (
1.9
), we demonstrate a simple embedding on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is an analogue of (
1.9
) for the PDE
y\partial_{x}f-\nu\partial_{yy}f=g
. To quantify the fractional differentiability, we define the following Besov-type seminorms based on finite differences:

\|f\|_{Q^{1}_{\partial_{y}}}:=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}h^{-1}\|f(x,y+h)-f(x,y)\|_{L^{2}}

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}:=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}h^{-1}\|f(x+\sgn(h)h^{2}y,y)-f(x,y)\|_{L^{2}}

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}:=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}h^{-1}\|f(x+h^{3},y)-f(x,y)\|_{L^{2}}\,.

We evidently have
\|f\|_{Q^{1}_{\partial_{y}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}
(see the characterization of Sobolev norms by finite differences in Evans
[
22
]
). The quantity
\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}
, which measures
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
derivative in the
y\partial_{x}
direction, will be controlled by the ‘interpolation inequality’ below. The analogous estimate for the heat equation is
f\in L^{2}_{t}H^{1}_{x}\cap H^{1}_{t}H^{-1}_{x}\subset H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
.

In the following, we require that the terms on the right-hand side of the inequalities (
1.10

1.12
) and (
1.15
) are
well defined and non-vanishing
(which is necessary in the optimization steps). Ultimately, we will demonstrate

Lemma 1.6

(Sample subelliptic estimate)
.

In the above notation,

(1.10)

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\ .

The explains, in part, the emergence of the powers of
\nu
in the main theorem. If we suppose
\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}\lesssim\nu^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
and
\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}\lesssim\nu^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, then we recover
\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\nu^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{6}}}
, analogous to the time-dependent subelliptic estimate (

by

in (

\|f\|_{Q^{1/(N+1)}_{\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}^{\frac{N+2}{N+3}}\|y^{N+1}\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}^{\frac{1}{N+3}}\,,

though we do not demonstrate this here (see Farkas and Lunardi

The subelliptic estimate (
1.10
) follows from combining the ‘bracket inequality’ and the ‘interpolation inequality’. The bracket inequality is based on the identity
e^{-Bt}e^{-At}e^{Bt}e^{At}=e^{t^{2}[A,B]+O(t^{3})}
for vector fields
A

Lemma 1.7

(Sample bracket inequality)
.

In the above notation,

(1.12)

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{Q^{1}_{\partial_{y}}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\,.

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathbb{R}
. If we demonstrate

(1.13)

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{Q^{1}_{\partial_{y}}}+\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}\,,

then we consider
f_{\lambda}(x,y)=f(\lambda x,y)
and optimize
\lambda>0
to complete the proof.
We write

(1.14)

\displaystyle f(x+h^{3},y)-f(x,y)

\displaystyle=f(x+h^{3},y)-f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y)

\displaystyle+f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y)-f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y+h)

\displaystyle+f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y+h)-f(x,y+h)

\displaystyle+f(x,y+h)-f(x,y)\,.

First, we treat the terms based on ‘flowing’ in the direction
\mp\partial_{y}
:

\|f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y)-f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y+h)\|_{L^{2}}=\|f(x,y+h)-f(x,y)\|\lesssim h\|f\|_{Q^{1}_{\partial_{y}}}\,.

Second, we treat the terms based on ‘flowing’ in the direction
\mp y\partial_{x}
:

\|f(x+h^{3},y)-f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y)\|_{L^{2}}=\|f(x+(y+h)h^{2},y+h)-f(x,y+h)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim h\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\,.

Combining the estimates completes the proof of (
1.13
).

The interpolation inequality is more difficult (see Section 5 in Hörmander’s paper

Lemma 1.8

(Example interpolation inequality)
.

In the above notation,

(1.15)

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,.

The main difficulty in (
1.15
) can be seen in the following way. Let
X_{0}=y\partial_{x}
and
e^{h^{2}X_{0}}f=f(x+h^{2}y,y)
. To achieve
\|e^{h^{2}X_{0}}f-f\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim h^{2}
, one differentiates the left-hand side in
h
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dh}\|e^{h^{2}X_{0}}f-f\|_{2}^{2}=4h\langle e^{h^{2}X_{0}}f-f,X_{0}f\rangle\,,

which is estimated by duality:
X_{0}f\in L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}
and
e^{h^{2}X_{0}}f-f\in L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}
. However,
\|\partial_{y}f\|_{L^{2}}
is not generally preserved under the flow of
X_{0}
, as we must commute
\partial_{y}
with
e^{h^{2}X_{0}}
. The chain rule gives
\partial_{y}e^{h^{2}X_{0}}f=h^{2}\partial_{x}e^{h^{2}X_{0}}f+e^{h^{2}X_{0}}\partial_{y}f
, and the situation looks bleak because
\partial_{x}f
is uncontrolled in
L^{2}
. Hörmander’s method involves the above computation for a smooth function
S_{\ell}f
, obtained by mollifying
f
in the direction
[Y,X_{0}]=\partial_{x}
at scale
\ell>0
. The additional mollification makes it possible to exploit the small prefactor
h^{2}
. The complete proof is contained in Appendix
A
.

Further difficulties

Foremost is the Taylor remainder term, which vanished in the calculation (
1.14
). In our setting, the left-hand side of the analogous bracket calculation contains
f(x+(b(y+h)-b(y))h^{2},y)
rather than
f(x+b^{\prime}(y)h^{3},y)
. This error is estimated in terms of further commutators. That is, to control
\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}
derivative in the direction
b^{\prime}(y)\partial_{x}
, we already require control of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
derivative in the direction
b^{\prime\prime}(y)\partial_{x}
,
\nicefrac{{1}}{{5}}
derivative in the direction
b^{(3)}\partial_{x}
, etc. Eventually, these errors can be absorbed by applying the trick
[Y,X_{0}]=[\varepsilon^{-1}Y,\varepsilon X_{0}]
to introduce small constants.

The interpolation inequality also exhibits a ‘cascading effect’ and requires mollification along the vector fields
[Y,X_{0}]
,
[Y,[Y,X_{0}]]
, etc. Finally, the boundaries in
y
when
D=(0,1)
and in the time
t
restrict the ‘admissible finite differences’

Comparison with hypocoercivity

A commonly used method to prove enhanced dissipation
[
8
]
is
hypocoercivity
, whose mathematical framework was developed by Villani in
[
43
]
. Hypocoercivity has roots in kinetic theory
4
4

4

See also the weighted energy method of Guo in
[
27
]
.
and was later adapted to PDEs of fluid dynamics in
[
24
,
6
]
. Its premise is to design a new energy functional
\Phi
which satisfies the differential inequality
\dot{\Phi}\leq-C_{0}\Phi
. Commonly,
\Phi
is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{x,y}}^{2}
and is ‘augmented’ by certain commutators; for example,

\Phi[f]:=\|f\|_{2}^{2}+a\nu^{2/3}\|\partial_{y}f\|_{2}^{2}+2b\nu^{1/3}\langle\partial_{x}f,\partial_{y}f\rangle+c\|\partial_{x}f\|_{2}^{2}

in the Couette case with suitable weights

. We should interpret
\partial_{x}
as the commutator
[\partial_{y},y\partial_{x}]
above. Perhaps the main advantage of hypocoercivity is that it is elementary, consisting only of well-chosen energy estimates and integration by parts.

Hypocoercivity naturally lives at higher regularity
\approx L^{\infty}_{t}H^{1}_{x,y}
than Hörmander’s method, which lives at the level of the basic energy estimate
\approx L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x,y}\cap L^{2}_{t,x}H^{1}_{y}
. As a consequence, hypocoercive methods encounter two technical difficulties that are absent from our approach:

•

L^{2}
stability estimates require further arguments that exploit the smoothing. In
[
24
,
8
]
, this is addressed by passing through the resolvent formalism: The hypocoercivity method estimates the pseudospectrum and, therefore, the semigroup. It is this step which contributes the logarithmic correction, which can be removed by the Gearhardt–Prüss-type theorem of Wei in
[
44
]
.
5
5

5

Notice, however, that resolvent-based methods may not generalize easily to unsteady flows. Our approach can be easily adapted to shear flows that vary on the timescale
O(\nu^{-(N+1)/(N+3)})
.
In the monotone shear case, one can use the smoothing more directly
[
15
]
. A different choice, popular in nonlinear problems, is simply to consider stability in more regular spaces
[
6
]
.

•

Hypocoercivity may not always generalize well to boundaries, which is why
[
8
]
does not achieve the optimal timescales in the Neumann case.

Finally, we mention that hypocoercivity’s main purpose is not to capture hypoelliptic regularization, although in the shear flow case it can be recovered by studying the equation mode-by-mode in
x
; see (2.6) in
[
8
]
for a definition of the corresponding functional
\Phi_{k}
. In our method, the enhanced dissipation is essentially a

of the hypoelliptic regularization, in the sense that the subelliptic estimate (

) controls a norm measuring fractional regularity in

using the energy estimates (
1.7

1.8
) and leads directly to the timescale (

Appendix A
Proof of sample interpolation inequality

Proof.

It suffices to prove the (apparently) weaker inequality

(A.1)

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}+\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}

for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}
. After rescaling in
y
, we have

\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\lambda\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}+\lambda^{-1}\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}.

Optimizing
\lambda
gives the full inequality. The optimization step is possible because, by assumption, each term on the RHS of (
A.1
) is non-zero.

For
\ell>0
, consider the mollification-in-
x
at scale
\ell^{3}
:

S_{\ell}f(x,y):=\ell^{-3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x-s,y)\varphi(\ell^{-3}s)\,ds.

We will require the properties

(A.2)

\|S_{\ell}f-f\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\ell\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}

and

(A.3)

\|\partial_{x}S_{\ell}f\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\ell^{-2}\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}.

Let
h\in\mathbb{R}
. We expand

\displaystyle\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}f-f\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}f-e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{\ell}f\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{\ell}f-S_{\ell}f\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|S_{\ell}f-f\|_{L^{2}}\,.

The first and third terms on the right-hand side are estimated in the same way, namely, by the property (
A.2
) of the mollification operator.
We focus on the second term. We have

\frac{d}{dh}\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{\ell}f-S_{\ell}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=4h\langle e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}f,e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}y\partial_{x}S_{h}f\rangle=4h\langle e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}^{*}S_{h}f,y\partial_{x}f\rangle\,,

which is estimated by
\dot{H}^{1}/\dot{H}^{-1}
duality:

h|\langle e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}^{*}S_{h}f,y\partial_{x}f\rangle|\lesssim h\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}^{*}S_{h}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}\,.

It remains to estimate
h\|\partial_{y}e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}^{*}S_{h}f\|_{L^{2}}
. We have

h\partial_{y}e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}^{*}S_{h}f=h^{3}\partial_{x}e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}^{*}S_{h}f+he^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}\partial_{y}S^{*}_{h}S_{h}f-h\partial_{y}S^{*}_{h}S_{h}f\,.

The second and third terms are estimated in
L^{2}
by
h\|\partial_{y}f\|_{L^{2}}
. For the 1st term, we allow
\partial_{x}
to hit the mollifier and use the property (
A.3
):

h^{3}\|\partial_{x}e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{h}^{*}S_{h}f\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim h^{3}\ell^{-2}\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}\lesssim\ell\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}

for
h\leq\ell
.
This gives

\left|\frac{d}{dh}\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{\ell}f-S_{\ell}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right|\lesssim(h\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}+\ell\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}})\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}\,.

In particular, for
h=\ell
, we have

\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}S_{\ell}f-S_{\ell}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim h^{2}(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}+\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}})\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}\,.

Combining all estimates with
h=\ell
, we have

\|e^{h^{2}y\partial_{x}}f-f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim h^{2}\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}^{2}+h^{2}(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}+\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}})\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}

or

\displaystyle\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}}^{2}+(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}+\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}_{\partial_{x}}})\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{Q^{1}_{\partial_{y}}}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}^{\nicefrac{{4}}{{3}}}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{1}_{y}}\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}+\|f\|_{Q^{1}_{\partial_{y}}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\|f\|_{Q^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{y\partial_{x}}}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\|y\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}_{x}\dot{H}^{-1}_{y}}\,,

Then Young’s inequality produces (
A.1
).
∎
