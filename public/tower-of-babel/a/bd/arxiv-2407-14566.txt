Generalization Error Analysis of Deep Backward Dynamic Programming for Solving Nonlinear PDEs
Du Ouyang, Jichang Xiao, Xiaoqun Wang

Abstract
We explore the application of the quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) method in deep backward dynamic programming (DBDP) [ 1 ] for numerically solving high-dimensional nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs). Our study focuses on examining the generalization error as a component of the total error in the DBDP framework, discovering that the rate of convergence for the generalization error is influenced by the choice of sampling methods. Specifically, for a given batch size m m , the generalization error under QMC methods exhibits a convergence rate of O ⁡ ( m − 1 + ε ) O(m^{-1+\varepsilon}) , where ε \varepsilon can be made arbitrarily small. This rate is notably more favorable than that of the traditional Monte Carlo (MC) methods, which is O ( m − 1 / 2 + ε ) O(m^{-1/2+\varepsilon}) . Our theoretical analysis shows that the generalization error under QMC methods achieves a higher order of convergence than their MC counterparts. Numerical experiments demonstrate that QMC indeed surpasses MC in delivering solutions that are both more precise and stable.

where

\mathscr{A}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(\Sigma\Sigma^{\top})_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}M_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

The objective of this paper is to obtain the solution of high-dimensional PDE (

\mathcal{X}_{t}=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}M(s,\mathcal{X}_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma(s,\mathcal{X}_{s})\mathrm{d}W_{s},\;\;\;0\leq t\leq T,

The greatest advantage of the Feynman-Kac formula is its ability to transform the solution of PDEs into the solution of BSDEs. This is beneficial because general methods for solving PDEs, such as finite element methods, depend on the partition of the state space and are subject to the “curse of dimensionality”. The schemes in
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
, using the time partition and regression, overcome the issue of dimensionality. These schemes can be applied up to dimension 6 or 7. However, the efficacy of the regression methods is constrained by the choice of the basis functions (e.g., polynomial functions). In cases of higher dimensions (e.g., 50), the sheer volume of necessary basis functions renders these approaches highly inefficient.

To address this issue, the pioneering deep BSDE method
[
13
,
14
]
applies deep neural networks to efficiently solve high-dimensional BSDEs (nonlinear PDEs). This approach utilizes the required initial values as parameters and employs feedforward neural networks to approximate each time step to solve a global optimization problem. In contrast, the Deep Backward Dynamic Programming (DBDP)
[
1
]
simultaneously estimates the solution and its gradient through the minimization of sequential loss functions via backward induction, showing superior stability and accuracy compared to
[
13
]
, as evidenced by several test cases. Based on
[
1
]
, Germain et al.
[
15
]
proposed a multistep version of DBDP. The deep splitting (DS) method
[
16
]
estimates the PDE solution via backward explicit local optimization problems, leveraging a neural network regression method to calculate conditional expectations.

In the aforementioned methods, the loss function is typically represented by an expectation of the states, which cannot be precisely calculated. To address this issue, empirical risk minimization (ERM) principle aims to apply the Monte Carlo (MC) method to sample states and using the sample mean (also called empirical risk) to estimate the loss function. Then the problem becomes finding a parameter that minimizes the empirical risk. Due to the ERM, the total error of the algorithm will be influenced by the generalization error (see Section
3
). The generalization error (also called stochastic error in
[
17
,
18
]
), which correlates with the sampling method and sample size, signifies the capacity of ERM. As a component of the total error, it affects the performance of algorithm during the learning phase. Therefore, studying the convergence order of the generalization error, which is not involved in
[
16
,
15
,
1
]
, is also important. Other research concerning the generalization error can be found in
[
19
,
20
,
4
,
21
,
17
,
18
,
22
]
.

The quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) method is an efficient numerical method widely used in financial engineering (see
[
23
,
24
]
). For
m
quadrature points, the QMC method has a convergence rate
O(m^{-1+\varepsilon})
with an arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
, which is asymptotically faster than the MC rate
O(m^{-1/2})
. The applications of QMC methods in machine learning have also made some progress. Dick and Feischl
[
25
]
applied QMC methods to reduce the computational effort required for data compression in machine learning. Liu and Owen
[
26
]
demonstrated that randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (RQMC) methods combined with stochastic quasi-Newton methods can significantly accelerate the optimization. Longo et al.
[
27
]
and Mishra et al.
[
28

utilized QMC methods to generate training data for training neural networks. Their results indicate that the QMC-based deep learning algorithm is more efficient than the MC-based one in approximating data-to-observables maps. Thus it is natural to ask whether the QMC methods can achieve higher efficiency compared to MC in solving nonlinear PDEs. Xiao et al
[
29
]
studied the efficiency of RQMC methods for solving linear Kolmogorov PDEs by using deep learning. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no related research for the nonlinear PDEs cases.

To this end, we decompose the total error of the DBDP method for solving nonlinear PDEs into three parts: the first part is the scheme error, which relates to the time partition; the second part is the approximation error, related to the structure of the network; and the third part is the generalization error (ignored by
[
1
]
), which is related to the sampling method and batch size. We study the convergence rate of the generalization error under MC and QMC frameworks. Our theoretical findings demonstrate that under QMC, the convergence rate of generalization error is
O(m^{-1+\varepsilon})
with
\varepsilon>0
being arbitrarily small, which is better than the MC case
O(m^{-1/2+\varepsilon})
. The efficacy of the QMC method is validated through a series of experiments. The numerical results reveal that the QMC method yields training outcomes with significantly reduced relative error in comparison to the MC method when evaluated against the analytical solution. Moreover, the results obtained by the QMC method exhibit enhanced stability, characterized by a notably lower variance.

4
The analysis of generalization error

4.1
Problem specification

We consider two basic types of nonlinear parabolic partial PDEs: the nonlinear heat PDEs and nonlinear Black-Scholes PDEs. For heat equations, the SDE is

\mathcal{X}_{t}=\eta+\overline{\mu}t+\overline{\sigma}W_{t},\;\;\;0\leq t\leq T,

(18)

where
\overline{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\overline{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\mathcal{X}_{t}=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{X}_{s})\overline{\mu}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{X}_{s})\overline{\sigma}\mathrm{d}W_{s},\;\;\;0\leq t\leq T,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{t,i}=\eta^{(i)}\exp\left(\left(\mu_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sigma_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)t+\left<\sigma_{i},W_{t}\right>\right),\ \ 1\leq i\leq d,

(20)

where
\mathcal{X}_{t,i}
,
\mu_{i}
and

i
-th components of

,
\overline{\mu}

-th row of

\overline{\sigma}=\sigma\bm{I}_{d}

,
\bm{1}_{d}
is the d-dimensional vector where all elements equal 1 and

4.2
Generalization error for Monte Carlo methods

As shown in Section

, the total error is bounded by the sum of three parts, namely the scheme error, the approximation error and the generalization error. In this section, we study the generalization error under the framework of MC. For simplicity, we drop the subscript
i
in (

Assume that there exists the optimal parameter
\theta^{*}
such that

\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta).

Since the expectation is usually intractable, we apply the ERM method to estimate
\theta^{*}
by the minimizer

of the empircal risk

\mathcal{L}^{(m)}\left(\theta\right)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}G(\bm{w}_{i};\theta)

(29)

where

are random samples satisfying
\bm{w}_{i}\sim W
.

We are interested in the generalization error defined by (see Theorem
1
and Corollary

\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*}).

The next lemma establishes the upper bound of the generalization error.

Lemma 3
.

For every

, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta)\right|

Proof.

The lower bound follows by
\theta^{*}=\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
. For the upper bounds, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{(m)})+\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{*})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{(m)})+\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta)\right|,

where the second inequality follows by
\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{(m)})\leq\mathcal{L}^{(m)}(\theta^{*})
.
∎

4.2.1
The convergence rate of mean error for MC

Under the MC framework,
(\bm{w}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
in (

, the mean generalization error satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}G(\bm{w}_{i};\theta)-\mathbb{E}\left[G(W;\theta)\right]\right|\right],

(30)

where
W
is a standard Gaussian random variable. As a result, it suffices to obtain the upper bound of the right hand side of (

Definition 2

(Rademacher complexity)
.

Suppose that
(\bm{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and take values in a metric space
S
. Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a class of real-valued borel-measurable function over
(S,\mathcal{B}(S))
(
\mathcal{B}(S)
is the Borel
\sigma

\mathcal{G}
with respect to the random sample
(\bm{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
is defined by

R_{m}(\mathcal{\mathcal{G}};(\bm{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{m})=\mathbb{E}\left[{\underset{g\in\mathcal{G}}{\mathrm{sup}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}g(\bm{x}_{i})}\right],

where
\left\{\xi_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. Rademacher variables with discrete distribution
\mathbb{P}\{\xi_{i}=1\}=\mathbb{P}\{\xi_{i}=-1\}=1/2
.

The next lemma provides an upper bound for the right hand side of (
30

[
47
, Lemma 26.2]
and
[
48
, Theorem 3.3]
for a detailed proof.

Lemma 4
.

Under the setting of Definition
2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\underset{g\in\mathcal{G}}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g(\bm{x}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{x})]\right|}\right]\leq 2R_{m}(\mathcal{\mathcal{G}};(\bm{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}).

The Rademacher complexity has the following upper bound.

Lemma 5
.

Under the setting of Definition
2
, suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a parameterized class of functions
\left\{\mathcal{F}(\cdot;\theta):\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\theta\in\Theta\right\}
, where

is the parametric space satisfying

and

\displaystyle\underset{x\in S}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\mathcal{F}(x;\theta_{1})-\mathcal{F}(x;\theta_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B_{1}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\underset{x\in S}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\mathcal{F}(x;\theta_{1})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B_{2}.

Then we have

\displaystyle R_{m}(\mathcal{G};(x_{i})_{i=1}^{m})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{6\sqrt{p}B_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\sqrt{\log\left(2B_{1}R\sqrt{m}\right)}.

For a detailed proof of Lemma

, we refer to
[
17

Theorem 2

In the cases of nonlinear heat PDEs and nonlinear Black-Sholes PDEs, suppose that we use the MC method to obtain the samples

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\right]=Cm^{-1/2}\log(m),

where

(R,\|\mathcal{S}_{1}\|_{\infty},D(\mathcal{S}_{1}),C_{fg})

Proof.

For every

be the truncated version of

satisfies the conditions of Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\theta^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\right]\leq

\displaystyle 2\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{1}_{\{\|W\|_{\infty}>K\}}G(W;\theta)\right]\right|

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\bm{1}_{\{\|W\|_{\infty}>K\}}G(W;\theta)\right|\right]+4R_{m}(\mathcal{G}^{(K)};(\bm{w}_{i})_{i=1}^{m})

\displaystyle 4\mathbb{E}\left[\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\mathrm{sup}}\left|\bm{1}_{\{\|W\|_{\infty}>K\}}G(W;\theta)\right|\right]+4R_{m}(\mathcal{G}^{(K)};(\bm{w}_{i})_{i=1}^{m}).

(31)

\displaystyle\left|G(W;\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{fg}d^{2}B^{2}_{\mathcal{S}_{1},R}\exp\{C_{2}\|W\|_{\infty}\},

4.3
Generalization error for quasi-Monte Carlo

In this section, we consider the QMC method and study the corresponding generalization error rate.

4.3.1
Quasi-Monte Carlo methods

QMC
[
50
]
is an efficient quadrature method to numerically solve the integral problems on the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
. While the MC method, relying on random sampling, achieves a convergence rate of
O(m^{-1/2})
with
m
quadrature points, QMC leverages low discrepancy sequences, such as the Halton sequence, Faure sequence and Sobol’ sequence
[
51
,
50
,
52
]
, which facilitates a faster convergence rate of
O(m^{-1+\varepsilon})
for an arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
. This enhanced convergence rate of QMC is grounded in the Koksma-Hlawka inequality
[
53
]
, linking the error to both the variation of the integrand in the sense of Hardy and Krause and the uniformity of sample points as measured by star discrepancy. More precisely, the Koksma-Hlawka inequality states that

\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f(\bm{y}_{i})-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\right|\leq V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)D_{m}^{*}(\{\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{m}\}),

(43)

where
V_{\mathrm{HK}}(f)
is the variation of
f
in the sense of Hardy and Krause and
D_{m}^{*}
is the star discrepancy of point set
\{\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{m}\}
with the magnitude
O(m^{-1}(\log m)^{d-1})
for low discrepancy point sets (see
[
50
]
for more details). The convergence rate of QMC is validated if the function
f
is of bounded variation in the sense of Hardy and Krause (BVHK).

The randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (RQMC) method, which includes random shifts
[
54
]
and scrambling
[
55
]
, merges the benefits of randomization with low discrepancy sequences to obtain unbiased estimations. If the integrand is bounded and smooth, the nested scrambled method can achieve a faster convergence rate of
O(n^{-3/2+\varepsilon})
, as detailed by
[
56
]
. Recently, Ouyang et al.
[
57
]
have demonstrated that this high convergence rate can also be maintained for unbounded and smooth integrands by employing appropriate importance sampling methods.

4.3.2
The convergence rate of mean error for QMC

Combining QMC methods, the empirical risk is defined by

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{(m)}(\theta):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}G(\Phi^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i});\theta),

(44)

where
\{\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{m}\}
is a low discrepancy point set and
\Phi^{-1}
is the inverse cumulative distribution function of standard Gaussian distribution, acting on each component of
\bm{y}_{i}
. In this paper, we consider the RQMC method with the scrambling approach (see Owen
[
55
]
for more details). Define

\widehat{\theta}^{(m)}:=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{(m)}(\theta).

(45)

By Lemma
3
, the mean generalization error satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big|\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{m}(\theta)\Big|\right]

(46)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[G(W;\theta)\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}G(\Phi^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i});\theta)\Big|\right].

Since
G(\Phi^{-1}(\cdot);\theta)
is an unbounded function (it can not be of BVHK), the Koksma-Hlawka inequality can not be directly used to provide a convergence rate for the right hand side of (
46
). To address this issue, Xiao et al.
[
29
]
applied the results in Owen
[
58
]
and obtained the convergence rate for the case of linear Kolmogorov PDEs. Utilizing Owen
[
58
]
method would become quite cumbersome for our case, given that the function
G
is more complex than those in Xiao et al
[
29
]
. Here, we recommend employing the results in Ouyang et al.
[
57
]
, where we only need to examine the growth conditions of
G
. We introduce the following function class from
[
57
]
.

Definition 3
.

For
h:\mathbb{R}^{3d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, define the exponential growth function class as

\mathcal{G}_{e}(A,B):=\left\{h\in\mathcal{S}^{3d}(\mathbb{R}^{3d}):\sup_{\bm{u}\subset 1:3d}|\partial^{\bm{u}}h(\bm{x})|\leq Be^{A|\bm{x}|}\right\},

where
\bm{u}\subset 1:3d=\left\{1,\dots,3d\right\}
and
\partial^{\bm{u}}:=\prod_{i\in\bm{u}}\partial/\partial x_{i}
,
\mathcal{S}^{3d}(\mathbb{R}^{3d})
is the function class where the functions have continuous mixed partial derivatives of order
1
.

The following lemma can be obtained from the proof of Corollary 4.10 in Ouyang et al.
[
57
]
, which gives the convergence rate of QMC methods regarding the function class
\mathcal{G}_{e}(A,B)
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\left\{\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{m}\right\}
be a RQMC point set such that each
\bm{y}_{j}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{3d}
and

D^{*}_{m}(\left\{\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{m}\right\})\leq C\frac{(\log m)^{d-1}}{m}\quad a.s.,

(47)

where
D^{*}_{m}
is the star discrepancy and
C
is a constant independent of
m
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{G}_{e}(A,B)}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}h(\Phi^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i}))-\mathbb{E}\left[h(W)\right]\Big|\right]=O(m^{-1+\varepsilon}),

(48)

where
W
is a standard
3d
-dimensional Gaussian random variable.

Noting that the nested scrambled Sobol’ point set satisfies the conditions in Lemma
6
, if we use this QMC point set
\{\bm{y}_{i}\}
in (
44
), then by Lemma
6
and (
46
), it suffices to verify that the function
G(\cdot;\theta)
is in the class
\mathcal{G}_{e}(A,B)
for some constant
A,B
. The main result of this section is the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

If we use nested scrambled Sobol’ point set in (
44
), then for the nonlinear heat PDEs and the nonlinear Black-Scholes PDEs, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\right]=O(m^{-1+\varepsilon})

(49)

for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

By the same method used in Lemma
3
, we have

0\leq\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\leq 2\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left|\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{m}(\theta)\right|.

(50)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big|\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{m}(\theta)\Big|\right]

(51)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[G(W;\theta)\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}G(\Phi^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i});\theta)\Big|\right].

We consider the growth of
G(\cdot;\theta)
.
Note that
G(\cdot;\theta)
is composed through addition, multiplication and composition of some base functions (such as quadratic functions, linear functions, activation functions), where the derivatives of any order of these functions are dominated by a polynomial. By the results in Appendix of Ouyang et al.
[
57
]
, there are constants
A,B
, such that for any
\theta\in\Theta
,

G(\cdot;\theta)\in\mathcal{G}_{e}(A,B).

(52)

Note that the nested scrambled Sobol’ point set satisfies the conditions in Lemma
6
. By Lemma
6
and combining (
51
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\theta}^{(m)})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[G(W;\theta)\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}G(\Phi^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i});\theta)\Big|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{G}_{e}(A,B)}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}h(\Phi^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i}))-\mathbb{E}\left[h(W)\right]\Big|\right]=O(m^{-1+\varepsilon}).

This proves the desired result.
∎

Theorem
2
and
4
establish the convergence rates of the generalization error under the MC and QMC methods. The results that the QMC method possesses a higher convergence rate
O(m^{-1+\varepsilon})
, indicating that, with sufficiently thorough training (achieving optimal parameters for each), the QMC method can achieve a smaller generalization error compared to the MC method. According to Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
(which deals with the decomposition of the total error), the QMC method potentially enables the final trained solution to have a smaller error. In the next section, we validate our theoretical results with several numerical examples.

6
Conclusion

Nonlinear PDEs (
1
) represent a general form of the linear Kolmogorov PDEs
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
, related to BSDEs by nonlinear Feymann-Kac formula
[
9
]
, which find extensive applications in financial mathematics
[
7
]
. Therefore, efficiently solving nonlinear PDEs constitutes a significant challenge.
We investigated the convergence orders of the generalization error within the DBDP
[
1
]
method under both the MC and QMC approaches. Our theoretical findings indicate that the generalization error under the QMC method exhibits a higher convergence order compared to the MC method. Theoretically, this suggests that the use of the QMC method for sampling during training yields superior outcomes. We validated these theoretical insights through several numerical experiments. The results obtained using the QMC method have smaller errors and improved stability. It is an intriguing prospect to explore the trade-off among scheme error, approximation error, and generalization error to achieve an optimal total error with reduced computational costs. This is left for future work.

]
