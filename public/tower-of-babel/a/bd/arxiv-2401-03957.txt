Genuinely sharp estimates for heat kernels on Weyl chambers
Krzysztof Stempak

Abstract
. We consider heat kernels on Weyl chambers corresponding to Laplacians subject to mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary conditions imposed on the boundary. Using purely analytic tools we prove genuinely sharp two-sided global estimates in the frameworks of orthogonal root systems and the dihedral root systems I 2 ​ ( 3 ) I_{2}(3) and I 2 ​ ( 4 ) I_{2}(4) .

1.
Introduction and statement of results

Heat kernel estimates attracted wide attention as useful tools of great importance in analysis. In the context of Euclidean domains, with the Dirichlet or
Neumann Laplacians as self-adjoint operators, a variety of settings were considered, including bounded and unbounded domains, domains with smoothness
regularity conditions (of different degrees) imposed on their boundaries, etc.

To give a flavor of such estimates we mention that for any convex domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Neumann heat kernel
p_{U}^{N}(t,x,y)
admits qualitatively
sharp global upper and lower
Gaussian bounds
of the type

p_{U}^{N}(t,x,y)\simeq\simeq\frac{1}{|B(x,\sqrt{t})\cap U|}\exp(-c\|x-y\|^{2}/t),\qquad x,y\in U,\quad t>0,

(1.1)

(see e.g.
[
4
, p. 4]
), where
B(x,r)
stands for the Euclidean ball of center
x
and radius
r
; see the end of this section, where the meaning
of the symbols “
\simeq\simeq
” and “
\simeq
” is explained. Similar estimates in more general
inner uniform domains
are also known;
see
[
4
, Section 3]
or
[
9
, Theorem 5.4]
.

For the Dirichlet heat kernel
p_{U}^{D}(t,x,y)
it was proved by Gyrya and Saloff-Coste, see
[
4
, Section 5]
or
[
9
, Theorem 5.7]
, that for an
unbounded
inner uniform domain

U
(cf.
[
4
, Section 3]
for the definition) it holds

p_{U}^{D}(t,x,y)\simeq\simeq\frac{h(x)h(y)}{\sqrt{V_{h^{2}}(x,\sqrt{t})V_{h^{2}}(y,\sqrt{t})}}\exp(-cd_{U}(x,y)^{2}/t),\qquad x,y\in U,\quad t>0;

(1.2)

here
d_{U}(x,y)
stands for the
inner distance
in
U
between
x
and
y
obtained by minimizing the length of curves joining
x
and
y
in
U

(thus, for convex
U
,
d_{U}(x,y)=\|x-y\|
),
h
is the
harmonic profile
of
U
(called also the
réduite
of
U
, see

[
9
, Definition 5.5]
or
[
4
, Section 4.1]
), and
V_{h^{2}}(x,r)=\int_{\{y\in U\colon d_{U}(x,y)<r\}}h^{2}
.

The aim of this paper is to discuss global upper and lower bounds of heat kernels on Weyl chambers. For a chamber
C_{+}
, related to a root system
R
in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
W=W(R)
as the corresponding Weyl group, these kernels, denoted
\{p_{t}^{\eta,C_{+}}\}_{t>0}
, are associated to nonnegative self-adjoint
realizations
-\Delta^{+}_{\eta}
in
L^{2}(C_{+})
of the Laplacian on
C_{+}
, subject to mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary conditions imposed on the
facets of
C_{+}
. These conditions are determined by a homomorphism
\eta\in{\rm Hom}(W,\,\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
, where
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}=\{1,-1\}

with multiplication, and
\eta=

det
and
\eta\equiv 1
(the latter denoted in the sequel by
triv

root systems in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and in the context of the dihedral root systems
I_{2}(3)
and
I_{2}(4)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Let

p_{t}^{(d)}(w)=(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\exp\big(-\|w\|^{2}/4t\big),\qquad w\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

be the
d

In the case of the orthogonal root system
R_{k}:=\{\pm e_{d-k+1},\ldots,\pm e_{d}\}

\eta\in{\rm Hom}(W(R_{k}),\,\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}

J_{\eta}=\{1\leq j\leq k\colon\eta(j)=1\}

Proposition 1.1

Let

p_{t}^{\eta,\mathbb{R}_{+,k}}(x,y)\simeq p_{t}^{(d)}(x-y)\prod_{j\in d-k+J_{\eta}}\frac{x_{j}y_{j}}{x_{j}y_{j}+t}.

(1.3)

The proof of Proposition

(1.4)

2.
Preliminaries

In this short section we collect all notions which are necessary for further reading. For details the reader can consult
[
12
]
and the references therein.

The notion of finite reflection group (
reflection group
for short) is based on the concept of a root system, a remarkably deep concept, with many ramifications in algebra and analysis. A
root system
is a finite set
R
of nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, called

roots
, such that
s_{\alpha}(R)=R
for every
\alpha\in R
, where

s_{\alpha}(x)=x-\frac{2\langle\alpha,x\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

is the orthogonal reflection in
\langle\alpha\rangle^{\bot}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle x,\alpha\rangle=0\}
, the hyperplane orthogonal to
\alpha
. Clearly,

\langle-\alpha\rangle^{\bot}=\langle\alpha\rangle^{\bot}
, and to avoid further repetitions of these hyperplanes it is additionally assumed that

R\cap\mathbb{R}\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}
. The
reflection group associated with

R
is the subgroup of
O(d)
generated by the reflections
s_{\alpha}
,

W=W(R)=\langle s_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in R\rangle
. The set
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\bigcup_{\alpha\in R}\langle\alpha\rangle^{\bot}
splits into an even number (equal to
|W|
)
of connected mutually congruent open components called
Weyl chambers
. A choice of
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\langle\alpha,x_{0}\rangle\neq 0

for every
\alpha\in R
, gives the partition
R=R_{+}\sqcup(-R_{+})
, where
R_{+}=\{\alpha\in R\colon\langle\alpha,x_{0}\rangle>0\}
is referred to as the
set of positive roots
. The partition distinguishes the chamber
C_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\forall\,\alpha\in R_{+}\,\,\,\langle x,\alpha\rangle>0\}
.
Geometrically, as an intersection of a finite number of open half-spaces with supporting hyperplanes passing through the origin,
C_{+}
is an
open polyhedral cone
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with vertex at the origin. Obviously,
C_{+}
is an unbounded convex domain.

In dimension 2 any root system is isomorphic (in the sense that the sets of the corresponding reflection lines are identical up to a rotation)
to
I_{2}(m)
for some
m\geq 1
, where
I_{2}(m)=\{z_{j}\colon j=0,\ldots,2m-1\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}
and
z_{j}=e^{i\pi j/m}
.
The reflection group
\mathcal{D}_{m}=W(I_{2}(m))
is called the
dihedral group
(frequently with restriction
m\geq 3
). Geometrically,
for
m\geq 3
this is the group of isometries of the regular
m
-gon centered at the origin with one of the vertices located at
i\simeq(0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

In
[
12
]
realizations of the Laplacian on Weyl chambers associated with a reflection group were investigated; the more general setting of an arbitrary reflection group
W
and an arbitrary open
W
-invariant set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
was treated by the author in
[
11
]
.
These realizations in
L^{2}(C_{+})
are labeled by homomorphisms
\eta\in{\rm Hom}(W,\,\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
, are denoted
-\Delta^{+}_{\eta}
and called the
\eta
-Laplacians on
C_{+}
.
Heuristically,
\eta\in{\rm Hom}(W,\,\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
serves for assigning signs to the facets
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}=\overline{C_{+}}\cap\langle\alpha\rangle^{\bot}
of
C_{+}
, which are the parts of
the boundary of
C_{+}
, by setting
{\rm sign}_{\eta}(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}):=\eta(s_{\alpha})
,
\alpha\in\Sigma
, where
\Sigma\subset R_{+}
is the (unique) system of
simple roots in
R_{+}
.

Each
-\Delta^{+}_{\eta}
is a nonnegative self-adjoint extension of the differential operator
f\mapsto-\Delta f
, the (minus) Laplacian on
C_{+}
, with
dense in
L^{2}(C_{+})
domain
C^{\infty}_{c}(C_{+})
. The Dirichlet and Neumann Laplacians on
C_{+}
are included and correspond to
\eta=

det
and
\eta\equiv 1
,
respectively, and the other
\eta
-Laplacians are in between. The
\eta
-heat semigroup
\{e^{-t(-\Delta^{+}_{\eta})}\}_{t>0}
consists of integral operators with
kernels
\{p_{t}^{\eta,\,C_{+}}\}_{t>0}
.
It was proved, see
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
11
, Corollary 1.2]
, that the
\eta
-heat kernel on
C_{+}
,
\{p_{t}^{\eta,C_{+}}\}_{t>0}
, is given by

p_{t}^{\eta,\,C_{+}}(x,y)=\sum_{g\in W}\eta(g)p_{t}^{(d)}(gx-y),\qquad x,y\in C_{+},

(2.1)

and its positivity on
C_{+}
was established in
[
12
, Theorem 3.4]
. On the boundary of
C_{+}
,
p_{t}^{\eta,\,C_{+}}
behaves as follows, see

[
12
, Proposition 2.2]
: for fixed
y\in C_{+}
and
\alpha\in\Sigma
, if
{\rm sign}_{\eta}(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})=-1
, then
p_{t}^{\eta,\,C_{+}}(\cdot,y)
vanishes on

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
, while if
{\rm sign}_{\eta}(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})=1
, then
p_{t}^{\eta,\,C_{+}}(\cdot,y)
is positive on the
(d-1)
-dimensional interior of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
. Finally we mention that the symmetry of
p_{t}^{\eta,\,C_{+}}(x,y)
in
x
and
y
is seen directly from (
2.1
).

3.
Orthogonal root system case

An introduction to this section can be found in
[
6
, Section 4.1]
,
[
11
, Section 6.1]
, and
[
12
, Section 5.1]
. We recall basic
facts for reader’s convenience.

Up to a rotation, any orthogonal root system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is isomorphic to the system
\{\pm e_{j_{1}},\ldots,\pm e_{j_{k}}\}
, where
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{k}=d

and
1\leq k\leq d
. Rather than to treat orthogonal systems in such generality it is convenient to consider slightly more ordered version, namely

R_{k}:=\{\pm e_{d-k+1},\ldots,\pm e_{d}\}
; in any case this limitation does not reduce generality of obtained results. Then, with the choice

R_{k,+}:=\{e_{d-k+1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
, the corresponding distinguished Weyl chamber is
\mathbb{R}_{+,k}=\mathbb{R}^{d-k}\times(0,\infty)^{k}
; for
d\geq 2
and
k=1

this is the upper half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d=k=1
this is the half-line
(0,\infty)
.

Moreover,
\,W(R_{k})\simeq\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{k}
(group isomorphism) and the action of any
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{j})_{j=1}^{k}\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{k}=\{1,-1\}

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is through

x\to\varepsilon x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-k},\varepsilon_{1}x_{d-k+1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{k}x_{d}).

Consequently, we identify
{\rm Hom}(\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{k},\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, where this time,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
with
addition modulo 2. In this identification a homomorphism
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
of the reflection group represented by
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{k}
into

\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
acts through
\varepsilon\to\varepsilon^{\eta}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{j}^{\eta_{j}}
for
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{j})_{j=1}^{k}
. The trivial homomorphism is represented
by
\textbf{0}:=(0,\ldots,0)
. The other distinguished homomorphism is represented by
\textbf{1}:=(1,\ldots,1)
, in the general case denoted as
det
.

Thus, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+,k}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
we have (see
[
12
, Proposition 2.4]
, cf. also
[
11
, Corollary 6.1]
)

p_{t}^{\eta,\mathbb{R}_{k,+}}(x,y)=\prod_{j=1}^{d-k}p_{t}^{(1)}(x_{j}-y_{j})\prod_{j=d-k+1}^{d}\Big(p_{t}^{(1)}(x_{j}-y_{j})+(-1)^{\eta(j-d+k)}p_{t}^{(1)}(x_{j}+y_{j})\Big).

(3.1)

By convention, the first product is assumed to be 1 if
k=d
. Analogous convention will be used in similar occurrences.
Notice that the right-hand side of (
3.1
) correctly reflects the behavior of
p_{t}^{\eta,\mathbb{R}_{+,k}}
on the boundary of
\mathbb{R}_{+,k}
: given
y\in\mathbb{R}_{+,k}
,

p_{t}^{\eta,\mathbb{R}_{+,k}}(x,y)
vanishes if
x
belongs to any of the facets
\mathcal{F}_{j}:=\langle e_{d-k+j}\rangle^{\bot}\cap\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+,k}}
,
j\in J_{\eta}
(if any),
and
p_{t}^{\eta,\mathbb{R}_{+,k}}(x,y)
is positive if
x
belongs to the
(d-1)
-dimensional interior of
\mathcal{F}_{j}
with
j\in J_{\eta}^{c}
(if any),
the complement of
J_{\eta}
in
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

We are now ready to prove Proposition
1.1
. In dimension one,
d=1
, for the Neumann and Dirichlet heat kernels on
(0,\infty)
,

p_{t}^{N}(x,y)=p_{t}^{(1)}(x-y)+p_{t}^{(1)}(x+y)
and
p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=p_{t}^{(1)}(x-y)-p_{t}^{(1)}(x+y)
, we have for
x,y>0
and
t>0
,

p_{t}^{N}(x,y)\simeq p_{t}^{(1)}(x-y)
and
p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\simeq\frac{xy}{xy+t}p_{t}^{(1)}(x-y)
; the latter is a consequence of
1-e^{-u}\simeq\frac{u}{u+1}
for
u>0
.
In dimension
d\geq 2
, due to the product structure of
p_{t}^{\eta,\mathbb{R}_{+,k}}(x,y)
(which is a general consequence of reducibility of the orthogonal
root system
R_{k}
), the proof is a simple tensorization of the one-dimensional result.
Indeed, consider just for the simplicity of notation only
\eta=\textbf{1}
(the case of the Dirichlet kernel, in the case of general
\eta

the changes are routine). We obtain for
x=(x^{\prime},x_{d-k+1},\ldots,x_{d})
,
y=(y^{\prime},y_{d-k+1},\ldots,y_{d})
, and
t>0
,

\displaystyle p_{t}^{\textbf{1},\mathbb{R}_{k,+}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{(d-k)}(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})\prod_{j=d-k+1}^{d}\Big(p_{t}^{(1)}(x_{j}-y_{j})-p_{t}^{(1)}(x_{j}+y_{j})\Big)

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{(d)}(x-y)\prod_{j=d-k+1}^{d}\Big(1-e^{-x_{j}y_{j}/t}\Big)

\displaystyle\simeq p_{t}^{(d)}(x-y)\prod_{j=d-k+1}^{d}\frac{x_{j}y_{j}}{x_{j}y_{j}+t}.

Remark 3.1
.

For
\eta=\textbf{0}
(the case of the Neumann heat kernel), when

\displaystyle\prod_{j=d-k+1}^{d}\frac{x_{j}y_{j}}{\sqrt{(x_{j}^{2}+t)(y_{j}^{2}+t)}}\,t^{-d/2}e^{-c_{1}\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{t}}

\displaystyle\lesssim p_{t}^{\textbf{1},\mathbb{R}_{+,k}}(x,y)\lesssim

. On the other hand, the right-hand

\displaystyle={\rm triv}:\qquad\Psi_{t,{\rm triv}}(x,y)=\cosh\big(\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{2t}\big)\cosh\big(\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{2t}\big)+\cosh\big(\frac{x_{2}y_{1}}{2t}\big)\cosh\big(\frac{x_{1}y_{2}}{2t}\big).

The formula

and (

and this directly shows positivity of

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sinh\big((x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})/4t\big)\sinh\big((x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})/4t\big),

which is, however, not sufficient to claim directly the positivity of
\Psi_{t,{\rm det}}
on

. (It is doubtful if
\Psi_{t,{\rm det}}
could be expressed
as a finite sum of explicitly positive summands.)

We remark that positivity of

on

),

(4.2)

4.1.
Estimates of
p_{t}^{\eta_{\mathcal{N}_{2}},C_{+}^{(4)}}

Proposition 4.1
.

We have, uniformly in
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in C_{+}^{(4)},\,y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in C_{+}^{(4)}
, and
t>0
,

p_{t}^{\eta_{\mathcal{N}_{2}},C_{+}^{(4)}}(x,y)\simeq\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{x_{1}y_{1}+t}\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{x_{2}y_{2}+t}\,p_{t}^{(2)}(x-y).

(4.3)

Proof.

By a simple homogeneity argument it suffices to establish (
4.3
) for
t=1/2
and hence we are reduced to verifying that for
0<x_{2}<x_{1}
and
0<y_{2}<y_{1}

it holds

\displaystyle e^{-\langle x,y\rangle}\Psi_{1/2,\mathcal{N}_{2}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{-\langle x,y\rangle}\big(\sinh(x_{1}y_{1})\sinh(x_{2}y_{2})+\sinh(x_{2}y_{1})\sinh(x_{1}y_{2})\big)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{x_{1}y_{1}+1}\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{x_{2}y_{2}+1}

(replacing the summand
1
by
1/2
, or vice versa, in the denominators of the last two factors is justified). We now use (
4.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\,\,\,e^{-\langle x,y\rangle}\Psi_{1/2,\mathcal{N}_{2}}(x,y)

\displaystyle\simeq e^{-x_{1}y_{1}-x_{2}y_{2}}\Big(\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{x_{1}y_{1}+1}e^{x_{1}y_{1}}\,\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{x_{2}y_{2}+1}e^{x_{2}y_{2}}+\frac{x_{2}y_{1}}{x_{2}y_{1}+1}e^{x_{2}y_{1}}\,\frac{x_{1}y_{2}}{x_{1}y_{2}+1}e^{x_{1}y_{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{x_{1}y_{1}+1}\,\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{x_{2}y_{2}+1}+\frac{x_{2}y_{1}}{x_{2}y_{1}+1}\,\frac{x_{1}y_{2}}{x_{1}y_{2}+1}\,e^{-(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{x_{1}y_{1}+1}\,\frac{x_{2}y_{2}}{x_{2}y_{2}+1}\Big(1+\frac{x_{1}y_{1}+1}{x_{2}y_{1}+1}\,\frac{x_{2}y_{2}+1}{x_{1}y_{2}+1}\,e^{-(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}\Big).

To finish, it is sufficient to check that

\phi_{2}(x,y):=\frac{x_{1}y_{1}+1}{x_{2}y_{1}+1}\,\frac{x_{2}y_{2}+1}{x_{1}y_{2}+1}\,e^{-(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}

is bounded from above for
0<x_{2}<x_{1}
and
0<y_{2}<y_{1}
; these constraints remain in force until the end of the proof.

Assume that
\frac{x_{1}}{2}\leq x_{2}<x_{1}
, which implies
x_{2}y_{1}+1>(x_{1}y_{1}+1)/2
, and we have

\phi_{2}(x,y)<\frac{x_{1}y_{1}+1}{x_{2}y_{1}+1}<2.

Analogously, in the symmetric case
\frac{y_{1}}{2}\leq y_{2}<y_{1}
we obtain

\phi_{2}(x,y)<\frac{x_{1}y_{1}+1}{x_{1}y_{2}+1}<2.

Therefore we are left with the case when
0<x_{2}<\frac{x_{1}}{2}
and
0<y_{2}<\frac{y_{1}}{2}
, i.e. when
x_{1}-x_{2}>\frac{1}{2}x_{1}
and
y_{1}-y_{2}>\frac{1}{2}y_{1}
. Then

\phi_{2}(x,y)<\frac{x_{1}y_{1}+1}{x_{2}y_{1}+1}\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}x_{1}y_{1}}

and if
x_{1}y_{1}\leq 2
, then
\phi_{2}(x,y)\leq 3
, while if
x_{1}y_{1}>2
, then using
ue^{-u}\lesssim 1
for
u>0
, gives

\phi_{2}(x,y)\lesssim\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{x_{2}y_{1}+1}\,\frac{1}{x_{1}y_{1}}<1

and we are done.
∎

Notice that the right-hand side of (
4.3
) correctly reflects the behavior of
p_{t}^{\eta_{\mathcal{N}_{2}},C_{+}^{(4)}}(x,y)
on the boundary of
C_{+}^{(4)}
:
for
y\in C_{+}^{(4)}
,
p_{t}^{\eta_{\mathcal{N}_{2}},C_{+}^{(4)}}(\cdot,y)
vanishes on the half-line
\{(s,0)\colon s\geq 0\}
, and is positive on the half-line

\{(s,s)\colon s>0\}
.

4.2.
Estimates of

Proposition 4.2
.

We have, uniformly in

,

(4.4)

Proof.

It suffices to establish (
4.4
) for
t=1/2
and hence the task reduces to verifying that for
0<x_{2}<x_{1}
and
0<y_{2}<y_{1}
we have

\displaystyle e^{-\langle x,y\rangle}\Psi_{1/2,\mathcal{N}_{1}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{-\langle x,y\rangle}\Big(\sinh\big((x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})/2\big)\sinh\big((x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})/2\big)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\sinh\big((x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})/2\big)\sinh\big((x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})/2\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{x_{1}y_{1}}{x_{1}y_{1}+1}\,\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})+1}.

We use (
4.2
) to obtain that
e^{-\langle x,y\rangle}\Psi_{1/2,\mathcal{N}_{1}}(x,y)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle e^{-x_{1}y_{1}-x_{2}y_{2}}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})+1}\,e^{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})/2}\,\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})+1}\,e^{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})/2}

\displaystyle+\frac{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})+1}\,e^{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})/2}\,\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})+1}\,e^{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})/2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})+1}\,\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})+1}

\displaystyle+\frac{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})+1}\,\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})+1}\,e^{-2x_{2}y_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})+1}\,\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})}{(x_{1}-x_{2})(y_{1}-y_{2})+1}

to finish it suffices now to check that

and
0<y_{2}<y_{1}
.

(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})+1<2\big((x_{1}+x_{2})y_{1}+1\big)

(4.5)

which for

.

We first observe that the right-hand side of (

) suggests the natural splitting of further reasoning into the following three cases that

and

with respect to 1.

Case (1)

\displaystyle 1-

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\big(\frac{(\sqrt{3}x_{1}-3x_{2})y_{2}}{2}\big)\sinh\big(\frac{(x_{1}+\sqrt{3}x_{2})y_{1}}{2}\big)+\exp\big(-\frac{(\sqrt{3}x_{1}+3x_{2})y_{2}}{2}\big)\sinh\big(\frac{(x_{1}-\sqrt{3}x_{2})y_{1}}{2}\big)}{\sinh x_{1}y_{1}}\simeq\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\frac{(x_{1}-\sqrt{3}x_{2})(y_{1}-\sqrt{3}y_{2})}{(x_{1}-\sqrt{3}x_{2})(y_{1}-\sqrt{3}y_{2})+1}\,\frac{(x_{1}+\sqrt{3}x_{2})(y_{1}+\sqrt{3}y_{2})}{(x_{1}+\sqrt{3}x_{2})(y_{1}+\sqrt{3}y_{2})+1}.

(5.1)

For
x,y\in C_{+}^{(3)}
we will use a modification of variables introduced in the preceding section,

X=\frac{(x_{1}-\sqrt{3}x_{2})y_{1}}{2},\quad Y=\frac{(x_{1}+\sqrt{3}x_{2})y_{1}}{2},\quad s=\sqrt{3}\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}},

so that
|s|<1
,
X>0
,
Y>0
. In these new variables, (

) is equivalent to

G(s,X,Y):=1-\frac{\exp(sX)\sinh Y+\exp(-sY)\sinh X}{\sinh(X+Y)}\simeq\frac{(1-s)X}{(1-s)X+1}\frac{(1+s)Y}{(1+s)Y+1},

(5.2)

uniformly in
X,Y>0
,
|s|<1
. Recall that
G
is positive in the truncated layer

\mathcal{L}_{\rm tr}=(-1,1)\times(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty),

and so is the expression in (
5.3
) below, denoted
S(s,X,Y)
in the beginning of Section
5.1
. Moreover,
G
vanishes on the boundary of

\mathcal{L}_{\rm tr}
, which is equivalent to the fact that
\Phi_{1/2,{\rm det}}(x,y)
vanishes if either
x
or
y
belong to the boundary of
C_{+}^{(3)}
.

To prove (
5.2
) we shall consider the main cases, when
X+Y<1
or
X+Y\geq 1
, and four subcases in the latter case; this will be done in two
subsequent sections. If
X+Y<1
, then also
(1-s)X<2
and
(1+s)Y<2
and, due to
\sinh(X+Y)\simeq X+Y
, (

) becomes

\sinh(X+Y)-\big(\exp(sX)\sinh Y+\exp(-sY)\sinh X\big)\simeq(1-s^{2})XY(X+Y).

(5.3)

If
X+Y\geq 1
, then (
5.2
) becomes

G(s,X,Y)\simeq\begin{cases}(1-s^{2})XY,&(1-s)X<1,\,(1+s)Y<1,\,\,\,\,(A)\\
(1-s)X,&(1-s)X<1,\,(1+s)Y\geq 1,\,\,\,\,(B)\\
(1+s)Y,&(1-s)X\geq 1,\,(1+s)Y<1,\,\,\,\,(C)\\
1,&(1-s)X\geq 1,\,(1+s)Y\geq 1.\,\,\,\,(D)\end{cases}

(5.4)

Notice that due to
G(s,Y,X)=G(-s,X,Y)
the second and third cases are symmetric.

In what follows, to avoid a multiplication of symbols, in the following two subsections we shall use the same characters (some of them already used in
Section
4
), like
S
,
P
,
\mathcal{R}
, and their variants, to denote different object but related to similar contexts. For instance,
\mathcal{R}
will
always stand for a region defined by conditions imposed on
s
,
X
, and
Y
.

5.1.
The case
X+Y<1

We commence proving (
5.3
) with the leading assumption
X+Y<1
. For
s,X,Y
real, denoting by
S(s,X,Y)
the left-hand side of (
5.3
), we have

S(s,X,Y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(X+Y)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}-\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(sX)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{Y^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-sY)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{X^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\Big].

We will show that

S(s,X,Y)=(1-s^{2})XY(X+Y)P(s,X,Y),\qquad(s,X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(5.5)

where

P(s,X,Y)\simeq 1,\qquad(s,X,Y)\in\mathcal{R},

(5.6)

and

\mathcal{R}=(-1,1)\times\{(X,Y)\colon X>0,Y>0,X+Y<1\}.

Expanding the summands
(X+Y)^{2k+1}
and using the Cauchy theorem mentioned in Section
4
, we rearrange the resulting summands and obtain

S(s,X,Y)=\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}S_{m}(s,X,Y),

where, for any
m\geq 3
,
S_{m}(s,X,Y)
is a sum of monomials in the variables
X
and
Y
of joint degree
m
, and coefficients depending
polynomially on
s
. Moreover, the last series converges absolutely. Notice that
S
does not include a constant
term, and monomials of joint degree 1 and 2 do not appear due to an easily seen cancellation. First, we aim to show that for every
m\geq 3
,

S_{m}(s,X,Y)=(1-s^{2})XY(X+Y)P_{m}(s,X,Y),\qquad(s,X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(5.7)

where, for any fixed
s
,
P_{m}(s,\cdot,\cdot)
is a polynomial of two variables
X
and
Y
of joint degree
m-3
. We now begin the analysis of
S_{m}

splitting the consideration onto odd and even cases.

Suppose first that
m\geq 3
is odd. Then
S_{m}
incorporates monomials in
X
and
Y
of joint degree
m
of two kinds. Firstly, we have the terms coming from
the expansion of
(X+Y)^{m}
,
m=2k+1
, except for the monomials
X^{m}
and
Y^{m}
which are canceled (see below). Therefore, the sum of monomials of the first kind is

S_{m}^{(1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{X^{j}Y^{m-j}}{j!(m-j)!}=XY\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}\frac{X^{j}Y^{m-j-2}}{(j+1)!(m-j-1)!}=:XYJ_{m}^{(1)}.

Secondly, included are the monomials that come from multiplication of terms with even exponents from the series representing
\exp(sX)
or
\exp(-sY)
,
and a term in the series representing
\sinh Y
or
\sinh X
, respectively, except for the monomials
X^{m}
and
Y^{m}
which are used to the just mentioned cancellation. Therefore, not including at this moment the minus sign, the sum of monomials of the second kind is

\displaystyle S_{m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\Big[\frac{(sX)^{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{Y^{m-2j}}{(m-2j)!}+\frac{(-sY)^{2j}}{(2j)!}\frac{X^{m-2j}}{(m-2j)!}\Big]

\displaystyle=XY\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\frac{s^{2(j+1)}}{(2(j+1))!(m-2j-2)!}\big(X^{2j+1}Y^{m-3-2j}+Y^{2j+1}X^{m-3-2j}\big)=:XYJ_{m}^{(2)}.

Putting all these monomials together gives
S_{m}=S_{m}^{(1)}-S_{m}^{(2)}=XY\big(J_{m}^{(1)}-J_{m}^{(2)}\big)
; thus,
XY
is singled out. Now, in the sum defining

J_{m}^{(1)}
we pair the terms with indices
2j
and
m-2-2j
,
j=0,\ldots,\frac{m-3}{2}
, and subtract the
(\frac{m-3}{2}-j)
-th term from
J_{m}^{(2)}
to obtain

\displaystyle J_{m}^{(1)}-J_{m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\frac{1-(s^{2})^{\frac{m-1}{2}-j}}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!}\Big(X^{2j}Y^{m-2-2j}+Y^{2j}X^{m-2-2j}\Big)

\displaystyle=(1-s^{2})(X+Y)\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\frac{Q_{m,j}(s)R_{m,j}(X,Y)}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!},

where (notice that
\frac{m-1}{2}-j\geq 1
for
0\leq j\leq\frac{m-3}{2}
)

Q_{m,j}(s):=\frac{1-(s^{2})^{\frac{m-1}{2}-j}}{1-s^{2}},\qquad R_{m,j}(X,Y)=\frac{X^{2j}Y^{m-2-2j}+Y^{2j}X^{m-2-2j}}{X+Y}.

Letting
Q_{m,j}(\pm 1)=\frac{m-1}{2}-j
makes
Q_{m,j}
continuous on
\mathbb{R}
;
Q_{m,j}
is then an even polynomial of degree
m-3-2j
, positive on
\mathbb{R}
.
Set
r(j,m):=\min(2j,m-2-2j)
so that
r(0,m)=0
and
r(j,m)\geq 1
for
j=1,\ldots,\frac{m-3}{2}
. Observing that
m-2-2r(j,m)\geq 1
is odd shows that

R_{m,j}(X,Y)
equals

(XY)^{r(j,m)}\frac{X^{m-2-2r(j,m)}+Y^{m-2-2r(j,m)}}{X+Y}=(XY)^{r(j,m)}\sum_{i=0}^{m-3-2r(j,m)}(-1)^{i}X^{m-3-2r(j,m)-i}Y^{i}.

Concluding, for
m
odd
S_{m}
is of the form (
5.7
) with

P_{m}(s,X,Y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\frac{Q_{m,j}(s)R_{m,j}(X,Y)}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!}.

(5.8)

Notice that
R_{m,j}
are polynomials in
X
and
Y
, positive for
X,Y>0
, and of degree
m-3
, hence
P_{m}(s,X,Y)
is a polynomial in
X
and
Y

of degree
m-3
with coefficients depending on
s
, positive for
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
X,Y>0
; for instance
P_{3}\equiv\frac{1}{2}
.

Assume now that
m\geq 4
is even. Then
S_{m}
incorporates all monomials in
X
and
Y
of joint degree
m
that come from multiplication of a term with odd
exponent from the series representing
\exp(sX)
and a term in the series representing
\sinh Y
, or a term with odd exponent from the series
representing
\exp(-sY)
and a term in the series representing
\sinh X
, respectively. Thus,

\displaystyle S_{m}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{m}{2}-1}\Big[\frac{(sX)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}\frac{Y^{m-2j-1}}{(m-2j-1)!}+\frac{(-sY)^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!}\frac{X^{m-2j-1}}{(m-2j-1)!}\Big]

\displaystyle=XY\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{m-2}{2}}\frac{s^{2j+1}}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!}\Big[Y^{2j}X^{m-2j-2}-X^{2j}Y^{m-2j-2}\Big]=:XYI_{m}.

Now, if
\frac{m}{2}
is odd, then the middle term in the above summation, i.e. the term corresponding to
j=\frac{m-2}{4}
, vanishes.
Therefore, whatever
\frac{m}{2}
is, pairing in
I_{m}
the terms with indices
j
and
\frac{m-2}{2}-j
gives

\displaystyle I_{m}

\displaystyle=s\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\rfloor-1}\frac{s^{2j}}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!}\big(1-(s^{2})^{\frac{m-2}{2}-2j}\big)\Big[Y^{2j}X^{m-2j-2}-X^{2j}Y^{m-2j-2}\Big]

\displaystyle=(1-s^{2})s\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\rfloor-1}\frac{Q_{m,j}(s)}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!}(XY)^{2j}\big(X^{m-4j-2}-Y^{m-4j-2}\big)

\displaystyle=(1-s^{2})(X+Y)(X-Y)s\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\rfloor-1}\frac{Q_{m,j}(s)R_{m,j}(X,Y)}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!},

where (notice that
\frac{m}{2}-2j-1\geq 1
for
0\leq j\leq\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\rfloor-1
)

Q_{m,j}(s):=s^{2j}\frac{1-(s^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}-2j-1}}{1-s^{2}}

is, after appropriate adjustment at
\pm 1
, an even positive on
\mathbb{R}
polynomial of degree
m-4-4j
, and

R_{m,j}(X,Y):=(XY)^{2j}\frac{(X^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}-2j-1}-(Y^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}-2j-1}}{X^{2}-Y^{2}}=(XY)^{2j}\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{m}{2}-2j-2}(X^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}-2j-2-i}(Y^{2})^{i}.

Thus, for
m
even,
S_{m}=XYI_{m}
is of the form (
5.7
) with

P_{m}(s,X,Y)=s(X-Y)\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\rfloor-1}\frac{Q_{m,j}(s)R_{m,j}(X,Y)}{(2j+1)!(m-2j-1)!}.

(5.9)

Since
Q_{m,j}
are positive and
R_{m,j}
are positive for
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}
, the last sum is positive for
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}
, and thus the sign of

P_{m}(s,X,Y)
is determined by the sign of
s(X-Y)
. For instance
P_{4}\equiv\frac{1}{6}s(X-Y)
.

Finally, we reached (
5.5
) with

P(s,X,Y):=\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}P_{m}(s,X,Y),\qquad(s,X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

and
P_{m}
given by (
5.8
) and (
5.9
). To complete the proof of (
5.3
), and finish the proof of (
5
) in the case
X+Y<1
, it remains to check (
5.6
); recall that
P
is positive on
\mathcal{L}_{\rm tr}
because
S
is.

The series
\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}P_{m}(s,X,Y)
is pointwise absolutely convergent (because
\sum_{3}^{\infty}S_{m}
is) and its sum is well-defined.
For continuity of
P
it suffices to check that the series is uniformly convergent on every cube
(-r,r)^{3}
,
r\to\infty
. This can be done by a direct calculation; see Appendix (C) for details.

With continuity of
P
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we now argue as follows. On the compact set
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
, the closure of
\mathcal{R}
,

P
is bounded from above which proves the required upper bound of
P
on
\mathcal{R}
. For the lower bound we shall use an argument of a topological flavor. Namely,
since
P(s,X,Y)
is positive on
\mathcal{R}
, it suffices to verify that
P
does not vanish on the boundary of
\mathcal{R}
. Continuity then implies
that
P
is separated from zero on
\mathcal{R}
. For the proof that
P\neq 0
, more precisely
P>0
, on
\partial\mathcal{R}
we split the argument according to the suitable partition of the boundary and check (slightly more than necessary) that:

(1)

P(s,0,0)>0
for
-1\leq s\leq 1
;

(2)

P(\pm 1,X,0)>0
for
0<X\leq 1
, or
P(\pm 1,0,Y)>0
for
0<Y\leq 1
;

(3)

P(s,X,0)>0
for
-1<s<1
and
X>0
, or
P(s,0,Y)>0
for
-1<s<1
and
Y>0
;

(4)

P(\pm 1,X,Y)>0
for
X>0
and
Y>0
.

Notice that the conclusions for the second parts of Items (2) and (3) follow from the conclusions for the first parts due to the equality
P(s,Y,X)=P(-s,X,Y)
, hence we shall discuss only the first parts. Observe also that we do not discuss the part of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{R}
being the intersection of the
plane
X+Y=1
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\mathcal{L}_{\rm tr}
, because
P>0
there.

For the proofs of Items (1) and (2) we need a formula on
P_{m}(s,X,0)
that follows from (
5.8
) and (
5.9
) and accompanying formulas for

R_{m,j}
(note that
R_{m,j}(X,0)=0
for
j\geq 1
). Namely, for
s,X\in\mathbb{R}
,

P_{m}(s,X,0)=\frac{Q_{m,0}(s)}{(m-1)!}\times\begin{cases}1,&m\geq 3\,\,{\rm odd},\\
sX^{m-3},&m\geq 4\,\,{\rm even}.\end{cases}

Item (1)
. Using the above, the positivity of
Q_{m,j}
gives for
-1\leq s\leq 1

P(s,0,0)=\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}P_{m}(s,0,0)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{2k+1,0}(s)}{(2k)!}>0.

Item (2)
. This time, since that
Q_{2k+1,0}(\pm 1)=k
and
Q_{2k,0}(\pm 1)=k-1
, we have for
0<X\leq 1

\displaystyle P(\pm 1,X,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}P_{m}(\pm 1,X,0)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{2k+1,0}(\pm 1)}{(2k)!}\pm\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{2k,0}(\pm 1)X^{2k-3}}{(2k-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k}{(2k)!}\pm\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{(k-1)X^{2k-3}}{(2k-1)!}>\frac{e}{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k}{(2k+1)!}=\frac{e}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\big(1-\frac{1}{e}\big)=\cosh 1-\frac{1}{2}>0.

For the proofs of Items (3) and (4) our strategy is as follows. Let us focus on the first part of Item (3).
The function
S
is real-analytic at every point
(s,X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so with
-1<s<1
and
X>0
fixed,
S(s,X,\cdot)
is a real-analytic function
of one variable with
Y=0
as a root. Since
S(s,X,Y)=XY(X+Y)(1-s^{2})P(s,X,Y)
, checking that
\partial_{Y}S(s,X,Y)_{Y=0}\neq 0
will imply that

Y=0
is a simple root of
S(s,X,\cdot)
and thus
P(s,X,0)\neq 0
. Positivity of
P
in
\mathcal{R}
actually shows that
P(s,X,0)>0
.

Item (3)
. Fix
s\in(-1,1)
and
X>0
. We shall check that
Y=0
is a simple root of the function
\Phi(Y)=S(s,X,Y)
.
We have
\Phi^{\prime}(Y)=\cosh(X+Y)-e^{sX}\cosh Y+se^{-sY}\sinh X
, hence
\Phi^{\prime}(0)=\cosh X-e^{sX}+s\sinh X
. We claim that
\Phi^{\prime}(0)>0
.
Let
f(s)=\cosh X-e^{sX}+s\sinh X
, with
X>0
fixed and
s
freed, so that
f^{\prime}(s)=-Xe^{sX}+\sinh X>0
if and only if
\frac{\sinh X}{X}>e^{sX}
.
Hence,
f(s)
increases on
(-1,s_{0})
and decreases on
(s_{0},1)
, where
s_{0}\in(0,1)
is such that
\frac{\sinh X}{X}=e^{s_{0}X}
. This together
with
f(\pm 1)=0
proves the claim.

Item (4)
. Fix
X>0
and
Y>0
. We shall check that
s=\pm 1
is a simple root of the function
\Psi(s):=S(s,X,Y)
.
More precisely, we shall check that
\Psi^{\prime}(\pm 1)\neq 0
. Indeed,
\Psi^{\prime}(s)=-Xe^{sX}\sinh Y+Ye^{-sY}\sinh X
and

\Psi^{\prime}(1)=-Xe^{X}\sinh Y+Ye^{-Y}\sinh X<0
since
\frac{\sinh X}{Xe^{X}}<\frac{e^{Y}\sinh Y}{Y}
for
X,Y>0
(
\frac{\sinh X}{Xe^{X}}
decreases
on
(0,\infty)
, while
\frac{e^{Y}\sinh Y}{Y}
increases on
(0,\infty)
). Analogous argument shows that
\Psi^{\prime}(-1)>0
.

5.2.
The case
X+Y\geq 1

We now continue and prove (
5.4
) with the leading assumption
X+Y\geq 1
.

Case (A)
:
(1-s)X<1,\,(1+s)Y<1
. Since now
\sinh(X+Y)\simeq\exp(X+Y)
, it follows that (
5.4
) (A) is equivalent to

\frac{\sinh(X+Y)}{e^{X+Y}}-\Big[e^{-(1-s)X}\frac{\sinh Y}{e^{Y}}+e^{-(1+s)Y}\frac{\sinh X}{e^{X}}\Big]\simeq(1-s^{2})XY.

But

\frac{\sinh\alpha}{\exp\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\big(1-e^{-2\alpha}\big),

so neglecting the factor
\frac{1}{2}
and with the change of variables
X\to X/2
,
Y\to Y/2
, our goal is

S(s,X,Y):=1-e^{-(X+Y)}-e^{-\frac{1-s}{2}X}\big(1-e^{-Y}\big)-e^{-\frac{1+s}{2}Y}\big(1-e^{-X}\big)\simeq(1-s^{2})XY,

(5.10)

uniformly in
s,X,Y
from the region

\mathcal{R}=\Big\{(s,X,Y)\colon|s|<1,\,X+Y\geq 2,\,\frac{1-s}{2}X<1,\,\frac{1+s}{2}Y<1\Big\}.

To achieve the goal we shall apply the method already used in the case
X+Y<1
. It is worth adding at this point that
P
is continuous and the arguments
for this are analogous to these establishing continuity of
P
considered in Section
5.1
.

Observe that
S
is a real-analytic function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, positive in the truncated layer
\mathcal{L}_{\rm tr}

and vanishing on the boundary of
\mathcal{L}_{\rm tr}
, and we have

S(s,X,Y)=-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-(X+Y))^{m}}{m!}+\Big[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(-\frac{1-s}{2}X\big)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-Y)^{n}}{n!}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(-\frac{1+s}{2}Y\big)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-X)^{n}}{n!}\Big].

We will show, and this is sufficient to obtain (
5.10
), that

S(s,X,Y)=(1-s^{2})XYP(s,X,Y),\qquad(s,X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(5.11)

where

P(s,X,Y)\simeq 1,\qquad(s,X,Y)\in\mathcal{R}.

(5.12)

Expanding the summands
(-(X+Y))^{m}
and using the Cauchy theorem mentioned before, we rearrange the resulting summands and obtain

S=\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}S_{m},

where, for any fixed
s
,
S_{m}=S_{m}(s,X,Y)
is a sum of monomials in the variables
X
and
Y
of joint degree
m
, and coefficients depending on
s
, with
the last series being absolutely convergent. Notice that
S
does not include a constant term, and monomials of joint degree 1 or 2 do not appear due to an easily seen cancellation.

For the analysis of
S_{m}
,
m\geq 3
, we first note that

\displaystyle S_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j!(m-j)!}\Big[\big(-\frac{1-s}{2}X\big)^{j}(-Y)^{m-j}+\big(-\frac{1+s}{2}Y\big)^{j}(-X)^{m-j}\Big]-(-1)^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{X^{j}Y^{m-j}}{j!(m-j)!}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{j!(m-j)!}\Big[\big(\frac{1-s}{2}\big)^{j}X^{j}Y^{m-j}+\big(\frac{1+s}{2}\big)^{j}Y^{j}X^{m-j}-X^{j}Y^{m-j}\Big].

Two summands from the first sum above corresponding to
j=0
, i.e.
\frac{1}{m!}(-Y)^{m}
and
\frac{1}{m!}(-X)^{m}
, are canceled by the summands from
the second sum that correspond to
j=0
and
j=m
.

We now continue rearranging the latter sum. For this, assuming for a moment that
m
is odd, we pair the terms in brackets
and corresponding to the indices
j
and
m-j

we obtain for

odd

If

is even, then the above formula remains essentially the same with the following modification: the summation now goes to

For

With this notation we further rearrange

For

Checking the last inequality is routine. (For instance,

(C). The aim of this part is to show that the conjectured estimate (

) is compatible with estimates proved in
[
4

the general framework of an arbitrary inner uniform domain, in the sense that (

Rather than to compare (

(so that

), we prefer to use a more explicit variant

(sLd for short, sometimes called simply

), which is a
subclass of
strongly Lipschitz domains
; this variant is taken from

An sLd is, up to rotation, the domain of the form

U_{\Phi}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\Phi(x^{\prime})<x_{d}\},\qquad x=(x^{\prime},x_{d}),

where
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}

, is a Lipschitz function; plainly,

is a domain above the graph of
\Phi
. Clearly, any Weyl chamber
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2

Any sLd is a
uniform domain
(see
[
4
, Proposition 6.6]
) and any convex sLd is an inner uniform domain. Thus, any Weyl chamber is an example
of a convex unbounded uniform domain. Therefore,
[
4
, Corollary 6.14]
(repeated also as
[
4
, Corollary 6.28]
; note that in both places the

says that

p_{t}^{{\rm det},C^{(4)}_{+}}(x,y)\simeq\simeq t^{-d/2}\frac{h(x)h(y)}{h(x_{*,\sqrt{t}})h(y_{*,\sqrt{t}})}\,e^{-c\|x-y\|^{2}/t},\qquad x,y\in C^{(4)}_{+},

where

for

, and

\vec{v}_{2}=(\tau^{+},\tau^{-}),\qquad\tau^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\pm\sqrt{2}},

is the unit vector generating the symmetry line of

, and
