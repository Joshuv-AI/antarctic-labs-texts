Constant-sized robust self-tests for states and measurements of unbounded dimension
Laura Mančinska, Jitendra Prakash, Christopher Schafhauser

Abstract
. We consider correlations, p n , x p_{n,x} , arising from measuring a maximally entangled state using n n measurements with two outcomes each, constructed from n n projections that add up to x ​ I xI . We show that the correlations p n , x p_{n,x} robustly self-test the underlying states and measurements. To achieve this, we lift the group-theoretic Gowers-Hatami based approach for proving robust self-tests to a more natural algebraic framework. A key step is to obtain an analogue of the Gowers-Hatami theorem allowing to perturb an “approximate” representation of the relevant algebra to an exact one. For n = 4 n=4 , the correlations p n , x p_{n,x} self-test the maximally entangled state of every odd dimension as well as 2-outcome projective measurements of arbitrarily high rank. The only other family of constant-sized self-tests for strategies of unbounded dimension is due to Fu (QIP 2020) who presents such self-tests for an infinite family of maximally entangled states with even local dimension. Therefore, we are the first to exhibit a constant-sized self-test for measurements of unbounded dimension as well as all maximally entangled states with odd local dimension.

Introduction

One of the key tasks in the development of reliable quantum technologies is the certification of quantum devices. This ensures that the devices are performing according to their specification. One of the ways such certification may be carried out is by using
self-testing
methods which enable us to infer the quantum-mechanical description of a device merely from classical observations (measurement statistics). We can then treat these devices as black boxes as we need not trust the inner workings of the system, a scenario which one refers to as
device-independence
. Beginning with
[
MY04
]
, in which the term was first coined, self-testing has found many applications, such as device-independent quantum cryptography
[
MY98
,
MY04
]
, delegated quantum computation
[
CGJV19
]
, entanglement detection
[
BvCA18a
,
BvCA18b
]
, investigating the structure of the quantum correlation set
[
CS17
,
GKW+18
]
, and quantum complexity theory
[
FJVY19
,
NV18
,
NW19
]
. Self-testing is also one of the ingredients behind the recent breakthrough result establishing that
\text{MIP}^{*}=\text{RE}

[
JNV+20
]
; which further implies a negative answer to the celebrated Connes’ embedding problem
[
Con76
]
from the theory of von Neumann algebras.

In this work, we prove that certain quantum strategies constructed from projections which sum up to some particular scalar times identity can be robustly self-tested from the quantum correlations that they induce. Specifically, we begin with
d\times d
projections
\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{P}_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) such that
\widetilde{P}_{1}+\dots+\widetilde{P}_{n}=xI_{d}
for some specific scalar
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The scalar
x
and the projections
\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{P}_{n}
have the property that whenever
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
are any other projections such that
P_{1}+\dots+P_{n}=xI
, then
P_{i}=I\otimes\widetilde{P}_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
in some basis. We then define a quantum strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
and its induced quantum correlation
\widetilde{p}
corresponding to these projections. We show that if one observes the quantum correlation
\widetilde{p}
induced from an arbitrary quantum strategy
\mathscr{S}
, then
\mathscr{S}
is related to
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
via a local isometry. For the robust case, we prove that if we observe a quantum correlation
p
such that
\|p-\widetilde{p}\|\leq\epsilon
, then its inducing strategy
\mathscr{S}
is “approximately” related to
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
via a local isometry.

Often times self-testing results are proven using ad-hoc techniques, which means that to obtain new results one essentially has to start from scratch. One notable exception is an approach that
uses perturbative representation theory of groups to establish robust self-testing
[
Vid18
,
CS17
,
CMMN20
]
. The basic idea is that in the ideal case one deduces algebraic relations which the measurement operators must satisfy on the quantum state. One then associates a suitable finite group such that one gets a representation of the group. This transfers to the well-studied field of the representation theory of finite groups and the problem reduces to identifying suitable irreducible representations of the group. In the robust case, one gets approximate versions of the algebraic relations which yield “approximate” representations of the group (with respect to state-dependent distance). A key tool used in the approximate case is the Gowers–Hatami theorem
[
GK17
,
Gow17
,
Vid18
]
which relates an “approximate” representation of a group to a representation of that group in some suitable sense.
A caveat to this approach is that, in general, it is not clear what group should be associated to the algebraic relations identified and if this is at all possible. In fact, it would be more natural to associate an
algebra
rather than a group to these algebraic relations. Moreover, there are known families of non-local games whose optimal strategies do not have an apparent underlying group yet an underlying algebra can easily be identified (
e.g.
binary constraint system games
[
CM14
]
beyond linear ones, synchronous games
[
PSS+16
,
HMPS19
]
, graph homomorphism
[
MR16
]
and isomorphism games
[
AMR+19
]
).

One of our main contributions is that we showcase how the above general method for self-testing from this
group
framework can be lifted to an
algebraic
framework. Instead of seeking an appropriate group to associate with the algebraic relations, we simply work with the algebra generated by those relations. To accomplish this, a major step is to obtain some sort of analogue of Gowers–Hatami theorem for algebras. We show how this can be done for a particular algebras, but the approach can be easily generalized to algebras arising from other synchronous correlations whenever a version of Gowers-Hatami Theorem holds; we expect this to be the case for algebras with well-understood representation theory such as finite dimensional algebras. The analogue we prove in this paper has a non-constructive (
\epsilon
,
\delta
)-dependence which is the most pressing question left open by the current work.

The following example illustrates the concept of robust self-testing and also outlines the method that we use to obtain our results. Consider the
CHSH game

[
CHSH69
,
CHTW04
]
, where two non-communicating players, Alice and Bob, want to win the game against a referee. The referee picks a pair of bits
(v,w)\in\{0,1\}^{2}
uniformly and sends
v
to Alice and
w
to Bob. Alice replies with a bit
i
and Bob replies with a bit
j
. Alice and Bob win the game if
vw=i+j\pmod{2}
. Classical strategies can only help them win the game with probability 3/4. On the other hand, quantum strategies provide a better winning probability of
\omega_{q}(\mathrm{CHSH})\approx 0.85
. Indeed, the following quantum strategy (which we term
canonical
)

(1.1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widetilde{A}_{0}=Z,\widetilde{A}_{1}=X}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widetilde{B}_{0}=\frac{Z+X}{\sqrt{2}},\widetilde{B}_{1}=\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt{2}}}}\right\}}}\right),

where
\varphi_{2}
is the maximally entangled state and
X,Z
are the Pauli matrices, achieves the maximum quantum winning probability.

Suppose that Alice and Bob use an unknown quantum strategy (in terms of observables) given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{A_{0},A_{1}}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{B_{0},B_{1}}}\right\}}}\right)

which also yields the maximum winning probability
\omega_{q}(\mathrm{CHSH})
. What can then be said about the strategy
\mathscr{S}
? It has been shown that in such a case, there exist isometries
V_{A}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{A}
and
V_{B}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}
for some Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}_{A},\mathcal{K}_{B}
, and a quantum state
\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}
such that

(1.3)

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi

\displaystyle=\varphi_{2}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}},\text{ and}

(1.4)

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})(A_{i}\otimes B_{j})\psi

\displaystyle=((\widetilde{A}_{i}\otimes\widetilde{B}_{j})\varphi_{2})\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}},\text{ for all }i,j\in\{0,1\}.

That is, all quantum strategies which yield the maximum quantum winning probability for the CHSH game are related to the canonical one via local isometries, which can be viewed as a rigidity property of
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
.

This rigidity result can be extended to the approximate case (which we term robust self-testing): say the winning probability
\omega_{q}(\mathscr{S})
obtained by the strategy
\mathscr{S}
in (
1.2
) above satisfies
|\omega_{q}(\mathscr{S})-\omega_{q}(\mathrm{CHSH})|\leq\epsilon,
for some
\epsilon\geq 0
. Then, in this case, there exist isometries
V_{A}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{A}
and
V_{B}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}
for some Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}_{A},\mathcal{K}_{B}
, and a quantum state
\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}
which satisfy approximate versions of Equations (
1.3
) and (
1.4
):

(1.5)

\displaystyle\|(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi-\varphi_{2}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\|

\displaystyle\leq f(\epsilon),\text{ and}

(1.6)

\displaystyle\|(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})(A_{i}\otimes B_{j})\psi-((\widetilde{A}_{i}\otimes\widetilde{B}_{j})\varphi_{2})\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\|

\displaystyle\leq f(\epsilon),\text{ for all }i,j\in\{0,1\},

for some
f(\epsilon)\geq 0
such that
f(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

There are many proofs for these results

]

which is isomorphic to the dihedral group of order eight). In the case when
\omega_{q}(\mathscr{S})=\omega_{q}(\mathrm{CHSH})
, the function
f:\mathcal{P}\to\mathscr{U}_{d_{A}}
(where
\mathscr{U}_{d_{A}}

, one gets an “approximate” group representation with respect to the state

. In the second step, to get the existence of isometries one invokes the Gowers–Hatami Theorem

Theorem 1.1

(Gowers–Hatami)
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\epsilon>0
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be a density matrix, and
f:G\to\mathscr{U}_{d}
be an
(\epsilon,\sigma)
-representation of
G
. Then, there exists
D\geq d
, an isometry
V\colon\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{D}
, and a representation
g\colon G\to\mathscr{U}_{D}
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f(a)-V^{*}g(a)V}}\right\|_{\sigma}\leq|G|\sqrt{\epsilon}
for all
a\in G
.

Finally, these two steps are stitched together to get the robust self-testing statements for the CHSH game.

For the proof of our results, we follow the blueprint mentioned above for the CHSH game with certain necessary modifications. As noted above, optimal strategies for the CHSH game may be described in terms of unitary representations of the Pauli group
\mathcal{P}
. Further, unitary representations of
\mathcal{P}
are in bijection with Hilbert space representations of the group C
∗
-algebra
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathcal{P})\cong\mathbb{C}^{4}\oplus\mathbb{M}_{2}
. In our situation, the strategies we consider do not arise from unitary representations of a group but will still arise from Hilbert space representations of a certain C
∗
-algebra
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
(which is not isomorphic to a group C
∗
-algebra). The C
∗
-algebra
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
is the universal C
∗
-algebra generated by projections
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
satisfying the algebraic relation
r_{1}+\dots+r_{n}=x1
. Studying the (approximate) representation theory of this C
∗
-algebra will provide an analogue of Theorem
1.1
for
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
, which leads to the desired self-testing results in Section
6
. Analogues of Theorem
1.1
are ubiquitous in C
∗
-algebra theory, but usually approximations are considered in operator norm (c.f.
[
Lor97
]
) or in the 2-norm arising from a tracial state (c.f.
[
HS18
]
). In our case, we will need to work with the 2-norm arising from an “approximately tracial” state which presents new difficulties (see Theorem
6.5
).

,

(2.3)

\displaystyle(X\otimes Y)\mathrm{vec}(D)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vec}(XDY^{T}).

For each
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the unit vector
\varphi_{d}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
is called the (
canonical
)
maximally entangled state
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then the diagonal matrix
D=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}I_{d}
satisfies
\mathrm{vec}(D)=\varphi_{d}
. For
X,Y\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
, using Identity (
2.3
) and the isometry property, we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle(X\otimes Y)\varphi_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathrm{vec}(XY^{T}),\text{ and}

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(X\otimes Y)\varphi_{d}},{\varphi_{d}}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\tr(XY^{T}).

3.
Synchronous quantum correlations

Let us briefly describe the framework of non-local games
[
CHTW04
]
in which the discussion of correlations and strategies takes a natural form. A
non-local game

\mathscr{G}
is played by two spatially separated players, Alice and Bob, against a referee. There are (non-empty) finite sets
I_{A}
and
I_{B}
, called the
question sets
, and
O_{A}
and
O_{B}
, called the
answer sets
, and a
rule function

\lambda\colon I_{A}\times I_{B}\times O_{A}\times O_{B}\to\{0,1\},

all of which are known to the players. However, once the game begins, the players cannot communicate with each other. The game begins with the referee randomly picking a pair of questions
(v,w)\in I_{A}\times I_{B}
according to some probability distribution
2
2

2

The probability distribution on
I_{A}\times I_{B}
should be given as part of the game, but as we are only interested in winning (or perfect) strategies, the probability distribution will not be relevant for us. For our purposes, it suffices to view
I_{A}\times I_{B}
with the uniform distribution, so each pair of questions is chosen with equal probability.
and sending
v
to Alice and
w
to Bob. Alice returns an answer
i\in O_{A}
to the referee, and so does Bob with
j\in O_{B}
. Alice and Bob win the round of the game if
\lambda(v,w,i,j)=1
, or otherwise they lose. The goal of Alice and Bob is to maximize their winning probability, and for this, they can take the help of either classical resources or quantum resources. It is well-known that for many games, quantum resources exhibit larger winning probabilities as compared to classical resources
[
CHTW04
]
. A classic example is the
CHSH game
as described in the Introduction; here
I_{A}=I_{B}=O_{A}=O_{B}=\{0,1\}
, and
\lambda(v,w,i,j)=1
if and only if
vw=i+j\pmod{2}
.

As the game is played for many rounds, an outside observer will see a probability distribution which encodes the strategies employed by the players. Let
p(i,j|v,w)
be the probability of Alice responding with
i
and Bob responding with
j
given that they received
v
and
w
, respectively.
Classical strategies
usually employ either a
deterministic
strategy (given by a function
I_{A}\times I_{B}\rightarrow O_{A}\times O_{B}
) or by using
local/shared randomness
. On the other hand,
quantum strategies
are given by a
quantum state
(usually
entangled
) shared by Alice and Bob and sets of
quantum measurements
. Specifically, we have the following definition.

Definition 3.1

(Quantum strategies and quantum correlations)
.

Given (non-empty) finite sets
I_{A},I_{B},O_{A},O_{B}
with
|I_{A}|=n_{A},|I_{B}|=n_{B},|O_{A}|=k_{A}
and
|O_{B}|=k_{B}
, a
quantum strategy

\mathscr{S}
is given by a triple:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{v,i}:i\in O_{A},v\in I_{A}}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F_{w,j}:j\in O_{B},w\in I_{B}}}\right\}}}\right),

where
\psi
is a unit vector (also referred to as a
quantum state
), for each
v\in I_{A}
the set
\{E_{v,i}:i\in O_{A}\}
is a POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}
, and for each
w\in I_{B}
, the set
\{F_{w,j}:j\in O_{B}\}
is a POVM on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
.

Each quantum strategy
\mathscr{S}
induces a collection of probability distributions called a
quantum correlation
as follows: for each
(v,w)\in I_{A}\times I_{B}
, define

(3.2)

\displaystyle p(i,j|v,w)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{w,j}}}\right)\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle,\quad\text{ for all }(i,j)\in O_{A}\times O_{B}.

Fixing
n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B}\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of quantum correlations arising from all choices of quantum strategies is denoted by
C_{q}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B})
. In particular, we define
C_{q}(n,k)\coloneqq C_{q}(n,n,k,k)
for all
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
p=(p(i,j|v,w))\in C_{q}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B})
is a quantum correlation, then the POVM assumptions imply that
p(i,j|v,w)\geq 0
for all
i,j,v
and
w
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in O_{A}\times O_{B}}p(i,j|v,w)=1,\quad\text{for all }(v,w)\in I_{A}\times I_{B}.

Moreover, quantum correlations satisfy
non-signaling conditions
; that is, they have well-defined
marginals

p_{A}(i|v)
and
p_{B}(j|w)
in the sense that

(3.3)

\displaystyle p_{A}(i|v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j^{\prime}\in O_{B}}p(i,j^{\prime}|v,w)=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in O_{B}}p(i,j^{\prime}|v,w^{\prime}),\text{ and}

(3.4)

\displaystyle p_{B}(j|w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i^{\prime}\in O_{A}}p(i^{\prime},j|v,w)=\sum_{i^{\prime}\in O_{A}}p(i^{\prime},j|v^{\prime},w),

for all
v,v^{\prime}\in I_{A},w,w^{\prime}\in I_{B},i\in O_{A},j\in O_{B}
. Indeed, using the definition of a POVM, Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) yield

(3.5)

\displaystyle p_{A}(i|v)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}}}}\right)\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad p_{B}(j|w)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{w,j}}}\right)\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle.

Thus
p_{A}(i|v)
is independent of
w
, and
p_{B}(j|w)
is independent of
v
. (In the context of non-local games, the non-signaling condition is imposed due to the assumption that the players may not communicate.)

Remark 3.2
.

The set
C_{q}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B})
of quantum correlations is convex for all natural numbers
n_{A},n_{B},k_{A}
and
k_{B}
. However, in general, it is not closed. This was first shown by Slofstra who proved that
C_{q}(184,235,8,2)
is not closed
[
Slo19
]
. Thereafter, non-closure of quantum correlations sets with smaller values of
n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B}
have been proved
[
DPP19
,
MR20
,
Col20
]
—in particular,
C_{q}(5,2)
is not closed as was first shown in
[
DPP19
]
.

Remark 3.3
.

For notational simplicity, we shall simply take

\displaystyle I_{A}=[n_{A}],I_{B}=[n_{B}],O_{A}=[k_{A}],\text{ and }O_{B}=[k_{B}]

in the definition of a quantum correlation.

Introduced in
[
PSS+16
]
, the subset of synchronous correlations arises naturally in the context of many non-local games, for example, the
graph coloring games

[
CHTW04
]
and
graph homomorphism games

[
MR14
]
. In this article, we shall be self-testing some quantum strategies yielding synchronous quantum correlations, and therefore here is a formal definition.

Definition 3.4
.

A correlation
(p(i,j|v,w))\in C_{q}(n,k)
is called
synchronous
if
k\coloneqq k_{A}=k_{B}
,
n\coloneqq n_{A}=n_{B}
, and
p(i,j|v,v)=0
for all
i,j\in[k]
and
v\in[n]
with
i\neq j
. We let
C_{q}^{s}(n,k)
denote the subset of all synchronous correlations of
C_{q}(n,k)
, and call it the set of
synchronous quantum correlations
.

A quantum strategy which induces a synchronous quantum correlation has several nice properties. For instance, when restricted to
\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi)
(resp.,
\mathrm{supp}_{B}(\psi)
), the measurement operators for Alice (resp., Bob) are projections, Bob’s measurement operators can be expressed in terms of Alice’s, and
X\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(X\otimes I)\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle
defines a
tracial state
(see Appendix
A
) on the C
∗
-algebra generated by Alice’s measurements
[
PSS+16
, Theorem 5.5]
. The next two lemmas show that if a correlation is “approximately” synchronous, then these properties “approximately” hold for its inducing strategy. These lemmas are not new and appear in various places in the literature in different guises, but for the sake of completeness, we provide proofs.

Since correlations are collections of probability distributions, it makes sense to work with the
1
-norm. That is, given two correlations
p_{1},p_{2}\in C_{q}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B})
, we define

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|p_{1}-p_{2}}}\right\|_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j,v,w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|p_{1}(i,j|v,w)-p_{2}(i,j|v,w)}}\right|.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
p\in C_{q}(n,k)
be induced by a strategy

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{v,i}:i\in[k],v\in[n]}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F_{w,j}:j\in[k],w\in[n]}}\right\}}}\right).

Let
\rho_{A},\rho_{B}
be the reduced density matrices of the density matrix
\rho=\psi\psi^{*}
. If
\|p-\widetilde{p}\|_{1}\leq\delta
for some synchronous
\widetilde{p}\in C_{q}^{s}(n,k)
and for some
\delta\geq 0
, then for all
i\in[k]
and
v\in[n]
,

(a)

\|(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi-(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi\|\leq\sqrt{\delta}
,

(b)

\|(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi-(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi\|\leq\sqrt{\delta}
,

(c)

\|(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi-(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi\|\leq\sqrt{\delta}
,

(d)

\|E_{v,i}-E_{v,i}^{2}\|_{\rho_{A}}=\|(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi-(E_{v,i}^{2}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi\|\leq 2\sqrt{\delta}
,

(e)

\|F_{v,i}-F_{v,i}^{2}\|_{\rho_{B}}=\|(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi-(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i}^{2})\psi\|\leq 2\sqrt{\delta}
.

Proof.

Using the fact that
0\leq E_{v,i}^{2}\leq E_{v,i}
and
0\leq F_{v,i}^{2}\leq F_{v,i}
for all
v\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\|(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi-(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}^{2}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i}^{2})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle-2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle-2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=p_{A}(i|v)+p_{B}(i|v)-2p(i,i|v,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{k}p(i,j|v,v)+p(j,i|v,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{k}|p(i,j|v,v)-\widetilde{p}(i,j|v,v)|+|p(j,i|v,v)-\widetilde{p}(j,i|v,v)|

\displaystyle\leq\|p-\widetilde{p}\|_{1}\leq\delta,

where the penultimate line is due to the fact that
\widetilde{p}
is synchronous. Thus (a) follows.

Similarly, to see (b), note that

\displaystyle\|(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi-(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v})\psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(E_{v,i}\otimes(I_{d_{B}}-F_{v,i}))\psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}^{2}\otimes(I_{d_{B}}-F_{v,i})^{2})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}\otimes(I_{d_{B}}-F_{v,i}))\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=p_{A}(i|v)-p(i,i|v,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{k}p(i,j|v,v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{k}|p(i,j|v,v)-\widetilde{p}(i,j|v,v)|

\displaystyle\leq\|p-\widetilde{p}\|_{1}\leq\delta.

Part (c) is similar.

The equalities in parts (d) and (e) follow from Equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).
Since
\|E_{v,i}\|\leq 1
and
\|F_{v,i}\|\leq 1
, we deduce from (a) that for all
v\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\|(E_{v,i}^{2}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi-(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\delta}\text{ and}

\displaystyle\|(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i}^{2})\psi-(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\delta}.

Using these two inequalities with (b) and (c) and applying triangle inequality, we get the inequalities in part (d) and (e).
∎

We record the special case of
\delta=0
in Lemma
3.5
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
p\in C_{q}^{s}(n,k)
be a synchronous correlation induced by a strategy

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{v,i}:i\in[k],v\in[n]}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F_{w,j}:j\in[k],w\in[n]}}\right\}}}\right).

Then, for all
i\in[k]
and
v\in[n]
,

(a)

(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi=(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi
,

(b)

(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi=(E_{v,i}^{2}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi
,

(c)

(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi=(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i}^{2})\psi
.

Moreover, if
\psi
is of full Schmidt rank
r=d_{A}=d_{B}
, then for all
v\in[n]
, the matrices
E_{v,i},F_{v,i}
are projections, and
F_{v,i}=D^{T}E_{v,i}^{T}(D^{-1})^{T}
, where
D\in\mathbb{M}_{r}
is such that
\mathrm{vec}(D)=\psi
.

Proof.

Statements (a), (b), and (c) follow immediately from the corresponding statements in Lemma
3.5
. When
\psi
has full Schmidt rank, the seminorms
\|\cdot\|_{\rho_{A}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\rho_{B}}
are norms, and so
E_{v,i}
and
F_{v,i}
are projections by statements (d) and (e) in Lemma
3.5
. Finally, (a) and Equation (
2.3
) imply
E_{v,i}D=DF_{v,i}^{T}
. Since
\psi
has full Schmidt rank,
D
is invertible, and the last equation follows.
∎

Lemma 3.7

(Approximately tracial state)
.

Let
p\in C_{q}(n,k)
be induced by a strategy

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E_{v,i}:i\in[k],v\in[n]}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F_{w,j}:j\in[k],w\in[n]}}\right\}}}\right).

Let
\rho_{A},\rho_{B}
be the reduced density matrices of the density matrix
\rho=\psi\psi^{*}
. Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{d_{A}}
(resp.,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{d_{B}}
) be the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
\{E_{v,i}\}_{v,i}
(resp.,
\{F_{w,j}\}_{w,j}
). If
\|p-\widetilde{p}\|_{1}\leq\delta
for some synchronous
\widetilde{p}\in C_{q}^{s}(n,k)
and for some
\delta\geq 0
, then the states
\tr_{\rho_{A}}\colon X\mapsto\tr(X\rho_{A})
on
\mathcal{A}
and
\tr_{\rho_{B}}\colon Y\mapsto\tr(Y\rho_{B})
on
\mathcal{B}
satisfy

(3.7)

\displaystyle|\tr_{\rho_{A}}(WX-XW)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\ell\sqrt{\delta}\text{ and}

(3.8)

\displaystyle|\tr_{\rho_{B}}(UY-YU)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\ell\sqrt{\delta},

respectively, where
W
(resp.,
U
) is any word in
\{E_{v,i}\}_{v,i}
(resp.,
\{F_{w,j}\}_{w,j}
) of length
\ell
,
X\in\mathcal{A}
with
\|X\|\leq 1
, and
Y\in\mathcal{B}
with
\|Y\|\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let
W=E_{v,i}W^{\prime}
where
W^{\prime}
is of length
\ell-1
,
i\in[k]
and
v\in[n]
. Then making use of Lemma
3.5
(a) a couple of times and that
\|E_{v,i}\|\leq 1
and
\|X\|\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\tr_{\rho_{A}}(WX)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v,i}W^{\prime}X\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(W^{\prime}X\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx_{\sqrt{\delta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(W^{\prime}X\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(W^{\prime}X\otimes F_{v,i})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(W^{\prime}X\otimes I_{d_{B}})(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx_{\sqrt{\delta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(W^{\prime}X\otimes I_{d_{B}})(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(W^{\prime}XE_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle.

Thus, continuing in this fashion for the whole length of
W^{\prime}
, we will obtain Expression (
3.7
). Similarly, Expression (
3.8
) holds.
∎

4.
Robust self-testing: definition and basic properties

In many cases, given a quantum correlation
\widetilde{p}
, there is a “unique” quantum strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
which induces the correlation
\widetilde{p}
, in the sense that, any other strategy
\mathscr{S}
which also induces the correlation
\widetilde{p}
is related to
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
by a
local isometry
. The way these two strategies
\mathscr{S}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
are related is what we term as a
local dilation
. For our purpose, we work with a more general definition of a
local
\epsilon
-dilation
which relates strategy
\mathscr{S}
to
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
in the case when the correlation
p
induced by
\mathscr{S}
is
\epsilon
-close to
\widetilde{p}
(in the 1-norm as in Equation
3.6
). This general definition is useful in practical scenarios where it is inevitable that errors creep in while conducting experiments.

Definition 4.1

(Local
\epsilon
-dilation)
.

Given
\epsilon\geq 0
and two finite-dimensional strategies

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}

\displaystyle=\big(\widetilde{\psi}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{A}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{B},\{\widetilde{E}_{v,i}:v\in[n_{A}],i\in[k_{A}]\},\{\widetilde{F}_{w,j}:w\in[n_{B}],j\in[k_{B}]\}\big),

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\{E_{v,i}:v\in[n_{A}],i\in[k_{A}]\},\{F_{w,j}:w\in[n_{B}],j\in[k_{B}]\}}}\right),

we say that
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a
local
\epsilon
-dilation
of
\mathscr{S}
if there exist isometries
V_{A}\colon\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{A}
and
V_{B}\colon\mathcal{H}_{B}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}
for some finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}_{A}
and
\mathcal{K}_{B}
, and a quantum state
\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B}
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi

\displaystyle\approx_{\epsilon}\widetilde{\psi}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\text{ and}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}}}\right)(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{w,j})\psi

\displaystyle\approx_{\epsilon}\big((\widetilde{E}_{v,i}\otimes\widetilde{F}_{w,j})\widetilde{\psi}\big)\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}

for all
i
,
j
,
v
, and
w
.

When
\epsilon=0
, we say that
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a
local dilation
of
\mathscr{S}
(instead of a local
0
-dilation). In this case, the approximations in Expressions (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) are replaced by equalities.

Remark 4.2
.

We are abusing notation slightly in the above definition. In Estimates (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), the vectors on the left belong to the Hilbert space
(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{A})\otimes(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B})
and the vectors on the right belong to the Hilbert space
(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{A}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{B})\otimes(\mathcal{K}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B})
. We are identifying these two Hilbert spaces via the unitary which flips the second and third tensor factors.

Remark 4.3
.

A note on the terminology that we have chosen: In the literature (for instance
[
ŠB20
]
), the phrase “equivalent up to a local isometry” is often used instead of “local dilation”. However, we feel that using the word “equivalence” is somewhat misleading, and that “local dilation” would be an appropriate replacement, since the term “local dilation” has a directional connotation (for instance, we can write
\mathscr{S}\xhookrightarrow{\epsilon}\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
to mean that
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a local
\epsilon
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}
) which is not present if we use the term “equivalence up to local isometry”.

Remark 4.4
.

Observe that if a strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
induces a correlation
\widetilde{p}
, and if
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a local dilation of any other strategy
\mathscr{S}
, then
\mathscr{S}
also induces the same correlation.

We now recall the definition of self-testing and robust self-testing.

Definition 4.5

(Self-testing)
.

A correlation
\widetilde{p}\in C_{q}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B})

self-tests
a strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
if for any strategy
\mathscr{S}
which also induces
\widetilde{p}
,
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a local dilation of
\mathscr{S}
.

Definition 4.6

(Robust self-testing)
.

A correlation
\widetilde{p}\in C_{q}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B})
self-tests a strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}

robustly
if
\widetilde{p}
self-tests
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
and the following condition is satisfied. For each
\epsilon\geq 0
, there exists
\delta\geq 0
, such that if there is some
p\in C_{q}(n_{A},n_{B},k_{A},k_{B})
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|p-\widetilde{p}}}\right\|_{1}\leq\delta
, and
p
is induced by a strategy
\mathscr{S}
, then
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a local
\epsilon
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}
.

For local
\epsilon
-dilations, we establish a transitivity statement which is helpful in reducing certain results to the case when the quantum state is of full Schmidt rank. In terms of the notation in Remark
4.3
, we establish

\displaystyle\big(\mathscr{S}_{3}\xhookrightarrow{\epsilon_{2}}\mathscr{S}_{2}\xhookrightarrow{\epsilon_{1}}\mathscr{S}_{1}\big)\Rightarrow\big(\mathscr{S}_{3}\xhookrightarrow{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}\mathscr{S}_{1}\big).

Lemma 4.7

(Transitivity of local
\epsilon
-dilations)
.

Let
\mathscr{S}_{1},\mathscr{S}_{2}
and
\mathscr{S}_{3}
be finite-dimensional quantum strategies. Let
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\geq 0
. If
\mathscr{S}_{1}
is a local
\epsilon_{1}
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}_{2}
, and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
is a local
\epsilon_{2}
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}_{3}
, then
\mathscr{S}_{1}
is a local
(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}_{3}
.

Proof.

Let the quantum strategies be given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{A,1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B,1},\{E_{v,i}^{(1)}:v\in[n_{A}],i\in[k_{A}]\},\{F_{w,j}^{(1)}:w\in[n_{B}],j\in[k_{B}]\}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{A,2}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B,2},\{E_{v,i}^{(2)}:v\in[n_{A}],i\in[k_{A}]\},\{F_{w,j}^{(2)}:w\in[n_{B}],j\in[k_{B}]\}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{3}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{3}\in\mathcal{H}_{A,3}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B,3},\{E_{v,i}^{(3)}:v\in[n_{A}],i\in[k_{A}]\},\{F_{w,j}^{(3)}:w\in[n_{B}],j\in[k_{B}]\}).

Since
\mathscr{S}_{1}
is a local
\epsilon_{1}
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}_{2}
, there are isometries
V_{A}\colon\mathcal{H}_{A,2}\to\mathcal{H}_{A,1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{A,1}
and
V_{B}\colon\mathcal{H}_{B,2}\to\mathcal{H}_{B,1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B,1}
for some finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}_{A,1},\mathcal{K}_{B,1}
, and there exist a quantum state
\psi_{\mathrm{junk},1}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B,1}
such that for all
v,w,i,j
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})(E_{v,i}^{(2)}\otimes F_{w,j}^{(2)})\psi_{2}\approx_{\epsilon_{1}}(E_{v,i}^{(1)}\otimes F_{w,j}^{(1)})\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk},1}.

Similarly, since
\mathscr{S}_{2}
is a local
\epsilon_{2}
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}_{3}
, there are isometries
W_{A}\colon\mathcal{H}_{A,3}\to\mathcal{H}_{A,2}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{A,2}
and
W_{B}\colon\mathcal{H}_{B,3}\to\mathcal{H}_{B,2}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B,2}
for some finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}_{A,2},\mathcal{K}_{B,2}
, and there exist a quantum state
\psi_{\mathrm{junk},2}\in\mathcal{K}_{A,2}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{B,2}
such that for all
v,w,i,j
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle(W_{A}\otimes W_{B})(E_{v,i}^{(3)}\otimes F_{w,j}^{(3)})\psi_{3}\approx_{\epsilon_{2}}(E_{v,i}^{(2)}\otimes F_{w,j}^{(2)})\psi_{2}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk},2}.

Define
U_{A}=(V_{A}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{A,2}})\circ W_{A}
, and
U_{B}=(V_{B}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{B,2}})\circ W_{B}
, and
\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}=\psi_{\mathrm{junk},1}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk},2}
. Clearly
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
are isometries and that for all
v,w,i,j

\displaystyle(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})(E_{v,i}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\otimes F_{w,j}^{(3)})\psi_{3}

\displaystyle=(V_{A}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{A,2}}\otimes V_{B}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{B,2}})(W_{A}\otimes W_{B})(E_{v,i}^{(3)}\otimes F_{w,j}^{(3)})\psi_{3}

\displaystyle\approx_{\epsilon_{2}}(V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{A,2}}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{B,2}})((E_{v,i}^{(2)}\otimes F_{w,j}^{(2)})\psi_{2}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk},2})

\displaystyle\approx_{\epsilon_{1}}(E_{v,i}^{(1)}\otimes F_{w,j}^{(1)})\psi_{1}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk},1}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk},2},

where we used (
4.4
) in the second approximation, and (
4.3
) in the third approximation. ∎

While working with synchronous quantum correlations one usually takes the quantum state to be full Schmidt rank. We show that this reduction step can also be captured with local dilation. We first need a lemma which is folklore.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{A}},Y\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{B}}
and
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
be such that
(X\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi=(I_{d_{A}}\otimes Y)\psi
. Then,
\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi)
is an invariant subspace of
X
, and
\mathrm{supp}_{B}(\psi)
is an invariant subspace of
Y
.

Proof.

If
\psi=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}\otimes\eta_{i}
is a Schmidt decomposition, then the matrix
D=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}\eta_{i}^{T}
satisfies
\mathrm{vec}(D)=\psi
. Note that
\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi)
is the column space of
D
. To show that
\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi)
is invariant under
X
, it suffices to show that
X\xi_{i}\in\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi)
for all
i\in[r]
. Using Identity (
2.3
), we see that
(X\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi=(I_{d_{A}}\otimes Y)\psi
is equivalent to
XD=DY^{T}
. Thus, for
i\in[r]
,

\displaystyle X\xi_{i}=X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha_{i}^{-1}D\overline{\eta}_{i}}}\right)=\alpha_{i}^{-1}(XD)(\overline{\eta}_{i})=\alpha_{i}^{-1}(DY^{T})(\overline{\eta}_{i})\in\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi),

as required. The invariance of
\mathrm{supp}_{B}(\psi)
under
Y
follows similarly. ∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
p=(p(i,j|v,w))\in C_{q}^{s}(n,k)
be a synchronous quantum correlation. Let
\mathscr{S}=(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\{E_{v,i}\},\{F_{w,j}\})
be a strategy inducing
p
. Then, there exists a strategy
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}=(\psi^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{r},\{E_{v,i}^{\prime}\},\{F_{w,j}^{\prime}\})
where
\psi^{\prime}
is of full Schmidt rank and
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
is a local dilation of
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Consider a Schmidt decomposition
\psi=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\alpha_{l}\xi_{l}\otimes\eta_{l}
, and let
\iota_{A}\colon\mathbb{C}^{r}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}
and
\iota_{B}\colon\mathbb{C}^{r}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
be isometries given by
\iota_{A}=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\xi_{l}e_{l}^{*}
and
\iota_{B}=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\eta_{l}e_{l}^{*}
. Then, the operators

(4.5)

\displaystyle E_{v,i}^{\prime}=\iota_{A}^{*}E_{v,i}\iota_{A},\qquad F_{w,j}^{\prime}=\iota_{B}^{*}F_{w,j}\iota_{B},

are themselves positive, and moreover, for each
v
and
w
, the sets
\{E_{v,i}^{\prime}:i\in[k]\}
and
\{F_{w,j}^{\prime}:j\in[k]\}
form POVMs. With
\psi^{\prime}=(\iota_{A}^{*}\otimes\iota_{B}^{*})\psi
, these above operators constitute the strategy
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
, and clearly
\psi^{\prime}
is of full Schmidt rank.

To see that
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
is a local dilation of
\mathscr{S}
, set
\mathcal{K}_{A}=\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{B}=\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
and define isometries
V_{A}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\to\mathbb{C}^{r}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}
and
V_{B}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}\to\mathbb{C}^{r}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
, by

\displaystyle V_{A}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\iota_{A}^{*}(\xi)\otimes\xi_{1}+e_{1}\otimes(I_{d_{A}}-\iota_{A}\iota_{A}^{*})(\xi),

\displaystyle V_{B}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\iota_{B}^{*}(\eta)\otimes\eta_{1}+e_{1}\otimes(I_{d_{B}}-\iota_{B}\iota_{B}^{*})(\eta),

where
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
. By Corollary
3.6
(a), we have
(E_{v,i}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi=(I_{d_{A}}\otimes F_{v,i})\psi
for all
v,i
. Then using Lemma
4.8
,
\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi)
is invariant under each
E_{v,i}
, and
\mathrm{supp}_{B}(\psi)
is invariant under each
F_{w,j}
, and since
\iota_{A}\iota_{A}^{*}
is the projection onto
\mathrm{supp}_{A}(\psi)
and
\iota_{B}\iota_{B}^{*}
is the projection onto
\mathrm{supp}_{B}(\psi)
, we have

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{w,j})\psi=(\iota_{A}\otimes\iota_{B})^{*}((E_{v,i}\otimes F_{w,j})\psi)\otimes(e_{1}\otimes e_{1}).

But then, using the invariance property,

\displaystyle(\iota_{A}\otimes\iota_{B})^{*}((E_{v,i}\otimes F_{w,j})\psi)

\displaystyle=(\iota_{A}^{*}\otimes\iota_{B}^{*})(\iota_{A}\iota_{A}^{*}\otimes\iota_{B}\iota_{B}^{*})(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{w,j})\psi

\displaystyle=(\iota_{A}^{*}\otimes\iota_{B}^{*})(\iota_{A}\iota_{A}^{*}\otimes\iota_{B}\iota_{B}^{*})(E_{v,i}\otimes F_{w,j})(\iota_{A}\iota_{A}^{*}\otimes\iota_{B}\iota_{B}^{*})\psi

\displaystyle=((E_{v,i}^{\prime}\otimes F_{w,j}^{\prime})\psi^{\prime}),

as desired. ∎

5.
Correlations from projections adding up to scalar times identity

We are interested in self-testing projections which sum up to (some specific) scalar times the identity. Such kind of projections have been studied in detail in
[
KRS02
]
, and we collect some of the material from there as needed.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Sigma_{n}
be the set of all scalars
x
such that there exist
n
projections
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
(possibly infinite dimensional), such that
R_{1}+\dots+R_{n}=xI_{\mathcal{H}}
. For
n\leq 3
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=\{0,1\},\qquad\Sigma_{2}=\{0,1,2\},\qquad\Sigma_{3}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1,\frac{3}{2},2,3}}\right\}.

For
n\geq 4
, the set
\Sigma_{n}
is described in
[
KRS02
]
. In particular, they show that the set
\Sigma_{4}
is countably infinite; whereas for all
n\geq 5
, the set
\Sigma_{n}
is uncountable with a nondegenerate interval subset. For our purpose, we do not require the full description of the set
\Sigma_{n}
. The following theorem is a distillation of Proposition 2, Theorem 3, and Theorem 4 from the aforementioned work.

For a definition of a universal C
∗
-algebra we refer the reader to Appendix
A
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
n=3
, set
\Lambda_{3}=\{\frac{3}{2}\}
; and for
n\geq 4
, set
\Lambda_{n}=\{x_{l}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
with the sequence defined recursively as follows:
x_{0}=0
, and for all
l\geq 1
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle x_{l}=1+\frac{1}{n-1-x_{l-1}}.

Then
\Lambda_{n}\subseteq\Sigma_{n}
for all
n\geq 3
. Moreover, if
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra with generators
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
and relations given as follows (here
1
is the unit of the algebra)

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\coloneqq\mathrm{C}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\,\big|\,r_{v}=r_{v}^{*}=r_{v}^{2},\;\forall\;v\in[n],\sum_{v=1}^{n}r_{v}=x1}}\right\rangle,

then for
x\in\Lambda_{n}
(
n\geq 3
), there is a unique irreducible representation (up to unitary equivalence) of the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
. Furthermore, this representation is of dimension
d
, where
x=\frac{b}{d}
is in its lowest terms.

The C
∗
-algebra
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
has the universal property that whenever there exist projections
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=xI_{\mathcal{H}}
, then there exists a representation
\pi\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\pi(r_{v})=R_{v}
for all
v\in[n]
.

If
x\in\{x_{l}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
with
x=\frac{b}{d}
in its lowest terms, the uniqueness property of
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
implies that if
\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{P}_{n}
are projections in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
satisfying
\widetilde{P}_{1}+\dots+\widetilde{P}_{n}=xI_{d}
, and
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
are projections such that
R_{1}+\dots+R_{n}=xI_{\mathcal{H}}
, then there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and a unitary
U\colon\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathcal{K}
such that
UR_{v}U^{*}=\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}}
for all
v\in[n]
.

We are now ready to define the synchronous quantum correlations that we will be self-testing.

Definition 5.2
.

Fix
n\geq 3
and
x\in\Lambda_{n}
with
x=\frac{b}{d}
in its lowest terms. Let
\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{P}_{n}
be projections in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
(by Theorem
5.1
) such that
\widetilde{P}_{1}+\dots+\widetilde{P}_{n}=xI_{d}
. Consider the synchronous correlation
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}=(\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(i,j|v,w))\in C^{s}_{q}(n,2)
induced by the strategy

(5.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widetilde{P}_{v},I_{d}-\widetilde{P}_{v}}}\right\}_{v=1}^{n},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widetilde{P}_{v}^{T},I_{d}-\widetilde{P}_{v}^{T}}}\right\}_{v=1}^{n}}}\right),

where
\varphi_{d}
is the maximally entangled state. We shall refer to this strategy as the
canonical strategy
for
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
.

We shall use the uniqueness result from Theorem
5.1
to show that the strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
given in Definition
5.2
is essentially the unique strategy which induces the correlation
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
—more precisely,
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
self-tests the strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
. This result, together with robustness, will be the focus of next section. First we show how the uniqueness result provides an explicit description of the C
∗
-algebra
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
x\in\Lambda_{n}
with
x=\frac{b}{d}
in lowest terms. Then
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\simeq\mathbb{M}_{d}
. In particular, if
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
are projections such that
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=xI_{d}
, then
\{R_{v}\}_{v=1}^{n}
generate
\mathbb{M}_{d}
as a
C^{*}
-algebra.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
, there is an irreducible representation
\pi\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. As
\pi
is irreducible,
\pi
must be surjective (using that
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is finite-dimensional)
[
EGH+11
, Theorem 3.2.2]
. We will show that
\pi
is injective and hence is an isomorphism. If this is not the case, then
\ker(\pi)
is a non-zero C
∗
-algebra and hence admits an irreducible representation
\phi_{0}\colon\ker(\pi)\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
on a (possibly infinite-dimensional) Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

[
Mur90
, Theorem 5.1.12]
. Then
\phi_{0}
extends to an irreducible representation
\phi\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})

[
Mur90
, Theorem 5.1.13]
which is necessarily not unitarily equivalent to
\pi
since
\ker(\pi)\neq\ker(\phi)
. This contradicts the uniqueness of the irreducible representation of
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
and hence shows that
\pi
is injective.
∎

We now collect some further consequences of the uniqueness property in Theorem
5.1
. The following lemma tells us that the projections
\{R_{v}\}_{v=1}^{n}
obtained through a representation of
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
have the same trace and are “symmetrically distributed”.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
x\in\Lambda_{n}
with
x=\frac{b}{d}
in its lowest terms. Let
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be projections such that
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=xI_{d}
. Then,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{d}(R_{v}R_{w})=\begin{cases}\frac{x}{n}&\text{ if }v=w,\\
\frac{x(x-1)}{n(n-1)}&\text{ if }v\neq w.\end{cases}

More generally, if
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{k}
(for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
) are projections such that
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=xI_{k}
, then
\tr(R_{v})
is constant for all
v\in[n]
, and
\tr(R_{v}R_{w})
is constant for all
v\neq w
.

Proof.

Since
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=xI_{d}
, by the universal property of
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
, there exists a representation
\pi\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that
\pi(r_{v})=R_{v}
for all
v\in[n]
. By Theorem
5.1
this representation is unique (up to unitary equivalence) and irreducible.

Now, choose a pair
(v,w)
with
v\neq w
and define a new representation
\sigma_{v,w}\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{v,w}(r_{k})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}R_{k}&\text{ if }k\neq v\text{ and }k\neq w,\\
R_{w}&\text{ if }k=v,\\
R_{v}&\text{ if }k=w.\\
\end{cases}

By the uniqueness of the irreducible representation
\pi
(Theorem
5.1
), there exists a unitary
U\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that
\sigma_{v,w}(r_{k})=U\pi(r_{k})U^{*}
for all
k
. But then,

\displaystyle\tr(R_{v})=\tr(\sigma_{v,w}(r_{w}))=\tr(U\pi(r_{w})U^{*})=\tr(\pi(r_{w}))=\tr(R_{w}).

Additionally, since
R_{1}+\dots+R_{n}=xI_{d}
, it follows that
\mathrm{tr}_{d}(R_{v})=\frac{x}{n}
for all
v\in[n]
.

Similarly, one can show that
\tr(R_{v}R_{w})
is constant for all
v\neq w
. Moreover, squaring
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=xI_{d}
, and taking normalized trace yields that
\mathrm{tr}_{d}(R_{v}R_{w})=\frac{x(x-1)}{n(n-1)}
, for all
v\neq w
.

For the final statement, let
\pi\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{k}
be the representation given by
\pi(r_{v})=R_{v}
for all
v\in[n]
. If
\pi_{0}\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{d}
is the unique irreducible representation, then
d
divides
k
and
\pi
is unitarily equivalent to
\pi_{0}^{\oplus(k/d)}
. So,
\mathrm{Tr}(R_{v}R_{w})=k\mathrm{tr}_{d}(\pi_{0}(r_{v}r_{w}))
for all
v,w\in[n]
. The result follows from the first part of the lemma.
∎

Remark 5.5

(The correlation table for
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
)
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
x\in\Lambda_{n}
with
x=\frac{b}{d}
in lowest terms. Let
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
be the quantum correlation as in Definition
5.2
. Then,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,w)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T}}}\right)\varphi_{d}},{\varphi_{d}}}}\right\rangle=\mathrm{tr}_{d}(\widetilde{P}_{v}\widetilde{P}_{w})=\begin{cases}\frac{x}{n}&\text{ if }v=w,\\
\frac{x(x-1)}{n(n-1)}&\text{ if }v\neq w,\end{cases}

where the second equality follows from Identity (
2.5
) and the third equality follows from Lemma
5.4
. Note that with these values of
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,w)
, all other values of
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(i,j|v,w)
can be deduced using the synchronous condition. Indeed, for all
v\neq w
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,2|v,w)=\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(2,1|v,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{n}-\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,w)\text{ and}

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(2,2|v,w)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2x}{n}+\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,w),

and for
v=w
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,2|v,v)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(2,1|v,v)=0\text{ and}

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(2,2|v,v)

\displaystyle=1-\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,v).

For
n\geq 3
and
x=\frac{n}{n-1}\in\Lambda_{n}
, Lemma
5.4
implies that
\tr(R_{v})=1
for projections
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{n-1}
satisfying
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=\frac{n}{n-1}I_{n-1}
. Thus, each
R_{v}
is the projection onto the span of some unit vector
\xi_{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
. The following corollary is then straightforward to verify.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Projections
\{R_{v}\}_{v=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{n-1}
which sum up to

centered at the origin. For instance, in dimension

\displaystyle\xi_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\

Then the projections

are onto the span of

(5.6)

\displaystyle R_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

For other

Finally, we require a result on the maximum eigenvalue of a certain matrix obtained from projections adding up to a scalar times identity.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
x\in\Lambda_{n}
with
x=\frac{b}{d}
in lowest terms. Let
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be projections such that
\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}=xI_{d}
. If
N=\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}\otimes R_{v}^{T}
, then
x
is the largest eigenvalue of
N
, and further, the eigenspace corresponding to
x
is the one-dimensional space spanned by the maximally entangled state
\varphi_{d}
.

Proof.

With
D=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}I_{d}
, we have
\mathrm{vec}(D)=\varphi_{d}
. Using Identity (
2.3
),

\displaystyle N\varphi_{d}=N\mathrm{vec}(D)=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathrm{vec}(R_{v}DR_{v})=x\mathrm{vec}(D)=x\varphi_{d}.

Thus
\varphi_{d}
is an eigenvector of
N
with eigenvalue
x
.

Since
N\geq 0
, the largest eigenvalue of
N
is
\|N\|
. Since

\displaystyle N=\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}\otimes R_{v}^{T}\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}\otimes I_{d}=xI_{d}\otimes I_{d}

it follows that
\|N\|\leq x
. Then
\|N\|=\sup_{\|\xi\|=1}|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{N\xi},{\xi}}}\right\rangle|
coupled with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{N\varphi_{d}},{\varphi_{d}}}}\right\rangle=x
, yields
\|N\|=x
.

We now show that the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue
x
is one-dimensional. Let
\varphi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a unit vector such that
N\varphi=x\varphi
. Let
B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be such that
\mathrm{vec}(B)=\varphi
. Then
N\varphi=x\varphi
is equivalent to
N\mathrm{vec}(B)=x\mathrm{vec}(B)
, which in turn, using Identity (
2.3
), is equivalent to

(5.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}BR_{v}=xB.

Now consider the unital quantum channel
\Phi\colon\mathbb{M}_{d}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}
defined by

(5.8)

\displaystyle X\mapsto\frac{1}{x}\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}XR_{v},\qquad X\in\mathbb{M}_{d}.

Then Equation (
5.7
) implies that
B
is a fixed point of the quantum channel. By
[
Wat18
, Theorem 4.25]
, it follows that
R_{v}B=BR_{v}
for all
v\in[n]
. By Lemma
5.3
, the projections
\{R_{v}\}_{v=1}^{n}
generate the whole of
\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Hence,
B
commutes with every element of
\mathbb{M}_{d}
, and we must have
B=\lambda I_{d}
, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. In other words,
\varphi=\mathrm{vec}(B)=(\lambda\sqrt{d})\varphi_{d}
, as required. ∎

6.
Robust self-testing of projections adding up to scalar times identity

We now set out to prove that the correlation
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
of Definition
5.2
robustly self-tests the canonical strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
therein.

We first show in Theorem

induces the correlation
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
then
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
must be a local dilation of
\mathscr{S}
. For this, we prove that the measurement operators of
\mathscr{S}
form a representation of the algebra
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
. Then invoking the irreducibility assumption we get isometries relating the strategy
\mathscr{S}
in hand with the canonical one.

To handle the robust case, we first formally define a suitable notion of an “approximate” representation. Then we show that if we have a strategy which induces a correlation within
\epsilon
-distance of
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
, then we get an “approximate” representation of the relation
r_{1}+\dots+r_{n}=x1
. Finally, to relate the approximate strategy with the canonical one, we establish an analogue of Gowers–Hatami Theorem for the C
∗
-algebra
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let

induced by the strategy

in Definition

\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{S}=(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\{P_{v},I_{d_{A}}-P_{v}\}_{v=1}^{n},\{Q_{w},I_{d_{B}}-Q_{w}\}_{w=1}^{n})

is of full Schmidt rank

and

are projections for all

Set

and let
\rho_{A}
and

be its reduced density matrices. Clearly,

of Corollary

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{P},{I_{r}}}}\right\rangle_{\rho_{A}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(P_{v}\otimes I_{r})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(P_{v}\otimes Q_{v})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,v)=x,

and

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{P},{P}}}\right\rangle_{\rho_{A}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,w=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(P_{v}P_{w}\otimes I_{r})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{v,w=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(P_{v}\otimes I_{r})\psi},{(P_{w}\otimes I_{r})\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,w=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(P_{v}\otimes I_{r})\psi},{(I_{r}\otimes Q_{w})\psi}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{v,w=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(P_{v}\otimes Q_{w})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{v,w=1}^{n}\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,w)=n\frac{x}{n}+n(n-1)\frac{x(x-1)}{n(n-1)}=x^{2}.

Therefore,

. Similarly, we can show that

. Then, we have

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(P_{v}\otimes Q_{v})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{v}^{T}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{A}}\otimes I_{\mathcal{K}_{B}})U\psi},{U\psi}}}\right\rangle

\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
is a full matrix algebra by Theorem
5.3
.
Define
\widetilde{\pi}_{k}\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\to\mathbb{M}_{r_{k}}
by
\widetilde{\pi}_{k}=\pi_{k}\circ\widetilde{\theta}
. Then
\widetilde{\pi}_{k}
is unital and completely positive. For each
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
q(\widetilde{\theta}(r_{i}))=\theta(r_{i})=q(A_{i})
, and hence
\widetilde{\theta}(r_{i})-A_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}_{\tau}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widetilde{\pi}_{k}(r_{i})-E_{i,k}}}\right\|_{\rho_{k}}^{2}=\tau\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\theta}(r_{i})-A_{i}}}\right)^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\theta}(r_{i})-A_{i}}}\right)}}\right)=0.

For each
k\geq 1
, as
\widetilde{\pi}_{k}
is a unital, completely positive map between finite-dimensional C
∗
-algebras, Stinespring’s Theorem
[
Pau02
, Theorem 4.1]
produces a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, a
*
-homomorphism
\sigma_{k}\colon\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k})
, and an isometry
W_{k}\colon\mathbb{C}^{r_{k}}\to\mathcal{H}_{k}
such that
W_{k}^{*}\sigma_{k}(a)W_{k}=\widetilde{\pi}_{k}(a)
for all
a\in\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
. In particular, for each
k\geq 1
and for all
i\in[n]
, we have

(6.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|W_{k}^{*}\sigma_{k}(r_{i})W_{k}-E_{i,k}}}\right\|_{\rho_{k}}=0.

Since
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}
has a unique irreducible representation
\mathscr{P}_{n,x}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}
given by
r_{i}\mapsto\widetilde{P}_{i}
,
for each
k\geq 1
, there are a natural number
s_{k}\geq 1
and unitary
U_{k}\colon\mathcal{H}_{k}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{s_{k}}
such that
U_{k}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{i}\otimes I_{s_{k}})U_{k}=\sigma_{k}(r_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
.
Define
V_{k}=U_{k}W_{k}\colon\mathbb{C}^{r_{k}}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{s_{k}}
. Then
V_{k}
is an isometry, and for all
i\in[n]
, we can rewrite Equation (
6.9
) as

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\big\|V_{k}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{i}\otimes I_{s_{k}})V_{k}-E_{i,k}\big\|_{\rho_{k}}=0.

In particular, for some large
k\geq 1
, we have
\big\|V_{k}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{i}\otimes I_{s_{k}})V_{k}-E_{i,k}\big\|_{\rho_{k}}\leq\epsilon
for all
i\in[n]
, which is a contradiction to Inequality (
6.8
).
∎

Remark 6.6
.

The proof of Theorem
6.5
is by contradiction and therefore the relation between
\epsilon
and
\delta
is not known. To find the explicit dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
is left as an open problem.

With the analogue of the Gowers–Hatami Theorem in hand, we now proceed to prove the claimed robustness result. We first need three intermediate lemmas.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be a positive matrix having at least two distinct eigenvalues and let
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\dots>\lambda_{l}\geq 0
be the distinct eigenvalues of
A
. Let
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a unit vector such that
\lambda_{1}-\epsilon\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{A\xi},{\xi}}}\right\rangle
for some
\epsilon\geq 0
. If
Q_{1}
is the projection onto the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
, then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{1}\xi}}\right\|^{2}\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}.

Proof.

By the Spectral Theorem,
A=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}
where
Q_{i}
be the projection onto the eigenspace of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
. Then,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{A\xi},{\xi}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{i}\xi}}\right\|^{2}
, and hence

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{i}\xi}}\right\|^{2}=\lambda_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{i}\xi}}\right\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{i}\xi}}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{i=2}^{l}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{i})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{i}\xi}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\sum_{i=2}^{l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{i}\xi}}\right\|^{2}=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Q_{1}\xi}}\right\|^{2}}}\right).

Rearranging this inequality gives our desired inequality. ∎

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}
be a unit vector and let
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{A}}
and
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{B}}
. Let
\rho_{A},\rho_{B}
be the reduced density matrices of the density matrix
\rho=\psi\psi^{*}
. Then

(6.10)

\displaystyle|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(X_{1}\otimes Y_{1}-X_{2}\otimes Y_{2})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|_{\rho_{A}}\|Y_{2}^{*}\|_{\rho_{B}}+\|Y_{1}-Y_{2}\|_{\rho_{B}}\|X_{1}^{*}\|_{\rho_{A}},

and

(6.11)

\displaystyle\|(X_{1}\otimes Y_{1}-X_{2}\otimes Y_{2})\psi\|

\displaystyle\leq\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|_{\rho_{A}}\|Y_{2}\|+\|Y_{1}-Y_{2}\|_{\rho_{B}}\|X_{1}\|.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality and Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(X_{1}\otimes Y_{1}-X_{2}\otimes Y_{2})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(I_{d_{A}}\otimes(Y_{1}-Y_{2}))\psi},{(X_{1}^{*}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi}}}\right\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{((X_{1}-X_{2})\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi},{(I_{d_{A}}\otimes Y_{2}^{*})\psi}}}\right\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|(I_{d_{A}}\otimes(Y_{1}-Y_{2}))\psi\|\|(X_{1}^{*}\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi\|

\displaystyle\qquad+\|((X_{1}-X_{2})\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi\|\|(I_{d_{A}}\otimes Y_{2}^{*})\psi\|

\displaystyle=\|Y_{1}-Y_{2}\|_{\rho_{B}}\|X_{1}^{*}\|_{\rho_{A}}+\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|_{\rho_{A}}\|Y_{2}^{*}\|_{\rho_{B}},

where we used identities (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) in the last equation.

The second claimed inequality is derived as follows:

\displaystyle\|(X_{1}\otimes Y_{1}-X_{2}\otimes Y_{2})\psi\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(X_{1}\otimes(Y_{1}-Y_{2}))\psi\|+\|((X_{1}-X_{2})\otimes Y_{2})\psi\|

\displaystyle\leq\|X_{1}\otimes I_{d_{B}}\|\|(I_{d_{A}}\otimes(Y_{1}-Y_{2}))\psi\|

\displaystyle\qquad+\|I_{d_{A}}\otimes Y_{2}\|\|((X_{1}-X_{2})\otimes I_{d_{B}})\psi\|

\displaystyle=\|X_{1}\|\|Y_{1}-Y_{2}\|_{\rho_{B}}+\|Y_{2}\|\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|_{\rho_{A}},

where the first inequality follows from the triangle inequality, and the second follows from the definition of the operator norm. ∎

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and let
P\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be a projection. Suppose
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|^{2}}}\right|\leq\epsilon_{1}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi-P\eta}}\right\|\leq\epsilon_{2}
for some
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\geq 0
. Then

(6.12)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi-\eta}}\right\|\leq\epsilon_{2}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|}}\right)\epsilon_{2}}.

Proof.

Let
P^{\perp}=I_{d}-P
. Then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P\eta}}\right\|^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P^{\perp}\eta}}\right\|^{2}
, and thus

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P^{\perp}\eta}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P\eta}}\right\|^{2}\leq\epsilon_{1}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P\eta}}\right\|^{2}=\epsilon_{1}+(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P\eta}}\right\|)(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P\eta}}\right\|)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{1}+(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P\eta}}\right\|)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi-P\eta}}\right\|\leq\epsilon_{1}+(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|)\epsilon_{2}.

Thus,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P^{\perp}\eta}}\right\|\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}+(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P\eta}}\right\|)\epsilon_{2}},
using which in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi-\eta}}\right\|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\xi-P\eta}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|P^{\perp}\eta}}\right\|
proves the lemma. ∎

Finally, we show that the correlation
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}\in C_{q}^{s}(n,2)
as in Definition
5.2
robustly self-tests the strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
mentioned therein.

Theorem 6.10

(Main result)
.

Let

and

. The synchronous correlation
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}\in C_{q}^{s}(n,2)
induced by the strategy
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
in Definition

\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
robustly.

Proof.

Theorem
6.1
states that
\widetilde{p}_{n,x}
self-tests
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
. We must show that for all
\epsilon>0
, there is a
\delta>0
such that if
p\in C_{q}(n,2)
is a correlation induced by a strategy
\mathscr{S}
and
\|p-\widetilde{p}_{n,x}\|_{1}\leq\delta
, then
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a local
\epsilon
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}
. Fix

.

With the notation of Definition
5.2
, let

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{v}^{T}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{M}_{d}

and note that
\widetilde{N}
is positive. By Lemma
5.7
,
x
is the largest eigenvalue of
\widetilde{N}
, and the eigenspace corresponding to

. In particular,
\widetilde{N}
has more than one eigenvalue—let
\lambda_{2}\geq 0
be the second largest eigenvalue of
\widetilde{N}
.

Let
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
be such that
\epsilon^{\prime}<(x-\lambda_{2})/(2n+1)
and

(6.13)

\displaystyle 2\epsilon^{\prime}+\beta+\sqrt{5\epsilon^{\prime}+4\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}+2\beta}\leq\epsilon\qquad\text{where}\qquad\beta\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{2(2n+1)\epsilon^{\prime}}{x-\lambda_{2}}}.

Apply Theorem
6.5
to produce
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that Condition (
6.7
) holds with
\delta^{\prime}
and
\epsilon^{\prime}
in place of
\delta
and
\epsilon
. Then define

(6.14)

\displaystyle\delta\coloneqq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\epsilon^{\prime},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{n+1}}}\right)^{4},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2m}}}\right)^{2}}}\right\}>0.

Let
p\in C_{q}(n,2)
be a correlation with
\|p-\widetilde{p}_{n,x}\|_{1}\leq\delta
and let

(6.15)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}=\big(\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}},\{E_{v},I_{d_{A}}-E_{v}\}_{v=1}^{n},\{F_{w},I_{d_{B}}-F_{w}\}_{w=1}^{n}\big)

be a strategy inducing
p
. We will show
\widetilde{\mathscr{S}}
is a local
\epsilon
-dilation of
\mathscr{S}
.

Let
\rho=\psi\psi^{*}\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{M}_{d_{B}}
and let
\rho_{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{A}}
and
\rho_{B}\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{B}}
be the reduced density matrices of
\rho
. By Lemma
6.3
,
(E_{1},\ldots,E_{n})
is a
(C\delta^{\frac{1}{4}},\rho_{A})
-representation of the relations given in (
5.2
), where

C\coloneqq\sqrt{n^{2}+(1+2x)\sqrt{\delta}}.

As
x\leq n
, we have
\delta\leq\epsilon^{\prime}<(x-\lambda_{2})/(2n+1)<\frac{1}{2}
. Now, since
x\leq n
and
\delta\leq 1
, we have

C\leq\sqrt{n^{2}+2n+1}=n+1.

Therefore,
C\delta^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq\delta^{\prime}
by the choice of
\delta
. Hence
(E_{1},\ldots,E_{n})
is a
(\delta^{\prime},\rho_{A})
-representation of the relations given in (
5.2
). Therefore, (a) and (b) of Condition
6.7
hold with
(\delta^{\prime},\rho_{A},d_{A})
in place of
(\delta,\rho,r)
. Since

, Lemma
3.7
implies that part (c) of Condition
6.7
also holds.

The conclusion of Condition
6.7
provides
r_{A}\in\mathbb{N}
and an isometry
V_{A}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{r_{A}}
with

(6.16)

\|E_{v}-V_{A}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes I_{r_{A}})V_{A}\|_{\rho_{A}}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\qquad\text{for all $v\in[n]$}.

Similarly, there are
r_{B}\in\mathbb{N}
and an isometry
V_{B}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d_{B}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{r_{B}}
such that

(6.17)

\|F_{v}-V_{B}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{v}^{T}\otimes I_{r_{B}})V_{B}\|_{\rho_{B}}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\qquad\text{for all $v\in[n]$}.

We work towards constructing a quantum state
\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{r_{B}}
such that

(6.18)

(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi\approx_{\epsilon}\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}.

Note that Inequalities (
6.16
) and (
6.17
) together with Lemma
6.8
imply

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\big(E_{v}\otimes F_{w}-(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T}\otimes I_{r_{A}}\otimes I_{r_{B}})(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\big)\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle}}\right|

(6.19)

\displaystyle\leq\|E_{v}-V_{A}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes I_{r_{A}})V_{A}\|_{\rho_{A}}+\|F_{w}-V_{B}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T}\otimes I_{r_{B}})V_{B}\|_{\rho_{B}}\leq 2\epsilon^{\prime}.

Let
N=\sum_{v=1}^{n}E_{v}\otimes F_{v}\in\mathbb{M}_{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{M}_{d_{B}}
. Taking
v=w
in Inequality (
6.19
) and summing over
v\in[n]
yields

(6.20)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{N\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(\widetilde{N}\otimes I_{r_{A}}\otimes I_{r_{B}})(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\big)\psi},{(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi}}}\right\rangle}}\right|\leq 2n\epsilon^{\prime}.

Note that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{N\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}p(1,1|v,v)
. Since
\|p-\widetilde{p}_{n,x}\|_{1}\leq\delta\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\sum_{v=1}^{n}\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,v)=x
, Inequality
6.20
implies

(6.21)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(\widetilde{N}\otimes I_{r_{A}}\otimes I_{r_{B}})(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\big)\psi},{(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi}}}\right\rangle\geq x-(2n+1)\epsilon^{\prime}.

Recall that by Lemma
5.7
,
x
is the largest eigenvalue of
\widetilde{N}
, and hence also of
\widetilde{N}\otimes I_{r_{A}}\otimes I_{r_{B}}
. Let
Q
be the projection on to the eigenspace of
\widetilde{N}\otimes I_{r_{A}}\otimes I_{r_{B}}
corresponding to
x
. Equation (
6.21
) and Lemma
6.7
yield

(6.22)

\alpha\coloneqq\|Q(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi\|\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{(2n+1)\epsilon^{\prime}}{x-\lambda_{2}}}}\right)^{1/2}>0,

where the last inequality holds by the choice of
\epsilon^{\prime}
. Then
\alpha^{-1}Q(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi
is a unit vector in the eigenspace of
\widetilde{N}\otimes I_{r_{A}}\otimes I_{r_{B}}
corresponding to
x
. As the eigenspace of
\widetilde{N}
is spanned by
\varphi_{d}
, there is a unique quantum state
\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r_{A}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{r_{B}}
such that
Q(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi=\alpha\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}
.

To simplify notation, let
\psi^{\prime}=(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi
, and note that

\displaystyle\big\|\psi^{\prime}-\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{-1}Q\psi^{\prime}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(1-Q)\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}+\big|1-\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{-1}\big|^{2}\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}+(\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{-1}-1)^{2}\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}+1-2\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|+\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2(1-\|Q\psi^{\prime}\|).

Therefore,

\|(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi-\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\|\leq\sqrt{2(1-\alpha)}\leq\sqrt{2(1-\alpha^{2})}.

Combining this inequality with Inequality (
6.22
),

(6.23)

\|(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\psi-\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(2n+1)\epsilon^{\prime}}{x-\lambda_{2}}}=\beta.

In particular, Estimate (
6.18
) holds.

We now work to show

(6.24)

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi\approx_{\epsilon}\big((\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T})\varphi_{d}\big)\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}

for all
v,w\in[n]
. Fix
v,w\in[n]
and, to declutter the notation, define

V\coloneqq V_{A}\otimes V_{B}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{v,w}\coloneqq\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T}\otimes I_{r_{A}}\otimes I_{r_{B}}.

By Inequalities (
6.11
), (
6.16
), and (
6.17
), we have

\|V^{*}P_{v,w}V\psi-(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi\|\leq\|V_{A}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes I_{r_{A}})V_{A}-E_{v}\|_{\rho_{A}}+\|V_{B}^{*}(\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T}\otimes I_{r_{B}})V_{B}-F_{w}\|_{\rho_{B}}\leq 2\epsilon^{\prime}.

Since
V
is an isometry, this implies

\|VV^{*}P_{v,w}V\psi-V(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi\|\leq 2\epsilon^{\prime}.

Combining this with Inequality (
6.23
) and the triangle inequality, we have

(6.25)

\|V(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi-VV^{*}P_{v,w}(\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}})\|\leq 2\epsilon^{\prime}+\beta.

Note that

(6.26)

\|P_{v,w}(\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}})\|^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T}}}\right)\varphi_{d}},{\varphi_{d}}}}\right\rangle=\widetilde{p}_{n,x}(1,1|v,v).

Further, since
p(1,1|v,w)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle{(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi},{\psi}}}\right\rangle
, Inequality (
6.10
) and parts (d) and (e) of Lemma
3.5
imply

(6.27)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\|(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi\|^{2}-p(1,1|v,w)}}\right|\leq\|E_{v}-E_{v}^{2}\|_{\rho_{A}}+\|F_{w}-F_{w}^{2}\|_{\rho_{B}}\leq 4\sqrt{\delta}\leq 4\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}.

Since
V
is an isometry and
\|p-\widetilde{p}_{n,x}\|_{1}\leq\delta\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
, Equation (
6.26
) and Inequality (
6.27
) imply

(6.28)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\|V(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi\|^{2}-\|P_{v,w}(\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}})\|^{2}}}\right|\leq\epsilon^{\prime}+4\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}.

is a projection, Inequalities (
6.25
) and (

) and Lemma
6.9
imply

\|V(E_{v}\otimes F_{w})\psi-P_{v,w}(\varphi_{d}\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}})\|\leq 2\epsilon^{\prime}+\beta+\sqrt{5\epsilon^{\prime}+4\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}+2\beta}\leq\epsilon,

and this proves Estimate (

). Similarly, for each

\displaystyle\big\|(I_{d_{A}}-E_{v})-V_{A}^{*}\big((I_{d_{A}}-\widetilde{P}_{v})\otimes I_{r_{A}}\big)V_{A}\big\|_{\rho_{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\big\|(I_{d_{B}}-F_{w})-V_{B}^{*}\big((I_{d_{B}}-\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T})\otimes I_{r_{B}}\big)V_{B}\big\|_{\rho_{B}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{\prime},

which follow from Inequalities (
6.16
) and (
6.17
), one can show

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\big(E_{v}\otimes(I_{d_{B}}-F_{w})\big)\psi

\displaystyle\approx_{\epsilon}\Big(\big(\widetilde{P}_{v}\otimes(I_{d}-\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T})\big)\varphi_{d}\Big)\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}},

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\big((I_{d_{A}}-E_{v})\otimes F_{w}\big)\psi

\displaystyle\approx_{\epsilon}\Big(\big((I_{d}-\widetilde{P}_{v})\otimes\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T}\big)\varphi_{d}\Big)\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle(V_{A}\otimes V_{B})\big((I_{d_{A}}-E_{v})\otimes(I_{d_{B}}-F_{w})\big)\psi

\displaystyle\approx_{\epsilon}\Big(\big((I_{d}-\widetilde{P}_{v})\otimes(I_{d}-\widetilde{P}_{w}^{T})\big)\varphi_{d}\big)\otimes\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}.

These last three estimates together with Estimates (
6.18
) and (

is a local

-dilation of
\mathscr{S}
.
∎

Implications

Corollary 7.1
.

Let

Appendix A
A short introduction to C*-algebras

In this appendix we give some overview of C
∗
-algebras that we have used in this article. Some general references on this subject are
[
Mur90
,
Pau02
]
.

A
C
∗
-algebra

\mathfrak{A}
is a subset of the set of bounded linear operators
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

\mathfrak{A}
is a unital (complex) algebra, that is, for
S,T\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we have
S+\lambda T\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\lambda S
, and there is an element
1_{\mathfrak{A}}\in\mathfrak{A}
which serves as the unit element for
\mathfrak{A}
,

(2)

\mathfrak{A}
is
*
-closed, that is, if
S\in\mathfrak{A}
, then its adjoint
S^{*}\in\mathfrak{A}
as well, and,

(3)

\mathfrak{A}
is closed in the operator norm.

Sometimes, such a C
∗
-algebra is also called a
concrete C
∗
-algebra
or a
C
∗
-algebra of operators
to distinguish it from an abstract C
∗
-algebra which we describe next.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a unital Banach algebra, that is,
\mathfrak{A}
is an algebra equipped with a norm
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|.}}\right\|
satisfying
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|ab}}\right\|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a}}\right\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|b}}\right\|
for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
, and
\mathfrak{A}
is complete (every Cauchy sequence converges). Assume that
\mathfrak{A}
has an involution operation
*:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A}
which satisfies
1_{\mathfrak{A}}^{*}=1_{\mathfrak{A}}
and

\displaystyle(a+\lambda b)^{*}=a^{*}+\overline{\lambda}b^{*},\qquad(ab)^{*}=b^{*}a^{*},\qquad\qquad(a^{*})^{*}=a,

for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. We say that
\mathfrak{A}
is an
abstract C
∗
-algebra
if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a^{*}a}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a}}\right\|^{2}
for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

A linear map
\pi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
between two abstract C
∗
-algebras is called a
*
-homomorphism
if it is unital
\pi(1_{\mathfrak{A}})=1_{\mathfrak{B}}
, and
\pi(ab)=\pi(a)\pi(b)
and
\pi(a^{*})=\pi(a)^{*}
for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Observe that
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with the operator norm is an abstract C
∗
-algebra and hence every concrete C
∗
-algebra is an abstract C
∗
-algebra. Conversely, every abstract C
∗
-algebra can be identified with a concrete C
∗
-algebra on some Hilbert space:

Theorem A.1

(Gelfand-Naimark-Segal)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be an abstract C
∗
-algebra. Then, there is some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and an isometric
*
-homomorphism
\pi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Thus, one may identify an abstract C
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
with the concrete C
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}:=\pi(\mathfrak{A})
. A
*
-homomorphism from a C
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
into some
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called a
representation
of
\mathfrak{A}
.

A
state
on a C
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
is a linear functional
\phi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\phi(a^{*}a)\geq 0
for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\phi(1_{\mathfrak{A}})=1
. A state
\phi
is called
tracial
if
\phi(ab)=\phi(ba)
for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
. We let
S(\mathfrak{A})
denote the set of all states on
\mathfrak{A}
. We say that a net of states
(\phi_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
converges to a state
\phi
if
\lim_{\lambda}|\phi_{\lambda}(a)-\phi(a)|=0
for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
, that is, if
\phi_{\lambda}
converges pointwise to
\phi
. This gives rise to a topology on
S(\mathfrak{A})
called the
weak
∗
-topology
, with respect to which
S(\mathfrak{A})
is compact.

Let
\pi_{1}:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
\pi_{2}:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
be two representations of a C
∗
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. We say
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are
unitarily equivalent
if there is some unitary
U:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that
\pi_{2}(a)=U\pi_{1}(a)U^{*}
for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
. A representation
\pi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
irreducible
if there is no invariant closed subspace of
\pi(\mathfrak{A})
apart from
\{0\}
and
\mathcal{H}
.

Give a non-empty set
A\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we define the
C
∗
-algebra generated by
A
, denoted
C^{*}(A)
, to be the smallest
C^{*}
-algebra containing
A
.

Let
G=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
be a non-empty set of
generators
, and let
R=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
be a finite set of
relations
where
p_{i}
is a polynomial in
2n
noncommuting variables
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},a_{1}^{*},\dots,a_{n}^{*}
. Let
\mathfrak{A}
be the free
*
-algebra generated by
G
. Then an
n
-tuple
(T_{1},\dots,T_{n})
in some
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
satsfiying the relations gives rise to a
*
-representation of
\mathfrak{A}
. For
a\in\mathfrak{A}
, define
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a}}\right\|=\sup\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\pi(a)}}\right\|:\pi\text{ is a *-representation of }(G,R)\}
. If
\|a_{i}\|<\infty
for all
i\in[n]
, then
\|\cdot\|
is a C
∗
-seminorm on
\mathfrak{A}
, that is,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a^{*}a}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a}}\right\|^{2}
for all
a\in\mathfrak{A}
. Letting
N=\{a\in\mathfrak{A}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a}}\right\|=0\}
, the completion of

with respect to the induced norm is called the

∗
-algebra
on
(G,R)
, denoted by
C^{*}(G,R)
. The universal property says that whenever there is an

-tuple
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of operators in
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying the relations

\pi:C^{*}(G,R)\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})
with

for all

. For more information on universal C

-algebras we refer the reader to
[
Bla06
, Section II.8.3]
.
