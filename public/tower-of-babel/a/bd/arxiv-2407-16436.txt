Universal Dynamics of a Passive Particle Driven by Brownian Motion
Urna Basu, P. L. Krapivsky, Satya N. Majumdar

Abstract
We investigate the overdamped dynamics of a ‘passive’ particle driven by nonreciprocal interaction with a ‘driver’ Brownian particle. When the interaction between them is short-ranged, the long-time behavior of the driven particle is remarkably universal—the mean-squared displacement (MSD) and the typical position of the driven particle exhibits the same qualitative behaviors independent of the specific form of the potential. In particular, the MSD grows as t 1 / 2 t^{1/2} in one dimension and log ⁡ t \log t in two spatial dimensions. We compute the exact scaling functions for the position distribution in d = 1 d=1 and d = 2 d=2 . These functions are universal when the interaction is short-ranged. For long-ranged interactions, the MSD of the driven particle grows as t ϕ t^{\phi} with exponent ϕ \phi depending on the tail of the potential.



I
Introduction

The stochastic dynamics of
N
interacting particles is a subject of long-standing
interest in statistical physics. This is a hard problem, except for a few
solvable cases mostly in one dimension. Even the simple two body problem
(N=2)
is often
hard. A two-particle system, with reciprocal interactions between them that depend only on their relative coordinate, can be reduced to a simpler one-body problem in an external potential in their relative co-ordinate. However, when the interaction is non-reciprocal, this simple change of coordinates does not work and the problem is nontrivial even for
N=2
. In this paper, we investigate the stochastic dynamics of two particles with a non-reciprocal interaction between them in all dimensions and derive exact results for the position fluctuations at asymptototically late times. We find rich and rather universal behaviors that are independent of the details of the interactions but depend only on the dimension and on whether the nonreciprocal interaction is short-ranged or long-ranged.

Our setup consists of two particles: we call one of them the ‘driver’ particle and the other a ‘passive’ or ‘driven’ particle. We consider a non-reciprocal (one-sided) interaction affecting only the passive particle. We assume that the driver particle performs a standard Brownian motion

[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, while the motion of the passive particle is overdamped and caused by a central force depending on the separation from the Brownian particle. In one dimension, the position
x(t)
of the driver particle and the position
y(t)
of the passive particle evolve according to

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=\eta(t),

(1a)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=f[x(t)-y(t)]\,.

(1b)

Here
\eta(t)
is a Gaussian white noise obeying standard relations,

\langle\eta(t)\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\eta(t_{1})\eta(t_{2})\rangle=2D\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\,.

(2)

Equations (
1a
)-(
1b
) provide a simple continuum model for the motion of a single vacancy in an exclusion process on a lattice. Specifically, a symmetric exclusion process on the square lattice with a single empty site (vacancy) was investigated in Refs.
[
4
,
5
]
. The vacancy performs a random walk, and whenever it occupies a site neighboring to a tagged particle, the tagged particle can hop, exchanging its position with the vacancy. The driver
x
-particle mimics the motion of this vacancy, which interacts with the tagged particle, modeled by
y
, via a short-ranged interaction.

For a more general scenario, the force acting on the passive particle depends only on the separation between the particles. The force is
necessarily potential in one dimension,
f(z)=-\partial_{z}V(z)
. We make a few assumptions about the force, although
many results remain valid in more general situations. We assume that the force
f(z)
is an odd function of
z
.
Equivalently, the potential
V(z)
is an even function of
z
. However, there is no restriction on the sign of the
potential, i.e., it can be either attractive or repulsive.

In higher dimensions, we assume that the force is central with the potential
V(\boldsymbol{r})=V(r)
that depends
only on the separation
\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{x-y}
between the driver and the driven particles.

The behavior of the position of the driver particle is well-known since it performs standard Brownian motion. The goal
is to determine the statistics of the position of the passive particle. For instance, we will explore how the
mean-squared displacement (MSD) grows with time and show that the behavior of the MSD is very different depending on whether the potential is short-ranged or long-ranged. One can thus distinguish between two types of forces
f(r)
where
r
represents the distance between the two particles in general dimensions:

1.

Short-ranged forces
. A short-ranged force
f(r)
is finite everywhere and quickly decays with distance, e.g., faster than any power of
1/r
for
r\gg R
, the typical range of the force.

2.

Long-ranged forces
. A long-ranged force is finite everywhere, but it decays algebraically,

f(r)\simeq\frac{A}{r^{a}}\quad\text{as}\quad r\to\infty,

(3)

with
a>0
. The force is repulsive if
A>0
, while it is attractive if
A<0
.

We focus on short-ranged regular forces (no singular behavior as
r\to 0
) and show that the emerging behaviors of the passive particle at late times are universal, e.g., independent of the details of the potential and whether the force is repulsive or attractive. For long-ranged forces, the behavior may depend on the decay exponent
a
in (
3
) and also on the sign of the amplitude
A
in (
3
), i.e., on whether the force is
asymptotically repulsive or attractive. For sufficiently large
a
, one anticipates the same behavior as for short-ranged forces. In fact, we show that in one dimension, the potentials (
3
) with
a>3/2
are effectively short-ranged, viz., the MSD of the passive particle grows in a universal way independent on the decay exponent
a
and the same as for the short-ranged potentials.

Before going into the details of computations, we first summarize our main results. We start with the case where the force
f(r)
on the driven particle is short-ranged and characterize its position fluctuations at late times. For simplicity we assume that the driven particle is initially at the origin (
\boldsymbol{y}(0)=0
). The symmetry of the force then ensures that the average position of the driven particle does not change with time, i.e.,
\langle\boldsymbol{y}(t)\rangle=0
. We find that the effect of the nonreciprocal interaction is captured by the mean-squared displacement (MSD) which shows a remarkable universal behavior. Irrespective of the specific form of the interaction, the MSD of the passive particle increases indefinitely with time in
d\leq 2
dimensions and saturates when
d>2
:

\sigma_{d}^{2}(t)\equiv\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\sim\left(\frac{V_{0}R}{D}\right)^{2}\times\begin{cases}\sqrt{Dt/R^{2}}&d=1\\
\ln(Dt/R^{2})&d=2\\
O(1)&d>2\end{cases}

(4)

Here
V_{0}
is the typical strength of the short-range potential,
R
is its range, and
y=|\boldsymbol{y}|
. The dimensionless quantity
V_{0}/D
plays the role of the Péclet number.

To appreciate the sub-diffusive growth laws (
4
), let us consider a lattice version in which a driver particle performs a random walk. In one dimension, in the late time limit, the random walker visits
\sim\sqrt{t}
sites
[
6
,
7
]
and hence it frequently returns to the visited sites and, on an average, spends
\sqrt{t}
time in each such site. The passive particle is affected when it is close to the random walker. This leads to a heuristic estimate for the typical displacement of the passive particle

\sum_{1}^{\sqrt{t}}\pm 1\sim t^{1/4},

(5)

which, in turn leads to the
\sqrt{t}
growth of the MSD of the driven particle.

In two dimensions, the random walker visits
t/\ln t
sites
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, so a visited site is visited on average
\ln t
times. The typical displacement is therefore

\sum_{1}^{\ln t}\pm 1\sim\sqrt{\ln t},

(6)

leading to the
\log t
growth of the MSD of the driven particle. For
d>2
, the random walker visits a site a few times and eventually wanders away and never returns
[
6
,
7
]
, so the typical spread of the driven particle remains finite.

It turns out that the complete position distribution of the driven particle is also universal and depends only on the dimension and not on the specific short-ranged potential. In one dimension, the MSD of the passive particle exhibits the same
\sqrt{t}
growth as that of a tagged particle in single-file diffusion
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. The distribution of the displacement of the tagged particle in single-file diffusion is Gaussian
[
13
]
. One could expect the same simple behavior for the displacement of the passive particle, but this is not so. The distribution of the passive particle position
y(t)
acquires a scaling form

P(y,t)\simeq\frac{1}{c_{1}\sigma_{1}(t)}\,{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(z),\qquad z=\frac{y}{c_{1}\sigma_{1}(t)}

(7a)

in the long time limit, with scaling function,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{2}}\Bigg[\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\,_{0}F_{2}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4};-\frac{z^{4}}{256}\right)

(7c)

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2\pi}\,|z|\,_{0}F_{2}\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{5}{4};-\frac{z^{4}}{256}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)\,_{0}F_{2}\left(\frac{5}{4},\frac{3}{2};-\frac{z^{4}}{256}\right)\Bigg].

(7d)

Here

{}_{0}F_{2}(a,b;z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}\,\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\,,

(7e)

is a hypergeometric function where
(a)_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(a+k)}{\Gamma(a)}
is the Pochhammer symbol
[
14
]
. The scaling function (
7d
) is rather complicated but universal, the specific form of the interaction potential only affects the constant
c_{1}
. The function
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(z)
is symmetric in
z
and has the asymptotic behaviors

{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(z)\simeq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi}\Gamma\left[\frac{1}{4}\right]-\frac{|z|}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\quad&z\to 0\\[20.00003pt]
\displaystyle\frac{2^{1/6}}{\sqrt{3\pi}\,z^{1/3}}\,\exp\!\left[-3\left(\frac{z}{4}\right)^{4/3}\right]\quad&z\to\infty.\end{cases}

(8)

Thus the scaling function has a cusp at
z=0
and decays super-exponentially as
z\to\infty
. A plot of this scaling function along with the asymptotic behaviours is shown in Fig.
1
(a).

In two dimensions, the distribution of the passive particle position is asymptotically isotropic, i.e., depends only
on
y=|\boldsymbol{y}|
, and has a scaling form

P(y,t)\simeq\frac{1}{[c_{2}\sigma_{2}(t)]^{2}}\,{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(z),\qquad z=\frac{y}{c_{2}\sigma_{2}(t)}.

(9a)

The scaling function reads,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,K_{0}(z),

(9b)

where
K_{0}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order zero.
The constant
c_{2}
is non-universal, viz., it depends on the specific form of the interaction potential.
The scaling function (
9b
) is again universal and, amusingly, simpler than the scaling function
(
7d
) in one dimension. It has the asymptotic behaviors

{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(z)\simeq\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\ln z+\frac{\ln 2-\gamma_{E}}{2\pi}\quad&z\to 0\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi z}}\,\exp(-z)\quad&z\to\infty,\end{cases}

(10)

where
\gamma_{E}=0.5772\dots
is the Euler constant. Thus the scaling function diverges logarithmically as
z\to 0
, while it decays exponentially for large
z
. A plot of the scaling function is shown in Fig.
1
(b).

We next investigate the scenario where the passive particle is driven by a long-ranged force of the form (
3
). We show that in one dimension, the MSD of the driven particle grows algebraically at late times,

\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\sim t^{\phi}\,.

(11)

The exponent
\phi
is chiefly determined by the decay exponent
a>0
of the force [cf. Eq. (
3
)]

\phi=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&a>\frac{3}{2}\\
2-a&1<a<\frac{3}{2}\\
\frac{2}{a+1}&0<a<1\quad(\text{repulsive})\\
1&0<a<1\quad(\text{attractive})\,.\end{cases}

(12)

The exponent
\phi
undergoes a freezing transition at
a=\frac{3}{2}
: for all
a>\frac{3}{2}
, the exponent has the same value
\phi=\frac{1}{2}
as for short-ranged potentials, while for
1<a<\frac{3}{2}
, the exponent depends on
a
as
\phi=2-a
. In the latter case, the driven particle still moves sub-diffusively but faster than in the case of short-ranged potentials. The scaled position distribution also depends on
a
in this case. At the transition point
a=3/2
, the growth of the MSD of the driven particle is logarithmically faster than that for short-ranged potentials,

\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\sim\sqrt{t}\,(\log t)^{2}\quad\textrm{for}\quad a=3/2.

(13)

The behavior in the
a>1
range remains largely universal, e.g., changing the sign of the potential does not affect the leading asymptotic behavior. However, in the
0<a<1
range, the nature of the potential plays a role, and qualitatively different behaviors emerge depending on whether the force is repulsive or attractive, i.e., whether the amplitude in Eq. (
3
) is
A>0
or
A<0
.

II.1

II
Short-ranged interaction

In this section, we explore the position fluctuations of the passive particle when the interaction potential is
short-ranged. Without loss of generality, we set
x(0)=0
and
y(0)=0
. Integrating Eq. (
1b
), we obtain

\displaystyle y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,f[x(\tau)-y(\tau)]

(14)

For simplicity, we use the one-dimensional notation; it is straightforward to generalize to higher dimensions [see Appendix
A
for details]. For short-ranged potentials, we anticipate that
|x(t)|\gg|y(t)|
. Hence, Eq. (
14
) reduces to a Brownian functional,

\displaystyle y(t)\simeq\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,f[x(\tau)].

(15)

Brownian functionals appear in various contexts both in physics and mathematics, and they are well studied
[
15
,
16
]
. The long-time approximation of the driven particle position being given by a Brownian functional allows us to investigate the nature of fluctuations of
y(t)
.

II.1
Mean-squared displacement

We start with computation of the second moment
\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle
, which is the same as the MSD since
\langle y(t)\rangle=0
due to our conventions about the intial condition. We are interested in the long time behavior, so we employ Eq. (
15
) and obtain

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle=2\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}dt_{1}\,\Big\langle f(x(t_{2}))f(x(t_{1}))\Big\rangle,

(16)

where we have used the symmetry of the two-point correlation of the force
\langle f(x(t_{1}))f(x(t_{2}))\rangle
under the exchange of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. The two-point correlation, for
t_{2}>t_{1}
, reads

\displaystyle\Big\langle f(x(t_{2}))f(x(t_{1}))\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{1}\,f(x_{2})f(x_{1})

(17)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{2}|\boldsymbol{1})\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{1}|\boldsymbol{0}).

We use the shorthand notations
2
=
(x_{2},t_{2})
,
1
=
(x_{1},t_{1})
, and
0
=
(0,0)
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{2}|\boldsymbol{1})
the probability density for the Brownian particle to move from
\boldsymbol{1}
to
\boldsymbol{2}
; similarly,
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{1}|\boldsymbol{0})
is the probability density to move from
\boldsymbol{0}
to
\boldsymbol{1}
. These propagators are Gaussian; e.g.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{2}|\boldsymbol{1})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D(t_{2}-t_{1})}}\,\exp\!{\left[-\frac{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}}{4D(t_{2}-t_{1})}\right]}\,.

(18)

To compute the two-point correlation (
17
), it is convenient to express the above propagator in Fourier space and substitute it in Eq. (
17
). Then, performing the
x
-integrals, we get [see Appendix
A
for the details]

\displaystyle\Big\langle f(x(t_{1}))f(x(t_{2}))\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{dk_{1}}{2\pi}\int\frac{dk_{2}}{2\pi}\tilde{f}(k_{1}-k_{2})\tilde{f}(k_{2})

(19)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp{[-D(k_{1}^{2}t_{2}+k_{2}^{2}(t_{1}-t_{2})]},\qquad

(20)

where
\tilde{f}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,e^{ikz}f(z)
is the Fourier transform of the force
f(z)
. Substituting (
20
) in (
16
), and performing the integrals over
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{D^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{1}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{\tilde{f}(k_{1}-k_{2})\tilde{f}(k_{2})}{k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}(k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2})}

(21)

\displaystyle\times\left[k_{1}^{2}(1-e^{-Dk_{2}^{2}t})-k_{2}^{2}(1-e^{-Dk_{1}^{2}t})\right].~~

(22)

The above expression gives the exact MSD for the process (
15
), but it cannot be evaluated without knowing the explicit form of the force. However, we are primarily interested in the long-time behavior where the process (
15
) provides an asymptotically exact description of the actual evolution of the passive particle. Therefore, we focus on the large-time behavior of Eq. (
22
) where we can advance further. To this end, let us consider the Laplace transform of the second moment,
M_{2}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-st}\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle
. From Eq. (
22
) we then have

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{s}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{1}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{\tilde{f}(k_{1}-k_{2})\tilde{f}(k_{2})}{(Dk_{1}^{2}+s)(Dk_{2}^{2}+s)}.\qquad

(23)

The large-time behavior of the MSD is controlled by the small
s
behavior of its Laplace transform
M_{2}(s)
. The small
s
behavior of the Laplace transform
M_{2}(s)
can be extracted systematically from Eq. (
23
), and it turns out that it is independent of the specific form of
\tilde{f}(k)
. See Appendix
A
for the details of the computation. Here, we only sketch the main steps.

For small
s
, the dominant contribution to the integrals in Eq. (
23
) comes from the region
-\Lambda\leq(k_{1},k_{2})\leq\Lambda
where
\Lambda\sim O(1)
is an ultraviolet cut-off. Consequently, it suffices to consider the small
k
behavior of
\tilde{f}(k)
, which is analytic for a short-ranged force. Hence, for small
k
, we must have,
\tilde{f}(k)=-ia_{1}k+O(k^{2})
where
a_{1}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,zV^{\prime}(z)
contains the information about the specific interaction potential. Using this approximation and performing the integrals in Eq. (
23
), we get

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)\simeq\frac{a_{1}^{2}\Lambda}{\pi(Ds)^{3/2}}

(24)

for small
s
[see Appendix
A
for the details]. The above equation immediately leads to

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq\frac{2a_{1}^{2}\Lambda}{(\pi D)^{3/2}}\,\sqrt{t}

(25)

for short-ranged forces in one dimension. Clearly, the specific form of the potential only affects the numerical factor
a_{1}
, and the asymptotic
\sqrt{t}
growth of the MSD of the driven particle is universal for short-ranged interactions. This sub-diffusive growth law is one of the main results of this paper.

It is straightforward to generalize the above analysis to higher dimensions. The
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
integrals in Eq. (
23
) become
d
-dimensional [see Eq. (
124
)]. For small
s
, the dominating contribution to the
k
-integrals comes again from the small
|k|
regime. For short-ranged rotationally symmetric interactions in two dimensions, we get
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k})=-ia_{2}{\boldsymbol{k}}+O(k^{2})
where
a_{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,z^{2}V^{\prime}(z)
contains the information about the interaction. Using the linear approximation for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k})
, the
k
integrals can be performed [see Appendix
A
]. Expanding the resulting expression for small
s
we arrive at

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)\simeq\frac{a_{2}^{2}}{8\pi^{2}D^{2}}\frac{\log(D/s)}{s},

(26)

which can be inverted to obtain the long-time behavior of the MSD in two dimensions:

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq\frac{a_{2}^{2}}{8\pi^{2}D^{2}}(\gamma_{E}+\log Dt),

(27)

where
\gamma_{E}
denotes the Euler constant. Once again, we find a universal behavior, namely a
\log t
growth of the MSD, in
d=2
.

Following the same analysis, in
d=3
we obtain

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)\simeq\frac{a_{3}^{2}\Lambda^{4}}{6\pi^{4}D^{2}s}+O(s^{-3/2}),

(28)

which, in turn, implies that the MSD eventually reaches a constant value in three dimensions. The same holds for all
d\geq 3
.

II.2
Position distribution

The universal nature of the MSD of the driven particle raises the obvious question of whether similar universal features are manifest in the full position distribution of the driven particle. To answer this question, we investigate the position distribution
P(y,t)
at large-times
t
using two different approaches. Both approaches apply in the long time limit and give exact scaled distributions. Their status is different, however. A more rigorous approach based on the Feynman-Kac formalism is challenging, and we implemented it only in some specific examples of short-ranged potentials in one dimension where we succeeded in computing the distribution explicitly (Sec.
II.3
). The second approach is less rigorous, and we call it a heuristic argument. This approach relies on the first-passage properties of the Brownian particle, and its obvious advantage is that the computations do not depend on the specific form of the
short-ranged potential that affects only a non-universal constant. We implemented the second approach both in one and two dimensions. Whenever both approaches are applicable, they give the same universal scaling behavior. Here, we show how to determine the scaling functions in the realm of the heuristic approach that is less rigorous but applies to arbitrary short-ranged potentials in one and two dimensions.

The position distribution
P(y,t)
of the driven particle is affected when it is close to the position
x(t)
of the Brownian particle. Hence, the displacement of the driven particle can be estimated as

\displaystyle y=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j},

(31)

where
N
denotes the number of times the Brownian particle comes close to the driven particle, and
\eta_{j}
denotes the displacement of the driven particle at each such instance. We have already used a simpler version of the above equation, viz. Eq. (
5
), for a heuristic estimate of the typical displacement of the driven particle. Equation (
31
) also looks like a heuristic estimate. However, the discrete formulation implied by Eq. (
31
) becomes asymptotically exact, and the instances when the interaction is non-negligible become independent. Indeed, the driven particle is much ‘slower’ than the Brownian particle, so it does not move far from its initial position. Hence, one can think about
N
as the number of returns of the Brownian particle to the origin. The potential must be
short-ranged to justify the discrete treatment of interaction instances. Thus the goal is to improve the naive estimate in Eq. (
5
) where we replaced
N
by a typical number of return events. In Eq. (
5
), we also used the simplest estimate
\eta_{j}=\pm 1
. This simplification only misses the non-universal constant
c_{1}
, but we can restore it at the end using the calculation of the MSD presented in Sec.
II.1
.

The above arguments imply that position distribution of the driven particle can then be expressed as

\displaystyle P(y,t)=\sum_{N}P(y|N)P(N|t)

(32)

where
P(y|N)
denotes the probability that the driven particle has a displacement
y
, given
N
return events, and
P(N|t)
denotes the conditional probability that there are
N
returns of the Brownian particle to the origin, during the interval
[0,t]
. Note that, for the sake of simplicity, we have used the same letter
P
to denote all probability distributions.

To compute
P(N|t)
we adopt a trajectory based approach. Let us consider a trajectory of the Brownian particle with
N
returns to the origin and let
\tau_{i}
denote the interval between
i-1
-th and
i
-th returns. The probability of such a trajectory is

\displaystyle P(N,\{\tau_{i}\}|t)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}g(\tau_{i})q(\tau_{N+1})\delta(t-\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}\tau_{i})

(33)

where
g(\tau)
denotes the first passage probability of the Brownian motion and

\displaystyle q(\tau_{N+1})=\int_{\tau_{N+1}}^{\infty}d\tau^{\prime}\,g(\tau^{\prime})

(34)

denotes the probability that there were no returns during the final interval
\tau_{N+1}
. Combining the contributions from all such trajectories with
N
returns we arrive at

\displaystyle P(N|t)=\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\tau_{i}\,g(\tau_{i})\,d\tau_{N+1}\,q(\tau_{N+1})\,\delta\Big(t-\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}\tau_{i}\Big).

(35)

It is convenient to consider its Laplace transform,

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(N,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}P(N|t)e^{-st}dt=[\tilde{g}(s)]^{N}\tilde{q}(s),

(36)

where
\tilde{g}(s)
and
\tilde{q}(s)
denote the Laplace transforms of
g(t)
and
q(t)
, respectively. These two quantities are related. Indeed, performing the Laplace transform of Eq. (
34
) and using
q(0)=1
we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(s)=\frac{1-\tilde{g}(s)}{s}\,.

(37)

In the following, we use Eq. (
35
) along with the known results for
P(y|N)
to compute
P(y,t)
in
d=1
and
d=2
.

In one spatial dimension, the driven particle can only move in two directions. The displacements, which can be set to unity as we argued before as along as we are interested only in the functional form of the scaled position distribution, are therefore
\eta_{i}=\pm 1
. The distribution of
y
for a given number,
N
, of interactions with the Brownian particle is asymptotically Gaussian:

\displaystyle P(y|N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi N}}\exp{\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{4N}\right)},

(38)

for
N\gg 1
. Next, we need to compute
\tilde{P}(N,s)
which requires determination of
\tilde{g}(s)
[cf. Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
)]. The first passage properties of one-dimensional Brownian motion are well studied and the exact form of
g(\tau)
is known
[
7
]
,

\displaystyle g(\tau)=\frac{|\Delta x|}{\sqrt{4\pi D\tau^{3}}}\exp{\left[-\frac{(\Delta x)^{2}}{4D\tau}\right]},

(39)

where
\Delta x
denotes the initial distance of the Brownian and driven particles. The corresponding Laplace transform is
\tilde{g}(s)=e^{-b\sqrt{s}}
with

b=\Delta x/\sqrt{D}
. We can set
b=1
as the precise value only affects the non-universal constant
c_{1}
which we fix otherwise [see Sec.
II.1
and Appendix
A
]. Using
\tilde{g}(s)=e^{-\sqrt{s}}
and Eq. (
37
) we have
\tilde{q}(s)=s^{-1}\big(1-e^{-\sqrt{s}}\big)
and deduce

\tilde{P}(N,s)=\frac{1-e^{-\sqrt{s}}}{s}\,e^{-N\sqrt{s}}

(40)

from Eq. (
36
). We are primarily interested in the large
t
behavior, hence, it suffices to consider the small
s
behavior of the Laplace transform,

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(N,s)\simeq\frac{e^{-N\sqrt{s}}}{\sqrt{s}}\,,

(41)

which we invert to find

\displaystyle P(N,t)\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\exp{\left(-\frac{N^{2}}{4t}\right)}.

(42)

Replacing the summation in Eq. (
32
) by integration we obtain

\displaystyle P(y,t)\simeq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dN}{\sqrt{4N\pi^{2}t}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{y^{2}}{4N}-\frac{N^{2}}{4t}\right].

(43)

Let us remark that this heuristic argument esentially maps the effective motion of the passive particle to a continuous-time random walk (CTRW) problem
[
17
]
. CTRW refers to a walker that performs
\pm 1
random walk of
N
steps,
but before every jump the walker waits at a lattice site for a random time
\tau

drawn, independently for each jump, from a waiting time distribution
g(\tau)
.

The distribution (
43
) can be expressed in a scaling form

\displaystyle P(y,t)\simeq\frac{1}{t^{1/4}}{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\left(\frac{y}{t^{1/4}}\right)

(44)

where the scaling function is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{du}{\sqrt{u}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{z^{2}}{8u}-u^{2}\right]\,.

(45)

The integral can be evaluated exactly leading to the announced scaling function (
7d
).
This is one of the central results of this work—for short-ranged interactions, the distribution of typical position
fluctuations of the driven particle in the large-time regime is universal and independent of the specific form of the
interaction potential. The asymptotic behaviors of the scaling function
\mathcal{F}_{1}(z)
are given in (
8
).
Note that the large
z
behavior in (
8
) is easier to extract by performing a saddle point
analysis of the integral representation (
45
), rather than from the formal expression
in (
7d
).

The universal nature of the position fluctuations of the driven particle is illustrated in Fig.
5

comparing the scaled position distribution obtained from numerical simulations with interaction potential
(
29
) and (
30
) with the analytical prediction Eq. (
7d
). The match is excellent.
We used a numerical factor to obtain the collapse for the two different interaction potentials. This non-universal
factor
c_{1}
appearing in Eq. (
7a
) depends on the specific form of the interaction and cannot be determined
from the heuristic argument presented above. In Sec.
II.3
, we discuss some specific examples where we
deduce this factor from an exact computation of the distribution
P(y,t)
.

This heuristic argument can be extended to two dimensions. In this case, the approximation (
31
) describes a two-dimensional random walker implying a Gaussian distribution of
y
for a given
N
:

\displaystyle P(y|N)=\frac{1}{4\pi N}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{|y|^{2}}{4N}\right].

(46)

The next step is to obtain
P(N|t)
, i.e., the probability that the two-dimensional Brownian particle
x
has
N
return events to the origin within a duration
t
, which is given by Eq. (
35
). A convenient way to find
P(N|t)
is again via its Laplace transform [see Eq. (
36
)]. Thus, we need
g(s)
, the Laplace transform of the first-passage time distribution. For a two-dimensional Brownian motion, the first-passage time is the time to return within a radial distance
a
of the origin, starting from a radial distance
b>a
. Let
S(t,a,b)
be the corresponding survival probability, i.e., the probability that, starting from a distance
b
, the Brownian particle has not returned within a distance
a
of the origin up to time
t
. It is known that the Laplace transform of
S(t,a,b)
is given by
[
18
]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}dt\,e^{-st}S(t,a,b)=\frac{K_{0}(b\sqrt{2s})}{sK_{0}(a\sqrt{2s})}\,,

(47)

where
K_{0}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order zero. The corresponding first-passage time distribution is given by
g(t)=-\partial_{t}S(t,a,b)
, so its Laplace transform reads

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(s)=\frac{K_{0}(b\sqrt{2s})}{K_{0}(a\sqrt{2s})}\,.

(48)

Since we are interested in the long-time regime, it suffices to consider the small
s
behavior of
\tilde{g}(s)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(s)=1+\frac{2\log(b/a)}{\log s}+O(s\log s).

(49)

Consequently

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(s)\simeq-\frac{c}{s\log s},

(50)

where
c=2\log(b/a)
is a non-universal constant. Using this result together with Eqs. (
36
)–(
37
) we can write

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(N,s)\simeq\frac{c}{s\log s}\exp{\bigg(\frac{cN}{\log s}\bigg)}

(51)

for small
s
and large
N
. From the above equation, one can get the large time behavior of
P(N,t)
by inverting the Laplace transform using Bronwich integral,

\displaystyle P(N,t)=-\frac{c}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{ds}{s\log s}e^{st}\exp{\bigg(\frac{cN}{\log s}\bigg)}

(52)

where
\Gamma
denotes the standard Bromwich contour which encloses all the singularties of the integrand. It is convenient to use a change of variable
z=st
which yields

\displaystyle P(N,t)=-\frac{c}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{dz}{z(\log z-\log t)}e^{z}\exp{\bigg(\frac{cN}{\log z-\log t}\bigg)}.~

This integral is dominated by the region where
z=O(1)
, i.e.,
\log z\ll\log t
. Thus

\displaystyle P(N,t)\simeq\frac{c}{\log t}\exp\left(-\frac{cN}{\log t}\right)\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{dz}{z}e^{z}

(53)

from which

\displaystyle P(N|t)\simeq\frac{1}{\log t}\exp{\left(-\frac{N}{\log t}\right)},

(54)

where, once again, we have put the non-universal constant
c=1
.

Substituting (
46
) and (
54
) into Eq. (
32
) and replacing summation by integration we arrive at

\displaystyle P(y,t)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi\log t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dN}{N}\exp{\Big(-\frac{y^{2}}{4N}-\frac{N}{\ln t}\Big)}

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ln t}{\mathcal{F}}_{2}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{\ln t}}\right)

(56)

with the scaling function given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(z)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{du}{u}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{z^{2}}{4u}-u\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,K_{0}(z)\,,

(57)

as announced in (
9b
).

). By adjusting the non-universal factor

The slow logarithmic growth of the MSD of the driven particle in our continuum model in two dimensions reproduces a similar anomalous growth of a tagged particle on a two dimensional lattice mediated by a single vacancy
[
4
,
5
]
.
Moreover, even the scaled position distribution of the tagged particle is recovered in Eq. (
9b
) in our simpler continuum model. The analysis of the lattice version in Refs.
[
4
,
5
]
is much more complicated than our case. Our analysis also has the advantage of leading to universal results independent of the short-ranged interaction potential, and even on the nature of the potential, i.e., whether it is attractive, repulsive, or a combination of both. An interesting attempt
[
19
]
of developing a continuum theory of vacancy-mediated diffusion relied on a mean-field approximation, and was also aimed at modeling the original lattice problem
[
4
,
5
]
. Our setting is continuous from the beginning, and we also treated arbitrary short-ranged interaction potentials. As often happens in exact sciences, a more general framework is frequently more tractable than a specific one.

For the one-dimensional symmetric exclusion process with a single empty site, the motion of the tagged particle is trivial—it first hops to the right (assuming that the vacancy was originally to the right of the tagged particle), then to the left, then to the right, etc. Thus in this case, the tagged particle is essentially confined only to two sites. A non-trivial vacancy-meditated diffusion is feasible on quasi-one-dimensional lattices, e.g., on the ladders. The emerging behavior is essentially the same as the behavior of our driven particle in one dimension. The analysis
[
4
]
of the vacancy-mediated diffusion on the ladders is lengthy and cumbersome, so the advantage of our continuous approach (in addition to its applicability to arbitrary short-ranged interaction potentials) is even more apparent.

The heuristic argument presented in this section does not depend on whether the interaction potential is attractive or repulsive. Consequently, we expect the results to hold irrespective of the sign of the force on the driven particle. This is shown in Fig.
7
for
d=1
, where we compare
P(y,t)
measured from numerical simulations for attractive (
V_{0}<0
) and repulsive (
V_{0}>0
) Gaussian interaction (
30
). The identical distribution in both the cases illustrates that the universal scaling function is independent of the sign of the interaction, i.e., whether the interaction is repulsive or attractive.

Although the heuristic approach used in this subsection predicts the exact scaling functions in
d=1
and
d=2
, the non-universal factors
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
remain unknown. We now present asymptotically exact computations in a few explicit examples which allow the determination of these non-universal factors.
The emerging scaling functions are the same, supporting the validity of the heuristic argument presented in this subsection.

II.3
Exact computations: Explicit examples

For short-ranged interactions in
d=1
, the Brownian functional form (
15
) allows for the exact computation of the position distribution
P(y,t)
, using the Feynman-Kac formalism. In this subsection, we derive the scaling function for a few simple interaction potentials.

To compute the full distribution
P(y,t)
for
y
at fixed time
t
, we consider the ‘Laplace transform’ of
P(y,t|x_{0})
, the distribution of
y

at time
t
, given that
x(0)=x_{0}
, with respect to
y

\displaystyle Q_{p}(x_{0},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\langle e^{-p\int_{0}^{t}f(x(\tau))d\tau}\Big\rangle

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-py}P(y,t|x_{0}),

where
p
denotes the conjugate variable. Note that the integration is over
-\infty<y<+\infty
, as opposed to
0
to
\infty
used for the standard Laplace transform. One can show
[
15
]
that
Q_{p}(x_{0},t)
in Eq. (
58
) satisfies the backward Feynman-Kac equation (where the starting point
x_{0}
is treated as a variable):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{p}(x_{0},t)=D\partial_{x_{0}}^{2}Q_{p}(x_{0},t)-pf(x_{0})Q_{p}(x_{0},t).

(59)

The function
Q_{p}(x_{0},t)
must satisfy the initial condition
Q_{p}(x_{0},0)=1
. This assertion follows from the definition in (
58
) and
the boundary conditions

\displaystyle Q_{p}(x_{0}\to\pm\infty,t)=1.

(60)

Indeed, if the particle starts at
\pm\infty
, at any finite time
t
, the value of
f(x)
in Eq. (
58
) is essentially zero since we assume that
f(x)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
. To find
P(y,t)
, we first need to solve Eq. (
59
) for arbitrary
x_{0}
and then set
x_{0}=0
and invert the Laplace transform in Eq. (
58
).

To solve Eq. (
59
), it is appropriate to take first the Laplace transform with respect to
t
by defining

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0},s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-st}Q_{p}(x_{0},t)

(61)

Taking the Laplace transform of Eq. (
59
) and using the initial condition
Q_{p}(x_{0},0)=1
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0},s)=-1

(62a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=D\partial_{x_{0}}^{2}-s-pf(x_{0})

(62b)

The boundary conditions

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0}\to\pm\infty,s)=\frac{1}{s}

(63)

follow from (
60
). In general, Eq. (
62
) is hard to solve for arbitrary
f(x_{0})
. So, we consider a couple of specific examples where
\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0},s)
can be computed explicitly.

II.3.1
Square potential

For the square potential

\displaystyle V(x)=\left\{\begin{split}V_{0}&\quad\text{for}\quad-R\leq x\leq R\cr 0&\quad\text{for}\quad|x|>R\end{split}\right.

(64)

the force is
f(x)=-V_{0}\delta(x+R)+V_{0}\delta(x-R)
. We thus need to solve Eq. (
62a
) with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=D\partial_{x_{0}}^{2}-s-pV_{0}[\delta(x_{0}-R)-\delta(x_{0}+R)].

(65)

The solution is presented in Appendix
B
. In particular, defining

\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)=\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0}=0,s)\,,

(66)

we find the exact expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\frac{2\,p^{2}\,V_{0}^{2}\,\sinh(R\,\sqrt{s/D}\,)}{p^{2}\,V_{0}^{2}\,\sinh(2R\sqrt{s/D}\,)-2sD\,e^{2R\sqrt{s/D}}}\right]\,.

(67)

Expanding
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)
in powers of
p
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)=\frac{1}{s}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\rho_{n}(s)\,p^{2n}

(68a)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(s)=\frac{V_{0}^{2n}}{s(sD)^{n}}\,\left(\frac{\sinh(2R\sqrt{s/D})}{2}\right)^{n-1}

(68b)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\sinh\left(R\sqrt{\frac{s}{D}}\right)\,e^{-2nR\sqrt{s/D}},

(68c)

where
(2n)!\,\rho_{n}(s)
represents the Laplace transform of the
(2n)
-th moment of
y(t)
. All odd moments vanish due to symmetry.
The coefficient
\rho_{1}(s)
of the first term of the expansion in
p^{2}
has the small
s
behavior

\rho_{1}(s)=\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{s^{2}D}\,\,\sinh(R\sqrt{s/D})e^{-2R\sqrt{s/D}}\simeq\frac{V_{0}^{2}R}{(sD)^{3/2}}

(69)

from which we deduce (performing the inverse Laplace transform) the large-time asymptotic of the MSD of the passive particle driven by the square potential

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq\frac{4RV_{0}^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi D^{3}}}\sqrt{t}\,.

(70)

To derive the full distribution
P(y,t)
in the large time regime, we consider the limit
s\to 0
and
p\to 0
in (
67
). To the leading order
we get,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}(\sqrt{s}-c_{1}^{2}p^{2})}.

(71)

where
c_{1}^{2}=V_{0}^{2}R/D^{3/2}
. The large-time position distribution is given by the double inverse Laplace transform of
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)
. This can be done using a set of integral representations [see Appendix
B
for the details]. First, we invert the Laplace transform with respect to
p
and find,

\displaystyle\hat{P}(y,s)\simeq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dN}{\sqrt{4\pi c_{1}^{2}Ns}}\,\,\exp{\Big[-N\sqrt{s}-\frac{y^{2}}{4c_{1}^{2}N}\Big]}.

(72)

Then, we invert the Laplace transform with respect to
s
to get,

\displaystyle P(y,t)\simeq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dN}{\sqrt{4\pi^{2}c_{1}^{2}Nt}}\,\,\exp{\Big[-\frac{y^{2}}{4c_{1}^{2}N}-\frac{N^{2}}{4t}\Big]}.

(73)

Making a change of variable
N=u\sqrt{4t}
, we get the scaling form for the position distribution

\displaystyle P(y,t)\simeq\frac{1}{c_{1}t^{1/4}}{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\Big(\frac{y}{c_{1}t^{1/4}}\Big).

(74)

The scaling function
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(z)
is the same as predicted by the heuristic argument, viz. it is given by the integral in Eq. (
45
) that reduces to (
7d
). The exact solution also provides the non-universal scale factor
c_{1}=V_{0}\sqrt{R}/D^{3/4}
.

II.3.2
Tent potential

For the tent potential (
29
), the corresponding force in
d=1
is

\displaystyle f(x)=\left\{\begin{split}\text{sgn}(x)\frac{V_{0}}{R}&\quad\text{for}~~|x|<R\cr 0&\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{split}\right.

(75)

In this case, the backward Fokker-Planck equation is also solvable, see Appendix
C
. As before, we compute the second moment by expanding
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)
around
p=0
, and inverting the Laplace transform of the coefficient of
p^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq\frac{4RV_{0}^{2}}{3\sqrt{\pi D^{3}}}\,\sqrt{t}\,.

(76)

To derive the position distribution
P(y,t)
of the driven particle we use the same procedure as before [see Appendix
C
for details] and find

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}(\sqrt{s}-c_{1}^{2}p^{2})}

(77)

with
c_{1}^{2}=RV_{0}^{2}/(3D^{3/2})
when
s\to 0
and
p\to 0
. This formula differs from Eq. (
71
) only by the nonuniversal numerical factor
c_{1}
. Thus, as expected, the scaling form (
74
) is the same for both potentials.

III
Long-Ranged interaction

In this section, we focus on long-ranged interaction between the driver and the driven particle, as in (
3
). For simplicity, we limit ourselves here to
d=1
, and consider long-ranged forces that decay as

f(z)\simeq\text{sgn}(z)\frac{A}{|z|^{a}}\quad\text{as}\quad|z|\to\infty,

(78)

with the exponent
a>0
. The force can be either repulsive
(A>0)
or attractive
(A<0)
.
Below we focus only on the MSD of the driven particle and show that at late times
it grows algebraically as
\sim t^{\phi}
where the exponent
\phi

depends on the range of
a
, as announced in (
12
). Hence, we consider
the different ranges of
a
separately below.

III.0.1
a>1
: sub-diffusive growth.

To see whether the MSD of the driven particle shows any universal behavior for long-ranged interactions, we start with Eq. (
23
), which gives the Laplace transform of the MSD. We can rely on Eq. (
23
) only when the typical displacement of the Brownian particle greatly exceeds a typical
displacement of the driven particle,
\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\ll t
. In other words, the driven particle exhibits sub-diffusive growth at late times.
We will justify this condition
a posteriori
.

We employ the same arguments as in the short-ranged interaction case. To extract from Eq. (
23
)
the small
s
behavior of the Laplace transform
M_{2}(s)
of the MSD of the driven particle, we need the
small
k
behavior of the Fourier transform of the force,
\tilde{f}(k)
. For long-ranged interaction of the type in (

), the
Fourier transform
\tilde{f}(k)
is non-analytic around

for

and its leading order behavior is

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(k)\sim\begin{cases}k&\text{for}~~a>2\cr|k|^{a-1}&\text{for}~~a\leq 2.\end{cases}

In this case, the small

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)\sim\begin{cases}s^{a-3}&\text{for}\quad 1\leq a\leq\frac{3}{2}\cr s^{-3/2}&\text{for}\quad a>\frac{3}{2}.\end{cases}

(80)

Correspondingly, the long-time asymptotic behavior of the MSD of the driven particle is given by

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\sim t^{\phi}\quad{\textrm{a}s}\quad t\to\infty\,,

(81)

with the exponent

\displaystyle\phi=\max\big(2-a,\tfrac{1}{2}\big).

(82)

The exponent
\phi
characterizing the growth of the MSD of the driven particle undergoes a ‘freezing

is a strictly decreasing function of

1<a<\frac{3}{2}

, for any

This proves the result announced in the first two lines of (

III.0.2
0<a<1

To complete the growth laws of the MSD, we now probe the behavior in the
0<a\leq 1
range. Unfortunately, we can no longer rely on the methods employed so far. Indeed, the driven particle is expected to be as mobile as the Brownian particle, or even super-diffusive, so the chief starting point of our analysis, the Brownian functional (
15
), is no longer applicable. We can advance, however, once we recognize the crucial
distinction between the attractive
A<0
and the repulsive
(A>0)
long-ranged forces in (
78
) with exponent
a
in the range
0<a<1
.

If the force is repulsive
(A>0)
, the passive particle eventually settles on one of the two half-lines,
y>0
or

y<0
, and remains there. Both outcomes are equiprobable if the starting positions coincide. In the former
case, we anticipate that the passive particle is pushed to a distance
y\gg\sqrt{Dt}
. In this case, the force
A/z^{a}
[cf.
Eq. (
78
)] simplifies to
A/y^{a}
. Therefore, the average position satisfies the deterministic equation

\dot{y}=\frac{A}{y^{a}}

(95)

in the leading order, from which

y=[A\,(a+1)\,t]^{\frac{1}{a+1}}

(96)

where
A>0
ince the force is repulsive) and
0<a<1
. This provides the third line of the result announced in (
12
). Thus in this case, the motion of the driven particle is asymptotically deterministic, albeit the direction of the motion is random. The scaling exponent obeys

\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{a+1}<1
when
0<a<1
, so the motion is super-diffusive and sub-ballistic. Let us now specialize to the potential in (
83
) or equivalently to the force
in (
84
) which, for large
z
, decays as

f(z)\simeq 2\mu V_{0}\,z^{-(1+2\mu)}
, indicating that
A=2\mu V_{0}
and
a=1+2\mu
in (
95
).
Since
0<a<1
, this indicates that
-1/2<\mu<0
.
In order that the force is repulsive, i.e.,
A>0
, we must have
2\,\mu,V_{0}>0
. Since

-1/2<\mu<0
is negative, we must choose
V_{0}<0
to have a repulsive force.
The result in (
96
) then states

y=[4\,V_{0}\,\mu\,(\mu+1)t]^{\frac{1}{2\,(\mu+1)}}\,,

(97)

applicable when
0>\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
and
V_{0}<0
.

Different behaviors of the driven particle emerge when the force is attractive. Subtracting (
1b
)
from (
1a
) we obtain a stochastic differential equation

\dot{z}=f(z)-\eta,

(98)

for the separation
z=y-x
. The probability distribution
P(z,t)
satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial P(z,t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[f(z)P(z,t)\right]+D\,\frac{\partial^{2}P(z,t)}{\partial z^{2}}\,.

(99)

The crucial observation is that for slowly decaying attractive forces (
3
) with exponent satisfying

0<a<1
, the driven particle is ‘slaved’ to the Brownian particle which is mathematically reflected by the
fact that the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation becomes stationary in the long-time limit. Seeking the
stationary solution we reduce (
99
) to

\frac{dP(z)}{dz}=\frac{f(z)}{D}\,P(z)

(100)

which is solved to yield

P(z)=C\,\exp\!\left[\int_{0}^{z}dw\,\frac{f(w)}{D}\right]

(101)

with amplitude
C
fixed by normalization. Specializing (
101
) to the potential (
83
) we
obtain

P(z)=C\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{V_{0}}{D(1+z^{2})^{\mu}}\right]

(102)

applicable when
0>\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
and
V_{0}>0
(since the force is attractive).

Since the driven particle is slaved to the Brownian particle, the MSD of the driven particle is

\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq 2Dt

(103)

in the leading order. The asymptotic behaviors (
96
) and (
103
) imply the
predictions for the exponent
\phi
in the
0<a<1
range announced in Eq. (
12
).

III.0.3
a=1

Finally, we discuss the marginal case
a=1
. The entire force distribution is necessary if the force is
attractive [cf. Eq. (
101
)], and as an illustration we take

f(z)=-\frac{2\,A\,D\,z}{1+z^{2}}\,.

(104)

The corresponding potential

V(z)=A\,D\,\ln(1+z^{2})

(105)

is a regularized
\mu=0
version of the potential (
83
). Alternatively, this potential is a
regularized two-dimensional Coulomb potential, although we use it in one dimension. We put the diffusion coefficient
D

into (
104
), so the amplitude
A
is dimensionless. The force (
104
) is used in numerous
applications, particularly in modeling the spreading of the momentum of two-level atoms in optical lattices

[
21
,
22
,
23
]
where
z
is the momentum of an atom,
D
describes momentum fluctuations which
lead to heating, and
f(z)
the cooling force. Hence, the Fokker-Planck equation with force (
104
) was the subject of several articles

[
24
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
; see Ref.
[
32
]
for a review. In applications to laser cooling,
A>0
, so in our terminology, the force is attractive. Hence, we can use known results in this case. A discrete lattice model of a particle moving in a logarithmic
potential was introduced by Gillis in the probability
literature
[
33
]
. For more recent studies
on the Gillis model and its generalizations,
see Refs.
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
Here we focus on the continuous-space version of the model,
briefly re-derive a few simple results using our terminology and
notations and cite more complicated results.

The force (
104
) is attractive when
A>0
, and the stationary solution becomes

P(z)=C(1+z^{2})^{-A}

(106)

This solution is normalizable when
A>\frac{1}{2}
. In this case we fix the amplitude from the normalization
requirement,
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,P(z)=1
, and arrive at

P(z)=\frac{\Gamma(A)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}\big)\,\Gamma\big(A-\frac{1}{2}\big)}\,(1+z^{2})^{-A}

(107)

The precise form (
107
) depends on the detailed form (
104
) of the force, but the threshold value

A_{*}=\frac{1}{2}
is universal. This assertion readily follows from the general stationary solution
(
101
).

The stationary solution (
107
) was noticed in
[
23
,
27
]
, and it was observed in optical
lattice experiments
[
27
]
. Notice, however, that the stationary solution (
107
) predicts finite
second moment
\langle z^{2}\rangle
only when
A>\frac{3}{2}
. The divergence of the second moment was
noticed in
[
28
]
where it was shown that when
\frac{1}{2}<A\leq\frac{3}{2}
, the stationary solution
(
107
) is valid only in the central region
|z|\ll\sqrt{t}
; in the tail regions the solution is
time-dependent. The behavior of the second moment can be deduced from a more comprehensive analysis

[
28
]
of the Fokker-Planck with force (
104
):

\langle z^{2}\rangle=\begin{cases}(2A-3)^{-1}&A>\frac{3}{2}\\
\sim t^{\frac{3}{2}-A}&\frac{1}{2}<A<\frac{3}{2}\\
2D(1-2A)t&0<A<\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

(108)

We observe that when the force (
104
) is weakly attractive,
0<A<\frac{1}{2}
, the separation between
the driver and the passive particle grows diffusively. This suggests to seek a scaling solution of the
Fokker-Planck equation with force (
104
):

P(z,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\,\mathcal{P}(\zeta),\qquad\zeta=\frac{z}{\sqrt{4Dt}}\,.

(109a)

In the range
0<A<\frac{1}{2}
, the scaled distribution

\mathcal{P}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{1}{2}-A\big)}\,\zeta^{-2A}\,e^{-\zeta^{2}}

(109b)

was found in Ref.
[
28
]
. The same scaling solution applies repulsive forces,
A<0
.

Using (
109a
)–(
109b
) one finds the diffusive growth

\langle z^{2}\rangle=2\,D\,(1-2A)\,t

(110)

asymptotically valid for
A<\frac{1}{2}
, i.e., for sufficiently weak attractive forces (
104
) as it
was already stated in Eq. (
108
) and for all repulsive forces (
104
). In this range, the MSD of the
driven particle is also expected to grow diffusively

\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq C(A)\,D\,t

(111)

The amplitude
C(A)
is unknown, apart from the obvious case of vanishing force when
y\equiv 0
and hence

C(0)=0
. The identity
\langle z^{2}(t)\rangle=\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle-2\langle x(t)y(t)\rangle+\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle
and the
knowledge of
\langle z^{2}(t)\rangle
, Eq. (
110
), and
\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle=2Dt
is insufficient since in this
marginal case the stochastic variables
x(t)
and
y(t)
are correlated and, in particular,
\langle x(t)y(t)\rangle\neq 0
. The correlator cannot vanish because this would lead to
C(A)=-4A
which is clearly impossible
for weakly attractive forces:
0<A<\frac{1}{2}
. Only for very strong repulsive forces (

naive prediction is asymtotically exact:
C(A)\simeq-4A
when
A\to-\infty
.

IV
Concluding Remarks

We have studied the motion of a passive particle driven by a non-reciprocal interaction with a Brownian particle. We find that the late-time position fluctuations of the driven particle are remarkably universal when the interaction potential is short-ranged. The MSD of the driven particle scales as
\sqrt{t}
in
d=1
and as
\log t
in
d=2
, and the position distributions are also universal and independent of the specific form of the potential that only affects the numerical scale factor. We calculate the scaling functions in
d=1
and
d=2
using heuristic arguments. We argue that our calculation is exact, modulo the scale factor, and support our claims by making rigorous calculations for a few specific interaction potentials and by numerical simulations. In
d>2
, the random walk is not recurrent, and therefore, in the case of the short-range interactions, the displacement of the passive particle from its initial position remains
finite in the long time limit. Furthermore, this displacement varies from realization to realization hence, there is no scaling behavior, and the distribution of the final displacement is expected to be non-universal.

We have also explored the motion of a passive particle in one dimension driven by long-ranged interaction potentials. We have found a rich set of behaviors chiefly determined by the exponent
a
describing the decay of the force,
f\sim z^{-a}
when
z\to\infty
. The MSD of the driven particle grows as
t^{\phi}
, with the exponent
\phi
undergoing a freezing transition at
a=\frac{3}{2}
: The same value
\phi=1/2
as for short-ranged potentials holds for
a>\frac{3}{2}
, while the exponent varies continuously in the
1<a<\frac{3}{2}
range where
\phi=2-a
. The behavior remains universal in the
a>1
range. The scaled position distribution is known only when
a>\frac{3}{2}
, where it coincides with the scaled distribution in a short-ranged potential. The scaled distributions in the
1<a<\frac{3}{2}
are unknown. The behavior in the
0<a<1
range is less universal but simpler than when
a\geq 1
. The behavior crucially depends on whether the potential is attractive or repulsive. If the potential is repulsive, the driven particle exhibits asymptotically self-averaging behavior—one of the two possible directions is eventually selected (this varies from realization to realization), and the motion is asymptotically deterministic. If the potential is attractive, the passive particle is effectively slaved to the Brownian particle. We studied the effect of long-ranged potentials in one dimension. The extension to higher dimensions is left for future work.

In this paper, we restricted ourselves to a force
f(r)
that is regular near
r=0
. It would be interesting to study a singular force that diverges at short distance as a power law
f(r)\simeq B\,r^{-b}
with a positive exponent
b>0
.

We mentioned the connection between our passive particle driven by a short-ranged non-reciprocal interaction with a Brownian particle and diffusion of a tagged particle mediated by a single vacancy
[
4
,
5
,
19
,
39
]
. The latter problem is traditionally studied in the realm of simple symmetric exclusion process where particles can hop only to nearest neighbors, so in the case of a single vacancy the number of possible moves is
2d
in the case of the hyper-cubic lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Symmetric exclusion processes in which long-ranged hops are allowed could be a counterpart of our problem with long-ranged potential. In a supplementary direction, a charged tagged particle mediated by a single vacancy exhibits intriguing behaviors
[
39
]
suggesting a similar model in our setting, perhaps just considering a force that is not
an odd function of the separation.

Arcsine laws are old but still striking characteristics of Brownian motion
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. One can ask about analogues of arcsine laws for the passive particle. For long-ranged forces, the answers could be easier
than for short-ranged forces. For instance, for forces decreasing as
z^{-a}
when
z\to\infty
with exponent

0<a<1
, the distribution of the fraction
\tau
of time spent by the passive particle on the right half-line,
y>0
, is
\frac{1}{2}\delta(\tau)+\frac{1}{2}\delta(\tau-1)
when the force is repulsive, and the same as for Brownian particle when the force is attractive.

In this work, we studied the most basic few-body system: A single driver particle and a single passive particle, symbolically a (1,1) system. Similarly, an
(m,n)
system consists of
m
Brownian particles and
n
passive particles supplemented by non-reciprocal forces exerted by every driver particle on every passive particle. The
(\infty,1)
system in which non-interacting Brownian particles are uniformly distributed in the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
space with density
\rho
is an amusing microscopic model of Langevin dynamics. The passive particle should behave
diffusively. The diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{D}
of the passive particle depends on the diffusion coefficient

D
of Brownian particles, their density
\rho
, the characteristic scale
V_{0}
of the potential, and its range

R
. On dimensional grounds,
\mathcal{D}=D\,\Phi(\rho R^{d},V_{0}/D)
, i.e., it depends on two dimensionless
parameters, the typical number
\rho R^{d}
of Brownian particles exerting the force on the passive particle, and
V_{0}/D
playing the role of the Péclet number. Computing
\Phi(\rho R^{d},V_{0}/D)
, e.g., for the Gaussian potential, is a promising avenue for future work.

Appendix A
Large-time behavior of the MSD

In this Appendix we provide the detailed derivation of the long-time behavior of the mean-squared displacement. We start with the
d
-dimensional
version of (
15
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}(t)\simeq\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\boldsymbol{f}(x(\tau)),

(112)

where
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{z})=-\nabla V(\boldsymbol{z})
denotes the vector force derived from the central potential
V(|z|)
. The corresponding mean-squared displacement is

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle=2\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}dt_{1}\left\langle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{2}))\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1}))\right\rangle

(113)

where we have used the fact that the two-point correlation is symmetric under the exchange
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Hence, it suffices to compute the two-point correlation for
t_{2}>t_{1}
, which is given by

\displaystyle\Big\langle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{2}))\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1}))\Big\rangle=

\displaystyle\int d\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\int d\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\,f(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})f(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})

(114)

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{2}|\boldsymbol{1})\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{1}|\boldsymbol{0})

(115)

where
2
=
(\boldsymbol{x}_{2},t_{2})
,
1
=
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},t_{1})
,
0
=
(\boldsymbol{0},0)
, and the propagator is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{2}|\boldsymbol{1})=\frac{1}{[4\pi D(t_{2}-t_{1})]^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp{\left[-\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1})^{2}}{4D(t_{1}-t_{2})}\right]}

(116)

in
d
dimensions where we have taken
t_{2}>t_{1}
. To compute the two-point correlation, we express the propagator as a Fourier integral and use it in Eq. (
115
) to yield

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{2}))\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1}))\right\rangle=\int d\boldsymbol{x_{1}}\int d\boldsymbol{x_{2}}\,\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})

(117)

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\exp{[-D\{k_{2}^{2}(t_{2}-t_{1})+k_{1}^{2}t_{1}\}]}

(118)

\displaystyle~~~\times\exp{[-i\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1})-i\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}_{1}]}.

(119)

where
k_{i}^{2}=|\boldsymbol{k}_{i}|^{2}
. Performing the
\boldsymbol{x}
integrals gives

\displaystyle\Big\langle\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{2}))\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{1}))\Big\rangle=\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(120)

\displaystyle\times\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2})\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})e^{-D[k_{1}^{2}t_{1}+k_{2}^{2}(t_{2}-t_{1})]}

(121)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k})=\int d\boldsymbol{z}\,e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{z})
denotes the
d
-dimensional Fourier transform of the force. Substituting Eq. (
121
) in (
113
) and performing the integrals over
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, we get,

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{D^{2}}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2})\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})}{k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}(k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2})}

(122)

\displaystyle\times\left[k_{1}^{2}(1-e^{-Dk_{2}^{2}t})-k_{2}^{2}(1-e^{-Dk_{1}^{2}t})\right].

(123)

The
k
-integrals are performed over the
d
-dimensional space. The above expression gives the exact MSD for the process (
112
), but it cannot be evaluated without knowing the explicit form of the force. However, since we are primarily interested in the long-time behavior, we can proceed a bit further. To this end, we consider the Laplace transform of the MSD with respect to time,
M_{2}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-st}\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle
. Performing the Laplace transform of Eq. (
123
) gives

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{s}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2})\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k}_{2})}{(Dk_{1}^{2}+s)(Dk_{2}^{2}+s)}.\qquad

(124)

We are interested in the large
t
behavior of the MSD, which is controlled by the small
s
behavior of its Laplace transform
M_{2}(s)
. The small
s
behavior of
M_{2}(s)
cannot be extracted by naively putting
s=0
in Eq. (
124
) since the resulting integral diverges. To extract the leading divergence of this double integral as
s\to 0
, we first split the integral into two

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)=\frac{2}{s}[I_{1}(s)+I_{2}(s)],

(125)

where
I_{1}(s)
and
I_{2}(s)
denote the contributions from the integral over the region where
(|\boldsymbol{k}_{1}|,|\boldsymbol{k}_{2}|)\leq\Lambda
with a constant cut-off
\Lambda\sim O(1)
, and outside this regime where
(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}|,|\boldsymbol{k}_{2}|)>\Lambda
, respectively.

In the
s\to 0
limit,
I_{2}(s)
converges to a constant. This can be seen by taking the limit in the integrand itself. Therefore

\displaystyle M_{2}(s)=\frac{2}{s}I_{1}(s)+O\left(\frac{1}{s}\right).

(126)

On the other hand,
I_{1}(s)
is expected to diverge in the
s\to 0
limit, and consequently the small
s
behavior of
M_{2}(s)
is determined by the contribution from
I_{1}(s)
. In the following we extract the small
s
behavior of
M_{2}(s)
, by analysing the asymptotic behavior of
I_{1}(s)
, for short and long-ranged forces, in different dimensions.

One dimension
. In one dimension

I_{1}(s)=\int_{-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\frac{d{k}_{1}}{2\pi}\int_{-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\frac{d{k}_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{\tilde{f}({k}_{1}-{k}_{2})\cdot\tilde{f}({k}_{2})}{(Dk_{1}^{2}+s)(Dk_{2}^{2}+s)}\,.

(127)

To extract the small
s
behavior, we first note that the symmetric nature of the potential ensures that it suffices to consider the only the region
(k_{1},k_{2})>0
. Using the scaled variables
q_{1}=k_{1}\sqrt{D/s}
and
q_{2}=k_{2}\sqrt{D/s}
, we recast Eq. (
127
) into

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{Ds}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{1}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{2}}{2\pi}

(128)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\tilde{f}(\sqrt{\frac{s}{D}}(q_{1}-q_{2}))\tilde{f}(\sqrt{\frac{s}{D}}q_{2})}{(q_{1}^{2}+1)(q_{2}^{2}+1)}\,.

(129)

Hereinafter we use the shorthand notation
\overline{\Lambda}\equiv\Lambda\sqrt{D/s}
.

We now analyze the short-ranged and long-ranged forces separately. For short-ranged forces, the Fourier transform of the force,
\tilde{f}(k)=-2i\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\sin(kz)V^{\prime}(z)
, is analytic, and to the leading order in
k
,
\tilde{f}(k)\simeq-ika_{1}
, where
a_{1}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,zV^{\prime}(z)
. Therefore

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{4a_{1}^{2}}{D^{2}}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{1}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{q_{2}(q_{2}-q_{1})}{(q_{1}^{2}+1)(q_{2}^{2}+1)}.

(130)

The
q_{2}
-integral is dominated by the contributions from large
q_{2}
, and hence

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{4a_{1}^{2}}{D^{2}}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{1}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{q_{1}^{2}+1}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{q_{2}^{2}}{q_{2}^{2}+1}\,.

(131)

Evaluating the integrals we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{\pi^{2}D^{2}}\tan^{-1}\overline{\Lambda}\left\{\overline{\Lambda}-\tan^{-1}\overline{\Lambda}\right\}.

(132)

Recalling that
\overline{\Lambda}\equiv\Lambda\sqrt{D/s}
, taking the small
s
limit, and keeping only the leading small
s
behavior of
M_{2}(s)
we arrive at Eq. (
24
).

For long-ranged forces the situation is a bit different since
\tilde{f}(k)
cannot be expanded in a Taylor series. The expected small
k
behavior is
\tilde{f}(k)\simeq b\,\text{Sgn}(k)|k|^{\alpha}
where
\alpha>0
and
b
is a constant dependent on the specific potential. In that case, we have, from Eq. (
129
),

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{4b^{2}s^{\alpha-1}}{D^{1+\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{1}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{q_{1}^{2}+1}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{q_{2}^{2\alpha}}{q_{2}^{2}+1}

(133)

where we have used the fact that, similar to the short-ranged case, the
q_{2}
integral is dominated by the contributions from large
q_{2}
. Performing the above integrals and taking the small
s
limit yields

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{b^{2}s^{\alpha-1}}{\pi D^{\alpha+1}}[A+Bs^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}+O(s^{\frac{3}{2}-\alpha})],

(134)

where
A
and
B
are
s
-independent constants. If
\alpha<1/2
, we have
I_{1}(s)\sim s^{\alpha-1}
; for
\alpha>1/2
,
I_{1}(s)\sim s^{-1/2}
, similar to the short-ranged scenario. Ignoring the sub-leading contributions we get Eq. (
80
) in the main text.

Two dimensions
. The Fourier transform of the rotational symmetric force reads

\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k})=-i\pi\frac{{\boldsymbol{k}}}{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,zJ_{1}(kz)V^{\prime}(z)

where
J_{1}(z)
is the Bessel function of the first kind of order one. Hence,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k})\simeq-ia_{2}{\boldsymbol{k}}
with
a_{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,z^{2}V^{\prime}(z)
for short-ranged forces in the leading order in
k
. The same arguments as in
d=1
lead to

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{a_{2}^{2}s}{D^{3}}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{1}}{2\pi}\frac{q_{1}}{q_{1}^{2}+1}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{q_{2}^{3}}{q_{2}^{2}+1}.

(135)

Performing the integrals, we get

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{a_{2}^{2}s}{4\pi^{2}D^{3}}\log\left(1+\overline{\Lambda}^{2}\right)\Big[\overline{\Lambda}^{2}-\log\left(1+\overline{\Lambda}^{2}\right)\Big].

Taking the leading small
s
behavior of the above expression we get the small
s
behavior of
M_{2}(s)
quoted in Eq. (
26
) where we have put
\Lambda=1
for simplicity.

Three dimensions
. In the leading order in
k
, we have
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}(\boldsymbol{k})\simeq-ia_{3}{\boldsymbol{k}}
with
a_{3}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,z^{3}V^{\prime}(z)
. Consequently, the
3d
version of Eq. (
131
) is given by,

\displaystyle I_{1}(s)\simeq\frac{a_{3}^{2}s}{(\pi D)^{4}}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{1}}{2\pi}\frac{q_{1}^{2}}{q_{1}^{2}+1}\int_{0}^{\overline{\Lambda}}\frac{dq_{2}}{2\pi}\frac{q_{2}^{4}}{q_{2}^{2}+1}.

(136)

Performing the integrals, taking the small
s
limit, and using it in Eq. (
126
), we get Eq. (
28
) in the main text.

Appendix B
Square potential

In this Appendix we provide the details for the computation of the MSD and position distribution for the square-well interaction potential. We start from the backward Feynman-Kac equation in the Laplace space (
65
), which is a second order homogeneous differential equation except at
x_{0}=\pm R
. To solve this equation, it is convenient to consider separately three regions and then match the solutions in the different regimes.

•

Region I (
x_{0}\geq R
): Here the solution of (
65
) satisfying the boundary condition
\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0}\to\infty,s)=1/s
is

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{s}+A_{1}e^{-x_{0}\,\sqrt{s/D}}

(137)

where
A_{1}
is an arbitrary constant to be determined. Hereinafter we usually suppress
s
variable, e.g.,
\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})\equiv\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0},s)
.

•

Region II (
-R\leq x_{0}\leq R
): In this region, the general solution reads,

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{s}+A_{2}e^{x_{0}\,\sqrt{s/D}}+B_{2}e^{-x_{0}\,\sqrt{s/D}}

(138)

where
A_{2}
and
B_{2}
are arbitrary constants.

•

Region III (
x_{0}\leq-R
): the general solution satisfying the boundary condition
\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})\to-\infty,s)=\frac{1}{s}

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{s}+A_{3}e^{x_{0}\,\sqrt{s/D}}

(139)

where
A_{3}
is an arbitrary constant.

To determine the unknown constants
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
,
B_{2}
and
A_{3}
, we use the fact that the solution
\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0},s)
is continuous at
x_{0}=R
and
x_{0}=-R
. Additionally, by integrating Eq. (
65
) across a small interval around
x=R
and
x=-R
, we find two jump conditions to be satisfied by the derivatives. Thus we have four conditions:

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{-})

\displaystyle D\partial_{x_{0}}\!\big[\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{+})-\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{-})\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle pV_{0}\tilde{Q}_{p}(R)

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{-})

\displaystyle D\partial_{x_{0}}\!\big[\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{+})-\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{-})\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-pV_{0}\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R)

By inserting the explicit solutions (
137
)–(
139
) into the above conditions we deduce four linear equations for four unknowns. After simplifications, they read,

\displaystyle A_{1}-A_{2}\rho^{2}-B_{2}

\displaystyle=0

(140)

\displaystyle(pV_{0}+\sqrt{sD})A_{1}+\sqrt{sD}(\rho^{2}A_{2}-B_{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{pV_{0}}{s}\rho

(141)

\displaystyle A_{2}+B_{2}\rho^{2}-A_{3}

\displaystyle=0

(142)

\displaystyle\sqrt{sD}(A_{2}-\rho^{2}B_{2})+(pV_{0}-\sqrt{sD})A_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{pV_{0}}{s}\,\rho.

(143)

where
\rho=e^{R\sqrt{\frac{s}{D}}}
.
Using Eq. (
138
) we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)\equiv\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0}=0,s)=\frac{1}{s}+A_{2}+B_{2}.

(144)

Solving the linear equations (
140
)–(
143
) and substituting them in (
144
) gives the result announced in
Eq. (
67
).

To obtain the position distribution in the large time regime, we start from Eq. (
71
). To invert the double Laplace transform, we use the integral representation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}-c_{1}^{2}p^{2}}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}dN\,\exp{[N(c_{1}^{2}p^{2}-\sqrt{s})]}

and the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\sigma^{2}N}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\exp{\Big[-\frac{y^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}N}-py\Big]}\equiv e^{p^{2}\sigma^{2}N}

to rewrite Eq. (
71
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)\simeq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dye^{-py}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dN}{\sqrt{4\pi c_{1}^{2}Ns}}\,e^{-N\sqrt{s}-\frac{y^{2}}{4c_{1}^{2}N}}\,.

(145)

The Laplace transform
\hat{P}(y,s)={\mathcal{L}}_{t\to s}P(y,t)
can then be read off from the above equation, which is given in Eq. (
72
). Finally, the Laplace transform with respect to
s
can be inverted using the identity

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}_{s\to t}\bigg[\frac{e^{-N\sqrt{s}}}{\sqrt{s}}\bigg]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\exp\!\bigg(-\frac{N^{2}}{4t}\bigg).

(146)

Completing these steps we arrive at Eq. (
73
).

Appendix C
Tent potential

To solve the backward Fokker-Planck equation (
62
) for the tent potential (
29
) in one dimension, we consider separately four regions: (I)
x_{0}\geq R
, (II)
0<x_{0}<R
, (III)
-R<x_{0}<0
and (IV)
x_{0}<-R
. The solutions in neighboring regions can then be matched using the appropriate boundary conditions.

•

|x_{0}|>R
: The force vanishes in these regions, and Eq. (
62
) reduces to

\displaystyle D\partial_{x_{0}}^{2}\tilde{Q}_{p}-s\tilde{Q}_{p}=-1

(147)

which is solved to yield

\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})=\begin{cases}C_{1}e^{x_{0}\sqrt{s/D}}+C_{2}e^{-x_{0}\sqrt{s/D}}+\frac{1}{s}&x_{0}<-R\\
G_{1}e^{x_{0}\sqrt{s/D}}+G_{2}e^{-x_{0}\sqrt{s/D}}+\frac{1}{s}&x_{0}>R\end{cases}

The boundary conditions (
63
) imply that
C_{2}=0=G_{1}
and therefore

\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle C_{1}e^{x_{0}\sqrt{s/D}}\,+\frac{1}{s}&x_{0}<-R\\
\displaystyle G_{2}e^{-x_{0}\sqrt{s/D}}+\frac{1}{s}&x_{0}>R\end{cases}

(148)

•

0<x_{0}<R
: In this region, the force is non-zero and Eq. (
62
) reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{0}}^{2}\tilde{Q}_{p}-\lambda_{+}^{2}\tilde{Q}_{p}=-1,\quad\lambda_{+}=\sqrt{\frac{s+pV_{0}/R}{D}}

(149)

which has the general solution

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{D\lambda_{+}^{2}}+A_{1}e^{x_{0}\lambda_{+}}+A_{2}e^{-x_{0}\lambda_{+}}\,.

(150)

•

-R<x_{0}<0
: In this region, Eq. (
62
) reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{0}}^{2}\tilde{Q}_{p}-\lambda_{-}^{2}\tilde{Q}_{p}=-1,\quad\lambda_{-}=\sqrt{\frac{s-pV_{0}/R}{D}}

(151)

which has the general solution,

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})=\frac{1}{D\lambda_{-}^{2}}+B_{1}e^{x_{0}\lambda_{-}}+B_{2}e^{-x_{0}\lambda_{-}}\,.

(152)

To determine the
s
-dependent constants
A_{1,2}
,
B_{1,2}
,
C_{1}
and
G_{2}
we note that
\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})\equiv\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0},s)
must be a continuous function of
x_{0}
. Moreover, by integrating (
62
) for the
f(x)
given by (
75
) across
x_{0}=\pm R
and
x_{0}=0
, we find that
\partial_{x_{0}}\tilde{Q}_{p}(x_{0})
must also be continuous. Thus

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(0^{-}),~~~~~\partial_{x_{0}}\tilde{Q}_{p}(0^{+})=\partial_{x_{0}}\tilde{Q}_{p}(0^{-}),

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{-}),~~~~\partial_{x_{0}}\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{+})=\partial_{x_{0}}\tilde{Q}_{p}(R^{-}),

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{-}),~~\partial_{x_{0}}\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{+})=\partial_{x_{0}}\tilde{Q}_{p}(-R^{-}).

Inserting the above solutions into these conditions we get six linear equations. We are particularly interested in the behavior of
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)\equiv\tilde{Q}_{p}(0,s)
, so we eliminate
C_{1}
and
G_{2}
leaving us with two long equations

\displaystyle A_{1}\left(1+\lambda_{+}\sqrt{\tfrac{D}{s}}\right)e^{\lambda_{+}R}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle A_{2}\left(1-\lambda_{+}\sqrt{\tfrac{D}{s}}\right)e^{-\lambda_{+}R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{pV_{0}/R}{s(s+pV_{0}/R)},

\displaystyle B_{1}\left(1-\lambda_{-}\sqrt{\tfrac{D}{s}}\right)e^{-\lambda_{-}R}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle B_{2}\left(1+\lambda_{-}\sqrt{\tfrac{D}{s}}\right)e^{-\lambda_{+}R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{pV_{0}/R}{s(s+pV_{0}/R)}

and two shorter equations

\begin{split}A_{1}+A_{2}-B_{1}-B_{2}&=\frac{2pV_{0}/R}{s^{2}-p^{2}V_{0}^{2}/R^{2}},\\
A_{1}-A_{2}&=\frac{\lambda_{-}}{\lambda_{+}}(B_{1}-B_{2}).\end{split}

These linear equations can be readily solved, but we omit long explicit formulae for
A_{1,2}
and
B_{1,2}
. We are primarily interested in the long-time and large
y
behavior, so it suffices to consider the small
s
and small
p
limiting behavior of
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)
. As before, we first compute the second moment. Expanding
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)
around
p=0
and taking the coefficient of
p^{2}
we obtain

{\mathcal{L}}_{t\to s}\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq\frac{2RV_{0}^{2}}{3}(Ds)^{-\frac{3}{2}}
. Performing the inverse Laplace transform we arrive at the announced result (
76
).

To compute the full distribution
P(y,t)
, we consider
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(s)
, keep the leading order contributions in the limit of
s\to 0
and
p\to 0
, and deduce the announced Eq. (
77
) which leads to the scaling form of the distribution.
