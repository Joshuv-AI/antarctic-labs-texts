Uncertainty relations based on state-dependent norm of commutator
Aina Mayumi, Gen Kimura, Hiromichi Ohno, Dariusz Chruściński

Abstract
We introduce two uncertainty relations based on the state-dependent norm of commutators, utilizing generalizations of the Böttcher-Wenzel inequality. The first relation is mathematically proven, while the second, tighter relation is strongly supported by numerical evidence. Both relations surpass the conventional Robertson and Schrödinger bounds, particularly as the quantum state becomes increasingly mixed. This reveals a previously undetected complementarity of quantum uncertainty, stemming from the non-commutativity of observables. We also compare our results with the Luo-Park uncertainty relation, demonstrating that our bounds can outperform especially for mutually unbiased observables.

The main idea of the present paper is to generalize (

Recently, we explored several generalizations of the BW inequality using a state-dependent norm

\displaystyle{V_{\rho}(A)V_{\rho}(B)}\geq\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\rm M}}\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}

\displaystyle{V_{\rho}(A)V_{\rho}(B)}\geq\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}\lambda_{\rm sm}}{\lambda_{\rm m}+\lambda_{\rm sm}}\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho},

While the first relation (

8

In this paper, we conduct a systematic and thorough comparison between our relations (

8

In particular, we analytically compute the averaged bounds for all these relations in qubit systems and show that our bounds surpass the Robertson and Schrödinger bounds as a state becomes more mixed.
Observing that the bounds in (

8

On the other hand, we observe that the LP bound outperforms our bounds on average. We next compare the cases of mutually unbiased observables, or traditionally a complementary pair of observables
[

64

65

II
Extending the Böttcher-Wenzel inequality

In this section, we briefly review the discussion in
[
62
]
, adjusting it with the application to the uncertainty relation in mind.
In this paper, we restrict ourselves to finite-level quantum systems associated with a
d
-dimensional complex Hilbert space
\mathop{\mathcal{H}}\nolimits\simeq\mathop{\mathbb{C}}\nolimits^{d}
. We use the standard Dirac notation with (ket) vectors
|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle\in\mathop{\mathcal{H}}\nolimits
, where their inner product and the norm are denoted by
\langle\psi,\phi\rangle
and
\|\psi\|:=\sqrt{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}
, respectively. Note that
|\psi\rangle\langle\phi|
is a linear operator. The adjoint matrix of a linear operator
A
is denoted by
A^{\dagger}
.
However,
n
-dimensional real vectors in
\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits^{n}
are distinctly denoted as
{\bm{a}}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}),{\bm{b}}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
, where the Euclidean inner product and norm are denoted by
|{\bm{a}}|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}}
,
{\bm{a}}\cdot{\bm{b}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}
, respectively.
We also use the notation
\overline{\alpha}
and
|\alpha|
to represent the complex conjugate and absolute value of a complex number
\alpha
, respectively.

For any (not necessarily normalized) density matrix
\rho
, we define a semi-inner product between matrices
A,B
by

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle_{\rho}:=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B\rho).

(9)

By the positive semi-definiteness of
\rho
and the cyclic property of the trace operation, it is straightforward to see (i)
\langle A,A\rangle_{\rho}\geq 0
, (ii)
\overline{\langle B,A\rangle_{\rho}}=\langle A,B\rangle_{\rho}
, and (iii)
\langle A,\alpha B+\beta C\rangle_{\rho}=\alpha\langle A,B\rangle_{\rho}+\beta\langle A,C\rangle_{\rho}
for all matrices
A,B,C
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathop{\mathbb{C}}\nolimits
.
The weighted Frobenius semi-norm is given as the induced semi-norm:

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\rho}:=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle_{\rho}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}A\rho)},

(10)

which satisfies (i)
\|A\|_{\rho}\geq 0
, (ii)
\|\alpha A\|_{\rho}=|\alpha|\|A\|_{\rho}
, and (iii)
\|A+B\|_{\rho}\leq\|A\|_{\rho}+\|B\|_{\rho}
for all matrices
A,B
and
\alpha\in\mathop{\mathbb{C}}\nolimits
.
The expressions (
9
) and (
10
) generalize the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product and Frobenius norm, respectively, when
\rho=\mathop{\mathbb{I}}\nolimits
.

Let
\lambda_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,d
) the eigenvalues of
\rho
arranged in descending order:
0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}
with the corresponding normalized eigenvectors
|\lambda_{i}\rangle
. Throughout this paper, the notations

\lambda_{\rm m}=\lambda_{1},\lambda_{\rm sm}=\lambda_{2}\ {\rm and}\ \lambda_{\rm M}=\lambda_{d}

represent the smallest, the second smallest and the largest eigenvalues of
\rho
, respectively. Using the eigenvalue decomposition
\rho=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|\lambda_{i}\rangle\langle\lambda_{i}|
, one has
\|A\|^{2}_{\rho}=\mathrm{Tr}A^{\dagger}A\rho=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\langle\lambda_{i}|A^{\dagger}A\lambda_{i}\rangle
. Since
\|A\|^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}A^{\dagger}A=\sum_{i}\langle\lambda_{i}|A^{\dagger}A\lambda_{i}\rangle
, we observe

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm m}\|A\|^{2}\leq\|A\|^{2}_{\rho}\leq\lambda_{\rm M}\|A\|^{2}.

(11)

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\rm M}\|[A,B]\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{\rm M}\|A\|^{2}\|B\|^{2}\leq\frac{2\lambda_{\rm M}}{\lambda_{\rm m}^{2}}\|A\|^{2}_{\rho}\|B\|^{2}_{\rho},

(12)

where in the second inequality we have used the BW inequality (
5
).
According to the numerical simulations, however, the bound (
12
) appears not to be tight and could be further improved.
In
[
62
]
, we proposed a conjecture for the tight bound:

Conjecture 1

For any positive definite matrix
\rho
and for any complex matrices
A,B
, we have

\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}+\lambda_{\rm sm}}{\lambda_{\rm m}\lambda_{\rm sm}}\|A\|^{2}_{\rho}\|B\|^{2}_{\rho}.

(13)

This inequality is sharp, meaning that there exist non-zero matrices
A,B
, in particular Hermitian matrices, that achieve equality.

Several remarks are in order:
First, unlike the BW inequality, this bound is far from trivial even for Hermitian matrices. This is mainly because there are essentially three non-commutative matrices involved:
A
,
B
, and
\rho
.
Second, we have conducted the following numerical optimization

\max_{A,B\neq 0}\frac{\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}}{\|A\|^{2}_{\rho}\|B\|^{2}_{\rho}}

for randomly generated positive matrix
\rho
up to size
d=15
and the results perfectly match the conjectured bound
\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}+\lambda_{\rm sm}}{\lambda_{\rm m}\lambda_{\rm sm}}
in (
13
).
Third, the bound has been proven for
d=2
, hence can be applied to qubit systems.
In Appendix
B
, we provide an independent proof from the one given in
[
62
]
, adjusted for Hermitian matrices.
Fourth, for any
\rho
, there are non-zero Hermitian matrices
A
and
B
, e.g.,
A=\lambda_{2}|\lambda_{1}\rangle\langle\lambda_{1}|-\lambda_{1}|\lambda_{2}\rangle\langle\lambda_{2}|
and
B=|\lambda_{1}\rangle\langle\lambda_{2}|+|\lambda_{2}\rangle\langle\lambda_{1}|
, that achieve equality in (
13
), thus proving the tightness part of (
13
).
Fifth, the bounds (
13
) (and also (
12
)) are generalizations of the BW inequality (
5
) when
\rho=\mathop{\mathbb{I}}\nolimits
noting that
\lambda_{\rm m}=\lambda_{\rm sm}(=\lambda_{\rm M})=1
.
Finally, the bound can be trivially extended to include positive-semidefinite matrices
\rho
by interpreting the bound in (
13
) as infinite when
\lambda_{\rm m}
equals zero.

III
Application to uncertainty relations

The proposed uncertainty relations (
7
) and (
8
) can be readily shown by the bounds (
12
) and (
13
) as follows.
For any pair of observables
A
and
B
, their variances are expressed by using semi-norms:

V_{\rho}(A)=\|\hat{A}\|^{2}_{\rho},\ \ \ \ V_{\rho}(B)=\|\hat{B}\|^{2}_{\rho},

where
\hat{A}:=A-\langle A\rangle_{\rho}\mathop{\mathbb{I}}\nolimits,\hat{B}:=B-\langle B\rangle_{\rho}\mathop{\mathbb{I}}\nolimits
.
Therefore, we derive relations (
7
) and (
8
) by applying
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
to (
12
) and (
13
) with a quantum state
\rho
and noting
[\hat{A},\hat{B}]=[A,B]
.
Summarizing, we have

Theorem 1

For a
d
-level quantum system, the following uncertainty relation holds between observables
A
,
B
under a state
\rho
:

\displaystyle V_{\rho}(A)V_{\rho}(B)\geq\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\rm M}}\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}.

(14)

Here
\lambda_{\rm m}
and
\lambda_{\rm M}
are the smallest and the largest eigenvalues of
\rho
.

Moreover, based on Conjecture
1
, which is substantiated by numerical computations and proved for qubit system, we have

Conjecture 2

For a
d
-level quantum system, the following uncertainty relation holds between observables
A
,
B
under a state
\rho
:

\displaystyle V_{\rho}(A)V_{\rho}(B)\geq\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}\lambda_{\rm sm}}{\lambda_{\rm m}+\lambda_{\rm sm}}\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}.

(15)

Here
\lambda_{\rm m}
and
\lambda_{\rm sm}
are the smallest and the second smallest eigenvalues of
\rho
.

For qubit systems one has therefore

Corollary 1

For a
2
-level quantum system, the following uncertainty relation holds

\displaystyle V_{\rho}(A)V_{\rho}(B)\geq\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho},

(16)

where
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
are eigenvalues of
\rho
.

For a pure qubit state
\lambda_{1}=0
and hence the above bound is trivial. However for a genuine mixed state the bound is nontrivial for any pair of non-commuting observables.

The bounds in both relations are composed of the commutator, so our relations resemble the Robertson relation. However, as we will see below, they are stronger than the Robertson relation (and even than the Schrödinger relation) as a state becomes more mixed.

In the following, we present a detailed comparison of the uncertainty relations (
14
) and (
15
) with the Robertson and Schrödinger relations (
1
) and (
2
), as well as the LP relation (
4
), specifically within the context of a qubit system.
The lower bounds of each uncertainty relation, in the order of Robertson, Schrödinger, Luo-Park, (
14
) and (
15
) are given respectively by

\displaystyle B_{\rm R}(A,B,\rho)=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|\langle[A,B]\rangle_{\rho}\Bigr|^{2},

(17a)

\displaystyle B_{\rm S}(A,B,\rho)=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|\langle[A,B]\rangle_{\rho}\Bigr|^{2}+\Bigl|\frac{1}{2}\langle\{A,B\}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle A\rangle_{\rho}\langle B\rangle_{\rho}\Bigl|^{2},

(17b)

\displaystyle B_{\rm LP}(A,B,\rho)=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|\langle[A,B]\rangle_{\rho}\Bigr|^{2}+

\displaystyle\quad\Bigl(\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{\rho}A\sqrt{\rho}A-\langle A\rangle_{\rho}^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{\rho}B\sqrt{\rho}B-\langle B\rangle_{\rho}^{2}\Bigr),

(17c)

\displaystyle B_{\rm 1}(A,B,\rho)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\rm M}}\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho},

(17d)

\displaystyle B_{\rm 2}(A,B,\rho)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}\lambda_{\rm sm}}{\lambda_{\rm m}+\lambda_{\rm sm}}\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}.

(17e)

We have
B_{\rm S}(A,B,\rho),B_{\rm LP}(A,B,\rho)\geq B_{\rm R}(A,B,\rho)
and
B_{\rm 2}(A,B,\rho)\geq B_{\rm 1}(A,B,\rho)
. The last inequality is seen by considering the order
\lambda_{\rm m}\leq\lambda_{\rm sm}\leq\lambda_{\rm M}
.
However, the quality of these bounds depends on the choice of the physical quantities
A
and
B
, as well as the state
\rho
. In order to conduct a fair comparison and observe the universal properties and trends, our strategy is to first compare the average bounds across all pairs of physical quantities by restricting to qubit systems (Sec.
III.1
) and second to compare the average over all pairs of mutually unbiased observables for any finite quantum system (Sec.
III.2
).

Average bounds over all pair of observables in qubit systems

In this section, we compare the bounds in (

Notice that, for qubit cases, the tighter bound (

Specifically, by expanding observables

and

by Pauli matrices
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
:

\displaystyle A=\sum_{i=1}^{3}a_{i}\sigma_{i},B=\sum_{i=1}^{3}b_{i}\sigma_{i},

(18)

with unit vectors
2
2

2

Here, normalizations are performed to eliminate non-essential uncertainties due to the magnitude of the operators. Additionally, note that the components of the identity operator are independent of the uncertainty, and therefore are disregarded.

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{2}(\mathop{\mathbb{I}}\nolimits+\sum_{k=1}^{3}c_{k}\sigma_{k}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm m}=\frac{1-\sqrt{2P-1}}{2},\lambda_{\rm sm}=\lambda_{\rm M}=\frac{1+\sqrt{2P-1}}{2}.

Moreover, a direct computation gives

\displaystyle B_{\rm R}(A,B,\rho)=|(\bm{a}\times\bm{b})\cdot\bm{c}|^{2},

(21a)

\displaystyle B_{\rm S}(A,B,\rho)=|(\bm{a}\times\bm{b})\cdot\bm{c}|^{2}+|\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}-(\bm{a}\cdot\bm{c})(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{c})|^{2},

(21b)

\displaystyle B_{\rm LP}(A,B,\rho)=|(\bm{a}\times\bm{b})\cdot\bm{c}|^{2},

\displaystyle{}+\Bigl(\sqrt{1-|\bm{c}|^{2}}+(1-|\bm{c}|^{2}-\sqrt{1-|\bm{c}|^{2}})\frac{(\bm{a}\cdot\bm{c})^{2}}{|\bm{c}|^{2}}\Bigr),

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Bigl(\sqrt{1-|\bm{c}|^{2}}+(1-|\bm{c}|^{2}-\sqrt{1-|\bm{c}|^{2}})\frac{(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{c})^{2}}{|\bm{c}|^{2}}\Bigr),

(21c)

\displaystyle B_{\rm 1}(A,B,\rho)=2\frac{P-\sqrt{2P-1}}{1+\sqrt{2P-1}}|\bm{a}\times\bm{b}|^{2},

(21d)

\displaystyle B_{\rm 2}(A,B,\rho)=2(1-P)|\bm{a}\times\bm{b}|^{2},

(21e)

where
\bm{a}\times\bm{b}
is the cross product.
As a side remark,

, appearing in (

Finally, by using general formulas (

\displaystyle\langle B_{\rm R}(A,B,\rho)\rangle_{\rm av}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{9}(2P-1):=B_{\rm R}(P),

\displaystyle\langle B_{\rm S}(A,B,\rho)\rangle_{\rm av}

\displaystyle=B_{\rm R}(P)+\frac{2}{9}(2P^{2}-4P+3),

(22b)

\displaystyle\langle B_{\rm LP}(A,B,\rho)\rangle_{\rm av}

\displaystyle=B_{\rm R}(P)+\frac{4}{9}\Bigl((1-P)+\sqrt{2(1-P)}\Bigr)^{2},

(22c)

\displaystyle\langle B_{\rm 1}(A,B,\rho)\rangle_{\rm av}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\frac{P-\sqrt{2P-1}}{1+\sqrt{2P-1}},

(22d)

\displaystyle\langle B_{\rm 2}(A,B,\rho)\rangle_{\rm av}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}(1-P).

(22e)

Figure

shows the graphs of averaged bounds (

) - (

) as functions of the purity
P
.
One observes that, as the state becomes more mixed, both our bounds (
22d
) and (
22e
) surpass those of Robertson and Schrödinger.
Specifically, the bound (
22e
) outperforms the Robertson bound if
P\leq P_{\rm R}:=7/8=0.875
and the Schrödinger bound if
P\leq P_{\rm S}=\sqrt{3}-1\simeq 0.732
.
This fact implies that our bounds are detecting a previously unidentified trade-off in quantum uncertainty arising from the non-commutativity of observables.

On the other hand, the LP bound exhibits a similar tendency to our bounds regarding the dependency on purity, and is outperforming them. However, this result is primarily an average behavior and, as seen below, our bound can provide a tighter bound for specific physical quantities.

III.2
Average bounds over all pair of mutually unbiased observables

Two non-degenerate observables
A
and
B
in
d
-level systems are said to be mutually unbiased (or complementary) if

|\langle a_{i}|b_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\quad\forall i,j=1,\ldots,d

where
\{|a_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{d}
and
\{|b_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{d}
are the set of normalized eigenvectors of
A
and
B
.
Physically speaking,
A,B
are mutually unbiased when one is most uncertain when the other is deterministic due to the Born’s rule
[
63
,
64
,
65
,
41
]
. Therefore, the trade-off in uncertainty relations is expected to be most significant between physical quantities that are mutually unbiased.
This is indeed manifested in the entropic uncertainty relation
[
16
]
.
In this section, we compare our bounds and others for this important class of observables.

Before discussing the general cases, let us first consider some specific examples in qubit systems.
Let
A
and
B
be mutually unbiased and consider the case where
A
and
\rho
are commutative, i.e., the situation where a state has no coherence of
A
.
In qubit systems, these conditions correspond to the assumptions that the corresponding vectors
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
are perpendicular (for mutual unbiasedness), and
{\bm{a}}
and
{\bm{c}}
are parallel:

\displaystyle\bm{a}\perp\bm{b},{\bm{a}}\parallel{\bm{c}}.

(23)

In this case, it is easy to see that both Robertson and Schrödinger bounds are zero (as
{\bm{a}}\times{\bm{b}}\perp{\bm{c}}
), and thus fail to capture the intrinsic complementarity within mutually unbiased observables.
On the other hand, both the LP bound (
21c
) and our bounds (
21d
) and (
21e
) do, since we have

\displaystyle B_{\rm LP}(A,B,\rho)

\displaystyle=(2(1-P))^{3/2},

\displaystyle B_{1}(A,B,\rho)

\displaystyle=2\frac{P-\sqrt{2P-1}}{1+\sqrt{2P-1}},

\displaystyle B_{2}(A,B,\rho)

\displaystyle=2(1-P).

Therefore, the bound (
21e
) outperforms the LP bound for any
P
, while the bound (
21d
) is inferior to the PL bound. In order to be slightly more general, consider mutually unbiased
A
and
B
(i.e.,
{\bm{a}}\perp{\bm{b}}
), but let the state
{\bm{c}}
lie in the plane composed of
{\bm{a}}
and
{\bm{b}}
.
Choosing a Cartesian coordinate system where the
x
-axis and
y
-axis are oriented in the directions of
{\bm{a}}
and
{\bm{b}}
, we can write
{\bm{a}}=(1,0,0),{\bm{b}}=(0,1,0),{\bm{c}}=\sqrt{2P-1}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)
with a parameter
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. Then, the LP bound (
21c
) is calculated as

q^{2}(1+(q-1)\cos^{2}\theta)(1+(q-1)\sin^{2}\theta)=q^{2}(q+(q-1)^{2}\frac{\sin^{2}{2\theta}}{4})

where
q=\sqrt{2(1-P)}
. Therefore, the LP bound takes the maximum when
\theta=\pi/4
:

\frac{q^{2}(1+q)^{2}}{4}.

and takes the minimum when
\theta=0
, which is the above case.
Since our bound (
21e
) remains to be the same
2(1-P)=q^{2}
, this is always greater than the PL bound for any
P
and
\theta
.

Now, we consider general mutually unbiased observables
A
and
B
in
d
-level systems and treat the case where
A
and
\rho
are commutative: Let
\{|j\rangle\}_{j=1}^{d}
be the common eigenbasis of
A
and
\rho
that simultaneously diagonalize them, so that their eigenvalue decompositions are given by
A=\sum_{j}a_{j}|j\rangle\langle j|
and
\rho=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|j\rangle\langle j|
. Let
B=\sum_{j}b_{j}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|
be an eigenvalue decomposition of
B
, where we assume the mutually unbiased condition:

|\langle j|b_{k}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\ \forall j,k=1,\ldots,d.

We can rewrite this condition using a phase information
\theta_{jk}\in\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits
by

\displaystyle\langle j|b_{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}e^{i\theta_{jk}}.

(24)

Notice that both Robertson and Schrödinger bounds vanish also in this general setting. This can be easily seen by using
[A,\rho]=0
and the cyclic property of trace as follows:

\displaystyle B_{\rm R}(A,B,\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}|\mathrm{Tr}[A,B]\rho|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}|\mathrm{Tr}B[\rho,A]|^{2}=0.

\displaystyle B_{\rm S}(A,B,\rho)

\displaystyle=0+\Bigl|\frac{1}{2}\langle\{A,B\}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle A\rangle_{\rho}\langle B\rangle_{\rho}\Bigl|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\mathrm{Tr}BA\rho-\mathrm{Tr}A\rho\mathrm{Tr}B\rho\Bigl|^{2}.

However, the last term vanishes as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}BA\rho-\mathrm{Tr}A\rho\mathrm{Tr}B\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}a_{j}\lambda_{j}b_{k}|\langle j|b_{k}\rangle|^{2}-(\sum_{i}a_{i}\lambda_{i})(\sum_{j,k}b_{k}\lambda_{j}|\langle j|b_{k}\rangle|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}(\sum_{j}a_{j}\lambda_{j})(\sum_{k}b_{k})-\frac{1}{d}(\sum_{i}a_{i}\lambda_{i})(\sum_{j}\lambda_{j})(\sum_{k}b_{k})

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore, we emphasize again that, in general, the Robertson and Schrödinger relations fail to capture the complementarity between mutually unbiased physical quantities.
On the other hand, both the LP bound (
17c
) and our bounds ((
17d
) and (
17e
)) are capable of detecting this complementarity, as the LP bound (
17c
) is given by the product of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}({A\sqrt{\rho}A\sqrt{\rho}})-(\mathrm{Tr}A\rho)^{2}=\sum_{j}a^{2}_{j}\lambda_{j}-\sum_{j,k}a_{j}a_{k}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k},

(25)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}({B\sqrt{\rho}B\sqrt{\rho}})-(\mathrm{Tr}B\rho)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}|\langle k|B|j\rangle|^{2}-(\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\langle j|B|j\rangle)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\sum_{l,m}b_{l}b_{m}e^{-i(\theta_{kl}-\theta_{jl})}e^{i(\theta_{km}-\theta_{jm})}

\displaystyle\quad{}-\frac{1}{d^{2}}(\sum_{j,k}b_{j}b_{k}),

(26)

and our bounds (
17d
) (resp. (
17e
)) are given by the product of
\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\rm M}}
(resp.
\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}\lambda_{\rm sm}}{\lambda_{\rm m}+\lambda_{\rm sm}}
) and the
\rho
-norm of the commutator:

\displaystyle\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}=\mathrm{Tr}(BA^{2}B\rho)+\mathrm{Tr}(B^{2}A^{2}\rho)-2\mathrm{Tr}(BABA\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\lambda_{k}a_{j}(a_{j}-2a_{k})|\langle j|B|k\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}a_{j}^{2}\langle j|B^{2}|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k}\lambda_{k}a_{j}(a_{j}-2a_{k})\sum_{l,m}b_{l}b_{m}e^{-i(\theta_{kl}-\theta_{jl})}e^{i(\theta_{km}-\theta_{jm})}

\displaystyle\quad{}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}a_{j}^{2}\sum_{l}b_{l}^{2}.

(27)

Now, we would like to compare the LP bound with our bounds, especially the tighter one (
17e
).
In order to conduct a general comparison, we take the average over all eigenvalues over the sphere
S_{d-1}\subset\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits^{d}
of a mutually unbiased pair of
A
and
B
.

For the LP bound, we can compute the averages of (
25
) and (
26
) over
{\bm{a}}\in S_{d-1}
and
{\bm{b}}\in S_{d-1}
independently; Using the formula (
45
) in Appendix
C
, it is easy to compute the average of (
25
) and (
26
), given respectively by

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{1}{d}\lambda_{j}-\sum_{j,k}\frac{\delta_{jk}}{d}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}^{2}=\frac{1-P}{d}

(28)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}-\frac{1}{d^{2}}=\frac{(\sum_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}})^{2}-1}{d^{2}}.

(29)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle B_{\rm LP}(A,B,\rho)\rangle_{{\rm av.}}=\frac{(1-\sum_{j}\lambda^{2}_{j})((\sum_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}})^{2}-1)}{d^{3}}.

(30)

To compute the average of our bound, using the formula (
45
) in Appendix
C
, one can compute the average of (
27
) over
{\bm{b}}
and then average further over
{\bm{a}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}\rangle_{{\rm av.}}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}-\frac{2}{d^{3}}+\frac{1}{d^{2}}=\frac{2(d-1)}{d^{3}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{4}&d=2\\
\frac{4}{27}&d=3\\
\frac{3}{32}&d=4\\
:\end{array}\right.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\langle B_{2}(A,B,\rho)\rangle_{{\rm av.}}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm m}\lambda_{\rm sm}}{\lambda_{\rm m}+\lambda_{\rm sm}}\frac{2(d-1)}{d^{3}}.

(36)

In the case of general dimensions, whether (
30
) or (
36
) provide a better bound depends on the eigenvalues of the state. However, in the case of
d=2
, we find that our bound always outperform the LP bound (See Figure (
2
)]).

IV
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced two uncertainty relations based on generalizations of the Böttcher-Wenzel inequality utilizing state-dependent norms of commutators.
The first relation (
14
) is mathematically proven, while the second tighter relation (
15
) is strongly supported by extensive numerical evidence and proven for the case of qubit systems.
Through systematic comparisons, we have demonstrated that our uncertainty relations can surpass the conventional Robertson and Schrödinger bounds, especially as the quantum state becomes increasingly mixed. This reveals a previously unrecognized aspect of quantum uncertainty originating from the non-commutativity of observables.
On the other hand, the Luo-Park relation exhibits a similar dependence on state purity and outperforms our bounds on average over all observables in qubit systems. However, when considering the important case of mutually unbiased observables, our tighter relation (
15
) can surpass the Luo-Park bound, potentially providing a better description of the complementarity between such observables.
Overall, our results shed new light on the role of non-commutativity in quantum uncertainty relations and may find applications in fields where such trade-off relations play a fundamental role, such as quantum metrology and quantum computing.

Appendix A
Proof of BW inequality for normal matrices

Here, let us introduce a simple proof of the BW inequality (
5
) given in
[
47
]
for the cases where either
A
or
B
is normal:

[Proof] Let
A
be a normal matrix, i.e.,
[A,A^{\dagger}]=0
and
A=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}|a_{i}\rangle\langle a_{i}|
be an eigenvalue decomposition of
A
. Let also
b_{ij}=\langle a_{i}|Ba_{j}\rangle
.
A direct computation shows
\|[A,B]\|^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}[A,B]^{\dagger}[A,B]=\sum_{i,j}|a_{i}-a_{j}|^{2}|b_{ij}|^{2}
. However, this is bounded from above by
\sum_{i\neq j}|a_{i}-a_{j}|^{2}|b_{ij}|^{2}\leq\sum_{i\neq j}2(|a|^{2}_{i}+|a|^{2}_{j})|b_{ij}|^{2}\leq 2\sum_{i\neq j}\|A\|^{2}|b_{ij}|^{2}\leq 2\|A\|^{2}\|B\|^{2}
, where we have used
\|A\|^{2}=\sum_{i}|a_{i}|^{2}
and
\|B\|^{2}=\sum_{i,j}|b_{ij}|^{2}
.
\blacksquare

Appendix B
Proof of BW inequality for qubit systems

In this appendix, we present an elementary proof of (
13
) for
d=2
.
For our application,
\rho
is a normalized density operator, and both
A
and
B
are assumed to be Hermitian. For the general proof, see
[
62
]
.

[Proof] Similar to (
18
), expand arbitrary Hermitian matrices
A,B
with Pauli basis:

\displaystyle A=a_{0}\mathop{\mathbb{I}}\nolimits+\sum_{i=1}^{3}a_{i}\sigma_{i},B=b_{0}\mathop{\mathbb{I}}\nolimits+\sum_{i=1}^{3}b_{i}\sigma_{i},

(37)

with real numbers
a_{0},b_{0}
and
{\bm{a}}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}),{\bm{b}}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Using Bloch vector representation (
19
) for
\rho
, one has

\displaystyle\|[A,B]\|^{2}_{\rho}=4|{\bm{a}}\times{\bm{b}}|^{2}

(38)

\displaystyle\|A\|^{2}_{\rho}=a_{0}^{2}+|{\bm{a}}|^{2}+2a_{0}{\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{a}}

(39)

\displaystyle\|B\|^{2}_{\rho}=b_{0}^{2}+|{\bm{b}}|^{2}+2b_{0}{\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{b}}

(40)

and
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}=\frac{1-|{\bm{c}}|^{2}}{4}
.
Therefore, inequality (
13
) is equivalent to

(a_{0}^{2}+|{\bm{a}}|^{2}+2a_{0}{\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{a}})(b_{0}^{2}+|{\bm{b}}|^{2}+2b_{0}{\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{b}})\geq(1-|{\bm{c}}|^{2})|{\bm{a}}\times{\bm{b}}|^{2}

for any
a_{0},b_{0}\in\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits
and
{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}},{\bm{c}}\in\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits^{3}
where
|{\bm{c}}|^{2}\leq 1
.
Since
(a_{0}^{2}+|{\bm{a}}|^{2}+2a_{0}{\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{a}})=(a_{0}+{\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{a}})^{2}+|{\bm{a}}|^{2}-({\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{a}})^{2}\geq|{\bm{a}}|^{2}-({\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{a}})^{2}
, and the same holds for
B
, it is enough to show

(|{\bm{a}}|^{2}-({\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{a}})^{2})(|{\bm{b}}|^{2}-({\bm{c}}\cdot{\bm{b}})^{2})\geq(1-|{\bm{c}}|^{2})|{\bm{a}}\times{\bm{b}}|^{2}.

(41)

By choosing
z
-axis in the direction of
{\bm{c}}
, one may write
{\bm{c}}=(0,0,r)
for
0\leq r\leq 1
, without loss of generality, so that (
41
) reads

\displaystyle(|{\bm{a}}|^{2}-r^{2}a_{3}^{2})(|{\bm{b}}|^{2}-r^{2}b_{3}^{2})\geq(1-r^{2})|{\bm{a}}\times{\bm{b}}|^{2}.

(42)

If we introduce compressed vectors
{\bm{a}}^{\prime}=(a_{1},a_{2},\sqrt{(1-r^{2})}a_{3})
,
{\bm{b}}^{\prime}=(b_{1},b_{2},\sqrt{(1-r^{2})}b_{3})
with a factor
\sqrt{1-r^{2}}
in
z
-direction, one can easily show

\displaystyle|{\bm{a}^{\prime}}\times{\bm{b}^{\prime}}|^{2}\geq(1-r^{2})|{\bm{a}}\times{\bm{b}}|^{2}.

(43)

(This can be readily shown by computing the vector components for the cross products.)
By noting
|{\bm{a}^{\prime}}|^{2}=(|{\bm{a}}|^{2}-r^{2}a_{3}^{2}),\ |{\bm{b}^{\prime}}|^{2}=(|{\bm{b}}|^{2}-r^{2}b_{3}^{2})
and
|{\bm{a}^{\prime}}|^{2}|{\bm{b}^{\prime}}|^{2}\geq|{\bm{a}^{\prime}}\times{\bm{b}^{\prime}}|^{2}
, inequality (
43
) implies (
42
).

\blacksquare

Appendix C
Uniform average on the sphere

In this section, we give a useful formula for the average over the uniform measure
d\mu
on the sphere
S_{d-1}\subset\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits^{d}
. The average of the function
f({\bm{x}})
of
{\bm{x}}\in\mathop{\mathbb{R}}\nolimits^{d}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle f({\bm{x}})\rangle_{{\rm av.}}:=\int_{S_{d-1}}d\mu f({\bm{x}}).

(44)

For any unit vector
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in S_{d-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle x_{j}x_{k}\rangle_{{\rm av.}}:=\int_{S_{d-1}}d\mu x_{j}x_{k}=\frac{\delta_{jk}}{d}.

(45)

For completeness, we provide an elementary proof of this formula below.

[Proof] By the rotational symmetry, it is clear that
\int d\mu x^{2}_{i}
does not depend on
i=1,\ldots,d
and also for
i\neq j=1,\ldots,d
,
\int d\mu x_{i}x_{j}
does not depend on the pair
i\neq j
: Let
c:=\int d\mu x^{2}_{i}
and
c^{\prime}:=\int d\mu x_{i}x_{j}\ (i\neq j)
. From the normalization condition,

1=\int_{S_{d-1}}d\mu\sum_{i}x^{2}_{i}=\sum_{i}\int_{S_{d-1}}d\mu x^{2}_{i},

and we get
\sum_{i}\int d\mu x^{2}_{i}=\frac{1}{d}
.
Next, observe that

\int d\mu(\sum_{i}x_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i}\int d\mu x^{2}_{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}\int d\mu x_{i}x_{j}=1+(d^{2}-d)c^{\prime}

On the other hand, if we choose another coordinate system
x^{\prime}_{i}
satisfying
x^{\prime}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i}x_{i}
, the left hand side is

\int d\mu(\sqrt{d}x^{\prime}_{1})^{2}=d\times\frac{1}{d}=1.

Therefore, we have
c^{\prime}=0
.
\blacksquare
