The Banach-Tarski Paradox
Katie Buchhorn

Abstract




Supervised by Adam Sierakowski and David Robertson

Abstract

In 1924, S. Banach and A. Tarski proved an astonishing, yet rather counterintuitive paradox: given a solid ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, it is possible to partition it into finitely many pieces and reassemble them to form two solid balls, each identical in size to the first.

This paper was written for the fulfilment of a
summer scholarship
during an undergraduate degree at the University of Wollongong. The aim of the paper is to provide a comprehensive proof of the Banach-Tarski paradox thereby introducing the notions of paradoxical and equidecomposable sets which are phrased in terms of group actions. Once the reader has a firm grasp of these ideas, the proof of the Banach-Tarski Paradox is fairly straight forward, provided we have the Axiom of Choice at our disposal.

1
Paradoxical Decompositions

Definition 1.1
.

A group
G
is a set together with a binary operation

\displaystyle G\times G\rightarrow

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle(g,h)\mapsto

\displaystyle gh

satisfying the following axioms:

(i)

Closure: if
a
,
b\in G
, then
ab\in G
.

(ii)

Identity: There is an identity element
e\in G
such that
ea=a=ae
, for every
a\in G
.

(iii)

Inverse: There must exist an inverse for each group element,

i.e., for each
a\in G
,
\exists a^{-1}\in G
such that
aa^{-1}=e=a^{-1}a
.

(iv)

Associativity: for all
a,b,c\in G
,
(ab)c=a(bc)
.

The operation with respect to which the group is formed is called the
group

operation
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G
be a group and let
X
be a set. Then a (left) group action of
G
on
X
is a function

G\times X\rightarrow X,

(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x

that satisfies the following axioms:

(i)

Associativity:
(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)
for all
g,h\in G
and
x\in X
.

(ii)

Identity:
e\cdot a=a
for all
x\in X
(where
e
denotes the identity element in
G
).

Definition 1.3
.

G
be a group acting on a set
X
and suppose
E\subseteq X
.
E
is
G
-paradoxical
if for some positive integers
m,n
there exists pairwise disjoint subsets
A_{1},...,A_{n},B_{1},...,B_{m}
of
E
and
g_{1},...,g_{n},h_{1},...,h_{m}\in G
such that

E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\cdot A_{i}\text{ and }E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}\cdot B_{i}

A good way to interpret this is to picture
E
containing two disjoint subsets
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}
each of which can be broken down into their namesakes
A_{i}
or
B_{i}
and then moved around consecutively via elements of the group
G
to cover all of
E
.

Note also that there is a stronger alternative; finding subsets
A_{1},...,A_{n},B_{1},...,B_{m}
of
E
which in fact
partition

E
(The only extra condition being that these subsets initially union to give
E
). This idea will be formally introduced later.

1.1
Free Groups

Free groups will be a recurring idea in the progression of the paper, so we will formally introduce the concept. If
S
is a set, the free group generated by
S
is the group of all reduced finite words with letters from
\{s,s^{-1}\mid s\in S\}
. A word is called reduced if it contains no pairs of adjacent letters
ss^{-1}
or
s^{-1}s
. The group composition is concatenation of words followed by reduction, that is, removing pairs of the mentioned forms. The rank of a group is the size of the generating set.

Theorem 1.4
.

A free group
\mathbb{F}_{2}
of rank 2 is
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-paradoxical, where
\mathbb{F}_{2}
acts on itself by left multiplication.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{2}
be the free group with the generating set
\{a,b\}
and let
\rho\in\{a,a^{-1},b,b^{-1}\}
. Now let
\Psi(\rho)
denote the set of elements in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
beginning with
\rho
.

\mathbb{F}_{2}
on two generators
a
and
b

It can be easily seen from Figure 1 that

\mathbb{F}_{2}=\{e\}\bigcup\Psi(a)\bigcup\Psi(a^{-1})\bigcup\Psi(b)\bigcup\Psi(b^{-1})

(1)

and that these subsets are pairwise disjoint. We are aiming to find group elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
that act on
\Psi(a)
and
\Psi(a^{-1})
to rearrange the subsets in such a way that their union will give
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and similarly for
\Psi(b)
and
\Psi(b^{-1})
.

\mathbb{F}_{2}
and its subsets

For any element
h\in F\setminus\Psi(a)
, this implies that
h\notin\Psi(a)
. So
h
does not begin with the letter

. Then, if

Now,

Notice that the following choice of subsets would have indeed

2
Equidecomposibility

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a set
X
, and let
A,B\subseteq X
. We say that
A
and
B
are
G
-congruent
if there exists
g\in G
such that
g\cdot A=B
.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose
G
acts on
X
and
A,B\subseteq X
.
A
and
B
are
G
-equidecomposable
(denoted
A\sim_{G}B
) if
A
and
B
can each be partitioned into the same finite number of respectively
G
-congruent pieces. Formally written,
A\sim_{G}B
if
A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
,
B=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}
with

A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset=B_{i}\cap B_{j}\text{ for }i<j\leq n,

and there are
g_{1},...,g_{n}\in G
such that, for each
i\leq n
,
g_{i}\cdot A_{i}=B_{i}
.

In plain terms this definition means to say that if you can take one subset,
A
, and break it apart into
n
pieces, rearrange those
n
pieces via the group action of
G
to form the
n
pieces that union together to form another subset
B
, then
A
and
B
are
G
-equidecomposable.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
G
acts on
X
and
E\subseteq X
. If the set
E
has two disjoint subsets
A
and
B
such that
A\sim_{G}E
and
B\sim_{G}E
, then
E
is
G
-paradoxical.

Figure 3
An illustration of Lemma 2.3

Proof.

We know that, because
A\subseteq E
and
E
are
G
-equidecomposable, that
A
and
E
can each be partitioned into the same number of respectively
G
-congruent pieces, so

A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\text{ and }E=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}\text{ for some $A_{i}\subseteq A$ and $E_{j}\subseteq E$}

\text{and, for each $i\leq n$, }g_{i}\cdot A_{i}=E_{i}\text{ with }g_{i}\in G

Similarly, for
B\subseteq E
and
E
,

B=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}B_{k}\text{ and }E=\bigcup_{l=1}^{m}E_{l}\text{ for some $B_{k}\subseteq A$ and $E_{l}\subseteq E$}

\text{and, for each $i\leq m$, }h_{i}\cdot A_{i}=E_{i}\text{ with }h_{i}\in G

Therefore,

E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}E_{i}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}\cdot B_{i}\text{ and }E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\cdot A_{i}

where each
A_{1},...,A_{n}
and each
B_{1},...,B_{m}
are pairwise disjoint, and
A
and
B
are also disjoint, so we can conclude that subsets
A_{1},...,A_{n},B_{1},...,B_{m}
of
E
are also pairwise disjoint. Hence
E
is
G
-paradoxical.
∎

3
Banach-Schroder-Bernstein Theorem

This theorem is an interesting concept to visualise, and acts as a platform for further study on equidecomposibility relationships. A detailed proof has been included for the readers’ ease.

Lemma 3.1
.

if
A\sim B
then there is a bijection
g:A\rightarrow B
such that
C\sim g(C)
whenever
C\subseteq A

Lemma 3.2
.

if
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\emptyset=B_{1}\cap B_{2}
, and if
A_{1}\sim B_{1}
and
A_{2}\sim B_{2}
, then
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\sim B_{1}\cup B_{2}
.

Theorem 3.3

(Banach-Schroder-Bernstein Theorem)
.

Suppose
G
acts on
X
and
A,B\subseteq X
. If
A
is G-equidecomposable to a subset of
B
, and if
B
is G-equidecomposable to a subset of
A
, then
A
is G-equidecomposable to
B
.

Proof.

Denote
B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}
and
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}
. Define
f:A\rightarrow B_{1}
and
g:A_{1}\rightarrow B
as bijections guaranteed by Lemma 3.1 as
A\sim B_{1}
and
B\sim A_{1}
.

Let
C_{0}=A\setminus A_{1}
and define inductively
C_{n+1}=g^{-1}f(C_{n})
. Now, let
C=\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=0}C_{n}
. We claim that

g(A\setminus C)=B\setminus f(C)

(2)

To see this, consider
x\in A_{1}
,

\displaystyle g(x)\in B\setminus f(C)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow g(x)\notin f(C)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow g(x)\notin f(\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=0}C_{n})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow g(x)\notin\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=0}f(C_{n})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow g(x)\notin f(C_{n})\text{ for all }n\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow g^{-1}g(x)\notin g^{-1}f(C_{n})\text{ for all }n\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x\notin C_{n+1}\text{ for all }n\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x\notin\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=0}C_{n+1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x\notin\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=1}C_{n}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x\notin C\text{ by }\ast

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x\in A\setminus C

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow g(x)\in g(A\setminus C)

\ast
provided
x\notin C_{0}\Leftrightarrow x\notin A\setminus A_{1}
. Which is true because
x\in A_{1}
.

So our claim is true. We now wish to show that
g(A\setminus C)\sim A\setminus C
. We can use Lemma 3.1 but first we need to convince ourselves that
A\setminus C\subseteq A_{1}
.

\displaystyle C=\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=0}C_{n}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow C_{0}\subseteq C

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow A\setminus C\subseteq A\setminus C_{0}=A_{1}

Indeed,
A\setminus C\sim g(A\setminus C)=B\setminus f(C)
* by equation (2). We know
C\subseteq A
, so by Lemma 3.1 we have
C\sim g(C)
**.

The fact that
(A\setminus C)\cap C=\emptyset=(B\setminus f(C))\cap f(C)
with (*) and (**) allows us to utilise Lemma 3.2, so
(A\setminus C)\cup C\sim(B\setminus f(C))\cup f(C)\Rightarrow A\sim B
.

∎

Theorem 3.4
.

\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
\mathbb{Z}
are G-equidecomposable, where G is the group of all bijections on
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

Let
N_{1}
be the set of even numbers (including zero), and
N_{2}
be the set of odd numbers. So

N_{1}\cup N_{2}=\{0,2,4,6,8....\}\cup\{1,3,5,7,9....\}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

Let
Z_{1}
be the set of positive integers (including zero) and
Z_{2}
be the set of odd integers. So

Z_{1}\cup Z_{2}=\{0,1,2,3,4....\}\cup\{-1,-2,-3,-4....\}=\mathbb{Z}

and
N_{1}\cap N_{2}=\emptyset=Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}
.

Define the bijection
g_{1}:N_{1}\rightarrow Z_{1}
as

\displaystyle g_{1}x

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2}

\displaystyle\therefore g_{1}\cdot N_{1}

\displaystyle=Z_{1},\text{ where }g_{1}\in G

Define the bijection
g_{2}:N_{2}\rightarrow Z_{2}
as

\displaystyle g_{2}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{x+1}{2}

\displaystyle\therefore g_{2}\cdot N_{2}

\displaystyle=Z_{2},\text{ where }g_{2}\in G

So
\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
\mathbb{Z}
each can be partitioned into two respectively
G
-congruent pieces, hence
(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})\sim\mathbb{Z}

∎

Theorem 3.5
.

S^{1}
is
SO_{2}
-equidecomposable to
S^{1}\setminus\{1\}
, where
SO_{2}
= group of rotations in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
S^{1}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=1\}
the circle radius 1 centred about the origin.

Proof.

Let
\theta
be a counterclockwise rotation by, say, 1 radians around the origin. Since
2\pi
is irrational we see that
\theta^{n}\cdot 1
never comes back to coincide with
1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}.

Let

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\{1,\theta\cdot 1,\theta^{2}\cdot 1,\theta^{3}\cdot 1,....\}\text{ and }A_{2}=S^{1}\setminus A_{1}

so that
A_{1}\cup A_{2}=S^{1}
and
A_{1}\cap A_{2}=\emptyset

Let

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\theta\cdot A_{1}=\{\theta\cdot 1,\theta^{2}\cdot 1,\theta^{3}\cdot 1,....\}

\displaystyle\text{ and }B_{2}

\displaystyle=1\cdot A_{2}=S^{1}\setminus A_{1},\text{where 1 is the identity element in }SO_{2}

so that
B_{1}\cup B_{2}=S^{1}\setminus\{1\}
and
B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset

∎

This theorem illustrates how you can take a circle, partition it into no more than two pieces, rearrange one piece via the group of rotations to give you two pieces of that original circle only now with a point missing. The choice of rotation was paramount here, ensuring that no multiple of
\theta
will be the identity rotation and hence fill the hole in the circle at 1.

B^{3}

Let

A_{0}
,
A

B_{0}=A_{0}=B^{3}\setminus C^{1}

B_{0}

.

Theorem 3.5 gives us that

4
The importance of independence

A set
S
of elements in a group are called
independent
if no non-trivial, reduced words using letters from
S
and their inverses is the identity. Hence, a pair of independent elements will generate a free subgroup or rank 2.

Theorem 4.1
.

There are two independent rotations about the axis through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence,
SO_{3}
contains a free subgroup of rank 2.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a counter clockwise rotation about the z-axis through an angle of
\theta=cos^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)
. Let
\tau
be a counter clockwise rotation about the x-axis through the same angle
\theta
.

We have

\sigma=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{3}{5}&\frac{-4}{5}&0\\
\frac{4}{5}&\frac{3}{5}&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)

and

\tau=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&\frac{3}{5}&\frac{-4}{5}\\
0&\frac{4}{5}&\frac{3}{5}\end{array}\right)

We wish to show that no non-trivial reduced word in
\{\sigma,\sigma^{-1},\tau,\tau^{-1}\}
is the identity homeomorphism.
We claim that conjugation by
\sigma
does not effect whether or not a word is the identity. To see this, let
y
be some non-trivial, reduced word in
\{\sigma,\sigma^{-1},\tau,\tau^{-1}\}
and let
\gamma=\sigma^{-1}\cdot y\cdot\sigma
. There are two cases;

(i)

y
contains either
\tau
or
\tau^{-1}
. Then
\gamma
must be non-trivial when reduced.

(ii)

y
does not contain either
\tau
or
\tau^{-1}
. Then, since
y
was assumed to be reduced,
y
can only be either a sequence of
\sigma
or a sequence of
\sigma^{-1}
of length
>
0. No complete reduction can occur in
\gamma
(leaving
\gamma
non-trivial when reduced), hence our claim is true.

In order to prove the theorem, we need only to consider words ending (on the right) with
\sigma^{\pm 1}
(words ending in
\sigma^{-1}
after conjugation by
\sigma
for the case when
y=...\sigma^{-1}\cdot\sigma^{-1}
, or
y=\sigma^{-1}
). We now claim that for any such word
\omega
the point
\omega(1,0,0)
will take the form
(a,b,c)/5^{n}
, where n is the integer length of
\omega
;
a,b
and
c
are all integers with

b
not divisible by 5. Given that zero is indeed divisible by 5, this claim implies that
\omega(1,0,0)\not=(1,0,0)
, so
\omega
is not the identity homeomorphism.

Let us prove this claim by induction;

For
n=1
, we have
\omega=\sigma^{\pm 1}
and so

\displaystyle\omega(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\pm 1}(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}3&\mp 4&0\\
\pm 4&3&0\\
0&0&5\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\\
0\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=(3,\pm 4,0)/5

which is of the required form.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
. Assume claim is true for
n=1,2,..,k-1
. Let
\omega_{n}
be any non-trivial, reduced word ending in
\sigma^{\pm 1}
. By assumption,
\omega_{n}(1,0,0)=(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})/5^{n}
, where
a_{n},b_{n},c_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b_{n}
not divisible by 5.

Write
\omega_{k}=\phi\omega_{k-1}
for any reduced word
\omega_{k}
, where
\mid\omega_{k}\mid=k
and
\phi\in\{\sigma,\sigma^{-1},\tau,\tau^{-1}\}
.There are four cases:

Case 1:

\omega_{k}=\sigma\omega_{k-1}

\displaystyle\omega_{k}(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\sigma\omega_{k-1}(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\sigma(a_{k-1},b_{k-1},c_{k-1})/5^{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5^{k}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{3a_{k-1}-4b_{k-1}}\\
{4a_{k-1}+3b_{k-1}}\\
{5c_{k-1}}\end{array}\right)

We can see that
a_{k}=3a_{k-1}-4b_{k-1}
,
b_{k}=4a_{k-1}+3b_{k-1}
,
c_{k}=5c_{k-1}
are all integers. We must check now to see if

b_{k}=4a_{k-1}+3b_{k-1}

(3)

is divisible by 5. To do this, we need to look now at 3 more cases;
\omega_{k}=\sigma\tau^{\pm 1}\omega_{k-2}
,
\omega_{k}=\sigma\sigma\omega_{k-2}
, where
\omega_{k-2}
is possibly the empty word.

For the first two cases, we already know that
b_{k-1}
is not divisible by 5, so what we really want to check is if
a_{k-1}
is divisible by 5, for then the addition of the two terms will yield a number not divisible by 5. We have

\displaystyle\omega_{k}(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\sigma\tau^{\pm 1}\omega_{k-2}(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\sigma\frac{1}{5^{k-2}}\times\frac{1}{5}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}5&0&0\\
0&3&\mp 4\\
0&\pm 4&3\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{a_{k-2}}\\
{b_{k-2}}\\
{c_{k-2}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\frac{1}{5^{k-1}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{5a_{k-2}}\\
{3b_{k-2}\mp 4c_{k-2}}\\
{4b_{k-2}\pm 3c_{k-2}}\end{array}\right)

Since
a_{k-1}=5a_{k-2}
,
a_{k-1}
is divisible by 5 when
\omega_{k}=\sigma\tau^{\pm 1}\omega_{k-1}
.

For the second case, we check to see if
b_{k}
is divisible by 5 based on the assumption that both
b_{k-1}
and
b_{k-2}
are not divisible by 5. We have

\displaystyle\omega_{k}(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\sigma\sigma\omega_{k-2}(1,0,0)

\displaystyle=\sigma\frac{1}{5^{k-2}}\times\frac{1}{5}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}3&-4&0\\
4&3&0\\
0&0&5\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{a_{k-2}}\\
{b_{k-2}}\\
{c_{k-2}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\frac{1}{5^{k-1}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{3a_{k-2}-4b_{k-2}}\\
{4a_{k-2}+3b_{k-2}}\\
{5c_{k-2}}\end{array}\right)

Consequences of the Banach-Tarski Paradox

The corresponding paradox for free groups, while somewhat surprising, is certainly not something that anyone would argue with. When this paradox is applied to 3- dimensional space it does go against our intuition, but very often our intuition is flawed.

By allowing the use of the Axiom of Choice, we can obtain sets which are so complicated that they cannot be assigned a measure consistent with the desired properties. This is precisely what happens in the Banach-Tarski paradox; most of the sets we have constructed are
nonmeasurable
; not Lebesgue measurable. So the Banach-Tarski Paradox doesn’t double the volume of the ball because the pieces in the decomposition cannot be assigned a volume - such sets would be far more intricate than the atomic structure of matter, and as such, could never be realised in real life. Such beauty exists only in the realm of mathematics.
