Non-density of the exceptional components of the Noether-Lefschetz locus
Gregorio Baldi, Bruno Klingler, and Emmanuel Ullmo

Abstract
. We study when the Picard group of smooth surfaces of degree d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 in ℙ 3 \mathbb{P}^{3} acquires extra classes. In particular we show that the so called exceptional components of the Noether-Lefschetz locus are not Zariski dense. This answers a 1991 question of C. Voisin. We also obtain similar results for the Noether-Lefschetz locus for suitable ( Y , L ) (Y,L) , where Y Y is a smooth projective threefold and L L a very ample line bundle. Both results are applications of the Zilber-Pink viewpoint recently developed by the authors for arbitrary (polarized, integral) variations of Hodge structures.

The locus

Each irreducible component of

Remark 4
.

If one takes into account the natural more complicated schematic
structure on each component of

2.
Some cases of Zilber-Pink

This section is a specialization of the results and vocabulary
introduced in
[
2
]
in a much greater
generality. To understand the (polarized) variation of Hodge structures (VHS)
\mathbb{V}\to S=U_{d}
interpolating the
primitive cohomology groups
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\textnormal{prim}}
, we look at the
associated holomorphic period map

(2.1)

\Phi:S^{\textnormal{an}}\to\Gamma\backslash D,

completely describing
\mathbb{V}
.
Here
S^{\textnormal{an}}
denotes the complex manifold analytification of
S
,
({\mathbf{G}},D)
denotes the generic Hodge datum of
\mathbb{V}

and

\Gamma\backslash D
is the associated Hodge variety. As famously
proved by Lefschetz
[
26
]
(see also
[
4
, Section
1.2]
, and the discussion in

[
2
, Rmk. 9.1]
), we have that

{\mathbf{G}}^{\operatorname{ad}}
is
\operatorname{SO}(2h^{2,0},h^{1,1}-1)
, where
h^{2,0}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{2,0}(X)
and
h^{1,1}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{1,1}(X)
, for some

[X]\in U_{d}
(from now on we write
h^{1,1}_{\textnormal{prim}}:=h^{1,1}-1
)
2
2

2

Implicitly we use the André-Deligne monodromy theorem, which rests upon the fixed-part and the semisimplicity
theorems, to observe that the monodromy is a normal subgroup of
the derived subgroup of the generic Mumford-Tate group.
. The
Noether-Lefschetz locus lies in the more general tensorial Hodge locus
of
\mathbb{V}_{s}
, namely the set of closed points
s\in S
where the
Mumford-Tate group of the Hodge structure
\mathbb{V}_{s}
drops:

\textnormal{HL}(S,\mathbb{V}^{\otimes}):=\{s\in S(\mathbb{C}):\mathbf{MT}(\mathbb{V}_{s})\subsetneq\mathbf{MT}(\mathbb{V})\}.

In particular:

(2.2)

\NL_{d}\subset\textnormal{HL}(S,\mathbb{V}^{\otimes})_{\textnormal{pos}}.

The fact that we can consider just the Hodge locus
\textnormal{HL}(S,\mathbb{V}^{\otimes})_{\textnormal{pos}}
of
positive
period dimension
comes from the bound recalled in
2

and the local/infinitesimal Torelli theorem of Griffiths
[
23
, Theorem
9.8,
Part I]
which asserts that the differential of the period
map
\Phi
is injective at any point
s\in S
(modulo the action of
the projective group
\mathbf{PGL}(3,\mathbb{C})
on
U_{d}
), as long as
d\geq 4
.

Following
[
2
]
, an irreducible
subvariety
Z
of
S
is called
special

(resp.
weakly special
) if any irreducible subvariety
W\subset S
strictly
containing
Z
has generic Mumford-Tate (resp. algebraic monodromy)
strictly bigger than the one of
Z
.

The next definition of
[
2
]
distinguishes special subvarieties between the ones having
the “expected” codimension and the ones that
don’t:

Definition 11
.

Let
Z\subset S
be a special
subvariety for
\mathbb{V}
, with generic Hodge datum
({\mathbf{G}}^{\prime},D^{\prime})
. It is
said to be
atypical
if
\Phi(S^{\textnormal{an}})
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash D^{\prime}
do not intersect generically along
\Phi(Z^{\textnormal{an}})
:

(2.3)

\codim_{\Gamma\backslash D}\Phi(Z^{\textnormal{an}})<\codim_{\Gamma\backslash D}\Phi(S^{\textnormal{an}})+\codim_{\Gamma\backslash D}\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash D^{\prime}\;\;,

Otherwise it is said to be
typical
. The
atypical Hodge locus

\textnormal{HL}(S,\mathbb{V}^{\otimes})_{\textnormal{atyp}}\subset\textnormal{HL}(S,\mathbb{V}^{\otimes})

(resp. the
typical Hodge locus

\textnormal{HL}(S,\mathbb{V}^{\otimes})_{\textnormal{typ}}\subset\textnormal{HL}(S,\mathbb{V}^{\otimes})
) is
the union of the atypical (resp. strict typical) special
subvarieties of
S
for
\mathbb{V}
.

Remark 12
.

The above definition makes implicitly use of the
algebraicity theorem of the Hodge locus for arbitrary Hodge classes

[
9
]
: Lefschetz (1,1) theorem will not suffices for our
purposes.

2.1.
Noether-Lefschetz components as special varieties

The upper bound
h^{2,0}
from
2
can also be seen as
follows (see for example
[
7
, (page 46)]
, for more details). Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Hodge-Lie algebra associated to

(\mathbf{G},D_{G})
. We have

2\dim\mathfrak{g}^{-2,2}=(h^{2,0}-1)h^{2,0}\;,

\dim\mathfrak{g}^{-1,1}=h^{2,0}\,h^{1,1}_{\textnormal{prim}}.

The condition defining
\NL_{d}
is the one of having an extra Hodge
vector in
\mathbb{V}
. In particular an irreducible component of
\NL_{d}
is
a special subvariety of
U_{d}
for
\mathbb{V}
of the form
\Phi^{-1}(\Gamma_{H}\backslash D_{H})^{0}
, where

\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{G}
is isomorphic, over the real numbers, to the group

\operatorname{SO}(2h^{2,0},h^{1,1}_{\textnormal{prim}}-1)
; and
(\cdot)^{0}
denotes an
irreducible component.

At the level of the Hodge-Lie algebra, we observe that

\mathfrak{h}^{-2,2}=\mathfrak{g}^{-2,2}
and that
\dim\mathfrak{h}^{-1,1}=h^{2,0}(h^{1,1}_{\textnormal{prim}}-1)
, as we are
parametrising the Hodge tuple

(h^{2,0},(h^{1,1}_{\textnormal{prim}}-1),h^{0,2})
.
The components of
\NL_{d}
thus have codimension in
U_{d}
at most equal
to

\codim_{\Gamma\backslash D}(\Gamma_{H}\backslash D_{H})=\codim_{D}D_{H}=\dim\mathfrak{g}^{-1,1}-\mathfrak{h}^{-1,1}=h^{2,0}\;.

The proof of

and the previous arguments show that the
above is a special case of
1
. In fact, this special
case of the Zilber-Pink conjecture was noticed a long time ago by de
Jong
[
14
, Sec. 3.2.2.]
and should be attributed to him (see also

[
2
, Sec. 3.6.]
for a history of the Zilber-Pink
conjecture, and a comparison with de Jong’s unpublished viewpoint).

4.
Zilber-Pink and the Noether-Lefschetz locus for arbitrary threefolds

To elucidate the role of the Zilber-Pink viewpoint, we conclude with a
general result on the distribution of the Noether-Lefschetz locus for
arbitrary
(Y,L)
where
Y
is a smooth projective threefold and
L
a
very ample line bundle on
Y
. Similar considerations apply also to
the case of quasi-smooth surfaces in a weighted projective space

\mathbb{P}(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})
(see
[
32
,
12
,
15
]
) as well as surfaces that arise as
complete intersections (see for example
[
24
]
). We
consider
U_{Y,L}\subset\mathbb{P}H^{0}(L)
, the parameter space of
smooth surfaces
X\subset Y
such that
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(X)=L

(i.e. surfaces in the same equivalence class as
L
), and define

\NL_{L}:=\{[X]\in U_{Y,L}:\operatorname{Pic}(Y)\to\operatorname{Pic}(X)\text{ is not a surjection}\}.

(See
[
29
,
4
]
for details about why the connected components of the Picard groups can be ignored).

Moishezon
[
30
]
found the exact conditions for a

Noether-Lefschetz theorem
to hold for
(Y,L)
, namely the
generic member of
U_{Y,L}
must have a non-zero evanescent

(2,0)
-cohomology class. Our exposition is inspired by the setting
considered by Lopez and Maclean
[
29
]
and their
open-ended question (see the last paragraph of page 2 in
op. cit.
):

Question 19

(Lopez-Maclean)
.

How large can the components of
\NL_{L}
be in comparison with
U_{Y,L}
?

We see
\NL_{L}
, in a very Hodge theoretic fashion, as the zero locus
of a vector bundle section. Let
\mathbb{V}\to U_{Y,L}
be the polarized

\mathbb{Z}
-VHS interpolating the
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})_{\operatorname{ev}}
, and assume from on that

h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}\neq 0
. See
[
29
, Sec. 2]
for a brief
discussion of vanishing cohomology (we very much follow their
notation and viewpoint, but we changed the terminology from
evanescent
cohomology to vanishing),
[
39
]
for a discussion about
primitive and vanishing cohomologies.

We simply present the general axiomatic statement, but several
concrete corollaries can be obtained. As the reader might notice, the
body of literature on these questions is quite large. We don’t attempt to survey the most general statements,
but limit ourselves to concrete applications of the Zilber-Pink
paradigm, that we hope will complement several nice ideas and
viewpoints that emerged from the study of the Noether-Lefschetz locus
of smooth surfaces. In the hope that the result below will have
further algebro-geometric applications, we refer also to

[
38
,
40
]
where various cycle theoretic
applications related to the knowledge of
\NL_{L}
are discussed (among
which one also finds Kollár’s counterexamples to the integral
Hodge conjecture).

Theorem 20
.

If the following are satisfied:

A1.

The algebraic monodromy group of
\mathbb{V}\to U_{Y,L}
contains the full special orthogonal
stabilizing the intersection form;

A2.

The infinitesimal Torelli theorem for
(U_{Y,L},\mathbb{V})
holds true;

A3.

\dim U_{Y,L}>h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}
(
>0
, as
assumed from the beginning);

then the locus
\NL_{L}
consists of countably many irreducible (strict)
algebraic subsets of
U_{Y,L}
, behaving as follows:

(1)

The union of the atypical components of
\NL_{L}
(in
particular the ones of codimension
<h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}
) is not Zariski
dense: its Zariski closure is contained in a finite union of strict
special subvarieties;

(2)

The union of the typical components is dense in
U_{Y,L}(\mathbb{C})

(in particular it is non-empty and they have codimension

h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}
).

(If one doesn’t wish to assume A2. it is enough to replace
“dimension” by “period dimension” and the result is still true.)
Regarding special cases the existence of components of
\NL_{Y}
of
maximal codimension, we refer also to
[
5
]
and

[
3
]
.

Remark 21
.

Several concrete cases can be obtained by referring to the opportune
results from the literature. For example, regarding A1. see (the
proof of)
[
8
, Thm. 9.1]
, for
A2.
[
19
]
(as well as
[
33
]
, for the
weighted projective case). Such results often require to replace
L

by
L^{d}
, for
d
big enough. We didn’t attempt to give the optimal
results.

Proof of
20
.

We study
(\mathbb{V},U_{Y,L})
via the period map
\Psi:U_{Y,L}\to\Gamma\backslash D
(which we know to be non-trivial, thanks to A1
and A2). Condition A3. asserts that we would expect (non-empty)
components of
\NL_{L}
of codimension
h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}
. This is exactly the
“admissible” condition needed to apply

[
25
, Thm. 1.6]
(see also the main result of
[
17
]
), which therefore
establishes the existence of components of the desired
codimension. In
op. cit.
, the authors actually prove that
A3. is equivalent to (2).

After this, the (analytic) density of the typical
components and the non-density of atypical ones follow from the
results of
Section

2
(that hold true for arbitrary bases
S
,
and VHS
\mathbb{V}
). Condition A3. is used in (1), only to ensure that
the atypical components of
\NL_{L}
are necessarly of positive dimension.
∎

Remark 22
.

The same proof also shows that, under the weaker condition
\dim U_{Y,L}\geq h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}
, one still obtains that
the components of
\NL_{L}
of codimension
<h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}
are not Zariski dense in
U_{Y,L}(\mathbb{C})
, and the ones of codimension

h^{2,0}_{\operatorname{ev}}
are analytically dense.
