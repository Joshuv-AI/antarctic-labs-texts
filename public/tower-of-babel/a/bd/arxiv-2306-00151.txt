Impact of Chiral-Transitions in Quantum Friction
Muzzamal I. Shaukat, Mário G. Silveirinha

Abstract
We theoretically investigate the role of chiral-transitions in the quantum friction force that acts on a two-level atom that moves with relative velocity v \displaystyle v parallel to a planar metallic surface. We find that the friction force has a component that is sensitive to the handedness of the atomic transition dipole moment. In the particular, we show that the friction force can be enhanced by an atomic transition with a dipole moment with a certain handedness, and almost suppressed by the dipole moment with the opposite handedness. Curiously, the handedness of the transition dipole moment that boosts the ground-state friction force is the opposite of what is classically expected from the spin-momentum locking. We explain this discrepancy in terms of the interaction between positive and negative frequency oscillators.

A physical picture of quantum friction is that when two bodies are set in a relative motion, the “image” dipoles lag behind the original fluctuations, resulting in energy loss. For instance, for an atom moving parallel to a metal surface, the energy transfer can be visualized as the atom inducing dipoles in the metal. These induced dipoles, due to the lack of motion and the dispersive properties of the material, lag behind the atom’s fluctuations. This lag results in a continuous transfer of energy from the atom to the metal slab, which is perceived as energy loss from the atom’s perspective. The friction force is mostly controlled by the material dispersion and dissipation.

The purpose of this work
is to elucidate the role of the atomic polarization, and in particular the role of chiral-transitions, in the quantum friction force. To this end, we consider a two-level system (qubit) moving with velocity
\displaystyle v
parallel to a metallic surface. Using a quasi-static approximation, we present a detailed study of the impact of the atomic transition dipole-moment on the friction force, discuss the role of surface plasmons and analyze the effect of material dissipation. Interestingly, we find that chiral-type atomic transitions can be the dominant friction mechanism. As intuitively expected, the handedness of a chiral-type atomic transition can greatly influence its contribution to the friction force due to the strong sensitivity of the coupling with surface plasmons. Intriguingly, we find that the atomic transitions that give the dominant contribution to the friction force have an handeness that is opposite to that expected from classical considerations. We offer an explanation for this property by taking into account that the interactions that lead to friction are associated with non-conserving energy terms.

The article is organized as follows. In Sect. II, we describe the theoretical model of the two-level system that interacts with a metallic surface. In Sect. III, we describe the theoretical formalism and provide general formulas for the friction force in terms of the system Green’s function. Then, in Sect. In addition, we present a detailed numerical study that highlights the impact of the atomic polarization and of the substrate loss. A summary of the main results is provided in Sect. V.

IV
Quasi-static approximation

IV.1
General formalism

In the following, it is assumed that the atom interacts with a metal surface.
In the non-relativistic limit, the friction force on the moving two-level atom is mainly determined by short-wavelength interactions with
\displaystyle k_{x}\sim\pm\omega_{0}/v
, i.e., it is ruled by the short-wavelength surface plasmons. Due to this reason, it is a good approximation to model the interactions of the atom with a metal substrate using a quasi-static formalism.

In a quasi-static description, one can assume that
\displaystyle k_{||}\gg\omega/c
and thereby
\displaystyle\omega^{2}/c^{2}\rightarrow 0,
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}\rightarrow k_{||}

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\rightarrow R\mathbb{k}_{||}\otimes\mathbb{k}_{||}/k^{2}_{||}

\displaystyle R
is the reflection coefficient for TM polarization, which for short-wavelengths can be approximated by:

R\approx-\frac{\varepsilon(\omega)-1}{\varepsilon(\omega)+1},

(21)

with
\displaystyle\varepsilon(\omega)
the permittivity of the metal.

In these conditions, the tensor
\displaystyle\bf{C}
[Eq. (
14

{\bf{C}}\approx-R\left(\omega\right)\left({{{\bf{k}}_{||}}+i{k_{||}}{\bf{\hat{z}}}}\right)\otimes\left({{{\bf{k}}_{||}}-i{k_{||}}{\bf{\hat{z}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}(\mathbb{r}_{0},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{dk_{y}}R\left(\omega\right)\frac{e^{-2k_{||}d}}{2k_{||}}

It is important to note that the Born-Markov approximation, used in the derivation of Eq. (
24

;
Jentschura2015
. Specifically, the Born-Markov result is only a valid approximation for the friction force when the kinetic energy is comparable to (or greater than) the atomic and material transition energies. This condition establishes a lower velocity bound below which the theory becomes invalid. This issue will be discussed further in Sect.
IV.3
.

We shall suppose that the metal permittivity is of the form
\displaystyle\varepsilon(\omega)=1-2\omega^{2}_{\rm sp}/\omega(\omega+i\Gamma_{c})
, where
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm sp}
denotes the surface plasmon resonance and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}
represents the damping (collision rate). In this case, it is possible to write the reflection coefficient
\displaystyle R
[Eq. (

\displaystyle\displaystyle R\left(\omega\right)=-\frac{{\omega_{{\rm{sp}}}^{2}}}{{2{{\omega}_{{\rm{sp}}}^{\prime}}}}\underbrace{\left[{\frac{1}{{{{\omega}_{{\rm{sp}}}^{\prime}}-i\Gamma_{c}/2-\omega}}+\frac{1}{{{{\omega}_{{\rm{sp}}}^{\prime}}+i\Gamma_{c}/2+\omega}}}\right]}_{g\left(\omega\right)}

(26)

where
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm sp}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\omega_{\rm sp}^{2}-(\Gamma_{c}/2)^{2}}

IV.2
Weak dissipation limit

Next, we consider the weak dissipation limit such that
\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Note that the friction force does not need to vanish in this limit, as there are decay channels due to the emission of plasmons, i.e., due to radiation emission.

In the
\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}\rightarrow 0^{+}

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits}\left\{{g\left(\omega\right)}\right\}=\pi\delta\left({\omega_{{\rm{sp}}}-\omega}\right)-\pi\delta\left({\omega_{{\rm{sp}}}+\omega}\right).

IV.4
Chiral-type transitions

Next, we investigate how chiral-type polarized states can affect the friction force (Fig.
4
). To this end, we consider a circularly polarized atom with a transition dipole moment
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}\sim\frac{1}{{\sqrt{2}}}\left({{\bf{\hat{x}}}\pm i{\bf{\hat{z}}}}\right)
. Note that the polarization curve is contained in the plane of motion (
\displaystyle xoz
plane), as that is configuration that facilitates the interaction with plasmons. For comparison, the figure also depicts the force associated with vertical polarization. As seen, the chiral-polarized states have a dramatic effect on the strength of the friction force. In particular, the force is enhanced for a transition dipole moment of the type
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}\sim\frac{1}{{\sqrt{2}}}\left({{\bf{\hat{x}}}-i{\bf{\hat{z}}}}\right)
, whereas it is nearly suppressed for a dipole moment with the opposite handedness
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}\sim\frac{1}{{\sqrt{2}}}\left({{\bf{\hat{x}}}+i{\bf{\hat{z}}}}\right)
. The different behavior is due to the spin-dependent term (proportional to
\displaystyle{\left({{\bm{\gamma}}\times{{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}}}\right)\cdot{\bf{\hat{y}}}}
) in Eq. (
33
). It can be shown that the handedness of the plasmons that co-propagate with the two-level atom (
\displaystyle+x
-direction) in the air region is such that the corresponding electric field satisfies
\displaystyle{\bf{E}}\sim{\bf{\hat{x}}}+i{\bf{\hat{z}}}
. Heuristically, from the spin-momentum locking
Bliokh
;
Jacob
, one might expect that the friction force should be boosted when
\displaystyle{\bf{E}}
matches
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}
, in contradiction with the results of Fig.
4
.

The different behavior can be justified as follows. When the atom velocity vanishes, the light-matter interactions are mainly determined by energy-conserving terms of the type
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n{\bf{k}}}{\hat{\sigma}_{+}}
. Such terms can lead to a resonant coupling because the field and atomic operators have frequencies with opposite signs (counter-rotating terms). Thus, in the absence of motion the strength of the resonant interactions are controlled by the overlap term
\displaystyle{{\bf{E}}_{n{\bf{k}}}}\cdot{{\bf{\gamma}}^{*}}
. In contrast, when the relative velocity is nontrivial, there are energy non-conserving terms of the type
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n{\bf{k}}}^{\dagger}{\hat{\sigma}_{+}}
, which can be resonant due to the Doppler shift suffered by the atomic frequency. Specifically, this can happen when the Doppler-shifted frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{0}+k_{x}v
becomes
\displaystyle negative
and matches the plasmon resonance
\displaystyle-\omega_{\rm{sp}}
. The energy non-conserving terms are the ones that activate the friction force when the system is initially prepared in the ground state. The strength of the corresponding interactions is determined by the overlap term
\displaystyle{\bf{E}}_{n{\bf{k}}}^{*}\cdot{{\bf{\gamma}}^{*}}
. The two overlap terms discussed previously (
\displaystyle{\bf{E}}_{n{\bf{k}}}\cdot{{\bf{\gamma}}^{*}}
and
\displaystyle{\bf{E}}_{n{\bf{k}}}^{*}\cdot{{\bf{\gamma}}^{*}}
) are evidently maximized for an opposite handedness of the transition dipole moment. This property justifies why the dipole handedness that maximizes the ground state friction force is opposite to the handedness that maximizes the classical light matter interactions in the absence of relative motion.

From a different perspective, one can observe that in the friction problem the interaction between the atom and the substrate leads to the emission of a plasmon with wave number
\displaystyle k_{x}=k_{\rm{P},-}<0
, oscillation frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{0}+k_{x}v=-\omega_{\rm{sp}}
and polarization
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}
. Due to the reality of the electromagnetic field, the plasmon with the negative oscillation frequency is equivalent to a plasmon with positive oscillation frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm{sp}}
but with polarization
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}^{*}
. Thus, when the interaction is mediated by
negative
frequencies, the resonant interaction between the plasmons and the atom corresponds to the matching of
\displaystyle{\bf{E}}\sim{\bf{\hat{x}}}+i{\bf{\hat{z}}}
with
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}^{*}
. This property justifies why the polarization that boosts the ground-state friction force satisfies
\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}\sim\frac{1}{{\sqrt{2}}}\left({{\bf{\hat{x}}}-i{\bf{\hat{z}}}}\right)
.

It is relevant to note that the impact of spin-momentum locking on the frictional force has been previously discussed in the context of quantum interactions between a Lorentz quantum harmonic oscillator and a metal surface

For time-reversal-invariant qubit systems, the presence of a chiral transition with a specific handedness necessarily implies the existence of a corresponding transition with the opposite handedness
Mario2019
. In such scenarios, a more accurate representation of a spin-integer qubit is a V-type qubit with two degenerate excited states connected by time-reversal symmetry. The electric dipole moments for transitions between the ground and excited states are related by complex conjugation. As a result, a time-reversal-invariant qubit typically exhibits effects associated with both chiralities. In qubits with a V-type structure, the most significant chiral transition is the one associated with the largest frictional force. As previously noted, this transition corresponds to the handedness opposite to what might be intuitively expected from spin-momentum locking.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that a chiral qubit with a V-type energy structure does not need to exhibit electromagnetic chirality in the conventional sense. This is because it lacks a magneto-electric coupling or a net gyrotropic response due to the coexistence of both chiralities.

V
Conclusion

In summary, using a non-relativistic formalism based on a modal expansion and the Born-Markov approximation, we derived explicit formulas for the friction force acting on a two-level system that moves with some velocity
\displaystyle v
with respect to a generic material substrate. The friction force is expressed in terms of the system’s Green’s function and is consistent with previously reported theories based on the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Furthermore, for the particular case of a metal described by a Drude dispersion model we employed a quasi-static approximation and derived analytical formulas for the quantum friction force in the weak dissipation limit. In particular, we have shown that the ground-state friction force is maximized for a velocity on the order of
\displaystyle v_{\rm{opt}}=\frac{4}{7}\left({{\omega_{0}}+{\omega_{{\rm{sp}}}}}\right)d
(in agreement with Ref.
Intravaia2016b
) and for an atomic transition frequency such that
\displaystyle{\omega_{0,{\rm{opt}}}}\approx\max\left\{{0,\frac{5}{4}\frac{v}{d}-{\omega_{{\rm{sp}}}}}\right\}
.

Our theory highlights the role of the transition dipole moment polarization in the frictional force, showing that atomic transitions associated with chiral-type polarizations may give the dominant contribution to the friction force in a realistic (multi-level) atom.
Furthermore, we have shown that counterintuitively the atomic transitions that boost the friction force are associated with dipoles with an handedness which is opposite to that expected from naive classical considerations based on the spin-momentum locking. We have demonstrated that this property is a consequence of the fact that the friction force arises from interactions between positive and negative frequency oscillators due to the Doppler effect.
In typical atomic systems constrained by time-reversal symmetry, chiral transitions occur in pairs with opposite handedness. In such cases, the energy structure of the atomic system resembles a V shape, and the chiral transition that maximizes the frictional force becomes the relevant contribution. To conclude, we point out that our approach can be easily generalized to different plasmonic platforms, e.g., Weyl semimetals
Weyl1
;
Weyl2
or materials with drift currents
Shapiro
;
VolokotinPRL
, even when the nonlocal effects are considered.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\omega_{n\mathbb{k}}>0}\frac{\omega_{n\mathbb{k}}}{2}\frac{1}{\omega_{n\mathbb{k}}+\omega}\mathbb{F}^{*}_{n\mathbb{k}}(\mathbb{r})\otimes\mathbb{F}_{n\mathbb{k}}(\mathbb{r}_{0}),

(34)

with poles in the
\displaystyle{\rm Re}\{\omega\}>0
and
\displaystyle{\rm Re}\{\omega\}<0
semi-spaces, respectively.

It is useful to write
\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}^{-}
in terms of the full Green’s function
\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}^{+}+{\mathcal{G}}^{-}
. To this end, we use the fact that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}
is analytic for
\displaystyle{\rm Re}\{\omega\}>0
. Integrating
\displaystyle\frac{1}{{{\omega_{0}}-\omega}}\mathcal{G^{-}}\left(\omega\right)
over the complex imaginary axis and using Cauchy’s (residue) theorem it follows that:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0})|_{{\rm Re}\{\omega_{0}\}>0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)}{\omega_{0}-i\xi}.

(35)

On the other hand, as the function
\displaystyle\frac{1}{{{\omega^{*}_{0}}+\omega}}\mathcal{G^{-}}
is analytic for
\displaystyle{\rm Re}\{\omega\}>0
, the Cauchy’s theorem implies that
\displaystyle 0=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{d\xi\frac{1}{{\omega_{0}^{*}+i\xi}}}{{\cal G}^{-}}\left({i\xi}\right)
.
It is evident from Eq. (
34
) that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)=[\mathcal{G^{+}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},-\omega^{*})]^{*}
, so that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)=[\mathcal{G^{+}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)]^{*}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{*}+i\xi}[\mathcal{G^{+}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)]^{*}.

(36)

Taking the complex conjugate of Eq. (
36
) and combining with Eq. (
35
), we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0})|_{{\rm Re}\{\omega_{0}\}>0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)}{\omega_{0}-i\xi},

(37)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G^{-}}+\mathcal{G^{+}}
.

Using again
\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{+}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)=[\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},-\omega^{*})]^{*}
and noting that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)
is real-valued, one sees that Eq. (
37
) implies that:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{+}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0})|_{{\rm Re}\{\omega_{0}\}<0}=\frac{-1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)}{\omega_{0}-i\xi}.

(38)

Hence, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0})|_{{\rm Re}\{\omega_{0}\}<0}=\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0})-\mathcal{G^{+}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0})|_{{\rm Re}\{\omega_{0}\}<0}
, it follows that for an arbitrary
\displaystyle\omega
(not lying on the imaginary axis)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{-}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(-\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)

(39)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{1}{\omega-i\xi}\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi),

where
\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}={\rm Re}\{\omega\}
and
\displaystyle u
is the unit step function. The subscript “0” of
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
was dropped to simplify the notations.
Similarly, it is possible to write

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{+}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)

(40)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\frac{1}{\omega-i\xi}\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi).

Equations (
39
)-(
40
) give the positive and negative frequency components of the Green’s function in terms of the full Green’s function.

In order to extend the formulas to the case of dissipative systems, we need to ensure that the integration path is fully contained in the upper-half frequency plane (UHP). This can be easily done noting that since
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{T}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)=\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r}_{0},\mathbb{r},-\omega)
, one can express
\displaystyle\mathcal{G^{\pm}}
as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(\pm\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)\mp\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}d\xi

(41)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)}{\omega-i\xi}+\frac{\mathcal{G}^{T}(\mathbb{r}_{0},\mathbb{r},i\xi)}{\omega+i\xi}\right].

Appendix B
Friction force as a function of the system Green’s function

In this Appendix, we write the friction force as a function of the system Green’s function.

To begin with, we consider a Fourier decomposition of the functions
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\pm}
introduced in Appendix
A
, such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle{{\cal G}^{\pm}}=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int{{\cal G}_{{k_{x}}}^{\pm}}d{k_{x}}.

(42)

Here, it is implicit that the system is invariant to translations in the
\displaystyle xoy
plane, so that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{k_{x}}
depends on
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle x_{0}
as
\displaystyle e^{ik_{x}(x-x_{0})}
.
Thus,
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{k_{x}}e^{-ik_{x}(x-x_{0})}
is the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\pm}
in the
\displaystyle x-x_{0}
variable.
Evidently, Eq. (
41
) implies that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{k_{x}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(\pm\omega^{\prime})\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega)\mp\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}d\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}(\mathbb{r},\mathbb{r}_{0},i\xi)}{\omega-i\xi}+\frac{\mathcal{G}_{-k_{x}}^{T}(\mathbb{r}_{0},\mathbb{r},i\xi)}{\omega+i\xi}\right],

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}=\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}^{+}+\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}^{-}
. We took into account that the Green’s function only depends on the relative difference (
\displaystyle x-x_{0}
) between
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle x_{0}
.

The friction force is written in terms of the functions
\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{v}^{\pm}
defined by Eq. (

).
By comparing Eq. (
9
) with Eq. (

) one sees that:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{v}^{\pm}\left({{\bf{r}},{{\bf{r}}_{0}},\omega}\right)=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int{{\mathcal{G}}_{{k_{x}}}^{\pm}\left({{\bf{r}},{{\bf{r}}_{0}},\omega+{k_{x}}v}\right)}d{k_{x}}.

(44)

Then, with the help of Eq. (
) one finds that for positive
\displaystyle v
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal G}_{v}^{+}\left({\omega_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-{I_{{\rm{nr}}}}+\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int\limits_{-{{\omega}_{0}}/v}^{+\infty}{d{k_{x}}}{\cal G}_{{k_{x}}}\left({{\bf{r}},{{\bf{r}}_{0}},{\omega_{0}}+{k_{x}}v}\right),

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal G}_{v}^{-}\left({\omega_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle{I_{{\rm{nr}}}}+\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{-{{\omega}_{0}}/v}{d{k_{x}}}{\cal G}_{{k_{x}}}\left({{\bf{r}},{{\bf{r}}_{0}},{\omega_{0}}+{k_{x}}v}\right),

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle{I_{{\rm{nr}}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{{{{\left({2\pi}\right)}^{2}}}}\int{d{k_{x}}}\int\limits_{{0^{+}}}^{\infty}d\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\left({\frac{{\cal G}_{{k_{x}}}\left({{\bf{r}},{{\bf{r}}_{0}},i\xi}\right)}{{{\omega_{0}}+{k_{x}}v-i\xi}}+\frac{{\cal G}_{{-k_{x}}}^{T}\left({{\bf{r}}_{0},{{\bf{r}}},i\xi}\right)}{{{\omega_{0}}+{k_{x}}v+i\xi}}}\right)}.

Substituting the above formulas into Eq. (
8
), one finds that the optical force satisfies (
\displaystyle m=x,y,z
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{e}(t)\mathcal{F}_{e}+\left(1-\mathcal{P}_{e}(t)\right)\mathcal{F}_{g}

(47)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{i}=2\left\{\mathcal{J}^{r}_{i}\mp\mathcal{J}^{nr}\right\}
(
\displaystyle i=e,g
), is written in terms of resonant (
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{r}
) and non-resonant (
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{nr}
) terms defined by:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{e}^{r}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\rm Re}{2\pi}\int_{-\omega_{0}/v}^{+\infty}dk_{x}\left\{\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}^{*}\cdot\partial_{m}\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}(\mathbb{r}_{0},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0}+k_{x}v)\cdot\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}\right\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{g}^{r}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\rm Re}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{-\omega_{0}/v}dk_{x}\left\{\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}^{*}\cdot\partial_{m}\mathcal{G}_{k_{x}}(\mathbb{r}_{0},\mathbb{r}_{0},\omega_{0}+k_{x}v)\cdot\bm{\tilde{\gamma}}\right\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\cal J}^{nr}=\frac{{{\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits}}}{{{{\left({2\pi}\right)}^{2}}}}\int{d{k_{x}}}\int\limits_{{0^{+}}}^{\infty}{d\xi\left[{\frac{{{{{\bf{\tilde{\gamma}}}}^{*}}\cdot{\partial_{i}}{\cal G}_{{k_{x}}}\left({{\bf{r}},{{\bf{r}}_{0}},i\xi}\right)\cdot{\bf{\tilde{\gamma}}}}}{{{\omega_{0}}+{k_{x}}v-i\xi}}}\right.}

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm{}}+{\left.{\frac{{{{{\bf{\tilde{\gamma}}}}^{*}}\cdot{\partial_{i}}{\cal G}_{-{k_{x}}}^{T}\left({{{\bf{r}}_{0}},{\bf{r}},i\xi}\right)\cdot{\bf{\tilde{\gamma}}}}}{{{\omega_{0}}+{k_{x}}v+i\xi}}}\right]_{{\bf{r}}={{\bf{r}}_{0}}}}

(49)

\displaystyle x
-direction). For
\displaystyle m=x

\displaystyle{\partial_{m}}=ik_{x}
in the previous formulas. Furthermore, as
\displaystyle{\cal G}\left({{\bf{r}},{{\bf{r}}_{0}},i\xi}\right)

\displaystyle{\cal J}^{nr}

11

\displaystyle{\cal J}^{nr}
typically contributes to the vertical (Casimir-type) force.
