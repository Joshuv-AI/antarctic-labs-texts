Thermodynamics of hybrid quantum rotor devices
Heather Leitch, Kenza Hammam, Gabriele De Chiara

Abstract
We investigate the thermodynamics of a hybrid quantum device consisting of two qubits collectively interacting with a quantum rotor and coupled dissipatively to two equilibrium reservoirs at different temperatures. By modelling the dynamics and the resulting steady state of the system using a collision model, we identify the functioning of the device as a thermal engine, a refrigerator or an accelerator. In addition, we also look into the device’s capacity to operate as a heat rectifier, and optimise both the rectification coefficient and the heat flow simultaneously. Drawing an analogy to heat rectification and since we are interested in the conversion of energy into the rotor’s kinetic energy, we introduce the concept of angular momentum rectification which may be employed to control work extraction through an external load.

Introduction

The exploration of quantum thermal machines is crucial in comprehending the intricacies of energy exchange on a quantum level, as evidenced by extensive research
Kosloff and Levy 2014
;
Binder et al. 2018
;
Mitchison 2019
;
Myers et al. 2022

;
Binder et al. 2018

;
Hammam et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2019
, which can lead to a new generation of highly performing and energy-efficient thermal devices for novel quantum technologies applications.

Autonomous thermal machines

;
Linden et al. 2010
;
Silva et al. 2016
;
Mitchison et al. 2016
;
Hammam et al. 2021
offer a remarkable opportunity to study these quantum effects since they are known for featuring the lowest level of control and energetic cost

, unlike reciprocating engines that operate in a cycle which consists of discrete strokes, for example, the four stroke engines that are employed in the Otto and Carnot cycles
Uzdin et al. 2015

There are several models of autonomous thermal machines that utilise various quantum systems.
For instance, a recent implementation of an autonomous quantum absorption refrigerator involved trapped ions

, and proposals for implementation exist in other platforms like circuit-QED architecture
Hofer et al. 2016

;

.

One of the main open problems in the area of quantum thermal machines is how to convert the work generated by the machine into mechanical motion. One promising avenue consists of attaching a quantum rotor to the engine. The work thus converted into motion, can then be extracted by means of a dissipative load

;
Seah et al. 2018a
;
Seah et al. 2018b
;

;

;
Van Horne et al. 2020
;
Culhane et al. 2022
;
Martins et al. 2023
.

In this paper, we look at a hybrid system consisting of two qubits and a rotor, see Fig.
1

In the limit of a short collision time, the evolution of the system is described by a local master equation (LME) whose thermodynamics is well understood and does not lead to any inconsistency

Within this framework, we find the conditions required for the device to operate as an engine, refrigerator and accelerator.

In the second version of the model, we also investigate the device’s potential to act as a heat rectifier, which would result in a non-symmetrical heat flow.
To this end, see Fig.

Additionally, motivated by the conversion of heat into mechanical motion, we introduce the concept of angular momentum rectification. This phenomenon is observed when swapping the temperatures of the external baths, resulting in a significant change in the rotor’s motional response. Similar to heat rectification, we evaluate this feature and demonstrate the device’s employability as a switch when coupling a load to the rotor, allowing for control of its motion.

Overall, our findings showcase the versatility of this hybrid quantum device and its potential to perform a range of useful functions, which could have significant implications for the experimental development of advanced quantum technologies, see for instance Refs.

II
Model

Our system consists of two qubits interacting with a rotor such that the system’s Hamiltonian is (
\hbar=1
):

H_{S}=H_{S,0}+H_{S,I},

(1)

where the first term is composed of the free Hamiltonian of the two qubits and the kinetic energy of the rotor

H_{S,0}=B_{1}\sigma_{1}^{z}+B_{2}\sigma_{2}^{z}+\frac{L_{z}^{2}}{2I},

(2)

with
L_{z}
and
I
the rotor’s angular momentum projection along
z
and moment of inertia, respectively. The operators
\sigma_{i}^{x},\sigma_{i}^{y},\sigma_{i}^{z}
are the Pauli operators for the qubits while
B_{i}
are the applied magnetic fields, with
i=1,2
. The collective three-way interaction depicted in Fig.
1
is described by the so-called “quantum mill” Hamiltonian
Gilz et al. 2013
;
Seah et al. 2018a

H_{S,I}=\lambda(\sigma_{1}^{+}\sigma_{2}^{-}e^{i\varphi}+\sigma_{1}^{-}\sigma_{2}^{+}e^{-i\varphi}),

(3)

where
\sigma_{i}^{\pm}=(\sigma_{i}^{x}+i\sigma_{i}^{y})/2
are ladder operators and
\varphi
is the angle operator canonically conjugated to
L_{z}
, i.e.
[e^{i\varphi},L_{z}]=-e^{i\varphi}

Roulet et al. 2017
. The three-body Hamiltonian
H_{S,I}
allows energy transfer from one qubit to another concurrently with the rotation of the rotor.
Three-body interactions have been proposed and experimentally realised in different quantum platforms, including ultracold atoms, trapped ions, Rydberg ions and superconducting circuits
Pachos and Plenio 2004
;
Abdi et al. 2015
;
Feng and Wang 2020
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Martins et al. 2023
;
Menke et al. 2022
. Though three-body interactions are not necessary to realise thermal machines
Hewgill et al. 2020
, they often lead to more powerful and efficient machines

.

Qubit 1 is coupled to a cold bath at inverse temperature
\beta_{1}
and qubit 2 is coupled to a hot bath at inverse temperature
\beta_{2}
, with
\beta_{2}<\beta_{1}
. The rotor is coupled to a dissipative load that allows it to reach a stationary rotation rate without accelerating indefinitely.
In the weak system-bath coupling regime, we assume the reduced dynamics of the system to be governed by a Lindblad-Markovian master equation:

\dot{\rho}_{S}=-i\commutator{H_S}{\rho_S}+\mathcal{L}_{1}[\rho_{S}]+\mathcal{L}_{2}[\rho_{S}]+\mathcal{L}_{r}[\rho_{S}],

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{i}
,
i=1,2,r
, are the Lindblad superoperators associated with the interaction of the system with the cold bath, the hot bath, and the dissipative load of the rotor, respectively.

In the rest of the paper, we consider two models (see Fig.
1
). In the first model (collision model, Fig.
1
(a)), the environment is modelled as a series of quantum harmonic oscillators sequentially interacting with the system. The resulting dynamics in the limit of short collisions lead to an LME. In the second model (spin-boson model, Fig.
1
(b)), each qubit is permanently coupled to an infinite ensemble of quantum harmonic oscillators in thermal equilibrium. The resulting dynamics leads to a GME.

II.1
Collision model

In this section we introduce the first model, depicted in Fig.
1
(a), in which each qubit is coupled to its environment through repeated interactions.
Each bath is modelled by an ensemble of identical bosonic modes such that their Hamiltonian is
H_{B}=H_{B,1}+H_{B,2}
, where

H_{B,i}=2B_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{i,k}^{\dagger}a_{i,k}.

(5)

The index
k
labels the bath’s field modes with
a_{i,k}(a_{i,k}^{\dagger})
being their annihilation (creation) operators, fulfilling the usual bosonic commutation relations:
[a_{i,k},a_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
and
[a_{i,k},a_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}]=0
. Each quantum harmonic oscillator interacts with the corresponding qubit for a small time
\tau
and then it is discarded.
Each oscillator in the cold [hot] bath is coupled to qubit 1 [2] with a coupling strength
g_{1}
[
g_{2}
] so that the system-bath interaction Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1,2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}g_{i}\left[\sigma_{i}^{-}a_{i,k}^{\dagger}+\sigma_{i}^{+}a_{i,k}\right].

(6)

Following the collision model approach, as in
De Chiara et al. 2018
;
Campbell and Vacchini 2021
;
Ciccarello et al. 2022
, we define

H=H_{S}+H_{B}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}H_{I}.

(7)

We prepare the baths in thermal states at inverse temperature
\beta_{i}
(
i=1,2
)

\rho_{B,i}=\frac{e^{-\beta_{i}H_{B,i}}}{\text{Tr}[e^{-\beta_{i}H_{B,i}}]},

(8)

and we define
\rho_{B}=\rho_{B,1}\otimes\rho_{B,2}
.

A collision occurring at a time
t
can be described by a unitary operator
\mathcal{U}=e^{-iH\tau}
such that the state of the system after the collision is expressed as

\rho_{S}(t+\tau)=\text{Tr}_{B}\left[\mathcal{U}\;\rho_{S}(t)\otimes\rho_{B}\;\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\right],

(9)

where
\rho_{S}(t)
is the state of the system before the interaction with the thermal baths.
Then, we apply the Baker-Campbell-Haussdorff formula

\displaystyle e^{iH\tau}Ae^{-iH\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A+i\tau\commutator{H}{A}+\frac{(i\tau)^{2}}{2!}\commutator{H}{\comm{H}{A}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\dots+\frac{(i\tau)^{n}}{n!}\commutator{H}{\dots\comm{H}{\comm{H}{A}}}+\dots,

which leads to the expansion of Eq. (
9
) in
\tau

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(t+\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(t)-i\tau\text{Tr}_{B}\commutator{H}{\rho_S(t)\otimes\rho_B}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{2}\text{Tr}_{B}\commutator{H_I}{\comm{H_I}{\rho_S(t)\otimes\rho_B}}+O(\tau^{2}).

By dividing both sides by
\tau
and taking the limit of
\tau\to 0
, we find the time derivative

\dot{\rho}_{S}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\frac{\rho_{S}(t+\tau)-\rho_{S}(t)}{\tau},

(12)

which fulfils the LME (dropping the explicit time dependence of
\rho_{S}
)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S}=-i\commutator{H_S}{\rho_S}+\sum_{i=1,2,r}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}].

(13)

The local thermal Lindblad superoperators acting on qubits
i=1,2
are given by:

\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}]=g_{i}^{2}\left(n_{i}\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{i}^{+}]+(n_{i}+1)\mathcal{D}[\sigma_{i}^{-}]\right)

(14)

where
\mathcal{D}[O]=O\rho_{S}O^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{O^{\dagger}O,\rho_{S}\}
represent the usual Lindblad dissipators
Breuer et al. 2002
.
For later convenience, we define the coupling constants between the system and each bath as

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(1-\chi),

\displaystyle g_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(1+\chi),

(15)

so that varying
-1<\chi<1
allows us to study the effect of the difference in coupling strength of the cold and hot baths.
The thermal occupations
n_{i}
are related to the baths’ inverse temperatures by:

n_{i}=[e^{2\beta_{i}B_{i}}-1]^{-1}.

(16)

Employing a similar treatment for the rotor, the action of the dissipative load corresponds to the Lindblad term
\mathcal{L}_{r}
Seah et al. 2018a
;
Stickler et al. 2018

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2I\gamma}{\beta_{r}}\left\{\mathcal{D}\left[\cos\varphi-\frac{i\beta_{r}\sin\varphi L_{z}}{4I}\right]\right.

(17)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\mathcal{D}\left[\sin\varphi+\frac{i\beta_{r}\cos\varphi L_{z}}{4I}\right]\right\},

where
\gamma
is the dissipation rate and
\beta_{r}
is the inverse temperature of the bath modelling the dissipative load. The effect of the dissipative load is twofold: it provides a frictional force allowing the rotor to reach a steady state, rather than continuing to accelerate, and it also allows the conversion of mechanical work from the rotor into the load.
In the absence of coupling to the two qubits, the rotor coupled to the dissipative load will reach a thermal equilibrium state at inverse temperature
\beta_{r}
.

The thermodynamics of collision models can be derived from the corresponding microscopic model.
Since the environmental harmonic oscillators are initially in thermal equilibrium, we obtain the heat flows as minus the energy change in the bath
De Chiara et al. 2018
:

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{i}^{\text{(loc)}}=-\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\text{Tr}\left[H_{B,i}\Delta\rho_{SB}\right]=\text{Tr}[H_{S,0}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}]],

(18)

where
\Delta\rho_{SB}=\mathcal{U}\;\rho_{S}(t)\otimes\rho_{B}\;\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}-\rho_{S}(t)\otimes\rho_{B}
. After some algebra we obtain more explicitly

\dot{Q}_{i}^{\text{(loc)}}=2B_{i}g_{i}^{2}\left(n_{i}\expectationvalue{\sigma_{i}^- \sigma_{i}^{+}}_{t}-(n_{i}+1)\expectationvalue{\sigma_{i}^+ \sigma_{i}^{-}}_{t}\right),

(19)

where

\expectationvalue{\sigma_{i}^+ \sigma_{i}^{-}}_{t}=\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{S}\sigma_{i}^{+}\sigma_{i}^{-}\right].

(20)

Similarly, the dissipative load attached to the rotor absorbs the heat current

\dot{Q}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}=\text{Tr}[H_{S,0}\mathcal{L}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}]],

(21)

where
\mathcal{L}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}
is given in Eq. (
17
).

For LMEs, besides the usual heat currents, there may be an additional work source, due to the “locality” of the collisions which model microscopically the dissipation, see Ref.
De Chiara et al. 2018
.
For the two qubits, this can be found as the total energy change of both the system and bath:

\displaystyle\dot{W}^{(Q)}=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\text{Tr}\left[(H_{S}+H_{B})\Delta\rho_{SB}\right],

(22)

which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{W}^{(Q)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left[g_{1}^{2}(2n_{1}+1)+g_{2}^{2}(2n_{2}+1)\right]

(23)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\expectationvalue{\sigma_1^+ \sigma_2^- e^{i\varphi}}_{t}+\expectationvalue{\sigma_1^- \sigma_2^+ e^{-i\varphi}}_{t}\right].

The work injected by the local baths into the qubits can be also expressed as:

\dot{W}^{\text{(Q)}}=\text{Tr}\left[H_{S,I}\left(\mathcal{L}_{1}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}]+\mathcal{L}_{2}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}]\right)\right],

(24)

while the corresponding extra work associated with the rotor’s dissipation can be written as:

\dot{W}^{(\text{r})}=\text{Tr}\left[H_{S,I}\mathcal{L}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}]\right].

(25)

Using Eqs. (
13
), (
18
), (
21
), (
22
) and (
25
), it can be proven that the first law of thermodynamics holds

:

\dot{U}_{S}=\dot{Q}_{1}^{\text{(loc)}}+\dot{Q}_{2}^{\text{(loc)}}+\dot{Q}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}+\dot{W}^{\text{(Q)}}+\dot{W}^{\text{(r)}},

(26)

where
\dot{U}_{S}={\rm Tr}\left[\dot{\rho}_{S}H_{S}\right]
is the rate of change of the system’s energy (assuming
H_{S}
to be time independent).
Since we are choosing
\gamma
to be small, the magnitude of
\dot{W}^{\text{(r)}}
is negligible compared to the heat flows and to
\dot{W}^{\text{(Q)}}
.

Throughout this paper, we are using the convention that heat flowing into the system or work done on the system, thus increasing its internal energy, has a positive sign and vice versa.

II.2
Spin-boson model

In our second model, we consider a variant of the spin-boson model, in which each qubit is permanently coupled to an infinite set of quantum harmonic oscillators in thermal equilibrium, see Fig.
1
(b). In the weak-coupling limit, the dynamics of the system can be described by
a global master equation with respect to the interaction between qubits. In this case, the jump operators are obtained by deriving the master equation in the basis of the system’s full Hamiltonian’s eigenstates. In order to derive the GME, we need to write the complete evolution of the system and the bath and then apply the Born-Markov and secular approximation in the eigenbasis of
H_{S}
. The rotor’s dissipative load is, for simplicity, still described by its local form in Eq. (
17
) assuming the corresponding dissipation rate
\gamma
is small compared to the coupling
\lambda
. For this reason the resulting equation, Eq. (
27
) should more accurately be dubbed a semiglobal master equation. A thorough derivation is reported in Appendix
A
and the resulting equation is:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\commutator{H_S}{\rho_S}+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{\text{(glob)}}[\rho_{S}]

(27)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{L}_{2}^{\text{(glob)}}[\rho_{S}]+\mathcal{L}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}[\rho_{S}].

where
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{\text{(glob)}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{\text{(glob)}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}
are the superoperators that account for the energy jumps induced by both the interaction and the thermal
baths.

Under these assumptions,
\dot{W}^{\text{(Q)}}=0
. Hence, the first law now reads

\dot{U}_{S}=\dot{Q}_{1}^{\text{(glob)}}+\dot{Q}_{2}^{\text{(glob)}}+\dot{Q}_{r}^{\text{(loc)}}+\dot{W}^{\text{(r)}}.

(28)

The work correction
\dot{W}^{\text{(r)}}
, under the assumption of small
\gamma
, is much smaller than the magnitude of the heat flows, so we will neglect it in the following.

The heat flows in terms of the superoperators
\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\text{(glob)}}
from the GME (
27
)

\dot{Q}_{i}^{\text{(glob)}}=\text{Tr}\left[H_{S}\mathcal{L}^{\text{(glob)}}_{i}[\rho_{S}]\right],

(29)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\text{(glob)}}_{i}
,

now act on the combined Hilbert space of the two qubits and the rotor.

III
Thermal machines

In this section, we discuss the possible functionings of our setup as thermal devices. To do this we will be using the LME, the heat flows and power defined in Eqs. (
21
) and (
24
). First, we introduce in Sec.
III.1
the rotor’s mechanical work which is a measure of the amount of work that can be extracted from the rotor’s kinetic energy. Then, in Sec.
III.2
, we list the possible modes in which the setup can operate as a thermal machine. Finally, we introduce heat rectification and angular momentum rectification as well as their measures in Sec.
III.3
.

III.1
The rotor’s mechanical work

When defining the work done by the rotor, there are several useful quantities that we may look at
Seah et al. 2018a
;
Seah et al. 2018b
.
The rate of change of the rotor’s kinetic energy can simply be defined as:

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\text{kin}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\langle L_{z}^{2}\rangle}{2I}=\frac{1}{2I}\text{Tr}[\dot{\rho}_{S}L_{z}^{2}]

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\text{int}}+\dot{Q}_{BA}.

(31)

In the preceding expression,
\dot{W}_{\text{kin}}
is split into two terms.
The first term represents the intrinsic power

\dot{W}_{\text{int}}=-\frac{i}{2I}\text{Tr}\left[\commutator{H_S}{\rho_S}L_{z}^{2}\right]

(32)

which is the rate of change in the kinetic energy that is due to the working medium exerting force on the rotor, and the second term is the back-action heat flow

\dot{Q}_{BA}=\frac{1}{2I}\text{Tr}\left[(\mathcal{L}_{1}[\rho_{S}]+\mathcal{L}_{2}[\rho_{S}]+\mathcal{L}_{r}[\rho_{S}])L_{z}^{2}\right],

(33)

which is due to angular momentum diffusion in the rotor and does not contribute to useful work. In Eq. (
33
) the Lindblad superoperators may be from the local or global master equation.
Note that the kinetic energy could be disordered, as
\langle L_{z}^{2}\rangle
does not take into account the direction of the rotor’s rotation. Also, the kinetic energy could come from the unwanted heating of the rotor.

To resolve this issue, we can look instead at the net power, i.e. the rate of change in kinetic energy exclusively in either the clockwise or anticlockwise direction. This net power is defined as

\dot{W}_{\text{net}}=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\langle L_{z}\rangle^{2}}{2I}=\frac{1}{I}\text{Tr}[\rho_{S}L_{z}]\text{Tr}[\dot{\rho}_{S}L_{z}].

(34)

Note that at the steady state, when
\dot{\rho}_{S}=0
, the kinetic and net power are zero.

Another useful quantity to study the capacity of the system to do work is the quantum ergotropy, which quantifies the maximum amount of extractable work by means of unitary transformations
Allahverdyan and Nieuwenhuizen 2002
;
Niedenzu et al. 2019
;
Touil et al. 2021
:

\mathcal{W}_{\text{erg}}=\text{max}_{U}\left\{\Tr[H_{S}\left(\rho_{S}-U\rho_{S}U^{\dagger}\right)]\right\}

(35)

where
U
is the unitary evolution of the system.

To assess the performance of our setup when operating as an engine, we use the efficiency of the engine, defined as

\eta=-\frac{\dot{W}^{(Q)}}{\dot{Q}_{2}}.

(36)

Since we are using thermal baths, the efficiency is bounded by the Carnot value at the lowest and highest temperatures,

\eta_{C}=1-\beta_{2}/\beta_{1}

To assess the functioning of the system as a refrigerator we employ the coefficient of performance (COP), defined as

III.3
Rectification

To study rectification, we will be using the GME and the heat flows that arise from it, as defined in Eq. (
29
). The global approach of the system lends itself well to the study of heat rectification as we may calculate the global heat flows in and out of the system as there is no external work done on the system.

Heat rectification is a form of asymmetric transport through a device when the two heat baths are swapped. It occurs due to the nonsymmetric arrangement of the microscopic constituents of a system. In our case this may be due to the different magnetic fields
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
applied to the two qubits, as well as the coupling to the baths.
In order to study rectification
Khandelwal et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2009
;
Bhandari et al. 2021
;
Riera-Campeny et al. 2019
;
Hewgill et al. 2021
, we calculate the heat flow into one of the baths coupled to the qubits, e.g.
\dot{Q}=\dot{Q}_{2}
. We then swap the temperatures of the two heat baths,
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
, keeping everything else the same (including the couplings to the baths) and recalculate the heat currents. The heat flow into the system is now

\dot{Q}^{\text{(swap)}}=\dot{Q}_{1}^{\text{(swap)}}

(38)

where (swap) indicates that the temperatures of the two heat baths have been swapped. We can then define the rectification parameter as

R=\left|\frac{\dot{Q}-\dot{Q}^{\text{(swap)}}}{\dot{Q}+\dot{Q}^{\text{(swap)}}}\right|.

(39)

If
R=0
, this means that heat can flow equally in both directions and there is no rectification,
\dot{Q}^{\text{(swap)}}=\dot{Q}
. For
0<R\leq 1
, the system acts as a rectifier. The maximum value
R=1
corresponds to an ideal rectifier where heat can only flow in one direction.

The problem with this definition of rectification is that we may be able to achieve a value of
R
close to
1
, but with heat flows that are almost zero, making the device not practically useful. To take into account both the rectification and the magnitude of the heat flow, we can introduce

J=\text{max}(|\dot{Q}|,|\dot{Q}^{\text{(swap)}}|)/(\lambda g^{2})

(40)

which is the maximum of the two heat flows. Note that we have divided by
\lambda g^{2}
so that
J
is dimensionless. Then, we can define the rectification performance’s measure as

Khandelwal et al. 2023

\Gamma_{\alpha}=\alpha R+(1-\alpha)J.

(41)

The parameter
\alpha
(
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
) allows us to study the trade-off between rectification and heat flow.

Not only can we study heat rectification, but we may also look at the rectification of the rotor’s angular momentum. In fact, we can think of the rotor as a windmill that is being rotated by the heat flowing through the qubits and capable, due to its angular momentum, of lifting weights (modelled with the dissipative load).

We define the angular momentum rectification coefficient to be of the form

R_{\rm angular}=\left|\frac{\expectationvalue{L_z}-\langle L_{z}^{\text{(swap)}}\rangle}{\expectationvalue{L_z}+\langle L_{z}^{\text{(swap)}}\rangle}\right|,

(42)

where
\expectationvalue{L_z}
and
\langle L_{z}^{\text{(swap)}}\rangle
are the steady-state expectation values of the
angular momentum operator in one configuration and in the configuration obtained by swapping the temperatures.

As before, since we are interested not only in the angular momentum’s rectification but also in its magnitude, we define

J_{\rm angular}=\text{max}(|\expectationvalue{L_z}|,|\langle L_{z}^{\text{(swap)}}\rangle|).

(43)

We may then vary
0\leq\chi\leq 1
again and calculate the corresponding
R
and
J
. The performance of the angular momentum rectification can be measured by the quantity

\Gamma_{\alpha,\text{angular}}=\alpha R_{\text{angular}}+(1-\alpha)J_{\text{angular}}.

(44)

Collision model: Thermal operations

Using the collision model, we calculate both the time-evolution of the system’s density matrix and the steady state obtained by solving the equation

We see that, depending on our choice of

From Fig.

V
Conclusions

We studied the out-of-equilibrium thermodynamics of a system consisting of two qubits and a quantum rotor coupled to two thermal heat baths. Two microscopic environment models were employed to analyse the dynamics, the system’s steady-state and find the corresponding heat flows and external power. Various definitions of work within the rotor were discussed, and we demonstrated that the addition of a dissipative load not only provides us a way of extracting work from the rotor, but also enables the system to reach a steady state.

Studying the steady state energy flows of the system leads to the identification of three operational regimes of the machine: an engine, a refrigerator and an accelerator.

Finally, we investigated both the heat and angular momentum rectification by introducing a figure of merit that depends on both the rectification parameter and the maximum heat current or angular momentum. If we are more interested in the magnitude of heat flow or angular momentum then we find that a higher-temperature hot bath allows for a larger
J
and
J_{\rm angular}
. On the other hand, for the case of heat rectification, a larger rectification parameter can be achievable with a lower temperature of the hot bath at the expense of heat, while for angular momentum rectification, we find that lowering the temperature of the hot bath can lead to a larger rectification parameter, albeit with a smaller angular momentum.

Appendix A
Derivation of the global master equation

To derive the global master equation we follow standard textbooks on open quantum systems (see for instance
Breuer et al. 2002
). First, we define the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
H_{S}
as

H_{S}\ket{\epsilon}=\epsilon\ket{\epsilon}

(45)

so that the corresponding projection operators are

\Pi(\epsilon)=\outerproduct{\epsilon}{\epsilon}.

(46)

In the spin-boson model, we assume each bath
i=1,2
to consist of an ensemble of non interacting harmonic oscillators with frequencies
\omega_{i,k}
:

H_{B}=\sum_{i}\sum_{k}\omega_{i,k}a_{i,k}^{\dagger}a_{i,k}.

(47)

The system-bath interaction Hamiltonian assumes the general form

H_{I}=\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes E_{i}^{\dagger}+A_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes E_{i},

(48)

where
A_{i}
and
E_{i}
are the system’s and bath’s operators, respectively. We set
A_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{-}
and
E_{i}=\sum_{k}g_{i,k}a_{i,k}
,
i=1,2
.
The system’s eigenoperators are defined as

A_{i}(\omega)=\sum_{\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon=\omega}\Pi(\epsilon)A_{i}\Pi(\epsilon^{\prime}),

(49)

and fulfill the relations

\displaystyle\commutator{H_{S}}{A_{i}(\omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\omega A_{i}(\omega),

(50)

\displaystyle\commutator{H_{S}}{A_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega A_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega).

(51)

In the interaction picture, we have for the system

e^{iH_{S}t}A_{i}(\omega)e^{-iH_{S}t}=e^{-i\omega t}A_{i}(\omega),

(52)

and for the bath:

E_{i}(t)=e^{iH_{B}t}E_{i}e^{-iH_{B}t}=\sum_{k}g_{i,k}a_{i,k}e^{-i\omega_{i,k}t}.

(53)

The two-time correlation functions are

\langle E_{i}^{\dagger}(s)E_{i}(0)\rangle=\sum_{k}g_{i,k}^{2}e^{i\omega_{i,k}s}n_{i}(\omega_{i,k}),

(54)

\langle E_{i}(s)E_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle=\sum_{k}g_{i,k}^{2}e^{-i\omega_{i,k}s}\left[n_{i}(\omega_{i,k})+1\right],

(55)

where
n_{i}
is the thermal occupation number of bath
i

n_{i}(\omega)=\text{Tr}[a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}\rho_{B_{i}}]=\frac{1}{e^{\beta_{i}\omega}-1}.

(56)

Then, an integral over a continuum of frequencies is applied

\int_{0}^{\infty}dse^{i\omega s}\langle E_{i}^{\dagger}(s)E_{i}(0)\rangle=J_{i}(\omega)n_{i}(\omega),

(57)

\int_{0}^{\infty}dse^{i\omega s}\langle E_{i}(s)E_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle=J_{i}(\omega)\left[n_{i}(\omega)+1\right],

(58)

where
J_{i}(\omega)
is the spectral density. Using these definitions, we can write the global master equation as

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\commutator{H_S}{\rho_S}

(59)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{\omega}J_{i}(\omega)n_{i}(\omega)\Bigl[A_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)\rho_{S}A_{i}(\omega)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{A_{i}(\omega)A_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)}{\rho_S}\Bigr]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{\omega}J_{i}(\omega)\left[n_{i}(\omega)+1\right]\Bigl[A_{i}(\omega)\rho_{S}A_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{A_{i}^{\dagger}(\omega)A_{i}(\omega)}{\rho_S}\Bigr].

We can add in the dissipative load “locally”, as in Eq. (
17
), as long as
\gamma
is small compared to the qubits-rotor coupling
\lambda
. This leads to the compact form of the master equation reported in Eq. (
27
).
From here on we will choose the spectral density to be Ohmic with a cutoff frequency
\Omega
:

J_{1}(\omega)=g^{2}(1-\chi)^{2}\frac{\omega\Omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\Omega^{2}},

(60)

J_{2}(\omega)=g^{2}(1+\chi)^{2}\frac{\omega\Omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\Omega^{2}},

(61)

where we select
\Omega=\omega_{\text{max}}
and
-1\leq\chi\leq 1
.

At this stage it is worth mentioning the intense debate on the comparison between LME and GME, see for instance Refs.
González et al. 2017
;
Hofer et al. 2017
. While alternative solutions that interpolate between the two approaches have been proposed
Cattaneo et al. 2019
, our GME approach coincides with the LME in the limit
\lambda\to 0
