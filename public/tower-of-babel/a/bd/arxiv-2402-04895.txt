Dynamic Coalition Portfolio Selection with Recursive Utility
Hanxiao Wang and Chao Zhou

Abstract




Abstract.

In this paper, we consider a dynamic coalition portfolio selection problem,
with each agent’s objective given by an Epstein–Zin recursive utility.
To find a Pareto optimum, the coalition’s problem is formulated as an optimization problem evolved by a multi-dimensional
forward-backward SDE. Since the evolution system has a forward-backward structure,
the problem is intrinsically time-inconsistent.
With the dynamic-game point of view,
we rigorously develop an approach to finding the equilibrium Pareto investment-consumption strategy.
We find that the relationship between risk aversion and EIS has more
influence on the coalition’s problem than that on the one-agent problem.
More interestingly, we show that the equilibrium Pareto consumption strategy associated
with the recursive utility is much more effective
than that associated with the CRRA expected utility,
which highlights the feature of recursive utility that the
marginal benefit of consumption can depend on the future consumption.

Keywords.
Portfolio selection, recursive utility, coalition optimization, cooperation game, time-inconsistency,
forward-backward SDE, equilibrium Pareto strategy.

AMS subject classifications.
91B10, 91B51, 91B70, 93E20, 49L12.

Introduction

Since the pioneering work by von Neumann and Morgenstern
[
41
]
,
coalitions have occupied the center stage for research within game theory.
In the social sciences, a minimal requirement on allocative efficiency is the so-called
Pareto optimality

(see Aziz, Brandt, and Harrenstein
[
2
]
, for example).
A well-known approach of finding Pareto optima is to solve a new optimization problem with the subjective
given by some convex combination of each agent’s subjective.
Then the optimum (if it exists) of the new problem is exactly a Pareto optimum of the coalition.

On the other hand,
it is well recognized that the recursive utility developed by Epstein and Zin
[
21
]

and Duffie and Epstein
[
12
]

is a milestone in macroeconomics and quantitative finance,
because it provides a simple but very useful tool to
separate investor’s risk aversion and elasticity of intertemporal substitution (EIS, for short).
From an economic theory view of point, this is much more reasonable than the standard constant relative risk aversion
(CRRA, for short) expected utility. Since then, the recursive utility has been widely applied on the study of asset pricing and
portfolio selection problems.
Another important feature of recursive utility is that the marginal
benefit of consumption depends not only on current
consumption but also (through the value function) on the trajectory of future consumption.
Such a property is playing a crucial role in the fast-growing green portfolios,
because one has to consider some sustainable development issues when modeling the performances
of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) investing;
see Pindyck and Wang
[
38
]
and Hong, Wang, and Yang
[
25
]
, for example.

Motivated by the above, we study a coalition portfolio selection problem,
with each agent’s performance described by an Epstein–Zin recursive utility
Y_{i}
.
We use
\gamma
,
\alpha
, and
\rho_{i}
to describe each agent’s risk aversion, EIS, and discount factor, respectively.
The main feature of our model is that the discount factor of each agent is allowed to be different from each each.
Indeed, since the discount rate is not only due to the interest rate,
it is more like a subjective preference for the time cost,
and then each agent should have an individual discount rate (see Dumas, Uppal, and Wang
[
16
]

and Borovička
[
6
]
). For example (see Gârleanu and Panageas
[
23
]
), the old and the young in a family usually have different preferences for the time risk (or the time cost), due to which forcing them to use the same discount rate is really unreasonable.

The purpose of this paper is to study the coalition’s investment-consumption performance when
the model involves heterogeneous discount rates.
Some interesting phenomenons produced by the heterogeneous discount rates and the recursive utility
are revealed.

The main contribution of this paper can be briefly summarized as follows.

(i) It is well-known (see EI Karoui, Peng, and Quenez
[
20
]
) by now that
the recursive utility is exactly the solution of some (nonlinear) backward stochastic
differential equation (BSDE, for short). Combining this with the wealth equation,
the coalition’s problem (denoted by Problem (C)) is reformulated as a maximizing problem with the controlled dynamics
described by a system of a forward SDE and a multi-dimensional BSDE. We find that Problem (C) is intrinsically time-inconsistent,
due to the appearance of backward state equations.
With the dynamic-game point of view introduced by Strotz
[
40
]
,
we rigorously develop an approach to constructing the equilibrium Pareto investment-consumption strategy.

(ii) We show that by applying the recursive utility, the effectiveness of equilibrium Pareto consumption strategy (EPCS, for short)
associated with CRRA utility can be significantly improved in the following sense:

\bar{c}_{i}(t)x=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}x,\qquad i=1,...,N,

Since the agents’ discount rates are heterogeneous,

Thus, it is not very effective to use the CRRA utility to model a dynamic coalition portfolio selection problem.
However, this inadequacy can be improved by replacing the CRRA utility by a recursive one.
Indeed, in the recursive case, the EPCS reads:

\bar{c}_{i}(t)x=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)}x,\qquad i=1,...,N.

),
but is proportional to current wealth for the log return (see Dai, Jin, Kou, and Xu
[
10
]
).

(iv) We show that the coalition manager only needs to decide the whole coalition’s investment strategy,
but has to make a decision for each agent’s consumption strategy.

2
The model and problem formulation

Consider the following forward SDE for the coalition’s wealth process
X
driven by a one-dimensional Brownian motion
W
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=\bigg[\nu X(s)+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(s)X(s)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}(s)X(s)\bigg]ds\\
&\quad+\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(s)X(s)dW(s),\qquad s\in[t,T],\\
X(t)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

where
\nu
is the riskless interest rate,
\mu>\nu
is the appreciation rate of one stock,

\sigma>0
is the volatility,
\pi_{i}\in L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T)
is agent
i
’s proportion of dollar amount
invested in the stock, and
c_{i}\in L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T)
is the consumption of agent
i
.
Here, the space
L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T)
is defined by

L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T)=\big\{\varphi:[0,T]\to[0,+\infty)\,|\,\varphi\hbox{ is essentially bounded}\big\}.

For simplicity, we use the following notations of the investment-consumption strategies from now on:

\pi=(\pi_{1},...,\pi_{N}),\qquad c=(c_{1},...,c_{N}).

Naturally, agent
i
wants to maximize his/her utility functional

J_{i}(t,x;\pi_{i},c_{i})=Y_{i}(t),

(2.2)

where
Y_{i}
, called an Epstein–Zin recursive utility
(see
[
12
,
13
,
15
,
20
]
, for example),
is determined by

Y_{i}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{T}g_{i}\big(c_{i}(r)X(r),Y_{i}(r)\big)dr+h_{i}(X(T))\bigg],\qquad s\in[t,T],

(2.3)

with

g_{i}(q,y)=\alpha^{-1}((1-\gamma)y)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big[q^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}((1-\gamma)y)^{{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big],

and

h_{i}(x)={x^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}.

The parameter
\gamma\in(0,1)
controls the
risk aversion of the agents,
(1-\alpha)^{-1}>0
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
gives the agents’ EIS,
and
\rho_{i}
is the subjective discount factor of agent
i
(which can be different for the different
i
).
If
\alpha=1-\gamma
, we have the additively separable aggregator:

g_{i}(q,y)=\alpha^{-1}\Big[q^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}(1-\gamma)y\Big],

(2.4)

and then

Y_{i}(s)=\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{T}e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(r-s)}\big(c_{i}(r)X(r)\big)^{\alpha}dr+e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(T-s)}X(T)^{\alpha}\bigg],\qquad s\in[t,T],

(2.5)

which is exactly the standard CRRA expected utility.
Unlike the CRRA utility (
2.5
), the recursive utility as defined by (
2.3
) disentangles risk
aversion from the EIS.

Since our model is formulated in a Markovian framework,
we only focus on the following Markovian solution to (
2.3
) for simplicity.

Proposition 2.1
.

Under the assumptions presented above, the following ODE admits a unique positive solution:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\theta}_{i}(s)+(1-\gamma)\theta_{i}(s)\bigg[(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(s)+\nu-{\gamma\sigma^{2}\over 2}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)\bigg)^{2}-{\rho_{i}\over\alpha}\bigg]\\
&\qquad+\alpha^{-1}(1-\gamma)\theta_{i}(s)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}c_{i}(s)^{\alpha}=0,\\
&\theta_{i}(T)=1.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.6)

Moreover,

Y_{i}(s)={\theta_{i}(s)\over 1-\gamma}X(s)^{1-\gamma},\qquad s\in[0,T]

(2.7)

is a solution to (
2.3
).

In our model, each agent
i
concerns with not only his/her own consumption
c_{i}

but also the coalition’s whole wealth
X
.
This is very natural in a coalition problem; for example, each family member usually
hopes to maximize both his/her own consumption amount
and the whole wealth possessed by the family.

The minimal requirement on allocative efficiency is the so-called Pareto optimality (see Aziz, Brandt, and Harrenstein
[
2
]
), which is defined as follows:

Definition 2.2
.

A pair
(\pi^{*},c^{*})\in(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}\times(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}
is called a
Pareto optimal investment-consumption strategy

if there exists no other pair
(\pi,c)\in(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}\times(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}
such that

J_{i}(t,x;\pi_{i},c_{i})\geqslant J_{i}(t,x;\pi^{*}_{i},c^{*}_{i}),\qquad i=1,...,N,

and at least one of the above
N
inequalities is strict.

To find a Pareto optimal investment-consumption strategy,
we introduce the following new problem: Maximize the following objective

J^{\lambda}(t,x;\pi,c)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}J_{i}(t,x;\pi_{i},c_{i}),

(2.8)

subject to (
2.1
) and (
2.2
),
where
\lambda_{i}\in(0,1)
with
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1
.

By the standard results in game theory (see Yong
[
47
, Proposition 3.2.1]
, for example),
we know that the optimal investment-consumption strategy
(\pi,c)
of the new problem
is a Pareto optimum of the coalition.
Since we focus on clarifying the performance of heterogeneous discount rates,
we take
\lambda_{i}={1\over N}
for
i=1,2,...,N
to reduce the additional heterogeneous terms.
In economic languages, all the agents are treated by the coalition equally.
Then the coalition problem’s objective (
2.8
) can be rewritten as follows:

J^{\lambda}(t,x;\pi,c)={1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}(t,x;\pi_{i},c_{i}).

(2.9)

3
Time-inconsistency of the model

In this section, we shall show that the Pareto optimum of the coalition is time-inconsistent in general.

For simplicity, we consider a special case of the model with
\alpha=1-\gamma
,
by which the recursive utility reduces to a CRRA one.
It follows that

\displaystyle J^{\lambda}(t,x;\pi,c)

\displaystyle={1\over\alpha N}\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(T-t)}\bigg)X(T)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{T}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(r-t)}\big(c_{i}(r)X(r)\big)^{\alpha}\bigg)dr\bigg].

(3.1)

For any fixed initial time
t\in[0,T)
,
we introduce the following dynamic problem over
[t,T]
: For any given
\tau\in[t,T)
,
maximize

\displaystyle J^{\lambda}(\tau,x;\pi,c)

\displaystyle={1\over\alpha N}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(T-t)}\bigg)X(T)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\tau}^{T}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(r-t)}\big(c_{i}(r)X(r)\big)^{\alpha}\bigg)dr\bigg],

subject to

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=\bigg[\nu X(s)+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(s)X(s)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}(s)X(s)\bigg]ds\\
&\quad+\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(s)X(s)dW(s),\qquad s\in[\tau,T],\\
X(\tau)&=x.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that the above is a standard optimal control problem,
because
t
is fixed in (
3.1
) and only plays a role as a parameter.
In the literature, such type of problems is usually called an
auxiliary problem
of the original problem.
By the standard DPP approach,
the optimal investment-consumption strategy
(\pi^{t,*},c^{t,*})
with initial time
t

can be explicitly obtained:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}^{t,*}(s)={\mu-\nu\over(1-\alpha)\sigma^{2}},\qquad s\in[t,T],

(3.2)

\displaystyle c_{i}^{t,*}(s)=\bigg({N\theta^{t}(s)\over e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(s-t)}}\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1},\qquad s\in[t,T],\quad i=1,...,N,

(3.3)

in which
\theta^{t}
is the unique positive solution to the following ODE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\theta}^{t}(s)+{\alpha(\mu-\nu)^{2}\over 2(1-\alpha)\sigma^{2}}\theta^{t}(s)+(1-\alpha)N^{1\over\alpha-1}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{\alpha\rho_{i}(s-t)\over\alpha-1}\bigg)\theta^{t}(s)^{\alpha\over\alpha-1}=0,\\
&\theta^{t}(T)={1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(T-t)}.\end{aligned}\right.

Since the relationship
\rho_{i}=\rho_{j}
does not hold in general,
it is easily shown that there exists at least an
i_{0}\in\{1,...,N\}
such that
c_{i_{0}}^{t,*}
depends on
t
.
Indeed, without loss of generality, we can suppose that there exist two indexes
i_{0},j_{0}\in\{1,...,N\}
such that
\rho_{i_{0}}\neq\rho_{j_{0}}
,
and
c_{j_{0}}^{t,*}
does not depend on
t
. Then

c_{i_{0}}^{t,*}(s)=\bigg({N\theta^{t}(s)\over e^{\alpha\rho_{i_{0}}(s-t)}}\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}=c_{j_{0}}^{t,*}(s)e^{\alpha(\rho_{j_{0}}-\rho_{i_{0}})(s-t)\over 1-\alpha},\qquad s\in[t,T].

Note that
c_{j_{0}}^{t,*}
does not depend on
t
and
e^{\alpha(\rho_{j_{0}}-\rho_{i_{0}})(s-t)\over 1-\alpha}

contains a parameter
t
, so the product
c_{i_{0}}^{t,*}
must depend on
t
.
Thus, the problem is time-inconsistent and the strategy given by (
3.2
) is a pre-committed optimum.

We remark that since
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
in general, we cannot simplify the above problem
as a one-agent investment-consumption problem with a CRRA utility and a quasi-exponential discount,
as studied by Ekeland, Mbodji, and Pirvu
[
18
]
.
By the equilibrium HJB equation approach
(see Yong
[
45
,
46
]
, for example) or the extended HJB equation approach
(see Björk, Khapko, and Murgoci
[
4
]
, for example),
the time-consistent equilibrium investment-consumption strategy (which is also called a sophisticated solution) associated with (
2.1
) and (
3.1
) can be obtained.
However, for the general case with
\gamma\neq 1-\alpha
, the situation of controlled backward state equation is not
avoidable, and then the approach developed in
[
45
,
46
,
4
]

cannot be applied.

4
The main result

In finance, time-consistency is an essential requirement of the practicable strategies.
When the problem is time-inconsistent, it is no longer clear what we mean by the word
“optimal”, because a strategy which is optimal for one choice of starting point in
time will generically not be optimal at a later point in time (see Björk, Murgoci, and Zhou
[
5
]
).
Thus, instead of finding a Pareto optimum,
we are going to find an equilibrium Pareto strategy for the coalition,
with the dynamic-game theoretic framework introduced by Strotz
[
40
]
.

4.1
Equilibrium Pareto strategy

Inspired by
[
27
,
45
,
4
]
, we introduce the following definition,
which gives the Pareto optimality in a dynamic-game sense.

Definition 4.1
.

We call
(\bar{\pi},\bar{c})\in(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}\times(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}

an
equilibrium Pareto investment-consumption strategy
(EPICS, for short)
of the coalition, if the following holds:

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}{J^{\lambda}(t,\bar{X}(t);\bar{\pi}^{\varepsilon},\bar{c}^{\varepsilon})-J^{\lambda}(t,\bar{X}(t);\bar{\pi},\bar{c})\over\varepsilon}\leqslant 0,

(4.1)

for any
t\in[0,T)
and
(\pi,c)\in(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}\times(L_{+}^{\infty}(0,T))^{N}
, with

\bar{\pi}_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)\triangleq\left\{\begin{aligned} &\bar{\pi}_{i}(s),\quad&s\in[t+\varepsilon,T];\\
&\pi_{i}(s),\quad&s\in[t,t+\varepsilon),\end{aligned}\right.\qquad i=1,...,N,

(4.2)

and

\bar{c}_{i}^{\varepsilon}(s)\triangleq\left\{\begin{aligned} &\bar{c}_{i}(s),\quad&s\in[t+\varepsilon,T];\\
&c_{i}(s),\quad&s\in[t,t+\varepsilon),\end{aligned}\right.\qquad i=1,...,N.

(4.3)

Remark 4.2
.

We only consider the closed-loop strategy in the paper.
The intuition behind
4.1
is similar to that in
[
27
,
45
,
4
]
.
At any given time
t
, the controller is playing a game with all his/her incarnations in the future by maximizing his/her
utility functional on
[t,t+\varepsilon)
, and knowing that he/she will lose the control of the system beyond
t+\varepsilon
.
This can be regarded a leader-follower game with infinitely many players.

Remark 4.3
.

We remark that there are two games in our model.
The agents not only play against their incarnations in the future, but also
play a cooperation game with each other.

4.2
Equilibrium HJB equation: Verification theorem and well-posedness

From Section
3
, we see that the pre-committed optimal strategy in the CRRA utility case
can be obtained by introducing an auxiliary problem. However, extending this approach to the recursive utility case is challenge.
In other words, it is not easy to find a pre-committed optimal strategy for the coalition in recursive utility case,
due to the appearance of controlled multi-dimensional BSDEs.
Thus, with the equilibrium strategy
(\bar{\pi},\bar{c})
having been obtained on
[t+\varepsilon,T]
,
the optimization problem on
[t,t+\varepsilon)
, which is the sophisticated problem of the original, still cannot be solved by the DPP approach. This brings some new difficulties to finding the equilibrium strategy (i.e., the sophisticated solution) in our model comparing with Yong
[
45
,
46
]
and Björk, Khapko, and Murgoci
[
4
]
.

Motivated by our recent work
[
43
]
with Yong, we introduce the following
equilibrium HJB equation

(or called an
extended HJB equation
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Theta^{i}_{t}(t,x)+\Theta^{i}_{x}(t,x)\bigg[\nu x+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}(t)x-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{c}_{i}(t)x\bigg]\\
&\qquad+{1\over 2}\Theta^{i}_{xx}(t,x)\bigg(\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}(t)x\bigg)^{2}+\alpha^{-1}\big[(1-\gamma)\Theta^{i}(t,x)\big]^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\\
&\qquad\times\Big[(\bar{c}_{i}(t)x)^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}\big((1-\gamma)\Theta^{i}(t,x)\big)^{{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big]=0,\qquad i=1,...,N,\\
&V_{t}(t,x)+V_{x}(t,x)\bigg[\nu x+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}(t)x-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{c}_{i}(t)x\bigg]\\
&\qquad+{1\over 2}V_{xx}(t,x)\bigg(\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}(t)x\bigg)^{2}+{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha^{-1}\big[(1-\gamma)\Theta^{i}(t,x)\big]^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\\
&\qquad\times\Big[(\bar{c}_{i}(t)x)^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}\big((1-\gamma)\Theta^{i}(t,x)\big)^{{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big]=0,\\
&\Theta^{i}(T,x)={x^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma},\qquad i=1,...,N,\qquad V(T,x)={x^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma},\end{aligned}\right.

in which the equilibrium investment-consumption strategy
(\bar{\pi},\bar{c})

(if it exists)
is determined by the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle{\cal H}(t,x,\Theta(t,x),V_{x}(t,x),V_{xx}(t,x),\bar{\pi}(t),\bar{c}(t))

\displaystyle\quad=\sup_{\pi,c}{\cal H}(t,x,\Theta(t,x),V_{x}(t,x),V_{xx}(t,x),\pi,c),

(4.4)

with

\displaystyle{\cal H}(t,x,\Theta(t,x),V_{x}(t,x),V_{xx}(t,x),\pi,c)

\displaystyle\quad=V_{x}(t,x)\bigg[\nu x+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}x-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}x\bigg]+{1\over 2}V_{xx}(t,x)\bigg(\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}x\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}(c_{i}x,\Theta_{i}(t,x))

\displaystyle\quad=V_{x}(t,x)\bigg[\nu x+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}x-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}x\bigg]+{1\over 2}V_{xx}(t,x)\bigg(\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}x\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha^{-1}\big((1-\gamma)\Theta^{i}(t,x)\big)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big[(c_{i}x)^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}\big((1-\gamma)\Theta^{i}(t,x)\big)^{{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big].

(4.5)

Clearly, if the equilibrium strategy exists, then we have

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}(t)x={(\nu-\mu)V_{x}(t,x)\over\sigma^{2}V_{xx}(t,x)},

(4.6)

and

\bar{c}_{i}(t)x=\left[NV_{x}(t,x)\right]^{1\over\alpha-1}[(1-\gamma)\Theta^{i}(t,x)]^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)},\qquad i=1,...,N.

(4.7)

Now we make the ansatz:

\displaystyle\Theta^{i}(t,x)={1\over 1-\gamma}x^{1-\gamma}\theta_{i}(t),\qquad V(t,x)={v(t)\over 1-\gamma}x^{1-\gamma}={\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)\over N(1-\gamma)}x^{1-\gamma}.

(4.8)

Then

\displaystyle\Theta^{i}_{x}(t,x)=\theta_{i}(t)x^{-\gamma},\quad\Theta^{i}_{xx}(x)=-\gamma\theta_{i}(t)x^{-\gamma-1},

\displaystyle V_{x}(t,x)={1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)x^{-\gamma},\qquad V_{xx}(t,x)=-{\gamma\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)x^{-\gamma-1}.

The equilibrium investment strategy (
4.6
) and the equilibrium consumption strategy (
4.7
) become

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}(t)x={(\mu-\nu)\over\gamma\sigma^{2}}x,

(4.9)

and

\bar{c}_{i}(t)x=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)}x,\qquad i=1,...,N,

(4.10)

respectively, with

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\theta}_{i}(t)+(1-\gamma)\theta_{i}(t)\bigg[\nu+{(\mu-\nu)^{2}\over 2\gamma\sigma^{2}}-\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{k}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)}\bigg]\\
&\quad-(1-\gamma)\rho_{i}\alpha^{-1}\theta_{i}(t)+\alpha^{-1}(1-\gamma)\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{\alpha\over\alpha-1}=0,\\
&\theta_{i}(T)=1.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.11)

We call (
4.11
) an
equilibrium ODE
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that the equilibrium ODE (
4.11
) has a positive solution.
Then the strategy
(\bar{\pi},\bar{c})
given by (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) is an EPICS of the coalition.

To guarantee the well-posedness of (
4.11
), we introduce the following conditions.

(A1)
The coefficients
\gamma
,
\alpha
, and
\rho_{i}
satisfy

\gamma\in(0,1),\qquad\max_{i=1,...,N}\rho_{i}\leqslant\alpha\nu+{(\mu-\nu)^{2}\over 2\gamma\sigma},

(4.12)

or

\gamma\in[1-\alpha,1).

(4.13)

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
(A1)
hold.
Then the equilibrium ODE (
4.11
) admits a unique positive solution.

Combining
Theorem 4.4
and
Theorem 4.5
together,
we have the following result immediately.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
(A1)
hold.

given by (

5
Interpretation and numerics

The equilibrium investment strategy is given by

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}(t)={(\mu-\nu)\over\gamma\sigma^{2}},\qquad t\in[0,T].

It is independent of the discount rate
\rho_{i}
, which coincides with the case of one-agent
optimal investment-consumption problem.
We remark that the coalition only needs to decide the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\pi}_{i}={(\mu-r)\over\gamma\sigma^{2}}
of each agent’s investment strategy.

The equilibrium consumption strategy is given by

\bar{c}_{i}(t)=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)},\qquad i=1,...,N.

It shows that the coalition needs to choose an explicit consumption strategy for each agent.
Thus, although the agents are playing a cooperation game, how to share the wealth is still much more troublesome than
making money itself.

5.1
Monotonic property of equilibrium consumption strategy

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(A1)
hold. Then for any two given discount rates
\rho_{j_{0}}\geqslant\rho_{i_{0}}\geqslant 0
,
the corresponding equilibrium consumption strategies
\bar{c}_{i_{0}}
and
\bar{c}_{j_{0}}
satisfy
\bar{c}_{i_{0}}\geqslant\bar{c}_{j_{0}}
if
\gamma\in(0,1-\alpha]
, and
\bar{c}_{i_{0}}\leqslant\bar{c}_{j_{0}}
if
\gamma\in[1-\alpha,1)
.

Proof.

Recall that the agents’ equilibrium consumption strategies are given by

\bar{c}_{i}(t)=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\gamma)(1-\alpha)},\qquad i=1,...,N.

From the proof of
Theorem 4.5
, we know that

\theta_{i_{0}}\geqslant\theta_{j_{0}}>0.

If
\gamma\in(0,1-\alpha]
, we have
{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\gamma)(1-\alpha)}\geqslant 0
, and then
\bar{c}_{i_{0}}\geqslant\bar{c}_{j_{0}}
.
Similarly, we have
\bar{c}_{i_{0}}\leqslant\bar{c}_{j_{0}}
if
\gamma\in[1-\alpha,1)
.
∎

The parameter
\gamma\in(0,1)
controls the risk aversion of the agents.
If the risk aversion coefficient
\gamma
is smaller than the reciprocal
(1-\alpha)
of the EIS
(1-\alpha)^{-1}
,
the agent with a smaller discount factor will be distributed more rewards
than the one with a bigger discount factor.
However, when the risk aversion coefficient
\gamma
is bigger than the reciprocal of the EIS,
the agent with a smaller discount factor will be distributed less rewards
than the one with a bigger discount factor.
Thus, the relation between the risk aversion and the EIS has an important influence on the coalition’s
taste of the discount factors.

5.2
Comparison with one-agent model

In the one-agent model, the wealth equation and the objective are given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)&=\Big[\nu X(s)+(\mu-\nu)\pi(s)X(s)-c(s)X(s)\Big]ds\\
&\quad+\sigma\pi(s)X(s)dW(s),\qquad s\in[t,T],\\
X(t)&=x,\end{aligned}\right.

and

J(t,\xi;\pi,c)=Y(t),

respectively, with

Y(s)=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{T}g(c(r)X(r),Y(r))dr+h(X(T))\bigg],\qquad s\in[t,T],

and

g(q,y)=\alpha^{-1}((1-\gamma)y)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big[q^{\alpha}-\rho((1-\gamma)y)^{{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big],\qquad h(x)={x^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}.

By the recursive DPP established by Peng
[
36
]
,
the optimal investment-consumption strategy
(\pi^{*},c^{*})
is given by

\pi^{*}(t)={(\mu-\nu)\over\gamma\sigma^{2}},\qquad c^{*}(t)=\theta(t)^{-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)},

(5.1)

with
\theta
being the unique positive solution of the following ODE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\theta}(t)+(1-\gamma)\theta(t)\left[\nu+{(\mu-\nu)^{2}\over 2\gamma\sigma^{2}}\right]\\
&\qquad-(1-\gamma)\rho\alpha^{-1}\theta(t)+(\alpha^{-1}-1)(1-\gamma)\theta(t)^{-\alpha\over(1-\gamma)(1-\alpha)}=0,\\
&\theta(T)=1.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.2)

We denote the optimal investment-consumption strategy associated with
\rho=\rho_{i}
by
(\pi^{*}_{i},c^{*}_{i})
.
It is well-known that for the one-agent problem, we always have
c^{*}_{i}\leqslant c^{*}_{j}
if
\rho_{i}\leqslant\rho_{j}
.
But for the multi-agent problem, if
\gamma\in(0,1-\alpha)
, we can have
\bar{c}_{i}\geqslant\bar{c}_{j}
for
\rho_{i}\leqslant\rho_{j}
.
This is a new feature of our model.

5.3
Comparison with CRRA model

In this subsection, we shall compare the equilibrium strategy derived in our model with that
derived in the CRRA utility case.

Pre-committed strategy with CRRA utility.

When
\gamma=1-\alpha
, the recursive utility reduces to a CRRA utility.
Recall from (
3.3
) that at time
t
, the pre-committed optimal consumption strategy
c^{t,*}_{i}
of agent
i
reads:

\displaystyle c_{i}^{t,*}(s)=\bigg({N\theta^{t}(s)\over e^{-\alpha\rho_{i}(s-t)}}\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1},\qquad s\in[t,T],

(5.3)

which depends on
i
. Since this strategy is time-inconsistent, it is not flexible.

Equilibrium strategy with CRRA utility.

Taking
\gamma=1-\alpha
in (
4.10
), we see that the equilibrium consumption strategy of agent
i
with CRRA utility is given by:

\bar{c}_{i}(t)=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1},\qquad t\in[0,T].

(5.4)

It is time-consistent, but it is surprising that
\bar{c}_{i}
does not depend on
i
.
In other words, the agents take the same consumption strategy as each other,
which is unreasonable from a economic view of point.

Equilibrium strategy with recursive utility.

The equilibrium consumption strategy of agent
i
in our model is given by

\bar{c}_{i}(t)=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)},\qquad t\in[0,T],\qquad i=1,...,N,

(5.5)

which is time-consistent and also depends on
i
.
Thus, the equilibrium consumption strategy in our model appears more reasonable than that associated with the CRRA utility.

Analysis for the above phenomena

From (
4.2
) and (
4.2
), with the ansatz (
4.8
), we see that the
equilibrium investment-consumption strategy
(\bar{\pi},\,\bar{c})
is determined by maximizing the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}(t,\theta(t),v(t),\pi,c)

\displaystyle=v(t)\bigg[\nu+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\bigg]-{1\over 2}\gamma v(t)\bigg(\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{g}_{i}(c_{i},\theta_{i}(t)),

(5.6)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{i}(c_{i},\theta_{i}(t))=\alpha^{-1}\theta_{i}(t)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big(c_{i}^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}\theta_{i}(t)^{{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\Big),

(5.7)

and

v={1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}.

(5.8)

Note that in the recursive utility case, the marginal benefit of agent
i
’s consumption
c_{i}
at time
t
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{c_{i}}\Big[{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{g}_{i}(c_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t))\Big]={\theta_{i}(t)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}c_{i}(t)^{\alpha-1}\over N},

(5.9)

which depends not only on agent
i
’s current consumption
c_{i}(t)
but also (through the value function
\theta_{i}(t)
) on the trajectory of
coalition’s future consumptions
\{c_{k}\}_{k=1,...,N}
.
It leads to that
\partial_{c_{i}}{\bm{H}}(t,\theta(t),v(t),\pi,c)
, given by

\displaystyle\partial_{c_{i}}{\bm{H}}(t,\theta(t),v(t),\pi,c)

\displaystyle=-v(t)+\partial_{c_{i}}\Big[{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{g}_{i}(c_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t))\Big]

\displaystyle=-v(t)+{\theta_{i}(t)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}c_{i}^{\alpha-1}\over N},

(5.10)

depends on both coalition’s value function
v
and agent
i
’s value function
\theta_{i}
.
Thus, the corresponding equilibrium consumption strategy
\bar{c}_{i}
of agent
i
depends on the parameter
i
.

In the CRRA utility case, the aggregator becomes an additively separable one:

\tilde{g}_{i}(c_{i},\theta_{i}(t))=\alpha^{-1}\Big(c_{i}^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}\theta_{i}(t)\Big),

(5.11)

and then the marginal benefit of agent
i
’s consumption
c_{i}
at time
t
reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{c_{i}}\Big[{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{g}_{i}(c_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t))\Big]=\partial_{c_{i}}\Big[{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha^{-1}\big(c_{i}^{\alpha}-\rho_{i}\theta_{i}(t)\big)\Big]={c_{i}(t)^{\alpha-1}\over N},

(5.12)

which only depends on agent
i
’s current consumption
c_{i}(t)
. Then,

\displaystyle\partial_{c_{i}}{\bm{H}}(t,\theta(t),v(t),\pi,c)

\displaystyle=-v(t)+{c_{i}^{\alpha-1}\over N}.

(5.13)

Thus, in this case the equilibrium consumption strategy
\bar{c}_{i}
of agent
i
is given by coalition’s value function
v
,
which is independent of the parameter
i
.

The more essential reason is that the time-inconsistent problem is an optimization problem
on the local time horizon
[t,t+\varepsilon]
. For the model with CRRA utility, the utility
is affected by the discount factor
\rho_{i}
only through the discounting function
e^{-\rho_{i}(s-t)}
. When
\varepsilon>0
is small enough,

e^{-\rho_{i}(s-t)}\approx 1
over
[t,t+\varepsilon]
for each
i=1,2,...,N
. Then the agents face almost the same
problem as each other over
[t,t+\varepsilon]
.
However, for the model with recursive utility, the discount factor
\rho_{i}
affects the utility
not only by the discounting function
e^{-\rho_{i}(s-t)}
but also by the value function
\theta_{i}
.
Thus, the agents’ problems can be still different from each other, even if
\varepsilon>0
is very small.

Appendix A: Proof of
2.1

If (
2.6
) admits a unique positive solution,
then (
2.7
) can be obtained by applying the Itô formula directly.
The uniqueness of positive solutions to (
2.6
) can be obtained by a standard method.
Thus, we only need to prove that (
2.6
) really admits a positive solution.
This suffices to show that if
\theta_{i},i=1,...,N
is a positive solution of (
2.6
)
on
[t_{0},T]
, then

{\delta}\leqslant\theta_{i}(s)\leqslant\kappa,\quad s\in[t_{0},T];\quad i=1,...,N,

for some positive constants
\delta,\kappa>0
independent of
t_{0}
.
Let
\bar{\theta}_{i}
be the unique solution to the following linear ODE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\bar{\theta}}_{i}(s)+(1-\gamma)\bar{\theta}_{i}(s)\bigg[(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(s)+\nu-{\gamma\sigma^{2}\over 2}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)\bigg)^{2}-{\rho_{i}\over\alpha}\bigg]=0,\\
&\bar{\theta}_{i}(T)=1.\end{aligned}\right.

Clearly,
\bar{\theta}_{i}>0
. By the comparison theorem of ODEs, we have

\theta_{i}(s)\geqslant\delta:=\inf_{r\in[0,T]}\bar{\theta}_{i}(r)>0,\qquad s\in[t_{0},T].

(6.1)

Now we establish the upper bound estimate of
\theta_{i}
.
If
\gamma\in(0,1-\alpha]
, then

\tilde{\theta}_{i}
be the unique positive solution to the following linear ODE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{i}(s)+(1-\gamma)\tilde{\theta}_{i}(s)\bigg[(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(s)+\nu-{\gamma\sigma^{2}\over 2}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)\bigg)^{2}-{\rho_{i}\over\alpha}\bigg]\\

\theta_{i}(s)\leqslant\kappa:=\sup_{r\in[0,T]}\tilde{\theta}_{i}(r),\qquad s\in[t_{0},T].

(6.2)

\theta_{i}(s)\leqslant\kappa:=\sup_{r\in[0,T]}\widehat{\theta}_{i}(r),\qquad s\in[t_{0},T].

(6.3)

&\quad+\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(s)X^{\varepsilon}(s)dW(s),\qquad s\in[t,t+\varepsilon),\\
X^{\varepsilon}(t)&=\bar{X}(t),\end{aligned}\right.

and

\displaystyle Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{T}g_{i}(\bar{c}_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r),Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(r))dr+h_{i}(X^{\varepsilon}(T))\bigg],\qquad s\in[t+\varepsilon,T];

\displaystyle Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{t+\varepsilon}g_{i}(c_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r),Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(r))dr+Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t+\varepsilon)\bigg],\qquad s\in[t,t+\varepsilon],

(6.4)

respectively.
By applying the Itô formula to the mapping

on

,
we get

).
Substituting the above into (
6.4
) yields that

\displaystyle Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{t+\varepsilon}g_{i}(c_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r),Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(r))dr+{\theta_{i}(t+\varepsilon)X^{\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}\bigg],\qquad s\in[t,t+\varepsilon].

Next, by applying the Itô formula to the mapping
s\mapsto{\theta_{i}(s)X^{\varepsilon}(s)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}
on
[t,t+\varepsilon]
, we have

\displaystyle Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{t+\varepsilon}g_{i}(c_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r),Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(r))dr+{\theta_{i}(t)\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\quad+{\theta_{i}(t+\varepsilon)X^{\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}-{\theta_{i}(t)\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{t+\varepsilon}g_{i}(c_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r),Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(r))dr+{\theta_{i}(t)\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{s}^{t+\varepsilon}X^{\varepsilon}(r)^{1-\gamma}\bigg[{\dot{\theta}_{i}(r)\over 1-\gamma}+\theta_{i}(r)\Big[\nu+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(r)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad-{\gamma\over 2}\theta_{i}(r)\sigma^{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)\Big)^{2}\bigg]dr\bigg].

(6.5)

By

\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\theta}^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s)+(1-\gamma)\theta^{\varepsilon}_{i}(s)\bigg[(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(s)+\nu-{\gamma\sigma^{2}\over 2}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(s)\bigg)^{2}-{\rho_{i}\over\alpha}\bigg]\\

Then

Y_{i}^{\varepsilon}(r)={\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma},\qquad r\in[t,t+\varepsilon],

which implies that

Substituting the above into (
6.5
) yields that

\displaystyle Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigg[\int_{s}^{t+\varepsilon}g_{i}(c_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r),Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(r))dr+{\theta_{i}(t)\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{s}^{t+\varepsilon}\bigg[{\dot{\theta}_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}+\theta_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r)^{-\gamma}\Big(\nu X^{\varepsilon}(r)+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r)\Big)-{\gamma\over 2}\theta_{i}(r)X^{\varepsilon}(r)^{-\gamma-1}\sigma^{2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}(r)\Big)^{2}X^{\varepsilon}(r)^{2}\bigg]dr\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[{\theta_{i}(t)\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}+\int_{t}^{t+\varepsilon}X^{\varepsilon}(r)^{1-\gamma}\bigg[{\dot{\theta}_{i}(r)\over 1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\quad+\theta_{i}(r)\Big(\nu+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(r)\Big)-{\gamma\over 2}\theta_{i}(r)\sigma^{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{-1}\Big(c_{i}(s)^{\alpha}\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}(r)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}-\rho_{i}\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}(r)\Big)\bigg]dr\bigg].

(6.6)

Then, by the standard estimates of SDEs, we have

\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\sup_{r\in[t,t+\varepsilon]}|X^{\varepsilon}(r)-\bar{X}(r)|^{2}\Big]\leqslant K\varepsilon.

Noting that
\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon)=\theta_{i}(t+\varepsilon)
and the derivatives
\dot{\theta}_{i}^{\varepsilon}
and
\dot{\theta}_{i}
are bounded,
we get

\sup_{r\in[t,t+\varepsilon]}|\theta_{i}^{\varepsilon}(r)-\theta_{i}(r)|^{2}\leqslant K\varepsilon,

where
K>0
is a constant which depends on the fixed
\pi
and
c
, but is independent of
\varepsilon
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[{\theta_{i}(t)\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over 1-\gamma}+\int_{t}^{t+\varepsilon}\bar{X}(r)^{1-\gamma}\bigg[{\dot{\theta}_{i}(r)\over 1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\quad+\theta_{i}(r)\Big(\nu+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(r)\Big)-{\gamma\over 2}\theta_{i}(r)\sigma^{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{-1}\Big(c_{i}(s)^{\alpha}\theta_{i}(r)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}-\rho_{i}\theta_{i}(r)\Big)\bigg]dr\bigg]+o(\varepsilon).

It follows that

\displaystyle J^{\lambda}(t,\bar{X}(t);\bar{\pi}^{\varepsilon},\bar{c}^{\varepsilon})={1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y^{\varepsilon}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[{\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over N(1-\gamma)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)+\int_{t}^{t+\varepsilon}\bar{X}(r)^{1-\gamma}{\cal H}^{\lambda}(r;\pi,c)dr\bigg]+o(\varepsilon),

where

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{\lambda}(r;\pi,c)

\displaystyle={\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dot{\theta}_{i}(r)\over N(1-\gamma)}+{1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(r)\bigg[\nu+(\mu-\nu)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(r)

\displaystyle\quad-{\gamma\sigma^{2}\over 2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}(r)\Big)^{2}\bigg]+{1\over N\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(c_{i}(s)^{\alpha}\theta_{i}(r)^{1-{\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}-\rho_{i}\theta_{i}(r)\Big).

Note that

\displaystyle J^{\lambda}(t,\bar{X}(t);\bar{\pi},\bar{c})=\mathbb{E}_{t}\bigg[{\bar{X}(t)^{1-\gamma}\over N(1-\gamma)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)+\int_{t}^{t+\varepsilon}\bar{X}(r)^{1-\gamma}{\cal H}^{\lambda}(r;\bar{\pi},\bar{c})dr\bigg],

and

{\cal H}^{\lambda}(r;\bar{\pi},\bar{c})=\max_{(\pi,c)}{\cal H}^{\lambda}(r;\pi,c)=0.

Then it is easilly checked that (
4.1
) holds, which completes the proof.

Appendix C: Proof of
Theorem 4.5

Similar to the proof of
2.1
, it suffices to show that if
\theta_{i},i=1,...,N
is a postive solution of (
4.11
)
on
[t_{0},T]
, then

{\delta}\leqslant\theta_{i}(s)\leqslant\kappa,\quad s\in[t_{0},T];\quad i=1,...,N,

for some positive constants
\delta,\kappa>0
independent of
t_{0}
.

Step 1. Monotonicity of
\theta_{i}
in
\rho_{i}
.

Without loss of generality, we let
\rho_{1}\geqslant\rho_{i}
for
i=2,...,N
.
Denote
\Delta\theta_{i}=\theta_{1}-\theta_{i}
.
Then

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta\dot{\theta}_{i}(t)+(1-\gamma)\Delta\theta_{i}(t)\bigg[\nu+{(\mu-\nu)^{2}\over 2\gamma\sigma^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg({\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\over\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\bigg]\\
&\qquad-(1-\gamma)\rho_{1}\alpha^{-1}\Delta\theta_{i}(t)+(1-\gamma)(\rho_{i}-\rho_{1})\alpha^{-1}\theta_{i}(t)\\
&\qquad+{1-\gamma-\alpha\over\alpha(1-\alpha)}\bigg({1\over N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{\alpha\over\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{1}\big[l\theta_{1}(t)+(1-l)\theta_{i}(t)\big]^{-\alpha\gamma\over(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)}dl\Delta\theta_{i}(t)=0,\\
&\Delta\theta_{i}(T)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Clearly, the above is a linear ODE with the unknown
\Delta\theta_{i}
and the nonhomogeneous term

(1-\gamma)(\rho_{i}-\rho_{1})\alpha^{-1}\theta_{i}
. Using the fact

(1-\gamma)(\rho_{i}-\rho_{1})\alpha^{-1}\theta_{i}(t)\leqslant 0,\qquad t\in[t_{0},T],

we have
\Delta\theta_{i}\leqslant 0
, which implies that

\theta_{1}\leqslant\theta_{i},\qquad i=2,...,N.

(6.7)

Step 2. Low bound estimate of
\theta_{i}
.

We now show that

\theta_{i}\geqslant\delta;\qquad i=2,...,N,

for some
\delta>0
.
From (
6.7
), we only need to prove
\theta_{1}\geqslant\delta
.

We first prove the result under the assumption (
4.12
).
Denote

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle=-(1-\gamma)\theta_{1}(t)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg({\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\over\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{-1}(1-\gamma)\theta_{1}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}\bigg({\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\over\theta_{1}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\bigg)^{\alpha\over\alpha-1}.

Note that

\displaystyle F(T)=(1-\gamma)(\alpha^{-1}-1)>0,

and

\theta_{1}(t)=1+\int_{t}^{T}\bigg\{(1-\gamma)\Big[\nu+{(\mu-\nu)^{2}\over 2\gamma\sigma^{2}}-\rho_{1}\alpha^{-1}\Big]\theta_{1}(s)+F(s)\bigg\}ds.

such that

on

(6.8)

If
\tau=t_{0}
, then the desired result is proved with
\delta=1
.
Thus, we assume that
\tau>t_{0}
. By the continuity of
\theta_{1}
, we get
\theta_{1}(\tau)=1

Note that

\theta_{i}(t)\geqslant\theta_{1}(t)\geqslant 1,\qquad t\in[\tau,T],\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad{1-\gamma-\alpha\over(1-\gamma)(1-\alpha)}<1,

\displaystyle F(\tau)

\displaystyle=\bigg[-(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}\Big)^{1\over 1-\alpha}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha^{-1}(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}(t)\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}\bigg]\bigg|_{t=\tau}

We next prove the result under the assumption (

Note that

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle=-(1-\gamma)\theta_{1}(t)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg({\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\over\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle\geqslant-(1-\gamma)\theta_{1}(t)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg({\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\over\theta_{i}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}}\bigg)^{1\over\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{-1}(1-\gamma)\theta_{1}(t){\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{1}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}(t)\right)^{1\over\alpha-1}\over\theta_{1}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}\left(\theta_{1}(t)^{1-\gamma-\alpha\over 1-\gamma}\right)^{1\over\alpha-1}}
