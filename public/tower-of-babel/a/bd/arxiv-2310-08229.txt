Heights of one- and two-sided congruence lattices of semigroups Supported by the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council [EP/S020616/1, EP/V002953/1 and EP/V003224/1] and the Australian Research Council [FT190100632].


Abstract
The height of a poset P P is the supremum of the cardinalities of chains in P P . The exact formula for the height of the subgroup lattice of the symmetric group 𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n} is known, as is an accurate asymptotic formula for the height of the subsemigroup lattice of the full transformation monoid 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} . Motivated by the related question of determining the heights of the lattices of left and right congruences of 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} , and deploying the framework of unary algebras and semigroup actions, we develop a general method for computing the heights of lattices of both one- and two-sided congruences for semigroups. We apply this theory to obtain exact height formulae for several monoids of transformations, matrices and partitions, including: the full transformation monoid 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} , the partial transformation monoid 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 n \mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{n} , the symmetric inverse monoid ℐ n \mathcal{I}_{n} , the monoid of order-preserving transformations 𝒪 n \mathcal{O}_{n} , the full matrix monoid ℳ ⁡ ( n , q ) \mathcal{M}(n,q) , the partition monoid 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} , the Brauer monoid ℬ n \mathcal{B}_{n} and the Temperley–Lieb monoid 𝒯 ​ ℒ n \mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n} . Keywords : Semigroup, semigroup action, unary algebra, (left/right) congruence, congruence lattice, height, modular element, transformation and diagram monoids, Schützenberger group. Introduction

The
height
of a poset
P
, denoted
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(P)
, is the supremum of the cardinalities of chains in
P
.
In
[
9
]
the height of the subgroup lattice
\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is computed:

Theorem 1.1

(Cameron, Solomon and Turull
[
9
, Theorem 1]
)
.

For
n\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{n}))=\lceil 3n/2\rceil-b(n),

where
b(n)
is the number of
1
s in the binary expansion of
n
.

The formula given in
[
9
]
in fact has an additional term
-1
. This discrepancy is caused by the (slightly) different definition of height (which they call length): while in
[
9
]
it is taken to mean the number of covering relationships in a longest chain (at least in the finite case), for us it is the number of elements.

The following
result concerning
the subsemigroup lattice of the full transformation monoid
\mathcal{T}_{n}
was obtained in
[
8
]
:

Theorem 1.2

(Cameron, Gadoleau, Mitchell, Peresse
[
8
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

For
n\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\geq e^{-2}n^{n}-2e^{-2}(1-e^{-1})n^{n-1/3}-o(n^{n-1/3}).

Theorem
1.1
can be viewed from a different angle, by recalling that the subgroups of a group
G
are in one-one correspondence with transitive permutation representations of
G
, or, equivalently, with left (or right) congruences of
G
. The notion of left congruences of a semigroup
S
will be introduced explicitly in Section
5
, but for now let us just recall that they
form a lattice, which we denote by
\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S)
.

So, motivated by the two results above one may ask: what is
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
?
Towards the end of this paper we will prove the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

For
n\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{r})).

Whereas the left and right congruence lattices of a group
are always isomorphic,
the same is in general not true for semigroups.
For example,
\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{T}_{3})
and
\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{T}_{3})
have size
120
and
287
, respectively.
Thus, one can equally ask about the height of the lattice
\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, where we obtain the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

For
n\geq 2
, we have

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=1+\sum_{r=1}^{n}S(n,r)\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{r})),

where
S(n,r)
is the Stirling number of the second kind.

Comparing Theorems
1.1
–
1.4
we observe that:

•

the height of the subgroup lattice (and hence of the left and right congruence lattices) of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
grows linearly with
n
, i.e. its degree;

•

the height of the subsemigroup lattice of the full transformation monoid
\mathcal{T}_{n}
grows linearly with
n^{n}
, i.e. its order;

•

the height of
\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
grows linearly with
n2^{n}
;
this follows from the combinatorial identity

\sum_{r=1}^{n}r\binom{n}{r}=n2^{n-1}
;

•

the height of
\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
grows linearly with

\sum_{r=1}^{n}rS(n,r)={B(n+1)-B(n)}\sim B(n+1)
, where
B(n)
is the
n
th Bell number; see OEIS,
[
1
, A005493]
.

We remark in passing that, for a finite or countably infinite semigroup
S
, the heights of
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(S)
,
\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S)
and

\operatorname{\sf RCong}(S)
can be as large as
|S|
or
2^{\aleph_{0}}
, respectively.
This can be seen for instance by considering null semigroups, for which all equivalence relations are congruences.

The current paper is devoted to building a theoretical framework within which we will be able to prove results like Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
Here is a brief summary.
We begin our development in Sections
2
and
3
by introducing some elements of general (universal) algebra concerning quotients, principal factors and Rees subalgebras.
We also recall the notion of a modular element in a lattice, which is the key property that enables inductive proofs of our main formulae; see Proposition
3.2
.
Then we specialise to unary algebras in Section
4
, where we prove a formula expressing the height of the congruence lattice of a unary algebra
A
in terms of heights of quotients corresponding to strongly connected components of
A
(Theorem
4.4
).
All the subsequent results in the paper have this result as their foundation.
We then move on to consider our main topic in Sections
5
–
10
, the heights of the one- and two-sided congruence lattices of a semigroup.
The fact that left congruences are intimately related with left
S
-acts (transformation representations), and that the latter are a special case of unary algebras, provides the necessary link with the preceding development,
and enables us to
express the height of

\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S)
in terms of heights of left
S
-acts corresponding to the
\mathscr{L}
-classes of
S
(Theorems
6.1
and
6.3
).
We then analyse such acts, and bring the associated Schützenberger groups into play, in Propositions
7.2
and
7.4
.
Putting these components together, we obtain a general formula for the height of
\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S)
, under certain additional assumptions, in Theorem
7.11
and Corollary
7.12
, of which Theorem
1.3
is a special instance.
We then note that all the foregoing considerations hold for the lattice of right congruences of
S
, via an obvious left-right duality, and state the main results in Section
8
.
The lattice of two-sided congruences is treated in Section
9
by considering
S
as an
(S,S)
-biact, yet another special case of unary algebras.
With the theoretical development now complete, the paper then culminates in Section
10
, where we give explicit formulae for the heights of the lattices of left, right and two-sided congruences for many of the most important and well-studied families of monoids in the literature.
In addition to the full transformation monoid
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, these include the partial transformation monoid
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}_{n}
, the symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, the monoid of order-preserving transformations
\mathcal{O}_{n}
, the full linear monoid
\mathcal{M}(n,q)
, the partition monoid
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the Brauer monoid
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and the Temperley–Lieb monoid
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
.

, and some calculated values in Table

Algebras: congruences,
\mathscr{G}
-classes and principal factors

In this section we introduce the basic concepts and notation for
universal
algebras; for a more systematic introduction see
[
7
]
.

A
signature
(or
type
) of algebras is an indexed family
F=(f_{i})_{i\in I}
of symbols, and associated integers
\ar(f_{i})\geq 0
called
arities
.
An
algebra
of type
F
is a pair
\mathcal{A}=(A,F^{\mathcal{A}})
, where
A
is a set, and
F^{\mathcal{A}}=(f_{i}^{\mathcal{A}})_{i\in I}
is an indexed collection
of operations
f_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}:A^{r_{i}}\rightarrow A
where
r_{i}=\ar(f_{i})
;
these are called the
basic
operations of

\mathcal{A}
.
When there is no danger of confusion we omit superscripts
\mathcal{A}
, thus identifying operation symbols
with the
operations they represent, and we also treat
F
and
F^{\mathcal{A}}
as sets.
Furthermore, we often identify the algebra
\mathcal{A}
with its underlying set
A
.
Note that in principle we allow an algebra to be empty, but this cannot be the case for an algebra whose signature contains constant (nullary) operations.

A
subalgebra
of an algebra
\mathcal{A}
is any algebra
\mathcal{B}=(B,F^{\mathcal{B}})
where
B\subseteq A
and

f_{i}^{\mathcal{B}}=f_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}\mathord{\restriction}_{B}
for all
i\in I
.
We write
\mathcal{B}\leq\mathcal{A}
to indicate that
\mathcal{B}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
. The collection of all subalgebras of
\mathcal{A}
forms a lattice under inclusion. This lattice is denoted by
\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{A})
.

A
congruence
of an algebra

(A,F)
is an equivalence relation
\sigma
on
A
that is
compatible
with each operation
f
from
F
, in the sense that:

(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{n},b_{n})\in\sigma\ \Rightarrow\ (f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),f(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}))\in\sigma.

The set
\operatorname{\sf Cong}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A})
of all congruences of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A}
is a lattice under inclusion, called the
congruence lattice
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A}
. The bottom and top elements of
\operatorname{\sf Cong}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}A})
are the
trivial (a.k.a. diagonal)
and
universal
relations:

\Delta_{A}=\{(a,a):a\in A\}\and\nabla_{A}=A\times A,

respectively. The
quotient

A/\sigma
of
A
by
\sigma
is the set
\{[a]:a\in A\}
of
\sigma
-classes with the induced operations

f^{A/\sigma}([a_{1}],\dots,[a_{n}]):=[f^{A}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})]
.

Consider an algebra
A
. For
a\in A
, we write
\langle a\rangle
for the subalgebra of
A
generated by
a
;
it consists of all elements of the form
f(a,\dots,a)
, where
f
is a
term operation
, i.e. an operation obtained by composing the basic operations.
In particular,
\langle a\rangle
contains all constants of
A
, i.e. the images of all nullary operations. We define a preorder
{\operatorname{\leq_{\mathscr{G}}}}={\leq_{\mathscr{G}^{A}}}
on
A
, which then yields an equivalence relation
\mathscr{G}=\mathscr{G}^{A}
on
A
and a partial order

\leq
on the
set
A/\mathscr{G}=\{G_{a}:a\in A\}
of
\mathscr{G}
-classes, as follows:

a\operatorname{\leq_{\mathscr{G}}}b\ \Leftrightarrow\ \langle a\rangle\subseteq\langle b\rangle,\hskip 10.00002pta\mathrel{\mathscr{G}}b\ \Leftrightarrow\ \langle a\rangle=\langle b\rangle,\hskip 10.00002ptG_{a}\leq G_{b}\ \Leftrightarrow\ a\operatorname{\leq_{\mathscr{G}}}b\hskip 20.00003pt{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{for $a,b\in A$.}}

(2.1)

The well-known Green’s relations
\mathscr{R}
,
\mathscr{L}
,
\mathscr{J}

and the associated pre-orders
on a semigroup will later be seen as special instances of these relations, which explains the choice of notation here.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
A
is an algebra, and if
B\leq A
is a subalgebra, then

{\leq_{\mathscr{G}^{B}}}={\leq_{\mathscr{G}^{A}}}\mathord{\restriction}_{B}\and\mathscr{G}^{B}=\mathscr{G}^{A}\mathord{\restriction}_{B}.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that the subalgebra of
B
generated by
b\in B
is the same as the subalgebra of
A
generated by
b
.
∎

Consider an algebra
A
and an arbitrary subset
X\subseteq A
.
Let
0
be a symbol not belonging to
A
, and let

\overline{X}=X\cup\{0\}.

We can turn
\overline{X}
into an algebra of the same signature as
A
in the following way, by setting, for any
n
-ary basic operation symbol
f
, and for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\overline{X}
:

\overline{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\begin{cases}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})&\text{if $x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We call
\overline{X}
the
principal factor

associated to

X
.
We note that
\overline{X}
need not be an algebra of the same ‘kind’ as
A
; e.g. if
A
is a group and
X\neq\varnothing
, then
\overline{X}
is never a group.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G})
, where
G
is a
\mathscr{G}
-class of an algebra
A

with no

constants
. If
a\mathrel{\sigma}0
for some
a\in G
, then
\sigma=\nabla_{\overline{G}}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
b\mathrel{\sigma}0
for all
b\in G
, so fix some such
b
. Since
a\mathrel{\mathscr{G}}b
, we have
b\in\langle a\rangle
,
and so
b=f(a,\ldots,a)
for some term function
f
of
A

of arity
>0
because
A
has no constants.
Using

a,b\in G
, we have

b=f(a,\ldots,a)=\overline{f}(a,\ldots,a)\mathrel{\sigma}\overline{f}(0,\ldots,0)=0.\qed

Lemma
2.3
need not be true if
A
has constant/nullary operations. For example, consider the case in which
A=\{a,b\}
, with
a
and
b
both constants, and no other operations. Since
A=\langle a\rangle=\langle b\rangle
, it follows that
A
is itself a
\mathscr{G}
-class. But the equivalence
\sigma
on
\overline{A}=A\cup\{0\}
with
\sigma
-classes
\{a,0\}
and
\{b\}
is a non-universal congruence, yet
a\mathrel{\sigma}0
.

In the following statement, for a poset
P
, we denote by
P^{\top}=P\cup\{\top\}
the poset obtained by adjoining a new top element
\top
to
P
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If a
\mathscr{G}
-class
G
of an algebra
A

with no constants
is also a subalgebra of
A
, then

\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G})\cong\operatorname{\sf Cong}(G)^{\top}\and\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G}))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(G))+1.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the first claim. For
\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(G)
define
\overline{\sigma}:=\sigma\cup\{(0,0)\}
.
We prove the result by showing that

\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G})=\{\overline{\sigma}:\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(G)\}\cup\{\nabla_{\overline{G}}\}.

(
\supseteq
).
For
\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(G)
, clearly
\overline{\sigma}
is an equivalence on
\overline{G}
. To check compatibility, suppose
f:A^{n}\to A
is an operation of
A
, and suppose
(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{n},b_{n})\in\overline{\sigma}
.
From the definition of
\overline{\sigma}
we have
a_{i}=0\ \Leftrightarrow\ b_{i}=0
(
i=1,\dots,n
).
If
a_{i}=0
for some
i
then

(\overline{f}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),\overline{f}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}))=(0,0)\in\overline{\sigma}.

Otherwise,
a_{i},b_{i}\not=0
for all
i
, in which case

(\overline{f}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),\overline{f}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}))=(f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),f(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}))\in\sigma\subseteq\overline{\sigma}.

(
\subseteq
).
By Lemma
2.3
, for
\nabla_{\overline{G}}\neq\tau\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G})
we have
\tau=\tau\mathord{\restriction}_{G}\cup\{(0,0)\}
.
But
G\leq\overline{G}
(as
G\leq A
by assumption), and so
\tau\mathord{\restriction}_{G}\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(G)
, which proves the inclusion, and
hence the lemma.
∎

3
Rees subalgebras, ideals and quotients

In this section we develop a general framework within which we can express the height of the congruence lattice of an algebra as a sum involving a special kind of subalgebra and an associated quotient; see Proposition
3.7
.

We denote the
down-set (a.k.a. principal ideal) and up-set (a.k.a. principal filter)
of an element
x
of a lattice
L
by

x^{\downarrow}=\{a\in L:a\leq x\}\and x^{\uparrow}=\{a\in L:a\geq x\},

respectively. For any chains
C_{1}\subseteq x^{\downarrow}
and
C_{2}\subseteq x^{\uparrow}
, their union
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
is a chain in
L
, with
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|\leq 1
. It quickly follows that

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(L)\geq\operatorname{\sf Ht}(x^{\downarrow})+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(x^{\uparrow})-1\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $x\in L$.}

(3.1)

The next result establishes a sufficient condition for this to become an equality.
Following Stanley
[
35
]
, we say that an element
x
in a lattice
L
is
modular

if it forms a modular pair (see
[
20
, Section V.2.5]
) with every other element of
L
, i.e. if

a\vee(x\wedge b)=(a\vee x)\wedge b\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a,b\in L$ with $a\leq b$.}

In practice, to show that
x
is modular, one only needs to check that
a\vee(x\wedge b)\geq(a\vee x)\wedge b
, as the reverse inequality always holds (when
a\leq b
).

Proposition 3.2
.

If
x
is a modular element in a lattice
L
,
then every chain in
L
embeds into the direct product
x^{\downarrow}\times x^{\uparrow}
, and consequently

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(L)=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(x^{\downarrow})+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(x^{\uparrow})-1
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a chain in
L
, and define a mapping

f:C\rightarrow x^{\downarrow}\times x^{\uparrow};\ c\mapsto(c\wedge x,c\vee x).

We need to show that
a\leq b\ \Leftrightarrow\ f(a)\leq f(b)
for all
a,b\in C
.
The direct implication is clear.
For the converse,
suppose
a,b\in C
satisfy
f(a)\leq f(b)
, i.e.
a\wedge x\leq b\wedge x
and
a\vee x\leq b\vee x
.
Since
C
is a chain, we have
a\leq b
or
b\leq a
. In the former case we are done.
If
b\leq a
, then
using modularity of
x
, we have

a=(a\vee x)\wedge a\leq(b\vee x)\wedge a=b\vee(x\wedge a)\leq b\vee(x\wedge b)=b,

as required.

Having established the first claim, it follows that
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(L)\leq\operatorname{\sf Ht}(x^{\downarrow}\times x^{\uparrow})=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(x^{\downarrow})+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(x^{\uparrow})-1
.
Combined with (
3.1
), this completes the proof.
∎

Proposition
3.2
provides a first tool for calculating the height of a congruence lattice
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A)
; one hopes to identify a congruence
\sigma
of
A
that is modular in the congruence lattice, and then
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A))
can be expressed in terms of the heights of
\sigma^{\downarrow}=[\Delta_{A},\sigma]
and
\sigma^{\uparrow}=[\sigma,\nabla_{A}]
.
In many classical varieties of algebras, such as groups and rings,
every congruence is modular in the congruence lattice
, e.g. see
[
7
, Exercises I.3.5 and I.3.6]
.
This is not true for algebras in general, or for unary algebras or semigroups in particular.

We now identify a special kind of subalgebra associated with a special kind of congruence; the congruences arising turn out to always be modular, and
will prove very useful in what follows.

Specifically, if
B\leq A
is such that the relation
\rho_{B}:=\nabla_{B}\cup\Delta_{A}
is a congruence on
A
, we say that

B
is a
Rees subalgebra
, and
\rho_{B}
a
Rees congruence
; the
Rees quotient
associated with
B
is
A/B:=A/\rho_{B}
.
These concepts were introduced in
[
38
]
. The definition there was couched in different (but equivalent) terms, and our defining property was established as
[
38
, Proposition 1.2]
.

There is a link between Rees quotients and principal factors from the previous section, in the special case of ideals.
Specifically,
B\leq A
is an
ideal
if for each
n
-ary operation
f
and all
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
, at least one of which belongs to
B
, we have

f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in B
.
We remark in passing that this definition generalises the notion of ideals in semigroups, but not in rings.
Immediate from the definition is the following:

Lemma 3.3
.

Every ideal
B\leq A
is a Rees subalgebra, and

A/B\cong\begin{cases}A&\text{if }B=\varnothing\\
\overline{A\!\setminus\!B}&\text{if }B\neq\varnothing.\end{cases}

The converse of Lemma
3.3
is not true. For example, any one-element subalgebra is a Rees subalgebra (whose associated Rees congruence is the trivial relation), but need not be an ideal. Concretely,
\{1\}
is a Rees subalgebra of a group, but is only an ideal if the group is trivial.

Proposition 3.4
.

If
A
is an algebra, and if
B\leq A
is a Rees subalgebra, then the Rees congruence
\rho_{B}
is modular in the lattice
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A)
, and hence

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}([\Delta_{A},\rho_{B}])+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A/B))-1.

Proof.

The second assertion follows from the first and Proposition
3.2
, upon noticing that
[\rho_{B},\nabla_{A}]\cong\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A/B)

by the Correspondence Theorem (see
[
7
, Theorem 6.20]
for instance).

To prove the first assertion, suppose
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A)
satisfy
\sigma_{1}\subseteq\sigma_{2}
.

As noted just before Proposition
3.2
, we just need to show that
(\sigma_{1}\vee\rho_{B})\cap\sigma_{2}\subseteq\sigma_{1}\vee(\rho_{B}\cap\sigma_{2})
.
To do so, suppose
(a,b)\in(\sigma_{1}\vee\rho_{B})\cap\sigma_{2}
.

Then
(a,b)\in\sigma_{2}
, and there exist
k\geq 1
and
c_{1},d_{1},\dots,c_{k},d_{k}\in A
such that

a\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}c_{1}\mathrel{\rho}_{B}d_{1}\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}c_{2}\mathrel{\rho}_{B}d_{2}\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}\dots\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}c_{k}\mathrel{\rho}_{B}d_{k}\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}b.

Assume that
k(\geq 1)
is as small as possible with respect to the existence of such a sequence.
We claim that
k=1
. To prove this, suppose instead that
k\geq 2
. Minimality of
k
implies that
c_{i}\neq d_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k
.
Hence, by the definition of
\rho_{B}
, we must have
c_{i},d_{i}\in B
for all
i
.
But then
a\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}c_{1}\mathrel{\rho}_{B}d_{k}\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}b
, which is a sequence of the same form and of length
1
.
This contradiction confirms that
k=1
. So now

a\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}c_{1}\mathrel{\rho}_{B}d_{1}\mathrel{\sigma}_{1}b.

(3.5)

But since
\sigma_{1}\subseteq\sigma_{2}
, it follows that

c_{1}\mathrel{\sigma}_{2}a\mathrel{\sigma}_{2}b\mathrel{\sigma}_{2}d_{1}
, and hence
(c_{1},d_{1})\in\rho_{B}\cap\sigma_{2}
.
The sequence (
3.5
) now implies
(a,b)\in\sigma_{1}\vee(\rho_{B}\cap\sigma_{2})
, completing the proof.
∎

In what follows we establish a condition under which the term

\operatorname{\sf Ht}([\Delta_{A},\rho_{B}])
in Proposition
3.4
can be replaced by
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B))
.
The next result shows that
\operatorname{\sf Ht}([\Delta_{A},\rho_{B}])\leq\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B))
always holds.

Lemma 3.6
.

For a Rees subalgebra
B\leq A
,
the mapping

[\Delta_{A},\rho_{B}]\rightarrow\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B);\ \sigma\mapsto\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{B}

is an embedding of lattices.

Proof.

For any
\sigma\in[\Delta_{A},\rho_{B}]
we have
\sigma=\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{B}\cup\Delta_{A\setminus B}
.
Hence, for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in[\Delta_{A},\rho_{B}]
, we have

\sigma_{1}\subseteq\sigma_{2}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \sigma_{1}\mathord{\restriction}_{B}\subseteq\sigma_{2}\mathord{\restriction}_{B}
,
and the lemma follows.
∎

As usual, we say a subalgebra
B\leq A
has the
congruence extension property
,
or that
B
is a
CEP subalgebra
, if
for every
\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B)
there exists
\sigma^{\prime}\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A)
extending it, i.e.
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\mathord{\restriction}_{B}
.
We say that
B
has the
diagonal congruence extension property
,
or that
B
is a
\Delta
-CEP subalgebra
, if
for every
\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B)
we have
\sigma\cup\Delta_{A}\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A)
.
It is easy to show that
B
is a
\Delta
-CEP subalgebra if and only if it is
both a CEP and Rees subalgebra
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
A
be an algebra.

(i)

If
B
is a
\Delta
-CEP subalgebra of
A
, then
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B)\cong[\Delta_{A},\rho_{B}]
, and hence

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B))+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A/B))-1.

(ii)

If
B_{0}\leq B_{1}\leq\dots\leq B_{k}=A
is
an ascending
chain of
\Delta
-CEP subalgebras of
A
, then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B_{0}))+\sum_{r=1}^{k}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B_{r}/B_{r-1}))-k.

Proof.

(i)
.
Given Proposition
3.4
and Lemma
3.6
, it suffices to show
that the mapping given in the latter is surjective.
This in turn follows from the fact that
(\sigma\cup\Delta_{A})\mathord{\restriction}_{B}=\sigma
for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B)
.

(ii)
.
This follows by induction on
k
.
The case
k=0
is vacuous.
The inductive step relies on part
(i)
and the observation that
B_{0}\leq\dots\leq B_{k-1}
is a chain of
\Delta
-CEP subalgebras of
B_{k-1}
.
∎

Analogous statements to Proposition
3.7
(i)
were given in
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
for subgroup lattices of groups,
and in
[
8
, Proposition 3.1]
for subsemigroup lattices of semigroups:

•

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(G))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(N))+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(G/N))-1
for any normal subgroup
N
of a group
G
.

•

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(S))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(I))+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(S/I))-1
for any ideal
I
of a semigroup
S
.

We mention in passing that using our terminology, the above statements can be proved by showing that
N
is modular in
\operatorname{\sf Sub}(G)
, and
I
is modular in
\operatorname{\sf Sub}(S)
.
The former is well known and easy to prove. The latter is equally easy to see.
Indeed, if
I
is an ideal of a semigroup
S
and if
U\leq V\leq S
are subsemigroups, then
U\vee I=U\cup I

(the join is in
\operatorname{\sf Sub}(S)
)
, so that

(U\vee I)\cap V=(U\cup I)\cap V=(U\cap V)\cup(I\cap V)=U\cup(I\cap V)=U\vee(I\cap V),

where the last equality holds because
U\cup(I\cap V)\leq S
.

4
Unary algebras: a height formula

We now put the developments from the previous two sections into the context of unary algebras, i.e. algebras in which all basic operations have arity
1
. Our main objective here is to obtain a formula that expresses the height of such an algebra in terms of the heights of the principal factors corresponding to its
\mathscr{G}
-classes, in the case that there are finitely many such classes. This will be achieved in Theorem
4.4
, which will form the basis for several subsequent results on semigroups in Sections
6
–
9
.

We begin by showing that Proposition
3.7
always applies in unary algebras.

Lemma 4.1
.

Any subalgebra of a unary algebra
A
is
both an ideal and a
\Delta
-CEP subalgebra (and hence a Rees subalgebra)
.

Proof.

For the first assertion, observe that the definition of a subalgebra and an ideal coincide in the unary case.

For the second assertion, let
B\leq A
and
\sigma\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B)
.
It is clear that
\sigma^{A}:=\sigma\cup\Delta_{A}
is an equivalence relation.
To show compatibility, let
(a,b)\in\sigma^{A}
, and let
f
be a basic (unary) operation.
If
(a,b)\in\sigma
then
(f(a),f(b))\in\sigma\subseteq\sigma^{A}
, because
\sigma
is a congruence on
B
.
Otherwise,
a=b
, hence
f(a)=f(b)
, and so
(f(a),f(b))\in\Delta_{A}\subseteq\sigma^{A}
.
∎

We now bring in the
\mathscr{G}
-relations of a unary algebra
A
.
In this case, for
a,b\in A
we have
a\leq_{\mathscr{G}}b
if and only if
a=f(b)
for some
term operation
f
of
A
.
The
\mathscr{G}
-classes of
A
are then the strongly connected components of the directed graph associated with
A
, where
a\rightarrow b
if and only if
f(a)=b
for some basic operation
f
.
The proof of our main result for this section will be by induction on the number of
\mathscr{G}
-classes. To make it work, we need some more information concerning maximal
\mathscr{G}
-classes in the
\leq
order from (
2.1
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a maximal
\mathscr{G}
-class of a unary algebra
A
, and let
B=A\!\setminus\!G
.

(i)

B
is a subalgebra of
A
.

(ii)

If
B\not=\varnothing
, then
A/B\cong\overline{G}
.

Proof.

(i)
. Let
a\in B
, and let
f
be an operation of
A
. We must show that
f(a)\in B
, i.e. that
f(a)\not\in G
. To do so, suppose to the contrary that
f(a)\in G
. Then
G=G_{f(a)}\leq G_{a}
(as
f(a)\in\langle a\rangle
), so it follows from maximality of
G
that
G=G_{a}
. But this implies
a\in G
, contradicting
a\in B=A\!\setminus\!G
.

(ii)
.
By part
(i)
and Lemma
4.1
,
B
is an ideal of
A
. The claim now follows from Lemma
3.3
.

∎

Corollary 4.3
.

If
G
is a maximal
\mathscr{G}
-class of a unary algebra
A
, and
B=A\!\setminus\!G
, then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A/B))=\begin{cases}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G}))&\text{if }G\neq A\\
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G}))-1&\text{if }G=A.\end{cases}

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemmas
4.2
(ii)
and
2.4
(and
A/\varnothing\cong A
).
∎

We are now ready for the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.4
.

If
A
is a unary algebra with finitely many
\mathscr{G}
-classes,
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
, then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A))=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G}_{r}))-k.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that for every
r\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, the class
G_{r}
is minimal in
\{G_{r},\dots,G_{k}\}
.
This means that for every
x\in G_{r}
, and every operation
f
of
A
, we have
{f(x)\in G_{1}\cup\dots\cup G_{r}}
.
It follows (keeping Lemma
2.2
in mind) that for every
r=1,\dots,k
the
set
B_{r}:=G_{1}\cup\dots\cup G_{r}
is a subalgebra of
A
with a maximal
\mathscr{G}
-class
G_{r}
.
We therefore have a chain of (
\Delta
-CEP) subalgebras
{\varnothing=B_{0}<B_{1}<\dots<B_{k}=A}
(cf. Lemma
4.1
). Combining
Proposition
3.7
(ii)
and Corollary
4.3
we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B_{0}))+\sum_{r=1}^{k}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B_{r}/B_{r-1}))-k

\displaystyle=1+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G}_{1}))-1+\sum_{r=2}^{k}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G}_{r}))-k=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\overline{G}_{r}))-k.

(Note here that the unique congruence on the empty algebra
B_{0}=\varnothing
is the empty relation, so that
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(B_{0})=\{\varnothing\}
has height
1
.)

∎

5
Preliminaries on semigroups

In this section we rapidly review the concepts and basic facts from the theory of semigroups that will be used in the remainder of the paper. For more background see
[
22
,
10
]
.

5.1
Basics: left, right and two-sided congruences

A
semigroup
is an algebra with a single associative binary operation, usually denoted by juxtaposition.
A
monoid
is a semigroup with identity. As usual, we write
S^{1}
for the
monoid completion
of a semigroup
S
; so
S^{1}=S
if
S
is a monoid, and otherwise
S^{1}=S\cup\{1\}
where
1
is a symbol not belonging to
S
, acting as an adjoined identity.

An equivalence relation
\sigma
on a semigroup
S
is said to be a
left congruence
if it is compatible with left multiplication:

(x,y)\in\sigma\ \Rightarrow\ (ax,ay)\in\sigma\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $a,x,y\in S$.}

Right congruences
are defined dually.
It is an easy fact that
\sigma
is a congruence (as defined in Section
2
) if and only if it is both a left and a right congruence.
The sets

\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S),\hskip 10.00002pt\operatorname{\sf RCong}(S)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\operatorname{\sf Cong}(S)

of all left, right and two-sided congruences are all lattices under inclusion. Each is a sublattice of the lattice

\operatorname{\sf Eq}(S)
of all equivalence relations on
S
.

The left congruences of a
group

G
are precisely the equivalence relations
whose equivalence classes are the left cosets of a subgroup, i.e. relations of the form

\lambda_{H}=\{(x,y)\in G\times G:x^{-1}y\in H\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }H\leq G.

Analogous statements hold for the right congruences, using right cosets.
The two-sided congruences of
G
are precisely the relations
\lambda_{N}
for any
normal
subgroup
N\unlhd G
. The next result quickly follows, with
\operatorname{\sf Sub}(G)
and
\operatorname{\sf NSub}(G)
denoting the lattices of subgroups and normal subgroups of
G
, respectively.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
G
is a group, then

\operatorname{\sf LCong}(G)\cong\operatorname{\sf RCong}(G)\cong\operatorname{\sf Sub}(G)\and\operatorname{\sf Cong}(G)\cong\operatorname{\sf NSub}(G).

5.2
Green’s relations

Green’s preorders and equivalences

[
21
]
on a semigroup
S
are defined as follows. For
x,y\in S
, we have

\displaystyle x\leq_{\mathscr{L}}y

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ S^{1}x\subseteq S^{1}y

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptx\leq_{\mathscr{R}}y

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ xS^{1}\subseteq yS^{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002ptx\leq_{\mathscr{J}}y

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ S^{1}xS^{1}\subseteq S^{1}yS^{1}

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in S^{1}y,

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in yS^{1},

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in S^{1}yS^{1}.

These preorders induce the equivalences

\mathscr{L}={\leq_{\mathscr{L}}}\cap{\geq_{\mathscr{L}}},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathscr{R}={\leq_{\mathscr{R}}}\cap{\geq_{\mathscr{R}}}\and\mathscr{J}={\leq_{\mathscr{J}}}\cap{\geq_{\mathscr{J}}}.

The remaining two Green’s equivalences are

\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{L}\cap\mathscr{R}\and\mathscr{D}=\mathscr{L}\vee\mathscr{R},

where the latter denotes the join in the lattice
\operatorname{\sf Eq}(S)
. It is well known that
\mathscr{D}=\mathscr{L}\circ\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{R}\circ\mathscr{L}

(where
\circ
denotes the usual composition of binary relations)
, and that
\mathscr{D}=\mathscr{J}
if
S
is finite.
We denote the
\mathscr{L}
-class of an element
x\in S
by
L_{x}
, and similarly for
\mathscr{R}
-,
\mathscr{J}
-,
\mathscr{H}
- and
\mathscr{D}
-classes. If
\mathscr{K}
is any of
\mathscr{L}
,
\mathscr{R}
or
\mathscr{J}
, then the set
S/\mathscr{K}=\{K_{x}:x\in S\}
of all
\mathscr{K}
-classes of
S
has an induced partial order
\leq
, given by

K_{x}\leq K_{y}\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\leq_{\mathscr{K}}y\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $x,y\in S$.}

One of the most important basic results concerning Green’s relations is the following; for a proof, see for example
[
22
, Lemma 2.2.1]
.

Lemma 5.2

(Green’s Lemma)
.

Let
x
and
y
be
\mathscr{R}
-related elements of a semigroup
S
, so that
y=xs
and
x=yt
for some
s,t\in S^{1}
. Then the maps

L_{x}\to L_{y};\ u\mapsto us\and L_{y}\to L_{x};\ v\mapsto vt

are mutually inverse bijections. Moreover, these maps restrict to mutually inverse bijections

H_{x}\to H_{y};\ u\mapsto us\and H_{y}\to H_{x};\ v\mapsto vt.

There is a left-right dual of the previous result, whose statement we omit, but throughout the article we will frequently refer to both formulations as Green’s Lemma. We note that Green’s Lemma, and many of the other coming results and arguments concerning Green’s relations, can be conveniently visualised through the use of so-called
egg-box diagrams
, as explained for example in
[
22
, Chapter 2]
.

A
\mathscr{J}
-class
J
of a semigroup
S
is
stable
if

x\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}sx\ \Rightarrow\ x\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}sx\and x\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}xs\ \Rightarrow\ x\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}xs\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $x\in J$ and $s\in S^{1}$.}

A stable
\mathscr{J}
-class is in fact a
\mathscr{D}
-class
[
24
, Proposition 2.3.9]
. A semigroup
S
is
stable
if every
\mathscr{J}
-class is stable. All finite semigroups are stable
[
29
, Theorem A.2.4]
. There is an obvious left-right dual of the next result, but again we will not state it.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
S
be a semigroup, let
x,y\in S
and
s\in S^{1}
, and suppose
x\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}y
. Then

(i)

sx\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}x\ \Leftrightarrow\ sy\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}y
,

(ii)

if the
\mathscr{J}
-class containing
x,y
is stable, then
sx\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}x\ \Leftrightarrow\ sx\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}x\ \Leftrightarrow\ sy\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}y\ \Leftrightarrow\ sy\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}y
.

Proof.

(i)
. By symmetry it suffices to establish the forwards implication.
Since
x\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}y
, we have
y=xt
for some
t\in S^{1}
. Since
\mathscr{L}
is a right congruence,
sx\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}x
implies
sxt\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}xt
, i.e.
sy\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}y
.

(ii)
. This follows immediately from
(i)
and stability.
∎

5.3
Acts

Let
S
and
T
be semigroups.
A
left
S
-act
is a unary algebra
A
with basic operations

\{\lambda_{s}:s\in S\}
satisfying the law
\lambda_{s}\lambda_{s^{\prime}}=\lambda_{ss^{\prime}}
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
. We typically write
s\cdot a=\lambda_{s}(a)
for
s\in S
and
a\in A
. In this way, the defining law says that

s\cdot(s^{\prime}\cdot a)=(ss^{\prime})\cdot a
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
and
a\in A
.
Dually,
a
right
T
-act
is a unary algebra
A
with basic operations
\rho_{t}:A\to A;\ a\mapsto a\cdot t
(
t\in T
),
satisfying

(a\cdot t)\cdot t^{\prime}=a\cdot(tt^{\prime})
for all
t,t^{\prime}\in T
and all
a\in A
.
We say that
A
is an
(S,T)
-biact
if it is simultaneously a left
S
-act and a right
T
-act, and each
\lambda_{s}
commutes with each
\rho_{t}
, i.e. if
(s\cdot a)\cdot t=s\cdot(a\cdot t)
for all
s\in S
,
t\in T
,
a\in A
.

Since acts are (unary) algebras, we can talk about their subacts and congruences, as well as their
\mathscr{G}
-relations and associated principal factors.

Next we outline a link between actions and congruences that is vital for the viewpoint of this paper.
Let
S
be a semigroup. Then
S
can be considered as a left
S
-act, right
S
-act and
(S,S)
-biact, where
\lambda_{s}
and
\rho_{s}
are the left and right multiplications by
s
.
We denote these three acts by
{}^{S}\!S
,
S^{S}
and
{}^{S}\!S^{S}
, respectively, and record the following immediate observation.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any semigroup
S
:

(i)

\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S)=\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\!S)
,
\operatorname{\sf RCong}(S)=\operatorname{\sf Cong}(S^{S})
and
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(S)=\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\!S^{S})
.

(ii)

The
\mathscr{G}
-equivalences of
{}^{S}\!S
,
S^{S}
and
{}^{S}\!S^{S}
are respectively Green’s relations
\mathscr{L}
,
\mathscr{R}
and
\mathscr{J}
of
S
. ∎

Remark 5.5
.

Unary algebras, including semigroup acts, can naturally be viewed as certain types of directed graphs, such as those introduced in

[
39
,
37
,
36
,
33
,
34
]
, and more recently in
[
2
]
. From this viewpoint, congruences of unary algebras could be replaced by congruences of the corresponding digraphs, and the results of Sections
6
–
8
can all be reformulated in this way.

5.4
Schützenberger groups

It is well known that the
\mathscr{H}
-classes of idempotents in a semigroup
S
are precisely the maximal subgroups of
S
.
In fact, there is a way to generalise this and associate a group to
every

\mathscr{H}
-class. These groups are known as
Schützenberger groups, and we review them briefly here; for more information, see for example
[
10
, Section 2.4]
or
[
3
, pp. 166–167]
.

Fix an
\mathscr{H}
-class
H
of a semigroup
S
. Define the set

T_{R}=T_{R}(H)=\{t\in S^{1}:Ht\subseteq H\}=\{t\in S^{1}:Ht=H\}=\{t\in S^{1}:Ht\cap H\neq\varnothing\}.

The equality of the three sets above follows from
Green’s Lemma. For
t\in T_{R}
, we define the map
\rho_{t}:H\to H
by
x\rho_{t}=xt
for all
x\in H
, and we set

\Gamma_{R}=\Gamma_{R}(H)=\{\rho_{t}:t\in T_{R}\}.

(It is worth noting that the map
t\mapsto\rho_{t}
is not necessarily injective:

different elements of
T_{R}
may well act on
H
in the same way.)

Then
\Gamma_{R}
is a group under composition, known as the (
right
)
Schützenberger group of
H
. It is a simply transitive subgroup of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{H}
, meaning that for all
h,h^{\prime}\in H
there exists a unique
\alpha\in\Gamma_{R}
such that
h\alpha=h^{\prime}
; it follows that
|\Gamma_{R}|=|H|
. The Schützenberger groups associated to any two
\mathscr{H}
-classes within the same
\mathscr{D}
-class of
S
are isomorphic. If
H
happens to be a group
\mathscr{H}
-class, then
\Gamma_{R}(H)\cong H
; indeed,
\Gamma_{R}
is the (right) Cayley representation of
H
in
\mathcal{S}_{H}
in this case.

Note that
\Gamma_{R}
was defined in terms of right translations.

There is also a
left Schützenberger group

\Gamma_{L}=\Gamma_{L}(H)
, defined dually in terms of left translations, which are written to the left of their arguments.

In fact, the groups
\Gamma_{R}
and
\Gamma_{L}
are isomorphic, with an explicit isomorphism given as follows. Fix some
h_{0}\in H
. For
\alpha\in\Gamma_{R}
, let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the unique element of
\Gamma_{L}
for which
h_{0}\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}h_{0}
. Then
\Gamma_{R}\to\Gamma_{L};\ \alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\prime}
is an isomorphism.
Note that the actions of
\Gamma_{R}
and
\Gamma_{L}
on
H
commute, in the sense that
(\beta h)\alpha=\beta(h\alpha)
for all
h\in H
,
\alpha\in\Gamma_{R}
and
\beta\in\Gamma_{L}
.
Because of the isomorphism, we will often denote either of
\Gamma_{R}(H)
or
\Gamma_{L}(H)
simply by
\Gamma(H)
, and call this
the
Schützenberger group of
H
.

We can also define a group structure on
H
itself, again with respect to some fixed
h_{0}\in H
. For
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
, let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
be the unique elements of
\Gamma_{R}
such that
h_{1}=h_{0}\alpha_{1}
and
h_{2}=h_{0}\alpha_{2}
, and define
h_{1}\star h_{2}=h_{0}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
. Note in fact that

h_{1}\star h_{2}=h_{1}\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{1}^{\prime}h_{2},

(5.6)

where
\Gamma_{R}\to\Gamma_{L};\ \alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\prime}
is the isomorphism above. Then
(H,\star)
is a group with identity
h_{0}
, and is independent (up to isomorphism) of the choice of
h_{0}
. Moreover, we have
(H,\star)\cong\Gamma_{R}\cong\Gamma_{L}
.
If
H
happens to be a group
\mathscr{H}
-class, then, taking
h_{0}
to be the idempotent in

, we have
h_{1}\star h_{2}=h_{1}h_{2}
for all
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
.

We will need the following basic result later in the paper. In the statement and proof, we keep the above notation, including the
\star
operation on
H
, defined with respect to the fixed element
{h_{0}\in H}
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
H
be an
\mathscr{H}
-class of a semigroup
S
.
For
s\in S^{1}
and
h\in H
the equality

us=u\star h
(resp.
su=h\star u
)
holds for some
u\in H
if and only if it holds for all
u\in H
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we only consider the equation
us=u\star h
, for which only the forwards implication needs proof. So, suppose
us=u\star h
for some
s\in S^{1}
and
u,h\in H
, and let
v\in H
be arbitrary.
By (
5.6
) we have
u\star h=u\alpha
, where
\alpha
is the unique element of
\Gamma_{R}
with
h=h_{0}\alpha
.
Since
us\in H
, it follows that
s\in T_{R}
, and hence
\rho_{s}\in\Gamma_{R}
.
Now,
u\alpha=u\star h=us=u\rho_{s}
.
Since
\Gamma_{R}
acts simply transitively on

it follows that
\alpha=\rho_{s}
.
Using (
5.6
) once more we have

vs=v\rho_{s}=v\alpha=v\star h
, as required.

∎

Lemma 6.2

If

{{}^{S}\overline{L}_{1}\cong{}^{S}\overline{L}_{2}}
.

Proof.

By the assumption and Green’s Lemma there exists

is an

\mathscr{R}
-class-preserving

We extend this to a bijection

by setting

, and
we claim that

-act morphism, and hence an isomorphism.
To prove this, let
x\in{}^{S}\overline{L}_{1}
and

If

(s\cdot x)f=0f=0=s\cdot 0=s\cdot(xf)
.
Otherwise

Theorem 6.3
.

Let

7
Left congruences and Schützenberger groups

In this section we build on the previous section by focussing on the left
S
-act
{}^{S}\overline{L}

associated to an arbitrary
\mathscr{L}
-class
L
of
S
.
The
\mathscr{H}
-classes of
S
contained in
L
and their Schützenberger groups will play an important role.
Thus, we will consider the following relation on
\overline{L}=L\cup\{0\}
:

\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}:=\mathscr{H}\mathord{\restriction}_{L}\cup\{(0,0)\}.

Lemma 7.1
.

If
L
is an
\mathscr{L}
-class of a semigroup
S
, then
\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L})
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
is an equivalence relation, so it remains to establish (left) compatibility.
To this end let
(u,v)\in\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
and
s\in S
. If
u=v=0
then
(s\cdot u,s\cdot v)=(0,0)\in\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
, so now suppose
u,v\in L
.
Lemma
5.3
(i)
gives
su\in L\ \Leftrightarrow\ sv\in L
. If
su,sv\not\in L
then

(s\cdot u,s\cdot v)=(0,0)\in\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
. Otherwise, when
su,sv\in L
, Green’s Lemma implies that the mapping

x\mapsto sx
is a bijection from
R_{u}
to
R_{su}
that preserves
\mathscr{H}
-classes. In particular,

(s\cdot u,s\cdot v)=(su,sv)\in\mathscr{H}\mathord{\restriction}_{L}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
, completing the proof.
∎

We now bring the Schützenberger groups of the
\mathscr{H}
-classes into play. For an
\mathscr{H}
-class
H
, we will work with the representation
(H,\star)
of
\Gamma(H)
, as introduced in Subsection
5.4
. Recall that this representation depends on an arbitrary, but fixed,
h_{0}\in H
, which will always be assumed as given.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
S
be a semigroup,
L
an
\mathscr{L}
-class of
S
, and
H
an
\mathscr{H}
-class
contained in

L
.
The mapping

\phi:[\Delta_{\overline{L}},\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}]\rightarrow\operatorname{\sf LCong}(H,\star);\ \sigma\mapsto\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{H}

is a lattice isomorphism. Consequently, the interval
[\Delta_{\overline{L}},\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}]
is isomorphic to the subgroup lattice

\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\Gamma(H))
of the Schützenberger group of
H
.

Proof.

To prove that
\phi
is well defined, let
\sigma\in[\Delta_{\overline{L}},\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}]
.
Certainly
\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{H}
is an equivalence on
H
. For (left) compatibility, suppose
{(h_{1},h_{2})\in\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{H}}
and
a\in H
.
Let
t\in S^{1}
be such that
th_{1}=a\star h_{1}
.
By Lemma
5.7
we also have
th_{2}=a\star h_{2}
.
But then
(a\star h_{1},a\star h_{2})=(t\cdot h_{1},t\cdot h_{2})\in\sigma
, which shows that
\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{H}
is left compatible.

We now show that

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{H}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}\mathord{\restriction}_{H}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in[\Delta_{\overline{L}},\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}]$,}}

which implies that
\phi
is an order-preserving injection.
With the forwards implication being clear, suppose
\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{H}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}\mathord{\restriction}_{H}
. Let
(x,y)\in\sigma
; we must show that
(x,y)\in\sigma^{\prime}
. This is clear if
x=y=0
, so suppose instead that
x,y\in L
. Since
\sigma\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
, we have
x,y\in H^{\prime}
for some
\mathscr{H}
-class
H^{\prime}\subseteq L
. By Green’s Lemma we can fix
s,t\in S^{1}
such that

H\to H^{\prime};\ u\mapsto su\and H^{\prime}\to H;\ v\mapsto tv

are mutually inverse bijections. Then
(tx,ty)=(t\cdot x,t\cdot y)\in\sigma\mathord{\restriction}_{H}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}\mathord{\restriction}_{H}
, and so

(x,y)=(stx,sty)=(s\cdot tx,s\cdot ty)\in\sigma^{\prime},

as required.

We complete the proof by showing that
\phi
is onto.
Let
\tau\in\operatorname{\sf LCong}(H,\star)
, and consider
\tau^{\sharp}
, the congruence of
\overline{L}
generated by
\tau
.
Certainly
\tau^{\sharp}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}^{\sharp}=\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
. To demonstrate that
\tau^{\sharp}\mathord{\restriction}_{H}=\tau
, it suffices to establish the forwards inclusion, so suppose
(x,y)\in\tau^{\sharp}\mathord{\restriction}_{H}
.

Using the standard description of the left congruence given by a set of generating pairs (e.g. see

[
23
, Lemma I.4.37]
),

there is a sequence

x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}=y

of elements of
\overline{L}
such that for each
0\leq i<k
,

(x_{i},x_{i+1})=(s_{i}\cdot u_{i},s_{i}\cdot v_{i})\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for some $s_{i}\in S^{1}$ and $(u_{i},v_{i})\in\tau$.}

Since each
(x_{i},x_{i+1})\in\tau^{\sharp}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
, and since
x_{0}=x\in H
, it follows that
x_{i}\in H
for all
i
. Note that
(s_{i}\cdot u_{i},s_{i}\cdot v_{i})=(s_{i}u_{i},s_{i}v_{i})
. As
s_{i}u_{i}=x_{i}\in H
and
(H,\star)
is a group, there
exists
h_{i}\in H
such that
s_{i}u_{i}=h_{i}\star u_{i}
. Lemma
5.7
tells us that
s_{i}v_{i}=h_{i}\star v_{i}
.
Combining this with the fact that
\tau
is a left congruence on
(H,\star)
and
(u_{i},v_{i})\in\tau
, it follows that

(x_{i},x_{i+1})=(s_{i}u_{i},s_{i}v_{i})=(h_{i}\star u_{i},h_{i}\star v_{i})\in\tau.

Hence
x=x_{0}\mathrel{\tau}x_{1}\mathrel{\tau}\cdots\mathrel{\tau}x_{k}=y
, as required.
∎

The next result follows by combining (

) with Propositions

and

(and the Correspondence Theorem).

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
L
be an
\mathscr{L}
-class of a semigroup
S
, let
H
be an
\mathscr{H}
-class
contained in

L
, and let
\Gamma
be the Schützenberger group of
H
. Then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L}))\geq\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\Gamma))+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L}/\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}))-1.

If
\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
is a modular element in
\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L})
then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L}))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\Gamma))+\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L}/\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}))-1.

When
|L/\mathscr{H}|=m
is finite, the act
{}^{S}\overline{L}/\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}
has size
m+1
, so it follows that

2\leq\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L}/\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}))\leq m+1.

Each of these bounds can be attained.
For example, consider the case in which
S
is a left zero semigroup of size
m
. Then
L=S
is itself a single
\mathscr{L}
-class, and since
L
is
\mathscr{H}
-trivial we have
{}^{S}\overline{L}/\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}\cong{}^{S}\overline{L}
. Moreover, every equivalence on
{}^{S}\overline{L}
is a congruence, so the height of
\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L})
, and hence of
\operatorname{\sf Cong}({}^{S}\overline{L}/\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}})
,
is
m+1
.
The attainment of the lower bound
2
is the subject of the discussion for the remainder of this section.

We will say that an algebra
A
is
congruence-free
if its only congruences are
\Delta_{A}
and
\nabla_{A}
.
This property is more commonly known as simplicity, but we will avoid this term, as it has a different meaning in semigroup theory. A congruence

is said to be

if
\sigma\neq\nabla_{A}
and there is no congruence
\tau\in\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A)
such that
\sigma\subsetneq\tau\subsetneq\nabla_{A}
.
By the Correspondence Theorem, this is equivalent to the quotient

being non-trivial and congruence-free,
and also to
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(A/\sigma)
having height
2
.

.

For an
\mathscr{L}
-class
L
of a semigroup

, the following are equivalent:

(i)

The left

-act

(iii)

or

\mathscr{H}
-class in

Proof.

(ii)
\ \Rightarrow\
(i)
. This is clear.

(i)
\ \Rightarrow\
(iii)
. Suppose

is maximal, and let

. By maximality,

is generated (as an

So there exists a sequence

{x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}=0}

of elements of
\overline{L}

,

(x_{i},x_{i+1})=(s_{i}\cdot u_{i},s_{i}\cdot v_{i})\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for some $s_{i}\in S^{1}$ and $(u_{i},v_{i})\in\mathscr{H}_{\overline{L}}\cup\{(x,y),(y,x)\}$.}

We cannot have

(iii)
\ \Rightarrow\

. It suffices to show that for any

if one of

, so now suppose

Definition 7.5
.

We say an

–

and

are two

is

is

constructed in the proof of Lemma

It turns out that

\mathscr{D}
-class containing

is stable and regular, as will be the case in all of our applications. To describe this condition, consider
a
\mathscr{D}
-class

of a semigroup

- and
\mathscr{L}
-classes contained in

are

(

) and

(

, respectively. The
\mathscr{H}

(

Although this matrix depends on the chosen indexing of the

.

We say a

-class

(resp.

) if no two rows (resp. columns) of

Clearly, this notion is of interest only for
\mathscr{D}
-classes that contain idempotents, i.e. the regular
\mathscr{D}
-classes.
For those we have the following, in which we must also assume stability:

Proposition 7.7
.

A stable, regular
\mathscr{D}
-class

is row-faithful if and only if some (and hence every)
\mathscr{L}
-class in

is
\mathscr{H}
-separable.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
).
Suppose
D
is row-faithful, and fix an
\mathscr{L}
-class
L\subseteq D
. To establish
\mathscr{H}
-separability of
L
, we will verify that condition
(iii)
from Proposition
7.4
holds.
Let
x,y\in L
with
{(x,y)\not\in\mathscr{H}}
. From
(x,y)\in\mathscr{L}
it follows that
R_{x}\not=R_{y}
. Since
D
is row-faithful, there exists an
\mathscr{L}
-class

such that (by symmetry)
R_{x}\cap L^{\prime}
contains an idempotent,

R_{y}\cap L^{\prime}
does not contain an idempotent. Now,

, as

. Thus, the proof will be complete if we can show that

.
This is certainly true if

, so suppose instead that

. Then

, and it follows from stability that

, whence

. Since

contains no idempotent, it follows from
[

that

. But we showed above that

, so we must in fact have

.

(
\Leftarrow
).
Aiming to prove the contrapositive, suppose

\mathscr{R}
-classes
R_{1},R_{2}\subseteq D

R_{1}\cap L\cap E(S)=\varnothing\ \Leftrightarrow\ R_{2}\cap L\cap E(S)=\varnothing\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all }L\in D/\mathscr{L}.

-class

)

Proposition 9.2
.

A

\mathscr{R}

(
\Rightarrow
).
To show

let

and

.

(
\Leftarrow

.

∎

Thus, in the presence of stability, given

(cf. Lemma

(cf. Proposition
7.2
)
.

To prove that

, the relation

Continuing to follow the proof of Proposition

is an arbitrary
\mathscr{H}
-class in
\mathscr{J}

be the
\mathscr{L}
-class containing

.
Since

The proof is completed by showing that

{\tau^{\sharp}\mathord{\restriction}_{H}\subseteq\tau}

As

•

List the

•

is the unique maximal congruence
of

.

(more details will be given below)
:

Before we look in detail at our selected examples, we make a remark about terminology.
All the semigroups in the above list are known to be regular, which, among other things, means that every
\mathscr{L}
-class and every
\mathscr{R}

10.1
The full transformation monoid
\mathcal{T}_{n}

The full transformation monoid
\mathcal{T}_{n}
consists of all mappings
[n]\rightarrow[n]
under the composition of mappings.

The mappings in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are written to the right of their arguments, and the composition is from left to right, i.e.
x(\alpha\beta)=(x\alpha)\beta
.

The order of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is
n^{n}
. In the following discussion we assume that
n\geq 2
. Green’s relations

\mathscr{D}(=\!\!\mathscr{J})
,
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{R}
are governed by ranks, images and kernels of mappings:

\alpha\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}\beta\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{rank}(\alpha)=\operatorname{rank}(\beta),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}\beta\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{im}(\alpha)=\operatorname{im}(\beta),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}\beta\ \Leftrightarrow\ \ker(\alpha)=\ker(\beta).

See for example
[
10
, Section 2.2]
.
In particular,
\mathcal{T}_{n}
has
n

\mathscr{D}
-classes, denoted
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
,
where
D_{r}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:\operatorname{rank}(\alpha)=r\}
. The minimal
\mathscr{D}
-class is
D_{1}
.
The
\mathscr{L}
-classes in
D_{r}
are in one-one correspondence with possible images of size
r
, and hence

|D_{r}/\mathscr{L}|=\binom{n}{r}
. Similarly, the
\mathscr{R}
-classes in
D_{r}
correspond to possible kernels with
r
blocks, and so

|D_{r}/\mathscr{R}|=S(n,r)
, the Stirling number of the second kind.
The Schützenberger groups of
\mathscr{H}
-classes in
D_{r}
are all isomorphic to the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{r}
.
Each non-minimal
\mathscr{D}
-class
D_{r}
(
2\leq r\leq n
) is
\mathscr{H}
-separable. This can be verified by checking that the matrix
M(D_{r})
is row- and column-faithful; alternatively see
[
15
]
.
The minimal
\mathscr{D}
-class
D_{1}
is also an
\mathscr{R}
-class, and contains
n
singleton
\mathscr{L}
-classes.

It now follows that each
\mathscr{L}
-class in
D_{1}
is separable, and we obtain a formula for the height of
\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n})

by applying Corollary
7.12
(i)
:

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{r})).

However,
R=D_{1}
, the unique
\mathscr{R}
-class in
D_{1}

is not an
\mathscr{H}
-class
(for
n\geq 2
).
Hence we would like to apply Corollary
8.3
(ii)
, for which we need to check that

\mathscr{H}_{R}
is the unique maximal congruence on
R^{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
.
To this end, let
\alpha,\beta\in R
be any two distinct constant mappings, say with
\operatorname{im}(\alpha)=\{x\}
and
\operatorname{im}(\beta)=\{y\}
, where
x\neq y
.
Further, let
\gamma,\delta\in R
be any constant mappings, say with the images
u
and
v
, respectively.
Let
\zeta\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
be any mapping satisfying

x\zeta=u
,
y\zeta=v
. Then
(\gamma,\delta)=(\alpha\cdot\zeta,\beta\cdot\zeta)\in(\alpha,\beta)^{\sharp}
. In other words,
(\alpha,\beta)^{\sharp}=\nabla_{R}
, proving the claim. So, applying Corollary
8.3
(ii)
, we obtain

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=1+\sum_{r=1}^{n}S(n,r)\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{r})).

Finally, for the height of
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, we apply Corollary
9.12
.
From the discussion above, we have
{\mathscr{H}_{L}=\nabla_{L}}
for any
\mathscr{L}
-class
L\subseteq D_{1}
, and
\mathscr{H}_{R}
is the unique maximal congruence on
R^{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
(where
{R=D_{1}}
). Consequently, the extra additive contribution in (
9.13
) is
1
(for
n\geq 2
) and we have

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=1+\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf NSub}(\mathcal{S}_{r}))
.
Now, it is known that

(\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf NSub}(\mathcal{S}_{r})))_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r\geq 1}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1},2,3,4,3,3,3,\dots),

(10.1)

and it follows that

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=3n-1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }n\geq 4.

The same result can, of course, be obtained by recalling Malcev’s description of congruences on
\mathcal{T}_{n}

[

]
: they in fact form a chain
of length

(for

).
By contrast, the lattices of one-sided congruences of

10.2
The partition monoid
\mathcal{P}_{n}

We now follow the same trajectory for the partition monoid
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, but somewhat more rapidly, again assuming that

.
For the definition of the product in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, see for example
[
13
]
, which also includes the following information regarding Green’s relations.
The
\mathscr{D}
-classes

of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
again consist of partitions of a fixed rank, but this time the available ranks are
r=0,1,\dots,n
, with
D_{0}
minimal.
The
\mathscr{L}
- and
\mathscr{R}
-classes in

correspond to partitions of
[n]
with
r
marked blocks,

where markings are used to indicate which blocks participate in transversals.
Consequently, the number of
\mathscr{R}

\mathscr{L}
-classes in

is

\sum_{k=r}^{n}S(n,k)\binom{k}{r}
.

The Schützenberger groups are again
\mathcal{S}_{r}
.
That each

is
\mathscr{H}
-separable for

was shown in
[
14
, Lemma 5.13]
.

Now consider the minimal
\mathscr{D}
-class
D_{0}
. Note that it is a
B(n)\times B(n)
rectangular band, where

is the
n

Fix an
\mathscr{L}
-class

and an
\mathscr{R}
-class

Neither
L
nor
R

\mathscr{H}
-class
(for

), so we want to show that
\mathscr{H}_{L}
and
\mathscr{H}_{R}
are the unique maximal congruences on

{}^{\mathcal{P}_{n}}\!L
and
R^{\mathcal{P}_{n}}
respectively.
We will show the latter statement, and the former can be deduced by duality.
So, let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in R
be arbitrary, with
\alpha\neq\beta
.
We want to show that
(\gamma,\delta)\in(\alpha,\beta)^{\sharp}
.
We do this by showing that
(\gamma,\theta),(\delta,\theta)\in(\alpha,\beta)^{\sharp}
, where
\theta\in R
is the unique partition with singleton lower blocks, and using transitivity.
Only the first of these needs to be proved, as the second will be analogous.
If
\gamma=\theta
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise,
\theta
has at most
n-1
lower blocks.
In the proof of
[
14
, Proposition 5.8]
it was shown that, swapping
\alpha
and
\beta
if necessary, there exists
\zeta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
such that

\gamma=\gamma\alpha\zeta
and
\gamma^{\prime}:=\gamma\beta\zeta
has one more lower block than
\gamma
.
Since
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are all
\mathscr{R}
-related in the present consideration, and since all are idempotents, we have
\gamma\alpha=\alpha
and
\gamma\beta=\beta
,
and so
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=(\alpha\cdot\zeta,\beta\cdot\zeta)\in(\alpha,\beta)^{\sharp}
.
Repeating this argument, and using transitivity, we arrive at
(\gamma,\theta)\in(\alpha,\beta)^{\sharp}
, as required.

We can now deploy Corollaries
7.12
(i)
and
8.3
(i)

to obtain

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{P}_{n}))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{P}_{n}))=B(n)+\displaystyle{\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{k=r}^{n}S(n,k)\binom{k}{r}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{r}))}.

For the two-sided congruences, we follow the same argument as for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, with two small differences: first, we have an extra
\mathscr{D}
-class, namely

; and secondly, the extra additive contribution in (

) is
2
, instead of
1
. Therefore we obtain:

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{P}_{n}))=3n+1\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $n\geq 4$.}

Again, this could also be obtained using the description of the lattice
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
from
[

]

10.3
Remarks concerning further semigroups

The calculations for the remaining semigroups from our list are all entirely analogous to what we have just seen for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and we will not go through them in any kind of detail. Instead, we present the required input data, alongside references to where it can be found in the literature, in Table

. To aid the reader in both inspecting the tables, and performing the calculations for additional semigroups not given in the tables, we give below some relevant commentary.
Towards the end of the section we will discuss two further monoids not included in the tables.

Combinatorial notation.

Various standard combinatorial sequences and arrays appear in the two tables. Here is a quick reference:

S(n,r)
, Stirling numbers of the second kind;

B(n)
, Bell numbers;

C_{n}
, Catalan numbers;

n!!
, double factorials;

\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}n\\
r\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]_{q}
, Gaussian binomial coefficients.

Indexing of
\mathscr{D}
-classes.

As we have already seen in the case of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the
\mathscr{D}
-classes in some of our monoids are naturally indexed by some sets of integers that are not necessarily of the form
\{1,\dots,n\}
, as in our theoretical results.
The starkest example of this is perhaps the Brauer monoid
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and the Temperley–Lieb monoid
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
, whose
\mathscr{D}
-classes are indexed by all integers from
[n]
of the same parity as
n
. To aid the translation, we have included a column in Table
1
to explicitly state the indexing. We have also added a column to specify the minimal
\mathscr{D}
-class: this is always the
\mathscr{D}
-class with the smallest index, i.e.
D_{0}
or
D_{1}
.

Schützenberger group contributions.

In all our examples, with the exception of the general linear monoid
\mathcal{M}(n,q)
, the Schützenberger group
\Gamma_{r}
of any
\mathscr{H}
-class in
D_{r}
is
either
\mathcal{S}_{r}
or trivial
.
For the symmetric group, its subgroup height is given in Theorem
1.1
, and its normal subgroup height in (
10.1
).

In some semigroups we will also encounter the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{0}
, which is trivial and has (normal) subgroup height
1
.

The case where all Schützenberger groups are trivial, as for example for
\mathcal{O}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
, leads to significant simplifications, as follows:

Remark 10.2
.

Suppose
S
is an
\mathscr{H}
-trivial semigroup, so that all its Schützenberger groups are trivial.
Then the expression
\sum_{r=1}^{n}m_{r}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\Gamma_{r}))
in Corollary
7.12
simplifies to just
\sum_{r=1}^{n}m_{r}=|S/\mathscr{L}|
, the number of
\mathscr{L}
-classes of
S
.
Similarly, the corresponding sum in Corollary
8.3
becomes
|S/\mathscr{R}|
, the number of
\mathscr{R}
-classes in
S
,
while the sum
\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf NSub}(\Gamma_{r}))
in Corollary
9.12
becomes just
n=|S/\mathscr{D}|
, the number of
\mathscr{D}
-classes.

The Schützenberger groups in the
\mathscr{D}
-class
D_{r}
of the general linear monoid
\mathcal{M}(n,q)
are isomorphic to the general linear group
\textup{GL}(r,q)
.
To the best of our knowledge, the heights of their subgroup lattices have not been determined, although
[
31
,
30
,
32
]

discuss in detail the heights of closely related groups of Lie type.
The normal subgroups of
\textup{GL}(r,q)
are known: with a small number of exceptions, they are precisely the subgroups of the group of scalar matrices, and the subgroups containing the special linear group; see
[
4
, Theorem 4.9]
. It is not clear whether this description is sufficient to give a closed formula for the height of the lattice of normal subgroups of
\textup{GL}(r,q)
; see
[
15
, Figure 11.1]
.
In our formulae, therefore, we keep the terms
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\textup{GL}(r,q)))
and
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf NSub}(\textup{GL}(r,q)))
.

Separability.

In all our examples, all non-minimal
\mathscr{D}
-classes
D_{r}
are
\mathscr{H}
-separable. This can be easily verified by the reader by considering the matrices
M(D_{r})
and checking they are row- and column-faithful.
Alternatively, this has been proved in
[
15
]
in the more general context of certain related categories.

There is one general, theoretical situation where
\mathscr{H}
-separability is guaranteed from the outset, namely for inverse semigroups with finitely many idempotents, where each matrix
M(D)
is always equivalent to the identity matrix up to permutation of rows and columns.
The reason for this is that in an inverse semigroup every
\mathscr{L}
- and every
\mathscr{R}
-class contains precisely one idempotent.
In particular, we have
|D/\mathscr{L}|=|D/\mathscr{R}|=|E(D)|
, the number of idempotents in
D
.
The above is true for
all

\mathscr{D}
-classes, including the minimal one, which is always necessarily a group.

Corollary 10.3
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup with finitely many
\mathscr{D}
-classes
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
,
and let
\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{n}
be the Schützenberger groups

(i.e. maximal subgroups)
associated with their
\mathscr{H}
-classes, respectively.

(i)

If each
D_{r}
has only finitely many idempotents then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf RCong}(S))=\sum_{r=1}^{n}|E(D_{r})|\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\Gamma_{r})).

Moreover, this is equal to
|E(S)|
if
S
is
\mathscr{H}
-trivial.

(ii)

If
S
is stable then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(S))=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf NSub}(\Gamma_{r})).

Moreover, this is equal to
n=|S/\mathscr{D}|
if
S
is
\mathscr{H}
-trivial. ∎

As a consequence, if
S
is a finite semilattice then

\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(S))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf RCong}(S))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(S))=|S|.

Relationship to classification results.

Just as was the case with
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}
above, all the two-sided congruences have been described for all the semigroups listed in Table
2
. The oldest and most well-known such results are due to Malcev for the full transformation monoid
[
26
]
and the general linear monoid
[
27
]
, and Liber for the symmetric inverse monoid
[
25
]
. These results have been gathered under a common methodological framework in
[
15
]
,
where the reader can also find references to the historical individual proofs.
From these descriptions, it is possible to infer the formulae for the height of the congruence lattice by straightforward inspection, with the possible exception of the general linear monoid, where perhaps the structure of the congruence lattice is not entirely obvious from the description, but see
[
15
, Section 11.4]
for a discussion of this point.

The reason we have opted to present these values in the way we did is two-fold:
1) The height is computed without the prior of knowledge of what all the congruences are,
so the present method can be regarded as being conceptually simpler than going via classification theorems.
2) The computation follows the same lines as that for one-sided congruences, emphasising the methodological proximity of the two.

In contrast with two-sided congruences, very little is known, by way of classification, for one-sided congruences of the semigroups discussed here. One exception is the symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}_{n}
, where useful detailed information about its left and right congruences can be obtained by utilising Brookes’ inverse kernel approach; see
[
6
]
for the general theory and
[
5
]
for applications to
\mathcal{I}_{n}
. Combining this with
[
9
, Theorem 7.4]
may offer an alternative route towards calculating
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{I}_{n}))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{I}_{n}))
.

Miscellaneous notes on the table entries.

The formulae for the heights of the lattices of one-sided congruences for
\mathcal{O}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
given in Table
2

are not identical to the sums obtained using the information from Table
1
.
The reason is that the latter sums simplify, and proving the equality of the two expressions is an elementary exercise.
Alternatively, we can recall that
\mathcal{O}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
are
\mathscr{H}
-trivial, and use Remark
10.2
.
To do this we need the total numbers of
\mathscr{L}
- and
\mathscr{R}
-classes in the monoids: for
\mathcal{O}_{n}
these are
2^{n}-1
and
2^{n-1}
respectively, and for
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
they are
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
and
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

The formulae for the height of the congruence lattices of semigroups whose maximal subgroups are the symmetric groups are valid for
n\geq 4
. For smaller values of
n
the heights deviate slightly from those formulae, due to the initial irregularity of
the sequence
(\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf NSub}(\mathcal{S}_{r})))_{r\geq 0}
; see (
10.1
).
These values can be found in Table
3
, which gives computed values for
n\leq 10
. The Semigroups package for GAP
[
18
,
28
]
is capable of computing all the congruences for some small values of
n
, which provides a verification of the given numbers.
As the family
\mathcal{M}(n,q)
of linear monoids depends on two parameters, we have not included any numerical data for it in Table
3
.

In the formulae for the heights of the one-sided congruence lattices of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
we have made a couple of indexing simplifications in the transition between Table
1
and Table
2
.
We have changed the summation parameter from
r
to
k
, where
r=n-2k
.
We have also used the fact that
2\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor+1
equals
n-1
when
n
is even and
n
when
n
is odd.

Partial Brauer monoid.

Another diagram monoid, not listed in Tables
1
and
2
, is the
partial Brauer monoid

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{B}_{n}
; it consists of all partitions in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
with block-sizes at most
2
. One of the consequences of
[
14
, Theorems 5.4 and 6.1]
is that the lattices
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
and
\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{B}_{n})
are isomorphic, and hence have the same height. The analogue is not true for one-sided congruence lattices (apart from trivial exceptions for small
n
). Indeed,
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{B}_{n}
again has
\mathscr{D}
-classes
D_{0},D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
, given by ranks, and Corollary
7.12
applies; see
[
14
, Section 6]
for the relevant separation properties. This gives a formula for
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{B}_{n}))
in terms of the parameters

|D_{r}/\mathscr{L}|=\binom{n}{r}a(n-r)
, where
a(k)
stands for the number of involutions on
[k]
; see

[
12
, Proposition 4.6]
.
Since these are strictly smaller than the corresponding parameters for
\mathcal{P}_{n}
itself, we have
\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{B}_{n}))<\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{P}_{n}))
for
n\geq 2
.

The same is true for the right congruence lattices.

Monoids of uniform block bijections.

For all the monoids discussed so far, the posets of
\mathscr{J}(=\mathscr{D})
-classes happen to be chains.
This is not a requirement for our formulas to apply. We conclude by briefly discussing a monoid with a more complicated
\mathscr{J}
-structure, namely
the monoid
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}
of all
uniform
block bijections from
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{*}
, i.e. those for which each block contains the same number of upper and lower points. The
\mathscr{D}
-classes of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}
are of the form
D_{\mu}
, where
\mu\vdash n
is an
integer partition
of
n
, i.e. a tuple
{\mu=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})}
for some
k\geq 1
, where
m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{k}\geq 1
are integers, and
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{k}=n
. Given such a
\mu\vdash n
, the
\mathscr{D}
-class
D_{\mu}
contains all block bijections of rank
k
, whose blocks contain
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
upper (and lower) points. As explained in
[
16
, Section 3]
, the parameters associated to such a
\mathscr{D}
-class are as follows. First, for
1\leq i\leq n
, we write
\mu_{i}
for the number of entries of
\mu
equal to
i
(so
n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\mu_{i}
). The number of
\mathscr{L}
- and
\mathscr{R}
-classes in
D_{\mu}
is equal to the number of set partitions of
[n]
whose blocks have sizes
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
, which is well known to be equal to

n!/\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}!(i!)^{\mu_{i}}
.
The Schützenberger group of any
\mathscr{H}
-class in
D_{\mu}
is isomorphic to the
Young subgroup

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}:=\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{\mu_{n}}
of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Applying Corollary
10.3
yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf LCong}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}))=\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf RCong}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\vdash n}\frac{n!\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Sub}(\mathcal{S}_{\mu}))}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}!(i!)^{\mu_{i}}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf Cong}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\vdash n}\operatorname{\sf Ht}(\operatorname{\sf NSub}(\mathcal{S}_{\mu})).

To the best of the authors’ knowledge
neither the one-sided nor two-sided congruences of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{*}
have been classified.
