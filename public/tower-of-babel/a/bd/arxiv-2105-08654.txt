Abstract
. In holomorphic dynamics, complex box mappings arise as first return maps to well-chosen domains. They are a generalization of polynomial-like mapping, where the domain of the return map can have infinitely many components. They turned out to be extremely useful in tackling diverse problems. The purpose of this paper is: - To illustrate some pathologies that can occur when a complex box mapping is not induced by a globally defined map and when its domain has infinitely many components, and to give conditions to avoid these issues. - To show that once one has a box mapping for a rational map, these conditions can be assumed to hold in a very natural setting. Thus we call such complex box mappings dynamically natural . Having such box mappings is the first step in tackling many problems in one-dimensional dynamics. - Many results in holomorphic dynamics rely on an interplay between combinatorial and analytic techniques. In this setting some of these tools are: - the Enhanced Nest (a nest of puzzle pieces around critical points) from [ KSS1 ] ; - the Covering Lemma (which controls the moduli of pullbacks of annuli) from [ KL1 ] ; - the QC-Criterion and the Spreading Principle from [ KSS1 ] . The purpose of this paper is to make these tools more accessible so that they can be used as a ‘black box’, so one does not have to redo the proofs in new settings. - To give an intuitive, but also rather detailed, outline of the proof from [ KvS , KSS1 ] of the following results for non-renormalizable dynamically natural complex box mappings: - puzzle pieces shrink to points, - (under some assumptions) topologically conjugate non-renormalizable polynomials and box mappings are quasiconformally conjugate. - We prove the fundamental ergodic properties for dynamically natural box mappings. This leads to some necessary conditions for when such a box mapping supports a measurable invariant line field on its filled Julia set. These mappings are the analogues of Lattès maps in this setting. - We prove a version of Mañé’s Theorem for complex box mappings concerning expansion along orbits of points that avoid a neighborhood of the set of critical points.

In one-dimensional holomorphic dynamics, it is often the case that the first step in understanding the dynamics of a family of mappings is to obtain a good combinatorial model for the dynamics in the family. Once that is at hand, one can go on to study deeper properties of the dynamics in the family, for example, rigidity, ergodic properties and the geometric properties of the Julia sets. It turns out that complex box mappings are often part of these combinatorial models.
Indeed,
recent progress on the rigidity question for a large family of non-polynomial rational maps, so-called
Newton maps

[
DS
]
made it clear that complex box mappings can be effectively used in the holomorphic setting well past polynomials: by “boxing away” the most essential part of the ambient dynamics into a box mapping one can readily apply the existing rigidity results without redoing much of the theory from scratch.

These developments motivated us to give a comprehensive survey of the dynamics of such mappings. The subtlety is that Definition

For

More generally, Douady and Hubbard went further by introducing the notion of a

each of which could play the role of the Julia set.

1.3.
Puzzle pieces of box mappings

One of the most important properties of complex box mappings is that they provide a kind of “Markov partition” for the dynamics of
F
on its filled Julia set: the components of
\mathcal{V}
and their iterative pullbacks are analogous to Yoccoz puzzle pieces for polynomials. Having such a topological structure is a starting point for the study of many questions in dynamics. So, following
[

,
KSS1
]
, for a given

, a connected component of

is called a
puzzle piece of depth
n
for
F
.
For
x\in\mathcal{V}
, the
puzzle piece of depth
n
containing
x
is the connected component of
F^{-n}(\mathcal{V})

containing
x
(when
x
escapes
\mathcal{U}
in less than
n
steps, this set will be empty).

It is easy to see that
F
properly maps a puzzle piece of depth
n+1
onto a puzzle piece of depth

, and any two puzzle pieces are either nested, or disjoint. In the former case, the puzzle piece at larger depth is contained in the puzzle piece of smaller depth.

Remark
.

In the literature, the puzzle pieces that are constructed for polynomials or rational maps are usually assumed to be closed. In the context of polynomial-like mappings, or generalized polynomial-like mappings, similar to ours, the puzzle pieces are often defined as open sets.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

The paper is organized as follows.

In the last subsection of the introduction, Section

In Section

We end Section

In Section

In Section

In Section

, and on the other hand, to make the underlying ideas and proof techniques better accessible to a wider audience beyond the experts.

The rest of the paper, namely Sections

2.1.
Finitely renormalizable polynomials with connected Julia set, see Figure

Suppose that

2.3.
Newton Maps, see Figure

Given a complex polynomial

Newton maps are arguably the largest family of rational maps, beyond polynomials, for which several satisfactory global results are known. This progress was possible due to abundance of touching points between the boundaries of components of the root basins: these touchings provide a rigid combinatorial structure. Similarly to the real-analytic mappings, Newton maps do not have global Böttcher coordinates. However, the local coordinates in each of the immediate basins of roots allow one to find local equipotentials and local (internal) rays that nicely co-land from the global point of view. ) that provide a partition of the Riemann sphere into pieces similar to Yoccoz puzzle pieces, see Figure
5

[

[

]

[
WYZ
]

[

]
, where the rigidity results for box mappings that we discuss in the present paper were applied to conclude rigidity of Newton dynamics.

[

follows from Theorem

and the discussion in Section

[

in order to prove local connectivity for the boundaries of root basins for Newton maps.

2.4.3.
Box mappings in McMullen’s family

In
[
QWY
]
, puzzle pieces were constructed for certain McMullen maps
f_{\lambda}\colon z\mapsto z^{n}+\lambda/z^{n}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,
n\geqslant 3
. This family includes maps
with a Sierpiński carpet Julia set. In contrast to the previously discussed examples, where the puzzle pieces where constructed using internal and external rays, the pieces constructed in
[

are bounded by so-called

: these are forward invariant curves that intersect the Julia set in uncountably many points and whose union separates the plane. Properly truncated in neighborhoods of

(the latter is a super-attracting fixed point of

, and

into puzzle pieces. Using these pieces, a complex box mapping can be induced. Similarly to the examples above, the main results of
[

]
can be then obtained by importing the corresponding results on box mappings.

2.5.
Outlook and questions

In this subsection we want to mention some research questions related to the notion of complex box mapping and to the techniques presented in this paper.

2.5.1.
Combinatorial classification of analytic mappings via box mappings

The classification of box mappings goes via a combinatorial construction involving itineraries with respect to curve families discussed in Section
5.4
. For polynomials and rational maps one often uses
trees and tableaux to obtain combinatorial information. This is encoded
in the
pictograph
introduced in
[
dMP
]
for the case where infinity is a super attracting
fixed point whose basin is infinitely connected.
It would be interesting to explore the relationship in more depth.

2.5.2.
Metric properties of analytic mappings via box mappings

Complex box mappings play a crucial role in the study of the measure theoretic dynamics of rational mappings and the fractal geometry of their Julia sets. This has been a very active area of research, and here we just provide a snapshot of some of the results in these directions. Some of the natural questions in this setting concern the following:

(1)

The existence and properties of conformal measures supported on the Julia set, and the existence and properties of invariant measures that are absolutely continuous with respect to a conformal measure.

(2)

Finding combinatorial or geometric conditions on a mapping that have consequences for the measure or Hausdorff dimension of its Julia set,
i.e.
if the measure is positive or zero, or whether the Hausdorff dimension is two or less than two.

(3)

When is the Julia set holomorphically removable
7
7

7

Let
\Omega\subseteq\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
be a domain,
E\subset\Omega
be a compact set, and
f\colon\Omega\setminus E\to\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
be a holomorphic map. A set

(holomorphically) removable
if
f
extends to a holomorphic mapping on the whole

.
?

(4)

There are several different quantities which are related to the complexity of a fractal or expansion properties of a mapping on its Julia set, among them, the Hausdorff dimension, the hyperbolic dimension, and the Poincaré exponent. While it is known that these quantities are not always all equal
[
AL3
]
, in many circumstances they are, and it would be very interesting to characterize those mappings for which equality holds.

The most complete results are known for rational mappings that are weakly expanding on their Julia sets, for example see
[
PRL2
,
RLS
]
. Both conformal measures and absolutely continuous invariant measures as in (
1
) are well-understood. It is known that whenever the Julia set of such a mapping is not the whole sphere that it has Hausdorff dimension less than two, and for such mappings all the aforementioned quantities in (

For infinitely renormalizable mappings with bounded geometry and bounded combinatorics,
[
AL1

establishes existence of conformal measures; equality of the quantities mentioned in (

The existence of mappings with positive area (and hence with Hausdorff dimension two) Julia set, but with hyperbolic dimension less than two was proved in
[
AL3
]
.

A final class of mappings for which many such results are known are non-renormalizable quadratic polynomials. It was proved in
[
Lyu1
,
Shi
]
that their Julia sets have measure zero, in
[
Ka
]
that they are removable, and in
[
Prz
]
that their Hausdorff and hyperbolic dimensions coincide.

Further progress on these questions for rational maps (or polynomials)
will most likely involve complex box mappings, and moreover
any such questions could also be asked for complex box mappings.
Indeed, this point of view is taken in, for example,
[
PZ
]
.

2.5.3.
When can box mappings be induced?

Once one has a box mapping for a given analytic map, one can use the tools
discussed in this paper. Therefore it is very interesting to find more
classes of maps for which box mappings exist:

Question 1
.

Is it true that for every rational (or meromorphic) map with a non-empty Fatou set one has an associated
non-trivial box mapping?

We say that a box mapping
F
is
associated
to a rational (meromorphic) map
f
if every branch of
F
is a certain restriction of an iterate of
f
, and the critical set of
F
is a subset of the critical set of the starting map
f
. Furthermore, here we say that a box mapping is
non-trivial
if the critical set of the box mapping is non-empty
and it satisfies the no permutation condition (see Definition
4.1
).

In fact, the authors are not aware of any general procedure which associates a non-trivial box mapping
to a transcendental function. Examples of box mappings in the complement of the postsingular set for transcendental maps were constructed in
[
Dob
]
.

Question 2
.

Are there examples of rational maps whose Julia set is the entire sphere
and for which one cannot find an associated non-trivial box mapping?

For example, for
topological Collet–Eckmann
rational maps,
[
PRL2
]
uses the strong expansion properties
to construct box mappings. The previous two questions ask whether one can also do this when there is no such expansion. Similarly:

Question 3
.

Can one associate a box mapping to a rational map with Sierpiński carpet
Julia set beyond the examples discussed previously (where symmetries are used)?

Another class of maps for which the existence of box mappings is not clear is when
one has neutral periodic points:

Question 4
.

Let
f
be a complex polynomial with a Siegel disk
S
. Under which conditions is it possible to construct a puzzle partition of a neighborhood of
\partial S
and use this partition to associate a complex box mapping to
f
?

There is a recent result in this direction, namely, in
[
Yan
]
a puzzle partition was constructed for polynomial Siegel disks of bounded type rotation number. Using this partition and the Kahn–Lyubich Covering Lemma (see Lemma
7.10
), local connectivity of the boundary of such Siegel disks was established. In that paper, the author exploits the Douady–Ghys surgery and the Blaschke model for Siegel disks with bounded type rotation number. This model allows one to construct “bubble rays” growing out of the boundary of the disk. These rays, properly truncated, then define the puzzle partition. However, the resulting puzzle pieces have more complicated mapping properties than traditional Yoccoz puzzles (for example, they develop slits under forward iteration). Hence it is not clear at this point
whether the tools presented in this paper can be applied (see also Section
6.2
).

2.6.
Further extensions

The notion of complex box mapping has been extended in two directions. The first of these considers
multivalued
generalized polynomial-like maps
F\colon U\to V
. This means that we consider open sets
U_{i}
and a holomorphic map
F_{i}\colon U_{i}\to V
on each of these sets. If these sets
U_{i}
are not assumed to be disjoint, the map
F(z):=F_{i}(z)
when
z\in U_{i}
becomes multivalued. Such maps are considered in
[
LvS1
,
LvS2
,
She2
]
as a first step to obtain a generalized polynomial-like map
with moduli bounds
(because the Yoccoz puzzle construction may not apply). As is shown in those papers one can often work with such multivalued generalized polynomial-like maps almost as well as with their single valued analogues.

The other extension of the notion of complex box mapping is to assume that
F
is
asymptotically holomorphic
(along, for example, the real line) rather than holomorphic. Here we say that
F
is asymptotically holomorphic of order
\beta>0
along some set
K
if

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}F(z)=O(\dist(z,K)^{\beta-1})
.
This point of view is considered in
[
CvST
]
and
[
CdFvS
]
. For example, in the latter paper

C^{3+\alpha}
-interval maps with
\alpha>0
are considered. Such maps have an asymptotically holomorphic extension to the complex plane of order
3+\alpha
.
The analogue of the Fatou–Julia–Sullivan theorem and a topological straightening theorem is shown in this setting. In particular, these maps do not have wandering domains and their Julia sets are locally connected.

3.

A remark on the definitions of

S_{0}
. Let
f\colon z\mapsto z^{2}
,

Y
be the disk of radius 16 centered at the origin and
X=f^{-1}(Y)
. Choose real symmetric conformal mappings
H_{0}:S_{0}\to X
and
H_{1}:\mathcal{V}\to Y
which sends the origin to itself. Let
m_{a}=\frac{z-a}{1-\bar{a}z}
be the family of real symmetric Möbius transformations of
Y
.
Consider the family of mappings

F_{a}=H_{1}^{-1}\circ m_{a}\circ f\circ H_{0}:S_{0}\to\mathcal{V}.

For
a=0,

F_{a}
is a real-symmetric, polynomial-like mapping with a super-attracting critical point at 0 of degree 2, which is a minimum for the mapping restricted to its real trace. As
a
varies along the positive real axis from 0, the critical value of the mapping
F_{a}
varies along the negative real axis from 0 until it escapes
\mathcal{V}\setminus S_{0}
. Thus the family of mappings
F_{a}|_{S_{0}}
is a full real family of mappings
[
dMvS
]
, and hence it contains a mapping conjugate to the real Fibonacci mapping. Let
F\colon S_{0}\to\mathcal{V}
denote this mapping. Now we have defined
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{U}
tiles
\mathcal{V}
.

Let us now show that under
F
a full measure set of points in
\mathcal{U}
converge to
(-3/2,0)\in\partial\mathcal{V}
.
First, recall that the filled Julia set
K(f)
of the quadratic Fibonacci mapping has measure zero
[
Lyu1
]
. Since
F:S_{0}\to\mathcal{V}
is a polynomial-like mapping that is quasiconformally conjugate to the quadratic Fibonacci mapping, and quasiconformal mappings are absolutely continuous, we have that the filled Julia set of
F\colon S_{0}\to\mathcal{V}
has measure zero. We will denote this set by
K_{0}
. Since the set of points whose orbits eventually enter
K_{0}
is contained in the union of the countably many preimages of
S_{0}
, we have that the set of points that eventually enter
K_{0}
has measure zero too. From the construction of
F
, we have that every puzzle piece contains points that map to
S_{0}
, so we have that almost every point in
K(F)
either accumulates on the critical point of
F
or converges to
\partial\mathcal{V}
. By the comment above, we may assume that this set of points is disjoint from the preimages of
K_{0}
.

Let us show that a.e. point in
X_{1}=\{z;F^{i}(z)\notin S_{0},i\geqslant 0\}
converges to
(-3/2,0)
.
Suppose not. Then there exists a set
X_{1}^{\prime}\subset X_{1}
for which this is not the case.
Let
z_{0}
be a Lebesgue density point of
X_{1}^{\prime}
. Let
Q_{k}
be the puzzle piece containing

z_{0}
of level
k
. Then
F^{k-1}(Q_{k})=R
for some rectangle
R
with
R\cap S_{0}=\emptyset
.
By the above claim and the Koebe Distortion
Theorem a definite proportion of
Q_{k}
is mapped into the set
W
of points which converge
monotonically to
(-3/2,0)
, thus contradicting that
z_{0}
is a Lebesgue density point of
X_{1}^{\prime}
.

Let
X_{0}
be the set of points which enter
S_{0}
and
which are not eventually mapped into the zero Lebesgue measure set
K_{0}
.
Let
X_{0}^{\prime}
be the set of points in
X_{0}
which do not converge to
(-3/2,0)

and let
z_{0}
be a Lebesgue density point of
X_{0}^{\prime}
. Note that by the previous paragraph a.e. point
in
X_{0}^{\prime}
enters
S_{0}
infinitely many times.
Let
r_{0}\geqslant 0
be minimal so that
F^{r_{0}}(z_{0})
is contained in
S_{0}
.
Let
k_{0}>r_{0}
be minimal so that
F^{k_{0}}(z_{0})\in S_{i}
for some
i>0
, and let
R_{k_{0}}
denote the square of
S_{i}
that contains
F^{k_{0}}(z_{0})
. Inductively, define
r_{\ell}>r_{\ell-1}
minimal so that
F^{r_{\ell}}(z_{0})\in S_{0},
and
k_{\ell}>r_{\ell}
, minimal so that
F^{k_{\ell}}(z_{0})\in S_{i},i>0,
and let
R_{k_{\ell}}
be the square so that
F^{k_{\ell}}(z_{0})\in R_{k_{\ell}}
. By the choice of

K
at the start of the proof, the distortion of the mapping
F^{k_{\ell}}|_{Q_{\ell}}\colon Q_{\ell}\to R_{k_{\ell}}
where
Q_{\ell}=\Comp_{z_{0}}F^{-k_{\ell}}(R_{k_{\ell}})
is bounded by
K
.
We have already proved that in each rectangle
R\subset S_{i}
,
i\geqslant 1
a definite proportion of points converge monotonically to the boundary point
(-3/2,0)
; hence at arbitrarily small scales around
z_{0}
, a definite mass of points escapes is mapped into the set
W
(which converge to
(-3/2,0)\in\partial\mathcal{V}
), which implies that
z_{0}
cannot be a Lebesgue density point of
X_{0}^{\prime}
.
∎

4.
Dynamically natural box mappings

In this section we introduce the concept of a
dynamically natural
complex box mapping. These are the maps for which various pathologies from Section
3
disappear, and which arise naturally in the study of rational maps on
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
.

In order to define the concept, let us start by introducing two dynamically defined subsets of the non-escaping set.

4.1.
Orbits that avoid critical neighborhoods

The first subset consists of points whose orbits avoid a neighborhood of
\Crit(F)
.
Let
A\subset K(F)
be a finite set and
W
be a union of finitely many puzzle pieces. We say that
W
is a
puzzle neighborhood
of
A
if
A\subset W
and each component of
W
intersects the set
A
.

If
\Crit(F)\neq\emptyset
, define

K_{\text{off-crit}}(F):=\left\{x\in K(F)\colon\exists W\text{ puzzle neighborhood of }\Crit(F):\orb(x)\cap W=\emptyset\right\};

otherwise, i.e. when
F
has no critical points, we set
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F)\equiv K(F)
.

It is easy to see that the set
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F)
is forward invariant with respect to
F
.

4.2.
Orbits that are well inside

The second subset consists of any point in
K(F)
whose orbit from time to time visits components of
\mathcal{U}
that are well-inside of the corresponding components of
\mathcal{V}
. More precisely, let

m_{F}(x):=\modulus\left(\Comp_{x}\mathcal{V}\setminus\overline{\Comp_{x}\mathcal{U}}\right),

and for a given
\delta>0
, set

K_{\delta}(F):=\left\{y\in K(F):\limsup_{k\geqslant 0}m_{F}(F^{k}(y))>\delta\right\}.

Define

K_{\text{well-inside}}(F):=\bigcup_{\delta>0}K_{\delta}(F).

A component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
is said to be
\delta
-well-inside
\mathcal{V}
if
m_{F}(x)>\delta
for some (and hence for all)
x\in U
. Thus the set
K_{\text{well-inside}}(F)
consists of the points
x\in K(F)
whose orbit visits infinitely often components of
\mathcal{U}
that are
\delta
-well inside for some
\delta=\delta(x)>0
. By definition, the set
K_{\text{well-inside}}(F)
is forward invariant with respect to
F
.

4.3.
Dynamically natural box mappings

Definition 4.1

(No permutation condition and dynamical naturality)
.

A complex box mapping
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
satisfies the

no permutation condition
if

(1)

for each component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
there exists
n\geqslant 0
so that
F^{n}(U)\setminus\mathcal{U}\neq\emptyset
.

The mapping
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is called

dynamically natural
if it
additionally
satisfies the following assumptions:

(2)

the Lebesgue measure of the set
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F)
is zero;

(3)

K(F)=K_{\text{well-inside}}(F)
.

If
F
does not satisfy the no permutation condition, then there exists a component

U
of
\mathcal{U}
and an integer
k>0
so that
F^{k}(U)=U
. Notice that
this implies that
U,\dots,F^{k-1}(U)
are all components of
\mathcal{V}
and that
F
cyclically permutes these components.

Let us return to the pathologies described in Theorem
3.1
.
Each of the box mappings
F_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, is not dynamically natural in the sense of Definition
4.1
: for the map
F_{i}
the respective condition
(i)
in that definition is violated. We see that

•

the box mapping
F_{1}
has no escaping points in
\mathcal{U}_{1}
;

•

the non-escaping set of
F_{2}
is equal to
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F_{2})
, and hence
F_{2}
provides an example of a box mapping with
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F_{2})
of non-zero Lebesgue measure;

•

the wandering disk constructed for
F_{3}
does not belong to
K_{\text{well-inside}}(F_{3})
,
and hence
K(F_{3})\setminus K_{\text{well-inside}}(F_{3})
is non-empty.

Moreover, the example constructed in Proposition
3.2
is also not a dynamically natural box mapping as it violates condition (
2
) of the definition of naturality.

The following lemma implies that no dynamically natural box mapping has the pathology described in Theorem
3.1
(
1
).

Lemma 4.2

(Absence of components with no escaping points)
.

If
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is a complex box mapping that satisfies the no permutation condition (Definition
4.1
(
1
)), then

•

J_{K}(F)\neq\emptyset
;

•

each component of
K(F)
is compact;

•

if
\mathcal{U}
has finitely many components, then
K(F)
and
J_{\mathcal{U}}(F)=J_{K}(F)
are compact.

Proof.

Take a nested sequence
P_{1}\supset P_{2}\supset\dots
of puzzle pieces. Then for each
i\geqslant 0
either
P_{i+1}=P_{i}
, or
P_{i+1}
is compactly contained in
P_{i}
. If
F
satisfies the no permutation condition, then necessarily
\bigcap P_{i}
is compactly contained in
\mathcal{V}
and so
J_{K}(F)\neq\emptyset
. The second and third assertions also follow.
∎

4.4.
Motivating the notion of ‘dynamically natural’ from Definition
4.1

First of all, condition (
1
) prohibits
F
to simply permute components of
\mathcal{U}
. Equivalently, it guarantees that each component of
\mathcal{U}
has escaping points under iteration of
F
. This is clearly something one should expect from a mapping induced by rational maps: for example, under this simple condition, as we saw in Lemma
4.2
, each component of
K(F)
is a compact set.

Furthermore, under this condition we can further motivate assumption (
2
), see the remark after Corollary
4.4
: for such complex box mappings a.e. point either converges to the boundary of
\mathcal{V}

or accumulates to the set of critical fibers. For any known complex box mapping which is induced by a rational map, the boundary of
\mathcal{V}
does not attract a set of positive Lebesgue measure, and therefore automatically
(
2
) holds. Thus it makes sense to assume (
2
).

Assumption (
3
) is imposed because one is usually only interested in points
that visit the ‘bounded part of the dynamics’ infinitely often.

In fact, as we will show in Proposition
4.5
, under the no permutation condition it is always possible to improve any box mapping to be dynamically natural by taking some further first return maps; this way of “fixing” general box mappings is enough in many applications.

Remark
.

In
[
ALdM
]
a different condition was used to rule out the pathologies we discussed in Section
3
. That paper is concerned with unicritical complex box mappings
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}
consists of a single domain. In
[
ALdM
]
it is assumed that
\mathcal{U}
is
thin
in
\mathcal{V}
where
\mathcal{U}
is called
thin
in
\mathcal{V}
if there exist
L,\epsilon>0
, such that for any point
z\in\mathcal{U}
there is a open topological disk
D\subset\mathcal{V}
of
z
with
L
-bounded geometry at
z
such that

\modulus(\mathcal{V}\setminus\overline{D})>\epsilon,
and
\area(D\setminus\mathcal{U})/\area(D)>\epsilon
.
See Section 5 and Appendix A of
[
ALdM
]
for results concerning this class of mappings. In particular, when
\mathcal{U}
is thin in
\mathcal{V}
we have that
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F)
has measure zero. On the other hand, the requirement that
\mathcal{U}
is thin is
\mathcal{V}
is
a stronger geometric requirement than
K(F)=K_{\text{well-inside}}(F)
(compare Figure
15
).

4.5.
An ergodicity property of box mappings

In this subsection, we study an ergodicity property in the sense of typical behavior of orbits of complex box mappings that are not necessarily dynamically natural, but satisfy condition the no permutation condition from Definition
4.1
. The results of this subsection will be used later in Section
4.6
, where we will show how to induce a dynamically natural box mapping starting from an arbitrary one, and in Section
12
, we will strengthen the results
in this subsection and study invariant line fields of box mappings.

Let us say that
\mathcal{F}
is a
critical fiber
of
F
if it is
the intersection of all the puzzle pieces containing a critical point.
We say that this set is a
recurrent critical fiber
if there exist iterates

n_{i}\to\infty
so that some (and therefore all) limit points of
F^{n_{i}}(\mathcal{F})
are contained in
\mathcal{F}
(we refer the reader to Section
5
for a detailed discussion on fibers and types of recurrence).

Lemma 4.3

(Ergodic property of general box mappings)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a complex box mapping that satisfies the no permutation condition. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the union of recurrent critical fibers of
F
. Define

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=\{z\in K(F)\colon F^{i}(z)\to\partial\mathcal{V}\mbox{ as }i\to\infty\},

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=\{z\in K(F)\colon F^{i_{j}}(z)\to\mathcal{C}\mbox{ for some sequence }i_{j}\to\infty\}

and

X=K(F)\setminus(X_{1}\cup X_{2}).

Then
X
is forward invariant. Moreover, if
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
is a forward invariant set of positive Lebesgue measure, then
there exists a puzzle piece
\mathcal{J}
of
F
so that
\area(\mathcal{J}\cap X^{\prime})=\area(\mathcal{J})
.

Proof.

That
X
is forward invariant is obvious.
For any integer
n\geqslant 0
, let
Y_{n}
be the union of all critical puzzle pieces of depth
n
for
F
containing a recurrent critical fiber.
Define
E_{n}\subset K(F)
to be the set of points whose forward orbits are disjoint from
Y_{n}
. By construction,
(E_{n})
is a growing sequence of forward invariant sets
and
X^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}E_{n}
. If
X^{\prime}
has positive Lebesgue measure, then there exists
n_{0}
so that
E_{n_{0}}\cap X^{\prime}
has positive Lebesgue measure. Let
z_{0}\in E_{n_{0}}\cap X^{\prime}
be a Lebesgue density point of this set.

Starting with
\mathcal{V}^{0}:=\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{U}^{0}:=\mathcal{U}
and
F_{0}:=F
, for each
n\geqslant 1
inductively define
F_{n}\colon\mathcal{U}^{n}\to\mathcal{V}^{n}
to be the first return map under
F_{n-1}
to the union
\mathcal{V}^{n}
of all critical components of
\mathcal{U}^{n-1}
. Note that
\Crit(F_{n})\subset\Crit(F)
and
K(F_{n})
contains all recurrent critical fibers of
F
(it is straightforward to see that fibers of
F_{n}
are also fibers of
F
for each
n\geqslant 0
).

We may assume that there exists
k\geqslant 0
so that the
F_{k}
-orbit of
z_{0}
visits non-critical components of
\mathcal{U}^{k}
infinitely many times. Indeed, otherwise the
F
-orbit of
z_{0}
accumulates at a recurrent critical fiber contrary to the definition of
E_{n_{0}}
. Fix this mapping
F_{k}
.

Case 1:
the orbit
z_{n}:=F_{k}^{n}(z_{0})
,
n=0,1,\ldots
visits non-critical components of
\mathcal{U}^{k}
infinitely many times, but only finitely many different ones. Write
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
for the union of these finitely many components and consider
K^{\prime}:=\{z\in E_{n_{0}}\colon F^{n}_{k}(z)\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\,\,\,\forall n\geqslant 0\}
. By Lemma
4.2
, the closure of
K^{\prime}
is a subset of
E_{n_{0}}
, and hence of
K(F)
. Therefore, there exists a point
y\in\omega(z_{0})\cap E_{n_{0}}
.
Let
\mathcal{J}_{0}\ni y
be a puzzle piece of depth
n_{0}
, and let
D\Subset\mathcal{J}_{0}
be another puzzle piece containing
y
of depth larger than
n_{0}
; such a compactly contained puzzle piece exists again because of Lemma
4.2
. Take the moments
n_{i}
so that
F_{k}^{n_{i}}(z_{0})\in\mathcal{J}_{0}
and let
A_{i}:=\Comp_{z_{0}}F_{k}^{-n_{i}}(\mathcal{J}_{0})
. Then
F_{k}^{n_{i}}\colon A_{i}\to\mathcal{J}_{0}
is a sequence of univalent maps with uniformly bounded distortion when restricted to
B_{i}:=\Comp_{z_{0}}F_{k}^{-n_{i}}(D)
.
Therefore
\modulus(A_{i}\setminus\overline{B}_{i})
is uniformly bounded from below.
We also have that
A_{i^{\prime}}
is contained in
B_{i}
for
i^{\prime}>i
sufficiently large.
It follows that
\diam B_{i}\to 0
as
i\to\infty
.
Thus, by the Lebesgue Density Theorem,
\area(D\cap E_{n_{0}})=\area(D)
, and hence
\area(D\cap X^{\prime})=\area(D)
as
X^{\prime}
is forward invariant. The claim of the lemma follows with
\mathcal{J}=D
.

Case 2:
the orbit
z_{n}
visits infinitely many different non-critical components of
F_{k}
. If
n_{s}
is the first moment the orbit
z_{n}
visits a new non-critical component
U_{s}
of
\mathcal{U}^{k}
, then the pullbacks of
U_{s}
under
F_{k}
back to
z_{0}
along the orbit hit each critical fiber at most once. Let
n_{s}^{\prime}>n_{s}
be the first subsequent time that the orbit
z_{n}
enters
a critical component of
\mathcal{U}^{k}
. If such an integer
n_{s}^{\prime}
exists, then we have that

F_{k}^{n_{s}^{\prime}}\colon\Comp_{z_{0}}F_{k}^{-n^{\prime}_{s}}(\mathcal{V}^{k})\to\mathcal{V}^{k}
has degree bounded independently of
s
.
We can proceed again as in Case 1 (there are only finitely many critical components of
\mathcal{U}^{k}
).
If
n_{s}^{\prime}
does not exist, then we have that
F_{k}^{n}\colon\Comp_{z_{0}}F_{k}^{-n}(\mathcal{V}^{k})\to\mathcal{V}^{k}
has uniformly bounded degree for all
n\geqslant n_{s}
. Since we assumed that
z_{0}
does not converge to the boundary,
there exists an accumulation point
y\in\mathcal{V}^{k}\cap\omega(z_{0})
with the property that

\mathcal{J}_{0}:=\Comp_{y}\mathcal{V}^{k}
is not a component of
\mathcal{U}^{k}
.
Note that
y\in\overline{\mathcal{U}^{k}}
, but
y
may or may not lie in
\mathcal{U}^{k}
. Each component of
\mathcal{U}^{k}\cap\mathcal{J}_{0}
is compactly contained in
\mathcal{J}_{0}
. This allows us to choose an open topological disk
D\Subset\mathcal{J}_{0}
so that
y\in D
and such that
D
contains at least one puzzle piece
\mathcal{J}
. We conclude similarly to the previous case that
\area(D\cap X^{\prime})=\area(D)
. Thus
\area(\mathcal{J}\cap X^{\prime})=\area(\mathcal{J})
as
\mathcal{J}\subset D
.
∎

Corollary 4.4

(Typical behavior of orbits)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a complex box mapping that satisfies the no permutation condition and such that
each puzzle piece of
F
either contains a point
w
so that
F^{n}(w)
is a critical point for some
n\geqslant 0
, or it contains an open set disjoint from
K(F)
(a
gap
).
Then for a.e.
z\in K(F)
either

(1)

F^{i}(z)\to\partial\mathcal{V}
as
i\to\infty
, or

(2)

the forward orbit of
z
accumulates to the fiber of a critical point.

Proof.

Let
X\subset K(F)
be as in the previous lemma. For each
n\geqslant 1
, let
X^{\prime}_{n}\subset X

be the set of points
z\in X
so that the orbit of
z
remains outside critical
puzzle pieces of depth
n
.
By the previous lemma if
X^{\prime}_{n}
has positive Lebesgue measure, then there exists a puzzle piece
\mathcal{J}
so that
\area(\mathcal{J}\cap X_{n}^{\prime})=\area(\mathcal{J})
. But, under the assumption of the corollary, either
\mathcal{J}
contains a gap, which is clearly impossible since
\area(\mathcal{J}\cap X_{n}^{\prime})=\area(\mathcal{J})
, or there exists a puzzle piece
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{n}\subset\mathcal{J}
which is mapped under some iterate of
F
to a critical piece
of depth
n
, which contradicts the definition of the set
X^{\prime}_{n}
since also
\area(\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{n}\cap X_{n}^{\prime})=\area(\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\prime})
. Hence the set
X^{\prime}_{n}

has zero Lebesgue measure. It follows that the set
X^{\prime}=\cup X^{\prime}_{n}

of points in
X
whose forward orbits stay outside
some
critical puzzle piece also has measure zero.
The corollary follows.
∎

Remark
.

For a polynomial or rational map, preimages of critical points are dense in the Julia set
(except if the map is of the form
z\mapsto z^{n}
or
z\mapsto z^{-n}
).
This means that if a box mapping is associated to a polynomial or rational map,
then the assumption of Corollary
4.4
is satisfied. The conclusion of that corollary motivates the assumption
\area(K_{\text{off-crit}}(F))=0
in the definition of dynamical naturality (Definition
4.1
).

4.6.
Improving complex box mappings by inducing.

We end this section by showing how to “fix” a general complex box mapping for it to become dynamically natural. In fact, every box mapping satisfying the no permutation condition from the definition of naturality, subject to some mild assumptions, induces a dynamically natural box mapping that captures all the interesting critical dynamics; working with such induced mappings is enough in many applications. The following lemma illustrates how it can be done in detail.

Proposition 4.5

(Inducing dynamically natural box mapping)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a box mapping that satisfies the no permutation condition.
Assume that each component of
\mathcal{V}
contains a critical point, or
contains an open set which is not in
\mathcal{U}
. Then we can induce a dynamically natural box mapping
from
F
in the following sense.

Take
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
to be a finite union of puzzle pieces of
F
so that
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
is a nice set compactly contained in
\mathcal{V}
,
and let
\Crit_{\hat{\mathcal{U}}}(F)\subset\Crit(F)
be a subset of critical points whose orbits visit
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
infinitely many times.
Then there exists an induced, dynamically natural box mapping
F_{1}\colon\mathcal{U}_{1}\to\mathcal{V}_{1}
such that
\Crit_{\hat{\mathcal{U}}}(F)\subset\Crit(F_{1})\subset\Crit(F)
.

Remark
.

A typical choice for
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
would be puzzle pieces of
\mathcal{U}
that contain critical
points and critical values of
F
.

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

Let us define two disjoint nice sets
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathcal{V}
as follows. For the first set, we let

\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{c\in\Crit(F)}\DomL_{c}\hat{\mathcal{U}},

where the union is taken over all points in
\Crit(F)
whose orbits intersect
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
. For the second set, we define
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime\prime}
to be the union of puzzle pieces of depth
n\geqslant 0
containing all critical points in
\Crit(F)\setminus\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}
(if this set is empty, we put
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
). Moreover, we can choose
n
sufficiently large so that
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{V}_{1}=\emptyset
and so that
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime\prime}
is compactly contained in
\mathcal{V}
; the latter can be achieved due to Lemma
4.2
.

Now let
\mathcal{V}_{1}:=\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, and let
F_{1}\colon\mathcal{U}_{1}\to\mathcal{V}_{1}

5.
Combinatorics and renormalization of box mappings

The combinatorics of critical points of complex box mappings are similar to those of complex polynomials. As is often the case in holomorphic dynamics, the tools used to study the orbit of a critical point depend on the combinatorial type of the critical point. In this section, we start by recalling the definitions of a fiber, of recurrent, persistently, and reluctantly recurrent critical points, and of a combinatorial equivalence of box mappings. We end this section by defining the notion of renormalization for complex box mappings.

In this section,
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is a complex box mapping, not necessarily dynamically natural, but that satisfies the no permutation condition from Definition

.

We now want to relate the existence of non-repelling periodic points for a complex box mapping to its renormalization.

(Non-repelling cycles imply renormalization)

5.4.

By Definition

6.
Statement of the QC-Rigidity Theorem and outline of the rest of the paper

We are now ready to state the main result from
[

]

Theorem 6.1

(Rigidity for complex box mappings)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a dynamically natural complex box mapping that is non-renormalizable. Then

(1)

each point
x\in K(F)
is contained in arbitrarily small puzzle pieces.

(2)

If
F
carries a measurable invariant line field supported on a forward invariant set

X\subseteq K(F)
of positive Lebesgue measure,
then

(a)

there exists a puzzle piece
\mathcal{J}
and a smooth foliation on
\mathcal{J}
with the property that
\area(X\cap\mathcal{J})=\area(\mathcal{J})
and the foliation is tangent to the invariant line field a.e. on
\mathcal{J}
;

(b)

the set
Y=\bigcup_{i\geqslant 0}F^{i}(\mathcal{J})
intersects the critical set of
F
and each such critical point is strictly preperiodic.

(3)

Suppose
\widetilde{F}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
is another dynamically natural complex box mapping for which there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
so that

(a)

\widetilde{F}
is combinatorially equivalent to
F
w.r.t.
H
, and so in particular
H(\mathcal{U})=\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
,

(b)

\widetilde{F}\circ H=H\circ F
on
\partial\mathcal{U}
, i.e.
F
is a conjugacy on
\partial U
,

Then
F
and
\widetilde{F}
are quasiconformally conjugate, and this conjugation agrees with
H
on
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{U}
.

In the following sections, we sketch the proof of Theorem
6.1
. Our aim is to make it more accessible to a wider audience.

.

7.
Complex bounds, the Enhanced Nest and the Covering Lemma

Complex bounds, also known as
a priori
bounds for a complex box mapping
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
concern uniform geometric bounds on arbitrarily small, combinatorially defined puzzle pieces. We will focus on two types of geometric control:
moduli

It is not possible to control the geometry of every puzzle piece, and to obtain complex bounds, one must restrict ones attention to a chosen, combinatorially defined, nest of critical puzzle pieces, and show that one has complex bounds for the puzzle pieces in this nest. One example of a nest of puzzle pieces is the principal nest. In this section, we will discuss some of the geometric properties of the principal nest. We will also describe the Enhanced Nest, which was introduced in
[
KSS1
]
. It is combinatorially more difficult to define than the principal nest and it is only defined around persistently recurrent critical points, but it also provides much better geometric control on the puzzle pieces than the principal nest, and it is especially useful for mappings with more than one critical point.

7.2.
The principal nest

Let
c\in\Crit(F)
be a critical point. The
principal nest
(around
c
) is a nested collection of puzzle pieces
V^{0}\supset V^{1}\supset V^{2}\supset\ldots
such that

c\in V^{0},\text{ and }V^{i+1}=\DomE_{c}V^{i}\text{ for every integer }i\geqslant 0.

Note that we can start the principal nest from an arbitrary strictly nice puzzle piece
V^{0}
containing
c
. Clearly, if
c
is combinatorially non-recurrent, then the principal nest is finite. Otherwise, the nest consists of infinitely many puzzle pieces. Moreover, each
V^{n}
contains
\fib(c)
, and hence all the critical points in
\fib(c)
.

Associated to such the principal nest we have a sequence of return times
(p_{n})
defined as
V^{n+1}=\Comp_{c}F^{-p_{n}}(V^{n})
;
p_{n}
is the first time the orbit of
c
returns back to
V^{n}
. Since the mapping from
V^{n+1}
to
V^{n}
is a first return mapping, and puzzle pieces are nice sets, the sets

F^{j}(V^{n+1}),F^{j^{\prime}}(V^{n+1})
are pairwise disjoint for
0<j<j^{\prime}\leqslant p_{n}
. Thus all the maps
F^{p_{n}}\colon V^{n+1}\to V^{n}
have uniformly bounded degrees.

7.2.1.
Geometry of the principal nest

Let us describe some of the results concerning the geometry of the principal nest for unicritical mappings.
Suppose that
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is a dynamically natural complex box mapping with the properties that

•

\mathcal{V}
consists of exactly one component,

•

there exists exactly one component
U_{0}
of
\mathcal{U}
that is mapped as a degree
d

\mathcal{V}
which is ramified at exactly one point, and

•

every component
U

\mathcal{U}
,
U\neq U_{0}
is mapped diffeomorphically onto
\mathcal{V}
.

Let
V^{0}=\mathcal{V}
and

V^{0}\supset V^{1}\supset V^{2}\supset\dots

be the principal nest about the critical point
c

There are two combinatorially distinct types of returns
F^{p_{n}}\colon V^{n+1}\to V^{n}
:

and

. We say that the return mapping from
V^{n+1}
to

is

. Otherwise the return is called

For quadratic mappings, the following result is fundamental.

Theorem 7.2
.

[

,

, Decay of Geometry]

Let
n_{k}
denote the subsequence of levels in the principal nest so that
F^{p_{n_{k}-1}}\colon V^{n_{k}}\to V^{n_{k}-1}
is a non-central return. There exists a constant
C>0
so that

\modulus\left(V^{n_{k}}\setminus\overline{V}^{n_{k}+1}\right)>Ck.

∎

For example, by the Koebe Distortion Theorem this result implies that the landing maps to
V^{n_{k}+1}
become almost linear as

. This result played a crucial role in the proofs of quasiconformal rigidity and density of hyperbolicity in the quadratic family.

For higher degree maps, even cubic mappings with two non-degenerate critical points and unicritical cubic mappings, Decay of Geometry does not hold; however, the geometry is bounded from below. The following result required new analytic tools — the Quasiadditivity Law and Covering Lemma — of
[

]

Theorem 7.3

(
[

,

]
)
.

Let
n_{k}
denote the subsequence of levels in the principal nest so that
F^{p_{n_{k}-1}}\colon V^{n_{k}}\to V^{n_{k}-1}
is a non-central return. There exists a constant

\modulus\left(V^{n_{k}}\setminus\overline{V}^{n_{k}+1}\right)>\delta.

∎

Even for unicritical mappings, this difference makes the study of higher degree mappings quite a bit different from the study of unicritical mappings with a critical point of degree 2.

In general, there is no control of the moduli of the annuli
V^{n}\setminus\overline{V}^{n+1}
when the returns are central.
Observe that when the return mapping from
V^{n+1}
to

Consequently, when there is a long cascade of central returns; that is, when

For unicritical mappings, the complex

7.3.
Inducing persistently recurrent box mappings

Recall that a box mapping
F^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is
induced
from

if
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
and

consist of puzzle pieces of

and the branches of

are the iterates of some branches of
F

Definition 7.4

(Persistently recurrent box mapping)
.

We say that a non-renormalizable dynamically natural box mapping
F
is
persistently recurrent
if every critical point of
F
is persistently recurrent and its orbit accumulates at every critical fiber of
F
. The latter is equivalent to the property that
\Crit(F)=[c]
for some, and hence for every
c\in\Crit(F)
(see notation in Section
5.2
).

The next lemma shows that starting from an arbitrary box mapping
F
(non-renormalizable and dynamically natural) and a persistently recurrent critical point
c\in\Crit(F)
we can extract an induced box mapping
F^{\prime}
that is persistently recurrent and contains
c
, and hence
\fib(c)
, in its non-escaping set. More precisely:

Lemma 7.5

(Inducing persistently recurrent box mappings)
.

Let
F
be a dynamically natural box mapping that is non-renormalizable, and
c\in\Crit(F)
be a persistently recurrent critical point of
F
. Then there exists an induced persistently recurrent box mapping
F^{\prime}
such that
c\in\Crit(F^{\prime})\subset\Crit(F)
.

Proof.

By definition of persistent recurrence, the set
[c]
consists only of persistently recurrent critical points. Choose an integer
m
large enough so that each critical puzzle piece of depth at least
m
contains a single critical fiber. Let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{n}
be the union of all puzzle pieces of
F
of depth
n\geqslant m
intersecting
[c]
; this is a finite union of critical puzzle pieces containing only persistently recurrent critical fibers. Let
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{n}
be the union of the components of
\Dom(\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{n})
intersecting
[c]
, and define a box mapping
F^{\prime}_{n}\colon\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{n}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{n}
to be the restriction to
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime}
of the first return map to
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{\prime}
under
F
.

Let us show that there exists an
n(\geqslant m)
such that the only critical points of
F^{\prime}_{n}
are the ones in
[c]
. This will be our desired box mapping
F^{\prime}
.

Assume the contrary, and let
U_{n}\subset\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{n}
and
V_{n}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{n}
be a pair of components containing, respectively,
c^{\prime}\in[c]
and
c^{\prime\prime}\in[c]
, and such that
\Crit(F^{\prime})\cap U_{n}\not\subset[c]
. This means that if
F^{s}\colon U_{n}\to V_{n}
,
s=s(n)
is the corresponding branch of the first return map, then one of the puzzle pieces in the sequence
F(U_{n}),F^{2}(U_{n}),\ldots,F^{s-1}(U_{n})
contains

which is either a non-persistently recurrent critical point or a persistently recurrent critical point with

,

must all visit arbitrarily deep puzzle pieces around each other. In particular,

must intersect

cannot be reluctantly recurrent: the critical point

will then have only finitely many children, contrary to the definition of reluctant recurrence. We arrived at a contradiction.
∎

8.1.

The following lemma is well-known (see for example

Lemma 8.1

(Sewing Lemma)

Let

Then the map

\Phi(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\varphi_{1}(z)\quad\text{ if }z\in E\\
&\varphi_{2}(z)\quad\text{ if }z\in U\setminus E\\
\end{aligned}\right.

. Moreover,

Lemma 8.2

Let

\Phi_{n}
.

In this way, we obtained a sequence
(\Phi_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
of
\max(K,K^{\prime})
-quasiconformal maps on
V
. By compactness of quasiconformal maps of uniformly bounded dilatation, this sequence has a sub-sequential limit. Moreover, since the maps in the sequence agree on more and more disks
B_{i}
, all sub-sequential limits are the same and equal to the map
\Phi
. The conclusion of the lemma follows.
∎

8.2.
Lifts of combinatorial equivalence and the boundary marking

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
and
\widetilde{F}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be a pair of complex box mappings that are combinatorially equivalent in the sense of Definition
5.4
w.r.t. to some
K
-quasiconformal
homeomorphism
H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
such that
H(\mathcal{U})=\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
and
\widetilde{F}\circ H=H\circ F
on
\partial U
for each component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
. By Remark
4
after Theorem
6.1
, the latter condition means that if
\hat{H}\colon\cl\mathcal{V}\to\cl\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
is a continuous homeomorphic extension of
H
to the closures, then

\hat{\widetilde{F}}\circ\hat{H}|_{\partial U}=\hat{H}\circ\hat{F}|_{\partial U}

for each component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
; here
\hat{F}
and
\hat{\widetilde{F}}
are the corresponding homeomorphic extensions of
F
and
\widetilde{F}
to the closures. Let
P
and
\widetilde{P}
be a pair of corresponding puzzle pieces for
F
and
\widetilde{F}
in the sense of Section
5.4
.

Definition 8.3

(Boundary marking)
.

We say that a homeomorphism
h\colon P\to\widetilde{P}

respects the boundary marking (induced by
H
)
if
h
extends to a homeomorphism
\hat{h}
between
\cl P
and
\cl\widetilde{P}
and
\hat{h}|_{\partial P}
agrees with the corresponding lift of
\hat{H}
; that is, if
k\geqslant 0
is the depth of
P
, then

\hat{\widetilde{F}}{}^{k}\circ\hat{h}|_{\partial P}=\hat{H}\circ\hat{F}^{k}|_{\partial P}.

Let
K
be the quasiconformal dilatation of
H
. The definition of combinatorial equivalence then gives us a
K
-qc homeomorphism that respects the boundary marking between any two corresponding puzzle pieces of depths
0
and
1
. The next lemma is a simple observation that shows that such
H
gives rise to a
K^{\prime}
-qc homeomorphism that respects the boundary marking for a pair of corresponding arbitrary deep puzzle pieces; of course,
K^{\prime}\geqslant K
can be arbitrarily large.

Lemma 8.4

(Starting qc maps respecting boundary)
.

Let
P
,
\widetilde{P}
be a pair of corresponding puzzle pieces of depth
k\geqslant 0
for
F
,
\widetilde{F}
. Then there exists a
K^{\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
h\colon P\to\widetilde{P}
that respects the boundary marking induced by
H
.

Proof.

Let
V
be the component of
\mathcal{V}
so that
F^{k}(P)=V
. If
F^{k}\colon P\to V
is a conformal isomorphism, then, by combinatorial equivalence (Definition
5.4
),
\widetilde{F}^{k}\colon\widetilde{P}\to\widetilde{V}
is also a conformal isomorphism. In this situation, we can define
h:=\widetilde{F}^{-k}\circ H\circ F^{k}
for the branch
\widetilde{F}^{-k}
that maps
\widetilde{V}
over
\widetilde{P}
. Clearly,
h
is a
K
-qc map that respects the boundary marking.

Suppose
v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\in V
are the critical values of the map
F^{k}\colon P\to V
. By combinatorial equivalence, the map
\widetilde{F}^{k}\colon\widetilde{P}\to\widetilde{V}
must have the same number of critical values with the same branching properties (i.e. if
c\in P
is a critical point of local degree
s\geqslant 2
and so that
F^{k}(c)=v_{i}
, then the corresponding critical point
\widetilde{c}
must have local degree
s
and is mapped by
\widetilde{F}^{k}
to the corresponding critical value
\widetilde{v}_{i}
).

Choose an arbitrary smooth diffeomorphism
L\colon V\to V
so that
L(v_{i})=H^{-1}(\widetilde{v}_{i})
and so that
L=\text{id}
in some small neighborhood of
\partial V
. Then
H\circ L\colon V\to\widetilde{V}
is a
K^{\prime}
-qc homeomorphism that maps the critical values of
F^{k}|_{P}
to the critical values of
\widetilde{F}^{k}|_{\widetilde{P}}
and respects the boundary marking. Such homeomorphism can be now lifted as above: define
h=\widetilde{F}^{-k}\circ H\circ L\circ F^{k}
(for appropriate choices of inverse branches). By construction,
h
is
K^{\prime}
-qc homeomorphism between
P
and
\widetilde{P}
that respects the boundary marking, as required.
∎

8.3.
The Spreading Principle

The Spreading Principle is a dynamical tool which makes it possible to construct quasiconformal conjugacies outside of some puzzle neighborhood
\mathcal{Y}
of
\Crit(F)
between two mappings
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
and
\widetilde{F}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
provided that one has an initial quasiconformal mapping
H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
which is a combinatorial equivalence and a conjugacy on
\partial\mathcal{U}
between
F
and
\widetilde{F}
, and a quasiconformal mapping on each component
P
of
\mathcal{Y}
to the corresponding component
\widetilde{P}
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
that respects the boundary marking (in the sense of Definition
8.3
).

In the context of polynomials, the Spreading Principle was proven in
[
KSS1
, Section 5.3, page 769]
and
[
KvS
]
. Below we state and prove the corresponding version for dynamically natural box mappings. Our statement is slightly more general than the one in
[
KSS1
,
KvS
]
as it allows to take into account some pre-existing conjugacy on some part of the postcritical set.

Theorem 8.5

(Spreading Principle)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
and
\widetilde{F}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be a pair of dynamically natural complex box mappings and
H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism that

•

provides a combinatorial equivalence between
F
and
\widetilde{F}
, is a conjugacy on the boundary of
\mathcal{U}
, and moreover, it

•

conjugates
F
to
\widetilde{F}
on
X_{S}:=\{F^{i}(\fib(c))\colon c\in S,i\geqslant 0\}
for some subset
S\subset\Crit(F)
(possibly empty).

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a nice puzzle neighborhood of
\Crit(F)\setminus S
such that
X_{S}\cap\mathcal{Y}=\emptyset
, and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
be the corresponding neighborhood of
\Crit(\widetilde{F})\setminus\widetilde{S}
for
\widetilde{F}
. Further suppose that there exists a
K^{\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
that respects the boundary marking induced by
H
. Then
\varphi
extends to a
\max(K,K^{\prime})
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
such that:

(1)

\Phi=\varphi
on
\mathcal{Y}
;

(2)

for each
z\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{Y}
,

\widetilde{F}\circ\Phi(z)=\Phi\circ F(z);

(3)

\Phi|_{\mathcal{V}\setminus\DomL(\mathcal{Y})}
is
K
-quasiconformal;

(4)

\Phi(P)=\widetilde{P}
for every puzzle piece
P
that is not contained in
\DomL(\mathcal{Y})
,
and
\Phi\colon P\rightarrow\widetilde{P}
respects the boundary marking induced by
H
.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the collection of puzzle pieces of depth
n\geqslant 0
for
F
.

Since
X_{S}\cap\mathcal{Y}=\emptyset
and
X_{S}
is forward invariant under
F
, it follows that
X_{S}\cap\DomL(\mathcal{Y})=\emptyset
; the same is true for the corresponding sets
\widetilde{X}_{\widetilde{S}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
. Moreover, there exists a puzzle neighborhood
\mathcal{X}
of
S
consisting of puzzle pieces of the same depth, say
m
, with the property that
m
is larger than the depths of the components of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}\cap\mathcal{Y}=\emptyset
. Note that
\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{X}
is a nice set by construction.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a collection of all critical puzzle pieces that are not contained in
\mathcal{Y}
but do intersect
\Crit(F)\setminus S
. This is a finite set. Using Lemma
8.4
, for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
let us pick a quasiconformal map
h_{C}\colon C\to\widetilde{C}
that respects the boundary marking induced by
H
; suppose
K_{0}
is the maximum over the quasiconformal dilatations of
h_{C}
for
C\in\mathcal{C}
.

We now want to construct a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\psi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
that respects the boundary marking. Let
Q
be a component of
\mathcal{X}
, and
k\geqslant 0
be minimal so that
F^{k}(Q)
either is a component of
\mathcal{V}
, or is in
\mathcal{C}
. Now define a map
\psi_{Q}\colon Q\to\widetilde{Q}
by the formula
\widetilde{F}^{k}\circ\psi_{Q}=f\circ F^{k}
, where

(8.1)

f=\left\{\begin{aligned} &H&\text{ if }&F^{k}(Q)\text{ is a component of }\mathcal{V},\\
&h_{F^{k}(Q)}&\text{ if }&F^{k}(Q)\in\mathcal{C}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Since none of the puzzle pieces in the sequence
Q,F(Q),\ldots,F^{k}(Q)
intersects
\mathcal{Y}
(as
X_{S}\cap\mathcal{Y}=\emptyset
), and
F
is conjugate to
\widetilde{F}
on
X_{S}
, we conclude that the map
\psi_{Q}
is a well-defined quasiconformal homeomorphism with dilatation
\max(K,K_{0})
. By construction,
\psi_{Q}
respects the boundary marking induced by
H
. We define
\psi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
component-wise using the maps
\psi_{Q}
. Note that the dilatation of
\psi
does not depend on the choice of the puzzle neighborhood
\mathcal{X}
, and as the depth of the neighborhood
\mathcal{X}
tends to
\infty
, the set where the dilatation of
\psi
is equal to
K_{0}
shrinks to zero (in measure).

Finally, let us adjust
\varphi
,
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
as follows. Set
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{X}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
, and define

\varphi^{\prime}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\varphi(z)&\text{ if }&z\in\mathcal{Y},\\
&\psi(z)&\text{ if }&z\in\mathcal{X}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Defined this way,
\varphi^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}^{\prime}
is a
K^{\prime\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism between nice puzzle neighborhoods of
\Crit(F)
and
\Crit(\widetilde{F})
, with
K^{\prime\prime}:=\max(K,K^{\prime},K_{0})
, and this homeomorphism respects the boundary marking.

Let
Q
be a component of
\DomL(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
, and
k\geqslant 0
be the landing time of the orbit of
Q
to
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. Since
\Crit(F)\subset\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
, the map
F^{k}\colon Q\to F^{k}(Q)
is a conformal isomorphism. Therefore, we can pull back
\varphi^{\prime}
and define a
K^{\prime\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\varphi^{\prime}_{Q}\colon Q\to\widetilde{Q}
by the formula
\widetilde{F}^{k}\circ\varphi^{\prime}_{Q}=\varphi^{\prime}\circ F^{k}
. Since
\varphi^{\prime}
respects the boundary marking induced by
H
, so does
\varphi^{\prime}_{Q}
.

Let us now inductively define a nested sequence of sets
Y_{0}\supset Y_{1}\supset Y_{2}\ldots
so that:

•

Y_{0}
is the union of all puzzle pieces in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
;

•

Y_{n+1}
is the subset of
Y_{n}
consisting of puzzle pieces of depth
n+1
that are not components of
\DomL(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
.

For each puzzle piece
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
that is contained in
Y_{n}
, it follows that none of the pieces in the orbit
Q,F(Q),\ldots
can lie in
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
(because otherwise
Q
would lie in
Y_{\ell-1}\setminus Y_{\ell}
for some
\ell\leqslant n
). Therefore, there exists the minimal
k=k(Q)\geqslant 0
so that
F^{k}(Q)
either is in
\mathcal{C}
, or is a component of
\mathcal{V}
. By a similar reasoning as above, in both cases the map
F^{k}\colon Q\to F^{k}(Q)
allows to pull back homeomorphisms consistently and to define a homeomorphism
H_{Q}\colon Q\to\widetilde{Q}
by the formula
\widetilde{F}^{k}\circ H_{Q}=f\circ F^{k}
, where
f
is as in (
8.1
). Defined this way, the map
H_{Q}
is
\max(K,K_{0})
-quasiconformal map that respects the boundary marking induced by
H
.

We proceed by inductively defining a sequence of homeomorphisms
(\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
,
\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
as follows. Set
\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{0}=H
, and for each
n\geqslant 0
define

\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{n+1}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{n}(z)\quad\text{ if }z\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{n+1},\\
&H_{Q}(z)\quad\text{ if }z\in Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\text{ and }Q\subset Y_{n+1},\\
&\varphi^{\prime}_{Q}(z)\quad\text{ if }z\in Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\text{ and }Q\not\subset Y_{n+1}.\end{aligned}\right.

By the Gluing Lemma (Lemma
8.2
), the map
\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
is a
K^{\prime\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism for each
n\geqslant 0
. Note that the sequence
(\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{n})
eventually stabilizes on
\mathcal{V}\setminus\bigcap_{n}Y_{n}
. The set
E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}):=\bigcap_{n}Y_{n}
consists of points in
\mathcal{V}
which are never mapped into
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
, and hence
E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})\subset K_{\text{off-crit}}(F)
. Since
F
is dynamically natural,
E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
has zero Lebesgue measure. Hence
\mathcal{V}\setminus E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
is a dense set. We conclude that the sequence
(\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}_{n})
converges the limiting
K^{\prime\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\Phi^{\mathcal{X}}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
. This map depends on the depth of
\mathcal{X}
. Letting this depth go to
\infty
, in the limit we obtain the required quasiconformal map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
with quasiconformal dilatation equal to
\max(K,K^{\prime})
. For this homeomorphism the properties (
1
), (
2
) and (
4
) follow directly from the construction, while property (
3
) follows from the facts that
\area(E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}))=0
and that
H
conjugates
F
and
\widetilde{F}
on
\bigcup_{c\in S}\fib(c)
.
∎

\eta

\modulus(\Omega\setminus\cl(P))\geqslant\eta,\quad\modulus(\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\cl(\varphi(P)))\geqslant\eta;

(2)

|\bar{\partial}\varphi|\leqslant k|\partial\varphi|
holds almost everywhere on
\Omega\setminus X
.

Then there exists a
K
-quasiconformal map
\psi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
such that

\psi=\varphi
on
\partial\Omega
. ∎

9.
Sketch of the proof of the QC-Rigidity Theorem

In this section we will explain how to combine shrinking of puzzle pieces (Theorem
6.1
(
1
)), complex bounds (Theorem
7.1
), the QC-Criterion (Theorem
8.6
) and the Spreading Principle (Theorem
8.5
) to prove qc rigidity for non-renormalizable dynamically natural complex box mappings. The argument follows that of
[
KSS1
, Section 6]
.

Complex bounds from Theorem
7.1
imply the following lemma, which gives us that for any point
z
, the orbit of which accumulates on a critical fiber, there are arbitrarily deep puzzle pieces containing
z
that satisfy the geometric hypotheses of the QC-Criterion. The details are verbatim as in
[
KSS1
, Corollary 6.3]
(simplified in several places as we do not have to deal with (eventually) renormalizable critical points).

Lemma 9.1
.

[
KSS1
, Corollary 6.3 (Geometric control for landing domains)]

Let
F
be a non-renormalizable dynamically natural complex box mapping. Then for every integer
n\geqslant 0
there exists a nice puzzle neighborhood
W
of
\CritP(F)
such that for every component
U
of
\DomE(W)
the following hold:

•

U
has
\eta
-bounded geometry;

•

the depth of
U
is larger than
n
, and if
P
is the puzzle piece of depth
n
containing
U
, then
\modulus(P\setminus\overline{U})\geqslant\eta
,

where
\eta>0
is a constant independent of
n
.

Furthermore, if
\widetilde{F}
is another non-renormalizable dynamically natural box mapping combinatorially equivalent to
F
, then taking
\eta
smaller if necessary, the same claims as above hold for
\widetilde{F}
and the corresponding objects with tilde.∎

The following lemma implies that we can modify a combinatorial equivalence
H
from Theorem
6.1
(
3
) so that it becomes a conjugacy on the orbits of critical points in
\Crit(F)\setminus\CritP(F)
(if this set is not empty).

Lemma 9.2

(Making
H
partial conjugacy)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
and
\widetilde{F}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be non-renormalizable dynamically natural box mappings that are combinatorially equivalent with respect to some
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
that satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
6.1
(
3
). Suppose
\Crit(F)\setminus\CritP(F)\neq\emptyset
. Then there exists a
K^{\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
H^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
so that

•

H^{\prime}|_{\partial\mathcal{U}}=H|_{\partial\mathcal{U}}
;

•

H^{\prime}
is a combinatorial equivalence between
F
and
\widetilde{F}
;

•

\widetilde{F}\circ H^{\prime}=H^{\prime}\circ F
on
\partial\mathcal{U}
;

•

H^{\prime}
conjugates
F
to
\widetilde{F}
on
\left\{F^{i}(c)\colon c\in\Crit(F)\setminus\CritP(F),i\geqslant 0\right\}
.

Proof.

Recall that
S:=\Crit(F)\setminus\CritP(F)
consists of non-recurrent critical points whose orbits do not accumulate on other critical points (we can talk about points rather than fibers since by Theorem
7.1
(
1
) all critical fibers are trivial). Therefore, we can find a sufficiently deep puzzle neighborhood
\mathcal{U}_{0}
of
\Crit(F)
consisting of puzzle pieces of the same depth, say
n_{0}\geqslant 0
, so that the orbit of
F(S)
is disjoint from
\mathcal{U}_{0}
, and hence from
\DomL(\mathcal{U}_{0})
. For simplicity we assume that
F(S)
contains no critical points; otherwise, the modification of the argument below is straightforward. By increasing
n_{0}
we can also assume that each component of
\mathcal{U}_{0}
contains only one critical point.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathcal{U}_{0}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{0}
be a
K^{\prime}
-quasiconformal map that respects the boundary marking induced by
H
constructed by Lemma
8.4
. Clearly,
K^{\prime}\geqslant K
and can be arbitrarily large. Let
H_{0}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be the
K^{\prime}
-qc extension of
\varphi
by the Spreading Principle (Theorem
8.5
). If
P
is a sufficiently small puzzle piece around a point
x\in\orb(F(S))
, then
P
is not contained in
\DomL(\mathcal{U}_{0})
by our choices, and thus by property (
4
) of the Spreading Principle,
H_{0}(P)=\widetilde{P}
. Since puzzle pieces shrink to points (Theorem
6.1
(
1
)), by letting the depth of such
P
’s go to zero we conclude that
H_{0}(F^{k}(c))=\widetilde{F}^{k}(\widetilde{c})
for every
c\in S
and
k\geqslant 1
.

Now define
H^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
by
H^{\prime}=\widetilde{F}^{-1}\circ H_{0}\circ F
on the components of
\mathcal{U}_{0}
containing the points of
S
and
H^{\prime}=H_{0}
elsewhere. As
H_{0}(F^{k}(c))=\widetilde{F}^{k}(\widetilde{c})
for all
k\geqslant 1
and
c\in S
, the map

H^{\prime}
is well-defined and it is continuous because
\widetilde{F}\circ H_{0}=H_{0}\circ F
on
\partial\mathcal{U}_{0}
. It follows that
H^{\prime}
is the required
K^{\prime}
-qc map.
∎

Lemmas
9.2
,
9.1
and the QC-Criterion (Theorem

) imply the following proposition (which is similar to the claim in
[
KSS1
, Section 6.4]
). The conclusion of this proposition will then be an input into the Spreading Principle later in the proof of qc rigidity of complex box mappings.

Proposition 9.3

(Uniform control of dilatation)
.

Let
F
and
\widetilde{F}
be non-renormalizable dynamically natural box mappings that are combinatorially equivalent with respect to some homeomorphism
H
that satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
6.1
(
3
).

Then there exists
K>0
such that for every critical point
c\in\Crit(F)
and the corresponding critical point
\widetilde{c}\in\Crit(\widetilde{F})
, and every

there exists a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism respecting the boundary marking between the corresponding critical puzzle pieces of depth
n
containing
c
and
\widetilde{c}
.

Proof.

Suppose
F
and
\widetilde{F}
are combinatorially equivalent with respect to a
k
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
H
. By Lemma
9.2
, we can assume that
H
is a conjugacy between
F
and
\widetilde{F}
on the orbits of points in
\Crit(F)\setminus\CritP(F)
.

Let
Q
,
\widetilde{Q}
be the pair of corresponding puzzle pieces of depth
n
containing
c
, respectively
\widetilde{c}
. If
c\in\Crit(F)\setminus\CritP(F)
, then the required homeomorphism between

and
\widetilde{Q}

As was discussed in Section

12.
Ergodicity properties and invariant line fields

In this section, we present the proof of Theorem

(

By Theorem

12.2.
Line fields

Recall that for a holomorphic map

and a set

in the domain of

on

is the assignment

of a real line through each point

so that the slope of

is a measurable function of

if

and the differential

transforms the line
\mu(z)
at

to the line
\mu(f(z))
at
f(z)
.
The assumption that the line field is measurable, implies that Lebesgue almost every

is a point
of almost continuity: for any
\epsilon>0

\frac{\area(\{z\in D(x,r):|\mu(z)-\mu(x)|\geqslant\epsilon\})}{\area(D(x,r))}\to 0

as

(here

stands for the disk of radius

centered at

). Alternatively one can define

as a measurable

\mu(z)d\bar{z}/dz
with

. We say that the line field

on an open set

if
\mu=\bar{\varphi}/|\varphi|
a.e. on

\varphi=\varphi(z)dz^{2}
is a holomorphic quadratic differential on

. We say that the line field is

if
\varphi
has no zeroes. In that case
\mu

induces a foliation on

given by trajectories of
\varphi
.

The absence of measurable line fields was proved by McMullen for infinitely renormalizable quadratic polynomials with complex bounds

[

, in
[

]
for covering maps of the circle

[

]

for non-renormalizable polynomials
[

]
and for certain real transcendental maps
[

]
.

A.1.
First entry and return constructions

One of the most fundamental constructions to study recurrent orbits is that of
first return
and
first entry
. The next three lemmas explain in steps the construction and its properties.

Lemma A.1

(Orbits of entry components are disjoint)
.

Let
f\colon U\to V
,
U\subset V
be a map between two sets. Pick a set
A\subset U
and for each point
x\in U
so that the orbit of
x
under
f
intersects
A
, define
k_{x}
to be the
entry time
in
A
, i.e. the smallest integer
k\geqslant 1
such that
f^{k}(x)\in A
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
D_{n}:=\{x\in U\colon k_{x}=n\}
. Then the sets

D_{n},f(D_{n}),\ldots,f^{n-1}(D_{n})

are pairwise disjoint for every
n
.

Proof.

Immediately from the definition,
f^{p}(D_{n})\subseteq D_{n-p}
and
D_{n}\cap D_{m}=\emptyset
for every
p<n
and
m\neq n
. Therefore, if
f^{p}(D_{n})\cap f^{s}(D_{n})\neq\emptyset
for some
p\neq s
, then
D_{n-p}\cap D_{n-s}\neq\emptyset
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Boundary maps to boundary for nice sets)
.

If in the previous lemma we additionally assume that
f
is continuous and
A
is a nice open set, then every connected component of
D_{n}
:

•

is nice and open;

•

is either contained in
A
, or is disjoint from
A
;

•

after exactly
n
steps maps over a connected component of
A
.

Proof.

For simplicity assume that
D_{n}
and

are connected. Then
D_{n}
is nice since
A
is nice. By continuity of

,
D_{n}
must be open. Because

. This yields the second property. The last property follows since

, again by continuity.
∎

Recall the definitions of the first entry, landing and return maps from Section

(Box mappings from strictly nice unions)

Let

Lemma A.4

Remark
.

Note that

A.2.
Koebe Distortion Theorem, bounded geometry and qc mappings

Theorem A.6

(Invariant Koebe Distortion Theorem)
.

Let
\Delta\subset D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a pair of open topological disks with
\modulus(D\setminus\overline{\Delta})\geqslant\mu>0
. Let
f\colon D\to\mathbb{C}
be a univalent map. Then there is a constant
C(\mu)
such that the
distortion
of
f
on
\Delta
is bounded by
C(\mu)
:

\frac{|f^{\prime}(x)|}{|f^{\prime}(y)|}\leqslant C(\mu)\quad\text{ for every }\quad x,y\in\Delta.

Moreover,
C(\mu)\to 1
as
\mu\to\infty
. ∎

Lemma A.7

(Annulus pull-back and geometry control under branched covering)
.

Let
f\colon U^{\prime}\to V^{\prime}
be a branched covering of degree at most
D
between open topological disks, and suppose
V\subset V^{\prime}
is a topological disk with
\modulus(V^{\prime}\setminus\overline{V})\geqslant\mu>0
. Let
U\subset U^{\prime}
be a component of
f^{-1}(V)
. Then

\modulus(U^{\prime}\setminus\overline{U})\geqslant\frac{\mu}{D}.

If we additionally assume that
V
has
\eta
-bounded geometry, then
U
has
\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}(\eta,\mu,D)
-bounded geometry. ∎

There are several equivalent definitions for the notion of quasiconformal homeomorphism.
The one which seems to be the most natural in our context is the one
given by Heinonen and Koskela
[
HK
]
:

Definition A.8
.

For open domains
\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{C}
and
K\geqslant 1
, a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
is called

K
-quasiconformal
(or
K
-qc
for short) if for each
x\in\Omega
,

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\sup_{|y-x|=r}|\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)|}{\inf_{|y-x|=r}|\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)|}\leqslant K<\infty.

This definition also motivates why the notion of bounded geometry
appears in the QC-Criterion (Theorem
8.6
).
