Truncation in Differential Hahn Fields
Santiago Camacho

Abstract
Being closed under truncation for subsets of generalized series fields is a robust property in the sense that it is preserved under various algebraic and transcendental extension procedures. Nevertheless, in Chapter 4 of this dissertation we show that generalized series fields with truncation as an extra primitive yields undecidability in several settings. Our main results, however, concern the robustness of being truncation closed in generalized series fields equipped with a derivation, and under extension procedures that involve this derivation. In the last chapter we study this in the ambient field 𝕋 \mathbb{T} of logarithmic-exponential transseries. It leads there to a theorem saying that under a natural “splitting” condition the Liouville closure of a truncation closed differential subfield of 𝕋 \mathbb{T} is again truncation closed.

Chapter 1
Introduction

Consider the Taylor expansion

\log(1-t)\ =\ -t-\frac{t^{2}}{2}-\frac{t^{3}}{3}-\frac{t^{4}}{4}-\cdots\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{-t^{n}}{n}.

In a typical calculus course we use the Taylor polynomials
0
,
-t
,
-t-t^{2}/2,\ldots
to approximate the value of
\log(1-t)
for
t
close to zero. These Taylor polynomials correspond to truncations of the Taylor series for
\log(1-t)
. Intuitively speaking, truncations of a series correspond to omitting smaller terms, and can be used to approximate the original series. There is an interest in embedding various classes of functions into series rings and fields.
A classical instance is given by associating Taylor series to analytic functions.
In the case of complex meromorphic functions the association is made with Laurent series, and the corresponding truncations would correspond to Laurent polynomials, that is,
elements of
\mathbb{C}[t,t^{-1}]
. For other classes of functions, Laurent series are not enough, and a more general version is required.
Given an ordered abelian group
\Gamma
and a field
{\mathbf{k}}
, the Hahn series field
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
over
{\mathbf{k}}
consists of all formal series
\sum_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}t^{\gamma}
where all
f_{\gamma}\in{\mathbf{k}}
and the set of
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
f_{\gamma}
is nonzero is a well ordered subset of
\Gamma
. For more details on
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
, see Section
2.2
. This is in fact a generalization since for
{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbb{C}
and
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
this gives the usual field
\mathbb{C}((t))
of Laurent series over
\mathbb{C}
.
Truncation in this general setting is a lot more subtle than in the case of Laurent series. One result in this dissertation is that joining the notion of truncation to the valued field structure of a Hahn field results in undecidability; see Chapter
4
. Despite this “negative” feature of truncation, one can prove many “positive” things about it as we will
show in this dissertation.

For various reasons (notational and traditional) we prefer to consider asymptotics at infinity, using a large variable
x
rather than a small variable
t=x^{-1}
. Thus for
x>1
,

\log(1-\frac{1}{x})\ =\ -\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}-\frac{1}{3x^{3}}-\cdots\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{-x^{-n}}{n}

and the truncations of
\log(1-\frac{1}{x})
will be
0,\ -\frac{1}{x},-\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{2x^{2}},\ldots
mimicking the concept of omitting “small” terms.

Truncation first appears in a model theoretic setting when Mourgues and Ressayre
[
12
]
proved that every real closed field is isomorphic to a truncation closed
subfield of a Hahn field over
\mathbb{R}
. They use this result to prove that every real closed field
R
has an
integer part
, that is, a subring
Z
of
R
such that for every
x\in R
there is a unique
z\in Z
such that
z\leq x<z+1
.
This goes as follows: Let
R
be a truncation closed subfield of
\mathbb{R}((t^{\Gamma}))
, and
let
\mathcal{U}
be the additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}((t^{\Gamma}))
consisting of the series
\sum_{\gamma<0}f_{\gamma}t^{\gamma}
.
Then an integer part of
R
is given by
(R\cap\mathcal{U})\oplus\mathbb{Z}
.
Fornasiero
[
9
]
generalized the truncation result of Mourgues and Ressayre by showing that every henselian valued field of characteristic zero is isomorphic to
a truncation closed subfield of a Hahn field over its residue field
[
9
]
.
Van den Dries, Marker and Macintyre used the preservation of being truncation closed under various extension procedures to prove undefinability of certain functions in the o-minimal expansion
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{an},\exp}
of the real field
[
7
]
. Van den Dries gathered many of these results and expanded on them in
[
3
]
.
Another potential role of truncation, prominent in
[
13
]
, is that it enables transfinite
induction on the (well-ordered) support of series. This is a new
tool in valuation theory, not fully exploited so far. We do use this tool at various places.

There is increasing interest in Hahn fields
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
with a ‘good’
derivation, and being truncation closed is significant
in that context for similar reasons. Investigating this is one of the goals of this dissertation.
The ‘good’ derivations are introduced in Chapter
6
. With a ‘good’ derivation on
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
,
it is easy to show that the differential subfield of
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
generated by a truncation
closed subset is again truncation closed: see Section
6.1
. It is a more difficult to show that
adjoining the solutions in
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
of first-order linear
differential equations over a truncation closed differential subfield of

{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
to this subfield
preserves the property of being truncation closed: this is basically
the content of Theorem
6.6
.

The next goal is to apply this to the differential
field
\mathbb{T}
of (logarithmic-exponential) transseries, and
related transseries
fields. Now
\mathbb{T}
is not a Hahn-field-with-good-derivation as above,
but its definition does involve iterating a construction where at
each step
one builds a Hahn-field-with-good-derivation whose coefficient field
is the previously constructed Hahn-field-with-good-derivation.
In this way we have been able to prove our main result to the effect that
under some mild assumption
on a truncation closed differential subfield of
\mathbb{T}
that its
Liouville closure in
\mathbb{T}
is also truncation closed,
where the Liouville closure of a differential subfield of
\mathbb{T}

is the smallest differential subfield that is closed under exponentiation and
taking antiderivatives.

We now turn to a more detailed description of the various chapters.
Chapter 2 contains the preliminaries for the rest of the dissertation. We cover the basics about ordered abelian groups, Hahn series, valued fields, and differential fields that are used later. In Chapter 3 we consider the operation of truncating Hahn series. We recall known results to the effect that
the property (for subsets of Hahn fields) of being truncation closed is preserved under several extension procedures. We also introduce the notion of an infinite part
\mathcal{U}
for a valued field, and we develop the notions of truncation with respect to
\mathcal{U}
and support of an element with respect to
\mathcal{U}
. This can be thought of as an attempt to
define truncation in the first-order setting of valued fields. Chapter 4 contains undecidability results for valued fields with a monomial group and an infinite part: using some facts
about monadic second-order logic we show that Hahn fields equipped with a monomial group and truncation have an undecidable theory. We apply this to show that the exponential field of transseries with a predicate for its group of transmonomials is undecidable. We also prove a variant of the well-known fact that the differential field of Laurent series is undecidable. In Chapter 5 we introduce “supported operators” and prove some basic facts about these as a tool to be used in the
later chapters. In Chapter 6 we consider derivations on Hahn fields for which the logarithmic derivative of every monomial lies in the coefficient field. We show that for such a derivation, the differential field generated by a truncation closed subset is again truncation closed, and that being truncation closed is preserved under adjoining solutions of certain types of differential equations of the form
Y^{\prime}+bY+c=0
. In Chapter 7 we apply this to the differential field
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
of exponential transseries. Although
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
is not a Hahn field, it is an increasing union of (differential) Hahn fields where the notion of truncation still makes sense and is of interest. But for positive results in this setting about preserving the property of being truncation closed we need to introduce a sort of analogue of this property for sets of monomials, which we call splitting. In Chapter 8 we consider the field
\mathbb{T}
of logarithmic-exponential transseries as a union of copies of
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
and prove our main result. We conclude by briefly discussing some possibilities
of expanding on the results in this dissertation.

Chapter 2
Preliminaries

In this chapter we summarize basic facts about valued fields, especially Hahn fields, and valued differential fields, as needed
in later chapters. We try to be self contained as to the various algebraic notions involved, and thus we include also some preliminaries on valuation theory and differential algebra.
Almost all of this can be found in more detail and with proofs in
[
1
, Chapters 2, 3, 4]
.

We let
m,n
range over
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, the set of natural numbers. Rings will have a multiplicative identity
1
. We identify
\mathbb{Z}
in the usual (unique) way with a subring of any ring of characteristic zero. We let
K^{\times}
denote the multiplicative group of a field
K
. For a field
K
of characteristic zero, we identify
\mathbb{Q}
with a subfield of
K
in the usual way.

2.1
Ordered Sets and Groups

Ordered sets

An
ordered set

S
is a set equipped with a distinguished
total
ordering
\leq
. If
S
is an ordered set with ordering
\leq
we let
\geq,<,
and
>
have the usual meaning.
Subsets of
S
will be considered as ordered sets with the ordering induced by
\leq
.

For an ordered set
S
and a subset
B
of
S
we set
S^{>B}:=\{s\in S:s>b\text{ for all }b\in B\}
, and
S^{>s}:=S^{>\{s\}}
for an element
s\in S
. Similarly we define
S^{<B},S^{\leq B},S^{\geq B}
. We set
S_{\infty}:=S\cup\{\infty\}
and extend the ordering of
S
to an ordering on
S_{\infty}
by
s<\infty
for every
s\in S
.

Let
S
be an ordered set. Then
S
is said to be
well-ordered
if every non-empty subset of
S
has a minimum element with respect to
\leq
.
A subset
B
of
S
is said to be
convex in

S
if for all
a,b,c\in S
with
a<b<c
and
a,c\in B
we have
b\in B
. The
convex hull
of
B\subseteq S
is the smallest subset of
S
, under inclusion, that is convex and contains
B
.

Ordered abelian groups

Let
\Gamma
be an additively written
ordered abelian group
, that is, an abelian group with an ordering such that for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Gamma

\alpha<\beta\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha+\gamma<\beta+\gamma

If
\Gamma
is an ordered abelian group we write
\Gamma^{>}
instead of
\Gamma^{>0}
; similarly with
\Gamma^{<},\Gamma^{\leq},\Gamma^{\geq}
. Additionally we set
\Gamma^{\neq}=\Gamma\setminus\{0\}

Given
\gamma\in\Gamma
we set
|\gamma|=\begin{cases}\gamma\text{ if }\gamma\geq 0\\
-\gamma\text{ if }\gamma<0.\end{cases}

Ordered abelian groups will be considered as
\mathbb{Z}
-modules in the usual way.

We consider the archimedean equivalence relation on an ordered abelian group
\Gamma
where the archimedean class of an element
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma
is given by

[\gamma_{0}]\ :=\ \{\gamma\in\Gamma\ :\ |\gamma_{0}|\leq n|\gamma|\text{ and }|\gamma|\leq n|\gamma_{0}|\text{ for some }n\ >\ 0\}.

We will consider the set of archimedean classes as an ordered set where

[\gamma]\ \leq\ [\delta]\text{ iff }|\gamma|\ \leq\ n|\delta|\text{ for some }n.

Thus
[0]=\{0\}
, and
[0]\leq[\gamma]
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
, with equality iff
\gamma=0
; moreover

[\gamma]\ <\ [\delta]\text{ iff }n|\gamma|\ <\ |\delta|\text{ for all }n.

The
rank
of
\Gamma
is the number of archimedean classes of
\Gamma^{\neq}
; it equals the number of nontrivial convex subgroups of
\Gamma
if the rank is finite. An ordered abelian group of rank 1 is also said to be
archimedean
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\Delta,\Theta\subseteq\Gamma
are well-ordered, then
\Delta+\Theta
is well-ordered, and for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
there are only finitely many
(\delta,\theta)\in\Delta\times\Theta
with
\delta+\theta=\gamma
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G\subseteq\Gamma^{>}
be a well ordered set.
Then

G^{*}\ :=\ \sum G\ :=\ \{g_{1}+\cdots+g_{n}\ :\ g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in G\}

is well ordered and for every
\gamma\in G^{*}
there are finitely many tuples
(n,g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
, with
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
elements in
G
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}=\gamma
.

Valuations on abelian groups

A valuation on an (additively written) abelian group
\Gamma
is a surjective map
v:\Gamma\rightarrow S_{\infty}
, where
S
is a linearly ordered set, such that for
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma

(VG1)

v(\alpha)=\infty\iff\alpha=0
,

(VG2)

v(\alpha)=v(-\alpha)
,

(VG3)

v(\alpha+\beta)\geq\min\{v(\alpha),v(\beta)\}
.

A
valued abelian group
is a triple
(\Gamma,S,v)
where
\Gamma
is an ordered abelian group,
S
is an ordered set and
v:\Gamma\rightarrow S_{\infty}
. It is easy to show that if
v(\alpha)<v(\beta)
then
v(\alpha+\beta)=v(\alpha)
; indeed

v(\alpha)\ =\ v(\alpha+\beta-\beta)\ \geq\ \min(v(\alpha+\beta),v(\beta))\ \geq\ v(\alpha).

We note that given any element
s\in S
, the subsets

B(s):=\{\gamma\ :\ v(\gamma)>s\}\text{ and }\overline{B}(s):=\{\gamma:v(\gamma)\geq s\}

of
\Gamma
form subgroups with the group operation inherited from
\Gamma
. We denote their quotient by
G(s):=\overline{B}(s)/B(s)
.

Hahn products

Let
(G_{s})_{s}\in S
be a family of nontrivial ordered abelian groups indexed by a totally ordered set
S
. Consider the product group
\prod_{s\in S}G_{s}
whose elements are the sequences
g=(g_{s})_{s\in S}
such that
g_{s}\in G_{s}
for each
s\in S
. For such
g
, we define the support of
g
as the set
\{s\in S:g_{s}\neq 0\}
.

We define the
Hahn product
of the family to be the subgroup
H[(G_{s})_{s}]
of
\prod_{s\in S}G_{s}
with well ordered support. We can construe
G:=H[(G_{s})_{s}]
as an ordered valued abelian group, by equipping it with the
Hahn valuation

v:G\rightarrow S_{\infty}
given by
v(g)=\min(\operatorname{supp}(g))
for
g\in G\neq
and
v(0)=\infty
, and the order given by
0<g
iff
0<g_{s_{0}}
for
s_{0}=v(g)
.

2.2
Hahn Series

By a
monomial set
we mean an ordered set
\mathfrak{M}
whose
elements are thought of as monomials and whose ordering is
denoted by
\preccurlyeq
(or
\preccurlyeq_{\mathfrak{M}}
if we need to
indicate the dependence on
\mathfrak{M}
); the corresponding strict ordering, reverse ordering, and strict reverse ordering are then
denoted by
\prec
,
\succcurlyeq
, and
\succ
, respectively. A subset of the monomial set
\mathfrak{M}
is said to be
well-based
if it is well-ordered with respect to the reverse ordering
\succcurlyeq
, equivalently, there is no infinite sequence
\mathfrak{m}_{0}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{1}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{2}\prec\dots
in the subset.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a monomial set, let
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
range over
\mathfrak{M}
, and let
C
be an (additively written) abelian group. By a

Hahn series with coefficients in
C
and monomials in
\mathfrak{M}

we mean a function
f:\mathfrak{M}\to C
whose support

\operatorname{supp}f\ :=\ \{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:\ f(\mathfrak{m})\neq 0\}

is well-based as a subset of
\mathfrak{M}
.
We often write such
f
as a series
\sum_{\mathfrak{m}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
,
where
f_{\mathfrak{m}}:=f(\mathfrak{m})
.
We define the
Hahn space

C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
to be the (additive) abelian group whose elements are
the Hahn series with coefficients in
C
and monomials in
\mathfrak{M}
,
with addition as suggested by the series notation.

Let
(f_{i})_{i\in I}\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{I}
. We say that
(f_{i})

is
summable
(or
\sum_{i}f_{i}
exists) if

•

\bigcup_{i}\operatorname{supp}(f_{i})
is well-based;

•

for each
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
there are only finitely many
i
with
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f_{i})
.

If
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
is summable, we define its sum
f=\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
by
f_{\mathfrak{m}}=\sum_{i}f_{i,\mathfrak{m}}
. If
I
is finite, then
(f_{i})
is summable and
\sum_{i}f_{i}
is the usual finite sum.
If
(f_{i})
and
(g_{i})\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{I}
are summable, then so is
(f_{i}+g_{i})
, and
\sum_{i}f_{i}+g_{i}=\sum_{i}f_{i}+\sum_{i}g_{i}
. If
I
and

J
are disjoint sets and
(f_{i})\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{I}
and
(f_{j})\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{J}
are summable, then
(f_{k})_{k\in I\cup J}
is summable
and
\sum_{k\in I\cup J}f_{k}=\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}+\sum_{j\in J}f_{j}
.
Finally, if the
family
(f_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in I\times J}
of elements
f_{i,j}\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is summable, then

\sum_{j}f_{i,j}
exists for every
i
, and
\sum_{i}(\sum_{j}f_{i,j})
exists, and
likewise
\sum_{j}(\sum_{i}f_{i,j})
exists, and

\sum_{i,j}f_{i,j}\ =\ \sum_{i}\big(\sum_{j}f_{i,j}\big)\ =\ \sum_{j}\big(\sum_{i}f_{i,j}\big).

It is convenient to augment
\mathfrak{M}
by an element
0\notin\mathfrak{M}
and
extend
\preccurlyeq
to an ordering of
\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
, also denoted by
\preccurlyeq
, by declaring

0\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}
(and thus
0\prec\mathfrak{m}
) for all
\mathfrak{m}
.
For
f\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
we define its
leading monomial

\mathfrak{d}(f)\in\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
by

\mathfrak{d}(f):=\max\operatorname{supp}f\ \text{ if }f\neq 0,\qquad\mathfrak{d}(f)=0\ \text{ if }f=0.

This allows us to introduce a transitive and reflexive binary relation
\preccurlyeq
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
by
f\preccurlyeq g
if and only if
\mathfrak{d}(f)\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{d}(g)
; replacing in this equivalence
\preccurlyeq
by
\prec
,
\succcurlyeq
, and
\succ
, respectively, we introduce likewise transitive
binary relations
\prec
,
\succcurlyeq
, and
\succ
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.
We also define the equivalence relation
\asymp
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
by

f\asymp g:\Leftrightarrow\ \mathfrak{d}(f)=\mathfrak{d}(g)
.

Hahn rings

By a
monomial group
we mean a multiplicative ordered abelian group whose elements are
thought of as monomials. Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a monomial group; we use the same notations as for monomial sets; in particular, the
ordering is denoted by
\preccurlyeq
, and we let
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}

range over
\mathfrak{M}
.
We recall the following basic fact about well-based subsets of
\mathfrak{M}
:

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\mathfrak{G},\mathfrak{H}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
are well-based, then
\mathfrak{G}\mathfrak{H}
is well-based, and for every
\mathfrak{m}
there are only finitely many
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{h})\in\mathfrak{G}\times\mathfrak{H}
with
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{m}
.

Next, let
C
be
a commutative ring with
1\neq 0
. Lemma
2.3
allows us to define
for
f,g\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
their product
fg\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
by

(fg)_{\mathfrak{h}}:=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{h}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}g_{\mathfrak{n}}
, so

\operatorname{supp}fg\ \subseteq\ (\operatorname{supp}f)(\operatorname{supp}g).

With this product operation
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is a commutative ring with subrings
C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\preccurlyeq 1}]]
and
C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\succcurlyeq 1}]]
. We have the ring embedding of
C
into
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
sending
c\in C
to
f\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with
f_{1}=c
and
f_{\mathfrak{m}}=0

for
\mathfrak{m}\neq 1
; we identify
C
with a subring
of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
via this embedding. We also have the group embedding of

\mathfrak{M}
into the
multiplicative group of units of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
sending
\mathfrak{m}
to

f\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with
f_{\mathfrak{m}}=1
, and
f_{\mathfrak{n}}=0
for

\mathfrak{n}\neq\mathfrak{m}
; we identify
\mathfrak{M}
with a subgroup of this multiplicative group of units via this embedding, and identify the element
0
of
\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
with the zero element of the ring
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. This has the effect that restricting the binary relation

\preccurlyeq
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
to
\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
gives back the originally given ordering
\preccurlyeq
on
\mathfrak{M}\cup\{0\}
, and
likewise with
\prec
,
\succcurlyeq
, and
\succ
. Note that
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{\prec 1}=C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\prec 1}]]
.

Here is another key fact about well-based subsets of
\mathfrak{M}
:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a well-based subset of
\mathfrak{M}^{\prec 1}
. Then:

(i)

\mathfrak{G}^{*}:=\bigcup_{n}\mathfrak{G}^{n}
is well-based;

(ii)

for every
\mathfrak{m}
there are only finitely many
tuples
(n,\mathfrak{g}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{g}_{n})
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{g}_{n}\in\mathfrak{G}
and
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{g}_{n}
.

For
\varepsilon\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{\prec 1}
the family
(\varepsilon^{n})
is summable and
(1-\varepsilon)\sum_{n}\varepsilon^{n}=1
, by Lemma
2.4
. More generally,
let
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
be a tuple of distinct variables and let

F\ =\ F(t)\ =\ \sum_{\nu}c_{\nu}t^{\nu}\in C[[t]]\ :=\ C[[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]]

be a formal power series over
C
; here the sum ranges over all multiindices
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and

c_{\nu}\in C
,
t^{\nu}:=t_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{\nu_{n}}
. For any tuple
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n})
of
elements of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{\prec 1}
the family
(c_{\nu}\epsilon^{\nu})
is summable, where
\varepsilon^{\nu}:=\varepsilon_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\varepsilon_{n}^{\nu_{n}}
. Put

F(\varepsilon)\ :=\ \ \sum_{\nu}c_{\nu}\varepsilon^{\nu}\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{\preccurlyeq 1}\ =\ C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\preccurlyeq 1}]].

Fixing
\varepsilon
and varying
F
we obtain a
C
-algebra morphism

F\mapsto F(\varepsilon)\ :\ C[[t]]\to C[[\mathfrak{M}]].

Assume that
C
contains
\mathbb{Q}
as a subring.
Then we have the formal power series

\exp(t)\ :=\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}t^{i}/i!\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]],\qquad\log(1+t)\ :=\ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j-1}t^{j}/j\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]]

with
\exp(t_{1}+t_{2})=\exp(t_{1})\exp(t_{2})
and
\log(1+t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{1}t_{2})=\log(1+t_{1})+\log(1+t_{2})

in
\mathbb{Q}[[t_{1},t_{2}]]\subseteq C[[t_{1},t_{2}]]
. Also

\log\big(\exp(t)\big)=t
and

\exp\big(\log(1+t))=1+t

\mathbb{Q}[[t]]\subseteq C[[t]]

Valued Fields

Valuation rings and valued fields

A
valuation ring
is an integral domain in which the set of ideals is totally ordered by inclusion. Here are a few characterizations of valuation rings:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an integral domain. The following are equivalent:

(i)

The ring
\mathcal{O}
is a valuation ring.

(ii)

For every element

in the field of fractions

of
\mathcal{O}
, either

or

is in
\mathcal{O}
.

(iii)

For all

, either

or
a\in b\mathcal{O}
.

The set of non-units of a valuation ring is an ideal. In fact, it is the unique maximal ideal of the valuation ring. Thus any valuation ring
\mathcal{O}
is a local ring.

A valued field is a field
K
equipped with a valuation ring
\mathcal{O}
such that

is the field of fractions of
\mathcal{O}
.
The following are examples of valued fields:

•

Any field

with

itself as the valuation ring. In this case we say that the valuation of

is trivial.

•

The field of Laurent series in
x^{-1}
over
\mathbb{C}
with valuation ring

\mathcal{O}\ :=\ \left\{f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}x^{-n}\right\}

•

In fact, any Hahn field
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
equipped with

\mathcal{O}\ :=\ \{f\in{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma})):\ \operatorname{supp}(f)\subseteq\Gamma^{\geq}\}

.

•

Any Hausdorff field

(a field of germs at

of continuous real functions) together with

\mathcal{O}\ :=\ \left\{f\in K:\ f\text{ is eventually bounded}\right\}

•

The field

, equipped with the valuation ring

\mathcal{O}\ :=\ \left\{\frac{a}{b}:\ a,b\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ and }b\notin p\mathbb{Z}\right\}

where

is a fixed prime number.

Residue fields

Given a local ring

we define the

,

It is sometimes useful to distinguish valued fields by the characteristic of both itself and its residue field. We say that

of

Dominance relations

A

on a field

is a binary relation
\preccurlyeq
on
K
such that for all

(DR1)

1\not\preccurlyeq 0
,

(DR2)

f\preccurlyeq f
,

(DR3)

f\preccurlyeq g
,
g\preccurlyeq h

\Rightarrow

f\preccurlyeq h
,

(DR4)

f\preccurlyeq g
or
g\preccurlyeq f
,

(DR5)

f\preccurlyeq g

\Rightarrow

hf\preccurlyeq hg
,

(DR6)

f\preccurlyeq h
,
g\preccurlyeq h

\Rightarrow

f+g\preccurlyeq h
.

Let

be a dominance relation on the field

. We define some further (asymptotic) binary relations

on

in terms of

\displaystyle f\succcurlyeq g

\displaystyle:\iff g\preccurlyeq f;\qquad f\prec g:\iff f\preccurlyeq g\text{ and }g\not\preccurlyeq f;\qquad f\succ g:\iff g\prec f;

\displaystyle f\asymp g

\displaystyle:\iff f\preccurlyeq g\text{ and }g\preccurlyeq f;\qquad f\sim g:\iff f-g\prec f.

We also say that

dominates

if

, that

strictly dominates

if

, and that

is
asymptotic
to

if

. The relation

is an equivalence relation on

; reflexivity and symmetry are straightforward from the definition and transitivity follows from DR3 and symmetry.
Alternatively we note that by DR2 and DR3 we have that
\preccurlyeq
is a preorder on

. If

, then

. It is easy to check that

is an equivalence relation on

.

on

\infty>\gamma

•

,

an extend

to

by

all of

Proposition 2.6

Proof.

Assume (i) and define the binary relation

∎

Let

We always consider the Hahn field

Henselian fields

A valued field

if for every polynomial

and every

and

. Hahn fields are henselian. Here are some useful characterizations of henselianity:

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
K
be a valued field. The following are equivalent:

(i)

is henselian.

(ii)

For every

such that

.

(iii)

For every

, there is

such that

.

(iv)

For every

and

with

, there is

such that

.

Lemma 2.8
.

If a valued field is algebraically maximal, then it is henselian.
The converse holds in the equicharacteristic zero case.

Let
R
be a commutative ring. A

such that for all

Let

.
Let

for all

of

is closed under the derivation of

, and is accordingly viewed as a differential subring of

. Given

denote the smallest differential

that contains

and

, and we call

the

over

.

is a differential ring whose underlying ring is a field of characteristic zero; then the ring of constants is also a field.

Let

and suppose that
x^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{O}
. Then
1/x^{\prime}\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
, so
x/x^{\prime}\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
,
(1/x^{\prime})^{\prime}\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
, hence
(x/x^{\prime})^{\prime}\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
. But
(x/x^{\prime})^{\prime}=x(1/x^{\prime})^{\prime}+1\in 1+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
, a contradiction.
∎

If
\der({\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}})\subseteq{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
, we equip
{\mathbf{k}}
with the derivation
a+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}\mapsto\der(a)+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}:\mathcal{O}/{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}/{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
. The condition

\der({\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}})\subseteq{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
means that derivatives of “small” elements (elements of
{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
) are small, so we say that
\der
is
small
(or that

has

) if this condition is satisfied.
If
K
has small derivation, then we refer to

with the induced derivation as the
differential residue field
of
K
.

Continuous derivations

We now equip
K
with the valuation topology. Even though it is not part of the definition of “valued differential field”, the
only case of interest to us is when the derivation is continuous. If the derivation on
K
is continuous, then for every
P\in K\{Y\}
the function

is continuous.

If
\der
is small, then
\der
is continuous. In fact, continuous derivations are just “small derivations in disguise” as shown by the next result (
[
1
, Lemma 4.4.7]
):

Lemma 2.11
.

The following conditions on
K
are equivalent:

(i)

\der:K\rightarrow K
is continuous;

(ii)

for some
a\in K^{\times}
we have
\der{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}\subseteq a{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
;

(iii)

for some
a\in K^{\times}
the derivation
\derdelta:=a^{-1}\der
is small.

Asymptotic fields and differential-valued fields

The material from this subsection can be found in more detail in
[
1
, Section 9.1]
. Let
K
be a valued differential field. We say that
K
is
asymptotic
if for all
f,g\in K^{\times}
with
f,g\prec 1
,

f\prec g\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f^{\prime}\prec g^{\prime}.

The derivation of an asymptotic field is continuous (with respect to the valuation topology). We say that
K
is a
differential-valued field
, or just
\operatorname{d}
-valued
, if
K
is asymptotic and
\mathcal{O}=C+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
. If
K
is
\operatorname{d}
-valued, then its constant field is naturally isomorphic to its residue field via the residue map. For the next lemma, see for example
[
1
, Proposition 9.1.3]
.

Lemma 2.12
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

K
is
\operatorname{d}
-valued;

(ii)

\mathcal{O}=C+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
and for all
f,g\in K^{\times}
,
f,g\prec 1\Rightarrow f^{\dagger}\succ g^{\prime}
.

H-fields

For more details about
H
-fields, see
[
1
, Chapter 10]
. An
H
-field is by definition an ordered differential field
K
such that:

(i)

for all
f\in K
, if
f>C
, then
f^{\prime}>0
;

(ii)

\mathcal{O}=C+{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
, where
\mathcal{O}=\{g\in K\ :\ |g|\leq c\text{ for some }c\in C\}
and
{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
is the maximal ideal of the convex subring
\mathcal{O}
of
K
.

An
H
-field

is considered also as a valued differential field by taking the above
\mathcal{O}
as the valuation ring. This makes
H
-fields into differential-valued fields.
If
K
is an
H
-field with

, then its valuation topology coincides with its order topology.
Hardy fields containing
\mathbb{R}
as a subfield are
H
-fields.
The
H
in “
H
-field” is in honor of the pioneers Borel, Hahn, Hardy, and Hausdorff, three of which share the initial
H
.

An
H
-field

is said to be
Liouville closed
if it is a real closed
H
-field and for all
a\in K
there exist
y,z\in K^{\times}
such that
y^{\prime}=a
and
z^{\dagger}=a
.
An
H
-field

is Liouville closed if and only if

is real closed and every equation

with

has a nonzero solution in

.

Chapter 3
Truncation

In this Chapter we formally introduce the notion of truncation and state some well known results that showcase how the notion of a truncation closed subset of a Hahn field is robust in the sense that it is preserved under several extension procedures. In Section 2.2 we introduce the notion of an
infinite part of a valued field
as a first-order counterpart to truncation. It allows us to extend the notion of truncation outside of the Hahn field setting. We also
consider some cases with archimedean value group where this first-order version of truncation necessarily agrees with truncation in an ambient Hahn field

.

3.1
Truncation in Hahn Fields

In this section
\mathfrak{M}

For

and

in

is

P(t^{\gamma_{0}}+t^{\gamma})\neq t^{\gamma_{0}}
.

Note that
\mathcal{O}=C\oplus{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{U}
is a
C
-vector subspace of
K
such that
K=\mathcal{U}\oplus\mathcal{O}
,
\mathcal{U}
is closed under multiplication in
K
, and
\mathcal{U}\supseteq\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}
.
We call such
\mathcal{U}
an
infinite part of

K
.
For
a\in K
we define its
\mathcal{U}
-
part
to be
the unique
u\in\mathcal{U}
such that
a=u+b
for some
b\in\mathcal{O}
, and we denote the

\mathcal{U}
-part of
a
by
a|_{\mathcal{U}}
.

Example
.

The Hahn field
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with coefficient field
C
and
monomial group
\mathfrak{M}
, and the usual valuation given by its valuation ring

C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\preccurlyeq 1}]]\ :=\ \{f\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]:\ \operatorname{supp}f\subseteq\mathfrak{M}^{\preccurlyeq 1}\},

has infinite part

C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}]]\ :=\ \{f\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]:\ \operatorname{supp}f\subseteq\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}\}.

We call this the
canonical infinite part
of the Hahn field
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.

More generally, suppose
K
is a truncation closed subfield of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with
C\subseteq K
and
\mathfrak{M}\subseteq K
.
We consider
K
as a valued subfield of this Hahn field,
and equip
K
with its monomial group
\mathfrak{M}
and its coefficient field
C
. Then
K
has infinite part
K\cap C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}]]
.

Truncation with respect to an infinite part

For any monomial
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
we define the
truncation of

a

at

\mathfrak{m}

with respect to

\mathcal{U}
, as
a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}}:=\mathfrak{m}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}a)|_{\mathcal{U}}
.
Note that for
\mathfrak{m}=1
we get
a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{1}=a|_{\mathcal{U}}
.
Note also that for the canonical infinite part

\mathcal{U}
of the Hahn field
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
we have
a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}}=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}
.

The following rules are easy consequences of the definition above:

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
a,b\in K
,
c\in C
, and
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then:

(i)

(a+b)|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}+b|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}
and

(ca)|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}=c\cdot a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}
;

(ii)

a\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}

\iff

a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}=0
;

(iii)

a\succ\mathfrak{m}

\iff

a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\sim a
;

(iv)

a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\preccurlyeq a
, by (2) and (3);

(v)

a-a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}
;

(vi)

a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}=b|_{\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}=0\Rightarrow(ab)|_{\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}=0
;

(vii)

a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\neq 0\Rightarrow a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\succ\mathfrak{m}
;

(viii)

\mathfrak{m}\cdot a|_{\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}=(\mathfrak{m}a)|_{\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}
;

(ix)

\mathfrak{m}|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{n}}=\mathfrak{m}
if
\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}
, and
\mathfrak{m}|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{n}}=0
if
\mathfrak{m}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{n}
.

The following lemmas give a commutativity property for truncation with respect to
\mathcal{U}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
a\in K
and
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then

(a|_{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\ =\ (a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathcal{U}}\ =\ a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\max(1,\mathfrak{m})}.

Proof.

Let
a=u+b
with
u\in\mathcal{U}
,
b\in\mathcal{O}
. Consider first the case
\mathfrak{m}\preccurlyeq 1
. Then

\displaystyle(a|_{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\

\displaystyle=\ u|_{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=\ \mathfrak{m}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}u)|_{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=\ \mathfrak{m}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}u)\ =\ u.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\

\displaystyle=\ u|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}+b|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=\ u+b|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}.

Now
b|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\preccurlyeq b
by (iv) of Lemma
3.8
, so

b|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathcal{O}
, and thus

(a|_{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}=u=(a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}})|_{\mathcal{U}}
. Since
u=a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{1}
, this proves the lemma for
\mathfrak{m}\preccurlyeq 1
.
Next, assume
\mathfrak{m}\succ 1
. Then by (i) and (ii) of Lemma
3.8
,

\displaystyle a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\

\displaystyle=\ \ u|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}+\ b|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=\ \ u|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=\ (a|_{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}.

On the other hand,
(a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathcal{U}}=\big(\mathfrak{m}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}a)|_{\mathcal{U}}\big)|_{\mathcal{U}}=\mathfrak{m}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}a)|_{\mathcal{U}}=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}
, since
\mathcal{U}
is closed under products.
∎

For the proof of Lemma
3.10
, note that for
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}
and
a\in K
we have
(\mathfrak{m}a)|_{\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}=\mathfrak{m}\cdot a|_{\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
a\in K
and
\mathfrak{m}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{n}
in
\mathfrak{M}
. Then

(a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}\ =\ (a|_{\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}})|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\ =\ a|_{\mathfrak{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}.

Proof.

To simplify notation we omit in this proof superscripts
\mathcal{U}
in expressions like
a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}}
.
Using Lemma
3.9
,

(a|_{\mathfrak{m}})|_{\mathfrak{n}}\ =\ \big(\mathfrak{m}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}a|_{\mathcal{U}})\big)|_{\mathfrak{n}}\ =\ \mathfrak{m}\cdot\big((\mathfrak{m}^{-1}a)|_{\mathcal{U}}\big)|_{\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{m}}

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =\ \mathfrak{m}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}a)|_{\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{m}}\ =\ (\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{m}^{-1}a)|_{\mathfrak{n}}\ =\ a|_{\mathfrak{n}}.

In a similar way we get
(a|_{\mathfrak{n}})|_{\mathfrak{m}}=a|_{\mathfrak{n}}
.
∎

\mathcal{U}
-support

For
a\in K
we define the
\mathcal{U}
-
support of

a
as

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)\ =\ \{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}:a-a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\asymp\mathfrak{m}\}.

When
K
is a Hahn field
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
and
\mathcal{U}
is its canonical infinite part, this agrees with the
usual support
\operatorname{supp}a
. Most of the following rules are easy to verify:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
a,b\in K
and
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then:

(i)

\mathfrak{m}\cdot\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)=\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathfrak{m}a)
,

(ii)

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a+b)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)\cup\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(b)
and
\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}ca=\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}a
for
c\in C^{\times}
,

(iii)

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)\cap\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(b)=\emptyset

\Rightarrow

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a+b)=\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)\cup\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(b)
,

(iv)

a\asymp\mathfrak{m}\Rightarrow\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)
,

(v)

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)\preccurlyeq a
,

(vi)

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathfrak{m})=\{\mathfrak{m}\}
,

(vii)

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}})=\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)^{\succ\mathfrak{m}}
and
\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a-a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}})=\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)^{\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}}
,

(viii)

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)\succ\mathfrak{m}\ \Leftrightarrow\ a=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\ \Leftrightarrow\ a\in\mathfrak{m}\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

We only do (vii), since (i)–(vi) are easy to verify, and (viii) is an easy consequence of (vii), using also (viii) of Lemma
3.8
for the second equivalence. We have
a=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}+c\mathfrak{m}+b
for unique
c\in C
and
b\prec\mathfrak{m}
, by (v) in Lemma
3.8
. Hence by (ii), (v), and (vi),

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)^{\succ\mathfrak{m}}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}})
.
Moreover,

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}})\succ\mathfrak{m}
by Lemma
3.10
,
and then applying (ii), (v), (vi) to
a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}}=-a-c\mathfrak{m}-b
yields

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}})\subseteq\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)
,
which gives the first equality of (vii). As to the second equality,
(ii), (v), (vi) and the first equality give

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)^{\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}}\ \subseteq\ \operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(c\mathfrak{m}+b)\ =\ \operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a-a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}})\ \preccurlyeq\ \mathfrak{m},

so by (ii) and the first equality,

\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a-a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}})\subseteq\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)^{\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{m}}
.
∎

Good Infinite Parts

As before,
\mathcal{U}
is an infinite part of
K
. We say that
\mathcal{U}
is
good
if for all
a,b\in K
we have
\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(ab)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(b)
. In view of the definability of truncation with respect to
\mathcal{U}
(in the valued field
K
equipped with
\mathfrak{M}
,
C
,
\mathcal{U}
) and the first-order nature of the
\mathcal{U}
-support, being good is also of first-order nature. We do not know whether being good is a consequence of the definition of “infinite part”.
Note that the canonical infinite part of a Hahn field
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is good, and so is the infinite part
K\cap C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}]]
of
K
when
K\supseteq C(\mathfrak{M})
is a truncation closed subfield of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.

Assume
\mathcal{U}
is good and
K
is a truncation closed valued subfield of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, with the same
C
and
\mathfrak{M}
as for
K
, so
C(\mathfrak{M})\subseteq K
. Here “truncation closed” is of course with respect to truncation in the Hahn field
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.
Thus for
a\in K
and
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
we have in principle two truncations, namely
a|_{\mathfrak{m}}
as an element of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
and the truncation
a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}}
with respect to

\mathcal{U}
. Set

E\ :=\ \{a\in K:\ a|_{\mathfrak{m}}=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathcal{U}}\text{ for all }\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}\}.

Thus
\operatorname{supp}(a)=\operatorname{supp}_{\mathcal{U}}(a)
for all
a\in E
.

Lemma 3.12
.

E
is a truncation closed subring of
K
with
C[\mathfrak{M}]\subseteq E
.

Proof.

Clearly,
E
is truncation closed in
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, and
E
is a
C
-linear subspace of
K
that contains
C
and
\mathfrak{M}
. Let
a,b\in E
; it remains to show that then
ab\in E
. We do so by induction on
(o(a),o(b))
with respect to the lexicographic ordering. If either
o(a)=0
or
o(b)=0
, then
a=0
or
b=0
, in which case we are done. Let
o(a)>0
and
o(b)>0
, and
\mathfrak{d}\in\mathfrak{M}
. We want to show
ab|_{\mathfrak{d}}=ab|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}
. Assume that
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(a)
and
\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(b)
are such that
\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{d}
. Then

a=a_{1}+a_{2}\text{ with }a_{1}:=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}\ \text{ and }\ b=b_{1}+b_{2}\text{ with }b_{1}=b|_{\mathfrak{n}},

and
ab=a_{1}b+a_{2}b_{1}+a_{2}b_{2}
. Hence

(ab)|_{\mathfrak{d}}\ =\ (a_{1}b)|_{\mathfrak{d}}+(a_{2}b_{1})|_{\mathfrak{d}}\ \text{ and }\ (ab)|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}\ =\ (a_{1}b)|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}+(a_{2}b_{1})|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}.

By induction we have
(a_{1}b)|_{\mathfrak{d}}=(a_{1}b)|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}
and
(a_{2}b_{1})|_{\mathfrak{d}}=(a_{2}b_{1})|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}
, so
ab|_{\mathfrak{d}}=ab|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}
.
If for all monomials
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(a)
and
\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(b)
we have
\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}\succ\mathfrak{d}
, then
ab|_{\mathfrak{d}}=ab=ab|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}
where the last equality uses (8) of Lemma
3.11

and the assumption that
\mathcal{U}
is good.
∎

This seems as far as we can go under first-order assumptions on

\mathcal{U}
. In the next subsection we indicate some cases with archimedean
\mathfrak{M}
where
a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}}=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}
for all
a\in K
and
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
.

The Archimedean Case

In this subsection we assume that
\mathfrak{M}
is archimedean and
K
is a valued subfield of the Hahn field
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with the same
C
and
\mathfrak{M}
as for
K
. In the next lemma we do not assume that
\mathcal{U}
is good, but derive it from the lemma. Let
\mathfrak{m}
range over
\mathfrak{M}
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Suppose
K=C(\mathfrak{M})
. Then
\mathcal{U}=\bigoplus_{\mathfrak{m}\succ 1}C\mathfrak{m}
.

Proof.

The key point is the easily verified observation that

F\ :=\ \{a\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]:\ \operatorname{supp}(a)^{\succ\mathfrak{m}}\text{ is finite for all }\mathfrak{m}\}

is a subfield of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
containing
K
.
∎

It is worth saying a bit more on this case. Since

C\cup\mathfrak{M}
is truncation closed,
K=C(\mathfrak{M})
is truncation closed in
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. By Lemma
3.13
there is only one infinite part of
K
, in particular,
\mathcal{U}=K\cap C[[\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}]]
and so
\mathcal{U}
is good and

a|^{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathfrak{m}}=a|_{\mathfrak{m}}
for all
a\in K
and all
\mathfrak{m}
. We also
note that the field
F
defined in the proof of that lemma is the closure of
C[\mathfrak{M}]
(and of
C(\mathfrak{M})
) in
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with respect to the valuation topology. Thus

F
is henselian as a valued subfield of
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, by
[
1
, Corollary 3.3.5]
. Note also that
F
is truncation closed in
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
and has, just like
K
, only one infinite part, which is the same as
that of
K
, namely
\bigoplus_{\mathfrak{m}\succ 1}C\mathfrak{m}
.

In the remainder of the section we assume that
\mathcal{U}
is good. Let
E
be the subring of
K
defined in the previous subsection. We can say more about
E
in the archimedean case that we are considering in this subsection:

Lemma 3.14
.

E
is a subfield of
K
.

Proof.

Let
f\in E
with
f\neq 0
, so
f=c\mathfrak{m}(1-\epsilon)
with
c\in C^{\times}
,
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
and
\epsilon\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
. Then
\epsilon\in E
and
f^{-1}=c^{-1}\mathfrak{m}^{-1}(1-\epsilon)^{-1}
. Hence
f^{-1}\in E
if and only if
(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\in E
. Let
\mathfrak{d}\in\mathfrak{M}
be given. Since
\mathfrak{M}
is archimedean, we have
n
such that
\epsilon^{n}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{d}
, so
(1-\epsilon)^{-1}=1+\epsilon+\cdots+\epsilon^{n}b
with
b\preccurlyeq 1
. Then

(1-\epsilon)^{-1}|_{\mathfrak{d}}\ =\ (1+\epsilon+\cdots+\epsilon^{n-1})|_{\mathfrak{d}}\ =\ (1+\epsilon+\cdots+\epsilon^{n-1})|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}\ =\ (1-\epsilon)^{-1}|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}.

Thus
(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\in E
.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose
f\in C[[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]]
and
f(a)\in K
for all
a\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{n}
. Then
f(a)\in E
for all
a\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}_{E}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}^{n}_{E}
. Let
\mathfrak{d}\in\mathfrak{M}
be given. Take
m
such that
\epsilon_{i}^{m}\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{d}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. We have
f=g+t_{1}^{m}h_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}^{m}h_{m}
where
g\in C[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
is of degree
<m
in
t_{i}
for every
i
, and
h_{i}\in C[[t_{1},\ldots t_{n}]]
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Then
f(\epsilon)=g(\epsilon)+b
with
b\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{d}
, so
b|_{\mathfrak{d}}=b|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}=0
, and thus
f(\epsilon)|_{\mathfrak{d}}=f(\epsilon)|_{\mathfrak{d}}^{\mathcal{U}}
.
∎

Corollary 3.16
.

If
K
is henselian, then
E
is a henselian valued subfield of
K
.

Proof.

Assume
K
is henselian, and let
P(X)=1+X+a_{1}X^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}X^{n+1}\in E[X]
with
a_{i}\in{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}_{E}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then
P
has a unique zero
y\preccurlyeq 1
in
K
. It suffices to show that
y\in E
. By the implicit function theorem we have
y=f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
for some power series
f\in C[[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]]
. Using the previous Lemma, we conclude that
y\in E
.
∎

Corollary 3.17
.

Suppose
C
has characteristic 0 and
K
is henselian. Then
E
is algebraically closed in
K
.

Proof.

Since
E
is henselian and of equicharacteristic zero, it is algebraically maximal by
[
1
, Corollary 3.3.21]
. It remains to note that
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is an immediate extension
of
E
in view of
E\supseteq C(\mathfrak{M})
.
∎

Chapter 4
Undecidability Results for Hahn Fields with Truncation

In this chapter we consider some model theoretic properties of (valued) Hahn fields with truncation. We show (Corollary
4.10
) that such structures
are very wild in the sense that they can even interpret the theory of
(\mathbb{N};+,\times)
via an interpretation of
(\mathbb{N},\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N});+,\in)
. In particular, they
are undecidable; this answers a question posed informally by van den Dries some years ago. As an application we show in Section 3.3 that the exponential field
\mathbb{T}
of transseries with its canonical monomial group
G^{\operatorname{LE}}
is undecidable.
In Section 3.4 we indicate for Hahn fields with truncation a definable binary relation with “bad” properties such as the strict order property and the tree property of the second kind. In Section 3.5 we record an undecidability result for valued Hahn fields
C((t^{\mathbb{Q}}))
with the derivation
d/dt
; this doesn’t involve truncation.

In connection with Theorem
4.5
the author would like to thank Philipp Hieronymi and Erik Walsberg for bringing monadic second-order logic to his attention.

4.1
The Set of Natural Numbers in Monadic Second-Order Logic

We start with the following well-known result.

Theorem 4.1
.

The theory of
(\mathbb{N};+,\times)
is undecidable.

Monadic second-order logic

Given a structure
\mathcal{M}=(M;\ldots)
,
monadic second-order logic of

\mathcal{M}
extends first-order logic over
\mathcal{M}
by allowing quantification over subsets of
M
.
More precisely it amounts to considering the two-sorted structure
(M,\mathcal{P}(M);\ldots,\in)
, where the usual interpretation is given to
\in\subseteq M\times\mathcal{P}(M)
.
The following positive result and its proof appear in
[
10
]
, and are in contrast with the negative Corollary
4.4
.

Theorem 4.2
.

The theory of
(\mathbb{N},\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N});\in)
is decidable.

Lemma 4.3
.

Multiplication on
\mathbb{N}
is definable in
(\mathbb{N},\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N});+,\in)
.

Proof.

If the multiplication of consecutive numbers is defined, then general multiplication of two natural numbers can be defined in terms of addition:

n=mk\iff(m+k)(m+k+1)=m(m+1)+k(k+1)+n+n.

If divisibility is defined, then multiplication of consecutive numbers is defined by

n=m(m+1)\iff\forall k(\in\mathbb{N})(n|k\leftrightarrow[m|k\wedge(m+1)|k]).

Divisibility can be defined using addition by

m|n\iff\forall S\big(0\in S\wedge\forall x(x\in S\rightarrow x+m\in S)\rightarrow n\in S\big)\,

where the variable
S
ranges over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
and the variable
x
ranges over
\mathbb{N}
.
Since addition is a primitive, multiplication is defined in
(\mathbb{N},\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N});+,\in)
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

The theory of
(\mathbb{N},\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N});+,\in)
is undecidable.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
4.1
.
∎

4.2
Hahn Fields with Truncation

Let
K={\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
be a Hahn field with nontrivial value group
\Gamma
. We consider
K
below as an
L_{\mathcal{O},\mathfrak{M},\mathcal{U}}
-structure where

L_{\mathcal{O},\mathfrak{M},\mathcal{U}}\ :=\ \{0,1,+,\times,\mathcal{O},\mathfrak{M},\mathcal{U}\},

and the unary predicate symbols
\mathcal{O},\mathfrak{M},
and
\mathcal{U}
are interpreted respectively as the valuation ring, the canonical monomial group
t^{\Gamma}
, and the canonical additive complement to
\mathcal{O}
. For
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
\mathfrak{m}=t^{\gamma}
we set
f|_{\mathfrak{m}}:=f|_{\gamma}
. Then we have the equivalence (for
f
,
u\in K
)

f|_{1}=u\iff u\in\mathcal{U}\ \&\ \exists g\in\mathcal{O}(f=u+g),

showing that truncation at
1
is definable in the
L_{\mathcal{O},\mathfrak{M},\mathcal{U}}
-structure
K
.
For
\mathfrak{m}\in t^{\Gamma}
and
f\in K
we have

f|_{\mathfrak{m}}=g\iff(\mathfrak{m}^{-1}f)|_{0}=\mathfrak{m}^{-1}g,

showing that the operation

4.3
Undecidability of the Exponential Field of Transseries with a Predicate for its Multiplicative Group of Transmonomials

The field
\mathbb{T}
of transseries is a real closed field extension of
\mathbb{R}
with a canonical exponential operation

\exp:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}

that extends the usual exponentiation
with base
e
on
\mathbb{R}
. The elements of
\mathbb{T}
are called
transseries
or
logarithmic-exponential series
. See
[
1
, Appendix A]
for a detailed
construction of
\mathbb{T}
. Below in Chapter 8 we repeat some key features
of this construction in connection with the canonical derivation of
\mathbb{T}
, but in this section the derivation plays no role, and we only list here
some general facts about
\mathbb{T}
that are relevant in connection with the undecidability results below.

First, it is known from
[
6
]
that
\mathbb{T}
as an exponential field is an elementary extension of the real exponential field
\mathbb{R}_{\exp}
. The field
\mathbb{T}
also has a natural valuation on it given by the valuation ring

\{f\in\mathbb{T}:\ |f|\leq n\text{ for some }n\},

which makes
\mathbb{T}
a valued field with its subfield
\mathbb{R}
as a field of representatives for the residue field.
The theory of
\mathbb{T}
as a
valued exponential field
has also been analyzed, leading to model-completeness in a natural language, with various
tameness properties such as weak o-minimality (and thus NIP) as a consequence; this is a special case of results in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
.

As a valued field,
\mathbb{T}
has a distinguished monomial group
G^{\operatorname{LE}}
whose elements are called
transmonomials
or
logarithmic-exponential monomials
. In fact,

\mathbb{T}
is construed as a truncation closed subfield of the Hahn field
\mathbb{R}[[G^{\operatorname{LE}}]]
, with
\mathbb{R}(G^{\operatorname{LE}})\subseteq\mathbb{T}
. As a valued field with monomial group, the theory of
\mathbb{T}
is also well-understood as a special case of the AKE-theorems, with AKE standing for Ax, Kochen, and Ersov. In particular, this complete theory is axiomatized by the
axioms for real closed fields with a nontrivial convex valuation and a monomial group of positive elements.

We now show that these positive results do not go through if we equip the valued field
\mathbb{T}
simultaneously with its canonical exponentiation and its distinguished monomial group
G^{\operatorname{LE}}
. In fact, the theory of this structure is undecidable in a rather strong way, and it turns out that we don’t even need the valuation. The basic reason is that
G^{\operatorname{LE}}=\exp\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{U}=\{f\in\mathbb{T}:\ \operatorname{supp}f\succ 1\}
, in other words,
\mathcal{U}
is the natural infinite part of
\mathbb{T}
, that is,
\mathcal{U}
is the intersection of
\mathbb{T}
with the canonical additive complement to the valuation ring of
\mathbb{R}[[G^{\operatorname{LE}}]]
. In particular,
\mathcal{U}
is definable in the exponential field
\mathbb{T}
equipped with
G^{\operatorname{LE}}
.

Using this, we can define truncation on
\mathbb{T}
(with respect to
\mathcal{U}
) in the same way we defined truncation for
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
. The same proof of Theorem
4.5
goes through to show:

Theorem 4.11
.

The valued exponential field
\mathbb{T}
equipped with its monomial group
G^{\operatorname{LE}}
interprets the structure
(\mathbb{N},\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N});+,\in)

Corollary 4.12
.

The theory of the valued exponential field
\mathbb{T}
equipped with its monomial group
G^{\operatorname{LE}}
is undecidable.

Note that the valuation ring of
\mathbb{T}
is convex. Thus we can define the valuation ring from the infinite part
\mathcal{U}
and the ordering, since

\mathcal{O}\ =\ \{f\in K:\ |f|\leq u\text{ for all positive }u\in\mathcal{U}\}.

Furthermore, the ordering is definable from the field structure, so we obtain:

Corollary 4.13
.

The theory of the exponential field
\mathbb{T}
with monomial group
G^{\operatorname{LE}}
is undecidable.

4.4
Witnessing the Strict Order Property and a Tree Property

We have already shown how
(\mathbb{N};+,\times)
can be interpreted in the
L
-structure
K
and thus we know that it has the strict order property and the tree property of the second kind among others. In this section we make explicit a binary relation that witnesses these properties inside
K
.

The independence property

Let
L
be a language and
\mathcal{M}=(M;\ldots)
an
L
-structure. We say that an
L
-formula
\phi(x;y)

shatters
a set
A\subseteq M^{x}
if for every subset
S
of
A
there is
b_{S}\in M^{y}
such that for every
a\in A
we have that
M\models\phi(a;b_{S})
if and only if
a\in S
. Let
T
be an
L
-theory. We say that
\phi(x;y)
has the
independence property with respect to
T
, or
IP
for short, if there is a model
M
of
T
, such that
\phi(x;y)
shatters an infinite subset of
M^{x}
.

For a partitioned formula
\phi(x;y)
we let
\phi^{opp}(y;x)=\phi(x;y)
, that is,
\phi^{opp}
is the same formula
\phi
but where the role of the parameter variables and type variables is exchanged.

Lemma 4.14
.

A formula
\phi(x;y)
has
\operatorname{IP}
if
\phi^{opp}
has
\operatorname{IP}
.

Proof.

By compactness the formula
\phi(x;y)
shatters some set
\{a_{J}:J\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\}
. Let the shattering be witnessed by
\{b_{I}:I\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\}
. Let
B=\{b_{I_{i}}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
be such that
I_{i}=\{Y\subseteq\mathbb{N}:i\in Y\}
. Then we have

\models\phi(a_{J},b_{I_{i}})\iff i\in J,

and thus
\phi^{opp}
shatters
B
.
∎

The Strict Order Property

We say that a formula
\phi(x;y)
has the Strict Order Property, or
\operatorname{SOP}
for short, if there are
b_{i}\in M^{y}
, for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\phi(M^{x},b_{i})\subset\phi(M^{x},b_{j})
whenever
i<j
.

Proposition 4.15
.

The formula
\varphi(x;y)
, defining the relation
R
as in section
4.2
, has
\operatorname{SOP}
.

Proof.

Let
\Theta=\{\theta_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
be any subset of
\Gamma
such that
\theta_{i}<\theta_{j}
for
i<j
, and consider the set
\{f_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t^{\theta_{i}}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Note that
\varphi(K,f_{m})\subset\varphi(K,f_{n})
for
m<n
.
∎

The tree property of the second kind

We say that a formula
\phi(x;y)
has the
tree property of the second kind
, or
\operatorname{TP2}
for short, if there are tuples
b_{j}^{i}\in M^{y}
, for
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any
\sigma:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
the set
\{\phi(x;b^{i}_{\sigma(i)}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is consistent and for any
i
and
j\neq k
we have
\{\phi(x;b^{i}_{j}),\phi(x;b^{i}_{k})\}
is inconsistent.

Lemma 4.16
.

If
\phi(x;y)
has
\operatorname{TP2}
then
\phi
has
\operatorname{IP}
.

Proof.

Let
\{\phi(x,b^{i}_{j})\}_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
witness
\operatorname{TP2}
for
\phi(x;y)
. Fix
j
. Without loss of generality we will assume that
j=0
. Consider the set
\{b^{i}_{0}\}
. Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. By
\operatorname{TP2}
there is
a_{I}\in M^{x}
such that

\mathcal{M}\models\phi(a_{I};b^{i}_{j})\iff(i\in I\text{ and }j=0,\text{ or }i\notin I\text{ and }j=1).

Thus by Lemma
4.14

\phi(x;y)
has IP.
∎

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
A=\{a_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq M^{x}
and
B=\{b_{I}:I\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})\}\subseteq M^{y}
. Assume that there is
\phi(x;y)
such that for any fixed
b_{I}\in B

\models\phi(a;b_{I})\iff\text{ there is }i\in I\text{ such that }a=a_{i}.

Then
\phi
has
\operatorname{TP2}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi
,
A
, and
B
be as in the hypothesis of the Lemma. Let
P=\{p_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\}
be the set of primes where
p_{i}\neq p_{j}
for
i\neq j
. We construct
A^{i}_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
recursively as follows:

•

A^{0}_{j}:=\{p_{j}^{n_{0}}:n_{0}>0\}

•

A^{i}_{j}:=\{p_{n_{i}}^{m}:n_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\ m\in A^{i-1}_{j}\}.

So for example
A^{1}_{2}=\{p_{n_{1}}^{p_{2}^{n_{0}}}:n_{1},n_{0}>0\}
.

Claim 1
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
we have that
\bigcap_{i<n}A^{i}_{\alpha(i)}\neq\emptyset
.

It is not hard to check that

p_{\alpha(0)}^{\text{\reflectbox{$\ddots$}}^{p_{\alpha(n-1)}}}\in\bigcap_{i<n}A^{i}_{\alpha(i)}.

Claim 2:
For fixed
i
, and
j\neq k
we have
A^{i}_{j}\cap A^{i}_{k}=\emptyset
.

For simplicity in notation we prove the case where
i=1
. Let
m\in A^{1}_{j}\cap A^{1}_{k}
. Then
m=p_{m_{1}}^{p_{j}^{m_{0}}}=p_{n_{1}}^{p_{k}^{n_{0}}}
. Since
p_{j}^{m_{0}}
and
p_{k}^{n_{0}}
are nonzero, we have that
p_{m_{1}}=p_{n_{1}}
, and thus
p_{j}^{m_{0}}=p_{k}^{n_{0}}
. Similarly, since
m_{0}
and
n_{0}
are nonzero we conclude that
p_{j}=p_{k}
, and thus
j=k
.

Now let
b^{i}_{j}=b_{A^{i}_{j}}
. By compactness, together with Claim 1, we get that the set
\{\phi(x;b^{i}_{\sigma(i)}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is consistent.
By the hypothesis of the Lemma, together with claim 2, we get that for any
i
and
j\neq k
we have
\{\phi(x;b^{i}_{j}),\phi(x;b^{i}_{k})\}
is inconsistent.
∎

If
\phi(x;y)
and
A
are as in the lemma, we say that
\phi(x;y)
and
B

only shatter

A

in

M
. Note that in this case

is in fact a definable set.

Proposition 4.18
.

The formula
\varphi(x;y)
, defining the relation
R
as in section
4.2
, has
\operatorname{TP2}
.

Proof.

Let
\Theta
be a well-ordered subset of
\Gamma
and consider the sets

t^{\Theta}=\{t^{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\},\text{ and }B=\left\{\sum_{\delta\in\Delta}t^{\delta}:\Delta\subseteq\Theta\right\}.

It is clear then that
\varphi(x;y)
and
B
only shatter

, and thus by Lemma
4.17
the formula
\varphi(x;y)
has
\operatorname{TP2}
.
∎

Corollary 4.19
.

The formula
\varphi(x;y)
, defining the relation
R
as in section
4.2
, has
\operatorname{IP}
.

Proof.

The result follows directly from proposition
4.18
and lemma
4.16
.
∎

4.5
Undecidability for some Differential Hahn Fields

In this section we consider the Hahn field
K=C((t^{\Gamma}))
where
C
is a field of characteristic zero, and
\Gamma
is either
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Q}
. We equip
K
with the derivation
t\frac{d}{dt}
that is trivial on
C
and sends
t^{\gamma}
to
\gamma t^{\gamma}
. We prove below that the theory of the valued differential field
K
is undecidable. This result is folklore for
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
but we include it here since it is not easily found in the literature.

Lemma 4.20
.

\{c\in C:\ y^{\dagger}=cf^{\dagger}\text{ for some }y,f\in K^{\times},f\not\asymp 1\}=\mathbb{Q}

Proof.

Let
y=at^{r}(1+\delta)
and
f=bt^{s}(1+\epsilon)
in
K^{\times}
be such that
y^{\dagger}=cf^{\dagger}
with
a,b,c\in C
,
r,s\in\Gamma
,
s\neq 0
, and
\delta,\epsilon\in K^{\prec 1}
. Then
r+\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{1+\delta}=c(s+\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{1+\epsilon})
, so
r=cs
and thus
c=\frac{r}{s}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
For the other direction it is clear that if
c=\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
y=t^{p}
and
f=t^{q}
satisfy
y^{\dagger}=cf^{\dagger}
.
∎

Theorem 4.21
.

The theory of the valued differential field
K
is undecidable.

Proof.

By the Lemma above the subring
\mathbb{Q}
of
C
is (existentially) definable in the valued differential field
C((t^{\Gamma}))
. Applying Julia Robinson’s result from
[
14
]
we obtain that the theory is undecidable.
∎

Using the derivation
t\frac{d}{dt}
is only done to simplify certain expressions: it is clear that Theorem
4.21
goes through with
\frac{d}{dt}
as the derivation instead of
t\frac{d}{dt}
.

The valuation ring of the Hahn field
C((t^{\mathbb{Z}}))
is definable in the field
C((t^{\Gamma}))
by a theorem of Ax, (see for example
[
8
]
). Therefore:

Corollary 4.22
.

The theory of the differential field
C((t^{\mathbb{Z}}))
is undecidable.

Chapter 5
Operators with Support

Throughout
C
is an abelian group (additively written),
\mathfrak{M}
is a monomial set, and
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
range over
\mathfrak{M}
.
In the first section we define
strong
operators on the Hahn space

C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
and prove Lemma
5.4
to the effect that certain maps from
C\mathfrak{M}
to
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
can be extended to strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. This is useful in constructing strong derivations, see for example the proof of Lemma
7.12
.

In the second section
C
is a commutative ring and
\mathfrak{M}
a monomial group. This allows us to introduce the more restricted
notions of

operators and

operators on the Hahn ring

. Corollary

says how to use small operators in order to construct supported operators; this will be needed in the proof of Lemma

5.1
Strong Operators

Consider the Hahn space
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.
An
operator on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is by definition a map

P\ :\ C[[\mathfrak{M}]]\rightarrow C[[\mathfrak{M}]].

An operator
P
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is said to be

additive
if
P(f+g)=P(f)+P(g)
for all
f,g\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. If
P
and
Q
are additive operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, then so are
P+Q
and
-P
defined by
(P+Q)(f):=P(f)+Q(f)
and

(-P)(f):=-P(f)
. The set of additive operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with the above addition and multiplication given by composition is a ring with the null operator
O
as its zero element,
the identity operator
I
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
as its identity element, and

P+(-P)=O
for
P
in this ring.
We call an operator
P
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]

strongly additive
, or
strong
for short, if for every summable family
(f_{i})
in
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
the family
(P(f_{i}))
is summable and
\sum P(f_{i})=P(\sum f_{i})
. Strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
are additive, and the null operator
O
and the identity operator

I
are
strong. The following is a routine consequence of the definition of “strong”.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
P,Q
are strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, then so are
-P
,
P+Q
,
PQ
.

Thus the set of strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is a subring of the ring
of additive operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.
Let
(P_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. Then we say that
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
exists
if
for all
f\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
the family
(P_{i}(f))_{i\in I}

is summable.
If
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
exists, then the operator
f\mapsto\sum_{i}P_{i}(f)

on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is additive, and is denoted by
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
(or by
\sum P_{i}
if
I
is clear from the context). We do not claim that if

\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
exists, then
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
is strong.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(P_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.

(i)

If
I
is finite, then
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
exists, and

\sum P_{i}

is the usual finite sum of additive operators.

(ii)

If
I
and
J
are disjoint sets, and
(P_{j})_{j\in J}
is also a family of strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, and both
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
, and
\sum_{j\in J}P_{j}
exist, then
\sum_{k\in I\cup J}P_{k}
exists and equals
\sum_{i}P_{i}+\sum_{j}P_{j}
.

(iii)

If
\sigma:I\rightarrow J
is a bijection and
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
exists, then
\sum_{j\in J}P_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}
exists and equals
\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(P_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of strong operators on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
such that
\sum_{i}P_{i}
exists, and let
Q
be a strong operator on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. Then

\sum_{i}P_{i}Q
and
\sum_{i}QP_{i}
exist, and are equal to

(\sum_{i}P_{i})Q
and
Q(\sum_{i}P_{i})
, respectively.

The next lemma modifies a result by
van der Hoeven
[
11
, Proposition 3.5]
and is sometimes useful in
showing that an operator is strong.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
C
be a commutative ring with
1\neq 0
. Let

\phi:C\mathfrak{M}\rightarrow C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
be a map such that:

(i)

\phi((a+b)\mathfrak{m})=\phi(a\mathfrak{m})+\phi(b\mathfrak{m})
for all
a,b\in C
and
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
;

(ii)

\operatorname{supp}(\phi(c\mathfrak{m}))\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\phi(\mathfrak{m}))\cup\{\mathfrak{m}\}
for all
c\in C
and
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
;

(iii)

for every well-based
\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
,
the family
(\phi(\mathfrak{g}))_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}
is summable.

Then
\phi
extends uniquely to a strong operator on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.

Proof.

Let
f=\sum f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. We first show that the family
(\phi(f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}))_{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)}
is summable. Indeed, by (ii),

\bigcup_{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)}\operatorname{supp}(\phi(f_{m}\mathfrak{m}))\ \subseteq\ \left(\bigcup_{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)}\operatorname{supp}\phi(\mathfrak{m})\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}(f).

Since the right-hand side is well-based by (iii), so is the left-hand side.

Let any
\mathfrak{n}
be given. Then by (ii) again,

\displaystyle\{\mathfrak{m}\ \in\operatorname{supp}(f):\ \mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(\phi(f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}))\}\

\displaystyle\subseteq\ \{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f):\ \mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(\phi(\mathfrak{m}))\cup\{\mathfrak{m}\}\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\ \{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f):\ \mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(\phi(\mathfrak{m}))\}\cup\{\mathfrak{n}\}.

The right-hand side is finite by (iii), and so is the left-hand side. Thus

\phi
extends to an operator
\psi
on

C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
by
\psi(f):=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}(f)}\phi(f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m})=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}}\phi(f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m})
.
Now let
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
be a summable family in
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, and set
\mathfrak{G}:=\bigcup_{i\in I}\operatorname{supp}(f_{i})
. We claim that
\big(\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g})\big)_{(i,\mathfrak{g})\in I\times\mathfrak{G}}
is summable. First,

\bigcup_{(i,\mathfrak{g})\in I\times\mathfrak{G}}\operatorname{supp}(\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g}))\ \subseteq\ \bigcup_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}\operatorname{supp}(\phi(\mathfrak{g}))\cup\mathfrak{G},

so the left-hand side is well-based.
Secondly, let
\mathfrak{n}
be given. Then

\{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}:\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g}))\}\subseteq\{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}:\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(\phi(\mathfrak{g}))\}\cup\{\mathfrak{n}\},

so the left-hand side is finite.
For
i\in I
and
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}
we have

\mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g}))\ \Rightarrow\ f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\neq 0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathfrak{g}\in\operatorname{supp}f_{i}

and that
for a fixed
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}
the set
\{i\in I:\mathfrak{g}\in\operatorname{supp}(f_{i})\}
is finite.
Thus the set

\{(i,\mathfrak{g})\in I\times\mathfrak{G}:\ \mathfrak{n}\in\operatorname{supp}(\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g}))\}

is finite, and
our claim that
\big(\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g})\big)_{(i,\mathfrak{g})\in I\times\mathfrak{G}}
is summable
has been established.
It follows that

(\psi(f_{i}))_{i\in I}
is summable and

\sum_{i\in I}\psi(f_{i})\ =\ \sum_{i\in I}\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g})\ =\ \sum_{(i,\mathfrak{g})\in I\times\mathfrak{G}}\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g})\ =\ \sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}\sum_{i\in I}\phi(f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g})

=\ \sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}\phi\left(\big(\sum_{i\in I}f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\big)\mathfrak{g}\right)\ =\ \psi\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}\big(\sum_{i\in I}f_{i,\mathfrak{g}}\big)\mathfrak{g}\right)\ =\ \psi\left(\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}\right),

showing that
\psi
is strong.
∎

Strongly additive maps

For use in the last chapter we now consider a second monomial set
\mathfrak{N}
and define a map

P:C[[\mathfrak{M}]]\to C[[\mathfrak{N}]]
to be
strongly additive
if for every summable family
(f_{i})
in
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
the family
(P(f_{i}))
is summable in
C[[\mathfrak{N}]]
and
\sum P(f_{i})=P(\sum f_{i})
. Thus the trivial map sending every
f\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
to
0\in C[[\mathfrak{N}]]
is strongly additive.

Suppose
P
as above is strongly additive. Then
P
is additive:
P(f+g)=P(f)+P(g)
for
f,g\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. Moreover,
-P
is strongly additive, and if
Q:C[[\mathfrak{M}]]\to C[[\mathfrak{N}]]
is also a strongly additive map, then
P+Q
is strongly additive. Given also a monomial set
\mathfrak{G}
and a strongly additive map
Q:C[[\mathfrak{G}]]\to C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, the composed map

P\circ Q:C[[\mathfrak{G}]]\to C[[\mathfrak{N}]]
is strongly additive.

5.2

In this section
C
and
\mathfrak{M}
are equipped with product operations that
make
C
a commutative ring and
\mathfrak{M}
a
monomial group.
To say that an operator
P
on

has support

is additive,
\mathfrak{G}
is well-based, and
\operatorname{supp}P(g)\subseteq\mathfrak{G}\operatorname{supp}(g)

\mathfrak{G}

.

Let

on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
has support
\operatorname{supp}f
. In particular, it is additive, and so

P
on

the additive operator
fP
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
given by
(fP)(g):=f\cdot P(g)
.
In particular, the null operator
O
has support
\emptyset
, and the identity operator
I
has support
\{1\}
. Here is a key fact about supported operators:

Lemma 5.5
.

Every supported operator on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
is strong.

Proof.

Suppose the operator
P
on
C[[\mathfrak{M}]]
has support
\mathfrak{G}
, and let
(f_{i})\in C[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{I}
be a summable family. Then

\bigcup_{i}\operatorname{supp}P(f_{i})\ \subseteq\ \mathfrak{G}\cdot\bigcup_{i}\operatorname{supp}f_{i}

and the latter is a well-based subset of
\mathfrak{M}
. Let

\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
be given. Then there are only finitely many pairs
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{h})\in\mathfrak{G}\times\bigcup_{i}\operatorname{supp}(f_{i})
with
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{h}
. This yields a finite
I(\mathfrak{m})\subseteq I
such that
i\in I(\mathfrak{m})

whenever
i\in I
,
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{h})\in\mathfrak{G}\times\operatorname{supp}(f_{i})
, and

\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{h}
. In particular, for every
i\in I

with
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}P(f_{i})
we have
i\in I(\mathfrak{m})
.
Thus
\big(P(f_{i})\big)
is summable. Also
f=g+h
with
f:=\sum_{i}f_{i}
,
g:=\sum_{i\in I(\mathfrak{m})}f_{i}
and
h:=\sum_{i\notin I(\mathfrak{m})}f_{i}
, so

P(f)=P(g)+P(h)
with
\mathfrak{m}\notin\operatorname{supp}P(h)
. Hence

P(f)_{\mathfrak{m}}\ =\ P(g)_{\mathfrak{m}}\ =\ \sum_{i\in I(\mathfrak{m})}P(f_{i})_{\mathfrak{m}}\ =\ \sum_{i}P(f_{i})_{\mathfrak{m}}\ =\ \big(\sum_{i}P(f_{i})\big)_{\mathfrak{m}},

and thus
P(f)=\sum_{i}P(f_{i})
.
∎

The following two lemmas are rather obvious:

Lemma 5.6

If

In this chapter
{\mathbf{k}}
is a
differential

\der
(possibly trivial), and

\alpha,\beta,\gamma
range over
\Gamma
, and fix an additive map

In the first section we consider how the map

gives rise to a strong derivation on the Hahn field

extending the given derivation

of

In the second section we
consider how to solve differential equations
y-ay^{\prime}=f
for

6.1
Extending the Derivation on the Coefficient Field to the Hahn Field

The map
c
allows us to extend
\der
to a derivation on the field
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
, also denoted by
\der
, by declaring for
f=\sum_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}t^{\gamma}\in{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
that

\der\left(f\right)\ :=\ \sum_{\gamma}\Big(\der(f_{\gamma})+f_{\gamma}c(\gamma)\Big)t^{\gamma}.

Thus
(t^{\gamma})^{\prime}=c(\gamma)t^{\gamma}
.
For
f\in{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
we have
\operatorname{supp}f^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}f
, so

. In particular,
\der
is small, and the natural field isomorphism from

{\mathbf{k}}

\mathcal{O}/{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}
of

is an isomorphism of differential fields.
What are the constants of
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))

{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
is

Lemma 6.1
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathbf{k}}

(ii)

(iii)

, hence

f^{\dagger}\asymp 1
.

Thus the valued differential field
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
is asymptotic under the conditions of Lemma
6.1
.

Let

6.2
Adjoining Solutions of
y-ay^{\prime}=f

We wish to preserve truncation closedness under
adjoining solutions of differential equations

y-ay^{\prime}\ =\ f\qquad(a,f\in{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma})),\ a\prec 1).

This differential equation is expressed more suggestively as

(I-a\der)(y)=f
, where
I
is the identity operator on

{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
and
a\der
is considered as a (strong)
operator on
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
in the usual way. Note that
a\der

is even small as defined earlier, hence

I-a\der
is bijective, with inverse

(I-a\der)^{-1}\ =\ \sum_{n}(a\der)^{n}

Thus the above differential equation has a unique solution

y=(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)
in
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
. This is why we now turn our attention to the operator
(I-a\der)^{-1}
.

For
n\geq 1
,
0\leq m\leq n
we define
G^{n}_{m}(X)\in\mathbb{Z}\{X\}
recursively as follows:

•

G^{n}_{0}=0
,

•

G^{n}_{n}=X^{n}
,

•

G^{n+1}_{m}=X(\der(G^{n}_{m})+G^{n}_{m-1})
for
1\leq m\leq n
.

This recursion easily gives

(a\der)^{n}\ =\ \sum_{m=1}^{n}G^{n}_{m}(a)\der^{m}\qquad(n\geq 1),

hence

(I-a\der)^{-1}=I+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\sum_{m=1}^{n}G^{n}_{m}(a)\der^{m}.

(1)

Since
G^{n}_{m}(X)
is homogeneous of degree
n
, we have

G^{n}_{m}(a)\in{\mathbf{k}}t^{n\alpha}
for
a\in{\mathbf{k}}t^{\alpha}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
R
is a truncation closed differential subring of

{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
,
a\in R\cap{\mathbf{k}}t^{\Gamma}
,
a\prec 1
,
f\in R
, and
(I-a\der)^{-1}(g)\in R

for all
g\triangleleft f
. Then all proper truncations of
(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)
lie in
R
.

Proof.

We have

(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)=f+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\sum_{m=1}^{n}G^{n}_{m}(a)\der^{m}(f)
.
Also
a=a_{\alpha}t^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>0
, hence
\operatorname{supp}\left(G^{n}_{m}(a)\der^{m}(f)\right)\subseteq n\alpha+\operatorname{supp}f
for

1\leq m\leq n
. Consider a proper truncation
(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)|_{\gamma}

of
(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)
; we have to show that this truncation lies in
R
.
The truncation being proper gives
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
and
\beta\in\operatorname{supp}f
with

\gamma\leq N\alpha+\beta
.
Let
f_{1}:=f|_{\beta}
and
f_{2}:=f-f_{1}
. Then
f_{1},f_{2}\in R
and

(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)\ =\ (I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{1})+f_{2}+\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\sum_{m=1}^{n}G^{n}_{m}(a)\der^{m}(f_{2}).

Using
\operatorname{supp}f_{2}\geq\beta
and truncating at
\gamma
gives

(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)|_{\gamma}\ =\ (I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{1})|_{\gamma}+f_{2}|_{\gamma}+\left.\left(\sum^{N-1}_{n=1}\sum_{m=1}^{n}G^{n}_{m}(a)\der^{m}(f_{2})\right)\right|_{\gamma},

which lies in
R
, since
f_{1}\triangleleft f
and thus
(I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{1})\in R
.
∎

The key inductive step is provided by the next lemma.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
R
be a truncation closed differential subring of
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
. Let
a,f\in R
be such that
a\prec 1
and for all
b,g\in R
,

(i)

g\triangleleft f\Rightarrow(I-a\der)^{-1}(g)\in R
,

(ii)

b\triangleleft a\Rightarrow(I-b\der)^{-1}(g)\in R
.

Then all proper truncations of
(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)
lie in
R
.

Proof.

Assume
(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)|_{\gamma}\triangleleft(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)
. Then we have

\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}a
and
\beta\in\operatorname{supp}f
, and
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
such that

\gamma\leq N\alpha+\beta
.
Put
a_{1}:=a|_{\alpha}
,
a_{2}:=a-a_{1}
,
f_{1}:=f|_{\beta}
, and
f_{2}:=f-f_{1}
.
Set
P=a_{1}\der
,
Q=a_{2}\der
, so
a\der=P+Q
,
P,Q
are small, and

\displaystyle(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)\

\displaystyle=\ (I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{1})+(I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{2})

\displaystyle=\ (I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{1})+\big(I-(P+Q)\big)^{-1}(f_{2}).

Now
Q(I-P)^{-1}=\sum_{n}QP^{n}
is small, and we have the identity

\displaystyle I-(P+Q)\

\displaystyle=\ \big(I-Q(I-P)^{-1}\big)(I-P),\ \text{ so}

\displaystyle\big(I-(P+Q)\big)^{-1}\

\displaystyle=\ (I-P)^{-1}\big(I-Q(I-P)^{-1}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(I-P)^{-1}\big(Q(I-P)^{-1}\big)^{m},\text{ hence}

\displaystyle(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)\

\displaystyle=\ (I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{1})+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(I-P)^{-1}\big(Q(I-P)^{-1}\big)^{m}(f_{2}).

For
m\geq N
we have
\operatorname{supp}\big(Q(I-P)^{-1}\big)^{m}(f_{2})\geq\gamma
, so truncating at
\gamma
yields

(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)|_{\gamma}\ =\ (I-a\der)^{-1}(f_{1})|_{\gamma}+\left.\left(\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}(I-P)^{-1}\big(Q(I-P)^{-1}\big)^{m}(f_{2})\right)\right|_{\gamma}.

Since
f_{1}\triangleleft f
the first summand of the right hand side lies in
R
. Since
a_{1}\triangleleft a
, we have
(I-P)^{-1}(h)\in R
for all
h\in R
, and thus, using
a_{2},f_{2}\in R
,

\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}(I-P)^{-1}(Q(I-P)^{-1})^{m}(f_{2})\in R.

Therefore
(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)|_{\gamma}\in R
.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
E
be a truncation closed differential subfield
of
{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
. Let
\widehat{E}
be the smallest differential subfield of

{\mathbf{k}}((t^{\Gamma}))
that contains

and is closed under

for all

is truncation closed.

Proof.

Let

be a maximal truncation closed differential subfield of
\widehat{E}

containing

. (Such

exists by Zorn’s Lemma.) It suffices to show that

. Assume

. Then there exist

and

with

(I-a\der)^{-1}(f)\notin F
. Take such

and

for which

(\alpha,\beta)
is minimal for lexicographically ordered pairs of ordinals,
where
\alpha
is the order type of the support of

and
\beta
that of

. By minimality of

6.5
to

, and

. Since

is a

, this contradicts the maximality of

∎

6.3
Exponentiation

Our aim is to apply the material above
to the differential field
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
of purely exponential transseries.
The construction of
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
involves an
iterated formation of Hahn fields, where at each step we apply
the following general procedure stemming from
[
7
]
(but copied from
[
1
, Appendix A]
).

Define a
pre-exponential ordered field

to be a tuple
(E,A,B,\exp)
such that

1.

E
is an ordered field;

2.

A
and
B
are additive subgroups of
E
with
E=A\oplus B
and
B

convex in
E
;

3.

\exp\colon B\to E^{\times}
is a strictly increasing group morphism (so
\exp(B)\subseteq E^{>}
).

Let
(E,A,B,\exp)
be a pre-exponential ordered field. We view
A
as the part of
E
where exponentiation is not yet defined, and accordingly we introduce
a “bigger” pre-exponential ordered field
(E^{*},A^{*},B^{*},\exp^{*})
as follows:
Take a
multiplicative
copy
\exp^{*}(A)
of the ordered additive
group
A
with order-preserving isomorphism

\exp^{*}\colon A\to\exp^{*}(A)
,
and put
E^{*}\ :=\ E[[\exp^{*}(A)]]
. Viewing
E^{*}
as an ordered Hahn field over
the ordered coefficient field
E
, we set

A^{*}\ :=\ E[[\exp^{*}(A)^{\succ 1}]],\qquad B^{*}\ :=\ (E^{*})^{\preccurlyeq 1}\ =\ E\oplus(E^{*})^{\prec 1}\ =\ A\oplus B\oplus(E^{*})^{\prec 1}.

Note that
\exp^{*}(A)^{\succ 1}=\exp^{*}(A^{>})
. Next we extend

\exp^{*}
to
\exp^{*}\colon B^{*}\to(E^{*})^{\times}
by

\exp^{*}(a+b+\varepsilon)\ :=\ \exp^{*}(a)\cdot\exp(b)\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{n}}{n!}\qquad(a\in A,\ b\in B,\ \varepsilon\in(E^{*})^{\prec 1}).

Then
E\subseteq B^{*}=\operatorname{domain}(\exp^{*})
, and
\exp^{*}
extends
\exp
. Note that
E<(A^{*})^{>}
(but
\exp^{*}(E)
is cofinal in
E^{*}
if
A\neq\{0\}
). In particular, for
a\in A^{>}
, we have

\exp^{*}(a)\in\exp^{*}(A^{>})\ \subseteq\ (A^{*})^{>},\ \text{ so $\exp^{*}(a)\ >\ E$.}

Assume also that a derivation
\der
on the field
E
is given that respects
exponentiation, that is,
\der(\exp(b))=\der(b)\exp(b)
for all

b\in B
. Then we extend
\der
uniquely to a
derivation on
the field
E^{*}
, also denoted by
\der
, by requiring
that
\der
is strong as an operator on the Hahn field
E^{*}

over
E
, and that
\der(\exp^{*}(a))=\der(a)\exp^{*}(a)

for all
a\in A
. This falls under the general construction at the beginning of this section with
{\mathbf{k}}=E
and
\mathfrak{M}=\exp^{*}(A)
by taking the additive function

c:\exp^{*}(A)\to E
given by
c(\exp^{*}(a))=\der(a)
.
This extended derivation on
E^{*}
again respects
exponentiation in the sense that

\der(\exp^{*}(b))=\der(b)\exp^{*}(b)
for all

b\in B^{*}
.

Exponential ordered fields

For the sake of completeness and because we use this notion in the next chapters
we define an
exponential ordered field
to be an ordered field
E
equipped with a strictly increasing group morphism

\exp:E\to E^{\times}
from the additive group of
E
into its multiplicative group. Note that then
\exp(E)\subseteq E^{>}
, and
that this is basically the case of a pre-exponential ordered field

(E,A,B,\exp)
with
A=\{0\}
and
B=E
.

Chapter 7
Increasing Unions of Differential Hahn Fields

As we mentioned in the Introduction,
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
is an increasing union of differential Hahn fields. Here we abstract from some details of its construction in order to
better focus on features that matter for truncation.
In the first section we present the general set-up of constructing
an increasing union
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}=\bigcup_{n}{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
of
differential Hahn fields
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}={\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}]]
. In the second section we show how
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
fits into this setting.
In the third section we define
splitting
and show that various
extension procedures preserve being truncation closed under a splitting assumption.
In the fourth section we extend the derivation of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
to its completion
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, where
\mathfrak{M}=\bigcup_{n}\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
.
In the fifth section we show how to integrate in
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}

if the latter is
transserial
.
The main result of this chapter is Theorem
7.21
in the sixth section, to the effect that being truncation closed is preserved under an extension
procedure that involves integration as well as adjoining solutions of certain subsidiary first-order linear differential equations. This will be crucial in the next (and last) chapter.

7.1
The Basic Set-Up

Let
{\mathbf{k}}
be a field of characteristic zero and
\mathfrak{M}
a monomial group with distinguished subgroups
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
,
n=0,1,2,\dots
, such that, with

\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}:=\mathfrak{M}_{0}\cdots\mathfrak{M}_{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
, we have:

1.

\mathfrak{M}=\bigcup_{n}\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
;

2.

\mathfrak{M}_{m}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{n}
for all
m<n
and
\mathfrak{m}_{n}\in\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}_{n}
.

Thus
\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
is a convex subgroup of
\mathfrak{M}
. We also have the direct product group
\mathfrak{M}_{0}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{M}_{n}
with a group isomorphism

\mathfrak{M}_{0}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{M}_{n}\to\mathfrak{M}^{(n)},\quad(\mathfrak{m}_{0},\dots,\mathfrak{m}_{n})\mapsto\mathfrak{m}_{0}\cdots\mathfrak{m}_{n}

and for an element
(\mathfrak{m}_{0},\dots,\mathfrak{m}_{n})\in\mathfrak{M}_{0}\times\cdots\times\mathfrak{M}_{n}
with
\mathfrak{m}_{n}\neq 1
we have

\mathfrak{m}_{0}\cdots\mathfrak{m}_{n}\succ 1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \mathfrak{m}_{n}\succ 1.

Setting
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}:={\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}]]
we have

{\mathbf{k}}_{0}={\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{0}]]
and field extensions

{\mathbf{k}}\ \subseteq\ {\mathbf{k}}_{0}\ \subseteq\ {\mathbf{k}}_{1}\ \subseteq\ \cdots\subseteq\ {\mathbf{k}}_{n}\ \subseteq\ {\mathbf{k}}_{n+1}\ \subseteq\ \cdots.

When convenient we identify
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
with the
Hahn field
{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n}]]
over
{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
in the usual way
(where
{\mathbf{k}}_{-1}:={\mathbf{k}}
by convention). We set

{\mathbf{k}}_{*}\ :=\ \bigcup_{n}{\mathbf{k}}_{n},\quad\text{ a truncation closed subfield of the Hahn field }{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]].

More generally, a set
S\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
is said to be truncation closed
if it is truncation closed as a subset of the Hahn field
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. Recall from Chapter 3 that if
E\supseteq{\mathbf{k}}
is a truncation closed subfield of
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, then

\mathfrak{M}_{E}:=E\cap\mathfrak{M}=\operatorname{supp}E\cap\mathfrak{M}
is a subgroup of
\mathfrak{M}
with

E\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{K}]]
.

Closure under small exponentiation

The function
\exp:{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{\prec 1}\to{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
given by

\exp(\varepsilon)\ =\ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{i}/i!

maps
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}^{\prec 1}
bijectively onto
1+{\mathbf{k}}_{*}^{\prec 1}
. Thus if
E
is a subfield of

{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
, then its small exponential closure
E^{\operatorname{exp_{s}}}
is also a subfield of

{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
. And if
E\supseteq{\mathbf{k}}
is a truncation closed
subfield of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
, then
E^{\operatorname{exp_{s}}}\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}_{*}\cap{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{E}]]
and
E^{\operatorname{exp_{s}}}
is truncation closed.

The valuation

Take an order reversing group isomorphism
v:\mathfrak{M}\to\Gamma
onto an
additive ordered abelian group
\Gamma
. Then
v
extends to the valuation

v:{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]^{\times}\to\Gamma
given by
v(f)=v(\max\operatorname{supp}f)
. Note that for

a\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}={\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n}]]
we have:

a\prec_{\mathfrak{M}_{n}}1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ va>v(\mathfrak{M}^{(n-1)})\quad(\text{with }\mathfrak{M}^{(-1)}:=\{1\}).

Using that the
\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
are convex in
\mathfrak{M}

it follows that
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
is dense in the Hahn field

{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
with respect to the valuation topology given by
v
. Note also that for
f\in{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
,

f\succ\mathfrak{M}^{(n-1)}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f\succ{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}.

Equipping
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
with a derivation

We now assume that
{\mathbf{k}}
is even a differential field, and that for
every
n
there is given an additive map
c_{n}:\mathfrak{M}_{n}\to{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
.
Then we make
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
into a differential field by recursion on
n
:
{\mathbf{k}}_{0}={\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{0}]]
is equipped with the derivation
\der_{0}
given by the derivation of
{\mathbf{k}}
and the additive map
c_{0}
, and

{\mathbf{k}}_{n+1}={\mathbf{k}}_{n}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}]]
is equipped with the derivation
\der_{n+1}
given by the
derivation
\der_{n}
of
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}

and the additive map
c_{n+1}:\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}\to{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
. Thus
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
is a differential field extension of
{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
.
We make
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
into a differential field so that it contains every

{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
as a differential subfield. We let
\der
denote the derivation of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
, so
\der
extends each
\der_{n}
. It follows easily that

(\mathfrak{m}_{0}\cdots\mathfrak{m}_{n})^{\dagger}\ =\ c_{0}(\mathfrak{m}_{0})+\cdots+c_{n}(\mathfrak{m}_{n}),\qquad(\mathfrak{m}_{0}\in\mathfrak{M}_{0},\dots,\mathfrak{m}_{n}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}).

This suggest we define the additive function
c:\mathfrak{M}\to{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
by

c(\mathfrak{m}_{0}\cdots\mathfrak{m}_{n})\ :=\ c_{0}(\mathfrak{m}_{0})+\cdots+c_{n}(\mathfrak{m}_{n}),\qquad(\mathfrak{m}_{0}\in\mathfrak{M}_{0},\dots,\mathfrak{m}_{n}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}),

so
c
extends each
c_{n}
, and
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}=c(\mathfrak{m})
for all
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

If
f\in{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
and
f\prec 1
, then
\der(f)\prec 1
.
(
In other words:
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
has small derivation.
)

Proof.

Let
f\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
and
f\prec 1
. We show by induction on
n
that
\der(f)\prec 1
. For
n=0
this follows from
\der(f)\preccurlyeq f
. Let
n\geq 1
, so
f=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}

with all
f_{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
. Then

f\ =\ f_{1}+\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{\prec 1}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m},\qquad f_{1}\prec 1,

and thus
\der(f_{1})\prec 1
by the inductive assumption. Also

\der\big(\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{\prec 1}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\big)\ =\ \sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{\prec 1}}\big(\der(f_{\mathfrak{m}})+c_{n}(\mathfrak{m})f_{\mathfrak{m}}\big)\mathfrak{m}\ \prec\ 1,

which gives
\der(f)\prec 1
.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
f\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}^{\times}
. Then
f^{\dagger}\preccurlyeq g
for some
g\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
.

Proof.

We have
f=f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}(1+\varepsilon)
with

f_{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}^{\times}
,
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}
, and
\varepsilon\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}^{\prec 1}
. It remains to use that

f^{\dagger}\ =\ f_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\dagger}+c_{n}(\mathfrak{m})+\frac{\der(\varepsilon)}{1+\varepsilon},

with
\der(\varepsilon)\prec 1
by the previous lemma.
∎

The next result is about the constant field of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
and is an easy consequence of Lemma
6.1
.

Corollary 7.3
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

for every
n
the map
c_{n}:\mathfrak{M}_{n}\to{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
is injective and
c_{n}(\mathfrak{M}_{n})\cap{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}^{\dagger}=\{0\}
;

(ii)

{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
has the same constant field as
{\mathbf{k}}
.

We obtained
\der_{n}
as a strong operator on
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
when the latter is viewed as a Hahn field over

{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
, but an easy induction on

shows that
\der_{n}
is actually a
strong operator on the Hahn field

over
{\mathbf{k}}
. This fact will be used tacitly at various places in this chapter. Example:

\der\big(\exp(\varepsilon)\big)\ =\ \der(\varepsilon)\cdot\exp(\varepsilon)\qquad\text{for }\varepsilon\in{\mathbf{k}}_{*}^{\prec 1}.

It follows that if
E
is a differential subfield of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}

(that is,
\der E\subseteq E
), then so is

.

One natural question we could ask at this point is the following:
Is the differential field generated by a truncation closed subset of

truncation closed? The answer is no, even in the case where

, as we shall see at the end of the next section.

7.2
The Main Example:
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}

We now indicate how

\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
is a union
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
as above. Here

{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{0}=x^{\mathbb{R}}
, so

{\mathbf{k}}_{0}\ =\ \mathbb{R}[[x^{\mathbb{R}}]]\ =\ \mathbb{R}((t^{\mathbb{R}}))\quad\text{ with }\ t\ :=\ x^{-1}.

We equip
{\mathbf{k}}_{0}
with the strongly additive
\mathbb{R}
-linear derivation

\der_{0}=x\cdot\frac{d}{dx}
, that is, for
f=\sum_{r}f_{r}x^{r}\in{\mathbf{k}}[[x^{\mathbb{R}}]]
we have
\der_{0}(f)=\sum_{r}rc_{r}x^{r}
.
Thus
\der_{0}
is the derivation on the Hahn field
\mathbb{R}[[x^{\mathbb{R}}]]

over
\mathbb{R}
that corresponds to the trivial derivation on
\mathbb{R}
and the function
c_{0}:x^{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
c_{0}(x^{r})=r
. Next, we make

{\mathbf{k}}_{0}
into a pre-exponential ordered field

({\mathbf{k}}_{0},A_{0},B_{0},\exp_{0})
:

1.

for the ordering of
{\mathbf{k}}_{0}
we take its ordering as an
ordered Hahn field over
\mathbb{R}
; in fact, we have no other choice, since
{\mathbf{k}}_{0}
is real closed;

2.

A_{0}:=\mathbb{R}[[\mathfrak{M}_{0}^{\succ 1}]]
, and
B_{0}:=\mathbb{R}[[\mathfrak{M}_{0}^{\preccurlyeq 1}]]
;

3.

\exp_{0}(r+\epsilon)\>=\ e^{r}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{i}/i!
.

The derivation
\der_{0}
respects exponentiation.
This is stage
0
of our construction. At stage

we have
a pre-exponential ordered field
({\mathbf{k}}_{n},A_{n},B_{n},\exp_{n})

with a derivation
\der_{n}
on
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
that respects exponentiation. Moreover,
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}={\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n}]]

(with
{\mathbf{k}}_{-1}=\mathbb{R}
),

A_{n}={\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{\succ 1}]]
and
B_{n}={\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n}^{\preccurlyeq 1}]]
. Applying to
({\mathbf{k}}_{n},A_{n},B_{n},\exp_{n})

the construction that extends
(E,A,B,\exp)
in the previous section to
(E^{*},A^{*},B^{*},\exp^{*})
yields a pre-exponential ordered field

({\mathbf{k}}_{n+1},A_{n+1},B_{n+1},\exp_{n+1})
.
Thus

{\mathbf{k}}_{n+1}\ =\ {\mathbf{k}}_{n}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}]],\qquad\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}\ =\ \exp_{n+1}(A_{n}).

As in the previous section we now extend
\der_{n}
uniquely to a derivation

\der_{n+1}
on
{\mathbf{k}}_{n+1}
by requiring that
\der_{n+1}
is strong as an operator on the Hahn field
{\mathbf{k}}_{n+1}
over

{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
with

\der_{n+1}(\exp_{n+1}(a))=\der_{n}(a)\exp_{n+1}(a)

for all

. This extended derivation on

respects exponentiation and is the derivation on the Hahn field

over

that corresponds to the derivation
\der_{n}
on

{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
and the additive function

c_{n+1}\ :\ \mathfrak{M}_{n+1}\to{\mathbf{k}}_{n},\qquad c_{n+1}\big(\exp_{n+1}(a)\big)\ :=\ \der_{n}(a)\quad(a\in A_{n}).

In this way we obtain field extensions

Closure under exponentiation

Let
E
be a subfield
of
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
. We say that
E
is
closed under exponentiation
if
\exp(E)\subseteq E
. We define the
closure of
E
under exponentiation

to be the smallest subfield
F\supseteq E
of
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
that is closed under exponentiation;
we denote this
F
by
E^{\exp}
. Note that if
\der E\subseteq E
, then
\der E^{\exp}\subseteq E^{\exp}
.

Lemma 7.6
.

If
E\supseteq\mathbb{R}
is truncation closed, then so is
E^{\exp}
.

Proof.

Let
K\supseteq E\supseteq\mathbb{R}
be a truncation closed subfield of
E^{\exp}
, and suppose

K\neq E^{\exp}
. It is enough to show that then there exists a truncation closed subfield
L
of
E^{\exp}
that properly contains
K
. If
K
is not closed under small exponentiation, then
L:=K^{\operatorname{exp_{s}}}
works.

Assume that
K
is closed under small exponentiation. Let
a\in K
with
\operatorname{e}^{a}\notin K
be such that
o(a)
is minimal.
We have
a=a|_{1}+r+\varepsilon
with
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon\in K^{\prec 1}
, so
\operatorname{e}^{a}=\operatorname{e}^{a|_{1}}\cdot\operatorname{e}^{r}\cdot\exp(\varepsilon)

with
\operatorname{e}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}\subseteq K
and
\exp(\varepsilon)\in K
. Thus by the minimality of
o(f)
we have
a=a|_{1}
, that is,
\operatorname{supp}a\subseteq\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}
.
Then
\operatorname{e}^{a}\in\mathfrak{M}
by Lemma
7.4
, and so
L:=K(\operatorname{e}^{a})
is truncation closed.
∎

As promised, we now give an example of a truncation closed subfield of
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
such that the differential subfield of
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
generated by it is not truncation closed:

An example inside
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
where being truncation closed is not preserved.

Take two strictly decreasing sequences

\alpha_{0}>\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots,\text{ and }\beta_{0}>\beta_{1}>\beta_{2}\cdots

of real numbers such that
\alpha_{n}>\beta_{0}
and
\beta_{n}>0
for all
n
,
and the elements
g:=\sum_{n}\alpha_{n}x^{\alpha_{n}}
and
h:=\sum_{n}\beta_{n}x^{\beta_{n}}
of
{\mathbf{k}}_{0}=\mathbb{R}[[x^{\mathbb{R}}]]
are
d
-algebraically independent.
We now have the monomial

f\ =\ \exp\left(\sum_{n}x^{\alpha_{n}}+\sum_{n}x^{\beta_{n}}\right)\in\mathfrak{M}_{1},

so the subfield
\mathbb{R}(f)
of
{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
is truncation closed by (i) and (ii) of Proposition
3.2
, but
\mathbb{R}\langle f\rangle
, the differential field generated by
\mathbb{R}(f)
in
{\mathbf{k}}_{1}
, is not truncation closed.
(Note that
\mathbb{R}\langle f\rangle
is also the differential ring
generated by
\mathbb{R}(f)
in
{\mathbf{k}}_{1}
.) To see this, note that
g+h=f^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}\langle f\rangle
, so if
\mathbb{R}\langle f\rangle
were truncation closed, then both
g
and
h
would be in
\mathbb{R}\langle f\rangle
, making the differential transcendence degree of
\mathbb{R}\langle f\rangle
greater than
1
, a contradiction.

7.3
Splitting

In this section
E\supseteq{\mathbf{k}}
is a truncation closed subfield of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
. (We are in the general setting of Section
7.1
.)
The example at the end of the previous section shows that for the differential subfield of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
generated by
E
to be truncation closed, we are forced to make extra assumptions on
E
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
. We say that
S

splits
if for all
{\mathfrak{m}_{0}\cdots\mathfrak{m}_{n}}\in S

with
\mathfrak{m}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}_{i}
for
i=0,\dots,n
we have

{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}\in S
for
i=0,\dots,n
. Thus if
S\neq\emptyset
splits, then
1\in S
. Splitting is a monomial analogue of
truncation closed
. The “extra assumptions” involve splitting:

Lemma 7.7
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{M}_{E}:=E\cap\mathfrak{M}
splits, and

c(\mathfrak{M}_{E})\subseteq E
. Then the differential
subring
R
of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
generated by
E
is truncation closed,
and thus the differential subfield
\mathbb{Q}\langle E\rangle
of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
generated by
E
is truncation closed as well.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
is a subgroup of
\mathfrak{M}
and

E\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{E}]]
, and that
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}\cap{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{E}]]
is a
differential subfield of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
. Thus
R\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}_{*}\cap{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{E}]]
. Let
A\supseteq E
be any truncation closed subring of
R
such that
A\neq R
. It is enough to show that then there is a truncation closed subring of
R
that properly contains
A
. Take
n
minimal such that for some
f\in A\cap{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
we have
\der(f)\notin A
, and take such
f
with
o(f)

minimal. We claim that every proper truncation of
\der(f)
lies in
A
. (As a consequence,
A[\der(f)]
is a truncation closed subring of
R
that properly contains
A
.) The case
n=0
of the claim is clear. Let
n\geq 1
. Then

f=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
with all
f_{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
, and so

g\ :=\ \der(f)\ =\ \sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}}\big(\der(f_{\mathfrak{m}})+c_{n}(\mathfrak{m})f_{\mathfrak{m}}\big)\mathfrak{m}\ =\ \sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}}g_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m},\quad\text{where }g_{\mathfrak{m}}\ :=\ \der(f_{\mathfrak{m}})+c_{n}(\mathfrak{m})f_{\mathfrak{m}}.

Such a proper truncation equals

g|_{\mathfrak{p}}
where

\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{supp}(g)
, so
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{n}
,
\mathfrak{q}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n-1)}
,

\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n}
. In the rest of the proof
\mathfrak{m}
ranges over
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
. Then

g|_{\mathfrak{p}}\ =\ \sum_{\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}}g_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}+(g_{\mathfrak{n}}|_{\mathfrak{q}})\mathfrak{n}.

We have
\der(\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m})=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}}g_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
, and
\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
is a proper truncation of

f
and thus lies in
A
, with
o(\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m})<o(f)
, so

\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\succ\mathfrak{n}}g_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}\in A
.
Now
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{M}_{E}
, so
\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{M}_{E}
since
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
splits. As
A
is truncation closed, we have
f_{\mathfrak{n}}\mathfrak{n}\in A
, and so
f_{\mathfrak{n}}\in A\cap{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
. The minimality of
n
gives
\der(f_{\mathfrak{n}})\in A
,
so
g_{\mathfrak{n}}=\der(f_{\mathfrak{n}})+c(\mathfrak{n})f_{\mathfrak{n}}\in A
, hence
(g_{\mathfrak{n}}|_{\mathfrak{q}})\mathfrak{n}\in A
, and thus
g|_{\mathfrak{p}}\in A
, as promised.
∎

The proof of Lemma
7.7
shows that if
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
splits
and
c(\mathfrak{M}_{E})\subseteq E
, then

\mathfrak{M}_{E}=\mathfrak{M}_{R}=\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{Q}\langle E\rangle}
, so the assumption that
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
splits and
c(\mathfrak{M}_{E})\subseteq E
is inherited by
R
and
\mathbb{Q}\langle E\rangle
from
E
. The same is true
for the (truncation closed) henselization
E^{\text{h}}
of the field
E
in

{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
, since

E^{\text{h}}\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}_{*}\cap{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}_{E}]]
, and thus

\mathfrak{M}_{E^{\text{h}}}=\mathfrak{M}_{E}
. Note also that if

\der E\subseteq E
, then
c(\mathfrak{M}_{E})\subseteq E
.

It will be useful to consider some more extension procedures and see
when splitting and the like are preserved.
Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a subset of
\mathfrak{M}
. Then
E[\mathfrak{G}]
and

E(\mathfrak{G})
are truncation closed by (i) and (ii) of Proposition
3.2
. Let
\mathfrak{N}
be the subgroup of
\mathfrak{M}
generated by
\mathfrak{G}
over
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
. Then
E(\mathfrak{G})\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{N}]]
, and
thus
\mathfrak{M}_{E(\mathfrak{G})}=\mathfrak{N}
. Note that if
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
and
\mathfrak{G}
split, then so does

\mathfrak{N}
, and if
c(\mathfrak{M}_{E}),c(\mathfrak{G})\subseteq E(\mathfrak{G})
, then
c(\mathfrak{N})\subseteq E(\mathfrak{G})
. Here is a consequence:

Lemma 7.8
.

Assume
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
splits. Let
F
be the algebraic closure of

E
in
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. Then
F
is truncation closed,
F\subseteq{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{F}
splits. If
c(\mathfrak{M}_{E})\subseteq E
, then

c(\mathfrak{M}_{F})\subseteq E
. If
\der E\subseteq E
, then
\der F\subseteq F
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{G}:=\{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{M}:\ \mathfrak{g}^{n}\in\mathfrak{M}_{E}\text{ for some }n\geq 1\}
. Then
\mathfrak{G}
equals
\mathfrak{N}
as defined in the remarks above, and

\mathfrak{G}
splits. By Lemma
3.3
we have
F=E(\mathfrak{G})^{\text{h}}
, so

\mathfrak{M}_{F}=\mathfrak{G}
, and thus previous remarks apply to give the desired result.
∎

With
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}=\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
as in the previous section,
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
is real closed, and then
F
as in the lemma above is the real closure in

{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
of its ordered subfield

.

Closure under exponentiation

In this subsection
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}=\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
as in Section
7.2
.

Lemma 7.9
.

If
\mathfrak{M}_{E}
splits, then

\mathfrak{M}_{E^{\exp}}
splits.

Proof.

Let
K\supseteq E
be a truncation closed subfield of
E^{\exp}
such that
\mathfrak{M}_{K}
splits, and suppose

K\neq E^{\exp}
. It is enough to show that then there exists a truncation closed subfield
L
of
E^{\exp}
that properly contains
K
such that
\mathfrak{M}_{L}
splits. If
K
is not closed under small exponentiation, then
L:=K^{\operatorname{exp_{s}}}
(with
\mathfrak{M}_{L}=\mathfrak{M}_{K}
) works.

Assume that
K
is closed under small exponentiation. Let
a\in K
with
\operatorname{e}^{a}\notin K
be such that
o(a)
is minimal.
We have
a=a|_{1}+r+\varepsilon
with
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon\in K^{\prec 1}
, so
\operatorname{e}^{a}=\operatorname{e}^{a|_{1}}\cdot\operatorname{e}^{r}\cdot\exp(\varepsilon)

with
\operatorname{e}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}\subseteq K
and
\exp(\varepsilon)\in K
. Thus by the minimality of
o(f)
we have
a=a|_{1}
, that is,
\operatorname{supp}a\subseteq\mathfrak{M}^{\succ 1}
.
Then
\operatorname{e}^{a}\in\mathfrak{M}
by Lemma
7.4
, and so
L:=K(\operatorname{e}^{a})
is truncation closed. It remains to show that
\mathfrak{M}_{L}
splits.
With
\mathfrak{m}:=\operatorname{e}^{a}
, it is enough to show that
\{1,\mathfrak{m}\}
splits by earlier remarks.
Take
n
with
a\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
. We can assume that
n
here is minimal in the sense that there is no
b\in K\cap{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
with
o(b)\leq o(a)
and

\operatorname{e}^{b}\notin K
. Then

a=a_{n}+a_{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
with
a_{i}\in A_{i}
for

and
a_{n}\neq 0
. Each partial sum
a_{n}+a_{n-1}+\cdots+a_{i}
is a truncation of
a
and so
lies in
K
, hence all

. If
a_{i}=0
for all

, then
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}
, and so

splits and we are done. Suppose

for some

. Then

, so
\operatorname{e}^{a_{n}}\in K
and

has the property that

and

, contradicting the minimality of

.

7.4
Extending the Derivation on
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
to
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]

Recall from Section
7.1
that

\der_{n}
is a strong operator on the Hahn field
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}={\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}]]
over
{\mathbf{k}}
.
Recall also that
\der
denotes the common extension of the
\der_{n}
to a derivation of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
.
Next we show how
\der
extends naturally to
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. The results of this section are not used later, but are included for their independent interest.
They might also become useful when trying to extend the results in this chapter and the next to the completions of
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
and
\mathbb{T}
.

Lemma 7.12
.

The derivation
\der
of
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
extends uniquely to a strong operator
on the Hahn field
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
over
{\mathbf{k}}
. This operator is a derivation on
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.

Proof.

We first show that the restriction of
\der
to
{\mathbf{k}}\mathfrak{M}
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
5.4
. Let
\mathfrak{m}
range over
\mathfrak{M}
. It is clear that for
a,b\in{\mathbf{k}}
,

\der((a+b)\mathfrak{m})\ =\ \der(a\mathfrak{m})+\der(b\mathfrak{m}),\qquad\operatorname{supp}\der(a\mathfrak{m})\ \subseteq\ \big(\operatorname{supp}\der(\mathfrak{m})\big)\cup\{\mathfrak{m}\}.

Let
\mathfrak{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
be well-based; we need to show that the family

(\der(\mathfrak{g}))_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}
is summable.
Towards a contradiction, assume
\bigcup_{\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}}\operatorname{supp}\der(\mathfrak{g})
is not well-based. Then we have a sequence

\mathfrak{m}_{0}\mathfrak{g}_{0}\ \prec\ \mathfrak{m}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\ \prec\ \mathfrak{m}_{2}\mathfrak{g}_{2}\ \prec\ \cdots

such that
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{G}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}c(\mathfrak{g}_{i})
for all
i
. Passing to a subsequence we arrange that

(\mathfrak{g}_{i})
is constant or strictly decreasing. The first case gives

\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}
with
\mathfrak{g}_{i}=\mathfrak{g}
for all
i
, and would give a strictly increasing sequence
(\mathfrak{m}_{i})

in
\operatorname{supp}c(\mathfrak{g})
, which is impossible. So
(\mathfrak{g}_{i})
is strictly decreasing. Passing once again to a subsequence we
arrange that either for some
n
all
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
, or there are
n_{0}<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n_{i})}\setminus\mathfrak{M}^{(n_{i}-1)}
for all
i
. Consider first the case that
n
is such that

\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
for all
i
,
and thus
\mathfrak{m}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
for all

i
. But
\big(\der_{n}(\mathfrak{g}_{i})\big)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable, with

\sum_{i}\der_{n}(\mathfrak{g}_{i})=\sum_{i}c(\mathfrak{g}_{i})\mathfrak{g}_{i}
, and the fact that the latter
sum exists in
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}]]

contradicts
\mathfrak{m}_{0}\mathfrak{g}_{0}\prec\mathfrak{m}_{1}\mathfrak{g}_{1}\prec\cdots
. Next,
suppose
n_{0}<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
are such
that
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n_{i})}\setminus\mathfrak{M}^{(n_{i}-1)}
for all
i
. Then
\mathfrak{g}_{i}=\mathfrak{h}_{i}\mathfrak{n}_{i}
with
\mathfrak{h}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n_{i}-1)}

and
\mathfrak{n}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n_{i}}\setminus\{1\}
for all
i
. It
follows that
(\mathfrak{n}_{i})
is strictly decreasing, and so

(\mathfrak{m}_{i}\mathfrak{g}_{i})
would be too, a contradiction.

Now, let
\mathfrak{m}
be given and suppose towards a contradiction that

\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}\der\mathfrak{g}
for infinitely many
\mathfrak{g}\in\mathfrak{G}
. Then

\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{supp}\der\mathfrak{g}_{i}
for a sequence

\mathfrak{g}_{0}\succ\mathfrak{g}_{1}\succ\mathfrak{g}_{2}\succ\cdots
in
\mathfrak{G}
. Passing to a subsequence we
arrange that either for some
n
all
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
, or there are
n_{0}<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n_{i})}\setminus\mathfrak{M}^{(n_{i}-1)}
for all
i
. If
n
is such
that all
\mathfrak{g}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
, then
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}
,
contradicting that
\sum_{i}\der_{n}(\mathfrak{g}_{i})
exists in
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}^{(n)}]]
. Next,
suppose
n_{0}<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}

Lemma 7.13

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

The direction (ii)

\der(f)\notin{\mathbf{k}}

7.5

{\mathbf{k}}_{*}

In this section we assume that the initial derivation on
{\mathbf{k}}
is trivial. (This is the case for
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}
where
{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbb{R}

1
has no antiderivative in
{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
.
More generally:

Lemma 7.14
.

Let
g\in{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. Then
1\notin\operatorname{supp}\der(g)
.

Proof.

With
g=\sum g_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
(where all
g_{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathbf{k}}
) we have

\der(g)=\sum g_{\mathfrak{m}}c(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m}
. For
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{0}
we have
c(\mathfrak{m})\in{\mathbf{k}}
, with
c(1)=0
, and thus
1\notin\operatorname{supp}g_{\mathfrak{m}}c(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m}
. Next, consider a monomial
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}\setminus\mathfrak{M}_{0}
. Then we have
n\geq 1
with
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}_{0}\cdots\mathfrak{m}_{n}
,
\mathfrak{m}_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}_{i}
for

and
\mathfrak{m}_{n}\neq 1
,
hence
g_{\mathfrak{m}}c(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m}\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}\mathfrak{m}_{n}
, in particular,

1\notin\operatorname{supp}g_{\mathfrak{m}}c(\mathfrak{m})\mathfrak{m}
.
∎

Thus if
f\in{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
and
1\in\operatorname{supp}f
, then
f\notin\der{\mathbf{k}}[[\mathfrak{M}]]
. Under a reasonable assumption on
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
(being transserial),

is the only obstruction for
f\in{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
to have an antiderivative in
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
, by the next lemma. The term “transserial” is suggested by the fact that by Lemma
7.5
the field
\mathbb{T}_{\exp}

of purely exponential transseries has the property we are now defining: we call
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}

transserial
if
c_{0}:\mathfrak{M}_{0}\to{\mathbf{k}}
is injective and
c(\mathfrak{m})\succ\mathfrak{M}^{(i-1)}
for all

and

\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{i+1}\setminus\{1\}
. Note that if
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
is transserial, then

is injective.

Lemma 7.15
.

Assume
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
is transserial. Let
f\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
be such that
1\notin\operatorname{supp}f
. Then
f\in\der{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
.
For
n=0
we have
f=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{0}^{\neq 1}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
(with all
f_{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathbf{k}}
) and then
f=\der(g)
for
g=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{0}^{\neq 1}}\frac{f_{\mathfrak{m}}}{c(\mathfrak{m})}\mathfrak{m}
. Assume the lemma holds for a certain
n
, and suppose

f\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n+1}
and
1\notin\operatorname{supp}f
. We have

f=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}}f_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
with all
f_{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
, and
1\notin\operatorname{supp}f_{1}
. The inductive assumption gives
f_{1}=\der(g_{1})
with
g_{1}\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
. Next, let

\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}
,
\mathfrak{m}\neq 1
; we wish to find
g_{\mathfrak{m}}\in{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
such that
\der(g_{\mathfrak{m}})+g_{\mathfrak{m}}c(\mathfrak{m})=f_{\mathfrak{m}}
,
that is,
\big(\der_{n}+c(\mathfrak{m})I_{n}\big)(g_{\mathfrak{m}})=f_{\mathfrak{m}}

(where
I_{n}
is the identity operator on
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}
), equivalently,

\big(I_{n}+c(\mathfrak{m})^{-1}\der_{n}\big)(g_{\mathfrak{m}})\ =\ c(\mathfrak{m})^{-1}f_{\mathfrak{m}}.

Using
c(\mathfrak{m})\succ\mathfrak{M}^{(n-1)}
we obtain from Section
6.2
that the additive operator

I_{n}+c(\mathfrak{m})^{-1}\der_{n}
on the Hahn field
{\mathbf{k}}_{n}={\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}[[\mathfrak{M}_{n}]]
over
{\mathbf{k}}_{n-1}
is bijective, so we do have a unique solution
g_{\mathfrak{m}}
as desired, and moreover
g_{\mathfrak{m}}=0
whenever
f_{\mathfrak{m}}=0
.
This yields the element
g:=g_{1}+\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}^{\neq 1}}g_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathfrak{m}
of
{\mathbf{k}}_{n+1}
satisfying
f=\der(g)
.
∎

Corollary 7.16
.

If
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
is transserial, then
f\in\der{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
for all
f\in{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
with

.

In the proof of Lemma

we saw how inverting operators

for

Lemma 7.17
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{k}}_{*}
is transserial. Then

.

Proof.

We verify condition (i) of Corollary

.
Thus we have to show that for every

Thus for transserial

differ by an element of

Lemma 7.18

Suppose

Proof.

We show by induction on

-valued, let

;

We have

, and

Proof.

With

∎

Proof of Theorem

Let

be a truncation closed differential subfield of
\mathbb{T}
such that

splits. Our job is to show that then the Liouville

of

in
\mathbb{T}
is truncation closed, and that

splits. First we observe that
x\in K^{\operatorname{Li}}
, and thus the iterated logarithms

are also in

.
Moreover,

is still a truncation closed

\mathbb{T}
and

for all

. We can also assume
K
is not Liouville closed. For such
K
it is enough to show: there exists a differential field extension
L\subseteq K^{\operatorname{Li}}
of
K
such that
K\neq L
,
L
is truncation closed, and
L\cap G^{\operatorname{LE}}
splits. We now treat separately the
various cases that may occur because of the
H
-field

not being Liouville closed:

Case 1:
K
is not real closed.
Then we can take for
L
the real closure of
K
in
\mathbb{T}
, by Lemma
8.8
.

Case 2:
K
is not closed under exponentiation.

Then we can take for
L
the exponential closure of
K
in
\mathbb{T}
,
by Propositions
8.1
and
8.2
.

Case 3:
f\in K
is such that

.

We begin by observing that
\mathfrak{e}_{n}{\downarrow}_{n}=\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
, and that if

, then no element of
\mathbb{R}[[\ell_{m}^{\mathbb{R}}]]^{\operatorname{E}}
has

, and take

such that

,

,

Then Theorem

8.4
Future Directions

In this section we discuss possible ways of extending the results in this dissertation. First, there are other natural extension procedures for differential subfields

K\supseteq\mathbb{R}
of
\mathbb{T}
besides taking the Liouville closure in
\mathbb{T}
. For example,
\mathbb{T}
is
linearly surjective
, and every
K
as above has a
linearly surjective closure
in
\mathbb{T}
. (A differential field
E
is said to be
linearly surjective
if every linear differential equation
y^{(n)}+a_{1}y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_{n}y=b
with
n\geq 1
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b\in E
has a solution in
E
. For
K
as above there is a smallest linearly surjective differential subfield of
\mathbb{T}
containing
K
, the

linearly surjective closure of
K
in
\mathbb{T}
.) The obvious question is whether Theorem
8.3
goes through for the
linearly surjective closure of
K
in
\mathbb{T}
instead of its Liouville closure in
\mathbb{T}
. Going beyond linear differential equations, the most powerful
elementary property of
\mathbb{T}
is that it is
newtonian
. (We refer to
[
1
, Chapter 14]
for a precise definition.) If
K
as above has
asymptotic integration
(as defined in
[
1
, Section 9.1]
), then
K
has a
newtonization

in
\mathbb{T}
(a smallest newtonian differential subfield of
\mathbb{T}
that contains
K
), and one can ask if Theorem
8.3
goes through for the newtonization
instead of the Liouville closure.

Another natural direction: try to connect the material above to surreal numbers. We refer to
[
2
]
for the description of a canonical embedding of

\mathbb{T}
as an exponential ordered field into the exponential ordered field
\mathbf{No}
of surreal numbers, with
x\in\mathbb{T}
corresponding to
\omega\in\mathbf{No}
. For subsets of
\mathbf{No}
, the notion of being
initial
plays a key role, and is similar to being truncation closed for subsets of Hahn fields. Elliot Kaplan has shown that the image of
\mathbb{T}
in
\mathbf{No}
under the canonical embedding is initial. This raises natural questions for truncation closed subfields
K\supseteq\mathbb{R}
of
\mathbb{T}
:

•

if
K\cap G^{\operatorname{LE}}
splits, does it follow that the image of
K
in
\mathbf{No}
is initial?

•

if the image of
K
in
\mathbf{No}
is initial, does it follow that
K\cap G^{\operatorname{LE}}
splits?
