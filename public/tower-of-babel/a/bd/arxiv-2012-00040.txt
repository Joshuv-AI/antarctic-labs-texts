Entanglement negativity at the critical point of measurement-driven transition
Bowen Shi, Xin Dai, Yuan-Ming Lu

Abstract
We study the entanglement behavior of a random unitary circuit punctuated by projective measurements at the measurement-driven phase transition in one spatial dimension. We numerically study the logarithmic entanglement negativity of two disjoint intervals and find that it scales as a power of the cross-ratio. We investigate two systems: (1) Clifford circuits with projective measurements, and (2) Haar random local unitary circuit with projective measurements. Remarkably, we identify a power-law behavior of entanglement negativity at the critical point. Previous results of entanglement entropy and mutual information point to an emergent conformal invariance of the measurement-driven transition. Our result suggests that the critical behavior of the measurement-driven transition is distinct from the ground state behavior of any unitary conformal field theory.

Can these critical phenomena be captured by an effective field theory? As observed in previous studies

;
Li et al. 2020
;

One way this might work is to match the entanglement behavior of the measurement-driven transition to that of some CFT ground-state. For a unitary CFT ground state, the mutual information of two disjoint intervals is known to depend on the full operator content of the theory

;
Calabrese et al. 2011
;
Calabrese et al. 2011

The same question arises for entanglement negativity: what does the scaling of negativity imply about the nature of the measurement-drive transition? As a main result of this paper, we numerically observe a power-law dependence of the logarithmic entanglement negativity (
4

5

III.3
Different “Quantumness” of Haar vs. Clifford circuit models

The Clifford circuit model and the Haar circuit model have almost identical scaling exponents within the error bar, for both the mutual information and the entanglement negativity, as shown in Fig.
5
. However, we have observed a slight difference in the “quantumness” of these two models, which we describe below.

For a generic mixed quantum states, while mutual information (
2
) measures the total amount of (both classical and quantum) correlation between two disjoint intervals
A
and
B
, the entanglement negativity vanishes for any separable states and therefore characterizes the quantum entanglement between the two intervals. Consequently the ratio of the negativity to the mutual information provides a measure of how much correlation comes from quantum entanglement between two regions
A
and
B
, i.e. the “quantumness” of the state. To compare the Clifford and the Haar circuit models, we define the following quantity:

R(\eta)\equiv\left(\frac{E_{N}^{A|B}(\eta)}{I_{A,B}(\eta)}\right)_{\textrm{Haar}}\left/\left(\frac{E_{N}^{A|B}(\eta)}{I_{A,B}(\eta)}\right)_{\textrm{Clifford}}\right.

(7)

to feature the excess amount of “quantumness” in the Haar circuit model as compared to Clifford circuit model.
\eta
is the cross-ratio defined in (
5
).

Our numerical data suggests that
R(\eta)
depends weakly on the cross-ratio
\eta
:

R(\eta)=R_{0}\eta^{\delta},\quad R_{0}=2.94,\,\delta=-0.04\pm 0.39.

(8)

R(\eta)\approx 3
in the whole range of cross-ratio that we simulate. While this does not imply any difference in the effective field theory description of the two models, this does suggest that there is a larger fraction of quantum entanglement in the Haar circuit model than the Clifford circuit model. Therefore the Haar circuit model appears to be more quantum than the Clifford circuit model at the measurement-driven transition.

IV
Summary and discussion

In this work, we have numerically studied the correlation and entanglement between two disjoint intervals at the measurement-driven phase transition in 1D Clifford and Haar random unitary circuits. We focus on the mutual information (
2
) and the logarithmic entanglement negativity (
4
). The late time quantum states are generically mixed on the union of the two intervals. Therefore, the mutual information measures the total correlation between the two intervals, whereas the entanglement negativity characterizes the quantum entanglement between the two intervals. We numerically simulate 1D systems with a periodic boundary condition, up to a system size
L=768
for the Clifford circuit model and
L=20
for the Haar circuit model. We identified a power-law behavior of the (logarithmic) entanglement negativity at small interval sizes. For a pair of disjoint intervals
A
and
B
,

E_{N}^{A|B}\propto\eta^{\Lambda}\quad\textrm{with }\,\Lambda\approx 3

(9)

at
\eta\ll 1
, where
\eta
is the cross-ratio for the pair of disjoint interval defined in (
5
).

It is interesting to compare (
9
) with recently obtained power-law behavior of the mutual information
Li et al. 2019
.

I_{A,B}\propto\eta^{\Delta}\quad\textrm{with }\,\Delta\approx 2

(10)

at
\eta\ll 1
. The fact that the scaling dimension
\Lambda>\Delta
suggests that the quantum entanglement between two disjoint intervals decays faster than the correlation as we increase the distance between the intervals. Moreover, we have observed that the Haar circuit model is “more quantum” than the Clifford circuit model, by comparing the ratio (
7
) of the entanglement negativity to mutual information in the two models.

As the main conclusion of this work, the power-law dependence of negativity on the cross-ratio indicates that the description of the measurement-driven transition in random unitary circuits is distinct from the ground state of any unitary conformal field theory.
Previous studies on the scaling of single interval von Neumann and Renyi (entanglement) entropies, as well as the mutual information for two disjoint intervals, point to an emergent conformal invariance of the critical point
Li et al. 2020
. However, the precise nature of the CFT describing the phase transition is unclear
Li et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
. Remarkably, unlike the power-law mutual information between two disjoint intervals, in a unitary CFT ground state, the negativity always decays faster than any power law of cross-ratio
\eta
for small
\eta

Calabrese et al. 2012
;
Calabrese et al. 2013
. Therefore, the power-law behavior of entanglement negativity provides direct evidence that the entanglement property of the measurement-driven transition is distinct from that of the ground state of any unitary CFT.

Conformal symmetry may, instead, enter the story through statistical mechanical models. In such cases, the resulting property does not necessarily match the ground state properties
Jacobsen and Saleur 2008
.
Previously, Vasseur et al.
Vasseur et al. 2019
argued a vanishing central charge (
c=0
) for the CFT corresponding to the measurement-driven transition, and indicated that this CFT belongs to a class of non-unitary Logarithmic CFT. This is achieved by using the replica trick and mapping the entanglement entropy in a random unitary circuit with measurements to the change of free energy of a 2D statistical mechanical model w.r.t. twisting the boundary condition
Nahum et al. 2018
;
Bao et al. 2020
;
Jian et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2020
. The critical point of the statistical mechanical model belongs to the
(Q!)
-state Potts model in the
Q\rightarrow 1
limit, in the universality class of 2D percolation, perturbed by a relevant 2-hull operator. In the current work, inspired by recent numerical evidence of emergent conformal invariance in the mutual information, we assume the critical behavior of the measurement-driven transition is described by a CFT ground state, analogous to continuous phase transitions in equilibrium. The power-law behavior of entanglement negativity hence suggests this CFT cannot be unitary. Currently, it is not clear how to exactly map the entanglement negativity to a 2D statistical mechanics model, employing the idea of Ref.
Vasseur et al. 2019
. This is an interesting question to be addressed in the future.

While the power-law negativity in this work provides an extra constraint on the critical theory, many questions remain open for this entanglement phase transition. For example, how to extract other critical exponents of this dynamical critical point? What is the precise nature of the conformal symmetry that emerged at the critical point of the measurement-driven phase transition? How to understand the scaling power that we observed? We leave these questions to future works.

A.1
Stabilizer states

We will consider a quantum system built up from a set of qubits. The number of qubit of the whole system is
L
. In other words, the total Hilbert space is

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\otimes L},\quad\mathcal{H}_{2}=span\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}.

(11)

Below, we define three concepts: stabilizer group, stabilizer generators, and stabilizer states.

Definition A.1
.

(stabilizer group)
The stabilizer group
\mathcal{G}
is defined to be the Abelian group generated by the following operators
\{g_{1},g_{2},\cdots,g_{L}\}
acting on
{\mathcal{H}}
. These operators, which we shall call stabilizer generators, satisfy:

1.

Each
g_{i}
is a product of Pauli operators. In other words, it is a product of operators acting on individual sites, and on each site, the operator (up to a phase factor) can be chosen from the set
\{I,X,Y,Z\}
. Here
I
is the identity,
X
,
Y
,
Z
are single-qubit Pauli operators.

2.

g_{i}^{\dagger}=g_{i}
and
g_{i}g_{j}=g_{j}g_{i}
,
\forall i,j
.

3.

The set of operators are independent, i.e.

g_{i}\neq\prod_{j\neq i}g_{j}^{s_{j}},\quad s_{j}=0,1

(12)

Remark
.

The definition implies
g_{i}^{2}=1
. It follows from the definition that
|\mathcal{G}|=2^{L}
, where
|\mathcal{G}|
is the number of group element.
Due to the independence condition, the stabilizer generators can independently take
\pm 1
. For each choice, there is a unique quantum state (up to the overall phase factor) that has these as the eigenvalues.

Definition A.2
.

(stabilizer Hamiltonian)
We define the following Hamiltonian as the stabilizer Hamiltonian of
\mathcal{G}
:

H=-\sum_{j=1}^{L}\,g_{j},

(13)

where
\{g_{1},g_{2},\cdots,g_{L}\}
is the set of stabilizer generators.

Definition A.3
.

(stabilizer state)
The stabilizer state of
\mathcal{G}
is the unique quantum state (up to an overall phase factor)
|\psi\rangle
that satisfies

h|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle,\quad\forall h\in\mathcal{G}.

(14)

Remark
.

The stabilizer state is the unique ground state of the stabilizer Hamiltonian (
13
).

We define the following projector:

P\equiv\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{G}}h\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad P=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\prod_{j=1}^{L}(1+g_{j}).

(15)

It satisfies

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{\dagger}

(16)

\displaystyle P^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P.

(17)

In fact,
P
is the projector to the ground subspace of the stabilizer Hamiltonian and therefore

P=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|.

(18)

In this way, we get a neat formula for the ground state density matrix.

Let us consider a subsystem
A
of the system and let the system be
AB
. Let
L_{A}
be the number of qubits in
A
.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{A}
be the subgroup of stabilizers supported on
A
. See the formal definition below.

Definition A.4
.

\mathcal{G}_{A}\equiv\{h_{A}\,|h_{A}\otimes 1_{B}\in\mathcal{G}\}.

(19)

It is easy to say
\mathcal{G}_{A}
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
.

The reduced density matrix of the stabilizer state on subsystem
A
,
\rho_{A}\equiv{\rm Tr}_{B}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, can be written as

\rho_{A}=\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{A}|}{2^{L_{A}}}P_{A},\quad P_{A}\equiv\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{A}|}\sum_{h_{A}\in\mathcal{G}_{A}}h_{A}

(20)

or simply

\rho_{A}=\frac{1}{2^{L_{A}}}\sum_{h_{A}\in\mathcal{G}_{A}}h_{A}.

(21)

One easily verifies that
P_{A}
in (
20
) is a projector, and therefore

\rho_{A}^{2}=\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{A}|}{2^{L_{A}}}\rho_{A}.

(22)

A density matrix satisfying (
22
) must have a flat entanglement spectrum. In other words, all the nonzero eigenvalues of
\rho_{A}
are the same:
\lambda={|\mathcal{G}_{A}|}/{2^{L_{A}}}
. Furthermore,
|\mathcal{G}_{A}|=2^{m_{A}}
for some nonnegative integer
m_{A}
.

A.2
Entanglement entropy of stabilizer states

With the knowledge of the stabilizer states discussed above, it is straightforward to calculation its entanglement entropy. For a stabilizer state
\rho_{A}
, defined in (
20
), we have

S(\rho_{A})=S_{\alpha}(\rho_{A})=(L_{A}-m_{A}),\quad\alpha\in[0,1)\cup(1,\infty)

(23)

Here
S(\rho_{A})=-{\rm Tr}(\rho_{A}\log_{2}\rho_{A})
is the von Neumann entropy and
S_{\alpha}
is the Renyi entropy of order
\alpha
, defined as

S_{\alpha}(\rho_{A})=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log_{2}[{\rm Tr}\,(\rho_{A})^{\alpha}].

(24)

Note that Renyi entropy is not defined for
\alpha=1
. Nevertheless, Renyi entropy at the limit
\alpha\to 1
(in both
1^{+}
and
1^{-}
direction) gives us the von Neumann entropy.
As an aside, we can infer from (
23
) that
m_{A}\leq L_{A}
.

A practically useful method is to write the binary vectors of a known set of stabilizer generators
\{g_{1},\cdots,g_{L}\}
as a
L\times 2L
matrix over
F_{2}
:

M=(M_{A}|M_{B}),

(25)

where
M_{A}
is a
L\times 2L_{A}
matrix and
M_{B}
is a
L\times 2L_{B}
matrix; we have partitioned the systems into
A
and
B
of length
L_{A}
and
L_{B}=L-L_{A}
. Here each stabilizer generator is mapped to a row of length
2L
. (Explicitly, we shall choose the binary representations
I=(00)
,
X=(10)
,
Z=(01)
,
Y=(11)
.)

We have the formula:

m_{A}=L_{AB}-{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(M_{B}),

(26)

where the rank
{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(M_{B})
is defined over field
F_{2}
.

It is easy to see, the von Neumann entropy of a stabilizer state,
\rho_{A}
of (
20
), is

S(\rho_{A})={\rm Rank}_{[2]}(M_{A})-L_{A}.

(27)

Example A.1
.

Let
L=2
and the set of stabilizer generators be
\{XX,ZZ\}
. Then the matrix
M
reads

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&0&1&0\\
0&1&0&1\end{array}\right).

(28)

Let
L_{A}=1
, then

M_{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(M_{A})=2.

(29)

According to (
27
), we have
S(\rho_{A})=1
.

A.3
Entanglement negativity basics

Entanglement negativity is a bipartite mixed state entanglement measure. While it goes back to (Renyi
\alpha=1/2
) entanglement entropy for a pure state, for a mixed state it is in general different from any linear combinition of entanglement entropies. It characterizes the quantum entanglement between two regions
A
and
B
for a possibly mixed state
\rho_{AB}
, rather than the correlations between
A
and
B
. For example, we may consider a
separable
state

\rho_{AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\,\rho^{i}_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}^{i}

(30)

where
\{p_{i}\}
is a probability distribution and
\rho^{i}_{A}
,
\rho^{i}_{B}
are density matrices.
In general, a separable state can have nontrivial correlation between the two subsystems because, it is possible that the
mutual information

I_{A,B}(\rho_{AB})\equiv S(\rho_{A})+S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho_{AB})>0.

(31)

However, for a separable state, the correlation is classical. This correlation can be generated from local operation and classical communication (LOCC). Therefore, it cannot be used as a resource for quantum teleportation.

As a quantity non-increasing under LOCC
Vidal and Werner 2002
, (logarithmic) entanglement negativity can characterize the quantum entanglement between
A
and
B
, for a mixed state
\rho_{AB}
.

Definition A.5
.

(logarithmic entanglement negativity)
For
\rho_{AB}
, let us define the logarithmic entanglement negativity as

E_{N}^{A|B}(\rho_{AB})=\log_{2}\lVert\rho_{AB}^{T_{A}}\rVert_{1}.

(32)

Below, we explain what is
T_{A}
and the trace norm
\lVert A\rVert_{1}
.

To define
T_{A}
, we need to specify an orthonormal basis of subsystem
A
, and the operation
T_{A}
will (in general) be different if we pick a different basis. (Nevertheless, one can show that different
T_{A}
will result in the same trace norm
\lVert\rho_{AB}^{T_{A}}\rVert_{1}
. For this reason, we do not need to care about the basis choice.

Let us pick an orthonormal basis
\{|i_{A}\rangle\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
for
T_{A}
and for convenience, we also pick an orthonormal basis
\{|j_{B}\rangle\}
for
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Then,
T_{A}
can be defined as the linear transformation on the operators acting on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
that satisfies

(|i_{A},j_{B}\rangle\langle i^{\prime}_{A},j^{\prime}_{B}|)^{T_{A}}=|i^{\prime}_{A},j_{B}\rangle\langle i_{A},j^{\prime}_{B}|.

(33)

The trace norm
\lVert A\rVert_{1}
is defined as

\lVert A\rVert_{1}\equiv{\rm Tr}\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}.

(34)

When
A
is a Hermitian operator,
\lVert A\rVert_{1}
equals to sum of the absolute values of the eigenvalues of
A
.
Note that
\rho_{AB}^{T_{A}}
is a Hermitian operator when
\rho_{AB}
is a density matrix.

Remark
.

If
\rho_{AB}=|\psi_{AB}\rangle\langle\psi_{AB}|
is a pure state density matrix, then

E_{N}^{A|B}(\rho_{AB})=S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\rho_{AB}).

(35)

Entanglement negativity is more interesting if we consider a tripartite pure state
|\psi_{ABC}\rangle
or consider a mixed state
\rho_{AB}
.

A.4
Entanglement negativity of stabilizer states

Let
|\psi_{ABC}\rangle
be a stabilizer state on a tripartite system
ABC
.
We trace out the subsystem
C
and get

\rho_{AB}=\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{AB}|}{2^{L_{AB}}}P_{AB},\quad P_{AB}\equiv\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{AB}|}\sum_{h_{AB}\in\mathcal{G}_{AB}}h_{AB}.

(36)

This fact has been discussed around (
20
).

Let us denote the set of stabilizer generators of the subgroup
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
as
\{h_{AB}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{AB}}
, where the integer
m_{AB}
is the number of stabilizer generators of
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
.
Then, a question is how to calculate
E_{N}^{A|B}({\rho_{AB}})
efficiently given these stabilizer generators.

We find that it is useful to define a
m_{AB}\times m_{AB}
symmetric matrix
J
over field
F_{2}
:

J_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\{h^{i}_{A},h^{j}_{A}\}=0,\\
0&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(37)

where we have factorized the stabilizer generators as

h_{AB}^{i}=h_{A}^{i}\otimes h_{B}^{i}.

(38)

Note that,
J_{ii}=0
for all
i
. Therefore,
J
may also be treated as a skew-symmetric matrix over
F_{2}
.

Theorem A.1
.

For a stabilizer density matrix
\rho_{AB}
in (
36
),

E_{N}^{A|B}(\rho_{AB})=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J),

(39)

where
{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)
is the rank of
J
over field
F_{2}
.

Theorem
A.1
is useful in the calculation of the entanglement negativity of stabilizer states. See Sec.
A.5
below for the proof.

Theorem A.2
.

For a stabilizer density matrix
\rho_{AB}
in (
36
),

2E_{N}^{A|B}(\rho_{AB})\leq I_{A,B}(\rho_{AB}).

(40)

Remark
.

The same bound does not generalize to arbitrary quantum states. A simple way to see this is to observe that we can find a pure state
|\varphi_{AB}\rangle
on a 2-qubit system such that
S_{\frac{1}{2}}(\rho_{A})\geq\lambda S(\rho_{A})
for any real number
\lambda
. Here
\rho_{A}
is the reduced density matrix of
|\varphi_{AB}\rangle
.

Proof.

We only need to show

{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)\leq m_{AB}-m_{A}-m_{B}.

(41)

This is because we can rewrite the left-hand side of (
40
) using Theorem
A.1
and rewrite the right-hand side using (
23
).

For the calculation of
{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)
, we have the freedom to choose the set of stabilizer generators of
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
. Let us choose the following set

\{h_{A}^{i}\otimes 1_{B}\}_{i=1}^{m_{A}}\cup\{1_{A}\otimes h_{B}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{m_{B}}\cup\{l_{A}^{k}\otimes r_{B}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{m_{AB}-m_{A}-m_{B}},

(42)

where
\{h_{A}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{A}}
is the set of generators of
\mathcal{G}_{A}
and
\{h_{B}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{m_{B}}
is the set of generators of
\mathcal{G}_{B}
. We have factorized the stabilizer generators of
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
into a product on
A
and
B
.

In the basis (
42
), the matrix
J
takes a simple form

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\end{array}\right)^{\oplus m_{a}}\oplus\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\end{array}\right)^{\oplus m_{b}}\oplus J^{\prime},

(43)

where
J^{\prime}
is a symmetric
(m_{AB}-m_{A}-m_{B})\times(m_{AB}-m_{A}-m_{B})
matrix over field
F_{2}
, defined according to

J^{\prime}_{kk^{\prime}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\{l^{k}_{A},l^{k^{\prime}}_{A}\}=0,\\
0&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(44)

It is obvious from (
43
) that
{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)\leq m_{AB}-m_{A}-m_{B}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Below are two simple examples that illustrate the calculation.

Example A.2
.

Let
L_{A}=L_{B}=1
and the set of stabilizer generators of
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
be
\{XX,ZZ\}
. Then the matrix
J
is
2\times 2
:

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)=2.

(45)

Therefore, according to Theorem
A.1
,
E_{N}^{A|B}(\rho_{AB})=1
. This result makes sense because the stabilizer state for this case is a Bell state

|\psi_{AB}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle),

(46)

written in the
Z
-basis.

Example A.3
.

Let
L_{A}=L_{B}=L_{C}=1
and the set of stabilizer generators of
\mathcal{G}
be
\{ZZI,IZZ,XXX\}
. Then the subgroup
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
has a unique generator
ZZ
, and therefore the matrix
J
is
1\times 1
:

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\end{array}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)=0.

(47)

Therefore, according to Theorem
A.1
,
E_{N}^{A|B}(\rho_{AB})=0
. This result makes sense because the stabilizer state for this case is the GHZ state

|\psi_{ABC}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle),

(48)

written in the
Z
-basis.

A.5
The proof of Theorem
A.1

The following three lemmas directly lead to the proof of Theorem
A.1
.

Lemma A.3
.

{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)
is invariant under the change of the generators of
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
.

Proof.

Any change of generators can be done by a sequence of (1) permutations of generators (2) multiplying one generator to another. These operations induce an operation on the matrix
J
:

1.

Switch two rows
a\leftrightarrow b
and then switch two columns
a\leftrightarrow b
. (Here,
a\neq b
.)

2.

Add the
a
-th row to the
b
-th row and then add the
a
-th column to the
b
-th column over the field
F_{2}
. (Here,
a\neq b
.)

It is easy to see that these two operations do not change
{\rm Rank}_{[2]}(J)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

It is possible to choose a “standard basis” of stabilizer generators of
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
:

\cup_{i=1}^{m_{a}}\{a^{i}_{AB},b^{i}_{AB}\}\cup_{s=1}^{m_{c}}\{c^{s}_{AB}\},

(49)

such that

1.

The nonnegative integers
m_{a}
and
m_{c}
satisfy

2m_{a}+m_{c}=m_{AB}.

(50)

2.

When we write

\displaystyle a^{i}_{AB}

\displaystyle=a^{i}_{A}\otimes a^{i}_{B},

(51)

\displaystyle b^{j}_{AB}

\displaystyle=b^{j}_{A}\otimes b^{j}_{B},

\displaystyle c^{s}_{AB}

\displaystyle=c^{s}_{A}\otimes c^{s}_{B},

we have

\displaystyle\,\{a_{A}^{i},b_{A}^{i}\}=0,

\displaystyle\forall i,

(52)

\displaystyle\,[a_{A}^{i},b_{A}^{j}]=0,

\displaystyle i\neq j,

\displaystyle\,[a_{A}^{i},a_{A}^{j}]=[b_{A}^{i},b_{A}^{j}]=0,

\displaystyle\forall i,j,

\displaystyle\,[a_{A}^{i},c_{A}^{s}]=[b_{A}^{i},c_{A}^{s}]=0,

\displaystyle\forall s,i,

\displaystyle\,[c_{A}^{s},c_{A}^{t}]=0,

\displaystyle\forall s,t.

Equivalently, in the standard basis (
49
), we have a block-diagonal
J
:

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)^{\oplus m_{a}}\oplus\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\end{array}\right)^{\oplus m_{c}}.

(53)

Proof.

Note that the matrix
J
, defined in (
37
) can also be treated as a skew-symmetric matrix over
F_{2}
. (On
F_{2}
, we have
1+1=0
.) We can apply the standard method to bring it to the standard form (
53
). The procedure is to applying a sequence of pairs of row and column operations, where the column operation is similar to the row operation. This sequence of operations is suitable for our purpose because it corresponds to a sequence of redefinition of stabilizer generators. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

In terms of the number
m_{a}
defined above,

E_{N}^{A|B}(\rho_{AB})=m_{a}.

(54)

Proof.

The proof is based on a few simple observations:

1.

Let
\{h_{AB}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{AB}}
be the set of generators of stabilizer group
\mathcal{G}_{AB}
. Let

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{AB}^{i}\equiv(h_{AB}^{i})^{T_{A}}.

(55)

Then
\{\tilde{h}_{AB}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{AB}}
generates another stabilizer group, which we may denote as
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{AB}
.

2.

A simple property of partial transpose:

[(\lambda_{A}\otimes\lambda_{B})\cdot(\mu_{A}\otimes\mu_{B})]^{T_{A}}=(\mu_{A}^{T_{A}}\cdot\lambda_{A}^{T_{A}})\otimes(\lambda_{B}\cdot\mu_{B}).

(56)

3.

We must have

(h^{i}_{AB}h^{j}_{AB})^{T_{A}}=\pm\tilde{h}^{i}_{AB}\tilde{h}^{j}_{AB}

(57)

because that each stabilizer generator is a tensor product of factors acting on
A
and
B
.
We obtain “
+
” if
h^{i}_{A}
and
h^{j}_{A}
commute and we obtains “
-
” if
h^{i}_{A}
and
h^{j}_{A}
anti-commute.

With these simple observations, we find that the density matrix
\rho_{AB}
, written the standard basis (
49
) as

\rho_{AB}=\frac{1}{2^{L_{AB}}}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{a}}(1+a^{i}+b^{i}+a^{i}b^{i})\prod_{s=1}^{m_{c}}(1+c^{s}),

(58)

is of the following form after the partial transpose:

\rho_{AB}^{T_{A}}=\frac{1}{2^{L_{AB}}}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{a}}(1+\tilde{a}^{i}+\tilde{b}^{i}-\tilde{a}^{i}\tilde{b}^{i})\prod_{s=1}^{m_{c}}(1+\tilde{c}^{s}).

(59)

Because different stabilizer generators
\{\tilde{a}^{i},\tilde{b}^{j},\tilde{c}^{s}\}
can independently take
\pm 1
, one can easily verify (
54
). This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Data collapse details

Near the critical point
(p\approx p_{c})
, we expect the physical quantities of interest, e.g., entanglement entropy, mutual information and entanglement negativity to scale as a function of
(p-p_{c})L^{1/\nu}
. Here the dimensionless number
\nu
as related to the correlation length by

\xi\sim|p-p_{c}|^{-\nu}.

(60)

The function can be different for different quantities.

To calculate
p_{c}
and
\nu
, we perform data collapse for the mutual information
I_{A,B}
and the entanglement negativity
E_{N}^{A|B}
in the random Clifford circuit model with measurements:

\displaystyle I_{A,B}=f((p-p_{c})L^{1/\nu})

(61)

\displaystyle E_{N}^{A|B}=\widetilde{f}((p-p_{c})L^{1/\nu}),

(62)

where we have fixed the two intervals to be antipodal regions with length
|A|=|B|=L/8
whereas
f(\cdot)
and
\widetilde{f}(\cdot)
are functions.

We further perform data collapse for random Haar circuit model with measurements. This time, we consider the difference of half-chain entanglement entropy:

S_{1}(p)-S_{1}(p_{c})=g((p-p_{c})L^{1/\nu}),

(63)

where
g(\cdot)
is a function.

By following the protocol in Ref.
Houdayer and Hartmann 2004
, we find that
p_{c}=0.16(0.26),\nu=1.07(1.35)
in the Clifford (Haar) case. The results for
p_{c}
in both models are consistent with Ref.
Li et al. 2019
and
Skinner et al. 2019
. For the critical exponent
\nu
, our result in the Clifford circuit model is close to that of Ref.
Li et al. 2019
, but our results of Haar random circuit model differs with
\nu=2.01
in Ref.
Skinner et al. 2019
. This is understandable, as in the random Haar case the finite size effect is more significant.
