The effect of singularities and damping on the spectra of photonic crystals
Konstantinos Alexopoulos, Bryn Davies

Abstract
Understanding the dispersive properties of photonic crystals is a fundamental and well-studied problem. However, the introduction of singular permittivities and damping complicates the otherwise straightforward theory. In this paper, we study photonic crystals with a Drude-Lorentz model for the permittivity, motivated by halide perovskites. We demonstrate how the introduction of singularities and damping affects the spectral band structure and show how to interpret the notion of a “band gap” in this setting. We present explicit solutions for a one-dimensional model and show how integral operators can be used to handle multi-dimensional systems.



1
Introduction

Photonic crystals present interesting and useful wave properties. Even very simple photonic crystals, such as those composed of periodically alternating layers of non-dispersive materials, can display exotic dispersive properties. As a result, they are able to support band gaps: ranges of frequencies that are unable to propagate through the material
[
22
]
. These band gaps are the fundamental building blocks of the many different wave guides and wave control devices that have been conceived. Notable examples include flat lenses
[
17
]
, invisibility cloaks
[
14
]
, rainbow trapping filters
[
24
]
and topological waveguides
[
8
]
.

When working at certain electromagnetic frequencies (which often includes the visible spectrum), it is important to take into account the oscillatory behaviour of the free electrons in a metal. This behaviour leads to resonances at characteristic frequencies and gives metals a highly dispersive character (even before the introduction of macroscopic structure, as in a photonic crystal). Several different models exist to describe this behaviour. Most models are variants of the Lorentz oscillator model, whereby electrons are modelled as damped harmonic oscillators due to electrostatic attractions with nuclei
[
12
]
. A popular special case of this is the Drude model, in which case the restoring force is neglected (to reflect the fact that most electrons in metals are not bound to any specific nucleus, so lack a natural frequency of oscillation). Many other variants of these models exist, for instance by adding or removing damping from the various models,
cf.

[
18
]
or
[
11
]
, and by taking linear combinations of the different models, as in
[
19
]
.

A key feature that unites dispersive permittivity models is the existence of singularities in the permittivity. The position of these poles in the complex plane, which correspond to resonances, are one of the crucial properties that determines how a metal interacts with an electromagnetic wave. In conventional Lorentz models the poles appear in the lower complex plane
[
12
]
. The imaginary part of the singular frequency is determined by the magnitude of the damping, and the singularities accordingly fall on the real line if the damping is set to zero. In the Drude model, the removal of the restorative force causes the singularities to fall at the origin and on the negative imaginary axis.

A particularly important example of dispersive materials, that are central to the motivation for this study, are halide perovskites. They have excellent optical and electronic properties and are cheap and easy to manufacture at scale
[
13
]
. As a result, they are being used in many applications, including optical sensors
[
7
]
, solar cells
[
21
]
and light-emitting diodes
[
25
]
. The dielectric permittivity of halide perovskites has been shown to depend heavily on excitonic transitions, leading to a permittivity that has symmetric poles in the lower complex plane
[
13
]
.

There are a range of methods that can be used to capture the spectra of photonic crystals. For one-dimensional systems, explicit solutions typically exist and transfer matrices are particularly convenient. These were used for Drude materials in
[
20
]
and for undamped Lorentz materials in
[
11
]
, for example. In multiple dimensions, studies often resort to numerical simulation (for instance with finite elements). A valuable approximation strategy is a multi-scale asymptotic method known as high-frequency homogenisation
[
6
]
, which can be extended to approximate the dispersion curves in dispersive media
[
23
]
.

In this work, we will study photonic crystals composed of metals with permittivity inspired by that of halide perovskites, in the sense that it has symmetric poles in the lower complex plane. After setting out the Floquet-Bloch formulation of the periodic problem in section
2
, we will study the one-dimensional periodic Helmholtz problem in section
3
. We retrieve the dispersion relation which characterizes the halide perovskite system and show how its properties depend on the characteristics of the dispersive permittivity (namely, being real or being complex and having poles either on or below the real axis). Finally, in section
4
, we use integral methods and asymptotic analysis in order to obtain the dispersion relation for the two- and three-dimensional cases, showing how to extend our analysis to multi-dimensional photonic crystals.

2
Problem setting

2.1
Initial Helmholtz formulation

Let us consider
N\in\mathbb{N}
particles
D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{N}
which together occupy a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d\in\{1,2,3\}
. The collection of particles
\Omega
will be the repeating unit of the periodic photonic crystal. We suppose that permittivity of the particles is given by a Drude–Lorentz-type model, given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta\omega^{2}-i\gamma\omega},

(2.1)

where
\varepsilon_{0}
denotes the background dielectric constant and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are positive constants.
\alpha
describes the strength of the interactions,
\beta
determines the natural resonant frequency and
\gamma
is the damping factor. This is motivated by the measured permittivity of halide perovskites, as reported in
[
13
]
. We choose to use this expression as a canonical model for dispersive materials whose permittivities have singularities in the complex frequency space. Notice that (
2.1
) is singular at two complex values of
\omega
. These are given by

\omega^{*}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2\beta}\Big(-i\gamma\pm\sqrt{4\beta-\gamma^{2}}\Big).

(2.2)

By varying the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
we can force these singularities to lie in the lower half of the complex plane (
\gamma>0
), on the real line (
\gamma=0
) or to vanish completely (
\beta=\gamma=0
). We will make use of this property when trying to interpret the dispersion diagrams we obtain in the following analysis. We suppose that the particles are surrounded by a non-dispersive medium with permittivity
\varepsilon_{0}
. We assume that the particles are non-magnetic, meaning the magnetic permeability
\mu_{0}
is constant on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We consider the Helmholtz equation as a model for the propagation of time-harmonic waves with frequency
\omega
. This is a reasonable model for the scattering of transverse magnetic polarised light (see
e.g.

[
15
, Remark 2.1]
for a discussion). The wavenumber in the background
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
is given by
k_{0}:=\omega\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}
and we will use
k
to denote the wavenumber within
\Omega
. Let us note here that, from now on, we will suppress the dependence of
k_{0}
and
k
on
\omega
for brevity. We, then, consider the system of equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u+\omega^{2}\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}u=0\ \ \ &\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\Delta u+k_{0}^{2}u=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
u|_{+}-u|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}|_{+}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
u(x)-u_{in}(x)&\text{ satisfies the outgoing radiation condition as }|x|\to\infty,\\
\end{cases}

(2.3)

where
u_{in}
is the incident wave, assumed to satisfy
(\Delta+k_{0}^{2})u_{in}=0
, and the appropriate outgoing radiation condition depends on the dimension of the problem and of the periodic lattice.

2.2
Periodic formulation

We will assume that the collection of
N
particles is repeated in a periodic lattice
\Lambda
. We suppose that the lattice has dimension
d_{l}
, in the sense that there are lattice vectors
l_{1},\dots,l_{d_{l}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
which generate
\Lambda
according to

\displaystyle\Lambda:=\{m_{1}l_{1}+\dots+m_{d_{l}}l_{d_{l}}\ |\ m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

(2.4)

The fundamental domain of the lattice
\Lambda
is the set
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

\displaystyle Y:=\{c_{1}l_{1}+\dots+c_{d_{l}}l_{d_{l}}\ |\ 0\leq c_{1},\dots,c_{d_{l}}\leq 1\}.

(2.5)

The dual lattice of
\Lambda
, denoted by
\Lambda^{*}
, is generated by the vectors
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d_{l}}
satisfying
\alpha_{i}\cdot l_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
for
i,j=1,\dots,d_{l}
. Finally, the
Brillouin zone

Y^{*}
is defined by

Y^{*}:=(\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}\times\{\mathbf{0}\})/\Lambda^{*},

(2.6)

where
\mathbf{0}
is the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d-d_{l}}
. The Brillouin zone
Y^{*}
is the space that the reduced unit cell of reciprocal space.

Figure 1:
A periodic array of halide perovskite particles. Here, we have six particles
D_{1},\dots,D_{6}
repeated periodically in one dimension. Each of them has the halide perovskite permittivity
\varepsilon(\omega)
defined by (
2.1
).

The periodic structure, denoted by
\mathcal{D}
, is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\left(\bigcup_{m\in\Lambda}D_{i}+m\right).

Hence, the problem we wish to study is the following:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u+\omega^{2}\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}u=0\ \ \ &\text{ in }\mathcal{D},\\
\Delta u+k_{0}^{2}u=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{D},\\
u|_{+}-u|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}|_{+}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},\\
u(x_{l},x_{0})&\text{ satisfies the outgoing radiation condition as }|x_{0}|\to\infty.\\
\end{cases}

(2.7)

2.3
Floquet-Bloch theory

In order to study the problem (
2.7
), we will make use of Floquet-Bloch theory
[
10
]
. Let us first give certain definitions which will help with the analysis of the problem.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
f(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is said to be
\kappa
-quasiperiodic, with quasiperiodicity
\kappa\in Y^{*}
, if
e^{-i\kappa\cdot x}f(x)
is
\Lambda
-periodic.

Definition 2.2

(Floquet transform)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The Floquet transform of
f
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[f](x,\kappa):=\sum_{m\in\Lambda}f(x-m)e^{-i\kappa\cdot x},\ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \kappa\in Y^{*}.

We have that
\mathcal{F}[f]
is
\kappa
-quasiperiodic in
x
and periodic in
\kappa
. The Floquet transform is an invertible map
\mathcal{F}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow L^{2}(Y\times Y^{*})
, with inverse given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}[g](x)=\frac{1}{|Y^{*}_{l}|}\int_{Y^{*}}g(x,\kappa)\mathrm{d}\kappa,\ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
g(x,\kappa)
is extended quasiperiodically for
x
outside of the unit cell
Y
.

Let us define
u^{\kappa}(x):=\mathcal{F}[u](x,\kappa)
. Then, applying the Floquet transform to (
2.7
), we obtain the following system:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u^{\kappa}+\omega^{2}\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}u^{\kappa}=0\ \ \ &\text{ in }\mathcal{D},\\
\Delta u^{\kappa}+k_{0}^{2}u^{\kappa}=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{D},\\
u^{\kappa}|_{+}-u^{\kappa}|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},\\
\frac{\partial u^{\kappa}}{\partial\nu}|_{+}-\frac{\partial u^{\kappa}}{\partial\nu}|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},\\
u^{\kappa}(x_{d},x_{0})&\text{ is $\kappa$-quasiperiodic in $x_{d}$},\\
u^{\kappa}(x_{d},x_{0})&\text{ satisfies the $\kappa$-quasiperiodic radiation condition as }|x_{0}|\to\infty.\\
\end{cases}

(2.8)

The solutions to (
2.8
) typically take the form of a countable collection of spectral bands, each of which depends continuously on the Bloch parameter
\kappa
. The goal of our analysis is identifying and explaining the gaps between the spectral band. At frequencies within these band gaps, waves do not propagate in the material and their amplitude decays exponentially. As a result, they are the starting point for building waveguides and other wave control devices.

For real-valued permittivities, it is straightforward to define band gaps as the intervals between the real-valued bands:

Definition 2.3

(Band gap for real permittivities)
.

A frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
is said to be in a band gap of the periodic structure
\mathcal{D}
if it is such that (
2.8
) does not admit a non-trivial solution for any
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.

We are interested in materials for which the permittivity takes complex values, corresponding to the introduction of damping to the model. In which case, we elect to keep the frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
as a real number but allow the Bloch parameter
\kappa
to take complex values. In which case, the imaginary part of
\kappa
describes the rate at which the waves amplitude decays. It should be noted that it is also quite common to do the opposite by forcing
\kappa
to be real and allowing
\omega
to be complex valued, as in
[
4
,
23
]
for example.

In the real-valued case, it is clear that
\kappa
belongs to the Brillouin zone
Y^{*}
(which has the topology of a torus, due to the periodicity in
\kappa
). When
\kappa
is complex valued, its real part still lives in
Y^{*}
but its imaginary part can take arbitrary values. Thus,
\kappa
lives in a space that is isomorphic to
Y^{*}\times\mathbb{R}
.

In our setting, which is a damped model that is characterized by a complex permittivity, we have that
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
and it is less clear how to define a band gap. Intuitively, a band gap is a range of frequencies at which the damping is particularly large. Hence, we provide a modified definition for the notion of a band gap for complex permittivities in terms of local maxima of the amplitude decay:

Definition 2.4

(Band gap for complex permittivities)
.

We define a band gap for complex permittivities to be the set of frequencies
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
for which (
2.8
) admits a non-trivial solution with quasiperiodicity
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
and
|\Im(\kappa)|
is at a local maximum.

We will first study the problem in the one-dimensional setting. In one dimension, the problem is easier to manipulate and we are able to retrieve explicit expressions. Hence, we can get a variety of results concerning the characteristics of the quasiperiodic system. In particular, our main goal is to obtain the dispersion relation, an expression which relates the quasiperiodicities
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
with the frequencies
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, and study its properties. Then, in section
4
we will provide an analysis for higher dimensional systems and we will give the equivalent relation.

Let us first treat the Helmholtz problem (
2.8
) in the one-dimensional case. We will work on the interval
[-1,1]
, with
[-1,0)
denoting the background and
[0,1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(x,\omega)}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)+\omega^{2}\mu_{0}u(x)=0,

(3.1)

on the domain
[-1,1]
, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x,\omega):=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{0},\ \ &x\in[-1,0)\ \ \text{(background)},\\
\varepsilon(\omega),\ \ &x\in[0,1]\ \ \quad\text{(particle)},\end{cases}

(3.2)

with the boundary conditions

\displaystyle u(1)=e^{2i\kappa}u(-1)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}(1)=e^{2i\kappa}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}(-1).

(3.3)

3.1
Dispersion relation

We will now retrieve an expression for the solution to (
3.1
). Let us define the quantities

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}:=\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{c}:=\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}}.

(3.4)

For many of the results that follow, the crucial quantity will be the contrast between the material inside the particles and the background medium. With this in mind, we introduce the frequency-dependent contrast
\rho
as

\rho(\omega):=\frac{\sigma_{c}}{\sigma_{0}}=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}}}.

(3.5)

Then, the following expression holds for the solution to (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u
denote a solution to (
3.1
). Then,
u
is given by

\displaystyle u(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}\rho\sin\Big(\sigma_{0}x\Big)+\mathcal{B}\cos\Big(\sigma_{0}x\Big),&x\in[-1,0)\\
\mathcal{A}\sin\Big(\sigma_{c}x\Big)+\mathcal{B}\cos\Big(\sigma_{c}x\Big),&x\in[0,1],\end{cases}

(3.6)

where
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}
are two constants.

Proof.

We know that a solution to (
3.1
) must be given by

\displaystyle u(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}_{1}\sin\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}x\Big)+\mathcal{B}_{1}\cos\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}x\Big),\ \ \ \ &x\in[-1,0),\\
\mathcal{A}_{2}\sin\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}}x\Big)+\mathcal{B}_{2}\cos\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}}x\Big),\ \ \ \ &x\in[0,1],\end{cases}

(3.7)

where
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
are constants to be defined. This, also, gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}_{1}\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}\cos\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}x\Big)-\mathcal{B}_{1}\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}\sin\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}x\Big),\ \ \ \ &x\in[-1,0),\\
\mathcal{A}_{2}\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}}\cos\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}}x\Big)-\mathcal{B}_{2}\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}}\sin\Big(\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}}x\Big),\ \ \ \ &x\in[0,1].\end{cases}

Now, from the boundary transmission conditions in (
2.7
), we require

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}u(x)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}u(x)\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}(x)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}(x).

These conditions mean we must have that
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{2}
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\sqrt{{\varepsilon(\omega)}/{\varepsilon_{0}}}\mathcal{A}_{2}=\rho(\omega)\mathcal{A}
,
which gives the desired result.
∎

Using the boundary conditions (
3.3
), we can obtain the dispersion relation for the one-dimensional problem. This is a well-known result, that first appeared in a quantum-mechanical setting

and has since been shown to describe a range of periodic classical wave systems also

Theorem 3.2

(Dispersion relation)
.

Let
u
denote the solution to (
3.1
) along with the boundary conditions (
3.3
). Then, for
u
to be non-trivial, the quasiperiodicities
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies the dispersion relation

\displaystyle\cos(2\kappa)=\cos(\sigma_{0})\cos(\rho\sigma_{0})-\frac{1+\rho^{2}}{2\rho}\sin(\sigma_{0})\sin(\rho\sigma_{0}).

(3.8)

Proof.

From Lemma
3.1
, we have that
u
is given by (
3.6
). Then, using (
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Big[\sin(\sigma_{c})+e^{2i\kappa}\rho\sin(\sigma_{0})\Big]\mathcal{A}+\Big[\cos(\sigma_{c})-e^{2i\kappa}\cos(\sigma_{0})\Big]\mathcal{B}=0,\\
\Big[\sigma_{c}\cos(\sigma_{c})-e^{2i\kappa}\rho\sigma_{0}\cos(\sigma_{0})\Big]\mathcal{A}-\Big[\sigma_{c}\sin(\sigma_{c})+e^{2i\kappa}\sigma_{0}\sin(\sigma_{0})\Big]\mathcal{B}=0.\end{cases}

(3.9)

We observe that for (
3.1
) to have a non-zero solution, it should hold

\displaystyle\Big[\sin(\sigma_{c})+e^{2i\kappa}\rho\sin(\sigma_{0})\Big]\cdot\Big[\sigma_{c}

\displaystyle\sin(\sigma_{c})+e^{2i\kappa}\sigma_{0}\sin(\sigma_{0})\Big]+

\displaystyle+\Big[\cos(\sigma_{c})-e^{2i\kappa}\cos(\sigma_{0})\Big]\cdot\Big[\sigma_{c}\cos(\sigma_{c})-e^{2i\kappa}\rho\sigma_{0}\cos(\sigma_{0})\Big]=0,

which gives

\displaystyle\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)}e^{4i\kappa}+\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon(\omega)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\sin(\sigma_{0})\sin(\sigma_{c})-2\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)}\cos(\sigma_{0})\cos(\sigma_{c})\right]e^{2i\kappa}+\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)}=0.

(3.10)

Making some algebraic rearrangements, we observe that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}}}\Big[\cos(\sigma_{0})\cos(\sigma_{c})-\cos(2\kappa)\Big]-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{0}}\sin(\sigma_{0})\sin(\sigma_{c})=0.\end{aligned}

(3.11)

Finally, making the substitutions
\sigma_{c}=\rho\sigma_{0}
and
\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)}=\rho\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

3.2
Properties of the dispersion relation

The dispersion relation (
3.8
) describes the behaviour of the periodic system and reveals the relationship between the quasiperiodicities
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
, the frequencies
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and the permittivity
\varepsilon(\omega)
of the material. We can use it to derive some simple results about the dispersion curves. The first thing to understand is the symmetries of the dispersion curves.

Lemma 3.3

(Opposite quasiperiodicities)
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
be a complex quasiperiodicity satisfying the dispersion relation (
3.8
) for a given frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, the opposite quasiperiodicity, i.e.
-\kappa
, satisfies the same dispersion relation.

Proof.

We just have to use that
\cos(\cdot)
is an even function. Then, if
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
is such that (
3.8
) holds, from the fact that
\cos(-2\kappa)=\cos(2\kappa)
, we get that
-\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
also satisfies (
3.8
). This concludes the proof.
∎

It is with Lemma
3.3
in mind that we are able to plot only the absolute values of the imaginary parts in Figure

and the subsequent figures.

3.2.1
Real and imaginary parts

In the analysis that will follow, it will be useful to be able to describe the behaviour of the real and imaginary part of the quasiaperiodicity with respect to the permittivity. In particular, we will decompose both the quasiperiodicity
\kappa
and
\rho
into real and imaginary parts, and we will derive this dependence from the dispersion relation. Since
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
and
\rho\in\mathbb{C}
, let us define:

\displaystyle\kappa=\kappa_{1}+i\kappa_{2}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \rho=\rho_{1}+i\rho_{2},

(3.12)

with
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. We will also define
\mathscr{L}_{1}
and
\mathscr{L}_{2}
, which depend on
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathscr{L}_{1}(\omega):=&\cos(\sigma_{0})\cos(\sigma_{0}\rho_{1})\cosh(\sigma_{0}\rho_{2})-\frac{\sin(\sigma_{0})}{2(\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2})}\Big[\rho_{1}(1+\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2})\sin(\sigma_{0}\rho_{1})\cosh(\sigma_{0}\rho_{2})-\\
&-\rho_{2}(\rho_{2}^{2}-1+\rho_{1}^{2})\cos(\sigma_{0}\rho_{1})\sinh(\sigma_{0}\rho_{2})\Big],\end{aligned}

(3.13)

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathscr{L}_{2}(\omega):=&\cos(\sigma_{0})\sin(\sigma_{0}\rho_{1})\sinh(\sigma_{0}\rho_{2})+\frac{\sin(\sigma_{0})}{2(\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2})}\Big[\rho_{2}(\rho_{2}^{2}-1+\rho_{1}^{2})\sin(\sigma_{0}\rho_{1})\cosh(\sigma_{0}\rho_{2})+\\
&+\rho_{1}(1+\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2})\cos(\sigma_{0}\rho_{1})\sinh(\sigma_{0}\rho_{2})\Big],\end{aligned}

(3.14)

where we note that
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
and
\sigma_{0}
all depend on the frequency
\omega
, as specified in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
). Then, we have the following result.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}

3.3
The effect of singularities and damping

As mentioned before, the dispersion relation of the halide perovskite particles leads to dispersion curves which are not trivial to understand in terms of the traditional viewpoint of band gaps. In order to understand the behaviour, we will examine each distint feature of the halide perovskite permittivity, to understand the effect it has on the spectrum of the photonic crystal.

In particular, we will begin with the simplest case when the permittivity is real and constant with respect to the frequency
\omega
. Then, we will introduce a dispersive behaviour to the permittivity by adding singularities at non-zero frequencies. We will initially suppose that these poles lie on the real axis and will study the behaviour close to these regions. Finally, we will study the effect of introducing a complex permittivity, corresponding to damping. Taken together, these results will allow us to explain the spectra the halide perovskite photonic crystal.

3.3.1
Constant permittivity

The first case we will consider is the one of a real-valued, non-dispersive permittivity, constant with respect to the frequency

and
\{\omega^{(-)}_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,\infty}
gives us the desired result.

We note that for the neighborhood of the form
\omega\in(\omega^{*},\omega^{*}+\delta]
for
\delta>0

\omega\downarrow\omega^{*}
.
∎

In addition to the occurrence of a countable number of band gaps close to the pole, in Figure (
5

\Im(\kappa)
of the quasiperiodicity
\kappa
as the frequency

approaches a permittivity pole. In fact, we see that close to a pole,

becomes arbitrarily big. This due to the resonance occurring here and is strongly related to the existence of infinitely many band gaps close to the pole. Actually, it is a corollary of Theorem
3.9
.

Corollary 3.9.1
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
be the associated quasiperiodicity satisfying the dispersion relation (
3.8
) and let
f(\omega)
be the function defined in (
3.34
). Let
\omega^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
denote a pole of the undamped permittivity
\varepsilon(\omega)
, given by (
3.33
). Then, for all
K>0
, there exists
\delta>0
, such that for all
p\in[-K,K]
, there exists
\omega_{p}\in[\omega^{*}-\delta,\omega^{*})
such that
\Im(\kappa(\omega_{p}))=p
. That is,
|\Im(\kappa(\omega))|
takes arbitrarily large values as
\omega\uparrow\omega^{*}
. The same result holds as
\omega\downarrow\omega^{*}
.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.9
, we have that for all
K>0
, there exists
\delta>0
, such that, for all
\omega\in[\omega^{*}-\delta,\omega)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1+\rho(\omega)^{2}}{2\rho(\omega)}>K.

We have that
\cos(\sigma_{0}(\omega))\cos(\rho(\omega)\sigma_{0}(\omega))
remains bounded close to
\omega^{*}
and because of the continuity of
\sin(\cdot)
, we can take
\sin(\sigma_{0}(\omega))>0
in
[\omega^{*}-\delta,\omega)
. Then, it follows that, for all
K>0
, in
[\omega^{*}-\delta,\omega)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1+\rho(\omega)^{2}}{2\rho(\omega)}\sin\Big(\sigma_{0}(\omega)\Big)>K.

But, from Theorem (
3.9
), we have the existence of infinitely many points
\omega^{(+)}_{0}<\omega^{(-)}_{0}<\omega^{(+)}_{1}<\omega^{(-)}_{1}<\dots
in
[\omega^{*}-\delta,\omega)
, for which,
\sin\Big(\rho(\omega)\sigma_{0}(\omega)\Big)
oscillates between 1 and -1 in each of the intervals of the form
[\omega^{(+)}_{0},\omega^{(-)}_{0}]
,
[\omega^{(-)}_{0},\omega^{(+)}_{1}]
,
\dots
, denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots.
This implies that, for all
K>0
, for all
p\in[-K,K]
, there exists
\omega_{p}\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\dots,
such that
f(\omega_{p})=p
. Since this holds for all
K>0
, it translates to
f
oscillating and taking all values between
+\infty
and
-\infty
in
[\omega^{*}-\delta,\omega^{*})
as we get closer to
\omega^{*}
.

Now, from (
3.8
) and (
3.34
), we get that

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{-i}{2}\ln\Big(f(\omega)\pm\sqrt{f(\omega)^{2}-1}\Big),

(3.42)

where
\ln(\cdot)
denotes the complex logarithm. We see that

\displaystyle\Im(\kappa)=\frac{1}{2}\Re\Big(\ln\Big(f(\omega)\pm\sqrt{f(\omega)^{2}-1}\Big)\Big).

Although, since we are using the complex logarithm, we have that

\displaystyle\Re\Big(\ln\Big(f(\omega)\pm\sqrt{f(\omega)^{2}-1}\Big)\Big)=\ln\Big|f(\omega)\pm\sqrt{f(\omega)^{2}-1}\Big|.

Hence, since we have shown that in
[\omega^{*}-\delta,\omega^{*})
, the function
f(\omega)
oscillates between
+\infty
and
-\infty
, we have that
\Big|f(\omega)\pm\sqrt{f(\omega)^{2}-1}\Big|
has the same behaviour in

, but the oscillation takes place between

and
+\infty
. Finally, since

is an increasing function, we obtain the desired result. Let us note that the proof is the same when we consider
\omega\downarrow\omega^{*}
, with the slight change that we consider neighborhoods of the form
(\omega^{*},\omega^{*}+\delta]
.

∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let

) with complex-valued, non-dispersive permittivity given by (

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to+\infty}|\Im(\kappa)|=+\infty.

Proof.

Let us recall that
\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to\infty}|\mathscr{L}_{1}|=\lim_{\omega\to\infty}|\mathscr{L}_{2}|=+\infty,

since applying this on (

3.3.4
Discussion

The analysis in this section can be used to understand the dispersion diagram for the halide perovskite photonic crystal that was presented in Figure
3
. There are two crucial observations. First, we saw in Section
3.3.2
that the introduction of singularities in the permittivity led to the creation of countably infinitely many band gaps in a neigbourhood of the pole, when the pole falls on the real axis. However, this exotic behaviour is not seen in Figure
3
, due in part to the introduction of damping causing the poles to fall below the real axis. This effect can also be exaplined in terms of the results in Section
3.3.3
, where we saw that the introduction of damping to a simple non-dispersive model smoothed out the band gaps. The behaviour shown in Figure
3
is a combination of these phenomena.

4
Multiple dimensions

Let us now treat the periodic structures in two- and three-dimensions. In this case, to be able to handle the problem concisely using asymptotic methods, we are interested in the case of small resonators. We will assume that there exists some fixed domain
D
, which the the union of the
N
disjoint subsets
D=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\dots\cup D_{N}
, such that
\Omega
is given by

\Omega=\delta D+z,

(4.1)

for some position
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
, and characteristic size
0<\delta\ll 1
. Then, making a change of variables, the quasiperiodic Helmholtz problem (
2.8
) becomes

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u^{\kappa}+\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\varepsilon(\omega)\mu_{0}u^{\kappa}=0\ \ \ &\text{ in }\mathcal{D},\\
\Delta u^{\kappa}+\delta^{2}k_{0}^{2}u^{\kappa}=0&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{D},\\
u^{\kappa}|_{+}-u^{\kappa}|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},\\
\frac{\partial u^{\kappa}}{\partial\nu}|_{+}-\frac{\partial u^{\kappa}}{\partial\nu}|_{-}=0&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},\\
u^{\kappa}(x_{d},x_{0})&\text{ is $\kappa$-quasiperiodic in $x_{d}$},\\
u^{\kappa}(x_{d},x_{0})&\text{ satisfies the $\kappa$-quasiperiodic radiation condition as }|x_{0}|\to\infty.\\
\end{cases}

(4.2)

We will also make an additional assumption on the dimensions of the nano-particles. This will allow us to prove an approximation for the values of the modes
u|_{D_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,N,
on each particle. The assumption is one of
diluteness
, in the sense that the particles are small relative to the separation distances between them. To capture this, we introduce the parameter
\rho_{i}
to capture the size of the reference particles
D_{1},\dots,D_{N}
. We define
\rho_{i}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(\text{diam}(D_{i}))
where
\text{diam}(D_{i})
is given by

\text{diam}(D_{i})=\sup\{|x-y|:x,y\in D_{i}\}.

(4.3)

We will assume that each
\rho_{i}\to 0
independently of
\delta
. This regime means that the system is dilute in the sense that the particles are small relative to the distances between them.

We will first present certain general results for this system. Then we will give a more qualitative description of the two-dimensional setting.

4.1
Integral formulation

Let
G^{k}(x)
be the outgoing Helmholtz Green’s function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined as the unique solution to

\displaystyle(\Delta+k^{2})G^{k}(x)=\delta_{0}(x)\ \ \text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

along with the outgoing radiation condition, where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac delta. It is well known that
G^{k}
is given by

\displaystyle G^{k}(x)=\begin{cases}-\frac{i}{4}H^{(1)}_{0}(k|x|),\ \ \ &d=2,\\
-\frac{e^{ik|x|}}{4\pi|x|},&d=3,\\
\end{cases}

(4.4)

where
H_{0}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of first kind and order zero. We define the quasiperiodic Green’s function
G^{\kappa,k}(x)
as the Floquet transform of
G^{k}(x)
in the first
d_{l}
coordinate dimensions, i.e.

\displaystyle G^{\kappa,k}(x)=\begin{cases}-\frac{i}{4}\sum\limits_{m\in\Lambda}H^{(1)}_{0}(k|x-m|)e^{im\cdot\kappa},\ \ \ &d=2,\\
-\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum\limits_{m\in\Lambda}\frac{e^{ik|x-m|}}{|x-m|}e^{i\kappa\cdot m},&d=3.\\
\end{cases}

(4.5)

Then, from
[
2
,
3
]
, we know that (
2.8
) has the following integral representation expression.

Theorem 4.1

(Lippmann-Schwinger integral representation formula)
.

A function
u^{\kappa}
satisfies the differential system (
2.8
) if and only if it satisfies the following equation

\displaystyle u^{\kappa}(x)-u^{\kappa}_{in}(x)=-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)\int_{\mathcal{D}}G^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}(x-y)u(y)\mathrm{d}y,\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(4.6)

where the function
\xi:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
describes the permittivity contrast between
\mathcal{D}
and the background and is given by

\xi(\omega)=\mu_{0}(\varepsilon(\omega)-\varepsilon_{0}).

4.2
Dispersion relation

We will now retrieve an expression which relates the subwavelength resonances of the system and the quasiperiodicities. The method used is similar to the one developed in
[
3
]
for systems of finitely many particles.

4.2.1
Matrix representaion

Let us define the following
\kappa
-quasiperiodic integral operators

\displaystyle K^{\kappa,r}_{D_{i}}:u|_{D_{i}}\in L^{2}(D_{i})\rightarrow-\int_{D_{i}}G^{\kappa,r}(x-y)u(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|_{D_{i}}\in L^{2}(D_{i})

(4.7)

and

\displaystyle R^{\kappa,r}_{D_{i}D_{j}}:u|_{D_{i}}\in L^{2}(D_{i})\rightarrow-\int_{D_{i}}G^{\kappa,r}(x-y)u(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|_{D_{j}}\in L^{2}(D_{j}).

(4.8)

Then, as in
[
3
]
, the scattering problem has the following matrix representation:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{1}}&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{2}D_{1}}&\dots&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{N}D_{1}}\\
-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{1}D_{2}}&1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{2}}&\dots&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{N}D_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{1}D_{N}}&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{2}D_{N}}&\dots&1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{N}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\kappa}|_{D_{1}}\\
u^{\kappa}|_{D_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
u^{\kappa}|_{D_{N}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u^{\kappa}_{in}|_{D_{1}}\\
u^{\kappa}_{in}|_{D_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
u^{\kappa}_{in}|_{D_{N}}\end{pmatrix}

Since the scattered field is fully determined by the value within each resonator, we will introduce the notation

\displaystyle u_{i}^{\kappa}:=u^{\kappa}|_{D_{i}},\ \ i=1,\dots,N.

(4.9)

Then, the resonance problem is to find
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
, such that there exists
(u^{\kappa}_{1},u^{\kappa}_{2},\dots,u^{\kappa}_{N})\in L^{2}(D_{1})\times L^{2}(D_{2})\times\dots\times L^{2}(D_{N})
,
u^{\kappa}_{i}\neq 0
, for
i=1,\dots,N,
such that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{1}}&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{2}D_{1}}&\dots&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{N}D_{1}}\\
-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{1}D_{2}}&1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{2}}&\dots&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{N}D_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{1}D_{N}}&-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{2}D_{N}}&\dots&1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{N}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\kappa}_{1}\\
u^{\kappa}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
u^{\kappa}_{N}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(4.10)

4.2.2
Resonances

Let us now retrieve the relation between the subwavelength resonant frequencies and the quasiperiodicities, obtained by studying the solutions to (
4.10
). We will first recall a definition and a lemma which will help in the analysis of the problem.

Definition 4.2
.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\lfloor N\rfloor:\mathbb{N}\to\{1,2,\dots,N\}
a modified version of the modulo function,
i.e.
the remainder of euclidean division by
N
. In particular, for all
M\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists unique
\tau\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
with
0<r\leq N
, such that

M=\tau\cdot N+r.

Then, we define
M\lfloor N\rfloor
to be

M\lfloor N\rfloor:=r.

We recall the diluteness assumption that we have made on our system, which is captured by considering small particle size
\rho
. We define
\rho:=\tfrac{1}{2}\max_{i}(\text{diam}(D_{i}))
where
\text{diam}(D_{i})
is given by

\text{diam}(D_{i})=\sup\{|x-y|:x,y\in D_{i}\}.

(4.11)

The next lemma is a variation of Lemma 2.6 in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
i=1,\dots,N
, we denote
u^{\kappa}_{i}=u^{\kappa}|_{D_{i}}
, where
u^{\kappa}
is a resonant mode, in the sense that it is a solution to (
4.6
) with no incoming wave. Then, for characteristic size
\delta
of the same order as
\rho
, we can write that

\displaystyle u^{\kappa}_{i}=\langle u^{\kappa},\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa}\rangle\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa}+O(\rho^{2}),\ \ i=1,\dots,N,

(4.12)

as
\rho\to 0
, where
\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa}
denotes the eigenvector associated to the particle
D_{i}
of the potential
K_{D_{i}}^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}
and
\rho>0
denotes the particle size parameter of
D_{1},\dots,D_{N}
. Here,
\delta
and
\rho
are of the same order in the sense that
\delta=O(\rho)
and
\rho=O(\delta)
. In this case, the error term holds uniformly for any small
\delta
and
\rho
in a neighbourhood of 0.

Let us now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.4
.

The resonance problem, as
\delta\to 0
and
\rho\to 0
, with
\delta=O(\rho)
and
\rho=O(\delta)
, (
4.10
) in dimensions
d=2,3
, becomes finding
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\det\Big(\mathcal{K}^{\kappa}(\omega)\Big)=0,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\kappa}(\omega)_{ij}:=\begin{cases}\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{i+1\lfloor N\rfloor}}\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa},\phi_{\kappa}^{(i+1\lfloor N\rfloor)}\rangle,&\text{ if $i=j$,}\\
-\mathscr{A}^{\kappa}_{i}(\omega,\delta)\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{j}D_{i}}\phi_{\kappa}^{(j)},\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa}\rangle\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{i+1\lfloor N\rfloor}}\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)},\phi_{\kappa}^{(i+1\lfloor N\rfloor)}\rangle,&\text{ if $i\neq j$,}\end{cases}

(4.13)

Here,
k_{0}=\omega\sqrt{\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}}
and

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{\kappa}_{i}(\omega,\delta):=\frac{\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)}{1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)\lambda^{(i)}_{\kappa}},\quad i=1,...,N.

(4.14)

with
\lambda_{\kappa}^{(i)}
and
\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa}
being the eigenvalues and the respective eigenvectors associated to the particle
D_{i}
of the potential
K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}}
, for
i=1,2,\dots,N
.

Proof.

We will provide an outline of the proof of this result, since it follows the exact same reasoning as, for example, the proof of Theorem 2.8 in
[
3
]
. Using the following pole pencil decomposition,

\displaystyle\Big(1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}}\Big)^{-1}(\cdot)=\frac{\langle\cdot,\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)}\rangle\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)}}{1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)\lambda^{(i)}_{\kappa}}+R_{i}[\omega](\cdot),\quad i=1,\dots,N,

(4.15)

we get that (
4.10
) is equivalent to the system of equations

\displaystyle u^{\kappa}_{i}-\frac{\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)}{1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)\lambda^{(i)}_{\kappa}}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1,j\neq 1}^{N}\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{j}D_{i}}u^{\kappa}_{j},\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)}\rangle\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{ for each }i=1,\dots,N.

The above system is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{i+1\lfloor N\rfloor}}u^{\kappa}_{i},\phi^{(i+1\lfloor N\rfloor)}_{\kappa}\rangle-\frac{\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)}{1-\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi(\omega)\lambda^{(i)}_{\kappa}}\sum\limits_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{j}D_{i}}u^{\kappa}_{j},\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa}\rangle\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{i+1\lfloor N\rfloor}}\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa},\phi^{(i+1\lfloor N\rfloor)}_{\kappa}\rangle=0.

From Lemma
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle u^{\kappa}_{i}\simeq\langle u^{\kappa},\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)}\rangle\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)},

which gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\kappa}(\omega)\begin{pmatrix}\langle u^{\kappa},\phi^{(1)}_{\kappa}\rangle\\
\langle u^{\kappa},\phi^{(2)}_{\kappa}\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle u^{\kappa},\phi^{(N)}_{\kappa}\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(4.16)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\kappa}(\omega)_{ij}:=\begin{cases}\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{i+1\lfloor N\rfloor}}\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa},\phi_{\kappa}^{(i+1\lfloor N\rfloor)}\rangle,&\text{ if $i=j$,}\\
-\mathcal{A}^{\kappa}_{i}(\omega,\delta)\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{j}D_{i}}\phi_{\kappa}^{(j)},\phi^{(i)}_{\kappa}\rangle\langle R^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{i+1\lfloor N\rfloor}}\phi_{\kappa}^{(i)},\phi_{\kappa}^{(i+1\lfloor N\rfloor)}\rangle,&\text{ if $i\neq j$.}\end{cases}

(4.17)

Then, for the system to have a non-trivial solution, we require

\displaystyle\det\Big(\mathcal{K}^{\kappa}(\omega)\Big)=0,

which gives the desired result.

4.3
The two-dimensional case

In the particular case of a two-dimensional system, it is possible to provide a more detailed and simplified version of the result in Theorem
4.4
. In particular, we will consider the setting where the periodic structure
\mathcal{D}
is composed of
N\in\mathbb{N}
resonators, denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, which are repeated periodically in the lattice
\Lambda
. Let us recall that in the dimension

, the
\kappa

G^{\kappa,r}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle G^{\kappa,k}(x)=-\frac{i}{4}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}H^{(1)}_{0}\Big(k|x-m|\Big)e^{im\cdot\kappa},

(4.18)

where
H^{(1)}_{0}
denotes the Hankel function of the first kind of order zero and has the following asymptotic expansion in terms of a small argument:

\displaystyle H^{(1)}_{0}(s)=\frac{2i}{\pi}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{s^{2m}}{2^{2m}(m!)^{2}}\left(\log(\hat{\gamma}s)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{j}\right).

(4.19)

4.3.1
Integral operators

We define the integral operators
K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}:L^{2}(D_{i})\to L^{2}(D_{i})
and
K^{\kappa,(0)}_{D_{i}}:L^{2}(D_{i})\to L^{2}(D_{i})
by

\displaystyle K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}[u](x):=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(\hat{\gamma}\delta k_{0})\int_{D_{i}}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}e^{im\cdot\kappa}u(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|_{D_{i}},

\displaystyle K^{\kappa,(0)}_{D_{i}}[u](x):=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{D_{i}}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}\log\Big(|x-y-m|\Big)e^{im\cdot\kappa}u(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|_{D_{i}},

and the integral operators
R^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}D_{j}}:L^{2}(D_{i})\to L^{2}(D_{j})
and
R^{\kappa,(0)}_{D_{i}D_{j}}:L^{2}(D_{i})\to L^{2}(D_{j})
by

\displaystyle R^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}D_{j}}[u](x):=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(\hat{\gamma}\delta k_{0})\int_{D_{i}}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}e^{im\cdot\kappa}u(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|_{D_{j}},

\displaystyle R^{\kappa,(0)}_{D_{i}D_{j}}[u](x):=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{D_{i}}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}\log\Big(|x-y-m|\Big)e^{im\cdot\kappa}u(y)\mathrm{d}y\Big|_{D_{j}},

for
i=1,\dots,N
. We will provide some results which will help us in the analysis of the problem.

Definition 4.5

We define the integral operators
M^{\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}}
and
N^{\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{j}}
for

\displaystyle M^{\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}}:=K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}+K^{\kappa,(0)}_{D_{i}}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ N^{\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}D_{j}}:=R^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}D_{j}}+R^{\kappa,(0)}_{D_{i}D_{j}}.

(4.20)

From the asymptotic expansion of the Hankel function in (

Proposition 4.6

For the integral operators

4.3.2
Spectral results

We have the following spectral results for the operators
K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}
and
K^{\kappa,\delta k_{0}}_{D_{i}}
, for
i=1,\dots,N
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\nu^{(\kappa)}_{-1,i}
and
\Psi_{-1,i}^{(\kappa)}
denote a non-zero eigenvalue and the associated eigenvector of the operator
K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}
, for
i=1,\dots,N
. Then,

\displaystyle\nu^{(\kappa)}_{-1,i}=-\frac{|D_{i}|}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}e^{im\cdot\kappa}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \Psi_{-1,i}^{(\kappa)}=\hat{\mathbbm{1}}_{D_{i}},

(4.22)

for
i=1,\dots,N
, where
\hat{\mathbbm{1}}_{D_{i}}:=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{D_{i}}}{\sqrt{|D_{i}|}}
and
|D_{i}|
denotes the volume of
D_{i}
.

Proof.

From the definition of
K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}
, for
i=1,\dots,N
, we observe that
K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}
is independent of
x\in D_{i}
, and so, normalizing on
L^{2}(D_{i})
, we get

\displaystyle\Psi_{-1,i}^{(\kappa)}=\hat{\mathbbm{1}}_{D_{i}}.

Then, the following must hold:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nu_{-1,i}^{(\kappa)}\hat{\mathbbm{1}}_{D_{i}}=K^{\kappa,(-1)}_{D_{i}}[\hat{\mathbbm{1}}_{D_{i}}]&\Rightarrow\nu_{-1,i}^{(\kappa)}\hat{\mathbbm{1}}_{D_{i}}=-\frac{|D_{i}|}{2\pi}\hat{\mathbbm{1}}_{D_{i}}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}e^{im\cdot\kappa}\\
&\Rightarrow\nu_{-1,i}^{(\kappa)}=-\frac{|D_{i}|}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\Lambda}e^{im\cdot\kappa}.\end{aligned}

This concludes the proof.
∎

.

5
Conclusion

We have used analytic methods to understand the dispersive nature of photonic crystals fabricated from metals with singular permittivities. In particular, we considered a Drude–Lorentz model inspired by halide perovskites that has poles in the lower complex plane. For a one-dimensional system, we characterised the effect that each feature of this model has on the dispersion relation. In particular, we showed that the introduction of singularities leads to the creation of countably many band gaps near the poles, whereas the introduction of damping smooths out the band gap structure. Finally, we showed how the integral methods developed in
[
2
,
3
]
can be used to extend this theory to multi-dimensional systems.
