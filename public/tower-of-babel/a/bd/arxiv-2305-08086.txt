A note on an application of discrete Morse theoretic techniques on the complex of disconnected graphs
Anupam Mondal, Pritam Chandra Pramanik

Abstract
Robin Forman’s highly influential 2002 paper A User’s Guide to Discrete Morse Theory presents an overview of the subject in a very readable manner. As a proof of concept, the author determines the topology (homotopy type) of the abstract simplicial complex of disconnected graphs of order n n (which was previously done by Victor Vassiliev using classical topological methods) using discrete Morse theoretic techniques, which are purely combinatorial in nature. The techniques involve the construction (and verification) of a discrete gradient vector field on the complex. However, the verification part relies on a claim that doesn’t seem to hold. In this note, we provide a couple of counterexamples against this specific claim. We also provide an alternative proof of the bigger claim that the constructed discrete vector field is indeed a gradient vector field. Our proof technique relies on a key observation which is not specific to the problem at hand, and thus is applicable while verifying a constructed discrete vector field is a gradient one in general. Keywords. disconnected graph, abstract simplicial complex, discrete Morse theory, gradient vector field, homotopy. 2020 MSC: 57Q70 (primary), 55U05, 05E45

Robin Forman developed discrete Morse theory as a combinatorial analogue of (smooth) Morse theory in

2
Preliminaries

In this note, by a
graph
(
of order
n
), we always mean a simple, finite, undirected, labelled graph on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The edge set of a graph
G
is a collection of
2
-subsets of its vertex set, and we denote it by
E(G)
. Since, we consider labelled graphs, two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
of the same order are the same if and only if
E(G_{1})=E(G_{2})
. In other words, we consider a pair of graphs to be different if they (i.e., their edge sets) are different up to labelling, even if they are the same
up to a graph isomorphism
.
We denote the edge consisting of the pair of vertices
i
and
j
, i.e., the edge
joining

i
and
j
, by
(i,j)
(or,
(j,i)
). If
(i,j)
is an edge in
G
, then by
G-(i,j)
, we mean the graph (of the same order) with the edge set
E(G)\setminus\{(i,j)\}
. On the other hand, if
(i,j)\notin E(G)
, then
G+(i,j)
denotes the graph (of the same order) with the edge set
E(G)\cup\{(i,j)\}
.

An (
abstract simplicial
)
complex
is a (finite, nonempty) collection, say
\mathcal{K}
, of finite sets with the property that every subset (including the empty set) of a set in the collection is also in the collection
\mathcal{K}
. A member of the collection
\mathcal{K}
is called a
simplex
of
\mathcal{K}
. The
dimension
of a simplex
\sigma
, denoted by
\dim(\sigma)
, is the number
|\sigma|-1
. If
\dim(\sigma)=d
, then
\sigma
is a
d
-dimensional simplex
(or simply, a
d
-simplex
). We denote a
d
-simplex
\sigma
by
\sigma^{(d)}
whenever necessary.

We note that the collection of the edge sets of all disconnected (labelled) graphs on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
is an abstract simplicial complex, and we denote it by
\mathcal{N}_{n}
. Abusing the notation, we consider the disconnected graphs themselves (instead of their edge sets) as the simplices of
\mathcal{N}_{n}
.

2.1
The basics of discrete Morse theory

First, we introduce the notion of a
discrete vector field
and a (discrete)
gradient vector field
on a complex as described in
[
2
,
3
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Discrete vector field)
.

A discrete vector field
\mathscr{V}
on a complex
\mathcal{K}
is a collection of ordered pairs of simplices of the form
(\alpha,\beta)
such that

(i)

\alpha\subsetneq\beta
,

(ii)

\dim(\beta)=\dim(\alpha)+1
,

(iii)

each simplex of
\mathcal{K}
is in
at most
one pair of
\mathscr{V}
.

If the simplex
\alpha^{(p)}
is paired off with the simplex
\beta^{(p+1)}
in
\mathscr{V}
(i.e.,
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathscr{V}
), then sometimes we pictorially represent it as
\alpha\rightarrowtail\beta
, and we say
\alpha
(or
\beta
) is the
tail
(or
head
)
of an arrow
in
\mathscr{V}
.

Given a discrete vector field
\mathscr{V}
on a complex
\mathcal{K}
, a
\mathscr{V}
-path is a sequence of simplices

\alpha_{0}^{(d)},\beta_{0}^{(d+1)},\alpha_{1}^{(d)},\beta_{1}^{(d+1)},\ldots,\alpha_{k}^{(d)},\beta_{k}^{(d+1)},\alpha_{k+1}^{(d)}

such that for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, the pair
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})\in\mathscr{V}
and
\beta_{i}\supsetneq\alpha_{i+1}\neq\alpha_{i}
. Such a path is a
non-trivial closed path
if
k\geq 0
and
\alpha_{k+1}=\alpha_{0}
.

Definition 2.2

(Gradient vector field)
.

A gradient vector field on a complex
\mathcal{K}
is a discrete vector field
\mathscr{V}
on
\mathcal{K}
which does not admit non-trivial closed
\mathscr{V}
-paths.

Definition 2.3

(Critical simplex)
.

Let
\mathscr{V}
be a gradient vector field on a complex
\mathcal{K}
. A nonempty simplex
\alpha
is a
critical simplex
(with respect to
\mathscr{V}
) if one of the following holds:

(i)

\alpha
does not appear in any pair of
\mathscr{V}
, or

(ii)

\alpha
is a
0
-simplex and
(\emptyset,\alpha)\in\mathscr{V}
.

We note that gradient vector fields can also be realized as, and often referred to in literature as
acyclic matchings
.

We recall that a CW complex is a topological space built recursively by gluing cells (which are homeomorphic copies of balls) of increasing dimension. The fundamental theorem of discrete Morse theory
[
2
,
3
]
states that if
\mathcal{K}
is a complex and
\mathscr{V}
is a gradient vector field on
\mathcal{K}
, then (the geometric realization of)
\mathcal{K}
is homotopy equivalent to a CW complex with exactly one cell of dimension
p
for each critical simplex (with respect to
\mathscr{V}
) of dimension
p
. The following is a useful corollary of the fundamental theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
3
]

If
\mathcal{K}
is a complex and
\mathscr{V}
is a gradient vector field on
\mathcal{K}
such that the only critical simplices are one
0
-simplex and
k
simplices of dimension
d
, then
\mathcal{K}
is homotopy equivalent to the wedge of
k
spheres of dimension
d
.

3
Construction of a discrete vector field
\mathscr{V}
on
\mathcal{N}_{n}

For the sake of completeness, we describe the construction of a discrete vector field
\mathscr{V}
on
\mathcal{N}_{n}
, as done in
[
3
, Section 5]
, in brief here. Construction of
\mathscr{V}
is done in stages, by first considering the edge
(1,2)
and then the vertices
3,4,\ldots
in the increasing order. At the first stage we have the following discrete vector field:

\mathscr{V}_{12}=\{(G,G+(1,2)):G\in\mathcal{N}_{n},(1,2)\notin E(G),G+(1,2)\in\mathcal{N}_{n}\}.

We note that any
G\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
, which is unpaired in
\mathscr{V}_{12}
, has two (connected) components with the vertices
1
and
2
belonging to different components, and vice versa.

Next, we consider a graph
G
that is unpaired in
\mathscr{V}_{12}
such that the vertices
1
(or
2
) and
3
are in the same component of
G
, and
G
doesn’t contain the edge
(1,3)
(or
(2,3)
). We extend
\mathscr{V}_{12}
by pairing off all such
G
with
G+(1,3)
(or
G+(2,3)
). Let
\mathscr{V}_{3}
denote the resulting discrete vector field. We note that any
G\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
, which is unpaired in
\mathscr{V}_{3}
, contains the edge
(1,3)
(or
(2,3)
), and have the property that the graph
G-(1,3)
(or
G-(2,3)
) has three components, one containing the vertex
1
, one containing the vertex
2
, and one containing the vertex
3
(and vice versa).

Next, we consider a graph
G
that is unpaired in
\mathscr{V}_{3}
, and pair it with
G+(1,4)
, or
G+(2,4)
, or
G+(3,4)
if possible (we note that
at most
one of these graphs is unpaired in
\mathscr{V}_{3}
). Let
\mathscr{V}_{4}
denote the resulting extended discrete vector field. We continue in this fashion, considering the vertices
5
,
6,\ldots,n
in the increasing order. Let
\mathscr{V}_{i}
denote the discrete vector field constructed after the consideration of vertex
i
, and finally
\mathscr{V}\coloneqq\mathscr{V}_{n}
.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.1
assuming
\mathscr{V}
to be a gradient vector field

As demonstrated in
[
3
, Section 5]
, with the assumption that
\mathscr{V}
is a gradient vector field on
\mathcal{N}_{n}
, we may determine the homotopy type of
\mathcal{N}_{n}
and settle Theorem
1.1
. First, we may verify that the only unpaired graphs in
\mathscr{V}
are the forests with two components (i.e., a disjoint union of two trees), with the vertices
1
and
2
belonging to different components, and both component trees have the property that the vertex labels are increasing along every ray starting from the vertex
1
or the vertex
2
. There are exactly
(n-1)!
such graphs, and they each have (
n-2
) edges. Thus, there are
(n-1)!
critical
(n-3)
-simplices, only one critical
0
-simplex (viz., the graph with the single edge
(1,2)
), and no other critical simplices. Consequently, Theorem
1.1
follows from Theorem
2.4
.

6
Concluding remarks

Here we remark that although discrete Morse theoretic techniques are purely combinatorial and
generally computationally efficient in nature, one needs to be meticulous while applying them. While exploring the structure of a complex via these techniques and performing various computations, we are usually required to start with a sufficiently “good” gradient vector field. One primary challenge often lies in showing that a constructed discrete vector field is a gradient one (i.e., justifying its “acyclicity”). Our proof makes use of the following observation which is useful for verifying the acyclicity of a constructed discrete vector field in general.

Observation 6.1
.

Let
\mathscr{V}
be a discrete vector field on a complex and
\gamma:

\alpha_{0},

\beta_{0},

\alpha_{1},

\ldots,

\alpha_{k},

\beta_{k},

\alpha_{k+1}
be a
\mathscr{V}
-path such that
\beta_{0}=\alpha_{0}\cup\{x\}
(with
x\notin\alpha_{0}
) and
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{0}\setminus\{y\}
(with
y\in\alpha_{0}
). If
\gamma
is a closed
\mathscr{V}
-path, then

(i)

the set
\{2,\ldots,k\}
contains an (least) element
i
such that
\alpha_{i}
doesn’t contain
x
, and

(ii)

the set
\{3,\ldots,k+1\}
contains an (least) element
j
such that
y\in\alpha_{j}
.

In other words, if the assumption that there is an (least)
i\in\{2,\ldots,k\}
such that
x\notin\alpha_{i}
(or, there is a (least)
j\in\{3,\ldots,k+1\}
such that
y\in\alpha_{j}
), leads to a contradiction for an arbitrary
\gamma
, then there is no closed
\mathscr{V}
-path. Consequently,
\mathscr{V}
is a gradient vector field.
