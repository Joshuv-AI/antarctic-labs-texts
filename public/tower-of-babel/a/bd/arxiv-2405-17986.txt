Abstract
It is well known that linear and non-linear dissipative port-Hamiltonian systems in finite dimensions admit an energy balance, relating the energy increase in the system with the supplied energy and the dissipated energy. The integrand in the dissipation term is then a function of the state variable. In this note, we answer the question of when this is possible for linear port-Hamiltonian systems in infinite dimensions.



keywords

Port-Hamiltonian systems, infinite-dimensional systems, dissipation, real part, domain

1
Introduction

The port-Hamiltonian modeling paradigm is so appealing as it provides an elegant mathematical framework for energy-based modeling and analysis of multi-physics systems, see, e.g.,
Van Der Schaft and Jeltsema 2014
. Port-Hamiltonian systems, providing in addition various coupling capabilities, are therefore very well-suited to describe the energy flows, energy conservation and interconnection of physical systems in a wide range of applications, such as robotics (
Angerer et al. 2017
), renewable energy systems (
Tõnso et al. 2023
), and adaptive building (
Schaller et al. 2024
), just to name a few.

A conventional input-state-output port-Hamitonian ODE-system has the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{x}&=A(x)\nabla H(x)+B(x)u\\
y&=B(x)^{*}\nabla H(x),\end{split}

(1)

where
H
is the system’s Hamiltonian describing the energy in the system and
A
is pointwise dissipative, i.e.,
\operatorname{Re}A(x):=\frac{1}{2}(A(x)+A(x)^{*})
is negative semi-definite. Solutions of the system can be easily shown to satisfy the energy balance relation, which is given by

\displaystyle H(x(t_{2}))

\displaystyle-H(x(t_{1}))

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}y^{*}u\,dt-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|[-\operatorname{Re}A(x)]^{1/2}\nabla H(x)\|^{2}\,dt,

where
t_{2}>t_{1}\geq 0
. While the left-hand side describes the increase of energy in the system on the time horizon
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, on the right-hand side we have the difference between the energy supplied to the system
\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}y^{*}u\,dt
and the dissipated energy
\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|[\operatorname{Re}A(x)]^{1/2}\nabla H(x)\|^{2}\,dt
. In the special linear case, where
H(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
and
A(x)\equiv A
, the dissipation term has the simple form
\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|R^{1/2}x(t)\|^{2}\,dt
, where
R:=-\operatorname{Re}A=-\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})\geq 0
.

If the state space is an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space and
A
is a bounded operator, the above definition of the dissipation operator
R
still makes sense. However, in applications the operator
A
is usually unbounded, and
A+A^{*}
is defined on the intersection of subspaces
\operatorname{dom}A\cap\operatorname{dom}A^{*}
. This subspace is the object of investigation in
Arlinskií and Tretter 2020
, where it is proved in particular that there even exist generators
A
of analytic
C_{0}
-semigroups for which this intersection is trivial:
\operatorname{dom}A\cap\operatorname{dom}A^{*}=\{0\}
. One of the results of the present note is that for such generators there however exists a self-adjoint operator
R
such that the energy balance holds for mild solutions
x
with the dissipation term
\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|R^{1/2}x(t)\|^{2}\,dt
.

Infinite-dimensional linear port-Hamiltonian systems have been investigated in a variety of works (see, e.g.,
Augner 2019
;
Jacob and Kaiser 2019
;
Jäschke et al. 2023
;
Rashad et al. 2020
;
Villegas 2007
). The standard book on the topic is surely
Jacob and Zwart 2012
, which mainly covers boundary control problems of port-Hamiltonian PDEs on a one-dimensional spacial domain. This setting has been generalized to multi-dimensional domains in
Skrepek 2021
. Recently, a wide class of linear infinite-dimensional port-Hamiltonian systems has been introduced in
Philipp et al. 2023
, which is based on the framework of Staffan’s system nodes (
Staffans 2005
) and admits both boundary and distributed control. For the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves to distributed control, here.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we briefly present the setting of the paper and discuss the problem of representing the dissipation term in the energy balance relation. Next, in Section
3
, it is shown that there always exists a linear operator
\mathcal{R}
, mapping initial value
x_{0}
and control
u
to a function
\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t)
such that the dissipation rate can be represented as
\|\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t)\|_{A}^{2}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{A}
denotes the graph norm of
A
. Finally, in Section
4
we prove that there exists a self-adjoint operator
R
such that the dissipation rate can be written in the form
\|R^{1/2}x\|^{2}
if and only if the quadratic form
x\mapsto\operatorname{Re}\langle Ax,x\rangle
is closable.

If

•

, it does not make sense because

, in general;

•

is symmetric, but might not be self-adjoint. Its domain of definition
\operatorname{dom}R=\operatorname{dom}A\cap\operatorname{dom}A^{*}
might even be the trivial subspace
\{0\}
. On the other hand, Example
2
exposes a maximal dissipative operator

\operatorname{dom}R

\mathcal{H}

In this note it is our aim to replace the expression

\mathbf{r}

\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{r})=\operatorname{dom}A

\mathbf{r}[x,y]:=-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\langle Ax,y\rangle+\langle x,Ay\rangle\big),\qquad x,y\in\operatorname{dom}A.

The form
\mathbf{r}
is called the

of the form
(x,y)\mapsto\langle Ax,y\rangle
. Note that
\mathbf{r}[x]:=\mathbf{r}[x,x]=-\operatorname{Re}\langle Ax,x\rangle\geq 0
for
x\in\operatorname{dom}A
.

3
Representation in terms of initial condition and control

In what follows, let
\|x\|_{A}:=(\|x\|^{2}+\|Ax\|^{2})^{1/2}
for
x\in\operatorname{dom}A
and

\mathcal{H}_{A}=(\operatorname{dom}A,\|\cdot\|_{A}).

Proposition 1
.

There exists a linear operator

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{H}\times L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{U})\to L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{H}_{A})

such that for
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
,
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{U})
, the associated mild solution
x(t)=x_{u}(t;x_{0})
, and
T>0
we have

H(x(T))-H(x_{0})=\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Re}\langle u,y\rangle\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t)\|_{A}^{2}\,dt.

(5)

If
u_{1}=u_{2}
on
[0,T]
, then also
\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u_{1})=\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u_{2})
on
[0,T]
for all
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, and the restriction operator
\mathcal{R}_{T}:\mathcal{H}\times L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{U})\to L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}_{A})
is bounded.

{pf}

For
x\in\operatorname{dom}A
we have
\mathbf{r}[x]\leq\|Ax\|\|x\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{A}^{2}
,
x\in\operatorname{dom}A
. Hence, there exists a bounded non-negative self-adjoint operator
M
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}
such that
\mathbf{r}[x,y]=\langle Mx,y\rangle_{A}
for
x,y\in\operatorname{dom}A
.

Let
T>0
be fixed but arbitrary. For
(x_{0},u)\in\operatorname{dom}A\times C^{1}(0,T;\mathcal{U})
define

\mathcal{R}_{T}(x_{0},u)(t):=M^{1/2}x_{u}(t;x_{0}),\quad t\in[0,T].

Let
x(t):=x_{u}(t;x_{0})
. Then
\|\mathcal{R}_{T}(x_{0},u)(t)\|_{A}^{2}=\mathbf{r}[x(t)]
and thus

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}(x_{0},u)(t)\|_{A}^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Re}\langle u,y\rangle\,dt-\tfrac{1}{2}\|x(T)\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Re}\langle Bu(t),x(t)\rangle\,dt+\tfrac{1}{2}\|x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|B\|\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|\|x(t)\|\,dt+\tfrac{1}{2}\|x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|B\|\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\,\mathcal{U})}\|x\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|x_{0}\|^{2}.

Now, as

\displaystyle\|x(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|S(t)x_{0}\|+\int_{0}^{t}\|S(t-s)Bu(s)\|\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{0}\|+\|B\|\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|\,ds,

we obtain

\|x\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})}\,\leq\,\sqrt{2T}\|x_{0}\|+T\|B\|\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{U})}.

Hence, one easily sees that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{T}(x_{0},u)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}_{A})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T}\|B\|\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\,\mathcal{U})}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|x_{0}\|

for all
(x_{0},u)\in\operatorname{dom}A\times C^{1}(0,T;\mathcal{U})
. Therefore, and since
\operatorname{dom}A\times C^{1}(0,T;\,\mathcal{U})
is dense in
\mathcal{H}\times L^{2}(0,T;\,\mathcal{U})
, the linear operator
R_{T}
uniquely extends to a bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}\times L^{2}(0,T;\,\mathcal{U})
, which we also denote by
\mathcal{R}_{T}
. The identity (
5
) then extends by boundedness to
(x_{0},u)\in\mathcal{H}\times L^{2}(0,T;\,\mathcal{U})
.

Finally, it is easily seen that for
T_{2}>T_{1}>0
and
u\in L^{2}(0,T_{2};\mathcal{U})
we have

\mathcal{R}_{T_{2}}(x_{0},u)|_{[0,T_{1}]}=\mathcal{R}_{T_{1}}(x_{0},u|_{[0,T_{1}]}).

Therefore, for
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{U})
it makes sense to define
\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t):=\mathcal{R}_{T}(x_{0},u|_{[0,T]})(t)
for any
T\geq t
.
\square

Example 2
.

Let
Ax=x^{\prime}
,
\operatorname{dom}A=\{x\in H^{1}(0,1):x(1)=0\}
, in
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(0,1)
. Then

generates the contractive
C_{0}
-semigroup

[S(t)x](\omega)=\begin{cases}x(t+\omega)&\text{if $t+\omega\leq 1$}\\

It is easily verified that the operator

from the proof of Proposition
1
is given by

M^{1/2}x=\frac{e\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{e^{4}-1}}x(0)\sinh(1-\,\cdot\,),\quad x\in\operatorname{dom}A.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t)=\frac{e\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{e^{4}-1}}x_{u}(t;x_{0})(0)\cdot\sinh(1-\,\cdot\,),

where (with

Since

, for the dissipation rate we get
\|\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t)\|_{A}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}|x_{u}(t;x_{0})(0)|^{2}

In particular, this shows that in this special case the operator

, mapping to

4
Representation in terms of the state

In the finite-dimensional case, we have that
-\operatorname{Re}\langle Ax,x\rangle=\|R^{1/2}x\|^{2}
, where
R=\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})
. In this section we consider the case where there exists a non-negative (possibly unbounded) self-adjoint operator
R
in
\mathcal{H}
with
\operatorname{dom}R^{1/2}\supset\operatorname{dom}A
such that for
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{U})
we have

\|\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t)\|_{A}=\|R^{1/2}x_{u}(t;x_{0})\|\qquad\text{for a.e.\ $t\geq 0$}.

(6)

Recall that a non-negative symmetric form
\mathbf{a}
on
\mathcal{H}
is said to be
closed
, if for any sequence
(x_{n})\subset\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{a})
with
x_{n}\to x
(
n\to\infty
) and
\mathbf{a}[x_{n}-x_{m}]\to 0
(
n,m\to\infty
) we have
x\in\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{a})
and
\mathbf{a}[x_{n}]\to\mathbf{a}[x]
(
n\to\infty
). The form
\mathbf{a}
is called
closable
if it admits a closed extension. Equivalently,
\mathbf{a}
is closable if
x_{n}\to 0
and
\mathbf{a}[x_{n}-x_{m}]\to 0
as
n,m\to\infty
imply
\mathbf{a}[x_{n}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a non-negative self-adjoint operator
R
in
\mathcal{H}
such that (
6
) holds for all
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{U})
if and only if the form
\mathbf{r}
is closable.

{pf}

“
\Rightarrow
” Let the form
\mathbf{r}
be closable. Denote by
\overline{\mathbf{r}}
the closure of
\mathbf{r}
. Then, by the second representation theorem (see
Kato 1995
), there exists a non-negative self-adjoint operator
R
in
\mathcal{H}
such that
\operatorname{dom}(\overline{\mathbf{r}})=\operatorname{dom}R^{1/2}
and

\overline{\mathbf{r}}[x,y]=\langle R^{1/2}x,R^{1/2}y\rangle\quad\text{for all $x,y\in\operatorname{dom}(\overline{\mathbf{r}})$}.

In particular, we have
\operatorname{dom}A\subset\operatorname{dom}R^{1/2}
. Let
u\in C^{1}([0,T],\mathcal{U})
and
x_{0}\in\operatorname{dom}A
be arbitrary. Then for
t\in[0,T]
the classical solution
x:=x_{u}(\,\cdot\,;x_{0})
satisfies

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}H(x(t))

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\langle\dot{x},x\rangle+\langle x,\dot{x}\rangle\big)=\operatorname{Re}\langle x,\dot{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\langle x,Ax+Bu\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\langle B^{*}x,u\rangle+\mathbf{r}[x]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\langle u,y\rangle-\|R^{1/2}x\|^{2}.

Integrating this over
[0,T]
shows that
(u,x)
satisfies the energy balance

\tfrac{1}{2}\|x(T)\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|x_{0}\|^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{Re}\langle u,y\rangle\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\|R^{1/2}x\|^{2}\,dt.

(7)

Now, let
u\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathcal{U})
and
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
be arbitrary. Then there exist sequences
(u_{n})\subset C^{1}([0,T],\mathcal{U})
and
(z_{n})\subset\operatorname{dom}A
such that
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{1}(0,T;\mathcal{U})
and
z_{n}\to x_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}
. Set
x_{n}(t):=x_{u_{n}}(t;z_{n})
and
x(t):=x_{u}(t;x_{0})
. Then

\displaystyle\|x_{n}

\displaystyle(t)-x(t)\|

\displaystyle=\left\|T(t)(z_{n}-x_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}T(t-s)B(u_{n}(s)-u(s))\,ds\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|z_{n}-x_{0}\|+\|B\|\|u_{n}-u\|_{1}\big),

where
C=\sup_{s\in[0,t_{2}]}\|T(s)\|
. In particular,
x_{n}\to x
uniformly on
[0,t_{2}]
.

Set
u_{nm}:=u_{n}-u_{m}
and
x_{nm}:=x_{n}-x_{m}
. Then
x_{nm}=x_{u_{nm}}(\,\cdot\,;z_{n}-z_{m})
. Hence,
(u_{nm},x_{nm})
satisfies (
7
) and we obtain
\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\|R^{1/2}x_{nm}(t)\|^{2}\,dt\to 0
as
n,m\to\infty
. That is,
(R^{1/2}x_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})
and thus converges to some
w\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})
. In particular, there exists a subsequence
(x_{n}^{\prime})
of
(x_{n})
such that
R^{1/2}x_{n}^{\prime}(t)\to w(t)
for a.e.
t\in[0,t_{2}]
. From the closedness of
R^{1/2}Q
we infer that
x(t)\in\operatorname{dom}R^{1/2}Q
and
R^{1/2}x(t)=w(t)
for these

so that
R^{1/2}x=w\in L^{2}(0,t_{2};\mathcal{H})
. It is now clear that
(u,x)
satisfies (
7
) and (
6
).

“
\Leftarrow
” Assume now that there exists
R\geq 0
in
\mathcal{H}
such that (
6
) holds. We shall show that the form
\mathbf{b}[x,y]:=\langle R^{1/2}x,R^{1/2}y\rangle
with
\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{b})=\operatorname{dom}R^{1/2}
is a closed extension of
\mathbf{r}
, and hence
\mathbf{r}
is closable. By polarization, since
\mathbf{b}
is closed by definition and
\operatorname{dom}A\subset\operatorname{dom}R^{1/2}=\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{b})
, it suffices to show that
\mathbf{r}[x]=\mathbf{b}[x]
for
x\in\operatorname{dom}A
.
For this, let
x_{0}\in\operatorname{dom}A
be arbitrary and consider the trivial control

. Then

is a classical solution to

,

, and is thus contained in
\operatorname{dom}A

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}[x(t)]

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Re}\langle Ax(t),x(t)\rangle=-\operatorname{Re}\langle\dot{x}(t),x(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=-H^{\prime}(x(t))\dot{x}(t)=\|R^{1/2}x(t)\|^{2}

. In particular, for

\mathbf{r}[z]=\mathbf{b}[z]

Example 4
.

(a) The operator
A

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(0,1)

(b) If

] or a Timoshenko beam [where

,

.

Remark 5
.

We may reduce the closability of the form
\mathbf{r}
to the closability of an operator. Indeed, since
A
is maximal dissipative, so is
A-I
, and
A-I
is invertible. Hence, the operator

Q:=-\tfrac{1}{2}\big((A-I)^{-1}+(A-I)^{-*}\big)

is well defined, bounded and non-negative self-adjoint. It is now easily seen that

\big\|Q^{1/2}(A-I)x\big\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}+\mathbf{r}[x],\qquad x\in\operatorname{dom}A.

Hence,
\mathbf{r}
is closable if and only if
Q^{1/2}(A-I)
is closable.

The following corollary shows that the form
\mathbf{r}
is closed for a large class of maximal dissipative operators.

Corollary 6
.

If
A
generates an analytic semigroup, then there exists a non-negative self-adjoint operator
R
in
\mathcal{H}
such that (
6
) holds for all
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
u\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{U})
.

{pf}

Indeed, in this case,
T:=-A
is an m-sectorial operator in the sense of
Kato 1995
. The form
\mathbf{t}[x,y]:=\langle Tx,y\rangle
,
x,y\in\operatorname{dom}T
, is then sectorial with some angle
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and closable (see Thm. VI.1.27 in
Kato 1995
). Define two symmetric forms
\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{k}
by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{h}}[x,y]

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{t}[x,y]+\overline{\mathbf{t}[y,x]})

\displaystyle{\mathbf{k}}[x,y]

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{1}{2i}(\mathbf{t}[x,y]-\overline{\mathbf{t}[y,x]}),

respectively. Then
\mathbf{t}={\mathbf{h}}+i{\mathbf{k}}
and, since
\mathbf{t}[x]
is in the sector
S_{\theta}
, we have
|{\mathbf{k}}[x]|\leq(\tan\theta){\mathbf{h}}[x]
for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(\mathbf{t})
. Hence, if
x_{n}\to 0
and
{\mathbf{h}}[x_{n}-x_{m}]\to 0
, then also
{\mathbf{k}}[x_{n}-x_{m}]\to 0
so that
\mathbf{t}[x_{n}-x_{m}]\to 0
, which implies
\mathbf{t}[x_{n}]\to 0
because
\mathbf{t}
is closable. In particular,
{\mathbf{h}}[x_{n}]=\operatorname{Re}\mathbf{t}[x_{n}]\to 0
. Thus,
\mathbf{r}=-{\mathbf{h}}
is closable.
\square

We would like to point out that it was shown in
(
Arlinskií and Tretter 2020
, Theorem 3.1)
that there exist generators
A
of analytic semigroups (even with compact resolvent) for which
\operatorname{dom}(\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*}))=\operatorname{dom}A\cap\operatorname{dom}A^{*}=\{0\}
. Hence, in this case, the self-adjoint operator
R
from Corollary
6
is not related with
\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})
at all.

5
Conclusion

We considered the dissipation rate of infinite-dimensional linear input-state-output port-Hamiltonian systems with distributed control. We proved that there always is a linear operator mapping initial state
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and control
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{U})
to a function
\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+},\mathcal{H}_{A})
such that the dissipation rate can be expressed as
\|\mathcal{R}(x_{0},u)(t)\|_{A}^{2}
. We furthermore proved that the dissipation rate can be written as
\|R^{1/2}x_{u}(t;x_{0})\|^{2}
with a non-negative self-adjoint operator
R
if and only if the form
\mathbf{r}
is closable. We provided an example where this is not the case and proved that it always holds if
A
generates an analytic semigroup. Future research consists of extending the results to port-Hamiltonian system nodes as introduced in
Philipp et al. 2023
.
