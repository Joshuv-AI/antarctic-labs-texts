A New Non-Linear Density Fluctuations Stochastic Partial Differential Equation With a Singular Coefficient of Relevance to Polymer Dynamics and Rheology: Discussions, Proofs of Solution Existence, Uniqueness, and a Conjecture
Ludovic Goudenège 1 and Liviu Iulian Palade 2

Abstract
In this paper we consider an entirely new - previously unstudied to the best of our knowledge - type of density fluctuations stochastic partial differential equation with a singular coefficient involving the inverse of a probability density. The equation was recently introduced by Schieber [ 36 ] while working on a new polymer molecular dynamics approach that pertains to the generally called polymer reptation (aka tube) theory. The corresponding probability density is the solution of an evolution equation (a stochastic transport) defined on a dynamical one-dimensional subspace. A peculiarity of the here studied equation is its very singular pattern, even though it exhibits a well-posed structure. As a first step towards furthering the understanding of this new class of equations we prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions under suitable conditions. Moreover, capitalizing on the assumed gradient structure, we prove that the existing solutions converge to an equilibrium density.

In a melt polymer material sample, the macromolecular chains are highly entangled with each other, hence the motion of a single chain is strongly influenced by the collective presence and dynamics of the surrounding neighbours. Such an influence is geometrically idealized by considering an individual chain as being confined to a tube with deformable walls - hence the name of the theory. Because the tube largely hinders the transverse motion, the large scale geometric rearrangement of the chain configuration takes place inside the tube walls via a snake-like reptational motion along its own length. As the chain is geometrically approximated as a concatenation of deformable segments (one encounters a many body problem), in the de Gennes - Doi - Edwards theory a chain segment is subjected to a three-stage consecutive motion: a sudden deformation immediately followed by a quick process of retraction to its original length, process continued by a slower reptation motion of relaxation out of the original tube. Actually the reptational diffusion also affects the shape of the both tube extremities in this way: the part of the tube which is emptied as the chain retracts will cease to exist (thus a constraint is released) while a new tube portion is formed at the other extremity and surrounds the diffusing chain. Next, all these dominant interactions are accounted for in a so-called configurational probability density diffusion equation - typically a parabolic partial differential equation (which may be deterministic or stochastic in nature). Its solution constitutes the key ingredient for obtaining the stress tensor which ultimately enters the momentum balance system of equations describing the macroscopic fluid motion. For some recent modeling results of the tube model in applied rheology see
[

The reptation theory (together with the kinetical approach) is popular in the area of polymer rheology. Since it was published in its original form in 1978-79 (see

Such undertakings include - but not restricted to - tube length diameter fluctuations, constrained release, fluctuating slip links and slip springs, inhomogeneities associated with density fluctuations. Many of these theoretical improvements were tested via advanced Monte Carlo simulations performed on stochastic equations.

Problem Presentation and Main Results

At first sight, this equation could be seen as a Cahn-Hilliard-Cook stochastic partial differential equation, and in simple cases, the existence and uniqueness could be obtained by known results.
The Cahn-Hilliard-Cook equation is a model that describes phase separation in a binary alloy (see
[
5
]

[

[

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\rho=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(M(\rho)\nabla\mu\right)+\dot{\xi}&\text{ on }\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
\\
\mu=V^{\prime}(\rho)-2\alpha\Delta\rho,&\text{ on }\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
\\
\nabla\rho\cdot\nu=0=\nabla\mu\cdot\nu,&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D},\\

(2.1)

\displaystyle t
denotes the time variable,
\displaystyle\alpha
is a small non-negative parameter,
\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot

In the deterministic case, the equation is obtained by taking the gradient of the free energy
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\alpha>0

Indeed, in this new model, the mobility

This new model will lead to multiple mathematical difficulties, in particular concerning the multiplicative noise, which could be a hard question.
All of these difficulties will not be solved in this paper, but we will give hints for possible direction of improvements. The aim of this article is to give the first mathematical structure as a starting point for future research in
tube model

Remark again that the initial data can be randomized (with bounded inverse first moment, i.e. bounded energy) to obtain the same result.

Finally, in the case
\displaystyle\alpha>0
, the same computation leads to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{E}(\rho(t,\cdot))+\frac{1}{2}\inf_{t\in[0,T]}|M(\rho(t,\cdot))|\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\mu\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}(\rho_{0})+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}C(Q,\varepsilon,\rho)\mathrm{d}t\right]+\alpha\left|\int_{0}^{T}\langle\Delta\rho,\Delta\mu\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right|

but the last term is not controlled by integrability condition given in Definition
2.1
, and it seems unreasonable to expect such controlability.

We write the operators on these spaces

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}

(Hemicontinuity) For all

3.1
Existence of solution

One has the following existence Theorem, standing actually for the first part of Theorem
•
‣
1.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that there exists a regular abstract formulation satisfying classical assumptions.
Given an initial data in the Banach space
\displaystyle\mathrm{V}
, then, for all
\displaystyle T>0
, there exists a unique solution
\displaystyle(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
in the sense of Definition
3.1
up to time
\displaystyle T
.

Proof.

We have supposed that the classical assumptions are satisfied, thus we do not have to verify them. However, we will show how they may be obtained with assumptions on mobility
\displaystyle M
, potential
\displaystyle V
and operator
\displaystyle Q
, or on regularized versions of these objects.

Usually, to prove the existence of a solution of a singular equation, we need to prove that a regularized version of the equation is well-posed with estimates being independent of the regularization procedure.
The possible regularized version may be some finite dimensional approximation (like Galerkin), a truncation of the non-linearity, a Yosida regularization, or a numerical approximation.
Thus, all future approaches will have to use the following calculations and to prove that the estimates are independent of the regularization procedure.

In Theorem
3.1
, we proved that some solution could exist in a fixed domain and that the Itô’s formula (being used for a priori estimates) suggests that the operator
\displaystyle A
has the form
\displaystyle A:X\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\langle M(X)V^{\prime\prime}(X)\nabla X,\cdot\rangle
plus extra additive terms involving Laplace operator when
\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0
. It also suggests that the operator
\displaystyle B
has the form
\displaystyle B:X\mapsto(w\mapsto\langle\cdot,\nabla^{*}Q(X)w\rangle)
. Denote
\displaystyle a:=x\mapsto M(x)V^{\prime\prime}(x)
the non-linearity (or, more precisely, a regularized version of it) to simplify the notations in the following computations. It is assumed that it is non-negative, as suggested by the expected physical properties of the convex potential
\displaystyle V
and the non-negative mobility
\displaystyle M
.
We now illustrate the difficulties in obtaining the expected bounds.

Hemicontinuity.

Suppose that the function
\displaystyle a
is continuous and bounded from below by non-negative constant (locally on balls of
\displaystyle\mathrm{V}
).
Let
\displaystyle u,v,w\in\mathrm{V}
. Let
\displaystyle(\lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
\displaystyle\lambda
as
\displaystyle k
grows to infinity, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|2\,_{\mathrm{V}^{*}}\langle A(u+\lambda_{k}v)-A(u+\lambda v),w\rangle_{\mathrm{V}}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\langle a(u+\lambda_{k}v)\nabla(u+\lambda_{k}v)-a(u+\lambda v)\nabla(u+\lambda v),\nabla w\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\langle\left(a(u+\lambda_{k}v)-a(u+\lambda v)\right)\nabla(u+\lambda_{k}v),\nabla w\rangle\right|+\left|\langle a(u+\lambda v)(\lambda_{k}-\lambda)\nabla v,\nabla w\rangle\right|.

Since the sequence
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}
is bounded, thus the sequence
\displaystyle u+\lambda_{k}v
lives in a ball
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{V}
. On this ball, the function
\displaystyle a
is continuous and bounded. Let
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
be fixed, there exists
\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}>0
small enough such that
\displaystyle|\lambda_{k}-\lambda|<\delta_{\epsilon}
implies
\displaystyle|a(u+\lambda_{k}v)-a(u+\lambda v)|<\epsilon

and thus for large index
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
the first term is bounded by

\displaystyle\epsilon\sup_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\|x\|_{\mathrm{V}}\|w\|_{\mathrm{V}}
and the second term is bounded by
\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}\ |a(u+\lambda v)|\ \|v\|_{\mathrm{V}}\|w\|_{\mathrm{V}}
, which ensures convergence to
\displaystyle 0
and continuity.

Weak Monotonicity.

Let
\displaystyle u,v,w\in\mathrm{V}
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\,_{\mathrm{V}^{*}}\langle A(u)-A(v),v-u\rangle_{V}+\|B(u)-B(v)\|_{HS}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle a(u)\nabla u-a(v)\nabla v,\nabla(v-u)\rangle+\|\nabla^{*}Q(u)-\nabla^{*}Q(v)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{D}}a(u)|\nabla(u-v)|^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{D}}(a(u)-a(v))\nabla v\cdot\nabla(v-u)+\|\nabla u\ Q^{\prime}(u)-\nabla v\ Q^{\prime}(v)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}.

At this point, in order to obtain the bound
\displaystyle c\|u-v\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}
, we see that the first term is not a problem since
\displaystyle a
is assumed to be non-negative. It can help to control other problematic terms.
For the second term, we certainly need some globally Lipschitz regularity for the function
\displaystyle a
, but which is not precisely clear at this point since mobility and potential are singulars.
For the third term, we certainly need the regularity and boundedness of the derivative of
\displaystyle Q
. Remark that in the additive case (
\displaystyle B
is a constant), this term does not appear.
Remark also that if it exists an extra term with
\displaystyle\alpha>0
involving Laplace operator, then we have an other term with the good sign which can help to control the problematic terms.

Coercivity.

The coercivity corresponds to a control at infinity of the equation.
The leading term is
\displaystyle c_{1}\|u\|^{2}_{H}
, which should absorb the operator
\,{}_{\mathrm{V}^{*}}\langle A(u),u\rangle_{\mathrm{V}}
,
\displaystyle B
or a supplementary term
\displaystyle c_{2}\|u\|^{q}_{\mathrm{V}}
.

Consider the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\,_{\mathrm{V}^{*}}\langle A(u),u\rangle_{\mathrm{V}}+\|B(u)\|_{2}^{2}+c_{2}\|u\|^{q}_{\mathrm{V}}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\langle a(u)\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle+\|\nabla u\ Q^{\prime}(u)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+c_{2}\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle^{q}+c_{2}\langle u,u\rangle^{q}.

We have to bound these terms by
\displaystyle c_{1}\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}
with
\displaystyle c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
but remember that
\displaystyle c_{2}>0
.
It is clear that the term
\displaystyle c_{1}\|u\|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}
will not control the terms involving
\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{V}}
since it would imply a Poincaré inequality in reversed order, thus the only term with the good sign is the first one.
This term should control the term with the constant
\displaystyle c_{2}
which forces the constant
\displaystyle c_{2}
not to be large, and
\displaystyle a
should be lower bounded by constant
\displaystyle c_{2}>0
. The second term involves also the gradient of
\displaystyle u
, thus it is a supplementary term to control.
The last term involves only the norm of
\displaystyle u
in the Hilbert space
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}
thus it is controlled by
\displaystyle c_{2}(1+\|u\|^{2})
at least when
\displaystyle 1<q\leq 2
.

Boundedness.

Recall that the norm in
\displaystyle\mathrm{V}^{*}
is defined as the supremum of the quantity
\,{}_{\mathrm{V}^{*}}\langle A(u),v\rangle_{\mathrm{V}}
when
\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathrm{V}}\leq 1
. Let
\displaystyle v\in\mathrm{V}
, we are looking for an estimates

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\,{}_{\mathrm{V}^{*}}\langle A(u),v\rangle_{\mathrm{V}}\right|:=\frac{1}{2}\left|\langle a(u)\nabla u,\nabla v\rangle\right|\leq c_{3}\|u\|_{\mathrm{V}}^{q-1}+g.

It is now clear that the constant
\displaystyle c_{3}
should control the function
\displaystyle a
which is expected to be bounded from above.
∎

Path-wise Uniqueness

The path-wise uniqueness will be proven in both cases with the same strategy using Gronwall’s Lemma.

.

Let
\displaystyle(\rho^{i},W)
,
\displaystyle i=1,2

\displaystyle W

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\rho^{1}(t)-\rho^{2}(t)\|\right]\leq\exp(ct)\mathbb{E}\left[\|\rho^{1}(0)-\rho^{2}(0)\|\right].

\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{R}
under the assumption of weak monotonicity.

Assuming the same initial data

In the simplified formulation with singular potential, the uniqueness follows from dissipativity of the non-linearity.

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{-1}

Convergence of Invariant Measures

We say that the measure
\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle\rho_{0}
, whose distribution is
\displaystyle\Pi
, the solution
\displaystyle(\rho(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
is such that its distribution at time
\displaystyle t
is again
\displaystyle\Pi
.
Such an invariant measure can be easily calculated (for instance in finite dimensional case) as eigenvector of an adjoint operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{*}
of the semi-group
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
of the Markovian evolution.

Usually, we prove that an approximated version (described by some index
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
) of the equation possesses a semi-group which is Strong Feller (see .e.g
[
32
]
for details) and irreducible. By Doob theorem (see
[
20
]
) we deduce that there exists a unique and ergodic invariant measure
\displaystyle\Pi^{n}
defined on the abstract Hilbert space
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}
.

Denoting
\displaystyle\rho^{n}(t;x)
the solution starting form the initial data
\displaystyle x\in\mathrm{H}
, we can define for all Borel bounded test functions
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathrm{H})
the quantity

\mathcal{P}^{n}_{t}\phi(x):=\mathbb{E}[\phi(\rho^{n}(t;x))]

which is the semi-group of the Markovian evolution.

For instance, using Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula (assuming inversibility of covariance operator and other technical assumptions), it can be seen that
\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}^{n}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies, for all
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathrm{H})
,
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle t>0
, the following estimate

|\mathcal{P}^{n}_{t}\phi(x)-\mathcal{P}^{n}_{t}\phi(y)|\leq C(n)\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{\sqrt{t}}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\|x-y\|_{\mathrm{H}},\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathrm{H},

(6.1)

where we expect that the constant
\displaystyle C(n)
could be independent of the parameter
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Furthermore, the irreducibility is usually proven using a control-type argument, which is quite clear in the case of (non-degenerate) additive noise.

If the variable
\displaystyle\lambda
is non-positive, then there is exponential contraction, and the law of the solution converges exponentially fast to the invariant measure.

6.2
Simplified version with singular potential

To prove the existence of invariant measure, first, we need to have a reference measure in the abstract Hilbert space.
It is usually given by the asymptotic law of the solution of the linear part of the equation.
For instance, if the operators
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are linear and constant, we can define explicitly the solution
\displaystyle Z(t;x)
of the linear equation with initial data
\displaystyle x\in\mathrm{H}
which is given by

Z(t;x)=e^{-tA/2}x+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)A/2}B\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

(6.3)

As easily seen this process is in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty[;\mathrm{H})
(see
[
13
]
). In particular, the mean of
\displaystyle Z
is constant and the law of the process
\displaystyle Z(t;x)
is the Gaussian measure on
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}
:

\gamma(t)=\mathcal{N}\big(e^{-tA/2}x,\Sigma_{t}^{2}\big),\text{ with }\Sigma_{t}^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-sA/2}BB^{*}e^{-sA/2}\mathrm{d}s

with
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(m,\Sigma^{2})
the standard notation for the Normal Law of mean
\displaystyle m
and covariance operator
\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}
.
If we let
\displaystyle t\rightarrow+\infty
, the law of
\displaystyle Z(t;x)
converges to the Gaussian measure on
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}
:

\gamma:=\mathcal{N}\left(0,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sA/2}BB^{*}e^{-sA/2}\mathrm{d}s\right).

When
\displaystyle B
commutes with
\displaystyle A
, we can obtain an explicit representation of the covariance operator
\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}:=B(-A)^{-1}B^{*}
.

What about the invariant measure of the non-linear equation?
It is classic knowledge that if the equation (
1.1
) may be approximated by a gradient system in
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}
which can be rewritten as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{n}+\left(\frac{1}{2}A^{n}X_{t}^{n}+\nabla V^{n}(X_{t}^{n})\right)\mathrm{d}t=B\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
\\
X_{0}^{n}=x\in\mathrm{H},\end{array}\right.

(6.4)

where
\displaystyle\nabla
denotes the gradient in the Hilbert space
\displaystyle\mathrm{H}
,
then one deduces there exists an unique and ergodic Gibbs-type invariant measure
\displaystyle\Pi^{n}
given by:

\Pi^{n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{Z^{n}}\exp(-\mathcal{E}^{n}(x))\gamma(\mathrm{d}x),

where
\displaystyle Z^{n}
is a normalization constant and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}
is the energy related to the potential
\displaystyle V^{n}
(as in (
1.3
)).
We need finish the proof by showing that the sequence
\displaystyle(\Pi^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to the measure

P_{\text{eq}}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp(-\mathcal{E}(x))\mathds{1}_{x\in K}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x),

where for all
\displaystyle x\in L^{2}(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x):=\int_{0}^{1}V(x(s))\mathrm{d}s
(as in (
1.3
) but on fixed domain
\displaystyle(0,1)
).

Of course, we need a strong assumption on the limit measure
\displaystyle P_{\text{eq}}
, precisely we ask that the quantity
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x)
is well-defined for
\displaystyle\gamma
almost all
\displaystyle x\in\mathrm{H}\cap K
(but possibly infinite with the convention that
\displaystyle\exp(-\infty)=0
).

In the previous section, one can notice that the integrability condition (
6.2
) of the invariant measure
\displaystyle P_{\text{eq}}
is deduced from the strict monotonicity.

Naturally, we expect that the energy is well-posed on the space of non-negative functions

K=\{x\in\mathrm{L}^{2}:\;x\geq 0\}

assuming we have a non-negative potential
\displaystyle V
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that
\displaystyle V^{n}
is non-negative, non-increasing and converges from below to
\displaystyle V
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
, but converges to
\displaystyle+\infty
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{-}
. Then we have the convergence

\displaystyle\nu^{n}\mathop{\rightharpoonup}_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\nu.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,1),\mathbb{R})
. We want to prove that

\int_{\mathrm{H}}\psi(x)\exp({-\mathcal{E}^{n}(x)})\gamma(\mathrm{d}x)\quad\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\quad\int_{\mathrm{H}}\psi(x)\exp({-\mathcal{E}(x)})\mathds{1}_{\rho\in K}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x).

(6.5)

We first prove that for (
\displaystyle\gamma
-almost surely)
\displaystyle x\in\mathrm{H}
we have

\exp({-\mathcal{E}^{n}(x)})\quad\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\quad\exp({-\mathcal{E}(x)})\mathds{1}_{x\in K}.

(6.6)

Since the reference measure
\displaystyle\gamma(C([0,1]))=1
, we can restrict our attention to
\displaystyle x\in C([0,1])
.
Then if
\displaystyle x\notin K
there exists
\displaystyle\delta_{x}>0
such that
\displaystyle Leb(\{s\in[0,1]:x(s)\leq-\delta_{x}\})>0
,
\displaystyle Leb
being the Lebesgue measure.
Then, since
\displaystyle V^{n}(x)
is non-negative and non-increasing on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}:=(0,\infty)
, we have:

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0\leq\exp({-\mathcal{E}^{n}(x)})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}V^{n}(x(s))1_{\{x\leq-\delta_{x}\}}\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}V^{n}(-\delta_{x})1_{\{x\leq-\delta_{x}\}}\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\exp\left(-V^{n}(-\delta_{x})Leb(\{x\leq-\delta_{x}\})\right).

By assumption, this latter term converges to zero as
\displaystyle n
grows to infinity.

Now, for
\displaystyle x\in K
,
\displaystyle V^{n}(x(s))

\displaystyle V(x(s))
almost everywhere as
\displaystyle n
grows to infinity. Moreover
\displaystyle 0\leq V^{n}(x(s))\leq V(\sup\|x\|)
, and by the Dominated Convergence Theorem we deduce (
6.6
).
Finally, (

7
Concluding With A Conjecture

We took on to proving the existence and uniqueness of solutions to a previously unstudied class of non-linear stochastic partial differential equations with a singular coefficient that has recently been introduced
[
36
]
within the framework of polymer dynamics theories geared towards polymer physics and rheology.

The equations under scrutiny in this paper are simplified versions of the originally suggested for being studied.
We have shown that there exists solutions to these approximated problems under classical assumptions and abstract formulation.
It appears at this stage that it is plausible to obtain existence of solution results for the more general singular problem just alluded to by extracting properly defined convergent sequences of approximated solutions, and this upon obtaining enough a priori estimates, and passing afterwards to the limit.
For instance, using an
\displaystyle\varepsilon
approximation (by Yosida regularization, or globally Lipschitz potential, or dissipative potential), one may achieve the results by compactness related arguments.
Using Picard’s fixed-point iteration procedure, it seems also plausible to obtain convergence to a solution.

We can state the following conjecture based on the existence of an abstract formulation which encompasses the space of functions and the non-linearity.
