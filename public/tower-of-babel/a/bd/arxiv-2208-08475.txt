Abstract
. We present an example of a complete Riemannian plane with precisely two injective geodesics – up to reparameterization. The example arises as a perturbation of a surface of revolution with contracting end. The last section is devoted to open problems.



1.
Introduction

The geodesics of a complete, noncompact Riemannian manifold
M
can be divided into the following three classes:

Definition 1.1
.

A geodesic
c\colon\mathbb{R}\to M
is either

(i)

proper (as a map from
\mathbb{R}
to
M
), or

(ii)

bounded, i.e
c(\mathbb{R})
is a bounded subset of
M
, or

(iii)

oscillating, i.e. neither proper nor bounded.

Proper geodesics are alternatively called “divergent” or “escaping” in the literature.

Since the end of the
19^{\text{th}}
century questions concerning the existence and quantity of geodesics in one of these classes have found continuous interest in differential geometry
and in the theory of dynamical systems, see the contributions by H.v. Mangoldt
[
11
]
and by J. Hadamard
[
8
]
as early examples for this interest.

Another important property that a geodesic
c\colon\mathbb{R}\to M
may or may not have is injectivity. This is of particular interest if
\dim M=2
. Note that we require injectivity of
c
as a
map defined on
\mathbb{R}
, in particular periodic geodesics will not be injective. A geodesic
c\colon\mathbb{R}\to M
is proper and injective if and only if
c
is an embedding. Adopting the
terminology introduced in
[
4
]
we define:

Definition 1.2
.

An embedded geodesic line in
M
is the image
c(\mathbb{R})
of an injective, proper geodesic
c\colon\mathbb{R}\to M
.

Note that embedded geodesic lines need not be length-minimizing. Conversely, if a geodesic
c\colon\mathbb{R}\to M
is length-minimizing between any two of its points, then
c(\mathbb{R})
is an
embedded geodesic line. Such lines will be called
straight lines
.

Our main result sheds light on the following question:

Question 1.3
.

What is the maximal number
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that every complete Riemannian plane has at least
n
embedded geodesic lines?

It is proved in
[
2
]
that
n\geq 1
, i.e. every complete Riemannian plane contains an embedded geodesic line. Here we show:

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a complete Riemannian plane with precisely two embedded geodesic lines.

So Theorem
1.4
implies
n\leq 2
. We have the following conjecture, cf. also the end of
[
3
]
:

Conjecture 1.5
.

Every complete Riemannian plane has at least two embedded geodesic lines.

If this conjecture is true we conclude from Theorem
1.4
that
n=2
.

Actually we prove a little more than is stated in Theorem
1.4
, namely the existence of a complete Riemannian plane with only two injective geodesics - up to reparameterization.
This example is constructed by a compactly supported perturbation of the rotationally invariant metric
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
described below.

Throughout the paper we will parameterize
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
by standard polar coordinates

(r,\theta)\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\to(0,\infty)\times S^{1},

where
S^{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
1
1

1

We follow the time-honored abuse of notation and denote coordinates and coordinate functions by the same symbols.

The coordinate function
r
extends to
(0,0)
by
r(0,0)=0
. In these coordinates the unperturbed metric
g
is given by

g=dr^{2}+f^{2}\circ rd\theta^{2},

where
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathbb{R}_{>0})
satisfies

(1.1)

f(r)=\sin r\text{, if }r\in\left(0,\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)\text{,}

and

(1.2)

f^{\prime}(r)<0\text{, if }r>\frac{\pi}{2}\text{, and }\lim_{r\to\infty}f(r)=0.

Note that on the set
\left(0,\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)\times S^{1}
the metric
g
coincides with the metric of the round sphere of radius one in geodesic polar coordinates.
In particular,
g
extends to a smooth metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is obviously complete. To avoid confusion with angles on
S^{1}
, we set
R_{0}:=\frac{\pi}{2}
and

R_{1}:=\frac{3\pi}{4}
. In Section
2
and
3
we will prove

Proposition 1.6
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and a
C^{\infty}
-family of Riemannian metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
, such that the following is true:

(i)

g_{0}=g
and
g_{\varepsilon}
and
g
coincide outside the set

\{(r,\theta)|\;R_{0}<r<R_{1},\;0<\theta<\pi\}.

(ii)

If
0<|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
then
(\mathbb{R}^{2},g_{\varepsilon})
has precisely two injective geodesics - up to reparameterization.

The proof of Proposition
1.6
depends primarily on the scattering method from
[
7
]
. The metric
g_{\varepsilon}
will scatter the
g
-geodesics hitting the circle
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}

orthogonally at the point
(R_{1},\theta)
so as to intersect the circle
\{R_{0}\}\times S^{1}
at the point
(R_{0},\theta)
under the angle
\frac{\pi}{2}+\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
determined by a
deflection function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\colon S^{1}\to\left(-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\right)
, see Section
2
for details.

Finally we note that the special properties of the metric
g
, in particular condition (
1.1
), are requested in order to simplify the proof of Proposition
1.6
. In principle, one
could prove this perturbation result for a much larger class of rotationally symmetric metrics. But since the only purpose of Proposition
1.6
is to prove Theorem
1.4
we tried to
minimize the necessary arguments.

Throughout the paper geodesics will be parameterized by arclength.

2.
The perturbed metrics
g_{\varepsilon}

The construction of the metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
follows the method used to prove the more general Theorem 1 in
[
7
]
. We choose a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(S^{1},\mathbb{R})

satisfying the following conditions:

(2.1)

\varphi\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+\theta\right)=-\varphi\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)\text{, for all }\theta\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

(2.2)

\varphi^{-1}(0)=\left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}\cup[\pi,2\pi]

The functions
\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\varphi
will play the role of the deflection functions mentioned at the end of Section
1
. Note that (
2.2
) implies:

(2.3)

\text{If }\varepsilon\neq 0\text{ and }\theta\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\cup\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right)\text{ then }\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\neq 0.

As a consequence of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) we have

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)d\theta=0.

Hence we can define a function
l_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(S^{1},\mathbb{R})
by

(2.4)

l_{\varepsilon}(\theta):=\frac{\pi}{4}-\int_{0}^{\theta}\sin\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)d\theta.

We will always assume that
|\varepsilon|
is so small that
|\varphi_{\varepsilon}|<\frac{\pi}{4}
and
l_{\varepsilon}>0
. Note that

(2.5)

l_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=\frac{\pi}{4}\text{ for }\theta\in[\pi,2\pi]\text{, and }l_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\neq\frac{\pi}{4}\text{ if }\varepsilon\neq 0\text{ and }\theta\in(0,\pi).

Recall that we have set
R_{0}=\frac{\pi}{2}
,
R_{1}=\frac{3\pi}{4}
. In particular we have
R_{1}-R_{0}=\frac{\pi}{4}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Suppose
g_{\varepsilon}
is a metric such that the
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesics intersecting
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
orthogonally, foliate
[R_{0},R_{1}]\times S^{1}
and such that the

g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic starting at
(R_{1},\theta)
orthogonally to
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
hits
\{R_{0}\}\times S^{1}
at
(R_{0},\theta)
with angle
\frac{\pi}{2}+\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
.
Let
L_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
denote the
g_{\varepsilon}
-length of this geodesic connecting
(R_{0},\theta)
to
(R_{1},\theta)
. Then the first variation formula implies that
L_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\theta)=-\sin\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
,
i.e. there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
L_{\varepsilon}=l_{\varepsilon}+c
. In particular, we see that the condition

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)d\theta=0

is necessary for the existence of
g_{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and for every
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
a Riemannian metric
g_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a diffeomorphism

\Phi_{\varepsilon}\colon\{(s,\theta)|\;\theta\in S^{1},0\leq s\leq l_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\}\to[R_{0},R_{1}]\times S^{1}

satisfying the following properties:

(i)

g_{0}=g
and
\Phi_{0}(s,\theta)=(R_{0}+s,\theta)
for all
(s,\theta)\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{4}\right]\times S^{1}
.

(ii)

g_{\varepsilon}
and
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
are smooth as functions of all three variables.

(iii)

g_{\varepsilon}
and
g
coincide outside the set
(R_{0}+\varepsilon_{0},R_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})\times(0,\pi)
.

(iv)

For every
\theta\in S^{1}
the curve

c_{\varepsilon,\theta}\colon[0,l_{\varepsilon}(\theta)]\to[R_{0},R_{1}]\times S^{1},\quad c_{\varepsilon,\theta}(s)=\Phi_{\varepsilon}(s,\theta),

is a
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic satisfying

\dot{c}_{\varepsilon,\theta}(0)=\cos\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\partial_{r}|_{(R_{0},\theta)}+\sin\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\partial_{\theta}|_{(R_{0},\theta)},

and
\dot{c}_{\varepsilon,\theta}(l_{\varepsilon}(\theta))=\partial_{r}|_{(R_{1},\theta)}
, and
(r\circ c_{\varepsilon,\theta})^{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}>0
.

(v)

For
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
we have
c_{\varepsilon,\frac{\pi}{2}}([0,l_{\varepsilon}(\frac{\pi}{2})]=[R_{0},R_{1}]\times\{\frac{\pi}{2}\}
.

Note that (iv) implies
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(0,\theta)=(R_{0},\theta)
and
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(l_{\varepsilon}(\theta),\theta)=c_{\varepsilon,\theta}(l_{\varepsilon}(\theta))=(R_{1},\theta)
. Except for differences in terminology
the proof of Proposition
2.2
follows from the arguments given in
[
7
]
. We present a complete proof of Proposition
2.2
in the appendix since some
properties of the construction that are relevant for our application are not explicitly mentioned in
[
7
]
.

3.
Injective
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesics

In this section we prove Proposition
1.6
and, consequently, Theorem
1.4
. We rely on the family of metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
from Proposition
2.2
. First we collect some well known
facts concerning the geodesics of the unperturbed metric
g=g_{0}
. Since
g=dr^{2}+f^{2}\circ rd\theta^{2}
is invariant under rotations, i.e
\partial_{\theta}
is a Killing
vector field for
g
, the geodesic flow of
g
has a second integral, namely Clairaut’s integral
I
. We define
I
on the unit tangent bundle
S\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
g
by

I\colon S\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[-1,1],\quad I(v)=g(\partial_{\theta},v).

There are the following types of
g
-geodesics
c\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

(3.1)

If
I(\dot{c})=0
then
c
is orthogonal to all the circles
\{r\}\times S^{1}
,
r>0
, and, modulo translation of the parameter, we may assume that
r\circ c(0)=0
. Then
there exists
\theta\in S^{1}
such that
c
is given in polar coordinates by

c(s)=\begin{cases}(s,\theta),&\text{ for }s>0\\
(-s,\theta+\pi),&\text{ for }s<0.\end{cases}

These geodesics will be called
radial
.

(3.2)

If
|I(\dot{c})|=1
then properties (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) of
f
imply that
r\circ c=\frac{\pi}{2}=R_{0}
. Hence
c
parameterizes the circle
\{R_{0}\}\times S^{1}
by arclength.

(3.3)

If
0<|I(\dot{c})|<1
let
r_{-}\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
and
r_{+}\in\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\infty\right)
be defined by
f(r_{-})=f(r_{+})=|I(\dot{c})|
. Using the definition of
I
one can find

s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
l_{0}>0
such that
c(s_{0}+2nl_{0})\in\{r_{-}\}\times S^{1}
and
c(s_{0}+(2n+1)l_{0})\in\{r_{+}\}\times S^{1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, while
(r\circ c)^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s\in\cup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(s_{0}+2nl_{0},s_{0}+(2n+1)l_{0})
and
(r\circ c)^{\prime}(s)<0
for
s\in\cup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(s_{0}+(2n-1)l_{0},s_{0}+2nl_{0})
. In particular, we have
c(\mathbb{R})\subseteq[r_{-},r_{+}]\times S^{1}
.

Next we exhibit the injective
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesics whose existence is claimed in Proposition
1.6
(ii). Let
c_{0}
denote a radial
g
-geodesic intersecting the circle
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}

in the points
(R_{1},0)
and
(R_{1},\pi)
, and let
c_{1}
denote a radial
g
-geodesic intersecting
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
in the points
(R_{1},\frac{\pi}{2})
and
(R_{1},\frac{3\pi}{2})
.
Recall that all the metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
coincide with
g
on
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
. So, being orthogonal to
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
is independent of
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
c
be a
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic that intersects
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
orthogonally at one of the points
(R_{1},0),\;(R_{1},\frac{\pi}{2}),\;(R_{1},\pi)
, or
(R_{1},\frac{3\pi}{2})
. Then

c(\mathbb{R})=c_{0}(\mathbb{R})
or
c(\mathbb{R})=c_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, up to parameterization these correspond to precisely two
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesics. Moreover, these
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesics are injective.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
(iii) the radial
g
-geodesic
c_{0}
is a
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesics for every
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
and
c_{0}
intersects
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
orthogonally in the points

(R_{1},0)
and
(R_{1},\pi)
. Hence
c(\mathbb{R})=c_{0}(\mathbb{R})
if
c
is a
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic intersecting
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
orthogonally at
(R_{1},0)
or at
(R_{1},\pi)
. Since

\varphi(\frac{\pi}{2})=\varphi(\frac{3\pi}{2})=0
a
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic intersecting
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
orthogonally at
(R_{1},\frac{\pi}{2})
or at
(R_{1},\frac{3\pi}{2})
satisfies
c(\mathbb{R})=c_{1}(\mathbb{R})

by Proposition
2.2
(iii),(iv), and (v).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

If
c
is a
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic satisfying
c(\mathbb{R})=c_{0}(\mathbb{R})
then, by Proposition
2.2
(iii), there exist
a\in\{-1,1\}
,
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
c(s)=c_{0}(as+s_{0})
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. On the
other hand, if
\varepsilon\neq 0
and
c
is a
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic satisfying
c(\mathbb{R})=c_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then the segment
[R_{0},R_{1}]\times\{\frac{\pi}{2}\}
is part of both
c(\mathbb{R})
and
c_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, but its

g_{\varepsilon}
-length
l_{\varepsilon}(\frac{\pi}{2})
is different from its
g
-length
R_{1}-R_{0}=\frac{\pi}{4}
, see (
2.5
).

Before we start the formal proof of Proposition
1.6
we give a brief outline. We assume that
c
is an injective
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic and
|\varepsilon|>0
is small. We want to show that
c

intersects
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
orthogonally at one of the points
(R_{1},0)
,
(R_{1},\frac{\pi}{2})
,
(R_{1},\pi)
, or
(R_{1},\frac{3\pi}{2})
. Then Proposition
1.6
will follow from Lemma

3.1
. First we will see that the ends of
c
lie on radial geodesics, see Lemma
3.5
and
3.6
. Then, using property (
2.2
) of
\varphi
, we will prove that a radial geodesic
coming in from infinity and hitting
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
at a point
(R_{1},\theta)
with
\theta\notin\{0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}\}
will be deflected by
g_{\varepsilon}
so that the other end of
c
will
not be radial, in contradiction to the preceding statement.

The formal proof of Proposition
1.6
will rely on the following Lemmas
3.3
-
3.7
.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{1})
and every
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic
c
satisfying
r\circ c(0)\geq R_{1}
and
\dot{c}(0)\neq\partial_{r}|_{c(0)}
there exists

s>0
such that
r\circ c(s)=R_{0}
.

Proof.

Otherwise we could find sequences
\varepsilon_{i}\to 0
and
g_{\varepsilon_{i}}
-geodesics
c_{i}
such that
r\circ c_{i}(0)\geq R_{1}
,
\dot{c}_{i}(0)\neq\partial|_{c_{i}(0)}
, and
r\circ c_{i}(s)>R_{0}
for all

s>0
. Since the
c_{i}
are
g
-geodesics as long as they are contained in
[R_{1},\infty)\times S^{1}
we can use (3.1)-(3.3) to find a sequence
t_{i}\geq 0
such that
r\circ c_{i}(t_{i})=R_{1}

and
(r\circ c_{i})^{\prime}(t_{i})\leq 0
. Using Proposition
2.2
(ii) we see that a subsequence of the sequence of
g_{\varepsilon_{i}}
-geodesics
s\to c_{i}(t_{i}+s)
converges compactly to a
g
-geodesic

c
such that
r\circ c(0)=R_{1}
,
(r\circ c)^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
, and
r\circ c(s)\geq R_{0}
for all
s>0
. But, according to (3.1)-(3.3), a
g
-geodesic with these properties does not exist.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
c
be an injective
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic. If
J_{c}=\{s\in\mathbb{R}|\;r\circ c(s)\leq R_{0}\}\neq\emptyset
, then
J_{c}
is an interval of length
\pi
.

Proof.

Recall that
[0,R_{0}]\times S^{1}
with the metric
g_{\varepsilon}
is isometric to a round hemisphere of radius one. Since
c
is injective it does not parameterize the boundary great circle

\{R_{0}\}\times S^{1}
. Hence, if
I
is a component of
J_{c}
then
c|_{I}
parameterizes half of a great circle in this hemisphere. Since any two such half great circles intersect, we see
that
J_{c}
is connected and an interval of length
\pi
.
∎

Combining Lemma
3.3
and
3.4
we deduce

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
c
be an injective
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic and
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{1}
. If
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
r\circ c(s)\geq R_{1}
, then
\dot{c}(s)\in\{-\partial_{r}|_{c(s)},\partial_{r}|_{c(s)}\}
.

Proof.

Otherwise we can use Lemma
3.3
to obtain
s_{-}<s<s_{+}
such that
r\circ c(s_{-})=r\circ c(s_{+})=R_{0}
. So Lemma
3.4
implies
[s_{-},s_{+}]\subset J_{c}
, in contradiction
to
s\notin J_{c}
.
∎

Next we use the fact that
g_{\varepsilon}
and
g
coincide outside
(R_{0}+\varepsilon_{0},R_{1}-\varepsilon)\times S^{1}
to prove:

Lemma 3.6
.

There exists
0<\varepsilon_{2}<\varepsilon_{0}
such that there is no injective
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic
c\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,R_{1}]\times S^{1}
if
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2})
.

Proof.

Otherwise there exist sequences
\varepsilon_{i}\to 0
and injective
g_{\varepsilon_{i}}
-geodesics
c_{i}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,R_{1}]\times S^{1}
. Using Lemma
3.4
and translation of the parameter we may
assume that
c_{i}([0,\infty))\subset[R_{0},R_{1}]\times S^{1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
c
be a limit of the
g_{\varepsilon_{i}}
-geodesics
\tilde{c}_{i}(s)=c_{i}(i+s)
. Then
c
is a
g
-geodesic and
c(\mathbb{R})\subset[R_{0},R_{1}]\times S^{1}
. From (3.1)-(3.3) we infer that
c
parameterizes the circle
\{R_{0}\}\times S^{1}
, in particular
\dot{c}(s)=\pm\partial_{\theta}|_{c(s)}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\lim_{i\to\infty}\dot{\tilde{c}}_{i}(0)=\dot{c}(0)=\pm\partial_{\theta}|_{c(0)}
, and since
g_{\varepsilon_{i}}
and
g
coincide on
[0,R_{0}+\varepsilon_{0}]\times S^{1}
, we see that, for almost all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
the
\tilde{c}_{i}
are
g
-geodesics parameterizing a great circle of the spherical metric
g|_{[0,R_{1}]\times S^{1}}
. In particular, these
\tilde{c}_{i}
are periodic, in contradiction to the assumed
injectivity of the
c_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{3}\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
such that the following holds for all
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{3})
and every
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic
c
. If
c(0)=(R_{0},\theta)
and
|\sphericalangle(\dot{c}(0),\partial_{r}|_{c(0)})|<\varepsilon_{3}\max|\varphi|
, then there exists
l>0
such that
c([0,l])\subset[R_{0},R_{1}]\times S^{1}
and
c(l)\in\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
. If, moreover,
\dot{c}(0)\neq\dot{c}_{\varepsilon,\theta}(0)
then

\dot{c}(l)\neq\partial_{r}|_{c(l)}
.

Note that obviously
\dot{c}(l)\neq-\partial_{r}|_{c(l)}
.

Proof.

The first part of the claim follows from the fact that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}g_{\varepsilon}=g
in the
C^{\infty}
-topology, see Proposition
2.2
(ii). To prove the second part note that Proposition
2.2
(iv)
implies that
c_{\varepsilon,\theta}
is the only
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic in
[R_{0},R_{1}]\times S^{1}
that starts at
(R_{0},\theta)
and ends orthogonally on
\{R_{1}\}\times S^{1}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.6
.

We redefine
\varepsilon_{0}
as the minimum of
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}
from Lemma
3.3
,
3.6
, and
3.7
. We consider an injective
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic

4.
Historical remarks and open problems

For noncompact, complete Riemannian surfaces that are not homeomorphic to the plane, the cylinder, or the Möbius band, strong results concerning existence and quantity of
bounded, oscillating, or proper geodesics have been given in
[
8
]
and
[
12
]
. In the case of the cylinder there is the following open problem that is
analogous to Conjecture
1.5
, but maybe a little simpler:

Conjecture 4.1
.

On every complete Riemannian surface
S
homeomorphic to the cylinder
S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
there exist two disjoint embedded geodesic lines.

Note that there is always a straight line connecting the ends of
S
, and one would expect the existence of a min-max embedded geodesic line in its complement.

The existence of the total curvature
\int_{M}K\,dA
of a complete Riemannian plane
M
is a strong condition that helps to control the behaviour of geodesics, cf.
[
9
]
.
From S. Cohn-Vossen’s famous result
[
5
, Satz 6]
, one knows that
\int_{M}K\,dA\in[-\infty,2\pi]
if
\int_{M}K\,dA
exists as an extended real number. Note that

\int_{M}K\,dA=2\pi
for the surfaces
(\mathbb{R}^{2},g_{\varepsilon})
considered in the previous sections.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
M
be a complete Riemannian plane without simple closed geodesics.

(a)

Then there exists an injective geodesic through every point of
M
.

(b)

If, additionally,
\int_{M}K\,dA
exists, then there is an embedded geodesic line through every point of
M
.

Note that the condition
\int_{M}K^{+}\,dA<2\pi
implies both, the non-existence of simple closed geodesics and the existence of
\int_{M}K\,dA
.

Statement (a) is proved in
[
2
, Theorem 2]
, and relies on an idea from
[
6
]
. Statement (b) follows from (a) combined with
[
1
, Theorem 3]
.
It is an open question if (b) is true without the additional assumption that
\int_{M}K\,dA
exists.

Combining
[
9
, Theorem 3.5.2 (1)]
, with
[
9
, Theorem 3.7.4]
, one obtains the following strong result on the existence of straight lines that is reminiscent
of “visibility results” in the case of non-positive sectional curvature.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
M
is a complete Riemannian plane and
\int_{M}K\,dA<0
. Then there exist uncountably many straight lines in
M
.

Note that complete Riemannian planes
M
with
\int_{M}K\,dA>0
do not admit straight lines by
[
6
, Satz 5]
. More recently A. Carlotto and C. De Lellis
[
4
]

employed a new min-max method to find embedded geodesic lines. They consider complete Riemannian planes
M
of non-negative curvature that are asymptotic to a conical surface
in a strong sense. In particular, these planes have total curvature
\int_{M}K\,dA\in[0,2\pi)
. They find uncountably many embedded geodesic lines of min-max type. In particular these lines
have Morse index one if the curvature is positive everywhere. Moreover, in contrast to the pure existence result Theorem
4.2
, one has very precise control over the asymptotic
behaviour of these lines.

Note that complete Riemannian planes
M
satisfying
0<\int_{M}K\,dA<2\pi
are asymptotic to a cone over a circle of length smaller than
2\pi
with respect to pointed
Gromov-Hausdorff convergence, see
[
9
, Theorem 3.7.2]
. Although this type of convergence is much weaker than the one required in
[
4
]
, the following
question seems natural:

Question 4.4
.

Do the methods from
[
4
]
generalize to the case of general complete Riemannian planes
M
with
0<\int_{M}K\,dA<2\pi
?

Finally we mention the following question which came up during the work on this paper, see also
[
10
]
.

Question 4.5
.

Does there exist a Riemannian metric on
S^{2}
with an injective geodesic
c\colon[0,\infty)\to S^{2}
that is not asymptotic to a simple closed geodesic?
