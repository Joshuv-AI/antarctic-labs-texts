Integrality over ideal semifiltrations updated and improved version of undergraduate work from 2010
Darij Grinberg

Abstract
Abstract. We study integrality over rings (all commutative in this paper) and over ideal semifiltrations (a generalization of integrality over ideals). We begin by reproving classical results, such as a version of the “faithful module” criterion for integrality over a ring, the transitivity of integrality, and the theorem that sums and products of integral elements are again integral. Then, we define the notion of integrality over an ideal semifiltration (a sequence ( I 0 , I 1 , I 2 , … ) \left(I_{0},I_{1},I_{2},\ldots\right) of ideals satisfying I 0 = A I_{0}=A and I a ​ I b ⊆ I a + b I_{a}I_{b}\subseteq I_{a+b} for all a , b ∈ ℕ a,b\in\mathbb{N} ), which generalizes both integrality over a ring and integrality over an ideal (as considered, e.g., in Swanson/Huneke [ 5 ] ). We prove a criterion that reduces this general notion to integrality over a ring using a variant of the Rees algebra. Using this criterion, we study this notion further and obtain transitivity and closedness under sums and products for it as well. Finally, we prove the curious fact that if u u , x x and y y are three elements of a (commutative) A A -algebra (for A A a ring) such that u u is both integral over A ⁡ [ x ] A\left[x\right] and integral over A ⁡ [ y ] A\left[y\right] , then u u is integral over A ⁡ [ x ​ y ] A\left[xy\right] . We generalize this to integrality over ideal semifiltrations, too.



Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to state (and prove) some theorems and proofs
related to integrality in commutative algebra in somewhat greater generality
than is common in the literature. I claim no novelty, at least not for the
underlying ideas, but I hope that this paper will be useful as a reference (at
least for myself).

gives rise to a canonical ring homomorphism
\iota:A\rightarrow B
(sending each
a\in A
to
a\cdot 1_{B}\in B
). This ring
homomorphism, in turn, induces a ring homomorphism
\iota^{n\times n}:A^{n\times n}\rightarrow B^{n\times n}
(which acts on an
n\times n
-matrix by applying
\iota
to each entry of the matrix).

We are also going to work with matrices over
U
(that is, matrices whose
entries lie in
U
). This might sound somewhat strange, because
U
is not a
ring; however, we can still define matrices over
U
just as one defines
matrices over any ring. While we cannot multiply two matrices over
U

(because
U
is not a ring), we can define the product of a matrix over
A

with a matrix over
U
as follows: If
P\in A^{\alpha\times\beta}
is a matrix
over
A
, and
Q\in U^{\beta\times\gamma}
is a matrix over
U
(where

\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
), then we define the product
PQ\in U^{\alpha\times\gamma}
by setting

\left(PQ\right)_{x,y}=\sum\limits_{z=1}^{\beta}P_{x,z}Q_{z,y}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }x\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,\alpha\right\}\text{ and }y\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,\gamma\right\}.

(Here, for any matrix
T
and any integers
x
and
y
, we denote by
T_{x,y}

the entry of the matrix
T
in the
x
-th row and the
y
-th column.)

It is easy to see that whenever
P\in A^{\alpha\times\beta}
,
Q\in A^{\beta\times\gamma}
and
R\in U^{\gamma\times\delta}
are three matrices,
then

\left(PQ\right)R=P\left(QR\right).

(4)

This is proven in the same way as the fact that the multiplication of matrices
over a ring is associative.

Now define a matrix
V\in U^{n\times 1}
by setting

V_{i,1}=m_{i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}.

Define another matrix
S\in A^{n\times n}
by setting

S_{k,i}=a_{k,i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }k\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}\text{ and }i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}.

Then, for any
k\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(uV\right)_{k,1}

\displaystyle=u\underbrace{V_{k,1}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=m_{k}\\
\text{(by the definition of }V\text{)}\end{subarray}}=um_{k}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle\left(SV\right)_{k,1}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{S_{k,i}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=a_{k,i}\\
\text{(by the definition of }S\text{)}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{V_{i,1}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=m_{i}\\
\text{(by the definition of
}V\text{)}\end{subarray}}=\sum\limits\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{k,i}m_{i}.

Hence, the equality (
3
) rewrites as
\left(uV\right)_{k,1}=\left(SV\right)_{k,1}
. Since this holds for every

k\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, we conclude that
uV=SV
. Thus,

0=uV-SV=uI_{n}V-SV=\left(uI_{n}-S\right)V.

(5)

Here, the “
S
” in “
uI_{n}-S
” means not the matrix
S\in A^{n\times n}

itself, but rather its image under the ring homomorphism
\iota^{n\times n}:A^{n\times n}\rightarrow B^{n\times n}
; thus, the matrix
uI_{n}-S
is a
well-defined matrix in
B^{n\times n}
.

Now, let
P\in A\left[X\right]
be the characteristic polynomial of the
matrix
S\in A^{n\times n}
. Then,
P
is monic, and
\deg P=n
. Besides,

P\left(X\right)=\det\left(XI_{n}-S\right)
, so that
P\left(u\right)=\det\left(uI_{n}-S\right)
. Thus,

\displaystyle P\left(u\right)\cdot V

\displaystyle=\det\left(uI_{n}-S\right)\cdot V=\underbrace{\det\left(uI_{n}-S\right)I_{n}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\operatorname{adj}\left(uI_{n}-S\right)\cdot\left(uI_{n}-S\right)\\
\text{(by Lemma \ref{lem.adj},}\\
\text{applied to }M=uI_{n}-S\text{)}\end{subarray}}\cdot V

\displaystyle=\left(\operatorname{adj}\left(uI_{n}-S\right)\cdot\left(uI_{n}-S\right)\right)\cdot V

\displaystyle=\operatorname{adj}\left(uI_{n}-S\right)\cdot\underbrace{\left(\left(uI_{n}-S\right)V\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=0\\
\text{(by
\eqref{pf.Theorem1.BA.short.matprod=0})}\end{subarray}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{by
\eqref{(PQ)R=P(QR)}}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Since the entries of the matrix
V
are
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{n}
, this
yields
P\left(u\right)\cdot m_{k}=0
for every
k\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
. Now, from
U=\left\langle m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{n}\right\rangle_{A}
, we obtain

\displaystyle P\left(u\right)\cdot U

\displaystyle=P\left(u\right)\cdot\left\langle m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{n}\right\rangle_{A}=\left\langle P\left(u\right)\cdot m_{1},P\left(u\right)\cdot m_{2},\ldots,P\left(u\right)\cdot m_{n}\right\rangle_{A}

\displaystyle=\left\langle 0,0,\ldots,0\right\rangle_{A}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }P\left(u\right)\cdot m_{k}=0\text{ for any }k\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

This implies
P\left(u\right)=0
(since every
v\in B
satisfying
vU=0

satisfies
v=0
). Thus, Assertion
\mathcal{A}
holds. Hence, we have proved
that
\mathcal{B}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{A}
.

Proof of the implication
\mathcal{A}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{D}
.
Assume that Assertion
\mathcal{A}
holds. Then, there exists a
monic polynomial
P\in A\left[X\right]
with
\deg P=n
and
P\left(u\right)=0
. Consider this
P
.

Let
U
be the
A
-submodule
\left\langle u^{0},u^{1},\ldots,u^{n-1}\right\rangle_{A}
of
B
. As in the Proof of the implication
\mathcal{A}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
, we can show that
U
is an
n
-generated

A
-module, and that
1\in U
and
uU\subseteq U
.

Now, it is easy to show that

u^{i}\in U\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for any }i\in\mathbb{N}.

(6)

[Indeed, this follows easily from
uU\subseteq U
and
1\in U
by induction
on
i
.]

Hence, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\left(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\right)\in A^{m+1}
, we have
\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}u^{i}\in U

(since
U
is an
A
-module, and thus is closed under
A
-linear combination).

Now, the definition of
A\left[u\right]
yields

\displaystyle A\left[u\right]

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}u^{i}\ \mid\ m\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\left(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\right)\in A^{m+1}\right\}

\displaystyle\subseteq U\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}u^{i}\in U\text{ for any }m\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\left(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\right)\in A^{m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle u^{0},u^{1},\ldots,u^{n-1}\right\rangle_{A}.

Combining this with the (obvious) relation
\left\langle u^{0},u^{1},\ldots,u^{n-1}\right\rangle_{A}\subseteq A\left[u\right]
, we find

A\left[u\right]=\left\langle u^{0},u^{1},\ldots,u^{n-1}\right\rangle_{A}
.

Thus, Assertion
\mathcal{D}
holds. Hence, we have proved that
\mathcal{A}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{D}
.

Proof of the implication
\mathcal{D}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
.
Assume that Assertion
\mathcal{D}
holds. Then,
A\left[u\right]=\left\langle u^{0},u^{1},\ldots,u^{n-1}\right\rangle_{A}
.

Let
U
be the
A
-submodule
A\left[u\right]
of
B
. Then,
U
is an

n
-generated
A
-module (since
U=A\left[u\right]=\left\langle u^{0},u^{1},\ldots,u^{n-1}\right\rangle_{A}
). Besides,
1=u^{0}\in A\left[u\right]=U
. Finally,
U=A\left[u\right]
yields
uU\subseteq U
. Thus,
Assertion
\mathcal{C}
holds. Hence, we have proved that
\mathcal{D}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
.

Now, we have proved the implications
\mathcal{A}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{D}
,

\mathcal{D}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{C}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{A}
above. Thus, all four
assertions
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are
equivalent, and Theorem
1.1
is proven.

1.2
Transitivity of integrality

Let us now prove the first and probably most important consequence of
Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
A
be a ring. Let
B
be an
A
-algebra. Let
v\in B

and
u\in B
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
v
is

m
-integral over
A
, and that
u
is
n
-integral over
A\left[v\right]
. Then,
u
is
nm
-integral over
A
.

(Here, we are using the fact that if
A
is a ring, and if
v
is an element
of an
A
-algebra
B
, then
A\left[v\right]
is a subring of
B
, and
therefore

is an
A\left[v\right]
-algebra.)

Proof 1.7

(Proof of Theorem
1.6
.)
.

Since
v
is
m
-integral over
A
, we have

A\left[v\right]=\left\langle v^{0},v^{1},\ldots,v^{m-1}\right\rangle_{A}
(this is the Assertion
\mathcal{D}
of Theorem
1.1
, stated
for
v
and
m
in lieu of
u
and
n
).

Since
u
is
n
-integral over
A\left[v\right]
, we have
\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]=\left\langle u^{0},u^{1},\ldots,u^{n-1}\right\rangle_{A\left[v\right]}
(this is the Assertion

\mathcal{D}
of Theorem
1.1
, stated for
A\left[v\right]
in
lieu of
A
).

Let
S=\left\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\right\}
. Then,
S
is a finite set with size
\left|S\right|=nm
.

Let
x\in\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]
. Then, there
exist
n
elements
b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}
of
A\left[v\right]
such
that
x=\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}b_{i}u^{i}
(since

b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}
.

For each
i\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\right\}
, there exist
m
elements

a_{i,0},a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,m-1}
of
A
such that
b_{i}=\sum\limits\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{i,j}v^{j}
(because
b_{i}\in A\left[v\right]=\left\langle v^{0},v^{1},\ldots,v^{m-1}\right\rangle_{A}
). Consider these
a_{i,0},a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,m-1}
. Thus,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\underbrace{b_{i}}_{=\sum\limits\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{i,j}v^{j}}u^{i}=\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits\limits_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{i,j}v^{j}u^{i}=\sum\limits\limits_{\left(i,j\right)\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\right\}}a_{i,j}v^{j}u^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{\left(i,j\right)\in S}a_{i,j}v^{j}u^{i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }\left\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\right\}\times\left\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\right\}=S\right)

\displaystyle\in\left\langle v^{j}u^{i}\ \mid\ \left(i,j\right)\in S\right\rangle_{A}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }a_{i,j}\in A\text{ for every
}\left(i,j\right)\in S\right).

So we have proved that
x\in\left\langle v^{j}u^{i}\ \mid\ \left(i,j\right)\in S\right\rangle_{A}
for every
x\in\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]
. In other words,
\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]\subseteq\left\langle v^{j}u^{i}\ \mid\ \left(i,j\right)\in S\right\rangle_{A}
. Conversely,
\left\langle v^{j}u^{i}\ \mid\ \left(i,j\right)\in S\right\rangle_{A}\subseteq\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]
(this is trivial). Combining these two relations,
we find
\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]=\left\langle v^{j}u^{i}\ \mid\ \left(i,j\right)\in S\right\rangle_{A}
. Thus, the

A
-module
\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]
is

nm
-generated (since
\left|S\right|=nm
).

Let
U=\left(A\left[v\right]\right)\left[u\right]
. Then, the

A
-module
U
is
nm
-generated. Besides,
U
is an
A
-submodule of
B
, and
we have
1\in U
and
uU\subseteq U
.

Thus, the element
u
of
B
satisfies the Assertion
\mathcal{C}
of
Theorem
1.1
with
n
replaced by
nm
. Hence,
u\in B
satisfies
the four equivalent assertions
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{C}
and

\mathcal{D}
of Theorem
1.1
, all with
n
replaced by
nm
. Thus,

u
is
nm
-integral over
A
. This proves Theorem
1.6
.

1.3
Integrality of sums and products

Before the next significant consequence of Theorem
1.1
, let us show
an essentially trivial fact:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
A
be a ring. Let
B
be an
A
-algebra. Let
a\in A
.
Then,
a\cdot 1_{B}\in B
is
1
-integral over
A
.

Proof 1.9

(Proof of Theorem
1.8
.)
.

The polynomial
X-a\in A\left[X\right]

is monic and satisfies

\deg\left(X-a\right)=1
; moreover,
evaluating this polynomial at
a\cdot 1_{B}\in B
yields
a\cdot 1_{B}-a\cdot 1_{B}=0
. Hence, there exists a monic polynomial
P\in A\left[X\right]
with
\deg P=1
and
P\left(a\cdot 1_{B}\right)=0
(namely, the
polynomial
P\in A\left[X\right]
defined by
P\left(X\right)=X-a
).
Thus,
a\cdot 1_{B}
is
1
-integral over
A
. This proves
Theorem
1.8
.

The following theorem is a standard result, generalizing (for example) the
classical fact that sums and products of algebraic integers are again
algebraic integers:

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
A
be a ring. Let
B
be an
A
-algebra. Let
x\in B

and
y\in B
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
x
is

m
-integral over
A
, and that
y
is
n
-integral over
A
.

(a)
Then,
x+y
is
nm
-integral over
A
.

(b)
Furthermore,
xy
is
nm
-integral over
A
.

Our proof of this theorem will rely on a simple lemma:

Lemma 1.11
.

Let
A
be a ring. Let
C
be an
A
-algebra. Let

x\in C
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
P\in A\left[X\right]
be a monic polynomial

. Define a polynomial

Proof 1.12

(Proof of Lemma
1.11
.)
.

Recall that
P
is a monic polynomial
with
\deg P=n
; hence, we can write
P
in the form

P=X^{n}+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}X^{i}

(7)

for some
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}\in A
. Consider these
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}
.

Now,

\displaystyle Q\left(X\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(X-x\right)=\underbrace{\left(X-x\right)^{n}}_{=X^{n}+\left(\text{lower order terms}\right)}+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}\left(X-x\right)^{i}}_{=\left(\text{lower order
terms}\right)}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{here, we have substituted }X-x\text{ for
}X\text{ in \eqref{pf.Theorem5.b.1}}\right)

\displaystyle=X^{n}+\left(\text{lower order terms}\right),

where “lower order terms” means a sum of

. Hence,

is a monic

. This proves Lemma
1.11
.

Proof 1.13

(Proof of Theorem
1.10
.)
.

Since
y
is
n
-integral over
A
, there
exists a monic polynomial

. Consider this

.

(a)
Let

. Then,

, so that

Now, define a polynomial

. Then, Lemma

is a

. Also, substituting

.

Q\in C\left[X\right]
with

is
n
-integral over

. In

is

-integral over

(since

is

-integral over

. This proves
Theorem
1.10

(a)
.

(b)
Recall that
P\in A\left[X\right]
is a monic polynomial with

\deg P=n
. Thus, there exist elements

of

.

(8)

\displaystyle Q\left(xy\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(xy\right)^{n}}_{=x^{n}y^{n}}+\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}x^{n-k}\underbrace{a_{k}\left(xy\right)^{k}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=a_{k}x^{k}y^{k}\\

\text{(by

Q\in\left(A\left[x\right]\right)\left[X\right]

xy
is
n
-integral over

A\left[x\right]
. Hence, Theorem
1.6
(applied to

and

is

-integral over

. This proves
Theorem
1.10

(b)
.

Integrality over ideal semifiltrations

2.1
Definitions of ideal semifiltrations and integrality over them

We now set our sights at a more general notion of integrality.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of ideals of
A
. Then,
\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
is called an
ideal semifiltration
of

A
if and only if it satisfies the two conditions

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=A;

\displaystyle I_{a}I_{b}

\displaystyle\subseteq I_{a+b}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for every }a\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }b\in\mathbb{N}.

Two simple examples of ideal semifiltrations can easily be constructed from
any ideal:

Example 2.2
.

Let
A
be a ring. Let
I
be an ideal of
A
. Then:

(a)
The sequence
\left(I^{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
is
an ideal semifiltration of
A
. (Here,
I^{\rho}
denotes the
\rho
-th power
of
I
in the multiplicative monoid of ideals of
A
; in particular,
I^{0}=A
.)

(b)
The sequence
\left(A,I,I,I,\ldots\right)=\left(\begin{cases}A,&\text{if }\rho=0;\\
I,&\text{if }\rho>0\end{cases}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
is an ideal semifiltration of
A
.

Proof 2.3

(Proof of Example
2.2
.)
.

This is a straightforward exercise
in checking axioms.

(Proof of Proposition

We know that

2.2
Polynomial rings and Rees algebras

In order to study integrality over ideal semifiltrations, we shall now
introduce the concept of a Rees algebra – a subalgebra of a polynomial ring
that conveniently encodes an ideal semifiltration of the base ring. This,
again, generalizes a classical notion for ideals (namely, the Rees algebra of
an ideal – see
[
5
, Definition 5.1.1]
). First, we recall a basic fact:

Definition 2.8
.

Let
A
be a ring. Let
B
be an
A
-algebra. Then, there
is a canonical ring homomorphism
\iota:A\rightarrow B
that sends each
a\in A
to
a\cdot 1_{B}\in B
. This ring homomorphism
\iota
induces a canonical
ring homomorphism
\iota\left[Y\right]:A\left[Y\right]\rightarrow B\left[Y\right]
between the polynomial rings
A\left[Y\right]
and

B\left[Y\right]
that sends each polynomial
\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}Y^{i}\in A\left[Y\right]
(with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\left(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\right)\in A^{m+1}
) to the polynomial

\sum\limits\limits_{i=0}^{m}\iota\left(a_{i}\right)Y^{i}\in B\left[Y\right]
. Thus, the polynomial ring
B\left[Y\right]
becomes an
A\left[Y\right]
-algebra.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
be an ideal semifiltration of
A
. Thus,
I_{0},I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
are ideals of
A
, and we have
I_{0}=A
.

Consider the polynomial ring
A\left[Y\right]
. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
the subset
I_{i}Y^{i}
of
A\left[Y\right]
is an
A
-submodule of the

A
-algebra
A\left[Y\right]
(since
I_{i}
is an ideal of
A
). Hence,
the sum
\sum\limits\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i}Y^{i}
of these
A
-submodules must
also be an
A
-submodule of the
A
-algebra
A\left[Y\right]
.

Let
A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]

denote this
A
-submodule
\sum\limits\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i}Y^{i}
of the

A
-algebra
A\left[Y\right]
. Then,

\displaystyle A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i}Y^{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}a_{i}Y^{i}\ \mid\ \left(a_{i}\in I_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}\right)\text{, }\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.\text{and }\left(\text{only finitely many
}i\in\mathbb{N}\text{ satisfy }a_{i}\neq 0\right)\vphantom{\sum\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{P\in A\left[Y\right]\ \mid\ \text{the }i\text{-th coefficient
of the polynomial }P\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.\text{lies in }I_{i}\text{ for every }i\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Clearly,
A\subseteq A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]
, since

A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]=\sum\limits\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i}Y^{i}\supseteq\underbrace{I_{0}}_{=A}\underbrace{Y^{0}}_{=1}=A\cdot 1=A.

Hence,
1\in A\subseteq A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]
. Also, the
A
-submodule
A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]
of
A\left[Y\right]
is an

A
-subalgebra of the
A
-algebra
A\left[Y\right]
(by
Lemma
2.10
below), and thus is a subring of
A\left[Y\right]
.

This
A
-subalgebra
A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]
is called the
Rees algebra
of the ideal
semifiltration
\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
A
be a ring, and let
\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
be an ideal semifiltration of
A
. Then, the

A
-submodule
A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]
of
A\left[Y\right]
is an
A
-subalgebra of the
A
-algebra

A\left[Y\right]
.

Proof 2.11

(Proof of Lemma
2.10
.)
.

This is an easy exercise. (Use

I_{a}I_{b}\subseteq I_{a+b}
to prove that
A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]
is closed under multiplication.)

.

Let
A
be a ring. Let
B
be an
A
-algebra. Let

\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}
be an ideal semifiltration of

.

and its

between ideal semifiltrations of a ring

and graded

-subalgebras of the

:

.

Let

be a ring. Consider the polynomial ring

as a graded

(a)

5

5

Let

. Let

. Let

Consider the polynomial ring

Proof 2.15

(Proof of Theorem

.)
.

\Longrightarrow:
Assume that

, there exists some

is

Theorem
2.14
.

\Longleftarrow:
Assume that
uY
is
n
-integral over
A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]
. Thus, there exists a
monic polynomial
P\in\left(A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]\right)\left[X\right]
with

and

. Consider this

. Since

\left(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\right)\in\left(A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]\right)^{n+1}

. Consider this

. Note that

(since

is monic and
\deg P=n
).

For every

, we have
p_{k}\in A\left[\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\ast Y\right]=\sum\limits\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i}Y^{i}
, and thus there exists a sequence
\left(p_{k,i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}

, such that
\left(p_{k,i}\in I_{i}\text{ for every }i\in\mathbb{N}\right)
, and such that only finitely

(since

\sum\limits\limits_{k=0}^{n}p_{k,n-k}u^{k}=0

is
n
-integral over

\left(A,\left(I_{\rho}\right)_{\rho\in\mathbb{N}}\right)

\Longleftarrow

2.14
