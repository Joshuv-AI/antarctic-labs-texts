Summations by parton showers of large logarithms in electron-positron annihilation Preprint: DESY 20-182
Zoltán Nagy, Davison E. Soper

Abstract
In a companion publication, we have explored how to examine the summation of large logarithms in a parton shower. Here, we apply this general program to the thrust distribution in electron-positron annihilation, using several shower algorithms. The method is to work with an appropriate integral transform of the distribution for the observable of interest. Then, we reformulate the parton shower calculation so as to obtain the transformed distribution as an exponential for which we can compute the terms in the perturbative expansion of the exponent.



I
Introduction

A parton shower event generator can provide a QCD based approximation for a cross section
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v})
for an observable
J
to take a value
\bm{v}
in hadron-hadron, lepton-hadron, or electron-positron collisions. For example
J
could denote the transverse momentum distribution in the Drell-Yan process and
\bm{v}
could be
\bm{v}=\bm{k}_{\mathrm{T}}
. Suppose that the observable
J
is infrared safe with a scale
\hat{Q}^{2}_{J}(\bm{v})
substantially greater than
1\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
. Then we can, at least in principle, omit a model for hadronization in the event generator. This leaves us with just an event generator based on a parton shower, which uses parton splitting functions based on the soft and collinear singularities of QCD. Running the parton shower event generator gives us an approximation
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
for the cross section
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v})
.

The QCD perturbative expansion for
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v})
will contain logarithms,
L=\log(\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}/\hat{Q}_{J}^{2}(\bm{v}))
, where
\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}
is the scale of the hardest interaction in the event. Typically one finds perturbative contributions to
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v})
proportional to
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}(\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2})L^{2n}
. If
\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}
is close to
\hat{Q}_{J}^{2}(\bm{v})
, then we do not need the parton shower at all. Rather, we can use just fixed order perturbation theory. However, if
1\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}\ll\hat{Q}_{J}^{2}(\bm{v})\ll\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}
, and
L^{2}\gtrsim 1/\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}(\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2})
, then fixed order perturbation theory is not adequate. One must try to sum the contributions at each order of perturbation theory that have the most powers of
L
. Since the splitting functions in a parton shower reflect the soft and collinear singularities of QCD and since it is these singularities that lead to the appearance of the logarithms
L
, we may hope that a parton shower provides an adequate approximation to the cross section
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v})
.

We caution the reader that we do not expect that a given parton shower algorithm correctly sums the logarithms for
all
infrared safe observables that generate large logarithms in perturbation theory. Thus we would not speak of a next-to-leading-log parton shower, without specifying just what logs are correctly summed.

For some observables
J
one can derive an analytical approximation,
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{analytical})
, to
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v})
that sums the large logarithms in an appropriate sense. In some cases
NSpT
;
NSdglap
, it is also possible to find an analytical formula that well approximates the shower result
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
. Then one can tell whether
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
agrees with
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{analytical})
to the accuracy with which
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{analytical})
sums the large logarithms. However, this is usually difficult.

Normally, the approximation
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
obtained with a parton shower is limited to a numerical result obtained by averaging over many generated events. In the limit of very large hard scattering scales
\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}
,
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
should match
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{analytical})
. However, for
\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}
in the kinematic range of experiments,
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
contains effects that are numerically important but are not included in
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{analytical})
. Thus it is difficult to tell whether
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
agrees with
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{analytical})
.

One approach to comparing
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
to
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{analytical})
is to directly calculate
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v};\mathrm{shower})
for a sequence of very large hard scattering scales
\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}
that are far from the range of experiments. This approach can work

, and in fact we use it to a limited extent in this paper. However, it is difficult to maintain the required numerical accuracy at very large values of
\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2}
in a practical parton shower event generator.

In an analytical approach, one typically starts by taking an appropriate integral transform of
\hat{\sigma}_{J}(\bm{v})
. Then one calculates a cross section
\sigma_{J}(\bm{r})
depending on a variable or variables
\bm{r}
. The cross section
\sigma_{J}(\bm{r})
contains logarithms
L(\bm{r})
that are large when
\bm{r}
approaches a limit. For instance, one might take the Fourier transform of the
\bm{k}_{\mathrm{T}}
distribution in the Drell-Yan process. Then
\bm{r}
is the transverse position, usually called
\bm{b}
. The logarithm is
L=\log(\bm{b}^{2}\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2})
, which is large when
\bm{b}^{2}\to\infty
.

The aim of this paper is to redesign the calculation of the parton shower cross section so that it produces the same result for
\sigma_{J}(\bm{r})
as before but so that it produces a calculation of
\sigma_{J}(\bm{r})
and not a cross section for other observables. The redesigned calculation gives
\sigma_{J}(\bm{r})
as an exponential of a quantity that can be expanded in powers of the shower splitting operator. The leading order term in the exponent is simple and is the candidate for the summation of large logarithms produced by the shower. If the higher order contributions to the exponent are suitably small, then they to not interfere with the summation represented by the leading order terms.

We have presented a general formulation of this program in Ref.
NSlogsum
. The general formulation applies to hadron-hadron collisions, lepton-hadron collisions, and electron-positron collisions and to a general infrared-safe observable
J
. In this paper, we apply the general formalism to a simple example. We examine the thrust distribution in electron-positron annihilation. In this case, the original variable
\bm{v}
is
\tau=1-T
, where
T
is the thrust. We take the Laplace transform of the
\tau
distribution, so that the transformed variable
\bm{r}
is the Laplace transform variable
\nu
.

There is much to be learned from this example. In particular, we learn that the shower result for
\sigma_{J}(\nu)
depends on some details of the parton shower algorithm that one might have thought are not important.

III
Preview

In this paper, we propose a way to gain more direct access to the summation of large logarithms in a parton shower than by simply running the shower and examining the result numerically. The analysis adapts the general formulation of this program in Ref.
NSlogsum
to the practical analysis of first order parton shower algorithms. Our example is the thrust distribution in electron-positron annihilation. Here is a brief preview.

•

We are interested in the thrust distribution
g(\tau)
with
\tau=1-T
, where
T
is the thrust.

•

As in analytical approaches, we work with the Laplace transform
\tilde{g}(\nu)
of
g(\tau)
.

•

\tilde{g}(\nu)
contains large logarithms,
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}(\mu_{\textsc{h}}^{2})^{n}\log^{j}(\nu)
with
j\leq 2n
.

•

We suppose that we know the proper summation of the
\log(\nu)
factors in full QCD at a certain level of accuracy, but a leading order parton shower is not full QCD. We wish to know what result the parton shower gives.

•

The result of simply running the shower and examining the result numerically can be expressed as in Eq. (
5
),

\tilde{g}(\nu)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{h}}}\big({1}\big|\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{U}(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2},Q^{2})\,\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)\;.

(18)

Here
\sigma_{\textsc{h}}
is the total hard scattering cross section and
({1}|\cdots|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}})
indicates an ensemble average in the statistical state
|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}})
representing the perturbative hard scattering. Then
\mathcal{U}(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2},Q^{2})
represents the operator on the statistical space that generates the shower. This gives us states consisting of tens of partons. We could measure any operator
\mathcal{O}_{J}
that we like in this many-parton state. We apply a simple operator
\mathcal{O}(\nu)
that measures the Laplace transformed thrust distribution on this state.

•

In this paper, we rewrite
\tilde{g}(\nu)
in the form

\tilde{g}(\nu)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{h}}}\big({1}\big|\mathbb{T}\exp\!\big(\mathcal{I}(\nu)\big)\mathcal{O}(\nu)\big|{\rho_{\textsc{h}}}\big)\;.

The notation

indicates an ordering instruction for the exponential, as in Eq. (
7
) and later in Eq. (
48
). In the example used in this paper, the operator

applied to the hard state

•

With this form, we have expressed

in terms of the exponential of an operator

2
2

2

The operator

•

In some cases, we can show analytically that

•

i\in L
. Then,

\tau=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Q^{2}}}\left[\sum_{i\in R}(E_{i}-\vec{p}_{i}\cdot\vec{n}_{\textsc{t}})+\sum_{i\in L}(E_{i}+\vec{p}_{i}\cdot\vec{n}_{\textsc{t}})\right].

(24)

Using the thrust axis, we define
\pm
components of vectors by

p^{\pm}=\left[p_{i}^{0}\pm\vec{p}\cdot\vec{n}_{\textsc{t}}\right]/\sqrt{2}\;.

(25)

Then we can write

\tau=\tau_{R}+\tau_{L}\;,

(26)

where, using
Q^{2}=2Q^{+}Q^{-}
with
Q^{+}=Q^{-}
,

\begin{split}\tau_{R}=\sum_{i\in R}\frac{p_{i}^{-}}{Q^{-}}\;,\quad\quad\tau_{L}=\sum_{i\in L}\frac{p_{i}^{+}}{Q^{+}}\;.\end{split}

(27)

In order to use a parton shower to analyze the thrust distribution, we begin with the cross section

g(\tau)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textsc{h}}}\frac{d\sigma}{d\tau}\;,

(28)

where
\sigma_{\textsc{h}}
is the hard scattering cross section, equal to
d\sigma/d\tau
integrated over
\tau
. We wish to analyze the small
\tau
behavior of
g(\tau)
. For this purpose, it is standard to work with the Laplace transform of
g(\tau)
,

\tilde{g}(\nu)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\tau\ e^{-\nu\tau}g(\tau)\;.

(29)

The coefficient of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}
in the perturbative expansion of
g(\tau)
is not a normal function but is a distribution with
\log^{j-1}(\tau)/\tau
singularities at
\tau=0
. In order to work with normal functions, we define the integral of
g(\tau)
,

f(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\!d\bar{\tau}\ g(\bar{\tau})\;.

(30)

The coefficients in the perturbative expansion of
f(\tau)
are functions with
\log^{j}(\tau)
integrable singularities. The cross section
g(\bar{\tau})
vanishes for
\bar{\tau}>1/2
, so
f(\tau)=1
for
\tau>1/2
.

Consider the Laplace transform of
f(\tau)
:

\tilde{f}(\nu)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\tau\ e^{-\nu\tau}f(\tau)\;.

(31)

We have

\begin{split}\tilde{f}(\nu)={}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\tau^{\prime}\ e^{-\nu\tau^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{\tau^{\prime}}\!d\tau\ g(\tau)\\
={}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\tau\ g(\tau)\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\!d\tau^{\prime}\ e^{-\nu\tau^{\prime}}\\
={}&\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\tau\ g(\tau)\,e^{-\nu\tau}\;.\end{split}

(32)

Thus

\tilde{f}(\nu)=\frac{\tilde{g}(\nu)}{\nu}\;.

(33)

The function
f(\tau)
is given by the inverse Laplace transform of
\tilde{f}(\nu)
:

f(\tau)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\int_{C}\!d\nu\ e^{\nu\tau}\,\frac{\tilde{g}(\nu)}{\nu}\;.

(34)

The contour
C
runs from
\nu_{0}-{\mathrm{i}}\infty
to
\nu_{0}+{\mathrm{i}}\infty
parallel to the imaginary
\nu
axis, where
\nu_{0}>0
so that the contour is to the right of the singularity of
\tilde{g}(\nu)/\nu
at
\nu=0
.

We expect the coefficient of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}
in the perturbative expansion of
f(\tau)
to contain terms proportional to
\log^{j}(\tau)
for
\tau\to 0
. To see how this translates to
\tilde{g}(\nu)
, we can start by noting that

f(\tau)=\tau^{A}\implies\tilde{g}(\nu)=\Gamma(1+A)\,\nu^{-A}\;.

(35)

Thus

\begin{split}f(\tau)={}&\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{j}}{j!}\,\log^{j}(\tau)\implies\\
\tilde{g}(\nu)={}&\Gamma(1+A)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-A)^{j}}{j!}\,\log^{j}(\nu)\;.\end{split}

(36)

Matching powers of
A
, we learn that logarithms of
\tau
for small
\tau
translate into logarithms of
\nu
for large
\nu
.

We wish to use the parton shower formalism to find an analytical formula that sums the logarithms of
\nu
in
\tilde{g}(\nu)
. We can then compare what we find to the standard QCD formula that sums these logarithms. The final step needed to obtain something that can be compared to experiment would be to perform the inverse Laplace transform (
34
). This step is the same for the parton shower method or the normal analytical methods. We discuss this step only briefly in this paper.

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[k]}(\mu^{2};\nu)={}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big[}{\mathcal{Y}^{[k-1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)\,\mathcal{O}(\nu)\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big]_{\mathbb{P}}}\\

Similarly, we can write Eq. (

We now illustrate this for the first two orders. At order 1, Eq. (

At order

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[2]}(\mu^{2};\nu)={}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big[}{\mathcal{Y}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)\,\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})\,\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big]_{\mathbb{P}}}\\
&-\mathcal{Y}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)\;.\end{split}

From Eq. (

\mathcal{Y}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)=\int_{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}^{\mu^{2}}\!\frac{d\bar{\mu}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}^{2}}\ \mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\bar{\mu}^{2};\nu)\;.

For

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[2]}(\mu^{2};\nu)={}&\int_{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}^{\mu^{2}}\!\frac{d\bar{\mu}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}^{2}}\ {\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big[}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big[}{\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{S}(\bar{\mu}^{2})\,\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big]_{\mathbb{P}}}\\
&\quad\quad\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\big[}{\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})\,\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\big]_{1-\mathbb{P}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big]_{\mathbb{P}}}\;.\end{split}

Here we use the abbreviation

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\big[}{\mathcal{A}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\big]_{1-\mathbb{P}}}=\mathcal{A}-{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big[}{\mathcal{A}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big]_{\mathbb{P}}}\;.

where

\lambda_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}}=\big({1}\big|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mu^{2};\nu)\big|{\{p,f,c,c\}_{2}}\big)\;.

(81)

VII
Evaluation of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)

(85)

This is a convenient form for calculations.

VIII
Change in
\tau
induced by a splitting

The operator
\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})\,\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)
appears in Eq. (
85
) for
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
. This operator is

\begin{split}\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})\,\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)={}&e^{-\nu\tau_{\textrm{op}}}\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})e^{+\nu\tau_{\textrm{op}}}\;.\end{split}

(86)

The operator
\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})
is a sum of operators,

\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}_{l}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})\;,

(87)

where
l
is the label of the parton that splits. When we apply
\mathcal{S}_{l}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})
to a state
|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}})
, the splitting operator creates a new state
|{\{\hat{p},\hat{f},\hat{c},\hat{c}^{\prime}\}_{m+1}})
as long as
l\leq m
. For
l>m
,
\mathcal{S}_{l}
just gives zero. The operators
\tau_{\textrm{op}}
measure the values of
\tau
before and after the splitting. Thus

\begin{split}\big({\{\hat{p},\hat{f},\hat{c},\hat{c}^{\prime}\}_{m+1}}\big|\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{S}_{l}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})\,\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}}\big)\hskip-204.85974pt&\\
={}&e^{-\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)}\big({\{\hat{p},\hat{f},\hat{c},\hat{c}^{\prime}\}_{m+1}}\big|\mathcal{S}_{l}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}}\big)\;,\end{split}

(88)

where
\tau=\tau(\{p\}_{m})
and
\hat{\tau}=\tau(\{\hat{p}\}_{m+1})
. Thus we need to know how
\tau
changes in a splitting. We are looking for the leading contributions to logarithms of
\nu
, so we can use the approximations that
\tau
is small and that the splitting is nearly soft or collinear.

We start with momenta
\{p\}_{m}
and suppose that the parton that splits is in the right thrust hemisphere,
l\in R
. The splitting produces a new parton
l
and a parton
m+1
. After the splitting, we have partons with momenta
\{\hat{p}\}_{m+1}
.

The emission of a parton changes the thrust axis. However, in the case of a nearly soft or collinear splitting of a parton in a state with small
\tau
, the thrust axis changes by very little. For this reason, we calculate
\tau(\{\hat{p}\}_{m+1})
for the new parton state using the thrust axis of the old parton state
\{p\}_{m}
. We also assume that after the splitting partons
l
and
m+1
are still in the right thrust hemisphere.

Now we turn to the calculation of
\hat{\tau}-\tau
. We use the definition, Eqs. (
26
) and (
27
), to write

\begin{split}\hat{\tau}-\tau={}&\frac{\hat{p}_{l}^{-}+\hat{p}_{m+1}^{-}-p_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in R\\
i\neq\{l,m+1\}\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}^{-}-p_{i}^{-}}{Q^{-}}+\sum_{i\in L}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}^{+}-p_{i}^{+}}{Q^{+}}\;.\end{split}

(89)

Now we need to evaluate
({\hat{p}_{l}^{-}+\hat{p}_{m+1}^{-}-p_{i}^{-}})/{Q^{-}}
and
(\hat{p}_{i}^{\pm}-p_{i}^{\pm})/Q^{\pm}
. Following the notation of Appendix B of Ref.
NSThreshold
, we define

\begin{split}h_{\pm}={}&(1+y\pm\lambda)/2\;,\\
\lambda={}&\sqrt{(1+y)^{2}-4a_{l}y}\;,\\
a_{l}={}&\frac{Q^{2}}{2p_{l}\cdot Q}\;,\end{split}

(90)

where
y
was defined in Eq. (
2
). We suppose that
y\ll 1
. We define a lightlike vector
n_{l}
by

n_{l}=\frac{2p_{l}\cdot Q}{Q^{2}}\,Q-p_{l}\;.

(91)

Note that
n_{l}
is independent of the normalization of
Q
.

We write the momentum vectors for partons
l
and
m+1
after the splitting as

\begin{split}\hat{p}_{l}={}&h_{+}z\,p_{l}+h_{-}(1-z)n_{l}+k_{\perp}\;,\\
\hat{p}_{m+1}={}&h_{+}(1-z)p_{l}+h_{-}z\,n_{l}-k_{\perp}\;,\end{split}

(92)

where
k_{\perp}\cdot p_{l}=k_{\perp}\cdot n_{l}=0
. The splitting is specified by
y
, the momentum fraction
z
in Eq. (
92
), and the azimuthal angle
\phi
of
k_{\perp}
. The magnitude of
k_{\perp}
is determined by the condition
\hat{p}_{l}^{2}=0
or
\hat{p}_{m+1}^{2}=0
:

-k_{\perp}^{2}=z(1-z)y\,2p_{l}\cdot Q\;.

(93)

Define

P_{l}=\hat{p}_{l}+\hat{p}_{m+1}=h_{+}p_{l}+h_{-}n_{l}\;.

(94)

This gives us
P_{l}^{2}=2p_{l}\cdot Q\,y
. Using these results we obtain

(Q-p_{l})^{2}=(Q-P_{l})^{2}\;.

(95)

We require that momentum be conserved in the splitting, so that

Q-p_{l}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}p_{i}\;,\qquad Q-P_{l}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq l\end{subarray}}^{m}\hat{p}_{i}\;.

(96)

The relation (
95
) allows the
\hat{p}_{i}
for
i\notin\{l,m+1\}
to be obtained from the
p_{i}
by a Lorentz transformation,

\hat{p}_{i}^{\mu}=\Lambda^{\mu}_{\nu}\,p_{i}^{\nu}\;,\hskip 28.45274pti\notin\{l,{m\!+\!1}\}\;.

(97)

The needed Lorentz transformation can be a small boost in the
p_{l}
-
Q
plane. Let

p_{i}=\alpha_{i}p_{l}+\beta_{i}n_{l}+p_{i,\perp}\;,

(98)

where
p_{i,\perp}\cdot p_{l}=p_{i,\perp}\cdot n_{l}=0
. Then define
\hat{p}_{i}
for
i\notin\{l,m+1\}
by

\hat{p}_{i}=e^{\omega}\alpha_{i}p_{l}+e^{-\omega}\beta_{i}n_{l}+p_{i,\perp}\;.

(99)

The needed boost angle is small:

\omega=y+\mathcal{O}(y^{2})\;.

(100)

Using Eq. (
97
) in Eq. (
89
), we have

\begin{split}\hat{\tau}&-\tau=\frac{\hat{p}_{l}^{-}+\hat{p}_{m+1}^{-}-p_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in R\\
i\neq l\end{subarray}}(\Lambda^{-}_{\nu}-\delta^{-}_{\nu})\frac{p_{i}^{\nu}}{Q^{-}}+\sum_{i\in L}(\Lambda^{+}_{\nu}-\delta^{+}_{\nu})\frac{p_{i}^{\nu}}{Q^{+}}\;.\end{split}

(101)

We will see momentarily that
(\hat{p}_{l}^{-}+\hat{p}_{m+1}^{-}-p_{l}^{-})/Q^{-}
is small, of order
y
. This allows
\hat{\tau}-\tau
to be of order
y
.

In the third term, for
i\in\mathrm{R}
,
(\Lambda^{\mu}_{\nu}-\delta^{\mu}_{\nu})
is of order
y
. The thrust axis defines the
\pm
components of vectors in Eq. (
101
). If
p_{l}
were exactly aligned with the thrust axis, then the only nonvanishing index choice for
\Lambda^{-}_{\nu}
would be
\nu=-
. But
p_{i}^{-}/Q^{-}\ll 1
for
i\in\mathrm{R}
, since this quantity is of order
\tau
and we suppose that
\tau\ll 1
. This restriction on the index choices is not exact. However, for
i\in\mathrm{R}
, the components
p_{i}^{\nu}/Q^{-}
for
\nu\in\{1,2\}
are of order
p_{i}^{\nu}/Q^{-}\sim[{p_{i}^{+}p_{i}^{-}}]^{1/2}/Q^{-}
, which is at most of order
\sqrt{\tau}
. The component
p_{i}^{\nu}/Q^{-}
for
\nu=+
can be of order 1. However,
\Lambda^{-}_{+}=\Lambda^{--}
is at most of order
y^{2}
since
\Lambda=\exp(\omega w)
where
\omega
is given by Eq. (
100
) and the first order contribution to
\Lambda^{--}
vanishes because the generator matrix
w^{\mu\nu}
is antisymmetric. Thus the second term in Eq. (
101
) is of order
y
times a small factor, either
\tau
,
\sqrt{\tau}
, or
y
. The same reasoning applies to the third term.

We conclude that the only surviving term in Eq. (
101
) is the first:

\begin{split}\hat{\tau}-\tau\approx{}&\frac{\hat{p}_{l}^{-}+\hat{p}_{m+1}^{-}-p_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}\;.\end{split}

(102)

We have

\begin{split}\frac{\hat{p}_{l}^{-}+\hat{p}_{m+1}^{-}-p_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}\approx{}&\frac{(1-a_{l})yp_{l}^{-}+a_{l}yn_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}\;.\end{split}

(103)

With our kinematic conventions,

\begin{split}\frac{p_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}={}&\frac{1-\cos\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}})}{2a_{l}}\;,\\
\frac{n_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}={}&\frac{1+\cos\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}})}{2a_{l}}\;,\end{split}

(104)

where

\cos\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}})=\frac{|\vec{p}_{l}\cdot\vec{n}_{\textsc{t}}|}{|\vec{p}_{l}|}\;.

(105)

This gives us

\frac{\hat{p}_{l}+\hat{p}_{m+1}^{-}-p_{l}^{-}}{Q^{-}}\approx\xi_{l}\,y\;,

(106)

where

\xi_{l}=1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2a_{l}}\right)[1-\cos(\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}}))]\;.

(107)

That is

\hat{\tau}-\tau\approx\xi_{l}\,y\;.

(108)

The same result holds for
l\in L
if we change
1-\cos(\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}}))
to
1+\cos(\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}}))
.

If we are splitting the quark or the antiquark in the two parton state created initially in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation, then
a_{l}=1
and
\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}})=0
. Then
\xi_{l}=1
.

In the general case,
0<1-\cos(\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}}))<1
for
l\in R
and
1/2<(2a_{l}-1)/(2a_{l})<1
, so

0<\xi_{l}<1\;.

(109)

We get
\xi_{l}\to 0
only when
\theta_{l}\to\pi/2
and parton
l
is very soft,
1/a_{l}\to 0
. Notice that there is no singularity for
\theta_{l}\to\pi/2
, so there is no singularity for
\xi_{l}\to 0
. There is a singularity for
\theta(l,\vec{n}_{\mathrm{T}})\to 0
for all partons
l
. This corresponds to
\xi_{l}\to 1
. Thus in the general case we can treat
\xi_{l}
as being close to 1. We will argue in Appendix
A
that for the purpose of finding next-to-leading logarithms of
\nu
we can simply set
\xi_{l}
to 1.

We conclude that the effect of the operators
\mathcal{O}(\nu)
in a splitting of parton
l
can be approximated by

\begin{split}\mathcal{O}(\nu)\,\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}_{l}(\mu^{2})&\,\mathcal{O}^{-1}(\nu)\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}}\big)\\
\approx{}&\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}_{l}(\mu^{2})\,e^{-\nu\xi_{l}^{\mathrm{op}}y}\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}}\big)\;,\end{split}

(110)

where
\xi_{l}^{\mathrm{op}}
is an operator that, acting on a state
|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{m}})
, has eigenvalue
\xi_{l}
as defined in Eq. (
107
) as long as
l\leq m
. For
l>m
, we can simply define
\xi_{l}
to have eigenvalue 1. We recall that
\xi_{l}
is generally of order 1 and equals 1 exactly in the case of a splitting of one of the partons in a two parton state. Using this in Eq. (
85
) gives us

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}&(\mu^{2};\nu)\approx-\sum_{l}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big[}{\mathcal{S}_{l}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\big]_{\mathbb{P}}}(1-e^{-\nu\xi_{l}^{\mathrm{op}}y})\;.\end{split}

(111)

IX
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}
for a quark-antiquark state

For the
q\bar{q}
state created initially in electron-positron annihilation, Eq. (
111
) simplifies considerably. First, the index
l
denoting the parton that splits can take only the values
l=1
(for the quark) and
l=2
(for the antiquark). Each choice gives the same result, so we can take
l=1
and multiply by two. Also, the color factors are trivial. In
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[}{\mathcal{S}_{l}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}]_{\mathbb{P}}}
we encounter color operators
\bm{T}_{1}\cdot\bm{T}_{1}
,
\bm{T}_{2}\cdot\bm{T}_{2}
, and
\bm{T}_{1}\cdot\bm{T}_{2}
, where
\bm{T}_{i}\cdot\bm{T}_{j}=\sum_{a}T_{i}^{a}T_{j}^{a}
and
T_{i}^{a}
inserts a color matrix
T^{a}
on parton line
i
. The operators
\bm{T}_{1}\cdot\bm{T}_{1}
and
\bm{T}_{2}\cdot\bm{T}_{2}
simply give an eigenvalue
C_{\mathrm{F}}
times the unit color operator, while
\bm{T}_{1}\cdot\bm{T}_{2}
gives
-C_{\mathrm{F}}
. This gives us a result of the form

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};&\nu)\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{2}}\big)\\
\approx{}&-(1-e^{-\nu y})\,\lambda(y)\big|{\{p,f,c,c^{\prime}\}_{2}}\big)\;.\end{split}

(112)

The eigenvalue
\lambda(y)
is obtained in a straightforward calculation from the
q\to q+\mathrm{g}
splitting functions used in
Deductor

NScolor
. There is an integral over the splitting variables
z
and
\phi
. The
\phi
integral is trivial and gives simply a factor
2\pi
. The integration over the momentum fraction
z
remains,

\begin{split}\lambda(y)={}&2C_{\mathrm{F}}\int_{0}^{1}\!dz\ \Big\{\frac{\alpha_{s}(\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}(1-z)yQ^{2})}{2\pi}\ f_{\mathrm{sing}}(z,y)\\
&\quad+\frac{\alpha_{s}(\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}yQ^{2})}{2\pi}\ f_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,y)\Big\}\;.\end{split}

(113)

The argument of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
contains the standard factor
\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}
, Eq. (
13
), and, in the first term, a factor
(1-z)
, as in Eq. (
12
) with
a_{l}=1
. The functions
f_{\mathrm{sing}}(z,y)
and
f_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,y)
are taken directly from
Deductor
and are quite complicated. However, they are simple in the relevant limits,
y\to 0
with fixed
z
and
y\to 0
with
1-z\propto y
. In these limits, they are

\begin{split}f_{\mathrm{sing}}(z,y)\approx{}&\frac{2}{1-z+y}-2\;,\\
f_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,y)\approx{}&1-z\;.\end{split}

(114)

Note that
f_{\mathrm{sing}}(z,0)+f_{\mathrm{reg}}(z)=(1+z^{2})/(1-z)
is just the DGLAP splitting kernel for
q\to q+\mathrm{g}
. However in
f_{\mathrm{sing}}(z,y)
the singularity at
(1-z)\to 0
is regulated by adding
y
in the denominator.

We have written these results in the form used in
Deductor
. In
f_{\mathrm{sing}}(z,y)
, we could recognize that the second term could have been transferred to
f_{\mathrm{reg}}(z,y)
.

We would now like to compare this to the standard results for the summation of logs of
\tau
in Ref.
thrustsum
. We begin by inserting Eq. (
114
) into Eq. (
113
):

\begin{split}\lambda(y)\approx{}&2C_{\mathrm{F}}\int_{0}^{1}\!dz\ \Big\{\frac{\alpha_{s}(\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}(1-z)yQ^{2})}{2\pi}\ \frac{2}{1-z+y}\\
&\quad-\frac{\alpha_{s}(\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}(1-z)yQ^{2})}{2\pi}\ 2\\
&\quad+\frac{\alpha_{s}(\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}yQ^{2})}{2\pi}\ (1-z)\Big\}\;.\end{split}

(115)

We will want to evaluate this approximately for small
y
in such a way that if we expand the result in powers of
\alpha_{s}(yQ^{2})
we retain all terms proportional to
\alpha_{s}^{n}(yQ^{2})\log^{n}(y)
and
\alpha_{s}^{n}(yQ^{2})\log^{n-1}(y)
. After integrating over
\bar{\mu}^{2}=yQ^{2}
as in Eq. (
48
), this will give contributions
\alpha_{s}^{n}(Q^{2})\log^{n+1}(\nu)
and
\alpha_{s}^{n}(Q^{2})\log^{n}(\nu)
. These are the leading log (LL) and next-to-leading log (NLL) terms. In
\lambda(y)
, we neglect contributions proportional to fewer powers of
\log(y)
or to powers of
y
.

In order to carry out this approximate evaluation, we note first that we can use

\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}(A\mu^{2})=\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}(\mu^{2})-\beta_{0}\log(A)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(\mu^{2})+\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{3})\;,

(116)

where
\beta_{0}=(11C_{\mathrm{A}}-2n_{\mathrm{f}})/(12\pi)
. Then we can omit the
\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}(1-z)
factor in the argument of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
in the second term in Eq. (
115
) and the
\lambda_{\mathrm{R}}
in the third term, since these terms do not have
1/(1-z+y)
singularities that could produce
\log(y)
factors after integration. In the first term, there is a
1/(1-z+y)
singularity. For this term, we need to keep the
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
contribution in Eq. (
116
). After performing the
z
integration in the last two terms, this gives us

\begin{split}\lambda(y)\approx{}&4C_{\mathrm{F}}\int_{0}^{1}\!d(1-z)\ \frac{1}{1-z+y}\\
&\times\frac{\alpha_{s}((1-z)yQ^{2})-\beta_{0}\log(\lambda_{\mathrm{R}})\,\alpha_{s}^{2}((1-z)yQ^{2})}{2\pi}\ \\
&\quad-3C_{\mathrm{F}}\,\frac{\alpha_{s}(yQ^{2})}{2\pi}\ \;.\end{split}

(117)

Now we note that the
y
in the denominator in the first term of Eq. (
117
) places an effective lower cutoff on
(1-z)

XII
Parton shower at next-to-leading log

We have seen that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mu^{2};\nu)
has the proper perturbative structure to allow
\tilde{g}(\nu)
as given by a leading order parton shower using the
\Lambda
-ordered
Deductor
algorithm to exponentiate correctly at the leading log level.

First, the operator
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
, constructed from one power of the shower splitting operator
\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})
has the right structure to reproduce the known QCD result
thrustsum
at LL accuracy and even at NLL accuracy, provided that the argument the running coupling
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
in
\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})
is properly defined. For
\mathcal{I}^{[1]}(\nu)
, we can state this in terms of an expansion in powers of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}(Q^{2}/\nu)
. We consider the integral
\mathcal{I}^{[1]}(\nu)
of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
defined in Eq. (
136
). When the running
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}
in
\mathcal{I}^{[1]}(\nu)
is expanded in powers of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}(Q^{2}/\nu)
, the coefficients of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}(Q^{2}/\nu)\log^{n+1}(\nu)
, that is the LL coefficients, are correct and the coefficients of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}(Q^{2}/\nu)\log^{n}(\nu)
, the NLL coefficients, are also correct.

Second, each of the operators
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[k]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
for
k\geq 2
has the right structure so that in the integral
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
, the coefficient of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}(Q^{2}/\nu)\log^{n+1}(\nu)
, which contributes to the exponent in
\tilde{g}(\nu)
at LL accuracy, vanish. That is, the coefficient
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}_{n}(\nu)
of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}(Q^{2}/\nu)
in
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
contains at most
n
powers of
\log(\nu)
.

This LL exponentiation property arises from two features of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[k]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
. First,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[k]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
is suppressed for
\mu^{2}\gg Q^{2}/\nu
and for
\mu^{2}\ll Q^{2}/\nu
, so that only the integration region
\mu^{2}\sim Q^{2}/\nu
contributes to
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
and no factor of
\log(\nu)
arises from integrating over
\mu^{2}
from
Q^{2}/\nu
to
Q^{2}
. Second,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[k]}(Q^{2}/\nu;\nu)
at order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}(Q^{2}/\nu)
contains at most
n
factors of
\log(\nu)
.

Now, if the coefficients of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}(Q^{2}/\nu)\log^{n}(\nu)
in
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
were to vanish for
k\geq 2
, then
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
would not contribute to
\tilde{g}(\nu)

\tilde{g}(\nu)
would come from the expansion of the running coupling in
\mathcal{I}^{[1]}(\nu)

Deductor
algorithm generates the known QCD result at NLL accuracy.

Remarkably, this is the case: in
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
for
k\geq 2
the coefficients
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}_{n}(\nu)
of

\log(\nu)
for large
\nu

The proof in Appendix
A

Deductor

NSColoriPi

XIV
Numerical behavior of the thrust distribution

We have seen that the operator
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mu^{2};\nu)
directly generates the Laplace transform
\tilde{g}(\nu)
of the thrust distribution according to Eq. (
134
). The first order term
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[1]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
in this operator is obtained from the shower splitting function for a first order
\Lambda
-ordered parton shower. We have further seen that this term generates the known
thrustsum
summation of logarithms of
\tau
at the NLL level as long as the shower splitting function is suitably defined. Furthermore, the higher order terms
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{[k]}(\mu^{2};\nu)
obtained from this first order shower splitting function generate only contributions beyond the NLL level.

According to Eq. (
130
), same result for
\tilde{g}(\nu)
as in Eq. (
134
) is obtained by running the
\Lambda
-ordered shower and measuring the Laplace transform of the thrust distribution. However, we do not need to take the Laplace transform. We can simply run the
\Lambda
-ordered shower and measure the thrust distribution
g(\tau)
, as in Eq. (
37
). Will this give the same result as the NLL analytical result listed in Eqs. (
124
) and (
125
)?

In this section, we try this experiment. It is not useful to set
Q^{2}=M_{\mathrm{Z}}^{2}
, which would be relevant for LEP (Large Electron Positron) experiments because a parton shower needs an infrared cutoff. We can take the cutoffs on allowed shower splittings to be
\Lambda>1\ \mathrm{GeV}
and
k_{\mathrm{T}}>1\ \mathrm{GeV}
, but then there is not much range between
(1\ \mathrm{GeV})^{2}
and the starting scale
Q^{2}
of the shower. The result is that there is not a wide range in
\tau
in which we can examine the dependence of
g(\tau)
on
\log(1/\tau)
free of the effects of the infrared cutoffs. Instead, we retain
(1\ \mathrm{GeV})^{2}
cutoffs but set
Q^{2}=(10\ \mathrm{TeV})^{2}
. We then run the
\Lambda
-ordered
Deductor
shower with the LC+ approximation for color
NScolor
. We turn off the top quark, so that the shower is based on 5-flavor QCD.

We compare
\tau g(\tau)
according to
Deductor
with
\tau g(\tau)
according to the NLL formula, Eqs. (
124
) and (
125
), in Fig.
3
. We see that the
Deductor
curve is a bit higher than the NLL curve around
\tau=0.01
and a bit lower at the smallest values of
\tau
. Generally, the results agree to within about 0.01.

Do these results agree within the expected errors?

•

The
Deductor
shower produces contributions beyond the NLL level. If we look at
\tau=0.01
so that
\log(1/\tau)=4.6
, NNLL terms lack a factor 4.6 compared to NLL terms. A simple calculation shows that the NLL terms contribute approximately
-0.03
to
\tau g(\tau)
at
\tau=0.01
. Thus we might expect that the NNLL terms in
Deductor
would contribute
\pm 0.03/4.6\approx\pm 0.007
to
\tau g(\tau)
. This gives us an error estimate from terms in
Deductor
beyond NLL of
\pm 0.007
.

•

There are typically about 20 parton splittings between the 10 TeV scale at which the shower starts and the 1 GeV scale at which it ends. We cannot be confident that there are not 0.1% errors for each splitting resulting from approximations within the
Deductor
code, so we cannot rule out a 2% systematic error in
g(\tau)
resulting from these approximations. A 2% error on the value
\tau g(\tau)\approx 0.2
at
\tau=0.01
amounts to an error of
\pm 0.004
in
\tau g(\tau)
.

•

The infrared cutoffs have some effect. The most important effect comes from the limit on the transverse momentum in a splitting, which we set to
k_{\mathrm{T}}>1\ \mathrm{GeV}
. To test for sensitivity to this cutoff, we change the cut to
k_{\mathrm{T}}>3\ \mathrm{GeV}
. In the range
0.0005<\tau<0.2
, we find that this change in cutoff produces a change in
\tau g(\tau)
that is generally smaller than 0.003. Thus we estimate an error of
\pm 0.003
in
\tau g(\tau)
due to the influence of the infrared cutoff.

•

The
Deductor
splitting kernel omits the
\beta_{1}
term in Eq. (
119
) for evaluating the dependence of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}((1-z)yQ^{2})
on
(1-z)
. This changes the
Deductor
result at the NLL level. We examine this effect below.

•

The LC+ approximation used by default in
Deductor
is not the same as exact color. This introduces spurious terms of order
1/N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
times logarithms of
1/\tau
into the LC+
Deductor
result, where
N_{\mathrm{c}}=3
is the number of colors. We examine this effect below.

We examine the effects of missing NLL terms and of color in Fig.
4
. Here the NLL curve is copied from Fig.
3
and the
Deductor
curve from Fig.
3
is displayed as a dashed (black) line. The remaining two curves are modified versions of the curves in Fig.
3
.

We first address the fact that
Deductor
omits the
\beta_{1}
term for evaluating the dependence of
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}((1-z)yQ^{2})
on
(1-z)
. This means that in the summation of logarithms of
\log(1/\tau)
,
Deductor
is missing the term
-(\beta_{1}/\beta_{0})\log((1-\lambda^{2})/(1-2\lambda))
in
f_{2}(\lambda)
in Eq. (
128
). In order to see the effect of this term, we calculate the ratio

r(\tau)=\frac{g_{\mathrm{NLL}}(\tau)}{g_{\mathrm{NLL}}^{\mathrm{mod}}(\tau)}\;,

(153)

where
g^{\mathrm{mod}}_{\mathrm{NLL}}(\tau)
is obtained by omitting the term
-(\beta_{1}/\beta_{0})\log((1-\lambda^{2})/(1-2\lambda))
in the calculation of
g(\tau)
. Then we correct the
Deductor
result for
g(\tau)
by multiplying it by
r(\tau)
. We plot the corrected
Deductor
curve in Fig.
4
. We see that the corrected
Deductor
curve is quite close to the uncorrected curve. However the difference is visible in Fig.
4
and acts in the direction of reducing the discrepancy between the analytical summation of logarithms and the numerical
Deductor
result.
7
7

7

In a future version of
Deductor
, we may add this contribution to the splitting kernel, although its practical effect is quite small.

We next address the fact that in Fig.
3
we used the default color approximation in
Deductor
, the LC+ approximation
NScolor
. This approximation is an improvement over the leading color approximation, but it is far from being exact. In the LC+ approximation, we replace the exact first order splitting function
\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})=\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\mu^{2})+\mathcal{S}^{[0,1]}(\mu^{2})
by an approximate version
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{LC+}}(\mu^{2})=\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}_{\mathrm{LC+}}(\mu^{2})+\mathcal{S}^{[0,1]}_{\mathrm{LC+}}(\mu^{2})
.
Deductor
has the option of expanding in powers of
\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{LC+}}(\mu^{2})
and keeping terms up to and including
[\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{LC+}}(\mu^{2})]^{n}
, where
n
can be chosen by the user
NSColorTheory
. In order to assess what difference a more exact treatment of color could make, we plot in Fig.
3
the result of calculating the thrust distribution at 10 TeV with
n=2
. We have corrected this result using the factor
r(\tau)
from Eq. (
153
). Of course, using
n=2
slows the calculation down, increasing the statistical errors. Within the statistical errors, we find that improving the color treatment makes no difference.

In summary, we have made a numerical comparison of the expected NLL result for the thrust distribution and a direct calculation using a
\Lambda
-ordered parton shower with a global momentum mapping, setting
Q^{2}
to
(10\ \mathrm{TeV})^{2}
so as to allow
\log(1/\tau)
to be adequately large to provide a real test. We have found good agreement within the estimated errors.

The default ordering variable in

\Lambda

8
8

8

For

where the vector
k_{\perp}
is orthogonal to the momentum
p_{l}
of the emitting parton and to
Q

p_{l}
and the momentum
p_{k}
of the dipole partner parton.
We can define
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}(\nu)
with
k_{\mathrm{T}}

78
) and (
151
). We simply set the scale parameters to

With

\Lambda
-ordered shower from Sec.
XI
and Appendix
A
is lost. However, it appears that we can still get cancellation of

factors in

at large
\log(\nu)

and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[}{\cdots}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}]_{1-\mathbb{P}}}
operation in the last line of Eq. (
78
), representing real emissions and virtual emissions, cancel each other. A complete proof is beyond the scope of this paper, but we present an argument that makes this conclusion plausible in Appendix
B
.

XVI
Effect of the momentum mapping for
\Lambda
ordering

Recall from Sec.
VIII
that in a splitting
p_{l}\to\hat{p}_{l}+\hat{p}_{m\!+\!1}
, we always have
p_{l}\neq\hat{p}_{l}+\hat{p}_{m\!+\!1}
. In order to conserve momentum, we need to map the momenta
p_{i}
into new momenta
\hat{p}_{i}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}\hat{p}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\;.

(157)

In the
Deductor
algorithm, this is accomplished by using a Lorentz transformation
NSI

\hat{p}_{i}^{\mu}=\Lambda^{\mu}_{\nu}\,p_{i}^{\nu}\;,\hskip 28.45274pti\notin\{l,{m\!+\!1}\}\;.

(158)

The Lorentz transformation is defined to be a boost in the plane of
p_{l}
and
Q
. We have found in Sec.
VIII
that the boost angle
\omega
is small, of order
y
, and that the effect of this small Lorentz transformation on the thrust is small compared to the order
y
effect produced by the splitting itself.

For any parton shower, one will need a momentum mapping that preserves the total momentum. The
global
mapping produced by a Lorentz transformation is not the only possibility. A more widely used
local
choice is provided by the Catani-Seymour dipole splitting formalism
CataniSeymour
or the local mapping in
Pythia

SjostrandSkands
. For the Catani-Seymour choice, we start with the parton
l
that splits and its dipole partner

XVIII
Conclusions

We have explored how to gain direct access to how a parton shower sums large logarithms, following the general program outlined in Ref.
NSlogsum
. In this paper, we have limited ourselves to electron-positron annihilation and to just one observable, the thrust distribution. We have, however, looked at results for more than one shower algorithm.

The method that we propose works with the appropriate integral transform of the distribution of interest. In this case, we need the Laplace transform
\tilde{g}(\nu)
, Eq. (
29
), of the thrust distribution. We seek to find how
\tilde{g}(\nu)
behaves for large
\nu
.

We rearrange the cross section calculation so as to write
\tilde{g}(\nu)
in the form from Eq. (
134
),

\begin{split}\tilde{g}(\nu)={}&\exp\!\Big(\big({1}\big|\mathcal{I}(\nu)\big|{\{p,f,c,c\}_{2}}\big)\Big)\;.\end{split}

(164)

Here
|{\{p,f,c,c\}_{2}})
is a color and flavor singlet
q\bar{q}
basis state with
p_{1}+p_{2}=Q
and the operator
\mathcal{I}(\nu)
is an integral,

\mathcal{I}(\nu)=\int_{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}^{Q^{2}}\!\frac{d\bar{\mu}^{2}}{\bar{\mu}^{2}}\,\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bar{\mu}^{2};\nu)\;.

(165)

We expand
\mathcal{I}(\nu)
in powers of the shower evolution operator
\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})
. Then the coefficients
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
, proportional to
k
powers of
\mathcal{S}(\mu^{2})
, can be further expanded as

\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\left[\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}(Q^{2}/\nu)}{2\pi}\right]^{n}\mathcal{I}^{[k]}_{n}(\nu)\;,

(166)

in which the strong coupling is evaluated at a fixed scale
Q^{2}/\nu
. Thus the shower result is quite directly expressed in exponentiated form in terms of an operator
\mathcal{I}(\nu)
with a known perturbative expansion.

For the
Deductor
shower algorithm with either
\Lambda
or
k_{\mathrm{T}}
ordering,
\mathcal{I}^{[1]}(\nu)
provides the standard NLL summation of
\log(\nu)
factors.

We emphasize in this paper writing the appropriate integral transform of the distribution of interest, such as the thrust distribution, as an exponential and examining the exponent
\mathcal{I}(\nu)
. However, it is also possible to simply look directly at the distribution of interest as it is generated by a given parton shower. For this, one needs to simulate collisions at large values of
Q^{2}
. We have not pushed this method to nearly as large a value of
Q^{2}
as in Ref.

There are several avenues available for future research that extends the results of this paper.

First, the method of this paper applies to several observables in electron-positron annihilation. We have tried variations on the shower algorithm examined, but have looked at only one observable, the thrust distribution. It is certainly worthwhile to see what patterns emerge if we look at other observables.

Second, the method developed in Ref.
NSlogsum
applies to observables in hadron-hadron collisions as well as in electron-positron collisions. It is of interest to see how this method works in practice for some hadron-hadron observables, starting with the
k_{\mathrm{T}}
distribution in the Drell-Yan process.

Third, we construct numerical implementations of
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{2}(\nu)
and
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{3}(\nu)
for the particular observable examined and for several shower algorithms. This allows one to test numerically if the large
\nu
behaviors of
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{2}(\nu)
and
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{3}(\nu)
are consistent with NLL summation. When we find for a certain shower algorithm that NLL summation fails at the level of
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{2}(\nu)
or
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{3}(\nu)
, then NLL summation fails for that shower algorithm and observable. However, if NLL summation is not spoiled by
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{2}(\nu)
or
\mathcal{I}^{[2]}_{3}(\nu)
, it could still fail in
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}_{n}(\nu)
for some larger values of
k
and
n
. Thus it would be valuable to have numerical implementations of
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}_{n}(\nu)
for some larger values of
k
and
n
. Then one would have more stringent numerical tests of NLL summation for a given shower algorithm and a given observable.

Fourth, it would be helpful to have analytical insight into the behavior of the operators
\mathcal{I}^{[k]}(\nu)
for
k\geq 3
in cases that are similar to the thrust distribution using a
k_{\mathrm{T}}
-ordered shower.

We close with the observation that it is expecting a lot to expect that a first order shower algorithm will sum logarithms at the LL or NLL level. If we had a parton shower based on splitting functions at order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{N}

NSAllOrder
;
NSlogsum
, then we could expect to correctly produce contributions to
\mathcal{I}(\nu)
of order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\log^{j}(\nu)
with
n\leq N
,
j\leq n+1
. We might not correctly produce contributions of order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\log^{j}(\nu)
with
n>N
,
j\leq n+1
because we lack the order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}
contributions to the shower splitting functions. However, contributions of order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\log^{j}(\nu)
with

should vanish because these contributions can never be provided by
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}
contributions to the shower splitting functions. Currently, all that we have (in several variations) is a first order shower,
N=1
. Thus we can expect to correctly produce contributions of order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{1}\log^{2}(\nu)
and
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{1}\log^{1}(\nu)
. We can also expect to obtain exponentiation of logarithms of
\nu
: contributions of order
\alpha_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\log^{j}(\nu)
with
j>n+1
should vanish. With care, we can hope to have LL or NLL summation of
\log(\nu)
factors, but this relies on incorporating the most important parts of higher order splitting operators into the first order operator
\mathcal{S}
.

(235)

With these approximations, we have

\begin{split}\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)\approx{}&\nu e^{-\eta}\frac{k_{\mathrm{T}}}{|Q|}\;.\end{split}

(236)

With the same approximations, we obtain for the change in thrust produced by an emission from parton 2 with the dipole partner being parton 3,

\begin{split}\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)\approx{}&\nu e^{\eta}\frac{k_{\mathrm{T}}}{|Q|}\;.\end{split}

(237)

Again, this is for
|\eta|\gg 0
. For the soft emission region near
\eta=0
, there is the possibility that
p_{4}
is in the opposite thrust hemisphere from the parton that emitted it, so that the thrust calculation changes.

These relations are significant because the second emission is accompanied by a measurement function
1-\exp(-\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau))
. The measurement function is approximately 1 for
1\ll\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)
but approximately zero for
\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)\ll 1
. Thus we effectively integrate over the range

\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)>1\;.

(238)

The boundary of this integration region is indicated in Fig.
14
as straight lines with the labels
\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)=1
.

There is one more restriction on the integration range for the second splitting. We are analyzing a
k_{\mathrm{T}}
ordered shower, so

k_{\mathrm{T}}<\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}\;.

(239)

The line
k_{\mathrm{T}}=\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}
is indicated in Fig.
14

To analyze Eq. (

k_{\mathrm{T},\star}

at the point labelled with a star in Fig.

in Eq. (

e^{\eta}\,\frac{k_{\mathrm{T}}}{|Q|}\approx e^{\tilde{\eta}}\,\frac{\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}}{|Q|}\;,\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{collinear}\;.

(240)

Then using Eq. (

\tilde{\eta}

\frac{k_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}}{Q^{2}}\approx\frac{\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)}{\nu\tau}\,\frac{\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}^{2}}{Q^{2}}\;,\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{collinear}\;.

(241)

The point labelled with a star in Fig.
14

(242)

Since in the dominant integration region
\nu\tau<1
, we conclude that
k_{\mathrm{T},\star}>\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}

k_{\mathrm{T}}=\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[}{\mathcal{S}^{[1,0]}(\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}},\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi})\,e^{-\nu\tau}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}]_{\mathbb{P}}}

(243)

Our analysis above has assumed that the first emission is at large rapidity,
\tilde{\eta}\gg 0
. What happens when
\tilde{\eta}\approx 0
? The approximations that we have used are not adequate in this situation, so it might seem that there is nothing that we can say. However, we can examine what happens when
\tilde{\eta}
is large enough that the approximations are still valid, but
\tilde{\eta}
becomes smaller and smaller. Start with Eq. (
240
) for the collinear line in Fig.
14
and use Eq. (
221
) to eliminate
\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}
and Eq. (
236
) to eliminate
k_{\mathrm{T}}
, giving

e^{2\eta}\approx\frac{\nu\tau}{\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)}\,e^{2\tilde{\eta}}\;,\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{collinear}\;.

The point labelled with a star in Fig.
14
is the intersection of the collinear line and the line
\nu(\hat{\tau}-\tau)=1

e^{2\eta_{\star}}\approx\nu\tau\,e^{2\tilde{\eta}}\;.

(245)

In the effective integration range for the first splitting, we have
\nu\tau<1
. Thus

\eta_{\star}<\tilde{\eta}\;.

(246)

This tells us that when the rapidity of the first splitting becomes small,
\tilde{\eta}\to 0

\eta_{\star}\to 0

14

The cancellation will fail on a certain surface in the integration region. On this surface, the splitting variables for the second emission are given by

(k_{\mathrm{T}},\eta)\approx(k_{\mathrm{T},\star},\eta_{\star})\;.

(247)

In this region, the second emission is collinear rather than both soft and collinear, so that the emission probability does not match the constant that appears in the region in which the second emission is both soft and collinear. However in the virtual subtraction the second emission is both soft and collinear so that the emission probability is this constant. Thus the emission probabilities do not match between the real emission and the subtraction.

The surface of non-matching probabilities is specified as follows. If
k_{\mathrm{T}}=k_{\mathrm{T},\star}
, then the line
k_{\mathrm{T}}=\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}
in Fig.
14

(k_{\mathrm{T},\star},\eta_{\star})

\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}=k_{\mathrm{T},\star}

\tau
for the fist emission is given by

\tilde{\eta}\approx\log(\nu)+\log\!\left(\frac{\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}}{Q}\right)\;.

(248)

The transverse momentum for the first emission varies in the range

-\log(\nu)\ll\log\!\left(\frac{\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}}{Q}\right)\ll-\frac{1}{2}\log(\nu)\;.

(249)

Here the lower limit is from Eq. (

(k_{\mathrm{T}},\eta)\approx(k_{\mathrm{T},\star},\eta_{\star})

\begin{split}\eta\approx{}&\tilde{\eta}\;,\\
\log\!\left(\frac{k_{\mathrm{T}}}{Q}\right)\approx{}&\log\!\left(\frac{\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{T}}}{Q}\right)\;.\end{split}

(250)

Thus the integration region inside which cancellation fails is one dimensional, so we are left with a contribution to
