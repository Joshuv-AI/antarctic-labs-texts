Second-order adiabatic expansions of heat and charge currents with nonequilibrium Green’s functions.
Sebastián E. Deghi, 1 and Raúl A. Bustos-Marún 1,2

Abstract
Due to technological needs, nanoscale heat management, energy conversion and quantum thermodynamics have become key areas of research, putting heat pumps and nanomotors center stage. The treatment of these particular systems often requires the use of adiabatic expansions in terms of the frequency of the external driving or the velocity of some classical degree of freedom. However, due to the difficulty of getting the expressions, most works have only explored first-order terms. Despite this, adiabatic expansions have allowed the study of intriguing phenomena such as adiabatic quantum pumps and motors, or electronic friction. Here, we use nonequilibrium Green’s functions, within a Schwinger-Keldysh approach, to develop second-order expressions for the energy, heat, and charge currents. We illustrate, through two simple models, how the obtained formulas produce physically consistent results, and allow for the thermodynamic study of unexplored phenomena, such as second-order monoparametric pumping.

II
General theory

II.1
Generic Hamiltonian

To define the different parts of the system and their associated creation
(annihilation) operators, we introduce the total Hamiltonian
H

that describes the
total system
. It consists of a core region

S
connected to several macroscopic reservoirs
\alpha
(the leads).
The core region, which typically has nanometric dimensions, will be
referred to as the
local system
. The total Hamiltonian takes
the form

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\underset{\alpha}{\sum}H_{\alpha}+H_{S}+\underset{\alpha}{\sum}V_{\alpha},

where
H_{S}
, in general, corresponds to a time-dependent local
system,
H_{\alpha}
describes the time-independent
\alpha
-lead,
and the term
V_{\alpha}
contains the time-independent interaction
between the local system and the
\alpha
-lead. The Hamiltonian of
the local system depends parametrically on time through a multidimensional
vector
\overrightarrow{X}
, which describes the mechanical degrees
of freedom. The local system’s Hamiltonian assumes the following form
when the second quantization is applied

\displaystyle H_{S}

\displaystyle=\underset{l,s}{\sum}h_{l,s}\left(\overrightarrow{X}\right)d_{l}^{\dagger}d_{s}.

(1)

In the notation we have used, the operators
d_{l}^{\dagger}
and

d_{l}
create or annihilate, respectively, an electron within the
local system. The Hamiltonians of the leads can be written as

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\underset{k}{\sum}\epsilon_{\alpha k}c_{\alpha k}^{\dagger}c_{\alpha k},

(2)

where
\epsilon_{\alpha k}
is the energy of the
\alpha
-lead in
the state
k
,
c_{\alpha k}^{\dagger}
creates an electron in the

\alpha
-lead with the state
k
, whereas the
c_{\alpha k}
annihilates
it. For simplicity, the index
k
also includes the electron’s spin.
Finally, the tunneling interaction describes the coupling between
the local system and the
\alpha
-leads

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\underset{k,l}{\sum}\left(t_{\alpha k,l}c_{\alpha k}^{\dagger}d_{l}+t_{\alpha k,l}^{*}d_{l}^{\dagger}c_{\alpha k}\right),

where
t_{\alpha k,l}
are the tunneling amplitudes between the leads
and the local system.

II.2
Dynamics of non-equilibrium open quantum systems

Within the context of the Schwinger-Keldysh approach for the NEGF
formalism
[

,
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
]

we define the elements of the retarded and advanced Green’s
functions,
\mathcal{G}^{R}
and
\mathcal{G}^{A}

respectively. For a system that evolves, not necessarily in an equilibrium
process, from
t^{\prime}
to
t
times, they
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{l,s}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)\left\langle\left\{d_{s}\left(t\right),d_{l}^{\dagger}\left(t^{\prime}\right)\right\}\right\rangle,

(3)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{s,l}^{A}\left(t^{\prime},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{G}_{l,s}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)\right]^{*},

(4)

where
\left\{\bullet,\bullet\right\}
is the anticommutator and

\left\langle\bullet\right\rangle
is the quantum expectation value.

For the states of the local system, the lesser Green’s
function elements are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{l,s}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left\langle d_{l}^{\dagger}\left(t^{\prime}\right)d_{s}\left(t\right)\right\rangle,

(5)

whereas for the states that propagate between the local system and
leads, the lesser Green’s function elements are

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\alpha k,l}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left\langle c_{\alpha k}^{\dagger}\left(t^{\prime}\right)d_{l}\left(t\right)\right\rangle.

(6)

Furthermore, the retarded and advanced self-energies are assumed to
be stationary.
2
2

2

For our purposes, stationary implies that the self-energies only depend
on the time difference
t-t^{\prime}
, which means that both
V_{\alpha}
and
H_{\alpha}
are independent of the mechanical
degrees of freedom.
The elements of the retarded self-energy for the
\alpha
-lead take
the form

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha,l,s}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{k}{\sum}t_{l,\alpha k}g_{\alpha k}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)t_{\alpha k,s},

(7)

where

\displaystyle g_{\alpha k}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\epsilon_{\alpha k}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)}.

(8)

Hence, the elements of total retarded self-energy comprise the sum
of all leads

\displaystyle\Sigma_{l,s}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{\alpha}{\sum}\Sigma_{\alpha,l,s}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right).

(9)

To obtain the advanced self-energy, we calculate the adjoint of the
retarded one

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha,s,l}^{A}\left(t^{\prime},t\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\Sigma_{\alpha,l,s}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)\right]^{*}.

(10)

Likewise, the elements of the lesser self-energy of the
\alpha
-lead
are given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha,l,s}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{k}{\sum}t_{l,\alpha k}g_{\alpha k}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)t_{\alpha k,s},

(11)

where

\displaystyle g_{\alpha k}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi i}{\hbar}f_{\alpha}\left(\epsilon_{\alpha k}\right)e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\epsilon_{\alpha k}\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)}.

(12)

Here,
f_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha
-lead’s Fermi-Dirac distribution.
To conclude, the elements of the total lesser self-energy are

\displaystyle\Sigma_{l,s}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{\alpha}{\sum}\Sigma_{\alpha,l,s}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right).

Given the essential operators, the next step is to delve into the
quantum dynamics, which in Schwinger-Keldysh formalism is expressed
by the integrodifferential Dyson equation in terms of the retarded
Green’s function

\displaystyle-i\hbar\partial_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)+\mathcal{G}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)H_{S}\left(t^{\prime}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle+\int\mathcal{G}^{R}\left(t,t_{1}\right)\Sigma^{R}\left(t_{1},t^{\prime}\right)dt_{1}.

Here, the Hamiltonian of the local system, retarded Green’s
function and self-energy are given by Eqs. (
1
),
(
3
) and (
9
),
respectively.

At this point, we can apply many techniques to solve Eq. (
13
).
One way is to use numerical methods
[
45
]
. Another one
is to develop an adiabatic expansion of the observables. To carry
out the latter technique, we must decompose the defined operators
and Dyson’s equation into two-time scales, which is
done through the use of a Wigner transform.

II.3
Adiabatic expansion for retarded Green’s function

The idea is to separate the fast microscopic dynamics, followed by
the electrons, from the slow macroscopic changes, determined by the
classical mechanical degrees of freedom. Once the time scales have
been distinguished, a gradient expansion will be carried out on the
slow variables. First of all, we have to define the following transform
of time variables

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{t+t^{\prime}}{2},

(14)

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=t-t^{\prime}.

(15)

The time scale
T
is called the slow variable, while
\tau
is
the fast variable. The Wigner transform for the retarded Green’s
function is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{G}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon\tau}d\tau,

whereas its inverse transformation is

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon\tau}d\varepsilon.

For the elements of the retarded self-energy described in Eq. (
7
),

\Sigma^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)\equiv\Sigma^{R}\left(\tau\right)
, the
Wigner transform becomes a Fourier transform, where

\displaystyle\Sigma^{R}\left(\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\Sigma^{R}\left(\tau\right)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon\tau}d\tau,

and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha,l,s}^{R}\left(\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\alpha,l,s}\left(\varepsilon\right)-i\Gamma_{\alpha,l,s}\left(\varepsilon\right).

(16)

Here, the level-width functions
\Gamma_{\alpha,l,s}
are given by
3
3

3

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha,l,s}\left(\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\underset{k}{\sum}\delta\left(\varepsilon-\epsilon_{\alpha k}\right)t_{l,\alpha k}t_{\alpha k,s}.

The level-shift functions
\Delta_{\alpha,l,s}
can be calculated

.
After applying the adjoint operator to Eq. (

we get the advanced self-energy

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha,l,s}^{<}\left(\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=2if_{\alpha}\left(\varepsilon\right)\Gamma_{\alpha,l,s}\left(\varepsilon\right).

Given the operators in the energy domain, we must apply the Moyal
product to Eq. (
13

yielding (see Appendix

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}i\hbar\partial_{T}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)+\varepsilon\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)e^{\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left(\overleftarrow{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{T}-\overleftarrow{\partial}_{T}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\right)}\Sigma^{R}\left(\varepsilon\right)

(18)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)e^{\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left(\overleftarrow{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{T}-\overleftarrow{\partial}_{T}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\right)}\hat{H}_{S}\left(T\right),

The above expression implies that the unknown retarded green’s function

\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}
can be expressed as an infinite sum
over its derivatives, which are also unknown in principle. To solve
this, we have to implement an iterative approach (see Appendix
B
)
which starts at zero-order by approximating
\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}

by the adiabatic Green’s function
G^{R}
, defined as

\displaystyle G^{R}

\displaystyle=\left[\varepsilon I-H_{S}\left(T\right)-\Sigma^{R}\left(\varepsilon\right)\right]^{-1}.

(19)

The above adiabatic (or frozen) Green’s function
G^{R}
is formally
equal to
\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)

in the limit of infinitely slow mechanical degrees of freedom, which
constitute a form of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.

By the chain rule, the slow-time
T
derivatives of the Hamiltonian
of the local system, up to the second order, are

\displaystyle\partial_{T}H_{S}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\varLambda_{\nu}\dot{X}_{\nu},

(20)

\displaystyle\partial_{T}^{2}H_{S}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\varLambda_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}_{\mu}\dot{X}_{\nu}+\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\varLambda_{\nu}\ddot{X}_{\nu}.

(21)

Here,
\dot{X}_{\nu}
and
\ddot{X}_{\nu}
are the time derivatives
of the mechanical degree of freedom
\nu
,
\partial_{T}X_{\nu}

and
\partial_{T}^{2}X_{\nu}
respectively. Additionally, the elements
of the matrices
\varLambda_{\nu}
and
\varLambda_{\mu\nu}
are
given by

\displaystyle\left[\varLambda_{\nu}\right]_{l,s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial h_{l,s}}{\partial X_{\nu}},

\displaystyle\left[\varLambda_{\mu\nu}\right]_{l,s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}h_{l,s}}{\partial X_{\mu}\partial X_{\nu}}.

Then, applying the iterative method twice to second-order terms, the
resulting retarded Green’s function takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}

\displaystyle\simeq G^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\Omega_{1,\nu}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)\dot{X}_{\nu}

(22)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}\right)^{2}\left\{\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\Omega_{11,\mu\nu}^{R}\dot{X}_{\mu}\dot{X}_{\nu}+\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\Omega_{2,\nu}^{R}\ddot{X}_{\nu}\right\}.

To make the notation more compact, we have defined the following operators

\displaystyle\Omega_{1,\nu}^{R}

\displaystyle=\Xi_{1}\left[G^{R},\varLambda_{\nu},G^{R}\right],

\displaystyle\Omega_{11,\mu\nu}^{R}

\displaystyle=\Xi_{11}\left[G^{R},K_{\mu\nu}^{R},G^{R}\right],

\displaystyle\Omega_{2,\nu}^{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Xi_{2}\left[G^{R},\varLambda_{\nu},G^{R}\right].

where we have also introduced the definitions

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}^{R}

\displaystyle=\varLambda_{\mu}G^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)\varLambda_{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\varLambda_{\mu\nu},

and

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}\left[A,B,C\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}A\right)BC-AB\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}C\right),

\displaystyle\Xi_{2}\left[A,B,C\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{2}A\right)BC-2\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}A\right)B\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}C\right)

\displaystyle+AB\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{2}C\right),

\displaystyle\Xi_{11}\left[A,B,C\right]

\displaystyle=\Xi_{2}\left[A,B,C\right]-A\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}^{2}B\right)C.

Take into account that the order to which the Planck’s constant is
elevated is consistent with the order of the adiabatic expansion.
In this sense, it can be used as a "book-keeping"
parameter to keep track of the expansion order for a given term.

Finally, to calculate the advanced Green function, we use the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{A}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)\right]^{\dagger}.

\displaystyle e^{\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left(\overleftarrow{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{T}-\overleftarrow{\partial}_{T}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{\varepsilon}\right)}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{A}\left(T,\varepsilon\right).

\Sigma^{<}
are given in Eq. (

To solve this, we first insert in Eq. (

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{<}

\displaystyle\simeq G^{<}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\Omega_{1,\nu}^{<}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)\dot{X}_{\nu}

(24)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}\right)^{2}\left\{\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\Omega_{11,\mu\nu}^{<}\dot{X}_{\mu}\dot{X}_{\nu}+\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\Omega_{2,\nu}^{<}\ddot{X}_{\nu}\right\},

Where the adiabatic lesser Green function is

\displaystyle G^{<}

\displaystyle=G^{R}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)\Sigma^{<}\left(\varepsilon\right)G^{A}\left(T,\varepsilon\right),

\displaystyle=\Xi_{11}\left[G^{R},K_{\mu\nu}^{R},G^{<}\right]+\Xi_{11}\left[G^{<},K_{\mu\nu}^{A},G^{A}\right]

\displaystyle+\Xi_{11}\left[G^{R},\varLambda_{\mu}G^{<}\varLambda_{\nu},G^{A}\right],

\displaystyle\Omega_{2,\nu}^{<}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\Xi_{2}\left[G^{<},\varLambda_{\nu},G^{A}\right]+\Xi_{2}\left[G^{R},\varLambda_{\nu},G^{<}\right]\right\}.

In the above expressions we have introduced the following term to

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}^{A}

\displaystyle=\varLambda_{\mu}G^{A}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)\varLambda_{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\varLambda_{\mu\nu}.

III
Observables

We have already developed explicit formulas for evaluating up-to-second-order
corrections to the adiabatic Green’s functions. In the following section,
we will apply these results to obtain close expressions for the second-order
adiabatic corrections of three observables of interest within quantum
transport: charge, energy, and heat currents.

III.1
Charge current

For an
\alpha
-lead, the average charge current through it can be
written as the mean value of the time derivative of the number operator

N_{\alpha}

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-e\left\langle\dot{N}_{\alpha}\left(t\right)\right\rangle,

(25)

where
N_{\alpha}=\underset{k}{\sum}c_{\alpha k}^{\dagger}c_{\alpha k},
and

e
is the modulus of the electron charge. Then, applying Ehrenfest’s
theorem, the commutation relations for fermions, and using Eqs. (
5
)
and (
6
)
in Eq. (
25
), we get a formula
for the charge current in terms of the lesser Green’s function

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2e\textrm{Re}\left\{\underset{k,l}{\sum}t_{\alpha k,l}\mathcal{G}_{l,\alpha k}^{<}\left(t,t\right)\right\}.

(26)

To evaluate the time-dependent current
I_{\alpha}
, we must proceed
in accord with the Langreth’s rules
[
47
,
41
]
,
yielding

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2e\int\textrm{Re}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}\left(t^{\prime},t\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\mathcal{G}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\left(t^{\prime},t\right)\right]\right\}dt^{\prime}.

(27)

The next step is to insert the Wigner transform of the Green’s functions
into Eq. (
27
)
and make a gradient expansion, the resulting expression reads (see
Appendix
D
)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{2e}{h}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{N}}{N!}\int\textrm{Re}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left\{C_{\alpha,N}^{I}\right\}\right\}d\varepsilon,

(28)

where

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,N}^{I}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}\right)^{N}\left(\partial_{T}^{N}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}+\partial_{T}^{N}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{<}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\right).

It is important to highlight that, although this expression is exact,
its explicit evaluation necessarily requires approximations. For this
purpose, we will use the up-to-second-order adiabatic expansion
of the Green’s functions found previously. After some algebra, one
can find the up-to-second-order adiabatic expansion of the charge
current, giving

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\simeq I_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}+I_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}+I_{\alpha}^{\left(1,1\right)}+I_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}.

(29)

The first two terms of the above expression are well known. The former,

I_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}
, is equivalent to Landauer’s formula,
while the second one,
I_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}
, is the quantum
pumping contribution to the charge current
[
29
,
16
]

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=-e\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)},

(30)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=-e\underset{\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}\dot{X}_{\nu}.

(31)

Here, we have introduced the terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon.

We acquire the preceding definition for practical purposes, which
will be useful later, being the kernel’s integrals
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{h}\textrm{Re}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left(\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\textrm{Im}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}G^{R}\varLambda_{\nu}\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}+\partial_{\varepsilon}G^{<}\varLambda_{\nu}\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}\right)\right\},

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=G^{r}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}\left(\varepsilon\right)+G^{<}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\left(\varepsilon\right),

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=G^{A}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\left(\varepsilon\right).

The remaining terms in Eq. (
29
)
constitute the second-order adiabatic terms of the charge current.
These formulas can be written as

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=-e\underset{\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}\ddot{X}_{\nu},

(32)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=-e\underset{\mu,\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}\dot{X}_{\mu}\dot{X}_{\nu}.

(33)

The current derivatives
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}=-\frac{1}{e}\frac{\partial I_{\alpha}}{\partial\ddot{X}_{\nu}}

and
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}=-\frac{1}{e}\frac{\partial^{2}I_{\alpha}}{\partial\dot{X}_{\mu}\partial\dot{X}_{\nu}}

are

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

The above kernels of integrals are

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi}\textrm{Re}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left\{\partial_{\varepsilon}^{2}G^{R}\varLambda_{\nu}\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}+\partial_{\varepsilon}^{2}G^{<}\varLambda_{\nu}\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}\right\}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{\pi}\textrm{Re}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left\{\Phi_{\mu\nu}^{\left(2\right)}\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}+\Phi_{\mu\nu}^{\left(3\right)}\Phi_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}\right\}\right\},

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu\nu}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}G^{R}K_{\mu\nu}^{R}\right),

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu\nu}^{\left(3\right)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\partial_{\varepsilon}G^{R}\varLambda_{\mu}G^{<}\varLambda_{\nu}+\partial_{\varepsilon}G^{<}K_{\mu\nu}^{A}\right).

III.2
Energy current

Analogous to the charge current, the energy current flowing in the

\alpha
-lead
\dot{E}_{\alpha}
is defined as the mean value of
the time derivative of the Hamiltonian of the
\alpha
-lead
H_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\dot{H}_{\alpha}\left(t\right)\right\rangle.

(34)

As we proceeded in the previous section for the charge current, we
will apply Ehrenfest’s theorem, the commutation relations for fermions,
and Eqs. (
5
)
and (
6
)
into Eq. (
34
). This gives

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-2\textrm{Re}\left\{\underset{k,l}{\sum}\epsilon_{\alpha k}t_{\alpha k,l}\mathcal{G}_{l,\alpha k}^{<}\left(t,t\right)\right\}.

(35)

Note that the essential difference between Eqs. (
26
)
and (
35
)
are the energy weights. This suggests employing a method akin to the
charge currents strategy. However, despite using the same assumption,
there are some subtle deviations from the reasoning for load currents,
as illustrated in Appendix
E
.
The final formula is

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-2\textrm{Re}\left\{i\hbar\int\textrm{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}^{R}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}\left(t^{\prime},t\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\mathcal{G}^{<}\left(t,t^{\prime}\right)\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\left(t^{\prime},t\right)\right]\right\}dt^{\prime}.

(36)

Unlike Eq. (
27
),
the energy current formula shown above contains time derivatives over
self-energies. Up to this point, the expression for energy current
seems to be similar to the charge current. By applying the Wigner
transform and the gradient expansion to Eq. (
36
)
we arrive at (see Appendix
F
)

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{h}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{N}}{N!}\int\varepsilon\textrm{Re}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left\{C_{\alpha,N}^{I}\right\}\right\}d\varepsilon

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{h}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{N}}{N!}\int\textrm{Re}\left\{\textrm{tr}\left\{C_{\alpha,N}^{E}\right\}\right\}d\varepsilon.

(37)

The additional term
C_{\alpha,N}^{E}
is given by
4
4

4

Each of the
C_{\alpha,N}^{I}
functions are dimensionless, whereas
the
C_{\alpha,N}^{E}
functions have energy units.

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,N}^{E}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2}\right)^{N+1}\left(\partial_{T}^{N+1}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}+\partial_{T}^{N+1}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{<}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\right).

After introducing the second-order adiabatic expansion of the retarded
and lesser Green’s functions into Eq. (
37
),
we get the adiabatic expansion up to the second-order of the energy current

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\simeq\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}+\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}+\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(1,1\right)}+\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}.

(38)

The adiabatic energy current is

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}=\int\varepsilon\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

(39)

and its first correction
\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}
takes
the form

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\underset{\nu}{\sum}\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}\dot{X}_{\nu},

(40)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\varepsilon\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon.

These terms are equivalent to those found previously by other authors,
see for example
49
. However, the last two terms
of Eq. (
38
)
represent original expressions, to our knowledge. They read as

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\underset{\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}\ddot{X}_{\nu},

(41)

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\alpha}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\underset{\mu,\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}\dot{X}_{\mu}\dot{X}_{\nu},

(42)

where the energy fluxes take the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\varepsilon\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\varepsilon\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon.

Note that, by comparing the adiabatic expansion of the charge current
Eq. (
29
)
with the energy one (Eq.
38
),
we recover, order-by-order, the straightforward interpretation of
the energy current. It comes from the integration of all particles
entering and leaving a given lead, but multiplied by the energy each
particle carries. Mathematically this is a consequence of a subtle
cancellation of the second term of Eq. (
37
).

III.3
Heat current

The heat current flowing through the
\alpha
-lead is the last observable
we will study up to the second order. We can determine the expectation
value of heat current based on the first law of thermodynamics
[
11
,
4
]
.
The total energy gained or lost by a lead
\alpha
is the sum of
the heat and the work done by the particles being exchanged with the
local system. In this way, the heat current
J_{\alpha}
is given
by

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\dot{E}_{\alpha}-\mu_{\alpha}\left\langle\dot{N}_{\alpha}\left(t\right)\right\rangle.

(43)

The formal definition of Eq. (
43
)
contains two mean values that we developed in subsections
III.1

and
III.2
. Using the previous results, we can
straightforwardly write the adiabatic expansion, up to the second order,
of the heat current, just as we have done before

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\simeq J_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}+J_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}+J_{\alpha}^{\left(1,1\right)}+J_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}.

Employing Eqs. (
30
-
33
)
and (
39
-
42
),
we found

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)},

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\underset{\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}\ddot{X}_{\nu},

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\underset{\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}\dot{X}_{\nu},

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\underset{\mu,\nu}{\sum}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}\dot{X}_{\mu}\dot{X}_{\nu},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}-\mu_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathcal{J}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}-\mu_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathcal{J}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}-\mu_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{\dot{\mathcal{E}}}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}-\mu_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}.

Likewise, the above results can be written as integrals, giving

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathcal{J}}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\varepsilon-\mu_{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}^{\left(0\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\varepsilon-\mu_{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(1\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathcal{J}}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\varepsilon-\mu_{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\nu}^{\left(2\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\varepsilon-\mu_{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,\mu\nu}^{\left(1,1\right)}\left(T,\varepsilon\right)d\varepsilon.

Thus far, we have offered a complete set of quantum transport observables
to understand how the second-order corrections work. These results
became a starting point to explore thermodynamic properties from a
quantum point of view.

IV
Models

In the previous sections, we formulated the theory of adiabatic expansions
up to the second order for a set of fundamental observables. Now, we will
evaluate the charge, energy, and heat currents for two different time-dependent
devices. In studying these examples, we have three goals in mind.
First, we want to illustrate the usage of the developed formulas and
their utility for the thermodynamic analysis of nanomachines. Second,
we want to test their validity by showing that the results they produce
are physically reasonable. In particular, we will show that energy
and particle number are conserved at all times and for all parameters
in the order-by-order expansions
[
4
]
. This involves
the comparison of proven first-order formulas with the above-derived
second-order terms. The first two points are shown in the first analyzed
device, an atomic rotor in contact with two fixed leads (see Fig.

1
). The
second example consists of an oscillating quantum point contact (see
Fig.
3
).
There we want to illustrate the kind of phenomena that can arise from
the second-order terms of the observables. In this sense, we should
recall that traditional adiabatic quantum pumping
[
29
]
,
requires the movement of at least two out-of-phase parameters. Instead,
the studied device shows monoparametric pumping thanks to the second-order
terms of charge currents, without requiring anharmonicities
[
50
]

or quantum interference effects mediated by magnetic fields
[
51
]
.

IV.1
Driven atomic rotor

To model the atomic rotor we assume a minimal tight-binding model
consisting of a one-site system attached to two identical conduction
channels (the leads) through which an electrical current can flow.
We will name each of them as
L
and
R
. The isolated site is linked
to the rotor and represents a quantum dot with a site energy
\epsilon_{d}
.
Two semi-infinite tight-binding chains set up the leads, where
t_{L}

and
t_{R}
are the tunneling between them and the rotor
[
17
]
,
as sketched in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Representation of a bidimensional atomic rotor attached to two leads.

To keep matters simple, we assume only the hopping terms change with
time, having a linear dependence on the distance between the rotor
and the respective leads. Furthermore, we force the atomic rotor to
follow a circular trajectory with a fixed radius
R_{0}
and a given
angular frequency
\omega
. Based on this outline, we arrive at (see
Appendix
G
)

\displaystyle t_{L}

\displaystyle=t_{\mathrm{max}}\left(1+a-a\sqrt{1+\beta\left(1-\sin\left(\omega t\right)\right)}\right),

(44)

\displaystyle t_{R}

\displaystyle=t_{\mathrm{max}}\left(1+a-a\sqrt{1+\beta\left(1-\cos\left(\omega t\right)\right)}\right),

(45)

where
t_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximum (in absolute value) tunneling
amplitude,
a
sets the decay of the hopping terms with the distant
between the dot and the leads, and

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=2\frac{R_{0}L}{\left(L-R_{0}\right)^{2}}.

The description of the local system requires at least a three-site
Hamiltonian, which includes the site energy of the central dot and
the first sites of the leads. In this way, we circumvent the limitation
mentioned earlier of time-independent self-energies
\Sigma^{r,a,<}\left(\varepsilon\right)
.
The resulting adiabatic Green’s functions
G^{r,a,<}
, whose explicit
forms can be found in
[
17
]
, do not commute, in general,
with each other or
\Lambda
, as would be the case for a single site
local system; see the example of Ref.
36
. Thus,
despite its simplicity, the analyzed example provides a challenging
test for the developed formulas.

Once we set up the model, we apply the charge current formulas. For
this purpose, we rewrite Eq. (
29
)
(see Appendix
G
)
for the current over the
L
-lead as

\displaystyle I_{L}

\displaystyle=\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(0\right)}+\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1\right)}\omega+\left(\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1,1\right)}+\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(2\right)}\right)\omega^{2}.

(46)

The term
\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(0\right)}
is the adiabatic contribution
given by Eq. (
30
),

\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1\right)}
is the first-order correction
dictated by Eq. (
31
),
and
\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1,1\right)}
and
\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(2\right)}

stand for the second-order corrections of the charge current, given
by Eqs. (
32
)
and (
33
)
respectively.

The procedure used for the charge current can be extended to the remaining
studied observables. For example, the heat current formula takes the
form

\displaystyle J_{L}

\displaystyle=\varOmega_{J_{L}}^{\left(0\right)}+\varOmega_{J_{L}}^{\left(1\right)}\omega+\left(\varOmega_{J_{L}}^{\left(1,1\right)}+\varOmega_{J_{L}}^{\left(2\right)}\right)\omega^{2}.

(47)

Despite their complexity, our goal is to ensure the validity of the
formulas. To achieve this point, we will use the order-by-order energy
conservation formulas for quantum transport
[
4
]
.
For our model, it reads

\displaystyle Q_{J_{L}}^{\left(n\right)}-\frac{\delta\mu_{L}}{e}Q_{I_{L}}^{\left(n\right)}+Q_{J_{R}}^{\left(n\right)}-\frac{\delta\mu_{R}}{e}Q_{I_{R}}^{\left(n\right)}+W^{\left(n-1\right)}

\displaystyle=0.

(48)

The above formula holds for any integer
n
, where
n
represents
the order of the adiabatic expansion of the quantity of interest,

W^{\left(n-1\right)}
is the
n-1
order of the work done by the
rotor in a single cycle (see Appendix
I
),

Q_{J_{\alpha}}^{\left(n\right)}
is the heat pumped per revolution
of the rotor to the
\alpha
-lead, and
Q_{I_{\alpha}}^{\left(n\right)}

is the pumped charge in a cycle. These quantities are evaluated as:

\displaystyle W^{\left(n-1\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{{\mathop{\int}\limits}}\overrightarrow{F}^{\left(n-1\right)}\cdot\overrightarrow{X}dt

(49)

\displaystyle Q_{J_{\alpha}}^{\left(n\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{{\mathop{\int}\limits}}J_{\alpha}^{\left(n\right)}dt,

(50)

\displaystyle Q_{I_{\alpha}}^{\left(n\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{{\mathop{\int}\limits}}I_{\alpha}^{\left(n\right)}dt.

(51)

Here,
J_{\alpha}^{\left(n\right)}
and
I_{\alpha}^{\left(n\right)}

are defined in Section
III
,
\tau
is the period
of the movement, and
\overrightarrow{F}^{\left(n-1\right)}
is the

n-1
order of the adiabatic expansion of the electronic force
\overrightarrow{F}
.
The latter is a vector whose components are defined by

\displaystyle F_{\nu}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\hbar\mathrm{Tr}\left[\Lambda_{\nu}\mathcal{G}^{<}\left(t,t\right)\right].

It is important to highlight that the fulfillment of Eq. (

with second-order corrections of the heat and charge currents involves

and

[
16

17
]

A further magnitude of significance is the total energy pumped to

\alpha
-lead, which reads:

\displaystyle Q_{\dot{E}_{\alpha}}^{\left(n\right)}

\displaystyle=Q_{J_{\alpha}}^{\left(n\right)}-\frac{\delta\mu_{L}}{e}Q_{I_{\alpha}}^{\left(n\right)}.

Rewriting Eq. (

using the above formula, we obtain a version of the first law of thermodynamics
which relates the total energy of electrons flowing between leads
and the mechanical work. However, in this version electron energy
and mechanical work are related through the different orders of their
adiabatic expansion.

Finally, charge conservation is another property that must be satisfied

\displaystyle Q_{I_{L}}^{\left(n\right)}+Q_{I_{R}}^{\left(n\right)}

\displaystyle=0.

Fig. (

V
Conclusion

Throughout this work, we have presented expressions, based on NEGFs
within a Schwinger-Keldysh approach, for the second-order adiabatic
expansion of three different observables of quantum transport (energy,
heat, and charge currents). To our knowledge, NEGF second-order expansions
of heat and energy currents have not been treated before. Moreover,
the explicit formulas for the observables are general and ready to
be used in a wide range of systems. We only assumed that self-energies
are time-independent, which is naturally accomplished for most quantum
transport problems.

We have illustrated how the developed formulas produce physically
consistent results (including order-by-order energy and particle conservation
laws) using two simple models. We must emphasize that order-by-order
energy conservation strictly involves the comparison of the here-developed
expressions (second-order heat and charge currents) with a well-established
first-order expression (first-order current-induced force). Moreover,
we have shown in both examples how the developed formulas provide
useful thermodynamical information about the devices. Additionally,
in the second model, we have analyzed a phenomenon where second-order
monoparametric pumping takes place. This provides a glimpse of the
type of phenomenon that our expressions allow one to describe.

As for possible extensions of the present work, it would be interesting
a deeper exploration of second-order phenomena and their potential
use, as well as the development of similar formulas for other observables
like current-induced forces (also known as electron wind forces
[
54
]
)
or spin-transfer torque
[
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
]
.

Once again, the procedure is to apply the Wigner transform to the

M_{\alpha}
operator and afterward the inverse transform. Then,
the gradient expansion of Eq. (
80

takes the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\frac{1}{N!}\left(-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\right)^{N}\binom{N}{j}C_{\alpha,N,j}^{M}\left(T,\varepsilon\right),

(82)

where

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,N,j}^{M}

\displaystyle=\left(-1\right)^{j}\partial_{T}^{N-j}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{j}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\partial_{T}^{j}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N-j}\widetilde{\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}}

\displaystyle+\left(-1\right)^{j}\partial_{T}^{N-j}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{j}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{<}\partial_{T}^{j}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N-j}\widetilde{\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}}.

where
\widetilde{\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}}
is the Wigner
transform of
\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}\left(t^{\prime},t\right)
.

The charge current formula, shown in Eq. (
72
),
can be compared with the above transform. The main difference between
both lies in the self-energies, where the one given in Eq. (
82
)
implies the Wigner transform of the self-energy time derivative. To
find the expressions for these terms, we just need to use the definitions
of the Wigner coordinates, given in Eqs. (
14
)
and (
15
), followed by
the chain rule. We then apply the Wigner transform to the resulting
expressions and assume that the self-energies, which are solely energy-dependent
(
\varSigma_{\alpha}\left(\varepsilon\right)\equiv\varSigma_{\alpha}
),
vanish at high and low energies. The result is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{T}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}-i\frac{\varepsilon}{\hbar}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<},

(83)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\partial_{t^{\prime}}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{T}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}-i\frac{\varepsilon}{\hbar}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}.

(84)

Then, plugging the Eqs. (
83
)
and (
84
)
into Eq. (
82
),
together with the conditions of Eqs. (
73
)
and (
74
),
the Wigner transform of
M_{\alpha}
can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\frac{1}{N!}\left(-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\right)^{N-1}C_{\alpha,N}^{M}\left(T,\varepsilon\right),

(85)

where

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,N}^{M}

\displaystyle=\partial_{T}^{N}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\left(\varepsilon\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}\right)+\partial_{T}^{N}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{<}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\left(\varepsilon\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\right).

We can apply the chain rule to
\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\left(\varepsilon\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}\right)

giving

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\left(\varepsilon\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}+N\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N-1}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<},

(86)

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\left(\varepsilon\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}+N\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N-1}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}.

(87)

These results allow us to rewrite the
\widetilde{M}_{\alpha}
of
Eq. (
85
)
in a closed form

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\varepsilon}{\hbar}\widetilde{F}_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrel{{\mathop{\sum}\limits}}\frac{1}{N!}\left(-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\right)^{N}C_{\alpha,N}\left(T,\varepsilon\right),

(88)

where the term
\widetilde{F}_{\alpha}
is that defined for the charge
current (see Eq. (
75
)),
and

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,N}

\displaystyle=\partial_{T}^{N+1}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{R}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{<}+\partial_{T}^{N+1}\mathcal{\widetilde{G}}^{<}\partial_{\varepsilon}^{N}\varSigma_{\alpha}^{A}.

Finally, we need to apply the inverse Wigner transform to Eq. (
88
)
and then put this into the energy current definition [Eq. (
81

The result is Eq. (
37

Appendix G
Driven atomic rotor model

In subsection
IV.1
, we provide a brief
account of the fundamental constituents of the atomic rotor model.
We will use two semi-infinite tight-binding chains to represent the
leads attached to the quantum dot and include the first site of each
chain as part of the local system. Then, the Hamiltonian of the local
system reads:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{S}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{0}&-t_{L}&0\\
-t_{L}&\epsilon_{d}&-t_{R}\\
0&-t_{R}&\epsilon_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

The adiabatic retarded Green’s function, given by Eq. (
19
),
takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\varepsilon\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_{S}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{L}^{R}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{R}^{R}\right)^{-1},

(89)

where the self-energies coming from the decimation of the left and
right leads (
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{L}^{R}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{R}^{R}

respectively) are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{L}^{R}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{0}^{R}\left(\varepsilon\right)&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{R}^{R}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&\Sigma_{0}^{R}\left(\varepsilon\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
\Sigma_{0}^{R}
is the self-energy (in the energy domain)
of a semi-infite tight-binding chain. It is often expressed as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}^{R}\left(\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{0}\left(\varepsilon\right)-i\Gamma_{0}\left(\varepsilon\right),

(90)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=t_{0}\begin{cases}\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}-\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}\right)^{2}-1}&\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}\geq 1,\\
\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}&1\geq\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}\geq-1,\\
\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}\right)^{2}-1}&-1\geq\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=t_{0}\begin{cases}0&\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2V_{0}}\geq 1,\\
\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}\right)^{2}}&1\geq\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}\geq-1,\\
0&-1\geq\frac{\varepsilon-\epsilon_{0}}{2t_{0}}.\end{cases}

Above,
\epsilon_{0}
and
t_{0}
are, respectively, the site energy
and the hopping between neighboring sites of the tight-binding chain.

In our model, the mechanical DOF affects only
t_{L}
and
t_{R}
,
which can be taken as generalized coordinates related to the position
of the rotor
\theta
by some function. Then, the matrix operator

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\theta}
can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varLambda}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial t_{L}}{\partial\theta}\boldsymbol{\varLambda}_{t_{L}}+\frac{\partial t_{R}}{\partial\theta}\boldsymbol{\varLambda}_{t_{R}}.

Here, the matrix operators
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varLambda}}_{t_{L}}

and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\varLambda}}_{t_{R}}
, necessary to calculate
the charge and heat currents (see sections
III.1

and
III.3
), as well as the electronic force
(see Section
I
), are:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varLambda}_{t_{L}}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varLambda}_{t_{R}}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

So far, we have provided a general tight-binding model for a quantum
dot connected with two leads. The next stage requires the specification
of the geometric parameters of the atomic rotor. Typically the dependence
of hopping parameters with the distance are modeled by exponential
functions. In our case, that would mean taking

\displaystyle t_{L}

\displaystyle=t_{m}e^{a\left(1-\frac{\Delta r_{L}}{r_{0L}}\right)},

(91)

\displaystyle t_{R}

\displaystyle=t_{m}e^{a\left(1-\frac{\Delta r_{R}}{r_{0R}}\right)}.

(92)

where
\Delta r_{L}
and
\Delta r_{R}
are the spatial distances
between the quantum dot and the closest chain sites to the
L
and

R
leads, respectively, while
r_{0L}
and
r_{0R}
are the smallest
values of
\Delta r_{L}
and
\Delta r_{R}
along the trajectory.
Using polar coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\Delta r_{L}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{L^{2}+R_{0}^{2}-2R_{0}L\sin\left(\theta\right)},

(93)

\displaystyle\Delta r_{R}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{L^{2}+R_{0}^{2}-2R_{0}L\cos\left(\theta\right)}.

(94)

where
\theta
,
L
, and
R_{0}
are defined in Fig.
1
,
and
r_{0L}
and
r_{0R}
satisfy the condition

\displaystyle r_{0L}

\displaystyle=r_{0R}=L-R_{0}.

(95)

Plugging the Eqs. (
93
),
(
94
)
and (
95
)
into Eqs. (
91
)
and (
92
)
make the model available to evaluate charge and heat currents. However,
since our purpose is to outline typical behaviors of the currents
and not to focus on the particularities of the used models, we decided
to use a linearized version of Eqs. (
91
)
and (
92
).
In this way, we arrive at Eqs. (
44
)
and (
45
).

Taking into account the model, we are now able to put the charging
current calculation into action. For a fixed radius and angular position
given by
\theta=\omega t
, we rewrite Eqs. (
30
),
(
31
), (
32
)
and (
33
)
as

\displaystyle I_{L}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(0\right)},

\displaystyle I_{L}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1,1\right)}\omega^{2},

\displaystyle I_{L}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1\right)}\omega,

\displaystyle I_{L}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(2\right)}\omega^{2},

where

\displaystyle\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=-e\left(\mathcal{N}_{L,t_{L}}^{\left(1\right)}\frac{\partial t_{L}}{\partial\theta}+\mathcal{N}_{L,t_{R}}^{\left(1\right)}\frac{\partial t_{R}}{\partial\theta}\right),

\displaystyle\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(2\right)}

\displaystyle=-e\left(\mathcal{N}_{L,t_{L}}^{\left(2\right)}\frac{\partial^{2}t_{L}}{\partial\theta^{2}}+\mathcal{N}_{L,t_{R}}^{\left(2\right)}\frac{\partial^{2}t_{R}^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\varOmega_{I_{L}}^{\left(1,1\right)}

\displaystyle=-e\left(\mathcal{N}_{L,t_{L}t_{L}}^{\left(1,1\right)}\left(\frac{\partial t_{L}}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{N}_{L,t_{R}t_{R}}^{\left(1,1\right)}\left(\frac{\partial t_{R}}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}\right)

and

Appendix I
Electronic forces

To verify the order-by-order energy conservation, the evaluation of
the work done by the electronic forces at a given order
n
is necessary.
The work
W
at a given order
n
is

\displaystyle W^{\left(n\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{{\mathop{\int}\limits}}\left(\underset{\nu}{\sum}F_{\nu}^{\left(n\right)}\dot{X}_{\nu}\right)dt,

where
\dot{X}_{\nu}
is the slow time derivative of the classical
mechanical degree of freedom
\nu
,
F_{\nu}^{\left(n\right)}
is
the
n
-th order of the adiabatic expansion of the electronic force

F_{\nu}
. The expressions for
F_{\nu}^{\left(n\right)}
up to
first-order are well known (see for example Refs.
59
,
17
).
They read

\displaystyle F_{\mu}^{\left(0\right)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\textrm{tr}\left\{\varLambda_{\mu}G^{<}\right\}d\varepsilon,

\displaystyle F_{\mu}^{\left(1\right)}

\displaystyle=-\underset{\nu}{\sum}\gamma_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}_{\nu},

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi}\int\textrm{tr}\left\{G^{<}\varLambda_{\mu}\partial_{\varepsilon}G^{R}\varLambda_{\nu}-G^{<}\varLambda_{\nu}\partial_{\varepsilon}G^{A}\varLambda_{\mu}\right\}d\varepsilon.
