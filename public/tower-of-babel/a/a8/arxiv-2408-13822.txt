Informativeness and Trust in Bayesian Persuasion
Reema Deori, Ankur A. Kulkarni

Abstract
A persuasion policy successfully persuades an agent to pick a particular action only if the information is designed in a manner that convinces the agent that it is in their best interest to pick that action. Thus, it is natural to ask, what makes the agent trust the persuader’s suggestion? We study a Bayesian persuasion interaction between a sender and a receiver where the sender has access to private information about a source and the receiver attempts to recover this information from messages sent by the sender. The sender crafts these messages in an attempt to maximize its utility which depends on the source symbol and the symbol recovered by the receiver. Our goal is to characterize the min-max equilibrium utility of the sender, called the Stackelberg game value , and the amount of true information revealed by the sender during persuasion. We find that the Stackelberg game value is given by the optimal value of a linear program on probability distributions constrained by certain trust constraints . These constraints encode that any signal in a persuasion strategy must contain more truth than untruth and thus impose a fundamental bound on the extent of obfuscation a sender can perform in any attempt to persuade the receiver. We define informativeness of the sender as the minimum value of the expected number of symbols truthfully revealed by the sender in any accumulation point of a sequence of ε \varepsilon -equilibrium persuasion strategies, and show that it is given by the optimal value of another linear program. Informativeness gives a fundamental bound on the amount of information the sender must reveal to persuade a receiver. Closed form expressions for the Stackelberg game value and the informativeness are presented for structured utility functions. This work generalizes our previous work [ 1 ] where the sender and the receiver were constrained to play only deterministic strategies and a similar notion of informativeness was characterized. Comparisons between the previous and current notions are discussed.



1
Introduction

Consider a social-media influencer who makes money through endorsement offers from brands based on the number of purchases made using the links affiliated to her account. The products she reviews are of varying quality, whereby she faces a dilemma between maximizing sales and maintaining trust. For example, when she is paid for the promotion of a poor quality product, honesty will lead to low sales. However, frequent misleading reviews will hurt her too, since this action would result in her followers losing trust in her honest reviews as well. A better choice in such scenarios appears to be to maintain some ambiguity: promote the poor quality product as an average quality product by giving average scores to both an average quality product and the poor quality product, thereby partially misleading her followers and also partially winning their trust. In other words, the influencer’s optimal policy ‘lies’

in striking a balance between maintaining trust through honesty and maximizing sales through prevarication.

Our aim is to understand how much truth there is in such an influencer’s words. We model an interaction between a strategic informed sender and an uniformed receiver. The sender persuades the receiver by committing to a randomized signaling policy crafted with the aim of maximizing its utility. The receiver on the other hand attempts to know the true information of the sender.
Our contribution is a characterization of the sender’s expected utility in a Stackelberg equilibrium
[

as a linear program. We also get a linear programming characterization of the
minimum amount of truth
revealed to the receiver in any Stackelberg equilibrium. In the process we uncover a key element of such problems: persuasion must be subject to “trust constraints” for it to work. The influencer’s optimal policy must reveal some truth.

We studied the same game previously in
[
1
]
but under the restriction that both players play only deterministic strategies. Thus, the sender’s objective in this game was to pick an ‘encoding’ strategy that persuades the receiver to recover the majority of the symbols as the sender’s preferred choice of symbols. Our study in

informativeness of a sender

vertex clique cover number

In this paper, we take this thought forward allowing the players to play randomized strategies where obfuscation takes a more subtle and interesting form.

1.1
Main findings

Recall the dilemma the influencer had between honesty and prevarication, or equivalently between retaining the trust of her followers and maximizing her revenue. Intuitively, to maintain the trust of its followers, the influencer must always reveal a greater degree of truth than untruth in every review.
Thus, any review, say a “low” rating, is trustworthy only if it is mostly given to low quality products.
Our central contribution lies in formalizing this intuition.

We formulate the above setting as that of Bayesian persuasion and solve for a min-max Stackelberg equilibrium solution in behavioural strategies.
We find that even in simple problem classes, finding the Stackelberg equilibrium value (SGV) of the sender involves a long and complex calculation. Our first result bypasses this difficulty: it shows that the equilibrium expected utility of the sender is characterized by a
linear program
(LP) on probability distributions; these distributions are required to meet certain
trust constraints
. These constraints encode that any signal must contain more truth than untruth and thus impose a fundamental bound on the extent of obfuscation a sender can perform. These constraints make the receiver trust the sender about the truth of some symbols and accordingly makes the rational receiver choose a best response which recovers some symbols as symbols preferred by the sender. We measure the information revealed by the sender by the minimum value of the expected number of symbols truthfully revealed by the sender in any accumulation point of a sequence of
\varepsilon
-Stackelberg equilibrium strategies. This quantity serves as a fundamental bound on the amount of truth revealed in any equilibrium strategy and is a measure of the informativeness of this interaction for the receiver. We call this quantity the
informativeness
of the sender and show that, despite a complex definition, it is also given by another linear program. A corollary of our analysis is that the sender will opt for full information disclosure at equilibrium
if and only if
there is full alignment of objectives with the receiver.
Using this result, we prove that whenever the non-negative terms in the utility of the sender are positive and constant, the loss of information is identical in all persuasion strategies. For such utility functions, we prove that the SGV is a constant multiple of the amount of loss of information at equilibrium.

We end our paper by deriving closed form expressions for the SGV and informativeness for three different classes of utility functions. We introduce the
obfuscation graph
of a utility function and characterize the SGV and informativeness exactly for utilities for which this graph is chain, cycle or star. We conclude by comparing the notion of informativeness with the notion of informativeness in
[
1
]
where the players are allowed to play only deterministic strategies.

Informativeness serves as a measure of information content in a utility function, much like entropy does for a probability distribution. It enjoys some natural properties and has a clean characterization. This work, following up on our previous work
[
1
]
and similar works in a screening setting
[
3
,
4
]
furthers our understanding information exchange in multiagent interactions.

2.2
Sender-receiver game

We consider a source alphabet
{\cal X}
of size
q
; each element of
{\cal X}
is called a
symbol
. Let
{\cal Y}
be the set of possible
signals
that the sender can assign to the elements of
{\cal X}
. We assume
|{\cal X}|=|{\cal Y}|
. The source generates a symbol
X
uniformly at random with a probability
{\mathbb{P}}(X=x)=\frac{1}{q},\forall x\in{\cal X}
. The sender maps
X
randomly to a signal
Y\in{\cal Y}
according to some distribution
\pi\in{\cal P}({\cal X}|{\cal Y})
. The receiver attempts to recover
X
from
Y
using a distribution
\sigma\in{\cal P}({\cal Y}|{\cal X})
.
If the receiver recovers the source symbol
x\in{\cal X}
as
x^{\prime}\in{\cal X}
then the sender obtains utility
\mathscr{U}(x^{\prime},x)
, where
\mathscr{U}:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The sender attempts to maximize this utility by choosing
\pi
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathscr{U}(x,x)=0,\forall x\in{\cal X}
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{S}:=\{\pi|\pi\in{\cal P}({\cal X}|{\cal Y})\}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{R}:=\{\sigma|\sigma\in{\cal P}({\cal X}|{\cal Y})\}
denote the collection of the strategies of the sender and the receiver respectively. For any
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
, let

{\cal Y}(\pi):=\{y\in{\cal Y}|\exists x\in{\cal X}\text{s.t.}\pi(y|x)>0\}

be the set of possible signals used by the sender with positive probability when it plays
\pi
. Let
\widehat{X}
denote the symbol recovered by the receiver. Thus, for a given pair of
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
and
\sigma\in{\mathcal{A}}_{R},
the joint distribution of
X,Y,\widehat{X}
is given by

{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi,\sigma}(x,y,{\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{})=\frac{1}{q}\pi(y|x)\sigma({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}|y).

(1)

The receiver’s goal is to choose
\sigma\in{\mathcal{A}}_{R}
to maximize the probability of correct recovery of the symbols generated at the source. This is equivalent to maximizing

{\cal R}(\pi,\sigma):=q{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi,\sigma}(X=\widehat{X})=\sum_{x\in{\cal X},y\in{\cal Y}}\pi(y|x)\sigma(x|y).

The set of strategies of the receiver which maximize
{\cal R}(\pi,\sigma)
is called the
best response set
,
\mathscr{B}(\pi),

\mathscr{B}(\pi)=\{\sigma\in{\mathcal{A}}_{R}(\pi)|\ \sigma\in\arg\max\limits_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{A}}_{R}(\pi)}{\cal R}(\pi,\sigma)\}.

Clearly,
\mathscr{B}(\pi)
can be also be expressed as

\mathscr{B}(\pi)=\{\sigma\in{\mathcal{A}}_{R}|{\rm supp}(\sigma(\bullet|y))\equiv\arg\max\limits_{x}\pi(y|x)\}.

(2)

For a
\pi
and
\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi),
let

\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\pi,\sigma):=\{{\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}\in{\cal X}|\ \exists y\in{\cal Y}(\pi)\text{s.t.}\sigma({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}|y)>0\},

(3)

be the set of symbols which have positive probability of getting recovered when the sender and the receiver play
\pi
and
\sigma
respectively. For a
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
and an
x\in{\cal X}
, we define the support of
\pi(\cdot|x)
as

E_{x}(\pi):=\{y\in{\cal X}:\pi(y|x)>0\}.

The sender’s goal is to pick a
\pi
which maximizes its expected utility, i.e., to maximize,

U(\pi,\sigma):=\sum\limits_{x\in{\cal X}}\sum\limits_{y\in{\cal Y}(\pi)}\sum\limits_{\hat{x}\in{\cal X}}\pi(y|x)\sigma(\hat{x}|y)\mathscr{U}(\hat{x},x).

(4)

From this point forward,
U(\pi,\sigma)
will be referred to as the
expected utility
of the sender. For any strategy
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
, let,

\begin{split}\underline{U}(\pi)=\min\limits_{\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)}U(\pi,\sigma)\text{and}\overline{U}(\pi)=\max\limits_{\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)}U(\pi,\sigma)\end{split}

(5)

denote the corresponding
worst case expected utility
(WCEU) and the
best case expected utility
(BCEU) obtained by the sender, respectively. Let

\underline{\mathscr{B}}(\pi)=\{\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)|U(\pi,\sigma)=\min\limits_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)}U(\pi,\sigma^{\prime})\}

(6)

denote the collection of all those best response strategies of the receiver which give the sender the worst case expected utility when the sender plays
\pi.
Let

D(\pi)=\{\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)|\sigma(x|y)\in\{0,1\},\forall x\in{\cal X},y\in{\cal Y}\}

(7)

be the collection of
deterministic
best response strategies of the receiver and let

\overline{D}(\pi)=\{\sigma\in D(\pi)|U(\pi,\sigma)=\max\limits_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)}U(\pi,\sigma^{\prime})\}.

(8)

be the collection of all those deterministic best response strategies of the sender which give the sender the
best case expected utility
.

We study this sender-receiver interaction as Bayesian persuasion setting and seek a
min-max Stackelberg equilibrium
where the sender commits first. Thus,
\pi^{*}\in\arg\sup\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}}\underline{U}(\pi)

is a
Stackelberg equilibrium strategy
of the sender and

\mathscr{U}^{*}:=\sup\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}}\underline{U}(\pi),

(9)

is the
Stackelberg game value
(SGV).
We also introduce another quantity
\overline{\mathscr{U}}^{*}

\overline{\mathscr{U}}^{*}:=\sup\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}}\overline{U}(\pi)

(10)

to quantify the supremum of the best case expected utility of the sender.

In our study we find that there exists a sender strategy
\overline{\pi}^{*}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
which attains
\overline{\mathscr{U}}^{*}
, i.e. results in
\overline{U}(\overline{\pi}^{*})=\overline{\mathscr{U}}^{*}.
Unfortunately, there seem to generically exist utility functions (see Example
3.2.1
below) for which the supremum
\mathscr{U}^{*}
is not attained by any
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
. But remarkably, we find that
\mathscr{U}^{*}=\overline{\mathscr{U}}^{*}.
Since a Stackelberg equilibrium need not exist, we base our analysis on the
\varepsilon
-
Stackelberg equilibrium
defined below.

Definition 2.1

(
\varepsilon
-Stackelberg equilibrium strategy) Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
. A strategy
\pi^{*}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
is a
\varepsilon
-Stackelberg equilibrium strategy (
\varepsilon
-SES) of the sender if

\mathscr{U}^{*}\geq\underline{U}(\pi^{*})\geq\mathscr{U}^{*}-\varepsilon.

(11)

If
\pi^{*}
is a
\varepsilon
-SES of the sender then every
\sigma^{*}\in\mathscr{B}(\pi^{*})
is a
\varepsilon
-SES of the receiver when the sender plays
\pi^{*}
.

If
\varepsilon=0
, then we shall call the pair of
\pi^{*}
and
\sigma^{*}\in\mathscr{B}(\pi^{*})
a pair of
Stackelberg equilibrium strategies
. In the following section we present our analysis of the Stackelberg game value.

LP formulation for the SGV

In this section we prove that the SGV is given by the optimal value of a linear program. For computing the SGV, one would ordinarily compute
\underline{U}(\pi)
for every strategy in
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
, and thereafter optimize over
\pi

3.5

3.1
Trust constraints

Before we present the actual LP formulation, we provide a bit of motivation. For any pair of
\pi

\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)

It is easy to check that

In other words, the probability of recovering

\sigma^{*}

\begin{split}\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)&=\mu^{*}(i|x),\qquad\forall i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*}),\forall x\in{\cal X}\text{and}\\
\sigma^{*}(i|y_{i})&=1,\forall i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*}).\end{split}

(20)

Clearly
\sigma^{*}
is deterministic and
{\cal Y}(\pi^{*})=\{y_{i}|i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})\}
which results in
|{\cal Y}(\pi^{*})|=|\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})|
. Note that
\mu^{*}
satisfies the trust constraints. As a consequence,

\pi^{*}(y_{i}|i)\geq\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x),\forall x\in{\cal X},\forall i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})

(21)

which makes
\sigma^{*}\in D(\pi^{*})
.
Since
\mu^{*}({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}|x)\equiv\sum\limits_{y\in{\cal Y}}\pi^{*}(y|x)\sigma^{*}({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}|y)
and
V(\mu^{*})=U(\pi^{*},\sigma^{*})
, we get
\mu^{*}\equiv(\pi^{*},\sigma^{*})
.

Next we show that
\sigma^{*}\in\overline{D}(\pi^{*})
. Notice that if
\sigma^{*}\notin\overline{D}(\pi^{*})
, then there exists another
\sigma^{\prime}\in\overline{D}(\pi^{*})
such that
U(\pi^{*},\sigma^{*})<U(\pi^{*},\sigma^{\prime})
. In such scenario, we can construct another
\mu^{\prime}
from the pair of
\pi^{*}
and
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
V(\mu^{*})<V(\mu^{\prime})
. But
\mu^{\prime}
is feasible for
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})
which is a contradiction to the assumption that
\mu^{*}
is optimal. Therefore,

\sigma^{*}\in\overline{D}(\pi^{*})
. Now
\pi^{*}
can be categorized into two different classes based on the relationship between
\underline{U}(\pi^{*})
and
V(\mu^{*})
.

Case 1.

\underline{U}(\pi^{*})=V(\mu^{*})
: For any
\pi^{*}
of this class, the result follows trivially.

Case 2.

\underline{U}(\pi^{*})<V(\mu^{*})
: For every
\pi^{*}
of this class there exists a distinct
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(\pi^{*})
such that
U(\pi^{*},\sigma^{\prime})<U(\pi^{*},\sigma^{*})=V(\mu^{*})
. Our main goal is to come up with a sequence
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k\rightarrow\infty}
such that
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\underline{U}(\pi_{k})={\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U}))
. To ensure this we want
\underline{U}(\pi_{k})
to approach
U(\pi^{*},\sigma^{*})
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. We construct our
\pi_{k}
’s in such a way that
\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}},
the corresponding
\pi_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*}.
This give us control over the value of
\underline{U}(\pi_{k})
for every
k
since there exists only one best response strategy. We also want the construction to ensure that
k\rightarrow\infty
,
({\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U}))-\underline{U}(\pi_{k}))\rightarrow 0.
This will prove our theorem. To proceed with the construction, we introduce the following two sets. For a
i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})
and
x\in{\cal X}
, let

\begin{split}Q(i)&=\{x\in{\cal X}|x\neq i,\pi^{*}(y_{i}|i)=\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)\},\\
Z(x)&=\{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})|x\in Q(i)\}.\end{split}

Q(i)
is the collection of all those symbols
x\in{\cal X}
for which equality holds in (
21
). Hence,
Q(i)
comprises of all
x\in{\cal X}
distinct from
i
that the receiver can map
y_{i}
to in a best response.

Z(x)
is the collection of all
i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})
for which equality holds in (
21
) for the fixed
x
. Hence,
Z(x)
represents the collection of all signals distinct from
y_{x}
that the receiver can map to
x
as a best response. To construct such a
\pi_{k}
from
\pi^{*}
, we need to define
\pi_{k}(\bullet|x)
for all
x\in{\cal X}.
First we partition
{\cal X}
into two main classes based on the structure of
\mu^{*}
and
\pi^{*}
.

Class 1.

x\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})
: This class can be further subdivided into the following two classes:

(a)

x\notin\cup_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})}Q(i)
:
For such
x
, define

\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)=\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x),\forall i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*}).

Clearly,
\pi_{k}(\bullet|x)
is a probability distribution.

(b)

x\in\cup_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})}Q(i)
: For such an
x
of this class and an
y_{i}\in Z(x)
, we define

\begin{split}\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)&=\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)-\frac{\delta}{k},\\
\end{split}

(22)

where
\delta>0
is small.
To balance the weight of the distribution, we define:

\begin{split}\pi_{k}(y_{x}|x)&=\pi^{*}(y_{x}|x)+|Z(x)|\frac{\delta}{k}\\
\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)&=\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x),\forall i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})\backslash(Z(x)\cup\{x\}).\end{split}

(23)

This definition ensures that
\pi_{k}(\bullet|x)
is a probability distribution.

Class 2.

x\notin\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})
: This class can be further subdivided into two classes:

(a)

x\notin\cup_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})}Q(i)
: For an
x
of this class we define

\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)=\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x),\forall y_{i}\in{\cal Y}(\pi^{*}).

Clearly,
\pi_{k}(\bullet|x)
is a probability distribution.

(b)

x\in\cup_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})}Q(i)
: For every
x
in this class let

\begin{split}\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)&=\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)-\frac{\delta}{k},\forall i\in Z(x)\\
\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)&=\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x),\forall i\in\widehat{\mathcal{X}}(\mu^{*})\backslash Z(x).\end{split}

(24)

Next for every
x
belonging to this class consider a distinct
y_{x}\in{\cal Y}\backslash{\cal Y}(\pi^{*})
. Let
{\cal Y}^{\prime}(\pi^{*})
be the collection of all such
y_{x}
. Let

\begin{split}\pi_{k}(y_{x}|x)&=|Z(x)|\frac{\delta}{k},\forall y_{x}\in{\cal Y}^{\prime}(\pi^{*}).\end{split}

(25)

From the construction of
\pi_{k}
, it is evident that
{\cal Y}(\pi_{k})={\cal Y}(\pi^{*})\cup{\cal Y}^{\prime}(\pi^{*})
, a constant independent of
k
and
\pi_{k}\rightarrow\pi^{*}
.
Now we prove that this construction ensures

\pi_{k}(y_{i}|i)>\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x),\forall x\in{\cal X}\backslash\{i\},\forall y_{i}\in{\cal Y}(\pi_{k}).

(26)

Observe that

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)

\displaystyle\geq\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x),\forall i\neq x\text{and}

(27)

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(y_{x}|x)

\displaystyle\geq\pi^{*}(y_{x}|x),\forall x\in{\cal X}.

(28)

We will first categorize
y_{i}\in{\cal Y}(\pi_{k})
into three classes:

(A)

y_{i}\in{\cal Y}^{\prime}(\pi^{*})
: For every
y_{i}
of this class
\pi_{k}(y_{i}|i)>0
and
\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)=0,\forall x\in{\cal X}\backslash\{i\}
. As a consequence (
26
) holds for this class.

(B)

y_{i}\in{\cal Y}(\pi^{*})
and
Q(i)=\phi
:
Clearly from (
21
), for such a
y_{i}
,

\pi^{*}(y_{i}|i)>\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x),\forall x\neq i.

(29)

Observe that
\forall x\neq i,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(y_{i}|i)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\geq}\limits^{(a)}}\pi^{*}(y_{i}|i)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt>}\limits^{(b)}}\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\geq}\limits^{(c)}}\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x),

where
(a)
follows from (
28
),
(b)
follows from (
29
) and
(c)
follows from (
27
). Hence, (
26
) holds.

(C)

y_{i}\in{\cal Y}(\pi^{*})
and
Q(i)\neq\phi
: For every
y_{i}
of this class, we have
\forall x\neq i
,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(y_{i}|i)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{(a)}}\pi^{*}(y_{i}|i)+|Z(i)|\frac{\delta}{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\geq}\limits^{(b)}}\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)+|Z(i)|\frac{\delta}{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt>}\limits^{(c)}}\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x).

Here
(a)
follows from
(
23
)
and
(b)
follows from (
27
). Finally
(c)
follows since
Z(i)
is non-empty.
Therefore, we can conclude that (
26
) holds. Thus, for all
k
,
\mathscr{B}(\pi_{k})=\{\sigma_{k}\}
, where
\sigma_{k}(i|y_{i})=1,\forall y_{i}\in{\cal Y}(\pi_{k})
. This shows that
\pi_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*},\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}.
Consequently, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\underline{U}(\pi_{k})=U(\pi_{k},\sigma_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{x\in{\cal X}}\sum\limits_{y_{i}\in{\cal Y}(\pi_{k})}\pi_{k}(y_{i}|x)\mathscr{U}(i,x).

Since
{\cal Y}(\pi_{k})
is independent of
k
, we get
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\underline{U}(\pi_{k})=U(\pi^{*},\sigma^{*})={\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})).
This proves our lemma.

We can now proceed to prove our main theorem, where we show that
{\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U}))=\mathscr{U}^{*}
.

Theorem 3.5

For any utility function
\mathscr{U}
,

\mathscr{U}^{*}={\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})).

Proof
: To prove our result, we begin by first showing that
\mathscr{U}^{*}
is upper bounded by
{\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U}))
. Fix a
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
and a
\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)
. We can construct a unique
\mu\equiv(\pi,\sigma)
. Therefore,
\underline{U}(\pi)\leq V(\mu)
. Thus,

\small\mathscr{U}^{*}\leq{\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})).

(30)

The previous lemma guarantees the existence of a sequence of strategies
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
whose WCEU converges to
{\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U}))
, i.e.,

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\underline{U}(\pi_{k})={\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})).

(31)

But from the definition of
\mathscr{U}^{*}
,
\underline{U}(\pi_{k})\leq\mathscr{U}^{*}.
Using (
30
) and (
31
) we get
\mathscr{U}^{*}={\rm OPT}(\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})).

Next as a corollary we prove that the sequence of strategies constructed in Lemma
3.4
is a sequence of
\varepsilon
-SES. Additionally, we also show that SGV must be the
best case expected utility
for a strategy of the sender.

Corollary 3.6

1.

Let
\mu^{*}
be an optimal solution of
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})
. Then for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a
\varepsilon_{k}
-SES
\pi_{k}
such that
\varepsilon_{k}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}0
and
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}\pi
, where
\mu^{*}\equiv(\pi,\sigma)
for some
\sigma\in D(\pi)
.

2.

Given any
\mathscr{U}
,

\max\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}}\overline{U}(\pi)=\sup\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}}\underline{U}(\pi)=\mathscr{U}^{*}.

(32)

Proof
: For any optimal solution
\mu^{*}
of
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})
, consider the sequence
\{\pi_{k}\}
and
\sigma^{*}\in D(\pi^{*})
constructed in Lemma
3.4
, where
\pi^{*}=\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{k}.
Let
\varepsilon_{k}=\mathscr{U}^{*}-\underline{U}(\pi_{k})
. From Theorem
3.5
it is clear that
\varepsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. This proves part
1
of the corollary.

From Theorem
3.5
, it is evident that
V(\mu^{*})=\mathscr{U}^{*}
, where
\mu^{*}
solves
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U}).
If
\max\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}}\overline{U}(\pi)>\mathscr{U}^{*},
then there exists a pair of
\pi^{\prime}
and
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}(\pi^{\prime})
such that
U(\pi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})>\mathscr{U}^{*}
. Thus, there exists a
\mu^{\prime}\equiv(\pi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
. Accordingly,
\mathscr{U}^{*}=V(\mu^{*})<V(\mu^{\prime})
which is a contradiction. This proves (
32
).

Eq (
32
) gives a robustness to our conclusions – they hold under both the pessimistic (max-min) Stackelberg equilibrium and also under the optimistic (max-max) one.

3.2.1
An illustrative example

Theorem
3.5
provides us an alternative way for computing the SGV. A brute-force approach would require us to compute the worst case expected utility for every strategy of the sender and find the supremum over all such values to arrive at the SGV. Solving the linear program
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})
is instead significantly simpler. In the following example, we illustrate this.

Example 3.1.

Let
{\cal X}=\{1,2\}
and consider a utility function
\mathscr{U}_{1}:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\mathscr{U}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}
. Here each
(i,j)^{th}
entry represents
\mathscr{U}_{1}(i,j)
. Let
{\cal Y}=\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
.
We will categorize the strategies in
{\mathcal{A}}_{S}
into two different classes based on the size of the signal space.

Case 1.

|{\cal Y}(\pi)|=1
:
Let
{\cal Y}(\pi)=\{y\}
WLOG. Let
C_{1}
denote the collection of
\pi
’s in this class. If
\pi\in C_{1}
then
\pi(y|x)=1,\forall x\in{\cal X}.
Thus,
\mathscr{B}(\pi)={\mathcal{A}}_{R}
. But

\displaystyle U(\pi,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\pi(y|1)\sigma(2|y)\mathscr{U}(2,1)+\pi(y|2)\sigma(1|y)\mathscr{U}(1,2)

\displaystyle=-\sigma(2|y)+\sigma(1|y).

Therefore,
\underline{U}(\pi)=-1
. Thus,
\sup\limits_{\pi\in C_{1}}\underline{U}(\pi)=-1.

Case 2.

|{\cal Y}(\pi)|=2
: Let
C_{2}
denote the collection of all strategies in this class. Let
\pi\in C_{2}
and
\pi(y_{1}|1)=p
and
\pi(y_{2}|2)=q
, where
p,q\in[0,1]
. The strategies in
C_{2}
can be further categorized into distinct classes based on the values of
p
and
q
.

(A)

p+q=1,p,q\in(0,1)
: Let
A
denote the collection of all strategies in this class. Clearly for a
\pi\in A
, there exists a
\sigma\in\mathscr{B}(\pi)
, where
\sigma(2|y_{1})=\sigma(2|y_{2})=1
. Hence
\underline{U}(\pi)=U(\pi,\sigma)=-p
. And therefore
\sup\limits_{\pi\in A}\underline{U}(\pi)=-1.

(B)

p+q>1,p,q\in(0,1)
: Let
B
denote the collection of all strategies of this class. The strict inequality ensures that every
\pi\in B
must belong to
{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*}
and
\mathscr{B}(\pi)=\{\sigma\},
where
\sigma(1|y_{1})=1
and
\sigma(2|y_{2})=1
. Hence,
U(\pi,\sigma)=\underline{U}(\pi)=(1-q)+(1-p)(-1)=p-q.
Therefore,
\sup\limits_{\pi\in B}\underline{U}(\pi)=1.
Note that this supremum is not attained in class
B.

(C)

p+q<1,p,q\in(0,1)
: Proceeding in a similar manner as above, we get
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*}
and
\underline{U}(\pi)=q-p
which gives us
\sup\limits_{\pi\in C}\underline{U}(\pi)=1.
Note that this supremum is not attained in class
C.

(D)

p=1,q=1
: Clearly every symbol will get recovered correctly in the unique best response strategy. Therefore,
\sup\limits_{\pi\in D}\underline{U}(\pi)=0.

(E)

p=1,q\in[0,1)
: Since
|{\cal Y}(\pi)|=2
, we must have
q>0
which makes every
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*}
. For a
\pi
in this class, we have
\underline{U}(\pi)=1-q
. Thus,
\sup\limits_{\pi\in E}\underline{U}(\pi)=1.
Note that this supremum is not attained in class
E.

(F)

p\in[0,1),q=1
: Since
|{\cal Y}(\pi)|=2
, we must have
p>0
which makes
\pi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*}
. If
\pi
is from this class then
\underline{U}(\pi)=p-1
. Thus,
\sup\limits_{\pi\in F}\underline{U}(\pi)=0.

(G)

p\in[0,1),q\in[0,1)
: Notice that every
\pi\in G
belongs to
{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*}
. Further, we have

(a)

p=0,q\in(0,1)
:
\underline{U}(\pi)=q
, which gives us
\sup\limits_{\pi\in G(a)}\underline{U}(\pi)=1.
Note that this supremum is not attained in class
G(a).

(b)

p=0,q=0
:
\underline{U}(\pi)=0
, which gives us
\sup\limits_{\pi\in G(b)}\underline{U}(\pi)=0.

(c)

p\in(0,1),q=0
:
\underline{U}(\pi)=-p
, which gives us
\sup\limits_{\pi\in G(c)}\underline{U}(\pi)=0.

Therefore, we can conclude that
\mathscr{U}_{1}^{*}=1
. Clearly, if a SES exists then it must belong to one of the four classes in
\{B,C,E,G(a)\}
. But we have seen that the supremum is not attained in any of these classes. Therefore, a SES does not exist for
\mathscr{U}_{1}
.

Next we compute
\mathscr{U}_{1}^{*}
using
\textbf{P}(\mathscr{U}_{1})
. Let
r=\mu(1|1)
and
s=\mu(2|2)
. Notice that by plugging in the values of utility in
\textbf{P}(\mathscr{U}_{1})
, the linear program is transformed to the following LP, where

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U}_{1}):\max_{(r,s)}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad s-r\quad\quad\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{s.t.}r\geq 1-s,0\leq r,s\leq 1.\end{gathered}

(33)

It is easy to see that
{\rm OPT}(\textbf{P}(\mathscr{U}_{1}))=1
which is attained under the unique solution
(\mu^{*}(1|1)=r=1,\mu^{*}(2|1)=1-r=0,\mu^{*}(1|2)=1-s=1,\mu^{*}(2|2)=s=0)
. Therefore,
{\rm OPT}(\textbf{P}(\mathscr{U}_{1}))=1=\mathscr{U}_{1}^{*}.

Clearly, the LP gives the
SGV
far more easily compared to the above calculations.

Another benefit of solving the LP is that using the optimal solution of the LP we can construct a
\varepsilon
-SES if no strategy of the sender attains the SGV (like this example). By glancing at our
\mu^{*}
, we know that the sender can benefit by making the receiver recover all symbols as
1
. Using
\mu^{*}
, we construct a sequence of
\varepsilon
-SES,
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k}
, with
\varepsilon=\frac{0.1}{k}
for every
\pi_{k}
, where
{\cal Y}(\pi_{k})=\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
and
\pi_{k}(y_{1}|1)=1,\pi_{k}(y_{1}|2)=1-\frac{0.1}{k},\pi_{k}(y_{2}|2)=\frac{0.1}{k}
. Notice that
\pi_{k}
belongs to class
E
for all
k
and thus
\pi_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{S}^{*},\forall k
. Hence,
\underline{U}(\pi_{k})=1-\frac{0.1}{k}
, implying
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\underline{U}(\pi_{k})\rightarrow 1=\mathscr{U}_{1}^{*}.
Similar
\varepsilon
-SES strategies can be constructed in classes
B,C
and
G(a)
above.

\square

3.2.2
Interpretation of
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})
as an assignment problem

The linear program
\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{U})
can also be viewed as an assignment problem
[
11
]
with additional constraints. Let
{\cal X}
denote the set of
q
tasks and also a set of
q
agents, where every agent is allocated
q
tasks across its working hours. Let
\mu
denote a probability distribution where
\mu(\bullet|x)
is the portion of working hours of agent
x
being allocated to perform the task
\bullet\in{\cal X}
. Another constraint imposed on a feasible allocation policy is that the portion of working hours of agent
x
allocated for performing the task
{\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}
can never exceed the portion of working hours of agent
{\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}
allocated on the task
{\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}
. This is precisely the trust constraint. A reward value of
\mu({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}|x)\mathscr{U}({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{},x)
is obtained if
x
allocates
\mu({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}|x)
towards
{\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}
, where
\mathscr{U}:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Thus, for any
\mathscr{U}
, the objective of this assignment problem is to find an allocation policy
\mu\in{\cal P}({\cal X}|{\cal X})
for every
x\in{\cal X}
which maximizes the expected reward, i.e., maximizes
\frac{1}{q}\sum\limits_{x,{\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}\in{\cal X}}\mu({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{}|x)\mathscr{U}({\widehat{x\mkern 2.8mu}\mkern-2.8mu}{},x).

We would like to emphasize that we are unaware of any assignment problem of this structure studied in the literature.

4
Informativeness of the sender

This section is dedicated to quantifying the minimum amount of information revealed by the sender at equilibrium. We call this quantity the
informativeness
of the utility function of the sender. We show that informativeness can be characterized by a linear program and that loss of information is imminent if there is a misalignment of interest between the players for at least one symbol. Additionally, we also show that informativeness has a linear relationship with the SGV for structured utility functions.

In our previous work
[
1
]
, we studied a similar setting where the players were constrained to play deterministic strategies.
We defined informativeness denoted by
{\cal I}(\mathscr{U})
as the minimum the number of symbols that are correctly recovered by the receiver in any equilibrium. We showed that
{\cal I}(\mathscr{U})
is given the
vertex clique cover number
of the strong sender graph graph defined below.

Definition 4.1

(Strong sender graph)

G_{s}(\mathscr{U})=({\cal X},E)
is the strong sender graph of a utility function of the sender
\mathscr{U}
where
(x,x^{\prime})\in E
if
\mathscr{U}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0
and
\mathscr{U}(x^{\prime}x)\geq 0
.

Thus, from
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
, we have

{\cal I}(\mathscr{U})=\theta_{v}(G_{s}(\mathscr{U})),

(34)

where
\theta_{v}(G)
is the vertex clique cover number of any graph
G
.

We define informativeness for our setting in the following way.

Definition 4.2

(Informativeness of the utility function) Let
\{\pi_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
denote a sequence of
\varepsilon
-SES such that
\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\underline{U}(\pi_{\varepsilon})=\mathscr{U}^{*}
. The informativeness of a utility function of the sender
\mathscr{U}
, denoted by
\mathfrak{I}(\mathscr{U})
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(\mathscr{U}):=\inf\limits_{\{\pi_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\cal R}(\pi_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon}),\text{where}\sigma_{\varepsilon}\in\mathscr{B}(\pi_{\varepsilon}).\text{\hskip-8.53581pt\hskip-8.53581pt}

(35)

We introduce the notion of an

Definition 5.1

Let

with respect to node

if

Let
M
denote any

of
{\cal G}

denote the weight of the matching
M

\nu({\cal G})

with the

First, we prove that the SGV of any utility function is lower bound by the maximum possible weight of a matching in the corresponding obfuscation graph.

Proposition 5.1

For any

\nu({\cal G}(\mathscr{U}))\leq\mathscr{U}^{*}.

: Let

for every feasible

5.1

In this section, we characterize the SGV for those utility functions whose obfuscation graphs are stars, chains, and cycles. First, we prove that if the obfuscation graph of a utility function is a star, then the SGV is equal to the sum of the weight of all the edges in the graph. For a chain

\mu^{\prime}

6
Conclusion

In any persuasion setting, it is natural to ask, what makes the receiver trust the sender’s suggestion? We addressed this question in our paper. We characterized the equilibrium strategies of the sender through a linear program with trust constraints. These constraints ensured that every feasible persuasion policy reveals enough information to persuade the receiver to pick a particular action. We found that revealing some true information is mandatory and quantified the minimum amount of information that needs to be revealed in any equilibrium using another linear program.
