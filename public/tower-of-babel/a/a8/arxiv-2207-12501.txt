Pinned Distance Sets Using Effective Dimension
D. M. Stull

Abstract
In this paper, we use algorithmic tools, effective dimension and Kolmogorov complexity, to study the fractal dimension of distance sets. We show that, for any analytic set E ⊆ ℝ 2 E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} of Hausdorff dimension strictly greater than one, the pinned distance set of E E , Δ x ​ E \Delta_{x}E , has Hausdorff dimension of at least 3 4 \frac{3}{4} , for all points x x outside a set of Hausdorff dimension at most one. This improves the best known bounds when the dimension of E E is close to one.



1
Introduction

The
distance set
of a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

\Delta E=\{\|x-y\|\mid x,y\in E\}
.

A famous conjecture of Erdös, the
distinct distances problem
, states that for finite sets, the size of
\Delta E
is nearly linear in terms of the size of
E
. Guth and Katz
[
7
]
, in a breakthrough paper, settled the distinct distances problem in the plane.

Falconer posed an analogous question for the case that
E
is infinite, known as Falconer’s
distance set problem
. When
E
is infinite, we measure its size using Hausdorff dimension. Falconer’s original conjecture is that, for any Borel set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\dim_{H}(E)>n/2
,
\Delta E
has positive measure. Even in the planar case, the distance problem is still open. However there has been substantial progress in the past few years. The current best known bound is due to Guth, Iosevich, Ou and Wang
[
6
]
, who proved that if a planar set has
\dim_{H}(E)>5/4
, then
\mu(\Delta E)>0
. In fact, they showed under this hypothesis, that the pinned distance set
\Delta_{x}
has positive measure, for many
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see below).

In addition to Falconer’s original question, exciting progress has been made on several variants of the problem
[
23
,
25
,
9
,
27
]
. One interesting direction is to prove lower bounds on the Hausdorff dimension of
\Delta E
. Another is to understand the Hausdorff dimension of
pinned distance sets
. If
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the pinned distance set is

\Delta_{x}E=\{\|x-y\|\mid y\in E\}
.

The conjecture for pinned distance sets is that, if
\dim_{H}(E)>n/2
, then for “most” points
x
,
\dim_{H}(\Delta_{x}E)=1
. Recently, there has been exciting progress on the dimensional variants of the distance set problem. For planar pinned distance sets, Liu
[
13
]
showed that, if
\dim_{H}(E)=s\in(1,5/4)
, then for many
x
,
\dim_{H}(\Delta_{x}E)\geq\frac{4}{3}s-\frac{2}{3}
. Shmerkin
[
26
]
improved this bound for small values of
s
. Specifically, he showed that, for any planar Borel set with
\dim_{H}(E)=s\in(1,1.04)
,
\dim_{H}(\Delta_{x}E)\geq 2/3+1/42\approx 0.6904
for many
x
. Furthermore, for the non-pinned version of the problem, Shmerkin proved that, if
\dim_{H}(E)\in(1,1.06)
, then
\dim_{H}(\Delta E)\geq 2/3+2/57\approx 0.7017
.

In this paper, we improve the bound on the dimension of pinned distance sets for small values of
\dim_{H}(E)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an analytic set with Hausdorff dimension strictly greater than one. Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
outside a set of Hausdorff dimension at most
1
,

\dim_{H}(\Delta_{x}E)\geq\frac{s}{4}+\frac{1}{2}
,

where
s=\dim_{H}(E)
.

To prove this theorem, we use
effective dimension
. Effective, i.e., algorithmic, dimensions were introduced
[
14
,
1
]
to study the amount of randomness of points in Euclidean space. The effective dimension,
\dim(x)
, of a Euclidean point
x
is a real value which measures the asymptotic density of information of
x
. Recent work has shown that algorithmic dimension is not only useful in effective settings, but, via the point-to-set principle, can be used to solve problems in geometric measure theory
[
17
,
19
,
18
,
28
,
29
]
.

Before giving the full details, we give a brief description of the main ideas involved in the proof of Theorem
1
. A main component of the proof is bounding the complexity of
orthogonal directions
, which may be of independent interest.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
, the projection of
x
onto
e
is
p_{e}x=e\cdot x
. The behavior of fractal dimensions under orthogonal projections is one of the most studied topics in geometric measure theory. Recently, orthogonal projections have been studied using effective methods
[
18
,
29
]
. In the language of effective dimension, if
e
is random relative to
x
, then we have tight bounds on the complexity of
p_{e}x
. However, for general directions, optimal bounds are unknown. In Section
3
, we improve known bounds on the complexity of
p_{e}x
for directions with high initial complexity (but which are not necessarily random everywhere).

The proof of our projection result relies on analyzing the small scale structure of the complexity function
K_{r}(x)
. Similar ideas have been used in the classical setting
[
26
,
9
]
. We give a more thorough overview of the proof of these results in Section
3
.

In the second part of the proof of Theorem
1
, we prove an analogous bound for the
effective
dimension of distances between
points
. This proof combines the projection theorem and a naive lower bound of the complexity of distances in a bootstrapping argument. We expand on the intuition behind this proof in Section
5
.

The final part of the proof of Theorem
1
is a reduction to its effective counterpart, Theorem
28
. We show that, if
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is analytic and
\dim_{H}(E)>1
, then for any
x
outside a set of Hausdorff dimension at most
1
, there is a
y\in E
so that
x,y
satisfy the conditions necessary for the effective theorem to hold.

This reduction relies on a result of Orponen on radial projections
[
24
]
. Orponen’s theorem has been used in much of the recent work on distance sets. We note that this result is the primary reason we require
\dim_{H}(E)>1
. We combine Orponen’s theorem with recent work on independence results in algorithmic information theory
[
29
]
to complete the proof of the reduction.

Preliminaries

2.1
Kolmogorov complexity and effective dimension

The
conditional Kolmogorov complexity
of binary string
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{*}
given a binary string
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{*}
is the length of the shortest program
\pi
that will output
\sigma
given
\tau
as input. Formally, the conditional Kolmogorov complexity of
\sigma
given
\tau
is

K(\sigma\mid\tau)=\min_{\pi\in\{0,1\}^{*}}\left\{\ell(\pi):U(\pi,\tau)=\sigma\right\}\,,

where
U
is a fixed universal prefix-free Turing machine and
\ell(\pi)
is the length of
\pi
. Any
\pi
that achieves this minimum is said to
testify
to, or be a
witness
to, the value
K(\sigma\mid\tau)
. The
Kolmogorov complexity
of a binary string
\sigma
is
K(\sigma)=K(\sigma\mid\lambda)
, where
\lambda
is the empty string. We can easily extend these definitions to other finite data objects, e.g., vectors in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, via standard binary encodings. See
[
12
]
for details.

The
Kolmogorov complexity
of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
at
precision

r\in\mathbb{N}
is the length of the shortest program
\pi
that outputs a
precision-
r
rational estimate for
x
. Formally, this is

K_{r}(x)=\min\left\{K(p)\,:\,p\in B_{2^{-r}}(x)\cap\mathbb{Q}^{m}\right\}\,,

where
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
denotes the open ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered on
x
. The
conditional Kolmogorov complexity
of
x
at precision
r
given
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
at precision
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

K_{r,s}(x\mid y)=\max\big\{\min\{K_{r}(p\mid q)\,:\,p\in B_{2^{-r}}(x)\cap\mathbb{Q}^{m}\}\,:\,q\in B_{2^{-s}}(y)\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n}\big\}\,.

When the precisions
r
and
s
are equal, we abbreviate
K_{r,r}(x\mid y)
by
K_{r}(x\mid y)
. As a matter of notational convenience, if we are given a non-integral positive real as a precision parameter, we will always round up to the next integer. Thus
K_{r}(x)
denotes
K_{\lceil r\rceil}(x)
whenever
r\in(0,\infty)
.

A simple, but useful property of Kolmogorov complexity is the following.

Lemma 2
.

(Case and J. Lutz
[
3
]
)

There is a constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n,r,s\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

K_{r}(x)\leq K_{r+s}(x)\leq K_{r}(x)+K(r)+ns+a_{s}+c\,,

where
a_{s}=K(s)+2\log(\lceil\frac{1}{2}\log n\rceil+s+3)+(\lceil\frac{1}{2}\log n\rceil+3)n+K(n)+2\log n
.

We may
relativize
the definitions in this section to an arbitrary oracle set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. We will frequently consider the complexity of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

relative to a point

y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e., relative to an oracle set
A_{y}
that encodes the binary expansion of
y
is a standard way. We then write
K^{y}_{r}(x)
for
K^{A_{y}}_{r}(x)
. Note that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

K_{s,r}(x\mid y)\geq K^{y}_{s}(x)-O(\log r)-O(\log s),

(1)

for every
s,r\in\mathbb{N}

One of the most useful properties of Kolmogorov complexity is that it obeys the
symmetry of information
. That is, for every
\sigma,\tau\in\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,

K(\sigma,\tau)=K(\sigma)+K(\tau\mid\sigma,K(\sigma))+O(1)\,.

We will need the following technical lemmas which show that versions of the symmetry of information hold for Kolmogorov complexity in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The first Lemma
3
was proved in our previous work
[
19
]
.

Lemma 3

(
[
19
]
)
.

For every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\geq s
,

(i)

\displaystyle|K_{r}(x\mid y)+K_{r}(y)-K_{r}(x,y)\big|\leq O(\log r)+O(\log\log\|y\|)\,.

(ii)

\displaystyle|K_{r,s}(x\mid x)+K_{s}(x)-K_{r}(x)|\leq O(\log r)+O(\log\log\|x\|)\,.

A consequence of Lemma
3
, is the following.

Lemma 4

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\varepsilon>0
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. If
K^{x}_{r}(z)\geq K_{r}(z)-O(\log r)
, then the following hold for all
s\leq r
.

(i)

\displaystyle K^{x}_{s}(z)\geq K_{s}(z)-O(\log r)\,.

(ii)

\displaystyle K_{s,r}(x\mid z)\geq K_{s}(x)-O(\log r)\,.

J. Lutz
[
14
]
initiated the study of effective dimensions (also known as
algorithmic dimensions
) by effectivizing Hausdorff dimension using betting strategies called
gales
, which generalize martingales. Mayordomo showed that effective Hausdorff dimension can be characterized using Kolmogorov complexity
[
22
]
. In this paper, we use this characterization as a definition.

The
effective Hausdorff dimension
of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

\dim(x)=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{K_{r}(x)}{r}\,.

2.2
The Point-to-Set Principle

The following
point-to-set principles
show that The Hausdorff dimension of a set can be characterized by the effective dimension of its individual points. The first point-to-set principle, for a restricted class of sets, was implicitly proven by J. Lutz
[
14
]
and Hitchcock
[
8
]
.

A set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
effectively compact
if the set of open rational covers of
E
is computably enumerable. We will use the fact that every compact set is effectively compact relative to some oracle.

Theorem 5

(
[
14
,
8
]
)
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be such that
E
is effectively compact relative to
A
. Then

\dimH(E)=\sup\limits_{x\in E}\dim^{A}(x)\,.

Remark
.

In fact this point-to-set principle can be generalized to
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
subsets, but we do not need the full generality.

J. Lutz and N. Lutz
[
15
]
improved this result to hold for
any
set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, at the cost of introducing an oracle.

Theorem 6

(Point-to-set principle
[
15
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\dimH(E)=\adjustlimits{\min}_{A\subseteq\mathbb{N}}{\sup}_{x\in E}\dim^{A}(x).

In order to apply Theorem
5
to the pinned distance sets, we need the following well known fact of computable analysis. We give a brief sketch of the proof for completeness.

Proposition 7
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact set and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be an oracle relative to which
E
is effectively compact. Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\Delta_{x}E
is effectively compact relative to
(x,A)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and denote the distance function from
x
by
f
:
f(y)=\|x-y\|
. Recall that an open set
O
is
computably open
if the set of open rational balls with rational centers contained in
O
is computably enumerable. It is clear that for any open rational interval
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
f^{-1}(a,b)
is computably open, relative to
x
.

Consider the following Turing machine
M
, which, given oracle
(x,A)
and given any finite set rational intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
as input does the following.
M
begins enumerating a set
\mathcal{B}^{i}=B^{i}_{1},B^{i}_{2},\ldots
of open, rational, balls whose union is
f^{-1}(I_{i})
. For each finite subset
S
of
\cup_{i}\mathcal{B}^{i}
,
M
checks if
S
covers
E
. Note that this is possible, since
E
is effectively compact relative to
A
. If it does,
M
accepts. Thus
\Delta_{x}E
is computably compact relative to
(x,A)
. Since
x
was arbitrary, the conclusion follows.
∎

2.3
Classical dimension

For every
E\subseteq[0,1)^{n}
, define the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff content at precision
r
by

h^{s}_{r}(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i}d(Q_{i})^{s}\,|\,\bigcup_{i}Q_{i}\text{ covers }E\text{ and }d(Q_{i})\leq 2^{-r}\right\}
,

where
d(Q)
is the diameter of ball
Q
. We define the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
E
by

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}h^{s}_{r}(E)
.

Remark
.

It is well-known that
\mathcal{H}^{s}
is a metric outer measure for every
s
.

The
Hausdorff dimension
of a set
E
is then defined by

\dim_{H}(E)=\inf\limits_{s}\{\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\infty\}=\sup\limits_{s}\{\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=0\}
.

We will make use of the following facts of geometric measure theory (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
,
[
2
]
).

Theorem 8
.

The following are true.

1.

Suppose
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact and satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)>0
. Then there is a compact subset
F\subseteq E
such that
0<\mathcal{H}^{s}(F)<\infty
.

2.

Every analytic set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
subset
F\subseteq E
such that
\dim_{H}(F)=\dim_{H}(E)
.

For two outer measures
\mu
and
\nu
,
\mu
is said to be
absolutely continuous with respect to

\nu
, denoted
\mu\ll\nu
, if
\mu(A)=0
for every set
A
for which
\nu(A)=0
.

2.4
Helpful lemmas

In this section, we recall several lemmas which were introduced by Lutz and Stull
[
19
,
18
]
and which will be used frequently throughout the paper.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
and
t,r\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that the set

N=\{w\in B_{2^{-t}}(x)\mid p_{e}w=p_{e}x\text{ and }K_{r}(w)\leq K_{r}(x)\}

was covered by
B_{2^{-r}}(x)
. Then, given a
2^{-r}
-approximation of
(p_{e}x,e)
and a
2^{-t}
-approximation of
x
, we can compute (an approximation of)
x
by simply enumerating all
w\in N
. The following lemma formalizes this intuition.

Lemma 9

(
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose that
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\eta,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
satisfy the following conditions.

(i)

K_{r}(z)\leq\eta r+\frac{\varepsilon r}{2}
.

(ii)

For every
w\in B_{2^{-t}}(z)
such that
p_{e}w=p_{e}z
,

K_{r}(w)\geq\eta r+\min\{\varepsilon r,r-s-\varepsilon r\}\,,

whenever
s=-\log\|z-w\|\leq r
.

Then for every oracle set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,

K_{r,r,t}^{A}(z\mid e,z)\leq K^{A}_{r,r,t}(p_{e}z\mid e,z)+3\varepsilon r+K(\varepsilon,\eta)+O(\log r)\,.

The next lemma shows that the precision to which we can compute
e
given
x,w
such that
p_{e}x=p_{e}w
depends linearly on the distance of
x
and
w
.

Lemma 10

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
e\in S^{1}
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
p_{e}z=p_{e}w
. Then

K_{r}(w)\geq K_{t}(z)+K_{r-t,r}(e\mid z)+O(\log r)\,,

where
t:=-\log\|z-w\|
.

We will commonly need to lower the complexity of points at specified positions. The following lemma shows that conditional complexity gives a convenient way to do this.

Lemma 11

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\eta\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there is an oracle
D=D(r,z,\eta)
with the following properties.

(i)

For every
t\leq r
,

K^{D}_{t}(z)=\min\{\eta r,K_{t}(z)\}+O(\log r)\,.

(ii)

For every
m,t\in\mathbb{N}
and

,

K^{D}_{t,r}(y\mid z)=K_{t,r}(y\mid z)+O(\log r)\,,

and

K_{t}^{z,D}(y)=K_{t}^{z}(y)+O(\log r)\,.

(iii)

If
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
satisfies
K^{B}_{r}(z)\geq K_{r}(z)-O(\log r)

K_{r}^{B,D}(z)\geq K_{r}^{D}(z)-O(\log r)\,.

(iv)

For every

,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n},w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

In particular, this oracle
D
encodes
\sigma
, the lexicographically first time-minimizing witness to

We will also need the following analog of Lemma
9
. The proof of this Lemma is nearly identical to that of Lemma
9
, however, we give the proof for completeness.

Lemma 12
.

Suppose that
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
t<r\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\eta,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}

(i)

K_{r}(y)\leq\left(\eta+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)r

(ii)

For every

where

K_{r,t}^{A,x}(y\mid y)\leq K^{A,x}_{r,t}(\|x-y\|\mid y)+3\varepsilon r+K(\varepsilon,\eta)+O(\log r)\,.

Suppose
x,y,r,\eta,\varepsilon

Define an oracle Turing machine

\displaystyle|\|x-y\|-\|x-p\||

\displaystyle\leq\|y-p\|

\displaystyle<2^{-r}.

Thus on input

Let

5
Effective dimension of distances

In this section, we prove the point-wise analog of the main theorem of this paper. That is, we prove the following.

Theorem 28
.

Suppose that
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
e=\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|}
,
d>1
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
satisfy the following.

(C1)

\dim^{A}(x)>1

(C2)

K^{x,A}_{r}(e)=r-O(\log r)
for all
r
.

(C3)

\dim^{A}(y)\geq d
.

(C4)

K^{x,A}_{r}(y)\geq K^{A}_{r}(y)-O(\log r)
for all sufficiently large
r
.

(C5)

K^{A}_{r}(e\mid y)=r-o(r)
for all
r
.

\dim^{x,A}(\|x-y\|)\geq\frac{d}{4}+\frac{1}{2}
.

Before giving the details, we briefly describe the overall strategy of the proof. We first note that the conditions (C1)-(C5) ensure that the conditions of Theorem
13
and Theorem
22
hold.

In Theorem
22
, we gave a naive lower bound on the complexity
K^{x}_{r}(\|x-y\|)
, namely, that

K^{x}_{r}(\|x-y\|)\gtrsim\frac{K_{r}(y)}{2}.

(35)

The key property is that it bounds the complexity of
\|x-y\|
as a function of the complexity of
y

at every precision
.

The bound (
35
) is not strong enough, in general, to imply Theorem
28
by itself. However, we can leverage (
35
)) to improve the bound on
K^{x}_{r}(\|x-y\|)
by combining (
35
)) with Theorem
13
. Informally, at precision
r
, we judiciously choose a precision
t<r
. Using (
35
)), we have

K^{x}_{t}(\|x-y\|)\gtrsim\frac{K_{t}(y)}{2}.

(36)

We use a separate argument (described below) which applies Theorem
13
to lower bound the complexity of the remaining bits of
\|x-y\|
, i.e., to bound
K^{x}_{r,t}(\|x-y\|)
. By choosing
t
to be the precision at which
K_{t}(y)=\frac{r}{2}
, we are able to conclude that

K^{x}_{r,t}(\|x-y\|)\geq\frac{r}{2}.

(37)

With this choice of

, (
36
) implies that
K^{x}_{t}(\|x-y\|)\geq\frac{r}{4}
. Combining this with (
37
) yields the desired bound.

We now briefly describe the argument using Theorem
13
. Informally, we prove that there are no points
z
close to
y
such that
\|x-z\|=\|x-y\|
. To do this, we use an

. That is, we prove if there were such a

then the complexity of
x
would be smaller than it actually is. To perform the incompressibility argument, we show that
x
is computable given
y,z
and few additional bits.

This relies on the following observation. Suppose that
\|x-z\|=\|x-y\|
, and let
w
be the midpoint
y
and

. Then
p_{e^{\prime}}w=p_{e^{\prime}}x
, where
e^{\prime}=\frac{z-y}{\|z-y\|}
. Thus, to compute
x
given
y,z
, we only need the information of
K(x\mid p_{e^{\prime}}x,e^{\prime})
. Bounding the complexity of
K(x\mid p_{e^{\prime}}x,e^{\prime})
is exactly the content of Theorem
13
.

We now formalize this intuition. We begin with the argument that
x
is computable given
y
and
z
.

Lemma 29
.

.

Proof.

Assume that

(53)

Let

\displaystyle K^{A}_{r}(x)+K^{A}_{r}(y)

\displaystyle=K^{A}_{r}(x)+K^{A}_{r}(y\,\mid\,x)+O(\log r)

\displaystyle=K^{A}_{r}(x,y)+O(\log r)

\displaystyle=K^{A}_{r}(x,y,e)+O(\log r)

Note that

\displaystyle K^{A}_{r}(y\,|\,e^{\bot},x)

\displaystyle\leq K^{A}_{r}(y\,|\,e^{\bot},p_{e^{\bot}}x)+O(\log r)

\displaystyle=K^{A}_{r}(y\,|\,e^{\bot})-r+o(r),

\displaystyle K^{A}_{r}(y)

16
]
.

Lemma 36
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be compact and
0<\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)<\infty
for some
s>1
. Let
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{s}|_{E}
. Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
A
is computably compact relative to
E
, and
\mu
is computable relative to
A
. Then the following hold.

1.

The set

N_{1}=\{e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}\,|\,(\exists^{\infty}r)\,K^{x,A}_{r}(e)<r-4\log r\}
.

has
\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}
measure
0
.

2.

The set

N_{2}=\{y\in E\mid(\exists^{\infty}r)\,K^{A,x}_{r}(y)<K^{A}_{r}(y)-8\log r\}

has
\mu
-measure
0
.

Proof.

We begin by proving (1). For every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, let

U_{r}=\{B_{2^{-r}}(q)\mid q\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}\text{ and }K^{A,x}(q)<r-4\log r\}
.

Then
|U_{r}|\leq 2^{r-4\log r}
, since there are at most
2^{r-4\log r}
programs of length less than
r-4\log r
. By definition, for every
R\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bigcup_{r>R}U_{r}
is an open cover of
N_{1}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\mathcal{S}^{1}}(N_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{r>R}\sum\limits_{B\in U_{r}}2^{-r}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{r>R}2^{r-4\log r}2^{-r}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r>R}\frac{1}{r^{4}},

and the conclusion follows.

The proof of (2) is similar to (1), but requires Levin’s coding theorem
[
10
,
11
]
, a deep result of algorithmic information theory. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{r}
be the set of all half-open dyadic cubes at precision
r
. Each dyadic cube
Q
can be uniquely identified with dyadic rational. Let
p_{\mu}:\mathbb{D}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the following discrete semimeasure defined on the dyadic rationals

p_{\mu}(d)=\frac{\mu(Q_{d})}{\mu(E)}
,

where
Q
is the unique dyadic half-open box centered at
d
. Given oracle access to
A
,
p_{\mu}
is lower semicomputable. Case and Lutz
[
3
]
, generalizing Levin’s coding theorem, showed that, for every precision
r
and every dyadic
d
of precision
r
,

2^{-K^{A}(d)+K^{A}(r)+c}>p_{\mu}(d),

(57)

for some fixed constant
c
.

With this machinery in place, the rest of the proof is similar to the argument for (1). For every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, let

W_{r}=\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r}\mid K^{A,x}(d_{Q})<K^{A}(d_{Q})-4\log r\}
.

Note that, by
[
4
]
,
K^{A,x}_{r}(y)=K^{A,x}_{r}(d_{Q})+4\log r
for every precision
r
, where
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{r}
is the dyadic cube at precision
r
containing
y
. Therefore, the set
\bigcup_{r>R}W_{r}
is a cover of
N_{2}
. Applying Kraft’s inequality and (
57
) we deduce that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\geq\sum\limits_{r>R}\sum\limits_{Q\in W_{r}}2^{-K^{A,x}(d_{Q})}

[Kraft inequality]

\displaystyle\geq\sum\limits_{r>R}\sum\limits_{Q\in W_{r}}2^{-K^{A}(d_{Q})+4\log r}

\displaystyle>\sum\limits_{r>R}\sum\limits_{Q\in W_{r}}2^{4\log r-K^{A}(r)-c}p_{\mu}(d_{Q})

\displaystyle\geq\sum\limits_{r>R}\sum\limits_{Q\in W_{r}}2^{2\log r-c}p_{\mu}(d_{Q})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r>R}\sum\limits_{Q\in W_{r}}2^{2\log r-c}\frac{\mu(Q_{d})}{\mu(E)}

\displaystyle>\frac{R}{2^{c}\mu(E)}\sum\limits_{r>R}\sum\limits_{Q\in W_{r}}\mu(Q_{d})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{R}{2^{c}\mu(E)}\mu(N_{2}),

and the conclusion follows.
∎
