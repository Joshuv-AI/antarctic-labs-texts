The tt*-Toda equations of A n A_{n} type
Martin A. Guest, Alexander R. Its, and Chang-Shou Lin

Abstract
. In previous articles we have studied the A n A_{n} tt*-Toda equations of Cecotti and Vafa, giving details mainly for n = 3 n=3 . Here we give a proof of the existence and uniqueness of global solutions for any n n , and a new treatment of their asymptotic data, monodromy data, and Stokes data.

1.
The equations

The tt*-Toda equations of
A_{n}
type are:

(1.1)

2(w_{i})_{{t\bar{t}}}=-e^{2(w_{i+1}-w_{i})}+e^{2(w_{i}-w_{i-1})},\ w_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\to\mathbb{R},\ i\in\mathbb{Z}

where the functions
w_{i}
are subject to the conditions

w_{i}=w_{i+n+1}
(periodicity),

w_{i}=w_{i}(|t|)

(radial condition),

w_{i}+w_{n-i}=0

(“anti-symmetry”).
These equations are a version of the 2D periodic Toda equations, and they first
appeared as formula (7.4) in the article
[
3
]
of Cecotti-Vafa.
We refer to that article (and our previous articles listed in the references) for background information and for some of the surpisingly deep relations with physics and geometry.
An important feature of (
1.1
) is that it is the Toda system associated
to a certain Lie algebra
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
(isomorphic to
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
), which
will be defined in the next section.

Results on the globally smooth solutions were given, mainly in the
case
n=3
, in our previous work
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
.
Results for the case
n=1
were given by McCoy-Tracy-Wu (see
[
5
]
),
and for the case
n=2
by Kitaev
[
16
]
,
and our method gives alternative proofs of these.

The methods of
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
extend in principle to the case of general
n
. Nevertheless it would be laborious to use exactly the same arguments. In the current article we give a new proof of the existence
and uniqueness of global solutions. We also give a more systematic description of the monodromy data and a more efficient application of the Riemann-Hilbert method. Many of these improvements are due to our (implicit) use of the Lie-theoretic point of view from
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
, although the proofs presented here can be understood without
knowledge of Lie theory.

The existence of a family of solutions parametrized by points of

\mathcal{A}=\{m\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\ |\ m_{i-1}-m_{i}\geq-1,m_{i}+m_{n-i}=0\},

can be established by p.d.e. methods
(cf. our previous articles
[
12
]
,
[
9
]
,
the Higgs bundle method of Mochizuki in
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
and the proof given here in section
10
).
However
p.d.e. methods provide only minimal information on the properties of the solutions. By using the
Riemann-Hilbert method we shall be able to give more detailed information.

We consider first the
solutions parametrized by points of the interior

\mathring{\mathcal{A}}=\{m\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\ |\ m_{i-1}-m_{i}>-1,m_{i}+m_{n-i}=0\}

of this region;
we refer to these as “generic solutions”. For such solutions we have:

(I) The asymptotics of
w_{0},\dots,w_{n}
at
t=0
:
for
0\leq i\leq n
,

w_{i}(x)=-m_{i}\log x-\tfrac{1}{2}\log l_{i}+o(1),\quad x\to 0

where

l_{i}=\textstyle{(n+1)}^{m_{n-i}-m_{i}}\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+1}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+1}}{n+1}\right)}\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+2}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+2}}{n+1}\right)}\cdots\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+n}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+n}}{n+1}\right)},

m^{\prime}_{i}=m_{i}-i
, and
x=|t|
;
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
we put
m^{\prime}_{i}=m^{\prime}_{i+n+1}+n+1
.
(For a subfamily of solutions
this formula was already known from
[
23
]

(II) The asymptotics of
w_{0},\dots,w_{n}
at
t=\infty

for
j=1,2,\dots,d
,

\displaystyle w_{0}(x)\sin j\tfrac{\pi}{n+1}

\displaystyle+w_{1}(x)\sin 3j\tfrac{\pi}{n+1}+\cdots+w_{d-1}(x)\sin(2d-1)j\tfrac{\pi}{n+1}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{n+1}{8}\ s_{j}\ (\pi L_{j}x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-2L_{j}x}+O(x^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-2L_{j}x}),\quad x\to\infty

where

L_{j}=2\sin\tfrac{j}{n+1}\pi
and we write

or

The real number

is the

\omega^{m_{0}+\frac{n}{2}},\omega^{m_{1}-1+\frac{n}{2}},\dots,\omega^{m_{n}-n+\frac{n}{2}}

\omega=e^{{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,}/{(n+1)}}

Thus we have

the significance of these linear equations will be explained later.

The explicit relation between the asymptotic data at
t=0
(the numbers
m_{i}
)
and the asymptotic data at

(the numbers

) constitutes a

(III) Explicit expressions for the monodromy data (Stokes matrices and connection matrices) of the
isomonodromic o.d.e. (

) associated to

.

This is a linear o.d.e. in a
complex variable
\lambda
whose coefficients depend on
w
(and hence on
t,\bar{t}
). It has poles of order
2
at
\lambda=0,\infty
.

The meaning of “isomonodromic” is that the monodromy data at these poles is independent of
t,\bar{t}
. Thus, to each solution
w_{0},\dots,w_{n}
of (
1.1
), we can assign a collection of
monodromy data.
We shall show in section
8
that the monodromy data associated to
w_{0},\dots,w_{n}
is equivalent to the “Stokes numbers”
s_{i}
which appear in the above formula for the
asymptotics at
t=\infty
.

In order to obtain these results on the global solutions, we make a thorough study of a wider class of
solutions, namely those defined on regions of the form
0<|t|<\epsilon
.
We refer
to such solutions as “smooth near zero”.
They are constructed from a “subsidiary o.d.e.” which
has poles of order
2,1
at
\lambda=0,\infty
.
For these solutions, the Stokes matrices are given in Theorem
6.12
and the connection matrices are given in Theorem
6.21
, Corollary
6.22
.

We show (Corollary
8.13
) that the global solutions can be characterized by a condition on the
connection matrices of the subsidiary o.d.e., which can be expressed most intrinsically as follows:

Corollary 1.1
.

A solution
w_{0},\dots,w_{n}
which is smooth near zero
is a global solution if and only if all Stokes matrices and connection matrices for the subsidiary o.d.e. lie in
the group
\textrm{SL}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
.

A more precise statement is given in Corollary
8.13
.
Our proof is indirect; it relies on explicit computations of
Stokes matrices and connection matrices.

We conclude with some comments on the
“non-generic global solutions”, i.e. those which correspond to the boundary
points of the region
\mathcal{A}
. It turns out that most of our methods apply equally well to this case.
We have restricted attention to the generic solutions in this article only because the construction of solutions near zero is easier in this case. Our argument can be summarized as follows. First we construct solutions which are smooth near zero.
Then we
use the Riemann-Hilbert method to extend the range of solutions towards infinity, and
make contact with the global solutions. To complete the
argument we use the p.d.e. results of section
10.1
. The results of section
10.2

are not actually needed at this point.

In fact it is possible to proceed in the other direction. Namely, we could start
with the p.d.e. results of section
10
, which apply to all global solutions, generic or non-generic. The
Riemann-Hilbert method of section
7
applies without change, taking
the monodromy data (Stokes matrices and connection matrices)
as an Ansatz, and it produces solutions which are smooth near infinity. The results of section
10.2
are now essential, in order
to connect the Stokes data with the asymptotic data at zero.

Arguing in
“reverse order” in this way, we see that the above statements (I), (II), (III)
hold also the non-generic case, except that (I) must be replaced by the following weaker statement:

\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{w_{i}(x)}{\log x}=-m_{i}.

Thus this argument alone would not produce the second
term in the asymptotics of
w
at zero. Nevertheless, it shows that
the Stokes matrices and connection matrices for the global solutions in the non-generic case are
given by exactly the same formulae as in the generic case.

To produce the second
term in the non-generic case, the “forward argument” (starting with solutions near
zero) has to be modified, and then the second term will be quite different from that in (I) above.
In the case
n=3
, these modifications can be found in
[
11
]
.

2.1.
The main connection form
\alpha

Equation (

d\alpha+\alpha\wedge\alpha=0

for the following connection form:

Definition 2.1

\alpha=(w_{t}+\tfrac{1}{\lambda}W^{T})dt+(-w_{{\bar{t}}}+\lambda W)d{\bar{t}},

where

w=\diag(w_{0},\dots,w_{n}),\ \ W=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}&\!e^{w_{1}\!-\!w_{0}}&&\\
\hline\cr&&\ \ddots&\\
\hline\cr&&&e^{w_{n}\!-\!w_{n\!-\!1}}\\
\hline\cr\vphantom{(w_{0})_{(w_{0})}^{(w_{0})}}\!\!e^{w_{0}\!-\!w_{n}}&&&\end{array}\right).

Here

denotes the transpose of

At this point we just take

Thus,

\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}_{n+1}\mathbb{C}

We shall make use of the following automorphisms.

\displaystyle\tau(X)

\displaystyle=d_{n+1}^{-1}Xd_{n+1}

\displaystyle\tau(V)

\displaystyle=d_{n+1}^{-1}Vd_{n+1}

\displaystyle\sigma(X)

\displaystyle=-\Delta\,X^{T}\Delta

\displaystyle\sigma(V)

\displaystyle=\Delta\,V^{-T}\,\Delta

\displaystyle c(X)

\displaystyle=\Delta\bar{X}\Delta

\displaystyle c(V)

Writing

\displaystyle=(L_{\mathbb{R}}G)^{-1}(L_{\mathbb{R}}G)_{t}dt+(L_{\mathbb{R}}G)^{-1}(L_{\mathbb{R}}G)_{\bar{t}}d{\bar{t}}

For future calculations it will be convenient to put

4.1.

As initial Stokes sector we take

\Omega^{(0)}_{k}=\{\zeta\in\tilde{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}\ |\ \theta^{(0)}_{k}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}<\text{arg}\zeta<\theta^{(0)}_{k-\scriptstyle\frac{1}{n+1}}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\}

Singular directions

independent Stokes factors, or

independent Stokes matrices.

From the symmetries of

at the end of section

obtain corresponding symmetries of

For this we use the formulation of

Our contour is

Lemma 7.6

For

Proof.

We have

On the ray

\displaystyle x^{2}\zeta(\omega^{i}-\omega^{j})

\displaystyle=-rx^{2}(\omega^{j}-\omega^{i})e^{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}}

\displaystyle=-rx^{2}(\omega^{j}-\omega^{i})e^{-{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,\arg(\omega^{j}-\omega^{i})}

\displaystyle=-rx^{2}|\omega^{j}-\omega^{i}|,

as
\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}=-\theta^{(0)}_{k}=-\arg(\omega^{j}-\omega^{i})
.
Thus,

e^{x^{2}\zeta d_{n+1}}\ e_{i,j}\ e^{-x^{2}\zeta d_{n+1}}=e^{-rx^{2}|\omega^{j}-\omega^{i}|}\,e_{i,j}

Similarly,

e^{\frac{1}{\zeta}d_{n+1}^{-1}}\ e_{i,j}\ e^{-\frac{1}{\zeta}d_{n+1}^{-1}}=e^{\frac{1}{\zeta}(\omega^{-i}-\omega^{-j})}e_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{r}(\omega^{i}-\omega^{j})e^{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{r}(\omega^{j}-\omega^{i})e^{-{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,\arg(\omega^{j}-\omega^{i})}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{r}|\omega^{j}-\omega^{i}|.

&&\ \ddots&&\\

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
m\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}
.
Let
s=\mu_{1}(m)
.
Then there exists exactly one
\hat{c}
with the
property
\mu(m,\hat{c})=(s,(1,\dots,1))
. We denote this by
\hat{c}^{\id}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
6.22
the condition
\mu_{2}(m,\hat{c})=(1,\dots,1)

means

\textstyle(\hat{c}_{i})^{2}=\scriptstyle{(n+1)}^{m_{n-i}-m_{i}}\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+1}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+1}}{n+1}\right)}\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+2}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+2}}{n+1}\right)}\cdots\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+n}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+n}}{n+1}\right)}.

This determines
\hat{c}_{i}
as all
\hat{c}_{i}
are positive.
∎

It follows that the monodromy data of
w^{\infty}_{s}
coincides with
the monodromy data of

w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}^{\id}}
if
m
corresponds to
s
, i.e.
s=\mu_{1}(m)
.
We are going to prove that, when
m\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}
, there exist global solutions which
coincide near
t=0
with
w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}^{\id}}
and near
t=\infty
with
w^{\infty}_{s}
.

8.3.
Global solutions and their monodromy data

When
m=0
we have
s=0
, and
this data corresponds to the trivial solution
w=0
of (
1.1
), which (obviously)
is a globally smooth solution.
The following result, which makes use of most of
the calculations in this article, extends this to an open neighbourhood of the trivial solution:

Theorem 8.5
.

There exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
0
in
\mu_{1}(\mathring{\mathcal{A}})
such that,
if
s\in V
, then the local solution
w^{\infty}_{s}
at
t=\infty
is in fact smooth on the interval
0<|t|<\infty
, i.e. is a globally smooth solution.

Proof.

The strategy of the proof is the same as that in section 5 of
[
10
]
, using
the “Vanishing Lemma”. Namely, we shall show that
the “homogeneous” Riemann-Hilbert problem,
in which the condition
Y|_{\zeta=\infty}=I
is replaced by the condition
Y|_{\zeta=\infty}=0
,
has only the trivial solution
Y\equiv 0
. It follows from this (Corollary 3.2 of
[
5
]
) that the original problem is solvable.

We shall work with a modified (but equivalent) Riemann-Hilbert problem on the contour consisting of
the two rays with arguments
\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
,
\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
(Figure
7
).
This contour divides
\mathbb{C}
into
two half-planes. We denote the upper region by
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and the lower region by
\mathbb{C}_{-}
. Explicitly,

\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\ |\ \tfrac{\pi}{2(n+1)}<\text{arg}\zeta<\pi+\tfrac{\pi}{2(n+1)}\}.

Our Riemann-Hilbert problem is motivated (in analogy with section
7.2
) by considering first the holomorphic functions

\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{\scriptstyle 3/2}
on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{5/2}
on
\mathbb{C}_{-}
. By

\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{3/2},\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{5/2}

we mean the functions defined on
\bar{\mathbb{C}}_{+},\bar{\mathbb{C}}_{-}

by the procedure of section
7.2
, i.e. they are obtained
from
\Psi^{(\infty)}_{3/2}
,
\Psi^{(\infty)}_{5/2}
by projecting to the reference sector

[-\frac{\pi}{n+1},2\pi-\frac{\pi}{n+1})
.

\mathbb{C}_{+}
(above heavy line) and
\mathbb{C}_{-}
(below heavy line).

Let us recall that the sectors

\Omega^{(\infty)}_{3/2}
,
\Omega^{(\infty)}_{5/2}
in
\tilde{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}

(which were used to define

\Psi^{(\infty)}_{3/2}
,
\Psi^{(\infty)}_{5/2}
)
were defined, respectively, by the conditions

0<\text{arg}\zeta<\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{n+1}
,

\pi<\text{arg}\zeta<2\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{n+1}

These are indicated by the heavy circular segments in Figure

It follows that
\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{3/2}(\zeta)=\Psi^{(\infty)}_{3/2}(\zeta)

for
\zeta\in\bar{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
. On the other hand
\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{5/2}(\zeta)=\Psi^{(\infty)}_{5/2}(\zeta)

only when

; for
{-\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\leq\arg\zeta\leq\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
we have
\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{5/2}(\zeta)=\Psi^{(\infty)}_{5/2}(e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,}\zeta)
.

We need the jump matrices on the rays which separate
\mathbb{C}_{+}
from
\mathbb{C}_{-}
.
For
\zeta
on the
\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray we have

\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{\scriptstyle\frac{3}{2}}(\zeta)=\Psi^{(\infty)}_{\scriptstyle\frac{3}{2}}(\zeta)

\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{\scriptstyle\frac{5}{2}}(\zeta)=\Psi^{(\infty)}_{\scriptstyle\frac{5}{2}}(\zeta)

so the jump matrix is

S^{(\infty)}_{3/2}

On the

\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{5/2}(\zeta)=\Psi^{(\infty)}_{5/2}(e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,}\zeta)

which is equal to

(

Next, we replace the

\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}
by

Y_{k}(\zeta)=(e^{w}\Omega^{-1})^{-1}\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}(\zeta)e(\zeta)^{-1}

as in Definition
7.3
,
where

e(\zeta)=e^{\frac{1}{\zeta}d_{n+1}^{-1}}e^{x^{2}\zeta d_{n+1}}

eS^{(\infty)}_{3/2}e^{-1}
,

eS^{(\infty)}_{5/2}e^{-1}

Motivated by this, we can now formulate our Riemann-Hilbert problem: to reconstruct (holomorphic) functions

Y_{3/2},Y_{5/2}
on
\bar{\mathbb{C}}_{+},\bar{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
such that the jump matrix on the

\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray is
eS^{(\infty)}_{3/2}e^{-1}
, and
the jump matrix on the
\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray is
eS^{(\infty)}_{5/2}e^{-1}
.

In order to apply the theory of
[
5
]
we must show that these jump matrices approach
I
exponentially as
\zeta\to\infty
along either ray. For this we need the following modification of Lemma

7.6
, which can be proved in exactly the same way:

Lemma 8.6
.

For
\zeta=re^{{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,(\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}+\alpha)}
, i.e. for
\zeta
in the
\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}+\alpha
ray
(
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

e(\zeta)\,e_{i,j}\,e(\zeta)^{-1}=e^{(-rx^{2}-\frac{1}{r})|\omega^{j}-\omega^{i}|\cos\alpha+{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,(-rx^{2}+\frac{1}{r})|\omega^{j}-\omega^{i}|\sin\alpha}\,e_{i,j}
.
∎

Let us consider now the behaviour of
eS^{(\infty)}_{3/2}e^{-1}
on the

\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray. We have

S^{(\infty)}_{\scriptstyle\frac{3}{2}}=Q^{(\infty)}_{\scriptstyle\frac{3}{2}}\dots Q^{(\infty)}_{1+\scriptstyle\frac{n}{n+1}}Q^{(\infty)}_{2}\dots Q^{(\infty)}_{{\scriptstyle\frac{3}{2}}+\scriptstyle\frac{n}{n+1}}

so it suffices show that

eQ^{(\infty)}_{k}e^{-1}
approaches
I
exponentially
on the
\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray
as
\zeta\to\infty
,
for each
k\in\{\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{n+1},\dots,\frac{3}{2}+\frac{n}{n+1}\}
.

Adjacent
\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}
rays are separated by
\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{n+1}
,
and the
\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray lies strictly between the

\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{1+\scriptstyle\frac{n}{n+1}}
and
\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{2}
rays. It follows that, if we write

\pi+{\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}}=\,\underline{\theta\!}\,^{(\infty)}_{k}+\alpha_{k}
, then we have

-\frac{\pi}{2}<\alpha_{k}<\frac{\pi}{2}

for each
k\in\{\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{n+1},\dots,\frac{3}{2}+\frac{n}{n+1}\}
.
As all

here, the lemma shows that all

approach

exponentially as
\zeta\to\infty
.

A similar argument applies to the jump matrix on the
\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray.

As in the case of the Riemann-Hilbert problem in section
7.3
, it follows that the Riemann-Hilbert problem
(with
Y|_{\zeta=\infty}=I
) is solvable when

x
is sufficiently large.
The Vanishing Lemma argument will allow us to establish solvability, not just for large

, but for all
x\in(0,\infty)
.

For this we consider the homogeneous Riemann-Hilbert problem, in which we require

Y|_{\zeta=\infty}=0
(in contrast to the Riemann-Hilbert problem based on the functions
\,\underline{\!\Psi\!}\,^{(\infty)}
,
for which we had

Y|_{\zeta=\infty}=I
).

For brevity let us denote the
\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray by
\Gamma_{1/2}
and the

\pi+\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}
ray by
\Gamma_{3/2}
, and the jump matrices
on these rays by
G_{1/2}
,
G_{3/2}
. If
Y^{0}_{5/2}
,
Y^{0}_{3/2}
solve
the homogeneous Riemann-Hilbert problem, we aim to find sufficient conditions which ensure
that
Y^{0}_{5/2}=0
,
Y^{0}_{3/2}=0
.

As in Proposition 5.1 of
[
10
]
, Cauchy’s Theorem implies that

(a)
\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}Y^{0}_{5/2}(\zeta)\ \overline{Y^{0}_{3/2}(\bar{\zeta}e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,/2n+2})}^{\,T}\ d\zeta=0

(b)
\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}Y^{0}_{3/2}(\zeta)\ \overline{Y^{0}_{5/2}(\bar{\zeta}e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,/2n+2})}^{\,T}\ d\zeta=0

where
\Gamma=\Gamma_{3/2}\cup\Gamma_{1/2}
. Let us add (a) and (b) together, and
use the fact that
Y^{0}_{5/2}=Y^{0}_{3/2}G_{3/2}
on
\Gamma_{3/2}
,
and
Y^{0}_{3/2}=Y^{0}_{5/2}G_{1/2}
on
\Gamma_{1/2}
. From the resulting equation
we deduce that, if

G_{3/2}(\zeta)+\overline{G_{3/2}(\bar{\zeta}e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,/2n+2})}^{\,T}>0
on
\Gamma_{3/2}
,
and

G_{1/2}(\zeta)+\overline{G_{1/2}(\bar{\zeta}e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,/2n+2})}^{\,T}>0
on
\Gamma_{1/2}
, then
Y^{0}_{5/2}
and
Y^{0}_{3/2}
must both
be identically zero.

For any
s
, and for
x
sufficiently large this criterion is satisfied, as
G_{1/2}
and
G_{3/2}

are then close to the identity, so we recover the fact stated earlier that the original
Riemann-Hilbert problem is solvable near
x=\infty
. For
s=0
the criterion is satisfied (for all

), as then
G_{1/2}=I=G_{3/2}
. Let us now consider the behaviour of

H_{3/2}=G_{3/2}(\zeta)+\overline{G_{3/2}(\bar{\zeta}e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,/2n+2})}^{\,T}

and

H_{1/2}=G_{1/2}(\zeta)+\overline{G_{1/2}(\bar{\zeta}e^{2\pi{\scriptscriptstyle\sqrt{-1}}\,/2n+2})}^{\,T}

when

is close to zero. A Hermitian matrix is positive definite if and only if
its minors are positive, and (in our situation, by the formula for
G
and the lemma above) the
minors are polynomials in variables

\lambda_{i,j}s_{i,j}
where
|\lambda_{i,j}|<1
for all
i,j
.

If all
\lambda_{i,j}s_{i,j}
are replaced by

(i.e.

is replaced by

), then
we obtain the matrices
S^{(\infty)}_{3/2}+\overline{S^{(\infty)}_{3/2}}^{T}

S^{(\infty)}_{5/2}+\overline{S^{(\infty)}_{5/2}}^{T}

These are positive definite when
s=0
, hence for all

V
of
s=0
.
As
|\lambda_{i,j}|<1
,
the original matrices
H_{3/2}
and
H_{1/2}
are also positive definite
for all

. This completes the proof of Theorem
8.5
.
∎

Remark 8.7
.

We chose the rays with arguments
\scriptstyle\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}

in the above proof for compatibility with the proof in

]

Corollary 8.8
.

For
s\in V
we have
w^{\infty}_{s}|_{(0,\epsilon)}=w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}^{\id}}
.

Proof.

For any
x\in(0,\epsilon)
, both
w^{\infty}_{s}|_{(0,\epsilon)}
and

w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}^{\id}}
are solutions to the same Riemann-Hilbert problem. Hence
they coincide.
∎

In the case

it is easy to verify that

.
In
[
10
]
, in the case
n=3
, the precise region

of positivity was calculated, and is a proper subset of
\mu_{1}(\mathring{\mathcal{A}})
.
For the purposes of this article it will be sufficient to know that

is a non-empty
open set, as we shall now appeal to p.d.e. theory to deduce that every point of

\mu_{1}(\mathring{\mathcal{A}})
corresponds to a global solution.

Theorem 8.9
.

There is a bijection between solutions
w
of the
tt^{*}
-Toda equations (
1.1
)
on
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
such that

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{w(|t|)}{\log|t|}=-m,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}w(|t|)=0

and points

.

The proof will be given in an appendix (section

).

Restricting attention to
\mathring{\mathcal{A}}
, it follows from the construction of section
2
that the solution corresponding to

in
Theorem
8.9
must have Stokes data
s=\mu_{1}(m)
, but we do not yet know the
connection matrix data. Theorem
8.5
allows us to obtain this:

Corollary 8.10
.

Let
m\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}
, and let

w_{m}^{\text{pde}}
be the corresponding solution given by Theorem

. Then
w_{m}^{\text{pde}}
must have

.

Proof.

For
s\in V
we know that
w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}^{\id}}
is a
global solution of the type in Theorem
8.9
, where

. By the bijectiveness property it

. Hence
w_{m}^{\text{pde}}
has

for all

. But

for all

.
∎

Strictly speaking, the corollary (and its proof) should have been phrased in terms of

rather than

exists for arbitrary solutions of (

), whereas we have defined

for

.

We conclude that the solutions

with

are precisely our solutions

8.4.
Summary of results

From the point of view of the family of local solutions
w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}}
constructed in
section
2
, we have obtained the following characterizations of global solutions:

Corollary 8.12
.

Let
m\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)
w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}}
is a globally smooth solution on
(0,\infty)
,

(2)
\hat{c}=\hat{c}^{\id}
.

(3) The connection matrix
E_{1}
satisfies
E_{1}=E_{1}^{\id}
, i.e.
e=(1,\dots,1)
.

(4) The connection matrix
D_{1}
satisfies

\Omega D_{1}t^{m}=\Delta(\overline{\Omega D_{1}t^{m}})\Delta
, i.e.
\Omega D_{1}t^{m}

belongs to the group

\textrm{SL}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (1),(2),(3) follows immediately from the discussion above.
The equivalence of (3) and (4) is a consequence of formula (
5.6
)
in Theorem
5.2
, i.e.

E_{1}=\tfrac{1}{n+1}D_{1}t^{m}\,\Delta\,(\overline{D_{1}t^{m}})^{-1}(\bar{Q}^{(0)}_{\frac{n}{n+1}})^{-1}d_{n+1}^{-1}\,C.

Namely, as in the proof of
Theorem
6.19
, this is equivalent to

E_{1}=D_{1}t^{m}\,\Delta\,(\overline{D_{1}t^{m}})^{-1}d_{n+1}^{-1}\,E_{1}^{\id}
, hence (3) is equivalent to

D_{1}t^{m}\,\Delta\,(\overline{D_{1}t^{m}})^{-1}d_{n+1}^{-1}=I.

Multiplying by
\Omega
we obtain

\Omega D_{1}t^{m}\,\Delta=\Omega d_{n+1}(\overline{D_{1}t^{m}})
.
As
\Omega d_{n+1}\bar{\Omega}^{-1}=\Delta
(cf. the formulae in
Appendix A of
[
10
]
), we obtain (4).
∎

The significance of
\Omega D_{k}t^{m}
is that it is the connection
matrix which relates the “undiagonalized form”
\Phi^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1}

of
\Phi^{(0)}_{k}
(see section
4.3
) to

\Phi^{(\infty)}
(see section
4.4
), namely we have the relation

\Phi^{(\infty)}=(\Phi^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1})(\Omega D_{k}t^{m}).

The Stokes factors based on
\Phi^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1}
are
\Omega Q^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1}
.
Using these, a further characterization can be obtained:

Corollary 8.13
.

w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}}
is a globally smooth solution on
(0,\infty)
if and only if
all Stokes factors
\Omega Q^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1}
and all connection matrices

\Omega D_{k}t^{m}
lie in
the group
\textrm{SL}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

From (4) of Corollary
8.12
, the condition

\Omega D_{1}t^{m}\in\textrm{SL}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
alone implies that

w^{0}_{m,\hat{c}}
is a global solution. Conversely,
we claim first that
\Omega Q^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1}\in\textrm{SL}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
for
all
m\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}
.
This means

\Delta\overline{\Omega Q^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1}}\Delta=\Omega Q^{(0)}_{k}\Omega^{-1}
. This
condition is equivalent to

d_{n+1}\overline{Q^{(0)}_{k}}d_{n+1}^{-1}=Q^{(0)}_{k}
, as
\Omega^{-1}\Delta\bar{\Omega}=d_{n+1}

(see the end of the proof of Corollary
8.12
).
By (
4.4
), this is equivalent to the reality of the Stokes factors
\tilde{Q}^{(0)}_{k}
,
which we have already established in section
6.2
. This completes the proof
of the claim. In view of the formula (
5.3
), which expresses all
D_{k}

in terms of
D_{1}
and the
Q^{(0)}_{k}
,
it follows that
\Omega D_{k}t^{m}\in\textrm{SL}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
for all
k
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary
8.13
is quite natural, as

\hat{\alpha}\left|{}_{|\lambda|=1}\right.
is
an
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}^{\Delta}_{n+1}\mathbb{R}
-valued connection form. However, we do not know a direct
proof of the above characterization which exploits this fact.

Collecting together the asymptotic results at
t=0

(Proposition
2.7
, Corollary
6.22
) and at
t=\infty

(Theorem
7.10
), we can now
give the asymptotic data of our global solutions:

Corollary 8.14
.

Let
m\in\mathring{\mathcal{A}}
. Let
w
denote the corresponding global solution. Then:

(1) The asymptotic data at
t=0
of
w
is given by

w_{i}=-m_{i}\log|t|-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(\hat{c}^{\id}_{i})^{2}+o(1),

where
(\hat{c}^{\id}_{i})^{2}=

\textstyle{(n+1)}^{m_{n-i}-m_{i}}\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+1}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+1}}{n+1}\right)}\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+2}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+2}}{n+1}\right)}\cdots\frac{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n-i}-m^{\prime}_{n-i+n}}{n+1}\right)}{{\displaystyle\Gamma}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{i}-m^{\prime}_{i+n}}{n+1}\right)}.

(2) The asymptotic data at
t=\infty
of
w
may be expressed as

\displaystyle w_{0}\sin p\tfrac{\pi}{n+1}+w_{1}

\displaystyle\sin 3p\tfrac{\pi}{n+1}+\cdots+w_{\frac{n-1}{2}}\sin np\tfrac{\pi}{n+1}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{n+1}{8}\ s_{p}\ (\pi L_{p}x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-2L_{p}x}+O(x^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-2L_{p}x})

where
L_{p}=2\sin\tfrac{p}{n+1}\pi
. Here

ranges over
1,2,\dots,\frac{n+1}{2}
,
so that we have
\frac{n+1}{2}
linear equations for

w_{0},\dots,w_{\frac{n-1}{2}}
.
∎

These formulae constitute a solution of the “connection problem”, i.e. they give a precise relation between the asymptotics at zero and the asymptotics at infinity.

Remark 10.1

Suppose

\lim\limits_{|x|\to+\infty}u_{i}(x)=0

\begin{cases}\Delta u_{1}=(a+1)u_{1}-u_{2}+O(|u|^{2}),\\
\hskip 20.00003pt\cdots\\

\hskip 20.00003pt\cdots\\

Since the matrix of coefficients on the right hand side
is positive definite, standard estimates (a straightforward application of the maximum principle) show that

First we establish the monotonicity of solutions of (

)-(
10.2
) with respect to

\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
u
and
v
be two solutions of (
10.1
)-(
10.2
)
with
\gamma^{(1)}=(\gamma_{1}^{(1)},\dots,\gamma_{n}^{(1)})
and
\gamma^{(2)}=(\gamma_{1}^{(2)},\dots,\gamma_{n}^{(2)})
. If
\gamma_{i}^{(1)}<\gamma_{i}^{(2)},1\leq i\leq n,
then
u(x)\geq v(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

We shall apply the maximum principle. We remark that, in the following argument, it is sufficient to assume that
u(x)
is a supersolution, i.e.,
\Delta u_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(u)
.

Step 1:

v_{1}\leq u_{1}
.
If not,
v_{1}>u_{1}
at some point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. By the assumption
\gamma^{(2)}_{1}<\gamma_{1}^{(1)},
the boundary conditions imply that
(v_{1}-u_{1})(x_{1})=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}(v_{1}-u_{1})(x)>0
for some
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. The maximum principle gives

e^{av_{1}(x_{1})}-e^{au_{1}(x_{1})}-(e^{(v_{2}-v_{1})(x_{1})}-e^{(u_{2}-u_{1})(x_{1})})\leq 0.

Since
e^{av_{1}(x_{1})}-e^{au_{1}(x_{1})}>0,
we obtain
e^{(v_{2}-v_{1})(x_{1})}-e^{(u_{2}-u_{1})(x_{1})}>0,
hence

(v_{2}-u_{2})(x_{1})>(v_{1}-u_{1})(x_{1})>0.

The assumption
\gamma^{(2)}_{2}<\gamma_{2}^{(1)}
again implies that

(v_{2}-u_{2})(x_{2})=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}(v_{2}-u_{2})(x)>0

for some
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.
Repeating this process for
1\leq i\leq n-1,
we find that there exists
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(v_{i}-u_{i})(x_{i})=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}(v_{i}-u_{i})(x)>0

and applying the maximum principle to the
i
-th equation at
x_{i}
yields

(v_{i+1}-u_{i+1})(x_{i})>(v_{i}-u_{i})(x_{i}).

For the maximum at
x_{i+1}
, we have

(v_{i+1}-u_{i+1})(x_{i+1})\geq(v_{i+1}-u_{i+1})(x_{i})>(v_{i}-u_{i})(x_{i}).

At
i=n
, we have, for some
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
,

(v_{n}-u_{n})(x_{n})=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}(v_{n}-u_{n})(x)>(v_{n-1}-u_{n-1})(x_{n-1})>0.

Applying the maximum principle to the n-th equation at
x_{n}
yields

e^{(v_{n}-v_{n-1})(x_{n})}-e^{(u_{n}-u_{n-1})(x_{n})}-(e^{-bv_{n}(x_{n})}-e^{-bu_{n}(x_{n})})\leq 0,

which implies
e^{-bv_{n}(x_{n})}-e^{-bu_{n}(x_{n})}>0
, and then
v_{n}(x_{n})<u_{n}(x_{n})
.
This contradicts
(v_{n}-u_{n})(x_{n})>0
.
Thus we have
v_{1}\geq u_{1}
.

Step 2:
Assume
v_{i-1}\leq u_{i-1}
. We want to prove that
v_{i}\leq u_{i}
. If not,
v_{i}>u_{i}
at some point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. By the assumption
\gamma_{i}^{(2)}>\gamma_{i}^{(1)}
, the boundary conditions imply that
(v_{i}-u_{i})(x_{i})=\max\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}(v_{i}-u_{i})(x)>0
for some
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. The maximum principle at
x_{i}
yields

e^{(v_{i}-v_{i-1})(x_{i})}-e^{(u_{i}-u_{i-1})(x_{i})}-(e^{(v_{i+1}-v_{i})(x_{i})}-e^{(u_{i+1}-u_{i})(x_{i})})\leq 0.

By the assumption
v_{i-1}\leq u_{i-1}
and
(v_{i}-u_{i})(x_{i})>0
, we have
(v_{i+1}-v_{i})(x_{i})-(u_{i+1}-u_{i})(x_{i})>0
, i.e.,
(v_{i+1}-u_{i+1})(x_{i})>(v_{i}-u_{i})(x_{i})

Since
v_{i}(x)\leq u_{i}(x)
for

we have
(\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nu)(x)\leq(\nabla v_{i}\cdot\nu)(x)
for

where

is the outward normal at

.
By integrating (
10.1
), we have

\begin{split}\int_{B_{R}}(e^{av_{1}(x)}-e^{-bv_{n}(x)})dx&=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nabla v_{i}\cdot\nu)(x)d\sigma-\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\pi\gamma_{i}\\
&\geq\int_{\partial B_{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nu)(x)d\sigma-\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\pi\gamma_{i}\\
&=\int_{B_{R}}(e^{au_{1}(x)}-e^{-bu_{n}(x)})dx.\end{split}

Then we have
u(x)\equiv v(x),
that is, uniqueness holds.
∎

We denote the solution in Lemma
10.3
by
u^{R}(x)
. From the uniqueness, it follows that
u^{R}(x)=u^{R}(|x|)
.
By studying

as

we shall prove our existence theorem for (

).

Theorem 10.4
.

Subject to conditions (
10.2
) and (
10.3
),
the system (
10.1
) has a unique solution on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

We first consider
\gamma_{i}
such that (
10.3
) holds strictly and also

\gamma_{i}<0
for all
i
.
We shall show that
u^{R}(x)
converges
for any sequence
R\to+\infty
.
Note that, since
\gamma_{i}<0
, we have
u_{i}^{R}(x)=u_{i}^{R}(|x|)\geq 0
by Lemma
10.2
,
and also that
u_{i}^{R}(x)
increases as
R
increases.

By integrating (
10.1
), we have

(10.6)

\begin{split}&R\frac{du_{1}^{R}}{dr}(R)-\gamma_{1}=\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{au_{1}^{R}(\rho)}-1)d\rho-\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{(u_{2}^{R}-u_{1}^{R})(\rho)}-1)d\rho\\
&R\frac{du_{i}^{R}}{dr}(R)-\gamma_{i}=\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{u_{i}^{R}-u_{i-1}^{R})(\rho)}-1)d\rho-\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{(u_{i+1}^{R}-u_{i}^{R})(\rho)}-1)d\rho\\
&R\frac{du_{n}^{R}}{dr}(R)-\gamma_{n}=\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{u_{n}^{R}-u_{n-1}^{R})(\rho)}-1)d\rho-\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{-bu_{n}^{R}(\rho)}-1)d\rho.\end{split}

Summing both sides, we have

(10.7)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{au_{1}^{R}(\rho)}-1)d\rho=\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{-bu_{n}^{R}(\rho)}-1)d\rho+R\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{du_{i}^{R}}{dr}(R)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}.\end{split}

Since
u_{i}^{R}(|x|)\geq 0
, we have
\frac{du_{i}^{R}}{dr}(R)\leq 0
.
Thus

\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{au_{1}^{R}(\rho)}-1)d\rho\leq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i},

which implies

is bounded, hence
u_{1}(x)=\lim\limits_{R\to+\infty}u_{1}^{R}(x)

exists. We obtain
also

\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(e^{au_{1}(\rho)}-1)d\rho\leq\lim\limits_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\rho(e^{au_{1}^{R}(\rho)}-1)d\rho\leq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}.

Further, (

) implies that

\int_{0}^{R}\rho(1-e^{-bu_{n}^{R}(\rho)})d\rho+R\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(-\tfrac{du_{i}^{R}}{dr}(R)\Big)

.
Since each term of the above is nonnegative,

R\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|\frac{d}{dr}u_{i}^{R}(R)|

are bounded as

. By
(
10.6
), we can prove by induction on

that

is bounded as

, and

\lim\limits_{R\to\infty}Ru_{i}^{R}(R)\to 0
.

Let

. Clearly

is a solution of (
10.1
),
and the integrals of

,
e^{u_{i+1}(x)-u_{i}(x)}-1

\mathbb{R}^{2}

From this, it is not difficult to see that

,

. By integrating (

) in a neighbourhood of

, we see that, for any
\epsilon>0
, there is a

\left|r\tfrac{du_{i}^{R}}{dr}(r)-\gamma_{i}\right|\leq\epsilon

,

.

Similar arguments work when

for all

, that is,

converges to

as

and the integrals of

because

satisfies (
10.1
) and the boundary condition (

).

For general
\gamma
such that (
10.3
) holds strictly, we can choose
\gamma^{(1)}
and
\gamma^{(2)}
such that

\gamma_{i}^{(2)}<\gamma_{i}<\gamma_{i}^{(1)}

\gamma_{i}^{(2)}<0,\gamma_{i}^{(1)}>0

Let

be the solution of (

. Then we have

Since

Since both

. The behaviour

) as before.

Finally, we consider a

which does not satisfy (

Theorem 10.5

If

We begin by establishing an identity which will be used in the proof of Lemma

Lemma 10.6
.

Suppose
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
is a smooth solution of (
10.1
) in
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then the following Pohozaev identity holds:

\displaystyle\tfrac{2}{a}\int_{\Omega}(e^{au_{1}}-1)dx+2\sum_{i=2}^{n}\int_{\Omega}(e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}-1)dx+\tfrac{2}{b}\int_{\Omega}(e^{-bu_{n}}-1)dx

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{a}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)(e^{au_{1}}-1)ds+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)(e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}-1)ds

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{b}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)(e^{-bu_{n}}-1)ds-\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nabla u_{i})\tfrac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial v}ds+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}ds.

Proof.

We multiply (
10.1
) by
x\cdot\nabla u_{i}
and then integrate over

\Omega
. Integration by parts gives the left hand term

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}(x\cdot\nabla u_{i})\Delta u_{i}dx\\
=&\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nabla u_{i})\tfrac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\nu}ds-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}dx-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}x\cdot\nabla(|\nabla u_{i}|^{2})dx\\
=&\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nabla u_{i})\tfrac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\nu}ds-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}ds.\end{split}

For the right hand terms, we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}(x\cdot\nabla u_{1})(e^{au_{1}}-e^{u_{2}-u_{1}})dx+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\int_{B_{r,1}}(x\cdot\nabla u_{i})(e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}-e^{u_{i+1}-u_{i}})dx\\
&+\int_{\Omega}(x\cdot\nabla u_{n})(e^{u_{n}-u_{n-1}}-e^{-bu_{n}})dx\\
=&\int_{\Omega}x\cdot\nabla u_{1}e^{au_{1}}dx+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\int_{\Omega}x\cdot(\nabla u_{i}-\nabla u_{i-1})e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}dx-\int_{\Omega}x\cdot\nabla u_{n}e^{-bu_{n}}dx\\
=&\tfrac{1}{a}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)(e^{au_{1}}-1)ds+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)(e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}-1)ds\\
&+\tfrac{1}{b}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu)(e^{-bu_{n}}-1)ds-\tfrac{2}{a}\int_{\Omega}(e^{au_{1}}-1)dx\\
&-2\sum_{i=2}^{n}\int_{\Omega}(e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}-1)dx-\tfrac{2}{b}\int_{\Omega}(e^{-bu_{n}}-1)dx.\end{split}

Equating these gives the identity stated in the lemma.
∎

Lemma 10.7
.

Assume that
u(x)
is a smooth radial solution of (

It follows that

Proof.

Step 1:
We shall prove that there exist
\beta_{i}>0
such that
|u_{i}(x)|\leq-\beta_{i}\log|x|
for small

.
Without loss of generality we take

. For any

For

are positive constants to be chosen later. For

On the other hand, for

\begin{split}&e^{aw_{1}}-e^{w_{2}-w_{1}}=\dfrac{1}{(r(r_{0}-r))^{a\beta_{1}}}-\dfrac{1}{(r(r_{0}-r))^{\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}}},\\
&e^{w_{i}-w_{i-1}}-e^{w_{i+1}-w_{i}}=\dfrac{1}{(r(r_{0}-r))^{\beta_{i}-\beta_{i-1}}}-\dfrac{1}{(r(r_{0}-r))^{\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i}}},\\
&e^{w_{n}-w_{n-1}}-e^{-bw_{n}}=\dfrac{1}{(r(r_{0}-r))^{\beta_{n}-\beta_{n-1}}}-\dfrac{1}{(r(r_{0}-r))^{-b\beta_{n}}}.\end{split}

If we choose

\begin{split}&\Delta w_{1}\leq e^{aw_{1}}-e^{w_{2}-w_{1}}\\
&\Delta w_{i}\leq e^{w_{i}-w_{i-1}}-e^{w_{i+1}-w_{i}},i=2,\dots,n-1,\\
&\Delta w_{n}\leq e^{w_{n}-w_{n-1}}-e^{-bw_{n}}.\end{split}

w_{i}(r)=+\infty,

or

for all

At

Step 2:

e^{au_{1}},e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}},e^{-bu_{n}}\in L^{1}(B_{1}),i=2,\dots,n
.
As in the proof of Lemma

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial B_{r,1}}(x\cdot\nabla u_{i})\tfrac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\nu}ds-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial B_{r,1}}(x\cdot\nu)|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}ds\\

\begin{split}&\tfrac{2}{a}\int_{B_{r,1}}e^{au_{1}}dx+2\sum_{i=2}^{n}\int_{B_{r,1}}e^{u_{i}-u_{i-1}}dx+\tfrac{2}{b}\int_{B_{r,1}}e^{-bu_{n}}dx\\

Step 3:
We prove that

a smooth harmonic function on

,

Lemma 10.8
.

Suppose
u(x)
is a smooth radial solution of (
10.1
) on

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Then

\lim\limits_{|x|\to+\infty}u(x)=0
.

Proof.

Step 1:
We prove that
u(x)
is bounded as
|x|\to+\infty
.
We construct a supersolution as in Lemma
10.7
. Choose any
a\beta_{1}>\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}>...>\beta_{n}-\beta_{n-1}\geq 2

v(x)=-\log(r-s_{1})(s_{2}-r),r=|x|

where
s_{2}>s_{1}>0

\frac{s_{2}-s_{1}}{2}=\alpha<1

\begin{split}\Delta v(x)&=\dfrac{s_{1}}{r(r-s_{1})^{2}}+\dfrac{s_{2}}{r(s_{2}-r)^{2}}\\
&=\big(\dfrac{s_{1}}{r}(s_{2}-r)^{2}+\dfrac{s_{2}}{r}(r-s_{1})^{2}\big)\big((r-s_{1})(s_{2}-r)\big)^{-2}.\end{split}

By noting that
(r-s_{1})(s_{2}-r)\leq(\frac{s_{2}-s_{1}}{2})^{2}=\alpha^{2}<1

\begin{split}&\Delta w_{1}\leq e^{aw_{1}}-e^{w_{2}-w_{1}},\\
&\Delta w_{i}\leq e^{w_{i}-w_{i-1}}-e^{w_{i+1}-w_{i}},i=2,...,n-1\\
&\Delta w_{n}\leq e^{w_{n}-w_{n-1}}-e^{-bw_{i}}\end{split}

where

\beta_{i}

In particular, if

and

Step 2:
We prove that
\lim\limits_{|x|\to+\infty}u_{i}(x)=0

Set

\overline{l}=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\{\overline{\lim}_{|x|\to+\infty}u_{i}(x)\}.

\overline{\lim}_{|x|\to+\infty}u_{i_{0}}(x)=\overline{l}

Since
u_{i}(x)
is bounded, standard p.d.e. estimates show that there is a subsequence (still denoted by

) of

such that

as

and such that

is a smooth solution of (

) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Further,
v
is bounded and

v_{i}(x)\leq v_{i_{0}}(0)=\overline{l},\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mbox{\text{ and }}1\leq i\leq n.

If
i_{0}=n
, by applying the maximum principle to
v_{n}
we obtain

-bv_{n}(0)\geq v_{n}(0)-v_{n-1}(0).

Thus

\overline{l}\geq v_{n-1}(0)\geq(1+b)v_{n}(0)=(1+b)\overline{l},

which implies
\overline{l}\leq 0
.

If
1<i_{0}<n
, by applying the maximum principle to
v_{i_{0}}
we obtain

v_{i_{0}+1}(0)-v_{i_{0}}(0)\geq v_{i_{0}}(0)-v_{i_{0}-1}(0)\geq 0.

Thus
v_{i_{0}+1}(0)\geq v_{i_{0}}(0)
. Hence
v_{i_{0}+1}(0)=v_{i_{0}}(0)
. Repeating this argument, we have
v_{i_{0}}(0)=\cdots=v_{n}(0)=\overline{l}
, which implies
\overline{l}\leq 0
.

If
i_{0}=1
, then again the maximum principle yields

\overline{l}\geq v_{2}(0)\geq(1+a)v_{1}(0)=(1+a)\overline{l},

and then we have
\overline{l}\leq 0
as well.

Applying this argument to
\hat{u}_{i}(x)=-u_{n+1-i}(x)
, we obtain

-\underline{\lim}_{|x|\to+\infty}u_{i}(x)=\overline{\lim}_{|x|\to+\infty}\hat{u}_{i}(x)\leq 0.

Thus
\underline{\lim}_{|x|\to+\infty}u_{n+1-i}(x)\geq 0
for all
i
. This implies
\lim\limits_{|x|\to+\infty}u_{i}(x)=0
. This proves Lemma
10.8
.
∎

Lemma
10.7
gives (i) and (ii) of Theorem
10.5
, and
Lemma
10.8
gives (iii). Thus we have completed the proof of Theorem
10.5
.
