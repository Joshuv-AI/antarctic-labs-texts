The random Weierstrass zeta function I. Existence, uniqueness, fluctuations
Mikhail Sodin, Aron Wennman, Oren Yakir

Abstract
We describe a construction of random meromorphic functions with prescribed simple poles with unit residues at a given stationary point process. We characterize those stationary processes with finite second moment for which, after subtracting the mean, the random function becomes stationary. These random meromorphic functions can be viewed as random analogues of the Weierstrass zeta function from the theory of elliptic functions, or equivalently as electric fields generated by an infinite random distribution of point charges.

Introduction and overview

Let
{\Lambda}
be a stationary random point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

d\geq 2
, and let

n_{\Lambda}=\sum_{{\lambda}\in{\Lambda}}\delta_{\lambda}

We take the probability space of

and

\big\{{\Lambda}\in\Omega:n_{\Lambda}(B)=k\big\},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\quad B\text{ Borel subset of }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Stationarity of

amounts to invariance of
\mathbb{P}
under translations, i.e. under the maps
T_{x}:\Omega\to\Omega
, where
T_{x}{\Lambda}={\Lambda}-x

Denote by

the (first) intensity of

Question 1
.

For which stationary point processes
{\Lambda}
does there exist a stationary random vector field

V_{\Lambda}
with
\operatorname{div}V_{\Lambda}=n_{\Lambda}-c_{\Lambda}m
in the sense of distributions?

Probably, the first instance of such a field is due to Chandrasekhar,

[

that, for the Poisson point process

in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
the stationary vector field

can be defined by the regularized series

V_{\Lambda}(x)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\,\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{x-{\lambda}}{|x-{\lambda}|^{3}}-\kappa c_{\Lambda}x,

where the summation is over
{\lambda}\in{\Lambda}
(here and elsewhere,
we skip
{\Lambda}
under the summation sign), and
\kappa=4\pi/3
is the
volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Chatterjee-Peled-Peres-Romik
[

gave the rigorous proof of this for the Poisson point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

d\geq 3
.

On the other hand, such a stationary field (with a very mild regularity) does not exist

but, probably, is not news for experts. Well-studied examples of stationary planar point

and the zero set of the Gaussian Entire Function

17
,
22
]

For the limiting Ginibre ensemble this also follows from Theorem

this is known to experts. For the zero set of the Gaussian Entire Function

the field

(F^{\prime}/F)(z)-\bar{z}

Plausibly, the stationary field exists for two- and three-dimensional Coulomb-type charged
systems studied by physicists and mathematicians; see the survey
papers
[
21
,
26
,
27

20

Question 2

For which stationary planar point processes
\Lambda

In this paper we will provide an answer to Question

for point processes with a finite second moment, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[n_{\Lambda}(B)^{2}]<\infty

In Section

One of our findings is Theorem

{\Lambda}
is ergodic, it states

(i)

The spectral condition

\displaystyle{\int_{|\xi|\leq 1}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}<\infty}

(ii)

The sum
\displaystyle{\sum_{{\lambda}\in{\Lambda},\,1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R}{\lambda}^{-1}}
converges

as

.

(iii)

For some random constant

\Psi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\Psi-\pi c_{\Lambda}\bar{z}

Moreover, any solution

The spectral condition (i) can be thought of as a

Before we proceed, a few words about definitions are in order.
By a stationary random vector field we mean a measurable map

Related work.

There is a certain resemblance between the questions studied here

In physics papers, Lebowitz-Martin
[

[

[
31
]
)

Wide-sense stationary point processes.

The main tool in the proofs of the most of our results
will be the spectral theory of generalized point processes,
developed in the 1950-ies by Itô, Gelfand, and Yaglom.
The proofs will not use the translation-invariance of the distribution
of the point process in its full strength, but rely
only on the translation-invariance of the mean and of
the correlations of the point process.
For this reason, with some obvious modifications,
the corresponding results remain valid
for wide-sense stationary point processes
{\Lambda}
.

2
The second-order structure of stationary point processes

2.1
The spectral measure

Let
{\Lambda}
be a stationary point process in
\mathbb{C}
with a finite second moment, that is, we assume that

\mathbb{E}[n_{\Lambda}(B)^{2}]<\infty

for any bounded Borel set
B
.

The spectral measure
of
{\Lambda}
is a non-negative locally finite measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
on

\mathbb{C}
such that the “Parseval formula” holds:

{\sf Cov}\bigl[n_{\Lambda}(\varphi),n_{\Lambda}(\psi)\bigr]=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\overline{\widehat{\psi}(\xi)}\,{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)=\langle\widehat{\varphi},\widehat{\psi}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho_{\Lambda})}\,,

(2.1)

where
\varphi,\psi\in\mathfrak{D}
,
n_{\Lambda}(\varphi)
denotes the linear statistic

n_{\Lambda}(\varphi)=\sum_{{\lambda}\in{\Lambda}}\varphi({\lambda})\,,

and
\widehat{\varphi}
,
\widehat{\psi}
are the Fourier transforms, i.e.,

\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\varphi(x)\,{\rm d}m(x)\,.

Existence of the spectral measure follows from a version of
the Bochner theorem, see Gelfand-Vilenkin
[
13
, Ch III, §3]
.
In the physics literature the spectral measure is commonly assumed to have a density,
known as the
the structure function
.

Similarly, one defines the spectral measure for stationary random measures,
as well as for generalized stationary
random processes (stationary random distributions).

It is also worth mentioning that the spectral measures of stationary point processes
(as well as of stationary random measures) are
always
translation-bounded

[
8
, Ch. 8]
, that is,
for every
r>0
,

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\rho_{\Lambda}\bigl(\{\xi\colon|\xi-z|\leq r\}\bigr)<\infty
.
Hence, for every
a>2
,

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{1+|\xi|^{a}}<\infty\,.

(2.2)

Remark 2.1
.

While the formula (
2.1
) initially holds for
\varphi,\psi\in\mathfrak{D}
,
it readily extends to more general test
functions (and even some tempered distributions) by taking
the closure in
L^{2}(\rho_{\Lambda})
. In fact, the Fourier image of
\mathfrak{D}

is dense in
L^{2}(\rho_{\Lambda})
.
This follows from the fact that
\mathfrak{D}
is dense in the space
\mathcal{S}

of Schwartz functions and that
the Fourier transform is
a topological isomorphism of
\mathcal{S}
. But in
\mathcal{S}
any
convergent sequence is bounded by
C(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-2}
, so applying
the bounded convergence theorem, we find that the relation
(
2.1
) holds for any pair of test functions
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}
.
To see that the Fourier image is dense in the full
L^{2}
-space,
it is sufficient to show that the closure of
\mathcal{S}
in
L^{2}(\rho_{\Lambda})

contains any bounded continuous
function
f\in L^{2}(\rho_{\Lambda})
with compact support.
But this is again a direct consequence of the translation-boundedness of

\rho_{\Lambda}
and the bounded convergence theorem applied to
f_{j}=f*h_{j}
, where

h_{j}(\xi)=j^{2}h(j\xi)
,
h\in\mathcal{S}
,
\int_{\mathbb{C}}h{\rm d}m=1
, as
j\to\infty
.

2.2
The reduced covariance measure

The spectral measure of a point process is the Fourier transform of “the reduced
covariance measure”
\kappa_{\Lambda}
, which is a signed measure on
\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle{\sf Cov}\bigl[n_{\Lambda}(\varphi),n_{\Lambda}(\psi)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)\overline{\psi(z^{\prime})}\,{\rm d}\kappa_{\Lambda}(z-z^{\prime})\,{\rm d}m(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\Bigl[\int_{\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)\overline{\psi(z-w)}\,{\rm d}m(z)\Bigr]\,{\rm d}\kappa_{\Lambda}(w)\,,

see Daley and Vere–Jones
[
8
, Ch. 8]
(their notation is slightly
different from the one we use here). Recalling that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[n_{\Lambda}(\varphi)n_{\Lambda}(\overline{\psi})\bigr]=\iint_{\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)\overline{\psi(z^{\prime})}\,{\rm d}\nu_{\Lambda}(z-z^{\prime})\,{\rm d}m(z)+c_{\Lambda}\,\int_{\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)\overline{\psi(z)}\,{\rm d}m(z)\,,

where
c_{\Lambda}
is the first intensity of the
point process
\Lambda
(i.e., the mean number of points
of
\Lambda
per unit area) and
\nu_{\Lambda}
is
the reduced two-point measure of
{\Lambda}
, we get
that
\kappa_{\Lambda}=\tau_{\Lambda}+c_{\Lambda}\delta_{0}
, where

\tau_{\Lambda}=\nu_{\Lambda}-c_{\Lambda}^{2}\,m
is the
(reduced) truncated two-point measure of
{\Lambda}
.
Note that in the physics literature it is tacitly assumed that
the measures
\nu_{\Lambda}
and
\tau_{\Lambda}
have
densities, called the two-point function and truncated
two-point function, respectively.

Similarly, the reduced covariance measure is defined for random
stationary processes and random stationary measures in
\mathbb{C}
.

The total variation of any reduced covariance measure
\kappa_{\Lambda}

(and therefore of the reduced truncated measure
\tau_{\Lambda}
) is
also translation-bounded
[
8
, Ch. 8]
.

It is worth mentioning
that for many point processes the tails
of the measure
\tau_{\Lambda}
decay very fast,
which means that on high frequencies the spectral
measure is close to the Lebesgue measure
c_{\Lambda}m
.
On low frequencies the behavior of the spectral measure
is governed by the Stillinger-Lovett sum rules, which control
the zeroth and the second moments of
\kappa_{\Lambda}

[
21
]
.

2.3
The conditional intensity of
{\Lambda}

We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\sf inv}\subset\mathcal{F}

the sigma-algebra of translation-invariant events in
\mathcal{F}
. The random
variable

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}=\pi^{-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[n_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{D})\big|\mathcal{F}_{\sf inv}\bigr]\,,

(2.3)

is known as the
conditional intensity
of
{\Lambda}
.
Clearly,
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}]=c_{\Lambda}
, the first intensity
of the point process.
Furthermore, by the ergodic theorem, one can show that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{n_{\Lambda}(R\,\mathbb{D})}{\pi R^{2}}=\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}

(2.4)

both almost surely and in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
(see
[
9
, Theorem 12.2.IV]
).
It is also not difficult to show (see
[
9
, Exercise 12.2.9]
) that

{\sf Var}[\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}]=\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\{0\})
. Putting these pieces together, we arrive at

{\sf Var}[\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}]=\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\{0\})=\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\Bigl[\frac{n_{\Lambda}(R\,\mathbb{D})}{\pi R^{2}}\Bigr]\,.

(2.5)

Hence, the random variable

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}
does not degenerate to the deterministic intensity
c_{\Lambda}

if and only if
n_{\Lambda}(R\,\mathbb{D})
asymptotically has the variance
of the maximal possible order
R^{4}
, i.e., “hyper-fluctuates”, and this in turn
is equivalent to
\rho_{\Lambda}(\{0\})>0
.
Note that if the point process
{\Lambda}
is ergodic,
then the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{\sf inv}
contains
only events of probability
0
or
1
,
and therefore,
\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}
is constant.

The simplest example of a point process with
a spectral measure having an atom at the origin
is a random mixture of two independent
Poisson processes having different intensities
(such processes are called Cox point processes).
For a more general construction, take
{\Lambda}
to be any ergodic point process with spectral
measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
, and denote by
L

a positive non-degenerate random variable with
{\sf Var}[L]<\infty
. Then

{\Lambda}^{\prime}=L^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\Lambda}
is a point process
with finite second moment.
In view of (
2.4
), we get that

\mathfrak{c}_{{\Lambda}^{\prime}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{n_{{\Lambda}^{\prime}}(R\,\mathbb{D})}{\pi R^{2}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{n_{{\Lambda}}(L^{\frac{1}{2}}R\,\mathbb{D})}{\pi R^{2}}=c_{\Lambda}L,

and hence (
2.5
) gives that
\rho_{{\Lambda}^{\prime}}(\{0\})=c_{\Lambda}^{2}\,{\sf Var}[L]
.

2.4
Examples

Here, we will make a short stop to present several examples of
stationary two-dimensional point processes, which
we kept in mind starting this work. For all these examples,
the spectral measure can be computed without much effort.

The Poisson point process.

In this case, the two-point function identically equals
c_{\Lambda}^{2}
(
c_{\Lambda}
is
the intensity of the Poisson process), the truncated two-point function vanishes,

\rho_{\Lambda}=c_{\Lambda}^{2}m
, and (
2.1
) is nothing but
the classical Parseval-Plancherel formula.

The limiting Ginibre ensemble.

This is the large
N
limit of
the eigenvalues of the Ginibre ensemble of
N\times N
random matrices with
independent standard complex Gaussian entries. One important feature of
the limiting ensemble is its determinantality, which means that its

k
-point functions can be expressed in terms of the determinants

r(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\pi^{-k}e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|z_{i}|^{2}}\det(e^{z_{i}\bar{z}_{j}})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\,,

see, for instance,
[
17
, Section 4.3.7]
.
This immediately yields the simple expression
-\pi^{-2}e^{-\pi|z|^{2}}

for the truncated two-point function and that
c_{\Lambda}=\pi^{-1}
, which, in turn, yields
that the spectral measure is absolutely continuous with the density

\pi^{-1}(1-e^{-\pi|\xi|^{2}})
.

Zeroes of the Gaussian Entire Function.

The Gaussian Entire Function (GEF, for short) is defined by the random Taylor series

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\zeta_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}

with independent standard complex Gaussian
coefficients
\zeta_{n}
. The most basic facts about
GEFs and their zeroes can be found in
[
17
,
22
]
.
The two-point function and the spectral measure of the zero point process
were explicitly computed by Forrester-Honner
[
11
]

and Nazarov-Sodin
[
23
]
. The intensity is given by
c_{\Lambda}=\pi^{-1}
,
the truncated two-point function
equals
h(|z|)
, where

h(r)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}r^{2}}\,r^{2}(\coth r-1)\,,

while the
spectral measure is absolutely continuous with the density

\pi^{3}|\xi|^{4}\,\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1}{\ell^{3}}\,e^{-\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}/\ell}\,.

“Stationarized” random Gaussian perturbation of the lattice.

This is a stationary point process defined as

{\Lambda}^{a}=\bigl\{\nu+\zeta^{a}_{\nu}+U\bigr\}_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
,
where
\zeta^{a}_{\nu}
are independent complex-valued
Gaussian random variables with the variance
a>0
,
and
U
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{2}
and is independent of all
\zeta^{a}_{\nu}
s.
In this case, the spectral measure is also not difficult to compute
(see, for instance, Yakir
[
35
, §3]
). It is a sum of an absolutely
continuous measure, which is similar to the one of the limiting Ginibre ensemble,
and a discrete measure with atoms at
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
,

\rho_{{\Lambda}^{a}}=(1-e^{-2a\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}})m+\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}e^{-2a\pi^{2}|\nu|^{2}}\delta_{\nu}\,.

Moreover, the reduced covariance measure
\kappa_{{\Lambda}^{a}}
is given by

\kappa_{{\Lambda}^{a}}=\delta_{0}-m+\sum_{\nu\neq 0}(2a\pi)^{-1}e^{-|z-\nu|^{2}/(2a)}m.

This can be obtained by a direct computation on the spatial side,
or by taking the inverse Fourier transform of
\rho_{{\Lambda}^{a}}
.
In the limit as
a\to 0
, we obtain the randomly shifted lattice

{\Lambda}=\bigl\{\nu+U\bigr\}_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
with the purely atomic spectral measure

\rho_{\Lambda}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\delta_{\nu}
.

3
Reciprocal sums over stationary point processes

3.1
Convergence of three series

Recall the notation
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
for the probability
space on which the point process
{\Lambda}
is defined.
To define the random Weierstrass zeta function,
we need three lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
{\Lambda}
be a stationary point process in
\mathbb{C}
having a
finite second moment. Then almost surely and in

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
,

\sum_{|{\lambda}|\geq 1}\frac{1}{|{\lambda}|^{3}}<\infty\,.

For
R>1
, we set

\Psi_{\ell}(R)=\sum_{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{\ell}}\,,\qquad\ell=1,2\,.

(3.1)

The behavior of these two sums as
R\to\infty
will be important for us.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\Psi_{2}(R)-\Psi_{2}(\infty)|^{2}\bigr]=0\,.

The convergence of
\Psi_{1}(R)

.

.

Let

,

It is worth mentioning that the conditional convergence of the

with

, when

3.2
Proof of the three lemmas

The
Bessel function of order
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is given by

J_{\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{{\rm i}(x\sin\theta-\nu\theta)}{\rm d}\theta.

We will frequently use two basic properties
of the Bessel function, namely the asymptotic
formulas

J_{\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{\nu!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{\nu}+o(x^{\nu}),\qquad\text{as }\,x\to 0,

(3.2)

and

\sup_{|x|\geq 1}|x|^{3/2}\left|J_{\nu}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\left(x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right|<\infty.

(3.3)

For the proof of both facts see, for instance,
[
32
, Ch.7]
.

3.2.1
Proof of Lemma
3.1

Denote by
\widetilde{\Psi}_{3}(R)=\sum_{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R}|{\lambda}|^{-3}
.
We will show that the limit
\widetilde{\Psi}_{3}(\infty)
exists both
almost surely and in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\Psi}_{3}(R)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\{1\leq|x|\leq R\}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\rm d}n_{\Lambda}(x)]}{|x|^{3}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}]\int_{\{1\leq|x|\leq R\}}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{|x|^{3}}\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s^{2}}<\infty\,,

which implies that
\widetilde{\Psi}_{3}(R)
converges almost surely,
as the sum consists of positive terms.

To show that
\widetilde{\Psi}_{3}(\infty)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
,
we use the Parseval identity
(
2.1
) to move to the spectral side, which gives

\displaystyle{\sf Var}\left[\widetilde{\Psi}_{3}(R)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\int_{\{1\leq|x|\leq R\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{|x|^{3}}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\int_{1}^{R}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}|\xi|t\cos\theta}{\rm d}\theta\right)\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{2}}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|2\pi\int_{1}^{R}\frac{J_{0}(2\pi|\xi|t)}{t^{2}}{\rm d}t\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi).

The integrand above is bounded uniformly in
R
. Thus, we need to check
how fast it decays as
|\xi|
becomes large. For this, we use the
asymptotic formula (
3.3
) for the Bessel function
and see that, for
|\xi|\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{1}^{R}\frac{J_{0}(2\pi|\xi|t)}{t^{2}}{\rm d}t\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{|\xi|^{3/2}}\int_{1}^{R}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{7/2}}+\frac{1}{|\xi|^{1/2}}\left|\int_{1}^{R}\frac{\cos\left(2\pi|\xi|t-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{t^{5/2}}\,{\rm d}t\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{|\xi|^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{|\xi|^{3/2}}\left[1+\frac{1}{R^{5/2}}+\int_{1}^{R}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{7/2}}\right].

Therefore,

\sup_{R\geq 1}\left|\int_{1}^{R}\frac{J_{0}(2\pi|\xi|t)}{t^{2}}{\rm d}t\right|^{2}\lesssim\min\{1,|\xi|^{-3}\}

and the function on the RHS is
{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}
-integrable.
Hence, we can apply the dominated convergence theorem and deduce that

\widetilde{\Psi}_{3}(R)
converge in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
as well.
∎

3.2.2
Proof of Lemma
3.2

Lemma
3.2
states that the random variables

\Psi_{2}(R)=\sum_{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R}{\lambda}^{-2}

converge in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
as
R\to\infty
to a limiting random
variable
\Psi_{2}(\infty)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
. This will follow once we
show that
\{\Psi_{2}(R)\}_{R\geq 1}
satisfies
the Cauchy criterion in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
,
which we do by a computation. For any
R\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\Psi_{2}(R)\right]=c_{\Lambda}\int_{\{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R\}}\frac{{\rm d}m({\lambda})}{{\lambda}^{2}}=0\,,

and by the Parseval formula (
2.1
), applied with

\varphi(x)=\displaystyle{x^{-2}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{R\leq|x|\leq R^{\prime}\}}

for any
R^{\prime}>R
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Psi_{2}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{2}(R)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\int_{\{R\leq|x|\leq R^{\prime}\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x^{2}}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}\left|\int_{\{R\leq|x|\leq R^{\prime}\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x^{2}}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi).

We may rewrite the inner integral on the RHS as

\displaystyle\int_{\{R\leq|x|\leq R^{\prime}\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{-2{\rm i}\chi}\int_{R}^{R^{\prime}}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}|\xi|r\cos\theta}e^{-2{\rm i}\theta}{\rm d}\theta\right)\frac{{\rm d}r}{r}

\displaystyle\qquad=-2\pi e^{-2{\rm i}\chi}\int_{R}^{R^{\prime}}\frac{J_{2}(2\pi|\xi|r)}{r}\,{\rm d}r

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=e^{-2{\rm i}\chi}\left(\frac{J_{1}(2\pi R^{\prime}|\xi|)}{R^{\prime}|\xi|}-\frac{J_{1}(2\pi R|\xi|)}{R|\xi|}\right)\,,

where
\chi=\arg\xi
, and where in the last equality we used
that
(J_{1}(x)/x)^{\prime}=-J_{2}(x)/x
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Psi_{2}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{2}(R)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}\left|\frac{J_{1}(2\pi R^{\prime}|\xi|)}{R^{\prime}|\xi|}-\frac{J_{1}(2\pi R|\xi|)}{R|\xi|}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi).

By the near-origin asymptotics
(
3.2
) of the Bessel functions,

J_{1}(x)/x
is
bounded near the origin, and, together with the asymptotic formula
(
3.3
) and
the translation-boundedness of
\rho_{\Lambda}
, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{R^{\prime}\geq R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Psi_{2}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{2}(R)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{R\to\infty}\left[\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\{0<|\xi|\leq R^{-1/2}\})+\int_{\{|\xi|\geq R^{-1/2}\}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{(1+R|\xi|)^{3}}\right]=0.

That is,
\Psi_{2}(R)
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.
∎

3.2.3
Proof of Lemma
3.3

We start by computing
\mathbb{E}[|\Psi_{1}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{1}(R)|^{2}]

for
R^{\prime}>R
. For any
R\geq 1
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\Psi_{1}(R)\right]=c_{\Lambda}\int_{\{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R\}}\frac{{\rm d}m({\lambda})}{{\lambda}}=0

and so, for any
R^{\prime}>R
, the Parseval formula (
2.1
) gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Psi_{1}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{1}(R)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\int_{\{R\leq|x|\leq R^{\prime}\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}\left|\int_{\{R\leq|x|\leq R^{\prime}\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi).

Since

e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}=e^{-\pi{\rm i}(\xi\bar{x}+\bar{\xi}x)}=\bar{\partial}_{x}\left(\frac{e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}}{-\pi{\rm i}\xi}\right),

we can use the Cauchy-Green formula to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\{R\leq|x|\leq R^{\prime}\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\xi}\left(\int_{\{|x|=R^{\prime}\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x}-\int_{\{|x|=R\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\xi}}{|\xi|}\frac{J_{0}(2\pi R^{\prime}|\xi|)-J_{0}(2\pi R|\xi|)}{\xi},

and plugging this into the above formula for

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Psi_{1}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{1}(R)\right|^{2}\right]
gives

{}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Psi_{1}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{1}(R)\right|^{2}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}\left(J_{0}(2\pi R^{\prime}|\xi|)-J_{0}(2\pi R|\xi|)\right)^{2}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}.

(3.4)

Since
J_{0}
is bounded, we can use (
3.4
)
and the asymptotic formula (
3.3
) for
J_{0}
to get

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{R^{\prime}\geq R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Psi_{1}(R^{\prime})-\Psi_{1}(R)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{R\to\infty}\left[\int_{\{0<|\xi|\leq R^{-1/4}\}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}+\frac{1}{R}\int_{\{|\xi|\geq R^{-1/4}\}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{3}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{R\to\infty}\left[R^{-3/4}\int_{\{0<|\xi|\leq 1\}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}+\frac{1}{R}\int_{\{|\xi|\geq 1\}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{3}}\right]=0.

That is,
\{\Psi_{1}(R)\}_{R\geq 1}
is Cauchy in

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.3
Translation properties of reciprocal sums

The random variables
\Psi_{\ell}(R)
are
defined by summation over annuli centered
at the origin. It will be essential to understand
the effect of translating the center of summation
in these sums. This amounts to understanding
the summation over the
lunar domains

formed as the symmetric difference of two large disks with different centers.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
{\Lambda}
be a stationary point process in
\mathbb{C}
with finite second moment,
with conditional intensity

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}=\pi^{-1}\mathbb{E}[n_{\Lambda}({\mathbb{D}})\mid\mathcal{F}_{{\sf inv}}]
.
Then, for any
u,v,z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{|{\lambda}-u|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-v|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}(\overline{u-v})\right]=0.

Although we will need this in the paper,
we remark that the convergence is locally uniform in
u,v,z
.

In the case when
{\Lambda}
is the Poisson point process, Lemma
3.4

was proved by Chatterjee, Peled, Peres and Romik in
[
7
, Lemma 8]

where they obtain the analogous result
for
d\geq 3
, but the same proof works also for
d=2
.

Proof.

By stationarity of
{\Lambda}
, it suffices to prove the lemma for
z=0
.
We will assume that
R
is large enough so that
both
u
and
v
are contained inside
R\mathbb{D}
.
By the Cauchy-Green formula it holds that

\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}=\pi\bar{u}
, and thus

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{|{\lambda}-u|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}\Big]=\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}]\left(\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}\right)=\pi c_{\Lambda}\,\bar{u}.

Introduce the notation

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{\lambda}-u|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-v|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\pi c_{\Lambda}(\overline{u-v})\,,\quad\text{and}\ \ Y=\pi\big(\mathfrak{c}_{{\Lambda}}-c_{\Lambda}\big).

Clearly,
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[X\,|\,Y]=Y(\overline{u-v})
, since the
law of the point process
{\Lambda}
, conditioned
on
Y
, has the intensity
\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\sf Var}\left[X-Y(\overline{u-v})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X-Y(\overline{u-v})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|X\right|^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left|Y(\overline{u-v})\right|^{2}\qquad\text{(Pythagoras' theorem)}

\displaystyle={\sf Var}[X]-\pi^{2}|u-v|^{2}\,{\sf Var}(\mathfrak{c}_{{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle={\sf Var}[X]-\pi^{2}|u-v|^{2}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\{0\}),\qquad\text{(by \eqref{eq:claim1})}.

Plugging in the definition of
X
and
Y
yields that

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\sf Var}&\left[\sum_{|{\lambda}-u|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-v|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}(\overline{u-v})\right]\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\left(\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}-\int_{\{|x-v|\leq R\}}\right)e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)-\pi^{2}|u-v|^{2}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\{0\})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}\left|\left(\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}-\int_{\{|x-v|\leq R\}}\right)e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)=I_{1}+I_{2}\,,\end{split}

(3.5)

where,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\int_{\{0<|\xi|\leq R^{-1/4}\}}\left|\left(\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}-\int_{\{|x-v|\leq R\}}\right)e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)\,,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\int_{\{|\xi|\geq R^{-1/4}\}}\left|\left(\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}-\int_{\{|x-v|\leq R\}}\right)e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)\,.

Since the integrand is bounded by some constant

C=C(u,v)>0
(independent of
R
), we can bound
I_{1}
as

I_{1}\lesssim_{u,v}\rho_{{\Lambda}}\left(\{0<|\xi|\leq R^{-1/4}\}\right)\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}0.

To bound the second integral
I_{2}
, we use the Cauchy-Green
formula to compute the inner integral:

\displaystyle\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\frac{{\rm d}m(x)}{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{-{\rm i}\pi\xi}\int_{\{|x-u|\leq R\}}\frac{\bar{\partial}_{x}\left(e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot x}\right)}{x}{\rm d}m(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{-{\rm i}\xi}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\{|z-u|=R\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}\frac{{\rm d}z}{z}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\xi}\int_{\{|z-u|=R\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}\frac{{\rm d}z}{z}-\frac{1}{{\rm i}\xi}.

Plugging back the above in the definition of
I_{2}
gives us

I_{2}=\int_{\{|\xi|\geq R^{-1/4}\}}\left|\frac{g_{u}^{R}(\xi)-g_{v}^{R}(\xi)}{2\pi\xi}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi),

where

g_{u}^{R}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\int_{\{|z-u|=R\}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot z}\frac{{\rm d}z}{z}=e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot u}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}|\xi|R\cos\theta}\frac{Re^{{\rm i}\theta}}{Re^{{\rm i}\theta}+u}{\rm d}\theta.

By the standard stationary phase bound
(see Proposition
A.1
in the Appendix),
for any
u\in\mathbb{C}
we have

\left|g_{u}^{R}(\xi)\right|\lesssim_{u}(1+R|\xi|)^{-1/2}
.
Hence, we see that

\displaystyle I_{2}\lesssim_{u,v}\int_{\{|\xi|\geq R^{-1/4}\}}

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}(1+R|\xi|)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\,\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\mathbb{D})+\frac{1}{R}\int_{\{|\xi|\geq 1\}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{3}}\xrightarrow{R\to\infty}0.

Plugging back the bounds on
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
into
(
3.5
), we get that

\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{|{\lambda}-u|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-v|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}(\overline{u-v})\right]\leq\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{R\to\infty}\left(I_{1}+I_{2}\right)=0

which gives the lemma.
∎

4
Fields and potentials with stationary increments

4.1
The Weierstrass zeta function

There is an evident analogy with
the classical Weierstrass zeta function
from the theory of elliptic functions.
Suppose for a moment that
{\Lambda}
is
a non-degenerate lattice in
\mathbb{C}
, then

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)=\frac{1}{z}+\sum_{{\lambda}\in{\Lambda}\setminus\{0\}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\frac{1}{{\lambda}}+\frac{z}{{\lambda}^{2}}\Bigr)\,.

In our context, it is more convenient to use a
different normalization, which goes back to Eisenstein.
Letting

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\,\sum_{0<|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}+\sum_{{\lambda}\in{\Lambda}\setminus\{0\}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\frac{1}{{\lambda}}+\frac{z}{{\lambda}^{2}}\Bigr)-\Psi_{2}z\,,

and noting that, for each
R
,

\sum_{0<|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}=0,

we find that

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}\,.

Let
c_{\Lambda}
be the inverse area of the fundamental domain of
{\Lambda}
. Then,
it is not difficult to show (see Taylor
[
30
, Appendix]
) that

the function
\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\pi c_{\Lambda}\bar{z}
is
{\Lambda}
-invariant
.
Note that in this case we have the limiting relation

c_{\Lambda}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{n_{\Lambda}(R\,\mathbb{D})}{\pi R^{2}}.

It is worth to mention that in
[
30
]
Taylor computed
the Fourier expansions of the functions
\zeta_{\Lambda}(z+a)-\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)
and

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\pi c_{\Lambda}\bar{z}
.

4.2
The random Weierstrass zeta function

We return to the probabilistic setting, and let
{\Lambda}
be a stationary
point process with finite second moment, and recall the quantities

\Psi_{1}(R)
and
\Psi_{2}(R)

defined in (
3.1
).
Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
in Section
3

allow us to define the random meromorphic function

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\sum_{|{\lambda}|<1}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\sum_{|{\lambda}|\geq 1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\frac{1}{{\lambda}}+\frac{z}{{\lambda}^{2}}\Bigr)-\Psi_{2}(\infty)z\,,

(4.1)

where
\Psi_{2}(\infty)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\Psi_{2}(R)
.
Note that, for any
R>1
,

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)=\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\Psi_{1}(R)+\bigl(\Psi_{2}(R)-\Psi_{2}(\infty)\bigr)z+\sum_{|{\lambda}|>R}\frac{z^{2}}{{\lambda}^{2}(z-{\lambda})}\,.

(4.2)

Again by Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, the last two terms on
the right-hand side of (
4.2
) tend to zero as
R\to\infty
,
where the convergence is in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
and is locally uniform in
z
.
This hints that the function
\zeta_{\Lambda}
is not too far from being a stationary one.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
{\Lambda}
be a stationary point process in
\mathbb{C}
having a finite second moment.
Then the random meromorphic function
\zeta_{\Lambda}
, as defined in (
4.1
),
has stationary increments. That is, for any
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
the distribution of the random meromorphic function

{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)=\zeta_{\Lambda}(z+a)-\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)
is stationary.

Proof.

We have

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)\\
=\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\Psi_{1}(R)+(\Psi_{2}(R)-\Psi_{2}(\infty))z+\chi_{R}({\Lambda},z)z^{2}\,,

where

\chi_{R}({\Lambda},z)=\sum_{|{\lambda}|>R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{2}(z-{\lambda})}.

Hence, by Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}-\Psi_{1}(R)\bigg|^{2}=0\,,

for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
fixed. The above,
together with the “lunar lemma”
(Lemma
3.4
), gives us

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\sum_{|{\lambda}-z|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{z}-\Psi_{1}(R)\bigg|^{2}=0\,,

(4.3)

and therefore,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\zeta_{{\Lambda}}(z+a)-\zeta_{{\Lambda}}(z)-\sum_{|{\lambda}-z-a|\leq R}\frac{1}{z+a-{\lambda}}+\sum_{|{\lambda}-z|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{a}\bigg|^{2}=0

for all
z,a\in\mathbb{C}
.

Since
{\Lambda}
is stationary and

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}=\mathfrak{c}_{T_{a}{\Lambda}}
,
for each
R\geq 1
the random functions

z\mapsto\sum_{|{\lambda}-z-a|\leq R}\frac{1}{z+a-{\lambda}}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-z|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}+\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{a}

are stationary. But then the limiting random function

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z+a)-\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)

is stationary as well (see
Claim
4.2
below,
which we record separately for later use).
In fact, the proof of the claim shows that

\zeta_{\Lambda}(\cdot+a)-\zeta_{\Lambda}(\cdot)=H_{a}(T_{z}{\Lambda})

(4.4)

where
H_{a}
is the
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
-limit

H_{a}({\Lambda})=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\Big(\frac{1}{a-{\lambda}}+\frac{1}{{\lambda}}\Big).

(4.5)

This completes the proof, modulo the proof of
Claim
4.2
.
∎

Claim 4.2
.

For
R>0
, let
f_{R,{\Lambda}}

be stationary random functions, and
assume that there exist a random function

f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}
such that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|f_{R,{\Lambda}}(z)-f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}(z)\right|^{2}\right]=0

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}
is stationary as well.

Proof.

By the definition of stationarity, there exist random variables
h_{R}

such that, for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
{\Lambda}\in\Omega
and for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have that

f_{R,{\Lambda}}(z)=h_{R}(T_{z}{\Lambda}).

By assumption, there exists another
random variable,
h_{\infty}
, such that

\mathbb{E}\left[|h_{R}({\Lambda})-h_{\infty}({\Lambda})|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|f_{R,{\Lambda}}(0)-h_{\infty}({\Lambda})|^{2}\right]\to 0,\qquad R\to\infty.

Moreover, by the invariance of
\mathbb{P}
we have
(h_{R}-h_{\infty})\circ T_{z}\to 0
as well.
We claim that for a.e.
{\Lambda}\in\Omega
and for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}(z)=h_{\infty}(T_{z}{\Lambda}).

(4.6)

Indeed, wherever
h_{\infty}
is defined,

\displaystyle f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}(z)-h_{\infty}(T_{z}{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}(z)-f_{R,{\Lambda}}(z)+f_{R,{\Lambda}}(z)-h_{R}(T_{z}{\Lambda})+h_{R}(T_{z}{\Lambda})-h_{\infty}(T_{z}{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}(z)-f_{R,{\Lambda}}(z)+h_{R}(T_{z}{\Lambda})-h_{\infty}(T_{z}{\Lambda}).

Since both
f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}(z)-f_{R,{\Lambda}}(z)
and
(h_{R}-h_{\infty})\circ T_{z}
tend to

0
in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
and since
R>0
was arbitrary, the right-hand side must vanish.
Consequently, the representation (
4.6
) for
f_{\infty,{\Lambda}}
follows.
∎

As a corollary to Theorem
4.1
, we observe that
the distribution of the random meromorphic function

\wp_{\Lambda}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}-\zeta^{\prime}_{\Lambda}(z)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\,\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{(z-{\lambda})^{2}}

is stationary (as above, the convergence is in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

and is locally uniform in
z
). To obtain an equivariant
representation of
\wp_{\Lambda}
similar to (
4.4
)
it suffices to note that

\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{2}}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-z|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{2}}\Big|^{2}\leq\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{R\to\infty}\frac{2}{(R-|z|)^{4}}\mathbb{E}\big|n_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{D}(0,R)\setminus\mathbb{D}(z,R)\big)\big|^{2}=0,

(4.7)

where the last equality follows from the crude bound

\mathbb{E}\big[n_{\Lambda}\big(\mathbb{D}(0,R)\setminus\mathbb{D}(z,R)\big)\big]^{2}\lesssim_{z}R^{2},

(4.8)

which in turn is obtained by a simple covering argument and the triangle inequality.
This yields the representation

\wp_{\Lambda}(z)=P(T_{z}{\Lambda})
, where

P({\Lambda})=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{2}}.

(4.9)

Looking ahead a little, we note that the representation (
4.2
) of
\zeta_{\Lambda}

suggests that existence of the

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
-limit

\lim_{R\to\infty}\Psi_{1}(R)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\,\sum_{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}

(4.10)

becomes equivalent to existence of a stationary vector field

V_{\Lambda}(z)
. In its turn, it appears that existence of the
limit (
4.10
) is easy to check on the spectral side.

5
The invariant field

5.1
Existence

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a stationary point process in

\mathbb{C}
having a finite second moment,
and denote by
\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}
the conditional
intensity of
{\Lambda}
on translation-invariant events.
Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

The spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}
satisfies

\int_{\{0<|\xi|\leq 1\}}\frac{{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)}{|\xi|^{2}}<\infty\,.

(b)

The sum

\Psi_{1}(R)=\sum_{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}

converges in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
to the limit
\Psi_{1}(\infty)
.

(c)

For some constant
\Psi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, the random field

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\Psi-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{z}

is stationary.

If any of the three conditions (a)–(c) hold,
we choose the particular normalization

V_{\Lambda}(z)=\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\Psi_{1}(\infty)-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{z}

(5.1)

for the stationary random field.

We remark that in Condition (b), it is in fact sufficient
to assume that
\Psi_{1}(R_{j})
is
convergent in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
along a sequence
R_{j}\to\infty
.
Indeed, one can show that this condition directly implies (a).
We will not pursue the details here.

Proof.

The implication (a)
\Rightarrow
(b) is exactly Lemma
3.3
.
It remains to prove the implications (b)
\Rightarrow
(c)
and (c)
\Rightarrow
(a).

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c).

We let
\Psi=\Psi_{1}(\infty)
and proceed to show that
V_{\Lambda}
, as given in
(
5.1
), is stationary.
By the relation (
4.3
) combined with
Condition (b) in the theorem, we get

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\,V_{\Lambda}(z)-\sum_{|{\lambda}-z|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}\,\bigg|^{2}=0\,.

Since
{\Lambda}
is stationary, the random functions

z\mapsto\sum_{|{\lambda}-z|\leq R}\frac{1}{z-{\lambda}}

are stationary for all
R\geq 1
.
The stationarity of
V_{\Lambda}
then follows from
Claim
4.2
.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(a).

First we prove that, for any
\varphi\in\mathfrak{D}
, the random variable

\zeta_{{\Lambda}}(\varphi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\zeta_{{\Lambda}}\varphi\,{\rm d}m

is in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
. Let
R
be large enough so that

\varphi
is supported in
\frac{1}{2}R\mathbb{D}
. Then

\zeta_{{\Lambda}}(\varphi)=\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}({\lambda})+\int_{\mathbb{C}}\chi_{R}({\Lambda},z)z^{2}\,\varphi(z)\,{\rm d}m(z)\\
\qquad+\Psi_{1}(R)\int_{\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)\,{\rm d}m(z)+\bigl(\Psi_{2}(R)-\Psi_{2}(\infty)\bigr)\int_{\mathbb{C}}z\,\varphi(z)\,{\rm d}m(z),

(5.2)

where
C_{\varphi}
is the Cauchy transform of
\varphi
, and

\chi_{R}({\Lambda},z)=\sum_{|{\lambda}|>R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{2}(z-{\lambda})}.

Since the Cauchy transform
\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}
is a bounded on
\mathbb{C}
,

\Big|\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}({\lambda})\Big|\lesssim_{\varphi}n_{{\Lambda}}(R\mathbb{D})
,
which implies that the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.2
)
belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.
By Lemma
3.1
, we know that

\sup_{z\in{\sf spt}(\varphi)}\mathbb{E}\left|\chi_{R}({\Lambda},z)\right|^{2}\leq 2\sum_{|{\lambda}|\geq R}\frac{1}{|{\lambda}|^{3}}<\infty

which implies that the second term in the sum is in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.
The random variable
\Psi_{1}(R)
satisfies the bound

|\Psi_{1}(R)|\leq\sum_{1\leq|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{|{\lambda}|}\leq n_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{D}(0,R)),

and the right-hand side has finite second moment by assumption,
so we conclude that the third term on the
right-hand side of (
5.2
)
also lies in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.
Finally, Lemma
3.2
tells us that the last
term on the right-hand side of (
5.2
)
is in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
as well,
and all together we get that
\zeta_{{\Lambda}}(\varphi)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.

Let now
\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)=\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\Psi-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{z}
.
Then
\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(\varphi)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})

for any test function
\varphi
, which implies that
\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}
has a spectral measure

\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}
. In view of
[
34
, Theorem 5]
, the identity

\bar{\partial}\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}=\pi(n_{\Lambda}-\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}m)
gives the relation

\rho_{{\Lambda}}=|\xi|^{2}\,\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}\quad\text{on }\,\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

(5.3)

Since
\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}
is locally finite, the result then follows
by solving for
\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}
in (
5.3
).
For the reader’s convenience, we sketch a proof of
(
5.3
).

Claim 5.2
.

Assume that
\mathcal{V}
is a stationary random function such that
for some random constant
c\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

\bar{\partial}\mathcal{V}=\pi(n_{\Lambda}-c\,m)

in the sense of distributions, and suppose
moreover that
\mathcal{V}(\varphi)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
for any
\varphi\in\mathfrak{D}
.
Then

\rho_{{\Lambda}}=|\xi|^{2}\,\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}\qquad\text{on }\,\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

(5.4)

Proof of Claim
5.2
.

Fix
\varphi\in\mathfrak{D}
.
Since
\bar{\partial}\mathcal{V}=\pi(n_{\Lambda}-cm)
, we have

\pi(n_{\Lambda}-cm)(\varphi)=-\mathcal{V}(\bar{\partial}\varphi),

whence,

{\sf Var}\big[(n_{\Lambda}-cm)(\varphi)\big]=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}{\sf Var}\big[\mathcal{V}(\bar{\partial}\varphi)\big].

Rewriting both sides in terms of the corresponding spectral measures
(note that the spectral measure of
n_{\Lambda}-cm
may differ from
\rho_{\Lambda}
by
at most an atom at the origin)
and using that

\widehat{\bar{\partial}\varphi}(\xi)=\pi{\rm i}\xi\,\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)
,
we get

\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}|\widehat{\varphi}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}}+a|\widehat{\varphi}(0)|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\xi|^{2}|\widehat{\varphi}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{{\Lambda}}},

(5.5)

for some constant
a
.
Next, we recall that the Fourier transforms of functions in
\mathfrak{D}
are dense
in the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}
(see Remark
2.1
).
For an arbitrary compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,
we approximate its indicator function
{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{K}
by a
uniformly bounded sequence
(\varphi_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}
,
converging to
{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{K}
pointwise. Passing to the limit in
(
5.5
), we get

\rho_{{\Lambda}}(K)=\int_{K}|\xi|^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{{\Lambda}}}(\xi),

which gives (
5.3
).
∎

With the proof of Claim
5.2
complete, we are done with the
proof of Theorem
5.1
.
∎

Let us note
that the possible atom at the origin of the spectral measure

\rho_{\Lambda}
is irrelevant for the spectral condition (a).

The spectral measures of the zero process of GEFs, of the limiting Ginibre ensemble and
of the stationarized random perturbation of the lattice satisfy spectral condition (a).
Hence, for these point processes, the generalized random function
V_{\Lambda}
is stationary,
while for the Poisson process it only has stationary increments.
All this can be proved directly for each of these processes.
Theorem
5.1
provides us with a unified reason for this phenomenon.

Remark 5.3
.

The following set of conditions on the reduced covariance measure
\kappa_{\Lambda}
yields
the spectral condition (a) in Theorem
5.1
:

(
\kappa_{1}
)

existence of the
1
st moment:
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}|s|\,{\rm d}|\kappa_{\Lambda}|(s)<\infty
;

(
\kappa_{2}
)

the zeroth sum-rule:
\kappa_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{C})=0
.

Indeed, existence of the first moment of
\kappa_{\Lambda}
yields that the spectral measure
\rho_{\Lambda}

is absolutely continuous with a non-negative
C^{1}
-smooth density
h
.
By condition (
\kappa_{2}
),
h
vanishes at the origin. Since
h
is
continuously differentiable, we conclude that
h(\xi)=O(|\xi|)
as
\xi\to 0
, which yields
the spectral condition (a).

The zeroth sum-rule (
\kappa_{2}
) is known to imply suppressed fluctuations of

n_{\Lambda}
(see, for instance,
[
21
, Section 1C]
). Ghosh and Lebowitz

[
12
]
observed that the combination of
(\kappa_{2})

with a stronger than
(\kappa_{1})
decay of correlations
yields an interesting geometric property of
{\Lambda}
known as number-rigidity.

5.2
Uniqueness

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}
be a generalized random function satisfying the following properties:

(
\alpha
)
It is stationary.

(
\beta
)
There exists a random constant
c
such that

\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)+\pi c\bar{z}
is meromorphic with with poles exactly at
\Lambda
,
all simple and with unit residue.

(
\gamma
)
For any test function
\varphi\in\mathfrak{D}
,
the random variable

\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}\varphi\,{\rm d}m

lies in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.

Then the spectral condition
(a)
of Theorem
5.1
holds, and the
random fields
\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}
and
V_{\Lambda}

differ by a constant in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
which is measurable with respect
to
\mathcal{F}_{\text{\sf inv}}
, the sigma-algebra of translation invariant events.

Proof.

By Claim
5.2
, the spectral measure
\rho_{\mathcal{V}}
of
\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}

agrees with
|\xi|^{-2}\rho_{\Lambda}
outside the origin, and hence the spectral
condition (a) in Theorem
5.1
holds.
We may therefore speak about the random function
V_{\Lambda}
, and we have

{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}(\xi){\rm d}\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\xi)={1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}(\xi){\rm d}\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}(\xi).

We next observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg|\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\widehat{\nu}_{R}|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\widehat{\nu}_{R}|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\xi)+\big(\rho_{\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})-\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})\big)|\widehat{\nu}_{R}(0)|^{2}\,,

where
\nu_{R}
is the current of integration
\nu_{R}(f):=\int_{\{|z|=R\}}f(z){\rm d}z

with respect to the differential
{\rm d}z

along
|z|=R
. Hence

\widehat{\nu}_{R}(\xi)=\int_{\{|z|=R\}}e^{-2\pi i\xi\cdot z}{\rm d}z,

so it follows that
\widehat{\nu}_{R}(0)=0
. As a consequence, the atom at the
origin does not matter, so by
repeating the same calculation backwards we arrive at

\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z\bigg|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\int_{\{|z|=R\}}V_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z\bigg|^{2}.

Because
\int_{|z|=R}\bar{z}\,{\rm d}z=2\pi{\rm i}R^{2}
,
the residue theorem gives us that

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)\,{\rm d}z=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}(\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)+\pi c\bar{z})\,{\rm d}z-\pi cR^{2}=n_{{\Lambda}}(R\mathbb{D})-\pi cR^{2}

(5.6)

and the same holds with
\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}
replaced by
V_{\Lambda}

and
c
replaced by
\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}
. But then

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{n_{\Lambda}(R\mathbb{D})}{R^{2}}-\pi c\,\Big|^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{n_{\Lambda}(R\mathbb{D})}{R^{2}}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\Big|^{2}\Big],

and juxtaposing this identity with the fact that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{n_{\Lambda}(R\mathbb{D})}{R^{2}}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\Big|^{2}\Big]=0,

(5.7)

we get that
c=\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}
, almost surely.

Let
G_{\Lambda}=V_{\Lambda}-\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}
, so that

G_{\Lambda}
is a stationary random entire function.
If we choose the test-function
\varphi
to be radial with total integral 1, then

\displaystyle\left(V_{\Lambda}-\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}\right)(T_{z}\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z+w)G_{\Lambda}(w)\,{\rm d}m(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r)r\int_{0}^{2\pi}G_{\Lambda}(z+re^{i\theta})\,{\rm d}\theta\,{\rm d}r

\displaystyle=2\pi\,G_{\Lambda}(z)\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r)r\,{\rm d}r=2\pi\,G_{\Lambda}(z),

where we used the mean value property of holomorphic functions to
arrive at the second equality. The LHS is the
difference of two random variables in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
,
and the variance of each term is independent of
z
.
Hence
G_{\Lambda}
is a random entire function with

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{E}\,|G_{\Lambda}(z)|^{2}<\infty.

(5.8)

Armed with this, we get the bound

\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{|G_{\Lambda}(z)|}{1+|z|^{5/2}}\,{\rm d}m(z)\bigg|\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}t}{t^{3/2}}<\infty,

which, together with positivity, implies that the random variable

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{|G_{\Lambda}(z)|}{1+|z|^{5/2}}\,{\rm d}m(z)

is finite almost surely. The mean value property implies that

\displaystyle|G_{\Lambda}(\zeta)|\leq\frac{1}{\pi|\zeta|^{2}}\int_{\{|z-\zeta|\leq|\zeta|\}}

\displaystyle|G_{\Lambda}(z)|\,{\rm d}m(z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{|\zeta|^{5/2}}{|\zeta|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{|G_{\Lambda}(z)|}{1+|z|^{5/2}}{\rm d}m(z)\lesssim|\zeta|^{1/2}

for all
|\zeta|\geq 1
, so in view of Liouville’s
theorem
G_{\Lambda}
is almost surely constant.

Finally, to see that
G_{\Lambda}(0)
is measurable with
respect to
\mathcal{F}_{\sf{inv}}
, we note that

G_{\Lambda}(0)=\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}\int_{\{|z|\leq R\}}\Big(V_{\Lambda}(z)-\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)\Big)\,{\rm d}m(z)

for all
R\geq 1
. By Wiener’s ergodic theorem

[
3
, Theorem 3]
, we get that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\,\bigg|\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}\int_{\{|z|\leq R\}}\Big(\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(z)-V_{\Lambda}(z)\Big)\,{\rm d}m(z)-\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{V}_{\Lambda}(0)-V_{\Lambda}(0)\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sf{inv}}\big]\bigg|=0\,,

which immediately implies that

G_{\Lambda}(0)=\mathbb{E}\big[G_{\Lambda}(0)\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sf{inv}}\big]
in
L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.
That is,
G_{\Lambda}(0)
is measurable with respect
to
\mathcal{F}_{\sf{inv}}
, and we are done.
∎

.

5.3
Fluctuations

The relations

\partial_{\bar{z}}V_{\Lambda}=\pi(n_{\Lambda}-\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda})
and

\partial_{z}V_{\Lambda}=-\wp_{\Lambda}

allow one to readily relate the spectral
measures and the reduced covariance measures
of these functions to the ones of the point process
{\Lambda}
.

5.3.1
The functions
\mathsf{\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}

and
\wp_{\Lambda}

Here we only assume that
{\Lambda}
is a stationary random
planar point process having finite second moment.
Then, by Theorem
4.1
, the random meromorphic functions

\mathsf{\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)=\zeta_{\Lambda}(z+a)-\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)
,
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
and

\wp_{\Lambda}(z)=\lim_{a\to 0}\frac{1}{a}\,\mathsf{\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)

are stationary. Since their second moments are infinite pointwise, we treat them
as generalized stationary random processes on the space
\mathfrak{D}

of test-function by

\mathsf{\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}(\varphi)=\zeta_{\Lambda}(T_{-a}\varphi-\varphi)
,
where
T_{w}\varphi(z)=\varphi(z+w)
, and by

\wp_{\Lambda}(\varphi)=-(\partial_{z}\zeta_{\Lambda})(\varphi)=\zeta_{\Lambda}(\partial_{z}\varphi)
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
{\Lambda}
be a stationary point process in
\mathbb{C}
having a finite second moment.
Then,

{\rm d}\rho_{\mathsf{\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}}(\xi)={1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}(\xi)\,\frac{|1-e^{2\pi{\rm i}a\cdot\xi}|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}}\,{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)+\pi^{2}|a|^{2}\rho_{\Lambda}(\{0\})\delta_{0}(\xi)\,,

and as a consequence

{\rm d}\rho_{\mathsf{\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{a}}(\xi)={1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}(\xi)\,\frac{|1-e^{2\pi{\rm i}a\cdot\xi}|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}}\,{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)
.
Moreover, we have

{\rm d}\rho_{\wp_{\Lambda}}(\xi)=\pi^{2}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}(\xi){\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)\,.

Proof.

We begin by determining the spectral measure of
{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}
.
We have
\bar{\partial}{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}=\pi(n_{T_{a}{\Lambda}}-n_{{\Lambda}})
,
and the spectral measure for
\pi(n_{T_{a}{\Lambda}}-n_{{\Lambda}})
equals

\pi^{2}|1-e^{2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot a}|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)
.
For any
\varphi\in\mathfrak{D}
, we have

{\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{{\Lambda}}(\bar{\partial}\varphi)=-\pi(n_{T_{a}{\Lambda}}-n_{\Lambda})(\varphi),

which in turn implies that
{\sf Var}\big({\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{{\Lambda}}(\bar{\partial}\varphi)\big)=\pi^{2}\,{\sf Var}\big((n_{T_{a}{\Lambda}}-n_{\Lambda})(\varphi)\big)
.
Moving to the Fourier side, we get that for all
\varphi\in\mathfrak{D}
,

\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\xi|^{2}|\widehat{\varphi}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\big|1-e^{2\pi{\rm i}a\cdot\xi}\big|^{2}|\widehat{\varphi}|^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{{\Lambda}},

and hence,

\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}}=\frac{\big|1-e^{2\pi{\rm i}a\cdot\xi}\big|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}}\,\rho_{{\Lambda}}\,\qquad\text{on}\ \,\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

(5.9)

It remains to analyze
\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})
.

Theorem

yields a useful representation of the reduced
covariance measure
\kappa_{V_{\Lambda}}
of the field
V_{\Lambda}
.
We denote by

the logarithmic potential of a signed measure

U^{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\frac{1}{|z-s|}\,{\rm d}\mu(s)

(provided that the integral on the RHS exists).

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose that reduced covariance measure
\kappa_{\Lambda}
satisfies assumptions

(\kappa_{1})
and
(\kappa_{2})
of Remark
5.3
.
Then the reduced covariance measure of
V_{\Lambda}
equals

{\rm d}\kappa_{V_{\Lambda}}=2\pi U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}\,{\rm d}m\,.

Proof.

To prove the proposition, we show that

2\pi\widehat{U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}={1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\{\xi\neq 0\}}|\xi|^{-2}\widehat{\kappa_{\Lambda}}
.
We treat both sides as Schwartz distributions and understand the
Fourier transforms in the sense of distributions.

First, we note that
\Delta U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}=-(2\pi)^{-1}\kappa_{\Lambda}
, and therefore,

\widehat{\kappa_{\Lambda}}=2\pi|\xi|^{2}\widehat{U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}
.
Hence, on the test functions
\varphi
with
0\notin{\rm spt}(\varphi)
,
the distributions
2\pi\widehat{U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}

and
|\xi|^{-2}\widehat{\kappa_{\Lambda}}
coincide.
I.e., the distribution

\nu=2\pi\widehat{U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}-|\xi|^{-2}\widehat{\kappa_{\Lambda}}

is supported by the origin, and therefore,
is a (finite) linear combination
of the delta-function and its partial
derivatives. We need to show that
\nu=0
.

Fix a non-negative
C^{\infty}
-smooth function

\gamma
with a compact support, normalized by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}\gamma\,{\rm d}m=1
,
and let
\gamma_{a}(z)=a^{-2}\gamma(z/a)
,
a>0
.
This is a convolutor on the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

of tempered distributions, and, for any
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,

f\ast\gamma_{a}\to f
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, as
a\to 0
.
The Fourier transform
\widehat{\gamma_{a}}=\widehat{\gamma}(az)

is a
C^{\infty}
-smooth, fast decaying multiplier on the
Fourier side of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, boundedly tending to
1

pointwise, as
a\to 0
.
Consider the product

2\pi\widehat{U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}}\cdot\widehat{\gamma_{a}}=|\xi|^{-2}h_{\Lambda}\cdot\widehat{\gamma_{a}}+\nu\cdot\widehat{\gamma_{a}},

where
h_{\Lambda}
is the density of
\widehat{\kappa_{\Lambda}}
.
The inverse Fourier transform of the LHS equals

2\pi U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}}\ast\gamma_{a}=U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}\ast\gamma_{a}}
.
The measure
\kappa_{\Lambda}\ast\gamma_{a}
enjoys
the same properties
(\kappa_{1})
and
(\kappa_{2})
as
\kappa_{\Lambda}
.
Hence, the logarithmic potential

U^{\kappa_{\Lambda}\ast\gamma_{a}}
tends to
0
as
z\to\infty
.
By the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, the inverse Fourier transform of

|\xi|^{-2}h_{\Lambda}\cdot\widehat{\gamma_{a}}
also tends to zero as
z\to\infty
.
Hence, the same holds for the inverse Fourier transform of
the distribution
\nu\cdot\widehat{\gamma_{a}}
.
But the inverse Fourier transform of the distribution
\nu

is a polynomial (of
{\rm Re}(z)
and
{\rm Im}(z)
),
and therefore, the inverse Fourier transform of the
distribution
\nu\cdot\widehat{\gamma_{a}}
is also a polynomial.
We conclude that
\nu\cdot\widehat{\gamma_{a}}=0
for all
a>0
,
and therefore,
\nu=0
.

Assuming that the equivalent conditions of Theorem

We start with the entire function represented by the Hadamard product

(6.2)

Remark 6.1

Appendix A
The stationary phase bound

The purpose of this section is to prove the following simple stationary
phase bound. It is standard, but we did not find a textbook reference for the
version we need.

Proposition A.1
.

There exists a universal constant
C
, such that for any
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,

\left|\int_{a}^{b}e^{-{\rm i}\omega\cos(\theta)}f(\theta){\rm d}\theta\right|\leq C\,\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}+\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\right),

for
\omega\geq 1
and
0\leq a<b\leq 2\pi
.

Proof.

If the critical points of the phase (i.e.
0
,
\pi
)
are bounded away from the end-points,
we may isolate the end-points with the help
of a cut-off function. The conclusion then
follows by estimating the end-point contributions
with the van der Corput Lemma
(see Proposition 2, Ch. VIII in
[
29
]
),
and the contributions from the interior
of
[a,b]
with the standard stationary
phase bound for an interior critical point
(see Theorem 7.7.5 in
[
15
]
).

Thus, it suffices to consider integrals

\int_{a}^{b}e^{-{\rm i}\omega\cos(\theta)}f(\theta){\rm d}\theta

for
a=0
and small
b>0
. The change of variables
\theta=\theta(x)

defined by
\cos(\theta)=1-x^{2}/2
is smooth and
bijective as a map
\theta:[0,b^{\prime}]\to[0,b]
, where
b^{\prime}>0
. Moreover,

\theta(x)
,
\theta^{\prime}(x)
are both uniformly bounded, and
\theta(x)=x+O(x^{2})

uniformly on
[0,b^{\prime}]
as
b\to 0
. We rewrite
the integral using this change of variables

\int_{0}^{b}e^{-{\rm i}\omega\cos(\theta)}f(\theta){\rm d}\theta=\int_{0}^{b^{\prime}}e^{-{\rm i}\omega x^{2}}f(\theta(x))\theta^{\prime}(x){\rm d}x=:\int_{0}^{b^{\prime}}e^{-{\rm i}\omega x^{2}}g(x){\rm d}x

where we have introduced the function
g(x)=f(\theta(x))\theta^{\prime}(x)
,
and where
b^{\prime}=b+O(b^{2})
as
b\to 0
.
It is clear from the properties of
\theta(x)
that

|g|\leq C|f|
and that
|g^{\prime}|\leq C|f^{\prime}|
, for some universal constant
C
.

An integration by parts shows that

\int_{0}^{b^{\prime}}e^{-{\rm i}\omega x^{2}}g(x){\rm d}x=g(b^{\prime})\int_{0}^{b^{\prime}}e^{-{\rm i}\omega x^{2}}{\rm d}x-\int_{0}^{b^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{x}e^{-{\rm i}\omega y^{2}}{\rm d}y\right)g^{\prime}(x){\rm d}x.

(A.1)

The Gaussian integral satisfies

\left|\int_{0}^{b^{\prime}}e^{-{\rm i}\omega x^{2}}{\rm d}x\right|=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2\omega}}\big|\mathrm{erf}\big(b^{\prime}\sqrt{{\rm i}\omega}\big)\big|\leq C\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}}

for
\omega,b^{\prime}>0
, so the first term on the RHS of (
A.1
) is
bounded above by
C\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lVert f\rVert
.
The second term can be estimated as follows

\left|\int_{0}^{b^{\prime}}\left(\int_{0}^{x}e^{-{\rm i}\omega y^{2}}{\rm d}y\right)g^{\prime}(x){\rm d}x\right|\leq\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{1}(0,b^{\prime})}\left|\int_{0}^{x}e^{-{\rm i}\omega y^{2}}{\rm d}y\right|\leq C\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence, the proof is complete.
∎

\{|z|=R\}
, implies that

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\frac{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)}{z}{\rm d}z=\Big[\zeta_{\Lambda}(a)-\sum_{|{\lambda}-a|<R}\frac{1}{a-{\lambda}}\Big]-\Big[\zeta_{\Lambda}(0)-\sum_{|{\lambda}|<R}\frac{1}{-{\lambda}}\Big].

But the right-hand side equals

\Big[\zeta_{\Lambda}(a)-\sum_{|{\lambda}-a|<R}\frac{1}{a-{\lambda}}-\Psi_{1}(R)-\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{a}\Big]-\Big[\zeta_{\Lambda}(0)-\sum_{|{\lambda}|<R}\frac{1}{-{\lambda}}-\Psi_{1}(R)\Big]+\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\bar{a}

and by
invoking the representation (
4.3
) of
\zeta_{\Lambda}

we see that the first
two terms on the right-hand side tend to zero in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
as
R\to\infty
. Hence, we get

\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\Big[\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\frac{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)}{z}{\rm d}z\Big]=\pi^{2}|a|^{2}{\sf Var}[\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}]=\pi^{2}|a|^{2}\rho_{\Lambda}(\{0\}).

Putting this together with the above representation for

\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})
, we see that

\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\zeta_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})=\pi^{2}|a|^{2}\rho_{\Lambda}(\{0\}),

which completes the proof. ∎

B.2
The atom of
\rho_{\wp_{\Lambda}}

We next finalize the proof of Theorem
5.7
, which
amounts to verifying that
\rho_{\wp_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})=0
.
The starting point is the analogous formula to (
B.1
), namely

\rho_{\wp_{{\Lambda}}}(\{0\})=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\lim_{R\to\infty}\left|\frac{J_{1}(2\pi R|\xi|)}{\pi R|\xi|}\right|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{\wp_{\Lambda}}(\xi).

We next observe that

\frac{J_{1}(2\pi R|\xi|)}{\pi R|\xi|}=\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}\widehat{{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}}_{R\mathbb{D}}(\xi)\,,

and since the LHS is bounded above by an
L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\rho_{\wp_{\Lambda}})
-integrable function,
the dominated convergence theorem gives that

\rho_{\wp_{{\Lambda}}}(\{0\})=\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\left[\wp_{{\Lambda}}\Big(\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{R\mathbb{D}}\Big)\right]

Using the familiar formula,

\displaystyle\text{p.v.}\int_{R\mathbb{D}}\frac{1}{(z-{\lambda})^{2}}\,{\rm d}m(z)=\begin{cases}0&\text{for}\ |{\lambda}|<R,\\
\frac{\pi R^{2}}{{\lambda}^{2}}&\text{for}\ |{\lambda}|>R,\end{cases}

we get the representation

\wp_{\Lambda}({1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{R\mathbb{D}})=\pi R^{2}(\Psi_{2}(\infty)-\Psi_{2}(R))\,.

By Lemma
3.2
,
\wp_{{\Lambda}}({1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{R\mathbb{D}})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
and

\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\left[\wp_{{\Lambda}}\Big(\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{R\mathbb{D}}\Big)\right]=\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\left[\Psi_{2}(\infty)-\Psi_{2}(R)\right]=0,

which completes the proof. ∎

B.3
The atom of
\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}

We turn to proving the last remaining statement of Theorem
5.8
, i.e.,
that
\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})=0
.
Arguing as in Appendix
B.1
, we find that

\displaystyle\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\lim_{R\to\infty}(J_{0}(2\pi R|\xi|))^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{C}}(J_{0}(2\pi R|\xi|))^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{V_{\Lambda}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\left[\frac{1}{2{\pi{\rm i}}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\frac{V_{\Lambda}(z)}{z}\,{\rm d}z\right].

For any given
R\geq 1
, a.s. , there are no points of
{\Lambda}
which
lie on the circle
\{|z|=R\}
. By a residue computation, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\pi{\rm i}}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\frac{V_{\Lambda}(z)}{z}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\pi{\rm i}}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\frac{\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)-\Psi_{1}(\infty)}{z}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\Lambda}(0)+\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\Psi_{1}(\infty)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{|{\lambda}|<1}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}+\sum_{|{\lambda}|<R}\frac{1}{{\lambda}}-\Psi_{1}(\infty)=\Psi_{1}(R)-\Psi_{1}(\infty)\,.

But Lemma
3.3
implies that

{\sf Var}\big[\Psi_{1}(R)-\Psi_{1}(\infty)\big]=0

which completes the proof. ∎

B.4
The atom of
\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}}

To conclude the proof of Theorem
6.2
, it only remains to prove that

\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}}(\{0\})=\frac{\pi^{2}|a|^{4}}{4}\rho_{\Lambda}(\{0\}).

We again use the method introduced in Appendix
B.1
,
in particular the formula

\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}}\left(\{0\}\right)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{C}}(J_{0}(2\pi R|\xi|))^{2}\,{\rm d}\rho_{{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}}(\xi)=\lim_{R\to\infty}{\sf Var}\Big[\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{z}\Big].

We start with the representation

{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(z)=\lim_{S\to\infty}\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq S}\big(\log|z-({\lambda}-a)|-\log|z-{\lambda}|\big)-\frac{1}{2}\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}\big(2\mathrm{Re}\,\bar{a}z)-|a|^{2}\big)

and the formula

\frac{1}{2\pi R}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}\log|z-w|\,|{\rm d}z|=\begin{cases}\log R&|w|\leq R\\
\log|w|&|w|>R\end{cases}.

Combining these two, we get

\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\{|z|=R\}}{\sf\Delta}_{a}\Pi_{\Lambda}(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{z}=\lim_{S\to\infty}\left[\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq S,\;|{\lambda}-a|\geq R}\log|{\lambda}-a|-\sum_{R\leq|{\lambda}|\leq S}\log|{\lambda}|\right]\\
+\sum_{|{\lambda}-a|\leq R}\log R-\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\log R-\frac{1}{2}\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}|a|^{2},

where we have used the fact that
\mathrm{Re}\,(\bar{a}z)
has zero
mean over circles centered at the origin.
By subtracting and adding back
\sum_{|{\lambda}-a|\geq R,\,|{\lambda}|\leq S}\log|{\lambda}|
,
we may rewrite the right-hand side as

\sum_{|{\lambda}-a|\geq R}\big(\log|{\lambda}-a|-\log|{\lambda}|\big)+\left[\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-a|\leq R}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\pi\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda}|a|^{2}.

In view of the spectral condition,
Lemma
3.3
on the convergence of the sequence

(\Psi_{1}(R))_{R\geq 1}
gives that the
first term tends to zero in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.
To obtain the result, if suffices to show that the variance of

{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{|{\lambda}|\leq R}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}-\sum_{|{\lambda}-a|\leq R}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}\right]={\sf Var}\left[\sum_{{\lambda}\in A}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}-\sum_{{\lambda}\in B}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}\right]

tends to zero as
R\to\infty
,
where we use the notation
A
and
B
for the lunar domains

A=\{|{\lambda}|\leq R,\;|{\lambda}-a|\geq R\}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\{|{\lambda}|\geq R,\;|{\lambda}-a|\leq R\}.

The corresponding linear statistic is

f_{R}({\lambda})=\big({1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{A}({\lambda})-{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{B}({\lambda})\big)\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}=:f_{R,A}(\xi)-f_{R,B}(\xi),

so the variance equals

{\sf Var}\left[\sum_{{\lambda}\in A}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}-\sum_{{\lambda}\in B}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}|\widehat{f}_{R}(\xi)|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi),

where we have used the fact that
\widehat{f}_{R}(0)=0
to infer
that the origin does not contribute to the variance.
We need to estimate
|\widehat{f}_{R}(\xi)|^{2}
from above,
and by symmetry it suffices
to consider only
f_{R,A}
.

Since
\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}=O(R^{-1})
on
A
, the naive bound

|\widehat{f}_{R,A}(\xi)|\leq\lVert f_{R,A}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{C})}

gives that
|\widehat{f}_{R,A}(\xi)|^{2}=O(1)
as
R\to\infty
for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
.
In order to complete the proof, we need better estimates valid for large
|\xi|
.
An application of Green’s formula shows that

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{R,A}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot{\lambda}}{\rm d}m({\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}}\int_{A}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}\Delta\left(e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot{\lambda}}\right){\rm d}m({\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}}\int_{\partial A}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\xi\cdot{\lambda}}\left[-2\pi{\rm i}\log\frac{|{\lambda}|}{R}\big\langle\xi,N({\lambda})\big\rangle+\left\langle\tfrac{1}{\bar{\lambda}},N({\lambda})\right\rangle\right]|{\rm d}{\lambda}|,

where
N
denotes the outward unit normal to
A
. Parameterizing the two
circular arcs that make up the boundary
\partial A
,
we find that

\widehat{f}_{R,A}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}}\big(J_{1}(\xi,R,a)+J_{2}(\xi,R,a)\big),

where
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are the integrals

J_{1}(R,\xi,a)=\frac{1}{R}\int_{s_{1}}^{t_{1}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}R|\xi|\cos(\theta)}{\rm d}\theta

(B.3)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{2}(R,\xi,a)=&-2\pi{\rm i}|\xi|\int_{s_{2}}^{t_{2}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}R|\xi|\cos(\theta)}\log\big|1+\tfrac{ae^{-{\rm i}\theta}}{R}\big|\cos(\theta){\rm d}\theta\\
&+\frac{1}{R}\int_{s_{2}}^{t_{2}}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}R|\xi|\cos(\theta)}\mathrm{Re}\,\Big[\tfrac{1}{1+R^{-1}\bar{a}e^{-{\rm i}\theta}}\Big]{\rm d}\theta\end{split}

(B.4)

respectively. Here,
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
are positive parameters with
0\leq s_{i}\leq t_{i}\leq 2\pi
,
depending on
R
and
a
and
\xi
.
For large
|\xi|
, we may thus use the standard stationary phase bound

\left|\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\omega\cos(\theta)}f(\theta){\rm d}\theta\right|\lesssim\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}([\alpha,\beta])}+\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{1}([\alpha,\beta])}\big)

from Proposition
A.1
in the Appendix
to obtain the estimates

\big|J_{1}(R,\xi,a)\big|\lesssim|\xi|^{-\frac{1}{2}}R^{-\frac{3}{2}}

and

\big|J_{1}(R,\xi,a)\big|\lesssim_{a}|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{-\frac{3}{2}}+\xi^{-\frac{1}{2}}R^{-\frac{3}{2}}

for the two contributions
J_{1}(R,\xi,a)
and
J_{2}(R,\xi,a)
for
\widehat{f}_{R,A}(\xi)
.
Putting everything together, we arrive at

|\widehat{f}_{R,A}(\xi)|^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi^{4}|\xi|^{4}}\big|J_{1}(\xi,R,a)+J_{2}(\xi,R,a)\big|^{2}\lesssim_{a}\min\big\{1,R^{-3}|\xi|^{-3}\big\}.

Since the corresponding bound holds also for
\widehat{f}_{R,B}
, we get

{\sf Var}\left[n_{\Lambda}(f_{R})\right]=\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}\big|\widehat{f}_{R}(\xi)\big|^{2}{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi)\lesssim_{a}\int_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}\min\big\{1,R^{-3}|\xi|^{-3}\big\}{\rm d}\rho_{\Lambda}(\xi),

and hence the result follows by invoking the dominated convergence theorem.
∎
