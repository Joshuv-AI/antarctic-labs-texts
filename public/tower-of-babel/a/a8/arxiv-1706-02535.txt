Mendeleev Table: a Proof of Madelung Rule and Atomic Tietz Potential
Eugene D. BELOKOLOS

Abstract
We prove that a neutral atom in mean-field approximation has O ⁡ ( 4 ) {\rm O}(4) symmetry and this fact explains the empirical [ n + l , n ] [n+l,n] -rule or Madelung rule which describes effectively periods, structure and other properties of the Mendeleev table of chemical elements.

3
Atomic potential in the mean-field

and semi-classical approximations

The Hamiltonian of a free neutral atom is

\displaystyle H_{\rm A}=\sum_{k=1}^{Z}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{k}-\frac{Z}{\vec{r}_{k}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,k=1}^{Z}\frac{1}{\vec{r}_{ik}}=\sum_{k=1}^{Z}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{k}+v(\vec{r}_{k})\right),

\displaystyle v(\vec{r}_{k})=-\frac{Z}{\vec{r}_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{Z}\frac{1}{\vec{r}_{ik}},

where
v(\vec{r})
describes the electron interactions with the atomic nucleus and other electrons.
Since it is good approximation to describe the electrons in an atom in terms of the electron configuration,
i.e., the electron distribution on one-particle states,

\displaystyle n,\ l,\ m,\ \sigma,

therefore we may consider the electron interaction
v(\vec{r})
in the mean-field approximation
with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{k=1}^{Z}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{k}+V(\vec{r}_{k})\right),

where
V(\vec{r})
is the mean-field atomic potential. Since we consider a one-particle angular momentum, quantum number
l
as a good quantum number, this potential has to be central,

\displaystyle V(\vec{r})=V(r).

For the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle H\Psi=E\Psi

we look for the ground state solution in the Slater determinant form

\displaystyle\Psi=\det||\psi_{j}(r_{k})||,

where one-particle wave function
\psi_{k}(r)
satisfies the one-particle Schrödinger equation
describing an electron in a 3-dimensional central potential
V(r)
,

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V(r)\right)\psi_{j}(r)=E_{j}\psi_{j}(r).

In the semiclassical approximation, solutions of this equation have the form

\displaystyle\psi(r)=\exp(iS(r)),

where
S(r)
is the classical action.

3.1
The Thomas–Fermi atomic potential

Since in the case under consideration we have three integrals of
motion (energy
E
, angular momentum
L
, and its projection
L_{z}
) our problem is integrable
by quadratures due to the Arnold–Liouville theorem, and the radial action looks as follows

\displaystyle S(r)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{r}\left[2(E-V(r))-\frac{I_{\theta}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}{\rm d}r,

where the radial action variable is

\displaystyle I_{r}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\left[2(E-V(r))-\frac{I_{\theta}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}{\rm d}r.

In the semiclassical approximation we should change action variables by quantum numbers,

\displaystyle I_{r}=n_{r}(E,l),\qquad I_{\theta}=l.

In this way we come to the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization rule

\displaystyle n_{r}(E,l)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}(E,l)}^{r_{+}(E,l)}\left[2E-2V(r)-\frac{l^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\mathrm{d}r.

Let us set in the latter expression
E=0
, then

\displaystyle n_{r}(0,l)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}(0,l)}^{r_{+}(0,l)}\left[-V(r)-\frac{l^{2}}{2r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\mathrm{d}r.

Thus the number of bound states
N
in the atom is

\displaystyle N=\sum_{l=0}^{l^{\max}}n_{r}(0,l)2(2l+1).

Approximately a value
N
looks as follows

\displaystyle N\simeq\int_{l=0}^{l^{\max}}n_{r}(0,l)2(2l+1)\mathrm{d}l

\displaystyle\hphantom{N}{}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\int_{l=0}^{l^{\max}}\left[-V(r)-\frac{l^{2}}{2r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}2(2l+1)\mathrm{d}l\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\hphantom{N}{}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\frac{2}{3}\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\left[-V(r)\right]^{3/2}4r^{2}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3\pi^{2}}\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\left[-V(r)\right]^{3/2}4\pi r^{2}\mathrm{d}r=\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\rho(r)4\pi r^{2}\mathrm{d}r.

This is the well-known asymptotic formula
[
29
]
for the number of bound states in a central potential
with the density of bound states
\rho(r)
equal to

\displaystyle\rho(r)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3\pi^{2}}[-V(r)]^{3/2}.

The electrostatic potential of atomic electrons
\phi(r)=-V(r)
satisfies the Poisson equation
\Delta\phi(r)=-4\pi\rho
,
and therefore

\displaystyle\Delta\phi(r)=\frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3\pi}\phi^{3/2}(r),

\displaystyle\phi(r)r=Z,\qquad r\to 0,\qquad\phi(r)=0,\qquad r\to\infty.

We can present the Thomas–Fermi potential
\phi(r)
in such a way

\displaystyle\phi(r)=\frac{Z}{r}\chi(x),\qquad x=\frac{r}{R_{0}},\qquad R_{0}=bZ^{-1/3},\qquad b=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)^{2/3}\simeq 0.885.

For large
Z
the ground state energy
E_{\rm TF}(Z)
of the Thomas–Fermi atom is the asymptotics for
a ground state energy
E_{\rm HF}(Z)
of the Hartree–Fock atom
[
20
,
21
]
:

\displaystyle\lim_{Z\to\infty}E_{\rm HF}(Z)/E_{\rm TF}(Z)=1.

The Thomas–Fermi potential does not depend on quantum numbers although naturally it should do. Nevertheless,
let us calculate the
Z_{l}
for the Thomas–Fermi potential
[
8
]
.

The effective potential
u_{l}(r)
is

\displaystyle u_{l}(r)=-\phi(r)+\frac{(l+1/2)^{2}}{2r^{2}}=-\frac{(l+1/2)^{2}}{2r^{2}}[\zeta_{l}x\chi(x)-1],

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{l}=\frac{2ZR_{0}}{(l+1/2)^{2}}=2b\frac{Z^{2/3}}{(l+1/2)^{2}}.

Conditions for the first appearance of the energy level with certain
l
are

\displaystyle u_{l}(r)=0,\qquad u_{l}^{\prime}(r)=0

or, which is the same,

\displaystyle u_{l}(x)=0,\qquad u_{l}^{\prime}(x)=0.

It is equivalent to equations

\displaystyle\zeta_{l}x\chi(x)-1=0,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x\chi(x))=0,

which have a solution

\displaystyle x_{0}\simeq 2.104,\qquad\chi(x_{0})\simeq 0.231,\qquad x_{0}\chi(x_{0})\simeq 0.486,

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime}(x_{0})\simeq-0.110,\qquad\zeta_{l}=[x_{0}\chi(x_{0})]^{-1}\simeq 2.056.

This means that

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{l}^{\rm TF}}{(2l+1)^{3}}=\left(\frac{\zeta_{l}}{8b}\right)^{3/2}=0.155,

and hence

\displaystyle Z_{l}^{\rm TF}\simeq 0.155(2l+1)^{3}.

In the general case
Z_{l}^{TF}
is not integer and we have to write

\displaystyle Z_{l}^{\rm TF}=\big|0.155(2l+1)^{3}\big|,

where
|x|
means the integer which is the closest to the real
x
.

If we change in this formula the coefficient
0.155
to
0.169\simeq 1/6
we get a better agreement of
the values
Z_{l}^{TF}
with that in the Mendeleev table (see
[
12
]
and
[
17
, Section 73]
). In this case
the r.h.s. of the formula will coincide with the first summand of the Klechkovski–Hakala expression
for
Z_{l}
(
2.1
) obtained with the
[n+l]
-rule.

This example shows that the Thomas–Fermi theory yields only some approximation to the
[n+l,n]
-rule.

According to the above discussion the electrons in the atom in the mean-field approximation interact by means of
central atomic potential, having geometrical
{\rm O}(3)
symmetry. The Thomas–Fermi theory takes into account
the
{\rm O}(3)
symmetry of the atomic Hamiltonian with central potential. We do the next step.
The Hamiltonian with any central potential
has dynamical
{\rm O}(4)
symmetry
[
4
,
10
,
25
]
, similar to the Hydrogen
atom
[
5
,
9
,
28
]
. Therefore, the atomic Hamiltonian must have an additional
integral of motion in involution with respect to integrals of motion corresponding to
{\rm O}(3)
symmetry.
Further we shall show that such additional integral of motion in involution does exist for the atomic Hamiltonian
and leads to
[n+l,n]
-rule.

3.2
The Tietz atomic potential

We begin with certain basic facts on symmetry and integrability in the classical Hamiltonian systems
(see
[
3
, Sections 49–51]
,
[
16
, Section 52]
and
[
33
]
).

Let us assume that on a symplectic
2n
-dimensional manifold there are
n
functions in involution

\displaystyle F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\qquad\{F_{i},F_{j}\}=0,\qquad i,j=1,\dots,n.

Consider a level set of functions
F_{i}

\displaystyle M_{f}=\{x\colon F_{i}(x)=f_{i},\,i=1,\dots,n\}.

Suppose that
n
functions
F_{i}
are independent on
M_{f}
(i.e., the
n

1
-forms
dF_{i}
are linearly independent in every point of
M_{f}
).

Then

1.

M_{f}
is a smooth manifold, invariant with respect to the phase flow with the Hamiltonian
H=F_{1}
.

2.

If the manifold
M_{f}
is compact and connected, then it is diffeomorphic to
n
-dimensional torus

\displaystyle T^{n}=\{(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n})\ (\mbox{mod}\ 2\pi)\}.

3.

Phase flow with the Hamiltonian
H
defines on
M_{f}
a quasi-periodic movement
with angular variables
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n})
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\phi_{i}}{{\rm d}t}=\omega_{i}(f),\qquad\phi_{i}(t)=\phi_{i}(0)+\omega_{i}t,\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

Instead of functions
F=(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
it is possible to define new functions

I=(I_{1}(F),\dots
,
I_{n}(F))
which are called action variables and which together with angle variables
form in the neighborhood of manifold
M_{f}
the canonical system of action-angle coordinates.

4.

Canonical equations with the Hamiltonian function are integrable in quadratures.

5.

If frequencies
\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})

are degenerated, i.e., if there exists such an integer-valued vector
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
that

\displaystyle(k,\omega)=0,

then there appears one more single-valued function
F_{n+1}
which is in involution with functions
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}
.

Now let us go back to our problem: an electron in the central atomic potential. In this case the 3-dimensional electron movement
is reduced to 2-dimensional one in the plane perpendicular to the orbital momentum vector. We describe this movement
in the semiclassical approximation by the action

\displaystyle S=\int^{r}\sqrt{2[E-V(r)]-\frac{I_{\theta}^{2}}{r^{2}}}\mathrm{d}r

with radial
(r,I_{r})
and orbital
(\theta,I_{\theta})
action-angle variables, where

\displaystyle I_{r}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int^{r_{\max}}_{r_{\min}}\sqrt{2[E-V(r)]-\frac{I_{\theta}^{2}}{r^{2}}}\mathrm{d}r.

And so in our problem we have a quasi-periodic movement on 2-dimensional torus which is described by the Fourier series

\displaystyle G(t)=\sum_{l_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{l_{1},l_{2}}\exp(l_{1}\phi_{r}+l_{2}\phi_{\theta})=\sum_{l_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{l_{1},l_{2}}\exp[(l_{1}\omega_{r}+l_{2}\omega_{\theta})t]

with 2 basic frequencies

\displaystyle\omega_{r}=\frac{\partial E}{\partial I_{r}},\qquad\omega_{\theta}=\frac{\partial E}{\partial I_{\theta}}.

In the general case these frequencies are independent. But under certain circumstances, as we have,
when new additional integral appears, they may become degenerate (or commensurate, or resonance),

\displaystyle q\omega_{r}=p\omega_{\theta},\qquad q,p\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad{\rm g.c.d.}(p,q)=1,

where
{\rm g.c.d.}(p,q)
means the greatest common divisor of the integers
p
and
q
.
This condition leads to important consequences.

1.

Since the latter relation is equivalent to

\displaystyle q\frac{\partial E}{\partial I_{r}}=p\frac{\partial E}{\partial I_{\theta}}

it means that energy
E
depends on
pI_{r}+qI_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle E=E(pI_{r}+qI_{\theta}).

And due to the Bohr–Sommerfeld semiclassical quantization rule

\displaystyle I_{r}\rightarrow n_{r},\qquad I_{\theta}\rightarrow l,

we have

\displaystyle E=E(pn_{r}+ql).

2.

We have the additional independent integral of motion.

3.

The canonical variables of the problem are separated in several systems of coordinates.

For example, for the Kepler–Coulomb potential
1/r
we have
p=q=1
, energy
E
depends on
principal quantum number
n=n_{r}+l+1
, the canonical variables of the problem are separated in polar and parabolic
coordinates.

Let us study the similar situation for the atomic potential. Consider a system of equations

\displaystyle I_{r}(E)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}(E)}^{r_{+}(E)}\left[2E-2V(r)-\frac{I_{\theta}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\mathrm{d}r,

\displaystyle M_{p,q}=pI_{r}+qI_{\theta},\qquad q,p\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad{\rm g.c.d.}(p,q)=1,

the first of which is the standard relation between action variables
I_{r}
,
I_{\theta}
, and energy
E

in central potential and the second one is relation between
I_{r}
,
I_{\theta}
arising as result of frequencies
degeneracy.

We shall study equations at the energy
E=0
at which new bound states appear from continuous spectrum, when

Z
grows. Then differentiating equations with respect to
I_{\theta}
we come to the equation

\displaystyle\pi\alpha=\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\big[-2V(r)r^{2}-I_{\theta}^{2}\big]^{-1/2}I_{\theta}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r},

where

\displaystyle\alpha=-\frac{\partial I_{r}}{\partial I_{\theta}}=\frac{q}{p}\in\mathbb{Q},\qquad q,p\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad{\rm g.c.d.}(p,q)=1.

From this equation we deduce an expression for atomic potential
V(r)
. A similar integral equation arising
in problem for generalized tautochrone (isochrone) curve was solved
by Abel
[
1
]
. We shall use his method in the form presented in
[
16
, Section 12]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Equation

\displaystyle\pi\alpha=\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\big[{-}2V(r)r^{2}-L^{2}\big]^{-1/2}L\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r}

(3.1)

has a solution

\displaystyle V_{\alpha,\beta,R}(r)=-\frac{\beta}{r^{2}[(r/R)^{1/\alpha}+(R/r)^{1/\alpha}]^{2}},

where
\beta
and
R
are certain constants, and its deformations.

If the potential
V_{\alpha,\beta,R}(r)
coincides at small
r
with the Kepler–Coulomb potential

\displaystyle V_{\alpha,\beta,R}=-Z/r,\qquad r\to 0,

then we have

\displaystyle\alpha=q/p=2,\qquad\beta=ZR,

and the potential takes the following form

\displaystyle V(r)\equiv V_{2,ZR,R}(r)=-\frac{Z}{r(1+(r/R))^{2}}.

Remark 3.2
.

According to the theorem in the neighborhood
E\simeq 0
we have

\displaystyle E=E(n+l)

and this fact proves the first part of the
[n+l,n]
-rule.
A second part of the
[n+l,n]
-rule is a consequence of the oscillation theorem.

Proof 3.3
.

Let us rewrite equation (
3.1
) in the form

\displaystyle\pi\alpha=\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\big[w(x)-L^{2}\big]^{-1/2}L\mathrm{d}x,

where

\displaystyle x=\ln(r/R),\qquad r=R\exp(x),

\displaystyle w(x)=-2V(r)r^{2}\qquad\mbox{at}\quad r=R\exp(x),

\displaystyle w_{0}=\max w(x),

are new variables and
R
is a parameter.
We assume that
w(x)
is the one-well potential
and therefore the inverse function is two-valued,
i.e., the values
w
are reached in two points
x_{-}(w)
and
x_{+}(w)
. We shall assume also that

x_{-}(w)\leq x_{+}(w)
and at
w_{0}
we have
x_{-}(w_{0})=x_{+}(w_{0})
. As a result we obtain

\displaystyle\pi\alpha=\int_{L^{2}}^{w_{0}}\big(w-L^{2}\big)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{+}}{\mathrm{d}w}-\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{-}}{\mathrm{d}w}\right)L\mathrm{d}w.

Multiplying this equality by
\big(L^{2}-w_{1}\big)^{-1/2}2\mathrm{d}L
and integrating from
(w_{1})^{1/2}

to
(w_{0})^{1/2}
we get

\displaystyle 2\pi\alpha\int_{(w_{1})^{1/2}}^{(w_{0})^{1/2}}\big(L^{2}-w_{1}\big)^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}L

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\int_{(w_{1})^{1/2}}^{(w_{0})^{1/2}}2L\mathrm{d}L\int_{L^{2}}^{w_{0}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{+}}{\mathrm{d}w}-\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{-}}{\mathrm{d}w}\right)\big[\big(w-L^{2}\big)\big(L^{2}-w_{1}\big)\big]^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\int_{w_{1}}^{w_{0}}\mathrm{d}w\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{+}}{\mathrm{d}w}-\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{-}}{\mathrm{d}w}\right)\int_{(w_{1})^{1/2}}^{(w)^{1/2}}\big[\big(w^{2}-L^{2}\big)\big(L^{2}-w_{1}\big)\big]^{-1/2}2L\mathrm{d}L.

Since

\displaystyle\int_{(w_{1})^{1/2}}^{(w_{0})^{1/2}}\big(L^{2}-w_{1}\big)^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}L=\operatorname{Arcosh}(w_{0}/w_{1})^{1/2},

\displaystyle\int_{(w_{1})^{1/2}}^{(w)^{1/2}}\big[\big(w^{2}-L^{2}\big)\big(L^{2}-w_{1}\big)\big]^{-1/2}2L\mathrm{d}L=\pi,

this equality acquires the following form

\displaystyle 2\alpha\operatorname{Arcosh}(w_{0}/w_{1})^{1/2}=(x_{+}(w)-x_{-}(w))\Big|_{w_{1}}^{w_{0}}.

Taking into account that
x_{-}(w_{0})=x_{+}(w_{0})
and setting
w_{1}=w
we come to the equality

\displaystyle x_{+}(w)-x_{-}(w)=2\alpha\operatorname{Arcosh}(w_{0}/w)^{1/2}.

This equality defines only the difference
x_{-}(w)-x_{+}(w)
of two functions
x_{-}(w)
and
x_{+}(w)
, any
of which remains actually undefined. It means that there exists the infinite set of potentials which satisfy
the latter equation and differ by deformations which do not change the difference of two values
of
x
corresponding to one value of
w
. Among these potentials there is a symmetric potential with the property

x_{+}(w)=-x_{-}(w)\equiv x(w)
. For this potential in this case

\displaystyle w(x)=w_{0}/\cosh^{2}(x/\alpha),

or, in the previous notations,

\displaystyle V_{\alpha,\beta,R}(r)=-\frac{\beta}{r^{2}[(r/R)^{1/\alpha}+(R/r)^{1/\alpha}]^{2}},

where
\beta=2w_{0}
. These potentials are degenerate for arbitrary value of the parameter
\alpha
.
In order for the degenerate potential to coincide with Coulomb potential at small
r

\displaystyle V_{\alpha,\beta,R}=-Z/r,\qquad r\to 0,

we must have

\displaystyle\alpha=q/p=2,\qquad\beta=ZR.

Then this potential takes the following form

\displaystyle V(r)\equiv V_{2,ZR,R}(r)=-\frac{Z}{r(1+(r/R))^{2}}.

∎

In this potential the total number of bound states
N
is equal to the total number of electrons
Z
if

\displaystyle N=\frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3\pi}\int[-V(r)]^{3/2}{\rm d}r=\big(9/2R^{3}\big)=Z,

i.e.,

\displaystyle R=(9/2Z)^{1/3}\simeq 1.651Z^{-1/3},

and we have finally

\displaystyle V(r|Z)=-\frac{Z}{r(1+(r/R(Z)))^{2}}.

For the given electron configuration we must use the Klechkovski–Hakala
formulas for
Z
. In this case the potential will depend on quantum numbers.

We can present this potential as the sum

\displaystyle V(x)=-\frac{(Z/R)}{x(1+x)^{2}}=-\frac{Z}{R}\left[\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{(x+1)}-\frac{1}{(x+1)^{2}}\right],\qquad x=\frac{r}{R},

where the first summand describes the Coulomb attraction of the atomic nucleus for the single electron and
two other summands describe a nucleus screening by the other electrons.

T. Tietz
[
30
]
proposed the potential

\displaystyle V(r)=-\frac{Z}{r[1+(r/R)]^{2}}

as a good rational approximation to the Thomas–Fermi potential and used it for calculation of various atomic
properties and explanation of the periodic system elements
[
31
]
(see also
[
34
]
). Further we
shall call this potential as the Tietz atomic potential.

Due to the proximity of the Tietz and Thomas–Fermi potentials it is very likely that at large
Z

a ground state energy of the Tietz atom,
E_{\rm T}(Z)
, is an asymptotics for
the ground state energy of the Hartree–Fock atom,
E_{\rm HF}(Z)
:

\displaystyle\lim_{Z\to\infty}E_{\rm HF}(Z)/E_{\rm T}(Z)=1.

Yu.N. Demkov and V.N. Ostrovski
[
7
]
pointed out that this potential is a particular case of a so called
focussing (in other words “degenerate”) potentials studied in connection to certain problems of optics
by J.C. Maxwell
[
23
]
and V. Lenz
[
19
]
.

The Tietz potential is a rational function, and thus we can easily do various calculations with it. For example, we can calculate the atomic spectrum.

4
Semiclassical atomic spectrum for the Tietz atomic potential

The Bohr–Sommerfeld semiclassical condition of quantization for the Tietz atomic potential is

\displaystyle n_{r}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_{-}}^{r_{+}}\left[2E+\frac{2Z}{r(1+(r/R))^{2}}-\frac{(l+1/2)^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]^{1/2}{\rm d}r.

In the scaled quantum numbers

\displaystyle x=\frac{r}{R},\qquad\epsilon=\frac{2ER^{2}}{(l+1/2)^{2}},\qquad\nu_{r}=\frac{n_{r}}{l+1/2},\qquad\eta_{l}=\frac{2ZR}{(l+1/2)^{2}}

the Bohr–Sommerfeld equation takes the form

\displaystyle\nu_{r}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}\left[\epsilon+\frac{\eta_{l}}{x(1+x)^{2}}-\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right]^{1/2}{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}\frac{\sqrt{P_{4}(x)}}{x(1+x)}{\rm d}x,

where
P_{4}(x)
is a 4-th degree polynomial

\displaystyle P_{4}(x)=\epsilon x^{2}(1+x)^{2}+\eta_{l}x-(1+x)^{2},

and boundaries of integration are real non-negative zeros of this polynomial.
Therefore scaled radial quantum number
\nu_{r}
is a period of the elliptic integral
with scaled energy
\epsilon
and scaled charge
\eta_{l}
as the parameters.
We can obtain an atomic spectrum in the semiclassical approximation

\displaystyle\epsilon=f(\nu_{r},\eta_{l}),

by means of inversion of the elliptic integral.

Let us study this problem in the framework of perturbation theory in the energy
\epsilon
.
We present the Bohr–Sommerfeld semiclassical equation in the form

\displaystyle\nu_{r}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}\left[\epsilon+\frac{\eta_{l}x-(1+x)^{2}}{x^{2}(1+x)^{2}}\right]^{1/2}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\hphantom{\nu_r}{}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}\left[\epsilon+\frac{(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-})}{x^{2}(1+x)^{2}}\right]^{1/2}{\rm d}x,

where

\displaystyle\eta_{l}x-(1+x)^{2}=(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-}),

\displaystyle x_{\pm}^{2}-(\eta_{l}-2)x_{\pm}+1=0,

\displaystyle x_{\pm}=\frac{(\eta_{l}-2)\pm\sqrt{(\eta_{l}-2)^{2}-4}}{2}=\frac{(\eta_{l}-2)\pm\sqrt{\eta_{l}(\eta_{l}-4)}}{2},

\displaystyle-(\eta_{l}-4)>\epsilon>-\frac{1}{4}\eta_{l}(\eta_{l}-4).

At the first approximation we have

\displaystyle\nu_{r}\simeq J_{0}+\epsilon J_{1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\nu_r}{}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}[(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-})]^{1/2}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x(1+x)}+\epsilon\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}\frac{x(1+x)}{[(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-})]^{1/2}}{\rm d}x,

where

\displaystyle J_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}[(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-})]^{1/2}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x(1+x)}=\sqrt{\eta_{l}}-2,

\displaystyle J_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}\frac{x(1+x)}{[(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-})]^{1/2}}{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{16}\big(3\eta_{l}^{2}-8\eta_{l}\big).

The energy spectrum in the semiclassical theory is

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n,l}=-\frac{J_{0}-\nu_{r}}{J_{1}}=-16\frac{\sqrt{\eta_{l}}-2-\nu_{r}}{\big(3\eta_{l}^{2}-8\eta_{l}\big)}=-16\frac{(l+(1/2))\sqrt{\eta_{l}}-2l-1-n_{r}}{(l+(1/2))\big(3\eta_{l}^{2}-8\eta_{l}\big)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\epsilon_{n,l}}{}=-16\frac{(l+(1/2))\sqrt{\eta_{l}}-(n+l)}{(l+(1/2))\big(3\eta_{l}^{2}-8\eta_{l}\big)}=-16\frac{\sqrt{2ZR}-M}{(l+(1/2))\big(3\eta_{l}^{2}-8\eta_{l}\big)},

where we have used the Madelung number
M=n+l
.

Since

\displaystyle\epsilon_{M,l}=\frac{2E_{M,l}R^{2}}{(l+(1/2))^{2}},

we have the following expression for the atomic spectrum in the atomic units

\displaystyle E_{M,l}=-8\frac{(\sqrt{2ZR}-M)(l+(1/2))}{\big(3\eta_{l}^{2}-8\eta_{l}\big)R^{2}},\qquad\eta_{l}=\frac{2ZR}{(l+(1/2))^{2}}.

If

\displaystyle\sqrt{2ZR}=M

then
E_{M,l}=0
, and all states
(M,l)
are degenerate with respect to
l
. Thus at energy
E=0
there appears the full set of energy levels
(n,l)
with
M=n+l
. Since
R=(9/2Z)^{1/3}
the equality
M=\sqrt{2ZR}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle Z=(1/6)M^{3}.

We can write down a complete perturbation series

\displaystyle\nu_{r}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}J_{k}.

(4.1)

Since all integrals

\displaystyle J_{k}=\frac{c_{k}}{\pi}\int_{x_{-}}^{x_{+}}\left\{\frac{x^{2}(1+x)^{2}}{[(x_{+}-x)(x-x_{-})]}\right\}^{k-(1/2)}\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle c_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(3/2)}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma((3/2)-k)},\qquad k\geq 2,

are divergent we should take their regularized values. i.e., valeur principale.
Inverting series (
4.1
) by means of the Bürmann–Lagrange theorem
we can obtain an expression for atomic spectrum in the semiclassical approximation

\displaystyle\epsilon=f(\nu_{r},\eta_{l}).

We plans to compare this atomic spectrum (both eigenvalues and eigenfunctions) with those
presented in articles
[
18
,
32
]
.

5
Conclusion

For the Mendeleev periodic system of elements we have proved the empirical
(n+l,n)
-rule
which explains very efficiently the structure and properties of chemical elements.
In order to prove this rule we are forced to build for the Hamiltonian describing an electron in
central atomic potential one more integral of motion in involution in addition to energy and
orbital integral. In this case the atomic potential appears to be the Tietz potential.
For the Tietz potential we have calculated the atomic energy spectrum in the semiclassical approximation.

In addition, we are going to study spectrum of the Schrödinger operator with the Tietz potential,
when the radial part of wave function satisfies the confluent Heun equation
and compare energy levels, obtained in such a way, with the NIST Atomic Spectra Database.

For the atom in the paper we have studied the non-relativistic atomic Hamiltonian. We plan to consider
also the relativistic Dirac Hamiltonian.

It is interesting also to study isospectral many-well deformation of the Tietz potential.

Questions studied in the paper are related only to the ground state of atoms. But according to
experimental data the weakly excited atomic states also follow to
[n+l,n]
-rule
[
15
]
. It may be
of interest for physical chemistry, especially for understanding chemical reactions rules.

We hope to do that in the near future.
