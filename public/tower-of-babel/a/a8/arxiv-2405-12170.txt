Deformation of Residual Intersections Thanks: The first was partially supported by grants CAPES-PrInt Project 88881.311616/2018-00, Finance Code 001, FAPERJ 2025, APQ1 and CNPq-Universal No. 406377/2021-9.
Hamid Hassanzadeh and Kevin Vasconcellos

Abstract
. It is shown that in a Cohen-Macaulay local ring, the generic linkage of an ideal I I is a deformation of the arbitrary linkage of I I . This fact does not need I I to be a Cohen-Macaulay ideal. The same holds for s s -residual intersections of I I when s ≤ ht ⁡ ( I ) + 1 s\leq\Ht(I)+1 . Under some slight conditions on I I , one further generalizes this principle to encompass any s s -residual intersection.



1.
Introduction

One of the main facts connecting linkage theory and deformation theory is Buchweitz’s
[
Buc82
]
result:

Let
P=k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket
the power series ring and
R=P/I
a reduced ring. If
I
is in the linkage class of a complete intersection then
T^{2}(R/k,R)=0
, i.e.
R
is unobstructed.

Here
T^{i}(R/k,R)
are upper cotangent functors:
T^{0}(R/k,R)
is the module of
k
-derivations Der
{}_{k}(R,R)
,
T^{1}(R/k,R)
corresponds to the space of isomorphism classes of first-order infinitesimal deformations of
R
over
k
, and
T^{2}(R/k,R)
contains the obstructions for lifting infinitesimal deformations of
R
. Therefore
R
is called rigid over
k
if
T^{1}(R/k,R)=0
and nonobstructed if
T^{2}(R/k,R)=0
.

Rigidity is not preserved through an arbitrary linkage class. However Kustin and Miller
[
KM84
, Theorem 4.2]
show that this property is maintained for “semi-generic” linkage. Then it is highly important to see when the generic linkage is a deformation of an arbitrary linkage.

In their seminal paper
[
HU87
]
Huneke and Ulrich determine the structure of linkage (especially licci ideals) through the study of generic linkage. One of the key tools in this study is the following result,
[
HU87
, Proposition 2.14]
:

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a local Gorenstein ring, and
I
and
J
two
Cohen-Macaulay
ideals of
R
which are linked and of positive grade. Considering any first generic linkage
L_{1}(I)
there exists a prime ideal
\mathfrak{q}\in\Spec(R[{\textbf{x}}])
that contains
\mathfrak{m}
such that
(R[{\textbf{x}}]_{\mathfrak{q}},L_{1}(I)_{\mathfrak{q}})
is a deformation of
(R,J)
.

The attention of our work is the Cohen-Macaulay condition in this result.
While it may seem initially that this condition is not overly restrictive, the evolution of the theory, in the aforementioned work and subsequent publications like
[
HU88
]
and
[
HU90
]
, led the authors to refine their theory by focusing on Strongly Cohen-Macaulay ideals instead of Cohen-Macaulay ideals.

Surprisingly, we found out that for generic linkage to be a deformation of an arbitrary linked ideal, no conditions must be imposed on
I
. Furthermore, this fact is true for any local Cohen-Macaulay ring
(R,\mathfrak{m})
rather than Gorenstein ring in the above setting. In the last result of this work,
4.8
we show that the same result extends to the class of
(\htt(I)+1)
-residual intersections too.

The achievement is due to the study of residual intersection through
Kitt
ideals defined in
[
BH19
]
. It is shown in
3.4
that the generic Kitt ideals always specialize to the arbitrary Kitt ideal. In
3.10
we determine when this specialization is via a regular sequence. The question about the deformation of residual intersection then reduces to the cases where Kitt coincides with the residual intersection. To this end, we carefully study the codimension of Kitt ideal in
4.7
which enables us to employ previous results in
[
Has12
]
and
[
BH19
]
. Thence establishes the above surprising fact
4.8
.

For undefined notations mentioned above, we refer to the Preliminaries section.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some of our work’s basic definitions and notations. Unexplained notations are taken from the standard books
[
Mat89
]
and
[
BH93
]
.

We start by recalling the definitions of
s
-residual intersections.
Let
R
be a commutative Noetherian ring and
I
an ideal of
R
with
\grade(I)=g
. Considering
s\geq g
an integer, then

•

An
(algebraic)
s
-residual intersection
of
I
is a proper ideal
J
of
R
such that
\grade(J)\geq s
and
J=(\mathfrak{a}:_{R}I)
for some ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset I
which is generated by
s
elements.

•

A
geometric
s
-residual intersection
of
I
is an algebraic
s
-residual intersection
J
of
I
such that
\grade(I+J)\geq s+1.

•

An
arithmetic
s
-residual intersection
of
I
is an algebraic
s
-residual intersection such that
\mu_{R_{\mathfrak{p}}}((I/\mathfrak{a})_{\mathfrak{p}})\leq 1
for all prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\supseteq(I+J)
with
\grade(\mathfrak{p})\leq s
. (
\mu
denotes the minimum number of generators.)

The standard definition, cited from
[
HU88
]
, involves substituting the grade of an ideal with its height when
R
is a Cohen-Macaulay local ring.

One of the main tools in our study of residual intersections is
Residual Approximation Complexes
. The theory started and developed in
[
Has12
]
,
[
HN16
]
,
[
CNT19
]
and
[
BH19
]
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq I
finitely generated ideals of
R
. Consider
\mathbf{f}=f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
and
\mathbf{a}=\>a_{1},\dots,a_{s}
systems of generators of
I
and
\mathfrak{a}
, respectively. Let
\Phi=[c_{ij}]
be an
r\times s
matrix in
R
such that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{s}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&f_{2}&\cdots&f_{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}&\cdots&c_{1s}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}&\cdots&c_{2s}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{r1}&c_{r2}&\cdots&c_{rs}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
S=R[T_{1},\dots,T_{r}]
be the standard polynomial extension of
R
in
r
indeterminates and

\gamma_{j}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}c_{ij}T_{i}\in S_{1}

for
1\leq j\leq s
. Then we consider the
\mathcal{Z}
-complex,
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{f};R)
, of Herzog-Simis-Vasconcelos
[
HSV83
]
.

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow Z_{r}(\mathbf{f};R)\otimes_{R}S[-r]\rightarrow Z_{r-1}(\mathbf{f};R)\otimes_{R}S[-r+1]\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow Z_{0}(\mathbf{f};R)\otimes_{R}S\rightarrow 0,

where
Z_{i}(\mathbf{f};R)
is the
i
-th module of Koszul cycles of the sequence
\mathbf{f}
. In the sequel we denote the sequence
\underline{\gamma}=\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{s}
and consider the new complex given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}=\Tot\big(\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{f};R)\otimes_{S}K_{\bullet}(\underline{\gamma},S)\big),

where
K_{\bullet}=K_{\bullet}(\underline{\gamma},S)
is the Koszul complex of the sequence
\underline{\gamma}
. Notice that the
i
-th component of this complex is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{i}=\bigoplus\limits_{k+j=i}(Z_{k}(\mathbf{f};R)\otimes_{R}S(-k))\otimes_{S}S^{\binom{s}{j}}(-j)\simeq\bigoplus\limits_{k+j=i}(Z_{k}(\mathbf{f};R)\otimes_{R}S^{\binom{s}{j}})(-i).

Consider the ideal
\mathfrak{t}=(T_{1},\dots,T_{r})\subseteq S
. Tensoring the complex
\mathcal{D}_{\bullet}
with the Čech complex
\check{C}^{\bullet}_{\mathfrak{t}}(S)
, one can proceed as on the construction of the Koszul-Čech complexes and create a
\mathbb{Z}
-indexed family of complexes
{}_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{+}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{a},\Phi)
, where for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, one has

{}_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{+}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{a},\Phi):\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>0\rightarrow H^{r}_{\mathfrak{t}}(\mathcal{D}_{r+s})_{(k)}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow H^{r}_{\mathfrak{t}}(\mathcal{D}_{r+k})_{(k)}\xrightarrow{\tau_{k}}(\mathcal{D}_{k})_{(k)}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow(\mathcal{D}_{0})_{(k)}\rightarrow 0.

Hence we have the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
R
be a ring,
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq I
finitely generated ideals. Consider
\mathbf{f}=\>f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
and
\mathbf{a}=\>a_{1},\dots,a_{s}
systems of generators of
I
and
\mathfrak{a}
, respectively and let
\Phi=[c_{ij}]
be an
r\times s
matrix as above. For any integer
k
, the complex
{}_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{+}_{\bullet}=
{}_{k}\mathcal{Z}^{+}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{a},\Phi)
constructed as above is called the
k
-th residual approximation complex
with respect to the system of generators
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{a}
of
I
and
\mathfrak{a}
, respectively and the matrix
\Phi
.

Among the whole family, we focus on
k=0
and we denote this complex simply as
\mathcal{Z}^{+}_{\bullet}
.

Theorem 2.2

R

\mathfrak{a}\subseteq I

\mathbf{a}=\>a_{1},\dots,a_{s}

Let

\zeta_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{ij}e_{i}

\Gamma_{\bullet}=R\langle\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{s}\rangle

We call
\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)

of

\mathfrak{a}

In what follows, we will list some of the properties of

Theorem 2.3

Let

be a Noetherian ring,
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq I
two ideals of
R

and
s
a non-negative integer. Suppose that

and
\mathfrak{a}
are generated by
r
and

(i)

The ideal
\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)

\mathfrak{a}
and
I

[
BH19

(ii)

Denoting by

\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)=\mathfrak{a}+\langle\Gamma_{\bullet}\cdot\tilde{H}_{\bullet}\rangle_{r}

[
BH19
, Theorem 4.23]

(iii)

One always has that
\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)\subseteq J

\mu(I/\mathfrak{a})\leq 1

\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)=J

[
HN16
, Theorem 4.4]
,
[
Has12
, Theorem 2.11]
;

(iv)

\Kitt(0,I)=0:_{R}I

\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a})=(1)

\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},(1))=\mathfrak{a}

(v)

Given ideals

\mathfrak{a}\subseteq I_{1}\subseteq I

\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)\subseteq\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I_{1})

[
BH19
, Theorem 4.23]

(vi)

\mathfrak{p}
of

(vii)

Kitt specializes. Specifically, for any regular element

(viii)

Let

an
s
-residual intersection. If

\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)=J

[

, Proposition 5.10]
;

(ix)

If

(x)

(xi)

\overline{\Kitt_{R}(\mathfrak{a},I)}=\Kitt_{\bar{R}}(\bar{\mathfrak{a}},\bar{I})

[

(xii)

Assume

(xiv)

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a
d
-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay local ring,
I
an ideal of
R
with
\grade(I)=g
and
s

(i)

One says that

\mu_{R_{\mathfrak{p}}}(I_{\mathfrak{p}})\leq\htt(\mathfrak{p})

(ii)

(s-2)-

(s-2)-
residual intersection

(iii)

Let
\mathbf{f}=f_{1},\dots,f_{r}

I

k

I

\SD_{k}

\depth(H_{i}(\mathbf{f};R))\geq\min\{d-g,d-r+i+k\}

\SD

\SD_{0}

\depth(Z_{i}(\mathbf{f};R))\geq\min\{d-r+i+k,d-g+2,d\}

(iv)

The arithmetic rank of an ideal
J
is the minimum number of generators of an
ideal with the same radical as that of
J

(xii)
of
2.3

the quotient ideal

intersection. The condition on

\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})=r

\mathfrak{p}\supseteq I

[

(xii)
of

In
[
BH19

Example 2.5

Let

Definition 4.1

Given the notation established above, one defines the

One sees from the following proposition that having height at least

We keep the notation of the beginning of this section.

Proposition 4.2

Let

be a local Noetherian formally equidimensional ring,

an ideal and

\htt(\R(s,\mathbf{f}))\leq s

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that
\htt(\R(s,\mathbf{f}))>s

Denote
\mathfrak{a}=(\mathbf{a}^{\prime})\subseteq S
and let

. Observe that

{(\mathfrak{a},t):_{S[t]}(I,t)=((\mathfrak{a}:_{S}IS),t)}

Furthermore, note that

\displaystyle\htt\big((\mathfrak{a},t):_{S[t]}(IS,t)\big)=\htt((\mathfrak{a}:_{S}I),t)=\htt(\R(s,\mathbf{f}))+1>s+1

Since formal equidimensionality is stable under localization and polynomial extension
[
HIO88
, Theorem 18.13]
,

[

for the non-local ring

(I,t)S\subseteq\overline{(\mathfrak{a},t)}

(\mathfrak{a},t)
. Recall that
\mathfrak{a}
is a generic subideal of

.
By specialization

to

(I,t)
is integral over the principal ideal
(t)
. Since

.
∎

Notice that the structure of generic

.
Similarly, to what occurs with the generic Kitt, it can be demonstrated that the generic

In his Ph.D. thesis, V. Bouça proved that for any
s
-generated ideal

We noted this conclusion may not always hold. The following example is a counterexample.

Example 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field,
R=\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\dots,x_{3}]
and
T=\mathbb{K}[s,t]
. Let
\mathfrak{a}=(x_{2}^{3}-x_{1}x_{3}^{2},\>x_{0}x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}x_{3},\>x_{1}^{3}-x_{0}^{2}x_{2})\subset R

Let

\displaystyle I=\mathfrak{a}:_{R}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(x_{2}^{3}-x_{1}x_{3}^{2},\>x_{0}x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}x_{3},\>x_{1}^{3}-x_{0}^{2}x_{2},\>x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}-x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})

\displaystyle=:(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4})\supset\mathfrak{a}.

Since
J=\mathfrak{a}:_{R}I=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, one concludes that
\htt(\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I))=\htt(J)=4
. Now consider
S=R[U_{1},\dots,U_{12}]
the polynomial extension of

in twelve indeterminates and let
\alpha=\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in S
where

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&f_{2}&f_{3}&f_{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&U_{5}&U_{9}\\
U_{2}&U_{6}&U_{10}\\
U_{3}&U_{7}&U_{11}\\
U_{4}&U_{8}&U_{12}\end{bmatrix}.

Computations in
Macaulay2
shows that
\htt(\Kitt^{\mathfrak{g}}(3,\mathbf{f}))=\htt(\RI(3,\mathbf{f}))=3
. Hence one obtains an specialization
\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)
of
\Kitt^{\mathfrak{g}}(3,\mathbf{f})
such that
\htt(\Kitt^{\mathfrak{g}}(3,\mathbf{f}))<\htt(\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I))
.

Taking this direction, we proved that, under more restrictive conditions on the ring, the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\htt(\Kitt^{\mathfrak{g}}(s,\mathbf{f})_{\mathfrak{n}})\geq\htt(\Kitt(\mathfrak{a},I)_{\mathfrak{m}})

for any maximal ideal
\mathfrak{n}\subseteq S
containing
\Kitt^{\mathfrak{g}}(s,\mathbf{f})+(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{n}\cap R
,
\mathbf{x}=U_{11}-c_{11},\dots,U_{rs}-c_{rs}
and

are such that
a_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}c_{ki}f_{k}
for all

Two lemmas are required to prove this result.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
elements of
R
. Consider
S=R[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
the polynomial extension of
R
in
n
indeterminates and let
\mathfrak{n}
be an ideal of
R
containing
\mathfrak{c}:=(X_{1}-c_{1},\dots,X_{n}-c_{n})
. Then

(i)

If
\mathfrak{n}
is a proper ideal of
S
, then
\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)S\subset S
also is a proper ideal of
S
;

(ii)

If
\mathfrak{n}
is a maximal ideal of
S
, then
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{c}+\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)S
.

Proof:

(i)
Suppose by contradiction that
\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)=S
. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathfrak{n}
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in S
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(c)g_{k}(X)=1.

Applying the division algorithm, there are
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\in\mathfrak{c}
such that
f_{k}=h_{k}+f_{k}(c)
for all
k=1,\dots,m
. Thus

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(f_{k}(X)-h_{k}(X))g_{k}(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(X)g_{k}(X)-\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}(X)g_{k}(X)\in\mathfrak{n}+\mathfrak{c}=\mathfrak{n},

giving a contradiction.

(ii)
By using the division algorithm, one can see that
\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{c}+\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)S
. Hence, since
\mathfrak{n}
is maximal, it is enough to show that
\mathfrak{c}+\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)S
is a proper ideal. Suppose by contradiction that
\mathfrak{c}+\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)S=S
. Let
h\in\mathfrak{c}
,
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathfrak{n}
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in S
such that

\displaystyle h(X)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(c)g_{k}(X)=1.

Evaluating in
(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
, one gets that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(c)g_{k}(c)=1
, contradicting the item
(i)
. Hence
\mathfrak{c}+\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)
is a proper ideal of
S
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
R
be a Noetherian equi-codimensional ring i.e.

\mathfrak{m}

of

\htt(\mathfrak{n})=\dim(S)=\dim(R)+n.

Proof:
Let
\mathfrak{n}
be a maximal ideal of

containing
\mathfrak{c}:=(x_{1}-c_{1},,\dots,x_{n}-c_{n})
. Firstly notice that, since
\mathfrak{n}
contains
\mathfrak{c}

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{c}+(\mathfrak{n}\cap R)S
. Indeed, by

\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{c}+\big(\{f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\>\>;\>\>f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathfrak{n}\}\big)S=:\mathfrak{c}+\mathfrak{a}.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{c}+(\mathfrak{a}\cap R)S=\mathfrak{c}+(\mathfrak{n}\cap R)S\subseteq\mathfrak{n}+\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}

and the equality follows. As

is a prime ideal of

, then
\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{c}+\mathfrak{m}S
for some maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of

Evaluating this expression in

\mathfrak{n}\cap R=\mathfrak{m}
. Applying

\displaystyle\htt_{S}(\mathfrak{n})=\htt_{R}(\mathfrak{m})+\dim\bigg(\frac{S_{\mathfrak{n}}}{\mathfrak{m}S_{\mathfrak{n}}}\bigg)=\dim(R)+\dim\bigg(\frac{S_{\mathfrak{n}}}{\mathfrak{m}S_{\mathfrak{n}}}\bigg).
