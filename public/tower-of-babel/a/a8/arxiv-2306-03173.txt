Linear Distance Metric Learning with Noisy Labels
Meysam Alishahi alishahi@cs.utah.edu, Anna Little little@math.utah.edu, Jeff M. Phillips jeffp@cs.utah.edu

Abstract
In linear distance metric learning, we are given data in one Euclidean metric space and the goal is to find an appropriate linear map to another Euclidean metric space which respects certain distance conditions as much as possible. In this paper, we formalize a simple and elegant method which reduces to a general continuous convex loss optimization problem, and for different noise models we derive the corresponding loss functions. We show that even if the data is noisy, the ground truth linear metric can be learned with any precision provided access to enough samples, and we provide a corresponding sample complexity bound. Moreover, we present an effective way to truncate the learned model to a low-rank model that can provably maintain the accuracy in the loss function and in parameters – the first such results of this type. Several experimental observations on synthetic and real data sets support and inform our theoretical results.



Meysam Alishahi, Anna Little, and Jeff M. Phillips

1
Introduction

The goal of distance metric learning is to map data in a metric space into another metric space in such a way that the distance between points in the second space optimizes some condition on the data.
Early work in this area focuses mostly on the Euclidean to Euclidean setting, and specifically on the case of learning linear transformations. For data
{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, it attempts to learn a Mahalanobis distance
{\rm d}_{\boldsymbol{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
as

{\rm d}_{\boldsymbol{M}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}}=\sqrt{(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})^{t}{\boldsymbol{M}}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})}
.
This is a metric on the original space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as long as
{\boldsymbol{M}}
is positive definite. We can decompose
{\boldsymbol{M}}
as
{\boldsymbol{M}}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{t}
for
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then
{\boldsymbol{A}}
can be used as a linear map so that
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{X}}
is another point set of
n
points in
d
dimensions; for
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{t}\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{t}\boldsymbol{y}
in the new space,
\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}}
is equivalent to the standard Euclidean distance
\|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\|
.

Linear metric learning has been studied in
Weinberger and Saul 2009
for kNN classification, in
Torresani and Lee 2006
;
Wang and Zhang 2007
;
Xiang et al. 2008
via margin/distance optimization, in
Sugiyama 2007
;
Bar-Hillel et al. 2005
via discriminant analysis, and in
Nguyen et al. 2017
via Jeffrey divergence.
Many of these linear methods also propose kernelized versions, and kernelized metric learning was also considered in
Mika et al. 1999
;
Roth and Steinhage 1999
.
But the current state of the art uses arbitrarily complex neural encoders that attempt to optimize the final objective with very little restriction on the form or structure of the mapping
(
Ermolov et al. 2022
)
. Merging with the area of feature engineering
(
Nargesian et al. 2017
;
Shi et al. 2009
)
, these approaches are an integral element of information retrieval
(
Patel et al. 2021
;
Ramzi et al. 2022
)
, natural language processing
(
Mikawa et al. 2011
;
Li et al. 2017
)
, and image processing
(
Chopra et al. 2005
)
. To access further details, readers can refer to two well-conducted surveys
Bellet et al. 2013
;
Kulis 2013
.

In this work, we revisit
linear
distance metric learning. We posit that there are two useful extremes in this problem, the anything-goes non-linear approaches mentioned above, and the very restrictive linear approaches. The linear approaches exhibit a number of important properties which are essential for certain applications:

•

When the original coordinates of the data points have meaning, but for instance are measured in different units (e.g., inches and pounds), then one may want to retain that meaning and interpretability while making the process invariant to the original underlying (and often arbitrary) choice of units.

•

Many geometric properties such as linear separability, convexity, straight-line connectivity, vector translation (linear parallel transport) are preserved under affine transformations. If such features are assumed to be meaningful on the original data, then they are retained under a linear transformation.

•

Some physical equations, such as those describing ordinary differential equations (ODEs) can be simulated through a linear transform
(
Sutherland and Parente 2009
)
. We will demonstrate an example application of this (in Section
5.5
) where because of changing units, it is not clear how to measure distance in the original space, and locality based learning can be more effectively employed after a linear transformation.

While several prior works have already explored linear distance metric learning
(
Weinberger and Saul 2009
;
Torresani and Lee 2006
;
Sugiyama 2007
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
, they often reduce to novel optimization settings where specially designed solvers and analysis are required. For instance,
Ying and Li 2012
utilize a clever subgradient descent formulation to ensure the learned
{\boldsymbol{M}}
retains its positive-definiteness.
In our work we provide a simple and natural formulation that converts the linear distance metric learning task into a simple supervised convex gradient descent procedure, basically a standard supervised classification task where any procedure for smooth convex optimization can be employed.

1.1
Formulation

Specifically, we assume
N
i.i.d. observations
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and each pair is given a label
\ell_{i}\in\{{\rm Far,Close}\}
. Our goal is to learn a positive semi-definite (p.s.d.) matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
and threshold
\tau\geq 0
so that
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{2}\geq\tau
if
\ell_{i}={\rm Far}
and
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{2}<\tau
if
\ell_{i}={\rm Close}
.
Towards solving this we formulate an optimization problem

\min_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\tau&\geq 0\\
{\boldsymbol{M}}&\succeq 0\end{array}}R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=\min_{\begin{array}[]{cc}\tau&\geq 0\\
{\boldsymbol{M}}&\succeq 0\end{array}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\ell_{i};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

(1)

where
L(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\ell_{i};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is a loss function that penalizes the mismatch between the observed label and the model-predicted label. Then we propose to optimize this in an (almost, except for
\tau\geq 0
) unconstrained setting, where we can apply standard techniques like (stochastic) gradient descent:

\min_{\begin{array}[]{l}\tau\in\mathbb{R}\\
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\end{array}}R_{N}({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{t},\tau)

1.2
Our core results

We analyze this simple, flexible, and powerful formulation and show that:

•

This optimization problem is convex over
{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau
. Moreover, while optimizing over
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is not convex, we can leverage an observation of
Journée et al. 2010
to show that the minimizer
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
over the unconstrained formulation generates
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})^{t}
, which is the minimizer over the convex, but (positive semi-definite) constrained formulation over
{\boldsymbol{M}}
.

•

The sample complexity of this problem is
N_{d}(\varepsilon,\delta)=O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\log\frac{1}{\delta}+d^{2}\log\frac{d}{\varepsilon}))
. More specifically, let
f
be the pdf of the distribution from which difference pairs
\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}
are drawn, let
\ell
be the (noisy) label associated with a pair, and let
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=\mathbf{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\sim f}[L(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)]
be the expected loss.
Then, given
N_{d}(\varepsilon,\delta)
observations,
|R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})|\leq\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
is the minimizer of
R_{N}
.

•

If the labels
\ell_{i}

are observed with unbiased noise, and the loss function is chosen appropriately to match that noise distribution, then
R_{N}
still approximates
R
, and in fact, the minimizers
\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\tau}
of
R_{N}
converge to the true minimizers
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*}
of
R
.

•

Returning a low-rank approximation
\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}
to
\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}
can achieve bounded error with respect to
|R(\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})|
and
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}-{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\|+|\hat{\tau}-\tau^{*}|
, as elaborated on just below. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such dimensionality reduction result of this kind.

1.3
Reasonable Choice for the Loss Function
L
: Logistic noise

We assume the labeling of {Close, Far} through the evaluation of
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{2}
is noisy.
We will prove that if the noise comes from the Logistic distribution, then

L(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\ell_{i};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=-\log\sigma(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{2}-\tau))

serves as an excellent theoretical choice; here
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
is known as the
Logistic
function.
We note that prior work
(
Guillaumin et al. 2009
)
also considered this special case of our formulation, and provided an empirical study on face identification;
however they did not theoretically analyze this formulation.

As mentioned, our work will show that this form of
L
is indeed optimal under a Logistic noise model. We also show that if one assumes a different noise model (e.g., Gaussian), then a different loss function would be more appropriate.
Furthermore, we show that irrespective to the amount of unbiased noise, we are able to recover the ground truth parameters if we observe enough noisy data, and we provide precise sample complexity bounds.
Section
5
also confirms these theoretical results with careful experimental observations and demonstrates that the method is in fact robust to misspecification of the noise model.

1.4
Dimensionality Reduction

It is natural to ask if linear distance metric learning approaches can be used for linear dimensionality reduction. That is, if one restricts to a rank-
k
positive semi-definite
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}
, then we can write
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}^{t}
where
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
. Hence
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}
can be used as a linear map
x^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}^{t}x
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Yet optimizing
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}^{t},\tau)
with
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
is not only non-convex, the optimization has non-optimal local minima
(
Journée et al. 2010
)
.

Another natural approach is to run the optimization with a full rank
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, and then truncate
{\boldsymbol{A}}
by rounding down its smallest
d-k
singular values to
0
. As far as we know, no previous analysis of a distance metric learning (DML) approach has shown if this is effective; a direction (singular vector of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
) associated with a small singular value of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
could potentially have out-sized relevance towards cost function
R_{N}
that we seek to optimize, and this step may induce uncontrolled error in
R_{N}
.

In this paper, we detail some reasonable assumptions on the data necessary for this singular value rounding scheme to have provable guarantees. The key assumption is that the width of the support of the data distribution is bounded by
\sqrt{F}
(for some parameter
F
),
and either this support must include measure in regions which assign labels of both Close and Far, or the unbiased noise must be large enough to generate some of each label.

Specifically we consider the following algorithm.

1) Sample
N=N_{d}(\varepsilon,\delta)
pairs
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
from a Lebesgue measurable distribution with the width of support at most
\sqrt{F}
in each direction.

2) Solve for
\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\hat{\tau}\geq 0
in
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{t},\tau)
using any convex gradient descent solver.

3) For positive integer
k\leq d
, set the
d-k
smallest singular values of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
to
0
, resulting in low-rank matrix
\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{k}
. Let
\gamma
be the value of the
(d-k)
-th singular value of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}
(the largest one rounded to
0
).

4) Return
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k}=\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{k}\hat{{\boldsymbol{A}}}_{k}^{t}
, a rank-
k
positive semi-definite matrix.

For this algorithm (formalized in Theorems
14
and
15
), we claim with probability at least
1-\delta

|R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})|\leq\varepsilon+F\gamma^{2}.

Thus, for instance if we draw
\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}
from a unit ball (so
F=1
), and set
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\varepsilon
, and assume that
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
has
d-k
eigenvalues less than
\varepsilon^{\prime}/4
, then with probability at least
1-\delta

|R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Outline

In Section
2
we more carefully unroll this model formulation, and in Section
3
we show sample complexity and convergence results, including under noise.
In Section
4
we discuss the optimization procedure and show that the unconstrained optimization approach is provably effective, and useful for dimensionality reduction tasks.

Model and Key Observations

This section contains the model, the underlying assumptions, and some observations used throughout the paper.

2.1
Data and Model Assumptions

We work under the following data model throughout the paper.
We assume there exists some positive semi-definite
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
that defines a Mahalanobis distance that we seek to discover. We observe pairs of data points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but we only consider the differences of the pairs
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
. Moreover, we assume that all observations
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are i.i.d. from some unknown distribution with pdf
f(\boldsymbol{z})
.

We also assume there exists a threshold
\tau^{*}
which generates labels
\ell_{i}\in\{{\rm Close,Far}\}
; more specifically,
z_{i}
is Close if and only if

\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}+\eta_{i}<\tau^{*}\,,

(Label Assumption)

where
\eta_{i}
is a noise term. Each
\eta_{i}
is generated i.i.d. from a distribution
{\rm Noise}(\eta|0,s)
which
comes form a location-scale family of distributions with location
0
and scale parameter
s>0
.
For different distributions,
s
can have a different meaning. For example, for the normal distribution,
s
is the standard deviation. For mathematical convenience, we overload “Close” = -1 and “Far” = +1 so
\ell_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
. As

\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}+\eta_{i}<\tau^{*}
is equivalent to
\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|_{\frac{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}{t}}^{2}+\frac{\eta_{i}}{t}<\frac{\tau^{*}}{t}
for any
t>0
,
scaling
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
,
\tau^{*}
, and
s
by a common parameter
t
does not change the labeling distribution, so w.l.o.g., we remove this degree of freedom in the analysis presented in this paper by setting
s=1
. Thus what the techniques in this paper ultimately recover is actually
\frac{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}{s}
and
\frac{\tau^{*}}{s}
. In other words, we will see that our model is identifiable if
s=1
. Moreover, the following lemma indicates that for the noiseless case, the model is identifiable provided that
\tau=1
.
The following formalization is proved in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 1
.

Given two pairs
({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1})
and
({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})
such that
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}\succeq 0
and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}>0
, if the two functions

\boldsymbol{z}\mapsto\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\tau_{1}\geq 0\right\}}
and
\boldsymbol{z}\mapsto\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}-\tau_{2}\geq 0\right\}}
agree for all
\boldsymbol{z}
, then

\frac{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}{\tau_{1}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}{\tau_{2}}.

In order to be able to solve our optimization challenges and bound the sample complexity, we need to make a few simple assumptions on
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*}
and the data distribution. We have the following assumption on the model:

\|{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\beta\;\text{ and }\;\tau^{*}\in[0,B]\,.

(Model Assumption)

Note that as we have assumed that
s=1
,
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
and
\tau^{*}
, as well as the upper bounds
\beta
and
B
, have been scaled by
1/s
.
Since
\beta
and
B
later appear in the sample complexity (see Section
5.3.2
), noise affects the sample complexity through these terms.

Next we assume something about the data we observe.
Assume
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
N
i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution with probability density function
f(\boldsymbol{z})
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Hence
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{z}){\rm d}x=1
which implies that the set
\{\boldsymbol{z}\colon f(\boldsymbol{z})\neq 0\}
has a positive Lebesgue measure.
We also assume that the probability density function
f(\boldsymbol{z})
has bounded support, i.e.,

\max\{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}\colon f(\boldsymbol{z})\neq 0\}\leq F.

(Data Assumption)

By this assumption, we know that almost surely
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}\leq F
.
Also, by the
Data Assumption
, for each

{\boldsymbol{M}}\in{\mathcal{M}_{d}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:{\boldsymbol{M}}\text{ is p.s.d., }\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{2}\leq\beta\right\},

we have

\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}\leq\beta F
.
When it is clear by context, we sometimes write
\mathcal{M}
for
{\mathcal{M}_{d}}
.
Accordingly, for
\boldsymbol{z}\sim f(\boldsymbol{z})
, almost always

\left|\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau\right|\leq\max\{B,\beta F\}\quad\quad\forall({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in{\mathcal{M}_{d}}\times[0,B].

Since the sign of
\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau
determines the label of
\boldsymbol{z}
, it is reasonable to assume that

B\leq\beta F,

(Meta Assumption)

which implies

\left|\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau\right|\leq\beta F\quad\quad\forall({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in{\mathcal{M}_{d}}\times[0,B].

(2)

2.2
Convexity

Our core objective is optimizing
R_{N}
or
R
over
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
such that
{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq 0,\tau\geq 0
.
In the same vein as regular supervised learning scenarios, we consider loss functions that are convex with respect to the contribution of each data point
(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\ell_{i})
. To show the loss functions over the space of valid parameters are convex, we first show that any convex combination of two valid models’ parameters is still a valid parameter and for any two models with parameters
({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1})
and
({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})
and an interpolation parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have

\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\lambda{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}+(1-\lambda){\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}-(\lambda\tau_{1}+(1-\lambda)\tau_{2})=\lambda(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\tau_{1})+(1-\lambda)(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}-\tau_{2}).

Hence, the convex interpolation of the two models gives us another model with parameter
({\boldsymbol{M}}=\lambda{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}+(1-\lambda){\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau=\lambda\tau_{1}+(1-\lambda)\tau_{2})
which is in the space of the valid parameters. Coupled with a convex loss function, this implies that minimizing
R_{N}
or
R
over
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
with
{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq 0
and
\tau\geq 0
is a convex optimization problem. Hence any critical point is a global minimum.
However, restricting
{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq 0
under gradient descent is non-trivial since generically the gradient may push the solution out of that. While manifold optimization methods have been developed for other matrix optimization challenges
(
Balzano et al. 2010
;
Vandereycken 2013
)
, we develop a simpler unconstrained approach in this work.

3
Noise Observations and Optimal Loss Functions

Recall that
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
N
i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution with probability density function
f(\boldsymbol{z})
with bounded support. As the noise distribution
\mathrm{Noise}(\eta|0,1)
makes the labeling probabilistic,
for a given
\boldsymbol{z}\sim f(\boldsymbol{z})
, the probability that the corresponding label is
1
can be computed as

\displaystyle P(\ell=1|\boldsymbol{z};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

\displaystyle=P(\eta>\tau-\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2}-\tau}{\rm Noise}(\eta|0,1){\rm d}\eta

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2}-\tau),

(3)

where
\Phi_{\rm Noise}(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{a}{\rm Noise}(\eta|0,1){\rm d}\eta
.
Observe that

P(\ell=-1|\boldsymbol{z};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=1-P(\ell=1|\boldsymbol{z};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=\Phi_{\rm Noise}(-1(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2}-\tau)).

Consequently, we have

\boldsymbol{z},\ell\sim g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=f(\boldsymbol{z})\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right),

where
g
is the density of the random variable
(z,\ell)
with respect to
\mu_{L}\otimes\nu
(the product of the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{L}
and the counting measure
\nu
).
Our theoretical results hold under quite general assumptions on the noise. In particular,
the noise should be symmetric, continuous, non-zero everywhere, and
-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}
should be convex and
\zeta
-Lipschitz on
[-\beta F,\beta F]
. Throughout the paper, we refer to any noise satisfying these assumptions as a
simple noise model
, and specifically consider the Logistic, Normal, Laplace, and Hyperbolic secant (HS) distributions as special cases. Table

, and in the remainder of this article we will refer to
{\rm Noise}(\eta|0,1)
as
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
for brevity.

-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}

,

Since we have a probabilistic model, we now use the MLE method to estimate
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*}
.
As one of the main contributions of this work, we will prove that this method works and deduce the corresponding sample complexity.
The average negative log-likelihood of the given data
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{N}
and their labels
\ell_{i},\ldots,\ell_{N}
as a function of
{\boldsymbol{M}}
and
\tau
is

\displaystyle{\rm NLL}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log g(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\ell_{i};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\log f(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})+\log p(\ell_{i}|\boldsymbol{z}_{i};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log f(\boldsymbol{z}_{i})}_{\text{independent of ${\boldsymbol{M}},\tau$}}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p(\ell_{i}|\boldsymbol{z}_{i};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)}_{\text{the loss function}}.

Therefore, to solve the MLE, we need to find a p.s.d. matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
and a
\tau\in[0,B]
minimizing

\displaystyle R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p(\ell_{i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i};{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right).

Thus the optimization problem we are dealing with is

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq 0,\tau\geq 0}R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau),

(5)

where
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2}-\tau)\right)
.

We will justify that solving this optimization problem with high probability ends up in a guaranteed approximation of
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
.
For fixed but arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau
, using Chebyshev’s inequality (or directly by the Law of Large Numbers), we know that

R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
tends to (in measure induced by
g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
)

\displaystyle R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{z,\ell\sim g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)

(6)

\displaystyle=-\int g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)d\boldsymbol{z}d\ell

provided that
\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)
has a bounded variance, which is the case since

f(\boldsymbol{z})
has bounded support.
Thus we can view
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
as the empirical risk function and
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
as the true risk function.
However, we only have access to
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
.
The rest of this subsection can be summarized as follows:

1.

We prove that both functions
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
and
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
are convex, so we are dealing with convex optimization problems.

2.

We prove that
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is uniquely minimized at
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
.

3.

We show that
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
converges uniformly in measure to
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
. We also bound the corresponding error for an arbitrarily given confidence bound.

4.

Combining these results, we conclude that minimizing
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is a good proxy for minimizing
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
.

Theorem 2
.

The true loss
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is uniquely minimized at
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
.

Proof.

First, note that

\displaystyle R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z},\ell\sim g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}\left(\log\frac{\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*}))}{\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau))}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z},\ell\sim g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}\left(\log\frac{f(\boldsymbol{z})\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*}))}{f(\boldsymbol{z})\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau))}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm D_{KL}}(g(\boldsymbol{x},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|g(\boldsymbol{x},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau))\geq 0.

This indicates that
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
takes its minimum at
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
. Moreover, for any
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{+},\tau^{+})
at which
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
attains its minimum,

{\rm D_{KL}}(g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{+},\tau^{+}))=0.

This implies that
g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})=g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{+},\tau^{+})
almost everywhere
(according to the probability measure induced by
g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,1\}
).
Since
\mu_{L}(\{\boldsymbol{z}\colon f(\boldsymbol{z})>0\})>0
(Lebesgue measure) and
\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\cdot)
is one-to-one, we can conclude that there is a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that
\mu_{L}(S)>0
and for every
\boldsymbol{z}\in S
,

\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*}=\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{+}}-\tau^{+}.

By Lemma
16
in Appendix
B
, we then conclude

{\boldsymbol{M}}^{+}={\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
and
\tau^{+}=\tau^{*}
.
∎

Although we have proved that the true loss
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is uniquely minimized at
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
, in reality, we do not have access to the true loss, but only to the empirical loss
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
.
Next we will show that
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is uniformly close to
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
as
N
gets large, and then conclude that instead of minimizing
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
, we can minimize
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
to approximate
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
. Note that
for two given p.s.d.
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}
,

|\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}|\leq\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}.

(7)

For proof, see Appendix
D
: Observation
3
.
In the next lemma, using this inequality, we prove that the true loss and empirical loss are both Lipschitz with respect to the metric

{\rm d}(({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1}),({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2}))=\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}\|_{2}+|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|.

Lemma 3
.

If
\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\cdot)
is
\zeta
-Lipschitz, then, for any given
({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1}),({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})\in\mathcal{M}\times[0,B]
,

1.

|R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})|<\zeta(F+1){\rm d}(({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1}),({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})),

2.

|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1})-R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})|<\zeta(F+1){\rm d}(({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1}),({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})).

Proof.

We start with the proof of the first inequality.
In view of Equation
6
, we have

\displaystyle|R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z},\ell}\Big[\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\tau_{1})\right)-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}-\tau_{2})\right)\Big]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z},\ell}\Big|\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\tau_{1})\right)-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}-\tau_{2})\right)\Big|

(
\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\cdot)
is
\zeta
-Lipschitz)

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\Big[\Big|(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\tau_{1})-(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}-\tau_{2})\Big|\Big]

(using
7
)

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\Big|\boldsymbol{z}^{t}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})\boldsymbol{z}\Big|+\zeta\mathbb{E}\Big[|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\|({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})\|_{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}\Big]+\zeta|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|

(
Data Assumption
)

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\|({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})\|_{2}F+\zeta|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|

\displaystyle<\zeta(F+1){\rm d}\big(({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1}),({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})\big),

where
(\boldsymbol{z},\ell)\sim g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
(see Equation
4
). Note that

R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=-\expt_{(\boldsymbol{z},\ell)\in_{u}\{(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\ell_{i})\colon i\in[N]\}}\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right),

where
(\boldsymbol{z},\ell)\in_{u}\{(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\ell_{i})\colon i\in[N]\}
indicates uniform distribution over
\{(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\ell_{i})\colon i\in[N]\}
.
If we use the uniform distribution over

\{(\boldsymbol{z}_{i},\ell_{i})\colon i=1,\ldots,N\}
in place of
g
, then, with an identical approach, we have the second statement.
∎

As
-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\cdot)
is a decreasing function, using Equation
2
, we have

0\leq-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)\leq-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}(-\beta F)=T,

(8)

which indicates that the random variables
-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)
are bounded by a value
T
; see Table
1
.
In the next theorem, we prove that, with high probability, the empirical loss
R_{N}
is everywhere close to the true loss
R
. We provide a sketch of the proof here; for the complete proof, see Appendix
D
. The full bound for
N_{d}(\varepsilon,\delta)
appears in
14
; it is
a polynomial function of
F,T,\log(B),\log(\beta)
, but for simplicity of presentation we omit the precise dependence on these constants.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that the noise model
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
is simple.
For any
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, define

N_{d}(\varepsilon,\delta)=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big[\log\frac{1}{\delta}+d^{2}\log\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\Big]\right).

If

N>N_{d}(\varepsilon,\delta)
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in\mathcal{M}\times[0,B]}\left|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\right|<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Set
\alpha=\frac{\varepsilon}{3\zeta(F+1)}
.
Consider
\mathcal{E}=\{({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i});i=1,\ldots,m=m(\alpha)\}
as an
\alpha
-cover for
\mathcal{M}\times[0,B].

By Lemma
3
, for every
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
, there exists an index
i\in[m]
such that

|R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

which concludes

\left|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\right|\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}+|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})|.

Using an appropriate upper bound for
m(\alpha)=\frac{B}{\alpha}(4\beta d\sqrt{d}/\alpha)^{d^{2}}
in Lemma
17
, and applying a Chernoff-Hoeffding bound and union bound, we conclude the desired result (for full details, see Appendix
D
).
∎

Note that combining Theorem
2
and Theorem
4
, we conclude that minimizing
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is a good proxy for minimizing
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
.
We restate this result in the next theorem.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that the noise model
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
is simple.
For any given
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, if
N>N(\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\delta)
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
for any point
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
minimizing
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
, we have

0<R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})<\varepsilon.

Proof.

As
N>N(\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\delta)
, by Theorem
4
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\left|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\quad\quad\text{for all }({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in\mathcal{M}\times[0,B].

Consequently, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

\displaystyle<R_{N}(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\quad\quad\text{($R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)$ minimized at $(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})$)}

\displaystyle<R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

implying that

0\leq R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\leq\varepsilon.

∎

In the next four subsections, we consider the simple noise models determined by the Logistic, Gaussian, Laplace, and Hyperbolic secant distributions.

3.1
Logistic Distribution as the Noise

The probability density function of the Logistic distribution is

{\rm L}(x|\mu,s)=\frac{1}{4s}{\rm sech}^{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{2s}\right),

where
\mu
is the mean,
s
is the scale parameter of this distribution, and
\frac{s^{2}\pi^{2}}{3}
is
the variance.
The Logistic distribution looks very much like a normal distribution and is sometimes used as an approximation for it. In this subsection, we assume that the noise has a Logistic distribution with
\mu=0
and
s=1
, i.e.,

{\rm Noise}(\eta)={\rm L}(\eta|0,1)
(since scaling
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*}
, and
\eta
does not change the labeling distribution, w.l.o.g. we may assume
s=1
). The Cumulative distribution function of
{\rm L}(x|0,1)
is the sigmoid function

\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
, i.e.,

\Phi_{\rm L}(x)=\sigma(x).

As the Logistic noise is simple with
\zeta=1
(see Table
1
), we can apply Theorem
4
.
Plugging
\sigma(x)
in for
\Phi_{\rm Noise}(x)
in Optimization Problem
5
, we obtain

R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sigma\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2}-\tau)\right)
.

Since in the Logistic case we have a closed form for
\Phi_{\rm L}(x)=\sigma(x),
the loss function becomes computationally easier to work with.
As the main setting for the paper, we will thus assume that the noise comes from a Logistic distribution, although we consider other noise models and loss functions in our experiments.

3.2
Normal Distribution as the Noise

If we assume the noise has a Normal instead of a Logistic distribution,
then
\Phi_{\rm Noise}(x)
becomes a probit function instead of the sigmoid function.
Indeed, if we set
{\rm Noise}(\eta)=\mathcal{N}(\eta|0,1)
, then

\Phi(a)=\Phi_{\rm Noise}(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{a}\mathcal{N}(\eta|0,1){\rm d}\eta,

which is known as the
probit function
.
Once again plugging
\Phi
into
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
in Optimization Problem
5
, we obtain

R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\Phi\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2}-\tau)\right)
.
Unfortunately, the probit function has no closed-form formula, so optimizing
R_{N}
is not as simple as in the Logistic case. However we will observe in Section
5
that since the Logistic and Gaussian distributions are very similar, the Logistic loss does well under Normal noise.

3.3
Laplace Distribution as the Noise

As the third natural option for noise, we assume that

{\rm Noise}(\eta)={\mathcal{}\rm Laplace}(\eta|0,1)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|\eta|}.

In this setting,

\Phi_{{\rm Laplace}}(a)=\int_{-\infty}^{a}\frac{1}{2}e^{-|\eta|}{\rm d}\eta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}e^{a}&a\leq 0\\
\\
1-\frac{1}{2}e^{-a}&a\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

Similar to the Logistic case, we obtain a closed-form formula for
\Phi_{{\rm Laplace}}(a)
,
so that this setting is also convenient to work with.
Note that

-\log\Phi_{{\rm Laplace}}(a)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-a+\log 2&a\leq 0\\
\\
-\log\left(1-\frac{1}{2}e^{-a}\right)&a\geq 0\end{array}\right.\,,

which yields a closed-form formula for
R_{N}
.

3.4
Hyperbolic Secant Distribution as the Noise

The hyperbolic secant distribution is a continuous probability distribution whose probability density function is

{\rm HS}(\eta|\mu,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\sigma}{\rm sech}(\frac{\pi}{2\sigma}(\eta-\mu))

and whose Cumulative distribution function is

\Phi_{\rm HS}(\eta|\mu,\sigma)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(\exp({\frac{\pi}{2\sigma}(\eta-\mu)})).

The mean and variance of this distribution are
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
respectively.
As the last option for noise, we consider
{\rm Noise}(\eta)={\rm HS}(\eta|0,1)
.
In this case, we have

\Phi_{\rm HS}(a)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\left(\exp({\frac{\pi}{2}\eta})\right).

Plugging
\Phi_{\rm HS}
into
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
in Optimization Problem
5
, we obtain

R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left[\arctan\left(\exp\left({\frac{\pi}{2}\left(-\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{2}-\tau)\right)}\right)\right)\right]+{\rm Constant}\,,

and we can ignore the constant term.

4
Algorithms, Approximation, and Dimensionality Reduction

In this part, we mainly explore some properties of Optimization Problem
5
and consider the potential ways to solve it.

4.1
How to Solve Optimization Problem
5

We will prove that solving Optimization Problem
5
yields close approximations of the parameters
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
and
\tau^{*}
.
We restate the optimization problem here:

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq 0,\tau\geq 0}R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau).

(9)

As we need to enforce
{\boldsymbol{M}}
to be p.s.d., using gradient descent directly is difficult.
Notice that
{\boldsymbol{M}}
is p.s.d. if and only if
{\boldsymbol{M}}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}
for some
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{d\times k}
where
k\leq d
; in this case
{\boldsymbol{M}}
indeed has rank at most
k
.
Therefore, if we replace
{\boldsymbol{M}}
by
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}
and optimize over
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, we no longer need to maintain the p.s.d. condition on
{\boldsymbol{M}}
. Then the optimization problem can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\tau\geq 0,{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}R_{N}({\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top},\tau).

(10)

If we set
k=d
, then Optimization
10
is equivalent to Optimization
5
.
The only downside of this reformulation is that we lose the convexity by this change of variable.
So we are dealing with a non-convex optimization, and thus there may be no guarantee that gradient descent will converge to a global minimum.
Fortunately, the next theorem proved by
Journée et al. 2010
resolves this issue.
We remind the reader that for convex optimization problems, global minimums and stationary points are equivalent.

Theorem 6

(
Journée et al. 2010
)
.

A local minimizer
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
of Problem
10
provides a stationary point (global minimum)
{\boldsymbol{M}}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})^{\top}
of Problem
9

if
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
is rank deficient
({\rm rank}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})<k)
.
Moreover, if
d=k
, then any local minimizer
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
of Problem
10
provides a stationary
point (global minimum)
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})^{\top}
of Problem
9
.

So, we can use gradient descent for
k=d
to find a local minimum
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
of Problem
9
, then using this theorem we know that

{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})^{\top}
is a global minimum of Problem
5
.
Another possible approach is to try some
k<d
, and if
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
is rank deficient, then again
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})^{\top}
is a global minimum of Problem
5
.
However even if we know that the solution for Problem
9
has rank
r<k
, we might find
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
to be full rank. Indeed, setting
k>r
does not imply that
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})^{\top}
is the global minimum of Problem
9
. Moreover, Problem
9
is a generalization of Low-Rank Semi-definite Programming, which is known to be an NP-Hard problem
(
Anjos and Wolkowicz 2002
)
(weighted Max-Cut is a special case of it), which indicates that solving it when
k<d
might be a difficult task.

4.2
How Well is
(\mathbf{M}^{*},\tau^{*})
Approximated?

Throughout this section, for simplicity of notation and w.l.o.g we assume that
\zeta F=1
.
In this section, we will see that, with high probability, we can approximate
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
with any given precision if
N
is large enough.
Recall that Theorem
2
establishes that
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is uniquely minimized at
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
.
Theorem
5
asserts that if
N
is large enough, the value of the true loss on parameters minimizing the empirical loss, i.e.
R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
, is close to the minimum of the true loss,
R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
. Although we can infer from this theorem that the error at the ground truth parameters
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
is close to the error at
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
, it is still possible that
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
and
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
are far from each other with respect to the metric

{\rm d}(({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*}),(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau}))=\|{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}\|_{2}+|\tau^{*}-\hat{\tau}|.

Recall that the random variable
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is generated from an unknown distribution with probability density function
f(\boldsymbol{z})
with bounded support.
Let us define the
L_{1}(f)
-norm of
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
as

\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\|_{L_{1}(f)}=\int f(\boldsymbol{x})\left|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}{\boldsymbol{M}}\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}.

This is a norm on the vector space
\{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\colon{\boldsymbol{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\text{ is symmetric },\tau\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Note that if
\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\|_{L_{1}(f)}=0
, then Lemma
16
along with the fact that
\mu_{L}\left(\{\boldsymbol{x}\colon f(\boldsymbol{x})>0\}\right)>0
implies
{\boldsymbol{M}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
and
\tau=0
. The other required properties follow by standard reductions.
This norm naturally induces the following
L_{1}(f)
-metric

\displaystyle\|({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},\tau_{1})-({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2},\tau_{2})\|_{L_{1}(f)}

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{x})\left|(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}-\tau_{1})-(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}-\tau_{2})\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}.

(11)

Theorem 7
.

Assume that the noise model
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
is simple and set
\omega=\min\limits_{|\eta|\leq\beta F}{\rm Noise}(\eta)
; see Table
1
. Then for all
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in\mathcal{M}\times[0,B]

R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\geq\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}\left(\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Define

\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)}}
to be the measure induced by the probability density function

g(\boldsymbol{x},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)=f(\boldsymbol{z})\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)).

Note that

\displaystyle R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z},\ell\sim g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}\left(\log\frac{\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*}))}{\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau))}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z},\ell\sim g(\boldsymbol{z},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}\left(\log\frac{f(\boldsymbol{z})\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*}))}{f(\boldsymbol{z})\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau))}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm D_{KL}}(g(\boldsymbol{x},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|g(\boldsymbol{x},\ell;{\boldsymbol{M}},\tau))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}}-\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)}}\right\|_{\text{TV}}\right)^{2}

where
\|\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}}-\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)}}\|_{\text{TV}}
is the total variation of the signed measure
\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}}-\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)}}
and
the last line follows from Pinsker’s inequality (see
Brillinger 1964
).
So to find a lower bound for
|R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})|
, it suffices to find a lower bound for
\|\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}}-\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)}}\|_{\text{TV}}
.
To this end,

\displaystyle\|\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})}}-\mu_{{}_{({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)}}\|_{\text{TV}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)\right)-\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\right)\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}{\rm d}\ell

(Mean value theorem)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|\Phi_{\rm Noise}^{\prime}(\xi(\boldsymbol{z},\ell))\left[\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)-\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\right]\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}{\rm d}\ell

(
\Phi_{\rm Noise}^{\prime}(\cdot)={\rm Noise}(\cdot)
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int f(\boldsymbol{z}){\rm Noise}(\xi(\boldsymbol{z},\ell))\left|(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)-(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}{\rm d}\ell

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\min_{|\xi|\leq\beta{F}}{\rm Noise}(\xi)\right)\times\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)-(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}{\rm d}\ell

\displaystyle=\omega\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)-(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\omega\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)},

where the first step follows from Scheffé’s Lemma, which relates the total variation distance to the
L_{1}
-norm (see Lemma 2.1 in
Tsybakov 2008
), the second step
is true since Mean Value Theorem implies that there is a

\xi(\boldsymbol{z},\ell)
between
\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)
and
\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})
such that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm Noise}^{\prime}(\xi(\boldsymbol{z},\ell))\Big[\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)-

\displaystyle\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\Big]

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}-\tau)\right)-\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\right),

and the fourth step is true since
|\xi(\boldsymbol{z},\ell)|\leq\beta F
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}^{2}.

∎

The next corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorems
5
and
7
. It indicates that, with high probability,
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
can
approximate
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
with any given precision with respect to the
L_{1}(f)
-metric defined in
11
provided
that
N
is large enough with respect to that precision.

Corollary 8
.

Assume that the noise model
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
is simple.
For
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, if
N>N\left(\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\omega^{2},\delta\right)
, then with probability at least
1-\delta

\|(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}\leq\varepsilon.

The
L_{1}(f)
-metric is dependent on the distribution
f(\boldsymbol{z})
, which is unavoidable. The following lemma bounds this norm under a general condition on
f
, and thus provides a more intuitive result (see Appendix
F
for the proof).

Lemma 9
.

If
f(\boldsymbol{z})\geq c>0
for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in B^{d}(1)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\|z\|_{2}\leq 1\}
, then for all
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in\mathcal{M}\times[0,B]

\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}\geq\frac{c\pi^{d/2}}{20\Gamma(d/2+1)}\left(\frac{1}{18}\right)^{d}{\rm d}(({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})).

In particular, if
f(\boldsymbol{z})
is uniform on the unit ball
B^{d}(1)
, then

\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}\geq\frac{1}{20}\left(\frac{1}{18}\right)^{d}{\rm d}(({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})).

Combining Theorem
7
and Lemma
9
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 10
.

For simplicity of notation, set

C(d)=\frac{c\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}
.
Let
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
be a simple noise model and set
\omega=\min\limits_{|\eta|\leq\beta A}{\rm Noise}(\eta)
.
If
f(\boldsymbol{z})\geq c>0
for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in B^{d}(1)
, then

R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\geq\frac{\omega^{2}C(d)^{2}}{800\times 18^{2d}}{\rm d}^{2}\left(({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\right).

In particular, if
f(\boldsymbol{z})
is uniform on
B^{d}(1)
, then
C(d)=1
and thus

R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\geq\frac{\omega^{2}}{800\times 18^{2d}}{\rm d}^{2}(({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})).

Now, combining Theorems
5
and
10
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 11
.

Let
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
be a simple noise model and set
\omega=\min\limits_{|\eta|\leq\beta A}{\rm Noise}(\eta)
.
Assume
f(\boldsymbol{z})\geq c>0
for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in B^{d}(1)
.
For any given
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, if
N>N(\frac{\omega^{2}\varepsilon^{2}C^{2}(d)}{800\times 18^{2d}},\delta)
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
, any point
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
minimizing
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
satisfies

{\rm d}((\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau}),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*}))<\varepsilon.

We remark that we have not attempted to optimize the constants which appear in the sample complexity bound in Theorem
11
.

4.3
Rank-Deficient Case

No work prior to the present paper has proved that we can recover the matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
when it is not full rank, or bounds the effect of truncating the derived
\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}
to a low-rank
\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}
.
Theorem
11
indicates that for any given
\varepsilon>0
,
\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\|_{2}<\varepsilon
and
|\hat{\tau}-\tau^{*}|<\varepsilon
if
N
is large enough.
This will guarantee that there will be a small eigenvalue of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
for every small eigenvalue of
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}
be two given
n\times d
matrices.
If
\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}\|_{2}<\varepsilon
, then
|\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1})-\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})|<\varepsilon
, where

\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{j})
is the
i
-th singular value of matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{j}
for
j=1,2
.

Proof.

Set
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{r}={\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}
. Because of the definition of the spectral norm,

\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{r}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}}<\varepsilon.

Write
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}={\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{r}
and for each
i\in[d]
, notice

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\min_{\dim(W)=n-i+1}\max_{\underset{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}=1}{x\in W}}\left\|({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{r})\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\dim(W)=n-i+1}\max_{\underset{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}=1}{x\in W}}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}+\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{r}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\dim(W)=n-i+1}\max_{\underset{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}=1}{x\in W}}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}+\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon+\min_{\dim(W)=n-i+1}\max_{\underset{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}=1}{x\in W}}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1})+\varepsilon.

With a similar approach, we can prove that

\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1})\leq\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})+\varepsilon
,
which implies that

|\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1})-\sigma_{i}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})|<\varepsilon\quad\quad\quad\forall i\in[d],

which completes the proof.
∎

Using this lemma, we obtain the following lemma

Lemma 13
.

Let
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}>0
be given and assume the ground truth
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
has
k
eigenvalues greater than or equal to
\alpha_{1}

and
k^{\prime}
eigenvalues less than or equal to
\alpha_{2}-3\varepsilon
with
k+k^{\prime}\leq d
.
If the noise model
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
is simple and
N>N(\frac{\omega^{2}\varepsilon^{2}C^{2}(d)}{800\times 18^{2d}},\delta)
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\|_{2}<\varepsilon
,
the number of eigenvalues of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
which are greater than
\alpha_{1}-\varepsilon
is at least
k
, and
the number of eigenvalues of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
which are less than
\alpha_{2}-2\varepsilon
is at least
k^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume that
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
minimizes
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
.
By Theorem
11
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\|_{2}<\varepsilon
.
Now, Lemma
12
implies the result.
∎

The next theorem gives us a better understanding of the eigenvalue perturbation of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
when
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
is not full rank.

Theorem 14
.

Assume that the noise model
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
is simple and
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
has rank
0<r<d
.
For a given
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, if
0<3\varepsilon<\sigma_{r}({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*})

and
N>N(\frac{\omega^{2}\varepsilon^{2}C^{2}(d)}{800\times 18^{2d}},\delta)
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
has exactly
d-r
eigenvalues less than
\varepsilon
and the rest
r
eigenvalues are at least
\frac{2}{3}\sigma_{r}({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*})
. So if we truncate the eigenvalues of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
which are less than
\varepsilon
to zero, we obtain
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k}
of rank
r
for which
\|{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{r}\|_{2}<2\varepsilon
.

Proof.

As Lemma
13
holds with
\alpha_{1}=\sigma_{r}({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}),\alpha_{2}=3\varepsilon,k=r,k^{\prime}=n-r
, we have

\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\|_{2}<\varepsilon
,
the number of eigenvalues of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
which are greater than
\alpha_{1}-\varepsilon>\frac{2}{3}\sigma_{r}({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*})
is
r
, and the number of eigenvalues of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
which are less than
\alpha_{2}-2\varepsilon=\varepsilon
is
n-r
. Now,

\|{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{r}\|_{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}\|_{2}+\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{r}\|_{2}\leq 2\varepsilon

completes the proof.
∎

As we are not given
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
, in practice we are unaware of its rank or spectral properties.
Since we only have access to
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
,
the next theorem establishes that the loss function still converges under eigenvalue truncation when the corresponding eigenvalues of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
are small.

Theorem 15
.

Assume that the noise model
{\rm Noise}(\eta)
is simple.
For a given
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, assume that
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
minimizes
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
for
N>N\left(\varepsilon/2,\delta\right)
.
Define threshold
\gamma
such that
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
has
d-k
eigenvalues which are less than
\gamma
. Let
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k}
be the rank
k
matrix obtained from
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
by setting these
d-k
eigenvalues to zero. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

0<R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})<\gamma+\varepsilon.

Proof.

As
\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k}\|_{2}<\gamma
, using the proof of the first inequality in Lemma
3
, we obtain

|R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})-R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k},\hat{\tau})|<\zeta F\gamma=\gamma,

since we have assumed
\zeta F=1
. On the other hand, by Theorem
5
, we have

0<R(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})<\varepsilon.

Combining these two inequalities implies the desired inequality.
∎

Combining Theorems
10
and
15
, we also have

{\rm d}\left((\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k},\hat{\tau}),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\right)<\frac{20\sqrt{2}\times 18^{d}}{\omega C(d)}(\gamma+\varepsilon).

Thus, if
\gamma\leq\varepsilon\frac{\omega C(d)}{20\sqrt{2}\times 18^{d}}
, then

{\rm d}\left((\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k},\hat{\tau}),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\right)<2\varepsilon,
implying
\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}\|_{2}<2\varepsilon
. Thus spectral truncation of the empirical
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
yields a good approximation of
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
.

4.4
Invariance to Changes in Unit of Input

Clearly, the learned
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
and
\hat{\tau}
are dependent on the units of feature space.
So, as an interesting question, we can study the behavior of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
and
\hat{\tau}
if we change the units in the original feature space.
Mainly, we want to prove that if we change the units in feature space, we do not need to solve a
new optimization problem to learn a new
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
and
\hat{\tau}
. Instead, we can recover these parameters from the already solved optimization problem.
Assume that we have a non-singular matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{d\times d}
which changes the units and rotates the space of features, and
let
\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{i}={\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
and
\ell_{i}^{\prime}=\ell_{i}
. We
want to solve the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq 0,\tau\geq 0}R^{\prime}_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\,,

(12)

where

\displaystyle R^{\prime}_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sigma\left(\ell^{\prime}_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sigma\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{M}}{\boldsymbol{U}}}-\tau)\right)

Since
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is non-singular, Optimization Problem
5
is minimized at
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau}
if and only if Optimization Problem
12

is minimized at
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{\prime}={{\boldsymbol{U}}^{-1}}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{-1},\hat{\tau}^{\prime}=\hat{\tau}
.
Hence, the solution is invariant to the choice of units, given knowledge of the conversion.

5
Experimental Results

In Section
3
, we described the optimal loss functions for four different noise distributions (Logistic, Normal, Laplace, and Hyperbolic Secant). As the Normal noise model ends up with a probit in the loss function, and the probit function has no closed-form formula, we will not use this model in the experiments. Also, since in practice we generally do not know the noise distribution, we evaluate the performance of each model under a variety of possible noise distributions, thus testing the robustness of each model to misspecification of the noise.
We investigate how the resulting accuracy depends on the sample complexity, amount of noise, and noise misspecification.

We start with synthetic data, described in Section
5.1
, so we can run precisely controlled experiments which are reported in Sections
5.2
and
5.3
.
In Section
5.4
, we compare our model performance with DML-eig (
Ying and Li 2012
).
Then in Section
5.5
we apply our methods to some real data experiments well suited to our proposed algorithm.
All experimental results are reproducible; see the GitHub repository by
Alishahi et al. 2023
containing data and source codes.

5.1
Data Generation

We start with
d
random positive real values
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
and then we randomly generate a
d\times d
covariance matrix
\Sigma
whose eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
.
To this end, we first randomly and uniformly generate an orthonormal matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{d\times d}
and then set
\Sigma={\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{D}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}
, where
{\boldsymbol{D}}
is the
d\times d
diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
.
We then independently sample
2N
points
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{y}_{N}
from
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\Sigma)
to generate
N
pairs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
for
i=1\ldots,N
.
Next we select
d
nonnegative random real values
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{d}\geq 0
as the eigenvalues of the ground truth
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
,
and randomly generate
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
to be a random positive semi-definite matrix with eigenvalues

\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d}
, as we did for
\Sigma
.
We have

\mathbb{E}\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{M^{*}}^{2}\right)=2{\rm tr}\left(\Sigma M^{*}\right)

provided that
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\sim h(\boldsymbol{x})
,
where
h(\boldsymbol{x})
is a pdf for which
{\rm Cov}_{X\sim h}(X)=\Sigma
.
We now choose
\tau^{*}>0
not far from
\mathbb{E}\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{M^{*}}^{2}\right)
so that we obtain a sufficient number of pairs
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
labeled as both Close and Far. More specifically, in Sections
5.2
-
5.4
, we consider the following setting.

•

We assume that the rank of
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}_{10\times 10}
is
5
and randomly and uniformly generate
5
nonzero eigenvalues from
[0,1]
.
With two-digit precision, we obtain
0.32,0.89,0.59,0.13,0.14
as the
5
nonzero eigenvalues of
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
.

•

We randomly and uniformly select
10
nonzero numbers from
(0,1]
as the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
. With two digit precision, we obtain

0.73,\ 0.7,\ 0.68,\ 0.59,\ 0.47,\ 0.45,\ 0.21,\ 0.19,\ 0.11,\ 0.04

as the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
.

•

As we are dealing with fixed random seeds, we obtain
\mathbb{E}\left(\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{M^{*}}^{2}\right)\approx 1.7
.

•

To obtain roughly balanced data, we set
\tau^{*}=1.3
and generate
20000
data points. We split the data into
15000
training and
5000
test points.

We now describe the label generation. Note that in the theoretical formulation of the problem, we assume that the noisy labeling process depends on
\|x-y\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}
.
However one could also assume that the noise changes labels directly, independently of
\|x-y\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}
. We thus study both of the following settings empirically.

•

Noise affects the labeling through
\|x-y\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}
(
Label Assumption
).

We consider a noise distribution
{\rm Noise}(0,s)
with zero mean and scale parameter
s
from the Logistic, Gaussian, Laplace, and Hyperbolic Secant distributions.
We then generate

\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N}\sim{\rm Noise}(0,s)
.
For each pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
, we set
\ell_{i}=1
(“Far”) if
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}+\eta_{i}\geq\tau^{*}
, and we set
\ell_{i}=-1
(“Close”) if

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}+\eta_{i}<\tau^{*}
.
We save these labels as
D_{\text{noisy}}
.
We also save the non-noisy labels to check the model’s robustness against noise.
However, we do not use these labels during training.
Indeed, for each pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
, we set
\ell^{*}_{i}=1
if
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}\geq\tau^{*}
and we set
\ell^{*}_{i}=-1
if

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}^{2}<\tau^{*}
.
We save these labels as
D^{*}
.

•

Noise directly affects the labeling (Noisy Labeling).

Here we assume that the noise affects the labels directly by randomly flipping them.
We first generate
D^{*}
as described in the previous paragraph.
Then for each
i=1,\ldots,N
, we flip a coin whose head chance is
p
. If the coin is tails, we set
\ell_{i}=\ell_{i}^{*}
; otherwise, we set

\ell_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
randomly with the same chance. We save these labels as
D_{\text{noisy}}
.
In expectation,
p/2
fraction of the labels are mislabeled in
D_{\text{noisy}}
. Although the amount of noise is the same as in the previous setting, i.e. the same number of mistakes are made, this regime is more challenging because in the first case the majority of mistakes occur close to the boundary, while the noisy labeling case results in “big” mistakes. We thus
expect performance to be worse.

As a default, in both settings we set the noise parameter so that
10\%
of the points are mislabeled.

5.4
Comparing to DML-eig

Inspired by a work of
Xing et al. 2002
,

developed an eigenvalue optimization framework (called DML-eig) for learning a Mahalanobis metric. They define an acceptable optimization problem and elegantly reduce it to minimizing the maximal eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix problem
(

;

)
. In their formulation, given pairs of similar data points and pairs of dissimilar data points, the goal is to learn a Mahalanobis metric which preserves similarity and dissimilarity.
More specific, they look for a p.s.d. matrix

to maximize the minimal squared distances between dissimilar pairs while the sum of squared distances between similar pairs is at most
1
. This setting is comparable to ours since we also look for a matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
(and also a threshold
\tau^{*}
) to distinguish labeled far pairs of points from labeled close pairs of points. Their work did not study how noise can affect their model, nor if it could potentially recover a “ground truth” model that generates the dissimilar and similar labels.
However, we can compare our model to theirs empirically by passing our similarities and dissimilarities to their model and checking whether their model can handle noise or recover ground truth parameters.
We can also use the
\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}_{\text{eig}}
learned by their model with the best
\hat{\tau}
to see how well they can predict the labels.

In this section we use our methods to solve some real data challenges which demand or benefit from a learned Mahalanobis distance. The first one is from a physical simulation where we aim to find a reduced order model that needs to pass through a linear projection. Thus the learned scaling must be linear, and is desired to be low rank.
The second one is a consumer satisfaction story, and instead of using pairs of data points

\boldsymbol{z}

Normalizing data.

The method does not converge when we input the raw data as provided to our solver; this is due to the different scaling of coordinates.
The magnitude of some coordinates of the data are huge (temperature) and some are very small (CO2 percentage).
So in the gradient descent, the learning rate is the same for all variables; the algorithm does not show convergence on the variable with very small values, even after a large number of steps. We can solve this problem by doing coordinate-wise normalization of the data, thus scaling all coordinates similarly, and this process is labeled
Normalized data
in Table
4
. However, even in this setting, we do not have a good parameter recovery; see the last two rows of the second column of Table
4
with about

\hat{\boldsymbol{M}}/\hat{\tau}

To analyze this situation, we compute the estimated covariance matrix from the data, we observe that the variance of the data in some directions is almost zero. Note that these directions are not along coordinates, they are a linear combination of the coordinates, so coordinate-wise normalization does not correct for it.
Note that in the theoretical results, we have the parameter recovery only if the support of data distribution has a nonzero Lebesgue measure which is effectively not the case here.

To address the remaining issue, note that if we change the behavior of
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k}
only for those directions that the data variance is zero, then the distance in the projected space remains almost always the same and thus the labeling remains the same as well.
This implies that there is no unique
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
to recover.
However, if we rescale the data and
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
by
\sqrt{C}
and the data by

Indeed, if we set

{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}_{new}=\sqrt{C}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})^{\top}\sqrt{C}
has a negligible effect in the directions where

has a small variance. As the gradient descent initiates

with very small entries and it will receive no substantial update in those directions, finally we recover

There is also a threshold such that if the satisfaction value is greater, then the passenger is happy with the service. We here assumed that such a metric and threshold can be modeled via our linear distance metric learning approach. More-so, that threshold can vary among different persons, resulting in a noise labeling process which our formulation accounts for. Thus, the linear distance metric learning approach provides not just a classifier, but also an interpretable generative model that adds extra transparency to the task solution.

6
Related Work on Linear DML

A considerable amount of works have been devoted to distance metric learning (
Bar-Hillel et al. 2005
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Sugiyama 2007
;
Torresani and Lee 2006
;
Wang and Zhang 2007
;
Xiang et al. 2008
). Although recent work has focused primarily on nonlinear distance metric learning, the works most relevant to this article are more classic linear approaches. The method we developed can be categorized as fully supervised linear metric learning in which the scalability is in terms of both number of examples and dimension. Ours has bounded sample complexity is
O(\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{d}{\varepsilon})
in the dimension
d
for
\varepsilon
error in our loss function, and in practice we run gradient descent, where each iteration is linear in the data size
N
and has quadratic dependence for dimension
d
. Related works do not provide sample complexity bounds.
Note that our method learns a Mahalanobis distance (a positive semi-definite matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
) and a threshold
\tau
.
We next compare with the most similar prior work.

6.1
Constrained Optimization Approaches

Xing et al. 2002
provide the first method to learn a Mahalanobis distance
by maximizing the sum of distances between points in the dissimilarity set (
\mathcal{D}
) under the constraint that the sum of squared distance between points in the similarity set (
\mathcal{S}
) is upper-bounded:

\displaystyle\max\limits_{{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq 0}

\displaystyle\sum_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\in\mathcal{D}}d_{\boldsymbol{M}}(x_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\in\mathcal{S}}d^{2}_{\boldsymbol{M}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\leq 1.

It can be shown that this is a convex optimization which was solved by a proximal gradient ascent which, in each step, takes a gradient ascent step of the objective function, then projects back to the set of constraints, the cone of p.s.d. matrices.
The projection to the p.s.d. cone uses full eigen-decomposition with
O(d^{3})
time complexity. So, as the dimension gets large it quickly gets intractable, while in our method we only deal with computing the
Mahalanobis distance which takes
O(d^{2})
time complexity.

Note that the model proposed by
Xing et al. 2002
takes into account all the information of similar and dissimilar pairs by aggregating all similarity constraints together as well as all dissimilarity constraints. In contrast, the DML-eig method proposed by
Ying and Li 2012
maximizes the minimum distance between dissimilar pairs instead of maximizing the sum of their distances. They develop a subgradient ascent procedure to optimize their formulation which does not require a projection, but still uses an
O(d^{3})
eigendecomposition step.
Intuitively, this model prioritizes separating dissimilar pairs over keeping similar pairs close. Experimentally, they show that their method outperforms
Xing et al. 2002
. In Subsection
5.4
we experimentally compare our model with DML-eig showing that our approach works better in terms of performance, accuracy, and dealing with noise.

6.2
Unconstrained Optimization over
A

Taking into account the fact that any p.s.d matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
can be decomposed into
{\boldsymbol{M}}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}
,
Goldberger et al. 2004
define the expected leave-one-out error of a stochastic nearest neighbor classifier in the projection space induced by
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. They defined the probability that
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is similar (close) to
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
as

p_{ij}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\frac{\exp\left({-\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}}\right)}{\sum_{k\neq i}\exp\left({-\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}}\right)},\quad p_{ii}=0

and the probability that
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is correctly classified as

p_{i}=\sum_{\{j:(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\in\mathcal{S}\}}p_{ij}.

To learn
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, they solve
\arg\max\limits_{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\sum_{i}p_{i}
.
This is not a convex optimization and thus leads to a local maximum rather than a global one.

Using an MLE approach,
Guillaumin et al. 2009
define a Logistic loss function which matches a special case of our loss function when the noise comes from a Logistic distribution. They start with the assumption that the similarity is determined via a Bernoulli distribution whose success probability (being similar) is
\sigma(\tau-d_{\boldsymbol{M}}(x_{i},x_{j}))
. Maximizing the likelihood under this probabilistic model then yields an approximation of
{\boldsymbol{M}}
and
\tau
. This is exactly the approach we take in Subsection
3.1
. However, instead of directly assuming such a model, we derive our model from certain noise assumptions and thus obtain the best theoretical model possible under these assumptions. We also prove that alternate (nonLogistic) formulations of the loss function are the MLE solution under different noise assumptions. Moreover, we conclude that each of these models is capable of parameter recovery with a sufficiently large sample size.

In each of the above settings, we can rewrite the optimization function in terms of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{M}}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}
. This makes the problem unconstrained which is a good advantage since it eliminates expensive sub-gradient projection and moreover, we can restrict
{\boldsymbol{A}}
to be rectangular inducing a low-rank
{\boldsymbol{M}}
. The main limitation of this formulation (even when
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a square matrix) is that it is non-convex and thus subject to local maxima. However, in our formulation, a result by
Journée et al. 2010
resolves this issue.

6.3
Empirical Loss Minimization Framework

There are some other related works proposing an empirical loss minimization framework; for a thorough review see Chapter 8 of the book by
Bellet et al. 2015
. The prediction performance of the learned metric has been studied in some works such as
Balcan et al. 2008
;
Jin et al. 2009
;
Bellet et al. 2011
;
Bellet et al. 2012a
;
Bellet et al. 2012b
;
Guo and Ying 2014
;
Cao et al. 2016
.
Broadly speaking, for an unknown data probability distribution, they considered different meaningful constrained cost functions as the true risk and then they studied the convergence of the empirical risk to the true risk.
As their setting is a bit different from ours, we recall their data assumptions here.
Given labeled examples

\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\colon i=1,\ldots,N\}
where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|\leq F
and
y_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, they define a similar (close) pair set
\mathcal{S}
and a dissimilar (far) pair set
\mathcal{D}
as follows:

\mathcal{S}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\colon y_{i}=y_{j}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\colon y_{i}\neq y_{j}\}.

Note that the pairs here are not i.i.d. as in our formulation.
From
O(n)
given data points, we can feed our cost function with only
n
i.i.d. pairs while they can do it with
O(n^{2})
pairs. It might give the impression that these methods should allow for much stronger convergence results, but it is not the case.

In the following we briefly review some of their results and then compare them to ours. The primary outcome of these findings is to demonstrate the overall reliability of a metric learning approach, rather than offering precise estimations of the generalization loss.

Following the idea of maximum margin classifiers,
Jin et al. 2009
adapted the uniform stability framework (
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
and McDiarmid’s inequality) to metric learning to obtain a generalization bound. They considered

C({\boldsymbol{M}})=\frac{2c}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i<j}L\left(y_{ij}(1-\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|^{2}_{F}

(13)

as the regularized empirical cost function where
y_{ij}=1
if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}
and
y_{ij}=-1
if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{D}
and
L(z)
is a standard loss function which is
\zeta
-Lipschitz.
As a main result, they proved that the empirical loss
C({\boldsymbol{M}})
converges in probability measure to the true cost
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},y}L\left(y(1-\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|^{2}_{F}
with the sample complexity
O\left({s(d)^{2}\ln 1/\delta\over\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

where
s(d)
comes from a constraint
{\rm trace}({\boldsymbol{M}})\leq s(d)
, where the hidden constant depends on
\zeta,F
.
Note that if
s(d)
is considered a constant, this provides a sample complexity independent of dimension; but it may be that the best
{\boldsymbol{M}}
minimizing the cost function does not follow this constraint. Comparing to our sample complexity (Theorem
4
),
both bounds share almost the same dominant part (assuming
d
is fixed).
However, they use
O(n^{2})
pairs in their optimization while we only use
n
; thus our optimization framework is more scalable in
n
.
In our setting, we can sample
O(n)
disjoint pairs from the
O(n^{2})
pairs to simulate our required i.i.d. samples.

Bian and Tao 2011
considered a similar loss (without the regularizer term).
They theoretically and empirically studied the convergence of the empirical risk in this setting for some appropriate choice of
L
, focusing on log loss and hinge loss. They proved that the empirical loss converges (in probability measure) to the corresponding true loss with the rate
O(1/\sqrt{n})
. This bound does not resolve the sample complexity since it is presented as a function of
n
without working out the dependencies to the other parameters. They also concluded that the minimizer of the empirical loss
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\hat{\tau})
converges in measure to the minimizer of the true loss
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
as
n
goes to infinity, but does not identify specific conditions that must hold for this to be true, or finite sample bounds, as our work does.

In a sequel,
Bian and Tao 2012
examined a data assumption that closely resembled ours, along with empirical and accurate losses like our own.
They demonstrated that the empirical loss converges to the true loss on the optimal model, with a sample complexity comparable to ours in terms of
\varepsilon,\delta
, and
d
.
However, it is worth noting that their study did not include noise in their setting, it was not proven that their optimization model is theoretically optimal under some generating model parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
as is done in this article,
and they did not investigate the recovery of ground truth parameters.

Extending the robustness framework (
Xu and Mannor 2012
) to metric learning,
Bellet and Habrard 2015
studied the deviation between the true and empirical loss.
The cost function they worked with is again similar to the one in Equation
13
. They proved that the empirical loss converges in probability measure to the corresponding true loss.
We can simplify their result to the sample complexity bound
O({s(d)+\ln(1/\delta)\over\varepsilon^{2}})
where
s(d)
can be exponentially large in terms of
d
.
It should be noted that the constant appearing in their data assumption impacts the constant in this sample complexity bound.
For a fixed
d
, comparing our complexity bound with theirs,
we again can see that the dominant parts are almost the same while their algorithm operates on
n^{2}
distances for a set of
n
points. Afterwards
Guo and Ying 2014
;
Cao et al. 2016
, employing a different similarity learning optimization problem,
established a comparable error bound in terms of Rademacher average which is upper bounded by a function of data bound
F
.
More precisely, under our data assumption, we can translate their error bound to a sample complexity bound which depends linearly on

d
and has the dominant term
O({\ln(1/\delta)\over\varepsilon^{2}})
. It is similar to the other above-mentioned bounds.

In the following we briefly recall some advantages of our model compared to the above mentioned methods.

•

All methods discussed above model metric learning as an optimization problem which penalizes mismatches, including using constrained optimization on
{\boldsymbol{M}}
. However, they do not prove that these optimization problems are theoretically optimal under some generating model parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
as is done in this article.

•

These algorithms deal with a more expensive optimization problem which uses
O(n^{2})
pairs for
n
points while our method uses only
O(n)
pairs. Despite the extra information, these algorithms do not lead to a more favorable scaling between the sample size
n
and the error
\varepsilon
compared to our method.

•

These other methods do not provide recovery guarantees on generating parameters as we do. This in turn allows us to provide low-rank approximation and dimensionality reduction results, since we can bound the effects of truncating small model parameters.

•

Furthermore, since we derive the loss functions from various noise models, we can recover these model parameters even in the presence of (correctly modeled) noise.

6.4
Information Theoretic Modeling

Nguyen et al. 2017
assume that

\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}})
and
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}})
.
For any linear transformation
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
, this can be written

\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{A}})=f_{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})

and

\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}}{\boldsymbol{A}})=g_{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}).

Their goal is to find
{\boldsymbol{A}}
maximizing Jeffrey divergence, i.e., to solve the following optimization problem;

\max\limits_{{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}{\rm KL}(f_{\boldsymbol{A}},g_{\boldsymbol{A}})+{\rm KL}(g_{\boldsymbol{A}},f_{\boldsymbol{A}}),

where
{\rm KL}
stands here for Kullback-Leibler divergence. As both distributions
g_{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
f_{\boldsymbol{A}}
are multivariate Gaussian, one can compute
{\rm KL}(f_{A},g_{A})+{\rm KL}(g_{A},f_{A})
as a function of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and reduce the optimization problem to

\max\limits_{{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}{\rm tr}\left(({\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{A}})^{-1}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}}{\boldsymbol{A}})+({\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}}{\boldsymbol{A}})^{-1}({\boldsymbol{A}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{A}})\right)\,.

Setting the derivative of this objective function to zero, they present a solution to this non-convex optimization problem. In practice, they use MLE to replace
\Sigma_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}}
by their sample estimations, and since the formulation is not convex, the identified answer may be a local optimum.

6.5
Low-Rank Metric Learning

As explained in Section
1
, linear distance metric learning approaches can be used for linear dimensionality reduction (see
Wang and Sun 2015
). When we are dealing with high dimensional space or a huge number of data points, solving
Optimizations
5
(or Optimizations
10
for
k=\Theta(d)
) will be costly. As reducing the matrix size in these optimizations reduces the complexity of search spaces, to resolve this issue, we can think of adding some low-rank constraint to these optimizations, e.g.,
{\rm rank}({\boldsymbol{M}})\ll d
in Optimizations
5
or
k\ll d
in Optimizations
10
. As a downside, it turns these optimization problems non-convex and thus the regular approaches such as gradient decent tend to fail easily (see
Mu 2016
;
Wen and Yin 2013
).

Liu et al. 2019
, dealing with this challenge, introduced a fast low-rank metric learning method that worked well for several data points as benchmarks. Although they still have to face a non-convex optimization, as a standard way, they employed manifold optimization methods
(
Shukla and Anand 2015
;
Balzano et al. 2010
;
Vandereycken 2013
)
to overcome the issue. As they worked with a different input setting as in linear DML, we are not able to present a direct comparison between their approach and ours.

7
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper we provide and analyze a simple and elegant method to solve the linear distance metric learning problem. That is, given a set of iid pairs of points labeled close or far, our method learns a Mahalanobis distance that maintains these labels for some threshold. This arises when in data analysis one needs to learn how to compare various coordinates, which may be in different units, but not introduce non-linearity for reasons of interpretability, equation preservation, or maintaining linear structure.
Our method reduces to unconstrained gradient descent, has a simple sample complexity bound, and shows convergence in a loss function and in parameter space. In fact, this convergence holds even under noisy observations.

Moreover, our method is the first approach to show that the learned Mahalanobis distance can be truncated to a low-rank model that can provably maintain the accuracy in the loss function and in parameters.

Finally, we demonstrate that this method works empirically as well as the theory predicts. We can obtain high accuracy (over
99\%
) and parameter recovery (less than
1.01
multiplicative error) on noiseless and noisy data, and on synthetic and real data. For instance, even if
45\%
of the data is mislabeled we can with very high accuracy recover the true model parameters.
Additionally we show this simple solution nearly matches the best engineered solutions on two real world data challenges.

7.1
Limitations

In our formulation of linear DML, we assume that we are given
N
i.i.d. observations of pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and each pair is given a label
\ell_{i}\in\{{\rm Far,Close}\}
. We discuss the Airline Passenger Satisfaction modeling problem in Section
5.5.2
where by always setting
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
to the origin, this is a direct and natural modeling: each observation generates one pair.
However, in other real-world settings, we are only provided with
n
observations with the ability to query if any pair has label
{\rm Far}
or
{\rm Close}
. This can induce
\Theta(n^{2})
pairs, but they are not i.i.d.
In practice, one would like to use all of these pairs, or perhaps restricted to some local constraints in a neighborhood. But since these are not i.i.d., our analysis does not apply.
What we can do is randomly partition the data into
n/2
pairs; now if the original
n
observations are i.i.d., then these pairs are also i.i.d. from some distribution, and our analysis holds. While this only generates
N=O(n)
pairs, not
O(n^{2})
, our analysis shows error converges at a rate of roughly
1/\sqrt{N}
. This basically matches the convergence rate for known methods which use all
\Theta(n^{2})
pairs, and converge at a rate
1/\sqrt{n}
. Managing such dependency, and potentially improving to a
1/n
convergence rate, is a challenging direction to consider for future work.

Another limitation in our modeling in the noisy setting, is that we prove strong convergence and parameter recovery, only when the loss function corresponds with the noise model generating the data. In practice one does not always know the noise model, and in fact there may not be one well-defined noise model. As a result, a user must chose a loss function.
In this case, we generically recommend the Logistic loss. It is widely used, has a closed form, the cdf is
1
-log-Lipschitz, and as discussed, it is fairly similar to other common noise models. Moreover, we show empirically that it performs nearly as well under other noise types we considered as it does under Logistic noise.

Finally, the analysis requires several clearly stated assumptions on the model
Model Assumption
(that the parameters
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}
and
\tau^{*}
are bounded) and the data
Data Assumption
(that the
2
-norm of the data is bounded). If we do not have such bounds, then our analysis does not have a guarantee. In fact, we observe in the experiment on Equations of State for Combustion Simulation in Section
5.5.1
that without properly normalizing, the algorithm performs poorly. This normalization has the effect of properly shaping the data, and as a result the optimal model, so that it satisfies these assumptions. In this case our algorithm converges quickly to small loss and recovers the near-optimal parameters.

\mathcal{X}

\varepsilon

Lemma 17

There exists an
\varepsilon
-cover

Appendix D
Uniform Convergence of
R_{N}
to
R

Although we have proved in Theorem
2
that the true loss
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is uniquely minimized at
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
, in reality, we do not have the true loss.
Indeed, we only have access to the empirical loss
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
.
In this part, broadly speaking, we will show that
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
is uniformly close to
R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
as
N
gets large, and then, we conclude, instead of minimizing
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
, we can minimize
R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
to approximately find
({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})
.

In the next lemma, we will see that if the two p.s.d. matrices are close via spectrum norm, then the Mahalanobis norm defined based on these two matrices are also close.

Observation 3

(Equation
7
)
.

For given two p.s.d.
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}
,

|\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}|\leq\|{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}.

Proof.

Using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the definition of the spectral norm, we obtain

\displaystyle|\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}}-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2}}|

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})\boldsymbol{x}|

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\|({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})\boldsymbol{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|({\boldsymbol{M}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{2})\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2},

concluding the inequality.
∎

As
-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}(\cdot)
is a decreasing function, using Equation
2
, we have

0\leq-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)\leq-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}(-\beta A)=T,

which indicates that the random variables
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
’s are bounded.
Whenever we are dealing with a summation of bounded i.i.d. random variables,
one strong concentration inequality to use is Chernoff-Hoeffding bound.
This inequality states if
X_{1},...,X_{N}
are
N
independent random variables such that

{\displaystyle X_{i}\in[a_{i},b_{i}]}
almost surely for all
i
, and

\displaystyle S_{N}=\frac{X_{1}+\cdots+X_{N}}{N}
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}\left(\left|S_{n}-\mathrm{E}\left[S_{n}\right]\right|\geq\alpha\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-{\frac{2N^{2}\alpha^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}}\right).

Since,

\displaystyle R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}-\log\Phi_{\rm Noise}\left(\ell_{i}(\|\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)\right)

and
\mathbb{E}(R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau))=R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)
,
we can use Chernoff-Hoeffding bound to control the probability that

|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)|
is large.

Lemma 18
.

If
E=\{({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})\colon i=1,\ldots,m=m(\alpha)\}
is an
\alpha
-cover for
\mathcal{M}\times[0,B]
, then

\displaystyle P\big(|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})|\geq\alpha\text{ for some $i\in[m]$}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2me^{-\frac{2N\alpha^{2}}{T^{2}}}.

Proof.

Consider a fixed
i\in[m]
.
For simplicity of notation, set
Z=-\log\sigma\left(\ell(\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{i}}-\tau_{i})\right)
and
Z_{j}=-\log\sigma\left(\ell_{j}(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|_{{\boldsymbol{M}}_{i}}-\tau_{i})\right)
.
As we explained above,

Z\in[0,T]
, see Table
1
, and, using Chernoff-Hoeffding Inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle P\big(|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})-R({\boldsymbol{M}}_{i},\tau_{i})|\geq\alpha\big)

\displaystyle=P\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{j}-E(Z)\Big|\geq\alpha\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\frac{2N\alpha^{2}}{T^{2}}}.

Now, using union bound, we have the desired inequality.
∎

In the next theorem, we prove that, with high probability, the empirical loss
R_{N}
is everywhere close to the true loss

[ Theorem

, Restated]

To prove the assertion, we can equivalently prove

Set

So far, we have proved that

Using triangle inequality, it concludes

\displaystyle\left|R_{N}({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-R({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\right|\leq

Via Lemma

\alpha

for

On the other hand,

In Theorem
7
and Corollary
8
, we
study the connection between
L_{f}(f)
norm and the sample complexity of our problem. However, as the

-metric is dependent on the distribution

, which is unavoidable, it is not very intuitive. We indeed prefer some more informative norms such as spectral norm. However, to this end, we must restrict the distribution

.

•

We here assume that there is a constant
c>0
, such that
f(\boldsymbol{z})\geq c
for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in B^{d}(1);
recall we assume almost surely
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}\leq F=1
in this section, and
B^{d}(1)=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|\boldsymbol{z}\|\leq 1\}
.

We next prove a statement similar to Corollary
8
but in terms of the
{\rm d}
-metric instead of the
L_{1}(f)
-metric. The following definition is also needed for the following two results.
For
0\leq a<b\leq 1
, where
z_{1}
is the first coordinate of
\boldsymbol{z}
, define

{\rm Cone}(a,b)=\left\{\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\mid 3z_{1}^{2}\geq 2\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}\ \text{ and }a\leq z_{1}\leq b\right\}.

(15)

Lemma 20
.

If
f(\boldsymbol{z})\geq c>0
for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in B^{d}(1)
, then for all
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in\mathcal{M}\times[0,B]

\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}\geq\frac{c\pi^{d/2}}{20\Gamma(d/2+1)}(\frac{1}{18})^{d}{\rm d}\left(({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\right).

In particular, if
f(\boldsymbol{z})
is uniform on unit disk, then for all
({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)\in\mathcal{M}\times[0,B]

\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}\geq\frac{1}{20}(\frac{1}{18})^{d}{\rm d}\left(({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\right).

Proof.

We remind that

\displaystyle\|({\boldsymbol{M}},\tau)-({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\|_{L_{1}(f)}

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-\tau)-(\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}-\tau^{*})\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\Big|\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}(\underbrace{\hat{{\boldsymbol{M}}}-{\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}}_{=\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}})\boldsymbol{z}-(\underbrace{\tau-\tau^{*}}_{=\bar{\tau}})\Big|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\tau}\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}.

Note that
\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}
is a symmetric matrix. So, there are a real value orthonormal matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and a real value diagonal matrix
{\boldsymbol{D}}
such that

\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}={\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{D}}{\boldsymbol{U}}
. Let the vector
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
denote the diagonal of
{\boldsymbol{D}}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\lambda_{1}=\max\{|\lambda_{1}|,\ldots,|\lambda_{d}|\}.

One can verify that
\lambda_{1}=\|\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}\|_{2}.

As
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is orthonormal, we have

\displaystyle\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}}\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\tau}\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{z})\left|({\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{z})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{D}}({\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{z})-\bar{\tau}\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\int f({\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z})\left|\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{D}}\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\tau}\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\int f({\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z})\left|\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}-\bar{\tau}\right|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

Note that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda_{1}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{2}
and, for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in{\rm Cone}(0,1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}z_{1}^{2}-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\lambda_{1}z_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\left(2z_{1}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(2z_{1}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2})\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}z_{1}^{2}

Set
q=\lambda_{1}+|\bar{\tau}|={\rm d}\left((\bar{{\boldsymbol{M}}},\bar{\tau}),({\boldsymbol{M}}^{*},\tau^{*})\right)
.
We next consider two cases based on

and

.

•

|\bar{\tau}|\leq 0.1q

and thus, if

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}z_{1}^{2}-\bar{\tau}

\displaystyle\geq q\left(\frac{9}{20}z_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{10}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{20}\mu_{f}({\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}{\rm Cone}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},1))

(16)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{cq}{20}\int_{\boldsymbol{z}\in{\rm Cone}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},1)\cap B^{d}(1)}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{cq}{20}\times{\rm Volume}\left({\rm Cone}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},1)\cap B^{d}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{cq}{20}\times{\rm Volume}\left({\rm Cone}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{cq}{20d}\left[\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}V^{d-1}(\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}))-\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}V^{d-1}(\sqrt{\frac{1}{6}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{cq}{20}\frac{\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{d3^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}\left[\sqrt{2}-\frac{1}{2^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\right].

(17)

•

|\bar{\tau}|>0.1q
. It implies

and thus

\displaystyle\Big|\bar{\tau}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}\Big|

\displaystyle\geq|\bar{\tau}|-\lambda_{1}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q}{20}\quad\text{ if $\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{18}$}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int f({\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z})\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}-\bar{\tau}\Big|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q}{20}\int_{\boldsymbol{z}\in B^{q}(\frac{1}{18})}f({\boldsymbol{U}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}){\rm d}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{20}\mu_{f}(B^{q}(\frac{1}{18}))

(18)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{cq}{20}\times{\rm Volume}\left(B^{q}(\frac{1}{18})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{cq}{20}\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}(\frac{1}{18})^{d}

(19)

Now Lower bounds (

) and (

5.1

30

At the starting state with
