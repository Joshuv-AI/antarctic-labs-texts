Longwave nonlinear theory for chemically active droplet division instability Preprint: APS/123-QED
Mohammad Abu Hamed, Alexander A. Nepomnyashchy

Abstract
It has been suggested recently that growth and division of a protocell could be modeled by a chemically active droplet with simple chemical reactions driven by an external fuel supply. This model is called the continuum model. Indeed it’s numerical simulation reveals a shape instability which results in droplet division into two smaller droplets of equal size resembling cell division Zwicker et al. 2017 . In this paper, we investigate the reduced version of the continuum model, which is called the effective model. This model is studied both in the linear and nonlinear regime. First, we perform a linear stability analysis for the flat interface, and then we develop a nonlinear theory using the longwave approach. We find that the interface at the leading order is governed by the modified Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation. Therefore the interface is subject to a logarithmic blow up after a finite time. In addition, an expression for the interface local velocity is obtained.



I
INTRODUCTION

Over the past years, the dynamics and stability of active droplets
undergoing chemical reactions is a subject of extensive
studies
Lach et al. 2016
. In addition to numerous technological
applications, active droplets provide a model of a biological cell.
Specifically, they exhibit self-propulsion
Seemann et al. 2016
, growth and
spontaneous division
Macia and Sole 2007
;
Browne et al. 2010
;
Patashinski et al. 2012
;
Giomi and DeSimone 2014
.

Recently, a simple model of the active droplet division has been suggested
in
Zwicker et al. 2017
, which includes only two components
A
and
B
.
The droplet material
B
, which is surrounded by a solvent, is subject to
a spontaneous chemichal reaction

B\rightarrow A.

(1)

Molecules
A
are soluble, hence they leave the droplet and move to the
solvent, where they induce the chemical reaction,

A+C\rightarrow B+C^{\prime};

(2)

here
C
is the fuel and
C^{\prime}
is the product. Finally, material
B

diffuses inside the droplet thus completing the reaction cycle, see Fig
2
.

The problem was considered using two models,
(i) the continuum model with a diffuse interface between phases and a
continuous reaction function; (ii) the effective model with a sharp
interface and piecewise linear reaction function. In the numerical
simulations carried out for a spherical droplet, depending on the
droplet’s parameters, three different scenarios have been observed:
(i) the droplet shrinks until it disappears;
(ii) the droplet grows toward stationary radius where the influx is balanced
by the efflux across the
interface, and therefore it coexists with the surrounding (stable state);
(iii) the droplet undergoes shape instability where any small shape
deformation triggers the elongation along one axis until droplet division.

In the present paper, we investigate the effective model suggested in

Zwicker et al. 2017
by means of the nonlinear stability theory. In Sec.

II
, we briefly describe the mathematical models.
In Sec.
III
, we study the instability development of a motionless
one-dimensional interphase boundary. The instability criterion is
obtained, and the equation governing the boundary evolution is derived in
the limit of long waves. In Sec.
IV
, the analysis is done for a
moving boundary. In Sec.
V
, the general case of a
two-dimensional moving interface is considered.

II
FORMULATION OF THE PROBLEM

In the present section, we briefly describe the models formulated in the
supplementary
information (SI) of Ref.
Zwicker et al. 2017
.

II.1
The continuum model

Let us consider a segregated binary solution with component
B
dissolved in solvent
A
. The field
u
describes the concentration of droplet material
B
both inside and outside the droplet, therefore the phase with a high equilibrium concentration of
B
,
u_{-}^{(0)}
, forms droplets in the ocean of the phase with a low
concentration,
u_{+}^{(0)}
. The free energy density function has a double well shape,

f(u)=\frac{b}{2(\Delta u)^{2}}\left(u-u_{-}^{(0)}\right)^{2}\left(u-u_{+}^{(0)}\right)^{2}

where the positive parameter
b
characterizes the molecular interactions and entropic contributions, and

\Delta u=u_{-}^{(0)}-u_{+}^{(0)}>0
.

The state of the system is described by the free energy functional

F[u]=\int_{V}\left(f(u)+\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)\textrm{d}\upsilon,

where
V
is the volume of the system,
\textrm{d}\upsilon
is a volume element, and
\kappa
is a coefficient,
which determines the surface tension
and the interface width
Cahn and Hilliard 1958
. Consequently, the chemical potential is given by the variational derivative,

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\delta F}{\delta u}=

\displaystyle\frac{b}{|\Delta u|^{2}}\left(u-u_{-}^{(0)}\right)\left(u-u_{+}^{(0)}\right)\left(2u-u_{-}^{(0)}-u_{+}^{(0)}\right)-\kappa\nabla^{2}u.

The component
B
is subject to diffusion and chemical reactions. Hence, the material concentration field
dynamics is governed by the reaction diffusion equation,

u_{t}=\nabla\cdot(m(u)\nabla\mu)+s(u),

(3)

where
m(u)
is a mobility coefficient of the component
B
, and the reaction function
s(u)

is designed
to be linear in the phases outside and inside the droplet, and smoothly interpolated by a cubic polynomial
p_{3}(u)

in some interval
u_{c}^{-}<u<u_{c}^{+}
, where
u_{c}^{\pm}
are some characteristic concentrations,

u_{c}^{-}<u_{-}^{(0)}
,
u_{c}^{+}>u_{+}^{(0)}
, see Fig.
1
,

s(u)=\begin{cases}\nu^{+}+k^{+}(u_{+}^{(0)}-u),&u\leq u_{c}^{+},\\
p_{3}(u),&u_{c}^{+}\leq u\leq u_{c}^{-},\\
-\nu^{-}+k^{-}(u_{-}^{(0)}-u),&u_{c}^{-}\leq u,\end{cases}

(4)

here
k^{\pm}
are the reaction rate outside and inside the droplet respectively, and similarly
\nu^{\pm}
are the
reaction fluxes at equilibrium concentration. We do not present here the explicit expression for

p_{3}(u)
, but we notice that the polynomial
p_{3}(u)
is uniquely determined.

II.2
The effective sharp-interface model

The model described above determines a diffuse boundary between the phases; its width is

\delta\sim(\kappa/b)^{1/2}
. Below we assume that the width of the transition layer
\delta
is small with
respect to any other characteristic scales of the problem, and apply the sharp interface limit. Within that
limit, two boundary conditions are applied at the sharp interface. The first one is the continuity of the
chemical potential across the interface,

\mu(u^{-})=\mu(u^{+}),

(5)

and the second one is the Laplace pressure jump across the interface,

(u^{-}-u^{+})\mu(u^{-})+f(u^{+})-f(u^{-})=2\sigma H,

(6)

where
u^{-}
and
u^{+}
are the concentration values at the interface
inside and outside the droplet, respectively,
\sigma=(\Delta u)^{2}\sqrt{\kappa b}/6
is the
surface tension or the free
energy per unit of area of the interface, and
H
is the
local mean curvature of the interface,

H=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}),

(7)

where
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are principal curvatures of the interface.
Equations (
5
) and (
6
)
are the coexistence conditions of the inside and outside phases that are separated by the droplet interface
Zwicker 2013
,
Weber et al. 2018
.

Plot of the source function
s(u)
in (
4
) that behaves as cubic polynomial when

u_{c}^{-}<u<u_{c}^{+}
and linear otherwise.

The next step of the model simplification is the linearization of equations (
3
), (
4
). The source
function (
4
) is approximated by a piecewise linear function,

s^{L}(u)=\begin{cases}\nu^{+}+k^{+}(u_{+}^{(0)}-u),&\text{outside the droplet},\\
-\nu^{-}+k^{-}(u_{-}^{(0)}-u),&\text{inside the droplet}.\end{cases}

Later on, we will refer the signs (
+
) and (
-
) for the values outside and inside the droplet, respectively.

Linearizing equation (
3
) around the values
u_{-}^{(0)}
and
u_{+}^{(0)}
inside and outside the droplet
respectively yields
the linear equations,

\partial_{t}u^{\pm}=D^{\pm}\nabla^{2}u^{\pm}-m^{\pm}\kappa\nabla^{4}u^{\pm}+s^{L}(u),

(8)

where
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
are the concentration of the droplet material
B
outside and inside the
droplet respectively,

D^{\pm}
and
m^{\pm}
are constant coefficients.
Below we omit the bi-harmonic terms
m^{\pm}\kappa\nabla^{4}u^{\pm}
in equations (

),
following the estimates presented in Ref.
Zwicker et al. 2017
.

In the small surface tension limit,
\sigma\ll\Delta u/(H\beta)
, where
\beta=2/(b\Delta u)

is
the coefficient that describes the Laplace pressure effect on the interface’s concentration, the equilibrium conditions (
5
) and (
6
) are approximated as follows,

\displaystyle u^{-}\sim u_{-}^{(0)}+\beta\sigma H,

\displaystyle u^{+}\sim u_{+}^{(0)}+\beta\sigma H.

The interface dynamics is governed by

v_{n}=\hat{\textbf{n}}\cdot\frac{\textbf{j}^{-}-\textbf{j}^{+}}{u^{-}-u^{+}},

(9)

where
v_{n}
is the normal velocity of the interface,
\hat{\textbf{n}}
is a unit vector normal to the interface, and
\textbf{j}^{\pm}=-D^{\pm}\nabla u^{\pm}
are the diffusion fluxes outside and inside the droplet, respectively. Recall again that
u^{\pm}
in (
9
) are the concentrations evaluated outside and inside the droplet interface, respectively.

II.3
Formulation of the nondimensional local problem

Both the continuum model and the effective model of the previous sections were studied numerically in the
framework of a spherical droplet in a spherical coordinate system centered at the droplet center (SI
Zwicker et al. 2017
). In
this paper, we concentrate on the region where the division starts, see Fig.
2
. In that region,
we formulate the effective model in the infinite space using Cartesian coordinates
(x,y,z)
, i.e.,
the droplet dynamics is reduced to the dynamics of a distorted
flat
interface that could be
described by the function
z=q(x,y,t)
, (see Fig.
2
for the one dimensional description). The goal of
the next sections is to understand the dynamics of such interface using only analytical tools both in
the linear and nonlinear regime.

material

We begin with the formulation of the effective model in a nondimensional form. We introduce the
concentration, length, and time scales, respectively,

\Delta u=u_{-}^{(0)}-u_{+}^{(0)},\quad L^{+}=\sqrt{\frac{D^{+}}{k^{+}}},\quad T^{+}=\frac{1}{k^{+}};

then we define the scaled variables and fields,

\displaystyle(x,y,z)=L^{+}(x^{*},y^{*},z^{*}),\quad t=T^{+}t^{*},

\displaystyle u(x,y,z,t)=(\Delta u)\ u^{**}(L^{+}x^{*},L^{+}y^{*},L^{+}z^{*},T^{+}t^{*})=

\displaystyle(\Delta u)\ u^{*}(x^{*},y^{*},z^{*},t^{*}),

\displaystyle q(x,y,t)=L^{+}q^{**}(L^{+}x^{*},L^{+}y^{*},T^{+}t^{*})=L^{+}q^{*}(x^{*},y^{*},t^{*}),

\displaystyle H(x,y,t)=(L^{+})^{-1}H^{**}(L^{+}x^{*},L^{+}y^{*},T^{+}t^{*})=

\displaystyle(L^{+})^{-1}H^{*}(x^{*},y^{*},t^{*}).

As a result, we obtain six nondimensional parameters of the system,

\displaystyle N^{\pm}=\frac{\nu^{\pm}}{k^{+}\Delta u},\quad U^{-}=\frac{u_{-}^{(0)}}{\Delta u},\quad B=\frac{\beta\sigma}{2L^{+}\Delta u},

\displaystyle D=\frac{D^{-}}{D^{+}},\quad K=\frac{k^{-}}{k^{+}}.

(10)

Denote
U^{+}=\frac{u_{+}^{(0)}}{\Delta u}=U^{-}-1
and drop the stars, then the local effective model of subsection
II.2
takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{+}=\nabla^{2}u^{+}-u^{+}+U^{+}+N^{+},\ z>q(x,y,t),

(11a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{-}=

\displaystyle D\nabla^{2}u^{-}-Ku^{-}+KU^{-}-N^{-},\quad z<q(x,y,t),

(11b)

\displaystyle u^{\pm}=U^{\pm}+2BH(x,y,t);\quad z=q(x,y,t),

(11c)

the nondimensional form of the front dynamics equation is

\displaystyle\left(u^{-}-u^{+}\right)\partial_{t}q=\left(-\partial_{x}q,-\partial_{y}q,1\right)\cdot\left(\nabla u^{+}-D\nabla u^{-}\right),

\displaystyle z=q(x,y,t).

(12)

III
STABILITY ANALYSIS FOR STATIONARY ONE DIMENSIONAL INTERFACE

III.1
Stationary state of the flat interface

First, let us consider the flat
stationary
interface
q(x,t)=\bar{q}
; without losing
of generality, we may assume that
\bar{q}=0
. The local curvature vanishes in this case,
H=0
. Also, we assume that the
concentration field is translationally invariant along the

and

-

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}\bar{u}^{+}-\bar{u}^{+}+U^{+}+N^{+}=0,\quad z>0,

\displaystyle D\partial_{z}^{2}\bar{u}^{-}-K\bar{u}^{-}+KU^{-}-N^{-}=0,\quad z<0,

(13b)

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{\pm}=U^{\pm},\quad z=0;

(13c)

Equations (

13c

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{+}=U^{+}+N^{+}\left(1-\textrm{e}^{-z}\right),

(14a)

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{-}=U^{-}-\frac{N^{-}}{K}\left(1-\textrm{e}^{z\sqrt{K/D}}\right).

(14b)

Considering the front dynamics in equations (

\displaystyle N^{+}=N^{-}\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}.

Equation (

In view of equation (

therefore,

determines the excess concentration of the droplet material far from the droplet. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sigma=\left(-\omega^{2}B+N^{+}\right)\sqrt{\sigma+\omega^{2}+1}+

III.3
Longwave nonlinear theory

In the present section, we perform a detailed derivation of the closed longwave nonlinear

Recall that the effective model (

First let us perform the shift transformations

At the next order,
O(\varepsilon^{6})
, we will obtain a closed equation
governing the interface dynamics
q_{0}(\xi,\tau)
. It holds that,

\displaystyle u_{6\zeta\zeta}^{+}-u_{6}^{+}=(E^{+}+\zeta F^{+})\textrm{e}^{-\zeta},\quad\zeta>0,

\displaystyle u_{6\zeta\zeta}^{-}-\frac{K}{D}u_{6}^{-}=(E^{-}+\zeta F^{-})\textrm{e}^{\zeta\sqrt{K/D}},\quad\zeta<0,

\displaystyle u_{6}^{\pm}=\Phi\left(\frac{3}{2}q_{0\xi}^{2}q_{0\xi\xi}-q_{1\xi\xi}\right),\quad\zeta=0,

\displaystyle q_{0\tau}=u_{6\zeta}^{+}-Du_{6\zeta}^{-}-q_{0\xi}\left(u_{4\xi}^{+}-Du_{4\xi}^{-}\right),\quad\zeta=0,

(31)

where
E^{\pm}
, and
F^{\pm}
are tedious expression that include
q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},u_{4}^{\pm}
, and their derivatives, see Appendix
A
. One can calculate,

\displaystyle u_{6}^{+}=\left[u_{6}^{+}(\zeta=0)-\left(\frac{E^{+}}{2}+\frac{F^{+}}{4}\right)\zeta-\frac{F^{+}}{4}\zeta^{2}\right]\textrm{e}^{-\zeta},

\displaystyle u_{6}^{-}=\Big[u_{6}^{-}(\zeta=0)+\left(\frac{E^{-}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}-\frac{DF^{-}}{4K}\right)\zeta

\displaystyle+\frac{F^{+}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}\zeta^{2}\Big]\textrm{e}^{\zeta\sqrt{K/D}},

therefore equation (
31
) yields the following amplitude equation for the interface at
leading order,

\partial_{\tau}q_{0}=-\alpha_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{2}q_{0}-\beta_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{4}q_{0}+\gamma_{1}\left(\partial_{\xi}^{2}q_{0}\right)^{2},

(32)

where,

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\Lambda_{2}^{+}\left(1+\frac{D}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\Phi\left(1+D\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}\right)+\frac{\Lambda_{c}^{+}}{4}\left(1+\left[\frac{D}{K}\right]^{3/2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=\frac{\Phi}{4}\left[3(D-1)+\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}(1-K)\right].

(33)

Let us emphasize that the nonlinear term in the obtained amplitude equation is different from the

(\partial_{\xi}q_{0})^{2}
term characteristic for the
Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation
, which is typical for
instabilities of reaction fronts and phase transition fronts. The reason is that we consider here the longwave
instability of a
motionless
reaction front (see (
III.1
)), while the above-mentioned nonlinear term
is related to the nonlinearity of the expression for
v_{n}
(see (
9
), and (
12
)). Note that equation (
32
) can be
transformed to the
Sivashinsky equation
,

\partial_{\tau}Q=-\alpha_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{2}Q-\beta_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{4}Q+\gamma_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{2}(Q^{2}),

(34)

where
Q=\partial_{\xi}^{2}q_{0}
.

In a contradistinction to the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation, which describes the
development of a spatio-temporal chaos
Yamada and Kuramoto 1976
,
the temporal evolution of a disturbance governed by the Sivashinsky equation leads to formation of singularity. Below we briefly describe the
results of the paper
Bernoff and Bertozzi 1995
that contains a detailed description of that singularity.

Near the singularity point,

\partial_{\xi}^{2}(Q^{2})\gg\partial_{\xi}^{2}Q,

therefore at the leading order one
can disregard the first term in the right-hand side of (
34
). By applying the transformation

(\beta_{1}/\gamma_{1})g(\xi,\bar{\tau})=q_{0\xi\xi}
, and
\bar{\tau}=\beta_{1}\tau
on
equation (
32
), one obtains the universal equation

g_{\bar{\tau}}+\partial_{\xi}^{4}g-\left(g^{2}\right)_{\xi\xi}=0.

Its self-similar solution,

\displaystyle g(\xi,\bar{\tau})=(\bar{\tau}_{c}-\bar{\tau})^{-1/2}f(r),

\displaystyle\quad r=\frac{\xi-\xi_{c}}{(\bar{\tau}_{c}-\bar{\tau})^{1/4}}

describes the formation of a singularity at finite time
\bar{\tau}=\bar{\tau}_{c}
in the
point
\xi=\xi_{c}
. Here

f
is a smooth function which satisfies an ordinary differential equation,

\frac{1}{2}\left(f+\frac{1}{2}rf_{r}\right)=(f^{2})_{rr}-\partial_{r}^{4}f,\quad-\infty<r<\infty.

For the singularity of
q(\xi,\tau)
, the following relations have been obtained

Bernoff and Bertozzi 1995
:

\displaystyle q(\xi_{c},\tau)\sim-\frac{\gamma_{1}C}{\bar{\beta}}\ln(\tau_{c}-\tau)+A_{0}+O\Big(\sqrt{\tau_{c}-\tau}\Big),

(35a)

\displaystyle C\approx 2.01,

\displaystyle q(\xi,\tau)\sim-\frac{4\gamma_{1}C}{\bar{\beta}}\ln|\xi-\xi_{c}|+B_{0}+

(35b)

\displaystyle O\left(\sqrt{\tau_{c}-\tau},\frac{\tau_{c}-\tau}{(\xi-\xi_{c})^{4}}\right),\quad(\tau_{c}-\tau)^{1/4}\ll|\xi-\xi_{c}|\ll 1,

where
A_{0}
, and
B_{0}
are constant.

Note that the singularity of the interface distortion propagates towards the droplet
phase (that would correspond to the droplet division) only if
\gamma_{1}<0
.

IV
MOVING FRONT ANALYSIS

If the relation (
III.1
) is violated, the flat front moves.
In this section we perform the analysis of a moving one dimensional front solution both in the linear and
nonlinear regime.
Therefore we assume a moving front
q(x,t)=vt+h(x,t)
, and consider a traveling solution
u(x,z,t)=\tilde{u}(x,\tilde{z},t)
, where

\tilde{z}=z-vt
. We drop the tildes then equations (
11a
-
12
) take the form,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{+}=\nabla^{2}u^{+}+vu_{z}^{+}-u^{+}+U^{+}+N^{+},\quad z>h(x,t),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{-}=

\displaystyle D\nabla^{2}u^{-}+vu_{z}^{-}-Ku^{-}+KU^{-}-N^{-},\quad z<h(x,t),

\displaystyle u^{\pm}=U^{\pm}+2BH(x,t),\quad z=h(x,t),

\displaystyle\left(u^{-}-u^{+}\right)(v+h_{t})=

\displaystyle\left(-h_{x},1\right)\cdot\left(\nabla u^{+}-D\nabla u^{-}\right),\quad z=h(x,t).

A moving flat front solution takes the form,

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{+}=U^{+}+N^{+}\left(1-\textrm{e}^{zr_{-}}\right),\quad r_{-}=\frac{-v-\sqrt{v^{2}+4}}{2}

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{-}=U^{-}-\frac{N^{-}}{K}\left(1-\textrm{e}^{zr_{+}}\right),\quad r_{+}=\frac{-v+\sqrt{v^{2}+4DK}}{2D},

while the flux balance (
III.1
) gives

v+N^{+}r_{-}+\frac{DN^{-}}{K}r_{+}=0.

(36)

The dispersion relation (
21
) is generalized to

\displaystyle\sigma=m_{-}(\omega^{2}B+N^{+}r_{-})-Dm_{+}\left(\omega^{2}B-\frac{N^{-}r_{+}}{K}\right)-

\displaystyle N^{+}r_{-}^{2}-\frac{DN^{-}}{K}r_{+}^{2},

\displaystyle m_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-v-\sqrt{v^{2}+4(\sigma+\omega^{2}+1)}\right)<0,

\displaystyle m_{+}=\frac{1}{2D}\left(-v+\sqrt{v^{2}+4D(\sigma+D\omega^{2}+K)}\right)>0.

Following the procedure of the nonlinear longwave analysis described in Section

III.3
, and
choosing the velocity scaling
v=\varepsilon^{4}V
,
V=V_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}V_{2}+\cdots
in addition to (
25
), we obtain the
relations

\displaystyle\Lambda_{c}^{+}-\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}\Lambda_{c}^{-}=0,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}^{+}-\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}\Lambda_{2}^{-}=V_{0},

(37)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\Lambda_{c}^{+}+\frac{D}{K}\Lambda_{c}^{-}\right)+(1+\sqrt{DK})\Phi=0,

which are in agreement with (
36
).
Finally, the moving interface dynamics at the leading order is governed by the following
equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}h_{0}+\alpha_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{2}h_{0}+\beta_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{4}h_{0}-\frac{V_{0}}{2}\left(\partial_{\xi}h_{0}\right)^{2}-\gamma_{1}\left(\partial_{\xi}^{2}h_{0}\right)^{2}=V_{2},

\displaystyle V_{2}=\frac{V_{0}}{2}\left(\Lambda_{c}^{+}+\frac{\Lambda_{c}^{-}}{K}\right).

(38)

We may write
h_{0}=V_{2}\tau+\psi
, then we obtain the
modified Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation

Bernoff and Bertozzi 1995

\psi_{\tau}+\alpha_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\psi+\beta_{1}\partial_{\xi}^{4}\psi-\frac{V_{0}}{2}(\partial_{\xi}\psi)^{2}-\gamma_{1}(\partial_{\xi}^{2}\psi)^{2}=0,

(39)

which contains two nonlinear terms.

The structure of the obtained equation can be understood in the following way. The normal
velocity
v_{n}
of the one-dimensional phase transition boundary is determined by its curvature

\kappa=2H
. If the curvature is small and slowly depends on the coordinate, the long-wave
expansion for
v_{n}
is

v_{n}\sim V-\alpha_{1}\kappa-\beta_{1}\partial_{s}^{2}\kappa+\gamma_{1}\kappa^{2}+\ldots,

(40)

where
V
is the velocity of the flat boundary,
\partial_{s}
is the derivative along the
boundary,
\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}
and
\gamma_{1}
are constant coefficients
Frankel and Sivashinsky 1988
.
For small deformations, expression (
40
) leads to (
38
). The term proportional to

(\partial_{\xi}\psi)^{2}
has a kinematic origin: it is caused by the relation

v_{n}=\frac{\partial_{t}h}{\sqrt{1+(\partial_{x}h)^{2}}}.

Therefore, that term, which is characteristic for the standard Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation, is
ubiquitous in deformational instabilities of moving fronts.
The term proportional to
(\partial_{\xi}^{2}\psi)^{2}
, which is caused by the dependence of the normal
velocity on the curvature, is the same as in (
32
), and it creates a finite-time
singularity, which corresponds to creation of a caustic for the curve moving according to
(
40
).

V
DYNAMICS OF THE TWO DIMENSIONAL INTERFACE

The generalization of the derivation presented above to the moving two dimensional interface
q(x,y,t)=vt+h(x,y,t)

is straightforward.

Expression (
7
) is replaced with

H=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\nabla^{2}_{\perp}q+q_{xx}q_{y}^{2}-2q_{x}q_{y}q_{xy}+q_{yy}q_{x}^{2}}{(1+|\nabla_{\perp}q|^{2})^{3/2}},

where

\nabla^{2}_{\perp}q=q_{xx}+q_{yy},\quad|\nabla_{\perp}q|^{2}=q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2}.

Using the expansion

h(x,y,t)=h_{0}+\varepsilon h_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}h_{2}+...,

and rescaling the coordinates as
\xi=\varepsilon x
,
\eta=\varepsilon y
,
we obtain the following expansion of the curvature,

\displaystyle H=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}h_{0}-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}h_{1}+\varepsilon^{4}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}h_{2}+

\displaystyle\frac{3}{4}(h_{0\xi}^{2}h_{0\xi\xi}+h_{0\eta}^{2}h_{0\eta\eta})+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(h_{0\xi}^{2}h_{0\eta\eta}+h_{0\eta}^{2}h_{0\xi\xi})+h_{0\xi}h_{0\eta}h_{0\xi\eta}\Big]+....

The results of the linear theory of section
III
are unchanged, with
\omega
being the modulus of the wavevector.
The weakly nonlinear analysis gives the following evolutionary equation of the interface at the leading order,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\psi=-\alpha_{1}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\psi-\beta_{1}\nabla_{\perp}^{4}\psi+\gamma_{2}\left(\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\psi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{V_{0}}{2}|\nabla_{\perp}\psi|^{2}-\delta\left(|\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{x}|^{2}|+\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{z}|^{2}\right),

(41)

where
h_{0}=V_{2}\tau+\psi
and

\delta=\frac{\Phi}{2}(1+\sqrt{DK})\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{D}{K}}\right),\quad\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{1}+\delta.

Using the relation

\left(\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\psi\right)^{2}-\left(\left|\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{x}\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla_{\perp}\psi_{y}\right|^{2}\right)=2\left(\psi_{xx}\psi_{yy}-(\psi_{xy})^{2}\right),

we can rewrite equation (
41
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\psi=-\alpha_{1}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\psi-\beta_{1}\nabla_{\perp}^{4}\psi+\gamma_{1}\left(\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\psi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{V_{0}}{2}|\nabla_{\perp}\psi|^{2}+2\delta(\psi_{xx}\psi_{yy}-\psi_{xy}^{2}).

(42)

The evolution equation (
42
) corresponds to the longwave limit of the following
expression for the normal velocity
Frankel and Sivashinsky 1988
:

v_{n}\sim V-\alpha_{1}\kappa-\beta_{1}\nabla_{s}^{2}\kappa+\gamma_{1}\kappa^{2}+2\delta G+...,

where
\kappa=2H
and

G=\frac{q_{xx}q_{yy}-q_{xy}^{2}}{(1+|\nabla_{\perp}q|^{2})^{2}}

is the Gaussian curvature of the surface.
The development of the singularity for that equation was studied in
Bernoff and Bertozzi 1995
.
The one-dimensional case, (
32
) is recovered.

VI
Conclusion

We have obtained the condition on the system parameters (
III.1
)
to achieve the coexistence of two phases separated by the motionless interface. In the case of moving front, an
exact expression of the local interface normal velocity has been obtained (
37
),(
38
).
We have carried out the linear stability analysis of the flat interface and found a monotonic
longwave instability. A weakly nonlinear theory near the instability threshold has been
developed. The local interface dynamics is governed by the modified Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation
that develops a logarithmic singularity after a finite time (
32
),(
39
),(
41
).
