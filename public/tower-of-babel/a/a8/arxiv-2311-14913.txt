Tensor Unfolding Characterization
Shih Yu Chang

Abstract
Tensors play a pivotal role in the realms of science and engineering, particularly in the realms of data analysis, machine learning, and computational mathematics. The process of unfolding a tensor into matrices, commonly known as tensor unfolding or matricization, serves as a valuable technique for simplifying the representation of tensors with higher orders. In this study, we initially derive unfolded matrices from a specified tensor over a B’ezout ring using a matrix equivalence relation. We proceed to elucidate the relationships between eigenvalues and eigenvectors within these unfolded matrices. Additionally, we employ the localization approach outlined by Gerstein to ascertain the count of distinct matrix equivalence classes present among the unfolded matrices.



Index terms—
Tensors, unfolding, B’ezout ring, matrix equivalence.

Introduction

Tensors play a crucial role in the fields of science and engineering, especially in the context of data analysis, machine learning, and computational mathematics. We discuss how the following fields are affected by tensors. Tensors are employed in simulations and modeling in physics and engineering. They help represent physical quantities that vary in multiple dimensions. For example, stress and strain tensors are used in materials science and structural engineering simulations
[
1
]
. Tensors are fundamental to the field of machine learning, particularly in deep learning. Neural networks, the backbone of deep learning models, use tensors to store and process data. Tensors facilitate the flow of information through the network, enabling the training and inference processes
[
2

3
]
. In engineering, signals are often represented as tensors. For instance, in the field of image processing, tensors are used to represent and process signals, allowing for tasks such as image denoising, compression, and feature extraction
[
4
]
. Tensors are used in numerical methods and simulations to solve partial differential equations and other mathematical problems. They enable efficient representation and manipulation of large datasets in numerical computations
[
5
]
. In summary, tensors serve as a foundational concept in various scientific and engineering disciplines, providing a powerful mathematical framework for representing and analyzing complex data structures. Their impact is particularly pronounced in fields where data is multi-dimensional and intricate computations are required.

Unfolding a tensor into matrices, often referred to as tensor unfolding or matricization, is a technique used to simplify the representation of higher-order tensors. This process is particularly common in the context of tensor decompositions, where the goal is to express a high-dimensional tensor as a sum of simpler components. Unfolding helps in applying well-established linear algebraic methods that operate on matrices, making it easier to analyze and manipulate the data
[
6
]
. An unfolded tensor refers to a matrix created by systematically rearranging the entries of the tensor into a two-dimensional array. Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a Bézout ring. Consider a tensor space in
\mathfrak{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{M}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{M}}
and a matrix space in
\mathfrak{R}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}\times\mathbb{I}_{M}}

, as follows:

\displaystyle(\psi_{r},\psi_{c}):\mathfrak{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{M}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{M}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}\times\mathbb{I}_{M}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=[b_{\bm{i}_{M},\bm{j}_{M}}]

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\bm{B}=[b_{\psi_{r}(\bm{i}_{M},\bm{I}_{M}),\psi_{c}(\bm{j}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})}],

(1)

where
\psi_{r}(\bm{i}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})\in[\mathbb{I}_{M}]
and
\psi_{c}(\bm{j}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})\in[\mathbb{I}_{M}]
are two index mapping functions for the row part and the column part, respectively. For example, we can have the following mapping relations for
\psi_{r}(\bm{i}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})
and
\psi_{c}(\bm{j}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})
expressed by

\displaystyle\psi_{r}(\bm{i}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle i_{1}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{M}(i_{k}-1)\prod\limits_{\ell=1}^{k-1}I_{\ell},

(2)

and

\displaystyle\psi_{c}(\bm{j}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle j_{1}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{M}(j_{k}-1)\prod\limits_{\ell=1}^{k-1}I_{\ell}.

(3)

Many works, for example

) to relate tensor spectrum theorem and tensor SVD for the tensor

Theorem 1

Given a tensor
\mathcal{B}=[b_{\bm{i}_{M},\bm{j}_{M}}]\in\mathfrak{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{M}\times I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{M}}
and two unfolding maps
(\psi_{r},\psi_{c})
and
(\psi^{\prime}_{r},\psi^{\prime}_{c})
with
\psi_{r}=\psi_{c}
and
\psi^{\prime}_{r}=\psi^{\prime}_{c}
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{B}=[b_{\psi_{r}(\bm{i}_{M},\bm{I}_{M}),\psi_{c}(\bm{j}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})}],

(7)

and

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{\prime}=[b_{\psi^{\prime}_{r}(\bm{i}_{M},\bm{I}_{M}),\psi^{\prime}_{c}(\bm{j}_{M},\bm{I}_{M})}].

(8)

Then,
\bm{B}^{\prime}
and
\bm{B}
have the same eigenvalues, and the eigenvector of
\bm{B}^{\prime}
is
\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{v}
given
\bm{B}=\lambda\bm{v}
, where
\bm{P}
is the permutation matrix from the index map given by
\psi_{r}
to the index map given by
\psi^{\prime}_{r}
.

Proof:

From Eq. (
6
), we have

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{\prime}=\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{B}\bm{P}\Longleftrightarrow\bm{P}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{P}^{-1}=\bm{B}.

(9)

If
\bm{B}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}
, then we have

\displaystyle\bm{P}\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}\Longleftrightarrow\bm{B}^{\prime}\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{v}.

(10)

If
\bm{v}
is an eigenvector of
\bm{B}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
, then
\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{v}
is an eigenvector of
\bm{B}^{\prime}
with the same eigenvalue. By interchanging the roles of
\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}^{\prime}
,
\bm{B}^{\prime}
and
\bm{B}
have the same eigenvalues and the eignevector of
\bm{B}^{\prime}
is
\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{v}
given
\bm{B}=\lambda\bm{v}
.

3.1
The Local-Global Principle of Smith Normal Form

Let
\mathfrak{R}
be a Bézout ring with quotine field
\mathfrak{R}\subset\mathbb{F}
, and let
p
be a fixed prime element of
\mathfrak{R}
. Then, every element
f\in\mathbb{F}
can be expressed by the following form

\displaystyle f=\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}p^{a},

(40)

where
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathfrak{R}
and they are relative prime to
p
, and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since the integer
a
is determined by
f
uniquely, we use the symbol
\mbox{ord}_{p}f
to represent this
a
value. The
localization
of
\mathfrak{R}
with respect to the prime ideal
p
, denoted by
\mathfrak{R}_{p}
, can be defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{(p)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{f\in\mathbb{F}|\mbox{ord}_{p}f\geq 0\}.

(41)

Note that
\mathfrak{R}_{(p)}
is a principal ideal domain, and the proper ideals of
\mathfrak{R}_{(p)}
satisfy
(p)\supset(p^{2})\supset(p^{3})\supset\cdots
.

Given unfolded matrices
\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}^{\prime}
, we say that
\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}^{\prime}
is equivalent over
\mathfrak{R}
, denoted by
\bm{B}\sim\bm{B}^{\prime}
, if

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{\prime}=\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{B}\bm{P},

(42)

where matrix entries of
\bm{B}^{\prime},\bm{B}\in\mathfrak{R}
. On the other hand, we say that
\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}^{\prime}
is equivalent over
\mathfrak{R}_{(p)}
, denoted by
\bm{B}\sim_{p}\bm{B}^{\prime}
, if

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{\prime}=\bm{Q}^{-1}\bm{B}\bm{P},

(43)

where matrix entries of
\bm{B}^{\prime},\bm{B}\in\mathfrak{R}_{(p)}
. If
\bm{B}\in\mathfrak{R}_{(p)}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}\times\mathbb{I}_{M}}
, then
\bm{B}
has a
Smith normal form
over
\mathfrak{R}_{(p)}
expressed by

\displaystyle\bm{B}\sim_{p}\mbox{diag}\left(p^{a_{1}},\cdots,p^{a_{r}},0,\cdots,0\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}S_{p}(\bm{B}),

(44)

where integers

and

. We have the following theorem about

Theorem 2

Let
\bm{B},\bm{B}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}\times\mathbb{I}_{M}}

, we have
\bm{B}\sim\bm{B}^{\prime}
if and only if
\bm{B}\sim_{p}\bm{B}^{\prime}
for all prime ideals

. Besides, we also have

\displaystyle S(\bm{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{p\in P}S_{p}(\bm{B}),

(45)

where

is the

of
\bm{B}
, and

is a set of nonassociated primes.

Let

represent the unique prime ideals whose powers appear as elementary divisors of the matrix
\bm{B}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle S(\bm{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{diag}\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}^{a_{1,k}},\cdots,\prod\limits_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}^{a_{r,k}},0,\cdots,0\right),

\displaystyle S_{p}(\bm{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{diag}\left(\overbrace{1,\cdots,1}^{r},0,\cdots,0\right).

3.2
The Number of Equivalence Classes

Let
\sigma(\mathfrak{R})
be the set of
\sim
-equivalanece classes for the matrix
\bm{B}\in\mathfrak{R}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}\times\mathbb{I}_{M}}
provided Eq. (
42
). We use
\tilde{\bm{B}}
to represent the equivalence class of the matrix
\bm{B}
and define the following addition operation with respect to any two equivalence classes
\tilde{\bm{B}}
and
\tilde{\bm{D}}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{B}}+\tilde{\bm{D}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\widetilde{B\oplus D},

(49)

where
\oplus
is the direct sum operation. Similarly, we also define the following product operation with respect to any two equivalence classes
\tilde{\bm{B}}
and
\tilde{\bm{D}}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{B}}\tilde{\bm{D}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\widetilde{B\otimes D},

(50)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product of two matrices. We have the following theorem to characterize
(\mathfrak{R})
via polynomials.

Theorem 3

Let
\sigma(\mathfrak{R})
be the set of
\sim
-equivalanece classes for the matrix
\bm{B}\in\mathfrak{R}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}\times\mathbb{I}_{M}}
. There exists an injective semiring homomorphisim, denoted by
\Psi
, which is given by

\displaystyle\Psi:\sigma(\mathfrak{R})\rightarrow\prod\limits_{p\in P}\mathbb{Z}^{+}[x_{p}],

(51)

where
\mathbb{Z}^{+}[x_{p}]
is the semiring of polynomials in
x_{p}
with nonnegative integral coefficients.

Proof:

We will apply the localization technique to
\sigma(\mathfrak{R})
. Considering
\bm{B}
with the following Smith normal form

\displaystyle S_{p}(\bm{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{diag}\left(\overbrace{1,\cdots,1}^{n_{p,0}},\overbrace{p,\cdots,p}^{n_{p,1}},\cdots\overbrace{p^{r_{p}},\cdots,p^{r_{p}}}^{n_{p,r_{p}}}\right),

(52)

then, we define

\displaystyle\Psi_{p}(\tilde{\bm{B}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{r_{p}}n_{p,k}x_{p}^{k},

(53)

where
n_{p,k}
are nonnegative integers. Note that R.H.S. of Eq. (
53
) belongs to
\mathbb{Z}^{+}[x_{p}]
. From Eq. (
49
) and Eq. (
50
), the map provided by Eq. (
53
) is a homomorphism map since

\displaystyle\bm{B}\oplus\bm{D}

\displaystyle\sim_{p}

\displaystyle S_{p}(\bm{B})\oplus S_{p}(\bm{D}),

\displaystyle\bm{B}\otimes\bm{D}

\displaystyle\sim_{p}

\displaystyle S_{p}(\bm{B})\otimes S_{p}(\bm{D}).

(54)

By setting

\displaystyle\Psi(\tilde{\bm{B}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\prod\limits_{p\in P}\Psi_{p}(\tilde{\bm{B}}).

(55)

We have
\Psi
is a homomorphism, and injective by Theorem
2
. This theorem is proved.

\hfill\Box

From Theorem
3
, we can have the following corollary to determine the number of equivalence classes in the set
\sigma(\mathfrak{R})
, represented by
|\sigma(\mathfrak{R})|
.

Corollary 1

Let
\sigma(\mathfrak{R})
be the set of
\sim
-equivalanece classes for the matrix
\bm{B}\in\mathfrak{R}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}\times\mathbb{I}_{M}}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle|\sigma(\mathfrak{R})|=\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}}H_{\mathbb{I}_{M}-1}^{k+1}\right)^{|P|},

(56)

where
|P|
is the number of distinct nonassociated primes in
P
.

Proof:

According to Eq. (
52
), we have

\displaystyle n_{p,0}+n_{p,1}+\cdots+n_{p,r_{p}}=\mathbb{I}_{M}.

(57)

For
r_{p}=k
, Eq. (
57
) can be expressed by

\displaystyle n_{p,0}+n_{p,1}+\cdots+n^{\prime}_{p,k}=\mathbb{I}_{M}-1,

(58)

where
n_{p,0}\geq 0
,
n_{p,1}\geq 0
,
\cdots
,
n^{\prime}_{p,k}\geq 0
, and
0\leq k\leq\mathbb{I}_{M}
. By applying
formula of combination with repetitions to Eq. (
58
), there are
H_{\mathbb{I}_{M}-1}^{k+1}
solutions of
n_{p,0},n_{p,1},\cdots,n^{\prime}_{p,k}
in Eq. (
58
), i.e., there are

H_{\mathbb{I}_{M}-1}^{k+1}
different
\Psi_{p}(\tilde{\bm{B}})
given

Because

, the number of different
\Psi_{p}(\tilde{\bm{B}})
can be determined by

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\mathbb{I}_{M}}H_{\mathbb{I}_{M}-1}^{k+1}.

(59)

Finally, from Theorem
3
(Eq. (
55
)), this corollary is proved.
