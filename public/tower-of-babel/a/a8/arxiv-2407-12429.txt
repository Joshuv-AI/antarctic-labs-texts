Quantum beats of a macroscopic polariton condensate in real space
R.V. Cherbunin, A. Liubomirov, D. Novokreschenov, A. Kudlis, A.V. Kavokin

Abstract
We observe experimentally the harmonic oscillations of a macroscopic many-body wavefunction of the bosonic condensate of exciton-polaritons confined in an elliptical trap. The oscillations are caused by quantum beats between two size-quantized states of the polariton condensate split in energy due to the ellipticity of the trap. The eigen-functions of these states are p p -shape orbitals tilted with respect to the main and the short axes of the trap. The beats between these states manifest themselves in a macroscopic periodical redistribution of the polariton density in real space. The control of frequency, amplitude and phase of the beats is achieved by sending non-resonant laser pulses to specific spots inside the trap. We visualize the condensate wave-function dynamics on a streak-camera and map them to the Bloch sphere demonstrating the implementation of Hadamard and Pauli-Z operations. The decay time of the observed oscillations is on a nanosecond scale. It exceeds the individual exciton-polariton life-time by two orders of magnitude and the coherence-time of the condensate by one order of magnitude.



1
Introduction

Quantum beats are a wide-spread phenomenon that is characteristic of a coherent dynamics of a two-level quantum system excited in a superposition state
Mitsunaga and Tang 1987
;
Dodd et al. 1964
;
Haroche et al. 1973
. The period of the beats is determined by the energy splitting of two participating quantum states
Dodd et al. 1964
, while their decay time characterizes the decoherence processes that are necessarily present in any quantum system coupled to an environment
Haroche et al. 1973
;
Shah 2013
. In condensed matter physics, quantum beats caused by size-quantization of electron
Roskos et al. 1992
, hole
Schmitt-Rink et al. 1992
or exciton
Göbel et al. 1990
wave-functions, Zeeman splitting
Bar-Ad and Bar-Joseph 1991
, spin-orbit
Trifonov et al. 2019
, hyperfine
Kotur et al. 2016
interactions
etc
have been documented. Quantum beats are typically detected by optical methods, including time-resolved photoluminescence
Alexandrov 1964
, pump-probe
Mitsunaga and Tang 1987
, four-wave mixing
Erland and Balslev 1993
, Faraday-rotation
Yugova et al. 2009
spectroscopy, spin noise
Müller et al. 2010
and photon-echo
Kurnit et al. 1964
spectroscopies as well as several other techniques. These approaches enable detection of the oscillations of intensity, polarisation, coherence degree and/or other measurable characteristics of light caused by quantum beats in crystals, molecules and other matter objects coupled to light
Scully and Zubairy 1997
.
In this context, bosonic light-matter quasiparticles, namely, exciton-polaritons
Deng et al. 2002
;
Kasprzak et al. 2006
;
Carusotto and Ciuti 2013
represent a unique laboratory for studies of the optical manifestations of quantum beats. In the strong exciton-photon coupling regime in semiconductor microcavities, the quantum beats between exciton-polariton eigen-modes frequently referred to as Rabi-oscillations have been observed since mid-1990s
Norris et al. 1994
;
Cao et al. 1995
. Later on, a number of experiments demonstrated the complex coherent oscillatory dynamics of the polarization and intensity of light emitted by linear combinations of various polariton states
Dominici et al. 2021
;
Renucci et al. 2005
that e.g. led to generation of full Bloch light beams
Dominici et al. 2021
. A fundamental factor that limits the decay time of polariton quantum beats is life-time of an individual exciton polariton which is typically less than hundred picoseconds even in high-quality semiconductor microcavities
Mukherjee et al. 2019
. Nevertheless, some oscillatory phenomena with much longer decay times in many-body bosonic condensates of exciton-polaritons have been recently theoretically discussed
Kim et al. 2020
;
Rayanov et al. 2015
;
Nalitov et al. 2019
and experimentally observed
Sigurdsson et al. 2022
;
Barrat et al. 2023
. It has been argued that the combination of strong optical non-linearity caused by repulsive polariton-polariton interactions, stimulated scattering of polaritons and dissipative coupling between different polariton condensates may lead to the limit-cycle solutions
Kim et al. 2020
, dynamical attractors
Rayanov et al. 2015
;
Sun et al. 2024
and even to polariton time-crystals
Nalitov et al. 2019
. The decay time of oscillations characteristic of non-linear phenomena such as limit cycles strongly exceeds the life-time of individual exciton-polaritons and, theoretically, tends to infinity. In contrast to the quantum beats characteristic of a linear two-level quantum system whose frequency is simply given by the splitting between two participating energy levels, the limit cycle oscillations have their dynamics governed by a variety of factors including the polariton concentration, polariton-polariton interaction constants, spatial dependence of the polariton lifetime
etc

Rayanov et al. 2015
.

In the present work, we report on the experimental observation of regular oscillations of a trapped exciton-polariton condensate which persist over at least
1\penalty\ \textup{ns}
. The frequency of the oscillations is defined by the splitting between size-quantization levels of the polariton condensate confined in a trap and it can be tuned either by changing the ellipticity of the trap or by modifying its potential with use of control pulses of light. In order to check that the observed dynamics is not linked with any dynamical attractor of a limit cycle type, we perturb the state of the condensate by sending control laser pulses. By mapping these dynamics to the Bloch sphere, we observe how a closed loop trajectory characteristic of quantum beats is changed upon arrival of control pulses. We observe no relaxation dynamics characteristic of an attractor. Based on these observations, we conclude that the observed oscillations are quantum beats experienced by the polariton condensate as a whole entity. These beats have several unique features: (i) they persist over times orders of magnitude longer than the single-polariton lifetime, (ii) they involve several thousands of polaritons composing the condensate, (iii) a pronounced real space dynamics is observed. The macroscopic many-body wavefunction of a polariton condensate changes on a length-scale of over
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
with a periodicity of about
100\penalty\ \textup{ps}
. This manifestation of coherent real-space dynamics of a macroscopic many-body object paves the way to realisation of polariton qubits as discussed in recent publications
Barrat et al. 2023
;
Xue et al. 2021
;
Kavokin et al. 2022
;
Ricco et al. 2024
.

2
Setting the trap

We study bosonic condensates of exciton-polaritons created by a non-resonant
cw
optical excitation in a planar GaAs-based microcavity. The sample description and its characteristic optical spectra can be found in
Aladinskaia et al. 2023
. We use a spatial-light modulator (SLM) to create an elliptical trap for exciton-polaritons. We take advantage of the driven-dissipative nature of exciton-polariton condensates in order to achieve bosonic condensation at the energy states other than the ground state. We carefully chose the design of the trap and the pumping intensity in order to make sure that the ensemble of exciton-polaritons populates a selected pair of energy levels of the trapping potential. The eigen wave-functions corresponding to these states represent two-dimensional
p
-shape orbitals.

4
Streak-camera measurements

Streak-camera measurements allow to visualise time- and space-resolved images of polariton condensates. In contrast to the time-resolved interferometry technique used e.g. in
Barrat et al. 2023
, the streak-camera does not provide information on the phase of the condensate: it only detects the intensity of emitted light. This might seem as a disadvantage, but in the case of quantum beats associated with the intensity oscillations, this technique appears to be more sensitive than interferometry. In particular, the decay time of beats of the intensity of photoluminescence appears to exceed significantly the decay time of the beats of the first-order coherence extracted from the interferometry measurements
Barrat et al. 2023
. Indeed, the decay of coherence oscillations is set by the temporal coherence of the condensate, while the intensity beats caused by real-space oscillations of the condensate wave-function decay with the spatial coherence of the system that is much more robust to dephasing. From our measurements, we conclude that the spatial coherence time is definitely longer than 1 ns. We anticipate that this time is actually much longer, having in mind that the streak-camera signal is averaged over about 1 million of single-pulse measurements. Despite of the averaging, the beats in streak-camera signal persist over more than 1 ns, which means that after each individual pulse they last significantly longer.

Another important remark concerns the role of an elliptical trap. In an ideal circular trap, quantum beats between
p
-orbitals would not cause any intensity oscillations. It is the ellipticity of the confining potential which makes the interference between
p_{x}
- and
p_{y}
-orbitals manifest itself in real-space quantum beats. The magnitude of intensity oscillations detected by streak-camera is dependent on the overlap of
p_{x}
and
p_{y}
orbitals as we discussed above.

We demonstrate the spatial distribution of the polariton density obtained experimentally and numerically at different moments of time within one such period in figure
2
. Remarkably, we clearly observe many periods of the real-space quantum beats of a macroscopic many-body wavefunction of a condensate composed by about 10 thousand exciton-polaritons. It should be noted that in order to maximize the magnitude of intensity oscillations detected by streak-campera we use a sequence of 8 control pulses incident to the same spot at equal time intervals, as dashed lines in figure
2
(b) show. A simple increase of the light power of one pulse does not allow us to obtain a large oscillation amplitude, since the initialization pulse does not create the condensate in the trap, but only disturbs one created by the constant pump laser. Moreover, the perturbation caused by the control pulse must not exceed the splitting between the dipole states in the trap for the two-level model to remain valid. This critical perturbation corresponds to the pump power of about
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro W}
, in our case. To overcome this limitation, we used a train of pulses as a control beam. The repetition frequency in this sequence of pulses was chosen equal to the condensate oscillation frequency. As a result, a parametric resonance of the oscillation amplitude was observed (see Supplementary Material, Figure S9). An increase in the amplitude of oscillations with an increase in the number of pulses in the sequence takes place until
NT<\tau
, where
N
is the number of pulses,
T
is the pulse repetition period,
\tau
is the decay time of quantum beats. In our case, the largest number of pulses that satisfied this condition is
N=
8. The sequence of eight pulses is generated with use of an optical set-up described in the Supplemental Materials.

We find a very good agreement of the measurements with predictions of a two-level quasi-analytical model. In particular, the period of oscillations varies in time in full agreement with the theoretical calculation that considers the effect of a control pulse as a time-dependent perturbation. After reaching the stationary oscillation regime, we observe pronounced intensity beats which may be fitted by an harmonic function:
a(x,y)\sin{[\omega t + \phi_0(x,y)]}
at each spatial point. This confirms that the dynamics of the system can be described in terms of the beats between two discrete quantum states of a polariton condensate in the trap.

5
Mapping to the Bloch sphere

It is instructive to map the observed quantum beats to a Bloch sphere. We do it by fitting the time-resolved tomography images recorded by the streak-camera with use of a two-level model introduced above. The oscillatory dynamics of the system manifests itself in the precession of its quantum state on the surface of the Bloch sphere as panel (f) in figure
3
shows. The two-level model captures the main features of the experiment and it correctly predicts the system evolution triggered by control pulses.

It is important to emphasize also that the observed beats do not exhibit a characteristic phenomenology of a limit cycle which is commonly encountered in nonlinear systems.

In particular, the dynamics of the studied quantum system on a Bloch sphere does not seem to be affected by any attractor. The system is not tending towards a specific point or a region on the Bloch sphere as time goes.

The frequency of the oscillations is mainly dependent on the splitting of
p_{x}
and
p_{y}
orbitals of a polariton condensate confined in the elliptical trap. The system is highly controllable, as its state can be switched to any other point on a Bloch sphere by action of a control pulse (a sequence of control pulses) as we show in the next section.

6
Hadamard and Pauli operations

The poles of the Bloch sphere may be considered as the computational basis states
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
. Once a polariton condensate is placed in a superposition of two eigen states of a trap, it may be considered as a qubit. Using non-resonant optical pulses focused at different locations of the trap we have been able to change the state of the trapped condensate. These changes manifest themselves in changing frequency, amplitude and phase of the quantum beats observed in the streak-camera images of the condensate. Mapping the state of the condensate to the Bloch sphere we observed how the studied quantum system moves from a pole of the sphere to its equatorial plane (figure
3
(f,g)). This constituted an example of the Hadamard operation given by:

The time-dependent potential created by 8 control pulses perturbed the system sufficiently to move its quantum state on the surface of the Bloch sphere.
In order to implement the high fidelity Hadamard operation, we have measured the dependence of the amplitude of the beats induced by the sequence of control pulses on the pump power and compared it with the theoretical prediction (Fig. 3 (c)). The black line in Fig. 3(c) shows the predicted fidelity of the Hadamard operation for a pulse centered at the red spot in the panel (e).
Carefully choosing the spot hit by the sequence of control pulses we have been able to achieve a fidelity of the Hadamard operation of over 0.9. The theoretical estimate for the fidelity is much higher, about 0.9999, as one can see from the calculated fidelity map shown in figure

We note that the experimental dynamics of the polariton qubit appears to be somewhat different from the theoretical prediction as the panel 3(d) illustrates. While the model predicts symmetric real space oscillations of the axis of the condensate wave-function with respect to its initial position, in the experiment these oscillations are highly asymmetric. Most likely, this asymmetry comes from the asymmetry of the effective potential acting upon the polariton condensate, that is constituted by optically induced permanent and time-dependent components as well as the built-in anisotropy of the sample. It can be reproduced theoretically by adding a phenomenological decay term to the model, as we show in the Supplementary Material.

We conclude that the superposition state of a polariton condensate in a trap may preserve its spatial coherence over sufficiently long times to enable one performing logic operations. The trapped condensate can be efficiently controlled by short laser pulses. The read-out scheme developed here allows for full quantum tomography measurements of the presumed qubit. On the other hand, it remains to be verified if an entangled state of two such qubits can be created. The latter is crucial for the implementation of two-qubit gates and further scaling up of polartion quantum networks. We anticipate a new twist in the development of quantum polaritonics once the violation of the Bell inequality for a couple of polariton condensates is demonstrated (see also the section "Polariton vs superconducting qubits" in the Supplementary Material).

7
Discussion

The mechanisms of phase locking between different quantum states of polariton condensates are yet to be revealed. Most likely, an interplay of stimulated scattering, radiative decay and ballistic propagation of polaritons in the plane of a microcavity sample is responsible for the build-up of long-range coherence of polariton condensates in the real space. At the initial stages of this build-up the density of polaritons in the system must be quite low. Polariton-polariton interactions aren’t expected to play an important role in this regime. The decoherence caused by acoustic phonon scattering is likely to intervene at later stages. The very first polaritons are expected to be born in random superpositions of the system eigen states. Once created, a polariton may stimulate scattering of other polaritons to the same quantum state. The efficiency of stimulated scattering depends on the spatial overlap between the wavefunction of the first polariton and the exciton reservoir. Quantum states characterised by the highest overlap with the reservoir must have the best chances to grow polariton condensates. Most likely, at the first stages of their formation, polariton condensates share properties of the first polaritons that play roles of their seeds. Thus, the condensates start growing in superposition states. Dephasing that occurs much later, may randomise the phase of a condensate as a whole and also it may affect the spatial distribution of polaritons. The latter seems to be much less likely than the former though. In our experiments, streak-camera averages the emission intensity collected after millions of pulses. Still, we are able to observe the intensity beats whose amplitude and phase are characterised by well defined patterns. This indicates that every initial pulse brings the condensate to essentially the same superposition of
p_{x}
- and
p_{y}
-orbitals. The uncertainty in the initial phase of the condensate wave-functions after each initialization may not affect the spatial distribution of the condensate density detected by the streak-camera. The phase locking of
p_{x}
- and
p_{y}
-orbitals imposed by the control pulse survives much longer than the phase of the condensate as a whole. This conclusion is consistent with the recent experiments on persistent currents of exciton-polaritons in a ring geometry
Yao et al. 2023
.

8
Conclusions

Surprisingly, bosonic condensates of exciton-polaritons which receive and lose particles every femtosecond demonstrate quantum beats persisting on a nano-second timescale. The measurements of quantum beats of a polariton condensate in an elliptical trap make us conclude that the spatial coherence of a polariton condensate survives orders of magnitude longer than its temporal coherence. This remarkable robustness of the spatial coherence is characteristic of polariton superfluids, in general. Besides that, the spatial distribution of phase in polariton condensates maybe stabilised due to the stimulated scattering of incoherent polaritons to a single quantum state occupied by the condensate. Further studies are needed to check if non-classical (entangled) states of polariton condensates in coupled traps may be generated, and, if yes, for how long these non-classical states would survive. The answer to this crucial question will determine the future of polaritonics as a platform for quantum computing.
