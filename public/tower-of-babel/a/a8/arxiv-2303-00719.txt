Gapped boundary of (4+1)d beyond-cohomology bosonic SPT phase
Xinping Yang, Meng Cheng

Abstract
In this work we study gapped boundary states of ℤ N \mathbb{Z}_{N} bosonic symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases in (4+1)d, which are characterized by mixed ℤ N \mathbb{Z}_{N} -gravity response, and the closely related phases protected by C N C_{N} rotation symmetry. We show that if N ∉ { 2 , 4 , 8 , 16 } N\notin\{2,4,8,16\} , any symmetry-preserving boundary theory is necessarily gapless for the root SPT state. We then propose a (3+1)d ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} gauge theory coupled to fermionic matter as a candidate boundary theory for N = 2 , 4 , 8 , 16 N=2,4,8,16 , where the anomalous symmetry is implemented by invertible topological defects obtained from gauging (2+1)d chiral topological superconductors. For the C N C_{N} case, we present an explicit construction for the boundary states for N = 2 , 4 , 8 , 16 N=2,4,8,16 , and argue that the construction fails for other values of N N .



I
Introduction

The concept of bulk-boundary correspondence is fundamental to the theory of topological phases. It is most well-understood when the bulk is an invertible symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phase, where the boundary has an ’t Hooft anomaly of the symmetry group that protects the bulk SPT phase
Chen et al. 2013
. The presence of a ’t Hooft anomaly puts nontrivial constraints on the low-energy dynamics, and in particular excludes a trivially gapped symmetric ground state. More generally, any low-energy theory realized in the system must have the given anomaly. Generally, three options are possible for the boundary theory: gapless, symmetry breaking or a symmetry-preserving topologically ordered phase (when the boundary has spatial dimension
D
greater than 1).

The last possibility, namely the boundary forming a symmetry-enriched topological (SET) phase
Vishwanath and Senthil 2013
;
Chen et al. 2015
with the ’t Hooft anomaly, has been extensively investigated in the past few years. In particular for
D=2
, general theories of SET phases in both bosonic and fermionic systems have been formulated
Barkeshli et al. 2019
;
Teo et al. 2015
;
Tarantino et al. 2016
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2022a
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2022b
;
Aasen et al. 2021
. Systematic methods to compute ’t Hooft anomalies given a SET phase have been given. It is also known that certain ’t Hooft anomalies can not be matched by any SET in
D=2
, thus any symmetry-preserving theory must be gapless. Known examples of ‘‘symmetry-enforced gaplessness” involve continuous and anti-unitary symmetry group
1
1

1

There are also examples involving finite higher-form symmetries, see e.g. [
Cordova and Ohmori 2019
].

\mathrm{SO}(5)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}
symmetry
Wang et al. 2017
;
Geraedts et al. 2017
.

The focus of this work is
D=3
, where a full theory of SET phases is not available yet. A necessary ingredient of such a theory is a complete understanding of the structure of ’t Hooft anomaly. It is well-known that ’t Hooft anomalies are classified by SPT phases in one dimension higher. Interestingly, in (4+1)d there is a class of bosonic SPT phases protected by unitary symmetry, that goes beyond the well-known “group-cohomology” classification. Such “beyond-cohomology” SPT phases can be understood as decorating lower-dimensional invertible topological phases on symmetry defects. The physical characterization of these beyond-cohomology SPT phases turns out to be rather subtle. An argument based on topological quantum field theory (TQFT) consideration suggests that for
N=2
, the nontrivial phase can be characterized by the ground state having an odd
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

Fidkowski et al. 2019

We will study the boundary theory of the root
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

N=2,4,8,16

\mathbb{Z}_{N}

II.1
Defect decoration

To see how to construct the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
BC SPT phase, it would be instructive to start from
G=\mathrm{U}(1)
and then break it down to
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. The group-cohomology SPT phases are classified by
\mathcal{H}^{5}[\mathrm{U}(1),\mathrm{U}(1)]=\mathbb{Z}
, which can be characterized by the (4+1)d quantum Hall response. The beyond-cohomology phases (
\mathcal{H}^{2}[\mathrm{U}(1),\mathbb{Z}]=\mathcal{H}^{1}[\mathrm{U}(1),\mathrm{U}(1)]=\mathbb{Z}
) can be constructed as follows: suppose the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry is spontaneously broken so the system is in a superfluid phase. In a (4+1)d superfluid, the vortices are codimension-2 domain walls (i.e. spatially they are surfaces). It is well-known that by proliferating the vortices one can restore the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry and enter an insulating state. To create a nontrivial BC SPT state, the vortices are decorated by (2+1)d invertible states. Once the U(1) symmetry is broken down to
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, a U(1) vortex should be viewed as a junction fusing
N
fundamental
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
domain walls together.

The “beyond-cohomology” SPT phases can thus be understood as decorating (2+1)d nontrivial invertible states on junctions of symmetry defects
Chen et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2021
. Recall that invertible phases in (2+1)d form a
\mathbb{Z}
group, generated by the so-called
E_{8}
state with chiral central charge
c_{-}=8
. It has the simplest edge theory being a chiral
(E_{8})_{1}
conformal field theory (CFT). We then consider decorating the codimension-2 tri-junctions of symmetry defects, which are surfaces in 4D space, with invertible states. A tri-junction is labeled by a pair of group elements
(\mathbf{g,h})\in G
. Fusing this pair of defects gives a
\mathbf{gh}
domain wall in
G

n(\mathbf{g,h})\in\mathbb{Z}
, evaluated at each tri-junction. Physically
n(\mathbf{g,h})=c_{-}/8
is the
E_{8}
state decorated on the tri-junctions. In order to get a short-range entangled state, it is necessary that the decoration patterns on defect configurations which can be locally deformed to each other are (adiabatically) equivalent. This requirement is equivalent to imposing the 2-cocycle condition on
n(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
when we consider a junction that fuses three symmetry defects
\mathbf{g,h,k}
into
\mathbf{ghk}
. In addition, the following procedure does not change the underlying phase of matter: we create a pair of invertible states labeled by
m(\mathbf{g})
and

\mathbf{g}

n(\mathbf{g,h})\rightarrow n(\mathbf{g,h})+m(\mathbf{g})+m(\mathbf{h})-m(\mathbf{gh})

is a 2-cocycle defined up to 1-coboundary and its classification is given by
\mathcal{H}^{2}[G,\mathbb{Z}]

Mathemtically, we can understand such defect decoration by looking at the explicit expression of the 2-cocycle. For
G=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, a 2-cocycle in
\mathcal{H}^{2}[\mathbb{Z}_{N},\mathbb{Z}]
can be written in this canonical form

n(a,b)=\frac{s}{N}(a+b-[a+b]_{N}),

(1)

where

denote the elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

takes values in
\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}

N

N

A key question here is what (2+1)d invertible states can be decorated consistently on the
\mathrm{U}(1)

While we do not have a direct way to check the consistency at the level of a wavefunction, it is useful to consider the following QFT arugment: assuming that the system can be described by a relativistic field theory, then we can study the theory on a general curved manifold (in Euclidean spacetime) and its response to background

(2)

III
Bosonic
C_{N}
SPT in (4+1)d

A drawback of our discussions of the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
BC SPT phases is that it is entirely based on topological partition functions, and at the moment we do not have a concrete microscopic model for them. In this section we turn to a different but closely related symmetry
C_{N}

N

\mathbb{Z}_{N}
SPT phases with

SPT phases because there is a one-to-one correspondence between these two SPT phases, known as the crystalline correspondence principle

Let us elaborate on the relation between the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
SPT phases and the
C_{N}
ones. Starting from a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
SPT phase, we create a symmetry-breaking state in the following way: insert
N

\mathbb{Z}_{N}
domain walls in a
C_{N}
symmetric configuration.
This system breaks
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
and
C_{N}

C_{N}^{\prime}

N
domain walls fuse together to a codimension-2 defect, which is the state that lives at the rotation center. Therefore, from a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
SPT state we can always construct a
C_{N}
SPT state. For the other direction, consider a continuum QFT with continuous spatial symmetry (i.e. SO(
D

D
is the spatial dimension). The
C_{N}
rotation can always be written as a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
internal symmetry transformation combined with the corresponding rotation in SO
(D)

\mathbb{Z}_{N}
symmetry is in the corresponding
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
SPT phase.

III.1
Block construction for
C_{N}
SPT

Let us carry out the block construction for
C_{N}

C_{N}

\mathbb{Z}_{N}
internal symmetry on the rotation axis. Now there are two possibilities: a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

\mathbb{Z}_{N}

\mathbb{Z}_{N}
beyond-cohomology SPT phases. In Appendix

we compute the group structure of the

, and
\mathbb{Z}_{3N}\times\mathbb{Z}_{N/3}
for
3\mid N

SPT, the rotation center can be decorated with any (2+1)d invertible topological phase. Unlike the internal

III.2
Gapped boundary conditions

Below we will study this problem using the mathematical framework of modular tensor category (MTC), also known as the anyon theory in physics literature
Kitaev 2006
. In this formalism, a topologically ordered phase in (2+1)d is fully described in terms of the universal data of the low-energy quasi-particle excitations, i.e. the anyons. The universal data describe the fusion and braiding properties of the anyons. Alternatively, this collection of data also suffices to specify the (2+1)d TQFT associated with the topological phase. We should note that the MTC description does not fully determine the edge property, i.e. the chiral central charge
c_{-}
. In fact, it can be shown that the MTC (or the anyon theory) determines
c_{-}
mod 8. Physically the ambiguity precisely comes from stacking
E_{8}
states in the bulk, which does not affect the anyon excitations but can change edge
c_{-}
by integer multiples of 8.

Gapped boundaries of a topological phase can also be described in this formalism
Kitaev and Kong 2012
;
Eliens et al. 2014
;
Levin 2013
;
Kong 2014
;
Lan et al. 2015
;
Neupert et al. 2016
;
Cong et al. 2017
. Each gapped boundary is associated to a unique (composite) anyon object, which determines which anyons can condense on the boundary. This object is called the Lagrangian algebra, denoted by
\mathcal{A}
below.
For Abelian anyons, the condensed anyons form a Lagrangian subgroup
Levin 2013
. In this case, it is relatively simple to state the condition for a set of anyons to condense: they must all be bosons, and the mutual braiding statistics between them must all be trivial. In addition, the number of anyons in the Lagrangian group must be the square root of the total number of anyons. The definition of the algebra in the general case is reviewed in Appendix
E
.

We can now define the notion of Witt equivalence for topological phases. Two topological phases
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are Witt equivalent, if
\mathcal{B}_{1}\boxtimes\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
has a fully gapped interface to an invertible state (i.e. some copies of
E_{8}
states). Here

\boxtimes
denotes the operation of stacking two systems, and
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
is the mirror image of
\mathcal{B}_{2}
. In other words, there is a gapped interface between
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
as long as we are allowed to freely stack copies of
E_{8}
states. Mathematically, two MTCs
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are Witt equivalent if
\mathcal{B}_{1}\boxtimes\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
has a Lagrangian algebra. The Witt equivalence classes of MTCs form an Abelian group, known as the Witt group of MTCs.

With these definitions, the first two conditions of
N
-gappability implies that the MTC
\mathcal{B}
has a minimal order
N
in the Witt group. However, it is also known that the structure of the Witt group is highly constrained
Drinfeld et al. 2010
;
Davydov et al. 2010
;
Davydov et al. 2011
: the order of elements in the Witt group can not be any odd integer. In fact, the only possible finite values are
2^{n}
with
1\leq n\leq 5
(there are obviously elements of infinite order). From this point of view, we can immediately rule out any odd
N\geq 3
in the construction.

In our problem there is also a global symmetry
G
, i.e. the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
group of cyclic permutations of the
N
layers, and the gapped boundary must preserve this global symmetry. Therefore it is necessary to incorporate global symmetry in the categorical formalism. First of all, the global symmetry can act on the anyons in nontrivial ways. In this case, the action is given by the layer permutation. There are more subtle aspects of symmetry actions in the topological phase but they do not occur in our system, so we will not go into details. For more details on symmetry-enriched topological phases in (2+1)d, see Ref. [
Barkeshli et al. 2019
].

Next, we will need to understand whether a given gapped boundary, or the Lagrangian algebra, can preserve the global symmetry. Clearly, the set of condensed anyons must be invariant under the global symmetry (layer permutations in our case), otherwise the symmetry is explicitly broken. For a condensed anyon
a
, we define
G_{a}
as the subgroup of
G
that keeps
a
invariant. Then the condensed anyon
a
should carry a well-defined charge under the symmetry group
G_{a}
. Here by “charge” we mean a one-dimensional representation of
G_{a}
, i.e. a homomorphism from
G_{a}
to U(1). Different choices of these charges correspond to different types of symmetry-preserving gapped boundary conditions. Again we note that here our description is heuristic, and a more precise formulation is given in Appendix
E
.

To understand the physical consequence of the symmetry charges of the condensed anyons, it is useful to consider the nature of the gapped boundary. By definition, the gapped boundary is the interface between the topological phase and an invertible state, which is often taken to be the vacuum. However, in the presence of global symmetry, the invertible state could be a nontrivial SPT state. The nature of this invertible state is determined by the symmetry charges of the condensate
Cheng and Williamson 2020
;
Qi et al. 2019
. Here we are mainly interested in the case where the invertible state has no nontrivial SPT order. Following Ref. [
Qi et al. 2019
], we study the problem by gauging the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
symmetry in the
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
theory, the result of which is denoted by
[\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}]/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. The interface then becomes one between the gauged theory
[\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}]/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, and a
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory obtained from gauging the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
SPT state (possibly stacked with an
E_{8}
state). After gauging, the Lagrangian algebra in
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
is “lifted” to one in
[\mathcal{B}^{\otimes N}]/\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, however the lifting requires additional data, i.e. the symmetry charges of the condensed anyons. Once the Lagrangian algebra and its lifting are given, one can apply the theory of anyon condensation to determine the nature of the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory after condensation. We leave the details of the derivations in Appendix
F
.

Below we apply this theory to several examples. In particular, we will consider a family of anyon theories known as Kitaev’s 16-fold ways
Kitaev 2006

III.2.2
Spin
(2n+1)_{1}

Let us now turn to Spin
(2n+1)_{1}

(2n+1)_{1}

\mathds{1},\sigma

\sigma\times\sigma=\mathds{1}+\psi
and with a topological twist factor
\theta_{\sigma}=e^{\frac{i\pi(2n+1)}{8}}

We first enumerate all bosons in
[\text{Spin}(2n+1)_{1}]^{\boxtimes 16}
. There are
2^{15}
Abelian bosons, which are bound states with an even number of fermions. There is also a non-Abelian boson
(\sigma,\cdots,\sigma)
, with quantum dimension
(\sqrt{2})^{16}=256

\mathcal{A}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{0}}a+128(\sigma,\sigma,\cdots,\sigma).

(7)

Unfortunately, due to the large multiplicity

, we are not able to obtain an explicit structure of the condensate. Therefore we adopt a different approach here. We condense only the Abelian subset
\mathcal{A}_{0}
of

to obtain a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
toric code (TC) phase. From there, we try to construct a
C_{16}
symmetry-preserving gapped boundary of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\mathcal{A}
in

without breaking the
\mathbb{Z}_{16}
symmetry. This can be easily proven using the anyon condensation theory, see Appendix

We first condense the Abelian subgroup
\mathcal{A}_{0}
of
\mathcal{A}
, resulting in a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
toric code (TC) phase. It is easy to see the only deconfined anyons are the Abelian anyons, and
(\sigma,\dots,\sigma)

of the toric code phase. While

is deconfined, it is invariant under fusion with any of the condensed bosons, so it must split into direct sums of

and
m

In the following denote
g
as the cyclic permutation of the 16 layers. Namely,
g
generates the
\mathbb{Z}_{16}
symmetry group.
The
\mathbb{Z}_{16}
symmetry should be preserved by the condensation, so the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
TC is enriched by the
\mathbb{Z}_{16}
symmetry. Following the general classification
Barkeshli et al. 2019
, first we need to know how the generator
g
permutes anyon types. Given that the permutation must preserve the fusion and braiding properties of anyons, there are two possibilities for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

does not permute, or
g
swaps
e
and
m
. In the latter case, there is no symmetric gapped boundary. This is because the only Lagrangian subgroups for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\mathds{1}+e
and
\mathds{1}+m
and neither of them is invariant under
e\leftrightarrow m
. Therefore, a symmetric gapped boundary requires that
g
does not permute
e
and
m
anyons.

To further determine the action of
g
on anyon
\psi
, we take a slightly different approach. The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
toric code can be obtained by gauging the 2D fermion parity of 16 copies of
p_{x}+ip_{y}
superconductors. In the block construction, we place the 16 copies in a
C_{16}
-invariant configuration, all terminating at the rotation axis. Then we mirror-fold them into a stack of
p_{x}+ip_{y}
superconductors. The
g

e
and
m

for

P=\prod_{i}\gamma_{i}
. After gauging, the fermion parity flux with even and odd local fermion parity (
P=\pm 1
) become the
e
and
m
anyons.

Now under the
g

\gamma_{i}\rightarrow\gamma_{[i+1]_{16}}
, so we find

P=\prod_{i}\gamma_{i}\rightarrow\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{16}\gamma_{1}=-P.

(8)

Therefore, the
g

symmetry after gauging. As a result, there is no symmetric gapped boundary.

However, we can fix this issue by modifying the transformation of the Majorana modes to the following form:

\begin{gathered}\gamma_{i}\rightarrow\gamma_{i+1},i=1,\dots,15\\
\gamma_{16}\rightarrow-\gamma_{1}.\end{gathered}

(9)

Under this transformation, the local parity

remains unchanged. However, it then follows that

acts as
\gamma_{i}\rightarrow-\gamma_{i}
for all

We thus conclude that the
\psi

and

C_{16}
-symmetric gapped boundary of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
toric code phase to an invertible state.

Notice that in the original construction, we have 16 layers of Ising theories, and the
C_{16}
rotation acts as the cyclic permutation. One can show that there is no nontrivial symmetry fractionalization for this symmetry. It is natural to postulate that after condensing
\mathcal{A}_{0}
one is led precisely to the toric code with

on
\psi

C_{16}
-symmetric gapped boundary at the rotation center to an invertible state.

However we are not able to precisely determine the nature of the invertible state besides its chiral central charge
c_{-}=8
. So this (3+1)d fermionic
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory may exist on the boundary of the root
C_{16}
BC SPT state, possibly stacked with another (in-cohomology) SPT state.

We now construct the codimension-1 invertible topological defects that implement the

(equivalent to
\nu

superconductors) into the fermionic theory before gauging, and then couple the system to a

Let us examine how the symmetry acts on various objects in the theory. First we review the low-energy excitations of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory. There are two elementary types of excitations: a fermionic
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
particle, and a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

To see what is going on when a flux loop

\nu

When

During this process, the worldlines of the four intersection points of the link with the domain wall precisely trace out the Hopf link, which correspond to a full braiding between two of them and result in a phase factor
\pm e^{\frac{i\pi\nu}{4}}

\nu\equiv 2

Now we describe the boundary TQFT for

(2n)_{1}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(2n)_{1}

\psi

(2n)_{1}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory. Consequently,

or

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(2n)_{1}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(2n)_{1}

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

V
Discussions and conclusions

In this work we have studied symmetry-preserving gapped boundary states for (4+1)d BC SPT phases protected by
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
and
C_{N}
symmetries. We show that for
N\notin\{2,4,8,16\}
, no such boundary states exist for the root
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
SPT phases. We then propose that for
N=2,4,8,16
a candidate boundary topological order is a fermionic
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory, where the anomalous symmetry is generated by topological superconductor defects. We provide explicit constructions of the boundary theory for
C_{N}
SPT phases for
N=2,4,8,16
.

One immediate question left open from our analysis is the 32-gappability of Spin
(2n+1)_{2n+1}
. Given that the simplest of the series, SU(2)
6
, already has 7 anyon types, it is challenging to classify the Lagrangian algebras in SU(2)
{}_{6}^{\boxtimes 32}
. We conjecture that Spin
(2n+1)_{2n+1}
is not 32-gappable.

An interesting question for future works is to construct possible gapless boundary theories for general
N
, or even the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry group. For
N=2
, a gapless boundary theory was constructed in Ref. [
Huang 2020
].

It will also be interesting to clarify the relation between the bosonic
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
(or
C_{N}
) SPT phases and the fermionic ones. The fermionic phases can be realized by non-interacting fermions and the natural boundary states are Weyl fermions. One can imagine that certain fermionic phases are actually adiabatically connected to a bosonic one with trivial gapped fermions. In fact, this provides a possible route to construct gapless boundary states for many values of
N
if the bosonic phase can be “embedded” into a non-interacting fermionic one. This is the case for all odd
N
, so a possible boundary theory is obtained by gauging fermion parity in a (3+1)d Weyl fermion.

We have discussed the 0-form symmetry in a fermionic
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory in (3+1)d. The full symmetry group includes
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
1-form and 2-form symmetries, and together with the
\mathbb{Z}_{16}
0-form symmetry they are expected to form a 3-group
Barkeshli et al. 2022a
. The 1- and 2-form symmetries and their anomalies are analyzed in Ref. [
Barkeshli et al. 2022b
]. It is important to fully understand the structure and the anomaly of the 3-group. In addition, one can also consider non-invertible defects to get an even richer structure (conjecturally a fusion 3-category).

Appendix C
Witt group of MTCs

In this section we will review the definition of the Witt group, as well as some known facts about it.

First we define the notion of Witt equivalence between two (2+1)d topological phases. Two topological phases
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are Witt equivalent, if
\mathcal{B}_{1}\boxtimes\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
has a fully gapped interface to an invertible state (i.e. some copies of
E_{8}
states). Here
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
is the mirror image of
\mathcal{B}_{2}

\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
as long as we are allowed to freely stack copies of
E_{8}
states. Mathematically, two MTCs
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are Witt equivalent if
\mathcal{B}_{1}\boxtimes\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
is a quantum double (Drinfeld center of some fusion category).

Below we review known results about the torsion subgroup of the Witt group, particularly for Abelian MTCs. We adopt notations in Ref. [
Bonderson 2012
] for MTCs. In particularly,
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(k)}
refers to an Abelian topological order with
N
Abelian anyons labeled by
[a]_{N}

, and

denotes mod
N
. The fusion rules are given by addition:
[a]_{N}\times[b]=[a+b]_{N}
. The topological twist factor
\theta_{[a]_{N}}=e^{i\frac{\pi k}{N}a^{2}}
. Notice that for odd
N
,

must be an integer. While for even
N
,

can be an integer or half-integer, but only half-integer values give modular theories.
\mathbb{Z}_{2n}^{(1)}
represents the topological order of the familiar
\mathrm{U}(1)_{2n}
Chern-Simons theories.

The Witt group of all Abelian MTCs, denoted by
\mathcal{W}_{\text{pt}}
following the notation in Ref. [
Davydov et al. 2011

\mathcal{W}_{\text{pt}}=\bigoplus_{p\text{ prime}}\mathcal{W}_{\text{pt}}(p).

(16)

For each prime

p=2

\mathcal{W}_{\text{pt}}(2)=\mathbb{Z}_{8}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}

p\equiv 1\,(\text{mod }4)

Appendix D
Lattice model realization

Given the subtleties in the TQFT classification of SPT phases, it is highly desirable to find microscopic constructions of the nontrivial states. For the
N=2

Fidkowski et al. 2019
] (see also [

] for an alternative construction, which applies to

3

. The key ingredient in this construction is a quantum cellular automata (QCA), or a locality-preserving unitary, that disentangles the 3-fermion WW state. In addition, the QCA exactly squares to
1
. With such a QCA, a wavefunction of a consistent, equal-weight superposition of decorated domain wall states, as well as a commuting projector parent Hamiltonian, can be written down.

It is not clear whether similar constructions can be generalized to other
N>2
cases. If we simply generalize the construction in [
Fidkowski et al. 2019
], according to the action in (
3
), the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
domain wall is decorated by a (3+1)d gapped state, partition function of which is given by
e^{\frac{2\pi i}{N}\int p_{1}}
. In addition, the disentangling QCA for this state must have order
N
. The constraints on QCAs could be seen from the Witt group of (2+1)d modular tensor categories (MTC), which are mathematical theories describing the universal bulk properties of topological phases. While a complete topological classification of QCAs in (3+1)d is still unknown, there is a growing body of evidences
Haah et al. 2018
;
Haah 2021
;
Shirley et al. 2022
suggesting that they are classified by the Witt group of (2+1)d MTCs. We review the definition of Witt group in Appendix
C
. Conjecturally, a QCA that disentangles a WW model with the input MTC in a nontrivial Witt class is topologically nontrivial. The partition function for the WW model is
e^{\frac{2\pi ic_{-}}{24}\cdot p_{1}}
, where
c_{-}
is the chiral central charge of the input MTC. Assuming that this conjectured classification of QCA is correct, we conclude that in order to generalize the construction to
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, we would need to find a topological phase with chiral central charge
\frac{48}{N}
mod 8 and with order
N
in the Witt group.

However, as already mentioned in Section
III.2
, by the constraint of the order of elements in the Witt group. The only possible finite values for
N
are
2^{n}
with
1\leq n\leq 5
(there are obviously elements of infinite order). This immediately rules out any odd

in the construction.

Even for
N
that divides 48, when there exist order-
N
elements in the Witt group, there is a further constraint. If the MTC has an order
N
element in the Witt group, the corresponding QCA conjecturally is also of order
N
, meaning that
N
-th power of the QCA is a finite-depth local unitary circuit. However, for the construction to work, the
N
-th power needs to be exactly
1
. So far this has only been done for the 3-fermion QCA with

, and to the best of our knowledge, no other known examples of QCA satisfy this property.

]. We extensively use the language of unitary modular tensor category (UMTC) for (2+1)d topological phases. A brief summary of UMTC in this context can be found in the appendix of [
Cheng and Williamson 2020
].

A gapped boundary corresponds to a Lagrangian algebra of the bulk MTC. Physically the Lagrangian algebra indicates which bulk anyons are condensed on the boundary
Kong 2014
;
Levin 2013
.

The mathematical theory of the gapped boundary takes into account the local process of annihilating a condensable anyon
a
on the boundary. Similar to fusion/splitting spaces, we associate a vector space for local operators that annihilate
a
, denoted as
V^{a}
, with basis vector
|a;\mu\rangle
. The dimension of this vector space is the “multiplicity”
n_{a}
of
a
in the Lagrangian algebra. Obviously we must have
n_{1}=1
.

Diagrammatically, the condensation process is represented by an anyon line terminating on a wall representing the boundary. We also attach a label at the termination point which represents the state of the boundary condensation space. When
n_{a}=1
it can be suppressed.

Figure 3:
A diagrammatic representation of the M symbol.

An important property of the algebra is the following “
M
symbol”:

|a;\mu\rangle|b;\nu\rangle=\sum_{c,\lambda}[M^{ab}_{c}]^{\mu\nu}_{\lambda}|c;\lambda\rangle.

(25)

The definition is illustrated diagrammatically in Fig.
3
.

Next we impose consistency conditions on the
M
symbols. We can apply
M
moves to three anyon lines terminating
a,b,c
on the boundary, but in different orders, which leads to a variation of the pentagon equation:

\sum_{e,\sigma}[M^{ab}_{e}]^{\mu\nu}_{\sigma}[M^{ec}_{d}]^{\sigma\lambda}_{\delta}[F^{abc}_{d}]_{ef}=\sum_{\psi}[M^{af}_{d}]^{\mu\psi}_{\delta}[M^{bc}_{f}]^{\nu\lambda}_{\psi}

(26)

The
M
symbols also have gauge degrees of freedom, originating from the basis transformation of the boundary condensation space
V^{a}
:
\widetilde{|a;\mu\rangle}=\Gamma^{a}_{\mu\nu}|a;\nu\rangle
, where
\Gamma^{a}_{\mu\nu}
is a unitary transformation. The
M
symbol becomes

[\tilde{M}^{ab}_{c}]^{\mu\nu}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\Gamma^{a}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\Gamma^{b}_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}[M^{ab}_{c}]^{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}[\Gamma^{c}]_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}^{-1}.

(27)

M
symbols are affected by the gauge transformation of bulk fusion space as well.

It is convenient to fix the gauge for the following symbols:

[M^{1a}_{a}]^{\mu}_{\nu}=[M^{a1}_{a}]^{\mu}_{\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}.

(28)

Braiding puts further constraints on the
M
symbols. Since the anyons condense on the boundary, it should not matter in which order the anyon lines terminate on the boundary.

[M^{ba}_{c}]^{\nu\mu}_{\lambda}R^{ab}_{c}=[M^{ab}_{c}]^{\mu\nu}_{\lambda}.

(29)

There is a similar condition for the inverse braiding.

It was shown in Ref. [
Cong et al. 2017
] that these conditions are equivalent to the mathematical definition of a commutative, connected and separable Frobenius algebra
\mathcal{A}=\bigoplus_{a}n_{a}a
in a braided tensor category, with the algebra morphism
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
precisely given by the
M
symbol.

E.1
Symmetry-preserving condensation

We now give a precise definition of anyon condensation that preserves the global symmetry
Bischoff et al. 2019
. This builds on top of the algebraic theory of (2+1)d symmetry-enriched topological phases, known as the
G
-graded braided tensor category, reviewed in [
Cheng and Williamson 2020
]. For a more complete account see [
Barkeshli et al. 2019
]. A key fact that we will use is that in a topological phase enriched by symmetry group
G
, the symmetry action on anyons is fully specified by the following data:
\rho_{\mathbf{g}},U_{\mathbf{g}}(a,b;c)
and
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})
. Here
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
denotes a permutation of anyon labels, and in the following we write
{}^{\mathbf{g}}a\equiv\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(a)
.
U_{\mathbf{g}}(a,b;c)
are unitary transformations acting on the fusion spaces.
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})
are phase factors that describe projective symmetry transformations on individual anyons.
\rho,U
and
\eta
need to satisfy consistency conditions given in [
Barkeshli et al. 2019
]. Again we refer to [
Barkeshli et al. 2019
] for their definitions and properties. In the present case of the
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
MTC,
\rho
is the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
cyclic permutation, and
U
and
\eta
are both
1
if we use the natural gauge choice where the
F
and
R
symbols of
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
are simply given by the Cartesian products of those of
\mathcal{B}
.

In the following
a,b,c,\dots
denote anyons in the condensate, unless otherwise specified. We assume
n_{a}=1
whenever
a
belongs to the condensate, so we omit the index for the boundary condensation space.
Since the boundary is fully gapped and symmetric, we can posit that for each
\mathbf{g}
there exists at least one
\mathbf{g}
-defect that can be absorbed without creating any additional excitations on the boundary.

Figure 4:
A diagrammatic representation of the
\chi
symbol.

Here
\chi_{a}(\mathbf{g})
is a phase factor. Physically,
\chi_{a}(\mathbf{g})
encodes the
\mathbf{g}
action on the condensed anyon, illustrated in Fig.
4
. When there is more than one condensation channel,
\chi
should be replaced by a unitary transformation acting on the condensation space.

If we slide a vertex which splits a
\mathbf{gh}
-defect to
\mathbf{g}
- and
\mathbf{h}
-defects over a boundary vertex, we find

\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})=\frac{\chi_{a}(\mathbf{gh})}{\chi_{{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}a}(\mathbf{h})\chi_{a}(\mathbf{g})}.

(30)

We can also consider fusion of condensable anyons on a boundary. For
a,b,c
in the condensate, sliding a
\mathbf{g}
line over the diagrammatic equation that defines
M
symbol, one finds

M^{{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}a,{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}b}_{{}^{\bar{\mathbf{g}}}c}U_{\mathbf{g}}(a,b;c)=M^{ab}_{c}\frac{\chi_{a}(\mathbf{g})\chi_{b}(\mathbf{g})}{\chi_{c}(\mathbf{g})}.

(31)

We believe that these two conditions Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
) are sufficient and necessary for the condensation to preserve symmetry. Mathematically,
\chi_{a}(\mathbf{g})
defines an algebraic isomorphism for each
\mathbf{g}
. The consistency conditions guarantee that one has a
G
-equivariant algebra structure on
\mathcal{L}

Bischoff et al. 2019
.

Note that for a
\mathbf{g}
-invariant anyon
a
,
\chi_{a}(\mathbf{g})
can be interpreted as the
\mathbf{g}
charge carried by
a
. In particular, it means that
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})=\frac{\chi_{a}(\mathbf{gh})}{\chi_{a}(\mathbf{g})\chi_{a}(\mathbf{h})}
. Below, in all our examples the bulk has no symmetry fractionalization so we make the canonical choice that
\eta_{a}(\mathbf{g,h})=1
.

E.2
Condensation in Spin
(2n)_{1}
layers

Below we focus on the cases of Spin
(2n)_{1}^{\boxtimes r}
. We will only study the
n=1,2
cases.
n=4
has been treated in Ref. [
Qi et al. 2019
], and the other values of
n
are similar. We start from a few general results that apply to all
n
. As before, anyons are denoted by a
r
-tuple.

Denote the Lagragian subgroup as
\mathcal{A}
for the system, which has two types of group elements. The subgroup
\mathcal{A}_{0}
consists of all bosons
\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0},\dots\}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
made of an even number of fermions while the rest of the group elements are expressed as
\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{a}_{0}
, where
\mathbf{v}
is the fermion parity flux.

We observe that
F^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{c}_{0}}=1
. So
M^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}
forms a 2-cocycle over
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. We will postulate that
M^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}=1
in our solutions.

To simplify Eqn.
26
, we make the following gauge choices:
By using the
\Gamma^{\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{a}_{0}}
gauge freedoms, we set
M^{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{a}_{0}}=1
. Then using
\Gamma^{\mathbf{v}}
we set
M^{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}=1
. From the consistency equations we find the following expressions:

\begin{split}M^{\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}&=F^{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}},\\
M^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}_{0}}&=\frac{M^{\mathbf{a}_{0}+\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{v}}}{M^{\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{v}}},\\
M^{\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}_{0}}&=M^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}}.\end{split}

(32)

Now
M^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}}
has to satisfy

M^{2\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{a}_{0}+\mathbf{b}_{0}+\mathbf{c}_{0},\mathbf{v}}M^{\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{v}}F^{\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{c}_{0}}=\\
M^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}}M^{\mathbf{c}_{0},\mathbf{v}}F^{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{a}_{0},2\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{b}_{0}+\mathbf{c}_{0}}.

(33)

We can write a general solution of the consistency equations
M^{\mathbf{a,b}}=M_{0}^{\mathbf{a,b}}\omega^{\mathbf{a,b}}
, consisting of a special solution
M_{0}^{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
of Eqn.
33
and a group 2-cocycle
\omega^{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
over
\mathcal{A}
. Then
\omega
will be fixed through Eqn.
29
.

E.3
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
-symmetric Lagrangian algebra of Spin
(4)_{1}^{\boxtimes 4}

We denote the anyons in Spin
(4)_{1}^{\boxtimes 4}
by
\mathbf{a}=(\vec{a}_{1},\vec{a}_{2},\vec{a}_{3},\vec{a}_{4})
, where
\vec{a}_{i}=(a_{i}^{1}\;a_{i}^{2})
are defined mod 2. The F and R symbols are given by

\begin{split}F^{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}}&=\exp\left(\frac{i\pi}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\vec{a}_{i}\cdot(\vec{b}_{i}+\vec{c}_{i}-[\vec{b}_{i}+\vec{c}_{i}]_{2})\right),\\
R^{\mathbf{a,b}}&=\exp\left[\frac{i\pi}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\vec{a}_{i}\cdot\vec{b}_{i}\right].\end{split}

(34)

The
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
symmetry generator
\mathbf{g}
acts on the anyons in the following way:

\rho:(\vec{a}_{1},\vec{a}_{2},\vec{a}_{3},\vec{a}_{4})\rightarrow(\vec{a}_{2},\vec{a}_{3},\vec{a}_{4},\vec{a}_{1}).

(35)

It is obvious that F and R symbols are invariant under
\rho
:

F^{\rho(\mathbf{a}),\rho(\mathbf{b}),\rho(\mathbf{c})}=F^{\mathbf{a,b,c}},R^{\rho(\mathbf{a}),\rho(\mathbf{b})}=R^{\mathbf{a,b}}.

(36)

Therefore the corresponding
U
symbols are all 1. We can then set all the
\eta
symbols to 1 as well.

It will also be convenient to pick a set of generators for the Lagrangian subgroup. We will use the following set:

\begin{split}&\mathbf{v}_{1}=(1\;0,1\;0,1\;0,1\;0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{2}=(1\;1,1\;1,0\;0,0\;0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{3}=(0\;0,1\;1,1\;1,0\;0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{4}=(0\;0,0\;0,1\;1,1\;1)\end{split}

(37)

Together they form a
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4}
group. Then any group element
\mathbf{a}
can be expanded in terms of the generators:
\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\tilde{a}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
.

Following the procedure in Section
E.2
, we find the special solution
M_{0}^{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
:

\begin{split}&M_{0}^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}}=e^{\frac{i\pi}{2}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{0}},\end{split}

(38)

and the group 2-cocycle
\omega^{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
:

\omega^{\mathbf{a,b}}=e^{i\pi(\tilde{a}_{2}\tilde{b}_{3}+\tilde{a}_{3}\tilde{b}_{4})}.

(39)

We find the following solution of
\chi
:

\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{g})=e^{i\pi(1-\tilde{a}_{4})(\tilde{a}_{2}+\tilde{a}_{3})}.

(40)

and

\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{g}^{2})=e^{i\pi[(1-\tilde{a}_{4})\tilde{a}_{3}+(1-\tilde{a}_{3})\tilde{a}_{2}]}.

(41)

One can easily verify that all
\mathbf{g}
-invariant anyons have
\chi=1
. The same is true for all
\mathbf{g}^{2}
-invariant anyons.

E.4
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
-symmetric Lagrangian algebra of
\mathrm{U}(1)_{4}^{\boxtimes 8}

We denote the anyons in
\mathrm{U}(1)_{4}^{\boxtimes 8}
by a 8-tuple
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{8})
, where
a_{i}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
are defined mod 4. Since the theory is Abelian, fusion will be denoted additively. We will use short-hand notations for the F and R symbols
(F^{\mathbf{a,b,c}}_{\mathbf{a+b+c}})_{\mathbf{a+b,b+c}}\equiv F^{\mathbf{a,b,c}},R^{\mathbf{a,b}}_{\mathbf{a+b}}=R^{\mathbf{a,b}}
. They are given by

\begin{split}F^{\mathbf{a,b,c}}&=\exp\left(i\pi\mathbf{a}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{b+c-[b+c]_{4}}}{4}\right),\\
R^{\mathbf{a,b}}&=\exp\left(\frac{i\pi}{4}\mathbf{a\cdot b}\right).\end{split}

(42)

The
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
symmetry generator
\mathbf{g}
acts on the anyons in the following way:

\rho:(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{8})\rightarrow(a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{1}).

(43)

It is obvious that F and R symbols are invariant under
\rho
:

F^{\rho(\mathbf{a}),\rho(\mathbf{b}),\rho(\mathbf{c})}=F^{\mathbf{a,b,c}},R^{\rho(\mathbf{a}),\rho(\mathbf{b})}=R^{a,b}.

(44)

Therefore the corresponding
U
symbols are all 1. We can then set all the
\eta
symbols to 1 as well.

The Lagrangian subgroup is generated by the following anyons:

\begin{split}&\mathbf{v}_{1}=(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{2}=(2,2,0,0,0,0,0,0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{3}=(0,2,2,0,0,0,0,0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{4}=(0,0,2,2,0,0,0,0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{5}=(0,0,0,2,2,0,0,0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{6}=(0,0,0,0,2,2,0,0)\\
&\mathbf{v}_{7}=(0,0,0,0,0,2,2,0).\end{split}

(45)

Together they generate a
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}\times\mathbb{Z}_{4}
group. We find the solution to be

\begin{split}&M_{0}^{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{v}}=e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{0}},\\
\end{split}

(46)

and

\omega^{\mathbf{a,b}}=e^{i\pi(\tilde{a}_{2}\tilde{b}_{3}+\tilde{a}_{3}\tilde{b}_{4}+\tilde{a}_{4}\tilde{b}_{5}+\tilde{a}_{5}\tilde{b}_{6}+\tilde{a}_{6}\tilde{b}_{7})}.

(47)

The solution for
\chi
is given by

\chi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{g})=e^{i\pi(1-\tilde{a}_{7})(\tilde{a}_{2}+\tilde{a}_{4}+\tilde{a}_{6})}.

(48)

In this case, the only
\mathbf{g}
-invariant anyons are generated by
\mathbf{v}_{1}
, so obviously
\chi_{\mathbf{v}_{1}}(\mathbf{g})=1
. The same is true for other symmetry transformations.

Appendix F
Gauging
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
cyclic permutation symmetry

We describe the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauging of
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
, where the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
generator
g
acts as

(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{N})\rightarrow(a_{N},a_{1},\cdots,a_{N-1}).

(49)

We will denote the gauged theory by
\mathcal{U}
. Anyons in
\mathcal{U}
will be labeled by
(x_{g^{r}},\chi)
, where
g^{r}
is the symmetry flux, and
\chi
is an irreducible representation of the stabilizer group. Here since
G
is Abelian we can view
\chi
as a group homomorphism from the stabilizer group to
\mathrm{U}(1)
. For example, if
r=0
, then
x
runs through all orbits of anyons
[\mathbf{b}]
in
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
under
G
, where
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{N})
is a representative element of the orbit. Its stabilizer group, i.e. the subgroup of
G
that keeps the anyon
\mathbf{b}
invariant, is denoted by
G_{\mathbf{b}}
. When the stabilizer group is strictly smaller than
G
, we call
([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{\mathbf{b}})
a superposition anyon.
In general, the label
x
can actually be chosen as an element in
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes(N,r)}
.

Among all
(x_{g^{r}},\chi)
there is one with the minimal quantum dimension, which we will call
(\mathds{1}_{g^{r}},\chi)
(for
r=0
,
(\mathds{1}_{1},1)
is the identity anyon). We have the following fusion rule:

(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{(N,r)},\mathds{1},\cdots,\mathds{1})\times(\mathds{1}_{g},\chi)=(\mathbf{a}_{g},\chi).

(50)

Here
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{(N,r)})
. Therefore
d_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi)}=d_{\mathbf{a}}d_{(\mathds{1}_{g^{r}},1)}
.

To compute
d_{(\mathds{1}_{g^{r}},1)}
, we use the fact that the total quantum dimension squared of all
g^{r}
defects must be equal to
D_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}

Barkeshli et al. 2019
. That is,

\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes(N,r)}}d_{\mathbf{a}}^{2}d_{\mathds{1}_{g^{r}}}^{2}=D_{\mathcal{B}}^{2(N,r)}d_{\mathds{1}_{g^{r}}}^{2}=D_{\mathcal{B}}^{2N}.

Thus
d_{\mathds{1}_{g^{r}}}=D_{\mathcal{B}}^{N-(N,r)}
.

We also have the topological twist factors for the fluxes:

\theta_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi)}=\theta_{\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}}}\chi(g^{r}).

(51)

Here
\theta_{\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}}}
is the topological twist factor of the
\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}}
defect. It can be chosen to be
\theta_{\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}}}=\theta_{\mathbf{a}}^{\frac{(N,r)}{N}}
, but we do not need its value in this section.

Now we consider the S matrix elements between fluxes and anyons. The entries of S matix could be computed from the topological spins and the fusion coefficients:

S_{x,y}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{z}N^{xy}_{z}d_{z}\frac{\theta_{x}\theta_{y}}{\theta_{z}}.

(52)

We will use this to derive an important property of
S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a}),([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})}
, where
[\mathbf{b}]_{1}
denotes a superposition anyon (i.e.
G_{\mathbf{b}}
is smaller than
G
). First we consider the fusion between a superposition anyon
([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})
and a bare charge
(\mathds{1}_{1},\chi)
, where
\chi
is a 1D rep. of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. Following Ref. [
Barkeshli et al. 2019
], we have

([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})\times(\mathds{1}_{1},\chi)=([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b}\cdot\chi|_{G_{\mathbf{b}}}).

(53)

Here
\chi|_{G_{\mathbf{b}}}
is the restriction of
\chi
to
G_{\mathbf{b}}
.

Suppose
\chi|_{G_{\mathbf{b}}}
is trivial. For such a
\chi
, using
53
we get:

([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})\times(\mathds{1}_{1},\chi)=([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b}),

(54)

which implies that:

(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a})\times([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})\times(\mathds{1}_{1},\chi)=(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a})\times([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b}).

(55)

Let us define
{\Lambda}_{b}
as the group of
\chi
’s which restrict to identity on
G_{\mathbf{b}}
. It is easy to see that
\Lambda_{b}
is all the irreps on
G/G_{b}
. Then Eq. (
55
) implies

(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a})\times([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})=\sum_{\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}}}N_{\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},[\mathbf{b}]_{1}}^{\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}}}\sum_{\chi\in\Lambda_{b}}(\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}},\chi_{c}\chi).

(56)

While
N_{\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},[\mathbf{b}]_{1}}^{\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}}}
and
\chi_{c}
are undetermined, they are not needed for our purpose.

Using the definition (
52
), we obtain

\begin{split}&S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a}),([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})}\\
&=\frac{\theta_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a})}\theta_{([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})}}{{D_{\mathcal{U}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}}}N_{\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},[\mathbf{b}]_{1}}^{\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}}}d_{(\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}},\chi_{c})}\sum_{\chi\in\Lambda_{b}}\theta_{(\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}},\chi_{c}\chi)}^{-1}.\end{split}

(57)

Let us consider the sum over
\chi\in\Lambda_{b}
, which can be simplified further to

\begin{split}\sum_{\chi\in\Lambda_{b}}\theta_{(\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}},\chi_{c}\chi)}^{-1}=\theta_{(\mathbf{c}_{g^{r}},\chi_{c})}^{-1}\sum_{\chi\in\Lambda_{b}}\chi^{-1}(g^{r}).\end{split}

(58)

First, we assume
g^{r}\in G_{b}
. By the definition of
\Lambda_{b}
,
\chi(g^{r})=1
so the sum evaluates to
|\Lambda_{b}|
. Then if
g^{r}\notin G_{b}
, the Schur’s orthogonality theorem applied to
G/G_{b}
shows that the sum should be 0. To summarize, we have shown that

S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a}),([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})}\propto\delta_{g^{r}\in G_{\mathbf{b}}}.

(59)

Let us now consider what happens when
g^{r}\in G_{\mathbf{b}}
. It implies that
b
has a period
(N,r)
, i.e. write
b=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{N})
, then
b_{i}=b_{i+(N,r)\text{ mod }N}
. Thus for this caculation, we can group
(N,r)
consecutive layers into one “layer”, with total number of layers
\tilde{N}=\frac{N}{(N,r)}
. It is also convenient to define
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=(b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{(N,r)})\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes(N,r)}
. Therefore, the problem reduces to computing the
S
matrix between a
(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},1)
defect, and
[\mathbf{b}]_{1}=(\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{b}})
. We will account for the charges later.

Now we use a geometric picture to compute the S matrix. A cylinder with a
\mathbf{a}_{{g}^{{r}}}
defect line can be viewed as a
\tilde{N}
-layer cylinder, with a
g^{r}
cyclic permutation branch cut, which is topologically equivalent to a single-“layer” cylinder with the topological order described by
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
. On the cylinder there is a Wilson line of the
\mathbf{a}
anyon. We then compute the eigenvalue of the Wilson loop of
([\mathbf{b}]_{1},1)
along the other non-contractible cycle in two ways. Note that in the alternative picture, the Wilson loop of
([\mathbf{b}_{1},1])
becomes a Wilson loop of
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
. We thus have:

\frac{S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},1),([\mathbf{b}]_{1},1)}}{S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},1),(\mathds{1}_{1},1)}}=\frac{S^{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbf{a}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}}}{S^{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbf{a}\mathds{1}}}

(60)

where
S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},1),(\mathds{1}_{1},1)}=\frac{d_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},1)}}{D_{\mathcal{U}}}
,
S^{\mathcal{B}}_{a\mathds{1}}=\frac{d_{\mathbf{a}}}{D_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}}=\frac{d_{\mathbf{a}}}{D_{\mathcal{B}}^{(N,r)}}
. Using
d_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},1)}=D_{\mathcal{B}}^{N-(N,r)}d_{\mathbf{a}}
and
D_{\mathcal{U}}=ND_{\mathcal{B}}^{N}
, we have:

\begin{split}S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},1),([\mathbf{b}]_{1},1)}&=\frac{d_{\mathbf{a}}D_{B}^{N-(N,r)}}{ND_{\mathcal{B}}^{N}}\frac{D_{\mathcal{B}}^{(N,r)}}{d_{\mathbf{a}}}S^{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbf{a}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{N}S^{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbf{a}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}}.\end{split}

(61)

Including the contribution from the charge
\chi_{b}
, we find

S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(\mathbf{a}_{g^{r}},\chi_{a}),([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})}=\frac{1}{N}\chi_{b}(g^{r})S^{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mathbf{a}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}}\delta_{g^{r}\in G_{\mathbf{b}}}.

(62)

Now we study gapped boundaries of
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
from the gauging perspective. Suppose we have a Lagrangian algebra
\mathcal{A}_{0}
of
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
. It can be “lifted” to a condensable algebra
\mathcal{A}
in the gauged theory
\mathcal{U}
. Namely, we can first break
\mathcal{A}_{0}
into orbits under
G
, and each orbit becomes a single anyon after gauging.

\mathcal{A}_{0}=\sum_{[\mathbf{a}]}n_{\mathbf{a}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in[\mathbf{a}]}\mathbf{b}.

(63)

Here
[\mathbf{a}]
is an orbit under
G
, with
\mathbf{a}
being a representative element. After gauging, it becomes

\mathcal{A}=\sum_{[\mathbf{a}]}n_{\mathbf{a}}([\mathbf{a}]_{1},\chi_{\mathbf{a}}).

(64)

Roughly speaking,
\chi_{\mathbf{a}}
is the symmetry charge carried by the
\mathbf{a}
anyon when it condenses.
The assignment of
\chi_{\mathbf{a}}
is not arbitrary. In fact, because
U
and
\eta
are all
1
in our example, the
\chi_{\mathbf{a}}
’s are precisely those defined in Sec.
E.1
.

By definition, condensing
\mathcal{A}_{0}
in
\mathcal{B}^{\boxtimes N}
results in a trivial theory (the Vec MTC). If the condensation preserves the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
symmetry, condensing
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{U}
should result in a deconfined
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory, denoted by
\mathcal{D}
. In the same convention, anyons in
\mathcal{D}
will be denoted by
(g^{r},\chi)
. The vacuum is
\mathds{1}\equiv(1,1)
. Note that depending on the choice of the charges
\chi_{\mathbf{a}}
’s, the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory may be twisted. We are in particular interested in whether there exists a choice of
\chi
’s such that the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory is not twisted.

We briefly recall a few basic facts about anyon condensation from a MTC
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{D}
. It is useful to think of the anyon condensation as defining a gapped interface between the theories
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{D}
. To fully describe this process, it is also necessary to define the theory of (possibly confined) excitations on the interface, called
\mathcal{T}
.
\mathcal{D}
is the “deconfined” subcategory of
\mathcal{T}
.

The relations between the three theories are encoded in the restriction map
r
and the lifting map
l
.
An anyon
\alpha
in the
\mathcal{U}
theory can be “restricted” to the
\mathcal{T}
theory:

r(\alpha)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}n_{\alpha,t}t.

(65)

where the
n_{\alpha,t}
’s are non-negative integers.
On the other hand, a particle in the
\mathcal{T}
can be lifted back to the
\mathcal{U}
theory:

l(t)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{U}}n_{\alpha,t}\alpha.

(66)

Clearly
l(\mathds{1})
is the Lagrangian algebra
\mathcal{A}
. We are mostly interested in
n_{\alpha,t}
’s when
t\in\mathcal{D}
.

The integers
n_{\alpha,t}
should satisfy various constraints. We will only need the following condition: the restriction/lifting maps should commute with the modular matrices of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{D}
. More specifically,
for
\alpha\in\mathcal{U}
and
t\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{U}}S_{\alpha\beta}n_{\beta,t}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{D}}n_{\alpha,s}S_{st}.

(67)

Secondly, for a given
t\in\mathcal{U}
, all
\alpha
’s with
n_{\alpha,t}\neq 0
share the same topological twist factor and
\theta_{\alpha}=\theta_{t}
.

We will denote

l((g,1))=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{B}}w_{g}(a)(a_{g},1),

(68)

where
w_{g}(a)
is a non-negative integer. We shall use (
67
) to constrain
w_{g}(a)
, which can then determine the topological twist factor of
(g,1)
.

Setting
\alpha=(a_{g},1)
,
t=\mathds{1}
in (
67
), we have the left-hand side given by

\sum_{([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{b})\in\mathcal{A}}S^{\mathcal{U}}_{(a_{g},1),([\mathbf{b}]_{1},\chi_{\mathbf{b}})}n_{\mathbf{b}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{b}=g(\mathbf{b})}\chi_{\mathbf{b}}S_{ab}^{\mathcal{B}}n_{\mathbf{b}}.

(69)

The RHS is
\frac{1}{N}w_{g}(a)
. So we find

\sum_{\mathbf{b}=g(\mathbf{b})}\chi_{\mathbf{b}}S^{\mathcal{B}}_{ab}n_{\mathbf{b}}=w_{g}(a).

(70)

Here
\mathbf{b}=g(\mathbf{b})
means
\mathbf{b}=(b,b,\cdots,b)
.
Similar relations can be derived for other
g^{r}
. Given that the S matrix elements are generally not integers, it is a nontrivial consistency check that the sum on the left-hand side yields a non-negative integer.

Let us now apply the relation to
\mathcal{B}=\text{Spin}(2n)_{1}
theory. In this case,
b
runs over all anyons in
\mathcal{B}
and with
n_{\mathbf{b}}=1
we have

\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}S_{ab}^{\mathcal{B}}\chi_{\mathbf{b}}=w_{g}(a).

(71)

We have shown in that
\chi_{\mathbf{b}}=1
for all
\mathbf{b}=g(\mathbf{b})
is allowed for
n=1,2
and
4
, and in fact for all other values of
n
. With this choice, the unitarity of S matrix implies that
w_{g}(a)=2\delta_{a,\mathds{1}}
. Therefore,
\theta_{(g,1)}=\theta_{(\mathds{1}_{g},1)}=1
and the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory is not twisted.

In the case of the Ising theory with
N=16
, the LHS becomes

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}(1+128\sqrt{2}\chi_{\sigma}+\chi_{\psi})=w_{g}(\mathds{1}),\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-128\sqrt{2}\chi_{\sigma}+\chi_{\psi})=w_{g}(\psi),\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1-\chi_{\psi})=w_{g}(\sigma).\end{split}

(72)

Regardless of the choice of
\chi_{\sigma}
and
\chi_{\psi}
,
w_{g}(\mathds{1})
and
w_{g}(\psi)
can not be an integer, and the only possible integer value for
w_{g}(\sigma)
is 0. We conclude that such a symmetry-preserving condensation in Ising
⊠16
does not exist. This confirms the more heuristic argument in Sec.
III.2.2
.
