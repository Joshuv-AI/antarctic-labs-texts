Asymptotics of feature learning in two-layer networks after one gradient-step
Hugo Cui, Luca Pesce, Yatin Dandi, Florent Krzakala, Yue M. Lu, Lenka Zdeborová, Bruno Loureiro

Abstract
In this manuscript, we investigate the problem of how two-layer neural networks learn features from data, and improve over the kernel regime, after being trained with a single gradient descent step. Leveraging the insight from [ 1 ] , we model the trained network by a spiked Random Features (sRF) model. Further building on recent progress on Gaussian universality [ 2 ] , we provide an exact asymptotic description of the generalization error of the sRF in the high-dimensional limit where the number of samples, the width, and the input dimension grow at a proportional rate. The resulting characterization for sRFs also captures closely the learning curves of the original network model. This enables us to understand how adapting to the data is crucial for the network to efficiently learn non-linear functions in the direction of the gradient – where at initialization it can only express linear functions in this regime.

Introduction

A common deep learning intuition behind the unreasonable effectiveness of neural networks is their capacity to effectively adapt to the training data which makes them superior to kernel methods. While kernel methods and their finite width approximations are known to be data-hungry (see e.g.
[
3
]

Their adaptivity and capacity to learn features from data are behind the success in efficiently solving problems from image classification to text generation. A large part of our current theoretical understanding of neural networks stems from the investigation of their lazy regime where features are

learned during training. This includes a set of works that fall under the umbrella of Gaussian processes

[

and the Lazy regime
[

Different theoretical works have offered sharp separation results between kernel and feature learning regimes (see e.g.

,

,

In this paper, we follow this line of work and provide an exact high-dimensional description of the test error achieved by a sRF, which we use to model a two-layer network after a
single, large, gradient step
. Our work provides a sharp asymptotic treatment of a setting where feature learning is modeled in a non-perturbative, high-dimensional regime, with a model able to express non-linear functions beyond polynomials. This analysis quantitatively illustrates the benefits of feature learning over the lazy regime.

Our
main contributions

•

Exact asymptotics for sRF –
We provide a sharp asymptotic characterization of the test error, alongside a set of summary statistics, for a sRF. We discuss analytically, and provide numerical support, why sRFs constitute good approximations for two-layer neural networks with first layer weights trained with a large learning rate gradient step. The derivation leverages the (non-rigorous) replica method from statistical physics

Feature learning –
We provide an extensive discussion on how feature learning leads to a drastic improvement in the generalization performance over random features in a data-limited regime, demonstrating a clear and quantitative separation with respect to kernel method and random feature models. In particular, we derive both upper and lower bounds on the generalization error and discuss under which conditions they are tight.

3
Main Technical Results

Our main technical results are a tight asymptotic characterization of the test error achieved by sRFs (defined in
6
), which we argue provide good models for two-layer networks trained with a single large gradient step followed by a ridge regression on the readout weights. We provide compelling numerical support that this theoretical characterization captures very closely the learning curves of two-layer networks trained following the protocol (
3
), and that sRF thus provide a valuable analytical playground to understand the learning and behaviour of the latter. These results are enabled through the expression of the parameters of an equivalent sRF model in terms of the training parameters of the original network in subsection
3.1
, which can in turn be mapped to an equivalent Gaussian model in subsection
3.2
. Subsection
3.3
finally states the tight asymptotic characterization of the test error.

3.1
Asymptotics of the first layer weights after one (large) gradient step

The first step is to derive an explicit asymptotic expression for the hidden-layer weights
W^{(1)}
after one (large) gradient step. In the following, we show that the learning problem introduced in Section

Definition 3.1

(sRF model)

We define a

c

\displaystyle g_{F,\bm{a}}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\bm{a}^{\top}\sigma\left(Fx\right)

\displaystyle F=W+r\frac{\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}.

(7)

As discussed above, after a large gradient step, the first layer weights

\displaystyle W^{(1)}=W^{(0)}+\Delta+r\frac{\check{\bm{u}}\check{\bm{v}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}},

\displaystyle c=1+\frac{\tilde{\eta}^{2}\check{h}_{1}^{2}h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}}

\displaystyle h_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{z}[z\sigma(z)],

Conditional Gaussian equivalence

The sharp characterization of the test performance, which we state in Result

Result 3.2

Consider the sRF model with weights

\displaystyle\bm{\varphi}^{g}(\bm{x})\overset{d}{=}\mu_{0}(\kappa)\bm{1}_{p}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)W\bm{x}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{p}),

(14)

\mu_{0}(\kappa),\mu_{1}(\kappa),\mu_{2}(\kappa)
defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(\kappa)=\Ea_{z}{\sigma(z+r\kappa)},

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\kappa)=\frac{1}{c}\Ea_{z}{z\sigma(z+r\kappa)},

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(\kappa)=\sqrt{\Ea_{z}{\sigma^{2}(z+r\kappa)-c(\mu_{1}(\kappa))^{2}-(\mu_{0}(\kappa))^{2}}},

with expectations bearing over

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{a}}_{\lambda}=\underset{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{1}}\left(y^{\mu}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\bm{a}^{\top}\phi(\bm{x})\right)^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\bm{a}\lVert^{2}.

(16)

4.3
What can be learned with a single step?

While training a two-layer network with large gradient steps allows it to escape the linear limitations of, e.g. RF models, it generically only learns the target up to small test error, yet not perfectly. \displaystyle\underset{\lambda\geq 0}{\inf}~\epsilon_{g}\!\geq\!\underset{b_{1},b_{2}}{\inf}~\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\sigma_{\star}(\kappa)-b_{1}\mu_{0}(\kappa)-b_{2}\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\right]^{2}.

4.4
More variability means better feature learning

The discussion of subsection
4.3
thus affords an insightful perspective on the learning of sRF models in terms of approximating the target activation
\sigma_{\star}
in a two-dimensional functional space.
Interestingly, introducing variability in the readout layer at initialization leads to an even richer functional basis, and hence greater expressivity of the network. As we further discuss in Appendix

\bm{a}^{(0)}
is no longer proportional to
\bm{1}_{p}
, but is rather initialized from a distribution over a finite vocabulary

of size

–e.g.

– the equivalent feature map of the associated sRF takes the form:

\displaystyle\bm{\varphi}^{g}(\bm{x})\!\!=\!\!\begin{pmatrix}\scriptstyle\scriptstyle\mu_{0}(u_{1}\kappa)\\
\scriptstyle\mu_{0}(u_{2}\kappa)\\
\vdots\\
\scriptstyle\mu_{0}(u_{p}\kappa)\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\scriptstyle\mu_{1}(u_{1}\kappa)\\
\scriptstyle\mu_{1}(u_{2}\kappa)\\
\vdots\\
\scriptstyle\mu_{1}(u_{p}\kappa)\end{pmatrix}\odot W\bm{x}+\begin{pmatrix}\scriptstyle\mu_{2}(u_{1}\kappa)\\
\scriptstyle\mu_{2}(u_{2}\kappa)\\
\vdots\\
\scriptstyle\mu_{2}(u_{p}\kappa)\end{pmatrix}\odot\bm{\xi}

(31)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication,
\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{p})
, and
\bm{u}\not\propto\bm{1}_{p}
has entries which can now take a finite number of different values. The coefficients of the equivalent map (
31
) are thus neuron-dependent and thus afford a richer functional basis
\{\mu_{0}(\omega\cdot),\tilde{\mu}_{1}(\omega\cdot)\}_{\omega\in V}
, thereby allowing the network to express a larger class of functions. As a matter of fact, the functional space spanned by these functions is generically of dimension
2|V|
for non-uniform readout initializations
\bm{a}^{(0)}
, compared to just
2
in the uniform readout case. A sharp asymptotic characterization of the test error for the case of non-uniform readout initialization with finite vocabulary is further provided in Appendix
D
.

We briefly discuss the limiting case of interest
\lambda,\alpha_{0},\tilde{\eta}\rightarrow\infty
, for which the equivalent feature map
\bm{\varphi}^{g}(\bm{x})
(
31
) reduces to its first term
(\mu_{0}(u_{i}\kappa))_{i=1}^{p}
. Further, observe that
\mu_{0}(u_{i}\kappa)
can be viewed as a one-dimensional neuron acting on the one-dimensional input variable
\kappa
with a random weight

. Standard results on approximation errors for random feature mappings of finite-dimensional inputs

29

imply that a large class of functions can be approximated from the network features
\bm{\varphi}^{g}(\bm{x})
, provided the vocabulary size
|V|
is large enough. Similar random features approximations have been leveraged in
[
1
,
13
,
10
]
. The equivalent feature map (
31
) provides an intuitive picture on how such random feature mappings of low-dimensional inputs can naturally emerge in the setting of the learning of a two-layer network.

Finally, note that the bounds (
26
) and (
27
) can be readily generalised to non-uniform readout initializations, provided one replaces in the discussion of subsection
4.3
the two-dimensional functional basis
\mathrm{span}(\mu_{0},\tilde{\mu}_{1})
in the case of uniform initialization by the richer functional space
\mathrm{span}(\{\mu_{0}(\omega\cdot),\tilde{\mu}_{1}(\omega\cdot)\}_{\omega\in V})
for non-uniform initialization. For instance, the lower bound (

) thus involves in the non-uniform case the distance between the target
\sigma_{\star}
and
\mathrm{span}(\{\mu_{0}(\omega\cdot),\tilde{\mu}_{1}(\omega\cdot)\}_{\omega\in V})
.

Conclusion

We provided a tight asymptotic description of the learning of spiked random features models, which we show provides a quantitative approximation for a two-layer neural network after training its first layer with a large, single, gradient step, in the limit where the number of samples, the hidden layer width, and the input dimension are proportionally large. Our results sharply characterize the corresponding feature map, and how it achieves a test error which non-perturbatively improves over the kernel regime. Crucially, the sRF can efficiently approximate non-linear functions –beyond polynomials– in the direction of the gradient – sizeably improving upon RFs (modeling the network at initialization), which can only express linear functions. We further discuss bounds for the test error and which functions are learnable after a single gradient step. Finally, extensive numerical support is provided to illustrate our findings.

We believe the present work opens exciting research avenues, paving the way towards a tight theoretical understanding of feature learning in gradient-trained networks. Prominent among these research directions is the extension of our results to readout initialization with generic (not necessarily finite) support. to a finite number of gradient steps, and ultimately fully trained networks.

\displaystyle W^{(1)}=W^{(0)}+\Delta+\frac{\check{\bm{u}}\check{\bm{v}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{p}},

\displaystyle\Delta=W^{(0)}+\frac{\eta}{n_{0}\sqrt{p}}\mathrm{diag}(\bm{a}^{(0)})\left(\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{0}}\check{\sigma}(W^{(0)}\bm{x}^{\mu})f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\bm{x}^{\mu}\right).

(35)

We noted
h_{1}
the first Hermite coefficient of the student activation
\sigma

\check{\sigma}(\cdot)=\sigma^{\prime}(\cdot)-h_{1}

A.2

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta h_{1}\bm{a}^{(0)}\right\lVert=d\tilde{\eta}h_{1}.

We now turn to the second term. As a mean of independent vectors, it concentrates in probability. We thus compute the expectation of the squared norm:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{0}}f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\bm{x}^{\mu}\right\lVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}[f_{\star}(\bm{x})^{2}x_{i}^{2}]+\frac{(n_{0}-1)}{n_{0}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{x}[f_{\star}(\bm{x})x_{i}]^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}[f_{\star}(\bm{x})^{2}x_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{z}[\sigma_{\star}(z)^{2}]+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}})=h_{2}^{\star}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}[f_{\star}(\bm{x})x_{i}]=\mathbb{E}_{z}[\sigma_{\star}^{\prime}(z)]\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}=h_{1}^{\star}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{0}}f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu})\bm{x}^{\mu}\right\lVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{dh_{2}^{\star}}{n_{0}}+\frac{(n_{0}-1)h_{1}^{\star 2}}{n_{0}}=\frac{h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}}+h_{1}^{\star 2}.

(41)

Thus

\displaystyle r=\frac{\tilde{\eta}h_{1}}{\sqrt{\beta}}\left(\frac{h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}}+h_{1}^{\star 2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

(42)

A.3
Spike/target alignment

We now turn to the spike/target alignment, defined as the overlap between the (normalized) target weights
\theta
and spike
v
.

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{v}\sqrt{d}}{d\lVert\bm{v}\lVert}=\frac{\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{0}}\sigma_{\star}(z^{\mu})z^{\mu}}{\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{0}}f_{\star}(x^{\mu})x^{\mu}\right\lVert}.

(43)

We noted
z^{\mu}\equiv\nicefrac{{\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\mu}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
. The numerator converges by the law of large numbers to
h^{\star}_{1}
, while the numerator was computed in the previous subsection. All in all, we reach

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{h_{1}^{\star}}{\left(\frac{h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}}+h_{1}^{\star 2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}

(44)

A.4
Bulk norm

By symmetry, all rows of the bulk
\Delta
(
35
) asymptotically share the same norm
c_{\Delta}
. The norm of a row
w
of
W=W^{(0)}+\Delta
thus has asymptotic norm

\displaystyle\lVert w_{i}\lVert=\sqrt{1+c_{\Delta}^{2}+2c_{W\Delta}}

(45)

where

\displaystyle c_{\Delta}=\left\lVert\frac{\eta}{n_{0}p}\left(\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{0}}\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x^{\mu})f_{\star}(x^{\mu})x^{\mu}\right)\right\lVert

(46)

\displaystyle c_{W\Delta}=\frac{\eta}{n_{0}p}\left(\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n_{0}}\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x^{\mu})f_{\star}(x^{\mu})w^{(0)\top}x^{\mu}\right),

(47)

where with a slight abuse of notation we dropped the row index
i
, since all rows are equivalent.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[c_{\Delta}^{2}]=\frac{\eta^{2}}{n_{0}p^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x)^{2}\sigma_{\star}(\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})^{2}x_{i}^{2}]+\frac{(n_{0}-1)\eta^{2}}{n_{0}p^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x)\sigma_{\star}(\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})x_{i}]^{2}

(48)

Because
w^{(0)\top}x,\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}},x_{i}
all have asymptotically vanishing
\Theta_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}})
correlations, the first summand reads to leading order

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x)^{2}\sigma_{\star}(\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})^{2}x_{i}^{2}]=\check{h}_{1}^{2}h_{2}^{\star}

(49)

The second summand reads, using Stein’s lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x)\sigma_{\star}(\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})x_{i}]

\displaystyle=w^{(0)}_{i}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\check{\sigma}^{\prime}(w^{(0)\top}x)\sigma_{\star}(\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})]+\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x)\sigma_{\star}^{\prime}(\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})]

\displaystyle=\Theta_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}})

(50)

We used

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}[\check{\sigma}(z)]=\mathbb{E}_{z}[\check{\sigma}^{\prime}(z)]=0

(51)

respectively by definition of
\check{\sigma}
and because
\sigma
is odd. Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[c_{\Delta}^{2}]=\frac{\eta^{2}\check{h}_{1}^{2}h_{2}d}{n_{0}p^{2}}+\Theta_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}})=\frac{\tilde{\eta}^{2}\check{h}_{1}^{2}h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}}+o_{d}(1).

(52)

The cross-term
c_{\Delta W}
can similarly be computed as

\displaystyle c_{W\Delta}=\frac{\eta}{p}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\check{\sigma}(w^{(0)\top}x)\sigma_{\star}(\nicefrac{{\theta^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})w^{(0)\top}x]

(53)

Again because all variables in the expectation are weakly correlated, and because the average of
\check{\sigma}
is zero, we have that
c_{W\Delta}
is vanishing to leading order.

\displaystyle c_{W\Delta}=o_{d}(1)

(54)

Finally, we thus have

\displaystyle c=1+\frac{\tilde{\eta}^{2}\check{h}_{1}^{2}h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}}

(55)

A.5
Summary of the mapping

In summary, we have derived the asymptotic formulae for the equivalent sRF:

\displaystyle c=1+\frac{\tilde{\eta}^{2}\check{h}_{1}^{2}h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}\beta^{2}}

(56)

\displaystyle r=\frac{\tilde{\eta}h_{1}}{\sqrt{\beta}}\left(\frac{h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}}+h_{1}^{\star 2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

(57)

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{h_{1}^{\star}}{\left(\frac{h_{2}^{\star}}{\alpha_{0}}+h_{1}^{\star 2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}},

(58)

which recovers equation (
9
) of Result
3.1
.

Appendix B
Derivation of Result
3.2

In this section we detail the heuristic derivation of Result
3.2
, which shows the asymptotic equivalence of the sRF feature map (
13
) with the conditional feature map (
14
). This mapping is at the core of the exact asymptotic description detailed in the next appendix.

We first remark that, since the data is Gaussian,
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
, we can decompose it as

\displaystyle x=\kappa+x^{\perp},

(59)

where
\kappa\equiv\nicefrac{{v^{\top}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and we noted
x^{\perp}\equiv\Pi^{\perp}x
the component orthogonal to the spike, with Gaussian statistics
x^{\perp}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Pi^{\perp}).
The sRF feature map (
13
) thus reads

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\sigma\left(\kappa 1_{p}+\kappa\frac{Wv}{\sqrt{d}}+Wx^{\perp}\right)

(60)

Conditioning on the projection
\kappa
along the spike, this corresponds to a RF feature map with random biases
\kappa 1_{p}+\kappa\nicefrac{{Wv}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
. One can compute the population mean and covariance of
\varphi_{x}
along the exact same lines as e.g.
[
24
]
, leading to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[\varphi(x)_{i}]=\tilde{\kappa}^{0}_{i}.

(61)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{x}[\varphi(x)_{i},\varphi(x)_{j}]=\tilde{\kappa}^{1}_{i}\tilde{\kappa}^{1}_{j}(W)_{i}^{\top}\Pi^{\perp}(W)_{j}+\delta_{ij}(\kappa^{*}_{i})^{2}.

(62)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}^{0}_{i}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{z}[\sigma(\kappa+\nicefrac{{(W)_{i}^{\top}v}}{{\sqrt{d}}}+z)]

(63)

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}^{1}_{i}\equiv\mathbb{E}_{z}[z\sigma(\kappa+\nicefrac{{(W)_{i}^{\top}v}}{{\sqrt{d}}}+z)]

(64)

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}^{*}_{i}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{z}[\sigma(\kappa+\nicefrac{{(W)_{i}^{\top}v}}{{\sqrt{d}}}+z)^{2}]-(\tilde{\kappa}^{1}_{i})^{2}-(\tilde{\kappa}^{0}_{i})^{2}}

(65)

In other words, the features
\varphi(x)

share the same second order statistics
as the noisy features

\displaystyle\varphi^{g}(x)_{i}\equiv\tilde{\kappa}^{0}_{i}+\tilde{\kappa}^{1}_{i}(W)_{i}^{\top}x^{\perp}+\tilde{\kappa}^{*}_{i}\xi_{i}

(66)

where
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a stochastic noise. Note that in the definition of the
\kappa^{0,1,*}_{i}
coefficients (
63
),
\nicefrac{{(W)_{i}^{\top}v}}{{\sqrt{d}}}=\Theta_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}})
, so one can expand them as

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}^{0}_{i}\equiv\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z}[\sigma(\kappa+z)]}_{\mu_{0}(\kappa)}+\nicefrac{{(W)_{i}^{\top}v}}{{\sqrt{d}}}\mu_{1}(\kappa)

(67)

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}^{1}_{i}\equiv\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z}[z\sigma(\kappa+z)]}_{\mu_{1}(\kappa)}+\nicefrac{{(W)_{i}^{\top}v}}{{\sqrt{d}}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z}[z\sigma^{\prime}(\kappa+z)}_{\equiv\mu_{3}(\kappa)}]

(68)

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}^{*}_{i}=\underbrace{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{z}[\sigma(\kappa+z)^{2}]-(\kappa^{1})^{2}-(\kappa^{0})^{2}}}_{\mu_{2}(\kappa)}+O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}})

(69)

So

\displaystyle\varphi^{g}(x)_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\mu_{0}(\kappa)+\mu_{1}(\kappa)(W)_{i}^{\top}x+

(70)

\displaystyle+\mu_{3}(\kappa)\times\frac{(W)_{i}^{\top}v}{\sqrt{d}}\times(W)_{i}x^{\perp}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)\xi_{i}+\xi_{i}\Theta_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}})

(71)

Finally, note that the
\mu_{3}(\kappa)
term is subleading compared to the second term, and should be asymptotically unconsequential for the learning, and can thus be safely neglected. By the same token, the last term is a subleading correction to the noise term and can be neglected. Therefore, one finally reaches

\displaystyle\varphi^{g}(x)\equiv\mu_{0}(\kappa)1_{p}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)W^{\top}x+\mu_{2}(\kappa)\xi

(72)

which recovers (
14
) in Result
3.2
.

Appendix C
Derivation of Result
3.3

In this Appendix, we detail the derivation of Result
3.3
. The derivation leverages the replica method from statistical physics
[
16
,
17
]
in its replica-symmetric formulation, in conjunction with Result
3.2
. On a technical level, the derivation builds upon the formal equivalence between the distribution of the equivalent features
\bm{\varphi}^{g}(x)
(
14
) with a mixture of Gaussians with infinitely many
\kappa-
indexed clusters, similarly to the superstatistical approach of
[
57
,
66
]
in another setting.

First observe that for any test function
\phi(\hat{\bm{a}})
of the minimizer
\hat{\bm{a}}
of (
4
),

\displaystyle\phi(\hat{\bm{a}})=\underset{\omega\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\frac{1}{Z}\int d\bm{a}\phi(\bm{a})e^{-\omega\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}[\bm{a}]},

(73)

where we remind that
R[\bm{a}]
denotes the empirical risk (
4
), and we denoted

\displaystyle Z\equiv\int d\bm{a}e^{-\omega\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}[\bm{a}]}

(74)

the normalization factor, also known as the
partition function
in statistical physics. It is therefore important to the generating function associated with the measure (
73
), namely
\mathbb{E}\ln Z
. Such computations can be addressed using the
replica
method from statistical physics
[
16
,
17
]
, leveraging the identity

\displaystyle\ln Z=\underset{s\to 0}{\lim}\frac{Z^{s}-1}{s}.

(75)

The backbone of the derivation thus lies in the computation of
\mathbb{E}Z^{s}
.

C.1
Reminder of setting and notations

Because of Result
3.2
, we take as a starting point that one can assume the sRF features
\bm{z}=\bm{\varphi}(x)
(
13
) are distributed as

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}(\kappa)\bm{1}_{p}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)W\bm{x}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}(\kappa)\bm{1}_{p}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}}\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa W\bm{v}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)W\Pi^{\perp}\bm{x}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)\bm{\xi}

(76)

where
\kappa\equiv\frac{\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{z}}{\sqrt{d}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\bm{u}=\bm{1}_{d},\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d}),\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{p})
. We remind that

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\Ea_{w}{\sigma(w+r\kappa)}

(77)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\Ea_{w}{w\sigma(w+r\kappa)}

(78)

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Ea_{w}{\sigma^{2}(w+r\kappa)-c(\mu_{1}(\kappa))^{2}-(\mu_{0}(\kappa))^{2}}},

(79)

where expectations bear over
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c)
. Finally, we consider a generic target function

\displaystyle f_{\star}(\bm{x})=\sigma_{\star}(\kappa,\nicefrac{{\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Pi^{\perp}\bm{x}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}).

(80)

Note that single-index models considered in the main text constitute a special case of this family of functions, which we consider here for generality.
We look at the empirical risk minimization over
\bm{a}
of

\displaystyle R[\bm{a}]=\sum\limits_{\mu=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\star}(\bm{x}^{\mu}),\nicefrac{{\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{z}^{\mu}}}{{\sqrt{p}}})+g(\bm{a}),

(81)

for generic convex loss
\ell
and
\ell_{2}
regularizer
g(\cdot)=\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}}\lVert\cdot\lVert^{2}
. We shall provide a detailed derivation in this generic case and only specialize to the particular case of square loss addressed in Result
3.3
at the end of the derivation.

C.2
Replica computation

The replicated partition function reads

\displaystyle Z^{s}=\int\prod\limits_{r=1}^{s}d\bm{a}_{r}e^{-\omega g(\bm{a}_{r})}\left[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x}\prod\limits_{r=1}^{s}e^{-\omega\ell(f_{\star}(\bm{x}),\nicefrac{{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}\bm{z}}}{{\sqrt{p}}})}}_{(\star)}\right]^{n}

(82)

We now expand
(\star)
.

\displaystyle(\star)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa,\bm{x}^{\perp},\bm{\xi}}\prod\limits_{r=1}^{s}e^{-\omega\ell(\sigma_{\star}(\kappa,\lambda_{\star}),\mu_{0}(\kappa)m_{r}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta_{r}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)g_{r}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)h_{r})}

(83)

We defined the summary statistics

\displaystyle m_{r}=\frac{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}\bm{1}_{p}}{\sqrt{p}},

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}=\frac{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}W\bm{v}}{\sqrt{dp}},

\displaystyle q^{1}_{rr^{\prime}}=\frac{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}\Omega\bm{a}_{r^{\prime}}}{p},

\displaystyle q^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}=\frac{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}\bm{a}_{r^{\prime}}}{p},

(84)

and local fields

\displaystyle g_{r}=\frac{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}W\Pi^{\perp}\bm{x}}{\sqrt{p}},

\displaystyle h_{r}=\frac{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}\bm{\xi}}{\sqrt{p}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\star}=\frac{\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Pi^{\perp}\bm{x}}{\sqrt{d}}

(85)

where

\displaystyle\Omega=W\Pi^{\perp}W^{\top}.

(86)

One can then rewrite
(\star)
more compactly as

\displaystyle(\star)=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\lambda_{\star},\{\lambda_{r}\}_{r=1}^{s}}e^{-\omega\sum\limits_{r=1}^{s}\ell(\sigma_{\star}(\kappa,\lambda_{\star}),\mu_{0}(\kappa)m_{r}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta_{r}+\lambda_{r})}\right]

(87)

where the fields
\lambda_{\star},\lambda_{r}
are Gaussian with statistics

\displaystyle\langle\lambda_{r}\lambda_{r^{\prime}}\rangle=\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}q^{1}_{rr^{\prime}}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)^{2}q^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\langle\lambda_{\star}^{2}\rangle=\frac{\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Pi^{\perp}\bm{\theta}}{d}\equiv\rho

\displaystyle\langle\lambda_{r}\lambda_{\star}\rangle=\mu_{1}(\kappa)\frac{\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}W\Pi^{\perp}\bm{\theta}}{\sqrt{pd}}\equiv\mu_{1}(\kappa)\psi_{r}

(88)

Introducing Dirac’s deltas in their Fourier form to enforce the definitions of the summary statistics
m_{r},\zeta_{r},q^{1,2}_{rr^{\prime}},\psi_{r}
, the replicated partition function then reads

\displaystyle Z^{s}=

\displaystyle\int\prod\limits_{r=1}^{s}dm_{r}d\hat{m}_{r}d\zeta_{r}d\hat{\zeta}_{r}d\psi_{r}d\hat{\psi}_{r}\prod\limits_{r,r^{\prime}}dq^{1}_{rr^{\prime}}d\hat{q}^{1}_{rr^{\prime}}dq^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}d\hat{q}^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}\underbrace{e^{-\sqrt{p}\sum\limits_{r}m_{r}\hat{m}_{r}-\sqrt{dp}\sum\limits_{r}(\zeta_{r}\hat{\zeta}_{r}+\psi_{r}\hat{\psi}_{r})-p\sum\limits_{a\leq b}(q^{1}_{rr^{\prime}}\hat{q}^{1}_{rr^{\prime}}+q^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}\hat{q}^{2}_{rr^{\prime}})}}_{e^{s\omega p\Psi_{t}}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int\prod\limits_{r}d\bm{a}_{r}e^{-\omega\sum\limits_{r}g(\bm{a}_{r})}e^{+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{s}\bm{a}_{r}^{\top}(\hat{m}_{r}\bm{1}_{p}+\hat{\zeta}_{r}W\bm{v}+\hat{\psi}_{r}W\Pi^{\perp}\bm{\theta})+\sum\limits_{r\leq r^{\prime}}(\hat{q}^{1}_{rr^{\prime}}\bm{w}_{r}^{\top}\Omega\bm{w}_{r^{\prime}}+\hat{q}^{2}_{rr^{\prime}}\bm{w}_{r}^{\top}\bm{w}_{b})}}_{e^{s\omega p\Psi_{w}}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\lambda_{\star},\{\lambda_{r}\}_{r=1}^{s}}e^{-\omega\sum\limits_{r=1}^{s}\ell(\sigma_{\star}(\kappa,\lambda_{\star}),\mu_{0}(\kappa)m^{a}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta^{a}+\lambda_{r})}\right)\right]^{n}}_{e^{\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{\beta}}s\omega p\Psi_{y}}}

(89)

We will refer to these three terms respectively as the trace, entropic, and energetic terms. Note that all terms in the exponents are a priori
\Theta_{d}(d)
, thus large. It is therefore possible to compute the integral over the summary statistics
m_{r},\zeta_{r},q^{1,2}_{rr^{\prime}},\psi_{r}
and respective conjugate variables using Laplace’s method, thereby reducing the problem to finding the extremum of
s\Psi_{t}+s\Psi_{w}+\alpha s\Psi_{y}
.

C.3
Replica symmetric ansatz

The extremization remains challenging on several levels, notably because it involves
s^{2}+5s
variables, and the replica method requires to take the limit
s\to 0
. To make the problem tractable, we will seek the extremum assuming it has the
replica-symmetric
form

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r,r^{\prime}\leq s,~~q^{\iota}_{rr^{\prime}}=\delta_{rr^{\prime}}(r^{\iota}-q^{\iota})+q^{\iota}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r\leq s,~~m_{r}=m

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r\leq s,~~\zeta_{r}=\zeta

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r\leq s,~~\psi_{r}=\psi

(90)

for
\iota=1,2
, and similarly for the hat variables:

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r,r^{\prime}\leq s,~~\hat{q}^{\iota}_{ab}=\delta_{ab}(-\frac{1}{2}\hat{r}^{\iota}-\hat{q}^{\iota})+\hat{q}^{\iota}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r\leq s,~~\hat{m}_{r}=\hat{m}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r\leq s,~~\hat{\zeta}_{r}=\hat{\zeta}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq r\leq s,~~\hat{\psi}_{r}=\hat{\psi}

(91)

Like in e.g.
[
63
]
, we also introduce the variance order parameters

\displaystyle V_{\iota}=r_{\iota}-q_{\iota},

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\iota}=\hat{r}_{\iota}+\hat{q}_{\iota}.

(92)

C.4
Computing the free energy

Entropic term

The computation of the entropic term follows from the derivation of
[
67
]
, with but minor changes. The entropic term thus reads,

\displaystyle p\omega\Psi_{w}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}}\ln\int d\bm{a}e^{-\omega g(\bm{a})-\frac{1}{2}\bm{a}^{\top}(\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p})\bm{a}+\bm{a}^{\top}\left(\hat{m}1_{p}+\hat{\zeta}W\bm{v}+\hat{\psi}W\Pi^{\perp}\bm{\zeta}+\left(\hat{q}_{1}\Omega+\hat{q}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}\right)}

(93)

In the
\omega\to\infty
limit, rescaling the conjugate statistics as

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\iota}\to\omega^{2}\hat{q}_{\iota},

\displaystyle\hat{m},\hat{V}_{\iota},\hat{\zeta},\hat{\psi}\to\omega\hat{m},\omega\hat{V}_{\iota},\omega\hat{\zeta},\omega\hat{\psi},

(94)

the entropic potential can finally be rewritten in the
\omega\to\infty
limit as

\displaystyle\Psi_{w}=-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\bm{\xi})

(95)

where we define the Moreau envelope

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{g}(\bm{\xi})=\underset{\bm{a}}{\inf}\left\{g(\bm{a})+\frac{1}{2}\bm{a}^{\top}(\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p})\bm{a}-a^{\top}\left(\hat{m}\bm{1}_{p}+\hat{\zeta}W\bm{v}+\hat{\psi}W\Pi^{\perp}\bm{\theta}+\left(\hat{q}_{1}\Omega+\hat{q}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}\right)\right\}

(96)

For the case
g=\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}}\lVert\cdot\lVert^{2}
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle\Psi_{w}=\frac{1}{2p}\Tr[\left(\hat{m}^{2}\bm{1}_{p}\bm{1}_{p}^{\top}+\hat{\zeta}^{2}W\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\top}W^{\top}+\hat{\psi}^{2}W\Pi^{\perp}\bm{\theta}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Pi^{\perp}W^{\top}+\hat{q}_{1}\Omega+\hat{q}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)\left(\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}+\lambda\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}].

(97)

We neglected cross-terms of the form
\bm{1}_{p}\bm{v}^{\top}W^{\top},\bm{1}_{p}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}W^{\top},W\bm{v}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Pi^{\top}W^{\top}
in the numerator, which are expected to be asymptotically subleading. For example, one expects

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\Tr[\bm{1}_{p}\bm{v}^{\top}W^{\top}\left(\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}+\lambda\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)^{-1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\bm{v}^{\top}W^{\top}\left(S^{-1}+\frac{\hat{V}_{1}}{d}\frac{S^{-1}Wvv^{\top}W^{\top}S^{-1}}{1-\frac{\hat{V}_{1}}{d}\Tr[Wvv^{\top}W^{\top}S^{-1}]}\right)\bm{1}_{p}

\displaystyle=O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}}),

(98)

where we introduced the shorthand
S=\hat{V}_{1}WW^{\top}+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p}+\lambda\mathbb{I}_{p}
and used the Sherman-Morrison lemma. The last scaling follows from the fact that since the vectors
W\bm{v},\bm{1}_{p}
are weakly correlated, their scalar product –with the uncorrelated matrix
S^{-1}
in the middle– should be
\bm{v}^{\top}W^{\top}S^{-1}\bm{1}_{p}=O_{d}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{d}}})
. A similar reasoning holds for the other cross-terms.

Energetic term

The energetic term is the same as for a Gaussian mixture computation as in e.g.
[
62
,
68
,
69
,
70
]
for Gaussian mixture distributions, with the
\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}
playing the role of the sum over clusters. In other words, the equivalent feature map (

\kappa

\bm{v}

\displaystyle\Psi_{y}=-\mathbb{E}_{\kappa,\xi,y}\mathcal{M}(\xi,\kappa,y)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\xi,\kappa,y)=\underset{x}{\inf}\left\{\frac{1}{2V(\kappa)}(x-\mu_{1}(\kappa)\psi y-\sqrt{\rho q(\kappa)-\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}\psi^{2}}\xi)^{2}+\ell(\sigma_{\star}(\kappa,y),\mu_{0}(\kappa)m+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta+x)\right\}

(100)

we noted

\displaystyle q(\kappa)=\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}q_{1}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)^{2}q_{2},

\displaystyle V(\kappa)=\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}V_{1}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)^{2}V_{2}.

(101)

in e.g.
[
68

or
[
70

, i.e. the overlaps associated to cluster index

. As previously mentioned, the equivalent map (

\kappa

Trace term

Note that in contrast to e.g.
[

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{V}_{1}q_{1}-\hat{q}_{1}V_{1})-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{V}_{2}q_{2}-\hat{q}_{2}V_{2})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}m\hat{m}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\psi\hat{\psi}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\zeta\hat{\zeta}

Putting everything together, the free energy is

\displaystyle f=

\displaystyle\underset{q_{\iota},V_{\iota},m,\psi,\zeta,\hat{q}_{\iota},\hat{V}_{\iota},\hat{m},\hat{\psi},\hat{\zeta}}{\mathrm{extr}}\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{V}_{1}q_{1}-\hat{q}_{1}V_{1})-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{V}_{2}q_{2}-\hat{q}_{2}V_{2})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\psi\hat{\psi}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\zeta\hat{\zeta}

Saddle point equations for the square loss

of the Moreau envelope for

\displaystyle x^{\star}(\kappa,\xi)=\frac{V(\kappa)\left(\sigma_{\star}(\kappa,y)-\mu_{0}(\kappa)m-\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta-\mu_{1}(\kappa)\psi\right)+\sqrt{\rho q(\kappa)-\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}\psi^{2}}\xi+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\psi y}{1+V(\kappa)}.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{m}\mathbb{E}_{\kappa,\xi}\mathcal{M}(\xi,\kappa)

C.7
Test error

We conclude this Appendix by deriving the sharp asymptotic characterization for the test error. Using once more Result

, the test error reads

\displaystyle\epsilon_{g}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\sigma_{\star}(\kappa,\nicefrac{{\bm{\theta}^{\top}\Pi^{\perp}x}}{{\sqrt{d}}})-\nicefrac{{\hat{a}^{\top}\varphi^{g}(x)}}{{\sqrt{p}}}\right)^{2}

C.8
Additional discussion

Before closing the present Appendix, we provide in this subsection further discussion of the results and highlight possible extensions.

Bias–

Let us briefly sketch in this subsection how bias terms may be incorporated into the analysis. Consider the two-layer neural network

\displaystyle f_{W,\bm{a},b}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}+b),

(129)

where
b\in\mathbb{R}
is a bias that acts uniformly on all hidden-layer neurons. Then, conditional on
b
, the equivalent feature map still is of the form (
14
), with coefficients

\begin{split}\mu_{0}(\kappa,b)&=\Ea_{z}{\sigma(z+r\kappa+b)}\\
\mu_{1}(\kappa,b)&=\frac{1}{c}\Ea_{z}{z\sigma(z+r\kappa+b)}\\
\mu_{2}(\kappa,b)&=\sqrt{\Ea_{z}{\sigma^{2}(z+r\kappa+b)-c(\mu_{1}(\kappa))^{2}-(\mu_{0}(\kappa))^{2}}}.\end{split}

(130)

The analysis of the present Appendix still goes through, by treating
b
as an additional network parameter to be replicated over. In fact, the only difference in introducing
b
appears at the level of the Moreau envelope
\mathcal{M}
, in which all the
\mu_{0,1,2}(\kappa)
coefficients are replaced by
\mu_{0,1,2}(\kappa+b)
. Finally, the corresponding saddle-point equation on
b
should impose
\partial_{b}\mathcal{M}=0
, thereby imposing the value of the learned bias
\hat{b}
. This allows to improve the lower-bound (
27
) as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{g}\geq\underset{b_{1},b_{2},b}{\inf}\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\Bigg[\Bigg(\sigma_{\star}(\kappa)-\mu_{0}(\kappa,b)b_{1}-\mu_{1}(\kappa,b)\kappa b_{2}\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg].

(131)

In other words, the bias allows an extra degree of freedom in the functional basis expressible by the network, thus allowing additional expressivity. We have addressed the case of uniform bias. While the case of generic non-uniform bias is unfortunately not presently in the reach of the techniques of this manuscript, the case where the components are untrained, but can take finitely many values, can be analyzed along similar lines as the case of non-uniform
u
. We briefly discuss this in Appendix
D
.

Larger rank spikes–

Let us now discuss the case where instead of a spike
\nicefrac{{1_{p}v^{\top}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
, we have a larger rank term
\nicefrac{{1_{p\times k}v^{\top}}}{{\sqrt{d}}}
, with
k=\Theta_{d}(1)
and where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
is now a low-rank matrix instead of a vector. The equivalence of result
3.2
can be readily adapted as follows. Naming
v_{1},...,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
k
columns of
v
, and conditioning on
\kappa_{1},...,\kappa_{k}
the
k
projections of the data along these directions, the equivalent feature map (
14
) retains the same expression, with the coefficient
\mu^{\prime}_{0,1,2}(\kappa_{1},...,\kappa_{k})=\mu_{0,1,2}(\kappa_{1}+...+\kappa_{k})
now becoming multivariate.

Noisy target–

Finally, let us mention how one may adapt the analysis to a noisy target function
f_{\star}(x)+\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The only point where this intervenes is during the evaluation of the energetic potential
\Psi_{y}
, which is identical to e.g.
[
34
]
, Appendix A. The only effect is to change the distribution of the variable
y
in the expectation over the Moreau envelope, and we refer the interested reader to e.g.
[
34
]
for a full expression.

What is the effect of the network width, as measured by

A brief comment on incorporating bias

As mentioned in Appendix
C
, we can accommodate the case of non-uniform quenched bias in the analysis, following identical lines as the one presented above, when the components of the bias can take values in a finite vocabulary

as a function of the values of

Appendix F
Spectrum of The Features Empirical Covariance Matrix

Figure 8:

In this section, we derive a tight asymptotic expression for the spectrum of the empirical covariance of the sRF features
\{\varphi(\bm{x}^{\mu})\}_{\mu=1}^{n}
. Before starting the derivation, let us first remind that the
population
covariance is given by the conditional Gaussian equivalence
3.2
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}[\varphi(\bm{x})\varphi(\bm{x})^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\left(\mu_{0}(\kappa){\bm{1}}_{p}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\kappa\mu_{1}(\kappa)W{\bm{v}}\right)^{\otimes 2}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}W\Pi^{\perp}W^{\top}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)^{2}\mathbb{I}_{p}\right]

(172)

where
\cdot^{\otimes 2}
denotes the outer product of a vector with itself.

We now turn to the characterization of the empirical covariance. Let us denote by
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
the matrix with rows
\{\varphi(\bm{x}^{\mu})\}_{\mu=1}^{n}
, and the empirical covariance
\Sigma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}\Psi^{\top}\Psi
. Note that up to zero eigenvalues, this matrix has the same spectrum as the conjugate kernel
\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}\Psi\Psi^{\top}
. In order to access the spectrum of
\Sigma
, we aim to compute the Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle s(z)=\mathbb{E}_{\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}}\Tr[(\Sigma+z\mathbb{I}_{p})^{-1}]

(173)

for some complex number
z
. This computation can be achieved again using the replica method, first observing the identity

\displaystyle s(z)=-2\partial_{z}\mathbb{E}_{\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\ln\det[2\pi(\Sigma+z\mathbb{I}_{p})^{-1}]\equiv 2\partial_{z}\Phi(z).

(174)

We introduced the free entropy
\Phi(z)
, which can be computed as

\displaystyle\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}}\ln\underbrace{\int d\bm{w}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\top}(\Sigma+z\mathbb{I}_{p})\bm{w}}}_{\equiv Z(z)}.

(175)

Once more, the generative function
\mathbb{E}_{\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}}\ln Z(z)
can be evaluated using the replica method, leveraging the identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}}\ln Z(z)=\underset{s\to 0}{\lim}\frac{\ln(\mathbb{E}_{v} Z(z)^s)-1}{s}.

(176)

The problem thus reduces to the computation of the replicated partition function
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}Z(z)^{s}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{\bm{x}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}}Z(z)^{s}

\displaystyle=\int\prod\limits_{a=1}^{s}d\bm{w}_{a}e^{-\frac{z}{2}\sum\limits_{a}\lVert\bm{w}_{a}\lVert^{2}}\prod\limits_{\mu}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{a}(\nicefrac{{\bm{w}^{\top}_{a}\varphi(\bm{x})}}{{\sqrt{p}}})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\prod\limits_{a=1}^{s}d\bm{w}_{a}e^{-\frac{z}{2}\sum\limits_{a}\lVert\bm{w}_{a}\lVert^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{a}(\mu_{0}(\kappa)m_{a}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta_{a}+\lambda_{a})^{2}}.\right]^{n}

(177)

Like in Appendix
C
, we introduced the order parameters

\displaystyle m_{a}\equiv\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\bm{1}_{p}}{\sqrt{p}},

\displaystyle\zeta_{a}\equiv\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}W\bm{v}}{\sqrt{dp}}.

(178)

We also introduced the local fields
\{\lambda_{a}\}_{a}
, which are Gaussian with zero mean and joint statistics

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{a}\lambda_{b}]=\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}q^{1}_{ab}+\mu_{2}(\kappa)q^{2}_{ab},

(179)

denoting the summary statistics

\displaystyle q^{1}_{ab}\equiv\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\Omega\bm{w}}{p},

\displaystyle q^{2}_{ab}\equiv\frac{\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\bm{w}}{p},

(180)

where we remind
\Omega=W\Pi^{\perp}W^{\top}
. The replicated partition function can thus be written as

\displaystyle Z^{s}=

\displaystyle\int\prod\limits_{a=1}^{s}dm_{a}d\hat{m}_{a}d\zeta_{a}d\hat{\zeta}_{a}\prod\limits_{a,b}dq^{1}_{ab}d\hat{q}^{1}_{ab}dq^{2}_{ab}d\hat{q}^{2}_{ab}\underbrace{e^{-\sqrt{p}\sum\limits_{r}m_{a}\hat{m}_{a}-\sqrt{dp}\sum\limits_{a}(\zeta_{a}\hat{\zeta}_{a})-p\sum\limits_{a\leq b}(q^{1}_{ab}\hat{q}^{1}_{ab}+q^{2}_{ab}\hat{q}^{2}_{ab})}}_{e^{sp\Psi_{t}}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int\prod\limits_{a}d\bm{w}_{a}e^{-\frac{z}{2}\sum\limits_{a}\lVert\bm{w}_{a}\lVert^{2}}e^{+\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}(\hat{m}_{a}\bm{1}_{p}+\hat{\zeta}_{a}W\bm{v})+\sum\limits_{a\leq b}(\hat{q}^{1}_{ab}\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\Omega\bm{w}_{b}+\hat{q}^{2}_{ab}\bm{w}_{a}^{\top}\bm{w}_{b})}}_{e^{sp\Psi_{w}}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\{\lambda_{a}\}_{a=1}^{s}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{a=1}^{s}(\mu_{0}(\kappa)m_{a}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta_{a}+\lambda_{a})^{2}}\right)\right]^{n}}_{e^{\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{\beta}}sp\Psi_{y}}},

(181)

an expression bearing great similarity with that reached in Appendix
C
. In fact, formally, this expression is equivalent to the finite-temperature (
\omega=1
) version of the one discussed in Appendix
C
, in the particular case of a regularization
\lambda=z
, and with a loss function
\ell(y,z)=\ell(z)=z^{2}
. Identical computational steps can then be followed to reach a tight characterization of the free entropy. Again, we assume a RS ansatz

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a,b\leq s,~~q^{1}_{ab}=\delta_{ab}V_{1}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a,b\leq s,~~q^{2}_{ab}=\delta_{ab}V_{2}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a\leq s,~~\zeta_{a}=\zeta

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a\leq s,~~m_{a}=m

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a,b\leq s,~~\hat{q}^{1}_{ab}=-\delta_{ab}\nicefrac{{\hat{V}_{1}}}{{2}}

(182)

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a,b\leq s,~~\hat{q}^{2}_{ab}=-\delta_{ab}\nicefrac{{\hat{V}_{2}}}{{2}}

(183)

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a\leq s,~~\hat{\zeta}_{a}=\hat{\zeta}

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq a\leq s,~~\hat{m}_{a}=\hat{m}.

(184)

Following identical steps as Appendix
C
, one finally reaches

\displaystyle\Phi(z)=

\displaystyle\underset{V_{1},V_{2},m,\zeta,\hat{V}_{1},\hat{V}_{2},\hat{m},\hat{\zeta}}{\mathrm{extr}}\frac{\hat{V}_{1}V_{1}+\hat{V}_{2}V_{2}}{2}-\frac{m\hat{m}}{\sqrt{p}}-\frac{\zeta\hat{\zeta}}{\sqrt{\beta}}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det\left[z\mathbb{I}_{p}+\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Tr[(z\mathbb{I}_{p}+\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p})^{-1}\left(\hat{m}^{2}\bm{1}_{p}\bm{1}_{p}^{\top}+\hat{\zeta}^{2}W\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\top}W^{\top}\right)]

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\frac{\left(\mu_{0}(\kappa)m+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta\right)^{2}}{1+V(\kappa)}\right]-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\ln(1+V(\kappa))

(185)

Expunding the extremization conditions with respect to
m,\hat{m},\zeta,\hat{\zeta}
imposes

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0=\hat{m}\Tr[(z\mathbb{I}_{p}+\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p})^{-1}\bm{1}_{p}\bm{1}_{p}^{\top}]\\
\zeta=\hat{\zeta}\Tr[(z\mathbb{I}_{p}+\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p})^{-1}W\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\top}W^{\top}]\\

\hat{\zeta}=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\frac{\left(\mu_{0}(\kappa)m+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\zeta\right)\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa}{1+V(\kappa)}\right]\end{cases}

(186)

Combining the last two equations imposes

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\frac{\left(-\mu_{0}(\kappa)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\frac{\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\mu_{0}(\kappa)}{1+V(\kappa)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\frac{\mu_{0}(\kappa)^{2}}{1+V(\kappa)}\right]}+\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa\right)\mu_{1}(\kappa)\kappa}{1+V(\kappa)}\right]\zeta

(187)

from which it generically follows that
\zeta=0
, which also implies
m=\hat{m}=\hat{\zeta}=0
. Finally, the extremization conditions with respect to
V_{1,2},\hat{V}_{1,2}
yield the simple equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}V_{2}=\Tr[(z\mathbb{I}_{p}+\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p})^{-1}]\\
V_{1}=Tr[(z\mathbb{I}_{p}+\hat{V}_{1}\Omega+\hat{V}_{2}\mathbb{I}_{p})^{-1}\Omega]\\
\hat{V}_{1}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\frac{\mu_{1}(\kappa)^{2}}{1+V(\kappa)}\right]\\
\hat{V}_{2}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\frac{\mu_{2}(\kappa)^{2}}{1+V(\kappa)}\right]\end{cases}

(188)

Note how these equations correspond exactly to the ones of Result
3.3
, replacing the regularization

. Finally, we observe that
\partial_{z}\Phi(z)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}V_{2}
, which implies that the Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle s(z)=V_{2},

(189)

with

a solution of the system of equations above. The spectrum can then be extracted from the Stieltjes transform, and matches well with numerical simulations on sRF features, see Fig.

.

Spike

The previous study allowed to characterize the asymptotic bulk eigenvalue distribution of the empirical features covariance. Note that there is a single spike
\lambda_{\mathrm{spk.}}=\Theta_{d}(d)
not captured by the analysis, which relies on a Stieltjes argument
z=\Theta_{d}(1)
. It is however reasonable to expect this spike eigenvalue to coincide with that of the population covariance. This eigenvalue should then be asymptotically equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{spk.}}}{d}=\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\kappa\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[c_{0}(\kappa)^{2}].

(190)
