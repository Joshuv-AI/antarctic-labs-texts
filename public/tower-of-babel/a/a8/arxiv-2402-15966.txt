Data-driven micromorphic mechanics for materials with strain localization Note: Postprint version. Note: Published version: J. Ulloa, L. Stainier, M. Ortiz, and J.E. Andrade. Data-driven micromorphic mechanics for materials with strain localization. Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering , 2024. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2024.117180
Jacinto Ulloa, Laurent Stainier, Michael Ortiz, José E. Andrade

Abstract
This paper explores the role of generalized continuum mechanics, and the feasibility of model-free data-driven computing approaches thereof, in solids undergoing failure by strain localization. Specifically, we set forth a methodology for capturing material instabilities using data-driven mechanics without prior information regarding the failure mode. We show numerically that, in problems involving strain localization, the standard data-driven framework for Cauchy/Boltzmann continua fails to capture the length scale of the material, as expected. We address this shortcoming by formulating a generalized data-driven framework for micromorphic continua that effectively captures both stiffness and length-scale information, as encoded in the material data, in a model-free manner. These properties are exhibited systematically in a one-dimensional softening bar problem and further verified through selected plane-strain problems.



1
Introduction

It is well-acknowledged that computational models based on classical Cauchy/Boltzmann continua strongly deviate from reality when microstructural effects modulate the material response. This is evident in the case of solids exhibiting different forms of strain localization, a ubiquitous process resulting from material instability, often related to softening responses or non-associative evolution laws. Examples include shear bands in granular materials driven by complex particle interactions
Alonso-Marroquin and Vardoulakis 2005
,
Andò et al. 2012
,
Karapiperis and Andrade 2021
, damage and fracture in quasi-brittle materials governed by evolving microcracks
Bažant 1986
,
Labuz et al. 2001
,
Zhao et al. 2018
, and ductile failure in metals emerging from the interplay between dislocations and void growth
Asaro and Rice 1977
,
Cuitiño and Ortiz 1996
,
Tvergaard and Niordson 2004
.
The inadequacy of classical theories to describe such behaviors arises from their simple nature, preserving a continuum representation of the material at arbitrarily small scales. The de facto approach to this problem in the mechanics community is to introduce, in one way or another, length scales that reflect the heterogeneities of the microstructure.
Aside from strain localization, this approach also allows the description of size effects
Bažant 1999
,
Ortiz et al. 2000
,
Gao and Huang 2001
,
Forest et al. 2000
and wave dispersion phenomena
Madeo et al. 2015
,
Misra and Poorsolhjouy 2016
,
Dayal 2017
.

From a computational perspective, the central dilemma of standard local models with strain localization is the loss of ellipticity of the boundary value problem (BVP), leading to pathological mesh dependence in finite element simulations with vanishing energy dissipation. This issue emerged in the 1970s and has since been addressed by constitutive models on various fronts. A conventional approach involves endowing internal variables with non-local effects. A substantial body of work exists in this context for gradient plasticity
Mühlhaus and Alfantis 1991
,
Hutchinson and Fleck 1997
,
Gurtin and Anand 2009
,
Bardella 2010
,
Martínez-Pañeda and Niordson 2016
,
Scherer et al. 2021
,
Ariza et al. 2023
, gradient damage
Peerlings et al. 2004
,
Comi 1999
,
Marigo et al. 2016
, and integral-type non-local constitutive models
Pijaudier-Cabot and Bažant 1987
,
Bažant and Jirásek 2002
,
Grassl and Jirásek 2006
. Furthermore, non-local effects emerge naturally in fracture modeling via phase-field approaches
Bourdin et al. 2000
,
Marigo et al. 2016
, which can be equipped with additional regularization mechanisms
Miehe et al. 2016
,
Miehe et al. 2017
,
Rodriguez et al. 2018
,
Lancioni and Alessi 2020
,
Suh et al. 2020
,
Kristensen et al. 2020
,
Ulloa et al. 2021a
, and peridynamics
Silling 2000
,
Lipton 2014
,
Bhattacharya et al. 2023
.

It is noteworthy that before these developments, the need for generalized continuum theories for microstructured media had long been recognized, starting with the seminal framework of
micropolar
continua developed by the Cosserats
Cosserat and Cosserat 1909
. Therein, a continuum material point is endowed with independent rotations, associated with the microstructure, whose gradient enters the formulation and serves as a regularization measure. The more general theory of
micromorphic
continua was later elaborated in the works of
Eringen and Suhubi 1964
,
Mindlin 1964
, and
Germain 1973a
, incorporating a full, generally non–symmetric, micro-deformation tensor as an additional set of degrees of freedom. This framework recovers several models as special cases; for instance, the micropolar theory is recovered when the micro-deformation tensor is purely skew-symmetric, while the second-gradient theory is recovered when the micro-deformation equals the compatible displacement gradient. Moreover, the notion of micromorphic degrees of freedom has been extended to plasticity
Forest and Sievert 2003
,
Regueiro 2009
,
Bryant and Sun 2019
,
Ryś et al. 2020
,
Lindroos et al. 2022
and damage
Aslan et al. 2011
,
Miehe et al. 2017
,
Brepols et al. 2017
,
Yin et al. 2022
variables. For a thorough treatise of micromorphic continuum theories, we refer the reader to
Forest 2009
and
Neff et al. 2014
.

All the references above are examples of continuum theories with constitutive models. In particular, the classical stress-strain relation in Cauchy continua–a phenomenological equation itself–is complemented by generalized stress-strain relations. Depending on the modeling assumptions, the number of additional elasticity parameters may increase significantly. Moreover, modeling inelastic behaviors, such as plasticity or damage, further requires an additional set of parameters and, perhaps more importantly, constitutive functions and evolution equations tailored to match experimental results. The inevitable caveat of this conventional modeling paradigm is epistemic uncertainty, particularly for complex material behavior.

The work of
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
on data-driven computing presents a novel perspective that aims to resolve this shortcoming of constitutive modeling in a general context. This framework enforces the fundamental balance laws of continuum mechanics, as in classical problems, but extracts the material behavior directly from empirical data rather than from a constitutive model. The method works for both elastic
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
,
Conti et al. 2018
and inelastic materials
Eggersmann et al. 2019
,
Ciftci and Hackl 2022
and has been extended to finite deformations
Nguyen and Keip 2018
,
Conti et al. 2020
,
Kuang et al. 2023
, composite structures
Xu et al. 2020
, dynamics
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2018
,
Garcia-Suarez et al. 2023
, viscoelasticity
Salahshoor and Ortiz 2022
, fracture mechanics
Carrara et al. 2020
, and molecular dynamics
Bulin et al. 2023
. Data-driven computing has also been used in a multi-scale setting to capture the response of real granular materials
Karapiperis et al. 2021a
, including breakage mechanics in crushable sand
Ulloa et al. 2023
. All these works are based on Cauchy continua and are thus unable to handle material instabilities. An exception is found in
Karapiperis et al. 2021b
, where data-driven computing is extended to micropolar media, a framework suitable for shear-banding driven by significant micro-rotations.
Moreover, an idea for data-driven analysis using strain gradients is given in
Kamasamudram and Stainier 2023
based on Lipschitz regularization.

The present study presents a generalized data-driven mechanics framework for micromorphic continua. The motivation for this framework stems from softening materials with strain localization, where standard data-driven computing yields mechanically admissible states that fail to capture the length scale of the material, i.e., the expected spatial distribution of the state variables. This limitation and its resolution via micromorphic mechanics are showcased using synthetic data in a 1D softening bar governed by a gradient damage model. The framework is further validated in 2D problems considering damage and non-associative plasticity. Remarkably, no a priori data assignment is required for the proposed framework to capture the reference response in the presence of strain localization.

2
Overview of generalized micromorphic continua

2.1
Problem setting

Consider a solid body
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\{1,2,3\}
, evolving in a time interval
\mathrm{I}=[0,T]
. In a classical Cauchy theory with linearized kinematics, the motion of the continuum solid is fully characterized by the macro-displacement field
\bm{u}:\Omega\times\mathrm{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Conversely,
the theory of micromorphic continua
Eringen and Suhubi 1964
,
Mindlin 1964
further considers that attached to each macro-coordinate
\bm{x}\in\Omega
is a
micro-continuum
body
\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}
, subject to independent deformations and defined through micro-coordinate axes
\xi_{i}
whose origin moves with the macro-displacement (figure 1). Then, the motion of the microstructured solid is given by a
micro-displacement
field, conveniently expressed as the sum of the macro-displacement and a fluctuation term:

\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})=\bm{u}(\bm{x})+\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{\xi}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{ij}=\partial v_{i}/\partial\xi_{j}}}\,.

Evidently, in this form, the micro-deformation field
\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}:\Omega\times\mathrm{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is homogeneous in
\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}
and thus independent of
\bm{\xi}\in\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}
, resulting in a micromorphic theory of degree 1
Germain 1973a
.

The micromorphic continuum of degree 1 involves the following generalized strain measures:

Compatible strain

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}=\sym\,\nabla\bm{u}\,,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})\,;

(1)

Relative strain

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}=\nabla\bm{u}-\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\,,

2.3
Material behavior

2.3.1
Elasticity

As in classical Cauchy continua, conventional theories for generalized continua invoke constitutive models to establish the relation between
\{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu}\}
and
\{\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta}\}
. In the case of linear elasticity, a linear mapping
(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta})\mapsto\{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu}\}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta})
is employed, usually in terms of a
quadratic Helmholtz energy density
\psi\coloneqq{\psi}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta})
, such that

\bm{\sigma}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\bm{\tau}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{\gamma}}\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\bm{\mu}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{\zeta}}\,.

(8)

A fairly general form can be written as

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\psi}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big(\bm{\varepsilon}:\bm{\mathsf{C}}:\bm{\varepsilon}+\bm{\gamma}:\bm{\mathsf{D}}:\bm{\gamma}+\bm{\varepsilon}:\bm{\mathsf{E}}:\bm{\gamma}+\bm{\zeta}\,{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{.}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\raisebox{2.80554pt}{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{.}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.80554pt}{\hbox{\hbox{\ignorespaces.}}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}\,\bm{\mathsf{A}}\,{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{.}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\raisebox{2.80554pt}{\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{.}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.80554pt}{\hbox{\hbox{\ignorespaces.}}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}\,\bm{\zeta}\big)\,,}}

(9)

where
\bm{\mathsf{C}}
,
\bm{\mathsf{D}}
,
\bm{\mathsf{E}}
(4th-order), and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
(6th-order) are generalized elasticity tensors. For isotropic materials, these tensors involve up to 18 parameters
Mindlin 1964
, most of which are difficult to obtain experimentally. Of course, this general model includes several special cases with fewer parameters (table

For instance, the micropolar model, with
\textstyle\chi
purely skew-symmetric, preserves 6 parameters. Moreover, the coupling tensor
\bm{\mathsf{E}}
is often assumed to vanish for the sake of simplicity

Broese et al. 2018

\textstyle\chi

Neff et al. 2014
is reduced from 9 to 7 parameters.

2.3.2
Inelasticity

Extensions of generalized continuum theories to plasticity, damage, and other inelastic phenomena
Forest 2009
typically resort to the framework of thermomechanics with internal variables. In this setting, we introduce a generic set of internal variables
\mathbf{q}
and consider free energies depending on both
\mathbf{q}
and (possibly) its gradient, i.e.,
\psi\coloneqq{\psi}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta},\mathbf{q},\nabla\mathbf{q})
. The internal variables obey evolution laws, generally stated as differential inclusions of the form

\frac{\delta\psi}{\delta\mathbf{q}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta},\mathbf{q},\nabla\mathbf{q})\;+\;\partial{\phi}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})\ni\bm{0}\,.

(10)

Here,
\phi\coloneqq{\phi}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})\geq 0
is a convex dissipation potential complying with the second law of thermodynamics. Evidently, the constitutive relations (
8
) are now functions of
\mathbf{q}
and thus become history-dependent. Models of this type can be derived rigorously from the principle of virtual power
Maugin 1980
or, more generally, from the energetic formulation
Ortiz and Stainier 1999
,
Mielke and Roubíček 2015
, as shown for a gradient damage model in
A.1
. It is worth noting that the dissipation potential may also depend on the current state, which allows us to consider non-associative models
Francfort and Mora 2018
,
Ulloa et al. 2021b
.
A.2
presents a plasticity model of this type.

2.4
Data-driven approach

In the standard formulation outlined above, even classical Cauchy continuum models are susceptible to epistemic uncertainty, particularly when dealing with complex inelastic behavior. This challenge is further amplified in the framework of generalized continua, requiring additional constitutive functions and parameters. Thus, in this study, we shift our focus towards the realm of data-driven mechanics
Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz 2016
for inelastic materials
Eggersmann et al. 2019
and extend this approach to encompass generalized micromorphic continua. In this setting, instead of relying on constitutive relations and evolution equations such as (
8
) and (
10
), we assume that the material response in terms of generalized strains
\{\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta}\}
and stresses
\{\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu}\}
is encapsulated in a finite data set, presumably obtained from experimental measurements or high-fidelity numerical simulations. We shall then make direct use of this data to solve the fundamental equations (
1
)–(
3
) and (
6
)–(
7
), without resorting to an empirical model for closure.

Of course, embarking on this path presupposes the availability of data sets comprising generalized coordinates
\{(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\zeta}),(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu})\}
. Measurements of compatible strains and the corresponding Cauchy stresses,
(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\sigma})
, may be obtained, for instance, from high-fidelity virtual simulations and standard homogenization theory, as done in recent works on granular media
Karapiperis et al. 2021a
,
Gorgogianni et al. 2023
,
Ulloa et al. 2023
. Acquiring data for the generalized quantities
(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\tau})
and
(\bm{\zeta},\bm{\mu})
poses an additional challenge. In this context, a data-driven framework for micropolar continua using micro-rotation and couple-stress data directly extracted from LS-DEM simulations of sand has been recently presented
Karapiperis et al. 2021b
.
Extending virtual measurements to encompass generalized micromorphic continua appears feasible in light of previous works on this topic (see
B
).

In this study, we focus not on data acquisition but on developing a data-driven framework that incorporates generalized stress-strain data to simulate materials with strain localization. Consequently, we limit our studies in the sequel to synthetic data obtained from reference micromorphic constitutive models (
A
),
while possibilities for identifying material data from other sources in practical scenarios are outlined in
B
. The following section presents the proposed data-driven formulation.

3
Set-oriented data-driven formulation of micromorphic continua

This section presents the extension of data-driven computing to micromorphic materials. In agreement with most references on data-driven mechanics, we express the formulation in a discretized form, i.e., considering a finite-dimensional system. Consequently, the fundamental kinematic relations (
1
)–(
3
) and balance laws (
6
)–(
7
) are restated in a finite element setting. The resulting equations are employed in the statement of the data-driven problem that follows.

3.1
Discrete kinematics and balance equations

Let us consider hereafter an isotropic solid discretized into
N
nodes and
M
material points, leading to the assembly of nodal displacement and external force vectors denoted as
\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu=\{{\bm{u}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{f}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathrm{ext}}=\{{\bm{f}}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, respectively. Similarly, the nodal micro-deformations and double forces are collected in vectors denoted as
\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu=\{{\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\chi}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{m}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathrm{ext}}=\{{\bm{m}}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\chi}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, respectively. Here, the size of the nodal micro-deformation vector,
n_{\chi}
, depends on the symmetry conditions. For instance,
n_{\chi}=n^{2}
for the full first-order micromorphic model and
n_{\chi}=n(n+1)/2
for the microstrain simplification (see table
1
).

On the other hand, the time interval
\mathrm{I}
is discretized into a finite number of time steps
0=t^{0}<\dots<t^{k}<t^{k+1}<\dots<t^{K}=T
. Hereinafter, unless required for clarity, we denote a quantity at a given time
t^{k}
as
\Box^{k}\coloneqq\Box(t^{k})
and omit the superscript at the next time step, so that
\Box\coloneqq\Box^{k+1}
.

In this section, second- and higher-order tensors evaluated at a point in space are expressed in their Voigt representation. Then, the discrete form of the kinematic relations (
1
)–(
3
) reads

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}=\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu\;+\;\bm{g}^{u}_{e}\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{e}=\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu\;-\;\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}_{e}=\mathbf{B}^{\zeta}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu\;+\;\bm{g}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{e}\,,

(13)

where
\bm{g}^{u}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\varepsilon}}
and
\bm{g}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\zeta}}
follow from Dirichlet boundary conditions, with
n_{\varepsilon}=n(n+1)/{2}
and
n_{\zeta}=n_{\chi}(n_{\chi}+1)/2
. Similarly, the balance equations (
6
)–(
7
) take the discrete forms

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\sigma}_{e}\;+\;\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\tau}_{e}\Big]-\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{f}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathrm{ext}}=\bm{0}\,,

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\zeta\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\mu}_{e}\;-\;\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\tau}_{e}\Big]-\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{m}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathrm{ext}}=\bm{0}\,,

(15)

where
w_{e}
is an integration weight. In these expressions,
\mathbf{N}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\chi}\times Nn_{\chi}}
is the shape function matrix for the nodal micro-deformation vector
\bm{\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu}
while
\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\varepsilon}\times Nn}
,
\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\chi}\times Nn_{\chi}}
, and
\mathbf{B}^{\zeta}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\zeta}\times Nn_{\chi}}
are standard discrete differential operators.

3.2
The data-driven problem

The data-driven problem requires a set-oriented reformulation of the discrete system described above. In this setting, the state of the solid at a material point
e
is defined in terms of generalized coordinates
\mathbf{z}_{e}
in a local phase space
\mathrm{Z}_{e}
. For the present micromorphic formulation, we have, in general,

\mathbf{z}_{e}\coloneqq(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e},\bm{\gamma}_{e},\bm{\zeta}_{e},\bm{\sigma}_{e},\bm{\tau}_{e},\bm{\mu}_{e})\in\mathrm{Z}_{e},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{Z}_{e}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\varepsilon}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\chi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\zeta}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\varepsilon}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\chi}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\zeta}}\,.

The local phase space is endowed with the following metric (cf. (
9
) with
\bm{\mathsf{E}}=\bm{0}
):

\|\mathbf{z}_{e}\|^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}\cdot\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}+\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}^{-1}\bm{\sigma}_{e}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{e}+\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\bm{\gamma}_{e}\cdot\bm{\gamma}_{e}+\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}^{-1}\bm{\tau}_{e}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{e}+\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{e}\bm{\zeta}_{e}\cdot\bm{\zeta}_{e}+\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{e}^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{e}\cdot\bm{\mu}_{e}\Big)\,.

(16)

Here,
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\varepsilon}\times n_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
,
\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\chi}\times n_{\chi}}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
, and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\zeta}\times n_{\zeta}}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
are positive-definite matrices of a purely numerical nature, in principle, unrelated to the material behavior. Globally, the local coordinates
\mathbf{z}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{z}_{e}\in\mathrm{Z}_{e}\}_{e=1}^{M}\in\mathrm{Z}
represent points in the global phase space
\mathrm{Z}=\mathrm{Z}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathrm{Z}_{M}
. In view of the discrete formulation of section
3.1
, the global distance metric reads

\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\|\mathbf{z}_{e}\|^{2}\,.

(17)

Setting the metric tensors at the same order of magnitude of the elasticity parameters of a reference material has shown to yield good convergence behavior
Eggersmann et al. 2021a
. However, using the secant moduli, identifiable from the data, seems to give better results during softening stages (section
4
). We further note that a more objective choice of metric tensors consists of evaluating optimal moduli
Karapiperis et al. 2021a
.

We proceed to define a subset of
\mathrm{Z}
containing all mechanically admissible states:

\mathrm{E}^{k+1}\coloneqq\Big\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{Z}\ \colon\ \text{\eqref{eq:epsilon_disc}--\eqref{eq:eqmu_disc} }\ \text{at }\ t=t^{k+1}\Big\}\,,

(18)

which may be viewed as a
generalized equilibrium set
evaluated at time
t^{k+1}
. On the other hand, rather than finding closure to the discrete system (
11
)–(
15
) by invoking constitutive relations (section
2.3
), we assume that the material response is encoded in a
material data set

\mathrm{D}=\mathrm{D}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathrm{D}_{M}
, where
\mathrm{D}_{e}\subset\mathrm{Z}_{e}
is the local data set containing generalized stress-strain coordinates accessible to the material point
e
. We account for history-dependent, inelastic responses by defining a suitable subset
\mathrm{D}^{k+1}\subset\mathrm{D}
that contains, at time
t^{k+1}
, data points compatible with certain constraints imposed on the stress-strain history
Eggersmann et al. 2019
. In this context, let us assume that a generic internal variable
\mathbf{q}
is appended to the data set, such that a realization
\mathbf{q}_{\,e}
is attached to every data point
\mathbf{z}_{e}\in\mathrm{D}_{e}
. Then, we consider a time-evolving data set evaluated at time
t^{k+1}
:

\mathrm{D}_{e}^{k+1}\coloneqq\Big\{\mathbf{z}_{e}\in\mathrm{D}_{e}\ \colon\ \Phi\big(\mathbf{q}_{\,e}^{k+1},\{(\bm{\varepsilon}_{e},\bm{\gamma}_{e},\bm{\zeta}_{e},\bm{\sigma}_{e},\bm{\tau}_{e},\bm{\mu}_{e})^{s}\}_{s\leq k+1}\big)\leq\bm{0}\Big\}\,,

(19)

where
\Phi
is a generic function establishing a constraint to be fulfilled by the generalized stress-strain history and the internal variable state. Then, an evolving global data set can be defined at time
t^{k+1}
as
\mathrm{D}^{k+1}=\mathrm{D}_{1}^{k+1}\times\dots\times\mathrm{D}_{M}^{k+1}
. It bears emphasis that the availability of an internal state variable
\mathbf{q}_{\,e}
does not entail the existence of a constitutive model; this variable must be measurable and included in the database alongside
\mathbf{z}_{e}\in\mathrm{D}_{e}
.
For instance, in recent works on data-driven computing for granular materials, LS-DEM simulations
Kawamoto et al. 2016
have been used by
Karapiperis et al. 2021a
to obtain energy quantities and enforce thermodynamic constraints, and by
Ulloa et al. 2023
to measure a breakage state variable and enforce irreversibility conditions in crushable media
.

With the above definitions in hand, the time-discrete data-driven problem may be written as

\inf_{\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{D}^{k+1}}\,\inf_{\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{E}^{k+1}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}\|^{2}=\inf_{\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{E}^{k+1}}\,\inf_{\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{D}^{k+1}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}\|^{2}\,.

(20)

This problem is typically solved using a fixed-point iteration scheme. Fixing an estimate for the mechanically admissible state
\mathbf{z}^{*}\in\mathrm{E}^{k+1}
, the problem

\mathbf{y}^{*}=\arginf_{\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{D}^{k+1}}\,\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z}^{*}\|^{2}

(21)

is solved using a local nearest neighbor search for the data points
\mathbf{y}_{e}\in\mathrm{D}_{e}^{k+1}
. Then, fixing the estimate
\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\mathrm{D}^{k+1}
, the problem

\mathbf{z}^{*}=\arginf_{\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{E}^{k+1}}\,\|\mathbf{y}^{*}-\mathbf{z}\|^{2}

(22)

is solved enforcing the mechanical constrains embedded in (
18
). The kinematics are enforced directly by replacing the generalized strains (
11
)–(
13
) in the local metric
\|\mathbf{z}_{e}-\mathbf{y}^{*}_{e}\|
, using equations (
16
)–(
15
). On the other hand, the balance equations (
14
)–(
15
) are enforced by means of Lagrange multipliers collected in vectors
\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{u}
and
\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}
. In light of equation (
17
), we obtain the Euler-Lagrange equations

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}\,\big(\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\bm{\varepsilon}_{e}^{*}\big)+\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\,\big(\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\mathbf{B}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\bm{\gamma}^{*}_{e}\big)\Big]=\bm{0}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\zeta\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{e}\,\big(\mathbf{B}^{\zeta}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\bm{\zeta}^{*}_{e}\big)-\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\,\big(\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\bm{\gamma}_{e}^{*}\big)\Big]=\bm{0}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\sigma}_{e}+\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\tau}_{e}\Big]-\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{f}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathrm{ext}}=\bm{0}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\zeta\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\mu}_{e}-\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\,\bm{\tau}_{e}\Big]-\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{m}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathrm{ext}}=\bm{0}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle w_{e}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}^{-1}\big(\bm{\sigma}_{e}-\bm{\sigma}^{*}_{e}\big)-w_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{u}=\bm{0}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle w_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}^{-1}\big(\bm{\tau}_{e}-\bm{\tau}^{*}_{e}\big)-w_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{u}+w_{e}\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}=\bm{0}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle w_{e}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{e}^{-1}\big(\bm{\mu}_{e}-\bm{\mu}^{*}_{e}\big)-w_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\zeta}_{e}\,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}=\bm{0}\,.

(29)

Upon substitution and rearrangement, we obtain the following linear system to be solved for
(\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu,\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{e}+\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\Big]\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{e}\Big]\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu=\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}\,\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}_{e}+\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\bm{\gamma}^{*}_{e}\Big],

(30)

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\Big]\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{u}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu-\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\zeta\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\zeta}_{e}+\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{e}\Big]\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu=\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\bm{\gamma}^{*}_{e}-\mathbf{B}^{\zeta\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{e}\,\bm{\zeta}^{*}_{e}\Big],

(31)

and the following linear system to be solved for
(\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{u},\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}})
:

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{e}+\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\mathbf{B}^{\gamma}_{e}\Big]\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{u}-\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{e}\mathbf{N}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{e}\Big]\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\bm{\lambda}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}=\mkern 1.2mu\underline{\mkern-1.2mu\mathbf{f}\mkern-1.2mu}\mkern 1.2mu^{\mathrm{ext}}-\sum_{e=1}^{M}w_{e}\Big[\mathbf{B}^{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\sigma}^{*}_{e}+\mathbf{B}^{\gamma\,\mathrm{T}}_{e}\bm{\tau}^{*}_{e}\Big],

(32)

4
Numerical simulations

This section presents representative numerical simulations that showcase the ability of data-driven micromorphic computing to capture responses with strain localization. For the sake of conciseness, we limit the study to synthetic data sampled from the solution of reference BVPs. These data are then employed in data-driven computations that aim to replicate the reference model response. Hence, the main challenge for the data-driven algorithm will be to assign suitable data to each material point.

In the examples that follow, a single data set is generated for all material points, such that
\mathrm{D}_{e}^{k+1}\equiv\mathrm{D}^{k+1}
, and the nearest neighbor search (
21
) is performed in a random order. Moreover, the history dependence of the material response is considered by imposing irreversibility conditions in the data set (
19
):

\mathrm{D}_{e}^{k+1}\coloneqq\Big\{\mathbf{z}_{e}\in\mathrm{D}_{e}\ \colon\ 0\leq q^{k+1}-q^{k}\leq\mathtt{TOL}_{q}\Big\}\,.

(37)

Here,
q
is an irreversible internal state variable, taken as the damage variable in sections
4.1
and
4.2
and as the equivalent plastic strain in section
4.3
. Moreover,
\mathtt{TOL}_{q}
is a tolerance parameter introduced to avoid large jumps in data sets that include loading-unloading and softening branches.

Remark 4.1
.

The data set parametrization (
37
) assumes access to the internal state variable
q
, computed from the reference model in the current numerical study. However, for the application of this framework to real material data, this irreversible quantity must be accessible through empirical measurements. In this context, a measure of energy dissipation, computed from grain-scale simulations of granular materials, has been effectively employed
Karapiperis et al. 2021a
. Additionally, a breakage state variable, measurable in experiments or grain-scale simulations, was used in a recent study
Ulloa et al. 2023
focusing on crushable granular media.

5
Conclusions and perspectives

We have presented a data-driven formulation for generalized micromorphic continua. Its enhanced capabilities compared to the standard Cauchy-continuum framework have been illustrated in problems with strain localization without assigning data sets to specific material points a priori. The response of a 1D softening bar, assumed to be governed by a gradient damage model, has been studied systematically. It is shown numerically that local minima found by the standard data-driven framework may capture the global response but not the spatial distribution of the state variables expected from the reference data. In contrast, the proposed framework captures both the global response and the generalized stress-strain profiles, effectively extracting the material length scale through the solution of the data-driven BVP. The framework has been further validated in 2D for a V-notched sample undergoing fracture in tension and a biaxial sample with shear-banding, showing, in both cases, accurate predictions.
These results suggest that data-driven micromorphic mechanics offers promising opportunities for capturing post-critical, inelastic behavior including both the global response and the failure mode. Moreover, the proposed framework is general, including any subcase of the full first-order micromorphic theory.

The study paves the way for subsequent developments on various fronts. Firstly, practical applications of the present framework (beyond the proof-of-concept, synthetic studies considered here) require access to stress-strain data from microstructured material responses, without resorting to a micromorphic constitutive model.
B
provides several possibilities in this direction. Moreover, the high-dimensional space of generalized stress-strain states poses additional challenges stemming from the
curse of dimensionality
. Hence, the present framework may benefit from numerical schemes with dimensionality reduction
He et al. 2021
,
Ibáñez et al. 2018
adapted to the micromorphic case. Moreover, improved numerical strategies, e.g., using tensor voting
Eggersmann et al. 2021b
, manifold learning
Bahmani and Sun 2022
, or adaptive sampling
Gorgogianni et al. 2023
may be instrumental for accuracy, particularly with limited material data. Finally, the present study does not delve into the data structure, using, at all times, a single data set for all material points. This approach serves as a proof of concept but is by no means efficient. More dedicated techniques that efficiently navigate through generalized stress-strain data may be envisaged, perhaps adopting a clustering approach
Eggersmann et al. 2021a
or quantum computing
Xu et al. 2024
.

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\|\mathrm{tr}\,\dot{\bm{\zeta}}^{\mathrm{p}}\|}{3a_{4}A_{\theta}}\big[a_{4}(A_{\theta}-A_{\varphi})\|\mathrm{tr}\,\bm{\mu}\|+a_{6}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}\big]&\text{if}\quad\|\mathrm{tr}\,\dot{\bm{\zeta}}^{\mathrm{p}}\|\geq\frac{a_{4}}{a_{3}}\sqrt{6}A_{\theta}\|\mathrm{dev}\,\dot{\bm{\zeta}}^{\mathrm{p}}\|\,,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

With these functions in hand, one can show that the energy balance equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(\bm{u},\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}^{\mathrm{p}})+\mathcal{R}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}}^{\mathrm{p}};\bm{\sigma})-\int_{\Gamma^{u}_{\mathrm{N}}}\bar{\bm{t}}\cdot\dot{\bm{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S-\int_{\Gamma^{u}_{\mathrm{D}}}\bm{t}\cdot\dot{\bar{\bm{u}}}\,\mathrm{d}S=0

(58)

and the first-order stability condition

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{E}(\bm{u},\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}^{\mathrm{p}})(\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\tilde{\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}}^{\mathrm{p}})+\mathcal{R}(\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\tilde{\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}}^{\mathrm{p}};\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu})-\int_{\Gamma^{u}_{\mathrm{N}}}\bar{\bm{t}}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S\geq 0\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\{\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\tilde{\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}}^{\mathrm{p}}\}\in\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\times\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\times\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\times\tilde{\mathcal{Y}},

(59)

where

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

,
yield the following governing equations in strong form:

Stress equilibrium

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dive\,(\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\tau})=\bm{0}\ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega\,,\\
(\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\tau})\cdot\bm{n}=\bar{\bm{t}}\ \ \text{on}\ \ \Gamma^{u}_{\mathrm{N}}\,;\end{cases}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dive\,\bm{\mu}+\bm{\tau}=\bm{0}\ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega\,,\\

(61)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f^{\varepsilon}(\bm{\sigma})\leq 0,\quad f^{\gamma}(\bm{\tau})\leq 0,\quad f^{\zeta}(\bm{\mu})\leq 0\ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega\,,\\

Appendix B
On (generalized) stress-strain data acquisition

The apparent need for a generalized framework to capture responses with strain localization raises the natural question of whether the required generalized stress-strain data can be obtained from microstructured material responses in a practical scenario, i.e., without resorting to synthetic data from micromorphic constitutive models. We argue that homogenization towards generalized continua can be applied in concert with the present framework in applications to specific material systems. We discuss various possibilities below, compiling relevant references on the topic.

B.1
From heterogeneous standard materials to generalized continua

We first consider scenarios where the microstructure is described by standard, e.g., Cauchy-based, models, while the effective behavior is captured by generalized continua. Examples are heterogeneous materials with inclusions or voids and a weak separation of scales. For these cases, the link between the (classical and heterogeneous) microscopic behavior and the (generalized and homogeneous) macroscopic state has been established in several studies that can be taken as a point of departure.

Depending on the application, we may pivot towards micropolar or second-gradient continua. In both cases, homogenization schemes are available to obtain generalized stress-strain states from the microscopic response. The homogenization of Cauchy-based heterogeneous materials towards micropolar continua has been addressed in several works
Forest and Sab 1998
,
Feyel 2003
,
De Bellis and Addessi 2011
, which provide effective micropolar stress-strain states from the response of representative volumes. The same applies to second-gradient continua, for which second-order homogenization
Kouznetsova et al. 2002
,
Kouznetsova et al. 2004
,
Nguyen et al. 2013
schemes are available. In this case, we must deal with the requirement of
\mathrm{C}^{1}
continuity in the numerical implementation.

Of course, the present work addresses the more general case of micromorphic mechanics. In this broader setting, we may rely on several developments on the homogenization of heterogeneous materials towards micromorphic continua, some of which are listed below.

•

An early contribution to micromorphic homogenization is found in the work of
Forest 2002
. Therein, kinematic averaging rules result from minimizing the difference between the actual, microscopic displacement
\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})
and the approximating ansatz
\bm{u}(\bm{x})+\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{\xi}
(cf. figure
1
):

\min_{(\bm{u},\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}})}\Big\langle\|\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})-\bm{u}(\bm{x})-\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{\xi}\|^{2}\Big\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\,,

(63)

leading to

\bm{u}(\bm{x})=\big\langle\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\big\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}},\hskip 20.00003pt\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}(\bm{x})=\big\langle\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\otimes\bm{\xi}\big\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\cdot\big\langle\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}\big\rangle^{-1}_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\,,

(64)

from which the gradients
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{u}
and
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}
readily follow. The microdeformation is identified as the first moment of the microscopic displacement. An immediate issue is that these kinematic volume averages cannot be written as surface integrals, hindering the macro-micro transition, i.e., the possibility of applying the kinematic quantities as boundary conditions in representative volumes. This problem can be addressed by adopting polynomial ansatzes
Forest and Trinh 2011
,
Jänicke et al. 2009
based on certain ad hoc assumptions. A more consistent approach can be found in
Jänicke and Steeb 2012
, employing the concept of
minimal loading conditions
, where the averaging relations descending from (
63
) (and the corresponding gradients) are imposed as global constraints in the microscale BVP. The corresponding generalized stresses follow as the Lagrange multipliers of the constrained BVP
Hütter 2019
.

•

While the frameworks above follow the micromorphic theory based on the principle of virtual power
Mindlin 1964
,
Germain 1973a
, an alternative framework is found in
Hütter 2017
,
Hütter 2019
, which takes, instead, the averaging-based micromorphic formulation of
Eringen 1968
as the point of departure. In particular, noting that
Eringen 1968
did not delve into the kinematics and did not provide explicit expressions for the average kinetics,
Hütter 2017
derived consistently the following averaging rules:

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})=\big\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\big\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}|}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{n}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{\xi})\otimes\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}S-\big\langle\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\big\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\,,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}|}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{n}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{\xi})\otimes\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}S\,,

with
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{m}}
the standard Cauchy stress tensor at the microscale and
\bm{n}^{\mathrm{m}}
the unit normal at
\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}
. The kinematic averaging rules are identical to equation (
64
) and the gradients thereof, to be imposed as
global constraints
, adopting the concept of minimal loading conditions
Jänicke and Steeb 2012
.

•

The previous approach is not suitable for materials with voids because it relies on kinematic volume constraints, requiring the microscopic displacement field to be defined everywhere inside the RVE. Indeed, in the microscale BVP, the generalized stresses appear as volume forces, which cannot be carried by voids. For such cases,
Hütter 2019
proposed to replace the standard volume averaging
\langle\Box\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}
in equation (
64
) with a modified kinematic averaging operator

\big\langle\Box\big\rangle_{\mathrm{M}}\coloneq\frac{1}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}|\big\langle H_{\mathrm{M}}(\bm{\xi})\big\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}}H_{\mathrm{M}}(\bm{\xi})\,\Box\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\,,

(66)

where
H_{\mathrm{M}}
is a weighting function that vanishes inside the voids. In this way, a scheme fully compatible with FE
2
is established for various applications.

•

Another approach presented in
Alavi et al. 2021
employs a quartic expansion for the microscopic displacement field. This method is able to establish surface integrals for all kinematic and kinetic quantities from variational principles.

•

A different framework, suitable for composites with soft inclusions or voids, is found in
Biswas and Poh 2017
. Therein, the microdeformation tensor is defined as the average distortion within the microheterogeneities, taking the form of a surface integral over internal interfaces:

\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}(\bm{x})=\big\langle\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\big\rangle_{\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}|}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}}\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\otimes\bm{n}^{\mathrm{i}}(\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}S\,,

(67)

where
\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}\subset\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}
is the domain covered by inclusions or voids. If this condition is imposed in the microscale BVP as

\min_{(\bm{u},\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}})}\bigg\{\int_{\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\sigma^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi}):\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x}):\bigg(\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}|}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}}\bm{v}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\otimes\bm{n}^{\mathrm{i}}(\bm{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}S\bigg)\bigg\}\,,

(68)

together with suitable boundary conditions, the generalized stresses follow as

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}|}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{n}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{\xi})\otimes\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}S\,+\,\frac{|\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}|}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}|}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x})\,,

(69)

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}(\bm{x})=-\frac{|\Omega^{\mathrm{i}}|}{|\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}|}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{x})\,,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2|\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}|}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{m}}}\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{n}^{\mathrm{m}}(\bm{\xi})\otimes\bm{\xi}\otimes\bm{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}S\,,

encompassing a clear computational strategy that yields micromorphic stress-strain states from composite microstructures. The method has been used successfully in an FE
2
setting
Biswas et al. 2019
,
Zhi et al. 2022
and applied to tetra-chiral structures
Biswas et al. 2020
.

•

Lastly, the method proposed by
Rokoš et al. 2019
for structures involving pattern transformations deserves mention. The authors incorporate a predefined fluctuation field in the kinematic ansatz, considering the main microstructural deformation modes. As a result, a homogenized micromorphic continuum naturally emerges at the macroscale.

B.2
From discrete material systems to generalized continua

In addition to the homogenization schemes described above, it is important to note that the homogenization of microstructured materials with inherent rotational degrees of freedom naturally yields effective micropolar quantities, e.g., in cellular and metamaterials
Chen et al. 1998
or granular media
Ehlers et al. 2003
. Indeed, homogenized micropolar stress-strain states were applied recently in data-driven computing
Karapiperis et al. 2021a
. Furthermore, homogenization from discrete systems has also been shown to yield micromorphic effective states
Regueiro et al. 2015
,
Misra and Poorsolhjouy 2015
,
Misra et al. 2020
,
Ehlers and Bidier 2020
, which can be employed for data generation in related applications.

B.3
Data-driven identification

Another interesting opportunity arising from the present work, in the context of data sampling, is to extend the recent data-driven identification framework of
Stainier et al. 2019
to micromorphic continua (or subcases thereof). In this setting, a data-driven problem supplied with generalized strain data and enforced to comply with the associated balance equations is expected to yield the corresponding generalized stresses. Therefore, a framework of this type is an excellent candidate for data acquisition.

In summary, several opportunities exist for extracting generalized stress-strain states from microstructured material responses, allowing the generation of the required datasets for our current data-driven scheme. Therefore, future developments should focus on implementing homogenization schemes or data-driven identification methods for data acquisition in applications specific to material systems.
