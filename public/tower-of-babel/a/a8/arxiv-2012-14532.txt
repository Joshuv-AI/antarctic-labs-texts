Average-distance problem with curvature penalization for data parameterization: regularity of minimizers
Xin Yang Lu, Dejan Slepčev

Abstract
. We propose a model for finding one-dimensional structure in a given measure. Our approach is based on minimizing an objective functional which combines the average-distance functional to measure the quality of the approximation and penalizes the curvature, similarly to the elastica functional. Introducing the curvature penalization overcomes some of the shortcomings of the average-distance functional, in particular the lack of regularity of minimizers. We establish existence, uniqueness and regularity of minimizers of the proposed functional. In particular we establish C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} estimates on the minimizers.

It is often of interest to use even simpler objects to approximate measures, namely parameterized curves.
Let

We should point out that the curvature penalty was actually already introduced in the elastica functional, which is one of the earliest functionals of the calculus of variations. The elastica problem was proposed in 1691 by Jacob Bernoulli, who studied
“the bendings or curvatures of beams, drawn bows, or of springs of any kind, caused
by their own weight or by an attached weight or by any other compressing forces….”,
[
21
]
, page 17.
Indeed, if the measure

is purely atomic, then the term

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}d(x,\Gamma_{\varphi})^{p}\,{\operatorname{d}}\mu
is a type of “pulling” force on the minimizer,
while length (resp. curvature) term

(resp.
\int_{0}^{L(\varphi)}|\kappa_{\varphi}|^{2}\,{\operatorname{d}}\mathcal{L}^{1}
)
account for the elastic forces.
The elastica problem was studied by Bernoulli
[
1
]
who provided a partial solution.
Subsequently, Euler described in
[
18
]
two radically different solutions. Both of these works played a fundamental role in the history of the calculus of variations and their influence extends to this day. For
a comprehensive look at the history of elastica problem see the article of Levien
[

and the doctoral thesis of Goss
[

]
.

Problem
1.3
is also related to the notion of splines
[
12
]
. In particular the cubic smoothing spline for the given collection of data points
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\>:\>x_{i}\in[0,1],\;y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}
where
x_{i}<x_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\dots,n-1

is the function

minimizing

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f(x_{i}))^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime\prime}(x)dx.

Between the points

and
x_{i+1}
the function

is a cubic polynomial, while at the points
x_{i}
,

is differentiable, but the second derivative typically has a jump.
As in our problem
\varepsilon
controls the level of smoothness. In the
\varepsilon\to\infty
limit, the problem converges to the best linear fit, while in the
\varepsilon\to 0+
limit the solutions converge to the interpolating cubic spline.

The main difference compared to our problem is that if one considers splines as curves in 2D,
x\mapsto(x,f(x))
then one coordinate,
x
is given a-priory, and it already specifies the order in which the points are visited. The lack of information/requirement about the order in which the data points are to be approximated is a major source of complication for our problem. Among other things it is the reason why one needs to add the length penalty, as otherwise the problem does not have a minimizer.

\Gamma_{\gamma}
is not a singleton. Without loss of generality,
we can assume

\inf_{n}|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|>0
. This because by assumption
we have
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
uniformly.
As
\gamma_{n}
are arc-length parameterized,
|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|
is equal to its length

, with
\mathcal{H}^{1}
denoting the Hausdorff-1 measure.
By the lower semicontinuity of

(Golab’s theorem) we then have

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma_{n})\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)>0

Note:

(1)

\{\gamma_{n}\}\to\gamma
uniformly, i.e.
\{\gamma_{n}\}

is bounded in
L^{\infty}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(2)

since the speed of
\{\gamma_{n}\}
is equal to
|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|
a.e.,
hypothesis
\sup_{n}|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|<+\infty
gives that
\{\gamma_{n}\}
is bounded in
W^{1,\infty}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})

(3)

the curvature

describes the variation of the unit tangent derivative,
and absolute continuity of
\kappa_{\gamma_{n}}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure (which follows from

\kappa_{\gamma_{n}}=|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|^{-1}\frac{\,{\operatorname{d}}}{\,{\operatorname{d}}s}\Big(\frac{\gamma_{n}^{\prime}}{|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|}\Big)=|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|^{-1}\frac{\gamma_{n}^{\prime\prime}}{|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|}.

Since
\inf_{n}|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|>0
, (

) implies that

\{\gamma_{n}\}
is bounded in
W^{2,2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Thus there exists
\tilde{\gamma}\in W^{2,2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a subsequence
\{\gamma_{n_{k}}\}

converging to
\tilde{\gamma}
in the weak topology of
W^{2,2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since the embedding

is compact,

\{\gamma_{n_{k}}\}
converges to
\tilde{\gamma}
strongly in
W^{1,2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})

hence
\{\gamma_{n_{k}}\}
converges to
\tilde{\gamma}
strongly in

Since by hypothesis
\{\gamma_{n_{k}}\}
converges to
\gamma
strongly in

it follows
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma
. Strong convergence of

\gamma

implies

L(\gamma_{n_{k}})=\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma_{n_{k}}^{\prime}|\,{\operatorname{d}}s\to\int_{0}^{1}|\gamma^{\prime}|\,{\operatorname{d}}s=L(\gamma).

To achieve convergence for the whole sequence, note that if there exists
a subsequence
\{\gamma_{n_{h}}\}
such that
\lim_{h\to+\infty}L(\gamma_{n_{h}})\neq L(\gamma)
,
then the above construction allows to further extract a sub-subsequence

\{\gamma_{n_{h(j)}}\}
converging to
\gamma
in the strong topology of

,
which in turn implies
\lim_{j\to+\infty}L(\gamma_{n_{h(j)}})=L(\gamma)
.
Contradiction.

∎

{\displaystyle{\sup_{n}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\kappa_{\varphi_{n}^{*}}\right|^{2}\,{\operatorname{d}}\mathcal{L}^{1}<+\infty}}

3.
Regularity

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.4
.
As a consequence, we have Corollaries
3.3

and
3.4
, which estimate (for minimizers) the mass projecting on a subset
in relation to its length. This proves that Problem
1.3

is effectively a better candidate for application in data parameterization
than Problem
1.2
, since it does not exhibit the undesirable
properties described in (
*
) and (
**
). The results are proven for
generic (finite) dimension. However
while
Theorem
1.4
is proven for generic measures,
Corollaries
3.3
and
3.4
are restricted to
absolutely continuous measures.

We first prove a weaker regularity result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given
d\geq 2
, a finite measure
\mu\geq 0
compactly supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and parameters
\lambda,\varepsilon>0
,
p\geq 1
,
any minimizer
\varphi\in\argmin_{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{E}_{\mu}^{\lambda,\varepsilon,p}
is
C^{1,1/2}
regular with

\big(\forall t_{0},t_{1}\in[0,L(\varphi)]\big)\qquad|\varphi^{\prime}(t_{1})-\varphi^{\prime}(t_{0})|\leq\left(\frac{\big(\!\diam\supp(\mu)\big)^{p}}{\varepsilon}|t_{1}-t_{0}|\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be an arbitrary minimizer.
Definition (
1.2
) requires that
\varphi\in H^{2}([0,L(\varphi)];\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
which,
by Sobolev embedding,
implies that
\varphi^{\prime}
is continuous.

Thus

\varphi
is
C^{1}
regular.
Hölder inequality gives

\displaystyle\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(t_{1})-\varphi^{\prime}(t_{0})|^{2}}{|t_{1}-t_{0}|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|t_{1}-t_{0}|}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\kappa_{\varphi}|\,{\operatorname{d}}\mathcal{L}^{1}\right)^{2}\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\kappa_{\varphi}|^{2}\,{\operatorname{d}}\mathcal{L}^{1}{\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mu}^{\lambda,\varepsilon,p}(\varphi)}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\big(\!\diam\supp(\mu)\big)^{p}}{\varepsilon},}

with the last inequality due to the fact that
\varphi
has no more energy
compared to any singleton
\psi:\{0\}\longrightarrow\{z\}
,

, i.e.

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}^{\lambda,\varepsilon,p}(\varphi)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mu}^{\lambda,\varepsilon,p}(\psi)\leq\int_{\supp(\mu)}|x-z|^{p}\,{\operatorname{d}}\mu(x)\leq\diam(\supp(\mu))^{p}.

The proof is thus complete.
∎

Note that the proof of Lemma
3.1
is quite simple, and
consists of comparing the curvature term with condition (
2.2
),
without any kind of construction. As one may expect,
this kind of proof can be refined, and stronger regularity results
can be established.

3.1.
Lipschitz regularity of derivatives

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}|\varphi^{\prime}(t^{*}+h)-\varphi^{\prime}(t)|\leq Y.

|P-\varphi(t_{0})|\leq D\end{subarray}}\gamma_{P}([t_{0},t_{1}]).

To estimate the length of the trajectory

, direct computation gives

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\gamma_{P}^{\prime}(t)|\,{\operatorname{d}}t

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left|\varphi^{\prime}(t)+\sum_{j=2}^{d}P_{j}\left.\frac{\,{\operatorname{d}}}{\,{\operatorname{d}}s}X_{s}e_{j}\right|_{s=t}\right|\,{\operatorname{d}}t\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|\,{\operatorname{d}}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left|\sum_{j=2}^{d}P_{j}\left.\frac{\,{\operatorname{d}}}{\,{\operatorname{d}}s}X_{s}e_{j}\right|_{s=t}\right|\,{\operatorname{d}}t

\displaystyle=\xi+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\sqrt{\sum_{j=2}^{d}P_{j}^{2}\left|\left.\frac{\,{\operatorname{d}}}{\,{\operatorname{d}}s}X_{s}\right|_{s=t}\right|^{2}}\,{\operatorname{d}}t\overset{\eqref{est j}}{\leq}\xi+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\sqrt{Y^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{d}P_{j}^{2}}\ \,{\operatorname{d}}t

\displaystyle=\xi+\xi|P-\varphi(t_{0})|Y.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{d}\Bigg(\supp(\mu)\cap\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\Delta_{t}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{P\in\Delta_{t_{0}}:|P-\varphi(t_{0})|\leq D\}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\gamma_{P}^{\prime}(t)|\,{\operatorname{d}}t\,{\operatorname{d}}P

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{est traj}}{\leq}\xi\int_{\{v\in\Delta_{t_{0}}:|P-\varphi(t_{0})|\leq D\}}\left(1+|P-\varphi(t_{0})|Y\right)\,{\operatorname{d}}P

\displaystyle=\xi\int_{0}^{D}\int_{\{P\in\Delta_{t_{0}}:|P-\varphi(t_{0})|=r\}}\left(1+rY\right)\,{\operatorname{d}}P\,{\operatorname{d}}r

\displaystyle=\xi\int_{0}^{D}\omega_{d-1}r^{d-1}\left(1+rY\right)\,{\operatorname{d}}r

\displaystyle=\xi\omega_{d-1}\left(\frac{D^{d}}{d}+\frac{D^{d+1}}{d+1}Y\right).

\displaystyle\mu\left(\supp(\mu)\cap\bigcup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\Delta_{t}\right)

concluding the proof.

4.
Appendix: an ode for curvature.

Here we derive the Euler-Lagrange equation for the energy
\mathcal{E}
, when the reference measure

\mu
has finitely many atoms.
Let
\gamma:[0,L]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an arc-length parameterized minimizer of
\mathcal{E}^{\lambda,\varepsilon,p}_{\mu}
.
Let
\varphi:[0,L]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{2}
regular perturbation.
We show that, in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
the Euler-Lagrange equation is up to parameters, the same as the Euler-Lagrange equation for elastica functional,
[
26
]
:

\kappa^{\prime\prime}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}C_{1}}\kappa^{3}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}C_{2}}\kappa=0.

This is expected, as minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
are
“elastica-like”.

We recall that elastica curves are solutions of the so-called “elastica problem”,
one of the earliest examples of nonlinear displacement problems,
first proposed by Bernoulli in 1691.
There the integrated squared curvature quantifies the elastic stress, while the average-distance term describes the “pulling force” of the weight hanging
on the elastica.

We first estimate the first variation of the curvature term

K(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{L}|\gamma^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\,{\operatorname{d}}s
.
Given
h>0
,

K(\gamma+h\varphi)-K(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{L}|\gamma^{\prime}+h\varphi^{\prime}|^{-1}\bigg|\frac{\,{\operatorname{d}}}{\,{\operatorname{d}}s}\bigg(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}+h\varphi^{\prime}}{|\gamma^{\prime}+h\varphi^{\prime}|}\bigg)\bigg|^{2}\,{\operatorname{d}}s.

Direct computation gives

\displaystyle\frac{\,{\operatorname{d}}}{\,{\operatorname{d}}h}K(\gamma+h\varphi)\Bigg|_{h=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\Big[-\langle\gamma^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime}\rangle|\gamma^{\prime\prime}|^{2}+2\Big\langle\gamma^{\prime\prime},\varphi^{\prime\prime}-\gamma^{\prime\prime}\langle\gamma^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime}\rangle-\langle\varphi^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime}\rangle\gamma^{\prime}-\langle\varphi^{\prime\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle\gamma^{\prime}\Big\rangle\Big]\,{\operatorname{d}}s

(4.1)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}[3(|\gamma^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}+2\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}]\varphi\,{\operatorname{d}}s+2\gamma^{\prime\prime}\varphi^{\prime\prime}\bigg|_{0}^{L},

Now we estimate the first variation of the length:

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{0}^{L}[|\gamma^{\prime}+h\varphi^{\prime}|-|\gamma^{\prime}|]\,{\operatorname{d}}x

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{L}\frac{h\gamma^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime}+O(h^{2})}{|\gamma^{\prime}+h\varphi^{\prime}|+|\gamma^{\prime}|}\,{\operatorname{d}}x.

Without loss of generality, we take
\gamma
to be arc-length parameterized, i.e.

|\gamma^{\prime}|=1
a.e.
Dividing by
h
and then taking the limit
h\to 0
gives

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\gamma^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime}}{2|\gamma^{\prime}|}\,{\operatorname{d}}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\gamma^{\prime\prime}\varphi\,{\operatorname{d}}x+\frac{\lambda}{2}\gamma^{\prime\prime}\varphi\bigg|_{0}^{L}.

Moreover,
recall that all the masses projected on the knots, hence the
average distance term remains unchanged.
Combining with (
4.1
) and
considering perturbations
\varphi
which are compactly supported within
(0,L)
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}\Big[\frac{\lambda}{2}\gamma^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon[3(|\gamma^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}+2\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}]\Big]\varphi\,{\operatorname{d}}x=0,

i.e.,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\gamma^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon[3(|\gamma^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\gamma^{\prime})^{\prime}+2\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}]=\frac{\lambda}{2}\gamma^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon[3(2\langle\gamma^{\prime\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}\rangle\gamma^{\prime}+|\gamma^{\prime\prime}|^{2}\gamma^{\prime\prime})+2\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}]=0.

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, using Frenet-Serret formulas, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}=\overset{\to}{t},

\displaystyle\qquad\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\kappa\overset{\to}{n},

(4.4)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}=\kappa^{\prime}\overset{\to}{n}-\kappa^{2}\overset{\to}{t},

\displaystyle\qquad\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}=\kappa^{\prime\prime}\overset{\to}{n}-3\kappa\kappa^{\prime}\overset{\to}{t}-\kappa^{3}\overset{\to}{n},

hence (
4.2
) becomes

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}\kappa\overset{\to}{n}+\varepsilon\Big[6\langle\kappa\overset{\to}{n},\kappa^{\prime}\overset{\to}{n}-\kappa^{2}\overset{\to}{t}\rangle\overset{\to}{t}+2(\kappa^{\prime\prime}\overset{\to}{n}-3\kappa\kappa^{\prime}\overset{\to}{t}-\kappa^{3}\overset{\to}{n})\Big]=\Big(\frac{\lambda}{2}\kappa+2\varepsilon[\kappa^{\prime\prime}-\kappa^{3}]\Big)\overset{\to}{n},

i.e.

\frac{\lambda}{2}\kappa+2\varepsilon[\kappa^{\prime\prime}-\kappa^{3}]=0.

Finally, note the segment

\gamma((t_{0},t_{1}))
minimizes the (sum of) length and curvature terms, i.e.

\displaystyle\gamma_{|(t_{0},t_{1})}\in\argmin

\displaystyle\bigg\{\lambda L(\sigma)+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{L(\sigma)}|\kappa_{\sigma}|^{2}\,{\operatorname{d}}s:

\displaystyle\;\;\,\sigma\text{ is an arc-length parameterized curve with endpoints }\gamma(t_{0})\text{ and }\gamma(t_{1})\bigg\}.
