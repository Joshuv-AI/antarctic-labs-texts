Central Limit Theorem for Gram-Schmidt Random Walk Design
Sabyasachi Chatterjeelabel=e1]sc1706@illinois.edu, Partha S. Deylabel=e2]psdey@illinois.edu, Subhajit Goswamilabel=e3]goswami@math.tifr.res.in

Abstract
We prove a central limit theorem for the Horvitz–Thompson estimator based on the Gram–Schmidt Walk (GSW) design, recently developed in Harshaw et al. 2022 . In particular, we consider the version of the GSW design, which uses randomized pivot order , thereby answering an open question raised in the same article. We deduce this under minimal and global assumptions involving only the problem parameters, such as the (sum) potential outcome vector and the covariate matrix. As an interesting consequence of our analysis, we also obtain the precise limiting variance of the estimator in terms of these parameters, which is smaller than the previously known upper bound. The main ingredients are a simplified skeletal process approximating the GSW design and concentration phenomena for random matrices obtained from random sampling using Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs.

Introduction

We are interested in the statistical problem of estimating the
average treatment effect
(ATE).
This is one of the canonical problems in causal inference, and provides valuable insights into the effectiveness or impact of a particular treatment or intervention. The setup is the following.

Suppose there are
n
units or individuals with each individual
i
having two
potential outcomes

a_{i}
and
b_{i}
corresponding to two possible treatments. We can think of
a_{i}
and

b_{i}
as the responses of the individual
i
that we would have observed if we had administered treatment
A
or
B
(respectively) to that individual. We will denote the ATE by
\tau
, defined as

\tau=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-b_{i}).

(1.1)

Let us set up some notations to be used throughout. Let
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the two
potential outcome vectors and
\bm{\mu}\coloneqq\bm{a}+\bm{b}
be the
sum potential outcome
vector
.

1.1
Horvitz–Thompson Estimator

One of the classical methods to estimate the ATE is the
Horvitz–Thompson estimator
.
Let
z_{i}=\pm 1
denote whether treatment
A
or
B
is administered. The vector
\bm{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is called the
design vector
. It then just estimates the ATE by
taking the difference of the empirical means of the observed potential outcomes, namely,

\widehat{\tau}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i:z_{i}=+1}\frac{a_{i}}{\mathbb{P}[z_{i}=1]}-\sum_{i:z_{i}=-1}\frac{b_{i}}{\mathbb{P}[z_{i}=-1]}\right).

It is well known (see
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
) that

\widehat{\tau}
is unbiased
if
\mathbb{P}[z_{i}=1]\in(0,1)
for all units
i\in[n]
. In this paper, we consider designs where each unit is equally likely to receive either treatment,
i.e.,
,
\mathbb{P}[z_{i}=1]=\mathbb{P}[z_{i}=-1]=\tfrac{1}{2}
. The
distribution of
\widehat{\tau}
clearly depends on the design
\bm{z}
. One choice for
\bm{z}
is the i.i.d.
design where the variables
z_{i}
’s i.i.d. Rademacher variables. It is not hard to check (see
e.g.,

(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Lemma 1.2)
) that the Horvitz–Thompson estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm Rad}}
based on the i.i.d. design has variance given by

\E[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm Rad}}-\tau]^{2}=\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm Rad}}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}.

(1.2)

The setting that we are interested in here is when in addition to our observation of one of the potential
outcomes
a_{i}
or
b_{i}
for the
i
-th unit, we also observe a covariate vector
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
each unit
i\in[n].
A natural question that arises at this point is

whether it is possible to use the information in the covariates to improve the Horvitz–Thompson Estimator?

1.2
Gram–Schmidt Walk Design

Recently,
Harshaw
et al. 2022
proposed a solution to the above-posed question by
using the Gram–Schmidt Walk algorithm from
Bansal
et al. 2018
to change the sampling
strategy of the design vector
\bm{z}
. This algorithm is reviewed in Section
1.3
. The authors obtain a random vector
\bm{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
(final output of the so-called Gram–Schmidt walk), which no longer consists of i.i.d. Rademacher entries. However, they consider the same exact form of the Horvitz–Thompson estimator as in (
1.1
). They show the following mean squared error bound for their estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{{{\rm gs}}}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Theorem 4.1)

Let
X
be an
n\times d
covariate matrix with rows
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Let
\xi:=\max_{i\in[n]}\left\lVert\bm{x}_{i}\right\rVert
and
\phi\in(0,1)
be an algorithm parameter, called the robustness parameter, fixed
beforehand. Then, for the Horvitz–Thompson estimator based on the GSW design with parameter

\phi
, we have the following upper bound on the mean squared error:

n\E[\widehat{\tau}_{{{\rm gs}}}-\tau]^{2}\leqslant\inf_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\phi n}\|\bm{\mu}-X\bm{\beta}\|^{2}+\frac{\xi^{2}\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}}{(1-\phi)n}\right).

(1.3)

Suppose we choose
\bm{\beta}=\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}
such that
X\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}=\proj_{{\rm ColSp}(X)}(\bm{\mu})
, where
\proj_{\colsp(X)}
is the (orthogonal) projector
onto the column space of
X
. The first term on the right-hand side above will then scale like

\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{\mu}-\proj_{\colsp(X)}(\bm{\mu})\|^{2}

which is typically
\Theta(1)
unless the (sum potential) outcome vector
\bm{\mu}
lies “too close” to
\colsp(X)
.
Also we can expect
\xi^{2}
and
\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}
to be
O(d)
as they are norms of
d
-dimensional
vectors. Therefore, the second-term scales
like
O({d^{2}}/{n})
which, if
d=o(\sqrt{n})
, is a lower order term than the first term. Therefore, in
such a regime, the above theorem ensures that the Horvitz–Thompson estimator

\widehat{\tau}_{{{\rm gs}}}
based on the Gram–Schmidt Walk design (setting
\phi
very close to
1
)
satisfies the two qualitative properties when compared to
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm Rad}}

(see (
1.2
) above).

1.

If the covariates are predictive of the outcome vector
\bm{\mu}
,
i.e.,
\|\bm{\mu}-\proj_{\colsp(X)}(\bm{\mu})\|^{2}
is significantly smaller than
\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}
then

\widehat{\tau}_{{{\rm gs}}}
has significantly smaller mean squared error than
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm Rad}}
.

2.

However, even if the covariates are not predictive of
\bm{\mu}
, since
\|\bm{\mu}-\proj_{\colsp(X)}(\bm{\mu})\|^{2}
is bounded by
\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}
and the second term in the
right-hand side of (
1.3
) is negligible compared to the first term, mean squared error of
\widehat{\tau}_{{{\rm gs}}}
is never too much greater than the mean squared error of
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm Rad}}.

The first property has been termed as
Covariate Balance
and the second property has been
termed as
Robustness
in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
. In this way, the Horvitz–Thompson
estimator using the Gram–Schmidt Walk (GSW) design achieves both covariate balance and robustness. It
has also been argued in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
that the GSW design enjoys
certain advantages over other existing design approaches in the causal inference literature such as
rerandomization and designs based on matching pairs. Overall, it is probably fair to say that the GSW
design, although a recent entrant to the causal inference design toolbox, has already become one of
its prominent tools. In the next section, we review the GSW design algorithm;
see
Bansal
et al. 2018
,
Harshaw
et al. 2022
for more detailed discussions on the
algorithm.

1.3
Review of Gram–Schmidt walk algorithm

We briefly describe the Gram–Schmidt random walk design in this section using the so-called
randomized pivot ordering
. See Section 3 and Section A1.1 in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
for
further details. Define

\mathbf{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\phi}I_{n}\\
\xi^{-1}\sqrt{1-\phi}X^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}

(1.4)

where
\xi\coloneqq\max_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{x}_{i}\|
. We start with
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{{{\rm gs}}}\coloneqq[n]
,
\bm{z}^{{{\rm gs}}}_{0}=\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The algorithm at round
t\in[n]
.

1.

If
p^{{{\rm gs}}}_{t-1}\notin\mathcal{A}_{t}
, choose a pivot
p_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}
uniformly at random from
\mathcal{A}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}
, otherwise set
p_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}=p_{t-1}^{{{\rm gs}}}
.

2.

Compute a
step direction

\bm{u}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\begin{split}\bm{u}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}\leftarrow\argmin_{\bm{u}}\>\>\>\>\>\>\>&\|\mathbf{B}\bm{u}\|^{2}\\
\mbox{subject to }\>&\bm{u}[i]=0\mbox{ for all }i\notin\mathcal{A}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}\\
&\bm{u}[p_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}]=1.\end{split}

3.

Setting

\Delta\coloneqq\{\delta\in\mathbb{R}:\bm{z}^{{{\rm gs}}}_{t-1}+\delta\bm{u}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}\in[-1,1]^{n}\},

let
\delta^{+}\coloneqq|\sup\Delta|
and
\delta^{-}\coloneqq|\inf\Delta|
. Next define
\delta_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}
as follows:

\delta_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}=\begin{cases}\delta^{+}\>\>\text{with probability}\>\>\frac{\delta^{-}}{\delta^{+}+\delta^{-}}\\
\delta^{-}\>\>\text{with probability}\>\>\frac{\delta^{+}}{\delta^{+}+\delta^{-}}\end{cases}

4.

Now update

\bm{z}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}=\bm{z}_{t-1}^{{{\rm gs}}}+\delta_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}\bm{u}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}

(1.5)

and
\mathcal{A}_{t+1}^{{{\rm gs}}}=\mathcal{A}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}\setminus\{i\in[n]:|\bm{z}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}[i]|<1\}
.

5.

Increment the index
t\leftarrow t+1
and go to 1, unless
t=n
.

1.4
Central Limit Theorem

Apart from estimating the ATE, it is also of interest to give confidence intervals for the ATE. Towards
this end,
Harshaw
et al. 2022
also studied the distributional properties of
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}-\tau.
In particular, they showed that
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}-\tau
is subgaussian-
\sigma
random
variable where
\sigma
is given by the right-hand side in (
1.3
). As shown in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
,
this fact allows one to construct confidence intervals valid for any sample size. Moreover, the authors also derived a central limit theorem (CLT) for
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}
and showed how this theorem
can be used (in the large sample case) to construct confidence intervals that are narrower than
those based on concentration inequalities for a subgaussian random variable. The CLT
in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
was proved for the version of the Gram–Schmidt Walk design which
uses a
fixed
ordering of the pivots. It is based on somewhat strong assumptions on the matrix

X
along with the requirement that
n^{2}\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}]
,
i.e.,
the (scaled) variance of the
estimator
itself
, be at least
cn
(as in the i.i.d. case) which is
unknown
a priori in relation
to the
parameters

\bm{\mu}
and
X
. The authors there conjectured, see

(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Section 6.3)
, that the CLT should also hold for the version which uses

randomized pivot ordering
. The main focus of our article is to prove this conjecture under
minimal and global assumptions involving
only
the design matrix
X
and the outcome vector

\bm{\mu}
.

1.5
The main results

Recall that the covariate matrix
X
and the outcome vector
\bm{\mu}
both naturally depend on the
number of units
n
. Similarly the number of covariates
d
and the robustness parameter
\phi
may
also depend on
n
. In the sequel, we let

\bm{\mu}=X\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}+\bm{v}\mbox{ with }\bm{v}^{\intercal}X=\bm{0},

(1.6)

i.e.,

\bm{v}
is the orthogonal projection of
\bm{\mu}
onto
{\rm ColSp}(X)^{\perp}
. We are now ready to state the three regularity conditions which we will use to show the asymptotic normality. These are “simplified” versions of much more

general
assumptions under which we can derive the CLT in Theorem
1.3
below.

Assumption 1.1

(Outcome regularity)
.

\frac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\leqslant\frac{C}{\log^{3+c}(n)}
whereas
\|\bm{\beta}_{{\rm ls}}\|^{2}\leqslant C\log n
for some positive constants
c
and
C
.

Assumption 1.2

(Covariate regularity)
.

The smallest singular value of the covariate matrix
X
satisfies

\sigma_{{\rm min}}(X)\geqslant\sqrt{cn}
for some positive constant
c
. Also the maximum row norm of the covariate matrix,
\xi=\max_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{x}_{i}\|
,
is bounded as
\xi^{2}\leqslant Cd\log n
for some positive contant
C
.

Assumption 1.3

(Non-degeneracy)
.

\|\bm{v}\|^{2}\geqslant\log^{2+c}(n)
for some positive constant
c
.

A detailed discussion of the necessity and minimality of these assumptions in comparison to their counterparts in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
is provided in
the next subsection.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1.1
–
1.3
hold. Also, suppose that
d
is fixed and
\phi
is bounded away from
0
and
1
. Then the limiting distribution of the Horvitz–Thompson
estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}
under the Gram–Schmidt Walk design is normal:

\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}-\tau}{\sqrt{\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}]}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm law}}N(0,1).

(1.7)

Furthermore, in this case, we have the following asymptotic formula for the
variance:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}]}{\|\bm{v}\|^{2}/n^{2}}=1

(1.8)

where
\bm{v}=\proj_{\colsp(X)^{\perp}}(\bm{\mu})
is the orthogonal
projection of the outcome vector
\bm{\mu}
onto
\colsp(X)^{\perp}
.

Remark 1.1
.

In Theorem
1.2
, we assume conditions
1.1
–
1.3
to keep the exposition simpler. One can prove a CLT under other assumptions as long as the error term converges to zero. Also, one can apply a similar idea for the case when
\mathbb{P}[z_{i}=1],\mathbb{P}[z_{i}=-1]\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
fixed.

1.6
Main contributions and comparisons with literature

In this Section, we briefly discuss the major contributions of the article.

1.

Generality of assumptions on
\bm{\mu}
and
X
.
We compare our assumptions vis-à-vis the assumptions made
in
(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Section 6.3)
facilitating the CLT.

(a)

Outcome Regularity.
The analogous assumption in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, namely
Assumption 6.4, is expressed in terms of the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm of the outcome vector
\bm{\mu}
:

\|\bm{\mu}\|_{\infty}=O(\log^{c}(n)).

If we assume that
\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}\geqslant cn
in the “typical” scenario, we can reinterpret this condition as

\frac{\|\bm{\mu}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{\mu}\|}=O\left(\frac{\log^{c}(n)}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(1.9)

On the other hand, we formulate our condition in terms of the “residual” outcome vector
\bm{v}={\rm Proj}_{\colsp(X)^{\perp}}(\bm{\mu})
which turns out to be the right object to look at based on our
analysis. Note however that while the covariate matrix
X
is known to the experimenter prior to
the treatment assignment,
\bm{\mu}
and hence
\bm{v}
is unavailable to her. Compared to
(
1.9
), our assumption on
\bm{v}
as a vector satisfying

\frac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{v}\|}=O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{3+c}(n)}\right)

is significantly weaker. This
outcome regularity assumption essentially puts a density constraint on
\bm{v}
;
i.e.,
\bm{v}
cannot be too sparse. The assumption in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
interpreted as
(
1.9
) above basically says that
\bm{\mu}
should have
effectively

\Omega\left(\tfrac{n}{\log^{c}(n)}\right)
many non-zero entries of roughly equal size. In comparison,
our assumption allows
\bm{v}
which is very sparse, in the sense that it can be supported on

\log^{c}(n)
many elements of roughly equal size. It is not difficult to see that the effective support of

\bm{\mu}
or
\bm{v}
should diverge to
\infty
at some rate for any CLT to hold. Our assumption on

\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}
is very mild and can in fact be easily deduced from conditions like
\|\bm{\mu}\|_{\infty}^{2}=O(\log n)
and
Assumption
1.2
on
X
.

(b)

Covariate Regularity.
Assumption 6.5 in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
stipulates that

\sigma_{\min}(X_{m})\geqslant\sqrt{cm}
for all
m\geqslant n^{1/2-\epsilon}
where
X_{m}
is the
submatrix of dimensions
m\times d
given by the first
m
rows of
X
after ordering them
according to the ordering of pivots. In our understanding, this assumption is necessary essentially because they work with a fixed pivot ordering. Since we work with the
randomized pivot
ordering
, this randomization actually allows us to only require a
global
condition on the full
covariate matrix instead of its many submatrices. We believe that our Assumption
1.2
on the covariate matrix
X
is both general and
minimal. Assumption 6.5 in
Harshaw
et al. 2022
also requires a logarithmic upper bound on
the so-called
incoherence
of the submatrix
X_{m}
for all
m\geqslant n^{1/2-\epsilon}
which we
do not need at all
. Finally, our assumption that the maximum squared
\ell^{2}
-norm of
\bm{x}_{i}
’s
(
\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is at most
Cd\log n
is also present in
(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Assumption 6.5)

and is motivated by similar reasons.

(c)

Non-degeneracy.
The non-degeneracy criterion presented
by
(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Assumption 6.6)
is the blanket assumption that
n^{2}\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}]
is at least
cn
like in the case of i.i.d. design. In particular, it is not clear what this assumption means in terms of the problem parameters
\bm{\mu}
and
X
. In comparison,
our analysis yields that
n^{2}\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}]
is
asymptotically equivalent
to
\|\bm{v}\|^{2}

(
1.8
). Consequently, our Assumption
1.3

suggests a non-degeneracy condition involving solely the super-logarithmic divergence of

\|\bm{v}\|^{2}
which is a known function of the problem parameters
\bm{\mu}
and
X
. Also since

n^{2}\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}]
is asymptotically equivalent to
\|\bm{v}\|^{2}
, our condition is
significantly weaker
compared to
(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Assumption 6.6)
as it replaces the
linear lower bound in the latter with only a poly-logarithmic bound.

2.

A precise and smaller asymptotic variance.

A very important byproduct of our analysis is that we obtain an asymptotically
exact
variance control for the estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}
as given by (
1.8
).
It is not difficult to see that (see for instance the brief discussion following Theorem
1.1
)
the best possible upper bound on
n^{2}\var[\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}]
allowed by Theorem
1.1
is
\frac{1}{\phi}\|\bm{v}\|^{2}
. Since
\phi\in(0,1)
, our formula (
1.8
) shows that
the
true
variance is
smaller
by the factor
\phi
and as such is
independent
of the
robustness parameter
\phi
. This is particularly beneficial for statisical applications.

3.

Power of randomization.
As already mentioned, we prove our CLT for the GSW design
with randomized pivot ordering as opposed to the fixed pivot ordering in
Harshaw
et al. 2022

where it was posed as a conjecture. This randomization plays a crucial role in our analysis and
enables us to derive our result under such minimal conditions as discussed above. We harness its
strength in this paper in three different (but related) ways. Firstly, randomization lies at the heart of our
definition of the
skeletal
process which carries most of our analysis and yields the correct
formula for the asymptotic variance in (
1.8
) (see Section
2.1

below). Secondly, we use it to take advantage of the concentration phenomenon for the sum of a random
sample without replacement of size
k
out of
n
unit-rank matrices when
1\ll k\ll n
. We use the
Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs for this purpose (see Proposition
3.2
).
Finally, we use some classical moments formulae for the sample mean of a random sample without
replacement (see Proposition
3.1
) propelled by the need to exploit the
orthogonality between
\bm{v}
and
\colsp(X)
. All of these point towards the fact that the
randomized pivot ordering is perhaps the “natural” setup to perform the GSW design.

1.7
Proof sketch

We deduce Theorem
1.2
as a special instance of a more general result. Below we let
Y

denote the matrix
\xi^{-1}\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{1-\phi}}{\sqrt{\phi}}\,X
(the motivation for choosing
this matrix is given below (
2.1
) in the next section).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\phi\in(0,1)
be bounded away from
0
and
d\leqslant n
. Also let

\kappa\coloneqq\tfrac{n}{\sigma_{\min}(Y)}=\tfrac{n}{\lambda_{\min}(Y^{\intercal}Y)}

(
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
being the smallest eigenvalue) and
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any vector satisfying
\bm{v}^{\intercal}X=0
,
i.e.,
\bm{v}
is orthogonal to
\colsp(X)
. Then we have, with
\bm{z}_{n}^{{\rm gs}}
as given by (
1.5
),

\frac{\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}},\bm{v}\rangle}{\sqrt{\var\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}},\bm{v}\rangle}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm law}}N(0,1)

(1.10)

provided

\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{1/2}\,\frac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\,\kappa^{2}\log n=0.

(1.11)

Furthermore, in this case, we have the following asymptotic formula for the variance:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\var\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}},\bm{v}\rangle}{\|\bm{v}\|^{2}}=1.

(1.12)

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We assume Theorem
1.3
and proceed to deduce Theorem
1.2
from it. Recall that

Y=\xi^{-1}\textstyle\frac{\sqrt{1-\phi}}{\sqrt{\phi}}\,X.

Since
d
is bounded and
\phi
is bounded away from
0
and
1
,
Assumptions
1.1
-
1.2
imply condition (
1.11
) for
\bm{v}=\proj_{{\rm ColSp}(X)^{\perp}}(\bm{\mu})
with
\kappa\leqslant C\log(n)

and hence
(
1.10
) and (
1.12
) hold for this choice of
\bm{v}

by Theorem
1.3
. On the other hand, from

(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Lemma 1.1)
one can write

\widehat{\tau}_{{\rm gs}}-\tau=\frac{1}{n}\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{\rm gs}},\bm{\mu}\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{\rm gs}},X\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}\rangle+\frac{1}{n}\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{\rm gs}},\bm{v}\rangle.

(1.13)

However, in view of Theorem
1.1
applied to the case when
\bm{\mu}=X\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}
, we can bound

\var\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{\rm gs}},X\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}\rangle\leqslant\frac{\xi^{2}\|\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}\|^{2}}{(1-\phi)}.

But since
\|\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}\|^{2}\leqslant C\log n
is bounded and
\xi^{2}\leqslant C\log n
by
Assumptions
1.1
and

1.2
respectively (recall that
d
is bounded), we immediately
obtain

\var\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{\rm gs}},X\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}\rangle\leqslant\frac{C\log^{2}(n)}{1-\phi}

for some constant
C>0
. Therefore, from (
1.12
) and
Assumption
1.3
, we immediately deduce (
1.8
) as well as

\frac{\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{\rm gs}},X\bm{\beta}_{\textrm{ls}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\var\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}},\bm{v}\rangle}}\mbox{ converges to $0$ in probability.}

Together with (
1.10
), this yields the CLT for

Remark 1.2

(Relaxing the assumptions for Theorem
1.2
)
.

It is clear from the statement of Theorem
1.3
as well as the proof above that there are
flexibilities for relaxing or modifying the

The rest of the article is devoted to proving Theorem

. It is not difficult to see that

is a martingale sequence (see

. However, running this recipe for the process

is far from being straightforward. The GSW design introduces smeared but

which makes it very hard to control the martingale

introduced in

2.1
renders this dependence “well-behaved” and the explicit

is the projection along
\bm{v}

of an i.i.d. Rademacher linear combination of vectors obtained by applying the classical Gram-Schmidt
process to a uniformly random permutation of the columns of a matrix

. In Section
5

we prove the CLT for

and compute its variance in the process. This part involves, among other

until which the two underlying

the difference between time

and

Since we want to prove the CLT for the random variable

where we omit the dependence on
\bm{v}
. It follows from our definitions in the previous subsection that the process

(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=0}^{n}
. Furthermore in view of (

),

matrix, and

(recall (

(a row vector) denoting the

where

\left\lVert B\bm{u}\right\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{1-\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D\bm{y}_{p}}\leqslant 1+\zeta^{2}(1+\zeta^{2})=\Cr{C:xi}

(3.2)

(recall (

\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle=\left\langle B\bm{u},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\

\begin{split}\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle=\left\lVert B\bm{u}\right\rVert^{2}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}]^{\intercal}(I_{a}-Y_{\star}DY_{\star}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}]\end{split}

(3.3)

where

\begin{split}\left\lVert B\bm{u}\right\rVert^{-2}\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle^{2}&=\left\lVert B\bm{u}\right\rVert^{2}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}]^{\intercal}(I_{a}-Y_{\star}DY_{\star}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}]\right)^{2}\\
&=\mathcal{Q}+(\left\lVert B\bm{u}\right\rVert^{2}-1)\mathcal{Q}\end{split}

(3.4)

(3.5)

Proof.

)

(3.8)

Also since

3.2
Some results on random sampling without replacement

Recall from Observation
2.2
that
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is a random sample of size
n-t+1
from

[n]
without replacement. In the currect subsection we will gather some results about the behavior of
the sum of numbers or matrices indexed by
[n]
evaluated over
\mathcal{A}_{t}
. For clarity of
presentation, we will state these results for a general family of objects
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
— which
would be either real numbers or matrices — and a random sample
\mathcal{A}
of size
a
from

[n]
without replacement. Denote by
\pr
and
\E
the corresponding probability measure and
expectation respectively and consider the random variable
W=W(\mathcal{A})
defined as

W=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}x_{i}\,\mbox{ so that }\E[W]=\frac{a}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}.

Our first result concerns the concentration of
W
around
\E[W]
when
x_{i}
’s are matrices with bounded operator norm.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
denote
d\times d
symmetric matrices with
\max_{i\in[n]}\|x_{i}\|_{{\rm op}}\leqslant 1
. Then for any
a\in[n]
and
x\geqslant 0
, we have

\pr\left[\left\lVert W-\E[W]\right\rVert_{{\rm op}}\geqslant x\right]\leqslant 2d\cdot\exp(-nx^{2}/2a(n-a)).

(3.18)

Proof.

We use Stein’s method for exchangeable pairs to prove the concentration inequality. Let
\pi
be a
random uniform permutation of
[n]
. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}\{\pi(i)\mid i\in[a]\}
. Thus, we
can define

W=\sum_{i\in[a]}x_{\pi(i)}.

We create an exchangeable pair in the following way. Let
(I,J)
be a uniform sample from the set

\{(i,j)\mid 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\}

independent
of
\pi
. Define

\pi^{\prime}=\pi\circ(I,J)\mbox{ and }W^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in[a]}x_{\pi^{\prime}(i)}\,.

One can check that

\Delta W:=W^{\prime}-W=(x_{\pi(J)}-x_{\pi(I)})\cdot\mathds{1}\{I\leqslant a<J\}.

In particular, we have

\E[\Delta W\mid\pi]=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\left(a\sum_{j>a}x_{\pi(j)}-(n-a)\sum_{i\leqslant a}x_{\pi(i)}\right)=-\alpha\cdot\left(W-\E[W]\right)

where
\alpha=\frac{2}{n-1}
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\E[\Delta W^{2}\mid\pi]

\displaystyle=\E[(x_{\pi(J)}^{2}+x_{\pi(I)}^{2}-x_{\pi(I)}x_{\pi(J)}-A_{\pi(J)}x_{\pi(I)})\cdot\mathds{1}\{I\leqslant t<J\}\mid\pi]

\displaystyle\leqslant\E[(x_{\pi(J)}^{2}+x_{\pi(I)}^{2})\cdot\mathds{1}\{I\leqslant t<J\}\mid\pi]\leqslant 2\pr\left[I\leqslant t<J\right]\cdot I_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{4a(n-a)}{n(n-1)}\cdot I_{d},

i.e.,

\frac{1}{2\alpha}\E[\Delta W^{2}\mid\pi]\leqslant\frac{a(n-a)}{n}\cdot I_{d}.

Here, for symmetric matrices “
\leqslant
” means positive definite ordering.
Using
(
Mackey et al. 2014
, Theorem 4.1)
, we get for all
x\geqslant 0

\pr\left[\lambda_{\min}(W-\E[W])\leqslant-x\right]\vee\pr\left[\lambda_{\max}(W-\E[W])\geqslant x\right]\leqslant d\cdot e^{-nx^{2}/(2a(n-a))}.

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

The following result gives formulae for the moments of
W
when
x_{i}
’s are real numbers. Their proofs
are relatively standard, which we provide for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be real numbers satisfying
\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=0
. Then for any
a\in[n]
, we have

\begin{split}&\E\left[W\right]=0,\,\E[W^{2}]=\frac{a(n-a)}{(n)_{2}}\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{2},\mbox{ and }\\
&\E[W^{4}]=\frac{3(a)_{2}(n-a)_{2}}{(n)_{4}}\big(\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{2}\big)^{2}+\frac{a(n-a)}{(n)_{2}}\left(1-\frac{6(a-1)(n-a-1)}{(n-2)(n-3)}\right)\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{4},\end{split}

(3.19)

where
(n)_{k}\coloneqq n(n-1)\ldots(n-k+1)
is the
k
-th downward factorial of
n
.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be a uniform random permutation of the set
[n]
. It is clear to see that
\{\pi(i)\mid 1\leqslant i\leqslant a\}
has the same distribution as
\mathcal{A}
. So we can take
W=\sum_{i=1}^{a}x_{\pi(i)}
.
The zero-mean result follows from the fact that
\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=0
. For the second moment result, we note that
\E[W^{2}]=a\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}]+a(a-1)\E[x_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(2)}]
. Moreover, we have

\E[x_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(2)}]=\E\left[x_{\pi(1)}\cdot\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i\neq\pi(1)}x_{i}\right]=-\frac{1}{n-1}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}]

and
\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{2}.
Similarly, for the fourth moment, we get

\displaystyle\E[W^{4}]

\displaystyle=a\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{4}]+4(a)_{2}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{3}x_{\pi(2}]+3(a)_{2}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}x_{\pi(2)}^{2}]

\displaystyle\quad+6(a)_{3}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}x_{\pi(2)}x_{\pi(3)}]+(a)_{4}\E[x_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(2)}x_{\pi(3)}x_{\pi(4)}].

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\E[x_{\pi(1)}x_{\pi(2)}x_{\pi(3)}x_{\pi(4)}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{n-3}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}x_{\pi(2)}x_{\pi(3)}],

\displaystyle\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}x_{\pi(2)}x_{\pi(3)}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n-2}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}x_{\pi(2)}^{2}+x_{\pi(1)}^{3}x_{\pi(2)}],

\displaystyle\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{3}x_{\pi(2)}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n-1}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{4}]

\displaystyle\text{and }\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}x_{\pi(2)}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{2}\cdot\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{2}]-\frac{1}{n-1}\E[x_{\pi(1)}^{4}].

Simplifying, we get the result.

\blacksquare

\begin{split}&\|Y_{t}\|_{\frob}^{2}={\rm Tr}(Y_{t}^{\intercal}Y_{t})=\sum_{j\in[d]}\lambda_{j}(Y_{t}^{\intercal}Y_{t})\\
&\leqslant\frac{n-t+1}{n}\sum_{j\in[d]}\lambda_{j}(Y^{\intercal}Y)+\frac{n-t+1}{2n}\sum_{j\in[d]}\lambda_{\min}(Y^{\intercal}Y)\\
&=\frac{3}{2}\frac{n-t+1}{n}\cdot{\rm Tr}(Y^{\intercal}Y)=\frac{3}{2}\frac{n-t+1}{n}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\end{split}

where
\lambda_{j}(\cdot)
denotes the
j
-th largest eigenvalue of the corresponding (hermitian)
matrix and in the third step we used the Weyl’s inequality (cf. (
3.26
)).

\blacksquare

We will in fact use this result for the proof of our next lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

We have for any
t\in[n]
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\Big(\bigcap_{s\in[t]}\mathcal{G}_{1,s}\Big)^{c}\right]\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in[t],\,i\in\mathcal{A}_{s}\setminus\{p_{s}\}}|\bm{z}_{s}[i]|^{2}\right]}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}

(3.29)

where

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in[t]}\,\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{s}\setminus\{p_{s}\}}|\bm{z}_{s}[i]|^{2}\right]\leqslant\frac{C\zeta^{2}\kappa^{2}}{n-t}+C\zeta^{2}nd\exp\left(-c\,\frac{n(n-t+1)}{\kappa^{2}\zeta^{4}t}\right).

(3.30)

Proof.

The bound (
3.29
) is just an application of the Markov’s inequality in view of the
definition of
\mathcal{G}_{1,s}
in (
2.6
). Towards proving (
3.30
), let us first recall that we have

\bm{z}_{s}=\sum_{j\in[s]}\delta_{j}\bm{u}_{j}

and in view of (
3.1
) we can write for any
i\in\mathcal{A}_{j}\setminus\{p_{j}\}
,

\bm{u}_{j}[i]=-\frac{\bm{y}_{i}^{\intercal}D_{j}\bm{y}_{p_{j}}}{1-\bm{y}_{p_{j}}^{\intercal}D_{j}\bm{y}_{p_{j}}}.

Therefore we have for any
i\in\mathcal{A}_{s}\setminus\{p_{s}\}
,

\bm{z}_{s}[i]=-\bm{y}_{i}^{\intercal}\sum_{j\in[s]}\frac{D_{j}\bm{y}_{p_{j}}}{1-\bm{y}_{p_{j}}^{\intercal}D_{j}\bm{y}_{p_{j}}}\,\delta_{j}.

Within this proof let us denote

\bm{v}_{j}=\frac{D_{j}\bm{y}_{p_{j}}}{1-\bm{y}_{p_{j}}^{\intercal}D_{j}\bm{y}_{p_{j}}}\mbox{ and }\bm{V}_{s}=\sum_{j\in[s]}\delta_{j}\bm{v}_{j}.

Just like the martingales in Section
2.1
, it is routine to check that
\{\bm{V}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
is a vector-valued martingale adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=0}^{n}
. Equipped
with the above notations we can write for any
i\in\mathcal{A}_{s}\setminus\{p_{s}\}
,

|\bm{z}_{s}[i]|\leqslant\|\bm{V}_{s}\|\|y_{i}\|\leqslant\zeta\|\bm{V}_{s}\|

where we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and hence,

\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{s}\setminus\{p_{s}\}}|\bm{z}_{s}[i]|^{2}\leqslant\zeta^{2}\|\bm{V}_{s}\|^{2}.

Since
\{\bm{V}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
is a martingale,
\{\|\bm{V}_{t}\|^{2}\}_{t=1}^{n}
becomes a submartingale
sequence. Therefore, we can use the Doob’s maximal inequality to deduce

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in[t],\,i\in\mathcal{A}_{s}\setminus\{p_{s}\}}|\bm{z}_{s}[i]|^{2}\right]\leqslant\E\|\bm{V}_{t}\|^{2}.

(3.31)

Further we can write,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{V}_{t}\|^{2}\right]&=\sum_{s\in[t]}\E\left[\|\delta_{s}\bm{v}_{s}\|^{2}\right]\leqslant C\sum_{s\in[t]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}_{s}\|^{2}\right]=C\sum_{s\in[t]}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{y}_{p_{s}}^{\intercal}D_{s}^{2}\bm{y}_{p_{s}}}{(1-\bm{y}_{p_{s}}^{\intercal}D_{s}\bm{y}_{p_{s}})^{2}}\right]\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity2}}}{{\leqslant}}C\sum_{s\in[t]}\E\left[\bm{y}_{p_{s}}^{\intercal}D_{s}^{2}\bm{y}_{p_{s}}\right]\end{split}

where in the second step we used the fact that
|\delta_{s}|\leqslant 2
almost surely. We will now bound the
expectations on the right by first partitioning into the events
\mathcal{G}_{2,s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2,s}^{c}

and then bounding the corresponding terms separately.

\begin{split}\sum_{s\in[t]}&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{y}_{p_{s}}^{\intercal}D_{s}^{2}\bm{y}_{p_{s}}\mathrm{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,s}}\right]\leqslant\sum_{s\in[t]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|D_{s}\|_{{\rm op}}^{2}\|\bm{y}_{p_{s}}\|^{2}\mathrm{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,s}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Ctopbnd}}}{{\leqslant}}C\sum_{s\in[t]}\frac{\kappa^{2}}{(n-s+1)^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p_{s}}\|^{2}\right]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm Obs.}~\ref{obs:swor}}}{{=}}&\,\,C\kappa^{2}\sum_{s\in[t]}\frac{1}{(n-s+1)^{2}}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{y}_{i}\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:zeta}}}{{\leqslant}}C\zeta^{2}\kappa^{2}\sum_{s\in[t]}\frac{1}{(n-s+1)^{2}}\,\leqslant\,\frac{C\zeta^{2}\kappa^{2}}{n-t}.\end{split}

On the other hand, we can write

\begin{split}\sum_{s\in[t]}&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{y}_{p_{s}}^{\intercal}D_{s}^{2}\bm{y}_{p_{s}}\mathrm{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,s}^{c}}\right]\leqslant\zeta^{2}\sum_{s\in[t]}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{G}_{2,s}^{c}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:smallevent2}}}{{\leqslant}}C\zeta^{2}nd\exp\left(-c\,\frac{n(n-t+1)}{\kappa^{2}\zeta^{4}t}\right)\end{split}

where in the first inequality we used the fact that

\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D_{s}^{2}\bm{y}_{p}\leqslant\|D_{s}\|_{{\rm op}}^{2}\|\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:zeta}+~\eqref{def:YD}}}{{\leqslant}}\zeta^{2}.

Adding the previous two bounds yields us (
3.30
).

\blacksquare

4
Equivalence of CLT for different processes

The main result of this section is the equivalence of CLT between
M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}}
and
M_{n}
(see
Section
2.1
for the definitions of all the relevant martingales).

Proposition 4.1
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.3
, we have

\frac{M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm law}}N(0,1)\,\mbox{ if and only if }\,\frac{M_{n}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm law}}N(0,1).

(4.1)

We need some intermediate results capturing the closeness between different related random
variables in order to prove Proposition
4.1
. Our first result gives an upper bound on the
probability that
M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m}
and
\widetilde{M}_{n-m}
are different.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
m\in[n]
satisfying
m\geqslant 6\Cr{C:xi}\zeta^{2}\kappa
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[|M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m}-\widetilde{M}_{n-m}|>0\right]\leqslant\frac{C\zeta^{2}\kappa^{2}}{m}+C\,nd\exp\left(-c(\kappa\zeta^{2})^{-2}m\right).

(4.2)

Proof.

We know from Lemma
3.3
that
M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m}
and
M_{n-m}
are
identical on the event
\mathcal{E}_{1,n-m}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n-m}

provided

n-m<n+1-6\Cr{C:xi}\zeta^{2}\kappa
,
i.e.,
m\geqslant 6\Cr{C:xi}\zeta^{2}\kappa
. Hence we only need to show that the
probability of the event
(\mathcal{E}_{1,n-m}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,n-m})^{c}
is bounded by the right-hand side
in (
4.2
). But this follows from Lemmas
3.4
and

3.5
along with a union bound for bounding the probability of the event
\mathcal{E}_{2,t}^{c}
.

\blacksquare

Our next lemma gives an upper bound on the
\ell^{2}
-distance between
M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n}
and

M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m}
as well as between
\widetilde{M}_{n}
and
\widetilde{M}_{n-m}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
m\in[n]
, we have

\max\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n}-M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m})^{2}\right],\>\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{M}_{n}-\widetilde{M}_{n-m})^{2}\right]\right)\leqslant Cd\max(1,\zeta^{2})\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}m^{3}.

(4.3)

Proof.

Let us examine the difference

\Delta_{m,n}\coloneqq M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n}-M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m}.

(4.4)

In view of the first item under
in our definition of the relevant processes, we can
write

\begin{split}\Delta_{m,n}=\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\delta^{{{\rm gs}}}_{t}\left\langle B\bm{u}^{{{\rm gs}}}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\end{split}

(4.5)

with

\tau\coloneqq\min\{t>n-m:\mathcal{A}^{{{\rm gs}}}_{t}=\emptyset\}

where we adopt the convention that the minimum of an empty set is
n
,
i.e.,
the limit of the above
summation is empty. Henceforth, in this proof we will drop the superscript “
{{\rm gs}}
” in order to
avoid the notational clutter. This is particularly appropriate because our argument relies only on
properties shared by
\{\widetilde{M}_{t}^{{{\rm gs}}}\}_{t=0}^{n}
and
\{\widetilde{M}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{n}
and hence works
mutatis mutandis
for
\{\widetilde{M}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{n}
.

Since the event
\{\tau>t\}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
measurable (
2.14
), it follows from the definitions of the
terms involved (see Section
2
) that the partial sums of
\Delta_{m,n}
form a
martingale sequence relative to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=0}^{n}
. Hence, in view of orthogonality of
martingale differences, we can write:

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{m,n}^{2}]\\
&=\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{t}^{2}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\right]\leqslant C\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\right]^{2}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity3}}}{{=}}C\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\big]\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity2}}}{{\leqslant}}C\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\right]\end{split}

(4.6)

where in the second step we used the fact that
|\delta_{t}|\leqslant 2
almost surely and
a_{t}=|\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}|
. Here we used the event
\{\tau>t\}
to ensure that
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is non-empty so that
(
3.2
) and (
3.3
) apply. We will frequently use the following elementary
inequality on quadratic forms. Let
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A,B\in R^{n\times n}
be symmetric and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be an n.n.d. matrix. Then we have,

\bm{x}^{\intercal}(A-B)C(A-B)\bm{x}\leqslant 2(\bm{x}^{\intercal}ACA\bm{x}+\bm{x}^{\intercal}BCB\bm{x}).

(4.7)

We can use this to bound the right-hand side of (
4.6
) by writing,

\begin{split}&\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\right]\\
\,\leqslant&\>\>\>\>\>2\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\right]\\
&+2\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p_{t}}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\right].\end{split}

(4.8)

Now we will separately bound the two terms on the right-hand side of the above display. For the
second term, we can bound its
t
-th summand as follows (with
\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}
kept implicit and

p_{t}
denoted by
p
to reduce the notational clutter):

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}\left(D_{t}^{-1}-I_{d}\right)D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right]\\
&\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right]=\sum_{j\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}[:\,,j]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leqslant\sum_{j\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]\|^{2}\|Y_{t}[:\,,j]\|^{2}\right]\leqslant\zeta^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{j\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{A}_{t}|^{2}\right]\leqslant\zeta^{2}d\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}m^{2}.\end{split}

Here in the very first term we used the fact that the matrix
\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}
is
bounded by the identity matrix
I_{a_{t}}
in the
Loewner order
. Similarly in the first inequality we used that
D_{t}-D_{t}^{2}
is at most
D_{t}
in the Loewner order. In the third inequality, we used the
bound
\|D_{t}\|_{{\rm op}}\leqslant 1
(
3.17
). The fourth inequality uses the Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality whereas in the last inequality we used the fact that
|\mathcal{A}_{t}|\leqslant n-t+1
for any
t\in[n]
.
Summing this from
t=n-m+1
to
n
yields the bound
C\|v\|_{\infty}^{2}dm^{3}.

As for the first term in (
4.8
), we can similarly write

\begin{split}&\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}_{t}]\mathds{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\right]\leqslant\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right]\\
\leqslant&\,\|v\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{A}_{t}|\leqslant\|v\|_{\infty}^{2}m^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore combining the bounds from the previous two displays and plugging it into
(
4.8
) and subsequently into (
4.6
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{m,n}^{2}\right]\leqslant Cd\max(1,\zeta^{2})\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}m^{3}.

This gives us the required bound in (
4.3
) for both
\{M_{t}\}_{t=0}^{n}
and
\{\widetilde{M}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{n}
(see the discussion following the definition of
\Delta_{m,n}
in (
4.5
)).

\blacksquare

Finally we need the following result on the
\ell^{2}
-distance between
M_{n}
and
\widetilde{M}_{n}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any integer
m\in{[n]}
we have the following bound,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&[(\widetilde{M}_{n}-M_{n})^{2}]\\
&\leqslant C\cdot\Cr{C:xi}^{3}\left(\left(m+(d+\kappa)\log en\right)\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}+Cdn\zeta^{2}\exp\left(-c\frac{m}{\kappa^{2}\zeta^{4}}\right)\right)+C\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\|\bm{v}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(4.9)

Proof.

Recalling the definitions of
M_{t}
and
\widetilde{M}_{t}
from (
2.16
), we can write

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{t}-M_{t}

\displaystyle\quad=\underbrace{\sum_{s\in[t]}\delta_{s}\left(1-\|B\bm{u}_{s}\|^{-1}\right)\left\langle B\bm{u}_{s},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle}_{A_{t}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s\in[t]}(\delta_{s}-\eta_{s})\|B\bm{u}_{s}\|^{-1}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{s},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle}_{B_{t}}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[(\widetilde{M}_{n}-M_{n})^{2}]\leqslant 2\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[B_{n}^{2}\right].

We now observe that both
A_{t}
and
B_{t}
are
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=0}^{n}
-martingales with mean

0
. Hence by the orthogonality of martingale differences, we can write

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}^{2}\right]=\sum_{t\in[n]}&\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{t}^{2}\left(1-\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{-1}\right)^{2}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\\
\leqslant C\sum_{t\in[n]}&\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{-2}\right)^{2}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\\
=C\sum_{t\in[n]}&\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{4}}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity5}}}{{=}}C\sum_{t\in[n]}&\mathbb{E}\left[(\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}-1)^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{t}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity2}}}{{\leqslant}}C\cdot\Cr{C:xi}\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[(\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}-1)\mathcal{Q}_{t}\right]\end{split}

where in the first inequality we used the facts that
|\delta_{t}|\leqslant 2
almost surely and that
\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|\geqslant 1
which we noted after (
3.2
).

We now proceed to bound the expectations in the last line above. In the sequel, we will use the
notation
a_{t}=n-t+1=|\mathcal{A}_{t}|
(recall Observation
2.2
) and also use
p
instead
of
p_{t}
. For any
t\in[n]
, we obtain from (
5.24
) proved in Section
5
below that

\mathbb{E}\left[(\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-1)\mathcal{Q}_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leqslant\frac{2\Cr{C:xi}}{n-t+1}\,\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{j}^{2}\bm{y}_{j}^{\intercal}D_{t}\bm{y}_{j}+\frac{2\Cr{C:xi}^{2}}{n-t+1}\,\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}].

We bound the expectation of the quantity on the right-hand side above (summed over
t
in
[n]
) in
a
delicate
bound (
5.20
) proved as part of Lemma
5.4
in the next
section which finally yields

\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]^{2}\leqslant C\cdot\Cr{C:xi}^{3}\left((m+\kappa\log en)\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}+d\log en\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}+Cdn\zeta^{2}\exp(-c(\kappa\zeta^{2})^{-2}m)\right).

(4.10)

Next we will bound
\mathbb{E}\left[B_{n}^{2}\right]
. By the martingale property, we can write

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[B_{n}\right]^{2}\\
&=\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[(\delta_{t}-\eta_{t})^{2}\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{-2}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\leqslant C\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{t}^{2}\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{-2}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:MttildeMt}}}{{=}}C\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{n}^{2}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{form:sigmansq}}}{{=}}C\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\|\bm{v}\|^{2}\end{split}

where in the second step we used the definitions of
\delta_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
from (
2.9
),
(
2.11
) and (
2.13
) along with the bound given by (
3.22
). Combined
with (
4.10
), this yields (
4.9
).

\blacksquare

We are now ready to prove Proposition
4.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

It is enough to show that

\frac{1}{\|\bm{v}\|}|\,M_{n}-\widetilde{M}_{n}|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm P}}0

(4.11)

and

\frac{1}{\|\bm{v}\|}\,|M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n}-\widetilde{M}_{n}|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm P}}0

(4.12)

where
{\rm P}
indicates
convergence in probability
. Recall that there is a parameter

\varepsilon_{n}
that goes in the definition of
\widetilde{M}_{n}
. To this end, we choose

\varepsilon_{n}=\min\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log en}},\frac{1}{3}\right)

and proceed to show (
4.11
)
and (
4.12
).

To show (
4.11
) it suffices to show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|\bm{v}\|^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{n}-\widetilde{M}_{n})^{2}\right]=0.

(4.13)

For this, we can set

m=C\kappa^{2}\zeta^{4}\log en

for an appropriately large enough constant
C
and use Lemma
4.4
to deduce (
4.12
) provided

\lim_{n\to\infty}\max(m^{1/2},d^{1/2}(\log n)^{1/2},\kappa^{1/2}(\log n)^{1/2})\frac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{v}\|}=0

(recall that
d\leqslant n
) which follows from the first part of assumption (
1.11
) in
Theorem
1.3
. We also need to show that
m
as chosen above is at
most
n
for all
n
large enough. But this follows from assumption (
1.11
) in view of the facts that

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\leqslant\tfrac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{v}\|}
and

\kappa=\frac{n}{\lambda_{{\rm min}}(Y^{\intercal}Y)}\geqslant\frac{dn}{\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:zeta}}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{dn}{n\zeta^{2}}=\zeta^{-2}

(4.14)

(notice that
\zeta^{-1}
is bounded (
2.2
) since
\phi
is bounded away from
0
by our
assumption).

In order to show (
4.12
), we again set

m=C\kappa^{2}\zeta^{4}\log en

for some suitably large constant
C
and write

M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n}-\widetilde{M}_{n}=\underbrace{M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n}-M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m}}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{M^{{{\rm gs}}}_{n-m}-\widetilde{M}_{n-m}}_{T_{2}}+\underbrace{\widetilde{M}_{n-m}-\widetilde{M}_{n}}_{T_{3}}.

(4.15)

We can use Lemma
4.3
and a similar reasoning as before to argue that both

\|\bm{v}\|^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1}^{2}\right]
and
\|\bm{v}\|^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{3}^{2}\right]
converge to
0
under
assumption (
1.11
). This implies that
T_{1}
and
T_{3}
converge to
0
in probability. On the other hand, we have
m\geqslant 6\Cr{C:xi}\zeta^{2}\kappa
for large enough
n
in view
of (
4.14
). Hence we can use Lemma
4.2
to show that
\mathbb{P}[T_{2}>0]\rightarrow 0
which implies that
T_{2}
converges to
0
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Together these yield (
4.12
) in view of (
4.15
) completing the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

\blacksquare

5
Asymptotic normality of
M_{n}
and the proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we conclude the proof of Theorem
1.3
by combining
Proposition
4.1
from Section
4
and the CLT for
M_{n}
proved in
Proposition
5.1
below. The latter is the main result of this section.

Proposition 5.1
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.3
, we have

\frac{M_{n}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm law}}\N(0,1).

The proof of Proposition
5.1
occupies the majority of the remainder of this article. But
before that let us finish the proof of Theorem
1.3
assuming this result.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We obtain from Propositions
4.1
and
5.1
that,

\frac{M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm law}}\N(0,1)

(5.1)

(recall that
\langle\bm{z}_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}},\bm{v}\rangle=M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}}
). However, since
\phi
is bounded away from
0
and the matrix
B
in definition
2.1
and in

Harshaw
et al. 2022
(see (
1.4
)) only differ by a factor
1/\sqrt{\phi}
, it follows
from
(
Harshaw
et al. 2022
, Theorem 6.1)
that
\tfrac{M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}}}{\|\bm{v}\|}
is a subgaussian variable with variance parameter
1/\phi
. In particular, the random variables
\tfrac{(M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}})^{2}}{\|\bm{v}\|^{2}}
are
uniformly integrable
in
n
. Hence by classical
results on uniform integrability, this implies together with (
5.1
):

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\var\left[M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}}\right]}{\|\bm{v}\|^{2}}=1\mbox{ and hence }\frac{M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}}}{\sqrt{\var\left[M_{n}^{{{\rm gs}}}\right]}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\rm law}}\N(0,1)

which finishes the proof.

\blacksquare

We now return to the

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

We will verify Lindeberg’s conditions for the CLT of the triangular array of martingales
\{M_{t}\}_{t=0}^{n}
(recall that we keep the dependence on
n
implicit). To this end, let us define the
conditional quadratic variation

Q_{n}
of
M_{n}
as

Q_{n}=\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\mbox{ where }\Delta_{t}\coloneqq M_{t}-M_{t-1}=\eta_{t}\left\langle\frac{B\bm{u}_{t}}{\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

(5.2)

(recall (
2.16
) as well the recursive definition of the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t}
from (
2.14
) in Section
2
). Notice that
\mathbb{E}[Q_{n}]=\var[M_{n}]\eqqcolon\sigma_{n}^{2}
. Lindeberg’s conditions are implied by Lyapunov’s moment
condition which in our case are summarized below (see, e.g.,
Hall and
Heyde 1980
):

(i)

\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\rightarrow 1
,

(ii)

\frac{1}{\|\bm{v}\|^{4}}\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{4}]\rightarrow 0,\mbox{ and}

(iii)

\frac{Q_{n}}{\|\bm{v}\|^{2}}\rightarrow 1\mbox{ in probability}

as
n\to\infty
.
We now verify each of these three conditions.

Verifying condition (i).

The following observation is crucial for our proof:

\sum_{t\in[n]}\left\langle\frac{B\bm{u}_{t}}{\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}=\|\bm{v}\|^{2}.

(5.3)

Let us first show (i) using this claim. In view of the description of the underlying stochastic processes in Section
2
,
observe that
|\eta_{t}|=1
for all
t\in[n]
and hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{t}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{t}^{2}\left\langle\frac{B\bm{u}_{t}}{\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\frac{B\bm{u}_{t}}{\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\right].

Therefore, by (
5.3
) and the orthogonality of martingale differences, we get

\sigma_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[M_{n}^{2}]=\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}\|^{2}\right]=\|\bm{v}\|^{2}

(5.4)

which finishes the verification of condition (i).

It remains to verify (
5.3
). Recall from the discussion after
(
2.16
) that the vectors
\left(\tfrac{B\bm{u}_{1}}{\|B\bm{u}_{1}\|},\ldots,\tfrac{B\bm{u}_{n}}{\|B\bm{u}_{n}\|}\right)
form an ONB for
\colsp(B)
where

B=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}\\
Y^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}

(recall (
2.1
)) and hence

\sum_{t\in[n]}\left\langle\frac{B\bm{u}_{t}}{\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}=\|{\rm Proj}_{\colsp(B)}(\bm{v})\|^{2}.

However, since

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}=B\bm{v}

(recall that
\bm{v}^{\intercal}Y=\bm{0}
), (
5.3
) follows.

Verifying condition (ii).
We need the following moment bound.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
m\in[n]
, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{t}^{4}\right]\\
&\quad\leqslant C\Cr{C:xi}^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\bm{v}\|^{2}+Cd\Cr{C:xi}^{2}\zeta^{8}\left(\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\ m^{3}+\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\frac{\kappa^{4}}{m}+n^{3}\|\bm{v}\|^{4}\exp\left(-c(\kappa\zeta^{2})^{-2}m\right)\right).\end{split}

One can now check that by setting

m=C\kappa^{2}\zeta^{4}\log en

for a suitably large enough constant
C
, the right hand side above converges to
0
as soon as

\lim_{n\to\infty}d^{1/4}\frac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{v}\|}\kappa^{3/2}(\log n)^{3/4}=0

(5.5)

(recall that
d\leqslant n
and
\kappa\geqslant\zeta^{-2}
by (
4.14
)) which is implied by the first part of assumption (
1.11
) in
Theorem
1.3
. However, we also need to ensure that
m
, as chosen above, is at
most
n
for all large enough
n
. But this follows from (
5.5
) in view of the facts that

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\leqslant\tfrac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}}{\|\bm{v}\|}
and (
4.14
).
We now proceed to the

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Since
\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{u}(p_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t})
, we have in view of (
3.3
) and (
3.17
),

\begin{split}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle=\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}].\end{split}

(5.6)

where, like in the previous sections, we use the shorthands
a_{t}=|\mathcal{A}_{t}|=n-t+1

(Observation
2.2
) and
p=p_{t}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\Delta_{t}^{2}&\leqslant C\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{-2}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Butinprod}}}{{=}}C\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity4}}}{{=}}C\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{t}\end{split}

(5.7)

where in the first inequality we used the fact that
|\delta_{t}|\leqslant 2
almost surely and in the final step,

\mathcal{Q}_{t}\coloneqq\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}.

Consequently,

\begin{split}\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{t}^{4}\right]\leqslant C\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{4}\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{2}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity2}}}{{\leqslant}}C\cdot\Cr{C:xi}^{2}\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{2}\right].\end{split}

(5.8)

Let us now expand
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{2}
.

\begin{split}&\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{2}\\
&=\big(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\big)^{4}\leqslant C\left(\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}e_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{4}+\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}e_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{4}\right)\\
&=C\left(v_{p}^{4}+\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}\bm{y}_{p}\right)^{4}\right)=C\left(v_{p}^{4}+\left(\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}\bm{y}_{p}\right)^{2}\right).\end{split}

(5.9)

We will estimate the expected values of each of the two terms separately. The first term can be dealt
with in a simple manner.

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[v_{p}^{4}\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[v_{p}^{4}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n-t+1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{i}^{4}\right]\leqslant\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}\>\>\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n-t+1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{i}^{2}\right]\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm Obs.}~\ref{obs:swor}}}{{=}}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\frac{\|\bm{v}\|^{2}}{n}.\end{split}

(5.10)

The second term, on the other hand, requires a more delicate treatment. In particular, we will take
advantage of the
cancellations
inherent in the relation
\bm{v}^{\intercal}Y=0
(see (
2.3
)) in
order to obtain
favorable
bounds on the moments of
\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}
. This is
needed because we stipulated very weak upper bound on the density of the vector
\bm{v}

in (
1.11
) as already discussed in the introduction. To see this, consider the “good” scenario when
d=1
and

y_{i}
’s are same (observe that
y_{i}
’s are scalars in this case). A simple algebra then reveals that the
best possible bound one can obtain in this generality is the following:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}\bm{y}_{p}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant C(\zeta)\,\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\,\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{t}|^{4}}{|\mathcal{A}_{t}|^{4}}=C(\zeta)\,\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}.

Summing it over
t\in[n]
, we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\,\frac{\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}}{\|\bm{v}\|^{4}}=0

which is a
much
stronger requirement than the one we stipulated in (
1.11
).

In order to exploit the relation
\bm{v}^{\intercal}Y=0
, we will use Proposition
3.1
to derive the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
t\in[n]
, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\right]\leqslant Cd\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\left(\frac{(n-t+1)^{2}(t-1)^{2}}{n^{4}}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{4}+\frac{(n-t+1)(t-1)}{n^{2}}\zeta^{2}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\right)\\
&\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{2}\right]\leqslant\frac{(n-t+1)(t-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{j\in[d]}\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}^{2}y_{ij}^{2}\leqslant\frac{(n-t+1)(t-1)}{n(n-1)}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}.\end{split}

(5.11)

Proof.

Firstly we can bound,

\begin{split}\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}=\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{i}y_{ij}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}\leqslant d\sum_{j\in[d]}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{i}y_{ij}\right)^{4}\end{split}

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Now taking expectation on both sides and using

\bm{v}^{\intercal}Y=0
, we obtain from the second formula given in (
3.19
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant Cd\sum_{j\in[d]}\left(\frac{(n-t+1)^{2}(t-1)^{2}}{n^{4}}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}^{2}y_{ij}^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{(n-t+1)(t-1)}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}^{4}y_{ij}^{4}\right).

Next, we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[d]}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}^{2}y_{ij}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\sum_{j\in[d]}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{ij}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\left(\sum_{j\in[d]}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{ij}^{2}\right)^{2}=\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{4}.

Also,

\sum_{j\in[d]}\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}^{4}y_{ij}^{4}\leqslant\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\sum_{j\in[d]}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{ij}^{4}\leqslant\zeta^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\sum_{j\in[d]}\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{ij}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:zeta}}}{{\leqslant}}\zeta^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}.

(5.12)

The last three displays finish the proof of the fourth moment bound. To prove the second moment
bound, we can argue similarly. All we have to do differently is to use the second moment formula in
Proposition
3.1
.

\blacksquare

Now returning to the second term in (
5.9
), we start with some matrix algebra.

\begin{split}&\left(\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}\bm{y}_{p}\right)^{2}\leqslant\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left\|D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}\right\|_{{\rm op}}^{2}\\
=&\,\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left({\rm Tr}(D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t})\right)^{2}=\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}.\end{split}

Here in the second step we used the observation that
\Tr(A)=\|A\|_{{\rm op}}
for any rank 1
matrix
A
. Taking conditional expectation
over

p
,
i.e.,
w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we then get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\frac{1}{n-t+1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\|y_{i}\|^{4}\cdot\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(5.13)

We will bound this term in two different ways based on how large
t
is. Firstly we can bound,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n-t+1}&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\|y_{i}\|^{4}\cdot\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\leqslant\zeta^{4}\|D_{t}\|_{{\rm op}}^{4}\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\\
&\leqslant\zeta^{4}\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\end{split}

(5.14)

where in the last step we used the fact that
\|D_{t}\|_{{\rm op}}\leqslant 1
from (
3.17
).

Taking expectations on both sides and using the fourth moment bound in Lemma
5.3
and subsequently plugging it into (
5.13
), we
deduce

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leqslant Cd\,\zeta^{4}\,\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\left(\frac{(n-t+1)^{2}(t-1)^{2}}{n^{4}}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{4}+\frac{(n-t+1)(t-1)}{n^{2}}\zeta^{2}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(5.15)

From this we get the following bound for any
m\in[n]
:

\begin{split}&\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leqslant Cd\,\zeta^{4}\,\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\,\left(\frac{m^{3}}{n^{2}}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{4}+\frac{m^{2}}{n}\zeta^{2}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\right)\\
&\leqslant Cd\,\zeta^{8}\,\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\ m^{3}.\end{split}

(5.16)

where in the final step we used the fact that
\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\leqslant n\zeta^{2}
(
2.2
).

Next we deal with the sum over
t\in[n-m]
which requires additional work. To this end, let us
recall the event
\mathcal{G}_{2,t}
from (
2.6
):

\mathcal{G}_{2,t}=\left\{\|Y_{t}^{\intercal}Y_{t}-\frac{n-t+1}{n}Y^{\intercal}Y\|_{{\rm op}}\geqslant\frac{n-t+1}{2n}\lambda_{\min}\big(Y^{\intercal}Y\big)\right\}.

We can bound the expectation
on
the event
\mathcal{G}_{2,t}
as follows:

\begin{split}&\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}}\right]\\
=&\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}}\right]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:yp4qtcondexpect}}}{{=}}&\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\frac{1}{n-t+1}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\|y_{i}\|^{4}\cdot\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}}\right]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:zeta}+\eqref{eq:Ctopbnd}}}{{\leqslant}}&\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n-t+1}\cdot\frac{n-t+1}{n}\cdot\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}}\right]\\
\leqslant&\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n-t+1}\cdot\frac{n-t+1}{n}\cdot\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|D_{t}\|_{{\rm op}}^{4}\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}}\right]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Ctopbnd}}}{{\leqslant}}&\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\frac{C\zeta^{2}}{n}\cdot\frac{\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\kappa^{4}}{(n-t+1)^{4}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\right].\end{split}

Now using the fourth moment bound in (
5.11
) to bound the expectation term in the
last step above, we further obtain

\begin{split}&\,\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}}\right]\\
\leqslant&\,\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\frac{Cd\,\zeta^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}}{n}\cdot\frac{\|Y\|_{\frob}^{4}\kappa^{4}}{(n-t+1)^{4}}\left(\frac{(n-t+1)^{2}(t-1)^{2}}{n^{4}}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}+\frac{(n-t+1)(t-1)}{n^{2}}\zeta^{2}\right)\\
\leqslant&\,Cd\,\zeta^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\cdot\|Y\|_{\frob}^{4}\kappa^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}}{n^{5}}\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\frac{(t-1)^{2}}{(n-t+1)^{2}}+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n^{3}}\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\frac{(t-1)}{(n-t+1)^{3}}\right)\\
\leqslant&\,Cd\,\zeta^{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\cdot\|Y\|_{\frob}^{4}\kappa^{4}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{mn^{3}}\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{m^{2}n^{2}}\right)\leqslant\,Cd\,\zeta^{8}\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{4}\frac{\kappa^{4}}{m}.\end{split}

(5.17)

where in the last step we again used the fact that
\|Y\|_{\frob}^{2}\leqslant n\zeta^{2}
(
2.2
).
Bounding the same expectation
on
the
unlikely
event
\mathcal{G}_{2,t}^{c}
, is relatively
simpler in view of Lemma
3.4
.

\begin{split}&\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{y}_{p}\|^{4}\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}^{2}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}^{c}}\right]\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:yp4qtcondexpect}+~\eqref{def:zeta}}}{{\leqslant}}\zeta^{4}\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\|D_{t}\|_{{\rm op}}^{4}\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}^{c}}\right]\\
\leqslant&\,\zeta^{4}\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\|^{4}\|Y_{t}\|_{\frob}^{4}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{2,t}^{c}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:zeta}}}{{\leqslant}}\zeta^{8}\|\bm{v}\|^{4}\sum_{t=1}^{n-m}(n-t+1)^{2}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{2,t}^{c}]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:smallevent2}}}{{\leqslant}}&\,Cd\zeta^{8}\|\bm{v}\|^{4}n^{3}\ \exp\left(-c(\kappa\zeta^{2})^{-2}m\right)\end{split}

(5.18)

where in the second step we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to deduce

\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{4}\leqslant\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\|^{4}\|Y_{t}\|^{4}

along with the fact that

.

Finally we obtain the lemma by collecting the bounds in (

) after taking expectations on both sides and adding over

).

\blacksquare

Lemma 5.4

Now let us set

Proof of Lemma

Proof of (
5.19
)
. To this end, let us recall from (

\begin{split}\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{-2}\left\langle B\bm{u}_{t},\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\

(5.23)

with
\mathcal{Q}_{t}=\left(\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\right)^{2}

\begin{split}&(\|B\bm{u}_{t}\|^{2}-1)\mathcal{Q}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:identity2}}}{{\leqslant}}\Cr{C:xi}\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D_{t}\bm{y}_{p}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:qformineq}+~\eqref{eq:Butnormbnd}}}{{\leqslant}}2\Cr{C:xi}\,\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D_{t}\bm{y}_{p}\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]+2\Cr{C:xi}^{2}\,\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}].\end{split}

We now take conditional expectations on both sides with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
for each of the two

|\mathcal{A}_{t}|=a_{t}=n-t+1
).

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{y}_{p}^{\intercal}D_{t}\bm{y}_{p}\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\frac{1}{n-t+1}\,\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{j}^{2}\bm{y}_{j}^{\intercal}D_{t}\bm{y}_{j},\text{ whereas}\\
\mathbb{E}&\left[\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\bm{e}_{p}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\frac{1}{n-t+1}\,\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\\

where in the last step we used the fact that
D_{t}-D_{t}^{2}
is at most
D_{t}
in Loewner order. Putting together the last two displays, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[(\left\lVert B\bm{u}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}-1)\mathcal{Q}_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leqslant\frac{2\Cr{C:xi}}{n-t+1}\,\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{j}^{2}\bm{y}_{j}^{\intercal}D_{t}\bm{y}_{j}+\frac{2\Cr{C:xi}^{2}}{n-t+1}\,\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}].

(5.24)

As to the first term on the right hand side of (

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{t}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]&=\frac{1}{n-t+1}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}(I_{a_{t}}-Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})^{2}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\\

Proof of (
5.20
)
. Let us start with the first term in

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in[n]}\frac{1}{n-t+1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}v_{j}^{2}\bm{y}_{j}^{\intercal}D_{t}\bm{y}_{j}\right]&\leqslant\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in[n]}\frac{1}{n-t+1}\,{\rm Tr}(Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal})\right]\\

(5.25)

Tackling the second term in
\mathcal{QF}
, which we henceforth refer to as

, requires a careful

) while verifying

\begin{split}S_{n}&=S_{n-m}+\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\frac{1}{n-t+1}\,\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}D_{t}Y_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]\\

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=n-m+1}^{n}\frac{1}{n-t+1}\,\|\bm{v}[\mathcal{A}_{t}]^{\intercal}Y_{t}\|^{2}\right]\\

where in the last step we used the fact that

Next we bound the expectation of

Proof of (
5.21
)
. Finally it remains to show the facts about

. Let us

Hence,

where

is the inverse permutation of
\pi
defined as
\pi(t)=p_{t}
. Since

is
also a uniformly random permutation, note that,

\mathbb{E}\left[H(\theta_{1})\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\in[n]}\sum_{s\in[n]}\frac{1}{s}\cdot\mathds{1}_{t+s>n}=\frac{1}{n}\,\sum_{s\in[n]}\frac{1}{s}\cdot\sum_{t\in[n]}\mathds{1}_{t+s>n}=1.

(5.31)

Also,

\mathbb{E}\left[H(\theta_{1})^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\in[n]}H(t)^{2}\leqslant H(n)^{2}

(5.32)

and

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[H(\theta_{1})H(\theta_{2})\right]&=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{t\in[n]}H(t)\cdot\sum_{s\neq t}H(s)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{t\in n}H(t)(n-H(t))\\
&=\frac{1}{n-1}(n-\mathbb{E}\left[H(\theta_{1})^{2}\right])\leqslant\frac{n}{n-1}.\end{split}

(5.33)

Thus
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}\right]=\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert^{2}
in view of (

\displaystyle\var[T_{n}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]-\|\bm{v}\|^{4}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j\in[n]}v_{j}^{2}H(\theta_{j})\Big]^{2}-\|\bm{v}\|^{4}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{t\in[n]}v_{t}^{4}\Big)\mathbb{E}\left[H(\theta_{1})^{2}\right]+\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant s\neq t\leqslant n}v_{s}^{2}v_{t}^{2}\Big)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[H(\theta_{1})H(\theta_{2})\right]-\|\bm{v}\|^{4}

6
Outlook

Our analysis in this paper raises a few questions, which we mention here as possible directions to pursue for the future.

1.

It will be interesting to get a sub-Gaussian tail probability bound with the improved limiting variance that we get here.

2.

Incorporating the simplification from the skeletal process in the algorithm itself might reduce the running time.

3.

Incorporating randomization in an online version of the algorithm might help analyze the online process under
milder
assumptions, but it is not immediately apparent. In this article, we studied the offline version of the GSW design algorithm, and pivot randomization played a crucial role in the analysis.

4.

The GSW design algorithm can be thought of as a discrete localization process where we want to sample a random assignment vector from a distribution “minimizing” a given cost function. In the present
case, the GSW design satisfies

\max\left\{\phi\cdot\left\lVert\cov(\bm{z})\right\rVert,(1-\phi)\cdot\left\lVert\cov(\xi^{-1}X^{\intercal}\bm{z})\right\rVert\right\}\leqslant 1,

and the algorithm behaves as a discrete coordinate-wise localization process.
In general, the distribution of
the random assignment vector minimizing the cost function will be unknown; it will depend on the covariate matrix
X
and the robustness parameter

\phi
. It will be interesting to incorporate the techniques from localization literature to develop an algorithmic construction for a general cost function.

5.

Our approach to finding and analyzing the skeletal process is general. This can be used to analyze the Horvitz–Thompson estimator based on the GSW design in non-uniform probability assignment
cases (see Remark
1.1
). It is, therefore, natural to ask if one can
generalize the strategy in different directions, e.g., in handling non-linear constraints.
