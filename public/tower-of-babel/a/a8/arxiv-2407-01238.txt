Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Recognizers for Activities of Daily Living
Gabriele Civitarese, Michele Fiori, Priyankar Choudhary, Claudio Bettini

Abstract
. The sensor-based recognition of Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) in smart home environments enables several applications in the areas of energy management, safety, well-being, and healthcare. ADL recognition is typically based on deep learning methods requiring large datasets to be trained. Recently, several studies proved that Large Language Models (LLMs) effectively capture common-sense knowledge about human activities. However, the effectiveness of LLMs for ADL recognition in smart home environments still deserves to be investigated. In this work, we propose ADL-LLM, a novel LLM-based ADL recognition system. ADL-LLM transforms raw sensor data into textual representations, that are processed by an LLM to perform zero-shot ADL recognition. Moreover, in the scenario where a small labeled dataset is available, ADL-LLM can also be empowered with few-shot prompting. We evaluated ADL-LLM on two public datasets, showing its effectiveness in this domain.

1.
Introduction

Sensor-based Human Activity Recognition (HAR) is a well-known research topic extensively studied in pervasive computing
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
.
The HAR task involves determining the most likely activity performed by a subject through the intelligent analysis of sensor data collected from one or multiple devices (e.g., wearables, environmental, radio-based). The adopted sensing technologies influence the granularity at which activities can be recognized. For instance, using inertial sensors in mobile/wearable devices makes it possible to detect physical activities like running, walking, and standing still. On the other hand, environmental sensors are particularly useful for recognizing high-level Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) like cooking, taking medicines, or watering plants in smart-home environments, also considering that subjects at home are less inclined to carry or wear devices all the time.

Among the many applications of sensor-based ADL recognition in the areas of energy management, safety, well-being, and healthcare, the long-term monitoring of the ADLs performed by elderly subjects in their homes gained significant importance
(
Rashidi and Mihailidis, 2012
)
.
The continuous advances in the medical domain significantly extended the world population’s life expectancy
(
Chen et al., 2023
)
. While this is surely an outstanding achievement, aging also brings several challenges to society. Among the aging-related issues, cognitive decline is one of the greatest health threats of old age, with nearly
50\%
of adults over the age of
85
afflicted with Alzheimer’s disease
(
Bishop et al., 2010
)
. Detecting the early symptoms of cognitive decline is crucial to implementing primary or secondary preventive therapeutic strategies
(
Hudon et al., 2020
)
.
One of the main markers used by clinicians to assess the cognitive status of a subject is represented by ADLs, whose impairment (i.e., difficulty or inability to perform them independently) is correlated to a rapid functional decline and a higher likelihood of progression to dementia
(
Jefferson et al., 2008
)
.
However, the subjects at risk usually have only a few sporadic visits with medical experts (e.g., neurologists, neuropsychologists, and geriatricians). Such visits often fail to accurately diagnose cognitive decline, especially when it may be revealed by subtle behavioral changes. Indeed, besides a few instrumental tests, and neuro-psychological evaluations at the time of visit, the clinicians mainly rely on possibly inaccurate reports provided by the subjects themselves or their caregivers. It is hence of great value to implement smart telemedicine strategies to continuously and unobtrusively monitor the subjects at risk while keeping them in the comfort of their homes.

In the last decades, several research groups proposed the use of unobtrusive sensing infrastructures in smart homes to continuously recognize ADLs in smart homes, to identify digital markers that may indicate the onset of cognitive decline
(
Khodabandehloo et al., 2021
;
Grammatikopoulou et al., 2024
)
.
For the sake of privacy, most of the existing solutions do not consider cameras or microphones (that may not be accepted in home environments), but they are usually based on environmental sensors monitoring the interaction of the subject with the home infrastructure (e.g., motion sensors, door sensors, pressure sensors).

However, the majority of the proposed approaches to automatically detect ADLs from sensor data require training deep learning models
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
, thus necessitating the collection of large datasets in the home environment (possibly labeled with the ground truth reporting what activity is being performed). Labeled data collection in these settings is privacy-intrusive, costly, and prohibitive
(
Babangida et al., 2022
)
. Another well-known line of research in this domain considers the adoption of purely knowledge-based approaches
(
Chen and Nugent, 2009
)
. The rationale behind these methods is that common-sense knowledge about human activities (e.g., a person usually prepares their meals in the kitchen, turns on the stove, and uses cooking instruments) can be modeled with a set of rules. Knowledge-based ADL recognition is performed by mapping sensor data windows to semantic concepts and performing rule-based reasoning. The advantage of this strategy is that it eliminates the problem of collecting labeled data in smart environments.
However, the design and implementation of knowledge models require the expertise of domain experts and knowledge engineers, and their capability of handling uncertainty and generalizing is typically limited
(
Civitarese et al., 2019
)
.

The recent
success of Large Language Models (LLMs) paved the way to LLM-based human activity recognition. Preliminary results in the state-of-the-art indicate that LLMs implicitly encode the knowledge about human activities, and hence they can potentially be used to cope with labeled data scarcity
(
Liu et al., 2023
;
Ji et al., 2024
)
. Given these promising results, we hypothesize that LLMs could be also leveraged to process data from environmental sensors in smart-home environments to reveal the ADLs performed by the monitored subjects, without the need to collect any labeled data.
We expect that LLMs would generalize across individuals and environments without requiring any patient- or environment-specific training data, thus offering a scalable approach. Moreover, an LLM-based approach would be more flexible in terms of the set of recognizable ADLs (that is usually fixed in supervised approaches).

In this work, we propose ADL-LLM, a method leveraging pre-trained LLMs to recognize ADLs in smart-home environments. At its core, ADL-LLM transforms raw sensor data into natural language sentences describing the high-level events observed in the home. ADL-LLM uses a specifically designed prompting strategy to query the LLM about the most likely ADL performed by the subject. Despite ADL-LLM does not require training data, we show that a small labeled dataset can further improve the recognition rate thanks to a few-shot prompting mechanism.

The main contributions of this paper are the following:

•

We propose ADL-LLM, a novel LLM-based method for zero-shot sensor-based ADL recognition in smart home environments.

•

Our results on two public datasets show that zero-shot ADL-LLM reaches recognition rates comparable (and sometimes better) than standard supervised approaches.

•

We extend ADL-LLM to perform few-shot prompting when
a small labeled dataset is available, by leveraging a semantic-based example selection strategy. Our experiments show that few-shot ADL-LLM outperforms standard supervised approaches in labeled data scarcity scenarios.

We provide the source code implementing our system to make our results reproducible

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Data Scarcity in Human Activity Recognition

The labeled data scarcity problem in Human Activity Recognition (HAR) is one of the major challenges limiting large-scale real deployments, and several research groups have been working on this problem for several years. Three categories of approaches have been proposed to mitigate labeled data scarcity: data-driven, knowledge-based, and neuro-symbolic.

2.1.1.
Data-Driven Methods

Most existing approaches to mitigate labeled data scarcity rely on deep learning models. The most common strategies are semi-supervised learning
(
Abdallah et al., 2018
)
, transfer learning
(
Dhekane and Ploetz, 2024
)
, and self-supervised learning
(
Haresamudram et al., 2022
)
.
Semi-supervised approaches use small labeled datasets to initialize the model, which is incrementally updated using pseudo-labels inferred on the unlabeled data stream
(
Abdallah et al., 2018
)
. Examples of semi-supervised methods proposed for HAR are self-learning, co-learning, active learning, and label propagation. On the other hand, transfer learning methods leverage models pre-trained on a source domain with a significant amount of labeled data, then adapted in a target domain with labeled data scarcity
(
Sanabria et al., 2021
;
Soleimani and Nazerfard, 2021
)
.
Self-supervised learning is a specific transfer learning approach, where the model is pre-trained on massive unlabeled datasets to obtain a reliable feature extractor
(
Haresamudram et al., 2022
;
Jain et al., 2022
;
Hiremath et al., 2022
)
. The pre-trained feature extractor is then fine-tuned only using a limited amount of labeled data on the target domain.
Existing approaches for self-supervised learning have been proposed for data obtained by wearable and mobile devices, since large datasets (e.g., Biobank
(
Yuan et al., 2024
)
, Capture-24
(
Chan Chang et al., 2021
)
) are available in this domain for model pre-training.
To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of self-supervised learning approaches for smart-home ADL recognition, because in this domain there are only a few small datasets, and learning a comprehensive representation that accommodates the diverse layouts of different homes is still an open challenge.

ADL-LLM can be considered a sort of zero-shot transfer learning approach, where a model pre-trained on language is used to recognize activities from a textual representation of sensor data.

2.1.2.
Knowledge-Based Methods

Several research groups investigated whether common-sense knowledge about human activities could be leveraged to completely avoid labeled data collection. These approaches proposed the use of logic formalisms (e.g., ontologies) to model the relationships between sensor events and activities
(
Civitarese et al., 2019
;
Chen and Nugent, 2009
)
.
However, building such logic models requires skills in the formalism model (e.g., OWL2 in the case of ontologies) and significant domain expertise. This task is usually performed by small groups of persons. Building a comprehensive knowledge model capturing all the possible ways of performing an activity is challenging even for experts, and we are not aware of public ontologies comprehensively covering all the possible human activities and context situations.

ADL-LLM has the same goal of knowledge-based approaches: using common-sense knowledge about human activities to recognize them. However, instead of relying on manually designed logic models, ADL-LLM leverages the knowledge implicitly encoded in pre-trained LLM to guarantee better generalization capabilities.

2.1.3.
Neuro-Symbolic Methods

Neuro-Symbolic AI (NeSy) methods combine the best from data-driven and knowledge-based worlds. Indeed, the goal of NeSy models is to combine symbolic reasoning and machine learning to mitigate data scarcity and improve the model’s interpretability
(
Hitzler and Sarker, 2022
)
.
Considering the HAR domain, logic formalisms have been used to refine the output of the data-driven model
(
Bettini et al., 2020
)
or to infuse knowledge inside deep learning models
(
Xing et al., 2020
;
Arrotta et al., 2024b
)
. In these methods knowledge constraints allow the model to capture high-level patterns without learning them from data, thus mitigating labeled data scarcity. However, NeSy methods have been only proposed for wearable-based HAR and their applicability to smart home ADLs recognition has not been explored yet.

For certain aspects, the capabilities of LLMs can be considered as Neuro-Symbolic since they can implicitly mimic reasoning
(
Fang et al., 2024
)
. Hence, ADL-LLM could be considered as a NeSy model whose data-driven component attempts to mimic symbolic reasoning.

2.2.
Using LLMs for Human Activity Recognition

In the following, we present relevant research works using LLMs in the HAR domain, distinguishing supervised and zero-shot techniques. Supervised methods combine LLMs and labeled HAR datasets for specific tasks (e.g., prediction, classification), while zero-shot techniques directly utilize pre-trained LLMs to identify activities on unlabeled sensor data.

2.2.1.
LLM-based approaches for supervised activity recognition

Several research groups studied how to use LLMs for the sensor-based HAR domain.
The most common approach is to fine-tune an LLM with labeled sensor datasets, abstaining an LLM specialized on a specific domain
(
Liu et al., 2023
;
Okita et al., 2023
)
.
Other works adopted multi-modal models to align sensor data embeddings and text embeddings leveraging contrastive learning to improve the recognition rate
(
Zhou et al., 2023
)
.
In Neuro-Symbolic HAR, some works used LLMs to infuse common-sense knowledge about human activities into a deep learning model
(
Arrotta et al., 2024a
)
. LLMs have also been leveraged to
transform sensor data windows first into sentences in natural language, and then into embeddings to improve the recognition rate and generalization capabilities of supervised models
(
Thukral et al., 2024
)
.
Finally, since LLMs are pre-trained to predict the next word in a sentence, some research groups fine-tuned them also to predict future sensor events
(
Takeda et al., 2023
;
Graule and Isler, 2023
)
.

2.2.2.
Zero-shot approaches for human activity recognition using LLMs

Similarly to ADL-LLM, other works aim at recognizing human activities using LLMs without any training phase.
For instance, some works show that LLMs can reason on raw inertial sensor data from mobile/wearable devices to distinguish between a limited set of low-level physical activities
(
Ji et al., 2024
;
Hota et al., 2024
)
.
Another line of research leverages a pre-trained model generating 3D motion sequences from text
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
to generate synthetic labeled data from wearable/mobile devices
(
Leng et al., 2024
)
.
Considering smart environments, ChatGPT has been used for zero-shot activity recognition
(
Xia et al., 2023
)
. However, this work assumes the availability of several sensors capturing the interaction of the subject with many household items (e.g., specific food ingredients), which is unrealistic in real-world deployments. In this work, we consider a more realistic setting considering the sensors that are usually deployed in smart homes for ADL recognition (e.g., motion sensors, magnetic sensors, plug sensors).
Finally, another work uses an LLM to provide a label to each cluster of raw sensor data time windows (collected in an initial deployment phase)
(
Gao et al., 2024
)
. A supervised classifier is then trained using the pseudo-labels obtained through the LLM.
However, such an approach is susceptible to the quality of clustering and, once the supervised classifier is trained, it may fail in capturing behavioral changes or activities not performed while collecting unlabeled data. Differently from that work, ADL-LLM aims at leveraging the flexibility of LLMs for each observed time window.

3.
The ADL-LLM framework

In this section, we present ADL-LLM: our LLM-based method for ADL recognition in smart home environments.

3.1.
Problem Formulation

We consider a classic problem formulation for ADL recognition.
A smart home is equipped with a set
\mathbf{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{m}\}
of environmental sensors. Examples are magnetic sensors revealing open-close events (e.g., doors, cabinets, etc.), motion/presence sensors revealing movement/presence in specific areas, and smart plugs revealing the use of appliances.
There is a set of target activities of interest
\mathbf{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n}\}
, usually suggested by clinicians.
As in most works in this domain, we consider a smart home environment inhabited by a single subject or with a sensing infrastructure able to properly associate a sensor’s activation with the subject that activated it.
Based on the interaction of the subject with the environment, the smart home generates a continuous stream of sensor activations and de-activations. This stream is partitioned in fixed-time windows of
\tau
seconds with a given overlapping factor
o
.
Given a window
w_{t}
, the goal of ADL-LLM is to determine the activity
A\in\mathbf{A}
that the subject was more likely performing in the interval
[t,t+\tau]
.

3.2.
Overall architecture of ADL-LLM

We assume that ADL-LLM runs on a trusted platform.
In the case of the medical application, it may run on a telemedicine platform
as one of the components of a complex system that continuously monitors subjects at risk of cognitive decline.
Figure
1
shows the overall architecture of the ADL-LLM framework.

Figure 1
.
Overall architecture of ADL-LLM. When the pool of examples is empty ADL-LLM, acts as a
zero-shot
ADLs recognition method. Otherwise, it is a

The smart home generates a continuous stream of raw sensor data that is pre-processed by the
Sensor State Generation
module to obtain a stream of sensor states. Intuitively, a sensor state represents the timespan in which a sensor is
active
(e.g., “
the TV in the living room is ON from 12:34 to 13:02’
’). Then, the stream of sensor states is segmented in fixed-time windows by the
Segmentation
module.

Since LLMs are specialized in natural language text, the main idea of ADL-LLM is to generate a natural language representation for each window, so that an LLM can “reason” on it leveraging its implicit common-sense knowledge.
The
Window2Text
module is in charge of transforming windows of sensor states into natural language sentences describing what happened in the home during that window.
The LLM receives as input a
System Prompt
(i.e., instructions about the task it has to perform) and a
User Prompt
(i.e., the textual representation of the window enriched with additional instructions for the LLM by the
User Prompt Construction
module). The output of the LLM is finally processed by the
Activity Label Extraction
module to obtain the most likely activity performed by the subject.

ADL-LLM works in two different modalities:

•

Zero-Shot
, where no labeled data is available

•

Few-Shot
, where a small labeled dataset is available

Indeed, in certain circumstances, it may be possible to collect a small dataset of sensor data windows labeled with the corresponding ADL. For instance, when the sensing infrastructure is physically installed in the home, the technicians may ask the subject to perform a limited number of activities (as a sort of "system calibration" phase). The data associated with these activities is manually labeled and stored in the
Pool of Examples
. In few-shot ADL-LLM, when processing a window, the
System Prompt
is enriched by the
Semantic-based Example Selection
module, which picks from this pool the samples that are the most “semantically” similar to the textual representation of currently processed window. The selected samples have the role of further guiding the LLM on the ADLs recognition task. In the LLM domain, this approach is known as
few-shot prompting
, and it is widely used to improve the output of LLMs
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
.

3.3.
The main components of ADL-LLM

In the following, we describe the main modules of ADL-LLM, i.e., those used both in the zero-shot and the few-shot modalities.

3.3.1.
Sensor States Generation

The smart home generates a continuous stream of binary sensor events
Stream=\langle\dots,E_{t-1}^{S_{l}},E_{t}^{S_{m}},E_{t+1}^{S_{q}},\dots\rangle
. Each
E_{t}^{S}
represents the activation or the deactivation of a sensor
S
at a specific time
t
. For instance, consider a magnetic sensor on the fridge. This sensor generates an ON event when activated (when the subject opens the fridge) and an OFF event when deactivated (when the subject closes the fridge). Other sensors share a similar behavior, like motion and pressure sensors. For sensors that generate continuous values, as the smart plug that continuously detects the power consumption of specific appliances, we use a threshold on the detected energy consumption to infer when the appliance is turned ON and OFF. For sensors that provide almost continuous values but are not directly associated with activation and deactivation events, like the temperature sensor, we discretize the values in different relevant ranges (e.g., for the temperature:
15
-
18
°C
,
19
-
24
°C
,
24
-
30
°C
,
>
30
°C
). Suppose that the thermostat in the living room detected
18
°C at
t_{i}
and then
19
°C
at time
t_{i+1}
. In this case, the stream includes a deactivation event at time
t_{i}
for the temperature being in the range
15
-
18
°C
, and an activation event at time
t_{i+1}
for the temperature being in the range
19
-
24
°C
.

We convert the stream of events into a stream of sensor states, where a sensor state
st(\sigma_{i},t_{s},t_{e})
denotes that the sensor state
\sigma_{i}
(derived from
S_{i}
) is true during the time interval
[t_{s},t_{e}]
. For instance,
st(\textit{FridgeDoorOpen},\textit{12:32pm},\textit{12:33pm})
represents the fact that the fridge door was open in the interval
[\textit{12:32pm},\textit{12:33pm}]
. As another example, the
st(\textit{NearTheStove},\textit{11:45am},\textit{12:00pm})
represent the fact that the motion sensor near the stove detected the subject’s movements from
11:45am
to
12:00pm
.

More formally, we generate a sensor state
st(\sigma,t_{s},t_{e})
when the stream contains two events
E_{t_{s}}^{S}
and
E_{t_{e}}^{S}
with
t_{s}<t_{e}
being the instants in which the events of activation and de-activation of sensor
S
occurred,
\sigma
indicates a property revealed by the activation state of
S
, and there is no
E_{t_{q}}^{S}
such that
t_{s}<t_{q}<t_{e}
.

3.3.2.
Segmentation

ADL-LLM employs a fixed-time segmentation of the stream of sensor states, considering windows of
\tau
seconds and an overlap factor
o
. Intuitively, each window is associated with all the sensor states whose interval intersects with the window interval. More formally, a window

w_{t}
over the interval
[t,t+\tau]
is associated with the sensor states
st(\sigma,t_{s},t_{e})
such that
t_{s}\leq t+\tau
and
t_{e}\geq t
.

Note that a window may be associated with multiple states even derived from different sensors. Based on the time intervals, we distinguish three categories that will be useful for text generation:

•

Inner states
: this is the case of
t_{s}\geq t
and
t_{e}\leq t+\tau
. This represents the simple case of the state occurring completely within the window.

•

Already active states
: this is the case of
t_{s}<t
. These states are already active before the start of the window.

•

Persistent states
: this is the case of
t_{e}>t+\tau
. These states persist after the end of the window.

Note that a state may be both
Already active
and
Persistent
when
t_{s}<t
and

t_{e}>t+\tau
.

3.3.3.
Window2Text

The
Window2Text
module converts a window
w_{t}
into its corresponding textual representation
T(w_{t})
. In the following, we provide a high-level description of how this conversion is done:

•

Each generated
T(w_{t})
begins by reporting the window’s length (
\tau
seconds) and the approximate time of the day (in the format hh:mm am/pm). The first was found useful to provide a temporal context to the LLM since
\tau
is usually shorter than the typical duration of an ADL; The second was found useful for the LLM to exploit correlations between common ADLs and the time of the day in which they are typically executed (e.g.,
preparing dinner
,
preparing lunch
).

•

After reporting the temporal context,
T(w_{t})
describes what happened in the home during
w_{t}
from the subject’s perspective (e.g., "the subject opened the fridge"). This choice, rather than considering a sensor’s perspective as done in other works
(
Thukral et al., 2024
)
(e.g., "the magnetic sensor on the fridge was activated"), leverages the LLM implicit knowledge of how humans perform activities. Indeed, subject-based descriptions are likely more common in LLM training data, and hence they may lead to more accurate results than indirectly interpreting actions through sensor events.

•

The subject’s perspective description of a window
w_{t}
has the following structure:

–

First, it reports the location where the subject is at the beginning of the window (if this information can be inferred from sensor data).

–

Then, it reports the
Already active states
(e.g., “the subject is already sitting on the couch”).

–

Finally, for each room visited by the subject during
w_{t}
(in chronological order), the description includes the actions (derived by the states) performed by the subject during
w_{t}
generated leveraging the
Inner
and
Persistent
states.

•

To transform each state into actions described in natural language, we implemented simple rules leveraging its category, its property
\sigma
, its relative temporal order with the other states in
w_{t}
, its duration, and the semantic location of the corresponding sensor. Hence,
Window2Text
assumes the availability of meta-data describing how to map each sensor
S
to its location in the home and the corresponding
\sigma
.

Since LLMs often struggle in reasoning with numbers and mathematics, in
Window2Text
we preferred to avoid absolute timestamps, considering instead relative temporal relationships between states by adopting temporal adverbs (e.g.,
“Then”
,
“After
3
seconds”
) and reporting the duration of the states (e.g.,
“for
5
seconds”
). Since we provide an anchor to absolute time by reporting the approximate time of the day at the beginning of the window description,
the reported events are also approximately anchored in time.
The above strategy is based on the observation that LLMs tend to perform better with qualitative information, as they can focus on understanding the sequence and relationships of sensor states rather than managing exact time calculations.
In Section
4.4.3
, we compare our approach with an alternative prompt using exact timestamps.

3.3.4.
System prompt

The system prompt is the set of instructions defining the general task assigned to the LLM and is independent of the specific window being considered. Our prompt first asks the LLM to act as an activity recognition system. Then, it provides information about the rooms in the smart home, the main interactions with the environment that can be captured by the available sensors, and the list of activities of interest.
Since this information depends on the specific smart environment and application, it must be provided during the installation phase.

3.3.5.
User Prompt Construction

The user prompt contains the information about the specific window
w_{t}
for which the LLM has to predict the most likely activity.
The user prompt contains:

•

The textual representation
T(w_{t})
of the window obtained with
Window2Text
(described in Section
3.3.3
).

•

Instructions about selecting only one activity among the target ones.

•

The sentence “Reason step by step”, used to instruct the LLM to perform intermediate “reasoning” steps before providing the predicted ADL. This technique is well-known as Chain Of Thought (COT), it usually leads to increasing the correctness of the LLM’s output
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
, and proved to be useful even in our system.

3.3.6.
LLM-based ADLs Recognition and Activity Label Extraction

The system prompt and the user prompt are provided as input to the LLM for each window
w_{t}
, and the LLM provides a textual description as output, reporting some facts supporting the selection of the predicted activity.
Figure
4
shows an example of the output returned by the LLM.

Figure 4
.

Finally, the
Activity Label Extraction
module uses regular expressions to extract only the activity label from the LLM’s output. In rare cases, despite the instructions, the LLM provides an activity label that does not syntactically match any of the candidate activities in
A
. For instance, instead of
snacking
, in a few cases, the LLM returned as output
preparing late night snack
or
preparing midnight snack
. In these cases, the
Activity Label Extraction
module uses the cosine similarity to compare the text embeddings of the labels. The label with the highest similarity to the predicted output is selected as the final prediction.

3.4.
Few-shot ADL-LLM components

When a small dataset of labeled sensor data is available, ADL-LLM can be improved by implementing few-shot prompting. This is achieved by extending the zero-shot architecture with two components described below.

3.4.1.
Building a Pool of Examples

In some cases, obtaining a limited amount of sensor data with the associated ground truth (ADLs) may be possible. For example, a technician may perform annotation during an initial calibration phase of the system, or some limited dataset collected in similar settings as the deployment target may become available.
Let
\mathbf{D}
be this dataset composed of a set of pairs
(w,A)
where
w
is a window of raw sensor data, while
A
is the corresponding activity label.
We convert
\mathbf{D}
to a set
\mathbf{E}
called
Pool of Examples
. This set contains the textual representation of the windows in
\mathbf{D}
(leveraging
Window2Text
) associated with the corresponding activity label. More formally, for each pair
(w,A)\in\mathbf{D}
,
\mathbf{E}
includes a pair
(T(w),A)
. Note that, since
\mathbf{E}
is a set, there is only one occurrence for each pair
(T(w),A)
. We also remove from
\mathbf{E}
all the examples sharing the same textual description
T(w)
but associated with different labels. Indeed, such examples represent patterns shared by multiple activities, and considering these as additional information may be misleading for the LLM.

3.4.2.
Semantic-based Example Selection

In principle, we may
include the whole set
\mathbf{E}
in each user prompt as additional context for ADL-LLM. However, this would introduce excessive costs (both in terms of money and performance), since each prompt would include a long sequence of textual examples. Hence, following the Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)
principle
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
, we include only the
k
examples in
\mathbf{E}
that are the most semantically similar to the input window.
More formally, given a textual representation
T(w_{t})
of a window
w_{t}
currently processed by ADL-LLM, we compute the corresponding embedding
\phi_{w_{t}}=\Phi(T(w_{t}))
,
where
\Phi
is a function (e.g., a pre-trained LLM) mapping sentences into a latent space representation (i.e., their embedding).
Then, for each pair
(T(w),A)\in\mathbf{E}
, we compute the pairwise cosine similarity between
\phi_{w_{t}}
and
\phi_{w}=\Phi(T(w))
.
We enrich the system prompt with the
k
pairs
(T(w),A)\in E
that are associated with the highest pairwise similarity between
\phi_{w_{t}}
and
\phi_{w}
. The pseudo-code for
Semantic-Based Example Selection
is summarized in Algorithm

4.
Experimental Evaluation

4.1.
Datasets

We evaluated ADL-LLM using two public datasets: MARBLE
(
Arrotta et al., 2021
)
and UCI ADL
(
Ordónez et al., 2013
)
. We chose these datasets because they include sensor data from a wide variety of sensors (e.g., magnetic, plug, motion) that we are currently considering on a project involving data collection in the homes of subjects with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). While the majority of works in the literature utilize the well-known CASAS datasets
(
Cook et al., 2012
)
, which primarily include data from motion sensors, these datasets are limited in their ability to precisely characterize the specific activities performed by subjects. Although data-driven models can identify low-level patterns within motion sensor data, our goal is to leverage the common-sense knowledge implicitly encoded in LLMs. Preliminary experiments suggested that using LLMs with datasets mostly based on motion sensors can not reliably recognize ADLs.

4.1.1.
MARBLE

The MARBLE dataset
(
Arrotta et al., 2021
)
includes data collected from
12
subjects in both single and multi-inhabitant scenarios.
The dataset was collected using the following environmental sensors: magnetic sensors to capture the opening and closing of various drawers and cabinets, pressure sensors to detect when a subject was sitting at the dining table or on the sofa in the living room, and smart plugs to monitor the usage of the stove and television. Additionally, the dataset includes data from an Android application on the user’s smartphone, which automatically detects incoming and outgoing phone calls. Finally, the dataset incorporates data from a smartwatch worn by the user, providing micro-localization information (based on an infrastructure using Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) beacons) at the room level.

We performed our experiments only considering the single-inhabitant scenarios.
Moreover, while the dataset also included inertial sensor data from the smartwatch (e.g., continuous acceleration on three axes), we ignore this type of data as it can not be directly transformed into a prompt in natural language.

The dataset includes the following
13
activities:
answering phone
,
clearing table
,
preparing hot meal
,
preparing cold meal
,
eating
,
entering home
,
leaving home
,
making phone call
,
setting up table
,
taking medicines
,
using PC
,
washing dishes
, and
watching TV
.

In our experiments, we do not consider the
washing dishes
activity since it is mainly characterized by inertial sensors in smartwatch data (not used by ADL-LLM). Moreover, we merged the
making phone call
and
answering phone call
activities into a single activity called
phone call
.

4.1.2.
UCI ADL

The UCI ADL dataset
(
Ordónez et al., 2013
)
includes data about the ADLs performed by two subjects in their own homes. The two homes are named
Home A
and
Home B
in the dataset. The overall data collection duration is
35
days:
14
days for Home A and
21
days for Home B.

Different environmental sensors are considered: PIR sensors (installed on doors, bathroom sink, stove, and shower areas) to detect the subject’s movements, pressure sensors to detect when the subject was sitting on the couch and the bed, magnetic sensors to detect when the subject was opening and closing cupboards or cabinets, and plug sensors to detect the usage of microwave and toaster.

Home A and Home B sensing infrastructure have some differences. Compared to Home A, Home B has a slightly more limited sensing infrastructure since it is not equipped with the PIR sensor over the stove, the plug sensor to detect the usage of the toaster, and the magnetic sensor to detect interactions with the bathroom cabinet. Nonetheless, Home B differently from Home A, includes additional PIR sensors installed on the kitchen, bedroom, and living room doors.

The dataset covers the following activities:
preparing breakfast
,
preparing lunch
,
preparing dinner
,
snacking
,
personal care
(labeled as grooming in the dataset),
showering
,
leaving
,
relaxing on couch
(labeled as
spare time/TV
in the dataset),
sleeping
, and
toileting
. Note that
preparing dinner
occurs only in the dataset collected in Home B.
In our experiments, we ignored the
toileting
activity since the real semantics of this activity and of the flush sensor were not clear in the dataset (and it was not reported in the dataset’s documentation).

4.2.
Supervised baselines

To show the effectiveness of ADL-LLM, we compare it with three state-of-the-art supervised approaches for ADL recognition.

•

MLP

(
Diallo and Diallo, 2021
)
: As the simplest baseline, we adopted a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) architecture that was proposed for smart-home HAR.

•

BiLSTM
(
Liciotti et al., 2020
)
: Also known as
Bidirectional LSTM
. It represents a standard recurrent neural network based on LSTM, processing windows in both forward and backward directions.

•

DeepConvLSTM

(
Ordóñez and Roggen, 2016
)
: Also known as
Deep Convolutional and LSTM Recurrent Neural Network
. It combines convolutional and LSTM layers to effectively capture both spatial and temporal features.

These baselines are representative because they reflect the most effective architectures used in the literature for supervised ADL recognition. We did not include methods specifically designed for labeled data scarcity scenarios because we are not aware of state-of-the-art approaches specifically designed for this setting.
As we already mentioned in Section
2
, methods like self-supervised learning or neuro-symbolic approaches have been proposed only for mobile/wearable-based HAR. Smart-Home ADL recognition introduces different challenges, since there are no large datasets available for pre-training, and different homes may have significantly different sensor layouts.

4.3.
Experimental Setup

4.3.1.
Implementation

We implemented a prototype of ADL-LLM in Python 3.10.12 language using the langchain 0.1.17 library.
As LLM for ADLs recognition, we used the OpenAI
‘gpt-3.5-turbo-0125’
model (setting temperature to
0
), while we used the OpenAI
‘text-embedding-3-small’
model to compute embeddings from the textual representation of windows for few-shot prompting. Based on what is suggested in the literature, we used
16
-second windows with
80\%
overlap for the MARBLE dataset
(
Arrotta et al., 2021
)
and
60
seconds windows with
80\%
overlap for the UCI ADL dataset
(
Ordónez et al., 2013
)
.

Considering the supervised baselines, we used the code available in public repositories (DeepCASAS
2
2

2

https://github.com/danielelic/deep-casas/
and DeepConvLSTM
3
3

3

https://github.com/STRCWearlab/DeepConvLSTM/
). We slightly modified the existing implementations to ensure compatibility with our software environment and data structures, while faithfully reproducing the architectures described in the literature. In all of our baselines, the LSTM layers have
64
units, while for the convolutional block of DeepConvLSTM we used a single convolutional layer with
8

2
x
2
filters. Before feeding it to the networks, environmental sensor data is converted into multivariate binary time-series following a standard approach in the literature
(
Van Kasteren et al., 2008
)
.

4.3.2.
Dataset splitting strategy

Since our major contribution is a zero-shot approach, in principle we could have used the entire dataset as a test set.
However, we also need a training set
for training the supervised baselines and as an example dataset for the few-shot ADL-LLM.
For this reason, we divided each dataset into
30\%
for the training set and
70\%
for the testing set. This splitting reflects a labeled data scarcity scenario, where only a limited labeled dataset is available. The training set is not used in any way by zero-shot ADL-LLM, while it is used to train the supervised baselines and to generate the pool of examples in few-shot ADL-LLM.

In our experiments, the training and test sets contain data from the same user. This is partly because some of the considered datasets include only one user and partly because in real deployments the small Pool of Examples used by few-shot ADL-LLM may be collected from the target home during the system’s initial deployment.
Nevertheless, no window of sensor data in the training set appears in the test set, ensuring that the model is not exposed to data leakage.
Note that the supervised baselines would likely exhibit even lower performance using a user-based split; hence the advantage of zero-shot ADL-LLM would be even clearer.

4.3.3.
Simulating data scarcity scenarios

In order to evaluate the effectiveness of few-shot ADL-LLM we consider different labeled data availability scenarios to populate the pool:
100\%
,
50\%
,
25\%
,
10\%
, and
5\%
of the training set. In these settings, the supervised baselines use the available labeled data for training. We expect that, in data scarcity scenarios, few-shot ADL-LLM outperforms the supervised baselines.

5.
Discussion

5.1.
Segmentation

In this work, we segmented sensor data using fixed-size windows, which is a standard approach in the literature. However, while this approach makes sense to train machine learning classifiers and find low-level patterns, this may be less suitable for LLMs. Indeed, fixed-size segments capture only a small portion of the activity, and they often include transitions between different activities.

We will explore dynamic segmentation strategies based on change-point detection
(
Aminikhanghahi and Cook, 2017a
)
. Indeed, dynamic segmentation can be leveraged to detect significant changes in sensor data distribution that are likely associated with changes in the performed activities
(
Aminikhanghahi and Cook, 2017b
)
. We believe that dynamic segmentation would allow LLMs to process more meaningful information to reliably perform ADL recognition.

5.2.
Open-world HAR

For the sake of this work, we constrained the LLM in predicting the most likely activity among a set of candidates. This is the most common approach in the ADLs recognition literature.
However, this is a limitation, since in real-life deployments we can not foresee in advance all the possible activities carried out by the subject.
Moreover, the answers from the model may “overfit” on the specific activity sets, especially when there is a one-to-one mapping between activities in the set and sensor events.

We will explore whether the flexibility of pre-trained LLMs can also be effective in open-world HAR, where there is no fixed set of activities to consider. One of the main challenges will be that the LLM may provide different labels for the same activity (e.g., eating may also be recognized as dining, snacking, having lunch, etc.). Hence, we believe that in such settings it will be necessary to semantically cluster the predicted labels.

5.3.
Cloud vs Local LLM processing

In our experiments, we adopted cloud-based LLM models due to their effectiveness and to simplify the implementation. However, outsourcing information about personal activities to such untrusted third parties raises several privacy concerns. Indeed, personal data about human activities may reveal private habits and health issues
(
Bettini and Riboni, 2015
)
.
Moreover, since we use the LLM for each sensor data time window, ADL-LLM may be too costly when using cloud-based LLMs.

Our vision is that open-source LLMs should be used in real-world implementations of ADL-LLM. Since the deployment of such models may be prohibitive in smart-home gateways, we believe that they should run on high-performance computing machines in a trusted domain (e.g., in a telemedicine infrastructure considering a medical application).
When this is not possible, compressed versions (e.g., quantized versions and/or with reduced number of parameters) may be even deployed in the home domain (e.g., in a home gateway powered with a GPU).
However, since these models are currently less effective, we expect a significant drop in the recognition rate.

5.4.
Hallucinations

LLMs are subject to hallucinations, that is the generation of content that may be irrelevant, made-up, or inconsistent with the input data.
In our use of LLMs, a hallucination may lead to a wrong prediction of the activity performed by the subject. It may also lead to an output that is not compliant with the instructions (e.g., predicting an activity not included in the given set or using a different format).
The impact of the first is similar to the one due to miss-predictions obtained with classic data-driven models. In our results, the F1 score captures this impact together with other possible factors like for example the effectiveness of the pre-processing.

Hallucinations leading to non-compliant outputs never occurred with the advanced models we adopted in our experiments, while they may occur when using simpler and smaller LLMs (as we previously discussed in Section
5.2
).
To avoid this problem, a test can be added to check whether the output activity is in the required set and, if necessary, ask the LLM to correct its prediction.

In general, hallucination mitigation actions can be taken. For example, the ADL-LLM’s system prompt adopts the Chain of Thought (CoT) that is known to mitigate the problem, even though we did not observe an advantage in terms of recognition rates in our experiments besides improving interpretability. Few-shot prompting also reduces the risk of hallucination. In future work, we will explore alternative approaches (e.g., ensembles of different LLMs).

5.6.
Robustness to noisy sensor data

Similarly to related work in this area, we test our algorithms on public datasets that usually have undergone a data cleaning pre-processing.
Sensors in real-world deployments may generate incorrect/noisy data or experience faults leading to missing data in the resulting data stream. This problem negatively impacts any HAR system independently of the underlying model: from classic data-driven solutions to our ADL-LLM.
In data-driven supervised approaches, the larger the available training set the more resilience to data noise is achieved.
However, real-world scenarios are often characterized by limited (or none) labeled data availability.
In the literature, common knowledge is known for improving the robustness to noise of HAR models in labeled data scarcity settings
(
Arrotta et al., 2024b
)
. Considering ADL-LLM, the common knowledge encoded in the LLM should help to identify activities even when some of the sensors are noisy or fail to provide data.
Nonetheless, when data is too noisy or the amount of missing information is significant, both ADL-LLM and classic data-driven solutions would lead to poor results.
In future work, we may perform experiments to evaluate the actual robustness of LLMs against sensor noise, by leveraging the real-world smart home data that we are currently collecting from the homes of elderly subjects in the context of a project in collaboration with neurologists. We may also design possible prompting strategies to make the LLM aware that the processed windows may be noisy.

6.
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we presented ADL-LLM: a zero-shot method for ADL recognition in smart-home environments. We showed that LLMs can reach recognition rates close to the ones of supervised approaches, with the benefit of not requiring labeled data.
While we evaluated ADL-LLM on public datasets, we intend to investigate its effectiveness in the specific medical context of neurology, considering data from real subjects at risk of cognitive decline.
We are currently collaborating with clinicians in the SERENADE project
5
5

5

https://ecare.unimi.it/pilots/serenade/
, collecting data from the smart homes of a significantly large group of subjects, mostly living alone and diagnosed with Mild Cognitive Impairment. Our long-term objective in this project is a fine-grained recognition of the onset of cognitive decline. The sensor data that we are collecting in these homes is unlabeled, and we plan to adopt zero-shot ADL-LLM to capture the performed ADLs in this setting and work from there on detecting behavioral changes and anomalies under the guidance of clinicians.
