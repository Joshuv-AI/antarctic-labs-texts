The Brinkman-Fourier System with Ideal Gas Equilibrium
Jan-Eric Sulzbach, Chun Liu

Abstract
In this work, we will introduce a general framework to derive the thermodynamics of a fluid mechanical system, which guarantees the consistence between the energetic variational approaches with the laws of thermodynamics. In particular, we will focus on the coupling between the thermal and mechanical forces. We follow the framework for a classical gas with ideal gas equilibrium and present the existences of weak solutions to this thermodynamic system coupled with the Brinkman-type equation to govern the velocity field.

Although a lot can be modeled in the classical setting, it has its limitations encompassing of reality.

The new and general approach of this paper is that we start from a given free energy function and dissipation functional.
Next, employing the basic laws of thermodynamic in combination with the energetic variational approach and appropriate constitutive relations, we derive a system of partial differential equations describing the thermodynamics of the fluid.
Due to the generality of this approach it can be easily adapted to different regimes.

In this paper we will focus on the ideal gas equilibrium, i.e. the equation of state for the internal energy and the pressure depends linearly on the density and absolute temperature.
For the momentum equation we choose a Brinkman-type diffusion, i.e an interpolation between the Stokes and Darcy’s law.
The details can be found in Section 2.

The aim of this paper is to prove existence of local-in-time weak solutions of the following system of partial differential equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\nabla p=\mu\Delta u-\nu\rho u,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s+\dv(su)=\Delta+\dv\big(\frac{\kappa\nabla\theta}{\theta}\big),

(1.3)

\theta

The entropy production rate is denoted by

\theta
and

Moreover, the pressure is defined as

and corresponds to the ideal gas case and the dissipation part in the momentum equation corresponds to the Brinkman-type equation.
The entropy is defined as
-s=k_{2}\rho(\log\rho-\frac{3}{2}(\log\theta+1))

In addition, we aim at a better understanding of the stability and dependence on the initial data and second law of thermodynamics as expressed in
[

In Section 3 we obtain the weak formulation of this system consisting of equations (

Moreover, we derive a priori bounds to the equations.

In Section 4 we state the existence theorem

2
Derivation of the System

In this section we derive the general model describing the thermal effects of a fluid with the example of the ideal gas case.
For the fluid model we consider a Brinkman-type equation.

The unknown variables in the system are:

1.

a non-negative measurable function
\rho=\rho(t,x)
the mass density;

2.

a vector field
u=u(t,x)
denoting the velocity field of the fluid;

3.

a positive measurable function
\theta=\theta(t,x)
the absolute temperature.

The new approach in the derivation of thermodynamic models focuses on the free energy of the system
\psi(\rho,\theta)
as a starting point and then applies the laws of thermodynamics and the energetic variational approach to obtain the complete model.

The notion of free energy is a useful concept in classical thermodynamics
[
Bai99
]
,
[
Sal01
]
because the change in the free energy is the maximum amount of work that a thermodynamic system can perform in a process at constant temperature, and its sign indicates whether a process is thermodynamically favorable or forbidden.
In the following derivation we use the definition of the Helmholtz free energy.
The second thermodynamic concept we use is the entropy.
Entropy can be understood as the measure of disorder of the system.
Note that for fixed density the entropy is a convex function in the temperature and that for a fixed temperature the entropy is a convex function in the density.

In the derivation we try to keep the statements as general as possible, since we can apply this framework to different settings, i.e. the porous media case or the Allen-Cahn and Cahn-Hilliard model.

From the thermodynamics of the ideal gas,
[
McQ76
]
and
[
BRR00
]
, we know that the relation between internal energy and the product of temperature and density is linear and similar for the relation between the pressure and the product of temperature and density.
Working backwards from this observation have the following definitions.

For the ideal gas we have the following definition of the
free energy

\displaystyle\psi(\rho,\theta)=k_{2}\theta\rho\log\rho-k_{1}\rho\theta\log\theta.

(2.1)

The
entropy
of a system is defined as follows

\displaystyle s(\rho,\theta):=-\psi_{\theta}=-\rho\big(k_{2}\log\rho-k_{1}(\log\theta+1)\big),

(2.2)

where
\psi_{\theta}
denotes the derivative of
\psi
with respect to
\theta
.

Remark 2.1
.

We note that the
\theta\log\theta
in the free energy is the weakest convex function with faster than linear growth.
Thus we have a non-vanishing contribution of the temperature in the entropy.

Moreover, the convexity of the free energy with respect to the temperature allows us to solve the equation of the entropy (
2.2
) for
\theta
.
For the given choice of the free energy this can be done explicitly

\displaystyle\theta(\rho,s)=\frac{1}{e}\rho^{k_{2}/k_{1}}e^{s/k_{1}\rho}

whereas in the general case this can be only done implicitly.

The
internal energy
is the Legendre transform in
\theta
of the free energy, i.e

\displaystyle e(\rho,\theta):=\psi-\psi_{\theta}\theta=\psi+s\theta=k_{1}\rho\theta.

(2.3)

Remark 2.2
.

Instead of having
(\rho,\theta)
as state variables, we can also consider
(\rho,s)
as new state variables yielding

\displaystyle e_{1}(\rho,s)=e(\rho,\theta(\rho,s))=\frac{k_{1}}{e}\rho^{1+k_{2}/k_{1}}e^{s/k_{1}\rho}.

This interpretation of the free energy is crucial because in the laws of thermodynamics we have
\rho
and
s
as state variables.

We assume that we have a closed system, i.e.
\rho
satisfies the following
continuity equation

\displaystyle\rho_{t}+\dv(\rho u)=0

(2.4)

Thus integrating over the domain
\Omega
and assuming that the microscopic velocity
u
satisfies
u\cdot n=0
on the boundary we obtain the
conservation of mass

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\rho(t,x)dx=0.

(2.5)

In addition, we assume that
\theta
moves with the particle and that the kinematics of the temperature
\theta
are governed by a
transport equation for the temperature
.
In other words, the temperature
\theta
is transported along the trajectory of the flow map with the velocity
u
.

Remark 2.3
.

The assumption of the transport of the temperature
\theta
with the particles is suitable for situations like the ideal gas, where the temperature is not determined by the environment or the case of a solution, where the temperature is determined by the temperature of the solvent.

Remark 2.4
.

We note that we have a weak duality of the time evolution of the temperature and the total derivative of the entropy in the following way.

If
\theta
evolves as
\frac{d}{dt}\theta=\theta_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\theta
then by testing this equation with the entropy
s
in the weak form yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\theta_{t}s+u\cdot\nabla\theta s\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}s_{t}\theta+\dv(su)\theta\,dx.

Thus the
kinematics of the entropy
are

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}s=s_{t}+\dv(su)

(2.6)

which are crucial in applying the laws of thermodynamics c.f. equations (
2.15
) and (
2.16
).

Next, we choose the
total energy
and
dissipation
as follows

\displaystyle E^{tot}=\int_{\Omega}\psi(\rho,\theta),~~\mathcal{D}^{tot}=\int_{\Omega}\nu\rho u^{2}+\mu|\nabla u|^{2}

and employ the energetic variational approach to derive the forces
[
GKL17
]
.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that dissipation depends on both
u
and
\nabla u
and hence
\mu
and
\nu
can be seen as interpolation parameters between the two pure cases.

Using the least action principle we have

\displaystyle A(x(t))=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}dt=-\int_{0}^{T}E^{tot}dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\psi(\rho(x,t),\theta(x,t))dxdt,

where
A
denotes the action and
\mathcal{L}
is the Lagrangian of the action.
Since we have no contribution of the kinetic part in the total energy the variation of the action yields

\displaystyle\delta_{x}A(x)=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}f_{cons}\delta_{x}dxdt.

The next step is to compute the variation of the action, where we first rewrite it in Lagrangian coordinates

\displaystyle A(x(X,t))=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{X}}\psi\bigg(\frac{\rho_{0}(X)}{\det F},\theta_{0}(X)\bigg)\det Fdxdt,

where
F
denotes the deformation gradient
F=\frac{\partial x}{\partial X}
.
We set
y(X,t)=\tilde{y}(x(X,t),t)
and the variation
x^{\epsilon}=x+\epsilon y
,
F^{\epsilon}=\frac{\partial x^{\epsilon}}{\partial X}
.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}A(x^{\epsilon}(X,t))

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{X}}\psi\bigg(\frac{\rho_{0}(X)}{\det F^{\epsilon}}\theta_{0}(X)\bigg)\det F^{\epsilon}dXdt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{X}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\psi\bigg(\frac{\rho_{0}(X)}{\det F},\theta_{0}(X)\bigg)\times

\displaystyle~~~~~~~\bigg[\frac{-\rho_{0}}{(\det F)^{2}}\det F\tr\big(F^{-1}\frac{\partial y}{\partial X}\big)\bigg]\det FdXdt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{X}}\psi\bigg(\frac{\rho_{0}(X)}{\det F},\theta_{0}(X)\bigg)\bigg[\det F\tr\big(F^{-1}\frac{\partial y}{\partial X}\big)F^{-1}\bigg]dXdt.

We transform the integral back to Eulerian coordinates and obtain

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{t}^{x}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\psi\big(\rho,\theta\big)\rho\nabla_{x}\cdot\tilde{y}+\psi\big(\rho,\nabla_{x}\phi,\theta\big)\nabla_{x}\cdot\tilde{y}dxdt.

Integration by parts yields

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{t}^{x}}\bigg[\nabla_{x}\bigg(\rho\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\rho}\bigg)-\nabla_{x}\psi\bigg]\cdot\tilde{y}dxdt

\displaystyle~~~-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}^{x}}\bigg(-\rho\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\rho}+\psi\bigg)\tilde{y}\cdot ndS_{x}dt

where
\tilde{y}\cdot n=0
and thus the boundary terms equate to 0. Putting everything together we have

\displaystyle\delta_{x}A(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{t}^{x}}\nabla\big(\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi\big)\cdot\tilde{y}\,dxdt.

for an arbitrary smooth vector
\tilde{y}(x,t)
satisfying
\tilde{y}\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

Thus we have

\displaystyle f_{cons}=\nabla\big(\rho\psi_{\rho}-\psi\big)=:\nabla p,

where we define the gradient of the pressure to equal the conservative force.
By this definition the
pressure law
satisfies the following relation

\displaystyle p(\rho,\theta)=\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi=k_{2}\rho\theta.

(2.7)

Lemma 2.6
.

The pressure satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla p=\rho\nabla\psi_{\rho}+s\nabla\theta.

Proof.

From the definition of the pressure we have
p(\rho,\theta)=\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi
and thus we compute

\displaystyle\nabla p(\rho,\theta)

\displaystyle=\nabla(\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi)=\rho\nabla\psi_{\rho}+\psi_{\rho}\nabla\rho-\nabla\psi

\displaystyle=\rho\nabla\psi_{\rho}+\psi_{\rho}\nabla\rho-\psi_{\rho}\nabla\rho-\psi_{\theta}\nabla\theta=\rho\nabla\psi_{\rho}+s\nabla\theta.

∎

Remark 2.7
.

From classical thermodynamics and statistical mechanics we know that the
ideal gas law
is

\displaystyle pV=NkT

where
V
is the volume,
N
the number of particles and
k
the Boltzmann constant
[
Sal01
]
.
Using tools from statistical mechanics, we can derive that

\displaystyle S(N,V,E)\equiv S(N,V^{2/3}E)

and as a consequence for a reversible adiabatic process we obtain

\displaystyle V^{2/3}E=const.

This relates the internal energy and the pressure as follows

\displaystyle e(\rho,\theta)=\frac{3}{2}p(\rho,\theta).

Thus we obtain a relation between the two constants
k_{1}
and
k_{2}

\displaystyle k_{1}=\frac{3}{2}k_{2}.

Following the maximum dissipation law, we now compute the variation of the total dissipation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\delta_{u}\mathcal{D}=\int_{\Omega}\big(\nu\rho u-\mu\Delta u\big)\tilde{u}dx=\int_{\Omega}f_{diss}\tilde{u}.

Using the classical
Newton’s force balance

\displaystyle f_{cons}+f_{diss}=0

yields a
Brinkman-type equation

[
Bri47
]
,
[
DB87
]
, which interpolates between the Darcy’s law and the Stokes equation

\displaystyle\nabla p=\mu\Delta u-\nu\rho u.

(2.8)

Remark 2.8
.

An overview over the energetic variational approach can be found in
[
LLZ05
]
.
More recent approaches in adding the thermodynamics into the framework of the energetic variational approach can be found in
[
LWL18
]
and
[
AL19
]
.

The next step is to verify the physicality of the given free energy.
Thus, combining the above results, we can show that the
Gibb’s equation
is satisfied

\displaystyle\theta Ds(\rho,\theta)=De(\rho,\theta)+p(\rho,\theta)D\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho}\bigg),

(2.9)

where we use the notation
Df
to denote a total differential.
Moreover we can interpret this in terms of classical thermodynamics as follows

\displaystyle E=TS-pV~~\text{ and }DE=TDS-pDV.

For further details in classical thermodynamics we refer to
[
McQ76
]
,
[
BRR00
]
and
[
Sal01
]
.

Next, we provide two useful basic Lemmas.

Lemma 2.9
.

The internal energy as a function of the density and entropy satisfies

\displaystyle{e_{1}}_{s}(\rho,s)=\theta

Proof.

Note that
\psi_{1}(\rho,s)=\psi(\rho,\theta)
. Then,
e_{1}(\rho,s)=e(\rho,\theta)=\psi+s\theta
and thus

{e_{1}}_{s}={\psi_{1}}s+\theta+s\theta_{s}=\psi_{\theta}\theta_{s}+\theta+s\theta_{s}=\theta.

∎

Lemma 2.10
.

The internal energy in terms of density and entropy is related to the free energy in terms of density and temperature

\displaystyle\psi_{\rho}(\rho,\theta)={e_{1}}_{\rho}(\rho,s).

Proof.

We compute the right hand side and obtain

\displaystyle{e_{1}}_{\rho}(\rho,s)=\psi_{\rho}+\psi_{\theta}\theta_{\rho}+s\theta_{\rho}=\psi_{\rho}.

∎

Now, we state the
laws of thermodynamic
.
The first law of thermodynamics relates the total derivative of the internal energy with work and heat

\displaystyle De_{1}=\text{ work }+\text{ heat},

(2.10)

where both the work and heat are in divergence form and more specific the heat term can be expressed as
\nabla\cdot q
with
q
being the heat flux.

We recall that the total change in a quality can be expressed as a divergence term plus some additional term.
Applying this to the entropy we obtain

\displaystyle s_{t}+\dv(su)=\nabla\cdot j+\Delta,

(2.11)

where the kinematics for the entropy are related to the transport of the temperature as expressed in equation (
2.6
).
And where we denote
j
as the entropy flux and
\Delta
as the entropy production rate.

The second law of thermodynamics states that the entropy production is non-negative:

\displaystyle\Delta\geq 0.

(2.12)

In order to derive a consistent model we need to supplement our equations by constitutive relations.
The relation between the heat flux
q
and the entropy flux
j
is given by
Durhem relation

\displaystyle j\theta=q,

(2.13)

and the relation between the heat flux
q
and the absolute temperature
\theta
is given by the
Fourier’s law

\displaystyle q=\kappa\nabla\theta,

(2.14)

where
\kappa
is the heat conductivity.

In order to find the expression for the entropy production rate
\Delta
we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}e_{1}(\rho,s)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[e_{1,\rho}\rho_{t}+e_{1,s}s_{t}\big]dx

(2.15)

Using the kinematics for the density
\rho
from equation (
2.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[e_{1,\rho}\big(-\nabla\cdot(\rho u)\big)+e_{1,s}s_{t}\big]dx

Applying Lemma
2.10
yields

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[-\nabla\cdot\big(e_{1,\rho}\rho u\big)+\big(\nabla\psi_{\rho}\rho\big)\cdot u+e_{1,s}s_{t}\big]dx

In order to have the full expression for the gradient of the pressure we have to incorporate the term
s\nabla\theta
which can only occur if the kinematics for the entropy are as in equation (
2.6
). And by equation (
2.11
) we have

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[-\nabla\cdot\big(e_{1,\rho}\rho u+e_{1,s}su\big)+\big(\nabla\psi_{\rho}\rho+s\nabla e_{1,s}\big)\cdot u

\displaystyle~~~~~+e_{1,s}\big(\nabla\cdot j+\Delta\big)\big]dx

By Lemma
2.9
and the Durhem equation (
2.13
) we have

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[-\nabla\cdot\big((e_{1,\rho}\rho+\theta s)u\big)+\big(\nabla\psi_{\rho}\rho+s\nabla\theta\big)\cdot u

\displaystyle~~~~~+\nabla\cdot q-\frac{q\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}+\theta\Delta\big]dx

Now, we can apply Lemma
2.6
to obtain

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[-\nabla\cdot\big(e_{1,\rho}\rho u+\theta su\big)+\nabla\cdot q+\nabla(\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi)\cdot u

\displaystyle~~~~~-\frac{q\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}+\theta\Delta\big]dx

From the definition of the pressure and the absence of external forces and heat sources we have that

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[\nabla p\cdot u-\frac{q\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}+\theta\Delta\big]dx

where we used that the divergence terms equal to zero under the boundary conditions
u\cdot n=0
and
\nabla\theta\cdot n=0
. Thus we have

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[\big(\mu\Delta u-\nu\rho u\big)\cdot u-\frac{q\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}+\theta\Delta dx\big]

and integration by parts yields

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[-\mu|\nabla u|^{2}-\nu\rho u^{2}-\frac{q\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}+\theta\Delta\big]dx

(2.16)

where we used that by equation (
2.8
)
\nabla p=\mu\Delta u-\nu\rho u
.
Since there are no external forces or heat sources in our system the total internal energy must be conserved and we obtain that

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{1}{\theta}\bigg(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\frac{\kappa|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\bigg).

(2.17)

We note that the second law of thermodynamics
\Delta\geq 0
is satisfied as long as
\theta>0
.
Thus

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}e_{1}(\rho,s)dx=0

(2.18)

and the total entropy is increasing

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)dx=\int_{\Omega}s_{t}+\dv(su)dx=\int_{\Omega}\dv j+\Delta\geq 0.

(2.19)

Combining the above results we are now able to state our system of equations.

The
model equations
are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s+\dv(su)

\displaystyle=\dv\bigg(\frac{\kappa\nabla\theta}{\theta}\bigg)+\Delta

\displaystyle\nabla p

\displaystyle=\mu\Delta u-\nu\rho u,

where

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{1}{\theta}\bigg(|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\frac{\kappa|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\bigg)

together with the state equations

\displaystyle-s(\rho,\theta)

\displaystyle=k\rho\big(\log\rho-\frac{3}{2}(\log\theta+1)\big)

\displaystyle p(\rho,\theta)

\displaystyle=k\rho\theta.

Remark 2.11
.

The generality of this new approach in the derivation of the dynamics of thermodynamic systems extends beyond the simple ideal gas case with applications in the porous media equation or the Cahn-Hilliard equation, where the only adaption to the new model takes place in the free energy.

3
Preliminaries

Starting from the derivation in the previous section we develop a thermodynamically consistent mathematical model based on the unknown variables
(\rho,\theta,u)
satisfying the following properties

1.

the problem admits a local-in-time solution for any initial data of finite energy;

2.

the total energy of the system remains constant in the absence of external forces or heat sources;

3.

the entropy of the system is increasing, i.e. the system evolves to a state maximizing the entropy;

4.

weak solutions coincide with classical solutions provided they are smooth enough.

(3.16)

\phi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
and

\displaystyle\sigma\geq\frac{1}{\theta}\bigg(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\frac{\kappa|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\bigg).

(3.17)

Minimal regularity of solutions required:

\displaystyle\theta>0~\text{ a.a on }(0,T)\times\Omega,~~\theta\in L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega),~~\nabla\theta\in L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega)

\displaystyle,~~q>1

(3.19)

\displaystyle s\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),~~\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta}\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)

(3.20)

\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\theta}\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),~~\frac{\rho|u|^{2}}{\theta}\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),~~\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)

.

(3.21)

Formal interpretation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s+\dv(su)=\sigma+\dv\bigg(\frac{\kappa\nabla\theta}{\theta}\bigg)~~\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega

,

(3.22)

\displaystyle s(0,\cdot)=s_{0},~~\nabla\theta\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=0,~~~~~~~~

(3.23)

where

\displaystyle-s=k\rho(\log\rho-\frac{3}{2}(\log\theta+1)).~~~~~~~

(3.24)

3.2
A Priori Estimates

The first observation we make is that the
conservation of mass
holds, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho(t,\cdot)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\,dx=M_{0}~~\text{for a.a. }t\in(0,T).

(3.25)

The second estimate that follows from the balance of energy is the
energy estimate

\displaystyle ess\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\,dx\leq C(\rho_{0},\theta_{0}).

(3.26)

From the second law of thermodynamics we observe that the
total entropy
of the system
S
defined as
S(t):=\int_{\Omega}s(t,x)dx
is non-decreasing, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}s(t,\cdot)\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega}s_{0}\,dx~~\text{for a.a }t\in(0,T).

(3.27)

In the next step we show the
positivity of the absolute temperature
.

First, we state a Lemma that for for a finite initial entropy the temperature is positive on a set with positive Lebesgue measure.
To this end, we define two regions in the
(\rho,\theta)
-plane:

•

non-degenerate region: low density and/or sufficiently large temperature

\displaystyle\rho\leq\overline{Z}\theta,~~\text{for some }\overline{Z}>0;

•

degenerate region: high density and/or very low temperature

\displaystyle\rho>\overline{Z}\theta,~~\text{for some }\overline{Z}>0.

Next, we set

\displaystyle s_{\infty}=\lim_{\theta\to 0}s(\rho,\theta)\geq-\infty~~\text{for any fixed }\rho.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=2,\,3
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Assume that the non-negative functions
\rho,\,\theta\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho dx=M_{0},~~\int_{\Omega}sdx>M_{0}s_{\infty}+\delta~~\text{for some }\delta>0.

Then there are
\underline{\theta}>0
and
V_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\big|\{x\in\Omega|~\theta(\cdot,x)>\underline{\theta}\}\big|\geq V_{0}.

The Lemma and the idea of the proof are a slight modification of a result by Feireisl and Novotný in
[
FN09
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Proof.

Assume there exist sequences
\rho_{n},\theta_{n}
satisfying the assumptions and such that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\to\rho~~\text{in }L^{1}(\Omega),~~\int_{\Omega}\rho dx=M_{0},~~\big|\{x\in\Omega|~\theta_{n}(x)>\frac{1}{n}\}\big|<\frac{1}{n}.

Then
\theta_{n}\to 0
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, by the definition of the entropy we have

\displaystyle|s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})|\leq\rho_{n}(1+|\log\rho_{n}|+|\log\theta_{n}|).

In the non-degenerate region we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\{\rho_{n}\leq\overline{Z}\theta\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{\{\rho_{n}\leq\overline{Z}\theta\}}\rho_{n}(1+|\log\rho_{n}|+|\log\theta_{n}|)dx

\displaystyle\leq c(\overline{Z})\int_{\Omega}\theta_{n}(1+|\log\rho_{n}|+|\log\theta_{n}|)dx\to 0

Thus,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\{\rho_{n}\leq\overline{Z}\theta\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx\leq 0.

Moreover, in the degenerate region we have

\displaystyle\int_{\{\rho_{n}>\overline{Z}\theta_{n}\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx=\int_{\{Z\theta_{n}\geq\rho_{n}>\overline{Z}\theta_{n}\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx+\int_{\{\rho_{n}>Z\theta_{n}\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx,

where

\displaystyle\int_{\{Z\theta_{n}\geq\rho_{n}>\overline{Z}\theta_{n}\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx\leq S(\overline{Z})Z\int_{\Omega}\theta_{n}dx\to 0.

Thus we conclude that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\{\rho_{n}>Z\theta_{n}\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx>M_{0}s_{\infty}~~\text{for any }Z>\overline{Z}.

However, this leads to a contradiction as

\displaystyle\int_{\{\rho_{n}>Z\theta_{n}\}}s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})dx\leq S(Z)\int_{\{\rho_{n}>Z\theta_{n}\}}\rho_{n}dx\to S(Z)M_{0},

where we used the notation

\displaystyle s(\rho,\theta)=\rho S(Z),~~Z=\frac{\rho}{\theta},~~\lim_{Z\to\infty}S(Z)=s_{\infty}.

∎

Thus we have shown that
\int_{V}\log\theta\,dx
is finite.
Next, a version of Poincare’s inequality provides the following.

Corollary 3.2

(
[
FN09
, Proposition 2.2]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=2,\,3
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Let
V\subset\Omega
be a measurable set such that
|v|\geq V_{0}>0
.
Then there exists a constant
c(V_{0})
such that

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq c(V_{0})\bigg(\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\int_{V}|v|dx\bigg).

Applying this result to
v=\log\theta
we obtain the following estimate for the temperature.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\log\theta|^{2}+|\nabla\log\theta|^{2}dxdt\leq c(data).

(3.28)

This implies the
positivity of the absolute temperature
with a possible exception on a set of measure zero.

4
Existence Theory

The ultimate goal of the forthcoming chapter is to show existence of weak solutions to the system of equations (
3.2
)-(
3.24
).

Theorem 4.1

(Local-in-time Existence)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n=2,\,3
be a bounded domain of class
C^{2,\nu}
,
\nu\in(0,1)
.
Assume that the data satisfies the initial conditions

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega)~~\text{for some }p\geq 3,~~\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}dx=M_{0}>0,

\displaystyle E_{0}=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\theta_{0}dx<\infty,~~s(\rho_{0},\theta_{0})\in L^{1}(\Omega).

In addition, let the initial density and temperature be positive, i.e.

\rho_{0}(x)>0
and
\theta_{0}(x)>0
for all
x\in\Omega
.
Then there exists a time
T>0
such that the system of equations for the thermal effects of an ideal gas in the Brinkman model admits a weak solution
(\rho,u,\theta)
on
(0,T)\times\Omega)
in the sense specified before, i.e.
(\rho,u,\theta)
satisfy relations (
3.2
)-(
3.24
).

The proof of the main result consists of several steps outlined as follows:

•

The continuity equation is regularized with an artificial viscosity term and the entropy production equation is replaced by the balance of internal energy.

•

Approximate solutions are obtained by a fixed point method.

•

Performing the first limit we convert the balance of internal energy to an approximate entropy production equation containing an additional small parameter.

•

We pass to the limit in the regularized continuity equation and finally pass to the limit in the entropy production equation.

Remark 4.2
.

When the energy law also contains a kinetic part the Brinkman-type equation with additional inertial term becomes the compressible Navier-Stokes-Fourier system for which the ideal gas case is still open
[
FN09
]
.

Remark 4.3
.

In the following subsections we set the parameter
k
to equal one.
This allows to simplify the computations without changing the main theorem.

4.1
Approximate scheme

The first step in the proof of Theorem
4.1
is to regularize the equations (
3.2
)-(
3.24
).

The
continuity equation
is regularized by means of an artificial viscosity term

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)=\epsilon\Delta\rho~~\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

(4.1)

with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle\nabla\rho\cdot n=0~~\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,

(4.2)

and the initial condition

\displaystyle\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0,\delta},

(4.3)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{0,\delta}\in C^{2,\nu}(\overline{\Omega}),~~\inf_{x\in\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}>0,~~\nabla\rho_{0,\delta}\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(4.4)

The
Brinkman-type equation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\dv\phi dx=\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla u:\nabla\phi dx+\int_{\Omega}\nu\rho u\phi dx,

(4.5)

for any test function
\phi\in X_{n}
, where

\displaystyle X_{n}\subset C^{2,\nu}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)

(4.6)

is a finite dimensional vector space endowed with the Hilbert structure of the Lebesgue space
L^{2}
and the functions satisfy

\displaystyle\phi=0~~\text{on }\partial\Omega~~\text{ no-slip boundary conditions.}

(4.7)

Instead of the entropy production, we consider a modified
internal energy
equation of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}e_{\delta}+\dv(e_{\delta}u)-\kappa\Delta\theta&=\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}-\rho\theta\dv u+\delta\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\\
&+\epsilon\delta(\rho^{\Gamma}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}-\delta\theta^{5}~~\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\end{split}

(4.8)

with the Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle\nabla\theta\cdot n=0~~\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,

(4.9)

and the initial condition

\displaystyle\theta(0,\cdot)=\theta_{0,\delta},

(4.10)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{0,\delta}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega),~~ess\inf_{x\in\Omega}\theta_{0,\delta}>0.

(4.11)

Here

\displaystyle e_{\delta}(\rho,\theta)=\rho\theta.

(4.12)

Moreover, the approximate
internal energy balance
holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta(t)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}\theta_{0,\delta}dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}-\delta\theta^{5}\,dxd\tau.

(4.13)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

The quantities
\epsilon
and

are small positive parameters, yielding better estimates of the approximate scheme.

4.2
Solvability of the approximate system

The second step is to show existence of classical solutions to the approximate system.

Theorem 4.4

(Global existence for the approximate system)
.

Let
\epsilon
,
\delta
be given positive parameters. Under the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
there exists a
\Gamma_{0}>0
such that for all
\Gamma>\Gamma_{0}
the approximate system (

The idea of the proof follows standard arguments:

•

Given the velocity field
u

•

After solving the continuity equation we determine the temperature
\theta
of the quasilinear parabolic problem, i.e. the internal energy equation, where
u,~\rho

•

To close the loop, the solution

Lemma 4.5

(
[

Let

In this part we focus on the quasilinear parabolic problem for the unknown temperature

First, we state a comparison principle.

Suppose that

).
∎

In addition, we observe the importance of the term

Lemma 4.9

(
[

, Lemma 3.3]
)
.

Let the data

belong to the regularity class as specified above and let the initial data

Then any strong solution

4.3
Uniform estimates

In this section we establish uniform bounds, similar to those in chapter 3.
The existence of such uniform bounds guarantees the global existence of the approximate solution in the desired spaces.
Moreover, these estimates play a crucial role in the limit passage in the following sections.

First, from the approximate continuity equation it follows that the total mass of the system still remains constant in time, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho(t)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}dx=M_{0,\delta}~~\text{for all }t\in[0,T].

(4.25)

Next, taking
u
as a test function in the Brinkman-type equation (
4.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\dv u-|\nabla u|^{2}-\rho|u|^{2}dx=0.

(4.26)

From the approximate internal energy equation (
4.8
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta(t)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}\theta_{0,\delta}dx+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}-\epsilon\delta\theta^{5}dxdt.

(4.27)

Instead of working with the internal energy balance we manipulate the equation (
4.8
) to get an approximate entropy production.
To this end, we recall from the classical theory of thermodynamics that

\displaystyle\frac{de}{dt}=W+Q~~\text{and }T\frac{ds}{dt}=T\Delta+Q.

Therefore, we compute

\displaystyle~~~-\partial_{t}\big(\rho(\log\rho-\log\theta-1)\big)-\dv\big(\rho(\log\rho-\log\theta-1)u\big)

\displaystyle=-\rho_{t}(\log\rho-\log\theta-1)-\rho_{t}+\frac{\rho\theta_{t}}{\theta}-(\log\rho-\log\theta-1)\dv(\rho u)

\displaystyle~~~\,-\rho u\cdot\nabla(\log\rho-\log\theta-1)

\displaystyle=(\log\theta-\log\rho)(\rho_{t}+\dv(\rho u))+\frac{\rho\theta_{t}}{\theta}+\dv(\rho u)-u\cdot\nabla\rho+\frac{\rho}{\theta}u\cdot\nabla\theta

\displaystyle=(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)\epsilon\Delta\rho+\rho\dv u+\frac{\rho}{\theta}(\theta_{t}+u\cdot\nabla\theta)+\epsilon\Delta\rho

\displaystyle=(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)\epsilon\Delta\rho+\rho\dv u+\frac{1}{\theta}\big((\rho\theta)_{t}+\dv(\rho\theta u)\big)

which gives the relation between the internal energy and the entropy of the system.

Then, dividing the internal energy balance by
\theta
yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s+\dv(su)-\frac{\Delta\theta}{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta}(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2})+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}-\delta\theta^{4}

\displaystyle+\epsilon\Delta\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1).

Rewriting this expression yields the approximate entropy equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s+\dv(su)-\dv(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta}(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta})+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}-\delta\theta^{4}

\displaystyle+\epsilon\Delta\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1).

For higher regularity we modify the entropy production rate slightly and obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}s+\dv(su)-\dv(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta})&=\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}\\
&-\delta\theta^{4}+\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\Delta\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1).\end{split}

(4.28)

The two approximate equations give rise to the following estimates, where we multiplied equation (
4.28
) by an arbitrary positive constant
\overline{\theta}
and integrated over the space time domain.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H_{\delta,\overline{\theta}}(\rho,\theta)(\tau)dx+\overline{\theta}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}+\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}H_{\delta,\overline{\theta}}(\rho,\theta)(0)dx+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}+\delta\theta^{4}-\epsilon\overline{\theta}\Delta\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)dxdt,

where
H_{\delta,\overline{\theta}}(\rho,\theta)=e-s=\rho\theta+\rho(\log\rho-\log\theta-1)
.
Integration by pats in the last term yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}H_{\delta,\overline{\theta}}(\tau)dx+\overline{\theta}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta}(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}dxdt\\
&+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}dxdt\\
&=\int_{\Omega}H_{\delta,\overline{\theta}}(0)dx+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}+\delta\theta^{4}+\epsilon\overline{\theta}\frac{\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}dxdt~~\text{for all }\tau\in[0,T].\end{split}

(4.29)

We observe that we can control the terms on the right-hand side by the terms on the left-hand side and the initial data.

Indeed, the quantity
\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}
is dominated for low temperatures by its counterpart
\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}
.
Moreover, we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\overline{\theta}\frac{\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\bigg|\epsilon\overline{\theta}\frac{\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}\bigg|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\overline{\theta}\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}dxdt+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\overline{\theta}|\nabla\rho|^{2}dxdt,

where we can bound both terms with terms on the left-hand side of equation (
4.29
) for sufficiently small
\epsilon
.

Now, we can summarize the estimates as follows

\displaystyle ess\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\int_{\Omega}H_{\delta,\overline{\theta}}(\rho,\theta)(t)dx\leq c

(4.30)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta}\bigg(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\bigg)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}dxdt\leq c

(4.31)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\epsilon\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}+\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}dxdt\leq c,

(4.32)

where c is a positive constant depending on the initial data but is independent of
T,\,\epsilon,\,\delta
and
n
.

4.4
Limit Passage

The third step is to pass to the limit in the approximate/ regularized system.
This is done in three steps.
First, we let
n\to\infty
. This is the approximation step in the Fourier series for the Brinkman-type equation.
Next, we pass to the limit in
\epsilon
as
\epsilon\to 0
, i.e. the additional regularity term for the density
\rho
.
And finally, we let
\delta\to 0
.
This is the most crucial step because only here we will have a smallness condition on the time interval in which the weak solution exists.

Each step utilizes similar ideas: from finding uniform bounds to the div-curl lemma.
For the details of the theorems used in these steps we refer to the appendix.

4.4.1
Limit
n\to\infty

Let the vector space
X
be defined as

\displaystyle X\equiv\bigcup_{n}X_{n}~~\text{is dense in }H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

For
\epsilon>0
and
\delta>0
fixed let
(\rho_{n},u_{n},\theta_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of approximate solutions.

In addition to the uniform estimates (
4.30
)-(
4.32
) we obtain the following estimates:

From the quasilinear parabolic equation for the absolute temperature
\theta
we obtain that
0\leq\underline{\theta}\leq\theta_{n}(t,x)\leq\overline{\theta}
for all
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega
.
Hence,
\|\nabla u_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c
and together with the boundary condition
u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
we have
u_{n}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then, by the Sobolev embedding
u_{n}\in L^{6}(\Omega)
.
The boundedness of the sequence then implies that

\displaystyle u_{n}\to u~~\text{weakly in }H^{1}(\Omega).

(4.33)

From the kinetic equation it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\epsilon\|\nabla\rho_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u_{n}\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\rho_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}.

By the interpolation inequality we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|\rho_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\epsilon\|\nabla\rho_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(M_{0,\delta},\epsilon,\|u\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)},\Omega).

Thus the sequence
\rho_{n}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.
Therefore we can assume that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\to\rho~~\text{weakly-(*) in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

(4.34)

By applying Poincare inequality to the additional term in equation (
4.32
) we get

\displaystyle\rho_{n},~\rho_{n}^{\Gamma/2}~~\text{bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

(4.35)

and by interpolation

\displaystyle\rho_{n}~~\text{bounded in }L^{\Gamma}(0,T;L^{3\Gamma}(\Omega)).

(4.36)

By the boundedness of the entropy production rate we obtain that

\displaystyle\nabla\theta_{n}^{\Gamma/2}~~\text{bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))

(4.37)

and

\displaystyle\theta_{n}~~\text{bounded in }L^{5}((0,T)\times\Omega).

(4.38)

In addition,
\nabla\log\theta_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
This implies that
\theta_{n},~\theta_{n}^{\Gamma/2}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and we obtain

\displaystyle\theta_{n}\to\theta~~\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

(4.39)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta^{3}}dxdt\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta_{n}^{3}}dxdt.

(4.40)

By the standard Sobolev embedding we derive the higher integrability estimates of
\theta_{n}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\nabla\theta_{n}~~\text{bounded in }L^{\Gamma}(0,T;L^{3\Gamma}(\Omega)).

(4.41)

As a byproduct, we get that

\displaystyle\log\theta_{n}~~\text{bounded in }L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega)~\text{for any finite }q\geq 1.

(4.42)

Now, we are able to pass to the limit in the equations with respect to the parameter
n
.

The limit in the Poisson equation is obtained via the standard Galerkin argument, where we note that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho_{n}\theta_{n}\dv u_{n}dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\big(\|\rho_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}+\|\theta_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\big).

(4.43)

Thus we can pass to the limit in the Brinkman equation by the bounds established before

\displaystyle-\rho u+\Delta u=\nabla(\rho\theta)~~\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega.

(4.44)

From the kinetic equation we have

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}-\epsilon\Delta\big)[\rho_{n}]=-\nabla\rho_{n}\cdot u-\rho_{n}\dv u_{n},

where the terms on the right-hand side are bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{3/2}(\Omega))
.
Thus

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\to\rho~~\text{ a.e. in }(0,T)\times\Omega

(4.45)

and we can let
n\to\infty
in the approximate continuity equation to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)=\epsilon\Delta\rho~~\text{a.e. in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

(4.46)

where the density
\rho
is a positive function satisfying

\displaystyle\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\cdot n|_{\partial\Omega}=0~~\text{for a.a. }t\in(0,T)

(4.47)

in the sense of traces, together with the initial data

\displaystyle\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0,\delta}.

(4.48)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\nabla\rho_{n}\to\nabla\rho~~\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

(4.49)

where we used that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho_{n}^{2}(\tau)dx+2\epsilon\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho_{n}|^{2}dxdt\to\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\delta,0}^{2}dx-\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{2}\dv udxdt

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{2}\dv udxdt=\int_{\Omega}\rho^{2}(\tau)dx+2\epsilon\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|^{2}dxdt.

Now, we can consider the limit in the entropy balance equation.
The main difficulty here is to show the strong convergence of the temperature.
To this end, we apply the Div-Curl Lemma discovered by Tartar
[
Tar75
]
to the function
U
and
V
, specified below.
The details off the div-curl Lemma can be found in the Appendix.

We rewrite the equation (
4.28
) in the following form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}s&+\dv(su)-\dv(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta})-\epsilon\dv(\nabla\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1))\\
&=\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}\\
&+\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\big(\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}\big),\end{split}

(4.50)

where we used that

\displaystyle\epsilon\Delta\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)=\epsilon\dv(\nabla\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1))-\epsilon\big(\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\theta}{\theta}\big).

Setting

\displaystyle U=[s,su-\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta}-\epsilon\nabla\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)]~~\text{and }~V=[\theta,0,0,0]

we can check the assumptions for the Div-Curl Lemma.

The temperature
\theta
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
and
curl(V)
yields only spatial partial derivatives and thus is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
which is compact embedded into
W^{-1,2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.
By the uniform estimates obtained before we note that the right-hand side of equation (
4.50
) is bounded in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
and therefore precompact in
W^{-1,s}((0,T)\times\Omega)
provided
s\in[1,\frac{4}{3})
.
Thus it remains to show that
U
is bounded in a better space than
L^{1}
.
To see this we note that

\displaystyle|s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})|\leq c(\rho_{n}+\rho_{n}|\log\rho_{n}|+\rho_{n}|\log\theta_{n}|)

and by the uniform estimates
s
is bounded in
L^{\Gamma/3}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.
In addition,
su
is bounded in
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, where
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{\Gamma}
provided
\Gamma>6
.

For the other terms we have that
\nabla\log\theta_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
and

\displaystyle\epsilon\nabla\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)~~\text{is bounded in }L^{\frac{2\Gamma}{\Gamma+6}}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Then the Div-Curl Lemma states that

\displaystyle\overline{s(\rho,\theta)\,\theta}=\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}\theta,

(4.51)

where the symbol
\overline{F(u)}
denotes the weak
L^{1}
-limit of the sequence
F(u_{n})
of composed functions.

The goal is now, to conclude that we have almost everywhere convergence of
\theta
.
By the definition of the entropy we have

\displaystyle s(\rho,\theta)=-\rho(\log\rho-\log\theta-1),

where we note that the entropy
s
is increasing in the temperature.
Thus,

\displaystyle\overline{\rho\log(\theta)\theta}\geq\overline{\rho\log\theta}\theta.

(4.52)

Moreover, by the strong convergence of
\rho_{n}
see equation (
4.45
), we have

\displaystyle\overline{\rho\log(\theta)\theta}=\rho\overline{(\log\theta)\theta}.

Combining the above equations we infer that

\displaystyle\overline{\log(\theta)\theta}=\overline{\log\theta}\theta.

By the strict convexity of the function
x\log x
we have that

\displaystyle\theta_{n}\to\theta~~\text{a.e in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

(4.53)

For details in the argument we refer to Appendix.

Now, we can take the limit in the approximate entropy equation.
To this end, we first turn the equation into an inequality by applying Youngs inequality to the
\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\theta
term.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}s+\dv(su)-\dv(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta})-\epsilon\dv(\nabla\rho(\log\theta-\log\rho-1))\\
\geq&\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}\\
&+\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho},\end{split}

(4.54)

As a consequence from the previous results we can identify the limits of the individual terms.

\displaystyle s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})

\displaystyle\to s(\rho,\theta)~~\text{in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle s(\rho_{n},\theta_{n})u_{n}

\displaystyle\to s(\rho,\theta)u~~\text{weakly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

The almost all convergence of
\theta_{n}
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta_{n}}

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{\theta}~~\text{in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\nabla\log\theta_{n}

\displaystyle\to\nabla\log\theta~~\text{weakly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\theta_{n}^{\Gamma-1}\nabla\theta_{n}

\displaystyle\to\theta^{\Gamma-1}\nabla\theta~~\text{weakly in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)~p>1.

To control the
\epsilon
-term we note that

\displaystyle|\epsilon(\log\theta_{n}-\log\rho_{n}-1)\nabla\rho_{n}|\leq c|\nabla\rho_{n}|(|\log\theta_{n}|+|\log\rho_{n}|+1)

and all terms on the right are bounded in
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for some
p>1
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle\epsilon(\log\theta_{n}-\log\rho_{n}-1)\nabla\rho_{n}\to\epsilon(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)\nabla\rho~~\text{weakly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Identifying the limit in the remaining terms of the entropy production rate yields

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla u_{n}}{\sqrt{\theta_{n}}}

\displaystyle\to\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{\theta}}~~\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla\rho_{n}}{\sqrt{\rho_{n}}}

\displaystyle\to\frac{\nabla\rho}{\sqrt{\rho}}~~\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Gamma\rho_{n}^{\Gamma-2}+2}\nabla\rho_{n}

\displaystyle\to\sqrt{\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2}\nabla\rho~~\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega).

These convergence results are sufficient to perform the weak limit in the approximate entropy equation as
n\to\infty
.
We note that the inequality is preserved under the weak limit due to the lower semi-continuity of convex superposition operators.
This allows us to conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)\big(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta}-\epsilon(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\phi dxdt\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}\bigg)\phi dxdt\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}\bigg)\phi dxdt\leq-\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{\delta,0},\theta_{\delta,0})\phi(0,\cdot)dx\end{split}

(4.55)

for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
.

Thus we can conclude that after the first limit the quantities satisfy the following system of equations.

The
approximate continuity equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)=\epsilon\Delta\rho~~\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

(4.56)

with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle\nabla\rho\cdot n=0~~\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,

(4.57)

and the initial condition

\displaystyle\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0,\delta}.

(4.58)

The
Brinkman-type equation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\dv\phi dx=\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla u:\nabla\phi+\nu\rho u\cdot\phi dx,

(4.59)

for any test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
with

\displaystyle\phi=0~~\text{on }\partial\Omega~~\text{ no-slip boundary conditions.}

(4.60)

The
approximate internal energy balance

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta(t)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}\theta_{0,\delta}dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}-\delta\theta^{5}\,dxd\tau.

(4.61)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

The
approximate entropy inequality

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)\big(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta}-\epsilon(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\phi dxdt\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}\bigg)\phi dxdt\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}\bigg)\phi dxdt\leq-\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{\delta,0},\theta_{\delta,0})\phi(0,\cdot)dx\end{split}

(4.62)

for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
with
\phi\geq 0
.

Rewriting the last equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{\delta,0},\theta_{\delta,0})\phi(0,\cdot)dx-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)\big(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta}-\epsilon(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\phi dxdt\\
&\geq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}\bigg)\phi dxdt\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}\bigg)\phi dxdt\end{split}

for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
with
\phi\geq 0
we note that the left-hand side of the equation can be understood as a non-negative linear form on the space of smooth function with compact support in
[0,T)\times\overline{\Omega}
.
By the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a regular, non-negative Borel measure
\Sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}
on
[0,T)\times\overline{\Omega}
that can be extended to
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)\big(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta}-\epsilon(\log\theta-\log\rho-1)\nabla\rho\big)\cdot\nabla\phi dxdt\\
&+\langle\Sigma_{\epsilon,\delta},\phi\rangle_{[\mathcal{M},C]([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})}=-\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{\delta,0},\theta_{\delta,0})\phi(0,\cdot)dx\end{split}

(4.63)

for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
with
\phi\geq 0
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}

\displaystyle+\epsilon\delta(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2)|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\epsilon\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}.

4.4.2
Limit
\epsilon\to 0

The next step is to let
\epsilon\to 0
in the approximate system.
To this end, let
(\rho_{\epsilon},u_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon})
be a solution of equations (
4.56
)-(
4.63
).

Then, similar to the previous part we obtain the following estimates independent of
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon>0}\bigg\{\essup_{t\in(0,T)}\int_{\Omega}H_{\delta,\overline{\theta}}(t)\,dx\bigg\}<\infty

(4.64)

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon>0}\bigg\{\Sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}\big[[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\big]\bigg\}<\infty.

(4.65)

This implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon>0}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1})|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}\,dxdt\bigg\}<\infty,

(4.66)

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon>0}\bigg\{\epsilon\delta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\Gamma\rho^{\Gamma-2}+2\big)|\nabla\rho|^{2}\,dxdt\bigg\}<\infty,

(4.67)

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon>0}\bigg\{\epsilon\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}{\rho}\,dxdt\bigg\}<\infty.

(4.68)

As in the previous part we conclude that

\displaystyle\rho_{\epsilon}^{\Gamma/2}~~\text{bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

(4.69)

\displaystyle\theta_{\epsilon}^{\Gamma/2}~~\text{bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

(4.70)

Thus from the Brinkman equation we obtain

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}~~\text{bounded in }H^{1}(\Omega),

(4.71)

and

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}\to u~~\text{weakly in }H^{1}(\Omega).

(4.72)

Passing to the limit in the Brinkman equation yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla u:\nabla\varphi dx+\int_{\Omega}\nu\rho u\cdot\varphi dx-\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\dv\varphi dx=0,

(4.73)

for any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
with
\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

Multiplying the approximate continuity equation by
\rho_{\epsilon}
and integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}^{2}(t)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho_{\epsilon}|^{2}dxdt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}^{2}dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}^{2}\dv u_{\epsilon}dxdt.

Thus, we observe that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\epsilon}\nabla\rho_{\epsilon}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

(4.74)

\displaystyle\rho_{\epsilon}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

(4.75)

and in particular

\displaystyle\epsilon\nabla\rho_{\epsilon}\to 0~~\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

(4.76)

This yields that

\displaystyle\rho_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon}\to\rho u~~\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

(4.77)

Thus, we can pass to the limit in the approximate continuity equation as
\epsilon\to 0
and the limit
\rho
satisfies the integral identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\rho\partial_{t}\phi+\rho u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt+\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}dx=0

(4.78)

for any test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
i.e.
\rho,\,u
satisfy in the following equation in the sense of distributions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)=0.

(4.79)

It remains to pass to the limit in the approximate entropy equation.
To this end, we once more need to show strong convergence of the absolute temperature.
The idea is again to show uniform estimates for
\theta_{\epsilon}
and then apply the Div-Curl Lemma.

Taking a closer look at the bounds of the entropy production rate, we derive that

\displaystyle\theta_{\epsilon}^{\Gamma/2}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\theta_{\epsilon}^{-1}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{3}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\theta_{\epsilon}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{5}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\log\theta_{\epsilon}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{2}(0,T:H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Now, for the application of the Div-Curl Lemma we use the same idea as in the previous limit case.

Setting

\displaystyle U_{\epsilon}=\bigg[s(\rho_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon}),s(\rho_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon})u_{\epsilon}+\frac{\nabla\theta_{\epsilon}}{\theta_{\epsilon}}+\epsilon\big(\log\theta_{\epsilon}-\log\rho_{\epsilon}-1\big)\nabla\rho_{\epsilon}\bigg],~~V_{\epsilon}=[\theta_{\epsilon},0,0,0]

(4.80)

we observe that

\displaystyle\dv U_{\epsilon}=\Sigma_{\epsilon,\delta},~~\text{and }\curl V_{\epsilon}

are relatively precompact in
W^{-1,s}(\Omega)
for
s\in[1,\frac{3}{2})
.
The boundedness of
U_{\epsilon}
and
V_{\epsilon}
in
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for some
p>1
can be shown as follows.

The sequence
\theta_{\epsilon}
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
and for the sequence
U_{\epsilon}
we use the uniform estimates (
4.64
)-(
4.68
) and the special structure of
s(\rho_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon})
to conclude that it is bounded in
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for some
p>1
.
Moreover,
\epsilon(\log\theta_{\epsilon}-\log\rho_{\epsilon}-1)\to 0
weakly in
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle\overline{s(\rho,\theta)\theta}=\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}\theta.

(4.81)

By the monotonicity of the entropy and the weak convergence we conclude that up to a subsequence

\displaystyle\theta_{\epsilon}\to\theta~~\text{a.a. in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

(4.82)

In addition, we have that the limit temperature is positive a.a. on the set
(0,T)\times\Omega
, more precisely

\displaystyle\theta^{-3}\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

(4.83)

Now, we can let
\epsilon\to 0
in the approximate entropy equation.

Using the previous relations we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa\nabla\theta_{\epsilon}}{\theta_{\epsilon}}\to\frac{\kappa\nabla\theta}{\theta}~~\text{weakly in }L^{p}(0,T;\Omega),

for some
p>1
.

Applying the Div-Curl Lemma once more with
V_{\epsilon}=[u_{\epsilon},0,0,0]
and
U_{\epsilon}
as before, we observe that

\displaystyle s(\rho_{\epsilon},\theta_{\epsilon})u_{\epsilon}\to\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}u~~\text{weakly in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)

for some
p>1
.

The terms appearing in
\sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}
are weakly lower semi-continuous as established in the previous part.
Moreover, the
\epsilon
-dependent terms are non-negative.
Hence we can conclude that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\epsilon,\delta}\to\sigma_{\delta}~~\text{weakly in }\mathcal{M}\big([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\big),

where
\sigma_{\delta}
is a positive measure on
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sigma_{\delta}\geq\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\big(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta^{\Gamma-2}\big)|\nabla\theta|^{2}\big)+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{3}}+\delta\theta^{5}.

It remains to show that
\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}=s(\rho,\theta)
.
To this end, we have to show the strong convergence in the densities.
We follow the ideas presented in
[
FN09
, Chapter 3.6]
.

The first step is to introduce the test function
\varphi(x)
for the momentum equation, where

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\xi(x)\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}]~~\text{and }\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Taking
\varphi
as an admissible test function in the Brinkman-type equation (
4.59
) yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\epsilon\theta_{\epsilon}\dv\varphi dx=\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla u_{\epsilon}:\nabla\varphi+\nu\rho_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon}\cdot\varphi dx

Taking into account the specific form of the test function and integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\xi\bigg(\rho_{\epsilon}\theta_{\epsilon}\rho_{\epsilon}-\mu\nabla u_{\epsilon}:R[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}]-\nu\rho_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}]\bigg)dx=\sum_{i=1}^{2}I_{i,\epsilon},

(4.84)

where

\displaystyle I_{1,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}\theta_{\epsilon}\nabla\xi\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}]dx

\displaystyle I_{2,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\epsilon}:\nabla\xi\otimes\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}]dx

and the symbol
R
denotes the double Riesz transform.

Repeating the same argument for the limit of the Brinkman equation (
4.73
) with the test function
\varphi
being

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\xi(x)\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]~~\text{and }\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

yields the following

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\xi\bigg(\rho\theta\rho-\mu\nabla u:R[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]-\nu\rho u\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]\bigg)dx=\sum_{i=1}^{2}I_{i},

(4.85)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\nabla\xi\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]dx

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\Omega}\nabla u:\nabla\xi\otimes\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]dx.

From the previous estimates we recall that

\displaystyle\rho_{\epsilon}\to\rho~~\text{ in }C_{weak}([0,T],L^{2}(\Omega)).

Taking into account relations (4.64) - (4.77) we observe that the integral
I_{i,\epsilon}
converges to its counterpart
I_{i}
for
i=1,2
and we infer

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\xi\bigg(\rho_{\epsilon}\theta_{\epsilon}\rho_{\epsilon}-\mu\nabla u_{\epsilon}:R[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}]-\nu\rho_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}]\bigg)dx

(4.86)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\xi\bigg(\rho\theta\rho-\mu\nabla u:R[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]-\nu\rho u\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]\bigg)dx

(4.87)

Moreover, the last terms on the left-hand side of the equality converges to the last term on the right-hand side.
The next step is to rewrite the following term

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\xi\mu\nabla u:R[\mathbb{1}_{\Omega}\rho]dx=\int_{\Omega}\mu R:[\xi\nabla u]\rho dx

where we used the properties of the double Riesz transform
[
FN09
, Chapter 11]
and we observe that we can write

\displaystyle\mu R:[\xi\nabla u]=\mu\xi\dv u+\mu\omega(u),

where
\omega(u)=R:[\xi\nabla u]-\xi R:[\nabla u]
is the commutator.
Applying a result by Coifman and Meyer
[
CM75
]
and the previous bounds we obtain that

\displaystyle\omega(u_{\epsilon})\rho_{\epsilon}\to\overline{\omega(u)}\rho~~\text{weakly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

(4.88)

This yields

\displaystyle\overline{\omega(u)}=\omega(u).

The proof of the convergence in (4.88) is shown by applying the Div-Curl Lemma to

U_{\epsilon}=[\rho_{\epsilon},\rho_{\epsilon}u_{\epsilon}]~~\text{and }V_{\epsilon}=[\omega(u_{\epsilon}),0,0,0]

Hence we obtain the following weak compactness identity for the effective pressure

\displaystyle\overline{\rho\theta\rho}-\mu\overline{\rho\dv u}=\overline{\rho\theta}\rho-\mu\rho\dv u.

(4.89)

The final step is to multiply the continuity equation on
G^{\prime}(\rho_{\epsilon})
, with
G
being a smooth and convex function.
Then as
\epsilon\to 0
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\overline{G(\rho)}(t)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\big(G^{\prime}(\rho)\rho-G(\rho)\big)\dv u}dxdt\leq\int_{\Omega}G(\rho_{0})dx

for all
t\in(0,T)
and we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\overline{\rho\log\rho}(t)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\rho\dv u}dxdt=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\log\rho_{0}dx.

Via the theory of renormalized solutions by DiPerna and Lions
[
DL89
]
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\log\rho(t)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho\dv udxdt\leq\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\log\rho_{0}dx.

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\rho\theta\rho}\geq\overline{\rho\theta}\rho

(4.90)

and as a consequence of of equation (4.89)

\displaystyle\overline{\rho\dv u}\geq\rho\dv u.

(4.91)

Combining both estimates implies

\displaystyle\overline{\rho\log\rho}=\rho\log\rho

(4.92)

which yields the desired strong convergence of the density as the function
x\log x
is convex, i.e.

\displaystyle\rho_{\epsilon}\to\rho~~\text{ a.a. in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

(4.93)

This allows us to identify
\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}=s(\rho,\theta)
.

Having eliminated the
\epsilon
-dependent terms, we summarize the results.
For any
\delta>0
we have constructed a trio
(\rho,u,\theta)
solving the following equations.

The
continuity equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)=0~~\text{in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

(4.94)

with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle\nabla\rho\cdot n=0~~\text{on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,

(4.95)

and the initial condition

\displaystyle\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0,\delta}.

(4.96)

The
Brinkman-type equation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\dv\phi dx=\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla u:\nabla\phi dx+\int_{\Omega}\nu\rho u\cdot\phi dx,

(4.97)

for any test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
with

\displaystyle\phi=0~~\text{on }\partial\Omega~~\text{ no-slip boundary conditions.}

(4.98)

The
approximate internal energy balance

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta(t)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}\theta_{0,\delta}dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}-\delta\theta^{5}\,dxd\tau.

(4.99)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

The
approximate entropy inequality

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)\big(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla\theta}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\phi dxdt\\
&+\langle\sigma_{\delta},\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{M}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}]}=-\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{\delta,0},\theta_{\delta,0})\phi(0,\cdot)dx\end{split}

(4.100)

for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\delta}\geq\frac{1}{\theta}\bigg(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\big(\frac{1}{\theta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\theta^{\Gamma-1}\big)|\nabla\theta|^{2}+\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}+\delta\theta^{5}\bigg).

(4.101)

4.4.3
Limit
\delta\to 0

The last step in this proof is to let
\delta\to 0
.
To this end, let
(\rho_{\delta},u_{\delta},\theta_{\delta})
be a solution to the approximate system (
4.94
)-(
4.101
).
We recall that the total mass of the system is conserved, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\delta}(t,\cdot)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}dx~~\text{for any }t\in[0,T].

(4.102)

We assume that

\displaystyle\rho_{0,\delta}\to\rho_{0}~~\text{in }L^{1}(\Omega),

(4.103)

and thus the bound is uniform for
\delta\to 0
.

Next, we apply the reverse Young’s inequality to the energy balance equation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{S(\rho/\theta)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}(\rho^{2}+\theta^{2})(t)dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta(t)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0,\delta}\theta_{0,\delta}dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}-\delta\theta^{5}dxd\tau,

(4.104)

where
S(h)=S(1/h)>0
for
h>0
is the Specht radius
[
Tom02
]
.
Since
S(h)\to\infty
as
h\to 0
we need the ratio
\rho/\theta
to be bounded from below and above.
This follows from the fact that we chose positive initial data
\rho_{0}
and
\theta_{0}
and from the previous sections.
As a consequence the solution may exist only for a short time
T^{*}>0
.

Next, is the dissipation balance

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H_{\overline{\theta}}(\rho,\theta)(t)dx+\overline{\theta}\sigma_{\delta}\big[[0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}\big]=\int_{\Omega}H_{\overline{\theta}}(\rho_{0,\delta},\theta_{0,\delta})dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta}{\theta^{2}}+\delta\theta^{4}dxd\tau

(4.105)

satisfied for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
.
Noting that the terms
\delta/\theta^{2}
and
\delta\theta^{4}
are absorbed in the entropy production
\sigma_{\delta}
and the uniform bounds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H_{\overline{\theta}}(\rho_{0,\delta},\theta_{0,\delta})dx\leq c~~\text{uniformly for }\delta\to 0

(4.106)

hold, we obtain the following uniform estimates depending only on the initial data:

\displaystyle\essup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\rho_{\delta}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c,

(4.107)

\displaystyle\essup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\theta_{\delta}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq c,

(4.108)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\delta}\big[[0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}\big]

\displaystyle\leq c.

(4.109)

have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\log\theta_{\delta}|^{2}dxdt\leq c,

(4.110)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}+\rho|u|^{2}}{\theta_{\delta}}dxdt\leq c,

(4.111)

\displaystyle\delta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta_{\delta}^{3}}+\theta^{5}dxdt\leq c,

(4.112)

\displaystyle\delta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\theta^{\Gamma-2}|\nabla\theta_{\delta}|^{2}dxdt\leq c.

(4.113)

Using that

\displaystyle\nabla\log\theta_{\delta}~~\text{bounded in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)

we obtain that

\displaystyle\nabla\theta_{\delta}~~\text{bounded in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega).

(4.114)

Moreover applying Lemma 3.1 and Proposition 3.2 we have that

\displaystyle\log\theta_{\delta}~~\text{bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

(4.115)

Combining the above estimates, we see that

\displaystyle\theta_{\delta}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))

(4.116)

and especially by the Sobolev embedding

\displaystyle\theta_{\delta}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega)).

(4.117)

Next, we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\delta}|^{3/2}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla u_{\delta}|^{3/2}}{\theta_{\delta}^{3/4}}\theta_{\delta}^{3/4}\leq c\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla u_{\delta}|^{2}}{\theta}+c\int_{\Omega}\theta^{4}_{\delta}\leq c.

(4.118)

Using the assumption that
\rho_{0}\in L^{3}(\Omega)
and testing the continuity equation with
\rho^{2}
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\rho\rho^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\dv(\rho u)\rho^{2}dx=0.

Integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{3}dx\leq\frac{2}{3}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{3}|\dv u|\,dx.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|\rho_{\delta}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{3}\leq c\big(\|\rho_{\delta}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{3}\big)^{3}+c\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{L^{3/2}(\Omega)}^{3/2},

(4.119)

where we used an inverse type of the Jensen inequality
[
Tak+99
]
.
Note, this inequality requires lower and upper bounds on the density
\rho_{\delta}
almost everywhere.
Thus, we have an ordinary differential equation of the type

\displaystyle x^{\prime}\leq c_{1}x^{3}+C_{2}.

This is an ODE with Lipschitz right-hand side and by Picarc-Lindeloeff Theorem the solution exists for a small time
T
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega).

(4.120)

Now, we can conclude from the Brinkman equation that

\displaystyle\|\rho u^{2}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\delta}^{2}\theta^{2}_{\delta}\,dx\leq c\|\rho_{\delta}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{3}+\|\theta_{\delta}\|^{6}_{L^{6}(\Omega)},

where we used that
\rho
is non-negative at least for a small time
T^{*}
.
And thus by the Poincare inequality

\displaystyle u_{\delta}~~\text{is bounded in }H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(4.121)

We remark that by repeating the previous two steps we obtain that

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}~~\text{is bounded in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega)

(4.122)

for some
p>2
at least for a small time
T>0
.

Now, we have all the necessary uniform estimates together in order to pass to the limit in the equations.

For the continuity equation we get

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}\to\rho~~\text{weakly in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)

(4.123)

for some
p\geq 2
and similarly

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}u_{\delta}\to\rho u~~\text{weakly in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)

(4.124)

for some
p>1
.

After passing to the limit in the continuity equation as
\delta\to 0
the limit satisfies the integral identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(\rho\partial_{t}\phi+\rho u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt+\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}dx=0

(4.125)

for any test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
i.e.
\rho,\,u
satisfy in the following equation in the sense of distributions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\dv(\rho u)=0.

(4.126)

For the Stokes equation we have

\displaystyle u_{\delta}\to u~~\text{weakly in }H^{1}(\Omega)

(4.127)

and

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}\theta_{\delta}\to\overline{\rho\theta}~~\text{weakly in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)

(4.128)

for some
p>1
.
Thus the limit satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nu\rho u\cdot\varphi+\int_{\Omega}\mu\nabla u:\nabla\varphi dx+\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\dv\varphi dx=0,

(4.129)

for any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
with
\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
or in the sense of distributions

\displaystyle-\nu\rho u+\mu\Delta u=\nabla(\rho\theta).

(4.130)

Before passing to the limit in the approximate entropy equation we need to show the pointwise convergence of the temperature.
Again as in the previous sections the idea is to apply the Div-Curl Lemma.
Setting

\displaystyle U_{\delta}=\bigg[s(\rho_{\delta},\theta_{\delta}),s(\rho_{\delta},\theta_{\delta})u_{\delta}+\frac{\nabla\theta_{\delta}}{\theta_{\delta}}],~~V_{\delta}=[\theta_{\delta},0,0,0]

(4.131)

we observe that

\displaystyle\dv U_{\delta}=\sigma_{\delta},~~\text{and }\curl V_{\delta}

are relatively precompact in
W^{-1,s}(\Omega)
for
s\in[1,\frac{3}{2})
.

Indeed,using equations (
4.114
)-(
4.117
) we see that

\displaystyle\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1}_{\delta}\nabla\theta_{\delta}=\delta\frac{\Gamma}{2}\theta_{\delta}^{1/4}\theta_{\delta}^{\Gamma/2-1/4}\nabla\theta_{\delta}^{\Gamma/2}.

Hence, we can conclude

\displaystyle\delta\theta^{\Gamma-1}_{\delta}\nabla\theta_{\delta}\to 0~~\text{in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)

as
\delta\to 0
for a certain
p>1
.
In addition, since
\theta_{\delta}>0
for a.a.
(t,x)
we obtain

\displaystyle\delta\frac{1}{\theta_{\delta}^{3}}\to 0~~\text{in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)

as
\delta\to 0
.

The boundedness of
U_{\delta}
in
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for some
p>1
can be shown as follows.

For the sequence
U_{\delta}
we use the uniform estimates (
4.107
)-(
4.109
) and the special structure of
s(\rho_{\delta},\theta_{\delta})
to conclude that it is bounded in
L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for some
p>1
.
Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle\overline{s(\rho,\theta)\theta}=\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}\theta.

(4.132)

By the monotonicity of the entropy and the weak convergence we conclude that up to a subsequence

\displaystyle\theta_{\delta}\to\theta~~\text{a.a. in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

(4.133)

In addition, we have that the limit temperature is positive a.a. on the set
(0,T)\times\Omega
.

It remains to show that
\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}=s(\rho,\theta)
.
We proceed as in the previous section by showing the strong convergence of the density.
From the bounds obtained in equations (
4.107
)-(
4.117
) we see that the methods from the
\epsilon
-limit can be applied in this setting too, cf. equations (
4.84
)-(
4.93
).

Using the weak lower semi-continuity of convex functionals, we can let
\delta\to 0
in the approximate entropy balance to conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\overline{s(\rho,\theta)}\big(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi\big)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\kappa\nabla\theta}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\phi dxdt\\
&+\langle\sigma;\phi\rangle_{[\mathcal{M};C]([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})}=-\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{0},\theta_{0})dx\end{split}

(4.134)

for any
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})
.
Here
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})
is a weak-*-limit in the space of measures
\mathcal{M}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})
of the sequence
\sigma_{\delta}
.
Using the lower weak semi-continuity of convex functionals and the fact that all
\delta
-dependent terms in the entropy production rate are non-negative, we obtain that

\displaystyle\sigma\geq\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\frac{|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\big).

(4.135)

The last step is to take the limit in the internal energy balance.
By equation (
4.128
) we can pass to the limit and obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta(t)dx=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\theta_{0}dx~~\text{for a.a. }t\in[0,T].

(4.136)

This completes the proof of the theorem.

4.5
Higher regularity

In the proof of Theorem
4.1
we have noted that the weak solutions constructed by the approximate scheme satisfy better regularity and integrability properties.

Theorem 4.11

(Regularity of weak solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n=2,\,3
, be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Assume that the initial data
\rho_{0}
,
E_{0}
and
s_{0}
satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem 4.1.

Then, in addition to the minimal regularity assumptions required in equations (3.2), (3.6), (3.10), (3.14)-(3.16), there holds:

i)

The weak solution satisfies

\displaystyle\rho\in C_{weak}([0,T];L^{3}(\Omega))\cap C([0,T];L^{1}(\Omega)),

(4.137)

\displaystyle u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(4.138)

\displaystyle\theta\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T:l^{2}(\Omega)),

(4.139)

\displaystyle\log\theta\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

(4.140)

ii)

The entropy satisfies

\displaystyle ess\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)(t,\cdot)\phi\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{0},\theta_{0})\phi\,dx~~\text{for any }\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),~\phi\geq 0.

(4.141)

If in addition,
\theta_{0}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
then

\displaystyle ess\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Omega}s(\rho,\theta)(t,\cdot)\phi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}s(\rho_{0},\theta_{0})\phi\,dx~~\text{for all }\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}).

(4.142)

Proof.

The integrability properties follow directly from the proof of the existence of weak solutions.

The strong continuity of the density is a general property of the transport equation in the context of renormalized solutions.

The last part of the proof follows step 3 in the proof of Theorem 3.2 in
[
FN09
]
.

5
Conclusion and Remarks

In this section we conclude with several remarks.

The first one is that in the first part of this paper we showed how to apply the energetic variational approach
[
LLZ05
]
and
[
HL+10
]
in the setting of fluid mechanics and combine it with the temperature in a natural way.
This leads to the general framework of the free energy as starting point for the thermodynamics of fluids.
With the choice of free energy (in terms of temperature and the
state variable, the phase field variable here), and the entropy production,
as well as the kinematics/transport of these variables, one should be
able to uniquely determine the system.

Second, we observe the importance of the Laplacian in the velocity term.
To this end , we recall the following estimates obtained from the continuity and momentum equation and the entropy production rate.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nu\rho|u|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla u|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\rho\theta\dv u

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma+1}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\gamma+1}+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\gamma+1}\dv u

\displaystyle=0

(5.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\theta}\big(\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+\nu\rho|u|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq c

(5.3)

We note that equation (
5.3
) gives a uniform bound on
\frac{\mu|\nabla u|^{2}}{\theta}
and this bound is then used in equation (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) to obtain further estimates.
If we were to let the parameter
\mu
go to
0
we would loose these bounds, i.e. the control of the gradient of
u
and thus we cannot achieve the ultimate goal to consider the ideal gas under a Darcy-type law.

Next, we note that adding a memory/ evolutionary term to the Brinkman-type equation, i.e.
(\rho u)_{t}
does not change analysis of the model.
In addition, as noted earlier in Remark
4.2
, if we had a kinetic term in the total energy we would obtain the incompressible Navier-Stokes-Fourier system for which the existence of weak solutions for the ideal gas case is still open
[
FN09
]
and
[
FNS11
]
.
Thus our result gives in a sense a ”lower limit” existence result of the full compressible Navier-Stokes-Fourier system, where the difference and crucial aspect is the additional nonlinear term in the momentum equation.

Finally, we want to remark that for a similar system of equations with only Darcy-type dissipation we are able to show the well-posedness of the system in a critical Besov space
[
LS21
]
. The difference in these two approaches is that the first one uses energy methods for finding the weak solution, whereas the second one uses scaling arguments and the algebra structure provided by the critical Besov space.
