Space Curves and Solitons of the KP Hierarchy. I. The 𝒍 \boldsymbol{l} -th Generalized KdV Hierarchy
Yuji KODAMA and Yuancheng XIE

Abstract
It is well known that algebro-geometric solutions of the KdV hierarchy are constructed from the Riemann theta functions associated with hyperelliptic curves, and that soliton solutions can be obtained by rational (singular) limits of the corresponding curves. In this paper, we discuss a class of KP solitons in connections with space curves, which are labeled by certain types of numerical semigroups. In particular, we show that some class of the (singular and complex) KP solitons of the l l -th generalized KdV hierarchy with l ≥ 2 l\geq 2 is related to the rational space curves associated with the numerical semigroup ⟨ l , l ​ m + 1 , … , l ​ m + k ⟩ \langle l,lm+1,\dots,lm+k\rangle , where m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 and 1 ≤ k ≤ l − 1 1\leq k\leq l-1 . We also calculate the Schur polynomial expansions of the τ \tau -functions for those KP solitons. Moreover, we construct smooth curves by deforming the singular curves associated with the soliton solutions. For these KP solitons, we also construct the space curve from a commutative ring of differential operators in the sense of the well-known Burchnall–Chaundy theory.

Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to identify algebraic curves associated with particular class of soliton solutions of the KP hierarchy. It is known that solutions of the KP equation can be constructed from
any
algebraic curves
[
15
]
. A solution from a smooth curve is a
quasi-periodic
solution, and some soliton solutions can be constructed by a rational (singular) limit of the curve with only ordinary double points (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
20
,
29
]
). In particular, the cases corresponding to the KdV and nonlinear Schödinger equations are well-studied, in which the algebraic curves are given by the hyperelliptic curves (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
20
]
). Recently, there are several papers dealing with some
non-hyperelliptic cases, e.g., so-called
(n,s)
-curves, where the authors construct the Klein
\sigma
-functions over these curves (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
17
,
21
]
).
It seems, however, that almost no result has been reported for the cases with more general algebraic curves. Because of the difficulty in finding a canonical homological basis for the general algebraic curves, it may be quite complicated to compute
explicitly a rational limit of these curves and the corresponding Riemann theta functions (see
[
21
]
). On the other hand, a large number of real regular soliton solutions of the KP hierarchy has been classified in terms of finite Grassmann manifolds
{\rm Gr}(N,M)
(see, e.g.,
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
). Recently, there are some progress on the study concerned with the connections between the algebraic-geometric solutions and these soliton solutions
[
1
,
2
,
22
,
23
]
.

In this paper, we take the first step to identify explicit forms of algebraic curves associated with certain class of KP solitons. We consider complex (singular in general) soliton solutions
in terms of the complex Grassmannians. Here what we mean by “soliton” is that the underlying

M
-dimensional space
\mathbb{C}^{M}
of the Grassmannian for the solution expressed by the so-called

\tau
-function
is spanned by only “exponential” functions. We also introduce the “generalized” soliton solutions which are mixed with rational functions in addition to the exponential functions (see
[
22
]
), which are obtained by multiple degenerations of the soliton parameters.
In particular, choosing a special point of the Schubert cell of the Grassmannian, the
\tau
-function corresponding to a generalized soliton has a Schur polynomial expansion whose leading polynomial is determined by the Young diagram corresponding to the Schubert cell.
The main tool in this paper is the Sato universal Grassmannian which is the moduli space of the formal power series solutions of the KP hierarchy
[
22
,
23
,
26
,
27
]
.

We briefly give a background of the Sato theory of the KP hierarchy and the algebro-geometric solutions associated with algebraic curves. Sato’s theory of KP hierarchy associates a point of the
universal Grassmannian manifold (UGM) with a solution of the KP hierarchy. Each solution of
the KP hierarchy is given by the so-called
\tau
-function, which is a function of infinitely many variables
(t_{n}\colon n=1,2,\ldots)
. It is known that an algebro-geometric solution of the KP hierarchy can be expressed by the Riemann theta function
[
3
,
15
,
18
]
. A method of algebro-geometric solutions was proposed in
[
15
]
, and
these solutions are constructed by the following way.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
and
\mathcal{L}
a holomorphic line bundle of degree
g
over it. Let
p_{\infty}
be a nonsingular point of
\mathcal{C}
,
z
a local parameter around
p_{\infty}
. Let
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{L}(*\infty))
be meromorphic sections of
\mathcal{L}
with poles only at
p_{\infty}
. Identifying
\mathcal{L}
with a degree
g
divisor
D
on
\mathcal{C}
, this can be represented as a space of meromorphic functions
U
on a small neighborhood of
p_{\infty}
.
Such
U
represents a point of UGM, and denoted by
U={\rm Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\mathcal{B}\}
with the Sato frame
\mathcal{B}
representing a basis of the space of meromorphic functions
with poles at the specified divisors
D+*\infty
on
\mathcal{C}
. Then the Sato frame representing a point of UGM determines the coefficients of the Schur polynomial expansion of the
\tau
-function.
In the present paper, we only consider the set of meromorphic functions with a pole only at
p_{\infty}
, i.e.,
D=gp_{\infty}
. (The general case will be discussed in a future communication
[
31
]
)
Let
W=H^{0}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}(*\infty))
be the set of meromorphic functions on
\mathcal{C}
with a pole only at
p_{\infty}
. We note that
W
is a commutative algebra graded by the order of poles at
p_{\infty}
and
U
is a module over
W
, where we have

z^{g}W=U\in\text{UGM}

[
29
]
. We also note that the order of poles of elements in
W
defines a numerical semigroup
S
.

It turns out that the algebraic curve associated with the generalized soliton solution discussed in this paper is a “singular space curve”. The strategy to find the curve is as follows.

1.

Given a
\tau
-function associated with a generalized soliton solution, we identify the corresponding Sato frame
\mathcal{B}
by embedding the solution into UGM as a point
U={\rm Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\mathcal{B}\}
.

2.

We define a basis
\mathcal{A}
for a maximal graded commutative subalgebra
{\mathcal{R}}

in
\mathbb{C}\big[z^{-1}\big]
, over which
U
is an
{\mathcal{R}}
-module. The basis
\mathcal{A}
is filtered by the order of poles at
p_{\infty}
,
and in the cases under consideration it has the form
\mathcal{A}=z^{-g}f(z)\mathcal{B}
with some polynomial
f(z)
with
f(0)=1
.

3.

We identify a (singular) space curve
\mathcal{C}
whose affine part is given by

\text{Spec}({\mathcal{R}})
. In the case of a smooth curve
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, there is an identification,

{\rm Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\mathcal{A}\}=H^{0}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{O}(*\infty)\big)
and
z^{g}H^{0}\big(\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\mathcal{O}(*\infty)\big)\in\text{UGM}
.
The numerical semigroup
S
represents the order of poles of the basis
\mathcal{A}
, and the genus of
S
is the arithmetic genus of the curve
\mathcal{C}
.

In Section

In Section

In Section

In Section

In Section

2
The generalized soliton solutions

and the Schur expansion of the
\boldsymbol{\tau}
-function

The KP equation is a nonlinear partial differential equation in the form,

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\big({-}4\partial_{3}u+6u\partial_{1}u+\partial_{1}^{3}u\big)+3\partial_{2}^{2}u=0,

(2.1)

with the partial derivatives
\partial_{n}^{k}u=\frac{\partial^{k}u}{\partial t_{n}^{k}}
.
The KP equation admits an infinite number of symmetries (commuting flows), and
these flows are parametrized by the “time” variables
t_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The set of all commuting flows is called the KP hierarchy, whose first member is the KP equation.

The KP soliton solutions are classified in terms of finite dimensional Grassmannians.
Here we briefly summarize these previous results for the background of the present paper
(also see Appendix
A
).

2.1
The
\boldsymbol{\tau}
-functions for the generalized soliton solutions

The
\tau
-function of the KP hierarchy with the time variables
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)
is introduced by

\displaystyle u(t)=2\partial_{1}^{2}\ln\tau(t).

(2.2)

The soliton solutions are constructed as follows.
Let
\{f_{i}(t)\colon 1\leq i\leq N\}
be a set of linearly independent functions
f_{i}(t)
of
infinite variables
t=\{t_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
satisfying the following system of linear equations,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}f_{i}=\partial_{1}^{n}f_{i}\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq N\quad\text{and}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.3)

The
\tau
-function is then given by the Wronskian with respect to
t_{1}
-variable,

\displaystyle\tau(t)=\text{Wr}(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N}).

(2.4)

(See (
A.8
) in Appendix
A
, and also
[
9
]
for the details.)

As a fundamental set of the solutions of (
2.3
), we take the exponential functions,

\displaystyle E_{j}(t)={\rm e}^{\theta_{j}(t)}\qquad\text{with}\quad\theta_{j}(t):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{j}^{n}t_{n}\qquad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,M,

where
\kappa_{j}
’s are arbitrary constants.
For the soliton solutions, we consider
f_{i}(t)
as a linear combination of the exponential solutions,

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i,j}E_{j}(t)\qquad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(2.5)

where
A:=(a_{i,j})
is an
N\times M
constant matrix of full rank,
{\rm rank}(A)=N
,
Then the
\tau
-function (
2.4
) is expressed by

\displaystyle\tau(t)=\big|E(t)A^{\rm T}\big|,

(2.6)

where
A^{\rm T}
is the transpose of the matrix
A
, and
E(t)
is given by

\displaystyle E(t)=\begin{pmatrix}E_{1}&E_{2}&\cdots&E_{M}\\
\partial_{1}E_{1}&\partial_{1}E_{2}&\cdots&\partial_{1}E_{M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial_{1}^{N-1}E_{1}&\partial_{1}^{N-1}E_{2}&\cdots&\partial_{1}^{N-1}E_{M}\end{pmatrix}\!.

(2.7)

Note here that the set of exponential functions
\{E_{1}(t),\ldots,E_{M}(t)\}
gives a basis of the
M
-dimensional space
of the null space of the operator
\prod_{i=1}^{M}(\partial_{1}-\kappa_{i})
, and we call it a “basis”
of the KP soliton. Then the set of functions
\{f_{1}(t),\ldots,f_{N}(t)\}
represents an
N
-dimensional subspace
of
M
-dimensional space spanned by the exponential functions. This leads naturally to the structure
of a finite Grassmannian
{\rm Gr}(N,M)
, the set of
N
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{M}
.
Then the
N\times M
matrix
A
of full rank can be identified as a point of
{\rm Gr}(N,M)
.
The
{\rm Gr}(N,M)
has a decomposition called the Schubert decomposition given by

\displaystyle{\rm Gr}(N,M)=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\subset(M-N)^{N}}X_{\lambda},

(2.8)

where
\lambda
is a Young diagram contained in an
N\times(M-N)
rectangular diagram
denoted by
(M-N)^{N}
, and
X_{\lambda}
is the Schubert cell which consists of the
N\times M

matrices whose pivots are given by
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{N}\leq M
with
i_{k}=M-N+k-\lambda_{k}
.
In terms of the pivot indices, the Young diagram
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})

is expressed as

That is, the pivot indices of the matrices in
X_{\lambda}
appear at the vertical paths in a lattice path starting from the top right corner and ending at the bottom left corner in the Young diagram
\lambda
as shown in the figure above.

We also have the following formula of the
\tau
-function using the Binet–Cauchy lemma (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
),

\displaystyle\tau(t)=\sum_{I\in\binom{[M]}{N}}\Delta_{I}(A)E_{I}(t),

where
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{N}\}
is an
N
element subset in
[M]=\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
,
\Delta_{I}(A)
is the
N\times N
minor with the column vectors indexed by
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}\}
,
and
E_{I}(t)
is the
N\times N
determinant of the same set of the columns in (
2.7
), which is given by

\displaystyle E_{I}(t)=\prod_{k<l}(\kappa_{i_{l}}-\kappa_{i_{k}})\,E_{i_{1}}(t)\cdots E_{i_{N}}(t).

The minor
\Delta_{I}(A)
is also called the Plücker coordinate, and the
\tau
-function represents a
point of
{\rm Gr}(N,M)
in the sense of the Plücker embedding,
{\rm Gr}(N,M)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}\big({\wedge}^{N}\mathbb{C}^{M}\big)\colon A\mapsto\big\{\Delta_{I}(A)\colon

I\in\binom{[M]}{N}\big\}

(see, e.g.,
[
9
]
).

2.2
The Schur function expansion of the
\boldsymbol{\tau}
-function

Expanding the exponential function
E_{j}(t)
with respect to the powers of
\kappa_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{j}(t)={\rm e}^{\theta_{j}(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{j}^{n}p_{n}(t),

where
p_{n}(t)
is the elementary Schur polynomials. That is,
E_{j}(t)
is the generating function of
the elementary Schur polynomials, which are given by

\displaystyle p_{n}(t)=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+\cdots+ni_{n}=n}\frac{t_{1}^{i_{1}}t_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots t_{n}^{i_{n}}}{i_{1}!i_{2}!\cdots i_{n}!}.

Note that the elementary Schur polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{k}p_{n}(t)=\partial_{1}^{k}p_{n}(t)=p_{n-k}(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{m}(t)=0\qquad\text{if}\quad m<0.

Using the elementary Schur polynomials, the
\tau
-function can be expressed as follows.
First note that

\displaystyle(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N})=(E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{M})A^{\rm T},

where
A^{\rm T}
is the transpose of the
N\times M
matrix
A
. Expanding the exponential functions, we have

\displaystyle(E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{M})=(1,p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)K,

where
K
is the
\infty\times M
Vandermonde matrix,
K=\big(\kappa_{j}^{i-1}\big)
for

1\leq i
and
1\leq j\leq M
, i.e.,

\displaystyle K=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
\kappa_{1}&\kappa_{2}&\cdots&\kappa_{M}\\
\kappa_{1}^{2}&\kappa_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\kappa_{M}^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix}\!.

(2.9)

Note that each function
f_{i}(t)
in (
2.5
) has the following expansion,

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i,j}\kappa_{j}^{n}p_{n}(t)\qquad\text{for}\quad i=1,2,\ldots,N.

The
\tau
-function (
2.6
) is then expressed by

\displaystyle\tau(t)=|EA^{\rm T}|=\left|\begin{pmatrix}1&p_{1}&p_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
0&1&p_{1}&p_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&1&p_{1}&p_{2}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}\,K\,A^{\rm T}\right|.

(2.10)

In this paper, we include the
generalized
bases which
contain derivatives of the exponential functions with respect to the parameters
\kappa_{j}

[
22
]
. For example, we consider the following derivative,

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{j}^{(q)}(t):=\frac{1}{q!}\frac{\partial^{q}}{\partial\kappa_{j}^{q}}\big(\kappa_{j}^{q}E_{j}(t)\big)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{q+n}{n}\kappa_{j}^{n}p_{n}(t).

(2.11)

Note that
\hat{E}_{j}^{(0)}=E_{j}
.
Then one can take the following basis,

\displaystyle\big(\hat{E}_{1}^{(q_{1})}(t),\hat{E}_{2}^{(q_{2})}(t),\ldots,\hat{E}_{M}^{(q_{M})}(t)\big),

(2.12)

where
q_{j}
’s are nonnegative integers. The
\tau
-function in this case can be expressed in
the form (
2.10
) by replacing the Vandermonde matrix
K
in (
2.9
) with

\displaystyle\hat{K}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\[2.15277pt]
\binom{q_{1}+1}{1}\kappa_{1}&\binom{q_{2}+1}{1}\kappa_{2}&\cdots&\binom{q_{M}+1}{1}\kappa_{M}\\[6.45831pt]
\binom{q_{1}+2}{2}\kappa_{1}^{2}&\binom{q_{2}+2}{2}\kappa_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\binom{q_{M}+2}{2}\kappa_{M}^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\end{pmatrix}

(2.13)

which we call the
generalized
Vandermonde matrix. Then the functions
\hat{f}_{i}(t)
for the
\tau
-function, denoted by
\hat{\tau}=\text{Wr}(\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{N})
, have the expansion,

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i,j}\hat{E}_{j}^{(q_{j})}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i,j}\binom{q_{j}+n}{n}\kappa_{j}^{n}p_{n}(t).

(2.14)

Remark 2.1
.

The soliton solutions generated by the
\tau
-function with the basis (
2.11
) contain
rational functions of the variables
t=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)
, hence they are singular in general.

Now we consider the expansion of the
\tau
-function, which we call the Schur expansion of the
\tau
-function:
Using the Binet–Cauchy lemma, one can expand the
\tau
-function (
2.4
) in terms of
the Schur polynomials,

\displaystyle\tau(t)=S_{\lambda}(t)+\sum_{\hat{\mu}\supset\lambda}c_{\hat{\mu}}S_{\mu}(t),

(2.15)

where
S_{\lambda}(t)
is the Schur polynomial associated with the Young diagram
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
defined by

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}(t)=|(p_{\lambda_{i}+j-i}(t))|.

The constant
c_{\hat{\mu}}
in (
2.15
) are the
N\times N
minors of the
\infty\times N
matrix
KA^{\rm T}
(or
\hat{K}A^{\rm T}
).
In particular, the first constant
c_{\lambda}
, which is normalized to be one, is the minimal minor
of
KA^{\rm T}
(or
\hat{K}A^{\rm T}
) in the lexicographical order of the row indices
\{l_{0},l_{1},\ldots,l_{N-1}\}
for
0\leq l_{0}<l_{1}<\cdots<l_{N-1}

with the relation,

\displaystyle l_{k}=\lambda_{N-k}+k\qquad\text{for}\quad 0\leq k\leq N-1.

With this relation, the Schur polynomial associated with the Young diagram
\lambda
is given by

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}(t)=\text{Wr}(p_{\lambda_{N}},p_{\lambda_{N-1}+1},\ldots,p_{\lambda_{1}+N-1})=\text{Wr}(p_{l_{0}},p_{l_{1}},\ldots,p_{l_{N-1}}).

(2.16)

Notice that in (
2.15
) if
A
is totally nonnegative, then
\lambda=\varnothing
and

\tau(0)=1
(note that a real KP soliton is regular, if and only if
A
is totally nonnegative
[
10
]
). Here, we are interested in the singular solutions, where
\tau(0)=0
. In particular, we look for the expansion (
2.15
) with a particular matrix
A
so that the leading Schur polynomial has the largest possible weight. Recall that the weight of
S_{\lambda}(t)
is defined by the number of boxes in
\lambda
, denoted by
|\lambda|=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}
. Note that the weight of
t_{n}
is
n
.

We then have the following theorem, which identifies the leading Schur polynomial of the
\tau
-function for each Schubert cell associated with the Young diagram
\lambda
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Fixed a Schubert cell
X_{\lambda}\subset{\rm Gr}(N,M)
, there exists a unique element
A\in X_{\lambda}
, so that the leading term of the Schur expansion of the
\tau
-function (
2.10
)
generated by
A
and
K
in (
2.9
)
(
or
\hat{K}
in (
2.13
)
)
is
S_{\lambda}(t)
.
Here, the pivot indices, say
\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{N}\}
, of the matrix
A
are related to
\lambda
with

\displaystyle i_{k}=M-N+k-\lambda_{k}\qquad\text{for}\quad k=1,\ldots,N.

Proof.

Assume

The Sato universal Grassmannians

Here we give a brief description of the Sato universal Grassmannian (see, e.g.,
[

27

for further details).
Let

be the vector space of formal Laurent series,

\displaystyle V:=\mathbb{C}((z))=\bigg\{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}z^{n}\colon c_{n}=0\text{ for }n\ll 0\bigg\},

which has a decomposition,

\displaystyle V=V_{\varnothing}\oplus V_{0},

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lccccc}\pi\colon&V&\longrightarrow&V_{\varnothing},\\[2.15277pt]

\displaystyle U={\rm Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\big\{\phi_{-i}(z)\colon i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\big\},

.

Each function

Proof.

First we have the following formulas (see, e.g.,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}z^{n}\partial_{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}p_{n}(\tilde{\partial})\qquad\text{and}\qquad p_{n}(\tilde{\partial})p_{m}(t)\big|_{t=0}=\delta_{n,m},

where

\displaystyle\bigg(\partial_{1},\frac{1}{2}\partial_{2},\frac{1}{3}\partial_{3},\ldots\bigg).

That is, the operator

replaces

with

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}z^{n}\partial_{n}}p_{m}(t)\big|_{t=0}=p_{m}(t+[z])\big|_{t=0}=p_{m}([z])=z^{m}.

From the formulas of the

),
note that the function

Then place

-th column place in the Sato frame, so that

This implies the formula in the proposition. The constant

We note that the exponential functions

Remark 3.2
.

The subset

also gives a point of UGM, which is different from the point given by

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}z^{n}\partial_{n}}\,\hat{g}_{i}(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\hat{f}_{i}([z])\exp\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\alpha_{n}z^{nl}\bigg).

\displaystyle\tau=\text{Wr}(\hat{g}_{1},\ldots,\hat{g}_{N})={\rm e}^{N\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}t_{nl}}\text{Wr}(\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{N}),

Let us start with a brief introduction to the numerical semigroups (see, e.g.,

4.1
The numerical semigroups

A numerical semigroup
S
is a subset of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, such that it

(1)

contains
0
, i.e.,
0\in S
,

(2)

is closed under addition, i.e., for any
a,b\in S
,
a+b\in S
, and

(3)

has a finite complement in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, i.e.,
|\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus S|<\infty
.

The finite set
\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus S
is called
gap sequence
or
gaps
of
S
.

It is known that every numerical semigroup is generated by a finite set
\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle S=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle:=\bigg\{\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}\colon a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\bigg\}.

It is also well known that
S
is a numerical semigroup if and only if
\gcd\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}=1
.
Every numerical semigroup has a unique finite set which generates
S
. We also have the following notions for the numerical semigroup.

(a)

The (arithmetic) genus of
S
is
g(S)=|\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus S|
.

(b)

The Frobenius number
F(S)
is the finite greatest positive integer in
\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus S
.

A numerical semigroup can be expressed by a Young diagram, denoted by
\lambda(S)
as follows. The southeast boundary
of the Young diagram gives a path labeled by the numbers from
0\in S
starting at the northeast corner, so that the elements in
S
appear on the vertical edges, hence the gaps on the horizontal edges, in the boundary path as shown below.

Here
0,s_{i},s_{j}\in S
and
g_{m},F(S)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus S
. Note that the Young diagram

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
is expressed by
\lambda_{k}=F(S)-N+k-s_{k-1}
with

.

4.2
The numerical semigroups of type
\boldsymbol{\langle l,lm+1,\ldots,lm+k\rangle}

In this paper, we consider the numerical semigroups of type
\langle l,lm+1,lm+2,\ldots,lm+k\rangle
,
where
l\geq 2,m\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq l-1

Proposition 4.1
.

The Frobenius number
F(S)
and the genus
g(S)
of the numerical semigroup
S=\langle l,lm+1,lm+2,\ldots,lm+k\rangle
are given by

\displaystyle F(S)=mln_{l,k}-1,\qquad g(S)=mn_{l,k}\bigg(l-1-\frac{k}{2}(n_{l,k}-1)\bigg),

where
{n_{l,k}=\big\lceil\frac{l-1}{k}\big\rceil}
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the case of
m=1
.
Since
l
is the smallest generator of
S
and
l+k
is the largest one, there exists
the largest number denoted by
n_{l,k}
such that

for

.
This largest number

is given by

n_{l,k}=\big\lceil\frac{l-1}{k}\big\rceil
.
This means that any number

is in
S
, and
we have
F(S)=ln_{l,k}-1

Let
[a,b]
denote the set of numbers

Since the gaps are the integers in the set,

\displaystyle[1,l-1]\cup[l+k+1,2l-1]\cup\cdots\cup[(l+k)(n_{l,k}-1)+1,ln_{l,k}-1],

the number of gaps are counted by

\displaystyle g(S)=\sum_{n=0}^{n_{l,k}-1}(l-1-kn)=n_{l,k}\bigg(l-1-\frac{k}{2}(n_{l,k}-1)\bigg).

For the case with
m>1

m

\displaystyle\hat{K}A^{\rm T}=\big(\big[B^{(q-1)}\big]^{n_{1}}_{m},\big[B^{(q-2)}\big]^{n_{2}}_{m},\ldots,\big[B^{(0)}\big]^{n_{q}}_{m}\big).

Each element
[B^{(j)}]^{n_{q-j}}_{m}
is the
\infty\times mn_{q-j}
matrix,

\displaystyle\big[B^{(j)}\big]^{n_{q-j}}_{m}=\big(\big[B^{(j)}(\kappa_{1})\big]^{n_{q-j}},\big[B^{(j)}(\kappa_{2})\big]^{n_{q-j}},\ldots,\big[B^{(j)}(\kappa_{m})\big]^{n_{q-j}}\big).

The
n
th column of the
\infty\times n_{q-j}
matrix
\big[B^{(j)}(\kappa_{i})\big]^{n_{q-j}}
is given by

\displaystyle\text{Col}_{n}\big(\big[B^{(j)}(\kappa_{i})\big]^{n_{q-j}}\big)=\bigg(\!\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{l-n},l\binom{l+j-n}{j}\kappa_{i}^{l-n},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{l-1},l\binom{2l+j-n}{j}\kappa_{i}^{2l-n},0,\ldots\!\bigg)^{\rm T}\!.

(3) Consider the embedding
\sigma_{K}\colon A\mapsto\widetilde{\hat{K}A^{\rm T}}
. Then the corresponding element of UGM can be expressed as follows: For each
0\leq j\leq q-1
and
1\leq n\leq n_{q-j}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{j,n}(z,\kappa_{i}):=\big(z^{-N+1},z^{-N+2},\ldots,z^{-1},1,z^{1},\ldots\big)\text{Col}_{n}\big(\big[B^{(j)}(\kappa_{i})\big]^{n_{q-j}}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\psi_{j,n}(z,\kappa_i)}{}=lz^{-N+1}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\binom{rl+j-n}{j}(\kappa_{i}z)^{rl-n}=lz^{-N+1}\frac{1}{j!}\frac{{\rm d}^{j}}{{\rm d}u^{j}}\bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}u^{rl+j-n}\bigg)\bigg|_{u=\kappa_{i}z}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\psi_{j,n}(z,\kappa_i)}{}=lz^{-N+1}\frac{1}{j!}\frac{{\rm d}^{j}}{{\rm d}u^{j}}\bigg(\frac{u^{l+j-n}}{1-u^{l}}\bigg)\bigg|_{u=\kappa_{i}z},

which form the set of
N
elements in the base
\{\phi_{-i}(z)\colon i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
for a point of UGM, i.e., we assign

\displaystyle\{\phi_{-i}(z)\colon 0\leq i\leq N-1\}=\{\psi_{j,n}(z,\kappa_{i})\colon 0\leq j\leq q-1,\,1\leq n\leq n_{q-j},\,1\leq i\leq m\}.

And the rest in the base
\{\phi_{-i}(z)\colon i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is given by
\{\phi_{-i}(z)=z^{-i}\colon i\geq N\}
.

(4) Then we note that the set
\{\phi_{-i}(z)\colon 0\leq i\leq N-1\}
as a base of an
N
-dimensional vector space is equivalent to

\displaystyle\bigg\{\frac{z^{-N-s_{j}+l}}{(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l})^{j+1}}\colon 0\leq j<q,\,0\leq s_{j}<n_{q-j},\,1\leq i\leq m\bigg\}.

The other elements of the base are given by
\{\phi_{-i}(z)=z^{-i}\colon i\geq N\}
.

Now multiplying the base by
z^{N-mql}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q}
, we have a set of polynomials in
z^{-1}
of the form
\{\phi_{-i}(z)=z^{-i}F_{i}(z)\colon i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, where
F_{i}(z)
is a polynomial in
z
with
F_{0}(z)=1
and
\deg(F_{i}(z))<i
for
i\geq 1
. More precisely, we have the following:
First we have

\displaystyle\frac{z^{-(mq-1)l-s_{j}}}{\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{j+1}}\prod_{p=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q}=z^{-(mq-1)l-s_{j}}\prod_{p\neq i}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-1-j}.

Let us now express a set of basis for these meromorphic sections.

(i)

For
s_{j}=0
, we have
0\leq j\leq q-1
, and one can have an equivalent base for this case, that is,

\displaystyle\bigg\{z^{-(mq-1)l}\prod_{p\neq i}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-1-j}\colon 0\leq j\leq q-1,\,1\leq i\leq m\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\equiv\big\{1,z^{-l},z^{-2l},\ldots,z^{-(mq-1)l}\big\}=\big\{z^{-pl}\colon 0\leq p\leq mq-1\big\}.

Here the number of elements is
mq
.

(ii)

For each
s_{j}
with
1\leq s_{j}\leq k
, we have
0\leq j\leq q-2
. Then we write

\displaystyle z^{-(mq-1)l-s_{j}}\prod_{p\neq i}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-1-j}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=z^{-(mq-1)l-s_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)\bigg(\prod_{p\neq i}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q-1}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-2-j}\bigg),

and an equivalent base can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bigg\{z^{-(mq-1)l-s_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)\bigg(\prod_{p\neq i}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q-1}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-2-j}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\equiv\bigg\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)z^{-pl-s_{j}}\colon m\leq p\leq mq-1,\,1\leq i\leq m\bigg\}.

Here the number of elements in the base is
m(q-1)
for each
1\leq s_{j}\leq k
.

(iii)

For each
s_{j}
with
k+1\leq s_{j}\leq 2k
, we have
0\leq j\leq q-3
. Then
an equivalent base is

\displaystyle\bigg\{z^{-(mq-1)l-s_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{2}\bigg(\prod_{p\neq i}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q-2}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-3-j}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\equiv\bigg\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{2}z^{-pl-s_{j}}\colon 2m\leq p\leq mq-1,\,1\leq i\leq m\bigg\}.

This continues to
(q-2)k+1\leq s_{j}\leq(q-1)k
for just
j=0
, and this gives

\displaystyle\bigg\{z^{-(mq-1)l-s_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-1}\prod_{p\neq i}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\equiv\bigg\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{i}z)^{l}\big)^{q-1}z^{-pl-s_{j}}\colon(q-1)m\leq p\leq mq-1\bigg\},

whose number of elements is just
m
for each
s_{0}
in
(q-2)k+1\leq s_{0}\leq(q-1)k
.

Notice that the total number of elements in the bases in (i) through (iii) is just
N=mq(1+k(q-1)/2)
.
Then we have a base
\mathcal{A}(z)=\mathcal{A}_{1}(z)\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}(z)
for a subspace of
\mathbb{C}\big[z^{-1}\big]
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(z):=\left\{z^{-p(r)l-s_{j}(r)}\prod_{p=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{r}\colon\begin{array}[]{lllll}0\leq r\leq q-1,\\[2.15277pt]
rm\leq p(r)\leq mq-1,\\[2.15277pt]
(r-1)k+1\leq s_{j}(r)\leq rk\end{array}\right\}\!,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}(z):=\bigg\{z^{-mql-i+N}\prod_{p=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)^{q}\colon i\geq N\bigg\}.

(5) Consider the index set for the minimal degrees of the polynomials in
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle S=\left\{p(r)l+s(r)\colon\begin{array}[]{lllll}0\leq r\leq q-1,\\[2.15277pt]
rm\leq p(r)\leq mq-1,\\[2.15277pt]
(r-1)k+1\leq s(r)\leq rk\end{array}\right\}\bigcup\,\{mql+i\colon i\geq 0\}.

We now show that the set
S
forms a numerical semigroup of type
\langle l,lm+1,lm+2,\ldots,lm+k\rangle
. It is obvious that
0\in S
and
|\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus S|<\infty
.
The closeness under the addition is given as follows: Write
p(r)
and
s(r)
for
1\leq r\leq q-1
as

\displaystyle p(r)=rm+\alpha,\qquad 0\leq\alpha\leq m(q-r)-1,

\displaystyle s(r)=(r-1)k+\beta,\qquad 1\leq\beta\leq k.

Then each element can be expressed as

\displaystyle p(r)l+s(r)=\alpha l+(lm+\beta)+(r-1)(lm+k),

which is in the span
\langle l,lm+1,\ldots,lm+k\rangle
. For the element
mql+i
with
i\geq 0
, we first write

\displaystyle i=\gamma l+\delta\qquad\text{with}\quad 0\leq\delta\leq l-1.

Then noting
l-1\leq qk
, we have

\displaystyle\delta=\mu k+\nu\qquad\text{with}\quad 0\leq\mu\leq q\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\nu\leq k-1,

which leads to

\displaystyle mql+i=\mu(ml+k)+\gamma l+m(q-\mu)l+\nu.

When
k=1
, we have
\nu=0
and
mql+i
is in the span
\langle l,lm+1\rangle
.
For the case with
k>1
and
\nu>0
, we have
\mu<q
, hence

\displaystyle m(q-\mu)l=m(q-\mu-1)l+ml.

Therefore the element
mql+i
is in the span
\langle l,lm+1,\ldots,lm+k\rangle
for all
i\geq 0
.

We introduce the following variables,

\displaystyle x=z^{-l}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{i}=z^{-ml-i}\prod_{p=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{p}z)^{l}\big)\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq k.

Then, the subspace spanned by the basis
\mathcal{A}
can be expressed by a polynomial ring,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}=\mathbb{C}[x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}]/\mathcal{P},

where the prime ideal
\mathcal{P}
is encoded in the set of
2\times 2
minors of the
following
2\times(k+1)
matrix,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{k}&G(x)\\
F(x)^{l-k}&F(x)^{l-k-1}y_{1}&\cdots&F(x)^{l-k-1}y_{k-1}&y_{1}^{l-k-1}y_{k}\end{pmatrix}\!.

(6.1)

Here the functions
F(x)
and
G(x)
are given by

\displaystyle F(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\kappa_{j}^{l}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad G(x)=xF(x).

Note that the spectrum
{\rm Spec}({\mathcal{R}})
gives the affine part of a singular curve in the
(k+1)
-dimensional space of
(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
. We also remark that this space curve is an irreducible
component of the singular curve defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\big\{(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k+1}\colon y_{i}^{l}=G(x)^{i}F(x)^{l-i},\,i=1,\ldots,k\big\}.

(6) Let
\lambda(S)
be the Young diagram for the numerical semigroup
S
.
Using the Binet–Cauchy formula, we can see that the
\tau
-function has the expansion,

\displaystyle\tau(t)=\left|\begin{pmatrix}1&p_{1}&p_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
0&1&p_{1}&p_{2}&\cdots&\cdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&1&p_{1}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}\hat{K}\,A^{\rm T}\right|=S_{\lambda}(\hat{t})+\sum_{\mu\supset\lambda}c_{\mu}(\kappa)S_{\mu}(\hat{t}),

where
\hat{t}=\{t_{n}\colon n\neq ml~\text{for any~}m\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
c_{\mu}(\kappa)
is a homogeneous symmetric polynomial of
\big(\kappa_{1}^{l},\ldots,\kappa_{m}^{l}\big)
, which is given by the
N\times N
minor of the matrix
\hat{K}A^{\rm T}
with the rows indexed by the diagram
\mu
. Also note that the Young diagram associated with the matrix
A

is just
\lambda(S)
(see Theorem
2.2
), i.e.,
A\in X_{\lambda(S)}\subset{\rm Gr}(N,M)
. The pivot index of
A
is given by

\displaystyle i_{k}=s_{k-1}+1\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq k\leq N=mq\bigg(1+\frac{1}{2}k(q-1)\bigg),

where
s_{j}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,N-1
are the first
N
ordered elements in
S
.

7
Deformation of the singular space curves for soliton solutions

Her we construct a smooth space curve for the numerical semigroup of type

\langle l,lm+1,\ldots
,
l(m+1)-1\rangle
, i.e.,
k=l-1
. Then the smooth curve naturally degenerates to the singular curve associated with a soliton solution of the
l
-th generalized KdV hierarchy.

Let us recall that the singular curve for the soliton solution corresponding to this type has the local coordinates,

\displaystyle x=z^{-l},\qquad y_{i}=z^{-i}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\kappa_{j}^{l}\big)\qquad(1\leq i\leq l-1).

Then we have the following algebraic relations (i.e., (
6.1
) with
k=l-1
),

\displaystyle p_{i,j}:=y_{i}y_{j-1}-y_{j}y_{i-1}=0\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i<j\leq l,

(7.1)

with
y_{0}:=F(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(x-\kappa_{j}^{l})
and
y_{l}:=G(x)=xF(x)
.
These relations can be expressed by the
2\times 2
minors of the following
2\times l
matrix,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{l-2}&y_{l-1}&G(x)\\
F(x)&y_{1}&\cdots&y_{l-3}&y_{l-2}&y_{l-1}\end{pmatrix}\!.

Note that the relations in (
7.1
) give a prime ideal in
\mathbb{C}[x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{l-1}]
.

Inspired by the paper
[
12
]
(also see
[
24
]
),
we consider the following deformation,

\displaystyle F(x)\longrightarrow\tilde{F}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\lambda_{j}^{l}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad G(x)\longrightarrow\tilde{G}(x)=x\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\lambda_{m+j}^{l}\big),

(7.2)

where
\lambda_{j}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,2m
are all distinct.
The singular limit is then given by coalescing two moduli parameters of the space curve,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{l},\,\lambda_{m+j}^{l}\longrightarrow\kappa_{j}^{l}\qquad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,m.

One should note here that the singularity obtained by the above process is an
ordinary
l
-tuple
point singularity

[
6
, p. 247]
. When
l=2
, the hyperelliptic case, this is an ordinary double point singularity
[
20
,
29
]
.

The smoothness of the curve can be shown as follows:
We consider a commutative ring defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{C}[x,y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{l-1}]/\mathcal{P},

where the prime ideal
\mathcal{P}
is given by the relations in (
7.1
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{p_{i,j}=y_{i}y_{j-1}-y_{j}y_{i-1}=0\colon 1\leq i<j\leq l\}\qquad\text{with}\quad y_{0}=\tilde{F}(x),\quad y_{l}=\tilde{G}(x).

(Hereafter we will omit the
\,\widetilde{}\,
on the functions
F(x)
and
G(x)
.)
Then the affine part of the curve associated with the soliton solution is given by
\text{Spec}(\mathcal{R})
. We now show that
{\rm Spec}({\mathcal{R}})
is non-singular.

Proposition 7.1
.

For every
(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{l-1})
satisfying all
p_{i,j}=0
, the following Jacobian
\mathcal{U}
has rank
l-1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}:=\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}p_{i,j},\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{1}}p_{i,j},\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{l-1}}p_{i,j}\bigg)_{1\leq i<j\leq l}.

Here
p_{i,j}
in each column of the matrix
\mathcal{U}
are arranged in the following way,

\displaystyle\big(p_{1,j}~(2\leq j\leq l);~p_{2,j}~(3\leq j\leq l);~\ldots;~p_{i,j}~(i+1\leq j\leq l);~\ldots;~p_{l-1,l}\big)^{\rm T}.

Proof.

The
\binom{l}{2}\times l
Jacobian matrix has the following structure.

(1) For the columns with
p_{1,j}
(
2\leq j\leq l
), we have the
(l-1)\times l
matrix,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-y_{2}F^{\prime}&2y_{1}&-F&0&0&\cdots&0\\
-y_{3}F^{\prime}&y_{2}&y_{1}&-F&0&\cdots&0\\
-y_{4}F^{\prime}&y_{3}&0&y_{1}&-F&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-y_{l-1}F^{\prime}&y_{l-2}&0&\cdots&0&y_{1}&-F\\
-(FG)^{\prime}&y_{l-1}&0&\cdots&0&0&y_{1}\end{pmatrix}\!.

(2) For the columns with
p_{i,j}

(i+1\leq j\leq l)
and
2\leq i\leq l-3
, we have the
(l-i)\times l
matrix,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&-y_{i+1}&2y_{i}&-y_{i-1}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&-y_{i+2}&y_{i+1}&y_{i}&-y_{i-1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&-y_{i+3}&y_{i+2}&0&y_{i}&-y_{i-1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&-y_{l-1}&y_{l-2}&0&\cdots&0&y_{i}&-y_{i-1}\\
-y_{i-1}G^{\prime}&0&\cdots&0&-G&y_{l-1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&0&y_{i}\end{pmatrix}\!,

where the first nonzero entry in each row corresponds to
\partial p_{i,j}/\partial y_{i-1}
.

(3) For the last three rows, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&-y_{l-1}&2y_{l-2}&-y_{l-3}\\
-y_{l-3}G^{\prime}&0&\cdots&0&-G&y_{l-1}&y_{l-2}\\
-y_{l-2}G^{\prime}&0&\cdots&0&0&-G&2y_{l-1}\end{pmatrix}\!.

We consider the cases:
(a)

F(x)=0
,
(b)

G(x)=0
, and
(c)

F(x)G(x)\neq 0
.

(a)

F(x)=0
implies
x=\lambda_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq m
. Then, we have
y_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\dots,l-1
.
Then the Jacobian matrix has the following nonzero entries,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{1,l}}{\partial x}\bigg|_{F=0,y=0}=-F^{\prime}G,\qquad\frac{\partial p_{i,l}}{\partial y_{i-1}}\bigg|_{F=0,y=0}=-G\qquad(2\leq i\leq l-1).

This implies
{\rm rank}(\mathcal{U})=l-1
.

(b)

G(x)=0
implies
x=0
or
x=\lambda_{m+j}
for some
1\leq j\leq m
. Then, we have
y_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\dots,l-1
.
Then the Jacobian matrix has the following nonzero entries,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{1,l}}{\partial x}\bigg|_{F=0,y=0}=-FG^{\prime},\qquad\frac{\partial p_{1,j}}{\partial y_{j}}\bigg|_{F=0,y=0}=-F\qquad(2\leq j\leq l-1).

This implies
{\rm rank}(\mathcal{U})=l-1
.

(c)

For the case
F(x)G(x)\neq 0
, we have all non zero variables
(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{l-1})
. Let us consider the null space of
\mathcal{U}^{\rm T}
, the transpose of
\mathcal{U}
.
Each vector in
{\rm null}\big(\mathcal{U}^{\rm T}\big)
can be found is the following form: For
3\leq i\leq l
, we have
(l-i+1)\times\binom{l}{2}
matrix whose rows satisfy
r\mathcal{U}=(0,\ldots,0)
,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&y_{i}&-y_{i-1}&0&\cdots&0&y_{1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&y_{i+1}&0&-y_{i-1}&0&\cdots&0&y_{1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&y_{l-1}&0&\cdots&0&-y_{i-1}&0&\cdots&0&y_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&y_{l}&0&\cdots&0&0&-y_{i-1}&0&\cdots&0&y_{1}&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}\!,

where the first nonzero entry in each row is at
(i-2)
-th place, and
y_{l}=G
. The total number of these rows is
\binom{l-1}{2}
. This implies that the nullity of
\mathcal{U}^{\rm T}
is
\binom{l-1}{2}
, hence
the rank of
\mathcal{U}
is
\binom{l}{2}-\binom{l-1}{2}=l-1
.

This proves that the affine variety given by
{\rm Spec}({\mathcal{R}})
is a smooth curve.
∎

A Riemann surface associated with the curve
\mathcal{C}
can be obtained by adding one point
\infty
to the affine smooth curve given by
{\rm Spec}({\mathcal{R}})
. At
\infty
, we introduce
the variables,

\displaystyle\underline{x}=\frac{1}{x},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underline{y}_{i}=\frac{y_{i}}{x^{m+1}}\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq l-1.

Then we have a commutative ring,

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathcal{R}}}=\mathbb{C}[\underline{x},\underline{y}_{1},\ldots,\underline{y}_{l-1}]/\underline{\mathcal{P}},

where the prime ideal
\underline{\mathcal{P}}
is given by

\displaystyle\underline{\mathcal{P}}=\left\{\underline{p}_{i,j}=\left|\begin{matrix}\underline{y}_{i}&\underline{y}_{j}\\
\underline{y}_{i-1}&\underline{y}_{j-1}\end{matrix}\right|\!\colon 1\leq i<j\leq l\right\}\!,

with

\displaystyle\underline{y}_{0}=\underline{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(1-\lambda_{j}^{l}\underline{x}\big),\qquad\underline{y}_{l}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(1-\lambda_{m+j}^{l}\underline{x}\big).

In a similar manner, we can prove that the affine curve given by
{\rm Spec}(\underline{{\mathcal{R}}})
is
also smooth. Then the Riemann surface associated to the curve
\mathcal{C}
is obtained by
patching these affine curves.
In terms of the notion of the Weierstrass semigroup
[
25
]
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 7.2
.

The numerical semigroup of type
\langle l,lm+1,lm+2,\ldots,l(m+1)-1\rangle
is Weierstrass for
m\geq 2
.

Finally, we remark that the spectrum
{\rm Spec}({\mathcal{R}})
is given by a projection of

{\rm Spec}\big(\widehat{{\mathcal{R}}}\big)
with the following ring
\widehat{{\mathcal{R}}}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{R}}}:=\mathbb{C}[x,w_{1},w_{2}]/\big(w_{1}^{l}-F(x),w_{2}^{l}-G(x)\big).

We note a natural ring homomorphism
{\mathcal{R}}\to\widehat{{\mathcal{R}}}
with

\displaystyle y_{i}=w_{1}^{l-i}w_{2}^{i}\qquad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,l-1.

This leads to a projection,

\displaystyle{\rm Spec}\big(\widehat{{\mathcal{R}}}\big)\longrightarrow{\rm Spec}({\mathcal{R}}).

We conclude the section with the following remark about the cases for the generalized
soliton solutions, i.e.,
k<l-1
.

Remark 7.3
.

In the case of
k=1
in (
6.1
), we have just one relation,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&G(x)\\
F(x)^{l-1}&y_{1}^{l-1}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{which gives}\quad y_{1}^{l}=G(x)F(x)^{l-1},

which is a singular plane curve. One can deform the curve with

\displaystyle\begin{cases}F(x)^{l-1}\longrightarrow\displaystyle{\prod_{i=0}^{l-2}F_{i+1}(x)\qquad\text{with}\quad F_{i+1}}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\lambda_{im+j}^{l}\big),\\[8.61108pt]
G(x)\longrightarrow\displaystyle{x\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\lambda_{lm-j}^{l}\big)},\end{cases}

which then gives a smooth plane curve, called cyclic
(l,lm+1)
-curve,

\displaystyle y_{1}^{l}=G(x)\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}F_{i}(x)=x\prod_{j=1}^{lm}\big(x-\lambda_{j}^{l}\big).

The cases with
l=2
are just the hyperelliptic ones. The singular curve associated to the generalized soliton solution is obtained by coalescing
l
points of the
(l,lm+1)
-curve,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{l},\lambda_{m+j}^{l},\ldots,\lambda_{(l-1)m+j}^{l}\longrightarrow\kappa_{j}^{l}\qquad\text{for}\quad j=1,\ldots,m.

For the cases with
1<k<l-1
, it seems in general that
the singular curve is not smoothable in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{k+1}

[
8
,
24
]
. One of a special case was found in
[
12
]
for
l=6
,
m=2
and
k=4
, i.e., the corresponding numerical semigroup is of type
\langle 6,13,14,15,16\rangle
. One can also show that the case of
\langle 4,4m+1,4m+2\rangle
(i.e.,
l=4
and
k=2
) has the following “smooth” deformation. The ring of the polynomials
{\mathcal{R}}
is given by

\mathbb{C}[x,y_{1},y_{2}]/{\tilde{\mathcal{P}}}
, where the prime ideal is given by

\displaystyle\big\{y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}F_{2}(x),y_{2}^{2}-G(x)F_{1}(x)\big\},\qquad\text{from}\quad\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}&y_{2}&G(x)\\
F_{1}(x)F_{2}(x)&F_{1}(x)y_{1}&y_{1}y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!,

where
F_{1}(x)
,
F_{2}(x)
and
G(x)
are given by

\displaystyle F_{1}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\lambda_{j}^{4}\big),\qquad F_{2}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\lambda_{m+j}^{4}\big),\qquad G(x)=x\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\lambda_{2m+j}^{4}\big).

This smooth curve is an irreducible component of the intersection of the hypersurfaces given by

\displaystyle y_{1}^{4}=G(x)F_{1}(x)F_{2}(x)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{2}^{2}=G(x)F_{1}(x).

We will discuss the deformation problem for the cases with
2\leq k\leq l-2
for
l\geq 5
in a future communication.

8
Spectral curves for soliton solutions

In this section, we identify the singular space curve given by (
7.1
) as a spectral curve
of commuting differential operators in the Lax–Sato formulation of the KP hierarchy (see Appendix
A
, and also, e.g.,
[
9
]
for the formulation). This is an extension of the well-know theorem
by Burchnall and Chaundy
[
7
]
. We here consider the cases for the soliton solutions
corresponding to
k=l-1
and the generalized soliton solutions corresponding to
k=l-2
. The general case with
1\leq k\leq l-3
will be left for the readers.

8.1
Spectral curve for the soliton solution (
\boldsymbol{k=l-1}
)

We first recall that the
\tau
-function of the soliton solution is given by
\tau(t)=\big|E(t)A^{\rm T}\big|
in (
2.6
), where the
m\times lm
matrices
A
is given by

\displaystyle A=I_{m}\otimes\Omega_{l}^{1},\qquad\text{where}\quad\Omega_{l}^{1}=\big(1,\omega_{l},\omega_{l}^{2},\ldots,\omega_{l}^{l-1}\big)\qquad\text{with}\quad\omega_{l}=\exp\bigg(\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{l}\bigg),

and
E(t)
whose base exponential functions (
5.1
) are given by

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(0)}]_{m}=(\mathsf{E}(\kappa_{1}),\ldots,\mathsf{E}(\kappa_{m}))\qquad\text{with}\quad\mathsf{E}(\kappa_{j})=(E_{1}(t,\kappa_{j}),\ldots,E_{l}(t,\kappa_{j})).

Here
E_{i}(t,\kappa_{j})
is the exponential function given in (
2.18
), i.e.,

\displaystyle E_{i}(t,\kappa_{j})=\exp\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(\kappa_{j}\omega^{i-1}\big)^{n}t_{n}\bigg).

Now we define the following time-operator,

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}:=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)\partial_{(m-i)l+\alpha}\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\alpha\leq l-1,

where
\sigma_{i}(\kappa)
is the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree
i
in
\big(\kappa_{1}^{l},\ldots,\kappa_{m}^{l}\big)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\lambda-\kappa_{j}^{l})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)\lambda^{m-i}.

(8.1)

Then we have the following proposition.

Proposition 8.1
.

The
\tau
-function generated by
(A,E(t))
above satisfies

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}\tau(t)=0\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\alpha\leq l-1.

Proof.

Since the
\tau
-function depends only on the exponential functions
E_{s}(t,\kappa_{j})
for
1\leq s\leq l
, it is sufficient to show that each
E_{s}(t,\kappa_{j})
satisfies

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}E_{s}(t,\kappa_{j})=0\qquad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq\alpha\leq l-1.

Direct computation shows

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}E_{s}(t,\kappa_{j})=\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)\big(\kappa_{j}\omega_{l}^{s-1}\big)^{(m-i)l+\alpha}\bigg)E_{s}(t,\kappa_{j}).

Since
\omega_{l}^{l}=1
, the term in the parenthesis in the equation becomes

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)\kappa_{j}^{(m-i)l}\bigg)\big(\omega_{l}^{s-1}\kappa_{j}\big)^{\alpha}=0,

where we have used (
8.1
) with
\lambda=\kappa_{j}^{l}
. This proves the proposition.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

Note here that the equation
T_{\alpha}\tau(t)=0
does not depend on the matrix
A
.
This implies that any soliton solution of the
l
-th generalized KdV hierarchy satisfies
Proposition
8.1
. Also note that the number of the free parameters in the
matrix
A
is
m(l-1)
, which is the genus of the corresponding numerical semigroup, i.e.,
g(S)=m(l-1)
.

Since the solutions of the
l
-th generalized KdV hierarchy can be expressed by
a single
\tau
-function, they also satisfy
T_{\alpha}v_{i}=0
, where
v_{i}
’s are from the
l
-th differential operator
L^{l}
of (
A.7
)
in Appendix
A
.

Now we define the following differential operators of order
ml+\alpha
in
\partial
,

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}:=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)B_{(m-i)l+\alpha}\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\alpha\leq l-1,

where
B_{\beta}
is the differential operator of order
\beta
defined in (
A.1
), i.e.,
B_{\beta}=(L^{\beta})_{\geq 0}
. Then we have
the following proposition.

Proposition 8.3
.

The
l
differential operators
\big\{L^{l},L_{1},\ldots,L_{l-1}\big\}
mutually commute, i.e.,

(a)

\big[L_{\alpha},\,L^{l}\big]=0
for any
1\leq\alpha\leq l-1
, and

(b)

[L_{\alpha},\,L_{\beta}]=0
for any
1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq l-1
.

Proof.

(a)
Using the Lax equation (
A.1
), the commutator becomes

\displaystyle\big[L_{\alpha},L^{l}\big]=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)\big[B_{(m-i)l+\alpha},L^{l}\big]=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)\partial_{(m-i)l+\alpha}\big(L^{l}\big)=T_{\alpha}\big(L^{l}\big).

Since
T_{\alpha}\tau=0
(Proposition
8.1
), we have
T_{\alpha}(L^{l})=0
.

(b)
Using the Zakharov–Shabat equations in (
A.3
), the commutator becomes

\displaystyle[L_{\alpha},\,L_{\beta}]=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{i+j}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)\sigma_{j}(\kappa)[B_{(m-i)l+\alpha},B_{(m-j)l+\beta}]

\displaystyle\hphantom{[L_\alpha,\,L_\beta]}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\sigma_{j}(\kappa)T_{\alpha}(B_{(m-j)l+\beta})-\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)T_{\beta}(B_{(m-i)l+\alpha}).

Again, note that the coefficients of the differential operators
B_{n}
depend on
t
only through the
\tau
-function.
This implies that
T_{\alpha}(B_{\beta})=0
for any
1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq l-1
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8.4
.

The statement
(b)
in Proposition
8.3
is a direct consequence of
(a)
due to a result of Schur
[
28
]
(see also
[
19
]
).

In order to find the spectral curve given by the commuting differential operators
\big\{L^{l},L_{1}
,
\ldots,L_{l-1}\big\}
, we first note the following lemma.

Lemma 8.5
.

For the wave function
\phi=W\phi_{0}
in (
A.4
), we have

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}\phi=\bigg(z^{-ml-\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{j}z)^{l}\big)\bigg)\phi\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\alpha\leq l-1.

Proof.

We first note that

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}\phi=\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)B_{(m-i)l+\alpha}\phi=T_{\alpha}\phi,

where we have used
\partial_{n}\phi=B_{n}\phi
in (
A.2
).
Then we write the wave function in the dressing form
\phi=W\phi_{0}
as in (
A.5
) with (
A.4
).
Noting again that the dressing operator depends on
t
only through the
\tau
-function, we have

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}\phi=WT_{\alpha}\phi_{0}=W\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)z^{-(m-i)l-\alpha}\bigg)\phi_{0}=z^{-ml-\alpha}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}(\kappa)z^{il}\bigg)\phi

\displaystyle\hphantom{T_\alpha\phi}{}=z^{-ml-\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{j}z)^{l}\big)\phi.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Now using the coordinates in (
3.8
), i.e.,

\displaystyle x=z^{-l},\qquad y_{\alpha}=z^{-ml-\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(1-(\kappa_{j}z)^{l}\big)\qquad\text{for}\quad\alpha=1,\ldots,l-1,

we have

\displaystyle L^{l}\phi=x\phi,\qquad L_{\alpha}=y_{\alpha}\phi\qquad\text{for}\quad\alpha=1,\ldots,l-1.

Then from Lemma
8.5
, the following theorem is immediate (see Section
7
).

Theorem 8.6
.

The eigenvalues of the commuting differential operators
\{L^{l},L_{1},\ldots,L_{l-1}\}
satisfy
the following relations,

\displaystyle p_{i,j}=y_{i}y_{j-1}-y_{j}y_{i-1}=0\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i<j\leq l,

where
y_{0}=F(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\big(x-\kappa_{j}^{l}\big)
and
y_{l}=G(x)=xF(x)
.

Remark 8.7
.

Theorem
8.6
can be considered as an extension of the well-known theorem by Burchnall and Chaundy
[
7
]
on the commuting pair of differential operators of positive order.

8.2
Spectral curve for the generalized soliton solution with
\boldsymbol{k=l-2}

In this case, we have
q=n_{l,k}=\big\lceil\frac{l-1}{l-2}\big\rceil=2
, hence the matrix
A
in (
5.4
) is an
lm\times 2lm
matrix given by

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}\otimes[\Omega_{l}]^{1}&0\\
0&I_{m}\otimes[\Omega_{l}]^{l-1}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{with}\quad[\Omega_{l}]^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{l}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\Omega_{l}^{n}\end{pmatrix}\!.

The base functions in (
5.1
) are given by

\displaystyle\big(\big[\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}\big]_{m},\big[\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(0)}\big]_{m}\big)\qquad\text{with}\quad\big[\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}\big]_{m}=\big(\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}(\kappa_{1}),\ldots,\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}(\kappa_{m})\big),

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}(\kappa_{j})=\big(E^{(1)}_{1}(t,\kappa_{j}),\ldots,E^{(1)}_{l}(t,\kappa_{j})\big)\qquad\text{with}\quad E^{(1)}_{i}(t,\kappa_{j})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa_{j}}\kappa_{j}E_{i}(t,\kappa_{j}).

Since the matrix
A
and the base functions consist of
m
copies of the single case with different
\kappa_{j}
’s, we here consider the case for
m=1
. The general case for
m>1
follows in the similar manner as the case with
m=1
. The
\tau
-function for this case has the following expansion,

\displaystyle\tau(t)=\big|E(t)A^{\rm T}\big|=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\Delta_{I_{i,\hat{j}}}(A)E_{I_{i,\hat{j}}}(t)\qquad\text{with}\quad I_{i,\hat{j}}=\big(i,l+1,\ldots,\widehat{l+j},\ldots,2l\big),

where
\widehat{l+j}
is the missing column index of the matrix
A
and the matrix
E(t)
is given by

\displaystyle E(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}(\kappa)&\mathsf{E}(\kappa)\\
\partial_{1}\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}(\kappa)&\partial_{1}\mathsf{E}(\kappa)\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\partial_{1}^{l-1}\hat{\mathsf{E}}^{(1)}(\kappa)&\partial_{1}^{l-1}\mathsf{E}(\kappa)\end{pmatrix}\!.

Then the exponential function
E_{I_{i,\hat{j}}}(t)
is expressed by the
l\times l
Wronskian determinant,

\displaystyle E_{I_{i,\hat{j}}}(t)=\text{Wr}\big(E^{(1)}_{i}(t,\kappa),E_{1}(t,\kappa),\ldots,\widehat{E_{j}(t,\kappa)},\ldots,E_{l}(t,\kappa)\big),

where the column of
{E_{j}(t,\kappa)}
is removed from the matrix
E(t)
.
Then we have the following proposition.

Proposition 8.8
.

The
\tau
-function for the generalized soliton solution of type
\langle l,l+1,\ldots,2l-2\rangle
satisfies

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}\tau(t)=0\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\alpha\leq l-2,

where
T_{\alpha}=\partial_{l+\alpha}-\kappa^{l}\partial_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

First recall that
T_{\alpha}E_{i}(t,\kappa)=0
for any
i
. Then we have

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}E_{i}^{(1)}(t,\kappa)=-\frac{\partial T_{\alpha}}{\partial\kappa}\kappa E_{i}(t,\kappa)=l\kappa^{l+\alpha}\big(\omega_{l}^{i-1}\big)^{\alpha}E_{i}(t,\kappa).

We then note that
T_{\alpha}E_{I_{i,\hat{j}}}
assumes nonzero value only if
i=j
, and

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}E_{I_{i,\hat{i}}}(t)=(-1)^{i-1}l\kappa^{l+\alpha}\big(\omega_{l}^{i-1}\big)^{\alpha}\text{Wr}(E_{1}(t,\kappa),\ldots,E_{l}(t,\kappa)).

Now applying
T_{\alpha}
on the
\tau
-function, we have

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}\tau(t)=l\kappa^{l+\alpha}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{l}(-1)^{i-1}\big(\omega_{l}^{i-1}\big)^{\alpha}\Delta_{I_{i,\hat{i}}}(A)\bigg)\text{Wr}(E_{1}(t,\kappa),\ldots,E_{l}(t,\kappa)).

The term in the parenthesis is expressed by the
l\times l
determinant,

\displaystyle\left|\begin{matrix}1&\omega_{l}^{\alpha+1}&\omega_{l}^{2(\alpha+1)}&\cdots&\omega_{l}^{(l-1)(\alpha+1)}\\
1&\omega_{l}&\omega_{l}^{2}&\cdots&\omega_{l}^{l-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\omega_{l}^{l-1}&\omega_{l}^{l-2}&\cdots&\omega_{l}\end{matrix}\right|

which vanishes for
1\leq\alpha\leq l-2
, and not zero for
\alpha=l-1
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8.9
.

Unlike the case of the soliton solution, one should note that for the generalized soliton solution,

T_{\alpha}\tau=0
is true only for
1\leq\alpha\leq l-2
and depends also on the matrix
A
.

Proposition
8.8
implies that the generalized soliton solutions of
type
\langle l,l+1,\ldots,2l-2\rangle
also have
Proposition
8.3
and Lemma
8.5
but only for
1\leq\alpha\leq l-2
.

Appendix A
The Lax–Sato formulation of the KP hierarchy

Here we give a brief summary of the Lax–Sato formulation of the KP hierarchy and
the
l
-th generalized KdV hierarchy. We also give the Wronskian
formula of the
\tau
-function for the KP solitons.

A.1
The
\boldsymbol{l}
-th generalized KdV hierarchy

The Sato theory of the KP hierarchy is formulated on the basis of a pseudo-differential operator,

\displaystyle L=\partial+u_{2}\partial^{-1}+u_{3}\partial^{-2}+\cdots,

where
\partial
is a derivative satisfying
\partial\partial^{-1}=\partial^{-1}\partial=1
and the generalized Leibniz rule,

\displaystyle\partial^{\nu}f\cdot=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\nu}{k}\big(\partial_{1}^{k}f\big)\partial^{\nu-k}\cdot,

for any smooth functions
f
. (Note that the series terminates if and only if
\nu
is a nonnegative integer.) Then the KP hierarchy can be written in the Lax form,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}(L)=[B_{n},\,L]\qquad\text{with}\quad B_{n}=(L^{n})_{\geq 0}\qquad(n=1,2,\ldots),

(A.1)

where
(L^{n})_{\geq 0}
represents the polynomial (differential) part of
L^{n}
in
\partial
.
The solution of the KP equation (
2.1
) is given by
u=2u_{2}
. The Lax equation (
A.1
)
is also given by the compatibility condition of the linear system,

\displaystyle L\phi=z^{-1}\phi,\qquad\partial_{n}\phi=B_{n}\phi,

(A.2)

where
\phi
is called the wave function of the Lax pair
(L,B_{n})
. The compatibility among the equations
\partial_{n}\phi=B_{n}\phi
, which is
\partial_{n}\partial_{m}\phi=\partial_{m}\partial_{n}\phi
, gives

\displaystyle\partial_{m}(B_{n})-\partial_{n}(B_{m})+[B_{n},B_{m}]=0,

(A.3)

which is called the Zakharov–Shabat equations.

The variable
z\in\mathbb{C}
in (
A.2
) may be considered as a local coordinate at
\infty

in the spectral space of
L
. Note that if the functions
u_{i}
’s
are all zero, then we have
L=\partial
and
B_{n}=\partial^{n}
and the wave function, denoted by
\phi_{0}
, is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(z;t)=\exp\bigg(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t_{n}}{z^{n}}\bigg).

(A.4)

The wave function
\phi
is then expressed in the dressing form,

\displaystyle\phi=W\phi_{0}\qquad\text{with}\quad W=1-w_{1}\partial^{-1}-w_{2}\partial^{-2}-\cdots,

(A.5)

where the pseudo-differential operator
W
is called the dressing operator. Notice that all the functions
u_{i}
’s in
L
can be determined by
w_{j}
’s in
W
through

\displaystyle L=W\partial W^{-1}.

For example, we have

\displaystyle u_{2}=\partial_{1}w_{1},\qquad u_{3}=\partial_{1}w_{2}+w_{1}\partial_{1}w_{1},\qquad\ldots.

Then, from the Lax equation, the dressing operator
W
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{n}(W)=B_{n}W-W\partial^{n}\qquad\text{for}\quad n=1,2,\dots,

(A.6)

which is sometimes called the Sato equation.

The
l
-th generalized KdV hierarchy is the
l
-reduction of the KP hierarchy defined by

\displaystyle L^{l}=\big(L^{l}\big)_{\geq 0},

that is, the
l
-th power of
L
becomes a differential operator. This means that the functions
u_{i}
’s
are determined by
l-1
variables in
L^{l}
in the form,

\displaystyle L^{l}=\partial^{l}+v_{2}\partial^{l-2}+v_{3}\partial^{l-3}+\cdots+v_{l-1}\partial+v_{l}.

(A.7)

Also note that those variables are determined by
w_{i}
’s in
W
. From (
A.1
), the
l
-reduction gives the constraints,

\displaystyle\partial_{nl}(L)=0\qquad\text{for}\quad n=1,2,\ldots,

that is, all the variables
v_{i}
’s do not depend on the times
t_{nl}
. The original KdV hierarchy
is given by the 2-reduction, and the solutions do not depend on the times
t_{2n}
.

A.2
\boldsymbol{N}
truncation and the
\boldsymbol{\tau}
-function

Here we explain the Wronskian formula of the
\tau
-function.
First note that a finite truncation of
W
with some positive integer
N
, given by

\displaystyle W=1-w_{1}\partial^{-1}-w_{2}\partial^{-2}-\cdots-w_{N}\partial^{-N},

is invariant under (
A.6
). We then consider the
N
-th order differential equation,

\displaystyle W\partial^{N}f=f^{(N)}-w_{1}f^{(N-1)}-w_{2}f^{(N-2)}-\cdots-w_{N}f=0,

where
f^{(n)}=\partial_{1}^{n}f
. Let
\{f_{i}\colon i=1,\ldots,N\}
be a fundamental set of solutions of
the equation
W\partial^{N}f=0
. Then the functions
w_{i}
’s are given by

\displaystyle w_{i}=-\frac{1}{\tau}p_{i}\big({-}\tilde{\partial}\big)\tau\qquad\text{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

where
p_{i}(x)
is the elementary Schur polynomial of degree
i
and
\tilde{\partial}=\big(\partial_{1},\frac{1}{2}\partial_{2},\frac{1}{3}\partial_{3},\ldots\big)
. Here
\tau
is the
\tau
-function given by
the Wronskian form,

\displaystyle\tau=\text{Wr}(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{N})=\left|\begin{matrix}f_{1}&f_{2}&\cdots&f_{N}\\
\partial_{1}f_{1}&\partial_{1}f_{2}&\cdots&\partial_{1}f_{N}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial_{1}^{N-1}f_{1}&\partial_{1}^{N-1}f_{2}&\cdots&\partial_{1}^{N-1}f_{N}\end{matrix}\right|.

(A.8)

For the time-evolution of the functions
f_{i}
, we consider the following (diffusion) hierarchy,

\displaystyle\partial_{n}f_{i}=\partial_{1}^{n}f_{i}\qquad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq N,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

which gives the solution of the Sato equation (
A.6
). Then the solution of the KP equation
can be expressed in terms of the
\tau
-function by

\displaystyle u(t)=2u_{2}(t)=2\partial_{1}w_{1}(t)=2\partial_{1}^{2}\ln\tau(t).
