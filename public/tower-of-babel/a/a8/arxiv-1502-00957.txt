Abstract
We propose a reliable direct imaging method based on the reverse time migration for finding extended obstacles with phaseless total field data. We prove that the imaging resolution of the method is essentially the same as the imaging results using the scattering data with full phase information. The imaginary part of the cross-correlation imaging functional always peaks on the boundary of the obstacle. Numerical experiments are included to illustrate the powerful imaging quality.



1
Introduction

We consider in this paper inverse scattering problems with phaseless data which aim to find the support of unknown obstacles embedded in a known background medium from the knowledge of the amplitude of the total field measured on a given surface far away from the obstacles. Let the sound soft obstacle occupy a bounded Lipschitz domain
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with
\nu
the unit outer normal to its boundary
\Gamma_{D}
.
Let
u^{i}
be the incident wave and the total field is
u=u^{i}+u^{s}
with
u^{s}
being the solution of the following acoustic scattering problem:

\displaystyle\Delta u^{s}+k^{2}u^{s}=0\ \ \ \ \mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\bar{D},

(1)

\displaystyle u^{s}=-u^{i}\ \ \ \ \ \mbox{on }\Gamma_{D},

(2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{r}\Big(\frac{\partial u^{s}}{\partial r}-\mathbf{i}ku^{s}\Big)\rightarrow 0\ \ \mbox{as }\ \ r=|x|\rightarrow+\infty,

(3)

where
k>0
is the wave number. The condition (
3
) is the outgoing Sommerfeld radiation condition which guarentees the uniqueness of the solution. In this paper, by the radiation or scattering solution we always mean the solution satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition (
3
). For the sake of the simplicity, we mainly consider the imaging of
sound soft obstacles in this paper. Our algorithm does not require any a priori information of the physical properties of the obstacles such as penetrable or non-penetrable, and for non-penetrable obstacles,
the type of boundary conditions on the boundary of the obstacle. The extension of our theoretical results for imaging other types of obstacles will be briefly considered in section 4.

In the diffractive optics imaging and radar imaging systems, it is much easier to measure the intensity of the total field than the phase information of the field
[
9
,
10
,
26
]
. It is thus very desirable to develop reliable numerical methods for reconstructing obstacles with only phaseless data, that is, the amplitude information of the total field
|u|
. In recent years, there have been considerable efforts in the literature to solve the inverse scattering problems with phaseless data. One approach is to image the object with the phaseless data directly in the inversion algorithm, see e.g.
[
10
,
19
]
. The other approach is first to apply the phase retrieval algorithm to extract the phase information of the scattering field from the measurement of the intensity and then use the retrieved full field data in the classical imaging algorithms, see e.g.
[
11
]
. We also refer to
[
1
]
for the continuation method and
[
17
,
13
,
14
]
for inverse scattering
problems with the data of the amplitude of the far field pattern. In
[
15
]
some uniqueness results for phaseless inverse scattering problems have been obtained.

The reverse time migration (RTM) method, which consists of back-propagating the complex conjugated scattering field into the background medium and computing the cross-correlation between the incident wave field and the backpropagated field to output the final imaging profile, is nowadays a standard imaging technique widely used in seismic imaging
[
2
]
. In
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, the RTM method for reconstructing extended targets using acoustic, electromagnetic and elastic waves at a fixed frequency is proposed and studied. The resolution analysis in
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
is achieved without using the small inclusion or geometrical optics assumption previously made in the literature.

In this paper we propose a direct imaging algorithm based on reverse time migration for imaging obstacles
with only intensity measurement
|u|
with point source excitations. We prove that the imaging resolution of the new algorithm is essentially the same as the imaging results using the scattering data with the full phase information, that is, our imaging function always peaks on the boundary of the obstacles. To the
best knowledge of the authors, our method seems to be the first attempt in applying non-iterative method for reconstructing obstacles with phaseless data except
[
9
]
in which a direct method is considered for imaging a penetrable obstacle under Born approximation using plane wave incidences. We will extend the RTM method studied in this paper for electromagnetic probe waves in a future paper.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we introduce our RTM algorithm for imaging the obstacle with phaseless data. In section 3 we consider the resolution of our algorithm for imaging sound soft obstacles. In section 4 we extend our theoretical results to non-penetrable obstacles with the impedance boundary condition and penetrable obstacles.
In section 5 we report several numerical experiments to
show the competitive performance of our phaseless RTM algorithm.

2
Reverse time migration method

In this section we introduce the RTM method for inverse scattering problems with phaseless data.
Assume that there are
N_{s}
emitters and
N_{r}
receivers uniformly distributed respectively on
\Gamma_{s}=\partial B_{s}
and
\Gamma_{r}=\partial B_{r}
, where
B_{s},B_{r}
are the disks of radius
R_{s},R_{r}
respectively. We denote by
\Omega
the sampling domain in which the obstacle is sought. We assume the obstacle
D\subset\Omega
and
\Omega
is inside
B_{s},B_{r}
.

Let
u^{i}(x,x_{s})=\Phi(x,x_{s})
, where
\Phi(x,x_{s})=\frac{\mathbf{i}}{4}H^{(1)}_{0}(k|x-x_{s}|)
is the fundamental solution of
the Helmholtz equation with the source at
x_{s}\in\Gamma_{s}
, be the incident wave and
|u(x_{r},x_{s})|=|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})+u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})|
be the phaseless data received at
x_{r}\in\Gamma_{r}
, where
u^{s}(x,x_{s})
is the solution to the problem (
1
)-(
3
) with
u^{i}(x,x_{s})=\Phi(x,x_{s})
. We additionally assume that
x_{s}\neq x_{r}
for all
s=1,2,...,N_{s},r=1,2,...,N_{r}
, to avoid the singularity of the incident field
u^{i}(x,x_{s})
at
x=x_{r}
. This assumption can be easily satisfied in practical applications. In the following, without loss of generality, we assume
R_{r}=\tau R_{s},\tau\geq 1
.

Our RTM algorithm consists of back-propagating the corrected data:

\Delta(x_{r},x_{s})=\frac{|u(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}-|u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}

(4)

into the domain using the fundamental solution
\Phi(x_{r},z)
and then computing the imaginary part of the cross-correlation between
u^{i}(z,x_{s})
and the back-propagated field.

Algorithm 2.1
.

(RTM for Phaseless data)

Given the data
|u(x_{r},x_{s})|=|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})+u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})|
which is the measurement of the total field at
x_{r}\in\Gamma_{r}
when the point source is emitted at
x_{s}\in\Gamma_{s}
,
s=1,\dots,N_{s}
,
r=1,\dots,N_{r}
.

1^{\circ}
Back-propagation: For
s=1,\dots,N_{s}
, compute the back-propagation field

v_{b}(z,x_{s})=-\frac{2\pi R_{r}}{N_{r}}\sum^{N_{r}}_{r=1}\Phi(x_{r},z)\Delta(x_{r},x_{s}),\ \ \ \ \forall\ z\in\Omega.

(5)

2^{\circ}
Cross-correlation: For
z\in\Omega
, compute

I(z)=-k^{2}\mathrm{Im}\,\left\{\frac{2\pi R_{s}}{N_{s}}\sum^{N_{s}}_{s=1}u^{i}(z,x_{s})v_{b}(z,x_{s})\right\}.

(6)

It is easy to see that

I(z)=-k^{2}\mathrm{Im}\,\left\{\frac{(2\pi)^{2}R_{s}R_{r}}{N_{s}N_{r}}\sum^{N_{s}}_{s=1}\sum^{N_{r}}_{r=1}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\Delta(x_{r},x_{s})\right\},\ \ \forall\ z\in\Omega.

(7)

This is the formula used in our numerical experiments in section 5. By letting
N_{s},N_{r}\to\infty
, we know that (
7
) can be viewed as an approximation of the following continuous integral:

\hat{I}(z)=-k^{2}\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\Delta(x_{r},x_{s})ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s}),\ \ \forall\ z\in\Omega.

(8)

We remark that the above RTM imaging algorithm is the same as the RTM method in
[
3
]
except that the input data now is
\Delta(x_{r},x_{s})
instead of
\overline{u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})}
. Hence, the code of the RTM algorithm for imaging the obstacle with phaseless data requires only one line change from the code
of the RTM method for imaging the obstacle with full phase information.

3
The resolution analysis

In this section we study the resolution of the Algorithm 2.1. We first introduce some notation. For any bounded domain
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
, let

\|u\|_{H^{1}(U)}=(\|{\rm NewA}\phi\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}+d_{U}^{-2}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2})^{1/2}
be the weighted
H^{1}(U)
norm
and

\|v\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}=(d_{U}^{-1}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+|v|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}^{2})^{1/2}
be the weighted
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
norm,
where
d_{U}
is the diameter of
U
and

\displaystyle|v|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}=\left(\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}ds(x)ds(y)\right)^{1/2}.

By scaling argument and trace theorem we know that there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
d_{D}
such that for any
\phi\in C^{1}(\bar{D})
,

\|\phi\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{D})}+\|\partial\phi/\partial\nu\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{D})}\leq C\max_{x\in\bar{D}}(|\phi(x)|+d_{D}|{\rm NewA}\phi(x)|).

(9)

The following stability estimate of the forward acoustic scattering problem is well-known
[
8
,
20
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
g\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{D})
, then the scattering problem:

\displaystyle\Delta w+k^{2}w=0\qquad\mbox{\rm in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\bar{D},\ \ \ \ w=g\ \ \ \ \mbox{\rm on }\Gamma_{D},

(10)

\displaystyle\sqrt{r}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}-\mathbf{i}kw\right)\to 0,\ \ \ \ \mbox{as }r\to\infty,

(11)

admits a unique solution
w\in H^{1}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\bar{D})
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\|\partial w/\partial\nu\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{D})}\leq C\|g\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{D})}
.

The far field pattern
w^{\infty}(\hat{x})
, where
\hat{x}=x/|x|\in S^{1}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|x|=1\}
, of the solution of the scattering problem (
10
)-(
11
) is defined as (cf. e.g.
[
8
, P. 67]
):

w^{\infty}(\hat{x})=\frac{e^{\mathbf{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{8\pi k}}\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\left[w(y)\frac{\partial e^{-\mathbf{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial w(y)}{\partial\nu(y)}e^{-\mathbf{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\right]ds(y).

(12)

It is well-known that for the scattering solution of (
10
)-(
11
) (cf. e.g.
[
3
, Lemma 3.3]
)

-\,\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{D}}w\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial\nu}ds=k\int_{S^{1}}|w^{\infty}(\hat{x})|^{2}d\hat{x}.

(13)

Now we turn to the analysis of the imaging function
\hat{I}(z)
in (
8
). We first observe that

\Delta(x_{r},x_{s})=\overline{u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})}+\frac{|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}+\frac{u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})\overline{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}.

(14)

This yields

\displaystyle\hat{I}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k^{2}\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\overline{u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})

(15)

\displaystyle-k^{2}\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\frac{|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})

\displaystyle-k^{2}\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\frac{u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})\overline{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s}).

The first term is the RTM imaging function with full phase information in
[
3
]
and thus can be analyzed by the argument there. Our goal now is to show the last two terms at the right hand side of (
15
) are small. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have
|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})|\leq C(1+kd_{D})^{2}(kR_{r})^{-1/2}(kR_{s})^{-1/2}
for any
x_{r}\in\Gamma_{r},x_{s}\in\Gamma_{s}
.

Proof.

We first recall the following estimates for Hankel functions
[
6
, (1.22)-(1.23)]
:

|H^{(1)}_{0}(t)|\leq\left(\frac{2}{\pi t}\right)^{1/2},\ \ |H^{(1)}_{1}(t)|\leq\left(\frac{2}{\pi t}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{2}{\pi t},\ \ \ \ \forall t>0.

(16)

By the integral representation formula, we have

u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})=\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\left(u^{s}(y,x_{s})\frac{\partial\Phi(x_{r},y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial u^{s}(y,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(y)}\Phi(x_{r},y)\right)ds(y).

(17)

By (
9
) we have

\displaystyle\|\Phi(x_{r},\cdot)\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{D})}+\|\partial\Phi(x_{r},\cdot)/\partial\nu\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{D})}\leq C(1+kd_{D})(kR_{r})^{-1/2}.

The lemma follows now from Lemma
3.1
and the fact that
u^{i}(y,x_{s})=-\Phi(y,x_{s})
for
y\in\Gamma_{D}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
|H^{(1)}_{0}(t)|\geq[2/(5\pi e)]|\ln t|
for any
t\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

We use the following integral formula
[
22
,
6
]

\displaystyle H^{(1)}_{0}(t)=-\frac{2\mathbf{i}}{\pi}e^{\mathbf{i}t}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{e^{-rt}}{r^{1/2}(r-2\mathbf{i})^{1/2}}dr,\ \ \ \ \forall t>0,

where
\mathrm{Re}\,(r-2\mathbf{i})^{1/2}>0
for
r>0
. By the change of variable

\displaystyle|H^{(1)}_{0}(t)|\geq\frac{2}{\pi}\mathrm{Re}\,\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{e^{-s}}{s^{1/2}(s-2\mathbf{i}t)^{1/2}}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{e^{-s}}{s^{1/2}|s-2\mathbf{i}t|}\sqrt{\frac{|s-2\mathbf{i}t|+s}{2}}ds

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{e^{-s}}{|s-2\mathbf{i}t|}ds

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{5\pi}\int^{1}_{t}\frac{e^{-s}}{s}ds,

where in the last inequality we have used
|s-2\mathbf{i}t|\leq 5s
for
s\geq t
.
This completes the proof by noticing that
\int^{1}_{t}s^{-1}e^{-s}ds\geq e^{-1}\int^{1}_{t}s^{-1}ds=e^{-1}|\ln t|
.
∎

The following lemma gives an estimate of the second term at the right hand side of (
15
).

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

\displaystyle\left|k^{2}\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\frac{|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})\right|\leq C(1+kd_{D})^{4}(kR_{s})^{-1/2}.

Proof.

Denote
\Omega_{k}=\{(x_{r},x_{s})\in\Gamma_{r}\times\Gamma_{s}:|x_{r}-x_{s}|<1/(2k)\}
.
Let
x_{r}=R_{r}(\cos\theta_{r},\sin\theta_{r})
,
x_{s}=R_{s}(\cos\theta_{s},\sin\theta_{s})
, where
\theta_{r},\theta_{s}\in[0,2\pi]
. Since
R_{r}=\tau R_{s}
,
\tau\geq 1
, we obtain easily

|x_{r}-x_{s}|=R_{s}\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}-2\tau\cos|\theta_{r}-\theta_{s}|}\geq 2R_{s}\sqrt{\tau}\sin\frac{|\theta_{r}-\theta_{s}|}{2}

(18)

by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Now for
(x_{r},x_{s})\in\Omega_{k}
, we have then either
\frac{|\theta_{r}-\theta_{s}|}{2}\leq\theta_{0}
or
\pi-\theta_{0}\leq\frac{|\theta_{r}-\theta_{s}|}{2}\leq\pi
, where
\theta_{0}=\arcsin\frac{1}{4kR_{s}\sqrt{\tau}}\in(0,\pi/2)
.

By (
16
) and Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\left|\int\hskip-5.0pt\int_{\Omega_{k}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\frac{|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})\right|

(19)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+kd_{D})^{4}(kR_{s})^{-3/2}(kR_{r})^{-3/2}\int\hskip-5.0pt\int_{\Omega_{k}}|\ln(k|x_{r}-x_{s}|)|ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s}).

By (
18
) we have

\displaystyle\int\hskip-5.0pt\int_{\Omega_{k}}|\ln(k|x_{r}-x_{s}|)|ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\int\hskip-5.0pt\int_{\Omega_{k}}\ln\left(2kR_{s}\sqrt{\tau}\sin\frac{|\theta_{r}-\theta_{s}|}{2}\right)d\theta_{r}d\theta_{s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\pi R_{r}R_{s}\left|\int^{\theta_{0}}_{0}\ln(2kR_{s}\sqrt{\tau}\sin t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CR_{r}R_{s},

where we have used integration by parts in obtaining the last inequality. Substituting the above estimate into
(
19
) we obtain

\left|\int\hskip-5.0pt\int_{\Omega_{k}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\frac{|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})\right|\leq Ck^{-2}(1+kd_{D})^{4}(kR_{s})^{-1}.

(20)

Next we estimate the integral in
\Gamma_{r}\times\Gamma_{s}\backslash\bar{\Omega}_{k}
. Since
t|H_{0}^{(1)}(t)|^{2}
is an increasing function of
t>0

[
24
, p. 446]
, we have
for
(x_{r},x_{s})\in\Gamma_{r}\times\Gamma_{s}\backslash\bar{\Omega}_{k}
,
|x_{r}-x_{s}|\geq 1/(2k)
, and thus

\displaystyle|x_{r}-x_{s}||u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{32}k^{-1}\left|H_{0}^{(1)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\right|^{2}=Ck^{-1},

which implies by using Lemma
3.2
and (
16
) again that

\left|\int\hskip-5.0pt\int_{\Gamma_{r}\times\Gamma_{s}\backslash\bar{\Omega}_{k}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\frac{|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})|^{2}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})\right|\leq Ck^{-2}(1+kd_{D})^{4}(kR_{s})^{-1/2}.

(21)

This completes the proof by combining the above estimate with (
20
).
∎

\displaystyle\left|k^{2}\int\hskip-5.0pt\int_{Q_{\delta}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})\frac{\overline{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}}{u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})\right|\leq C(1+kd_{D})^{2}(kR_{s})^{-1/2}.

The following lemma is essentially proved in

We also need the following slight generalization of Van der Corput lemma for the oscillatory integral
[
12
, P.152]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For any
-\infty<a<b<\infty
, for every real-valued
C^{2}
function
u
that satisfies
|u^{\prime}(t)|\geq 1
for
t\in(a,b)
. Assume that
a=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}=b
is a division of
(a,b)
such that
u^{\prime}
is monotone in each
interval
(x_{i-1},x_{i})
,
i=1,\cdots,N
. Then for any function
\phi
defined on
(a,b)
with integrable derivative, and for any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\left|\int^{b}_{a}e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(t)}\phi(t)dt\right|\leq(2N+2)\lambda^{-1}\left[|\phi(b)|+\int^{b}_{a}|\phi^{\prime}(t)|dt\right].

Proof.

By integration by parts we have

\displaystyle\int^{b}_{a}e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(t)}dt=\left[\frac{e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(t)}}{\mathbf{i}\lambda u^{\prime}(t)}\right]^{b}_{a}-\int^{b}_{a}e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(t)}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf{i}\lambda u^{\prime}(t)}\right)dt.

Since
u^{\prime}
is monotone in each interval
(x_{i-1},x_{i})
,
i=1,\cdots,N
, and
|u^{\prime}(t)|\geq 1
in
(a,b)
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int^{b}_{a}e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(t)}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf{i}\lambda u^{\prime}(t)}\right)dt\right|\leq\sum^{N}_{i=1}\lambda^{-1}\left|\int^{x_{i}}_{x_{i-1}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{u^{\prime}(t)}\right)dt\right|\leq 2N\lambda^{-1},

which implies
|\int^{b}_{a}e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(t)}dt|\leq(2N+2)\lambda^{-1}
. For the general case, we denote
F(t)=\int_{a}^{t}e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(s)}ds
and use integration by parts to obtain

\displaystyle\int^{b}_{a}\phi(t)e^{\mathbf{i}\lambda u(t)}dt=\phi(b)F(b)-\int^{b}_{a}F(t)\phi^{\prime}(t)dt.

This completes the proof by using
|F(t)|\leq(2N+2)\lambda^{-1}
. ∎

The following theorem is the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
z\in\Omega
, let
\psi(x,z)
be the scattering solution to the problem:

\Delta\psi(x,z)+k^{2}\psi(x,z)=0\ \ \ \ \mbox{\rm in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\bar{D},\ \ \ \ \psi(x,z)=-\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(x,z)\ \ \mbox{\rm on }\Gamma_{D}.

(32)

Then if the measured field
|u(x_{r},x_{s})|=|u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})+u^{i}(x_{r},x_{s})|
with
u^{s}(x,x_{s})
satisfying the problem (
1
)-(
3
) with the incident field
u^{i}(x,x_{s})=\Phi(x,x_{s})
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{I}(z)=k\int_{S^{1}}|\psi^{\infty}(\hat{x},z)|^{2}d\hat{x}+w_{\hat{I}}(z),\ \ \ \ \forall z\in\Omega,

where
|w_{\hat{I}}(z)|\leq C(1+kd_{D})^{4}(kR_{s})^{-1/2}+C(1+kd_{D})^{2}(1+k|z|)^{2}(kR_{s})^{-1}
.

Proof.

By (
15
), Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.10
we are left to show that

\displaystyle-k^{2}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(z,x_{s})\Phi(x_{r},z)\overline{u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})ds(x_{s})=k\int_{S^{1}}|\psi^{\infty}(\hat{x},z)|^{2}d\hat{x}+\zeta(z),

with
|\zeta(z)|\leq C(1+kd_{D})^{2}(1+kd_{D}+k|z|)^{2}(kR_{s})^{-1}
. This can be done by a similar argument as that in
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
. For the
sake of completeness, we include a sketch of the proof here.

By the corollary of Helmholtz-Kirchhoff identity in
[
3
, Lemma 3.2]
, for any
z,y\in\Omega
,

k\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(x_{r},z)\overline{\Phi(x_{r},y)}ds(x_{r})=\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)+w_{r}(z,y),

(33)

where
|w_{r}(z,y)|+k^{-1}|w_{r}(z,y)|\leq C(1+k|y|+k|z|)^{2}(kR_{r})^{-1}
. Now by the integral representation formula

\displaystyle u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})=\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\left(u^{s}(y,x_{s})\frac{\partial\Phi(x_{r},y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial u^{s}(y,x_{s})}{\partial\nu(y)}\Phi(x_{r},y)\right)ds(y),

we have

\displaystyle k\int_{\Gamma_{r}}\Phi(x_{r},z)\overline{u^{s}(x_{r},x_{s})}ds(x_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\Big[\overline{u^{s}(y,x_{s})}\frac{\partial\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\overline{u^{s}(y,x_{s})}}{\partial\nu(y)}\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)\Big]ds(y)+\zeta_{1}(z,x_{s}),

where by (
9
)

\displaystyle|\zeta_{1}(z,x_{s})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\Big[\overline{u^{s}(y,x_{s})}\frac{\partial w_{r}(z,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial\overline{u^{s}(y,x_{s})}}{\partial\nu(y)}w_{r}(z,y)\Big]ds(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+kd_{D})^{2}(1+kd_{D}+k|z|)^{2}(kR_{s})^{-3/2}.

By the definition of the imaging function
\hat{I}(z)
, we have then

\hat{I}(z)=-\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\Big[v_{s}(y,z)\frac{\partial\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\partial v_{s}(y,z)}{\partial\nu(y)}\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)\Big]ds(y)+\zeta_{2}(z),

(34)

where
v_{s}(y,z)=k\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\Phi(x_{s},z)\overline{u^{s}(y,x_{s})}ds(x_{s})
and

\displaystyle|\zeta_{2}(z)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\left|\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\Phi(x_{s},z)\zeta_{1}(z,x_{s})ds(x_{s})\right|

(35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+kd_{D})^{2}(1+kd_{D}+k|z|)^{2}(kR_{s})^{-1}.

Taking the complex conjugate we get

\displaystyle\overline{v_{s}(y,z)}=k\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\overline{\Phi(x_{s},z)}u^{s}(y,x_{s})ds(x_{s}).

Therefore,
\overline{v_{s}(y,z)}
can be viewed as the weighted superposition of
u^{s}(y,x_{s})
. Then
\overline{v_{s}(y,z)}
satisfies the Helmholtz equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}\overline{v_{s}(y,z)}+k^{2}\overline{v_{s}(y,z)}=0\ \ \ \ \mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\bar{D}.

On the boundary of the obstacle
\Gamma_{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{v_{s}(y,z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\overline{\Phi(x_{s},z)}u^{s}(y,x_{s})ds(x_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\overline{\Phi(x_{s},z)}\Phi(y,x_{s})ds(x_{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)-w_{s}(z,y),\ \ \ \ \forall y\in\Gamma_{D},

where, similar to (
33
),
|w_{s}(z,y)|+k^{-1}|w_{s}(z,y)|\leq C(1+k|y|+k|z|)^{2}(kR_{s})^{-1}
.
By the definition of
\psi
we have

\displaystyle\hat{I}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\Big[\overline{\psi(y,z)}\frac{\partial\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\overline{\partial\psi(y,z)}}{\partial\nu(y)}\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)\Big]ds(y)+\zeta_{3}(z),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\frac{\overline{\partial\psi(y,z)}}{\partial\nu(y)}\psi(y,z)ds(y)+\zeta_{3}(z).

where we have used the boundary condition of
\psi
on
\Gamma_{D}
in the second inequality and

\displaystyle\zeta_{3}(z)=\zeta_{2}(z)+\mathrm{Im}\,\int_{\Gamma_{D}}\Big[\overline{\phi(y,z)}\frac{\partial\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)}{\partial\nu(y)}-\frac{\overline{\partial\phi(y,z)}}{\partial\nu(y)}\mathrm{Im}\,\Phi(z,y)\Big]ds(y).

Here
\phi(y,z)
is the solution of scattering problem (
10
)-(
11
) with the boundary condition
g=w_{s}(z,y)
. Now by (
9
) and (
35
) we then obtain

\displaystyle|\zeta_{3}(z)|\leq C(1+kd_{D})^{2}(1+kd_{D}+k|z|)^{2}(kR_{s})^{-1}.

Now the theorem is proved by using (
13
).
∎

We remark that
\psi(x,z)
is the scattering solution of the Helmholtz equation with the incoming field

J_{0}(k|x-z|)
.
It is well-known that
J_{0}(t)
peaks at
t=0
and decays like
t^{-1/2}
away from the origin. The source of the problem (
32
) will peak at the boundary of the scatterer
D
and becomes small when
z
moves away from
\partial D
. Thus we expect that the imaging function
\hat{I}(z)
will have a large contrast at the boundary of the scatterer
D
and decay outside the boundary
\partial D
. This is indeed observed in our numerical experiments.
