Introduction

Let
n\geq 2
be a natural number, and let

among integers. We treat

as fixed throughout this work, and all the constants may depend on it.

By strong approximation, the modulo
q

\pi_{q}\colon\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
is onto. We study this map as follows. Let
\left\|\cdot\right\|\colon\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the operator norm. Given

F_{T}:=\left\{\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}):\left\|\gamma\right\|\leq T\right\}

is finite. Therefore, restricting the map
\pi_{q}
to

gives us a finite problem of throwing balls into bins.
In this work, we consider the problem of when the balls fill all of the bins, but first, let us remark on the related problem of filling
almost all
of the bins.

By the pigeon-hole principle, filling almost all of the bins can only happen if
|F_{T}|\geq(1+o(1))|\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})|
. To make this explicit, we notice that

|\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})|=q^{n^{2}-1+o(1)},

and Duke-Rudnick-Sarnak (
[
5

) proved that for some constant
C_{n}
,

(1.1)

|F_{T}|=C_{n}T^{n^{2}-n}(1+o(1)).

This implies that to fill almost all of the bins, or alternatively, to lift almost all the elements of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
to
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, one needs at least

.

Recently, it was proven that this order of magnitude for

1.1.
Proof outlines

The main contribution of the paper is
Theorem

1.2
.
We exhibit a suitable element of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
such that any lift of it to

\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z})
must already require entries of size
q^{2+o(1)}
.
This is based on the fact that given
x\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
, the entries of its lifts to

\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z})
must satisfy a(n inhomogeneous) linear equation
\mod q^{2}

given by the tangent space to
x
in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
.
We will choose
x
to be a suitable diagonal matrix so that the resulting linear
equation has small coefficients but a large inhomogeneous term, which forces at least
one entry of any lift of
x
to be large.
Using these ideas, we can prove the following result.

Proposition 1.4
.

Assume that for
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, there is
\beta\in(\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
with
|n\beta|\geq K^{-1}q^{2}

and
|\beta^{n}|\leq K
for some
K\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Then there is a diagonal
x\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
such that every lift
\gamma
of it to
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
satisfies
\|\gamma\|\geq q^{2}/nK^{2}
.

The absolute value in the proposition is the absolute value on
\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z}
defined by
|a|=\min\{|a^{\prime}|:a^{\prime}\in a+q^{2}\mathbb{Z}\}
.

The construction in
1.4
depends on finding a suitable

\beta\in(\mathbb{Z}/q^{2}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
that satisfies the hypotheses in the proposition.
Its existence is guaranteed by the following result.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every
k>0,n>0
, there is a constant
C
such that the following holds.
For every
q>0
, there are
\alpha,\beta\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
with

|\alpha|<C
,
\beta^{n}=\alpha
and
|n\beta|>C^{-1}q^{1-1/k}
.

Notice that
1.4
and
Theorem

1.5
together imply
Theorem

1.2
.
(We need to apply
Theorem

1.5
with
q^{2}
in the role of
q
.)

The proof of
Theorem

1.5
uses ideas from additive combinatorics, in particular
Bohr Sets
.
We give some more details.
In the first part of the proof, we find a suitable collection of elements

\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}
such that
|\beta_{j}^{n}|<C
for all
j
.
Then we aim to show that
|n\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\beta_{m}^{a_{m}}|
is large for some choice
of

.
In that case,
\beta=\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\beta_{m}^{a_{m}}
can be taken in the theorem.

With this in mind, we suppose to the contrary that the Bohr set

\displaystyle B(\{\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}

\displaystyle\cdots\beta_{m}^{a_{m}}\},q^{-1/k})

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}:|x\cdot\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\beta_{m}^{a_{m}}|<q^{1-1/k}\text{ for all choices of $a_{j}$}\}

contains
n
, and hence crucially it is non-trivial.
Then we show that this Bohr set can be approximated by a generalized arithmetic
progression of rank
r\leq k
.
The fact that the rank

can be bounded independently of

is a non-standard
feature that we have thanks to our choice of the radius, and this is of critical
importance for the success of our argument.

We observe that the set of frequencies
\{\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\beta_{m}^{a_{m}}\}

is “almost invariant” under multiplication by each
\beta_{j}
.
We use this to show that multiplication by
\beta_{j}
induces a linear “almost action” on the
generalized arithmetic progression approximating the Bohr set,
and we show that for each
\beta_{j}
, there corresponds some matrix
B_{j}\in M_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
.
We will see that the
B_{j}
commute and their

This will lead to a contradiction provided

\beta_{j}
are genuinely different in a suitable sense.

For the proof of

(1)

Finding a lift of the first

The purpose of this section is to prove

It follows immediately from the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1

Let

\alpha a_{1}+a_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}\equiv n\beta\mod q^{2},

where

In particular,

\left\|\gamma\right\|\geq\left|n\beta\right|/n|\alpha|.

This can be deduced from the fact that the preimage of

is an arbitrary lift.
However, we give an elementary proof for the reader’s convenience.

First, we record a trivial fact in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2

If

\max\{\left|a_{1}\right|,\ldots,|a_{n}|\}\geq\frac{T}{n\max\{|\alpha_{1}|,\ldots,\left|\alpha_{n}\right|\}}.

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\equiv(\beta\alpha^{-1}+qb_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{n}(\beta+qb_{i})\mod q^{2}

implies

\left\|\gamma\right\|\geq\max\{|a_{1}|,|a_{2}|\}\geq 8m^{2}\geq q^{2}/8.

One can also generalize this case to
n\geq 3
, by considering
q
of the form
q=n^{3}m
, but we omit the details.

3.
Finding Large n-th Roots

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.5
. Throughout this section,
n
and
k
are fixed, and all the constants may depend on them.
The proof splits into 2 cases.
In the main case, we need a sufficient supply of small primes
p
not dividing
q
, and also want sufficiently many
n
-th powers in
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
.
Both conditions are problematic if
q
is divisible by many primes. Luckily, when the conditions fail, there is a different method, which we discuss first.

Let
P
be the set of primes with
\gcd(p-1,n)>1
and
p\nmid n
.
(For example, if
n
is even,
P
is the set of all odd primes not dividing
n
).
The set
P
is the set of primes not dividing
n
, and such that there is a non-trivial
n
-th root of unity in
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
for every
m>0
. We consider
-1
to be non-trivial here.

We say that
q
has a
small
P
factor
if in the decomposition into primes
q=p_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{l}^{m_{l}}
, there is
p_{j}\in P
with
p_{j}^{m_{j}}<q^{1/k}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
q
has a small
P
factor. Then there is
\beta\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

such that
\beta^{n}=1
and
\left|n\beta\right|>q^{1-1/k}-n
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
p_{1}^{m_{1}}<q^{1/k}
and

p_{1}\in P
. Denote
q^{\prime}=q/p_{1}^{m_{1}}
and notice that
q^{\prime}\geq q^{1-1/k}
.

Let
1\neq a\in\mathbb{Z}/p_{1}^{m_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
be such that
a^{n}\equiv 1\mod p_{1}^{m_{1}}
.
Using the Chinese remainder theorem, choose
\beta\in\left(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}

such that

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\equiv 1\mod q^{\prime}

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\equiv a\mod p_{1}^{m_{1}}

Then
\beta^{n}\equiv 1\mod q
, and we can write
n\beta=n+a^{\prime}q^{\prime}

for some
|a^{\prime}|\leq p_{1}^{m_{1}}/2
.
The important fact is that
a^{\prime}\neq 0
, because
n\beta\not\equiv n\mod p^{m_{1}}
.
Then
|n\beta|\geq\left|n+a^{\prime}q^{\prime}\right|\geq q^{\prime}-n\geq q^{1-1/k}-n
.
∎

3.1
handles the case when
q
has a small
P
factor.
From now on we assume that
q
has no small
P
factors.
We begin with an outline of the argument.
The first step is to find a supply of small
n
-th powers in
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
,
which is the purpose of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
m
, there is
C>0
such that the following holds.
Let
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>C}
be without small P factors.
Then there
are
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
such that each

\alpha_{i}
has an
n
-th root
\beta_{i}\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, (i.e.,
\beta_{i}^{n}=\alpha_{i}
),

|\alpha_{i}|<C
and when lifted to elements of
\mathbb{Z}
,
(\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{j})^{1/n}\notin\mathbb{Q}
for
i\neq j
.

We defer the proof to
Section

3.1
and continue our outline.
Our strategy is to show that the set

(3.1)

\{\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\beta_{m}^{a_{m}}:0\leq a_{i}<n\}

contains an element
\beta
such that
|n\beta|
is large, if
m
is chosen sufficiently big in terms of
n
and
k
,
and where
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}
are the elements from the previous lemma.
Clearly, all elements of (
3.1
) satisfy that
\beta^{n}
is small, so we will be done.

To achieve our aim, we need the notion of Bohr sets.
For an arbitrary set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
of frequencies and for
\rho\in[0,1]
,
we write

B(A,\rho)=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}:|xa|\leq\rho q\text{ for all $a\in A$}\},

and call it a Bohr set.

We will consider the Bohr set
B(A,\rho)
with the set (
3.1
) in the role
of
A
and with
\rho=q^{-1/k}
.
A naive heuristic argument suggests that such a Bohr set should be of size
approximately
\rho^{|A|}q
, so
B(A,\rho)
should be trivial, that is equal to

\{0\}
, provided
A

is sufficiently large.
We will show that this is indeed the case for our choice of
A
if
m

is chosen large enough.
This will finish our proof because
n\notin B(A,\rho)
means that there is some

a\in A
with
|na|>\rho q=q^{1-1/k}
.

It is well known that Bohr sets are closely approximated by generalized arithmetic progressions (
[
25
, § 4.22]
).
We will need the following version.

Lemma 3.3

(Bohr set lemma)
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
c>0
, there is a constant
C
such that
the following holds.
Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
be such that
\gcd(A\cup\{q\})=1

and let
\rho<C^{-1}q^{-1/(k+1)}
.
Then there is
C^{-1}\rho<\rho^{\prime}<\rho
,

r\leq k
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
,
l_{1},\ldots,l_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

such that

(1)

|u_{i}a|\leq c\rho^{\prime}q
for all
a\in A
, i.e.,
u_{i}\in B(A,c\rho^{\prime})
,

(2)

B(A,\rho^{\prime})\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}u_{i}:|n_{i}|\leq l_{i}\right\}\subset B(A,C\rho^{\prime})
,

(3)

if
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq C^{-1}l_{i}/\rho^{\prime}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}u_{i}\in B(A,t\rho^{\prime})

for some
1\leq t\leq{\rho^{\prime}}^{-1}/2
, then
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq Ctl_{i}
, and

(4)

the map
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\to\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}u_{i}
is injective
for
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq C^{-1}l_{i}/\rho^{\prime}
.

The proof of this lemma follows
[
25
]
,
and we defer it to
Section

3.2
,
but we point out an important feature of the above formulation.
Namely, that we can bound
r
, the rank of the generalized arithmetic progression,
independently of the size of the set of frequencies
A
.
This is possible, because of our assumption that the radius
\rho
is very small.
This feature will be important for us because our argument will require us to choose
the set
A
to be sufficiently large in terms of (the upper bound we have for)
r
.

We observe that the set (
3.1
), which we use in the role of
A
,
is “almost invariant” under multiplication by each

\beta_{i}
, and we will show in the next lemma that this induces a dual “almost action”
on the Bohr set.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
D\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, there is
C\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that the following holds.
Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
such that
\gcd(A\cup\{q\})=1
, and let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}

such that
\beta A\subset A\cup\alpha A
and
|\alpha|\leq D
.
Let
\rho\in(0,C^{-1})
,
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}

and
l_{1},\ldots,l_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that

(1)

u_{i}\in B(A,D^{-1}\rho)
,

(2)

B(A,\rho)\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}u_{i}:|n_{i}|\leq l_{i}\right\}\subset B(A,D\rho)
, and

(3)

If
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq D^{-1}l_{i}/\rho
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}u_{i}\in B(A,t\rho)

for some
1\leq t\leq{\rho}^{-1}/2
, then
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq Dtl_{i}
.

Then there is a matrix
B=(B_{i,j})\in M_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
such that

\beta(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\equiv B(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\mod q

and
B_{i,j}\leq Cl_{j}/l_{i}
for all
i
,
j
.
(Recall that the notation
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
is understood as a column vector.)

We postpone the proof of this lemma to
Section

3.3
,
and now we finish the proof of
Theorem

1.5
in a few strokes.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

By
3.1
, we may assume that
q
has no small
P
-factors.

Let
m=n^{k}+1
(the reason for this choice will be explained right at the end of the proof), and let
C_{1}
,

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
be as in
Lemma

3.2
, i.e.,
\left|\alpha_{s}\right|<C_{1}
. Let
\beta_{s}
be some
n
-th root of
\alpha_{s}
.

Consider the set

A=\left\{\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\beta_{m}^{a_{m}}:0\leq a_{s}<n\right\}.

Let
\rho=q^{1/k}
.
We suppose to the contrary that the theorem is false.
As we already discussed, this implies that
|na|\leq\rho q
for all
a\in A
,
and hence
n\in B(A,\rho)
.
In particular, we get
r\geq 1
when we apply
Lemma

3.3

for

.

Choose
c=C_{1}^{-1}
in
Lemma

3.3
, and let
C_{2}
be the constant

C
in that lemma.
Note that
\rho<C_{2}^{-2}q^{-1/(k+1)}
if
q
is sufficiently large.
By
Lemma

3.3
, there is
\rho<\rho^{\prime}<C_{2}\rho
,
0<r\leq k
,

u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
and
l_{1},\ldots,l_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
items (1)–(4) in the lemma hold.
We can now apply
Lemma

3.4
for each pair
\alpha_{s},\beta_{s}
in the
role of
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\rho=\rho^{\prime}
and
D=C_{2}
.
Denoting by
C_{3}
the constant
C
in
Lemma

3.4
, we get
matrices
B^{(s)}
with

\beta_{s}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\equiv B^{(s)}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\mod q

and
B^{(s)}_{ij}\leq C_{3}l_{j}/l_{i}
.

We observe that

\displaystyle B^{(s_{1})}B^{(s_{2})}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})

\displaystyle\equiv\beta_{s_{1}}\beta_{s_{2}}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})

\displaystyle\equiv B^{(s_{2})}B^{(s_{1})}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\mod q.

The

entries of both matrices

and
B^{(s_{2})}B^{(s_{1})}

are bounded by
rC_{3}^{2}l_{j}/l_{i}\leq C_{2}^{-1}l_{j}/\rho^{\prime}
provided

is sufficiently large.
By the injectivity property in item (4) of
Lemma

3.3
,
we get
B^{(s_{1})}B^{(s_{2})}=B^{(s_{2})}B^{(s_{1})}
.
A very similar argument gives
(B^{(s)})^{n}=\alpha_{s}I
.

We finally get
m=n^{k}+1
commuting matrices
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n^{k}+1}\in M_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
,

, such that
B_{s}^{n}=\alpha_{s}I
.
Since the minimal polynomial of
B_{s}
has no double roots,

B_{s}
is diagonalizable, and since all the matrices commute,
there is a matrix
P\in M_{r}(\mathbb{C})
such that all the matrices

are diagonal.
Moreover, the diagonal of

is of the form
\alpha_{s}^{1/n}(\zeta_{n}^{t_{s1}},\ldots,\zeta_{n}^{t_{sr}})
,
for
\alpha_{s}^{1/n}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\zeta_{n}=\exp(2\pi i/n)
and
0\leq t_{sj}\leq n-1
.
By the pigeon-hole principle, there are
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
with
t_{s_{1}j}=t_{s_{2}j}
for all

.
This implies that
B_{s_{1}}
is a scalar multiple of
B_{s_{2}}
, that is,
B_{s_{1}}=\left(\alpha_{s_{1}}/\alpha_{s_{2}}\right)^{1/n}B_{s_{2}}
.
This is a contradiction since those are integer matrices and
\left(\alpha_{s_{1}}/\alpha_{s_{2}}\right)^{1/n}\notin\mathbb{Q}
.
∎

3.1.
Finding small
n
-th powers

In this section, we prove
Lemma

3.2
.
We begin by recording a simple consequence of
q
not having small
P
factors.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
m>0
, there is
C>0
such that if
q
has
no small
P
factors, there are at least
m
different primes
p\in P

such that
p<C
.

Proof.

By definition,
q
having no small
P
factors implies that there are at most

k
primes in
P
that divide
q
.
Therefore, it suffices to find
m+k
primes
p
with
\gcd(p-1,n)>1
.
This follows from Dirichlet’s theorem.
Indeed, there are infinitely many primes
p
with
p\equiv 1\mod n
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.2
.

There is a group homomorphism
f\colon\left(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}\to\left(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}/\left(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times n}
,
where
(\cdot)^{\times n}
denotes the set of invertible

n
-th powers in a commutative ring.
We claim that under the given conditions, the image of
f
is of bounded size
C\leq(2n)^{\omega(n)+k}
, where
\omega(n)
is the number of distinct prime factors of
n
. Indeed, if
q=\prod p_{i}^{m_{i}}
,
then

\left(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}/\left(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times n}\cong\prod\left(\left(\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}/\left(\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times n}\right).

At most
\omega(n)+k
of the
p_{i}
-s are either dividing
n
or
\gcd(p_{i}-1,n)>1
.
Since
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is either cyclic or
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
times
a cyclic group (if
p=2
,
m\geq 3
), for those primes
|(\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/(\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{Z})^{\times n}|\leq 2n
.
For all the other primes, since
\gcd((p-1)p,n)=1
,
|\left(\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}/\left(\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times n}|=1
.
This proves our claim about the size of the image of
f
.

Take
m+C
primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m+C}
as in
Lemma

3.2
. It can be done with each prime bounded by a constant depending on
m
(and also
n
and
k
). Choose for each element
a
in
f(\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{C+m}\})
an element
p_{a}
such that
f(p_{a})=a
. There are at least
m
remaining primes.
For each of the
m
remaining primes
p_{j}
there is
p_{a}
with
f(p_{j})=f(p_{a})
.
But then
\alpha_{j}=p_{j}p_{a}^{n-1}
satisfies
f(\alpha_{j})=1
, and the
\alpha_{j}
are the required elements.
∎

3.2.
Bohr sets

We turn to the proof of
Lemma

3.3
. We will need the following lemma from the geometry of numbers, which is a combination of the theorems of Mahler and John.

Lemma 3.6

(Discrete John’s theorem)
.

For every
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, there is a constant
D>0
such that the following holds.
Let
\Gamma\leq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a lattice of rank
r
, and let

B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be convex and symmetric. Suppose
\dim\mathrm{span}(\Gamma\cap B)=r
.
Then there is a basis
w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}
of
\Gamma
and
l_{1},\ldots,l_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

such that for every
\alpha\geq 1
,

\left\{\sum n_{i}w_{i}:\left|n_{i}\right|\leq\alpha l_{i}\right\}\subset\Gamma\cap\alpha DB,

and

\Gamma\cap\alpha B\subseteq\left\{\sum n_{i}w_{i}:\left|n_{i}\right|\leq\alpha Dl_{i}\right\}.

The proof is essentially the same as in
[
25
, Lemma 3.36]
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

We follow the proof of
[
25
, Lemma 4.22]
.
We define a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{A}
by

\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{A}+\Big\{\left(\frac{xa}{q}\right)_{a\in A}:x\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\Big\}.

We have a map

\Phi\colon\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\to\Gamma/\mathbb{Z}^{A}

given by

\Phi(x)=\left(\frac{xa}{q}\right)_{a\in A}+\mathbb{Z}^{A}.

By definition, the map is onto and the kernel is

\left\{b\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}:ba=0\text{ for all $a\in A$}\right\}=\{0\}

by our condition on
A
.
So
\mathbb{Z}^{A}
is a subgroup of index
q
in
\Gamma
.

Let
K_{\rho}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{A}:\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}<\rho\right\}
. Then
by definition,

B(A,\rho)=\Phi^{-1}(K_{\rho}\cap\Gamma).

We now claim that for
\rho<1/(2(k+1)q^{1/(k+1)})
,

\dim\mathrm{span}(K_{\rho}\cap\Gamma)\leq k
.
Indeed, if we have linearly independent
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}\in K_{\rho}\cap\Gamma
,
then also

\sum m_{i}v_{i}\in K_{1/2}\cap\Gamma

for
|m_{i}|<\rho^{-1}/2(k+1)
, and by linear independence, all the elements are different.
For every
i
, the number of such
m_{i}
is
2\lfloor\rho^{-1}/2(k+1)\rfloor+1\geq\rho^{-1}/2(k+1)
,
so the total number of elements in
K_{1/2}\cap\Gamma
is at least
(\rho^{-1}/2(k+1))^{k+1}>q
.
On the other hand, every two elements
\gamma_{1}\neq\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma\cap K_{1/2}
belong to different cosets of
\Gamma/\mathbb{Z}^{A}
, so the index of
\mathbb{Z}^{A}
in
\Gamma
is more than
q
.
This is a contradiction.

Let
D
be an upper bound for the constant denoted by the same letter in

Lemma

3.6
when it is applied for some
r\leq k
.
Since
\dim\mathrm{span}(K_{\rho^{\prime}}\cap\Gamma)\leq k
, if
C\geq\left(c^{-1}D\right)^{k}
, there is
C^{-1}\rho<\rho^{\prime}\leq\rho
such that
\mathrm{span}(K_{cD^{-1}\rho^{\prime}}\cap\Gamma)=\mathrm{span}(K_{\rho^{\prime}}\cap\Gamma)
.
Denote
V=\mathrm{span}(K_{\rho^{\prime}}\cap\Gamma)
, and then
\Gamma\cap V
is a lattice in
V
.

We apply
Lemma

3.6
with
B=K_{cD^{-1}\rho^{\prime}}
and

\Gamma\cap V
in the role of
\Gamma
.
Then there is a basis
w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}\in\Gamma
such that

(1)

w_{i}\in K_{c\rho^{\prime}}
for all
i
,

(2.i)

each element in
K_{\rho^{\prime}}\cap\Gamma
can be written as
\sum n_{i}w_{i}
with
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq c^{-1}D^{2}l_{i}
,

(2.ii)

\sum n_{i}w_{i}\in K_{D^{2}\rho^{\prime}}\cap\Gamma
for
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq c^{-1}D^{2}l_{i}
,

(3)

if
\sum n_{i}w_{i}\in K_{t\rho^{\prime}}
for some
1\leq t\leq{\rho^{\prime}}^{-1}/2
, then
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq c^{-1}D^{2}tl_{i}
, and

(4)

\sum n_{i}w_{i}\in K_{1/2}\cap\Gamma
for
\left|n_{i}\right|\leq(1/2c){\rho^{\prime}}^{-1}l_{i}
.

Now we take
u_{i}=\Phi^{-1}(w_{i}+\mathbb{Z}^{A})
and replace

by

.
We get the required properties.
To deduce the injectivity property in item (4), we use the fact that

contains at most one element from each coset of
\mathbb{Z}^{A}
.
∎

Proof
Lemma

3.4

We claim that
\beta u_{i}\in B(A,\rho^{\prime})
for all

.
Indeed, if

\beta a\in A
, or
\beta a=\alpha a^{\prime}
for some

Using item (1), in the first case

So
\beta u_{i}\in B(A,\rho)

Using the first inclusion in item (2), we find a matrix

with

\beta(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\equiv B(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\mod q.

To this end, fix some index

We notice that for

Now we use item (3) with

4.
Lifting to
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})

We prove

in this section.

Lemma 4.4

For every

Lemma 4.5

Let

be a natural number and

Proof of
Lemma

4.4

By

4.5
, the number of times that a prime

Now fix some

Given such a prime with

This gives at most

\displaystyle\left\{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:(a+xq,b+yq)_{Pq}\neq 1,\left|x\right|\leq T,\left|y\right|\leq T\right\}

For the general case, we need some lemmata.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
k<n
.
The following three conditions are equivalent:

(1)

There is a matrix in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with first
k
rows
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
.

(2)

There is a rational subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

are a basis of the lattice
\Gamma=V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

(3)

For every prime
p
,

are linearly independent as elements
of the vector space
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n}
over the finite field
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
.

This is standard, but we give the proof for the reader’s convenience.

Proof.

For a proof that the second condition implies the first, see
[
23
, Lecture VIII §1]
.
The converse is trivial.

To prove the equivalence of the first and third conditions, look at the
k\times n
matrix
A=(v_{i,j})
whose rows are
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
.

Notice that both conditions are invariant by the following two sets of elementary operations, which can be seen as elementary row and column operations on the matrix

(1)

For some
1\leq i\neq j\leq k
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
, replace
v_{i}
by
v_{i}+\alpha v_{j}
.

(2)

For all
1\leq i\leq k
, for some
1\leq j\neq l\leq n
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
, replace
v_{i,j}
by
v_{i,j}+\alpha v_{i,l}
.

If the
\gcd
of the entries of
v_{1}
is not
1
, both conditions obviously fail. If the
\gcd
of the entries of
v_{1}
is
1
, we may assume (by using operations of type (2)) that
v_{1}
is equal to
v_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
and (by using operations of type (1)) that
v_{i,1}=0
for
i>1
.
Repeating this procedure, we see that either both conditions fail, or we can
transform the vectors using the above two types of operations such that
v_{i,i}=1

and
v_{i,j}=0
for
i\neq j
for all
i
and
j
.
In this case, both conditions are easily seen to hold.
∎

We will extend

to

The rows of

4.2.
Completing the matrix

We prove
4.2
in this section.

We recall that the covolume
d(\Gamma)
of a lattice
\Gamma\leq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of rank
k
is
defined as the volume of the fundamental domain of
\Gamma
in the subspace
V=\mathrm{span}(\Gamma)
.
We note that if
\Gamma\leq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
then
d(\Gamma)^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof of
4.2
.

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}
be the rows of
B
. Consider the lattice

\Gamma
spanned by
b_{1}\ldots,b_{n-1}
, and let
V=\mathrm{span}(\Gamma)
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be a vector that completes
B
to a matrix in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Then we can write
v=u+w
, with
w\in V
and
u\in V^{\perp}
.
We know that
\|u\|_{2}\|v_{1}\land\ldots\land v_{n-1}\|_{2}=1
, and since
\|v_{1}\land\ldots\land v_{n-1}\|

is the square root of a non-zero integer,
\|u\|_{\infty}\leq\|u\|_{2}\leq 1
.

We can write
w=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}b_{i}
for some
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and if
[\alpha_{i}]

is the closest integer to
\alpha_{i}
it holds that

\|w-\sum[\alpha_{i}]b_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum\frac{1}{2}\|b_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{n}{2}T.

We can complete
B
using the vector
v-\sum[\alpha_{i}]b_{i}
,
and the result follows.
∎

4.3.
Proof of
Lemma

1.6

We can find all elements of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with entries in the first
n-1
rows
bounded by
T
and entries in the last row bounded by
T^{2}

by performing the following steps.

(1)

Choose a sublattice
\Gamma\leq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of rank
n-1
with
d(\Gamma)\leq O(T^{n-1})
, where
d(\Gamma)
is the covolume of the lattice as above.

(2)

Choose a basis of
\Gamma
of
n-1
elements whose norm is bounded by
T
.
These will be the first
n-1
rows of the matrix.

(3)

Choose a vector from a suitable translate of
\Gamma
of norm bounded by
T^{2}
.
This will be the last row of the matrix.
Indeed, observe that any two possible choices for the last row differ by an element
of
\Gamma
.

We estimate now the number of choices in each of the steps above, which will give
an upper bound for the required count.

By
[
22
, Theorem 2]
,

|\{\Gamma\leq\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\operatorname{rank}(\Gamma)=n-1,d(\Gamma)\leq S\}|\leq C_{n}S^{n}

for all
S\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

By
[
22
, Lemma 2]
,

(4.1)

|\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\|\gamma\|\leq T\}|\leq C_{n}\frac{T^{n-1}}{d(\Gamma)}.

Thus, we have at most
O(T^{(n-1)^{2}}/d(\Gamma)^{n-1})
choices to make in the
second step for any given
\Gamma
.

Similarly, using
Equation

4.1
for
T^{2}
in place of
T
, we
see that we have at most
O(T^{2(n-1)}/d(\Gamma))
choices in the third step.

Recall that
d(\Gamma)
is always the square root of an integer, so it is at least
1
.
The number of choices in the above three steps when we pick a lattice
\Gamma
in
the dyadic range
2^{j}\leq d(\Gamma)<2^{j+1}
is

O\Big(2^{jn}\cdot\frac{T^{(n-1)^{2}}}{2^{j(n-1)}}\cdot\frac{T^{2(n-1)}}{2^{j}}\Big)=O(T^{n^{2}-1}).

We sum this up for

, which yields the claim.

5.
Actions on affine space and projective space

In this section, we discuss the natural extension of the problems of this paper to the action on affine space and projective space. Namely, we consider the natural action of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
on

A_{q}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{n}:\gcd(q,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=1\}

(considered as column vectors) and
P_{q}=A_{q}/(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

d(x,y):=\min\{\log\|\gamma\|:\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}),\gamma\cdot x=y\}.

The triangle inequality is satisfied since
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm. Notice that the distance is not symmetric for
n\geq 3
, but is symmetric for
n=2

We start by considering almost-diameter. We recall that
|A_{q}|=q^{n+o(1)}
, and
|P_{q}|=q^{n-1+o(1)}

Equation

1.1

1.1

[
8
]
.

For every
\epsilon>0
, and for

\epsilon
, the following holds.

.

(2)

For every
(x,y)\in P_{q}^{2}

\epsilon|P_{q}|^{2}
, it holds that

.

As far as we know, the only case when this conjecture is known is for

with

prime and

[
13
]
. We refer to
[
3
,
2
,

]
for some progress towards the general conjecture, which seems to be within reach, at least for

and

[
1
]
for optimal lifting for the action on flags.

For
n=2
, stronger results are expected.

Conjecture 5.2
.

Let
n=2
. For every
\epsilon>0
, and for
q
large enough depending on
\epsilon
, the following holds.

(1)

For every
x\in A_{q}
, for all but
\epsilon|A_{q}|
of
y\in A_{q}
, it holds that
d(x,y)\leq(1+\epsilon)\log q
.

(2)

For every
x\in P_{q}
, for all but
\epsilon|P_{q}|
of
y\in P_{q}
it holds that
d(x,y)\leq(1/2+\epsilon)\log q
.

This conjecture follows from Selberg’s eigenvalue conjecture, see
[
7
]
, where this fact is stated somewhat differently. Selberg’s conjecture is still open, but a recent breakthrough of Steiner
[
24
]
showed a corresponding result for (some) projective actions of quaternion algebras. Unconditionally, using the best-known bounds
[
14
]
on Selberg’s conjecture one can deduce a theorem when the right-hand side is multiplied by
32/27
.

We now consider the diameter of the spaces.
We introduce the limiting exponents

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}(A_{q})

\displaystyle:=\limsup_{q\to\infty}\max_{x,y\in A_{q}}\{d(x,y)/\log q\},

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}(A_{q})

\displaystyle:=\liminf_{q\to\infty}\max_{x,y\in A_{q}}\{d(x,y)/\log q\},

\displaystyle\overline{\alpha}(P_{q})

\displaystyle:=\limsup_{q\to\infty}\max_{x,y\in P_{q}}\{d(x,y)/\log q\},

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}(P_{q})

\displaystyle:=\liminf_{q\to\infty}\max_{x,y\in P_{q}}\{d(x,y)/\log q\}.

In what follows, we make some observations, which allow us to determine one of the four
quantities precisely, and give some estimates for the other three.
The next statement summarizes our results.

Proposition 5.3
.

We have

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq\underline{\alpha}(A_{q})\leq 1+\frac{1}{n},

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\alpha}(A_{q})\leq 1+\frac{1}{n-1},

\displaystyle\frac{n-1}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\underline{\alpha}(P_{q})\leq\overline{\alpha}(P_{q})=1.

In the
n=2
case,
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is symemtric.
Moreover,
5.2
, if true, implies that any point
x\in P_{q}

is of distance at most
(1/2+o(1))\log q
from
99\%
of the points in the space.
However, the above result gives
\overline{\alpha}(P_{q})=1
, which means that there
are pairs of points of distance
(1+o(1))\log q
, at least
for some values of
q
, which is the maximum allowed by

5.2
up to a factor of size
1+o(1)
.

We start with the lower bounds.

Proof of the lower bounds in
5.3
.

Consider
e=(1,0,\ldots,0)\in A_{q}
(recall that we always consider the vectors as column vectors, with the usual action). Then finding
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\gamma\cdot e\equiv x\equiv(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\mod q
is the same as finding
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with first column
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\mod q
. In particular, there is always
x
such that
d(e,x)\geq\log q-O(1)
, so

\underline{\alpha}(A_{q})\geq 1
.

The argument for
P_{q}
is similar, but we have to consider elements up to multiplication by
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. By a pigeon-hole argument, there is always
x\in P_{q}
such that
d(e,x)\geq\frac{n-1}{n}\log q-O(1)
.
This gives
\underline{\alpha}(P_{q})\geq\frac{n-1}{n}
.

For
q
of the special form
q=2q^{\prime}
,
we can consider the element
x=(1,q/2,0,\ldots,0)
.
Notice that multiplication by any element of
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

will leave the second coordinate unchanged, so
d(e,x)\geq\log q+O(1)
, and we
have

\overline{\alpha}(P_{q})\geq 1
.
∎

Our next objective is the upper bound for
P_{q}
.
We borrow ideas from Sarnak’s proof of the
n=2
case of
Theorem

1.3

in
[
21
]
.
We need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

For every
x\in P_{q}
there is
x^{\prime}\in P_{q}
with first coordinate
1
such that

\max\{d(x,x^{\prime}),d(x^{\prime},x)\}\leq 2\log\log_{q}+O(1).

Proof.

Let
x=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. We will choose
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with first row
(1,\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and the other rows agree with the identity matrix, so
x^{\prime}=\gamma x=(a_{1}+\sum\alpha_{i}a_{i},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})
.

The goal is to choose
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
so that
a_{1}^{\prime}=a_{1}+\sum\alpha_{i}a_{i}
will be coprime to
q
, since then
x^{\prime}
is equivalent to an element with first coordinate
1
. Denote
d_{2}=\gcd(q,a_{1},a_{2})
and for
i=3,\ldots,n
,
d_{i}=\gcd(d_{i-1},a_{i})
. Iwaniec’s bound
[
11
]
on the Jacobsthal function implies that the arithmetic progression
a_{1}+\alpha a_{2}
,
\alpha=0,1,\ldots
contains an element with
\gcd(q,a_{1}+\alpha a_{2})=d_{2}
and
\alpha=O((\log q)^{2})
, see
[
21
, Page 8]
for more details. We pick
\alpha_{2}
to satisfy these properties. Then iteratively for
j=3,\ldots,n
, we find
\alpha_{j}=O((\log q)^{2})
such that
\gcd(q,a_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\alpha_{i}a_{i})=d_{j}
. For
j=n
, this implies that
\gcd(q,a_{1}+\sum\alpha_{i}a_{i})=d_{n}=1
. Then

\max\{\log\|\gamma\|,\log\|\gamma^{-1}\|\}\leq 2\log\log(q)+O(1),

and the claim follows.
∎

Proof of
\overline{\alpha}(P_{q})\leq 1
.

Let
x,y\in P_{q}
.
We use the lemma to find
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in P_{q}
with first coordinates equal to
1
and

\max\{d(x,x^{\prime}),d(y^{\prime},y)\}\leq 2\log\log_{q}+O(1).

Notice that there is a unipotent matrix
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with coordinates bounded by
q
such that
\gamma\cdot x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
. Then

d(x,y)\leq d(x,x^{\prime})+d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+d(y^{\prime},y)\leq\log q+4\log\log q+O(1),

which proves
\overline{\alpha}(P_{q})\leq 1
.
Recall that the triangle inequality is always satisfied by
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)

on
P_{q}
even when it is not a metric.
∎

It remains to prove the upper bounds on
\underline{\alpha}(A_{q})
and

\overline{\alpha}(A_{q})
.
These follow immediately from the following two lemmata.

We recall that
d(\Gamma)
denotes the covolume of a lattice
\Gamma\leq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of rank
k
. By Minkowski’s theorem (
[
23
, Lecture II, §1]
), there is a constant
C_{k}
such that there is some
v\in\Gamma\backslash\{0\}
with

(5.1)

\|v\|\leq(C_{k}d(\Gamma))^{1/k}.

Given
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we can define the perpendicular lattice by

\Gamma^{\perp}=\{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\langle\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=0\text{ for all $\gamma\in\Gamma$}\}.

By
[
22
, Corollary of Lemma 1]
,

(5.2)

d(\Gamma)=d(\Gamma^{\perp})

if
\Gamma
is primitive.

Lemma 5.5
.

For every
x\in A_{q}
, there is
x^{\prime}\in A_{q}
with first coordinate
0
such that
d(x,x^{\prime})\leq(1/(n-1))\log q
.

Moreover, if
q
is prime, then we can improve the bound to
(1/n)\log q
.

Proof.

Let
x=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, and consider its coordinates as integers bounded by
q
. Define the lattice

\Gamma=\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\sum a_{i}b_{i}=0\}.

This is a primitive lattice of rank
n-1
, and by
Equation

5.2
,

d(\Gamma)\leq\|(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\|=O(q).

(Note that
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
may be imprimitive.)
By
Equation

5.1
, there is
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\Gamma
with
|b_{i}|=O(q^{1/(n-1)})
. One has a matrix
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with first row
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
, and one can actually find such a matrix with norm bounded by
O(\max_{i}\{|b_{i}|\})=O(q)
. We leave this fact to the reader. (One way to prove this is to extend the vector
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
to a vector space basis using some short vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and then apply the procedure in
[
23
, Lecture V §2]
. See also
[
23
, Lecture X §4]
.)This implies that
x^{\prime}=\gamma\cdot x
is the required element, with
d(x,x^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{n-1}\log q+O(1)
.

If
q
is prime, we can look at the lattice

\Gamma=\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\sum a_{i}b_{i}=0\mod q\}.

This is a lattice of full rank and index
q
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, so
d(\Gamma)=q
, and by
Equation

5.1
, there is
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\Gamma
with
|b_{i}|=O(q^{1/n})
. Denote
d=\gcd(b_{1},..,b_{n})
. If
q
is large enough,
d<q
, and since
q
is prime,
d\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. This implies that
\frac{1}{d}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\Gamma
. Since the
\gcd
of all elements of the last vector is
1
, we can continue as before, finding
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with norm bounded by
O(q^{\frac{1}{n}})
and first row
\frac{1}{d}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
. Then
x^{\prime}=\gamma\cdot x
has first coordinate
0
and
d(x,x^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{n}\log q+O(1)
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

If
x^{\prime}\in A_{q}
has first coordinate 0 and
y\in A_{q}
is arbitrary, then
d(x^{\prime},y)\leq\log q+\log\log q+O(1)
.

Proof.

There is a matrix
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
such that
Ax^{\prime}=y
. By
4.1
and
4.2
, there is a matrix
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with norm bounded by
O(q\log q)
such that
\gamma\mod q
agrees with
A
on the last
n-1
columns. Since the first coordinate of
x^{\prime}
is
0
, this implies that
\gamma x^{\prime}=y
. The claim follows.
∎
