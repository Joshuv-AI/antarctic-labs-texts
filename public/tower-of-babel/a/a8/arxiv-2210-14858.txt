Variational Matrix Product State Approach for Non-Hermitian System Based on a Companion Hermitian Hamiltonian
Zhen Guo, Zheng-Tao Xu, Meng Li, Li You, Shuo Yang

Abstract
Non-Hermitian systems exhibiting topological properties are attracting growing interest. In this work, we propose an algorithm for solving the ground state of a non-Hermitian system in the matrix product state (MPS) formalism based on a companion Hermitian Hamiltonian. If the eigenvalues of the non-Hermitian system are known, the companion Hermitian Hamiltonian can be directly constructed and solved using Hermitian variational methods. When the eigenvalues are unknown, a gradient descent along with the companion Hermitian Hamiltonian yields both the ground state eigenenergy and the eigenstate. With the variational principle as a solid foundation, our algorithm ensures convergence and provides results in excellent agreement with the exact solutions of the non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (nH-SSH) model as well as its interacting extension. The approach we present avoids solving any non-Hermitian matrix and overcomes numerical instabilities commonly encountered in large non-Hermitian systems.

Variational principle.—

The VMPS method, like other Hermitian variational methods, relies on the fact that a ground state has the lowest energy.
To go beyond this Hermitian paradigm, the first step is to define the ground state of a non-Hermitian system and its energy gradient.
Two common definitions of ground states are used, depending on whether the real or imaginary parts of energy are minimized, with the state denoted by
|{\rm SR}\rangle
or
|{\rm SI}\rangle
, respectively.
For convenience, the ground state mentioned below refers to the right-eigenvector
|r\rangle
unless otherwise specified, i.e.,
H|r\rangle=e|r\rangle
.
To ensure numerical stability, we impose normalization conditions on the left and right eigenstates separately, such that
\langle l|l\rangle=1
and
\langle r|r\rangle=1
.
A normalized right-eigenvector has the form
|r\rangle=|x\rangle/\sqrt{\langle x|x\rangle}
, and the corresponding expectation energy becomes
e(|x\rangle)=\langle x|H|x\rangle/\langle x|x\rangle
.
Because
e(|x\rangle)
is not a holomorphic function, the derivative is not well-defined or cannot be used for straightforward optimization
Utreras-Alarcón et al. 2019
.
Nevertheless, if the real and imaginary parts of the energy are treated as two real-valued functions of complex variables, the Wirtinger derivative
Wirtinger 1927

\partial_{\langle x|}\triangleq\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\Re\{|x\rangle\}}+i\partial_{\Im\{|x\rangle\}})
can be adopted instead
sm
:

\partial_{\langle x|}\Re\{e(|x\rangle)\}=\frac{(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle}-\frac{\langle x|(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle^{2}}.

(1)

Naively, one expects to use Eq. (
1
) for gradient descent to find the
|\rm SR\rangle
ground state of a non-Hermitian system.
The gradient
\partial_{\langle x|}\Re\{e(|x\rangle)\}
should become zero at the end of iterations, resulting in
(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle\propto|x\rangle
.
However, such a condition shows that the converged
|x\rangle
is an eigenvector of
H+H^{\dagger}
rather than
H
.
The states consequently obtained are usually not eigenstates of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
.
Therefore, the conventional variational principle is no longer directly suitable for non-Hermitian systems, unless a proper cost function is available.

Algorithm.—

We propose to take the eigenvector residual norm

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)

\displaystyle\triangleq\left|H|x\rangle-e(|x\rangle)|x\rangle\right|^{2}

(2)

as the cost function.
It is worth noting that any eigenstate of
H
, not just the ground state, fulfills
\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)=0
, and
\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)
is always real and non-negative.
The Wirtinger derivative of
\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)
is simplified to
sm

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle x|}\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)=

\displaystyle[H^{\dagger}-e^{*}(|x\rangle)][H-e(|x\rangle)]|x\rangle

(3)

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}(H,e(|x\rangle))|x\rangle.

Our goal is then reduced to finding the lowest-energy state satisfying
\mathbb{G}(H,e(|x\rangle))|x\rangle=0
.

Before attempting to solve the above equation, we first establish a relation between the companion Hermitian Hamiltonian
\mathbb{G}(H,\varepsilon)
and the original non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
.
Here
\mathbb{G}(H,\varepsilon)
is non-negative definite and Hermitian for any arbitrary
\varepsilon
.
Using singular value decomposition

H-\varepsilon=USV^{\dagger},\;U^{\dagger}U=V^{\dagger}V=I,

(4)

\mathbb{G}(H,\varepsilon)
is decomposed to

\mathbb{G}(H,\varepsilon)=(H^{\dagger}-\varepsilon^{*})(H-\varepsilon)=VS^{2}V^{\dagger},

(5)

where singular values
s_{i}
in
S
are sorted in descending order.
We can see that
\mathbb{G}(H,\varepsilon)
has the smallest eigenvalue
s_{n}^{2}=0
if and only if
H
has an eigenvalue
\varepsilon
.
Furthermore, the vector
V_{n}
is a shared eigenstate of
H
and
\mathbb{G}(H,\varepsilon)
, with eigenvalues
\varepsilon
and
s_{n}^{2}=0
, respectively.

For non-interacting systems, the energy
e
of a many-body ground state can be obtained from summing up single-particle energies.
Finding the ground state of a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
therefore reduces to finding the zero-energy ground state of the companion Hermitian Hamiltonian
\mathbb{G}(H,e)
, for which the powerful VMPS approach can be employed.
In practice, given a finite virtual bond dimension
D
of MPS, the ground energy of
\mathbb{G}(H,e)
after convergence will retain a tiny non-zero value
\eta
, and
s_{n}=\sqrt{\eta}
measures whether
D
is large enough.
Hereafter, we will refer to the aforementioned algorithm with supplied eigenenergies as the Hermitianized variational matrix product state (HVMPS) method, which is guaranteed to converge according to the standard VMPS method.

When eigenvalues are not provided or unknown, a gradient descent method facilitated by the companion Hermitian Hamiltonian can determine the ground energy and the corresponding eigenstate simultaneously, and this will be called the gradient variational matrix product state (GVMPS) method.
For simplicity, we illustrate GVMPS using a parity-time (
\mathcal{PT}
) symmetric system since its many-body ground energy is always real.
More details and its application to non-
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric models are given in Supplemental Material.

The Wirtinger derivative of
s_{n}(\varepsilon)
with respect to
\varepsilon
reads
sm

\partial_{\varepsilon^{*}}s_{n}=\frac{\varepsilon-V_{n}^{\dagger}HV_{n}}{2s_{n}}.

(6)

One may employ the gradient descent method to find the smallest
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}
that minimizes
s_{n}(\varepsilon)
, such that
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}
becomes the ground state energy of
H
and the corresponding eigenvector
V_{n}
is obtained at the same time.
However, when
s_{n}(\varepsilon)
approaches its minimum, the gradient is usually close to zero, which slows down the convergence and even results in instabilities
sm
.
This can be avoided by manually setting the gradient to
\varepsilon-V_{n}^{\dagger}HV_{n}
and using an adaptive learning rate
sm
.
Regarding the initial value of
\varepsilon
, we note that

\Re\left\{e(|x\rangle)\right\}=\frac{\langle x|(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle}\geq\tau,

(7)

where
\tau
is the smallest eigenvalue of the Hermitian matrix
(H+H^{\dagger})/2
.
Therefore, all eigenvalues of
H
have real parts greater than
\tau
, making
\tau
a good starting point for determining the eigenvalue of
|\rm SR\rangle
.
Since the gradient descent only depends on one scalar parameter
\varepsilon
and there is no other local minimum from the initial value
\tau
to the desired energy, the GVMPS method is found to be efficient and well-converged.

Both the HVMPS and GVMPS approaches we propose avoid directly solving non-Hermitian problems, and the companion Hermitian Hamiltonian helps to prevent typical numerical instabilities of non-Hermitian systems.
Some of our benchmark results are presented below.

Non-interacting model.—

We first test for non-interacting systems using the 1D nH-SSH model, which exhibits interesting topological properties and has recently received a lot of attention
Yao and Wang 2018
;
Lieu 2018
;
Chang et al. 2020
;
Han et al. 2021
;
Xi et al. 2021
.
The Hamiltonian takes the following form

\displaystyle H_{0}=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i}\left[(t+\gamma/2)\penalty\ a^{\dagger}_{i}b_{i}+(t-\gamma/2)\penalty\ b^{\dagger}_{i}a_{i}\right.

(8)

\displaystyle\left.+b^{\dagger}_{i}a_{i+1}+a_{i+1}^{\dagger}b_{i}\right],

with one unit cell composed of two sites.
The intra-cell hopping is non-reciprocal and characterized by a non-Hermitian strength
\gamma
, while the inter-cell hopping is Hermitian and set as unit strength.
High-order exceptional points for this model

;
Chang et al. 2020
;
Heiss 2012
;
Tzeng et al. 2021
appear at

.

Since MPS methods are more accurate and efficient for finding low entanglement states that satisfy the area law, it is necessary to investigate the entanglement properties throughout the parameter space to determine which regions are more favourably described by MPS.
Under periodic boundary condition (PBC), previous study
Guo et al. 2021
on the bi-orthogonal entanglement entropy (EE)

;
Chang et al. 2020
;
Herviou et al. 2019b
;

Since our algorithms only focus on the right eigenstate and the bi-orthogonal condition may induce extra numerical difficulties

The bipartite EE between subsystem

and its complementary part

is given by
S_{A}=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}\mathrm{ln}\rho_{A})
, where
\rho_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\bar{A}}\rho^{rr}

Usually,

Conclusion.—

We propose two efficient MPS methods for non-Hermitian systems.
If the eigenenergies of a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian are known, HVMPS can be employed to find its eigenstates by solving a companion Hermitian Hamiltonian.
When the eigenenergies are unknown, HVMPS can be combined with gradient descent and upgraded to GVMPS, such that both ground energy and wave function can be obtained.
These methods provide accurate results for non-interacting and interacting non-Hermitian systems.
Since no non-Hermitian matrix is directly solved, our approach mitigates all known numerical difficulties of typical non-Hermitian systems and ensures convergence through the variational principle.
The algorithms presented here are robust for finding many-body wave functions of a general Hamiltonian, which could pave the way for studying large-scale strongly correlated non-Hermitian systems.

Appendix A
Wirtinger derivative of energy

The expectation energy of a Hermitian matrix
M
with respect to a complex state
|x\rangle\triangleq|r\rangle+i|i\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(|x\rangle)=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle x|M|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle r|M|r\rangle+\langle i|M|i\rangle+i\langle r|M|i\rangle-i\langle i|M|r\rangle}{\langle r|r\rangle+\langle i|i\rangle}.

Based on matrix calculus, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle r|}\langle x|M|x\rangle=M^{T}|r\rangle+M|r\rangle+iM|i\rangle-iM^{T}|i\rangle,

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle i|}\langle x|M|x\rangle=M^{T}|i\rangle+M|i\rangle+iM^{T}|r\rangle-iM|r\rangle,

and

\partial_{\langle r|}\langle x|x\rangle=2|r\rangle,\;\partial_{\langle i|}\langle x|x\rangle=2|i\rangle.

(12)

According to the definition of Wirtinger derivative, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle x|}\langle x|M|x\rangle

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\langle r|}\langle x|M|x\rangle+\frac{i}{2}\partial_{\langle i|}\langle x|M|x\rangle=M|x\rangle,

(13)

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle x|}\langle x|x\rangle

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\langle r|}\langle x|x\rangle+\frac{i}{2}\partial_{\langle i|}\langle x|x\rangle=|x\rangle.

(14)

Combined with the chain rule it gives

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle x|}\mathcal{E}(|x\rangle)=\frac{M|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}-\frac{\langle x|M|x\rangle|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle^{2}}.

(15)

The expectation energy of a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
is given by
e(|x\rangle)=\langle x|H|x\rangle/\langle x|x\rangle
.
By substituting
M=(H+H^{\dagger})/2
into Eq. (
15
), we obtain the derivative of the real part.

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle x|}\Re\{e(|x\rangle)\}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\langle x|}\frac{\langle x|(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle}

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle}-\frac{\langle x|(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle^{2}}.

Similarly, the derivative of the imaginary part is obtained by replacing
M=(H-H^{\dagger})/(2i)

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle x|}\Im\{e(|x\rangle)\}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\langle x|}\frac{\langle x|(H-H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2i\langle x|x\rangle}

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{(H-H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2i\langle x|x\rangle}-\frac{\langle x|(H-H^{\dagger})|x\rangle|x\rangle}{2i\langle x|x\rangle^{2}}.

Given a Hermitian matrix
M
, the Wirtinger derivative of
\langle x|M|x\rangle^{2}
is

\partial_{\langle x|}\langle x|M|x\rangle^{2}=2\langle x|M|x\rangle\partial_{\langle x|}\langle x|M|x\rangle=2\langle x|M|x\rangle M|x\rangle.

(18)

Together with Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) we obtain

\partial_{\langle x|}\frac{\langle x|M|x\rangle^{2}}{\langle x|x\rangle}=\frac{2\langle x|M|x\rangle M|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}-\frac{\langle x|M|x\rangle^{2}|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle^{2}}.

(19)

The eigenvector residual norm is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)\triangleq\left|H|x\rangle-\frac{\langle x|H|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}|x\rangle\right|^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x|H^{\dagger}H|x\rangle-\frac{\langle x|H|x\rangle\langle x|H^{\dagger}|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x|H^{\dagger}H|x\rangle-\frac{\langle x|(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle^{2}+\langle x|i(H-H^{\dagger})|x\rangle^{2}}{4\langle x|x\rangle}.

In the last step above we have replaced the multiplication of a complex number
\langle x|H|x\rangle
and its complex conjunction by the square summation of its real and imaginary parts.
Since
H^{\dagger}H
,
(H+H^{\dagger})
, and
i(H-H^{\dagger})
are always Hermitian, using Eqs. (
13
) and (
19
) we obtain the Wirtinger derivative of
\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle x|}\mathcal{N}(|x\rangle)

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{\dagger}H|x\rangle-\frac{\langle x|(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle(H+H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle}

\displaystyle+\frac{\langle x|(H-H^{\dagger})|x\rangle(H-H^{\dagger})|x\rangle}{2\langle x|x\rangle}

\displaystyle+\frac{\langle x|H|x\rangle\langle x|H^{\dagger}|x\rangle|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(H^{\dagger}-\frac{\langle x|H^{\dagger}|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}\right)\left(H-\frac{\langle x|H|x\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}\right)|x\rangle.

The bi-orthogonal formalism is an interesting approach for treating non-Hermitian systems.
However, when the bi-orthogonal overlap between ground states

We compute

6
-unit-cell nH-SSH model using exact diagonalization, and the results are shown in Fig.

steadily decreases to zero.

, extreme large numbers will appear in
|l\rangle
and
|r\rangle

When our HVMPS algorithm is used instead, the scaling behavior for larger systems is free of the aforementioned numerical difficulties.
As illustrated in Fig.

(b),

decays exponentially while remaining accurate with growing system size.

The fast decay of

is an universal phenomenon in parity-time (
\mathcal{PT}
) symmetric system with a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian satisfying
PHP=H^{\dagger}
, where
P
denotes the spatial parity operator.
Combined with the eigen-decomposition

H=\sum\limits_{i}e_{i}|r_{i}\rangle\langle l_{i}|,

(22)

one finds that

H={(PHP)}^{\dagger}=\sum\limits_{i}e_{i}^{*}P|l_{i}\rangle\langle r_{i}|P,

(23)

which shows

\langle l_{i}|=\langle r_{i}|P

(24)

for all modes with real eigenvalues.
If the wave functions are localized at one end of the chain,
|\langle l_{i}|r_{i}\rangle|=|\langle r_{i}|P|r_{i}\rangle|
will be small.
This is thought to be a common feature of non-Hermitian systems and is called skin effect

It is worth noting that Eq. (

\mathcal{PT}

\mathcal{PT}

Appendix D
The gradient variational matrix product state (GVMPS) algorithm

D.1
Starting point and the converged state

For a general non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, the gradient descent generates a flow in the complex plane of energies, with eigenvalues of
H
acting as attractors.
Starting from any position, the energy ends up at one of the attractors, and the converged eigenstate depends on the starting point.

In the main text, we demonstrate the ground enengy of
(H+H^{\dagger})/2
is a good starting point to find
|\rm SR\rangle
with the smallest real eigenvalue.
Likewise, the ground state energy of
(H-H^{\dagger})/2
is an ideal beginning for determining
|\rm SI\rangle
with the smallest imaginary eigenvalue.
The
|\rm SI\rangle
ground state of
H
is also the
|\rm SR\rangle
ground state of
-iH
.
Furthermore, we can find the eigenenergy with the largest absolute value in any direction in the complex plane by applying a global rotation to the whole spectra.
Namely, solving the
|\rm SR\rangle
ground state of
e^{i(\pi-\theta)}H
yields the greatest absolute eigenenergy of
H
in direction
\theta
.

D.2
Gradient of the objective function
s_{n}(\varepsilon)

The objective function for gradient descent is chosen as the smallest singular value
s_{n}(\varepsilon)
in singular value decomposition

H-\varepsilon=USV^{\dagger},

(25)

where
U^{\dagger}U=V^{\dagger}V=I
.
The differential form of the unitary condition is

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}U^{\dagger}U+U^{\dagger}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}U=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V^{\dagger}V+V^{\dagger}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V=0.

(26)

Therefore,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}U^{\dagger}U
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V^{\dagger}V
are skew-Hermitian with their diagonal elements pure imaginary.
Left-multiplying by
U^{\dagger}
and right-multiplying by

to the differential form of Eq. (

-U^{\dagger}Vd\varepsilon=U^{\dagger}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}US+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S+S\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V^{\dagger}V.

(27)

By comparing the real parts of the
n
-th diagonal elements from both sides,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s_{n}
is found to satisfy

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s_{n}=-\Re\{U^{\dagger}_{n}V_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\}.

(28)

On the other hand, the differential of a real-valued function

with a complex-valued argument
\varepsilon
can be expressed as

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s_{n}=2\Re\{\partial_{\varepsilon^{*}}s_{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon^{*}\}=2\Re\{(\partial_{\varepsilon^{*}}s_{n})^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\}.

(29)

Comparing Eq. (
28
) with Eq. (
29

V^{\dagger}HV-\varepsilon=V^{\dagger}US
, we obtain

\partial_{\varepsilon^{*}}s_{n}=-\frac{V^{\dagger}_{n}U_{n}}{2}=\frac{\varepsilon-V_{n}^{\dagger}HV_{n}}{2s_{n}}.

(30)

where

(
U_{n}

n

(
U
) with the smallest singular value

As

satisfies

, implying that
V_{n}
and
U_{n}
are approximately the right and left eigenvectors of

.
As mentioned in Appendix
C

decreases to zero exponentially with system size for the

Appendix F
Many-body particle distributions

To evaluate the many-body ground state distribution, we consider a general non-Hermitian single-particle model
H=\vec{a^{\dagger}}M\vec{a}
with dimension
L
.

H
can be diagonalized as
H=\vec{a^{\dagger}}S\Lambda S^{-1}\vec{a}\triangleq\vec{d^{\dagger}}\Lambda\vec{b}
, where
\Lambda={\rm Diag}(\{\lambda_{i}\})
,
\Re\{\lambda_{i}\}\leq\Re\{\lambda_{j}\}
for
i<j
, and

\begin{array}[]{lll}\{b_{i},b_{j}\}=0,&\{b^{\dagger}_{i},b^{\dagger}_{j}\}=0,&\{b_{i},b^{\dagger}_{j}\}\neq\delta_{ij};\\
\{d_{i},d_{j}\}=0,&\{d^{\dagger}_{i},d^{\dagger}_{j}\}=0,&\{d_{i},d^{\dagger}_{j}\}\neq\delta_{ij};\\
\{d_{i},b_{j}\}=0,&\{d^{\dagger}_{i},b^{\dagger}_{j}\}=0,&\{b_{i},d^{\dagger}_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}.\end{array}

(33)

Although neither
b_{i}
nor
d_{i}
satisfies the fermion anti-commutation relations, together they form a set of bi-fermionic operators
Chang et al. 2020
;
Alsallom et al. 2021
.

For nonredundant indices in ascending order
\{i_{j}|1\leq j\leq p\}
,
d^{\dagger}_{i_{1}}d^{\dagger}_{i_{2}}\cdots d^{\dagger}_{i_{p}}|0\rangle
is an
p
-particle right eigenvector of
H
with energy
\sum_{1\leq j\leq p}\lambda_{i_{j}}
, and the corresponding left eigenvector is
\langle 0|b_{i_{p}}b_{i_{p-1}}\cdots b_{i_{1}}
.
The particle distribution of the
p
-particle state
|\mathrm{SR}\rangle=d^{\dagger}_{1}d^{\dagger}_{2}\cdots d^{\dagger}_{p}|0\rangle
on the
m
-th site is given by

\displaystyle\langle n_{m}\rangle_{p}=

\displaystyle\frac{\left|a_{m}d^{\dagger}_{1}d^{\dagger}_{2}\cdots d^{\dagger}_{p}|0\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left|d^{\dagger}_{1}d^{\dagger}_{2}\cdots d^{\dagger}_{p}|0\rangle\right|^{2}}

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\sum\limits_{\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}}a_{m}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq p}S_{q_{i},i}a^{\dagger}_{q_{i}}|0\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left|\sum\limits_{\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq p}S_{q_{i},i}a^{\dagger}_{q_{i}}|0\rangle\right|^{2}}.

Here
\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}
represents one of the
p
-permutations of the set
\{1,2,\cdots,L\}
, the total number of which is
L!/(L-p)!
.
We rearrange the order of creation operators using the fermion anti-commutation relations.

\prod_{1\leq i\leq p}a_{q_{i}}^{\dagger}|0\rangle=|\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}\rangle=\epsilon_{\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}}|[q_{(L)}^{(p)}]\rangle,

(35)

where
[q_{(L)}^{(p)}]
denotes one of the
p
-combinations of the set
\{1,2,\cdots,L\}
with
q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{p}
, the total number of which is
\frac{L!}{p!(L-p)!}
.

\epsilon_{\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}}
denotes

\epsilon_{\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}}=\begin{cases}+1&\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}\mathrm{\textrm{ is an even permutation of }}[q_{(L)}^{(p)}],\\
-1&\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}\mathrm{\textrm{ is an odd permutation of }}[q_{(L)}^{(p)}],\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(36)

When combined with
\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq p}S_{q_{i},i}
and summed over all terms contributing to the same state, the coefficient in front of
|[q_{(L)}^{(p)}]\rangle
is the determinant of a sub-matrix of
S
selected from row indices
[q_{(L)}^{(p)}]
and column indices
(1,2,\cdots,p)
, denoted as

\sum\limits_{\{q_{(L)}^{(p)}\}}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq p}S_{q_{i},i}a^{\dagger}_{q_{i}}|0\rangle=\sum_{[q_{(L)}^{(p)}]}\det(S_{[q_{(L)}^{(p)}],[p]})|[q_{(L)}^{(p)}]\rangle.

(37)

Now we consider the extra annihilation operator
a_{m}
in the numerator of Eq. (
34
).
It is clear that only terms with exactly one
q_{j}=m
contribute to the summation.
Using the fermion anti-commutation relation, we have

a_{m}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq p}a^{\dagger}_{q_{i}}|0\rangle={(-1)}^{(j-1)}\prod\limits_{q_{i}\neq m}a^{\dagger}_{q_{i}}|0\rangle.

(38)

Thus, similar to Eq. (
37
),

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\{q^{(p)}\}}a_{m}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq p}S_{q_{i},i}a^{\dagger}_{q_{i}}|0\rangle

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{[q_{(L|\bar{m})}^{(p-1)}]}\det(S_{(m,[q_{(L|\bar{m})}^{(p-1)}]),[p]})|[q_{(L|\bar{m})}^{(p-1)}]\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{[q_{(L|\bar{m})}^{(p-1)}]}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(-1)^{j-1}S_{m,j}\det(S_{[q_{(L|\bar{m})}^{(p-1)}],[p|\bar{j}]})|[q_{(L|\bar{m})}^{(p-1)}]\rangle.

Here,
[q_{(L|\bar{m})}^{(p-1)}]
denotes one of the
(p-1)
-combinations of the set
\{1,2,\cdots,m-1,m+1,\cdots,L\}
with
q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{p-1}

\frac{(L-1)!}{(p-1)!(L-p)!}
.

[p|\bar{j}]
denotes
(1,2,\cdots,j-1,j+1,\cdots,p)
.

The squared norm in Eq. (
34
) involves a multiplication of two determinants, similar to the Cauchy-Binet formula

\displaystyle\det(AB)=

\displaystyle\det(A_{[M],[N]}B_{[N],[M]})

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{[q_{(N)}^{(M)}]}\det(A_{[M],[q_{(N)}^{(M)}]})\det(B_{[q_{(N)}^{(M)}],[M]}),

where
A
and
B
are
M\times N
and
N\times M
matrices with
N>M
.
Using this formula from right to left, Eq. (
34
) is simplified to

\displaystyle\langle n_{m}\rangle_{p}

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}(-1)^{i+j}S^{\dagger}_{i,m}S_{m,j}\det(S^{\dagger}_{[p|\bar{i}],[L|\bar{m}]}S_{[L|\bar{m}],[p|\bar{j}]})}{\det(S^{\dagger}_{[p],[L]}S_{[L],[p]})}.

To calculate bi-orthogonal particle distributions constructed by

and

operators, we simply replace all

in Eq. (

.

The results of Eq. (

Using the Cauchy-Binet formula,

can be calculated in at most

To prevent loss of precision for large systems

The remarkable agreement between single-particle results and HVMPS results confirms the validity of our method.

\displaystyle={(S^{-1})}_{2N-k+1,[2N]}X.

When there are more than half-filled particles, it is more convenient to start from the full-filled state
|2N\rangle

A direct derivation gives

\displaystyle d_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots d_{p}^{\dagger}|0\rangle

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{2N}\cdots b_{p+1}d_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots d_{2N}^{\dagger}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{2N}\cdots b_{p+1}|2N\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{q_{(2N)}^{(2N-p)}\}}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq 2N-p}{(S^{-1})}_{2N-i+1,q_{i}}a_{q_{i}}|2N\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{q_{(2N)}^{(2N-p)}\}}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq 2N-p}{(S^{-1})}_{i,q_{i}}X_{q_{i}q_{i}}a_{q_{i}}|2N\rangle.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle b_{1}\cdots b_{2N-p}|2N\rangle=\sum_{\{q_{(2N)}^{(2N-p)}\}}\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq 2N-p}{(S^{-1})}_{i,q_{i}}a_{q_{i}}|2N\rangle.

(45)

The
X_{q_{i}q_{i}}

a_{q_{i}}
without changing particle distributions.
Therefore,
d_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots d_{p}^{\dagger}|0\rangle
can be replaced by
b_{1}\cdots b_{2N-p}|2N\rangle

\displaystyle\langle n_{m}\rangle_{2N-p}=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle 0|d_{2N-p}\cdots d_{1}a_{m}^{\dagger}a_{m}d_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots d_{2N-p}^{\dagger}|0\rangle}{\langle 0|d_{2N-p}\cdots d_{1}d_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots d_{2N-p}^{\dagger}|0\rangle}

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle 2N|b^{\dagger}_{p}\cdots b^{\dagger}_{1}a_{m}^{\dagger}a_{m}b_{1}\cdots b_{p}|2N\rangle}{\langle 2N|b^{\dagger}_{p}\cdots b^{\dagger}_{1}b_{1}\cdots b_{p}|2N\rangle}

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\langle 2N|b^{\dagger}_{p}\cdots b^{\dagger}_{1}a_{m}a_{m}^{\dagger}b_{1}\cdots b_{p}|2N\rangle}{\langle 2N|b^{\dagger}_{p}\cdots b^{\dagger}_{1}b_{1}\cdots b_{p}|2N\rangle}.
