On the structure factor of jammed particle configurations on the one-dimensional lattice
Jean-Marc Luck

Abstract
A broad class of blocked or jammed configurations of particles on the one-dimensional lattice can be characterized in terms of local rules involving only the lengths of clusters of particles (occupied sites) and of holes (empty sites). Examples of physical relevance include the metastable states reached by the zero-temperature dynamics of kinetically constrained spin chains, the attractors of totally irreversible processes such as random sequential adsorption, and arrays of Rydberg atoms in the blockade regime. The configurational entropy of ensembles of such blocked configurations has been investigated recently by means of an approach inspired from the theory of stochastic renewal processes. This approach provides a valuable alternative to the more traditional transfer-matrix formalism. We show that the renewal approach is also an efficient tool to investigate a range of observables in uniform ensembles of blocked configurations, besides their configurational entropy. The main emphasis is on their structure factor and correlation function.

Introduction

Blocked or jammed configurations are met in many guises
in the statistical physics of complex systems,
including first and foremost glassy and granular materials
(see
[
1

2

In this work we focus our attention onto

Various situations of physical significance

Many examples of blocked configurations in one dimension
correspond to the attractors of some underlying zero-temperature dynamics
launched from an infinite-temperature disordered initial state.
Within this setting, these blocked configurations may be viewed as one-dimensional zero-temperature analogues
of the metastable states (also known as valleys, pure states, inherent structures, or quasi-states)
which are met in higher-dimensional or mean-field models at finite temperature

4

A broad variety of kinetic spin models

[

,

]

disordered models, such as the Ising spin glass

The zero-temperature dynamics of the above models is irreversible and strongly non-ergodic.
Some of these kinetic models can be exactly mapped onto the dynamics
of the deposition of hard objects on the one-dimensional lattice.
Prototypical examples are RSA (random sequential adsorption)
or CSA (cooperative sequential adsorption),
where particles or clusters are deposited irreversibly and sequentially
on an initially empty substrate
[

,
24
,
25
]
.
The process stops when no further object can be inserted into the system,
which is left in a blocked or jammed configuration
with a non-trivial density or coverage.

Another more recent motivation to investigate blocked configurations of particles

namely the physics of ultracold atoms.
Trapped Rydberg atoms appear as a promising benchmark for what concerns
quantum computation, simulation and information processing

.
To close this panorama with an example outside the realm of physics,

where houses are sequentially built on an infinite array of pre-drawn plots along a beach,

from at least one of the side directions.
New houses are successively introduced
until a blocked configuration is reached

2
Model and simple observables

2.1
The model

Let us first recall the statistical model of blocked configurations introduced in
[
44
]
.
Configurations are semi-infinite sequences of particles
(or occupied sites, noted
{\bullet}
) and of holes (or empty sites, noted
{\circ}
).
Such a configuration
{\cal C}
is described by the lengths
i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},\dots

of the successive clusters of particles and holes.
It is therefore either of the form

{\cal C}_{I}=\underbrace{{\bullet}\cdots{\bullet}}_{i_{1}}\underbrace{{\circ}\cdots{\circ}}_{j_{1}}\underbrace{{\bullet}\cdots{\bullet}}_{i_{2}}\underbrace{{\circ}\cdots{\circ}}_{j_{2}}\cdots

(2.1)

if the first site is occupied,
or of the form

{\cal C}_{J}=\underbrace{{\circ}\cdots{\circ}}_{j_{1}}\underbrace{{\bullet}\cdots{\bullet}}_{i_{1}}\underbrace{{\circ}\cdots{\circ}}_{j_{2}}\underbrace{{\bullet}\cdots{\bullet}}_{i_{2}}\cdots

(2.2)

if the first site is empty.

Blocked configurations are defined by the following local rules:

•

All lengths
i_{1},i_{2},\dots
of particle clusters
belong to some set
{\cal I}
of positive integers.

•

All lengths
j_{1},j_{2},\dots
of hole clusters
belong to some set
{\cal J}
of positive integers.

In the above,
{\cal I}
and
{\cal J}
are two prescribed finite or infinite subsets
of the positive integers, which entirely define the configurational model.
Hereafter we always deal with the uniform statistical ensemble
obtained by attributing equal weights to all blocked configurations
{\cal C}

constructed as above.
We assume that the model is non-trivial,
namely that both sets
{\cal I}
and
{\cal J}
are non-empty
and at least one of them contains more than one integer.

2.2
Configurational entropy

To set the stage, we begin by recalling the analysis of the configurational entropy.
This quantity, denoted by
S_{\star}
, describes the exponential growth

{\cal N}_{N}\sim\exp(NS_{\star})

(2.3)

of the number
{\cal N}_{N}
of configurations
{\cal C}_{N}
on a finite system of length
N
.
No overhang is allowed in the construction,
so that the rightmost cluster ends exactly at site
N
.
We also introduce the numbers
{\cal N}_{N,I}
and
{\cal N}_{N,J}

of finite configurations
{\cal C}_{N,I}
and
{\cal C}_{N,J}
beginning with a cluster of particles or of holes,
in correspondence with (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).

The key observation made in
[
44
]

is the existence of a formal analogy between the present setting
and the theory of stochastic renewal processes
[
46
,
47
,
48
]

(see
[
49
,
50
]
for presentations in the physics literature).
According to their usual definition,
renewal processes take place on the semi-infinite time axis
(t>0)
and evolve from a given initial condition.
Hence considering semi-infinite configurations with prescribed initial condition
is the most appropriate framework for exploiting to the full the analogy between the present problem and renewal processes.
From a more technical viewpoint, this analogy also leads one to introduce the generating series of the numbers of configurations

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{I}(z)=\sum_{N\geq 1}{\cal N}_{N,I}z^{N},\qquad{\cal N}_{J}(z)=\sum_{N\geq 1}{\cal N}_{N,J}z^{N},

\displaystyle{\cal N}(z)=\sum_{N\geq 0}{\cal N}_{N}z^{N}.

(2.4)

We have

{\cal N}(z)=1+{\cal N}_{I}(z)+{\cal N}_{J}(z),

(2.5)

where the initial term
{\cal N}_{0}=1
is conventional.
The series
{\cal N}_{I}(z)
and
N_{J}(z)
obey linear renewal equations of the form

{\cal N}_{I}(z)=I(z)(1+{\cal N}_{J}(z)),\qquad{\cal N}_{J}(z)=J(z)(1+{\cal N}_{I}(z)),

(2.6)

where

I(z)=\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}z^{i},\qquad J(z)=\sum_{j\in{\cal J}}z^{j}

(2.7)

are the series associated with the sets
{\cal I}
and
{\cal J}

defining the statistical ensemble.
Solving the linear equations (
2.6
), we obtain the results

{\cal N}_{I}(z)=\frac{I(z)(1+J(z))}{1-I(z)J(z)},\qquad{\cal N}_{J}(z)=\frac{J(z)(1+I(z))}{1-I(z)J(z)}

(2.8)

and

{\cal N}(z)=\frac{(1+I(z))(1+J(z))}{1-I(z)J(z)}.

(2.9)

As
z
increases from 0 to 1,
the product
I(z)J(z)
increases from
I(0)J(0)=0

to
I(1)J(1)={\left|{\cal I}\right|}{\left|{\cal J}\right|}\geq 2
(possibly infinite).
There is therefore a single value
z_{\star}
of
z
in the range
0<z_{\star}<1
such that
the denominator of (
2.9
) vanishes, i.e.,

I(z_{\star})J(z_{\star})=1.

(2.10)

The configurational entropy entering the exponential growth law (
2.3
) reads

S_{\star}=-\ln z_{\star}.

(2.11)

Throughout the following, along the lines of
[
44
]
,
we restrict this study to the rational class of models
where both generating series

I(z)=\frac{A_{I}(z)}{B_{I}(z)},\qquad J(z)=\frac{A_{J}(z)}{B_{J}(z)}

(2.12)

are rational functions, i.e., ratios of polynomials in
z
.
This class encompasses all examples considered below,
and virtually all situations of physical significance
(see
[
44
]
for more details).
The formula (
2.9
) then takes the form

{\cal N}(z)=\frac{C(z)}{D(z)},

(2.13)

where
C(z)
and
D(z)
are polynomials in
z
.
The rational fraction (
2.13
) is assumed to be irreducible.
The degree of
D(z)
is denoted by
\Delta
.
Its smallest zero
z_{\star}
is real, positive, and simple.
The degree
\Delta
provides a natural measure of the complexity
of configurational models in the rational class.

2.3
Local observables

Our main goal is to show that the renewal method recalled above
provides an efficient tool to evaluate
a good deal of observables in the uniform ensemble, besides its configurational entropy.
The case of the Hendricks-Teller model,
considered in
A
,
serves as a warming up for what follows.
The remainder of this section is devoted to local observables
such as boundary probabilities
and the length distribution of particle and hole clusters.
Section
3
will be about the main subject of this work,
namely the structure factor and the correlation function.

Boundary probabilities.

Our first observables are the boundary probabilities
W_{I}
(resp.
W_{J}
)
that an infinitely long configuration
{\cal C}

begins with a cluster of particles (resp. a cluster of holes), i.e.,

W_{I}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{{\cal N}_{N,I}}{{\cal N}_{N}},\qquad W_{J}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{{\cal N}_{N,J}}{{\cal N}_{N}}.

(2.14)

We have
{\cal N}_{N}={\cal N}_{N,I}+{\cal N}_{N,J}
for
N\geq 1
, and so
W_{I}+W_{J}=1
, as expected.
All generating series entering (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) have poles at
z=z_{\star}
,
so that all numbers of configurations grow as
\exp(NS_{\star})
.
We have therefore

W_{I}=\lim_{z\to z_{\star}}\frac{{\cal N}_{I}(z)}{{\cal N}(z)},\qquad W_{J}=\lim_{z\to z_{\star}}\frac{{\cal N}_{J}(z)}{{\cal N}(z)}.

(2.15)

The formulas (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) yield

\displaystyle W_{I}=\frac{I(z_{\star})}{1+I(z_{\star})}=\frac{1}{1+J(z_{\star})},

\displaystyle W_{J}=\frac{J(z_{\star})}{1+J(z_{\star})}=\frac{1}{1+I(z_{\star})}.

(2.16)

Distributions of cluster lengths and their moments.

We now turn to the length distributions of the clusters of particles and of holes,
i.e.,

p_{I,k}={\mathop{\rm Prob}}(i=k),\qquad p_{J,k}={\mathop{\rm Prob}}(j=k),

(2.17)

where
i
(resp.
j
) stands for the length of any particle cluster (resp. any hole cluster)
in an infinitely long configuration.
Consider particle clusters for definiteness.
All such clusters are interchangeable, so that we have

p_{I,k}={\mathop{\rm Prob}}(i_{1}=k)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{{\cal N}_{N,I,k}}{{\cal N}_{N,I}},

(2.18)

where
{\cal N}_{N,I,k}
is the number of configurations of length
N
beginning
with a cluster of particles of length
i_{1}=k
.
The corresponding generating series reads

{\cal N}_{I,k}(z)=\sum_{N\geq 1}{\cal N}_{N,I,k}z^{N}=z^{k}\,\chi_{I}(k)(1+{\cal N}_{J}(z)),

(2.19)

where

\chi_{I}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&(k\in{\cal I}),\\
0&(k\notin{\cal I})\end{array}\right.

(2.20)

is the indicator function of the set
{\cal I}
.
In line with (
2.15
), we have

p_{I,k}=\lim_{z\to z_{\star}}\frac{{\cal N}_{I,k}(z)}{{\cal N}_{I}(z)}.

(2.21)

We thus obtain

p_{I,k}=\frac{z_{\star}^{k}}{I(z_{\star})}\,\chi_{I}(k),

(2.22)

and similarly

p_{J,k}=\frac{z_{\star}^{k}}{J(z_{\star})}\,\chi_{J}(k).

(2.23)

In particular,
the mean lengths of particle and hole clusters read

\displaystyle{\langle i\rangle}=\sum_{k\geq 1}k\,p_{I,k}=\sum_{k\in{\cal I}}\frac{kz_{\star}^{k}}{I(z_{\star})}=\frac{z_{\star}I^{\prime}(z_{\star})}{I(z_{\star})}=z_{\star}I^{\prime}(z_{\star})J(z_{\star}),

\displaystyle{\langle j\rangle}=z_{\star}I(z_{\star})J^{\prime}(z_{\star}).

(2.24)

Here and throughout the following,

{\langle.\rangle}
denotes an average over the uniform ensemble of blocked configurations.
The mean distance between consecutive particle clusters
(or, equivalently, between consecutive hole clusters) is therefore

\ell={\langle i\rangle}+{\langle j\rangle}=z_{\star}(I^{\prime}(z_{\star})J(z_{\star})+I(z_{\star})J^{\prime}(z_{\star})).

(2.25)

The particle density can be obtained as

\rho=\frac{{\langle i\rangle}}{\ell}=\frac{I^{\prime}(z_{\star})J(z_{\star})}{I^{\prime}(z_{\star})J(z_{\star})+I(z_{\star})J^{\prime}(z_{\star})}.

(2.26)

An equivalent expression was derived by another route in
[
44
]
.
Higher moments of the cluster lengths can be determined along the same lines:

\displaystyle{\langle i^{p}\rangle}=J(z_{\star})\Bigl(z\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\Bigr)^{p}I(z)\Big|_{z=z_{\star}},

\displaystyle{\langle j^{p}\rangle}=I(z_{\star})\Bigl(z\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\Bigr)^{p}J(z)\Big|_{z=z_{\star}}.

(2.27)

We have especially

\displaystyle{\langle i^{2}\rangle}=z_{\star}J(z_{\star})(I^{\prime}(z_{\star})+z_{\star}I^{\prime\prime}(z_{\star})),

\displaystyle{\langle j^{2}\rangle}=z_{\star}I(z_{\star})(J^{\prime}(z_{\star})+z_{\star}J^{\prime\prime}(z_{\star})).

(2.28)

3
General results on structure factor and correlation function

3.1
Definitions

Each configuration
{\cal C}
can be alternatively described by the occupation numbers

\eta_{m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&(\hbox{site $m$ is occupied}),\\
0&(\hbox{site $m$ is empty}).\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

The correlation functions of the model are

{\langle\eta_{m}\rangle}
,
{\langle\eta_{m}\eta_{l}\rangle}
, and so on,
where averages are taken over the uniform ensemble.
Translation invariance and clustering of correlations hold
in the bulk of the system, i.e., far from its boundary.
We have in particular

{\langle\eta_{m}\rangle}\to\rho,\qquad{\langle\eta_{m}\eta_{l}\rangle}-{\langle\eta_{m}\rangle}{\langle\eta_{l}\rangle}\to C_{m-l}

(3.2)

as
m
and
l
are simultaneously large,
the difference
m-l
being kept fixed.
The particle density
\rho
is given by (
2.26
).
The connected pair correlation function
C_{n}

is an even function of
n
which falls off to zero
as the distance
{\left|n\right|}
between both sites gets large.
It is invariant under the exchange of particles and holes
(
\eta_{m}\longleftrightarrow 1-\eta_{m}
).

The (random) Fourier amplitude
G_{N}(q)
and intensity
S_{N}(q)

associated with the first
N
sites read

G_{N}(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}mq}\eta_{m},\qquad S_{N}(q)=\frac{{\left|G_{N}(q)\right|}^{2}}{N}.

(3.3)

Our main interest is in the structure factor

S(q)=\lim_{N\to\infty}{\langle S_{N}(q)\rangle}.

(3.4)

This self-averaging quantity identifies with the Fourier transform
of the correlation function
C_{n}
:

S(q)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}C_{n}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}nq}.

(3.5)

3.2
Fourier amplitudes

In order to determine the Fourier amplitudes,
it is advantageous to split the definition (
3.3
),
denoting by
G_{N,I}(q)
(resp.
G_{N,J}(q)
)
the Fourier amplitude of a finite configuration of type
{\cal C}_{N,I}
(resp.
{\cal C}_{N,J}
).
These amplitudes obey the renewal equations

\displaystyle G_{N,I}(q)=a(i_{1},q)+{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}qi_{1}}G_{N-i_{1},J}(q),

\displaystyle G_{N,J}(q)={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}qj_{1}}G_{N-j_{1},I}(q),

(3.6)

with

a(i_{1},q)=\sum_{m=1}^{i_{1}}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}mq}=\frac{{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}{1-{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}\,\left(1-{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}qi_{1}}\right).

(3.7)

In (
3.6
), the amplitude denoted by
G_{N-i_{1},J}(q)

is associated with the
N-i_{1}
atoms of the configuration
{\cal C}_{I}

numbered from
i_{1}+1
to
N
.
It is a probabilistic copy of the amplitude
G_{N-i_{1},J}(q)
.
Similarly, the amplitude denoted by
G_{N-j_{1},I}(q)

is associated with the
N-j_{1}
atoms of the configuration
{\cal C}_{J}

numbered from
j_{1}+1
to
N
.
It is a probabilistic copy of the amplitude
G_{N-j_{1},I}(q)
.

Let us introduce the generating series

\displaystyle\Gamma_{I}(z,q)=\sum_{N\geq 1}\sum_{{\cal C}_{N,I}}G_{N,I}(q)z^{N},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{J}(z,q)=\sum_{N\geq 1}\sum_{{\cal C}_{N,J}}G_{N,J}(q)z^{N},

\displaystyle\Gamma(z,q)=\Gamma_{I}(z,q)+\Gamma_{J}(z,q).

(3.8)

The renewal equations (
3.6
) entail that these quantities obey

\displaystyle\Gamma_{I}(z,q)=\gamma(z,q)+I(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})\Gamma_{J}(z,q),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{J}(z,q)=J(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})\Gamma_{I}(z,q),

(3.9)

with

\gamma(z,q)=\frac{{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}{1-{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}\left(I(z)-I(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})\right)\left(1+{\cal N}_{J}(z)\right).

(3.10)

Solving the linear equations (
3.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{I}(z,q)=\frac{\gamma(z,q)}{1-I(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})J(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{J}(z,q)=\frac{J(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})\gamma(z,q)}{1-I(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})J(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})},

\displaystyle\Gamma(z,q)=\frac{(1+J(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}))\gamma(z,q)}{1-I(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})J(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})}.

(3.11)

In the limit of an infinitely long sample,
in line with (
2.15
),
the mean Fourier amplitude is given by

{\langle G(q)\rangle}=\lim_{z\to z_{\star}}\frac{\Gamma(z,q)}{{\cal N}(z)}.

(3.12)

Using (
2.9
), (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we are left with

{\langle G(q)\rangle}=\frac{{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}{1-{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}\,\frac{1+J(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})}{1+I(z_{\star})}\,\frac{I(z_{\star})-I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})}{1-I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})J(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})}.

(3.13)

This result diverges at small wavevectors as

{\langle G(q)\rangle}\approx\frac{\rho}{{\rm i}q}\qquad(q\to 0),

(3.14)

where the particle density
\rho
is given by (
2.26
).

3.3
Structure factor

The structure factor
S(q)
encodes bulk properties of the model.
Boundary conditions are therefore irrelevant,
so that it is sufficient to consider configurations of type
{\cal C}_{I}
.
The simplest route to evaluate
S(q)
consists in eliminating
amplitudes of the form
G_{N,J}(q)

from the renewal equations (
3.6
), obtaining thus

G_{N,I}(q)=a(i_{1},q)+{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q(i_{1}+j_{1})}G_{N-i_{1}-j_{1},I}(q),

(3.15)

with the same convention as in (
3.6
),
and so

\displaystyle G_{N,I}(q){\overline{G}}_{N,I}(q)

\displaystyle=a(i_{1},q){\overline{a}}(i_{1},q)

(3.16)

\displaystyle+a(i_{1},q){\rm e}^{{\rm i}q(i_{1}+j_{1})}{\overline{G}}_{N-i_{1}-j_{1},I}(q)

\displaystyle+{\overline{a}}(i_{1},q){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q(i_{1}+j_{1})}G_{N-i_{1}-j_{1},I}(q)

\displaystyle+G_{N-i_{1}-j_{1},I}(q){\overline{G}}_{N-i_{1}-j_{1},I}(q).

Here and throughout the following, a bar denotes complex conjugation.

Let us introduce the generating series

\Sigma(z,q)=\sum_{N\geq 1}\sum_{{\cal C}_{N,I}}G_{N,I}(q){\overline{G}}_{N,I}(q)z^{N}.

(3.17)

This quantity is given by the sum of four terms,
in correspondence with the four lines of the right-hand side of (
3.16
), namely

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(z,q)=\frac{2I(z)-I(z{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})-I(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})}{2(1-\cos q)}\,\left(1+{\cal N}_{J}(z)\right),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}(z,q)=\frac{{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}{1-{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}}(I(z{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})-I(z))J(z{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}){\overline{\Gamma}}_{I}(z,q),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}(z,q)=\frac{{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}}{1-{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}}(I(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})-I(z))J(z{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})\Gamma_{I}(z,q),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{4}(z,q)=I(z)J(z)\Sigma(z,q).

(3.18)

We have therefore

\Sigma(z,q)=\frac{\Sigma_{1}(z,q)+\Sigma_{2}(z,q)+\Sigma_{3}(z,q)}{1-I(z)J(z)}.

(3.19)

In the limit of an infinitely long sample,
in line with (
2.15
),
the structure factor is given by

S(q)=\lim_{z\to z_{\star}}\frac{\Sigma(z,q)}{z{\cal N}_{I}^{\prime}(z)},

(3.20)

where the denominator

z{\cal N}_{I}^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{N\geq 1}N{\cal N}_{N,I}z^{N}

(3.21)

takes care of the factor
N
in the definition (
3.3
) of
S_{N}(q)
.
This quantity has a double pole at
z=z_{\star}
, of the form

z{\cal N}_{I}^{\prime}(z)\approx\frac{\ell\,I(z_{\star})(1+J(z_{\star}))}{(1-I(z)J(z))^{2}},

(3.22)

where the length
\ell
is given by (
2.25
).

Some algebra using (
3.18
), (
3.19
), (
3.22
)
leads us to the following expression for the structure factor:

S(q)=\frac{\Phi({{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})+\Phi({{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})}{2\ell\,(1-\cos q)},

(3.23)

with

\Phi({{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})=\frac{(I(z_{\star})-I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}))(J(z_{\star})-J(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}))}{1-I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})J(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})}.

(3.24)

The formula (
3.23
) is the key result of this work.
First of all,
this expression is invariant under the interchange of particles and holes, as it should be.
For all models in the rational class under consideration,
the structure factor
S(q)
can be reduced to a rational function of
\cos q
.
Numerous explicit examples will be given hereafter
(see (
4.4
), (
4.10
), (
4.15
), (
5.7
), (
5.11
), (
6.6
), (
6.10
)).
Its denominator is a polynomial in
\cos q
whose degree is generically
\Delta-1
,
where
\Delta
is the degree of the polynomial
D(z)
introduced in (
2.13
).
The structure factor has poles at the complex values
\pm q_{m}
of the wavevector, such that

{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q_{m}}=\frac{z_{\star}}{z_{m}},

(3.25)

where the
z_{m}
are the zeros of
D(z)
, except the smallest one
z_{\star}
.
There are
\Delta-1
such zeros, counted with their multiplicities.
The
z_{m}
are either real, or they occur in complex conjugate pairs.
They are assumed to be ordered such that

z_{\star}<{\left|z_{1}\right|}\leq{\left|z_{2}\right|}\leq\dots,\qquad 0<{\mathop{\rm Im}\,}q_{1}\leq{\mathop{\rm Im}\,}q_{2}\leq\dots

(3.26)

The structure factor at zero wavevector,

S(0)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}C_{n},

(3.27)

is nothing but the compressibility of the model.
This quantity describes the linear growth of the variance
of the total particle number,

M=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\eta_{m},

(3.28)

in a finite system of size
N
:

{\mathop{\rm var}\,}M={\langle M^{2}\rangle}-{\langle M\rangle}^{2}\approx S(0)\,N.

(3.29)

Expanding (
3.23
) in
q
,
and using (
2.24
) and (
2.28
),
we obtain

S(0)=\frac{{\langle j\rangle}^{2}\bigl({\langle i^{2}\rangle}-{\langle i\rangle}^{2}\bigr)+{\langle i\rangle}^{2}\bigl({\langle j^{2}\rangle}-{\langle j\rangle}^{2}\bigr)}{\ell^{3}}.

(3.30)

An equivalent formula was derived by another route in
[
44
]
,
where
S(0)
is denoted by
c_{2}
.

The structure factor at wavevector
\pi
,

S(\pi)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}C_{n},

(3.31)

can be referred to as the staggered compressibility.
It reads

S(\pi)=\frac{(I(z_{\star})-I(-z_{\star}))(J(z_{\star})-J(-z_{\star}))}{2\ell\,(1-I(-z_{\star})J(-z_{\star}))}.

(3.32)

The structure factor exhibits systematic extinctions
in cases where the lengths of all clusters of particles (or of holes)
are multiples of some fixed integer
K=2,3,\dots

This phenomenon is to be expected.
Consider for definiteness the case of particle clusters.
If all their lengths are multiples of
K
,

I(z)
is a rational function of
z^{K}
,
so that the expression (
3.23
) of
S(q)
vanishes whenever
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}qK}=1
,
but
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}\neq 1
,
i.e., for all wavevectors of the form

q=\frac{2\pi m}{K}\qquad(m\neq 0\;\hbox{mod}\;K).

(3.33)

In the particular case of a symmetric ensemble,
where particles and holes play similar roles,
such that
{\cal I}={\cal J}
,
I(z_{\star})=J(z_{\star})=1
,
and
\rho=1/2
, (
3.23
) and (
3.30
) boil down to

S(q)=\frac{1}{\ell(1-\cos q)}\,\frac{1-I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}})}{(1+I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}))(1+I(z_{\star}{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q}}))},

(3.34)

S(0)=\frac{{\langle i^{2}\rangle}-{\langle i\rangle}^{2}}{4{\langle i\rangle}}.

(3.35)

The above expressions bear a striking resemblance with those of the Hendricks-Teller model
(see (
1.11
), (
1.13
)).

3.4
Correlation function

The correlation function is given by (see (
3.5
))

C_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}q}{2\pi}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}nq}S(q).

(3.36)

Setting
y={\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}
and using (
3.23
), this reads

C_{n}=-\frac{1}{\ell}\oint\frac{{\rm d}y}{2\pi{\rm i}}\,\frac{y^{n}}{(y-1)^{2}}\bigl(\Phi(y)+\Phi(1/y)\bigr).

(3.37)

The integration contour in (
3.37
) is the unit circle,
along which the integrand is regular.
The differential element
{\rm d}y/(y-1)^{2}
is invariant if
y
is changed into
1/y
.
This ensures the expected symmetry
C_{n}=C_{-n}
.

Henceforth we consider
n\geq 0
for definiteness.
The integral in (
3.37
) receives contributions
from the poles of
\Phi(1/y)
at
y_{m}={\rm e}^{{\rm i}q_{m}}=z_{\star}/z_{m}
(see (
3.25
)).

Let us assume for a while that all the zeros
z_{m}
are simple and that
\Phi(\infty)
is finite.
In this situation, the correlation function is a finite sum of decaying exponentials:

C_{n}=\sum_{m=1}^{\Delta-1}A_{m}\Bigl(\frac{z_{\star}}{z_{m}}\Bigr)^{n}\qquad(n\geq 0),

(3.38)

with amplitudes

A_{m}=\frac{z_{\star}}{\ell}\,\frac{2-I(z_{\star})J(z_{m})-I(z_{m})J(z_{\star})}{(z_{\star}-z_{m})^{2}(I^{\prime}(z_{m})J(z_{m})+I(z_{m})J^{\prime}(z_{m}))}.

(3.39)

The correlation therefore decays exponentially with distance, as

C_{n}\sim{\rm e}^{-n/\xi},

(3.40)

where the correlation length reads

\xi=\frac{1}{\ln{\displaystyle{\displaystyle{\left|z_{1}\right|}\over\displaystyle z_{\star}}}}=\frac{1}{{\mathop{\rm Im}\,}q_{1}}.

(3.41)

Let us now relax the two assumptions above.
If the zero
z_{m}
is not simple, but has multiplicity
m\geq 2
,
the corresponding amplitude
A_{m}
in (
3.38
)
is replaced by a polynomial in
n
of degree at most
m-1
.
If
\Phi(\infty)
is not finite,
then
\Phi(y)
grows as
y^{\mu}
at large
y
, where
\mu
is some positive integer.
As a consequence, the formula (
3.38
) for
C_{n}

might be modified for the first few values of the distance
n
(
n=0,\dots,\mu-1
).
In all cases, the correlation length is given by (
3.41
).

The correlation function admits an appealing alternative expression,
which holds in full generality.
Consider the function
\Phi({{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})
defined in (
3.24
).
This function equals unity in the limit
{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}\to 0
,
whereas it vanishes for
q\to 0
as

\Phi({{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})\approx-{\rm i}qR,

(3.42)

with

R=\frac{z_{\star}I^{\prime}(z_{\star})J^{\prime}(z_{\star})}{I^{\prime}(z_{\star})J(z_{\star})+I(z_{\star})J^{\prime}(z_{\star})}=\frac{{\langle i\rangle}{\langle j\rangle}}{\ell}=\rho(1-\rho)\ell.

(3.43)

Moreover, the function
\Phi({{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})
is regular at least for
{\left|{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}}\right|}<1
.
Its poles are indeed situated at
{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q_{m}}
(see (
3.25
)).
It can therefore be expanded as

\Phi({{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})=\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}r_{a}(1-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}aq}).

(3.44)

The amplitudes
r_{a}
are real, but not positive in general.
They obey the sum rules

\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}r_{a}=1,\qquad\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}ar_{a}=R.

(3.45)

Moreover, the rational expression (
3.24
) of
\Phi({{\rm e}^{{\rm i}q}})

implies that the amplitudes
r_{a}
obey a linear recursion of the form

\lambda_{0}r_{a}+\lambda_{1}r_{a+1}+\lambda_{2}r_{a+2}+\cdots=0,

(3.46)

except for finitely many initial values of
a
.
The number of terms involved in this recursion is generically
\Delta
,
the degree of the polynomial
D(z)
introduced in (
2.13
).
The coefficients
\lambda_{0},\,\lambda_{1},\,\lambda_{2}\,\dots

are constants dictated by the model.

In terms of the amplitudes
r_{a}
, the formula (
3.23
) for the structure factor reads

S(q)=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}r_{a}\,\frac{1-\cos aq}{1-\cos q}.

(3.47)

Inserting this expression into (
3.36
), we obtain

C_{n}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}r_{a}I_{a,n},

(3.48)

with

I_{a,n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}q}{2\pi}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}nq}\,\frac{1-\cos aq}{1-\cos q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}a-{\left|n\right|}&(a>{\left|n\right|}),\\
0&(a\leq{\left|n\right|}).\end{array}\right.

(3.49)

Let us henceforth consider
n\geq 0
.
The correlation function has the following expression
in terms of the amplitudes
r_{a}
:

C_{n}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{a=n+1}^{\infty}(a-n)r_{a}.

(3.50)

Using (
3.43
) and (
3.45
), this formula can be recast as

C_{n}=\rho(1-\rho)-\frac{1}{\ell}\biggl(n-\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}(n-a)r_{a}\biggr).

(3.51)

The correlation function therefore obeys the three-term recursion

2C_{n}-C_{n-1}-C_{n+1}=-\frac{r_{n}}{\ell}\qquad(n\geq 1).

(3.52)

As a consequence, the
C_{n}
also obey the linear recursion relation (
3.46
), i.e.,

\lambda_{0}C_{n}+\lambda_{1}C_{n+1}+\lambda_{2}C_{n+2}+\cdots=0,

(3.53)

except for finitely many initial values of
n
,
and
C_{n}
and
r_{n}
share the same exponential decay (
3.40
).

The expression (
3.51
) yields in particular

C_{0}=\rho(1-\rho),\qquad C_{1}=\rho(1-\rho)-\frac{1}{\ell}.

(3.54)

These two results are expected.
The first one is obvious from the definition of
C_{0}
.
The second one expresses that the densities of
{\bullet}{\circ}
and
{\circ}{\bullet}
cluster interfaces
have the expected value, namely

\lim_{m\to\infty}{\langle\eta_{m}(1-\eta_{m+1})\rangle}=\rho-\rho^{2}-C_{1}=\frac{1}{\ell}.

(3.55)

At larger distances (
n\geq 2
), the correlation function is not universal,
in the sense that it depends on the individual amplitudes
r_{a}
.
We have

C_{2}=\rho(1-\rho)-\frac{2-r_{1}}{\ell},\qquad C_{3}=\rho(1-\rho)-\frac{3-2r_{1}-r_{2}}{\ell},

(3.56)

and so on.

4
Three simple examples

In this section we illustrate the above general results on the structure factor
S(q)

and on the correlation function
C_{n}

by considering three simple examples of statistical ensembles,
already studied in
[
44
]
,
which virtually exhaust all cases with
\Delta=1
or
\Delta=2
.

4.1
Flat ensemble

The simplest statistical ensemble of all is the flat one,
where all cluster lengths are permitted.
This corresponds to
{\cal I}={\cal J}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
, hence

I(z)=J(z)=\frac{z}{1-z},

(4.1)

so that

D(z)=1-2z,

(4.2)

and so
\Delta=1
.
We have

z_{\star}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\rho=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\ell=4.

(4.3)

Equation (
3.23
) reads

S(q)=\frac{1}{4},

(4.4)

and so

C_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n0}}{4},

(4.5)

where
\delta_{ab}
is the Kronecker symbol.

The absence of correlations between the occupancies of different sites
reflects the property that each site of the lattice is independently either occupied or empty.
Concomitantly, the sum entering (
3.38
) is empty for
\Delta=1
.

4.2
Isolated empty sites

Our second example is defined by the condition that empty sites (i.e., holes) are isolated.
The ensemble where occupied sites (i.e., particles) are isolated is related
to the present one by exchanging the roles of particles and holes.
Both models share the same structure factor and correlation function.
These ensembles have been considered in several contexts,
including the attractors of repulsion processes
[
51
]

and packings of disks in narrow channels
[
52
,
53
]
.

This example corresponds to
{\cal I}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and
{\cal J}=\{1\}
, hence

I(z)=\frac{z}{1-z},\qquad J(z)=z,

(4.6)

so that

D(z)=1-z-z^{2},

(4.7)

and so
\Delta=2
.
We have

\displaystyle z_{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\approx 0.618033,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{5+\sqrt{5}}{10}\approx 0.723606,\qquad\ell=\frac{5+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 3.618033.

(4.9)

Equation (
3.23
) reads

S(q)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}\,(3+2\cos q)},

(4.10)

and so

C_{n}=\frac{1}{5}\left(-\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\qquad(n\geq 0).

(4.11)

The correlation function is a pure decaying exponential,
in agreement with (
3.38
) for
\Delta=2
.

4.3
Even particle clusters

Our third example is defined by the condition that clusters of particles have even lengths.
This corresponds to

{\cal I}=\{2,4,6,\dots\}
and
{\cal J}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
, i.e.,

I(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{1-z^{2}},\qquad J(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}.

(4.12)

Here again,

D(z)=1-z-z^{2}

(4.13)

and
\Delta=2
, so that
z_{\star}
is given by (
4.8
).
We have

\rho=\frac{5-\sqrt{5}}{5}\approx 0.552786,\qquad\ell=\frac{5+3\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 5.854101.

(4.14)

Equation (
3.23
) reads

S(q)=\frac{2\,(1+\cos q)}{\sqrt{5}\,(3+2\cos q)}.

(4.15)

The structure factor vanishes for
q=\pi
.
This is an extinction of the form (
3.33
) with
K=2
.
The correlation function reads

C_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n0}}{\sqrt{5}}-\frac{1}{5}\left(-\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\qquad(n\geq 0).

(4.16)

The expression for
n=0
is modified with respect to the decaying exponential (
3.38
).
This is an instance of the situation described below (
3.41
), with
\mu=1
.

shows plots of the structure factor
S(q)

against
q/\pi
for the three ensembles investigated in this section.

5
The
k
-mer deposition model

The sequential deposition of
k
-mers is a fully irreversible process
where
k
-mers (clusters of
k
particles) are deposited at random positions
on an initially empty one-dimensional lattice.
The process stops when the system reaches a blocked (or jammed) configuration
where no further
k
-mer can be inserted any more
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
.
The integer
k\geq 2
is the only parameter of the model.
The sequential deposition of dimers (
k=2
) is equivalent to a dimerization model
investigated long ago by Flory
[
57
]
.

The statistical ensemble of blocked configurations of
k
-mers
has been studied by various approaches
[
41
,
42
,
34
,
44
,
43
]
.
In
[
44
]
it has been shown that this ensemble
can be described in terms of independent clusters,
and therefore studied within the present renewal formalism,
with
{\cal I}=\{k,2k,3k,\dots\}
and
{\cal J}=\{1,2,\dots,k-1\}
, so that

I(z)=\frac{z^{k}}{1-z^{k}},\qquad J(z)=\frac{z-z^{k}}{1-z}.

(5.1)

The polynomial
D(z)
can be reduced to

D(z)=1-(z^{k}+z^{k+1}+\cdots+z^{2k-1}),

(5.2)

and so

\Delta=2k-1.

(5.3)

For all values of the integer
k
,
the structure factor is expected to exhibit systematic extinctions
at wavevectors that are multiples of
2\pi/k
(see (
3.33
)).

5.1
The first few values of
k

It is interesting to start by considering the first few values of
k
,
where closed-form formulas can be derived.

Dimers (
k=2
).

This case is the simplest of all.
We have

D(z)=1-z^{2}-z^{3},

(5.4)

and so
\Delta=3
.
We thus recover that

z_{\star}=\frac{1}{6}\bigl((100+12\sqrt{69})^{1/3}+(100-12\sqrt{69})^{1/3}-2\bigr)\approx 0.754877

(5.5)

is the reciprocal of the so-called plastic number
[
44
]
,
whereas

\rho=\frac{2(7+2z_{\star}+3z_{\star}^{2})}{23}\approx 0.822991.

(5.6)

Even in this simple case, (
3.23
) yields a rather lengthy expression for the structure factor.
Repeated use of
D(z_{\star})=0

reduces this expression to a minimal form
where the highest power of
z_{\star}
is
\Delta-1
(here, 2).
We shall do this reduction throughout the following.
We thus obtain

S(q)=\frac{2z_{\star}(1-z_{\star})(1+\cos q)}{A_{2}(\cos q)},

(5.7)

with

\displaystyle A_{2}(\cos q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3-z_{\star}^{2}-2(4-5z_{\star}-5z_{\star}^{2})\cos q

(5.8)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 4(3-2z_{\star}-4z_{\star}^{2})\cos^{2}q.

The extinction at
q=\pi
is clearly visible on (
5.7
).

Trimers (
k=3
).

In this case we have
\Delta=5
, but
D(z)
factors as

D(z)=(1+z^{2})(1-z^{2}-z^{3}),

(5.9)

so that
z_{\star}
is again given by (
5.5
),
whereas

\rho=\frac{3(26+7z_{\star}-2z_{\star}^{2})}{115}\approx 0.786377.

(5.10)

Equation (
3.23
) yields

S(q)=\frac{(1+2(1-z_{\star})\cos q)(1+2\cos q)^{2}}{A_{3}(\cos q)},

(5.11)

with

\displaystyle A_{3}(\cos q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(1-2z_{\star})(2+3z_{\star})+2(9+z_{\star}-13z_{\star}^{2})\cos q

(5.12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 4(4-3z_{\star})(2+3z_{\star})\cos^{2}q-8(3-6z_{\star}-10z_{\star}^{2})\cos^{3}q

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 16(1-2z_{\star})(2+3z_{\star})\cos^{4}q.

The extinction at
q=2\pi/3
is clearly visible on (
5.11
).

The formulas (
5.7
) and (
5.11
)
show that the complexity of the analytical expression of
S(q)

increases fast with the integer
k
.
The structure factor indeed exhibits more and more detailed structures as
k
increases,
as testified by figure
2
.

5.2
Scaling behavior at large
k

Static observables of the
k
-mer deposition model
have been shown in
[
44
]
to exhibit an unusual behavior at large
k
,
where logarithmic corrections to scaling are ubiquitous.
These corrections to scaling already show up in
z_{\star}
.
Setting

z_{\star}=\exp\left(-\frac{u_{\star}}{k}\right),

(5.13)

we find that
u_{\star}
obeys the transcendental equation

u_{\star}{\rm e}^{u_{\star}}\approx k.

(5.14)

This equation holds up to corrections of relative order
1/k
,
which will be consistently neglected throughout the following.
The solution to (
5.14
) reads

u_{\star}\approx W(k),

(5.15)

in terms of the Lambert
W
function.
To leading order, this reads

u_{\star}\approx\ln k.

(5.16)

More precisely, setting

\lambda=\ln k,\qquad\mu=\ln\lambda=\ln\ln k,

(5.17)

we obtain the full asymptotic series

u_{\star}=\lambda-\mu+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}+\frac{\mu(\mu-2)}{2\lambda^{2}}+\frac{\mu(2\mu^{2}-9\mu+6)}{6\lambda^{3}}+\cdots

(5.18)

The main outcomes of the scaling analysis performed in
[
44
]

which are relevant for the present purpose are as follows:

\rho\approx\frac{u_{\star}}{u_{\star}+1},\qquad\ell\approx\frac{u_{\star}+1}{u_{\star}}\,k.

(5.19)

The scaling analysis recalled above can be extended to the structure factor
all over its scaling regime where
q
scales as the inverse of
k
.
Introducing the rescaled wavevector

Q=kq,

(5.20)

we have the estimates

\displaystyle I(z_{\star})\approx\frac{u_{\star}}{k},\qquad I(z_{\star}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}q})\approx\frac{u_{\star}\,{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}Q}}{k},

\displaystyle J(z_{\star})\approx\frac{k}{u_{\star}},\qquad J(z_{\star}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}q})\approx\frac{k}{u_{\star}\mp{\rm i}Q},

(5.21)

so that (
3.23
) evaluates to

S(q)\approx k\,\frac{u_{\star}}{u_{\star}+1}\,\frac{2(1-\cos Q)}{2u_{\star}^{2}(1-\cos Q)+2u_{\star}Q\sin Q+Q^{2}}.

(5.22)

The second factor of this expression is equal to the particle density
\rho

(see (
5.19
)).
The third factor mainly depends on the rescaled wavevector
Q
.
The presence of powers of
u_{\star}

however generates logarithmic corrections to this main scaling behavior.
We have in particular

S(0)\approx\frac{ku_{\star}}{(u_{\star}+1)^{3}}.

(5.23)

An equivalent formula was derived by another route in
[
44
]
.
The expression

C_{0}=\rho(1-\rho)\approx\frac{u_{\star}}{(u_{\star}+1)^{2}}

(5.24)

translates to the non-trivial identity

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}Q\,\frac{1-\cos Q}{2u_{\star}^{2}(1-\cos Q)+2u_{\star}Q\sin Q+Q^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{u_{\star}+1}.

(5.25)

The scaling form (
5.22
),
with its fast decay in
1/Q^{2}
,
corroborates the most salient feature of figure
2
,
namely that most of the intensity concentrates onto smaller and smaller values of
q

as
k
is increased.

Let us focus our attention onto the leading peak of the structure factor,
located slightly before the first extinction at
q=2\pi/k
,
whose height grows rather fast with

The scaling behavior of the position
Q_{\rm max}=kq_{\rm max}
of this peak

The peak position obeys the transcendental equation

u_{\star}=\frac{Q_{\rm max}(2(1-\cos Q_{\rm max})-Q_{\rm max}\sin Q_{\rm max})}{2(1-\cos Q_{\rm max})(Q_{\rm max}-\sin Q_{\rm max})}.

(5.26)

Some algebra yields the following asymptotic series
in inverse powers of
u_{\star}
:

\displaystyle Q_{\rm max}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\left(1-\frac{1}{u_{\star}}+\frac{1}{u_{\star}^{2}}+\frac{\pi^{2}-3}{3u_{\star}^{3}}+\cdots\right),

(5.27)

\displaystyle S_{\rm max}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{\pi^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{u_{\star}}-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3u_{\star}^{3}}+\cdots\right).

(5.28)

The peak height
S_{\rm max}
therefore grows linearly with
k

i.e., faster than

(see (
5.23

by a factor growing as
(\ln k)^{2}
.

Dashed lines with corresponding colors

6
Arrays of Rydberg atoms

This section is devoted to blocked configurations of arrays of Rydberg atoms
on a one-dimensional optical lattice.
We consider the simple setting recalled in section
1
,
where each lattice site occupied by a Rydberg atom
must have at least
b
empty sites on either side.
The integer
b\geq 1
, referred to as the blockade range,
is the only parameter of the model.

Our aim is to investigate the uniform ensemble of all blocked configurations,
where no further atom can be inserted into the system.
Along the lines of earlier works
[
34
,
43
,
44
]
,
blocked configurations consist of isolated occupied sites (the Rydberg atoms)
separated by clusters of holes whose length is at least
b
,
in order to obey the blockade constraint, and at most
2b
,
since an extra Rydberg atom could be inserted in the middle of an empty range of size
2b+1
.

Within the present formalism,
clusters of particles and of holes respectively correspond to

{\cal I}=\{1\}
and
{\cal J}=\{b,b+1,\dots,2b\}
, and so

I(z)=z,\qquad J(z)=\frac{z^{b}(1-z^{b+1})}{1-z}.

(6.1)

The polynomial
D(z)
can be reduced to

D(z)=1-(z^{b+1}+z^{b+2}+\cdots+z^{2b+1}).

(6.2)

This expression coincides with (
5.2
),
with the identification

k=b+1.

(6.3)

We have in particular

\Delta=2b+1.

(6.4)

There is indeed an equivalence, at the level of single configurations,
between blocked configurations of
k
-mers and of Rydberg atoms.
A Rydberg atom followed by
b
empty sites (to its right) can be mapped onto a
k
-mer,
where
b
and
k
are related by (
6.3
).
The mapping between configurations of
k
-mers and of Rydberg atoms
is however not unique
[
43
,
44
]
.
Many observables pertaining to both models are simply related to each other.
For instance, the density of the
k
-mer deposition model
is
k
times larger than the corresponding density of Rydberg atoms.

As a consequence of the above correspondence,
the structure factors of
k
-mers and Rydberg atoms with
b=k-1
share the same denominator.
The latter is indeed a polynomial in
\cos q
that is essentially dictated by
D(z)
.
Both structure factors have different numerators,
if only because there are no extinctions in the case of Rydberg atoms.

The scaling analysis of the

7
Discussion

This work follows on from a previous paper in collaboration with Krapivsky
[
44
]
.
There, we have put forward an alternative approach,
inspired from the theory of renewal processes,
to study
statistical ensembles of constrained configurations of particles on a one-dimensional lattice,
and especially to determine their configurational entropies.
Here, we demonstrate that a range of observables pertaining to such ensembles,
including chiefly their structure factor and correlation function,
can also be investigated by means of the renewal approach.
It is worth recalling that the scope of this approach is restricted to local constraints
which are expressible in terms of the lengths of clusters of occupied and empty sites.

We have emphasized the pivotal importance of the class of rational models,
which encompasses all examples considered in this paper,
and virtually all situations of physical significance.
Within this rational class,
the complexity of a statistical ensemble is measured
by the degree
\Delta
of the polynomial
D(z)
introduced in (
2.13
).
The integer
\Delta
is fully analogous to the dimension of the transfer matrix
in the more traditional transfer-matrix approach.
To mention just one example,
the decomposition (
3.38
) of the correlation function
as a sum of
\Delta-1
decaying exponentials
has a perfect analogue in terms of the subleading eigenvalues of the transfer matrix.

To close, we briefly tackle the situation of ensembles
of blocked configurations beyond the rational class.
To take a striking example, if
{\cal I}=\{1,2,4,8,\dots\}
,
i.e., the allowed lengths of particle clusters consist of the powers of two,
the corresponding generating series

I(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{2^{k}}

(7.1)

is a prototypical lacunary series,
having the full unit circle of the complex
z
-plane as a natural boundary.
As a consequence, for a generic set
{\cal J}
, the structure factor
S(q)
has a natural boundary
along some curve in the complex
q
-plane.
The structure of the correlation function
is therefore exceedingly more complex than the finite sum (
3.38
).
It would be desirable to have even one physically motivated model of this kind.

It is a pleasure to thank Paul Krapivsky for extensive discussions which motivated this work.

Appendix A
The Hendricks-Teller model

In this appendix we illustrate
the renewal approach used in the body of this paper on a simpler structural model,
namely the Hendricks-Teller model
[
45
]
, defined as follows.
Identical pointlike atoms are put along an infinite half-line at random positions
x_{n}

such that
x_{0}=0
,
and the distances

\ell_{n}=x_{n}-x_{n-1}

(1.1)

are independent and identically distributed with some continuous distribution
\rho(\ell)

such that
{\langle\ell^{2}\rangle}
is convergent.

The (random) Fourier amplitude
G_{N}(q)
and Fourier intensity
S_{N}(q)

of the first
N
atoms read

G_{N}(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}qx_{n}},\qquad S_{N}(q)=\frac{{\left|G_{N}(q)\right|}^{2}}{N}.

(1.2)

Our main interest is in the structure factor

S(q)=\lim_{N\to\infty}{\langle S_{N}(q)\rangle},

(1.3)

which is a self-averaging quantity.

The Fourier amplitudes obey the renewal equation

G_{N}(q)={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q\ell_{1}}+{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q\ell_{1}}G_{N-1}(q).

(1.4)

The mean Fourier amplitude can be derived
by averaging (
1.4
) over the atomic positions.
This yields

{\langle G_{N}(q)\rangle}=g(q)+g(q){\langle G_{N-1}(q)\rangle},

(1.5)

where

g(q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}q\ell}\rho(\ell)\,{\rm d}\ell

(1.6)

is the Fourier transform of the distribution of interatomic distances.
In the limit of an infinitely long sample,
the mean amplitude therefore reads

{\langle G(q)\rangle}=\frac{g(q)}{1-g(q)}.

(1.7)

This result diverges at small wavevectors as

{\langle G(q)\rangle}\approx\frac{1}{{\rm i}q{\langle\ell\rangle}}\qquad(q\to 0).

(1.8)

The structure factor can be derived by multiplying (
1.4
)
by its complex conjugate, using (
1.2
), and averaging over the atomic positions.
This yields

N{\langle S_{N}(q)\rangle}=1+{\langle G_{N-1}(q)\rangle}+{\langle{\overline{G_{N-1}}}(q)\rangle}+(N-1){\langle S_{N-1}(q)\rangle}.

(1.9)

In the
N\to\infty
limit,
the structure factor therefore reads

S(q)=1+{\langle G(q)\rangle}+{\langle{\overline{G}}(q)\rangle}.

(1.10)

Using (
1.7
), we thus recover the main result of
[
45
]
for the present model, i.e.,

S(q)=\frac{1-g(q){\overline{g}}(q)}{(1-g(q))(1-{\overline{g}}(q))}.

(1.11)

Figure 6:
Structure factor (
1.14
) of the Hendricks-Teller model
with a uniform distribution of interatomic distances over the interval
[0,a]
,
plotted against
qa/(2\pi)
.

Let us mention a few properties of the above formula.
Whenever the distance distribution
\rho(\ell)
is continuous,
its Fourier transform
g(q)
falls off to zero at large
q
,
and so the structure factor goes to the limit

S(\infty)=1.

(1.12)

Its value at
q=0
,

S(0)=\frac{{\langle\ell^{2}\rangle}-{\langle\ell\rangle}^{2}}{{\langle\ell\rangle}^{2}},

(1.13)

is nothing but the reduced variance of interatomic distances.
This quantity may be either smaller or larger than unity.

The exponential distance distribution
\rho(\ell)=(1/a)\exp(-\ell/a)

yields a Poissonian distribution of atoms, for which we have
S(q)=1
for all
q
, as expected.

A simple yet non-trivial example
is provided by a uniform distribution of interatomic distances over some interval
[0,a]
.
The ensuing structure factor

S(q)=\frac{(qa)^{2}+2(\cos qa-1)}{(qa)^{2}+2(1-\cos qa-qa\sin qa)}

(1.14)

is a function of the product
qa
, starting from
S(0)=1/3

and exhibiting slowly damped oscillations with period
2\pi
around its limit
S(\infty)=1

(see figure
6
).
